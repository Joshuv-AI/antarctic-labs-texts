INDETERMINATE1-mediated expression of FT family genes is required for proper timing of flowering in Brachypodium distachyon
Authors: Bing Liu, Daniel P. Woods, Weiya Li, Richard M. Amasino
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe timing of flowering is a critical developmental process in the plant life cycle, and many species adapted to temperate climates use seasonal cues such as day-length and temperature for proper timing. A screen for mutants in short-day (SD)-mediated vernalization in Brachypodium distachyon identified INDETERMINATE1 (ID1) as a regulator of FT-LIKE9 (FTL9)—a member of the FLORIGEN family of genes involved in SD vernalization. BdID1 (INDETERMINATE1 in B. distachyon) is also involved in LD (long days) flowering and cold-mediated vernalization by regulating expression of FLORIGEN family genes. Thus, BdID1 is a key flowering promoter required for several flowering pathways in the temperate grass B. distachyon.

Optimal timing of flowering is one of the most critical developmental processes in the plant life cycle. Many plant species have evolved the ability to flower in response to the perception of seasonal cues, such as changing temperature and day-length, to ensure flowering, and seed production occurs during favorable conditions (1, 2). In many plant species, a prolonged period of winter cold (vernalization) is required for plants to flower in the spring (1, 3). In addition to the cold of winter, some plants can also sense the short day-lengths (SD) of winter as another seasonal cue that can confer the competence to flower in spring through a process called SD vernalization (4–6). Notably, in many plant species, cold or SD exposure alone is not sufficient to induce flowering; exposure to an additional cue such as the lengthening of days experienced during spring is also required. Thus, in these plant species, cold- or SD-mediated vernalization confers developmental competence to flower (1, 3). The requirement for vernalization, via either cold or SD exposure, combined with inductive long days (LD) ensures that plants flower in the spring (7, 8).

Most accessions of the temperate grass model Brachypodium distachyon (B. distachyon) require cold vernalization to become competent to flower in favorable LD photoperiods (8, 9). Recently, we and others have shown that B. distachyon contains orthologs of major “vernalization” genes described in wheat and barley including VERNALIZATION1 (VRN1), VERNALIZATION2 (VRN2), and VERNALIZATION3 (VRN3) that are functionally conserved with respect to their role in flowering (10–12). VRN3 is more commonly known as FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT) and the B. distachyon version will be referred to as FT1 (FLOWERING LOCUS T1). FT encodes a mobile protein known as florigen that travels from leaves to the shoot apical meristem and is essential for initiation of flowering in angiosperms (13, 14). In B. distachyon, FT1 is only expressed in LD (12). Given the importance of FT1 to flowering, it is not surprising that multiple pathways, including the vernalization pathway, have evolved that affect the balance of promoting or repressing FT1 expression to optimize the timing of flowering for reproductive success.

In B. distachyon, wheat, and barley, the MADS box transcription factor VRN1 is induced quantitatively by exposure to cold, and the cold induction of expression is maintained after cold exposure (8, 15–18). VRN1 is a key flowering promoter; knockdown of VRN1 results in extremely delayed flowering under inductive LD photoperiods and overexpression of VRN1 results in rapid flowering (8, 19, 20). The CO-like gene VRN2 is a floral repressor in B. distachyon, wheat, and barley that is expressed most highly in LD; however, unlike the situation in wheat and barley, VRN2 expression does not decrease during vernalization in B. distachyon but rather increases, and VRN2 is not repressed by VRN1 (8, 19, 20). Thus, after cold exposure, the induction of VRN1 in B. distachyon overrides the basal repressive action of VRN2 resulting in elevated FT1 expression and flowering (19, 20). Furthermore, FT1 also promotes VRN1 expression under inductive LD conditions, forming a positive feedback loop that accelerates flowering (10, 16, 21, 22).

Temperate grasses, such as B. distachyon, wheat, barley, rye, and oat, also have a SD vernalization response (4–6, 23). However, only in B. distachyon have any components of the SD vernalization pathway been identified; specifically, SD expression of FTL9 (FT-LIKE9) is necessary for VRN1 and FT1 to be induced after shifting to inductive LD to trigger flowering (6). FTL9 expression is dependent on day-length with elevated expression levels in SD and barely detectable levels in LD because the FTL9 repressor VRN2 is most highly expressed in LD (6, 24). Furthermore, constitutive expression of FTL9 in LD causes rapid flowering demonstrating that expression of FTL9 is sufficient to create a vernalized state (6). Thus, the transient expression of the FLORIGEN/FT homolog FTL9 during SD establishes competence for VRN1 and FT1 to be expressed subsequently in LD.

In maize, mutations in the INDETERMINATE 1 (ZmID1) gene cause plants to remain in a prolonged vegetative growth stage; when severe id1 mutants transition to “flower,” they produce an inflorescence-like structure with vegetative shoot-like characteristics without fully converting to floral meristems (25, 26). This phenotype is associated with low levels of expression of ZCN8, one of the maize FTL (FT-Like) genes (27). ID1 encodes a zinc finger (ZF) domain–containing protein, with four ZFs arranged in tandem, which were named Indeterminate Domains (IDD) (26, 28). Rice id1 mutants also have an extremely delayed flowering phenotype under inductive SD conditions and never flower under LD photoperiods, demonstrating a conserved role in flowering regulation in maize and rice (29–31). Rice OsID1 directly targets the promoter regions of the FLORIGEN/FT genes Hd3a and RFT1 to activate their expression (32).

IDD-containing proteins are specific to plants and divided into eight monophyletic lineages (26, 33–35). Since the first IDD protein, ZmID1, was identified, more IDD proteins have been functionally characterized and shown to be involved in a range of biological processes in plants (for review, see refs. 33 and 35). However, the role of IDD proteins in the flowering of temperate grasses that have a vernalization requirement and flower in response to LD has not been explored.

To identify floral promoters required for the SD vernalization flowering pathway, we screened for mutants that exhibit delayed flowering even after a saturating SD vernalization treatment. From this screen, we identified the ortholog of ZmID1, BdID1 (INDETERMINATE1 in B. distachyon). Transcriptomic analyses indicate that BdID1 is required for FT1, FT2, FTL12, and FTL13 expression under inductive LD photoperiods and for FTL9 expression under noninductive SD photoperiods. The electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) and a dual-luciferase assay indicate that BdID1 directly binds to the promoters of FT1 and FTL9 to activate their expression. Furthermore, overexpression of FT1 in the id1 background results in rapid flowering similar to overexpressing FT1 in the wild type. In addition, we show that a previously uncharacterized FTL gene, FTL4, is up-regulated in the id1 mutant, and plants overexpressing FTL4 display a strong delayed flowering phenotype under inductive LD conditions, indicating that FTL4 is a repressor of flowering. This study provides phenotypic and molecular characterization of ID1 in temperate grasses.

Results and Discussion

Isolation of a Delayed-Flowering Mutant in Response to SD Vernalization in B. distachyon.

To identify genes involved in the SD vernalization flowering pathway in the model temperate grass B. distachyon, we screened for SD vernalization–insensitive mutants that were delayed in flowering when grown in 14-h LD after a 9-wk SD treatment [(6); SI Appendix, Fig. S1]. We identified a mutant with an extremely delayed flowering phenotype that was due to a mutation in INDETERMINATE1 (ID1) (Fig. 1A). The id1 mutant flowers, on average, 95 d after shifting from a 9-wk SD treatment to LD with 26 leaves on the parent culm, whereas wt Bd21-3 flowers at 20 d with 12 leaves on the parent culm (Fig. 1 B and C). The rate of leaf initiation in Bd21-3 and id1 is similar demonstrating that the temporal delay of flowering in id1 is not due to slower growth.

To determine whether the id1 mutant is completely nonresponsive to SD vernalization or has a quantitative response to the length of SD exposure, we conducted a time course in which plants were grown in SD from 5 to 12 wk before the shift to 14-h LD. The Bd21-3 response is saturated by 7 wk of SD exposure as previously reported (6). However, although id1 flowers slightly earlier after the shift from longer SD exposures, there is no significant difference in leaf number at flowering between shorter and longer SD exposures (Fig. 1 B and C), indicating that id1 is fully insensitive to SD vernalization. Note that B. distachyon grows, albeit slowly, during the SD vernalization treatment, and thus, plants subjected to longer SD treatments were more developmentally advanced upon the shift to LD than plants subjected to shorter SD treatments; therefore, it is expected that a fully SD vernalization–insensitive mutant would nevertheless form fewer leaves prior to flowering after the shift to LD as presented in Fig. 1B.

The Extremely Delayed Flowering Phenotype Is Caused by a Mutation in INDETERMINATE 1 in B. distachyon.

To identify the causative lesion in the mutant, we generated an F2 mapping population (n = 494) by crossing the mutant with the polymorphic accession Bd3-1, which has a similar SD vernalization requirement as Bd21-3 [(6); SI Appendix, Fig. S1) and is thus a useful partner to map causative genes in Bd21-3 mutants (6, 36). The mutation is recessive (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A) and maps to a 2.5-Mb interval in the 27-Mb region on chromosome 3 using previously developed indel markers [(36), SI Appendix, Table S1]. To fine-map the causative lesion, whole-genome sequencing of pooled DNA from 100 delayed flowering mutant plants from a segregating F2 population was conducted. Analysis of the sequencing results confirmed that the mutation is in the same region indicated by indel markers (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B), and the only candidate mutation in this region predicted to have a strong effect on the encoded protein is a G-to-A change in a splice acceptor site in the first intron of Bradi3g26910 (id1-1) which encodes the ortholog of the maize INDETERMINATE 1 (ID1) gene [(28) and Fig. 1D]. Additionally, we found a missense mutation (G to A) in ID1 resulting in a change in a conserved amino acid residue from glycine to glutamate (SI Appendix, Figs. S4A and S6). The B. distachyon ID1 gene consists of three exons and two introns, and the mutation in the splice acceptor of the first intron leads to incorrect splicing of the mRNA, resulting in a shift in the reading frame and a premature stop codon (Fig. 1D). This mutant was back-crossed three times with Bd21-3. Genotyping the final back-crossed segregating population using Derived Cleaved Amplified Polymorphic Sequence (dCAPs) markers based on the G to A splice site lesion indicates that the delayed flowering phenotype is fully linked to the id1-1 mutation (SI Appendix, Fig. S3).

We also identified another allele, id1-2 (NaN0962), from the publicly available sodium azide B. distachyon mutant population (https://jgi.doe.gov/indexed-collection-of-brachy-mutants/) (37). Surprisingly, the lesion in id1-2 is identical to the splice site lesion of the id1-1 mutant allele, but the id1-2 allele does not contain the additional missense mutation that is present in id1-1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). To distinguish between id1-1 and id1-2, we designed another dCAPs marker based on the missense mutation that is unique to id1-1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). id1-2 plants have a similar delayed flowering phenotype as id1-1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). Complementation tests in the F1 and F2 generations from a cross between id1-1 and id1-2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C) indicate that id1-1 and id1-2 are indeed allelic, confirming that the causative mutation of the delayed flowering phenotype is due to mutations in ID1.

The IDD is specific to plants, and IDD-containing proteins form a large family (33). Using the ID-domain sequence, we identified 13 paralogs of BdID1 in B. distachyon (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Phylogenetic analysis using 96 IDD-containing proteins from Arabidopsis thaliana, Oryza sativa, Zea mays, Triticum aestivum, Hordeum vulgare, and B. distachyon indicates that BdID1 is in a monocot-specific clade with OsID1 and ZmID1 (26, 28–31) consistent with a previous analysis indicating that the ID1 clade is specific to monocots [(35), SI Appendix, Fig. S5]. Maize ID1 is required for proper timing of flowering and also for proper inflorescence development because the severe id1 mutant produces aberrant inflorescences with vegetative characteristics (28). In rice, in addition to impacting the timing of flowering, an id1 mutation results in small seeds (30). However, the only visibly detectable phenotype in the B. distachyon id1 mutant is a strong delay of flowering; inflorescence and seed development are similar to the wt. Sequence alignments of ID1 from rice, B. distachyon, and maize showed that the N-terminal region, especially the ID-domain sequence, is highly conserved. However, the sequence after the IDD is divergent, which might contribute to the different range of functional roles of ID in the three species (SI Appendix, Fig. S6).

Analysis of BdID1 Expression.

We determined the relative levels of BdID1 mRNA in developing leaves at a variety of different stages as well as in spikelets and root tissue (SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). BdID1 is expressed in all tissues with the highest expression level in developing leaves, consistent with the expression pattern in maize (38). In maize, however, ID1 mRNA was barely detected in roots, whereas BdID1 mRNA is readily detected in B. distachyon roots, although mRNA levels are not as high as in immature leaves (SI Appendix, Fig. S7A).

To evaluate the expression of ID1 under different photoperiods, plants were grown in 8-, 12-, 16-, and 20-h day-lengths. There is no difference in BdID1 mRNA levels under different photoperiods, indicating that BdID1 is not affected by the length of the day (SI Appendix, Fig. S7B). We also determined whether BdID1 exhibited a diurnal expression pattern over a 24-h cycle. In 20-h LD day-lengths, mRNA levels of BdID1 were similar at all time points; however, in 8-h SD, mRNA levels oscillated with a peak at zeitgeber time (ZT) 16 (SI Appendix, Fig. S7C). In maize, the transcript levels (as well as protein levels) of ZmID1 also do not exhibit diurnal expression under LD (38).

There is a wide range of flowering behavior among B. distachyon accessions in response to day-length and the duration of cold exposure needed to saturate the vernalization response (8, 39). To explore whether some of the variation in flowering behavior might be associated with differences in the relative expression levels of BdID1, we evaluated expression levels in the extremely rapid flowering accession Bd21, the rapid flowering accession Bd21-3, and a delayed flowering accession Bd1-1. As shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S7D, there was no significant difference in BdID1 mRNA levels in different accessions grown in 16-h LD. Also, after 3 or 8 wk of cold exposure, BdID1 expression was similar in all accessions, indicating that BdID1 expression is not modulated by a vernalizing cold treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S7D). Thus, the level of BdID1 expression does not appear to contribute to variation in flowering behavior among accessions.

BdID1 Is Required for the Proper Expression of FTL9 in SD.

To investigate how ID1 affects flowering in the SD vernalization pathway, we examined the expression of FTL9 in the id1 mutant. FTL9 is a FLORIGEN/FT homolog for which transient SD-specific expression confers competence to flower but does not trigger flowering like FLORIGEN/FT [(6); SI Appendix, Fig. S8]. The youngest fully expanded leaves at the four-leaf stage under SD conditions were harvested at 4-h intervals over a 24-h period. In Bd21-3, the transcription levels of FTL9 started to increase at ZT 12 and reached a peak at dawn. In contrast, FTL9 was barely detected at all time points in the id1 mutant (Fig. 2A), indicating that BdID1 is necessary for the proper expression of FTL9 in SDs.

To determine whether upregulation of FTL9 could promote id1 flowering, we introduced UBI:FTL9 into the id1 mutant. Constitutive expression of FTL9 from the UBIQUITIN promoter (UBI) in wt plants results in plants behaving as if they had been exposed to SD vernalization [(6), Fig. 2C]. Constitutive expression of FTL9 in the id1 background resulted in more rapid flowering, but the UBI:FTL9/id1 plants were still delayed by nearly a month compared to UBI:FTL9 in wt plants (Fig. 2 C and D). As discussed below, the lack of full rescue of the id1 mutant phenotype by constitutive FTL9 expression is due to the requirement of BdID1 for proper expression of other FT family genes that are required for flowering.

The expression of FTL9 is inversely correlated with VRN2 at different day-lengths [(6), SI Appendix, Fig. S13H]. To determine whether downregulation of FTL9 in id1 is caused by an increase of VRN2 mRNA levels, we evaluated the expression of VRN2 over a 24-h diurnal cycle. Although the relative expression levels of VRN2 in SD are not as high as those in LD in Bd21-3 (6), an apparent peak was seen at the beginning of dusk (ZT 8) (Fig. 2B). At this time point, the mRNA levels of VRN2 were not significantly different between Bd21-3 and id1, indicating that BdID1 impacts FTL9 expression levels independently of VRN2.

Loss of BdID1 Significantly Delays Flowering under Inductive LD.

Loss or reduction of FTL9 activity does not affect B. distachyon flowering under strongly inductive 20-h day-lengths consistent with the SD-vernalization specificity of FTL9 (6). However, id1 flowering was delayed under 20-h LDs with or without a saturating SD vernalization treatment (Fig. 3 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S9), indicating that BdID1 is required for other flowering pathways in addition to the SD-vernalization flowering pathway.

B. distachyon is an obligate LD plant, requiring photoperiods of at least 14 h or longer in order to flower even if vernalized (8). In 14-, 16-, and 20-h day-lengths, id1 mutants exhibited an extremely delayed flowering phenotype (Fig. 3 A and B). Even under strongly inductive 20-h LD conditions, id1 mutants flower at around 120 d after germination with 23 leaves on its parent culm, whereas Bd21-3 flowers at 30 d with 8 leaves on the parent culm (Fig. 3 A and B).

BdID1 Is Required for Activating FTL Genes.

VRN1 and FT1 are important promoters of flowering under LD in B. distachyon. Thus, we examined the expression of both genes in the id1 mutant background. Under 20-h LD conditions, mRNA was isolated at 4-h intervals over 24 h. We harvested a sample at ZT 22 to capture a time point in the dark. The expression patterns of VRN1 and FT1 during LD were similar in Bd21-3; both genes increase in the middle of the day and reach a peak at the end of the day (ZT 20), followed by decreased expression during the dark (Fig. 3 C and D). By contrast, VRN1 and FT1 were barely detectable across all time points tested in the id1 mutant (Fig. 3 C and D). Thus, an id1 lesion leads to loss of FT1 expression in LD as well as loss of FTL9 expression in SDs (Fig. 2A). As discussed in the next section, the reduction of VRN1 expression in id1 is likely to be a result of the reduction in FT1 expression.

FT1 is likely to the primary gene functioning providing florigen activity in B. distachyon because knockdown or knockout mutants of FT1 results in extremely delayed flowering (40) and overexpression of FT1 in B.distachyon results in extremely rapid flowering under noninductive 8-h photoperiods and also in a phyC mutant background (PHYTOCHROME C is an essential light receptor required for photoperiodic flowering in B. distachyon) (36). Overexpression of FT1 in the id1 mutant also results in the same rapid flowering as overexpression in wt Bd21-3 (Fig. 4 A and B), indicating that FT1 is one of the targets of BdID1 and that the strongly delayed flowering of id1 mutants in LD (SI Appendix, Fig. S9) is due to greatly reduced FT1 expression.

Photoperiod pathway genes PHOTOPERIOD1 (PPD1) and CONSTANS (CO1) are promoters of flowering in B. distachyon that are required for the activation of FT1 expression in LD (24, 41). Thus, we were interested to determine to what extent ID1 impacts the expression of PPD1 and CO1. The relative expression level of CO1 is lower in the id1 mutant than in Bd21-3 (SI Appendix, Fig. S10A). In rice and maize, the mRNA levels of Hd1 (the rice CO ortholog) and CONZ1 (the maize CO ortholog) are also decreased in id1 mutants (30, 31, 42). Thus, a component of the promotion of FT1 by ID1 may be a result of the promotion of CO1 expression by ID1. However, Hd1 and the FT1 homologous genes Hd3a and RFT1 in rice are the direct targets of OsID1 (32, 43) indicating that ID1 impacts FT1 expression both directly as well as indirectly via the rice CO1 ortholog. PPD1 was also lower in the id1 mutants than in Bd21-3 (SI Appendix, Fig. S10B). PPD1 is down-regulated in co1 mutants in wheat, and PPD1 interacts with CO1 at the protein level (44). The decreased mRNA levels of PPD1 in id1 mutants might result from lower CO1 expression. Thus, ID1 may regulate FT1 expression in part via CO1 and PPD1 but also by directly binding to the FT1 promoter.

DNA selection and amplification binding assays indicate that ZmID1 binds specifically to an 11 bp-motif in vitro (5′-TTTGTCGTTTT-3′) (45), whereas in rice, it has been reported that OsID1 binds to a 6 bp motif (5′-TTTGTC-3′) present in rice florigen genes that is a subset of the 11 bp-motif (32, 43). Additionally, DNA affinity purification sequencing in Arabidopsis indicates that IDD-containing proteins (IDD2/4/ 5/7/11) bind this 6-bp motif (http://neomorph.salk.edu/dap_web/pages/index.php) (46). Indeed, the motif (5′-TTTGTC-3′) is also present in the promoter region of B. distachyon FT1 (Nucleotides −289 to −284, upstream of the TSS) and FTL9 (Nucleotides −457 to −452, upstream of the TSS). To evaluate whether BdID1 binds to the same motifs found in FTL promoters in maize and rice, we performed an EMSA with BdID1 and regions of the FT1 and FTL9 promoters containing this motif (SI Appendix, Table S1). As is shown in Fig. 4C, GST-BdID1, but not GST, bound to FT1 and FTL9. We then used a dual-luciferase reporter assay to evaluate whether BdID1 directly induces FT1 and FTL9 expression. Expression of BdID1 strongly increased the expression of the firefly luciferase reporter driven by the FT1 and FTL9 promoters in B. distachyon protoplasts (Fig. 4D). Thus, BdID1 appears to directly activate FT1 and FTL9 expression by binding to the conserved promoter motifs similar to the situation in maize and rice. Unlike B. distachyon and many other temperate grasses, the subtropical grasses rice and maize do not have a vernalization requirement, and their flowering is induced in SD photoperiods. Thus, although B. distachyon flowers in response to different environmental cues than maize and rice, in the three species, id1 mutants exhibit extremely delayed flowering phenotypes under all tested conditions because ID1 acts downstream of environmental-sensing pathways by the direct regulation of FT.

BdID1 impacts the expression of FT1 under LD and FTL9 under SD indicating that BdID1 might broadly impact the expression of FTL genes, which motivated us to explore the expression of other FT family genes in the id1 mutant background. FT2 is the most closely related paralog of FT1 (6). FT2 knockout or knockdown mutants have a minor impact on affecting flowering time, but overexpression of FT2 strongly accelerates flowering (47, 48). The relative expression level of FT2 is much lower than that of FT1 in Bd21-3 which is consistent with the degree to which ft2 and ft1 mutations affect flowering, and, like FT1, FT2 has a diurnal expression pattern in which expression levels begin to increase at ZT 12 and reach a peak in the middle of the dark (ZT 22) (Fig. 3 D and E). Also, like FT1, FT2 expression levels are greatly reduced in the id1 mutant throughout a diurnal cycle, especially at ZT 22 (Fig. 3E).

We then evaluated the effect of an id1 mutation on the expression of three additional FTL genes, FTL10, FTL12, and FTL13, which are more distantly related paralogs to FT1 and FT2 (6, 11, 49). FTL10 is a paralog of FTL9 resulting from a gene duplication in grasses (6). Constitutive expression of FTL10 in rice (OsFTL10), barley (HvFT3), and B. distachyon strongly promote flowering in SD (6, 50, 51). FTL10 is expressed at a very low level with a peak at ZT 20, and expression was much reduced in the id1 mutant, especially at ZT 20 (Fig. 3F). FTL12 and FTL13 are closely related paralogs. The expression level of FTL12 is decreased in the id1 mutant, especially at ZT 4, ZT 8, and ZT 22 (Fig. 3G). In the wt, FTL13 expression increases starting at ZT16 and peaks at the end of dark in Bd21-3, but at most time points, FTL13 was barely detectable in the id1 mutant (Fig. 3H).

Mutations in or overexpression of FT1 can impact the expression of some FT family genes. For example, knockdown of FT1 results in lower levels of FT2 and FTL12 mRNA but does not affect FTL9 and FTL10 expression (40). OsFTL1, the ortholog of FT2, is also expressed at lower levels in the rtf1/Hd3a double mutant (52). Therefore, a component of the greatly reduced expression of certain FTL genes in the B. distachyon id1 mutant might be due to reduced FT1 expression. However, our observation that the expression level of all FT family genes analyzed is reduced in id1, including FTL9, FTL10, and FTL13, which are not affected by alteration of FT1 activity (40), supports a model in which ID1 is a general regulator of FT family genes in B. distachyon.

The function of FTL12 and FTL13 has not been studied in B. distachyon. Overexpression of barley HvFT4, which is a member of the FTL12 clade, delays flowering and spikelet initiation under LD conditions, and this delay is associated with reduced expression of three AP1-like genes, VRN-H1, HvBM3, and HvBM8, in the leaves and meristem (53). However, overexpression of BdFTL12 (the B. distachyon ortholog of HvFT4 encoding a protein that is 94% similar to HvFT4) using the UBI (UBI:FTL12) in B. distachyon did not affect the timing of flowering (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). Interestingly, in the subtropical grass maize, another member of the FT family ZCN26, which corresponds to FTL12 in B. distachyon, is increased in the maize id1 mutant (42). However, overexpression of ZCN26 driven by the UBI in maize does not alter the floral transition (27). There are 6 additional FTL genes (FTL4, FTL5, FTL6, and FTL7a/b/c) in B. distachyon that are more distantly related to the FT1 clade (11). No functional studies of these six FTL genes have been reported in B. distachyon or other grass species (49).

In maize, ZCN8 appears to be the FT family member accounting for most of the florigen activity, and the extremely delayed flowering of maize id1 mutants is associated with decreased ZCN8 levels (27). BdFTL10 is the B. distachyon ortholog of ZCN8 and groups in a clade with barley and wheat FTL3 and FTL5 (6). Although when overexpressed FTL10 strongly promotes flowering (6), the very low level of FTL10 expression in wt indicates that FTL10 plays a minor a role in B. distachyon flowering in contrast to the more prominent role of orthologs in maize, barley, and wheat (6). Nevertheless, the very low levels of FTL10 mRNA appear to be reduced further in the id1 mutant (Fig. 3F) consistent with an ancestral role for ID in FTL10 regulation.

BdID1 Is Required for FT1 Activation, But Not VRN1 Expression, in Response to Cold Vernalization.

Many accessions of B. distachyon require cold-promoted vernalization for flowering, especially when grown under 14 to 16 h of light (8, 9), and vernalization is associated with VRN1 up-regulation (8). Previous studies (8, 54, 55) and our results (SI Appendix, Figs. S13 B and E) indicate that VRN1 and FT1 interact in a positive feedback loop in grasses. VRN1 mRNA levels are reduced in the id1 mutant under LD inductive conditions (Fig. 3C). To determine whether the reduced VRN1 expression in the id1 mutant is due to a direct effect of ID1 on VRN1 or an indirect effect of the id1 lesion impacting FT1 expression, we evaluated whether VRN1 expression is affected in the id1 mutant in response to cold vernalization in SD—a condition in which FT1 is not induced.

First, we conducted a vernalization time course to determine whether vernalization causes id1 to flower more rapidly. Plants were shifted to 16-h LD at normal growth temperature after 3-, 4-, 5-, 6-, 7-, and 8-wk cold vernalization in SD. We found that 3 wk of cold vernalization significantly accelerates Bd21-3 flowering consistent with previous studies (8, 9) and that id1 exhibits a robust vernalization response after 5 wk of cold exposure (Fig. 5 A and B). Although id1 mutants respond to cold vernalization, they were still delayed in flowering even after 8 wk of cold exposure compared with Bd21-3 (Fig. 5 A and B); thus, the duration of cold exposure required for accelerating flowering is lengthened in id1, and the delayed flowering of id1 cannot be fully rescued by vernalization.

We then evaluated the possible role of ID1 in VRN1 and FT1 expression during and after cold vernalization (Fig. 5C). Prior to cold exposure, Bd21-3 and id1 mutants were grown in 12-h day-lengths to the third leaf stage (Fig. 5C); at this stage, VRN1 and FT1 mRNA levels are quite low (Fig. 5D, NV). Plants were then shifted to cold in SD (8-h light/16-h dark), which mimics the natural cold vernalization conditions (Fig. 5C, during cold). VRN1 expression significantly increases with longer cold exposure in Bd21-3, but FT1 expression remains low during cold (Fig. 5 D, Upper and Middle). After 2 and 4 wk of cold exposure, there was no significant difference in VRN1 expression between Bd21-3 and the id1 mutant (Fig. 5 D, Upper). Notably, a 4-wk cold treatment is saturate the vernalization response in Bd21-3, and at this 4-wk time, VRN1 expression levels were the same in Bd21-3 and the id1 mutant, indicating that BdID1 does not affect cold-mediated VRN1 induction. With longer cold treatments (6- and 8-wk), VRN1 continued to increase in both Bd21-3 and id1, but VRN1 expression levels in id1 were not elevated to the same degree as in Bd21-3 (Fig. 5 D, Upper).

That VRN1 is not a direct target of BdID1 is consistent with the expression profiles of VRN1 and FT1 after shifting to inductive LDs. As shown in Fig. 5 C and E, 10 d after shifting to LD, FT1 is dramatically up-regulated in Bd21-3 after a 4-, 6-, and 8-wk cold treatment. In the id1 mutant, FT1 was undetectable after 4 wk of vernalization (Fig. 5 E, Middle), but VRN1 expression was comparable to that in Bd21-3 (Fig. 5 E, Upper). Notably, a 4-wk cold exposure is a saturating vernalization treatment that enables Bd21-3 to flower rapidly after shifting to LD conditions and strongly induces VRN1 expression (8). The reduced level of FT1 expression, but not of VRN1, after cold exposure in the id1 mutants indicates that BdID1 is involved in regulating FT1 expression post vernalization. Indeed, even after 6- and 8-wk of cold exposure, FT1 was still not expressed in the id1 mutant (Fig. 5 E, Middle). Similar to barley and wheat (17, 54), a positive feedback loop between FT1 and VRN1 exists in B. distachyon [(8, 15), SI Appendix, Fig. S11 B and E]. Although VRN1 mRNA levels increased after longer cold treatments, they were still lower in the id1 mutants relative to Bd21-3 (Fig. 5 E, Upper), which might result from the lower FT1 levels in id1 mutants in the positive feedback loop between FT1 and VRN1.

Another possibility for the lower levels of VRN1 after longer cold treatments in the id1 mutant relative to wt could be altered VRN1 epigenetic modification. In Arabidopsis, for example, cold-induced epigenetic silencing of FLOWERING LOCUS C (FLC) requires VERNALIZATION INSENSITIVE 3 (VIN3) which is expressed only after a certain duration of cold treatment at which point VIN3 combines with Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2) to form a PRC2-VIN3 complex that establishes H3K27me3 repressive chromatin marks at FLC resulting in reduced FLC expression (56–58). A vin3 mutation prevents the full establishment of H3K27me3 at FLC (57). In B. distachyon, VRN1 is enriched with repressive chromatin marks such as H3K27me3 before cold (20, 59, 60), and cold vernalization results in lower H3K27me3 at VRN1 and an increase in the active mark H3K4me3 (20, 59, 60). Loss of ID activity may interfere with this cold-mediated epigenetic activation of VRN1. A role for ID in chromatin modification in grasses was shown in rice in which ID1 interacts physically with the H3K4 and H3K36 methyltransferase SDG722 to promote flowering (43). In addition, ID1 in maize promotes histone H3 hyperacetylation at ZCN7 and ZCN8 and affects the production of their antisense transcript (61).

We also overexpressed VRN1 in the id1 background to determine whether VRN1 could rescue the delayed flowering of id1 similar to FT1 overexpression. As shown in Fig. 5 F and G, VRN1 partially rescued the delayed flowering phenotype of id1; compared with UBI:VRN1 in the Bd21-3 background, UBI:VRN1 id1 flowers 23 d later. It has been shown in barley that VRN1 directly targets the promoter of FT1 for activation (21), and FT1 is strongly induced when VRN1 is overexpressed in wheat and B. distachyon (8, 62), indicating that the FT1 activation by VRN1 binding might be conserved in temperate grasses. FT1 was not as strongly induced by overexpression of VRN1 in the id1 mutants as in Bd21-3 (SI Appendix, Fig. S12), indicating that the maximal induction of FT1 by VRN1 depends on BdID1.

FT2 Expression Increases during Vernalization Similar to Expression of VRN1.

In wheat and B. distachyon, FT2 transcripts increase during vernalization (47, 60). Our data show that FT2 increases quantitatively similar to VRN1 with increasing duration of cold (Fig. 5D). However, the cold-mediated increase in FT2 was significantly lower in id1 than in Bd21-3 in contrast to what we observed with VRN1 (Fig. 5D), indicating that BdID1 is required for the activation of FT2 during a vernalizing cold treatment. Furthermore, the mRNA levels of FT2 were still lower in the id1 mutants compared with Bd21-3 post vernalization (Fig. 5 E, Lower). Although RNAi knockdown of FT2 is reported to have a weak effect on flowering time in nonvernalized B. distachyon (47), the increased FT2 expression in response to cold indicates that FT2 might function in enhancing the vernalization-mediated flowering response.

Modulating VRN1, FT1, and VRN2 Levels Does Not Alter BdID1 Expression.

As discussed above, VRN1 and FT1 participate in a positive feedback loop in which they enhance each other’s expression (10, 16, 21, 22). Given the prevalence of feedback loops in biology and that VRN1 and FT1 expression is lower in the id1 mutant, we evaluated whether ID1 might participate in the VRN1 and FT1 feedback loop. Knockdown or constitutive expression of VRN1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S13C) or constitutive expression of FT1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S13F) did not significantly affect BdID1 expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S13 A and D), indicating that BdID1 is upstream of VRN1 and FT1 and does not participate in a feedback loop with these genes.

VRN2 is a potent repressor of FT1 and FTL9 (6, 8). Therefore, we evaluated whether VRN2 repression of FT1 and FTL9 might involve modulation of BdID1 expression. No changes of BdID1 mRNA levels were observed in plants expressing either an amiVRN2 to reduce VRN2 levels or a UBI:VRN2 construct to increase VRN2 levels (SI Appendix, Fig. S13 G and I) indicating that VRN2 does not modulate BdID1 expression. Additionally, knockdown of VRN2 in the id1 mutant using amiVRN2 results in earlier flowering than in id1 without the VRN2 knockdown, but amiVRN2/id1 plants are more delayed in flowering than in the amiVRN2 line (SI Appendix, Fig. S14). Although both VRN1 and FT1 are increased in amiVRN2 lines (19), amiVRN2/id1 is similar phenotypically to the id1 mutant in which VRN1 is constitutively expressed from the UBI (UBI:VRN1/id1), whereas constitutive expression of FT1 in the id1 mutant (UBI:FT1/id1) results in rapid flowering. This indicates that the observed increase of FT1 in amiVRN2 plants (19) might be due to elevation of VRN1.

Transcriptomic Analysis of the id1 Mutant.

To investigate the effect of BdID1 on gene transcription at a genome-wide level, we conducted RNA-sequencing of the id1 mutant and Bd21-3. Plants were grown in 8- and 20-h day-lengths, and RNA was prepared from leaves in the middle of the light period. In SD, 2,287 genes are down-regulated, and 1,372 genes are up-regulated in the id1 mutant relative to Bd21-3, and in LD, 1,964 genes are down-regulated and 1,727 genes are up-regulated (P < 0.01 and fold-change > 2; Fig. 6A, SI Appendix, Fig. S15, and Dataset S1).

GO enrichment analysis revealed that mis-regulated genes in the id1 mutant were associated with many biological processes, including responses to abiotic and biotic stimuli, metabolic pathways, and developmental pathways, indicating that BdID1 is involved in diverse biological processes in addition to plant flowering (SI Appendix, Figs. S16 and S17) consistent with studies in other species. For example, the Arabidopsis IDD4 protein contributes to induced immunity via modulating salicylic acid and jasmonic acid homeostasis (63), and other IDD proteins have been implicated in regulating gibberellin and auxin signaling (43, 64–69). However, except for the delayed flowering phenotype, we did not observe any visible phenotypes of the id1 mutant compared with Bd21-3 in well-fertilized plants in growth chamber conditions. It will be interesting to investigate, using specific growth conditions and assays, whether loss of BdID1 has a molecular or morphological phenotype related to any of the pathways revealed to be perturbed at the transcriptomic level by GO enrichment analysis.

All tested IDD-containing proteins in Arabidopsis (46) and ID1 in maize (45) and rice (32, 43) can bind to the 6-bp (5′-TTGTC-3′) DNA motif. Given that BdID1 can also bind to this 6-bp (5′-TTGTC-3′) DNA motif present in FTL gene promoter regions (Fig. 4C), we explored whether genes with this motif are enriched in our RNA-seq dataset of genes mis-regulated in the id1 mutant. A genome-wide survey revealed 11,621 genes that contain the 6-bp motif in a promoter region (Dataset S2). However, there was no enrichment for genes containing the motif in the genes that are mis-regulated in the id1 mutant (SI Appendix, Fig. S18), perhaps because BdID1 is required for expression of other regulators in addition to FT and FTL genes and the genes downstream of these other regulators comprise a large percentage of the genes mis-regulated in the id1 mutant.

Analysis of differentially expressed genes in the RNA-seq data set revealed that ID1 is required for full-level expression of all the expressed FTL genes except FTL4 (Bradi4g35040, not to be confused with barley HvFT4 for which the B. distachyon ortholog is BdFTL12/Bradi1g38150). FTL4, which is distantly related to FT1, is up-regulated in the id1 mutant under both LDs and SDs (Fig. 6A). In temperate grasses, most FTL-like genes are promoters of flowering that, when constitutively expressed, result in rapid flowering or competence to flower (6, 8, 24, 40, 47, 48). Given that FTL4 is up-regulated in id1 whereas other FTL genes are down-regulated suggests that FTL4 might act as a floral repressor. Indeed, we found that constitutive expression of FTL4 in Bd21-3 delays flowering by 15 d under a 20-h highly inductive LD photoperiod (Fig. 6 B and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S19). Thus, elevated FTL4 expression represses flowering and its elevated expression level in id1 might contribute to the delayed flowering of id1. Recently, the rice FTL4 ortholog, OsFTL4, was reported to be a flowering repressor, indicating the conserved function of repressing flowering among the FTL4 proteins in grasses (70).

There are 57 MADS-box genes in B. distachyon (71). In addition to VRN1, 9 other MADS-box genes are mis-regulated under inductive LDs in the id1 mutant (Fig. 6 D and E). MADS-box genes encode transcription factors involved in many biological processes, especially in controlling flowering time and floral organ identity (72–74). MADS3 (BdFUL3) is one of the most highly down-regulated genes in the id1 mutant, and the paralog MADS10 (BdFUL2) is also reduced in the id1 mutant (Fig. 6 D and E). FUL2 and FUL3 in wheat have redundant functions promoting flowering, and overexpression of these genes in B. distachyon results in rapid flowering (75, 76). In addition, MADS25 and MADS13 (orthologs of SOC1 in Arabidopsis), BdMADS14 (ortholog of AGAMOUS in Arabidopsis), and BdMADS16 (ortholog of PISTILLATA in Arabidopsis) are all significantly downregulated in the id1 mutant (Fig. 6 D and E). Notably, MADS1, which is the orthologue of SOC1, is also decreased in the maize id1 mutant (42).

Summary

We have shown that the expression of all FT family genes that are expressed at moderate levels in wt is altered in id1 mutants. Specifically, the expression of FT1, FT2, FTL9, FTL10, FTL12, and FTL13 is strongly reduced, whereas the expression of FTL4 is increased in id1 mutants. We find that the FT family gene FTL4 is a repressor of flowering in contrast to other characterized FT family members in B. distachyon that are promoters of flowering.

In temperate grasses that have been studied, FT1 is a major contributor to florigen activity, and FT1 expression is regulated by both the photoperiod and vernalization pathways. The photoperiod pathway begins with light perception via phytochromes and proceeds through CO1 and PPD1. There are two vernalization pathways in B. distachyon corresponding to the two seasonal cues of winter—SD and cold—and both pathways lead to increased expression of the positive FT1 regulator VRN1. The SD vernalization pathway results in the induction of FTL9 which enables VRN1 to be up-regulated in subsequent LD (6). Expression levels of the FT1 regulators in these pathways, VRN1, FTL9, CO1, and PPD1, are reduced in id1 mutants, and thus a portion of the strong reduction of FT1 expression in id1 mutants may be due to lower expression of these positive regulators of FT1. However, we have also shown that ID1 binds directly to a region of the FT1 and FTL9 promoters and thus is likely to play a direct role in promoting FT1 and FTL9 transcription. Therefore, in B. distachyon, ID1 appears to be a key regulator of the expressed members of the FT family of genes as well as a regulator of other genes involved in the control of flowering time.

Methods

Plant Growth Conditions and Flowering Time Measurements.

Seeds were imbibed at 4 °C for 3 d, then placed into soil (Pro-mix FPX) and grown under different day-lengths of ~200 µmol of white light provided by 4,100 K T5 fluorescent bulbs. Plants were fertilized weekly with Powergrow MasterBlend 4-18-38 (0.315 g/l), calcium nitrate (0.315 g/l), and magnesium sulfate (0.158 g/l) adjusted to pH 6.0. For vernalization treatments, germinated seeds were placed at 4 °C under 8-h SD for 3 wk to 9 wk, then shifted to 16-h day-length at ~22 °C to assess the effectiveness of the vernalization treatments. Flowering (time and extent of development from germination to fully emerged spikelets) was assessed as previously described (6, 15). For all experiments, at least 10 plants were used to evaluate flowering time and number of leaves formed on the primary culm. The difference in flowering time and leaf number between Bd21-3 and mutant was assessed using the Student’s t test.

SD Vernalization–Insensitive Mutant Screen and BdID1 Gene Identification.

Seed from self-pollinated, mutagenized plants were screened as previously described (36). Seeds were imbibed at 4 °C for 3 d, and then directly placed into soil and grown in an 8-h photoperiod at ~22 °C during the light phase and 18 °C during the dark phase in a growth chamber for SD vernalization. After a saturating 9-wk SD vernalization treatment, plants were shifted to 14-h LD photoperiods. Potential SD vernalization–insensitive mutants were defined as plants that had yet to flower after 40 d of growth in LD after the shift from SD. To map mutations, we used a modified BSA-sequencing bioinformatic protocol (77). The Galaxy platform [https://usegalaxy.org/, (78)] was used to process the whole genome sequencing data. QC on the genome sequence was performed as previously reported (77). Quality reads were mapped to one parent of F2 population, Bd3-1, for QTL mapping, and version 3 of Bd21 for the variant was annotated by Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (9, 79). An SNP-index was calculated at each SNP position by dividing the total reads by the reads that differ from the reference. Variants with an SNP-index between 0.75 and 1 were plotted in R to identify regions enriched for nearly homozygous SNPs. To identify the candidate variants, BSA-sequencing data of phyC-1 were used as a control to subtract background variants by using the dplyr package in R [https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=dplyr, (36)]. Nearly homozygous variants in the mapping region were annotated using SnaEff (80). The id1 mutant was backcrossed three times with Bd21-3 for all experiments.

Phylogenetic Analysis.

A total of 96 IDD proteins were obtained from A. thaliana, O. sativa, Z. mays, T. aestivum, H. vulgare, and B. distachyon using the IDD from BdID1 as bait. The alignment of the full-length amino acid sequences of ID orthologs in different species was conducted via the ClustalW program (81). Phylogenetic tree analysis was performed using MEGA 4.0 software (82) with the following settings: Neighbor-Joining for tree inference, complete deletion option for each class analysis, and bootstrap test of 1,000 replicates for internal branch reliability.

Gene Expression Analysis.

RNA extraction and quantitative RT-PCR were performed as previously described (8) using the gene-specific primers listed in SI Appendix, Table S1.

Generation of UBI:FTL9 id1, UBI:FT1 id1, UBI:VRN1 id1, amiVRN2 id1, UBI:FTL4, and UBI:FTL12 Plants.

UBI:FTL9, UBI:FT1, UBI:VRN1, and amiVRN2 lines used in this study have been previously described (6, 8, 19). These lines were crossed to the id1 mutant, and the primers used for genotyping are listed in SI Appendix, Table S1. For UBI:FTL4 and UBI:FTL12 plants, FTL4 and FTL12 cDNA were amplified from Bd21-3 using the primers in SI Appendix, Table S1 and cloned into pENTR-D-TOPO (Life Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cloning the FTL4 and FTL12 cDNA into pANIC10A was performed as described previously (8). The resulting construct was then transformed into chemically competent Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain Agl-1. Plant callus transformation was performed at the Wisconsin Crop Innovation Center as described (83).

RNA-seq and Bioinformatic Analysis.

RNA was extracted from newly expanded fourth leaves in the middle of 8-h and 20-h photoperiods. Three replicates per genotype were used. Library creation, quality controls, read alignment, read counting, and gene expression analysis were performed with ngsPipeline (https://github.com/YuhaoL/ngsPipeline) as described previously (84). In brief, over 30 million raw paired-end reads were trimmed with Cutadapt v3.2. The cleaned paired-end reads were aligned to the B. distachyon genome v3.1 (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/brachypan) by HISAT2 v2.1.0 package (85). Samtools v1.9 (86) was used to extract the unique mapped reads. Then, FeatureCounts v1.6.2 was used for counting reads for each annotated gene (86). DEseq2 v1.22.2 (87) was used to identify the differentially expressed genes [fold change ≥2 and padj (the adjusted P value) ≤ 0.01]. Gene annotation and GO enrichment analysis were performed as previously reported (http://geneontology.org/) (88–90). The significantly enriched biological processes (gene number > 10, P.adj < 0.001) were further analyzed. The significantly enriched biological processes (gene number > 10, P.adj < 0.001) were selected to generate the GO analysis figure (SI Appendix, Fig. S16) and the cellular interaction plot (SI Appendix, Fig. S17).

EMSA.

Full-length cDNA of BdID1 was amplified using primers in SI Appendix, Table S1, and then cloned into the pGEX-4T-3 expression vector, resulting in GST-BdID1. Protein expression and purification were performed using a kit according to the manufacturer’s directions (GE Healthcare). EMSA was performed using the LightShift™ Chemiluminescent EMSA kit (20148, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). In brief, 5 mg GST-BdID1, 0.02 mM probe labeled with Biotin (SI Appendix, Table S1), 2 mL binding buffer, 0.5 mL poly (dI-dC), or 100-fold unlabeled probe as a competitor were incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The mixture was subjected to electrophoresis at 100 V for 2 h using 5% polyacrylamide gel, and then transferred to a positive-charged nylon membrane (INYC00010, Millipore, MA, USA). After ultraviolet cross-linking, the membrane was treated with the LightShift Chemiluminescent EMSA Kit for detection.

Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay.

Promoters (FT1 and FTL9) were amplified and inserted into the pGREEN-0800-LUC vector using the primers listed in SI Appendix, Table S1. For the reporter construct, full-length cDNA of BdID1 fused to the sequence encoding hemagglutinin (HA) was amplified and inserted into pDONR-ZEO (Life Technologies) resulting in pDONR-ZEO-BdID1. pDONR-ZEO-BdID1 was recombined into pANIC10A according to the manufacturer’s protocol. B. distachyon protoplast isolation and transformation were performed using 14-d-old seedlings grown under 16-h LD conditions. Briefly, seedlings were excised and incubated in enzyme buffer A (1.5% cellulose RS, 0.75% macerozyme, 0.6 M mannitol, 10 mM MES, 10 mM CaCl2, and 0.1% bovine serum albumin, pH 5.7) for 4 h. After releasing the protoplast by adding W5 buffer (154 mM sodium chloride, 125 mM CaCl2, 5 mM KCl, and 2 mM MES, pH 5.7), the harvested cells were resuspended in MMG buffer (0.4 M mannitol, 15 mM MgCl2, and 4 mM MES, pH 5.7). Then, 10 mg of each vector was added to the protoplasts with an equal volume of 40% PEG buffer (40% PEG4000, 0.2 M mannitol, and 0.1 mM CaCl2) for transformation, and then the protoplasts were washed two times using W5 buffer and resuspended in the W5 buffer. After incubating for 12 to 16 h, the protoplasts were collected, and firefly and Renilla luciferase were quantified using a dual-luciferase assay kit (E1910, Promega).
