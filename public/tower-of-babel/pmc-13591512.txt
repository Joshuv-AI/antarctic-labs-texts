Gender Disparities Among Mitral Transcatheter Edge-to-Edge Repair and Transcatheter Mitral Valve Replacement Operators in the United States
Authors: Smitha N Gowda, Emily Cendrowski, Atefeh Ghorbanzadeh, Teodora Donisan, Trevor Simard, Mohamad Alkhouli, Kimberly Holst, Mackram Eleid, Charanjit Rihal, Mayra E Guerrero
Published: 2026

Despite efforts to promote diversity in cardiology, women remain markedly underrepresented in procedural subspecialties. They account for only 5% of percutaneous coronary intervention operators and 7% of cardiothoracic surgeons.1,2 Among 1806 transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) operators in 2018, only 3.9% were women, with only 1.5% interventional cardiologists.3

We hypothesized that gender disparities are even more pronounced for transcatheter mitral procedures, including transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) and transcatheter mitral valve replacement (TMVR). We analyzed national Medicare data to evaluate gender representation, temporal trends, and geographic distribution among US operators performing these procedures.

Operators performing mitral TEER and TMVR between 2015 and 2023 were identified from the Medicare Provider Utilization and Payment Database using Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes 33418 and 0483T, respectively. Physicians performing 10 or fewer procedures annually were excluded because the Medicare CMS dataset redacts these data to protect patient privacy. Variables included operator self-reported gender, as captured in the Medicare database, specialty, location, and annual procedural volume. The study used publicly available deidentified data and was therefore exempt from institutional review.

In 2023, 318 operators performed >10 TEER procedures: 88% (n = 280) interventional cardiologists and 12% (n = 38) cardiac surgeons. Only 7 (2.2%) were women—all interventional cardiologists. Of 6165 total TEER procedures, women performed 107 (1.7%), whereas men performed 6058 (98.3%). Median annual volumes were 16 for men vs 13 for women.

Between 2015 and 2023, annual TEER volume increased nearly 5-fold (1145 to 6165), and the number of operators rose from 60 to 318. Women operator numbers, however, remained nearly static (2 in 2015, 9 in 2020, and 7 in 2023), with women performing 1.6% to 2.8% of annual procedures (Figure 1). In 2023, 44 states (88%) had no women operators performing >10 TEERs annually.

For TMVR, 10 operators performed >10 procedures in 2022 (6 interventional cardiologists and 4 cardiac surgeons), including 1-woman interventional cardiologist (10%). Of 178 TMVR procedures, women performed 16 (8.9%). Median annual volumes were 17 for men vs 16 for women. Despite a 10-fold increase in TMVR procedures (12-180) between 2018 and 2023, the number of women operators did not increase (Figure 1).

These findings highlight profound and persistent gender disparity among US mitral TEER and TMVR operators. Although procedural and operator volumes have expanded, the proportion of women operators remains exceedingly low. Compared with 3.9% women representation among TAVR operators,3 the proportion of women TEER operators in our analysis was smaller, at 2.2%. Moreover, 88% of US states had no women TEER operator performing >10 cases annually—more than double the 38% reported for TAVR. The proportionally fewer women TEER/TMVR operators compared with TAVR may be, to an extent, explained by lower overall procedural volumes for mitral interventions and the exclusion of operators performing ≤10 procedures annually.

Informal surveys among women structural interventionalists also suggest similar views, including overall lower mitral procedural volumes and the concentration of mitral procedures among cath lab leadership and mid- to late-career interventionalists. Although the number of women interventionalists is increasing, there remain very few women catheterization lab directors, department chairs, and service line directors, and persistent challenges in career retention continue. Despite the increasing proportion of women matriculating into medical school, women representation in cardiology remains disproportionately low, particularly within procedural subspecialties. Underrepresentation of women in interventional cardiology affects workforce diversity, clinical leadership, and patient enrollment in research. Khan et al4 found that greater women representation in clinical trial leadership was associated with higher enrollment of women, with each 1% increase in women authors corresponding to a 19% increase in women participants.

Despite initiatives promoting gender equity, progress has been slow. Hospitals with women in leadership roles were 4-fold more likely to have women left atrial appendage occluder implanters; however, only 0.7% of women served as cath lab directors and 2.6% as chiefs of cardiology.5 The scarcity of women among mitral TEER and TMVR operators is likely to limit women’s leadership and representation in pivotal transcatheter mitral trials.

Additional evidence shows women operator representation impacts patient outcomes. Rong et al6 found no difference in TAVR outcomes between men and women operators, refuting stereotypes about procedural competence. Other studies have shown that gender discordance between patients and physicians is associated with worse outcomes in women with cardiovascular disease.7 Similarly, surgical data have demonstrated that surgeon-patient gender discordance is associated with higher rates of adverse postoperative outcomes in female patients treated by male surgeons.8

The recent WomEn in interventional Cardiology: A qualitative REsearch (WECARE) study9 assessed barriers to women’s representation in interventional cardiology and procedural participation. Key challenges were variable recruitment and promotional experiences, persistent gender bias, variable mentorship, and work-life balance constraints. Importantly, most participants also expressed strong professional satisfaction and commitment to the field.

We echo the study’s emphasis on moving from individual adaptation to systemic accountability by institutional and professional societies. This includes the need for structured training, mentorship networks, equitable parental policies, workforce flexibility, and transparent accountability measures.9 We also encourage women to remain engaged in structural interventions, including as second operators, as procedural volumes continue to grow. We call upon the Society for Cardiovascular Angiography & Interventions (SCAI) Women in Innovation (WIN) committee to invest in and develop pathways for women structural operators, similar to the SCAI-WIN Complex and High-Risk Interventional Procedures (CHIP) fellowship.

Our analysis was limited by self-reported gender and exclusion of operators performing ≤10 procedures annually. Non-Medicare and nonbinary data were unavailable, although these limitations are unlikely to bias gender comparisons. We were also unable to control practice-related differences such as institutional setting and years in practice, both of which may influence procedural volumes.

From 2015 to 2023, women remained underrepresented among mitral TEER and TMVR operators, performing <5% of procedures nationwide. Despite rapid procedural growth, there has been no proportional increase in women operators, and most states lack a single woman mitral operator. Focused, system-level strategies to improve mentorship, equitable training and involvement in structural interventions, leadership development, and supportive institutional support are urgently needed to ensure equitable representation in structural heart interventions.
