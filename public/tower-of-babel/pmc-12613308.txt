Design, synthesis, and evaluation of 1, 3-dioxo-N-phenylisoindoline-5-carboxamide derivatives as potent reversible inhibitors of human monoamine oxidase B with neuroprotective properties
Authors: Mengxue Mu, Yu Zhang, Bo Wang, Yun Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA series of 1, 3-dioxo-N-phenylisoindoline-5-carboxamide derivatives were designed, synthesised and evaluated as potent human monoamine oxidase B (hMAO-B) inhibitors with neuroprotective properties. The structure-activity relationship (SAR) was summarised. The most potent compound identified in the study, compound 16, exhibited significant hMAO-B inhibition (IC50 = 0.011 μM) and remarkable selectivity (selectivity index > 3636) over hMAO-A. Kinetic analysis confirmed that compound 16 acted as a mixed-type, reversible hMAO-B inhibitor. Molecular docking studies provided insights into the interactions between the inhibitor and the enzyme. In cellular assays, compound 16 significantly reduced the production of nitric oxide (NO) and tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated BV-2 cells. Additionally, compound 16 also exhibited notable antioxidant activity. These findings suggested that compound 16 could be developed as a promising lead for further investigation.

Introduction

Monoamine oxidase (MAO) is a mitochondrial-bound enzyme that is highly expressed in both neuronal and non-neuronal tissues. MAO catalyses the oxidative deamination of various exogenous and endogenous monoamines1. There are two subtypes of human MAO (hMAO): hMAO-A and hMAO-B, which consist of 527 and 520 amino acids, respectively. Studies have shown that the intron-exon structure of both hMAO-A and hMAO-B is identical2, yet they differ in substrate specificity and tissue distribution. The hMAO-A isoform preferentially metabolises larger endogenous amines such as norepinephrine, serotonin, and epinephrine3, while hMAO-B has a more limited substrate specificity and primarily oxidises compounds like 2-phenylethylamine and benzylamine4.

Overactivation of MAO can disrupt the metabolic balance of neurotransmitters in the brain, leading to the production of neurotoxic aldehydes and reactive oxygen species (ROS) during the metabolism of monoamine neurotransmitters5. Evidence suggests that the expression and activity of hMAO-B significantly increase in the brains of patients with Parkinson’s disease (PD) and Alzheimer’s disease (AD)6–8. This disruption may result in oxidative stress, neurotransmitter depletion, heightened lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial damage, neuronal apoptosis, and neuroinflammation. Consequently, excessive hMAO-B activity plays a significant role in the context of neurodegenerative diseases like AD and PD. Inhibiting hMAO-B provides a promising therapeutic strategy for increasing the concentration of neurotransmitters such as dopamine and reducing oxidative stress in the brain. Currently, MAO-B inhibitors (e.g., selegiline, rasagiline and safinamide) are prescribed for the treatment of PD, and some have also been studied in clinical trials involving AD patients9–12.

MAO-B inhibitors are generally classified as reversible or irreversible. First and second-generation drugs, including selegiline and rasagiline10,13, are irreversible inhibitors, while third-generation reversible inhibitors include safinamide14 (Figure 1A). The sustained inhibition caused by irreversible inhibitors may raise safety concerns and result in pharmacological side effects9,15, including potential immunogenicity of enzyme-inhibitor complexes, adverse pharmacokinetic properties, and prolonged effects that can lead to permanent inactivation of MAO-B and disruption of various enzyme-mediated physiological processes. Studies also demonstrated that irreversible MAO-B inhibitors are less effective for the long-term treatment of neurodegenerative diseases, whereas selective reversible inhibitors show better therapeutic outcomes16–18. Therefore, more efforts are focused on discovering reversible MAO inhibitors in the future.

(A) Structures of MAO-B inhibitors selegiline, rasagiline and safinamide; (B) Structures and potencies of previously described hMAO-B inhibitors and the design strategy of 1, 3-dioxo-N-phenylisoindoline-5-carboxamide derivatives.

Recent studies have revealed that the compound phthalimide, which features an isoindoline 1, 3-dione core with an imide ring, is a weak inhibitor of hMAO-B (IC50 = 134 μM) and could serve as a promising scaffold for developing novel reversible hMAO-B inhibitors19–21. Manley King et al. reported that substituting the C5 position of phthalimide with properly substituted groups could significantly increase the inhibitory activity22. For example, compound 5b, bearing a 5-benzyloxy group substituted on the phthalimide ring, exhibited an IC50 value of 0.043 μM, which was 3116-fold more potent than phthalimide. In the previous study, we found that the N-phenylamide group was also a useful structure for the design of hMAO-B inhibitors23,24. The N-phenylamide substituted compound 1a exhibited an IC50 value of 7.66 μM. Inspired by the above results, we hypothesised that substituting the phthalimide at the C5 position with the N-phenylamide group could yield novel potent hMAO-B inhibitors (Figure 1B).

In this study, we aim to synthesise and evaluate a series of 1, 3-dioxo-N-phenylisoindoline-5-carboxamide derivatives as potential novel hMAO-B inhibitors. Additionally, we will assess the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities of the found active compounds, highlighting their potential for development as lead compounds for the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases.

Materials and methods

All commercial reagents and solvents were obtained from suppliers and used without purification. Reactions were monitored by TLC using glass plates pre-coated with silica gel containing a fluorescent indicator. Column chromatography was carried out on silica gel (200–300 mesh). Melting points were measured on the OptiMelt automated melting point system.1H NMR and 13C NMR spectra were recorded using the Bruker BioSpin Ultrashield 400 NMR system, with TMS as an internal standard. The purity of compounds used for biological evaluation (> 95%) was detected using 254 and 365 nm UV monitors on the Waters Ultimate HPLC system. The purity of the compound is calculated based on the percentage of the peak area of the analysed compound, and the retention time (tR) is measured in minutes. All new compounds were further characterised by high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS), which was recorded on Agilent Technologies 6530 Q-TOF. Fluorescence spectrophotometry was carried out using Multiskan Mk3 fluorescence spectrophotometer. The human recombinant enzymes MAO-A (product No. M7316) and MAO-B (product No. M7441) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (Shanghai, China). Selegiline, rasagiline, and clorgyline were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (Shanghai, China). Safinamide was purchased from Energy Chemical Ltd. (Shanghai, China). These inhibitors were used as the positive inhibition controls in hMAO-B and hMAO-A inhibition tests. Other reagents used for enzyme activity testing were obtained from Sigma Aldrich (Shanghai, China). Curcumin (purity 98%) was purchased from Energy Chemical Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The mouse TNF-α ELISA kit, reactive oxygen species assay kit and nitric oxide assay kit were purchased from Beyotime Biotech Inc (Shanghai, China).

General procedure for the synthesis of 1, 3-dioxo-N-phenylisoindoline-5- carboxamide derivatives 1–18, 20–24

1, 3-dioxo-N-phenylisoindoline-5-carboxamide derivatives 1–18 and 20–24 were synthesised according to the protocol outlined in Scheme 1. Trimeric anhydride (a, 0.3 mmol) and ammonium chloride (1 mmol) were dissolved in dimethylformamide (DMF, 0.4 ml). Then, triethylamine (0.4 mmol) was added. The mixture was first stirred at 35 °C for 30 min and then heated to an external temperature of 160 °C. The solution was allowed to reflux for 5 h. After cooling to room temperature, the solution was diluted with distilled water. The white precipitate formed was then filtered off to give the intermediate b, which was directly used in the next step without further purification.

Synthesis of compounds 1–24.

2 mmol aromatic acid intermediates (b or d) and 2 mmol aromatic amines (c or e) were dissolved in dichloromethane. Then, 2 mmol 1–(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDCI) and 0.2 mmol 4-dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP) were added. The reaction was stirred at room temperature and monitored by TLC until completed. After the reaction, the mixture was diluted with water and extracted with ethyl acetate. The organic mixture was dried over anhydrous MgSO4, filtered and concentrated under reduced pressure. The residue was purified by chromatography column with ethyl acetate/petroleum ether to afford the title compounds.

Synthesis of compound 19

As illustrated in Scheme 1, the intermediate b (3 mmol) was dissolved in 0.4 ml thionyl chloride, the mixture was kept stirred at 80 °C and the reaction was monitored by TLC until completed. The organic solvent was removed under reduced pressure and the crude intermediate product f was used without further purification.

3 mmol intermediate f and 3 mmol 3, 5-dichloroaniline g was dissolved in 20 ml dichloromethane. Then, 1 ml triethylamine was added to the reaction flask. The reaction progress is detected by TLC. After the reaction was complete, the organic solvent was removed under reduced pressure. Extraction to obtain the organic phase and the crude product was purified by column chromatography and recrystallization.

MAO-A/B inhibition assays in vitro

The inhibition assays for hMAO-A and hMAO-B were performed according to validated methods25,26. The tested compounds (20 μL/well) and hMAO-A/B (80 μL/well) were diluted with a buffer solution (50 mM, pH 7.4) to final concentrations for the test compound and 12.5 µg/mL (hMAO-A) or 75 µg/mL (hMAO-B) for the enzymes. The mixture was added to a 96-well black plate and mixed thoroughly. After incubation at 37 °C for 15 min, a working solution (100 μL/well, 200 µM Ampliflu Red, 2 U/mL HRP, 2 mM substrate) was added. The plate was incubated at 37 °C for an additional 15 min, and the fluorescence intensity was detected on a Multifunctional Microplate Reader with an excitation (545 nm) and emission (590 nm), respectively. Controls were prepared similarly: hMAO-A and hMAO-B were pre-incubated in the absence of inhibitors as negative controls, and with known hMAO inhibitors [Clorgyline (hMAO-A), Safinamide, and Rasagiline (hMAO-B)] as positive controls. Based on the results of the hMAO-B screening assay, the inhibition rate of the compound on hMAO-B was measured at different concentrations. GraphPad Prism 8 was used to analyse the experimental data and calculate the IC50 value.

Enzyme kinetics

The compound with the highest inhibitory activity was selected for kinetic studies and tested at concentrations of 0, IC50, 1.5 × IC50, 2 × IC50, and 2.5 × IC50, with substrate concentrations of 0.5, 1, 1.5, and 2 mM. A Lineweaver-Burk plot was used to investigate the inhibition type of the active compound.

Cell experiment

BV2 mouse microglial cells (RRID: CVCL_0182, catalogue number GDC0311) and SH-SY5Y human neuroblastoma cells (RRID: CVCL_0019, catalogue number GDC0311) were purchased from China Centre for Type Culture Collection. These cells were cultured in Dulbecco modified Eagle medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin (Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd, China) and 100 μg/ml streptomycin (Gibco), The cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 95% air and 5% CO2.

Reactive oxygen species level test

SH-SY5Y cells were seeded at a density of 8 × 104 cells/well into 24-well plates. After 24 h, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were pre-treated with different concentrations (1, 10 and 20 μM) of the active compounds for 24 h. Trolox was used as the positive control (20 μM). Subsequently, 500 μL tert-butyl hydroperoxide (TBHP, 100 μM) was added and incubated at 37 °C for 0.5 h. Then, 500 μL 2′, 7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA, 10 μg/mL) was added to each well and kept in the dark for 0.5 h. According to the protocol of the reagent kit, ROS levels were analysed under a fluorescence microscope (excitation at 488 nm). The fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Nitric oxide inhibition experiment

BV2 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well into 48-well plates. After 24 h, the culture medium was removed and the cells were pre-treated with different concentrations (1, 10 and 20 μM) of compounds for 24 h. Curcumin was used as the positive control (10 μM). Then, the cells were stimulated with LPS (100 ng/mL) and incubated in a 37 °C incubator for 24 h. Finally, 50 μL of Griess I and Griess II were added sequentially and mixed. The optical density (OD) value was measured by a Multifunctional Microplate Reader. The concentration of nitric oxide (NO) was calculated based on the standard curve obtained from the reagent kit.

Elisa

BV2 cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well into 96-well plates. After 24 h, the culture medium was removed, and the BV2 cells were pre-treated with different concentrations (1, 10 and 20 μM) of the active compounds for 24 h. Curcumin was used as the positive control (10 μM). Then, the cells were stimulated with LPS (100 ng/mL) and incubated in a 37 °C incubator for 24 h. The supernatant was then removed, and the levels of TNF-α in the cell supernatant were determined according to the instructions of the ELISA kit. The TNF-α levels were calculated based on the standard curve.

Cell viability assay

BV2 cells and SH-SY5Y cells (8 × 103/well) were seeded in 96-well plates. The plate was incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 until the cells fully adhered to the plate. The culture medium was then removed, and 100 μL of medium containing different concentrations of the compounds (1, 5, 10, 15 and 20 μM) was added to each well in the experimental group. The control group received culture medium without compounds. After 24 h of incubation, 80 μL of DMEM and 20 μL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) were added to each well. The plate was further incubated for 4 h, and then 150 μL of DMSO was added to each well to completely dissolve the purple formazan crystals. The OD value of each well was measured at a wavelength of 570 nm using a Multifunctional Microplate Reader. The results were compared with those of the control group.

Results and discussion

Chemistry

Compounds 1, 3–18, 20–24 were synthesised as illustrated in Scheme 1A. The commercially available reagent trimellitic anhydride (a) was reacted with ammonium chloride at 160 °C to give the intermediate trimellitimide (b). Then the intermediate b was reacted with corresponding arylamines in the presence of 1–(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDCI) and 4-dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP) to give the final products.

Compound 2 was prepared by reacting benzoic acid (d) with aniline (e) as described above (Scheme 1B). For the synthesis of compound 19, the intermediate b was firstly converted to 1, 3-dioxoisoindole-5-carbonyl chloride (f) by refluxing with thionyl chloride. Then the intermediate f was reacted with 3, 5-dichloroaniline (g) to give the final product (Scheme 1C). Most of these compounds with halogen atoms were obtained with relative low yields, which might be likely due to the electron-withdrawing effects of halogen atoms and therefore affecting the nucleophilic substitution of the amino group.

Compounds 1–3, 8, 11, 14, 20 and 24 were known compounds which had been previously reported27–29. Other 16 compounds were novel compounds. The 1H NMR spectra of the novel compounds revealed two characteristic singlet peaks in the range of 10.50–11.60 ppm, corresponding to the NH protons of the carboxamide and phthalimide groups, respectively. The signals in the 7.90–8.42 ppm region were assigned to the aromatic protons of the phthalimide moiety, while those in the 6.70–7.90 ppm range reflected the protons of the N-phenyl group. A set of signals in the range of 3.76–3.87 ppm was attributed to the methoxy protons attached to the N-phenyl moiety. For the 13C NMR spectra, two characteristic signals were observed at 168.90–169.10 ppm and 163.70–165.0 ppm, corresponding to the phthalimide carbonyl carbon and the carboxamide carbon, respectively. The chemical shift in the 55.50–55.68 ppm range indicated the presence of a methoxy carbon substituted on the N-phenyl moiety.

Biological evaluations

Inhibitory activities of monoamine oxidases

All synthetic compounds were screened for their inhibitory effects on hMAO-B and hMAO-A using a fluorescence-based assay. The hMAO-B inhibition rate (%) of tested compounds was assessed at 1 μM and the hMAO-A inhibition rate (%) was assessed at 40 μM. Rasagiline, an irreversible inhibitor, and safinamide, a reversible inhibitor, were utilised as positive controls for hMAO-B inhibition. Clorgyline was utilised as a positive control for hMAO-A inhibition.

As shown in Table 1, rasagiline and safinamide exhibited significant inhibitory activities on hMAO-B, demonstrating 87.2% and 82.8% inhibition at 1 μM, with IC50 values of 0.096 μM and 0.0365 μM, ­respectively, consistent with previous reports. The parent compound, 1, 3-dioxo-N-phenylisoindoline-5-carboxamide (1), displayed moderate inhibition of hMAO-B (25.0%) with an IC50 value of 3.25 μM, which was 41-fold more potent than phthalimide. Replacing the phthalimide moiety of compound 1 with the phenyl group (2) resulted in a marked decrease in inhibition (< 5%), indicating that the phthalimide moiety was essential for inhibitory activity. Substituting the N-phenyl group of 1 with a benzyl moiety (3) also led to a reduction in inhibition (< 5%), suggesting that the N-phenyl group is also required for the inhibition. We then explored the effects of introducing various substituents at different positions on the N-phenyl ring of compound 1.

At the ortho position of the N-phenyl group of compound 1, the introduction of -F resulted in increased inhibitory activity (4, 31.4% inhibition), while substituting with -Cl or -Br at the same position did not enhance inhibitory activity (5–6, < 5% inhibition). At the meta position, the introduction of -F yielded minimal inhibition (7, < 5%), whereas -Cl or -Br significantly increased inhibitory activity. The resulted compounds 8 and 9 showed 63.9% and 81.3% inhibition with the IC50 values of 0.29 and 0.18 μM, respectively. At the para position, the introduction of -F, -Cl, and -Br also resulted in relatively higher inhibition (10–12, 44.2%, 52%, and 15.7% inhibition, respectively) compared to compound 1. Therefore, we concluded that introducing halogen atoms at the meta and para positions of the N-phenyl group in compound 1 generally enhanced inhibitory potency, with -Br at the meta position being particularly preferable.

Compounds with additional halogen substitutions on the N-phenyl group were further investigated. Results indicated that compounds with two halogen atoms (13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19) exhibited remarkable inhibitory potency against hMAO-B. The most potent compound was 16, substituted with 3-Br and 4-F, which had an IC50 value of 0.011 μM. Compounds 15, 14 and 18 showed comparable inhibitory activities with IC50 values of 0.012, 0.014, and 0.015 μM, respectively. The 3, 4-difluoro substituted compound 13 and the 3-F, 4-Cl substituted compound 17 exhibited relatively lower inhibitory activities with IC50 values of 0.024 and 0.029 μM, respectively. Compound 19, with 3, 5-dichloro substitution, showed weaker inhibition among those bearing two halogen atoms, with an IC50 value of 0.055 μM. These results suggest that substituting the N-phenyl ring of 1 with two halogen atoms at the meta and para positions would significantly enhance the inhibitory potency.

For comparison, we examined the effect of introducing the electron-donating group -OCH3 on the N-phenyl ring of 1. Both compounds with 3-OCH3 or 4-OCH3 (20 and 21) exhibited minimal inhibition (< 5%). Moreover, replacing the 3-Br and 4-Cl groups of the active compounds 15 and 14 with -OCH3 resulted in significantly decreased inhibitory potency for compounds 22 and 23. As the 3, 4-dimethoxyl substituted compound 24 also exhibited little inhibition (< 5%), we hypothesised that introducing electron-donating groups like -OCH3 to the N-phenyl group was unfavourable for enhancing inhibitory potency.

To investigate whether the synthetic compounds were selective hMAO-B inhibitors, the inhibition on hMAO-A of these compounds were also evaluated. As illustrated in Table. 1, the positive control clorgyline demonstrated 79.9% inhibition at 0.1 μM. However, all the tested compounds showed less than 50% inhibition even at 40 μM. According to the definition of selectivity index (SI, IC50(hMAO-A)/IC50(hMAO-B)), the active compounds 13–18 all exhibited SI values greater than 1379, which was superior to that of rasagiline (SI = 1067)13. Especially, the most potent hMAO-B inhibitor 16 exhibited an SI value greater than 3636. Thus, we concluded that the potent inhibitors identified in this work were selective hMAO-B inhibitors.

Kinetics study

To investigate the mechanism of enzyme inhibition by active compounds, the representative compound 16 was subject to an enzyme kinetics study and the Lineweaver-Burk plot was constructed with the compound at various concentrations. As illustrated in Figure 3(A), the lines in the plots had diverse slopes and intercepts on the both x- and y- axis, indicating that the inhibition mode of compound 16 on hMAO-B was mixed inhibition30,31. As no allosteric sites of hMAO-B have been identified so far and the mixed inhibition of inhibitors might also occur when ligands bind to the enzyme active site in a “tight-binding inhibition” way32, the detailed inhibitory mechanism of compound 16 remains unclear and requires further investigation using crystallographic technique.

Reversibility study

To confirm the reversibility of the inhibition of active compounds on hMAO-B, the most potent compound 16 was selected as the representative compound and assessed in the time-dependent assay and dialysis assay as the previous reports23,33. The irreversible inhibitor rasagiline and the reversible safinamide were used as the positive controls. hMAO-B was firstly incubated with the inhibitors for 15 min and then the substrate was added to the enzyme-compound complex. The enzyme activity (% of the control) was determined within 180 min. As illustrated in Figure 3(B), when the enzyme was incubated with rasagiline, the activity of hMAO-B weakened continuously throughout the incubation period after adding the substrate, indicating that the inhibition was irreversible. However, when the enzyme was incubated with safinamide, the enzymatic activity began to increase gradually after adding the substrate for 48 min, indicating that the inhibitor could be replaced by the substrate and the enzymatic activity could be recovered. Similar changes of the enzymatic activity were observed when treating the enzyme with 16. The activity of hMAO-B began to recover since 72 min after adding the substrate, which revealed the inhibition of compound 16 on hMAO-B was reversible.

The results of the dialysis assay further confirmed the reversible inhibition of compound 16. In the dialysis assay, the irreversible inhibitor selegiline was used as the positive control. hMAO-B was first incubated with the tested inhibitors for 15 min, then the enzyme-inhibitor complex was dialysed overnight. The activities of the enzyme were analysed before and after the dialysis, as illustrated in Figure 3(C). After dialysis, the enzyme activity of the irreversible inhibitor selegiline group did not significantly decrease compared to the pre-dialysis group. In contrast, for compound 16, the enzyme activity increased from 24.62% to 86.24% after dialysis. These results demonstrated that compounds 16 were reversible hMAO-B inhibitors.

Molecular docking studies

To elucidate the inhibitory interactions between the active compounds and hMAO-B, a molecular docking study was conducted using MOE software. The representative compounds phthalimide, 1, and 16 were docked into the active site of the co-crystal structure of hMAO-B (PDB code: 2V5Z). As depicted in Figure 4(A), phthalimide was situated within the substrate cavity formed by residues Cys172, Ile198, Tyr398, and Tyr435 et al. The pyrrolidine-2, 5-dione ring oriented towards FAD, with the C5 position directed towards the entrance cavity formed by Pro102, Pro104, Trp119 and Phe168, which is notably hydrophobic and accommodating to aromatic groups. The primary interaction observed was hydrophobic interaction.

For compound 1, the phthalimide moiety occupied the substrate cavity and the pyrrolidine-2, 5-dione ring was positioned closer to FAD. The carboxy oxygen at C1 and the NH proton established hydrogen bonds with a conserved water molecule (HOH768) in the active site. The N-phenyl ring of compound 1 occupied the entrance cavity, linked to the C5 position of the phthalimide moiety via an amide linker (Figure 4A). It is reasonable to conclude that binding to both the entrance and substrate cavities significantly enhances the interaction with the enzyme. The calculated lower binding free energy suggested that compound 1 possesses a higher binding affinity for hMAO-B compared to phthalimide (−6.94 kcal/mol versus −4.76 kcal/mol, Table S1).

Compound 16 displayed a similar docking pose to compound 1 within the active site of hMAO-B (Figure 4B-C). The carbonyl oxygen at C1 and the NH proton in the phthalimide moiety formed hydrogen bonds with HOH768, while the N-phenyl ring occupied the entrance cavity. Furthermore, the substituents 3-Br and 4-F on the phenyl ring were deeply embedded in the highly lipophilic halogen-binding pocket located within the entrance cavity, which may further strengthen the binding interaction with the enzyme. The lower binding free energy of compound 16 (−7.69 kcal/mol) compared to compound 1 also indicated a higher affinity for hMAO-B.

For comparison, the docking poses of phthalimide, compound 1, and 16 were superimposed with safinamide in the co-crystal structure of hMAO-B (Figure 4D). The phthalimide ring of these compounds occupied the substrate cavity. The polar -NH and carbonyl groups at the C1 position of compounds 1 and 16 formed hydrogen bonds with HOH768, which was also observed for the carbonyl group of safinamide. Similarly to safinamide, compounds 1 and 16 adopted an extended conformation within the active site, positioning their N-phenyl rings in the entrance cavity of the enzyme. The halogen atoms of compound 16 overlapped with that of safinamide, suggesting that this region of the active site might be particularly favourable for halogen binding.

In addition, we also explored the mechanism underlying the inhibitory selectivity of the active compounds towards hMAO-B over hMAO-A through docking analysis. The most active compound, 16, which exhibited the highest SI, was docked into the active site of hMAO-A (Figure 4E). Due to the smaller and singular substrate cavity of hMAO-A compared to hMAO-B, compound 16 adopted a more twisted conformation within the hMAO-A active site. The phthalimide moiety was located near FAD, where the carbonyl oxygen at C1 and the NH proton formed hydrogen bonds with a conserved water molecule (HOH726). The phenyl ring of compound 16 was positioned on the opposite side, exhibiting a nearly vertical orientation relative to the phthalimide ring, thus minimising overlap with surrounding residues (e.g., Cys323, Thr336, and Leu337). However, this twisted conformation of compound 16, along with potential structural conflicts, likely contributed to less favourable interactions with hMAO-A, which is supported by the fact that the calculated binding free energy of compound 16 with hMAO-A was significantly higher than that with hMAO-B (−5.12 kcal/mol versus −7.69 kcal/mol, Table S1), indicating substantially lower binding affinity to hMAO-A. Consequently, steric hindrance may significantly impede the binding of active compounds to hMAO-A, thereby affecting their inhibitory activity.

Anti-oxidation assay

To evaluate the efficacy of the active compounds in inhibiting intracellular ROS formation, representative compound 16 was selected and assessed in the TBHP-stimulated SH-SY5Y cells. The cells were incubated with compound 16 at concentrations ranging from 1 to 20 µM for 24 h, followed by treatment with TBHP at 100 µM for 30 min. Following incubation, intracellular ROS formation was assessed using the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA. Compared to the control group, the TBHP-treated group produced a large amount of ROS in the cells (Figure 5A-B). While pre-treatment with Trolox (20 µM) significantly decreased the level of intracellular ROS, a reduction of approximately 43% was observed compared to the TBHP-treated group (Figure 5C and Figure 6A). Pre-treatment with compound 16 also exhibited significant inhibitory effects against ROS production induced by TBHP (Figure 5D-F). As the concentration of compound 16 increased, the level of intracellular ROS gradually decreased, and reductions of approximately 49% to 77% were observed in TBHP-stimulated SH-SY5Y cells (Figure 6A). Thus, these results indicated that compound 16 exhibited radical scavenging activity and could effectively inhibit intracellular ROS formation.

Elisa

To investigate the anti-inflammatory effects of the active compounds, representative compound 16 was selected and evaluated for the inhibitory effects on the release of pro-inflammatory mediators. The levels of NO and TNF-α were measured in BV2 cells stimulated with LPS in the presence and absence of the tested compound. As shown in Figure 6 B-C, LPS alone significantly increased the release of NO and TNF-α, with levels evaluated at 20-fold and 62-fold, respectively, compared to the control groups. Pre-treatment with curcumin (10 µM) significantly decreased the level of NO, a reduction of approximately 50.6% was observed compared to the LPS-alone treated group. As for the compound 16, ­pretreating the BV2 cells with the compound at concentrations of 1, 10, and 20 µM for 24 h, followed by LPS treatment, led to a dose-dependent decrease in the levels of NO and TNF-α. Compared to the LPS-only treatment groups, reductions of approximately 10% to 40% for NO and 10% to 30% for TNF-α were observed. These results clearly indicated that compound 16 effectively inhibited the release of NO and TNF-α in LPS-stimulated BV2 cells, suggesting its potential for development as an anti-neuroinflammation agent.

Conclusion

In conclusion, a series of 1, 3-dioxo-N-phenylisoindoline-5-carboxamide derivatives have been designed, synthesised, and evaluated as a novel class of hMAO-B inhibitors. Most of these compounds exhibited significant inhibitory activities against hMAO-B, with IC50 values in the sub-micromolar range. Structure-activity relationship (SAR) analyses revealed that the introduction of halogen atoms, particularly the 3-bromo and 4-fluoro groups at the meta and para positions of the N-benzene ring, significantly enhanced the inhibitory activity. The most potent compound identified in this study, compound 16, exhibited an IC50 value of 0.011 μM and a selectivity index (SI) greater than 3636, superior to those of the currently available drugs, rasagiline and safinamide. Kinetic and reversibility studies confirmed that compound 16 acts as a mixed-type, reversible inhibitor of hMAO-B. Molecular docking studies elucidated the interactions between the enzyme and the inhibitor, clarifying the rationale for the observed selectivity for hMAO-B over hMAO-A. Furthermore, compound 16 demonstrated significant antioxidant and anti-neuroinflammatory activities in vitro.

However, there are two issues in this study that could be addressed in future research. First, the detailed inhibitory mechanism of the hit compound on hMAO-B remains unclear. Second, the drug-like properties of the hit compound, including CYP450 inhibition and plasma stability, need to be evaluated prior to subsequent in vivo studies.

Nevertheless, the findings of this study present a series of novel hMAO-B inhibitors with neuroprotective activity which may serve as promising leads for further investigation.
