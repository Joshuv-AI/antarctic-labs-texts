Sustainable zein-based bioplastic films reinforced with silver nanoparticle-embedded casein nanocomposites for active fruit packaging: characterization and functional assessment
Authors: Ko-Chi Chen, You-Ren Lai, Min-Cheng Lin, Su-Chun How
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to synthesize silver nanoparticle-embedded casein nanocomposites (AgNP@CN) and incorporated them into zein (ZN)-derived bioplastic films to enhance their functional properties. The shape, size/dimension, morphology, surface charge, and surface chemistry of AgNP@CN were investigated. After forming AgNP@CN-functionalized ZN bioplastic films (AgNP@CN-ZN), the bioplastic films were characterized. Incorporation of AgNP@CN improved the mechanical properties, thermal stability, surface hydrophobicity, and UV resistance of ZN films. Additionally, the water vapor barrier properties of AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films were comparable to or superior to those of conventional plastics. In terms of food packaging, the AgNP@CN-ZN films effectively reduced weight loss and enzymatic browning in banana peels as a food model. Accordingly, the silver content in banana pulp wrapped with AgNP@CN-ZN film was 5.57 × 10−5 mg/kg, well below the specific migration limit. Overall, the research highlights the feasibility of using nanoparticle-protein composites to reinforce the functional properties of bioplastics for sustainable packaging applications.Keywords: Zein bioplastic film, Reinforcement additives, Casein protein, Silver nanoparticles, Functional properties, Packaging application

Introduction

Global demand for plastic products is consistently rising, with the plastic packaging industry projected to grow at an annual rate surpassing 3% (Green.Earth, 2026; Ncube, Ude, Ogunmuyiwa, Zulkifli, & Beas, 2021). Disposable plastics, designed for single use and disposal, constitute a significant portion of plastic waste, with over 8 million tonnes entering marine ecosystems each year (Green.Earth, 2026; Ncube et al., 2021; Yerramsetti, 2024). According to estimates provided by the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP, n.d), the annual generation of plastic waste is approximately 400 million tonnes. Furthermore, projections indicate that the production of primary plastics may reach 1100 million tonnes by the year 2050. Thus, plastic pollution is a widespread global environmental challenge characterized by the accumulation of plastic waste across diverse ecosystems. This issue leads to terrestrial and marine contamination, biodiversity loss, and contributes to climate change through greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from manufacturing, disposal, and decomposition (Sadhu, Garg, & Kumar, 2018). Furthermore, non-degradable microplastics (generally less than 5 mm) are generated by the degradation of plastic products and pose significant risks to human health and the environment (Shen et al., 2020). In light of growing concerns about plastic pollution, protein-based bioplastics have been identified as green and sustainable alternatives to conventional fossil-derived plastics, thereby contributing to the mitigation of plastic pollution problems (Patel, Murali, Choudhary, Purnima, & Pal, 2025).

Protein-based bioplastics, derived from animal and plant sources such as whey protein, casein, soy protein, gluten, and zein, offer a more sustainable option to replace traditional petroleum-derived plastics by reducing dependence on fossil fuels, diminishing environmental impacts, and lowering the carbon footprint of plastic production (Ding et al., 2025; Patel et al., 2025). Because protein-based bioplastics can degrade into non-toxic substances under certain environmental conditions, their biodegradable nature plays a crucial role in mitigating global plastic pollution (Lai et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2025). Unfortunately, the utilization of protein-derived bioplastics in packaging is limited by their inferior mechanical properties, poor barrier performance to gases and moisture, and low thermal stability and water resistance. Despite the fact that the protein-based bioplastics are environmentally friendly and can contribute to a circular economy, it is essential to address the aforesaid drawbacks/limitations associated with their properties. In response to the global challenges posed by plastic pollution, it is imperative to explore functional fillers and reinforcement additives to improve the performance of protein-based bioplastics. Examples of functional fillers and reinforcement additives used in protein-based bioplastics include: (a) metallic nanoparticles, (b) antimicrobial agents, (c) essential oil emulsions, (d) cellulose nanocrystals, (e) amyloid aggregates, (f) hydroxyapatite, and (g) montmorillonite (Abookleesh, Upadhyay, & Ullah, 2024; Dissanayake et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2025; Tan, Han, Zhang, Li, & Shang, 2021; S. Yang et al., 2024).

Zein (ZN) is a primary protein component and abundantly available in protein wastes and by-products produced during corn oil extraction, the bioethanol industry, and the wet milling process of maize starch. Statistics reveal that global production of protein waste from the cereal industry exceeds 250 million tons (Mt) annually (Peydayesh, Bagnani, Soon, & Mezzenga, 2022). Additionally, the proportion of ZN in maize is higher than in other protein fractions from source counterparts, such as rapeseed protein, rice bran protein, sunflower protein, oat protein, and soy protein (Peydayesh et al., 2022; J. Zhou et al., 2022). However, ZN is mainly regarded as a protein waste or used as animal feed rather than as a nutrient for human consumption because of its low water solubility and unbalanced amino acid profile. From a sustainability standpoint, the ZN, a type of plant-based protein, is a promising sustainable feedstock for bioplastic production due to its high overall sustainability footprint, low greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, environmental friendliness, and low environmental impact (Lai et al., 2025; Peydayesh et al., 2022; J. Zhou et al., 2022).

Nowadays, increasing studies have focused on the development of ZN-based functional materials across various fields, such as nanotechnology, separation engineering, biochemical engineering, and food science (Corradini et al., 2014; Lai et al., 2025; Matheus, Castro, Ferreira, Forster-Carneiro, & Colpini, 2025; Paliwal & Palakurthi, 2014). Thanks to its biodegradability, biocompatibility, film-forming properties, renewability, cost-effectiveness, and abundance in natural sources, ZN is considered a potential candidate for use as a building block in bioplastic development (Chou et al., 2026; Lai et al., 2025). Additionally, the aforesaid remarkable features foster an idea of harnessing ZN as packaging materials for food preservation and promote the exploitation of ZN-derived bioplastics as sustainable alternatives to fossil-based plastics. Several instances of ZN-derived packaging materials are listed as follows: (1) ZN amyloid fibrils/chitosan bioplastic films (Chou et al., 2026), (2) ZN-TiO2 nanotube arrays composite films (Cheng et al., 2024), (3) ZN/gum arabic edible films (X. Wang et al., 2024), (4) ZN amyloid fibrils/methylcellulose bioplastic films (Lai et al., 2025), (5) ZN and β-cyclodextrin MOFs packaging films (L. Yang et al., 2025), (6) ZN loaded with thyme essential oil nanofiber films (Ansarifar & Moradinezhad, 2021), and (7) gelatin/ZN nanofiber films (X. Wu, Liu, He, Liu, & Shao, 2023).

Despite the numerous advantageous properties of ZN-based films, it is imperative to recognize their limitations for food packaging applications, including mechanical brittleness and reliance on solubilizing and plasticizing agents during film fabrication (S. Wang et al., 2023). Recent research has demonstrated that incorporating co-proteins is an alternative strategy to improving the mechanical properties of ZN films while reducing plasticizer content (Erickson, Renzetti, Jurgens, Campanella, & Hamaker, 2014). A study demonstrated that ZN films became more flexible and less homogeneous upon the incorporation of gliadin, attributed to differences in self-assembly between ZN and gliadin (Gu & Wang, 2013). Evidence has revealed that introducing sodium caseinate into ZN films can enhance tensile strength, thermal stability, surface hydrophobicity, and water vapor barrier due to high compatibility and strong synergistic interactions between sodium caseinate and ZN (S. Wang et al., 2023). Another study showed that composite films based on whey protein and ZN outperformed films prepared from single whey protein or ZN in terms of ultimate tensile strength and barrier properties (Ghanbarzadeh & Oromiehi, 2008). Likewise, the water resistance and ductility of gelatin films improved with the addition of ZN, indicating an increase in the intramolecular or intermolecular hydrogen-bonding interactions between gelatin and ZN (Ahammed, Liu, Khin, Yokoyama, & Zhong, 2020). Therefore, combining ZN with complementary co-proteins has gained attention as a reinforcement approach to overcome its intrinsic brittleness and improve the overall mechanical, thermal, and barrier properties of ZN-based composite films.

Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs), defined as silver materials with dimensions of approximately 1–100 nm, have garnered significant attention for their unique properties and potential applications in reaction engineering, electrochemical engineering, and biomedical engineering (Abou El-Nour, Eftaiha, Al-Warthan, & Ammar, 2010; Ravindran, Chandran, & Khan, 2013). Given that AgNPs exhibit potent antibacterial activity against pathogens, recent studies have incorporated AgNPs as nanofillers to enhance the antibacterial properties of film materials used in active packaging (Awasthi et al., 2025; Carbone, Donia, Sabbatella, & Antiochia, 2016). However, due to their high surface free energy, the aggregation/agglomeration of AgNPs led to increased particle size, reduced thermodynamic stability, decreased surface-to-volume ratio, and loss of unique optical properties driven by surface plasmon resonance (Lai, Lai, Wang, Kuo, & Lin, 2022). Moreover, solid evidence indicated that the antibacterial activity of AgNPs depends on their size, shape, and the stabilizer, thereby affecting the interaction between AgNPs and microbial cells (Bélteky et al., 2021; Zewde, Ambaye, Stubbs Iii, & Raghavan, 2016). Hence, their tendency to aggregate would reduce their surface area and influence their interaction(s) with microbial cells, which eventually hampers the antibacterial effectiveness of AgNPs. While the antimicrobial properties of AgNPs are recognized, their potential health risks and migration into food matrices remain a main concern. Since the excessive release of silver cations or the migration of AgNPs can cause cytotoxic effects in humans, the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) has established migration limits (<0.05 mg of Ag per kg of food) for AgNPs in food-contact materials (Carbone et al., 2016; EFSA Panel on Food Contact Materials et al., 2021). To that end, a promising approach proposed in this study is the embedding of AgNPs in a CN carrier/support, which could prevent AgNP agglomeration and their migration into foods while enhancing their colloidal stability, biocompatibility, and functional properties (Y.-H. Chen et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2024).

Due to their biocompatibility, biodegradability, and renewability, protein-derived bioplastics are recognized as environmentally friendly and sustainable alternatives to petroleum-derived plastics. This work focused on synthesizing silver nanoparticle-embedded CN nanocomposites (AgNP@CN) and employed them as functional fillers and reinforcement additives in ZN-derived bioplastic film. Apart from examining the effects of AgNP@CN incorporation on the functional properties of ZN bioplastic films, the possibility of harnessing AgNP@CN-functionalized ZN bioplastic films (AgNP@CN-ZN) as food packaging materials was evaluated. The AgNP@CN was characterized and then incorporated into the ZN matrix to form bioplastic films. The microstructure of the ZN bioplastic film became more uniform and compact upon AgNP@CN incorporation. Infrared analysis showed that the interaction between AgNP@CN and ZN was intermolecular hydrogen bonding. As the AgNP@CN content increased, the AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic film exhibited higher tensile strength, elongation at break, and decomposition temperature, indicating enhanced mechanical properties and thermal stability. The bioplastic film with high AgNP@CN content exhibited high surface hydrophobicity, low hygroscopicity, superior ultraviolet resistance, and water vapor barrier efficacy comparable to that of conventional fossil-derived plastics. In packaging applications, the weight loss and browning extent of banana peel were reduced after packaging with an AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic film. Overall, this research provides an example of applying nanoparticle-protein hybrids as functional fillers to reinforce the properties of ZN-derived bioplastics.

Materials and methods

Materials

Casein (CN) sodium salt from bovine milk used to synthesize nanocomposites, silver nitrate (AgNO3) served as precursor, zein (ZN) utilized to produce bioplastic films, and polyethylene glycol (PEG; average molecular weight = ∼400 g/mol) acts as a plasticizer were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and ethanol solution (95 v/v%) were obtained from Nihon Shiyaku (Japan) and Echo Chemical Company (Taiwan). Unless specifically stated otherwise, all chemicals and reagents utilized in this work were acquired from Sigma-Aldrich (USA).

Preparation of silver nanoparticle-embedded casein nanocomposites (AgNP@CN)

The experimental procedure for preparing silver nanoparticle-embedded casein nanocomposites (AgNP@CN) is shown in Fig. 1A. 1 wt% CN solution was prepared by dissolving an appropriate amount of CN sodium salt in 0.05 M NaOH solution. Then, the CN solution was mixed with a 10 mM AgNO3 solution at a volume ratio of 3:2. The solution containing CN and precursor silver nitrate was incubated at room temperature and stirring at 300 rpm for 10 days. The formation of AgNP@CN was verified by the color change from colorless to yellowish in the resulting solution. Finally, the Ag@CN-containing solution was freeze-dried, and the resulting AgNP@CN powder was stored at room temperature.

Schematic diagram of the experimental procedures for (A) preparing silver nanoparticle-embedded CN nanocomposites (AgNP@CN), (B) synthesizing CN-functionalized ZN bioplastic films (CN-ZN films), and (C) fabricating AgNP@CN-functionalized ZN bioplastic films (AgNP@CN-ZN films).

Synthesis of casein-functionalized zein bioplastic films (CN-ZN films)

The experimental procedure for synthesizing CN-functionalized ZN bioplastic films (CN-ZN films) is depicted in Fig. 1B. The 20 wt% zein (ZN) solution was prepared by dissolving an appropriate amount of ZN powder in an ethanol solution (60 v/v%). Next, the blend solutions containing ZN and CN were prepared with different ZN:CN mass ratios of 10:0, 10:0.3, and 10:0.6. Upon adjusting to pH 11 using NaOH solution, the aforesaid solutions were added with plasticizer PEG and then mixed at 65 °C for 2 h. Afterwards, the film-forming solution was poured into a mold (dimensions: 6 cm × 6 cm × 0.7 cm) and allowed to stand at room temperature for 24 h. Finally, the CN-ZN films were dried at 50 °C until the solvent evaporated, then removed from the mold for subsequent experiments and applications. The CN-ZN films with different ZN:CN mass ratios of 10:0, 10:0.3, and 10:0.6 are designated CN-ZN-0, CN-ZN-0.3, and CN-ZN-0.6, respectively.

Synthesis of AgNP@CN-functionalized zein bioplastic films (AgNP@CN-ZN films)

The experimental procedure for fabricating AgNP@CN-functionalized ZN bioplastic films (AgNP@CN-ZN films) is illustrated in Fig. 1C. Blend solutions containing ZN and AgNP@CN were prepared with different mass ratios of 10:0.3 and 10:0.6. After adjusting to pH 11 with NaOH solution, the solutions were mixed with plasticizer PEG and stirred at 65 °C for 2 h. Then, the film-forming solution was added to a mold (dimensions: 6 cm × 6 cm × 0.7 cm) and left at room temperature for 24 h. Finally, the AgNP@CN-ZN films were dried at 50 °C until the solvent evaporated, removed from the mold, and used for subsequent experiments. The CN-ZN films with different ZN:AgNP@CN ratios of 10:0.3 and 10:0.6 are named AgNP@CN-ZN-0.3 and AgNP@CN-ZN-0.6, respectively.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

A 10 μL sample solution containing CN or AgNP@CN was added dropwise onto the surface of carbon-stabilized copper grids, and the sample-coated copper grids were then left to stand for 30 s. Next, 1% (w/v) uranyl acetate (UA) was employed to negatively stain the CN or AgNP@CN coated on copper grids. After 10 s for negative staining, the sample-coated copper grids were dried at room temperature for 30 min. Finally, the morphological characteristics of CN or AgNP@CN deposited on the surface of copper grids were examined and photographed using a transmission electron microscopy (TEM) equipped with a Gantan model 782 CCD camera (H-7650, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 75 kV.

Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) and ζ-potential measurement

1 mL of CN or AgNP@CN solution at a concentration of 30 mg/mL was pipetted in a disposable cuvette (Malvern, DST0012) with a 1 cm light path. To determine the hydrodynamic size distribution, average hydrodynamic diameter (Dh), and polydispersity index (PDI), the dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis of CN and AgNP@CN was performed using a Zetasizer instrument (Zetasizer Pro, Malvern Panalytical, Westborough, MA, USA) at a laser wavelength of 633 nm, a scattering angle of 173°, and a refractive index of 1.33. For ζ-potential measurement, 1 mL of solution containing CN or AgNP@CN at a concentration of 30 mg/mL was added to a disposable folded capillary cell (Malvern, DTS1070). The electrophoretic mobility of CN and AgNP@CN was measured using a Zetasizer instrument, and the ζ–potential of CN and AgNP@CN was computed based on the Henry equation.

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)

The CN and AgNP@CN were first dehydrated by freeze-drying. Next, the CN or AgNP@CN was ground and blended with potassium bromide (KBr) at a mass ratio of 1:100. The resulting powder was compressed for 60 s to produce tablets. Finally, a Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer (Spectrum 100, Perkin Elmer, Shelton, CT, USA) under a resolution of 4 cm−1, a wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm−1, and 8 scans was utilized to obtain FTIR spectra of CN and AgNP@CN.

Attenuated Total Reflectance-Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR)

The functional groups of CN-ZN and AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films were analyzed using attenuated total reflectance-Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (JASCO V-6700), and the ATR-FTIR spectra of these films were obtained accordingly. The ATR-FTIR was operated with the following settings: a resolution of 4 cm−1, a wavenumber ranges from 750 to 4000 cm−1, and 40 scans.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

The CN-ZN and AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films were dried at 50 °C for 24 h, and pieces of 5 mm × 5 mm were cut from the films using a stainless-steel razor blade. Next, the aforesaid film samples were coated with platinum using an ion sputter film deposition system (E-1010, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Prior to observing the surface and internal microstructures of the film samples, the platinum-coated films were mounted on an SEM specimen holder with the surfaces and cross-sections facing upward. Finally, the microstructures of CN-ZN and AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films were investigated and photographed using scanning electron microscopy (SU8000, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) at different magnifications and an accelerating voltage of 15 kV.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

The CN-ZN and AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films were weighed and placed in a platinum crucible. Then, their thermal stability and properties were examined using a thermogravimetric analyzer (PYRIS 1, Perkin Elmer, Shelton, CT, USA). The thermogravimetric analysis was conducted at 105 °C for 10 min to remove moisture, followed by heating from 105 to 800 °C at 10 °C/min.

Universal material testing (UMT)

The mechanical properties of CN-ZN and AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films were determined following the ASTM D882–18 standard test method for tensile properties of thin film with minor modifications (Bernstein, 2021; Lin et al., 2025). In a typical experiment, the mechanical properties of bioplastic films were investigated using a universal material testing machine (LLOYD, LRX, Poole, UK) with a tensile rate of 10 mm/min. The obtained tensile stress-strain curves of CN-ZN and AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films were analyzed to determine their tensile strength (MPa) and elongation at the break (%).

Water content angle (WCA)

An aliquot of 10 μL deionized water was carefully dropped onto the surface of the bioplastic films and allowed to equilibrate for 15 s, ensuring that the water droplet reached a steady state and a stable contact angle after the droplet spreading (Ponomar et al., 2022). After adjusting the aperture and focus, the water droplet-deposited bioplastic films were photographed, and the water contact angles (θ) were measured using a LAUDA surface analyzer (LAUDA Scientific, LSA60, USA) according to the ASTM D7334–08 standard with minor modifications (D-08, 2008).

Ultraviolet (UV) resistance

The ultraviolet (UV) resistance of CN-ZN and AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films was investigated using a UV test card. The bioplastic films were first cut into the shape of the capital letter “TTU” for Tatung University and then placed on the surface of the UV test card. After that, the bioplastic film-covered UV test cards were illuminated with UV light (Analytik Jena UVGL-25, 4 watts) at 254 nm for 5 min. The extent of color change on the test card was analyzed using ImageJ software (version 1.54d, Wayne Rasband, National Institute of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) with the RGB color model to evaluate the UV resistance of the bioplastic films.

Food packaging application

To assess the feasibility of using CN-ZN and AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films for food packaging, banana peels were first packaged with these films and stored at room temperature for 3 days. The weight loss of unpackaged and packaged banana peels was determined by measuring their weights before and after storage. Also, the browning extent of unpackaged and packaged banana peels before and after storage was determined by analyzing digital images of both unpackaged and packaged banana peels via ImageJ software with the CIEL⁎a⁎b⁎ color space (Lin et al., 2025). Food migration test

For the migration analysis, banana pulps were collected from bananas wrapped with and without AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films after 3 days of storage. The banana pulps were ground and dried at 80 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, 200 mg of pulverized dry banana pulp was digested using a microwave digestion system (MARS 5, CEM, USA) with 10 mL of HNO₃ at 180 °C for 20 min. After digestion, the samples were diluted to 50 mL with deionized water and filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane filter. The Ag concentration in the filtrates was quantified using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES, PerkinElmer Optima 2000 DV, USA). The experimental procedure was modified based on the reported method (Demirbas & Karsli, 2025).

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were obtained from at least three independent measurements (n ≥ 3). Furthermore, these pieces of experimental data were calculated and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses, including calculation of the mean, SD, and p-values, were performed using OriginPro 2024 and Microsoft Excel. Unless otherwise specified, the statistical significance was determined using the Student's t-test with a criterion of p-value <0.05.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of silver nanoparticle-embedded casein nanocomposites (AgNP@CN)

To characterize the physicochemical properties of CN and AgNP@CN, an assemblage of spectroscopic and microscopic techniques was used to examine their optical properties, hydrodynamic size distributions, surface charge, surface chemistry, and morphological features. Previous literature indicates that aspartic acid (Asp) and glutamic acid (Glu) from CN protein could serve as reducing and capping agents, inducing the reduction of silver cations and the formation of AgNPs, and ultimately providing carboxylate and amine functional groups on the AgNP surface (Kolankowski et al., 2023; Pryshchepa et al., 2019). Presented in Fig. 2A is the UV–vis spectra of CN and AgNP@CN, revealing that the absorption peak at ∼285 nm was associated with the chromophore and aromatic rings of residues (i.e., tryptophane (Trp), tyrosine (Tyr), and phenylalanine (Phe)) in the CN protein (Lai, Wang, & Lin, 2024). Upon in situ reduction of silver cation in the CN solution, the intense absorption peak at ∼420 nm in the UV–vis spectrum of AgNP@CN was representative of characteristic localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) of AgNPs embedded into the CN matrix, indicating the synthesis of spherical AgNPs with a size of ∼10 nm (Lai et al., 2024; Lai, Lai, et al., 2022).

As depicted in Fig. 2B, the broad peak at ∼3395 cm−1 and narrow peak at ∼3200 cm−1 that emerged in the FTIR spectrum of CN were representative of the stretching vibration of O—H and N—H groups. The characteristic peaks of amide I (∼1659 cm−1), amide II (∼1535 cm−1), and amide III (∼1234 cm−1) appeared in the FTIR spectrum of the CN were associated with the vibration of C <svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" version="1.0" width="20.666667pt" height="16.000000pt" viewBox="0 0 20.666667 16.000000" preserveAspectRatio="xMidYMid meet"><metadata> Created by potrace 1.16, written by Peter Selinger 2001-2019 </metadata><g transform="translate(1.000000,15.000000) scale(0.019444,-0.019444)" fill="currentColor" stroke="none"><path d="M0 440 l0 -40 480 0 480 0 0 40 0 40 -480 0 -480 0 0 -40z M0 280 l0 -40 480 0 480 0 0 40 0 40 -480 0 -480 0 0 -40z"/></g></svg> O stretching, N—H bending, and C—N stretching of the peptide linkage in protein backbone (Hsieh et al., 2025). The characteristic peak corresponding to N—O stretching vibration was seen at ∼1450 cm−1 in the FTIR spectrum of CN (Pryshchepa et al., 2019). Once the formation of AgNPs embedded into the CN matrix, the peaks related to amide I, amide II, and amide III of CN were shifted to ∼1655, ∼1542, ∼1248 cm−1, indicating that the functional groups of peptide linkage in the protein backbone interacted with the surface of AgNPs to prevent aggregation/agglomeration of AgNPs (Ashraf et al., 2013; Tavaf, Tabatabaei, Khalafi-Nezhad, Panahi, & Hosseini, 2015). The peak seen at ∼1397 cm−1 corresponding to the carboxyl groups (–COOH) of the CN vanished, and a new peak associated with the Ag—O group was observed at ∼881 cm−1 (Basta, Lotfy, Mahmoud, & Abdelwahed, 2020; Liu & Guo, 2009). This solid evidence suggested the binding of AgNPs onto the CN matrix through the interaction(s) between AgNPs and the carboxyl groups of the CN.

To examine the size and surface charge of CN and AgNP@CN, we employed DLS analysis and ζ-potential measurements to determine their hydrodynamic size distributions, polydispersity indices (PDI), average hydrodynamic diameters (Dh), and ζ-potentials. As shown in Figs. 2C–D, the single size distribution for the peak at ∼229 nm and the double size distribution with the peaks at ∼90.45 nm and ∼ 4072 nm were observed in CN and AgNP@CN, respectively. As a result, AgNP@CN exhibited a higher PDI value than CN, indicating the presence of AgNP@CN and its aggregated counterpart in the solution. The average hydrodynamic diameter of AgNP@CN was found to be ∼64.6 nm, signifying that a pronounced amount of AgNP@CN in the form of nanocomposites instead of aggregated counterparts existed in the solution. Our results also revealed that the ζ-potential of AgNP@CN was more negative than that of CN, indicating that negatively charged AgNPs were formed and embedded within the CN matrix. Accordingly, this observation likely stemmed from the more negative charge of AgNPs, which resulted in a higher overall negative charge on AgNP@CN than on bare CN (Kung, Chang, Lai, Li, & How, 2025).

The morphological feature of AgNPs embedded in CN matrix was spherical, uniform particle size, and high dispersity (see Fig. 2E–F). Accordingly, the particle diameter of AgNPs embedded in CN matrix were measured by analyzing the TEM micrographs using the ImageJ software. Our findings showed that the particle diameter of AgNPs embedded in the CN matrix was ∼10 nm, which is consistent with aforesaid LSPR result of AgNP@CN. Given that the AgNPs were embedded in the CN matrix, the hydrodynamic diameter of AgNPs obtained from DLS would be interfered with CN (Lai et al., 2022; Lai, Lai, et al., 2022). Thus, it is deemed reasonable that the hydrodynamic size of AgNPs embedded in the CN matrix is larger than their actual sizes observed via TEM.

Previous studies have demonstrated that casein can effectively stabilize AgNPs and prevent nanoparticle agglomeration through steric stabilization and protein–nanoparticle interactions (Ashraf et al., 2013; Bora & Mishra, 2016). Ashraf et al. (2013) reported that casein-mediated AgNPs remained uniformly dispersed and could be readily re-dispersed in aqueous solution even after freeze-drying treatment. Similarly, Bora and Mishra (2019) observed that casein-mediated AgNPs exhibited excellent storage stability for up to 80 days without obvious aggregation. Since the AgNPs in the present study were synthesized using a similar in situ preparation strategy, the obtained nanoparticles were considered AgNP@CN nanocomposites rather than free AgNPs. In Fig. 2F, the dark spherical nanoparticles (partially indicated by red arrows) correspond to AgNPs, which were observed to be homogeneously dispersed without obvious aggregation. However, due to the relatively low magnification and the low electron contrast of the protein matrix, the surrounding casein structure could not be clearly distinguished in this image. Therefore, higher-magnification TEM-EDX analysis was further performed to clarify the relationship between AgNPs and the casein matrix (see Fig. 2G). The TEM-EDX elemental mapping results demonstrated the co-localization of Ag signals with the C, O, and N elements originating from casein. Moreover, AgNPs were observed to be uniformly distributed within the protein matrix without obvious phase separation or aggregation. These results strongly suggest that AgNPs were successfully immobilized and embedded within the casein matrix, thereby forming AgNPs@CN nanocomposites.

Macroscopic and microscopic observations of AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films

Depicted in Fig. 3A are the physical appearances of CN-ZN-0, CN-ZN-0.3, CN-ZN-0.6, AgNP@CN-ZN-0.3, and AgNP@CN-ZN-0.6, showing that those bioplastic films were successfully synthesized in this study. Based on our observations, the ruptured and fragile appearance of CN-ZN-0 was likely due to the intrinsic brittleness and mechanical instability of the protein (Peydayesh et al., 2021; Peydayesh et al., 2023). Unlike CN-ZN-0, the synthesized CN-ZN-0.3, CN-ZN-0.6, AgNP@CN-ZN-0.3, and AgNP@CN-ZN-0.6 retained their intact appearance, indicating that either CN or AgNP@CN could enhance the structural integrity of ZN bioplastic films. Upon incorporating AgNP@CN, the color of the ZN bioplastic film shifted from yellowish to dark brown, likely due to the optical effect caused by the LSPR of AgNPs.

Upon CN or AgNP@CN incorporation, the surface and internal microstructures of CN-ZN-0.3 and AgNP@CN-ZN-0.3 films were more uniform and compact than those of the pure ZN film (i.e., CN-ZN-0). We can conjecture that CN or AgNP@CN can fill the microscopic voids between biopolymer chains, thereby forming a dense and smooth microstructure in CN-ZN-0.3 and AgNP@CN-ZN-0.3 films (Karthick et al., 2022). When ZN is blended with other biopolymers in appropriate ratios, additional noncovalent interactions (e.g., hydrophobic associations and hydrogen bonding) can improve compatibility, resulting in a more uniform and homogeneous surface (Pena Serna & Lopes Filho, 2015). In the case of the CN-ZN-0.3 film, the incorporation of CN likely enhances robust interactions between hydrophobic ZN domains and the polar/charged groups of CN. This facilitates the formation of hydrogen bond networks between CN and ZN, ultimately resulting in a more uniform film microstructure (S. Wang et al., 2023). The influences of metal NP incorporation on the microstructure of ZN film have been shown to correlate with protein–metal NP interactions and metal coordination with protein functional groups (Mei et al., 2017; Yoosaf et al., 2019). With further increases in CN or AgNP@CN content, the microstructures of CN-ZN-0.6 and AgNP@CN-ZN-0.6 films exhibited pleated bulges and corrugated protrusions. It was speculated that the stacking and deposition of CN or AgNP@CN within the ZN matrix led to their accumulation on the surface and within the internal microstructures of bioplastic films, thereby forming pleated bulges (Lai et al., 2023). The possible mechanism governing the effects of CN or AgNP@CN addition on the microstructure of ZN films is illustrated in Figs. 3D–E.

Mechanical and thermal properties of AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films

Presented in Fig. 5A is the tensile strength of CN-ZN-0, CN-ZN-0.3, CN-ZN-0.6, AgNP@CN-ZN-0.3, and AgNP@CN-ZN-0.6. Our results revealed no statistically significant differences in the tensile strength of CN-ZN-0, CN-ZN-0.3, and CN-ZN-0.6. In addition, the tensile strengths of bioplastic films followed the order: AgNP@CN-ZN-0.6 > AgNP@CN-ZN-0.3 > CN-ZN bioplastic films. The values of elongation at break of bioplastic films followed the order: AgNP@CN-ZN-0.6 > AgNP@CN-ZN-0.3 > CN-ZN-0.6 > CN-ZN-0.3 > CN-ZN-0 (see Fig. 5B).

Histograms of (A) tensile strength and (B) elongation at break of CN-ZN-0, CN-ZN-0.3, CN-ZN-0.6, AgNP@CN-ZN-0.3, and AgNP@CN-ZN-0.6. (Different geometric patterns above bars indicate significant differences (p < 0.05)) (C) Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and (D) differential thermogravimetry (DTG) curves of CN-ZN-0, CN-ZN-0.6, and AgNP@CN-ZN-0.6.

Evidence indicates that the effects of nanoparticle (NPs) incorporation on the mechanical characteristics of polymeric films are strongly associated with the content, dispersion, and particle size of NPs within the polymeric matrix (Bhatnagar et al., 2024; Guo, Xu, Yang, Li, & Wu, 2023; M. Yang et al., 2022; Zare, Rhee, & Hui, 2017). As inorganic NPs are uniformly dispersed in a polymeric matrix, they can efficiently transfer mechanical stress across the material interface and restrict polymer chain mobility, thereby improving the mechanical strength of polymeric films. However, excessive NPs in the polymer matrix can diminish the interfacial area between the NPs and the matrix. Moreover, because the specific surface area and the effective volume fraction of NPs decrease through aggregation/agglomeration, there may be a reduction in tensile strength and interfacial/interphase properties of polymeric films in the presence of NPs.

Based on our results, the AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films exhibited higher tensile strength than CN-ZN bioplastic films, while their tensile strength was increased with the content of AgNP@CN. This observation is attributed to the high specific surface area of AgNPs, which facilitates robust interfacial interactions with biopolymer chains (e.g., ZN and CN), thereby improving stress transfer under applied load (Karthick et al., 2022; Mirkhalaf, Yazdani Sarvestani, Yang, Jakubinek, & Ashrafi, 2021). Moreover, the interfacial bonding strength of AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films was enhanced by intermolecular hydrogen bonding between AgNP@CN and the ZN matrix, thereby facilitating the effective transfer of tensile stress across the material interface (Lin et al., 2025; Y. Zhou, Fan, & Chen, 2016). Therefore, even if some aggregates were observed in SEM micrographs of AgNP@CN-ZN films, the AgNP@CN-ZN films still exhibited higher tensile strength owing to the optimal NP content, well-dispersed NPs, and strong interactions between proteins and NPs.

Our findings indicated that the flexibility of pure ZN film (CN-ZN-0) was improved upon the introduction of CN or AgNP@CN into the ZN matrix. The possible reason for the increased ductility and flexibility observed in AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films compared to CN-ZN films is attributable to the uniform dispersion of AgNP@CN within the ZN matrix. This uniform dispersion enhances the interfacial adhesion between the biopolymer matrix and the nanoscale filler, improving stress transfer and promoting shear-yielding deformation that dissipates energy through polymer chain slippage rather than brittle fracture (Chou et al., 2026; Mo et al., 2021).

Figs. 5C–D present the TGA and DTG curves of CN-ZN-0, CN-ZN-0.6, and AgNP@CN-ZN-0.6, indicating that the films' weight loss increases with temperature. In the initial stage (< 300 °C), a slight weight loss is observed, which can be attributed to the evaporation of moisture and residual low-molecular-weight components contained in the bioplastic films. At elevated temperatures (∼300–400 °C), the thermal degradation of the CN-ZN-0, CN-ZN-0.6, and AgNP@CN-ZN-0.6 was found, which is attributed to the breakage of the biopolymer backbone (Cao, Chen, Wang, Chen, & Wang, 2017). As a result, the thermal degradation peak at a higher temperature (∼500–600 °C) was observed in the DTG curve of AgNP@CN-ZN-0.6, indicating that AgNP@CN-ZN-0.6 had greater thermal stability than CN-ZN-0 and CN-ZN-0.6. This could be attributed to intramolecular hydrogen bonding between AgNP@CN and ZN molecules, thereby enhancing the thermal resistance of ZN bioplastic films, consistent with previous reports in which inorganic fillers enhanced the thermal resistance of biopolymer-based films (Abdollahi, Alboofetileh, Rezaei, & Behrooz, 2013; Jamróz, Kulawik, & Kopel, 2019). Based on these results, AgNP@CN incorporation can reinforce the mechanical properties and thermal stability of ZN bioplastic films.

Surface wettability and wet stability of AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films

As revealed in Fig. 6A, the water contact angles (WCAs) of CN-ZN-0, CN-ZN-0.3, CN-ZN-0.6, AgNP@CN-ZN-0.3, and AgNP@CN-ZN-0.6 were determined to be 56.49 ± 13.38°, 85.47 ± 10.12°, 91.28 ± 3.02°, 68.90 ± 4.75°, and 83.14 ± 2.50°, respectively. Despite the fact that ZN, as a fundamental component of bioplastic films, is recognized as a primary prolamine and hydrophobic substance, the surface wettability of pure ZN films (i.e., CN-ZN-0) is also influenced by the surface roughness and microstructure (X. Chen et al., 2019). Due to its rough surface microstructure, CN-ZN-0 exhibited a low water contact angle and high surface hydrophilicity (X. Chen et al., 2019). When CN or AgNP@CN was incorporated, the increase in surface hydrophobicity of bioplastic films was likely due to interactions between these additives and the hydrophilic functional groups of ZN, thereby shielding the free hydrophilic functional groups on the film surface. Another reason is that the surface microstructure of ZN bioplastic films in the presence of CN or AgNP@CN became denser and smoother, resulting in reduced surface roughness and increased water contact angles (S. Wang et al., 2023). Compared with CN-ZN films, the AgNP@CN-ZN films showed lower average water contact angle (WCAs) values, indicating the relatively hydrophilic surface of AgNP@CN-ZN films. These results were likely related to hydrophilic functional groups and surface charge at the AgNP–protein interface, which introduce additional polar groups in the AgNP@CN–ZN films that can interact with water molecules (Yang, Guo, Yao, Mei, & Tang, 2019). Our results also showed statistically significant differences in WCA values between AgNP@CN-ZN-0.3 and CN-ZN-0.3, CN-ZN-0.6, or AgNP@CN-ZN-0.6. We conjecture that the addition of more CN or AgNP@CN could interact with the polar groups of ZN, thereby hampering the interaction of water molecules with free polar functional groups on the film surface (S. Wang et al., 2023). Taken together, this could explain why AgNP@CN-ZN-0.3 exhibited greater surface hydrophilicity than the CN-ZN films, rather than the pure ZN film (i.e., CN-ZN-0).

Histograms of (A) water contact angle, (B) moisture content, (C) water solubility, and (D) water absorption of CN-ZN-0, CN-ZN-0.3, CN-ZN-0.6, AgNP@CN-ZN-0.3, and AgNP@CN-ZN-0.6. (Different geometric patterns above bars indicate significant differences (p < 0.05)).

As depicted in Figs. 6B–C, there are no statistically significant differences in moisture content and water solubility among the bioplastic films. The moisture content and water solubility of all films were below 12% and 35%, respectively. Our results indicated that the CN-ZN and AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films exhibited low hygroscopicity at room temperature and ambient humidity, as well as high wet stability upon immersion in aqueous solutions, likely due to the intrinsic hydrophobicity of ZN (Lai et al., 2025). However, the water absorption of bioplastic films followed the trend: CN-ZN-0 > CN-ZN-0.3 > AgNP@CN-ZN-0.3 > CN-ZN-0.6 > AgNP@CN-ZN-0.6 (see Fig. 6D). The AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films with higher AgNP@CN content showed the lowest water absorption among all bioplastic films, indicating that the AgNP@CN incorporation could prevent the water penetration into the film structure. Owing to the increased surface hydrophobicity and denser microstructure of ZN bioplastic film after AgNP@CN incorporation, AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films showed high water resistance and low porosity, which ultimately reduced water penetration and diffusion into the film architecture (S. Wang et al., 2023).

Water vapor resistance and ultraviolet resistance of AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films

To investigate the effects of CN or AgNP@CN incorporation on water vapor barrier properties of ZN bioplastic film, we determined the water vapor permeability (WVP) of CN-ZN and AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films (see Fig. 7A). Our results revealed that the WVP values of CN-ZN-0, CN-ZN-0.6, and AgNP@CN-ZN-0.6 were determined to be ∼9.01 × 10−9, ∼7.60 × 10−9, and ∼ 6.65 × 10−9 g m m−2 h−1 Pa−1, respectively, indicating enhanced water vapor barrier efficacy of ZN bioplastic film upon incorporation of CN or AgNP@CN. Several points can be made from WVP results: (1) Considering that the WVP of films is associated with film polarity and the interaction of water molecules with the film, the decrease in polarity and surface hydrophilicity of ZN bioplastic films after CN or AgNP@CN incorporation improved their barrier efficacy against water vapor (Le Tien et al., 2000). (2) Their homogeneous surface, low porosity, and small pore size confer superior resistance to water vapor penetration in the CN-ZN and AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films (Fu et al., 2022). (3) The introduction of CN or AgNP@CN in ZN bioplastic film resulted in the increase in tortuosity of water vapor diffusion through the film architecture, thereby raising the water vapor barrier efficacy of ZN bioplastic film (Lin et al., 2025).

For comparison purposes, the WVP values of CN-ZN bioplastic film, AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic film, fossil-derived plastics, bio-based plastics, biodegradable plastics, and protein-based bioplastics were summarized in Fig. 7B (Taylor & Taylor, 2023; F. Wu, Misra, & Mohanty, 2021; Ye Zhang et al., 2024). Our results indicated that the water vapor barrier efficacy of our CN-ZN and AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films outperformed bioplastics derived from proteins (e.g., soy protein, pea protein, kafirin protein, and keratin protein) and well-known biodegradable plastics (e.g., polylactic acid (PLA), polyhydroxyalkanoate (PHA), polycaprolactone (PCL), and polybutylene succinate (PBS), etc). Furthermore, the water vapor barrier efficacy of our CN-ZN and AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films was comparable to or superior to that of conventional fossil-derived plastics, such as polyethylene terephthalate (PET), polypropylene (PP), polyvinylidene chloride (PVDC), and polyethylene (PE). Aside from fossil-derived plastics, CN-ZN and AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films exhibited WVP values of comparable magnitude to those of bio-based plastics, including bio-PE and polyethylene furanoate (PEF). Thus, the CN-ZN and AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films show great promise as packaging materials and as alternatives to conventional fossil-based plastics.

The results in Fig. 7C show that the color of the UV test card without a film coverage altered from colorless to purple after UV illumination. Conversely, the area of the UV test card covered by the bioplastic films remained colorless upon UV exposure. As the photoswitchable molecular structure changed from a closed-ring form to an open-ring form under UV illumination, the color of the UV test card turned from colorless to purple (T. Zhang, Fu, Chen, Cui, & Liu, 2016). To gain further insight into the UV resistance of CN-ZN and AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films, the extent of color change of the UV test card covered by these films was analyzed using RGB color evaluation (see Fig. 7D). The magnitude of the RGB value of the UV test card covered by AgNP@CN-ZN-0.6 exceeded that of the UV test card subjected to UV irradiation at 50 μW/cm2. In contrast, the RGB value magnitude of the UV test card covered by CN-ZN-0.6 was comparable to that of the test card exposed to UV irradiation at the same intensity. The UV resistance mechanism of CN-ZN and AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films involves three factors: light reflection, light scattering, and light absorption (He et al., 2024; He et al., 2025). First, the high opacity of CN-ZN and AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films endows them with UV resistance performance through light reflection and light scattering. Second, the amino acid residues, such as tyrosine (Tyr), histidine (His), tryptophan (Trp), and phenylalanine (Phe), in CN and ZN possess aromatic rings and chromophores that effectively block UV light by absorbing UV photons. Third, owing to the LSPR-induced optical effect of AgNPs, the AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic film can absorb UVA light (λ = 320–400 nm), thereby enhancing its UV resistance. Based on our proposed mechanism, this could reasonably explain why the AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic film presented greater UV resistance performance than the CN-ZN bioplastic film.

Application of AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films as food packaging materials

To assess the feasibility of using CN-ZN and AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films as food packaging materials, the weight loss and appearance quality of unpackaged banana peel and banana peel packaged with CN-ZN or AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films were measured and compared. The physical appearance of the unpackaged banana peel was observed to exhibit browning and mold growth, whereas the packaged banana peel showed the opposite (see Figs. 8A–D). Possible reasons for this difference in physical appearance of unpackaged and packaged banana peel include the following: (a) The CN-ZN and AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films can provide a physical barrier that prevents microbial contamination and inhibits browning caused by oxidation processes in food (Du et al., 2023). (b) AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films can serve as active packaging materials that protect against physical damage and suppress bacterial and mold growth responsible for food spoilage, owing to the antimicrobial activity of AgNPs (Vanlalveni et al., 2021).

(E) Percentage of weight loss of unpackaged banana peels and those packaged with different films after storage periods for 3 days. (F) Difference in the color of unpackaged banana peels and those packaged with different films as measured using CIEL⁎a⁎b⁎ color parameters. (Different geometric patterns above bars indicate significant differences (p < 0.05)).

As seen in Fig. 8E, the average weight loss of banana peels after three days of storage followed the sequence: unpackaged banana peel > CN-ZN-0 packaged banana peel > AgNP@CN-ZN-0.6 packaged banana peel > CN-ZN-0.6 packaged banana peel. Our results indicated a statistically significant difference in the weight loss of unpackaged and packaged banana peel, suggesting that using CN-ZN or AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films as packaging materials could retain the moisture content of banana peel and reduce its transpiration rate (Hailu, Seyoum Workneh, & Belew, 2014; Lin et al., 2025). It is reasonable to conjecture that the superior water vapor barrier efficacy of CN-ZN and AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films could reduce the moisture loss from packaged banana peel.

Despite the fact that the water absorption of AgNP@CN-ZN-0.6 films was lower than that of CN-ZN-0.6, reduced water absorption of bioplastic films does not necessarily correlate with decreased weight loss of the packaged fruit. Water absorption primarily indicates the hydrophilicity and swelling behavior of bioplastic films and is commonly used to assess the polymer matrix's affinity for water molecules (Lin et al., 2025). In contrast, the weight loss of fruit packaged with active films during storage is influenced by multiple film- and system-level factors, including microstructural heterogeneity and water vapor permeability (which regulate moisture transfer and transpiration rates), gas barrier properties (which influence respiration rate and metabolic activity), and antioxidant and antimicrobial activities (which defend against spoilage from microbial contamination and browning caused by oxidative damage) (Chou et al., 2026; Du et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2025).

As observed in Fig. 8F, the total color difference (ΔE⁎) of banana peels after three days of storage followed the order: unpackaged banana peel (70.51) > CN-ZN-0 packaged banana peel (45.19) > AgNP@CN-ZN-0.6 packaged banana peel (38.84) > CN-ZN-0.6 packaged banana peel (40.88). Based on the CIEL⁎a⁎b⁎ color space, the ΔE⁎ values between samples exceed 3, indicating that the colors of different samples are visually distinct (López-Ortiz et al., 2024). Unlike unpackaged banana peel, the ΔE⁎ values of banana peel packaged with CN-ZN or AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films were comparable, suggesting a similar appearance quality. Our results demonstrated that CN-ZN and AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films could be employed as packaging materials, maintain the appearance quality of food, and delay fruit enzymatic browning and oxidation (Tang, Kuo, & Liao, 2024).

The food migration results showed that the Ag contents in banana pulp wrapped with and without AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films were 2.97 × 10−5 and 5.57 × 10−5 mg/kg dry weight (DW), respectively. These values indicate that no significant Ag migration into the banana pulp was detected during the 3-day storage period. In addition, the banana peel itself may serve as a natural protective barrier that further limits direct Ag migration into the edible pulp. Moreover, the detected Ag concentrations were substantially lower than the specific migration limit of 0.01 mg/kg food established by Commission Regulation (EC) No. 450/2009 for non-authorized substances used in active food contact materials. These findings suggest that the AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films exhibited minimal Ag migration under the tested storage conditions and demonstrate their potential applicability for food packaging applications.

Discussion

ZN is the main protein in maize endosperm, representing about 45–50% of total protein in corn (Shukla & Cheryan, 2001). The tertiary structure of ZN forms through hydrophobic interactions and intramolecular disulfide bonds, while its secondary structure is mainly rich in α-helices (∼68%) (Chou et al., 2026). Its high content of hydrophobic amino acids, such as glutamine (∼20–25%), leucine (∼19–21%), proline (∼10–12%), and alanine (∼10–12%), makes ZN easily soluble in non-polar solvents (e.g., ethanol) (Shukla & Cheryan, 2001). ZN consists of four protein fractions: α-zein (∼75%), β-zein (∼15%), γ-zein (∼7.5%), and δ-zein (∼1–5%). The α-zein consists of two main polypeptides with molecular weights of 19 and 22 kDa. The γ-zein contains two polypeptides with molecular weights of 16 and 27 kDa. The β-zein and δ-zein have molecular weights of about 14 kDa and nearly 10 kDa, respectively (Yingying Zhang, Xu, Zhang, Hu, & Luan, 2022).

Under room-temperature conditions, the reduction kinetics of Ag+ ions became significantly slower than those under heating conditions; therefore, a longer reaction time was required to ensure sufficient formation and stabilization of AgNP@CN nanocomposites. To monitor the nanoparticle formation process, UV–visible spectroscopy analysis was conducted at different reaction times, and the corresponding results are shown in Fig. S1. A characteristic surface plasmon resonance (SPR) absorption peak of AgNPs appeared at approximately ∼420 nm after 1 day of reaction, indicating the initiation of AgNP formation. The absorbance intensity gradually increased with increasing reaction time. Notably, a substantial increase in absorbance was observed between days 2 and 8, suggesting rapid nanoparticle formation during this period. After day 8, the increase in absorbance gradually approached a plateau by day 10, indicating that nanoparticle formation had nearly reached completion. Under these conditions, the conversion efficiency reached approximately 80.8%. Therefore, a reaction time of 10 days was selected to ensure sufficient nanoparticle formation while minimizing the presence of residual Ag+ ions, which could otherwise interfere with subsequent experiments and affect reproducibility.

Although prolonged reaction times may potentially raise concerns regarding nanoparticle aggregation, casein played an important role in stabilizing the nanoparticles during the synthesis process. During routine characterization, the particle size and polydispersity index (PDI) of the AgNP@CN nanocomposites remained relatively stable throughout the reaction period, indicating good colloidal stability without obvious aggregation (data not shown). Furthermore, after completion of the reaction, the AgNP@CN nanocomposites were immediately freeze-dried for preservation. Prior to film preparation, the freeze-dried samples were re-dispersed and their particle size was re-evaluated to confirm the stability and reproducibility of the nanocomposite system.

Given that the mechanical properties of packaging materials/films are associated with their ability to withstand mechanical stresses, preserve integrity, and prevent physical damage or contamination of the food product, these properties are critically important for ensuring food quality and safety during storage, transportation, and processing in the food industry (Sangroniz et al., 2019; Shah et al., 2023; Shi et al., 2025). For comparison purposes, the tensile strength of AgNP@CN-ZN films developed in our study and other packaging materials reported in the literature (e.g., commercial petroleum-derived plastic film, commercial bioplastic film, silver-functionalized starch-, polysaccharide-, or protein-based films) were summarized in Table S1. Several points could be drawn as follows: (1) Aside from the AgNP@CN-ZN films having tensile strength on the same order of magnitude as conventional plastics and bioplastics (e.g., polyethylene terephthalate (PET) and polylactic acid (PLA)), their tensile strength was also comparable to that of polyethylene (PE) and polyvinyl chloride (PVC) (Shi et al., 2025). (2) The AgNP@CN-ZN films prepared in this study showed better tensile strength than some AgNP-functionalized starch-, polysaccharide-, and protein-based packaging films. It is evident that the AgNP@CN-ZN films exhibited superior mechanical properties compared with some biomass-based packaging films functionalized with AgNPs reported in previous studies. (3) Given that our AgNP@CN-ZN films were found to have tensile strength comparable to most AgNP-functionalized biomass-based packaging films, their mechanical stiffness and rigidity are also similar to those of these packaging films. Taken together, these pieces of evidence indicate that our AgNP@CN-ZN films possess mechanical strength comparable to or better than that of AgNP-functionalized biomass-based films and commercial plastics and bioplastics used for food packaging. We believe this investigation demonstrates the applicability of AgNP@CN-ZN films with suitable mechanical properties for practical food packaging applications.

Despite the fact that the possibility of harnessing AgNP@CN-ZN films as packaging materials has been demonstrated, their manufacturing costs and packaging applications warrant further investigation. Presented in Table S2 is the cost analysis for AgNP@CN-ZN film production, revealing the estimated cost of AgNP@CN-ZN film production. The market price of ZN is ∼10–50 $/kg, depending on purity and grade (Made-in-China, 2026). The CN is typically priced at ∼6–30 $/kg, depending on CN type and protein content (Accio, 2026). The AgNPs are reported to cost ∼70–80 $/kg, depending on particle size, dispersion/solvent, and grade (IndiaMART, 2026). Upon scale-up of polymeric film production via industrial aqueous processing and casting, the AgNP@CN-ZN films are estimated to add ∼0.5–1.0 $/kg due to processing overhead (Lin et al., 2025; Xu & Li, 2023). Based on the formulation for AgNP@CN-ZN film fabrication, the preliminary cost analysis indicated that the films are estimated to cost ∼11–22 $/kg. Even though the price of AgNP@CN-ZN films is relatively higher than commercial PLA bioplastics (∼1.6–2.2 $/kg), their prices are comparable to those of biomass-based packaging films (∼5.1–16.7 $/kg) (Do Kim et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2025).

Several AgNP-modified polymeric hybrids/composites have previously been reported, and the possible interaction(s) between AgNPs and polymeric matrices have been investigated accordingly. In the literature, the AgNP-doped chitosan/polycaprolactone nanocomposite films were synthesized using green methods. Accordingly, these films were characterized for their physicochemical properties and assessed for antimicrobial activity against Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Candida albicans. As a result, weak interactions between the polymer's polar groups and the AgNP surfaces were observed. Furthermore, the interfacial interactions between AgNPs and polymer chains involved coordination, physical adsorption, and hydrogen bonding mediated by surface-bound species or adsorbed water (Pekdemir et al., 2025). Another study found that AgNP-decorated polylactic acid/polycaprolactone (PLA/PCL) nanocomposite films exhibited antibacterial activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. In addition, the shape memory property of PLA/PCL composite films was improved by AgNP decoration, owing to reversible covalent bonds and supramolecular interactions (Selcuk Pekdemir et al., 2023).

Protein-based bioplastics provide significant advantages over starch-based and other biomass-derived bioplastics, particularly in packaging and food preservation. Complex amino acid structures enable the formation of strong intermolecular networks, leading to higher tensile strength and improved structural integrity compared to the brittle nature of starch-based films, which often require plasticizers or blending to enhance mechanical properties (Lamp, Kaltschmitt, & Dethloff, 2022; Nandane & Jain, 2015). Given that protein-derived films offer superior barrier properties to prevent oxidative spoilage and extend the shelf life of food items, it is apparent that protein-derived films exhibit lower oxygen permeability compared to starch-derived films (Chang et al., 2019). Furthermore, the variety of structural and functional groups in proteins endows protein-based films with modulated functional properties through processing methods, chemical and/or physical crosslinking, and functional additives, such as antimicrobial nanoparticles. The versatility of functional groups and the ease of surface modification expand the design possibilities of protein-based bioplastics beyond those of chemically uniform polysaccharide- and starch-based bioplastics (Abe et al., 2021; Poulose, Jönkkäri, Hedenqvist, & Kuusipalo, 2021).

As summarized in Table S3, numerous studies have consistently demonstrated that AgNP-containing biopolymer films exhibit broad-spectrum antibacterial activity against both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, particularly E. coli and S. aureus. For example, flaxseed protein–alginate films incorporated with AgNPs showed inhibition zones of 2.6–6.5 mm against E. coli and S. aureus, respectively (Dash, Kumar, Kumari, & Malik, 2021). Similarly, chitosan/PVA films loaded with AgNPs exhibited inhibition zones up to 16.07 mm against E. coli (D. Yang et al., 2023). Whey protein-based films containing biosynthesized AgNPs demonstrated inhibition zones ranging from 13 to 19.7 mm against several foodborne pathogens, including S. aureus, E. coli, Salmonella Enteritidis, and Listeria monocytogenes (Çağrı Mehmetoğlu, Sezer, & Erol, 2021). In addition, PVA/xanthan gum films containing green-synthesized AgNPs exhibited inhibition zones of 18.79 mm and 20.21 mm against E. coli and S. aureus, respectively (Yu et al., 2025). These findings strongly support the antimicrobial potential of AgNP-incorporated films for active food packaging applications.

The antimicrobial mechanism of AgNPs has been widely attributed to the interaction between Ag+ ions and bacterial cell membranes, which can increase membrane permeability, induce oxidative stress, disrupt enzymatic activity, and inhibit DNA replication (Feng, Fan, He, & Ma, 2024). Moreover, the nanoscale dimensions of AgNPs provide a high surface-area-to-volume ratio, thereby enhancing contact with microbial cells and improving antibacterial efficiency. Previous studies have also indicated that smaller AgNPs generally exhibit stronger antimicrobial activity due to their enhanced cellular interaction and Ag+ release behavior (Bora & Mishra, 2019).

Conclusion

In summary, silver nanoparticle-embedded casein nanocomposites (AgNP@CN) were successfully synthesized and used as functional fillers and reinforcement additives to enhance the functional properties of zein (ZN)-derived bioplastic films. The characterization of AgNP@CN using UV–vis spectroscopy and electron microscopy indicated that the size of AgNPs embedded in CN nanocomposites was determined to be ∼10 nm. Furthermore, the results from the polydispersity index, hydrodynamic size distribution, and ζ-potential revealed that AgNP@CN exhibited uniform dispersion, a hydrodynamic diameter of ∼90.45 nm, and a negative surface charge. Upon incorporation of AgNP@CN into ZN matrix, as-synthesized AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films showed superior tensile strength, elongation at break, thermal stability, and surface hydrophobicity compared to pure ZN bioplastic films, which is attributed to strong interfacial hydrogen bonding and uniform microstructures. Our study found that the water vapor barrier efficacy of AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films was comparable to or superior to that of biodegradable plastics and conventional plastics, including the well-known polylactic acid (PLA), polyethylene terephthalate (PET), and polyethylene (PE). In addition, a higher AgNP@CN content in the bioplastic film resulted in better UV resistance, likely due to chromophores from aromatic residues in the protein and the LSPR-induced optical effect of AgNPs. Our results showed that there was no significant Ag migration into the banana pulp from the AgNP@CN-ZN film, which is below the migration limit of 0.01 mg/kg in food. According to packaging results, AgNP@CN-ZN bioplastic films used as packaging materials could maintain the appearance quality and delay enzymatic browning of banana peel. Taken together, our investigation demonstrates the feasibility of using nanoparticle-protein composite materials for packaging applications.

Despite the high estimated cost of AgNP@CN-ZN films in this study, there is no doubt that they still offer various advantages over commercial plastics and bioplastics. More specifically, the AgNP@CN–ZN films exhibited high water vapor barrier capacity, superior wet stability, robust mechanical strength, outstanding UV-shielding performance, and good antimicrobial activity, making them suitable candidates for active food packaging. Owing to these functional properties, the AgNP@CN–ZN films are particularly suitable as packaging materials for wrapping and preserving perishable products (e.g., sliced fresh fruits and fresh-cut vegetables) and refrigerated foods (e.g., raw meats and seafood), which demand high microbial safety, freshness, quality, and extended shelf life. Overall, the AgNP@CN–ZN films offer a promising packaging option for preserving perishable and refrigerated foods, providing enhanced barrier, mechanical, and antimicrobial properties that help prevent microbial spoilage and quality loss.
