Fine-Grained Interpretation of Political Opinions in Large Language Models
Jingyu Hu, Mengyue Yang, Mengnan Du, Weiru Liu

Abstract
Studies of LLMs’ political opinions mainly rely on evaluations of their open-ended responses. Recent work indicates that there is a misalignment between LLMs’ responses and their internal intentions. This motivates us to probe LLMs’ internal mechanisms and help uncover their internal political states. Additionally, we found that the analysis of LLMs’ political opinions often relies on single-axis concepts, which can lead to concept confounds. In this work, we extend the single-axis to multi-dimensions and apply interpretable representation engineering techniques for more transparent LLM political concept learning. Specifically, we designed a four-dimensional political learning framework and constructed a corresponding dataset for fine-grained political concept vector learning. These vectors can be used to detect and intervene in LLM internals. Experiments are conducted on eight open-source LLMs with three representation engineering techniques. Results show these vectors can disentangle political concept confounds. Detection tasks validate the semantic meaning of the vectors and show good generalization and robustness in OOD settings. Intervention Experiments show these vectors can intervene in LLMs to generate responses with different political leanings.



Introduction

Despite the success of large language models (LLMs) in many fields and tasks, there is growing public concern about the ethical implications of LLMs.
Many studies show that LLMs can replicate and even amplify societal biases
(
Bender et al. 2021
;
Wan et al. 2023
)
.
Such social bias can appear as racial or gender stereotypes, or as a preference for certain values in responses.
This issue becomes especially tricky and subtle in the political domain since LLMs favouring specific values can unconsciously influence the decisions of the policy-makers.

Some studies have revealed that LLMs exhibit systematic political biases and have attempted to evaluate and correct them
(
Rozado 2024
;
Motoki, Pinho Neto, and Rodrigues 2024
)
.

Piao et al. 2025
designed LLMs-based multi-agent systems to simulate its potential social impacts, it was discovered that LLMs can exhibit opinion polarization phenomena similar to the human society. These assessments are generally prompt-based, directly asking LLMs about their left- or right-leaning position on some selected topics.
Recent research has begun exploring possibilities of assessing political bias via tuning LLMs’ parameters.
For example, PoliTune
(
Agiza, Mostagir, and Reda 2024
)
used left-leaning and right-leaning datasets for Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO) and ultimately produced two fine-tuned LLMs with opposing political preferences. This work indicates that the internal parameters of LLMs are highly sensitive to data selection with different viewpoints, and can be manipulated through data to favour a particular stance.

Nevertheless, these strategies still treat LLMs as black boxes, assessing their explicit political bias via generated outputs while neglecting the role of internal intentions.

Marks et al. 2025
show that LLMs can appear to achieve the intended objectives while their hidden intentions remain misaligned.
If such misalignment contains implicit hidden biases, those biases can subtly influence users’ opinions: studies have found that approximately one-fifth of users can shift their values after interacting with LLMs
(
Ju et al. 2025
;
Potter et al. 2024
)
.
This motivates us to explore LLMs’ internal mechanisms to understand how different political leanings emerge and change within LLMs.

Recent work on LLMs’ interpretability indicates the possibility of understanding and steering LLM behaviours by learning their internal feature representations
(
Turner et al. 2023a
;
Panickssery et al. 2023
)
. The core idea of representation engineering is to discover the hidden states of each layer of LLMs, train vectors representing concepts occurring in these representations, and use these vectors to detect LLMs’ internal state and steer LLM outputs to align with given concepts by intervention on specific representations.
For example, one can train left-leaning or right-leaning vectors to steer an LLM’s outputs left or right.
Although representation learning has been discussed in many fields, it is still largely unexplored in the politics domain. This paper aims to introduce a systematic exploration of LLMs’ internal political concepts via representation engineering techniques.

Meanwhile, there is a unique challenge in LLMs’ political discussions different from other domains: most current work operates on political datasets within a single left-right axis, however, in real political scenarios, the boundary between left and right can be subtle, which can lead to conceptual confounds between ‘left’ and ‘right’.
For example, the political compass
1
1

1

https://www.politicalcompass.org/
notes that France’s National Front, popularly described as ‘far right’, actually supports left-leaning economic policies. Some recent work has tried to include a second axis (e.g., the progressive-conservative axis) to supplement the left-right spectrum; however, the essential issue remains unaddressed: what exactly do the concepts of ‘left’ and ‘right’ mean?
Due to cultural differences, definitions of left and right are not as clear as physiological concepts like gender or age. The ‘left is right’ phenomenon
(
Wojcik, Cislak, and Schmidt 2021
)
can occur, leading to unconscious concept confounds.
As the model is pre-trained on real-world data, LLMs can also contain similar inconsistencies, where content described as right-leaning might actually contain left-leaning views, causing LLMs to misassociate some right-leaning concepts with left-leaning ones.

To disentangle these conceptual confounds and enhance transparency in LLMs’ political learning, our work proposes a fine-grained political learning framework with mechanistic interpretability techniques.
Specifically, we first construct datasets representing left- and right-leaning statements across four dimensions: economic, diplomatic, civil and society. Then interpretability techniques are applied to extract politically disentangled representations from these datasets, which enable us to derive corresponding political concept vectors.
The concept vector correlation analysis shows we can correctly identify the disentangled concept representations.
We also designed a detection task to predict the political leaning for a given statement using the concept vectors. The detection performance verifies that the inferred concept vectors have semantic meaning and perform well in both in-distribution and out-of-distribution settings.
Further intervention experiments show that these concept vectors can modify the LLMs’ internal representations and ultimately steer the models to generate content aligned with the leaning of the political concept vector.

Overall, this work aims to design a fine-grained framework based on representation engineering to more transparently and meticulously explore the internal political states of LLMs. The contributions can be summarized in three folds:

•

Fine-grained Political Framework
We extended the single-axis political analysis to four dimensions and constructed the corresponding fine-grained political dataset to mitigate political concept confounds.

•

Internal Political Concept Vector Learning

Based on the designed framework, we applied representation engineering techniques to learn disentangled political concept vectors from LLM internals. The experiments were conducted across eight open-source LLMs using three interpretable representation engineering methods (CAA, RepE, and Linear Probing).

•

Robust Performance on Detection and Intervention Tasks
We evaluated the effectiveness of these concept vectors on LLM internals detection and intervention tasks.
The results show that these vectors achieved strong detection performance in both in-distribution and out-of-distribution settings. Moreover, these vectors can successfully intervene in LLM outputs to generate statements with different political leanings.

Interpretable Representation Engineering in LLMs

Studies on LLMs’ representation learning show that each layer’s internal representations encode rich information like semantic content, concepts, factual knowledge, and attributes
(
Gurnee and Tegmark 2023
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Jin et al. 2025
)
. Also, the different sub-regions of the representation space appear to specialise in different tasks. This suggests the LLM’s hidden states are learnable and interpretable, and has motivated growing interest in LLMs’ mechanistic interpretability (MI). Activation representation engineering is one MI technique, with its core idea being to learn a set of concept vectors that can be used to detect and intervene in LLM internal states. Many concept vector learning algorithms including RepE
(
Zou et al. 2023
)
, CAA
(
Belinkov 2022
)
, linear probing and other variations
(
Zhao et al. 2024
;
Chu et al. 2024
;
Ousidhoum et al. 2021
)
have been proposed and widely discussed
(
Li et al. 2023
;
Tak et al. 2025
;
Huang and Wang 2025
)
. These methods essentially follow a similar process: given sets of positive and negative prompts that respectively present a desired information/function and their opposite as the dataset, the activation vector can be calculated by applying methods to learn the representation differences between prompt pairs within LLMs’ each layer.

Detection and intervention are two types of tasks for evaluating the effectiveness of learned vectors.
Reading includes locating emergent representations of the desired information in LLMs’ internals.
The detecting quality is usually evaluated by performance on the designed classification task, with related discussions including detecting LLMs’ hallucination
(
Ji et al. 2024
)
, deception
(
Goldowsky-Dill et al. 2025
)
, and fact-checking ability
(
Marks and Tegmark 2023
;
He et al. 2024
)
.
Intervention is related to activation steering
(
Turner et al. 2023a
)
, which injects learned vectors back into LLM internal representations during its forward pass at certain layer(s). Recent studies have shown this intervention can steer LLMs’ internal states and guide LLMs to generate desired responses
(
Dathathri et al. 2019
;
Subramani, Suresh, and Peters 2022
)
.
Related discussions on steering include many aspects like psychology
(
Tak et al. 2025
)
, safety
(
Ball, Kreuter, and Panickssery 2024
;
Xu et al. 2024
)
, interactive applications
(
Turner et al. 2023a
)
, unified framework
(
Bhalla et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2024
;
Im and Li 2025
)
.
The intervention quality is generally evaluated through designed metrics based on user experience, LLM-as-a-Judge score, or variations in output token logits score
(
Pres et al. 2024
;
Turner et al. 2023b
)
.
To better learn, detect, and steer the political states of LLMs, this work aims to apply these techniques to the political domain—where interpretability remains largely unexplored.

LLMs Architecture

LLMs response process can be intuitively viewed as applying a pre‐trained neural network
f
that maps the initial sentence (prompt) to a distribution over the collection of possible choices for the next token, then extends the sentence autoregressively to form the response.
We refer to
(
Duetting et al. 2024
;
Chatzi et al. 2024
)
for a formal description of this process

Autoregressive Token Generation
Given a prompt
p_{0}
which provides instructions for LLM generation, the tokenizer first encodes
p_{0}
into a model-readable token sequence
s_{0}
according to the LLM token vocabulary
\mathcal{V}
.
The LLM produces the next token for
s_{0}
by sampling a new token
\tau_{1}\sim f(s_{0})
and constructing
s_{1}=s_{0}\oplus\tau_{1}
(where
\oplus
denotes concatenation). It then repeats the process by sampling
\tau_{k}\sim f(s_{k})
and setting
s_{k}=s_{k-1}\oplus\tau_{k}
until a predefined stopping criterion is reached (e.g., the
k
-length limit, an end-of-sentence token). The tokenizer eventually decodes
s_{k}
into human-readable text based on
\mathcal{V}
as the LLMs response.

Layer-wise Forward Pass

The common architecture of
f
is a decoder‐only transformer pre‐trained on massive natural language datasets. Specifically,
f
consists of
n
layers for representation inference. Each layer
\ell\in\{1,\dots,n\}
shares the similar structure, including a multi‐head attention block
\mathrm{MHA}^{\ell}
and a feed‐forward network block
\mathrm{FFN}^{\ell}
.
Given
h_{s}^{\ell}
as the hidden representation of a token sequence
s
at layer
\ell
, the hidden representation at the next layer
h_{s}^{\ell+1}
is calculated as follows:

h_{s}^{\ell+1}=h_{s}^{\ell}+\mathrm{MHA}^{\ell}\bigl(h_{s}^{\ell}\bigr)+\mathrm{FFN}^{\ell}\!\bigl(h_{s}^{\ell}+\mathrm{MHA}^{\ell}(h_{s}^{\ell})\bigr).

(1)

The forward pass of
f(s)
includes passing these embeddings layer by layer, then transforming and normalising the last layer
h_{s}^{n}
back to the space with
\mathcal{V}
size, from which we obtain tokens sampling distribution over the next token.

LLMs Representation Engineering

LLMs representation engineering usually includes learning, detecting and intervening in the hidden states within the LLMs. Our objective is to learn political opinion vectors via datasets with contrastive political statements, and use the learned vectors for detecting and intervening in LLM political opinions.

Data Structure

To better describe the structure of our constructed data, we first introduce three terms: dimensions set(
Dim
), concepts set(
C
), and topics set(
T
).
The dimensions set refers to our four fine-grained dimension set—Economic, Diplomatic, Civil, and Society—and can be denoted as
\mathrm{Dim}\mathord{=}\{\mathrm{eco},\mathrm{dip},\mathrm{civil},\mathrm{soc}\}.

The discussion of concepts and topics takes place within each dimension
d\in\text{Dim}
. Specifically, concepts set is denoted as
C^{d}=\{C^{d}_{L},C^{d}_{R}\}
, which refers to the left-leaning and right-leaning definitions within
d
.
The topics set can be written as
T^{d}=\{\,T^{d}_{\text{t}}\}_{\text{t}=1}^{m}
, which refers to
m
themes related to dimension
d
.
For example, in the economic dimension (
d\mathord{=}\mathrm{eco}
), concept
C^{d}
can be economic equality (
C^{d=\mathrm{eco}}_{L}
) and market (
C^{d=\mathrm{eco}}_{R}
), and possible economic topics can be jobs (
T_{1}^{d=\mathrm{eco}}
), taxes (
T_{2}^{d=\mathrm{eco}}
), banking (
T_{3}^{d=\mathrm{eco}}
).

Our constructed dataset covers all dimensions and is denoted as
{\mathcal{D}=\{D^{\mathrm{eco}}\cup D^{\mathrm{dip}}\cup D^{\mathrm{civil}}\cup D^{\mathrm{soc}}\}}
.
Each constituent set
D^{d}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
contains both left-leaning statements and right-leaning statements within dimension
d
. Specifically, these statements combine the corresponding left-leaning concept
C^{d}_{\text{L}}
or right-leaning concept
C^{d}_{\text{R}}
with the given topic
T^{d}_{\text{t}}
to reflect different political learning statements on the given dimension and topic.

D^{d}=\{S^{d}_{\text{L,t}},S^{d}_{\text{R,t}}\}^{m}_{\text{t}=1},

(2)

S^{d}_{\text{L,t}}=C^{d}_{\text{L}}\odot T^{d}_{\text{t}},S^{d}_{\text{R,t}}=C^{d}_{\text{R}}\odot T^{d}_{\text{t}}.

(3)

Here
\odot
refers to the combination of the concept and topic. The implementation details are discussed in the experimental section.

Political Concept Vector Learning

Based on the representation linear hypothesis
(
Park, Choe, and Veitch 2023
;
Elhage et al. 2022
)
, which suggests that concepts are encoded linearly within model representations, many methods have been proposed to learn a concept vector
\vec{u}^{\,\ell}
that encodes the underlying LLMs concept information. CAA
(
Belinkov 2022
)
, RepE
(
Vogel 2024
;
Zou et al. 2023
)
and Linear Probing
(
Ousidhoum et al. 2021
)
are three techniques used in this paper for political concept vector learning.
To avoid concept confounds, we implement these methods to learn the concept vector
\vec{u}^{\ell}
in different dimensions. Our insight is that LLM representations of data from different dimensions are disentangled (no overlap across dimensions). The disentangled concept vectors thus can be learned from the disentangled representations. The method details are as below.

Both CAA and RepE learn concept vectors through pairs of contrastive statements.
For each contrastive pair
(S_{L},S_{R})
on topic
T^{d}_{t}
of dimension
d
, we denote its corresponding left-leaning statement as
S_{L}
and the right-leaning statement as
S_{R}
, where we assign
S_{L}:=S^{d}_{L,t}
and
S_{R}:=S^{d}_{R,t}
.
Intuitively, the hidden representations difference between these two
\text{Diff}(h^{\ell}_{S_{\text{L}}},h^{\ell}_{S_{\text{R}}})

captures how the LLM distinguishes the ‘left’ and ‘right’ differences at
\ell
-th layer.

CAA calculates the embeddings difference for each contrastive pair
(S_{\text{L}},\,S_{\text{R}})
, and takes the weighted mean of these differences as concept vector
\vec{u}^{\ell}
, where

\vec{u}^{\,\ell}\;=\;\sum_{S_{L}\in\mathcal{D}^{d}}\frac{h_{S_{L}}^{\,\ell}}{|\mathcal{D}^{d}|}\;-\;\sum_{S_{R}\in\mathcal{D}^{d}}\frac{h_{S_{R}}^{\,\ell}}{|\mathcal{D}^{d}|}.

(4)

RepE takes the first principal component of the dimension-reduced hidden representations across all contrastive pairs as
\vec{u}^{\ell}
. Dimensionality reduction can be either PCA-based or UMAP-based. Here we apply PCA and describe the process as: calculating representation differences between left-leaning and right-leaning pairs and gathering them into the matrix
\mathcal{H}
.
According to
(
Wu et al. 2025
)
, the first principal component
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{PCA}}
of
\mathcal{H}
(i.e., the unit vector that captures the largest variance along that direction) is denoted as
\vec{u}^{\,\ell}
. Unless otherwise specified, we set the default positive direction of
\vec{u}^{\ell}
to present lean left concept
C_{L}
, and its opposite as lean right concept
C_{R}
.

Unlike the above two methods, which require vectors to be learned from contrastive pairs,
linear probing learns the
\vec{u}^{\,\ell}
through supervised learning.
For every statement
S\in\mathcal{D}^{d}
, its representation
h_{S}^{\ell}
is set as the input, and its political leaning (left/right) is set as the label
\mathcal{Y}_{S}
. We apply the logistic regression model with L2 regularization as the classifier, as it is widely used and considered robust in many tasks
(
Kantamneni et al. 2025
)
.
The normalized weight
w_{c}^{\ell}
is considered as the corresponding concept vector.

\text{LogReg}(h_{S}^{\ell})=\bigl(1+\exp(-{\mathbf{w_{c}^{\ell}}}^{\top}h_{S}^{\ell})\bigr)^{-1},

(5)

\min_{\mathbf{w}^{\ell}_{\mathrm{c}}}\{\frac{1}{|\mathbf{h}^{\ell}|}\sum_{h_{S}^{\ell}\in\mathbf{h}^{\ell}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}\left(\mathcal{Y}_{S},\mathrm{LogReg}(h^{\ell}_{S})\right)\}.

(6)

Detection and Intervention

The learned vector
\vec{u}^{\,\ell}
can be used to detect and to intervene in the LLM’s internal political opinion. Detection means using
\vec{u}^{\,\ell}
to detect the model’s internal stance.
A straightforward example is to input a given sentence and use
\vec{u}^{\,\ell}
to predict how left-leaning or right-leaning LLMs regard it at
\ell
-th layer.
The predictions for RepE and CAA are based on the direction of the dot product between the test data and the learned concept vector. Linear probing predicts the class of test data by applying the sigmoid function to the linear combination (i.e., the dot product) of the test data and the learned weights, plus a bias term.

The learned vector can be applied to intervene in LLMs’ internals and lead LLMs’ behaviour toward a left- or right-leaning direction.
Technically, they add the vector(s) to the original representations at the certain layer(s) and let the forward pass proceed to obtain the steered output. The vector to be added can be scaled by a strength coefficient
\alpha
, which it determines how strongly we push the LLM toward the target political concept. The intervened representation at the
\ell
-th layer is denoted as
h^{\ell}_{\text{Intervene}}\;=\;h^{\ell}_{\text{s}}+\alpha\,\vec{u}^{\,\ell}
.

Disentangled Concept Vector Learning

CAA, RepE and Linear Probing are three methods we used for concept vector learning.
Following
(
Zou et al. 2023
;
Zhao et al. 2024
)
, we use the last token embedding in each sequence, which integrates information from all preceding tokens.
Since our defined dimensions are non-overlapping, we assume the training data embeddings under different dimensions are disentangled. By learning concept vectors across these distinct dimensions, we obtain disentangled representations that address the concept confounds. Specifically, for each layer of the LLMs, we generate eight fine-grained concept vectors (covering four dimensions with two opposing left-right-leaning) based on each layer’s training data embeddings.
We apply cosine similarity to measure the correlation among the eight concept vectors at each layer. The concept vectors are disentangled if they show high correlations within the same dimension and weak correlations across different dimensions.
The effectiveness of the learned political concept vectors is assessed via LLM detection and intervention tasks.

Last, we extended the analysis to multiple token generation and discussed how the interventions ultimatly affect LLMs’ response.

RQ1: Disentangle Political Concept Confounds

We first compare the representation distributions of different political concepts within LLMs layers to confirm that this hidden representation can be used to learn corresponding concept vectors. We then perform correlation analysis on the learned concept vectors to show the concept confounds phenomenon and demonstrate the effectiveness of disentanglement in our defined fine-grained lens.

Hidden Representations Comparison

Figure
5
visualises embedding comparisons of economic lean left and lean right statements across different layers in Llama and Mistral models. Overall, embeddings from different sides show limited differences in the early layers (with their representations almost overlapping), but as representations are passed layer by layer, their distributions become increasingly differentiated. The distinctions between different concepts become particularly pronounced after the middle layers. Similar phenomena are observed across other model families. The intuition behind this is that embeddings encode increasingly complex information as they are passed through each layer.
We further compared LLMs from the same model family but with different sizes.
The results show that the larger LLM (Llama-3-8B) reveal clearer distinctions than the smaller one (Llama-3-3B). It aligns with the previous discussion in LLMs’ linear structure
(
Marks and Tegmark 2023
)
.
These findings confirm that internal representations in LLMs encode different political concepts, therefore corresponding concept vectors can be learned from these representations.

Correlation Analysis

We use three introduced techniques (CAA, RepE, Linear Probing) to learn fine-grained political concept vectors via LLMs’ internal representations.
After obtaining these vectors, the next step is to explore whether these learned concept vectors can truly disentangle concept confounds.
We answer this via the correlation analysis among vectors.
Figure

From the results, concept vectors exhibit cross-dimensional correlations in the early layers: (1) Some show consistent correlations, like positive correlations between left-leaning opinions of ‘society-tradition’ and ‘civil-authority’. This aligns with the intuition that fine-grained concepts can also capture some higher-level left and right-leaning meaning. (2) However, there also emerge left and right concept confounds.
As RepE vectors correlations(
\ell=8

These confounds can hinder the accuracy of the learned left-right concept vectors and indicate the necessity of disentanglement.

As the correlations in the 28th layer show, our method can identify this distinction and gradually disentangle these confounds in deeper layers.
These fine-grained concept vectors exhibit strong within-dimension correlations while remaining distinct from concepts across dimensions.
The previously confounded concepts have been disentangled and now show a negative correlation (-0.17).
Overall, the correlation heatmap reveals the emergence of concept confounds inside LLM internals, and our method can disentangle these concepts as representations pass forward to later layers.

RQ2: Detection Ability of Political Concept Vectors

We designed the detection task for testing: it evaluates the vectors’ ability to detect relevant concepts of test data from their internal representations. Both the out-of-distribution tests and ablation studies validate the robustness and effectiveness of the political concept vectors we learned.

RQ3: Intervention Ability on LLMs

The above results demonstrate the effectiveness of our learned political concept vectors on the detection task.
We now investigate whether these vectors can be used to intervene in LLMs, thereby influencing the models in reverse.
Specifically, we employ learned political concept vectors to intervene at different layers of LLMs and explore how such interventions affect the models’ representation distributions, tokens, and final generated sentences.

Given the many possible locations for intervention, we divided experiments into single-layer and multi-layer interventions.
In the single-layer setting, we compare the representation distribution variations after intervention with the original representations to identify the range of intervention sites that most effectively influence the LLMs’ hidden states. We then conduct multi-layer interventions within this effective range to study how changes in these representations ultimately alter the LLMs’ token generation and final outputs.

Single Layer Intervention

Given a
n

i
and visualize representations at layer
j
(
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
) to explore the distribution shift. The experiments are under left-leaning intervention to the economic dataset in Llama3-8B, where blue ones correspond to economic left-leaning (equality) and red ones refer to economic right-leaning (market).

Fixed Visualization Layer
We fix a particular layer
j
for visualization, and investigate how interventions at different preceding layers
i

As Figure

Fixed Intervention Layer

Based on the finding that interventions at intermediate layers are most effective, we further fixed one layer

The results show that when we introduce a left-leaning intervention, the distributions of both left and right data shift. As representations forward through layers, the post-intervention left and right distributions gradually move toward the original left representation distribution (blue area). This demonstrates that the added intervention changes the internal representations of LLMs in the direction corresponding to the target concept.

Multi-Layer Intervention

After comparing the effects of single-layer interventions, we further explore the results of applying interventions across multiple layers.
The targeting intervention layers are from 15 to 25.
We first analyse how these interventions steer the LLM’s next-token generation, then examine their influence on LLM’s response (i.e., multiple tokens generation).

Next Token Intervention

To explore the intervention’s impact on the next token prediction, LogitLens is used to unembed the representation and show the tokens most likely to be predicted from that representation.
We extracted the top five most likely tokens at each layer as the LLM’s internal ‘intention’ on the given statement, and compared their variations. Figure
10
shows the token candidates when asking LLMs how they think about the statement ‘taxes should be increased on the rich to provide for the poor’ in terms of positive or negative social impact.

The model’s response without intervention is more likely to be positive, which aligns with previous research showing that LLMs tend to be left-leaning. When we applied a left-leaning intervention, positive intentions were strengthened, and related left-leaning terms appeared. When applying a right-leaning intervention, we found that the LLMs’ intentions regarding the given statement were steered toward the negative. However, these intentions were overridden in the last two layers, changing from ‘negative’ to ‘subject’. The ‘subject’ token can lead the model to generate output like ‘This is a subjective question, and I can’t provide an answer’.

Chen et al. 2024
observed a similar phenomenon when asking LLMs to predict sensitive features (e.g., gender). They refer to this as guardrail behaviour, which denotes the inconsistency between an LLM’s internal intention and its final output. We also found similar guardrail behaviour in Llama3-8B, where the model tends to be ‘neutral’ when asked for political opinions.
Through interventions, we found that if the input’s political leaning aligns with the intervention vector, the corresponding political tokens appear; conversely, when the intervention contradicts that opinion, the word ‘neutral’ is strengthened.

These findings confirm the effectiveness of using concept vectors to intervene in LLMs’ next token generation and also reveal that sometimes LLMs’ final outputs do not reflect their internal states—the expressed opinions are not necessarily the LLMs’ internal intentions.
Quantifying metrics to measure the inconsistency between the two and implementing values alignment techniques will be very meaningful in future work.

We further discuss the impact of the intervention on generating the response (i.e., multiple next tokens). The below boxes compare responses before and after RepE-based intervention of Mistral-7B to the same question (discussion on wealth inheritance). The responses are under three conditions—no intervention, left-leaning intervention, and right-leaning intervention.

Overall, the results align with the intuition that, under the left intervention, the answer shifts toward discussing the potential negative impact of inheritance, as an economically left-leaning opinion is equality-based and more in favor of redistributing property.
Adding the right-leaning intervention makes the answer supportive of inheritance, since an economically right-leaning opinion is market-oriented and prefers wealth accumulation.

Conclusion

Most work on LLMs politics is based on a single-axis left-right political spectrum, and there is a lack of discussion of the internal mechanisms by which political opinions are formed within LLMs.
Our work bridges this gap by introducing a fine-grained political representation learning framework to learn, interpret, and steer the internal states of LLMs.

Specifically, we extend the traditional left-right axis to a four-dimensional political space, and learn the corresponding left and right concept vectors along each dimension and on each layer within LLMs. Our experiments demonstrate that relying solely on the left-right spectrum can result in confounding between left-leaning and right-leaning concepts, whereas the fine-grained concept vectors learned in our framework enable the model to distinguish these subtle differences. Political opinion detection tasks show that our disentangled vectors carry meaningful semantic information and can be used to probe the political information encoded within LLMs. Moreover, these fine-grained vectors exhibit better generalization capabilities on out-of-distribution datasets compared to directly training left-right concept vectors.
We also use the learned vectors to intervene in the internal states of LLMs, with results indicating that the internal distributions shift toward the semantics represented by the concept vectors. This internal distributional shift can ultimately steer the LLMs to generate responses aligned with the political concept we used to intervene.

These findings suggest that we can capture political concepts and differentiate their subtle differences in LLMs’ internals. The captured concept vectors can be further used in detecting and intervening in LLMs’ hidden states.
Such studies can provide further insights and technical tools for more transparent and controllable auditing in AI governance.
Future work could involve defining more precise evaluation metrics to measure political bias in LLMs, comparing the political preferences of LLMs developed in different countries, and exploring the responses of applying multiple concept vectors simultaneously to intervene in LLM internals.
