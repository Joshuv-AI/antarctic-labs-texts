Development and usability evaluation of an audio-based cognitive behavioral therapy mobile app for depression in hemodialysis patients
Authors: Fariborz Narimanpour, Habibollah Pirnejad, Khadijeh Makhdoomi, Haedeh Feizipour, Zahra Niazkhani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHemodialysis patients (HDPs) often experience depression, which negatively impacts their treatment adherence and quality of life. Drug therapy for depression in HDPs is often limited due to its effectiveness and drug safety concerns. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) has emerged as a promising alternative. However, its delivery is hampered by various challenges, especially in this vulnerable population. We aimed to develop an innovative mobile application to overcome some of the challenges in delivering CBT for depression and evaluate its perceived usability among HDPs.Materials and methodsThis study was conducted in a nephrology department in a developing country between 2022 and 2023. Relevant topics were adapted into a CBT framework tailored to hemodialysis care and cultural context by a multidisciplinary expert panel including of a senior nephrologist with decades of clinical experience and a clinical psychologist before conversion into audio format for the application. Usability was assessed with 30 hemodialysis patients aged ≥ 18 years, receiving treatment for ≥ 3 months, with mild to moderate depression (Beck Depression Inventory short form (BDI-SF) score 5–15), owning Android smartphones, and possessing basic reading and writing literacy. This study focused on the development and usability evaluation of the application; clinical effectiveness was not assessed. Perceived usability was assessed using the Questionnaire for User Interface Satisfaction (QUIS).ResultsThe hemodialysis-tailored CBT program comprised seven sessions aimed at addressing stress and depression in HDPs through skills such as goal setting, problem solving, and coping with negative emotions using language carefully developed and validated by clinical experts familiar with the needs and literacy levels of this population. The content was transformed into 26 audio episodes, each approximately 15 min long, delivered via a user-friendly mobile app featuring educational CBT content, therapist communication, peer support, and reporting functions. Thirty HDPs participated in the usability evaluation; they were primarily aged 50 to 65 years, had predominantly elementary-level education, and more than one year of hemodialysis experience. They expressed high satisfaction, with an average usability score of 7.87 out of 9.ConclusionThe application achieved high usability scores among HDPs, supporting its feasibility and perceived usability as an audio-CBT delivery platform. These findings lay the groundwork for future studies to assess its clinical efficacy, cost-effectiveness, and role in providing psychological support in real-world care settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03732-7.Keywords: Dialysis, Depression, Cognitive behavioral therapy, CBT, Mobile health app, mHealth

Introduction

Despite its life-saving role in end-stage renal disease (ESRD), hemodialysis is associated with a range of physical and psychological consequences that can negatively impact overall quality of life [1]. Depression is one of the most common and serious psychological complications encountered by this vulnerable patient population. Based on self-reported depressive symptom screening using standardized scales such as the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) and Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), approximately 40% of hemodialysis patients (HDPs) globally and more than 60% in Iran report significant depressive symptoms [2, 3]. While these figures reflect symptom prevalence rather than formal diagnoses based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) published by the American Psychiatric Association, they nonetheless highlight an urgent need to address depression’s substantial impact on patient well-being, treatment adherence, clinical outcomes, and overall financial burden [4–6].

Depression imposes a significant burden on HDPs their caregivers, and healthcare providers. However, its treatment in HDPs is not straightforward. The evidence base for the efficacy of pharmacological interventions, such as antidepressants is limited in this population and raises concerns regarding potential drug interactions and adverse effects [7]. These concerns are further compounded by the challenges related to access and adherence. As a consequence, pharmacological treatments are not recommended for mild depression and should be used cautiously, with close monitoring, for moderate to severe depression in HDPs [8]. These complexities highlight the critical necessity for the development of alternative treatment modalities designed to address the distinctive circumstances and vulnerabilities of HDPs.

Psychological interventions, notably cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), have shown promising results in reducing depressive symptoms and improving quality of life in HDPs [9, 10]. CBT is a psychotherapeutic approach that aims to modify maladaptive cognitions and behaviors underlying depression [11]. Nevertheless, the implementation of CBT for this population is hindered by several obstacles, including time constraints, resource limitations, motivational barriers, transportation challenges, and societal stigma [12–14]. These challenges are further compounded by treatment-related factors such as physical fatigue, prolonged dialysis sessions, and rigid scheduling that limit patients’ energy and availability for in-person therapy [13–15]. Such factors may impede both initial engagement with therapy and the subsequent maintenance of treatment. Furthermore, the scarcity of mental health professionals, even in resource-rich settings, exacerbates the challenge of meeting the psychotherapeutic needs of this patient population [16]. Therefore, there is a pressing need to develop and evaluate innovative and feasible approaches for delivering CBT to HDPs with depression.

Previous studies have assessed the feasibility and efficacy of internet based CBT (iCBT) as a promising approach to overcome some of the abovementioned barriers [15, 17, 18]. Yet, iCBT delivered via desktop or laptop modules often faces usability challenges due to limited patient computer literacy, structured content formats, and less flexible interaction modes. Mobile applications, by contrast, offer intuitive touch interfaces, push notifications, and/or integrated audio features that are more user-friendly, flexible, and accessible [19], potentially overcoming many barriers that limit iCBT while preserving its core therapeutic principles [20–22]. These applications address key challenges of traditional CBT, such as commuting, scheduling constraints, and stigma [23, 24], while also offering greater affordability, interactivity, and real-time feedback [25]. For HDPs, who often have visual or physical impairments or limited literacy, audio-based mobile CBT can further reduce cognitive and technical demands, providing a more engaging and accessible therapeutic experience [26–28].

The design and delivery of mobile CBT can also be understood through established behavior change frameworks. The Behavioral Intervention Technology (BIT) model, as proposed by Mohr et al., defines both the conceptual and technological architecture of digital health interventions by clarifying the “why” (treatment goals), “what” (intervention components), and “how” (behavior change strategies and technical delivery) [29]. Complementing this, the Person-Based Approach (PBA) emphasizes the importance of tailoring digital interventions to the psychosocial context, needs, and perspectives of the target users, ensuring that behavior change techniques are acceptable, engaging, and feasible in real-world use [30].

For HDPs, who often experience treatment-related fatigue, restricted schedules, and fluctuating physical or cognitive energy, these frameworks provide a structure for designing flexible, low-burden, and context-sensitive interventions. In this context, the BIT model helps specify how CBT components can be adapted to short, modular audio sessions that fit within or around dialysis schedules, while the PBA ensures that these adaptations remain aligned with HDP’s motivational and usability needs. Accordingly, this study aimed to design, develop, and evaluate the perceived usability of an audio-based mobile CBT application for HDPs experiencing depressive symptoms. This usability study provides practical design insights and principles that can support future implementation and scaling of such interventions.

Materials and methods

Study setting

This study was conducted in the nephrology department of Urmia University of Medical Sciences in close collaboration with a dialysis center in a secondary care hospital in Iran from July 2022 to September 2023. It included three phases of content production on the basis of requirements in the hemodialysis care context, design and development, and usability assessment of a smartphone-based application for self-management of depression in HDPs. This application was designed, developed, and refined to incorporate the culturally sensitive, tailor-made content produced specifically for CBT in the HDP population, using the literature and input from clinical domain experts (i.e., nephrologist and clinical psychologist). HDPs then rated its usability. The overall flow of the study is summarized below (Fig. 1).

Overview of the study design

Participants and recruitment process

In this study, the content was iteratively reviewed and refined by a multidisciplinary expert panel comprising a senior nephrologist with over 35 years of clinical and hemodialysis care experience, two experienced hemodialysis nurses, a practicing clinical psychologist, two medical informatics specialists with clinical backgrounds, and a digital health innovation developer. This panel engaged in multiple rounds of in-depth adaptations to ensure the clinical relevance, and practical feasibility of the intervention content. Their combined expertise and close involvement with the target population enhanced the rigor and applicability of the content development process. Cultural adaptation was explicitly addressed by modifying language, examples, metaphors, and explanations to align with the local context, literacy levels, and values of HDPs. This process did not involve formal qualitative data collection or analysis. And HDPs were not directly involved mainly due to potential burden of the research on them and limited research timeframe.

For usability evaluation purposes, we invited HDPs from a hemodialysis unit who met the following criteria: age ≥ 18 years, hemodialysis treatment for ≥ 3 months, mild to moderate depression (as measured by the Beck Depression Inventory short form (BDI-SF) [11] with validated cutoffs of 5–9 = mild, 10–15 = moderate, prior Android smartphone ownership (to reduce novelty bias), reading and writing literacy, and informed consent to participate in the study. We excluded patients with any psychiatric disorder other than mild or moderate depression, mental retardation, cognitive, hearing, vision, or severe physical impairment, inability to use the app or those with concurrent severe stressful events during the study. We used convenience sampling to enroll the participants. Eligible patients were approached during their dialysis sessions.

Development of a hemodialysis-specific CBT app

Phase 1: content production on the basis of requirements in the context of hemodialysis care

The initial phase started with the extraction and analysis of user requirements, as well as requirements related to architecture and security issues for the application via a literature review and expert sessions.

Critically, the intervention’s design was grounded in the CBT framework of Hudson et al.’s ‘Improving Distress in Dialysis (iDiD)’ program [27], which applies core CBT principles (e.g., cognitive restructuring and behavioral activation) to address the unique psychological needs of dialysis patients. Guided by this evidence-based model, we adapted its seven-session CBT framework. This framework provides the theoretical foundation for session structure, focusing on: identifying/challenging negative automatic thoughts (cognitive theory), developing coping strategies (self-regulation theory), and enhancing problem-solving skills (social problem-solving theory).

The content for each session was meticulously extracted from both international and national literature and online educational resources (Supplementary material I), ensuring alignment with iDiD’s established mechanisms of change. Expert panel validation occurred through three rounds of structured review. Panelists evaluated topics against three criteria: (1) clinical accuracy (alignment with international CKD depression guidelines) (2), cultural appropriateness for Iranian HDPs, and (3) suitability for the health literacy levels of this population, based on their extensive field experience working with these patients. Topics required unanimous approval after discussion of discrepancies.

Then, they were prepared as use scenarios for each session and how to be presented in the app. This content was subsequently scripted into an audio format and refined post review by the expert panel. The finalized audio content was produced via online text-to-speech platforms and audio editing software and was completed with background music. The audio intervention retained Hudson et al.’s 7-session sequence and theoretical foundation (i.e., the vicious cycle model linking triggers, emotions, cognitions, behaviors, and physiology) [27]. Each session’s objectives directly correspond to the original framework. Table 1 provides a mapping of each adapted session to Hudson’s original objectives and techniques.

Phase 2: data model and technical development

In the second phase, the app’s conceptual, logical, and physical models were defined to guide the development of a user-friendly interface tailored to health-related needs. The initial design and prototype were critically reviewed by an expert panel, and their feedback was incorporated to refine the final structure. Development was carried out using Appeto1, a no-code platform built on the Flutter framework that supports secure native application generation. Appeto includes a content management system (CMS) with adjustable role-based permissions and secure user authentication. While its encryption protocols are not publicly disclosed, the platform complies with security standards required by major app distribution markets, ensuring secure data handling and transmission. Its proven use in managing large-scale financial transactions further supports the reliability and robustness of its infrastructure.

Phase 3: usability evaluation

We employed the validated Questionnaire for User Interface Satisfaction (QUIS) [28] exclusively to assess usability issues and interface acceptability, given the vulnerability of the study population. The QUIS questionnaire, a widely used tool, assesses various dimensions of usability and user satisfaction. These dimensions include screen layout, terminology, learning ease, system capabilities, and overall satisfaction. It consists of 27 items rated on a 10-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (very dissatisfied) to 9 (very satisfied) with demonstrated reliability and construct validity across healthcare applications. It is noteworthy that this instrument does not evaluate any clinical outcomes.

The participants were first trained on how to use the app during a 15-minute face-to-face tutorial. Then, they instructed to use it by themselves for ≥ 30 min/day over 7 consecutive days, completing all 7 CBT sessions before any assessment. The intervention comprised 26 audio episodes (total duration: ~5 h), structured into 7 sessions. During the pilot and feasibility phase, adjustments were made to the duration of intervention delivery to reduce patient burden and increase accessibility. While no back-end analytics were collected in this initial usability study, adherence was monitored through self-reported daily usage, which was considered sufficient for this pilot phase. Upon completion, the participants were invited to complete a researcher-administered, paper-based QUIS during their routine dialysis session. Usage adherence was verified via self-report during QUIS administration. The data were then analyzed via descriptive statistics of the mean, standard deviation, and median to evaluate the overall usability of the app.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Urmia University of Medical Sciences prior to commencement (ethics approval number: IR.UMSU.REC.1402.001) and followed the ethical principles of informed consent, confidentiality, and intellectual property rights. The participants were fully informed about the purpose and procedure of the study, the potential benefits and risks of using the app, and their right to withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences. They signed a written informed consent form before participating in the study. Both ethical approval and informed consent procedures were rigorously followed to ensure adherence to ethical standards and the protection of participants’ rights.

Results

The results are presented according to the three phases of context-specific content production, the physical design of the application, and its usability evaluation.

Requirement analysis and hemodialysis-specific content production

The specific CBT topics for HDPs, which were approved by the expert panel, encompassed seven sessions focused on kidney failure, dialysis, and depression management in health literacy-friendly language (see Sect. 2.3.1). The content included education on the sources of stress in ESRD patients, addressing distress, confronting negative emotions, and coping with unhelpful thoughts in the plain Persian language, which is understandable to low literacy patients. Additionally, it covered goal setting, problem solving, managing difficult social relationships, and preparing for the future, thereby offering comprehensive support for both psychological and physical challenges (Supplementary material II). The approved content was then converted into audio files with background music and organized into 26 episodes, each lasting approximately 15 min to avoid overburdening patients.

Design of the application

In the second phase of the study, we designed the app on the basis of conceptual, logical, and physical models including the audio scripts developed in the first phase. This phase focused on capturing the types of data, their attributes and relationships, and the concepts/rules to consider when navigating the application.

Rationale for the application’s name

This application was titled “Delodializ” to convey empathy and care for HDPs. The name combines “del,” meaning heart in Persian, symbolizing empathy, with “dialysis”, emphasizing the commitment to address the emotional and psychological needs of these patients. This name highlights the application’s purpose of providing empathetic support and connecting with patients’ struggles and experiences.

After the application was assessed by the expert panel, they provided feedback and suggestions for improvement. As a result, cheerful and warm colors, emoticons, and positive images were integrated to foster a positive mood for HDPs. Supplementary material III provides screenshots from the developed application.

Discussion

This study aimed to develop and evaluate the perceived usability of an audio-based CBT mobile application for HDPs with depression. We intended to explore whether such a mobile app design could potentially help bridge existing psychosocial barriers by increasing accessibility to psychological support. The participants in our study were highly satisfied with the app, which was codesigned with clinicians, and rated it as both highly useful and usable. While efficacy testing is essential, our usability validation (mean QUIS = 7.87/9) in this hard-to-reach population provides a critical foundation for future studies.

Previous studies have shown that mobile health apps can provide accessible, convenient, and cost-effective solutions for delivering psychological interventions to various populations [23, 31, 32]. Unlike conventional internet-based iCBT, mobile applications leverage intuitive touch-based interfaces and increasingly advanced smartphone capabilities. These features, particularly in low-income and developing regions, have the potential to substantially enhance the acceptability and accessibility of CBTs. However, few studies have explored the use of mobile health apps for delivering interventions specifically for HDPs [74] [33, 34], and none have used audio content as the primary mode of delivery. Delodializ’s audio-centric design specifically circumvents typing/navigation demands, making CBT accessible to low-literacy populations – a critical advantage in resource-limited settings.

In this study, we developed an innovative audio-based CBT with background music, offering a convenient format for HDPs, who often face challenges such as substantial disease burden, limited mobility, low literacy, or visual impairments. Our findings on usability and acceptability support the feasibility of this modality for delivering CBT content to HDPs with depression. Our audio-driven interface, requiring merely play/pause and swipe gestures, perceived highly usable among our patients who had mostly limited formal education. This usability findings indicates that mobile audio delivery has the potential to reduce typing or navigation demands that can limit iCBT adoption in patients with low computer literacy. This aligns with previous studies have highlighted the value of audio content as an educational tool in healthcare and mental health, particularly among individuals with lower socioeconomic status [35, 36]. Furthermore, music has been shown to positively impact mood, relaxation, pain perception, blood pressure, and the immune system [37–39]. To our knowledge, no prior studies have utilized audio content with music to deliver psychological interventions for HDPs. Unlike text-heavy iCBT platforms that require typing, reading, or complex navigation on a computer, Delodializ uses audio-centric content with simple gesture-based interactions (play/pause/swipe). Our design reduces technical and reading literacy demands, leverages the higher smartphone ownership among HDPs compared to laptops or computers, and enables use during dialysis sessions, offering practical advantages in both accessibility and usability.

However, it is important to note that audio content may not be suitable for individuals with hearing or cognitive impairments, and it may not fully meet the social support or interaction needs of HDPs with other patients or therapists [40, 41]. Therefore, this audio content should be viewed as a complementary tool rather than a substitute for professional help. It should be supplemented with other methods that facilitate interaction and social communication, such as video content, group meetings, and online forums. Future studies should explore additional factors that may influence the use and acceptance of audio content with music among HDPs, including music preferences and complex individual issues that may require more personalized or specialized interventions.

The development of innovative digital health solutions is a resource-intensive task. Designing a user-friendly and engaging application, especially in resource-poor settings, requires navigating limitations in time, budget, and the professional expertise needed for technical skills. This challenge can be addressed by utilizing accessible and affordable online application design platforms that do not require coding skills. No-code platforms assist in developing health IT applications by offering prebuilt templates, components, features, and tools [42]. We adopted a design approach that considered user needs and preferences on the basis of previous studies and expert panel feedback. This approach was informed by previous studies on iCBT interventions for HDPs (for example, see [16–18, 43]) and mobile health apps designed to deliver CBT interventions for other populations [44–48]. As a result, the designed application incorporates various features and functions to increase its effectiveness and improve the user experience and interaction [49–51]. After the introduction module, the main menu of the application presents a modular structure, categorized into sections such as the user account, Delodializ, support and empathy, report, and favorites, ensuring clear and easy navigation similar to other mobile health applications [46, 52]. For example, the user account section prioritizes security and privacy while allowing for personalized user profile settings, inspired by features found in other mobile health applications [53, 54].

The educational section of Delodializ was designed to align with prior studies that support psychoeducational interventions in managing depression (e.g [9, 19, 55–57]). The support and empathy components include modules such as therapist communication and peer support groups, while also provide access to internationally validated resources in the local language. These features were informed by research indicating that social support and user-therapist interaction may help reduce depressive symptoms and negative thoughts [58, 59] and can improve treatment adherence among HDPs [49, 50, 60, 61]. Similarly, evidence suggests that increasing HDPs’ awareness and knowledge of their condition may enhance self-management and mental well-being [62, 63]. While our application incorporated such evidence-based design principles, particularly culturally and linguistically tailored educational content, we did not evaluate its clinical effectiveness. Future studies are needed to determine whether culturally tailored education modules like ours can improve self-management and mental health outcomes in such vulnerable patient population.

Our evaluation of usability and user satisfaction showed that the designed features, including terminology, system capabilities, and attractive multimedia content, were well-received. While qualitative feedback from participants was limited in number, it was largely positive and aligned with our objective to provide a convenient platform for CBT delivery. These findings indicate the app’s potential as a user-friendly tool for improving access to CBT content, offering tailored audio sessions and interactive elements that extend beyond features of many existing CBT-based apps for patient populations [32, 47, 64–66]. That said, it is important to distinguish these usability results from therapeutic efficacy. Depression management outcomes require dedicated trials using validated clinical measures (e.g., BDI scores, session completion data). This pilot phase intentionally focused on usability rather than clinical outcomes, prioritizing foundational refinements in this vulnerable patient group.

The implementation of CBT interventions, whether traditional or internet-based, for HDPs, who are mostly elderly, presents several main challenges due to their physical limitations, cognitive decline, technological literacy issues, time constraints, and need for culturally sensitive content [67]. Addressing these challenges requires careful consideration also during the design phase. For example, evidence supports the need to adapt CBT content to the needs and values of different cultures to improve outcomes and sustain patient retention in therapy [68–70]. One of the main challenges in our study was the lack of culturally appropriate and high-quality CBT content that could effectively cater to the needs and preferences of HDPs in the study setting. To address this gap, we adopted a systematic and collaborative approach that involved conducting an integrative literature review and consulting with clinical experts. In adapting the content culturally, we drew inspiration from studies highlighting the significant influence of cultural factors on beliefs, behaviors, and health outcomes among patients undergoing CBT (see, for example [71, 72]. Our content includes a culture-specific section that addresses the impact of Iranian culture on the management of hemodialysis and depression, enhancing the relevance of CBT for the Iranian HDP population.

Delodializ incorporates core CBT principles into its design to enhance user engagement and ease of use. The interactive feedback system, including BDI-SF reporting and therapist messaging, encourages active participation by providing clear, immediate responses that help users track their progress. Peer forums create a low-pressure environment for users to practice social interactions, supporting comfort and confidence in using the app. Voice sessions guide users through structured exercises targeting common dialysis-related thoughts, presented in an accessible audio format. These design choices prioritize simplicity and user interaction, addressing usability needs specific to this patient population.

Limitations

Despite several innovations, this study has limitations to consider when interpreting the results. Formal content validation methods such as the Content Validity Index (CVI) or structured consensus techniques (e.g., Delphi method) were not employed due to limited access to specialized experts in our resource-constrained setting. Instead, an iterative consensus approach with a multidisciplinary expert panel was used. Future studies in better-resourced contexts should incorporate standardized quantitative validation methods to enhance rigor.

Next, while the intervention was developed based on previous evidence and expert input, it lacked a participatory design approach involving patients from the initial stages. Instead, patient feedback was obtained only after development. Participatory design has been shown to improve user satisfaction, acceptance, usability, and effectiveness [73]. The absence of patient involvement was primarily due to time constraints and concerns about burdening chronic patients. Consequently, we relied on our expert panel’s extensive expertise in content development, cultural tailoring, and literacy considerations, factors that should be kept in mind when interpreting our results.

The absence of negative responses in the QUIS warrants careful consideration. Although the anonymous, paper-based administration helped reduce social desirability bias, cultural norms emphasizing politeness and respect—particularly in Persian culture and among vulnerable patient populations toward their care providers—may have discouraged critical feedback. Nevertheless, the intervention’s simple audio format, requiring minimal interaction (play, pause, swipe), likely contributed to a genuinely positive usability experience. Future studies should consider involving independent third-party evaluators to further mitigate potential response bias.

Additionally, the use of convenience sampling and a small sample size limits generalizability, although the sample size is sufficient for this usability pilot. The short follow-up period and reliance on self-report measures further constrain the findings. Future research should employ more comprehensive usability assessments, such as “Think Aloud” protocols or interviews.

Moreover, owing to the study’s focus on development and usability assessment, we did not evaluate clinical outcomes, such as depression levels and participants’ quality of life, or the long-term cost-effectiveness of the intervention. While QUIS validated usability, clinical efficacy trials are needed to validate therapeutic benefits and future research should address these clinical and economic outcomes with larger studies and longer follow-up periods. Also, in future iterations, usage analytics (e.g., session completion rates, time logs) should be included for objective adherence tracking. Expanding the geographic scope is also necessary to assess the intervention’s suitability for scaling up.

Furthermore, while Delodializ successfully delivered core CBT components through audio sessions, we acknowledge limitations in therapeutic tooling. The absence of progress visualization, automated reminders for skills practice, and personalized assignment tracking represents a gap against established CBT delivery standards. Future iterations should consider implementing personalized dashboards suitable for low-literacy patients, visualizing mood trends against session milestones, push-notification reminders for skills rehearsal and BDI-SF assessments, therapist portals showing patient usage patterns and completion rates and data export functionality for clinical outcome analysis. These features can be critical for maintaining therapeutic momentum and reinforcing skill acquisition through structured practice between sessions.

Conclusion

In this pilot study, we developed Delodializ, an audio-based CBT mobile application specifically tailored for HDPs with depression, and assessed its perceived usability among HDPs. The application’s intuitive interface and culturally tailored audio content yielded high perceived usability scores and patient satisfaction, indicating its feasibility as a platform for delivering CBT content. However, our evaluation did not include pre-post measures of depressive symptoms, and thus no claims about clinical effectiveness can be made at this stage. Future studies should incorporate rigorous clinical outcome assessments to determine the therapeutic impact of Delodializ on depression in this vulnerable population.
