Torsion Theories in a non-pointed Context
Andrea Cappelletti, Andrea Montoli

Abstract
. We study a non-pointed version of the notion of torsion theory in the framework of categories equipped with a posetal monocoreflective subcategory such that the coreflector inverts monomorphisms. We explore the connections of such torsion theories with factorization systems and categorical Galois structures. We describe several examples of these torsion theories, in the dual of elementary toposes, in varieties of universal algebras used as models for non-classical logic, and in coslices of the category of abelian groups.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F}

The classical notion of torsion theory for abelian categories has strong connections with the ones of closure operator, radical, and factorization system. Similar connections still exist in the pointed, non-abelian case. In
[
3
]
torsion theories are studied in the context of homological
[
1
]
categories, namely pointed, regular, protomodular
[
2
]
categories. The name

categories is justified by the fact that, in these categories, non-abelian versions of the classical homological lemmas hold. In
[
3
]
the authors explore the connections between torsion theories, closure operators and radicals in homological categories. In
[
12
]
the relation between torsion theories and factorization systems is studied in normal
[
21
]
categories, i.e. pointed regular categories in which every regular epimorphism is a cokernel (every homological category is normal). In
[
13
]
the authors restrict their attention to those torsion theories (in homological categories) in which the reflector of the torsion-free subcategory is protoadditive
[
11
]
. A functor between pointed protomodular categories is protoadditive if it preserves split short exact sequences; equivalently, protoadditive functors can be defined as functors preserving the zero object and pullbacks of split epimorphisms along any morphism. This notion is the analogue of the one of additive functor between additive categories. In
[
13
]
the authors show, among other things, that a torsion theory with protoadditive reflector allows for an easy description of the central and normal extensions w.r.t. the Galois structure associated with the reflection. This permits to use the tools of categorical Galois theory (in the sense of
[
17
]
) to describe homology objects relative to such reflection.

More recently, torsion theories have been studied also in non-pointed contexts. In
[

]
the authors proposed a definition of torsion theory, suitable also for non-pointed categories, based on the notion of ideal of morphisms in the sense of

(\mathscr{T},\mathscr{F})
of full, replete subcategories of a category
\mathscr{C}
such that every morphism
T\to F
, with
T\in\mathscr{T}
and
F\in\mathscr{F}
, belongs to

and, moreover, for every object

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q}

with

and

The aim of this paper is to extend to this non-pointed context some of the results of
[
12
]
and
[
13
]
, and to provide several new examples of non-pointed torsion theories. Our framework will be the one of a category
\mathscr{C}
equipped with a posetal monocoreflective subcategory
\mathscr{Z}
such that the coreflector inverts monomorphisms. The objects of
\mathscr{Z}
will be considered the
trivial
objects, and we will study torsion theories with respect to the ideal of morphisms factoring through
\mathscr{Z}
. The main general example of our situation will be given by a regular category
\mathscr{C}
with initial object
0
, equipped with the subcategory
\mathscr{Z}
of regular quotients of
0
. This includes, as concrete examples, all varieties of universal algebras with at least one constant, their topological models, all elementary toposes and their duals, and every ideally exact category in the sense of
[
18
]
. Categories equipped with such a subcategory
\mathscr{Z}
have been considered in
[
5
]
to prove non-pointed versions of the classical homological lemmas. In our setting,
\mathscr{N}
-cokernels (that we will call
\mathscr{Z}
-cokernels, since the ideal of morphisms is determined by the class of objects
\mathscr{Z}
) do not always exist, not even those of the identity arrows. So, our torsion theories will not be, strictly speaking, examples of the situation considered in
[
16
]
, but they will be instances of the definition stated in
[
15
]
.

In Section
2
we describe our setting and recall the non-pointed notion of torsion theory. In Section
3
we establish, in the context of regular protomodular categories equipped with a posetal monocoreflective subcategory
\mathscr{Z}
such that the coreflector inverts monomorphisms, a correspondence between factorization systems
(\mathbb{E},\mathbb{M})
that are stable w.r.t. the class of arrows inverted by the coreflector of
\mathscr{Z}
and such that every arrow in
\mathbb{E}
is a
\mathscr{Z}
-cokernel, and torsion theories satisfying suitable conditions. In Section
4
we study which conditions are needed on a torsion theory to have a good behavior of the Galois structure induced by the reflection of the torsion-free subcategory. Section
5
is devoted to the description of several examples of non-pointed torsion theories, as well as their corresponding factorization systems and Galois structures. Some of the examples live in varieties of universal algebras used as models for non-classical logic, like MV-algebras or Heyting algebras; others are considered in the dual categories of some toposes; the last one lives in coslice categories of the category of abelian groups.

To ensure our desired results, it is necessary to introduce the following:

Definition 3.7

We say that the class
\mathscr{Z}
of zero objects, in a category

if for every

there exists an arrow

such that:

(a)

(b)

, there exists a (necessary unique) arrow

such that
\varphi\chi=v_{A}
.

This definition, despite seeming artificial, is actually highly consistent with the underlying idea behind the definition of these non-pointed categories with a class of zero objects

\mathscr{Z}
. In
[
5
]
, we considered as primary and main example the one given by a protomodular variety of universal algebras in which the theory has at least one constant; in this context, we define
\mathscr{Z}
to be the class of objects obtainable as quotients of the initial object. Since in such categories the arrow from the initial object to the terminal one is always a regular epimorphism, it follows that the terminal object belongs to the class

\mathscr{Z}
, making this class trivially terminal.

The assignment

To sum up, we have shown that, given a regular protomodular category
\mathscr{C}
with a fixed terminal class
\mathscr{Z}
of zero objects, starting from a torsion theory that satisfies Conditions (N) and (M) it is possible to construct a factorization system
(\mathbb{E},\mathbb{M})
which is stable with respect to the class of arrows inverted by
{\sf Z}
, and where every arrow in
\mathbb{E}
is a
\mathscr{Z}
-cokernel. Moreover, we have observed how such a factorization system allows for the definition of a torsion theory satisfying Conditions (N) and (M). It makes sense to ask whether these constructions are inverse to each other. The answer is affirmative, but to verify this we need the following:

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
(\mathscr{T},\mathscr{F})
and
(\mathscr{T}^{\prime},\mathscr{F})
be two torsion theories in a category
\mathscr{C}
with a fixed class
\mathscr{Z}
of zero objects. Then,
\mathscr{T}=\mathscr{T}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

It suffices to observe that the subcategories
\mathscr{T}
and
\mathscr{T}^{\prime}
are uniquely determined by the functors
{\sf T}
and
{\sf T}^{\prime}
. So,
{\sf T}(A)=K[\eta_{A}]={\sf T}^{\prime}(A)
.
∎

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a regular protomodular category with a fixed terminal class
\mathscr{Z}
of zero objects. The assignments just described between torsion theories and factorization systems are inverse to each other.

Proof.

On the one hand, let us start from a torsion theory
(\mathscr{T},\mathscr{F})
, consider the associated factorization system
(\mathbb{E},\mathbb{M})
and the induced torsion theory
(\mathscr{T}^{\prime},\mathscr{F}^{\prime})
. If we consider an object
F
, since
K[F\rightarrow{\sf V}(F)]=F
, we have the chain of equivalent conditions:
F\in\mathscr{F}
if and only if
F\rightarrow{\sf V}(F)
belongs to
\mathbb{M}
if and only if
F\in\mathscr{F}^{\prime}
. Therefore,
\mathscr{F}=\mathscr{F}^{\prime}
and so, by Lemma
3.11
,
(\mathscr{T},\mathscr{F})=(\mathscr{T}^{\prime},\mathscr{F}^{\prime})
. On the other hand, take a factorization system
(\mathbb{E},\mathbb{M})
, consider the associated torsion theory
(\mathscr{T},\mathscr{F})
and the induced factorization system
(\mathbb{E}^{\prime},\mathbb{M}^{\prime})
. Consider an arrow
e\colon A\rightarrow B
and observe that
e\in\mathbb{E}
if and only if
z\colon K[e]\rightarrow{\sf Z}(B)
belongs to
\mathbb{E}
. In fact, examine the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{K[e]}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{{\sf Z}(B)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B,}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces z}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces k}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lrcorner}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varepsilon_{B}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces e}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lrcorner}

which is both a pullback (it is constructed to compute the
\mathscr{Z}
-kernel of

) and a pushout (

is a
\mathscr{Z}
-cokernel). Therefore, if

, since
\varepsilon_{B}
is inverted by
{\sf Z}
, we get

; if

is always pushout stable, we get

if and only if

. Moreover, we see that

, and this is equivalent to requiring

(recall that

). To conclude, since
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
is pushout stable and pullback stable along arrows inverted by
{\sf Z}
, we get

if and only if

. It is a general result that every factorization system is uniquely determined by one of its classes, and so
(\mathbb{E},\mathbb{M})=(\mathbb{E}^{\prime},\mathbb{M}^{\prime})
.
∎

Unlike the previous one, the condition we are about to introduce is not a generalization of a well-known condition for torsion theories in pointed categories. Instead, it introduces a stability condition for torsion objects in our non-pointed context, which is trivially satisfied in the pointed case but necessary to guarantee the desired results. Before proceeding further, after presenting the following definition, we will take a closer look at how this condition relates to the results established so far.

Definition 4.14
.

5.1.
Semisimple and Perfect MV-Algebras

We briefly recall some notions concerning MV-algebras; more information can be found in
[
6
]
. An
MV-algebra
is an algebra
(A,\oplus,\lnot,0)
with a binary operation
\oplus
, a unary operation
\lnot
, and a constant
0
such that
(A,\oplus,0)
is a commutative monoid,
\lnot
is an involution,
\lnot 0
is the absorbing element of
\oplus
, and for every
x,y\in A

\lnot(\lnot x\oplus y)\oplus y=\lnot(\lnot y\oplus x)\oplus x.

The category
\mathbf{MV}
is the variety of universal algebras determined by this equational theory. Given an MV-algebra
A
, we define the constant
1
and the binary operations
\odot
,
\ominus
,
\rightarrow
and
d
as follows:

1\coloneqq\lnot 0;

x\odot y\coloneqq\lnot(\lnot x\oplus\lnot y);

x\rightarrow y\coloneqq\lnot x\oplus y;

x\ominus y\coloneqq\lnot(\lnot x\oplus y)=\lnot(x\rightarrow y)=x\odot\lnot y;

d(x,y)\coloneqq(x\ominus y)\oplus(y\ominus x)

(this last operation is called
distance
between
x
and
y
). In every MV-algebra
A
, it is possible to introduce a partial order: we say that
x\leq y
if and only if
\lnot x\oplus y=1
. With respect to this order, the MV-algebra
A
can be shown to be a distributive lattice. In this structure, the least element is
0
and the greatest element is
1
. In an MV-algebra
A
, an ideal
I
is a non-empty subset of
A
such that:

-

if
x\in I
and
y\leq x
, then
y\in I
(downward closure);

-

for any
x,y\in I
, the element
x\oplus y\in I
(closure under addition).

Given an ideal
I
of
A
, it is possible to introduce a congruence
\theta
on
A
induced by
I
: we say that

if and only if

. Moreover, we denote by
A/I
the algebra obtained as the quotient of

w.r.t. the congruence determined by
I
.
We define the
radical
of an MV-algebra

as the intersection of its maximal ideals, and we denote it with
\Rad(A)
. An MV-algebra is said to be

if its radical is trivial. We will also consider perfect MV-algebras, which are defined as follows: an MV-algebra A is said to be

if

(where, given

,

). In

the initial object is
\boldsymbol{2}\coloneqq\{0,1\}
and the terminal one is
\boldsymbol{1}\coloneqq\{0=1\}
; one can easily see that the terminal object is strict. So, the class of quotients of the initial object is

; this class will be our class

of zero objects. We will show that, with respect to

, the full subcategory of semisimple MV-algebras and the full subcategory of perfect MV-algebras constitute the torsion-free and torsion parts, respectively, of a torsion theory on

Proposition 5.4
.

The torsion theory
(p\mathbf{MV},s\mathbf{MV})
in
\mathbf{MV}
satisfies Conditions (M), (S), and (N).

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{MV}
is a regular category in which the terminal object is strict, then every torsion theory satisfies Condition (M) (as observed at the beginning of this section).
We continue by noting that
{\sf S}(A)=\boldsymbol{1}
if and only if
A=\boldsymbol{1}
: clearly,
A=\boldsymbol{1}
implies
{\sf S}(A)=\boldsymbol{1}
; conversely, if
{\sf S}(A)=\boldsymbol{1}
, then
1\in\Rad(A)
. Assume, for contradiction, that
1\neq 0
; then
1\in\Inf(A)
, which gives
1\leq 0
(and so
1=0
). This immediately implies the validity of (S): in fact, the only non-isomorphic arrow between zero objects is
\boldsymbol{2}\to\boldsymbol{1}
, but we have just seen that
{\sf S}(A)=\boldsymbol{1}
if and only if
A=\boldsymbol{1}
. Therefore, the validity of (S) reduces to observing that
\boldsymbol{2}\in s\mathbf{MV}
. Let us finally show that Condition (N) holds. We consider a morphism
f
and the diagram
{\sf P}(K[f])\xrightarrow{t}K[f]\xrightarrow{k}A\xrightarrow{f}B
, where
k=\Zker(f)
(in our non-pointed sense) and
t
is the inclusion of the perfect part of
K[f]
. We know that
K[f]=I\cup\lnot I
, with
I=f^{-1}(0)
.

We aim to show that
\Rad(I\cup\lnot I)=I\cap\Rad(A)
. In general, it can be verified that if
B
is a subalgebra of
A
, then
\Rad(B)=\Rad(A)\cap B
(this fact essentially follows from the representation
\Rad(B)=\Inf(B)\cup\{0\}
). Thus, we have:

\Rad(I\cup\lnot I)=(I\cup\lnot I)\cap\Rad(A)=(I\cap\Rad(A))\cup(\lnot I\cap\Rad(A)).

Next, we observe that if
\lnot I\cap\Rad(A)
is non-empty, then
I=A
. Indeed, let
a\in\lnot I\cap\Rad(A)
. Then
a\leq\lnot a
(since
a\in\Rad(A)
), and additionally,
\lnot a\in I
(because
a\in\lnot I
). Therefore, from
a\leq\lnot a
, it follows that
a\in I
. Consequently, we have
a\oplus\lnot a=1\in I
, which implies that
I=A
.
From this, we conclude that
\Rad(I\cup\lnot I)=I\cap\Rad(A)
holds whether
\lnot I\cap\Rad(A)
is empty (in which case it is obvious) or whether
\lnot I\cap\Rad(A)
is non-empty (since, in this case,
A=I
). Finally, we note that

{\sf P}(K[f])=\Rad(K[f])\cup\lnot\Rad(K[f])=(I\cap\Rad(A))\cup\lnot(I\cap\Rad(A)),

and therefore, the inclusion of
{\sf P}(K[f])
in
A
can be seen as the
\mathscr{Z}
-kernel of the quotient
A\to A/(I\cap\Rad(A))
.
∎

Remark 5.6
.

Let

be a subalgebra of an MV-Algebra

. We know that

. It follows that

) if and only if

. Therefore, if

. We begin by defining

have a semisimple

-kernel. Now, given an arrow

5.3.
Double Negation in Heyting Algebras

We recall that a
Heyting algebra
is an algebraic structure
(H,\lor,\land,1,0,\Rightarrow)
such that
(H,\lor,\land,1,0)
is a bounded lattice and the binary operation
\Rightarrow
satisfies

x\land y\leq z\text{ if and only if }x\leq y\Rightarrow z.

Given a Heyting algebra
H
, let
H_{\lnot\lnot}
denote the set of regular elements of
H
. An element
x\in H
is said to be
regular
if
\lnot\lnot x=x
(recalling that
\lnot x\coloneqq x\Rightarrow 0
). It is a known fact that
(H_{\lnot\lnot},\lor_{\lnot\lnot},\land,0,1,\Rightarrow)
is a Boolean algebra, where

x\lor_{\lnot\lnot}y\coloneqq\lnot(\lnot x\land\lnot y).

Therefore, this construction defines a functor

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{Heyt}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{Boole}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H_{\lnot\lnot}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{L_{\lnot\lnot},}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\sf F}(f)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\sf F}}

where
{\sf F}(f)
is simply the restriction of
f
to
H_{\lnot\lnot}
.
Since it is true that
\lnot\lnot(\lnot\lnot x)=\lnot\lnot x
, we can define the map

\displaystyle\lnot\lnot\colon H

\displaystyle\to H_{\lnot\lnot}

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto\lnot\lnot x.

It is known that this map is a surjective morphism of Heyting algebras. Specifically, this morphism is the
H
-component of the unit of the adjunction
{\sf F}\dashv{\sf i}
, where
{\sf i}\colon\mathbf{Boole}\rightarrow\mathbf{Heyt}
is the inclusion functor.

A Heyting algebra
H
is
pseudo-deterministic
(we thank Mariano Messora for the suggestion of the name) if, for every
x\in H
, either
\lnot x=1\text{ or }\lnot x=0.
Given a Heyting algebra
H
, we define
{\sf T}(H)\coloneqq\{x\in H\,|\,\lnot x=0\text{ or }\lnot x=1\}
. We recall that, in every Heyting algebra, the equations
x\Rightarrow 1=1
,
\lnot(x\land y)=x\Rightarrow\lnot y
,
\lnot(x\lor y)=\lnot x\land\lnot y
, and
\lnot(x\Rightarrow y)=\lnot\lnot x\land\lnot y
hold (a proof of these identities can be found in any book about Heyting algebras and intuitionistic logic). These identities can be used to prove that
{\sf T}(H)
is a Heyting algebra whose operations are induced by
H
. In particular, we get

\hfil\displaystyle\lnot(x\lor y)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\lnot x=1,\lnot y=1\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\qquad\qquad\lnot(x\land y)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\lnot x=0,\lnot y=0\\
1&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\hfil\displaystyle\lnot(x\Rightarrow y)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\lnot x=0,\lnot y=1\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

So,
{\sf T}(H)
is a pseudo-deterministic Heyting algebra. We denote by
\mathbf{PD}
the full subcategory of
\mathbf{Heyt}
whose objects are the pseudo-deterministic Heyting algebras. Moreover, the assignment described above establishes a functor

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{Heyt}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{PD}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{{\sf T}(H)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{{\sf T}(L),}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\sf T}(f)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\sf T}}

where
{\sf T}(f)
is given by the restriction of
f
to
{\sf T}(H)
. It is easy to observe that the inclusion of
{\sf T}(H)
in
H
is the
H
-component of the counit of the adjunction
{\sf j}\dashv T
, where
{\sf j}\colon\mathbf{PD}\rightarrow\mathbf{Heyt}
is the inclusion functor.

Since, in every Boolean algebra,
\lnot x=0
implies
x=1
and
\lnot x=1
implies
x=0
, we deduce that

\mathbf{Boole}\cap\mathbf{PD}=\{\boldsymbol{1},\boldsymbol{2}\}.

From this point on, we will consider as class of zero objects
\mathscr{Z}\coloneqq\mathbf{Boole}\cap\mathbf{PD}=\{\boldsymbol{1},\boldsymbol{2}\}
.

. It is clear that

. Conversely, if

, this implies that

in

, and as a result,

. Hence, once we prove that

constitutes a torsion theory, we immediately conclude that it satisfies Conditions (M), since

is a terminal class, and (S), using the same argument as at the beginning of the proof of Proposition

. Let us consider a morphism of Heyting algebras

, where

\mathbf{PD}
and

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\chi}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces t_{H}}

5.4.

\mathbf{sSet}

We denote by
\Delta

[n]\coloneqq\{0,1,2,\dots,n\}
, where the order is induced by the usual one of
\mathbb{N}
, and whose morphisms are the order-preserving maps. We recall that a

is a functor

. The category of simplicial sets, denoted by
\mathbf{sSet}
, has the simplicial sets as objects, and the natural transformations between them as morphisms. For every natural number

and every simplicial set

, we define

.

A simplicial set

can be seen as family of sets

, for each non-negative integer

, for

, and

, for

s_{i}s_{j}=s_{j}s_{i-1},\hskip 28.45274pti>j

d_{i}s_{j}=\begin{cases}s_{j-1}d_{i},&i<j\\
id,&i=j,j+1\\
s_{j}d_{i-1},&i>j+1.\end{cases}

This is the standard way to write the data of a simplicial set. The elements of

are called the

. Given that each

is an injective map, to simplify the notation, we will assume that these maps are inclusion maps.

is a two-valued elementary topos. Indeed, if we consider a simplicial set

. However, in the latter case, it follows that

. Therefore, we have

are precisely those such that

. We now define two full subcategories of

whose objects are:
