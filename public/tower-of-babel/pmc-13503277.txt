Data for famine and extreme emergencies: challenges and opportunities
Authors: Daniel Maxwell, Peter Hailey, Elena Naumova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-quality data are a pre-requisite for the development of models—whether of famine or food and nutrition-related emergencies. We highlighted challenges, related to data in famines or near-famine emergencies, including data availability, quality, timeliness, and sharing; the misuse of data could be a real threat for applying new advanced data analytic tools, such as artificial intelligence and machine learning. We also explored promising opportunities, such as democratization of data related to famine. With growing demands for real-time assessment and modeling of famine and extreme emergencies, it is increasingly important to understand how predictive models and emerging data-integration technologies are affected by biased, incomplete, inconsistent, or outdated data. As we push for addressing data constraints, we call for ethical data democratization to minimize the risks of data misuse, weaponization, and politization and to enable famine modeling for collective efforts to enhance early warning, real-time analysis, and response.Keywords: Famine, Food and nutrition-related emergencies, Data democratization, Ethical considerations

Introduction

The number of people experiencing the most severe hunger more than doubled between 2023 and 2024, with Gaza and Sudan among the worst hit. Depending on how one counts, there have been about 40 famines during the twentieth and twenty-first centuries—and at least five since 2010. The counts vary depending on the criteria used to define famine in the twentieth century. Since the mid 2000s, the technical criteria for determining famine have been set by the Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) Guidelines [1]. Very briefly, IPC criteria define a famine as: at least 20% of households facing extreme food insecurity; at least 30% of children under five years of age being acutely malnourished (low weight for height) or 15% by mid-upper arm circumference; and a crude death rate (from non-trauma causes) of at least 2 persons per ten thousand per day, in a specified population of at least 10,000 people in a given geographic area. As is obvious, therefore, a famine determination requires a lot of data. IPC is mainly a methodology for classifying the severity of acute food insecurity and malnutrition and results in a categorization across five different ‘phases,’ with famine being the most severe Phase 5. The data and analysis requirements to establish the phase designation are broadly the same for all phases. While there have only been five instances of famine determination in this century, this kind of data is needed for every crisis that might be a famine, and there have been at least 26 such instances since 2010 [2].

Collecting and analyzing data for lesser degrees of severity are daunting tasks. Collecting the data and analyzing situations that are famines or close to famine is an extremely fraught endeavor [2]. Famine is a failure of both governance and humanitarian response, so no government wants to hear about famine, and many humanitarian agencies are wary of the term as well. Stephen Devereux points out that contemporary famines are no longer primarily a function of production failures or natural disasters—they are almost without exception human-made catastrophes, either by intent or by neglect [3].

Typically, famine data analysis can be broken out into three major categories: 1—famine determination or classification; 2—causal analysis or the analysis of famine drivers and dynamics; and 3—forecasting or famine early warning. While classification analysis only happens relatively infrequently—annually or semi-annually at best—the analysis of crisis dynamics and early warning is a continuous activity. All these forms of analysis are dependent on substantial amounts of data—as well as experienced analysts who are familiar not only with the criteria and analytical methods, but also the context, the ever-changing nature of famine dynamics, and the politics of the data and analysis. In the twentieth century, famine analysis was largely retrospective—as much the province of historians as of humanitarian analysts—and the criteria for classification were largely related to total mortality, not to rates or prevalence. However, the analysis of famine and extreme crises that might evolve into famines is now very much carried out in real time, and therefore, the urgency of access to accurate, reliable data is paramount, but also a major challenge.

In this Viewpoint, we briefly review the challenges of these three types of famine analysis, primarily focusing on data-related issues. This overview was in part built on our evaluation of famine-risk contexts conducted from 2017 to 2021 [4], where we stated (p.16):

Famine means destitution, increased severe malnutrition, disease, excess death and the breakdown of institutions and social norms. Politically, it means a failure of governance – a failure to provide the most basic of protections. Because of both its human and political meanings, ‘famine’ can be a shocking term. This is turn makes the analysis of famine a very sensitive subject.

Given the described challenges, we also consider various opportunities that new approaches to data collection and analysis might offer to all three kinds of famine analysis. The emerging efforts to apply advanced data analytic tools, such as AI, ML and alike, are likely to inherit and potentially amplify the deficiencies and problems carried by the existing practices. The link to the rest of this Special Edition is obvious: modeling famine dynamics will necessarily rely on the availability of and access to the data required, and sometimes these data are missing or very difficult to obtain. As a result, many of these data and analysis challenges must be overcome or circumvented if famine modeling is to catch up with analytical modeling in other fields like climate forecasting or modeling public health crises.

Key challenges of famine analysis

From a multisectoral stakeholder point of view, there are many competing interests in the analysis of famine or extreme emergencies that border on famine, that result in major challenges. The major actors in this narrative include national governments, donors, humanitarian agencies (both international and local), information systems, and affected populations. Each has its own perspective but may have very different power to control data collection and analysis. In general, there are several challenges common to all three types of analysis such as classification, causal path determination, and early warning forecasting. These common issues are briefly described below.

Data collection, availability, and accessibility

Procedures, protocols, and quality are among the major constraints in data collection. These limitations can leave entire categories of data missing, creating critical obstacles to sound analysis. The most frequently “missing” data categories include mortality and nutrition from Standardized Monitoring and Assessment of Relief and Transitions (SMART) surveys, and specifically, the prevalence estimates of malnutrition that are critical to meeting the mandated requirements for methodological rigor. Information on food security tends to dominate these analyses, but while famine involves food insecurity, it results from much more than just inadequate dietary intake data. Information from health, water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) programs is also frequently missing.

But beyond these indicators that give a picture of current conditions and nutrition status, deeper causal analysis requires data on the drivers of the crisis (typically conflict, climate hazards and market hazards), as well as information on underlying vulnerability and mitigating factors. Monitoring climate and market conditions is relatively well established; monitoring conflict is less so, and as noted above, the presence of conflict makes all this difficult [5].

Historical data for the analysis of trends—an important component of contextual analysis—may be incomplete or non-existent or limited to specific subpopulations. The methodology of data collection, and therefore the collected data, may vary depending on the purpose for which data were collected. Famine analysis usually requires data that are representative of a general population, yet much humanitarian data are collected for program management purposes, and thus not necessarily representative of a population—only of the specific program participants.

Collecting information about mortality, malnutrition, vulnerability and coping can constitute an invasion of the privacy of very vulnerable populations. When the affected populations may live in areas under the control of armed groups fighting against the state, the compromising of such data may put people at physical risk. Ensuring the privacy and security of sensitive data is an equally important consideration, especially when dealing with vulnerable populations.

Access to affected populations is a major constraint, and a major reason for the data gaps noted above. Conflicts are the common causal factors across almost all contemporary famines or near-famine emergencies. Conflicts limit access to affected populations and thus to essential information needed for assessing current conditions (and thus for classification analysis) and understanding dynamics to enable forecasting. Access to affected populations may be constrained by actual insecurity, but it may also be constrained by bureaucratic requirements—particularly if the governments involved do not necessarily want current conditions to be publicized—whether there is conflict happening or another driver of the crisis. This can result in significant gaps in the data, making analysis very difficult.

Data sharing and timeliness

The ability for analysts to access all data on a timely basis is a major constraint. Data may exist, but unless it can be fed into an analysis—and that analysis can be cross-checked by other analysts—the data might not be very useful for detecting and preventing famine. Data sharing—or more specifically the reluctance to share data—was among the most frequently mentioned constraints to famine analysis more generally. Sometimes this may have to do with political considerations, but frequently it is more a worry that—given some of the constraints on data collection—data quality might not be great and thus reflect badly on the agency or team collecting the data. And finally, information is power, and whoever controls data tends to “control the narrative” on a given crisis or affected population. All of these are constraints to data sharing [4].

Data sharing is becoming more common, with platforms such as the Humanitarian Data Exchange (HDX) serving as a platform where data can be shared and aggregated. Yet, too often, data do not turn up in these locations until after its immediate usage for humanitarian planning and response (or early warning) has long since expired. Particularly regarding needs assessment or early warning, data are only useful for planning and response for a relatively limited time. Data might be useful for research purposes thereafter, but not for informing a timely response. As part of the Humanitarian Reset, triggered by the closure of USAID and other funding cuts, efforts are underway to make the timely sharing of data a more common practice—the impact has yet to be seen.

Data quality and manipulation of data and analysis

Data quality may suffer, particularly when access and time constraints are acute, and it may also be deliberately undermined or manipulated. IPC analysis in particular, but generally all forms of famine analysis, has quite high standards for data quality—many of which are very difficult to attain in conflict or access-constrained contexts. While data quality may be affected by incomplete or have lots of missing answers, poor sampling techniques, access and time constraints, limitations in enumerator training or supervision and a variety of other considerations. But sometimes there are also subtle and not so subtle attempts to subvert or manipulate data and data collection [6].

The reliance of data collection and analysis on funding sources is often raised as a constraint. The very agencies collecting and analyzing information are dependent on the results of the analysis for their budgets, creating at least the appearance of a conflict of interest. In many ways, IPC analysis—in which there are multiple sources of information and multiple parties to the analysis—is a check against this. Separating agencies that are tasked to collect data and conduct analysis (such as ACAPS, REACH-Impact Initiatives, or FEWS NET, the Famine Early Warning System Network—a US-funded famine information project) from implementing agencies is one way of addressing this issue. However, implementing agencies still dominate in data collection related to food security.

Much of the data used in famine classification analysis is collected and analyzed, at best, on a semi-annual basis—in many contexts only once per year, sometimes not at the most critical time of the year (which could be during the lean or pre-harvest season), and sometimes not even at the same time of year, making it difficult to compare across seasons, locations, livelihood zones, etc. Even with semi-annual assessments, these are large-scale efforts that involve multiple teams and lots of logistics—they are not something that can be conducted with sufficient frequency. Furthermore, a lot can change between these large-scale assessments, calling for more real-time or near-real-time monitoring and analysis [7].

Analytical constraints

Constraints to analysis may follow many of the same issues and they may be more overt as well. Examples of famine analysis being quashed or stopped by governments are rare (as famine itself is rare) yet have been documented. Perhaps more troubling is self-censorship by analysts to prevent intervention from governments. Most likely, the interest here is not just the degree of severity (as in IPC classifications) but the numbers related to ‘population in need’ or the so-called PIN numbers. This applies to current status assessment and forecasting future numbers, with the latter being the basis on which budgeting is to some extent based. Higher PIN numbers may mean more resources, but not so much so that they put numbers of affected people into Phase 5 or famine. One form of this was noted in the previously mentioned study, which showed increasing numbers of affected populations in each of the IPC phase classifications from Phase 1 through Phase 4, but none in Phase 5—the so-called ‘left-skewed but truncated’ distribution of population-in-need [4].

The use of qualitative information and expert judgment

Finally, and especially regarding understanding famine or extreme crisis dynamics, much of the information needed cannot be boiled down to quantitative data—even though current status assessment relies almost exclusively on quantitative data that vary in accuracy. Interpreting trends in terms of contextual background, such as livelihood systems, seasonality, national policy (and politics!), donor relations, and more, is all very important to understanding the dynamics that can lead to famine and extreme crisis. This in turn at least partially relies on expert judgment that is not easily translated into an algorithm [8]. Furthermore, expert judgment is often applied broadly, but the difficulty lies in making such judgments at a more granular level without local expertise. Conversely, expert judgment data are often not scalable unless local expertise is involved.

In preparing annual reports and assessments, there is a lack of agreement on the data aggregation methods across time and geospatial domains and across different regions and organizations. The issue of aggregating continuous data to categories whose boundaries may make sense but still retain an element of arbitrariness is not unique to famine and is quite dangerous in many fields. This may be exacerbated when continuous or discrete data with drastically different precision, accuracy, and coverage are mysteriously recalibrated and presented as a ‘magical’ score. This runs the risk of masking the complexity of underlying issues—such that what is a continuum of extreme outcomes is almost always reduced to a question of ‘if it or isn’t it’ (a famine, in this case). That is less a data question than an interpretation question, and it is compounded by dubious or non-existent data. Several recent studies have attempted to bridge the gap in famine modeling by relying more heavily on qualitative information and expert judgment [9, 10].

Misuse, misinformation, and disinformation

In the current stage of advancements in AI, misinformation and data manipulation are—and likely to continue to be—rapidly increasing and there are few current reliable safeguards and regulations. Data are political capital, with outright misinformation and disinformation, and with interested parties attempting to spin data and analysis (or what passes for ‘data and analysis’) to suit their agenda. There are some proposals on how governments or other entities can curb misinformation [11]. The need to combat misinformation requires redirecting already scarce resources. This often happens at the expense of building the infrastructure for data collection in affected areas and training skilled personnel to collect, process, and analyze data accurately.

Opportunities for famine modeling

Some challenges described above could be reframed as opportunities to create a roadmap for potential solutions. Advancements in information technology, data integration, and data democratization offer a promise for improvements if all engaged parties demonstrate the will to do so.

Wide adoption of information technology

The use of satellite imagery and geographic information systems to collect and analyze data on crop conditions, weather patterns, and land use provides valuable data sources that enhance famine and food security analysis—especially by improving data granularity and standardization to ensure spatial linkages. Leveraging mobile phones for real-time data collection and communication in remote areas offers additional insights. However, reliance on mobile technology must also grapple with the challenges associated with the representativeness of data—as well as the power of governments (or tech company owners) to shut off the entire network—as was seen in the Tigray crisis in 2020–2022 [12].

There is significant potential in utilizing ML and AI when working with complex, multi-disciplinary, and multi-methodological data sets [13, 14]. Multiple different initiatives have undertaken this exercise, such as the MERIAM project and the World Bank Joint Monitoring Report with mixed levels of success [15, 16]. We recognize that the development and implementation of AI-empowered data systems for programmatic needs in a timely and actionable mode are still a big challenge [17, 18]. Modern data dashboards should include multiple different types of data, as well as functions and situation analysis tools, and provide information on data completeness and reasons for missingness [19–23].

Leveraging the power of AI and ML could be effective and promising and help tackle some of the reasons for poor action or inaction only if the applied approaches provide transparency to both the data and the analysis, recognize and test assumptions, identify and address the bias and structural missingness. Utilizing advanced analytics to predict famine conditions and identify early warning signs with multisource data by integrating health data (like malnutrition rates, disease prevalence) with climates, conflicts, markets, and agricultural data to create more comprehensive famine models is underway [13–18, 24]. These types of models could expand sociopolitical analysis by incorporating sociopolitical factors, such as governance, cultural norms, political power, and economic conditions. These new types of models should accommodate variables reflecting processes that are moving at different speeds, have different periodicities, and are difficult to quantify. Such approaches would call for new types of requirements for international cooperation and partnership between governments, NGOs, and international organizations to share data and resources. Quite often, the problem is not just aggregation but the lack of sufficiently granular estimates, especially for data sourced by population surveys.

Ethical considerations and community involvement

Ethical considerations for data collection, sharing and analysis boil down to several principles established by the codes of conduct of various existing professional organizations. These ethical considerations include ensuring that vulnerable populations are not exploited, their privacy is protected, and the protocols and processes are transparent and fair [25, 26].

Under these principles, we can advocate for greater emphasis on highlighting the lived experience of affected populations, and empowering local communities to lead data collection efforts, ensuring that the data are culturally relevant and accurate. In famine contexts, data collection, sharing and analysis activities could put local communities at serious risk. Thus, a ‘do no harm’ approach is a pre-requisite. We could also think about a Code of Conduct for data and analysis in the humanitarian space. There are starting points like the ethical guidelines of the American Statistical Association (ASA), the International Statistical Institute (ISI), or other professional organizations [25, 26].

Reluctance to share data in real-time is a constraint, and sharing agreements may need to be put in place and be required by donors. The ethical reasoning should help to advance data anonymization—the process of transforming data so individuals cannot be directly or indirectly identified, while preserving its utility for analysis. The process of making data accessible is essential for advancing famine and food crisis modeling. It also provides the tools and training needed to bridge data gaps and to apply the ‘do no harm’ approach. We should also raise questions about how to teach data ethics and data literacy for humanitarians and how to raise awareness about data ownership, governance, and sovereignty [27].

Data democratization

Data democratization refers to the process of making data accessible and understandable to a broad range of users within an organization or society—regardless of their technical expertise—so that anyone can use data to make informed decisions. This concept has garnered much attention recently [28]. In ideal settings, and for less sensitive data, data democratization can empower people to make evidence-based decisions, promote transparency, foster innovation, and improve outcomes in fields like business, health, governance, and research. In humanitarian settings, there are three major constraints to data democratization and data sharing. The first is the need for broadly agreed standards that protect individual privacy and protect against potential harm by disclosing information that can be used against involved parties. The second is the widespread tendency in the humanitarian sector to withhold data from sharing. The third is the tendency for affected states and controlling non-state actors to prohibit either the collection or publication of data regarding areas or populations under their control. These factors continue to be major constraints in famine-affected and famine-risk contexts [4–7]. The notion of data democratization could be an alternative means of addressing some of these constraints.

Addressing protection concerns would require identifying data analysis tasks and statistically sound solutions to minimize re-identification risks. Common methods of data anonymization include several methods, such as data masking, aggregation, generalization, and differential privacy. Data masking replaces sensitive values with scrambled data, while generalization reduces the precision of data to prevent identification; differential privacy adds statistical noise to the data, allowing for accurate aggregate analysis while providing mathematical guarantees that individual records cannot be re-identified. Another emerging approach is the use of synthetic datasets, which are artificially generated data that mimic the statistical properties of real datasets but do not contain actual personal information, making them especially valuable for sharing and testing purposes without compromising privacy. These techniques should be carefully examined in terms of their ability to serve the right purpose—to help obscure direct and indirect identifiers while preserving sufficient information to support meaningful analysis, striking a balance between data utility and individual privacy.

Other constraints to data democratization are less amenable to technological solutions. Remote sensing and artificial intelligence have made some inroads on these issues but much of these data are commercial and not freely available unless it can be purchased. Humanitarian analysts have long advocated that data gathered using public funds should be treated as a public good—and be made widely available in near real time but as noted there are major constraints to this notion. Donors who pay for the data collection can help by making data sharing a requirement, and local communities that are involved in data collection should have a voice in all aspects of improving accuracy and relevance. In the longer term, the humanitarian information and analysis ecosystem needs a cultural shift putting the interests of affected populations ahead of political or fundraising concerns.

Conclusions

Famine modeling and related efforts have the potential to enhance real-time monitoring and improve humanitarian action, but their effectiveness depends on addressing significant data challenges. To address these challenges, we need greater financial investment in data collection and analysis infrastructure; implementation of policies and practices that promote data sharing, transparency, and collaboration among stakeholders; and a culture of data sharing agreements, enforced by governments, donors and affected communities. Data efforts are ongoing, but to truly support humanitarian programming, both data collection and analysis must occur in near real time to guide response and resource allocation, decision-making, and cost–benefit assessments. Information from 6 or 12 months ago may be useful for research, but it is too outdated to guide planning or early warning decisions. The push to democratize data will further expose political deficiencies and power dynamics, increasing the need for skilled humanitarian actors who can navigate the social, technical, logistical, and political challenges of today and tomorrow.
