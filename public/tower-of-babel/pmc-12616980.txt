Serum calprotectin as a biomarker for postoperative complications in colorectal surgery: a pilot study defining normal postoperative dynamics
Authors: Thomas von Ahnen, Martin von Ahnen, Melina Eck, Josefine Schardey, Stefan Schopf, Jan D`Haese, Florian Kühn, Hans Martin Schardey, Ulrich Wirth
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposesPostoperative infections are the common cause of morbidity and mortality in colorectal surgery. Calprotectin, an S100 protein, may be a potential marker. The aim was to define the postoperative course (PC) of calprotectin in serum (CIS), compared to white blood cell (WBC), C-reactive protein (CRP), lactate and procalcitonin (PCT).MethodsThis prospective, single-center study measured all biomarkers preoperatively, on the first, third and fifth postoperative days (POD). The endpoint was the PC of CIS compared to the WBC, CRP, PCT, and lactate. CIS between benign and malignant disease and between patients with and without complications was compared. Also a correlation was carried out.Results56 patients (14 rectum, 42 colon) were included. The postoperative CIS increased to preoperative values (p < 0.05). The preoperative CIS in malignant disease was higher (p < 0.05). CIS on the first and fifth POD was higher in the group with complications (p < 0.05). CIS and CRP, WBC and CRP on the third POD (p < 0.05) correlate in complications .ConclusionWe present the normal course of CIS and its potential function as a marker for systemic inflammation. We showed that postoperative CIS were significantly higher in complications. Although our study has several limitations, - namely a small sample size and heterogeneous surgical procedures -, serum calprotectin may be an interesting biomarker for future larger studies, particularly due to its increased specificity for intestinal inflammation.Trial registrationThis study is prospectively registered in DRKS-ID (Deutsches Register Klinischer Studien, German WHO Register): DRKS00027142, data of registration 29.11.2021. This is a WHO-recognized primary registry that meets the requirements of the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE). Introduction

Background

Postoperative infectious complications significantly influence morbidity and mortality in colorectal surgery [1]. Anastomotic leakage (AL) has detrimental effects on both’ perioperative and long-term outcomes. The incidence of AL ranges from 0.2% to 27.2% [2–4]. Early AL detection is key to decreasing morbidity, mortality, reoperation, and health care costs and has a negative impact on quality of life and cancer recurrence; therefore, prompt and timely diagnosis is crucial [3, 5]. Surgeons should be aware of subtle clinical signs and promptly initiate further diagnostic steps, including serum biomarkers, proper imaging, or even early reoperation [5]. Misleading clinical presentations can delay diagnosis [6–9] resulting in significant delays in emergency surgery. In good surgical practice, several clinical parameters, various biomarkers, imaging methods such as computed tomography and various scoring systems have been used to shorten the time to gastrointestinal inflammation caused by postoperative complications [5].

An ideal biological marker can predict infectious complications before clinical signs and symptoms appear [1]. However, such a marker has not yet been identified. Several biomarkers, such as white blood cell (WBC) count, C-reactive protein (CRP), lactate and procalcitonin (PCT), are available during the perioperative course. All of these markers have strengths and weaknesses.

Warschkow et al. found that WBC had lower diagnostic accuracy than CRP for early detection of septic complications [10]. The sensitivity ranges from 58% to 74% from postoperative day (POD) 5 to POD 7 [10–12]. The acute-phase protein CRP is frequently and widely used as a postoperative marker for infections. It increases within 6 to 48 h postoperatively and returns to baseline once inflammation subsides [5]. In diverticulitis, CRP correlates with disease severity [13]. Values > 5 mg/100 ml suggest diverticulitis, while a CRP > 20 mg/100 ml indicates perforation (PPV (positive predictive value) 69%) [13]. PCT has negative predictive values ranging from 95% to 100% and positive predictive values of up to 34% for AL [14]. Garcia-Granero et al. demonstrated that the best combination was PCT at postoperative day 5 (area under the curve = 0.86), with a cutoff of 0.31 ng/mL, achieved in 100% sensitivity, 72% specificity, 100% negative predictive value, and 17% positive predictive value [12]. Elevated serum lactate accurately predicted surgical site infection, with an AUROC of 0.6432 [15]. However, lactate lacks organ specificity. However, Haidi Abd El Zaher et al. have shown that the 5-day trajectories of combined CRP, PCT, and WBC had a better predictive power for AL than the daily isolated measurements [16].

Objectives

In recent years, the CIS has emerged as a valuable biomarker for multiple inflammatory diseases, such as chronic inflammatory bowel disease autoimmune diseases, such as arthritis, asthma, psoriasis, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, and intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy; and severe COVID-19 [17–23]. Calprotectin, an organ-specific S100 protein stored in neutrophils in the gastrointestinal tract, may be a promising marker for postoperative inflammatory complications. To date, only a few studies have investigated calprotectin in serum (CIS) as a marker for postoperative infections [5, 24].

We hypothesize that CIS may serve a potential biomarker for the detection of postoperative complications, although the normal postoperative course (PC) of CIS has not yet been defined.

The aim of this study was to define the normal PC of CIS and compared the postoperative course of CIS with the serum levels of WBC, CRP, lactate and PCT after colorectal surgery. Additionally, CIS was compared between patients with benign vs. malignant disease and those with and without complications. Correlation analyses among the biomarkers were also performed.

Materials and methods

Study design

The present study is a monocentric, prospective observational study on patients who underwent elective surgery with a colorectal anastomosis at the academic teaching hospital of the Ludwig-Maximilians University Munich in Agatharied, Hausham, Germany. The study was approved by the ethics committee by the Medical Faculty of Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München, Pettenkoferstr. 8a, 80,336 München (LMU 21–0822). The guidelines for good clinical practice according to the Declaration of Helsinki were followed.

Setting

The period of recruitment was from April 2022 to October 2023. White blood cell (WBC) counts [reference values: 4.4 × 10E3/µl-11.3 × 10E3/µl], C-reactive protein (CRP) levels [< 0.5 mg/dl], procalcitonin (PCT) levels [< 0.25 ng/ml], lactate levels [0.5–2.2 mmol/l] and the CIS concentration [< 2.9 µg/ml] were measured in the serum of all patients preoperatively (two days before surgery) and on the first, third, and fifth postoperative days (PODs). [Fig. 1] Postoperative complications were documented according to the Clavien‒Dindo classification.

Study design [WBC = Leukocytes, CRP=C- reactive protein, PCT= procalcitonin, lactate and CIS= Calprotectin in serum]

Participants

All patients who underwent elective surgery with a colorectal anastomosis were asked to participate in this study. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Patients with emergency intervention, chronic inflammatory bowel disease, chronic infections, autoimmune diseases, pregnant patients, patients with other neoplastic diseases, aged less than 18 years and who refused to participate in the study were excluded from the analysis.

Variables

Primary endpoint

The primary endpoint was to define the overall normal PC of CIS and comparison of the normal postoperative course of CIS with that of serum WBC, CRP, PCT, and lactate.

Secondary endpoint

As a secondary endpoint, comparisons of the CIS between patients with benign disease and those with malignant disease as well as between the groups with and without postoperative complications were performed. In addition correlation of the different biomarkers was analyzed.

Data sources/measurement

All samples are examined for hemolysis, icteric and lipemic as potential bias. CRP, PCT, lactate, and CIS were measured in serum. WBC counts were analyzed from EDTA blood using Fluorescence flow cytometry on the XN 1000 (SYSMEX). CRP was determined using a particle-enhanced immunoturbidimetric assay on the Cobas c-system (Roche). PCT was measured using an electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) on the Cobas c-system (Roche). Lactate was analyzed using a colorimetric test for quantitative determination in human plasma, also on the Cobas c-system (Roche). CIS was quantified via enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).

Bias

The patient cohort was screened for possible confounding factors for the determination of CIS this includes ongoing therapy with anti-TNF-alpha blockers, hemolysis, conjugated/unconjugated bilirubin and lipemia triglyceride [25]. No interference was detected up to the following concentrations: Hemoglobin 200 mg/dl (2 g/L), conjugated/unconjugated bilirubin 342 µmol/L and triglyceride 37 mmol/l.

Study size

Assuming a significant postoperative increase in CIS and aiming for a power of 0.95, a sample size calculation was performed using G*Power. An a priori t-test (Means: difference from constant, one-sample case) with an effect size of 0.5, α = 0.05, and power = 0.95 indicated that a minimum of 45 participants would be required. Accordingly, the recruitment period was set from April 2022 to October 2023.

Quantitative variables

To analyze the course of CIS, the following subgroups were formed:

an overall cohort,

an overall cohort,

a subgroup of patients with and without cancer (as tumors may influence serum calprotectin levels),

a subgroup of patients with and without cancer (as tumors may influence serum calprotectin levels),

a subgroup of patients with and without postoperative complications (as complications may increase CIS concentrations).

a subgroup of patients with and without postoperative complications (as complications may increase CIS concentrations).

Statistical methods

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) and t-tests were used to assess whether there was a statistically significant difference in CIS levels during the perioperative course. Correction for multiple testing was applied. For each group, mean values, standard deviations (SD), and ranges (min–max) were calculated. P-values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant and are marked with an asterisk. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 22. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and the area under the ROC curve (AUROC) were used to evaluate the diagnostic performance of CIS regarding sensitivity (SE) and specificity (SP). Assuming linear correlation, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated.

Results

Participants

A total of 56 patients were included in the prospective clinical study. (25 male/31 female). The mean age was 69 years with a SD ± 11 [45–90]. An ASA score of 1 was recorded for 3 patients, an ASA score of 2 for 32 and an ASA score of 3 for 21. 39 patients underwent surgery for colorectal carcinoma and 17 patients for benign disease. Among the 39 patients, 5 received neoadjuvant treatment. A total of 14 rectal and 42 colon resections were performed. 31 procedures were conducted using open surgery, and 25 via a minimally invasive approach. The surgical procedures included: 9 laparoscopic sigmoid resections, 23 open right hemicolectomies, 13 laparoscopic rectal resections, 5 Hartmann reversal operations, 3 laparoscopic right hemicolectomies, 1 open transverse resection, 1 open low anterior rectal resection, and 1 open sigmoid resection. Discussion

Calprotectin is a heterodimeric complex composed of two noncovalently associated calcium-binding proteins, S100A8 and S100A9. The S100 protein family consists of 25 members [26]. S100A8 and S100A9 are also known as myeloid-related proteins 8 and 14 or calgranulin A and B. Although the exact active form remains under investigation, calprotectin appears to be active only in its heterodimeric form [27]. Calprotectin is stored in the granulocyte cytosol in neutrophil granulocytes (40–60% of cytosolic protein content) [28]. It also exhibits both intracellular and extracellular functions. At lower concentrations, it is additionally found in monocytes, macrophages, and squamous epithelial cells [26, 28].

It regulates calcium homeostasis, interacts with the cytoskeleton as well as microtubules and plays a role in the intracellular trafficking of phagocytes. Gran et al. described the role of calprotectin in leukocyte transmigration in a mouse model [29]. Calprotectin functions as a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) or alarmin, thereby promoting local inflammatory response. This biomarker can be detected in plasma very early following cellular injury.Taubert and Teodora was able to show that after PCI with mechanically induced “plaque rupture” CIS levels rose significantly within one hour, peaking at two hours with a factor of 1.9 increase over baseline. In contrast, high senstive CRP and IL-6 levels increase constantly during 24 h [30]. This finding was corroborated in a second study involving patients undergoing inguinal hernia repair, where CIS increased significantly within 2 h after surgery—significantly faster than IL-6 (4 h) or CRP (24–36 h). This rapid response is due to the fact that calprotectin is already stored in neutrophils and is released immediately—no de novo synthesis process is required [31].

In gastroenterology, fecal calprotectin is an established biomarker for the diagnosis and monitoring of inflammatory activity in patients with inflammatory bowel disease. Patients with active inflammatory bowel diseases may exhibit up to a tenfold increase in fecal calprotectin levels [32]. Based on these considerations and the gut-derived origin of calprotectin, we hypothesized that it may be a valuable biomarker for detecting surgical complications in colorectal surgery patients. Therefore, it is not possible to measure possible calprotectin increases in the stool in cases of postoperative complications such as AL due to irregular bowel function. In a prospective study involving patients undergoing Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, the kinetics of both CIS and fecal calprotectin were measured preoperatively and six months postoperatively. While CIS levels decreased by 53.6%, fecal calprotectin levels increased by 79.7%, suggesting that CIS and fecal calprotectin reflect different aspects of the inflammatory response. CIS appears to be associated with systemic inflammation, whereas fecal calprotectin is more likely to reflect local intestinal inflammation and, due to its high negative predictive value, is suitable for excluding recurrences [33].

Therefore, for this study, we evaluated perioperative CIS levels in patients who underwent colorectal surgery. The normal CIS ranges between 0.1 and 1.6 µg/ml (reference value of our laboratory < 2.9 µg/ml) and can be elevated in numerous conditions, such as infection, abscess, inflammation, or cancer [34]. Calprotectin can also be detected in local septic complications such as abscesses due to neutrophil death [28], so the determination of calprotectin in drainage fluid could become relevant in future studies.

Key results

Across all praticaptins we observed a significant increase in postoperative CIS values compared to preoperative values, similar to the findings for WBC count and CRP. Additionally, there was a significantly greater preoperative value of the CIS in patients with malignant disease than in those with benign disease. We assume that this significant effect is caused by the tumor disease. Chen et al. demonstrated that S100A8/A9, S100A8, and S100A9 are the main promoters that contribute to tumor development, growth and metastasis by interfering with tumor metabolism and the microenvironment. Recent studies also highlight their potential as diagnostic and prognostic tumor biomarkers [35]. Due to the limited sample size, a correlation between CIS levels and UICC stage could not be established. Nonetheless, we hypothesize that CIS increases with tumor stage severity, warranting future investigation in larger cohorts. Although CIS is a marker for cell death [28], no elevated CIS level were observed in patients who received a neoadjuvant-treatment. This may be due to the small sample size (n = 5) and the 10-week interval between therapy and surgery. Future studies could explore CIS kinetics during neoadjuvant treatment for a more detailed understanding.

Other analyses

Rama et al. showed that the CIS in patients without complications was between 2 µg/ml and 7 µg/ml [5]. Our data confirm these findings. In patients without complications, the mean CIS was between 4.4 µg/ml and 9.1 µg/ml. According to Rama et al., in the first 5 PODs, the mean CIS tended to follow the pattern of CRP, although it was not as pronounced [5]. In contrast, our data showed that CIS followed a pattern similar to the WBC count, peaking on POD1, while CRP peaked on POD3.

Like Rama et al. [5]., we were also able to demonstrate a significant increase in the CIS on the first and fifth PODs in patients with complications (p < 0.05). In our study, one patient experienced an AL, resulting in a CIS of 15.8 µg/ml, 18.5 µg/ml and 18.4 µg/ml. In the current literature, an increase in the CIS on POD3 to 12.995 µg/ml on POD4 10.605 µg/ml and on POD5 10.955 µg/ml is associated with major complications (AL, Clavien‒Dindo ≥ 3) [5]. This increase is significantly greater than that seen with minor complications (Clavien–Dindo I–II) [5]. CIS, a sign of neutrophil activation, could be a promising early marker for an excessive inflammatory response. Due to the small number of patients with infectious complications, our data on the sensitivity and specificity of the CIS in the event of complications should be interpreted with caution. The most common minor complication in our cohort was prolonged postoperative atony, which also resulted in increased CIS levels. These values must be interpreted cautiously, as even constipation (obstipation) has been associated with increased calprotectin levels [36].

Like Rama et al. [5] (AUROC 0.82), we were also able to demonstrate the best correlation between CRP and CIS POD3 [(Pearson) 0.76]. However, we were unable to demonstrate the superiority of CIS over established markers such as CRP or WBC. Still, the observed trends are promising, and future studies should further explore the potential of CIS as part of a multimodal biomarker panel [5]. Given the lack of a single reliable predictive biomarker, we consider the combined use of CIS with other inflammatory markers as a promising strategy for improving early complication detection. However, our findings must be validated in larger studies.

Limitations

However, our study has several limitations: the number of patients was quite small and various surgical procedures were included, introducing heterogeneity. However, normal values for the CIS in the postoperative course have not yet been defined. According to previous works of Rama or Reisinger [5, 23], in our prospective study, no emergency procedures were included for ethical reasons. Additionally, no measurements were carried out on the 2nd or 4th day. Only one case of AL was observed, which limits the generalizability of predictive conclusions. CIS is measured via ELISA, requiring up to 24 h for processing, which is significantly longer than for WBC, CRP, PCT, or lactate. Additionally, this test is not available at every facility, so processing times vary considerably. The cost (~ 60 € per test) is also higher than that of conventional parameters [37].

Interpretation

To our knowledge, we describe the normal PC of CIS after colorectal surgery. Given the intestinal specificity of calprotectin, CIS appears to be a promising biomarker for further investigation. To better define normal postoperative CIS kinetics and evaluate its relevance for detecting anastomotic leakage, a larger sample size is required, especially since only one case of AL occurred in our cohort. Furthermore, the course of CIS in special cohorts, such as patients who underwent emergency procedures, e.g., for intestinal perforations, needs to be investigated. By correlating CIS with other biomarkers, we see a potential advantage for the early detection of postoperative complications.

Generalizability

In summary, our study contributes valuable data about the normal PC of CIS in patients with and without complications who underwent colorectal surgery for benign or malignant disease.
