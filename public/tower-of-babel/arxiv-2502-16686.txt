Puzzles in 3D Off-Shell Geometries via VTQFT
Cynthia Yan

Abstract




We point out a difficulty with a naive application of Virasoro TQFT methods to compute path integrals for two types of off-shell 3-dimensional geometries. Maxfield-Turiaci
[
1
]
proposed solving the negativity problem of pure 3d gravity by summing over off-shell geometries known as Seifert manifolds. We attempt to compute Seifert manifolds using Virasoro TQFT. Our results don’t match completely with Maxfield-Turiaci. We trace the discrepancies to not including the mapping class group properly. We also compute a 3-boundary torus-wormhole by extrapolating from an on-shell geometry. We encounter challenges similar to those observed in the comparison between the genuine off-shell computation of a torus-wormhole by Cotler-Jensen
[
2
]
and the extrapolation from an on-shell configuration.

. This agrees with VTQFT computation in near-extremal limit

\tilde{\rho}_{n}^{3d}(P,\bar{P})\approx 32\pi^{3}e^{S_{0}(J)}\lambda^{n}\tilde{\rho}_{n}^{2d}(P)

(2.42)

Therefore, we conclude that our VTQFT computation disagree with Maxfeidl-Turiaci because we didn’t take into account the mapping class group. (See Apendix
F
for mapping class groups of some 3d objects.) Intuitively, this could be understood as in our VTQFT computation different carved-out and filled-in tori are independent of each other, and our 2d computation is in a BF theory where all the boundaries factorize.

4
Discussion

In this paper, we showed that our VTQFT computations of Seifert manifolds and 3-boundary torus-wormholes do not fully align with the expectations from 3D gravity. We traced these discrepancies to the omission of contributions from the mapping class group. To correct this issue, one approach is to explicitly incorporate the mapping class group by hand into the Virasoro TQFT framework when considering off-shell objects. Additionally, alternative methods of extrapolating from on-shell to off-shell configurations, beyond taking the limit of vanishing matter insertions, could be explored.

A.1
Pure 3d gravity

Let us start with AdS

\gamma_{2}=\epsilon^{-2}dt_{E}d\phi

the partition function can be written as

Z(\beta,\theta)=\mathrm{Tr}\left[e^{-\beta H-i\theta J}\right]=\mathrm{Tr}\left[e^{2\pi i\tau(L_{0}-\frac{c}{24})-2\pi i\bar{\tau}(\bar{L}_{0}-\frac{c}{24})}\right]

JT gravity can be canonically quantized perturbatively on a disk topology and the resulting Hamiltonian describes a simple quantum mechanical system of a single degree of freedom

From the bulk perspective,

The pure gravity perturbative Hilbert space is now simply given by normalizable wavefunctions

\mathcal{H}_{\text{pure gravity}}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\,,\qquad\braket{\varphi|\varphi}=\int d\ell\,|\varphi(\ell)|^{2},

Review: Torus Wormhole

In this section, we review off-shell torus wormhole computation by Cotler-Jensen

B.1
2d Cylinder

In this section, we first give the partition function of a cylinder which is off-shell, then we compute a cylinder with one matter insertion of mass
\Delta
on each boundary with the limit
\Delta\rightarrow 0

20

Before we proceed to compute the partition functions, we need two more useful JT gravity tools. First there is the identity

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{\ell}d\ell\,\varphi_{s}(\ell)\varphi_{s^{\prime}}(\ell)e^{-\Delta\ell}=|V_{s,s^{\prime}}|^{2}=\frac{\Gamma(\Delta\pm is\pm is^{\prime})}{2^{2\Delta-1}\Gamma(2\Delta)}

(B.1)

In particular we take two special limits. When
s=s^{\prime}
and
\Delta\rightarrow 0

|V_{s,s}|^{2}\approx\frac{1}{\Delta\pi\rho_{0}(s)}

(B.2)

When
s,s^{\prime}\rightarrow 0
and
\Delta\rightarrow 0

|V_{s,s^{\prime}}|^{2}\approx\frac{4\Delta}{(\Delta^{2}+(s+s^{\prime})^{2})(\Delta^{2}+(s-s^{\prime})^{2})}

(B.3)

Second, in addition to partition functions and Hartle-Hawking states, we review what a propagator evaluates to

P_{\text{Disk}}(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ell,\ell^{\prime})=\includegraphics[valign,width]{figures/prop.png}

(B.4)

A propagator is a time evolution operator of a Hartle-Hawking wavefunction such that

\varphi_{\text{Disk},\beta+\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}}(\ell)=\int e^{\ell^{\prime}}d\ell^{\prime}\,P_{\text{Disk}}(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ell,\ell^{\prime})\varphi_{\text{Disk},\beta}(\ell^{\prime})

(B.5)

we can check
[
39
]
that the above relations are solved by

P_{\text{Disk}}(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ell,\ell^{\prime})=\int ds\,\rho_{0}(s)e^{-(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})s^{2}/2}\varphi_{s}(\ell)\varphi_{s}(\ell^{\prime})

(B.6)

On-shell

We can compute a cylinder with one insertion on each boundary by cutting through the geodesics connecting the two insertions and the resulting configuration is a propagator. We evaluate it in the limit mass of the insertions go to zero
\Delta\rightarrow 0
.

\displaystyle Z^{\text{on-shell}}_{\text{cylinder}}

\displaystyle=\includegraphics[valign,width]{figures/2doneptpic.png}

(B.7)

\displaystyle=\int e^{\ell}d\ell\,P_{\text{Disk}}(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ell,\ell)e^{-\Delta\ell}

(B.8)

\displaystyle=\int ds\,\rho_{0}(s)e^{-(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})s^{2}/2}|V_{s,s}|^{2}

(B.9)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{\pi\Delta}\int ds\,e^{-(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})s^{2}/2}

(B.10)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi\Delta}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})}}

(B.11)

Off-shell

We can compute the cylinder partition function by gluing two trumpets together taking into account the relative twist between them

\displaystyle Z^{\text{off-shell}}_{\text{cylinder}}

\displaystyle=\includegraphics[valign,width]{figures/cylindercom.png}

(B.12)

\displaystyle=\int db\,b\includegraphics[valign,width]{figures/cylindersevered.png}

(B.13)

\displaystyle=\int db\,b\,Z_{\text{Trumpet}}(\beta_{1},b)Z_{\text{Trumpet}}(\beta_{2},b)

(B.14)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\beta_{1}\beta_{2}}}{2\pi(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})}

(B.15)

Note that this gives different answer than the on-shell computation. But we can recover it by include winding in the off-shell computation. Winding term is a sum
e^{-\Delta\ell}
over all possible ways a geodesic can wind around the cylinder

\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\Delta b|n|}\approx\frac{2}{b\Delta}

(B.16)

Then the modified off-shell computation becomes

\displaystyle\tilde{Z}^{\text{off-shell}}_{\text{cylinder}}

\displaystyle=\int db\,b\,\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\Delta b|n|}\,Z_{\text{Trumpet}}(\beta_{1},b)Z_{\text{Trumpet}}(\beta_{2},b)

(B.17)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\Delta}\int db\,Z_{\text{Trumpet}}(\beta,b)Z_{\text{Trumpet}}(\beta^{\prime},b)

(B.18)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi\Delta}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})}}

(B.19)

\displaystyle=Z^{\text{on-shell}}_{\text{cylinder}}

(B.20)

We can summarize the result as

\text{on-shell}=\text{off-shell}+\text{winding}

(B.21)

However, we should note that this relation only works in one direction, i.e. if we know the off-shell computation and the winding we can recover the on-shell computation. However, if we only know the on-shell computation and the winding, because the on-shell computation is lacking the information about which parameter
b
we want to integrate over, we cannot recover the off-shell computation. This conceptually makes our life difficult, because in 3 dimensions, an on-shell computation is easier, and off-shell computation is what we want to know ultimately.

In order for the torus-wormhole to be on-shell, we need at least one insertion on each boundary.

If we take the operator dimension to zero for a torus wormhole, we can connect back to

B.2.1
Off-shell

As shown in Cotler-Jensen
[
2
]
, we can calculate the path integral of a torus-wormhole by quantizing the phase space of a cylinder
\times
time, which is two copies of this quantum mechanics, one right-moving and one left-moving.

Recall that for one particle with position
x(t)
and momentum
p(t)
, ordinary path-integral from
A
to
B
is given by

U(B;A)=\int_{x(t_{A})=x_{A}}^{x(t_{B})=x_{B}}\mathcal{D}x(t)\,e^{i\int_{t_{A}}^{t_{B}}dt\,L(x(t),\dot{x}(t))}

(B.29)

while the phase-space path-integral is given by

U(B;A)=\int_{x(t_{A})=x_{A}}^{x(t_{B})=x_{B}}\mathcal{D}x(t)\mathcal{D}p(t)\,e^{i\int_{t_{A}}^{t_{B}}dt\,\dot{x}(t)p(t)-i\int_{t_{A}}^{t_{B}}dt\,H(x(t),p(t))}

(B.30)

where on the exponential, the first term is the symplectic measure and the second term is the energy.

We parametrize the left-moving cylinder by
(b,s)
and the right-moving cylinder by
(\bar{b},\bar{s})
. To illustrate the quantization procedure, below we just focus on the left-moving cylinder. A cylinder can be viewed as two trumpets glued together. Near the region where two trumpets are glued to each other, set coordinate
\rho
to measure the distance to the joining locus (
\rho<0
is the left tube, and
\rho>0
is the other). The metric is

ds^{2}=d\rho^{2}+\cosh^{2}\rho\,\left(b\,dx+s\delta(\rho)\,d\rho\right)^{2}\quad\quad x\sim x+1

(B.31)

where
\delta(\rho)
implement the twist by distance
s
in the
x
direction. The symplectic form is given by

\Omega_{Cyl}=\alpha\,\delta b\wedge\delta s

(B.32)

On the left-moving cylinder, there are two boundaries, each boundary would contribute both to the symplectic form and to the energy. Now let’s just focus on one boundary, say the one that belongs to the trumpet on the left, and find its symplectic measure and energy. This was done in
[
13
]
.

The boundary condition we impose are

\left.g_{\theta\theta}\right|=\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\quad\quad\left.\phi\right|_{bdy}=\frac{\gamma}{\epsilon}\quad\quad\epsilon\rightarrow 0

(B.33)

where
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
is rescaled proper length along the boundary, so that the total length of the boundary is
2\pi/\epsilon
.

With this boundary condition, the boundary action is given by the Schwarzian

I_{bdy}=-\gamma\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta\,\mathrm{Sch}(\theta)

(B.34)

A trumpet can be obtained from a piece of hyperbolic space

ds^{2}=d\rho^{2}+\cosh^{2}\rho\,d\sigma^{2}\quad\quad\sigma\sim\sigma+b

(B.35)

The wiggly boundary is described by a function
\sigma(\theta)
. The boundary action becomes

I_{bdy}=-\gamma\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta\,\mathrm{Sch}(e^{-\sigma(\theta)},\theta)

(B.36)

The saddle point is given by

\sigma(\theta)=\frac{b}{2\pi}\theta

(B.37)

with the corresponding Schwarzian given by

\mathrm{Sch}(e^{-\frac{b}{2\pi}\theta},\theta)=-\frac{b^{2}}{2(2\pi)^{2}}

(B.38)

And we consider small fluctuations about the saddle point

\sigma(\theta)=\frac{b}{2\pi}(\theta+\varepsilon(\theta))

(B.39)

Expand in Fourier modes

\varepsilon(\theta)=\sum_{|n|\geq 1}e^{-in\theta}(\varepsilon_{n}^{(R)}+i\varepsilon_{n}^{(I)})

(B.40)

where we removed the
\varepsilon=1
mode because of
U(1)
symmetry of the trumpet. And to keep
\varepsilon(\theta)
real we need
\varepsilon_{n}^{(R)}=\varepsilon_{-n}^{(R)}
and
\varepsilon_{n}^{(I)}=-\varepsilon_{-n}^{(I)}
, so the independent variables are
\varepsilon_{n}^{(R)}
and
\varepsilon_{n}^{(I)}
for
n\geq 1
.

The symplectic measure is given by

\displaystyle\Omega_{bdy}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta\,\left(d\varepsilon^{\prime}(\theta)\wedge d\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}(\theta)-2\mathrm{Sch}(\theta)d\varepsilon(\theta)\wedge d\varepsilon^{\prime}(\theta)\right)

(B.41)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta\,\left(d\varepsilon^{\prime}(\theta)\wedge d\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}(\theta)+\frac{b^{2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}d\varepsilon(\theta)\wedge d\varepsilon^{\prime}(\theta)\right)

(B.42)

\displaystyle=4\pi\alpha\sum_{n\geq 1}(n^{3}+\frac{b^{2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}n)d\varepsilon_{n}^{(R)}\wedge d\varepsilon_{n}^{(I)}

(B.43)

and the energy is given by

\displaystyle\bar{L}_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{24}-\gamma\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta\,\mathrm{Sch}(\theta)

(B.44)

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{24}+\gamma\frac{b^{2}}{4\pi}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta\,\left(\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}(\theta)^{2}+\frac{b^{2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\varepsilon^{\prime}(\theta)^{2}\right)

(B.45)

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{24}+\gamma\frac{b^{2}}{4\pi}+2\pi\gamma\sum_{n\geq 1}(n^{4}+\frac{b^{2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}n^{2})\left((\varepsilon_{n}^{(R)})^{2}+(\varepsilon_{n}^{(I)})^{2}\right)

(B.46)

Now let’s set

\alpha=\frac{c-1}{48\pi}\quad\quad\gamma=\frac{c-1}{24\pi}

(B.47)

then

\displaystyle\Omega_{bdy}

\displaystyle=\frac{c-1}{48}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}\,\left(d\varepsilon^{\prime}(\theta)\wedge d\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}(\theta)+\frac{b^{2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}d\varepsilon(\theta)\wedge d\varepsilon^{\prime}(\theta)\right)

Appendix D
3-Boundary Wormhole in 2D

In this section we consider a slightly more complicated geometry: a 3-boundary wormhole, because it will provide us with important intuition when we go up to 3 dimensions. We first compute a 3-boundary wormhole with two matter insertions of mass
\Delta
on each boundary with the limit
\Delta\rightarrow 0
which is on-shell, we then compute the off-shell 3-boundary wormhole directly. Finally, we show that by combining the off-shell computation with three winding terms we can recover the on-shell computation.

On-shell

We insert 6 matter operators, i.e. 2 insertions on each boundary. In the limit
s,s^{\prime}\rightarrow
and
\Delta\rightarrow 0
we have the following

\displaystyle Z^{\text{on-shell}}

\displaystyle=\includegraphics[valign,width]{figures/3holematter3}

(D.1)

\displaystyle=e^{-S_{0}}\int dsds^{\prime}\,\rho_{0}(s)\rho_{0}(s^{\prime})|V_{s,s^{\prime}}(\Delta)|^{6}e^{-\sum\beta_{i}\frac{s^{2}}{2}}

(D.2)

\displaystyle\approx e^{-S_{0}}\int dsds^{\prime}\,\frac{s^{2}}{\pi}\frac{s^{\prime 2}}{\pi}\left(\frac{4\Delta}{(\Delta^{2}+(s+s^{\prime})^{2})(\Delta^{2}+(s-s^{\prime})^{2})}\right)^{3}

(D.3)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{e^{-S_{0}}}{8\Delta^{3}}

(D.4)

where we recalled that in the
s\rightarrow 0
limit

\rho_{0}(s)=\frac{s\,\sinh(2\pi s)}{2\pi^{2}}\approx\frac{s^{2}}{\pi}

(D.5)

Off-shell

On the other hand, without matter, we can cut the 3-boundary wormhole into 3 trumpets and a 3-hole sphere

\displaystyle Z^{\text{off-shell}}

\displaystyle=e^{-S_{0}}\includegraphics[valign,width]{figures/3holenomatter}

(D.6)

\displaystyle=e^{-S_{0}}\int_{0}^{\infty}b_{1}db_{1}\,b_{2}db_{2}\,b_{3}db_{3}\,\includegraphics[valign,width]{figures/3holedisect}

(D.7)

\displaystyle=e^{-S_{0}}\int_{0}^{\infty}b_{1}db_{1}\,b_{2}db_{2}\,b_{3}db_{3}\,Z_{trumpet}(\beta_{1},b_{1})Z_{trumpet}(\beta_{2},b_{2})Z_{trumpet}(\beta_{3},b_{3})

\displaystyle\hskip 170.71652pt\times V_{0,3}(b_{1},b_{2},b_{3})

(D.8)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\beta_{3}}{(2\pi)^{3}}}

(D.9)

where

Z_{trumpet}(\beta,b)=\frac{e^{-\frac{b^{2}}{2\beta}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\beta}}

(D.10)

Let’s now analyze the windings we need to include.
\ell_{2}
and
\ell_{3}
together could wind around
b_{1}
,
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{3}
together could wind around
b_{2}
, and also
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
together could wind around
\beta_{3}
. Thus we need to include three winding factors multiplied together each with effective mass
2\Delta

\frac{2}{b_{1}2\Delta}\frac{2}{b_{2}2\Delta}\frac{2}{b_{3}2\Delta}

(D.11)

and the off-shell computation get modified to

\displaystyle\tilde{Z}^{\text{off-shell}}

\displaystyle=e^{-S_{0}}\int_{0}^{\infty}b_{1}db_{1}\,b_{2}db_{2}\,b_{3}db_{3}\,\frac{1}{b_{1}b_{2}b_{3}\Delta^{3}}Z_{trumpet}(\beta_{1},b_{1})Z_{trumpet}(\beta_{2},b_{2})Z_{trumpet}(\beta_{3},b_{3})

\displaystyle\hskip 213.39566pt\times V_{0,3}(b_{1},b_{2},b_{3})

(D.12)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-S_{0}}}{8\Delta^{3}}

(D.13)

\displaystyle=Z^{\text{on-shell}}

(D.14)

so we get back our on-shell answer.

F.1.1
