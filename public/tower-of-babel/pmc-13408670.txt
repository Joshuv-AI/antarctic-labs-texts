The relationship between smartphone usage and sleep quality among university students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Mohamad Khairi Ishak, Farhana Yasmin, Jannatul Islam, Ateeq Ur Rehman Butt, Abdulrahman Mohammed Alamoudi, Md. Alamgir Hossain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Excessive and poorly timed smartphone use has raised growing concerns about sleep health, particularly among university students. This study examines the relationship between smartphone usage behaviors and sleep quality among university students in Bangladesh, focusing on daily usage duration, bedtime smartphone use, and night-time notifications. A cross-sectional design was employed, with data collected from 402 students using a structured online questionnaire. Sleep quality was measured using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and smartphone usage was assessed through self-reports. Bedtime smartphone use was significantly associated with poorer sleep quality (β = 0.732, p = 0.001), while daily usage duration showed a weaker but statistically significant correlation with global PSQI scores (r = 0.2200, p < 0.001). Night-time notifications were not a significant predictor (β = 0.321, p = 0.068). Multivariate analyses confirmed that the timing of smartphone use, particularly before bedtime, is a stronger determinant of sleep disturbance than overall daily exposure. These findings highlight the need for digital health interventions targeting evening smartphone engagement to improve sleep outcomes among university students. Future research should employ longitudinal designs and objective measures to establish causal pathways between smartphone behaviors and sleep quality.

Introduction

The widespread adoption of smartphones has significantly transformed daily life, particularly among university students who rely heavily on mobile devices for academic, social, and recreational purposes1. While smartphones offer numerous benefits, growing evidence suggests that excessive and poorly timed smartphone use may negatively affect sleep quality2. Sleep plays a crucial role in cognitive functioning, emotional regulation, and overall health, making sleep disturbances among students a major public health concern3.

Over the past decade, smartphones have become nearly ubiquitous among young adults, particularly university students, who represent one of the most intensive user groups4,5. Smartphones serve multiple functions, including academic learning, social networking, entertainment, and professional communication, but increasing concern has emerged regarding their potential impact on health outcomes, particularly sleep quality6. Sleep is a fundamental biological process essential for cognitive performance, emotional regulation, immune function, and overall well-being7. Insufficient or poor-quality sleep has been associated with adverse physical and psychological outcomes, including depression, anxiety, metabolic dysregulation, and impaired academic performance8. University students are particularly vulnerable to sleep disturbances due to academic stress, irregular schedules, and lifestyle factors9.

A growing body of literature suggests that excessive screen time, particularly before bedtime, is associated with delayed sleep onset, reduced sleep duration, and poorer sleep quality10. One proposed mechanism is the suppression of melatonin secretion due to exposure to blue-wavelength light emitted from smartphone screens, which disrupts circadian rhythms11,12. In addition, cognitive and emotional arousal from social media engagement and interactive content may prolong sleep latency and fragment sleep13. Prior studies have also distinguished between total daily screen time and specific patterns of smartphone use, such as bedtime use and night-time interactions. For example, Levenson et al. found that social media use was significantly associated with sleep disturbance among young adults14, while Vernon et al. reported that night-time smartphone interactions were linked to poorer subjective sleep outcomes15. Emerging evidence further suggests that the timing of smartphone use, particularly before bedtime, may have a stronger effect on sleep quality than overall daily duration16.

Despite increasing research on digital technology use and sleep, several gaps remain. First, many studies focus primarily on total screen time without adequately differentiating between usage timing and behavioral context, although existing evidence suggests that evening exposure may be especially harmful10,16. Second, findings regarding the strength and magnitude of the relationship between smartphone use and sleep disturbance remain inconsistent across studies, with some reporting moderate associations and others relatively small effect sizes14,17.Third, although circadian disruption may be more strongly influenced by evening light exposure and cognitive stimulation than by overall exposure alone, empirical comparisons within a single analytical framework remain limited18. In addition, fewer studies have simultaneously examined daily smartphone usage duration, bedtime use frequency, and night-time notifications using multivariate analysis, making it difficult to determine which behavioral components are the associated factors of poor sleep quality.

Against this background, a structured investigation is needed that simultaneously examines multiple dimensions of smartphone behavior, assesses sleep quality using a validated instrument, and evaluates both bivariate and multivariate relationships within a single analytical framework. The present study addresses this need by examining the relationship between smartphone usage behaviors and sleep quality among university students in Bangladesh, using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), a widely validated and reliable measure of global sleep quality18,19. This study makes three distinct contributions to the existing literature. First, it simultaneously models daily usage duration, bedtime smartphone use, and night-time notifications within a unified multivariate framework, enabling precise identification of which behavioral dimension most strongly predicts sleep disturbance, an analytical approach underutilized in prior research. Second, it provides a direct empirical comparison between usage timing and usage quantity as competing determinants of sleep quality, addressing an unresolved question that has persisted across the field. Third, it generates context-specific, PSQI-validated evidence from Bangladesh, a rapidly digitalizing South Asian setting that remains substantially underrepresented in digital sleep health research, thereby enhancing the geographical and cultural generalizability of findings. Together, these contributions aim to clarify the behavioral determinants of smartphone-related sleep disturbance and to inform evidence-based digital health interventions for university student populations.

Literature review

The relationship between smartphone usage and sleep quality has received growing scholarly attention in recent years, particularly among university students who represent one of the most intensive user groups of digital technology20. A substantial body of research suggests that excessive and poorly timed smartphone use may negatively influence sleep through biological, psychological, and behavioral mechanisms. This section reviews existing empirical evidence on smartphone usage patterns, bedtime behaviors, and their association with sleep disturbance, while identifying key gaps that justify the present study.

Smartphone use and sleep: a growing public health concern

The rapid expansion of smartphone ownership has fundamentally reshaped behavioral patterns, particularly among young adults and university students. Smartphones have become integrated into nearly every aspect of daily life, including education, communication, entertainment, and social interaction1. However, increasing evidence suggests that excessive and poorly timed smartphone use may negatively affect sleep health, which is a critical determinant of cognitive performance, emotional regulation, and overall well-being21,22.

Sleep disturbance among university students has emerged as a global concern. Research consistently shows that young adults experience high rates of insufficient and poor-quality sleep due to academic pressures, irregular schedules, and lifestyle behaviors23,24. More recent literature highlights digital technology use particularly smartphones as a significant behavioral contributor to sleep disruption14,17.

A systematic review and meta-analysis by Carter et al. found that access to or use of portable screen-based media devices was significantly associated with delayed sleep onset and reduced sleep duration17. Similarly, a large-scale cross-sectional study by Levenson et al. demonstrated a strong association between social media use and sleep disturbance among young adults, suggesting that interactive digital engagement may exacerbate sleep problems14.

Mechanisms linking smartphone use to sleep disturbance

Several biological and psychological mechanisms explain how smartphone use may impair sleep quality. One widely discussed pathway involves exposure to blue-wavelength light emitted by smartphone screens. Experimental evidence indicates that evening exposure to light-emitting electronic devices suppresses melatonin secretion, delays circadian rhythms, and reduces next-morning alertness11. This circadian misalignment can result in prolonged sleep latency and fragmented sleep. Beyond physiological effects, cognitive and emotional arousal associated with smartphone use may also disrupt sleep. Social media engagement, online messaging, and digital entertainment can increase psychological stimulation before bedtime, making it more difficult to transition into sleep25. In addition, fear of missing out (FoMO) and social comparison behaviors linked to social media use have been associated with bedtime procrastination and reduced sleep quality26. Another proposed mechanism is behavioral sleep displacement. Prolonged smartphone engagement may delay bedtime simply by replacing time that would otherwise be allocated to sleep. Exelmans and Van den Bulck 10 found that bedtime mobile phone use was significantly associated with later sleep onset and poorer subjective sleep quality among adults. This suggests that the timing of smartphone use may be more detrimental than overall duration.

Total screen time versus bedtime smartphone use

Although many studies focus on total daily screen time, emerging research emphasizes the importance of distinguishing between overall usage duration and bedtime-specific behaviors. For instance, Twenge et al.27 reported that adolescents who spent more time on electronic devices were more likely to experience insufficient sleep. However, subsequent studies suggest that nighttime use may be a more critical determinant of sleep outcomes than total daily exposure. It is demonstrated that adolescents who frequently used mobile phones at night experienced poorer sleep and subsequent psychosocial difficulties28. Similarly, a study by Lemola et al. found that nighttime media use was associated with shorter sleep duration and increased depressive symptoms29.

Recent evidence further supports the argument that bedtime smartphone use plays a particularly important role in sleep disruption. Lin et al. found that nighttime social media use was directly associated with poorer sleep quality, even after controlling for overall usage time30. These findings suggest that temporal patterns of smartphone engagement may be more relevant than cumulative daily exposure.

Smartphone use, sleep quality, and mental health

Sleep quality is closely intertwined with mental health outcomes, and smartphone use may indirectly influence psychological well-being through sleep disturbance. Poor sleep has been associated with increased risk of anxiety, depression, and emotional dysregulation8. Levenson et al. found that sleep disturbance mediated the relationship between social media use and depressive symptoms, suggesting a potential indirect pathway linking digital behavior to mental health outcomes14.

Moreover, systematic reviews indicate that problematic smartphone use often conceptualized as behavioral addiction is associated with both sleep disturbance and psychological distress31,32. Sohn et al. in a meta-analysis published in Human Behavior and Emerging Technologies, reported significant associations between problematic smartphone use and poorer sleep quality, depression, and anxiety among adolescents and young adults32. These findings underscore the multidimensional impact of smartphone behaviors on health and highlight the importance of identifying specific usage patterns that most strongly influence sleep.

Gaps in the existing literature

Despite the growing body of research, several gaps remain. First, many studies rely on simple correlation analyses and do not incorporate multivariate models that simultaneously examine daily usage duration, bedtime behaviors, and night-time notifications. Without such comprehensive analysis, it remains unclear which behavioral components independently predict sleep disturbance. Second, while substantial research has been conducted in Western contexts, evidence from diverse educational and cultural settings remains limited. Expanding research across different populations enhances generalizability and contextual understanding. Third, inconsistent findings regarding the strength of associations suggest the need for more refined modeling approaches. For example, some studies report moderate associations between screen time and sleep17, while others indicate relatively small effect sizes27. Clarifying these relationships requires robust statistical examination using validated sleep measures such as the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI).

Theoretical framework

Understanding the relationship between smartphone usage and sleep quality requires a multidimensional theoretical perspective that integrates biological, psychological, and behavioral processes. This study draws upon three complementary frameworks: (1) the Circadian Rhythm and Light Exposure Theory, (2) the Arousal Theory of Sleep Disturbance, and (3) the Displacement Theory of Media Use. Together, these frameworks provide a comprehensive explanation for how smartphone behaviors particularly bedtime use may impair sleep quality.

Circadian rhythm and light exposure theory

Circadian rhythm theory posits that sleep-wake cycles are regulated by endogenous biological clocks that respond to environmental light cues33. Exposure to short-wavelength (blue) light in the evening can suppress melatonin production, delay circadian timing, and impair sleep initiation. Experimental evidence demonstrates that the use of light-emitting electronic devices before bedtime significantly delays melatonin onset and reduces next-morning alertness11.

Recent research further confirms that artificial light exposure at night disrupts circadian regulation and is associated with poorer sleep outcomes34. Smartphones, which emit blue-enriched light and are frequently used in dark environments before sleep, may therefore interfere with circadian signaling. This mechanism suggests that the timing of smartphone use particularly in the evening may be more detrimental than total daily usage duration. Empirical findings support this theoretical pathway. Exelmans and Van den Bulck found that bedtime mobile phone use was significantly associated with poorer sleep quality, independent of total usage time10. These findings align with circadian rhythm theory by emphasizing the importance of evening exposure rather than cumulative exposure.

Arousal theory of sleep disturbance

The Arousal Theory proposes that increased physiological, cognitive, or emotional activation prior to bedtime interferes with the ability to initiate and maintain sleep35. According to this framework, activities that stimulate cognitive engagement or emotional reactivity can prolong sleep latency and fragment sleep architecture36.

Smartphone use particularly social media engagement, messaging, and interactive applications can elevate psychological arousal. Research suggests that nighttime social media use is associated with increased emotional stimulation and poorer subjective sleep quality37. Similarly, Levenson et al. reported that higher social media use was significantly associated with sleep disturbance among young adults, potentially due to heightened cognitive and emotional activation14.

Moreover, the concept of “Fear of Missing Out” (FoMO) has been linked to problematic smartphone use and sleep disturbance26. FoMO-driven engagement may increase nighttime checking behaviors, reinforcing cognitive arousal and delaying sleep onset. Thus, from an arousal perspective, bedtime smartphone use may disrupt sleep not only through light exposure but also through sustained psychological stimulation.

Displacement theory of media use

Displacement theory argues that time spent engaging in one activity necessarily reduces time available for another activity38. In the context of digital media, increased smartphone usage may displace time allocated to sleep, leading to delayed bedtimes and shorter sleep duration. Empirical evidence supports this behavioral explanation. Twenge et al.27 found that adolescents who spent more time on electronic devices were significantly more likely to report insufficient sleep. Similarly, Carter et al. in a systematic review and meta-analysis, concluded that screen-based media use is associated with delayed sleep onset and reduced sleep duration17. However, displacement theory alone does not fully explain sleep quality differences when total usage duration shows modest associations with sleep disturbance. This limitation highlights the importance of integrating displacement theory with circadian and arousal mechanisms to better understand the multidimensional impact of smartphone behaviors.

Methodology

This section outlines the research design and procedures used to examine the relationship between smartphone usage behaviors and sleep quality among university students. It describes the study design, sampling technique, data collection method, measurement of variables, and statistical analysis procedures employed to address the research objectives. The methodological approach was structured to ensure reliability, validity, and alignment between the research questions and analytical techniques.

Research design

This study employed a quantitative cross-sectional research design to examine the relationship between smartphone usage behaviors and sleep quality among university students. A cross-sectional approach was appropriate because it allows for the assessment of associations between variables at a single point in time and is widely used in behavioral and public health research.

Study area and population

The study targeted students enrolled in both public and private universities across Bangladesh. Bangladesh has experienced rapid expansion in higher education and digital connectivity over the past decade. According to the University Grants Commission (UGC) of Bangladesh39, the country currently has:

Sampling technique

This study employed a non-probability sampling technique, specifically convenience sampling, to recruit participants. The survey was distributed online, and students who were accessible and willing to participate were included in the study. Probability sampling was not feasible because a complete sampling frame of all university students was not available, and access to official student enrollment lists was restricted. As a result, random selection of participants could not be implemented. Convenience sampling was considered appropriate for this cross-sectional study, which primarily aimed to examine associations between smartphone usage behaviors and sleep quality rather than to estimate population parameters with national-level generalizability. This sampling approach is widely used in behavioral and health-related research involving university students due to practicality and accessibility. Although non-probability sampling limits the generalizability of the findings, the relatively large sample size (n = 402) enhances statistical power and supports the internal validity of the analyses conducted.

Data collection method

Data were collected using a structured online questionnaire administered through Google Forms. The survey link was distributed electronically to university students through academic communication channels and digital platforms. The online format was selected for its efficiency, cost-effectiveness, and ability to reach a diverse group of students across different universities and academic disciplines. The questionnaire consisted of closed-ended items organized into sections covering demographic characteristics, daily smartphone usage duration, bedtime smartphone use, night-time notifications, and sleep quality measured using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). Google Forms enabled automated data recording, reduced data entry errors, and ensured secure storage of responses. Participation was voluntary, and respondents provided informed consent before completing the survey. No personally identifiable information was collected, ensuring anonymity and confidentiality. The use of an online survey facilitated timely data collection and efficient preparation of the dataset for statistical analysis.

The internal consistency of the PSQI components was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. The calculated alpha value was 0.775, indicating acceptable to good reliability of the scale for measuring sleep quality within the study population.

Justification of data analysis procedure

The data analysis procedures were selected based on the research objectives and the nature of the variables. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics and key study variables. Pearson correlation analysis assessed linear relationships between smartphone usage and sleep outcomes. OLS and multiple linear regression were applied to examine the predictive effects of smartphone usage behaviors on continuous PSQI scores, while logistic regression was used to model poor sleep as a binary outcome. One-way ANOVA was conducted to compare mean sleep quality across usage categories. Together, these methods provided a comprehensive and appropriate approach for examining associated factors of sleep quality.

Statistical thresholds and software

All statistical analyses were primarily conducted in Python, using pandas and NumPy for data management, SciPy for correlation analysis, statsmodels and scikit-learn for regression modeling, and matplotlib for visualization. A two-tailed significance level of α = 0.05 was adopted, with 95% confidence intervals reported for regression estimates and adjusted odds ratios interpreted as statistically significant when confidence intervals did not include 1.0.

Discussion

This study investigated the relationship between smartphone usage behaviors and sleep quality among university students, with particular focus on the relative influence of daily usage duration, bedtime smartphone use, and night-time notifications40,41.

The most prominent finding was the significant association between bedtime smartphone use and poorer sleep quality (β = 0.732, p = 0.001), which outperformed daily usage duration (R² = 0.092 vs. 0.048) as a predictor of sleep disturbance. This finding is explained through two converging mechanisms. First, evening exposure to blue-wavelength light emitted by smartphone screens suppresses melatonin production and delays circadian rhythms, consistent with the Circadian Rhythm and Light Exposure Theory11. Second, interactive smartphone engagement, including social media, messaging, and entertainment, elevates cognitive and emotional arousal before sleep, prolonging sleep latency, as proposed by the Arousal Theory14. Fear of Missing Out (FoMO) further amplifies this effect by driving compulsive nighttime checking behaviors26. The simultaneous activation of both pathways explains why bedtime use exerts a disproportionately stronger influence on sleep quality than total daily exposure. These findings corroborate Exelmans and Van den Bulck10, who found bedtime mobile phone use independently associated with poorer sleep quality, and Lin et al.30, who demonstrated that nighttime social media use predicted sleep disturbance even after controlling for overall usage time.

Although daily smartphone usage duration showed a statistically significant but modest correlation with global PSQI scores (r = 0.2200, p < 0.001), its effect was considerably smaller than that of bedtime use. This is consistent with the Behavioral Displacement Theory, which attributes sleep disruption to the replacement of sleep time with screen time27. However, the modest effect size suggests that total exposure alone is insufficient to explain sleep quality differences, reinforcing the primacy of usage timing over usage quantity. This pattern aligns with Carter et al.17, whose meta-analysis linked screen-based media use to delayed sleep onset, and with Levenson et al.14, who demonstrated strong associations between social media engagement and sleep disturbance among young adults. Night-time notifications did not emerge as a significant predictor in either regression model (β = 0.321, p = 0.068), suggesting that passive intermittent interruptions are less disruptive than sustained active engagement before sleep. This distinction carries practical relevance, implying that interventions focused solely on notification management may be insufficient without addressing active bedtime smartphone behavior.

The present study extends existing literature by simultaneously examining three behavioral dimensions within a single multivariate framework, offering a more precise identification of which smartphone behaviors independently predict sleep disturbance. Additionally, the findings contribute context-specific evidence from Bangladesh, where 71.14% of students were classified as poor sleepers, a prevalence consistent with regional digital health concerns22 and comparable to figures reported in similar cross-sectional studies among university populations20,21. Importantly, the theoretical mechanisms proposed, circadian disruption, psychological arousal, and behavioral displacement, were not directly measured in this study. While the observed associations align with these frameworks, causal inferences remain beyond the scope of this cross-sectional design. Future longitudinal and experimental studies incorporating objective measures of light exposure, cognitive arousal, actigraphy-based sleep assessment, and smartphone tracking are needed to establish causal pathways and validate these theoretical links.

From a practical standpoint, the findings highlight bedtime smartphone use as the primary behavioral target for sleep health interventions. University-based programs encouraging smartphone-free periods of at least 30 min before sleep, alongside digital monitoring tools, may offer effective strategies for improving sleep outcomes. Broader implementation should also account for unmeasured confounders, including caffeine consumption, academic stress, physical activity, and mental health status, which future research should systematically incorporate to provide a more comprehensive understanding of sleep determinants in digitally connected student populations8,31.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that bedtime smartphone use is the strongest behavioral predictor of poor sleep quality among university students (β = 0.732, p = 0.001, R² = 0.092), exerting a substantially greater influence than total daily usage duration (R² = 0.048) or night-time notifications (p = 0.068). With 71.14% of students classified as poor sleepers, the findings carry significant public health urgency. These results shift the intervention focus from reducing overall screen time to specifically targeting evening smartphone engagement through digital curfews, university-based awareness programs, and behavioral sleep hygiene strategies. Future research should address the limitations of this cross-sectional, self-reported design by adopting longitudinal frameworks, objective sleep and usage measures, and controlled investigation of confounders including academic stress, mental health, and lifestyle factors, to establish causal pathways and strengthen the evidence base for digital sleep health interventions.
