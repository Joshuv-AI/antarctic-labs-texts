Real‐World Medication Adherence and Medication Reviews in Adults and Children With Cystic Fibrosis
Authors: Lieke Roeke, Liesbeth I van de Ven, Steffie E M Vonk, Jan J M Coremans, Edward Dompeling, Michiel A G E Bannier, Fatma Karapinar‐Çarkit, Judith de Ruijter‐van Dalem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPeople with cystic fibrosis (pwCF) have complex treatment regimens with cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) modulators and supportive therapies. Evidence on adherence across cystic fibrosis (CF) therapies using different assessment methods and the added value of medication reviews remains limited.Objective(s)This study compared adherence across CF therapies using three assessment methods and evaluated the contribution of medication reviews.MethodsThis cross‐sectional study (February to April 2025) included pediatric and adult pwCF at Maastricht University Medical Centre+. Adherence to CFTR modulators and supportive therapies (vitamin supplements, pancreatic enzymes, inhalers, and nebulizers) was assessed with pharmacy refill data (medication possession ratio, MPR); self‐reported adherence (Medication Adherence Report Scale‐5, MARS‐5), and structured medication reviews. The primary outcome was the proportion of pwCF adherent to CF medication per assessment method. Secondary outcomes were the within‐patient differences in adherence between CFTR and supportive therapies (McNemar's exact test) based on MPR and the number and acceptance of DRP‐interventions in the medication review.ResultsForty‐three pwCF (17 children, 26 adults; 39.5% female) were included. For CFTR modulators, adherence was 91.7% (MPR), 91.2% (MARS‐5), and 72.2% (medication review). For supportive therapies, adherence was 56.3%, 77.7%, and 77.4%, respectively. Four nonadherent pwCF were identified exclusively through medication review. Within‐patients, adherence was higher for CFTR modulators than for supportive therapies (p < 0.001). Medication reviews identified 51 DRPs in 41 pwCF (including nonadherence in 12 pwCF), all proposed interventions were accepted by the treating physician.ConclusionAdherence varied by medication class and assessment method. Medication reviews provided clinically relevant information in addition to refill‐based and self‐reported measures.Keywords: CFTR modulators, cystic fibrosis, medication adherence, medication review, real‐world data

Introduction

Cystic fibrosis (CF) is an autosomal recessive genetic disease affecting more than 160,000 people worldwide [1]. Managing CF requires a complex and demanding treatment regimen that typically includes multiple daily therapies, including cystic fibrosis transmembrane regulator (CFTR) modulators, pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy, vitamin supplementation, airway clearance techniques, and antibiotics for pulmonary exacerbations [1, 2], which can result in drug‐related problems (DRPs) [3].

The therapeutic landscape has changed markedly with the introduction of CFTR modulators. Unlike traditional therapies that primarily address symptoms, CFTR modulators target the underlying molecular defect and partially restore CFTR protein function [4]; thereby improving lung function, quality of life, and overall clinical stability [4]. Although the introduction of highly effective CFTR modulators has led to increasing simplification of treatment regimens, including de‐escalation of supportive therapies in selected clinically stable people with cystic fibrosis (pwCF), still many patients continue to use supportive therapies alongside CFTR modulators. Consequently, treatment burden remains substantial for many pwCF.

Sustained adherence to prescribed therapies is essential to optimize long‐term outcomes [5]. However, adherence in CF is challenging and likely differs across medication classes [1, 6, 7]. Reported adherence rates of supportive therapies in CF range from 35% to 75% [5, 8, 9]. Although a recent systematic review evaluated adherence to CFTR modulators, the available evidence remains largely descriptive and is based predominantly on pharmacy refill or prescription data [5, 7]. Refill‐based measures, like the medication possession ratio (MPR), estimate medication availability rather than actual intake and do not account for day‐to‐day variation or stockpiling, potentially resulting in overestimation of adherence [10, 11]. Self‐reported measures, such as the Medication Adherence Report Scale‐5 (MARS‐5), capture patients' perceptions of adherence but are prone to recall and social desirability biases [12]. In addition, studies have not evaluated within‐patient differences in adherence between CFTR modulators and supportive therapies.

Adherence measures alone may also fail to identify clinically relevant DRPs. However, medication reviews enable systematic identification of overtreatment, undertreatment, ineffective treatment, (potential) adverse drug reactions, incorrect dosing, and problems with medication use, including adherence [13]. Although pharmacist‐led medication reviews have demonstrated benefit in other chronic conditions [14], their role in CF care remains unclear.

Therefore, this study aimed to compare medication adherence at both therapy and patient level using three assessment methods: pharmacy refill data (MPR), self‐reported adherence (MARS‐5), and structured pharmacist‐led medication reviews. In addition, we evaluated within‐patient differences in adherence between therapies and explored the contribution of structured medication reviews to identifying adherence‐related issues, DRPs, and potential interventions.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

We conducted a single‐center cross‐sectional study between February 2025 and April 2025 at the CF center of Maastricht University Medical Centre+ (MUMC+). The study included pediatric (< 18 years) and adult (≥ 18 years) pwCF receiving routine care. Medication reviews were not part of standard care at the time of the study.

All pwCF received written and verbal information about the study. Adult pwCF and parents or legal guardians of minors provided written informed consent. The Medical Ethics Committee of MUMC+ (reference ID: 2025‐0021) waived formal ethical approval as the study fell outside the scope of the Dutch Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (WMO).

Study Population

Eligible pwCF had a confirmed diagnosis of CF according to established diagnostic criteria [15] and were prescribed at least one of any chronic CF therapy in 2024, including CFTR modulators, vitamin supplements, pancreatic enzymes, inhalers, or nebulizers. Participants had to be proficient in Dutch or English to complete study procedures and questionnaires. Participants legally incompetent to provide informed consent were excluded, except for minors whose parents or legal guardians consented on their behalf.

All eligible pwCF were invited by e‐mail to participate and provide written informed consent. Nonresponders received a reminder after two weeks and were subsequently contacted by telephone on three separate occasions at different times. Given the exploratory nature of the study, we did not perform a formal sample size calculation.

Data Collection

Age, sex, disease duration (years from diagnosis to inclusion), genotype, and CFTR modulator therapy were recorded at study inclusion. We retrospectively collected the highest percent predicted forced expiratory volume in one second (ppFEV1) recorded in 2024 for pwCF aged ≥ 6 years, total hospitalization days in 2024, number of pulmonary exacerbations defined as episodes requiring intravenous or oral antibiotic treatment in 2024 [16], and polypharmacy defined as concurrent use of ≥ 5 medications [17].

We assessed adherence using three methods: pharmacy refill data (MPR), self‐reported adherence (MARS‐5), and medication reviews.

MPR

Dispensing data were obtained from the nationwide medication registration system (NMRS) and a pharmaceutical wholesaler (Mediq, for Mucoclear) to assess refill‐based adherence. The NMRS includes dispensing data from all community and outpatient pharmacies in the Netherlands, provided patient consent has been given for information exchange between healthcare providers [18].

We calculated MPR as the total number of days supplied divided by the number of days in the observation period of one year for each medication. For pwCF initiating therapy during 2024, the observation period began at the first dispensing. To ensure reliable adherence estimation, we calculated MPR only when at least three dispensing events were available for the individual medication [19]. When treatment was intentionally discontinued as stated in the medical records, we calculated MPR up to the date of discontinuation if at least three dispensing events were available.

When a dose adjustment of the same medication occurred during the observation period (e.g., discontinuation of one strength and initiation of another), dispensing events were treated as part of one continuous therapy. Days supplied across different strengths were combined to calculate a single MPR for that medication. Dose adjustments were verified through medical record review to confirm that changes reflected intentional modification rather than nonadherence.

When pwCF switched sequentially between medications within the same therapeutic class during the observation period (e.g., switching between CFTR modulator therapies in line with evolving treatment guidelines and national prescribing policies [20]), and each medication met the ≥ 3 dispensing criterion, we likewise calculated the MPR per individual medication. Brand substitutions were considered equivalent. MPR calculations for inhalers were limited to medications prescribed for daily use and excluded as‐needed inhalers because the total number of days supplied could not be calculated.

At the therapy level, adherence was defined as MPR ≥ 80% per individual medication and within a class for a patient using two or more medications concurrently within the same therapeutic class, for example, elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor (ETI) with ivacaftor [21]. Participants were classified as nonadherent for that class if at least one medication within that class had an MPR < 80%.

MPR values exceeding 100% were capped at 100% for adherence classification. Values exceeding 120% were exploratory examined to assess if potential overuse occurred often.

At the patient level, pwCF were classified as nonadherent if at least one therapeutic class had an MPR < 80%.

MARS‐5

We assessed self‐reported adherence using the MARS‐5 questionnaire [22]. Participants aged ≥ 12 years completed the questionnaire themselves, whereas for children < 12 years, a parent or legal guardian completed the questionnaire. Participants completed the MARS‐5 separately for each medication class: CFTR modulators, vitamin supplements, pancreatic enzymes, inhalers (both daily use and as‐needed use), and nebulizers. Each item is scored on a 5‐point scale, resulting in total scores ranging from 5 to 25. At the therapy level, adherence was defined as a total score of ≥ 21 or a score of ≥ 4 on each individual item per individual therapeutic class [22]. At the patient level, pwCF were classified as nonadherent if at least one therapeutic class had a total score of < 21.

Medication Review

Participants who could be reached by telephone were invited to undergo a medication review conducted by a junior clinical pharmacist and a pharmacist specialized in CF. Reviews were performed via video consultation using the Systematic Tool to Reduce Inappropriate Prescribing (STRIP) method [13]. For pediatric pwCF, the medication review was conducted together with the parent or legal guardian, who provided information on the child's medication use and adherence.

The review included medication reconciliation, a structured assessment of medication use and adherence through an interview, pharmacotherapeutic analysis, identification and classification of DRPs according to the predefined STRIP domains [13], development of a treatment plan in collaboration with CF physicians, and a patient consultation to discuss the proposed changes. During the interview, actual medication use, deviations from the prescribed therapy, and reasons for nonadherence were explored. We classified DRPs according to STRIP domains: problems related to medication use or adherence, overtreatment, undertreatment, ineffective treatment, (potential) adverse drug reactions, and incorrect dosing [13]. We documented all proposed interventions and physician acceptance.

At the therapy level, participants were considered adherent when no DRP classified as a problem related to medication use or adherence was identified for any individual medication within a class. At the patient level, pwCF were classified as nonadherent if at least one DRP classified as problems related to medication use or adherence was identified in any therapeutic class.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome was the proportion of adherent pwCF assessed at both therapy and patient level using three methods: MPR, MARS‐5, and medication review. Secondary outcomes included within‐patient differences in adherence between CFTR modulators and supportive therapies based on MPR, the identification and classification of DRPs, and acceptance of proposed interventions by the treating physician.

Statistical Analyses

We used descriptive statistics to summarize baseline characteristics, adherence at therapy and patient level, and findings from medication reviews. Depending on distribution, continuous variables were presented as mean (standard deviation, SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Adherence across supportive therapies combined was summarized descriptively.

Within‐patient differences in MPR‐based adherence between CFTR modulators and supportive therapies were evaluated using McNemar's exact test. A two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

We performed all analyses using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Adherence Outcomes

At the therapy level, adherence based on MPR was 91.7% for CFTR modulators, 69.0% for vitamin supplements, 50.0% for pancreatic enzymes, 45.5% for inhalers (daily use), and 54.5% for nebulizers. Adherence based on MARS‐5 was 91.2% for CFTR modulators, 86.7% for vitamin supplements, 80.6% for pancreatic enzymes, 76.9% for inhalers (daily and as‐needed prescriptions), and 46.7% for nebulizers. Adherence based on medication review findings was 72.2% for CFTR modulators, 75.0% for vitamin supplements, 73.5% for pancreatic enzymes, 81.8% for inhalers, and 90.9% for nebulizers (Figure 1).

Discussion

This study compared medication adherence at both the therapy and patient levels using three assessment methods. Adherence estimates differed substantially depending on the method applied. While adherence to CFTR modulators was consistently high according to MPR and MARS‐5, it was lower when assessed through medication review. However, adherence to supportive therapies appeared to be lower based on MPR estimates and showed greater variability across assessment methods. The limited overlap between assessment methods underscores that each method captures different dimensions of adherence behavior. Relying on a single assessment method would have resulted in under‐detection of nonadherence. Structured pharmacist‐led medication reviews complemented pharmacy refill data and self‐reported adherence by identifying adherence‐related issues that were not always detected by these adherence measures. In addition, medication reviews identified clinically relevant DRPs in the majority of pwCF resulting in 51 interventions, all of which were accepted by the treating physician.

At the therapy level, adherence to CFTR modulators was consistently high according to MPR and MARS‐5, aligning with previously reported MPR‐based rates of 80%–100% [5]. In contrast, adherence to supportive therapies was lower and more variable, consistent with earlier reports ranging from 35% to 75% [5, 8, 9], although methodological differences limit direct comparison. The lower concordance between MPR and MARS‐5 for supportive therapies suggests that pwCF provide socially acceptable answers on the MARS‐5 questionnaire, or they may overestimate adherence to treatments that provide less immediate symptom relief or have a higher administration burden [23, 24].

When adherence was assessed through medication reviews, estimates for CFTR modulators were lower than those based on MPR or MARS‐5. Unlike refill‐based or self‐reported measures, medication reviews identify clinically relevant problems during a patient interview, where some pwCF may be more open about nonadherence [10, 11, 12]. Nevertheless, only three pwCF were identified as nonadherent by all three assessment methods, and each method identified additional pwCF as nonadherent, suggesting that a combination of assessment methods may be needed to capture possible nonadherent pwCF.

Differences in adherence between CFTR modulators and supportive therapies likely reflect differences in perceived benefit. CFTR modulators often lead to noticeable improvements in symptoms and clinical stability, whereas the effects of supportive therapies are less immediately visible. Previous research shows that adolescents and adults with CF report a reduced perceived need for medication when they feel asymptomatic [7, 25]. In addition, results from the SIMPLIFY trial indicate that discontinuing certain inhaled therapies in clinically stable patients receiving CFTR modulators does not necessarily lead to short‐term clinical deterioration [26]. Such findings may reinforce the perception that supportive therapies are less essential, which needs to be explored in further research. Furthermore, in the CFTR modulator era, clinicians increasingly de‐escalate supportive therapies in stable pwCF. National prescribing data from the Netherlands confirm a decline in inhaler and nebulizer use following widespread implementation of CFTR modulators [27].

Medication reviews identified DRPs and corresponding interventions in more than two‐thirds of pwCF, most commonly related to overtreatment, problems related to medication use or adherence, and potential adverse drug reactions. All proposed interventions were accepted by the treating physician, suggesting clinical relevance and feasibility within routine CF care. These findings extend previous work on the role of pharmacists in outpatient CF care [28, 29, 30] and demonstrate the added value of pharmacist‐led medication reviews in a mixed pediatric and adult population treated with CFTR modulators.

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of this study is its multimethod assessment of medication adherence, combining pharmacy refill data, self‐reported adherence, and medication reviews, including verification of medication changes through medical record review. By cross‐checking refill data with documented treatment modifications, we reduced the risk of misclassifying intentional discontinuation or clinician‐initiated de‐escalation as nonadherence.

Some limitations should be considered. Its cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference and does not capture longitudinal adherence patterns. The relatively small sample size limited statistical power for subgroup analyses, and the single‐center design may reduce generalizability. Selection bias is possible, as pwCF willing to participate in medication reviews may be more engaged in their care and may be more adherent. In addition, pharmacy refill data reflect medication dispensing rather than actual intake, and self‐reported adherence, including MARS‐5 and medication reviews, remains vulnerable to recall and social desirability biases. Finally, the findings for pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT) should be interpreted with caution, as adherence to pancreatic enzymes is inherently difficult to quantify. Dosing is individualized and intended to vary with meal size and fat content, meaning that prescribed regimens may not reflect appropriate daily use. This limitation highlights the importance of assessing adherence to PERT using complementary methods rather than relying on a single adherence measure.

Clinical Implications

These findings highlight the importance of comprehensive adherence assessment in CF care. Relying on a single measure may misrepresent medication use, particularly in the CFTR modulator era, where treatment de‐escalation is increasingly common. Structured medication reviews support individualized treatment optimization by identifying unnecessary or burdensome therapies, addressing practical medication use problems, and aligning treatment with current clinical status.

Conclusion

Combining pharmacy refill data, self‐reported adherence, and structured medication reviews provides a more complete evaluation of adherence behavior and DRPs. Integrating pharmacist‐led medication reviews into routine CF care may support individualized treatment optimization while balancing treatment burden and sustained clinical benefit in the CFTR modulator era.
