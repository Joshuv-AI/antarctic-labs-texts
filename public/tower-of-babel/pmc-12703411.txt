Specific effects on the reproductive function including fertility relevant for cumulative risk assessment of pesticide residues
Authors: European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), Karine Angeli, Adeline Cavelier, Tamara Coja, Federica Crivellente, Anna Lanzoni, Luc Mohimont, Monica Nepal, Dimitra Nikolopoulou, Andrea Terron, Anna F. Castoldi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccording to the ‘EFSA‐SANTE Action Plan on Cumulative Risk Assessment for pesticides residues’, EFSA, with the support of a working group, undertook the identification of specific effects on male and female reproductive function, including fertility, that are considered relevant for grouping pesticide residues and for assessing retrospectively the cumulative risk (CRA) deriving from consumers' dietary exposure. In this first report, two specific effects leading to two correspondent cumulative assessment groups (CAGs) were identified for male reproduction, namely, (1) altered mating performance and (2) decreased fertility. Four distinct CAGs were proposed for grouping chemicals affecting female reproduction: (1) altered mating performance, (2) decreased fertility, (3) altered gestation and (4) altered parturition. EFSA also defined the list of indicators (i.e. toxicological endpoints measurable in regulatory studies) describing each specific effect. Qualitative and quantitative information on these indicators will be collected from the toxicological assessment reports on individual pesticide active substances: indicators will be used to determine the inclusion of prioritised active substances and their metabolites into the respective CAG and to estimate the no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) and lowest observed adverse effect level (LOAEL) of each chemical in relation to the given specific effect. Additional endpoints (named ancillary findings), not sufficiently informative to define a specific effect but contributing to the overall evidence, will be collected for a limited number of substances identified as risk drivers, based on hazard and exposure considerations, to support the estimation of CAG‐membership probabilities. Additional criteria for including active substances into CAGs, the hazard characterisation methodology and the lines of evidence for assessing CAG‐membership probabilities are detailed in this report, which was sent for public consultation. The process of data extraction and actual establishment of the CAGs are instead beyond the scope of this report. This part of the CRA process was outsourced and will be dealt with in a separate report.

SUMMARY

EFSA was requested to perform a retrospective dietary cumulative risk assessment (CRA) for pesticide residues targeting the reproductive function including fertility, according to the agreed 2021 ‘EFSA‐SANTE Action Plan on Cumulative Risk Assessment for pesticides residues’. Due to the complexity of the CRA process, the full cycle of this CRA, which covers the four pillars of risk assessment (hazard identification, hazard characterisation, exposure assessment and risk characterisation), will require several sequential steps. The outcome of the whole work will therefore be presented in four separate reports published at different times along the CRA process.

The present report developed by an EFSA Working Group (WG) focuses on the first CRA step, i.e. hazard identification around reproductive function including fertility. It aims at defining the specific effects (i.e. precise toxicological insults) on male and female reproductive functions including fertility, that are relevant for grouping prioritised pesticide active substances (a.s.) and/or metabolites into cumulative assessment groups (CAGs) and for assessing retrospectively the cumulative risks deriving from consumers' dietary exposure. This document also defines the indicators of each specific effect (i.e. toxicological endpoints measurable in regulatory studies) used for the allocation of a.s. and/or metabolites to the respective CAG. It also describes in detail the methodology for setting up the CAGs, for characterising the pesticide residues included in these CAGs and for assigning to each of them a CAG membership probability. Implementation of this methodology and actual establishment of the CAGs, from the data extraction onwards, are beyond the scope of this document.

According to the EFSA Scientific Committee's framework, the golden standard for grouping chemicals is a common mode of action (MoA) and/or adverse outcome pathway (AOP). Overall, 32 relevant AOPs were identified as relevant for reproductive specific effects from the AOPwiki (Version 2.7, released on 30 March 2024): of these, 21 are applicable to males, 10 to females and one, the AOP492, to both sexes. Unfortunately, none has been endorsed by the OECD except for AOP212 (Histone deacetylase inhibition leading to testicular atrophy).

Although an endocrine‐disrupting (ED) MoA has been postulated for substances for which the ED assessment is performed according to ECHA/EFSA Guidance, since this information (ED MoA or not) is not available for all substances, grouping based on ED MoA is not possible.

Considering the limitations of the relevant AOPs mentioned above and the lack of information on the MoA leading to effects on reproductive function and fertility for several substances, the selected strategy was to group a.s. and metabolites into CAGs based on common adverse outcomes (AOs) captured as apical toxicity endpoints in regulatory studies and referred to as specific effects in the present report. For the male reproductive function, two specific effects leading to two correspondent CAGs were proposed, namely (1) altered mating performance and (2) decreased fertility. Four CAGs were suggested for grouping chemicals affecting the female reproductive function, i.e. (1) altered mating performance, (2) decreased fertility, (3) altered gestation and (4) altered parturition.

A list of indicators describing each specific effect was compiled separately for males and females. Examples of indicators are e.g. ‘Delayed time to mating’ and ‘decrease in male/female mating index’ for the allocation of a substance to the two CAGs for the specific effect ‘altered mating performance’ in males and females, respectively. Furthermore, for the CAGs, ‘decreased fertility’ different indicators were selected for males and females. For example, in males, these indicators were decreased male fertility index, decreased number of implantation sites, decreased litter size, changes in sperm parameters and changes in testes histopathology. The full list of indicators identified for each specific effect on male and female reproductive functions is presented in a dedicated section of the report.

The methodology described in this report foresees that relevant qualitative and quantitative information and data on prioritised individual substances will have to be extracted from the toxicity studies included in the draft assessment reports (DARs), renewal assessment reports (RARs) and/or other similar regulatory documents. The list of prioritised individual substances has been developed from the list of pesticide a.s. quantified in at least one food sample of the official monitoring programme of EU Member States over a 3‐year monitoring cycle from 2019 to 2021; the EFSA prioritisation methodology (EFSA, 2024) was applied and reproductive toxicity checked as identified in an EFSA peer review (if not yet fully concluded, at least discussed and agreed in an Experts' meeting). In addition, the list of prioritised substances is complemented by pesticide metabolites present in food and that have a reproductive toxicity profile. Data and information will be collected in an ad hoc data collection template developed by the EFSA WG in collaboration with the Austrian Agency for Health and Food Safety (AGES, Vienna, Austria) under a framework partnership agreement. The presence of one or more indicator(s) of the respective specific effect in the data collection will be used as the main criterion to trigger the inclusion of a given prioritised a.s. and/or metabolite into the respective CAG. Each a.s. or metabolite included in a CAG will be characterised by the identification of an overall no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) and an overall lowest observed adverse effect level (LOAEL) for the respective specific effect based on all available data from relevant repeated dose oral toxicity studies. Further details on the criteria applied for including substances into CAGs and for the hazard characterisation methodology are given in the report.

It is acknowledged that the amount, reliability, relevance and consistency of the evidence causally linking a substance to a specific effect vary between different substances, making the conclusion on the substances that actually belong to a CAG uncertain. This uncertainty, i.e. CAG‐membership probability, can be quantified by a weight of evidence (WoE) approach, estimating the probability that any substance actually causes the specific effect. This will be assessed for a limited number of substances per CAG, so‐called ‘risk drivers’, 1 selected on the basis of hazard and exposure considerations. The list of risk driver a.s. will be defined at the exposure assessment step and the underlying methodology will be fully described in the exposure report (also see Appendix A, Table A.1, step 13).

A comprehensive list of lines of evidence (LoE), which were assigned by the WG a different level (on a 5‐degree scale) of strength, will be used to assess the CAG membership probabilities of risk drivers. For these substances, the data collection will be expanded to cover endpoints other than indicators, so‐called ‘ancillary’ findings. These refer to changes in male/female reproductive parameters (e.g. changes in reproductive organ weight) that in isolation are not sufficient to support the direct inclusion of an a.s. into a CAG. However, if they are detected in studies where at least one indicator has been observed, ancillary findings can support the indicator(s) and increase the likelihood that the substance has been correctly allocated to a CAG.

Some recommendations concerning research on AOPs, investing in toxicity testing, better data reporting and data analysis are also included.

This report was sent for public consultation (see Annex B). Accounting for the relevant comments received and further discussions in the WG, the specific effects of male and female reproductive function, their respective indicators and ancillary findings, the criteria for inclusion of a substance into a CAG, the hazard characterisation methodology, the lines of evidence for CAG‐membership probability and the data collection template were revised as appropriate and finalised for publication.

INTRODUCTION

Cumulative risk assessment (CRA) has been defined as the analysis, characterisation and possible quantification of the combined risks to health or the environment from multiple agents or stressors (U.S. EPA, 2003). It differs from most assessments which consider the effects of one agent or stressor in isolation.

In Europe, Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 on maximum residue levels (MRLs) in or on food and feed of plant and animal origin 2 and Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009 concerning the placing on the market of plant protection products 3 requires thorough consideration of cumulative and potential synergistic effects of residues of plant protection products (PPPs).

To comply with these provisions, in 2007, EFSA and the Panel on plant protection products and their residues (PPR Panel) started the development of the necessary methodologies to carry out CRA of pesticide residues. This methodological development included a tiered approach for the assessment of cumulative risks of pesticide residues (EFSA PPR Panel, 2008), a guidance on the use of probabilistic methodology for modelling dietary exposure to pesticide residues (EFSA PPR Panel, 2012) and a procedure to establish cumulative assessment groups (CAGs) of pesticides on the basis of their toxicological profile (EFSA PPR Panel, 2013). In the latter opinion, the PPR Panel concluded that synergistic effects are less likely to occur at low doses/concentrations corresponding to the dietary exposure to mixtures of pesticides and are thus less relevant for CRA of pesticide residues in food (EFSA PPR Panel, 2013). In addition, the limited available empirical evidence suggests that synergisms at dietary exposure levels are rather rare, and that they experimentally occurred at unrealistically high concentrations (Hernández et al., 2017).

In 2020, EFSA issued the first two reports on retrospective CRA of pesticides that have acute effects on the nervous system and chronic effects on the thyroid gland (EFSA, 2020a, 2020b). These were followed in 2021 by a retrospective CRA regarding chronic acetyl cholinesterase inhibition (EFSA, 2021) and in 2022 by a retrospective CRA regarding craniofacial alterations (EFSA, 2022). Ongoing work comprises the retrospective assessment of the cumulative risks from co‐exposure to pesticide residues on the kidney, and on the liver, as well as the repetition of the retrospective CRA of pesticides affecting the thyroid based on updated data on exposure and toxicology.

In 2021, EFSA and SANTE agreed on a long‐term action plan on CRA for pesticide residues (EFSA‐SANTE, 2021). This plan entails that all toxicological effects of pesticides of relevance for CRA have to be identified by 2030 and CAGs for the respective organs and systems have to be established. This plan relies on two main pillars: prioritisation of active substances (a.s.) and organs/systems and cooperation with Member States' competent organisations.

BACKGROUND INFORMATION FOR SETTING SPECIFIC EFFECTS ON THE REPRODUCTIVE SYSTEM INCLUDING FERTILITY RELEVANT FOR PERFORMING A DIETARY CUMULATIVE RISK ASSESSMENT OF PESTICIDE RESIDUES

Reproductive system: Anatomy, physiology and function in mammals

The reproductive system comprises an intricate network of organs and structures functioning collectively to ensure the continuation of the species. In mammals, the organisation, morphology and function of male and female reproductive organs exhibit differences.

Male reproductive system and fertility

During embryogenesis (early in the second week of gestation for the rodent), primordial germ cells (PGC or gonocytes) migrating from the yolk sac wall organise in gonadal chords. Under the guidance of SRY (sex‐determining region Y gene on the Y chromosome), these initiate to form testicular structures. Early Sertoli cells in the gonadal cords surround the centrally located gonocytes, and both multiply maximally during the second half of gestation. Leydig cells occur after cord formation, starting gestational day (GD) 15 in the rat with proliferation continuing to GD 19, followed by regression continuing to postnatal day (PND) 4. High levels of testosterone produced by the fetal Leydig cells influence reproductive organogenesis late in gestation.

Postnatal male reproductive system development varies among species, as the timing of puberty varies across species, and is characterised by spermatogonia, spermatocytes and spermatids development (for the rat, see Figure 2).

In the adult male, the overall function of the reproductive system is to produce gametes (spermatozoa) capable of fertilisation, with consequent production of viable offspring. The key organ in this process is the testis.

Testis is anatomically and functionally similar across the mammalian species, including humans. In the adult, the testis is located in the extra‐abdominal scrotal sac. It is covered by the thick, fibrous tunica albuginea and the visceral tunica vaginalis. The testis contains seminiferous tubules compactly arranged in convoluted loops; in the interstitium, among tubules, there are Leydig cells, macrophages, blood vessels and nerves and supporting stroma; the testis contains a fluid (interstitial testicular fluid) rich in protein and testosterone. The arrangement of seminiferous tubules slightly differs across mammalian species: in rodents, they are arranged circumferentially, whereas in dogs and primates, they are organised in lobules. The seminiferous tubules are lined by the seminiferous epithelium composed of basally located Sertoli cells supporting maturing germ cells; these are organised in synchronised populations at different maturation stages (spermatogonia, spermatocytes, round spermatids and elongating spermatids). Seminiferous tubules converge through the tubuli recti at each end of the tubular loops in the rete testis, which is continuous with the efferent ductules and epididymis.

The production of sperm through spermatogenesis and the synthesis of androgens are regulated by the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis, which is further elaborated in the following section (OECD Guidance Document (GD) No. 106, 2009; Creasy et al., 2013).

Once released from the seminiferous epithelium, spermatozoa reach the epididymis via the rete testis and ductules efferentes, where they complete maturation, acquiring motility as well as fertilising ability. The epididymis also plays crucial roles encompassing sperm transport, concentration, protection and long‐term storage in its tail (i.e. cauda epididymis) (Cornwall, 2009; Cornwall et al., 2007; Hermo & Robaire, 2002; James et al., 2020; Sullivan & Mieusset, 2016). The vas deferens serves as a conduit for sperm connecting the tail of the epididymis to the urethra.

The accessory sex glands include the prostate, coagulating gland (anterior prostate), seminal vesicle, bulbourethral gland and preputial gland. There are species‐specific differences in the presence and appearance of these glands. For example, ampullary and preputial glands do not have human counterparts. The human prostate is organised into distinct zones, whereas the rodent prostate contains multiple lobes and a pair of coagulating glands (anterior lobes) adjacent to the seminal vesicles (Ittmann, 2018). Accessory glands produce secretions that are added to the spermatozoa following sexual stimulation and during ejaculation, contributing to the formation of the seminal fluid.

Hormonal regulation of the male reproductive system (see Bennett et al., 2013; Clement & Giuliano, 2015; Dwyer & Quinton, 2019; OECD GD No. 106, 2009).

There are various levels of hormonal regulation of the reproductive tissues, encompassing the endocrine control involving the HPG axis, as well as another tier of regulation that involves autocrine and paracrine interactions among the Sertoli cells, germ cells, Leydig cells, peritubular cells, testicular macrophages and endothelial cells of the interstitial vasculature.

In the classical pathway, the cascade of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis involves a series of interactions among three main components: the hypothalamus, the pituitary gland and the testes. Gonadotropin‐releasing hormone (GnRH) is secreted by the hypothalamus, and it is transported through the bloodstream to the anterior pituitary gland (adenohypophysis), where it stimulates the synthesis and release of two key gonadotropins, namely the luteinising hormone (LH) and the follicle‐stimulating hormone (FSH), in a pulsatile manner. LH primarily acts on specific receptors expressed on Leydig cells to activate the production and secretion of sex hormones, particularly testosterone (the primary male sex hormone) and dihydrotestosterone (DHT). Testosterone exerts both intratesticular effects, specifically on spermatogenesis, and peripheral effects, which extend to accessory sex organs and non‐reproductive organs such as the brain, muscle, bone and skin. In many peripheral tissues, testosterone undergoes metabolism, including the conversion to oestradiol, which is a significant reaction in species other than rodents. Feedback inhibition of LH and hypothalamic GnRH is mediated by circulating levels of testosterone, DHT and oestradiol, with the relative importance of these molecules varying depending on the species. On the other hand, FSH primarily acts on the Sertoli cells within the testes, stimulating the growth and maturation of sperm cells within the seminiferous tubules. Prolonged activation of GnRH receptors can result in receptor desensitisation. In this pathway, testosterone exerts negative feedback on LH release, while inhibins and activins regulate FSH signalling and sperm production. This cascade of events within the HPG axis ensures the proper regulation of male reproductive functions, including sperm production and testosterone synthesis, which are crucial for male fertility and overall reproductive health (Figure 3).

Female reproductive system and fertility

The female reproductive system is a functional unit dedicated to the production of gametes, sperm transport and sperm–oocyte interaction leading to fertilisation, embryo–fetal development and delivery. It is composed of various elements with functional and endocrinologic interdependence on one another. It is characterised by cyclicity, i.e. under the initiation and regulation by the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian (HPO) axis, the reproductive tract shows well defined and sequential functional and morphological modifications.

Elements of anatomy

Anatomically, the female reproductive system includes the ovaries, oviducts or Fallopian tubes, uterus, cervix and vagina.

The ovary is the primary reproductive gland, producing the female gametes (eggs or ova or oocytes); it is paired and situated within the pelvic cavity on either side adjacent to the uterus. The outer portion of the ovary, the cortex, contains the ovarian follicles and corpora lutea, the inner or central portion, the medulla, contains interstitial glands, larger calibre blood vessels and nerve fibres. Ovarian follicles can be morphologically differentiated in primordial, primary, secondary, tertiary follicles and preovulatory (Graafian) follicles, representing different stages of maturation (see below).

Following ovulation, the released oocyte moves to the uterus through the oviducts or fallopian tubes (Avilés et al., 2015). These are paired tubes composed of the infundibulum (adjacent to the ovary, including the fimbriae), the ampulla and the isthmus (which joins the uterus). The fimbriae and infundibulum collect the oocyte and convey it to the ampulla where fertilisation typically occurs. Oviduct mucosa includes ciliated and non‐ciliated cells, along with mucous glands that facilitate the movement of the (fertilised) ovum towards the uterus and its nourishment.

The uterus is a fibromuscular organ, with variable shape and dimensions (weight and size) across the species and subjected to change during pregnancy and hormonal fluctuations. It is composed of three layers: the endometrium, lining the luminal surface and composed of a mucosa organising in tubular glands embedded in a specialised and highly cellular stroma; the myometrium, a smooth muscle layer with inner circular and outer longitudinal fibres; and the adventitia (Jones & Lopez, 2014).

Via the cervix, the uterus connects to the vagina, a distensible, thin‐walled tubular structure with a mucosa composed of a stratified epithelium, which undergoes changes across the oestrous cycle, serving as a reliable histologic indicator of the stage of the cycle in rodents (Sato et al., 2016; Westwood, 2008). The vulva is the external portion of the genitalia and surrounds the vaginal opening.

Follicular development in the ovary begins during the fetal stage when germ cells transform into oogonia, which develop into primordial follicles. These follicles remain dormant until puberty, when the pituitary gland secretes FSH and luteinising hormone (LH), triggering them to mature into primary and then secondary follicles. During this process, granulosa cells proliferate, and fluid‐filled cavities called antra begin to form, increasing oestrogen production. The follicles continue to grow into antral follicles, and eventually, the preovulatory or Graafian follicle reaches its peak size, leading to an LH surge that triggers ovulation. Post‐ovulation, the follicle transforms into the corpus luteum, which secretes hormones to prepare the endometrium for potential pregnancy. If fertilisation does not occur, the corpus luteum degenerates into the corpus albicans, marking the end of the cycle.

Fertilisation and pregnancy

If sperm is present in the fallopian tube and successfully fertilises the egg, the resulting zygote undergoes multiple divisions while progressing through the fallopian tube towards the uterus, whereas the blastocyst is prepared for embedding into the uterine mucosa (implantation). Successful implantation leads to pregnancy, where the blastocyst matures into an embryo and eventually a fetus. In the absence of fertilisation, the egg proceeds through the fallopian tube to reach the uterus, where in humans and non‐human primates, it disintegrates and sheds along with the uterine lining during menstruation.

Cyclicity and Hormonal Regulation of the Female Reproductive System (Boyd et al., 2018; Haschek et al., 2010; OECD GD No. 106, 2009; Vogazianou, 2019)

The female reproductive system shows cyclic functional and morphological changes (cycles); each cycle begins with follicular growth and maturation, followed by ovulation (release of the ovum from the follicle), and the subsequent formation and regression of the corpus luteum.

Cycling is regulated by the HPO axis, which coordinates hormonal signals through mechanisms of positive or negative feedback. The cyclic changes within the ovary are mainly regulated by LH and FSH, whereas the cyclic changes of the uterus and vagina are dependent on ovarian steroids.

The pulsatile secretion of GnRH from the hypothalamus stimulates the anterior pituitary to secrete FSH and LH, which in turn stimulate the development of ovarian follicles and the production of ovarian steroids. FSH stimulates the development of the follicular zona granulosa and triggers the expression of LH receptors by granulosa cells. LH stimulates the synthesis and secretion of androstenedione and, to a lesser extent, testosterone by the theca interna of the follicle; these androgens are utilised by granulosa cells as substrates in the synthesis of oestrogen. The rise in oestrogen levels suppresses the release of GnRH by the hypothalamus, as well as directly inhibits pituitary secretion of both LH and FSH. Negative feedback control of pituitary FSH secretion is also achieved by the peptide inhibin, produced by the granulosa cells of the maturing follicle as depicted in Figure 4.

Declining FSH levels cause follicular atresia of all the recruited follicles, except for the dominant follicle.

Once peak oestrogen levels are reached, GnRH and gonadotrophin secretion cease. At this point, oestrogen starts promoting again both hypothalamic GnRH release and anterior pituitary responsiveness to GnRH. This positive oestrogenic modulation of hypothalamic–pituitary function results in a preovulatory luteinising hormone‐releasing hormone (LHRH) surge and corresponding surge in LH which results in ovulation.

Following ovulation, the follicular granulosa and thecal cells undergo luteinisation to form the corpus luteum. This structure functions for a specific period known as pseudopregnancy. The corpus luteum initially secretes progesterone independently but requires stimulation by luteotropic hormones like LH and prolactin for sustained activity. Prolactin disrupts normal oestrus cycles by supporting the functional state of the corpus luteum. Progesterone secretion by the corpus luteum is crucial for endometrial development in preparation for potential embryo implantation and maintains vaginal mucification. If fertilisation does not occur, the corpus luteum regresses, allowing the return to normal reproductive cyclicity.

The oestrous cycle in rodents and the menstrual cycle in humans represent fundamental differences in reproductive physiology. Rodents, such as rats, experience a relatively short and frequent oestrous cycle lasting around 4–6 days. This cycle consists of distinct phases including pro‐oestrus, oestrus, metoestrus and dioestrus (see Table 1). During this cycle, multiple follicles mature simultaneously and ovulation results in the release of multiple ova, allowing for potential multiple pregnancies. In contrast, the menstrual cycle in humans spans approximately 28 days, characterised by phases of menstrual shedding, follicular (proliferative) and luteal (secretory) phases. While multiple follicles start to mature in the human ovary, typically only one dominant follicle fully matures and releases an ovum per cycle. This singular ovulation pattern aligns with the human reproductive strategy, emphasising a higher investment in each pregnancy and a longer reproductive interval compared to rodents (Boyd et al., 2018).

There is an absence of healthy (non‐atretic) tertiary follicles. Several smaller vesicular follicles start to grow.

Immature basophilic corpora lutea formed after the current ovulation have a central fluid‐filled cavity and no significant fibrous tissue proliferation.

There are no healthy large late tertiary follicles, but many growing follicles of various types are present.

The corpus luteum is characterised by a central fluid‐filled cavity, slightly basophilic cells and no fibrous tissue.

Tertiary follicles increase in number but are smaller than those observed at pro‐oestrus.

A large corpus luteum with fibrous tissue formation in the central cavity is observed.

Chemically induced alteration of the reproductive function including fertility

Exposure to toxicants can have a substantial impact on reproductive function by disrupting various sites, including the hypothalamus, pituitary gland and male and female reproductive organs/systems. These disruptions can occur through direct and indirect mechanisms and an effect on one site often leads to subsequent changes at other sites. Therefore, it is essential to comprehensively examine a broad range of endpoints, such as organ weights, histopathology, gamete parameters, hormonal measurements and fertility endpoints. This comprehensive approach increases the likelihood of identifying toxicity, distinguishing primary toxic effects from secondary consequences, and enhances the ability to detect isolated random changes that may not have biological significance.

Males

Chemical exposure may impact testicular, epididymal and male accessory sex gland morphology and/or function. Impact of chemical exposure on testes: Testicular toxicants can target specific cellular components within the testes (germ cells, Sertoli cells, Leydig cells as well as interstitial macrophages and blood vessels), with a common outcome being the disruption of the process of spermatogenesis. Germ cells can undergo degeneration and depletion through direct cytotoxic effects or hormonal effects. The distribution pattern of the stage and germ cell type affected may be used to infer the mechanism of the injury, and a careful examination of the seminiferous epithelium can provide valuable information. Cytotoxicity typically targets proliferating spermatogonia, mitotically active and unprotected by the blood‐testis barrier (e.g. ethane‐methane sulfonate causes necrosis of spermatogonia by irreversible DNA damage). Hormonal disruption can lead to a decrease in the production of androgens (such as testosterone) preventing the androgen‐dependent development of germ cells. Such condition can occur following the administration of oestrogens or high doses of testosterone (Greaves, 2012; OECD GD No. 106, 2009). Effects at the level of the hypothalamus and pituitary gland (e.g. determining LH suppression affecting Leydig cell function) or androgen receptor blockade can also induce decreased testosterone levels/effects. In the spermatogenic cycle, Stages IX–XII and Stages VII/VIII are more sensitive to the level of testosterone. Early effects of androgen deficiency are recognised by degeneration of round spermatids and pachytene spermatocytes in stage VII/VIII tubules of rats. Long‐standing conditions can result in overall depletion of the seminiferous epithelium and tubular atrophy. Sertoli cell toxicity manifests primarily through loss of function, as these cells are resistant to death, or morphologically as vacuolation. The molecular mechanisms underlying these changes include disruptions in cytoskeletal function, metabolic injury resulting in decreased metabolic support of germ cells and effects on Leydig cell steroidogenesis or paracrine factors, which secondarily affect Sertoli cell function (Creasy et al., 2012; Woldemeskel, 2017). Subsequently, germ cells undergo multifocal degeneration with the formation of multinucleate spermatid aggregates, retention of spermatids /delayed spermiation (e.g. Boric acid, dichloroacetic acid and bromoacetic acid), phagocytosis of spermatocytes (e.g. 1,3‐Dinitrobenzene tri‐o‐cresylphosphate) and exfoliation of germ cells into the seminiferous tubular lumen associated with spermatogenic disruption in the testis (e.g. sloughing of the adluminal portion of Sertoli cells along with their attached germ cells induced by colchicine or vinblastine). With prolonged exposure at sufficiently high doses of toxicant, the tubules may undergo atrophy with seminiferous epithelium's disorganisation and germ cell loss, lined only by Sertoli cells. If the injury is sufficiently severe or prolonged, Sertoli cell function is compromised and spermatogenesis is not possible any longer (Creasy et al., 2012). Other effects leading to impaired spermatogenesis can include fluid disturbances. Impaired fluid reabsorption in the efferent ducts/caput epididymis, distal obstruction in the excurrent duct system (rete testis, efferent ducts or initial segment), increased secretion of seminiferous tubule fluid by Sertoli cells or inhibition of peritubular contractions can result in increased seminiferous tubular fluid and fluid backpressure to the seminiferous epithelium, consequently leading to germ cell degeneration. Inadequate fluid reabsorption is observed e.g. following the administration of endothelin antagonists, the fungicide carbendazim and a 5HT agonist. On the contrary, excessive fluid reabsorption can result in sperm stasis/sperm granuloma (e.g. a‐Chlorohydrin) and blockage (Creasy et al., 2012; Creasy & Chapin, 2018; Piner et al., 2002). Vascular damage within the testis can lead to ischaemic injury varying in severity, depending on the compound involved and exposure conditions. Vasoactive compounds such as serotonin, histamine and epinephrine can induce vascular changes in the testis, typically resulting in less severe effects on germ cells and causing patchy tubular atrophy. In contrast, severe disturbances in the vascular endothelium, as caused by cadmium, impact all testicular components, including germ cells, Sertoli cells, Leydig cells, peritubular myoid cells and the interstitial vasculature. This vast damage leads to coagulative necrosis of all testicular structures and may be followed by secondary effects such as inflammation, fibrosis and mineralisation (Piner et al., 2002). Impact of chemical exposure on epididymis: The epididymis is a crucial organ for sperm maturation, and toxicants can significantly perturb its structure and function, leading to altered sperm maturation processes. To comprehensively demonstrate the impact of toxic insults on the epididymis, it is important to evaluate multiple epididymal segments (as lesions can be region‐specific, such as in the caput, corpus or cauda) and compartments (e.g. sperm vs. epithelium). Characterising toxicity in the epididymis is complex due to the potential for both direct and indirect alterations.Direct toxicants, such as methyl chloride and cyclophosphamide, can affect the structure and function of the epididymal interstitium and epithelium, altering luminal fluid composition and sperm quality. These chemicals can cause exfoliation of principal cells, changes in epithelial cell height in the caput and corpus regions, loss of clear cells in the proximal cauda, degeneration and necrosis (De Grava Kempinas and Klinefelter, 2015; Vidal & Whitney, 2014). Epididymal injury is commonly accompanied by inflammation, followed by the development of sperm granuloma (Creasy, 2001).Indirect toxic effects, such as androgen deprivation or secondary effects due to testicular toxicity, can impact the epididymis significantly. As an androgen‐dependent tissue, in the absence of adequate androgen level, the epididymis can experience several morphological changes such as decreased weight, presence of sloughed testicular germ cells in the epididymal lumen, reduced sperm content and ductal atrophy. Less commonly, epithelial single cell necrosis/apoptosis may occur in specific segments of the caput epididymis. This can result from the suppression of androgen synthesis in the testis, as seen with ethane methanesulfonate (EDS), or from direct inhibition of the androgen receptor in the epididymis, as with hydroxyflutamide (De Grava Kempinas & Klinefelter, 2015; Vidal & Whitney, 2014).However, both direct and indirect mechanisms can alter sperm transit and/or induce sperm stasis, impacting maturation or directly damaging sperm, compromising the epididymal sperm quantity and quality, such as sperm motility, sperm morphology and sperm composition. These endpoints contribute significantly to overall fertility (i.e. fertilising ability and normal embryo development; Klinefelter & Hess, 1998). Impact of chemical exposure on male accessory sex glands: The secretory function of seminal vesicles and prostate is androgen‐dependent and is susceptible to circulating testosterone concentrations. Decrease in organ weight with or without manifesting morphologically (from epithelium single cell necrosis/apoptosis to atrophy with reduction in acinar/vesicle lumina) and secretory activity can result from endocrine disruption mechanisms like a marked decrease in testosterone biosynthesis, LHRH receptor antagonism, androgen receptor antagonism and aromatase inhibition. Conversely, androgen receptor agonism may increase organ weight, possibly evident in increased acinar/vesicle size and secretion amount and epithelial hypertrophy/hyperplasia. Additionally, the secretory function may be affected by inhibitors of 5‐alpha reductase, which metabolises testosterone to dihydrotestosterone. As with Leydig cell atrophy, subtle changes can be difficult to recognise by qualitative morphologic evaluation, and organ weight is usually much more sensitive than histopathology for detecting decreased functionality activity at the endpoint. Worth to mention is inflammation, generally occurring as a response to tissue injury or necrosis associated with urogenital bacterial infections, that could impact on fertility (OECD GD No. 106, 2009).

Females

Mechanistically, chemicals can induce direct effects and indirect effects on the female reproductive system.

Direct effects include cytotoxicity to oocytes and/or granulosa cells [e.g. heavy metals, industrial chemicals (see Junaid et al., 1997)] and irradiation affecting small follicles, cisplatin and cyclophosphamide affecting medium or large antral follicles (see Plowchalk & Mattison, 1992). Also, direct effects are oestrogen‐ or progesterone‐like activity, such as exaggerated effects of endogenous 17b‐estradiol (E2) or progesterone (P4) due to increased serum concentrations or an imbalance of these two hormones. Morphological changes in this case can include atrophic or hypertrophic/hyperplastic changes, compatible with the induced changes in the hormonal milieu (see below) (Hooser et al., 1994). Inhibition of angiogenesis can also determine morphologic and functional alterations in the corpus luteum (decreased or increased in number, with central haemorrhage and degeneration) with secondary effects in the uterus and vagina (Hall et al., 2016, Patyna et al., 2008).

Indirect effects are in most cases due to the perturbation of the hormonal pathway and can be mediated via interference of hypothalamic–pituitary pathways, manifesting as ovarian changes with downstream effects on the reproductive tract (and mammary gland).

Chemically induced hormonal perturbations can manifest morphologically according to three main patterns (OECD GD No. 106, 2009): Atrophy of the ovary, uterus and vagina: It can be observed in the case of compounds exerting an antiestrogenic effect, or it can be caused by decreased gonadotropin production in the pituitary gland, decreased follicular development in the ovary or decreased ovarian steroidogenesis. Aromatase inhibitors are known compounds inducing such conditions. They can impair the conversion of androgens to oestrogens by the zona granulosa/theca cells of the ovarian follicles, with consequent reduced endogenous production of ovarian oestrogens and widespread histopathological atrophic changes (ovaries, uterus and vagina). In case of reduced production of ovarian oestrogens, impaired positive feedback to HPO could cause a disruption of the preovulatory LH source with ovarian increased follicular atresia and follicular cysts. Interstitial cell hypertrophy and hyperplasia may also occur following the administration of anti‐oestrogens due to increased levels of gonadotropins (Yuan & Foley, 2002). Ovarian atrophy, uterine and vaginal hypertrophy and hyperplasia: It can be noted in the case of compounds exerting oestrogenic effect. Such compounds could on one side determine negative feedback at the hypothalamic–pituitary level, with inhibition of the release of gonadotrophins and consequent ovarian atrophy; on the other side, they can stimulate the uterus and vagina, with organ hypertrophy/hyperplasia. Histopathological findings include ovarian atrophy, hypertrophy of the uterine luminal and/or glandular epithelium, myometrial hypertrophy and increased vaginal epithelial thickness with hyperkeratinisation (Biegel et al., 1998; Yamasaki et al., 2002). Compounds with oestrogenic effects include ethinyl oestradiol (oestrogen agonist) and tamoxifen/raloxifene (selective oestrogen receptor modulators) (OECD GD No. 106, 2009). Oestradiol valerate, a synthetic steroidal oestrogen, can also induce follicular cysts in association with high FSH levels and low LH (Grosser et al., 1987). Hypertrophy/hyperplasia of the vagina, uterus and ovary due to increased pituitary hormone concentrations. This can be noted with compounds with a gonadotropic or luteotropic effect. High gonadotropic levels trigger continuous follicle maturation and corpora lutea formation, increased ovarian activity with elevated oestrogen and progesterone levels and proliferative changes in the uterus and vagina. In the vagina, increased and persistent mucification can occur. High prolactin (PRL) levels lead to increased size and/or number of corpora lutea (persistent corpora lutea e.g. in mice and rats) and mammary gland lobuloalveolar hyperplasia. Example substances are analogues of LH, LHRH, FSH and prolactin, and dopamine‐depleting compounds (e.g. reserpine). Ethylene glycol monomethyl ether (EGME) also promotes luteal survival and progesterone production indirectly through upregulation of prolactin secretion (Taketa et al., 2011).

Regulatory assessment of pesticides for effects on reproduction and fertility

Test guidelines of reproductive function and fertility in regulatory settings

In the regulatory setting, reproductive function is largely assessed in reproductive toxicity studies provided to fulfil data requirements described in the legal text (e.g. Regulation (EC) 1107/2009, Regulation (EU) 283/2013, Regulation (EU) 528/2012), supported by all other available information including physicochemical properties, toxicokinetic data, in silico predictions and data on structural analogues, in vitro and in vivo animal data published in scientific literature, other general toxicity studies, medical records and epidemiological data.

Reproductive toxicity studies may include generational studies (OECD Test Guideline (TG) 415, OECD TG 416, OECD TG 443) as well as reproduction screening tests (OECD TG 421, OECD TG 422). In the TG 421, 422, 415, 416 and 443 studies, both sexes are treated, and therefore, it is usually not possible to determine whether an effect on reproductive performance is due to the female, the male or both without considering the other affected reproductive endpoints.

In the same studies, the observed effects may in theory result from the exposure in utero, or from in‐life exposure, at any time of the life. Therefore, unless it can be concluded from the affected endpoints and/or generation that the effects result for the exposure during a well‐defined exposure window, the cumulative exposure assessments will need to cover consumer populations of all ages. In the case that stratification could be done, this will be considered during the uncertainty analysis and supported by appropriate sensitivity analysis.

Challenges in regulatory tools investigating reproductive performance in regulatory assessments

A weight‐of‐evidence approach (WoE) is followed in the assessment of all available data to determine whether there is a pattern of adversity on sexual function and fertility. Reproductive toxicity studies are only performed in rodents, the rat being the preferred species in respective guideline studies. For animal welfare purposes and due to the large number of animals used in these studies, the EU legislation prohibits their repetition, unless a clear justification indicates that this is necessary (e.g. data available are not reliable and/or not sufficient to conclude). Inconsistency in the findings among available guideline studies or in comparison to findings reported in studies published in the open literature adds to the uncertainty which should be described and quantified, if possible. Deviations from the OECD TG requirements, such as missing measurements on some reproductive toxicity endpoints or poor reporting of the methodology and/or results, are additional factors to be considered in the uncertainty analysis and assessment of study reliability and adequacy. For instance, for some active substances, only a two‐generation reproductive toxicity study conducted according to a former version of the OECD TG 416, i.e. version from 1983, is available. Therefore, for these substances, some parameters relevant for detecting adverse effects on sexual function and fertility were not investigated, e.g. sperm parameters, oestrus cyclicity and follicle counts. More information on differences in the analysis of parameters between OECD 416 versions are explained in detail in Table 3 (males) and 6 (females). More weight is given to studies with higher reliability when drawing the overall conclusion.

Information on non‐rodent species may be obtained from the 90‐day (OECD TG 409, 1998) or 1‐year (OECD TG 452, 2018) repeated dose toxicity studies in the dog. However, it is often difficult to interpret the toxicological relevance of reproductive effects in the dog. In the 90‐day dog study, dosing could begin when animals are as young as 4–6 months, and therefore, at study termination, sexual maturity may not have been achieved. In pubertal dogs, spermatogenesis is relatively inefficient and not all tubules mature at the same time. As a result, the pubertal testes may contain few germ cells having undergone complete spermatogenesis but also some degenerating cells and the epididymes may only have few or no sperm in the lumen and often increased number of giant cells. Upon histological examination, it may be indistinguishable if the observed alterations (e.g. testicular atrophy, aspermia or low number of sperm count, giant spermatids) are due to sexually immature animals or to toxicity. In the female dog, vaginal cytology can be used to track the oestrous cycle, but the long duration and variability of the canine cycle makes this impractical in most nonclinical toxicology settings. Furthermore, reliable comparison of mean values of effects in reproductive parameters in treated dogs to the concurrent control mean values is hampered by the poor statistical power due to the low number of animals used in the study (typically 4 dogs/sex/group). For the 1‐year dog studies, similar considerations apply and confounding factors due to the long‐term treatment during the peripubertal period would still be present. Additionally, the 1‐year dog study is not a data requirement anymore. As a result of all these limitations in retrieving reliable reproductive toxicity information, studies in dogs are excluded from the parameters of male and female reproductive function and fertility described under Sections 2.3.2 and 2.3.3 and from data collection for the establishment of respective CAGs (see also Section 4.2.6).

The identification and characterisation of general systemic toxicity as also described in the revised OECD GD No. 150 (2018) (e.g. signs of animal stress, mortality, changes in body weight and food consumption) is critical in deciding whether a substance exerts direct effect(s) on the reproductive system at doses below those eliciting systemic toxicity, or the observed adverse reproductive effects are only evidenced at doses where overt systemic toxicity is observed, and thus, they may be considered secondary, non‐specific consequences of the poor animal condition and loss of regenerative capacity. The criteria used by ECHA (2024) to account for excessive systemic toxicity for classifying substances for reproductive toxicity are considered in this project as a pragmatic approach to conclude on the inclusion of a substance into a CAG (see Section 4.3).

In the context of endocrine disruption (ED) assessment, when the pattern of reproductive effects is deemed adverse, further investigation on the MoA is performed to conclude on the biological plausibility that the adverse effects are (exclusively) caused via an endocrine‐mediated MoA. Biological plausibility is stronger for the ‘EATS‐mediated’ parameters (including sperm motility/morphology/number, oestrus cyclicity, vaginal smears, reproductive organ weight and histopathology) in comparison to effects considered ‘sensitive to, but not diagnostic of, EATS parameters’ (including fertility, time to mating, conception, gestation length, dystocia, number of corpora lutea and implantations, number and maturation of ovarian follicles) (ECHA/EFSA guidance). In case there is no conclusive evidence on biological plausibility, or other MoA cannot be excluded, further empirical data need to be generated (see Section 3.1 on MoA/AOP) on the substance under evaluation to demonstrate the MoA and assist the assessment of toxicological significance and human relevance of the observed effect(s) (see also Section 2.4). MoA data may also be needed to assist interpretation of adverse effects on sexual function and fertility that are only observed in offspring when in adulthood. In case similar findings are not observed in parental generation animals, developmental origin cannot be excluded. As previously stated, currently available OECD test guidelines on reproductive toxicity studies (see Table 2) and repeated dose short‐term toxicity studies (OECD TG 407, 408, 409) do not include measurements of male and female reproductive hormones and/or other mechanistic information, which are only available on a case‐by‐case basis (e.g. in the context of ED assessments or to assess human relevance).

Detailed testicular histopathology examination (e.g. using Bouin's fixative, paraffin embedding and transverse sections of 4–5 μm thickness) is conducted on the P and F1 males (cohort 1A and if needed, cohort 1B) in the EOGRT study (OECD TG 443, 2018) and the revised OECD TG 416 (2001), as well as the revised 90‐day oral toxicity study in rodents (OECD TG 408, 2018), in order to identify treatment‐related effects on testis differentiation and development, and on spermatogenesis, including retained spermatids, missing germ cell layers or types, multinucleated giant cells or sloughing of spermatogenic cells into the lumen (OECD GD No. 151, 2013). Details on the performance of fixation and histological examination are included in the OECD GD No. 106 (2009). In addition, detailed histological examination of the testes is essential in study protocols where the pre‐mating dosing period in males is limited (e.g. OECD TG 421, OECD TG 422), and therefore, fertility may not be a particularly sensitive indicator of testicular toxicity. Although detailed histological examination of testes is foreseen in several OECD test guidelines, the information is often missing from pesticide risk assessment dossiers, either because older versions of the study protocols were used (e.g. OECD TG 416 from 1983, OECD TG 408 from 1998) or because the study protocol is not part of the core data requirements (e.g. OECD TG 421, OECD TG 422). Overall, the data that are present in the RAR are expected to reflect the standard at the time when the study was performed. Detailed histopathological examination of the ovaries covering the follicular, luteal and interstitial compartments of the ovary, as well as the epithelial capsule and ovarian stroma (OECD GD No. 43, 2008) is not foreseen in the available OECD TGs and may be provided only when ovaries are target organs.

Interpretation of reproductive findings in regulatory toxicological studies and their relevance to humans

The ultimate aim of regulatory toxicological studies is the determination of potential hazard to human reproduction. Although alternative test methods can be expected to play an increasing role in the assessment of chemicals' reproductive toxicity, only animal models with hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis integrity are intrinsically capable of capturing the complex biological processes involved in reproduction and account for absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion of the tested chemicals. Reproductive processes are highly conserved through mammalian species and the rat has been the species of choice for multigenerational testing studies for decades.

In respect to reproductive physiology similarities between humans and rats, effects on fertility in rodents seem to be a good indicator for effects in humans and by default the relevance to humans should be assumed, unless the opposite has been demonstrated (OECD GD No. 43, 2008). However, differences between rat and human in anatomy, physiology and timescales in normal biological processes must be taken into consideration when translating reproductive findings in rat to the human condition. A major difference to be considered is that humans are of relatively low fertility and thus may be at greater risk from reproductive toxicants than rats. The use of multiple endpoints in reproductive toxicity studies and the standard uncertainty factor of 10 for interspecies variability are expected to cover for such differences.

Predictivity of the rat model for men fertility

As reported by Mangelsdorf et al. (2003), male rats and rabbits remain fertile even when their sperm count is approximately 10% and 1% of control levels, respectively. In humans, sperm concentration is lower than in rodents and even a small decrease would lead to fertility issues. Indeed, fertility is considerably reduced in men with sperm count being 30% of the physiological level. Male rats have a large excess of spermatozoa in their ejaculates. Therefore, effect on fertility index alone in a rat reproductive toxicity study is not a sensitive indicator to predict fertility in humans. As such, additional parameters should be included in the overall assessment of male fertility. A highly sensitive endpoint is histopathology of the testes, preferably using a refined method describing different stages of spermatogenesis. Other sensitive endpoints are sperm parameters such as sperm count, sperm morphology and sperm motility. The weights of reproductive organs including accessory glands, i.e. testis, epididymis, prostate, seminal vesicles provide useful additional information of confirmatory nature. Regarding sperm parameters, sperm motility was found to be in some cases more sensitive than histopathology because direct effect on the epididymis or the sperm can affect motility (OECD GD No. 43, 2008). Also, changes in sperm morphology and sperm counts should not be discarded in the absence of histological lesions (OECD GD No. 43, 2008). Overall, changes in histopathology and/or sperm parameters should be considered as indicative of a potential effect on fertility in humans even if fertility in rats is not affected.

Predictivity of the rat model for women fertility

While for both rats and humans the primordial follicle stock serves as a reserve for the entire reproductive life span of the adult, menopause in women is the consequence of follicular exhaustion within the ovary, while in rats reproductive senescence results from initial centrally mediated changes with alterations in hypothalamic function (Finch, 2014; Gore et al., 2000; Vidal, 2017). In reproductive studies in rats, a change in follicle number often precedes a change in organ weight or histopathology. The magnitude of the reduction in the number of follicles will determine whether there is an effect on reproductive performance (OECD GD No. 43, 2008).

In respect to the particular concern of a depletion of follicle reserves in women (premature ovarian insufficiency, premature menopause), decreased follicle counts observed in rat studies should be considered as indicative of a potential effect on fertility in women even if no other reproductive endpoint is affected.

ADME considerations for the predictivity of animal models to human fertility

Although possible species‐specific differences in pharmacokinetics parameters (including metabolism) may occur, adverse effects on reproductive function observed in rats are assumed to be predictive of the same effect in humans in the absence of data proving the contrary. For active substances, no data on human ADME parameters are generally available. A comparative in vitro metabolism study, conducted on animal and human test systems, is required for each active substance. The results of this study may be used to determine the human relevance of adverse effects observed in animal species in a weight of evidence approach. However, a species‐specific effect can only be tentatively attributed to the formation of a ‘unique’ metabolite and further mechanistic studies on the plausibility of the hypothesis are needed (EFSA PPR Panel, 2021).

DATA AND METHODOLOGIES

Methodology for hazard identification: Specific effects and indicators of reproductive function

Definitions of ‘specific effect’, ‘indicator’ and ‘ancillary finding’

In the context of this report, a specific effect is defined as the precise toxicological insult that can result from a combined action of pesticides and is relevant for grouping pesticides into CAGs for CRA (EFSA PPR Panel, 2013). The criteria set by the EFSA PPR Panel (2013), i.e. adversity, systemic and unambiguous nature of the effect (primary effect, MoAs/Adverse Outcome Pathways (AOPs)) and relevance to humans, are applied by the WG by expert judgement to identify specific effects on reproductive function based on relevant toxicity endpoint(s), i.e. indicator(s). It should be noted that ‘specific effects’ are not necessarily described by a single toxicity endpoint but rather by a set of endpoints that make up an effect of greater biological complexity, e.g. decreased fertility.

An indicator of a specific effect is a toxicological endpoint identifiable in toxicological studies reflecting the specific effect and useful for characterising the toxicological potency of the pesticide causing that effect. A single or a set of indicators can be used to identify a specific effect. These parameters will be collected for all substances.

An ancillary finding is a change in a male/female reproductive parameter that in isolation does not trigger the direct inclusion of an active substance into a CAG. However, since it can support the indicator(s), it can increase the likelihood that an active substance has been correctly allocated to a CAG. Ancillary findings will only be collected for risk drivers (a.s. identified on the basis of hazard and exposure considerations among the list of prioritised substances) in a second step of the data collection as additional evidence that supports CAG membership probability.

A risk driver is defined as a pesticide/commodity combination contributing, in Tier II of the cumulative exposure calculation, at least 5% of the cumulative exposures exceeding the 99th percentile estimate in at least one of the assessed populations. For detailed information on the methodology used to identify risk drivers, see EFSA (2022).

Hazard‐driven criteria for grouping pesticides into cumulative assessment groups (CAGs)

Among all possible effects of chemicals on the reproductive function including fertility, those relevant for grouping pesticides into CAGs were identified using hazard‐driven criteria as advised by the guidance of the EFSA Scientific Committee on scientific criteria for grouping chemicals into assessment groups for human risk assessment of combined exposure to multiple chemicals (EFSA Scientific Committee, 2021).

Mechanistic information on endocrine disruption from ToxCast

Several specific effects used to develop the CAGs for reproductive toxicity are also used for the assessment of the endocrine‐disrupting properties of pesticide active substances and can therefore benefit not only of the currently available or under development AOPs but also of the regulatory documents developed to help in their identification. The OECD GD 150 (2018) and the ECHA/EFSA ED GD (EFSA, 2018) indicate which of these specific effects should be considered as a likely consequence of the disruption of an endocrine pathway, based on current toxicological and endocrinological knowledge. In addition, they propose a methodology based on the AOP/MOA conceptual framework to contextualise the available evidence for postulating the most likely affected endocrine modality. Although this is restricted to the oestrogenic, androgenic and steroidogenesis (EAS) pathways, where feasible, allocation of substances can be refined based on a postulated MOA, even if the available information is only the specific effect. Additional substance‐specific mechanistic information can be retrieved from the U.S. EPA dashboard ToxCast programme which is offering high‐throughput screening data for the assessment of the EAS activities that can be used as an alternative for regulatory guideline studies. Several pesticides are included in the database and for several substances in addition to the high‐throughput screening data, computational models for the E and A modalities are also available. Along the regulatory peer review pesticides authorisation process, several active substances have been assessed for their endocrine‐disrupting properties following the ECHA/EFSA ED GD. This information can be used for the identification, characterisation and MOA analysis of specific effects affecting reproduction through an endocrine‐mediated MOA and this can also in a mechanistically based refinement of the groups, if necessary.

Using common toxicological effects to identify specific effects

Considering the limitations of the relevant AOPs mentioned above and the lack of information on the MoA leading to effects on reproductive function and fertility for several a.s. and metabolites, the WG considered appropriate to group the a.s. and metabolites in the respective CAGs based on the common adverse outcomes captured as apical toxicity endpoints in regulatory studies, when these can be reasonably assumed to result from a molecular initiating event (MIE) or/and KEs taking place in the reproductive system.

The specific effects (i.e. toxicological effects, AOs) of pesticides on male and female reproductive function and fertility, and their indicators, were defined by the WG using scientific knowledge and expert judgement.

Methodology for substance prioritisation

To minimise the resources needed for the establishment of CAGs and to focus on the a.s. predominating in food, EFSA developed and implemented a prioritisation methodology (EFSA, 2024). In brief, this methodology is based on short‐ and long‐term exposure assessments for each individual pesticide quantified in at least one food sample of the official monitoring program of EU Member States over a 3‐year monitoring cycle from 2019 to 2021. These assessments were performed by probabilistic modelling in 30 population groups, covering different age groups (adults, children and toddlers) in 17 countries. The threshold to prioritise a pesticide was an acute or chronic hazard quotient (HQ), ratio between the acute or chronic exposure from monitoring studies to the acute reference dose (ARfD) or acceptable daily intake (ADI), respectively, equal to or exceeding 0.1 (10% of the reference value) at P99.9 of the exposure distribution in at least one of the 30 population groups under consideration. This resulted in a priority list of 67 substances, of which 21 reached the threshold in acute exposure assessments only, 15 in long‐term exposure assessments only and 31 in both acute and long‐term exposure assessments.

The REPRO CRA will not cover effects due to acute exposure. The WG recognised that, mechanistically, acute exposure can influence the outcome of some specific effects. However, the WG is of the opinion that separation of acute vs. chronic exposure determining the effect is not practically feasible and only chronic exposure will be used based on the study designs. In addition, the WG recognised that setting of ARfD is generally not based on reproductive (i.e. fertility) endpoints. Only 46 substances will be considered from the list of prioritised substances based on long‐term exposure. The WG noted that the metabolites of these prioritised substances are not included in the monitoring studies, and therefore, they could be missed. Therefore, any metabolite of these 46 prioritised substances that is spotted in the available documentation and considered as a reproductive toxicant will be included in the data collection. EFSA will check if relevant reproductive toxicity data have in the meantime been submitted on these substances and whether these data have been peer reviewed (in an expert meeting).

In the context of this work, it is acknowledged that the definition of the MTD (maximum tolerated dose), commonly taken as a reference point to determine whether an effect is direct or secondary to other systemic effects, is challenging and a matter of debate, since it can vary depending on the endpoint considered and the regulatory framework.

As a pragmatic approach, the WG decided to consider the criteria used by ECHA for classifying substances for reproductive toxicity (see Guidance on the Application of the CLP Criteria Version 6.0 – January 2024 https://echa.europa.eu/documents/10162/2324906/clp_en.pdf/58b5dc6d‐ac2a‐4910‐9702‐e9e1f5051cc5, p. 402).

Specifically, this Guidance states: ‘All findings on reproductive toxicity should be considered for classification purposes irrespective of the level of parental toxicity. A comparison between the severity of the effects on fertility/development and the severity of other toxicological findings must be performed.’……………..

‘Adverse effects on fertility and reproductive performance seen only at dose levels causing marked systemic toxicity (e.g. lethality, dramatic reduction in absolute body weight, coma) are not relevant for classification purposes. There is no established relationship between fertility effects and less marked systemic toxicity. Therefore, it should be assumed that effects on fertility seen at dose levels causing less marked systemic toxicity are not a secondary consequence of this toxicity. However, mating behaviour can be influenced by parental effects not directly related to reproduction (e.g. sedation, paralysis), and such effects on mating behaviour may not warrant classification……..’

Consequently, in this context, the WG concurred that an indicator will always trigger the inclusion of a substance into a CAG unless it is observed at doses causing excessive toxicity (e.g. death, severe prostration of the animal, dramatic reduction in absolute body weight, coma, sedation, paralysis).

In addition, limitations reported by the authors of the source documents and identified during the data collection and flagged as impacting the validity of the study, should also be considered in order to conclude if such limitations might have an impact on the allocation of a substance into a CAG.

Based upon all considerations, a substance can be included in one or several CAGs.

Instructions for the hazard characterisation of the substances included in the CAGs

This section illustrates the methodology to be followed for characterising each substance included in a CAG.

Each active substance or metabolite included in a CAG will be characterised by the assignment of an overall no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) and an overall lowest observed adverse effect level (LOAEL) for the respective specific effect based on the totality of the available oral toxicity studies.

All the indicators are considered equally relevant for the setting of the NOAEL/LOAEL of the respective specific effect, although they may be weighed differently for CAG membership probability (see CAG membership probability under 7).

All available information across studies, species, generations and sexes will be considered. The use of sub‐acute studies (e.g. 28‐day studies, preliminary range‐finding reproductive toxicity studies) can be considered to define a NOAEL and a LOAEL only in the case that these studies are sufficiently powered (i.e. with at least 10 animals/sex/group).

In each single study, a NOAEL/LOAEL for the specific effect will be identified. In case a study failed to identify a NOAEL and only provided a LOAEL for an indicator(s) of interest, a NOAEL will be derived from this LOAEL by applying a default uncertainty factor (UF) of 10. Although the EFSA guidance on default values to be used in the absence of measured data (EFSA Scientific Committee, 2012) recommends defining the size of such UF on a case‐by‐case basis, the concerned studies will not be reassessed, and, as a systematic approach, the same default UF will be used unless a more specific extra UF is already used in the assessment report. This conservative UF will then be taken into account in the uncertainty analysis.

In view of the setting of the overall NOAEL/overall LOAEL for the specific effect, the most sensitive species is identified as the species in which the lowest LOAEL for any indicator has been observed for that specific effect. This lowest observed LOAEL is adopted as the overall LOAEL of the substance. The overall NOAEL of the substance, to be used in the exposure calculations, is the corresponding NOAEL of the same study.

Merging of studies to account for dose spacing for the same indicator should only be considered, in the rare case of repetition of studies in the same laboratory which only differ by dose selection (e.g. equivalent quality, comparable time of conduct, experimental design (same OECD TG version), same species, strain, administration oral route (e.g. studies performed by gavage cannot be combined with studies where the substance has been administered in the diet), same indicators), and given that it is properly justified.

BMD analysis

Once the risk drivers are identified from the cumulative exposure calculations, benchmark dose (BMD) analysis might be applied, if data allow, in the case a refinement of these calculations is needed. This would be the case, for instance, if the total margin of exposure (MOET) 6 at the 99.9th percentile of the exposure distribution is in the range of the threshold for regulatory consideration.

Instructions for CAG membership probability

Introduction

The amount, reliability, relevance and consistency of evidence for causing the specific effects listed in Tables 9 and 10 (Section 4.1) vary between substances, with some being more likely to actually belong to a CAG than others. This can be quantified by assessing the probability that any substance actually causes the specific effect. In this report, this probability is referred to as CAG membership probability. While in principle the CAG membership probability should be assessed for all substances included in a CAG, this process is resource‐consuming (see below) and experience has shown that it has limited impact, since the outcome of a CRA is in most cases driven by a limited number of substances.

CAG membership probability will therefore be assessed only for the substances emerging as risk drivers from the cumulative exposure assessment. It should be noted that CAG composition will not be changed as a result of this exercise, even if the outcome is a low probability for the substance to belong to a given CAG. In case some risk drivers show a low CAG membership probability, this will likely result in an overestimation of the risk that will be considered in the UA and risk characterisation steps of the CRA.

CONCLUSIONS

The present report mainly defines the specific effects for male and female reproductive function and the relative indicators (i.e. toxicological endpoints measurable in regulatory studies) for the allocation of pesticides (a.s. and/or metabolites) to the respective CAGs; it is therefore focused on hazard identification.

Two specific effects and homonymous CAGs were proposed for male reproduction, namely (1) Altered mating performance and (2) Decreased fertility, while 4 distinct CAGs were suggested for grouping chemicals affecting female reproduction based on (1) altered mating performance, (2) decreased fertility, (3) altered gestation and (4) altered parturition.

A list of indicators describing each specific effect was defined as a main criterion that triggers the inclusion of a given a.s./metabolite into the respective CAG. More refined inclusion criteria and a hazard characterisation methodology were elaborated by the WG for being implemented in the next step of the CRA process by AGES.

Finally, this report illustrates the LoE for assessing the CAG membership probability, namely the probability that the a.s./metabolites included in the CAGs actually cause the effect of interest.

This report was sent for public consultation (see Annex B). Taking into consideration the relevant comments received and further discussions in the WG, the text and the key decisions taken were revisited as needed and finalised for final publication. The actual establishment and characterisation of the CAGs, from the data extraction onwards, will instead be performed in a second step by AGES and published separately.

RECOMMENDATIONS

General recommendation: In the absence of fully defined and validated AOPs, the proposed specific effects on reproductive function including fertility are exclusively based on adverse outcomes observed in regulatory studies. As AOPs can provide valuable information for the CRA of pesticides altering the reproductive function and in line with the indications of EFSA Scientific Committee (2021), it is recommended to further develop AOPs by focusing on the adverse outcomes that are described in the pesticide data set.Ongoing research on the development of an AOP network (AOPN) on mammalian reproductive toxicity arising from disruption of the EAS modalities is expected to assist the identification of knowledge gaps that can be prioritised for further AOP development (Zilliacus et al., 2024). Initiatives such as the Methods2AOP conceptual data model coordinated by the EU Reference Laboratory for alternatives to animal testing (EURL ECVAM), aim at linking test methods to key events within AOPs and at promoting a broader use of AOP‐based approaches in regulatory risk assessments (Wittwehr et al., 2023). In addition, ongoing research on weight of evidence for cross‐species conservation of androgen receptor‐based biological activity (Vliet et al., 2023) and the advancement of cross‐species extrapolation of the effects of chemicals in regulatory toxicology (Lalone et al., 2021) may facilitate the human relevance assessment of available AOPs. A recent initiative aims at developing a roadmap to define a NAM‐based integrated testing strategy (ITS) to meet DART regulatory requirements (Fritsche, 2024).
