Pulmonary versus extrapulmonary tuberculosis among diagnosed tuberculosis cases in Bangladesh: Evidence from a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Md Mayin Uddin Hasan, Mohammad Injamul Hoq, Saifur R Chowdhury
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTuberculosis remains one of the primary infectious diseases in Bangladesh that affects both adults and children. Poor diagnostic criteria and insufficient reporting, especially of pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) and extrapulmonary tuberculosis (EPTB) in children, limit the prevalence estimation, resulting in insufficient preventive strategies. This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to estimate the pooled proportions of PTB and EPTB among pediatric and adult diagnosed tuberculosis cases in Bangladesh, and to summarize the associated factors.MethodsWe searched PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Library, and BanglaJOL to retrieve articles published between January 1, 2000, and May 31, 2025. Eligible studies included retrospective studies that reported PTB and/or EPTB prevalence in pediatrics (<18 years) or adults (≥18 years) diagnosed tuberculosis cases in Bangladesh. We used the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist for Prevalence Studies to assess the quality of the studies. Random-effects models estimated pooled proportions and I2, τ2, Cochran’s Q, and meta-regression assessed heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses compared proportion by age group.ResultsEleven studies (6 pediatrics, 5 adults) met the eligibility criteria. In patients with tuberculosis, the overall pooled proportion across all ages was 44.4% for PTB (95% CI: 35.9–53.3%; I2 = 93%) and 47.1% for EPTB (95% CI: 34.0–60.7%; I2 = 95%). Among pediatric populations, the pooled proportion of PTB was 44.5% (95% CI: 35.9–53.4%; I2 = 76%), while EPTB accounted for 55.5% (95% CI: 46.6–64.1%; I2 = 77%). In adults, PTB proportion was 44.2% (95% CI: 28.8–61.2%; I2 = 97%), and EPTB proportion was 37.4% (95% CI: 17.0–63.6%; I2 = 97%). Subgroup analysis revealed no significant difference in the proportions of PTB and EPTB between pediatric and adult groups. The most notable reported associations with PTB and EPTB in pediatrics included young age, exposure to tuberculosis patients, and male sex and, in adults, elderly age, female sex, low socioeconomic status, low education level, and comorbidities.ConclusionsFindings suggest a high proportion of PTB and EPTB in diagnosed tuberculosis cases in Bangladesh, with a relatively higher proportion of EPTB in children than adults. Key associated factors included exposure to tuberculosis patients, sex, low socioeconomic status, and other related comorbidities. Strengthening diagnostic capacity, expanding contact tracing, and addressing the associated factors through targeted public health interventions are necessary to enhance tuberculosis control efforts.Keywords: Pulmonary tuberculosis, Extrapulmonary tuberculosis, Tuberculosis, Prevalence, Bangladesh

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis remains a significant health problem and causes a considerable burden of infectious diseases, with millions of deaths annually [1]. Pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) is a chronic, highly contagious bacterial disease that usually involves the lungs but can also affect other organ systems, leading to extrapulmonary tuberculosis (EPTB) [2], [3].

Globally, the burden of TB remains substantial. Over the last few decades, it has been among the top ten causes of death in the world. In 2020, it was estimated that 10 million new TB cases were reported, which increased to 10.6 million in 2024, along with approximately 1.3 million attributed deaths, including 167,000 cases of dual infection with HIV [4], [5]. Children represent a particularly vulnerable group. In 2021, they constituted about 11% of all TB cases but accounted for 14% of TB-related mortality [6]. In 2021, 1.2 million children and young adolescents developed TB, with 209,000 deaths [7]. Although TB is preventable and curable, it has been a significant public health issue, especially in low- and middle-income countries, mainly because of multidrug-resistant (MDR-TB) and extensively drug-resistant tuberculosis (XDR-TB) strains [8], [9]. The highest TB burdens are observed in Sub-Saharan Africa, Eastern Europe, and South Asia [5].

Bangladesh ranked seventh in the list of countries with the highest burden of TB in the world, with 379,000 new cases of TB reported in 2022. The World Health Organisation has designated Bangladesh as a high-burden country for multidrug-resistant/rifampicin-resistant TB (MDR/RR-TB) for the 2021–2025 period [10], [11]. Since introducing the Directly Observed Treatment, Short-Course (DOTS) strategy in 1993, significant progress has been made in controlling TB, including higher case detection rates and better treatment outcomes [12]. As a result, prevalence declined from 870 to 79.4 per 100,000 between 1987 and 1988 and 2007–2009 [13], [14]. However, the burden remains 5–6 times higher among the poor and rural population and fivefold higher in the population from the lowest versus the highest socioeconomic quartiles, with many low-income individuals still disconnected from formal TB programs [15], [16].

Recent studies in Bangladesh have defined heterogeneous epidemiological patterns of TB among different demographic and socioeconomic groups. EPTB is mainly seen in people aged 25–45 years and is more common in females [17], [18], [19], while PTB is more common in older people and has a higher male prevalence [17], [20]. Socioeconomic status, housing conditions, poor ventilation, overcrowding, and biomass fuel use are known determinants of high TB risk [20], [21].

Variability in diagnostic practices and underreporting, especially in pediatric and EPTB cases due to their unusual presentation, are barriers to effective disease control [22]. The lack of consolidated epidemiological data, particularly for pediatric and EPTB cases, hinders effective policy-making, clinical management, and research planning. This highlights the need for a systematic review and meta-analysis to synthesize existing evidence and provide robust, consolidated prevalence estimates. This information would be invaluable for strengthening the National TB Control Program in Bangladesh, improving clinical decision-making, and improving health outcomes.

This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to estimate the pooled proportion of pulmonary and extrapulmonary TB among pediatric and adult populations diagnosed with TB in Bangladesh.

Methods

The study protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251136922). To ensure methodological rigor, the reporting of this review was aligned with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 [23].

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they reported the prevalence of either PTB or EPTB among pediatric (aged < 18 years) and/or adult (aged ≥ 18 years) diagnosed tuberculosis cases in Bangladesh. We considered original research articles with cross-sectional, retrospective, descriptive, and mixed-method designs. Only studies published in English that provided extractable prevalence data were included. Studies were excluded if they were case series, qualitative studies, reviews, editorials, or commentaries. Prevalence estimates lacking adequate methodological descriptions or data to derive them were also excluded. Records and reports containing duplicate data were also filtered out.

Search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive literature search to identify relevant studies reporting the proportions of PTB and EPTB among diagnosed TB cases in Bangladesh. Four electronic databases—PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Library, and BanglaJOL (a database of Bangladeshi scholarly articles)—were systematically searched for articles published from January 1, 2000, to May 31, 2025. The search strategy included combinations of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords such as “tuberculosis,” “pulmonary tuberculosis,” “extrapulmonary tuberculosis,” “pediatric tuberculosis,” “adult tuberculosis,” and “Bangladesh.” Boolean operators “AND” and “OR” were used to combine search terms effectively across population, condition, and geographic filters. Reference lists of included studies were also manually reviewed to identify additional eligible publications. The PRISMA flow diagram illustrates the selection process (Fig. 1).

A Prisma Flow diagram of the selection process of the study.

Study selection process

Search results retrieved from the databases were imported into EndNote version 20.0 (Clarivate LLC, 2022, USA), where duplicate records were systematically identified and removed. The study selection process was carried out in three stages: identification, screening, and eligibility assessment. Two independent reviewers (MUH and MIH) screened all retrieved records. Titles and abstracts were first assessed for eligibility, followed by a full-text review of studies that met the initial criteria. Any disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached.

Data extraction

Two independent reviewers, MIH and MUH, extracted data on a standardised Excel sheet designed for this study. The retrieved information included the name of the first author, publication year, study setting, study design, total sample size, participants' age range, proportion of PTB and EPTB (in percentage), and the diagnostic methods used to establish TB cases. The extraction phase was discussed and agreed upon, with any discrepancies resolved by a third reviewer.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Prevalence Studies. Designed to evaluate nine major areas, including sampling strategy, sample size adequacy, study setting, measurement reliability, and statistical analysis, this tool enables each study to be rated as high (≥80%), moderate (60–79%), or low quality (<60%) based on the percentage of criteria fulfilled (See Supplementary Table 2). After the two reviewers independently reviewed the scores, they reconvened and discussed the differences. This checklist was adapted from a previous meta-analysis conducted in Bangladesh [24].

Statistical analysis

We estimated the overall pooled proportion of PTB and EPTB among diagnosed TB cases, combined across adults and children, and separately for adults and children. The methodology established by Neyeloff et al. (2012) was followed for the meta-analysis to estimate the pooled proportions of PTB and EPTB among diagnosed TB cases across different subgroups [25]. A random-effects model (DerSimonian-Laird method) was used to estimate pooled proportion, accounting for between-study variability. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test, the I2 statistic (with values > 75% indicating substantial heterogeneity), and between-study variance (τ2). Subgroup analyses were conducted to compare TB proportions between pediatric and adult populations, and differences between subgroups were tested using chi-square (χ2) tests under random-effects models. Substantial heterogeneity (I2 > 75%) was addressed through multivariable meta-regression, with Study Setting (Tertiary vs. Peripheral) and Diagnostic Method (Bacteriological vs. Clinical) as moderators. The R2 statistic was used to quantify the proportion of between-study variance explained by these methodological factors. Publication bias was evaluated using Egger’s regression test, Begg’s rank correlation test, the trim-and-fill method, and Rosenthal’s fail-safe N. Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the robustness of the pooled proportion estimates for PTB and EPTB. All statistical analyses and visualisations, including forest and funnel plots, were conducted using R software (version 4.4.3) and Python (version 3.12).

Results

Study selection

Initially, 329 records were retrieved from the databases, including 302 from international databases and 27 from BanglaJOL. After removing duplicates, 177 articles remained for title and abstract screening, of which 158 were excluded for not reporting prevalence data or not meeting other inclusion criteria. Thirty-nine full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 24 were excluded for various reasons (e.g., inappropriate study type, such as review articles, insufficient data, or reporting outcomes not relevant to pulmonary or extrapulmonary tuberculosis). Finally, 11 studies were included in the quantitative synthesis (meta-analysis), with six conducted in pediatric populations and five in adult populations. Most studies were conducted in five of the eight administrative divisions of the country, with the highest concentration in areas surrounding the capital, Dhaka (See supplementary Fig. 1).

Studies in Pediatrics

Six studies (n = 749) on pediatric tuberculosis diagnosed cases were eligible. Most were conducted in urban healthcare settings, primarily at Dhaka Shishu Hospital and the Bangladesh Medical University outpatient departments in Dhaka, with one study at Sir Salimullah Medical College. All studies included children aged 0–18 years and reported prevalence estimates for PTB and EPTB, providing insight into the pediatric TB burden in major urban healthcare facilities in Bangladesh (Table 1).

Studies in adults

Five studies (n = 1286) on diagnosed adult tuberculosis were eligible. Most were conducted in urban Dhaka facilities, including Shyamoli Chest Clinic and Bangladesh Medical College Hospital, while the others were in rural or semi-rural areas, such as Netrokona, Tangail, Mymensingh, Kishoreganj, and multiple districts, including Rajshahi, Chapai Nawabganj, Sirajgonj, Moulvibazar, and Chittagong. These studies reported pulmonary TB prevalence, with several also reporting the adult TB burden across Bangladesh (Table 1).

Quality assessment of included studies

Methodological quality assessment using the JBI Critical Appraisal Tool showed variability across the included studies. Among pediatric studies (n = 6), two were rated as high quality (7/8, 87.5%), three as moderate quality (5/8, 62.5% to 6/8, 75%), and one as low quality (4/8, 50%). Among adult studies (n = 5), one achieved a high-quality rating (8/8, 100%), three were of moderate quality (5/8, 62.5% to 6/8, 75%), and one was of low quality (4/8, 50%) (Supplementary Table 2).

Proportion of pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) in diagnosed tuberculosis cases

Across all participants (n = 2,035), 946 cases of pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) were reported. Based on the random-effects model, the pooled proportion of PTB across pediatric and adult populations was 44.4% (95% CI: 35.9%–53.3%). Substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies (I2 = 93%, τ2 = 0.3299, p < 0.01). Among 749 pediatric participants, 343 cases of PTB were reported. The pooled proportion of PTB in the diagnosed pediatric population was 44.5% (95% CI: 35.9–53.4%). Heterogeneity across studies was substantial, with I2 = 76%, τ2 = 0.1551, and p < 0.01. Among 1,286 adult participants, 609 cases of PTB were reported. The pooled proportion of PTB in adults was 44.2% (95% CI: 28.8–61.2). Considerable heterogeneity was noted, with I2 = 97%, τ2 = 0.5876, and p < 0.01. Subgroup analysis indicated no difference in PTB prevalence between pediatric and adult groups, with a subgroup test result of χ2 = 0.00, df = 1, p = 0.98 under the random-effects model (Fig. 2).

Proportion of extrapulmonary tuberculosis (EPTB) in diagnosed tuberculosis cases

Across all participants (n = 2,035), 851 cases of extrapulmonary tuberculosis (EPTB) were reported. Using the random-effects model, the pooled proportion of EPTB across pediatric and adult populations was 47.1% (95% CI: 34.0%–60.7%). Heterogeneity was considerable (I2 = 95%, τ2 = 0.8227, p < 0.01). Among 749 pediatric participants, 406 cases of EPTB were reported. The pooled proportion of EPTB in pediatric populations was 55.5% (95% CI: 46.6–64.1%). Substantial heterogeneity was observed, with I2 = 77%, τ2 = 0.1551, and p < 0.01. Among 1,286 adult participants, 445 cases of EPTB were reported. The pooled proportion of EPTB in adults was 37.4% (95% CI: 17.0%–63.6%). Substantial heterogeneity was observed, with I2 = 97%, τ2 = 1.4570, and p < 0.01. Subgroup analysis showed no statistically significant difference in EPTB prevalence between pediatric and adult groups under the random-effects model (χ2 = 1.63, df = 1, p = 0.20) (Fig. 3).

Meta-regression

A multivariable meta-regression was performed to investigate the substantial heterogeneity observed (I2 > 90%). The analysis identified Diagnostic Method as a significant driver of EPTB prevalence (p = 0.004), explaining 56.47% of the between-study variance. In contrast, PTB prevalence was not significantly influenced by the study setting or diagnostic approach (see Supplementary Table 6).

Assessment of Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s regression test, Begg’s rank correlation test, the trim-and-fill method, and Rosenthal’s fail-safe N. Egger’s test showed no evidence of significant funnel plot asymmetry (t = 0.45, p = 0.66), indicating a low likelihood of small-study effects. Begg’s test also provided a consistent result (τ = –0.38, p = 0.12). The trim-and-fill method suggested a slightly more negative adjusted effect estimate, although heterogeneity remained high. Rosenthal’s fail-safe N indicated that additional null studies would not change the non-significant result (See Supplementary Table 3). The funnel plot appeared symmetrical (See Supplementary Fig. 4).

Reported factors associated with tuberculosis in Pediatrics and adults

Reported associations for tuberculosis in children commonly included young age (particularly under 10 years), male sex, and contact with TB-infected individuals.

Socioeconomic determinants such as urban residence, poverty, overcrowding, and limited access to healthcare were also frequently reported. In adults, reported associations included smoking, low educational and income levels, unemployment, comorbidities (e.g., HIV, diabetes, renal disease), and poor living conditions. Environmental and healthcare-related issues were also noted, including inadequate ventilation, delayed diagnosis, and overcrowding in households or healthcare facilities. While both groups were affected by social and environmental vulnerabilities, adults were strongly associated with behavioral and comorbidity-related factors (See Supplementary Table 7).

Discussion

Proportions of pulmonary and extrapulmonary tuberculosis in diagnosed tuberculosis Cases

This systematic review and meta-analysis estimated the pooled proportions of PTB and EPTB among diagnosed TB cases in Bangladesh. The estimated pooled proportion was 44.4% (95% CI: 35.9%–53.3%) for PTB and 47.1% (95% CI: 34.0%–60.7%) for EPTB. These findings reflect diagnostic and reporting practices for PTB and EPTB in Bangladesh, not the true distribution of these diseases. This high proportion of EPTB is notable compared to other developing and developed countries, highlighting a unique epidemiological profile in Bangladesh.

Bangladesh has a significantly higher proportion of EPTB among diagnosed TB cases compared to both developing and developed countries. In developing countries in South Asia, such as India and Pakistan, PTB is more prevalent than EPTB among diagnosed TB cases. In India, national estimates suggest that EPTB accounts for 15–20% of diagnosed TB cases, with PTB comprising 80–85% [35]. A hospital-based study in central India reported 73.53% PTB and 26.47% EPTB, while a study in Mangalore found 58.33% PTB and 41.67% EPTB [36], [37]. A similar observation based on national surveillance data in Pakistan indicates that EPTB accounts for about 20% of notified cases, whereas a multicentric study reported 29.2% EPTB and 70.8% PTB [38]. These differences show how population-level surveillance data and hospital-based studies identify different patient groups: surveillance systems might underreport EPTB because they depend on pulmonary diagnostic pathways, whereas hospital-based datasets could overrepresent severe or unusual cases. Nonetheless, in both types of studies, PTB remains more common than EPTB.

High EPTB proportions are also observed in the Mediterranean region of Saudi Arabia, ranging from 28% to 32%, with significant heterogeneity among EPTB subtypes [39]. A study at Assuit Chest Hospital in Egypt reported EPTB in 64.14% of TB patients, higher than in PTB [40]. Similarly, a Turkish study reported 72% EPTB cases compared to 17.6% PTB cases [41]. Again, these hospital-based datasets may reflect referral bias toward more complex cases.

Comparisons with high-income countries must also consider migrant status as a major epidemiological confounder. A review of 1,270,896 TB cases in 32 EU/EFTA countries (1995–2017) showed that 45.2% of TB cases among migrants were EPTB, compared with 21.7% in non-migrants; the highest proportion was observed in Northern Europe (41.8%) and the lowest in Eastern Europe (17.4%). Migrants from Southeast Asia had the highest risk of EPTB (∼62%) [42]. And in the United States, EPTB accounts for 19–22% of diagnosed TB cases, with PTB ranging from 74 to 76% [43], [44].These patterns indicate that higher EPTB proportions in high-income settings mainly result from the demographic composition of TB cases rather than inherent differences in disease epidemiology. The higher proportion of EPTB in Bangladesh may be attributed to several factors. The limitations of diagnosis, i.e., the paucibacillary nature of childhood disease and the logistical challenges of obtaining sputum specimens, can lead to an over-classification of EPTB compared to pulmonary tuberculosis (TB) [8]. Socioeconomic factors like poverty, malnutrition, and high population density likely contribute to the elevated EPTB burden [9]. Though the prevalence of HIV in Bangladesh is low, HIV co-infection is a well-documented predisposing factor to EPTB and may be especially pertinent in some populations [10]. In contrast, India and Pakistan, which have more developed programmatic infrastructures, have higher proportions of pulmonary TB, which may indicate better diagnostic sensitivity of pulmonary cases [35], [38]. In high-income environments, such as the United States, improved diagnostic tools, most notably molecular assays, including the Xpert MTB/RIF platform, enable accurate distinction between PTB and EPTB, thereby leading to parity in their proportions elsewhere [44].

Variations in the methods used to diagnose tuberculosis across countries make it difficult to compare disease prevalence. Investigative procedures are also highly variable in Bangladesh. Some studies continue to rely on sputum smear microscopy, whereas others have adopted more sensitive methods, such as GeneXpert, which affects case detection rates and introduces heterogeneity [45]. India also exhibits heterogeneous practices; national surveys are generally based on standardized criteria, but regional studies can use divergent diagnostic criteria [35]. Similar trends are observed in Pakistan, with significant differences in access to and the quality of diagnosis between urban and rural areas [38]. In the United States, the uniform use of advanced diagnostics results in better diagnostic accuracy, but can also overlook subclinical cases [43]. These differences in diagnostic approaches, along with differences in access to healthcare and reporting mechanisms, help explain the differences in the proportions of pulmonary and extrapulmonary tuberculosis reported.

Age-Specific differences

The meta-analysis revealed distinct age-specific patterns in TB presentation in Bangladesh. Among pediatric populations (aged < 18 years), EPTB was more prevalent at 55.5% (95% CI: 46.6%–64.1%) than PTB at 44.5% (95% CI: 35.9%–53.4%). In contrast, among adults (aged ≥ 18 years), PTB was more common at 45.5% (95% CI: 31.9–59.9) than EPTB at 36.9% (95% CI: 16.8%–62.9%).

Pediatric epidemiological data in Pakistan show that the most common extrapulmonary tuberculosis (EPTB) localizations in children were lymphatic nodes (72.4%) and the abdominal cavity (19.9%). Weight loss was also identified as a significant predictor of EPTB in the 0–4-year-old (adjusted odds ratio: 2.80, 95% confidence interval: 1.05–7.47, P = 0.039) and 10–14-year-old (adjusted odds ratio: 2.79, 95% confidence interval: 1.28–6.07, P = 0.010) age groups [46]. At the same time, a Danish and Greenlandic study reported that age, sex, and ethnicity regulate the prevalence of EPTB. The highest incidence was found among Somali people (56.5%), followed by Asian participants (58.5%), and Danish patients accounted for only 21.4% of EPTB cases [47].

The high prevalence of Extrapulmonary Tuberculosis (EPTB) among children in Bangladesh is consistent with the global trends, where youths are more susceptible to EPTB due to unique immunological characteristics and limitations in diagnosis [45], [48]. Pediatric disease is often paucibacillary, which may make it harder to establish PTB, thus leading to a relative overdiagnosis of EPTB [45]. Environmental exposures, including close contact with infectious adults 57 also influence the anatomic distribution of disease in children. Conversely, adults have a greater prevalence of Pulmonary Tuberculosis (PTB); this preponderance can be explained by greater exposure to infectious sources and behavioral risk factors (such as smoking), which are significantly more common in this age group [49].

Associated factors

In the pediatric population, reported associated factors were very young age (particularly below ten years), male sex, direct contact with TB-infected persons, poverty, poor housing conditions, high population density, urban residence, and poor access to health care. These factors are the basis of the high burden of EPTB in children, since the majority of EPTB types are paucibacillary and thus challenging to diagnose [45], [48]. In adult populations, reported associated factors were smoking, low educational and income levels, unemployment, comorbidities like diabetes and renal disease, poor living conditions, inadequate ventilation, delayed diagnosis, and occupational factors. Adults play a more significant role in behavioral determinants and comorbid conditions, which is why PTB is more prevalent [49]. These results are similar to those found in high-burden countries like India and Pakistan, where poverty, overcrowding, and HIV co-infection are still major reported associations [35], [38]. The TB burden is worsened by high population density and inadequate access to healthcare in Bangladesh [45]. The increasing prevalence of diabetes, a known risk factor, further contributes to the TB burden [49]. To mitigate these reported associations, it is necessary to improve socioeconomic conditions, expand access to healthcare, implement infection prevention strategies, and coordinate the management of comorbidities through health services.

Strengths and limitations

The systematic review is the first to comprehensively examine the proportions of PTB and EPTB among diagnosed TB cases in Bangladesh, incorporating studies conducted across various populations and geographic locations. Adherence to PRISMA guidelines ensures methodological rigor and reproducibility [23]. Variability and bias are addressed in random-effects models, and quality appraisal tools, such as the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist, increase the reliability of findings [24]. However, limitations include substantial heterogeneity (I2 > 90%), which is a recognised characteristic of prevalence meta-analyses due to variance compression in large samples [50]. To rigorously address this, we employed a random-effects model and meta-regression, consistent with standard methodological recommendations [51]. Our meta-regression suggests that over half of the heterogeneity in EPTB prevalence is driven by the shift from clinical to bacteriological diagnostics. This highlights the 'diagnostic gap' in earlier studies and suggests that the true burden of pediatric EPTB in Bangladesh may be higher than previously estimated when advanced molecular tools are unavailable. The reported associations rely on heterogeneous observational data, often lacking multivariable adjustment. Because we did not weight by study quality or use pooled estimates, these factors should be viewed as descriptive correlations rather than causal risk factors. Variations in prevalence likely reflect differing diagnostic criteria (sputum smear vs. GeneXpert) and potential diagnostic substitution bias in children. Specifically, the high pediatric EPTB proportion (55.5%) may reflect the difficulty of confirming paucibacillary pulmonary TB rather than true biological predominance. These findings should therefore be interpreted with caution [52]. The scarcity of pediatric data is a particular issue, given the high burden of EPTB among children.

Conclusions

This systematic review and meta-analysis highlight a substantial burden of both PTB and EPTB in Bangladesh, with pooled proportions estimates of 44.4% (95% CI: 35.9%–53.3%) and 47.1% (95% CI: 34.0%–60.7%), respectively in diagnosed TB cases reflect broad patterns across diverse study settings, populations, and diagnostic approaches rather than precise or population-level prevalence estimates. However, the findings should be interpreted cautiously, given the considerable heterogeneity across studies. EPTB is higher in pediatric populations (55.5%) compared to adults (37.4%), a disparity likely driven by diagnostic challenges and social determinants such as poverty and inadequate healthcare access. The persistent TB burden may be largely attributable to improved case detection through advanced diagnostic tools, such as GeneXpert, rather than a genuine increase in incidence. Addressing this challenge will require expanding access to modern diagnostics—especially for children—tackling socioeconomic barriers, and integrating TB control programs with the management of comorbid conditions. Future research should focus on longitudinal investigations and pediatric TB to accelerate progress toward the WHO End TB Strategy goals.
