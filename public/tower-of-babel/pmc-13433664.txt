Predicting and analyzing the dielectric constant of polyvinyl alcohol reinforced with different nanofillers using machine learning modeling
Authors: Behzad Vaferi, Mohsen Dehbashi, Ali Hosin Alibak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) is a widely used polymer in many applications. The nanoparticles’ incorporation into the polyvinyl alcohol structure can significantly modify the electrical characteristics by benefiting the properties of both polymer and nanofillers. Although the dielectric constant of PVA-based nanocomposites has been comprehensively measured, no general predictive model currently exists for its estimation. So, this study develops a robust, data-driven framework to predict the dielectric constant of PVA nanocomposites as a function of nanofiller type, filler concentration, temperature, and frequency. First, a comprehensive dataset of 1698 experimental measurements was gathered from the literature, covering nanofillers including CuO, TiO2, ZnO, Al2O3, GO, V2O5, SrTiO3, and PbO. Feature relevance was first analyzed using multiple linear regression, followed by the implementation and comparison of six machine learning models, i.e., categorical boosting, gradient boosting (GradBoost), extreme gradient boosting, least-squares support vector regression, multilayer perceptron neural network, and adaptive neuro-fuzzy inference system. Among these, the GradBoost model demonstrated superior predictive performance and generalization capability. The GradBoost model achieved high accuracy in cross-validation (1359 samples) with AARD = 3.38%, RMSE = 3.49, MAE = 0.83, and R = 0.99926, and maintained strong performance on the external test set (339 samples) with AARD = 9.32%, RMSE = 4.24, MAE = 1.76, and R = 0.99877. The proposed model represents the first generalized predictive tool for estimating the dielectric constant of PVA-based nanocomposites across multiple nanofillers and operating conditions. This approach provides a practical and accurate alternative to experimental study, enabling accelerated design and optimization of polymer nanocomposites for electronic and dielectric applications.

Introduction

The dielectric constant (relative permittivity) εr (Eq. 1) is a ratio of the electric permittivity of a material ε to the electric permittivity of vacuum ε0.1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ e_{{\mathrm{r}}} = e/e_{0} $$\end{document}

A material with a high dielectric constant has better electrical insulation properties and is suitable for electrical and electronic applications where preventing electrical leakage is crucial [1]. The dielectric constant is a fundamental material property that governs the energy storage capacity of dielectric materials [2]. The electric energy density (uE) in linear isotropic materials is defined based on Eq. Materials with high dielectric constants are better choices for storing electrical energy and use in related devices [3]. This is particularly important in power electronics [4] and energy harvesting [5] systems. Changes in dielectric constant can be exploited in sensors [6], where variations can indicate changes in environmental conditions [7], like humidity and pressure. This property allows for the development of sensitive and responsive sensing tools.

Although polymers are among the most utilized materials in daily life and industry, their dielectric constants are not high enough [8] and generally range from 2 to 10 [9]. This weakness becomes more critical due to the undesired impact of this small dielectric constant value on the polymer’s performance in various applications, from energy storage to sensing and electromagnetic interference shielding.

So, researchers added different nanoparticles within the polymers’ matrix to fabricate nanocomposites with a significantly high dielectric constant value [10–13]. The presence of nanoparticles can enhance interfacial polarization effects [14], finally improving the overall dielectric response of nanocomposites [15]. Therefore, by changing the type [16] and concentration [17] of nanoparticles within the polymer matrix, engineers can tailor the dielectric properties to meet specific application requirements, enhancing performance in targeted areas. The literature approves that the dielectric constant of nanocomposites varies with the polymer [18] and nanofiller [19] types, nanofiller concentration within the polymer matrix [20], and the frequency of the applied electric field [21].

Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) is characterized by its superior film-forming capabilities, biodegradability, and chemical stability [22, 23]. It is possible to significantly improve the PVA dielectric constant by adding zinc oxide (ZnO) [24, 25], graphene oxide (GO) [26], alumina (Al2O3) [27], vanadium oxide (V2O5) [28], Strontium titanate (SrTiO3) [29], copper oxide (CuO) [30], lead oxide (PbO) [31], and titanium dioxide (TiO2) [32] nanofillers to its structure. PVA-based nanocomposites maintain enhanced dielectric and mechanical properties [33] and align with environmental sustainability goals [34]. The PVA-based nanocomposites are versatile materials with many applications in electronics [35], biodegradable packaging [36], environmentally friendly energy storage [37], biomedical engineering [38], electronic textiles [39], water treatment [40], and pollution control [41].

Studies have shown that embedding ZnO nanostructures into the PVA matrix elevates its dielectric response due to the synergistic effect of ZnO’s semiconducting properties and PVA’s insulating nature, making these composites suitable for various applications [42]. Incorporating green synthesized zinc sulphide (ZnS) nanoparticles in the PVA body has enhanced structural, morphological, thermal, and dielectric properties under different frequency and temperature ranges [11]. Using GO to reinforce PVA/polypyrrole blend exhibits heightened dielectric constants and reduced dielectric losses, positioning them as promising materials for high-frequency or embedded capacitors [43]. The effect of graphene addition into the PVA matrix has also been explored, and its potential application in flexible dielectric materials design has been observed [44]. Moreover, adding phosphotungstic acid (PTA) into the PVA matrix results in chemically crosslinked nanocomposites with considerable improvements in electrical and dielectric properties, which are suitable for proton-conducting applications [45]. Incorporating up to 50 wt% of TiO2 nanoparticles in PVA has demonstrated a doubling in PVA’s dielectric constant without a concurrent increase in leakage current [46]. Research on PVA-based nanocomposite films containing CuO nanoparticles has revealed a significant reduction in the optical band gap of PVA, with implications for new energy states and enhanced optical dielectric constant [47]. Using Eeonomer 200F® as a nanofiller has notably improved PVA’s thermal stability and mechanical properties, demonstrating its utility in high-k dielectric material for embedded capacitor applications [48]. Additionally, PVA//polystyrene sulfonic acid reinforced with carbon black nanoparticles exhibits enhanced dielectric performance, suggesting their suitability as high-k materials for flexible energy storage applications [49]. Hybrid polymer nanocomposites’ structural, electrical, and optical properties for potential use in optoelectronic and dielectric applications have been investigated [50].

Furthermore, the study of PVA-based nanocomposites in energy harvesting and storage applications has gained considerable attention [51]. Integrating piezoelectric nanoparticles like barium titanate (BaTiO3) into the PVA matrix has been explored for its potential in energy-harvesting devices [52]. Such nanocomposites have enhanced piezoelectric properties, essential for converting mechanical energy into electrical energy and for self-powered sensors [53] and wearable electronics [54].

The literature has heavily focused on experimental measurement of the dielectric constant of the neat PVA as well as PVA-based nanocomposites. On the other hand, no empirical or intelligent models have been proposed to anticipate the dielectric constant of PVA-based nanocomposites. Since the dielectric constant of nanocomposites is a multidisciplinary problem and depends on thermal, electrical, and nanomaterial properties [24–32], the conventional regression methods are not reliable enough to simulate this problem. Therefore, the current study relies on applying several traditional and tree-based machine learning paradigms to correlate the dielectric constant of neat PVA and PVA-based nanocomposites with nanofiller type, nanofiller concentration within the PVA matrix, frequency, and temperature. Such straightforward models can monitor the effect of thermal, electrical, and nanomaterial properties on the dielectric constant of PVA-based nanocomposites without wasting time and expense.

Literature data for the dielectric constant of PVA-containing nanocomposites

A wide-ranging and valid databank composed of the numerical values of input and output variables of a given system is required to construct any data-driven model. Section 3.2 will outline that this databank is needed for cross-validation and testing phases.

All the experimentally measured values for the dielectric constant of the PVA polymer and PVA-containing nanocomposites have been collected from the literature [24–32] and utilized for the model development. The literature has already measured the dielectric constant of the PVA polymer and PVA-containing nanocomposite containing various nanofillers, i.e., ZnO [24, 25], GO [26], Al2O3 [27], V2O5 [28], SrTiO3 [29], CuO [30], PbO [31], and TiO2 [32]. The literature reveals that the dielectric constant of PVA-based nanocomposites depends on nanofiller type, nanofiller dosage in the PVA structure, temperature, and frequency [24–32]. Since the type of nanofillers is qualitative, the molecular weight has been used to represent them quantitatively. The PVA polymer is considered an imagined nanocomposite with zero percent of nanofillers.

Methods

Least-squares support vector regression

Least-squares support vector regression (SVR) is a supervised machine learning method that applies a kernel function (linear, quadratic, polynomial, tangent hyperbolic, and Gaussian) to transfer independent variables to a multi-dimensional computational space [56]. This transformation tactic helps linearly correlate the dependent variable (y) to its influential features (x) based on Eq. Multi-layer perceptron neural network

Adaptive neuro-fuzzy inference system

In the simplest form, an adaptive neuro-fuzzy inference system (ANFIS) can be viewed as a combination of an ANN and fuzzy logic. This hybrid method is also classified as a supervised learning algorithm. In its topology, the ANFIS model includes five successive and interconnected layers, each one having a specific role, e.g., fuzzification, interference, normalization, interpolation, and output. The cluster radius and optimization algorithm are two important hyperparameters of the ANFIS model that must be appropriately determined.

Categorical boosting algorithm

As another ensemble learning algorithm, categorical boosting (CatBoost) works similarly to the GradBoost decision tree [62]. The GradBoost uses the label encoding tactic to implement the categorical variables. However, CatBoost applies the target encoding strategy to convert categorical features into numerical representations without needing extensive preprocessing. Like the GradBoost method, the CatBoost also builds an ensemble of decision trees stage-wise, where each tree is trained to minimize the residual errors from the previous trees.

Extreme gradient boosting algorithm

Extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) [63] handles regression tasks by building an ensemble of decision trees in a stage-wise mode, where each tree is trained to estimate the residuals of the previous trees’ predictions. Like the GradBoost, the XGBoost starts with an initial prediction, typically the average value of the target variable in the training phase. Then, it iteratively adds new trees to minimize the deviation between actual and estimated target values by the gradient descent technique. Cross-validation and testing stages

Cross-validation and testing are essential stages in developing almost all machine learning algorithms [64]. During cross-validation, data-driven models learn the relationship between output and input variables. An optimization algorithm adjusts the model parameters using known input and output values, while appropriate hyperparameters are selected through trial-and-error or search-based methods. In this study, 80% of the dataset (1359 out of 1698 samples) is used for five-fold cross-validation.

Furthermore, the testing phase assesses a trained model's ability to generalize to new situations not observed during cross-validation. The model uses the input variables from unseen datasets to predict the target, allowing for a comparison between the estimated and actual values. This study used 20% of the collected data (339 out of 1698 datasets) for testing.

Results and discussion

Relevancy checking

Multiple linear regression (MLR) is a practical approach to infer the relevance between dependent and independent variables in multi-input and single-output (MISO) systems from their historical datasets [68]. This method correlates output and input variables in terms of a linear equation. The sign and magnitude of the coefficients of this linear equation show the direction and strength of an inherent correlation between output and input variables [68]. This study applies the MLR technique to distinguish the direction and strength of dielectric constant dependency on nanofiller type, nanofiller concentration within the PVA matrix, temperature, and frequency.

Since the molecular weight of nanofillers (Mw), nanofiller dosage in the PVA body (ω), temperature (T), logarithm of frequency (Lf), and dielectric constant (ε) have different magnitudes, they are first scaled into a similar range, i.e., [0 1]. Measured and predicted frequency dependences of pure and SrTiO3-doped PVA dielectric constant at 298 K [29]

Both experimental and simulated results indicate that the dielectric constant of the involved materials decreases with increasing frequency. This behavior is due to different mechanisms such as polarization, charge carrier mobility, and free charge motion [70, 71], which vary with applied electric field frequency. Typically, at low frequencies, the dielectric constant is usually higher due to the slower response of dipoles and charge carriers to the electric field. As frequency increases, there is often a decrease in the dielectric constant. This is due to the inability of dipoles to respond quickly enough to the changing field, resulting in reduced polarization.

It can also be seen that the SrTiO3 concentration in the PVA matrix has a complex impact on the dielectric constant. Indeed, the dielectric constant decreases by increasing the concentration of the SrTiO3 nanoparticles up to 10 wt%, and it then increases by increasing the nanofiller dosage in the PVA structure. This behavior may be described by the fact that at low concentrations, the dielectric constant tends to increase steadily due to enhanced polarization at the filler–polymer interface [72]. Upon reaching a critical concentration (percolation threshold), there can be a sharp increase in the dielectric constant due to the formation of conductive pathways. Excessive filler loading can lead to agglomeration, which may reduce the overall dielectric properties by disrupting the uniformity of the composite.

Figure 10 illustrates the simultaneous impact of nanofiller dosage and frequency on the dielectric constant of the pure PVA and PbO-doped PVA using both experimental [31] and modeling profiles. Limitations of the current work

Although the proposed model demonstrates strong predictive capability for estimating the dielectric constant of PVA-based nanocomposites, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the model relies entirely on previously published experimental data, which may introduce inconsistencies due to variations in measurement techniques, sample preparation methods, and reporting standards across different sources. Although outlier detection was performed, hidden biases or systematic errors in the literature data may still affect model generalization. Second, the input features are limited to nanofiller type, concentration, temperature, and frequency; other potentially influential parameters, such as nanoparticle size, morphology, dispersion quality, interfacial interactions, and processing conditions, were not included due to a lack of consistent data availability. Third, the proposed model can only be applied to predict the dielectric constant in the range of input/output variables reported in Table 1.

It must be noted that the reported cross-validation metrics represent aggregated out-of-fold performance obtained from the fivefold cross-validation procedure. Because fold-wise performance statistics were not retained during the original analysis, standard deviations and confidence intervals cannot be computed retrospectively. Future work will retain fold-wise metrics or employ repeated cross-validation to enable formal uncertainty quantification.

Discussion

This work predicts the dielectric constant of neat polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and PVA-based nanocomposites using the machine learning methodology. Six machine learning models, including both traditional (MLP-ANN, least-squares SVR, ANFIS) and tree-based ensemble methods (GradBoost, CatBoost, and XGBoost), were developed and validated using 1698 experimental data points collected from the literature. These models consider nanofiller type, nanofiller concentration, temperature, and frequency as input features. The main findings of this study can be summarized as follows:Statistical significance analysis using standardized regression coefficients and p-values (p < 0.001) confirmed that all selected input variables have a statistically meaningful influence on the dielectric constant, supporting their inclusion in the modeling framework.The multiple linear regression results are physically consistent with dielectric theory, indicating an increase in dielectric constant with increasing temperature and decreasing frequency.Comparative model evaluation demonstrated that the Gradient Boosting exhibited the best predictive performance and generalization ability, achieving high accuracy in both cross-validation (RMSE = 3.49, AARD = 3.38%, MAE = 0.83, R = 0.99926) and external testing (RMSE = 4.24, AARD = 9.32%, MAE = 1.76, R = 0.99877), indicating strong robustness against unseen data.The consistency between cross-validation and testing performance further confirms the stability and reliability of the proposed model, reducing concerns of overfitting.
