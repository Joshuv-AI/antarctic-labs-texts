Unified Unbiased Variance Estimation for Maximum Mean Discrepancy: Robust Finite-Sample Performance with Imbalanced Data and Exact Acceleration under Null and Alternative Hypotheses
Shijie Zhong, Yikun Yang, Da Gong, Jiangfeng Fu

Abstract
The maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) is a kernel-based nonparametric statistic for two-sample testing, whose inferential accuracy depends critically on variance characterization. Existing work provides various finite-sample estimators of the MMD variance, often differing under the null and alternative hypotheses and across balanced or imbalanced sampling schemes. In this paper, we study the variance of the MMD statistic through its U-statistic representation and Hoeffding decomposition, and establish a unified finite-sample characterization covering different hypotheses and sample configurations. Building on this analysis, we propose an exact acceleration method for the univariate case under the Laplacian kernel, which reduces the overall computational complexity from 𝒪 ⁡ ( n 2 ) \mathcal{O}(n^{2}) to 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n ) \mathcal{O}(n\log n) .



1
Introduction

The fundamental problem of nonparametric two-sample testing is to detect differences between two probability distributions given access only to samples drawn from them. Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) is a kernel-based nonparametric statistic proposed for this task
[
6
,
11
]
. When equipped with a characteristic kernel, MMD defines a metric on the space of probability distributions, and thus equals zero if and only if the two distributions are identical
[
22
,
27
]
. It transforms the distance measure between any two distributions into a linear operation in the Hilbert space defined by a kernel function, which greatly facilitates computations. For this reason, this method has attracted increasing attention in recent years. In particular, MMD has been successfully applied to tasks such as domain adaptation
[
23
]
and generative modeling
[
29
]
, and has shown promising potential in other areas, such as Bayesian inference and model calibration tasks
[
31
,
32
]
.

The inferential reliability of MMD, particularly in constructing hypothesis tests and confidence intervals, depends crucially on an accurate characterization of its variance. However, the asymptotic behavior of the unbiased MMD statistic is non-trivial, leading to distinct variance structures under different regimes. Under the null hypothesis, the statistic is a degenerate U-statistic, with a limiting distribution given by an infinite weighted sum of chi-square random variables. Under the alternative hypothesis, it converges to a Gaussian distribution
[
11
,
3
]
. This dichotomy makes variance estimation challenging, especially in practice where sample sizes from the two distributions often differ. Existing work has addressed various special cases: Gretton et al.
[
11
]
provided the unbiased variance estimator under the null hypothesis with equal sample sizes; Sutherland and Deka
[
28
]
characterized the unbiased MMD estimator as a U-statistic under balanced sampling; and Wei et al.
[
30
]
proposed a method under the alternative hypothesis with unequal sample sizes by treating the MMD estimator as a unified generalized U-statistic
[
18
,
25
]
, although their variance estimator is based on asymptotic approximations and remains biased in finite-sample scenarios. In this context, a unified formulation capable of providing unbiased variance estimation under both hypotheses while accommodating unequal sample sizes remains of significant theoretical and practical interest.

To address these limitations, our approach employs the Hoeffding decomposition to explicitly construct orthogonal projection terms
[
26
,
14
]
, providing a streamlined representation of the MMD variance estimator. This formulation implicitly incorporates the covariance components present in
[
30
]
, while simplifying the overall structure. The orthogonal structure further enables a unified and smooth modeling of the variance transition across the null and alternative hypotheses. Beyond its theoretical elegance, the unbiased estimator’s algebraic clarity facilitates a concise, tensorized implementation in modern deep learning frameworks
[
1
,
24
]
, offering a practical and interpretable objective for kernel optimization aimed at maximizing test power
[
29
,
13
,
21
,
7
]
.

Beyond the theoretical perspective, accurately estimating the MMD variance poses significant computational challenges, as naive summation incurs quadratic complexity, straining both memory and computational resources and limiting the practical deployment of recent theoretical advances. Existing acceleration strategies exhibit clear trade-offs: global approximation methods, such as subsampling or random feature projections, scale well to high-dimensional data but introduce inherent approximation errors
[
33
,
34
]
. Analytical acceleration offers an alternative, restructuring independent summations into ordered linear recursions
[
2
,
17
]
, a strategy previously applied in classical statistics for measures like Kendall’s tau and distance covariance to reduce complexity from
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
to
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)

[
19
,
16
]
. In the MMD context, Bodenham and Kawahara
[
5
]
leveraged the semigroup property of the Laplace kernel to achieve linear-time acceleration of the MMD mean (euMMD) for univariate settings. However, this computational advantage has not yet been extended to unbiased variance estimation, where handling complex second-order terms, including Frobenius norms of kernel matrices, norms of row-sum vectors, and cross-matrix interactions, prevents existing linear-recursion schemes from providing exact results
[
28
]
.

To bridge this gap, we exploit the analytical properties of the Laplace kernel
[
16
,
5
]
to reformulate all terms in the variance expression as a system of linear prefix sums. This reformulation enables exact computation of the unbiased MMD variance with quasi-linear time complexity
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section
2
reviews the necessary background, including the definition of MMD, U-statistics, and the Hoeffding decomposition, as well as existing unbiased MMD acceleration techniques based on sorting methods. Section
3
derives the variance expression via the Hoeffding decomposition and presents the corresponding unbiased estimator. Section
4
introduces an accelerated variance computation method based on the Laplace kernel and the sorting methods. Finally, Section
5
presents numerical experiments demonstrating the proposed method’s performance.

2
Background

2.1
Maximum Mean Discrepancy

Assume
\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{X^{\prime}}{\sim}\mathbb{P}
and
\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{Y^{\prime}}{\sim}\mathbb{Q}
are independent copies. The maximum mean discrepancy is defined as
[
11
,
22
]
:

\mathrm{MMD}^{2}\left(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{X^{\prime}}}\left[k\left(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{X^{\prime}}\right)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{Y^{\prime}}}\left[k\left(\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{Y^{\prime}}\right)\right]-2\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y}}\left[k\left(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y}\right)\right]

(1)

Given i.i.d. finite samples
\mathbf{X}=\left\{x_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\mathbf{Y}=\left\{y_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{m}
from
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
, respectively, an unbiased estimator of the squared MMD is given by
[
6
]
:

\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq i^{\prime}}k(x_{i},x_{i^{\prime}})+\frac{1}{m(m-1)}\sum_{j\neq j^{\prime}}k(y_{j},y_{j^{\prime}})-\frac{2}{nm}\sum_{i,j}k(x_{i},y_{j})

(2)

which is a linear combination of two single-sample U-statistics and one generalized U-statistic
[
26
]
.

The effectiveness of MMD hinges on the use of characteristic kernels, for which the kernel mean embedding uniquely represents probability distributions in the associated reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)
[
27
,
9
]
. Under this condition, MMD induces a proper metric on distributions, ensuring that any difference between them is detectable
[
8
,
10
]
.

In practice, translation-invariant kernels are particularly appealing due to their simple analytic form and favorable computational properties, as characterized by Bochner’s theorem. Two commonly used examples are the Gaussian kernel and the Laplace kernel

k_{\mathrm{G}}(x,x^{\prime};\sigma)=\exp\left(-(x-x^{\prime})^{2}/{2\sigma^{2}}\right)

(3)

k_{\mathrm{L}}(x,x^{\prime};\sigma)=\exp\left(-{{|x-x^{\prime}|}/{\sigma}}\right)

(4)

where the kernel parameter
\sigma
controls the scale of the feature space and directly influences the test power. A common choice is the median heuristic
[
12
]
, where
\sigma
is set to the median of all pairwise sample distances. This approach requires no prior knowledge of the underlying distributions and is widely regarded as a standard baseline in the literature.

Finally, although
\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2}
enjoys favorable theoretical properties, its computation relies on the explicit construction of kernel matrices, resulting in a computational complexity of
\mathcal{O}(d(n+m)^{2})
. Letting
N=n+m
, this complexity can be written as
\mathcal{O}(dN^{2})

[
5
]
. In large-scale datasets or scenarios where moderate sample sizes require repeated evaluations, such quadratic computational cost severely limits the practical applicability of MMD-based statistical inference.

2.2
U-statistics and Hoeffding Decomposition

Depending on the sample source, U-statistics can be classified into single-sample U-statistics and generalized U-statistics. Both types form the theoretical foundation of the MMD estimator
[
26
]
. Let finite samples be
\mathbf{X}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
i.i.d. from
\boldsymbol{X}\sim\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbf{Y}=\{y_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
i.i.d. from
\boldsymbol{Y}\sim\mathbb{Q}
, where
\boldsymbol{X}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}
are independent. Consider a function
\theta(\boldsymbol{X})=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{X}}[h(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})]
,
whose unbiased estimator is

U_{n}=\binom{n}{r}^{-1}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{r}\leq n}h(x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{r}}),

(5)

where
r
denotes the order of the U-statistic and
n
the sample size. For the single-sample terms in the
\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2}
estimator (Eq.
2
), the kernel
k(x_{i},x_{i^{\prime}})
acts on pairs of samples, corresponding to the case where
r=2
.

Define the conditional expectation projections as

h_{c}(x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{c}})=\int h(x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{c}},x_{i_{c+1}},\dots,x_{i_{r}})\,d\mathbb{P}(x_{i_{c+1}})\cdots d\mathbb{P}(x_{i_{r}}),\quad 1\leq c\leq r.

(6)

and
\tilde{h}(x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{r}})=h(x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{r}})-\theta(\boldsymbol{X})
is the centered kernel.

The orthogonal projection components
g_{c}
are recursively defined by
[
14
,
4
]
:

\displaystyle g_{1}(x_{i_{1}})

\displaystyle=\tilde{h}_{1}(x_{i_{1}}),

\displaystyle g_{2}(x_{i_{1}},x_{i_{2}})

\displaystyle=\tilde{h}_{2}(x_{i_{1}},x_{i_{2}})-g_{1}(x_{i_{1}})-g_{1}(x_{i_{2}}),

\displaystyle g_{c}(x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{c}})

\displaystyle=\tilde{h}_{c}(x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{c}})-\sum_{s=1}^{c-1}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{s}\leq c}g_{s}(x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{s}}),\quad c\geq 3.

(7)

These components satisfy the zero-mean property:
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{X}}[g_{c}(x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{c}})]=0
.

Define
S_{c,n}=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{c}\leq n}g_{c}(x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{c}})
,
then the Hoeffding decomposition yields by
[
15
]
:

U_{n}-\theta(\boldsymbol{X})=\sum_{c=1}^{r}\binom{r}{c}\binom{n}{c}^{-1}S_{c,n}.

(8)

For parameters involving interactions between two distributions
\theta(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})=\mathbb{E}[h(x_{1},\dots,x_{r_{1}};y_{1},\dots,y_{r_{2}})]
, the corresponding generalized U-statistic is defined as

U_{n,m}=\frac{1}{\binom{n}{r_{1}}\binom{m}{r_{2}}}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r_{1}}\leq n}\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<\cdots<j_{r_{2}}\leq m}h(x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{r_{1}}};y_{j_{1}},\dots,y_{j_{r_{2}}}),

(9)

where
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
denote the orders of the U-statistic for the two samples, respectively. For the cross-sample interaction term in the
\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2}
estimator, the kernel
k(x_{i},y_{j})
involves a single observation from each distribution, which corresponds to the case where
r_{1}=1
and
r_{2}=1
.

For the generalized U-statistic
U_{n,m}
defined in Eq.
9
, its asymptotic variance are discussed in
[
20
]
. Following the recursive logic of the single-sample case, we specify the orthogonal projection components
g_{c_{1},c_{2}}
for the generalized case as follows.

Let
\tilde{h}=h-\theta(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})
be the centered kernel. For
0\leq c_{1}\leq r_{1}
and
0\leq c_{2}\leq r_{2}
, we define the joint conditional expectation projections as:

\tilde{h}_{c_{1},c_{2}}(x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{c_{1}}};y_{j_{1}},\dots,y_{j_{c_{2}}})=\mathbb{E}[\tilde{h}\mid x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{c_{1}}},y_{j_{1}},\dots,y_{j_{c_{2}}}].

(10)

The orthogonal projection components
g_{c_{1},c_{2}}
are recursively defined as:

g_{c_{1},c_{2}}(x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{c_{1}}};y_{j_{1}},\dots,y_{j_{c_{2}}})=\tilde{h}_{c_{1},c_{2}}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq s_{1}\leq c_{1}\\
0\leq s_{2}\leq c_{2}\\
0<s_{1}+s_{2}<c_{1}+c_{2}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\mathscr{S}_{s_{1},s_{2}}}g_{s_{1},s_{2}}(\cdot),

(11)

where
\mathscr{S}_{s_{1},s_{2}}
represents all subsets of size
s_{1}
from
\{x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{c_{1}}}\}
and size
s_{2}
from
\{y_{j_{1}},\dots,y_{j_{c_{2}}}\}
.
These components satisfy
\mathbb{E}[g_{c_{1},c_{2}}]=0
for all
c_{1}+c_{2}>0
.

Define the partial sums as
S_{c_{1},c_{2},n,m}=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{c_{1}}\leq n}\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<\dots<j_{c_{2}}\leq m}g_{c_{1},c_{2}}(x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{c_{1}}};y_{j_{1}},\dots,y_{j_{c_{2}}})
, the generalized Hoeffding decomposition is then expressed as:

U_{n,m}-\theta(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c_{1}=0,\dots,r_{1}\\
c_{2}=0,\dots,r_{2}\\
(c_{1},c_{2})\neq(0,0)\end{subarray}}\binom{r_{1}}{c_{1}}\binom{r_{2}}{c_{2}}\binom{n}{c_{1}}^{-1}\binom{m}{c_{2}}^{-1}S_{c_{1},c_{2},n,m}.

(12)

2.3
Lossless acceleration of the unbiased MMD estimator with the Laplace kernel

For univariate samples, directly computing Eq.
2
requires

\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
computational complexity. Based on several lemmas for the
Laplacian kernel presented in Appendix
A
, Bodenham and Kawahara
[
5
]
proposed a
lossless acceleration strategy that exploits the ordering structure of the
samples, reducing the complexity to
\mathcal{O}(N\log N)
. The procedure is
summarized as follows.

First, by applying Lemmas 1 and 2 in Appendix
A
, Eq.
2
can be
rewritten in the following form:

\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{\mathrm{eu}}^{2}\left(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}\right)=2\left(\alpha_{1}T_{1}+\alpha_{2}T_{2}-\alpha_{3}T_{3}\right)

(13)

where the coefficients are defined as
\alpha_{1}={1}\big/[{n\left(n-1\right)}]
,
\alpha_{2}={1}\big/[{m\left(m-1\right)}]
,
\alpha_{3}={1}\big/[{nm}]
. The summation terms correspond to the lower triangular parts of the kernel matrices and are given by
T_{1}=\sum_{i=2}^{n}{\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i-1}{k\left(x_{i},x_{i^{\prime}}\right)}}
,
T_{2}=\sum_{j=2}^{m}{\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{j-1}{k\left(y_{j},y_{j^{\prime}}\right)}}
,
T_{3}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}{k\left(x_{i},y_{j}\right)}}
.

To compute
T_{1}
, the sample sequence
\mathbf{X}
is first sorted in ascending
order, i.e.,
x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq\cdots\leq x_{n}
.
Define the forward cumulative sum
R_{i}^{\mathbf{X}}=\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i-1}{k\left(x_{i},x_{i^{\prime}}\right)}
and the exponential difference between adjacent samples
D_{i}^{\mathbf{X}}=\exp\left(-{{\left(x_{i}-x_{i-1}\right)}/{\sigma}}\right)
. According to Proposition 4 in the appendix
A
, the following recurrence relation holds:

R_{i}^{\mathbf{X}}=\left(R_{i-1}^{\mathbf{X}}+1\right)\cdot D_{i}^{\mathbf{X}}.

(14)

As a result,
T_{1}
can be computed in linear time as

T_{1}=\sum_{i=2}^{n}{R_{i}^{\mathbf{X}}}.

(15)

Similarly, for
T_{2}
, sorting
\mathbf{Y}
in ascending order

y_{1}\leq y_{2}\leq\cdots\leq y_{m}
yields

T_{2}=\sum_{j=2}^{m}{R_{i}^{\mathbf{Y}}}.

(16)

For the cross-sample term
T_{3}
, a merged-set strategy is adopted. Let

\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{X}\cup\mathbf{Y}
be the combined sample set, and sort it
as

z_{1}\leq z_{2}\leq\cdots\leq z_{n+m}
.
Define the total lower-triangular sum over the merged set as
T_{4}=\sum_{k=2}^{n+m}{\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k-1}{k\left(z_{k},z_{k^{\prime}}\right)}}
. By set decomposition,
T_{4}
contains all terms in
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
, and
T_{3}
, and thus
T_{3}=T_{4}-T_{1}-T_{2}
.

The quantity
T_{4}
can be efficiently computed using the same linear recurrence
R_{k}^{\boldsymbol{Z}}=\left(R_{k-1}^{\boldsymbol{Z}}+1\right)\cdot D_{k}^{\boldsymbol{Z}}
, which gives

T_{4}=\sum_{k=2}^{n+m}{R_{k}^{\boldsymbol{Z}}}.

(17)

The core logic of the above procedure is summarized in Algorithm
1
, which achieves a computational complexity of
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
.

Algorithm 1

TriSSL: Triangular Sorted Sum of Laplacians

1:

Ordered sample sequence
\mathbf{S}=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{l}\}
, kernel parameter
\sigma

2:

Lower-triangular summation result
T

3:

R_{1}\Leftarrow 0
;
T\Leftarrow 0

4:

for

i=2
to
l

do

5:

D_{i}\Leftarrow\exp\bigl(-(s_{i}-s_{i-1})/\sigma\bigr)

6:

R_{i}\Leftarrow(R_{i-1}+1)\cdot D_{i}

7:

T\Leftarrow T+R_{i}

8:

end

for

9:

return

T

3
Unbiased estimator of Maximum Mean Discrepancy variance

3.1
Variance derivation of the unbiased MMD estimator

Based on the theory of U-statistics and the Hoeffding decomposition presented in Section
2.2
, this subsection provides a rigorous derivation of the variance of the unbiased estimator
\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2}
.

First, we define the components in Eq.
1
as

\theta_{\boldsymbol{X}}:=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{X^{\prime}}}\left[k\left(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{X^{\prime}}\right)\right];\,\theta_{\boldsymbol{Y}}:=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{Y^{\prime}}}\left[k\left(\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{Y^{\prime}}\right)\right];\,\theta_{\boldsymbol{XY}}:=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y}}\left[k\left(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y}\right)\right].

Correspondingly, the components in Eq.
2
are denoted as

A:=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq i^{\prime}}k(x_{i},x_{i^{\prime}});\,B:=\frac{1}{m(m-1)}\sum_{j\neq j^{\prime}}k(y_{j},y_{j^{\prime}});\,C:=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j}k(x_{i},y_{j}).

According to the Hoeffding decomposition theorem (Eq.
8
and
12
), the deviation of each component can be expanded into a sum of linear terms and second-order residual terms.

For the within-
\boldsymbol{X}
component,

A-\theta_{\boldsymbol{X}}=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{g_{1,A}\left(x_{i}\right)}+\frac{2}{n\left(n-1\right)}\sum_{1\leq i<i^{\prime}\leq n}{g_{2,A}\left(x_{i},x_{i^{\prime}}\right)}

(18)

where
g_{1,A}\left(x_{i}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{X^{\prime}}}\left[k\left(x_{i},\boldsymbol{X^{\prime}}\right)\right]-\theta_{\boldsymbol{X}}
,
g_{2,A}\left(x_{i},x_{i^{\prime}}\right)=k\left(x_{i},x_{i^{\prime}}\right)-g_{1,A}\left(x_{i}\right)-g_{1,A}\left(x_{i^{\prime}}\right)-\theta_{\boldsymbol{X}}
.

Similarly, for the within-
\boldsymbol{Y}
component,

B-\theta_{\boldsymbol{Y}}=\frac{2}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}{g_{1,B}\left(y_{j}\right)}+\frac{2}{m\left(m-1\right)}\sum_{1\leq j<j^{\prime}\leq m}{g_{2,B}\left(y_{j},y_{j^{\prime}}\right)}

(19)

where
g_{1,B}\left(y_{j}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime}}\left[k\left(y_{j},\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime}\right)\right]-\theta_{\boldsymbol{Y}}
,
g_{2,B}\left(y_{j},y_{j^{\prime}}\right)=k\left(y_{j},y_{j^{\prime}}\right)-g_{1,B}\left(y_{j}\right)-g_{1,B}\left(y_{j^{\prime}}\right)-\theta_{\boldsymbol{Y}}
.

For the cross-sample component,

C-\theta_{\boldsymbol{XY}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{g_{1,CX}\left(x_{i}\right)}+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}{g_{1,CY}\left(y_{j}\right)}+\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}{g_{2,C}\left(x_{i},y_{j}\right)}}

(20)

where
g_{1,CX}\left(x_{i}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{Y}}\left(k\left(x_{i},\boldsymbol{Y}\right)\right)-\theta_{\boldsymbol{XY}}
,
g_{1,CY}\left(y_{j}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{X}}\left(k\left(\boldsymbol{X},y_{j}\right)\right)-\theta_{\boldsymbol{XY}}
,
g_{2,C}\left(x_{i},y_{j}\right)=k\left(x_{i},y_{j}\right)-g_{1,CX}\left(x_{i}\right)-g_{1,CY}\left(y_{j}\right)-\theta_{\boldsymbol{XY}}
.

Unlike the approach in
[
30
]
, which directly applies the general properties of generalized U-statistics by treating the entire MMD as a single monolithic entity, we leverage the natural form of the MMD estimator as a combination of distinct U-statistic components. By combining Eq.
18
,
19
, and
20
, the deviation of the unbiased estimator can be expressed as:

\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2}-\mathrm{MMD}^{2}=\mathcal{T}_{1}+\mathcal{T}_{2}

(21)

here,
\mathcal{T}_{1}
denotes the sum of first-order projection terms,

\mathcal{T}_{1}=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\left(\mathcal{U}\left(x_{i}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{U}\left(x_{i}\right)\right]\right)}+\frac{2}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\left(\mathcal{V}\left(y_{j}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{V}\left(y_{j}\right)\right]\right)}

(22)

where the influence functions are defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}\left(x_{i}\right)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}}\left[k\left(x_{i},\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{Y}}\left[k\left(x_{i},\boldsymbol{Y}\right)\right]

(23)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}\left(y_{j}\right)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime}}\left[k\left(y_{j},\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime}\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{X}}\left[k\left(\boldsymbol{X},y_{j}\right)\right]

(24)

The term
\mathcal{T}_{2}
corresponds to the second-order residual terms,

\mathcal{T}_{2}=\frac{2}{n\left(n-1\right)}\zeta_{2,A}+\frac{2}{m\left(m-1\right)}\zeta_{2,B}-\frac{2}{nm}\zeta_{2,C}

(25)

where
\zeta_{2,A}=\sum_{1\leq i<i^{\prime}\leq n}{g_{2,A}\left(x_{i},x_{i^{\prime}}\right)}
,
\zeta_{2,B}=\sum_{1\leq j<j^{\prime}\leq m}{g_{2,B}\left(y_{j},y_{j^{\prime}}\right)}
,
\zeta_{2,C}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}{g_{2,C}\left(x_{i},y_{j}\right)}}
.

Since different orders in the Hoeffding decomposition are orthogonal, i.e.,

\mathrm{Cov}(H_{1},H_{2})=0

[
15
]
, the variance of
\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2}
admits the exact decomposition

\operatorname{Var}\left(\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2}\right)=\mathrm{Var}\left(\mathcal{T}_{1}\right)+\mathrm{Var}\left(\mathcal{T}_{2}\right)

(26)

where the variance of the linear term
\operatorname{Var}(\mathcal{T}_{1})
captures the dominant fluctuations of the sample means, while the variance of the remainder term
\operatorname{Var}(\mathcal{T}_{2})
characterizes the second-order interactions induced by the kernel.

3.2
Simplification and unbiased estimation of the variance

3.2.1
Simplification and unbiased estimation of first-order projection terms

To translate the theoretical decomposition in Eq.
26
into a practical and computable form, we leverage the fundamental independence between the two sample sets, and the orthogonal representation allows for a decoupled estimation of the variance components. Specifically, for the first-order term
\mathcal{T}_{1}
, the mutual independence of
\boldsymbol{X}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}
ensures that the covariance between the sample-specific fluctuations vanishes. Consequently, the variance of the linear term simplifies to the weighted sum of marginal variances:

\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\mathcal{T}_{1})=\frac{4(n-2)}{n(n-1)}\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\mathcal{U})+\frac{4(m-2)}{m(m-1)}\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\mathcal{V}),

(27)

where
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\mathcal{U})
and
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\mathcal{V})
represent the variances of population projections. To realize this empirically, we replace these population expectations with their empirical projections:

For a sample point
x_{i}\in\mathbf{X}
, the continuous projection
\mathcal{U}(x_{i})
is discretized using sample averages as

\hat{\mathcal{U}}(x_{i})=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq i}k(x_{i},x_{i^{\prime}})-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}k(x_{i},y_{j})

(28)

The unbiased estimator of the variance
\operatorname{Var}(\mathcal{U}(X))
is therefore given by the sample variance:

\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\mathcal{U})=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\hat{\mathcal{U}}(x_{i})-\bar{\hat{\mathcal{U}}}\right)^{2}

(29)

where
\bar{\hat{\mathcal{U}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\mathcal{U}}(x_{i})\big/n
and an analogous estimator can be obtained for
\hat{\mathcal{V}}(y_{j})
and
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\mathcal{V})
:

\hat{\mathcal{V}}(y_{j})=\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{j^{\prime}\neq j}k(y_{j},y_{j^{\prime}})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k(x_{i},y_{j})

(30)

\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\mathcal{V})=\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\hat{\mathcal{V}}(y_{j})-\bar{\hat{\mathcal{V}}}\right)^{2}

(31)

where we avoid the appearance of complex cross-covariance terms that are prone to estimation bias in finite samples (as seen in
[
30
]
). By projecting the estimator onto orthogonal Hoeffding components, we decompose the variance into a streamlined structure of marginal variances and interaction residuals. This not only simplifies the analytical form but also ensures the robustness of the estimator in imbalanced scenarios where
n\neq m
.

3.2.2
Simplification and unbiased estimation of second-order residual terms

Parallel to the treatment of the linear term, the variance of the second-order remainder
\mathcal{T}_{2}
can be simplified by exploiting the mutual orthogonality of the
g_{2}
components across different sample pairs. Since
g_{2,A}
,
g_{2,B}
, and
g_{2,C}
are constructed to be zero-mean and mutually uncorrelated under the independent sampling of
\boldsymbol{X}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}
, the variance
\operatorname{Var}(\mathcal{T}_{2})
avoids any cross-product terms. This allows us to expand the second-order variance into a weighted sum of the dispersion of each interaction kernel:

\operatorname{Var}(\mathcal{T}_{2})=\operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{2\zeta_{2,A}}{n(n-1)}\right)+\operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{2\zeta_{2,B}}{m(m-1)}\right)+\operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{2\zeta_{2,C}}{nm}\right)

(32)

As an illustrative example for the within-sample component
A
, consider the second-order sum
\zeta_{2,A}=\sum_{1\leq i<i^{\prime}\leq n}g_{2,A}(x_{i},x_{i^{\prime}})
. Since
g_{2,A}
is a centered orthogonal projection component, for any two distinct index pairs
{i,i^{\prime}}
and
{j,j^{\prime}}
, if the pairs do not coincide exactly, then
\mathrm{Cov}(g_{2,A}(x_{i},x_{i^{\prime}}),g_{2,A}(x_{j},x_{j^{\prime}}))=0
.
As a consequence, the variance of the sum equals the sum of the variances of the individual terms:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{2\zeta_{2,A}}{n(n-1)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{n^{2}(n-1)^{2}}\cdot\binom{n}{2}\operatorname{Var}(g_{2,A})

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{n^{2}(n-1)^{2}}\cdot\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\mathbb{E}[g_{2,A}^{2}]=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\mathbb{E}[g_{2,A}^{2}]

(33)

Similarly, for the cross-sample mixed component
C
, since
x_{i}
and
y_{j}
are independent, the covariance is non-zero only when the indices coincide exactly:

\operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{2\zeta_{2,C}}{nm}\right)=\frac{4}{n^{2}m^{2}}\cdot(nm)\mathbb{E}[g_{2,C}^{2}]=\frac{4}{nm}\mathbb{E}[g_{2,C}^{2}]

(34)

At this stage, Eq.
32
can be reformulated as

\operatorname{Var}(\mathcal{T}_{2})=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\mathbb{E}[g_{2,A}^{2}]+\frac{2}{m(m-1)}\mathbb{E}[g_{2,B}^{2}]+\frac{4}{nm}\mathbb{E}[g_{2,C}^{2}]

(35)

To further simplify, Consider the orthogonality of first-order components (Section
2.2
) and the nesting property of conditional expectations,

\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{Z}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{Z}|\boldsymbol{X}]]=\mathbb{E}[(\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{Z}|\boldsymbol{X}])^{2}]
,
which allows the squared second-order orthogonal components to be simplified. Taking
g_{2,A}
as an example, where
g_{2,A}(x,x^{\prime})=k(x,x^{\prime})-g_{1,A}(x)-g_{1,A}(x^{\prime})-\theta_{\boldsymbol{X}}
. The expectation of the squared term and expanding yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[g_{2,A}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[k(x,x^{\prime})^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[g_{1,A}(x)^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[g_{1,A}(x^{\prime})^{2}]+\theta_{\boldsymbol{X}}^{2}

(36)

\displaystyle-2\mathbb{E}[k(x,x^{\prime})\cdot g_{1,A}(x)]-2\mathbb{E}[k(x,x^{\prime})\cdot g_{1,A}(x^{\prime})]-2\theta_{\boldsymbol{X}}\mathbb{E}[k(x,x^{\prime})]

(37)

\displaystyle+2\mathbb{E}[g_{1,A}(x)g_{1,A}(x^{\prime})]-2\theta_{\boldsymbol{X}}\mathbb{E}[g_{1,A}(x)]-2\theta_{\boldsymbol{X}}\mathbb{E}[g_{1,A}(x^{\prime})]

(38)

Using the facts that
\mathbb{E}[g_{1,A}(x)]=0
,

\mathbb{E}[k(x,x^{\prime})\cdot g_{1,A}(x)]=\mathbb{E}[g_{1,A}(x)^{2}]
,
and
\mathbb{E}[g_{1,A}(x)g_{1,A}(x^{\prime})]=0
, the above expression simplifies to

\mathbb{E}[g_{2,A}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[k(x,x^{\prime})^{2}]-2\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{X}}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{X^{\prime}}}[k(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{X^{\prime}})]\right)^{2}\right]+\theta_{\boldsymbol{X}}^{2},

(39)

analogous derivation can be obtained for
\mathbb{E}[g_{2,B}^{2}]
:

\mathbb{E}[g_{2,B}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[k(y,y^{\prime})^{2}]-2\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{Y}}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{Y^{\prime}}}[k(\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{Y^{\prime}})]\right)^{2}\right]+\theta_{\boldsymbol{Y}}^{2}

(40)

Consider the mixed component
g_{2,C}(x,y)=k(x,y)-g_{1,CX}(x)-g_{1,CY}(y)-\theta_{\boldsymbol{XY}}
, which arises from the interaction between samples
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
. Since
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
are independent, the first-order projection terms
g_{1,CX}(x)
and
g_{1,CY}(y)
are also independent. Expanding the squared term and eliminating zero-mean cross terms yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[g_{2,C}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[k(x,y)^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[g_{1,CX}^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[g_{1,CY}^{2}]+\theta_{\boldsymbol{XY}}^{2}

\displaystyle-2\mathbb{E}[k(x,y)g_{1,CX}(x)]-2\mathbb{E}[k(x,y)g_{1,CY}(y)]+2\theta_{\boldsymbol{XY}}\mathbb{E}[k(x,y)]

(41)

Using again the projection property
\mathbb{E}[k(x,y)g_{1,CX}(x)]=\mathbb{E}[g_{1,CX}^{2}+\theta_{\boldsymbol{XY}}g_{1,CX}]=\mathbb{E}[g_{1,CX}^{2}]
, the expression simplifies to

\mathbb{E}[g_{2,C}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[k(x,y)^{2}]-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{X}}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{Y}}[k(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})]\right)^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{Y}}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{X}}[k(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})]\right)^{2}\right]+\theta_{\boldsymbol{XY}}^{2}

(42)

By applying the inclusion-exclusion principle, the unbiased estimator can be expressed entirely in terms of unconstrained matrix operations
[
28
]
. Specifically, using the falling factorial
(n)_{k}
and the centered kernel matrices
\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{XX}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{YY}
(with zero diagonals), the expectation
\mathbb{E}[g_{2}^{2}]
can be written as a linear combination of the squared Frobenius norms, the squared row and column sums, and the squared total sum of the kernel matrices.

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{E}[g_{2,A}^{2}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n)_{2}}\|\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{XX}\|_{F}^{2}-\frac{2}{(n)_{3}}\left(\|\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{XX}\mathbf{1}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{XX}\|_{F}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{(n)_{4}}\left((\mathbf{1}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{XX}\mathbf{1})^{2}-4\|\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{XX}\mathbf{1}\|_{2}^{2}+2\|\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{XX}\|_{F}^{2}\right)

(43)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{E}[g_{2,B}^{2}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(m)_{2}}\|\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{YY}\|_{F}^{2}-\frac{2}{(m)_{3}}\left(\|\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{YY}\mathbf{1}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{YY}\|_{F}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{(m)_{4}}\left((\mathbf{1}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{YY}\mathbf{1})^{2}-4\|\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{YY}\mathbf{1}\|_{2}^{2}+2\|\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{YY}\|_{F}^{2}\right)

(44)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{E}[g_{2,C}^{2}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{nm}\|\mathbf{K}_{XY}\|_{F}^{2}-\frac{1}{n(m)_{2}}\left(\|\mathbf{K}_{XY}^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\mathbf{K}_{XY}\|_{F}^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{m(n)_{2}}\left(\|\mathbf{K}_{XY}\mathbf{1}_{m}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\mathbf{K}_{XY}\|_{F}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{(n)_{2}(m)_{2}}\left((\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{XY}\mathbf{1}_{m})^{2}-\|\mathbf{K}_{XY}\mathbf{1}_{m}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\mathbf{K}_{XY}^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\mathbf{K}_{XY}\|_{F}^{2}\right)

(45)

where
(n)_{k}={n!}\big/{(n-k)!}
denotes the falling factorial.

3.2.3
The unbiased estimator of MMD variance

Using the simplification results provided in Eq.
29
,
31
,
43
,
44
, and
45
, the full analytical expression of
\operatorname{Var}(\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2})
is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}\left(\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{4(n-2)}{n(n-1)}\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\mathcal{U})+\frac{4(m-2)}{m(m-1)}\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\mathcal{V})

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{n\left(n-1\right)}\widehat{\mathbb{E}[g_{2,A}^{2}]}+\frac{2}{m\left(m-1\right)}\widehat{\mathbb{E}[g_{2,B}^{2}]}+\frac{4}{nm}\widehat{\mathbb{E}[g_{2,C}^{2}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{4(n-2)}{n(n-1)^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\hat{\mathcal{U}}(x_{i})-\bar{\hat{\mathcal{U}}}\right)^{2}+\frac{4(m-2)}{m(m-1)^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\hat{\mathcal{V}}(y_{j})-\bar{\hat{\mathcal{V}}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{(n)_{2}^{2}}\|\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{XX}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{2}{(m)_{2}^{2}}\|\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{YY}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{4}{n^{2}m^{2}}\|\mathbf{K}_{XY}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{4}{(n)_{2}(n)_{3}}\left(\|\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{XX}\mathbf{1}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{XX}\|_{F}^{2}\right)-\frac{4}{(m)_{2}(m)_{3}}\left(\|\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{YY}\mathbf{1}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{YY}\|_{F}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{4}{n^{2}m(m)_{2}}\left(\|\mathbf{K}_{XY}^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\mathbf{K}_{XY}\|_{F}^{2}\right)-\frac{4}{m^{2}n(n)_{2}}\left(\|\mathbf{K}_{XY}\mathbf{1}_{m}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\mathbf{K}_{XY}\|_{F}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{(n)_{2}(n)_{4}}\left((\mathbf{1}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{XX}\mathbf{1})^{2}-4\|\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{XX}\mathbf{1}\|_{2}^{2}+2\|\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{XX}\|_{F}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{(m)_{2}(m)_{4}}\left((\mathbf{1}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{YY}\mathbf{1})^{2}-4\|\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{YY}\mathbf{1}\|_{2}^{2}+2\|\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{YY}\|_{F}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{4}{n(n)_{2}m(m)_{2}}\left((\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{XY}\mathbf{1}_{m})^{2}-\|\mathbf{K}_{XY}\mathbf{1}_{m}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\mathbf{K}_{XY}^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\mathbf{K}_{XY}\|_{F}^{2}\right)

(46)

Unlike previous works that rely on large-sample asymptotic approximations
[
30
]
, our derived estimator is exactly unbiased for any finite sample sizes
n
and
m
. By employing a combinatorial approach based on falling factorials
(n)_{k}
, we explicitly cancel out the systematic bias that typically arises when sample means are substituted for population expectations. This makes our estimator particularly robust in small-sample regimes or highly imbalanced settings (
n\ll m
).

Remark 1
.

Under the null hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{0}:\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{Q}
, the unbiased MMD statistic exhibits a "degenerate" behavior. In this regime, the population projections vanish, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}}\left[k\left(x,\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{Y}}\left[k\left(x,\boldsymbol{Y}\right)\right]\equiv 0

(47)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}\left(y\right)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime}}\left[k\left(y,\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime}\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{X}}\left[k\left(\boldsymbol{X},y\right)\right]\equiv 0,

(48)

causing the
O(n^{-1})
first-order variance
\operatorname{Var}(\mathcal{T}_{1})
to drop to zero. Consequently, the total population variance is determined solely by the
O(n^{-2})
second-order residual term
\mathcal{T}_{2}

[
11
,
30
]
.

Crucially, this theoretical degeneracy does not imply that the empirical variance of
\hat{\mathcal{U}}(x_{i})
and
\hat{\mathcal{V}}(y_{j})
disappears. In finite samples, the empirical projections (Eqs.
28
and
30
) retain fluctuations due to the inherent coupling of sample pairs. Our estimator in Eq. (
3.2.3
) incorporates these fluctuations by integrating
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
into a single analytical form. As a result, our variance estimate is generally larger than those based on asymptotic approximations (e.g.,
[
11
]
), as it explicitly captures high-order coupling terms that are typically neglected. This "unified" property is a significant advantage: under
\mathcal{H}_{1}
, the
O(n^{-1})
terms naturally dominate, while under
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, the
O(n^{-2})
terms automatically take over, which provides a more conservative and robust variance estimate.

4.2.1
Accelerated computation of first-order components

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{U}}(x_{i})

(57)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{V}}(y_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m-1}\bigl(R_{j}^{\mathbf{Y}}+L_{j}^{\mathbf{Y}}\bigr)-\frac{1}{n}\bigl(\mathcal{A}_{y\to x}(j)+\mathcal{Z}_{y\to x}(j)\bigr).

(58)

4.2.2
Accelerated computation of second-order components

To achieve
O(N)
computation of the second-order terms, we leverage the bandwidth scaling property (Lemma 4.1). Specifically, while the row sums of the kernel matrix
\mathbf{K}
are computed using the base bandwidth
\sigma
, the squared kernel terms required for the Frobenius norm
\|\mathbf{K}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i,j}k(x_{i},x_{j})^{2}
are equivalent to a Laplace kernel with bandwidth
\sigma/2
.

Therefore, we introduce a second pass of prefix-suffix accumulators with
\sigma/2
, denoted as
\{R^{\text{sq}},L^{\text{sq}}\}
for within-sample matrices and
\{\mathcal{A}^{\text{sq}},\mathcal{Z}^{\text{sq}}\}
for cross-sample matrices:

For the
X
single-sample kernel matrix:

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{XX}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(R_{i}^{\mathrm{sq},\mathbf{X}}+L_{i}^{\mathrm{sq},\mathbf{X}}),

(59)

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{XX}\mathbf{1}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(R_{i}^{\mathbf{X}}+L_{i}^{\mathbf{X}})^{2},

(60)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{1}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{XX}\mathbf{1})^{2}

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(R_{i}^{\mathbf{X}}+L_{i}^{\mathbf{X}})\Big)^{2}.

(61)

For the
Y
single-sample kernel matrix:

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{YY}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}(R_{j}^{\mathrm{sq},\mathbf{Y}}+L_{j}^{\mathrm{sq},\mathbf{Y}}),

(62)

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{YY}\mathbf{1}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}(R_{j}^{\mathbf{Y}}+L_{j}^{\mathbf{Y}})^{2},

(63)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{1}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{YY}\mathbf{1})^{2}

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{m}(R_{j}^{\mathbf{Y}}+L_{j}^{\mathbf{Y}})\Big)^{2}.

(64)

For the cross-sample kernel matrix:

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{K}_{XY}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{sq}}_{x\to y}(i)+\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{sq}}_{x\to y}(i)),

(65)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{K}_{XY}\mathbf{1}_{m}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mathcal{A}_{x\to y}(i)+\mathcal{Z}_{x\to y}(i))^{2},

(66)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{K}_{XY}^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\mathcal{A}_{y\to x}(j)+\mathcal{Z}_{y\to x}(j))^{2},

(67)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{XY}\mathbf{1}_{m})^{2}

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mathcal{A}_{x\to y}(i)+\mathcal{Z}_{x\to y}(i))\Big)^{2}.

(68)

Based on the theoretical analysis, Algorithm

presents an accelerated procedure for computing the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) with the Laplace kernel

Efficiency of variance estimation

To evaluate the computational efficiency and scalability of the proposed accelerated framework for large-scale variance estimation, we benchmark its runtime and memory performance against the traditional
O(n^{2})

n=10^{6}

Figure 3:
Double-logarithmic comparison of runtime and memory consumption for both algorithms under unequal sample sizes

Under the double-logarithmic scale, the performance divergence between the two paradigms is striking. In terms of computational time, the traditional algorithm exhibits a rigid quadratic growth pattern (
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
). Conversely, the proposed accelerated algorithm demonstrates a remarkably mild growth rate that aligns with its theoretical
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)

The contrast in memory consumption is even more pronounced. The kernel matrix method suffers from a rapid expansion in space usage; at

In contrast, the accelerated algorithm maintains an almost flat trajectory in the log-log plots. Its memory footprint remains consistently below the 1 MB threshold across all tested scales—essentially negligible compared to system background usage. This "near-zero" space overhead confirms that our method successfully shifts the bottleneck of MMD variance estimation from hardware capacity to basic CPU cycles.

Conclusion

In this work, we studied the variance of the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) statistic from the perspective of its U-statistic representation and Hoeffding decomposition. Our contributions are threefold:

•

Unified finite-sample variance characterization:
We established analytical expressions for the MMD variance that hold across different hypotheses (null and alternative) and sample configurations (balanced and unbalanced). This provides a consistent framework for understanding variance behavior in practical two-sample testing scenarios.

•

Exact acceleration for univariate MMD:
For the Laplace kernel in the univariate case, we developed an exact, quasi-linear-time algorithm that reduces the computational complexity from
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
to
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
while preserving the full Hoeffding variance estimator. This allows efficient, accurate variance computation even for large sample sizes.

Overall, the proposed framework builds a bridge between theoretical understanding and practical computation, making variance estimation and inference in finite-sample two-sample testing more robust and efficient.

Appendix A
Auxiliary Lemmas for accelerated computation with the Laplacian kernel

Lemma 1
.

Let
\{z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{m}\}\subset\mathbb{R}
and let

f:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a symmetric function, i.e.,

f(x,y)=f(y,x)
. Then

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{i}f(z_{i},z_{j})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=i}^{m}f(z_{i},z_{j}).

Lemma 2
.

Under the conditions of Lemma 1, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{m}f(z_{i},z_{j})=2\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}f(z_{i},z_{j}).

Lemma 3
.

Let
\beta:\{1,2,\dots,m\}\to\{1,2,\dots,m\}
be any permutation. Then

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}f(z_{i},z_{j})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}f\!\left(z_{\beta(i)},z_{\beta(j)}\right).

Proposition 4
.

Let
\{z_{1},\dots,z_{m}\}\subset\mathbb{R}
and denote the order statistics by

z_{(1)}\leq z_{(2)}\leq\cdots\leq z_{(m)}
.
Consider the Laplacian kernel with bandwidth
\sigma>0
,

k_{\mathrm{L}}(x,y)=\exp\!\left(-{|x-y|}\big/{\sigma}\right)
.
Define

S:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}k_{\mathrm{L}}(z_{i},z_{j})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\exp\!\left(-[{z_{(i)}-z_{(j)}}]\big/{\sigma}\right).

Then
S
can be computed recursively as follows. Set
S_{1}=R_{1}=0
, and for

k=2,\dots,m
, define

\displaystyle D_{k}

\displaystyle=\exp\!\left(-[{z_{(k)}-z_{(k-1)}}]\big/{\sigma}\right),

\displaystyle R_{k}

\displaystyle=(R_{k-1}+1)\,D_{k},

\displaystyle S_{k}

\displaystyle=S_{k-1}+R_{k}.

Then
S=S_{m}
.
