High utility of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid metagenomic next-generation sequencing approach for etiological diagnosis of pneumonia
Authors: Lingyu Jiang, Lin Han, Yonglong Zhong, Meng Zhang, Jianliang Li, Guanhua Rao, Shulin Xiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFor patients with pneumonia, the rapid detection of pathogens is still a major global problem in clinical practice because traditional diagnostic techniques for infection are time-consuming and insensitive. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) is a novel technique that has the potential to improve pathogen diagnosis. This study aimed to investigate the microbiological diagnostic ability of mNGS compared with conventional culture and to determine the optimal time to test patients for pneumonia.MethodsA prospective study using data from June 2020 to June 2021 was performed at a tertiary teaching hospital in China. We included 56 patients from all adult patients with a clinical diagnosis of pneumonia. Blood and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) samples were taken for simultaneous mNGS and conventional culture testing.ResultsAll 56 patients underwent both conventional culture and mNGS. Of these patients, 37 were diagnosed with severe pneumonia and 17 were diagnosed with non-severe pneumonia. The top three pathogenic bacteria detected by mNGS were Acinetobacter baumannii, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Enterococcus faecium was detected more frequently in the non-severe pneumonia group (4 vs. 0, p < 0.05). The findings revealed that the detection rate of mNGS (84%) was superior to that of conventional culture methods (48%). Notably, the percentage of mNGS-positive BALF samples (46/56, 82.14%) was significantly greater than that of blood samples (27/56, 48.21%). The etiological comparison demonstrated that mNGS-positive samples, which received clinical approval, tended to be associated with a more normalized temperature, lower PCO2 levels, and a higher SOFA score than mNGS-negative samples (p = 0.022, p = 0.0.028, and p = 0.038, respectively).ConclusionsIn this study, we discovered that the etiology of lung infections frequently involves multiple pathogens. The use of mNGS in BALF is instrumental for detecting nonviral pathogens associated with lung infections. Although the rate of positive blood NGS results is significantly influenced by various clinical factors, for patients suspected of having viral, Legionella, or tsutsugamushi infections, plasma mNGS could serve as a complementary diagnostic tool.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-024-10108-6.Keywords: Next-generation sequencing, Diagnostic, Pneumonia

Introduction

Pulmonary infections are the most prevalent and overwhelming infectious diseases. Severe pneumonia is associated with serious morbidity and mortality among hospitalized patients, especially older patients, despite advances in rapid diagnostic tests, treatment options, and vaccine strategies [1, 2]. The success of treating lung infections hinges on selecting an appropriate antimicrobial agent. Rapid, sensitive, and specific detection of the infectious pathogen is crucial for determining the clinical progression and outcome of a patient with sepsis. Furthermore, early failure to identify pathogens is one of the reasons for the high mortality rate of patients with pneumonia [3]. Microbiology laboratories play a vital role in the principal detection of infections using microscopic examination, culture, identification, drug sensitivity, and so on. However, most of these methods are time-consuming and lack sensitivity [4]. This may be partly because of the frequent use of antibiotic therapy before pathogen detection test sampling. Hence, initial antibiotic therapy is often empirical and nonspecific for severe pneumonia, which may lead to delays in targeted anti-infective treatment, increasing the prevalence of antibiotic-resistant microorganisms, and impeding effective therapy. These challenges have accelerated the exploration of accurate diagnostic strategies.

As a new technique, metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) has been widely used in clinical practice in recent years [5–7]. Since the application of mNGS, untargeted sequencing of DNA/RNA has enabled the rapid etiological diagnoses of complex critical cases involving a broader range of bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites when compared to conventional culture and blood culture methods [8, 9]. For severely ill patients, the mNGS diagnostic technique can aid in the identification of infectious agents and enhance diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic effectiveness, potentially resulting in more favorable clinical outcomes [10]. As an evolving diagnostic tool, there are still deficiencies in some areas. The value of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and blood mNGS in pulmonary infection remains to be further validated, and whether there are differences in their roles needs to be verified, which poses new challenges for clinical treatment. The sub-group of patients served most appropriately by NGS needs to be further clarified because of low clinically significant positive results in some conditions. Hence, identifying the best opportunity and optimal specimens for etiological tests is crucial. However, at present, the best timing for mNGS detection in patients with pneumonia has not been evaluated. Our study was undertaken to evaluate the diagnostic performance of mNGS for detecting pathogens, including bacteria, fungi, and viruses, in patients with pneumonia and to compare results with those of conventional methods. We also aimed to explore the best method for diagnosing pneumonia to avoid the performance of unnecessary costly tests.

Materials and methods

Patient population and study design

This single-center prospective observational study evaluated data collected from June 2019 to June 2021 from patients with pneumonia admitted to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) of the Peoples Hospital of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, which is a tertiary hospital with 28 beds.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria: (1) Age ≥ 18 years (2), ICU stay > 24 h (3). Meeting the diagnostic criteria for pneumonia or severe pneumonia [11] (4). Paired mNGS and conventional culture results, with a sampling interval of less than 24 h. Exclusion criteria: (1) Incomplete clinical data, including microbiological data (2). Failure to acquire enough samples for paired mNGS and conventional culture analysis during ICU admission (3). Failure to meet the inclusion criteria (4). The patient refused to provide written consent. Informed consent was obtained from all patients involved.

After the patients were transferred to the ICU, we obtained samples from patients who newly developed pneumonia or whose symptoms worsened or showed persistent radiological progression after 48 to 72 h of initial antibiotic therapy. Fiberoptic bronchoscopy was used to acquire BALF for microbiological testing collected under the strict supervision of ICU staff. The collection of blood samples for mNGS analysis was carried out in synchronization with the timing of BALF sampling. All mNGS testing was performed by Genskey laboratory [12]. Because only mNGS was used to sequence DNA in this study, RNA viruses were not detected for analysis. A positive test result was defined as a comparison with a confirmed pathogen, including any of the final pathogens.

Metagenomic next-generation sequencing

The DNA extraction and library preparation protocol began with 5 ml of blood or BALF samples. The DNA from this sample was processed using the Genskey kit (model 2053 A, Genskey, Tianjin) to initiate the library preparation process, which involved PCR amplification and subsequent purification. The concentration of the resulting DNA libraries was accurately measured utilizing the Qubit® 1×dsDNA HS kit on the Qubit 4.0 fluorometer. With the optimized concentration, these libraries were then constructed with an NGS-specific library construction kit (model 2012B, Genskey, Tianjin). Following thermal denaturation of the libraries, they were mixed and prepared for DNB (DNA Nanoball) formation. Subsequently, all libraries were converted into single-stranded circular DNA and enriched with 2–3 quantitative sets to generate DNA nanospheres. To ensure the quality of the sequencing data, these DNA nanospheres were carefully loaded onto the sequencing chip and subjected to sequencing on the MGISEQ-200 platform (MGI, Shenzhen, China). Rigorous quality control measures were implemented prior to analysis, where raw reads were filtered using fastp (version 0.22.0) and Komplexity (version 0.3.6) to remove contaminants such as adapter sequences, low-quality reads, and reads with low complexity. Additionally, reads that aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) were excluded using bowtie2 (version 2.3.5.1), thereby enhancing the specificity and accuracy of the downstream analyses.

mNGS results Criteria

For mNGS report criteria, we followed the previous protocols for BALF samples [13] and Blood samples [14].

In brief, the criteria for BALF mNGS results involve filtering out significant sequence alignment anomalies and background contamination before identifying pathogens. For Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB), a positive result is declared if at least one read maps to the species or genus level, while nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) are considered positive if they rank in the top 10. For other bacteria, fungi, viruses, and parasites, the top 10 most abundant species are selected, and potential pathogens are reported based on clinical relevance.

For blood samples, negative controls were developed to filter out contaminants in mNGS from blood sampling and human flora. Organisms were retained only if they appeared at least five times more in samples than in controls.

The criteria for the final pathogenic diagnosis

(1) Multi-expert review mechanism: We adopted a multi-expert approach involving doctors with extensive clinical experience to conduct independent judgments. These doctors were able to interpret the same set of data from multiple angles based on their respective professional knowledge and clinical experience. (2) Expert-level review team: In classifying pathogens, we specifically invited two clinicians with backgrounds in infectious diseases at the deputy chief physician level or above to conduct independent judgments. If the judgments of the two doctors were consistent, the pathogen classification was determined directly. If a disagreement occurred, a third chief physician was invited to make a final determination, ensuring the authority and accuracy of the result. (3) Clear judgment process and standards: To ensure consistency and standardization in the judgment process, we established detailed judgment processes and standards. In cases where the etiological test results are negative, we determined them as clinically unconfirmed. For the interpretation of positive etiological test results, we followed a rigorous process to minimize the interference of personal subjective biases (Supplementary Fig. 1).

The final pathogenic diagnosis and accuracy of the test results were determined according to the standards based on clinical agreement (Supplementary Table 1).

Data collection

In this study, we gathered and documented the demographic and clinical profiles of the patients enrolled, including their age, initial symptoms, comorbid conditions, inflammatory biomarkers, duration of antibiotic therapy before the pathogen test, duration of ICU stay, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II score, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, and in-hospital mortality. Samples of suspected infections were then collected for analysis via mNGS. In this study, conventional culture methods, including BALF and blood culture, were used for evaluating patients with pneumonia. These cultures were performed within 24 h before and following the corresponding mNGS test. Given that viruses are not typically the predominant pathogens in pneumonia, the detection of viruses through mNGS was not included in the clinical approval process of this study.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as the means ± SDs, medians, interquartile ranges, or proportions (absolute and relative frequencies), as appropriate. The t test or Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare continuous variables, and the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical variables. The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and consistency were also statistically significant. Two machine learning methods, Lasso and random forest, were used to find the clinically appropriate time to submit the test. A p value ≤ 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Statistical analysis was performed by using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 24 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Patient characteristics

During the study period, 77 patients with pneumonia who underwent mNGS for infection in at least two different sites were admitted to the ICU. Five of these patients underwent repeated tests, and only the first test result for each patient was included in the final analysis. The following paired samples were excluded from this analysis: two blood and ascites samples, two blood and urine samples, two cerebrospinal fluid and BALF samples, one blood and tissue sample, one blood and cerebrospinal fluid sample, and one cerebrospinal fluid and BALF specimen. Thus, a total of 56 patients who had both blood and BALF samples tested using mNGS were included in the final analysis. These patients constituted our study cohort and were characterized by paired blood and BALF mNGS results (Table 1). The median age of these 56 patients, including 44 males, was 59 years, with the majority over 40 years old (45/56). The common symptoms presented by these patients included dyspnea (46.4%), fever (46.4%), and cough (32.1%).

The mNGS method detected up to 23 positive cases, accounting for 41% of the total 56 patients. Clinical significance of simultaneous mNGS in BALF and blood samples for pathogen identification in patients with severe pneumonia

Among patients with paired samples, one or more pathogens were detected in the BALF specimens of 22 individuals at a higher frequency than in their blood samples. By comparing the m NGS results from blood and BALF in the severe pneumonia group, we found that 59.46% (22/37) of the patients had the same pathogen detected in both the BALF and blood samples. For the non-severe pneumonia group, this concordance rate was 63.16% (12/19). By analyzing the reads of the same pathogen in the 22 patients, we revealed a pronounced abundance of certain pathogens, including Legionella, Tsutsugamushi, and viral reads, in blood samples, indicating a higher detection sensitivity within plasma than BALF. In contrast, BALF samples were found to be more conducive for detecting Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Jaegeri in the lung, underscoring the unique suitability of each specimen type for certain pathogen detection (Supplemental Table 2).

Optimizing mNGS in patients with pneumonia

To reduce the negative test rate, we investigated the optimal disease status for conducting mNGS. A comprehensive analysis of 25 serological indicators was performed on data from patients with clinically confirmed positive mNGS results. The findings revealed that clinically confirmed positive mNGS results in blood were more likely to be associated with lower temperature, lower PCO2, and higher SOFA scores than negative results (p = 0.022, 0.028, and 0.038, respectively) (Fig. 6). However, there was no statistical difference between the positive rate of mNGS for BALF and blood samples test (Table 2). In a thorough analysis of 50 patient cases where pathogens were confirmed through mNGS (metagenomic next-generation sequencing), we conducted a dual-dimensional examination of antibiotic usage: firstly, whether antibiotics were administered, and secondly, the duration of antibiotic use (≤ 3 days vs. >3 days). The statistical outcomes revealed no statistically significant difference in clinical acceptance among patients in either of these two dimensions (p-values of 0.40245 and 0.86249, respectively) (Table 3).

Discussion

Pneumonia is a common disease involving the alveoli and the distal bronchus of the lung tree in patients with acute respiratory infections. Over the past few years, the widespread use of broad-spectrum antibiotics has increased the complexity of the drug-resistance spectrum of community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) pathogens, rendering the diagnosis and treatment of CAP increasingly challenging. The pathogen diagnosis of severe pneumonia has always been a challenging issue that is especially common in patients with chronic diseases or who have been exposed to antibiotics and use ventilators. The standard methods for detecting pathogenic microorganisms involve bacterial smears, cultures, or nucleic acid testing from pharyngeal swabs, sputum, or BALF, and demonstrate restricted efficacy and are capable of identifying pathogenic microorganisms in only 25–40% of patients [15–17]. This indicates that with traditional etiological methods, 50% of patients with pneumonia have an unknown etiological diagnosis. Therefore, the timely, fast, and accurate identification of multiple pathogens remains invaluable for the successful management of these patients. Clinicians have been persistently endeavoring to secure positive etiological outcomes, exploring the efficacy and implications of various specimen tests. Research indicates that blood cultures are not advisable even in severe cases of pulmonary infections, as the positive rate is extremely low [18, 19]. The guidelines for pneumonia are continuously being updated. The new guidelines have established additional criteria for determining the necessity of conducting lower respiratory tract and blood specimen testing in cases of pulmonary infections. Specifically, the 2019 edition of the American IDSA/ATS guidelines advises against the use of routine BALF and blood culture testing. Instead, lower respiratory tract smear and culture, as well as blood culture pathogen detection, are only advocated in specific scenarios. These include hospitalized patients with severe pneumonia, those at risk of MRSA and Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection (including those with past infections), and patients who have been hospitalized in the last 90 days and have undergone parenteral antimicrobial therapy [20]. The updated recommendations in the guidelines are primarily motivated by the failure of cultures to alter patient outcomes, the relatively low positive rate of cultures, the potential for contaminated bacteria to promote inappropriate antimicrobial use, and the likelihood of prolonged durations of treatment due to positive blood cultures. These factors highlight the inherent limitations of traditional pathogen detection methods. Nevertheless, the guidelines on the potential value of mNGS testing are lacking.

This study demonstrated that mNGS offers superior pathogen detection and significantly more positive results in BALF and blood mNGS than traditional cultures of corresponding specimens, which is in line with the findings of Xie et al. [10]. Even compared with combined detection methods using traditional techniques such as BALF culture, PCR, and antibody detection, the pathogen detection rate achieved by mNGS in BALF stands notably higher at 84.5%, surpassing the rate of 26.8% [17]. In cases of pulmonary infection, the results from culture using BALF have always been significantly superior to those of blood cultures [21]. We conducted NGS on both BALF and blood samples from 56 patients and showed that the percentage of positive BALF samples was significantly greater than that of positive blood samples (80.36% vs. 48.21%). The results of this study also confirm the findings by Chen et al. [15]. According to the published literature, the percentage of positive mNGS results was as high as 84%, which was greater than that of traditional culture methods for both BALF and blood. Antibiotic exposure did not affect the percentage of positive mNGS results, which was similar to the results of previous studies [22]. For patients with pulmonary infection, BALF NGS is an important clinical detection method for identifying pathogenic bacteria.

Given the vast amount of sequence information obtained through mNGS and the diverse nature of pathogen species, the interpretation of these reports can be extremely difficult, increasing the risk of obtaining false positive results [23]. To date, there have been no rigorous standards established to differentiate whether pathogens detected by mNGS are truly pathogenic, colonized, or merely false positives. To address this issue, we combined all pathogen detection results with potentially useful information that may aid in the diagnosis of pulmonary infection pathogens, such as serum immune tests, β-glucan tests, galactomannan antigen tests, and many other clinical examinations. Additionally, the authenticity and reliability of the final pathogen detection results were confirmed by the comprehensive clinical interpretation of two deputy directors or higher-ranking doctors, thereby avoiding false positive rates. To our knowledge, this study is the first to evaluate the diagnostic value of NGS using clinically accepted criteria as the gold standard. In our study cohort, the clinical acceptance rate of mNGS for BALF samples (45/56, 80.36%) was significantly greater than that for blood samples (27/56, 48.21%). Ten patients had negative BALF test results, which was inconsistent with the clinical findings. In one patient, Acinetobacter baumannii was detected but was colonized based on clinical evaluation. When the BALF and blood mNGS results were combined, the clinical acceptance rate (83.93%, 47/56) was greater than that of either method alone. For patients with respiratory infections, the positive rate and clinical acceptance of BALF culture are greater, making it more suitable for the clinical diagnosis of pneumonia. This underscores the need to promptly identify the source of infection in various locations and rapidly collect pathogens from the infected site in patients with infection. Interestingly, our data revealed that the rate of positive blood NGS results was greater than that of traditional BALF pathogen detection (48.21% vs. 39.58%). Patients with severe pneumonia are prone to bloodstream infections. Blood NGS is an alternative test when BALF specimens are not available. As the bronchoscopy for collecting BALF samples is an invasive procedure, we recommend that patients with compromised cardiopulmonary function who may not tolerate this procedure should consider blood NGS testing. This would provide an alternative method of promptly determining the etiology and minimizing the risk of serious complications.

With the increasing clinical application of metagenomic testing, our understanding of the etiology of infectious diseases is likely to include a more comprehensive range of unculturable infectious agents and coinfections. A significant advantage of mNGS lies in its ability to identify numerous potential infectious agents in a single test, leading to improved clinical outcomes. In this study, 24 patients (42.86%) had mixed infections, and the optimal anti-infective treatment plan was adjusted based on these findings. Among the mixed infections, bacterial-fungal infections (44.3%) and bacterial-fungal-viral infections (35.7%) were relatively common [10]. This finding suggests that a significant proportion of patients with pneumonia may have complex microbial infections involving the presence of multiple pathogens. Such codetections can provide valuable insights into the pathogenesis of the disease and may inform more targeted therapeutic strategies. Simultaneously, we observed that Legionella, tsutsugamushi disease, and viral reads are more prevalent in blood samples. Conversely, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Acinetobacter baumannii can be detected more readily in BALF.

There are still several limitations to the use of mNGS. Similar to traditional blood cultures, the mNGS yield may be affected by the time of sample acquisition. A previous study by Grumaz et al. [24]. showed that the positive rate of mNGS results remained constant at different time points after sepsis onset, whereas the positive rate of blood cultures decreased at later time points. Another study also indicated that mNGS is less affected by prior antibiotic exposure. A study by Wang et al. [25] demonstrated that mNGS exhibited greater sensitivity than traditional methods in individuals with a higher cumulative steroid dosage, but the positive rate gradually decreased with increasing cumulative steroid dosage. This leads us to speculate that the percentage of positive blood samples may be related to the immune and inflammatory status of the patient. The length of hospital stay is also a factor that influences the positive detection rate [26]. Our results showed that compared to patients with negative blood NGS results, patients with positive blood mNGS results had lower body temperature, lower PCO2, and higher SOFA scores (p = 0.022, 0.028, and 0.038, respectively). These findings suggest that the internal environmental status of patients is affected by blood NGS and that blood NGS should be performed as soon as possible when there is significant organ dysfunction to clarify the etiology. Traditionally, it has been assumed that a heightened level of fever corresponds to a heightened positivity rate in blood cultures [27]. However, our research findings are surprising. In our study cohort, among the clinically validated mNGS-positive patients, the peak body temperatures recorded were not elevated, and further examination of those who tested positive solely by mNGS revealed no significant correlation with their body temperatures (p = 0.200). This revelation not only challenges conventional wisdom but also suggests that clinicians do not necessarily have to wait for patients to develop a high fever before proceeding with blood mNGS testing. As a result, this approach has the potential to expedite pathogen identification and guide earlier therapeutic interventions. The regulation of the physiological indicator PCO2 is intricate, notably ventilator usage has a significant influence. Despite the limited literature directly investigating the link between PCO2 and NGS positivity rates, we discovered that the prevalence of ventilator utilization was 83.0% in patients with positive NGS results. However, there were no significant changes in the serological indicators detected by mNGS of the BALF specimens.This may be because this study focused on lung infections, which primarily occur through respiratory tract transmission. The BALF results may be influenced by factors such as specimen collection. Considering the necessity of empirical anti-infective therapy in clinical practice, we have also explored the impact of antibiotic use on mNGS. Moreover, our analysis revealed no statistically significant variation in the clinical acceptance of mNGS results when considering both whether antibiotics were prescribed and the duration of their administration (≤ 3 days vs. >3 days). This finding aligns with previous research [28, 29], reinforcing that within the context of this study’s antibiotic usage parameters, neither the presence nor the duration of antibiotic treatment significantly influenced clinical acceptance. In the future, we aim to further consolidate these findings by augmenting our study with a larger sample size and incorporating additional control variables to undertake a comprehensive assessment of the merits and constraints of clinical indicators that influence mNGS. This study has the following limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small. To ensure the consistency of paired blood and BALF samples, we excluded patients whose mNGS blood and BALF samples were collected on different days. Second, we did not distinguish between hospital-acquired pneumonia and CAP. For hospital-acquired pneumonia, the use of anti-infective agents often reduces the positive rates of both routine testing and mNGS to some extent. Third, because it is difficult to determine mixed viral infections, our study analyzed only bacteria and fungi. Therefore, viruses that can also lead pneumonia were excluded, which may have affected the sensitivity of the NGS. Fourth, as this study was prospective, our analysis focused primarily on the influence of clinical laboratory indicators on NGS results. However, we acknowledge that we were unable to conduct an exhaustive examination of the operational aspects related to specimen collection.

Overall, by comparing and analyzing the etiological test results of blood and BALF samples from patients with lung infections, this study revealed that the etiologies of lung infections are often mixed. Among the current detection methods, BALF mNGS has the highest sensitivity for identifying nonviral pathogens in lung infections. However, when BALF samples cannot be obtained, we suggest that blood NGS can also be a valuable detection method for identifying the etiology, especially when Legionella, tsutsugamushi disease, and viruses are suspected to be the causative agents. Additionally, although we analyzed clinical indicators that may affect negative NGS results, there are certain limitations, and further large-sample studies are needed to increase the rate of positive mNGS results.
