Synergistic Effects of Knowledge Distillation and Structured Pruning for Self-Supervised Speech Models
Shiva Kumar C, Jitendra Kumar Dhiman, Nagaraj Adiga, Shatrughan Singh

Abstract
Traditionally, K nowledge D istillation (KD) is used for model compression, often leading to suboptimal performance. In this paper, we evaluate the impact of combining KD loss with alternative pruning techniques, including Low-Rank Factorization (LRF) and l 0 l_{0} regularization, on a conformer-based pre-trained network under the paradigm of Self-Supervised Learning (SSL). We also propose a strategy to jointly prune and train an RNN-T-based ASR model, demonstrating that this approach yields superior performance compared to pruning a pre-trained network first and then using it for ASR training. This approach led to a significant reduction in word error rate: l 0 l_{0} and KD combination achieves the best non-streaming performance, with a 8.9 % 8.9\% R elative W ord E rror R ate (RWER) improvement over the baseline, while LRF and KD combination yields the best results for streaming ASR, improving RWER by 13.4 % 13.4\% .

This paper extends the existing research by making the following contributions.

1.

We analyze the impact of combining KD loss with either LRF- or

2.

Considering a task-specific scenario, we propose a strategy to jointly prune and fine-tune an RNN-T(RNN Transducer)-based ASR model, demonstrating that this approach performs better than pruning a pre-trained network first and then using it for ASR training.
This improvement arises because gradually pruning the weights during ASR fine-tuning allows the network to dynamically recover and adjust to the aggressively pruned model weights, leading to better overall performance (Table

We use state-of-the-art conformer-based cascaded architecture for model compression. This architecture is particularly suitable for streaming and non-streaming ASR systems.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the combined effect of KD loss and pruning techniques for model compression in conformer-based speech SSL models, addressing both streaming and non-streaming scenarios in ASR.

Related Work

II-A

Structured Pruning Using
l_{0}
Regularization

The
l_{0}
regularization is a powerful technique for pruning the neural networks by directly penalizing the number of non-zero weights in the network.
We provide the key idea of this method, while more detailed explanations can be found in
[
11
]
.
Consider a frozen teacher model
f^{tea}(\cdot)
.
Also consider the student model
f^{stu}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
with learnable parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\{\theta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{L}
.
Each
\theta_{j}
represents a group of parameters that can be pruned, such as convolution channels, attention heads, or linear projections, with a total of
L
groups.
Consider a binary variable
z_{j}
(called mask), and the pruned version of
\theta_{j}
which is given by

\tilde{\theta}_{j}=\theta_{j}z_{j}
.
The masks
\boldsymbol{z}=\{z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{L}
, follow a probability distribution
q(\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{\alpha})
parameterized by
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\{\alpha_{j}\}_{j=1}^{L}
.
The parameter
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
regulates the sparsity levels of the model weights.
The objective for
l_{0}
regularized distillation is expressed as:

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}\end{subarray}}E_{\boldsymbol{z}\sim q}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{L}_{dist}\left(f^{tea}(\boldsymbol{x_{i}}),f^{stu}(\boldsymbol{x_{i}},\boldsymbol{\tilde{{\theta}}})\right)+\lambda||\boldsymbol{\tilde{{\theta}}}||_{0}\right]

(1)

where
\mathcal{L}_{dist}(\cdot)
is the distillation loss which can be
l_{1}
,
l_{2}
, or cosine distances
[
5
]

[
8
]
,
\{\boldsymbol{x_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are training samples and

\lambda>0
is a regularization weight.
Directly optimizing the
l_{0}
norm
\|\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}\|_{0}
in Equation (
1
) is non-differentiable.
To address this, Louizos
et al.
proposed a reparameterization trick that samples the binary variable
\boldsymbol{z}
from the Hard Concrete distribution
[
11
]
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1),\boldsymbol{s(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}=\mathrm{sigmoid}\left(\left(\log\frac{\boldsymbol{u}}{1-\boldsymbol{u}}+\log\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right)/\beta\right),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\bar{s}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=(r-l)\cdot\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})+l,\ \boldsymbol{z}=\min(1,\max(0,\bar{\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})))

where
\beta
is a constant that controls the sharpness of the distribution,
s(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
and the constants
l<0
and
r>0
stretch it into the range
[l,~r]
, after which it’s clamped to
[0,1]
, making the loss differentiable.
The final loss function is formulated as follows:

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\end{subarray}}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}\end{subarray}}E_{\boldsymbol{z}\sim q}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{L}_{dist}\left(f^{tea}(\boldsymbol{x_{i}}),f^{stu}(\boldsymbol{x_{i}},\boldsymbol{\tilde{{\theta}}})\right)\right]

(2)

\displaystyle+\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})-t)+\lambda_{2}(\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})-t)^{2}

where
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
denotes the percentage of pruned parameters (the current sparsity-level),
t
denotes the desired sparsity, and
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
are trainable Lagrangian multipliers
[
11
]
.

II-B

Structured Pruning Using Low-rank Factorization

Structured pruning via low-rank factorization
[
10
]
imposes sparsity by decomposing a weight matrix
\textbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
into smaller sub-matrices
\textbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
and
\textbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
, where
r
is the rank of factorized matrices and
r\leq min(m,n)

[
18
]
.
The factorization reduces the number of parameters from
mn
(in
\boldsymbol{W}
) to
r(m+n)
(in
\boldsymbol{A}
and
\boldsymbol{B}
), it is desirable to have
r(m+n)\leq mn
.
The rank
r
controls the degree of compression.
The redundant information in
\boldsymbol{A}
and
\boldsymbol{B}
is pruned using the learnable masks
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times 1}
(Section
II-A
) as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{W}=(\boldsymbol{A}\odot\boldsymbol{z})\boldsymbol{B}^{T}=\sum_{k=1}^{r}z_{k}\boldsymbol{a_{k}}\boldsymbol{b_{k}}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}

where
\odot
denotes point-wise multiplication,
\boldsymbol{a_{k}}
and
\boldsymbol{b_{k}}
represent the
k^{th}
column of
\boldsymbol{A}
, and
\boldsymbol{B}
, respectively.
In the implementation of this method, a linear layer
\boldsymbol{W}
is decomposed into two linear layers
\boldsymbol{A}
and
\boldsymbol{B}
, with the masks inserted between them.
In addition to the low-rank factorization, the objective function for learning the target sparsity of a model is identical to the
l_{0}
regularizer, as specified in Equation (
2

III-C

Pruning of the Pre-trained Network

The network’s parameters are predominantly dominated by dense layers (the linear projections), which account for
99.8\%
, while the remaining layers contribute only
0.2\%
.
Therefore, we concentrate our pruning efforts solely on the dense layers.

Following
[
5
,
14
]
, the pre-trained network undergoes a two-stage compression process, as depicted in Figure
1
.
In Stage
1
, a pre-trained network of size
0.6
billion (B) parameters is used as the teacher, and the student model is initialized as its copy along with the masks
\boldsymbol{z}
inserted into the conformer layers.
We apply the layer-wise distillation loss from Equation (
2
) which uses both
l_{1}
and
C

S

0.5

We experimented by employing the layer-wise distillation loss for various combinations of the conformer layers and found optimal results by selecting every
5^{th}
layer across all the
24
layers, specifically layers
1,5,10,15,20
, and
24
.
When applying LRF, we decompose a dense layer into two separate dense layers, followed by the mask insertion between these two newly created layers.
The masks
\boldsymbol{z}
are learned during distillation.
The dense layer weights of the student model (
0.6
B) from Stage
1
are pruned by removing columns (rows) of weight matrices based on the learned masks, resulting in a smaller, pruned student model (
0.1
B parameters).
In Stage 2, we refine the distillation of the student model (0.1B) produced in Stage 1, employing only the layer-wise distillation component of the loss, as outlined in Equation (

This step is crucial for preserving key information that might be lost during pruning.
The distillation in Stage

Finally, the pruned student model (

VI
Conclusions

Firstly, we analyze the impact of combining knowledge distillation loss with either low-rank factorization or
l_{0}
regularization-based pruning.
Our results reveal that this combined approach consistently outperforms low-rank factorization or
l_{0}
pruning alone.
Secondly, we propose and evaluate a novel strategy for task-specific ASR scenarios where pruning and fine-tuning are performed jointly.
This method achieves notable performance gains, with the ability to adapt and recover from aggressive pruning during the fine-tuning phase being a key factor for these improvements.
Additionally, we explore the application of advanced conformer-based cascaded architectures for model compression, demonstrating their effectiveness for both streaming and non-streaming ASR systems.
This work is the first to combine knowledge distillation loss with pruning techniques in conformer-based speech SSL models, addressing both streaming and non-streaming scenarios.
Future work will examine the computational efficiency of deploying pruned models in real-time applications, focusing on the challenges associated with their practical implementation.
