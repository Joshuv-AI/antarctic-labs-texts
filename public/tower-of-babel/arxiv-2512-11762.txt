Abstract
. Relative monads provide a controlled view of computation. We generalise the monadic metalanguage to a relative setting and give a complete semantics with strong relative monads. Adopting this perspective, we generalise two existing program calculi from the literature. We provide a linear-non-linear language for graded monads, LNL-RMM, along with a semantic proof that it is a conservative extension of the graded monadic metalanguage. Additionally, we provide a complete semantics for the arrow calculus, showing it is a restricted relative monadic metalanguage. This motivates the introduction of ARMM, a computational lambda calculus-style language for arrows that conservatively extends the arrow calculus.

1.
Introduction

Over the past 15 years,
relative monads

(
1
)
have emerged as a version of monads that provide a more controlled view of computational effects. They have applications in programming theory (e.g.
(
42
;
29
;
28
)
) as well as in more mathematical directions (such as
(
2
;
4
;
3
;
11
;
9
)
).
Ordinary, non-relative monads have caused a dramatic transformation across programming languages over the past 35 years, both in theory (e.g.
(
31
;
56
;
55
)
) and in practice (e.g.
(
66
;
53
)
), as they support programming with effects in a principled, functional setting. A natural question is whether relative monads can cause a similarly profound transformation. This is the starting point for this paper.

One of the driving forces behind the adoption of ordinary monads in computer science is the monadic metalanguage, a mini programming language that precisely defines the relationships between pure functional programs and programs with effects. In this paper, we provide a
relative monadic metalanguage
, a version of the monadic metalanguage that is suited to relative monads. We show that this relative monadic metalanguage encapsulates previous calculi, namely the graded monadic metalanguage (see
Section

5
and
(
32
)
) and the arrow calculus (see
Section

6
and
(
43
)
).

The key aspect of monads that supports their application in programming theory is the requirement of
strength
. This is a critical component of the interpretation of the monadic metalanguage. Roughly, it allows monads and side-effecting computation to occur in context.
To enable a study of the relative monadic metalanguage, we also study the notion of strength in the relative case, relating notions from the literature (
Section

4
).

1.1.
A relative monad primer

Recall that monads consist of a type constructor
T
together with methods for sequencing computations in
T
, typically a bind and return. In the ordinary monadic metalanguage,
if
A
is a type, then
TA
is a type of computation returning results in
A
. But
TA
is itself a type, just the same sort of thing as
A
, and so there is no problem with also writing
TTA
, the type of
computations that return results in the type of computations.

In many situations, this is useful, but in some situations, this higher-order type
TTA
is hard to manipulate mathematically, and it is also conceptually difficult.
The idea of relative monads is to forbid
TTA
by separating types into two different sorts. The sort of the type
A
(we call the sort
\mathcal{A}
) is different from the sort of the type
TA
(we call the sort
\mathcal{C}
), so that
TTA
is not allowed, because we cannot apply the monad type constructor to something of the monad type.

For a concrete example, we consider the probability monad
D
on the category of sets.
For a finite set
A
, the set
DA
is the set of probability distributions with
A
outcomes,

\textstyle DA\quad=\quad\{p:A\to[0,1]~|~\sum_{a\in A}p(a)=1\}\text{.}

This set
DA
is not finite, even though
A
is finite.
Although we
can
extend
D
to a constructor on all sets, the resulting set
DDA
is not a very familiar concept from probability theory or statistics. Random measures such as the Dirichlet distribution
are
common in statistics, but those typical examples are not contained in
DDA
.
Therefore, it is reasonable to want to focus on
DA
where
A
is a finite set. (For more discussion of this example, see
Section

3.2
.)

Generally, the setting for a relative monad is a
category
\mathcal{A}
(e.g. finite sets), another category
\mathcal{C}
(sets),
and a functor
J:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{C}
(every finite set is, in particular, a set).
Then a relative monad is also a functor
T:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{C}
, which enforces the discipline that one cannot apply
T
to itself. (See
Section

2
for the full definitions.)

1.2.
Relative monadic metalanguages

This paper revolves around the study of metalanguages for relative monads.
We generalise ordinary monadic metalanguages to this relative setting and enforce this separation of types into two sorts in our new language. This leads to a bifurcation which generalises the monadic programming paradigm in two directions.

Paradigm 1::

The first paradigm entails programs between
\mathcal{C}
types. In this paradigm, a relative monad is a
restriction
on where the computations may occur. In particular, a computation of a computation may not exist, so they may not be arbitrarily iterated. For example,
\mathcal{A}
types may be finite types, while the effectful programs may produce infinite results of type
\mathcal{C}
. The finite distributions monad, which encodes probabilistic choice, is one such example. While the restriction to
\mathcal{A}
may seem inhibitive, it greatly simplifies program reasoning and analysis.

Paradigm 2::

The second paradigm is concerned with programs between
\mathcal{A}
types. For
\mathcal{C}
types to have any utility, there must be a way to move from them back to the
\mathcal{A}
universe. Formally, this is usually instantiated as an adjunction, for example, in Linear Logic
(
8
)
, Call by Push Value
(
40
)
, and the Enriched Effect Calculus
(
15
)
. This paradigm permits computations that are beyond the expressiveness of the
\mathcal{A}
types to be performed in
\mathcal{C}
, before being transported back to programs in
\mathcal{A}
. This allows a more general notion of computation since
\mathcal{C}
types are not necessarily
first-class
, i.e. the types of inputs and outputs to programs, but can model more general behaviour than those in
\mathcal{A}
.

In programming language theory, and in the monadic metalanguage, we are mainly concerned with
strong monads

(
35
;
36
;
49
)
. When sequencing effectful programs together, non-strong monads are not sufficient because they do not allow the threading of contexts into later programs. Strong monads provide a method for pairing a
pure
context with a computational result to be passed into the next program.

To interpret a relative monadic metalanguage, it thus makes sense to define the notion of
strong relative monads

(
65
;
60
)
. However, there are subtleties to account for when making this generalisation. Many equivalent definitions of strong monads
(
49
)
are no longer equivalent when transported to the relative setting. Varying definitions of strong relative monads have been used to generalise and express a range of programming concepts
(
65
;
60
;
59
)
. To get a holistic view of strength for relative monads, we collate the definitions and outline the relationships between them. We provide a hierarchy of subsumptions and specify the criteria for when the subsumptions flatten into equivalences. In particular, we re-establish the connection between strong monads and enriched monads in the relative setting (
Theorem

4.6
).
These results feed into our understanding of the relative monadic metalanguage and are crucial for providing semantic proofs about its applications.

1.3.
Applications of the Relative Monadic Metalanguage

Using the theory we develop, we extend the relative monadic metalanguage to capture two concepts: graded monads and arrows. Both of these notions have been modelled by program calculi previously, but using the power of strong relative monads, we provide semantically backed program calculi for these concepts. Both lead to useful conservative extensions of the calculi in the previous literature.

1.3.1.
Graded monads

Graded monads
(
32
;
64
;
51
;
18
;
37
;
22
)
are a generalisation of monads that give a semantic version of
effect usage analysis
. They have been used to give semantic accounts of memory access analysis with the state monad, output analysis for the writer monad, and branch cuts in non-determinism (e.g.
(
29
;
32
)
). Moreover, they have been used in probabilistic programs to track non-deterministic choice
(
42
)
, and to track execution traces to permit programmable inference
(
41
)
. A big advantage of graded monads is that they can implement programs that are
cost-sensitive
. A graded monadic metalanguage can be used for cost analysis of programs
(
12
)
by keeping track of the resources in the grades. Graded monads have been established as an instance of enriched relative monads
(
48
)
, and in
Theorem

5.5
we extend this connection in the strong case. The correspondence enables our development of a relative monadic style language for graded monads (which we call LNL-RMM). This language follows paradigm 2, where it is built on top of the linear-non-linear calculus
(
8
)
. This calculus has two sorts of types, linear and non-linear, and constructs that allow transitions between them. Linear type theory is a
resource-sensitive
type theory that doesn’t allow discarding or duplication of variables in contexts. It is, therefore, suitable for reasoning directly with grades, since we often interpret grades as tracking of effect usage. In particular, for cost analysis of programs
(
12
)
, the linearity here is used to ensure we cannot do any computation for free. We therefore explicitly have additional
grade types
in the linear sort that allow us to directly program with grades. By encoding a strong relative monad over the linear to non-linear transition, we obtain a language for graded monads. In previous languages, gradings are explicit parameters, whereas ours are types within the language, and the graded monad is framed as a function from grades to a relative monad (
5
). These first-class grades enable more flexible programming and code reuse, as described in Section
5
.

1.3.2.
Arrows

Arrows are a programming language paradigm that generalise monads
(
25
)
. While monads are a tool for sequencing effectful computations, arrows model computation that is not strictly sequential. They have a broad range of applications in functional programs, such as for parsers and printers
(
27
)
, web interaction
(
25
)
, circuits
(
52
)
, graphical user interfaces
(
13
)
, and robotics
(
24
)
. The established connection between arrows and strong relative monads
(
1
;
65
)
motivates our development of a relative monadic language for arrows (which we call ARMM). This language also follows paradigm 2, where the standard programs are between
\mathcal{A}
types, while
\mathcal{C}
types model parametric computations on inputs of type
\mathcal{A}
. The language we develop is a conservative extension of the arrow calculus
(
43
)
(
Theorem

6.6
). It has all the expressiveness of arrows but additionally allows for variables of arrow computations, which promotes the reusability and flexibility of the programs. Moreover, it has more principled semantics, making it amenable to generalisations with additional type formers, other variants such as a linear language for arrows, or semantic proofs of language properties.

3.
Strong monads, strong relative monads and the relative monadic metalanguage

Program contexts are often larger than a single variable. If we want to sum two numbers together, then we already require more than one variable. However, monads do not have sufficient structure for interpreting the sequencing of computations with multiple free variables. For example, say we intend to sequence the following two effectful programs.

\Gamma\vdash u:TA\Gamma,x:A\vdash t:TB

Interpreted as morphisms, these become

{\llbracket u\rrbracket}:{\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket}\to T{\llbracket A\rrbracket},{\llbracket t\rrbracket}:{\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket}\times{\llbracket A\rrbracket}\to T{\llbracket B\rrbracket}.

To sequence these, we need to produce from
{\llbracket t\rrbracket}
a map
{\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket}\times T{\llbracket A\rrbracket}\to T{\llbracket B\rrbracket}
, i.e. apply Kleisli extension
under the context
\Gamma
. Strong monads provide the required semantics for such an operation. In the general setting, strong monads are defined with respect to monoidal products, of which the Cartesian product is an instance. We employ this setting for a linear type system (see
(
19
;
7
;
8
)
) in

3.2.
Strongly finitary monads in the relative monadic metalanguage

The relative monadic metalanguage can be thought of as a very simple
first-order programming language. The strength allows us to reason in context,
which is crucial for a programming language. The relative aspect
allows us to keep to a first order. Even if the ordinary monadic
metalanguage does not have explicit function types, it is
not really first-order, because it can talk about computations that return
computations (
TTA
). This type would be represented in Standard ML
with the type
unit -> (unit -> A)
, since functions of unit type are
thunks and
unit -> (-)
is a monad; notice that it is indeed higher
order there.

This cut-down, first-order setting is ideal for equationally presenting
notions of computational effect, without worrying about aspects of higher-order
computation that are often orthogonal. The idea is that a
computational effect can be presented by giving a first-order
programming language together with equations that it satisfies.
One can often add higher-order features separately, afterwards.

In this section, we demonstrate how this cut-down approach is already being taken in the literature, and can be
thought of as making essential use of an informal flavour of the relative monadic
metalanguage,
with examples from both probability and finitary
\mathbf{Set}
-monads (
Section

3.2.1
) and local store and more general finitary enriched monads (
Section

3.2.2
).

Recall that a

6.1.
The arrow relative monadic metalanguage (ARMM)

Identifying the models of the arrow calculus as a
W
-strong relative monad suggests that it can be extended to the framework of RMM. We begin by identifying the key features of the models in
Definition

6.1
to achieve this. The command judgement (
6
), while interpreted as a morphism in
\mathcal{A}
, is primarily used in the semantics as a morphism in
{\mathcal{A}\mathbf{Cat}(\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{op}},\mathcal{A})}
. The critical link is via the following isomorphism:

(7)

{\mathcal{A}\mathbf{Cat}(\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{op}},\mathcal{A})}(KA\times JB,TC)\cong\mathcal{A}(A,TCB).

The objects in
{\mathcal{A}\mathbf{Cat}(\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{op}},\mathcal{A})}
can be thought of as arrow types with parametric input. In
Definition

6.1
, the semantics of the types
JA
is given by the enriched Yoneda embedding, and so can be understood as pure arrow types with output
A
. Types
KA
have semantics via constant presheaves and do not interact with the relative monad structure. The role of these types is via (
7
); they permit the movement of variables between
\mathcal{A}
and
{\mathcal{A}\mathbf{Cat}(\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{op}},\mathcal{A})}
contexts. Finally, types
TA
are effectful arrow types with output
A
, parametric over some input.

However, the role of
{\mathcal{A}\mathbf{Cat}(\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{op}},\mathcal{A})}
is not essential apart from providing canonical interpretations of
J
and
K
. We can therefore abstract the explicit category
{\mathcal{A}\mathbf{Cat}(\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{op}},\mathcal{A})}
to an arbitrary Cartesian category
\mathcal{C}
, while maintaining Cartesian functors
J,K:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{C}
satisfying certain conditions. In particular, we require that parametric arrow types in
\mathcal{C}
can be abstracted over a pure input (capturing the isomorphism (
7
)), and abstracting pure arrows results in function types:

(8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(KA\times JB,X)

\displaystyle\cong\mathcal{A}(A,B\Rightarrow X)

(9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(A\times B,C)

\displaystyle\cong\mathcal{A}(A,B\Rightarrow JC)

Then, we can define the original arrow types as
A\leadsto B::=A\Rightarrow TB
and function types as
A\to B::=A\Rightarrow JB
. We additionally require a natural coercion from
K
to
J
to allow variables in the
J
context to be moved to variables in the
K
context. This is admissible in the arrow calculus and is semantically validated by the canonical map from the Yoneda embedding to the constant presheaf functor. It is derivable from (
8
) and (
9
) by applying the following isomorphism to the identity morphism.

\mathcal{A}(A,A)\cong\mathcal{A}(A\times 1,A)\cong\mathcal{A}(A,1\Rightarrow JA)\cong\mathcal{C}(KA\times J1,JA)\cong\mathcal{C}(KA,JA)

We thus define the arrow relative monadic metalanguage (ARMM) as follows.

\mathcal{A}
types:

\displaystyle A,B

\displaystyle::=1~|~A\times B~|~A\Rightarrow X

\mathcal{C}
types:

\displaystyle X,Y

\displaystyle::=1~|~X\times Y~|~JA~|~KA~|~TA

\mathcal{A}
contexts:

\displaystyle\Gamma,\Delta

\displaystyle::=\emptyset~|~\Gamma,a:A

\mathcal{C}
contexts:

\displaystyle\Phi

\displaystyle::=\emptyset~|~\Phi,x:X

The ARMM terms for
\mathcal{A}
-types are the standard terms for a Cartesian category. The ARMM terms for
\mathcal{C}
-types beyond the standard Cartesian ones are:

\frac{\Gamma;\Delta;\Phi\vdash_{\mathcal{C}}t:KA\Gamma,a:A;\Delta;\Phi\vdash_{\mathcal{C}}s:X}{\Gamma;\Delta;\Phi\vdash_{\mathcal{C}}\mathsf{let}\,K(a)\,\mathsf{be}\,t\,\mathsf{in}\,s:X}\frac{\Gamma;\Delta;\Phi\vdash_{\mathcal{C}}t:JA\Gamma;\Delta,a:A;\Phi\vdash_{\mathcal{C}}s:X}{\Gamma;\Delta;\Phi\vdash_{\mathcal{C}}\mathsf{let}\,J(a)\,\mathsf{be}\,t\,\mathsf{in}\,s:X}\frac{\Gamma;\Delta;\Phi\vdash_{\mathcal{C}}t:JA}{\Gamma;\Delta;\Phi\vdash_{\mathcal{C}}{\mathsf{return}\ t}:TA}\frac{\Gamma;\Delta;\Phi\vdash_{\mathcal{C}}t:TA\Gamma;\Delta;x:JA\vdash_{\mathcal{C}}s:TB}{\Gamma;\Delta;\Phi\vdash_{\mathcal{C}}\mathsf{do}\,x\leftarrow t\,\mathsf{in}\,s:TB}

Note that
\mathcal{C}
judgements have three contexts. The first two typing rules validate that context
\Gamma
is a
K
context and context
\Delta
is a
J
context, similar to the style of the arrow calculus. The third context

can have arbitrary
\mathcal{C}
types, such as computations, and permits categorical semantics via the general RMM framework. The latter two typing rules are the standard
J
-relative monad terms for
T
, where binding is restricted to
K
and
J
contexts. The ARMM terms for the interaction between
\mathcal{A}
-types and
\mathcal{C}
-types are:

\frac{\Gamma\vdash_{\mathcal{A}}u:A\Rightarrow X\Gamma,\Delta\vdash_{\mathcal{A}}v:A}{\Gamma;\Delta;\Phi\vdash_{\mathcal{C}}{u\bullet v}:X}\frac{\Gamma;a:A;\emptyset\vdash_{\mathcal{C}}t:X}{\Gamma\vdash_{\mathcal{A}}{\lambda(a:A).t}:A\Rightarrow X}\\
\frac{\Gamma\vdash_{\mathcal{A}}u:A}{\Gamma;\Delta;\Phi\vdash_{\mathcal{C}}K(u):KA}\frac{\Gamma,\Delta\vdash_{\mathcal{A}}u:A}{\Gamma;\Delta;\Phi\vdash_{\mathcal{C}}J(u):JA}\frac{\Gamma\vdash_{\mathcal{A}}u:A\Rightarrow JB\Gamma\vdash_{\mathcal{A}}v:A}{\Gamma\vdash_{\mathcal{A}}{u\ v}:B}

The full term calculus and equational theory of ARMM is given in
Section

B.4
.
Regular application and abstraction under the derivable function type
A\to B::=A\Rightarrow JB
are:

\frac{\Gamma\vdash_{\mathcal{A}}u:A\to B\Gamma\vdash_{\mathcal{A}}v:A}{\Gamma\vdash_{\mathcal{A}}{u\ v}:B}\frac{\Gamma,a:A\vdash_{\mathcal{A}}u:B}{\Gamma\vdash_{\mathcal{A}}{\lambda(a:A).J(u)}:A\to B}

Theorem 6.6.

The interpretation of the arrow calculus in ARMM is a conservative extension.

Proof.

From

, all models of the arrow calculus
(\mathcal{A},T)
are models of ARMM. Furthermore, the semantics of the interpretation of an arrow calculus term coincides, or corresponds via
\chi
, with its semantics as a model of the arrow calculus. Hence, the term model must validate all the equations of ARMM.
∎

Remark
.

The ARMM calculus could be generalised further by allowing binds in arbitrary contexts.

\frac{\Gamma;\Delta;\Phi\vdash_{\mathcal{C}}t:TA\Gamma;\Delta;\Phi,x:JA\vdash_{\mathcal{C}}s:TB}{\Gamma;\Delta;\Phi\vdash_{\mathcal{C}}\mathsf{do}\,x\leftarrow t\,\mathsf{in}\,s:TB}

This would make the models consist of a strong

-strong
J

to equate these notions of strength because
\mathcal{A}
is not cocomplete. However, an analogous conservative extension result may be possible by embedding
\mathcal{A}
into a cocomplete category, such as
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}
.

7.
Conclusion and future work

We have introduced a language RMM for strong relative monads and demonstrated a significant advantage to working with term calculi based on it. RMM generalises the monadic metalanguage
(
50
)
in two directions:

•

It allows us to
restrict
our notion of computation by programming with
\mathcal{C}
types that only allow computations on another sort of types
\mathcal{A}
. This was done in
Section

3.2
to restrict to computations on
finite
datatypes and thus provide a more fine-grained approach to computation.

•

It allows us to
expand
our notion of computation. By programming with
\mathcal{A}
types with
partial access
to an additional sort
\mathcal{C}
, it permits a broader notion of computations than what is allowed in
\mathcal{A}
. We saw in
Section

5
and
Section

6
that this allowed programming with cost-sensitive computations through graded monads and a wider variety of effects through arrows.

In particular, the languages LNL-RMM introduced in
Section

5
for graded monads and ARMM in
Section

6
for arrows:

(1)

Are no less general than their predecessors since we prove conservative extension theorems,
Theorem

5.13
and
Theorem

6.6
.

(2)

Are more expressive than their predecessors, where, in particular, LNL-RMM has grade types in the language, which allows for more flexible programming.

(3)

Come with built-in sound denotational semantics from the RMM, as shown in
Theorem

5.12
and
Theorem

6.5
.

The denotational semantics of all the example languages in the paper, including the new semantics provided for the arrow calculus (
Theorem

6.2
), was possible due to the notion of strong relative monad we introduced in
Section

3
, and its generalisations in
Section

4.2
. We justified that this definition of strong relative monads is the appropriate one for semantics of programming languages by establishing its equivalence to enriched relative monads (
Theorem

4.6
). We also extended the broader literature on relative monads
(
2
;
4
;
3
;
65
)
to facilitate the design of these languages.

Future work.

We have focused on two applications of relative monads: graded monads and arrows.
The positive results suggest that other applications of relative monads will also benefit from taking the relative monadic metalanguage as a starting point. One example is the
simple relational specification monads

(
45
)
, which are (strong) relative monads on the product functor
\mathbf{Set}\times\mathbf{Set}\to\mathbf{Set}
.

Since we have shown that the relative monadic metalanguage is a unifying starting point for
diverse aspects of programming languages, we envisage that more advanced methods from monad theory will benefit from a general analysis in terms of relative monads. We have in mind methods from program logics, such as monad liftings for logical relations
(
31
;
30
)
, which have not yet been investigated from the relative point of view.

Appendix B
Full term calculi and their equational theories

We require the following reflexivity, symmetry, transitivity, and substitution laws to hold for all judgements for the remainder of this appendix:

\frac{\Gamma\vdash u:X}{\Gamma\vdash u=u:X}\frac{\Gamma\vdash u=v:X}{\Gamma\vdash v=u:X}\frac{\Gamma\vdash u=v:X\Gamma\vdash v=w:X}{\Gamma\vdash u=w:X}\frac{\Gamma\vdash u:X\Gamma,x:X\vdash s=t:Y}{\Gamma\vdash s[u/x]=t[u/x]:Y}

We also require that all term constructors satisfy
