Heterogeneity of azithromycin resistance markers in Neisseria gonorrhoeae and challenges in implementing molecular assays for routine surveillance
Authors: Qianqin Yuan, Leshan Xiu, Junping Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeisseria gonorrhoeae is the second most prevalent bacterial sexually transmitted infection and a major contributor to the global antimicrobial resistance crisis. The World Health Organization (WHO) has designated N. gonorrhoeae as a high-priority pathogen due to its resistance to nearly all empirically recommended therapies. While dual therapy with ceftriaxone and azithromycin initially demonstrated efficacy, the rapid global rise in azithromycin resistance over the past five years threatens the efficacy of this regimen. Resistance mechanisms, including mutations in the 23S rRNA gene and overexpression of the MtrCDE efflux pump, are highly heterogeneous, complicating molecular diagnostic efforts. This review examines the evolving landscape of azithromycin resistance, highlighting key mechanisms of resistance, and recent epidemiological and genomic surveillance data. We discuss challenges in implementing molecular assays for routine resistance monitoring due to molecular heterogeneity in azithromycin resistance markers and emphasize the critical need for tailored approaches based on local genetic epidemiology. Enhanced surveillance and molecular diagnostics are essential to sustaining effective treatment strategies and combating gonococcal resistance.Keywords: Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Antimicrobial resistance, Marker, Heterogeneity, Molecular assay, Surveillance

Neisseria gonorrhoeae, the causative agent of gonorrhea, stands as the second most prevalent bacterial sexually transmitted infection worldwide and represents a significant contributor to the growing global antimicrobial resistance crisis [1,2]. The World Health Organization has identified N. gonorrhoeae as a pathogen of high priority due to its alarming ability to acquire resistance to all recommended antimicrobial agents used for empirical therapy [3,4]. The widespread emergence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in N. gonorrhoeae has been facilitated by the accumulation of resistance determinants, which can impose fitness costs or, in some cases, confer fitness advantages depending on the genetic and environmental context [5,6]. As a result, the treatment landscape for gonorrhea is increasingly constrained, with a single intramuscular dose of ceftriaxone (1 g) currently recommended by the World Health Organization as the first-line treatment for uncomplicated N. gonorrhoeae infection [7]. However, the therapeutic pipeline remains limited, with only a few new antimicrobial agents in late-stage clinical development.

Historically, the introduction of dual therapy with ceftriaxone and azithromycin was a strategic response to the growing concerns about ceftriaxone monotherapy failure [3]. This combination regimen not only targeted gonococcal infections but also provided therapeutic activity against Chlamydia trachomatis and Mycoplasma genitalium [8,9], which frequently co-occur with gonorrhea. The combination therapy was initially effective in some settings, as most ceftriaxone-resistant gonococcal strains remained susceptible to azithromycin [10]. This dual approach contributed to reducing the international spread of ceftriaxone resistance [11]. However, over the past five years, there has been a disturbing global rise in azithromycin resistance, undermining its effectiveness as part of dual therapy and posing a significant threat to the continued efficacy of current treatment protocols [2,3,12].

This rising resistance, coupled with the high global prevalence of gonorrhea, presents an escalating public health challenge [13]. Contributing factors include the extensive and often inappropriate use of antibiotics, suboptimal diagnostic practices, limited surveillance systems, and delayed updates to clinical management guidelines [14]. Additionally, N. gonorrhoeae's remarkable capacity to acquire or develop resistance further exacerbates the situation, suggesting that both the global burden of gonorrhea and the incidence of its severe complications will likely increase in the coming years [15]. Without improved global actions and research efforts, it is conceivable that gonorrhea could become increasingly difficult to treat, leading to more widespread morbidity, complications, and treatment failures.

Furthermore, the future therapeutic landscape for gonorrhea appears increasingly bleak [12]. Although two novel antimicrobials, zoliflodacin and gepotidacin, have recently been approved for the treatment of uncomplicated N. gonorrhoeae infections [16,17], few additional antimicrobial agents are currently in clinical development, leaving the broader treatment pipeline limited [12,18]. Concurrently, resistance to ceftriaxone itself continues to increase in many regions, and the emergence of gonococcal strains with concurrent resistance to both ceftriaxone and azithromycin has been reported in several WHO regions [19]. This dual resistance further complicates the treatment landscape and raises concerns about the long-term sustainability of dual antimicrobial therapy [15]. Moreover, the high cost of these treatments limits their widespread use in resource-limited settings, where AMR surveillance and control measures are often inadequate. This disparity further hampers efforts to prevent the emergence and global spread of gonococcal resistance. Given the growing challenges posed by AMR in N. gonorrhoeae, it is evident that the available dual therapy regimen may not be a long-term solution. New therapeutic strategies, enhanced global surveillance, and robust research efforts are urgently needed to ensure that gonorrhea remains treatable and that the threat of untreatable gonorrhea does not become a reality in the near future.

Current trends in azithromycin resistance and surveillance status

Azithromycin is an oral antibiotic commonly used to treat infections such as Chlamydia trachomatis and Mycoplasma genitalium, and it has demonstrated good efficacy against N. gonorrhoeae infections [20,21]. As part of the dual therapy regimen for gonorrhea, azithromycin is often prescribed in combination with ceftriaxone. Despite its initial effectiveness, the widespread use of azithromycin particularly in settings where antibiotics are easily accessible without prescription or regulation, has contributed significantly to the development of resistance. Over the past decade, azithromycin resistance has steadily increased in both high- and low-income countries, with notable geographic variability driven by local treatment practices, surveillance infrastructure, and antibiotic use patterns. Although azithromycin is no longer routinely recommended for the treatment of gonorrhoea in many settings, increasing resistance limits its utility as a therapeutic option and highlights the importance of continued AMR surveillance.

In an article by Daniel Golparian and colleagues [22], published in The Lancet Microbe, a retrospective genomic surveillance study of N. gonorrhoeae isolates collected across 21 European Economic Area (EEA) countries in 2020 was presented. The study, conducted through the European Gonococcal Antimicrobial Surveillance Programme (Euro-GASP), found that azithromycin resistance (EUCAST epidemiological cut-off value of MIC>1 mg/L) had risen to 9.4% (181 of 1932) in EEA countries, marking an increase from 8.0% (191 of 2375) reported in 2018 (Table 1). Similarly, recent studies [3,23,24] have reported a substantial increase in azithromycin resistance in many countries (Fig. 1), further limiting the utility of azithromycin as a therapeutic option and highlighting the need for sustained AMR surveillance. Consequently, clinicians are increasingly likely to encounter cases of N. gonorrhoeae treatment failure in the coming years. In the United States, azithromycin resistance in N. gonorrhoeae has steadily increased over the past few decades. It should be noted that there is currently no internationally agreed clinical breakpoint for azithromycin resistance in N. gonorrhoeae. Consequently, different surveillance programs and guidelines have adopted different interpretive criteria, including ECOFFs and surveillance thresholds. From 1992 to 2012, the resistance rate rose from 0.01% to 0.4%. Between 2013 and 2014, the proportion of N. gonorrhoeae isolates with an azithromycin MIC above the susceptibility cutoff (≥2 mg/L) surged by fourfold, from 0.6% in 2013 to 2.5% in 2014, as reported through the Gonococcal Isolate Surveillance Project. This upward trend continued nationally, reaching a high of 4.6% in 2018 [25]. By 2019, the proportion of isolates with an elevated azithromycin MIC of ≥2 mg/L had further risen to 5.9%, indicating a concerning rise in resistance [24]. Similarly, azithromycin resistance in N. gonorrhoeae has continued to increase globally, with notable geographic variation [26]. According to the WHO Global Gonococcal Antimicrobial Surveillance Programme (WHO GASP) 2019-2022 report [26], azithromycin-resistant isolates were reported by 46 (87%) of 53 countries in 2022, compared with 44 (76%) of 58 countries in 2018. Furthermore, 36 (68%) of 53 countries reported azithromycin resistance in at least 5% of isolates in 2022, highlighting the widespread dissemination of azithromycin resistance worldwide. Marked regional differences were observed. In the WHO Western Pacific region, seven (88%) of eight reporting countries detected azithromycin-resistant N. gonorrhoeae isolates in 2022, compared with five (71%) of seven countries in 2018. Five countries, including Brunei, Cambodia, China, Japan, and Singapore, reported azithromycin resistance in at least 5% of isolates in 2022, whereas Australia and New Zealand reported lower resistance levels. In particular, China has also reported a substantial burden of azithromycin resistance. Data from the China Gonococcal Resistance Surveillance Program (China-GRSP) showed that resistance to azithromycin reached 16.9% in 2022 [27], marking a substantial increase in resistance from 2017 to 2022. Conversely, all isolates reported from the Philippines remained susceptible to azithromycin. These findings emphasize the heterogeneous distribution of azithromycin resistance and highlight the importance of region-specific surveillance strategies to inform molecular diagnostic approaches.

Surveillance plays a critical role in tracking the spread of azithromycin resistance and informing treatment guidelines [2,28]. Global initiatives such as the Gonococcal Antimicrobial Surveillance Programme (GASP), established by the WHO in 1992, collect data from participating countries to assess trends in antimicrobial resistance and guide regional treatment recommendations [13,29]. However, surveillance efforts face significant challenges. While many high-income countries have robust surveillance systems in place, reporting is inconsistent in low- and middle-income countries due to limitations in laboratory capacity, funding, and infrastructure. Although global gonococcal AMR surveillance has expanded, only 77 countries and territories reported gonococcal AMR data to WHO GASP during 2019-2022 [26]. This gap in global surveillance limits the ability to monitor resistance trends comprehensively and impedes efforts to develop targeted interventions. In particular, the absence of surveillance data from many regions, especially in parts of Africa and Southeast Asia, means that the full extent of azithromycin resistance in N. gonorrhoeae remains unclear [3,13]. Furthermore, underreporting of treatment failures exacerbates the problem, as failures in dual therapy may be more widespread than current data suggest. While 81-83% of countries reported resistance to azithromycin between 2014 and 2016, the full scope of the problem remains unclear due to gaps in surveillance [13]. The true extent of resistance, especially in low-income and middle-income countries, may be significantly underreported. Expanded surveillance efforts, including better reporting mechanisms, data sharing, and capacity-building in low-resource settings, are essential to monitor the spread of resistance and inform treatment guidelines.

Mechanisms of azithromycin resistance

Azithromycin inhibits bacterial protein synthesis by binding to the peptidyltransferase region within domain V of the 23S rRNA in the 50S ribosomal subunit, thereby preventing translocation of peptidyl-tRNA and blocking the peptide exit channel [[30], [31], [32]]. However, resistance to azithromycin has emerged as a significant concern, complicating treatment strategies. This resistance can be attributed to a variety of genetic mechanisms [13,32], primarily involving modifications to the ribosomal target, efflux pump overexpression, and mutations in regulatory genes (Fig. 2).

The schematic illustrates the major molecular mechanisms that can contribute to reduced susceptibility or resistance to azithromycin. (1) Mutations in the mtrR promoter or coding region, as well as mosaic changes within the mtr loci, can increase expression of the MtrCDE efflux pump and enhance macrolide efflux. (2) Mutations in the 23S rRNA, particularly C2611T and A2059G, alter the macrolide-binding site of the 50S ribosomal subunit and are associated with increased azithromycin MICs. (3) Acquired erm genes, including ermB, ermC, and ermF, encode rRNA methylases that modify the 23S rRNA target and reduce macrolide binding. (4) Mutations in rplD and rplV, encoding the 50S ribosomal proteins L4 and L22, respectively, can alter the ribosomal target and contribute to reduced azithromycin susceptibility. (5) Increased expression of the MacAB efflux system may contribute to elevated azithromycin MICs. (6) The mef-encoded efflux systems can export macrolides and contribute to reduced azithromycin susceptibility. The relative contribution and prevalence of these mechanisms vary across N. gonorrhoeae populations and geographic settings.

One of the major mechanisms of azithromycin resistance in N. gonorrhoeae involves mutations in the 23S ribosomal RNA (rRNA), which serves as the binding site for the antibiotic [32,33]. Mutations at specific positions in the 23S rRNA can decrease azithromycin's affinity for its target, leading to resistance. The A2059G transition in the 23S rRNA gene (E. coli numbering) is particularly associated with high-level resistance [34]. Strains harboring this mutation in all four 23S rRNA gene alleles exhibit MICs of azithromycin as high as 256-4096 mg/L [32]. A single A2059G mutation in one allele can confer low-level resistance, but these strains can rapidly develop high-level resistance upon prolonged exposure to subinhibitory concentrations of azithromycin. Additionally, the C2611T mutation is linked to moderate resistance [2,32], with MICs ranging from 2 to 32 mg/L, when present in all four rRNA alleles. Methylation is another known mechanism for macrolide resistance. The modification of the ribosomal target is also influenced by the activity of rRNA methylases, such as those encoded by the erm genes (ermB, ermC and ermF), which methylate specific nucleotides in the 23S rRNA, blocking macrolide binding [35,36]. Although erm genes confer high-level resistance to erythromycin (MICs of 4-16 mg/L) and low-level resistance to azithromycin (MICs of 1-4 mg/L), their presence in N. gonorrhoeae isolates has become rare in recent years [37]. In clinical strains, the ermF and ermB genes have been detected, with some strains showing significant homology to ermF of Bacteroides fragilis. Despite this, the role of erm-mediated methylation in the evolution of macrolide resistance in N. gonorrhoeae is becoming less prominent. Several additional factors may contribute to azithromycin resistance in N. gonorrhoeae. Mutations in rplD and rplV, which encode ribosomal proteins L4 and L22, can also moderately increase azithromycin MICs without reaching resistance thresholds [38]. Substitutions like G70D in the rplD gene, encoding ribosomal protein L4, can lead to a 2.5∼4-fold increase in azithromycin MICs [30,39]. Tandem duplications in ribosomal protein L22 (e.g., ARAK at position 90 or KGPSLK at position 83) also result in higher MICs [30]. Collectively, these mechanisms underscore the complex interplay of genetic factors driving azithromycin resistance in N. gonorrhoeae.

In addition to target modification, overexpression of efflux pumps (including MtrCDE, MacAB and mef-encoded pumps) [32,40] is another critical mechanism of azithromycin resistance in N. gonorrhoeae. The MtrCDE efflux pump is the most well-characterized system that contributes to macrolide resistance. Mutations in the mtrR gene, which encodes the regulator of the MtrCDE pump, lead to increased expression of the efflux pump, resulting in reduced intracellular concentrations of azithromycin and other antibiotics [32]. The most common mutation in mtrR involves a substitution in the MtrR protein at position G45D [34]. Other mutations, such as G45S, A86T, and Y105H, have also been associated with resistance, although they are less frequently observed. A mosaic-like structure of the mtr gene has been reported to significantly raise the MIC of azithromycin in some strains [41], underscoring the complex genetic architecture underlying efflux pump-mediated resistance. Particularly, mosaic alleles are typically the result of recombination events at the mtrR and mtrD genes involving sequences from N. meningitidis or N. lactamica [42]. The acquisition of these mosaic alleles from other Neisseria species results in alterations in both the pump and its regulation, leading to increased resistance to azithromycin. Furthermore, the overexpression of MtrCDE efflux pumps can also occur due to mutations in the promoter regions of the mtrR and mtrCDE genes, which increase the transcription of these genes and enhance the efflux activity. In particular, the deletion of a single nucleotide in the 13-bp inverted repeat sequence in the mtrR promoter region is linked to elevated efflux pump expression and further contributes to the azithromycin resistance phenotype [43]. The MacAB and mef-encoded efflux pumps are other systems implicated in azithromycin resistance [32]. The MacAB pump is found in N. gonorrhoeae and has been shown to elevate the MIC of macrolides when overexpressed. The mefA gene encodes a membrane protein that actively exports macrolides out of the bacterial cell, similarly contributing to resistance. Although these pumps are less studied in N. gonorrhoeae, their presence in other bacteria suggests they may play a role in macrolide resistance in gonococci.

Challenges in molecular surveillance

Given the current situation, it is crucial to enhance surveillance to monitor and predict azithromycin resistance trends in N. gonorrhoeae, thereby ensuring the continued efficacy of recommended treatment regimens [2]. Fortunately, significant progress has been made over the past few decades in understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying antimicrobial resistance. This advancement has paved the way for the development of molecular assays to effectively monitor gonococcal azithromycin resistance. Historically, mutations in the 23S rRNA gene, particularly A2059G and C2611T, were the predominant and best-characterized mechanisms of azithromycin resistance and remain the principal determinants of high-level resistance. A systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrate that molecular assays targeting the A2059G mutation provide high accuracy in detecting high-level azithromycin resistance in N. gonorrhoeae [44]. Similarly, molecular detection methods focusing on the C2611T mutation exhibit strong accuracy in identifying moderate-level azithromycin resistance [45]. Several molecular assays have been developed based on these targets, but their effectiveness appears to be increasingly challenged [34,46,47].

Another key finding of the study by Daniel Golparian et al. was that only 10.5% (19 of 181) of azithromycin-resistant isolates collected in 2020 (MIC 2-32 and ≥ 256 mg/L) harboring mutations in the 23S rRNA target, which were previously identified as the primary cause of azithromycin resistance (Table 1). In contrast, approximately 89% (161 of 181) of the resistant isolates (MIC 2-8 mg/L) contained either mtrD/mtrR promoter mosaic 2 or semi-mosaic mtrD variants. Specifically, data from the European gonococcal population in 2018 showed that 24.1% (46 of 191) of azithromycin-resistant strains could be attributed solely to 23S rRNA mutations [48], while 69.6% (133 of 191) were attributable to the mtr mosaic. Azithromycin resistance has significantly increased, which the authors attribute primarily to the continued expansion of specific N. gonorrhoeae sequence typing for antimicrobial resistance (NG-STAR) clonal complexes (CCs), notably CC63, CC168, and CC213 (which harbor mtrD/mtrR promoter mosaic 2), as well as the emergence of the novel NG-STAR CC1031 (which carries semi-mosaic mtrD variant 13) [22].

Conclusion and future directions

The rapid rise in azithromycin resistance highlights the need for urgent action to preserve effective treatments for N. gonorrhoeae. Efforts to combat azithromycin resistance would greatly benefit from an enhanced capacity to predict its emergence and understand its dynamics. WHO has advocated for the development of molecular assays capable of detecting resistance markers to guide therapeutic decisions [3]. These assays hold promise for supporting antimicrobial stewardship initiatives and mitigating selection pressure. However, challenges in the validity, accuracy, and specificity of such assays pose obstacles to their widespread implementation in routine azithromycin surveillance within clinical settings, particularly across geographically distinct regions. Discrepancies in accuracy and specificity are likely attributable to the heterogeneity in molecular markers associated with azithromycin resistance observed across different geographic locations [49]. This heterogeneity presents a significant practical challenge for the implementation of molecular diagnostic technologies (Fig. 3). For instance, in regions characterized by a high prevalence of mtrD mosaic variants, an assay primarily targeting the 23S rRNA with lower specificity would yield a markedly low positive predictive value. It is imperative to tailor molecular targets utilized in molecular assays according to the local genetic epidemiology of N. gonorrhoeae to ensure their effectiveness [2,49].

Additionally, increased genomic epidemiological surveillance, supported by strengthened laboratory and research capacity, will be essential. These efforts will provide crucial insights into the genetic diversity and distribution of azithromycin resistance markers, informing the development of more accurate and regionally relevant molecular assays. Achieving this goal remains particularly challenging in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where routine whole-genome sequencing is often constrained by limited laboratory capacity and resources. In these settings, a pragmatic sentinel-site surveillance strategy may provide a feasible alternative. Representative sentinel laboratories could routinely perform antimicrobial susceptibility testing and periodically refer a subset of isolates to national or regional reference laboratories for genomic characterization. Rather than sequencing every isolate, periodic genomic surveillance of representative isolates would enable the timely detection of emerging resistance determinants while minimizing costs. To facilitate global surveillance and the timely updating of molecular diagnostic targets, a standardized minimum dataset, including antimicrobial susceptibility results, specimen source, geographic location, year of collection, and molecular resistance determinants where available, should be reported to the WHO GASP. Such a tiered surveillance framework would facilitate the continuous refinement of region-specific molecular assays while strengthening global surveillance of gonococcal antimicrobial resistance. Changes in treatment practices may also reshape the selection pressure acting on azithromycin resistance. The shift away from routine azithromycin–ceftriaxone dual therapy towards ceftriaxone monotherapy is expected to reduce direct azithromycin exposure for gonorrhoea treatment, which could potentially alter the trajectory of azithromycin resistance [11]. However, the magnitude and direction of this effect remain uncertain, as resistance dynamics may also be influenced by macrolide use for other infections, fitness effects, and the expansion or replacement of resistant gonococcal lineages. Continued phenotypic and genomic surveillance will therefore be essential to determine how evolving treatment practices affect the prevalence and diversity of azithromycin resistance determinants. Efforts should also focus on developing novel antimicrobials and combination therapies to address the diminishing therapeutic landscape. Collaboration among researchers, clinicians, and public health stakeholders will be critical to ensuring sustainable control of gonococcal infections in the face of evolving resistance challenges.
