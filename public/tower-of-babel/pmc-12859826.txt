Correlation analysis between the coronal and sagittal axis angles of both knees and lumbar lordosis in middle-aged and elderly patients with knee osteoarthritis
Authors: Xinxiang Tang, Yi Jiang, Anqi Wu, Sizheng Gu, Xinyao Hao, Xuerong Qiu, Xianlun Pang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractsBackgroundThe lumbar lordosis angle is essential for maintaining the sagittal balance of the spine and is closely linked to the incidence of low back pain. Both domestic and international studies have demonstrated that patients with knee osteoarthritis who also experience low back pain exhibit more severe osteoarthritic symptoms and greater functional impairment. Nevertheless, current research on knee osteoarthritis predominantly concentrates on the deformities of the knee joint itself, with few studies investigating the potential relationship between alterations in coronal and sagittal alignment angles of the knee—such as the femoral angle, femoral–tibial angle, tibial angle, femoral condylar–shaft angle, and tibial shaft angle—and changes in the lumbar lordosis angle.ObjectiveThis study aims to examine the relationship between the magnitudes of coronal and sagittal alignment angles of the knee joint and the lumbar lordosis angle in patients diagnosed with knee osteoarthritis.MethodsPatients who sought medical care at our hospital from January to December 2023 were selected for this study. Anteroposterior and lateral X-ray images of the knee joint, along with lateral X-ray images of the lumbar spine, were obtained to measure several parameters: femoral angle, femoral–tibial angle, tibial angle, knee joint space angle, femoral condylar–shaft angle, tibial shaft angle, and lumbar lordosis angle. Correlation analysis was conducted to investigate the relationship between the magnitudes of the coronal and sagittal alignment angles of the knee and the lumbar lordosis angle.ResultsA significant positive correlation exists between the magnitude of the tibial angle of the left knee and the lumbar lordosis angle in patients with knee osteoarthritis (P < 0.05). In contrast, no significant correlations were detected between the magnitudes of the femoral angle, femoral–tibial angle, femoral condylar–shaft angle, knee joint space angle, and tibial shaft angle in the left knee group and the lumbar lordosis angle. Similarly, the right knee group exhibited no significant correlations among the magnitudes of the femoral angle, femoral–tibial angle, tibial angle, femoral condylar–shaft angle, knee joint space angle, and tibial shaft angle with the lumbar lordosis angle.ConclusionThis study demonstrates a significant positive correlation between the magnitude of the left knee tibial angle and the lumbar lordosis angle in patients with knee osteoarthritis (P < 0.05). Both domestic and international research has indicated a negative correlation between the incidence of low back pain and the magnitude of the lumbar lordosis angle. Consequently, it can be inferred that alterations in the left knee tibial angle may serve as a potential predictive factor for the risk of concurrent low back pain in patients with knee osteoarthritis. This finding provides clinicians with a novel perspective for treating patients with knee osteoarthritis. While addressing knee pathology, it is also crucial to monitor the condition of the lumbar spine to facilitate a more comprehensive assessment of the patient’s overall health and to develop more effective treatment plans. This approach aims to improve patient prognosis and alleviate suffering.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03768-w.

Background

Osteoarthritis primarily affects the elderly and is closely associated with factors such as age, gender, obesity, sports injuries, compromised immune function, and inflammatory metabolism. The principal pathological feature is the ongoing degradation of the cartilage matrix, which leads to a progressive loss of cartilage tissue. The overall incidence of osteoarthritis in China is approximately 15% [1, 2]. Changes in axial alignment angles around the knee significantly contribute to alterations in lower limb alignment. In the development and progression of knee osteoarthritis, these changes in lower limb alignment, which result in stress imbalances, play a crucial role. Among the coronal axial alignment angles, the joint space angle, femoral angle, tibial angle, and femoral–tibial angle are correlated with the severity of knee osteoarthritis [3]. In the sagittal plane, the femoral condylar angle, tibial shaft angle, and patellar position are associated with sagittal balance.

Domestic and international studies have demonstrated that patients with knee osteoarthritis accompanied by low back pain experience more severe symptoms and functional impairment [4, 5]. In the context of traditional Chinese medicine, both “low back” and “knee” symptoms are often evaluated in conjunction. Low back pain is a prevalent musculoskeletal disorder, primarily characterized by discomfort in the lower back, lumbosacral region, and buttocks, with potential radiation to the lower extremities [6]. Alterations in sagittal spinal–pelvic alignment parameters have been shown to possess significant predictive value for the onset of low back pain [7]. When disease affects the spine or pelvis, compensatory adjustments occur in these structures to preserve trunk balance [8].

The lumbar lordosis angle is a crucial parameter in sagittal spinal–pelvic alignment. It denotes the curve of the lumbar spine that protrudes toward the abdomen, representing a normal physiological curvature essential for maintaining sagittal balance. This study aims to investigate the relationship between the coronal and sagittal axial alignment angles of the knee in patients with knee osteoarthritis and the lumbar lordosis angle. A reduction in the lumbar lordosis angle frequently triggers or exacerbates symptoms of low back pain.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS version 22.0. Initially, normality tests were conducted on the quantitative data. Independent sample t-tests were performed to compare the two groups for data that conformed to a normal distribution. Correlation analysis was utilized to evaluate the relationships among the lumbar lordosis angle, femoral angle, tibial angle, femoral–tibial angle, femoral condylar–shaft angle, tibial shaft angle, patellar position, and articular surface angle. A P-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. Data collection was carried out by three individuals, and reliability analysis revealed that the Cronbach’s alpha values all exceeded 0.8.

The results demonstrate a significant correlation between the tibial angle, defined as the lateral angle formed by the proximal tibial articular surface line and the axis of the upper tibia, and the lumbar lordosis angle in patients with left knee osteoarthritis. Conversely, no significant correlation was identified between these two variables in the normal left knee group, and no other angles were found to have a significant correlation with the lumbar lordosis angle.

In both the right knee control group and the right knee osteoarthritis group, no significant correlation was found between the coronal and sagittal axis angles of the knee joint and the lumbar lordosis angle. However, multiple regression analysis, conducted to account for the influence of other angles, revealed a P-value of 0.047 for the left knee tibial angle. This finding indicates a significant independent correlation between the left knee tibial angle and the lumbar lordosis angle after adjusting for the effects of other angles. Furthermore, the 95% confidence interval for the B value does not encompass 0, confirming the significance of the effect.

Discussion

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a common degenerative joint disorder. The presence of low back pain in KOA patients exacerbates symptom severity and functional impairment, profoundly affecting their daily lives. Alterations in the mechanical axis of the lower extremities and KOA are mutually causal. Once KOA manifests, changes in the mechanical axis of the lower extremities become unavoidable. Conversely, abnormal alignment of the mechanical axis can also trigger the onset of KOA [14].

Analysis of radiographic data from clinical cases, along with the collection of axial angles in the coronal and sagittal planes of the knee joint—including the femoral angle, tibial angle, femoral–tibial angle, knee joint space angle, femoral condylar angle, tibial shaft angle, and lumbar lordosis angle—was conducted. Statistical analysis using IBM SPSS 22.0 indicated no significant correlation between these angles and lumbar lordosis angles in the normal population for either the left or right knee. In contrast, among patients with knee osteoarthritis (KOA), a correlation was identified between the tibial angle in the coronal plane of the left knee and the lumbar lordosis angle; however, this correlation was not present in the right knee group.

The knee joint space angle functions as an indicator of the degradation of surrounding soft tissues, with the equilibrium of these tissues directly affecting the extent of medial displacement of the lower limb mechanical axis. In healthy individuals, the femoral and tibial joint surfaces are nearly parallel. However, as knee osteoarthritis (KOA) advances, the joint space angle progressively diminishes [15–18].

The lumbar lordosis angle is essential for maintaining the sagittal balance of the spine. Its development is intricately linked to the wedged morphology of the vertebral bodies and intervertebral discs. Alterations in these structures can further enhance the curvature of lumbar lordosis. Physiological lumbar lordosis redistributes the load to the posterior annulus fibrosus and the zygapophysial joints, thereby alleviating intradiscal pressure. These findings indicate that physiological lumbar lordosis provides a protective effect on the lower lumbar spine [19].

In studies conducted by Zhang Jianxin et al. [20, 21], a potential relationship between lumbar disc herniation and the lumbar lordosis angle was identified. This relationship is attributed to the fact that a reduced lumbar lordosis angle results in decreased anterior height of the intervertebral space and a corresponding increase in posterior height. The lumbar lordosis angle serves as a critical indicator of lumbar physiological curvature, which constitutes the third normal physiological curvature of the human spine and plays a vital role in spinal movement and load-bearing. Preserving normal physiological curvature can effectively diminish lumbar vibration, enhance lumbar elasticity, and mitigate injuries associated with intense activities. When overall degenerative changes occur in the lumbar spine, abnormal alterations in lumbar curvature can affect the intervertebral discs, facet joints, and muscle tissues.

Carrizo JP [22–24] et al. confirmed that patients with lumbar disc herniation exhibit a straighter lumbar curvature compared to healthy individuals. Degenerative changes in the lumbar spine can result in the loss of collagenous substances within intervertebral discs, which subsequently disrupts the mechanical structure of the lumbar spine and alters lumbar curvature. Specifically, a reduction in the lumbar lordosis angle directly contributes to low back pain. To alleviate this pain, patients often adopt compensatory postures; however, this behavior exacerbates lumbar instability, creating a vicious cycle characterized by “reduced lordosis angle → pain → compensatory posture → increased instability → further reduction in lordosis angle”, ultimately leading to a progressive worsening of symptoms.

Increases in the tibial angle, femoral–tibial angle, and joint space angle adversely affect knee joint cartilage, resulting in damage. Furthermore, as the joint space angle increases, the extent of cartilage damage in the knee joint intensifies [25]. Degenerative alterations in the tibiofemoral joint cartilage lead to a narrowing of the joint space, which disrupts the distribution of stress and the alignment of forces between the tibia and femur. This imbalance in the force transmission pathway compromises joint stability, thereby exacerbating cartilage damage and perpetuating a vicious cycle. To counteract this instability, the mechanical axis of the lower limb undergoes adaptive changes aimed at restoring functional balance within the joint.

The spine, pelvis, and lower extremities constitute a closely interconnected and continuous functional unit in biomechanics. This unit’s force transmission system facilitates load transfer and distribution from the spine through the pelvis to the lower extremities, along with dynamic interactions among these three components. A significant biomechanical correlation exists between the lumbar spine and the knee joint [26].

Changes in the structure and function of the knee joint can influence the mechanical environment of the lumbar spine. In the elderly, biomechanical alterations in the lower extremities resulting from knee osteoarthritis may lead to stress concentration at the lumbar facet joints, thereby disrupting the sagittal balance of the spine [27, 28].

Conversely, morphological alterations in the lumbar spine can directly affect the mechanics of the knee joint. Recent studies have demonstrated a significant correlation between the lumbar lordosis angle (LLA) and the lumbar offset distance (lumbar offset distance) [29]. Furthermore, the lumbar–knee offset distance (lumbar–knee offset distance), which serves as a quantifiable assessment parameter, has been recognized as a critical risk factor influencing changes in lower extremity alignment [30].

Under typical conditions, the body’s weight is transmitted from the lumbar spine and lower extremities to the feet. Alterations in the tibial angle can modify the load transmission pathway in the lower extremities and, by influencing lower extremity alignment, further disrupt the sagittal balance of the spine. This sequence of adaptive changes may lead to abnormal lower limb alignment (LLA) angles, which could serve as a predisposing factor for low back pain.

The discrepancy observed in the correlation analysis between the tibial angles of both lower extremities and the lumbar lordosis angle may arise from a complex interplay of multiple factors.

For instance, Chung J [31, 32] et al. found that the patient’s body position can exert a certain influence on the results of lower limb radiographic angle measurements. However, this influence was not reflected in the measurements of the femoral angle. Additionally, some studies [33] have revealed that the height, body mass, and BMI of volunteers exhibit a certain correlation with the various angle values of the knee joint. This may be the reason for the differences in results.

Inequality in leg length could also be a contributing factor to the asymmetry in the tibial angles. Patients with primary knee osteoarthritis often experience a higher incidence of leg length discrepancy, with osteoarthritis typically occurring on the shorter leg side. The greater the degree of leg length discrepancy, the more severe the deformity [34].

Furthermore, factors such as congenital embryonic developmental variations, genetic predispositions, asymmetrical postnatal growth and development, injuries, diseases, lifestyle choices, posture, and muscle imbalances can contribute to discrepancies in tibial angles bilaterally. For instance, slight differences in the growth rate or pattern of the lower extremities during development, childhood activity habits, and prolonged specific activities—such as playing football, crossing legs, or adopting improper sitting postures—can lead to uneven stress on the lower extremities.

Certain diseases can also significantly affect the symmetry of the tibial angles. For instance, spastic cerebral palsy can lead to muscle strength and tension imbalances, which in turn can affect the normal development and posture maintenance of the lower extremities, resulting in asymmetry in the tibial angles. Diseases that affect bone development, such as rickets and osteomalacia, can also cause differences in the tibial angles by altering the growth and remodeling processes of the bones [35].

Statistical analysis of the data revealed a correlation between the tibial angle of the left knee and the lumbar lordosis angle. This may be due to the fact that when the soft tissue balance of the knee is disrupted, the degree of medial shift in the lower extremity’s mechanical axis increases. The magnitude of the tibial angle influences the biomechanics of the lower extremity, and changes in lower extremity biomechanics lead to increased pressure on the lumbar spine, thereby affecting the size of the lumbar lordosis angle and, consequently, the progression of low back pain.

There are areas for improvement in this study, the foremost being the issue of insufficient sample size.

The samples in this study were sourced from a single hospital within a specific region, reflecting distinct regional characteristics. Although the study aimed to increase the sample size to achieve more robust statistical results, practical constraints hindered immediate implementation of this objective.

For instance, in 2023, our hospital’s spine surgery department admitted 2603 patients annually. This study organized imaging data for each patient and screened them according to research criteria. However, most patients’ imaging data did not meet the study’s requirements. This was primarily due to two reasons: first, our hospital’s spine surgery and joint surgery departments are separate, practicing specialized treatment; second, due to economic burdens, most patients only sought treatment for a specific condition and often refused additional examinations. This made it difficult for the research team to obtain the complete imaging data required for the study. This is the main reason for the insufficient sample size.

Additionally, before the study began, we could not determine whether meaningful positive results would be found, so we did not apply for related research projects, making it difficult to gain support from other centers or regions.

This study also presents opportunities for further investigation. Through the analysis of existing samples, it revealed clinically significant results that offer new insights into the assessment of concurrent low back pain risk in patients with knee osteoarthritis. Future endeavors, informed by these findings, may include applying for related projects, fostering collaboration with other medical centers and regions, increasing the sample size, and potentially yielding more robust conclusions.

Furthermore, the angles selected in this study were primarily in the coronal and sagittal planes, limiting all measurements to a single plane. However, knee joint movement is dynamic, and the lumbar lordosis angle also exhibits its own spatial torsion and dynamic changes. The static nature of this research introduces certain limitations, and future studies could incorporate research in the direction of kinesiology.

In summary, this research approach is significant for evaluating the risk of concurrent low back pain in patients with knee osteoarthritis. It has the potential to yield more reliable conclusions in the future, which could enhance clinical treatment and improve patient prognosis.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a significant positive correlation between the magnitude of the left knee tibial angle and the lumbar lordosis angle in patients with knee osteoarthritis (P < 0.05). In contrast, no significant correlations were found between the lumbar lordosis angle and the magnitudes of the femoral angle, femoral–tibial angle, femoral condylar–shaft angle, and tibial shaft angle within the left knee group. Likewise, in the right knee group, no significant correlations were detected between the lumbar lordosis angle and the magnitudes of the femoral angle, femoral–tibial angle, tibial angle, femoral condylar–shaft angle, and tibial shaft angle.

Additionally, both domestic and international research have indicated a negative correlation between the incidence of low back pain and the magnitude of the lumbar lordosis angle. Variations in the lumbar lordosis angle are closely associated with the occurrence of low back pain. A reduction in the lumbar lordosis angle often exacerbates symptoms of lumbar disc herniation, thereby triggering or intensifying low back pain in patients.

Consequently, changes in the magnitude of the left knee tibial angle may serve as a potential predictive factor for the risk of concurrent low back pain in patients with knee osteoarthritis. This finding provides clinicians with a novel perspective for treating individuals with knee osteoarthritis. While it is crucial to focus on knee pathology, monitoring the condition of the lumbar spine is also essential for a comprehensive assessment of patients’ overall health and the development of more effective treatment plans. This approach aims to improve patient prognosis and alleviate suffering.
