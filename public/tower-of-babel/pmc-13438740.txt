The impact of frailty on postoperative complications and resource utilization in patients with severe aortic stenosis undergoing transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI)
Authors: Hiroshi Yonekura, Tasuku Fujii, Koji Kawakami, Koichi Akiyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPatients with severe aortic stenosis undergoing transcatheter aortic valve implantation are usually older adults with multiple comorbidities and often frail. Frailty reflects increased vulnerability to adverse outcomes and is an important factor in this population; however, its impact on postoperative complications and healthcare resource utilization remains uncertain. We investigated the association between preoperative frailty and postoperative outcomes using a nationwide electronic medical records database in Japan. We included patients aged 65 years or older who underwent transcatheter aortic valve implantation between April 2013 and December 2021. Frailty was assessed with the Hospital Frailty Risk Score, with a score of 5 or higher indicating frailty. The primary outcome was acute kidney injury within seven days. Secondary outcomes included major adverse kidney events, blood transfusion within 30 days, and hospital length of stay. Among 781 patients, 237 (30.3%) were classified as frail. Acute kidney injury occurred in 8.8% of non-frail and 12.7% of frail patients. After adjustment for age, sex, comorbidities, baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate, surgical approach, anesthesia type, and hospital size, the odds ratio for acute kidney injury in frail patients was 1.42 (95% confidence interval, 0.83–2.45; P = 0.20). Hospital stay was significantly longer in frail patients, with an adjusted mean difference of 3.45 days. Preoperative frailty was not significantly associated with acute kidney injury or other complications but was associated with prolonged hospitalization. Frailty screening before transcatheter aortic valve implantation may help identify patients at risk of extended hospital stay and guide perioperative care.Key Words: frailty, acute kidney injury, transcatheter aortic valve implantation, aortic stenosis, real world data

INTRODUCTION

The global population is aging rapidly, with the World Health Organization estimating that by 2050 more than 2 billion people will be over 60 years old.1 Japan has the highest proportion of older adults worldwide, with more than 30% of the population aged 65 years or older.2 This demographic change poses major healthcare challenges, among which frailty has emerged as a critical concern. Frailty represents a decline in physiological reserve across multiple organ systems, leading to increased vulnerability to adverse outcomes after stressors such as surgery.3 Although frailty screening has proven useful in surgical populations, evidence on interventions remains limited.4,5

More than 40 definitions of frailty exist, including phenotype-based and deficit-accumulation models.6 The Hospital Frailty Risk Score (HFRS) applies a deficit-accumulation approach using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes, and its validity has been demonstrated across diverse populations,7,8 including Asian cohorts.9 For example, Shimizu et al showed that higher HFRS values predicted in-hospital mortality and postoperative complications in Japanese older adults with hip fractures.10

In the context of transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), frailty is recognized as a key determinant of outcomes. Shimura et al, using a Japanese multicenter registry, showed that functional frailty markers such as gait speed, grip strength, and serum albumin levels predict mortality.11 Moreover, Ueki et al reported that higher preoperative HFRS scores are associated with long-term mortality and functional decline after TAVI in late-elderly Japanese patients.12 Studies using large-scale United States (US) databases, including the National Inpatient Sample and Medicare data, also confirmed the predictive value of HFRS for mortality, complications, and extended hospital stay in patients undergoing TAVI.13,14

Despite these findings, the relationship between preoperative frailty and postoperative renal complications, particularly acute kidney injury (AKI), has not been fully clarified. Therefore, using a large-scale, nationwide database in Japan, this study aimed to evaluate whether preoperative frailty, measured by the HFRS, is associated with postoperative renal complications and healthcare resource utilization in older patients undergoing TAVI.

METHODS

This retrospective cohort study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.15 The study protocol was approved by Ethics Committee of Nagoya University (approval number, 2025-0210; August 13, 2025), which waived the requirement for informed consent owing to the use of anonymized data.

Data source

This nationwide, population-based retrospective cohort study utilized the Real World Data database, maintained by the Health, Clinic, and Education Information Evaluation Institute with support from Real World Data Co, Ltd. (Kyoto, Japan). As of 2022, the database contains anonymized patient records derived from electronic medical records, claims data, and Diagnosis Procedure Combination data from more than 240 medical institutions across Japan, covering approximately 26 million patients.16-18 The Diagnosis Procedure Combination system is a case-mix classification framework for acute inpatient care in Japan, analogous to the Diagnosis-Related Group system used in the US.16,18 Participating facilities include a variety of private and public hospitals, ranging from large university hospitals to small community hospitals.17

The database contains detailed patient-level information, including demographic characteristics; clinical data such as diagnoses and comorbidities at admission and complications during hospitalization, coded according to the ICD-10; claims data on medications, medical devices, and surgical or medical procedures defined using Japan-specific standardized K codes; laboratory test results from both outpatient and inpatient encounters; and discharge status.16-19 Because diagnoses and procedures are directly linked to the Japanese national fee schedule for reimbursement, the validity of these data is high, with recent studies reporting a specificity exceeding 90%.20,21

Study population

We included patients aged ≥65 years with severe aortic stenosis who underwent TAVI between April 2013 and December 2021. Patients were excluded if they had missing preoperative laboratory data required for the assessment of AKI, preoperative renal failure defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of <15 mL/min/1.73 m2, a history of renal transplantation, or were receiving dialysis.

Outcomes

We selected AKI as the primary outcome because the Real World Data database uniquely captures laboratory results, including serum creatinine and eGFR, enabling renal function to be objectively assessed using the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO). Such laboratory availability is uncommon in administrative databases and allows detailed evaluation of postoperative renal injury.16,17 AKI was defined solely by changes in serum creatinine in accordance with the criteria: an increase in serum creatinine of ≥0.3 mg/dL or an increase to ≥1.5 times the baseline value within 7 days of the index surgery. The baseline creatinine or eGFR was defined as the value obtained closest to the index surgery date within the preceding 90 days. Secondary outcomes included major adverse kidney events within 30 days (MAKE30), comprising death, new postoperative initiation of renal replacement therapy, and persistent renal dysfunction, which was defined as a final creatinine clearance ≥200% of the baseline value.22 By definition, follow-up durations were 7 days for AKI and 30 days for MAKE30. Additional secondary outcomes included the requirement for postoperative transfusion within 30 days of the index surgery and the length of hospital stay.

Exposure variables and covariates

Frailty was assessed using the HFRS, which estimates frailty risk based on ICD-10 codes, as originally developed by Gilbert et al8 in 2018. In this study, each patient’s HFRS was retrospectively calculated using ICD-10 codes recorded before hospital admission. The HFRS assigns weighted points across 109 specific ICD-10 codes, ranging from the highest for dementia in Alzheimer’s disease to the lowest for fever of unknown origin.10 Further details on the scoring methodology are available in the original publication by Gilbert et al.8 In accordance with previous studies,10,12,13 frailty was defined as an HFRS ≥5.

Preoperative eGFR was calculated from serum creatinine values and categorized as 15–29, 30–44, 45–59, or ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2. Comorbidities were assessed using selected Charlson and Elixhauser comorbidity measures.23 The Charlson Comorbidity Index has been validated in large administrative databases in Japan.24 Based on claims codes, anesthesia type was categorized as either general or local, and surgical approach as either trans-arterial or trans-apical. Hospital size was classified according to the number of beds: 100–299, 300–499, or ≥500 beds.

Covariates were specified a priori based on clinical plausibility and included patient demographics (age and sex), Charlson Comorbidity Index score category (0–2 or >2), baseline eGFR category, surgical approach (transfemoral or transapical), anesthesia type (general or local), and hospital bed category (<500 or ≥500).

Statistical analysis

Patient, surgical, and hospital characteristics were summarized separately for frail and non-frail patients. Continuous variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test, while categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages and compared using the chi-squared test.

To compare outcomes between the non-frail and frail groups, we used multivariable logistic regression to estimate adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs for binary outcomes, including preoperative frailty as the exposure variable. For continuous outcomes, specifically length of hospital stay, we used multivariable linear regression to estimate adjusted mean differences in days with corresponding 95% CIs.

A two-sided α level of 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, US).

RESULTS

Study cohort

A total of 1,235 patients who underwent TAVI between April 2013 and December 2021 were initially screened. Of these, 454 patients were excluded for the following reasons: age <65 years (n = 273), preoperative renal failure (eGFR <15 mL/min/1.73 m2), prior renal transplantation, or dialysis dependence (n = 25), and missing serum creatinine values (n = 156). Although 156 patients (12.6%) were excluded due to missing creatinine values, these cases exhibited almost complete missingness across other variables, including comorbidities, body mass index, and anesthesia type (Supplementary file, Table S1). This pattern suggests that the missing creatinine values reflected institutional differences in data availability rather than patient characteristics. The final analytic cohort consisted of 781 patients. Of these, 237 (30.3%) were classified as frail based on the HFRS ≥5 (Figure 1).

Primary and secondary outcomes

Table 3 presents primary and secondary outcomes by the HFRS in patients undergoing TAVI. The incidence of AKI within 7 days after surgery was higher in the frail group than in the non-frail group (12.7% vs 8.8%). In the adjusted logistic regression model, frailty was not significantly associated with AKI (adjusted OR, 1.42; 95% CI, 0.83–2.45; P = 0.20). The composite secondary outcome MAKE30, which included death, need for renal replacement therapy, or persistent renal dysfunction, occurred in 3.0% of frail patients and 3.3% of non-frail patients (adjusted OR, 0.99; 95% CI, 0.37–2.64; P = 0.98). The rates of postoperative transfusion were similar (34.6% frail vs 35.7% non-frail; adjusted OR, 1.27; 95% CI, 0.88–1.82; P = 0.20). However, the length of hospital stay was significantly longer in frail patients. The adjusted mean difference was +3.45 days (95% CI, 0.24–6.67; P = 0.036).

DISCUSSION

We investigated the association between preoperative frailty and postoperative AKI in patients undergoing TAVI for severe aortic stenosis, using a large, nationwide electronic medical records database that included laboratory results. In this study, approximately one-third of patients met the criteria for frailty, as defined by the HFRS. Although the crude incidence of AKI was higher among frail patients, frailty was not significantly associated with AKI, MAKE30, or transfusion requirements after adjusting for potential confounders. However, frailty was significantly associated with prolonged hospital stay.

In our study, 30.3% of patients were classified as frail, defined by the HFRS ≥5. The reported prevalence of frailty among older patients undergoing TAVI varies widely, from 5% to 83%,25 largely due to differences in how frailty is defined. Evidence from the US Medicare data reported that HFRS-defined frailty was present in approximately 47% of patients and was a predictor of 1-year mortality following TAVI, with risk increasing incrementally across frailty categories.13 Similarly, Abugroun et al, using the US National Inpatient Sample, reported that the frailty status was associated with increased odds of mortality, myocardial infarction, pericardiocentesis, pacemaker insertion, blood transfusion, and vascular complications, as well as prolonged hospital stay.14 These findings indicate that frailty, as defined by the HFRS, independently predicts mortality, procedural complications, and prolonged hospital stay in patients undergoing TAVI.

AKI is a relatively frequent complication after TAVI, occurring in 4.7–10.7% of cases, and has been associated with an increased risk of future dialysis and all-cause mortality.26,27 In a large US TAVI registry, the overall incidence of AKI decreased over time,26 from 27.3% in 2012 to 9.3% in 2018. In our study, the incidence of AKI within 7 days after surgery was 10%, which is consistent with previous reports. Frailty was not found to significantly predict postoperative AKI (12.7% in frail vs 8.8% in non-frail). Evidence regarding the association between frailty and AKI is scarce. A Japanese multicenter registry demonstrated that higher frailty severity, as defined by the Clinical Frailty Scale, was associated with an increased frequency of AKI, with an overall incidence of 9.4%.28 In contrast, a systematic review found conflicting results regarding the relationship between frailty and renal failure requiring dialysis.25 Causal inference across studies is challenging because both the definitions of AKI and the approaches used to define frailty vary widely. The minimally invasive nature of TAVI may mitigate renal stress compared with open cardiac surgery, thereby reducing the potential impact of frailty on postoperative kidney injury.

The absence of a significant association between frailty and MAKE30—a composite outcome including persistent renal failure, renal replacement therapy, and death within 30 days—should be interpreted with caution. The relatively low incidence of renal replacement therapy and mortality in our cohort may have limited statistical power to detect differences.

The observed association between frailty and prolonged hospital stay carries important clinical and health system implications. These observations are consistent with prior TAVI outcome studies.13,14,28 Prolonged length of stay among frail patients may reflect slower mobilization, a higher incidence of non-renal complications such as delirium, and increased complexity of discharge planning.

This study has methodological limitations. First, although a post hoc sample size assessment indicated that approximately 2,300 patients would be required to detect the observed difference in AKI incidence with a two-sided α of 0.05 and 80% power, our cohort included only 781 patients. This suggests that the study was underpowered to detect small to moderate effect sizes. Second, although we adjusted for additional clinically relevant variables, important confounders such as contrast agent volume and heart failure severity were not available in the Real World Data database. These unmeasured factors may have influenced the association between frailty and postoperative renal complications. Third, the focus on a Japanese elderly population may limit generalizability to other healthcare settings. Clinical practice patterns and patient characteristics differ between Japan and Western countries,29 potentially influencing the relationship between frailty and outcomes. Finally, reliance on administrative data introduces the possibility of coding inaccuracies. Because the HFRS relies on ICD-10 diagnosis codes, missing or inaccurate coding may affect frailty classification. Administrative databases inherently contain variability in coding quality, and functional aspects of frailty measurable by bedside instruments cannot be captured through diagnosis codes alone. Although these factors may reduce accuracy, the scalability, reproducibility, and ability to automate HFRS calculation across large populations make it a practical tool for population-level frailty screening using real-world hospital data.8 Moreover, although the HFRS ≥5 cutoff was selected based on previous Japanese studies,10,12,30 the validity of the HFRS itself and the optimal cutoff value have not been fully evaluated in Japanese populations. Further studies are needed to establish the appropriateness of the HFRS for Japanese older adults undergoing TAVI.

CONCLUSIONS

Preoperative frailty as assessed by the HFRS was not significantly associated with postoperative AKI, MAKE30, or transfusion requirements in elderly patients undergoing TAVI. However, frailty was associated with longer hospital stays. Incorporating frailty screening into preoperative evaluation may help identify patients at increased risk of extended hospitalization and support tailored perioperative care strategies aimed at optimizing recovery and resource utilization.
