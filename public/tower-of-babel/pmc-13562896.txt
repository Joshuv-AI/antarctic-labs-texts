Water Quality Index: Physicochemical and Microbiological Risk Assessment of Drinking Water in Maya City, Eastern Ethiopia
Authors: Andarge Zelalem, Tsegu Kiros, Tessema Toru, Abera Atumo Ante, Temesegen Achamo Orisho, Yehararwork Abebaw, Deressa Boja Adugna, Getachew Teshome Tilahun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate drinking water quality across different sources and seasons and to develop Water Quality Index (WQI) based on selected physicochemical and microbial parameters in Maya City Administration, Ethiopia.MethodsDrinking water samples (n=120) were analyzed for microbial and physicochemical analysis. Safety indicator bacteria were detected and counted using petrifilm, while the physicochemical parameters were determined using standard procedures. The WQI was computed following Brown’s weighted arithmetic approach.ResultsNearly 31% of the samples exceeded the Ethiopian standard limit (0/100 mL) set for total coliform, 23% for coliform, and 15% for E. coli. The occurrence of E. coli was significantly higher during the wet season (p = 0.014). The mean microbial WQI score was 24.44, with 35% under the risky category, and seasons showed no effect on WQI. The pH, electrical conductivity, temperature, turbidity, sodium ion, and zinc level were within the acceptable limits of the Ethiopian standard limit. About 93% of the samples were within the acceptable limit set for total dissolved solids. However, Ca2+ and total hardness exceeded the acceptable limit in 100% of samples, while Mg2+ exceeded the limit in 67.6% of samples. The WQI calculated using physicochemical parameters was within the acceptable category ranging from 2.63-9.26.ConclusionDespite the overall WQI shows suitability for drinking, the high occurrence of E. coli and coliform, together with elevated water hardness, remains a public health concern that requires mitigation measures.Keywords: drinking water, water quality index, physicochemical parameters, microbial risks, seasonal variation

1. Introduction

The safety of drinking water still remains a public concern in low- and middle-income countries. 1 Globally, the population is entirely dependent on unimproved drinking water sources, such as streams, rivers, and ponds.2,3 Report indicated that in 2022 at least 1.7 billion people worldwide depend on fecal contaminated water source for consumption.4-6 Consequently, the burden of waterborne disease is a common phenomenon mainly in developing countries.7-9 In Europe, about 2700 death was reported in 2016 due to water contamination. Taking the seriousness of the problem, the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 6 targeted universal access to water and sanitation by 2030. 10 Nearly 5 billion people globally for the coming two decades are estimated to have limited access to safe drinking water due to climate change and pollution. 11

Despite improvement in recent years,12,13 access to clean drinking water remains a major challenge in Ethiopia, where nearly 40% of the population still relies on unsafe sources. The drinking water acquired from unprotected sources causes diarrheal and gastrointestinal disease,14,15 chronic kidney diseases and cancers.16,17 The waterborne disease caused by bacteria,18-20 and virus 21 caused increased mortality. 22 Waterborne diseases pose challenges to productivity and healthcare system. 23 Drinking water sources are commonly subjected to point or non-point pollution by physical, chemical, and biological hazards.24,25 Moreover, open defecation practices in rural areas also compromise water safety and sanitation services.26-28

Chemical pollutants such pesticides, dyes, nitrates, and heavy metals are the priority pollutants of drinking water. 29 These contaminants may occur at any time and points from water source to end-user. Thus, regular water quality checkups and monitoring are recommended practices. 30 Nevertheless, drinking water quality assessment in urban and rural settings across different seasons is limited in Ethiopia. In this regard, World Health Organization (WHO) and United States Environmental Protection Agency (US EPA) recommend testing of hygiene indicator bacteria31,32 and selected physicochemical parameters to determine drinking water quality.

In water quality assessment, individual microbial and physicochemical parameters might have different values per the limits set by WHO and Ethiopian Standard Services (ESS), which makes it difficult to know the water quality status. To address such challenges, the water quality index (WQI) is used as a composite score computed based on core physicochemical and microbial parameters. 33 It describes water quality by combining the value of multiple parameters into a single value ranging between 0 and 100.34,35

In this regard, Brown’s weighted arithmetic index approach is widely used to assess water quality. 36 The previous studies in Ethiopia were mainly focused on determining water quality based on physicochemical and microbial analysis37-39 without determining water quality index. Studies on the effect of seasonal variation on water quality are also scarce in Ethiopia. Thus, this study aims to evaluate the drinking water quality in accordance with the standard limit set by ESS and WHO40,41 and to develop Water Quality Index (WQI) based on selected physicochemical parameters such as Electrical conductivity, total dissolved solids, turbidity, total hardness, chlorides, and iron etc. and microbial parameters comprised E. coli, coliform, and total coliform. Moreover, seasonal based microbial detection and quantification of E. coli (EC), coliform (CC), total coliform (TCC), enterobacteriaceae (EB), and aerobic plate count (AC) was done in Maya City administration, Eastern Hararghe.

2. Material and Methods

2.1. Description of the Study Area

Maya City Administration, geographically located between 9°24′40″N – 9°25′25″N and 42°02′00″E – 42°02′20″E, in eastern Hararghe, Oromia region (Figure 1). The city administration has an altitudinal range of 2000 – 2047 m a.s.l (Ralph et al, 2025) and annual rainfall of 600 to 1260 mm. The annual temperature varies within the range of 10oC – 25oC, and the annual rainfall within the range of 600 to 1260 mm. 42 The main rainy season (Kiremt) occurs every year from July to September, followed by autumn season, when December through February is typically dry. The short rains (Belg), from March to May, are considered the secondary wet season in the area.

Map of the study area showing the three sampling sites in Maya City, Eastern Hararghe, Ethiopia

The study area is typically characterized by underlying geological formations of Mesozoic sedimentary rock (mainly limestone and sandstone), Precambrian crystalline basement (primarily granite), and Quaternary sediments from old to young. 43 These geological settings influence water quality by controlling mineral dissolution, ground water chemistry, and contaminant transport. The Mesozoic limestone contributes for water hardness. The Precambrian granitic rocks release sodium, potassium, and silica during weathering, and make groundwater with lower mineralization. Moreover, the quaternary sediments serve as important aquifers. From the Maya City, three sampling sites (A, B, and C; the names of the sites were deliberately kept anonymous, Figure 1) were purposively selected to assess drinking water quality across different seasons.

The densely populated consumers in the area entirely relied on tap water distributed from different storage facilities. Despite some efforts of chlorination-based treatment at the centralized storage facilities, the old infrastructure, distribution network, poor facilities, opened local storage Roto tank, pipe leakages, and lack of follow-up and maintenance are the major challenges of water safety. These probably make cross contamination. Moreover, the quality of groundwater, which serves as a main source of drinking water, might be influenced by seasonal rainfall fluctuation and hydrochemistry.

2.2. Study Design and Sample Size

A laboratory-based cross-sectional study design was employed in Maya City Administration between December 2024 and May 2025 to assess water quality. A total of 120 samples were collected across three sites, in which samples were proportionally distributed in the study sites as A (n=50), site B (n=45), and site C (n=25) based on the number of water sources or distribution points. The samples were collected from different sources including piped households (n=31), administrative buildings (n=21), communal standpipes (n=23), water tanks (n=21), and boreholes (n=20). All samples were used to assess the microbial risks and loads of EC, CC, TCC, EB, and AC. However, only 74 drinking water samples, representing 61.7% of the total samples were used to assess the physicochemical parameters (pH, temperature, electrical conductivity, total dissolved solids, turbidity, alkalinity, total hardness, chemicals, and elemental analysis including heavy metals) according to the WHO and Ethiopian drinking water standard recommendations. The samples were collected in six consecutive months which is categorized as dry (December, January, and February) and secondary wet seasons (March, April, and May) to assess the impact of season and seasonality. Finally, the water quality index was computed by integrating different water quality parameters.

The reporting of this study conforms to the Equator guidelines; STROBE Statement-checklist of items that should be included in reports of cross-sectional studies, and the completed checklist provided as supplementary material.

2.2.1. Sampling Collection

Drinking water samples were collected from sources actively used by the community for domestic and drinking purposes, including supplies/sources from taps, boreholes, standpipes, and tankers in the study area. Moreover, only water sources which were accessible for extended period of sampling during wet and dry season were included in the study. The samples from each sampling point were collected following standard procedures provided by the World Health Organization and the United State Environmental Protection Agency.44,45 Prior to sample collection, the tap water was allowed to flow for about 2 min. The water samples were obtained from different points and sources along the pipeline distribution. A total of 20 samples were collected each month from the three sites. From each sampling point, three separate samples were taken for microbial, major ions, and physicochemical analysis. For microbial analysis, samples were collected using sterile 100 ml Whirl-Pak Thio- Bags containing 10 mg of sodium thiosulfate to inactivate possible chlorination. 44 The second samples were collected using a 1 L sterile polyethylene bottle, acidified and preserved with HNO3 for major ion analysis. The third samples were intended for physicochemical analysis. All samples were properly labeled, stored in an icebox (4-8oC), and transported to the laboratory at Haramaya University. The collected samples were processed within 8 hrs. for microbial analysis and 24 hrs. for physiochemical analysis.

2.3. Sample Analysis

2.3.1. Detection and Enumeration of Indicator Bacteria

From each collected water sample, 1 mL was plated in duplicate onto E. coli/Coliform Count Plates (ECC; 3 M Petrifilm Count Plates: St. Paul, MN - USA). All inoculated plates were incubated at 35 ± 2.0 °C for 24 ± 2.0 hrs. After incubation, the Petrifilm plates were examined for generic E. coli, coliform, and total coliform growth in accordance with AOAC Official Methods 998.08 and 991.14. 46 Red colonies associated with gas were considered and counted as coliform, and blue colonies associated with gas were considered and counted as E. coli. Both red and blue colonies associated with gas within 24 hrs of incubation were counted as total coliform. The Petrifilm plates were incubated for an additional 24 ± 2 hrs to detect any further E. coli growth. Similarly, Petriflm plates (EB and AC; 3M Petrifilm count plates; St. Paul, MN, USA) inoculated with 1 mL of each sample in duplicate were incubated at 35 ± 2.0 °C for 24 ± 2.0 hrs to detect and enumerate EB and AC count following the manufacturer’s instructions.

2.3. 2. Physicochemical and Elemental Ion Analysis

Water samples were analyzed for the physiochemical parameters such as pH, electrical conductivity (EC), total solids (TDS), turbidity, total hardness (TH), temperature, total suspended solid (TSS), and salinity as per the procedures described in other studies.47,48 The pH and temperature parameters were measured on-site during sample collection using digital battery-operated pH meter and thermometer, respectively. While the analysis of Na+, K+, Mg2+, Ca2+, Fe2+, Cu2+, Zn2+, Mn2+, and Cl- was conducted following standard procedures using an Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometer (AAS). 49

2.4. Statistical Data Analysis

The data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (IBM SPSS, ver.27, Armonk, NY). Descriptive statistics were conducted to determine the microbial and physicochemical parameters. Each parameter was interpreted in line with the standard limit set by ESS and the WHO. The proportion (%) of microbial risks at 95% confidence interval was determined for samples by indicator bacterial types. A one-way analysis of variance test was used to compare the microbial load across sites (A, B, and C). Chi-square (Χ2) analysis was used to determine the association between season and microbial safety status of drinking water. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to check for normality. Moreover, the microbial counts (CFU/100 mL) were log transformed to observe the trend across months. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

2.5. Ethics Declaration

Not applicable as no human subject was involved in the study.

3. Results

3.1. Microbial Quality of Drinking Water

A total of 120 drinking water samples were analyzed to determine the microbial safety level of drinking water. Samples analyzed were evaluated and interpreted in line with the drinking water standard limit set by Ethiopian Standard Services. 40 The highest rates of samples exceeding the limit of 0/100 mL were contaminated by total coliform (30.8%), followed by coliform (22.5%), and E. coli (15%). The majority (87.5%) of the samples were detected to be safe from enterobacteriaceae. The water samples indicated the lowest mean load of enterobacteriaceae (17.9 CFU/mL), followed by E.coli (42.1 CFU/mL). Overall, the findings revealed that the assessed drinking water samples were found contaminated by safety indicator bacterial, which signifies the presence of poor water hygiene and sanitary condition, which poses a public health threat in the studied area (Table 1).

3.2. Physicochemical Quality of Drinking Water

The physicochemical analysis, variation across sampling sites, and the proportion of water samples exceeding and complying with the ESS and WHO standard limit are presented in Table 4. In all examined samples, the pH, EC, temperature, turbidity, sodium ion, and zinc, were complied with the ESS standard limit set for drinking water. Further, the majority of the water samples (93.2%) showed that the total dissolved solids were within the standard limit. However, the drinking water samples analyzed demonstrated that the level of Ca2+ (100%), total hardness (100%), and potassium ions (93.5%) in the water exceeded the acceptable limit set by Ethiopian Standard service and World Health Organization. Similarly, the Mg2+, which is the contributor for total hardness along with Ca2+, also exceeded the standard limit in 67.6% of water samples. Despite most of the parameters were within the acceptable limits, the high level of water hardness, Mg2+, and Ca2+ remains to be water quality concern. The water hardness probably associated with Ca2+reduces the efficiency of water supply facilities and poses public health risks. Moreover, heavy metals, such as nickel, copper, and lead were not detected in all samples. Although iron is an important component of human nutrition, 32.4% of the analyzed samples exceeded the acceptable limit set for Fe2+ (>0.3 mg/L) with higher iron level observed in site B (45.8%) and site C (55.0%). The pH, temperature, Ca2+, Mg2+, TH, and Fe2+ levels of the water samples were significantly different (p < 0.05) across the sampling sites.

3.3. Water Quality Index

The overall microbial WQI was calculated based on E. coli, coliform, and total coliform count as shown in Figure 4. During the secondary wet season, the mean microbial WQI of drinking water samples from sites A and B was poor, falling within the WQI category of 26–50. However, the mean WQI was good in site A and B during dry season. The overall microbial mean score of WQI was 24.44, in which 35% of the samples were categorized as unsafe. The microbial WQI showed no significant difference between seasons (P > 0.05). Similarly, there were no significant differences in the WQI across sites (p > 0.05).

The water quality index was determined based on measured core physicochemical parameters (pH, EC, TDS, TH, turbidity, Fe2+, and chlorine). The results demonstrated that the physicochemical water quality index scores recorded within a range of 2.63-9.26. The drinking water quality index computed from physicochemical parameters showed good quality status (Figure 5). 4. Discussion

The Ethiopian Standard Services40,41 set strict limit (0/100 mL) for E. coli, coliform, and total coliform in drinking water. However, the present study revealed that water samples collected from three sites (A, B, and C) in Maya City Administration were contaminated by E. coli, coliform, total coliform, and enterobacteriaceae. Nearly 31% of the samples were detected positive for total coliform. Similar finding was reported in Bahir Dar city, Ethiopia, where 30% of the total coliform were detected in tap water samples. 52 The detection and enumeration of indicator bacteria in drinking water samples in general provides practical evidence for the presence of enteric pathogen risks, weak water sanitation and hygiene practices, fecal contamination, and poor infrastructure and distribution network. 53 For instance, the detection of total coliform in drinking water signifies that the water is microbiologically unsafe and the integrity of the water source, distribution system, and treatment efficiency is compromised. 54 Overall, the water contamination by microbes might be attributed to poorly managed water reservoirs, opened storages tanks, old distribution and supply facilities, and weak water treatment system.

The other serious health related issue in drinking water was that E. coli was detected in 15% of the samples. As E. coli is the golden safety indicator in drinking water, its presence is an indication of fecal contamination and poor water treatment approaches. 55 This contamination mainly occurs when water and its sources are infiltrated by human or animal feces and weak treatment system. 56 The contributing factors for E. coli may include poor protection of water sources, open reservoirs or distribution tankers, leakage during distribution, close proximity to latrines, agricultural runoff, or inadequate sanitation systems, and weak infrastructure. As per the findings of, 57 the presence of E. coli in drinking water signifies poor water quality and serious safety concern. The E. coli contamination was significantly higher during the secondary wet season (p < 0.05).

In Maya City Administration, rainfall is common during the secondary wet season from March through April. The rise in E. coli contamination might be facilitated by washed fecal matter, agricultural runoff, and the discharge of wastewater from poorly maintained latrines into drinking water sources. Moreover, the warmer temperatures and increased moisture also promote microbial growth and multiplication during the secondary wet season. In agreement with this finding, few studies in Ethiopia consistently show that seasonal peaks in E. coli contamination align with rainy periods.58-61

Similar report in Ghana indicated that fecal contamination of drinking water sources was significantly higher during rainy season. 62 The overall microbial water quality index (24.4) was almost near to risk category with microbial contamination slightly increased during secondary wet season. In agreement with these findings, study conducted in Mexico also highlighted the impacts of dry and wet seasons on microbial quality of drinking water. 63 In WQI score, high standard deviation (SD) from the mean was observed which may be attributed to intermittent contamination across samples, sample sources, and seasons.

The source of drinking water showed variation in the levels of microbial contamination. Water tankers and boreholes were found to be highly contaminated with safety indicator bacteria. These findings provide evidence of poor sanitation, unsafe handling practices, environmental pollution, and anthropogenic activities that pose risks to water safety. Similarly, a water quality assessment in Southern Nigeria revealed that borehole water was of poor water quality compared with other sources. 64 In contrast, a study conducted in Hafizabad District, Punjab Province of Pakistan showed that urban drinking water sources, such as tap water were of poor quality and unfit for consumption. 65 These findings highlight the need to improve treatment and reduce the risk of water contamination particularly in water sourced from tankers and boreholes.

The WQI calculated based on selected physicochemical parameters, such as pH, EC, total TDS, TH, turbidity, Fe2+, and chlorine revealed that the water was safe for drinking. Further, the analyzed water samples for pH, EC, temperature, turbidity, sodium ion, and zinc comply with standard limit set for drinking water.40,41 Additionally, the TDS were also falling in the acceptable limits for approximately 93% of the samples analyzed. In agreement with this finding, the majority of drinking water samples in Hawassa city were within the acceptable limit of TDS. 66

Heavy metals, such as lead (Pb), zinc (Zn), and copper (Cu), are among the most critical contaminants of drinking water with notable impact and toxicity on human health. 67 In this study, none of these metals were detected in the analyzed samples. This indicates a limited level of ground water contamination with municipal and industrial waste in the study area. Although the WQI based on physicochemical parameters is good, it was observed that some parameters still put health concern. For example, the Fe2+ level which exceeded the acceptable limit (>0.3 mg/L) in approximately 32% of samples poses a consumer’s health concern as its intake has been associated with hepato cellular carcinoma. 68 Therefore, pretreatment and/or source of substitution for drinking water may be warranted in the study area.

Another safety challenge was the TH, which exceeded the acceptable limit in all samples. It was also identified that the Ca2+, which was probably associated with water hardness, exceeded the limit in all samples. Corroborating these findings, other study by Shishaye and Nagari 69 also reported a high level of calcium in ground water of the same area, which originated from carbonate and silicate minerals in the local geology. In addition to its health impacts, 70 water hardness also reduces the efficiency of water supply facilities, and an emphasis should be given to mitigate the challenges. 71

Limitation

Although the number of samples collected provided sufficient data to evaluate the safety of drinking water through physicochemical and microbial analyses and to develop the Water Quality Index (WQI), a priori power analysis was not conducted to calculate the sample size for the study. The seasonal-based microbial analysis did not include the main rainy (June to August) and autumn (September to November) in its sampling, which was regarded as the limitation of the study. In the current study, the slight increase in microbial contamination during secondary wet season suggests that sampling during main rainy season and post rainy season may provide additional insights onto seasonal effects. Moreover, assessing the impact of seasonal variation on physicochemical parameters is also warranted. Thus, comprehensive water quality assessment including antimicrobial resistance on microbes recovered from water samples across seasons, and water handling and storage practices at household level may be needed to generate baseline data for drinking water mitigation.

Conclusion

This study was conducted in Maya City Administration, eastern Ethiopia and assessed drinking water safety using 120 samples collected from different sources. A drinking water quality index (WQI) was computed using physicochemical and microbial parameters to evaluate water quality. The microbial analysis performed using petrifilm revealed microbial contamination risks across water sources, with tanker and borehole-supplied water showed the highest level of contamination. Overall, one-third of the samples were contaminated with coliforms, and 15% were contaminated with E. coli, indicating poor sanitary conditions and fecal contamination in drinking water facilities. The occurrence of these contaminants suggests an increased risk of waterborne disease, caused by enteric pathogens, which may disproportionately affect vulnerable groups such as children, pregnant women, and elderly. The physicochemical parameter such as pH, electrical conductivity, temperature, turbidity, sodium ion, and zinc, were within the acceptable limits of Ethiopian standard. However, elevated water hardness poses a public health concern.

Despite the WQI indicating that the water suitable for drinking, fecal contamination, poor sanitary practices, and water hardness remain significant drinking water safety challenges in the study area. Thus, appropriate drinking water safety management practices should be implemented to ensure consumers’ safety.
