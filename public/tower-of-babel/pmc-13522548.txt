Dual-mode SOX2 regulation by miR-150 via mRNA suppression and autophagy controls stem cell fate
Authors: Xing Zhen, Hyebin Koh, Dong-Seok Lee, Jae-Won Huh, Seok-Ho Hong, Tae-Don Kim, Jong-Hee Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Autophagy plays a critical role in maintaining cellular homeostasis and regulating stem cell fate. Although microRNAs (miRNAs) are being increasingly recognized for their ability to modulate autophagy and pluripotency in human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs), the underlying molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood. This study defines a role for miR-150 deficiency in biasing ectoderm–mesoderm lineage specification in hPSCs and identifies SOX2 as a key downstream node regulated primarily through altered mRNA stability, with additional involvement of an autophagy-associated pathway. miR-150-deficient hPSCs were generated by CRISPR/Cas9-mediated deletion of the miR-150 locus. A range of molecular and cellular techniques were used to investigate how miR-150 regulates SOX2 and KLHL20 expression, modulates the KLHL20–ULK1 interaction, and influences autophagy-mediated SOX2 degradation. Directed differentiation protocols were used to determine the role of miR-150 in germ layer lineage specification. The loss of miR-150 led to increased SOX2 expression by stabilizing its mRNA, resulting in elevated SOX2 protein levels in hPSCs. Concurrently, miR-150 deficiency increased KLHL20 mRNA stability, disrupted ULK1-mediated autophagy, and inhibited SOX2 degradation. Functional assays demonstrated that miR-150 fine-tunes SOX2 expression through dual regulation of mRNA stability and autophagy via the KLHL20–ULK1 axis, maintaining a balanced commitment to the mesodermal and ectodermal lineages. These findings establish miR-150 as a regulator of SOX2 activity and autophagy in hPSCs. By targeting both SOX2 and the KLHL20–ULK1 axis, miR-150 coordinates SOX2 turnover through complementary mechanisms, ensuring precise mesodermal and ectodermal lineage commitment. This multiregulatory strategy provides new insights into how miRNAs integrate intracellular signaling pathways to direct stem cell fate decisions.

Introduction

During embryonic development, cells undergo a series of tightly coordinated processes, including organogenesis and differentiation into functional tissues. Early development involves the differentiation of pluripotent cells within the inner cell mass into epiblasts, which subsequently give rise to three germ layers—the ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm—a process regulated by growth differentiation factor signaling pathways.1 Human embryonic stem cells and induced pluripotent stem cells possess the unique ability to self-renew indefinitely and differentiate into all three germ layers, making them invaluable models for studying embryonic development, lineage specification, and contributions to tissue homeostasis, repair, and regeneration.2,3 Stem cell fate decisions—whether to maintain pluripotency or differentiate into specific lineages—are orchestrated by a complex interplay between intrinsic transcriptional networks (e.g., OCT4, SOX2, and NANOG) and extrinsic cues such as growth factors, extracellular matrix components, and mechanical stimuli.4 Understanding these regulatory mechanisms is crucial not only for advancing developmental biology but also for optimizing regenerative medicine and cell-based therapeutic approaches. The ability to precisely control the direction and efficiency of stem cell differentiation provides valuable insights for developing functional tissue engineering and cell replacement strategies.5 Among the key transcription factors that regulate stem cell differentiation and lineage commitment, SOX2 is well recognized for its pivotal role in maintaining pluripotency and controlling both proliferation and differentiation potential.6 Together with OCT4 and NANOG, SOX2 forms a core transcriptional network that governs stem cell fate decisions. Dynamic regulation of SOX2 is essential for directing stem cells toward specific lineages, particularly the neuroectoderm. In both mouse embryonic stem cells and induced pluripotent stem cells, SOX2 overexpression inhibits differentiation into endodermal and mesodermal lineages while promoting neural lineage commitment.7 Therefore, SOX2 activity is fundamental not only for determining differentiation trajectories but also for facilitating cellular reprogramming and tissue regeneration. Dysregulated SOX2 expression can disrupt stem cell function and lineage specification, leading to developmental and regenerative abnormalities.8

Autophagy, a conserved lysosome-dependent degradation and recycling process, is crucial for maintaining cellular homeostasis, responding to stress, and regulating metabolism. It also contributes to aging by clearing damaged organelles, protein aggregates, and metabolic waste.9 UNC-51-like autophagy kinase 1 (ULK1) serves as a key initiator of autophagy, forming a complex with FIP200, ATG13, and ATG101 to trigger autophagosome formation in response to nutrient deprivation or cellular stress. ULK1 activity is controlled by posttranslational modifications, particularly ubiquitination.10 Ubiquitination, which is mediated by E3 ubiquitin ligases and deubiquitinating enzymes, can either promote or inhibit autophagy depending on the cellular context. For example, the Cullin 3–KLHL20 E3 ligase complex catalyzes the K48-linked ubiquitination of ULK1, targeting it for proteasomal degradation and thereby terminating autophagy.11 Conversely, TRAF6-mediated K63-linked ubiquitination stabilizes ULK1, facilitating autophagy initiation.12 Additionally, the deubiquitinating enzyme USP20 stabilizes ULK1 by removing ubiquitin chains, further promoting autophagy activation.13 Recent evidence highlights the essential role of autophagy in stem cell self-renewal, proliferation, and lineage differentiation. For instance, FoxO3-regulated autophagy is indispensable for maintaining hematopoietic stem cell homeostasis.14 Moreover, autophagy influences SOX2 stability and expression through multiple pathways. Studies have indicated that autophagy activation can both promote the degradation and increase the stabilization of the SOX2 protein, depending on the regulatory context.15 Although a connection between autophagy and SOX2 regulation has been established, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. It is not yet known whether autophagy directly mediates SOX2 degradation or indirectly modulates its expression through upstream transcriptional, epigenetic, or microRNA (miRNA)-mediated mechanisms. Although autophagy has been implicated in the regulation of transcription factor homeostasis, how autophagy-associated mechanisms influence SOX2 regulation in hPSCs remains incompletely understood. In particular, the contribution of miRNA-mediated pathways linking autophagy to SOX2 homeostasis during early lineage specification has not been defined.

miRNAs, small noncoding RNAs, regulate gene expression posttranscriptionally by promoting mRNA degradation or inhibiting translation, thereby influencing diverse cellular processes. miR-150 is a well-characterized regulator of lineage specification in multiple stem and progenitor cell systems, particularly with respect to the dosage of the transcription factor that influences alternative fate decisions.16–18 Early lineage bifurcation in hPSCs is similarly sensitive to small changes in transcription factor levels,19,20 suggesting that miR-150 may perform a conserved regulatory function during early differentiation. Consistent with this notion, publicly available miRNA datasets indicate that miR-150 is detectable in hPSCs and dynamically regulated upon differentiation.21 Despite these well-characterized functions in differentiated and progenitor populations, whether miR-150 contributes to early lineage bias decisions at the level of hPSCs remains poorly understood. In particular, it is unclear whether miR-150 influences the balance between mesodermal and ectodermal specification during the transition from pluripotency to lineage commitment. Addressing this gap is important, as subtle posttranscriptional regulation at early developmental stages can have disproportionate effects on downstream lineage outcomes. In this study, we therefore investigated the role of miR-150 in hPSCs using a genetic loss-of-function approach, focusing on how miR-150 deficiency influences mesoderm–ectoderm lineage bias and the underlying molecular mechanisms governing this process.

In this study, we aimed to elucidate how miR-150 regulates pluripotency and germ layer specification in hPSCs. Our findings demonstrate that miR-150 not only modulates the expression of core pluripotency factors but also regulates autophagy-related pathways, thereby influencing early cell fate decisions. Through a combination of molecular assays and directed differentiation protocols, we identified miR-150 as a pivotal molecular link between autophagy regulation and stem cell developmental potential. These results provide new insights into how miRNAs integrate intracellular signaling networks to govern germ layer commitment.

Results

miR-150 deficiency promotes ectodermal and neural differentiation while impairing mesodermal development and hematopoietic cell formation

To investigate the regulatory role of miR-150 in hPSCs, we employed a CRISPR/Cas9-based system to generate stable miR-150-KO cell lines, enabling a comprehensive assessment of its function in stem cell self-renewal and differentiation (Fig. 1a). Genomic PCR analysis confirmed precise integration at the miR-150 locus with no detectable off-target insertions (Supplementary Fig. 1a, g). A miRNA expression assay further validated the successful knockout of miR-150 in KO cells (both miR-150-5p and miR-150-3p) (Supplementary Fig. 1b). We next confirmed the pluripotency of miR-150-KO hPSCs by evaluating key pluripotency markers (SSEA4, TRA-1-60, NANOG, and OCT4), which remained robustly expressed, indicating that pluripotency was preserved (Supplementary Fig. 1c, d). Karyotypic analysis revealed no chromosomal abnormalities following CRISPR/Cas9 editing, and cell viability assays confirmed that the loss of miR-150 did not affect cell survival (Supplementary Fig. 1e, f). To assess lineage differentiation potential, miR-150-KO hPSCs were subjected to trilineage differentiation using a commercial kit. Notably, the absence of miR-150 markedly suppressed mesoderm differentiation while enhancing ectoderm formation without significantly affecting endodermal differentiation (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 2a). Given this pronounced effect on the balance between mesodermal and ectodermal differentiation, we further conducted directed differentiation assays for both neural (ectodermal) and mesodermal lineages, including hematopoietic progenitor specification and maturation (Fig. 1c). In neural differentiation assays, on day 8, the expression of PAX6, a neural stem cell marker, and SOX2 significantly increased (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. 2b). To enable direct comparison with WT cells, we included pluripotency marker data alongside experimental groups (Supplementary Fig. 2b), supporting the interpretation of downstream differentiation phenotypes in the context of baseline pluripotency status. By day 15, miR-150-KO promoted neuronal differentiation and maturation, as indicated by increased expression of the mature neuronal marker Tuj1 (Fig. 1e). Conversely, hematopoietic differentiation assays demonstrated that miR-150 deficiency severely impaired mesoderm induction, accompanied by pronounced abnormalities in EB size and morphology (Supplementary Fig. 2c, d), leading to reduced differentiation of both CD34⁺/CD45⁺ hematopoietic progenitor cells and CD34⁻/CD45⁺ mature hematopoietic cells (Fig. 1f). Moreover, total cell yields during hematopoietic differentiation significantly decreased (Supplementary Fig. 2e), and notably, neural cells were unexpectedly observed in hematopoietic differentiation cultures (Fig. 1g, h). In the CFU assays, miR-150 deficiency caused a significant reduction in the number of CFUs, whereas the types of colonies (CFU-E, BFU-E, CFU-G, CFU-M, CFU-GM, and CFU-GEMM) remained unchanged (Fig. 1i, Supplementary Fig. 2f). To assess its physiological relevance beyond 2D culture, we examined the function of miR-150 in 3D blood vessel organoids (BVOs). Compared with 2D culture, miR-150 KO impaired organoid formation, resulting in the formation of smaller structures with reduced angiogenic sprouting (Supplementary Fig. 2g), indicating that the loss of miR-150 affects 3D morphogenesis and vascular development, in which cell–cell interactions and tissue architecture are better preserved. During spontaneous differentiation, the loss of miR-150 further enhanced neural lineage commitment, as evidenced by elevated PAX6 and SOX2 expression but suppressed mesodermal differentiation, as shown by reduced Brachyury and MIXL1 expression (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). Collectively, these findings indicate that miR-150 deficiency disrupts the balance of lineage specification in hPSCs, promoting ectodermal and neural differentiation at the expense of mesodermal and hematopoietic development. miR-150 deletion increases SOX2 mRNA stability, leading to SOX2 protein accumulation and impaired blood cell differentiation

The role of miR-150 as a regulator of mRNA expression has garnered considerable attention. RT–qPCR analysis of bioinformatically predicted targets revealed that the expression of SOX2, KLHL20, GOSR1, EGR2, SP1, MMP14, ADIPOR2, EP300, AKIRIN1, PHAX, and MLXIP was significantly upregulated in miR-150-KO hPSCs (Supplementary Fig. 4a). These genes are functionally linked to stem cell pluripotency and differentiation, ubiquitin-mediated regulation, transcriptional control, chromatin remodeling, metabolic signaling, and cellular organization—processes that are critical for lineage specification. Collectively, these findings indicate that miR-150 governs a broad regulatory gene network, thereby providing a foundation for future mechanistic investigations. Because OCT4, NANOG, and SOX2 form the core pluripotency transcriptional network and are sensitive to upstream miRNA-mediated regulation, we examined their expression after miR-150 deletion as an initial readout of pluripotency network stability.22 Although direct targeting of these factors by miR-150 has not been established, prior studies have implicated miR-150 in stem/lineage regulatory programs.23–25 Notably, SOX2 expression was significantly upregulated in the miR-150-deficient lines at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 2a–c). To determine whether miR-150 affects SOX2 mRNA stability, cells were treated with ActD, a classical transcriptional inhibitor. Degradation of SOX2 mRNA was markedly attenuated in miR-150-KO cells, suggesting that miR-150 facilitates SOX2 mRNA decay (Fig. 2d). To further explore the mechanism underlying SOX2 regulation, a miR-150-KO HEK293A cell line was generated using the CRISPR/Cas9 system (Supplementary Fig. 4b, c). A luciferase reporter assay revealed that the stability of the SOX2 3′UTR was significantly enhanced in the absence of miR-150. Cotransfection with miR-150 mimics (both 5p and 3p strands) substantially decreased reporter activity, indicating that miR-150 negatively regulates SOX2 expression by directly targeting its 3′UTR (Supplementary Fig. 4d). Further assays revealed that both the miR-150-5p and the miR-150-3p mimics reduced the stability of the SOX2 3′UTR, but the difference between the two strands was not significant (Supplementary Fig. 4e). miRWalk target prediction identified potential miR-150-5p binding sites within the SOX2 3′UTR, and experimental validation confirmed that miR-150-5p directly bound to the SOX2 3′UTR, leading to SOX2 repression (Fig. 2e). Treatment of wild-type cells with a miR-150-5p inhibitor resulted in a significant increase in SOX2 expression at both the RNA and protein levels (Fig. 2f, g). During hematopoietic differentiation, SOX2 expression decreased in both wild-type and miR-150-KO cells but remained high in KO cells across matched time points, as determined by immunoblot quantification. During neural differentiation, SOX2 levels were elevated in both groups, with higher levels of expression in miR-150-KO cells than in wild-type cells (Fig. 2h–k). Knockdown of SOX2 in miR-150-KO cells using siRNA rescued mesodermal differentiation and promoted the development of hematopoietic progenitor and mature hematopoietic cells (Fig. 2l–n). Furthermore, reintroduction of miR-150 mimics into miR-150-KO cells significantly suppressed SOX2 expression at both the RNA and protein levels, restoring mesodermal differentiation (Fig. 2o–q). siSOX2 and miR-150 mimic transfection partially mitigated the KO-associated differentiation defects. While full restoration to the WT baseline was not assessed in this experimental setup, these results support a contributory role for SOX2 upregulation in the observed KO phenotype. Rescue experiments used a miR-150 mimic without direct comparison of miR-150-5p and miR-150-3p, and strand-specific contributions to the rescue phenotypes cannot be quantitatively resolved in the current study. Although SOX2 is a core pluripotency factor, it also has a specific neural/ectodermal fate. SOX2 elevation in miR-150-KO hPSCs does not disrupt pluripotency but likely primes lineage bias during early differentiation, making miR-150 regulation critical at the pluripotency-to-lineage transition. miR-150 modulates hPSC autophagy

Increased SOX2 mRNA stability may increase the level of the SOX2 protein, but posttranslational regulation via protein degradation could also contribute. We therefore examined whether altered protein turnover affects SOX2 accumulation in miR-150-KO cells. Initial analyses revealed changes in autophagy markers, including reduced ULK1 and altered p62, suggesting impaired autophagy, which motivated further investigation of the role of autophagy in the increase in SOX2 expression. Concurrently, we assessed the ubiquitin–proteasome system by evaluating USP24 and USP20 expression (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b), and their levels were unchanged in miR-150-KO cells, suggesting that this UPS node is not involved; however, the involvement of other UPS components cannot be excluded. The elevated levels of SOX2 protein observed in the miR-150-KO cell lines imply dysregulation of SOX2 synthesis and/or degradation. Immunocytochemistry revealed that although SOX2 functions primarily as a nuclear transcription factor, it accumulates abnormally in the cytoplasm of miR-150-KO cells (Fig. 3a). To investigate whether autophagy, a major protein degradation pathway, contributes to SOX2 turnover, we performed costaining with LAMP1, a lysosomal marker, and SOX2. Minimal colocalization between cytoplasmic SOX2 and LAMP1 was detected, indicating that autophagy-mediated SOX2 degradation is limited in the absence of miR-150 (Fig. 3b). We next assessed autophagic activity using CYTO-ID staining, which labels autophagic vesicles. Compared with wild-type control cells, miR-150-KO cells exhibited reduced autophagy-related dye signaling (Fig. 3c, d), which is consistent with impaired autophagic activity. To evaluate autophagic flux directly, we employed the RFP-EGFP-Target reporter system, which distinguishes autophagosomes (yellow puncta; EGFP⁺/RFP⁺) from autolysosomes (red puncta; RFP⁺ only) on the basis of the pH sensitivity of EGFP (Fig. 3e). In miR-150-KO cells, we observed a significant accumulation of yellow puncta and a marked reduction in red puncta, indicating impaired autophagic flux, likely due to defective autophagosome–lysosome fusion or lysosomal degradation (Fig. 3f). To determine whether SOX2 itself is trafficked through the autophagy–lysosome pathway, we constructed an RFP-EGFP-SOX2 fusion reporter. Live-cell imaging revealed predominantly yellow puncta and a pronounced decrease in red-only puncta in miR-150-KO cells, suggesting that the delivery of SOX2 to lysosomes for degradation was inefficient (Fig. 3g). Because the RFP–EGFP–SOX2 reporter reflects overexpression and endogenous SOX2–LC3 colocalization was not directly assessed, our data are interpreted as supportive of lysosome/autophagy-associated regulation rather than definitive evidence of endogenous SOX2 trafficking or degradation. To further elucidate the molecular basis of impaired autophagy, we analyzed key components of the autophagy regulatory network, including the AKT–mTOR signaling pathway, the autophagy initiation kinase ULK1, and the downstream autophagy markers p62 and LC3. Quantitative analysis of LC3A/LC3B conversion revealed reduced LC3B levels in miR-150-KO cells, accompanied by p62 accumulation and decreased ULK1 expression, which is consistent with impaired autophagic flux. Although AKT activation was increased, mTOR activity was not significantly altered, suggesting compensatory or context-dependent regulation of autophagy signaling.26,27 Notably, both total ULK1 and phosphorylated ULK1 (p-ULK1) levels were significantly decreased in miR-150-KO cells, indicating a disruption in autophagy initiation. Consequently, p62 degradation and LC3B conversion were impaired, which was consistent with a blockade of autophagic progression (Fig. 3h). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that miR-150 deficiency disrupts autophagy initiation and flux, leading to defective lysosomal degradation and abnormal cytoplasmic accumulation of SOX2. Modulation of autophagy alleviates the differentiation bias caused by miR-150 deficiency in hPSCs

In miR-150-KO hPSCs, elevated SOX2 expression and impaired autophagy are associated with a disrupted mesoderm–ectoderm lineage balance. To determine whether modulating autophagy could directly restore this phenotype, we treated cells with RAPA, a well-established autophagy activator. RAPA treatment significantly increased the CYTO-ID fluorescence signal, confirming robust autophagy activation (Fig. 4a, b). RAPA also suppressed mTOR signaling, with comparable effects in both miR-150-KO and wild-type hPSCs (Fig. 4c). Notably, RAPA restored ULK1 expression and enhanced autophagic flux, as evidenced by increased degradation of the autophagy substrates p62 and LC3. Quantification of the p-ULK1/total ULK1 ratio revealed that the ratio remained comparable across groups despite significant changes in total ULK1 protein levels. This finding indicates that the observed rescue is driven by the restoration of ULK1 protein abundance rather than by altering its specific phosphorylation efficiency. This activation of autophagy promoted the degradation of SOX2 (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Fig. 5a). Because changes in autophagy-related markers alone do not definitively indicate defects in autophagic flux, we next tested whether lysosomal inhibition affects ULK1 and SOX2 protein homeostasis. Treatment with BafA1, a lysosomal inhibitor, markedly reduced ULK1 levels and led to abnormal accumulation of SOX2 (Fig. 4e), which is consistent with the lysosome-dependent regulation of these proteins in miR-150-KO cells. Similarly, treatment with the early-stage autophagy inhibitor 3-MA also suppressed ULK1 expression and caused SOX2 accumulation (Fig. 4f), further supporting a role for autophagy in regulating SOX2 turnover in miR-150-KO cells. To assess the regulatory effect of ULK1 on SOX2, we knocked down ULK1 in hPSCs, which markedly increased SOX2 protein levels (Supplementary Fig. 5b), supporting a role for ULK1 in controlling SOX2 protein abundance. Mechanistically, coimmunoprecipitation analysis revealed that the interaction between SOX2 and the selective autophagy adaptor p62 was significantly compromised in miR-150-KO cells (Supplementary Fig. 5c). Furthermore, we observed a marked reduction in SOX2 ubiquitination (Supplementary Fig. 5d), indicating a failure in the ubiquitin-dependent recognition required for autophagic degradation. Together, these results confirm that miR-150 regulates SOX2 protein stability by ensuring its targeting of the autophagy pathway. Neural differentiation assays demonstrated that RAPA treatment significantly mitigated the excessive neural lineage commitment observed in miR-150-KO hPSCs. RAPA treatment reduced the proportion of PAX6⁺ cells among both WT and miR-150-KO cells, indicating a largely genotype-independent effect on neuroectodermal differentiation under conditions of strong autophagy induction (Fig. 4g, Supplementary Fig. 5e). In contrast, more KO-specific responses to RAPA treatment (Fig. 4h, Supplementary Fig. 5f), reduced aberrant PAX6 upregulation (Fig. 4i), and improved CFU formation capacity (Supplementary Fig. 5g, h) were detected in mesoderm differentiation, suggesting lineage-dependent sensitivity to autophagy modulation. However, RAPA treatment during differentiation did not generally increase hematopoietic differentiation; instead, it exhibited an overall inhibitory effect (Supplementary Fig. 5i). Consistently, analysis of miR-150 expression revealed a progressive decrease during both hematopoietic and neural differentiation (Supplementary Fig. 5j). Together, these observations suggest that miR-150–regulated autophagy plays a regulatory role predominantly in the undifferentiated state. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that autophagy activation not only alleviates molecular abnormalities such as SOX2 accumulation but also functionally corrects the lineage imbalance caused by miR-150 deficiency. Forced autophagy activation can bypass the regulatory role of miR-150 in lineage specification. Our results suggest that miR-150 contributes to autophagic homeostasis under normal conditions and that loss of miR-150 is associated with reduced autophagic activity, whereas under conditions such as RAPA treatment, autophagy is strongly induced. Regulation of KLHL20 mRNA stability by miR-150 modulates autophagy in hPSCs

In miR-150-KO hPSCs, the expression of ULK1—a key initiator of autophagy—was markedly decreased at the protein level. Notably, the ULK1 mRNA levels did not differ significantly between the miR-150-KO and wild-type cells, suggesting that miR-150 regulates ULK1 posttranscriptionally (Fig. 5a). To investigate the mechanisms underlying ULK1 protein stability, cells were treated with CHX to block protein synthesis. CHX chase assays revealed that ULK1 degradation was substantially accelerated in miR-150-deficient cells, indicating enhanced protein turnover (Fig. 5b). Moreover, treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 effectively prevented ULK1 degradation, indicating that the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway is involved in ULK1 regulation (Fig. 5c). Consistent with these findings, MG132 treatment led to a pronounced increase in ULK1 ubiquitination in miR-150-KO cells, suggesting that miR-150 deficiency promotes ULK1 degradation through ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal pathways (Fig. 5d). We next focused on KLHL20, an E3 ubiquitin ligase that targets ULK1 for proteasomal degradation. KLHL20 expression was significantly upregulated at both the mRNA and protein levels in the miR-150-KO hPSCs compared with the wild-type controls (Fig. 5e, f), whereas the expression of other reported ULK1 ubiquitination-related regulators remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). These data suggest that miR-150 deficiency does not broadly alter ULK1 E3 ligase expression; however, potential functional redundancy or compensatory regulation among ULK1-targeting pathways cannot be excluded. Notably, RAPA treatment significantly reduced KLHL20–ULK1 colocalization, as determined by quantitative coimmunofluorescence analysis (Supplementary Fig. 6c), suggesting that autophagy activation is associated with altered spatial proximity of KLHL20 and ULK1. To biochemically define the molecular basis of this interaction, we performed coimmunoprecipitation of endogenous ULK1 in hPSCs. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 6d, KLHL20 coimmunoprecipitated with ULK1, providing direct evidence of their physical interaction. Crucially, this interaction was significantly enriched in the miR-150-KO cells compared with the WT controls. This differential binding indicates that miR-150 deficiency promotes the formation of the KLHL20-ULK1 complex, thereby facilitating ULK1 turnover. To elucidate the mechanism underlying KLHL20 upregulation, ActD chase assays were performed, revealing that KLHL20 mRNA degradation was markedly slower in miR-150-KO cells than in control cells, indicating enhanced mRNA stability (Fig. 5g). A luciferase reporter assay confirmed that miR-150 directly binds to the 3′UTR of KLHL20, resulting in significant repression of reporter activity (Supplementary Fig. 6e). Among the two strands, compared with miR-150-5p, miR-150-3p had a stronger inhibitory effect on KLHL20 expression (Supplementary Fig. 6f). RNAhybrid analysis predicted potential miR-150 binding sites within the KLHL20 3′UTR. Mutation of these sites abolished the inhibitory effect of miR-150-3p in luciferase assays, confirming the direct and sequence-specific nature of this interaction (Fig. 5h). Furthermore, transfection of a miR-150-3p inhibitor into wild-type hPSCs significantly increased KLHL20 expression, further indicating that miR-150-3p negatively regulates KLHL20 via its 3′UTR (Fig. 5i, j). During both hematopoietic differentiation and neural differentiation, a significant increase in KLHL20 mRNA expression was observed in miR-150-KO cells, with no detectable change at the protein level (Fig. 5k–n), suggesting that the regulation of KLHL20 by miR-150 is more prominent in the pluripotent stem cell state than during differentiation. To determine the functional role of KLHL20 in this context, siRNA-mediated knockdown of KLHL20 was performed. Silencing KLHL20 led to a marked increase in ULK1 protein levels and increased autophagic activity (Fig. 5o, p). Functionally, KLHL20 knockdown significantly improved mesodermal and hematopoietic differentiation in miR-150-deficient hPSCs, indicating that upregulated KLHL20 expression impaired lineage specification by promoting ULK1 degradation and suppressing autophagy (Fig. 5q, r). Consistent with these findings, transfection with a miR-150 mimic markedly suppressed KLHL20 mRNA expression, restored ULK1 levels, and promoted autophagy, confirming the regulatory role of the miR-150–KLHL20–ULK1 axis in lineage determination (Fig. 5s, t; Supplementary Fig. 6g, h). siKLHL20 and miR-150 mimic treatment partially alleviated the differentiation defects. Although the extent of recovery relative to the WT baseline remains to be fully quantified, the data indicate that modulating KLHL20 and miR-150 contributes to the rescue of the KO phenotype. In conclusion, miR-150 deficiency promotes KLHL20-mediated ubiquitin–proteasome degradation of ULK1, resulting in impaired autophagy and disrupted lineage differentiation. Autophagy-dependent degradation of SOX2 is promoted by miR-150 through modulation of the KLHL20–ULK1 pathway

Given the role of KLHL20-mediated ULK1 degradation in suppressing autophagy, we next examined whether this regulatory axis contributes to the aberrant accumulation of SOX2 observed in miR-150-deficient hPSCs. siRNA-mediated knockdown of KLHL20 expression significantly reduced SOX2 protein levels in miR-150-KO hPSCs, whereas the expression of the other core pluripotency markers OCT4 and NANOG remained unaffected (Fig. 6a, b). Conversely, SOX2 knockdown did not alter the expression of KLHL20, ULK1, or other autophagy-related markers, indicating that SOX2 functions downstream of the KLHL20–ULK1–autophagy axis (Fig. 6c). To exclude potential cross-reactivity, we first validated the specificity of the strand-specific inhibitors. RT‒qPCR analysis confirmed that the miR-150-5p inhibitor selectively suppressed the expression of miR-150-5p but did not affect the expression of miR-150-3p; conversely, the miR-150-3p inhibitor specifically reduced the expression of miR-150-3p (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Consistently, transfection of these specific inhibitors into wild-type cells recapitulated the upregulation of SOX2, further supporting a regulatory relationship between miR-150 and SOX2 stability (Fig. 6d). Although miR-150-5p is the predominant strand expressed in hPSCs and primarily regulates SOX2 mRNA stability, miR-150-3p inhibition resulted in a more pronounced increase in SOX2 protein levels. This finding is consistent with a dual regulatory mode in which miR-150-3p additionally targets KLHL20 to modulate autophagy-mediated SOX2 degradation. Functionally, dual knockdown of SOX2 and KLHL20 significantly increased both the proportion and absolute number of CD34⁻CD45⁺ hematopoietic cells during differentiation, suggesting that SOX2 and KLHL20 cooperatively regulate hematopoietic lineage commitment (Fig. 6e, f). Notably, treatment with a miR-150 mimic and combined knockdown of SOX2 and KLHL20 yielded comparable levels of hematopoietic differentiation, with no significant differences between the two conditions. These findings imply that the two interventions converge on a similar functional pathway that promotes hematopoietic lineage specification (Fig. 6g, h). siRNA knockdown partially improved KO-associated differentiation defects, which is consistent with the phenotype; however, because WT ± siRNAs were not included, the extent of rescue relative to that of the WT cannot be quantified in the current dataset. To assess the generalizability of these findings, we validated the key phenotypes in a previously established human iPSC line (BMC3).28 Consistent with our observations in H9 hPSCs, miR-150 knockout in iPSCs significantly impaired hematopoietic differentiation and increased PAX6-positive cell populations (Supplementary Fig. 7b, c). Furthermore, qPCR analysis confirmed the significant upregulation of SOX2 and KLHL20 in miR-150-deficient iPSCs (Supplementary Fig. 7d), supporting the broader relevance of the miR-150-KLHL20-ULK1-SOX2 regulatory axis across pluripotent stem cell lines. In summary, miR-150 regulates lineage specification by modulating autophagy and SOX2 stability through two distinct yet converging mechanisms. First, miR-150 suppresses KLHL20 expression by reducing its mRNA stability, thereby limiting the KLHL20-mediated ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of ULK1, which enhances autophagic activity. Autophagy regulation is essential for controlling SOX2 protein accumulation. Second, miR-150 directly decreases SOX2 mRNA stability, further lowering SOX2 levels. Discussion

This study reveals a previously unrecognized dual regulatory mechanism by which miR-150 governs both the maintenance of hPSC identity and the orchestration of lineage specification. Specifically, miR-150 modulates the expression of the core pluripotency transcription factor SOX2 at both the posttranscriptional and posttranslational levels. On the one hand, miR-150-KO enhances SOX2 mRNA stability, thereby sustaining its expression; on the other hand, it prevents SOX2 protein degradation by repressing the KLHL20–ULK1 axis, which mediates autophagy-dependent turnover of SOX2. Loss of miR-150 disrupts this finely balanced regulatory network, leading to aberrant accumulation of the SOX2 protein and a skewed differentiation trajectory that favors neuroectodermal commitment while impairing mesodermal and hematopoietic development. Collectively, these findings identify miR-150 as a pivotal epigenetic modulator of pluripotency and lineage determination, offering mechanistic insights that may guide the optimization of directed differentiation strategies in stem cell–based regenerative medicine. Recent research has increasingly revealed the pleiotropic roles of miR-150 across diverse biological contexts; however, its functions in hPSCs have remained largely unexplored. Traditionally, miR-150 has been recognized as a key regulator of hematopoiesis and immune cell differentiation. It promotes B-cell development by targeting c-Myb and modulates T-cell function through the suppression of FOXP1 and RC3H1 expression.23,29 In cancer, miR-150 exhibits tumor-suppressive activity in several malignancies; for instance, in osteosarcoma, it inhibits cell proliferation and metastasis by targeting oncogenes such as IGF2BP1, and in leukemia, it suppresses malignant hematopoiesis by downregulating key oncogenic factors, including MYB and FLT3, thereby impairing leukemia cell survival and proliferation.30,31 In stem cell biology, emerging evidence has begun to link miR-150 to lineage-specific differentiation. For example, in mesenchymal stem cells, miR-150 suppresses osteogenic differentiation by inhibiting irisin and modulating the p38/MAPK pathway,17 whereas in adipose-derived stem cells, it regulates adipogenesis through direct targeting of Notch3.32 Despite these advances, the role of miR-150 in maintaining pluripotency and governing early lineage decisions has remained largely undefined. Our study extends the biological significance of miR-150 by identifying it as a pivotal regulator of hPSC fate. We demonstrate that miR-150 controls differentiation through the following dual mechanisms: destabilizing SOX2 mRNA at the posttranscriptional level and promoting its autophagic degradation via the KLHL20–ULK1 pathway. These findings establish the first mechanistic link between miR-150 and autophagy in the regulation of stem cell fate. Given that autophagy is increasingly recognized as a crucial modulator of stem cell maintenance and differentiation,33 our work highlights the role of miR-150 at the intersection of autophagy signaling and developmental decision-making in hPSCs.

SOX2 is a master transcription factor that is essential for maintaining pluripotency through the formation of core regulatory networks with OCT4 and NANOG.34 However, SOX2 expression must be tightly regulated during differentiation. Overexpression of SOX2 biases hPSCs toward a neuroectodermal fate by inducing the expression of neural genes such as PAX6 and suppressing mesodermal and hematopoietic programs.35 Conversely, downregulation of SOX2 is necessary for the commitment of the mesendodermal lineage, including differentiation into cardiac, hematopoietic, and endodermal cell types.36 Previous work has reported that miR-150-5p–dependent suppression of SOX2 occurs in nonhPSC contexts. For example, Wu et al. validated SOX2 as a downstream target of miR-150-5p in an exosome/endothelial setting.37 In breast cancer cells, manipulation of the FOXD2-AS1/miR-150-5p axis alters the expression of stemness-associated factors, including OCT4, NANOG, and SOX2, although direct binding was demonstrated for FOXD2-AS1 and PFN2 in that study.38 These differences highlight that the effects of miR-150 on the pluripotency network can be context- and state dependent. Consistent with this, we observed robust SOX2 upregulation in miR-150-KO hPSCs, whereas OCT4 and NANOG remained largely unchanged under self-renewal conditions, suggesting that selective sensitivity and/or compensatory buffering occurred in the hPSC pluripotency circuitry. Our study revealed that miR-150 exerts dual control over SOX2 levels, directly by reducing SOX2 mRNA stability and indirectly by promoting its autophagic degradation through the ULK1–KLHL20 pathway. This two-pronged regulation is crucial for fine-tuning SOX2 abundance during the transition from pluripotency to lineage commitment. Autophagy-mediated SOX2 degradation not only governs protein turnover but also prevents excessive SOX2 accumulation, which could otherwise disrupt balanced developmental outcomes. These findings align with recent evidence highlighting the role of autophagy in modulating transcription factor availability during stem cell lineage transitions.15

Our findings reveal a previously uncharacterized regulatory axis in hPSCs in which miR-150 fine-tunes autophagy through posttranscriptional repression of KLHL20, an E3 ligase adaptor that mediates ULK1 ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. Specifically, we demonstrated that miR-150 reduces KLHL20 mRNA stability, thereby preventing ULK1 turnover and sustaining autophagy activity. This indirect regulatory circuit highlights the noncanonical role of miRNAs in modulating protein degradation pathways through the targeting of upstream modulators.39 ULK1 serves as a master initiator of autophagy, and its abundance must be controlled to maintain proper autophagic flux. Although previous studies have established that KLHL20 promotes ULK1 ubiquitination and degradation in certain cellular contexts,11 our data extend this paradigm by positioning miR-150 as an upstream regulator of KLHL20, thus linking miRNA-mediated gene regulation to autophagy control during stem cell fate determination. These findings introduce a new layer of complexity to ULK1 regulation, as its levels are governed not only by nutrient and stress signals but also by lineage-specific transcriptional and posttranscriptional mechanisms.40 Importantly, our study highlights the functional relevance of autophagy beyond its canonical role in cytoplasmic quality control.41 By modulating SOX2 degradation via ULK1, the autophagy pathway directly influences transcription factor availability and thereby shapes cell fate decisions. These findings support emerging evidence that autophagy acts as a key determinant of cell identity and differentiation, particularly during the early stages of embryonic development.15 RAPA exhibited stage-dependent effects, enhancing hematopoietic differentiation when applied at the undifferentiated/early stage but inhibiting it during lineage differentiation (Supplementary Fig. 5i). This likely reflects stage-specific mTOR–autophagy requirements and the fact that RAPA is a potent mTOR inhibitor rather than a selective autophagy activator. Consistent with our early-stage results, mTOR inhibition promotes mesendodermal differentiation and increases hemogenic endothelium and blood progenitor output.42 Conversely, intact mTOR activity is required for progenitor expansion and maturation, and RAPA suppresses proliferation during myeloid/megakaryocytic differentiation,43,44 supporting a model in which early modulation favors hemogenic programs while sustained inhibition limits lineage progression.

To establish the functional relevance of the miR-150–SOX2–autophagy axis in lineage fate determination, we conducted a series of rescue experiments that revealed strong mechanistic closure. Restoration of miR-150 expression using mimics or dual knockdown of KLHL20 and SOX2 effectively reversed the lineage skewing observed in miR-150-deficient hPSCs, rescuing mesodermal and hematopoietic differentiation. Similarly, pharmacological activation of autophagy with RAPA mitigated the neuroectodermal bias induced by the loss of miR-150, suggesting that the autophagic regulation of SOX2 stability is functionally consequential. Induced autophagy can overcome the regulatory constraint imposed by miR-150 during lineage specification. While miR-150 normally maintains autophagic balance, stimuli such as RAPA strongly activate autophagy, thereby bypassing the ability of miR-150 to control and promote hematopoietic fate commitment. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that miR-150 exerts a multilayered and target-divergent regulatory effect, coordinating both SOX2 mRNA stability and SOX2 protein turnover via autophagy. Our data indicate that the loss of miR-150 disrupts autophagy and lysosome-dependent regulation of SOX2, although future canonical flux assays (e.g., LC3-based analysis) are needed to elucidate the affected steps. The convergence of these two regulatory arms—one at the posttranscriptional level and the other through proteostatic control—enables robust, synergistic regulation of lineage fate. This dual mechanism is illustrated in our integrative model (Fig. 6i). Notably, the partial rescue of differentiation defects by either SOX2 knockdown or autophagy activation implies a degree of modular independence; however, their combined perturbation results in more complete phenotypic rescue, indicating potential synergistic crosstalk. We propose that these two regulatory arms may function in a sequential manner: miR-150-mediated repression of SOX2 mRNA may initiate early transcriptomic remodeling, followed by autophagic tuning of residual SOX2 protein to reinforce lineage commitment.

miR-150 has been widely reported as a tumor suppressor, particularly in hematologic malignancies, where reduced miR-150 expression is linked to leukemogenesis and its restoration suppresses tumor growth.45,46 Tumor-suppressive roles have also been described in several solid tumors, including pancreatic and breast cancers,47,48 whereas context-dependent oncogenic effects have been reported in specific cellular settings,49,50 underscoring the need for cautious interpretation. Accordingly, our findings should be viewed from a developmental biology perspective rather than as a therapeutic strategy involving permanent miR-150 modulation. Although miR-150 deficiency alters SOX2 homeostasis and biases lineage specification in hPSCs, these effects also highlight potential tumorigenicity risks associated with sustained miR-150 suppression. Any translational application would therefore require strict temporal and contextual control, which would favor transient, stage-restricted modulation during in vitro differentiation, together with rigorous safety assessments. Targeting downstream effectors, such as SOX2, or autophagy-associated pathways may represent a safer alternative to direct miR-150 manipulation. Although our study elucidates a novel dual mechanism by which miR-150 regulates hPSC fate through the modulation of SOX2 stability and autophagy, several limitations warrant further investigation. Some rescue experiments were designed as within-KO tests and therefore did not fully normalize the data to those of the WT. These data link the loss of miR-150 to altered SOX2 homeostasis during early differentiation, which is consistent with autophagy/lysosome-associated regulation, although additional orthogonal assays are needed to define the precise mechanisms involved. We acknowledge that BVOs are still an in vitro model and cannot fully capture in vivo signaling gradients, niche complexity, or systemic influences. Future studies using lineage-specific organoids or in vivo models (e.g., xenografts or embryos) will be important to define the broader relevance of the miR-150–SOX2–autophagy axis across development. Although SOX2 emerged as a major downstream effector, it is unlikely to be the sole mediator of the regulatory effects of miR-150. Given the broad targeting potential of miRNAs, other lineage-specific regulators or epigenetic modulators may also contribute to the observed phenotypes and should be identified through unbiased transcriptomic or proteomic screening. Future studies should address these gaps by leveraging advanced platforms such as human organoids or nonhuman primate embryo models to evaluate the spatiotemporal relevance of the miR-150–SOX2–autophagy axis during early lineage specification. In addition, exploring the role of miR-150 in directed differentiation protocols—particularly toward mesodermal or hematopoietic lineages—may reveal its potential as a molecular handle to increase lineage precision in regenerative medicine applications. Finally, although our study primarily employed the well-characterized H9 hESC line to dissect the miR-150–KLHL20–ULK1–SOX2 axis, we further validated the core phenotypes and target gene regulation in an independent iPSC line (BMC3). The consistent differentiation bias and SOX2 accumulation observed in both hESCs and iPSCs indicate that this pathway represents a conserved regulatory mechanism in human pluripotency. Nonetheless, given the intrinsic variability among hPSC lines, future studies using a broader panel of lines will be needed to fully assess the generality of this signaling axis.

In this study, we revealed a novel regulatory mechanism by which miR-150 governs pluripotency and lineage specification in hPSCs. Specifically, miR-150 regulates SOX2 expression through the following dual mechanisms: reducing SOX2 mRNA stability and modulating autophagy via the KLHL20–ULK1 pathway. Loss of miR-150 disrupts ULK1-mediated autophagic degradation of SOX2, leading to altered SOX2 protein levels and the skewing of stem cell differentiation toward ectodermal lineages at the expense of mesodermal commitment. These findings identify miR-150 as a critical node that integrates autophagy and stem cell fate decisions, providing new insights into the posttranscriptional regulation of early developmental processes. This study not only broadens our understanding of miRNA-mediated control of stem cell differentiation but also introduces new avenues for refining directed differentiation protocols in regenerative medicine and cell-based therapies.

Materials and methods

Ethics

All procedures involving hPSCs were conducted in accordance with the protocol and ethical guidelines approved by the Public Institutional Bioethics Committee designated by the Ministry of Health and Welfare (MOHW), Republic of Korea (IRB number: P01-202108-41-001).

Cell culture

WA09 (H9) hPSCs obtained from WiCell and BMC3 iPSCs previously established28 were cultured in TeSR™-E8™ medium (STEMCELL Technologies) on Matrigel-coated six-well plates (Corning; diluted 1:30 in KO-DMEM). The cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. For passaging, the cells were gently dissociated using ReLeSR™ (STEMCELL Technologies) and replated in TeSR™-E8™ medium supplemented with the ROCK inhibitor Y-27632 (5 μM; MedChemExpress) to increase cell survival. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with standard TeSR™-E8™ medium without the inhibitor for continued culture. HEK293A cells, obtained from the American Type Culture Collection, were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (P/S; Gibco). The cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere under standard culture conditions.

Statistical analysis

The data are expressed as the mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. For comparisons between two groups, a two-tailed Student’s t test was used, whereas multiple group comparisons were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with appropriate post hoc multiple-comparison tests. P values < 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance. Unless otherwise stated in the figure legends, all experiments were conducted in three independent biological replicates and normalized to the appropriate controls.
