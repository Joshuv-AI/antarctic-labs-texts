Grade three perfect ideals and length four self-dual resolutions
Lorenzo Guerrieri, Tymoteusz Chmiel, Xianglong Ni, Jerzy Weyman

Abstract
. Starting with a grade three perfect ideal I ⊂ R I\subset R , we demonstrate how to produce the a self-dual resolution of length four using the resolution of the original ideal. This process is also reversible. The main case of interest is when the grade three perfect ideal has type two, so the output complex resolves R / J R/J for a grade four Gorenstein ideal J J . This suggests that the structure theory of these two families of ideals should be closely related.



1.
Introduction

Although understanding the structure theory of ideals in complete generality seems hopelessly out of reach, the situation for perfect ideals appears more tractable, and has been marked with a number of important successes in the previous century. Most well-known are the cases of grade two perfect ideals and grade three Gorenstein ideals. The former comes as an easy consequence of the Hilbert-Burch theorem (
[
11
]
and
[
5
]
) on resolutions of cyclic modules with projective dimension two. Explicitly, every grade two perfect ideal is generated by the maximal minors of a
(n-1)\times n
matrix. The structure theory of grade three Gorenstein ideals was determined by Buchsbaum and Eisenbud in
[
4
]
, in which they show that any such ideal is generated by the submaximal pfaffians of a
n\times n
skew matrix, where
n
is odd.

Importantly, in each of the two aforementioned cases, taking a generic
(n-1)\times n
matrix (resp.
n\times n
skew matrix) yields a grade two perfect (resp. grade three Gorenstein) ideal. Thus, the structure theorems identify the generic examples for each family, and consequently they are “optimal” in the sense that there cannot be any further necessary relations among the entries of the (skew) matrix.

When we discuss structure theorems in the following, we refer to theorems of this type—ones that identify a generic example for a family, from which all other examples arise as specializations. With the case of grade two completely settled, as well as the case of grade three Gorenstein, the two next-simplest cases are: (1) grade three perfect ideals of type at least two, and (2) grade four Gorenstein ideals.

Progress in the former direction has primarily been aided by linkage. For example, the structure theory of grade three almost complete intersections was deduced as a consequence of the theorem for Gorenstein ideals in
[
4
]
in this manner. Continuing this, structure theorems for certain grade three perfect ideals directly linked to almost complete intersections were given in
[
1
]
and
[
18
]
. These examples are not exhaustive: even for deviation and type two, there exist ideals not covered by the structure theorem in
[
1
]
, see for instance
[
6
]
. Recently, a representation-theoretic approach was used in
[
8
]
to give structure theorems for all grade three perfect ideals of small type and deviation, but the general story remains largely mysterious.

For grade four Gorenstein ideals,
[
10
]
and
[
20
]
showed under mild hypotheses that any such ideal with at most six generators is a hypersurface section. However, this is not true if one increases the number of generators; examples generated by any odd
n\geq 7
number of elements are given in
[
12
]
, and there are also the well-known determinantal examples of
[
9
]
. An analysis of the resolutions of grade four Gorenstein ideals is given in
[
17
]
, but unfortunately it does not result in a theorem in the style of Buchsbaum-Eisenbud.

At a glance, it is not easy to say which of these two directions ought to exhibit a simpler structure theory. From the perspective of free resolutions, compared to the resolution of a grade three perfect ideal, there are fewer matrices to prescribe for the resolution of a grade four Gorenstein ideal owing to its self-duality (i.e. two matrices instead of three).

On the other hand, there are various constructions which take a grade
c
perfect ideal and produce a grade
c+1
Gorenstein ideal. To name a few:

•

One can take the sum of two geometrically linked ideals, as demonstrated in
[
16
]
and studied further in
[
19
]
.

•

There is the “doubling” construction, based on the existence of a canonical ideal, given in
[
12
, Lemma 3.2]
.

•

There is the “big from small” construction of
[
14
]
, later dubbed unprojection and analyzed further in
[
15
]
.

The existence of these constructions suggests that the structure theory of grade four Gorenstein ideals is at least as complicated as the situation for grade three perfect ideals.

Our contribution in this paper is another construction, distinct from the preceding, which (in a special case) produces a grade four Gorenstein ideal from a grade three perfect ideal of type two. Despite its narrow scope, this construction has a major advantage: it is
reversible
. Consequently, this construction could potentially be used to translate structure theorems from one family of ideals to the other, an idea which has traditionally been enabled by linkage. Informally, it also suggests that these two apparently orthogonal directions have comparable difficulty—unfortunately this difficulty appears to be quite high either way!

The organization of this paper is as follows.We fix notation and give the necessary commutative algebra background in §
2
. After that, we turn to our main construction: in §
3
we demonstrate how to produce a self-dual resolution of length four starting from a grade three perfect ideal, and in §
4
we show how to reverse this procedure. Finally, we conclude with some examples in §
5
, where we also discuss the primary application of this machinery to
[
8
]
in a future work: the classification of all grade four Gorenstein ideals on up to eight generators.

2.
Notation and background

2.1.
The ambient ring

Throughout, all rings considered are commutative and Noetherian. The primary case of interest is when they are furthermore either local or graded. In the latter case, we consider homogeneous ideals and their graded free resolutions.

All modules considered are finitely generated. For an
R
-module
M
, its grade is defined to be

\grade M\coloneqq\inf_{i}\{\Ext^{i}(M,R)\neq 0\}.

If
I\subset R
is an ideal, it is a customary abuse of notation to use
\grade I
to denote
\grade R/I
. By the above definition, the grade of the unit ideal is
\infty
. We say the module
M
is
perfect
if
\pdim_{R}M=\grade M
. This is equivalent to stating that
M
admits a finite free resolution whose dual is also acyclic; the dual then resolves
\Ext^{c}(M,R)
where
c=\grade M
. Again, we say an ideal
I
is perfect if the module
R/I
is.

We say that a grade
c
perfect module
M
is self-dual if
\Ext^{c}(M,R)\cong M
. If furthermore
M\cong R/J
for an ideal
J
, we say that the ideal
J
is
Gorenstein
. Note that if the ambient ring
R
were itself a Gorenstein ring, then this would exactly be the case in which the quotient
R/J
is also.

2.2.
Notation for free resolutions

The
format
of a finite free resolution
\mathbb{F}
is the sequence of ranks
(\rank F_{0},\rank F_{1},\ldots)
.

We fix two integers
p\geq 1
and
q\geq 3
. We will be concerned with resolutions of length three with format
(1,p+2,p+q,q-1)
, and self-dual resolutions of length four with format
(q-2,p+q,2(p+2),p+q,q-2)
. For the former, we will use the following notation for the complex:

\mathbb{A}\colon 0\to A_{3}\xrightarrow{d_{3}}A_{2}\xrightarrow{d_{2}}A_{1}\xrightarrow{d_{1}}A_{0}.

Here
A_{0}=R
,
A_{1}=R^{p+2}
,
A_{2}=R^{p+q}
, and
A_{3}=R^{q-1}
.

We will also deal with self-dual resolutions of length four of the form

\mathbb{B}\colon 0\to B_{0}^{*}\xrightarrow{\delta_{4}=\delta_{1}^{*}}B_{1}^{*}\xrightarrow{\delta_{3}=\delta_{2}^{*}}B_{2}^{*}\cong B_{2}\xrightarrow{\delta_{2}}B_{1}\xrightarrow{\delta_{1}}B_{0}.

Here
B_{0}=R^{q-2}
,
B_{1}=R^{p+q}
, and
B_{2}=H\oplus H^{*}
where
H=R^{p+2}
and the isomorphism
B_{2}^{*}\cong B_{2}
is the evident one.

If
\mathbb{B}
resolves
M
, then
M
is a grade 4 self-dual module. However, it is not clear whether every such module admits an explicitly self-dual resolution of the given form. This is known under the assumptions
M
is cyclic,
1/2\in R
, and either:

•

[
7
]

R=\Bbbk[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
is a graded polynomial ring over a field
\Bbbk
, with all variables of positive degree, and
M
is a graded module, or

•

[
13
]

R
is a complete regular local ring.

2.3.
Verifying acyclicity

We recall the Buchsbaum-Eisenbud acyclicity criterion:

Theorem 2.1

(
[
2
]
)
.

A complex of free
R
-modules

0\to F_{c}\xrightarrow{d_{c}}F_{c-1}\xrightarrow{d_{c-1}}\cdots\xrightarrow{d_{2}}F_{1}\xrightarrow{d_{1}}F_{0}

is exact if and only if, for all
i
from 1 to
c
,

•

\rank d_{i}+\rank d_{i+1}=\rank F_{i}
, and

•

\grade I_{\rank d_{i}}(d_{i})\geq i
.

Let
s\geq 0
be an integer. We say something holds
in grade
s
if it is true over all localizations
R_{\mathfrak{p}}
where
\mathfrak{p}
is a prime with
\grade\mathfrak{p}\leq s
. For example, a relation such as
d^{2}=0
holds if and only if it does in grade zero, i.e. generically.

A complex
\mathbb{F}
is acyclic in grade
s
exactly when the first condition above holds and we have
\grade I_{\rank d_{i}}(d_{i})\geq\min(i,s+1)
. The acyclicity of finite free complexes of length
c
can therefore be checked in grade
c-1
.

3.
From
\mathbb{A}
to
\mathbb{B}

Let

\mathbb{A}\colon 0\to A_{3}\xrightarrow{d_{3}}A_{2}\xrightarrow{d_{2}}A_{1}\xrightarrow{d_{1}}A_{0}\cong R.

be a free resolution over the ring
R
. We assume that its dual
\mathbb{A}^{*}
is also acyclic. If
R/I
is the module resolved by
\mathbb{A}
, this is equivalent to stating that the ideal
I
is either a grade three perfect ideal or the unit ideal; of course the former will be the case of interest. The alternating sum
\sum(-1)^{i}\rank A_{i}
is zero, so the format of
\mathbb{A}
is
(1,p+2,p+q,q-1)
for suitable integers
p
and
q
. For the purposes of the construction, we assume that
p\geq 1
(which is automatic if
\mathbb{A}
is not split) and also that
q\geq 3
.

3.1.
Multiplicative structure on
\mathbb{A}

We fix a differential graded algebra structure on
\mathbb{A}
, c.f.
[
4
]
. This gives us a multiplication
A_{m}\otimes A_{n}\to A_{m+n}
that is graded-commutative and satisfies the Leibniz rule. Since
\mathbb{A}
has length three, this multiplication is also strictly associative, but that will not be important for our purposes.

To be explicit, this multiplication can be chosen as follows. One begins by defining the product of degree one elements by taking any lift in the diagram below:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{3}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{1}}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bigwedge^{2}A_{1}}

Here the diagonal map sends
e_{1}\wedge e_{2}\mapsto d_{1}(e_{1})e_{2}-d_{1}(e_{2})e_{1}
. Then one defines the multiplication
A_{1}\otimes A_{2}\to A_{3}
by lifting in the below diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{3}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{1}\otimes A_{2}}

Here the diagonal map sends
e\otimes f\mapsto d_{1}(e)f+d_{2}(f)e
where the term

is computed using the multiplication
\bigwedge^{2}A_{1}\to A_{2}
already defined.

We observe that any two choices of
\bigwedge^{2}A_{1}\to A_{2}
differ by
d_{3}b
where

is some map
\bigwedge^{2}A_{1}\to A_{3}
.

Example 3.1
.

Let
\mathbb{A}
be the split complex

(3.1)

0\to A_{3}\to A_{3}\oplus M\to R\oplus M\to R.

where

. If

,

,

(

m_{(i)}
(

(

.

A particularly simple choice of multiplication on

, indeed the unique

-equivariant one, is given as follows:

•

The product
r_{(1)}\cdot m_{(1)}
is
m_{(2)}
. All products
m_{(1)}\cdot m^{\prime}_{(1)}

•

The product

. All products

Let

b(d_{2}(f)\wedge e)

3.2.
The construction

Choose a decomposition
A_{3}=C\oplus L
where
C\cong R^{q-2}
and
L\cong R^{1}
. We next describe the differentials of a complex

\mathbb{B}\colon 0\to C\xrightarrow{\delta_{4}}A_{2}\xrightarrow{\delta_{3}}(A_{1}^{*}\otimes L)\oplus A_{1}\xrightarrow{\delta_{3}^{*}}A_{2}^{*}\otimes L\xrightarrow{\delta_{4}^{*}}C^{*}\otimes L

which is self-dual (up to an overall twist).

•

The differential
\delta_{4}
is the composite
C\xhookrightarrow{i}A_{3}\xrightarrow{d_{3}}A_{2}
, i.e. the
(p+q)\times(q-2)
submatrix of
d_{3}
corresponding to
C\subset A_{3}
.

•

Let
\eta\colon A_{3}\to L
be the projection. The differential
\delta_{3}
is then given by

\delta_{3}(f)=(\eta((-)\cdot f),\,d_{2}(f))

for
f\in A_{2}
. Here the notation
\eta((-)\cdot f)
means the map sending
e\in A_{1}
to
\eta(e\cdot f)\in L
, where we view this map as an element of
A_{1}^{*}\otimes L=\Hom(A_{1},L)
.

We write
J=J_{C}
for the ideal
I_{q-2}(\delta_{4})
. Note that this construction is compatible with localization, or more generally with arbitrary base change preserving acyclicity of
\mathbb{A}
.

Remark 3.2
.

If
R
is graded and the differentials of
\mathbb{A}
are homogeneous of degree zero, then one may choose a multiplication on
\mathbb{A}
that is also homogeneous of degree zero with respect to this internal grading. With that choice, the differentials of
\mathbb{B}
will also be homogeneous of degree zero.

Lemma 3.3
.

As defined above,
\mathbb{B}
is a complex.

Proof.

From the remarks in §
2.3
, it suffices to check this in grade zero, i.e. over localizations
R_{\mathfrak{p}}
where
\grade\mathfrak{p}=0
. In particular,
\grade\mathfrak{p}\leq 2
so
\mathbb{A}\otimes R_{\mathfrak{p}}
becomes split exact. Thus we may reduce to the case that
\mathbb{A}
is split to begin with, and the claim follows from Lemma
3.5
.
∎

The preceding lemma is not difficult to prove directly using the Leibniz rule for the multiplication on
\mathbb{A}
, which we leave as a fun exercise for the reader. We offer this more “efficient” proof because we will leverage Lemma
3.5
to prove the next theorem also.

Theorem 3.4
.

Suppose that
\grade J\geq 4
. Then
\mathbb{B}
is an acyclic complex. In particular, either
\grade J=4
or
J=(1)
.

Proof.

Just as above, by §
2.3
it suffices to check
\mathbb{B}\otimes R_{\mathfrak{p}}
is acyclic for primes
\mathfrak{p}
with
\grade\mathfrak{p}\leq 2
, because we already assume that the minors of
\delta_{4}
have sufficient grade. For such a prime
\mathfrak{p}
,
\mathbb{A}\otimes R_{\mathfrak{p}}
is split exact. So we again reduce to the case that
\mathbb{A}
is split and use Lemma
3.5
.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

If
\mathbb{A}
is split exact, then
\mathbb{B}
is also.

Proof.

Writing
\mathbb{A}
in the form (
3.1
), by Example
3.1
there exists
b\colon\bigwedge^{2}A_{1}\to A_{3}
so that the multiplication
A_{1}\otimes A_{2}\to A_{3}
is given as follows:

\displaystyle r_{(1)}\otimes a_{(2)}

\displaystyle\mapsto a_{(3)}

\displaystyle r_{(1)}\otimes m_{(2)}

\displaystyle\mapsto b(m_{(1)}\wedge r_{(1)})

\displaystyle m_{(1)}\otimes a_{(2)}

\displaystyle\mapsto 0

\displaystyle m_{(1)}\otimes m^{\prime}_{(2)}

\displaystyle\mapsto b(m^{\prime}_{(1)}\wedge m_{(1)})

for
a\in A_{3}
,
r\in R
, and
m,m^{\prime}\in M
.

Thus the differential
\delta_{3}
has the block form

\begin{array}[]{cccc}&C&L&M\\[5.0pt]
R&&&\\
M&&&I\\
M^{*}\otimes L&&&*\\
L&&I&*\\
\end{array}

where the block for
M\to M^{*}\otimes L
is alternating. The differential
\delta_{4}
is just the inclusion
C\hookrightarrow C\oplus L\oplus M
, and it is straightforward to see that
\delta_{3}\delta_{4}
and
\delta_{3}^{*}\delta_{3}
are both zero. Furthermore,
I_{p+2}(\delta_{3})=(1)
and
\delta_{4}
is a split inclusion, so we conclude that
\mathbb{B}
is split exact.
∎

3.3.
Existence of a suitable
C\subset F_{3}

In view of Theorem
3.4
, it is natural to ask when there exists a choice of
C
so that
\grade J_{C}\geq 4
so that
\mathbb{B}
is actually a resolution. This is certainly a restriction, e.g. such a
C
obviously cannot exist if
\depth R=3
, but it is only a mild one:

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
R
be a local Noetherian ring with infinite residue field. Suppose that
\mathbb{A}
resolves a grade 3 ideal
I\subset R
. Then there exists a choice of
C
so that
\grade J_{C}=4
if and only if
I
is generically Gorenstein.

Proof.

Note that

I_{q-1}(d_{3})\subseteq J_{C}\subseteq I_{q-2}(d_{3})

where the first ideal equals
I
up to radical and thus has grade 3. The vanishing of
I_{q-2}(d_{3})
cuts out the locus where
\Ext^{3}(R/I,R)
cannot be generated by a single element, or equivalently the non-Gorenstein locus for
I
. Clearly
\grade J_{C}=4
implies
\grade I_{q-2}(d_{3})\geq 4
and thus
I
is generically Gorenstein, establishing the “only if” implication.

On the other hand, suppose that every choice of
C
results in
\grade J_{C}=3
. As
I
is a grade 3 perfect ideal, its minimal primes all have grade 3, and consequently each
J_{C}
has a minimal prime in common with
I
. Using that the residue field is infinite, a pigeonhole principle argument produces
C_{1},\ldots,C_{q-1}
which form a basis modulo
\mathfrak{m}
(as lines in
A_{3}^{*}
) and so that
J_{C_{i}}
all share the same common minimal prime with
I
. But then
\sum_{i}J_{C_{i}}=I_{q-2}(d_{3})
would also have grade 3, showing that
I
is not generically Gorenstein.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

Regardless of whether
\grade J=4
, the output complex
\mathbb{B}
will always be split exact in grade 2 by Lemma
3.5
. See also Remark
4.12
.

4.
From
\mathbb{B}
to
\mathbb{A}

We now discuss an inverse to the construction given in the preceding section. This is more subtle, and we will need to impose additional hypotheses on the ring
R
. For this section we will assume that
R
is a unique factorization domain containing

. It would be interesting to see if a different approach could eliminate the need for this hypothesis.

Throughout, we fix integers
p\geq 1
and
q\geq 3
. Let
C=R^{q-2}
,

, and

.
First, suppose we have a complex of the form

Let

be the part of
\delta_{3}

and let

be the part of

. Then we have that

given by the evident pairing of the two summands, then for

denotes the evaluation pairing

. Thus if

then the image of

is a (totally) isotropic subspace of

.

Now suppose we have a self-dual resolution

and we view the middle module as a (split) quadratic space with the form

. The collection of isotropic summands

corresponds to the

which we will abbreviate as

. Localizing at

determines a

.

For slightly clearer indexing, let

. We will henceforth assume that the isotropic summand

represents a point in the

. This is not a serious restriction: if

is a basis for

4.1.
Spinor structure on
\mathbb{B}

A spinor structure on
\mathbb{B}
will play the role that the DGA structure did in §

[

for details, as we will only give a brief summary here. The group

is a double cover of

V_{\mathrm{even}}=\bigwedge^{\mathrm{even}}H,\quad V_{\mathrm{odd}}=\bigwedge^{\mathrm{odd}}H.

By our assumptions regarding
H^{*}
and the image of

\OG\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}(V_{\mathrm{odd}})

\OG^{\prime}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}(V_{\mathrm{even}})

.

corresponds to

are given by the

Lemma 4.5
.

Suppose that

is local. Let

, and let

that does not belong to the same orbit as

(1)

There exists a basis
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
of
H

of

\begin{array}[]{ccc}e_{1}^{\prime}&0&0\\

\Span(e_{2}^{\prime},\ldots,e_{n}^{\prime})&0&I\\
\end{array}

(2)

\lambda(H^{*})=(1)
.

(3)

, then

Proof.

Let us first expand on the condition

. It is equivalent to say that there exists a basis

of
H
(and corresponding dual basis of
H^{*}
) so that
\lambda(e_{1}^{\prime})
is a unit, and
\lambda(e_{i}^{\prime})=0
for all

. Now consider

. We have that

\bigwedge^{\mathrm{odd}}H^{*}=\bigwedge^{\mathrm{even}}H^{\prime*}

on the left correspond to

on the right. The coordinate
\lambda(1)
being a unit says that

belongs to the big open cell consisting of isotropic summands that are given by the graph of a skew matrix

. The conditions

and

of

(and corresponding dual basis of

) so that

is spanned by the columns of the matrix

\Span(e_{2},\ldots,e_{n})&-b\mathbf{u}^{\top}&X\\

\Span(e_{2}^{\prime},\ldots,e_{n}^{\prime})&0&I\\

Remark 4.9

4.3.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
R
be a local Noetherian ring with infinite residue field, and suppose we have a map

\lambda\colon V_{\mathrm{odd}}^{*}=H^{*}\oplus\bigwedge^{3}H^{*}\oplus\cdots\to R

whose image is an ideal of grade
\geq 3
. Then, precomposing
\lambda
by an appropriate element of the spin group acting on
V_{\mathrm{odd}}^{*}
, we can arrange for
\grade\lambda(H^{*})\geq 3
.

Proof.

Let
J_{k}
denote the image of
\lambda
restricted to the first
k
components
H^{*}\oplus\cdots\oplus\bigwedge^{2k-1}H^{*}
. Suppose that
\grade J_{1}=c<3
, and let
P_{1},\ldots,P_{s}
be the grade
c
associated primes of
J_{1}
. We will write
\lambda_{1,\ldots,2k-1}
to mean the coordinate of
\lambda
on
e^{\prime}_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e^{\prime}_{2k-1}
, and similarly for other indices.

To achieve our goal, we will use the action of certain one-parameter subgroups of the spin group, namely those corresponding to the roots

•

\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j}
(
i\neq j
)

•

\epsilon_{i}+\epsilon_{j}
(
i\neq j
).

The one-parameter subgroups of the first kind generate
\SL(H)
. The action of
\SL(H)
on the vector
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
is just the standard representation. Since the residue field is infinite, it is well-known that we can arrange (using this action) so that the ideal generated by any set of
c
coordinates
\lambda_{i}
has grade
c
, with grade
c
associated primes
P_{1},\ldots,P_{s}
.

Let
m
be the smallest value such that there is a coordinate on
\bigwedge^{2m-1}H^{*}
that does not belong to
P_{i}
for some
i
. Such an
m
exists because
\grade J_{k}\geq 3>c
for
k
sufficiently large. Without loss of generality, say this coordinate is
\lambda_{1,\ldots,2m-1}
.

Acting on
\lambda
by the one-parameter subgroup associated to
\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}
gives
\lambda^{\prime}
where
\lambda^{\prime}_{1}=\lambda_{1}
,
\lambda^{\prime}_{2}=\lambda_{2}
, and

(4.2)

\lambda^{\prime}_{3,4,\ldots,2m-1}=\lambda_{3,4,\ldots,2m-1}+\alpha\lambda_{1,\ldots,2m-1}

where
\alpha
denotes the parameter. We will not need to consider what happens to the remaining coordinates.

We set
\alpha=1
, so that
\lambda^{\prime}_{3,4,\ldots,2m-1}\notin P_{i}
. Since
c<3
, at least
c
singletons
\lambda_{i}
are unchanged, and thus the overall effect is one of the following:

•

The set of grade
c
primes associated to
J_{1}
shrinks. If the set disappears entirely, then
c
increases.

•

The set of grade
c
primes associated to
J_{1}
stays the same, and
m
decreases by at least 1 in view of (
4.2
).

As we repeatedly apply this procedure, neither of these two possibilities can occur indefinitely, so this process must eventually terminate, which can only occur if
c\geq 3
.
∎

Proposition 4.11

Let

be a local Noetherian ring with infinite residue field, with

\mathbb{B}

This follows immediately by applying the preceding lemma in the case that

It is interesting to note that, in the proof of Lemma

5.
Examples

In the preceding sections, we have demonstrated the constructions in the simplest possible case, namely when the input is a split exact complex. This was sufficient to prove that the constructions are valid in general. In a future paper, we intend to thoroughly investigate the case when the input is a resolution of an ideal in the linkage class of a complete intersection (licci), with the aim of better understanding the relationship between grade 3 licci ideals of type 2 and grade 4 licci Gorenstein ideals.
