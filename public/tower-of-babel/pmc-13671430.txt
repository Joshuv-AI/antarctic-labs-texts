Targeting tumor-selective membrane-associated PCNA enables selective antibody–drug conjugate therapy
Authors: Muhammad Abu Ahmad, Nir Miriam Ungar-Malma, Alon Naumchik, Noy Machluf-Katz, Olga Radinsky, Sarah Adi Eisdorfer, Abedallh Gara, Yosef Istoyler, Irit Alon, Daniel Sevilla-Sanchez, Kerry S. Campbell, Bar Kaufman, Moshe Elkabets, Angel Porgador
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMembrane-associated proliferating cell nuclear antigen (mPCNA) is an attractive tumor-selective antigen for antibody–drug conjugate (ADC) development. We generated a chimeric IgG1 ADC, RC14-MMAE, to exploit three key properties of mPCNA that support its use as an efficient delivery platform. First, flow-cytometric and imaging analyses show abundant mPCNA surface expression on multiple tumor cell lines and in JIMT1 xenografts, providing accessible epitopes for binding. Second, comparative profiling against non-malignant counterparts and normal mouse tissues reveals minimal or undetectable mPCNA at the plasma membrane of healthy cells, indicating a sharp tumor–normal differential expected to limit off-tumor targeting and improve the therapeutic window. Third, live-cell internalization assays demonstrate that RC14 rapidly accumulates in LAMP1-positive lysosomes after engaging mPCNA, confirming efficient trafficking along the endolysosomal pathway required for protease-mediated cleavage of the valine–citrulline linker and release of monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE). Consistent with this target profile, RC14-MMAE exhibits potent antigen-dependent cytotoxicity in mPCNA-positive tumor models in NSG mice, favorable pharmacokinetics, sustained payload retention in serum, robust in vivo antitumor activity with limited overt toxicity, and a significantly increased fraction of F4/80⁺ tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells within dissociated tumors. These findings establish mPCNA as a functionally validated, tumor-selective internalizing target and support RC14-MMAE as a first-in-class mPCNA-directed ADC with broad translational potential.

Background

Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) integrate the target specificity of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) with the potent cytotoxicity of small-molecule drugs and have transformed the treatment landscape for several malignancies. 1-4 Among available payloads, MMAE has proven particularly effective, yet its clinical utility in unconjugated form is restricted by rapid systemic exposure and a steep dose–toxicity relationship leading to neuropathy, neutropenia, and other dose-limiting adverse events. 5-7 To overcome these limitations, MMAE is commonly coupled to antibodies via a valine-citrulline (Vc) dipeptide linker that remains stable in circulation but is cleaved by lysosomal proteases following internalization, thereby releasing active drug inside target cells. 4 , 8-10

Membrane-associated proliferating cell nuclear antigen (mPCNA) has recently emerged as a tumor-selective antigen that is aberrantly displayed on the surface of malignant cells but is largely absent from the plasma membranes of normal tissues. 11-13 mPCNA not only marks transformed cells but also participates in oncogenic signaling pathways, and additionally functions as an inhibitory ligand for the natural cytotoxicity receptor NKp44, thereby attenuating NK-cell effector responses, 14 making it an attractive target for therapeutic intervention. 15 , 16 However, most prior strategies have focused on nuclear PCNA and required cell-membrane disruption, limiting selectivity and increasing off-target toxicity; to date, no ADCs specifically directed against mPCNA have been described. 9 , 17-19 This differential localization therefore provides an attractive opportunity to direct ADCs specifically to cancer cells while sparing healthy counterparts. 14 , 20-22

The present study addresses this unmet need by developing and characterizing RC14, a chimeric human IgG1 mAb that recognizes an extracellular epitope of mPCNA. 14 , 23 RC14 binds selectively to mPCNA on a broad spectrum of cancer cell lines—including breast (JIMT1), prostate (PC-3), glioma (U251), and esophageal (KYSE-450)—with negligible binding to non-malignant counterparts. 11 , 12 , 14 By conjugating RC14 to a Vc-linked MMAE payload (RC14-MMAE), we aimed to exploit mPCNA as a shared, surface-accessible vulnerability across diverse solid tumors, combining blockade of mPCNA-associated signaling with precise intratumoral cytotoxicity and minimal systemic exposure to free drug. 24 The use of NSG mice bearing human tumors enables evaluation of ADC activity in the absence of a meaningful contribution from endogenous T, B, and functional NK cells. 25 Nevertheless, residual murine myeloid cells, particularly macrophages, may exert opposing effects on ADC efficacy by either limiting drug exposure or contributing to local antitumor activity. 26

In this manuscript, we systematically evaluate RC14-MMAE at the molecular, cellular, and in vivo levels. We define its structural properties and drug-to-antibody ratio (DAR), assess antigen binding and internalization, and examine cytotoxic and anti-adhesive effects across a panel of mPCNA-positive tumor cell lines. We further characterize the pharmacokinetics, serum stability, and linker behavior of RC14-MMAE in mice, and test its therapeutic efficacy, impact on proliferation, and ability to prevent metastasis in a JIMT1 xenograft model. Collectively, these studies establish mPCNA as a viable ADC target, validate RC14 as a versatile delivery vehicle for MMAE, and provide a preclinical foundation for the future development of mPCNA-directed ADCs in solid tumors.

Materials and methods

RC14 conjugation with VcMMAE

RC14 was conjugated to VcMMAE using a controlled reduction and coupling procedure. Antibody aliquots were thawed on ice to 6.6 mg/mL in PBS. Buffer was exchanged by centrifugation through a 100 kDa Amicon Ultra‑0.5 mL (Millipore, UFC510096) using Buffer I (0.15 M sodium acetate (Frutarum, 5553310), 10 mM EDTA (Thermo Scientific, 147855000), 100 mM NaCl (Bio‑Lab, 1236821), pH 4.5) at 14,000 × g for 10 min at 25 °C. TCEP·HCl was freshly dissolved in PBS (0.1 g in 10 mL, pH 2.6) to 35 mM, and RC14 (33.3 μM in Buffer I) was mixed with TCEP at a 1:100 molar ratio and incubated for 90 min at 37 °C with gentle agitation (300 rpm). The reduced antibody was buffer-exchanged into Buffer II (10 mM EDTA in PBS, pH 7.25) using a 10 kDa Amicon Ultra-0.5 mL device (Millipore, UFC501096), centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 10 min at 25 °C; the washing step with Buffer II was repeated five times to ensure complete buffer exchange, and the pH was confirmed to be ~7.0. For conjugation, VcMMAE (MCE, HY‑15575) was added at 1:5 or 1:10 drug:antibody molar ratios and incubated for 120 min at room temperature with agitation (300 rpm). The reaction mixture was purified by centrifugation through 10 kDa filters with PBS to remove unreacted components, using a protocol adapted from previously described methods. 22 , 27 , 28

Cell lines and culture conditions

JIMT1 (Cellosaurus CVCL_2077) was cultured as previously described. PC‑3 (ATCC CRL‑1435) and U251 (U‑251 MG; Cytion) were grown in DMEM (Gibco, 41965‑039), and KYSE‑450 (Cellosaurus CVCL_1353) was grown in RPMI‑1640 (Gibco, 21875‑034). All media contained 10% FBS (Gibco, A5256801), 1% L‑glutamine (Gibco, 25030‑024), penicillin‑streptomycin (Gibco, 15140‑122), non‑essential amino acids (Gibco, 11140‑035), sodium pyruvate (Gibco, 11360‑039), and HEPES (Gibco, 15630‑056).

Hydrophobic interaction chromatography and DAR determination of RC14-VcMMAE

Hydrophobic interaction chromatography (HIC) of the ADC was performed on a Waters 2695 HPLC system (Alliance, Milford, MA, USA) with a Sepax Proteomix HIC Butyl‑NP5 column (4.6 × 100 mm, 431NP5‑4610, Sepax). Samples (12 μg) were prepared in PBS with 1 M ammonium sulfate and loaded onto a column equilibrated with 75% buffer A (2 M ammonium sulfate in 25 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7.0) and 25% buffer B (25 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7.0) at 0.8 mL/min. Elution used a linear gradient over 18 min to 75% buffer B with 25% isopropanol, and chromatograms were recorded at 214, 248, and 280 nm; baseline correction was performed by subtraction of a PBS blank using Empower Pro software. Drug‑to‑antibody ratio (DAR) was determined by UV–visible spectrophotometry (DS‑11, DeNovix), and average DAR was calculated according to Hamblett et al. 8

Flow cytometry

Cells were detached with Versene (Gibco, 15040-033), washed twice with PBS, and incubated in complete DMEM for 1 h at 37 °C. They were then counted, washed, fixed with 0.05% sodium azide in 2% FBS/PBS, and incubated for 1 h on ice with RC14 or human IgG1 control antibodies. After staining with R‑phycoerythrin (PE)–conjugated goat anti‑human IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 109-116-088), cells were acquired on a CytoFLEX S flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter), and data were analyzed using CytExpert software.

ELISA assay for RC14-MMAE antigen binding and pharmacokinetics

Plasma levels of RC14 were quantified by indirect ELISA using recombinant human PCNA as the capture antigen. Ninety‑six‑well plates were coated overnight at 4 °C with PCNA (2 μg/mL in PBS, 70 μL/well) and blocked for 1.5 h at 37 °C with 1% BSA in PBS containing 0.05% Tween‑20 (Fisher Scientific, AAJ20605AP). Plasma (human plasma, CAT:H4522, Sigma-Aldrich) was subjected to a single freeze–thaw cycle, diluted 1:2500 in PBS, added in duplicate (70 μL/well), and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. A standard curve was prepared from serial dilutions of purified RC14 (30–50 ng/mL, 11–12 dilutions plus zero). Bound RC14 was detected with an HRP-conjugated anti-human IgG secondary antibody with minimal cross-reactivity to mouse immunoglobulins, whereas conjugated payload was detected with a rabbit anti-MMAE primary antibody (MedChemExpress, HY-P81056) followed by an HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody. (1:1000 in PBS‑T, 70 μL/well, 30 min at 37 °C), followed by TMB substrate (Surmodics, TMBW‑1000‑01; 70 μL/well). Absorbance at 650 nm was measured with a microplate reader, and RC14 concentrations were interpolated from the standard curve.

For pharmacokinetics, 8-week-old NSG mice received single intraperitoneal injections of mAb14 at 1, 5, or 10 mg/kg (n = 5 per group); all the data are included in the experiment. Blood was collected into EDTA‑coated vials at 45 min, 1.5 h, 3 h, 6 h, 12 h, 24 h, 36 h, 48 h, and 168 h (7 d) after dosing, with injection time defined as 0 min. Plasma was isolated and stored at −80 °C until ELISA analysis.

Immunofluorescence and internalization

JIMT1 cells were harvested with Versene, washed, and seeded at 3 × 104 cells per well in 8‑well μ‑slides (ibidi, 80806). Cells were incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO₂ with 2 μg/mL RC14 for 10 min, 15 min, 1 h, or 2 h, with IgG1 isotype (2 h) and anti‑CD107a (LAMP‑1, BioLegend, 328606; 0.4 μL/well) as controls in complete DMEM. After washing, cells were fixed with 4% PFA (Thermo Fisher, 47340), permeabilized with 0.05% Triton X‑100 (Sigma‑Aldrich, X100RS), and stained with APC‑anti‑human IgG1 (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 109‑135‑098) or PE‑anti‑mouse IgG1κ (BioLegend, 400114; 10 μg/mL in 2% FBS/PBS) and DAPI (BioLegend, 422801; 0.1 μg/mL in PBS). Samples were imaged on a ZEISS super‑resolution microscope, and image stacks were analyzed in ImageJ/Fiji using a custom 3D pipeline in which nuclei were segmented with a Cellpose‑derived mask, particle channels were smoothed and thresholded to generate 3D masks, nuclear voxels were excluded, and particle volumes and extra‑nuclear co‑localization (intersection masks) were quantified with the 3D ROI Manager; composite z‑slice montages and movies were generated for quality control. 29-31

In vivo efficacy of RC14-MMAE in JIMT1 xenografts

8-week-old NOD scid gamma (NSG) mice were housed under specific pathogen‑free conditions at Ben‑Gurion University of the Negev in accordance with Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee approval (IL56‑06‑2023E). Each mouse was subcutaneously implanted in the flank with 1 × 106 JIMT1 breast cancer cells. Twelve days later, mice were randomly assigned to treatment groups and received intraperitoneal injections of RC14-MMAE at 1, 5, or 10 mg/kg, human IgG1 at 10 mg/kg, or PBS as a control (n = 6 per group). For all procedures, mice were anesthetized by inhalation of oxygen supplemented with 3% isoflurane (Piramal, N0034A12B). Control animals received PBS (Sartorius, 02‑023‑1A) or human IgG1 (10 mg/kg, InVivoMab, BE0297) three times over two weeks. Tumor growth was monitored by serial caliper measurements every few days. All mice were euthanized on day 23 post-implantation using a gradual-fill carbon dioxide (CO₂) inhalation method, with CO₂ introduced into the chamber at a flow rate of 3–7 L/min (approximately 20%–30% chamber volume per minute) to minimize distress. Mice were monitored continuously until cessation of respiration. Death was confirmed by a secondary physical method (cervical dislocation) to ensure euthanasia was complete. Tumors were then immediately harvested for further analysis. All the data are included in the experiment. Animals were randomly assigned to treatment groups, and cages from different groups were interleaved on the same racks to avoid location bias. Injections and tumor measurements were performed at similar times of day on scheduled experiment days following a fixed order that did not depend on treatment group.

Ki67 immunohistochemistry staining

The staining was performed on formalin‑fixed, paraffin‑embedded tumor tissues. JIMT1 tumors were fixed in neutral buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4–5 μm, then deparaffinized and rehydrated through graded alcohols. Heat‑induced epitope retrieval was performed, endogenous peroxidase was quenched, and non‑specific binding was blocked with horse serum (Vector Laboratories, PK‑7200). Sections were incubated with rabbit anti‑Ki67 (Abcam, ab16667; 1:200), followed by a biotinylated anti‑rabbit secondary antibody and DAB chromogenic detection (Vector Laboratories, SK‑4100), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, mounted, and digitally analyzed in QuPath v0.2.3, with nuclear staining thresholds validated against matched negative controls to quantify Ki67‑positive tumor cells.

Results

Structural and functional analysis of RC14 following VcMMAE conjugation

We generated a novel ADC by conjugating RC14, an antibody that recognizes mPCNA, to the cytotoxic payload VcMMAE, as outlined in Figure 1A. Briefly, RC14 was reduced with TCEP·HCl to selectively open interchain disulfide bonds and expose reactive thiols, which were then reacted with the maleimide group of VcMMAE to form stable thioether linkages. The reaction mixture was subsequently purified to remove excess TCEP·HCl, unbound VcMMAE, and antibody fragments, yielding an RC14‑VcMMAE preparation enriched for intact antibody molecules.

Generation and characterization of RC14-VcMMAE. (A) Schematic illustration of the RC14-VcMMAE conjugation workflow, including partial reduction of interchain disulfide bonds with TCEP, removal of excess reductant, and coupling of the VcMMAE linker–payload to the reduced cysteines, followed by purification of the resulting antibody–drug conjugate (ADC). (B) HIC profiles of unconjugated RC14 (black trace) and RC14-MMAE (blue trace); both antibodies were analyzed at 0.8 mg/mL, and chromatograms were normalized to background signals from buffer, upstream wash fractions, and DMSO controls, revealing the expected shift to more hydrophobic species after VcMMAE conjugation. (C) Antigen-binding activity of RC14 before and after VcMMAE conjugation assessed by ELISA on mPCNA-coated plates; unconjugated RC14, unpurified RC14-MMAE, purified RC14-MMAE, and the RC14-MMAE flow-through fraction were titrated and dose–response curves were fitted using a four-parameter nonlinear regression model in GraphPad Prism v10, showing reduced maximal binding and modestly altered EC₅₀ values for the conjugated species relative to native RC14. (D) Intact-mass spectrometry analysis of RC14 (blue trace) and RC14-MMAE (black trace); spectra are shown over Z = +2 and Z = +1 charge states, with the unconjugated antibody giving a dominant peak at ~150,954 Da and the conjugated sample displaying additional higher-mass peaks corresponding to increasing numbers of VcMMAE payloads, confirming successful conjugation and a heterogeneous but well-defined drug-to-antibody ratio.

To confirm payload loading and the expected increase in hydrophobicity, RC14 and RC14‑MMAE were analyzed by hydrophobic interaction chromatography (Figure 1B). Whereas unconjugated RC14 showed a single, earlier‑eluting peak, RC14‑MMAE displayed multiple later‑eluting peaks, consistent with more hydrophobic species whose retention correlated with drug load. These chromatograms provide qualitative evidence of successful conjugation and heterogeneous drug loading on RC14.

Following conjugation, we assessed whether RC14 retained affinity for mPCNA. Antigen‑binding activity of RC14 before and after VcMMAE loading was compared by ELISA dose–response curves fitted with a four‑parameter logistic model (Figure 1C). Unconjugated RC14 showed the highest maximal OD and an EC₅₀ of ~0.157 ng/mL, whereas RC14‑MMAE before and after column purification displayed right‑shifted curves with lower plateaus and EC₅₀ values of ~0.63 and ~0.44 ng/mL, respectively. These data indicate that conjugation reduces overall binding capacity and modestly alters apparent potency, consistent with a fraction of molecules losing antigen‑binding competence, while the remaining species retain near‑native affinity for mPCNA. The RC14‑MMAE flow‑through fraction showed very weak binding and an unstable EC₅₀, indicating enrichment for species with severely impaired recognition.

Intact mass spectrometry was then used to compare RC14 and RC14–VcMMAE (Figure 1D). Unconjugated RC14 exhibited major peaks at ~150,954 Da (Z = +1) and ~75,493 Da (Z = +2), corresponding to the intact IgG1 in different charge states. After TCEP reduction and VcMMAE conjugation, additional higher‑mass peaks appeared at ~153,439 Da (Z = +1) and ~76,755 and ~78,098 Da (Z = +2), consistent with RC14 molecules bearing increasing numbers of payloads, while a residual ~150,954 Da peak indicated remaining unconjugated antibody. Together with the HIC and ELISA data, these intact‑mass profiles confirm successful generation of a structurally intact RC14–VcMMAE ADC with a heterogeneous but well‑defined payload distribution and preservation of the antibody backbone. Actual DAR species and an average DAR of 3.77 were determined independently by UV–Vis analysis (Supplementary Table 1), using the method of Hamblett et al. 8

RC14-VcMMAE selectively recognizes membranal PCNA and induces potent cytotoxicity across diverse tumor cell lines

mPCNA surface expression was assessed by flow cytometry in JIMT1, KYSE‑450, U251, and PC‑3 cells (Figure 2A). RC14 staining (blue histograms) shifted rightward relative to isotype controls in all lines, indicating detectable surface mPCNA, with highest intensity in PC‑3 and KYSE‑450 and lower, but clear, signal in JIMT1 and U251.

Dose‑dependent cytotoxicity was then measured for free MMAE (Figure 2B) and RC14‑MMAE (Figure 2C) over 72 h. RC14‑MMAE was tested from 8.3 nM (1.25 μg/mL) to 133.4 nM (20 μg/mL), and free MMAE at 251 nM and 503 nM, matching the MMAE content of 10 and 20 μg/mL IgG assuming a DAR of 3.77. PC‑3 was most sensitive, with a steep loss of viability at the lowest ADC doses and effects approaching free MMAE at higher concentrations. JIMT1 showed a graded, concentration‑dependent response, while U251 and KYSE‑450 exhibited intermediate sensitivity. At equivalent MMAE levels, RC14‑MMAE consistently produced greater loss of viability than free MMAE in all lines, supporting the benefit of antibody‑mediated delivery.

Crystal‑violet adhesion assays paralleled the lysis data (Figures 2D–F). Unconjugated RC14 at 66.7 nM had minimal effect on adhesion, whereas 251 nM free MMAE reduced adherent surface in all lines. RC14‑MMAE at 0, 8.3, 16.7, 33.3, and 66.7 nM caused a clear, dose‑dependent decrease in adherent area, though the absolute values were slightly higher than in lysis assays because dying cells can remain attached. In JIMT1, crystal‑violet images showed near‑complete clearing only with RC14‑MMAE (1.25–10 μg/mL), not with free MMAE.

A 72‑h lysis assay with 0–30 μg/mL RC14‑MMAE (Figure 2G) yielded distinct LC₅₀ values: 0.4637 μg/mL for PC‑3, 1.847 μg/mL for U251, and 10.15 and 11.72 μg/mL for JIMT1 and KYSE‑450, respectively. Because JIMT1 showed a consistently graded, dose‑dependent response across Figure 2A–G, we selected this line as the primary model for subsequent experiments to dissect RC14-MMAE dose–response relationships without excessive “all-or-none” cytotoxicity at minimal concentrations.

Pharmacokinetics, internalization, and serum stability of the RC14-VcMMAE antibody-drug conjugate

To characterize the in vivo stability and pharmacokinetics of RC14-MMAE in NSG mice, we performed a single-dose intraperitoneal administration followed by serial blood sampling over 48 h (Figure 3A). Mice were bled at baseline (0 h) and at 0.75, 1.5, 3, 6, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h post-dose; plasma RC14-MMAE concentrations were quantified by ELISA using PCNA-coated plates and interpolated from a standard curve of purified RC14-MMAE. Plasma pharmacokinetics were evaluated after single doses of 1, 5, or 10 mg/kg (Figure 3B). The average terminal half-life (t½) increased with dose, from ~7.8 h at 1 mg/kg to 37.7 h at 5 mg/kg and 58.6 h at 10 mg/kg, while Tmax occurred between 6–8 h for all groups, indicating relatively rapid absorption. Peak plasma concentrations (Cmax) rose with dose (≈44, 88, and 91 μg/L for 1, 5, and 10 mg/kg, respectively), and AUC₀–∞ increased from ~881 to 5116 and 7518 μg·h/L, consistent with dose-dependent systemic exposure; dose-normalized AUC values suggested more-than-proportional exposure at higher doses, reflecting prolonged circulation of RC14-MMAE as the dose increases. Together, these PK parameters demonstrate that RC14-MMAE achieves sustained plasma levels, particularly at 5 and 10 mg/kg, supporting effective in vivo target engagement over the first 1–2 d after administration (complete PK parameters are summarized in Supplementary Table 2A and B).

To confirm that internalized RC14 reaches lysosomal compartments, we quantified co-localization of RC14–mPCNA with the lysosomal marker LAMP-1 in JIMT1 cells (Figure 3C). Fluorescence intensity after 10–15 min of incubation with RC14 remained comparable to secondary-only and isotype controls, indicating predominantly surface-bound antibody with limited overlap with LAMP-1–positive vesicles (representative images for all groups are shown in Supplementary Figure 1). By 1–2 h, RC14 fluorescence increased markedly and showed a significant rise in LAMP-1 co-localization, consistent with efficient trafficking of the RC14–mPCNA complex into the endo-lysosomal pathway, a prerequisite for Val-Cit linker cleavage, MMAE release, and subsequent microtubule disruption and tumor-cell killing.

To assess the biochemical stability of the RC14-VcMMAE conjugate in human plasma, RC14-MMAE was incubated with filtered plasma from healthy donors at 37 °C for up to 168 h (Figure 3D). At the indicated time points, samples were passed through 30-kDa cut-off filters to remove low-molecular-weight components, leaving intact or partially loaded RC14-MMAE in the high-molecular-weight fraction, which was then quantified by ELISA detecting both RC14 and MMAE to report directly on payload retention. We next asked whether RC14-MMAE retains cytotoxic activity after prolonged serum incubation and whether released MMAE species gain measurable lytic activity (Figure 3E). RC14-MMAE was incubated in serum for 0, 24, 48, or 168 h, fractionated by 30-kDa filtration into a high-molecular-weight supernatant (enriched for intact or partially loaded RC14-MMAE) and a low-molecular-weight flow-through (containing free MMAE and other small molecules), and each fraction was applied to JIMT1 cells for a 72-h lysis assay. Across all time points, the supernatant fraction (gray bars) produced a marked reduction in viability to ~45%–65%, demonstrating that RC14-MMAE remains potently cytotoxic even after up to 168 h of serum incubation, whereas the flow-through fractions (blue bars) had little or no effect on viability, which remained close to 100%. These findings indicate that the cytotoxic activity after serum incubation is largely carried by high-molecular-weight RC14-MMAE, while any cleaved, low-molecular-weight MMAE present in the flow-through is functionally negligible at the tested dilutions.

Stability assays in human serum showed that the RC14 antibody backbone remained largely intact over 7 d, whereas the MMAE payload gradually dissociated from the conjugate (Figure 3F). Total RC14 decreased only modestly from ~100% at time 0 to ~90% at 168 h, indicating minimal antibody degradation, while the fraction of MMAE still attached to RC14 declined more substantially from ~100% to ~60% over the same period, with intermediate values of ~90%–95% at 24–48 h. Thus, RC14 itself is highly stable in human serum, whereas the VcMMAE linker–payload undergoes slow deconjugation over several days, providing a balance between in vivo stability and eventual drug release. Integrating the PK, stability, and functional lysis data indicates that RC14-MMAE delivers MMAE primarily within the first 1–2 d after dosing: circulating conjugate levels fall markedly over 48 h, particularly at 1 mg/kg, while payload attachment and ADC-mediated cytotoxicity remain high through 24–4 h and decline only later to ~60% payload retention by 168 h. Together, these findings support a pharmacological profile in which RC14-MMAE persists in the circulation long enough to reach tumors and release MMAE intracellularly, but does not maintain prolonged systemic exposure to free MMAE.

Dose‑dependent tumor regression and metastasis prevention by RC14-MMAE in NSG mice

The in vivo antitumor activity of RC14-MMAE was assessed in an NSG mouse xenograft model of JIMT1 breast cancer over a 23‑d experiment (Figure 4A). JIMT1 cells were implanted subcutaneously into both flanks on day 0, and tumors were allowed to establish for 12 d before treatment initiation. On day 12, mice were randomized into five groups: three treatment groups receiving RC14-MMAE at escalating doses and two control groups receiving either PBS or unconjugated RC14. All agents were administered intraperitoneally on days 12, 16, and 19, and tumor volumes were measured at each dosing time point and on intervening days until study termination on day 23, enabling assessment of both dose dependence and target specificity.

(C) Representative Ki67 immunohistochemistry images from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded JIMT1 tumors collected on day 23. Sections (4–5 μm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, subjected to heat-induced epitope retrieval, blocked with horse serum, and stained with rabbit anti-Ki67 (Abcam, ab16667; 1:200), followed by biotinylated anti-rabbit secondary antibody and DAB chromogenic detection, then counterstained with hematoxylin. (D) Quantification of Ki67-positive tumor cell nuclei per treatment group using QuPath v0.2.3. Nuclear detection thresholds were set and validated on matched negative controls, and Ki67-positive fractions were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with post-hoc multiple-comparison testing (**p < 0.0001, *p < 0.001, ns not significant). (E) Percentage of mice with visible lung metastases at day 23, determined by macroscopic scoring of lungs collected at necropsy from each treatment group. (F) Body-weight measurements for each group over the course of the experiment, recorded at the same time points as tumor volume measurements to monitor treatment-related toxicity.

RC14-MMAE produced a clear, dose‑dependent inhibition of JIMT1 tumor growth in NSG mice compared with both PBS and unconjugated RC14 controls (Figure 4B). Treatment with 5 and 10 mg/kg not only halted further expansion but reduced mean tumor volumes to approximately the levels measured around day 7 (≈35 mm3), which were lower than the volumes at day 12 when dosing began, indicating genuine tumor shrinkage rather than mere growth arrest. In contrast, tumors in the 1 mg/kg group grew more slowly than in control animals but continued to increase in size. Notably, after day 19, the mean tumor volume in the 10 mg/kg group appeared slightly higher and not statistically different from the 5 mg/kg group—consistent with extensive intratumoral necrosis and fluid accumulation at the highest dose rather than true residual viable tumor mass, as confirmed by macroscopic inspection of the extracted tumors. Overall, these data indicate that RC14-MMAE induces robust tumor regression at 5 and 10 mg/kg, with both doses clearly outperforming controls despite the confounding effect of necrotic swelling at the maximal dose.

Representative Ki67 immunohistochemistry in JIMT1 xenografts shows dense nuclear staining in controls and progressive loss of Ki67 positivity with increasing ADC dose (Figure 4C). Tumors from vehicle‑treated and unconjugated RC14 groups exhibit dense, widespread Ki67 positivity, consistent with highly proliferative lesions, whereas Ki67 staining is progressively diminished in tumors from mice treated with increasing doses of RC14-MMAE. Quantification of Ki67‑positive nuclei using QuPath confirms this dose‑dependent suppression of proliferation (Figure 4D). Both control groups display similarly high Ki67 indices of approximately 50%–70%, while the 1 mg/kg RC14-MMAE group shows a marked reduction to a mean of around 7.5%. At 5 and 10 mg/kg, Ki67 positivity is further reduced to <1%, indicating near-complete abrogation of proliferative activity in residual tumor tissue. Together, these IHC data demonstrate that RC14-MMAE treatment profoundly inhibits tumor cell proliferation in vivo in a clear dose‑dependent manner.

At study termination on day 23, lungs were collected from all NSG mice and scored for the presence or absence of visible JIMT1 metastatic lesions (Figure 4E). In the vehicle and unconjugated RC14 groups, approximately 80%–100% of lungs harbored metastases, indicating a high baseline propensity for distal spread in this model. In contrast, only about half of the mice treated with 1 mg/kg RC14-MMAE developed lung metastases; this proportion dropped further to ~10% in the 5 mg/kg group, while no metastases were detected in animals receiving 10 mg/kg, whose lungs appeared completely free of tumor involvement. These findings indicate that RC14-MMAE not only suppresses primary tumor growth but also markedly reduces, and at higher doses effectively prevents, the formation of distant lung metastases.

Throughout the study, all treatment groups, including those receiving 10 mg/kg RC14-MMAE, maintained stable or slightly increased body weight, and no overt clinical signs of systemic toxicity were observed (Figure 4F). Consistent with this, serum biochemistry in naïve (non–tumor-bearing) mice injected with PBS, unconjugated RC14, or RC14-MMAE at 5 or 10 mg/kg showed no or minimal dose-dependent abnormalities in liver (ALT, AST, alkaline phosphatase), kidney (urea, creatinine), pancreatic (amylase, lipase), or metabolic (glucose, albumin, total protein) parameters compared with vehicle controls (Supplementary Figure 2). These results indicate that RC14-MMAE is well tolerated in naïve mice at biologically active doses and does not produce detectable hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, or major metabolic disturbances in this preclinical setting.

We further compared treatment with free MMAE at an equimolar dose to 5 mg/kg RC14-MMAE in human tumor-bearing NSG mice. RC14-MMAE significantly inhibited tumor progression, resulting in lower tumor volumes over time and reduced terminal tumor weights, whereas free MMAE failed to improve tumor control relative to vehicle, and both groups displayed comparable tumor growth (Supplementary Figure 3A–C). In parallel, we monitored body weight as an indicator of systemic toxicity and observed that mice treated with RC14-MMAE maintained stable body weight similar to vehicle-treated controls (Supplementary Figure 3D). We also assessed systemic toxicity and organ damage by serum biochemistry (Supplementary Figure 3E) and liver histopathology (Supplementary Table 3), noting that free MMAE was associated with greater perturbations in hepatic enzymes and more indications of liver stress, whereas RC14-MMAE did not increase these parameters beyond the minimal changes observed in vehicle-treated animals. Finally, immune profiling of dissociated tumors revealed that free MMAE significantly decreased the fraction of CD45⁺ leukocytes among live, non-erythroid cells (Supplementary Figure 4A), consistent with broader myelosuppressive and immunodepleting effects of the free payload, whereas RC14-MMAE treatment selectively increased the proportion of F4/80⁺ macrophage-like cells within the same compartment (Supplementary Figure 4B), suggesting that targeted delivery preserves and may even enrich tumor-infiltrating myeloid populations that can contribute to local antitumor activity.

Discussions

RC14‑VcMMAE was developed to exploit mPCNA as a broadly expressed, tumor‑selective antigen and to concentrate a potent microtubule inhibitor in malignant cells while limiting exposure of normal tissues. Our data show that, although VcMMAE conjugation causes a modest reduction in the antigen-binding capacity of RC14, the conjugate retains strong and specific recognition of mPCNA, generates an ADC with favorable pharmacokinetics and serum stability, and mediates strong, dose‑dependent antitumor activity in vitro and in vivo with minimal overt toxicity. Together, these findings support RC14‑VcMMAE as a first‑in‑class mPCNA‑directed ADC and provide proof of concept that membrane‑associated PCNA is a shared therapeutic vulnerability across diverse solid tumors.

Flow‑cytometric analyses showed that RC14 selectively recognizes mPCNA on the surface of several human tumor cell lines, with clear but heterogeneous expression levels. PC‑3 and KYSE‑450 cells exhibited the highest surface staining, whereas JIMT1 and U251 displayed lower but still detectable mPCNA. Despite these differences, RC14‑MMAE induced robust, dose‑dependent cytotoxicity in all four lines, indicating that even modest mPCNA expression supports effective ADC engagement with a potent payload. This aligns with experience from other VcMMAE ADCs, which achieve nanomolar IC₅₀ values on target‑positive cells across a broad antigen‑density range. The strong activity of RC14‑MMAE in JIMT1 cells, which have relatively low surface mPCNA, suggests that mPCNA‑directed ADCs can tolerate substantial intratumoral heterogeneity and may benefit a wide patient population.

Across U251, JIMT1, KYSE‑450, and PC‑3 cells, RC14‑MMAE caused a steep, concentration‑dependent loss of viability after 72 h, whereas unconjugated RC14 had little effect and free MMAE at equivalent payload concentrations was consistently less potent. The LC₅₀ values for RC14‑MMAE fell in the low‑ to mid‑nanomolar range, similar to or slightly higher than those reported for benchmark VcMMAE ADCs such as T‑DM1 analogs and HER2‑ or EGFR‑directed constructs (IC₅₀ ≈ 0.05–2 nM, depending on the model). 32 This level of activity is expected given that the assays used unselected solid tumor lines, some with modest mPCNA expression rather than engineered overexpression. Crystal‑violet adhesion assays mirrored the lysis data and showed that RC14‑MMAE also impairs cell spreading and adherence, consistent with the microtubule‑disrupting mechanism of MMAE and observations for other auristatin‑based ADCs.

The in vivo doses required for efficacy are also in line with other VcMMAE ADCs. In the JIMT1 xenograft model, 5–10 mg/kg RC14‑MMAE given intraperitoneally produced marked tumor regression, near‑complete Ki67 suppression, and a strong reduction in lung metastases without measurable weight loss. Clinically used VcMMAE ADCs, such as polatuzumab vedotin and related vedotin‑class agents, are administered at 1.8–2.4 mg/kg every three weeks in patients, and preclinical mouse studies for these drugs typically use single or repeated doses in the 1–10 mg/kg range. 33 Thus, the exposures required for antitumor activity with RC14‑MMAE are within the range expected for a potent VcMMAE ADC and do not suggest an unusually narrow therapeutic index. 34 Importantly, serum chemistry in naïve mice treated at 5–10 mg/kg did not reveal dose‑dependent elevations in liver enzymes, renal markers, or pancreatic enzymes, further supporting a favorable preclinical safety margin. 35

Our biophysical characterization provides mechanistic insight into how conjugation affects RC14. HIC‑HPLC demonstrated increased hydrophobicity and a heterogeneous DAR distribution after conjugation, while intact‑mass analysis revealed higher‑mass species corresponding to multiple VcMMAE loads. ELISA binding curves showed that RC14‑MMAE has a lower maximal OD and modestly shifted EC₅₀ compared with native RC14, indicating reduced overall binding capacity but near‑native affinity among functional molecules. This pattern matches prior observations that hydrophobic auristatin payloads (VcMMAE/VcMMAF) can perturb antibody structure, promote aggregation, and diminish apparent affinity in a DAR‑ and site‑dependent manner. Because RC14‑MMAE still binds well enough to drive potent in vitro and in vivo activity, this affinity loss appears acceptable for mPCNA targeting; however, affinity‑preserving conjugation approaches, such as site‑specific or cysteine‑rebridging chemistries, could further optimize the balance between DAR, stability, and target recognition. 36

Pharmacokinetic and stability data indicate that RC14‑MMAE combines sufficient in vivo persistence with controlled payload release. After intraperitoneal dosing in NSG mice, RC14‑MMAE showed a Tmax of ~6–8 h and a terminal half‑life increasing from ~7.8 h at 1 mg/kg to ~38–59 h at 5–10 mg/kg, with more‑than‑proportional increases in AUC. These values fall within the range reported for other IgG1‑based VcMMAE ADCs in mice, which typically show half‑lives of 1–3 d depending on target and dose. 37 Serum‑stability assays showed that the RC14 backbone remains largely intact over 7 d, whereas the fraction of MMAE still conjugated declines gradually to ~60% by 168 h, with minimal loss during the first 24–48 h. Functional lysis assays after serum incubation further demonstrated that the high‑molecular‑weight fraction, enriched for intact RC14‑MMAE, remains potently cytotoxic, whereas the low‑molecular‑weight fraction containing released MMAE has negligible activity at the tested dilutions. Together, these findings support a pharmacological profile in which RC14‑MMAE circulates long enough to reach tumors, internalize, and release MMAE intracellularly without sustaining systemic exposure to free MMAE, an important feature for vedotin‑class ADC tolerability. 38-40

Our work also highlights future directions for RC14 as an ADC scaffold. Although MMAE provides a well‑validated proof of concept, alternative payloads could be used to tune potency, bystander effect, and toxicity. For example, replacing MMAE with MMAF or other auristatin analogs would alter membrane permeability and may lessen off‑tumor toxicity at the cost of reduced bystander killing. Non‑tubulin payloads, such as DNA‑damaging agents or topoisomerase I inhibitors like SN‑38, could help target tumors that are intrinsically resistant to microtubule disruption or avoid overlapping toxicities with standard regimens. 41 Second, linker technologies that increase hydrophilicity and control DAR have been shown to mitigate aggregation and improve exposure of auristatin ADCs, and such platforms could further refine RC14‑based constructs.

Human tumor–bearing NSG mice treated with free MMAE displayed a significantly reduced fraction of CD45⁺ leukocytes within the live, non‑erythroid tumor cell suspension, consistent with the known cytotoxic and immunodepleting effects of free MMAE on immune cells. 42 , 43 In contrast, RC14‑MMAE–treated mice showed a significantly increased fraction of F4/80⁺ macrophage‑like cells within the same compartment; however, this enrichment alone does not prove that these cells directly contributed to the ADC's therapeutic efficacy. Residual murine myeloid cells, particularly macrophages, may exert complex and potentially opposing effects on ADC performance by either limiting systemic drug exposure or supporting local antitumor activity: (i) FcγR‑expressing macrophages in peripheral organs can accelerate ADC clearance from the circulation and thereby diminish efficacy 25 , 44 ; (ii) tumor‑associated macrophages can internalize and process ADCs, enhancing local payload release and bystander killing of neighboring tumor cells; 44 and (iii) ADC‑induced death of human tumor cells may recruit or activate resident murine macrophages, promoting phagocytic clearance of dying cells and contributing to localized antitumor immunity. 42 , 45

Beyond single‑payload designs, bispecific and dual‑payload ADCs represent attractive next‑generation formats. 46 Bispecific ADCs combining two tumor antigens can address spatial heterogeneity and reduce the risk of antigen‑negative escape, and several HER2/HER3 and EGFR/c‑MET bispecific ADCs are now in preclinical or early clinical development. 4 Because mPCNA is expressed across multiple histologies, RC14 could be engineered as a bispecific antibody that simultaneously engages mPCNA and a second tumor antigen (for example HER2 or EGFR), thereby increasing avidity and selectivity for highly malignant cells. 47 Dual‑payload strategies, in which two mechanistically distinct drugs (such as MMAF and SN‑38) are loaded on the same antibody, have shown superior potency and the ability to overcome acquired resistance in preclinical models. 48 , 49 Conceptually, RC14 could be adapted to carry complementary payloads—such as a microtubule inhibitor together with a DNA‑damaging agent—to target both dividing and quiescent mPCNA‑positive cells while keeping the total dose of each payload lower than in single‑payload constructs. 50 , 51

Several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. The heterogeneity in LC50 values across cell lines, with PC‑3 being markedly more sensitive than KYSE‑450 or JIMT1, likely reflects differences in mPCNA density, internalization kinetics, and intrinsic susceptibility to microtubule disruption; similar factors are known to influence clinical responses to existing ADCs and may necessitate biomarker‑driven patient selection. Our in vivo work was conducted in immunodeficient NSG mice, which do not model Fc‑mediated effector functions or potential immunomodulatory effects of mPCNA targeting, and thus may underestimate both on‑target, off‑tumor toxicity and beneficial immune–tumor interactions. 52 In addition, RC14‑MMAE exhibits a heterogeneous DAR distribution and modest reduction in apparent binding capacity; although acceptable for proof of concept, more controlled conjugation chemistries could reduce batch‑to‑batch variability and further widen the therapeutic window. 44 Finally, while our PK and serum‑chemistry data in mice are encouraging, formal toxicology studies in additional species and combination studies with standard therapies will be required before clinical translation.

In summary, this work establishes mPCNA as a druggable cell‑surface antigen and RC14 as a versatile scaffold for targeted payload delivery. By integrating detailed analyses of conjugation, binding, internalization, pharmacokinetics, serum stability, and in vivo efficacy, we show that RC14‑MMAE achieves potent antitumor and antimetastatic activity at exposures comparable to other VcMMAE ADCs while maintaining a favorable preclinical safety profile. These results motivate further development of RC14‑based ADCs, including exploration of alternative linkers and payloads, bispecific formats, and dual‑payload strategies, with the goal of delivering durable benefit to patients with mPCNA‑positive solid tumors.
