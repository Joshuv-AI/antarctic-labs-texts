On the translational potential of atlases in precision oncology
Authors: Lucrezia Zorzi, Lucia Casella, Marco Dominietto, Enrico Capobianco
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe proliferation of publicly available imaging datasets, combined with widespread access to computational power, has boosted research in neuroscience, neurobiology, and systems biology while inspiring projects centered on building atlases. Atlases are methodological tools that provide global overviews of detailed thematic information, are usually organized in grids with assigned resolution to facilitate inference, and offer a reliable knowledge base from multimodal evidence data. Two critical goals have been generally addressed through atlases: (I) warehousing baseline information of normal anatomical structures under physiological (i.e., non-pathological) conditions; and (II) establishing a reference for typical anatomy across demographic groups by aggregating high-resolution imaging data that cover extensively diverse populations. Compared to more traditional atlases often referring to homogeneous groups of populations, the recent atlas developments have leveraged data multimodality and utilized machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) tools for inference purposes. Together with the possibility of representing normal variation within specific demographic cohorts and gaining usability and reliability in clinical applications, data multimodality is particularly impactful in precision oncology and personalized therapy. This review discusses the translational potential of atlases in cancer studies through their property of integrating multiple types of cancer data and inspiring predictive learning algorithms that account for the correlations between anatomical and imaging features with genetic and omics markers.Keywords: Atlases, brain, precision oncology, multimodal data, radiomics

Introduction

Background

An atlas in biology/biomedicine is usually conceived as a comprehensive collection of maps, charts, parcellations often centered on images, among other data types, illustrating the anatomic, physiological, molecular and/or genetic features at a specific macro-environmental (organ) or micro-environmental [cell: see for instance the process of building virtual cell with artificial intelligence (AI) support (https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.11654)] level. Due to the vast amount of detailed information usually needed, an atlas often requires two steps: (I) the organization and aggregation of information from multiple individuals; and (II) the generation of average representations of particular target features or standard characteristics in a healthy population. For general inference purposes, these atlases should be based on data integration solutions that are highly reproducible and generalizable (1-3).

In general, the analytical treatment of a disease depends on integrating first a few or many data sources and then efficiently assembling the related information in a template in which an atlas selection can be performed to assess the data descriptive or predictive value (4). Examples are provided by image data-driven atlases with spatio-temporal information obtained from heterogeneous multiple imaging modalities, usually collected in large amounts. Qualitatively, medical images bring various analytical and computational challenges and have inspired a wealth of solutions driven by machine learning (ML) methods and data fusion applications.

It is important to note that due to anatomical, patho-physiological, and molecular complexity present in organs affected by disease, some of the measurable characteristics become the object of investigation. A disease-driven atlas is an inference tool that emphasizes similarity/dissimilarity between healthy and altered conditions obtained from the integration of measured characteristics, then mapped over a template. Here, the data source heterogeneity and the various phases of data processing will be assessed in terms of their contributions toward improved diagnosis and treatment-related plans. To support the interpretation of results, spatial pattern analysis, interactions between cell types, visualizations of image segmentations, and clusters are possible atlas-guided strategies.

An atlas reference in terms of concept and development traditionally refers to the brain. Brain atlases present a few useful complexities that potentially translate to human tumor atlases. One is the inherent multidimensionality of cellular, structural, and molecular information usually associated with cancer characteristics. Another dynamic complexity refers to time variation. An example is offered by the Human Tumor Atlas Network (HTAN) (https://www.cancer.gov/about-nci/organization/dcb/research-programs/htan). Here, the imaging-generated data sets are targeted to biological discovery and aimed to integrate genetic, molecular, morphological, and spatial information from various sources (tissues, transcriptomes, single cells). In parallel, a main reference data source in cancer research is The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA) (https://www.cancerimagingarchive.net/), with collections of medical images from a variety of patient cohorts organized by disease type and imaging modality and often complemented by information on outcomes, treatment, genomic, etc.

Of note, the fact that inserting image data sets in the inference process implies the use of ML and statistical tools to proceed with atlas identification [see for instance (5)] and the combination of multiple evidence types into reference systems to systematically infer at both inter-patient level and across diseases. Ideally, for a profitable use of image features, efficient and standardized cross-sample data integration is needed. In practice, an assembly of supporting stroma, membranes, blood vessels, etc., information would be extensively needed, but remains a challenging step. Despite many proposed solutions for the automatic extraction of spatial information from images obtained from tissue specimens, the ongoing research efforts try to improve the annotation component and optimize the link between image features and clinical outcomes.

Rationale and knowledge gap

Our motivation considers the potential impact that atlases have as data/feature aggregators in precision oncology and personalized treatment, especially once integrated within ML/AI tools that facilitate algorithms’ explainability and improve model predictions (Figure 1). At a very general level, these developments are highly needed to enable improved patient outcomes and better optimized healthcare resources. More specifically, atlases can be the place of convergence of a wealth of diversified information and data that once assembled together maximize their contribution to predictive inference.

Overview of brain atlases

The Human Brain Project offers a main research infrastructure to decode the human brain, reconstruct the brain’s multiscale organization, and create brain-inspired information technology. Another relevant example is The Human Connectome Project that maps both structural and functional connections in the brain and deciphers the relationship between brain circuits and behavior (6). Field experts indicate with ‘brain parcellation’ the delineation of spatial partitions of the brain that are fundamental for understanding its organization and function. An important underlying assumption is that the human brain involves a dual perspective, a specialistic one through the role of local areas with their properties, and a global one centered on all the features enabling connectivity (7).

In fact, the organization of the human brain is governed by two fundamental principles: (I) functional integration into large-scale networks, which is realized through long-range connections; and (II) functional segregation into distinct regions, which is realized through local differentiation. These complementary characteristics are present in each brain area determining its regional composition and its specific interactions with other regions. As a result, distinct partitions characterize the brain with networks of closely interacting regions also including multiple discontinuous patterns. The complexity of such networks and patterns has been explored by classic histological brain mapping methods, together with imaging-based approaches across different dimensions (8-11).

In our view, it is especially imaging that has recently opened new scenarios in brain cancer research, by two significant perspectives: (I) the construction of imaging-specific and integrated atlases, with the latter still only preliminarily explored; and (II) the possibility to translate the methodological approaches across cancers in multiple organs. Atlases support research progresses by primarily identifying registration and segmentation targets for deep image analysis, functional activation, and statistical mapping. The expected scientific discovery impacts of atlases in both clinical practice and surgical planning leverage comparative evaluations of generated multimodal image data to be integrated within template maps (12). Then, structure identification, localization, delineation, labelling, and quantification can be algorithmically automated to the benefit of user’s efficiency and confidence in image interpretation (13).

Anatomical/structural atlases

Structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) brain parcellation is an important tool for research. Individual brain scans from several individuals can be combined to form a brain image bank and finally deliver a brain atlas—an anatomical representation of the brain showing group-wise brain features based on known function and/or anatomy. What is becoming a pervasive goal in most of the recent publications is the fact that an atlas provides a powerful medium for assessing ways to combine brain mapping findings obtained from different imaging modalities. This exciting practice with the goal of creating a universal map is challenged by the complexity of brain organization emerging at several biological levels and across several axes.

Limitations are currently present, for instance, when considering the complexity of information in different data sources. Despite advances of modern neuroimaging, like non-invasive three-dimensional (3D) datasets, two-dimensional (2D) histological atlases remain the gold standard for the broad community of neuroscientists. Other complexity pertains to data multimodality. As brain atlases are expression of digital database sources that capture the spatio-temporal distribution of physiological and anatomical metrics, the mapping within neuroanatomy metrics is potentially integrable by gene expression and connected omics components (14-16). Temporally, disease trajectories based on markers’ evolution could become available within established timeframes, while at the spatial level, an accurate tracking of signal-to-noise changes would be delivered from the original atlas resolution or further refinements of it. This could be very relevant in the monitoring of disease progression or control of metastasis.

In general, the core concept within the field of brain mapping is the use of a standardized, or stereotaxic, 3D coordinate frame for data analysis and reporting of findings from neuroimaging experiments. Amalgamating multiple brain parcellations into a single, standardized, curated list would offer researchers a valuable resource for evaluating replication of neuroimaging studies. Brain atlases are an important resource for neuroanatomical definition and often the basis for automated image analyses, which are likely to include population imaging studies. Overall, four atlas generations are distinguished: early cortical maps, print stereotactic atlases, early digital atlases, and advanced brain atlas platforms. However, the usefulness of human brain atlases depends not only on the atlas content but also on the functionality enabling and supporting various atlas-based applications as well as atlas availability (17,18).

Physiological/functional atlases

Structural analysis in brain atlases is usually complemented by functional imaging studies. Advances in ex vivo and in vivo imaging techniques may close the gap between histological and neuroimaging atlases by adding further atlas dimensions and help understand the complex interplay between the brain’s electrical activity, cellular/molecular composition, structural connectivity, vascular supply, and gene expression (19).

To elucidate such relationships, researchers may use network-based analyses and explore inter-individual variance regarding various types of signals. Another domain of applications involves functional neuroimaging studies that typically rely on group studies to identify the most consistent pattern of activation associated with a given cognitive operation. Anatomical parcellation of the brain and atlases may also be used in clinical practice as reference images to support diagnoses of age-related neurodegenerative disorders.

It is worth mentioning that to perform group studies using functional imaging data, individual brain images are usually transformed into a common coordinate space. This approach typically involves an automated process to spatially transform each individual’s brain image into the coordinate space. However, this transformation does not ensure that an identical point in the space corresponds to the same anatomical feature across all individuals. The two most widely used spaces in the neuroscience community are the Talairach space and the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space (Box 1).

To complement the above atlases, a statistical treatment focuses on a quantitative reflection of normal variability in anatomy, function, pathology, or other imaging measurements, and it allows us to establish a baseline against which abnormal images are to be compared for diagnostic or treatment planning purposes. It is usually built on medical images as MRI, computed tomography (CT), positron emission tomography (PET), and ultrasound (US) (21). Constructing a statistical atlas relies on a fundamental building block, namely deformable registration, which maps imaging data from many individuals to a common coordinate system, so that statistics of normal variability, as well as abnormal deviations from it, can be performed.

Use of atlases in oncology

Initially, the construction of brain atlases was severely limited by factors like the high costs of high-quality data from imaging and the accessibility of large-scale computational resources. In particular, imaging scans were expensive, time-consuming, and typically requiring advanced infrastructure (22), dedicated hardware, and specialized staff to process the scans and store the derived large datasets. The current imaging technological landscape offers a dramatic reduction of these barriers. For instance, MRI acquisition has become faster, more affordable, and widely available. Cloud computing and storage solutions are quite unexpensive and facilitate data integration at a fraction of the historical costs (23). This sort of democratization of the infrastructures has also accelerated the development of specialized atlases translating research in crucial medical areas like oncology and other fields.

The use of atlases in oncology has the potential to offer remarkable support for personalized medicine, significantly enhancing the ability to tailor treatments to individual patients. In contrast to the neurological field, where atlases are extensively used to study both the physiology and behavior of the healthy brain, as well as to investigate various neurological diseases, the oncological atlases are primarily focused on evaluating pathological conditions and examining multiple organs, the one directly affected by the tumor and those affected by (or potentially becoming targets of) metastatic progression. An advantage is that sources of complexity, like tissue diversity and data heterogeneity, are being examined within a simple and efficient template for representing information and data aggregation strategies.

The benefits of atlases in managing decisions that affect cancer patients are expected to translate into more accurate diagnosis, treatment and follow-up, due to the fact that atlases operate by averaging data across homogeneous groups of people but also facilitate extensive and standardized comparisons between groups, enhance disease classification among patients in both diagnostics and treatment, and allow high-quality inference through AI- and ML-driven tools. In precision oncology, these tools can unlock previously unexplored potential in two main areas. First, they can assist in identifying the optimal treatment strategies toward local tumor control to prevent progression and metastasis, and for minimizing unwanted side effects. Second, in longitudinal studies, atlases play a crucial role in effectively organizing and distributing large volumes of data carrying meaningful insights.

General developments

Cancer complexity demands a multidisciplinary approach and continuous research to improve a wide spectrum of decisions and actions that lead to therapeutic outcomes and include the management of adverse effects effectively. Precision medicine offers detailed insights into tumors and affected organs, while personalized treatment approaches tailor specific plans to meet individual patient needs.

Given the centrality of modern imaging techniques and the availability of genetic (24) and histological readouts (25), data types and modalities cover a large number of qualitative and quantitative features consolidated within an anatomical context. As an example, interesting research work was conducted to investigate the comparison of atlas-based and deep learning methods for organs at risk delineation on head-and-neck CT images using an automated treatment planning system (26).

Organizing and analyzing such extensive information can be challenging for traditional statistical approaches. By providing a well-structured framework for the data, systematic mapping, and clearer understanding of disease mechanisms, atlases can facilitate the development of personalized treatment strategies and assist clinicians in diagnosing cancer more accurately (27). We next prioritize treatment and data science centrality as major trends guiding some of the desired atlas developments and improvements.

Assessment of treatment efficacy

With AI’s support, atlas frameworks allow to uncover patterns that would otherwise remain invisible as these are not limited to a single dimension but may span across various dimensions. For instance, AI can detect spatial features in data at both macroscopic and microscopic levels, revealing correlations between tumor size, shape, and structure, or cellular-level changes indicative of disease progression. In other cases, AI allows to analyze temporal features, as seen in repeated time series acquisitions from longitudinal patient monitoring, which may reveal how tumors respond to treatment over time. Beyond the spatial and temporal dimensions, AI can also manage high-order complexities, synthesizing information across multiple variables to create models based on feature aggregations.

AI can integrate anatomical data with molecular and histopathological information, bridging the gap between different scales, from whole-body imaging to single-cell genomics (28). In doing so, AI systems not only reveal previously undetectable relationships but also predict clinical outcomes with remarkable accuracy. These insights can help oncologists tailor treatment plans to individual patients, identifying the most effective interventions based on unique biological profiles and disease trajectories. AI-driven tools explore the intricate web of interrelated features pertaining to gene expression profiles, protein interactions, and imaging biomarkers, for then suitably reducing the global data dimensionality and identify actionable insights with models that accurately select clinical variables predictive of response to treatment and disease progression (29,30).

Future developments

Hypothesis-driven atlases

The hypothesis-driven approach remains a cornerstone of scientific inquiry, emphasizing the importance of existing knowledge and theoretical frameworks to guide research and analysis in oncology. This approach formulates hypotheses based on prior studies, clinical observations, or established biological mechanisms, which researchers then test against new data. While presenting merits, the approach can constrain the exploration of unexpected findings or novel correlations that do not conform to the initial hypotheses (31,32).

The integration of AI’s analytics into hypothesis-driven atlases enhances model’s efficacy by providing ways to test and validate hypotheses more efficiently. For example, AI can rapidly analyze large datasets to confirm or refute hypotheses, enabling researchers to understand the underlying mechanisms of cancer progression, treatment resistance, and patient heterogeneity. This synergy can also lead to the refinement of existing theories and the development of new hypotheses based on data-driven insights fostering a cycle of continuous learning and innovation, ultimately advancing scientific understanding and improving patient care (33,34).

Data-driven atlases

The versatility of the data-driven approaches across different scientific disciplines drives innovation and improves the understanding of complex phenomena, often revealing insights that challenge existing paradigms and broaden the scope of inquiry. One complication that atlases govern well is the reliance on large volumes of data to uncover insights and inform clinical decisions without necessitating preconceived notions or established theories. Consequently, there is a shift of focus from traditional hypothesis-driven research based on prior knowledge to exploratory strategies revealing the value of data information represented in atlases. Therefore, this approach embraces uncertainty and allows to discover unexpected relationships and new patterns (35).

Here, machine and deep learning techniques play a crucial role in classifying diverse datasets—ranging from genomic sequences and proteomic profiles to imaging studies and electronic health records. For instance, processing high-dimensional data from tumor biopsies may help identify genetic mutations correlated with treatment responses avoiding bias from pre-existing hypotheses. This capability may accelerate the discovery of novel biomarkers and reveal interlinked biological factors, ultimately explaining genetic variation influence on drug metabolism and also drug efficacy (36,37).

Multimodality

An important research theme is imaging multimodality, where the priority is what modalities need to be combined and why, i.e., based on what expected results or gains with respect to single modality approaches. In radiomic studies, one expects that more effective radiomic models include a blend of imaging modalities rather than any single one.

A wider definition of multi-modality embraces other types of data associations, where disease phenotypes can be characterized (e.g., gene expression profiles, molecular subtypes, outcomes, etc.). In practice, the fusion of various modalities should translate into better model performance. However, from the standpoints of computational data science, the treatment of multimodality is complex and depends on many factors, mostly contextual and specific to the disease characteristics.

It is naturally expected that the relative importance of various types of data can contextually change with the type of cancer, the measurement (i.e., imaging), and also the patient (profile and history). Radiomic measurements, for instance, are likely to allow selection of significant features in different ways and according to specific imaging modality contributions. The final predictive models will therefore be affected by such significant quantifications organized into an encompassing model where significance can be tested.

ML and associated approaches

Brain atlases have appeared in oncological applications often combined with ML techniques. These approaches provide a detailed anatomical and physiological subdivision of brain regions, allowing for precise feature extraction in radiomic studies. The segmentation enables more refined analysis and ensures that the data is well-structured and highly informative, thus significantly enhancing the efficiency and accuracy of inference models.

In a recent study (38), brain atlas-derived features were used to predict glioblastoma (GBS) progression by analyzing patterns in features extracted from different brain regions. The anatomical subdivision provided by atlases allowed for a more accurate, region-specific characterization of tumor growth, and for tracking changes over time thus enabling longitudinal comparisons of tumor evolution. This structured approach has achieved high precision levels in analysis and prediction of GBS development.

While a robust volume of studies exists and is centered on ML applications and methods targeted to cancer [see, for instance, Machine Learning for Cancer Research-National Cancer Institute (NCI) (https://www.cancer.gov/about-nci/organization/cbiit/training/data-science-course/machine-learning), and also reviews like (39-43), among the many that are available] only a few examples are available to elucidate the role of ML in cancer atlases. A study aims to register and fuse together labelled information obtained from multiple normal brain atlases to form a new meta brain image useful for segmentation by a low-rank method (44). In another study with more than 13 thousand images of brain metastases collected and analyzed from 3,065 cancer patients, a predominant localization to high perfusion areas near the grey-white matter junction is reported, and a probabilistic brain metastasis risk model is proposed to enhance the therapeutic ratio of whole-brain radiotherapy (potentially reducing the cognitive side effects) (45).

Furthermore, the use of brain atlases promotes consistency in data sharing, facilitating comparisons of tumor characteristics across patients/studies. By providing a standardized framework, atlases ensure that results are reproducible across cohorts. Federated learning (FL) (Box 2) enhances the utility of atlas-based approaches in collaborative settings by enabling secure data integration across institutions. Considering three main legal and ethical priorities identified as education and training of healthcare professionals, offer of support and guidance throughout the use of AI systems, and integration of the necessary ethical and legal reflection at the heart of the AI tools (49), atlases seem not to create major barriers.

The synergy with ML automated feature extraction contributes to more accurate monitoring of tumor progression and personalized treatment strategies. Recently, the largest FL study to-date was published with data obtained from 71 sites and six continents and aimed to detect automatically GBS boundary. The use of atlases may facilitate further analyses in terms of segmentation and assessment of the quality of consensus models (50).

Radiomics has engaged ML scientists due to the need to develop complex data processing tasks such as tumor segmentation, image preprocessing, feature extraction, model development, testing, and validation. Usually, from the initial extraction of features describing distributions of signal intensities and spatial relationships, one steps to selecting features that inform specifically on texture, shape, statistical, and entropic aspects, etc. Therefore, from an analysis of features centered on intensity variation and gray levels, the information becomes more accurate with texture, showing patterns characterized by brightness, color, slope, size, and shape descriptors revealing geometric aspects characteristics.

Discussion

Starting from the generation of brain atlases and their consolidated applications and studies in neuroscience, we have considered whether valuable opportunities exist in oncological research and related clinical practice. For meaningful development and productive use of cancer atlases, a number of factors need to be assessed in view of future key research directions, starting from those with an established influence on reproducibility and generalizability of results.

Key research directions

Atlas data and models

The building blocks of each atlas are basically two: (I) the data obtained from different sources and potentially integrable within the atlas; and (II) the algorithmic treatment of the information in the data for building inference models. An ideal cancer atlas will combine genetic and/or omics data with imaging, and also clinical measurements, records, and test parameters. Depending on the cancer type, genetic markers, omics profiles, and targeted imaging will provide disease state characterization. At a methodological level, each data type processing will require specific statistical, machine and deep learning, AI, and big data approaches. These solutions will apply to a variety of contexts. A typical complexity will remain associated with the heterogeneity of data naturally responding to different scales and measurements to be harmonized. The data mapping, allowing the atlas construction, will involve choosing a resolution that allows efficiency in data processing. With imaging, for instance, the aggregation at the voxel level allows high atlas resolution and in turn superior detection accuracy and guidance relative to training set size and validation strategies.

Atlas variables and properties

In mapping tools such as brain atlases specifically designed to reveal descriptions of anatomy and pathology, both general variables (age, gender, etc.) and specific variables deserve consideration. Specificity is for instance provided by hemispheric asymmetry and anatomical correspondence, or spatial normalization methodologies. The diversity of these factors justifies the recourse to ad hoc applications for both research and clinical scopes. One challenge that arises is related to the development of standardization methods that ensure consistency across studies and patient populations. In such regards, atlas frameworks are to be considered inherently robust tools as they provide standard, detailed, and replicable information templates that facilitate validity verifications, consistency tracking, and comparative evaluations. Scalability is also an issue to consider with large populations, although stratifications may be enabled to reduce the sample size and the complexity.

The variability factor

A first critical aspect of creating robust atlases lies in capturing population variance across multiple individuals, which enhances our understanding of functional anatomy. Imaging techniques, especially MRI, facilitate large-scale acquisition of whole-brain images, which can be statistically summarized to reflect typical anatomical features as well as variations due to age, development, learning, and disease. By constructing probabilistic atlases that represent the mean and variance in organ structure across individuals, researchers can establish baselines against which abnormal patterns can be detected, further supporting diagnostic and therapeutic applications.

Inter-subject variability poses a fundamental challenge to the development of atlases, because is spatially pervasive and affects neurobiological properties. While group-based parcellation schemes can capture general organizational features, individual differences in size, shape, and position of pathological tissues may necessitate the adoption of advanced statistical and ML methods that accurately model variability. Note that addressing inter-subject variability is particularly crucial in oncology, where precise localization of tumorigenic regions influences directly any treatment decision. In parallel, designing such tools should also consider not to exclude sub-populations or otherwise gathering sufficient training data for all patient groups in support to equitable inference.

Imaging multimodality

A comprehensive view of atlases with reference to imaging includes multiple modalities. Combinations of MRI and PET images, possibly integrated with data from various sources, will shape multimodal approaches with an opportunity to merge multilayered information obtained across anatomical, functional, and molecular levels. This will suggest a holistic view of tumor biology and its interaction with surrounding structures by the achievement of a truly multidimensional understanding of oncological processes. This is essential for obtaining a comprehensive disease map of complex organs like the brain.

Clinical applicability

In a truly translational spirit, it is crucial for atlases to be clinically useful and effective when adopted. For this to happen, standardization is needed for use in retrospective, prospective, and multicentric studies. Standardized atlases facilitate consistent interpretations across clinical settings and promote data sharing, collaboration, and validation in large-scale studies. In multicentric studies, where variability in imaging protocols and patient demographics can impact outcomes, a unified atlas framework ensures that findings are comparable and reproducible, enhancing the reliability of oncology research and clinical applications.

Retrospective studies benefit from standardized atlases as they allow researchers to re-evaluate imaging data with consistent anatomical references, facilitating comparisons across different time points and patient cohorts. This reanalysis can yield new insights into disease progression and treatment responses not previously observed. In prospective studies, standardized atlases provide a reliable framework for building consistent imaging protocols and analysis methods. This consistency enables more accurate tracking of changes over time and location, especially in response to treatments, and helps validate findings across diverse clinical settings.

Then, standardized atlases facilitate consistent interpretations across different clinical environments and promote best practices in terms of data sharing and validation strategies in large-scale studies. In multicentric studies, where variability in imaging protocols and patient demographics can impact outcomes, a unified atlas framework ensures that findings are comparable and reproducible, enhancing the reliability of research results and the relevance of clinical applications (51).

Finally, huge opportunities exist to support the clinical development of personalized therapies in the next future by using multimodal atlases producing visualization maps customized to cancer functions and the changes in their characteristics. Such scenarios fit well with the so-called N-of-1 studies in which single patients are studied longitudinally.

Atlas performance assessments

An active area of research involves identifying atlas validation metrics for the assessment of success in the use of AI tools with regard to their training in complex research scenarios (ongoing work by the authors) or the possibility to adopt transfer learning strategies in the presence of data paucity or disparity, something that requires further investigation and strictly dependent on the chosen (or possible) atlas resolution.

By further considering and developing atlases in specific cancer contexts, such assessments are expected to depend on the understanding of the complexity and targetability of features related to tumor types, characteristics, etc. For instance, if the goal is to provide accurate descriptions of tumor growth dynamics and progression mechanisms, the benefit obtained from atlases offering compact representations and maps of imaging data combined with multidimensional molecular information might be AI/ML-supported by ad hoc quantitative scores, heat maps, and other types of summaries.

Ethical remarks

While greatly enhancing the potential of atlas-guided research and clinical decision support in oncology, AI’s use also raises important ethical and legal considerations. The integration of patient-derived imaging and multi-omics data into AI-driven atlases requires strict safeguards to protect privacy, ensure informed consent, and avoid biases that could lead to unequal treatment recommendations. Moreover, transparency in model design and interpretability of predictions are essential to maintain clinical trust and accountability. In this respect, regulatory initiatives which classify AI healthcare systems according to risk and requirements for transparency, data governance, and human oversight provide an important legal framework to ensure that atlas-based AI applications are deployed responsibly with minimal risks (52-54).

Conclusions

This study emphasizes the translational role of Atlases, and especially the potential support they may offer as tools facilitating cancer research. The rationale is based on a parallel with brain studies. After reviewing some of the established concepts and impacts in neuroscience, atlases are discussed in view of guiding precision oncology decisions leveraging the management and integration of multimodal data and structured information associated to disease characteristics.

We have suggested that classifications beyond structural and functional ones are needed, centered on data- versus hypothesis-driven inferences, a dichotomy that is clearly foundational to machine, deep, and even federated learning, and practically fits well with the current knowledge gaps.

Finally, we have reported key research directions for the future agenda, with special attention to the complexities related to atlas design and methodology. These include the choice of the best possible atlas resolution for gaining efficiency in data processing and the aim of consistency through the achievement of standardized solutions across studies and patient populations, while ensuring scalability and sufficient stratification power.

In conclusion, as effective translation implies clinical applicability and utility, we referred to imaging protocols and analysis methods having paramount importance in retrospective, prospective, and multicentric studies relatively to adaptation to different environments, promotion of best practices, and personalization of therapies supported by unambiguous validation strategies. We also noted that these future developments call necessarily for further abilities to assess the tool performance and manage all ethical aspects.
