Reasons for not drinking: Demographic variation and health risk awareness among abstainers and former drinkers in a national survey
Authors: Priscilla Martinez-Matyszczyk, Laurie A Drabble, Cat Munroe, Anthony Surace, Katherine J Karriker-Jaffe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeDespite a decline in drinking in the United States, current reasons for alcohol abstention remain underexplored. We aimed to estimate the prevalence of reasons for not drinking among lifetime abstainers and former drinkers, compare reasons across demographics, and examine associations between awareness of alcohol's health risks and not drinking for health reasons.MethodsWe used data from a 2024 nationally representative online survey of U.S. adults. Respondents reporting no past-year or lifetime alcohol use (n = 1772) rated the importance of five reasons for not drinking and indicated their awareness of alcohol health risks. We used bivariate comparisons and multivariate logistic regressions to examine subgroup differences in reasons for not drinking and associations with awareness of alcohol's health effects.ResultsThe most common reasons for not drinking were concerns about health and impaired control. In bivariate analysis, fewer gender and sexual minority individuals cited health and impaired control as reasons for not drinking, while more Black and Hispanic respondents endorsed health concerns and family/friend concerns as reasons. Awareness of alcohol's health risks was higher among those who cited health as a reason for not drinking. In multivariate analyses, gender, race, ethnicity, sexual identity, and awareness of alcohol's effect on cancer risk remained significant correlates of reasons for not drinking.ConclusionsThe most common reasons for not drinking related to concerns about health and impaired control, and reasons varied between population subgroups. These reasons for not drinking offer candidate content for future research testing alcohol reduction messaging.Keywords: Alcohol abstention, Reasons for not drinking, Health harms, Race/ethnicity, Gender, Sexual orientation

Introduction

Alcohol use is declining in the United States (US) (Slater & Alpert, 2025). This decline may reflect multiple factors, including the growing sober-curious movement (Myles et al., 2023) and increasing attention to alcohol's negative health effects (US Department of Health and Human Services, 2025). While reasons for not drinking have been studied for several decades (Bernards et al., 2009; Greenfield et al., 1989; Rosansky & Rosenberg, 2020), limited contemporary, population-based data are available about reasons for not drinking among former drinkers and lifetime abstainers in the US. Understanding reasons for not drinking among non-drinking groups in the current context of changing alcohol use can identify candidate content for future research testing alcohol reduction messaging with contemporary drinker populations.

Research shows that reasons for not drinking vary across populations. A systematic review found that reasons for not drinking among lifetime abstainers include a lack of interest in, and disliking the effects of, alcohol. Former drinkers reported having no interest in drinking and concerns about health as reasons for not drinking, while people with previous drinking problems were particularly concerned about health (Rosansky & Rosenberg, 2020). Few studies have found gender differences in reasons for not drinking (Rosansky & Rosenberg, 2020), while other factors, such as culture and religion, are notable correlates (Collins & McNair, 2002; Krause, 2003; Michalak et al., 2007; Rosansky & Rosenberg, 2020). Indeed, religion is particularly relevant to alcohol abstention among Black and African American people (Meyers et al., 2017; Wallace et al., 2003) and heterosexual women (Drabble et al., 2016). Although this literature elucidates multiple reasons for not drinking, there is scant information on which reasons for not drinking are salient in the current decline in US alcohol consumption or how it varies across demographic subgroups.

Because “health concerns” as a catch-all is often cited as a reason for not drinking, it is also important to understand how awareness of alcohol's effects on specific health outcomes, such as hypertension, diabetes, cancer, depression and anxiety, may be associated with this reason for not drinking. While it is reasonable to assume that awareness of alcohol's negative health effects would be associated with not drinking due to health concerns, there are reasons to examine this empirically. First, there is notable variation in awareness of alcohol's effects across alcohol-related conditions. For example, our previous work showed general population awareness that excessive alcohol use increases disease risk ranged from 61% for hypertension to 52% for diabetes and 42% for cancer (Martinez-Matyszczyk et al., 2026). Second, the idea that alcohol has protective cardiovascular or beneficial all-cause mortality effects persists (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2025), which can obscure appreciation of alcohol's negative health effects and/or limit strong endorsement to not drink for health reasons. Third, one's perceived risk and severity of an alcohol-related health condition may vary across these conditions (Wang et al., 2009). As purported by the Protection Motivation Theory (Balla & Hagger, 2025; Rogers, 1975), such threat appraisals are the first step in an individual's motivation to protect oneself from health harms and the adoption of health protection behaviors. Thus, awareness of particular health consequences from alcohol use with high perceived risks and/or severities may be associated with not drinking for health concerns, while those with lower perceived risks and/or severities may not.

Together, these gaps point to a need for contemporary, population-based estimates of reasons for not drinking among former drinkers and lifetime abstainers in the US, their variation across demographic subgroups, and the relationship between awareness of alcohol's specific health effects and not drinking for health reasons. Thus, the current study aimed to 1) estimate the prevalence of a strong endorsement of reasons for not drinking in the US among former drinkers and lifetime abstainers, 2) compare reasons for not drinking across demographic non-drinker subgroups, and 3) examine associations between awareness of alcohol's specific health risks (i.e., hypertension, diabetes, cancer, depression, and anxiety) and a strong endorsement of not drinking because one believes alcohol is bad for health.

Methods

Participants

We used data from the 2024 National Alcohol Survey (NAS), a nationally representative survey of US adults (N = 6392) recruited via address-based probability sampling and probability- and non-probability-based web panels. Data included oversamples of non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, sexual minority (SM; i.e., gay, lesbian, bisexual, queer, or another non-heterosexual identity) and gender minority (GM; i.e., self-identified transgender/non-binary, or whose sex assigned at birth differed from current gender identity) individuals to ensure adequate subgroup representation. We restricted the analytic sample to respondents eligible for the reasons-for-not-drinking module (i.e., lifetime abstainers or past-year former drinkers; n = 1772). For the model including health risk awareness as predictors, we further limited analyses to respondents who provided risk awareness data on all 3 items (n = 1592). The privacy rights of human subjects were observed, informed consent was obtained from study participants, and all study procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the [MASKED FOR REVIEW].

Measures

Reasons for not drinking

Participants rated the importance of five reasons for not drinking: “Alcohol impairs people's control over themselves”; “drinking is bad for my health”; “one or both of my parents have or had a drinking problem”; “my family or friends get upset when I drink alcohol”; and “I do not like the taste or smell of alcohol.” Response options ranged from not at all important (1) to very important (5). For each reason, we constructed a dichotomous variable indicating a “very important” response vs. all other responses. These measures are based on identified reasons from the literature (Greenfield et al., 1989; Huang et al., 2011; Rosansky & Rosenberg, 2020), and several have been included in the NAS series since 1984 (Greenfield & Klingemann, 1998). We also have used these measures in previous work (Karriker-Jaffe et al., 2018).

Health beliefs

Participants indicated their awareness that excessive drinking increases the risk of cancer, hypertension, diabetes, depression, and anxiety by responding to the question: “Which of the following health conditions do you think can result from drinking too much alcohol?” For each of the five conditions, we coded answers as no (0), yes (1), or unsure (2) and used them as three-level categorical variables. These items are derived from the National Cancer Institute's Health Information National Trends Survey (Finney Rutten et al., 2020).

Demographic covariates

We adjusted for age (in years), race/ethnicity (White vs. American Indian/Native American (AI/AN), Asian/Pacific Islander, Black, and Hispanic), sexual identity (heterosexual vs. sexual minority (SM)), gender identity (cisman vs. ciswoman and gender minority (GM)), education (less than high school vs. high school graduate/GED (General Educational Development), some college, or college graduate or more), marital status (single/formerly married/divorced vs. married/cohabitating), religiosity (how important religion is in one's life: not at all (1) to very important (4)), and drinker status (lifetime abstainer vs. former drinker).

Statistical analyses

To address Aim 1 to estimate prevalences of reasons for not drinking among lifetime abstainers and former drinkers, we calculated weighted proportions of each reason for not drinking. To address Aim 2 to compare reasons for not drinking across subgroups, in bivariate analyses we used chi-square tests for categorical demographic groups, and a Wald test for mean differences by age, and in multivariate analyses we used logistic regression models for each dichotomized reason for not drinking. For Aim 3, we added the 5 items on awareness of alcohol's health effects to the multivariate logistic regression model for not drinking because “alcohol is bad for health”. We used design-based domain analysis (subpopulation estimation) to account for stratification, clustering, and weights for all analyses using the SVY command structure in STATA version 18 (STATA, 2023). Under this analytic framework, the proportions we report are weighted population estimates for US adults who are lifetime abstainers or former drinkers.

Aim 2: Comparisons of reasons for not drinking across population subgroups

In bivariate analysis (see Table 2), fewer GM individuals cited health reasons (35.7%, p < 0.01) or loss of control (40.8%, p < 0.05) for not drinking compared to cisgender men (63.9% and 65.9%, respectively) and cisgender women (65.8% and 61.3%, respectively). Higher proportions of Black, Hispanic, and AI/AN respondents endorsed “drinking is bad for my health” and “my family or friends get upset when I drink” compared to White respondents. Lower proportions of SM individuals endorsed “alcohol impairs people's control” (51.9% vs. 64.1%, p < 0.01), “my family or friends get upset when I drink” (14.2% vs 23.1%, p < 0.05), and “drinking is bad for my health” (48.4% vs. 65.6%, p < 0.001) compared to heterosexual individuals.

In multivariate analysis (see Table 3), older age was associated with a higher likelihood of endorsing “do not like the taste,” and cisgender women were more likely to endorse “one or both of my parents had or have a drinking problem” and “do not like the taste” as abstention reasons compared to cisgender men. Black and Hispanic people were more likely to endorse “alcohol is bad for health”, “my family or friends get upset when I drink” and “do not like the taste” than White people. People for whom religiosity was “very important” were more likely to endorse “alcohol impairs control” and “drinking is bad for health” compared to people for whom religion was “not at all important”. Lifetime abstainers were more likely to endorse “alcohol impairs control” and “do not like the taste” compared to former drinkers.

Aim 3: Associations between awareness of health risks and not drinking for health

Please see Supplementary Table 1 for frequencies of responses to the items on awareness of alcohol's health risks. In bivariate analysis, awareness that alcohol increases cancer (p < 0.01), hypertension (p < 0.001) and diabetes (p < 0.01) risk were each associated with not drinking because “drinking is bad for my health”, while awareness about alcohol increasing depression (p = 0.10) and anxiety (p = 0.07) were not. In multivariate analyses, only awareness that alcohol increases cancer risk (OR = 1.71, 1.06–2.73, p < 0.05) remained significantly associated with “drinking is bad for my health.” In this awareness-adjusted model, Hispanic respondents (OR = 1.84, 95% CI 1.20–2.82, p < 0.01) and respondents who rated religion as very important (OR = 2.06, 95% CI 1.37–3.10, p < 0.001) remained significantly more likely to endorse not drinking because “drinking is bad for my health’, while SM respondents showed significantly lower endorsement of not drinking because “drinking is bad for my health” (OR = 0.63, 95% CI 0.40–0.99, p < 0.05) compared to heterosexual respondents.

Discussion

This study described the prevalence of reasons for not drinking among a US sample of former drinkers and lifetime abstainers, made comparisons across population subgroups, and examined associations between awareness of health risks and endorsing not drinking because one believes it is bad for one's health. Overall, the most common reasons for not drinking related to concerns about health and impaired control. However, reasons for not drinking varied between population subgroups defined by race, ethnicity, gender, and sexual identity.

We found that racial and ethnic minority participants reported higher endorsement of health and social reasons for not drinking than White participants. These differences may reflect alcohol-related cultural and social norms. For example, abstinence rates are higher among Black and Hispanic people compared to White people (Center for Behavioral Health Statistics and Quality, S. A. M. H. S. A., 2025), and cultural factors such as religion and family cohesion in Black and Hispanic communities may prompt alcohol abstention for health and social reasons (Cano et al., 2018; Meyers et al., 2017). Indeed, we conducted a sensitivity analysis omitting religiosity from the awareness-adjusted model for not drinking because “alcohol is bad for health” (data not shown), and observed a significantly higher likelihood among Black people (vs. White people) to endorse this item, suggesting that religiosity may be a salient pathway for not drinking for health reasons in this community. However, Hispanic people remained more likely to endorse “drinking is bad for health” even when controlling for religiosity and awareness of alcohol's health effects, suggesting that factors other than religiosity and health awareness contribute to the higher endorsement of not drinking for health reasons among Hispanic non-drinkers that should be explored in future research. The higher endorsement of family- and social-context reasons among Black and Hispanic non-drinkers identifies family and social framings as candidate content for messaging research among these populations, which may complement individual health-risk framings.

We also found lower endorsement of health reasons for not drinking among SM respondents in the awareness-adjusted model, though not in the demographics-only model. The emergence of a significant negative association when awareness of alcohol's health harms was controlled for suggests that, at comparable levels of health awareness, SM non-drinkers are less likely than heterosexual non-drinkers to endorse not drinking due to health concerns. Several mechanisms may underlie this finding. First, SM communities in general show higher overall drinking rates (Hughes et al., 2020; McCabe et al., 2022), consistent with our finding of a lower non-drinking prevalence among SM respondents compared to heterosexual respondents (28.4% vs. 36.2%). Therefore, it may be that abstention among SM non-drinkers is influenced by factors other than general health concern. Although not assessed in the current study, other studies highlight factors that may be particularly relevant to SM individuals such as personal histories of alcohol problems (Allen & Mowbray, 2016; Fish & Exten, 2020) or observations of alcohol's negative impacts in their communities (Boyle et al., 2020; Karriker-Jaffe et al., 2021). Second, the salience of alcohol-as-health-threat may be lower in SM communities, where health discourse often centers other priorities such as HIV (Beckham et al., 2024), mental health (Marchi et al., 2026), and personal safety (DiGuiseppi et al., 2022). Indeed, we observed higher levels of awareness that alcohol increases cancer and depression risk among SM compared to heterosexual individuals (see Supplementary Table 1). Along with the finding that SM non-drinkers are less likely than heterosexual non-drinkers to endorse not drinking due to health concerns, this suggests that these health effects from alcohol use may not feel as salient or relevant to them. Our study, however, did not assess perceived risk or susceptibility, nor did we examine other health priorities beyond those related to alcohol, which future research should examine. Third, differences within the SM subgroup, including religious affiliation and trauma history, may contribute to differential endorsement patterns beyond what our multivariate models adjusted for. Overall, alcohol's health effects may not be the most resonant frame for not drinking among SM populations, though this is speculative and future work should investigate this further. Formative research with SM communities is needed to identify framings that carry greater salience.

We also examined how awareness of alcohol's health risks related to endorsing not drinking because alcohol is bad for health and observed only awareness of alcohol's effects on cancer risk was associated with this reason for not drinking. This finding is consistent with the Protection Motivation Theory: cancer is perceived as both higher-risk and less controllable than other common chronic conditions (Wang et al., 2009), such that awareness of alcohol as a cancer risk factor may increase one's perceived susceptibility and risk severity which can in turn motivate protective behavior in ways that conditions perceived as more manageable or treatable do not. Future longitudinal or experimental studies are needed to establish whether increasing public awareness of alcohol's cancer risk reduces alcohol use via the reason to protect one's health, particularly given the 2025 Surgeon General's Advisory on alcohol and cancer (US Department of Health and Human Services, 2025).

Limitations

Findings should be interpreted in the context of the study's limitations. We did not define “excessive drinking” in the awareness questions, which may have introduced variability in responses between respondents. Items assessing awareness of health risks associated with alcohol consumption appeared near the end of the survey and were not completed by some participants. Because missingness is related to survey completion, estimates may be biased if completion was correlated with factors not included in our models. Also, the reasons for not drinking items included in this survey were not exhaustive and there are other reasons for not drinking, including known ones such as not drinking for religious reasons and cost considerations, and more contemporary ones, such as GLP-1 use and concerns about weight gain, that should be explored in future research. Relatedly, our binary operationalization of the reasons for not drinking items captured the strongest endorsement (i.e., “very important endorsement vs. all others), producing conservative prevalence estimates and conservative subgroup comparisons. Future work should explore alternative operationalizations and the full range of responses. Survey responses on sensitive topics such as alcohol use may be influenced by social desirability bias. Furthermore, it is not possible to infer causality in our findings, such as if knowledge of alcohol's effects on cancer leads to not drinking for health reasons, given the study's cross-sectional design.

Conclusions

Among the reasons for not drinking assessed in this survey, health and self-control reasons appear to be the most salient reasons to abstain from alcohol for nondrinkers, with patterns varying across subgroups. These reasons for not drinking offer candidate content for future research testing alcohol reduction messaging, particularly relevant given current declines in US drinking. Awareness of alcohol's effect on cancer is positively associated with not drinking because one believes alcohol is bad for health, pointing to cancer-focused messaging as a candidate strategy for future research to test in current drinker populations to reduce alcohol use. Future research should also examine which framings carry greatest salience within specific subgroups, particularly given the differential endorsement patterns observed in this study.
