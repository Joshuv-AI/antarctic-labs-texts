Transmission problems and domain decompositions for non-autonomous parabolic equations on evolving domains
Amal Alphonse, Ana Djurdjevac, Emil Engström, Eskil Hansen

Abstract
Parabolic equations on evolving domains model a multitude of applications including various industrial processes such as the molding of heated materials. Such equations are numerically challenging as they require large-scale computations and the usage of parallel hardware. Domain decomposition is a common choice of numerical method for stationary domains, as it gives rise to parallel discretizations. In this study, we introduce a variational framework that extends the use of such methods to evolving domains. In particular, we prove that transmission problems on evolving domains are well posed and equivalent to the corresponding parabolic problems. This in turn implies that the standard non-overlapping domain decompositions, including the Robin–Robin method, become well defined approximations. Furthermore, we prove the convergence of the Robin–Robin method. The framework is based on a generalization of fractional Sobolev–Bochner spaces on evolving domains, time-dependent Steklov–Poincaré operators, and elements of the approximation theory for monotone maps.

Introduction

Industrial applications involving molding typically result in parabolic PDEs with the non-standard feature of evolving or moving spatial domains. In order to illustrate this, consider the production of railway tracks. This process includes two crucial steps, as depicted in
Figure

1
. First, the rail is shaped, which involves hot rolling a steel beam with a rectangular cross section into a rail with an H-shaped cross section. Second, the newly molded rail is solidified by spraying water on its surface. A basic model for the temperature
u
of the rail is then given by a non-autonomous parabolic equation on an evolving domain. That is, for the evolving domain
\{\Omega(t)\}_{t\in[0,\infty)}
, the temperature
u
satisfies

\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{u}(t)-\nabla\cdot\bigl(\alpha(t)\nabla u(t)\bigr)+\bigr(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{w}(t)+\beta(t)\bigl)u(t)&=f(t)&&\text{in }\Omega(t),\\
u(t)&=\eta(t)&&\text{on }\partial\Omega(t),\\
u(0)&=0&&\text{in }\Omega(0).\end{aligned}\right.

(1)

Here, the time evolution is described by the material derivative

\dot{u}(t)=\partial_{t}u(t)+\mathbf{w}(t)\cdot\nabla u(t),

and the domain
\Omega(t)
and its boundary
\partial\Omega(t)
evolve according to the known velocity field
\mathbf{w}
. The precise geometry and assumptions on the problem data will be specified in
Sections

2
and
5
. For simplicity we will only consider
\eta\equiv 0
in (
1
), but non-zero time-dependent boundary conditions can be handled as done in
Section

6
.

Figure 1:
Left image: sketch of a steel beam
\Omega
at time
t
being reshaped via hot rolling and thereafter solidified by water cooling.
Right image: Decomposition of the steel beam at time
t
, where
\Omega(t)
is decomposed into
\Omega_{1}(t)
and
\Omega_{2}(t)=\Omega_{2,1}(t)\cup\Omega_{2,2}(t)
.

Other applications of parabolic equations on evolving domains and hypersurfaces, to name a few, include: the dynamics of bubbles rising in liquid columns
[
19
]
governed by the Navier–Stokes equations; tumor growth models
[
5
]
consisting of reaction-diffusion equations on surfaces evolving via forced mean curvature flows; spinodal decomposition of binary polymer mixtures
[
23
]
governed by the Cahn–Hilliard equation.

Parabolic equations on evolving domains are numerically challenging due to the time-dependent geometry and the need for implicit time integration. This all results in large-scale computations that require the usage of parallel and distributed hardware. In the context of stationary domains, the domain decomposition method is a common choice that gives rise to parallel discretizations. The basic idea for these methods is to decompose the domain associated to the equation into subdomains and thereafter communicate the results via the boundaries to the adjacent subdomains. As an example, when shaping the train rail one could, at a fixed time, decompose the rail into three subdomains, where the middle one is a small region around the deformation zone, see
Figure

1
. The computational benefit of this is that each subdomain can be given a tailored spatial mesh. For example, the deformation subdomain typically requires a finer mesh than the other subdomains. For a general introduction to domain decomposition methods we refer to
[
31
,
34
]
.

From a mathematical perspective non-overlapping domain decomposition methods can be designed by first proving that the original parabolic equation
Equation

1
is equivalent to a so-called
transmission problem
. For two disjoint evolving subdomains
\Omega_{i}(t)
,
i=1,2
, such that
\overline{\Omega}(t)=\overline{\Omega}_{1}(t)\cup\overline{\Omega}_{2}(t)
and
\Gamma(t)=\partial\Omega_{1}(t)\cap\partial\Omega_{2}(t)
, the strong form of the transmission problem becomes

\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{u}_{i}(t)-\nabla\cdot\bigl(\alpha(t)\nabla u_{i}(t)\bigr)+\bigr(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{w}(t)+\beta(t)\bigl)u_{i}(t)&=f_{i}(t)&&\text{in }\Omega_{i}(t),\\
u_{i}(t)&=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega_{i}(t)\setminus\Gamma(t)\\
&&&\quad\text{for }i=1,2,\\[5.0pt]
u_{1}(t)&=u_{2}(t)&&\text{on }\Gamma(t),\\
\alpha(t)\nabla u_{1}(t)\cdot\nu_{1}(t)+\alpha(t)\nabla u_{2}(t)\cdot\nu_{2}(t)&=0&&\text{on }\Gamma(t),\end{aligned}\right.

(2)

where
\nu_{i}(t)
is the unit outward normal vector of
\partial\Omega_{i}(t)
,
f_{i}(t)=\left.f(t)\right|_{\Omega_{i}(t)}
, and
u_{i}(t)=\left.u(t)\right|_{\Omega_{i}(t)}
.

The non-overlapping domain decompositions can then be derived by approximating the transmission problem. For example, consider the classic Robin–Robin method, first introduced in
[
27
]
. By taking linear combinations of the last two equations in
Equation

2
, one has the equivalent Robin conditions

\alpha(t)\nabla u_{1}(t)\cdot\nu_{i}(t)+s_{0}u_{1}(t)=\alpha(t)\nabla u_{2}(t)\cdot\nu_{i}(t)+s_{0}u_{2}(t)\quad\text{on }\Gamma(t)\text{ for }i=1,2,

and a method parameter
s_{0}>0
. Alternating between the subdomains then gives the Robin–Robin method as computing
(u^{n}_{1},u^{n}_{2})
for
n=1,2,\ldots
with

\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{u}^{n}_{1}(t)-\nabla\cdot\bigl(\alpha(t)\nabla u^{n}_{1}(t)\bigr)+\bigr(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{w}(t)+\beta(t)\bigl)u_{1}^{n}(t)&=f_{1}(t)&&\text{in }\Omega_{1}(t),\\
u^{n}_{1}(t)&=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega_{1}(t)\setminus\Gamma(t),\\
\alpha(t)\nabla u^{n}_{1}(t)\cdot\nu_{1}(t)+s_{0}u^{n}_{1}(t)&=&&&\\
\alpha(t)\nabla u^{n-1}_{2}(t)\cdot\nu_{1}(t)+&s_{0}u^{n-1}_{2}(t)&&\text{on }\Gamma(t),\\[5.0pt]
\dot{u}^{n}_{2}(t)-\nabla\cdot\bigl(\alpha(t)\nabla u^{n}_{2}(t)\bigr)+\bigr(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{w}(t)+\beta(t)\bigl)u_{2}^{n}(t)&=f_{2}(t)&&\text{in }\Omega_{2}(t),\\
u^{n}_{2}(t)&=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega_{2}(t)\setminus\Gamma(t),\\
\alpha(t)\nabla u^{n}_{2}(t)\cdot\nu_{2}(t)+s_{0}u^{n}_{2}(t)&=&&&\\
\alpha(t)\nabla u^{n}_{1}(t)\cdot\nu_{2}(t)+&s_{0}u^{n}_{1}(t)&&\text{on }\Gamma(t).\end{aligned}\right.

(3)

Here,
u_{2}^{0}
is an initial guess and
u_{i}^{n}(t)
approximates
u_{i}(t)=\left.u(t)\right|_{\Omega_{i}(t)}
. Note that the Robin–Robin method is sequential, but the computation of each
u_{i}^{n}
can be implemented in parallel when
\Omega_{i}(t)
is a union of nonadjacent subdomains, as is the case in
Figure

1
.

The well posedness of parabolic equations on evolving domains can be derived via the framework

,
21
,
29
]
. This type of analysis of full space-time methods has also been extended to parabolic equations given on solution-dependent evolving surfaces
[
22
]
.

Domain decomposition methods have been proposed in the context of parallel time integrators, as surveyed in
[
15
]
, and there are several studies concerning the convergence and other theoretical aspects of space-time decomposition methods applied to parabolic equations on stationary domains, see, e.g.,
[
11
,
12
,
14
,
17
,
1
,
16
,
18
]
. However, there is no simple extension of the standard elliptic theory
[
31
]
to parabolic problems on stationary domains, and certainly not to evolving domains. The main difficulty is that the standard variational setting for parabolic problems, with solutions in the space denoted in
Equation

4
, prevents one from deriving the equivalence between
Equation

1
and
Equation

2
. This is caused by the fact that functions with the regularity of
Equation

4
cannot be “glued” together into a new function with the same regularity, see
[
7
, Example 2.14]
.

The goals of this paper are therefore to

(1)

prove the equivalence between
Equation

1
and
Equation

2
, by introducing a suitable variational formulation;

(2)

demonstrate that the standard non-overlapping domain decomposition methods, including the Robin–Robin method, are well defined on evolving domains;

(3)

illustrate the applicability of the framework by proving that the Robin–Robin method is convergent when applied to non-autonomous parabolic equations on evolving domains.

The main tool used to achieve the first goal is a new variational formulation with solutions in the evolving domain generalization of the space

H^{1/2}\bigl((0,\infty);L^{2}(\Omega)\bigr)\cap L^{2}\bigl((0,\infty);H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\bigr);

see
Sections

3
,
4
,
5
, and
6
. This resolves the issue with “gluing” functions together without losing regularity, see
Section

7
. The
H^{1/2}
-approach is due to
[
26
]
for smooth stationary domains and extended to stationary Lipschitz domains in
[
7
]
. We have also explored a
H^{1/2}
-setting for domain decomposition methods for parabolic problems on stationary domains in
[
11
,
12
]
. The study
[
6
]
has also used a
H^{1/2}
-variational formulation in order to analyze boundary integral operators for the heat equation on evolving domains. In that work, the standard time derivative is considered instead of the material derivative, which leads to a quite different setting than ours.

The second and third goals are reached by reformulating the transmission problems and the non-overlapping domain decomposition methods in terms of time-dependent Steklov–Poincaré operators. As these operators become coercive in the new
H^{1/2}
-setting, one can prove that several domain decomposition methods are well defined via the Lax–Milgram theorem and show convergence for the Robin–Robin method via the abstract convergence result
[
28
]
, see
Section

8
. Note that this new approach even yields convergence in a stronger norm compared to the previous results on stationary domains
[
11
,
12
]
.

The continuous analysis, derived in this paper, is also expected to hold in the finite-dimensional case that arises after discretizing in space and time, e.g., by using space-time finite elements, see, e.g.,
[
32
]
. However, in order to limit the scope of the paper we will restrict ourselves to the continuous case. The features and implementations of full domain decomposition finite element discretizations will be studied elsewhere.

2
Evolving domains

Let us describe the geometric setting. Assume that we have a bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
n=2,3
, such that

\overline{\Omega(0)}=\overline{\Omega_{1}(0)}\cup\overline{\Omega_{2}(0)},

where
\Omega_{i}(0)
,
i=1,2
, are bounded Lipschitz domains that are disjoint with common boundary

\Gamma(0)=\partial\Omega_{1}(0)\cap\partial\Omega_{2}(0).

We assume that
\Gamma(0)
is a
(n-1)
-dimensional Lipschitz manifold. Note that all results will also be valid for the case
n=1
, but with a slightly altered notation. It is also possible to replace
\Omega_{i}(0)
by a union of
K_{i}
nonadjacent subdomains, i.e.,

\Omega_{i}(0)=\bigcup_{k=1}^{K_{i}}\Omega_{i,k}(0),

see
Figure

1
, without any change to the analysis.

We now consider
\Omega(0)
to be evolving in time, resulting in a domain
\Omega(t)
at a later time
t
. To this end introduce the velocity field

\mathbf{w}\colon{\mathbb{R}^{0}_{+}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}

together with the corresponding transformation
\Phi\colon{\mathbb{R}^{0}_{+}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
given by

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Phi_{t}(x)=\mathbf{w}(t,\Phi_{t}(x)),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\quad\Phi_{0}(x)=x,

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We will assume that this evolution has the properties described below.

Assumption 2.1
.

The velocity field
\mathbf{w}
generates a transformation
\Phi
such that

(i)

\Phi
is an element in
C^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{0}_{+}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
and satisfies the bound

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\|\Phi_{t}\|_{C^{1}(\overline{B_{r}},\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C<\infty

for every fixed ball
B_{r}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|x|<r\}
, where
C=C(r)
.

(ii)

The inverse map
\Phi_{-}:(t,x)\mapsto(\Phi_{t})^{-1}(x)
exists and satisfies the same regularity and bound as
\Phi
.

The domain at time
t
is then defined as

\Omega(t)=\Phi_{t}(\Omega(0)).

The above properties of
\Phi
imply that
\Omega(t)
is also a bounded Lipschitz domain with boundary
\partial\Omega(t)=\Phi_{t}\bigl(\partial\Omega(0)\bigr)
. Furthermore, the Jacobian
D\Phi_{t}(x)=\{\partial_{x_{j}}\Phi_{t}(x)_{i}\}_{i,j}
is well defined and its inverse is given by

(D\Phi_{t})^{-1}(x)=(D\Phi_{-t})\bigl(\Phi_{t}(x)\bigr).

We also introduce the determinants

J_{t}(x)=\det\bigl(D\Phi_{t}(x)\bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad J_{-t}(x)=\det\bigl(D\Phi_{-t}(x)\bigr)=1/J_{t}\bigl(\Phi_{-t}(x)\bigr).

Regarding the partition of the domain, let us write

\Omega_{i}(t)=\Phi_{t}\bigl(\Omega_{i}(0)\bigr),\quad i=1,2,

for the evolution of the disjoint components. Once again, the assumed properties of
\Phi
give that all interiors are mapped to interiors and boundaries are mapped to boundaries, thus we obtain the bounded Lipschitz subdomains
\Omega_{i}(t)
with the boundaries

\partial\Omega_{i}(t)=\Phi_{t}\bigl(\partial\Omega_{i}(0)\bigl),\quad i=1,2.

We also have

\Phi_{t}\bigl(\Gamma(0)\bigr)=\Phi_{t}\bigl(\partial\Omega_{1}(0)\cap\partial\Omega_{2}(0)\bigr)=\Phi_{t}\bigl(\partial\Omega_{1}(0)\bigr)\cap\Phi_{t}\bigl(\partial\Omega_{2}(0)\bigr)=\partial\Omega_{1}(t)\cap\partial\Omega_{2}(t),

and hence the interface
\Gamma(0)
between
\Omega_{1}(0)
and
\Omega_{2}(0)
is mapped onto the interface between
\Omega_{1}(t)
and
\Omega_{2}(t)
, which we shall call
\Gamma(t)
, i.e.,

\Gamma(t)=\partial\Omega_{1}(t)\cap\partial\Omega_{2}(t).

The set
\Gamma(t)
is again assumed to be an
(n-1)
-dimensional Lipschitz manifold. This setup is exemplified in
Figure

2
.

Figure 2:
An example of an evolving domain decomposition with an interior domain
\Omega_{1}
and an exterior domain
\Omega_{2}
.

Next, we observe a few more properties of
\Phi
. Let
\Omega(0)\subset B_{r}
, then
Assumption

2.1
yields that

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\sup_{x\in B_{r}}\left.|\Phi_{t}(x)\right|\leq C.

Hence, there exists a ball
B_{R}
that contains all trajectories of
\Phi
starting in
\Omega(0)
, i.e.,

\Omega(t)\subset B_{R}
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{0}_{+}
. We can therefore view
\Phi
and
\Phi_{-}
as maps restricted to
B_{r}
and
B_{R}
, respectively, where

\Phi\in C_{b}\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{0}_{+};C^{1}(\overline{B_{r}},\mathbb{R}^{n})\bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi_{-}\in C_{b}\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{0}_{+};C^{1}(\overline{B_{R}},\mathbb{R}^{n})\bigr).

(5)

Furthermore, there are constants
c,C>0
such that

c|x-y|\leq|\Phi_{t}(x)-\Phi_{t}(y)|\leq C|x-y|\quad\text{and}\quad c\leq\left.|J_{t}(x)\right|\leq C

(6)

for all
x,y\in B_{r}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}^{0}_{+}
. These bounds also hold for
\Phi_{-t}
and
J_{-t}
with
x,y\in B_{R}
.

3
Abstract time-evolving function spaces

In this section we start by generalizing parts of the abstract framework of
[
3
,
2
]
to a semi-infinite time interval. We then define a notion of a generalized Sobolev–Bochner space that allows in particular for fractional-in-time exponents. This theory will enable us to define the function spaces that we need on the various evolving domains and boundaries from the previous section. We begin with a notion of compatibility, see also
[
2
, Assumption 2.1]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
X\equiv\{X(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}^{0}_{+}}
be a family of real separable Hilbert spaces and let

\phi_{t}\colon X(0)\to X(t)

be a linear and invertible map, with its inverse denoted by
\phi_{-t}
. The pair
(X,\phi)
is said to be
compatible
if

(i)

\phi_{0}
is the identity,

(ii)

there exists a constant
C
independent of
t\in\mathbb{R}^{0}_{+}
such that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\phi_{t}u\right\rVert_{X(t)}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{X(0)}

\displaystyle\text{for all }u\in X(0),

\displaystyle\left\lVert\phi_{-t}u\right\rVert_{X(0)}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{X(t)}

\displaystyle\text{for all }u\in X(t),

(iii)

for all
u\in X(0)
, the map
t\mapsto\left\lVert\phi_{t}u\right\rVert_{X(t)}
is measurable.

If
(X,\phi)
is compatible, then as done in
[
3
,
2
]
, we may define the
L^{2}
-space as

L_{X}^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})=\bigl\{u:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\bigcup_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}X(t)\times\{t\}:\,t\mapsto\bigl(\bar{u}(t),t\bigr)\text{ such that }\phi_{-}\bar{u}\in L^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};X(0)\bigr)\bigr\}.

We will abuse notation and simply write
u
instead of
\bar{u}
. This is a separable Hilbert space, with the inner product

(u,v)_{L^{2}_{X}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\bigl(u(t),v(t)\bigr)_{X(t)}\mathrm{d}t;

compare with
[
3
, Theorem 2.8]
. The map

\phi\colon L^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};X(0)\bigr)\to L^{2}_{X}(\mathbb{R}_{+})

then acts as an isomorphism with an equivalence of norms, see
[
3
, Lemma 2.10]
. In addition, if
(X,\phi)
is compatible then so is
(X^{*},(\phi_{-})^{*})
, where
\phi^{*}:X(t)^{*}\to X(0)^{*}
denotes the dual map of
\phi
. One also has that
L^{2}_{X}(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{*}\cong L^{2}_{X^{*}}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and

\displaystyle\langle g,v\rangle_{L^{2}_{X^{*}}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\times L^{2}_{X}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\langle g(t),v(t)\rangle_{X(t)^{*}\times X(t)}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\langle\phi^{*}g,\phi_{-}v\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};X(0)^{*})\times L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};X(0))}.

See
[
3
, p.6 and Lemmas 2.14–15]
for the above statement.

Definition 3.2
.

For a space
Y\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, we use the notation

Y_{X}(\mathbb{R}_{+})=\bigl\{v\in L_{X}^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}):\,\phi_{-}v\in Y\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};X(0)\bigr)\bigr\}.

The norm on
Y_{X}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
is defined as

\displaystyle\|v\|_{Y_{X}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}=\|\phi_{-}v\|_{Y(\mathbb{R}_{+};X(0))}.

A consequence of the above is that the restricted map

\phi\colon Y\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};X(0)\bigr)\to Y_{X}(\mathbb{R}_{+})

is by definition an isometric isomorphism. Note that the above definition of
\|\cdot\|_{Y_{X}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}
yields an equivalent norm to
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}_{X}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}
for
Y=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, due to compatibility of
(X,\phi)
. However, the norms do not necessarily coincide.

Lemma 3.3
.

If
(X,\phi)
is compatible and
Y\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};X(0)\bigr)
is a separable Hilbert space, then
Y_{X}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
is a separable Hilbert space.

Proof.

Take a Cauchy sequence
\{y_{n}\}
belonging to
Y_{X}(\mathbb{R}_{+}).
By definition, for every
\epsilon>0
, there exists an
N
such that if
n,m\geq N
, we have

\left\lVert\phi_{-}y_{n}-\phi_{-}y_{m}\right\rVert_{Y(\mathbb{R}_{+};X(0))}=\left\lVert y_{n}-y_{m}\right\rVert_{Y_{X}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\leq\epsilon.

As
Y\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};X(0)\bigr)
is a Hilbert space,
\phi_{-}y_{n}\to z
in
Y\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};X(0)\bigr)
to some
z\in Y\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};X(0)\bigr)
. Since
Y\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};X(0)\bigr)
is a subset of
L^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};X(0)\bigr)
and we have compatibility, it follows that
\phi z\in L^{2}_{X}.
Hence,

\phi z\in Y_{X}(\mathbb{R}_{+}).
We have

\left\lVert y_{n}-\phi z\right\rVert_{Y_{X}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}=\left\lVert\phi_{-}y_{n}-z\right\rVert_{Y(\mathbb{R}_{+};X(0))}\to 0

as
n
tends to infinity. Thus, the sequence
\{y_{n}\}
is convergent in
Y_{X}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. This shows that every Cauchy sequence converges, hence it is complete and a Hilbert space.

For the separability, if
\{e_{i}\}
is a countable orthonormal basis of
Y\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};X(0)\bigr)
, we can write an arbitrary
z\in Y\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};X(0)\bigr)
as
z=\sum(z,e_{i})_{Y(\mathbb{R}_{+};X(0))}e_{i}.
Then we have

\textstyle{\phi z=\sum_{i}(z,e_{i})_{Y(\mathbb{R}_{+};X(0))}\phi e_{i}=\sum_{i}(\phi z,\phi e_{i})_{Y_{X}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\phi e_{i}.}

Since any element of
Y_{X}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
can be written as
\phi z
for some
z\in Y\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};X(0)\bigr)
and because
L^{2}_{X}
and
L^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};X(0)\bigr)
are isomorphic via
\phi
, we see that
\{\phi e_{i}\}
is a countable dense subset and thus the space is separable.
∎

Consider a space
Y\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and assume that
Y\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};X(0)\bigr)
is a separable Hilbert space. Next, introduce two compatible pairs
(X,\phi)
and
(Z,\phi)
, where
Z(t)\hookrightarrow X(t)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. The subset

Y\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};X(0)\bigr)\cap L^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};Z(0)\bigr)\subseteq L^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};X(0)\bigr)

then also becomes a separable Hilbert space when equipped with the inner product

u,v\mapsto(u,v)_{Y(\mathbb{R}_{+};X(0))}+(u,v)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};Z(0))}.

The same holds for
Y_{X}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\cap L^{2}_{Z}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\subseteq L^{2}_{X}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
by
Lemma

3.3
. The restriction of the map
\phi\colon L^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};X(0)\bigr)\to L^{2}_{X}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, i.e.,

\phi\colon Y\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};X(0)\bigr)\cap L^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};Z(0)\bigr)\to Y_{X}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\cap L^{2}_{Z}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),

is an isometric isomorphism.

4
Compatibility for spaces defined on evolving domains

In this section we wish to apply the theory of
Section

3
to concrete function spaces on evolving domains and boundaries. This mainly involves checking that compatibility holds in the sense of
Definition

3.1
. The results here improve those present in
[
3
,
2
,
4
]
because we assume much weaker regularity on the domains and the evolution than there.

Let
M\equiv\{M(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}^{0}_{+}}
be a family of Lipschitz domains, with
M(0)\subset B_{r}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
M(t)\subset B_{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, playing the role of
\Omega_{i}
or
\Omega
. Similarly, let
S
be a family of
(n-1)
-dimensional Lipschitz manifolds representing either
\partial\Omega_{i}
,
\partial\Omega
, or
\Gamma
. We refer to
[
24
, Sections 6.2–3]
and
[
30
]
for an in-depth treatment of Lipschitz manifolds and the related surface integrals.

In the setting of evolving domains, we will consider two families of compatible pairs given by the maps
\phi
and
\psi
relating to function spaces over
M
and
S
, respectively.

Definition 4.1
.

The maps
\phi
and
\psi
, together with their inverses, are identified as the compositions

(i)

\phi_{t}u=u\circ\Phi_{-t}\quad\text{and}\quad\phi_{-t}u=u\circ\Phi_{t}
,

(ii)

\psi_{t}u=u\circ\bigl(\Phi_{-t}|_{S(t)}\bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad\psi_{-t}u=u\circ\bigl(\Phi_{t}|_{S(0)}\bigr)
,

respectively.

Note that this choice of
\phi
and
\psi
trivially fulfills the first compatibility property of
Definition

3.1
. For notational simplicity, we also make use of the notation
\phi,\psi
for matrix-valued maps, e.g., for
A:M(0)\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
we can write

\phi_{-t}A(x)=(A\circ\Phi_{t})(x)=A\bigl(\Phi_{t}(x)\bigr).

The task is now to prove the remaining compatibility properties for the function spaces arising when deriving the weak formulation of
Equation

1
.

4.1
Spaces on the interior

Lemma 4.2
.

If
Assumption

2.1
holds then
(L^{2}(M),\phi)
and
(H^{1}(M),\phi)
are compatible pairs.

Proof.

We prove the
H^{1}
case as the
L^{2}
case follows by a simpler argument. To prove the second property of
Definition

3.1
, consider
u\in H^{1}\bigl(M(0)\bigr)
and
v=\phi_{t}u\in H^{1}\bigl(M(t)\bigr)
. One then has, pointwise a.e., the formulae

\begin{gathered}\nabla(\phi_{-t}v)=(D\Phi_{t})^{T}\phi_{-t}(\nabla v)\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\nabla u\ =(D\Phi_{t})^{T}\phi_{-t}(\nabla\phi_{t}u)\quad\Leftrightarrow\\
\phi_{-t}(\nabla\phi_{t}u)=(D\Phi_{t})^{-\mathrm{T}}\nabla u\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\phi_{-t}(\nabla\phi_{t}u)=(\phi_{-t}D\Phi_{-t})^{\mathrm{T}}\nabla u.\end{gathered}

This yields the identities

\displaystyle\left\lVert\phi_{t}u\right\rVert_{H^{1}(M(t))}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{M(t)}|\phi_{t}u|^{2}+|\nabla\phi_{t}u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{M(0)}\phi_{-t}\bigl(|\phi_{t}u|^{2}+|\nabla\phi_{t}u|^{2}\bigr)|J_{t}|\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}

\displaystyle=\int_{M(0)}u^{2}|J_{t}|+|(\phi_{-t}D\Phi_{-t})^{\mathrm{T}}\nabla u|^{2}|J_{t}|\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}.

(7)

By
Assumption

2.1
we have
D\Phi_{-}\in C({\mathbb{R}^{0}_{+}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
,
|J|\in C({\mathbb{R}^{0}_{+}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
, and the bound

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\sup_{x\in M(0)}|(\phi_{-t}D\Phi_{-t})^{\mathrm{T}}(x)|_{2}^{2}\,|J_{t}(x)|

(8)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\sup_{x\in M(0),y\in M(t)}|(D\Phi_{-t})(y)|_{F}^{2}|J_{t}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\sup_{x\in B_{r},y\in B_{R}}\max_{i,j=1,\ldots,n}6n^{2}|\partial_{y_{j}}\Phi_{-t}(y)_{i}|^{2}\,|\partial_{x_{j}}\Phi_{t}(x)_{i}|^{n}

\displaystyle\leq C(n)\bigl(\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\|\Phi_{-t}\|_{C^{1}(\overline{B_{R}},\mathbb{R}^{n})}\bigr)^{2}\bigl(\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\|\Phi_{t}\|_{C^{1}(\overline{B_{r}},\mathbb{R}^{n})}\bigr)^{n}<\infty,

where
|\cdot|_{2}
and
|\cdot|_{F}
refer to the Euclidean and Frobenius matrix norms respectively. Hence, the second term of
Equation

7
is bounded by
C\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(M(0),\mathbb{R}^{n})}
and a similar argument for the first term yields that

\left\lVert\phi_{t}u\right\rVert_{H^{1}(M(t))}\leq C\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{1}(M(0))}\quad\text{for all }u\in H^{1}\bigl(M(0)\bigr),

with a constant
C
independent of
t\in\mathbb{R}^{0}_{+}
. The reverse bound, i.e.,
\left\lVert\phi_{-t}u\right\rVert_{H^{1}(M(0))}\leq C\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{1}(M(t))}
, holds as the very same properties are assumed for
\Phi
and
\Phi_{-}
.

We finally consider the third property of
Definition

3.1
. Again by
Assumption

2.1
, we have that
|J|\in L^{\infty}\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};L^{\infty}(M(0))\bigr)
and hence
|J|\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to L^{\infty}\bigl(M(0)\bigr)
is strongly measurable. By Pettis’ theorem, it is also weakly measurable. For an element
v\in L^{\infty}\bigl(M(0)\bigr)
the functional
g(v)=\int_{M(0)}u^{2}v\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}
clearly satisfies
g\in L^{\infty}\bigl(M(0)\bigr)^{*}
since
u\in L^{2}\bigl(M(0)\bigr)
. Thus

t\mapsto g(|J_{t}|)=\int_{M(0)}u^{2}|J_{t}|\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}

is measurable. This yields measurability of the first term of the integral
Equation

7
. A similar argument gives measurability of the entire integral.
∎

4.2
Spaces on the boundary

Let us now address function spaces defined over boundaries.

Lemma 4.3
.

If
Assumption

2.1
holds then
\bigl(L^{2}(S),\psi\bigr)
is a compatible pair.

Proof.

For simplicity, we first consider the case
n=2
. The curve
S(0)
is then the union of finitely many open, overlapping sets

S_{\ell}(0)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x(\xi)=\tilde{A}_{\ell}^{-1}\bigr(\xi,\sigma_{\ell}(\xi)\bigl)^{\mathrm{T}}\text{ for }\xi\in[-a,a]\}.

Here,
\sigma_{\ell}\colon[-a,a]\to\mathbb{R}
are Lipschitz continuous maps, and
\tilde{A}_{\ell}
are affine transformations, i.e.,
\tilde{A}_{\ell}x=A_{\ell}x+b_{\ell}
, where
A_{\ell}
are orthonormal matrices with
\mathrm{det}A_{\ell}=1
. The curve integral over
S(0)
can then be defined as

\int_{S(0)}v\,\mathrm{d}s_{0}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\int_{-a}^{a}(\varphi_{\ell}v)\bigl(x(\xi)\bigr)\,|x_{\xi}(\xi)|\,\mathrm{d}\xi,

with the tangential derivative
x_{\xi}=A_{\ell}^{-1}(1,\sigma^{\prime}_{\ell})^{\mathrm{T}}\in L^{\infty}\bigl(S_{\ell}(0),\mathbb{R}^{2}\bigr)
, for any partition of unity
\{\varphi_{\ell}\}\subset C(S(0))
of the curve
S(0)
. Note that the regularity of
x_{\xi}
follows by the Lipschitz continuity of
\sigma_{\ell}
, see
[
24
, Theorem 6.2.14]
. Also observe that the denominator
|x_{\xi}|^{2}=1+(\sigma^{\prime}_{\ell})^{2}
is nonzero.

Let
y(\xi)=\Phi_{t}x(\xi)
, then
y_{\xi}=\mathrm{D}\Phi_{t}(x)x_{\xi}
. Furthermore, if
\{\varphi_{\ell}\}\subset C\bigl(S(t)\bigr)
is a partition of unity of the curve
S(t)
then
\{\psi_{-t}\varphi_{\ell}\}\subset C\bigl(S(0)\bigr)
becomes a partition of unity of
S(0)
. We then have

\displaystyle\int_{S(t)}v(y)\,\mathrm{d}s_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\int_{-a}^{a}(\varphi_{\ell}v)\bigl(y(\xi)\bigr)\,|y_{\xi}(\xi)|\,\mathrm{d}\xi

(9)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\int_{-a}^{a}(\varphi_{\ell}v)\bigl(\Phi_{t}x(\xi))\,\left|\mathrm{D}\Phi_{t}\bigr(x(\xi)\bigr)\frac{x_{\xi}(\xi)}{|x_{\xi}(\xi)|}\right|\,|x_{\xi}(\xi)|\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{S(0)}(\psi_{-t}v)(x)\,\omega_{2,t}(x)\,\mathrm{d}s_{0},

where
\omega_{2,t}(x)=|\mathrm{D}\Phi_{t}(x)\tau(x)|
with
\tau\in L^{\infty}\bigl(S(0),\mathbb{R}^{2}\bigr)
denoting the normalized tangent vector of
S(0)
. By
Assumption

2.1
we obtain that
\omega_{2}\in L^{\infty}\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};L^{\infty}\bigl(S(0))\bigr)
as

\displaystyle\omega_{2,t}(x)\leq|\mathrm{D}\Phi_{t}(x)|_{2}|\tau(x)|_{2}\leq\sup_{y\in S(0)}|\mathrm{D}\Phi_{t}(y)|_{F}\cdot 1

(10)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\sup_{y\in B_{r}}\max_{i,j=1,\ldots,n}n|\partial_{y_{j}}\Phi_{t}(y)_{i}|\leq C\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\|\Phi_{t}\|_{C^{1}(\overline{B_{r}},\mathbb{R}^{n})}<\infty

for a.e.
x\in S(0)
and every
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

From
Equation

9
it is clear that
\psi_{-t}v\in L^{1}\bigl(S(0)\bigr)
if
v\in L^{1}\bigl(S(t)\bigr)
and
\psi_{t}v\in L^{1}\bigl(S(t)\bigr)
if
v\in L^{1}\bigl(S(0)\bigr)
. Next, consider
u\in L^{2}\bigl(S(0)\bigr)
. Replacing
v
by

\psi_{t}u^{2}
in
Equation

9
yields that

\|\psi_{t}u\|_{L^{2}(S(t))}=\|u\,\sqrt{\omega_{2,t}}\|_{L^{2}(S(0))}\leq C\|u\|_{L^{2}(S(0))}.

Here, the constant
C
is uniform in time by
Equation

10
. The reverse bound of
Definition

3.1
follows by simply replacing
S(t)
with
S(0)
and vice versa in the above argumentation.

The measurability, i.e., the third property of
Definition

3.1
, can be shown in a similar way to
Lemma

4.2
. Hence,
\bigl(L^{2}(S),\psi\bigr)
is a compatible pair for
n=2
.

The compatibility for
n=3
follows in the same fashion with

S_{\ell}(0)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:x(\xi)=\tilde{A}_{\ell}^{-1}\bigr(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\sigma_{\ell}(\xi)\bigl)^{\mathrm{T}}\text{ for }\xi\in[-a,a]^{2}\},

x_{\xi}
replaced by
\partial_{\xi_{1}}x\,\times\,\partial_{\xi_{2}}x
, and
\omega_{2,t}
replaced by

\omega_{3,t}\bigr(x(\xi)\bigr)=\frac{|\mathrm{D}\Phi_{t}\bigl(x(\xi)\bigr)\partial_{\xi_{1}}x(\xi)\,\times\,\mathrm{D}\Phi_{t}\bigl(x(\xi)\bigr)\partial_{\xi_{2}}x(\xi)|}{|\partial_{\xi_{1}}x(\xi)\,\times\,\partial_{\xi_{2}}x(\xi)|}.

Note that the replaced terms are all in
L^{\infty}
,
|\partial_{\xi_{1}}x\,\times\,\partial_{\xi_{2}}x|^{2}=1+\sum_{i=1}^{2}(\partial_{\xi_{i}}\sigma_{\ell})^{2}
is nonzero, and the bound
Equation

10
holds as
Ax\times Ay=\det(A)A^{-\mathrm{T}}x\times y
.
∎

Regarding Sobolev spaces over the manifolds
S
, we introduce the space
H^{1/2}\bigl(S(t)\bigr)
defined as

\begin{gathered}H^{1/2}\bigl(S(t)\bigr)=\{u\in L^{2}\bigl(S(t)\bigr):\|u\|_{H^{1/2}(S(t))}<\infty\}\quad\text{with}\\
\|u\|_{H^{1/2}(S(t))}=\Big(|u|_{H^{1/2}(S(t))}^{2}+\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(S(t))}\Big)^{1/2}\quad\text{and}\\
|u|_{H^{1/2}(S(t))}=\Big(\int_{S(t)}\int_{S(t)}\frac{\left.|u(x)-u(y)\right|^{2}}{\left.|x-y\right|^{d}}\,\mathrm{d}s_{t}\,\mathrm{d}s_{t}\Big)^{1/2}.\end{gathered}

Denoting the extension by zero from
\Gamma(t)
to
\partial\Omega_{i}(t)
by
e_{\partial\Omega_{i}(t)}
, we also define the Lions–Magenes space as

\begin{gathered}\Lambda(t)=\{u\in L^{2}\bigl(\Gamma(t)\bigr):e_{\partial\Omega_{i}(t)}u\in H^{1/2}\bigl(\partial\Omega_{i}(t)\bigr)\}\quad\text{with}\\
\|u\|_{\Lambda(t)}=\|e_{\partial\Omega_{i}(t)}u\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega_{i}(t))}.\end{gathered}

By
[
34
, Lemma A.8]
one has the identification
\Lambda(t)\cong[H^{1/2}_{0}\bigl(\Gamma(t)\bigr),L^{2}\bigl(\Gamma(t)\bigr)]_{1/2}
, i.e.,
\Lambda(t)
is independent of
i=1,2
. The space
H^{1/2}\bigl(S(t)\bigr)
is a separable Hilbert space, and the same therefore holds for
\Lambda(t)
.

Lemma 4.4
.

If
Assumption

2.1
holds then
\bigl(H^{1/2}(S),\psi\bigr)
is a compatible pair.

Proof.

The proof is not dissimilar to the discussion in
[
4
, §5.4.1]
. With the same notation as in
Lemma

4.3
we have

\displaystyle|\psi_{t}u|_{H^{1/2}(S(t))}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{S(t)}\int_{S(t)}\frac{|\psi_{t}u(y)-\psi_{t}u(\hat{y})|^{2}}{|y-\hat{y}|^{n}}\,\mathrm{d}s_{t}\mathrm{d}s_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{S(t)}\int_{S(t)}\frac{|u(\Phi_{-t}(y))-u(\Phi_{-t}(\hat{y}))|^{2}}{|y-\hat{y}|^{n}}\,\mathrm{d}s_{t}\mathrm{d}s_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{S(0)}\int_{S(0)}\frac{|u(x)-u(\hat{x})|^{2}}{|\Phi_{t}(x)-\Phi_{t}(\hat{x})|^{n}}\omega_{n,t}(x)\omega_{n,t}(\hat{x})\,\mathrm{d}s_{0}\mathrm{d}s_{0}

(11)

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{S(0)}\int_{S(0)}\frac{|u(x)-u(\hat{x})|^{2}}{|x-\hat{x}|^{n}}\omega_{n,t}(x)\omega_{n,t}(\hat{x})\,\mathrm{d}s_{0}\mathrm{d}s_{0}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{S(0)}\int_{S(0)}\frac{|u(x)-u(\hat{x})|^{2}}{|x-\hat{x}|^{n}}\,\mathrm{d}s_{0}\mathrm{d}s_{0},

where the constant
C
is uniform in time. The bounds follow as
\Phi_{t}
and
\omega_{n}
fulfill
Equations

6
and
10
, respectively. Since the bound for
\|\psi_{t}u\|_{L^{2}(S(t))}
follows as in
Lemma

4.3
we have
\psi_{t}\colon H^{1/2}\bigl(S(0)\bigr)\to H^{1/2}\bigl(S(t)\bigr)
and

\|\psi_{t}u\|_{H^{1/2}(S(t))}\leq C\|u\|_{H^{1/2}(S(0))}.

A similar argument gives the same result for
\psi_{-t}
.

Finally, we prove the third property of
Definition

3.1
. By
Assumption

2.1
we have that
\Phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{0}_{+}\to C\bigl(S(0)\bigr)
and
\omega_{n}\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to L^{\infty}\bigl(S(0)\bigr)
are continuous. Hence, the integrand in (
11
) is continuous in time for all fixed
x,\hat{x}
excluding the zeros set where
\omega_{n,t}
is undefined and
x=\hat{x}
. The set violating the latter condition is again of measure zero. The said integrand is bounded from above by

C\frac{|u(x)-u(\hat{x})|^{2}}{|x-\hat{x}|^{n}}

via
Equations

6
and
10
, and this function is integrable and independent of time. We may therefore apply Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem to deduce that the right-hand side of (
11
) (and hence also
\|\psi_{t}u\|_{H^{1/2}(S(t))}
) is continuous with respect to
t
, thereby yielding the sought-after measurability.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

If
Assumption

2.1
holds then

e_{\partial\Omega_{i}(t)}\psi_{t}u=\psi_{t}e_{\partial\Omega_{i}(0)}u

for all
u\in L^{2}\bigl(\Gamma(0)\bigr)
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

4.3
, one obtains that
e_{\partial\Omega_{i}(t)}\psi_{t}u
and
\psi_{t}e_{\partial\Omega_{i}(0)}u
are in
L^{2}\bigl(\partial\Omega_{i}(t)\bigr)
.
We therefore have, for a.e.
x\in\partial\Omega_{i}(t)
,

\displaystyle(e_{\partial\Omega_{i}(t)}\psi_{t}u)(x)=\begin{cases}(\psi_{t}u)(x)&\text{if $x\in\Gamma(t)$}\\
0&\text{if $x\in\partial\Omega_{i}(t)\backslash\Gamma(t)$},\end{cases}

while on the other hand

\displaystyle(\psi_{t}e_{\partial\Omega_{i}(0)}u)(x)=(e_{\partial\Omega_{i}(0)}u)\bigl(\Phi_{-t}(x)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}u(\Phi_{-t}(x))&\text{if $\Phi_{-t}(x)\in\Gamma(0)$}\\
0&\text{if $\Phi_{-t}(x)\in\partial\Omega_{i}(0)\backslash\Gamma(0)$}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(\psi_{t}u)(x)&\text{if $x\in\Gamma(t)$}\\
0&\text{if $x\in\partial\Omega_{i}(t)\backslash\Gamma(t)$}.\end{cases}

Here we used that
\Phi_{t}\bigl(\partial\Omega_{i}(0)\bigr)=\partial\Omega_{i}(t)
and
\Phi_{t}\bigl(\Gamma(0)\bigr)=\Gamma(t)
.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

If
Assumption

2.1
holds then
(\Lambda,\psi)
is a compatible pair.

Proof.

Let
u\in\Lambda(0)
then
e_{\partial\Omega_{i}(0)}u\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega_{i}(0))
. By
Lemmas

4.4
and
4.5
, one obtains that

\psi_{t}e_{\partial\Omega_{i}(0)}u=e_{\partial\Omega_{i}(t)}\psi_{t}u\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega_{i}(t)).

From the definition of
\Lambda(t)
(cf.
[
10
, Lemma 4.1]
), there is a unique element
v\in\Lambda(t)
such that
e_{\partial\Omega_{i}(t)}v=e_{\partial\Omega_{i}(t)}\psi_{t}u
, i.e.,
\psi_{t}u=v
. Hence,
\psi_{t}
maps
\Lambda(0)
into
\Lambda(t)
.
By the definition of
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\Lambda(t)}
together
Lemmas

4.4
and
4.5
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\psi_{t}u\right\rVert_{\Lambda(t)}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert e_{\partial\Omega_{i}(t)}\psi_{t}u\right\rVert_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega_{i}(t))}=\left\lVert\psi_{t}e_{\partial\Omega_{i}(0)}u\right\rVert_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega_{i}(t))}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert e_{\partial\Omega_{i}(0)}u\right\rVert_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega_{i}(0))}=C\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\Lambda(0)}.

The same line of reasoning can be made for
\psi_{-}
and the measurability of
t\mapsto\left\lVert\psi_{t}u\right\rVert_{\Lambda(t)}
follows just as in
Lemma

4.4
. Hence,
(\Lambda,\psi)
is a compatible pair.
∎

To tie together the functions over
M
and

T_{\partial\Omega_{i}(t)}:H^{1}\bigl(\Omega_{i}(t)\bigr)\rightarrow H^{1/2}\bigl(\partial\Omega_{i}(t)\bigr)

The spaces

by

T_{i,t}\colon V_{i}(t)\rightarrow\Lambda(t),\qquad u\mapsto\left.(T_{\partial\Omega_{i}(t)}u)\right|_{\Gamma(t)},

[
10
, Lemma 4.4]
.

Lemma 4.7
.

If
Assumption

2.1
holds then

T_{\partial\Omega_{i}(t)}\phi_{t}u=\psi_{t}T_{\partial\Omega_{i}(0)}u\quad\text{and}\quad T_{i,t}\phi_{t}v=\psi_{t}T_{i,0}v

for all
u\in H^{1}\bigl(\Omega_{i}(0)\bigr)
and
v\in V_{i}(0)
.

Proof.

For
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega_{i}(0)})
, we have

T_{\partial\Omega_{i}(t)}(\phi_{t}\varphi)=T_{\partial\Omega_{i}(t)}\bigl(\varphi\circ\Phi_{-t})=(\varphi\circ\Phi_{-t})|_{\partial\Omega_{i}(t)},

with the second equality because
\varphi\circ\Phi_{-t}\in C(\overline{\Omega_{i}(0)})
due to
Equation

5
. We also have

\psi_{t}(T_{\partial\Omega_{i}(0)}\varphi)=\psi_{t}(\varphi|_{\partial\Omega_{i}(0)})=\varphi|_{\partial\Omega_{i}(0)}\circ(\Phi_{-t}|_{\partial\Omega_{i}(t)})=(\varphi\circ\Phi_{-t})|_{\partial\Omega_{i}(t)},

with the final equality because
\Phi_{t}
maps
\partial\Omega_{i}(0)
to
\partial\Omega_{i}(t)
.

For an arbitrary
u\in H^{1}\bigl(\Omega_{i}(0)\bigr)
take a sequence
\{u_{n}\}\subset C^{\infty}(\overline{\partial\Omega_{i}(0)})
converging to
u
. One then has the equality

T_{\partial\Omega_{i}(t)}(\phi_{t}u_{n})=\psi_{t}(T_{\partial\Omega_{i}(0)}u_{n}).

The trace operators
T_{\partial\Omega_{i}(s)}:H^{1}\bigl(\Omega_{i}(s)\bigr)\to H^{1/2}\bigl(\partial\Omega_{i}(s)\bigr)
,
s=0,t
, are continuous and, by
Lemmas

4.2
and
4.4
, the same holds for
\phi_{t}:H^{1}\bigl(\Omega_{i}(0)\bigr)\to H^{1}\bigl(\Omega_{i}(t)\bigr)
and
\psi_{t}:H^{1/2}\bigl(\partial\Omega_{i}(0)\bigr)\to H^{1/2}\bigl(\partial\Omega_{i}(t)\bigr)
. Hence, we obtain that the lemma’s first equality holds in
H^{1/2}\bigl(\partial\Omega_{i}(t)\bigr)
. The second equality follows by
Lemma

4.6
and the same line of argumentation. The only difference is that the restrictions are made to
\Gamma
instead of
\partial\Omega_{i}
, and
\{u_{n}\}\subset\{\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\partial\Omega_{i}(0)}):\left.\varphi\right|_{\partial\Omega_{i}(0)\backslash\Gamma(0)}=0\}
.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

If
Assumption

2.1
holds then
\bigl(H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),\phi\bigr)
,
\bigl(H^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{i}),\phi\bigr)
, and
(V_{i},\phi)
are all compatible pairs.

Proof.

We prove the
V_{i}
case as the others follow directly by combining
Lemmas

4.2
and
4.7
. For
u\in V_{i}(0)\subset H^{1}\bigl(\Omega_{i}(0)\bigr)
we have
\phi_{t}u\in H^{1}\bigl(\Omega_{i}(t)\bigr)
and
\left.(T_{\partial\Omega_{i}(0)}u_{n})\right|_{\partial\Omega_{i}(0)\backslash\Gamma(0)}=0
. As
\{\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\partial\Omega_{i}(0)}):\left.\varphi\right|_{\partial\Omega_{i}(0)\backslash\Gamma(0)}=0\}
is dense in
V_{i}(0)
, we can choose a sequence
\{u_{n}\}
in this dense subset such that it converges to
u
.
Lemma

4.7
then implies that

\displaystyle\left.(T_{\partial\Omega_{i}(t)}\phi_{t}u_{n}\bigr)\right|_{\partial\Omega_{i}(t)\backslash\Gamma(t)}

\displaystyle=\left.(\psi_{t}T_{\partial\Omega_{i}(0)}u_{n})\right|_{\partial\Omega_{i}(t)\backslash\Gamma(t)}

(12)

\displaystyle=(\left.u_{n}\right|_{\partial\Omega_{i}(0)})\circ(\left.\Phi_{-t}\right|_{\partial\Omega_{i}(t)\backslash\Gamma(t)})=\left.u_{n}\right|_{{\partial\Omega_{i}(0)\backslash\Gamma(0)}}=0.

Definition 5.1
.

The weak form of

1

(which all have homogeneous initial conditions) can be formulated as

The weak form can be derived as follows. First, note that we formally have the identity

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}u\bigl(t,\Phi_{t}(x)\bigr)=(\partial_{t}u+\nabla u\cdot\mathbf{w})\bigl(t,\Phi_{t}(x)\bigr)\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\phi_{-t}u=\phi_{-t}\dot{u}

\frac{d}{dt}J_{t}(x)=\nabla\cdot\mathbf{w}\bigl(t,\Phi_{t}(x)\bigr)J_{t}(x)\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}J_{t}=\phi_{-t}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{w})J_{t}.

(15)

Observe that if Jacobi’s formula holds then it implies that
\mathrm{d}|J_{t}|/\mathrm{d}t=\phi_{-t}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{w})|J_{t}|
.
Consider sufficiently regular functions
u,v
such that
\phi_{-t}\bigl(u(t)v(t)\bigr)
decays sufficiently rapidly as

tends to infinity and
\left.v(t)\right|_{S(t)}=0

Integration by parts in time together with

15

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{M(t)}\dot{u}v\,\mathrm{d}x_{t}\mathrm{d}t=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{M(0)}\phi_{-t}\dot{u}\,\phi_{-t}v|J_{t}|\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{M(0)}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\phi_{-t}u)\,\phi_{-t}v|J_{t}|\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{M(0)}\phi_{-t}u\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\phi_{-t}v|J_{t}|)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\int_{M(0)}\left.\phi_{-}(uv)\right|_{t=\tau}|J_{\tau}|\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}-\int_{M(0)}\left.(uv)\right|_{t=0}|J_{0}|\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{M(0)}\phi_{-t}u\,\bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\phi_{-t}v)|J_{t}|+\phi_{-t}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{w})|J_{t}|\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle-\int_{M(0)}\left.(uv)\right|_{t=0}\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{M(0)}\phi_{-t}u\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\phi_{-t}v)|J_{t}|\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}\mathrm{d}t-\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{M(t)}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{w})uv\,\mathrm{d}x_{t}\mathrm{d}t

The above equality and integration by parts in space yields

\displaystyle\quad=-\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{M(0)}\phi_{-t}u\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\phi_{-t}v)|J_{t}|\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}\mathrm{d}t-\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{M(t)}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{w})uv\,\mathrm{d}x_{t}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{M(0)}\left.(uv)\right|_{t=0}\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{M(t)}\alpha\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{w}+\beta)uv\,\mathrm{d}x_{t}\mathrm{d}t-\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\partial M(t)}\alpha\nabla u\cdot n\,v\,\mathrm{d}s_{t}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad=-\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{M(0)}\phi_{-t}u\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\phi_{-t}v)|J_{t}|\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}\mathrm{d}t+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{M(t)}\alpha\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+\beta uv\,\mathrm{d}x_{t}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{M(0)}\left.(uv)\right|_{t=0}\,\mathrm{d}x_{0},

(16)

which justifies the definition.

6
Temporal

-setting for evolving domains

In the rest of the paper we will make use of the Sobolev–Bochner spaces stated in

1
. Here,

\begin{gathered}H^{s}(I)=\{u\in L^{2}(I):\|u\|_{H^{s}(I)}<\infty\}\quad\text{with}\quad\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s}(I)}=|u|_{H^{s}(I)}^{2}+\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(I)}\\
\text{and}\quad|u|^{2}_{H^{s}(I)}=\int_{I}\int_{I}\frac{\left.|u(\tau)-u(t)\right|^{2}}{|\tau-t|^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathrm{d}t,\end{gathered}

for
s=1/2
or
s=1/4
and on the time intervals
I=\mathbb{R}_{+}
or
I=\mathbb{R}
. Furthermore,
H_{(0,.)}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
is the temporal Lions–Magenes space, i.e.,

H_{(0,.)}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})=\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}):e_{\mathbb{R}}u\in H^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})\}\quad\text{with}\quad\|u\|_{H_{(0,.)}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}=\|e_{\mathbb{R}}u\|_{H^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})},

where

denotes the extension by zero from
\mathbb{R}_{+}
to
\mathbb{R}
. We will also use the notation

\begin{gathered}a_{i}(u,v)=d_{i}(\phi_{-}u,\phi_{-}v)+c_{i}(u,v),\quad\text{where}\\
d_{i}(u,v)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\Omega_{i}(0)}u\,\partial_{t}v\,|J_{t}|\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}\mathrm{d}t\quad\text{and}\quad c_{i}(u,v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\Omega_{i}(t)}\alpha\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+\beta uv\,\mathrm{d}x_{t}\mathrm{d}t.\end{gathered}

We denote the corresponding bilinear forms on the whole domain
\Omega
by
a,d,c
.

7
Transmission problems on evolving domains

In this section we analyze the transmission problem on the evolving domain decomposition
Equation

2
. The weak formulation of
Equation

2
is to find
(u_{1},u_{2})\in W_{1}\times W_{2}
such that

\left\{\begin{aligned} a_{i}(u_{i},v_{i})&=\langle f_{i},v_{i}\rangle&&\text{for all }v_{i}\in\tilde{W}_{i}^{0},\,i=1,2,\\
T_{1}u_{1}&=T_{2}u_{2},&&\\
\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}a_{i}(u_{i},F_{i}\mu)-\langle f_{i},F_{i}\mu\rangle&=0&&\text{for all }\mu\in Z.\end{aligned}\right.

(34)

We introduce the spatial restriction operators

q_{i,0}\colon u\mapsto\left.u\right|_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega_{i}(0)}\quad\text{and}\quad q_{i}=\phi q_{i,0}\phi_{-},

where the maps
q_{i}\colon W\to W_{i}
and
q_{i}\colon\tilde{W}\to\tilde{W}_{i}
both become well defined and continuous. With the derived setting we are now able to “cut” and “glue together” functions without losing spatial or temporal regularity.

Lemma 7.1
.

Suppose that
Assumption

2.1
holds. If
u\in W
, then

(u_{1},u_{2})=(q_{1}u,q_{2}u)\in W_{1}\times W_{2}

and
T_{1}u_{1}=T_{2}u_{2}
. Conversely, if
(u_{1},u_{2})\in W_{1}\times W_{2}
and
T_{1}u_{1}=T_{2}u_{2}
, then
u=\phi v
with

v=\{\phi_{-}u_{1}\text{ on }\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega_{1}(0),\phi_{-}u_{2}\text{ on }\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega_{2}(0)\}

satisfies
u\in W
. Moreover, the same holds for
u\in\tilde{W}
and
(u_{1},u_{2})\in\tilde{W}_{1}\times\tilde{W}_{2}
.

Proof.

First, suppose that
u\in W
. Then, as
\phi_{-}
is an isomorphism on intersection spaces, we have

\phi_{-}u\in H_{(0,\cdot)}^{1/2}\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};L^{2}(\Omega(0))\bigr)\cap L^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};H^{1}_{0}(\Omega(0))\bigr).

It follows from
[
12
, Lemma 5]
that

q_{i,0}(\phi_{-}u)\in H_{(0,\cdot)}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};L^{2}(\Omega_{i}(0)))\cap L^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};V_{i}(0)\bigr)

and
T_{1,0}\left.(\phi_{-}u)\right|_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega_{1}(0)}=T_{2,0}\left.(\phi_{-}u)\right|_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega_{2}(0)}
. Applying the isomorphism
\phi
yields that
u_{i}=q_{i}u\in W_{i}
and the definition of
T_{i}
gives
T_{1}u_{1}=T_{2}u_{2}
.

Conversely, suppose that
u_{i}\in W_{i}
and
T_{1}u_{1}=T_{2}u_{2}
. Then we have

\phi_{-}u_{i}\in H_{(0,\cdot)}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};L^{2}(\Omega_{i}(0)))\cap L^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};V_{i}(0)\bigr)

and by the definition of
T_{i}
we have
T_{1,0}\phi_{-}u_{1}=T_{2,0}\phi_{-}u_{2}
. Therefore, it follows from
[
12
, Lemma 5]
that

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=\{\phi_{-}u_{1}\text{ on }\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega_{1}(0),\phi_{-}u_{2}\text{ on }\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega_{2}(0)\}

\displaystyle\in H_{(0,\cdot)}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};L^{2}(\Omega(0)))\cap L^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};H^{1}_{0}(\Omega(0))\bigr).

Thus, we have
u=\phi v\in W
. The argument for
\tilde{W}
and
\tilde{W}_{i}
is the same since it also holds on the reference cylinder
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega(0)
according to
[
12
, Lemma 5]
.
∎

Lemma 7.2
.

If
Assumptions

2.1
,
5.2
, and
5.3
hold, then

a(u,v)=\sum_{i=1}^{2}a_{i}(q_{i}u,q_{i}v)

for all
u\in W
and
v\in\tilde{W}
.

Proof.

First, let
u\in H^{1/2}_{(0,\cdot)}\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};L^{2}(\Omega)\bigr)
and consider the restriction operator

q_{i,0}\colon H^{1/2}\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};L^{2}(\Omega(0))\bigr)\to H^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};L^{2}(\Omega_{i}(0))),

which is continuous. The same holds if
H^{1/2}
is replaced by
H_{(0,\cdot)}^{1/2}
. For
v\in\phi_{-}(\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{0})
we have
q_{i,0}(\partial_{t}v)=\partial_{t}(q_{i,0}v)
and

\displaystyle d(u,v)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\Omega(0)}u\partial_{t}v|J_{t}|\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}\mathrm{d}t=\sum_{i=1}^{2}-\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\Omega_{i}(0)}u\partial_{t}(q_{i,0}v)|J_{t}|\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{2}d_{i}\bigl(q_{i,0}u,q_{i,0}v\bigr).

By
Lemma

6.3
, we have for every

that there exists a sequence

that converges to

. Then the continuity of

,

,
\phi_{-}
, and

gives

\displaystyle d(\phi_{-}u,\phi_{-}v)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}d(\phi_{-}u,\phi_{-}v_{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{2}d_{i}\bigl(q_{i,0}(\phi_{-}u),q_{i,0}(\phi_{-}v_{n})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{2}d_{i}\bigl(q_{i,0}(\phi_{-}u),q_{i,0}(\phi_{-}v)\bigr).

Second, let

. As

for a.e.

, one obtains

\begin{gathered}\left.\bigl(\nabla u(t)\bigr)\right|_{\Omega_{i}(t)}=\nabla\bigl(\left.u(t)\right|_{\Omega_{i}(t)}\bigr)\quad\text{and}\\
\left.u(t)\right|_{\Omega_{i}(t)}=u(t)\circ\bigl(\left.\Phi_{t}\right|_{\Omega_{i}(0)}\circ\left.\Phi_{-t}\right|_{\Omega_{i}(t)}\bigr)=\left.\bigl(\phi_{-t}u(t)\bigr)\right|_{\Omega_{i}(0)}\circ\left.\Phi_{-t}\right|_{\Omega_{i}(t)}=(q_{i}u)(t)\end{gathered}

for a.e.

. Hence, for every

we have

\displaystyle c(u,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\Omega(t)}\alpha\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+\beta uv\,\mathrm{d}x_{t}\mathrm{d}t=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\Omega_{i}(t)}\left.\bigl(\alpha\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+\beta uv\bigr)\right|_{\Omega_{i}(t)}\,\mathrm{d}x_{t}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\Omega_{i}(t)}\alpha\nabla(q_{i}u)\cdot\nabla(q_{i}v)+\beta(q_{i}u)(q_{i}v)\,\mathrm{d}x_{t}\mathrm{d}t=\sum_{i=1}^{2}c_{i}\bigl(q_{i}u,q_{i}v\bigr).

Combining these results for

and

gives the sought-after equality.

8
Steklov–Poincaré operators and convergence of the Robin–Robin scheme on evolving domains

The Steklov–Poincaré operators
S_{i},S\colon Z\to Z^{*}
are defined as

\langle S_{i}\eta,\mu\rangle=a_{i}(F_{i}\eta,F_{i}\mu)

and
S=S_{1}+S_{2}
. Moreover, we define the functionals
\chi_{i},\chi\in Z^{*}
as

\langle\chi_{i},\mu\rangle=\langle f_{i},F_{i}\mu\rangle-a_{i}(G_{i}f_{i},F_{i}\mu),

and
\chi=\chi_{1}+\chi_{2}
.

Remark 8.1
.

Although the bilinear forms
a_{i}
have different trial and test spaces, the Steklov–Poincaré operators have the same trial and test spaces, i.e.,
S_{i}\colon Z\to Z^{*}
. This is due to the fact that the spaces
W_{i}
and
\tilde{W}_{i}
share the trace space
Z
, see
[
7
, p. 507]
.

We will now prove the main properties of the Steklov–Poincaré operators, namely that they are bounded and coercive. We first have the following lemma, which is important for the coercivity of the Steklov–Poincaré operators.

Lemma 8.2
.

If
Assumptions

2.1
,
5.2
, and
5.3
hold then

\|F_{i}\eta\|_{W_{i}}\leq C\|F_{i}\eta\|_{L^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega_{i})}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}

for every
\eta\in Z
.

Proof.

By the definition of
F_{i}
in
Equation

33
one has
\phi_{-}F_{i}\eta\in Q_{i}
, and the embedding
Equation

24
then yields the bound

\|F_{i}\eta\|_{W_{i}}\leq C\bigl(\|\phi_{-}F_{i}\eta\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};H^{1}(\Omega_{i}(0)))}+\|\partial_{t}\phi_{-}F_{i}\eta\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};H^{-1}(\Omega_{i}(0)))}\bigr)^{1/2}.

Furthermore, as
u=F_{i}\eta
is a solution to
Equation

31
, the same calculations as in
22
together with the bound
23
for
g=0
gives

\|\partial_{t}\phi_{-}F_{i}\eta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};H^{-1}(\Omega_{i}(0)))}\leq C\|F_{i}\eta\|_{L^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega_{i})}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}.

Combining these results gives the desired estimate.
∎

Theorem 8.3
.

Suppose that
Assumptions

2.1
,
5.2
, and
5.3
hold. Then the Steklov–Poincaré operators
S_{i}
are bounded and also coercive, i.e.,

\langle S_{i}\eta,\eta\rangle\geq c\|\eta\|^{2}_{Z}\quad\text{for all }\eta\in Z.

(35)

Moreover, a similar result holds for
S
.

Proof.

By
Corollary

6.7
and the fact that
F_{i}\colon Z\to W_{i}
is bounded we have

\bigl|\langle S_{i}\eta,\mu\rangle\bigr|=\bigl|a_{i}(F_{i}\eta,F_{i}\mu)\bigr|\leq C\|F_{i}\eta\|_{W_{i}}\|F_{i}\mu\|_{\tilde{W}_{i}}\leq C\|F_{i}\eta\|_{W_{i}}\|F_{i}\mu\|_{W_{i}}\leq C\|\eta\|_{Z}\|\mu\|_{Z}

for all

, which shows that

is bounded. It follows from

8.2
that

\displaystyle\langle S_{i}\eta,\eta\rangle=a_{i}(F_{i}\eta,F_{i}\eta)\geq c\|F_{i}\eta\|_{L^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega_{i})}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}^{2}\geq c\|F_{i}\eta\|_{W_{i}}^{2}\geq c\|T_{i}F_{i}\eta\|_{Z}^{2}=c\|\eta\|_{Z}^{2}

\eta\in Z

is coercive. The result for

follows by summing the inequalities for
S_{i}
.

and

immediately yield that the operators satisfy the assumptions of the Lax–Milgram theorem and therefore we have the following corollary.

Corollary 8.4

The Steklov–Poincaré operators

The Steklov–Poincaré equation is to find

n=1,2,\dots
such that

\left\{\begin{aligned} S_{i}\lambda_{i}^{n}&=\chi-S\eta^{n-1}&&\text{for }i=1,2,\\
\eta^{n}&=\eta^{n-1}+s_{2}\lambda_{1}^{n}+s_{3}\lambda_{2}^{n}.&&\end{aligned}\right.

(39)

Analogous results to
Lemma

8.6
holds for the Dirichlet–Neumann and Neumann–Neumann methods. The following result gives that the three methods are well defined.

Corollary 8.7
.

Suppose that
Assumptions

2.1
,
5.2
, and
5.3
hold. The Dirichlet–Neumann, Neumann–Neumann, and Robin–Robin methods are well defined in the sense that each step of
Equations

38
,
39
, and
37
has a unique solution.

Proof.

For the Dirichlet–Neumann and Neumann–Neumann methods the results follow immediately from
Corollary

8.4
since this implies that the interface iterations have unique iterates. For the Robin–Robin method the result also follows since

\langle\mathcal{R}\mu,\mu\rangle\geq\|\mu\|_{L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}^{2}\geq 0\quad\text{for all }\mu\in Z,

which means that
s_{0}\mathcal{R}+S_{i}
is coercive and therefore an isomorphism for all
s_{0}>0
.
∎

In order to illustrate the applicability of the derived framework, we will next prove convergence of the Robin–Robin method. We begin by introducing a particular Gelfand triple.

Lemma 8.8
.

If
Assumption

2.1
holds, then
Z
is densely embedded into
L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.

Proof.

Note first of all that
Z\hookrightarrow L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
is clearly a continuous embedding. For density, let
\eta\in L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and define

\mu=\psi_{-}\eta\in L^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};L^{2}(\Gamma(0))\bigr).

Now recall that
H^{1/4}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
is dense in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and
\Lambda(0)
is dense in
L^{2}(\Gamma(0))
, see
[
10
, Lemma 4.2]
. Therefore the algebraic tensor product space
H^{1/4}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\otimes\Lambda(0)
is dense in
L^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};L^{2}(\Gamma(0))\bigr)
, see
[
35
, Theorem 3.12]
. It then follows that the larger space

H^{1/4}\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};L^{2}(\Gamma(0))\bigr)\cap L^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};\Lambda(0)\bigr)

is also dense in
L^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};L^{2}(\Gamma(0))\bigr)
. Hence, there exists a sequence

\mu_{n}\in H^{1/4}\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};L^{2}(\Gamma(0))\bigr)\cap L^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};\Lambda(0)\bigr)

that satisfies
\mu_{n}\to\mu
in
L^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{R}_{+};L^{2}(\Gamma(0))\bigr)
. It immediately follows from the fact that
\psi
is an isomorphism that the sequence
\eta_{n}=\psi\mu_{n}
satisfies
\eta_{n}\in Z
and
\eta_{n}\to\eta
in
L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. Since
\eta
was arbitrary this shows that
Z
is dense in
L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.
∎

By
Lemma

8.8
together with the fact that
Z
and
L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
are Hilbert spaces, we have the Gelfand triple

Z\hookrightarrow L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\hookrightarrow Z^{*},

with dense embeddings. We recall the Riesz isomorphism
\mathcal{R}\colon L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\rightarrow L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{*}
:

\langle\mathcal{R}\eta,\mu\rangle=(\eta,\mu)_{L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\quad\text{ for all }\eta\in L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),\,\mu\in Z.

(40)

We trivially have the bounds

\left.|\langle\mathcal{R}\eta,\mu\rangle\right|\leq\|\eta\|_{L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\|\mu\|_{L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\quad\text{ for all }\eta\in L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),\,\mu\in Z

(41)

and

\langle\mathcal{R}\eta,\eta\rangle\geq\|\eta\|_{L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}^{2}\quad\text{ for all }\eta\in Z.

(42)

The variational framework appears to be too general for analyzing the convergence of the Peaceman–Rachford iteration. Therefore, we introduce the Steklov–Poincaré operators as affine unbounded operators on
L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
before we prove that the iteration converges. To this end, let

\displaystyle D(\mathcal{S}_{i})

\displaystyle=\{\eta\in Z:S_{i}\eta-\chi_{i}\in L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{*}\},

\displaystyle D(\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle=\{\eta\in Z:S\eta-\chi\in L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{*}\},

and define the unbounded affine operators

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{i}

\displaystyle:D(\mathcal{S}_{i})\subseteq L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\rightarrow L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+}):\eta\mapsto\mathcal{R}^{-1}(S_{i}\eta-\chi_{i}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle:D(\mathcal{S})\subseteq L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\rightarrow L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+}):\eta\mapsto\mathcal{R}^{-1}(S\eta-\chi).

In
L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
the Steklov–Poincaré equation is to find
\eta\in D(\mathcal{S})
such that

\mathcal{S}\eta=0

(43)

and the Peaceman–Rachford iteration takes the following form: For each
n=1,2,\dots
, find
(\eta_{1}^{n},\eta_{2}^{n})\in D(\mathcal{S}_{1})\times D(\mathcal{S}_{2})
such that

\left\{\begin{aligned} (s_{0}I+\mathcal{S}_{1})\eta_{1}^{n}&=(s_{0}I-\mathcal{S}_{2})\eta_{2}^{n-1},\\
(s_{0}I+\mathcal{S}_{2})\eta_{2}^{n}&=(s_{0}I-\mathcal{S}_{1})\eta_{1}^{n}.\end{aligned}\right.

(44)

Here,
\eta_{2}^{0}\in D(\mathcal{S}_{2})
is a given initial guess. We now verify that
Equation

43
is indeed a restriction of the weak Steklov–Poincaré equation
Equation

36
.

Lemma 8.9
.

Suppose that
Assumptions

2.1
,
5.2
, and
5.3
hold. If
\eta\in D(\mathcal{S})
solves the
L^{2}
-Steklov–Poincaré equation
Equation

43
, then
\eta
also solves the weak Steklov–Poincaré equation
Equation

36
.

Proof.

From
Equation

43
we have

(\mathcal{R}^{-1}(S\eta-\chi),\mu)_{L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}=0\quad\text{ for all }\mu\in L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+}).

Therefore, we get by
Equation

40
that

\langle S\eta-\chi,\mu\rangle=(\mathcal{R}^{-1}(S\eta-\chi),\mu)_{L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}=0

for all
\mu\in Z
.
∎

A similar result holds for the Peaceman–Rachford iteration. The proof is left out since it is similar to the proof of
Lemma

8.9
.

Lemma 8.10
.

Suppose that
Assumptions

2.1
,
5.2
, and
5.3
hold. If
(\eta_{1}^{n},\eta_{2}^{n})_{n\geq 1}
solves the
L^{2}
-Peaceman–Rachford iteration
Equation

44
with
\eta_{2}^{0}\in D(\mathcal{S}_{2})
, then
(\eta_{1}^{n},\eta_{2}^{n})_{n\geq 1}
also solves the weak Peaceman–Rachford iteration
Equation

37
with the same initial guess.

Lemma 8.11
.

Suppose that
Assumptions

2.1
,
5.2
, and
5.3
hold. Then
\mathcal{S}_{i},\,i=1,2
, satisfy the monotonicity property

(\mathcal{S}_{i}\eta-\mathcal{S}_{i}\mu,\eta-\mu)_{L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\geq c\|\eta-\mu\|^{2}_{Z}\quad\text{ for all }\eta,\mu\in D(\mathcal{S}_{i}).

(45)

Moreover, for any
s_{0}\geq 0
the operators
s_{0}I+\mathcal{S}_{i}\colon D(\mathcal{S}_{i})\rightarrow L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),\,i=1,2
, are isomorphisms. Similar results hold for
\mathcal{S}
. In particular, there exists a unique solution to
Equation

43
and the iteration
Equation

44
is well defined.

Proof.

The monotonicity follows from
Equations

40
and
35
, since

\displaystyle(\mathcal{S}_{i}\eta-\mathcal{S}_{i}\mu,\eta-\mu)_{L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}

\displaystyle=\langle(S_{i}\eta-\chi_{i})-(S_{i}\mu-\chi_{i}),\eta-\mu\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle S_{i}(\eta-\mu),\eta-\mu\rangle\geq c\|\eta-\mu\|^{2}_{Z}

for all
\eta,\mu\in D(\mathcal{S}_{i})
. Let
\mu\in L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
be arbitrary. Then
\chi_{i}+\mathcal{R}\mu\in Z^{*}
, and by
Corollary

8.4
, there exists a unique
\eta\in Z
such that
(s_{0}\mathcal{R}+S_{i})\eta=\chi_{i}+\mathcal{R}\mu
in
Z^{*}
. Rearranging yields that
S_{i}\eta-\chi_{i}=\mathcal{R}(\mu-s_{0}\eta)\in L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{*}
, i.e.,
\eta\in D(\mathcal{S}_{i})
with

(s_{0}I+\mathcal{S}_{i})\eta=s_{0}\eta+\mathcal{R}^{-1}(S_{i}\eta-\chi_{i})=\mu.

Thus, we have shown that
(s_{0}I+\mathcal{S}_{i})
is an isomorphism. The proof for
\mathcal{S}
is similar and is therefore left out.
∎

In order to proceed with the convergence analysis, we require the following regularity of the solution to the weak parabolic equation
Equation

32
.

Assumption 8.12
.

The functionals

\mu\mapsto a_{i}(q_{i}u,F_{i}\mu)-\langle f_{i},F_{i}\mu\rangle,\quad{i=1,2},

are elements in
L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{*}
, where
u\in W
is the solution to
Equation

32
.

This assumption is somewhat implicit, but can be interpreted as the solution
u
having a normal derivative on
\Gamma
in
L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.

Lemma 8.13
.

Suppose that
Assumptions

2.1
,
5.2
,
5.3
, and
8.12
hold. If
\eta
solves the
L^{2}
-Steklov–Poincaré equation
Equation

43
then
\eta\in D(\mathcal{S}_{1})\cap D(\mathcal{S}_{2})
.

Proof.

Let
\eta\in D(\mathcal{S})
be the solution to
Equation

43
. By
Lemmas

8.5
and
7.3
, we have the identification
q_{i}u=F_{i}\eta+G_{i}f_{i}
, where
u\in W
is the solution to the weak parabolic equation
Equation

32
.
Assumption

8.12
then yields that

\displaystyle\mu\mapsto\langle S_{i}\eta-\chi_{i},\mu\rangle

\displaystyle=a_{i}(F_{i}\eta,F_{i}\mu)+a_{i}(G_{i}f_{i},F_{i}\mu)-\langle f_{i},F_{i}\mu\rangle

\displaystyle=a_{i}(q_{i}u,F_{i}\mu)-\langle f_{i},F_{i}\mu\rangle

is an element in
L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{*}
, i.e.,
\eta\in D(\mathcal{S}_{i})
for
i=1,2
.
∎

Lemma 8.14
.

Suppose that
Assumptions

2.1
,
5.2
,
5.3
, and
8.12
hold. Let
\eta
be the solution to the
L^{2}
-Steklov–Poincaré equation
Equation

43
and
(\eta_{1}^{n},\eta_{2}^{n})_{n\geq 1}
be the
L^{2}
-Peaceman–Rachford iterates
Equation

44
with
\eta_{2}^{0}\in D(\mathcal{S}_{2})
. Then we have the limit

(\mathcal{S}_{i}\eta^{n}_{i}-\mathcal{S}_{i}\eta,\eta^{n}_{i}-\eta)_{L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\rightarrow 0

(46)

as
n
tends to infinity.

Lemma

8.14
is a consequence of the abstract result
[
28
, Proposition 1]
. The latter requires the monotonicity
Equation

45
and the fact that the solution to
Equation

43
satisfies
\eta\in D(\mathcal{S}_{1})\cap D(\mathcal{S}_{2})
, which follows from
Lemma

8.13
. A simpler proof of
Lemma

8.14
can be found in
[
10
, Lemma 8.8]
.

We are now in a position to prove that the Robin–Robin method converges.

Theorem 8.15
.

Suppose that
Assumptions

2.1
,
5.2
,
5.3
, and
8.12
hold. Let
u
be the solution to the weak parabolic equation
Equation

32
and
\eta
be the solution to the
L^{2}
-Steklov–Poincaré equation
Equation

43
. The iterates
(\eta_{1}^{n},\eta_{2}^{n})_{n\geq 1}
of the
L^{2}
-Peaceman–Rachford iteration
Equation

44
converges to
\eta
, i.e.,

\|\eta^{n}_{1}-\eta\|_{Z}+\|\eta^{n}_{2}-\eta\|_{Z}\rightarrow 0

as
n
tends to infinity. Moreover the weak Robin–Robin approximation
(u_{1}^{n},u_{2}^{n})_{n\geq 1}
converges to
(u_{1},u_{2})=(q_{1}u,q_{2}u)
, i.e.,

\|u^{n}_{1}-u_{1}\|_{W_{1}}+\|u^{n}_{2}-u_{2}\|_{W_{2}}\rightarrow 0

as
n
tends to infinity.

Proof.

From
Equations

46
and
45
we obtain

\displaystyle\|\eta^{n}_{1}-\eta\|^{2}_{Z}+\|\eta^{n}_{2}-\eta\|^{2}_{Z}

\displaystyle\quad\leq C\bigl((\mathcal{S}_{1}\eta^{n+1}_{1}-\mathcal{S}_{1}\eta,\eta^{n+1}_{1}-\eta)_{L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}+(\mathcal{S}_{2}\eta^{n}_{2}-\mathcal{S}_{2}\eta,\eta^{n}_{2}-\eta)_{L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\bigr)\rightarrow 0

as
n
tends to infinity. By
Lemmas

8.5
,
7.3
, and
8.6
, one has the identities

(u_{1},u_{2})=(F_{1}\eta+G_{1}f_{1},F_{2}\eta+G_{2}f_{2})\quad\text{and}\quad(u_{1}^{n},u_{2}^{n})=(F_{1}\eta_{1}^{n}+G_{1}f_{1},F_{2}\eta_{2}^{n}+G_{2}f_{2}).

This together with the limit above and the fact that
F_{i}
is bounded yields that

\displaystyle\|u^{n}_{1}-u_{1}\|_{W_{1}}+\|u^{n}_{2}-u_{2}\|_{W_{2}}

\displaystyle=\|F_{1}(\eta^{n}_{1}-\eta)\|_{W_{1}}+\|F_{2}(\eta^{n}_{2}-\eta)\|_{W_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\bigl(\|\eta^{n}_{1}-\eta\|_{Z}+\|\eta^{n}_{2}-\eta\|_{Z}\bigr)\rightarrow 0

as
n
tends to infinity.
∎
