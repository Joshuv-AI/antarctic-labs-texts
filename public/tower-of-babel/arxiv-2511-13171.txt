Abstract
This paper presents an autonomous sensing framework for identifying and localizing multiple users in Fifth Generation (5G) networks using an Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) that is not part of the serving access network. Unlike conventional aerial serving nodes, the proposed UAV operates passively and is dedicated solely to sensing. It captures Uplink (UL) Sounding Reference Signals (SRS), and requires virtually no coordination with the network infrastructure. A complete signal processing chain is proposed and developed, encompassing synchronization, user identification, and localization, all executed onboard UAV during flight. The system autonomously plans and adapts its mission workflow to estimate multiple user positions within a single deployment, integrating flight control with real-time sensing. Extensive simulations and a full-scale low-altitude experimental campaign validate the approach, showing localization errors below 3 m in rural field tests and below 8 m in urban simulation scenarios, while reliably identifying each user. The results confirm the feasibility of infrastructure-independent sensing UAVs as a core element of the emerging Low Altitude Economy (LAE), supporting situational awareness and rapid deployment in emergency or connectivity-limited environments.



I
Introduction

The low-altitude economy (LAE) refers to the emerging ecosystem of aerial services operating within the lower portion of the airspace close to ground level. It is rapidly expanding thanks to advances in Unmanned Aerial Systems (UASs), the evolution of regulatory frameworks, and the deployment of
Fifth Generation (5G)
and future
Sixth Generation (6G)
networks
[
5
,
10
]
.

Recent progress in wireless communication, computing, and flight autonomy has transformed
Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs)
from simple mobile platforms into intelligent network nodes capable of communication, sensing, and onboard computation. This convergence is turning the low-altitude domain into a connected layer where aerial and terrestrial components cooperate to enable time-sensitive and data-driven services
[
5
]
.

Within this environment, mission-oriented applications, such as autonomous navigation, drone-based logistics, precision agriculture, and infrastructure inspection, critically depend on reliable wireless connectivity and real-time signal processing
[
9
]
.
A particularly relevant class of applications involves emergency and search-and-rescue operations, where drones provide rapid coverage, robust connectivity, and accurate localization even when terrestrial infrastructure is unavailable or impaired
[
8
,
15
,
11
]
.

Conventional
UAV
assisted localization schemes typically rely on aerial serving nodes that establish links with ground
User Equipments (UEs)

[
4
,
2
,
13
]
. In disaster response scenarios, however, the main goal of such
UAV
base stations is to maximize network coverage and connectivity rather than optimize geometric conditions for localization. Consequently, operational procedures that ensure adequate communication performance often do not align with those needed for high-accuracy user position estimation
[
20
]
.

Additionally, maintaining
Line-of-Sight (LoS)
connectivity with all ground users, which is an essential requirement for reliable localization
[
21
,
17
]
, is generally infeasible for a single
UAV
acting as an aerial base station. Although the
UAV
can maneuver to establish
LoS
with individual
UEs
, geometric constraints, occlusions, and flight limitations prevent simultaneous coverage of multiple spatially distributed users. To overcome this limitation, one or more
UAVs
can be deployed as dedicated sensing nodes operating independently from the communication infrastructure. These non-serving
UAVs
do not participate in user connectivity or data relaying; instead, they act as cooperative receivers that capture
Uplink (UL)
signals from connected
UEs
and perform localization solely based on signal measurements. Because their role is limited to reception and onboard processing, such sensing
UAVs
can be realized with relatively low hardware complexity, enabling scalable and cost-effective deployment.

This architectural
decoupling
increases flexibility in
UAV
placement, enables broader spatial coverage, and can substantially improve localization accuracy. However, it also introduces technical challenges. Sensing UAVs are not time or frequency synchronized with the cellular network, so they have no knowledge of users’ transmission timing or resource allocation. As a result, they cannot predict user transmissions, which complicates signal detection,
UE
identification, and position estimation. In addition,
UL
transmissions from multiple
UEs
may overlap in time and frequency, creating interference and degrading received signal quality
[
19
,
6
]
.

A notable limitation of existing studies adopting such non-serving or sensing-oriented UAV architectures lies in the highly idealized assumptions made at the signal level. Prior works often consider perfectly known pilot or beacon sequences with predefined resource allocations, rather than capturing and processing actual
5G

UL
waveforms
[
18
,
21
,
14
]
. While such abstractions simplify algorithmic evaluation, they do not address how localization signals can be extracted in real network conditions, where synchronization, scheduling, and multiplexing constraints must be explicitly considered.

In this work, we demonstrate the
autonomous synchronization, localization and identification
of
multiple
UEs
within a single flight using a
non-serving
UAV
capable of
on-board processing actual
5G

UL
signals
, requiring only
minimal initial coordination
with the network and
no mission time control
from it.

Paper Contributions

The main contributions of this paper can be summarized as follows.

•

We propose a fully autonomous framework that enables a single non-serving UAV to identify and localize multiple
5G
users using only
UL

Sounding Reference Signal (SRS)
. Unlike aerial serving nodes approaches, the UAV operates purely as a passive receiver, requiring no network synchronization or control. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first demonstration of practical multi-user identification and localization from real 5G
UL
signals captured by a UAV that is not part of the access network.

•

We introduce a new synchronization and user identification pipeline that exploits the
SRS
structure and cyclic shifts, combining coarse and fine timing synchronizations with a Matching Pursuit frequency extraction method to separate multiple users transmitting in the same band. The proposed method was shown to be robust to both delay and power spreads between
UEs
, as evidenced by our simulation and experimental results.

•

We develop a UAV centric weighted mean shift localization algorithm that operates on
SRS
derived metrics collected along the UAV trajectory. Despite relying on narrowband (1.4 MHz) SRS and lightweight hardware, the approach achieves high accuracy, below 3 m in rural and below 8 m in urban conditions, outperforming state-of-the-art
[
21
]

Angle of Arrival (AoA)
- and
[
4
]

Time Difference of Arrival (TDoA)
-based methods by approximately 5 m and 6 m, respectively. The method is validated not only through extensive simulations but also through an extensive outdoor experimental campaign.

•

We design and integrate an onboard platform combining a dual RX ADALM Pluto
Software Defined Radio (SDR)
, a Pixhawk flight controller, and an NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX, enabling all signal processing, mission control, and waypoint updates to be performed autonomously during flight. In this architecture,
autonomy
refers to the UAV’s ability to execute the sensing workflow without external intervention, including real-time
UL
signals processing, UE identification, position estimation, and the consequent update of navigation commands directly onboard.

II
Related Works

This section reviews prior research on UAV-assisted localization, focusing on experimental studies that provide practical benchmarks for real-world implementations. Many works are simulation-based and rely on idealized assumptions, while experimental studies reveal the challenges and limitations of operating UAVs in realistic cellular networks. The following discussion highlights these contributions and key constraints.

Research on UAV-assisted localization can be broadly divided into two categories. The first includes serving-UAV approaches, where the aerial platform acts as a base station or relay to provide both communication and positioning services. The second focuses on non-serving UAVs, which operate as passive receivers to estimate
UE
positions without participating in the network.

In serving-UAV systems, several experimental works have investigated UAV-assisted localization using time-based measurements. Esrafilian
et al.

[
4
]
presented one of the first real-world implementations of UAV-aided ToA localization in a 5G system using
OpenAirInterface (OAI)
, combining UAV motion with
UL
SRS measurements to jointly estimate
UE
and UAV positions via least-squares optimization. Similarly, Albanese
et al.

[
2
]
introduced SARDO, an autonomous UAV-based search-and-rescue system that localizes victims via their mobile phones without terrestrial infrastructure
[
11
]
. The UAV carries a lightweight LTE base station and estimates positions using Demodulation Reference Signal (DMRS)-based pseudo-trilateration and neural networks. Both studies demonstrate the feasibility of UAV-assisted localization with real cellular signals but share limitations of
Time-of-Arrival (ToA)
methods, including timing offsets from asynchronous UE clocks and the need for wide bandwidths, which may be impractical in emergencies.
The work in
[
15
]
explores the potential of an Integrated Sensing and Communication (ISAC) system to improve search-and-rescue operations, detailing methods for processing echoed signals from downlink transmissions to enhance target detection and classify possible victims by fusing Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) imagery with triangulation results from
UL
signals
[
12
,
7
]
.

Other experimental approaches have explored non-serving
UAVs
. Scazzoli
et al.

[
21
]
demonstrated an
AoA
-based method where a
UAV
receives
Physical Random
Access Channel (PRACH)
signals from a single
UE
, achieving about
25\,
m accuracy.
However, the system requires multiple antennas, and its
AoA
estimates become unreliable during
UAV
rotations, as Doppler shifts distort the measured signal phase.
PRACH
transmissions are also unpredictable, limiting their use for continuous non-serving UAV localization.
Petitjean
et al.

[
18
]
proposed a
Received Signal Strength Indicator (RSSI)
-based method with a UAV equipped with a
Universal Software Radio Peripheral (USRP)

SDR
to estimate the bearing of a ground transmitter. While effective in
LoS
conditions, it is sensitive to multipath and UAV altitude variations, and may converge to local
RSSI
maxima in complex environments.
Masrur and Güvenç
[
14
]
introduced a deep-learning framework trained on simulated data for 3D source localization, achieving around 18 m average error in real-world trials, but requiring many samples.
Table
I
summarizes the main experimental works in the literature. The reported complexity corresponds to the requirements of a hardware implementation, whereas accuracy values correspond to measurements obtained in rural scenarios.

Additional simulated studies highlight the benefit of trajectory optimization in
UAV
deployments. For example,
[
20
]

analyzes how different UAV mobility patterns affect data collection, evaluating a time–coverage efficiency metric. They show that circular and square trajectories offer the best trade-off between area coverage and efficiency.
Similarly,
[
3
]
evaluates several UAV flight paths for device discovery in post-disaster scenarios, showing that the most effective trajectory depends on the damage distribution.

These studies highlight the potential of UAV-assisted localization but also show practical limits, often due to partial autonomy, strict synchronization assumptions, and the need for complex or costly hardware.
In contrast, our work introduces a fully autonomous sensing UAV that performs synchronization, identification, localization, and mission planning entirely onboard using lightweight, low-cost hardware, enabling accurate multi-user localization with non-serving UAVs in real 5G environments.

IV
UEs synchronization, identification, and localization

In this section, we describe the signal processing procedures that enable the sensing
UAV
to autonomously synchronize, identify, and localize multiple
UEs
from their
UL

SRSs
.
The synchronization and
UE
identification stages run independently on each antenna mounted on the
UAV
. Localization then exploits antenna diversity by merging the outputs of all antennas.

IV-A

Coarse Time Synchronization via SRS Structure

In 5G systems, the transmitted data stream consists of consecutively concatenated
OFDM
symbols.
To correctly recover the transmitted information, the
gNB
receiver must apply a
DFT
to each received symbol over a precisely aligned
DFT
window.
Any misalignment causes interference between adjacent symbols, degrading signal quality, as illustrated in Fig.
4
. Since multiple
UEs
are generally located at different positions, their signals arrive at the
gNB
with varying propagation delays depending on their relative distances. To ensure alignment, the network periodically instructs each
UE
to apply a
Timing Advance (TA)
correction so that all signals arrive within the same
DFT
window at the
gNB
.

If the sensing
UAV
is co-located at the
gNB
position, it will receive all
UL
transmissions in mutual synchrony.
However, when the
UAV
operates away from the
gNB
location, the relative propagation delays differ, and the received streams become partially desynchronized.
Additionally, the sensing
UAV
has no knowledge of the
UEs
’ transmission start times.

To obtain a coarse time alignment, we exploit the inherent repetition structure of the
SRS
.
We define the detection metric

M[n]=\frac{|P[n]|^{2}}{R[n]^{2}},

(7)

where

\displaystyle P[n]

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}y[n+l]\,y^{*}[n+l+L],

(8)

\displaystyle R[n]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Biggl(\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}|y[n+l]|^{2}+\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}|y[n+l+L]|^{2}\Biggr).

The metric
M[n]
is bounded between 0 and 1.
This procedure provides coarse synchronization with any detected
SRS
, is robust against
CFO
(since the phase rotations cancel out in
P[n]
), and can be computed efficiently using recursive updates.

The
ToAs
of the
SRSs
received at the
UAV
from different
UEs
are generally mutually misaligned, since the
UAV
is not located at the
gNB
site where the
TA
aligns the streams.
For convenience, we select one arrival time
\tau_{\bar{p},\bar{u}}
as reference, with
\bar{u}\in\mathcal{U}
and
\bar{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{\bar{u}}
, and express all others relative to it

\Delta\tau_{p,u}=\tau_{p,u}-\tau_{\bar{p},\bar{u}}.

(9)

To reliably detect the presence of
SRSs
, the metric should have values close to unity (i.e.,
M[n]\approx 1
) at sufficiently high
SNR
.
Evaluating
M[n]
on the reference arrival index
n_{0}=\lceil\tau_{\bar{p},\bar{u}}/T_{s}\rceil
and neglecting noise, Appendix
A
proves that

M[n_{0}]\approx\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{p,u}|a_{p,u}|^{2}\,f_{0}(\Delta\tau_{p,u})}{\displaystyle\sum_{p,u}|a_{p,u}|^{2}\,g_{0}(\Delta\tau_{p,u})},

(10)

where
f_{0}(\Delta\tau_{p,u}),g_{0}(\Delta\tau_{p,u})\in[0,1]
depend on the temporal misalignment
\Delta\tau_{p,u}
.
Figure
5a
shows that both satisfy
f_{0}=g_{0}=1
whenever

-T_{\mathrm{CP}}\leq\Delta\tau_{p,u}\leq 0,

(11)

with
T_{\mathrm{CP}}
denoting the cyclic prefix duration.
Outside this interval, it holds that
g_{0}(\Delta\tau_{p,u})
\,>\,
f_{0}(\Delta\tau_{p,u})
, reducing
M[n_{0}]
. Figure
5b
illustrates the two outcomes.
If the relative arrival times
\Delta\tau_{p,u}\ \forall\,p,u
lie within the interval defined in (
11

Conversely, if the condition is never satisfied, the metric remains strictly below 1 (black curve).

In practice, signals from distant
UEs
typically exhibit much lower power than those from nearby
UEs
, i.e.,
|a_{p,u_{\mathrm{near}}}|^{2}
\,\gg\,
|a_{p,u_{\mathrm{far}}}|^{2}
.
Thus, their contribution to (
10
) is often negligible.
As shown later through numerical results, the proposed metric remains robust even when multiple
UEs
are received with comparable power levels, up to practical distances on the order of kilometers.

Once the synchronization metric
M[n]
is computed, the sensing
UAV
must identify the point at which it reaches its maximum value and exceeds a predefined threshold
M_{\mathrm{th}}
.
This point provides a coarse estimate of the
ToA
of the received
SRSs
and allows the receiver to achieve an initial time alignment. When received
SRSs
are synchronized, the timing metric exhibits a sharp rise followed by a plateau whose duration equals the
CP
length
L_{CP}
. This behavior arises because, when
n
is within the cyclic prefix interval, the two identical halves of the preamble align perfectly, contributing coherently to the correlation and yielding a constant maximum value of the metric rather than a single narrow peak. Therefore, to improve the detection, the metric
M[n]
is convolved with a rectangular window of length equal to
L_{CP}
.
This operation enhances the peak sharpness by transforming the plateau into a more localized maximum, facilitating reliable detection.
Additionally, if the receiving signals are very unsynchronized,
M[n]
may oscillate before the main peak. Filtering attenuates these side fluctuations and sharpens the main correlation peak.
The resulting filtered metric is

M_{F}[n]=\frac{1}{L_{CP}}\sum_{n^{\prime}=0}^{L_{CP}-1}M[n-n^{\prime}].

(12)

The initial detection instant
n_{sync}
is determined by

\hskip-2.84544ptM_{F}[n_{\mathrm{sync}}]\hskip-2.84544pt\geq\hskip-2.84544ptM_{\mathrm{th}}\hskip-2.84544pt\;\land\;\hskip-2.84544ptD[n_{\mathrm{sync}}\hskip-2.84544pt-1]\hskip-2.84544pt>\hskip-2.84544pt\delta\hskip-2.84544pt\;\land\;\hskip-2.84544ptD[n_{\mathrm{sync}}]\hskip-2.84544pt<\hskip-2.84544pt-\delta,

(13)

where
\delta
is a small positive quantity and
D[n]=M_{F}[n+1]-M_{F}[n]
is the discrete derivative of the metric. For moderate
SNRs
(
<20
dB) within the investigated subband, and when (
11
) holds
\forall p,u
, the
SNR
can be estimated as

\hat{\mathrm{SNR}}\approx\frac{\sqrt{M_{F}(n_{sync})}}{1-\sqrt{M_{F}(n_{sync})}}.

(14)

Consequently, the detection threshold
M_{\mathrm{th}}
can be selected according to the minimum
SNR
level that must be reliably detected
[
22
]
.

IV-B

Fine Synchronization and UE Identification

Once this initial timing is established, the
UAV
must: (i) separate the incoming signals, (ii) identify the transmitting
UEs
, (iii) localize them, (iv) refine the synchronization error.
Specifically, the residual synchronization error for
UE

u

\varepsilon_{p,u}=n_{p,u}-n_{sync},

(15)

where
n_{sync}
denotes the initial coarse synchronization instant and
n_{p,u}=\lceil\tau_{p,u}/T_{s}\rfloor

.

IV-B
1

This step consists of discriminating the individual

The received

\displaystyle y_{\text{SRS}}[n]=\sum_{u=1}^{U}\sum_{p=1}^{P_{u}}\hskip-4.26773pta_{p,u}x_{u}\!\left[n-L_{\text{CP}}-\varepsilon_{p,u}\right]e^{j2\pi(\nu_{p,u}+\nu_{u,\text{cfo}})\,nT_{s}},

\displaystyle y_{\text{SRS}}[n]=\sum_{u=1}^{U}\sum_{p=1}^{P_{u}}\hskip-4.26773pta_{p,u}\,s_{u}\!\bigl[(n-\varepsilon_{p,u})\bmod L\bigr]e^{j2\pi(\nu_{p,u}+\nu_{u,\text{cfo}})\,nT_{s}}.

Applying an

where

IV-B
2
Peaks Extraction

The dominant frequency components of the received signal are iteratively extracted using a Matching Pursuit algorithm
[
23
]
over a sinusoidal dictionary, i.e. the
DFT
bins.
If the dictionary resolution is coarse, oversampling and parabolic interpolation refine the estimates.
At each iteration, the strongest component is removed from the residual, and its amplitude and equivalent power are estimated.
The process continues until the
Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC)

[
16
]
indicates that adding further components no longer improves the model.
The
BIC
is computed as

\hskip-4.83691pt\text{BIC}^{(i)}\hskip-2.84544pt=\,\hskip-2.84544pti\log M_{\mathrm{SRS}}\hskip-2.84544pt+\hskip-2.84544ptM_{\mathrm{SRS}}\log\!\left(\hskip-2.84544pt\frac{1}{M_{\mathrm{SRS}}}\hskip-5.69046pt\sum_{m=0}^{M_{\mathrm{SRS}}-1}\hskip-5.69046pt|c^{(i)}[m]|^{2}\hskip-2.84544pt\right).

(21)

The procedure is summarized in Algorithm

.

IV-B
3
User Identification via Cyclic Shift

Note that, due to the different cyclic shifts applied, the frequencies associated with different
UEs
are typically well separated, which facilitates their extraction.
We can convert the estimated frequencies
\hat{f}^{(i)}
into the corresponding estimated cyclic shifts as

\hat{\alpha}^{(i)}=\left\lceil U\bigl(\hat{f}^{(i)}+1/(2U)\bigr)\right\rceil\bmod L,

(22)

where
U
denotes the total number of cyclic shifts assigned to the
UEs
(i.e. the number of users) within the considered band.
If the cyclic shifts are uniformly (i.e., maximally) spaced and
U
is even, to avoid a misdetection of the user–path tuple
(u,p)
, the synchronization error
\varepsilon_{p,u}
, must satisfy

\varepsilon_{p,u}\in\left(-L/(2U),\,L/(2U)\right].

(23)

Consequently, our goal is to maximize
L
\,=\,
N/K_{\mathrm{TC}}
by setting
K_{\mathrm{TC}}=2
, which corresponds to its minimum allowed value. Furthermore, depending on the size of the considered scenario, fewer cyclic shifts than the maximum per band (i.e.,
U_{\mathrm{max}}
\,=\,
8
) can be allocated, reducing
UE
identification errors.
Once the cyclic shift
\hat{\alpha}^{(i)}
is estimated, the identity of the transmitting
UE
can be unambiguously determined by pairing it with the corresponding frequency band
\mathcal{K}_{b}
.

IV-B
4
Fine synchronization

The timing synchronization described in Sec.
IV-A
allows us to coarsely synchronize to the received
SRS
signals.
This operation is required only once, since the
SRS
periodicity of the transmitting
UEs
is known by the
UAV
.
This allows us to maintain synchronization at subsequent receptions without recomputing the timing metric from scratch every
SRS
reception.

Starting from the initial synchronization instant
n_{sync}
, the synchronization points are updated at each
SRS
reception as

n_{q}=n_{sync}+qT_{\mathrm{per}}-\tilde{\varepsilon}(q),

(24)

where
q\in\mathbb{N}
,
T_{\mathrm{per}}
is the
SRS
periodicity and
\tilde{\varepsilon}(q)
is the filtered residual synchronization error, updated through

\tilde{\varepsilon}(q)=\beta\,\hat{\varepsilon}_{\bar{p},\bar{u}}(q)+(1-\beta)\,\tilde{\varepsilon}(q-1),

(25)

with
\beta\in[0,1]
controlling the weighting between the latest and previous estimates.

The residual synchronization error arises for two main reasons.
First, due to noise and mutual
SRS
interference, the initial
n_{sync}
is typically not perfectly aligned with the
SRS
peak but falls somewhere between replicas.
Second, even after fine synchronization, the
UAV
’s and
UEs
’ clock drift over time, introducing an increasing offset.

Since fine synchronization can only be accurately aligned with respect to a single received
SRS
(the others will remain slightly misaligned, except when the
UAV
is located in correspondence with the
gNB
site), the most powerful received
SRS
is typically chosen as the reference.
The residual error associated with this reference path at the
q
th
SRS
reception is then estimated as

\hat{\varepsilon}_{\bar{p},\bar{u}}(q)=\left\lfloor L\bigl(\hat{f}^{(1)}(q)-\hat{\alpha}^{(1)}(q)/8\bigr)\right\rfloor,

(26)

where the first iteration of the frequency estimation process always corresponds to the strongest received replica.

IV-C

Measurement Cleaning and False Positive Handling

After frequency estimation, the UAV obtains vectors of estimated metrics

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=[\hat{\gamma}^{(1)},\dots,\hat{\gamma}^{(i_{end})}]^{T}

and corresponding cyclic shifts

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=[\hat{\alpha}^{(1)},\dots,\hat{\alpha}^{(i_{end})}]^{T}
,
where each element represents a detected user-path pair
(u,p)
and
i_{end}
is the maximum number of iterations. To prepare for localization, only the strongest component per UE is retained.
For each
UE

u\in\mathcal{U}_{b}
, define

\mathcal{J}_{u}=\{j\mid\hat{\alpha}^{(j)}=\alpha_{u}\},\hat{\gamma}_{u}^{\max}=\max_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{u}}\hat{\gamma}^{(j)}
,
and discard all other replicas. The cleaned measurement vector is

\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\mathrm{SRS}}=[\,\hat{\gamma}_{1}^{\max},\dots,\hat{\gamma}_{U}^{\max}\,]
.
Missed detections due to low received power can be flagged for subsequent processing.

False positives may occur when other received signals exceed the detection threshold
M_{\mathrm{th}}
, typically from narrowband signals with slow variations.
In this case, the algorithm applies the nominal SRS preprocessing and a second
DFT
.
Under the SRS hypothesis, structured peaks appear at specific frequency bins; under random data, the spectrum is noise-like.
The
BIC
-based model selection automatically distinguishes these cases: if the first iteration is selected, a false positive is declared, and the synchronization metric
M[n]
is recomputed on newly acquired samples.

IV-D

UEs Localization

The accurate estimation of
UE
positions relies fundamentally on the correlation metric
M[n]
introduced in Section
IV-A
, which captures the temporal and frequency structure of the SRS signals.
Since each UAV is equipped with
N_{ant}
antennas, at every measurement instant
r
the receiver obtains, for each
UE

u
, the antenna-specific metric vector

\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\mathrm{SRS},ant}^{(u)}[r]=\big[\,\gamma_{\mathrm{SRS}}^{(u,1)}[r],~\dots,~\gamma_{\mathrm{SRS}}^{(u,N_{ant})}[r]\,\big]^{T}
.

To reduce antenna-dependent small-scale fading fluctuations while retaining the large-scale spatial trend relevant for localization, we select, for each
UE

u
, at the measurement instant
r
, the antenna whose
SRS
-derived metric exhibits the highest metric

\ell_{u}^{\star}[r]=\arg\max_{\ell\in\mathcal{A}}~\gamma_{\mathrm{SRS},ant}^{(u,\ell)}[r],

(27)

and define the selected measurement sequence as

\gamma_{\mathrm{SRS}}^{(u)}[r]\triangleq\gamma_{\mathrm{SRS},ant}^{(u,\ell_{u}^{\star}[r])}[r]
.
By stacking the selected sequences over time, we obtain

\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{SRS}}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\mathrm{SRS}}^{(1)}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\mathrm{SRS}}^{(U)}\end{bmatrix}^{\!T}\in\mathbb{R}^{U\times R}.

Each row is normalized independently:

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{SRS}}^{(u)}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\mathrm{SRS}}^{(u)}}{\max_{r}\gamma_{\mathrm{SRS}}^{(u)}[r]},\qquad u=1,\dots,U,

(28)

and unreliable measurements are discarded using

\mathcal{R}_{u}=\{\,r\mid\tilde{\gamma}_{\mathrm{SRS}}^{(u)}[r]\geq\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}\,\}.
Each valid sample is then mapped to the UAV position and localization weight:

\mathbf{p}_{u,r}=\mathbf{p}^{(2D)}[r],\quad w_{u,r}=\tilde{\gamma}_{\mathrm{SRS}}^{(u)}[r]
.

The localization is performed using a weighted mean-shift procedure

\hskip-5.69046pt\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{u}^{(i+1)}=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_{u}}\hskip-5.69046pt\mathbf{p}_{u,r}w_{u,r}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{p}_{u,r}-\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{u}^{(i)})^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{-1}(\mathbf{p}_{u,r}-\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{u}^{(i)})}}{\displaystyle\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_{u}}\hskip-5.69046ptw_{u,r}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{p}_{u,r}-\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{u}^{(i)})^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{-1}(\mathbf{p}_{u,r}-\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{u}^{(i)})}}

(29)

where

\mathbf{H}=\operatorname{diag}\!\left([h_{x}^{2},\;h_{y}^{2}]^{T}\right)
, and
h_{x}
and
h_{y}
denote the kernel bandwidths along the East–West and North–South directions, respectively. The algorithm iterates until

\|\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{u}^{(i+1)}-\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{u}^{(i)}\|<\delta,

and the final estimate is denoted by
\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{u}
.
The proposed localization workflow is summarized in Alg.
2
.

During the localization process, the
UAV
first moves to the center of the designated polygonal search area and then executes an initial perimeter scan.
The collected measurements are immediately processed onboard to provide a first coarse estimate for each
UE
position.
Then, the UAV autonomously transitions to a refinement phase, during which it performs a small-scale, high-resolution search (typically hexagonal) centered around each preliminary estimate.
This phase yields dense measurement clusters that further improve the localization accuracy in real time.
Once all
UEs
are refined, the UAV transmits the final estimates to the
GCS
and executes an autonomous return-to-home sequence, completing the multi-
UE
localization mission.

The first benchmark follows a

UAV

For a fair comparison, the benchmark methods operated over a
40\,\mathrm{MHz}

LoS

20\,\mathrm{MHz}

Each simulation deployed four
UEs
across each single band, while the benchmark methods localized a single

at a time using a
40\,\mathrm{MHz}
transmission, due to their higher sensitivity to interference.
Although this configuration was necessary for the benchmark schemes, it required higher
UE

SNR
, leading to faster
UE
battery depletion in real deployments.
It also increased receiver complexity and reduced the number of measurements per
UE

To evaluate the positioning method, we use the Localization Error (LE) metric, defined for the
u
th
UE
as
\mathrm{LE}_{\!u}=\sqrt{\left\|\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{u}-\mathbf{m}_{u}\right\|^{2}}.
The Average Localization Error (ALE) is then computed as the mean LE over all localized
UEs
.
The cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) of the positioning error obtained with the three methods, along with their corresponding ALE values, are shown in Figs.

10

As depicted, our approach achieves a localization improvement of about 5–6 m compared to
[
21
,
4
]
.

At low flight altitudes

AoA
tends to approach the horizon, which limits its localization capability.
In real deployments, it also requires multi-element arrays with precise geometry and calibration, which is difficult to support on small aerial platforms.

TDoA
based localization faces similar constraints: accurate multilateration needs sub-nanosecond synchronization, and even small timing offsets create meter-level errors.
Resolving closely spaced multipath components also demands large bandwidth, yet reflected paths often arrive only a few nanoseconds apart, which complicates delay estimation at moderate bandwidths. Increasing bandwidth alleviates this but requires high sampling rates and significant onboard processing, incompatible with lightweight
UAV
platforms.
To address the limitations of angle and delay based estimators, both sensitive to multipath and platform constraints, our method employs a signal consistency metric combined with kernel based weighting of the measurements collected along the UAV trajectory.

VI
Experimental Campaign

The
UAV
control and telemetry architecture used for experimental validation of the proposed
UEs
identification and localization system is described below.

VI-A

Testbed Configuration

The hardware configuration and software integration required to enable an autonomous drone capable of detecting one or more
UEs
within a defined area of interest are detailed here. The system consists of an autonomous
UAV
unit and a
GCS
. The setup of the former is shown in Fig.
11a
, while the latter consists of a laptop connected via Wi-Fi. During flight, the
UAV
transmits telemetry data to the
GCS
using a User Datagram Protocol (UDP) channel, with data serialization handled through Google Protocol Buffers (Protobuf). The telemetry stream includes position, altitude, velocity, and system status parameters. The received data are processed and visualized in real-time using a custom Live Drone GPS Tracker application developed with Dash and Leaflet. The autonomous
UAV
unit is further subdivided into a
SDR
, a
Central Processing Unit (CPU)
, and the
UAV
’s flight controller. Each component is described in detail below.

The core
Radio Frequency (RF)
component of the autonomous multi-
UE
localization system is based on the Analog Devices ADALM-Pluto Revision C, a low-cost
Commercial off-the-Shelf (CotS)

SDR
. To enhance spatial measurement diversity and fully exploit the capabilities of its integrated RFIC, the Pluto SDR was modified to expose its dual-channel receive functionality, as shown in Fig.
11a
. This customized SDR module, equipped with the stock dipole antennas, was mounted on the
UAV
platform, as shown in Fig.
11b
. The use of stock antennas provides a wide field of view, which is particularly advantageous during the initial broad-area search phase. To account for differences in channel gain, measurements were performed using a known source generated by an R&S SMA100B CW generator. The channels were found to be within
0.3\,
dB of each other when measuring the same source, so calibration was not required. The
SDR
exposes its functionalities and captures I/Q samples via USB using the Industrial Input/Output (IIO) protocol.

At the other end of the hardware stack is the flight controller, responsible for navigating the
UAV
to a specified location. The flight controller comprises a PixHawk 2.4.8 mounted on a standard F450 UAV frame. This choice ensures compatibility with ArduPilot, enabling autonomous waypoint-guided control and real-time flight data access. Additionally, testing of flight control scripts can be performed through software-in-the-loop simulations. The PixHawk controller exposes these functionalities via its telemetry ports, using the
Micro Air Vehicle Link (MAVLink)
protocol over a UART physical link.

We selected the Nvidia Jetson Orin NX development board as the
CPU
that controls the drone. Located between the
SDR
and the flight controller, the
CPU
is tasked with collecting data from both ends, processing it, and then providing new waypoints to the flight controller. The choice of a Jetson enables offloading to the GPU for processing-intensive tasks. The
SDR
is controlled via Python using Analog Devices’ provided packages, and, in the same framework, the flight controller is handled by the python DroneKit library.

The
GCS
consists of a computer that establishes a wireless link with the onboard processing unit of the
UAV
, implemented using an NVIDIA Jetson module. Communication between the
GCS
and the
UAV
is achieved via Wi-Fi, with mission parameters configured remotely through a secure
Secure Shell (SSH)
connection.

VII
Conclusion

This work demonstrated that a single sensing UAV, operating independently from the communication infrastructure, can reliably capture and interpret real
5G

UL

SRS
transmissions to identify and localize multiple
UEs
in a fully autonomous manner. Beyond validating the proposed synchronization and identification pipeline, the results highlight an important insight: non-serving
UAVs
are sufficient to achieve meter-level localization without requiring large arrays, wide bandwidths, or strict time synchronization.
The experimental campaign confirmed the method’s robustness under hardware limitations typical of lightweight UAV platforms.

Our findings indicate that sensing-oriented
UAVs
can complement existing terrestrial networks by providing on-demand situational awareness in areas that lack coverage or where infrastructure is temporarily unavailable.
Future work will focus on extending the proposed framework to cooperative multi-UAV operation, enabling distributed sensing, parallel
UE
tracking, and reduced mission times. Coordinated trajectories and shared measurements among multiple platforms have the potential to further enhance scalability, robustness, and situational awareness in complex or large-scale environments.

Expansion of the Detection Metric

Using the notation introduced in Section
IV-A
, the detection metric evaluated at the reference arrival point
n_{0}
can be expressed as

M[n_{0}]=\frac{|P[n_{0}]|^{2}}{|R[n_{0}]|^{2}}.

(30)

The correlation term
P[n_{0}]
expands as

\displaystyle P[n_{0}]=\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}\left(\sum_{u=1}^{U}\sum_{p=1}^{P_{u}}a_{p,u}\,y(lT_{s}-\Delta\tau_{p,u})e^{j2\pi\nu_{u,\mathrm{cfo}}lT_{s}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left(\sum_{v=1}^{U}\sum_{q=1}^{P_{v}}a_{q,v}^{*}\,y^{*}((l+L)T_{s}-\Delta\tau_{q,v})e^{-j2\pi\nu_{v,\mathrm{cfo}}(l+L)T_{s}}\right),

(31)

where we assumed
\nu_{u,\mathrm{cfo}}\gg\nu_{p,u}
.

and

(32)
