Bio-assisted synthesis, characterization, and antibacterial efficacy of titanium dioxide nanoparticles mediated by Pterolobium hexapetalum leaf extract
Authors: D Sushmitha, P R Jeyaramraja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe search for high-efficiency antimicrobial nanomaterials has intensified due to the escalating threat of antibiotic resistance. In this study, titanium dioxide nanoparticles (TiO2 NPs) were synthesized using aqueous leaf extract of Pterolobium hexapetalum. Physicochemical characterization confirmed the formation of a pure nanocrystalline anatase phase (XRD) with a primary crystallite size of 10–15 nm and an optical band gap of 3.18 eV. While UV–Visible spectroscopy showed characteristic absorption bands at 209.5 and 270.5 nm, DLS and Zeta potential (+ 9 mV) measurements indicated that the nanoparticles exist as aggregated colloidal clusters with a hydrodynamic diameter of 399.2 nm. FTIR and SEM verified the presence of plant-derived functional groups and a clustered, rough surface morphology. Biological evaluation via agar well diffusion, MIC, and MBC assays demonstrated that the TiO2 NPs significantly outperformed the parent leaf extract. At a dose of 100 µg/well, the TiO2 NPs produced inhibition zones of 22.67 mm for Staphylococcus aureus and 27.67 mm for Escherichia coli, surpassing the absolute zones produced by the ciprofloxacin (5 µg/well) control. The MIC values (31.25–62.5 µg/mL) and an MBC/MIC ratio of 2 indicate a potent, primarily bactericidal mode of action, achieving a two- to four-fold increase in potency over the crude extract. These findings establish P. hexapetalum–mediated TiO2 NPs as a promising platform for antimicrobial applications in wound care and decontamination technologies.Graphical abstract Keywords: Anatase, Antibacterial activity, Plant-mediated synthesis, Pterolobium hexapetalum, Titanium dioxide nanoparticles

Introduction

Nanotechnology has emerged as a transformative platform in biomedicine, environmental remediation, and materials science, largely because nanoscale materials display physicochemical properties that differ markedly from their bulk counterparts [1, 2]. Among engineered nanomaterials, metal and metal-oxide nanoparticles have been widely explored as antimicrobial agents, photocatalysts, and drug carriers [3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. However, concerns over the cost, limited availability, and potential cytotoxicity of noble metal nanoparticles such as silver and gold have stimulated interest in alternative materials that are cheaper, chemically robust, and more biocompatible [8, 9].

Titanium dioxide nanoparticles (TiO2 NPs) meet many of these criteria. TiO2 is abundant, inexpensive, and chemically stable, with high photocatalytic activity, a large specific surface area, and strong UV-blocking properties, which together underpin its use in water treatment, self-cleaning surfaces, sunscreens, and biomedical coatings [1, 10]. Compared with several other metal oxides and noble metal nanoparticles, TiO2 NPs are generally regarded as more biocompatible, and they have a long history of use as a food and cosmetic additive. Nevertheless, their biocompatibility is context-dependent, and nanoparticle size, dose, crystal phase, surface chemistry, and exposure conditions can influence oxidative stress and cytotoxicity; therefore, their biomedical use requires careful characterization and evaluation [11, 12]. Even so, TiO2 NPs can generate reactive oxygen species and surface interactions that inactivate a broad spectrum of microorganisms [8, 9]. These features make TiO2 NPs particularly attractive as multifunctional antimicrobial agents for controlling clinically important pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli [10].

Conventional physical and chemical routes for synthesizing TiO2 NPs, including sol–gel, hydrothermal and high-energy milling methods, typically require elevated temperatures, organic solvents and/or hazardous reducing agents and are often energy intensive [1, 13, 14]. In contrast, green or biogenic synthesis exploits biological systems, especially plant extracts, as capping and stabilizing agents, thereby minimizing the use of toxic chemicals, lowering energy inputs and improving the colloidal stability and biocompatibility of the resulting nanoparticles [8, 15, 16]. Phytochemicals including phenolics, flavonoids, alkaloids, terpenoids and organic acids can chelate titanium precursors, mediate nucleation and growth, and remain adsorbed as an organic corona that modulates surface charge, dispersion and interactions with biological targets [1, 10, 16]. Several comparative studies report that plant-mediated TiO2 NPs exhibit equal or superior antibacterial and photocatalytic performance relative to chemically synthesized or commercial TiO2, which has been attributed to smaller crystallite size, higher specific surface area and phytochemical-derived surface functionalities [13, 14, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. Collectively, these findings support plant-based synthesis as a viable route for producing value-added TiO2 nanomaterials with enhanced functional performance, while the magnitude of improvement remains system- and assay-dependent.

Pterolobium hexapetalum (Roth.) Sant & Wagh is a medicinal shrub used in traditional Indian medicine [22, 23, 24] and reported to contain flavonoids, phenolics, tannins, glycosides, saponins, and alkaloids [22, 23]. These phytochemicals suggest that the plant may serve as a suitable capping source for the synthesis of TiO2 NPs. In this context, the species represents an underexplored yet promising candidate for bioinspired nanomaterial synthesis.

While plant-mediated synthesis of TiO2 NPs is well-documented across various medicinal species, a review of current literature reveals that P. hexapetalum has not yet been utilized for this purpose. Existing research on this species remains confined to phytochemical profiling and pharmacological evaluations of crude extracts [22, 23], leaving a distinct gap at the interface of ethnopharmacology and nanotechnology. To address this, the present study details the first bio-assisted synthesis and characterization of P. hexapetalum-mediated TiO2 NPs, alongside an evaluation of their antibacterial efficacy against clinically relevant strains.

We hypothesize that the aqueous leaf extract of P. hexapetalum functions as a dual capping and stabilizing agent, facilitating the formation of stable, nanocrystalline TiO2 NPs. Furthermore, we anticipate that these synthesized NPs will demonstrate superior antibacterial activity against S. aureus and E. coli compared to the crude extract alone. This enhanced performance is likely driven by the “phytochemical corona” derived from the plant, which may improve colloidal stability and work synergistically with TiO2’s inherent photocatalytic and ROS-generating properties to augment bactericidal action [8, 9].

Accordingly, the objectives of this study were: (i) to synthesize TiO2 NPs using the aqueous leaf extract of P. hexapetalum; (ii) to characterize the resulting nanoparticles using UV–visible spectroscopy, dynamic light scattering, zeta potential analysis, X-ray diffraction, FTIR spectroscopy and SEM in order to elucidate their optical, structural and surface properties; and (iii) to evaluate and compare the antibacterial activities of the P. hexapetalum leaf extract and the plant-mediated synthesized TiO2 NPs against S. aureus and E. coli using agar well diffusion, MIC and MBC assays. By integrating a traditionally valued medicinal plant with nanotechnology, this work aims to develop a novel bioinorganic antimicrobial platform and to provide insight into the role of plant-derived phytochemicals in tailoring TiO2 NP properties and biological performance.

Materials and methods

Plant material

Fresh leaves from healthy plants of P. hexapetalum were collected between 7 and 8 am at Maruthamalai (Latitude 11.046024°; Longitude 76.854564°), Coimbatore, India, during December 2024. The collection of plant materials complied with local guidelines, and no permission or licence is required [25]. The leaves were washed with distilled water and air-dried in the shade. They were then homogenized into a fine powder using a mixer grinder (Maharaja Whiteline Smart), sieved, and then stored in amber bottles at 4 °C. The taxonomic identity of the plant was confirmed from the Botanical Survey of India, Southern Regional Centre, Coimbatore, Tamil Nadu. The voucher specimen has been deposited at the institutional herbarium (ID 2024-12-20-013).

Preparation of aqueous plant extract

Dried leaf powder (40 g) was extracted with 400 mL of distilled water (1 g : 10mL, w/v). The mixture was processed in a covered Erlenmeyer flask wrapped with aluminium foil on a temperature-controlled orbital shaker (300 rpm) at 50 °C for 1 h. This was done to minimize solvent evaporation and contamination while enhancing the extraction of water-soluble phytochemicals. After extraction, the slurry was cooled and filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper. The filtrate volume recovered was 308 ± 2 mL. This filtrate was used immediately for nanoparticle synthesis or was used to obtain dry extract for antibacterial assays. To obtain the dry extract, the filtrate was dried in triplicate in a pre-weighed crucible at 60 °C, and the dry weight was recorded as 6.42 ± 0.04 g. This corresponded to an extraction yield of 16.06 ± 0.09% (w/w). The dry weight was used to calculate the stock concentration (mg/mL), ensuring accurate dosing for subsequent experiments.

Plant-mediated synthesis of TiO2 NPs

For the plant-mediated synthesis of TiO2 NPs, the reaction conditions were chosen based on preliminary trials to ensure controlled hydrolysis and reproducible nanoparticle formation. A precursor solution was prepared by dissolving 72 µl of titanium tetraisopropoxide (TTIP; Sisco Research Laboratories) in absolute ethanol to reach a final volume of 50 mL, yielding a concentration of 5 mM. This anhydrous environment prevented premature hydrolysis of the precursor. Controlled hydrolysis was then initiated by dropwise addition of the TTIP-ethanol solution into 50 mL of the aqueous plant extract under vigorous magnetic stirring (500 rpm) at room temperature, resulting in a final TTIP concentration of 2.5 mM in the reaction mixture. The pH of the reaction mixture was 5.5. The stirring was continued for 5 h, a duration selected from preliminary trials to permit complete hydrolysis, condensation, nucleation, and surface stabilization of the nanoparticles under the chosen reaction conditions. The resulting suspension was centrifuged at 9500 rpm for 15 min. The pellet was washed thrice with distilled water and ethanol to remove unreacted precursors, dried at 55 °C, and finally calcined at 500 °C for 3 h in a muffle furnace to obtain the crystalline of TiO2 NP powder. The mass of TiO2 NPs produced was 16 mg.

Characterization of TiO2 NPs

UV-Visible spectroscopy

For UV-Vis spectrophotometric analysis employing Shimadzu UV Vis Spectrophotometer UV-3600, a stock suspension was prepared by dispersing 10 mg of the calcined powder in 10 mL of ultrapure water (1 mg/mL) via ultrasonication for 15 min. To minimize light scattering artifacts and ensure the absorbance remained within the linear range, this stock was further diluted to a concentration of 0.1 mg/mL prior to scanning from 200 to 800 nm.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta potential

The size distribution of TiO2 NPs was determined using DLS (Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS). Nanoparticles were dispersed in ultrapure water (pH 7) at a concentration of 0.01 mg/mL and subjected to 15 min ultrasonication to ensure uniform dispersion. Samples were equilibrated for 10 min at 25 °C before measurement. Scattered light intensity was recorded at 173° (backscatter mode) using the cumulants method to yield z-average hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity index. Zeta potential was simultaneously determined via electrophoretic light scattering in the same dispersion medium to assess surface charge and predict colloidal stability.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

XRD analysis was performed using the PANalytical X’Pert Pro (Spectris) to study the crystalline structure of synthesized TiO2 NPs. The measurements used Cu Kα₁ radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) at 40 kV and 30 mA. Analysis was performed over a range of diffraction angles (2θ) from 10⁰ to 90⁰ at a scanning rate of 2⁰/min. The crystallite size was estimated from the broadened XRD peaks using the Scherrer equation [26] as an approximate measure of nanocrystalline size; possible contributions from microstrain and instrumental broadening were not separately resolved.

FTIR analysis

FTIR spectra were acquired on a Shimadzu FTIR spectrometer to identify surface functional groups on the TiO2 NPs. Samples were prepared by thoroughly mixing the TiO2 NPs with spectroscopic-grade KBr and pressing the mixture into a transparent KBr pellet using a hydraulic press. Spectra were recorded over the 4000–500 cm− 1 range and analyzed to identify characteristic absorption bands associated with organic capping and stabilization of TiO2 NPs.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

SEM images of TiO2 NPs were acquired on an FEI Quanta 200 SEM. TiO2 NPs were dispersed in isopropanol (0.5 mg/mL) and sonicated for 10 min. A 5 µL aliquot was deposited on carbon tape mounted on an aluminum stub and air-dried. Representative images shown were recorded in secondary-electron (SE) mode at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV, spot size 3.5, and a working distance of 13.6 mm.

Antibacterial activity of plant extract and TiO2 NPs

Antibacterial activity by agar well diffusion

PSG Institute of Medical Sciences and Research provided bacterial strains. Bacterial strains of Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli were cultured overnight in Mueller–Hinton Broth (MHB) and adjusted to a turbidity equivalent to a 0.5 McFarland standard. Mueller–Hinton Agar (MHA) plates were prepared with a uniform depth of 4 mm. The bacterial suspension was spread evenly across the agar surface using a sterile cotton swab in three directions to ensure a confluent lawn. Wells (8 mm diameter) were aseptically punched using a sterile cork borer. Aliquots of 100 µl containing varying masses of plant extract or TiO2 NPs (25, 50, 75, and 100 µg per well) were loaded into the wells. The concentrations of TiO2 NPs were chosen based on preliminary range-finding experiments designed to identify a range that produced measurable inhibition zones for both test organisms. Ciprofloxacin (5 µg per well) was used as a reference positive control, while sterile distilled water served as the negative control. To allow for the radial diffusion of the treatments prior to bacterial growth, plates were refrigerated at 4 °C for 15 min before being incubated at 35 °C for 18 h. The zones of inhibition were measured along two perpendicular axes and the mean diameter (mm) was recorded. The antibacterial activity of TiO2 NPs and plant extract was evaluated based on the diameter of the inhibition zones. As agar diffusion depends on both antimicrobial activity and diffusion characteristics of the test substances, the results are presented as a semi-quantitative assessment, and the ciprofloxacin control was used as a reference standard for comparison of antibacterial susceptibility rather than for direct mass-based potency comparison.

Determination of MIC and MBC

The MIC was determined via the broth microdilution method in sterile 96-well microtiter plates, following CLSI M07 guidelines for aerobic bacteria. Two-fold serial dilutions of the test samples were prepared in MHB to achieve final concentrations ranging from 1000 down to 1.95 µg/mL. A 0.5 McFarland bacterial suspension (approx. 1 × 108 CFU/mL) was diluted 1 : 100 in MHB to reach a concentration of 1 × 106 CFU/mL. 50 µl of this diluted inoculum was added to each well containing 50 µl of the test sample, resulting in the standard final density of 5 × 105 CFU/mL. Then, 30 µl of 0.015% resazurin was added to each well as a redox indicator of cell viability. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration that prevented a colour change from blue (resazurin) to pink (resorufin). Ciprofloxacin (0.015–8 µg/mL) was included as a reference antibiotic control to verify bacterial susceptibility and assay performance. Because TiO2 NPs differ from conventional antibiotics in physicochemical properties and mechanism of antibacterial action, their MIC values were determined over a broader concentration range and are not intended for direct mass-based potency comparison with ciprofloxacin.

For the MBC, 10 µl aliquots were taken from all wells showing no visible growth (or remaining blue) and sub-cultured onto fresh MHA plates. These plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The MBC was identified as the lowest concentration that resulted in no colony growth.

Statistical analysis

All antibacterial assays (agar well diffusion/MIC/MBC) were performed in three independent biological replicates, and each condition was tested in duplicate technical replicates using freshly prepared inoculum. Zone of inhibition data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26). One-way ANOVA was used to assess differences among treatment groups, and Duncan’s Multiple Range Test (DMRT) was applied as a post-hoc multiple comparison test to determine which means differed significantly. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

The UV–Visible absorption spectrum of the biosynthesized TiO2 NPs (0.1 mg/mL in ultrapure water) recorded from 200 to 800 nm showed two prominent bands at 209.5 and 270.5 nm (Fig. 1). The band at 270.5 nm lies in the deep-UV region and is consistent with the optical response commonly reported for plant-mediated TiO2 NPs prepared with various plant extracts such as Eugenia caryophyllata, Impatiens rothii, Citrus reticulata [16, 27, 28]. However, the peak position in the UV–Vis spectrum alone cannot be used to determine the optical band gap directly. Therefore, Tauc plot analysis was performed to obtain a more reliable estimate of the band gap.

Tauc Plot and UV-vis absorbance spectra of TiO2 NPs

The optical band gap was estimated by plotting (⍺hv)2 against photon energy (hv) (Fig. 1). Extrapolation of the linear portion of the absorption edge to the energy axis gave a band gap value of 3.18 eV, which is in good agreement with the reported band gap of anatase TiO2 [29, 30, 31]. This result supports the assignment of the synthesized material as nanocrystalline anatase TiO2.

The absorption band at 209.5 nm may be associated with surface-related electronic transitions, such as O2−→Ti4+ charge transfer, and may also reflect the influence of plant-derived surface species associated with the nanoparticles [1, 32]. However, in the absence of the UV–Vis spectrum of the plant extract alone, this assignment should be considered tentative. The later FTIR analysis supports the presence of plant-derived functional groups on the nanoparticle surface, but the UV–Vis band itself is interpreted cautiously as a possible combined contribution from TiO2 electronic transitions and surface-associated phytochemical residues.

The spectrum is dominated by UV absorption with negligible absorbance in the visible region, which is expected for TiO2 NPs and is consistent with their intrinsic optical behaviour [33]. Overall, the UV–Vis spectrum together with the Tauc plot confirms the formation of TiO2 NPs with a band gap typical of anatase phase.

Dynamic light scattering revealed a Z-average hydrodynamic diameter of 399.2 nm with a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.526 for the biosynthesized TiO2 NPs (Fig. 2). Because DLS measures the hydrodynamic diameter of solvated particles and aggregates rather than primary crystallite size, this value is expected to exceed the XRD-derived crystallite size and suggests the presence of nanoparticle aggregates in suspension [34]. The PDI value of 0.526 indicates a highly polydisperse system with a broad size distribution, reflecting substantial heterogeneity in the colloidal dispersion [1, 34]. Such behaviour is common in biologically-synthesized TiO2 NPs, where phytochemical capping, interparticle interactions, and partial aggregation can produce hydrodynamic sizes in the hundreds of nanometres [28, 35]. The high PDI suggests that the suspension is not suitable for applications requiring monodisperse nanoparticles without further dispersion control, but it remains relevant for evaluating the antibacterial potential of aggregated biosynthesized TiO2. Overall, the DLS data confirm that the TiO2 NPs exist in aqueous medium as aggregated colloidal entities rather than as isolated primary crystallites.

Dynamic light scattering analysis of TiO2 NPs

The zeta potential of the TiO2 nanodispersion was + 9 mV (Fig. 3), indicating a slightly positive surface charge that likely arises from protonated surface hydroxyl groups and adsorbed biomolecules from the P. hexapetalum leaf extract. In colloidal systems, zeta potential values with low absolute magnitude are associated with weak electrostatic repulsion and poor dispersion stability [36]. Accordingly, the measured value of + 9 mV suggests poor colloidal stability and a strong tendency toward aggregation, rather than a stable dispersed state [37]. The low zeta potential is also consistent with the large hydrodynamic diameter (399.2 nm) and high PDI (0.526) observed in DLS, indicating that the nanoparticles exist predominantly as aggregated colloidal entities in aqueous dispersion. While plant-derived surface species may have influenced nanoparticle formation and surface properties, this study does not provide direct evidence for steric stabilization or long-term dispersion stability. Low zeta potential values are common in plant-mediated nanoparticle systems, where surface-bound biomolecules may provide limited stabilization despite an overall tendency to agglomerate [1, 35].

Zeta potential of TiO2 NPs

The XRD pattern of the biosynthesized TiO2 NPs (Fig. 4) exhibits distinct diffraction peaks at 2θ ≈ 25.3°, 37.8°, 48.0°, 53.9°, 55.1°, 62.7°, 70.3, 75.1, and 82.7°, indexed to the (101), (004), (200), (105), (211), (204), (220), (215), and (301) lattice planes, respectively. These reflections match well with the standard JCPDS card no. 21-1272 for tetragonal anatase TiO2, with no additional peaks corresponding to rutile or brookite, confirming the phase purity and successful formation of single-phase anatase nanoparticles [38]. The (101) reflection is the most intense peak in the pattern and is the characteristic dominant peak of anatase TiO2, confirming the formation of anatase phase [39]. Comparable with our findings, the TiO2 NPs synthesized using green tea extract exhibited a dominant anatase structure with a crystal size of 21.04 nm [40].

X-ray diffraction patterns of plant-mediated synthesized TiO2 NPs

The broadening of the anatase peaks indicates the nanocrystalline nature of the synthesized TiO2. The crystalline size was estimated using the Scherrer equation applied to the main anatase reflections (101, 004, 200, and 105), yielding an average crystallite size in the range of 10–15 nm. This value should be considered an approximate crystallite size, as peak broadening may also include contributions from microstrain and instrumental broadening, which were not separately resolved in this study. Nevertheless, the estimated crystallite size falls within the typical range reported for plant-mediated TiO2 NPs [1, 10, 41]. Such small crystallites provide a large specific surface area and abundant surface active sites, characteristics that are advantageous for catalytic and antimicrobial applications of biologically-synthesized TiO2 NPs [1, 10]. Overall, the XRD analysis confirms that the P. hexapetalum leaf extract route yields phase-pure, nanocrystalline anatase TiO2.

The crystallite size estimated from XRD was in the range of 10–15 nm, whereas DLS revealed a much larger hydrodynamic diameter of 399.2 nm with a high PDI. This difference is expected because XRD measures the size of coherent diffracting domains, i.e., individual crystallites, while DLS measures the hydrodynamic size of suspended species, which includes the solvation shell and, importantly, nanoparticle aggregates. The substantially larger DLS size therefore indicates that the TiO2 NPs are not present as isolated primary crystallites in water but rather as aggregated colloidal clusters. This interpretation is further supported by the low zeta potential value (+ 9 mV), which suggests weak electrostatic stabilization and a strong tendency toward aggregation. Taken together, the XRD, DLS, and zeta potential results provide a consistent picture of nanoscale anatase crystallites that form larger aggregates in aqueous dispersion.

The FTIR spectrum of the P. hexapetalum leaf extract (Fig. 5a) shows a broad band at 3302.13 cm−1, assigned to O–H stretching vibrations of hydroxyl-containing compounds such as water, phenols, and alcohols [28]. The bands at 1635.64 cm−1 and 1363.67 cm−1 are consistent with C = O/C = C and C–N or O–H bending vibrations, respectively, while the band at 1222.87 cm−1 is attributable to C–O or C–O–C stretching of oxygenated phytochemical constituents [42]. These features indicate that the leaf extract contains multiple oxygenated functional groups capable of participating in nanoparticle formation.

FTIR spectra of P. hexapetalum leaf extract (a) and bio-synthesized TiO2 NPs b

In the FTIR spectrum of the biosynthesized TiO2 NPs (Fig. 5b), the corresponding bands appear at 3400.50, 1695.43, 1517.98, 1354.03, and 1224.80 cm−1, with slight shifts in position and intensity relative to the crude extract. Such shifts suggest that plant-derived functional groups were involved in the stabilization process and remained associated with the nanoparticle surface after synthesis. However, FTIR alone cannot prove chemisorption of individual phytochemicals, so these bands are best interpreted as evidence of surface-associated organic residues rather than definitive molecular binding.

The comparison between the extract and nanoparticle spectra supports the role of P. hexapetalum phytochemicals in nanoparticle synthesis and surface modification. Nevertheless, the low zeta potential and high PDI observed in the colloidal characterization indicate that the resulting dispersion is only weakly stabilized and prone to aggregation, so any steric effect from surface-bound organic groups should be interpreted as limited and not as evidence of strong long-term colloidal stability.

SEM imaging of the P. hexapetalum–mediated TiO2 NPs at 3000× (Fig. 6a) and 2000× (Fig. 6b) revealed dense assemblies of irregular, plate-like and granular micrometre-scale agglomerates. The surfaces appear rough and fragmented, with many smaller grains decorating larger blocks, indicating a clustered morphology formed during drying. Such agglomerated structures are commonly observed in bio-assisted TiO2 preparations and are generally attributed to the high surface energy of nanocrystals, van der Waals interactions, and particle-particle association during sample preparation and drying [43, 44, 45].

SEM micrographs of TiO2 NPs. 3000× magnification, scale bar: 20 μm (a); 2000× magnification, scale bar: 40 μm b

Individual nanocrystallites in the 10–15 nm range inferred from XRD cannot be resolved at the magnifications used in SEM; therefore, the SEM images are interpreted only as evidence of the surface morphology and agglomeration state of the dried material. SEM typically shows aggregated masses while TEM or XRD confirms the underlying nanometric domains [28, 43, 45]. This strong aggregation may reduce suitability for applications that require a stable, highly dispersed nanoparticle suspension. However, the clustered morphology still offers a large external surface and may remain advantageous for surface-dependent functions such as antibacterial activity, where accessible active sites are important. Higher magnification SEM or TEM would be required to directly visualize the primary nanocrystallites; however, based on the XRD peak broadening and optical band gap analysis, the primary TiO2 domains are inferred to be in the nanometre range, while SEM primarily reveals their agglomerated morphology. Taken together, XRD and Tauc analysis confirm the nanocrystalline nature of TiO2, while DLS, zeta potential, and SEM indicate that these nanocrystals aggregate to form larger clusters in suspension and micron-scale agglomerates upon drying.

Ciprofloxacin was included as a reference antibiotic control for antibacterial assay validation, while the antibacterial activity of TiO2 NPs was assessed independently over a broader concentration range. Because agar diffusion and broth microdilution responses depend on both antimicrobial potency and physicochemical behavior, the inhibition zones and MIC/MBC values should be interpreted as qualitative or semi-quantitative indicators of antibacterial activity rather than as direct mass-based potency comparisons between TiO2 NPs and ciprofloxacin.

The agar well diffusion assay demonstrated dose-dependent antibacterial activity of both the P. hexapetalum leaf extract and the corresponding TiO2 NPs against S. aureus and E. coli (Tables 1 and 2; Fig. 7). For S. aureus, zones of inhibition produced by the plant extract increased from 12.33 mm at 25 µg/well to 20.67 mm at 100 µg/well, whereas TiO2 NPs produced larger zones ranging from 14.33 to 22.67 mm over the same concentration range. While the 100 µg/well dose of TiO2 NPs produced a larger absolute zone of inhibition than the ciprofloxacin standard (19.33 mm), it is important to note that ciprofloxacin achieved this substantial effect at a much lower concentration of only 5 µg/well, highlighting its superior potency. The considerable activity of the nanoformulation in the present study is consistent with reports that biosynthesized TiO2 NPs exhibit enhanced bactericidal effects compared to their source extracts [10, 46].

Against E. coli, both treatments showed higher inhibition than against S. aureus at equivalent doses, with TiO2 NPs again outperforming the crude extract. At a concentration of 100 µg/well, the TiO2 NPs and the plant extract achieved maximum inhibition zones of 27.67 mm and 24.67 mm, respectively; notably, the ciprofloxacin standard produced a substantial zone of 20.67 mm at a significantly lower concentration of only 5 µg/well. This heightened susceptibility of E. coli aligns with previous observations that TiO2 NPs, especially when capped with phytochemicals, generate reactive oxygen species and membrane damage leading to broad-spectrum antibacterial activity [9, 47]. Together with the known richness of P. hexapetalum in phenolic and flavonoid constituents [23], the enhanced activity of TiO2 NPs could simply result from the high surface area of TiO2 presenting the same phytochemicals more effectively.

The MIC and MBC data confirm that both the P. hexapetalum leaf extract and the synthesized TiO2 NPs possess potent antibacterial activity against S. aureus and E. coli (Table 3). For S. aureus, the plant extract showed MIC and MBC values of 125 and 500 µg/mL, respectively, whereas TiO2 NPs exhibited two-fold lower MIC (62.5 µg/mL) and four-fold lower MBC (125 µg/mL). A similar trend was observed for E. coli, where TiO2 NPs reduced the MIC/MBC from 62.5/250 µg/mL (extract) to 31.25/62.5 µg/mL. These reductions are statistically significant at p < 0.05. Ciprofloxacin, as expected, remained the most active agent, with MICs of 1 and 0.5 µg/mL and MBCs of 2 and 1 µg/mL against S. aureus and E. coli, respectively.

The MBC/MIC ratios for TiO2 NPs (2 for both organisms) indicate a primarily bactericidal effect, while the higher ratios for the crude extract (4) suggest comparatively weaker killing, consistent with CLSI interpretive criteria [48]. The greater susceptibility of E. coli relative to S. aureus agrees with previous studies reporting strong activity of TiO2-based nanomaterials against Gram-negative species, attributed to ROS-mediated membrane disruption and interaction with cell wall components [9, 49]. The improved potency of TiO2 NPs over the leaf extract alone likely reflects the high surface area and nanoscale contact interface, as well as synergistic contributions from capping phytochemicals that can facilitate adhesion and oxidative damage to bacterial cells [9, 50].

Limitations

This study provides baseline physicochemical characterization of freshly prepared TiO2 NP dispersions using UV–Vis, DLS, zeta potential, XRD, FTIR, and SEM. However, long-term colloidal stability was not assessed, and the large hydrodynamic size, high PDI, and low zeta potential suggest aggregation and limited dispersion stability in water. Additional EDX and TEM/SAED analyses would further confirm elemental composition and nanoscale crystallinity. The proposed antibacterial mechanisms remain tentative because direct evidence for ROS generation, membrane disruption, or other pathways was not obtained, and toxicity was not evaluated. These issues should be addressed in future studies to establish the practical applicability and safety of the biosynthesized TiO2 NPs.

Conclusion

This work successfully demonstrates the first time use of P. hexapetalum leaf extract as a robust capping and stabilizing matrix for the synthesis of nanocrystalline anatase TiO2. Comprehensive physicochemical characterization revealed the formation of TiO2 NPs with a primary crystal size of 10–15 nm and a characteristic optical band gap of 3.18 eV. Although the colloidal system exhibited a tendency toward aggregation, evidenced by a hydrodynamic diameter of 399.2 nm and a slightly positive zeta potential of + 9 mV, the phytochemical-capped surface maintained high functional reactivity. The successful indexing of the XRD patterns to the tetragonal anatase phase confirms that this plant-mediated route produces high-purity nanocrystals.

Biological evaluation highlighted the superior antibacterial potential of the TiO2 nanoformulation compared to the parent plant extract against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative pathogens. At a concentration of 100 µg/well, the nanoparticles produced significant inhibition zones of 22.67 mm for S. aureus and 27.67 mm for E. coli, which surpassed the absolute zones produced by the ciprofloxacin control at 5 µg/well. Furthermore, the nanoparticles achieved a two-fold reduction in MIC values compared to the crude extract, with an MBC/MIC ratio of 2, confirming a decisive bactericidal mode of action. While ciprofloxacin remained the more potent agent by mass with MICs of 0.5–1 µg/mL, the high activity of the bio-inorganic TiO2 complex suggests it is an excellent candidate for integration into antimicrobial coatings and wound care technologies. Future efforts must address long-term colloidal stability and detailed cytocompatibility profiles to ensure safe clinical and environmental application.
