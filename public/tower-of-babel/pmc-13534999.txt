Introduction

Musculoskeletal infections, encompassing a spectrum of conditions from postoperative site infections to complex spinal pathologies, remain a persistent challenge in orthopaedic surgery. 1 Their rising incidence globally, coupled with wide clinical variability in presentation and progression, has exposed the limitations of standardised treatment protocols. 2 Among these, spinal infections, particularly tuberculous and pyogenic spondylodiscitis are fraught with diagnostic ambiguities, therapeutic controversies, and considerable risk of neurological morbidity and functional impairment.3,4 Despite advancements in imaging modalities, microbiological diagnostics, and surgical technology, orthopaedic practitioners often face dilemmas that are under-supported by definitive evidence, highlighting the need for globally unified recommendations rooted in both science and expert experience.

Recognising this critical gap, the International Consensus Meeting on Infection ICM 2025 (Spine Section) was founded with the mission of orchestrating a collaborative framework through which leading experts in orthopaedic infection could converge, deliberate, and generate actionable, evidence-informed recommendations. The ICM 2025 (spine section) model aims synthesise prevailing literature but also to create a reproducible structure for global consensus development that accommodates geographic diversity, clinical nuance, and multidisciplinary insights.

The Infection ICM 2025 (Spine Section) meeting held in Istanbul represented a landmark initiative, bringing together more than 100 orthopaedic surgeons, infectious disease specialists, microbiologists, spine surgeons, and researchers from all over the world. Compared to prior ICM consensus efforts (eg, 2018), the 2025 process introduced several methodological advances, including centralised literature screening, refined consensus grading thresholds, and broader global participation spanning 33 countries. These innovations improved transparency, reproducibility, and implementability of recommendations. Further, the earlier ICM work in 2018 was non-specific or mostly on an arthroplasty background, and the current work is exclusively on spine infections, which makes it more relevant to the spine surgeons to aid in clinical practice decisions related to spinal infections. The central theme of this iteration focused on spinal infections, specifically tuberculous and pyogenic spondylodiscitis, owing to their distinct pathogenetic pathways, contrasting epidemiological burdens, and clinical decision-making complexity. Tuberculous spondylodiscitis remains endemic in several regions, yet lacks uniform guidelines on diagnosis and therapeutic duration.3,5 Pyogenic infections, while more frequently encountered in developed healthcare systems, suffer from diagnostic delays, empirical treatment practices, and surgical controversies.4,6 The dual-pathogen framework of this consensus allowed for parallel development of recommendations while respecting the unique considerations each domain presents.

To ensure transparency, reproducibility, and academic rigour, the ICM 2025 (Spine Section) adopted a multiphase methodology that spanned over 2 years and included a series of structured processes. Each phase was time-bound, with clear objectives and deliverables, facilitating accountability and ensuring broad participation. By structuring the work into these well-defined phases, the ICM 2025 (Spine Section) ensured the outcome of this effort is of practical usability for clinicians worldwide. Each recommendation carries not just the weight of evidence but the collective judgment of an internationally representative expert group. This paper seeks to document the full methodology behind the ICM 2025 (Spine Section) meeting from inception to final consensus with the intention of enabling reproducibility for future disease domains. This ICM 2025 (Spine Section) framework provides a scalable template for future consensus efforts in musculoskeletal care, aligning academic rigour with clinical realities across diverse healthcare landscapes.

Methodological Overview

ICM 2025 (Spine Section) Consensus

The ICM 2025 (Spine Section) was convened under the leadership of Prof. S. Rajasekaran (India), as delegated by the central ICM committee. The initiative aimed to develop globally applicable, evidence-informed recommendations addressing 2 key domains: tuberculous and pyogenic spondylodiscitis. A rigorous 11-phase methodology was employed to ensure transparency, inclusiveness, and academic rigour as shown in Figure 1.

Phases of events followed in ICM 2025 (Spine Section)

Phase I: Delegate Identification

A preliminary list of international spine experts comprising key opinion leaders, researchers, and clinicians was curated using academic databases and guidance from regional representatives. The selection emphasised geographic diversity and scholarly credibility. Eligibility criteria included peer-reviewed publications in spinal infection, a minimum of 5 years of clinical experience, and representation across geographic regions and disciplines (spine surgery, infectious disease, microbiology, radiology). Conflicts of interest were declared at the outset, and delegates with relevant conflicts were recused from voting on affected questions.

Phase II: Invitation & Commitment Confirmation

The spine section leadership sent formal invitations to shortlisted experts. Delegates were informed about the scope of engagement, including responsibilities for evidence synthesis and compulsory participation in the face-to-face consensus meeting in Istanbul.

Phase III: Clinical Question Collection

Participating delegates were requested to submit up to 10 clinically relevant questions on spinal infections encompassing both tuberculous and pyogenic spondylodiscitis that they believed required evidence-based guidance. These questions reflected areas of practical uncertainty in global spine care.

Phase IV: Compilation & Ranking of Questions

All submitted questions were compiled, de-duplicated, and structured into a unified list. This consolidated list was circulated among delegates for ranking. Questions were ranked using a standardised scoring instrument (scale 1-10) based on clinical relevance, feasibility, and impact. Tie-breaking was resolved by median score and, if needed, discussion within the core committee.

Phase V: Finalisation of Questions & Thematic Segregation

Questions receiving higher rankings were shortlisted. These were refined for clarity, reformulated using PICO principles, and organised into thematic domains. The resulting structure formed the backbone for both the Tuberculous and Pyogenic Spondylodiscitis sections.

Phase VI: Open Call for Collaboration

An open invitation was extended to delegates for participation in the evaluation of specific questions. Experts could opt to serve as either liaisons responsible for coordinating collaborators and finalising the recommendation draft or as collaborators who would engage in literature review and discussion under each question. Each question was supported by 1 liaison and 3-5 collaborators. Experts were limited to a maximum of 3 questions to ensure focused contribution.

Phase VII: Team Formation & Assignment

Following expert preferences and strategic allocation, teams were assigned to each question. This established the functional working groups responsible for conducting evidence synthesis and recommendation drafting.

Phase VIII: Literature Review & Data Synthesis

Under the oversight of the ICM 2025 (Spine Section) core committee, each team conducted a systematic search of databases including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and https://ClinicalTrials.gov. Searches were limited to studies published from inception till December 31, 2024 and available in English or with available translations. Grey literature was considered, if peer-reviewed or indexed. Each question was formatted into the PICO structure, and a centralised uniform literature search was made by the ICM 2025 (Spine Section) core committee in February 2025. The search records under each question were managed using the Rayyan platform 7 for centralised screening. Each question with the allotted liaison and the team of collaborators was invited to their respective questions by the ICM Spine core committee to proceed with the screening process. The screening process consisted of deduplication, title and abstract screening, followed by full-text screening to identify the relevant studies for further analysis. Reviews adhered to PRISMA guidelines. Inclusion criteria comprised peer-reviewed clinical studies, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses addressing spinal infection. Exclusion criteria included case reports, non-English publications without translation, and studies lacking extractable data. Risk-of-bias was assessed using appropriate tools per study design (eg, Cochrane RoB2 for RCTs, Newcastle-Ottawa Scale for observational studies), and QUADAS-2 for diagnostic accuracy studies. Where sufficient homogeneous data existed, meta-analyses were performed; otherwise, synthesis was narrative. Disagreements during evidence appraisal were resolved through structured team discussion, with the liaison adjudicating and escalation to the ICM 2025 (Spine Section) core committee if consensus was not reached.

Phase IX: Online Discussion & Document Posting

Draft documents and recommendations were uploaded to the ICM website for review. Inter-delegate discussions occurred asynchronously to refine recommendations, address conflicts, and build preliminary consensus ahead of the final meeting. Disagreements during asynchronous phases were resolved through structured moderation with defined timelines. Sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding low-attendance votes to confirm robustness of consensus outcomes.

Phase X: Face-to-Face Consensus Meeting

The consensus meeting was held in Istanbul, where all team members convened physically. Sessions were moderated by ICM 2025 (Spine Section) leadership and involved presentation of evidence summaries, structured discussion, and formal consensus voting on each recommendation. Agreement thresholds were predefined and applied consistently. Consensus grading utilised a categorical voting framework with the following thresholds: unanimous consensus: ≥90% agreement; strong consensus: 75%-89% agreement; moderate consensus: 60%-74% agreement; weak consensus (50%-59%), and no consensus (<50%). Evidence strength was concurrently graded as recommended by the GRADE working group into strong, moderate, low and very low as found appropriate. 8 Voting was conducted using an electronic device uniquely coded to each participant, ensuring anonymity and accurate recording of responses. Votes were cast immediately after presentation of the literature evidence and draft recommendations. Conflicts of interest were declared prior to participation, and delegates with relevant conflicts were recused from voting on those specific questions. Dominance bias was mitigated through structured moderation, equal opportunity for input, and reliance on electronic anonymous voting rather than verbal or visible voting methods. Regional practice variations were balanced by diverse geographic representation and equal weighting of all votes within the predefined consensus thresholds. Abstentions and non-responses were excluded from denominators. Quorum was defined as ≥80% of delegates present. Two independent raters graded evidence per question using GRADE principles. 8 Disagreements were adjudicated by the liaison, with escalation to the core committee if unresolved. Adaptations were applied where direct evidence was limited, ensuring transparency in grading.

Phase XI: Finalisation & Dissemination

The consolidated recommendations, reflecting broad expert agreement and evidence alignment were finalised into a unified document. This was prepared for clinical dissemination and future integration into guidelines, educational programs, and research platforms.

Results

In total, 72 clinically relevant questions were evaluated across 2 thematic domains: Tuberculous Spondylodiscitis (36 questions) and Pyogenic Spondylodiscitis (36 questions), within the ICM 2025 (Spine Section) framework, as shown in Tables 1 and 2, respectively. These were distributed across major clinical domains, including Screening & Initial Evaluation (12 questions), Imaging (10 questions), Microbiology & Histopathology (14 questions), Anti-Tuberculous/Antibiotic Therapy (12 questions), Response Monitoring (6 questions), Surgical Indications (10 questions), Surgical Techniques (5 questions), and Surgical Adjuncts (3 questions). For ease of interpretation, consensus levels are categorised as Unanimous (≥90%), Strong (75%-89%), Moderate (60%-74%), Weak Consensus (50%-59%), and No Consensus (<50%). Evidence strength is graded as Very Low, Low, Moderate, or High, following GRADE principles. 8 A domain-level summary is provided below to highlight the distribution of questions across screening, imaging, microbiology, therapy, and surgical domains. Of the 72 consensus items, 61 (85%) reached unanimous consensus (≥90% agreement), 7 (10%) achieved strong consensus (75%-89%), and 4 (5%) demonstrated moderate or weak consensus (<75%), reflecting areas of ongoing debate and the need for further research. Abstentions were excluded from denominators. A domain-level heatmap summarising consensus percentages by topic (screening, imaging, microbiology, therapy, surgery) is provided in Figure 2A and B for tuberculous and pyogenic spondylodiscitis, respectively. This domain-level overview highlights the breadth of topics addressed and the high level of agreement achieved across most clinical decision points. Each question was collaboratively addressed by a multidisciplinary international panel involving 132 experts across 33 countries, as shown in Figure 3. Contributors included spine surgeons, infectious disease specialists, microbiologists, radiologists, and evidence synthesis methodologists, ensuring a broad spectrum of global expertise and clinical applicability.

Recruitment and Segregation, and processing workflow of questions involved in the recommendation process

For every question, consensus statements were developed through structured literature review, iterative drafting, and graded voting using predefined thresholds. The recommendations spanned multiple domains, including screening and initial evaluation, imaging, microbiological workup, therapy planning, surgical techniques, and adjunctive strategies. Notably, specific topics, such as the timing and indication for surgical intervention in TB with neurological deficits, or the value of biopsy in culture-negative pyogenic infections, elicited uniform expert agreement supported by qualitative evidence grading. Evidence strength was concurrently graded based on literature quality and availability, with the majority of statements classified as a low to moderate level of evidence due to the inherent scarcity of high-quality studies in spinal infection domains.

Discussion

The ICM 2025 (Spine Section) initiative on spinal infection marks a transformative step in evidence-informed orthopaedic guideline development. By addressing two clinically distinct yet globally significant domains, tuberculous and pyogenic spondylodiscitis, the project demonstrated the feasibility of constructing high-value consensus recommendations through a multiphase, multinational collaboration. The rigorous methodology applied herein provided a replicable scaffold for future consensus efforts. The systematic progression from expert selection, topic generation, and team formation to structured literature review and phased consensus voting ensured intellectual diversity and methodological consistency. 132 contributors representing 33 countries participated across 72 questions, demonstrating strong engagement and geographic breadth. The thematic segregation of topics allowed granular exploration of diagnostic, therapeutic, and procedural challenges specific to each infectious entity. This manuscript serves as the methodological keystone for the ICM 2025 (Spine Section), with linked question-specific consensus papers providing detailed evidence tables and recommendation dossiers, all published together as a focus issue.

Our findings reveal that a high proportion of consensus statements achieved unanimous or strong agreement, despite the inherently low to moderate evidence levels surrounding spinal infections. This reflects the clinical urgency of these questions. It also highlights the value of expert synthesis where definitive literature is lacking. Domains such as microbiological workup, imaging interpretation, and surgical decision-making benefited from broad alignment. For example, recommendations favouring tissue biopsy even in typical radiological TB presentations, or cautious interpretation of persistent MRI changes post-treatment, show pragmatic utility shaped by global experience. Conversely, topics involving duration of therapy, role of neo-adjuvant anti-tuberculous treatment (ATT), and postoperative use of adjuncts like vancomycin powder or Vacuum-assisted closure (VAC) devices revealed moderate or split consensus, highlighting areas where data variability or regional practice differences prevail. These nuances emphasise the need to view consensus statements not as static mandates, but as adaptable tools reflective of current expert thought and evolving evidence.

One unique strength of this initiative was the dual-pathogen format. By segmenting recommendations into Tuberculous and Pyogenic Spondylodiscitis, the process respected the divergent diagnostic algorithms, therapeutic timelines, and regional epidemiology each condition demands. This thematic bifurcation not only enabled clearer clinical applicability but may serve as a structural template for future infectious consensus formats in spine or broader orthopaedic contexts. From a methodological standpoint, the use of centralised platforms like Rayyan for literature screening, enabling real-time online communication between team members, and tiered team roles (liaison vs collaborator) has streamlined collaboration while maintaining academic rigour. The structured grading system applied categorising consensus levels and aligning recommendations with GRADE-like evidence tiers offered transparency and reproducibility, addressing a long-standing gap in many surgical guideline frameworks. It is important to note that the outputs of ICM 2025 (Spine Section) represent structured expert consensus rather than formal clinical practice guidelines. While consensus recommendations provide valuable direction in evidence-limited domains, they are inherently constrained by the quality of available literature and the reliance on expert judgment. These statements should therefore be interpreted as adaptable frameworks to guide clinical decision-making, rather than prescriptive mandates.

While the ICM 2025 (Spine Section) methodology set new standards for global orthopaedic consensus efforts, its limitations must be acknowledged. Selection bias is inherent in consensus processes, as participating experts may not fully represent all global practice environments. Evidence scarcity was particularly pronounced in tuberculous spondylodiscitis, where high-quality randomised trials are lacking, and recommendations often relied on observational data or expert judgment. In such contexts, consensus statements provide pragmatic guidance but cannot substitute for definitive evidence. The recommendations are intended to be globally relevant, but their application must be adapted to local contexts. Clinicians in resource-limited settings may face constraints in access to advanced imaging, molecular diagnostics, or surgical adjuncts. For these environments, the consensus statements should be interpreted as flexible frameworks rather than rigid mandates. Recognising the dynamic nature of evidence, the ICM 2025 (Spine Section) has committed to iterative updates. As new data emerge, particularly from ongoing multicenter studies and registries, recommendations will be re-evaluated and regraded. This update pathway ensures that the guidance remains current and responsive to evolving scientific knowledge.

The 2025 spine recommendations represent a significant evolution from earlier ICM outputs. Compared to the 2018 ICM spine section, the 2025 process introduced methodological refinements such as centralised literature screening, standardised consensus bands, and expanded global participation. Importantly, several domains were regraded based on updated evidence, making the 2025 outputs more directive in areas such as biopsy protocols, antibiotic duration, and surgical indications. Within the broader ICM 2025 (Spine Section) program, the spine section exemplifies how consensus can be both rigorous and adaptable, serving as a methodological keystone for linked question-specific papers.

Conclusion

The ICM 2025 (Spine Section) methodology for spinal infection consensus marks a significant advancement in the structured development of global orthopaedic guidelines. By deploying a rigorous, 11-phase approach from expert selection and thematic question refinement to evidence synthesis and consensus voting, the initiative established reproducible standards for collaborative recommendation generation in musculoskeletal infection. Segmenting the consensus into pathogen-specific domains, tuberculous and pyogenic spondylodiscitis, not only enhanced clinical relevance but also accommodated diagnostic and therapeutic diversity across international practice settings. The high degree of consensus achieved across 72 key questions underscores the strength of collective judgment, even when operating within evidence-limited domains.

This methodological framework balances academic integrity with pragmatic decision-making and may serve as a template for future consensus efforts across other orthopaedic subspecialties. The ICM 2025 (Spine Section) emphasis on transparency, peer participation, and structured literature evaluation reinforces its role as a credible engine for harmonising infection care globally. In documenting this process, we offer a scalable blueprint for future initiatives aiming to address ambiguity in clinical care through inclusive expert collaboration and systematic methodology. This framework is designed to be iterative, allowing updates as new evidence emerges. Furthermore, its structured methodology can be adapted to other spine domains, such as degenerative conditions or trauma, thereby serving as a scalable model for future consensus efforts.
