Counterexample-Guided Synthesis of Robust Discrete-Time Control Barrier Functions
Erfan Shakhesi, Alexander Katriniok, W.P.M.H. (Maurice) Heemels

Abstract
Learning-based methods have gained popularity for training candidate Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) to satisfy the CBF conditions on a finite set of sampled states. However, since the CBF is unknown a priori , it is unclear which sampled states belong to its zero-superlevel set and must satisfy the CBF conditions, and which ones lie outside it. Existing approaches define a set in which all sampled states are required to satisfy the CBF conditions, thus introducing conservatism. In this paper, we address this issue for robust discrete-time CBFs (R-DTCBFs). Furthermore, we propose a class of R-DTCBFs that can be used in an online optimization problem to synthesize safe controllers for general discrete-time systems with input constraints and bounded disturbances. To train such an R-DTCBF that is valid not only on sampled states but also across the entire region, we employ a verification algorithm iteratively in a counterexample-guided approach. We apply the proposed method to numerical case studies.



I
Introduction

Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) are Lyapunov-like functions that guarantee the controlled invariance of their zero-superlevel set, defined as the set of all states for which a CBF is non-negative
[
1
]
. Such a function can be used in an online optimization problem to synthesize safe controllers by adjusting known nominal controllers. CBFs are applied both to continuous-time systems
[
1
]
and discrete-time systems
[
2
]
, where, in the latter case, they are called discrete-time CBFs (DTCBFs). DTCBFs offer potential for various applications, including their use within Model Predictive Control frameworks
[
3
]
.
Moreover, to guarantee safety under disturbances, the definitions of CBFs and DTCBFs have been extended to robust CBFs
[
4
]
and robust DTCBFs (R-DTCBFs)
[
5
]
. However, a key limitation of existing R-DTCBF formulations in the literature is that the associated constraint cannot be used in online optimization problems for safe controller synthesis due to the issue of an infinite number of constraints.

To synthesize robust (DT)CBFs, learning-based methods provide an attractive approach for training candidate robust (DT)CBFs based on a finite set of sampled states, as these methods are applied successfully in other settings, including training candidate Control Lyapunov Functions (CLFs)
[
6
]
and candidate Control Barrier Certificates (CBCs)
[
7
]
. Note that the term “candidate” indicates that the trained function is not necessarily valid over the entire region, but only on sampled states.
An additional and crucial difficulty in training candidate (DT)CBFs is that the CBF conditions are imposed only on the states within the zero-superlevel set of the resulting (DT)CBF, but this set is unknown
a priori
. To bypass this issue,
[
8
]
considers an explicit region in which
all
sampled states are required to satisfy the CBF conditions. This introduces
conservatism
, and in some applications, finding such a region may not be possible. Besides this work,
[
9
]
employs an iterative approach to approximate the maximal region in which the CBF conditions can be satisfied. However, not all states within this approximated region satisfy the conditions, and thus the trained function is not necessarily a valid (DT)CBF.

To ensure that a trained function is not only valid on
sampled
states but also on the entire region, two approaches are used in the literature: (i) sufficient sampling of the state space, such as
[
7
]
for CBCs, and
(ii) counterexample-guided
approaches, such as
[
6
]
for CLFs and
[
8
]
for CBFs.
Approach (i)
determines the maximum permissible distance between sampled states using Lipschitz constants and
applies
a threshold to ensure that satisfying the conditions at these states implies satisfaction throughout the region between them.
Although
conceptually elegant, it is computationally demanding with many samples and introduces conservatism. In approach (ii), a verification algorithm searches for a state that does not satisfy the required conditions across the entire region, not just at sampled states. This state is referred to as a counterexample. If a counterexample is found, it is added to the set of sampled states, and the candidate function is retrained. This procedure repeats until no counterexample is found. To the best of our knowledge, a counterexample-guided approach has not been applied to
(R-)DTCBFs
, likely due to the challenge of verifying candidate
(R-)DTCBFs
with trained neural network controllers in the discrete-time setting, where the (R-)DTCBF constraint is non-affine in the control input.

To overcome the above-mentioned challenges, our main contributions in this paper are:

•

We introduce a class of R-DTCBFs that can be used in an online optimization problem to synthesize safe controllers for systems with bounded disturbances (Section
II
).

•

We extend the verification algorithm for DTCBFs
introduced
in our previous work
[
10
]
by modifying its steps so that it either verifies that a candidate R-DTCBF
belongs
to the proposed class or falsifies it by providing a
counterexample
within a predefined tolerance (Section
III
). Our verification algorithm does not require a control policy, making it well-suited for a counterexample-guided approach to learning valid (R-)DTCBFs with less conservatism.

•

We design a loss function that reduces conservatism in
training
candidate (DT)CBFs compared to existing
methods
, promotes larger zero-superlevel sets, and enforces the R-DTCBF conditions on
designated
sampled states. We then propose a counterexample-guided approach to synthesize valid R-DTCBFs from the proposed class (Section
IV
).

•

We apply our proposed methods to various numerical case studies (Section
V
).

II
Robust Discrete-time Control Barrier Function

We consider discrete-time systems of the form

\displaystyle x_{t+1}=f(x_{t},u_{t})+w_{t},

(1)

where

For the system (

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\vcentcolon=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid s_{i}(x)\geqslant 0,~i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n_{s}\}\},

Definition 1
(Robust Controlled Invariance

For the

Definition 2
(Robust Safety)

The system (
1

\mathbb{U}
and
\mathbb{W}

\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and an initial state set

(\mathcal{S},X_{0})

such that for all trajectories of

for all

To guarantee robust safety for the system (
1

\mathcal{S}
.
To do so, the definition of discrete-time Control Barrier Functions
[
2
]
can be extended to robust discrete-time Control Barrier Functions, as in
[
5
]
.

Definition 3
(R-DTCBF)

Consider a function
\hskip-1.42271pth:\hskip-2.84544pt\mathbb{R}^{n}\hskip-2.84544pt\rightarrow\hskip-2.84544pt\mathbb{R}
with zero-superlevel set
\mathcal{C}\vcentcolon=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid h(x)\geqslant 0\}
. For the system (
1
) with the control admissible set

and the disturbance set

,

is a robust discrete-time Control Barrier Function (R-DTCBF), if there exists a
\gamma\in\mathcal{K}^{\leqslant\mathrm{id}}_{\infty}
such that for each

, there exists a control input

that satisfies

\displaystyle h(f(x,u)+w)-h(x)+\gamma(h(x))\geqslant 0\text{\hskip 5.12128ptfor all }w\in\mathbb{W}.

(3)

We also say that a control policy
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a friend of an R-DTCBF pair
(h,\gamma)
, if for each
x\in\mathcal{C}
,
\pi(x)\in\mathbb{U}
and
h(f(x,\pi(x))+w)-h(x)+\gamma(h(x))\geqslant 0
for all
w\in\mathbb{W}
.

Given that the disturbance
w_{t}
at
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
is unknown, the constraint (
3

, which provides a sufficient condition for the satisfaction of (

Lemma 1

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a Lipschitz continuous function on its zero-superlevel set
\mathcal{C}
. Suppose there exists a
\gamma\in\mathcal{K}_{\infty}^{\leqslant\text{id}}
such that, for each

, there exists a control input

satisfying the R-DTCBF constraint

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(x,u)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=h(f(x,u))-h(x)+\gamma(h(x))\geqslant L_{h}w_{\text{max}},

(4)

where

is a Lipschitz constant for

1

-safe, if

Proof:

By the definition of Lipschitz continuity, for all

\displaystyle L_{h}w_{\text{max}}\geqslant L_{h}\lVert

\displaystyle w\rVert\geqslant h(f(x,u))-h(f(x,u)+w).

(5)

By virtue of (
4
) and (
5
), there exists a
\gamma\in\mathcal{K}^{\leqslant\text{id}}_{\infty}
such that for each

exists that for all

\displaystyle h(f(x,u)+w)\geqslant h(x)-\gamma(h(x))\geqslant 0.

Hence,

is an R-DTCBF according to Definition

-safe.
∎

Given an R-DTCBF pair
(h,\gamma)
that satisfies Lemma
1
for system (
1
) with
\mathbb{U}
and
\mathbb{W}
, a nominal controller

at

\displaystyle u^{*}_{t}\in\arg\min_{u\in\mathbb{U}}~

\displaystyle~\lVert u-\pi_{\text{nom}}(x_{t})\rVert^{2}

(7a)

s.t.

\displaystyle~\mathcal{H}(x_{t},u)\geqslant L_{h}w_{\text{max}}.

(7b)

IV-A

Training Candidate R-DTCBFs

IV-A
1
Parameterization

We parameterize
h(x;\vartheta)
using a set of continuously differentiable basis functions and the vector of unknown coefficients
\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\vartheta}}
. Note that keeping the parameterization of
h
simple makes the online optimization problem (
7
) for synthesizing a safe controller more practical by ensuring reasonable computational time. Therefore, we opt not to parameterize
h
as a neural network (NN).

In contrast, the control policy
\pi
is only required to train an R-DTCBF and is not needed afterward. As a result, similar to
[
7
]
and
[
13
]
, we parameterize the control
policy
\pi_{i}
,
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
, with
\pi\vcentcolon=[\pi_{1}~\ldots~\pi_{m}]^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}
as the NN

\left\{\begin{aligned} &y^{(0)}\vcentcolon=x,\\
&y^{(j)}\vcentcolon=\mathrm{ReLU}(W^{(j)}y^{(j-1)}+b^{(j)}),~j\in\{1,2,\dots,l-1\},\\
&\pi_{i}(x;\kappa)\vcentcolon=\mathrm{Hardtanh}(W^{(l)}y^{(l-1)}+b^{(l)},u_{i,\text{min}},u_{i,\text{max}}),\end{aligned}\right.

where
\kappa\vcentcolon=\{W^{(j)},b^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{l}
represents the set of weight matrices and biases, and
l\in\mathbb{N}
is the number of layers. Here,
W^{(j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}\times d_{j-1}}
is a weight matrix,
b^{(j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}}
is a bias vector, and
d_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
is the number of neurons in the
j
-th layer,
j\in\{0,1,\ldots,l\}
. The number of neurons in the input and output layers are
d_{0}\vcentcolon=n
and
d_{l}\vcentcolon=1
, respectively, while the number of neurons in the hidden layers (i.e., for
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,l-1\}
) are user-specified. Moreover, the activation function
\mathrm{ReLU}
(rectified linear unit), defined as
\mathrm{ReLU}(y)\vcentcolon=\max(0,y)
, is applied element-wise in the hidden layers. For the output layer, we use
\mathrm{Hardtanh}
as the activation function, defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Hardtanh}(u_{i},u_{i,\text{min}},u_{i,\text{max}})\hskip-1.9919pt\vcentcolon=\hskip-1.9919pt\left\{\begin{aligned} &u_{i,\text{max}},\hskip 21.33955ptu_{i,\text{max}}\leqslant u_{i},\\
&u_{i},\hskip 14.51074ptu_{i,\text{min}}\leqslant u_{i}\leqslant u_{i,\text{max}},\\
&u_{i,\text{min}},\hskip 36.9318ptu_{i}\leqslant u_{i,\text{min}},\end{aligned}\right.

to ensure that the control policy
\pi
is admissible on the sampled states with respect to
\mathbb{U}
in (
8
).
Regarding
\gamma\in\mathcal{K}^{\leqslant\text{id}}_{\infty}
, we parameterize
\gamma
as a linear function for simplicity, i.e.,

\displaystyle\gamma(r)\vcentcolon=\gamma_{0}r,\quad r\in\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0},

(22)

where
\gamma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is an unknown constant that satisfies
\gamma_{0,\text{min}}\leqslant\gamma_{0}\leqslant\gamma_{0,\text{max}}\leqslant 1
, with
\gamma_{0,\text{min}},\gamma_{0,\text{max}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
being user-specified constants.

IV-A
2
Sampling and Designing the Loss Function

We require a finite set of sampled states
\mathcal{X}_{s}\subset\mathcal{S}
for the
conditions
of Lemma
1
and also a finite set of sampled states
\mathcal{X}_{u}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{S}
for
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
. These sampled states are
initially
selected randomly within their respective domains, after which counterexamples are iteratively added. In the
following
, we introduce loss terms that, when minimized to global optimality, promote satisfaction of the conditions in Lemma
1
and
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
on the sampled states. Ideally, the global minimum of each loss term should be zero to prevent trade-offs between their satisfaction. Therefore, we use the
\mathrm{ReLU}
function in each loss term.

Firstly, to promote the Lipschitz continuity of the trained function
h
on the set
\mathcal{C}
, we introduce the loss term

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{1}(\mathcal{X}_{s};\vartheta)\vcentcolon=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{s}}\mathrm{ReLU}\left(\mathrm{ReLU}\bigl(h(x;\vartheta)+\eta\bigr)\bigl(\lVert\nabla_{x}h(x;\vartheta)\rVert-\widetilde{L}_{h}\bigr)\right)

to enforce
\lVert\nabla_{x}h(x;\vartheta)\rVert\leqslant\widetilde{L}_{h}
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}_{s}
satisfying
h(x;\vartheta)>-\eta
, where
\eta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is sufficiently small. Here,
\widetilde{L}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
is a predefined value that serves as a Lipschitz constant for the resulting
h
on its zero-superlevel
set
\mathcal{C}
and is estimated based on the basis functions of
h
. Regarding Lemma
1
, since the R-DTCBF
constraint (
4
)
only needs to hold within
\mathcal{C}
, which is unknown, we design the loss term

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{X}_{s};\vartheta,\gamma_{0},\kappa)\vcentcolon=

\displaystyle\hskip-2.84544pt\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{s}}\hskip-2.84544pt\mathrm{ReLU}\left(\Gamma\bigl(h(x;\vartheta)\bigr)\left(\mathcal{H}\bigl(x,\pi(x;\kappa);\vartheta,\gamma_{0}\bigr)-\widetilde{L}_{h}w_{\text{max}}\right)\right),

where

\displaystyle\Gamma(z)\vcentcolon=\frac{-1}{1+e^{-c_{1}z}}+c_{2}.

Here,
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
are predetermined constants such that
h(x;\vartheta)>-\zeta
implies
\Gamma(h(x;\vartheta))<0
, and
h(x;\vartheta)\leqslant-\zeta
implies
\Gamma(h(x;\vartheta))\geqslant 0
, where
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is adjusted by the values of
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
and chosen to be sufficiently small. Therefore, if a sampled state is within the set
\mathcal{C}_{\zeta}\vcentcolon=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid h(x;\vartheta)>-\zeta\}
, then the R-DTCBF
constraint (
4
)
must be satisfied to prevent an increase in the loss term. Conversely, if a sampled state lies outside
\mathcal{C}_{\zeta}
, (
4
) must not be satisfied. In this case, the state does not enter the set
\mathcal{C}
at the next time instant. This steers the training of a candidate R-DTCBF to a
larger set
\mathcal{C}
. A larger
\mathcal{C}
reduces the conservatism of the safe controller synthesis (i.e., optimization problem (
7
)) and improves overall performance, for instance reducing tracking errors. Moreover, to ensure that
\mathcal{C}
is sufficiently large, we incorporate an additional loss term. If computing the area
\phi(\vartheta)
of the zero-superlevel set of the parameterized
h
is straightforward, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{3}(\vartheta)\vcentcolon=\mathrm{ReLU}\bigl(-\phi(\vartheta)+\phi_{0}\bigr),

to enforce
\phi(\vartheta)\geqslant\phi_{0}
, where
\phi_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is a predefined value. If computing
\phi(\vartheta)
is challenging, an alternative approach is to consider a finite set
\mathcal{X}_{I}
of initial states such that the resulting R-DTCBF
h
remains non-negative for all states in this set. In this case, we consider the loss term

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{3}(\mathcal{X}_{I};\vartheta)\vcentcolon=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{I}}\mathrm{ReLU}\bigl(-h(x;\vartheta)\bigr).

Moreover, to ensure that
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
holds on the sampled states, we consider the loss term

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{4}(\mathcal{X}_{u};\vartheta)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{u}}\mathrm{ReLU}\bigl(h(x;\vartheta)+\delta\bigr),

where
\delta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is chosen sufficiently small.
Lastly, regarding
\gamma
parameterized as in (
22
), we need to enforce that the unknown coefficient
\gamma_{0}
satisfies
\gamma_{0,\text{min}}\leqslant\gamma_{0}\leqslant\gamma_{0,\text{max}}\leqslant 1
. Thus, we consider the loss term

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{5}(\gamma_{0})\vcentcolon=\mathrm{ReLU}\bigl(-\gamma_{0}+\gamma_{0,\text{min}}\bigr)+\mathrm{ReLU}\bigl(\gamma_{0}-\gamma_{0,\text{max}}\bigr).

The loss function is then a weighted sum of the above-mentioned loss terms,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X}_{s},\mathcal{X}_{u};\vartheta,\gamma_{0},\kappa)\vcentcolon=\alpha_{1}\mathcal{L}_{1}(\mathcal{X}_{s};\vartheta)+\alpha_{2}\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{X}_{s};\vartheta,\gamma_{0},\kappa)+

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\alpha_{3}\mathcal{L}_{3}(\vartheta)+\alpha_{4}\mathcal{L}_{4}(\mathcal{X}_{u};\vartheta)+\alpha_{5}\mathcal{L}_{5}(\gamma_{0}),

where
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{5}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
are predefined.

Theorem 1

If
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X}_{s},\mathcal{X}_{u};\vartheta,\gamma_{0},\kappa)=0
, the trained pair
(h(x;\vartheta),\gamma)
satisfies the conditions of Lemma
1
and
\lVert\nabla_{x}h(x;\vartheta)\rVert\leqslant\widetilde{L}_{h}
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}_{s}
satisfying
h(x;\vartheta)\geqslant 0
, and
h(x;\vartheta)<0
holds for all
x\in\mathcal{X}_{u}
.

Proof:

It follows directly from the construction of
\mathcal{L}
.

IV-B

Counterexample-Guided Approach

The counterexample-guided approach, as detailed in Algorithm
4
, employs the proposed verification algorithm iteratively. If a counterexample is generated that violates safety (
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
) or violates the conditions of Lemma
1
(within a predefined tolerance), it is added to
\mathcal{X}_{u}
and
\mathcal{X}_{s}
, respectively, for retraining. This process repeats until an R-DTCBF pair
(h,\gamma)
is obtained that satisfies Lemma
1
and
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
for the system (
1
) with
\mathbb{U}
and
\mathbb{W}
, or until a predefined maximum time is reached, indicating that the approach is not successful.

Remark 3

Note that a trained control
policy
\pi
is not used to verify
(h,\gamma)
because training a control policy that is valid for all states would require a significantly larger number of sampled states, which we aim to avoid. Moreover, using
\pi
for verification would be slower due to its potential complexity.

Remark 4

When retraining
h
,
\gamma
, and
\pi
with Algorithm
3
, the parameters
\vartheta
,
\gamma_{0}
, and
\kappa
are initialized with the values obtained from the previous counterexample-guided iteration.

VI
Conclusions and Future Work

In this paper, we have introduced a class of robust discrete-time Control Barrier Functions (R-DTCBFs) that can be used to synthesize safe controllers. To construct such an R-DTCBF, we have proposed a counterexample-guided approach. As part of our future work, we aim to improve the method to accelerate the synthesis of R-DTCBFs for higher-dimensional systems, since the computational time of the proposed verification algorithm increases exponentially with the number of states.
