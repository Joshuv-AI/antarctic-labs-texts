Constrained β-Hairpins Targeting the EphA4 Ligand Binding Domain
Authors: Anne Marie Prentiss, Carlo Baggio, James Pagett, Anna O. Kulinich, Iryna M. Ethell, Kendall Muzzarelli, Zahra Assar, Maurizio Pellecchia
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The activity of the receptor tyrosine kinase EphA4 has been implicated in several pathologies including oncology (gastric and pancreatic cancers) and neurodegenerative diseases (amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and Alzheimer’s disease). However, advances in validating EphA4 as a possible drug target have been limited by the lack of suitable pharmacological inhibitors. Recently, we reported on the design of potent EphA4 agonistic agents targeting its ligand binding domain (LBD). Based on previous studies with a phage display cyclic peptide inhibitor, we designed a β-hairpin mimetic with high affinity for EphA4-LBD. These agents hold great promise for further validation and development of EphA4-based therapeutics. Moreover, our studies introduce a possible strategy for the design of constrained β-hairpin peptides.

Introduction

EphA4 is a receptor tyrosine kinase that is involved in bidirectional signaling via its ephrin ligands.1 The interactions between the ligand binding domain (LBD) of EphA4 and its ephrin ligands activate cytosolic domains that comprise a PDZ binding motif, a sterile alpha motif (SAM) domain, and a kinase domain that propagate various signaling events. Dysregulation of the ephrins-EphA4 axis has been implicated in several human malignancies, including cancer and neurodegeneration. Hence, ligands targeting EphA4-LBD could be used to further validate this target in several conditions including ALS,2 Alzheimer's disease (AD),3 spinal cord and brain injury,4−6 and oncology.7−10

Hence, the discovery of pharmacologically active agents targeting EphA4 is a significant, yet very challenging task. Recent efforts in this field include our own studies with small molecules derived by combinatorial library screening and structure-based lead optimizations11−14 and phage display-derived agents such as the cyclic peptide APY-d3.15 In this regard, APY-d3 was shown to adopt a β-hairpin conformation while binding to EphA4-LBD and to act in cells as an antagonist, reverting the receptor phosphorylation effects induced by the natural EphA4 ephrin ligand (ephrin-A5) in surrogate cellular assays.15 Here, in an attempt to further discover novel and more potent agents that target EphA4, we deployed an iterative structure-based design strategy that resulted in APY-d3 mimetics that are naturally constrained into a β-hairpin-like bioactive conformation.

We report on the design strategy guided by structural studies, including characterizations of binding by NMR spectroscopy measurements in solution with a 13Cε-Met-labeled sample of EphA4-LBD and [13C,1H] correlation spectra, isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) measurements, and X-ray crystallography studies. The most potent agents were also preliminarily assessed in cellular assays using primary cortical neurons. Our studies resulted in potent agonistic EphA4 peptide mimetics. Moreover, the design of the constrained peptide is of general applicability for the design of β-hairpin-like structures and hence can find other applications in drug design or in chemical and structural biology.

Results

Structure-Based Design of Linear β-Hairpin Peptide Mimetics Targeting EphA4-LBD

Our recent efforts in deriving novel EphA4 targeting agents focused on de novo design of agents targeting its ligand binding domain (LBD) using a strategy that involved NMR-based screening followed by structure-based optimizations.12,16 As an alternative, phage display was also deployed and led to the selection of the potent EphA4-LBD binder APY-d3 (Figure 1).15 The interactions between APY-d3 and EphA4-LBD were characterized via X-ray crystallography (PDB ID 5JR2) (Figure 1). The binding pose of APY-d3 is characterized by a β-hairpin-like conformation, bent around a critical β-ala residue at position 8, and constrained by an intramolecular disulfide bond between two Cys residues of the peptide. Under our experimental conditions, APY-d3 binds potently to EphA4-LBD with a Kd value as determined via isothermal titration calorimetry of 138 ± 8 nM (Figure 1C). Removal of the disulfide bond in APY-d3 resulted in compound 1 (Table 1), which presented a markedly reduced affinity for EphA4-LBD in the same experimental conditions (Kd = 436 ± 27 nM; Figure 1D). The loss of binding was likely due to an increased flexibility in the compound lacking the disulfide bond. This could be appreciated in solution by 1D 1H NMR spectroscopy measurements, observing the amide/aromatic region of the spectra of these peptides. The 1D 1H NMR spectrum of compound 1 displayed limited chemical shift dispersion, typical of a flexible and unstructured peptide (Figure 1B). On the contrary, the APY-d3 1D 1H NMR spectrum displayed a significantly increased dispersion, indicative of a more rigid conformation that likely resembles more closely the bound β-hairpin conformation, given the head-to-tail positioning of the disulfide bond.

Hence, we aimed to derive distinct molecules that could mimic the bent conformation of APY-d3 by replacing the flexible β-Ala residue in position 8 with moieties that could be predicted to constrain the peptide in a β-hairpin-like conformation (Table 1) also in the absence of the disulfide bond. Therefore, to assess if such agents could retain binding to EphA4-LBD, we tested each agent using the same isothermal titration calorimetry experiment as that reported for APY-d3 and compound 1. Most such agents (Table 1) were either inactive or poorly active, likely because the constraints positioned the two arms of the peptide unfavorably to bind EphA4-LBD. This could also be due to the tight space around the β-Ala atom in the APY-d3/EphA4-LBD complex.

“Constrained” β-Ala residues could be obtained by the introduction of a 2-aminocyclopentane carboxylic acid (compound 10) or a 2-aminocyclohexane carboxylic acid (compound 9), which have been reported to be useful in introducing conformationally constrained β-amino acids into folded peptides,17 and accordingly, both compounds showed some affinity for EphA4-LBD (Table 1). However, similar replacements in position 8 with nonbeta amino acids or with smaller cyclic aliphatic amino acids such as 2- or 3-azetidines resulted in inactivity in binding to EphA4-LBD (compounds 2–8, Table 1). However, we found that the introduction of 4-amino-5-methylthiophene-3-carboxylic acid (Table 1) resulted in compound 11 that was intriguingly more potent in this SAR study. Hence, to further investigate the property of this amino acid in the context of binding to EphA4-LBD, we conducted another round of SAR studies fixing the 4-amino-5-methylthiophene-3-carboxylic acid (Thio) in position 8 and varying amino acids at positions that have been previously found to be sensitive to binding to EphA4-LBD based on the structure and SAR studies with APY-d3.15,18 The dissociation constant for each of the resulting agents is reported in Table 2, as obtained via isothermal titration calorimetry measurements. These SAR studies also included modifications of the Ala residues that were included in lieu of the disulfide Cys residues. These included replacement with longer aliphatic residues including norleucine, leucine, or proline in the attempt to better mimic the interactions that in APY-d3 are elicited by the disulfide bond with a hydrophobic pocket of EphA4-LBD. Other substitutions explored possible replacements of the Arg in position 7, the Ser in position 9, and the Phe in position 6, in part based on previous SAR reported for APY-d315,18 and our own studies with agonistic peptides.13 These studies identified compound 22 as an agent that possessed a binding affinity for EphA4-LBD similar to that of APY-d3 but lacking the disulfide bond.

Characterization of Thiophene-Based EphA4-LBD Targeting Agents

To further characterize the binding properties of compound 22 (151G10) and related compounds in this series, we conducted additional biophysical studies. First, we obtained reference “nonconstrained” compounds by reintroducing the β-Ala in position 8 of selected optimized agents 22 and 23, leading to compounds 24 and 25, respectively, and measured the thermodynamics of binding of the agents for EphA4-LBD side by side (Table 3). As expected, the “nonconstrained” agents were less potent in binding EphA4-LBD compared to their corresponding agents containing the 4-amino-5-methylthiophene-3-carboxylic acid in position 8. Interestingly, replacing the constrained amino acid in position 8 resulted in a loss in entropy that was on balance greater than the observed increased enthalpy of binding (Table 3). A similar observation was made comparing APY-d3 with its nonconstrained version lacking the intramolecular disulfide bridge (Table 3, comparing APY-d3 with compound 1). This piece of data suggested that, like APY-d3, our selected agents, compounds 22 (151G10) and 23 (151H3), may be more structured in solution. This was confirmed by 1D 1H NMR measurements in solution side by side with their β-Ala nonconstrained respective agents (Figure 2).

Indeed, similar to what we observed with APY-d3 compared to compound 1 (its nonconstrained equivalent), the 1D 1H NMR spectra of agents 22 and 23 appear more dispersed compared to their nonconstrained corresponding agents 24 and 25, respectively (Figure 2), suggesting that, like APY-d3, the agents are folded in solution, which would justify the reduced losses in entropy upon binding compared to the nonconstrained compounds (Table 3). Simultaneously, a loss in binding entropy is observed in the constrained agents compared to their nonconstrained counterparts, which likely can be attributable to a greater conformational freedom of the nonconstrained peptides to engage more optimally with the target. On balance, those entropy/enthalpy losses in binding favor the constrained peptides in the cases of APY-d3 versus compound 1, and compound 22 versus compound 24, but almost similar dissociation constants were observed for 23 and 25 (Table 3). As discussed below, it is likely that changes in confirmation upon binding of compounds 23 and 25 may account for this effect.

Additional 2D [1H,1H] NMR studies in solution were also performed with compound 12 and its nonconstrained equivalent, clearly indicating an increased structure in the constrained peptide (Figure S2). To further detail the structural features of our constrained peptides, we solved the X-ray structure of compound 22 in complex with EphA4-LBD at a 2.5 Å resolution (Figure 3A). The geometry of binding of 22 to EphA4-LBD is structurally close to the bound conformation of APY-d3, with clear electron density positioning the 4-amino-5-methylthiophene-3-carboxylic acid within the binding site in a location that is similar to what is occupied by the β-Ala in APY-d3 (Figure 3A,B). The bound conformation of compound 22 follows a very similar intramolecular hydrogen bonding as APY-d3 typical of a β-hairpin (Figure 3C,D). However, the C-terminal amide protons in compound 22 further stabilize the conformation with additional intramolecular hydrogen bonding that could not be formed in APY-d3 (Figure 3C,D). Given the general similar backbone conformation of the two peptides, their side chains also assume very similar positions, with the norleucine of compound 22 occupying the space occupied with the disulfide bridge in APY-d3, and likewise, the other side chains also assumed very similar orientations (Figure 3E,F). Hence, not surprisingly, the interactions between compound 22 and EphA4-LBD are very similar to those observed in the complex between EphA4-LBD and APY-d3 (Figure 4A). However, we want to emphasize that while the conformation of EphA4-LBD in complex with compound 22 is overall very similar to what is observed in the EphA4-LBD bound to APY-d3, the JK loop is slightly displaced in the presence of compound 22 compared to that of EphA4-LBD bound to APY-d3 (Figure 4B).

Intriguingly, and perhaps more importantly, the structure of the complex with 22 would predict that an even more significant conformational rearrangement should take place to accommodate the biphenyl in position 6 of compound 23 (Table 3), as it would collide with residues of the JK loop and Met 164 in particular (Figure 4). To verify this hypothesis that larger structural changes in the loop regions take place in the presence of compounds 22 and 23, compared to APY-d3, we measured 2D [13C,1H] correlation spectra using a sample pf EphA4-LBD that was labeled with 13Cε-Met as we described previously,13,14 given that several Met residues are located at critical loop regions of EphA4-LBD and thus provide information on conformational changes in these regions upon binding (Figure 4).13,14 Interestingly, both APY-d3 and compound 22 caused large chemical shift perturbations for Met 115, located in the GH loop, and notably, compound 22 caused also larger perturbations for Met164, situated in the JK loop (Figure 4D). Intriguingly, compound 23 caused even larger and distinct chemical shift perturbations of Met 164 (Figure 4E), in agreement with the anticipated further opening of the JK loop that is predicted to be necessary to accommodate the biphenyl ring (Figure 4A–C). This observation could have important repercussions on the possible behavior of the agents as we previously identified that such an opening of the JK loop is associated with agonism of binding agents.13,14

To determine the ability of most potent compound agents to selectively bound to EphA4 versus other Eph receptors, we tested the final agents against EphA3 (sequence identity with EphA4-LBD ∼73%) and EphA2 (sequence identity with EphA4-LBD ∼55%). Using the same experimental conditions, we found that compounds 22 and 23 were not active in binding EphA2-LBD, while they only bound very weakly to EphA3-LBD (Figure S1). This piece of data identified compounds 22 and 23 as new lead agents targeting EphA4. The increased structural content of the constrained peptides is evident not only from binding studies and the bound conformation of compound 22 to EphA4-LBD but also corroborated by NMR studies in solution, including not only increased spectral dispersion in 1D 1H NMR compared to nonconstrained peptides but also by 2D NOESY measurements (Figure S2). Hence, our studies identified a possible strategy for the design of constrained peptides that can assume a β-hairpin-like structure.

EphA4 Phosphorylation in Primary Cortical Neurons

To assess the activity of selected agents on EphA4, we monitored the ability of the agents to cause the phosphorylation of the intracellular kinase domain in primary neurons, as we reported previously.13,14 Primary cortical neurons were treated with either 2.5 μg/mL of preclustered Fc (negative control), 2.5 μg/mL preclustered ephrinA1-Fc (positive control), or the peptide APY-d3 (as negative control) and test compounds 22 and 23 each at concentrations 1 or 10 μM, for 30 min at 37 °C under a 5% CO2/10% O2 atmosphere and then processed for Western blotting, as we described recently.13,14 This piece of data suggests that, unlike APY-d3, both compounds 22 and 23 induced receptor phosphorylation under these experimental conditions (Figure 5), with compound 23 resulting in being significantly more active than compound 22.

Discussion

While several studies point at EphA4 as a possible target for therapeutic interventions, the validation of this drug target for novel therapies has been hampered by the lack of suitable pharmacological agents that potently and selectively tackle this target.19 In addition, agents targeting the ligand binding domain may act as agonists or antagonists depending on their mode of binding and induced conformational changes upon complex formation.13,14 Among the most potent agents targeting EphA4-LBD are our recently reported agonistic agent 150D413,14 and a phage display-derived antagonistic peptide APY-d3.18 APY-d3 is constrained into a bioactive β-hairpin conformation via a head-to-tail disulfide bond, which makes the molecule perhaps less suitable for in vivo use in the current form, requiring additional modifications.15,20 To derive agents with constrained β-hairpin structures targeting EphA4-LBD but lacking the disulfide bridge, we replaced the central β-Ala amino acid in position 8 with various potential amino acids that could constrain the peptides into a β-hairpin-like conformation, and these studies resulted in the identification of compound 22 that presented a dissociation constant for binding to EphA4-LBD that is similar to that of APY-d3 using isothermal titration calorimetry measurements. Interestingly, compound 23, a derivative in which the amino acid in position 6 is replaced with a bulkier biphenyl side chain, induces larger conformational changes in EphA4-LBD as inferred by structural studies and NMR spectroscopy measurements with a 13Cε-Met-labeled sample of EphA4-LBD, as we reported recently.13,14 Indeed, recent studies13,14,21 using X-ray crystallography with the EphA4-LBD in complex with ephrin ligands or 150D4 suggested that a large conformational rearrangement of the EphA4-LBD JK loop in particular is observed after the binding of an agonist, presumably favoring the activation of the receptor. Our current studies with compound 22 in complex with Eph4-LBD reveal possibly some conformational changes of the JK loop in particular with compound 23. Accordingly, in our preliminary cellular studies with primary cortical neurons, both compound 22 and, more prominently, compound 23 activate EphA4 phosphorylation.

In conclusion, these agents constitute interesting ligands in an arsenal of chemical tools to further validate EphA4 as a possible target for pharmacological intervention. With the resurgence of therapeutic peptides,22,23 the proposed strategy could possibly be of general applicability and be used to generate additional stabilized β-hairpin-like structures targeting other receptors.

Experimental Procedures

Chemistry

General

All reagents and solvents were obtained from commercial sources, including the majority of Fmoc-protected amino acids and resins for solid-phase synthesis, as we reported recently.13,14,24−26 NMR spectra were used to evaluate the concentration of stock solutions and were recorded on a Bruker Avance III 700 MHz equipped with a TCI cryoprobe. High-resolution mass spectral data were acquired on an Agilent 6545 Q-TOF LC/MS instrument. RP-HPLC purifications were performed on a JASCO preparative system equipped with a PDA detector and a fraction collector controlled by a ChromNAV system (Jasco) on an XTerra C18 10 μm 10 mm × 250 mm (Waters). The purity of tested compounds was assessed by HPLC using an Atlantis T3 3 μm 4.6 × 150 mm2 column (H2O/acetonitrile gradient from 5 to 100% in 33 min). All compounds have a purity >95% (Figure S2). APY-d3 was initially synthesized by InnoPep (San Diego), while all other agents were synthesized in-house by standard solid-phase Fmoc peptide synthesis protocols, as we reported previously. Briefly, for each coupling reaction, 3 equiv of Fmoc-AA, 3 equiv of HATU, 3 equiv of Oxyma Pure, and 5 equiv of DIPEA in 1 mL of DMF were used. The coupling reaction was allowed to proceed for 1 h. Fmoc deprotection was performed by treating the resin-bound peptide with 20% piperidine in DMF twice. Peptides were cleaved from the Rink amide resin with a cleavage cocktail containing TFA/TIS/water/phenol (94:2:2:2) for 5 h. The cleavage solution was filtered from the resin and evaporated under reduced pressure, and the peptides were precipitated in Et2O, centrifuged, and dried in high vacuum.

For the synthesis of Fmoc-amino acids that were not commercially available, 1 equiv of the unprotected amino acid and 3.75 equiv of Na2CO3 were dissolved in tetrahydrofuran (THF)/H2O (1:1) and cooled to 0 °C. Fmoc chloride (1.1 equiv) was dissolved in THF and added dropwise to the mixture. The reaction was stirred for 2 h at 0 °C. The organic solvent was evaporated under reduced pressure, and the pH was lowered to 0 using concentrated HCl. The aqueous phase was extracted three times with AcOEt, and the collected organic phase was dried with Na2SO4, filtered, and evaporated. The resulting crude was purified using a CombiFlash Rf (Teledyne ISCO) using cyclohexane/ethyl acetate (10–100%). The purity of all tested agents was >95%.

Protein Expression and Purification

Expression and purification of EphA4-LBD were accomplished as described recently (Baggio ref.). Likewise, expression and purification of EphA4-LBD 13Cε/1Hε-Met-labeled were expressed as we previously described, adding a suspension of 100 mg of 13Cε/1Hε-methionine in 1 mL of DMSO per liter of an LB medium 10 min before induction. Similarly, constructs for selectivity measurements including the EphA3-LBD chimera (a construct of EphA3 that is identical to EphA4-LBD but containing the mutations Arg37Lys, Gly52His, Ile59Gly, Met60Val, Glu77Asp, Val157Met, Ile159Leu, Met164Leu, and Cys204Ala, to reflect the EphA3-LBD binding site) and EphA2-LBD were obtained as we described recently.13,14

Isothermal Titration Calorimetry Measurements

Isothermal titration calorimetry measurements were performed as we reported recently13 using the Affinity ITC autosampler from TA Instruments (New Castle, DE). Reverse titrations were performed at 25 °C, and the agents were dissolved in a buffer (25 mM Tris at pH 7.5 and 150 mM NaCl) containing a final DMSO concentration of 1%. The syringe was filled with a 200 μM solution of EphA4-LBD, EphA3-LBD chimera, or EphA2-LBD, and 20 injections of 2.5 μL each were performed into the cell containing a 40 μM solution of the compounds. The injections were made at 200 s intervals with a stirring speed of 75 rpm. All the solutions were kept in the autosampler at 4 °C. The analysis of the thermodynamics signatures and dissociation constant determination were performed by NanoAnalyze software (TA Instruments, New Castle, DE) and subsequently exported into Microsoft Excel.

Nuclear Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopy

1D 1H NMR spectra and 2D [13C,1H] correlation NMR spectra were acquired on a Bruker Avance III 700 MHz spectrometer equipped with a TCI cryoprobe and processed using TOPSPIN 3.6.1 (Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA) as we reported recently.13,14,24−26 For the 13Cε-Met-labeled samples, 2D [13C,1H]-HSQC experiments were acquired with 20 μM protein using 32 scans with 2048 and 128 complex data points in the 1H and 15N dimensions, respectively, at 298 K.

Molecular Modeling

Molecular models were analyzed using PyMol version 2.4.0 (Schrödinger, LLC), MOE 2019.0101 (Chemical Computing Group), or Chimera (http://www.cgl.ucsf.edu/chimera). Structural comparisons were carried out between the X-ray structures of compounds 22 and 23 in complex with EphA4-LBD and the structures of the complex between EphA4-LBD and APY-d3 (PDB ID 5JR2) or apo EphA4-LBD (PDB ID 2WO1).

X-ray Structure Determination

Crystallization was conducted using sitting drop vapor diffusion at 20 °C, with diffraction quality crystals grown in 0.1 M Bis-tris pH 6.7, 0.2 M MgCl2, 20% PEG 3,350, and 4% 1,3-butanediol. The 17.8 mg/mL EphA4/compound 22 complex was plated at a 0.5:0.5 μL ratio (protein:mother liquor). Crystals were frozen in a 20% ethylene glycol cryoprotectant, and data collection was conducted at the Diamond Light Source (I03) beamline. A data set was collected on a crystal that diffracted to 2.5 Å, and the diffraction data were processed in the C222 space group using XIA2 Dials. Molecular replacement for the data set was performed using a search model based on PDB ID 4W50 in Phaser, Phenix. Several rounds of refinement and model building were performed in the absence of peptides using COOT and CCP4. After the placement of the solvent molecules, the chemical model of the 5-Me-Thio and noncanonical amino acids were fitted into the remaining density and refined by Phenix. The remaining parts of the peptide were built manually using COOT and refined in the structure by Phenix. The top solution was refined using REFMAC, CCP4, and Phenix. The final crystal data statistics are listed in Table S1. The coordinates for the complex between compound 22 and EphA4 have been deposited in the PDB and will be released upon publication (PDB ID 9CY8).

Primary Cortical Neuron Cultures

Primary cortical neurons were isolated from cortices of C57BL/6 (JAX: 000664) mice at postnatal day (P) 0–1. Tissues were dissected, cut into 1–2 mm pieces, and treated with a papain/DNase I (0.1 M PBS/0.1% BSA/25 mM glucose/5% papain/1× DNase I [Sigma, no. D5025-15K]) solution for 20 min at 37C. Cells were mechanically dissociated and filtered using a 100 μm cell strainer. Neurons were plated on poly-d-lysine (0.5 mg/mL) and laminin (5 μg/mL) 6-well plates (350,000 per well of a 6-well plate) in Neurobasal media with 25 mM glutamine, 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and B27 supplement (Invitrogen, no. 17504-044). Cells were maintained under a 5% CO2/10% O2 atmosphere at 37 °C.

EphA4 Receptor Drug Treatment

EphA4 receptor drug treatment was performed as previously described13 with minor modifications. Briefly, ephrin A1-Fc (R&D Systems, no. 602-A1) and human Fc (R&D Systems, no. 110-HG) were preclustered by the incubation with goat antihuman IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch, no. 109-005-003) for 1 h at 4 °C. At 3 days in vitro (DIV), primary cortical neurons were treated with preclustered Fc (2.5 μg/mL), ephrin A1-Fc (2.5 μg/mL), 1 and 10 μM 151G10, 1 and 10 μM 151H3, and 1 and 10 μM APY-d3 for 30 min at 37 °C under a 5% CO2/10% O2 atmosphere and then processed for Western blotting analysis. DMSO (0.1%) was used as a negative control.

Immunoprecipitation and Western Blot Analysis

Immunoprecipitation and Western blot analysis were performed as previously described.13 Specifically, cells were collected and lysed in the lysis buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 1 mM sodium pervanadate, and protease inhibitor cocktail [1:100, Sigma, no. P8340]) at 4 °C for 30 min. Cell lysates were cleared by centrifugation at 13,500 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C and then incubated with protein-A agarose beads (Sigma, no. P1406) and an anti-EphA4 antibody (Invitrogen, no. 371600), for 2 h at 4 °C. Beads and cell lysates were boiled in a reducing sample buffer (Laemmli 2× concentrate, Sigma, no. S3401). Samples were briefly spun down, and the supernatant was run on an 8–16% Tris-glycine SDS-PAGE precast gel (Invitrogen, no. XP08160BOX). Proteins were transferred onto a Protran BA 85 nitrocellulose membrane (GE Healthcare) and blocked for 1 h at room temperature in 5% BSA. The blots were incubated with an antiphosphotyrosine antibody (BD Transduction, no. 610000) in Tris-buffered saline (TBS)/0.1% Tween 20/1% BSA at 4 °C overnight. Membranes then were washed 3 × 10 min with TBS/0.1% Tween 20/1% BSA and incubated with HRP-conjugated antimouse secondary antibodies at 1:5000 (Jackson ImmunoResearch, no. 715-035-150) for 2 h at room temperature in a TBS/0.1% Tween 20/1% BSA solution. Blots were further incubated with an ECL detection reagent (Thermo Scientific, no. 32106) and imaged using a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad). For reprobing, membrane blots were washed in a stripping buffer (2% SDS, 100 mM β-mercaptoethanol, and 50 mM Tris-HCl [pH 6.8]) for 30 min at 55 °C and then washed 5 × 5 min with TBST, blocked with 5% skim milk, and reprobed for EphA4 (Invitrogen, no. 371600). Blots were washed 3 × 10 min with TBS/0.1% Tween 20 and then incubated with antimouse HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies in TBS/0.1% Tween 20/1% BSA (Jackson ImmunoResearch, no. 715-035-150) for 2 h at room temperature. After the incubation, blots were washed 3 × 10 min with TBS/0.1% Tween 20 and developed as described earlier. The band density was analyzed by measuring the band and background intensity using Adobe Photoshop CS5.1 software.
