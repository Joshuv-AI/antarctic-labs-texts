Single-Cell RNA-Seq Reveals a Glycolysis-Biased EMT State in Keloid Keratinocytes That Amplifies the Pro-Inflammatory and Fibrotic Microenvironment
Authors: Zhaohe Li, Jia Chen, Jingxia Zhao, Xiaohan Yu, Haoyue Zhu, Huike Ma, Tingting Di, Weiwen Chen, Yan Wang, Ping Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKeloids are pathological fibroproliferative skin disorders resulting from abnormal wound healing, characterized by excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition and chronic local inflammation. While dermal fibroblasts are the primary effector cells in this process, the role of keloid keratinocytes (KKs) and their metabolic alterations in sustaining the pro-inflammatory and fibrotic microenvironment remains poorly understood.MethodsWe performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on 46,690 cells from keloid and normal skin tissues. Cellular metabolic activities were assessed using scMetabolism and scFEA, and transcriptional regulatory networks were analyzed via pySCENIC. In vitro experiments utilizing glycolysis inhibitors (2-DG and Fasentin) were conducted to validate the relationship between metabolic reprogramming and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT).ResultsscRNA-seq analysis identified a distinct subpopulation of basal keratinocytes (Basal1/2) exhibiting an arrested differentiation trajectory and elevated glycolytic activity. This metabolic shift is functionally coupled with an EMT-prone state and is regulated by transcription factors including REL and ESRRA. Pseudotime analysis revealed that these KKs transition into a terminal state characterized by high expression of the chemokine CCL2 and the stress-response gene ATF3. Cell-cell communication analysis indicated that these glycolytically active KKs maintain intensive crosstalk with a specific pro-inflammatory fibroblast subset (Fibroblasts3) via the ligand-receptor pair SEMA3C-EPHA2 and IGFBP3-ADGRG1 signaling pathways. In vitro experiments confirmed that pharmacological inhibition of glycolysis with 2-DG or Fasentin significantly attenuated the EMT-related features in KKs.ConclusionCollectively, glycolysis-driven EMT in KKs actively contributes to the pro-inflammatory and fibrotic signaling in keloids, suggesting that targeting this metabolic reprogramming could be a potential strategy for therapeutic intervention.

Introduction

Keloids are human-specific fibroproliferative skin disorders characterized by exuberant extracellular matrix (ECM) accumulation, persistent growth beyond the original wound margin, and frequent recurrence after standard therapies.1–3 Importantly, they are increasingly recognized not merely as localized fibrotic lesions, but as chronic inflammatory diseases, where persistent localized inflammation acts as a fundamental driver for continuous fibroblast activation and structural remodeling.4,5 They impose a substantial functional and psychosocial burden and display marked interindividual and anatomical variability, with higher prevalence in certain ancestries and an association with atopic comorbidities. Although intralesional corticosteroids, chemotherapeutic agents, and surgery with adjuvant therapies are widely used, their efficacy is limited by relapse and adverse effects, underscoring the need for mechanism-based strategies.6,7

In keloids, myofibroblasts are the principal collagen-producing effector cells. They may arise from multiple sources, including resident fibroblasts, perivascular cells, bone marrow–derived fibrocytes, and, of particular interest, epithelial and endothelial cells via EMT and endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT).8,9 Keratinocytes, which dominate the epidermis, can undergo EMT and actively contribute to the pro-inflammatory and fibrotic microenvironment and invasive growth in fibrotic skin through intense paracrine signaling and matrix remodeling.10–12 However, most single-cell studies in keloids have emphasized fibroblast heterogeneity and ECM remodeling, leaving the role of keratinocytes-especially their metabolic states and crosstalk with stromal cells-relatively underexplored.

Metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of proliferative and fibrotic pathologies.13 Keloid fibroblasts (KFs) exhibit a shift toward glycolysis under normoxia (Warburg effect), with elevated glucose uptake and lactate production. Inhibition of glycolysis with 2-deoxy-glucose (2-DG) has been shown to suppress the proliferation of KFs, and targeting the glycolytic enzyme phosphoglycerate kinase 1 (PGK1) could effectively inhibit KFs glycolysis, thereby reducing KFs proliferation, migration, invasion, and expression of type I collagen.14 Whether keloid keratinocytes (KKs) similarly engage a Warburg-like program that promotes EMT and disease aggressiveness remains a critical knowledge gap. Furthermore, aerobic glycolysis is not merely a supportive mechanism for cell proliferation and EMT; the substantial accumulation of glycolytic byproducts, such as lactate, and the resulting acidification of the microenvironment can function as potent inflammatory modulators.15,16 Consequently, further in-depth investigation of the metabolic reprogramming characteristics of KKs and their relationship to the maintenance of the chronic inflammatory and fibrotic niche is of significant importance for the development of new treatment strategies.

Single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) now enables precise cell-state mapping and intercellular communication inference.17 Prior keloid scRNA-seq studies emphasized fibroblasts and ECM biology. Here, we focused on keratinocytes to (i) define EMT-prone subpopulations, (ii) dissect keratinocyte-fibroblast crosstalk, (iii) resolve transcriptional regulons, and (iv) quantify metabolic reprogramming and its coupling to EMT. We integrated UCell and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) for pathway activity, CellPhoneDB for ligand-receptor networks, pySCENIC for regulons, and scMetabolism (VISION) and scFEA for pathway activity and flux inference, respectively. We provide evidence that the Warburg effect is a dominant feature of Basal keratinocytes in keloids, positively associated with EMT programs and linked to candidate signaling and transcriptional regulators. These findings nominate metabolic and signaling nodes as potential intervention points to break the chronic inflammatory cycle and mitigate EMT-like plasticity and pathological remodeling in keloids.

Material and Methods

Human Samples

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Beijing Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine, Capital Medical University (Approval No. 2022BL02-005-02). Written informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study for the publication of their clinical details. All samples were obtained from Beijing Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine, Capital Medical University, Beijing, China. The cohort consisted of three keloid tissues and two normal skin tissues. Inclusion criteria for keloid patients were: (1) clinical and histopathological diagnosis of active keloids; (2) no local treatments (intralesional corticosteroids, laser therapy, or radiation) within the 6 months prior to surgery. Normal skin tissues were obtained from healthy donors undergoing surgical excision for unrelated benign conditions (redundant prepuce or normal skin margins from cosmetic procedures). Exclusion criteria for all participants included severe systemic diseases, autoimmune disorders, or active systemic infections. Detailed demographic information, including age, sex, and anatomical site for all donors, is provided in Supplementary Table S1. The sample size (n=5) was determined based on the exploratory nature of the single-cell transcriptomic profiling and the strict viability requirements (>85%) for fresh tissue processing. To minimize pre-analytical bias, all samples were procured using a standardized surgical protocol and immediately processed for single-cell suspension within 1 hour of excision. This study was reported in accordance with the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data (RECORD) statement, an extension of the STROBE guidelines.

Cell Culture

Cell culture Human Keloid Keratinocytes (HKKs) and Normal Human Epidermal Keratinocytes (NHEKs) were commercially purchased from SUNNCELL Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Cells were cultured in Keratinocyte-SFM supplemented with 100 IU/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin in incubator (Thermo 321, USA) with a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37°C.

scRNA-Seq Sample Processing and Library Preparation

Fresh skin specimens were immediately washed with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove blood and debris. Full-thickness skin tissues, including both epidermis and dermis, were minced into small fragments (approximately 1–2 mm3) on ice. The tissue fragments were enzymatically dissociated into single-cell suspensions using the GEXSCOPE Tissue Dissociation Kit (Singleron Biotechnologies) in a specialized instrument at 37°C with continuous agitation, strictly following the manufacturer’s instructions. It is important to note that this specific dissociation protocol utilizes an enzyme cocktail optimized to maintain high viability of epithelial cells. We deliberately utilized this method to ensure comprehensive profiling of the keratinocyte population and the epidermal-dermal interface, although we acknowledge that this strategy yields a higher proportion of keratinocytes compared to protocols tailored for fibroblast enrichment. Following digestion, the reaction was terminated by adding excess PBS. The resulting suspension was filtered through a 40µm sterile cell strainer to remove undigested tissue fragments. Red blood cells were removed using a Red Blood Cell Lysis Buffer (Solarbio). The cell suspension was then centrifuged, and the pellet was resuspended in PBS. Cell viability and concentration were evaluated using trypan blue staining under a microscope; only samples with viability >85% were processed for downstream applications. Single-cell suspensions were adjusted to a concentration of approximately 1×105 cells/mL and loaded onto GEXSCOPE microfluidic chips (Singleron Biotechnologies). Single-cell RNA-seq libraries were constructed using the GEXSCOPE Single-Cell RNA Library Kit following the manufacturer’s standard pipeline, which includes cell barcoding, lysis, reverse transcription, cDNA amplification, and library construction. The quality of the final libraries was assessed using a Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. The libraries were pooled and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads.

Data Preprocessing and Quality Control

Raw reads were processed using CeleScope v1.15.0. Cell barcodes and unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) were extracted from R1, while adapters and poly-A tails were trimmed from R2. Cleaned reads were aligned to the human GRCh38 reference genome using STAR v2.6.1a. Gene-level quantification was performed with FeatureCounts v2.0.1 to generate a cell-by-gene UMI matrix. Downstream quality control and filtering were performed in Scanpy v1.8.1 (Python 3.7): cells with <200 genes, top 2% UMIs, or >50% mitochondrial reads were excluded; genes detected in fewer than 5 cells were removed. Normalization was performed by library-size adjustment and log-transformation. Highly variable genes (n=2000) were selected using the “seurat” method.

Dimensionality Reduction, Clustering and Cell Annotation

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on normalized data, and the first 20 PCs were used for clustering. Batch effects across donors were corrected with Harmony v1.0. Neighborhood graph construction, Louvain clustering (resolution 1.2), and visualization by Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) were conducted in Scanpy. Major cell lineages were annotated by canonical markers cross-referenced with SynEcoSysTM single-cell marker database. For detailed resolution, keratinocytes and fibroblasts were further reclustered (resolution 0.5) to identify subclusters. Doublets co-expressing multiple lineage markers were removed.

Downstream Analyses (Differential Expression, Trajectories, Metabolism, Regulons, and Cell-Cell Communication)

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using Scanpy’s rank_genes_groups function with Wilcoxon rank-sum test and Benjamini-Hochberg correction (adjusted p<0.05). Functional enrichment was assessed with clusterProfiler v3.16.1 (GO/KEGG) and GSEA. EMT and fibrogenic gene sets were quantified with UCell v1.1.0. The complete lists of genes utilized to define the EMT and fibrogenic signatures are provided in Supplementary Table S2. Developmental trajectories were reconstructed using Monocle2 v2.10.0 (DDRTree algorithm). To orthogonally infer the directionality and lineage hierarchy of cell state transitions, RNA velocity analysis was performed using the scVelo package, and the dynamic transitions among subclusters were further mapped using Partition-based graph abstraction (PAGA). Cell-cell interactions were inferred with CellPhoneDB v2.0. Metabolic pathway activity was scored using scMetabolism v0.2.1, while metabolic flux and metabolite abundances were inferred with scFEA v1.1.2. Transcription factor regulons were identified using pySCENIC v0.11.0(GRNBoost2→cisTarget→AUCell) with transcription factors (TFs) curated from AnimalTFDB.

Histology Assays

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) skin specimens were sectioned at 5 μm, deparaffinized in xylene, and rehydrated through graded ethanol to water. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed according to standard protocols. Masson’s trichrome staining was used to visualize collagen and ECM architecture (collagen, blue; cytoplasm/muscle, red; nuclei, black) following a Weigert’s iron hematoxylin-Biebrich scarlet/acid fuchsin-phosphomolybdic/phosphotungstic acid-aniline blue sequence. Picrosirius Red staining (Sirius Red in saturated picric acid) was applied to assess collagen content and fiber organization; slides were examined under brightfield and polarized light to evaluate birefringence and infer collagen fiber distribution. After staining, sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with resin. Images were acquired under identical exposure/magnification settings; scale bars are indicated in figure legends.

Western Blot

Skin tissues (total full-thickness skin) were homogenized in RIPA buffer with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (50 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, 0.2 mM PMSF, 0.1 mM NaF and 1.0 mM dithiothreitol, pH 7.4). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (Thermo Fisher). Equal amounts of protein (20 μg per lane) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA) at 120V for 60 min at 4°C. Membranes were blocked with 1% BSA and incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: E-cadherin (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat#14472, 1:1000), Vimentin (Boster, Cat#BM01353, 1:1000), Cytokeratin 19 (Boster, Cat#M02101-7, 1:1000), GLUT1 (Proteintech, Cat#21829-1-AP, 1:1000), and GAPDH (Proteintech, Cat#60004-1-lg, 1:5000). After washing, membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Bands were visualized using the chemiluminescent substrate. Target protein band intensities were normalized to the corresponding GAPDH levels to ensure equal loading across samples.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus (Takara). 1 μg of total RNA was reverse transcribed using a cDNA synthesis kit (Takara) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Real-time PCR was performed using the FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master and LightCycler96 System (Roche, Germany). Primers were listed as follows: GAGCAGGATGTTGATATGGTGTTTG and TCTCAATGCCTAGATCACCACGA for human PKM2, GACTTCCGCACAGAATTTGATG and GAATGTTACGGACAATCTCACCC for human HK2, AAGAGGCAGAGACTTGCGAAAC and TGGAGTCACACTGGCAAACCTT for human N-cadherin, GAGAACGCATTGCCACATACAC and GAGCACCTTCCATGACAGACCC for human E-cadherin; CACCCAGCACAATGAAGATCAAGAT and CCAGTTTTTAAATCCTGAGTCAAGC′ for GAPDH.

Immunofluorescence Confocal Microscopy

For tissue specimens, formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections underwent dewaxing and rehydration steps involving xylene and graded ethanol solutions. To expose antigenic sites, heat-induced antigen retrieval was performed using sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 15 min. Following incubation for 20min in 3% hydrogen peroxide to block endogenous peroxidase activity, the samples were rinsed thoroughly with distilled water for 5 min. Sections were then blocked with PBS containing 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against E-cadherin (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat#14472, 1:100), N-cadherin (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat#13116, 1:100), Vimentin (Boster, Cat#BM01353, 1:200), Cytokeratin 19 (Boster, Cat#M02101-7, 1:100), and GLUT1 (Proteintech, Cat#21829-1-AP, 1:100). For cell-based immunofluorescence, HKKs and NHEKs were seeded onto glass coverslips at a density of 1×105 cells/well. The coverslips were washed twice with cold PBS for 5 min each, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min. After blocking with PBS containing 1% BSA for 1 h, cells were incubated with primary antibody against ZO-1 (Proteintech, Cat#21773-1-AP, 1:100) overnight at 4°C. After washing three times with PBS, all tissue sections and cell coverslips were stained with Multi-rAb Plus 594-Goat Anti-Rabbit recombinant secondary antibody (Proteintech, Cat#RGAM004, 1:200) for 1 h at room temperature. The samples were then stained with diamidinophenylindole (DAPI) included in the Mounting Medium (Abcam, Cat#ab104139) for 10 min. Immunofluorescence visualization and quantification was conducted with ZEN Software (ZEISS, Germany).

Pyruvic Acid Detection

Intracellular pyruvic acid levels were quantified using a commercial pyruvate assay kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China, Cat#BC2205) following the manufacturer’s instructions. NHEKs and HKKs were lysed in ice‑cold buffer, and supernatants were collected after centrifugation (1000 g, 5 min). Equal amounts of protein were normalized across samples, and pyruvate content was measured by enzymatic conversion to a chromophore, with absorbance recorded at 505 nm using a microplate reader (SpectraMax, Molecular Devices). Pyruvate concentrations were calculated from a standard curve and expressed as nmol per mg protein.

Lactate Assay

Cellular lactate production was determined using a lactate assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, Cat#S0208S) according to the supplier’s protocol. NHEKs and HKKs were harvested and deproteinized. Lactate levels were quantified via an enzymatic reaction yielding a colorimetric product detected at 570 nm using a microplate reader. Standard curves generated with known lactate concentrations were used to calculate absolute values, which were normalized to cell number or total protein content to compare glycolytic activity between groups.

Cell Viability Assay

To assess the potential cytotoxicity of metabolic inhibitors, cell viability was determined using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8). HKKs were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with 2-Deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG, Proteintech, Cat#CM00910, 10 mM) and Fasentin (Proteintech, Cat#CM04264, 50 µM) for 24 hours. CCK-8 solution was added, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Viability was calculated as a percentage relative to the vehicle control.

Statistical Analysis

The displayed data is indicated by mean ± STD. Statistical analysis was performed by Student t test and one-way ANOVA, followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests when more than two treatments were compared. P ˂0.05 was considered statistically significant. To evaluate the statistical differences in the relative proportions of cell subclusters between normal skin and keloid tissues, group-wise Ro/e (ratio of observed to expected) analysis was conducted using the Chi-square test.

Results

A Single-Cell Atlas of Keloids Reveals Major Lineages and Keratinocyte Heterogeneity

To explore the cellular heterogeneity in keloids, we utilized scRNA-seq technology to compare keloid tissues from three patients with normal skin tissues from two healthy individuals. We obtained a total of 46,690 high-quality cells (keloid: 23,860; control: 16,495). After Harmony batch correction, Louvain clustering, and UMAP dimensionality reduction, cells were grouped into 24 clusters belonging to 6 major lineages: keratinocytes, sweat gland cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, mural cells, and immune cells (Figure 1A–C). The capture of the immune cell lineage (marked by the canonical genes TPSB2 and HLA-DRA) across all samples reflects both physiological resident immunity and pathological infiltrates. Notably, the variable immune proportions among keloid replicates capture the clinical heterogeneity of keloids, where inflammatory intensity naturally fluctuates with anatomical site and lesion developmental stage. Concurrently, keratinocytes consistently emerged as the most abundantly represented cell population across all samples (Figure 1D). Given this predominant abundance, we subsequently focused our in-depth transcriptomic analysis on the keratinocyte compartment to explore their specific pathogenic contributions. Keratinocytes were further subdivided into 11 clusters (Basal1, Basal2, Granular, Spinous1-7, Proliferating) (Figure 1E–H). Statistical comparison of the subset distributions revealed significant compositional shifts. Group-wise analysis using the Chi-square test demonstrated that the keloid group was significantly enriched in the Basal1, Granular, Spinous2, Spinous4, Spinous5, Spinous6, and Proliferating clusters, while the remaining subclusters were predominantly enriched in the normal skin group (Supplementary Figure S1). To further elucidate the functional consequences of these specific compositional shifts within the suprabasal layers, we performed cluster-specific GO enrichment analysis. In keloids, the depleted Spinous1 cluster exhibited a notable downregulation in the unfolded protein response (UPR). This compromised UPR capacity suggests an impaired ability to maintain endoplasmic reticulum homeostasis, which may contribute to the depletion of this specific spinous state. Concurrently, the depleted Spinous3 cluster showed downregulation in the regulation of peptidase activity and intrinsic apoptotic signaling pathways. Conversely, the expanded Spinous5 cluster in keloids was significantly enriched for epidermis development, keratinocyte differentiation, and epidermal cell differentiation, closely aligning with the epidermal hyperplasia commonly observed in keloid pathology. Furthermore, the expanded Spinous6 cluster exhibited a striking upregulation in metabolic terms, including oxidative phosphorylation and ATP metabolic process (Supplementary Figure S2). Fibroblast Subtypes Exhibit Distinct Functional Signatures Relevant to Fibrosis

We performed unsupervised clustering analysis on all fibroblasts which were subdivided into 7 clusters (Fibroblasts1-7) (Figure 2A–D). Among these clusters, Fibroblasts1 represented the major homeostatic population responsible for baseline dermal maintenance. While Fibroblasts2 and Fibroblasts3 exhibited substantial enrichment in extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling signatures alongside adipose-derived and inflammatory/pro-angiogenic traits, Fibroblasts5 and Fibroblasts6 were specifically characterized by pronounced myofibroblast features, and Fibroblasts4 and 7 displayed proliferative characteristics (Figure 2E and Supplementary Figure S3). Notably, these fibroblast clusters exhibited profound inter-sample heterogeneity. As shown in Figure 2D, the ECM-remodeling Fibroblasts5 was abundant in Keloid2, comparable between Normal2 and Keloid1, yet absent in Keloid3. Similarly, Fibroblasts7 was present in Normal1 but not Normal2. This variability accurately reflects the clinically heterogeneous and dynamic nature of individual cutaneous microenvironments. Group-wise analysis using the Chi-square test revealed that these myofibroblast-like subsets (Fibroblasts5 and 6), alongside Schwann cells, were significantly enriched in keloid tissues compared to normal skin (Supplementary Figure S4). This specific expansion of myofibroblast-like subsets strongly corroborates the enhanced fibrotic and ECM-depositing microenvironment characteristic of keloids. GO enrichment of differentially expressed genes in keloid fibroblasts highlighted terms predominantly associated with matrix remodeling, such as extracellular matrix organization, collagen metabolic process, and cell-substrate adhesion (Figure 2F). Basal Keratinocytes in Keloids Exhibit Pronounced EMT and Matrix Remodeling Signatures

To evaluate the transcriptomic propensity for fibrosis, UCell gene set scoring was performed across all keratinocyte subclusters. The heatmap of average expression revealed that the Basal1 and Basal2 clusters possessed the highest intrinsic baseline enrichment for both EMT and fibrotic signatures among all keratinocyte populations (Figure 3A). While the basal fibrogenic scores remained consistently high across conditions (Figure 3B), their EMT scores were significantly elevated in keloid tissues compared to normal skin (Figure 3C). These transcriptomic shifts were visually corroborated by single-cell UMAP feature plots, which demonstrated that cells exhibiting the highest fibrogenic and EMT characteristics were predominantly localized within the basal compartment, with the EMT signals being visibly intensified and expanded in the keloid group (Figure 3D and E). GSEA further confirmed the robust activation of EMT programs specifically within the Basal1 and Basal2 populations (NES=2.105, P<0.001; NES=2.596, P<0.001) (Figure 3F and G). Interestingly, the Granular cluster exhibited significant disease-specific upregulation in both EMT and fibrogenic signatures (Figure 3A and B). Rather than contradicting a basal-driven pathological model, this phenomenon strongly aligns with a kinetic consumption trajectory. The intense intrinsic coupling of EMT and matrix remodeling programs in basal cells likely drives their rapid loss of basal adhesion and accelerates their pathological differentiation. Consequently, although the Basal2 cluster is proportionally reduced in keloids, this numerical reduction reflects its rapid depletion to continuously fuel the massive downstream expansion of hyperactive suprabasal populations, such as the Granular cluster. Notably, subpopulation analysis revealed that these basal clusters robustly expressed basement membrane-associated collagens typically secreted by basal keratinocytes, such as COL7A1 and COL18A1 (Figure 3H and I), underscoring their highly active matrix-synthesizing state. Pseudotime Analysis Reveals an Aberrant Differentiation Trajectory in Keloid Keratinocytes

To reconstruct epidermal state transitions, we performed pseudotime trajectory analysis (Monocle 2) on the entire keratinocyte population (Supplementary Figure S7). The inferred trajectory captured a canonical differentiation path originating from the highly replicative Proliferating/Basal states. However, compared to normal skin, KKs exhibited a profound developmental deviation. Instead of distributing evenly along the trajectory, keloid-derived keratinocytes-particularly EMT-prone Basal and expanded Spinous subsets-aberrantly accumulated at the terminal branches (Figure 5A). Clustering dynamically expressed genes along the pseudotime revealed the molecular basis of this altered trajectory (Figure 5B). As cells progressed, early proliferative markers (eg, CDK1, CCNB1, ANLN) were downregulated. Yet, rather than acquiring a typical terminal differentiation signature, cells at the late pseudotime branches sustained the upregulation of a pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic gene module (Cluster 4). This terminal state is prominently characterized by genes associated with mechanical stress, matrix remodeling, and localized inflammation, including CYR61, CAV1, ATF3, and chemokine CCL2. Specifically, transcriptomic analysis demonstrated that these terminal keratinocyte subclusters exhibit elevated expression of the CCL2 transcript. To orthogonally validate these directional dynamics, we applied RNA velocity and PAGA analyses. Velocity streamlines confirmed a continuous differentiation flow from Basal/Proliferating states toward suprabasal Spinous layers (Figure 5C). Furthermore, the PAGA graph delineated the lineage hierarchy (eg, Basal1→Basal2, Spinous1→Spinous3) and definitively identified the keloid-enriched Spinous5 and Spinous6 clusters as aberrant terminal states (Figure 5D). Inferred Keratinocyte-Stromal Crosstalk Is Enhanced in Keloids

CellPhoneDB inference indicated that predicted interactions between keratinocytes (Basal1/2) and fibroblasts were more numerous and intense in keloids compared to normal skin (Figure 6A and B). Crucially, the analysis revealed highly specific, targeted interactions between discrete subclusters. In normal skin, the ALDH1A1-RA interaction was exclusively enriched between the Basal1 and Fibroblasts6 clusters, alongside broader baseline signaling axes such as COL3A1-ADGRG1 (Figure 6C and D). Conversely, in keloid Basal1/2 cells, specific ligands such as SEMA3C and IGFBP3, alongside receptors including EPHA2, SORT1, and ADGRG1, were significantly upregulated. Notably, this included highly restricted pathological crosstalk, such as the CXCL14-CXCR4 interaction directed primarily from the Basal2 cluster to the Fibroblasts4 cluster, as well as broader signaling via the IGFBP3-TREM2 axis (Figure 6E, F and Supplementary Figure S8). These targeted computational predictions suggest that basal keratinocytes do not act in isolation. Importantly, rather than being strictly characterized by a single phenotype, these specific basal subclusters concurrently exhibit enhanced glycolytic activity and profound EMT/fibrogenic signatures, representing a coupled glycolysis-biased EMT state. A Warburg-Like Metabolic Profile in Keratinocytes Links to EMT and Pathological State Transitions

Metabolic reprogramming is a recognized hallmark of fibroproliferative diseases. To investigate this in the epidermal compartment of keloids, scMetabolism analysis was performed, revealing a significant enrichment of glycolysis and gluconeogenesis, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) in both keloid-derived Basal1 and Basal2 subclusters (P < 0.0001) (Figure 7A and Supplementary Figure S9). Despite shared transcriptomic upregulations, scFEA indicated distinct flux dynamics. Specifically, Basal1 showed enhanced mid-glycolysis [glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate (G3P) to 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PG)] and fatty acid-to-TCA cycle flux, driving the intracellular accumulation of pyruvate, citrate, and succinate, alongside a synchronized depletion of G6P, G3P, and fatty acids. Conversely, Basal2 primarily exhibited OXPHOS-related flux combined with partial glycolysis (Figure 7B–D and Supplementary Figure S10). Overall, keloid basal keratinocytes displayed a Warburg-like metabolic state complemented by OXPHOS. Furthermore, we examined the glycolysis index in normal human epidermal keratinocytes (NHEKs) and human keloid keratinocytes (HKKs). Compared to NHEKs, the kinase expression and products of glycolysis were significantly increased in HKKs (Figure 8F–H). To explore if glycolysis supports the fibrotic nature of HKKs, we introduced 2-DG (10 mM) and fasentin (50 µM) to inhibit the process of glycolysis. Importantly, cell viability assays (CCK-8) confirmed that these concentrations did not induce significant cytotoxicity (Supplementary Figure S12). Both 2-DG and fasentin abolished EMT induction in HKKs (Figure 8I–L). These findings demonstrate that glycolytic activity is required to sustain the EMT phenotype in HKKs, suggesting that metabolic reprogramming is a crucial maintenance factor for their mesenchymal-like state.

Discussion

Keloids represent a complex pathological condition increasingly recognized as a chronic inflammatory skin disease rather than a localized fibroproliferative disorder.18,19 The persistence of a localized pro-inflammatory microenvironment is essential for driving continuous fibroblast activation and ECM deposition.20,21 While previous single-cell studies have predominantly focused on fibroblast heterogeneity and matrix remodeling,22,23 our study provides a comprehensive map of keratinocyte states, revealing how their metabolic reprogramming orchestrates the inflammatory and fibrotic niche in keloids. Specifically, our analysis delineates distinct functional roles for keratinocyte subpopulations that the Basal1 and Basal2 clusters act as the primary metabolic and EMT-driven engines at the dermo-epidermal junction, while the terminally differentiated Spinous and Granular clusters serve as the downstream pro-inflammatory signaling hubs.

A central finding of our study is the “Warburg-like” metabolic switch in keloid basal keratinocytes. We observed increased glycolytic flux and elevated levels of lactate and pyruvic acid in HKKs. Importantly, our findings of enhanced glycolysis in basal keratinocytes strongly align with recent evidence demonstrating that specific keloid basal keratinocytes uniquely reside in a hypoxic microenvironment, characterized by the robust expression of hypoxia markers such as HIF-1 and carbonic anhydrase 9.24 This localized hypoxia likely acts as a primary trigger for the anaerobic-like glycolytic shift (Warburg effect) observed in our study. From the perspective of immunometabolism, this metabolic reprogramming is not merely a passive byproduct of cellular activation.25 Extracellular lactate can act as a potent inflammatory mediator; its accumulation and the resulting acidification of the dermo-epidermal junction are known to suppress local immune surveillance and polarize local macrophages toward pro-fibrotic phenotypes, thereby sustaining chronic inflammation.15,26–28 Our results suggest that this glycolytic shift is functionally coupled with the acquisition of EMT,29,30 as pharmacological inhibition of glycolysis with 2-DG or Fasentin significantly attenuated the EMT phenotype.

At the transcriptional level, we identified REL as a master regulon in the Basal2 cluster. As a canonical subunit of the NF-κB signaling complex, REL is a key orchestrator of inflammatory transcriptional networks.31–33 The enrichment of the REL regulon provides a direct molecular link between the metabolic-driven EMT and classical inflammatory pathways in keloids.34–36 This suggests that the EMT-prone state is not a simple structural transition but a comprehensive phenotypic shift toward a pro-inflammatory cell identity.

Our findings further highlight that KKs serve as active signaling hubs in the inflammatory microenvironment. Pseudotime analysis identified a terminal stage characterized by the high expression of CCL2 (MCP-1) and ATF3.37,38 While our study demonstrates the robust transcriptomic expression of CCL2 rather than its direct protein secretion, this upregulation carries significant spatial and immunological implications. Recent studies have revealed that untreated human keloids harbor a significantly higher density of M1 and M2 macrophages in the superficial keloid dermis compared to deeper layers.39,40 The elevated CCL2 expression by these overlying terminal keratinocytes provides a compelling mechanism for the localized recruitment of these immune cells to the superficial layer. This supports the emerging concept that the superficial dermis, driven by keratinocyte crosstalk, acts as the primary pathogenic zone driving keloid formation.41 Furthermore, cell-cell communication analysis revealed intensified crosstalk between these glycolytically active keratinocytes and the Fibroblasts3 subcluster, which uniquely exhibited inflammatory and pro-angiogenic traits. Signaling axes such as SEMA3C-EPHA2 and IGFBP3-ADGRG1 likely mediate this epidermal-dermal relay, where reprogrammed keratinocytes instruct the stroma to maintain a persistent fibrotic and inflammatory state.42–44

Therapeutically, our study nominates the “Metabolic-Inflammatory-EMT” axis as a potential intervention target. Current keloid treatments often suffer from limited efficacy, possibly because they fail to address the underlying metabolic and inflammatory drivers.45,46 We demonstrated that inhibiting glycolysis can disrupt the EMT-like plasticity in vitro. Targeting this metabolic switch may simultaneously dampen the release of inflammatory mediators (like CCL2) and acidic byproducts (lactate), potentially breaking the self-perpetuating chronic inflammatory cycle. Despite these insights, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the sample size for scRNA-seq remains relatively small and restricted to a specific demographic subset from a single clinical center, which may introduce potential selection bias and not capture the full spectrum of inter-individual variability in keloids. Importantly, our cohort includes a mix of female and male patients, and there are inherent discrepancies in biopsy anatomical sites between the healthy controls (abdomen) and keloid patients (chest, earlobe, back). This patient heterogeneity and anatomical variance may introduce confounding factors, as mechanical tension, skin thickness, and regional microenvironments differ significantly across distinct body sites. Second, while we utilized in vitro pharmacological inhibition, the lack of in vivo lineage tracing or genetic knockdown models limits our ability to definitively establish the causal role of keratinocyte glycolysis in keloid formation. Third, our analysis primarily focused on transcriptomics; future studies integrating spatial transcriptomics and proteomics would provide a more detailed understanding of the localized metabolic-inflammatory niches.

In conclusion, our single-cell atlas provides new insights into the molecular pathogenesis of keloids and highlights the critical role of keratinocyte metabolism in shaping the disease’s inflammatory and fibrotic microenvironment.

Conclusion

Our study provides a high-resolution single-cell transcriptomic landscape of keloids, highlighting the previously underappreciated role of epidermal keratinocytes in orchestrating the pro-inflammatory and fibrotic microenvironment. We demonstrate that a “Warburg-like” glycolytic shift in specific basal keratinocyte subpopulations is functionally coupled with an EMT-prone state, governed by the inflammatory transcription factor REL. This metabolic reprogramming facilitates the elevated expression of potent chemokines, such as CCL2, thereby contributing to the maintenance of the chronic localized inflammatory niche and the activation of pathogenic fibroblast subsets. These findings establish a “Metabolism-Inflammation-EMT” axis in keloid pathogenesis and suggest that targeting the keratinocyte glycolytic switch could represent a promising therapeutic strategy to disrupt the self-perpetuating inflammatory cycle and mitigate pathological skin remodeling.
