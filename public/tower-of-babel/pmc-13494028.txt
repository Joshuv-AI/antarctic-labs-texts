HMGA2 identified via m6A-senescence multi-omics in laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Meng Jin, Lingmei Qu, Zhaonan Xu, Shuang Teng, Shuo Liu, Qiuying Li, Fengshuo Zhang, Qing Hao, Peng Wang, Rui Zhao, Bingrui Yan, Yanan Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractN6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification and cellular senescence have been individually implicated in tumor biology, but the landscape of m6A-related senescence genes in laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma (LSCC) remains underexplored. In this study, we conducted an integrated multi-omics analysis combining bulk and single-cell transcriptomics, somatic mutation and copy number variation data, and proteomics to explore the clinical and molecular impacts of m6A-related senescence. We identified 93 putative m6A-related senescence genes with prognostic value, defining molecular subtypes with distinct immune features, survival outcomes, and pathway activities. Further stratification using differentially expressed genes revealed additional heterogeneity, and a derived m6A-related senescence score (m6ASenScore) showed robust associations with prognosis and mutation burden. Single-cell analysis uncovered malignant epithelial subpopulations with subtype-specific metabolic and inflammatory profiles. Among five core candidate genes, HMGA2 was found to be significantly upregulated at the protein level. Functional experiments in LSCC cell lines showed that reducing HMGA2 levels inhibited cell growth, invasion, and migration. This study provides a comprehensive characterization of m6A-related senescence signatures and highlights HMGA2 as a potential therapeutic target in LSCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55980-1.Keywords: m6A, Senescence, HMGA2, Single-cell RNA sequencing, Laryngeal squamous cell carcinomaSubject terms: Biomarkers, Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Genetics, Oncology

Introduction

Laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma (LSCC) is a major subtype of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC) that arises from the laryngeal epithelium1. Despite advancements in diagnostic techniques and treatment methods in recent years, the overall survival of patients with LSCC has not significantly improved, particularly in advanced stages, where the prognosis remains poor2.

N6-methyladenosine (m6A) is the most common and evolutionarily conserved internal RNA modification in eukaryotes, dynamically regulated by methyltransferases (writers), demethylases (erasers), and reader proteins3. The m6A modification influences multiple aspects of RNA metabolism, including RNA processing, nuclear export, translation, and decay, and plays important roles in the initiation and progression of many human cancers4. Cellular senescence is characterized by stable cell cycle arrest and extensive phenotypic remodeling, and it has context dependent roles in tumor initiation and progression5–7. Recent evidence further suggests that senescence is evolutionarily conserved among different species and plays critical roles in aging, regeneration, and disease8. In addition, correlative studies suggest that m6A-related genes are associated with cellular senescence phenotypes and may influence oxidative stress, telomere length, DNA damage, and the senescence associated secretory phenotypes9–11. However, the landscape of putative m6A-related senescence genes in LSCC remains unclear, and their prognostic significance and potential biological relevance are still poorly understood.

In this study, we used an integrated multi-omics approach to systematically investigate the landscape of putative m6A-related senescence genes in LSCC. Based on transcriptomic correlation analysis, we identified 93 prognostic putative m6A-related senescence genes and established two molecular subtypes with differential survival outcomes and immune characteristics. Copy number variation (CNV) and mutation analyses further revealed frequent genomic alterations in these genes. A robust m6A-related senescence score (m6ASenScore) was developed for prognostic stratification. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) revealed the expression patterns of these genes in malignant epithelial cells of LSCC, and identified metabolically distinct subpopulations with different senescence signatures. Through proteomic screening, we identified HMGA2 as significantly upregulated in tumor tissues and validated its tumor promoting role through functional experiments. Together, these findings provide an integrated view of the association between m6A-related signatures and senescence related features in LSCC.

Materials and methods

Data collection

Expression profiles from bulk RNA sequencing, single-nucleotide variants (SNV), and clinical data for HNSC patients were sourced from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Thresholded gene level CNV data were obtained from the UCSC Xena browser (https://xenabrowser.net/)12. A list of human senescence related genes was retrieved from the Cell Senescence Gene database (CSGene) (http://csgene.bioinfo-minzhao.org/).

The expression count matrix included 566 samples, comprising 520 primary tumors, 2 metastatic samples, and 44 normal samples. The SNV dataset contained 508 samples and did not include metastatic samples. The CNV dataset contained 522 samples and did not include metastatic samples.

For survival analysis, one case with missing survival time was removed from the clinical dataset, and the remaining cases were matched with primary tumor samples from the expression matrix using patient level TCGA identifiers, yielding 520 cases. For clinicopathological analyses, cases with missing stage, T stage, N stage, age, or gender information were further excluded, leaving 418 cases. HPV status annotation was additionally obtained from the Head and Neck Squamous Cell Carcinoma (TCGA, Nature 2015) clinical dataset available through cBioPortal (https://www.cbioportal.org/).

For multivariable Cox regression analyses incorporating HPV status, cases with complete annotation for survival, age, gender, overall stage, and HPV status were retained, yielding 276 patients. Expression data were analyzed at the sample level, whereas survival and clinicopathological analyses were performed at the patient level. Detailed sample inclusion and data availability are provided in Supplementary Data S6.

Identification of putative m6A-related senescence genes

To identify putative senescence genes associated with m6A regulation, we first curated 23 known m6A regulators, writers (WTAP, METTL3, METTL14, METTL16, RBM15, RBM15B, VIRMA, ZC3H13), erasers (ALKBH5, FTO), and readers (IGF2BP1, IGF2BP2, IGF2BP3, YTHDC1, YTHDC2, YTHDF1, YTHDF2, YTHDF3, RBMX, LRPPRC, HNRNPC, HNRNPA2B1, FMR1)13,14. Bulk RNA-seq data from TCGA-HNSC (tumor samples only) were used to assess correlations between m6A regulators and senescence-related genes. Genes with zero mean expression were removed. Genes with Pearson correlation coefficients |r| > 0.3 and p < 0.05 were retained as putative m6A-related senescence genes for downstream analyses (Supplementary Data S1). No multiple testing correction was applied at this initial screening step. These genes were defined on the basis of transcriptomic correlation rather than direct evidence of m6A modification or reader binding.

Univariate and multivariate cox regression analysis

To explore genes associated with overall survival, we conducted univariate Cox regression analysis. The curated TCGA-HNSC gene expression matrix was obtained in TPM format. No additional normalization or batch correction was applied. Normal samples were excluded, the expression values were transformed as log2(TPM + 1), and the expression profiles were matched with survival data by sample ID before analysis. Hazard ratios (HRs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p-values for each gene were computed using the coxph function in the survival R package (version 3.8-3). Genes with p-values < 0.05 were regarded as prognostically relevant and included in subsequent analyses. Forest plots were generated with the forestplot package (version 3.1.6). Multivariable Cox regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the independent prognostic value of m6ASenScore and HMGA2 after adjustment for age, gender, overall stage, and HPV status in the TCGA-HNSC subset with complete clinical annotation. For grouped survival analyses, patients were classified into high and low groups using variable specific cutoff values.

Consensus clustering analysis

The ConsensusClusterPlus R package (version 1.72.0) was utilized to conduct consensus clustering, categorizing patients into molecular subtypes using two gene sets: m6A-related senescence genes and their corresponding prognostic differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The best number of clusters (k) was identified by analyzing the cumulative distribution function (CDF) and delta area graphs. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to confirm the strength of the clustering. Additionally, K-means clustering was applied to identify malignant epithelial subpopulations based on inferred CNV patterns in single-cell data.

Differential expression analysis

Different expression data formats were used according to the analytical purpose. For differential expression analysis of m6A-related senescence genes between tumor derived and normal samples, the genes retained after univariate Cox regression were extracted from the raw count data matrix. Genes with mean expression values of zero were removed, and differential expression analysis was performed using the edgeR (version 4.4.2) after normalization and dispersion estimation. Differentially expressed genes were defined as those with |log2FC| > 1 and FDR < 0.05. For comparison between the m6A-related senescence cluster (m6ASenCluster) A and B, TPM expression data were transformed as log2(TPM + 1) and analyzed using limma (version 3.62.2) packages15,16, with DEGs defined as those with |log2FC| > 1 and FDR < 0.05. For expression comparison between CDKN2A mutant and wild type groups, TPM data were also transformed as log2(TPM + 1). Normal samples were excluded, and tumor samples were matched to the mutation matrix using patient level TCGA identifiers. Candidate gene expression levels were then compared between groups using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and genes with P < 0.05 were visualized.

Immune infiltration analysis

The GSVA R package (version 2.2.0) was used to perform single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) to assess immune cell infiltration levels across molecular subtypes. Immune cell infiltration was estimated using a curated gene set collection representing 23 immune cell types, and the complete gene sets used in this analysis are provided in Supplementary Data S7. The resulting ssGSEA scores were further normalized by min-max scaling to reflect relative infiltration levels. Pearson correlation analysis was further performed between m6ASenScore and immune cell infiltration scores, and P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini Hochberg method.

Functional enrichment analysis

The clusterProfiler R package (version 4.14.6) was used to perform Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses17–19. For both analyses, terms were regarded as significant when the adjusted p-value (q-value) was under 0.05.

Construction of the m6ASenScore

PCA was performed on the expression matrix of prognostic DEGs to construct a scoring system, termed m6ASenScore. The m6ASenScore for each patient was calculated by summing the scores of the first two principal components, PC1 and PC2, which were extracted. The optimal cutoff value for m6ASenScore was determined using the surv_cutpoint function in the survminer R package (version 0.5.0) based on maximally selected rank statistics, with overall survival time and survival status as the input variables. Patients were then classified into high and low m6ASenScore groups according to the resulting cutoff value.

Somatic mutation and analysis

Masked somatic mutation data in MAF format were obtained from the TCGA-HNSC cohort through the GDC portal. The MAF files were imported into R using the maftools package (version 2.24.0). Multiple MAF files were merged using the merge_mafs function, and mutation summaries at both the sample and gene levels were extracted. The processed MAF object was saved for downstream analyses, including visualization of frequently mutated genes using waterfall plots. No additional variant calling pipeline was performed in this study.

Survival and clinical subgroup analysis

In order to determine the prognostic relevance of the m6ASenScore in HNSC, all patients were initially split into high and low score categories using a consistent cutoff value from the overall cohort. Using this classification, Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival analysis was conducted within different clinical subgroups to assess the consistency and robustness of the m6ASenScore in various clinical contexts. The log-rank test was employed to assess statistical significance, and all analyses were executed using the survival (version 3.8-3) and survminer (version 0.5.0) R packages.

scRNA-seq sample collection and processing

Primary tumor tissues (PT), adjacent normal tissues (AT, ≥ 2 cm from tumor margin), and lymph nodes metastasis (LN) were collected from three untreated LSCC patients at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University. The institutional ethics board (YJSKY2022-047) approved all procedures, and each participant provided informed consent. After resection, tissues were enzymatically digested into single-cell suspensions and filtered to remove debris. Red blood cells were lysed, and cell viability was confirmed to exceed 85%. The 10x Genomics Chromium Single Cell 3’ platform was used to construct single-cell libraries, which were then sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000. Raw sequencing data generated by the 10x Genomics platform were processed independently for each sample using Cell Ranger (version 5.0.0) for barcode demultiplexing and alignment to the GRCh38 human reference genome. No sample multiplexing strategy was used. The resulting gene expression matrices were imported into R (version 4.3.1) for downstream analysis using Seurat (version 5.1.0).

Quality control and cell type annotation

Raw single-cell expression matrices were analyzed in R (version 4.3.1) using Seurat (version 5.1.0). Cells with fewer than 300 or more than 7500 detected genes, mitochondrial gene content greater than 25%, or hemoglobin gene expression greater than 1% were excluded20. No dedicated algorithm-based doublet detection or removal step was performed, and stringent quality control filtering was used to reduce the influence of low-quality cells and potential mixed cell profiles. The data were normalized using the Seurat NormalizeData function, followed by identification of highly variable genes with FindVariableFeatures, scaling with ScaleData, and principal component analysis with RunPCA. Data from different samples were then merged, and batch effects were corrected using Harmony based on sample identity (orig.ident)21. The first 15 Harmony dimensions were selected for downstream analyses based on the elbow plot. Clustering was performed using FindNeighbors and FindClusters on the Harmony reduction with a resolution of 0.1, and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) visualization was generated using the same 15 Harmony dimensions.

Marker genes for each cluster were identified using FindAllMarkers, and the top marker genes in each cluster were examined to support cell type annotation. Cell types were then manually annotated based on canonical markers that have been widely used in previous studies, including CD3D (T cells), MS4A1 and MZB1 (B/plasma cells), PECAM1 (endothelial cells), KRT5 (epithelial cells), COL1A1 (fibroblasts), CD68 and CD163 (myeloid cells), and RGS5 and ACTA2 (pericytes/vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs))22–26. In addition, reference-based annotation was performed using the SingleR package (version 2.4.0) with the Blueprint/ENCODE reference dataset to further evaluate the robustness of the manual annotation27–29. The annotation was further checked by visualizing the expression patterns of representative marker genes across clusters and by comparison with the reference-based annotation results. Epithelial cells were extracted for subsequent analyses. A schematic workflow of the single-cell RNA-seq analysis pipeline is provided in Supplementary Fig S4.

Inference of CNV and malignant epithelial subclustering

The inferCNV package (version 1.18.1) was used to conduct CNV analysis. Epithelial cells from AT were designated as the reference group, while epithelial cells from PT and LN samples were used as observation groups. inferCNV was run with cutoff = 0.1, denoise = TRUE, HMM = TRUE, and min_cells_per_gene = 10, with chrY and chrM excluded from the analysis. The inferred CNV expression matrix was then subjected to K-means clustering with k = 5. No fixed absolute CNV score threshold was used. Instead, malignant epithelial cells were defined as tumor derived epithelial cells assigned to clusters showing broad CNV deviations relative to the AT epithelial reference cells, whereas reference AT epithelial cells were considered non-malignant. These malignant epithelial cells were subsequently subjected to downstream analysis using Seurat (version 5.1.0), including normalization, variable gene selection, data scaling, and principal component analysis. Based on the top 15 principal components (dims = 1:15), UMAP dimensionality reduction and clustering (resolution = 0.05) were performed to characterize intratumoral heterogeneity among malignant epithelial subsets.

Pseudotime analysis of malignant epithelial cells

Using Monocle2 (version 2.30.1), pseudotime analysis was performed on malignant epithelial cells to explore the relative transcriptional progression among malignant epithelial subclusters30. Expression matrices were taken from the Seurat object to build a CellDataSet, after which size factors and dispersion were estimated. Genes with mean expression ≥ 0.1 were selected for ordering, and dimensionality reduction was carried out using the DDRTree algorithm to infer trajectories. The inferred trajectories were subsequently interpreted together with metabolic and senescence-related features across malignant epithelial subclusters. Pseudotime values were assigned to each cell, and highly variable genes showing dynamic changes along pseudotime (q-value < 1e-50) were identified via the differentialGeneTest function. Expression patterns were visualized using trajectory heatmaps and representative senescence-related genes.

Metabolic pathway and functional enrichment analysis

Metabolic activity at the single-cell level was evaluated using the scMetabolism R package (version 0.2.1) with KEGG-based metabolic gene sets. The VISION method was employed without imputation to calculate pathway activity scores for each cell. For visualization across malignant epithelial subtypes, the pathway activity scores were further normalized within each pathway. Representative pathways, such as glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, and the TCA cycle, were highlighted using dot and violin plots. Differences in pathway activity scores among malignant epithelial subtypes were assessed using the Kruskal Wallis test, and the resulting P values were adjusted using the FDR method. Pairwise comparisons were further performed using the Wilcoxon rank sum test with FDR correction (Supplementary Table S3). Representative genes from the selected KEGG metabolic pathways were further visualized across malignant epithelial subtypes using a heatmap. For visualization of representative genes involved in glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, and the TCA cycle, the top 10 genes from each pathway were selected based on the variance in their mean expression across malignant epithelial subtypes.

To investigate the functional implications of cellular senescence, GSVA (version 2.2.0) was performed on log-normalized expression data. A senescence gene set composed of 23 prognostic putative m6A-related senescence genes, together with 17 selected KEGG pathways related to metabolism, immunity, and oncogenic signaling, was used to calculate enrichment scores. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted between senescence scores and pathway activity across epithelial subtypes. P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the FDR method, and correlations with FDR < 0.05 and |r| > 0.3 were considered significant. Results were displayed in a correlation heatmap.

Identification and scoring of core putative m6A-related senescence genes

To determine the core m6A-related senescence genes, four gene sets were intersected: (i) prognostic risk genes from univariate Cox analysis (uniCOX_risk), (ii) senescence genes positively correlated with m6A regulators (m6Acor_positive), (iii) upregulated DEGs with log2FC > 1 (DEGs_up), and (iv) genes upregulated in malignant epithelial cells (malignant_up). Five overlapping genes (PLK1, TGFB1, BIRC5, HMGA2, and CAV1) were identified as potential core putative m6A-related senescence genes based on transcriptomic and single-cell analyses.

Senescence was evaluated at the single-cell level using the UCell algorithm, which relies on the expression of these five genes31. Violin plots were used to visualize the distributions of scores calculated for malignant epithelial subtypes (Epi0-Epi3). Statistical comparisons between groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon test.

Proteomic data for validation

TMT-based quantitative proteomic data of paired LSCC and adjacent normal tissues were obtained from our previously published study32. In the present study, these data were used solely for validation of differentially expressed proteins identified through multi-omics analysis.

Clinical sample collection

Patients undergoing surgical resection at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University between April 2018 and January 2020 provided a total of 112 LSCC tissue samples and their corresponding adjacent non-tumor tissues (Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Data S2). Adjacent non-tumor tissues were collected intraoperatively from macroscopically normal areas without visible tumor invasion, with a minimum distance of 2 mm from the tumor margin in glottic LSCC and 5 mm in supraglottic LSCC, and were further confirmed to be free of tumor involvement by postoperative pathological examination. Preoperative radiotherapy or chemotherapy was not administered to any of the patients. All tumor specimens were confirmed by postoperative pathological diagnosis. Clinical and follow-up data, including overall survival information, were recorded. For patients with survival < 60 months, the actual survival time was used; for those surviving > 60 months, survival was censored at 60 months, as the endpoint was 5 years. Approval for this study was granted by the Institutional Review Board (YJSKY2024-386), with written informed consent obtained from every participant.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

LSCC and adjacent tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sliced into 4 μm sections. After deparaffinization, rehydration, and antigen retrieval, the sections were blocked with 5% BSA at room temperature for 30 min and incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-HMGA2 primary antibody (20795-1-AP, Proteintech, USA). The following day, after washing, sections were treated with a biotin-labeled goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody for 1 h, developed using DAB, counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, mounted, and imaged.

Cell culture, lentiviral transduction, and siRNA transfection

Human LSCC lines AMC-HN-8 (CVCL_5966; RRID: CVCL_5966) and TU686 (CVCL_4916; RRID: CVCL_4916) were acquired from iCell Bioscience Inc. (Shenzhen, China) and BeNa Culture Collection (Beijing, China), respectively. STR profiling reports provided by the suppliers for the purchased cell batches are included in the Supplementary Material 2. Both cell lines were confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination before use, and all experiments were performed using cells within the first five passages after recovery. AMC-HN-8 cells were cultured in DMEM (Meilunbio, MA0212), and TU686 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 (Meilunbio, MA0212); both media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (WILBER, FBS-SaHu-500) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Meilunbio, MA0110). Cells were kept at 37 °C under a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere and subcultured every 2 to 3 days to maintain viability.

Lentiviral vectors containing short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) aimed at HMGA2, along with a non-targeting negative control, were sourced from Shanghai GeneChem Co., Ltd. (China). Viral transduction was executed in AMC-HN-8 and TU686 cells, which were plated in 6-well culture plates, following the manufacturer’s instructions. To improve transduction efficiency, cells were infected with 8 µg/mL polybrene. After 24 h, the viral medium was replaced with fresh culture medium. Stable knockdown populations were selected using 1 µg/mL puromycin and subsequently expanded. The effectiveness of the knockdown was verified using qRT-PCR and western blot analysis. For transient knockdown experiments, AMC-HN-8 and TU686 cells were transfected with METTL3-targeting small interfering RNA (siRNA) or the corresponding negative control using a commercial transfection reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were harvested at the indicated time points after transfection, and knockdown efficiency was verified by qRT-PCR and western blot analysis. The effects of METTL3 knockdown on HMGA2 expression, as well as on senescence-related and SASP-related proteins, were further evaluated in both cell lines.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Using TRIzol reagent from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA), total RNA was extracted from LSCC cell lines according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified RNA was used to synthesize complementary DNA (cDNA) with the help of a commercial reverse transcription kit. Quantitative PCR utilized FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master (ROX) from Roche, Switzerland, with GAPDH acting as the reference gene. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate. The 2^-ΔΔCt method was used to determine relative mRNA expression levels. All experiments were independently repeated three times. Primer sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Western blot

Protein lysates from AMC-HN-8 and TU686 cells were obtained using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) with added protease inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime, ST506). Protein concentration was measured using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, P0010) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal amounts of protein (30 µg per sample), were loaded onto SDS-PAGE gels of appropriate concentration, separated by electrophoresis, and transferred to PVDF membranes selected according to the molecular weight of the target proteins. The membranes were blocked using 5% non-fat milk in TBST for an hour at room temperature, followed by an overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting HMGA2 (20795-1-AP, Proteintech, Chicago, USA) and β-actin (GB15003-100, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Membranes were treated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (GB23303, Servicebio) for 60 min at room temperature after washing. Protein bands were detected with an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (G2161, Servicebio) and imaged using a chemiluminescence detection system.

EdU cell proliferation assay

To assess cellular proliferation, AMC-HN-8 and TU686 cells were seeded into 96-well plates and incubated with 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) working solution (Elabscience, E-CKA377) at 37 °C for 2 h in 5% CO₂. Afterward, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized using 0.5% Triton X-100. Following the Click chemistry labeling of incorporated EdU, the nuclei were stained with DAPI. Fluorescent images were captured using a fluorescence microscope. The proliferation index was determined by the percentage of EdU-positive cells in five randomly chosen visual fields per well. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Transwell invasion assay

Cell invasion was measured using 24-well Transwell chambers featuring 8 μm pores, which were pre-coated with 200 µg/mL of Matrigel. After being deprived of serum for 24 h, 1 × 10⁵ cells in 200 µL of serum-free medium were placed into the upper chambers. The bottom chambers contained 600 µL of medium with 20% FBS serving as a chemoattractant. After incubating for 48 h at 37 °C, cells that did not invade were removed, and the membranes were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 1% crystal violet. Invaded cells were counted under 200× magnification from five random microscopic fields. Experiments were independently performed in triplicate.

Wound healing assay

Cells were cultured in 6-well plates until they were 90% confluent for migration evaluation. A scratch was made across the cell monolayer using a sterile 200 µL pipette tip. Detached cells were removed by PBS rinses, and serum-free medium was then applied. Images were taken at 0 and 24 h using an inverted microscope equipped for live cell imaging. Wound healing percentage was quantified using ImageJ software as follows: [(wound area at 0 h - wound area at 24 h) / wound area at 0 h] × 100%. The assay was independently repeated three times.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.0) and R software (version 4.3.1) were used for all statistical analyses. Quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The differences between the two groups were evaluated using either the Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, depending on suitability. A p-value less than 0.05 on both sides was deemed statistically significant. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times unless otherwise stated. KM survival curves were generated for both TCGA-HNSC and clinical cohorts, with patients dichotomized into high- and low-expression groups based on the median HMGA2 level. Survival differences were evaluated using the log-rank test.

Results

Genomic alterations and prognostic associations of m6A-related senescence genes in HNSC

Among the 429 putative m6A-related senescence genes evaluated, 64 were identified as risk genes (HR > 1) and 29 as protective genes (HR < 1) (Supplementary Data S3). Within the top 20 prognostic genes visualized, DKK1, PHB, AIMP2, PSMB5, and GAPDH demonstrated significant associations with reduced survival (Fig. 1A). Analysis of CNV across 92 prognostic genes revealed extensive genomic alterations. Recurrent amplifications were observed in CCND1, GAPDH, and AKT1, whereas deletions predominated in CDKN2A, PRKAA2, and TGFB1 (Fig. 1B). Somatic mutation analysis demonstrated mutations in 35.43% of samples, with CDKN2A exhibiting the highest mutation frequency (20%). Lower mutation frequencies (< 5%) were observed in EGFR, IGF1R, and KIT (Fig. 1C). Differential expression analysis between tumor derived and normal samples identified 23 significantly dysregulated putative m6A-related senescence genes (log2FC > 1, FDR < 0.05), with genes such as HMGA2, CDKN2A, and TERT upregulated in tumors, while ULK3, PPARG, and EHF were downregulated (Fig. 1D). Given the high mutation frequency of CDKN2A, we assessed its transcriptional impact. CDKN2A-mutant specimens exhibited significant upregulation of TGFB1, CAV1, and CDKN2A, concomitant with downregulation of ULK3, PRKAA2, PPARG, and BIRC5 (Fig. 1E). A prognostic interaction network was established to map regulatory relationships among differentially expressed genes (Fig. 2A).

m6A-related senescence-based molecular subtyping reveals heterogeneity in HNSC

Consensus clustering based on the expression profiles of 93 prognostic putative m6A-related senescence genes identified two molecular subtypes. The consensus matrix showed that k = 2 gave the clearest separation between the two clusters, with little overlap. The CDF and delta area plots also showed that increasing k brought only limited improvement in clustering stability. Therefore, k = 2 was chosen for the following analysis (Fig. 2B). Principal component analysis confirmed clear separation between subtypes (Fig. 2C). KM survival analysis demonstrated significantly reduced overall survival in m6ASenCluster A (log-rank p < 0.001) (Fig. 2D). Heatmap analysis showed distinct expression patterns of the 93 genes between clusters, with most genes highly expressed in m6ASenCluster A, which also correlated with adverse clinical features such as advanced stage, lymph node involvement, and higher mortality (Fig. 2E, Supplementary Fig S1A). Functional enrichment analysis based on GSVA indicated that m6ASenCluster A was significantly enriched in pathways related to cell adhesion (e.g., adherens junction, focal adhesion, tight junction), cancer progression (e.g., glioma, acute myeloid leukemia, endometrial cancer), and immune signaling (e.g., B cell receptor, chemokine, MAPK, ERBB pathways), whereas m6ASenCluster B showed enrichment in neurodegenerative, metabolic, and immune-regulatory pathways such as ALS, glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis, autophagy regulation, allograft rejection, and type I diabetes mellitus (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, immune infiltration analysis revealed cluster-specific immune landscapes, with m6ASenCluster A exhibiting higher enrichment of dendritic cells, monocytes, and helper T cells, whereas natural killer cells were more abundant in m6ASenCluster B (Fig. 3B).

Prognostic DEG-based subtyping reveals molecular heterogeneity and clinical associations in HNSC

We performed differential expression analysis and identified 343 (DEGs) (|log2FC| > 1, FDR < 0.05) (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Data S4). GO enrichment analysis revealed that these DEGs were significantly involved in biological processes such as extracellular matrix organization, immunoglobulin production, cell-substrate and cell-matrix adhesion, as well as developmental processes like gastrulation and endodermal differentiation. In terms of cellular components, they were enriched in structures such as the collagen-containing extracellular matrix, immunoglobulin complexes, and basement membranes. Molecular functions included extracellular matrix structural constituents, antigen binding, and various growth factor or receptor interactions (Fig. 3D). KEGG pathway analysis further indicated that these genes were involved in diverse cancer-related and immune-regulatory pathways, including PI3K-Akt signaling, ECM-receptor interaction, cellular senescence, JAK-STAT and IL-17 signaling, as well as pathways associated with viral infection, endocrine regulation, and inflammatory responses (Fig. 3E). Univariate Cox regression analysis of the 343 DEGs between m6ASenCluster A and B identified 208 prognostic genes, including 128 risk factors and 80 protective factors, with representative top 20 genes visualized in a forest plot (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Data S5). Based on the 208 prognostic DEGs, consensus clustering analysis identified three DEG-based subtypes. At k = 3, the consensus matrix showed clear and stable separation among the three clusters. The CDF and delta area plots indicated that higher k values led to greater cluster fragmentation with little further improvement. Therefore, k = 3 was selected for the following analyses (Fig. 4B). PCA confirmed clear separation among the three clusters, supporting the robustness of this stratification (Fig. 4C). KM survival analysis showed significant prognostic differences among the three clusters, with DEG-based cluster A associated with the poorest prognosis, DEG-based cluster B with the best, and DEG-based cluster C intermediate (p < 0.01) (Fig. 4D). A heatmap showed distinct expression patterns across the DEG-based clusters using the top 20 genes with the highest relative expression in each cluster, which were selected from the 208 prognostic DEGs, together with their associations with clinical features (Fig. 4E). Moreover, proportional analysis of clinical characteristics demonstrated distinct distributions across DEG-based clusters in terms of vital status, age, gender, stage, T stage, and N stage, further supporting the clinical relevance of these molecular subtypes (Supplementary Fig S1B).

Development and validation of m6ASenScore for immune profiling, mutational analysis, and prognostic stratification

We constructed an m6ASenScore based on PCA of the 208 prognostic DEGs, derived from the DEG-based cluster analysis (k = 3), to quantify the prognostic relevance of m6A-related senescence signatures in HNSC. This score was designed to integrate insights from both the initial m6ASenCluster (k = 2) and DEG-based cluster stratifications. Using maximally selected rank statistics, the optimal cutoff value for m6ASenScore was determined to be -5.843189, and patients were accordingly stratified into high and low m6ASenScore groups. In the TCGA-HNSC subset with complete annotation for age, gender, overall stage, and HPV status, multivariable Cox regression analysis showed that a high m6ASenScore remained independently associated with worse overall survival (HR = 2.56, 95% CI 1.36–4.84, P = 0.004), while age was also independently associated with overall survival in the same model (Supplementary Fig S2D). Figure 5A illustrates that samples from m6ASenCluster A predominantly align with DEG-based clusters A/C (high scores), while m6ASenCluster B aligns with DEG-based cluster B (low scores), reflecting a consistent prognostic pattern across the two clustering approaches. Immune profiling demonstrated positive associations between m6ASenScore and activated dendritic cells, monocytes, T follicular helper cells, and Th2 cells, contrasting with negative correlations for natural killer cells, with significance assessed using FDR adjusted P values (Fig. 5B). Somatic mutation profiling revealed a higher mutation rate in the high m6ASenScore group (96.8%, 337/348) than in the low-score group (88.7%, 134/151). TP53 had the highest frequency in the high group (79%), followed by TTN (35%), CDKN2A (23%), and FAT1 (22%) (Fig. 5C). Compared to the high group, the low-score group showed reduced mutation rates in TP53, FAT1, NOTCH1, PCLO, DNAH5, CASP8, and XIRP2, while USH2A remained unchanged. All other genes, including TTN (42%), PIK3CA (21%), NSD1 (18%), and KMT2D (17%), had increased mutation frequencies (Fig. 5D). Prognostic assessment in clinical subgroups further validated the robustness of m6ASenScore: in sex-stratified analysis, significant survival differences were observed only in males (p < 0.001) but not in females (P = 0.143) (Fig. 5E); in patients with stage III-IV disease, high-score cases showed significantly worse survival (P < 0.001), whereas stage I-II patients showed no significant difference (P = 0.072) (Fig. 5F). Similarly, both T1-2 (P = 0.013) and T3-4 (P = 0.009) subgroups exhibited inferior survival in the high-score group (Fig. 5G), and in the N1-3 subgroup, the score retained its predictive value (P < 0.001), though not in N0 patients (P = 0.289) (Fig. 5H).

Single-cell characterization and malignant epithelial identification in LSCC using scRNA-seq and inferCNV analysis

Comprehensive scRNA-seq analysis of 9 LSCC specimens including PT, AT, and LN from three patients, identified seven major cellular compartments after rigorous quality control and normalization: B/plasma cells, endothelial cells, epithelial cells, fibroblasts, myeloid cells, pericytes/VSMCs and T cells (Fig. 6A). These annotations were supported by marker genes identified in each cluster and were further checked using the expression patterns of canonical marker genes (Fig. 6B). In addition, reference-based annotation using SingleR with the Blueprint/ENCODE dataset showed broad agreement with the manual annotation at the major lineage level, particularly for the epithelial compartment (Supplementary Fig S2A-C). Based on the manual annotation, T cells and epithelial cells represented the largest populations, while fibroblasts displayed the highest transcriptional activity based on transcript count, followed by epithelial cells (Fig. 6C-D). Application of inferCNV to epithelial populations using AT epithelial cells as reference revealed pronounced CNV profiles in malignant cells from primary and metastatic sites. Unsupervised clustering using the K-means algorithm partitioned these epithelial cells into five distinct subclusters. Based on broad CNV deviations relative to the AT epithelial reference cells, four tumor associated clusters were classified as malignant (Fig. 6E-F).

Single-cell characterization of m6A-related senescence gene expression dynamics and functional states in malignant epithelial cells

Transcriptomic profiling of 23 prognostic putative m6A-related senescence genes in malignant LSCC epithelial cells revealed that 12 genes, including IL6, CCNA1, TERT, CAV1, and TGFB1, were significantly upregulated in malignant epithelial cells compared to non-malignant counterparts (Fig. 7A). UMAP based clustering identified four malignant epithelial subpopulations (Epi0-Epi3) (Fig. 7B). We profiled the activity of selected metabolic pathways with scMetabolism, revealing subtype-specific states: Epi3 showed the highest glycolytic activity, Epi1 was enriched for TCA cycle activity, and Epi0 demonstrated enhanced oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 7C). The expression patterns of the top 10 representative genes from each of glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, and the TCA cycle across malignant epithelial subtypes are shown in Supplementary Fig S2F. Monocle2 pseudotime trajectory positioned Epi0 at early differentiation stages, with Epi1 and Epi3 occupying distinct terminal branches (Fig. 7D).

Expression dynamics of the 23 prognostic putative m6A-related senescence genes along the pseudotime trajectory revealed five temporally distinct expression clusters (Fig. 7E). Cluster 1 genes (e.g., TERT, IL6) exhibited late-stage upregulation; Cluster 2 (e.g., TGFB1, CCNA1) showed mid-to-late expression peaks; Cluster 3 (e.g., PLK1, BIRC5) peaking mid-late; Cluster 4 genes (e.g., HMGA2, DKK1, TBX3) active early-mid; Cluster 5 (e.g., NCAM1) displayed high expression at both early and late pseudotime points. Spearman correlation analysis between senescence scores derived from the 23 prognostic putative m6A-related senescence genes and 17 selected KEGG hallmark pathways, with P values adjusted for multiple testing using the FDR method, revealed distinct regulatory profiles across malignant epithelial subtypes (Fig. 7F). In Epi1, senescence gene expression was positively correlated with multiple metabolic pathways, including glycolysis/gluconeogenesis (r = 0.30), oxidative phosphorylation (r = 0.35), pyruvate metabolism (r = 0.33), TCA cycle (r = 0.32), and the pentose phosphate pathway (r = 0.28), indicating a distinct metabolic remodeling pattern in this subtype. Epi2 demonstrated the strongest associations with immune-inflammatory and tumor-related signaling, with significant correlations observed for Toll-like receptor (r = 0.51), NOD-like receptor (r = 0.52), JAK-STAT (r = 0.47), MAPK (r = 0.54), p53 signaling (r = 0.53), antigen processing and presentation (r = 0.47), cell cycle (r = 0.50), apoptosis (r = 0.50), and mTOR signaling (r = 0.48), along with metabolic activity in the TCA cycle (r = 0.37). Epi3 exhibited moderate correlations across several tumor-related pathways, particularly p53, cell cycle, and MAPK signaling. In contrast, Epi0 showed limited pathway associations. Notably, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction exhibited a consistent negative correlation in Epi1, Epi2, and Epi3, being strongest in Epi1 (r = -0.25).

Functional validation of HMGA2 as a tumor-associated candidate in LSCC

We first identified five core putative m6A-related senescence candidates (PLK1, TGFB1, BIRC5, HMGA2, and CAV1) by intersecting four gene sets, and constructed a senescence score based on their expression (Fig. 7G). The violin plot revealed the highest score in the Epi1 malignant epithelial subtype (Fig. 7H). Among the five core candidates, proteomic analysis identified only HMGA2 as significantly upregulated in tumor tissues compared to adjacent tissues (P < 0.05, Fig. 7I). KM analysis of HNSC samples from the TCGA database showed that patients with elevated HMGA2 expression had significantly worse overall survival than those with lower expression levels (Fig. 8A). The median cutoff value for HMGA2 was identified as 2.349. In the TCGA-HNSC subset with complete annotation for age, gender, overall stage, and HPV status, multivariable Cox regression analysis confirmed that elevated HMGA2 expression remained independently associated with poorer overall survival (HR = 1.55, 95% CI 1.06–2.27, P = 0.025), while age also remained independently associated with overall survival in this model (Supplementary Fig S2E). Similarly, the survival analysis of 112 LSCC patients from the clinical cohort demonstrated that increased HMGA2 expression was linked to a negative prognosis (Fig. 8B). Immunohistochemical staining further demonstrated markedly higher HMGA2 expression in LSCC tissues compared with adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. 8C), which is consistent with a previous report33.

(B) Overall survival curves of LSCC patients stratified by high (red) and low (blue) HMGA2 expression (n = 112). (C) Representative immunohistochemical staining of HMGA2 in LSCC tissue and adjacent non-tumor tissue (brown spots indicate HMGA2-positive expression). (D) RT-qPCR analysis confirming efficient HMGA2 knockdown in LSCC cell lines AMC-HN-8 and TU686. (E) Western blot validation of HMGA2 protein knockdown in AMC-HN-8 and TU686 cell lines. To evaluate its functional significance, HMGA2 was knocked down using shRNA in two LSCC cell lines (AMC-HN-8 and TU686), and knockdown efficiency was confirmed at both the mRNA and protein levels by qRT-PCR and Western blot (Fig. 8D-E). Functional assays showed that HMGA2 knockdown significantly inhibited cell proliferation (EdU assay, P < 0.05), invasion (Transwell assay, P < 0.01/0.001), and migration (Wound healing assay, P < 0.01) (Fig. 8F-H). Collectively, these findings indicate that HMGA2 may contribute to the malignant phenotype of LSCC by promoting proliferation, invasion, and migration. To further investigate whether HMGA2 is associated with senescence related changes in LSCC cells, we examined the expression of senescence related proteins and SASP related proteins after HMGA2 knockdown. In both AMC-HN-8 and TU686 cells, HMGA2 knockdown was accompanied by increased expression of p16, p21, and p53, together with upregulation of IL-6 and TGFB1 (Supplementary Fig S3A).

We next performed METTL3 knockdown to assess whether HMGA2 associated changes may be linked to m6A-related regulation. qRT-PCR and western blot confirmed efficient reduction of METTL3 expression in both AMC-HN-8 and TU686 cells (Supplementary Fig S3B-C). METTL3 knockdown reduced HMGA2 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels (Supplementary Fig S3D-E). Notably, METTL3 knockdown was also accompanied by increased expression of p16, p21, and p53, as well as IL-6 and TGFB1 (Supplementary Fig S3F). Full length and unprocessed blot images are provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Discussion

Cellular senescence, a state of stable proliferative arrest, plays a complex role in cancer by contributing to both tumor suppression and tumor promotion processes through modulation of immune surveillance and tissue remodeling34,35. Recent bioinformatics analyses in HNSC have revealed the prognostic relevance of senescence-associated genes and proposed senescence-based molecular subtypes that correlate with immune infiltration and survival outcomes36. However, the expression patterns, functional states, and clinical implications of putative m6A-related senescence associated genes in LSCC remain largely unexplored. Given the limited size of publicly available LSCC bulk cohorts, we used the TCGA-HNSC cohort as a discovery framework and further evaluated the disease specific relevance of the findings in LSCC through single cell, proteomic, and functional analyses. In this study, we systematically investigated the landscape and functional consequences of m6A-related senescence genes at both bulk and single-cell levels.

We first identified 93 prognostic putative m6A-related senescence genes in the TCGA-HNSC cohort and observed frequent genomic alterations among these genes, including CNV amplifications in CCND1 and deletions in CDKN2A and PRKAA2. CDKN2A, a canonical senescence gene and cell cycle regulator34, showed the highest mutation frequency among prognostic genes and was also upregulated in tumor tissues. In CDKN2A-mutant samples, altered expression of several senescence-related genes was observed, including increased TGFB1 and CAV1 and decreased PRKAA2, PPARG, and BIRC5, suggesting a potential association between CDKN2A mutation status and the senescence gene network in the TCGA-HNSC cohort.

We defined two m6ASenClusters with distinct clinical and molecular profiles in the TCGA-HNSC cohort. m6ASenCluster A was associated with poor survival, advanced tumor stages, and enrichment of pro-oncogenic pathways such as focal adhesion, MAPK, and ERBB signaling. In contrast, m6ASenCluster B was enriched in neurodegenerative, metabolic, and immunoregulatory pathways, and also exhibited more favorable clinical outcomes in survival analyses. These findings suggest the functional diversity of senescence-related gene programs in the TCGA-HNSC cohort. Furthermore, ssGSEA-based immune infiltration profiling revealed distinct immune landscapes between the two m6ASenClusters. m6ASenCluster A showed higher enrichment of dendritic cells, monocytes, and helper T cells, whereas m6ASenCluster B was characterized by elevated levels of natural killer (NK) cells. These divergent patterns suggest that m6ASenCluster A may reflect a pro-tumor immune environment enriched for innate immune activation and inflammatory signals that, paradoxically, can promote immune suppression or tumor progression under chronic stimulation37,38. Conversely, the NK cell dominance in m6ASenCluster B may indicate a more effective anti-tumor immune surveillance status39. Together, these findings reinforce the functional heterogeneity of senescence-associated immune contexts in the HNSC discovery cohort.

Further stratification of patients in the TCGA-HNSC cohort based on 208 prognostic DEGs resulted in three DEG-based clusters with distinct clinical features and survival outcomes. DEG-based cluster A exhibited the poorest prognosis, while DEG-based cluster B showed favorable clinical associations. We then constructed the m6ASenScore based on PCA of prognostic DEGs. The m6ASenScore was strongly associated with mutation burden, immune microenvironment, and patient survival. Multivariable Cox regression analysis in the TCGA-HNSC subset with complete annotation for age, gender, overall stage, and HPV status further showed that m6ASenScore remained independently associated with overall survival, supporting its potential prognostic relevance in the TCGA-HNSC discovery cohort. TP53 is a well-established regulator of cellular senescence through its role in DNA damage response and p53-p21 signaling pathways40. The elevated TP53 mutation rate (79%) in the high m6ASenScore group may have implications for senescence regulation. On the other hand, high-score patients showed positive correlations with activated dendritic cells, monocytes, T follicular helper cells, and Th2 cells, but reduced NK cell abundance. These immune signatures indicate a potential immunosuppressive state, which may contribute to worse prognosis.

To further support the robustness of the cell type annotation, we additionally performed reference-based annotation using SingleR, which showed broad agreement with the manual annotation at the major lineage level, particularly for the epithelial compartment used in downstream analyses. Single-cell transcriptomic analysis revealed four malignant epithelial subtypes (Epi0-Epi3) in LSCC, each exhibiting distinct metabolic and transcriptional states. The identification of these four malignant epithelial subtypes (Epi0-Epi3) remains preliminary, as it was derived from a limited cohort of three LSCC patients, and requires validation in larger LSCC cohorts. To further characterize the biological heterogeneity of malignant epithelial subtypes, we next examined selected metabolism-related pathways. Epi3 showed high glycolytic activity, Epi1 was enriched for TCA cycle activity, and Epi0 exhibited elevated oxidative phosphorylation. Notably, Epi1 also exhibited the highest senescence score. Although these metabolic features should not be interpreted as canonical hallmarks of senescence, previous studies have shown that cellular senescence can be accompanied by mitochondrial and metabolic alterations5,41,42. Therefore, the coexistence of elevated senescence-associated scoring and enriched TCA cycle activity in Epi1 may reflect a senescence-associated metabolic remodeling pattern.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis placed Epi0 at the early stage of differentiation, consistent with its oxidative metabolic bias and limited signaling activity. In contrast, Epi1 and Epi3 occupied terminal branches, aligning with their enhanced metabolic and senescence-related profiles. Epi2, located at a terminal pseudotime branch, showed the strongest positive correlations between putative m6A-related senescence genes and hallmark tumor-related pathways including MAPK, p53, JAK-STAT, apoptosis, and cell cycle, suggesting that Epi2 may represent an immune-inflammatory subtype potentially contributing to aggressive tumor phenotypes. Pseudotime analysis of 23 m6A-related senescence genes revealed five temporally distinct expression patterns, indicating coordinated transcriptional regulation along epithelial differentiation trajectories.

Finally, we identified five core m6A-related senescence genes. Among them, HMGA2 showed significantly elevated protein abundance in tumor tissues based on proteomic analysis. By contrast, the other four core candidate genes did not show significant proteomic upregulation in the proteomic cohort analyzed in this study. This may reflect the common discordance between transcript and protein abundance, as well as inter sample heterogeneity and the limited sample size of the proteomic cohort, which may have reduced the power to detect protein level differences. Therefore, the absence of significant proteomic elevation does not necessarily exclude their potential biological relevance, but indicates that HMGA2 showed the most consistent cross platform support in the current study. Consistently, analysis of the TCGA-HNSC cohort together with our clinical LSCC cohort of 112 patients showed that high HMGA2 expression was associated with significantly worse overall survival. Moreover, multivariable Cox regression analysis in the TCGA-HNSC subset with complete annotation for age, gender, overall stage, and HPV status showed that elevated HMGA2 expression remained independently associated with poorer overall survival, further supporting its prognostic relevance within the TCGA-HNSC discovery cohort. Immunohistochemistry further confirmed that HMGA2 was upregulated in LSCC tissues compared with adjacent tissues. Subsequent functional experiments supported a tumor promoting role for HMGA2 in LSCC by showing that HMGA2 knockdown inhibited cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. In addition, HMGA2 knockdown was accompanied by increased expression of p16, p21, and p53, indicating that HMGA2 depletion was associated with senescence related changes in LSCC cells. The concurrent upregulation of IL-6 and TGFB1 further supports the involvement of selected SASP related factors, although these findings do not by themselves establish a complete SASP program. Moreover, METTL3 knockdown reduced HMGA2 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels and was accompanied by similar changes in p16, p21, p53, IL-6, and TGFB1, supporting that HMGA2 associated senescence related changes may be linked to m6A dependent regulation. Together, these findings support that HMGA2 may represent a biologically relevant tumor associated candidate and a potential therapeutic target in LSCC.

In summary, by integrating a TCGA-HNSC discovery analysis with LSCC focused validation, this study provides new insights into molecular subtyping and prognostic assessment related to putative m6A associated senescence features in LSCC. While the findings highlight potential links between m6A-related signatures, senescence programs, and immune heterogeneity, limitations remain. In this study, putative m6A-related senescence genes were defined primarily on the basis of transcriptomic correlation with known m6A regulators, rather than direct evidence of m6A modification or reader binding in LSCC. Therefore, these genes should be interpreted as candidate signals of association rather than confirmed m6A targets. Future studies using LSCC models and direct assays, such as MeRIP-seq or binding analyses focused on m6A readers, will be needed to clarify the causal roles of m6A in processes related to senescence. HMGA2 and METTL3 perturbation experiments provided preliminary support for an m6A related link to senescence associated changes in LSCC cells. Future studies using direct assays, such as MeRIP-seq, reader binding analyses, or targeted m6A editing, will still be needed to clarify the causal roles of m6A in senescence related processes. Another limitation is that transcriptomic discovery was performed in the TCGA-HNSC cohort, whereas most downstream validation was conducted in LSCC specific samples and models. In addition, although HPV status was incorporated into the multivariable Cox models in the TCGA-HNSC subset with complete annotation, the HPV positive cases in this subset were predominantly derived from non-laryngeal primary sites, and only a very small number corresponded to LSCC. Therefore, the HPV adjusted prognostic findings should be interpreted primarily within the HNSC discovery framework rather than as LSCC specific evidence. Therefore, whether these findings extend to other HNSC subsites remains to be determined. Future work should further investigate the causal roles of m6A in senescence related processes and extend multi-omic analyses to larger LSCC cohorts, while also evaluating the relevance of these findings across other HNSC subsites.

Conclusions

This study systematically characterized putative m6A-related senescence genes through multi-omics integration, combining TCGA-HNSC bulk data with LSCC single-cell and proteomic data. We identified 93 prognostic genes, defined molecular subtypes, and developed the m6ASenScore for prognostic stratification and association analysis with mutational features. Single-cell analysis revealed malignant epithelial subpopulations with distinct metabolic and inflammatory profiles. HMGA2 emerged as a key candidate gene, with high expression linked to poor survival and functional evidence supporting its tumor promoting role in LSCC. Together, these findings improve our understanding of putative m6A-related senescence genes in LSCC and highlight HMGA2 as a potential therapeutic target in this disease.

The raw sequence data reported in this paper have been deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive (Proteomics & Bioinformatics 2021) in National Genomics Data Center (Nucleic Acids Res 2024), China National Center for Bioinformation / Beijing Institute of Genomics, Chinese Academy of Sciences (GSA-Human: HRA017906) that are publicly accessible at https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa-human/.
