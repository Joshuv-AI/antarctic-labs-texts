Stent-Free Sirolimus DCB for Left Main STEMI
Authors: Jayachandran Ravichandran, Armando Tellez, Erick Marlon Avila Gil, Valentin Gonzalez-Iñiguez, Pablo Juan-Salvadores, Antonio De Miguel Castro, Guillermo Bastos Fernandez, Ramon Orozco, Andres Iñiguez Romo, Victor Alfonso Jimenez-Diaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeft main (LM) ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) is typically managed with urgent stent implantation according to current guidelines. Yet intravascular imaging has shown that plaque erosion may permit alternative strategies.Case SummaryA 43-year-old man with anterior STEMI and hypotension underwent angiography, revealing distal LM thrombus extending into the left anterior descending/left circumflex arteries. Postaspiration optical coherence tomography (OCT) confirmed plaque erosion without rupture. Following lesion preparation, a drug-coated balloon–only strategy was applied to all 3 vessels. Follow-ups at 20 days and 15 months confirmed vessel patency, satisfactory healing, and no recoil or neoatherosclerosis.DiscussionCurrent guidelines lack support for stent-free LM STEMI intervention. However, OCT-guided management allows for individualized treatment in young patients by characterizing lesion biology. Long-term follow-up demonstrates successful vessel healing, suggesting that avoiding permanent metallic scaffolds in selected cases may mitigate lifelong stenting risks.Take-Home-MessageOCT can identify plaque erosion and guide stent-free percutaneous coronary intervention in select left main STEMI cases, offering durable healing and flexibility in antithrombotic therapy.

History of Presentation

A 43-year-old man, active smoker with dyslipidemia and no previous cardiovascular disease, presented to a primary care center with 2.5 hours of retrosternal burning discomfort, diaphoresis, pallor, and nausea. His electrocardiogram demonstrated anterior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). After receiving a loading dose of aspirin and clopidogrel, he was transferred emergently for primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). On arrival, he was pale, diaphoretic, and hypotensive (90/50 mm Hg; Killip class III) with persistent chest discomfort.

Past Medical History

The patient had active tobacco use and dyslipidemia. He was otherwise healthy, with no diabetes, hypertension, or prior cardiac disease. He had no history of thrombotic disorders, bleeding, or previous interventions.

Investigations

Immediate coronary angiography demonstrated thrombotic occlusion of the distal left main (LM) extending into the left anterior descending (LAD) artery with TIMI flow grade 2, along with a large, nonocclusive thrombus in the ostial left circumflex (LCx) artery with TIMI flow grade 2. After wiring both branches, extensive manual thrombus aspiration was performed in the LM-LAD axis and proximal LCx, resulting in marked thrombus reduction and restoration of TIMI flow grade 3. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) identified a culprit substrate consistent with plaque erosion and excluded fibrous-cap rupture, cavity formation, significant calcification (including nodular calcium), and fibroatheroma; therefore, specialty balloons or mechanical debulking were not required. These OCT findings supported a stent-free revascularization strategy once an adequate lumen and procedural stability were achieved after lesion preparation.

Management

Lesion preparation was performed after extensive manual thrombus aspiration in the LM-LAD axis and proximal LCx. Sequential noncompliant balloon angioplasty was undertaken with balloons sized approximately 1:1 to the visually estimated reference vessel diameter (LCx 3.0-3.5 mm; LAD 3.5-4.0 mm; LM 4.0-4.5 mm) using high-pressure inflations. Full expansion without waist was confirmed in 2 orthogonal angiographic projections. Postlesion preparation OCT demonstrated an adequate lumen with low residual thrombus burden, mild residual stenosis, preserved bifurcation geometry, and no major edge dissection or significant intramural hematoma. Because both branches showed satisfactory expansion without carina compromise, kissing balloon inflation was not performed and drug-coated-balloon (DCB) inflation was kept as the final therapeutic step. Overall, lesion preparation met optimal criteria before DCB deployment (TIMI flow grade 3, residual stenosis <30%, and no flow-limiting dissection).

A sirolimus DCB-only strategy was then performed sequentially (SeQuent SCB, B. Braun Melsungen AG: LCx 3.5 × 20 mm; LAD 4.0 × 20 mm; and LM 4.0 × 15 mm). Post-DCB OCT confirmed satisfactory acute luminal gain and procedural stability. Although escalation to a potent P2Y12 inhibitor was initially planned after primary PCI, the patient developed 3 episodes of hematemesis within the first 24 hours, prompting temporary interruption of antiplatelet therapy and close monitoring. He remained hemodynamically stable without recurrent bleeding or hemoglobin decline, and clopidogrel was reintroduced 48 hours after PCI. The remainder of the hospitalization was uneventful, and he was discharged on day 8 on dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin and clopidogrel.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Twenty days after hospital discharge, the patient was readmitted with an episode of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, which was successfully managed pharmacologically. Given the recent LM STEMI and stent-free revascularization, repeat coronary angiography with OCT was performed to reassess the treated LM bifurcation and exclude an ischemic trigger. Angiography and OCT demonstrated excellent early healing, with preserved lumen dimensions and no evidence of residual thrombus, recoil, or complications. He was discharged on a direct oral anticoagulant plus clopidogrel. Long-term follow-up at 15 months after the index PCI showed sustained clinical stability. Repeat angiography demonstrated preserved lumen diameter of the distal LM bifurcation, LAD, and LCx, without significant restenosis. OCT confirmed durable late healing with maintained luminal dimensions and no evidence of late recoil, neoatherosclerosis, or adverse remodeling (Figure 1, Table 1). Discussion

In patients with STEMI, plaque disruption, either rupture or erosion, represents the predominant pathobiologic substrate, whereas calcified nodules and other nondisruptive calcified plaques account for only a minority of culprit lesions.1, 2, 3 This underlying biology has important implications for whether permanent metallic scaffolding is necessary in every case, particularly in younger patients with long life expectancy. LM STEMI carries a high risk of early hemodynamic deterioration and mortality. In this context, our case illustrates how detailed intravascular imaging can identify LM plaque erosion and support a carefully selected, stent-free revascularization strategy using a sirolimus DCB.

Guideline context

Current American and European revascularization guidelines consistently recommend urgent stent-based PCI for LM culprit STEMI when the anatomy allows, given the association among LM obstruction, hemodynamic compromise, and high early mortality.4,5 In nonacute settings, coronary artery bypass grafting remains the standard recommendation for many patients with significant LM disease, with PCI considered an alternative in selected patients after heart team discussion.6 Importantly, current guidelines do not endorse stent-free PCI or DCB therapy for LM lesions in the acute coronary syndrome setting. Accordingly, any stent-free LM strategy in STEMI should be considered exceptional and reserved for highly selected cases supported by robust intravascular imaging guidance. In our patient, OCT-defined plaque erosion and a stable postpreparation result provided the key prerequisites to consider this individualized stent-free approach.

Rationale for a stent-free sirolimus-DCB approach in a young LM STEMI patient

Several factors supported a stent-free, sirolimus DCB-only strategy in this case. First, OCT demonstrated a culprit substrate consistent with plaque erosion, without rupture cavity, calcified nodule, or other features requiring mechanical containment. After extensive thrombus aspiration and optimal balloon-based lesion preparation, angiography and OCT confirmed a stable result (TIMI flow grade 3, <30% residual stenosis, and no flow-limiting dissection), suggesting that permanent scaffolding was not mandatory once lumen geometry and stability had been achieved. Second, the patient was 43 years old, making the lifelong implications of distal LM bifurcation stenting particularly relevant. Third, subsequent clinical events highlighted the practical advantage of avoiding a permanent left main stent: early hematemesis required temporary interruption of antiplatelet therapy, and atrial fibrillation at ∼3 weeks (20 days) necessitated oral anticoagulation, scenarios in which complex bifurcation would have imposed a more constrained ischemic-bleeding tradeoff.

We selected sirolimus-DCB to deliver local antiproliferative therapy without a permanent implant; mechanistically, mammalian target of rapamycin inhibition combines antiproliferative effects with immunomodulatory properties that may be desirable when treating acute culprit lesions in a stent-free setting. Previous proof-of-concept observations showed that OCT may visualize crystalline sirolimus deposits immediately after sirolimus-DCB angioplasty, which is consistent with local drug transfer to the treated segment.7

Sequential treatment of LCx, LAD, and LM enabled homogeneous drug delivery across the bifurcation once adequate lesion preparation had been confirmed. OCT was chosen over intravascular ultrasound because the key question was culprit lesion biology rather than sizing alone. The higher resolution of OCT allowed in vivo assessment of fibrous-cap integrity and exclusion of rupture cavity or a calcified nodule, findings that directly informed selection of a stent-free strategy.8

Compared with previous LM DCB report, largely in stable disease, in-stent restenosis, hybrid approaches, or debulking-based “stentless” techniques, this case uniquely documents OCT-defined plaque erosion in culprit distal LM STEMI treated with a fully stent-free sirolimus-DCB approach, with serial OCT follow-up to 15 months. This strategy is not generalizable; it should be considered only in exceptional, imaging-guided cases in which a favorable substrate and stable postpreparation result are achieved.

Conclusions

OCT-guided, stent-free revascularization using a sirolimus DCB-only strategy may be feasible in highly selected cases of LM STEMI due to plaque erosion. In this patient, serial angiographic and OCT at ∼3 weeks and 15 months demonstrated durable lumen preservation and favorable vessel healing without late recoil or restenosis.
