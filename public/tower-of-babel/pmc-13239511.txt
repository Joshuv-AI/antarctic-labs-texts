Elevated Nitrogen and Phosphorus Levels Suggest High Metabolic Demands in Himalayan High‐Elevation Plants
Authors: Zuzana Chlumská, Martin Macek, Jan Altman, Michael Bartoš, Miroslav Dvorský, Martin Kopecký, Klára Řeháková, Lubomír Adamec, Jorge Gago, Kateřina Čapková, Jiří Doležal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNutrient contents in plant tissues reflect adaptations and environmental constraints, varying across and within species along ecological gradients. Interspecific differences reflect long‐term adaptations, while intraspecific variations may indicate acclimation to present conditions. Examining these responses across elevational ranges is therefore crucial for understanding how nutrients influence species performance. We investigated whether organ nutrients play a significant role at the upper limits of the world's highest occurring herbaceous angiosperms by analysing intraspecific and interspecific variations in leaf carbon (C), nitrogen (N), and phosphorus (P) contents in 22 species, and root N and P contents in 15 species along a gradient from 4560 to 6150 m a.s.l. in the Western Himalaya. Our findings reveal contrasting patterns in nutrient contents. Interspecifically, leaf N and P decreased and C:N increased with elevation, indicating selective pressure for adaptations to reduced nutrient availability. In contrast, intraspecific leaf N content generally increased with increasing elevation. Root N trend did not change interspecifically with elevation, while overall intraspecific trends increased. Surprisingly, subnival species at the highest localities around 6000 m had relatively high tissue N and P contents. This suggests that high‐elevation Himalayan herbs require high nutrient levels, likely for photosynthesis, to achieve a positive annual carbon gain during the short growing season and to accumulate sufficient root reserves. Elevated N and P content, especially at the highest localities, imply that the upper limits of the world's highest‐growing plants may not be limited by nutrient acquisition constraints.

Introduction

Nutrients are essential for plant growth and metabolic processes, with their levels and ratios in tissues, particularly C, N, and P, serving as indicators of how plants acquire and utilise resources in response to environmental factors, providing insights into their adaptive strategies and biological trade‐offs (Evans 1989; Lambers and Poorter 1992; Güsewell 2004; Reich and Oleksyn 2004; Onoda et al. 2017).

Studying nutrient dynamics along elevational gradients offers a unique opportunity to explore how plants adjust to abiotic conditions, providing insights into physiological limitations (Körner 1989; Sparks and Ehleringer 1997; Cordell et al. 1999; Kogami et al. 2001; Macek et al. 2012; Tan and Wang 2016). Studies on this topic have revealed inconsistent elevational nutrient patterns; however, most have been conducted mainly in temperate, humid ranges such as the Alps or the Rocky Mountains (Körner 1989; He et al. 2006; Monson et al. 2006; Albert, Thuiller, Yoccoz, Douzet, et al. 2010; Albert, Thuiller, Yoccoz, Soudant, et al. 2010; Sundqvist et al. 2013; Henn et al. 2018). Recent advances in knowledge on plants in subtropical Himalayas and adjacent regions, where plants reach the highest elevations, concern mostly trees and shrubs (Tan and Wang 2016; Zhao et al. 2016; Drollinger et al. 2017; Zong et al. 2020; Chen et al. 2024; Lu et al. 2025; but see Sigdel et al. 2023). There is particularly scarce information on high‐elevation herbaceous species growing above 5000 m (Chondol et al. 2024, 2025), and even less on the highest‐occurring angiosperms above 6000 m (Zhou et al. 2011; Macek et al. 2012; Dvorský et al. 2015; Dolezal et al. 2016).

Along elevational gradients, plants must adapt not only to decreasing temperature and shorter growing season but also to lower CO2 and O2 partial pressures, as well as variations in nutrient availability, soil moisture, and irradiance (Körner 2021). These diverse constraints lead to distinct elevational patterns in plant nutrient contents, depending on whether the comparison is within or among species (Körner 1989; Kichenin et al. 2013; Hong et al. 2014; Read et al. 2014; Yang et al. 2015). Diminishing soil nutrient availability at higher elevations may result in generally lower plant tissue nutrient content at the interspecific level, reflecting an evolutionary response of alpine plants to shared long‐term conditions and, for example, a shift towards a resource‐conservative strategy (Chapin 1980; Sundqvist et al. 2013). Conversely, decreasing temperatures may promote increased nutrient contents at the intraspecific level, as a result of physiological optimisation, since individuals at higher elevations must compensate for the short growing season by more efficient photosynthesis to meet annual sink demands (Reich and Oleksyn 2004; He et al. 2006). These differences highlight the need to examine both scales, as they represent a combination of long‐ and short‐term responses to environmental pressures (Anderegg 2023).

Leaf nutrients, including total C, N, and P, serve as indicators of photosynthetic efficiency, given that leaf N and P are essential for C assimilation (Evans 1989; Hikosaka 2004; Hernández and Munné‐Bosch 2015). Elevated tissue N and P contents are positively associated with C gain rate and growth rate but negatively correlated with lifespan, creating a contrast between fast‐growing, short‐lived taxa and slow‐growing, long‐lived ones (Lambers and Poorter 1992; Reich et al. 1992). N and P content in plant tissues also reflects their availability in the soil and the overall productivity of the ecosystem, typically showing a negative relationship with low temperatures (Reich and Oleksyn 2004; Vincent et al. 2014). The N:P ratios indicate the relative availability of each element in the environment (Koerselman and Meuleman 1996), while C:N ratios indicate tissue quality, with high levels characteristic of plants with high nutrient‐use efficiency in ecosystems with lower N availability (Zhang et al. 2020).

Surprisingly, alpine plants growing in cold, unproductive environments can exhibit high nutrient assimilation efficiency and accumulate substantial nutrient levels (He et al. 2006; Körner 2021; Chlumská et al. 2022). The pattern deviates from the general expectations outlined by the leaf economics spectrum theory (Wright et al. 2004), as alpine plants tend to be long‐lived (Chondol et al. 2023), and they do not use these nutrients for enhanced growth, probably because growth is more limited by cold than photosynthesis (Nagelmüller et al. 2016, 2017). Importantly, they benefit from reduced growth, particularly low stature, as it effectively decouples their ambient temperature from the low free‐air temperatures and thus helps to maintain a favourable leaf temperature for assimilation (Salisbury and Spomer 1964; Wilson et al. 1987; Liancourt et al. 2020; Körner 2021). Alpine plants are generally able to maintain high metabolic efficiency despite the unproductive environment to achieve a net positive carbon gain within a short growing season (Körner and Diemer 1987; Körner 1989; Lütz 2011).

In comparison to leaf nutrients, there has been limited exploration of nutrient levels in the belowground organs of alpine plants (Monson et al. 2006; Hong et al. 2014). Perennial plants in cold regions rely strongly on belowground organs for overwinter survival and regrowth (Lubbe et al. 2021), serving as a source of nutrients for metabolic processes (Chapin et al. 1990; Hartmann and Trumbore 2016). Alpine herbs can accumulate substantial C reserves as nonstructural carbohydrates (NSC) (Chapin et al. 1990; Hiltbrunner et al. 2021; Chlumská et al. 2022); however, information on N and P storage in belowground organs and their functional role remains scarce. Belowground organs contain N in the form of amino acids and proteins, both of which may contribute to frost protection (Bewley 2002; Tegeder and Masclaux‐Daubresse 2018; Ambroise et al. 2020), a function that could be particularly important for alpine plants. Phosphorus reserves, in turn, are mainly documented in leaves (inorganic phosphate; Yang et al. 2017). Comprehensive information on N and P storage in belowground organs for alpine environments is minimal and exists primarily as case studies (e.g., Lipson et al. 1996; Kleijn et al. 2005).

In this study, we investigate inter‐ and intraspecific elevational variations in leaf nutrient contents of alpine plants along an elevational gradient reaching the upper limit of plant distribution. We aimed to provide insights into the role of nutrient availability and allocation near distribution limits in alpine ecosystems. We hypothesised that (1) at the interspecific level, leaf N and P content will decrease with plant distribution optima, reflecting evolutionary adaptations to lower soil fertility; accompanied by an increase in root N and P as reserves in storage organs, reflecting a greater reliance on belowground nutrient reserves as an adaptation to harsh environmental conditions (H1). (2) At the intraspecific level, both leaf and root N and P content will increase towards the highest elevations as a result of physiological optimisation to compensate for a shorter growing season (H2).

To test our hypotheses, we investigated variations in leaf nutrient content (N, P, and C) in 22 plant species and in root nutrients (N and P) in 15 plant species along an elevational gradient ranging from 4560 to 6150 m a.s.l. in the Western Himalaya. The region hosts plant communities existing at the upper distributional limit of vascular plants, including the highest‐occurring plants on Earth, and encompasses vast unglaciated areas potentially available for colonisation (Dvorský et al. 2015, 2016). Our study design aimed to assess trends in nutrient allocation along the elevational gradient both among and within species to reveal possible adaptive strategies and physiological responses to extreme environmental conditions, and to provide insight into whether nutrient limitation contributes to species' upper elevational limits. To gain insights into how plant tissue nutrient content relates to soil nutrients, we also collected soil samples along the entire studied elevational gradient.

Materials and Methods

Study Site

Sampling was carried out in W Himalaya, Ladakh, India, along an elevational transect spanning from 4650 m a.s.l. on the western banks of Tso Moriri Lake (4522 m) to the upper limit of vascular plant distribution at 6150 m a.s.l. below Shukule peak (32.99° N 78.34° E,32.95° N 78.53° E, Figure S1). The regional climate is influenced by the rain shadow of the Great Himalayan Range, resulting in low annual precipitation of less than 250 mm. Overall glaciation is therefore low, and glaciers occur mainly above 5800 m. Vascular plants occur here up to 6150 m, at one of their highest‐ever recorded locations (Dvorský et al. 2015).

The elevational gradient in our study area is coupled with steep temperature (Table S1) and water‐availability gradients (Dvorský et al. 2011, 2017). The steppes in the lower part of the elevational gradient (ca. 4500–5300 m) are relatively warm and dry due to low precipitation and intense evapotranspiration. Towards the higher located alpine zone (ca. 5300–5600 m), the environment becomes cooler and wetter. The highest subnival zone (> 5600 m) is cold and relatively wet, and precipitation here takes the form of irregular snowfall that often melts by the next day during the growing season.

Overall, snow cover is thin and impermanent, even during winter; thus, plants are exposed to light for most of the year. The growing season in the study area is thus defined by temperature rather than by snow cover and begins when the daily mean soil temperature reaches 0°C (Dvorský et al. 2015, 2016; Dolezal et al. 2016), typically at the end of June. However, night temperatures can drop below −5°C even during the peak growing season, and the diurnal temperature amplitude is high (average diurnal range 18.7°C at 4700 m vs. 18.1°C at 6030 m; Macek, Dvorský, Kopecký, et al. 2021).

Studied Species

We selected 22 widespread species (Figure 1) spanning broad elevational ranges (median ~500 m; Table S2) within the study area. For elevational optima and full ranges across Ladakh, see Figure S2. Most of the species are hemicryptophytes (buds at or below ground level, protected by litter or soil), chamaephytes (buds above ground but close to the surface), and one species is a therophyte (an annual plant that survives winter as seeds). Most species are nonclonal, typically having a simple, persistent taproot or a taproot connected to shoots by belowground branches. A few clonal species have rhizomes or are root‐sprouters (Table 1; see also Klimešová et al. 2011).

Species typically found in dry steppes (ca 4500–5300 m) are Chamaerhodos sabulosa , Oxytropis spp., Ptilotrichum canescens (all parenchymatic roots), as well as Ajania spp. and Artemisia spp. (all woody roots). The grass species Elymus schrenkianus occurs in gravel deposits and grasslands along streams from the steppe to the alpine zone, alongside Poa attenuata , which continues even up to the subnival zone (ca 5600–6150 m). The tall herbs Delphinium brunonianum and Urtica hyperborea typically grow in screes, with the latter associated with streams and sites enriched by animal droppings. The therophyte Artemisia macrocephala can be found on fertile ruderalised places, typically on animal resting places. Species characteristic of alpine and coldest subnival zones include Potentilla pamirica , Waldheimia tridactylites , Saussurea gnaphalodes , and cushion plants Arenaria bryophylla , Thylacospermum caespitosum , and Ladakiella klimesii .

Sampling Design

Along the elevational transect, we sampled at least 5 individuals per locality for each a priori‐selected species list (Table 1), with a minimum of 3 localities per species along the studied elevational gradient (Table S2). Sampling localities along the elevational transect were selected ad hoc in the field based on the occurrence of target species, with a preferred elevational span between localities of either 50 or 150 m. In a few cases, the species were less abundant at a site, leading to fewer individuals being sampled (see Table S3). We started sampling at the lowest possible elevation in the study area, the shores of Lake Tsomoriri (local topographic minimum), and continued upslope through the adjacent valley (see Figure S1), selected because it hosts the highest‐occurring vegetation on Earth. Sampling extended to the highest elevation, 6150 m, at which plants still occur (Angel et al. 2016). The overall distribution limits of the focal species in the study region used in Figure S2 are based on our extensive distribution records collected throughout the entire Ladakh (Klimeš and Dickoré 2005; Dolezal et al. 2016; Dvorský et al. 2016, 2017; Macek, Dvorský, Klimeš, et al. 2021; Macek, Dvorský, Kopecký, et al. 2021). In total, we sampled 949 individuals at 97 distinct localities. For the total number of sampled individuals, locality elevations, and elevational span between localities, see Tables S2–S4. Due to logistical limitations, several species were collected at distinct localities over multiple years (2009–2012) to obtain a representative sample size and coverage of the distribution range of each target species within the study area. Interannual differences were then accounted for in the Data analysis (see Section 2.5).

We collected undamaged, fully developed adult individuals to avoid the influence of the initial rapid growth in juveniles or senescence effects, both of which can affect nutrient content. For 7 of the 22 species, only leaves were collected. For the remaining 15 species (Table S4), we collected both leaves and entire belowground organs (hereafter “roots”). We did not distinguish between roots and stem‐derived organs, such as below‐ground branches and rhizomes, as all may serve as storage organs. We collected all samples in August, during the peak growing season, to minimise phenological shifts across elevations and to ensure comparability among leaves at similar developmental stages.

Nutrient Analyses

Plant Sample Preparation

Fully expanded, vigorous, healthy leaves and healthy root parts were separated from the specimens in the field shortly after plant collection. The roots were cleaned from the soil in water from a glacial stream. Both leaves and roots were placed in porous transparent fabric bags and were air‐dried in the field to prevent moulding. In the laboratory, the samples were dried at 50°C and ground to a fine powder using a ball mill (Retsch MM 301) for subsequent analyses.

Total Leaf Carbon and Nitrogen

Approximately 3 mg of leaf samples were weighed into tin capsules and analysed on an elemental analyser coupled to IRMS (EA‐IRMS) to determine the total leaf C and N content. Samples from 2011 and 2012 were analysed at the Stable Isotope Facility, UC Davis, CA, USA (Sercon Integra2, Cheshire, United Kingdom). Briefly, samples were combusted at 1000°C in a reactor packed with chromium oxide and silvered copper oxide. Following combustion, oxides are removed in a reduction reactor (reduced copper at 650°C). The helium carrier then flows through a water trap (magnesium perchlorate and phosphorous pentoxide). N2 and CO2 are separated on a Carbosieve GC column (65°C, 65 mL min−1) before entering the IRMS. Leaf samples from 2010 were analysed at the Department of Plant Physiology, University of South Bohemia in České Budějovice (elemental analyser NC 2100 Soil, ThermoQuest CE Instruments, Rodano, Italy). The results of both facilities are comparable based on the test conducted on a different dataset.

Leaf Phosphorus and Root Nitrogen and Phosphorus

For root N determination, 0.7–0.9 mg of milled dry root was digested with H2SO4 at 300°C (Kjeldahl method) and analysed colourimetrically on a FIA‐star 5010 Analyzer (Foss Tecator AB, Högänäs, Sweden) in the Analytical Laboratory of the Institute of Botany, Czech Republic. The same approach was also applied for leaf samples of D. brunonianum and T. caespitosum collected in 2009. Previous analyses have shown that N contents measured by EA‐IRMS (see 2.4.2) and the FIA instruments are highly comparable (Pearson correlation = 0.88, R 2 = 0.78, RMSE = 0.38).

For leaf and root P determination, 1.5–2.0 mg of milled sample was digested with HClO4 at 175°C and colourimetrically analysed using the same FIA device.

Soil Sample Preparation

In 2008 and 2010, soil samples were collected along the elevational gradient at the plant sampling localities. At each locality, subsamples were extracted, each from a 10 cm2 area to a depth of 1–3 cm at five randomly selected positions located at least 10 cm from the nearest plant to minimise rhizosphere influence. The subsamples were then pooled into a single composite sample of approximately 150 g. The composite soil samples were air‐dried on aluminium plates in the field for 10 h to reduce moisture content. They were then placed in sterile 100 mL polypropylene bags (Nasco Whirl‐Pack) and transported to the laboratory for further analyses within 2 weeks. In the laboratory, the samples were oven‐dried at 100°C, ground, and sieved through a 2 mm mesh to remove roots and other large particles.

Soil Physico‐Chemical Analyses

Soil samples were analysed for major cations (Ca2+, Mg2+, K+, and Na+) using atomic absorption spectroscopy with a SpectrAA 640 (Varian Techtron). Total nitrogen and N‐NH4 + were determined colourimetrically following Kjeldahl digestion using an automatic FIAstar 5010 Analyzer (Tecator). N‐NO3 − levels could not be accurately measured due to logistical challenges that prevented the necessary cold preservation during storage. P‐PO4 3− content was assessed colourimetrically after HClO4 digestion, using a SHIMADZU UV‐1650 PC spectrophotometer. Exchange soil pH was measured potentiometrically in a 0.01 M CaCl2 suspension. Organic matter was measured after combustion at 450°C for 5 h. All chemical analyses were performed at the Analytical Laboratory of the Institute of Botany, Czech Republic, and followed standardised protocols established by the Association of German Agricultural Analytical and Research Institutes (Naumann and Bassler 1991).

Data Analysis

To explore how nutrient content varies with elevation at both the interspecific and intraspecific level, while accounting for non‐linear responses and species‐specific patterns, we fitted hierarchical generalised additive models (GAMs) (Pedersen et al. 2019) for each nutrient trait. GAMs allow flexible nonlinear relationships while controlling for response‐shape complexity through smooth‐term penalisation.

where s() represents smooth terms, bs = “fs” stands for factor smooths, bs=“ts” thin‐plate regression splines with null space penalisation, and bs = “re” for random effect splines, and k is basis dimension. Nutrient content was modelled as a continuous response variable. Sample elevation (“Elevation”) and species‐specific regional elevational optima (“Optimum”; see below for details) were included as continuous predictors. Species identity was included to account for consistent differences in baseline nutrient content among species and was fitted using a factor‐smooth [s(Species, bs = “fs”)]. Interspecific nutrient trends (H1) were assessed using a smooth term for species' regional elevational optima [s(Optimum); global optimum trend], capturing systematic variation in nutrient content among species positioned differently along the elevational gradient (see Table S2). For that, we used the regional elevation Optimum from Dvorský et al. (2017). The species' elevational Optimum represents the elevation with the highest occurrence probability within the Ladakh region, identified using logistic regression based on comprehensive plot‐based presence‐absence data.

A global smooth of elevation [s(Elevation)] described the shared intraspecific elevational response trend across all species (global elevational trend). In addition, species‐specific smooths of elevation [s(Elevation, by = Species)] allowed the shape of the elevational relationship to vary among species, capturing heterogeneity in intraspecific responses (H2).

Although the focal species are slow‐growing and long‐lived (up to 80 years; Doležal et al. 2018), accumulating nutrients over long periods, their actual nutrient contents may still reflect interannual climatic and phenological variability and local soil conditions. Therefore, we accounted for temporal and spatial sampling structure using random‐effect smooths.

A random‐effect smooth for unique species‐year combinations [s(SpecYear)], equivalent to a random intercept, captured year‐specific deviations in nutrient levels within species, while an additional random‐effect term [s(Elevation, SpecYear)] allowed elevation response structure to vary among species‐year combinations, equivalent to a random slope for elevation in a classical mixed‐effect model. Pseudoreplication at sampling locality was accounted for using a random‐effect smooth for locality [s(LocalityID)]. To assess whether interannual variability altered elevational nutrient responses, we compared nested GAMs that included year‐specific intercepts (SpecYear) and elevation‐by‐year (Elevation, SpecYear) random effects. To limit overfitting and maintain biologically realistic response shapes (Wood 2017), the maximum basis dimension of all smooth terms was restricted to k = 5, and the global smoothness penalty was increased, yielding more conservative (smoother) fits. The used global penalty value (γ = 5) reflects the typical sampling intensity of five individuals per species and site. Models were fitted using fast REML estimation.

Because sampling intensity differed among species and sampling localities (LocalityID), observations were weighted by the inverse number of individuals per species‐locality combination (normalised to unit mean), thereby reducing the influence of uneven replication within localities.

To visually separate intraspecific elevational responses from interspecific trends linked to species elevational optima, we generated model‐based predictions from the fitted GAMs using fixed‐effect smooths only, excluding all random‐effect terms to avoid locality‐ and year‐specific deviations from the general trends.

The global intraspecific elevational trend was visualised by predicting nutrient values across the full sampled elevation range while holding Optimum constant at the mean of species‐specific mean optima, to ensure that predictions reflect a representative community‐level optimum rather than a single species. Species identity was fixed to a dummy level to allow prediction, and species‐specific intercepts were excluded. As a result, the predicted curve represents the marginal effect of Elevation shared across species, independent of interspecific differences and sampling structure.

The global optima interspecific trends along species elevational optima were visualised by predicting nutrient values across the observed range of species Optima, while holding Elevation constant at its overall mean. Species identity was set to a dummy level to enable prediction, and species‐specific intercepts and species‐specific Elevation smooths were excluded from the prediction. Predicted values were then averaged across the grid to obtain a smooth representing the community‐level relationship between nutrient content and species Optima, independent of local Elevation and species‐specific effects.

Species‐specific elevational responses were visualised using predictions restricted to the sampled elevation range of each species. In all cases, predictions were based on marginal smooth effects and their associated confidence intervals.

To evaluate whether interspecific patterns in nutrient content were influenced by shared evolutionary history, we fitted phylogenetically informed generalised additive models (GAMs) as a robustness check. Species names were matched to the Open Tree of Life database (Hinchliff et al. 2015), and an induced subtree was generated for the species included in each analysis. Branch lengths were assigned using Grafen's method (Grafen and Hamilton 1989), and the resulting phylogenetic variance–covariance matrix was standardised and inverted to obtain a phylogenetic precision matrix. Phylogenetic dependence among species was incorporated into the GAM framework using a Markov random field (MRF) smooth for species identity, parameterised by the phylogenetic precision matrix Q [s(Species, bs = “mrf ”, xt = list(penalty = Q))]. This approach accounts for phylogenetic non‐independence in species‐level baseline nutrient content while retaining the same model structure for Optimum, global Elevation and species‐specific Elevation smooths, and random‐effect terms describing the sampling design.

For each nutrient, phylogenetically structured models were compared with the corresponding non‐phylogenetic GAMs using Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC). Results are presented primarily from the non‐phylogenetic GAMs, with phylogenetic analyses used as a robustness check.

To characterise general relationships between elevation and soil parameters across the study gradient, we fitted linear models (see Supporting Information). First‐ and second‐order polynomials of elevation (at which each soil sample was collected) were used as continuous independent variables, with soil parameter values specified as the dependent variables in the models. To select the best‐fitting model from the pair of competing models using first‐ and second‐order polynomials of elevation, we used AICc (small‐sample corrected Akaike's Information Criterion).

All analyses were performed in R, version 4.4.2 (R Core Team 2024). GAMs were fitted using the mgcv 1.9‐3 package (Wood 2017; Pedersen et al. 2019; Li and Wood 2020), with the bam() function employed for large datasets to improve computational efficiency. Phylogenetic dependence among species was incorporated via MRF smooths with the MRFtools 0.0–2 package (Pedersen and Simpson 2023). Phylogenetic trees were retrieved and processed via the rotl 3.1.1 package (Michonneau et al. 2016), and branch lengths were computed using Grafen's method with the ape 5.0 package (Paradis and Schliep 2019). For AICc statistics, the AICcmodavg 2.3‐4 package was used (Mazerolle 2023). All figures were made in the ggplot2 package (Wickham 2016).

Results

GAM models explained between 35% and 81% of the variability in individual nutrient traits (Table 2 and Table S5). A significant interspecific gradient in nutrient content associated with species' elevational Optima was found for foliar N, P, and C:N, whereas no such pattern was observed for root nutrient traits (Table 2 and Figure 2). We identified a significant shared (global) intraspecific Elevation gradient in leaf and root N content.

In most cases, differences in AIC between phylogenetic and non‐phylogenetic models were < 2, indicating little support for improved model fit when accounting for phylogeny (Figure S3 and Table S6). Phylogenetic models showed slightly stronger support for leaf C and N:P; the direction and shape of the Optima, however, were consistent between model types. Given the relatively small number of species included in the phylogenetic analyses (22 for leaf nutrients and 15 for root nutrients) and the limited influence of phylogenetic correction on model inference, we further present results from the model without phylogenetic correction in the main text; for results with phylogenetic correction, we refer to Figure S3 and Table S7.

Elevational Trends in Foliar and Root Nutrients at the Interspecific Level

At the interspecific level, trends along species' regional elevational Optima revealed consistent patterns for several nutrients (Figure 2 and Table 2; Table S5).

Leaf N and P content showed a strong, approximately linear decreasing interspecific pattern related to species' Optima (Figure 2a,b and Table 2; Table S5) along the elevational gradient studied. Species with lower elevational optima exhibited higher foliar N and P than species from higher elevations. In contrast, leaf N:P did not show an elevational trend related to species' Optima (Figure 2c, Figure S3a, Table 2 and Table S7), because both leaf N and leaf P exhibited Optimum‐related declines, resulting in no consistent interspecific pattern in their ratio (Figure 2a–c).

No Optimum‐related trends along the elevational gradient were found for root N, P, and N:P; and leaf C (Figure 2d–g, Figure S3b, Table 2, Tables S5 and S7).

We found a strong, approximately linear increasing Optima trend along the elevational gradient for leaf C:N (Figure 2h and Table 2; Table S5), indicating that species from lower elevations had lower leaf C:N than species with Optima at higher elevations. The foliar C:N Optimum trend was driven primarily by variation in leaf N rather than leaf C (see Figure 2a,h and Figure S3b).

Intraspecific Trends in Foliar and Root Nutrients Along the Elevational Gradient

At the intraspecific level, leaf N content exhibited a weak global increase along the elevational gradient studied (Figure 2a and Table 2; Table S5), indicating a shared global Elevation response across the species. Species‐specific deviations from this shared elevational trend were generally small, and most species closely followed the global Elevation pattern (Table S5). Three species ( Artemisia stracheyi , Chamaerhodos sabulosa , Oxytropis chiliophylla ) showed a stronger increase of leaf N with Elevation, and two species ( Oxytropis microphylla and Thylacospermum caespitosum ) showed opposite, decreasing trends with Elevation (Figure 2a and Table S5). Phylogenetically informed GAMs did not improve model fit for leaf N (Table S6). Overall, the model explained ca. 39% of the variation in leaf N.

We found no global effect of Elevation on leaf P across species along the gradient studied (Figure 2b and Table 2; Table S5). However, five species exhibited increasing leaf P trends: Ajania tibetica , Artemisia macrocephala , O. microphylla , Urtica hyperborea and Waldheimia tridactylites , while Poa attenuata exhibited an increasing hump‐shaped trend. Overall, the model explained ca. 47% of the variation in leaf P.

The global Elevation leaf N:P trend, similar to foliar P, did not show any relationship (Figure 2c and Table 2; Table S5) even after accounting for phylogenetic relatedness (edf ≈ 0, F = 0, p = n.s.; Figure S3a; Tables S6 and S7). A. macrocephala , O. microphylla and U. hyperborea deviated from the global pattern showing decreasing elevational trend (Figure S3a and Table S7). Overall, after accounting for phylogenetic relatedness, the model explained ca. 59% of the variation in leaf N:P (Table S7).

We found a weak positive global Elevation trend for root N (Figure 2d and Table 2; Table S5). Except for Oxytropis tatarica , which exhibited a negative elevational pattern, all other species deviating from the global trend, namely A. macrophylla , A. stracheyi , C. sabulosa , L. klimesii , and Saussurea gnaphalodes , showed steeply positive trends. Overall, the model explained ca. 75% of the variation in root N.

We found no global Elevation relationship in root P across species along the gradient studied (Figure 2e and Table 2; Table S5). Several species, namely A. macrocephala , Potentilla pamirica , Ptilotrichum canescens , S. gnaphalodes and W. tridactylites , deviated from the global trend by an increase in root P with elevation, and O. chiliophylla showed an increasing hump‐shaped pattern. Overall, the model explained ca. 36% of the variation in root P.

We found no global Elevation relationship in root N:P across species along the gradient studied (Figure 2f and Table 2; Table S5). Except for O. tatarica and P. canescens having a negative elevational trend, species A. stracheyi , C. sabulosa , L. klimesii , and P. attenuata possessed positive elevational relationships (Figure 2f and Table S5). Overall, the model explained ca. 49% of the variation in root N:P.

The global Elevation leaf C trend did not show any relationship (Figure 2g and Table 2; Table S5) even after accounting for phylogenetic relatedness (edf ≈ 0, F = 0, p = n.s.; Figure S3b; Tables S6 and S7). Except for O. chiliophylla , which showed a strong positive, nearly linear elevational trend, the other significant species, A. tibetica , S. gnaphalodes and W. tridactylites , exhibited weak, near‐flat elevational responses (Figure S3b and Table S7). Overall, after accounting for phylogenetic relatedness, the model explained ca. 83% of the variation in leaf C.

For leaf C:N, although the global Elevation smooth was nominally significant (p = 0.032; see Figure 2h and Table 2; Table S5), the term was effectively shrunk to zero (edf ≈ 0), indicating a negligible elevational effect on leaf C:N across species. We found a weak negative elevational trend for A. stracheyi , a U‐shaped increasing trend for P. attenuata , and weak positive trends for O. microphylla and W. tridactylites (Figure 2h and Table S5). Overall, the model explained ca. 51% of the variation in leaf C:N.

N content was consistently and substantially higher in leaves than in roots within individuals across species (paired t‐tests, p < 0.001; Table S8). The only exception was D. brunonianum , where root N was slightly higher than leaf N, but the difference was not significant. Root N exceeded leaf N content in some individuals of D. brunonianum (14 individuals/4 localities), L. klimesii (13/5), O. chiliophylla (4/1), S. gnaphalodes (11/5), and P. attenuata (2/2). Leaf P exceeded root P in most species (paired t‐tests; Table S9), except in O. chiliophylla and S. gnaphalodes , where the opposite pattern was observed, and in D. brunonianum, O. microphylla , and P. pamirica , where differences were not significant. Additionally, some individuals of A. stracheyi (4/1), D. brunonianum (10/4), L. klimesii (13/5), O. microphylla (8/3), O. tatarica (2/2), P. attenuata (17/6), and P. pamirica (7/3) exhibited higher root than corresponding leaf P values.

We found a significant positive correlation between foliar N and P in 13 out of 22 species, whereas a significant negative correlation was observed only in A. salsoloides (Table S10). Foliar and root N content showed a significant positive correlation in 2 out of 15 species; no significant correlation was observed in the remaining species. Foliar and root P content showed a significant positive correlation in 6 out of 15 species, while the others exhibited no significant correlation between the organs (Table S11).

Across nutrient traits, interannual variability was primarily manifested in random intercepts rather than slopes (Table S12). This indicates shifts in overall nutrient contents between years, while elevational trends remain largely stable.

Soil Nutrients and Physical Characteristics Along the Elevational Gradient

Along the elevational gradient studied, we observed significant hump‐shaped trends for total soil nitrogen (N), phosphate (P‐PO4 3−), potassium (K+), and organic matter content, peaking at mid‐elevations. Calcium (Ca2+) showed a significant linear decrease with elevation; sodium (Na+) exhibited a convex trend, with the lowest values at the mid‐elevations. No significant elevational trends were detected for ammonium (N‐NH4 +), magnesium (Mg2+), or pH (Figure S4, Tables S13 and S14).

Discussion

Our results suggest that nutrient patterns along elevational gradients reflect a combination of evolutionary filtering driven by lower soil fertility and physiological responses to an increasingly shorter growing season. Consistent with expectations of adaptation to reduced nutrient availability, species with higher elevational optima exhibited lower foliar nutrient contents at the interspecific level. In contrast, root nutrient contents did not vary systematically across species along the gradient, suggesting an overall importance of belowground reserves in the seasonal alpine environment. Intraspecific nutrient changes were modest, yet they suggest physiological optimisation along the elevational gradient, particularly for leaf and root N, which may contribute to plant performance near their upper distribution limits. The opposing inter‐ and intraspecific patterns reflect the coexistence of long‐term evolutionary adaptations and short‐term physiological plasticity along the elevational gradient.

Interspecific Changes in Foliar N, P and C

We observed a marked interspecific decrease in leaf N and P content (Figure 2a,b), suggesting that diminishing soil nutrient availability at higher elevations imposes selective pressure favouring species better adapted to a low‐nutrient environment.

This pattern is not contradicted by the alpine mid‐elevation peak in total soil N found (Figure S4), because it reflects stocks rather than plant‐available N. Cold conditions at high elevations suppress soil microbial activity (Huber et al. 2007; Hofmann et al. 2016) and mineralisation, so much of the N may be locked in soil organic matter (Makarov et al. 2003; Schimel and Mikan 2005; Gutiérrez‐Girón et al. 2015; Bonfanti et al. 2025), peaking in our study in the alpine zone (Figure S4), or in microbial biomass where it is not readily accessible to plants (Gerzabek et al. 2004; Cotrufo et al. 2013; de Vries and Bardgett 2016). Consequently, leaf N can decline where total soil N is high. Above the alpine zone, total soil N decreased, plausibly due to sparser vegetation (Dvorský et al. 2011, 2015) and, therefore, diminished organic‐matter input (Ohtsuka et al. 2008; Müller et al. 2017; Hou et al. 2021).

A similar stock‐availability decoupling, further shaped by the way plants regulate P uptake and recycling, likely influences our foliar P trends. Accordingly, leaf P content did not mirror the peak in soil phosphate content observed in the alpine zone (Figure 2b and Figure S4). Elevational foliar P dynamics are generally more variable than those of N (Reich and Oleksyn 2004; Sundqvist et al. 2013; Gerdol et al. 2018; Wojcik et al. 2021) and can be decoupled from soil P stocks through efficient P resorption from senescing tissues or additional sources of P, such as mycorrhizal associations (Kotilínek et al. 2017; Zhao et al. 2017; Hiiesalu et al. 2023).

At the low‐elevation, dry end of the gradient studied, in the steppes, total soil N and phosphate levels were lowest and increased with elevation towards the wetter alpine zone (Figure S4). Drought, like cold, can reduce soil microbial activity and nutrient transport to roots (Shaw and Harte 2001; Gavazov 2010; Schimel 2018). Interestingly, species with optima in the lowest and driest steppe habitat exhibited the highest foliar N overall (average N > 2.5% in 10 species; Table S4), and mostly also high P content (p > 0.2% in 6 species), suggesting they do not have problems with nutrient acquisition. Steppe plants cope with soil water deficit by developing deep roots and/or growing near streams, as reflected in their biomass allocation (Doležal et al. 2024) and isotopic composition (Liancourt et al. 2020). The paradox of low nutrient soil stocks alongside high nutrient tissue content may be explained by our sampling design, which was limited to the upper soil layers and bulk soil; nutrients may instead be concentrated in deeper, unsampled horizons or in rhizospheric “fertility islands” (Skujiņš 1981).

The increase in foliar C:N with elevation, observed at the interspecific level, was mainly driven by a decrease in leaf N along the elevational gradient (Figure 2a,g,h). Higher foliar C:N ratios found in species with optima at higher, colder elevations are characteristic of plants from N‐poor habitats, with typically higher nitrogen‐use efficiency. Such species, often with tough, long‐lived leaves, occupy the “slow end” of the plant economics spectrum (Wright et al. 2004; Lütz 2011). Surprisingly, most species in our dataset form soft leaves, presumably low in structural C, emerging in spring and typically senescing by the end of the short growing season (Diemer et al. 1992; Chondol et al. 2025). Species such as Artemisia minor, Delphinium brunonianum, Oxytropis chiliophylla, and Potentilla pamirica can contain up to 14% of their leaf dry matter in the form of NSC (Chlumská et al., unpubl. res.), with mean total leaf C ~36%–45% and leaf N ~1.7%–2.9%. A notable exception is the evergreen cushion Thylacospermum caespitosum, with higher leaf C (on average 49%), lower leaf N (~1.5%), and stiff leaves—traits consistent with a conservative strategy.

Despite the general decrease in foliar N at the interspecific level along the elevational gradient studied, species with the elevational optima at the highest elevations still maintained high absolute foliar N (on average ~2%, up to 3.8% of dry mass) and elevated foliar P content (~0.2%, up to 0.4%). Such high values—otherwise typical of plant species adapted to more productive environments, with high photosynthetic rates and short‐lived leaves (Reich et al. 1997; Wright et al. 2004)—may seem paradoxical in the unproductive alpine environment (Macek et al. 2025). However, since the growing season here can be as short as 2 months (Dvorský et al. 2015), high leaf nutrient contents commonly bound to high photosynthetic performance (Körner and Diemer 1987; He et al. 2006; but see Onoda et al. 2017; Chondol et al. 2025) likely enable high‐elevation plants to speed up the metabolism and achieve a net positive C balance (Körner 2021).

In the context of rapid climate change in the Himalaya (Dolezal, Jandova, Macek, Mudrak, et al. 2021), plants may benefit from a longer growing season. Elevation integrates several environmental drivers, but temperature is expected to change most strongly, whereas others (e.g., radiation, atmospheric pressure, wind) will remain largely constant (Körner 2021). Rising atmospheric CO2 may further alter plant carbon balance and C‐nutrient stoichiometry but is unlikely to fully remove soil‐related constraints on nutrient availability (Terrer et al. 2019). Dolezal et al. (2016) documented upslope expansion of several subnival species between 2001 and 2014 within our study site, driven by warming and accompanied by reduced vegetation cover and shifts in species composition. These compositional changes were primarily driven by variation in species cover rather than species turnover and were linked to changing moisture regimes and increased soil disturbance. The direction of vegetation change thus seems to depend on the interplay between temperature‐driven range expansion and disturbance‐mediated community reorganisation within the dry Western Himalaya.

Intraspecific Changes in Foliar N, P and C Content

In contrast to interspecific trends, species exhibited an overall increase in foliar N within their distribution range, whereas no clear elevational trend was detected for foliar P (Figure 2a,b). Several species showed significant increases in these elements, despite the presumed decrease in soil nutrient availability (Huber et al. 2007; Müller et al. 2017). These intraspecific trends may reflect greater investment in photosynthetic machinery (Evans 1989; He et al. 2006; but see Onoda et al. 2017), supported by the predominantly positive correlation we found between foliar N and P, which suggests a concurrent higher need for both nutrients (Reich et al. 2009; Geng et al. 2014; Ma et al. 2019). The role of leaf P is, however, more complex, as it serves not only as a component of assimilatory power but also of a membrane structure and plays a crucial role in growth and various metabolic processes (Lambers 2022; Suriyagoda et al. 2023). For some species that showed significant foliar N:P trends, limitation by one of these nutrients cannot be ruled out (Koerselman and Meuleman 1996; but see Xu et al. 2014). We note, however, that intraspecific elevational responses are based on a limited number of distinct sampling localities per species. Consequently, weak or absent elevational patterns may partly reflect limited statistical power rather than a true absence of biological response. For steppe species, the sampled elevational range represents only a truncated portion of their full distribution, and additional data from their lower distributional limits are needed to confirm the generality of the trends reported here.

Towards higher elevations, plants face an increasingly short growing season and must maximise C gain to fuel winter respiration, spring regrowth, reproduction, and protect tissues from freezing (Hiltbrunner et al. 2021; Körner 2021; Chlumská et al. 2022). In subtropical mountains, high irradiance results in high daytime canopy temperatures (Lütz 2011; Buchner et al. 2015; Rehakova et al. 2019) and, particularly when combined with low morning temperatures, can induce photoinhibition and formation of reactive oxygen species (Fetene et al. 1997; Ensminger et al. 2006). To withstand such harsh conditions, alpine plants synthesise substantial amounts of storage, stress‐mitigating, antifreeze, antioxidant, and light‐shielding C‐ and N‐rich compounds (Lütz 2011; Ma et al. 2015; Guangxin et al. 2016; Zhao et al. 2019; Chlumská et al. 2022). A powerful and resilient photosynthetic apparatus, requiring elevated levels of C, N and P, thus appears crucial for survival in alpine environments.

Changes in Root N and P Content

Contrary to our expectation that species with optima at higher elevations would increase root N and P storage, we found no interspecific trends in root nutrient contents (Figure 2d,e). This stability suggests that reliance on belowground reserves remains important across the entire gradient, possibly due to the strong seasonality of the alpine environment (Chapin et al. 1990). The absence of a directional pattern may also reflect the diversity of species strategies along the elevational gradient for acquiring and storing water and nutrients. In dry steppes, species manage water table either by having deep, coarse roots that are rich in structural C and relatively poor in N and P, as in Artemisia spp., or by growing closer to streams, as is typical for grasses, which allows them to possess shallower, parenchymatous roots with higher N and P content (Liancourt et al. 2020; Chondol et al. 2023). With increasing elevation and reduced water stress, the proportion of species with parenchymatous coarse roots, having a higher fraction of living cells, increases (Doležal et al. 2018), likely conferring greater physiological flexibility to cope with episodic stress. Plants from higher habitats experience persistent snow patches and soil disturbances and depend on nutrient storage to sustain respiration and rapid regrowth (Chapin et al. 1990; Körner 2021). Root‐associated fungal symbioses, which are often prominent in cold and otherwise stressful environments, may further influence N and P acquisition; their contribution to the observed interspecific pattern therefore cannot be ruled out (Kotilínek et al. 2017; Giesemann et al. 2020; Ruotsalainen et al. 2022; Hou et al. 2024). At the intraspecific level, root nutrient responses to elevation were weak and species‐specific. We found a modest increase only in root N (Figure 2d–f), suggesting a shared physiological adjustment to colder environments, potentially involving frost protection via N‐rich amino acids and proteins (Lütz 2011; Ambroise et al. 2020). In contrast, root P showed no consistent elevational trend despite substantial species‐level variation, indicating that P responses are likely governed by local soil conditions, microhabitat differences, and symbiotic associations (see Section 4.1) rather than by a common physiological response. Furthermore, biomass allocation among different types of belowground organs is known to vary along elevational gradients in alpine environments (Doležal et al. 2024). Therefore, its influence on belowground nutrient content within species cannot be ruled out, as composite belowground samples were used in this study. Further research is needed to reveal the exact cause of the trends we found.

Interestingly, in individuals of several species, root N and P content equaled or even exceeded foliar values, an uncommon pattern (e.g., Dijkstra et al. 2003; Tjoelker et al. 2005; Geng et al. 2014; Hong et al. 2014; Liu et al. 2019) supporting the idea that high belowground storage capacity is advantageous. The accumulation may indicate plants prioritise survival over growth (Chapin et al. 1990; Wiley and Helliker 2012; Dolezal, Jandova, Macek, and Liancourt 2021), or, importantly, that they cannot use the acquired nutrients because they approach their physiological limits, as especially growth is sensitive to low temperatures (Nagelmüller et al. 2017; Körner 2021; Jandova et al. 2025). Consistent with this idea, some individuals recorded at 6150 m, the uppermost locality inhabited by plants in the study region, had elevated root nutrient content (N 1.6%–3.0% and P 0.15%–0.23%).

A key question is how alpine plants at higher elevations attain such high tissue N and P contents, despite generally decreasing soil nutrient availability (Huber et al. 2007; Sundqvist et al. 2013). Several non‐exclusive mechanisms likely contribute. First, high‐elevation plants often allocate less biomass to aboveground stems, thereby saving nutrients for other organs (Körner and Renhardt 1987; Wu et al. 2013; Doležal et al. 2024). Second, their belowground organs remain physiologically active even at advanced age due to a high proportion of living cells (Doležal et al. 2018; Chondol et al. 2023), providing a higher capacity for nutrient uptake and retention over a long lifespan. Third, efficient nutrient resorption from ageing tissues is common in alpine species (Zhao et al. 2017; Zong et al. 2020; Körner 2021). Finally, species from higher habitats in the study area accumulate a substantial mass of dead leaves, providing a substrate for nutrient retention (Chondol et al. 2024). Together, these mechanisms may contribute to high nutrient levels observed in alpine plants, comparable to those found in plants from more productive ecosystems.

Conclusions

We found large among‐ and within‐species variation in plant nutrient contents across elevations in the arid Himalaya. At the interspecific level, foliar N and P generally decreased and C:N increased with elevation, indicating a selection for species better adapted to lower soil nutrient availability. In contrast, root nutrients did not change with elevation, suggesting an overall high need for nutrient storage in the seasonal alpine environment.

Intraspecifically, species mostly increased their leaf and root N content towards higher elevations, reflecting a high metabolic activity needed to cope with a harsh environment. Overall, elevated N and P content, especially at the highest localities, imply that the upper limits of the world's highest‐growing plants are less constrained by nutrient acquisition than by processes related to their utilisation, challenging the view that nutrient limitations primarily set these boundaries. Under rapid climate change in the Himalaya, high nutrient content may provide these species with physiological flexibility, potentially enabling them to benefit from the extended growing season.

Together, these results advance our understanding of alpine plant adaptive strategies and emphasise the importance of considering both interspecific and intraspecific variability when interpreting plant responses to stress in the world's highest vascular plant habitats.

Table S12: Variance partitioning of interannual variation (random effects) in nutrient‐elevation relationships using nested GAMs. To assess whether year‐to‐year variability affected baseline nutrient levels or the strength of elevational responses, we compared R 2 values from three nested generalised additive models (GAMs). The null model (R 2null) included main effects of Species, Elevation and Optimum, with LocalityID as a random effect. The second model additionally included a random intercept for Year × Species combinations (R 2intercept), capturing interannual variation in baseline nutrient levels. The third model further included a year‐specific random effect smooth term for Elevation within Species (R 2slope), representing interannual variability in elevational responses. Fractions of explained variability (ΔR 2) were calculated as differences between the respective model R 2 values. Full model details are provided in chap. 2.5.
