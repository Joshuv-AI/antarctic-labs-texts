Medicine price and affordability in China and selected ASEAN countries: a cross-country comparison with forward-looking implications for regional pooled procurement
Authors: Zhuangqi Li, Shuting Zhang, Qian Xu, Bao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe China-ASEAN regional medical procurement platform was launched in early 2025. However, the scope and operational mechanisms of the platform remain unclear. This study aims to assess medicine prices and affordability in China and ASEAN countries, explore potential implementation challenges of the platform, provide policy suggestions.MethodsWe selected commonly used medicines from four ATC categories (alimentary tract and metabolism, cardiovascular system, anti-infective for systemic use, nervous system). Prices were standardized to WHO defined-daily-dose (DDD) prices and converted into median price ratio (MPR) using Management Sciences for Health (MSH) international reference prices (IRP). All prices data were collected from official public sources and converted to US dollars using the official 2024 annual average exchange rate. Affordability was estimated the number of days’ statutory gross daily minimum wages required to purchase one DDD, with wage data obtained from the International Labor Organization (ILO). Descriptive statistics were performed.ResultsA total of 68 medicines were included, with 68, 68, 60, and 59 available in China, Thailand, Indonesia, and the Philippines, respectively. Median MPRs were 0.88 (IQR:0.46–3.49), 0.97 (IQR:0.50–2.20), 1.69 (IQR:0.77–3.16), 1.86(IQR:0.72–5.03), respectively, and 45.6%, 45.6%, 61.7%, and 67.8% of medicines were priced above the IRPs. Prices varied widely across and within countries. For cardiovascular medicines, median MPRs exceeded the IRPs in China 1.72(IQR:0.53–6.30), Indonesia 1.79(IQR:0.78–2.80), the Philippines 2.85(IQR:1.31–6.43), while Thailand achieved a lower price of 0.78(IQR:0.31–1.37). The overall affordability was higher in China, Indonesia and Thailand, where one DDD of medicine required less than 6% of a day’s wage, with median values of 4.8% (IQR:2.5%-19.3%), 5.1% (IQR:2.3%-9.6%), and 3.6% (IQR:1.8%-8.1%), respectively, compared with 14.0% (IQR:5.4%-37.7%) in the Philippines. Sensitivity analysis excluding extreme affordability values yielded similar results.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that understanding cross-country disparities in medicine prices and affordability may help inform the design of future regional purchasing strategies. Realizing the benefits of such joint procurement will require strong political commitment to establish a legal framework, enhance price transparency, harmonize regulations, and strengthen supply chains to ensure the platform’s effectiveness and sustainability.Keywords: regional medical procurement, cross-border medical procurement, China-ASEAN regional medical procurement platform, medicines price, affordability

Background

Global disparities in medicine affordability remain substantial, and regional inequalities are particularly pronounced, posing a critical barrier to achieving the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) [1]. The COVID-19 pandemic further exposed and intensified these challenges. Medicines account for 20–60% of total health expenditure in developing and transitional countries [2], while only 18% in countries of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) [3]. Furthermore, up to 90% of the population in developing countries rely on out-of-pocket payments to purchase medicines [4, 5], increasing the risk of financial hardship.

Within the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN), heterogeneity in economic development — from low-and-middle income countries (LMIC) to high-income countries (HIC)— results in substantial disparities in health resource allocation. In 2022, per capita health expenditure reached USD 4321 in Singapore and USD 666 in Brunei, but only USD 58 in Myanmar and USD 42 in Lao PDR [6]. Myanmar had the highest poverty rate in the region, reported 24.8% in 2017, followed by Cambodia, Lao PDR, and the Philippines [6]. Consequently, medicines remain unaffordable for large segments of the population and impose a considerable fiscal burden on government budgets across ASEAN.

Transnational cooperation is increasingly becoming a critical strategy for addressing complex public health challenges, and both United Nations and the WHO have repeatedly called for stronger collaborative efforts [7]. Public procurement, especially cross-regional joint procurement, has the potential to improve equitable access to medicines, reduce out-of-pocket expenses for patients, and lower pharmaceutical expenditure for health systems [8, 9]. Regional joint procurement initiatives have already been carried out in Latin America, East Africa, the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC), the Organization of Eastern Caribbean States, and several European Union countries [10]. However, there is no standardized model for establishing such mechanisms. Existing initiatives vary in contractual arrangements, governance structures, operational scope, product types, and each must be adapted to local political, economic and regulatory contexts [11].

Although the benefits of joint procurement are substantial, existing studies also suggest that the challenges may be even greater [10, 11]. Effective planning is crucial for successful implementation but is resource-intensive and must be refined progressively through practice [12]. Some studies have focused on the success factors of joint procurement and provided valuable insights [10, 13]. But current practices and research predominantly originate from Europe, Africa, the Americas and global level practices, with no documented examples yet emerging from Asia. An early study indicated that, at that time, regional pooled procurement in the Western Pacific Region (South East Asia and Pacific Island Countries) was considered unworkable [14]. Asia hosts the world’s largest population and faces a substantial burden of medicine expenditure [15, 16], while its healthcare and regulatory systems differ markedly from those in other regions. Therefore, existing experiences cannot be directly transferred, highlighting the need for context-specific evidence from Asia.

On January 9, 2025, the China-ASEAN Medical Regional Procurement Platform was officially launched, as the first intergovernmental cross-border medical procurement platform in Asia, aiming to improve access to quality medicines and medical products and strengthen healthcare collaboration between China and ASEAN member states [17, 18]. The establishment of this platform not only represents the first regional practice and evidence from Asia, but also serves as a potential model for future global health governance. However, the scope and operational mechanisms of procurement under this platform remain unclear, and substantial efforts are still required before it can fully achieve its intended functions. Medicine price and affordability play a pivotal role in selecting products for joint procurement catalogues and in establishment procurement priorities. Although numerous international studies have assessed medicine price and affordability [5, 19, 20], evidence from China and ASEAN countries remains limited, and much of the existing research is outdated [5, 21]. In addition, it remains unclear whether current conditions in the region facilitate or challenge the feasibility of joint procurement.

Therefore, this study aims to assess medicine prices and affordability in China and ASEAN countries. Due to data availability constraints, three ASEAN countries (Indonesia, Thailand, the Philippines) were included, and four therapeutic categories of medicines classified under Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) were selected. As the China-ASEAN regional medical procurement platform has only recently been launched and platform-level procurement data are not yet publicly available, this study uses existing national price data to provide a baseline assessment of medicine prices and affordability across these countries. The discussion further explores potential enablers and barriers to regional joint procurement under the platform, as well as principles for prioritizing candidate medicines for procurement, thereby providing preliminary insights to support policy development and implementation strategies.

Methods

Medicine selection

The burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) is increasing and has become the leading cause of death in ASEAN, with cardiovascular diseases, diabetes and chronic respiratory diseases accounting for over 80% of NCD-related early deaths [22]. China is also experiencing a growing NCD epidemic [23]. Accordingly, this study focused on medicines with four commonly ATC areas for NCDs, which are alimentary tract and metabolism, cardiovascular system, anti-infective for systemic use, nervous system. These medicines are in high demand and are generally recommended as first-line treatment in global or national treatment guidelines. Importantly, generic procurement price data were accessible for these medicines. Only injectable formulations, tablets and capsules were included in the analysis to ensure comparability across countries. Insulin products and fixed-dose combination therapies were not included because their formulation, delivery systems, and market availability vary substantially across countries, which limits the comparability of cross-country price and affordability estimates.

Data sources

Medicine price data of China were retrieved from public announcement of the ten waves of National Volume-Based Procurement (NVBP) [24]. For ASEAN member states, we searched official government websites to obtain medicine price data. Procurement prices were available for three countries. In Thailand, the Drug and Medical Supply Information Center of the Ministry of Public Health published reference medical prices for regular procurement, including minimum, median, and average price types [25]. In this study, we used the median price for analysis. For medicines supplied by more than one company, the median price across all listed suppliers was calculated. In Indonesia, procurement prices were obtained from the e-Catalogue website managed by the Government Goods/Services Procurement Policy Institute (LKPP) under the Ministry of Health, which reports procurement prices across different regions [26]. For each medicine, we calculated the median procurement price across regions for use in the analysis. In the Philippines, the Department of Health established the drug price reference index (DPRI) for all essential medicines procured in public sector [27], which provides procurement prices ranging from the lowest to the highest values. In our analysis, we used the median price. Malaysia does not publicly disclose procurement prices, and only patient medicine prices were available [28]. Procurement prices were similarly inaccessible in the other ASEAN countries. Therefore, only Thailand, Indonesia, and the Philippines were included in the analysis. Statutory gross daily minimum wages data were used to calculate the affordability of medicines. These data were obtained from the International Labor Organization (ILO) [29].

Medicine price adjustment

The original procurement prices for China used in our study ranged from 2019 to 2024, as medicines from different procurement waves were published in different years. To account for inflation and enable cross-country comparability, all medicines prices were adjusted to the 2024 base year using the annual general Consumer Price Index (CPI) published by the National Bureau of Statistics of China. The same CPI base year was applied consistently in the adjustment process to ensure accuracy. The procurement price data for Thailand, Indonesia and Philippines were obtained for 2024 and therefore did not require inflation adjustment. Prices were standardized to WHO defined-daily-dose (DDD) prices. Subsequently, all price data were converted to US dollars (US$) using the official 2024 annual average exchange rate (AAER). Following WHO/Health Action International (HAI) methodology, purchasing power parity (PPP) adjusted was not applied because the analysis focuses on procurement prices of generic medicines, which are trade in a global market with multiple suppliers [30].

Statistics analysis

Medicine prices were expressed as per defined daily dose (DDD) and converted into the WHO/HAI recommended median price ratio (MPR) by dividing each medicine’s price by its corresponding international reference price (IRP), consistent with previous studies. [5, 19, 20] It should be noted that the MPR is a relative indicator and does not represent a precise measure of current global price deviations. The IRPs were obtained from the Management Sciences for Health (MSH) International Medical Products Price Guide (2015 edition) [31], which reports median prices of multisource medicines offered to developing and middle-income countries by different suppliers. The IRPs were also adjusted to 2024 values using the annual CPI of the United States to ensue comparability with inflation-adjusted local prices. The results of price comparison were presented as MPR values with interquartile ranges (IQR) and visualized by forest plots.

Affordability was estimated using the statutory gross daily minimum wage, establishing the number of days’ wages required to purchase one DDD of treatment for common conditions. This calculation followed a modified version of the WHO/HAI methodology [30, 32]. The results of affordability were presented as median with IQR and visualized by box plots. A sensitivity analysis excluding medicines with affordability exceeding 400% of daily wage produced similar median estimates.

Descriptive statistical analysis were conducted to compare medicine prices and affordability across countries. The analysis focused on descriptive comparisons rather than examining the determinants of price variation.

Comparison of medicine price between each country and international reference price

For all medicines, the median MPR in China and Thailand was 0.88 (IQR:0.46–3.49) and 0.97 (IQR:0.50–2.20), respectively, indicating that procurement prices in these two countries were close to and lower than the IRPs. However, there still have 45.6% of medicines in both China and Thailand still exceeded the IRP. By contrast, the median MPRs in Indonesia and the Philippines were 1.69 (IQR:0.77–3.16) and 1.86(IQR:0.72–5.03), showing higher price than the IRP, with 61.7%, 67.8%of medicines prices above the IRP. Because medicine availability differed across countries, we conducted a supplementary analysis restricted to medicines available in all four countries to improve cross-country comparability. The median MPR were 0.79 (IQR:0.43–3.78) in China, 1.55(IQR:0.74–3.67) in Indonesia, 0.96(IQR:0.50–2.13) in Thailand, and 1.73(IQR:0.70–4.50) in the Philippines. These results were broadly consistent with those obtained from the country-specific medicine basket analysis.

Across different ATC therapeutic areas, medicine prices varied widely both among and within countries (Fig. 2). In alimentary tract and metabolism category, public procurements prices in China and Thailand were very close to the IRPs, whereas Indonesia and the Philippines paid 1.76–3.31 times the IRP. For cardiovascular medicines, the median MPR exceeded the IRP in China 1.72(IQR:0.53–6.30), Indonesia 1.79(IQR:0.78–2.80), and the Philippines 2.85(IQR:1.31–6.43), while Thailand achieved a lower price of 0.78(IQR:0.31–1.37).

In anti-infective for systemic use area, all four countries procured lower price than the IRPs. China had the lowest price in 0.62(IQR:0.38–0.84), followed by Philippines in 0.79(IQR:0.60–1.93), while Thailand and Indonesia had price close to IRPs. In contrast, median MPR of medicines for nervous system use were priced well above the IRP in all countries, with median MPRs ranging from 2.01 to 4.41.

Regarding dosage forms, injection in China were relatively affordable, with MPR of 0.74(IQR:0.41–2.85), and only 35.7% of medicines exceeded the IRPs. In Thailand, tablets or capsules showed comparatively lower price, with MPR was 0.90(IQR:0.41–2.11), and 42.5% of medicines priced higher than the IRPs.

Affordability of medicines in China and selected ASEAN countries

The overall affordability of the included basket of medicines was higher in China, Indonesia and Thailand than in the Philippines (Fig. 3). In the former three countries, purchasing one DDD of medicine required less than 6% of a day’s wage, with median values of 4.8% (IQR:2.5%-19.3%), 5.1% (IQR:2.3%-9.6%), and 3.6% (IQR:1.8%-8.1%), respectively. In contrast, in the Philippines, one DDD required 14.0% (IQR:5.4%-37.7%) of a day’s wage. A supplementary analysis using a common basket of medicines available in all four countries showed patterns. The median affordability values were 4.4% (IQR:2.4%-20.8%) in China, 4.7%(IQR:2.2%-11.1%) in Indonesia, 3.5%(IQR:1.8%-7.8%) in Thailand, and 13.0%(IQR:5.2%-33.8%) in the Philippines. Despite the generally favorable affordability levels, some medicines remained unaffordable in certain countries. Specially, three medicines required more than one full day’s wage to purchase one DDD in China, Indonesia and Thailand, compared with six medicines in the Philippines. Furosemide required more than one day’s wage in all countries, ranging from 133.9% of a day’s wage in Thailand to 524.3% in China. Lidocaine also showed substantial affordability challenges, requiring 422.9%, 186.6%, and 125.3% of a day’s wage in China, Thailand, and Indonesia, respectively. Moreover, affordability for specific medicines varied considerably among countries, for example, the proportion of a day’s wage needed to purchase one DDD of lidocaine ranged from 50.8% to 422.9%.

Across all four ATC therapeutic areas, overall affordability was generally high in all countries, with most medicines requiring less than 10% of a day’s wage to purchase one DDD (Fig. 4). In the alimentary tract and metabolism and anti-infective for systemic use category, only two and three medicines, respectively, required more than one full day’s wage. In contrast, cardiovascular medicines were notably less affordable. Among cardiovascular medicines, Thailand exhibited relatively better affordability (median:2.8%, IQR:1.1%-5.0%). Additionally, the Philippines showed relatively poor affordability (median:17.1%, IQR:5.6%-34.0%). The Philippines also consistently demonstrated lower affordability across all four therapeutic areas compared with the other countries. Nervous system medicines showed substantially less affordability than those in remaining therapeutic areas.

Discussion

This analysis reveals substantial cross-country disparities in the price and affordability of common NCDs medicines across China and selected ASEAN countries. Despite many medicines were generally affordable, a notable proportion still required more than one full day’s wage per DDD, and most were priced above the IRPs. The marked heterogeneity in price and affordability for the same medicines across countries may reflect fragmented procurement mechanisms, differences in bargaining power, and structural market inefficiencies. In addition, differences in medicines market structure, such as patent status, the availability of multisource products, and the level of supplier competition, may also contribute to cross-country price variation. Such variation suggests that certain countries may face disadvantages in price negotiation, particularly in smaller or less coordinated markets. Pooled pharmaceutical procurement has been associated with several potential benefits, including enhancing bargaining power, reducing purchase prices, and increasing equity and access to medicines among participating countries [10]. However, it should be noted that our study did not consider medicine stock status. As a result, availability may have been overestimated, since we assumed that medicines approved in a given market are accessible to patients. In this context, medicines that are urgently needed, exhibit high price levels, shortage of medicines, or demonstrate poor affordability may represent priority candidates for coordinated regional purchasing initiatives. Taken together, our findings may provide preliminary empirical insights for identifying potential target medicines within the China-ASEAN regional medical procurement platform, which could help inform future exploration joint purchasing strategies.

Although our study identified substantial disparities in medicine prices and affordability based on data from China and three ASEAN countries, the lack of publicly available procurement price information in the remaining ASEAN countries limited further analysis. Given their relatively lower levels of economic development, pharmaceutical industry scale, and regulatory capacity, it is reasonable to assume that medicine affordability in these countries may be even more constrained [33, 34]. Previous studies have also reported relatively low utilization and availability of certain medicines in Malaysia, suggesting persistent challenges in medicines access in ASEAN, even in countries with relatively high levels of economic development and pharmaceutical capacity [35]. However, the absence of reliance data risks inaccurate evaluation and prevents a comprehensive understanding of the true price landscape and affordability across the region. This information gap constrains the design and implementation of regional joint procurement policies [13]. Price opacity not only weakens countries’ bargaining power during negotiations but also hinders cross-country cost comparison and equitable resource allocation [13, 36–38]. Therefore, improving price transparency, or establishing bilateral or multilateral mechanisms for price information sharing, is essential for building an efficient and sustainable regional procurement system.

Cross-border collaboration has been suggested as a potential approach to achieving larger economic scale, obtaining lower prices, improving affordability, enhancing regional health equity, and reducing pharmaceutical budget burdens, particularly for small markets [10, 39]. However, previous practices and studies have consistently shown that cross-border procurement faces many challenges in coordination [12, 40, 41]. Institutional and operational differences across countries complicate collaboration, weaken negotiation efficiency, and increase transaction costs [11, 13]. In the context of China and ASEAN, these challenges may be even more pronounced due to substantial cross-country gaps, further increasing the complexity of collaboration.

One commonly cited barrier to cross-border joint procurement is that not all products are authorized or marketed in every participating country [42]. Different from European Union, where medicines can be authorized through the European Medicines Agency (EMA) via a centralized procedure, ASEAN has not yet established an integrated regulatory system for pharmaceuticals [43]. Several initiatives and coordination mechanisms have been introduced to promote regulatory harmonization [43, 44], however, member states still maintain independent regulatory, procurement and distribution systems, resulting in significant gaps in capacity, procedures, standards [43, 44]. According to the WHO Global Benchmarking Tool (GBT), the Singapore Health Sciences Authority (HSA), Thailand Food and Drug Administration (TFDA) and Vietnam Drug Administration of Vietnam (DAV) have reached maturity level 4 (ML4), ML3, ML3 [45, 46], respectively, indicating relatively strong regulatory capacity. In contrast, countries such as Myanmar, Cambodia, and Lao PDR often rely on approvals from other agencies to make regulatory decisions [47]. These regulatory inconsistencies may hinder the timely inclusion and circulation of medicines within a regional procurement platform, thereby limiting its effectiveness in improving access. The GCC Group Purchasing Program among seven Persian Gulf States has achieved great successful practices, which requires that all purchase vaccines be licensed either through its relatively Central Drug Registration Program or by two member states considered within the region to have National Regulatory Authorities (NRAs) capable of evaluating biological products [48]. The Central Drug Registration Program was the outgrowth of the joint purchasing system, and GCC hopes it can replace separate national licenses for all drugs and vaccines, reducing licensing burden of participating suppliers [48]. Therefore, The China-ASEAN regional medical procurement platform may potentially draw lessons from the GCC experience. Before a centralized ASEAN regulatory agency is established, medicines that are not yet approval in all member countries could be allowed for procurement if they are registered in at least two participating countries within the ML3 or higher regulatory capability.

Another challenge is the complexity and fragmentation of national procurement and supply chain management, especially in LMIC [49]. Although most ASEAN member states have enacted procurement legislation and established centralized procurement mechanisms [50], their actual system vary considerably and often combine multiple models, for example, the Philippines employ both centralized and decentralized purchasing arrangements [51]. Such heterogeneity in regulatory frameworks and distribution channels further restricts medicines from reaching patients efficiently. As the supply chain serves as the critical link between manufactures and patients [52], weaknesses in its management can exacerbate medicine shortages and increase costs, highlighting the need for system-level improvements [53]. Moreover, some studies suggest that regional procurement could serve as a catalyst for strengthening national supply chains, including enhancing the logistics management information systems [10], which are essential for accurate forecasting data. A stable financial flow is also essential for a resilient supply chain. Therefore, clear and standardized operational processes that define the core steps of regional procurement are required. Previous studies have emphasized that successful regional procurement depends not only on well-designed procedures, but also on strong political commitment and a solid legal foundation [10, 13, 54]. For instance, the Europe Union adopted the Joint Procurement Agreement for cross-border health threats [55], while the East African Community established a legal framework to support pooled procurement of medicines [56]. There is no doubt that the launch of the China -ASEAN regional procurement platform is supported by a strong political foundation, however, publicly available information indicates the absence of a formal legal framework to govern its operation. Establishing such a framework at an early stage will be essential to ensure clarity, legitimacy, and long-term sustainability.

Besides, medicine quality control varies widely across countries, and the lack of mutual recognition of quality standards or inspection results increases the burden of regulatory oversight and raises concerns about the safety and effectiveness of procured products [57, 58]. In addition, policy protection for local pharmaceutical companies in ASEAN and religious considerations would be hidden barrier to the implementation of regional procurement. Regional Collaborative Strategy for ASEAN Drug Security and Self-Reliance (ADSSR) issued in 2016, emphasized reducing reliance on imports medicines and enhancing internal supply chain resilience [59]. Indonesia has implemented policies that prioritize domestic production and restrict the procurement of non-local medicines in health institutions [60, 61]. Similar policies can be found in Malaysia and the Philippines, including import tariffs or pricing policies favoring local manufactures [62, 63]. Moreover, halal certification is required in Muslim-majority countries such as Indonesia, Malaysia, adding further complexity to pharmaceutical procurement and regulation [64]. In ASEAN, approximately 250 million consumers require halal-certified medicines [43], meaning that both the ingredients and manufacturing processes must comply with Islamic law. However, halal certification authorities and national drug regulatory agencies operate separately and have different regulatory priorities [43]. As a result, halal-certified products must undergo oversight from both authorities and be subject to separate on-site inspections, increasing regulatory burden. Without effective coordination mechanisms, these dual regulatory requirements may significantly delay product approval, hinder cross-border circulation of medicines, and ultimately undermine the feasibility of regional joint procurement. Thus, these regulatory and political factors must be considered and addressed in the future implementation of the procurement platform.

The above discussion mainly focuses on the potential external challenges associated with cross-country pooled procurement. However, several inherent economic factors should also be considered. While joint procurement may enhance bargaining power and reduce medicine prices, cross-border supply chains may also generate additional distribution and operational costs, which could increase with geographical distance. Some experts have suggested that these additional costs may partially offset the economic benefits obtained through pooled procurement [14]. Fortunately, China and ASEAN countries have established trade agreement that aim to gradually eliminate tariffs and non-tariff barriers on most goods. Such arrangements may help reduce distribution costs and facilitate cross-border pharmaceutical trade. Nevertheless, some additional operational costs may still be incorporated into the final medicine prices. In cases where distribution costs become excessively high, pharmaceutical companies may choose to collaborate with local manufacturers or establish local facilities to reduce logistics expenses. Therefore, when selecting candidate medicines for joint procurement, economic feasibility should also be considered. If certain medicines can already meet local demand through domestic production or supply in specific countries, they may not necessarily be prioritized for pooled procurement, as such arrangements may not be economically efficient.

Taken together, this study suggests that while most medicines included in our analysis were generally affordable, a notable proportion remained unaffordable and were priced above the IRPs. Considerable variation in prices and affordability across countries also suggests substantial room for negotiation in joint procurement. We further examined the heterogeneity in regulatory capacity across China and ASEAN countriesand found that persistent challenges related to medicine approval, quality assurance, supply chain management, and halal certification. Overcoming these barriers will require enhanced regulatory harmonization, price transparency, well-designed operational frameworks, and the early establishment of a legal foundation. In general, extensive efforts are required to align national procedures with regional collaboration mechanisms, particularly in the initial phase. Only through such coordinated efforts can the China–ASEAN regional procurement platform achieve effectiveness, sustainability, and equitable health impact.

Beyond summarizing the key findings and challenges discussed above, this study may help inform discussion on the identification of priority medicines for potential regional pooled procurement. Medicines exhibiting relatively high MPR values and poor affordability across countries could represent potential candidates for joint procurement. In practice, the selection of priority medicines should consider the overall potential benefits alongside additional criteria, such as unmet clinical needs, regulatory access barriers, shortage of medicines and potential additional operational and distribution costs. Developing a structured priority-setting framework that incorporates these factors could help generate a shortlist of medicines for which pooled procurement may achieve greater price reductions and improved access.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, our study only included three ASEAN member countries. Although these countries represent major markets with relatively developed pharmaceutical industries and higher purchasing capacity, they do not fully capture the heterogeneity of the entire ASEAN region. Other countries such as Myanmar, Lao PDR and Cambodia generally face more severe medicine shortages and financial constraints. Therefore, future research should expand the scope to include these countries to map a more comprehensive regional picture. Second, the analysis included a limited number of medicines within four ATC classifications. Although these categories are of high public health importance, the restricted scope may not fully capture the diverse disease burden and medicine needs across different countries. In particular, some important NCD medicines, such as insulin and fixed-dose combination therapies, were not include, which may limit the comprehensiveness of the affordability assessment. Our study proposed an approach to identifying potential candidate medicines for regional procurement, however, this alone may not be enough. Consulting national essential medicine lists and country-specific reports may also valuable for selecting medicines that reflect local health priorities more accurately. Third, procurement prices used in this study were based on publicly available reference procurement prices in Thailand and the Philippines, rather than actual transaction prices. Reference price may differ from final purchase prices due to negotiations, discounts, or procurement conditions. Therefore, the reported price levels may not fully capture the exact prices paid by procurement agencies. However, these reference prices still provide an important benchmark for procurement decisions and are widely used in cross-countries comparisons analyses when transaction prices are not publicly available. Fourth, the IRPs were obtained from the MSH International Medical Products Price Guide (2015 edition), which may not fully reflect current market conditions and may affect cross-country comparability. In our analysis, the IRPs were adjusted to 2024 values to partially mitigate this limitation. Fifth, while we compared medicine prices and affordability across countries, it did not empirically examine the underlying drivers of these differences. In particular, the analysis did not incorporate detailed indicators of medicine market structure, such as patent status, the availability of multisource products, or the level of supplier competition, which may influence price variation across markets. A deeper understanding of these determinants is crucial for informing price negotiation strategies and guiding procurement decision making. Thus, future research should incorporate these factors to better explain cross-country price differences. Finally, we discussed several potential challenges the China-ASEAN regional medical procurement platform from forward looking insights based on existing literature and publicly available information. Since the platform was only launched in early 2025 and has not yet begun actual operation, many operational details have not been disclosed and no empirical studies are currently available. Therefore, some of our perspectives may later be refined or clarified as internal policy documents or official guidelines become accessible.

Conclusions

This study is the first to compare prices and affordability across China and selected ASEAN member countries within the context of recently launched China-ASEAN regional medical procurement platform. Our findings reveal substantial cross-country disparities and propose a practical approach for identifying priority medicines for joint procurement, which should be complemented by other approaches, like country-special reports. These findings highlight the potential of the platform to improve access, reduce financial burden, and advance regional health equity. However, achieving these goals will require early establishment of a legal framework, improved price transparency, regulatory harmonization, and strengthened supply chain systems. With strong political commitment, addressing these challenges is essential to ensure the platform’s effectiveness, sustainability, and public health impact.
