Comparing outcomes of patients with early active rheumatoid arthritis initially treated on an inpatient or outpatient basis: a posthoc analysis of the CORRA trial
Authors: Anna Mai, Dietmar Krause, Renate Klaassen-Mielke, Katharina Meiszl, Nina Timmesfeld, Xenofon Baraliakos, Jürgen Braun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTreatment strategies of patients with active rheumatoid arthritis (RA) vary within and between countries. While most patients in Germany are treated on an outpatient basis, some are hospitalised (inpatients). In the recently published randomised CORRA (CORRA, CORticoid bridging in Rheumatoid Arthritis) trial, we studied two 12 week glucocorticoid (GC) bridging strategies in patients with early RA comparing high or low GC doses with placebo, followed by an extension phase of 9 months. Here, in this posthoc analysis, we compared 12 week outcomes of patients according to their initial treatment as inpatients or outpatients.MethodsInpatients initially spent 2–5 days (short-term) or 14 days (long-term) in one tertiary rheumatology hospital. Outpatients were mostly treated in rheumatology practices. There was no randomisation regarding the initial treatment strategy. The main endpoint of this posthoc analysis was Clinical Disease Activity Index (CDAI) remission at weeks 4, 8 and 12.ResultsData of 280 outpatients and 95 inpatients could be analysed. Inpatients were more often male, had less cardiovascular comorbidity, but higher baseline CDAI scores and more symptoms of depression compared with outpatients. At weeks 8 and 12, CDAI remission was more frequently observed in inpatients (week 8: 24.7 vs 14.9%; week 12: 30.5 vs 17.3%). These results were confirmed in a multivariable model: OR=2.43 (1.06; 5.55); p=0.035, and OR=2.91 (1.37; 6.14); p=0.005, respectively.ConclusionIn early active RA, initial inpatient treatment was associated with higher CDAI remission rates at weeks 8 and 12. This may be due to the initially more intense hospital care.Keywords: Rheumatoid Arthritis, Glucocorticoids, Epidemiology

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is an inflammatory rheumatic disease that usually takes a chronic course. The main goal of therapy is to put patients into remission as early as possible and to maintain this state as long as possible. Treatment strategies vary within a country and from country to country. While most patients can be treated on an outpatient basis in Germany, some are admitted to hospital, especially in the first weeks of antirheumatic treatment.

More than 30 years ago, results from a small non-randomised cohort with inpatient or outpatient treatment for RA showed that inpatient treatment provided better outcomes after 12 weeks but that there was no difference after 2 years.1 Ten years later, this early advantage in clinical outcomes was also demonstrated in a small, randomised trial, but with a 2.5-fold increase in costs.2 A systematic literature review of studies comparing inpatient and outpatient care found 16 studies, including four randomised controlled trials. Only two studies indicated some advantage of inpatient treatment over outpatient treatment. General conclusions about the value of inpatient treatment for RA patients could not be derived from the studies evaluated. None of the studies were from the German healthcare system. It was suggested that future studies should investigate this important question, considering the special circumstances of the German healthcare system.3

This posthoc analysis used data from the CORRA trial (CORticoid bridging in Rheumatoid Arthritis)4 to compare initial inpatient treatment with outpatient treatment in terms of their baseline findings and their outcomes, especially regarding Clinical Disease Activity Index (CDAI) remission as relevant outcome in everyday practice.

Methods

The CORRA trial was designed to compare a high-dose to a low-dose glucocorticoid (GC) bridging strategy in patients with active early RA, with a blinded intervention phase of 12 weeks followed by a follow-up phase of 40 weeks.4

Eligible participants were adult patients diagnosed with early active RA who met the 2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria for RA5 and had a symptom duration of less than 3 years. Disease activity had to be high or moderate according to the Disease Activity Score in 28 joints (DAS28)6 of >4 with at least three swollen joints.7

CORRA was conducted in 16 rheumatology private practices and one tertiary rheumatology hospital in North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany. Inpatients were hospitalised for short term (2–5 days) or long term (14 days).8 Decisions on the length of the hospital stay were based on disease severity at the time of admission.

After hospitalisation, inpatients were treated in the outpatient department of the tertiary rheumatology centre (Rheumazentrum Ruhrgebiet), while those initially treated as outpatients were mostly cared for in rheumatology practices.

Patients were randomly assigned to GC bridging with 60 or 10 mg prednisolone once daily (both tapered down to 0 mg within 12 weeks) or to placebo. All patients started methotrexate (MTX) treatment at a usual dosage of 15 mg/week. Intra-articular GC injections were allowed up to 40 mg triamcinolone acetonide every 4 weeks.4

At each visit (weeks 4, 8, 12, 26 and 52), patients were asked about adverse events and laboratory tests were performed. Questions about depressive symptoms were asked using the Patient Health Questionnaire-2.9 Adverse events were assigned to the corresponding MedDRA-coded system organ class. The German MedDRA V.17.1–21.0 was used.10

Objective and methods of this posthoc analysis

This posthoc analysis was set up to determine whether there were heterogeneities in the patient cohort with regard to the initial treatment modalities (outpatient vs inpatient treatment). In detail, the objective was to answer the following questions.

Are there differences in the baseline characteristics between outpatients and inpatients regarding demographic factors, comorbidities or disease activity?

Are there differences in the baseline characteristics between outpatients and inpatients regarding demographic factors, comorbidities or disease activity?

Are there differences in outcomes in terms of remission rates during and at the end of the randomised intervention phase (weeks 4, 8 and 12)?

Are there differences in outcomes in terms of remission rates during and at the end of the randomised intervention phase (weeks 4, 8 and 12)?

To answer the second question of this posthoc analysis, the main endpoint was CDAI remission (CDAI ≤2.8).11 Major secondary endpoints were disease activity assessed by DAS28 (with values <2.6 labelled as ‘minimal disease activity’)6 and Boolean remission (as defined by the American College of Rheumatology/European League Against Rheumatism Provisional Definition of Remission in Rheumatoid Arthritis for Clinical Trials)12 at weeks 4, 8 and 12.

The main endpoint was analysed using a generalised linear mixed effects model on the modified intention-to-treat population of the CORRA trial. Adjustments were made for GC bridging (CORRA study medication), triamcinolone injections, CDAI at baseline, sex, smoking status (current vs other), number of comorbidities (two or more vs less) and anti-CCP/rheumatoid factor (at least one positive vs both negative). To obtain information on differences between initial treatment modalities at follow-up time points, a corresponding interaction term was included. In addition, a two-way interaction between the GC bridging and the follow-up week was added in the model to exclude further confounding effects due to the GC bridging. To account for repeated measures per patient, a random patient effect was included in the model. The secondary outcomes DAS28 minimal disease activity, Boolean remission and the two laboratory parameters erythrocyte sedimentation rate and C-reactive protein were analysed in a comparable model. Depending on the type of outcome, a logistic or linear approach was used.

Risk factors at baseline, medication, drug accountability (defined by an intake of at least 80% of the study medication) and the subgroups with short-term as well as long-term inpatient treatment were analysed descriptively. Comorbidities were reported by the treating rheumatologist. Cardiovascular comorbidity comprised arterial hypertension, coronary heart disease and/or stroke/transient ischemic attack/impaired cerebral blood flow. Numbers and percentages of patients with adverse events over the CORRA study intervention period of 12 weeks are presented by type of treatment groups. Increase of intraocular pressure and haemoglobin A1c elevation were regarded as adverse events of special interest. P values for differences between the treatment groups were calculated using appropriate tests (t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for qualitative variables, χ2 test for quantitative variables). For statistical analyses, we used SAS software, V.9.4.

Results

Initially, 280 out of 375 patients (75%) were treated on an outpatient basis (outpatient group), while 95 patients (25%) were admitted to hospital (inpatient group), 77 of them (81%) for short-term (2–5 days) treatment, 18 (19%) for long-term (14 days) treatment (see figure 1 for complete trial flow).

Trial profile for post hoc analyses based on the CORRA study data. CORRA, CORticoid bridging in Rheumatoid Arthritis.

There were relatively more women in the outpatient group compared with the inpatient group (62.9% vs 44.2%). Outpatients had more cardiovascular comorbidity (41.9% vs 28.4%), but they less often reported depressive symptoms (30.1% vs 48.8). Other comorbidities were rare (up to 10%) and there were no significant differences between the groups. However, outpatients had a smaller number of swollen (mean 12.4 vs 14.4) and tender (mean 13.1 vs 17.0) joints and a lower baseline CDAI (mean 37.7 vs 44.2). At baseline, the percentage of patients with erosive disease on conventional radiographs was 60.4% in the outpatient—compared with 66.7% in the inpatient group (table 1).

After 8 weeks, 24.7% of the patients in the inpatient group compared with 14.9% in the outpatient group had reached CDAI remission (adjusted OR=2.43 (1.06; 5.55); p=0.035).

At week 12, the proportion of CDAI remissions was 30.5% versus 17.3% (adjusted OR=2.91 (1.37; 6.14); p=0.005) and the proportion of DAS28 minimal disease activity was 48.8% versus 34.3% (adjusted OR=2.20 (1.12; 4.30); p=0.022), accordingly (table 3).

The results of the unadjusted analysis were comparable (at week 8 CDAI remission (OR=1.94 (0.90; 4.17); p=0.089) and at week 12 CDAI remission (OR=2.32 (1.14; 4.71); p=0.02) as well as DAS28 minimal disease activity (OR=2.15 (1.12; 4.15); p=0.022)).

A subgroup analysis of the inpatient group (short-term stay vs long-term stay) revealed that both subgroups were very similar in terms of MTX and prednisolone dosage (table 2).

Discussion

The CORRA trial4 had shown that in patients with early active RA, initiation of treatment with MTX supported by high-dose GC bridging resulted in a more rapid decline in disease activity, but not in clinical or radiographic benefit at 1 year as compared with low-dose GC or placebo. Shortly after diagnosis, 280 CORRA patients (75%) were treated as outpatients, while 95 patients (25%) were admitted to hospital. In Germany, people who have a higher level of disease activity and/or have problems coping with their illness (eg, higher levels of depression) can receive more comprehensive inpatient treatment.

Looking at the data in more detail, we discovered heterogeneities in the patient groups based on the initial treatment modality, particularly between inpatients versus outpatients. As expected, both groups had different baseline characteristics. There were significantly more women in the outpatient (63%) than in the inpatient group (44%). This is notable because in larger RA cohorts, women make up almost 80% of the cohort and they tend to have worse scores in the American College of Rheumatology Core Data Set.13 14 Furthermore, approximately 70% of RA hospitalisations are caused by women,15 and this was different in our study. One possible explanation could relate to the working conditions of men who are more likely to be on sick leave in the year before diagnosis.16 17 This may lead to more hospitalisations of men hoping for faster improvement.

In addition, disease activity measures like swollen and tender joints or CDAI at baseline showed significantly higher scores and depressive symptoms were reported more frequently in inpatients (49% vs 30%) while comorbidities, especially cardiovascular disease, were more common in outpatients (42% vs 28 %). Generally, comorbidities were more frequent in our study than in a recently published large cohort recruited in different countries worldwide.18 However, our data are comparable to a recent German study in RA,19 which reported that the most common comorbidities were hypertension (62.5%), osteoarthritis (44.0%), depressive symptoms (31.8%) and osteoporosis (25.9%).

Importantly, throughout the study period, the mean doses of prednisolone (about 8 mg/day) and MTX (about 14 mg per week) were similar in the groups. There were more GC injections in outpatients compared with inpatients (15.2% vs 6.9%).

Serious adverse events were reported more frequently in the inpatient group, but a strikingly different pattern was not evident. The only apparent difference was seen in the category ‘General diseases and complaints at the place of administration’ (table 5) mainly including patients with aggravated disease activity. More in-depth comparisons of serious adverse events, however, were not feasible due to the relatively small number of cases.

However, even after adjusting for the differences in baseline characteristics as well as for dosage of the GC bridging, this posthoc analysis shows that an initial inpatient treatment of patients with early RA was associated with higher CDAI remission rates (weeks 8 and 12) as well as DAS28 minimal disease activity rate (week 12) in the short term compared with outpatient treatment. This is an important result because comparative data on these two strategies are scarce. However, what may be the reason for the observed differences?

One major difference between inpatient and outpatient treatment modalities is a completely different general setting in the hospital: patients are having daily visits, rapid diagnostic procedures, repeated short-term outcome monitoring, including an appropriate reaction to the efficacy and success of therapy and, clearly not least, every day physiotherapeutic interventions and often also patient education sessions. This is especially true for patients who stay in the hospital for 10–14 days—which was the case for almost 20% of inpatients. All of this is likely to not only causing a feeling of safety and confidence for the patients but it also has an impact on psychological well-being in general. This is likely to especially matter in somewhat depressed patients who, in the hospital, may have even seen a psychologist during their stay. This intensity is almost not possible to organise in an outpatient setting. In the outpatient setting, patients were informed about the disease, the treatment, the course of the disease, etc, but there was no structured education and no psychological support as with inpatients. They also received physiotherapy support depending on availability, usually less frequently than the inpatients. Taken together, the nature of an initial inpatient treatment is more complex. We did not perform a socioeconomic analysis but it is likely that the inpatient approach is more costly.

Our study has several weaknesses. There was no randomisation according to initial treatment modality. As the patients’ and referring rheumatologists’ decision-making processes for inpatient treatment are not known, a certain degree of selection bias could be possible here. We have adjusted the data for potential confounding factors. However, it is likely that not all relevant confounders were taken into account. The observation period is relatively short, and given the variable course of patients with RA, it is possible that the positive effects observed in this study may not last long. Therefore, our study results only apply to the first 3 months of early RA treatment, and we cannot make statements about long-term efficacy. Finally, inpatient data refer to only one hospital, while outpatient data refer to 16 practices resulting in a more heterogeneous outpatient treatment. However, despite all limitations, the observed differences in CDAI remission rates between the inpatient and outpatient setting are quite clear.

The generalisability of the study results depends on the availability of treatment options in outpatient and inpatient settings in different countries. If the intensity of educational and psychological support needed by patients with more severe disease activity is not available in the outpatient setting, inpatient treatment may be an option to improve the course of the disease.

Conclusion

Patients with early active RA initially treated on an inpatient basis had higher CDAI remission rates at weeks 8 and 12 compared with outpatients.
