Second-order variational analysis of PV–battery energy management using jacobi equations
Authors: M M Mundu, Mariam Basajja, Emmanuel Kweyu, J I Ssempewo, S N Nnamchi, Daniel Ejim Uti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing penetration of photovoltaic (PV) generation requires energy management strategies for PV–battery systems that are not only optimal but also stable and robust under variable solar generation and load demand. Classical optimal control approaches based on first-order optimality conditions ensure stationarity of solutions but provide limited observation into local stability and sensitivity to operational perturbations. This paper introduces a second-order variational framework based on Jacobi equations to analyze and design optimal PV–battery energy management trajectories. The proposed methodology quantifies state-of-charge (SOC) trajectory stability, explicitly identifies conjugate points that signal loss of local optimality, and characterizes time-dependent sensitivity to PV and load fluctuations through Jacobi fields. Numerical experiments on a representative 24-hour PV–battery system demonstrate the practical effectiveness of the approach, revealing critical periods of vulnerability and providing quantitative guidance for battery sizing, control-weight selection, and predictive operational planning. Results show that incorporating second-order optimality conditions enables rigorous stability verification and enhanced robustness compared with classical first-order methods. By extending conventional optimal control frameworks with stability-aware analysis, this work provides a mathematically grounded and practically relevant foundation for resilient energy management in renewable microgrids and residential PV–battery systems.Keywords: Photovoltaic–battery systems, Energy management, Second-order variational analysis, Jacobi equations, Stability and sensitivity, Conjugate points, Renewable energy microgridsSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The increasing penetration of photovoltaic generation combined with battery energy storage systems has transformed modern power systems by enabling higher renewable integration, enhanced flexibility, and improved reliability1–4. Central to the effective operation of PV–battery systems is the energy management problem, which seeks optimal charging, discharging, and power exchange strategies under time-varying solar generation and load demand5–8. Existing energy management approaches predominantly rely on first-order optimality conditions derived from optimal control or constrained optimization frameworks. While these methods are effective in identifying nominal optimal trajectories, they provide limited observations into the robustness and stability of such solutions under inevitable system perturbations. The increasing penetration of photovoltaic generation has intensified the need for efficient energy storage and advanced energy management strategies in distributed power systems9–11.

In practical operating environments, PV–battery systems are continuously subjected to uncertainties arising from solar irradiance fluctuations, load variability, and modeling inaccuracies12,13. Under such conditions, energy trajectories that satisfy first-order optimality conditions may exhibit significant sensitivity to small disturbances, potentially leading to performance degradation, constraint violations, or loss of optimality. First-order analysis, by construction, characterizes stationarity but does not capture the curvature of the energy functional governing system behavior. As a result, it is insufficient for assessing whether an optimal energy management strategy is locally minimizing, stable, or robust to perturbations.

The limitations of first-order analysis in energy management systems, particularly in assessing local optimality, stability, and robustness, necessitate the application of second-order variational analysis, where Jacobi equations play a fundamental role14. While Pontryagin’s Minimum Principle (PMP) is widely utilized for deriving first-order necessary conditions in optimal control problems15–20,21, it primarily characterizes stationarity and does not inherently capture the curvature of the energy functional or provide explicit verification of second-order sufficiency or stability properties (Sánchez et al., 2020,14.

Second-order variational analysis addresses these shortcomings by characterizing the behavior of small deviations around an optimal trajectory, with Jacobi equations naturally emerging as the governing equations for the second variation of the energy functional (Sánchez et al., 2020;14,22. The identification of conjugate points within the Jacobi framework is a critical indicator of the loss of local optimality, offering a precise criterion for stability and sensitivity analysis (Sánchez et al., 2020,22. Despite their theoretical significance in variational theory, Jacobi equations have not been extensively applied to the analysis of PV-battery energy management systems, even though robustness and stability are increasingly crucial for such systems.

Current research in PV-battery energy management predominantly focuses on various optimization and control strategies. Many studies employ PMP or dynamic programming (DP) for optimal energy management in PV-battery systems23–26,18,27–32. For instance, compared DP-based strategies with rule-based approaches like maximizing self-consumption (MSC) and time-of-use (TOU) for grid-connected PV-battery systems in office buildings, demonstrating the importance of proper operation strategies for desirable performance. Similarly, optimal energy management is explored for residential PV-integrated systems to maximize economic profit32,33, and for standalone PV-battery systems to maintain battery health and system efficiency29,34,30.

The energy management controller determines the battery charging and discharging strategy to maintain power balance while minimizing the objective functional defined in Equation (4). The integration of PV-battery systems faces challenges such as the intermittent nature of renewable energy sources and the nonlinear characteristics of batteries34. Data-driven methods, such as Gaussian process regression (GPR), are being explored for state-of-charge (SOC) estimation in lithium-ion battery packs to overcome the difficulties of explicit modeling due to cell inconsistencies35. Adaptive robust control frameworks are also proposed for hybrid PV-battery systems to manage their complex and nonlinear characteristics, multicomponent structures, and environmental disturbances. Furthermore, the optimization of battery capacity and energy management strategies is critical, especially considering battery degradation over time8,36. Wu et al. specifically investigated optimal battery capacity for grid-connected PV-battery systems, considering battery degradation to minimize life cycle cost8.

While these approaches address various aspects of energy management, the explicit incorporation of second-order variational analysis using Jacobi equations remains an area with significant potential. This would allow for a deeper understanding of the stability regions around optimal trajectories and the identification of points where the optimal solution might become unstable or highly sensitive to small perturbations. For instance, the stability of PV and battery energy storage systems integrated into weak grids has been investigated using small-signal models, but this does not explicitly leverage Jacobi equations for a broader stability analysis37. The concept of Jacobi stability for systems of second-order ordinary differential equations has been explored through the Kosambi–Cartan–Chern (KCC) theory, which can systematically analyze such stability.

The relevance of second-order conditions extends beyond just theoretical completeness; it offers practical benefits in designing more resilient and efficient energy management systems. By identifying conjugate points, engineers could anticipate potential instabilities and design controllers that actively avoid these critical regions, thereby enhancing the operational lifespan and reliability of the battery and PV components. This is especially pertinent given the increasing penetration of renewable energy sources and the complexity of hybrid energy systems that need to maintain stability and efficiency under variable conditions38. The development of advanced energy management systems for microgrids, including PV, wind, and battery integration, further underscores the need for robust control strategies that can handle stochastic inputs and maintain system stability20,38.

Ultimately, the integration of second-order variational analysis and Jacobi equations into the design and evaluation of PV-battery energy management systems represents an important step toward achieving truly optimal, stable, and robust solutions, moving beyond the limitations of purely first-order approaches. This would entail a shift from merely finding stationary points to understanding of the energy functional and ensuring that the identified optimal trajectories are indeed local minima that are resilient to the inherent uncertainties and disturbances in real-world energy systems.

Thus, this study addresses this gap by developing a second-order variational framework for PV–battery energy management based on Jacobi equations. The main contributions of this work are threefold. First, a continuous-time variational formulation of the PV–battery energy management problem is established, leading to the derivation of the Euler–Lagrange equations that characterize optimal energy trajectories. Second, the second variation of the energy functional is rigorously derived, and the associated Jacobi equations are formulated to analyze the stability and sensitivity of optimal solutions under perturbations in solar generation and load demand. Third, the theoretical findings are validated through a numerical case study, demonstrating how Jacobi-based stability analysis provides deeper insight into the robustness of energy management strategies beyond conventional first-order approaches. By explicitly integrating second-order variational theory into PV–battery energy management, this work advances the analytical foundations of renewable energy optimization and offers a principled framework for designing robust, stability-aware energy management strategies in high-renewable power systems.

Implications for robust energy management

Building on the identification of conjugate points in section "conjugate points and loss of optimality" and the sensitivity analysis to PV and load perturbations in Section "Sensitivity to solar and load perturbations", this section translates the variational results into practical analysis for robust and reliable energy management in PV–battery systems.

First, ensuring that the operation horizon lies before the first conjugate point guarantees locally stable PV–battery dispatch under small perturbations. The emergence of a conjugate point marks the horizon beyond which the trajectory may cease to be a strict local minimum. Consequently, by designing the system such that the first conjugate point lies beyond the operational horizon , operators can guarantee stability under small perturbations. This criterion directly informs the selection of the Lagrangian weighting parameters and the battery capacity , enabling energy dispatch strategies that remain locally optimal throughout operation.

Secondly, minimizing sensitivity to renewable and load fluctuations is essential for resilient performance. The inhomogeneous Jacobi equation provides a quantitative measure of SOC sensitivity to solar irradiance and demand variability. Conversely, intervals of elevated sensitivity can be anticipated in advance, allowing operators to maintain reserve battery capacity, adjust charging and discharging schedules, or apply real-time corrective actions to mitigate instability.

Finally, these observations support the systematic design of robust control strategies. Sensitivity information guides the implementation of feedback control laws that correct SOC deviations, while adaptive optimization enables trajectory reconfiguration when disturbances exceed expected bounds. Moreover, tuning the Lagrangian weights allows a controlled trade-off between energy efficiency and robustness, ensuring resilient operation under uncertainty.

From an operational and planning perspective, conjugate points provide a mathematically grounded criterion for defining stable operation horizons, while sensitivity analysis highlights periods of heightened risk requiring proactive intervention. By explicitly linking SOC sensitivity to battery capacity , the framework also supports informed system sizing decisions, ensuring adequate storage capacity to maintain robust performance under anticipated PV and load variability.

These results demonstrate that second-order variational analysis can support several practical energy-management decisions, including determination of safe dispatch horizons, identification of time intervals with elevated SOC sensitivity, and selection of weighting parameters that balance grid interaction against battery degradation risk. As a result, the proposed framework provides a mathematically grounded tool for designing stability-aware control strategies in residential and microgrid PV–battery systems.

Discussion and practical implications

The numerical and analytical investigations presented in this study establish a rigorous framework for evaluating stability, sensitivity, and robustness in PV–battery energy management systems. By combining second-order variational analysis with Jacobi equations, the proposed approach provides insights that extend beyond classical optimal control methods.

Conventional energy management approaches including rule-based control, first-order optimal control, and model predictive control primarily focus on minimizing operational cost functions under predefined constraints. These methods rely on first-order optimality conditions, such as Euler–Lagrange equations or Pontryagin’s Maximum Principle, to identify candidate optimal trajectories. While effective for determining stationary solutions, they do not explicitly assess whether these trajectories remain locally optimal under perturbations.

The Jacobi-based framework introduced in this study addresses this limitation by incorporating second-order optimality conditions, which enable the detection of conjugate points along the optimal trajectory. These points indicate potential loss of local minimality and provide a quantitative measure of solution robustness under variations in PV generation and load demand. Consequently, disturbances that may destabilize classical solutions can be anticipated, enabling more resilient energy management strategies.

The magnitude of the Jacobi field , quantifies the deviation of the battery state-of-charge from its optimal trajectory under perturbations, providing a direct indicator of system stability. Limitations of the study

While the proposed second-order variational framework provides a rigorous analytical tool for stability assessment of PV–battery energy management trajectories, several simplifying assumptions should be noted. First, the analysis is based on a quadratic objective functional, which ensures analytical tractability but may not capture all nonlinear economic dispatch objectives encountered in practice. Second, battery dynamics are represented using a single-state SOC model without explicit degradation or efficiency asymmetry effects. Third, solar generation and load demand are treated as deterministic inputs, whereas real operating environments involve stochastic variability. Finally, the framework is formulated in continuous time, and although a discrete implementation is provided, large-scale multi-storage systems may require additional numerical adaptations. Future work will extend the proposed approach to stochastic formulations, nonlinear degradation-aware battery models, and multi-energy storage coordination problems.

Conclusions

This paper introduced a second-order variational framework for PV–battery energy management based on Jacobi equations, enabling explicit assessment of stability, sensitivity, and enables verification of robustness of optimal operation. In contrast to classical first-order optimal control methods, which ensure stationarity but not local minimality, the proposed approach identifies conjugate points that define quantitative stability limits over the operational horizon.

Jacobi field analysis captures the time-varying sensitivity of battery state-of-charge to small PV generation and load perturbations, revealing critical periods of vulnerability. These sensitivity patterns provide direct operational value by informing battery sizing, control-weight tuning, and predictive control actions. Moreover, conjugate points establish a mathematically grounded horizon of trajectory validity, enabling scheduled re-optimization before loss of local optimality. Numerical experiments on a representative 24-hour PV–battery system validate the framework and demonstrate its computational feasibility, with the tridiagonal Jacobi system efficiently solved using the Thomas algorithm. Compared to classical first-order approaches, the proposed method extends optimal control by integrating robustness verification with actionable design and operational insights.

The analysis is limited by the assumption of linearized battery dynamics, quadratic cost functions, and predominantly deterministic PV and load profiles. Extension to large-scale or networked systems may require sparse numerical techniques or model-order reduction.

Beyond theoretical analysis, the proposed Jacobi-equation framework can be integrated into predictive energy-management systems to evaluate dispatch robustness under uncertainty. By identifying sensitivity growth regions and conjugate-point horizons, system operators can proactively adjust battery scheduling strategies, improve reserve allocation, and ensure reliable operation in high-renewable environments.

Future research will address nonlinear battery and PV models incorporating efficiency variation, degradation, and thermal effects through generalized Jacobi formulations. Stochastic and robust optimization extensions will account for uncertainty in renewable generation and demand, while multi-objective formulations will integrate economic, environmental, and peak-shaving objectives. Real-time evaluation of Jacobi fields for predictive control and stability monitoring, as well as applications to PV–battery microgrids, represent key directions for advancing resilient renewable energy systems.
