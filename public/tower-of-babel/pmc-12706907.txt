Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio for predicting postoperative mortality after hip fracture surgery: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Cheng Liu, Jingjing Zheng, Yang Bai, Lin Gan, Yanglin Gu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe current study aimed to systematically and quantitatively evaluate the prognostic value of the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) in predicting mortality among surgical patients with hip fractures through a systematic review and meta-analysis.DesignSystematic review and meta-analysis.MethodsFour databases—The Cochrane Library, PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science—were comprehensively retrieved to identify studies published from database inception to May 2025 that investigated NLR as a predictor of postoperative survival in hip fracture individuals. Two independent researchers performed study selection, data extraction, and quality assessment, with consistency verified via cross-checking. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata 15.0 for meta-regression and heterogeneity testing, while prognostic performance metrics (sensitivity, area under the curve [AUC], specificity) were synthesized via Meta-Disc 1.4.ResultsOur meta-analysis incorporated 12 studies, involving 10,015 individuals who underwent surgery for a hip fracture. With cut-off values ranging from 3.2 to 8.4, preoperative NLR demonstrated high prognostic efficacy for mortality prediction, yielding a combined sensitivity of 0.69 (95% CI 0.55–0.83), specificity of 0.84 (0.74–0.93), diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) of 12 (6–26), and an area under the summary receiver operating characteristic curve (sAUC) of 0.84 (0.81–0.87). Subgroup analysis of preoperative NLR thresholds revealed that higher cut-off values (NLR > 5) significantly improved prognostic performance. NLR exhibited superior sensitivity but slightly lower specificity in patients from developing countries, with particular prognostic utility for long-term mortality (≥ 1 year). Postoperative NLR showed moderate prognostic efficacy, with a combined sensitivity of 0.64 (0.57–0.70), specificity of 0.58 (0.55–0.61), DOR of 2 (2–3), and sAUC of 0.62 (0.58–0.66).ConclusionThis meta-analysis confirms that the preoperative NLR is a promising albeit preliminary predictor of postoperative mortality in hip fractures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06495-4.Keywords: Neutrophils, Lymphocytes, NLR, Hip fracture, Mortality

Introduction

Globally, hip fractures in older adults represent a major public health challenge [1, 2]. Hip fractures significantly diminish quality of life. Furthermore, the considerable medical expenses and long-term care requirements associated with hip fractures impose an ever-increasing socioeconomic burden on national healthcare systems [3]. Epidemiological models predict that, due to accelerated population aging, the global number of individuals with disabilities resulting from hip fractures could surpass 7.3 million by 2050. This alarming trend underscores the urgent need for systematic response strategies [4–6]. Clinical data reveal considerable variability in the excess mortality risk faced by such individuals within the first postoperative year, with reported mortality rates ranging from 8.4 to 36% [7, 8]. There are diverse surgical approaches for individuals with hip fractures, offering multiple options based on disease severity and risk [9, 10]. Therefore, identifying high-risk older hip fracture patients is important for selecting clinical procedures. Hip fractures lead to mortality and significant impairment in activities of daily living. Precise risk prognostication is crucial for optimizing clinical decision-making [11]. Furthermore, accurate identification of high-risk individuals may inform optimal surgical timing, intensive care management during treatment, and postoperative guidance [12, 13].

The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), defined as the ratio of absolute neutrophil counts to absolute lymphocyte counts in peripheral blood, can serve as a biomarker for identifying high-risk patients postoperatively [14]. The rationale for NLR's predictive capability in critically ill individuals lies in its dynamic integration and quantification of the imbalance between pro-inflammatory responses (neutrophilia) and adaptive immune suppression/exhaustion (lymphopenia) under severe stress [15]. This imbalance reflects the overactivation of the pro-inflammatory response and the relative suppression of anti-inflammatory and immune repair mechanisms. It promotes the risk of tissue injury and potentially compromises the recovery of organ function [16]. Consequently, an elevated NLR is an effective and accessible biomarker for gauging the severity of immune dysregulation, predicting disease progression, and evaluating mortality risk in critically ill individuals. Data from the past decade indicate that preoperative NLR independently predicts mortality across diverse critically ill groups, such as those admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) [17], older individuals undergoing emergency abdominal surgery [18], patients recovering from cardiac and major vascular procedures [19], and individuals post-surgery for various cancers. Moreover, NLR has been associated with survival rates in several common geriatric conditions, encompassing hypertension, coronary artery disease (CAD), ischemic stroke, chronic kidney disease (CKD), chronic heart failure (CHF), diabetes mellitus (DM), and diverse malignancies [20].

Within the orthopedic domain, existing investigations have substantiated the significant predictive value of the NLR for postoperative complications such as pneumonia and delirium in subjects with hip fractures [21]. Some studies have further suggested a potential association of a higher NLR with increased mortality risk after hip fracture surgery [22]. Nevertheless, further research is required to elucidate the prognostic utility of NLR for predicting mortality in hip fractures postoperatively.

Through a systematic literature review and meta-analysis, we evaluated the accuracy of NLR in predicting postoperative mortality in hip fracture patients. This analysis aims to inform clinical assessments of postoperative outcomes in these individuals.

Materials and methods

Registration

The present study was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO), with the ID CRD420251041499. The meta-analysis adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (PRISMA-DTA 2018) statement.

Search strategy

A comprehensive retrieval of four databases—Web of Science, Cochrane, EMBASE, and PubMed—was conducted up until June 2025 to identify original studies investigating the prognostic impact of NLR in individuals undergoing hip fracture surgery. A mix of MeSH and unstructured keywords was utilized, including 'lymphocytes', 'neutrophils', 'hip fracture', 'total hip replacement', 'hemiarthroplasty', and 'mortality'. A detailed strategy is provided in Table S1.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria comprised: (i) Study subjects: Individuals with unilateral proximal femur fractures due to trauma requiring surgery. Fracture types included femoral neck, intertrochanteric, and subtrochanteric fractures (defined as occurring within 5 cm of the lesser trochanter). Surgical procedures encompassed internal fixation (e.g., intramedullary nails, dynamic hip screws), hemiarthroplasty, or total hip arthroplasty. All fractures were confirmed radiographically, and periprosthetic, pathological (e.g., tumor), acetabular, and pelvic fractures were excluded. Individuals with severe polytrauma (e.g., life-threatening conditions such as head injury or thoracic or abdominal organ damage) were also excluded. (ii) Outcome definition: Clearly defined patient mortality status (e.g., in-hospital mortality, 30-day postoperative mortality, 1-year postoperative mortality). (iii) NLR measurement: Included studies were required to specify the collection time point of venous blood samples (preoperative or postoperative). Absolute counts of peripheral blood neutrophils and lymphocytes were obtained through laboratory testing to calculate the NLR. NLR is defined as the ratio of the absolute neutrophil count (× 109/L) to the absolute lymphocyte count (× 109/L) in peripheral blood. (iv) Data extractability: Study data must be directly extractable or calculable to provide the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUROC), negative predictive value (NPV), positive predictive value (PPV), negative likelihood ratio (NLR), positive likelihood ratio (PLR), false positives (FP), true positives (TP), false negatives (FN), and true negatives (TN) for predicting mortality. (v) Study design: Observational studies, including cohort, case–control, and cross-sectional studies.

Exclusion criteria included: (i) Reviews, meta-analyses, editorials, case reports or series, letters, conference abstracts, commentaries, and study protocols. (ii) Duplicate publications.

Literature screening and information collection

Literature screening

Two researchers screened the retrieved literature records independently per pre-defined eligibility criteria. The initial search outcomes were imported using EndNote 21 as the literature organization tool. Both researchers identified duplicate records separately, screened the titles and abstracts of retrieved articles, and further assessed eligible studies by downloading and reviewing full texts. Any discrepancies were addressed by consensus-based discussion. When no agreement could be achieved, a third researcher made an arbitration decision.

Information collection

Following the established inclusion criteria, information collection was conducted by two researchers independently utilizing a standardized form. The collected information primarily encompassed two major aspects: (i) Study characteristics and population information: This comprised the publication year, first author, country/region of the study, study design type, total sample size, number of survivors, number of deaths, mean age of the study population, sex distribution, the defined threshold (cut-off) value for NLR prediction, precise definition of mortality outcomes (e.g., in-hospital mortality, 30-day postoperative mortality, or 1-year postoperative mortality), and the follow-up timeframe for assessing these mortality outcomes. (ii) Prognostic performance data of NLR for predicting mortality: The core objective was to obtain data for 2 × 2 contingency tables, including TP, FP, FN, and TN. This data was preferentially acquired directly from 2 × 2 contingency tables reported in the original articles. If not directly provided, it was indirectly calculated and reconstructed based on reported sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, or points on the ROC curve, in conjunction with the number of deaths/survivors and the total sample size. Furthermore, if the original articles reported prognostic performance metrics derived from 2 × 2 tables (e.g., sensitivity, specificity, diagnostic odds ratio [DOR]), PLR, NLR, and their respective 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), this information was also collected. If insufficient information was present in the full text, the original study authors were reached out to by email. Following information collection by both researchers, their results were compared. Any discrepancies encountered during the collection process or result comparison were initially addressed through discussion. If disagreements persisted, they were submitted to a third researcher (or the project lead) for arbitration.

Literature quality evaluation

Two evaluators (Cheng Liu and Jingjing Zheng) assessed the risk of bias (ROB) of the eligible studies independently utilizing the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies 2 (QUADAS-2) tool [23], and the quality assessment process was completed employing RevMan 5.4 software. The QUADAS-2 tool systematically analyzed the ROB and clinical applicability across four core domains: patient selection, study flow and timing, reference standard, and index test.

Statistical analyses

Data synthesis and statistical analyses were performed via StataSE 15.0 software (Stata Corp LP, Texas, USA) and Meta-Disc 1.4 software. Preoperative and postoperative NLR were subjected to separate meta-analyses. In Meta-Disc 1.4, Spearman correlation analysis was carried out to evaluate threshold effects, where a strong positive correlation indicated the potential presence of such an effect. When no threshold effect was identified, data were pooled for further examination. To synthesize effect sizes such as sensitivity (SSEN), negative likelihood ratio (SNLR), specificity (SSPE), positive likelihood ratio (SPLR), and DOR, a bivariate random-effects model was applied, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) calculated for each. The area under the curve (AUC) was computed to assess prognostic accuracy, which was classified as high (AUC ≥ 0.9), moderate (0.7 ≤ AUC < 0.9), or low (AUC < 0.7). Forest plots and summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curves were also generated. Deeks' funnel plot asymmetry test was utilized to examine possible publication bias. Sensitivity assessments incorporated influence analysis and outlier identification to ascertain the robustness of the findings.

Quality assessment results

The literature was imported into Review Manager V.5.3, and QUADAS-2 was applied to appraise the quality of the 12 articles. The assessment results revealed concerns about the ROB in patient selection across seven studies, with one study deemed to be at high risk and six studies having an unclear risk. Regarding the index test, two studies had an unclear ROB, primarily due to the absence of a predefined prognostic threshold. Threshold variability is a common source of heterogeneity in diagnostic meta-analyses and can lead to a negative correlation between sensitivity and specificity across studies. This affects the stability and clinical interpretation of the pooled ROC curve. In summary, while most studies had sound designs in core methodological aspects, limitations in the present meta-analysis included insufficient representativeness in patient selection and inconsistency in prognostic thresholds. These limitations may lead to an overestimation of pooled prognostic performance metrics and restrict the generalizability of the findings to real-world clinical scenarios. The total data obtained from the eligible studies were entered into Meta-Disc 1.4 for analysis. The Spearman correlation coefficient between log sensitivity and log (1-specificity) was calculated. Main results

The results indicated that the pooled sensitivity for preoperative NLR in diagnosing postoperative mortality in hip fracture patients was 0.69 (95% CI 0.55–0.83), and the pooled specificity was 0.84 (0.74–0.93) (Fig. 4A). The pooled PLR was 4.4 (2.3–8.5), and the NLR was 0.36 (0.21–0.41) (Figure S1A). The DOR was 12 (6–26) (Figure S2A). The area under the SROC curve (sAUC) was 0.84 (0.81–0.87) (Fig. 3A). The Deek funnel plot of the eligible studies revealed no significant publication bias (p = 0.97) (Fig. 4A). The aforementioned findings collectively demonstrate that preoperative NLR possesses high prognostic value for predicting postoperative mortality in hip fractures.

Subgroup analysis

Given the significant heterogeneity observed, subgroup analyses and bivariate meta-regression were conducted. In the meta-analysis assessing preoperative NLR for predicting mortality after hip fracture surgery, subgroups were stratified by cut-off value (NLR > 5 vs. NLR ≤ 5), country (developed vs. developing countries), and follow-up duration (≥ 1 year vs. < 1 year). Through subgroup analysis, it was found that a high cut-off value (NLR > 5) demonstrated higher prognostic performance for postoperative mortality. Furthermore, NLR showed better predictive ability in developing countries compared to developed nations, suggesting that the predictive power of NLR may be more applicable in regions with relatively lower economic development. Additionally, subgroup analysis with a follow-up duration of ≥ 1 year indicated a stronger predictive efficacy of NLR, signifying its superior prognostic value for long-term mortality risk. Detailed outcomes are illustrated in Table 2. Meta-regression findings showed that the magnitude of the cut-off value (p = 0.00) was a source of heterogeneity in the preoperative NLR group, while country differences and follow-up duration were not. Forest plots are provided in Figure S3A.

Discussion

This investigation aimed to systematically evaluate the prognostic utility of NLR for postoperative mortality in hip fractures and to quantify its prognostic performance. Our analysis revealed that NLR is a meaningful prognostic marker of postoperative mortality, with preoperative NLR demonstrating significantly superior prognostic performance compared to postoperative NLR (AUC: preoperative 0.84 [95% CI 0.81–0.87] vs. postoperative 0.62 [95% CI 0.58–0.66]). For individuals with an approximate baseline postoperative mortality risk of 10%, a high preoperative NLR level could increase the post-test probability of mortality to approximately 33%. This finding underscores the potential clinical utility of NLR in identifying high-risk individuals. Identifying such individuals aids in making individualized surgical decisions, including selecting procedures, determining the timing of procedures, and deciding on procedure duration. It also provides critical support for refined perioperative care management [24, 25]. As a meta-analysis assessing NLR's prediction of mortality in patients undergoing hip fracture surgery, this meta-analysis integrated data from 12 studies involving 10,015 patients across six countries, thereby addressing a gap in high-quality evidence within this field. It provides crucial evidence-based support for utilizing this readily available and routinely obtained marker to predict long-term postoperative mortality in hip fractures, particularly older individuals, and challenges the conventional notion that postoperative inflammatory markers hold greater value.

Our findings indicate that preoperative NLR exhibits significantly superior prognostic performance for predicting postoperative mortality in hip fractures compared to postoperative NLR. This superiority can be attributed to preoperative NLR serving as a 'cleaner' signal, reflecting a patient's baseline immune reserve and intrinsic stress compensatory potential. Specifically, preoperative measurements circumvent the ineffective activation of neutrophils and iatrogenic lymphocyte suppression induced by surgical trauma itself, while also excluding confounding factors such as postoperative blood transfusions, analgesic medications, and complications [26]. This allows for the complete preservation of critical immune information that assesses a patient's capacity to withstand surgical insult and manage postoperative challenges. Existing research broadly substantiates the significant impact of surgical trauma on leukocyte subsets and the importance of baseline inflammatory and immune status on prognosis [27], providing a robust pathophysiological basis for the predictive value of preoperative NLR. Consequently, preoperative NLR offers a more reliable new basis for clinical risk stratification. Further prospective studies incorporating dynamic immune monitoring are warranted to validate this mechanism and explore its deeper pathways.

Meta-regression analysis further revealed that, in addition to the timing of measurement, differences in sample size constitute a key source of inter-study heterogeneity. Large-sample studies, due to broader inclusion criteria, exhibit increased patient heterogeneity, while small-sample studies may overestimate prognostic specificity due to insufficient correction for confounders (e.g., age, comorbidities) [28]. This suggests the need for a comprehensive appraisal of result reliability in clinical practice. A subgroup analysis of preoperative NLR showed that developed countries had higher specificity (0.86) but lower sensitivity (0.62). Conversely, developing countries exhibited higher sensitivity (0.78) and lower specificity (0.82). This disparity may be attributed to variations in healthcare systems, laboratory standardization, and population characteristics. More comprehensive perioperative management in developed countries (e.g., early surgery and supportive anti-infective care) could reduce non-inflammatory mortality and enhance the NLR's ability to identify patients at truly high risk. In regions with limited resources, inconsistent testing standardization, suboptimal management of comorbidities, and potential differences in race-related inflammatory responses could lead to more frequent NLR elevation. This would increase sensitivity but also raise the risk of false positives.

Therefore, the application of NLR necessitates adherence to principles of clinical scenario adaptation—developed countries are suited for precise screening of high-risk individuals, while developing countries are more appropriate for broad initial screening [29].

Our meta-analysis confirms that preoperative NLR possesses significant predictive value for long-term mortality exceeding one year. Notably, the NLR cut-off values employed for prognosis prediction exhibit considerable heterogeneity, ranging from 3.2 [30] to 10.8 [31]. This variability likely stems from differences in study populations, varying follow-up durations, and diverse statistical methodologies, posing a genuine challenge for standardized clinical translation of findings. Nevertheless, our subgroup analysis offers crucial insights into this heterogeneity: when a higher cut-off value (> 5) was applied, the model demonstrated superior prognostic performance (AUC = 0.84, 95% CI 0.81–0.87), with a specificity of 80%. This suggests robustness in identifying high-risk individuals. In contrast, a cut-off value of ≤ 5, while offering moderate sensitivity (62%), yielded a lower AUC (0.62), indicating limited discriminative capacity.

We posit that a threshold of > 5 might better approximate the critical pathophysiological point of irreversible immune-inflammatory imbalance. This involves the synergistic amplification effect of neutrophil-driven tissue injury cascades and immune senescence due to lymphocyte depletion [32]. This mechanistic hypothesis suggests that a higher threshold is more specific for mortality risk. Furthermore, extended follow-up periods (> 1 year) help exclude short-term confounding factors, such as acute postoperative inflammation. This highlights the fact that the preoperative NLR reflects chronic systemic inflammatory burden. While the heterogeneity of NLR thresholds restricts its immediate widespread adoption, our data support optimizing prognostic performance by employing a cut-off value of greater than five in specific clinical contexts, particularly for assessing long-term prognosis. Future prospective studies are needed to validate the generalizability of this threshold and to explore strategies for calibrating thresholds individually, thereby facilitating the transition of NLR from a research metric to a precise clinical tool. Additionally, several limitations of this investigation warrant attention. (i) Despite efforts to minimize bias by selecting as many homogeneous studies as possible, heterogeneity persisted among the eligible studies. (ii) The literature included in this meta-analysis consisted solely of retrospective observational studies, which limited causal inference and increased the ROB. Patient enrollment relied on historical records, which could have introduced selection bias. Additionally, data extraction is susceptible to information bias and definitional heterogeneity. While some studies adjusted for confounders, unmeasured factors such as lifestyle and genetic background may still interfere with the results. Consequently, the reported effect sizes may be overestimated, and the strength of the evidence is limited. This precludes direct interpretation as a causal association. Future high-quality prospective or interventional studies are required for validation. (iii) Notably, the Deeks' funnel plot asymmetry test (Figure S4B) revealed significant publication bias (p = 0.03) when incorporating studies for postoperative NLR analysis. This suggests that the predictive effect of postoperative NLR may be influenced by the overrepresentation of small positive studies, which could lead to an inflated pooled effect size. In contrast, Deeks' test was not statistically significant (p = 0.97) for the primary analysis of preoperative NLR, which is the focus of our meta-analysis, indicating a limited impact of publication bias on the main conclusions. Nevertheless, readers should be aware that the evidence base, dominated by retrospective studies, carries an overall potential for bias. High-quality prospective cohort studies are urgently needed to validate the predictive value of preoperative NLR. (iv) The eligible studies originated from diverse countries, and immune function can vary between different ethnic and sex groups. Consequently, large-scale, multicenter prospective clinical studies involving homogeneous ethnic populations are necessary to definitively establish the value of NLR in diagnosing postoperative mortality following hip fracture.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis suggests that the preoperative NLR is a promising albeit preliminary predictor of postoperative mortality in hip fractures. It exhibits significantly superior predictive performance compared to postoperative NLR. While this metric shows promise as a clinically useful risk stratification tool, it requires further validation in prospective studies.
