Hippocampal atrophy in untreated de novo Parkinson’s disease with obstructive sleep apnea
Authors: Kristína Burdová, Filip Růžička, Josef Mana, Pavel Filip, Jiří Nepožitek, Simona Dostálová, Pavla Peřinová, Ondrej Bezdicek, Petr Gregorovič, Filip Havlík, Evžen Růžička, Petr Dušek, Karel Šonka, Robert Jech
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOver the course of Parkinson’s disease (PD), there is considerable intersubject variability in gray matter (GM) loss across a wide range of subcortical structures and cortical areas, which is often predictive of the clinical trajectory of the disease. The biological or clinical factors underlying these individual differences are not well understood. Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a sleep-disordered breathing that is associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment and neurodegeneration. In this study, we investigated whether moderate-to-severe OSA in untreated de novo PD is linked to more severe GM atrophy compared to PD without OSA and a group of controls. High-resolution structural 3 T MRI T1 and T2-weighted scans were used to estimate subcortical GM volumes and hippocampal subfields, and to perform vertex-wise analyses of cortical thickness and cortical surface area. We found that the presence of OSA was associated with a significant bilateral reduction in hippocampal volume, particularly in the CA1, CA3, and subicular regions, an effect specifically observed in PD patients and not in the control group. These findings suggest that the co-occurrence of OSA and PD may be related to early structural brain changes, highlighting the importance of timely OSA diagnosis and management to potentially delay cognitive decline in PD.Subject terms: Parkinson's disease, Neurodegeneration

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is known to encompass a variety of different clinical phenotypes, each with a distinct spectrum of symptoms and prognosis1–5. In accordance with this, a number of neuroimaging studies have demonstrated considerable heterogeneity in gray matter (GM) loss across a range of subcortical structures and cortical areas over the course of the disease6–8. In particular, the degree of volumetric and shape alterations in the basal ganglia nuclei has been found to be closely related to both the extent of motor and cognitive impairment9–13. Similarly, accelerated thinning of the frontal and temporal-parietal cortices and reduction in hippocampal volume have been shown to be strongly correlated with cognitive decline and the development of dementia14–20. Moreover, research has indicated that structural changes in patients at risk for faster motor or cognitive decline can be identified from the initial stages of the disease, suggesting that the trajectory of brain atrophy may serve as a predictor of disease progression8,19,21–24. However, the biological or clinical factors that contribute to the observed variability in PD cases remain largely unknown.

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a sleep-disordered breathing characterized by repetitive episodes of complete (apnea) or partial (hypopnea) upper airway obstruction during sleep, resulting in intermittent nocturnal hypoxemia and sleep fragmentation25. The severity of sleep apnea is quantified by the Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI), which is defined as the number of apnea or hypopnea events per hour of sleep26. A growing body of evidence suggests that OSA is linked with an increased risk of cognitive impairment, including all-cause and Alzheimer’s-related dementia27–29. Likewise, an increased association between OSA and PD has been observed28,30. Some studies have indicated that up to 40-60% of patients with PD may have clinically significant OSA28,31. Furthermore, it appears that in addition to cognitive decline, OSA in PD may be also associated with worsened motor dysfunction32–34. However, to the best of our knowledge, the relationship of OSA and brain structural architecture in patients with de novo stage PD remains to be described. It is currently unclear whether the presence of OSA may contribute to the variability in GM atrophy observed in PD, and thus potentially influence or reflect the overall disease course.

The primary objective of this study was to determine whether significant OSA (OSA + , AHI ≥ 15) in untreated de novo PD (PD OSA + ) is associated with greater GM loss compared to de novo PD without OSA (PD OSA−) and control groups (CON OSA + , CON OSA−). In light of prior research suggesting that early-stage PD is primarily associated with morphological changes in subcortical structures23,35–39, our hypothesis was that subcortical volumes would be the most affected structures in relation to PD and OSA status. In addition, we hypothesized that the co-occurrence of an early-stage neurodegenerative disease (PD) and a sleep disorder (OSA) would have a greater impact on GM integrity than either condition alone. Finally, we hypothesized that de novo PD patients with OSA would exhibit greater cognitive or motor deficits compared to matched PD patients without OSA.

Results

Participants

Descriptive statistics are summarized in Table 1 and in Tables S4–5 in the Supplementary Material. The OSA+ status in our sample was characterized by a significantly higher percentage of males (p = 0.003), higher BMI (p < 0.001), higher uric acid levels (p < 0.008), and higher fasting glycemia plasma level (p < 0.014) compared to the OSA− regardless of groups of subjects. Slightly elevated glucose and hyperuricemia were likely related to the higher body mass index (BMI) observed in the OSA group. Patients with PD had significantly lower UPSIT scores (p < 0.001) than CON participants. There were no other significant differences in the descriptive variables between the CON and PD groups or significant interactions between GROUP and OSA. In particular, the PD (OSA ±) groups did not differ with respect to MDS-UPDRS I-III scores or MDS-UPDRS III subscores for axial symptoms, akinesia/rigidity, and tremor. Finally, RBD was diagnosed in 26% of PD OSA+ patients and 39% of PD OSA− patients, and there was no significant difference in frequency between the groups.

Subcortical volume segmentation

Adjusted volumes for the 16 subcortical structures (8 per hemisphere) and statistical results are summarized in Fig. 1 and Table 2. The full statistical analysis is presented in the Supplementary Data 1. Linear regression analyses revealed a significant GROUP*OSA interaction effect on bilateral (AGE-, SEX-, BMI- and TIV-adjusted) hippocampal volumes (left β = 0.93, p = 0.001, padj = 0.014; right β = 0.95, p = 0.002, padj = 0.014). Specifically, in the PD group, OSA+ subjects had significantly reduced both right (p < 0.001) and left (p < 0.001) hippocampal volumes compared to OSA− subjects, whereas in the CON group the difference between the hippocampal volumes of OSA+ and OSA− subjects did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 1, Table 2, Supplementary Data 1). A similar pattern of findings was observed bilaterally in the white matter, as well as in the right amygdala and the right caudate, without reaching significance at the adjusted 5% FDR. Finally, we conducted two additional sensitivity analyses: (1) including fasting plasma glucose and uric acid levels as covariates and (2) including MoCA scores; the main findings remained unchanged (see Tables S6, S7 and Supplementary Data 3, 5).

Hippocampal subfields

Detailed statistical analysis is provided in Table 3 and Supplementary Data 2, 4, 6. The GROUP*OSA interaction effect was detected bilaterally on volumes in most longitudinal hippocampal segments- head (left: β = 0.99, p < 0.001, padj = 0.005; right: β = 1.13, p < 0.001, padj = 0.005), body (left: β = 0.92, p = 0.002, padj = 0.009; right: β = 0.90, p = 0.005, padj = 0.013), tail (left: β = 0.89, p = 0.013, padj = 0.024), except for the right hippocampal tail (β = 0.53, p = 0.140, padj = 0.155). Within the same analysis, the linear regressions also showed a significant GROUP*OSA interaction effect bilaterally in the presubiculum-head (left: β = 0.99, p = 0.003, padj = 0.009; right: β = 0.93, p = 0.005, padj = 0.013) and body (left: β = 0.83, p = 0.013, padj = 0.024; right: β = 0.88, p = 0.014, padj = 0.024), CA1-head (left: β = 0.86, p = 0.006, padj = 0.013; right: β = 0.99, p = 0.003, padj = 0.010), CA3-head (left: β = 0.78, p = 0.024, padj = 0.034; right: β = 1.06, p = 0.005, padj = 0.013), and CA4DG-head (left: β = 0.73, p = 0.018, padj = 0.026; right: β = 1.00, p = 0.001, padj = 0.007), subiculum-body (left: β = 1.07, p < 0.001, padj = 0.005; right: β = 0.92, p = 0.012, padj = 0.024), as well as in the right parasubiculum (β = 1.12, p = 0.002, padj = 0.009), left subiculum-head (β = 0.82, p = 0.012, padj = 0.024), left CA3-body (β = 0.80, p = 0.030, padj = 0.040), and right HATA (β = 0.86, p = 0.015, padj = 0.024)). Thus, in PD OSA+ subjects, these results indicate that while volume reduction is relatively evenly distributed along the hippocampal longitudinal axis, along the hippocampal horizontal axis, the reduction is more pronounced bilaterally in the subicular complex (the parasubiculum, presubiculum, and subiculum) and the CA1 and C3 fields.

Cortical thickness and surface area

Vertex-wise analyses did not yield statistically significant results for the GROUP*OSA interaction or for the comparisons between PD-CON and OSA groups with respect to CT and SA.

Scheltens’ mediotemporal atrophy visual rating scale

In the logistic regression, the highest risk of pathological MTA was observed in men with PD/OSA+ (80%, 95% CI [57.2, 92.3] whereas the lowest was observed in women with PD/OSA− (7.7%, 95% CI [1.1, 39.1]).

The only statistically significant difference after adjusting for multiple comparisons was that between men with PD/OSA+ and women with PD/OSA− (OR = 48.1, z = 3.277, p = 0.001, padj = 0.012).

Moreover, men with PD/OSA+ also tended to have a higher risk of MTA pathology compared to men with PD/OSA− (OR = 4.57, z = 2.00, p = 0.046, padj = 0.138) and women with PD/OSA+ (OR = 24.0, z = 2.61, p = 0.009, padj = 0.054). Finally, men with PD/OSA− tended to have a higher risk of MTA pathology than women with PD/OSA− (OR = 10.5, z = 2.023, p = 0.043, padj = 0.138). On the other hand, neither the comparisons between women with PD/OSA− and women with PD/OSA + , nor the comparisons within the CON group reached the specified threshold for statistical significance (p ≥ 0.166, padj ≥ 0.399).

Neuropsychological assessment

No significant results were found with respect to the GROUP*OSA interaction in any of the observed tests at the adjusted 5% FDR. Nevertheless, a significant main effect of GROUP was observed in TMT-B (β = −0.57, p = 0.003, padj = 0.013) and GPT bilaterally (left: β = −0.59, p = 0.001, padj = 0.007; right: β = −0.80, p < 0.001, padj < 0.001) (Table S1, Fig. 2 and Figure S1), with patients with PD performing worse on average than CON. In the exploratory analysis of the effect of hippocampal subfield volumes on cognitive function, lower volume of several structures, primarily within the subicular complex of the left hippocampal body, were indicative of cognitive deficit in PD, particularly in declarative memory (RAVLT), psychomotor speed and attention (TMT-A), and potentially executive function (TMT-B and PST-C) (see Fig. 3, Figure S4 and Supplementary Data 7). Finally, the models of TMT-B and GPT were re-fitted via distributional regression to test their robustness to unequal variance in the data. The distributional models converged successfully and improved the modeling of the data distribution in general (Figure S2) and the group-specific standard deviation in particular (Figure S3). Estimates of model parameters were similar between frequentist and Bayesian distributional models (Table S2), suggesting that these results are likely robust to the unequal variances between CON and PD groups.

Discussion

This study investigated whether the degree of GM atrophy differed among the de novo untreated PD and CON individuals in relation to moderate-to-severe OSA. Consistent with our hypothesis, the results showed a significant GROUP and OSA interaction with the hippocampal volume in the PD OSA+ group. No additional OSA-related differences in PD were found in the volumes of other subcortical GM regions or in whole-brain analyses of CT and SA. In contrast to the PD groups, no OSA-related GM differences were observed in the CON groups, indicating that the co-occurrence of OSA and neurodegeneration might be linked to greater GM loss than either condition alone.

Our findings agree with several previous reports showing functional or structural disruption of hippocampal integrity in PD at different stages of the disease40–44. While there are mixed results in the literature regarding the presence and extent of hippocampal atrophy in early PD19,38,40,43,45, this atrophy usually becomes evident as the disease progresses and various cognitive deficits develop. Indeed, numerous neuroimaging and neuropathological studies have shown that both mild cognitive impairment (PD-MCI) and dementia (PDD) related to PD are often associated with reduced hippocampal volumes10,24,42,43,45,46. In line with the morphological findings, a growing number of evidence in recent years indicates that memory impairment is not only a common feature of PD-MCI and PDD, but also involves, to varying degrees, medio-temporal lobe deficit43,44,47,48. Even in de novo PD, up to 11% of patients have been shown to have severe episodic memory impairment due to both a combination of executive and hippocampal dysfunction47. Our study is the first to demonstrate an association between OSA and hippocampal atrophy in patients with de novo PD. Furthermore, the results indicate that the inconsistencies in findings regarding hippocampal atrophy in early PD, as observed in previous studies, may be attributed to the unconsidered influence of potential confounding factors, such as sleep disturbances.

Contrary to our hypothesis, the PD OSA+ and PD OSA- groups did not differ in motor and cognitive performance, either in total clinical scores or in individual motor subscales or cognitive measures. This finding may be explained by the fact that the clinically detectable cognitive deficit is more likely to correlate with the disintegration of brain networks and the disconnection of functionally interdependent regions, rather than with focal atrophy of a single structure49–52. Indeed, PD-MCI or PDD is usually not associated with hippocampal atrophy alone16,19,43,45. In this context, the lack of more widespread GM changes in the basal ganglia nuclei or thinning of the frontal and temporal-parietal cortices in the PD subjects in this study may indicate preservation of compensatory mechanisms and contribute to the observed lack of cognitive or motor differences between the PD groups. This is also consistent with the neuropsychological evaluation, showing that only 15 (27%) patients (6 PD OSA+ vs. 9 PD OSA− according to level II assessment) in our study had PD-MCI, which is insufficient frequency to demonstrate potential clinical or structural differences between the OSA groups, but comparable to the literature53,54. Nevertheless, the potential clinical relevance of our findings is suggested by the observation that reduced volumes in several subfields, mainly within the subicular complex of the left hippocampal body, were associated with worse cognitive function in PD (irrespective of OSA status), particularly in domains such as declarative memory, psychomotor speed, attention, and executive function.

Finally, as in Alzheimer’s disease (AD), early hippocampal atrophy has been identified as an independent predictor of later progression from intact cognition to PD-MCI and from PD-MCI to PDD8,10,21,24,45,55,56. Hippocampal atrophy in PD OSA+ subjects may therefore be an early predictor of poorer prognosis in terms of cognitive trajectory, which could not be captured by the cross-sectional design of our study. In this context, it is noteworthy that pathological MTA was identified in up to 80% of men with PD OSA+ even when using the visual Scheltens scale, suggesting that the hippocampal atrophy observed in our main FreeSurfer-derived volumetric analyses might be also detectable with standard clinical rating methods.

The precise nature of the relationship between brain pathology and OSA remains unclear. OSA and sleep fragmentation may result in brain damage through a number of mechanisms, including increased oxidative stress and neuroinflammation in neural tissue, along with impaired clearance of metabolic waste by the glymphatic system27,28,57–59. Importantly, the selective vulnerability of different brain regions, or distinct neuronal populations within these regions, to stressors that may ultimately lead to cell damage or death varies significantly60,61. In particular, experimental evidence has demonstrated that distinct neuronal populations within the hippocampus, specifically those in the CA1 region and the subiculum, exhibit a heightened susceptibility to both oxidative stress and neurodegenerative processes62,63. Therefore, our observation of a reduction in hippocampal volume, including specific hippocampal subfields, in the PDOSA + group seems to be consistent with the experimental findings and further with several neuroimaging and pathological studies that have documented hippocampal damage in subjects with OSA64–66. In addition, other consequences or comorbidities of OSA, such as arterial hypertension, altered cerebral hemodynamics, endothelial dysfunction, diabetes, and obesity, may contribute to brain and hippocampal pathology and potentiate neurodegeneration and cognitive deficits27,67–70. Although the vascular and metabolic aspects of OSA were not the primary focus of this study, it is noteworthy that both the CON and PD OSA+ groups exhibited a significantly higher BMI.

However, the presence of additional factors may lead to more pronounced hippocampal atrophy in PD compared to CON, as our findings indicate. Primarily, PD is associated with the widespread degeneration of neurotransmitter systems71–76. In particular, there is a reduction in dopaminergic, noradrenergic, serotoninergic, and cholinergic projections to the hippocampus, which play an essential role in maintaining its structural and functional integrity. Another potential mechanism by which the hippocampus may be affected is the deposition of α-synuclein in medial temporal structures, which may occur as a consequence of the disease progression pattern77. This is also consistent with our observed atrophy in the CA3 hippocampal subfield. Furthermore, this mechanism may act synergistically with age-related and AD pathologies such as extracellular β-amyloid and intracellular tau depositions, the levels of which also correlate with α-synuclein pathology in PD75,78. Interestingly, the initial neuropathological changes associated with AD predominantly affect the CA1 field and subiculum79–82, a finding similar to the pattern of atrophy in our research. The possible role of AD pathology is also consistent with recent meta-analytic evidence showing that individuals with OSA exhibit reduced CSF Aβ42 and elevated t-tau levels, along with increased amyloid burden on PET imaging83,84, as well as with neuropathological findings demonstrating a dose–response relationship between OSA severity and hippocampal Aβ and tau pathology65.

We can therefore speculate that AD co-pathology in PD may play a role in the occurrence of OSA and hippocampal atrophy that we observed. Such speculation may be also supported by a study showing that sleep apnea is significantly associated with hippocampal atrophy in asymptomatic amyloid-positive older adults85, and by the fact that volume loss in hippocampal subregions - particularly the subiculum - correlates with AD-related depositions in Lewy body disease86. Importantly, CA1 and the subiculum are output regions of the hippocampal formation that project to numerous subcortical structures that regulate sleep87. Thus, it can also be hypothesized that the observed changes in hippocampal volume in PD OSA+ subjects are not a consequence of OSA, but rather its primary cause. From this perspective, the presence of OSA in PD may be indicative of a specific phenotype of the disease in which there is an early impairment of mediotemporal structures that manifests as a sleep disorder.

This study has several limitations. First, given the relatively small number of subjects included in our study and the substantial heterogeneity of PD, it is not possible to draw definitive conclusions regarding the relationship between OSA and hippocampal atrophy. However, our study is the first to show this potential association, and further research is needed to confirm it. Second, another limitation of the present study is the lack of biomarker-based assessment of AD co-pathology. As no cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) or PET biomarkers of AD pathology were available, we cannot confirm or exclude a potential contribution of concomitant AD pathology to our structural findings. Clarifying the specific role of AD co-pathology in the relationship between OSA and hippocampal atrophy in PD was therefore beyond the scope of the present study and will require future biomarker-informed longitudinal investigations. Third, because the design of this study is observational and cross-sectional, it is not possible to draw causal inferences. Nevertheless, our findings may provide a basis for the design of future prospective longitudinal studies, and our cohort will also be followed longitudinally. Finally, the hippocampus was automatically segmented using both T1w and T2w images. Although the 0.7 mm isotropic resolution of the T2w scan is still sufficient to detect the SRLM (stratum radiatum lacunosum moleculare), which is critical for determining the borders between subfields, the precision of this approach has not been validated against high-resolution ultrahigh-field MRI or histological sections88,89. Consequently, the method may not accurately represent all anatomical features of the hippocampus. For this reason, caution is warranted in interpreting the hippocampal subfield findings in our study, although they are all consistent with several previous neuroimaging and neuropathological studies.

In conclusion, the results of this study associate hippocampal atrophy, particularly in the CA1 region, CA3 region, and subicular complex, in de novo PD with the presence of moderate to severe OSA. In addition, this study demonstrates that hippocampal atrophy may be present from the clinical onset of PD. Given that both hippocampal atrophy and moderate to severe OSA may be early predictors of cognitive decline and an overall poorer prognosis for PD over time, our study adds to the importance of detecting OSA as early as possible in the course of PD. However, further work is needed to confirm our findings and to evaluate the role and efficacy of early treatment of OSA in influencing the course of PD and the severity of neuropsychiatric and motor symptoms of the disease. Early detection of OSA may be critical. If hippocampal atrophy is secondary, early symptomatic treatment with continuous positive airway pressure may be key to modifying the course and symptoms of PD.

Methods

Participants

A total of 55 participants (mean age 62.4 ± 8.5 years, 20 females) with de novo and treatment-naïve PD were consecutively collected over a four-year period from the Movement and Sleep Disorders Centers of the Department of Neurology, First Faculty of Medicine, Charles University and General University Hospital in Prague. All patients with PD were diagnosed by an experienced movement disorder specialist according to the clinical diagnostic criteria of the Movement Disorder Society90. Inclusion criteria for the PD group were as follows: age 60 ± 15 years (to limit age variability in disease onset), motor symptom duration up to 3 years, no previous history of antiparkinsonian medication use, and the absence of clinical, imaging, or laboratory signs of atypical parkinsonism. Furthermore, a control group of 52 healthy individuals (CON; mean age 61.1 years ± 8.6, 15 females) was included. Control subjects were recruited from the general community via advertisement. Common exclusion criteria were the presence or history of other major neurological (i.e., ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke, epilepsy, and encephalitis) or psychiatric (i.e., history of psychosis) diagnoses, decompensated hypertension or diabetes, general contraindications to MRI, significant space-occupying brain lesions, and serious or terminal illness. Demographic, clinical or laboratory information of all participants is presented in Table 1 and Table S4–5 in the Supplementary Material. A comprehensive clinical and neurological examination was conducted on all subjects, which included the MDS-sponsored revision of the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). In addition, several subscores were extracted from the MDS-UPDRS III scale in PD: an axial score (items 1, 2, 3a, 9–14), an akinesia and rigidity score (items 3b–e, 4–8) and a tremor score (items 15–18). Finally, laboratory parameters (urea, creatinine, uric acid, fasting glucose, cholesterol, triglycerides, thyroid-stimulating hormone, and free thyroxine) and supplementary clinical measures (blood pressure, Fazekas score [41], and the Scheltens medial temporal atrophy [MTA] score [42]) were also recorded.

The study was approved by the ethics committee of the General University Hospital in Prague, and all participants provided written informed consent to participate in the study. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Polysomnography assessment

A one-night video-polysomnography (PSG; RemLogic software, version 3.4.1, Embla Systems) was conducted between the hours of 22:00 and 06:00 in accordance with the guidelines set forth by the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM)91. The PSG included the following: I) electrooculography (E1-M2, E2-M2), II) electroencephalography (F3-M2, C3-M2, O1-M2, F4-M1, C4-M1, O2-M1), III) surface electromyography bilaterally of the mentalis muscle, flexor digitorum superficialis muscle, and tibialis anterior muscle, IV) electrocardiography, V) measurements of nasal pressure, nasal and oral airflow, thoracic and abdominal respiratory effort, VI) oxygen saturation, VII) and microphone registration of respiratory sounds and vocalizations, in addition to digitally synchronized video monitoring. All features of the PSG were subjected to visual analysis in accordance with the AASM Manual for the Scoring of Sleep and Associated Events, version 2.2, 201591. The scoring of sleep stages, arousals, respiratory events, and limb movements was conducted by a trained scorer. The severity of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) was categorized using the apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) cutoff of 15, which is widely accepted as the threshold for the need for continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy. Absent or mild obstructive sleep apnea (OSA-) was defined as an AHI of less than 15, while moderate to severe OSA (OSA+) corresponded to an AHI of at least 1592,93. Within the PD group, 27 patients were classified as OSA+ compared to 26 individuals in the CON group. REM behavioral disorder (RBD) was diagnosed in accordance with the International Classification of Sleep Disorders, third edition (ICSD-3).

Neuropsychological assessment

All PD and CON were administered a battery of neuropsychological tests at enrollment to the study. The battery included assessment of I) declarative memory via Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT)94,95, II); attention via Trail Making Test, part A (TMT-A)96,97, and dot color naming (PST-D) as well as naming color of neutral words (PST-W) conditions from Prague Stroop Test98; III) executive function via Trail Making Test, part B96,97, and Prague Stroop Test, interference condition (i.e., naming color of contrasting color words, PST-C)98; and IV) processing speed via Grooved Pegboard Test (GPT)99. All participants were also administered Montreal cognitive assessment (MoCA) for cognitive screening100,101. Patients with MoCA scores below 26 were classified as having possible mild cognitive impairment in Parkinson’s disease (PD-MCI), Level I, in accordance with the current Level I diagnostic criteria of the International Parkinson and Movement Disorder Society (MDS)102,103.

Magnetic resonance imaging acquisition

The MRI acquisition was performed using a 3-Tesla Magnetom Skyra scanner (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). A T1-weighted (T1w) high-resolution structural scan was acquired using the magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo (MPRAGE) sequence, 1.0-mm isotropic resolution, with the repetition time (TR) = 2150 ms, time to echo (TE) = 2.47 ms, inversion time (TI) = 1000 ms and field-of-view (FOV) = 197 × 230 mm2. A T2-weighted (T2w) high-resolution structural scan was acquired using the SPACE sequence with 0.7-mm isotropic resolution, TR = 3200 ms, TE = 566 ms, FOV = 223 × 223 mm2.

Subcortical volume segmentation and surface-based morphometry analyses

Preliminary intensity normalization of the T1w scan was performed using the AFNI suite in the MacOS 12+ environment (Apple Silicon, M2 chip). Anatomical segmentation, subcortical labeling, and cortical reconstruction were based on the FreeSurfer recon-all pipeline (version 8.0.0 http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu) utilizing both T1w and T2w images. The details of these procedures have been described elsewhere104–107. The accuracy of the segmented volumes and any potential issues with the delineation of the cortex were examined visually on a slice-by-slice basis. When necessary, manual corrections were implemented, and the recon-all was re-run. After the segmentation process, subcortical white matter volume (1 per hemisphere) and gray matter volumes (7 per hemisphere), including the bilateral caudate nucleus, putamen, pallidum, thalamus, nucleus accumbens, amygdala, and hippocampus, were extracted from each subject. Total intracranial volumes (TIVs) were calculated using sequential adaptive multimodal segmentation (SAMSEG). Finally, following the reconstruction process, vertex-wise estimates of cortical surface architecture (CT- cortical thickness, SA- surface area) were registered to the FreeSurfer average template, and a Gaussian kernel (full-width at half-maximum- FWHM = 10 mm) was used to smooth the data.

Given the findings from the subcortical volume analysis (see Results), a secondary analysis was performed to examine hippocampal subfields using a built-in module of FreeSurfer and both T1w and T2w images (see Limitations). Fifteen segments were analyzed from the data in each hemisphere: the hippocampal head, the hippocampal body, the hippocampal tail, the parasubiculum, the hippocampus-amygdala-transition-area (HATA), the presubiculum-head, the subiculum-head, the CA1-head, the CA3-head, the CA4-head/molecular-layer-HP/GC-ML-DG-head (CA4DG-head), the presubiculum-body, the subiculum-body, the CA1-body, the CA3-body, the CA4-body/molecular-layer-HP/GC-ML-DG-body (CA4DG-body).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R statistical computing software (R Core Team 2024) or the Freesurfer environment (version 8.0.0 http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu). In accordance with the most recent recommendations of the American Statistical Association, which advise against basing scientific conclusions on whether a p value passes a specific threshold, we also calculated Shannon information (i.e., s-values) as -log₂ (p)108.

All demographic (AGE, EDUCATION, SEX) and descriptive clinical variables (RBD, MoCA, PD-MCI, BMI, AGE at first motor symptom, disease duration, MDS-UPDRS I, II, and III, UPSIT, Fazekas score, Scheltens score, and supplementary clinical or laboratory data) were described by their mean and SD if continuous and frequency if nominal separately for PD OSA−, PD OSA+, CON OSA−, and CON OSA+ groups of participants. The variables were compared by Gaussian (continuous variables) or logistic (binary variables) regression with GROUP (PD vs. CON), OSA (OSA+ vs. OSA−), and GROUP*OSA interaction as predictors when they were assessed in both PD and CON groups. For PD-specific variables, only OSA (OSA+ vs. OSA−) was used as a predictor.

Subcortical volumes were analyzed using a series of univariate linear regression models to assess associations with the factors of interest: GROUP (PD vs. CON), OSA (OSA+ vs. OSA−), and the GROUP*OSA interaction. AGE, SEX, TIV, and BMI were included as additive linear covariates. All continuous variables were standardized (i.e., centered on the mean and scaled by their in-sample SD) before the analysis, while nominal variables were entered via sum coding. The normality of the residuals was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and the homoscedasticity of the residuals was assessed through the Breusch-Pagan test. A threshold for rejecting the null hypothesis was adjusted for a 5% false discovery rate (FDR) in tests that included all main effects and interactions of interest (i.e., coefficients related to the GROUP and OSA variables) across all subcortical structures from both hemispheres. The test statistics consisted of t-values for each relevant regression coefficient computed by the lm() function.

In addition, hippocampal subfield volumes were examined in a series of post hoc univariate regression analyses with the same model specifications as previously described, focusing exclusively on the GROUP*OSA interaction. The threshold for rejecting the null hypothesis of a GROUP*OSA interaction was adjusted using a 5% false discovery rate (FDR) applied to all 32 comparisons conducted in this analysis.

Whole-brain surface-based cortical analyses of CT and SA were conducted on a vertex-wise level using general linear models (GLMs), as implemented in FreeSurfer. Given the principal focus on the differential effect of OSA between the PD and CON groups, we first performed an F-test to investigate the interaction (in either the intercept or slope) of GROUP by OSA (±). AGE, SEX, BMI, and TIV (only in the SA analysis) were used as additive covariates. In addition, group differences in CT and SA (PD vs. CON, OSA+ vs. OSA−) were assessed in two models using two sample t tests. The resulting models were corrected for multiple comparisons by applying Monte Carlo Null-Z simulation with a vertex-wise/cluster forming threshold of p < 0.001 and a cluster-wise p-threshold of P < 0.05.

In addition, given the findings from the subcortical volume analysis (see Results), Scheltens’ MTA rating scale was regressed on GROUP, OSA, SEX, and their two-way and three-way interactions using logistic regression. Based on this model, the expected probabilities of pathological MTA score for each GROUP/OSA/SEX combination were computed and compared using the emmeans R package. The threshold for rejecting the null hypothesis of no difference in the risk of pathological MTA between groups was set after adjusting for a 5% FDR for each within-GROUP (within PD and within CON) pairwise comparison.

Cognitive performance was analyzed using a series of univariate linear regression models to test the associations between each cognitive measure and GROUP (PD vs. CON), OSA (OSA+ vs. OSA−), and their interaction (GROUP*OSA). AGE, SEX, EDUCATION, and BMI were included as additive linear covariates. Prior to standardization, the response time variables were log-transformed and then inverted (i.e., multiplied by -1). Because it is generally accepted that the variance of response time variables increases with the mean109, we additionally re-fitted cognitive models with a statistically significant deviation from homoscedasticity using Bayesian distributional linear regression, which allows us to address heteroscedasticity by modeling it directly110. The re-fitted models included the same terms as the original models with the addition of the calculation of the residual standard deviation conditional on GROUP, OSA, GROUP*OSA interaction as well as AGE, SEX, EDUCATION, and BMI. The models were fitted using the built-in Hamiltonian Monte Carlo sampler in Stan (version 2.32.2), accessed via R version 4.3.3 using the “brms” package (R Core Team 2024; Stan Development Team 2020), with manually specified weakly informative priors but otherwise default “brms” settings (see the R code at https://github.com/josefmana/pdosa.git)110. To check the robustness of the results to heteroscedasticity, the parameter estimates of the Bayesian distributional models were then compared to the coefficients of the classical linear regressions.

Furthermore, to explore the potential predictive value of hippocampal subfields for cognitive deficit in PD, an exploratory analysis of the interaction between GROUP (PD vs. CON) and hippocampal subfield volumes was conducted. For this purpose, a set of univariate linear regressions was performed with each cognitive measurement as the outcome, and with GROUP, hippocampal subfield volumes, their interaction, and the same set of additive covariates as in the preceding analysis as predictors. Given the exploratory and hypothesis-generating nature of this analysis, no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied. The results of this analysis are therefore interpreted purely descriptively.

All regression equations (Table S3) can be found in the Supplementary Material.
