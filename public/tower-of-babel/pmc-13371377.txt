Climate variability and dengue incidence in southern Ecuador: an ecological analysis in two contrasting climatic settings (2019–2024)
Authors: Beatriz Quintero, Sara N. Cabrera-Jiménez, Karelys L. Ordóñez-Blacio, Solbey Morillo-Puente, Angélica X. Ramón-Ochoa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDengue transmission is modulated by climatic conditions through their effects on vector ecology and viral dynamics; however, these relationships are context-dependent and operate across multiple temporal scales. Evidence from ecologically diverse regions remains limited, particularly in settings where contrasting climatic regimes coexist within the same geographic area. This study aimed to characterize interannual climate variability and to evaluate immediate and lagged associations between temperature, precipitation, and dengue incidence in two climatically distinct cantons of southern Ecuador (Loja and Macará) from 2019 to 2024.MethodsAn ecological time-series study was conducted using weekly dengue case counts from the national surveillance system and meteorological data (minimum temperature, maximum temperature, and accumulated precipitation). Interannual variability was assessed using one-way ANOVA with Games-Howell post hoc comparisons. Climate-dengue relationships were first explored using Spearman correlation across lag structures from 0 to 16 weeks, complemented by Pearson correlation and covariance to describe linear association and joint variability. Temporal dependencies and potential non-linear patterns were examined using cross-correlation function (CCF) analysis and locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (LOESS). Multivariable generalized linear models with a negative binomial distribution were fitted separately for each canton to estimate independent and lagged effects of climatic variables, accounting for overdispersion in dengue case counts.ResultsBoth cantons exhibited significant interannual variability in temperature and precipitation, with greater climatic amplitude in Macará. Dengue incidence increased markedly in recent years, with earlier and more pronounced outbreaks in Macará compared with Loja. In exploratory analyses, minimum temperature and accumulated precipitation showed the most consistent lagged associations with dengue incidence, although these patterns varied by canton and year. In Macará, precipitation showed a strong and consistent association with dengue incidence across all lag models (IRR ≈ 1.01 per mm/week; all p < 0.001), representing the most consistent climatic predictor across lag periods. This finding was consistent with cross-correlation analysis, which identified precipitation as the strongest leading climatic signal (peak r = 0.48 at Lag 1), with significant positive correlations across multiple lag periods, supporting its role as a robust temporal predictor of dengue incidence in Macará. Mean temperature showed weaker and lag-dependent associations, with no consistent concurrent effect and positive associations emerging only at longer lag periods (12–16 weeks). However, cross-correlation analysis indicated that the temperature signal was weak (peak r < 0.15), suggesting a secondary and less consistent role compared with precipitation. In contrast, Loja presented heterogeneous and less temporally structured associations. However, given the low case burden and wide uncertainty, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.ConclusionsClimate-dengue relationships in southern Ecuador are heterogeneous and shaped by local climatic context and temporal dynamics. Precipitation was the climatic variable most consistently associated with dengue incidence, particularly in semi-arid settings. In temperate highland settings, observed associations may largely reflect shared seasonal patterns rather than independent climate-related effects. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating lag-specific and context-sensitive climate indicators into epidemiological surveillance and early warning systems and support the use of multivariable approaches to better characterize the combined and delayed associations between climatic factors and dengue incidence.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13643-6.

Background

Dengue has increased steadily in both burden and geographic distribution across South America over the past decade, with recent expansion linked to persistent thermal anomalies and large-scale environmental transformations [1]. Dengue transmission has shifted toward previously non-endemic areas, along with a sustained increase in incidence in urban environments exposed to high climate variability [1]. In Brazil, recent extreme climate conditions characterized by persistent rainfall and sustained temperatures have been associated with the extension of dengue transmission into traditionally cooler months, indicating a disruption of historical seasonal patterns and a reduced climatic constraint on transmission [2].

Current evidence indicates that the relationship between climate and dengue transmission is not linear, but rather follows risk curves shaped by thermal thresholds, with peak transmission occurring within intermediate temperature ranges and declines observed under both cooler and extreme heat conditions [3]. Global studies have confirmed that increasing temperature does not uniformly translate into higher transmission but instead modifies dengue incidence according to local climatic conditions, seasonality, and socio-environmental determinants [4]. These patterns highlight the need to analyze dengue within regional climatic frameworks, rather than extrapolating conclusions based solely on global averages [3].

The role of precipitation is equally complex and context dependent, as moderate rainfall favors the availability of breeding sites, while heavy precipitation may reduce larval density through mechanical flushing [5]. Long-term series analyses have shown that these effects are typically delayed rather than immediate, with rainfall influencing epidemic peaks after several weeks [5]. Similar dynamics have been reported in South Asia, where temperature and precipitation act with variable lags depending on the prevailing climate regime [6].

Beyond vector abundance, dengue transmission depends on physiological and virological processes that are highly temperature dependent, explaining why climate functions as a modulator rather than a sole determinant of incidence [3]. Experimental evidence indicates that elevated temperatures may reduce vector survival, alter blood-feeding behavior, and decrease viral replication efficiency, thereby potentially attenuating transmission when thermal conditions exceed optimal ranges [7]. These mechanisms help explain why extreme heat events are not always accompanied by proportional increases in dengue incidence [3].

In addition, dengue dynamics are shaped by virological and entomological factors that interact with climate, including serotype co-circulation, population immunity, and urban larval indices [8]. These ecological determinants can substantially modify associations between meteorological factors and incidence, generating heterogeneous patterns even under similar climatic conditions [8] Consequently, the evaluation of climate-dengue relationships require integrative analytical approaches incorporating environmental, entomological, and epidemiological data across multiple spatial [3].

In Ecuador, quantitative evidence on climate-dengue relationships remains limited, despite recent studies documenting sustained expansion of transmission across ecologically diverse regions of the country [9]. National historical series show a progressive re-emergence of dengue since the 1980s, with marked territorial heterogeneity [10]. However, most available studies lack formal, high-resolution climate analyses, limiting the identification of actionable environmental patterns [11].

Within this context, Loja Province provides a particularly informative setting, as it encompasses two cantons with contrasting climate regimes within the same geographic region. The canton of Loja exhibits a temperate highland climate (Cfb according to Köppen-Geiger classification), whereas Macará corresponds to a hot semi-arid tropical climate (BSh according to Köppen-Geiger), offering a natural framework for exploring climate-health associations under differentiated environmental conditions [12, 13].

Against this backdrop, the objectives of the present study were: (i) to characterize climate variability and interannual changes in Loja and Macará from 2019 to 2024, and (ii) to assess the immediate and lagged associations between temperature and accumulated precipitation and dengue incidence using a multivariable modeling approach based on negative binomial regression, considering temporal lags from 0 to 16 weeks to capture short- and medium-term delayed effects.

Methods

Study design and setting

An observational, ecological, and analytical study was conducted in the cantons of Loja and Macará (Loja Province, Ecuador) over the period 2019–2024. Both areas represent contrasting climatic settings within the same geographic region, allowing the assessment of climate-dengue associations under clearly differentiated environmental conditions (Fig. 1a and c).

Loja canton is in the central-northern part of the province within the inter-Andean region and constitutes the main urban and administrative center of the area. It lies at a mean elevation of 2,060 m above sea level and is classified as having a temperate highland climate (Cfb according to the Köppen-Geiger classification), with mean annual temperatures between 16 and 18 °C and annual precipitation exceeding 900 mm, mainly concentrated between January and April.

Macará canton is in the southwestern part of Loja Province, at the border with Peru, at an altitude of approximately 425 m above sea level. Its climate corresponds to a hot semi-arid tropical type (BSh according to Köppen-Geiger), with mean annual temperatures around 26 °C and maximum values frequently exceeding 35 °C during the dry season. Rainfall is concentrated between January and April, followed by a prolonged dry period.

Data sources and study population

All confirmed autochthonous dengue cases recorded in the national epidemiological surveillance system (SIVE-Alerta) of the Ministry of Public Health of Ecuador were included. The dataset consisted of weekly aggregated case counts at the canton level, corresponding to routine epidemiological notifications.

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Universidad Técnica Particular de Loja (UTPL), Ecuador (approval code: UTPL-CEISH-2025-01-INT-EO-RM-002). Authorization for the use of surveillance data was granted by the Zonal Health Coordination Office of Zone 7. As the dataset contained no personal identifiable information, individual informed consent was not required.

Daily meteorological data, including maximum temperature (°C), minimum temperature (°C), and precipitation (mm), were obtained from official meteorological stations (M0033-Loja and M1259-Macará) operated by the National Institute of Meteorology and Hydrology (INAMHI). Data was aggregated to weekly values to ensure temporal alignment with epidemiological reporting. Weekly temperature variables were calculated as the arithmetic mean of daily values within each epidemiological week, rather than using extreme values, in order to better represent sustained thermal exposure conditions. Weekly precipitation was computed as the cumulative sum of daily rainfall values.

For regression analyses, mean weekly temperature (Tmean) was derived as the average of maximum and minimum temperatures and used as the primary exposure variable to reduce collinearity between temperature measures. Weeks with missing meteorological data were excluded from the corresponding analyses (complete-case approach), as missingness was attributed to temporary gaps in station records rather than to systematic data absence. However, a non-negligible proportion of missing climatic data was identified, which reduced the number of observations available for some analyses. In addition, weekly dengue incidence showed heterogeneous temporal distribution, including periods with low case counts and a high proportion of weeks without reported cases in one of the study areas. These characteristics were considered during model specification in order to balance data availability, statistical robustness, and interpretability of the results.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive, comparative, and multivariable analytical approaches were applied. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics version 22 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and graphical and time-series analyses were performed using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided significance level of p < 0.05 was adopted, consistent with standard practices in epidemiological time-series analysis. Normality assumptions were assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests, which indicated non-normal distributions for all variables (p < 0.05). Descriptive statistics, including measures of central tendency and variability, are presented in Additional file 1: Tables S1, S2, S5, and S8.

To characterize interannual variability in climatic conditions, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied separately for each canton and climate variable (maximum temperature, minimum temperature, and accumulated precipitation). Despite deviations from normality, ANOVA was considered appropriate due to the balanced temporal structure of the data (approximately 52 observations per year), under which the method is robust to moderate violations of distributional assumptions. Importantly, ANOVA was used exclusively as a descriptive and comparative approach to evaluate differences in mean climatic values across predefined groups (years), and not as a method to model temporal dependence or to assess climate–dengue relationships. Post hoc comparisons were performed using the Games-Howell test to account for potential heterogeneity of variances. Duncan’s multiple range test was additionally applied for descriptive purposes to identify groups of years with similar climatic profiles, facilitating the interpretation of temporal clustering patterns without being used for inferential conclusions.

Prior to correlation analyses, the functional form of the relationships between climatic variables and dengue incidence was evaluated using bivariate scatter plots. These exploratory analyses showed predominantly monotonic patterns, supporting the use of Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) as the primary measure of association. Lagged associations were evaluated using weekly time-series data.

Lag structures were defined based on biological plausibility and epidemiological considerations and were operationalized as discrete intervals of 0, 4, 8, 12, and 16 weeks. Lag 0 was used to assess concurrent associations, whereas subsequent lags were selected to approximate early, intermediate, and delayed phases of transmission within the same epidemiological year. Although a continuous weekly lag framework was considered during model development, the final specification was based on discrete intervals after evaluating the characteristics of the dataset and the behavior of the models. Weekly dengue incidence showed overdispersion, heterogeneous temporal distribution, and low case density in certain periods, while climatic variables presented missing observations due to gaps in meteorological records. Under these conditions, more complex lag structures increased model instability and reduced interpretability. Therefore, a parsimonious lag framework based on discrete intervals was adopted. This approach allowed for a balance between biological interpretability and statistical stability, given the structure and limitations of the dataset.

From a biological perspective, these lag intervals reflect the sequential processes underlying dengue transmission, including rainfall-driven formation of larval habitats, immature mosquito development, adult emergence, gonotrophic cycles, viral extrinsic incubation in mosquitoes, intrinsic incubation in humans, and delays in diagnosis and reporting. These processes occur over several weeks rather than at a single time point, supporting the use of temporally aggregated lag windows.

Spearman’s correlation coefficients were calculated for each canton and year. Pearson correlation coefficients (r) and covariance were additionally computed as complementary measures to describe linear association and joint variability between variables, allowing a more detailed characterization of the magnitude and direction of climate-dengue relationships.

LOESS was applied separately for each canton as an exploratory, non-parametric approach to characterize the functional form of climate-dengue relationships prior to regression modelling and to identify potential non-linear patterns. These analyses were not used for inference but to inform the interpretation of model results. Cross-correlation function (CCF) analyses were also performed separately for each canton to evaluate the temporal direction and strength of associations between climatic variables and dengue incidence across lead and lag periods. Particular attention was given to the symmetry of correlations across positive and negative lags in order to distinguish potential predictive relationships from shared seasonal patterns.

To evaluate the independent and combined effects of climatic variables, generalized linear models with a negative binomial distribution were fitted separately for each canton. This approach was selected due to substantial overdispersion in weekly dengue counts, as indicated by variance-to-mean ratios greater than 1 (Loja = 4.11; Macará = 11.53), under which Poisson models would underestimate standard errors and lead to biased inference (Additional file 1: Table S12). Five models were constructed for each canton, corresponding to lag periods of 0, 4, 8, 12, and 16 weeks. Mean weekly temperature (Tmean) was used as the primary temperature predictor to address strong collinearity between maximum and minimum temperatures (Loja: r = 0.868; Macará: r = 0.957). Including both variables simultaneously resulted in unstable coefficient estimates and inflated standard errors. In preliminary model evaluation, more complex specifications, particularly those including highly collinear temperature variables, were associated with unstable parameter estimates and convergence issues, supporting the use of a simplified and stable modeling framework. Tmean was therefore used to retain the overall thermal signal while ensuring model stability. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using separate models including minimum temperature and maximum temperature individually, each combined with precipitation, to assess robustness and explore differential temperature effects. Correlation analyses were considered exploratory and descriptive, whereas multivariable negative binomial models were used as the primary inferential approach to estimate independent associations between climatic variables and dengue incidence. Associations were expressed as incidence rate ratios (IRR = exp(β)) with 95% confidence intervals and corresponding p-values.

Sources of bias and mitigation strategies

To minimize information bias, officially validated epidemiological and meteorological records from national institutions were used. Weekly aggregation of climate data was applied to harmonize temporal resolution with epidemiological reporting and to reduce the influence of extreme daily measurements.

Potential confounding by unmeasured non-climatic variables was partially addressed through stratification by canton and year, as well as by the use of multivariable regression models, allowing for context-specific assessment of associations. In addition, overdispersion in dengue case counts was explicitly accounted for by applying negative binomial regression models, thereby reducing the risk of biased standard errors and incorrect statistical inference.

Given the high correlation between maximum and minimum temperature, multicollinearity was addressed by using mean weekly temperature as the primary predictor in the main models, complemented by sensitivity analyses including each temperature metric separately. This approach minimized instability in coefficient estimates and improved model interpretability.

Temporal bias was minimized by defining lag structures within the same calendar year and excluding terminal weeks without complete follow-up, thereby avoiding cross-year overlap. However, potential bias related to shared seasonality cannot be fully excluded, particularly in settings with low case counts, where synchronous seasonal patterns between climate variables and dengue incidence may lead to spurious associations rather than true predictive relationships.

The exclusion of weeks with missing meteorological data may have introduced selection bias; however, these omissions were attributed to temporary gaps in station records rather than systematic data loss. Despite these mitigation strategies, limitations inherent to the ecological design persist, including the inability to establish individual-level causal relationships and the potential influence of residual confounding from unmeasured factors such as vector control interventions, human mobility, and changes in surveillance intensity.

In Loja, weekly mean maximum temperature ranged from 14.94 to 22.30 °C, with interannual differences of up to 1.30 °C in annual means. In Macará, weekly mean maximum temperature varied between 21.81 and 28.00 °C, with a wider interannual amplitude reaching 1.67 °C. These differences were statistically significant in both cantons (Loja: F = 16.543; p < 0.001; Macará: F = 12.810; p < 0.001; Table 1). A similar pattern was observed for minimum temperature. In Loja, weekly mean minimum temperature ranged from 14.04 to 17.52 °C, with interannual differences of up to 1.06 °C, whereas in Macará it varied between 20.32 and 25.99 °C, with an amplitude of 1.45 °C. Interannual variability was also statistically significant in both cantons (Loja: F = 19.342; p < 0.001; Macará: F = 8.947; p < 0.001; Table 1).

Post hoc comparisons consistently identified 2021 as the coldest year and 2023 as the warmest year in both cantons, with statistically significant differences relative to multiple comparison years (Additional file 1: Tables S3 and S6). Descriptive clustering using Duncan’s test further supported the presence of three general thermal groupings across the study period, corresponding to cooler (2021–2022), intermediate, and warmer (2023–2024) conditions, indicating a progressive shift toward higher temperature levels in recent years (Table 2).

Accumulated precipitation showed a markedly different pattern between cantons. In Loja, weekly rainfall ranged from 0.0 to 158.7 mm, with moderate interannual variability (F = 3.243; p = 0.008; Table 1). The lowest annual mean precipitation was observed in 2022 (6.75 mm), whereas 2021 represented the wettest year (21.01 mm). In contrast, Macará exhibited substantially greater variability, with weekly precipitation ranging from 0.0 to 308.4 mm and highly significant interannual differences (F = 60.210; p < 0.001; Table 1).

In Macará, rainfall followed a distinct temporal pattern characterized by a progressive decline between 2019 and 2022, followed by a marked increase in 2023, which represented an extreme precipitation year with statistically significant differences compared with all previous years. Although precipitation levels decreased in 2024, they remained significantly higher than those observed during the preceding dry period (Additional file 1: Table S9). Duncan’s test further highlighted this heterogeneity, identifying up to five distinct rainfall groupings in Macará, including 2023 as a clear outlier, whereas Loja exhibited only three partially overlapping subsets (Table 2). Overall, these findings indicate that both cantons experienced significant interannual climatic variability; however, Macará showed greater thermal and especially pluviometric instability, with more pronounced fluctuations and extreme events.

Temporal evolution of dengue cases

The temporal distribution of dengue cases revealed distinct transmission patterns between cantons over the study period, characterized by a progressive intensification of incidence of cases in recent years (Figs. 2a and b and 3a and b). In Loja, dengue activity remained low and sporadic between 2019 and 2022, with limited seasonal clustering and no sustained transmission periods. In 2019, a mild seasonal increase was observed between March and May (N = 30), but incidence remained low overall. During 2020 and 2021, case counts declined further and were characterized by isolated occurrences without temporal continuity. In 2022, a slight increase was observed, although transmission remained fragmented and without a clearly defined epidemic pattern.

A marked change in transmission dynamics was observed from 2023 onward. In that year, dengue incidence increased noticeably, with more consistent temporal clustering of cases, particularly between epidemiological weeks 24 and 36 (approximately June to early September). This pattern became more pronounced in 2024, when a sustained outbreak was observed, with higher weekly case counts and a clearer concentration of cases between epidemiological weeks 10 and 26 (approximately March to June), indicating a transition from sporadic transmission to an epidemic-like pattern (Fig. 2a and b).

In contrast, Macará exhibited a more dynamic and earlier shift in transmission patterns. No dengue cases were reported in 2019. However, dengue activity in 2020 and 2021 already showed greater temporal concentration compared with Loja, with identifiable periods of increased incidence. In 2022, transmission intensified further, with higher case counts and more defined clustering. The most pronounced changes occurred in 2023 and 2024, when Macará experienced earlier and more intense outbreaks compared with Loja, particularly during epidemiological weeks 20 to 34 (approximately May to August) in 2023 and weeks 6 to 24 (approximately February to June) in 2024. These outbreaks were characterized by higher weekly case counts and sharper temporal peaks, reflecting more favorable conditions for sustained transmission. The heatmap representation further illustrates the increasing intensity and concentration of cases in recent years, particularly in Macará (Fig. 3a and b).

Finally, dengue transmission in both cantons evolved from low and irregular patterns toward more structured and intense epidemic dynamics, with earlier onset and greater magnitude in Macará.

Climate-dengue associations and temporal dynamics

Time-series visualizations integrating weekly dengue incidence with climatic variables further supported these inter-canton differences (Figs. 4a and 5b). In Loja, weekly fluctuations in temperature and precipitation showed limited temporal correspondence with dengue incidence, consistent with the low and fragmented transmission pattern observed across most of the study period. In contrast, Macará showed a clearer temporal alignment between climatic variability and dengue activity, particularly in 2023 and 2024, when periods of increased precipitation and sustained warm conditions were followed by sharper rises in weekly case counts. These descriptive patterns suggest a stronger temporal alignment between climatic conditions and dengue transmission in Macará than in Loja.

Lagged analyses revealed heterogeneous and context-dependent associations between climatic variables and dengue incidence, with substantial variability across cantons, years, and lag periods (Tables 3, 4 and 5). In Loja, associations between climatic variables and dengue incidence were generally less consistent and showed no clearly structured temporal pattern. Both maximum and minimum temperature exhibited alternating positive and negative correlations across years and lag periods, without a stable directional trend. Positive associations were observed at longer lags in several years, particularly at 12–16 weeks, whereas inverse associations were more frequent at shorter lags. Covariance values were generally low to moderate, indicating limited joint variability between climatic variables and dengue incidence. Similarly, precipitation showed inconsistent associations, with both inverse and positive correlations depending on the year and lag, and no clear temporal clustering of effects.

In contrast, Macará displayed more structured and temporally coherent associations. Minimum temperature showed predominantly positive correlations with dengue incidence at intermediate and longer lags (8–16 weeks), with higher covariance values indicating stronger joint variability. Notably, inverse associations were observed at lag 0 in some years, suggesting that higher temperatures during the same epidemiological week were not immediately associated with increased dengue incidence, but rather preceded increases in subsequent weeks. Accumulated precipitation in Macará exhibited a more consistent pattern compared with Loja, with positive correlations concentrated at short to intermediate lags (4–8 weeks) and sustained effects up to 16 weeks. These associations were accompanied by higher covariance values, reflecting a stronger degree of shared variability with dengue incidence. In several years, precipitation also showed inverse associations at lag 0, followed by positive correlations at subsequent lags, suggesting a delayed effect consistent with the time required for vector proliferation and transmission dynamics.

Maximum temperature showed more variable associations in both cantons, with no consistent temporal pattern or directional effect. Although some positive correlations were identified at intermediate and longer lags, these were less stable across years compared with minimum temperature and precipitation.

Overall, these findings indicate that climate-dengue relationships are not uniform but depend on local climatic context and temporal structure. In Macará, associations were more consistent and temporally organized, particularly for minimum temperature and precipitation, whereas in Loja, associations were weaker and more heterogeneous. The presence of inverse associations at shorter lags and positive associations at longer lags further supports the importance of delayed effects in the relationship between climatic variables and dengue incidence.

LOESS analyses revealed distinct non-linear patterns between climatic variables and dengue incidence in each canton (Figs. 6 and 7). In Loja, minimum temperature showed a gradual positive trend emerging at higher temperature values, particularly at longer lags (12–16 weeks), whereas maximum temperature exhibited a non-monotonic pattern at shorter lags, with a peak around 18 °C followed by a decline. Precipitation showed no clear directional relationship, and wide confidence intervals reflected substantial uncertainty due to low case counts. In contrast, Macará presented more structured relationships: minimum temperature showed a non-linear association with peak dengue incidence around 24 °C, while precipitation exhibited an inverted U-shaped pattern, with highest incidence observed at intermediate rainfall levels (approximately 100–200 mm/week), declining at very high precipitation values.

Cross-correlation analyses further highlighted these differences in temporal structure between cantons (Figs. 8 and 9). In Loja, cross-correlation patterns were generally weak and relatively symmetric across positive and negative lags for both temperature variables, suggesting the absence of a dominant leading climatic signal and raising the possibility that observed associations may partly reflect shared seasonal variation rather than a clear predictive effect. In contrast, Macará showed a more coherent temporal structure, with accumulated precipitation emerging as the strongest leading climatic signal, reaching its highest correlation at short positive lags and maintaining significant positive correlations across multiple lag periods. Temperature variables in Macará showed weaker signals, consistent with a more limited and lag-dependent contribution compared with precipitation.

These inferential findings were consistent with the temporal structures and non-linear patterns identified in exploratory analyses.

In Loja, mean weekly temperature showed a consistent positive association with dengue incidence across all lag periods, with statistically significant incidence rate ratios (IRRs) ranging from 2.27 at lag 0 to 3.98 at 16 weeks (all p < 0.001; Table 6). The magnitude of association increased progressively with lag, suggesting a cumulative delayed effect of temperature on dengue incidence. In contrast, accumulated precipitation did not show a consistent independent association in multivariable models (all p > 0.07; Table 6), indicating a limited contribution of rainfall to dengue dynamics in this canton when adjusting for temperature. However, this association should be interpreted cautiously given the weak and symmetric temporal structure observed in exploratory analyses, which suggests that part of this relationship may reflect shared seasonal patterns rather than a clear climate-leading effect.

In Macará, accumulated precipitation was the most consistent predictor of dengue incidence, with statistically significant positive associations across all lag periods (IRR ≈ 1.01 per mm; all p < 0.001; Table 7). These findings indicate a strong and persistent association between rainfall and dengue incidence across short- and medium-term lags. Temperature in Macará showed a lag-dependent pattern in multivariable models. Mean temperature was inversely associated with dengue incidence at lag 0 (IRR = 0.71; 95% CI: 0.57–0.88; p = 0.002), non-significant at lags 1–2, and positively associated at longer lags, reaching statistical significance at 12–16 weeks (IRR 1.37–1.55; Table 7). This pattern suggests that higher temperatures do not immediately increase dengue incidence but may contribute to transmission through delayed mechanisms.

Sensitivity analyses using alternative temperature specifications (Tmin and Tmax) confirmed the robustness of the main findings (Additional file 1: Tables S13-S16). In Loja, both Tmin (IRR 2.50–4.90) and Tmax (IRR 2.01–3.16) showed consistent positive associations across all lag periods (all p < 0.001), with slightly higher effect estimates for Tmin. In Macará, Tmin showed a positive association from lag 2 onward (IRR 1.35–1.61), whereas Tmax showed a similar pattern with delayed onset from lag 3 (IRR 1.25–1.48). In all models, precipitation remained statistically significant across all lag periods (all p < 0.001).

Finally, the multivariable models demonstrated clear differences between cantons in the magnitude, direction, and temporal structure of the associations between climatic variables and dengue incidence, highlighting a stronger and more consistent role of precipitation in Macará and a predominantly temperature-driven pattern in Loja.

Discussion

The findings of this study indicate that the relationship between climatic variability and dengue incidence is not uniform across geographically proximate settings, even within the same province. Although both cantons exhibited interannual variability in dengue cases, the temporal structure and magnitude of transmission differed markedly between Loja and Macará, suggesting that local ecological and climatic conditions modulate transmission dynamics beyond simple exposure to temperature and rainfall.

In Macará, dengue incidence showed a more pronounced increase in recent years, with higher weekly case variability and broader epidemic peaks compared to Loja. In contrast, Loja exhibited lower overall incidence and more stable temporal patterns. These differences are consistent with the distinct climatic profiles observed between cantons, characterized by higher temperatures and greater precipitation variability in Macará compared with the cooler and more stable conditions in Loja. These differences are consistent with previous evidence indicating that dengue transmission is highly sensitive to local climatic thresholds, particularly temperature ranges that optimize mosquito survival, biting behavior, and viral incubation periods. Warmer environments tend to accelerate the extrinsic incubation period and increase vectorial capacity, whereas cooler climates may constrain transmission by slowing these biological processes [14]. However, this relationship is not linear, and transmission efficiency depends on maintaining temperatures within optimal ranges rather than simply increasing heat.

The observed heterogeneity between cantons also underscores the role of ecological context in shaping the response of dengue transmission to climatic variability. Studies conducted in diverse settings have shown that regions with climatic conditions closer to the optimal range for Aedes aegypti tend to exhibit stronger and more consistent associations between climate variables and dengue incidence, whereas areas outside these optimal conditions may present weaker or less predictable patterns [1]. This framework provides a plausible explanation for the clearer temporal signals observed in Macará compared to the more attenuated patterns in Loja.

The temporal analysis of dengue incidence in relation to climatic variables revealed that associations were not immediate but instead followed structured lag patterns that varied by canton and by type of climatic exposure. This finding supports the notion that climatic influences on dengue transmission are mediated through biological and ecological processes that unfold over time, rather than producing instantaneous effects on case incidence.

In both cantons, but more consistently in Macará, positive associations between temperature and dengue cases emerged at intermediate and longer lags, suggesting that increases in temperature may contribute to transmission dynamics several weeks after the initial exposure. This pattern is consistent with the time required for vector population growth, viral replication within the mosquito, and subsequent transmission to humans, all of which introduce inherent delays between climatic conditions and observed case counts [5]. The delayed effect of temperature has been widely reported and is often attributed to its role in accelerating the extrinsic incubation period and increasing mosquito activity within biologically favorable thresholds [14].

Precipitation showed a more complex temporal pattern, particularly in Macará, where earlier lag associations (4–8 weeks) were weaker or inconsistent, while stronger positive associations appeared at later lags (8–16 weeks). This delayed response is consistent with the role of rainfall in generating breeding sites, which subsequently lead to increases in vector density after a maturation period. The presence of lagged positive associations aligns with studies indicating that rainfall effects on dengue incidence typically manifest several weeks after accumulation, reflecting the time required for larval development and adult mosquito emergence [5, 15].

In addition, the consistency and strength of the precipitation signal observed in Macará across multiple analytical approaches, including correlation analyses, cross-correlation structure, LOESS patterns, and multivariable models, supports its role as the most consistently associated climatic factor of dengue transmission in this setting. The convergence of these independent methods strengthens the robustness of this finding and suggests that precipitation is consistently associated with temporally structured patterns of dengue incidence. This contrasts with temperature, which showed weaker and lag-dependent associations, reinforcing the context-specific importance of rainfall in semi-arid environments.

In contrast, the temporal structure of associations in Loja was less consistent, with weaker and less directional correlations across lag periods. This finding may reflect the influence of cooler temperatures and more stable climatic conditions, which can limit vector proliferation and reduce the sensitivity of transmission dynamics to short-term climatic fluctuations. Similar patterns have been described in highland or temperate regions, where climatic variability does not always translate into clear epidemiological responses due to suboptimal conditions for sustained transmission [1, 16]. These findings should be interpreted with caution, as the low case burden and the symmetric correlation patterns observed in Loja suggest that the associations may reflect shared seasonal dynamics rather than a true climate-leading effect. Therefore, results from Loja should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

The identification of these lag-dependent associations underscores the importance of incorporating temporal structure into the analysis of climate-dengue relationships. Approaches that consider only contemporaneous associations may underestimate or misrepresent the true influence of climatic variables, as the effects of temperature and precipitation are distributed over time and may interact with each other across different temporal windows.

Studies from the southern coast of Ecuador have shown that minimum temperature, accumulated rainfall, and local ecological conditions explain a relevant proportion of the interannual variability in dengue transmission [16]. At the national level, recent evidence has documented an intensification of dengue across ecologically diverse regions, highlighting spatial heterogeneity in transmission risk and the need for locally tailored approaches [9]. In this context, the patterns observed in Loja and Macará are consistent with the notion that climate-dengue relationships vary across settings and should be characterized at the local level.

At broader spatial scales, climate suitability models indicate a progressive expansion of dengue toward higher altitudes and more temperate regions in South America, driven by increasing temperatures and climatic variability [17]. Similarly, studies in Brazil have linked this expansion to thermal anomalies and intra-seasonal variability [1], while recent analyses suggest that part of the climate-related risk, particularly associated with drought and soil moisture variability, may be attributable to anthropogenic climate change [18]. Within this framework, the differences observed between cantons support the interpretation that local ecological conditions modulate the climatic signal.

Recent studies indicate that dengue transmission is influenced not only by local temperature and precipitation, but also by large-scale atmospheric forcings that shape these variables through teleconnections. A multinational analysis showed that a substantial proportion of dengue variability can be explained by ENSO dynamics, with effects depending on the intensity and spatial configuration of teleconnections [19]. These processes operate through large-scale atmospheric mechanisms that modify regional temperature, humidity, and precipitation patterns [19].

Consistent with this interpretation, a study conducted in Colombia identified strong associations between dengue incidence, ENSO variability, and local climate conditions, with region-specific effects and shorter temporal lags in areas more directly influenced by Pacific Ocean dynamics [20]. That study also demonstrated that these associations are non-stationary and context-dependent, underscoring the need to interpret climate-dengue relationships within their local environmental context [20].

Sub-seasonal variability and short-term atmospheric processes have also been highlighted in predictive models. A hydroclimatic forecasting study in Colombia showed that temperature, precipitation, humidity, and ocean-atmosphere interactions influence dengue incidence through their effects on vector development, viral replication, and human-vector contact patterns [21]. Notably, predictive performance was greater at horizons of three to six months, with sea surface temperature emerging as a key predictor, suggesting that part of the epidemiological signal may originate from remote climatic forcings [21]. Similarly, a spatiotemporal modeling study in Peru identified non-linear and lagged effects of temperature, precipitation, drought, and ENSO-related indices, demonstrating their combined contribution to dengue dynamics and outbreak forecasting [22].

The role of precipitation also appears to be non-linear and context dependent. Evidence from India indicates that moderate rainfall may increase transmission, whereas intense rainfall can reduce vector breeding through flushing effects [23]. Likewise, studies in Brazil have reported dengue outbreaks during relatively dry periods, suggesting that precipitation should be interpreted alongside other climatic and ecological factors [24]. In the present study, rainfall showed a consistent association with dengue incidence in Macará, whereas no significant relationship was observed in Loja, supporting the context-dependent nature of this variable.

At the regional level, ocean-atmosphere interactions have been shown to influence climatic extremes across South America, including rainfall and temperature anomalies, with heterogeneous effects [25]. These processes are particularly relevant for western countries such as Ecuador, where ENSO-related variability can substantially modify local climate conditions [26]. In addition, recent evidence has identified combinations of temperature, precipitation, and humidity associated with increased dengue incidence over temporal windows of up to 20 weeks, reinforcing the importance of considering interactions between climatic variables [27].

From a methodological standpoint, although most comparable studies rely on long-term time series and advanced approaches such as distributed lag non-linear models [5, 28, 29], the period analyzed in this study captures recent epidemiological dynamics under variable climatic conditions. The incorporation of weekly data and lag structures allowed the identification of both immediate and delayed associations. However, the analytical framework did not include an explicit autoregressive component, and therefore residual temporal dependence cannot be fully excluded. Given the relatively short time series and the need to preserve model stability, a parsimonious lag structure was adopted. Previous studies have reported climatic effects extending up to five months, which may not be fully captured within the temporal window considered [5, 15].

This study presents several strengths. The use of official surveillance data, combined with high-resolution climatic records and stratified analyses by canton and year, enabled the identification of distinct and context-dependent patterns. In particular, the more consistent and temporally structured associations observed in Macará, compared with the weaker signals in Loja, highlight the importance of local analyses for understanding dengue dynamics and for informing early warning strategies [30].

At the same time, several limitations should be acknowledged. The absence of key non-climatic variables, including vector control interventions, human mobility, and socioeconomic factors, may have introduced residual confounding. In addition, the analysis did not explicitly incorporate large-scale climatic oscillations or sub-seasonal atmospheric processes, which have been shown to influence local meteorological conditions through indirect mechanisms [5, 14].

From an applied perspective, these findings suggest that surveillance systems should incorporate climate-based thresholds adapted to local conditions [5]. In Macará, precipitation may serve as an early indicator of short-term risk, while the delayed effect of temperature could help anticipate transmission several weeks in advance. In contrast, in Loja, where associations were weaker and less temporally structured, climatic signals should be interpreted in conjunction with additional epidemiological and contextual information [17].

In conclusion, the analysis demonstrates that the relationship between climate and dengue incidence in Loja and Macará is context-dependent and shaped by local ecological conditions. Distinct patterns were identified between cantons: in Macará, precipitation showed a more consistent and temporally coherent association with dengue incidence, while temperature exhibited delayed effects over several weeks; in Loja, climatic associations were weaker and lacked a clear temporal direction. These findings are consistent with international evidence and reinforce the role of climate variability and climate change as key drivers of dengue expansion [1, 14, 18]. The integration of local climatic information into epidemiological surveillance systems is therefore essential to strengthen early warning capacity and improve preparedness in climatically heterogeneous regions.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the analysis covered a relatively short time span (2019–2024), which limits the ability to capture long-term climatic variability, including large-scale oscillatory phenomena such as the El Niño–Southern Oscillation, and their influence on dengue dynamics over extended periods. Second, the analysis was restricted to two cantons due to the availability and continuity of meteorological records, which constrains the geographical generalizability of the results beyond the study area. In addition, a non-negligible proportion of missing climatic data was identified due to gaps in meteorological station records, which required the exclusion of incomplete observations in the analytical models and reduced the number of observations available for some analyses, potentially affecting statistical power.

The structure of the epidemiological data also represents a limitation. Weekly dengue incidence showed heterogeneous temporal distribution, including periods with low case density and a high proportion of weeks without reported cases in one of the study areas, as well as marked interannual variability, including a full year without cases in Macará (2019). These characteristics, together with the observed overdispersion of count data, may limit the stability and precision of more complex statistical models and should be considered when interpreting the results.

In addition, the ecological design precludes inference at the individual level. The absence of key non-climatic covariates—including vector control interventions, human mobility, changes in surveillance intensity, and socioeconomic conditions—may therefore have introduced residual confounding. Additionally, shorter-lived atmospheric processes—such as regional circulation anomalies, synoptic weather events, or the transport of humid air masses between regions—were not explicitly evaluated and may have influenced local climatic conditions during the study period. This is particularly relevant given the heterogeneous patterns observed between cantons, which may reflect the influence of contextual factors not captured in the models. Although multivariable models were incorporated to address combined effects of climatic variables, residual confounding and complex non-linear interactions may not have been fully captured. Moreover, underreporting during 2020 related to healthcare disruptions associated with the COVID-19 pandemic may have influenced observed temporal trends in dengue incidence.

Although lagged negative binomial models and cross-correlation analyses were used to explore delayed associations, an explicit autoregressive component was not incorporated; therefore, residual temporal dependence cannot be fully excluded. Furthermore, ANOVA was applied as a descriptive method to compare interannual climatic variability and does not account for temporal autocorrelation. Lag structures were specified using discrete intervals (0, 4, 8, 12, and 16 weeks), which, while biologically interpretable and aligned with vector–virus–host dynamics, may not capture finer week-to-week variation or the full shape of the lag–response relationship. These analytical choices were made to balance interpretability, biological plausibility, and model stability, given the characteristics of the dataset, including limited time series length, missing data, and heterogeneous case distribution. Future studies with longer time series, improved data completeness, and higher event density should consider continuous lag approaches, as well as autoregressive or distributed lag nonlinear models, to more comprehensively characterize temporal dependence and lag–response relationships.

Conclusions

This study suggests that the relationship between climate variability and dengue incidence in southern Ecuador is heterogeneous, context-dependent, and strongly structured by temporal dynamics. Clear differences were identified between Loja and Macará, suggesting that dengue transmission is associated with local climatic and ecological conditions rather than following uniform patterns across geographically close settings.

Minimum temperature and accumulated precipitation emerged as the most consistently associated climatic factors, but their associations were not uniform. In Macará, precipitation showed consistent associations across short- and medium-term lags with dengue incidence, while temperature exhibited delayed and cumulative associations, being associated with higher dengue incidence several weeks later. In contrast, Loja presented weaker and less temporally structured associations, with temperature-related patterns distributed across both contemporaneous and delayed periods without a clear directional pattern, and no significant association with precipitation.

These findings highlight that climatic associations with dengue operate through distinct temporal patterns, including immediate, delayed, and persistent dynamics, which are compatible with underlying biological processes such as vector development, viral replication, and habitat availability. The convergence of results across exploratory correlations, multivariable regression models, cross-correlation analyses, and non-linear patterns provides coherent evidence of these associations and supports their epidemiological interpretation.

From a public health perspective, the results support the integration of climate-based indicators into surveillance systems, particularly in settings such as Macará, where rainfall and temperature provide temporally coherent signals that could be used for short- and medium-term risk anticipation. However, the contrasting patterns observed in Loja emphasize that climatic signals must be interpreted within their local context and should not be generalized across regions.

Overall, this study provides evidence that dengue transmission in climatically heterogeneous regions is associated with complex, non-linear, and time-dependent interactions between environmental factors and local ecological conditions. These findings reinforce the need for region-specific analytical approaches and for the incorporation of temporal dynamics into early warning systems. Future research should integrate climatic and non-climatic variables such as vector control, human mobility, and population immunity within non-linear modeling frameworks to improve short-term predictive capacity.
