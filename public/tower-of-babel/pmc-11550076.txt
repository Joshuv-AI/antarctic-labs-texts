Evaluating physico-mechanical properties of NaOH-treated natural fibres: Effects of polyolefin
Authors: Md Zillur Rahman, Md Abdul Hannan, Mohammad Zahirul Islam Mollah, Mohammad Bellal Hoque, Khulud Saad Al-mugren, Mohammad Rashed Iqbal Faruque
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The bottle gourd plant fibres (BGPF) and okra fibres were processed and refined (w/w 6 % NaOH) before being incorporated with polyolefin (polypropylene) for composite fabrication using a blending technique. The polyolefin matrix is used to develop composites with 5 % okra fibres and varying percentages (25, 30, 35 and 40 %) of BGPF. The results indicated that the "35 % BGPF +5 % okra +60 % polypropylene" composition achieved remarkable mechanical properties with tensile strength (26.95 MPa), tensile modulus (3.16 GPa), decreased elongation at break (1.71 %), bending strength (52.53 MPa), bending modulus (3.45 GPa), impact strength (14.25 kJ/m2), and hardness (69 D-shore). Moreover, these composites absorbed minimal water when a specific portion was submerged. The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) test on the composites revealed good fibre adhesion and adherence content between BGPF and okra fibres. Additionally, the thermo-gravimetric analysis and differential scanning calorimetry exhibited weight loss of composites during maximum cellulose degradation (80 %) at 483 °C. In the composites of the fibre-PP matrix, two distinct physical changes were observed indicating glass transition and degradation. However, the mechanical properties decreased after soil degradation. Thus, remarkable properties were achieved for the fibres-fibres and fibres-polyolefin (polypropylene) interfacial interactions.

Introduction

A composite is formed by blending two or more fibres with excellent characteristics. Such composites are slowly becoming popular in various industries and factories, as they are perceived as a novel invention of modern science. Its popularity is attributed to it being lighter than the traditional metals-based materials with decay counteraction, and chemical counteraction, bearing more weight, being durable, and being environment-friendly. Before the invention of polymer-based reinforced natural fibre composite materials, wood, metal, plastic, and concrete were employed for their practical application. Although combining polymer with natural or synthetic fibres produces a reinforced composite that is safe, sound, and conducive to a sustainable society [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]], newer alternatives are sought due to the issues with these synthetic reinforced composite materials. Meanwhile, petroleum-based composites are not cost-effective, and higher density of synthetic polymeric materials are also insalubrious environmentalists worldwide are against it because it is not environmentally friendly [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. Therefore, the search for composites using natural fibres to replace artificial fibres continues. Different types of natural fibres like bottle gourd, jute, okra [10], flax [11], and pineapple leaf fibres [12,13] have been blended in different ways for composite fabrication. Researchers are also experimenting with developing fibres-based polymers incorporating polymer matrix with natural fibres. Natural fibre-based polymers have benefits including lightweight, low manufacturing and materials, lower plumpness, excellent mechanical characteristics, lower petrochemical content, and damping. Furthermore, it is renewable, frictionless, reduces material wastage, eco-friendly etc [14,15].

People leave raw materials in places that are neither directly used nor welfare for humans. Such raw materials are being tested to create new materials that could be useful for human welfare, instead of becoming a waste. The bottle gourd plant fibres (BGPF) and okra plant fibres are considered farming dissipation. BGPF is green when raw and golden in colour when water-soaked and dried. When the naturally tall BGPF is soaked in water, the fibres can be pulled out from each knot to form 6–8 inches. These fibres present a high cellulose content (79.91 %) with 12.69 % hemicellulose which is important for reinforcing composites. The BGPF is suitable for composite manufacturing because it contains low lignin (7.62 %) fibre content. The additional chemical elements of this fibre include 0.31 % wax, 9.96 % moisture, and 0.92 % ash. This fibre has excellent mechanical characteristics like good tensile strength and temperature tolerance due to its density at 1.216 % [16,17].

In 2018, Saravanan N analysed bottle gourd and studied the surface modification on Lagenaria siceraria (LS) fibres. The Lagenaria siceraria sp was explored and characterised as agro-waste (the plant's stem). He demonstrated that the density of the raw LS fibres was 1.216 kg/m3, lower than that of the other fibres. The tensile strength of this fibre was 257–517 MPa with Young's modulus of 7–42 GPa. The fibre also demonstrated a melting temperature of 180–320 °C, 79.9–86 % cellulose content, 12.69 to 14.23 wt% reduction of hemicellulose content and 7.62 to 6.98 wt%. lignin content. He demonstrated and validated the application of products made of bottle-gourd fibres like industrial safety helmets, two-wheeler rider helmets, and two-wheeler bumpers. Since the 79.9 % cellulose content of LS fibre exhibited excellent tensile strength (257–717) MPa, thermal stability (339.1 °C), crystalline index (92.4 %), and crystalline size (7.2 nm), it was adopted by industries due to its effective lightweight, and eco-friendly composites [18]. In another study, the hybrid composites of sponge gourd, coir, and jute was fabricated using the hand lay-up technique to predict the use of these composites. The study utilised Taguchi analysis to optimise the combination of Wt% ratio of resin & hardener: 1:1, Wt% of resin & hardener in composite: 91 %, Wt% ratio of sponge gourd & jute: 3:1, and Wt% ratio of sponge gourd & coir: 1:1. The final maximum Brinell hardness (71.62) was in good agreement with the predicted responses with 4.58 % deviation [19].

Abelmoschus esculentus is an okra fruit tree, used vegetables are growing in all places in the world and all areas of Bangladesh. The tree is either discarded or used as fuel at the end of the fruiting season. The length of an okra tree is about 3–5 feet. Okra trees are usually collected from agricultural land and soaked in water for 15–20 days. Fibres are composed of soaked trees. The 60–70 % cellulose in okra fibre plays an important role in producing reinforced composite materials. Moreover, it has 15–20 %, 5–10 % lignin, and 3.4 % pectin. This fibre also contains 3.9 % fats. The density is 1.15–1.45 and the diameter of these fibres is 1.15–1.45 (g/cm3), 40–180 (μm). The moisture content of this fibre was estimated at 4–6 %. Okra fibres are suitable for composites, as they decay at very high temperatures, 220 °C [20].

In another study, a hybrid composite material was fabricated using jute, okra, and pineapple leaf fibres (PALF) and polypropylene (PP). A moulding technique was applied to produce the jute/PP, okra/PP, and PALF/PP composites. The mechanical test results for jute/PP composites included tensile strength (45 MPa), tensile modulus (2.2 GPa), bending strength (54 MPa), bending modulus (4.1 GPa), elongation at break (11 %), and impact strength (16 kJ/m2). Jute/PP also recorded 16.83 % and 152 % improvement of TS and TM over okra/PP composites. The composite hardness (Shore A) of okra/PP composites was also better than that of the jute/PP composites. Comparatively, PALF/PP composites exhibited increased TS, TM, BS, and IS properties by 242.11 %, 226.92 %, 100 %, and 1383.14 %, respectively. In short, the tensile strength was higher in PALF/PP composites (68.83 %) > jute/PP composite (16.88 %) > okra/PP composite [21].

In the automotive industry, the BGPF and okra plant fibres reinforced with polypropylene (PP) are employed to produce various automotive parts. This composite can substitute plastic and metal, such as industrial safety helmets, wheeler rider helmets, wheeler bumpers, and wheeler bumper frames [25]. Among the complexities related to manufacturing composites with BGPF and okra plant fibres include primary complexity and hydrophilicity. However, such complexities can be removed from the fibres by adding various chemicals for treatment. Despite the initial intricacies, the reinforced polymer-based composites, BGPF and okra plant fibres, have a massive dynamic [22,23].

Matrix is a critical and indispensable component for composite materials. The fibres or fibres layers and other material layers can significantly strengthen and improve the properties of the matrix. Polymer-based composites can be produced from various polymer matrices and fibres. The polymer matrix is a PP hard layer and granule [24], polyethene solid layer [25], disjointed polyester resin, epoxy resin liquid, and nylon. Many polymer matrices with composite fabrication have been used in various studies. Scientists prefer PP due to its features over the available polymeric matrices. PP has been used extensively in structural elements [[26], [27], [28], [29]] and the thermoplastic composites industry. The beneficial features of PP include low density, excellent flux life, good surface hardness, high softening point, scratch resistance, better friction counteraction, low wetness pickup, and lofty impact potency [30,31]. Therefore, scientists prefer PP sheets for reinforced PP-based composites.

Periphery alteration of natural fibres enhances the mechanical characteristics of the polymer composite material matrix. The weak parts of natural fibres are eliminated during this step due to the shift in the periphery. The weak elements of natural fibres like lignin, peat in, and hemicellulose reduce the hydrophilic characteristic of the fibres [32]. Hence, natural fibres are subjected to chemical treatment to be made into composite materials. Alkali treatment is the most popular and economical fibre treatment method available. This treatment removes materials from the fibres that inhibit the formation of composites by slightly changing the mechanical characteristics of the fibres. Enhancements of the fibre periphery hardness clean the outside of the fibres. This treatment also increases the surface roughness of the fibres to create better fibres/matrix adhesion. The better the adhesion between filler/matrix, the greater the energy absorption during impact fracture [33].

Due to the dynamic importance of using natural fibres in the land of biodegradable composite materials, the agricultural dissipation of bottle gourd and okra plants was selected to develop a hybrid composite made of BGFP and okra. The physicomechanical attitude analysis of the composite materials was also evaluated in this study. It is novel to use fibres to develop composites, as it adds new avenues to the variety of available composites. However, the physico-mechanical properties of bottle gourd and okra fibre-reinforced polymer-based composites are still untouched. Therefore, this study aims to assess the physico-mechanical and soil degradation properties of bottle gourd and okra fibre-reinforced polymer-based composites. Thermo-gravimetric analysis was also conducted to evaluate the effects of heat on the composites. SEM was employed to determine the fibre-matrix adhesion of the fabricated composites. The water uptake behaviour of prepared composites was also measured. Overall this study investigated the development of sustainable composite materials as an alternative to synthetic materials in engineering sectors.

Refinement of BGPFand okra plant fibres

The bottle gourd and okra plant fibres were washed with water several times to dispel the impermanent granules like natural oil, wax, dirt, and filth. After drying, the fibres were cut into 8-10-inch fibres. The fibres were submerged for 3 h in an alkali solution containing 6 % NaOH (w/w) before drying at 60 °C for 24 h. This step removes lignin, non-cellulosic material, and other impurities from the BGPF and okra fibres. Moreover, the NaOH treatment also smoothens the surface of the fibre.

PP-details, PP-sheet production

The properties of PP include the density of PP (0.89–0.91 g/cm3), tensile strength yield (50 mm/min): 36 MPa, elongation yield (50 mm/min): 10 %, bending modulus: 1650 MPa), impact strength (23 °C): 27 J/m, and melting point (130–170 °C). The PP sheet was produced using a heat-press machine at 180–200 °C and 5 tons’ of consolidation pressure (Fig. 2: Flow chart for composite preparation).Fig. 2Flow chart for PP sheet and PP-fibres composite production.Fig. 2

Flow chart for PP sheet and PP-fibres composite production.

Fabrication of composites

The composite was prepared by adding 5 % okra with 25, 30, 35, and 40 % of bottle gourd fibres for blending. The blended fibres and PP granules at 70, 66, 60, and 55 % were placed between two silicone papers to form a solid sheet using a heat press machine. The mixture of BGPF, okra fibres and PP sheet was performed using a heat press machine at 180–200 °C and 5 tons of consolidated pressure. The plates were left to cool under the same pressure for 10 min in a separate press machine. Then a mixture of fibres placed between the two PP sheets was transferred to the heat press machine to maintain the structure of the sheets. Firstly, PP sheets were produced by distributing PP solid granules in a template, whereas BGPF and okra composite using “BGPF + okra'' blended fibres. The hybrid composites were fabricated with different compositions, namely 70 % PP+25 % BGPF+ 5 % okra, 65 % PP + 30 % BGPF +5 % okra, 60 % PP + 35 % BGPF +5 % okra, and 55 % PP + 40 % BGPF +5 % okra.

Mechanical properties of composites

The tensile strength, bending strength, tensile modulus, bending modulus, and elongation at the break % of bottle gourd and okra plant fibres reinforced with PP-based composites were assessed. The tensile properties were measured using a Universal Testing Machine (AG-X Plus, 250KN-300KN, Shimadzu, Japan) under ASTMD638. This machine has a universal joint capacity of 300 kN, and an upper and lower head weight of 40 kg. The sample measurement of 165 mm × 13 mm × 4 mm was maintained using a dice. The composite bending properties (bending strength and bending modulus) were assessed using the same machine with a standard ASTMD790. The sample measurement used for this test was 127 mm × 12.7 mm × 4 mm. Four sample readings were averaged for every mechanical property test in this study.

Water uptake profile of composites

A water absorption test was conducted to determine the percentage absorbed by this composite. This sample of bottle gourd and okra plant fibres-reinforced PP-based composites was immersed in a beaker containing 500 mL of distilled water at room temperature for seven days. The weight of the samples was measured before immersion and at every 24-h interval. Once the samples were removed from the water, they were wiped with tissue paper and reweighed. The sample measurement for this analysis was 40 mm × 40 mm × 4 mm. The water uptake of the composites was assessed using a standard ASTMD570-98. Water uptake results were calculated using the formula:(1)Wateruptake(%)=Wetweight−DryweightDryweight×100

Scanning electron microscopic (SEM) analysis

The bottle gourd and okra plant fibre-reinforced PP composite structure was tested using SEM (SU1510, USA) with an acceleration voltage of 5 kV. The sample measurement for this analysis was 10 mm × 10 mm × 4 mm. The composite samples were placed on an aluminium disc plate.

Impact test of composites

The impact test measures various properties of this composite such as toughness, hardness, ductility, and strength. The IZOD impact tester assessed the impact test of BGPF and okra plant fibre-reinforced PP-based composites (ASTD56, Taiwan). The experiment consisted of a pendulum hanging hammer, attached to the frame for support and a measuring system with a 90° angle to measure the absorbed energy. The specimen size employed for this test was 80 mm × 10 mm × 4 mm. The sample was attached to a cantilever beam, where the hammer was released at 90°. When the hammer impact broke the sample, the pointer was displaced with an angle, before the test result was calculated.

Hardness test of composites

The hardness, viscoelasticity, shape, and quality of composites were analysed using hardness (Shore D-Durometer, USA). BGPF and okra plant fibre-reinforced PP-based composites were tested using the Shore D-Durometer Hardness machine with a standard ATMD2240. The shore D-Durometer method is used for more rigid materials. Firstly, the composite was placed on a hard flat surface together with an indenter and pressed to ensure it was parallel to the surface. Then, the sample's firm contact time and hardness of the composite were measured using a meter by applying pressure between the tools at different locations on the composite. The assessed stiffness was calculated using the Shore-D scale. Finally, the readings were averaged to obtain the final results.

Thermo-gravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetric (DSC) analysis of composites

The TGA of BGPF, okra plant fibres, and PP-based composites was performed using a Simultaneous Thermal Analyser (TA- SDT-650, USA). The physical changes in the mass of these composites were recorded over temperature vs. time by TGA when heated. The energy absorbed by these composites when heated or cooled (DSC analysis), was then measured using the same machine. This analysis of the BGPF and okra PP-based composite sample was taken at 10 mg in a melting pot heating rate of 10 °C/min in an N2-gas atmosphere of 5–500 °C.

Soil degradation of the composite

An experiment was conducted to determine the degradation rate of BGPF and okra plant fibres-reinforced PP-based composites in soil. To assess soil degradation, the composite sample was cut into 127 mm × 12.7 mm × 4 mm pieces which were then buried 6–7 inches deep in the soil. The moisture content of the soil was measured at 24 %–26 % where the samples were kept buried for up to 16 weeks. After 16 weeks, the samples were extracted from the soil, washed with pure distilled water, and dried for seven days. The samples were then kept at room temperature for 24 h for conditioning before the tensile characteristics were determined using a Shimadzu testing machine (Model AG-X Plus, 250KN-300KN, Japan).

Results and discussion

Refinement of the fibres

The surface of the fibres became rougher due to the chemical treatment, which improved its adherence to the matrix. The discontinuity particle-fibres-reinforced filler degrades the material strength when the homogeneity of the materials deteriorates. Thus, the impact strength can be increased through alkaline-treated fibres's superior stress transfer and dispersion compared to untreated fibres. The better adhesion between filler/matrix can yield greater energy absorption during impact fracture [33]. Alkali treatment separates the individual fibres in the bundles as it increases the aspect ratio of the smaller fibre particles and the roughness of the fibre surface, contributing to a stronger interfacial bond between the fibres and the matrix material [34]. The hydrophilicity of the fibres remains the key obstacle in developing natural fibre-reinforced composite (NFRC). NFRCs and reduced adhesion capacity due to hydrophilicity impact the composite's mechanical characteristics. The most efficient treatments to overcome the obstacle are chemical-based as they remove the contaminants and wax content from the fibre surface resulting in a rough and hydrophobic surface. The reinforced BGPF possesses a high content of α - cellulose content and a low microfibrillar angle (8.52–25°). The improved mechanical properties were achieved due to the uniform distribution of stress, whereby an adequate amount of load was transferred from fibre to fibre due to the uniform distribution of the applied force [37]. Moreover, the cellulose content of BGPF fibres with a low microfibrillar angle contributed significantly to this augmentation of mechanical properties. Soil degradation

Small sections of BGPF and okra plant fibres-reinforced PP-based composites were buried underground for 16 weeks. The samples were then subjected to a TS test (Fig. 9). The image revealed an unusually proportional reduction in the TS value of this composite to time. Following 16 weeks of degradation, the TS value of BGPF and okra plant fibres-reinforced PP-based composite was reduced by approximately 30.74 %, 29.14 %, 30.57 %, and 35.12 %. The composite suffered a significant rate of strength loss after 4 and 8 months of degradation. This composite was found in 28.50, 5.71, 11.40, and 8.50 % TS reduction. TGA analysis of composites

The TGA test was employed to determine the thermal degradation and thermal stability of reinforced PP-based composites blended with bottle gourd and okra fibres (25, 30, 35, and 40 wt%). Fig. 11 represents the TGA analysis results. The curves in the figure represent each composite thermal degradation and thermal stability. The temperature varied from 5 °C to 500 °C for each sample. The thermal decomposition of this composite consists of a two-step process that occurs under a nitrogen (N2 gas) atmosphere due to the fibre content and fibre types. The okra and bottle gourd fibres have different melting points and phase transitions. The initial results of this analysis revealed weight loss of 1.074 %, 2.152 %, 0.1.315 %, and 0.777 % at 100 °C due to the evaporation of moisture content or water from within the fibres [48]. As the temperature heat flow increased, the weight of fibres decreased to 11.23, 23.01, 30.48 and 23.58 % respectively at a temperature between 300 and 400 °C. Another primary reason for the weight loss of the composites was the thermal degradation of hemicellulose, which was observed between 400 and 500 °C, where the weight of the composites decreased by 80.51 %, 61.20 %, 50.028 % and 61.80 %, these findings are supported by the researcher where they obtained between 30 % and 35 % respectively based on the fibre content [49]. The highest weight loss of 81.50 % was recorded due to the degradation of cellulose at a heat flow increased up to 500 °C for the composite of "25 % BGPF+ 5 % Okra +70 % PP" and the lowest (50 %) weight loss obtained for the composition of "35 % BGPF + 5 % Okra +60 % PP". The phenomenon of fibre weight loss was observed between 250 and 500 °C. Comparatively, the TGA results of these composites were more significant than other natural fibres such as bamboo, hemp, jute, and kenaf fibres, where weight loss was observed at 321 °C, 308.2 °C, 298.2 °C, and 309.2 °C respectively. Nevertheless, a small degradation of the composites occurred after 483 °C, in which the samples lost 13 % in weight due to the decomposition of the lignin and wax in the fibres [50]. There was an insignificant degradation after 400 °C, because of the partial decomposition of lignin and other organic content of the fibres with a weight loss of about 8–12 % [51]. DSC analysis of composites

The DSC tests were performed on reinforced PP-based composites with 25, 30, 35, and 40 wt% fabrication of "BGPF and blended okra plant fibres". Two physical changes were observed during the DSC tests, the glass transition and the degradation of composite materials [52]. The decomposition at the onset and offset temperature [53] varied due to the composite's different fibre contents and matrix amounts. The first phase transition point (onset temperature) was observed at 164, 163, 164, and 164 °C, and the offset temperature point was observed at 182.64, 182.62, 182.64, 182.64 °C for the 25, 30, 35, 40 % BGPF and 5 % Okra fibres composites respectively. The physical changes for glass transition and degradation points (onset and offset) were recorded for the different compositions of BGPF and % okra fibre composites. The lowest (onset) and the highest (offset) temperatures were recorded depending on the content of the fibres and matrix for the glass transition and degradation (Fig. 11 a, b, c, d). The melting point is a range of temperature, usually polypropylene melting temperature is 160–170 °C [54,55]. This composite showed a melting point at a temperature ranging from 163± °C to 182 ±°C. The degradation was observed in the 441± °C to 483 ± °C. The composite's melting temperature (phase transition) was almost the same in all cases. However, okra and bottle gourd fibre contents may have distinct melting points, causing a two-stage degradation (phase transition).

Conclusions

This study demonstrated remarkable results in terms of, the strength of polyolefin with the natural fibres of bottle gourd and okra plant fibre composites. Excellent interaction of tensile and bending features was observed for 5 % okra +35 % bottle gourd+ 60 % PP-based composites. This study proved that the BGPF and okra plant fibre composite fabrications can improve tensile and bending capabilities while maintaining the cellulose at 79.91 % and hardness at 60–70 %. With a small amount of water absorption, the tensile strength decreased trend after soil degradation and thermal properties indicated physical changes of the composites. These remarkable findings led to the development of fibre-based composite materials fit for engineering applications. However, there are challenges in achieving consistent results due to fibre dispersion, moisture absorption, dimensional instability and incompatibility between the hydrophilic (okra and bottle gourd) fibres and hydrophobic polyolefin matrix. The inconsistent testing and quality of fibre, the fluctuating cost and accessibility of fibres derived from bottle gourd and okra plants, and the potential deterioration of the fibre's decreased heat resistance. Moreover, the homogeneous stress distribution of the okra and bottle gourd fibres decreased due to the adherence of fibres containing polyolefin groups. Composite testing may or may not be acceptable or beneficial depending on the consistency of the results. The measured properties of the composites may include exhibit mistakes, uncertainties, and variability due to these limitations and challenges.
