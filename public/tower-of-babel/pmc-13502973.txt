The Bacterial, Viral, and Microbiome Contributions in Cardiovascular Diseases: A Narrative Review
Authors: Shyamala Ravikoti, Vikas Bhatia, Mohanasundari Saykkulandai Kuppuswamy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. While traditional risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, and lifestyle behaviors are well-documented, emerging evidence highlights the role of microbial agents in contributing to cardiovascular risk. This narrative review explores the epidemiological evidence linking various microbes, including bacteria, viruses, and other pathogens, to cardiovascular diseases. The review focuses on mechanisms such as chronic inflammation, molecular mimicry, endothelial dysfunction, and direct microbial invasion of cardiovascular tissues. By synthesizing data from recent studies, this paper aims to focus the significance of microbes in the context of cardiovascular health, identify gaps in current knowledge, and suggest directions for future research.

INTRODUCTION

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain the leading global cause of mortality, accounting for an estimated 17.9 million deaths annually, representing 32% of all deaths worldwide. Of these deaths, approximately 85% result from heart attacks and strokes, with the majority occurring in low- and middle-income countries. Furthermore, 38% of premature deaths from noncommunicable diseases in 2019 were attributed to CVDs, underscoring the urgent need to address modifiable behavioral and environmental risk factors, including tobacco use, unhealthy diets, obesity, physical inactivity, harmful alcohol consumption, and air pollution. Early detection and timely management through counseling, lifestyle modification, and pharmacological interventions remain essential strategies for reducing the global burden of CVD.[1]

In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including India, the cardiovascular burden is further compounded by a high prevalence of infectious exposures and limited access to preventive care. Helicobacter pylori seroprevalence exceeds 60–70% in many South Asian populations, while chronic periodontal disease remains widespread due to constrained access to dental services.[234] Additionally, gut microbiome composition and metabolite profiles, including trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO) production, are strongly influenced by dietary transitions and socio-economic heterogeneity.[56] At the same time, disparities in access to statins, influenza vaccination, and structured infection-control and preventive services may limit the translation of emerging microbiome- and infection-targeted cardiovascular prevention strategies into routine clinical practice in these settings[78]

While traditional cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and smoking are well established in the pathogenesis of CVD, emerging evidence suggests that infectious agents and the human microbiome may also play an important role in cardiovascular risk and disease progression. Several bacterial pathogens, including Helicobacter pylori, Chlamydia pneumoniae, and Porphyromonas gingivalis, have been implicated in vascular inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and the development of atherosclerosis. Similarly, viral infections such as herpesviruses, influenza viruses, cytomegalovirus, hepatitis C virus, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) have been associated with increased cardiovascular risk through mechanisms involving chronic inflammation, immune activation, and vascular injury. In recent years, the gut microbiome has also emerged as a critical determinant of cardiovascular health, with microbial metabolites such as trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO) being linked to atherosclerotic processes and adverse cardiovascular outcomes.[9]

Epidemiological studies have demonstrated positive associations between markers of infection and increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality.[10] Experimental evidence further supports the role of microbes in accelerating atherosclerosis (AS), a chronic progressive disease of the arterial wall characterized by the accumulation of lipids, inflammatory cells, fibrous tissue, and calcium within the intima of medium- and large-sized arteries, leading to the formation of atheromatous plaques.[11] Microbial factors may contribute to CVD through multiple biological mechanisms, including chronic low-grade inflammation, molecular mimicry, endothelial dysfunction, metabolic alterations, and direct microbial invasion of vascular tissues.[1112]

Despite increasing recognition of the microbial–cardiovascular connection, current knowledge remains fragmented, with studies dispersed across the fields of microbiology, immunology, and cardiovascular medicine. Many existing reviews focus on individual pathogens or isolated mechanisms, leaving a need for a comprehensive synthesis of both mechanistic and epidemiological evidence linking microbial infections and microbiome dysbiosis with CVD risk.[13]

Therefore, this narrative review aims to synthesize current mechanistic and epidemiological evidence linking microbial pathogens and gut microbiome dysbiosis to cardiovascular disease and to discuss their implications for cardiovascular risk assessment, prevention strategies, and future public health interventions.

METHODS

This narrative review summarizes current evidence on the role of microbial infections and the microbiome in CVDs. A literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, and Scopus for studies published between January 2000 and March 2025. Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and Boolean operators (“AND,” “OR”) were used with terms including “Cardiovascular Diseases,” “Atherosclerosis,” “Microbiome,” “Microbial Infection,” “Gut Dysbiosis,” “Chlamydia pneumoniae,” “Helicobacter pylori,” “Porphyromonas gingivalis,” “Cytomegalovirus,” “Epstein–Barr Virus,” “Human Immunodeficiency Virus,” “SARS-CoV-2,” “Inflammation,” “Endothelial Dysfunction,” “Molecular Mimicry,” and “Trimethylamine-N-oxide.” Manual searches of reference lists were also performed.

Included studies examined associations between microbial factors and CVDs, including observational studies, randomized controlled trials, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses published in English-language peer-reviewed journals. Duplicate studies, conference abstracts, editorials, letters, non-peer-reviewed articles, and studies without specific cardiovascular outcomes were excluded. Titles, abstracts, and full texts were screened for relevance, and findings were synthesized narratively focusing on microbial mechanisms, inflammatory pathways, clinical outcomes, and preventive strategies.

MECHANISTIC PATHWAYS LINKING MICROBES TO CARDIOVASCULAR DISEASE

Microbes contribute to CVD through chronic inflammation, molecular mimicry, direct invasion, and altered lipid metabolism. These factors play central roles in the development of AS and other cardiovascular complications.

Chronic Inflammation: Microbial infections frequently cause systemic inflammation, which is a major contributor to the pathophysiology of atherosclerosis. Pathogens, such as bacteria, viruses, and fungi, stimulate the host immune system, resulting in the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and other immune mediators. People with microbial infections frequently have elevated levels of markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), and these markers are also linked to an increased risk of cardiovascular events. Chronic inflammation encourages endothelial dysfunction, a process that damages the inner lining of blood vessels and makes it easier for plaques to form in the arteries, which accelerates the progression of atherosclerosis and raises the risk of cardiovascular events, including myocardial infarction and stroke[1112] [Figure 1].

Conceptual framework: From microbial exposure to atherosclerosis and cardiovascular events via chronic inflammation

Molecular Mimicry: Microbial pathogens, including specific bacteria and viruses, can initiate autoimmune reactions through molecular mimicry, where their antigens closely resemble host proteins. This resemblance may prompt the immune system to mistakenly attack the body’s own tissues, particularly within the cardiovascular system. For instance, proteins from Streptococcus pyogenes can mimic cardiac myosin, triggering an immune response that damages heart tissue and contributes to conditions such as rheumatic heart disease. Similarly, infections caused by microbes like Chlamydia pneumoniae have been associated with atherosclerosis, potentially due to molecular mimicry leading to endothelial cell damage and plaque formation in the arteries.[12]

Direct Invasion: Certain pathogens can directly invade endothelial cells and cardiomyocytes, leading to localized inflammation and tissue damage. For example, Chlamydia pneumoniae, Helicobacter pylori, and the HIV can infect vascular endothelial cells, triggering inflammatory responses that contribute to the formation of atherosclerotic plaques. This invasion not only intensifies inflammation but also leads to endothelial dysfunction, a key precursor to AS. Additionally, some viral infections, such as those caused by Coxsackievirus, can result in myocarditis, which may progress to dilated cardiomyopathy and heart failure.[14]

Altered Lipid Metabolism: Microbial infections can impact lipid metabolism, a vital process for cardiovascular health. A key example is the production of trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO) by gut microbiota during the breakdown of dietary choline, which is present in red meat and certain other foods. TMAO has been found to interfere with lipid metabolism by increasing low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol levels and promoting the formation of foam cells—lipid-laden cells that contribute to atherosclerotic plaque development. Elevated TMAO levels are linked to greater plaque instability, making them more susceptible to rupture, which can result in thrombosis and cardiovascular events.[15] Additionally, other microbial metabolites may also alter lipid profiles and contribute to plaque destabilization, further elevating cardiovascular risk.[16]

BACTERIAL INFECTIONS AND CARDIOVASCULAR RISK

Chlamydia pneumoniae

Research has identified Chlamydia pneumoniae within atherosclerotic plaques, suggesting its involvement in CVD.[17] Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and immunohistochemistry have detected C. pneumoniae DNA and antigens in atheromatous lesions, which are rare in normal arterial tissue. Its presence is linked to vascular inflammation and coronary heart disease (CHD) progression.

Epidemiological studies show a higher prevalence of C. pneumoniae infection in smokers, indicating a link between smoking and chronic infection. Additionally, C. pneumoniae alters lipid metabolism by lowering high-density lipoprotein (HDL) and raising triglycerides, potentially worsening metabolic syndrome’s impact on CHD.[18] Some studies highlight interactions between C. pneumoniae and established CHD risk factors like diabetes, dyslipidemia, and smoking.[19] A significant association has been observed between C. pneumoniae seropositivity and diabetes in CHD patients over 50 years. High titers of C. pneumoniae IgA (≥1:64) and IgG (≥1:256) have also been linked to an increased risk of myocardial infarction, particularly when detected 1–5 years before the event.[20]

At the cellular level, C. pneumoniae activates endothelial cells by stimulating mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways (ERK1/2, p38-MAPK, JNK), leading to increased interleukin-8 (IL-8) expression, endothelial inflammation, and thrombosis. The infection is also associated with elevated matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) in coronary plaques, which may contribute to plaque destabilization and vascular remodeling, increasing cardiovascular risk.[21]

Antibiotic therapy targeting Chlamydia pneumoniae, particularly azithromycin, has been investigated as a potential strategy to reduce cardiovascular risk due to its antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties.[22] Although treatment has been shown to reduce inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) in C. pneumoniae-positive coronary artery disease (CAD) patients, large randomized clinical trials, including the ACADEMIC trial, demonstrated no significant reduction in cardiovascular events or antibody titers following azithromycin therapy.[23] This null finding is important because it challenges the hypothesis that C. pneumoniae plays a direct causal role in AS. Instead, it suggests that the pathogen may act as a contributing or associative factor rather than a primary driver of disease, or that infection may occur earlier in the disease process when antimicrobial therapy is less effective in altering established vascular pathology. While C. pneumoniae is not an independent CHD predictor, its role in inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and plaque instability suggests it may contribute to AS.

Helicobacter pylori

Epidemiological studies and meta-analyses have linked Helicobacter pylori infection to increased cardiovascular risk, particularly in AS and CAD. A meta-analysis of 13 studies found H. pylori seropositivity significantly associated with increased carotid intima-media thickness (CIMT), a marker for subclinical AS and future cardiovascular events.[2]

The infection is also linked to elevated inflammatory markers, including CRP, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), contributing to endothelial dysfunction and plaque formation.[24] Molecular mimicry may trigger autoimmune responses, potentially leading to extragastric conditions such as CVDs, diabetes, and neurological disorders.[25]

Strains carrying the cytotoxin-associated gene-A (cagA) antigen show a stronger association with myocardial infarction (MI), particularly in individuals under 65, with the highest risk observed in those under 55.[26] H. pylori may also disrupt lipid metabolism, lowering high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and increasing the low-density lipoprotein (LDL)/HDL ratio. Eradication of H. pylori has been linked to slight reductions in total cholesterol and LDL levels, suggesting its role in dyslipidemia and cardiovascular risk.[3]

Porphyromonas gingivalis

Porphyromonas gingivalis, a key pathogen in chronic periodontitis, has been strongly associated with increased CVD risk. Periodontitis induces systemic inflammation, and the spread of P. gingivalis and its virulence factors—lipopolysaccharides (LPSs), fimbriae, and gingipains—contributes to endothelial dysfunction, a key factor in atherogenesis.[27] Individuals with periodontitis show higher rates of endothelial dysfunction, arterial stiffness, and increased carotid intima-media thickness.[28]

Epidemiological studies link periodontitis to CAD, MI, and higher cardiovascular mortality, though risk estimates vary.[4] P. gingivalis LPS promotes the release of inflammatory cytokines like IL-6 and TNF-α, worsening vascular damage. Circulating endotoxins impair nitric oxide production, causing vasoconstriction and a pro-thrombotic state. Additionally, P. gingivalis heat shock proteins (HSPs) mimic human HSPs, potentially triggering autoantibody production and vascular injury.[29]

Intensive periodontal therapy (IPT) has been shown to reduce systemic inflammation, lower systolic blood pressure, and improve lipid profiles, leading to reduced cardiovascular risk.[30] Periodontal treatment also enhances endothelial function, as evidenced by improved flow-mediated dilation.[31]

However, these associations should be interpreted cautiously as important confounding factors such as socioeconomic status, smoking, dietary habits, healthcare access, and oral hygiene may independently contribute to both microbial exposure and cardiovascular risk, limiting causal inference from observational studies.

VIRAL INFECTIONS AND CARDIOVASCULAR DISEASE

Herpesviruses (cytomegalovirus and Epstein–Barr virus)

Exposure to cytomegalovirus (CMV) is linked to a 22% higher risk of CVD (RR: 1.22, 95% CI: 1.07–1.38, P = 0.002) and accounts for 13.4% of total CVD incidence.[32] Sero-epidemiological studies show elevated CMV antibody titers in coronary heart disease (CHD) patients, with CMV DNA detected in atherosclerotic plaques. Animal models confirm CMV-induced AS. CMV infection drives endothelial dysfunction, vascular inflammation, and smooth muscle proliferation, accelerating plaque formation. Human CMV (HCMV) affects endothelial and smooth muscle cells, causing apoptosis, proliferation, and lipid accumulation. Latent CMV reactivation triggers chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and thrombosis, worsening AS. It also disrupts lipid metabolism, promoting cholesterol deposition and plaque instability. Antiviral therapy reduces AS progression, highlighting CMV as a therapeutic target.[33]

Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) also contributes to cardiovascular risk. About 8.5% of chronic active EBV (CAEBV) patients develop coronary artery lesions (CALs).[34] EBV-associated myocarditis, pericarditis, and vasculitis cause endothelial damage and cardiac complications. Viral proteins, such as latent membrane protein 1 (LMP-1), increase vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), weakening vessel walls. Persistent EBV infection triggers immune activation, cytokine release, and chronic inflammation, raising the risk of coronary aneurysms, myocarditis, and heart failure.[35]

Human immunodeficiency virus

Individuals with HIV have an approximately 50% higher risk of MI compared with HIV-negative individuals, even after adjustment for traditional cardiovascular risk factors, as demonstrated in large cohort studies including the D: A: D collaboration and the Veterans Aging Cohort Study.[3637] They also face increased risks of stroke and pulmonary hypertension. Contributing factors include chronic viremia, low CD4+ counts, immune activation, traditional cardiovascular risk factors (especially smoking and hypertension), and coinfections such as hepatitis C virus and cytomegalovirus.

Importantly, the cardiovascular risk associated with antiretroviral therapy (ART) varies substantially by drug class and treatment era. Earlier ART regimens, particularly thymidine analog nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (e.g., zidovudine, stavudine) and ritonavir-boosted protease inhibitors, are strongly associated with dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, lipodystrophy, and accelerated AS, thereby contributing significantly to long-term cardiovascular risk. In contrast, modern ART regimens, especially integrase strand-transfer inhibitor (INSTI)-based combinations, have a substantially more favorable metabolic and lipid profile and are generally associated with a lower atherogenic risk, although weight gain and metabolic effects are increasingly recognized with some agents. Therefore, rather than exerting a uniform effect, ART has regimen-specific cardiovascular implications. Contemporary guidelines emphasise early initiation and sustained viral suppression with modern regimens as overall cardioprotective through reduction of systemic inflammation and immune activation, despite the persistence of residual cardiovascular risk.

HIV-associated CVD results from chronic inflammation, immune activation, and metabolic dysregulation, leading to endothelial dysfunction, prothrombotic states, and accelerated AS. Elevated inflammatory and immune activation markers (e.g. soluble CD163, soluble CD14, IL-6) and coagulation factors contribute to ongoing vascular injury. Although effective ART reduces systemic inflammation, residual risk persists even in virologically suppressed individuals. HIV-associated cardiomyopathy, ischemic heart disease, and heart failure arise from a combination of direct and indirect mechanisms, including myocardial inflammation, fibrosis, and microvascular dysfunction.[38]

SARS-CoV-2

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) significantly raises the risk of heart attack and ischemic stroke, especially within the first 2 weeks after infection, with the highest risk occurring on the day of exposure.[39] A meta-analysis of eight Chinese studies involving 46,248 patients found hypertension (17 ± 7%), diabetes mellitus (8 ± 6%), and CVD (5 ± 4%) to be common comorbidities among COVID-19 patients.[40] Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) enters host cells through binding of its spike (S) protein to angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptors expressed in the lungs, heart, and vascular endothelium, triggering inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and thrombogenesis. The virus also interacts with transmembrane serine protease 2 (TMPRSS2), which facilitates viral entry and enhances infectivity. Systemic inflammation, cytokine storm, and coagulopathy increase the risk of MI, ischemic stroke, arrhythmias, and other cardiovascular complications. Variants such as Delta and Omicron differ in transmissibility and virulence, while the long-term cardiovascular effects of the Omicron variant are still being investigated.[41]

Beyond the acute phase, COVID-19 contributes to Long COVID, characterized by persistent inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and a pro-thrombotic state that increases the risk of MI, arrhythmias, and heart failure. Elevated levels of IL-6, TNF-α, and CRP contribute to myocardial inflammation and cardiac fibrosis, while persistent viral reservoirs may sustain chronic immune activation. SARS-CoV-2-induced endothelial dysfunction reduces nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability, resulting in vascular stiffness and increased thrombotic risk. Flow-mediated dilation (FMD) studies have demonstrated persistent endothelial impairment in recovered individuals. COVID-19-associated coagulopathy (CAC), characterized by elevated D-dimer, fibrinogen, and von Willebrand factor (vWF), further predisposes individuals to thromboembolic complications.[42]

A study using United States Veterans Affairs data involving 153,760 COVID-19 patients demonstrated persistent post-30-day risks for cerebrovascular disorders, dysrhythmias, ischemic heart disease, pericarditis, myocarditis, heart failure, and thromboembolic disease, even among nonhospitalized individuals. The risks correlated with disease severity, emphasizing the importance of long-term cardiovascular monitoring after COVID-19 infection.[43] Similarly, the United Kingdom Biobank study reported a four- to fivefold higher risk of cardiac complications among COVID-19 survivors.[44]

Rare cases of myocarditis, pericarditis, and vaccine-induced immune thrombotic thrombocytopenia (VITT) have also been reported following COVID-19 vaccination. Myocarditis has been observed predominantly in young males after messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) vaccination, with an estimated incidence of approximately 1 in 5,000 to 1 in 10,000 doses in this subgroup, particularly following the second dose, whereas the population-level incidence is substantially lower; most cases are mild and self-limiting with favorable outcomes. VITT, a rare immune-mediated thrombotic disorder associated mainly with adenoviral vector vaccines, occurs at an estimated rate of 1 in 50,000–100,000 doses for the AstraZeneca vaccine. It is important to note that the Johnson & Johnson (Ad26.COV2.S) vaccine, another adenoviral vector vaccine previously associated with rare cases of VITT, was withdrawn from the U.S. market in May 2023 following declining demand and the availability of updated vaccine platforms, although it remains of historical relevance in safety analyses. Reported VITT rates for this vaccine were approximately 1 in 150,000–200,000 doses, primarily affecting women younger than 50 years of age.

Importantly, the risk of cardiovascular complications following SARS-CoV-2 infection is substantially greater than after vaccination. Studies indicate that COVID-19 infection is associated with significantly increased rates of myocarditis, thromboembolic events, and other cardiovascular complications, with myocarditis occurring more frequently after infection than following vaccination. Furthermore, thrombotic complications have been reported in 16–30% of hospitalized COVID-19 patients, reflecting the profound inflammatory and pro-coagulant effects of the infection. Large population-based studies consistently demonstrate that the benefits of COVID-19 vaccination in preventing severe disease, hospitalization, mortality, and cardiovascular complications greatly outweigh the rare risks of vaccine-associated adverse events. Continued post-marketing surveillance and pharmacovigilance remain essential for the early identification and management of rare adverse effects while maintaining public confidence in vaccination programs.[45]

Gut dysbiosis and atherogenesis

Gut microbiota play an important role in cardiovascular health through the production of bioactive metabolites that influence inflammation, lipid metabolism, and vascular function. One of the most extensively studied microbial metabolites is trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO). Trimethylamine (TMA), generated by gut bacteria during the metabolism of dietary choline, phosphatidylcholine, and L-carnitine found in red meat and eggs, is absorbed and oxidized in the liver by flavin monooxygenases to form TMAO. Elevated circulating TMAO levels have been associated with an increased risk of AS and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs).[46] Mechanistically, TMAO promotes atherogenesis by enhancing macrophage scavenger receptor expression (SR-A1 and CD36), thereby increasing oxidized LDL uptake and foam cell formation. It also activates inflammatory pathways, including the NLRP3 inflammasome, increases platelet reactivity, and interferes with reverse cholesterol transport and bile acid metabolism, collectively accelerating atherosclerotic plaque development.[6]

Beyond TMAO, several other gut microbiota-derived metabolites contribute to cardiovascular regulation. Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), such as acetate, propionate, and butyrate, produced during the fermentation of dietary fiber by commensal bacteria, exert protective effects by modulating immune responses, improving endothelial function, and regulating blood pressure through G-protein-coupled receptor signaling.[3] Alterations in bile acid metabolism by gut microbes can influence lipid homeostasis and inflammation through bile acid receptor signaling pathways, particularly the farnesoid X receptor (FXR), a nuclear receptor involved in cholesterol and bile acid regulation, and the G protein-coupled bile acid receptor TGR5, which modulates inflammatory and metabolic responses. These pathways are increasingly recognized as important regulators of cardiovascular risk. Changes in gut microbial composition have also been linked to CVD. Studies have reported reduced microbial diversity and shifts in bacterial taxa in individuals with AS, including increased abundance of pro-inflammatory species such as Enterobacteriaceae and decreased levels of beneficial SCFA-producing bacteria such as Faecalibacterium and Roseburia.[47] These microbial alterations contribute to chronic low-grade inflammation and metabolic dysregulation that favor atherogenesis.

Importantly, the relationship between the gut microbiome and CVD is bidirectional. Several commonly used cardiovascular medications can influence gut microbial composition and metabolic activity. For example, statins have been shown to alter microbial diversity and reduce pro-atherogenic metabolites, while metformin modifies gut microbial communities and increases SCFA-producing bacteria, contributing to improved metabolic and cardiovascular outcomes.[48] Conversely, microbial metabolism can also modify drug bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy, highlighting the emerging importance of host–microbiome interactions in cardiovascular pharmacology.

However, the relationship between gut dysbiosis and CVD is likely bidirectional. CVD itself, through altered intestinal perfusion, hemodynamic changes, systemic inflammation, and medication use, may secondarily modify gut microbial composition, making causal interpretation challenging.

Preventive strategies

Prevention strategies targeting microbial infections may contribute significantly to reducing CVD risk. Vaccination represents one of the most effective public health interventions in this context. Vaccines against Streptococcus pneumoniae and influenza virus have been associated with reductions in cardiovascular complications, particularly among high-risk individuals. In contrast, evidence regarding the cardiovascular benefits of human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination remains preliminary and is currently based mainly on mechanistic hypotheses and limited observational findings rather than robust epidemiological or clinical trial data. Among these, influenza vaccination has the strongest evidence base, with several randomized trials and meta-analyses demonstrating a significant reduction in MACEs, particularly among patients with established CVD or high cardiovascular risk. A meta-analysis by Udell et al.[7] reported that influenza vaccination was associated with a substantial reduction in cardiovascular events, including MI and cardiovascular mortality, highlighting its cardioprotective potential in vulnerable populations. These findings support the routine inclusion of influenza vaccination as part of comprehensive cardiovascular prevention strategies.

Emerging research also suggests potential benefits from vaccines targeting pathogens implicated in AS, such as Chlamydia pneumoniae and Porphyromonas gingivalis. Although these vaccines remain under investigation, they may represent future strategies for reducing infection-driven vascular inflammation and atherosclerotic progression.[49]

Improving hygiene, sanitation, and infection control measures plays an important role in reducing exposure to pathogens such as Helicobacter pylori and C. pneumoniae. Similarly, maintaining good oral hygiene and effective periodontal care can reduce chronic oral infections, thereby decreasing systemic inflammatory burden and improving endothelial function, which may contribute to better cardiovascular outcomes.[221]

Early identification of infection-related inflammation is also important in prevention. Screening for inflammatory biomarkers such as CRP and IL-6 may help identify individuals at increased cardiovascular risk associated with chronic infections. Advanced diagnostic tools, including polymerase chain reaction (PCR), serological assays, and imaging modalities such as carotid intima-media thickness (CIMT) and coronary artery calcium (CAC) scoring, can assist in detecting subclinical AS and monitoring disease progression in high-risk individuals. However, clinical trials evaluating antibiotic therapy for infections such as H. pylori and C. pneumoniae have shown mixed results in reducing cardiovascular events, suggesting that antimicrobial therapy alone may not be sufficient to alter established vascular pathology.[24]

Pharmacological interventions also play an important role in mitigating infection-related cardiovascular inflammation. In addition to lipid-lowering effects, statins possess anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties that may reduce vascular inflammation and improve endothelial function in patients with chronic infections. Anti-inflammatory therapies, including colchicine and interleukin-1 inhibitors, have also demonstrated effectiveness in reducing recurrent cardiovascular events by targeting inflammatory pathways involved in AS.[8] Furthermore, novel immunomodulatory approaches, including monoclonal antibody therapies, are being explored to address immune-mediated vascular damage associated with chronic infections.[50]

Lifestyle and microbiome-focused interventions also contribute to prevention strategies. Dietary modifications, such as reducing red meat intake to limit trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO) production, may support cardiovascular health by modulating gut microbial metabolism.[3] In addition, microbiota-targeted therapies, including prebiotics, probiotics, and fecal microbiota transplantation, are being investigated for their potential to restore gut microbial balance and reduce systemic inflammation linked to CVD.[47]

Finally, public health initiatives and awareness programs are essential to address the link between infections and CVD. Strengthening global epidemiological surveillance of infections such as H. pylori and C. pneumoniae can facilitate targeted prevention strategies and inform public health interventions. Continued research on pathogen-specific therapies, vaccines, and novel biomarkers—including TMAO and pathogen-specific antibodies—may further improve early detection, risk stratification, and preventive approaches for infection-associated CVD.

CONCLUSION

Growing evidence indicates that microbial infections and alterations in the human microbiome contribute to the development and progression of CVD through multiple biological mechanisms, including chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, immune activation, thrombogenesis, and metabolic dysregulation. Bacterial pathogens such as Helicobacter pylori, Chlamydia pneumoniae, and Porphyromonas gingivalis; viral agents including Human cytomegalovirus (HCMV), Epstein–Barr virus (EBV), HIV, and Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2); and fungal pathogens and gut microbiota-derived metabolites have all been implicated in vascular injury and atherogenesis. Among these, the strongest epidemiological evidence exists for HIV- and COVID-19-associated cardiovascular complications, whereas evidence for several other pathogens remains largely observational and mechanistic.

Despite substantial advances, important uncertainties remain regarding the causal relationship between microbial infections and CVD. Many reported associations are derived from observational studies, and interventional trials targeting specific pathogens have not consistently demonstrated reductions in cardiovascular events. These findings suggest that microbial factors may function as important contributors to vascular inflammation and disease progression rather than as independent primary causes of CVD. Furthermore, the complex interactions between host immunity, microbial communities, environmental exposures, and traditional cardiovascular risk factors remain incompletely understood.

RECOMMENDATIONS FOR PRACTICE AND RESEARCH

From a public health perspective, integrating infection prevention and inflammation control into cardiovascular risk reduction strategies may provide additional clinical benefits. Greater collaboration between cardiology, infectious disease, immunology, and microbiome research is essential to improve understanding of infection-associated cardiovascular risk and develop more comprehensive prevention strategies.

Future research should prioritize large longitudinal cohort studies, mechanistic investigations, and microbiome-based therapeutic trials to clarify causal pathways and identify reliable biomarkers of infection-associated CVD. Advances in multi-omics technologies, microbial genomics, and immunometabolic research may further improve risk stratification and support the development of personalized preventive and therapeutic approaches targeting host–microbe interactions.
