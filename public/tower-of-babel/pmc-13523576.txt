Atrial fibrillation ablation in patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy: Findings and outcomes of repeat ablation procedures
Authors: Jose F de Melo Junior, Daniella Haas, William H Swain, Shahid Karim, Samuel J Asirvatham, Freddy M Del-Carpio, Abhishek J Deshmukh, Christopher V DeSimone, Fatima M Ezzeddine, Paul A Friedman, Suraj Kapa, Ammar M Killu, Gurukripa G Kowlgi, Malini Madhavan, Siva K Mulpuru, Peter A Noseworthy, Alan M Sugrue, Nicholas Y Tan, Konstantinos C Siontis

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with atrial fibrillation (AF) and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) commonly require repeat catheter ablation procedures for AF management. The mechanisms underlying the increased AF recurrence rates are poorly defined.ObjectiveWe aimed to explore mapping findings and ablation outcomes among patients with HCM undergoing repeat AF ablation.MethodsIn this single-center study, 45 patients with HCM underwent repeat AF ablation procedures (2015–2023). Patients were age- and sex-matched 1:1 to control patients without HCM undergoing repeat AF ablation. Details from comprehensive electroanatomic mapping were compared. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were used to evaluate prognostic factors for recurrent AF in HCM.ResultsThe mean age in the HCM group was 57.5 ± 13.2 and 71% were male. The most common finding at repeat ablation was pulmonary vein (PV) reconnection (86.7% in the HCM group vs 75.5% in the control group, P = .34). Patients were followed for a median of 456 days (interquartile range 127–1148). 26 patients (57.8%) in the HCM group experienced recurrent AF during the follow-up period, compared with 23 patients (51.1%) in the control group (P = .52). No significant difference in AF recurrence was found between the 2 groups (log-rank P = .33). Left atrial volume index and early AF recurrence during the blanking period were both associated with AF recurrence in univariate analyses; however, these were not significant in multivariate analysis.ConclusionPV reconnection is commonly observed in patients with HCM undergoing repeat AF ablation. Repeat AF ablation outcomes in patients with HCM were comparable to those in patients without HCM.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Catheter ablation, Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Recurrence, Outcomes, Pulmonary vein isolation

Introduction

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is the most common hereditary cardiomyopathy, with a prevalence of at least 1 in 500 individuals.1 It is characterized by myofibril hypertrophy and disarray with associated atrial myocardial fibrosis, ischemia, and left atrial enlargement, which are known risk factors for the development of atrial fibrillation (AF).2 AF is the most common tachyarrhythmia in HCM, occurring at an annual incidence of approximately 2%.3 AF in HCM is linked to increased risk of stroke, heart failure, and functional disability.3 Patients with HCM and AF also experience a higher burden of symptoms because of reduced myocardial reserve and diastolic dysfunction.4

Catheter ablation is a common treatment strategy for managing AF in HCM, particularly in symptomatic patients. The outcomes of initial AF ablation in HCM patients tend to be less favorable than in the general AF population.5,6 Patients with HCM commonly require repeat ablation procedures for successful rhythm control.7 Non-pulmonary vein (PV) triggers have been identified as key contributors to AF recurrence in the general population,8 though the mechanisms of AF recurrence in HCM patients have not been established.

In this study, we sought to investigate the mechanisms underlying AF recurrence after initial catheter ablation. Results from electroanatomical mapping and clinical outcomes were compared from HCM to a control cohort.

Methods

Study design and cohort

This retrospective, non-randomized, single-center study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Mayo Clinic. The study conduct adheres to the standards of the Declaration of Helsinki.

We identified patients with an established diagnosis of HCM based on current guidelines who underwent repeat AF catheter ablation at Mayo Clinic, Rochester, MN, between January 2015 and December 2023. The cohort of HCM patients was matched in a 1:1 ratio by age (within ±3 years) and sex to a control group from a pool of 370 patients without HCM who also underwent repeat AF ablation within the same period. Age and sex were selected as matching variables because they represent fundamental, non-modifiable determinants of AF recurrence. The decision to recommend repeat AF ablation was at the discretion of the treating electrophysiologist. Importantly, there was no predefined or differential threshold for offering redo ablation between the HCM and control groups.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All consecutive patients with a confirmed diagnosis of HCM who underwent repeat AF ablation at our institution were included. Patients were required to have PV isolation (PVI) with or without additional ablation at their prior procedure to qualify for this study. Patients who had repeat procedures without left atrial mapping were excluded. Patients who had undergone ≥1 ablation(s) prior to referral to our institution were eligible, provided that detailed information regarding the previous procedures was available for review. In these cases, the first repeat AF ablation performed in our institution was considered the index repeat procedure. For patients who had ablation procedures exclusively at our institution, the second AF ablation (first redo ablation) was considered the index procedure. Patients with prior surgical AF ablations were eligible for inclusion provided they had at least 1 prior catheter-based AF ablation prior to their repeat procedure. Only adult patients who provided research consent were included in this study. All patient who met inclusion criteria were included in this study.

Data collection

Baseline clinical data, including demographics, comorbidities, medications, echocardiographic findings, and rhythm monitoring data, were extracted from the electronic health record (EHR). Procedural details related to the index repeat procedures, including mapping findings, patterns of PV reconnection, and ablation targets were obtained from procedure notes.

Ablation procedure

After informed consent, procedures were performed under general anesthesia. Vascular access was obtained with ultrasound guidance, and intravenous heparin was administered to maintain an activated clotting time of 300–350 seconds. Left atrium (LA) access was achieved via single or dual transseptal puncture under intracardiac echocardiography and fluoroscopy. A high-density electroanatomic LA map was created with a multipolar catheter during baseline rhythm (sinus rhythm, coronary sinus [CS] pacing, or AF/flutter). PV reconnection was defined as the presence of recovered conduction between a previously isolated PV and the LA, documented as either entrance or exit conduction, or both. The right atrium was mapped at the operator’s discretion depending on inducible atrial arrhythmia. Radiofrequency ablation was performed using open-irrigated force-sensing catheters, targeting electrogram attenuation and impedance drop, with specific ablation lesion end points varying among operators. Esophageal temperature monitoring was performed for ablation on the posterior LA. PVI was performed with point-by-point lesions, with entrance and exit block as the ablation end point, persisting at least 20 minutes after completion of the PVI. Post-ablation adenosine testing, and atrial flutter induction and trigger induction/mapping were dependent on the clinical scenario, mapping findings, and operator’s discretion. For non-PV trigger protocol, isoproterenol was infused with escalating rate up to 0.15–0.20 mcg/kg/min. Inducible atrial ectopy with or without AF initiations was mapped and targeted for ablation. When AF was repeatedly induced because of triggered ectopy or burst pacing, synchronized cardioversions were performed to facilitate mapping of the beat initiating AF. All cases were mapped with either PENTARAY, OCTARAY (Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA, USA), or HD Grid (Abbott, Abbott Park, IL, USA), depending on the mapping system.

Follow-up and end points

Follow-up data was obtained through comprehensive review of the institutional EHR and external medical records accessed using Care Everywhere (Epic Systems), an interoperable health information exchange platform that allows retrieval of clinical encounters, diagnostic studies, hospitalization records, and procedural reports from participating outside institutions. All available documentation, including clinic notes, electrocardiograms, rhythm monitoring reports, and hospitalization records, was reviewed to adjudicate AF recurrence, repeat procedures, and survival status.

In the primary analysis, patients were followed until the last available clinical follow-up in the EHR for a maximum of 5 years, or until they experienced recurrent AF, whichever occurred first. Recurrent AF was defined as any symptomatic or asymptomatic AF or atrial flutter of at least 30 seconds in duration documented by electrocardiogram (ECG), implanted cardiac device, or ambulatory rhythm monitoring occurring after the first 6 weeks (42 days) from the index redo AF ablation (time zero). Although recurrences within 6-week blanking period after the index ablation were tracked, they were not considered in the primary analysis.9,10 We also collected data on repeat AF ablations performed until the last clinical follow-up available in the EHR, but they were not included in the primary analysis if they occurred ≥5 years from time zero.

All patients undergoing AF ablation in our institution, irrespective of underlying substrate, undergo standardized post-procedural follow-up consisting of in-person 3-month follow-up that includes 12-lead ECG and 24-hour ambulatory rhythm monitor. Beyond the 3-month evaluation, additional rhythm monitoring is performed as clinically indicated, consistent with real-world practice.

Statistical analysis

Baseline patient characteristics and procedural data were presented as mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR) when appropriate. Differences in the frequency of PV and non-PV reconnections between the HCM and non-HCM groups were assessed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as indicated. A Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed to compare the time to recurrent AF between the 2 groups with a blanking period of 6 weeks. The log-rank test was used to assess the differences between the survival curves of the 2 groups. Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression was performed to identify risk factors for recurrent AF in the entire cohort, including HCM patients and controls. Variables that were statistically significant (P < .05) in the univariate analyses were subsequently included in a multivariate Cox regression model to identify independent predictors of recurrent AF. A separate Cox regression analysis was performed for the HCM cohort to evaluate risk factors specific to recurrent AF within this group. A significance level of P < .05 was considered statistically significant for all comparisons. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (SPSS Inc., Chicago IL).

Results

Patient characteristics

45 patients with HCM met the study inclusion criteria and were matched to 45 control patients without HCM. Baseline patients’ characteristics are shown in Table 1. The mean age was 57.5 years (±13.2) in the HCM group and 59.7 years (±13.7) in the control group. 71% of patients were male in both groups. The proportion of patients with paroxysmal AF, as opposed to persistent AF, was 75.6% in the HCM group and 84.4% in the control group (P = .29). 7 patients (15.6%) in the control group had a diagnosis of cardiomyopathy (3 with ischemic and 4 with dilated cardiomyopathy). The median time from initial AF diagnosis to repeat ablation was 6.12 years (IQR 3.96–9.67) in the HCM group and 4.02 years (IQR 2.42–8.44) in the control group (P = .20). The median time from the last AF ablation to the redo index ablation was 1.96 years (IQR 1.14–3.93) in the HCM group and 1.21 years (IQR 0.66–2.18) in the control group (P = .05). All patients underwent PVI at their previous procedure. Posterior wall isolation was performed in 5 patients in the HCM group and 1 patient in the control group. Overall, 36 patients in the HCM group and 31 patients in the control group underwent ablation of at least 1 non-PVI target in their previous ablation procedure (Table 2).

Patients were followed for a median of 456 days (IQR 127–1148) across both groups. Subsequent ablations

12 patients (26.7%) and 9 patients (20%) in the HCM and control groups, respectively, underwent subsequent repeat AF ablation. The median time to repeat AF ablation was 881 days (IQR 323–1745) in the HCM group, and 351days (187–1037) in the control group. Detailed procedure description for repeat ablation during follow-up was available for 9 patients in the HCM group and 8 patients in the control group. 2 patients (22.2%) in the HCM group and 4 patients (50%) in the control group had evidence of PV reconnection (P = .23). 7 patients (77.8%) in the HCM group and 6 patients (75%) in the control group had evidence of reconnection of previous linear ablation or posterior wall isolation (P = .89). 4 patients (44.4%) in the HCM group and 5 patients (62.5%) in the control group had recurrent AF during a median follow-up of 4 months after the subsequent repeat ablation (P = .46). The median time to recurrent AF was 93 days in the HCM group and 155 days in the control group (log-rank P = .49).

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the mapping findings and outcomes of repeat AF ablation in patients with HCM. We found that most patients, 89% in the HCM group and 79% in the control group, had at least 1 reconnected PV. Although the proportion of patients with at least 1 PV reconnected was numerically higher in the HCM group, this difference was not statistically significant between the 2 groups. The rate of recurrent AF after repeat ablation during a follow-up period of 15 months was >50% in both groups. Overall, these findings suggest that the outcomes of repeat AF ablation in patients with HCM are comparable to patients without HCM. In the entire study cohort, including HCM and control patients, LAVI and early AF recurrence during the blanking period were found to be associated with AF recurrence in a univariate analysis, but no clear predictors of AF recurrence were found in a multivariate analysis.

The outcome of AF ablation in HCM has been the subject of extensive investigation. Di Donna et al found that, after a follow-up period of 40 months from the initial AF ablation in patients with HCM, 52% of patients required a repeat procedure, and 33% experienced recurrent AF after redo ablation.11 Additionally, that study identified left atrial volume as one of the strongest predictors of recurrent AF, alongside other factors such as age, functional status, and persistent AF. Subsequent studies have continued to assess the recurrence rate of AF following redo ablation in HCM, with reported recurrence rates approximately 50% at 1 year,12,13 which aligns with the recurrence rate observed in our current study. In the general population, previous investigations have shown AF recurrence rate after redo ablation closer to 40%,14,15 slightly below the recurrence rate for HCM. Few studies have compared the recurrence rate between HCM and control groups. In a meta-analysis of 5 studies, the recurrence rate of AF after redo ablation in HCM was 48.2% vs 28.8% in control groups.16 Overall, we found a higher recurrence rate in control patients than previously reported in the literature, which may be because of differences in patient population, prevalence of risk factors for disease recurrence, and ablation technique and complexity. Accounting for these limitations and differences in patient cohorts, when considered alongside the existing body of evidence, our study suggests that patients with HCM tend to experience comparable outcomes following redo AF ablation to those observed in the general population.

In practical terms, the study findings encourage a less restrictive approach to redo AF ablation in HCM. Procedural decisions should be guided by symptom burden, prior ablation characteristics, comorbidities, and patient preference, rather than by the presence of HCM alone. Overall, the data reinforce that redo AF ablation remains a viable and clinically meaningful rhythm control strategy in patients with HCM. These considerations apply largely to first redo procedures, whereas the outcomes of multi-redo procedures in HCM (and in non-HCM controls) should be expected to be progressively less favorable. Further prospective studies with standardized rhythm monitoring and symptom assessment tools are needed to assess the effects of catheter ablation in reducing arrhythmia and quality of life burden. These were not possible to quantify in this study but remain important post-ablation clinical end points beyond the binary assessment of AF recurrence.

Previous analyses have shown an association between LA volume and AF recurrence.3,17, 18, 19 Left atrial (LA) volume has also been associated with major adverse cerebrovascular and cardiac events in patients with HCM.19 We found an association between LAVI and AF recurrence in a univariate analysis only, which may be because of the number of enrolled patients and lack of power to show a difference in a multivariate analysis. In our cohort, the LAVI was significantly higher in patients with HCM compared with the control group (52.8 vs 39.9 mL/m2 respectively) despite no major differences in overall patient outcomes between the groups. Moreover, outcomes stratified by LAVI showed no significant differences. Together, these findings suggest that advanced atrial remodeling as measured by LAVI should not automatically preclude consideration of repeat ablation.

In this cohort, we identified early recurrence of AF in the early post-procedural period as a predictor of long-term arrhythmia recurrence. Although we adopted a 6-week blanking period in our primary analysis, the concept of the blanking period post-ablation has been challenged in recent literature in the general AF population.20 In the current study focusing specifically on patients with HCM we identified early recurrence (<6 weeks) as a predictor of later recurrence in univariate analysis. This finding, while not definitive, should be considered in the prognostication considerations of patients with HCM undergoing catheter ablation of AF.

PV reconnection is frequently identified as the primary factor contributing to AF recurrence following an initial ablation.21,22 The rate of PV reconnection observed during repeat AF ablation in the general population has been reported between 60%–100%.22, 23, 24 Notably, PV reconnection remains prevalent even after a second ablation, with rates exceeding 90% in some cases.25 Although the rate of PV reconnection in the context of HCM has been less extensively studied, a few studies have provided useful insights. Santangeli et al13 (2013) found that PVs remained isolated in 82% of patients with late AF recurrence after initial ablation, whereas all 43 patients included in that study were found to have non-PV triggers, suggesting that such triggers seem to be primarily responsible for late AF recurrences. In a smaller cohort of 13 HCM patients undergoing repeat AF ablation, the rate of PV reconnection was 61.5%, which was notably lower than the 91.6% observed in a non-HCM control group.26 The current study challenges this notion by demonstrating that PV reconnection is present in approximately 9 of 10 patients with HCM undergoing redo ablation, a rate comparable to the control group. Furthermore, we observed a significantly lower prevalence of non-PV triggers, around 25% in both groups, though the intensity of non-PV trigger assessment was lower than reported in previous literature. The heterogeneity in non-PV trigger assessment and lesion sets in our study, reflecting operator-dependent, real-world ablation practices, limits our ability to draw definitive conclusions; thus, findings regarding the relative importance of PV reconnection vs non-PV triggers should be considered hypothesis-generating. Importantly, PV reconnection did not correlate with future AF recurrence, potentially because of its high prevalence in this cohorts. Overall, our findings underscore the importance of achieving durable PVI in HCM patients. However, further research is needed to identify which individuals might benefit from more extensive ablation strategies to improve long-term outcomes.

Limitations

As a retrospective, nonrandomized design, the study is subject to inherent risks of confounding. Significant baseline differences between the HCM and control groups, as expected considering the different phenotypes of these patients, could have influenced the results. The HCM cohort had significantly higher LAVI, reflecting more advanced atrial remodeling. However, despite this structural difference, AF recurrence rates after ablation were similar between the HCM and non-HCM groups. Although the HCM group had a statistically higher number of prior ablation procedures, the absolute difference was not clinically meaningful (1.13 vs 1.0 procedures per patient). Although hypertension was more prevalent in the control group, it was not associated with AF recurrence in the univariate analysis and, therefore, did not represent a true confounder. The study was based on a single, quaternary care center and the findings may not be generalizable to broader, more diverse patient populations. Our study primarily included patients undergoing radiofrequency ablation and the findings may not be generalizable to those treated with newer technologies. Future studies are warranted to evaluate the patterns of disease recurrence following pulsed field ablation in patients with HCM, as this emerging modality may be implicated with distinct mechanisms of arrhythmia recurrence.27 Patients in the HCM cohort had a significantly higher prevalence of implanted devices. This difference could theoretically increase the sensitivity of AF detection in the HCM relative to the non-HCM group. However, this potential bias would be expected to operate in a direction favoring detection of more recurrences in the HCM group, strengthening our results interpretation and conclusion.

Conclusion

This study suggests that PV reconnections are very commonly observed in patients with HCM undergoing repeat AF ablation, and this likely plays an important role in disease recurrence. Repeat AF ablation outcomes in patients with HCM appear similar to those in individuals without HCM, lending support to repeat ablation as a management option for patients with HCM similarly to those without HCM. Further research is needed to better understand the underlying mechanisms of AF recurrence in HCM and to refine treatment strategies for improved outcomes.
