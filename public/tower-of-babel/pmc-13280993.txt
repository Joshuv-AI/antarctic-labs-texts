Extraction, Purification, and Characterization of Tannase Produced by Aspergillus terreus PQ344456 and Its Textile Dye Degradation and Juice Clarification Features
Authors: Nuha M. Alhazmi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aspergillus terreus is a filamentous fungus of high biotechnological value, noted for its strong biosynthetic and fermentative capacities. In this study, tannase was produced via submerged fermentation using a soil-derived Aspergillus terreus strain (GenBank: PQ344456) identified through morphological and molecular screening. Tannase activity was markedly higher in submerged fermentation at 136.8 U/mL compared with liquid surface fermentation at 62.7 U/mL, confirming its superior performance. Kinetic analysis during the exponential phase revealed that tannase formation proceeded slightly faster than biomass accumulation, indicating partially growth-associated enzyme production. Optimal production was achieved at pH 5, 30 °C, and 1.5% tannic acid. Partial purification using organic solvents enhanced specific activity, and native PAGE analysis revealed a prominent high-molecular-weight band exceeding 250 kDa, suggesting the presence of a multimeric enzyme complex. FTIR analysis confirmed characteristic protein functional groups, including O–H and N–H stretching vibrations as well as an amide I band corresponding to peptide backbone integrity. Kinetic characterization using tannic acid followed Michaelis–Menten behavior and yielded a K m of 8.4 mg mL–1 and a V max of 4256 U mL–1. The partially purified enzyme demonstrated concentration-dependent dye decolorization in agar diffusion assays. Furthermore, application in fruit juices resulted in enhanced tannin hydrolysis, particularly in pomegranate, pineapple, and guava juices, and was accompanied by reduced turbidity and viscosity, improved clarity, and decreased microbial load.

Introduction

Enzymes are biological macromolecules that catalyze chemical reactions by lowering activation energies, thus enhancing reaction rates under mild conditions, and their high specificity and environmental compatibility have made them indispensable across industries such as food, textiles, pharmaceuticals, and environmental bioremediation. , Among these biocatalysts, tannase is noteworthy for hydrolyzing ester and depside bonds in hydrolyzable tannins to yield gallic acid and glucose; this enzyme, mainly of microbial origin, has been widely applied in processing tannin-rich agro-industrial wastes, in pharmaceutical precursor synthesis, and in improving beverage flavor profiles. , Fungi are known as a vital contributor to the dynamics of soil–plant ecosystems, participating in key processes that include soil structure stabilization, decomposition of organic residues, nutrient cycling, and the regulation of nutrient mobility and availability. Furthermore, mycorrhizal and filamentous fungi, including genera such as Aspergillus, Penicillium, Mucor, and Trichoderma, represent some of the most prevalent and extensively investigated beneficial soil microorganisms. The fungus Aspergillus terreus is a filamentous fungus widely distributed in soil and organic-rich environments and has garnered attention for both its metabolic versatility and its industrial applications. It is known to produce valuable secondary metabolites and various extracellular enzymes, making it a candidate for biotechnological exploitation. Nevertheless, certain strains of Aspergillus terreus have been reported to synthesize tremorgenic mycotoxins, secondary metabolic compounds capable of inducing sporadic or continuous muscular tremors in mammals. Enzymes also play vital roles in mitigating environmental pollution, particularly in textile wastewater treatment, where oxidative enzymes such as laccases and peroxidases enable efficient decolorization and detoxification of synthetic dyes under eco-friendly conditions, outperforming traditional chemical treatments, as enzymatic dye degradation provides milder conditions, fewer pollutants, and more biodegradable byproducts. , Additionally, in the fruit-juice processing industry, enzymes such as pectinases, cellulases, and hemicellulases are widely used for clarification, reduction of turbidity and viscosity, and enhancement of yield and sensory quality, thereby improving filtration, consumer appeal, and process economy. However, despite their remarkable catalytic efficiency, the large-scale industrial utilization of natural free enzymes is often restricted owing to their intrinsic structural fragility, unstable operability under process conditions, and poor reusability. Therefore, considering the broad industrial potential of enzymes, the specific biotechnological relevance of tannase, and the metabolic capacity of Aspergillus terreus, the present study aims to extract, purify, and characterize tannase produced by Aspergillus terreus and to assess its dual functionality in textile dye degradation and juice clarification, contributing to sustainable and environmentally responsible bioprocess development.

Materials and Methods

Microbial Strains Used

The author confirms that the same fungal strain of Aspergillus terreus, previously reported in his previously published scientific work and deposited in GenBank under the accession number PQ344456, was employed for the experimental work conducted in the present study. A concise summary of its isolation and identification procedures is provided below (Figure ).

Isolation and identification procedures for Aspergillus terreus strain (PQ344456) used in this study.

Production of Tannase Enzyme Using Submerged Fermentation (SmF)

A 5% v/v standard inoculum (1 × 106 spores/ml) was prepared from the preserved Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) slants of Aspergillus terreus. The inoculum was added into a 250 ml Erlenmeyer flask containing 50 mL of Czapek-Dox liquid medium containing (percent; w/v): 0.25% NaNO3, 0.1% KH2PO4, 0.05% MgSO4·7H2O, 0.05% KCl, and 1.0% tannic acid (as a sole carbon source), and the pH of the medium was adjusted to 6.0. The cultures were agitated at speeds of 0 (static), 50, 100, 150, 200, and 250 rpm using a rotary shaker (Certomart-H, B. Braun, Germany) at 30 °C for 4 days.

Correlation between Growth Kinetics and Tannase Production

Biomass was quantified as cell dry weight (CDW): culture aliquots were centrifuged, washed with distilled water, and dried to constant weight at 105 °C. Optical density was used as a rapid proxy during the exponential phase and later correlated to CDW by a calibration curve. Specific growth rate (μ) was calculated from ln­(X2/X1)/(t2–t1) measured during the exponential phase; doubling time (td) and generation rate (k) derived as td = ln2/μ and k = 1/td. Tannase activity was measured in parallel from culture supernatants to establish time-course correlations between growth phase and enzyme yield.

Effect of Varying Conditions and Techniques on Tannase Enzyme Production

Effect of Fermentation Technique Type on Tannase Production

In order to assess and compare efficiency and effectiveness of submerged fermentation technique as the main technique used for this study, production of tannase enzyme using liquid surface fermentation technique has been implemented. A 5% v/v inoculum was added into a 250 ml Erlenmeyer flask containing 50 mL of Czapek-Dox liquid medium; then for 4 days, the flasks were maintained in a static state without any agitation at 28 °C. A filter paper was used to filter the cultures at the final step of the fermentation stage. The dry weight of the cells was calculated using the previously mentioned procedure. The filtrate (supernatant) containing the tannase enzyme was used to calculate the yield.

Effect of Tannic Acid Concentration for Tannase Production

Tannase enzyme’s ability to be induced and inhibited has been acknowledged, as documented in various investigations; therefore, various concentration levels of tannic acid, varying from 0.5% to 2.5% (v/v), were employed in the SmF technique to produce tannase enzyme, and the yield has been recorded periodically.

Effect of Temperature on Tannase Production

To determine the ideal temperature degree to obtain the highest yield of enzyme production, A 5% v/v standard inoculum (1 × 106 spores/ml) was inoculated into a 250 ml Erlenmeyer flask containing 50 mL of Czapek-Dox liquid medium for 4 days at different temperature degrees ranging from 25 to 40 °C, and the content of produced tannase was estimated.

Effect of pH on Tannase Production

In regard to the assessment of the most optimal pH value in order to produce the highest amount of tannase enzyme, the Erlenmeyer flask was kept for 4 days after being inoculated with the prepared inoculum at 30 °C with varying pH values ranging from 3 to 7, and their influence on tannase enzyme yield was noted.

Tannase Enzyme Extraction and Purification

After fermentation, the biomass was removed by filtration, and the culture supernatant (crude enzyme extract) was collected and cooled to 4 °C for downstream purification. The crude filtrate was brought to 4 °C and slowly stirred, while a prechilled organic solvent was added dropwise to reach the desired saturation. First, acetone was added (sample to acetone ratio 1:3 v/v), and the mixture was incubated at −20 °C for 2 h; precipitated protein was collected by centrifugation at 10,000g for 15 min at 4 °C. Next, the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was gently air-dried, then resuspended in 0.05 M citrate buffer (pH 5.0). A second precipitation step employed cold ethanol (70% v/v) added to the resuspended enzyme solution, incubated at – 20 °C for 2 h, followed by centrifugation as above. Finally, to further refine purity, isopropanol precipitation was carried out by adding chilled isopropanol to 60% (v/v) final concentration, incubated at – 20 °C for 2 h and centrifuged at 10,000g for 10 min at 4 °C; the resulting pellet was resuspended in buffer for enzyme assay.

Characterization of Tannase Enzyme

Enzyme Activity Assay

Concerning measuring tannase enzyme activity, preserved PDA slants were used to cultivate a pure colony of Aspergillus terreus on fresh PDA agar plates at 25 °C for 96 h., and the clear zone’s diameter in millimeters that developed surrounding the fungal colony was calculated as an enzymatic index using the following equation EnzymaticIndex(EI)=(Diameteroftheclearzone−diameterofthemicrobialcolony)/diameterofthecolony

SDS-PAGE Analysis of Partially Purified Tannase

The partially purified tannase preparation was analyzed by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) to estimate its apparent molecular weight under denaturing conditions. Electrophoresis was carried out according to the Laemmli method using a 12% (w/v) resolving gel and a 4% (w/v) stacking gel prepared in Tris–HCl buffer. Prior to loading, enzyme samples were mixed with Laemmli sample buffer containing SDS and β-mercaptoethanol and heated at 95 °C for 5 min to ensure complete protein denaturation and reduction of disulfide bonds. Protein separation was performed in Tris–glycine–SDS running buffer (pH 8.3) at a constant voltage of 100–120 V until the tracking dye reached the bottom of the gel. A prestained molecular weight protein marker (10–180 kDa) was run in parallel to allow estimation of the molecular mass of the tannase band. Following electrophoresis, gels were stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 and subsequently destained using a methanol–acetic acid–water solution until distinct protein bands were clearly visualized. The apparent molecular weight of tannase was estimated by comparison with the protein marker using gel documentation software. ,

Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR)­characterization of Tannase

Purified tannase extract was lyophilized and ground to a fine powder prior to spectroscopic analysis. Approximately 1–2 mg of dried enzyme powder was thoroughly mixed with 100–200 mg spectroscopic-grade KBr and pressed into a transparent pellet (≈7–13 mm dia.) using a hydraulic press. Pellets were handled in a desiccator until analysis to avoid moisture-related artifacts. Spectra were recorded in transmission mode over 4000–400 cm–1 with 4 cm–1 resolution and 32 coadded scans; baseline correction and normalization were applied prior to spectral interpretation.

Effect of Temperature on Tannase Enzyme Activity

The influence of temperature on extracellular tannase activity was examined by incubating the standard reaction mixture containing 0.25 mL enzyme extract and 0.25 mL of 0.01 M methyl gallate substrate (prepared in 0.05 M citrate buffer, pH 5.0) at temperatures ranging from 25 to 60 °C. Each reaction was allowed to proceed for 10 min, after which it was terminated by the addition of rhodanine reagent followed by KOH, and absorbance was measured at 520 nm. The temperature-dependent activity was expressed relative to the maximum observed value.

Effect of pH on Tannase Enzyme Activity

The effect of pH on tannase activity was determined using 0.01 M methyl gallate prepared in a series of buffers (0.05 M) covering a pH range from 4 to 8: citrate buffer, phosphate buffer, and Tris–HCl buffer. Reaction mixtures containing enzyme and substrate were incubated at 37 °C for 10 min, followed by addition of rhodanine reagent and KOH to stop the reaction. Tannase activity was quantified spectrophotometrically at 520 nm. Relative activity was calculated as the percentage of the highest activity detected across the pH gradient.

Kinetic Analysis of Partially Purified Tannase

Kinetic parameters for the partially purified tannase were estimated using tannic acid as the substrate under the optimized assay conditions. Reactions proceeded in 0.05 M citrate buffer at pH 6.0, maintained at 50 °C, and allowed to run for a fixed period of 60 min. A series of tannic acid solutions was prepared across concentrations (0.125, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 16, 20, and 24 mg mL–1), covering a broad substrate range commonly employed in fungal tannase kinetic studies. At each substrate level, the reaction mixture consisted of tannic acid dissolved in citrate buffer and was equilibrated at 50 °C before initiating the reaction through addition of a constant volume of enzyme solution. After the 60 min incubation, tannase activity was quantified using the standard assay, and reaction velocities (v) were expressed as enzyme activity units per milliliter (U mL–1) and treated as apparent initial rates under conditions minimizing substrate depletion. Michaelis–Menten kinetic constants were obtained by fitting initial velocity values to the Michaelis–Menten equation v=Vmax[S]Km+[S] where v refers to the reaction velocity, V max denotes the maximum attainable velocity of the reaction, [S] corresponds to the substrate concentration, and K m represents the Michaelis constant. To further inspect the kinetic behavior, a Lineweaver–Burk double-reciprocal plot was generated by plotting 1/v versus 1/[S], following the equation shown below 1v=(KmVmax)(1[S])+1Vmax

The K m and V max values were derived from the slope and intercept of the resulting linear regression line. Overall, the kinetic evaluation followed procedures commonly applied in the characterization of fungal tannases. ,

Effect of Reaction Time on Tannase Enzyme Activity

To evaluate the effect of reaction time on enzyme activity, tannase assays were carried out by incubating enzyme–substrate mixtures (methyl gallate 0.01 M in citrate buffer, pH 5.0) at 37 °C for time intervals ranging from 10 to 80 min. At each time point, the reaction was terminated using rhodanine reagent followed by KOH, and absorbance was measured at 520 nm. The activity curve was plotted to determine the optimum reaction time for maximum product formation. This procedure was adapted from reaction-kinetics studies of fungal tannase.

Assessment of Textile Dye Degradation Properties of Tannase Enzyme

The dye decolorization potential of the purified tannase enzyme extract was evaluated using the agar well diffusion technique. Briefly, 20 mL of sterilized agar medium (composed of 0.5% peptone, 0.3% yeast extract, 0.1% KH2PO4, 0.05% MgSO4·7H2O, and 2% agar; pH 7.0) was poured into sterile Petri dishes and allowed to solidify. Subsequently, 100 μL of individual textile dyes, including reactive violet, reactive green, and reactive red textile dyes with concentrations covering the range from 0.05 to 0.5%, were evenly spread onto the surface of the plates before inoculation. After solidification, wells of 6 mm diameter were aseptically punched into the agar; then each well was filled with 100 μL of the purified tannase enzyme extract. The plates were incubated at 30 ± 2 °C for 72 h., and the formation of decolorized (clear) zones around each well was measured in millimeters.

Application of Fungal Tannase as a Juice Clarification Agent

Juices’ Tannin Content Clarification

The six varieties of fruits (apple, guava, pineapple, orange, strawberry, and pomegranate) were cut into smaller pieces and combined with water (1:1 w/v), and the juices were extracted using a blender. One hundred milliliters of each fruit juice was combined with 1 mL of fungal partially purified 0.5 and 1 mg/mL tannase individually and incubated at 28 °C for 2 h with agitation speed adjusted to 150 rpm using a rotary shaker. To investigate the potential of Tannase as a clarifying agent in fruit juices, untreated juice was used as a control and compared to treated juice.

Physicochemical Properties and Microbial Load of Tannase-Treated Juices

Physicochemical properties of tannase were measured and compared with the control sample. The turbidity of the juice, expressed in nephelometric turbidity unit (NTU), was measured using a digital turbidity meter. The pH was analyzed using a pH meter at 25 ± 1 °C. Viscosity, expressed in centipoise (cP), was analyzed using a viscometer (DV2TLV, Brookfield, WI) attached with an ultralow viscous adaptor at 25 ± 1 °C. Clarity and ascorbic acid content were measured. , Clarity was determined using a UV–visible spectrophotometer by determining transmittance (%T) at 660 nm, whereas ascorbic acid was evaluated using a titration method using oxalic acid and 2,6-dichlorophenolindophenol as the titration medium. The method proposed in the Pradhan et al. study was used to measure the antioxidant activity and is expressed as % DPPH scavenging activity. Juice samples were analyzed for survival of psychrophilic bacteria, yeast, and mold during storage time at 7 °C for 60 days using the plate count technique on plate count agar and potato dextrose (PDA) agar media.

Data Processing and Analysis

All experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates, each analyzed in technical triplicate. The resulting data were evaluated at the 5% significance level using IBM SPSS Statistics Server Version 23.0 (2015), as outlined by Duncan’s Multiple Range Test.

The process of tannase enzyme production through submerged fermentation was implemented for a course of time equal to 6 days. Initially, enzyme activity was undetectable, then increased to 11.4 U/mL at 24 h and 68.4 U/mL at 48 h, then increased further to 123.1 U/mL at 72 h. Maximum activity (136.8 U/mL) was recorded at 96 h, after which activity decreased to 116.3 U/mL at 120 h and 100.3 U/mL at 144 h. Biomass (cell dry weight) followed a similar course and reached its peak (2.746 g·L–1) at 96 h before declining (Table , Figure ).

SDS-PAGE Profiling of Partially Purified Tannase

SDS-PAGE analysis performed under nondenaturing conditions revealed a prominent high-molecular-weight protein band exceeding 250 kDa, corresponding to the upper limit of the molecular weight marker. The observed electrophoretic profile suggests that the tannase enzyme exists predominantly in a native oligomeric or multimeric form rather than as a monomeric polypeptide under the conditions tested. The high apparent molecular mass is consistent with previous reports describing multimeric structures of microbial tannases, particularly under native electrophoretic conditions (Figure ).

Kinetic Analysis of Partially Purified Tannase

The kinetic profile of the partially purified tannase was examined using tannic acid as the substrate under the selected assay conditions. As substrate levels increased, the reaction velocity rose in a manner that closely resembled classical Michaelis–Menten saturation kinetics, with enzyme activity climbing steadily as more tannic acid became available (Figure A). At the lower end of the concentration range, small increases in substrate led to pronounced gains in reaction rate, suggesting that enzyme performance was strongly limited by substrate availability at this stage. As concentrations increased further, however, the rate increments became noticeably smaller, hinting that the enzyme was gradually approaching its catalytic capacity. From these data, kinetic parameters were estimated, yielding a K m of 8.4 mg mL–1 and a V max of 4256 U mL–1, values derived through fitting to the Michaelis–Menten equation and cross-checked using Lineweaver–Burk linearization. These parameters describe, respectively, the substrate concentration needed to reach half-maximal velocity and the upper limit of catalytic output under the conditions tested. Across the examined concentration range, the reaction rate continued to rise toward saturation without forming a sharply defined plateau, a pattern that aligns well with the calculated kinetic constants. Consistent with this interpretation, the Lineweaver–Burk plot (Figure B) showed a clear linear relationship between 1/v and 1/[S]. The slope and intercept of this regression were used to extract K m and V max values, lending additional confidence to the reliability of the kinetic estimates.

Kinetic characterization of partially purified tannase. (A) Michaelis–Menten plot showing reaction velocity as a function of tannic acid concentration. (B) Lineweaver–Burk double reciprocal plot used for kinetic parameter determination.

Impact of Reaction Time on Enzyme Activity

Time-course assays revealed increasing product formation up to 60 min, where activity reached 177.2 U/mL. Shorter incubations yielded proportionally lower activities (36.4–165.5 U/mL for 10–50 min), and longer incubations beyond 60 min produced decreased measured activity (142.8 U/mL at 70 min and 136.3 U/mL at 80 min). These data indicated an optimal reaction time of 60 min under the assay conditions (Table , Figure ).

Physicochemical Parameters and Microbial Load of Tannase-Treated Juices

Physicochemical measurements demonstrated that tannase treatment reduced turbidity and viscosity and increased clarity (% transmittance) in all juices compared to untreated controls. For example, apple turbidity decreased from 897 NTU (control) to 412 NTU (0.5 mg/mL) and to 376 NTU (1.0 mg/mL). Viscosity and clarity values improved correspondingly. pH values showed minor shifts after treatment, while ascorbic acid content and antioxidant activity displayed modest declines with increasing enzyme concentration (Table ). Microbial count data over storage indicated general declines in psychrophilic bacterial counts and in mold/yeast counts for treated samples, with several treated samples showing nondetectable counts at later storage points compared with persistent counts in untreated controls (Tables and ).

Discussion

Microbial enzymes are widely recognized for their efficiency and versatility in industrial and biotechnological applications, owing to their extracellular nature, catalytic specificity, and adaptability to diverse process conditions. Among filamentous fungi, Aspergillus terreus has attracted considerable interest as a prolific producer of bioactive metabolites and industrially relevant enzymes. In this context, the present study demonstrates that a soil-derived strain of A. terreus is capable of producing substantial levels of extracellular tannase under submerged fermentation (SmF) conditions, with enzyme activity reaching a maximum at 96 h before declining at extended incubation times. This time-course profile closely mirrors the characteristic growth-associated production behavior reported for microbial tannases, where enzyme synthesis typically peaks during the late exponential or early stationary phase in parallel with maximal biomass accumulation, followed by a decline attributed to nutrient depletion, metabolite accumulation, proteolytic degradation, or catabolite repression. ,− The observed parallel increase in biomass and tannase activity, together with the higher rate of enzyme formation during the exponential growth phase, supports the classification of tannase as a predominantly growth-associated extracellular enzyme. Similar relationships between microbial growth and tannase secretion have been described for both fungal and bacterial systems, including Aspergillus species and Bacillus licheniformis, where enzyme synthesis accelerates during active growth and diminishes once growth slows. ,− The superior tannase activity obtained under submerged fermentation compared with liquid-surface fermentation further aligns with earlier reports emphasizing the advantages of SmF, particularly enhanced oxygen transfer, improved nutrient homogeneity, and more efficient extracellular enzyme secretion. ,,, In addition, cultivation parameters exerted a pronounced influence on enzyme production. Optimal tannase yields were achieved at moderate tannic acid concentrations (approximately 1.0–2.0%), while higher substrate levels resulted in inhibitory effects, consistent with previous findings that excessive tannin concentrations can impair fungal metabolism and enzyme biosynthesis. − ,, Likewise, optimal production temperatures around 28–32 °C and mildly acidic initial pH values are in good agreement with reported preferences for fungal tannases, particularly those derived from Aspergillus species. − , Partial purification of the enzyme using solvent-based precipitation led to modest increases in specific activity and recovery, reflecting its role as a preliminary downstream step rather than a definitive purification strategy. Comparable purification folds, typically in the range of 2–4-fold, have been widely reported for tannases from fungal and bacterial sources using ethanol or ammonium sulfate precipitation methods. ,, The appearance of clear hydrolysis halos on tannic acid agar plates further supports the extracellular nature of tannase production and is consistent with qualitative screening approaches commonly employed for tannase-producing microorganisms. , Native PAGE analysis revealed a prominent high-molecular-weight band exceeding 250 kDa, suggesting that the tannase exists in a multimeric form under nondenaturing conditions. Reported molecular weights of fungal tannases under denaturing SDS-PAGE typically range between 60 and 120 kDa, including approximately 90–100 kDa for Aspergillus niger tannase. , The higher apparent molecular mass observed here likely reflects oligomeric assembly rather than monomeric size, a phenomenon also reported in partially characterized microbial tannases. FTIR analysis of the partially purified enzyme revealed spectral features characteristic of proteinaceous materials, including O–H/N–H stretching and amide I bands, confirming retention of the enzyme’s peptide backbone following purification. Such findings are consistent with earlier reports in which FTIR spectroscopy was applied as a supplementary tool to verify enzyme identity while acknowledging its limited utility for detailed structural or purity assessment. ,, Functional characterization further showed that tannase activity was strongly dependent on temperature and pH, with optimal catalytic performance observed at elevated temperatures and near-neutral pH values, falling within the operational ranges reported for fungal and yeast tannases. ,, The Michaelis–Menten kinetic behavior observed in this study, together with the estimated K m and V max values, is consistent with previously reported tannase kinetics, where substrate affinity and catalytic efficiency vary widely depending on enzyme source, substrate type, and degree of purification. Comparable kinetic profiles have been described for tannases from Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Lactobacillus plantarum, supporting the placement of the present enzyme within the expected catalytic performance range of microbial tannases. ,, The qualitative decolorization of reactive textile dyes observed using agar diffusion assays aligns with earlier studies employing similar screening methods to demonstrate preliminary dye–enzyme interactions. As documented previously, such assays serve as qualitative indicators rather than quantitative measures of dye degradation and are typically used as an initial evaluation of enzymatic potential. ,, Finally, the laboratory-scale juice clarification experiments, characterized by reduced turbidity and viscosity, enhanced clarity, and partial tannin hydrolysis, are consistent with numerous reports describing tannase-mediated clarification of fruit juices. Similar physicochemical improvements have been widely reported, reinforcing both the observed trends and the laboratory-scale, application-oriented nature of the present findings. ,,,

Conclusion

Overall, the present study demonstrates that Aspergillus terreus is capable of producing tannase under laboratory-scale submerged fermentation conditions. The partially purified tannase exhibited measurable activity and showed interaction with synthetic dyes in qualitative decolorization assays, as well as improvement of selected physicochemical properties of fruit juices during clarification experiments. These findings indicate the feasibility of utilizing Aspergillus terreus-derived tannase for preliminary application studies at the laboratory level. However, further investigations involve comprehensive purification and quantitative performance evaluation, and comparative benchmarking are required to assess the enzyme’s functional efficiency and applicability beyond laboratory-scale conditions.
