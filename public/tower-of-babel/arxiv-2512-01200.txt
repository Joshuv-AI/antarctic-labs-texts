Role of Quantum Geometry in the Competition between Higgs Mode and Quasiparticles in Third-Harmonic Generation of Superconductors
Chang-geun Oh, Haruki Watanabe, Naoto Tsuji

Abstract
Collective modes in superconductors, such as the Higgs mode, offer deep insights into the nature of condensates. Third-harmonic generation (THG) is a primary tool for probing the Higgs mode, but its signal competes with that of quasiparticle excitations depending on impurity scattering rates. In particular, in the clean regime the standard BCS theory generally predicts the dominance of quasiparticle contributions. Here, we propose and demonstrate that the quantum geometry of electronic bands can be a key mechanism governing this competition. By developing a formalism that explicitly incorporates the quantum metric, and applying it to a tunable model of a dispersive-band superconductor, we show that the quantum metric can dramatically amplify the nonlinear light-Higgs coupling by several orders of magnitude. Our results establish that a large quantum metric can cause the Higgs mode to dominate the THG response, resolving the puzzle of Higgs and quasiparticle competition in the clean regime and identifying band geometry as a crucial ingredient for designing and understanding the nonlinear response of superconductors.



Introduction.

Superconductors are not merely perfect conductors but represent a macroscopic quantum state formed by the condensation of Cooper pairs

;

;
Higgs 1964
;
Guralnik et al. 1964
. In contrast, the Higgs mode remains a gapped excitation near the superconducting gap energy,
2\Delta

;
Méasson et al. 2014
;

;
Matsunaga et al. 2014
;

;
Vaswani et al. 2021
.

The THG signal, which appears under the resonance condition
2\Omega=2\Delta
(where
\Omega
is the frequency of the incident light), has been considered a powerful tool for probing the existence of the Higgs mode
Matsunaga et al. 2014
;
Tsuji and Aoki 2015
. However, this energy regime is also the threshold for the pair-breaking process of quasiparticles, making it a significant challenge to distinguish between the two contributions. According to the standard BCS theory for single-band superconductors in the clean limit, the THG signal is expected to be predominantly governed by quasiparticle excitations

However, experiments on materials such as NbN have indicated that the Higgs mode in fact dominates the THG resonance; this is supported by polarization-angle dependence measurements of THG

While this mechanism is plausible, the interpretation of experimental results often requires a detailed comparison to material-specific theoretical calculations. Thus, a universal, material-independent principle governing the competition between the Higgs and quasiparticle channels has remained a key open question in the field

However, the role of quantum geometry in conventional superconductors with dispersive bands—which constitute the vast majority of known materials—remains a largely unexplored and crucial question in the context of nonlinear optics, especially for its influence on collective modes like the Higgs mode.

In this paper, we show that the quantum geometry of the electronic wavefunction plays a key role in determining the relative contributions of the Higgs mode and quasiparticles in the THG response. We demonstrate that the light-matter interaction mediated by this quantum metric acts asymmetrically on the Higgs mode and quasiparticle channels, leading to a dramatic amplification of the nonlinear optical response of the Higgs mode.

Formalism.

We begin with a Hamiltonian for a multiband system (setting
\hbar=e=c=1
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}_{0}+\mathcal{H}_{\rm int},

(1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{k}\sigma\alpha\beta}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}\alpha\sigma}\,\mathcal{H}_{\alpha\beta}^{\sigma}(\bm{k})\,c_{\bm{k}\beta\sigma},

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\rm int}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\bm{k}\bm{k}^{\prime}\alpha\beta}U_{\alpha\beta}\,c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}\alpha\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{-\bm{k}\beta\downarrow}c_{-\bm{k}^{\prime}\beta\downarrow}c_{\bm{k}^{\prime}\alpha\uparrow}.

(3)

Introducing the path integral and decoupling the interaction in the pairing channel by a Hubbard–Stratonovich field
\Delta_{\alpha\beta}(\tau)
yields the action
S[c^{\dagger},c,\Delta^{*},\Delta]
; we defer standard steps to the Supplemental Material.

Minimal coupling to an external spatially uniform vector potential
\bm{A}(\tau)
is implemented by
\bm{k}\!\to\!\bm{k}-\bm{A}(\tau)
in
\mathcal{H}_{0}
. Note that for nonlocal electron interactions, the pairing function itself can couple to the gauge field
Oh and Watanabe 2024
.
Assuming a local pairing interaction, only the kinetic term is modified by the vector potential.
Diagonalizing
\mathcal{H}_{0}
at each
\bm{k}
provides band eigenstates
\ket{u_{l\sigma}(\bm{k})}
and dispersions
\epsilon_{l\sigma}(\bm{k})
. We assume (i) time-reversal and inversion symmetry for the normal-state band, (ii) a single band (labeled
m
, which we drop hereafter) dominates at the Fermi level, and (iii) uniform intra-orbital pairing
\Delta_{\alpha\beta}=\Delta\delta_{\alpha\beta}
, a key condition that renders the quantum geometric contribution manifest
Törmä et al. 2018
;
Törmä et al. 2022
;
Peotta and Törmä 2015
. Under these assumptions we project onto the single active band and obtain the effective low-energy action

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\Big\{N_{b}\frac{|\Delta|^{2}}{U}+\sum_{\bm{k},\sigma}c^{\dagger}_{\sigma}(\bm{k})[\partial_{\tau}+\epsilon_{\sigma}(\bm{k}-\bm{A})-\mu]c_{\sigma}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\sum_{\bm{k}}\big[\Delta\,f(\bm{k},\bm{A})\,c^{\dagger}_{\uparrow}(\bm{k})c^{\dagger}_{\downarrow}(-\bm{k})+\text{h.c.}\big]\Big\},

(4)

where
N_{b}
is the number of orbitals and the pairing overlap is

\displaystyle f(\bm{k},\bm{A})=\sum_{\alpha}u_{\alpha}^{*}(\bm{k}+\bm{A})u_{\alpha}(\bm{k}-\bm{A})=\braket{u_{\bm{k}+\bm{A}}|u_{\bm{k}-\bm{A}}}.

(5)

For a long-wavelength vector potential, we Taylor-expand
f(\bm{k},\bm{A})
. With time-reversal and inversion symmetry, the expansion reveals the quantum metric:

|f(\bm{k},\bm{A})|\approx 1-2\,g_{ij}(\bm{k})\,A_{i}A_{j}+\mathcal{O}(A^{4}),

(6)

with the quantum metric defined as
g_{ij}(\bm{k})=\text{Re}\braket{\partial_{i}u_{\bm{k}}|(1-P_{\bm{k}})|\partial_{j}u_{\bm{k}}}
, where
P_{\bm{k}}=\ket{u_{\bm{k}}}\bra{u_{\bm{k}}}
is the projection operator onto the Bloch state.

Introducing the Nambu spinor
\psi_{\bm{k}}(\tau)=(c_{\uparrow}(\bm{k}),c_{\downarrow}^{\dagger}(-\bm{k}))^{T}
, the inverse Nambu Green’s function reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{-1}(\bm{k},\tau)

\displaystyle=-\partial_{\tau}\tau_{0}-\xi_{\bm{k}}\tau_{3}+\Delta_{0}\tau_{1}-\Sigma(\bm{k},\tau),

(7)

where
\xi_{\bm{k}}=\epsilon_{\bm{k}}-\mu
and the self-energy due to amplitude fluctuation
\rho(\tau)
and the electromagnetic field (up to
O(A^{2})
) is

\displaystyle\Sigma(\bm{k},\tau)

\displaystyle=\rho(\tau)\tau_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{ij}^{2}\xi_{\bm{k}}\,A_{i}(\tau)A_{j}(\tau)\,\tau_{3}

\displaystyle\quad+2(\Delta_{0}+\rho(\tau))\,g_{ij}(\bm{k})\,A_{i}(\tau)A_{j}(\tau)\,\tau_{1}.

(8)

Expanding the fermion determinant to quartic order in fields and integrating out the fermions and the Higgs field
\rho
gives the effective electromagnetic action

S[A]=\sum_{ijkl}\int\!d\omega\;A_{ij}^{2}(-\omega)\,K_{ijkl}(\omega)\,A_{kl}^{2}(\omega).

(9)

The optical kernel
K_{ijkl}(\omega)
is the central result of our formalism; it decomposes into contributions from quasiparticles (qp) and the Higgs mode,
K_{ijkl}=K_{ijkl}^{\mathrm{qp}}+K_{ijkl}^{\mathrm{Higgs}}
. Crucially, each of these contributions can be further separated into a conventional term from the band dispersion (band) and a novel term from the quantum geometry (geom).

The quasiparticle contribution is explicitly decomposed as

\displaystyle K_{ijkl}^{\rm qp}(\omega)=\underbrace{\sum_{\bm{k}}\tfrac{1}{4}\partial_{ij}^{2}\xi_{\bm{k}}\partial_{kl}^{2}\xi_{\bm{k}}\chi_{33}(\bm{k},\omega)}_{K^{\mathrm{qp}}_{\mathrm{band}}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\sum_{\bm{k}}2\Delta_{0}\partial_{ij}^{2}\xi_{\bm{k}}g_{kl}(\bm{k})\chi_{13}(\bm{k},\omega)+4\Delta_{0}^{2}g_{ij}(\bm{k})g_{kl}(\bm{k})\chi_{11}(\bm{k},\omega)}_{K^{\mathrm{qp}}_{\mathrm{geom}}},

(10)

and the Higgs-mediated contribution reads

\displaystyle K_{ijkl}^{\rm Higgs}(\omega)

\displaystyle=-V_{ij}(\omega)\,\mathcal{G}_{H}^{0}(\omega)\,V_{kl}(\omega),

(11)

where
\mathcal{G}_{H}^{0}(\omega)
is the bare propagator for the Higgs amplitude mode. The Higgs-light coupling vertex
V_{ij}(\omega)
itself contains both band and geometric parts:

\displaystyle V_{ij}(\omega)=\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{k}}\partial_{ij}^{2}\xi_{\bm{k}}\chi_{13}(\bm{k},\omega)}_{V^{\text{band}}_{ij}}+\underbrace{2\Delta_{0}\sum_{\bm{k}}g_{ij}(\bm{k})\chi_{11}(\bm{k},\omega)}_{V^{\text{geom}}_{ij}}.

(12)

The bubble functions
\chi_{\alpha\beta}
are defined in Matsubara frequency as

\chi_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{k},i\omega_{m})=\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{i\omega_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathcal{G}_{0}(\bm{k},i\omega_{n})\tau_{\alpha}\mathcal{G}_{0}(\bm{k},i\omega_{n}+i\omega_{m})\tau_{\beta}\big]
, with
\mathcal{G}_{0}
being the mean-field Nambu Green’s function. Finally, the third-harmonic current for a monochromatic drive
A_{i}(t)=a_{i}e^{-i\Omega t}+a_{i}^{*}e^{i\Omega t}
is obtained by functional differentiation
j_{m}(t)=-{\delta S}/{\delta A_{m}(t)}
:

\displaystyle j_{m}(3\Omega)=-4\sum_{jkl}\big[K_{mjkl}^{\rm qp}(2\Omega)+K_{mjkl}^{\rm Higgs}(2\Omega)\big]\,a_{j}a_{k}a_{l}.

(13)

Application to a Quadratic Band Touching Model.

To isolate the role of quantum geometry, we employ a specially designed two-band model that hosts a quadratic band touching at the
\Gamma
point (
\bm{k}=0
). The explicit form and details are described in the SI.
The model is constructed to produce a familiar energy band dispersions, given by
E_{+}(\bm{k})=-2t(\cos k_{x}+\cos k_{y})
and
E_{-}(\bm{k})=-2t_{b}(\cos k_{x}+\cos k_{y})+4(t_{b}-t)
, as shown in Fig.
1
(a). A key feature of this model is that these energy dispersions remain fixed while the quantum geometry of the Bloch states is continuously tuned by a parameter
d_{\mathrm{max}}\in[0,1]
, which physically corresponds to the maximum quantum distance between Bloch states within the same band over the Brillouin zone and directly controls the quantum metric
Rhim et al. 2020
;
Oh et al. 2022
;
Oh et al. 2025a
.
Since all components of the quantum metric are proportional to its square (
g_{ij}\propto d_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}
),
d_{\mathrm{max}}=0
defines a geometrically trivial model, while
d_{\mathrm{max}}=1
represents a maximally nontrivial case.
This construction allows us to systematically isolate geometric effects on the THG response.

In our analysis, we focus exclusively on the upper electronic band,
E_{+}(\bm{k})
, (assuming
t_{b}<0
) and consider the Fermi level sufficiently far from the band touching point at
\bm{k}=\bm{0}
.
Provided that the superconducting gap and relevant excitation energies satisfy
\Delta_{0},\hbar\Omega,k_{B}T\ll\text{min}_{k\in\text{FS}}|E_{+}(k)-E_{-}(k)|
, interband pairing and virtual interband transitions are negligible, and the single-band effective theory introduced in the previous section is justified.

Correlation length.

Beyond the electromagnetic response, the quantum geometry of the Bloch states also fundamentally determines the spatial properties of the Higgs mode, such as its correlation length
\xi_{H}

Xiao and Hao 2025
, the characteristic spatial scale over which fluctuations in the amplitude of the superconducting order parameter remain correlated.
To investigate this, we analyze the effective action for the order parameter fluctuations in the absence of an external field (
\bm{A}=0
), focusing on the static, long-wavelength limit. As detailed in the Supplemental Material, by expanding the effective action for the amplitude mode
\rho(\bm{q})
to second order in momentum
\bm{q}
, we obtain a Ginzburg-Landau-type propagator
\braket{\rho(\bm{q})\rho(-\bm{q})}\propto(r_{\rho}+A_{ij}q_{i}q_{j})^{-1}
. From this, the Higgs correlation length tensor is identified as
\xi_{H,ij}^{2}=A_{ij}/r_{\rho}
.

Similar to the optical kernel, the stiffness tensor
A_{ij}
can be decomposed into a part from the band dispersion and a part from the quantum geometry:

\displaystyle\xi_{H,ij}^{2}=\xi_{\text{band},ij}^{2}+\xi_{\text{geom},ij}^{2},

(14)

where the explicit form of each term is given in the Supplemental Material. The geometric contribution
\xi_{\text{geom},ij}^{2}
is directly related to the quantum metric
g_{ij}(\bm{k})
.

We now quantify the geometric contribution to the correlation length using the quadratic band touching model. The calculation uses the same parameters as in the THG analysis in Fig.
1
(c,d).
Since the band dispersion in our model is independent of
d_{\mathrm{max}}
, the band contribution
\xi_{\text{band},xx}^{2}
remains constant.
Therefore, any variation in the total correlation length directly measures the effect of quantum geometry. Figure
2
(a) shows the calculated total squared correlation length,
\xi_{H,xx}^{2}
, which increases monotonically as a function of
d_{\text{max}}
. To illustrate the dramatic crossover in the underlying contributions, we plot the ratio
\xi_{\text{geom},xx}^{2}/\xi_{\text{band},xx}^{2}
in Fig.
2
(b). The ratio grows from zero to a value greater than one, signifying a clear transition from a band-dominated regime to one where the correlation length is overwhelmingly determined by quantum geometry.
This result unequivocally demonstrates that the quantum metric provides a significant contribution to the stiffness of the Higgs mode. While the stiffness against spatial variations of the order parameter’s amplitude is primarily determined by the kinetic energy of electrons, our finding reveals that the geometric properties of the Bloch wavefunctions themselves provide an additional contribution to this stiffness.
This enhancement of the correlation length is a direct manifestation of how the underlying geometry of the quantum states governs the macroscopic rigidity of the superconducting condensate’s amplitude.

To complement the above discussion of the Higgs correlation length and the stiffness tensor
A_{ij}
, we evaluate the superfluid stiffness
D_{ij}
for our model.
The superfluid stiffness
D_{ij}
decomposes into a conventional band contribution and a quantum-geometry contribution,
D_{ij}=D^{\mathrm{band}}_{ij}+D^{\mathrm{geom}}_{ij}

Liang et al. 2017
;
Peotta and Törmä 2015
.
The geometric part increases as
d_{\max}
increases, indicating that parameter regimes with an enhanced quantum metric not only strengthen the geometric part of
A_{ij}
but also increase the superfluid stiffness. Detailed expressions and the
d_{\max}
-dependence are provided in SI.

Conclusion.

In this work, we have theoretically investigated the role of quantum geometry in the nonlinear optical response of superconductors, focusing on the competition between the Higgs mode and quasiparticle contributions to THG. By developing a formalism based on a single-band projection of a multiband Hamiltonian, we explicitly derived the THG optical kernel, systematically decomposing it into band and geometric contributions.
Applying our formalism to a tunable quadratic band touching model, where the quantum geometry can be varied independently of the band structure, we have demonstrated that the quantum metric provides a significant contribution to the nonlinear light-matter coupling. Our key finding is that a prominent quantum geometry can enhance the THG response of the Higgs mode by several orders of magnitude. Furthermore, we have shown that the Higgs correlation length is also enhanced by the quantum geometry, directly linking the spatial rigidity of the condensate’s amplitude to the underlying wavefunction geometry.

Our findings provide a fundamental and general mechanism that can enhance the Higgs-mode contribution to THG.
While previous theories have invoked impurity scattering or phonon retardation effects that may depend on material details,
our work suggests that a large quantum metric can be a universal principle for achieving a strong Higgs response in dispersive-band systems in the clean regime. This shifts the focus from extrinsic factors to an intrinsic, geometric property of the electronic states themselves.

Experimentally, our work calls for a targeted search for strong Higgs responses in materials known to possess a large quantum metric, such as those with nearly flat bands. Probing the THG response while tuning the quantum geometry—for instance, by applying an electric field or strain—could provide a direct verification of our theory. Ultimately, our study establishes the engineering of quantum geometry as a new and promising route for controlling and amplifying the collective dynamics of superconductors.

Appendix A
Derivation of the third harmonic generation optical kernel

We start from the Hamiltonian without the electromagnetic field,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}_{0}+\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{int}},

(15)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{k}\sigma\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}\alpha\sigma}\mathcal{H}_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}^{\sigma}(\bm{k})c_{\bm{k}\alpha^{\prime}\sigma},

(16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{int}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\bm{k}\bm{k}^{\prime}\alpha\beta}U_{\alpha\beta}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}\alpha\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{-\bm{k}\beta\downarrow}c_{-\bm{k}^{\prime}\beta\downarrow}c_{\bm{k}^{\prime}\alpha\uparrow}.

(17)

We assume the two-body interaction acts only in the Cooper channel between
(\bm{k},\uparrow)
and
(-\bm{k},\downarrow)
.

Define the finite-temperature partition function

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}(c^{\dagger},c)\,e^{-S[c^{\dagger},c]},

(18)

\displaystyle S[c^{\dagger},c]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\!\left(\sum_{\bm{k}\sigma\alpha}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}\alpha\sigma}\partial_{\tau}c_{\bm{k}\alpha\sigma}+\mathcal{H}\right).

(19)

(For simplicity we set the system volume to unity.)

We decouple
\mathcal{H}_{\rm int}
by a Hubbard–Stratonovich (HS) transformation in the pairing channel:

\displaystyle\exp\!\Big(\sum_{\bm{k}\bm{k}^{\prime}\alpha\beta}U_{\alpha\beta}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}\alpha\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{-\bm{k}\beta\downarrow}c_{-\bm{k}^{\prime}\beta\downarrow}c_{\bm{k}^{\prime}\alpha\uparrow}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\rightarrow\int\mathcal{D}(\Delta^{*},\Delta)\;\exp\Big(-\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\frac{|\Delta_{\alpha\beta}|^{2}}{U_{\alpha\beta}}+\sum_{\bm{k},\alpha,\beta}(\Delta_{\alpha\beta}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}\alpha\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{-\bm{k}\beta\downarrow}+\mathrm{h.c.})\Big).

(20)

The action becomes

\displaystyle S[c^{\dagger},c,\Delta^{*},\Delta]=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\bigg\{\sum_{\bm{k},\sigma,\alpha,\beta}c^{\dagger}_{\alpha\sigma}(\bm{k})\big[(\partial_{\tau}-\mu)\delta_{\alpha\beta}+\mathcal{H}^{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{k})\big]c_{\beta}(\bm{k})\bigg\}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\bigg\{-\sum_{\bm{k},\alpha,\beta}\big[\Delta_{\alpha\beta}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}\alpha\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{-\bm{k}\beta\downarrow}+\mathrm{h.c.}\big]+\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\frac{|\Delta_{\alpha\beta}|^{2}}{U_{\alpha\beta}}\bigg\}.

(21)

Introduce the vector potential
\bm{A}
by minimal coupling

\mathcal{H}^{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{k})\ \to\ \mathcal{H}^{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{k}-\bm{A})
(setting
e=c=1
).
Note that for nonlocal electron interactions, the pairing function itself can couple to the gauge field
Oh and Watanabe 2024
.
Assuming a local pairing interaction, only the kinetic term is modified by the vector potential.
Then the action with gauge field reads

\displaystyle S[c^{\dagger},c,\Delta^{*},\Delta]=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\bigg\{\sum_{\bm{k},\sigma,\alpha,\beta}c^{\dagger}_{\alpha\sigma}(\bm{k})\big[(\partial_{\tau}-\mu)\delta_{\alpha\beta}+\mathcal{H}^{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{k}-\bm{A})\big]c_{\beta}(\bm{k})\bigg\}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\bigg\{-\sum_{\bm{k},\alpha,\beta}\big[\Delta_{\alpha\beta}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}\alpha\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{-\bm{k}\beta\downarrow}+\mathrm{h.c.}\big]+\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\frac{|\Delta_{\alpha\beta}|^{2}}{U_{\alpha\beta}}\bigg\}.

(22)

To analyze the physics in terms of electronic bands, we now perform a change of basis from the orbital basis (
c_{\alpha\sigma}
) to the band basis (
c_{l\sigma}
).
Once we use the unitary matrix
u_{l\alpha}^{\sigma}(\bm{k})
to diagonalize the non-interacting Hamiltonian and find the band dispersions
\epsilon_{l\sigma}(\bm{k})
where
l
is the band index, we can rewrite the kernel of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
as

\mathcal{H}_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}^{\sigma}(\bm{k})=\sum_{l}u_{l\alpha}^{\sigma}(\bm{k})\epsilon_{l\sigma}(\bm{k})u_{l\alpha^{\prime}}^{\sigma*}(\bm{k}).

(23)

The transformed fermion operators are
c_{l\sigma\bm{A}}(\bm{k})=\sum_{\alpha}u_{l\alpha}^{\sigma*}(\bm{k}-\bm{A})c_{\alpha\sigma}(\bm{k})
.
Then, the action becomes

\displaystyle S[c^{\dagger},c,\Delta^{*},\Delta]=S_{\text{kin}}+S_{\text{int}},

(24)

where

\displaystyle S_{\text{kin}}=\int^{\beta}_{0}d\tau~\bigg\{\sum_{\bm{k},\sigma}\sum_{l}[\partial_{\tau}+\epsilon_{l\sigma}(\bm{k}-\bm{A})-\mu]c^{\dagger}_{l\sigma\bm{A}}(\bm{k})c_{l\sigma\bm{A}}(\bm{k})\bigg\},

\displaystyle S_{\text{int}}=\int^{\beta}_{0}d\tau~\bigg\{\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\frac{|\Delta_{\alpha\beta}|^{2}}{U_{\alpha\beta}}-\sum_{\bm{k},\alpha,\beta,l,l^{\prime}}\left[\Delta_{\alpha\beta}\Big(u^{\uparrow*}_{l\alpha}(\bm{k-A})u^{\downarrow*}_{l^{\prime}\beta}(\bm{-k-A})\Big)c^{\dagger}_{l\uparrow\bm{A}}(\bm{k})c^{\dagger}_{l^{\prime}\downarrow\bm{A}}(-\bm{k})+h.c.\right]\bigg\}.

(25)

We consider time-reversal symmetry,
u_{\alpha}^{\downarrow}(-\bm{k})=u^{\uparrow*}_{\alpha}(\bm{k})
. Furthermore, we assume that a single band (labeled by index
m
) near the Fermi level dominates the low-energy physics. We therefore neglect all other bands. For simplicity, we drop the band index
m
from here on (
c_{m\sigma\bm{A}}\to c_{\sigma\bm{A}},u_{m\alpha}\to u_{\alpha}
).
We also assume intra-orbital uniform pairing

\Delta_{\alpha\beta}=\Delta\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}
,
which is important to capture quantum-geometric effects on superconductivity
Törmä et al. 2018
;
Törmä et al. 2022
;
Peotta and Törmä 2015
. Applying these approximations, the action simplifies to

\displaystyle S[c^{\dagger},c,\Delta^{*},\Delta]=

\displaystyle\int^{\beta}_{0}d\tau~\bigg\{N_{b}\frac{|\Delta|^{2}}{U}+\sum_{\bm{k},\sigma}[\partial_{\tau}+\epsilon_{\sigma}(\bm{k-A})-\mu]c^{\dagger}_{\sigma\bm{A}}(\bm{k})c_{\sigma\bm{A}}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle-\sum_{\bm{k}}\left[\Delta f(\bm{k},\bm{A})c^{\dagger}_{\uparrow\bm{A}}(\bm{k})c^{\dagger}_{\downarrow\bm{A}}(-\bm{k})+h.c.\right]\bigg\},

(26)

with

f(\bm{k},\bm{A})=\sum_{\alpha}u_{\alpha}^{*}(\bm{k}+\bm{A})u_{\alpha}(\bm{k}-\bm{A})=\langle m,\bm{k}+\bm{A}\,|\,m,\bm{k}-\bm{A}\rangle,

(27)

and
N_{b}
the number of orbitals. Note that
f(\bm{k},\bm{0})=1
and
f^{*}(\bm{k},\bm{A})=f(\bm{k},-\bm{A})
.
To second order in
\bm{A}
the overlap is

f(\bm{k},\bm{A})=|f(\bm{k},\bm{A})|\approx 1-2g_{ij}(\bm{k})A_{i}A_{j}+\mathcal{O}(A^{3}),

(28)

where
g_{ij}(\bm{k})
is the quantum metric of the Bloch states.
Here, we ignore the linear term in
A
, since it corresponds to the Berry connection. In the presence of both time-reversal and inversion symmetries, the Berry curvature vanishes, allowing for a choice of a real gauge where the Berry connection is locally zero
Xiao et al. 2010
.
Even in the presence of global topological obstructions, this linear term possesses odd parity with respect to momentum
k
and thus vanishes upon integration over the Brillouin Zone under time-reversal and inversion symmetries.
Crucially, this linear term is irrelevant to the third-harmonic generation, which originates from the nonlinear coupling between the Higgs mode and the electromagnetic field (
\sim A^{2}\rho
).

Introduce the Nambu spinor
\psi_{\bm{k}\bm{A}}(\tau)=(c_{\uparrow\bm{A}}(\bm{k}),\,c_{\downarrow\bm{A}}^{\dagger}(-\bm{k}))^{T}
. The action becomes

\displaystyle S[c^{\dagger},c,\Delta^{*},\Delta]=\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\left\{N_{b}\frac{|\Delta|^{2}}{U}+\sum_{\bm{k}}\psi_{\bm{k}\bm{A}}^{\dagger}(\tau)\,[-\beta G^{-1}(\bm{k},\tau)]\,\psi_{\bm{k}\bm{A}}(\tau)\right\},

(29)

with inverse Nambu propagator

\displaystyle G^{-1}(\bm{k},\tau)=\begin{pmatrix}-\partial_{\tau}-\xi_{\bm{k}-\bm{A}}&\Delta f(\bm{k},\bm{A})\\
\Delta^{*}f^{*}(\bm{k},\bm{A})&-\partial_{\tau}+\xi_{\bm{k}+\bm{A}}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\xi_{\bm{k}}=\epsilon_{\bm{k}}-\mu.

(30)

Split the order parameter into its saddle-point and amplitude fluctuation,

\Delta(\tau)=\Delta_{0}+\rho(\tau),

and expand to second order in
\bm{A}
:

\displaystyle\xi_{\bm{k}\mp\bm{A}}

\displaystyle=\xi_{\bm{k}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\partial^{2}_{ij}\xi_{\bm{k}}A_{i}A_{j}+\mathcal{O}(A^{3}).

(31)

We assume inversion symmetry
\xi_{\bm{k}}=\xi_{-\bm{k}}
so the linear (paramagnetic) term vanishes after
\bm{k}
-integration; only the
A^{2}
diamagnetic term survives in the present context.

Collecting terms, we write

\displaystyle S[\rho,c^{\dagger},c,A]

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{b}\beta}{U}\Delta_{0}^{2}+\frac{N_{b}}{U}\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\,[\rho(\tau)]^{2}+\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\sum_{\bm{k}}\psi_{\bm{k}\bm{A}}^{\dagger}(\tau)\,[-\mathcal{G}^{-1}(\bm{k},\tau)]\,\psi_{\bm{k}\bm{A}}(\tau),

(32)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{-1}(\bm{k},\tau)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{G}_{0}^{-1}(\bm{k},\tau)-\Sigma(\bm{k},\tau),

(33)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{0}^{-1}(\bm{k},\tau)

\displaystyle=-\partial_{\tau}\,\tau_{0}-\xi_{\bm{k}}\tau_{3}+\Delta_{0}\tau_{1},

(34)

\displaystyle\Sigma(\bm{k},\tau)

\displaystyle=\rho(\tau)\tau_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}\partial^{2}_{ij}\xi_{\bm{k}}A_{i}(\tau)A_{j}(\tau)\,\tau_{3}+2\big(\Delta_{0}+\rho(\tau)\big)\sum_{ij}g_{ij}(\bm{k})\,A_{i}(\tau)A_{j}(\tau)\,\tau_{1}.

(35)

Here
\tau_{0,1,2,3}
are Pauli matrices in Nambu space.

Transform to Matsubara frequency and integrate out fermions using

\displaystyle\int D(\psi^{\dagger},\psi)e^{-\int\psi^{\dagger}\mathcal{G}^{-1}\psi}=\text{det}(\beta\mathcal{G}^{-1})=\text{exp}\left(\mathrm{Tr}\ln[\beta\mathcal{G}^{-1}]\right).

(36)

Expanding the fermion determinant to quartic order in fields (i.e. up to
O(A^{4})
and quadratic in
\rho
), using
\text{ln}[\beta\mathcal{G}^{-1}]=\text{ln}[\beta\mathcal{G}_{0}]+\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}\frac{(-1)^{2n-1}}{n}(\mathcal{G}_{0}\Sigma)^{2n}
, and collecting terms relevant for third-harmonic generation, one obtains

\displaystyle S^{(2)}[\rho,A]

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{i\omega_{n}}\rho^{*}(i\omega_{n})\,[\mathcal{G}_{H}^{0}]^{-1}(i\omega_{n})\,\rho(i\omega_{n})

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{i\omega_{m}}\big[\rho^{*}(i\omega_{m})\,b(i\omega_{m})+b^{*}(i\omega_{m})\,\rho(i\omega_{m})\big]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{\bm{k},i\omega_{m}}\frac{1}{4}\,\partial_{ij}^{2}\xi_{\bm{k}}\partial_{kl}^{2}\xi_{\bm{k}}\,\chi_{33}(\bm{k},i\omega_{m})\,A_{ij}^{2}(-i\omega_{m})A_{kl}^{2}(i\omega_{m})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{\bm{k},i\omega_{m}}2\,\Delta_{0}\partial_{ij}^{2}\xi_{\bm{k}}\,g_{kl}(\bm{k})\,\chi_{13}(\bm{k},-i\omega_{m})\,A_{ij}^{2}(-i\omega_{m})A_{kl}^{2}(i\omega_{m})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{\bm{k},i\omega_{m}}4\,\Delta_{0}^{2}g_{ij}(\bm{k})g_{kl}(\bm{k})\,\chi_{11}(\bm{k},i\omega_{m})\,A_{ij}^{2}(-i\omega_{m})A_{kl}^{2}(i\omega_{m}),

(37)

where
\chi_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{k},i\omega_{m})=\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{i\omega_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}[\mathcal{G}_{0}(\bm{k},i\omega_{n})\tau_{\alpha}\mathcal{G}_{0}(\bm{k},i\omega_{n}+i\omega_{m})\tau_{\beta}]
.
The Higgs inverse propagator and source are

\displaystyle[\mathcal{G}_{H}^{0}(i\omega_{n})]^{-1}=\frac{2}{U}+\sum_{\bm{k}}\chi_{11}(\bm{k},i\omega_{n}),

(38)

\displaystyle b(i\omega_{m})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{k}}\sum_{ij}\partial_{ij}^{2}\xi_{\bm{k}}\chi_{13}(\bm{k},i\omega_{m})A_{ij}^{2}(i\omega_{m})

\displaystyle\quad+2\sum_{\bm{k}}\sum_{ij}\Delta_{0}g_{ij}(\bm{k})\chi_{11}(\bm{k},i\omega_{m})A_{ij}^{2}(i\omega_{m}).

(39)

In the above,
A_{ij}^{2}(i\omega)
denotes the Fourier transform of
A_{i}(\tau)A_{j}(\tau)
:

\displaystyle A_{ij}^{2}(i\omega)\equiv\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\,e^{i\omega\tau}\,A_{i}(\tau)A_{j}(\tau),

(40)

which is not equal to
A_{i}(i\omega)A_{j}(i\omega)
in general.

Integrating out the Higgs field
\rho
by completing the square yields

\displaystyle\int D[\rho]\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{2}\rho^{\dagger}\mathcal{G}_{H}^{-1}\rho+\frac{1}{2}\rho^{\dagger}b+\frac{1}{2}b^{\dagger}\rho\Big)=\mathcal{N}\det(\mathcal{G}_{H})\,\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{2}b^{\dagger}\mathcal{G}_{H}b\Big).

(41)

After analytic continuation
i\omega\to\omega+i0^{+}
and rearranging, the effective electromagnetic action can be written as

\displaystyle S[A]=\sum_{ijkl}\int\!d\omega\;A_{ij}^{2}(-\omega)\,K_{ijkl}(\omega)\,A_{kl}^{2}(\omega),

(42)

with the optical kernel decomposed as

\displaystyle K_{ijkl}(\omega)=K_{ijkl}^{\mathrm{qp}}(\omega)+K_{ijkl}^{\mathrm{Higgs}}(\omega).

(43)

The quasiparticle contribution is

\displaystyle K_{ijkl}^{\mathrm{qp}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{k}}\Bigg[\frac{1}{4}\,\partial_{ij}^{2}\xi_{\bm{k}}\;\partial_{kl}^{2}\xi_{\bm{k}}\;\chi_{33}(\bm{k},\omega)+2\,\Delta_{0}\,\partial_{ij}^{2}\xi_{\bm{k}}\;g_{kl}(\bm{k})\;\chi_{13}(\bm{k},\omega)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+4\,\Delta_{0}^{2}\;g_{ij}(\bm{k})\,g_{kl}(\bm{k})\;\chi_{11}(\bm{k},\omega)\Bigg],

(44)

and the Higgs-mediated contribution is

\displaystyle K_{ijkl}^{\mathrm{Higgs}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=-\Bigg[\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{k}}\partial_{ij}^{2}\xi_{\bm{k}}\;\chi_{13}(\bm{k},\omega)+2\,\Delta_{0}\sum_{\bm{k}}g_{ij}(\bm{k})\;\chi_{11}(\bm{k},\omega)\Bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\;\mathcal{G}_{H}^{0}(\omega)\;\Bigg[\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}\partial_{kl}^{2}\xi_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}\;\chi_{13}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\omega)+2\,\Delta_{0}\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}g_{kl}(\bm{k}^{\prime})\;\chi_{11}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\omega)\Bigg].

(45)

At zero temperature one obtains closed forms for the bubbles (retarded continuation). For example,

\displaystyle\chi_{11}(\bm{k},\omega)

\displaystyle=-\frac{4\xi_{\bm{k}}^{2}}{E_{\bm{k}}\,(4E_{\bm{k}}^{2}-\omega^{2})},

(46)

\displaystyle\chi_{13}(\bm{k},\omega)

\displaystyle=-\frac{4\xi_{\bm{k}}\Delta_{0}}{E_{\bm{k}}\,(4E_{\bm{k}}^{2}-\omega^{2})},

(47)

\displaystyle\chi_{33}(\bm{k},\omega)

\displaystyle=-\frac{4\Delta_{0}^{2}}{E_{\bm{k}}\,(4E_{\bm{k}}^{2}-\omega^{2})},

(48)

with
E_{\bm{k}}=\sqrt{\xi_{\bm{k}}^{2}+\Delta_{0}^{2}}
.
These forms follow from evaluating the Matsubara sums and performing analytic continuation; they satisfy
\chi_{11}+\chi_{33}=-4/(4E^{2}-\omega^{2})
and
\chi_{13}
is the mixed numerator
\propto\xi\Delta
.

The Higgs propagator reads

\mathcal{G}_{H}^{0}(\omega)\;=\;\Big[\,2\rho_{0}\arcsin\!\big(\tfrac{\omega}{2\Delta_{0}}\big)\,\Big]^{-1}\,\Big[\Big(\tfrac{2\Delta_{0}}{\omega}\Big)^{2}-1\Big]^{-1/2},

(49)

where
\rho_{0}
denotes the density-of-states factor entering the amplitude fluctuation kernel.

The electromagnetic current is obtained by functional differentiation

j_{m}(t)=-\frac{\delta S}{\delta A_{m}(t)}.

Working in the frequency domain and using the quartic effective action
S[A]
above, for a monochromatic drive

A_{i}(t)=a_{i}e^{-i\Omega t}+a_{i}^{*}e^{i\Omega t},

the
3\Omega
component of the current is

\displaystyle j_{m}(3\Omega)

\displaystyle=-4\sum_{jkl}K_{mjkl}(2\Omega)\;a_{j}\,a_{k}\,a_{l}

\displaystyle=-4\sum_{jkl}\big[K^{\mathrm{qp}}_{mjkl}(2\Omega)+K^{\mathrm{Higgs}}_{mjkl}(2\Omega)\big]\;a_{j}a_{k}a_{l}.

(50)

Appendix B
Derivation of the Higgs correlation Length

To derive the correlation length, we start from the action with a static, spatially varying order parameter
\Delta(q)
and no external field (
\bm{A}=0
). The Hamiltonian is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}_{0}+\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{int}},

(51)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{k}\sigma\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}\alpha\sigma}\mathcal{H}_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}^{\sigma}(\bm{k})c_{\bm{k}\alpha^{\prime}\sigma},

(52)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{int}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\bm{k}\bm{k}^{\prime}\alpha\beta}U_{\alpha\beta}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k+q}\alpha\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{-\bm{k}\beta\downarrow}c_{-\bm{k}^{\prime}\beta\downarrow}c_{\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}\alpha\uparrow}.

(53)

The partition function
\mathcal{Z}
and the action are written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}(c^{\dagger},c)e^{-S[c^{\dagger},c]},\quad\quad S[c^{\dagger},c]=\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau(\sum_{\bm{k}\sigma\alpha}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}\alpha\sigma}\partial_{\tau}c_{\bm{k}\alpha\sigma}+\mathcal{H}).

(54)

We decouple the interaction term using a Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation in the pairing channel:

\displaystyle\exp\left(\sum_{\bm{k}\bm{k}^{\prime}\alpha\beta}U_{\alpha\beta}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k+q}\alpha\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{-\bm{k}\beta\downarrow}c_{-\bm{k}^{\prime}\beta\downarrow}c_{\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}\alpha\uparrow}\right)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\int\mathcal{D}(\Delta^{*},\Delta)\exp\left(-\sum_{\alpha,\beta,\bm{q}}\frac{\left|\Delta_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{q})\right|^{2}}{U_{\alpha\beta}}+\sum_{\bm{k},\bm{q},\alpha,\beta}(\Delta_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{q})c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}\alpha\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{-\bm{k}\beta\downarrow}+h.c.)\right).

(55)

Then the action becomes

\displaystyle S[c^{\dagger},c,\Delta^{*},\Delta]=

\displaystyle\int^{\beta}_{0}d\tau\left\{\sum_{\bm{k},\sigma,\alpha,\beta}c^{\dagger}_{\alpha\sigma}(\bm{k})[(\partial_{\tau}-\mu)\delta_{\alpha\beta}+\mathcal{H}^{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta}]c_{\beta}(\bm{k})\right\}

\displaystyle+\int^{\beta}_{0}d\tau\left\{-\sum_{\bm{k},\bm{q},\alpha,\beta}[\Delta_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{q})c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}\alpha\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{-\bm{k}\beta\downarrow}+h.c.]+\sum_{\bm{q},\alpha,\beta}\frac{|\Delta_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{q})|^{2}}{U_{\alpha\beta}}\right\}

(56)

To analyze the physics in terms of electronic bands, we now perform a change of basis from the orbital basis (
c_{\alpha\sigma}
) to the band basis (
c_{l\sigma}
).
We then make key approximations: (i) we project the theory onto a single dominant band (labeled
m
, which we drop hereafter) near the Fermi level, (ii) we assume a uniform, intra-orbital pairing form,
\Delta_{\alpha\beta}(q)=\Delta(q)\delta_{\alpha\beta}
, and (iii) time-reversal symmetry. Applying these approximations, the action simplifies.

\displaystyle S[c^{\dagger},c,\Delta^{*},\Delta]=

\displaystyle\beta N_{b}\sum_{q}\frac{|\Delta(q)|^{2}}{U}+\beta\sum_{k,\sigma}[-i\omega_{n}+\epsilon_{\sigma}(\bm{k})-\mu]c^{\dagger}_{\sigma}(k)c_{\sigma}(k)

\displaystyle-\beta\sum_{k,q}\left[\Delta(q)f(\bm{k},\bm{q})c^{\dagger}_{\uparrow}(k)c^{\dagger}_{\downarrow}(-k+q)+h.c.\right],

(57)

where

f(\bm{k},\bm{q})=\sum_{\alpha}u_{\alpha}^{*}(\bm{k})u_{\alpha}(\bm{k}-\bm{q})=\braket{m,\bm{k}|m,\bm{k}-\bm{q}}
, which satisfies
f(\bm{k},\bm{0})=1
,
f(\bm{k},\bm{q})\approx 1-g_{ij}(\bm{k})q_{i}q_{j}/2+O(q^{4})
, and
N_{b}
is the number of orbitals.

By introducing the Nambu spinor
\psi({k})=\big(c_{\uparrow}({k})~~c_{\downarrow}^{\dagger}({-k})\big)^{T}
, the action can be written in a BdG form as

\displaystyle S[c^{\dagger},c,\Delta^{*},\Delta]=

\displaystyle\beta N_{b}\sum_{q}\frac{|\Delta(q)|^{2}}{U}+\sum_{k,{q}}\psi^{\dagger}(k)[-\beta\mathcal{G}^{-1}({k},{k-q})]\psi(k-q).

(58)

The inverse Nambu propagator
\mathcal{G}^{-1}
is identified as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{-1}(k,k-q)=\begin{pmatrix}(i\omega_{n}-\xi_{\bm{k}})\delta_{q,0}&\Delta(q)f(\bm{k},\bm{q})\\
\Delta^{*}(-q)f^{*}(\bm{k}-\bm{q},-\bm{q})&(i\omega_{n}+\xi_{\bm{k}})\delta_{q,0}\end{pmatrix}

(59)

where
\xi_{\bm{k}}=\epsilon_{\bm{k}}-\mu
.

We expand the order parameter field around the mean-field saddle point as

\displaystyle\Delta(q)=\Delta_{0}\delta_{q,0}+\rho(q)+i\Delta_{0}\theta(q),

(60)

where
\rho
is the amplitude fluctuation and
\theta
the phase fluctuation.

Then the propagator in Eq. (
59
) becomes
\mathcal{G}^{-1}=\mathcal{G}_{0}^{-1}+\Sigma
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{0}^{-1}(k)=\begin{pmatrix}i\omega_{n}-\xi_{\bm{k}}&\Delta_{0}\\
\Delta_{0}&i\omega_{n}+\xi_{\bm{k}}\end{pmatrix},

(61)

\displaystyle\Sigma(k,q)=A(q)f(\bm{k},\bm{q})\tau_{+}+A^{*}(-q)f^{*}(\bm{k}-\bm{q},-\bm{q})\tau_{-},

(62)

where
\tau_{\pm}=(\tau_{1}\pm i\tau_{2})/2
and
A(q)
=
\rho(q)+i\Delta_{0}\theta(q)
.
Note that
\theta(q)=\theta^{*}(-q)
,
\rho(q)=\rho^{*}(-q)
, since
\theta(x)
and
\rho(x)
are both real fields, and
f(\bm{k-q},-\bm{q})=f(\bm{k},\bm{q})=1-g_{ij}(\bm{k})q_{i}q_{j}/2
up to second order in
\bm{q}
.

Integrating out the fermionic fields, we write down the effecitve theory for the order parameters as

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{eff}}[\Delta^{*},\Delta]=\beta N_{b}\sum_{q}\frac{|\Delta(q)|^{2}}{U}+\beta\sum_{k}\xi_{\bm{k}}-\mathrm{Tr}~\mathrm{ln}[\beta\mathcal{G}^{-1}]

(63)

Since
\text{ln}[\beta\mathcal{G}^{-1}]=\text{ln}[\beta\mathcal{G}_{0}]+\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}\frac{(-1)^{2n-1}}{n}(\mathcal{G}_{0}\Sigma)^{2n}
, the action of the order quadratic
\rho
, i.e.,
O(\rho^{2})
, can be written as

\displaystyle S^{(2)}=\beta N_{b}\sum_{q}\frac{|\Delta(q)|^{2}}{U}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}[\mathcal{G}_{0}\Sigma\mathcal{G}_{0}\Sigma].

(64)

We consider static
q_{0}=0
. Furthermore, for simplicity, we introduce the dimensionless Higgs field
h(q)\equiv\rho(q)/\Delta_{0}
. A straightforward calculation leads to

\displaystyle S^{(2)}=\sum_{\bm{q}}\Big(h(-\bm{q})~~\theta(-\bm{q})\Big)\mathbf{\Gamma}(\bm{q})\begin{pmatrix}h(\bm{q})\\
\theta(\bm{q})\end{pmatrix},

(65)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Gamma}(\bm{q})\simeq\begin{pmatrix}r_{\rho}+A_{ij}q_{i}q_{j}&0\\
0&r_{\theta}+B_{ij}q_{i}q_{j}\end{pmatrix}+O(q^{4}),

(66)

with

\displaystyle r_{\rho}=\frac{N_{b}\Delta_{0}^{2}}{U}-\frac{\Delta_{0}^{2}}{2}\sum_{\bm{k}}\frac{\xi_{\bm{k}}^{2}}{E_{\bm{k}}^{3}}=\frac{\Delta_{0}^{4}}{2}\sum_{\bm{k}}\frac{1}{E_{\bm{k}}^{3}},

(67)

\displaystyle r_{\theta}=\frac{N_{b}\Delta_{0}^{2}}{U}-\frac{\Delta_{0}^{2}}{2}\sum_{\bm{k}}\frac{1}{E_{\bm{k}}}=0,

(68)

\displaystyle A_{ij}=\sum_{\bm{k}}\left[v_{i}(\bm{k})v_{j}(\bm{k})\frac{\Delta_{0}^{2}}{8E_{\bm{k}}^{7}}\left(-\Delta_{0}^{4}+8\Delta_{0}^{2}\xi_{\bm{k}}^{2}-\xi_{\bm{k}}^{4}\right)+\xi_{ij}(\bm{k})\frac{\xi_{\bm{k}}\Delta_{0}^{2}}{8E_{\bm{k}}^{5}}\left(-2\Delta_{0}^{2}+\xi_{\bm{k}}^{2}\right)+g_{ij}(\bm{k})\frac{\Delta_{0}^{2}}{2}\frac{\xi_{\bm{k}}^{2}}{E_{\bm{k}}^{3}}\right],

(69)

\displaystyle B_{ij}=\sum_{\bm{k}}\left[v_{i}(\bm{k})v_{j}(\bm{k})\frac{\Delta_{0}^{2}}{8E_{\bm{k}}^{5}}\left(2\Delta_{0}^{2}-\xi_{\bm{k}}^{2}\right)+\xi_{ij}(\bm{k})\frac{\xi_{\bm{k}}\Delta_{0}^{2}}{8E_{\bm{k}}^{3}}+g_{ij}(\bm{k})\frac{\Delta_{0}^{2}}{2}\frac{1}{E_{\bm{k}}}\right],

(70)

where
v_{i}(\bm{k})=\partial_{i}\xi_{\bm{k}}
and
\xi_{ij}(\bm{k})=\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\xi_{\bm{k}}
.
For these calculations, we use

\displaystyle T_{11}\equiv\mathrm{Tr}\!\big[\mathcal{G}_{0}(k+q)\tau_{1}\mathcal{G}_{0}(k)\tau_{1}\big]=\frac{2\big(\Delta_{0}^{2}-\omega^{2}-\xi_{k}\xi_{k+q}\big)}{(\omega^{2}+E_{k+q}^{2})(\omega^{2}+E_{k}^{2})},

(71)

\displaystyle T_{22}\equiv\mathrm{Tr}\!\big[\mathcal{G}_{0}(k+q)\tau_{2}\mathcal{G}_{0}(k)\tau_{2}\big]=\frac{2\big(-\Delta_{0}^{2}-\omega^{2}-\xi_{k}\xi_{k+q}\big)}{(\omega^{2}+E_{k+q}^{2})(\omega^{2}+E_{k}^{2})},

(72)

\displaystyle T_{12}\equiv\mathrm{Tr}\!\big[\mathcal{G}_{0}(k+q)\tau_{1}\mathcal{G}_{0}(k)\tau_{2}\big]=\frac{2\omega\big(\xi_{k+q}-\xi_{k}\big)}{(\omega^{2}+E_{k+q}^{2})(\omega^{2}+E_{k}^{2})},

(73)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{i\omega_{n}}\frac{1}{\omega_{n}^{2}+E^{2}}=\frac{1}{2E},\qquad\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{i\omega_{n}}\frac{1}{(\omega_{n}^{2}+E^{2})(\omega_{n}^{2}+E^{\prime 2})}=\frac{1}{2EE^{\prime}(E+E^{\prime})},

(74)

\displaystyle\Pi_{11}(k,k+q)\equiv\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{i\omega_{n}}T_{11}=\frac{\Delta_{0}^{2}-E_{k}E_{k+q}-\xi_{k}\xi_{k+q}}{E_{k}E_{k+q}\big(E_{k}+E_{k+q}\big)},

(75)

\displaystyle\Pi_{22}(k,k+q)\equiv\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{i\omega_{n}}T_{22}=\frac{-\Delta_{0}^{2}-E_{k}E_{k+q}-\xi_{k}\xi_{k+q}}{E_{k}E_{k+q}\big(E_{k}+E_{k+q}\big)}.

(76)

Thus, the correlation length is

\displaystyle\braket{h(\bm{q})h(-\bm{q})}\propto\frac{1}{r_{\rho}+A_{ij}q_{i}q_{j}},

(77)

\displaystyle\xi_{H,ij}^{2}=\frac{A_{ij}}{r_{\rho}}=\xi^{2}_{\text{band},ij}+\xi^{2}_{\text{geom},ij},

(78)

\displaystyle\xi^{2}_{\text{band},ij}=\frac{1}{r_{\rho}}\sum_{\bm{k}}\left[v_{i}(\bm{k})v_{j}(\bm{k})\frac{\Delta_{0}^{2}}{8E_{\bm{k}}^{7}}\left(-\Delta_{0}^{4}+8\Delta_{0}^{2}\xi_{\bm{k}}^{2}-\xi_{\bm{k}}^{4}\right)+\xi_{ij}(\bm{k})\frac{\xi_{\bm{k}}\Delta_{0}^{2}}{8E_{\bm{k}}^{5}}\left(-2\Delta_{0}^{2}+\xi_{\bm{k}}^{2}\right)\right],

(79)

\displaystyle\xi^{2}_{\text{geom},ij}=\frac{1}{r_{\rho}}\sum_{\bm{k}}g_{ij}(\bm{k})\frac{\Delta_{0}^{2}}{2}\frac{\xi_{\bm{k}}^{2}}{E_{\bm{k}}^{3}}

Appendix C
Quadratic Band Touching Model

Figure 3:

Real-space lattice models for (a)
d_{\mathrm{max}}=0
and
(b)
d_{\mathrm{max}}=1
.

The non-interacting Hamiltonian for the tunable quadratic band touching model used in the main text is given by

\mathcal{H}^{\uparrow}(\bm{k})=\mathcal{H}^{\downarrow*}(-\bm{k})=h_{0}(\bm{k})\tau_{0}+\bm{h}(\bm{k})\cdot\bm{\tau},

(81)

where the components are defined as:

\displaystyle h_{0}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=-2(t-t_{b})-(t_{b}+t)(\cos k_{x}+\cos k_{y}),

\displaystyle h_{x}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=4d_{\mathrm{max}}(t-t_{b})\sin(k_{x}/2)\sin(k_{y}/2),

\displaystyle h_{y}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=-2d_{\mathrm{max}}\sqrt{1-d_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}}(t-t_{b})(\cos k_{y}-1),

\displaystyle h_{z}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=(t-t_{b})\left[2(1-d_{\mathrm{max}}^{2})-\cos k_{x}+(2d_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}-1)\cos k_{y}\right].

This specific construction ensures time-reversal symmetry and that the band dispersions,
E_{\pm}(\bm{k})
, are independent of the parameter
d_{\mathrm{max}}
, which solely controls the quantum geometry of the Bloch states. The quantum metric for the upper band is given by

g_{xx}(\bm{k})=d_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}\cos^{2}(k_{x}/2)\sin^{2}(k_{y}/2)/\mathcal{D}(\bm{k})^{2}
,
g_{xy}(\bm{k})=-{d_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}\sin k_{x}\sin k_{y}}/({4\mathcal{D}(\bm{k})^{2}})
,
g_{yy}(\bm{k})={d_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}\cos^{2}(k_{y}/2)\sin^{2}(k_{x}/2)}/{\mathcal{D}(\bm{k})^{2}}
,
where
\mathcal{D}(\bm{k})=-2+\cos k_{x}+\cos k_{y}
. Note that these expressions defined for
\bm{k}\neq\bm{0}
.

Real-Space Lattice Representations

While the
k
-space Hamiltonian is well-defined for any
d_{\mathrm{max}}
, it is instructive to examine the real-space tight-binding models that correspond to the two limiting cases.

Geometrically trivial limit (
d_{\mathrm{max}}=0
)

In this case, the model describes two uncoupled orbitals (A and B) on a square lattice, each with simple nearest-neighbor hopping. The real-space Hamiltonian is:

H=\sum_{\bm{R}}\left[\epsilon_{A}a^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}a_{\bm{R}}+\epsilon_{B}b^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}b_{\bm{R}}\right]+\sum_{\bm{R},\nu=x,y}\left[-ta^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}a_{\bm{R}+\hat{\nu}}-t_{b}b^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}b_{\bm{R}+\hat{\nu}}+\text{h.c.}\right],

(82)

where
\epsilon_{A}=0
and
\epsilon_{B}=4(t_{b}-t)
are the on-site energies. This lattice is depicted in Fig.
3
(a).

Maximally nontrivial limit (
d_{\mathrm{max}}=1
)

This limit maps onto a more complex tight-binding model with anisotropic hopping and intricate, spatially dependent inter-orbital couplings. The Hamiltonian is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\sum_{\bm{R}}

\displaystyle\left[\epsilon_{A}a^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}a_{\bm{R}}+\epsilon_{B}b^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}b_{\bm{R}}\right]+\sum_{\bm{R},\nu=x,y}\left[t_{A,\nu}a^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}a_{\bm{R}+\hat{\nu}}+t_{B,\nu}b^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}b_{\bm{R}+\hat{\nu}}+\text{h.c.}\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{\bm{R}}\left[A(a_{\bm{R}}^{\dagger}b_{\bm{R}}-a^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}b_{\bm{R}-\hat{x}}-a^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}b_{\bm{R}-\hat{y}}+a^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}b_{\bm{R}-\hat{x}-\hat{y}})+\text{h.c.}\right],

(83)

where the parameters are
\epsilon_{A}=\epsilon_{B}=2(t_{b}-t)
,
t_{A,x}=t_{B,y}=-t
,
t_{A,y}=t_{B,x}=-t_{b}
, and
A=t_{b}-t
. This structure is shown in Fig.
3
(b).

Intermediate regime (
0<d_{\mathrm{max}}<1
)

For intermediate values of
d_{\mathrm{max}}
, constructing a corresponding local tight-binding model on a simple square lattice is challenging. However, the
k
-space model, which is mathematically well-defined across the entire range of
d_{\mathrm{max}}
, provides the essential theoretical framework for demonstrating how the system’s properties are smoothly governed by the underlying quantum geometry.

Appendix D
Superfluid stiffness

We calculate the superfluid stiffness from the effective action in Eq. (
32
).
To obtain the superfluid stiffness, we expand the action to
O(A^{2})
. Taking
\rho=0
for the static stiffness, the
A^{2}
self-energy becomes

\displaystyle\Sigma_{A^{2}}(\bm{k})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}\partial_{ij}^{2}\xi_{\bm{k}}\,A_{i}A_{j}\,\tau_{3}+2\Delta_{0}\sum_{ij}g_{ij}(\bm{k})\,A_{i}A_{j}\,\tau_{1}.

(84)

Since
\Sigma_{A^{2}}
is already
O(A^{2})
, the quadratic electromagnetic action is

\displaystyle S_{\rm eff}^{(A^{2})}=\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[\mathcal{G}_{0}\,\Sigma_{A^{2}}\right]+O(A^{4}).

(85)

The required Nambu traces are

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left[\mathcal{G}_{0}\tau_{3}\right]=\frac{2\xi_{\bm{k}}}{(i\omega_{n})^{2}-E_{\bm{k}}^{2}},\qquad\mathrm{Tr}\left[\mathcal{G}_{0}\tau_{1}\right]=-\frac{2\Delta_{0}}{(i\omega_{n})^{2}-E_{\bm{k}}^{2}}.

(86)

Using the Matsubara identity

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{i\omega_{n}}\frac{1}{(i\omega_{n})^{2}-E^{2}}=-\frac{1}{2E}\tanh\!\left(\frac{\beta E}{2}\right),

(87)

so that at
T=0
,

\displaystyle S_{\rm eff}^{(A^{2})}=\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{ij}A_{i}\left(\sum_{\bm{k}}-\frac{\xi_{\bm{k}}\,\partial_{ij}^{2}\xi_{\bm{k}}}{E_{\bm{k}}}+\frac{4\Delta_{0}^{2}g_{ij}(\bm{k})}{E_{\bm{k}}}\right)A_{j}.

(88)

Thus, the superfluid stiffness
D_{ij}
reads

\displaystyle D_{ij}=\sum_{\bm{k}}\left[-\frac{\xi_{\bm{k}}\,\partial_{ij}^{2}\xi_{\bm{k}}}{E_{\bm{k}}}+\frac{4\Delta_{0}^{2}g_{ij}(\bm{k})}{E_{\bm{k}}}\right].

(89)

The first and second terms corresponds to
D^{\mathrm{band}}_{ij}
and
D^{\mathrm{geom}}_{ij}
, respectively.
This result is identical to that found in
Liang et al. 2017
;
Peotta and Törmä 2015
.

4
shows the
d_{\mathrm{max}}
-dependence of the superfluid stiffness for the same parameters as in Fig. 1(b,c) of the main text. As
d_{\mathrm{max}}
increases, the superfluid stiffness is enhanced.
