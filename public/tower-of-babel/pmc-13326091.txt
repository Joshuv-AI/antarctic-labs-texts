Trauma symptoms, memory accuracy, and eyewitness suggestibility: a mediation analysis in a non-clinical sample
Authors: Musa Bozkurt, Neylan Ziyalar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEyewitness testimony is a key component of legal decision-making, yet it is highly vulnerable to cognitive and psychological influences. While trauma has been associated with alterations in memory processes, its role in eyewitness suggestibility remains unclear. In particular, it is not well understood whether trauma influences suggestibility directly or through related mechanisms such as dissociation or memory accuracy. The present study aimed to examine the relationship between trauma level and eyewitness memory performance, with a specific focus on memory accuracy and susceptibility to suggestive questioning, as well as the roles of dissociation and emotional arousal.MethodsParticipants were 104 undergraduate students categorized into low- and high-trauma groups based on their scores on the Posttraumatic Stress Diagnostic Scale (PDS). After viewing a traffic accident video, participants completed a 20-item post-event questionnaire consisting of yes/no questions designed to assess memory accuracy and susceptibility to suggestive questioning. The questionnaire included both accurate and misleading items. Correlational and mediation analyses were conducted as primary analyses using continuous trauma severity scores. Group-based descriptive comparisons were also conducted for exploratory purposes.ResultsIndividuals with higher trauma levels demonstrated significantly lower memory accuracy compared to those with lower trauma levels. Trauma was positively associated with dissociation and modestly associated with suggestibility, while memory accuracy showed a strong negative relationship with suggestibility. However, trauma group differences were not significant for suggestibility or response bias. Mediation analysis revealed that memory accuracy fully mediated the relationship between trauma and suggestibility, consistent with a pattern of full indirect association between trauma severity and suggestibility via memory accuracy. In contrast, dissociation did not emerge as a significant mediator. Additionally, emotional arousal was not significantly related to trauma, memory accuracy, or suggestibility.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that trauma-related vulnerability to suggestive questioning appears more strongly associated with cognitive factors, particularly memory accuracy, than with dissociative processes. The results highlight the importance of memory accuracy as a cognitive mechanism relevant to eyewitness reliability and underscore the value of considering individual differences in cognitive functioning when evaluating testimony from trauma-exposed individuals.Keywords: Trauma, Dissociation, Eyewitness memory, Memory accuracy, Suggestibility

Background

Eyewitness testimony refers to statements provided by individuals who have directly observed a crime or legally relevant event and is frequently relied upon as evidence in criminal investigations and court proceedings. In some cases, only eyewitness testimony is accepted as evidence in the process of clarifying forensic events [1]. It is also stated that even if other evidence contradicts witness statements, courts tend to make decisions in line with witness statements [2]. Despite its importance in legal decision-making, psychological research has consistently demonstrated that memory for events is reconstructive rather than a literal record of past experiences [3, 4]. Consequently, eyewitness accounts may be influenced by cognitive, social, and contextual factors during encoding and retrieval processes. Current research in cognitive and forensic psychology emphasizes that the reliability of eyewitness memory depends not only on the characteristics of the event itself but also on the psychological processes involved in remembering and reporting the event [5, 6].

One factor that has received considerable attention in forensic psychology is suggestibility, defined as the extent to which individuals accept suggestions or leading information when recalling an event [7]. Suggestibility is particularly relevant in investigative contexts because the manner in which questions are posed during interviews can shape witnesses’ recollections and influence the accuracy of their reports [8]. Research on suggestibility suggests that individual differences in cognitive functioning, emotional state, and situational pressures can affect how people respond to suggestive questioning [9]. Empirical research also indicates that suggestive questioning and social influences during recall may alter witnesses’ reports and increase the likelihood of memory inaccuracies, particularly when individuals experience uncertainty about their memories [10, 11]. Understanding the factors that contribute to susceptibility to suggestion is therefore essential for evaluating the reliability of eyewitness testimony.

It is important to distinguish between different forms of suggestibility described in the literature. One key distinction is between interrogative suggestibility, which involves social influence, compliance, and pressure during questioning [9, 12], and suggestibility examined within the misinformation paradigm, which is more closely related to memory processes. In the misinformation paradigm, exposure to misleading post-event information can influence how events are remembered and reported, sometimes leading to memory distortions or false memories [13, 14]. In the present study, suggestibility is examined within this memory-based framework using misleading yes/no questions presented after the event. Some of these questions include incorrect details, and suggestibility is defined as the tendency to accept these misleading suggestions. This approach captures susceptibility to suggested information at the time of retrieval, rather than the long-term integration of misinformation into memory. Therefore, suggestibility in this study is best understood as the acceptance of misleading information during questioning, reflecting cognitive processes related to memory evaluation, while also potentially being influenced by general response tendencies.

Among the psychological factors that may influence eyewitness memory, trauma-related symptoms have received increasing attention in recent years. Exposure to traumatic experiences can affect cognitive and emotional processes involved in memory encoding and retrieval.

Contemporary trauma theories suggest that traumatic stress may alter attentional processes, emotional regulation, and cognitive control, which in turn may influence how individuals process and recall emotionally significant events [15, 16]. Empirical studies indicate that individuals with elevated trauma symptoms show impairments in episodic memory functioning, including reduced recall specificity and increased fragmentation [17, 18]. Although this evidence derives primarily from studies of autobiographical memory for personal traumatic events, the underlying mechanisms — disruptions to attentional control, working memory, and strategic encoding processes — are not specific to personally experienced events. A witness with elevated trauma symptoms may therefore encode fewer correct details of an observed event not because the event was personally traumatic, but because pre-existing trauma-related cognitive vulnerabilities impair the quality of encoding under conditions requiring sustained attentional and mnemonic control. Such findings suggest that trauma-related psychological processes may influence eyewitness memory performance and potentially affect susceptibility to suggestion.

Another psychological process frequently associated with traumatic experiences is dissociation. Dissociation refers to disruptions in the normally integrated functions of consciousness, memory, identity, or perception, and is commonly discussed as a response to overwhelming stress [19, 20]. Research examining cognitive functioning in individuals with dissociative symptoms suggests that dissociation may be associated with alterations in attention, executive functioning, and memory processes [21, 22]. In the context of eyewitness memory, dissociative tendencies may influence how individuals encode and retrieve information about emotionally significant events. Some empirical studies have suggested that dissociative experiences may also be related to increased susceptibility to suggestion or difficulties resisting external influences during recall [23, 24]. These findings indicate that dissociation may represent an important factor in understanding individual differences in eyewitness suggestibility. A critical theoretical distinction must be noted, however, between trait dissociation — a relatively stable individual tendency to experience dissociative states, measured by instruments such as the Dissociative Experiences Scale — and state dissociation occurring at the time of the witnessed event, such as peritraumatic depersonalization or attentional disengagement during encoding. Theoretically, it is the latter that is most proximal to memory and suggestibility outcomes. The source monitoring framework (SMF; [25]) offers a useful account of the mechanism: eyewitness errors arise when individuals fail to correctly attribute the origin of information, mistaking post-event suggestions for genuine memories.

Dissociative disruptions during encoding may weaken the perceptual and contextual detail that normally marks memories as internally generated, thereby increasing vulnerability to external misinformation. Because the present study measures trait rather than state dissociation, null findings for dissociation should be interpreted with this measurement constraint in mind.

In addition to trauma-related symptoms and dissociation, emotional arousal experienced during an event may also influence eyewitness memory. Research in cognitive and affective neuroscience suggests that emotional arousal can substantially affect memory processes by directing attentional resources toward emotionally significant aspects of an event. While emotional arousal may enhance memory for central features of an event, it may simultaneously impair memory for peripheral details [26, 27]. According to the Yerkes–Dodson law, moderate levels of arousal may enhance performance, whereas very low or very high levels of arousal may impair it [28]. Recent research further indicates that emotionally intense events may increase the likelihood of memory distortions when individuals attempt to recall complex episodes involving multiple sources of information [29, 30]. In eyewitness contexts, emotionally arousing events such as accidents or violent incidents may therefore influence both the accuracy of memory and the degree to which individuals are susceptible to suggestion during questioning.

To better clarify how the main constructs in this study are related, it is useful to distinguish between antecedent factors, underlying mechanisms, and outcomes. Rather than having a direct effect on eyewitness suggestibility, trauma-related symptoms are expected to operate indirectly through their influence on memory processes. In this framework, trauma severity is conceptualized as an antecedent variable, memory accuracy as a proximal cognitive mechanism, and suggestibility as the outcome variable. Previous research shows that trauma is associated with disruptions in attention, as well as difficulties in encoding and retrieving information [31]. These difficulties may lead to weaker or less detailed memory representations.

According to the source monitoring framework [25], when memory traces are weak or unclear, it becomes harder to determine where the information came from. In such cases, individuals may rely more on external cues, including misleading post-event information, which can increase suggestibility. Previous research has consistently shown that susceptibility to misleading information is closely related to the strength and quality of memory representations. When memory traces are strong and detailed, individuals are more likely to resist misleading information, whereas weaker memory representations increase reliance on external cues [14, 32].

From this perspective, memory accuracy is considered a proximal mechanism linking trauma symptom severity to suggestibility, rather than an outcome on its own. Dissociation is examined as a possible alternative pathway, reflecting disruptions in awareness or attention. Emotional arousal is treated as a secondary factor that may affect encoding, but it is not central to the main model tested in this study.

Previous research has provided important insights into eyewitness memory; however, trauma symptom levels are rarely assessed in experimental studies using non-clinical samples. As a result, the role of individual differences in trauma has often been overlooked. A meta-analytic review by Deffenbacher et al. [33] demonstrated that high stress is associated with reduced eyewitness accuracy, consistent with the Yerkes–Dodson prediction that very high arousal impairs performance; yet the specific cognitive mechanisms and individual-difference variables that account for this relationship remain underspecified. Recent scholarship has further highlighted the importance of distinguishing between types of traumatic memory processes when theorizing about downstream cognitive outcomes [34]. Examining trauma symptom severity directly, rather than treating trauma as a binary variable, may therefore offer a more precise understanding of how prior psychological experiences shape eyewitness memory accuracy and suggestibility.

Based on this framework, it is hypothesized that individuals with higher levels of trauma symptom severity will show lower eyewitness memory accuracy. It is also expected that lower memory accuracy will be associated with greater susceptibility to misleading information.

Accordingly, trauma symptom severity is expected to be indirectly related to suggestibility through its association with memory accuracy. In addition, it is hypothesized that higher trauma symptom severity will be associated with greater dissociative experiences. Dissociation is also expected to be related to both suggestibility and memory accuracy, and will therefore be examined as a possible alternative pathway within the model. Given previous findings suggesting that emotional arousal can influence memory performance in complex ways, it is expected that arousal may also be associated with both memory accuracy and suggestibility. However, due to the mixed nature of the literature, no specific directional prediction is made.

Methods

Research design

The present study employed a cross-sectional correlational design to examine whether individuals with higher levels of trauma symptom severity differ from individuals with lower symptom levels in terms of eyewitness memory accuracy and susceptibility to suggestive questioning. Trauma severity was measured using the Posttraumatic Stress Diagnostic Scale (PDS) as a continuous variable; participants were also divided into low- and high-trauma groups based on established cutoff scores for descriptive and exploratory purposes, but primary inferential analyses used continuous PDS scores. The study utilized an eyewitness memory paradigm in which participants viewed a video depicting a traffic accident and subsequently answered a series of questions containing both accurate and misleading information. In addition, the study investigated the potential role of dissociative experiences and emotional arousal in explaining differences in eyewitness memory performance.

Participants

Participants were 104 undergraduate students recruited from the Departments of Psychology and Social Work at Bingol University. The sample consisted of 62 females and 42 males aged between 18 and 35 years (M = 22.31, SD = 2.77).

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 for a multiple regression model including up to five predictors, assuming a medium effect size (f² = 0.15), α = 0.05, and power = 0.80. The analysis indicated a required sample size of approximately 92 participants. The final sample of 104 participants exceeded this threshold. However, the study may have been underpowered to detect smaller effects.

Participants were divided into two groups based on their PDS scores. Individuals scoring between 0 and 10 were classified as the low-trauma group, whereas participants scoring 11 or higher were classified as the high-trauma group. Each group consisted of 52 participants.

Participants reporting a previous psychiatric diagnosis, psychiatric treatment, a history of psychotherapy, self-harm behavior, or substance abuse were excluded from the study. In addition, individuals who reported having previously experienced a traffic accident were excluded due to the potentially distressing nature of the video stimulus.

All participants provided informed consent prior to participation.

Measures

Post-Traumatic Stress Diagnostic Scale (PDS)

The PDS was developed by Foa et al. [35] and adapted into Turkish by Işıklı [36]. In our study, we used the Posttraumatic Stress Symptoms Subscale, which constitutes the third section of the four-part PDS. This subscale is a Likert-type measure scored between 0 and 3, with a total range of 0–51. Higher scores indicate greater negative impact of the event and the presence of posttraumatic stress symptoms. A symptom score of 10 or below is classified as mild; 11–20 as moderate; 21–35 as moderate to severe; and scores above 35 as severe. In the Turkish adaptation of the Posttraumatic Stress Symptoms Subscale, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for internal consistency across all items was found to be 0.92, and the item–total correlation coefficients ranged from 0.39 to 0.82.

Dissociative Experiences Scale (DES)

The DES, developed by Bernstein & Putnam [37], was adapted into Turkish by Şar [38]. Comprising 28 items, the scale is intended not for diagnostic purposes but for screening or rating dissociative experiences. A cutoff score of 30 has been suggested for identifying dissociative disorders. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient demonstrating the internal consistency of the scale was reported as 0.97, and the Guttman split-half coefficient as 0.95.

The video

We used a 6-minute portion of a 30-minute public service announcement on the consequences of using mobile phones while driving which was broadcast in the UK. In this video, there are images of a traffic accident resulting in death and injury. The publicly available video was previously used in various studies [39, 40].

Post-Event Information (PEI) checklist

Participants were asked to respond to 20 yes/no questions related to the content of the video they had watched. Ten of the questions corresponded to details that were actually present in the video, whereas the remaining ten questions referred to information that was not present in the video. The items were presented in a mixed order in order to prevent participants from identifying a pattern in the correct responses. The questions were adapted from the study conducted by Chan et al. [40].

Procedure

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Social Sciences Ethics Committee of Bingöl University (Approval No: 92342550/730.08.03/11861). Prior to participation, all participants provided informed consent and were informed that their participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences. Participants were also informed that they could request additional information about the study after the experiment and that psychological support services provided by the university counseling center would be available if needed.

Participants first completed a demographic information form, the Posttraumatic Stress Diagnostic Scale (PDS), and the Dissociative Experiences Scale (DES). Based on their PDS scores, participants were categorized into the low-trauma and high-trauma groups for descriptive purposes. After this categorization, participants watched a publicly available video depicting a traffic accident. Following the video, participants completed a Post-Event Information checklist consisting of yes/no questions assessing eyewitness memory accuracy and susceptibility to suggestive questioning. Finally, participants rated the extent to which the video affected them emotionally on a 9-point scale ranging from 1 (not affected at all) to 9 (extremely affected).

Based on participants’ responses to the checklist, three indices were derived in order to capture different aspects of eyewitness memory performance. Memory accuracy reflected the total number of correct responses across all items. Suggestibility was operationalized as the number of incorrect “yes” responses to suggestive questions referring to details that were not present in the video. In addition, a yes score was calculated to control for a potential response bias, reflecting the overall tendency to respond affirmatively to questions. Affirmative or “yes” responses to suggestive questions are commonly used as indicators of susceptibility to suggestion in interrogative suggestibility research [12, 41] (Fig. 1).

Discussion

In this study, we asked whether trauma symptom severity affects eyewitness memory accuracy and suggestibility, and whether dissociation or emotional arousal play any role in these relationships. A pretty consistent pattern emerged. Higher trauma symptoms went along with lower memory accuracy, and people with lower memory accuracy were more vulnerable to misleading post-event information. That indirect effect was significant. Neither dissociation nor emotional arousal, however, turned out to be meaningful contributors to suggestibility. Below, we discuss each of these findings in relation to our theoretical framework and the existing literature.

Trauma symptom severity and eyewitness memory accuracy

The negative association between trauma symptom severity and memory accuracy (r = − .27, p < .01) is consistent with a growing body of evidence indicating that trauma-related processes impair episodic memory functioning beyond the domain of personally experienced events [17, 18]. Brewin [31] proposed that traumatic stress disrupts the coordination between verbally accessible and situationally accessible memory systems, resulting in fragmented and less retrievable representations of experienced events. When this disruption extends to cognitive functions such as sustained attention and executive control, which are already known to be compromised by trauma [15], it is reasonable to expect a decline in the encoding of observed events, even if those events are not personally threatening. The present findings support this extension: participants in the high-trauma group encoded fewer correct details of a traffic accident they merely watched, not one they experienced.

This pattern is also consistent with the signal-based accounts of eyewitness memory described by Mickes et al. [10], which suggest that the fidelity of a memory report depends on the strength of the underlying memory signal at the time of testing. Consequently, any attentional, emotional, or neurobiological factor that degrades this signal during encoding will inevitably reduce subsequent accuracy. Trauma-related reductions in attentional control and working memory capacity represent exactly such factors. The finding that dissociation was also negatively correlated with memory accuracy (r = − .33) is compatible with evidence that dissociative tendencies are associated with impaired executive functioning and attention [21, 22], though the two predictors clearly share variance given their strong intercorrelation (r = .60).

Memory accuracy as the pathway to suggestibility

The central finding of the mediation analysis indicating that memory accuracy accounts for the association between trauma severity and suggestibility has clear theoretical grounding in the source monitoring framework [25]. According to this account, eyewitness errors arise not from deliberate fabrication but from failures of source attribution: when the original memory representation lacks perceptual detail and contextual specificity, it becomes difficult to distinguish what was actually witnessed from what was later suggested or inferred. The present data fit this account well. Trauma severity was negatively associated with memory accuracy (B = − 0.043, p = .008), and memory accuracy in turn negatively predicted suggestibility (B = − 0.290, p < .001), with the indirect effect falling clearly outside zero (95% CI [0.002, 0.024]). Once memory accuracy was in the model, the direct path from trauma to suggestibility was negligible and non-significant (B = 0.001, p = .871).

Mitchell and Johnson [32] demonstrated that source monitoring failures are most likely when memories lack qualitative features such as perceptual vividness and spatial or temporal detail, which normally mark them as internally encoded. When these cues are absent, individuals have little basis to reject post-event suggestions, which is precisely the situation that lower encoding quality creates. Dudek and Polczyk [42] provided converging evidence from a pre-registered study showing that memory distrust, acting as a disposition to doubt one’s own recollections, independently predicts suggestibility. This finding again points to the quality and confidence of the original memory representation as the critical variable. Taken together with the present findings, these studies converge on the same conclusion: in individuals whose memory encoding is compromised, whether by clinical levels of trauma symptoms or by the cognitive correlates of dissociation, post-event suggestions fill the gap left by absent or degraded memory traces.

A separate partial correlation analysis controlling for response bias (yes score) showed that the memory accuracy–suggestibility association remained strong (r = − .655, p < .001), ruling out the possibility that this relationship is an artefact of common method variance or a simple tendency to respond affirmatively. The group-level equivalence in yes scores across trauma groups further supports this interpretation. These converging checks strengthen confidence that the mediation findings reflect a genuine cognitive mechanism rather than a statistical confound.

Dissociation: a theoretically plausible but empirically non-significant pathway

Despite the strong theoretical case for dissociation as a mediator rooted in the source monitoring framework’s emphasis on encoding-time disruptions, the supplementary mediation analysis found no evidence for an indirect effect of trauma on suggestibility via the DES (indirect effect = 0.001, 95% CI [− 0.011, 0.011]). Trauma severity strongly predicted DES scores (B = 0.837, p < .001), confirming the well-established link between trauma and trait dissociation [43, 44]. But DES scores were essentially unrelated to suggestibility (B = 0.001, p = .898), a finding further corroborated by the bivariate correlation analyses (r = .15, ns). This null result does not straightforwardly falsify the dissociative account. Eisen and Carlson [23] found associations between dissociation and suggestibility that were specific to certain interview conditions; Sajjadi et al. [24] showed the relationship was moderated by trauma history and cognitive ability. What these studies share with the present null finding is a pattern of context-dependence: dissociation does not reliably predict suggestibility across all operationalizations and conditions. The more fundamental issue here is one of construct mismatch. The DES measures stable trait-level dissociative tendencies across everyday life, whereas the mechanism hypothesised to increase eyewitness errors is state dissociation at the time of encoding — attentional disengagement, depersonalisation, and disrupted contextual processing during the witnessed event itself. Instruments such as the Peritraumatic Dissociative Experiences Questionnaire (PDEQ; Marmar et al., 1997) capture this peritraumatic dimension and would have provided a more direct test of the theoretical prediction. The absence of such a measure is a limitation of the present study, and future research should administer both trait and state measures to disentangle these contributions.

Emotional arousal: methodological limits and theoretical considerations

Emotional arousal at the time of viewing was unrelated to memory accuracy, suggestibility, or trauma severity. This stands in some tension with Deffenbacher et al. [33] meta-analytic finding that high stress impairs eyewitness accuracy, a pattern broadly consistent with the Yerkes–Dodson principle that extreme arousal degrades performance. Mather and Sutherland [27] arousal-biased competition model adds nuance: arousal enhances encoding of attended central features while suppressing peripheral ones, producing a trade-off rather than uniform impairment. In crime and accident contexts, this can lead to an imbalance where the central action, such as the collision itself, is well-remembered while peripheral details are poorly recalled, creating a fine-grained pattern that a 20-item yes/no checklist may be insensitive to.

Beyond theoretical considerations, the arousal measure used here, consisting of a single 9-point retrospective item completed after all memory tasks, introduces several problems. Ratings made after completing a memory questionnaire reflect post-hoc appraisal, not encoding-time state, and are susceptible to memory-based reinterpretation of one’s own emotional experience. The item conflates arousal intensity with valence and cannot capture moment-to-moment fluctuation during viewing. Range restriction within a student sample watching a scripted video in a laboratory almost certainly further reduced the discriminative power of this measure. These limitations collectively explain the null association more parsimoniously than a conclusion that arousal is genuinely unrelated to eyewitness memory. Future work should use validated multi-item instruments such as physiological measures, administered during or immediately after event exposure.

Gender differences in memory accuracy

The descriptive data in Table 3 reveal an interaction-like pattern worth noting: males in the low-trauma group showed considerably higher memory accuracy than females in the same group (M = 15.43 vs. 13.81), whereas this gap nearly disappeared in the high-trauma group (M = 13.33 vs. 13.10). Russell et al. [45] scoping review of sex differences in eyewitness memory found that the direction of gender effects depends heavily on the type of material being remembered, with male advantages more consistent for action-oriented and spatial content, and female advantages for verbal and facial detail. The present paradigm involved a fast-moving traffic accident, which may have favoured the attentional style more common among male participants in the absence of elevated trauma symptoms.

The convergence of male and female performance in the high-trauma group is theoretically interesting, suggesting that elevated trauma symptoms may impose a floor-like constraint on encoding quality that overrides any individual or gender-related differences typically observed. This would suggest that trauma-related cognitive vulnerabilities are powerful enough to narrow the range of individual differences in memory performance. However, this interpretation is post-hoc and was not part of the pre-specified hypotheses. Gender was included as an exploratory variable, and the pattern observed here should be treated as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Replication in a larger sample with pre-registered gender predictions and theoretically motivated contrasts is needed before firmer conclusions can be drawn.

Trauma, memory, and forensic implications

From a forensic standpoint, the central finding that trauma-related reductions in memory quality are associated with greater acceptance of misleading post-event information has several implications for how the testimony of trauma-exposed witnesses is elicited and evaluated. The increased rate of errors observed alongside elevated trauma severity should not be interpreted as evidence of intentional deception or poor credibility in any simple sense. As Bozkurt and Ziyalar [46] meta-synthesis showed, trauma severity predicts inconsistency in witness statements, a pattern the present findings help to explain mechanistically: inconsistencies arise from the same encoding-quality deficit that makes individuals more susceptible to post-event suggestion. Witnesses who encode fewer correct details are not only less accurate when reporting from memory; they are also more vulnerable to having their already-uncertain memories shaped by the questions they are asked.

This has practical implications for investigative interviewing, as techniques that minimise post-event contamination, such as open-ended recall, the Cognitive Interview, and the NICHD Protocol, are likely to be particularly important when interviewing trauma-exposed witnesses precisely because these individuals are more susceptible to distortion introduced by suggestion.

The finding that trauma-related vulnerability is mediated by memory quality rather than by general response tendencies further implies that improving encoding conditions, for instance by reducing stress and cognitive load during interviewing, may reduce susceptibility to misinformation. Borinca et al. [34] demonstrated that the way traumatic memories are contextualised and processed matters for downstream psychological and social outcomes; an analogous argument applies here: the conditions under which trauma-exposed witnesses are interviewed will shape the extent to which trauma-related memory impairment translates into unreliable testimony.

Conclusions

The findings of the present study indicate that trauma symptom severity is associated with reduced eyewitness memory accuracy, and that lower memory accuracy in turn is associated with greater susceptibility to misleading post-event information. The indirect association between trauma severity and suggestibility through memory accuracy was statistically significant, whereas the path through dissociation was not, suggesting that cognitive rather than dissociative mechanisms may be more proximal to this relationship. These associations are cross-sectional and observational in nature; causal conclusions cannot be drawn from the present design. As participants became more likely to give incorrect answers, they tended to say “yes” more often. This pattern is consistent with previous research demonstrating that individuals with low memory confidence tend to rely more on external cues during recall, increasing their vulnerability to suggestive questioning [42, 47, 48]. When memory traces are weak or uncertain, individuals have less basis to evaluate the accuracy of post-event information, which may increase reliance on external sources and reduce the ability to discriminate between internally generated and externally provided details. These results highlight the importance of considering memory accuracy when evaluating the reliability of eyewitness testimony and suggest that interventions aimed at preserving memory integrity may reduce susceptibility to suggestion in individuals with elevated trauma levels.

The present study contributes to the literature by examining trauma severity, dissociation, memory accuracy, and suggestibility within a single model, clarifying their relative associations. While previous research has often examined these variables separately or emphasized dissociation as a key mechanism, the current findings suggest that memory accuracy may provide a more robust account of trauma-related vulnerability to suggestion, at least when dissociation is operationalized as trait rather than state dissociation. Furthermore, the absence of group differences in response bias suggests that these effects are driven by cognitive mechanisms rather than general response tendencies.

Limitations

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings. First, the sample consisted of undergraduate students from a single university, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Although student samples are commonly used in eyewitness memory research due to their accessibility and experimental control, they may not fully represent broader populations or individuals involved in real forensic contexts.

Additionally, the observational design and cross-sectional nature of the mediation analysis mean that temporal precedence cannot be established among trauma symptoms, memory accuracy, and suggestibility, all of which were assessed within the same session. The indirect associations observed are consistent with but do not confirm a causal pathway from trauma to memory to suggestibility. Third, the arousal measure was a single retrospective 9-point item administered after task completion; this cannot distinguish affective valence from arousal intensity or capture dynamic fluctuations during encoding, substantially limiting its construct validity and the ability to detect arousal effects. Fourth, dissociation was assessed using a trait measure (DES), whereas the mechanism theorized to link dissociation to eyewitness errors concerns state dissociation during encoding. The null mediation finding for dissociation therefore cannot be taken as a definitive test of the dissociative account. Fifth, both memory accuracy and suggestibility were derived from the same 20-item post-event questionnaire, introducing a degree of mathematical dependency between these indices; future studies should employ independent operationalizations or signal-detection approaches that separate discriminability from response bias. Sixth, the exclusion of participants with prior psychiatric diagnoses, psychotherapy history, substance use, and traffic accident experience limits generalizability to trauma-exposed populations in real forensic contexts, where such histories are common. Seventh, the final sample of 104 participants may have reduced statistical power to detect smaller effects, including some null findings reported here. Finally, the study relied on a video depicting a traffic accident to simulate an eyewitness experience. Although this approach is widely used in eyewitness memory research and allows for experimental control, it may not fully replicate the emotional intensity, personal relevance, and contextual complexity of real-life witnessed events. The forensic implications of these findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously: the present study used a non-clinical student sample, a brief yes/no questionnaire, self-reported trauma symptoms, and a single standardized video stimulus, and the results cannot be directly extended to real-world investigative interviewing or credibility assessment without further replication in more ecologically valid settings.
