Clinical and radiographic outcomes of pulpotomy materials in permanent teeth: a systematic review of calcium hydroxide, MTA, biodentine, and iRoot BP plus
Authors: Anggi Putri Riandani, Arief Cahyanto, Rana Abdelbaset Lotfy Diab, Ratih Widyasari, Atia Nurul Sidiqa, Hendra Dian Adhita Dharsono, Myrna Nurlatifah Zakaria
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPulpotomy is increasingly considered a viable treatment for permanent teeth with carious or traumatic pulp exposure, particularly when preserving pulp vitality is desired. Materials such as Calcium Hydroxide (CH), Mineral Trioxide Aggregate (MTA), Biodentine, and iRoot BP Plus have distinct properties. Although many studies report favorable outcomes, direct comparisons between these materials in permanent teeth remain limited, and evidence on their relative long-term performance is inconclusive. This systematic review aimed to evaluate and compare the clinical and radiographic outcomes of CH, MTA, Biodentine, and iRoot BP Plus in permanent teeth, providing evidence to guide material selection in clinical practice.MethodsA comprehensive search of Cochrane, PubMed, ScienceDirect, Google Scholar, and Sage Journals was conducted for studies published from 2000 up to mid 2025 using keywords such as Pulpotomy, Vital Pulp Treatment (VPT), Calcium Hydroxide (CH), MTA, Biodentine, and iRoot BP Plus. The review followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines and the PICOS framework. The quality of randomized controlled trials was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool, while cohort and case reports were evaluated using the JBI checklist.ResultsA total of 1,543 studies were initially identified, and 22 met the inclusion criteria, with follow-up periods ranging from 6 to 45.6 months. Risk of bias assessments indicated sufficient quality for data extraction. MTA demonstrated consistently high success rates (80%–100%), with several studies reporting complete clinical and radiographic success. iRoot BP Plus also showed excellent outcomes (84.6%–100%), with near perfect results in multiple studies. Biodentine achieved success rates from 70.7% to 100%, with most studies above 90%. CH showed more variable performance (37.5%–99.8%), though some trials reported outcomes comparable to MTA.ConclusionMTA and iRoot BP Plus demonstrated superior clinical and radiographic success, with Biodentine as a strong alternative. Although CH showed lower success rates, its long history of use supports its continued relevance. These findings should be interpreted cautiously due to study heterogeneity, the limited number of high-quality trials, and short follow-up in some cases. Further well-designed randomized controlled trials with long-term follow-up are needed to confirm these results and guide material selection for pulpotomy in permanent teeth.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07322-2.

Introduction

Irreversible pulpitis is a common consequence of deep carious lesions or traumatic pulp exposures that result in severe inflammation of the dental pulp. Traditionally, full pulpectomy/root canal therapy (RCT) has been the standard treatment for such cases, involving the complete removal of the inflamed pulp tissue from the entire root canal system [1]. However, full pulpectomy procedures are time-consuming, technique-sensitive, and result in the loss of pulp vitality, which compromises dentinogenesis, immune defense, and biomechanical strength [2, 3]. Preservation of pulp vitality is essential for maintaining the tooth’s natural defense mechanisms and supporting continued dentinogenesis, particularly in immature teeth. Furthermore, the sensory and mechanoreceptive functions of a vital pulp contribute to proprioception and the dissipation of occlusal forces, thereby enhancing the tooth’s resistance to masticatory stress [3–5].

Minimally invasive approaches in endodontics, particularly vital pulp therapy (VPT), have gained increasing attention for their ability to preserve healthy pulp tissue [2, 4]. VPT procedures, such as direct or indirect pulp capping and partial or complete pulpotomy are now accepted as viable alternatives to RCT, especially in teeth with symptomatic or asymptomatic vital pulp with no signs of periapical pathology [2]. By preserving the pulp vitality, these procedures maintain the natural tooth functions, such as proprioception, ongoing dentinogenesis, and immune surveillance, which are critical for tooth survival, especially in immature teeth with incomplete root development [6, 7].

Pulpotomy involves the removal of inflamed coronal pulp tissue while retaining the healthy radicular pulp, followed by placement of a biocompatible material to facilitate healing and regeneration, preserve the vitality of the remaining pulp, and stimulate the formation of a mineralized barrier known as the dentinal bridge [6–8]. Histologic studies indicate that carious pulp exposure does not always lead to total pulpal involvement [4]. This supports the rationale that removing the severely inflamed coronal pulp and sealing the remaining tissue with a bioactive material can effectively preserve pulp vitality [5]. Compared with direct pulp capping, pulpotomy also offers several advantages such as better control over inflamed tissue removal, improved haemostasis, and increased space for a durable seal; all contributing to higher clinical success rates [4, 9–11]. Multiple studies have demonstrated that pulpotomy in permanent teeth can achieve comparable success rates to RCT, suggesting it is a valid alternative for managing irreversible pulpitis in selected cases [12–15].

Over the years, calcium hydroxide (CH) has been the material of choice for VPT, due to its antimicrobial properties and ability to stimulate hard tissue formation. However, CH has several drawbacks, including degradation and dissolution in tissue fluids, and suboptimal quality of the hard tissue barrier [4, 5, 15]. More recently, calcium silicate-based cements (CSCs); such as Mineral Trioxide Aggregate (MTA), Biodentine, and iRoot BP Plus; have been introduced and widely adopted in endodontics [14–17]. These materials offer improved physical properties, superior sealing ability, and enhanced biological performance [15, 16]. CSCs are known for their high biocompatibility, bioactivity, antibacterial effects, and ability to promote mineralized tissue formation [14–17]. They have also been shown to reduce postoperative pain, and modulate inflammatory responses [18, 19].

Although pulpotomy has been the subject of numerous systematic reviews, there remains a notable gap in research that comprehensively compares the various materials used for pulpotomy in permanent teeth, particularly with respect to both clinical and radiographic outcomes. Our preliminary search revealed a distinct gap in the current literature. Most available systematic reviews have concentrated on pulpotomy in primary teeth or explored different clinical questions unrelated to the comparative effectiveness of pulpotomy materials in permanent teeth. Among the few that focused on materials, the scope was generally narrow, limited to pairwise comparisons or assessments of clinical or radiographic outcomes alone. To date, no comprehensive review has systematically compared calcium hydroxide with the three major calcium silicate–based cements; MTA, Biodentine, and iRoot BP Plus, while simultaneously evaluating both clinical and radiographic outcomes. The present systematic review integrates these materials within a single analytical framework, providing a robust and unified evidence base that bridges existing research gaps and offers clinically meaningful guidance for material selection in pulpotomy of permanent teeth.

Materials and methods

Study design and objective

This review was a qualitative systematic review conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and registered in PROSPERO international prospective register of systematic reviews (CRD42023481632). The objective was to evaluate clinical and radiographic outcomes of several pulpotomy materials, including CH, MTA, Biodentine, and iRoot BP Plus, through studies published from 2000 up to August 2025. A quantitative meta-analysis was not feasible due to substantial heterogeneity in study designs, populations, follow-up periods, and outcome definitions. Therefore, findings are presented narratively, with success rate ranges (and confidence intervals where available) reported to summarize the evidence.

Search strategy

The article search was carried out in the following databases: PubMed, Cochrane, ScienceDirect, Sage Journal, and Google Scholar to identify relevant studies. The search strategy used the following combination of terms: Pulpotomy, Vital Pulp Treatment (VPT), Calcium Hydroxide, MTA, Biodentine, Bioceramics, iRoot BP Plus. For the investigation, the search query “(“pulpotomy”) AND (“Calcium Hydroxide” OR “MTA” OR “BIODENTINE” OR “iRoot BP Plus” OR “iRoot”) AND (“Success rate” OR “outcome”)” was employed. The literature search was restricted to studies published between January 2000 to August 2025, corresponding to the timeframe established at the onset of the review process.

Data extraction and data items

Data were extracted on various study characteristics, including author name, year of publication, study design, pulpotomy materials used (CH, MTA, Biodentine, and iRoot BP Plus), sample size, follow-up period, and reported success rates. Clinical and radiographic outcomes were also recorded. Any discrepancies in data extraction were resolved through discussion and consensus between the reviewers.

Risk of bias (RoB) assessment

The risk of bias for each included clinical study was assessed independently by A.P.R and R.A.L.D using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool for randomized controlled trials and the JBI critical appraisal checklist for cohort studies and case reports. The assessment aimed to critically appraise the quality and potential biases of the selected studies. This comprehensive approach was designed to identify relevant studies investigating the success rates and outcomes of the pulpotomy materials included in this review.

Results

A total of 1,543 records were identified during the initial database search. After removing duplicates (221 studies) and inaccessible studies for which full texts could not be retrieved despite attempts via institutional subscriptions and available open-access sources (304 studies), 1018 studies were screened based on their titles and abstracts. Studies were carefully evaluated according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Research involving pulpotomy procedures on deciduous teeth, review articles, studies using pulpotomy materials other than those specified for this investigation, and studies lacking sufficient or relevant data to address the objectives of the present review were excluded. In total, 83 studies were eligible for screening, and 22 studies that met all the eligibility criteria were ultimately included in this systematic review (Fig. 1).

Risk of bias assessment was conducted using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and the JBI critical appraisal checklist for cohort studies and case series (Table 2; Fig. 2). The RoB assessment is presented in a traffic light plot, covering five bias domains: D1 (bias arising from the randomization process), D2 (bias due to deviations from intended interventions), D3 (bias arising from missing outcome data), D4 (bias in outcome measurement), and D5 (bias in the selection of reported results). A yellow circle indicates some concerns about bias, a green circle indicates a low risk of bias, while a red circle represents a high risk of bias. Of the 12 studies included in the Cochrane RoB 2 assessment, only one study raised concerns in the second domain due to limitations in isolation during treatment, as the use of a rubber dam was not feasible in all cases. The other 10 studies demonstrated an overall low risk of bias. Discussion

Pulpotomy is less invasive and cost-effective alternative to RCT in managing symptomatic teeth with vital pulp. However, despite its procedural simplicity, successful outcomes rely on proper case selection, accurate diagnosis, aseptic technique, effective bleeding control, use of biocompatible materials, and immediate coronal sealing [40].

Pulpotomy techniques are broadly classified as partial pulpotomy and full pulpotomy (Fig. 3). Partial pulpotomy, which involves removing 2–3 mm of inflamed coronal pulp, is typically indicated for deep caries with pinpoint exposure. In contrast, full pulpotomy involves removal of the entire coronal pulp and is more appropriate for irreversible pulpitis when bleeding can be controlled at the canal orifices [41]. The decision to perform a pulpotomy depends on achieving haemostasis; If bleeding persists, sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) applied for 10 min may help facilitate control [23].Fig. 3The procedure of partial pulpotomy (A) and full pulpotomy (B)

Vital pulp therapy (VPT) is considered clinically successful when there is no spontaneous pain, tenderness, swelling, sinus tract, abnormal mobility, or discomfort during chewing. Radiographically, success is indicated via the absence of internal or external root resorption, periapical and/or inter-radicular radiolucency, irregular calcification, or other pathologic changes, along with continued root development and apexogenesis in immature teeth. While the American Association of Endodontics (AAE) does not specify measures for VPT outcomes, the European Society of Endodontology (ESE) recommends follow-ups at 6–12 months and annually for up to 4 years [41–43].

The choice of pulpotomy material is critical to the long-term success of VPT in permanent teeth. Current evidence indicates that all four materials, CH, MTA, Biodentine, and iRoot BP Plus, demonstrate favourable clinical and radiographic outcomes when proper case selection and technique are applied. However, differences in their properties and performance can influence clinical decision-making. Calcium Hydroxide (CH) remains the gold standard in VPT due to its affordability and biological activity [44]. It dissociates into calcium and hydroxyl ions, creating a highly alkaline environment (pH = 11) that neutralizes osteoclastic lactic acid and activates alkaline phosphatase (ALP), which is crucial for hard tissue formation [42]– [43]. CH also promotes dentin regeneration by releasing growth factors and dentin matrix [4]. Despite its physical limitations, its sustained release of bioactive molecules such as bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) and transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) supports pulp healing and repair [4, 5, 15, 42].

Clinical success rates for CH pulpotomy range widely and are influenced by factors such as patient age, pulpal status, and operator technique [4, 5, 15, 25, 37, 42, 43]. Reported success rates range from 72.2% in immature permanent teeth, with 27.8% experiencing postoperative pain [37], to as high as 94%, where failures were linked to pulp necrosis and infection confirmed through clinical symptoms and radiographic findings [25]. Such failures are often associated with bacterial contamination from coronal microleakage or compromised dentinal bridges [25]. Radiographically, dentin bridge formation with CH was observed in over 90% of successful cases and in 85.7% of failed cases, although bridge formation was significantly more frequent and complete in successful outcomes [25]. Pulp canal obliteration was reported in approximately 2% of cases and was still considered a sign of favourable healing [25].

In traumatized immature permanent teeth, CH achieved an 82.9% clinical success rate for partial pulpotomy, with failed cases showing periapical radiolucency, while successful cases exhibited mineralized barrier formation [26]. A comparison between CH, Biodentine, and MTA for partial pulpotomy in carious immature permanent molars reported an overall success rate of 87%, with CH achieving 72.2% [37]. Radiographically, all groups showed a continuous and significant increase in mesial and distal root lengths, and CH showed a significantly greater mesial root length increase at 6–12 months compared with the other materials [37].

To overcome the limitations of CH, calcium silicate-based cements (CSCs) were developed as alternative bioactive materials. CSCs produce CH when calcium silicate reacts with phosphate-containing body fluids. Like CH, they release calcium ions (Ca²⁺), which activate intracellular signalling, promote dental pulp stem cell (DPSC) differentiation, and facilitate dentine bridge formation. They also release hydroxyl ions (OH⁻), raising pH to around 12.5 and contributing to antibacterial activity through hydroxyl radical formation. Additionally, CSCs suppress proinflammatory cytokines and stimulate interleukin-10, creating a favourable environment for dentin regeneration [15, 17, 45].

MTA is one of the most studied CSCs, consisting of tricalcium silicate, dicalcium silicate, tricalcium aluminate, dehydrated calcium sulphate, and bismuth oxide. Upon hydration, it forms a colloidal gel with an amorphous calcium oxide structure. Its setting is not hindered by blood or moisture and potentially stimulates cytokine release and interleukin production. Its high success rates in VPT are mainly attributed to its biocompatibility and superior sealing ability. Nevertheless, MTA has some drawbacks, including poor handling, long setting time, discoloration, and higher cost [4, 7, 28, 41, 42, 45].

Clinical studies report MTA clinical success rates of 93–100% for both carious and traumatic pulp exposures [3, 4, 7, 20, 22, 29, 30, 41, 42], with some studies achieving 100% success rate over follow-ups of up to 42 months [20, 29]. Failures are often linked to preoperative pain or tooth discoloration [3]. A lower success rate of 81.5% was reported in immature permanent teeth, likely influenced by the younger, more homogeneous patient population (ages 6–10 years), suggesting age-related factors may affect outcomes [21].

Radiographically, MTA-treated teeth demonstrated 93–100% success rates, with no periapical changes during follow-up, hard tissue barrier formation, and continued root development in immature teeth [20, 30]. It has also proven effective in complex cases such as partial pulp necrosis and chronic periradicular abscess, where radiographs revealed thickened root walls, narrowing of the apical foramen, and reduced periapical radiolucency [22].

Compared to CH, MTA induces faster and more uniform dentinal bridge formation with fewer tunnel defects, although similar success rates have been reported in some carious pulp exposure cases [9, 29]. In traumatised incisors, MTA pulpotomies achieved 100% success rate versus 87% with CH [46]. Both materials yield favourable partial pulpotomy outcomes, but larger pulp exposure areas (>5 mm²) increase the risk of unfavourable results, emphasizing the importance of case selection [33].

Biodentine, a second-generation CSC based on MTA technology, was developed to overcome its limitations. It incorporates accelerators and softeners to reduce setting time, while zirconium oxide is used as a radiopacifier instead of bismuth oxide to avoid tooth discoloration. Marketed as a “bioactive dentin substitute,” Biodentine facilitates pulp healing and dentine regeneration when applied directly to the pulp tissue [45, 47]. Despite its advantages, evidence on its performance in pulpotomy for permanent teeth remains limited [48, 49].

Clinical outcomes of Biodentine in full pulpotomy for permanent teeth with both carious and traumatic pulp exposures have been widely reported. Clinical success rates of 70.7–94% have been reported at 6–36 months, with failures generally associated with spontaneous pain or minor crown discoloration [23, 24, 28, 37]. One study reported a lower Biodentine success rate (70.7%), mainly due to restoration failures that might be attributed to the early placement of resin-modified glass ionomer cement (RMGIC) after only 12 min of setting, which may have reduced adhesion and compromised outcomes [28]. In traumatized teeth, success rates exceeded 90%, with transient cold sensitivity resolving over time [24]. Radiographically, Biodentine promoted dentine bridge formation in 25% of the cases, with complete healing or reduction of periapical lesions in most treated teeth; however, one case demonstrated internal resorption within a year despite apparent healing and was classified as a failure [23]. Another study reported dentine bridge formation and continued root development in 91% of treated teeth, highlighting Biodentine’s regenerative potential, particularly in immature permanent teeth [24].

Comparisons between Biodentine and MTA show comparable clinical outcomes, though MTA is more commonly associated with tooth discoloration [ON3]. Biologically, no difference in pulp response to the two materials has been reported [50]. In primary teeth, both MTA and Biodentine achieved a 100% clinical success rate and radiographic success rates of 92% and 80%, respectively [50]. Another study reported similar findings with clinical success rates of 97% for Biodentine and 92% for MTA, and radiographic success rates of 95% and 97%, respectively. In both groups, one clinical failure occurred, likely due to misdiagnosis, iatrogenic error, poor adaptation, or leakage of the final restoration [51].

iRoot BP Plus is a pre-mixed, calcium silicate–based bioactive cement marketed as EndoSequence Root Repair Material Putty (ERRM putty). It contains calcium silicate, calcium phosphate, zirconium oxide, and tantalum oxide, forming calcium silicate and hydroxyapatite hydrogels in dentinal tubules, enhancing its sealing ability and bioactivity. It relies on moisture from dentinal tubules for setting; however, insufficient moisture may delay the process. Compared to MTA, it offers similar antimicrobial effects without causing tooth discoloration, making it a promising alternative for vital pulp therapy [25, 38].

In a study involving 59 permanent teeth with irreversible pulpitis treated using either partial or full pulpotomy with iRoot BP Plus, clinical success rates were 90.9% for partial pulpotomy and 84.6% for full pulpotomy. Success was defined by absence of pain, tenderness, abnormal mobility, or signs of infection during a follow-up period of 3 months to over one year [ON2]. Similarly, a 90.4% success rate was observed in traumatized immature permanent teeth treated with partial pulpotomy, with no signs of failure [36]. Another study reported a 100% success rate for iRoot BP Plus in traumatized permanent teeth over 12–24 months, with no cases of abnormal mobility [25].

Radiographically, success rates for iRoot BP Plus reached 91.2% in carious teeth with irreversible pulpitis, based on the absence of periradicular or intraradicular pathology and continued root development in immature teeth [2]. Calcific bridge formation at the pulp-material interface was observed, with thickness increasing over time, indicating positive pulp healing [36]. Radiographic dentin bridges were reported in 92.3% of successful cases and in all failed cases, with an overall radiographic success rate of 99% [25]. Additional cases demonstrated a reduction in periapical radiolucency, increased root wall thickness, and apical closure following pulpotomy [22].

iRoot BP Plus demonstrates comparable or even superior clinical and radiographic outcomes to other CSCs. Compared to MTA, it offers the added benefit of eliminating tooth discoloration while maintaining strong antimicrobial and bioactive effects. Its pre-mixed formulation enhances ease of use, although its reliance on dentinal moisture can slightly prolong the setting time. Consistent calcific bridge formation and support for root maturation further underscore its effectiveness, particularly in immature and traumatized permanent teeth [25, 52–54]. In comparison to CH, iRoot BP Plus exhibits superior physical properties that enhance clinical performance. Failures with CH are often linked to bacterial infiltration through micro-cracks and decreased sealing ability in humid conditions. In contrast, iRoot BP Plus reacts with moisture to form a hydrated calcium silicate gel, maintaining its seal even in a moist environment. A randomized prospective controlled trial reported a 99% success rate for partial pulpotomies using iRoot BP Plus, compared to 93% for CH. Although no statistically significant difference was found in overall success rates, fewer failures occurred in the iRoot BP Plus group [36]. These findings highlight iRoot BP Plus as a robust and reliable material under varied clinical conditions.

Overall, CH, MTA, Biodentine, and iRoot BP Plus; demonstrate high clinical and radiographic success with proper case selection and technique. CH remains cost-effective option, though its limited barrier quality reduces sealing ability. MTA is highly reliable and with superior healing outcomes, but may cause tooth discoloration. Biodentine offers enhanced properties compared to MTA, such as reduced discoloration, easier manipulation, thicker consistency, faster setting time, and better mechanical properties; however, it is less radiopaque, requires triturating, and it still takes approximately 45 min to reach full strength, limiting its use in single-visit procedures. In contrast, iRoot BP Plus eliminates tooth discoloration and enables single-visit completion due to its pre-mixed formulation and rapid setting in moist environments. These features, combined with strong sealing ability, make it a promising alternative for pulpotomy. Despite the favourable outcomes of all these materials, further high-quality randomized controlled trials are needed to clarify long-term efficacy and comparative advantages. Future research should focus on their performance in teeth affected by caries or trauma to guide evidence-based clinical practice.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of the current evidence, CH, MTA, Biodentine, and iRoot BP Plus all demonstrate high clinical and radiographic success in pulpotomy for permanent teeth. While CH remains a cost-effective and well-established material, CSCs generally achieve higher success rates, with MTA supported by the strongest long-term evidence. iRoot BP Plus shows the highest reported outcomes, along with practical advantages such as ease of use, moisture tolerance, and lack of discoloration, making it suitable for single-visit and esthetic cases. Further high-quality trials are warranted to confirm these results across diverse clinical scenarios.
