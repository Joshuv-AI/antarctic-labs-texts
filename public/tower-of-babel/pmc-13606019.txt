Posterior Intradiscal Osteotomy With Posterior Approach Anterior Column Release at L5-S1 for Rigid Flat Back Deformity: A Single-Approach Alternative to ALIF or PSO for Sagittal Balance Restoration
Authors: Mohsen Rostami, Diego Soto Rubio, Sadegh Bagherzadeh, Negin Safari Dehnavi, Anjali Pradhan, Priyanka Pradhan, Katherine Elizabeth Baker, Sawyer Bauer, Joshua Vignolles-Jeong, Natalie Jun Bales, Erik Hayman, Puya Alikhani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND AND IMPORTANCE:Restoring distal lumbar lordosis at a fused L5-S1 junction is challenging in adult spinal deformity surgery, particularly in revision cases with limited anterior access. Posterior intradiscal osteotomy (IDO) with posterior approach anterior column release (P-ACR) provides a single-posterior approach for mobilizing the anterior column and restoring distal lumbar lordosis without the risks of retroperitoneal exposure. This report details the operative nuances and technical sequence for L5-S1 IDO with P-ACR and summarizes early outcomes from complex revision cases.CLINICAL PRESENTATION:Four patients with rigid sagittal imbalance, prior multilevel lumbar surgery, and near-complete L5-S1 autofusion underwent L5-S1 IDO with P-ACR. Three patients were nonambulatory preoperatively. In each case, the fused disc space was mobilized through controlled P-ACR, permitting insertion of an anteriorly positioned expandable cage at L5-S1. The mean lumbar lordosis improved from −28.6° preoperatively to −46.9° postoperatively (mean gain 18.3°). Sagittal vertical axis improved from 107.9 mm to 65.4 mm (mean reduction 42.6 mm, 39.4%). These radiographical gains were accompanied by reductions in pelvic tilt (−13.1°) and improvements in functional status and pain scores.CONCLUSION:L5-S1 IDO with P-ACR offers a posterior-only solution for restoring distal lumbar lordosis in patients with rigid lumbosacral deformity. By enabling anterior cage placement from a posterior corridor, this approach avoids the exposure-related risks of anterior lumbar interbody fusion and the morbidity of pedicle subtraction osteotomy while providing effective correction in heavily scarred revision fields. The stepwise description and illustrative cases may assist spine surgeons in incorporating this technique into practice.

In patients with prior lumbar arthrodesis or autofusion, the loss of motion and lordosis at L5-S1 flattens the lower arc of the lumbar spine, reduces sacral slope, and forces compensatory pelvic retroversion.1 Failure to restore lordosis at L5-S1 frequently results in residual imbalance, ongoing pain, and limited function.2

Anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF) and pedicle subtraction osteotomy (PSO) are well-established solutions in this scenario. ALIF provides generous access to the anterior column and allows placement of a highly lordotic graft, but at the cost of an abdominal or retroperitoneal exposure, with associated vascular and visceral risks.3 ALIF and release of the fusion mass may be also challenging in a fused L5-S1 because of proximity of vessels. PSO achieves powerful angular correction through vertebral body resection but is a major undertaking, with substantial blood loss and neurological risk.4

Posterior intradiscal osteotomy (IDO) at L5-S1 offers another path.5,6 Performed entirely from a posterior approach, IDO lengthens the anterior column through sectioning of the anterior longitudinal ligament (posterior approach anterior column release, P-ACR) while shortening the posterior column. Although ACR mostly refers to minimally invasive lateral approach for release of ALL and inserting a cage in the disk space,7 it should be mostly followed by posterior approach surgery for compression and achieving appropriate fusion.8,9 However, we propose that by P-ACR, both steps of the surgery can be performed in a single session, creating space for a lordotic interbody device and achieving fusion without the morbidity of anterior exposure or vertebral body resection.

CLINICAL PRESENTATION

This study is a technical report that reviews our patients who underwent P-ACR at L5-S1. The primary objective was to evaluate the feasibility and surgical challenges of using this technique for restoring sagittal balance and its effect on radiological parameters.

Patients provided informed consent, and no personally identifying patient information was included in this study, which was approved by the institutional review board (IRB No. Pro00023643).

Posterior IDO Technique

Under general anesthesia with neuromonitoring, the patient was positioned prone, and a standard midline posterior approach to the fusion mass is performed, preserving soft tissue attachments when feasible to minimize devascularization.

Instrumentation is extended proximally and distally according to the surgical plan. Smith–Peterson osteotomy is performed at L5-S1 to remove posterior fusion mass. Scar tissue from prior operations is dissected to identify the exiting L5 and traversing S1 nerve roots, followed by complete bilateral facetectomies for full visualization of the foramina and roots. The fused disk space is localized with lateral fluoroscopy. Bilateral entry into the fused disk space is achieved by breaching the posterior bony fusion mass at the annular level with chisels. Sequential disk shavers and osteotomes loosen the fusion mass and release the annulus in a controlled fashion, protecting neural structures (Figure 1A).

Stepwise posterior intradiscal osteotomy and posterior approach anterior column release at L5-S1. A, Exposure of the fused L5-S1 disk space after complete bilateral facetectomies. Osteotomes and disk shavers are used to breach the posterior fusion mass and gain access to the ankylosed disk, allowing controlled loosening of the posterior and lateral annulus while protecting the L5 and S1 nerve roots. B, Intradiscal release of the anterior longitudinal ligament performed from a posterior corridor. Sequential chisels are advanced under fluoroscopic guidance to mobilize the anterior column and permit angular correction without the need for anterior exposure. C, Final correction after bilateral placement of anteriorly positioned expandable cages at L5-S1. Posterior compression closes the osteotomy, restores segmental lordosis, and achieves stable lumbosacral alignment within a single posterior approach.

In cases of complete bony ankylosis, the anterior longitudinal ligament is released intradiscally using distractors and chisels under tactile feedback and fluoroscopic guidance (Figure 1B). This maneuver is performed deliberately and symmetrically to prevent asymmetric correction and to protect the anterior vascular structures. In partially fused disks, the ALL may be preserved; after releasing the posterior and lateral elements, a static anteriorly placed TLIF cage can be inserted to restore lordosis (Figure 1C).

Once the anterior column is mobilized, gradual distraction is applied using interbody spreaders under fluoroscopic control, allowing the ligamentous and annular tissues to stretch without undue tension on the L5 roots (Figure 2A and 2B). Bilateral high-lordosis (20°-30°) expandable cages are placed as far anteriorly as possible to engage the apophyseal ring, with incremental and symmetric expansion. Posterior compression across L5-S1 is applied to close the osteotomy and lock in the correction, with final rod tightening performed in the desired alignment.

Outcomes

The clinical characteristics and perioperative details of the 4 patients treated with L5-S1 IDO are summarized in Table 1. All of the patients underwent surgery by the senior surgeon (P.A.). The cohort had a mean age of 71.5 years (range 65-77). All patients had undergone at least one prior lumbar fusion and varying degrees of radicular symptoms or neurogenic claudication. Three of the 4 were functionally unable to ambulate at the time of presentation. Estimated blood loss averaged 750 mL (range 500-1000 mL).

In all cases, L5-S1 IDO allowed controlled mobilization of the fused disk space and a safe, deliberate release of the anterior column (Figures 3A-3D, 4A-4D, 5A-5D, and 6A-6D). This provided enough freedom to place expandable interbody cages anteriorly at L5-S1, which became the key point of correction in each reconstruction. Radiographical parameters before and after surgery are presented in Table 2. Across the series, improvements were seen in lumbar lordosis, sacral slope, and pelvic tilt, particularly in patients with marked preoperative retroversion. Segmental correction at L5-S1 was achieved in all cases, with a mean gain of 7.6°, and 3 patients demonstrated measurable improvement in global alignment (sagittal vertical axis). Patient 4 achieved substantial correction but developed Proximal Junctional Kyphosis at the upper instrumented vertebra during follow-up (Figure 6C and 6D). However, reoperation was not immediately required.

DISCUSSION

Traditional strategies for mobilizing the lumbosacral junction, most notably ALIF and PSO, are effective but carry important limitations. ALIF provides wide access to the anterior column and allows placement of high-lordosis grafts,10 yet it requires an anterior exposure that is associated with vascular injury, retroperitoneal complications, and may be contraindicated in patients with dense scarring, obesity, or prior abdominal surgery.3 IDO provides a posterior-only alternative. Although the mean segmental correction in our series (7.6°) was lower than typically reported for ALIF, IDO eliminates the morbidity of an anterior approach in the “hostile abdomen.” A standard L5 PSO can provide 30° to 35° of correction but is associated with high morbidity (blood loss, pseudarthrosis, and increases neurological risk). The IDO technique described here is significantly less morbid (mean EBL 750 mL) but also less powerful. It is not a substitute for PSO in cases requiring >25° correction. Instead, IDO fills a specific niche: moderate deformity in a revision setting where PSO is over-treatment and ALIF is contraindicated.4,11

Posterior IDO combined with P-ACR at L5-S1 provides an alternative pathway for achieving meaningful angular correction while avoiding the morbidity of anterior or combined approaches. Several authors, including Ramey et al6 and Cheung et al,5 have described the value of IDO and posterior ALL release as a means of restoring segmental lordosis without the need for retroperitoneal exposure. By performing both the release and the interbody reconstruction posteriorly, P-ACR consolidates the procedure into a single surgical corridor, which may reduce operative time and eliminate the risks associated with multistage approaches.12

Aligning with these observations, all 4 patients in our series presented with rigid lumbosacral deformity, previous surgical history, and significant disability. In every case, P-ACR with L5-S1 IDO permitted adequate mobilization of the anterior column despite complete or near-complete bony fusion. Placement of an anteriorly positioned expandable cage allowed controlled restoration of lordosis at the distal lumbar level, contributing significantly to global sagittal realignment. The improvements in pain and function across this cohort were consistent and clinically meaningful. Radiographically, all constructs remained stable at follow-up, and no patient demonstrated cage subsidence, implant failure, or loss of correction. These outcomes suggest that P-ACR with IDO can be safely applied even in multiple operated spines with significant scarring, where anterior access is hazardous or impractical.

This study is limited by its small sample size (N = 4) and retrospective nature. As noted in Patients 2 and 3, the technique may be limited in ankylosed spines where the posterior lever arm is insufficient to fully rupture a calcified ALL. Future studies with larger cohorts and long-term functional scoring are necessary.

CONCLUSION

While limited by small sample size and retrospective design, posterior IDO with P-ACR enabled effective lumbosacral correction through a single posterior corridor, offering a practical alternative when ALIF or PSO carry prohibitive risk and demonstrating clinical and radiographical improvement in complex revision deformity cases.
