Comparison of propofol and propofol–ketamine anesthesia on optic nerve sheath diameter in endovascular cerebral aneurysm procedures: a prospective randomized study
Authors: Eda Şahin, Nihal Gökbulut Özaslan, Gokhan Erdem, Güven Özkaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEndovascular treatment of unruptured intracranial aneurysms requires anesthetic strategies that maintain cerebral perfusion and hemodynamic stability while avoiding increases in intracranial pressure (ICP). Optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) measured by ultrasonography is commonly used as a noninvasive surrogate marker of ICP. This study compared propofol and propofol–ketamine anesthesia with respect to perioperative ONSD, cerebral oxygenation, and hemodynamic parameters during endovascular aneurysm procedures.MethodsIn this prospective, randomized, single-center, observer-blinded trial, 40 adults undergoing elective endovascular treatment of unruptured intracranial aneurysms were allocated to a propofol group (P, n = 20) or a propofol–ketamine group (KP, n = 20). Standardized anesthetic protocols and ventilation targeting normocapnia were used in both groups. ONSD, regional cerebral oxygen saturation (rSO₂), bispectral index (BIS), and hemodynamic variables were recorded at predefined perioperative time points. The primary outcome was the temporal change in mean ONSD. Mean ONSD was analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model including group, time, and group × time interaction. Secondary longitudinal outcomes were analyzed using exploratory mixed-effects models.ResultsBaseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable between groups. Total propofol consumption was significantly lower in the KP group, whereas remifentanil use showed a non-significant trend toward reduction. In the primary mixed-effects analysis, the group × time interaction for mean ONSD was not statistically significant, indicating that the temporal trajectory of ONSD did not differ between groups. The main effect of group was also not significant, whereas the main effect of time was significant. Exploratory analyses of secondary longitudinal outcomes showed significant group × time interactions for selected hemodynamic, ventilation-related, anesthetic depth, and cerebral oxygenation parameters, including DBP, MAP, EtCO₂, BIS, and mean rSO₂. Heart rate and SpO₂ trajectories were comparable between groups.ConclusionsIn this small randomized cohort of patients undergoing elective endovascular treatment of unruptured intracranial aneurysms, propofol and propofol–ketamine anesthesia showed comparable ONSD trajectories over time. The propofol–ketamine regimen was associated with reduced propofol consumption and exploratory evidence of differential perioperative hemodynamic and cerebral oxygenation profiles. However, the study was not powered to evaluate safety outcomes or rare adverse events. Larger neurosurgical trials are required before firm conclusions regarding safety and clinical feasibility can be drawn.Trial registrationThe study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier NCT07542015) on April 14, 2026. Background

Endovascular techniques have become increasingly preferred in the treatment of unruptured intracranial aneurysms because they are minimally invasive and provide favorable clinical outcomes [1]. During these procedures, anesthetic management is critical to maintain cerebral perfusion, ensure hemodynamic stability, and prevent fluctuations in intracranial pressure (ICP) [2].

Direct ICP monitoring is not routinely feasible in this population; therefore, noninvasive surrogate markers have gained importance. Ultrasonographic measurement of optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) provides a rapid, bedside, and reliable method for indirect ICP assessment and has been increasingly used in neurocritical and perioperative settings [3].

Propofol is widely used in neuroanesthesia owing to its favorable effects on cerebral metabolism and ability to reduce ICP [4]. Ketamine, historically avoided because of concerns about increasing ICP, has been reconsidered in recent years, particularly when administered in combination with other agents under controlled ventilatory conditions [5].

Despite the frequent use of propofol- and propofol–ketamine–based regimens in neurointerventional practice, comparative data on their effects on ICP dynamics during endovascular cerebral aneurysm procedures are limited. This study therefore aimed to evaluate the impact of propofol versus propofol–ketamine anesthesia on ONSD, as well as on cerebral oxygenation and hemodynamic parameters, in patients undergoing endovascular treatment of unruptured intracranial aneurysms [6].

Methods

This prospective, randomized, double-blinded, single-center study was conducted in the neurointerventional unit of a tertiary care hospital between June and December 2025, following approval by the Institutional Ethics Committee (Approval No: TABED 1-25-1345) and written informed consent from all participants. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The trial was subsequently registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT07542015).

This study was reported in accordance with the CONSORT guidelines for randomized controlled trials.

Adults aged 18–75 years with ASA (American Society of Anesthesiologists) physical status I–III and a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 15 who were scheduled for elective endovascular treatment of unruptured intracranial aneurysms were eligible for inclusion. Patients with ruptured aneurysms or aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage, ocular disease or previous eye surgery, known increased intracranial pressure, giant aneurysms, significant cardiovascular disease, emergency procedures, or neurological/psychiatric disorders impairing the ability to provide informed consent were excluded.

Patients were randomized to either the propofol group (P) or the propofol–ketamine group (KP) using a computer-generated randomization sequence. Allocation concealment was ensured using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes that were opened only after patient enrollment.

Study medications were prepared by an anesthesia technician who was not involved in patient management or outcome assessment and were supplied in identical 50-mL syringes to maintain blinding. The attending anesthesiologist, the investigator performing ONSD measurements, and the patients remained blinded to group allocation throughout the study.

All ONSD measurements were performed by a single anesthesiologist who had received specific training and certification in ocular ultrasonography/ONSD measurement under the supervision of the Department of Radiology. Before study initiation, the operator completed a structured training period and performed ONSD measurements in approximately 50 patients who were not included in the study. During the study, ONSD was measured bilaterally 3 mm posterior to the optic disc using a standardized ultrasound protocol. Four measurements (two eyes × sagittal and transverse planes) were obtained at each time point, and the mean value was used for analysis. As all measurements were performed by a single trained operator, formal intraobserver and interobserver variability analyses could not be conducted; this represents a limitation that has been acknowledged in the Discussion.

Measurements were performed at five predefined time points: before induction (T1), before intubation (T2), 1 min after intubation (T3), at the end of the procedure (T4), and in the recovery room (T5). These time points were prospectively selected to capture the perioperative phases during which the greatest changes in intracranial pressure were considered most likely to occur, including anesthetic induction, airway manipulation, emergence, and recovery. Additional measurements during the maintenance phase were not routinely performed because anesthesia depth, ventilation, and hemodynamic parameters were maintained within predefined target ranges using a standardized protocol.

Standard monitoring (ECG, noninvasive blood pressure, pulse oximetry) plus bispectral index (BIS) and near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) were applied. Anesthesia was induced in group P with propofol 2 mg/kg, fentanyl 1–2 µg/kg and rocuronium 0.6 mg/kg; in group KP with ketamine 0.5 mg/kg + propofol 2 mg/kg (1:5 mixture), fentanyl and rocuronium at the same doses. All patients were ventilated in volume-controlled mode with 6–8 mL/kg tidal volume, 50% air–oxygen mixture, EtCO₂ 30–40 mmHg, and PEEP 0–5 cmH₂O.

Maintenance of anesthesia consisted of propofol infusion (50–150 µg/kg/min) in Group P and a propofol–ketamine admixture (1:5) together with remifentanil infusion (0.05–0.2 µg/kg/min) in Group KP. The admixture was prepared immediately before use by mixing 40 mL of 1% propofol (10 mg/mL) with 8 mL ketamine (10 mg/mL) in a single 50-mL syringe, resulting in a fixed 1:5 ketamine-to-propofol ratio. The single-syringe admixture was chosen to ensure simultaneous administration of both agents at a fixed ratio throughout the procedure, simplify drug delivery using a single syringe pump, and maintain a standardized dosing ratio across all patients. Infusion rates were titrated to maintain a BIS value between 40 and 60. Hemodynamic management followed a predefined algorithm using fluids, anesthetic depth adjustment, ephedrine, and esmolol as needed.

Hemodynamic variables, SpO₂, EtCO₂, BIS, and regional cerebral oxygen saturation (rSO₂) were recorded at the same predefined time points (T1-T5).

The primary endpoint was change in ONSD from baseline (ΔONSD); mean ΔONSD (average of both eyes) was analyzed. Secondary outcomes included hemodynamic variables, SpO₂, EtCO₂, BIS, rSO₂, anesthetic drug consumption, recovery time, ICU and hospital stay, and complications.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median (minimum–maximum), and compared using Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.

Longitudinal changes in mean ONSD were analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model. Mean ONSD was calculated as the average of right and left ONSD measurements at each time point. The model included group, time, and the group × time interaction as fixed effects, with repeated measurements nested within patients. Time was treated as a categorical factor. The primary model term of interest was the group × time interaction, which tested whether the temporal pattern of ONSD differed between groups. An unstructured covariance matrix was used for the repeated measurements. Estimated marginal means with standard errors and 95% confidence intervals were reported. Bonferroni adjustment was applied for post-hoc pairwise comparisons.

Exploratory repeated-measures analyses were also performed for secondary longitudinal outcomes, including systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, mean arterial pressure, heart rate, SpO₂, EtCO₂, BIS, and mean rSO₂. For each outcome, a linear mixed-effects model was fitted with group, time, and the group × time interaction as fixed effects, with repeated measurements nested within patients. An unstructured covariance matrix was used. These secondary longitudinal analyses were considered exploratory and are reported in the supplementary material. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for the primary analyses, whereas secondary exploratory analyses were interpreted with caution.

Sample size calculation was performed using G*Power software version 3.1.9.7. Because no prior randomized study was available in an endovascular neurosurgical population comparing propofol and propofol–ketamine anesthesia with serial ONSD measurements, the calculation was based on the closest available perioperative ONSD study by Kim et al. [7], which reported a comparable between-group difference and variability in ONSD measurements. Using an approximate between-group difference of 0.3 mm and the corresponding standard deviations reported in that study, the effect size was estimated as 0.93. With a two-sided α of 0.05 and 80% power, a minimum of 20 patients per group, 40 patients in total, was required.

Results

A total of 46 patients were assessed for eligibility. Six patients were excluded before randomization: two due to preprocedural aneurysm rupture, one due to inability to cooperate or consent-related issues, and three due to changes in the planned procedure or treatment strategy. The remaining 40 patients were randomized to the propofol group (n = 20) or the propofol–ketamine group (n = 20). All randomized patients received the allocated intervention, completed follow-up measurements, and were included in the final analysis. No patients were lost to follow-up or excluded from the analysis after randomization (Fig. 1). Fig. 1CONSORT flow diagram of the study

Baseline demographic and perioperative characteristics were comparable between groups, including age, gender, BMI, ASA status, aneurysm location, anesthesia and procedure duration, fluid administration, recovery time, ICU and hospital length of stay, and complication rates (p > 0.05) (Table 1). Total propofol consumption was significantly lower in Group KP, whereas remifentanil use showed a non-significant trend toward lower values (p = 0.056). Two postoperative complications occurred in the propofol–ketamine group during the intensive care unit stay after completion of the endovascular procedure. One patient developed a postoperative seizure, and one patient experienced a postoperative thromboembolic event. Both complications resolved with appropriate treatment and did not result in permanent neurological sequelae.

Mean ONSD was analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model including group, time, and group × time interaction as fixed effects. The group × time interaction was not statistically significant, indicating that the temporal trajectory of mean ONSD did not differ between the propofol and propofol–ketamine groups [F(4, 38) = 1.686, p = 0.173]. The main effect of group was also not significant [F(1, 38) = 0.885, p = 0.353], whereas the main effect of time was significant [F(4, 38) = 433.354, p < 0.001]. Estimated marginal means showed no significant between-group differences at any time point after Bonferroni adjustment (Table 2). Although mean ONSD varied significantly over time, the pattern of change was similar between the two groups, supporting comparable perioperative ONSD profiles between anesthetic regimens.

For secondary longitudinal outcomes, exploratory linear mixed-effects models were performed for hemodynamic, oxygenation, and anesthetic depth parameters. The detailed results are presented in Supplementary Table S1. Significant group × time interactions were observed for DBP (F(4,38) = 3.914, p = 0.009), MAP (F(4,38) = 3.673, p = 0.013), EtCO₂ (F(4,38) = 3.742, p = 0.012), BIS (F(4,38) = 3.628, p = 0.013), and mean rSO₂ (F(4,38) = 4.666, p = 0.004). The group × time interaction did not reach statistical significance for SBP (F(4,38) = 2.545, p = 0.055), HR (F(4,38) = 1.823, p = 0.145), or SpO₂ (F(4,38) = 0.552, p = 0.699). Bonferroni-adjusted estimated marginal mean comparisons indicated that DBP and MAP were higher in the propofol–ketamine group at several intraoperative and early postoperative time points, whereas HR showed no significant between-group differences at any time point. These exploratory findings suggest differential temporal patterns for selected hemodynamic, ventilation-related, anesthetic depth, and cerebral oxygenation parameters; however, they should be interpreted as secondary and hypothesis-generating.

Hemodynamic parameters were similar at baseline. During intraoperative and early postoperative periods (T2–T5), systolic, diastolic, and mean arterial pressures were significantly higher in Group KP compared with Group P (p < 0.05), whereas heart rate remained comparable at all time points (p > 0.05) (Table 3).

SpO₂ did not differ between groups at T1, T2, or T4, but was significantly higher in Group KP at T3 and T5 (p < 0.05). EtCO₂ values did not differ significantly between groups at any measured time point (p > 0.05). BIS values were similar between groups at T1–T3, but were significantly higher in Group KP at T4 (p = 0.005). For cerebral oxygenation, right-sided rSO₂ was significantly higher in Group KP at T5, and left-sided rSO₂ was higher at T4 (both p < 0.05), with no significant differences at the other time points. No patient in either group required dopamine or developed new postoperative neurological deficits; postoperative GCS was 15 in all cases.

Discussion

In this prospective randomized study, the effects of propofol versus propofol–ketamine anesthesia on ICP dynamics, hemodynamics, and cerebral oxygenation were evaluated in patients undergoing endovascular treatment of unruptured intracranial aneurysms. The main findings are that both anesthetic regimens produced comparable ONSD profiles over time, while the propofol–ketamine combination provided improved hemodynamic stability and reduced propofol consumption.

ONSD is a well-established noninvasive surrogate marker of ICP, based on the anatomical continuity between the intracranial and perioptic subarachnoid spaces, and has been shown in systematic reviews and meta-analyses to correlate with invasively measured ICP with high sensitivity and specificity [8–10]. The observed ONSD changes were small, and no significant between-group differences were detected throughout the study. Although previous studies have suggested that ONSD changes of approximately 0.3–0.4 mm may be associated with only minimal ICP variation, this threshold was not prespecified in our study and was not validated against invasively measured ICP. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously.

The propofol–ketamine regimen was associated with significantly higher systolic, diastolic, and mean arterial pressures from induction to early recovery, while heart rate remained comparable between groups. This hemodynamic pattern reflects the opposing cardiovascular actions of the two agents, dose-dependent hypotension with propofol versus sympathomimetic effects with ketamine, and suggests a more balanced profile that may help preserve cerebral perfusion pressure during neurointerventional procedures [11, 12]. The significant reduction in total propofol requirements in the combination group further indicates a propofol-sparing effect that could limit dose-related adverse effects without compromising anesthetic depth.

Higher rSO₂ values at selected time points in the propofol–ketamine group may indicate improved cerebral oxygen delivery, likely mediated by better systemic hemodynamics, although these differences were modest and their clinical relevance remains uncertain [13]. These observations are in line with reports that ketamine–propofol combinations can support blood pressure and cerebral oxygenation in neurosurgical patients without causing deleterious increases in ICP when used with controlled ventilation and GABAergic agents.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. The sample size was calculated for the primary ONSD outcome and was not powered to evaluate safety outcomes or rare adverse events. In addition, the sample size calculation was based on the most relevant available perioperative ONSD study rather than on a directly comparable endovascular neurosurgical population, because no prior randomized study with a similar design and primary endpoint was available. Second, ICP was not monitored invasively; instead, ONSD was used as a noninvasive surrogate marker. Therefore, ONSD may not fully reflect absolute ICP values or capture very transient ICP changes. Third, although all ONSD measurements were performed by a trained and certified single operator using a standardized protocol, formal interobserver reliability could not be assessed. Moreover, because independent repeat measurements for reliability analysis were not prospectively recorded, intraobserver variability could not be quantified using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). This should be considered when interpreting the ONSD findings. Fourth, only intraoperative and early postoperative periods were evaluated, and long-term neurological outcomes were not assessed.

Finally, although the study was prospectively conducted following institutional ethics approval, with predefined study objectives, outcomes, and a statistical analysis plan established before patient enrollment, the trial was registered retrospectively. This should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Conclusion

In this small randomized cohort of patients undergoing elective endovascular treatment of unruptured intracranial aneurysms, propofol and propofol–ketamine anesthesia showed comparable ONSD trajectories over time. The propofol–ketamine regimen was associated with reduced propofol consumption and more stable arterial pressure profiles. However, this study was not powered to evaluate safety outcomes or rare adverse events. Therefore, larger neurosurgical trials are required before firm conclusions regarding safety and clinical feasibility can be drawn.
