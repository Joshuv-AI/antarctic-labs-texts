Cost-Effectiveness of the Smaller-Incision New Generation Implantable Miniature Telescope for Late-Stage Age-Related Macular Degeneration
Authors: Michele Basile, Ilaria Valentini, Elena Maria Calosci, Ilaria Sciomenta, Eugenio Di Brino, Filippo Rumi, Francesco Barca, Lorenzo De Angelis, Alfonso Savastano, Giuseppe Francione, Paola Sasso, Damiano Toro Mario, Rosa Giglio, Stanislao Rizzo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess the cost-effectiveness of the smaller-incision new-generation implantable miniature telescope (SING IMT) versus conventional standard care (CSC) from the Italian National Health Service (NHS) and societal perspectives.DesignModel-based cost–utility analysis.SubjectsA simulated cohort of patients with bilateral late-/end-stage age-related macular degeneration (AMD), based on a prospective multicenter study population (mean age, 76 years) eligible for SING IMT, compared with a clinically comparable cohort receiving CSC.MethodsA cohort Markov model with quarterly cycles and a lifetime horizon evaluated monocular SING IMT implantation, structured 6-month postoperative low-vision rehabilitation, and scheduled ophthalmologic follow-up. Costs included device acquisition, surgery, rehabilitation, follow-up, wet AMD treatment and monitoring, visual impairment and blindness, and adverse-event management. Costs and outcomes were discounted at 3% annually. Deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses assessed uncertainty.Main Outcome MeasuresIncremental costs, life years (LYs), quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), incremental cost–utility ratio, and net monetary benefit (NMB) at a €40,000/QALY willingness-to-pay threshold.ResultsIn the deterministic base case, SING IMT provided 1.59 additional LYs and 1.27 additional QALYs versus CSC. From the NHS perspective, its incremental cost was €21,760.60, yielding incremental cost-effectiveness ratios of €13,712.33 per LY and €17,137.15 per QALY gained; incremental NMB was €29,031.05. Including societal blindness-related costs, SING IMT reduced total per-patient costs (€35,446.33 vs €74,035.45; difference, −€38,589.12), primarily through lower lifetime blindness costs. Thus, from the combined NHS and societal perspective, SING IMT was dominant, with an incremental NMB of €89,380.77. Results were robust in sensitivity analyses; baseline age was the main source of variability, whereas surgical time, visit frequency, and unit costs had marginal effects.ConclusionsFor appropriately selected Italian patients with late-stage AMD, SING IMT is expected to be cost-effective versus CSC, providing clinically meaningful gains in quality-adjusted survival. It is cost-effective but not cost-saving from the NHS perspective alone and becomes dominant when societal costs are included, owing mainly to avoided blindness-related expenditure. Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a degenerative disease of the macula and one of the leading causes of central vision loss and legal blindness in high-income countries. Its burden is expected to increase substantially as a consequence of population ageing, with global cases projected to rise from approximately 196 million cases in 2020 to 288 million by 2040.1, 2, 3 Although improvements in prevention, diagnosis, and treatment have contributed to a slight decline in age-standardized rates, the absolute number of affected individuals continues to grow.4, 5, 6 In Italy, AMD affects approximately 1 million people, 200 000 to 300 000 patients presenting with advanced forms, and around 63 000 new cases are reported each year.7

Advanced AMD is associated with severe impairment of central vision, loss of functional independence, reduced quality of life, and substantial health care and social costs.8, 9, 10

The implantable miniature telescope (IMT) was developed to address this unmet need. The device magnifies the central image and projects it onto undamaged retinal areas, allowing improved near and distance vision. However, IMT implantation can reduce the field of view in the treated eye, making the complementary use of the fellow eye essential for peripheral vision, and requires a specialized postoperative rehabilitation program.11 The original IMT received US Food and Drug Administration approval in 2010 and demonstrated clinically meaningful visual and quality-of-life benefits in prospective multicenter studies.12 In particular, long-term follow-up data showed that approximately 90% of implanted eyes achieved gains of ≥2 lines on the ETDRS chart, with benefits sustained for up to 5 years.13,14

Smaller-incision new generation IMT (SING IMT) represents a second-generation evolution of this technology. It received Conformité Européenne marking for use in the European Union in 2020 and is currently marketed as a legacy device under Article 120(3) of Regulation (EU) 2017/745.15 It is designed to provide up to 2.7× magnification of the central visual field and a simplified implantation procedure thanks to a preloaded insertion system that requires smaller incisions. It is indicated for monocular implantation in patients aged at least 55 years, with stable visual acuity (VA) between 20/160 and 20/800, affected by bilateral end-stage AMD.16

Against this background, the present study aims to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of SING IMT compared with standard cataract surgery as the conventional standard of care (CSC) in patients with bilateral end-stage AMD from the perspective of the Italian National Health Service (NHS).

Methods

This study was reported by the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards 2022.17

Model Structure

The cost-effectiveness model was created in Microsoft Excel. A Markov model was adopted as the optimal structure to capture costs and health outcomes in patients with end-stage AMD treated with SING IMT. The model allows the estimation, for a simulated cohort of 1000 individuals, of aggregate costs and health outcomes and their comparison with those of a clinically comparable cohort managed a CSC. In the model, the CSC reflects the current management of patients with end-stage AMD for whom disease-modifying treatments are no longer effective. This pathway primarily consists of regular ophthalmological follow up, cataract surgery when clinically indicated, and low-vision rehabilitation services. Patients managed under CSC may receive optical and electronic low-vision aids, such as magnifying lenses, prismatic glasses, telescopic devices, and closed-circuit television systems, aimed at maximizing residual visual function, despite their well-recognized limitations in terms of field of view, usability, and patient adherence. No intraocular vision-enhancing implants are included in the CSC pathway. Consequently, the CSC represents a nonrestorative strategy focused on functional compensation and maintenance of autonomy, rather than on visual rehabilitation through surgical or implantable solutions.

Each patient enters the model in one of the health states, advanced geographic atrophy (GA) or advanced wet AMD, in both arms (SING IMT and CSC). In the CSC arm, a share of patients is eligible for cataract surgery, while the remainder follows the natural progression of AMD. Among those undergoing cataract surgery, the model distinguishes between a successful operation, resulting in partial visual recovery or stabilization, and an unsuccessful operation, in which the expected visual benefit is not achieved due to surgical complications or the severity of preexisting retinal damage; in both cases, further progression to loss of vision remains possible. Patients not eligible for surgery follow the natural course of AMD without surgical intervention. In the SING IMT arm, the telescope is implanted in only 1 eye; patients may develop adverse events (AEs), including device explantation or loss of VA, that may worsen outcomes by reducing quality of life and increasing costs.18

At any state, there is a persistent probability of death based on age-specific background mortality rates, which are incorporated into the model using Italian National Institute of Statistics estimates.19 The model runs on quarterly cycles, with transitions between states reflecting disease progression, treatment response, and the potential onset of AEs. Perspective and Discount Rate

The analysis was performed from 2 perspectives: (1) the Italian NHS payer perspective, capturing direct health care costs; and (2) the societal perspective, which additionally includes productivity losses and disability-related social transfer payments. In line with Italian health economics guidance, a 3% discount rate was applied to costs and outcomes and was used in the base–case analysis.20 The cost year is 2025.

Intervention and Comparators

The intervention evaluated in this analysis is the SING IMT, an implantable miniature telescope that provides up to 2.7× magnification of the central visual field and features a preloaded delivery system designed to simplify implantation and enable the use of smaller corneal incisions.16

The comparator was the CSC in patients with advanced AMD undergoing lens extraction without implantation of a vision-enhancing device.

Time Horizon

Because AMD has a substantial and long-lasting impact on quality of life, a lifetime time horizon was adopted to fully capture both the costs and the effectiveness of SING IMT over the entire course of the disease.

The model primarily adopts the characteristics of the cohort described in the IMT-002 study.18 The baseline mean age is 76 years (95% confidence interval [CI]: 75–77),21 with the male proportion set at 48.8% based on Italian demographic sources.24

Baseline visual status aligns with advanced-stage AMD: median ETDRS score of 25 letters (95% CI: 18.75–31.2521), with baseline GA prevalence of 79.2% (95% CI: 59.4–99.018). Operational thresholds for visual health states follow reference standards: legal blindness set at 17 and moderate low-vision threshold set at 35 ETDRS letters (visual standards), with additional International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-9/10 references for blindness definitions. Specifically, visual impairment categories were defined as: near-normal vision (≥54 ETDRS letters); moderate low vision (35–53 letters); severe low vision (18–34 letters); and blindness (≤17 letters), consistent with ICD-10 and International Council of Ophthalmology Visual Standards.

To reflect the impact of visual disability on survival, the model incorporates excess mortality risks associated with vision loss: odds ratio 2.34 for blindness, hazard ratios 1.13 for moderate low vision and 1.28 for severe low vision.22

In the CSC arm, it is assumed that 80% of patients are eligible for cataract surgery, while the annual recurrence rate of choroidal neovascularization is set at 1.1%,23 influencing progression toward worse vision states. Finally, for the societal perspective, costs associated with different vision states were estimated by applying cost coefficients expressed as a proportion of the cost of blindness. These coefficients were used to scale annual costs according to the severity of visual impairment. The coefficients were derived from a survey of Italian clinical experts, which indicated that 31.0% of patients present with moderate low vision and 19.0% with severe low vision.

Clinical Data Inputs

The clinical effectiveness of SING IMT was modeled using data from Boyer et al 2015, a prospective nonrandomized study reporting 60-month outcomes for the first-generation IMT device,13 which documents the improvement in VA over time in patients with advanced AMD, with an additional assumption extending the effect to 7 years (Table 2). The efficacy endpoint is expressed as the change in VA, measured in ETDRS letters, for each year following implantation; the duration of each effect is set at 12 months. Annual gains decrease progressively (17.5 → 16 → 14 → 12 letters), with the assumption of a plateau at 12 letters from the fourth to the seventh year.

Utilities

The utility values used in the model are derived from the literature (Table 4),26 quantifying vision-related quality of life across VA categories (convertible to modeled vision states). Utilities ranged from 0.89 (near-normal vision) to 0.40 (vision loss). These estimates were obtained from an older US-based study and were driven primarily by VA; consequently, other dimensions of health-related quality of life were not independently captured. A temporary disutility is applied for AEs during the cycle in which the event occurs (or for the relevant duration). Vision states in the Markov model were mapped to these utility categories.

Resource Use and Costs

Health care resource utilization and cost inputs were estimated using an activity-based costing approach, in line with published methodological recommendations.27, 28, 29 This approach was adopted to identify the main healthcare activities associated with the management of patients with late-stage AMD and to assign costs according to the volume and frequency of resources used along the care pathway. To inform resource-use assumptions, a structured expert elicitation survey was developed based on the available literature and current clinical practice pathways for patients with advanced AMD and visual impairment. The questionnaire was designed to capture the main cost drivers across model health states and clinical events, including ophthalmologic follow-up visits, orthoptic assessments, visual rehabilitation sessions, low-vision aid assessment and fitting, psychological or adaptation support, assistive device training, and specialist consultations. The survey was administered to a panel of 7 clinical experts, all with documented experience in the management of patients with advanced AMD and severe visual impairment. Experts were asked to estimate the type and frequency of healthcare resources used according to visual impairment severity, namely moderate low vision, severe low vision, and blindness, as well as resource use associated with specific clinical events, including AEs, disease progression, and transition to blindness. Detailed resource-use estimates and cost calculations are reported in Tables S1–S4 (available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org).

Expert-derived resource-use estimates were subsequently combined with Italian national reimbursement tariffs, obtained from the national outpatient tariff schedule (Nomenclatore Tariffario, Ministerial Decree 2017)30 and, where applicable, regional tariffs, to calculate annual management costs for each model health state and event-related costs. Expert opinion was therefore used to operationalize resource-use assumptions where direct empirical evidence was unavailable or insufficient, while unit costs were derived from official Italian reimbursement sources, consistently with the Italian NHS perspective adopted in the analysis. The clinician survey estimated the cost of severe (€ 331.02) and moderate (€ 173.96) visual impairment, and the cost of AEs (€ 524.21).

Outcomes

Key outcomes of the cost-effectiveness analysis were total costs, total life years (LYs), total quality-adjusted life years (QALYs, absolute and incremental), and incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs). The ICERs for each setting were compared with Italian-specific willingness-to-pay (WTP) thresholds of €40,000/QALY.20 In addition, results were expressed in terms of incremental net monetary benefit, calculated by valuing incremental QALYs at the selected WTP threshold and subtracting incremental costs, to provide a complementary measure of economic value.

Sensitivity Analysis

To assess the robustness of the model, sensitivity analyses were conducted, including deterministic sensitivity analysis and probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA). In the 1-way deterministic sensitivity analysis, ±20% variation was applied to all parameters. Then, PSA was performed using a Monte Carlo simulation and 10 000 iterations to explore the impact of simultaneous changes in multiple variables on model uncertainty. The PSA considered the simultaneous variation of all parameters according to their range of uncertainty and appropriate distributions (e.g., gamma for costs, beta for probabilities/utilities) to generate a distribution of incremental costs and QALYs. Results were summarized with cost-effectiveness planes and acceptability curves, reporting the probability of cost-effectiveness across WTP values.

Scenario Analysis

To assess the robustness of the base-case results and address uncertainty in key model assumptions, 3 prespecified scenario analyses were conducted.

•Scenario 1: Reduced time horizon (4 years). The base–case lifetime horizon was truncated to 4 years to evaluate the short-term cost-effectiveness of SING IMT.

Results

Base–Case Results

Table 7 presents the comparison of cost categories for SING IMT versus CSC. The total costs of SING IMT are driven almost entirely by the initial investment for the device and implantation (€20,909.72), which represents approximately 90% of the overall cost. In relation to the probability of the event, additional cost components include the management of AEs (€330.27) and potential explantation (€43.85), while follow-up costs remain limited. Age-related macular degeneration treatment and visit costs account for €191,72 in the SING IMT. Costs related to visual disability are slightly higher in the SING IMT arm within the evaluated time horizon (moderate visual impairment: €941.25; severe visual impairment: €810.15). From the NHS perspective, the total costs per patient associated with SING IMT amount to €23,297.95. When blindness-related societal costs (€12,148.38) are included, the combined NHS and societal cost rises to €35,446.33 per patient. In terms of health outcomes, SING IMT is associated with 8.60 LYs and 4.37 QALYs over the lifetime horizon.

The incremental analysis indicates that SING IMT provides meaningful health gains compared with CSC, with +1.59 LYs and +1.27 QALYs over the model horizon. From the NHS perspective, SING IMT is cost-effective with respect to CSC, delivering better outcomes at higher cost, with an ICER below the reference Italian WTP threshold of €40,000/QALY. Importantly, from the NHS payer perspective alone, SING IMT is cost-effective (i.e., it does not achieve dominance). From the societal perspective, the intervention is cost-saving, driven primarily by annual Italian National Social Security Institute disability payments (€ 13,712.33 per LY and €17,137.15 per QALY) avoided in patients who do not progress to blindness, reflecting the broader economic benefits associated with reduced functional impairment.

At a WTP of €40,000 per QALY, the incremental net monetary benefit is €29,031.05 from the NHS perspective and €89,380.77 from the societal perspective. These positive net monetary benefit values indicate that the monetized value of the additional QALYs gained with SING IMT more than offsets the incremental costs relative to CSC, confirming not only the cost-effectiveness of the intervention but also its strong economic attractiveness for decision-makers.

Sensitivity Analysis

Figure 2 presents the results of the deterministic sensitivity analysis. For all other parameters, the ICER remains negative, meaning that SING IMT continues to dominate the comparator and therefore remains cost-effective.

Scenario Analysis

Three prespecified scenario analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the base–case findings under alternative assumptions regarding time horizon, treatment durability, and mortality benefit. Overall, the results showed that the economic value of SING IMT was sensitive to the reduced time horizon assumption. In scenario 1, which adopted a shorter time horizon, SING IMT was not favorable, as the limited follow-up reduced the opportunity to capture downstream clinical and economic benefits, leading to less advantageous cost-effectiveness results. Conversely, scenario 2 supported the robustness of the base–case conclusions, with SING IMT remaining economically attractive when a waning treatment effect was considered. In scenario 3, where mortality benefits were excluded, SING IMT continued to provide quality-of-life gains and remained cost-effective from the NHS perspective, while becoming dominant when societal costs were included. Full scenario-specific results are reported in Tables S5–S7.

Discussion

This cost–utility analysis indicates that SING IMT is not only cost-effective, but cost-saving compared with CSC for appropriately selected patients with late-stage AMD in Italy. The finding of dominance, lower lifetime costs alongside improved health outcomes, is notable in the context of health economic evaluations of implantable ophthalmic technologies, which often demonstrate favorable cost-effectiveness but rarely net cost savings.

The primary mechanism underlying this dominance is the reallocation of expenditures over the patient’s lifetime. Although SING IMT entails substantial upfront costs related to the device, surgical implantation, and postoperative rehabilitation, these are more than offset by downstream savings resulting from reduced time spent in blindness. In the analysis, blindness-related costs, including long-term social care, are the largest component of the economic burden under CSC. Specifically, the dominant conclusion from the societal perspective was primarily driven by the inclusion of Italian National Social Security Institute disability payments associated with patients in the blindness health state. When societal costs were excluded, the incremental cost-effectiveness ratios were €13,712.33 per LY and €17,137.15 per QALY gained, both remaining below the WTP threshold. This assumption is the principal determinant of cost savings, and the magnitude of the economic benefit is highly sensitive to this parameter. Readers should note that from the NHS payer perspective, SING IMT is cost-effective but does not achieve dominance; the cost-saving conclusion requires inclusion of societal transfer payments. Consequently, even modest delays in progression to blindness translate into substantial economic benefits when evaluated over a lifetime horizon. Across all 3 scenarios, SING IMT consistently demonstrated cost-effectiveness from the NHS payer perspective and dominance from the combined NHS + societal perspective in the scenarios with longer time horizons. The most conservative result was observed at the 4-year horizon (scenario 1), where cost-effectiveness from the NHS perspective was not achieved, though the NHS + societal ICER per QALY approached the WTP threshold. These findings confirm the robustness of the base–case conclusions and highlight the critical role of time horizon and societal cost inclusion in determining the economic value of SING IMT.

These findings are consistent with original IMT evidence on the cost-utility of visual rehabilitation interventions. Previous studies have reported incremental cost–utility ratios for original IMT technologies in the range of approximately USD 16 000–19 000 per QALY gained.12

Importantly, in the Italian context examined here, SING IMT not only meets these international benchmarks but exceeds them by generating net cost savings. This reflects both the high societal costs associated with blindness and the structure of the Italian NHS and social care expenditure, where prolonged disability leads to sustained resource use beyond the healthcare sector. From a health system perspective, these results suggest that investment in effective vision-preserving technologies may yield returns not only in health outcomes but also in reduced long-term public spending.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these results. First, some resource-use inputs, particularly follow-up schedules and AE management pathways specific to Italian clinical practice, were derived from an expert clinician survey. Although expert elicitation is a common and often necessary approach for novel technologies with limited clinical setting data, it may introduce bias or variability. Reassuringly, deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses indicated that these parameters exert limited influence on the overall conclusions.

Second, the visual benefit observed in the SING IMT arm is the result of a combined pathway (cataract extraction + telescope implantation + structured postoperative rehabilitation). Available data do not permit isolation of the incremental contribution attributable specifically to the telescope device itself from the effects of cataract removal and intensive rehabilitation. The economic evaluation should therefore be interpreted as an assessment of the SING IMT pathway versus the CSC pathway, rather than as an evaluation of the telescope in isolation.

A further limitation concerns the transferability of clinical effectiveness data from the original IMT to the SING IMT. The efficacy inputs used in the model were derived from Boyer et al (2015),13 a prospective nonrandomized study reporting 60-month outcomes for the first-generation IMT device. No head-to-head randomized controlled trial comparing SING IMT directly with CSC is currently available, and long-term efficacy data specific to the SING IMT remain limited to short-term follow-up studies. The assumption of transferability between the 2 device generations is supported by their shared optical mechanism (2.7× central field magnification), identical patient eligibility criteria, and equivalent postoperative rehabilitation protocols; however, differences in surgical technique, incision size, endothelial cell loss profile, and potential long-term durability cannot be excluded and may introduce uncertainty into the modeled outcomes.

Another limitation regards the remaining uncertainty remains regarding the long-term persistence of VA gains beyond the duration observed in clinical follow-up studies. The model adopted a conservative assumption of a plateau in treatment effect through 7 years. Actual real-world durability could be shorter or longer; however, probabilistic sensitivity analysis demonstrated that the dominance of SING IMT over CSC is robust across plausible ranges of long-term effectiveness.

A further limitation concerns the assumed association between visual impairment and excess mortality. Although poorer vision has been associated with higher mortality, this relationship may not be entirely causal and could partly reflect residual confounding by age, frailty, comorbidities, functional status, and socioeconomic disadvantage. The results should therefore be interpreted with caution, particularly because the mortality assumption contributes to the estimated differences in LYs between treatment strategies. Nevertheless, the scenario analysis excluding any vision-related mortality effect showed that the main conclusions were maintained. In this more conservative setting, SING IMT remained cost-effective from the NHS perspective and dominant from the combined NHS and societal perspective. This suggests that the economic value of SING IMT is not solely dependent on an assumed survival benefit but is also driven by gains in quality-adjusted survival and by the reduction in long-term costs associated with blindness.

Furthermore, the model population is predominantly composed of patients with GA (79.2%), consistent with the Toro et al (2023) cohort from which baseline parameters were derived. Visual rehabilitation potential and functional outcomes may differ between GA and scarred neovascular AMD; accordingly, these findings may be more applicable to end-stage GA populations than to advanced neovascular AMD broadly. Third, the translation of continuous VA improvements into discrete vision states and associated utility values introduces structural uncertainty. Utility estimates were drawn from published literature and may not perfectly reflect Italian societal preferences or the specific experience of patients with end-stage AMD. Future studies incorporating Italian-specific utility weights or direct health-related quality of life measurement (e.g., EuroQol Five-Dimension Questionnaire mapping) in SING IMT recipients would enhance the external validity of these findings.

Finally, blindness-related costs are a major driver of the model results. If local estimates of blindness costs differ substantially, due, for example, to regional variation in access to social services or informal care, the magnitude of cost savings could change. Nonetheless, given that blindness is consistently associated with high economic burden across health care systems, the qualitative conclusion that interventions reducing blindness confer substantial economic value is unlikely to be altered. The present findings are specific to the Italian NHS and social security framework. The magnitude of cost savings may differ substantially in other health care systems where blindness-related social costs are structured differently, and direct generalization of these results to other countries is not recommended.

Conclusions

In a lifetime cost–utility model reflecting Italian clinical and costing contexts, SING IMT is expected to deliver meaningful gains in quality-adjusted survival and reduce total costs (i.e. duration and consequences of blindness) compared with CSC in patients with late-stage AMD. The intervention is cost-effective from the NHS payer perspective and (cost-saving) from the combined societal perspective, assessed individually for eligibility, anatomical suitability, and rehabilitation potential, potentially. These results are specific to the Italian NHS context and cannot be directly extrapolated to other healthcare systems.
