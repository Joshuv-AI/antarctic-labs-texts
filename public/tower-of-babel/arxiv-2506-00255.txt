Bicomplex Polar Weighted Homogeneous Polynomials
Yesenia Bravo, Inácio Rabelo, Agustín Romano-Velázquez

Abstract
. We study the topology of real polynomial maps ℝ 4 ​ n ⟶ ℝ 4 \mathbb{R}^{4n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4} expressed in terms of bicomplex variables and their conjugates, which we refer to as bicomplex mixed polynomials. We introduce the notion of polar weighted homogeneity, a property that generalizes the concept of weighted homogeneity in the complex setting. This leads to the existence of global and spherical Milnor fibrations. Moreover, we include a discussion on bicomplex vector calculus, a bicomplex holomorphic analogue of the Milnor fibration theorem, and a theorem of Join type that describes the homotopy type of the fibers of certain polynomials on separable variables. This extends previous works on mixed polynomials in complex variables and their conjugates.

Introduction

The bicomplex numbers, denoted by
\mathbb{BC}
, form a 4-dimensional commutative real algebra with zero divisors that generalizes the field of complex numbers. This algebra inherits a complex structure and possesses several properties that, in some contexts, make it more advantageous than the quaternions. Its foundational aspects and the theory of bicomplex holomorphic functions were first studied by the Italian school of Segre in a series of papers beginning with
[
30
]
. Other fundamental contributions are found in
[
24
,
28
]
, and in the book
[
21
]
. More recent interest in bicomplex structures and their applications is reflected in works such as
[
8
,
9
,
23
,
25
,
26
]
, and
[
3
]
, to name a few. A modern treatment is provided in the book
[
13
]
.

On the other hand, Milnor’s fibration theorem, introduced in
[
16
]
, is a landmark result in Singularity Theory that describes the topology of complex varieties near their critical points. Analogous statements for real analytic maps hold under stronger conditions. The first examples, given in
[
27
]
, of real analytic maps that satisfy an analogue of Milnor’s fibration theorem were real polynomial maps
\mathbb{C}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
written on complex variables and their conjugates, now known as
mixed polynomials
. These objects generalize their complex counterpart and play a central role in investigating the topology of singularities. For more details, see
[
19
]
and the references therein.

In
[
4
]
, a notion of polar weighted homogeneity, related to a
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-action on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, was introduced for mixed polynomials based on the works
[
29
]
and
[
27
]
. This leads to the existence of a global fibration and a Milnor fibration on the sphere, both defined on the complements of the zero set. Moreover, Join type theorem was proved following
[
17
]
. This result describes the homotopy type of the fiber of polar weighted homogeneous polynomials that decompose as a sum of polynomials on separable variables.

The main goal of this work is to study the topology of real polynomial maps
\mathbb{BC}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{BC}
, regarded as
bicomplex mixed polynomials
. For this, we generalize the results of complex and real analytic maps that we discussed previously. Using the trigonometric representation in bicomplex algebra, we define a
\mathbb{BC}^{*}
-action on
\mathbb{BC}^{n}
, where
\mathbb{BC}^{*}
denotes the non-zero divisors, and introduce a similar notion of polar weighted homogeneity. As a consequence, we derive the existence of fibrations results as before on the complement of the preimage of zero divisors. In the spherical case, we obtain two fibrations, whose base spaces are an open connected subset of
\mathbb{S}^{3}
and a complex quadric. Furthermore, a Join type theorem follows with minor modifications in the original proof.

We include a discussion on bicomplex vector calculus. While the definitions resemble those in the complex case, but it can be developed independently, as we shall see. In addition, we discuss a Milnor-type fibration theorem for bicomplex holomorphic maps and explain how the classical theory applies to the underlying real structures. The key idea of our work is that the language of bicomplex variables provides a natural and powerful framework for studying real polynomial maps
\mathbb{R}^{4n}\to\mathbb{R}^{4}
. Although real algebras have appeared before in Singularity Theory, their role in the context of fibrations has only recently been considered, as discussed in
[
14
]
.

1.
Bicomplex algebra

We refer the reader to
[
13
]
for more details about the results of this section. The set of bicomplex numbers is defined by

\displaystyle\mathbb{BC}=\{\lambda_{1}+\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\lambda_{2}:\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{C}(\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu)\},

where
\mathbb{C}(\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu)=\{a+\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mub:a,b\in\mathbb{R}\}
denotes the usual complex numbers and
\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu
is a second imaginary unit satisfying
\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\neq\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu
and
\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu=\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu
. From now on, by
\mathbb{C}
we mean the set
\mathbb{C}(\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu)
. A
bicomplex number

Z=\lambda_{1}+\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{BC}
can be identified with the pair
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
where
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
are complex numbers. Thus,
\mathbb{BC}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{2}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{4}\simeq\mathbb{H}
as real vector spaces, where
\mathbb{H}
denotes the quaternions. Therefore,
\mathbb{BC}
is a 4-dimensional commutative real algebra and a basis is given by
\langle 1,\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu,\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu,\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu\rangle
, where
\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu=\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu
. Beyond commutativity, the main difference with quaternions is the existence of zero divisors. Namely, define the elements

\displaystyle\textbf{e}=\frac{1+\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu}{2},\;\;\;\textbf{e}^{\dagger}=\frac{1-\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu}{2}.

The numbers
e
and
\textbf{e}^{\dagger}
have the following properties:

\displaystyle\textbf{e}\cdot\textbf{e}^{\dagger}=\textbf{e}^{\dagger}\cdot\textbf{e}=0;

\displaystyle\textbf{e}^{2}=\textbf{e},\;\;\left(\textbf{e}^{\dagger}\right)^{2}=\textbf{e}^{\dagger};

\displaystyle\textbf{e}+\textbf{e}^{\dagger}=1,\;\;\textbf{e}-\textbf{e}^{\dagger}=\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu.

The set
\langle\textbf{e},\textbf{e}^{\dagger}\rangle
is a complex basis for
\mathbb{BC}
. Thus, every
Z=\lambda_{1}+\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{BC}
can be uniquely written as:

\displaystyle Z=z_{1}\textbf{e}+z_{2}\textbf{e}^{\dagger},

where
z_{1}=\lambda_{1}-\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\lambda_{2}
and
z_{2}=\lambda_{1}+\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\lambda_{2}
. This is called the
idempotent representation
of
Z
. The elementary operations in
\mathbb{BC}
can be easily performed component by component on this basis. It is important to remark that the existence of the elements
e
and
\textbf{e}^{\dagger}
with the above properties is quite special and has no analogue in
\mathbb{C}
. Moreover, the set of invertible elements is described by

\displaystyle\mathbb{BC}^{*}:=\left\{Z\in\mathbb{BC}:Z=z_{1}\textbf{e}+z_{2}\textbf{e}^{\dagger},\;z_{1},z_{2}\neq 0\right\}.

We denote its complement, the set of zero divisors, by
\zd
. It follows that
\zd
is the union of two planes in
\mathbb{BC}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{4}
.

Another difference with the complex numbers is that the bicomplex numbers admit three types of conjugations. Let
Z=\lambda_{1}+\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\lambda_{2}
and write its idempotent representation as
z_{1}\textbf{e}+z_{2}\textbf{e}^{\dagger}
. We denote by:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\widetilde{Z}

\displaystyle=\bar{\lambda}_{1}-\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\bar{\lambda}_{2}=(\bar{\lambda}_{1}+\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\bar{\lambda}_{2})\textbf{e}+(\bar{\lambda}_{1}-\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\bar{\lambda}_{2})\textbf{e}^{\dagger}.

(1.2)

\displaystyle\widehat{Z}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}-\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\lambda_{2}=(\lambda_{1}+\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\lambda_{2})\textbf{e}+(\lambda_{1}-\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\lambda_{2})\textbf{e}^{\dagger}.

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$Z$}}}}Z

\displaystyle=\bar{\lambda}_{1}+\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\bar{\lambda}_{2}=(\bar{\lambda}_{1}-\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\bar{\lambda}_{2})\textbf{e}+(\bar{\lambda}_{1}+\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\bar{\lambda}_{2})\textbf{e}^{\dagger}.

In addition, these conjugations satisfy the following relations:

(1.4)

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=\frac{Z+\widehat{Z}}{2},\;\;\;\lambda_{2}=\frac{Z-\widehat{Z}}{2\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu},

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}_{1}=\frac{\widetilde{Z}+\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$Z$}}}}Z}{2},\;\;\;\bar{\lambda}_{2}=\frac{\widetilde{Z}+\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$Z$}}}}Z}{2\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu}.

It should be noted that expressing an arbitrary vector in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
using bicomplex variables requires all three conjugations.

1.1.
Trigonometric form

The existence of the three conjugations produces that any non-zero-divisor of
\mathbb{BC}
has two polar (or trigonometric) representation. Moreover, we have the usual Euclidean norm and a complex-valued norm:

\displaystyle\|Z\|

\displaystyle=\|\lambda_{1}+\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\lambda_{2}\|:=\sqrt{|\lambda_{1}|^{2}+|\lambda_{2}|^{2}},

\displaystyle\|Z\|_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}

\displaystyle=\|\lambda_{1}+\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\lambda_{2}\|_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}:=\sqrt{\lambda_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{2}^{2}},

with the following convention: if
\lambda_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{2}^{2}
is a non-negative real number, the square root is the usual, otherwise, we take the solution in which the imaginary part is positive. The set of complex numbers with positive imaginary parts is referred in the literature by
upper half-plane
and we shall denote its union with the positive horizontal axis by
\mathcal{H}^{+}
.A straightforward computation shows that
Z\in\mathbb{BC}^{*}
if and only if
\|Z\|_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
. This motivates us to consider the following set, called the
complex unit circle
:

\displaystyle\cc=\{(z,w)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:z^{2}+w^{2}=1\}.

If
Z=\lambda_{1}+\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{BC}^{*}
, then
\|Z\|_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}\neq 0
and we may write

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\|Z\|_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\lambda_{2}}{\|Z\|_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}}\right)^{2}=1.

We set

\displaystyle\cos\Theta=\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\|Z\|_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}},\;\;\;\sin\Theta=\frac{\lambda_{2}}{\|Z\|_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}},

for some complex angle
\Theta
. By the periodicity of the complex sine and cosine, there are infinitely many solutions for the system above, and we shall call any of them the
complex argument
of
Z
. Restricting
\operatorname{Re}(\Theta)\in[0,2\pi)
, this solution is called the
principal value
and denoted by
\arg_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}Z
. Thus, we may write

\displaystyle Z=\|Z\|_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\arg_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}Z},\quad e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\arg_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}Z}\in\cc.

This yields a polar coordinate system
(\omega,\Theta)
for
\mathbb{BC}^{*}
given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{+}\times\cc

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{BC}^{*}

\displaystyle(\omega,\Theta)

\displaystyle\mapsto\omega\left(\cos{\Theta}+\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\sin{\Theta}\right)

Associated with this representation, we have the projection
\pi_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}:\mathbb{BC}^{*}\longrightarrow\cc
given by

(1.5)

\displaystyle\pi_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}(Z)=\frac{Z}{\|Z\|_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}}.

This map admits an interesting geometric interpretation. Let us denote
\mathbb{S}^{3}_{0}:=\mathbb{S}^{3}\setminus\zd
the subset of the usual Euclidean sphere consisting of non-zero divisors. Notice that

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{3}_{0}=\{(\omega,\Theta)\in\mathbb{BC}^{*}:\|\omega\Theta\|=1\}.

Proposition 1.1
.

The projection
\pi_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}:\mathbb{S}^{3}_{0}\longrightarrow\cc
is equivalent to a restriction of the Hopf fibration of
\mathbb{S}^{3}
.

Proof.

The fibres
\pi_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}^{-1}(\Theta_{0})
are copies of
\mathbb{S}^{1}\cap\mathcal{H}^{+}
and are described by

\displaystyle\pi_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}^{-1}(\Theta_{0})=\left\{(\omega,\Theta_{0}):\|\omega\|=\|e^{-\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\Theta_{0}}\|\right\}.

Therefore, every invertible
Z\in\mathbb{S}^{3}_{0}
belongs to a fibre
\pi_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}^{-1}(\Theta_{0})
. Hence,

\mathbb{S}^{3}_{0}=\bigsqcup_{\Theta\in\cc}\pi_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}^{-1}(\Theta_{0}).

Now, the proof follows from the main theorem in
[
10
]
.
∎

Remark 1.2
.

A routine exercise shows that
\cc
is diffeomorphic to
T\mathbb{S}^{1}
, the tangent bundle of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, which is equivalent to the 2-sphere minus two points. Then the previous assertion says that the projection
\pi_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}
is the result of removing two circles corresponding to the intersection
\mathbb{S}^{3}\cap\zd
and that are mapped to two points in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

1.2.
Hyperbolic trigonometric form

Let
Z=z_{1}\textbf{e}+z_{2}\textbf{e}^{\dagger}\in\mathbb{BC}^{*}
where
z_{1}=r_{1}e^{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta_{1}}
and
z_{2}=r_{2}e^{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta_{2}}
. The
hyperbolic polar form
of
Z
is given by

\displaystyle Z=\left(r_{1}\textbf{e}+r_{2}\textbf{e}^{\dagger}\right)\left(e^{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta_{1}}\textbf{e}+e^{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta_{2}}\textbf{e}^{\dagger}\right).

The set

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}^{+}=\{\nu\textbf{e}+\mu\textbf{e}^{{\dagger}}:\nu,\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\}.

is called the
positive hyperbolic numbers
.
Let
Z=z_{1}\textbf{e}+z_{2}\textbf{e}^{\dagger}
and define

\|Z\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}=\|z_{1}\|\textbf{e}+\|z_{2}\|\textbf{e}^{\dagger}.

The set
\mathbb{D}^{+}
admits a partial order as follows: If
W=w_{1}\textbf{e}+w_{2}\textbf{e}^{\dagger}
, we say that
\|Z\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}\preceq\|W\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}
if and only if
\|z_{1}\|\leq\|w_{1}\|
and
\|z_{2}\|\leq\|w_{2}\|
. The following properties are immediate:

(1)

\|Z\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}=0
if and only if
Z=0
.

(2)

\|Z\cdot W\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}=\|Z\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}\cdot\|W\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}
.

(3)

\|Z+W\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}\preceq\|Z\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}+\|W\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}
.

Hence, each
Z\in\mathbb{BC}^{*}
can be uniquely written as
Z=\|Z\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}\lambda
, where
\|Z\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}\in\mathbb{D}^{+}
and
\lambda\in\ci\textbf{e}\times\ci\textbf{e}^{\dagger}
. We shall denote
\tr=\mathbb{S}^{1}\textbf{e}+\mathbb{S}^{1}\textbf{e}^{\dagger}
. As for complex numbers, there are infinitely many solutions to the equation
\theta=\theta_{1}\textbf{e}+\theta_{2}\textbf{e}^{\dagger}\in\tr
. Then we restrict,
\{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\}\in[0,2\pi)
and such a solution is called the
hyperbolic argument
and denoted by
\arg_{\mathbb{D}}Z
. Associated with this representation, we have the projection
\pi_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}:\mathbb{BC}^{*}\longrightarrow\tr
given by

(1.6)

\displaystyle\pi_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}(Z)=\frac{z_{1}}{\|z_{1}\|}\textbf{e}+\frac{z_{2}}{\|z_{2}\|}\textbf{e}^{\dagger},

which is the normalization of each coordinate of the idempotent representation of
Z
.

1.3.
Linear algebra

As a commutative ring, the theory of bicomplex modules can be developed as usual, and the idempotent representation of these numbers allows us to give a clear description of the
\mathbb{BC}
-linear transformations. First, let
A=(Z_{i,j})_{m\times n}
be a bicomplex matrix and decompose
Z_{i,j}=z^{1}_{i,j}\textbf{e}+z^{2}_{i,j}\textbf{e}^{{\dagger}}
for each pair
(i,j)
, where
z^{1}_{i,j},z^{2}_{i,j}\in\mathbb{C}
. It follows that
A
has a unique decomposition
A=A^{1}\textbf{e}+A^{2}\textbf{e}^{{\dagger}}
, where
A^{1}=(z^{1}_{i,j})_{m\times n}
and
A^{2}=(z^{2}_{i,j})_{m\times n}
are complex matrices. Let us denote the matrices of order
m\times n
with coefficients in an algebra
\mathbb{F}
by
\mn(m\times n,\mathbb{F})
. Then the following map defines an embedding
\mn(m\times n,\mathbb{BC})\hookrightarrow\mn(2m\times 2n,\mathbb{C})
:

(1.7)

\displaystyle A=A^{1}\textbf{e}+A^{2}\textbf{e}^{{\dagger}}\longmapsto\begin{pmatrix}A^{1}&0\\
0&A^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

The
\mn(m\times n,\mathbb{BC})
-action on
\mathbb{BC}^{n}
is thus described by a
\mn(2m\times 2n,\mathbb{C})
-action on
\mathbb{BC}^{n}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
A=A^{1}\textbf{e}+A_{2}\textbf{e}^{\dagger}\in\mn(m\times n,\mathbb{BC})
be a bicomplex matrix.

(1)

The bicomplex rank of
A
, denoted by
\rk(A)
, is the pair
\left(\rk(A^{1}),\rk(A^{2})\right)
, where
\rk(A^{i})
is the usual complex rank of
A^{i}
, for
i=1,2
.

(2)

The determinant of
A
is defined by

\displaystyle\dete(A)=\dete(A^{1})\textbf{e}+\dete(A^{2})\textbf{e}^{{\dagger}}\in\mathbb{BC},

where
\dete(A^{i})
is the complex determinant of
A^{i}
, for
i=1,2
.

Since the sum and product of a bicomplex scalar preserves the idempotent representation, the determinant, as defined above, is multilinear, and
\dete(I_{n})=1
, where
I_{n}
is the identity matrix. By the uniqueness of a function with this property, the function above is the legitimate bicomplex determinant. Moreover, notice that
A\in\mn(n,\mathbb{BC})
is invertible if and only if
A^{1}
and
A^{2}
are also invertible. This implies that
A
is invertible if and only if
\dete(A)\notin\zd
. Therefore, the set

\displaystyle\gl(n,\mathbb{BC})=\{A\in\mn(n,\mathbb{BC}):\dete(A)\notin\zd\},

form a group and the map (
1.7
) defines an embedding
\gl(n,\mathbb{BC})\hookrightarrow\gl(2n,\mathbb{C})
.

2.
Bicomplex vector calculus

According to the previous subsection, we consider the following hyperbolic norm on
\mathbb{BC}^{n}
: Let
Z\in\mathbb{BC}^{n}
and write
Z=z_{1}\textbf{e}+z_{2}\textbf{e}^{\dagger}
, where
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. We define

\displaystyle\|Z\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}:=\|z_{1}\|\textbf{e}+\|z_{2}\|\textbf{e}^{\dagger}\in\mathbb{D}^{+}.

In this section, we introduce the basic theory of bicomplex vector calculus in analogy with the calculus of several variables. The proofs are easy to deduce from these classical results (see, for instance,
[
31
]
and
[
11
]
). In this section,
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}_{1}\textbf{e}+\mathcal{U}_{2}\textbf{e}^{\dagger}\subset\mathbb{BC}^{n}
denotes an open subset, where
\mathcal{U}_{1}
and
\mathcal{U}_{2}
are open subsets of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Definition 2.1
.

A bicomplex map
F:\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{BC}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{BC}^{m}
is bicomplex differentiable at the point
Z\in\mathcal{U}
if there exists a bicomplex linear transformation
L_{Z}:\mathbb{BC}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{BC}^{m}
such that

(2.1)

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}H\to 0\\
\|H\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}\not\in\zd\end{subarray}}\frac{\|F(Z+H)-F(Z)-L_{Z}(H)\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}}{\|H\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}}=0.

The transformation
L_{Z}
is denoted by
DF_{Z}
and it is called the bicomplex derivative of
F
at
Z
. If
F
is differentiable at all points
Z\in\mathcal{U}
, we say that
F
is bicomplex holomorphic.

Notice that
\|H\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}\to 0
if and only if
H\to 0
. Thus, the definition above coincides with
[
13
, Definition 7.2.1]
and
[
22
, Theorem 3.3]
for
n=m=1
. We shall see that the hyperbolic norm allows us to apply the classical arguments of the calculus of several variables.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
F:\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{BC}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{BC}^{m}
be a bicomplex differentiable map at
Z\in\mathcal{U}
. Then, the linear transformation
L_{Z}
is unique.

Proof.

Suppose the existence of a second linear transformation
M_{Z}
with the above properties. One has

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}H\to 0\\
\|H\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}\not\in\zd\end{subarray}}\frac{\|L_{Z}(H)-M_{Z}(H)\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}}{\|H\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}H\to 0\\
\|H\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}\not\in\zd\end{subarray}}\frac{\|L_{Z}(H)-F(Z+H)+F(H)+F(Z+H)-F(H)-M_{Z}(H)\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}}{\|H\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}}

\displaystyle\preceq\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}H\to 0\\
\|H\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}\not\in\zd\end{subarray}}\frac{\|F(Z+H)-F(Z)-L_{Z}(H)\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}}{\|H\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}}+\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}H\to 0\\
\|H\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}\not\in\zd\end{subarray}}\frac{\|F(Z+H)-F(Z)-M_{Z}(H)\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}}{\|H\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}}

\displaystyle=0.

Let
W\in\mathbb{BC}^{n}
and notice that
\|\lambda W\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}\rightarrow 0
if
\lambda\rightarrow 0
. Thus, if
\|W\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}\neq 0
, we have that

\displaystyle 0=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\to 0\\
\|\lambda\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}\notin\zd\end{subarray}}\frac{\|L_{Z}(\lambda W)-M_{Z}(\lambda W)\|}{\|(\lambda W)\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}}=\frac{\|L_{Z}(W)-M_{Z}(W)\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}}{\|W\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}},

which implies
L_{Z}(W)=M_{Z}(W)
for all
W\in\mathbb{BC}^{n}
such that
\|W\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}\neq 0
.
∎

Definition 2.3
.

Let
F:\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{BC}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{BC}
be a bicomplex function. The
i
-th partial derivative of
F
at a point
W=(W_{1},\dots,W_{n})
is the following limit, if it exists,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F}{\partial Z_{i}}(W):=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}H\to 0\\
H\notin\zd\end{subarray}}\frac{F(W_{1},\dots,W_{i-1},W_{i}+H,W_{i+1},\dots,W_{n})-F(W)}{\|H\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}}.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
F=(F^{1},\dots,F^{m}):\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{BC}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{BC}^{m}
be a bicomplex map where each coordinate map is also a bicomplex map.

(1)

The map
F
is bicomplex holomorphic at
W\in\mathcal{U}
if and only if
F^{i}
is bicomplex holomorphic at
W
for every
i=1,\dots,n
.

(2)

If
F
is bicomplex holomorphic, then
DF_{W}=\left(DF^{1}_{W},\dots,DF^{m}_{W}\right)
.

(3)

If
F
is bicomplex holomorphic, then the partial derivatives of
F^{i}
at
W
exist for every
i=1,\dots,n
and

\displaystyle DF_{W}=\left(\frac{\partial F^{i}}{\partial Z_{j}}(W)\right)_{m\times n}

Proof.

For each
i
, the
i
-th entry of the bicomplex vector
F(W+H)-F(W)-L_{W}(H)
is
F^{i}(W+H)-F^{i}(W)-L_{W}^{i}(H)
, where
L^{i}_{W}
is the
i
-th row of
L_{W}
and
H\in\mathbb{BC}^{n}
is such that
H\to 0
and
\|H\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}\notin\zd
. One has

\displaystyle\frac{\|F^{i}(W+H)-F^{i}(W)-L_{W}^{i}(H)\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}}{\|H\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}}\preceq\frac{\|F(W+H)-F(W)-L_{W}(H)\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}}{\|H\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}}\preceq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\|F^{j}(W+H)-F^{j}(W)-L_{W}^{j}(H)\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}}{\|H\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}}.

A straightforward argument shows that these inequalities imply the first and the second assertions. In virtue of that, for the third item, we only need to consider the case
m=1
. In Definition
2.1
we take
H=(0,\dots,0,H_{i},0,\dots,0)
with
H_{i}\to 0
and
H_{i}\notin\zd
. This leads to the existence of the partial derivatives of every
F^{i}
and that
DF^{i}_{W}(E^{j})=\frac{\partial F^{i}}{\partial Z_{j}}
, where
E^{j}=(0,\dots,0,1,0,\dots,0)\in\mathbb{BC}^{n}
is the
j
-th canonical bicomplex vector. But this completely determines
DF^{i}_{W}
and we conclude.
∎

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
F:\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{BC}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{BC}^{m}
be a bicomplex holomorphic map.

(1)

F
is bicomplex holomorphic on each bicomplex variable separately.

(2)

F
has an idempotent representation

\displaystyle F(z_{1},z_{2})=f_{1}(z_{1})\textbf{e}+f_{2}(z_{2})\textbf{e}^{\dagger},

where
f_{i}:\mathcal{U}_{i}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{m}
is complex holomorphic, for
i=1,2
.

(3)

The derivative of
F
has an idempotent representation

\displaystyle DF_{(z_{1},z_{2})}=(Df_{1})_{z_{1}}\textbf{e}+(Df_{2})_{z_{2}}\textbf{e}^{\dagger}.

Proof.

It is enough to prove the statement for
m=1
. The first item follows from Theorem
2.4
and the last two assertions from item 1 and the analogous results for
n=1
in
[
13
, Theorems 7.6.3 and Corollary 7.6.6]
.
∎

Corollary 2.6

(Hartogs’ Theorem for bicomplex maps)
.

If
F:\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{BC}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{BC}
is bicomplex holomorphic on each bicomplex variable, then
F
is bicomplex holomorphic.

Proof.

Let us consider the idempotent representation of the limit in (
2.1
). The hypothesis implies that
f_{1}(z_{1})
and
f_{2}(z_{2})
are complex holomorphic functions on each
z_{ij},z_{ij}
, respectively, where
z_{i}=(z_{i1},\dots,z_{in})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
i=1,2
. By Hartogs’ Theorem for complex variables,
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are complex holomorphic functions on
\mathcal{U}_{1}
and
\mathcal{U}_{2}
, hence
F
is bicomplex holomorphic on
\mathcal{U}
.
∎

For
Z=\lambda_{1}+\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{BC}
, from equations (
1.1
), (
1.2
), and (
1.3
), we obtain the following complex differential operators:

(2.2)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial{Z}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{1}}+\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{2}}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\widehat{Z}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{1}}-\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{2}}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\widetilde{Z}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$\lambda$}}}}\lambda_{1}}-\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$\lambda$}}}}\lambda_{2}}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$Z$}}}}Z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$\lambda$}}}}\lambda_{1}}+\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$\lambda$}}}}\lambda_{2}}\right).

By writting

(2.3)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial Z_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}+\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}+\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{i}}+\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{i}}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\widetilde{Z}_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}+\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}-\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{i}}-\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{i}}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\widehat{Z}_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}-\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}-\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{i}}-\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{i}}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$Z$}}}}Z_{i}}

Now, a bicomplex-valued map

where

and similarly for the derivatives

.

.

3.
Bicomplex holomorphic Milnor fibration

In this section, we prove a Milnor fibration type theorem for bicomplex holomorphic functions. The proof follows from slightly modifying Milnor’s proof in
[
16
]
, which is also detailed in
[
2
]
. Let us fix some notations. A bicomplex holomorphic function
F:(\mathbb{BC}^{n},0)\longrightarrow(\mathbb{BC},0)
has an idempotent representation

\displaystyle F(z_{1},z_{2})=f_{1}(z_{1})\textbf{e}+f_{2}(z_{2})\textbf{e}^{\dagger},

where
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are holomorphic functions on separable variables, according to Corollary
2.5
. Moreover, in a small neighbourhood
\mathcal{V}
of
\mathbb{BC}^{*}
,
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
have isolated critical values in
0
, respectively, by Bertini-Sard theorem. We consider a different norm on
\mathbb{BC}^{n}
as follows. For
Z=z_{1}\textbf{e}+z_{2}\textbf{e}^{\dagger}\in\mathbb{BC}^{n}
we define

\displaystyle\|Z\|_{\mathbb{BC}}^{2}=\|z_{1}\|^{2}+\|z_{2}\|^{2}.

The bicomplex sphere will be

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{BC},\epsilon}^{4n-1}=\{Z\in\mathbb{BC}^{n}:\|Z\|_{\mathbb{BC}}=\epsilon\}.

The coordinate change from the idempotent to the polar representation will be denoted by

\displaystyle\Phi:\mathbb{BC}^{*}\longrightarrow\mathbb{BC}^{*}.

The trigonometric representation of
F(Z)
is
F(Z)=\|F(Z)\|_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\Theta(Z)}
and the intersection
K_{\epsilon}:=F^{-1}(\zd)\cap\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{BC},\epsilon}^{4n-1}
is called the
bicomplex link
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
F:(\mathbb{BC}^{n},0)\longrightarrow(\mathbb{BC},0)
be a bicomplex holomorphic function germ. Then the map

(3.1)

\displaystyle\varphi:=\frac{F(Z)}{\|F(Z)\|_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}}:\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{BC},\epsilon}^{4n-1}\setminus K_{\epsilon}\longrightarrow\cc

is a locally trivial smooth fibration for every sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
.

Remark 3.2
.

One may consider the hyperbolic norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{\bf k}\mkern 1.0mu}
instead of the complex one. This reduces to a product of the Milnor fibrations of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
on the base space
\tr
. We shall see that, from the complex norm, we obtain new and interesting constructions.

Theorem
3.1
will be a consequence of the following three lemmas.

Lemma 3.3
.

The map
\varphi
is a submersion.

Proof.

The map
\varphi
can be seen as the composition
\pi_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}\circ\Phi\circ F(u,v)
, where
F
is taken in the idempotent representation. Thus, it is enough to prove that

\displaystyle F:\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{BC},\epsilon}^{4n-1}\setminus K_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{BC}^{*}

\displaystyle(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle\longmapsto f_{1}(z_{1})\textbf{e}+f_{2}(z_{2})\textbf{e}^{\dagger}

is a submersion. By
[
2
, Corollary 3.11]
the fibres of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are transversal to
\mathbb{S}_{\epsilon/\sqrt{2}}^{2n-1}
for every sufficiently small
\epsilon
. More precisely, for
p=p_{1}\textbf{e}+p_{2}\textbf{e}^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{V}
, one has

\displaystyle T_{p_{1}}f_{1}^{-1}(p_{1})+T_{p_{1}}\mathbb{S}_{\epsilon/\sqrt{2}}^{2n-1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{C}^{n}\textbf{e},

\displaystyle T_{p_{2}}f_{2}^{-1}(p_{2})+T_{p_{2}}\mathbb{S}_{\epsilon/\sqrt{2}}^{2n-1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{C}^{n}\textbf{e}^{\dagger}.

Since

\left(T_{p_{1}}\mathbb{S}_{\epsilon/\sqrt{2}}^{2n-1}\right)\textbf{e}+\left(T_{p_{2}}\mathbb{S}_{\epsilon/\sqrt{2}}^{2n-1}\right)\textbf{e}^{\dagger}\subset T_{p}\left(\mathbb{S}^{4n-1}_{\mathbb{BC},\epsilon}\right),

the statement follows.
∎

From now on, we consider the vector field
\mathbb{V}
in
\cc
given by
\mathbb{V}(e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\Theta})=\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mue^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\Theta}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

There exists a complete vector field
\mathbb{W}
on
\mathbb{S}_{\epsilon}^{4n-1}\setminus K_{\epsilon}
that projects to
\mathbb{V}
by
\varphi
.

Proof.

Milnor proved the existence of a vector field
\mathbb{W}_{i}
on
\mathbb{S}_{\epsilon/\sqrt{2}}^{2n-1}
associated with the spherical fibration of
\Theta_{i}:=f_{i}/\|f_{i}\|
, where
i=1,2
(see
[
2
, Lemma 3.14]
). We shall see that
\mathbb{W}(z_{1},z_{2})=\mathbb{W}_{1}(z_{1})\textbf{e}+\mathbb{W}_{2}(z_{2})\textbf{e}^{\dagger}
is the desired vector field. As before, since
\mathbb{W}_{i}
is complete and tangent to
\mathbb{S}_{\epsilon/\sqrt{2}}^{2n-1}
, it follows that
\mathbb{W}
is complete and tangent to
\mathbb{S}_{\epsilon/\sqrt{2}}^{2n-1}\textbf{e}+\mathbb{S}_{\epsilon/\sqrt{2}}^{2n-1}\textbf{e}^{\dagger}
. Moreover, the integral curve
p_{i}(t)
of
\mathbb{W}_{i}
projects to the path
t\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Note that
p(t)=p_{1}(t)\textbf{e}+p_{2}(t)\textbf{e}^{\dagger}
is the integral curve of
\mathbb{W}
. Lastly, one can see that

\displaystyle\Theta\left(p(t)\right)

\displaystyle=\Phi\circ\left(\Theta_{1}(p_{1}(t)),\Theta_{2}(p_{2}(t)\right)=t

for every small
t>0
.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

The map
\varphi
is locally trivial.

Proof.

On a sufficiently small neighbourhood in
\cc
, we identify
e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\Theta}\simeq\Theta\in\mathbb{C}
. By Lemma
3.4
, there exists a lifting
\mathbb{W}
in
\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{BC},\epsilon}^{4n-1}\setminus K_{\epsilon}
of
\mathbb{V}
by the map
\varphi
that is also complete. Let us denote by
H_{t}(Z)
the integral curve of
\mathbb{W}
passing through
Z
. The following map defines a local trivialization for
\varphi
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}\times\varphi^{-1}(\Theta_{0})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\varphi^{-1}(\mathcal{U})

\displaystyle\left(\operatorname{Re}(\Theta+\Theta_{0}),\operatorname{Im}(\Theta+\Theta_{0}),Z\right)

\displaystyle\longmapsto H_{\operatorname{Re}(\Theta)}\circ H_{\operatorname{Im}(\Theta)}\circ Z,

where
\mathcal{U}=\{\Theta\in\cc:\|\Theta+\Theta_{0}\|<\delta\}
is a small neighbourhood of
\Theta_{0}
.
∎

Observe that, in general, we cannot decompose
\Theta(Z)
as
\Phi\circ(\Theta_{1}\textbf{e}+\Theta_{2}\textbf{e}^{\dagger})
. We discuss now some topological properties of
\varphi
. First, notice that,

\displaystyle K_{\epsilon}=\left\{Z\in\mathbb{BC}^{n}:\|Z\|_{\mathbb{BC}}=\epsilon,\;F(Z)\notin\mathbb{BC}^{*}\right\}.

Recall that the map
F
has the following idempotent representation

\displaystyle F(z_{1},z_{2})=f_{1}(z_{1})\textbf{e}+f_{2}(z_{2})\textbf{e}^{\dagger},

Thus,
K_{\epsilon}
can be identified with the link of the holomorphic function
f(z_{1},z_{2}):\mathbb{C}^{2n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
given by
f(z_{1},z_{2})=f_{1}(z_{1})f_{2}(z_{2})
. This gives the following.

Corollary 3.6

(
[
16
]
, Theorem 5.2)
.

The bicomplex link
K_{\epsilon}
is the link of the holomorphic function
f(z_{1},z_{2})
and thus it is
(2n-2)
-connected.

In addition, a typical fiber is described by

\displaystyle\varphi^{-1}(1)=\left\{Z\in\mathbb{BC}^{n}:\|Z\|_{\mathbb{BC}}=\epsilon,\;F(Z)\in\mathbb{BC}^{*},F(Z)=\|Z\|_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}\right\}.

Consider the following holomorphic functions on
\mathcal{U}
:

\displaystyle h_{1}(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=f_{1}(z_{1})+f_{2}(z_{2}),

\displaystyle h_{2}(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=f_{1}(z_{1})-f_{2}(z_{2}).

Then
h_{2}(z_{1},z_{2})\equiv 0
and
h_{1}(z_{1},z_{2})\neq 0
for all points in
\varphi^{-1}(1)
. Equivalently,
\varphi^{-1}(1)=K_{2,\epsilon}\setminus K_{1,\epsilon}
, where
K_{i,\epsilon}
is the usual link of the holomorphic function
h_{i}(z_{1},z_{2})
, for
i=1,2
.

Corollary 3.7
.

Assume further that the holomorphic functions
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
have isolated singularity at the origin and have no common irreducible components. Then the fundamental group of
\varphi^{-1}(1)
is isomorphic with
\mathbb{Z}
and the other homotopy groups are the same as those of a bouquet of spheres of dimension
2n-2
.

Proof.

By the works of Lê, see
[
7
]
for instance, the maps

\displaystyle\frac{h_{1}}{\|h_{1}\|}:

\displaystyle K_{2,\epsilon}\setminus K_{1,\epsilon}\longrightarrow\ci

\displaystyle h_{1}:

\displaystyle h_{2}^{-1}(0)\cap h_{1}^{-1}(\partial\mathbb{D}_{\delta})\cap\mathring{\mathbb{B}}_{\epsilon}^{4n}\longrightarrow\partial\mathbb{D}_{\delta}

are isomorphic locally trivial fibrations for
\epsilon,\delta>0
sufficiently small, where
\mathbb{D}_{\delta}
is the closed disk of radius
\delta>0
in
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathring{\mathbb{B}}_{\epsilon}^{4n}
is the open ball of radius
\epsilon>0
in
\mathbb{BC}^{n}
, both centered at origin. Furthermore, it is well-known that the homotopy type of these fibers is that of a bouquet of spheres of dimension
2n-2
, by
[
12
, §5.7 and 5.8]
. The conclusion follows by applying the exact sequence relating the homotopy groups of the total, base, and fiber spaces of a fibration.
∎

Theorem 3.8

(Tube fibrations)
.

(1)

There exists
\epsilon>0
and
\delta=\delta(\epsilon)>0
such that the restriction

(3.2)

\displaystyle F:\mathcal{N}(\epsilon,\delta)\longrightarrow\mathbb{BC}^{*}\cap\mathbb{B}^{4}_{\delta}

is a locally trivial fibration, where
\mathcal{N}(\epsilon,\delta)=\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}^{4n}\cap F^{-1}(\mathbb{BC}^{*}\cap\mathbb{B}^{4}_{\delta})
and
\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}^{4n},\mathbb{B}^{4}_{\delta}
are closed balls centered at the origin in
\mathbb{BC}^{n},\mathbb{BC}
with radius
\epsilon,\delta>0
, respectively.

(2)

There exists
\epsilon>0
and
\delta=\delta(\epsilon)>0
such that the restriction

(3.3)

F:\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}(\epsilon,\delta)\longrightarrow\cc

is a locally trivial fibration, where
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}(\epsilon,\delta)=\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}^{4n}\cap F^{-1}(\cc)
and

is the closed ball centered at the origin in

with radius

Proof.

The first map is a submersion by the

-property of the holomorphic functions

and

as we argued in Lemma

. The same is applied to the second map, where we consider the decomposition

∎

Remark 3.9
.

Notice that the total spaces of (

) and (

) have distinct dimensions. Therefore, the tube and the spherical bicomplex fibrations cannot be equivalent, which contrasts with the complex case.

4.
Mixed polynomials

In this section, we introduce the notion of mixed polynomials in bicomplex variables and the notion of polar weighted homogeneity. We present basic properties and examples motivated by the case of mixed polynomials on complex variables studied in
[
4
]
and
[
18
]
.

A
bicomplex mixed monomial in a variable

Z_{i}\in\mathbb{BC}
is bicomplex valued-function
\mathbb{BC}\longrightarrow\mathbb{BC}
of the form

\displaystyle Z_{i}(a_{i},b_{i},c_{i},d_{i}):=Z_{i}^{a_{i}}\widetilde{Z}_{i}^{b_{i}}\widehat{Z}_{i}^{c_{i}}\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$Z$}}}}Z_{i}^{d_{i}},

where
a_{i},b_{i},c_{i},d_{i}
are non-negative integers. Moreover, a
bicomplex mixed monomial
is a bicomplex valued-function
\mathbb{BC}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{BC}
of the form

\displaystyle Z(\nu,\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3}):=\prod_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}(\nu^{i},\mu_{1}^{i},\mu_{2}^{i},\mu_{3}^{i}),

where
\nu=(\nu^{i})
,
\mu_{j}=(\mu_{j}^{i})
are vectors of non-negative integers, for
j=1,2,3
. Lastly, a
bicomplex mixed polynomial
is a finite sum of the form

\displaystyle F(Z)=\sum_{\nu,\mu}\lambda_{\nu,\mu}Z(\nu,\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3}),

where
\lambda_{\nu,\mu}\in\mathbb{BC}\setminus\{0\}
.

For simplicity, we shall denote
F(Z)
by
F(Z,\widetilde{Z}),F(Z,\widehat{Z}),
and
F(Z,\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$Z$}}}}Z)
if
F
depends only on the respective variables and we refer to it by tilde, hat, or bar-mixed polynomials, respectively. A
singular point
of a bicomplex mixed polynomial is a singular point of the associated real polynomial map
\mathbb{R}^{4n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4}
.

4.1.
Idempotent representations

The idempotent representation of bicomplex numbers allows us to reduce mixed polynomials to complex maps whose coordinates are certain polynomials of the same type as
F(Z)
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
F(Z)=\sum_{\nu,\mu}\lambda_{\nu,\mu}Z(\nu,\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3})
be a bicomplex mixed polynomial, where
\lambda_{\nu,\mu}=\lambda_{\nu,\mu}^{1}\textbf{e}+\lambda^{2}_{\nu,\mu}\textbf{e}^{\dagger}
for each multi-index
\nu,\mu
. Then, up to a linear change of coordinates,
F:\mathbb{C}^{2n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}
has the following form:

\displaystyle F(z_{1},\bar{z}_{1},z_{2},\bar{z}_{2})=\sum_{\nu,\mu}\lambda_{\nu,\mu}^{1}z_{1}^{\nu}\bar{z}_{1}^{\mu_{1}}z_{2}^{\mu_{2}}\bar{z}_{2}^{\mu_{3}}\textbf{e}+\sum_{\nu,\mu}\lambda^{2}_{\nu,\mu}z_{2}^{\nu}\bar{z}_{2}^{\mu_{1}}z_{1}^{\mu_{2}}\bar{z}_{1}^{\mu_{3}}\textbf{e}^{\dagger}.

We shall denote
F(z_{1},z_{2})=(f_{1}(z_{1},z_{2}),f_{2}(z_{1},z_{2}))
and call it the idempotent representation of
F(Z)
.

Proof.

Let
Z=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n})\in\mathbb{BC}^{n}
and, for each
k
, write
Z_{k}=\lambda_{1k}+\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\lambda_{2k}
, where
\lambda_{lk}\in\mathbb{C}
for
l=1,2
. Every summand in the decomposition of
F
has the form

(4.1)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\nu,\mu}Z(\nu,\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3})=\lambda_{\nu,\mu}Z_{1}^{\nu^{1}}\dots Z_{n}^{\nu^{n}}\widetilde{Z}_{1}^{\mu_{1}^{1}}\dots\widetilde{Z}_{n}^{\mu_{1}^{n}}\widehat{Z}_{1}^{\mu_{2}^{1}}\dots\widehat{Z}_{n}^{\mu_{2}^{n}}\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$Z$}}}}Z_{1}^{\mu_{3}^{1}}\dots\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$Z$}}}}Z_{n}^{\mu_{3}^{n}}.

Now, we consider the idempotent representation of
Z_{k}
and define the following linear coordinate change
\psi:\mathbb{C}^{2n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2n}
by

\psi(z_{11},z_{12},\dots,z_{1n},z_{2n})=(u_{11},v_{21},\dots,u_{1n},v_{2n}),

where
z_{1k}=\lambda_{1k}-\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\lambda_{2k},\;z_{2k}=\lambda_{1k}+\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\lambda_{2k}

for all
k=1,\dots,n
. The product (
4.1
) is taken with respect to the basis
(\textbf{e},\textbf{e}^{\dagger})
, and by writing
F
on these coordinates we obtain the result.
∎

The hat, tilde, and bar-mixed cases are those for which all the vectors
\mu_{i},\mu_{j}
vanish for some pair
i,j=1,2,3
and we thus obtain:

Corollary 4.2
.

Up to a linear change of coordinates, the following statements hold true:

(1)

A tilde-mixed polynomial
F(Z,\widetilde{Z})
has an idempotent representation

\displaystyle F(z_{1},z_{2},\bar{z}_{1},\bar{z}_{2})=\sum_{\nu,\mu_{1}}\lambda_{\nu,\mu_{1}}^{1}z_{1}^{\nu}\bar{z}_{1}^{\mu_{1}}\textbf{e}+\sum_{\nu,\mu_{1}}\lambda_{\nu,\mu_{1}}^{2}z_{2}^{\nu}\bar{z}_{2}^{\mu_{1}}\textbf{e}^{\dagger}.

(2)

A hat-mixed polynomial
F(Z,\widehat{Z})
has an idempotent representation

\displaystyle F(z_{1},z_{2},\bar{z}_{1},\bar{z}_{2})=\sum_{\nu,\mu_{2}}\lambda_{\nu,\mu_{2}}^{1}z_{1}^{\nu}z_{2}^{\mu_{2}}\textbf{e}+\sum_{\nu,\mu_{2}}\lambda_{\nu,\mu_{2}}^{2}z_{2}^{\nu}z_{1}^{\mu_{2}}\textbf{e}^{\dagger}.

(3)

A bar-mixed polynomial
F(Z,\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$Z$}}}}Z)
has an idempotent representation

\displaystyle F(z_{1},z_{2},\bar{z}_{1},\bar{z}_{2})=\sum_{\nu,\mu_{3}}\lambda_{\nu,\mu_{3}}^{1}z_{1}^{\nu}\bar{z}_{2}^{\mu_{3}}\textbf{e}+\sum_{\nu,\mu_{3}}\lambda_{\nu,\mu_{3}}^{2}z_{2}^{\nu}\bar{z}_{1}^{\mu_{3}}\textbf{e}^{\dagger}.

4.2.
Polar weighted homogeneous property

Definition 4.3
.

Let
p_{j},u_{j},t_{j}
with
j=1,\dots,n
be positive integers such that

\displaystyle\gcd(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})=\gcd(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})=\gcd(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})=1.

For each
\Lambda\in\mathbb{BC}^{*}
consider the polar form
\Lambda=se^{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta}e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\Theta}
, where
s\in\mathbb{R}^{+},e^{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta}\in\ci
and
e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\Theta}\in\cc
. A polar
\mathbb{BC}^{*}
-action with radial weights
(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
, polar weights
(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
, and complex polar weights
(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})
is given by

\displaystyle\Lambda\cdot Z

\displaystyle=\left(s^{t_{1}}e^{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mup_{1}\theta}e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0muu_{1}\Theta}Z_{1},\dots,s^{t_{n}}e^{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mup_{n}\theta}e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0muu_{n}\Theta}Z_{n}\right),

\displaystyle\Lambda\cdot\widehat{Z}

\displaystyle=\left(s^{t_{1}}e^{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mup_{1}\theta}e^{-\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0muu_{1}\Theta}\widehat{Z}_{1},\dots,s^{t_{n}}e^{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mup_{n}\theta}e^{-\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0muu_{n}\Theta}\widehat{Z}_{n}\right),

\displaystyle\Lambda\cdot\widetilde{Z}

\displaystyle=\left(s^{t_{1}}e^{-\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mup_{1}\theta}e^{-\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0muu_{1}\overline{\Theta}}\widetilde{Z}_{1},\dots,s^{t_{n}}e^{-\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mup_{n}\theta}e^{-\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0muu_{n}\overline{\Theta}}\widetilde{Z}_{n}\right),

\displaystyle\Lambda\cdot\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$Z$}}}}Z

\displaystyle=\left(s^{t_{1}}e^{-\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mup_{1}\theta}e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0muu_{1}\overline{\Theta}}\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$Z$}}}}Z_{1},\dots,s^{t_{n}}e^{-\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mup_{n}\theta}e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0muu_{n}\overline{\Theta}}\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$Z$}}}}Z_{n}\right).

Notice that this action is a combination of
\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{S}^{1}
, and
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{\mathbb{C}}
-actions with weights.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
F:\mathbb{BC}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{BC}
be a bicomplex mixed polynomial,
a,c,d,d^{\prime}
non-negative integers such that
a,c>0
and
d>0
or
d^{\prime}>0
. We say that
F
is polar weighted homogeneous with radial weight type
(t_{1},\dots,t_{n};a)
, polar weight type
(p_{1},\dots,p_{n};c)
, and complex polar weight type
(u_{1},\dots,u_{n};d,d^{\prime})
if the following identity holds:

(4.2)

\displaystyle F\left(se^{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta}e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\Theta}\cdot(Z,\widetilde{Z},\widehat{Z},\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$Z$}}}}Z)\right)=s^{a}e^{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0muc\theta}e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mud\Theta}e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mud^{\prime}\overline{\Theta}}F(Z,\widetilde{Z},\widehat{Z},\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$Z$}}}}Z),\;\;s\in\mathbb{R}^{+},\;e^{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta}\in\ci,\;e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\Theta},e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\Theta}}\in\cc,

where
se^{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta}e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\Theta}\cdot(Z,\widetilde{Z},\widehat{Z},\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$Z$}}}}Z)
denotes the previous polar action of
\mathbb{BC}^{*}
.

Remark 4.5
.

Notice that the complex mixed polynomials in the idempotent representation
F(z_{1},z_{2})=(f_{1},f_{2})
are polar weighted homogeneous in the sense of
[
4
]
of the same radial and polar types as
F
but not simultaneously on the same variables in the case of the polar actions.

Before we proceed with examples, we state a property induced by the radial action on the discriminant.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
F(Z)
be a bicomplex polar weighted homogeneous polynomial. If
P\in\mathbb{BC}^{n}
is a critical point of
F
, then
s\cdot P
is also a critical point for all
s\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. In particular, the discriminant
\Delta_{F}
of
F
consists of a union of lines passing through the origin.

Proof.

By linearity, we may prove the statement for the idempotent representation
F=(f_{1}(z_{1},z_{2}),f_{2}(z_{1},z_{2}))
. One has that
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are radial weighted homogeneous of the same type as
F
. The Jacobian matrix
J_{F}
of
F
can be written in terms of mixed derivatives in relation to
z_{1},\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$z$}}}}z_{1},z_{2},\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$z$}}}}z_{2}
. This implies that each
j
-column
J_{F}^{j}
of this matrix satisfies

\displaystyle\left(J_{F}^{j}\right)^{T}(s\cdot P)=s^{a-t_{j}}J_{F}^{j}(P),

where
t_{j}
is the radial weight associated to
z_{1j},z_{2j}
(see
[
4
, Section 3.1]
). Hence, the
\mathbb{R}^{+}
-action preserves the rank of
F
. To conclude, suppose that
F
is radial weighted homogeneous with weight
d
. Let
X\in\Delta_{F}
and
s\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, then there exists a singular point
P
such that
F(P)=X
so that
F(s^{1/d}\cdot P)=sX
and the statement follows.
∎

See also
[
6
, Lemma 2.16]
for a similar statement applied to mixed polynomials. The next identities follow by taking the derivate of(
4.2
) with respect to
s
,
\theta
,
\Theta
, and
\overline{\Theta}
.

\displaystyle aF(Z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}\left[Z_{i}\frac{\partial F}{\partial Z_{i}}+\widetilde{Z}_{i}\frac{\partial F}{\partial\widetilde{Z}_{i}}+\widehat{Z}_{i}\frac{\partial F}{\partial\widehat{Z}_{i}}+\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$Z$}}}}Z_{i}\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$Z$}}}}Z_{i}}\right],

\displaystyle cF(Z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\left[Z_{i}\frac{\partial F}{\partial Z_{i}}+\widetilde{Z}_{i}\frac{\partial F}{\partial\widetilde{Z}_{i}}-\widehat{Z}_{i}\frac{\partial F}{\partial\widehat{Z}_{i}}-\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$Z$}}}}Z_{i}\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$Z$}}}}Z_{i}}\right],

\displaystyle dF(Z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}\left[Z_{i}\frac{\partial F}{\partial Z_{i}}-\widehat{Z}_{i}\frac{\partial F}{\partial\widehat{Z}_{i}}\right],

\displaystyle d^{\prime}F(Z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}\left[\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$Z$}}}}Z_{i}\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$Z$}}}}Z_{i}}-\widetilde{Z}_{i}\frac{\partial F}{\partial\widetilde{Z}_{i}}\right].

4.3.
Examples

Example 4.7

(Weighted homogeneous polynomials)
.

If
F(Z)=\sum_{\nu}\lambda_{\nu}Z^{\nu}
is a bicomplex weighted homogeneous polynomial, then there exist integers
p_{1},\dots,p_{n},d
for which

\displaystyle F\left(\Lambda^{p_{1}}Z_{1},\dots,\Lambda^{p_{n}}Z_{n}\right)=\Lambda^{d}F(Z),

for all
\Lambda\in\mathbb{BC}\setminus\{0\}
, in particular, for those in
\mathbb{BC}^{*}
. In this case, the weight
d^{\prime}=0
.

Example 4.8

(Mixed Pham-Brieskorn polynomials)
.

Let
F(Z)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}(a_{i},b_{i},c_{i},d_{i})
, where
a_{i},b_{i},c_{i},d_{i}
are non-negative integers for all
i
. Notice that if
b_{i}=c_{i}=d_{i}=0
it resembles the Pham-Brieskorn polynomials on complex variables. Consider weights
(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
,
(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
, and
(q_{1},\dots,q_{n})
. Let us study each monomial separately:

\displaystyle se^{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta}e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\Theta}\cdot Z_{i}(a_{i},b_{i},c_{i},d_{i})=s^{t_{i}(a_{i}+b_{i}+c_{i}+d_{i})}e^{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mup_{i}(a_{i}-b_{i}+c_{i}-d_{i})\theta}e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0muq_{i}(a_{i}-c_{i})\Theta}e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0muq_{i}(d_{i}-b_{i})\overline{\Theta}}Z_{i}.

For each
i=1,\dots,n
, let us suppose that

(4.3)

\displaystyle a_{i}-b_{i}>d_{i}-c_{i},\quad a_{i}>c_{i},\quad d_{i}-b_{i}\geq 0.

If
d_{i}-b_{i}>0
, we require further that
a_{i}-c_{i}=d_{i}-b_{i}
. It follows that
F(Z)
is polar weighted of radial, polar, and complex polar types

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{a_{1}+b_{1}+c_{1}+d_{1}},\dots,\frac{1}{a_{n}+b_{n}+c_{n}+d_{n}};1\right),

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{a_{1}-b_{1}+c_{1}-d_{1}},\dots,\frac{1}{a_{n}-b_{n}+c_{n}-d_{n}};1\right),

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{a_{1}-c_{1}},\dots,\frac{1}{a_{n}-c_{n}};1;1\right),

respectively. Since
\widetilde{X}\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$X$}}}}X=1
for all
X\in\cc
, if
b_{i}=d_{i}
,
F(Z)
reduces to a hat-mixed polar weighted polynomial of (equal) radial and polar, and complex polar types

(4.4)

\begin{split}&\left(\frac{1}{a_{1}+c_{1}},\dots,\frac{1}{a_{n}+c_{n}};1\right),\\
&\left(\frac{1}{a_{1}-c_{1}},\dots,\frac{1}{a_{n}-c_{n}};1;0\right).\end{split}

If
d_{i}=0
and
b_{i}\neq 0
for some
i
, we obtain a negative exponent in the complex polar action, and
F(Z)
does not satisfy the polar property. Moreover, if
b_{i}=c_{i}=0
, then
F(Z)
is a bar-mixed polynomial which is polar weighted only if
d_{i}=0
. Nevertheless, let us consider
d_{i}=c_{i}=0
for all
i
, so
F(Z)
is a tilde-mixed polynomial. One has that
F
admits an idempotent representation
F(z_{1},z_{2})=(f_{1}(z_{1},\bar{z}_{1}),f_{2}(z_{2},\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$z$}}}}z_{2}))
, where

and
f_{2}
are polar weighted with the same radial and polar types. The action on each component may be expressed in bicomplex terms as follows. Let
r\lambda\in\mathbb{BC}^{*}
, where
r\in\mathbb{D}^{+}
and
\lambda\in\tr
. For each monomial, one has

\displaystyle r\lambda\cdot Z_{i}^{a_{i}}\widetilde{Z}_{i}^{b_{i}}=r^{p_{i}(a_{i}+b_{i})}\lambda^{q_{i}a_{i}}\widetilde{\lambda}^{q_{i}b_{i}}Z_{i}^{a_{i}}\widetilde{Z}_{i}^{b_{i}}=r^{p_{i}(a_{i}+b_{i})}\lambda^{q_{i}(a_{i}-b_{i})}Z_{i}^{a_{i}}\widetilde{Z}_{i}^{b_{i}},

and according to this hyperbolic action,
F(Z,\widetilde{Z})
is polar weighted of the same types (
4.4
), where we replace
c_{i}
by
b_{i}
.

Example 4.9

(Mixed cyclic polynomials)
.

Let
F(Z)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}Z_{i}(a_{i},b_{i},c_{i},d_{i})Z_{i+1}+Z_{n}(a_{n},b_{n},c_{n},d_{n})Z_{1}

Example 4.10

(Join)
.

[

and

Proposition 4.11
.

Let
F(Z)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}(a_{i},b_{i},c_{i},d_{i})
be a mixed Pham-Brieskorn polynomial, where
a_{i},b_{i},c_{i},d_{i}
satisfy the conditions in (
4.3
) with the additional hypothesis that
a_{i}-b_{i},c_{i}-d_{i}>0
for all
i=1,\dots,n
. Then there exists a homomorphism (for the Euclidean topology)
\phi:(\mathbb{BC}^{*})^{n}\longrightarrow(\mathbb{BC}^{*})^{n}
such that
F\circ\phi(Z)=G(Z)
, where
G(Z)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}(a_{i}-b_{i},0,c_{i}-d_{i},0)
.

Proof.

Let
D=\{z_{11}z_{21}\dots z_{1n}z_{2n}=0\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2n}
and define a coordinate change
\phi:\mathbb{C}^{2n}\setminus D\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2n}\setminus D
given by
\phi(z_{1},z_{2})=w_{1}\textbf{e}+w_{2}\textbf{e}^{\dagger}
in
\mathbb{C}^{2n}
as follows. For each
i
, we take

(4.5)

\displaystyle w_{1i}

\displaystyle=z_{1i}\|z_{1i}\|^{k_{1}}\|z_{2i}\|^{k_{2}},

\displaystyle w_{2i}

\displaystyle=z_{2i}\|z_{1i}\|^{k_{1}}\|z_{2i}\|^{k_{2}},

where
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
are positive rational numbers that satisfy

\displaystyle k_{1}\left((a_{i}-b_{i})+(c_{i}-d_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=2b_{i},

\displaystyle k_{2}\left((a_{i}-b_{i})+(c_{i}-d_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=2d_{i}.

A direct computation shows that

\displaystyle\|z_{1i}\|=\frac{\|w_{2i}\|^{\frac{k_{1}+1}{k_{1}+k_{2}+1}}}{\|w_{1i}\|^{\frac{k_{1}}{k_{1}+k_{2}+1}}},

\displaystyle\|z_{2i}\|=\frac{\|w_{1i}\|^{\frac{k_{2}+1}{k_{1}+k_{2}+1}}}{\|w_{2i}\|^{\frac{k_{2}}{k_{1}+k_{2}+1}}}.

It follows that we may isolate
z_{1i}
and
z_{2i}
in (
4.5
) and
\phi
is an invertible map. Finally, we obtain

\displaystyle w_{1i}^{a_{i}-b_{i}}w_{2i}^{c_{i}-d_{i}}

\displaystyle=z_{1i}^{a_{i}}\bar{z}_{1i}^{b_{i}}z_{2i}^{c_{i}}\bar{z}_{2i}^{d_{i}},

\displaystyle w_{1i}^{c_{i}-d_{i}}w_{2i}^{a_{i}-b_{i}}

\displaystyle=z_{1i}^{c_{i}}\bar{z}_{1i}^{d_{i}}z_{2i}^{a_{i}}\bar{z}_{2i}^{b_{i}}.

as desired.
∎

5.
Fibrations

For complex polar weighted homogeneous polynomials, the results in
[
4
]
show that the zero is an isolated critical value. This implies later that one has an associated locally trivial fibration. This is generalized to bicomplex polynomials by considering the set of zero divisors. Additionally, we show the existence of spherical fibrations with base spaces being the quadric
\cc
and
\mathbb{S}^{3}_{0}
. Henceforth, we shall denote
V_{F}=F^{-1}(\zd)
. First, we present an example.

Example 5.1
.

Let
F:\mathbb{BC}^{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{BC}
be given by
F(Z,W)=Z^{2}+W^{2}
. Then
F
is weighted homogeneous of type
(1,1;2)
. If we set
Z=\lambda_{1}+\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\lambda_{2}
and
W=\alpha_{1}+\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\alpha_{2}
, where
\lambda_{i},\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
, for
i=1,2
, then the corresponding holomorphic map
F:\mathbb{C}^{4}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}
is given by:

\displaystyle F(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})=(\lambda_{1}^{2}-\lambda_{2}^{2}+\alpha_{1}^{2}-\alpha_{2}^{2},2\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}+2\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}).

If
P=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{4}
is a critical point, then
z_{2}=\pm\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0muz_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}=\pm\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\alpha_{1}
. That is,
\Sigma_{F}=\zd\times\zd
. Notice that
F(\zd\times\zd)=0\in\zd
. We shall see that the fact of the image of critical points being a subset of zero divisors is a general property for polar weighted polynomials.

Proposition 5.2

(Proposition 3.2,
[
4
]
)
.

Let
F
be a bicomplex polar weighted homogeneous polynomial. Then every
U\in\mathbb{BC}^{*}
is a regular value.

Proof.

Let
Z\in\mathbb{BC}^{n}
such that
F(Z)=U\in\mathbb{BC}^{*}
. We shall see that the orbit of the action has four linearly independent real tangent vectors at
Z
. Moreover, their image by
DF_{Z}
has real dimension 4 and so the rank of
DF
is maximal at such a point
Z
. We may write
F=\|F\|e^{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta_{F}}e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\Theta_{F}}
, provided that
U
is not a zero divisor neither zero. We have that:

\displaystyle V_{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dr}\left(r\cdot Z\right)_{r=1}=\left(t_{1}r^{t_{1}-1}Z_{1},\dots,t_{n}r^{t_{n}-1}Z_{n}\right)_{r=1}=\left(t_{1}Z_{1},\dots,t_{n}Z_{n}\right),

\displaystyle V_{\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\theta}\left(e^{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta}\cdot Z\right)_{\theta=0}=\left(\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mup_{1}e^{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mup_{1}\theta}Z_{1},\dots,\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mup_{n}e^{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mup_{n}\theta}Z_{n}\right)_{\theta=0}=\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\left(p_{1}Z_{1},\dots,p_{n}Z_{n}\right),

\displaystyle V_{\Theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\Theta}\left(e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\Theta}\cdot Z\right)_{\Theta=0}=\left(\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0muu_{1}e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0muu_{1}\Theta}Z_{1},\dots,\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0muu_{n}e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0muu_{n}\Theta}Z_{n}\right)_{\Theta=0}=\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\left(u_{1}Z_{1},\dots,u_{n}Z_{n}\right).

Let us denote
P=(1,0,0)
the unit element
1
in the polar form and assume, without lost of generality, that
d>0
. Then:

\displaystyle dF_{Z}\left(V_{r}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dr}\left[F(r\cdot(Z,\widetilde{Z},\widehat{Z},\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$Z$}}}}Z))\right]_{|P=(1,0,0)}=\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^{a}\|F\|,\theta_{F},\Theta_{F}\right)_{P=(1,0,0)}=a\|U\|\frac{\partial}{\partial r},

\displaystyle dF_{Z}\left(V_{\theta}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\theta}\left[F(\theta\cdot(Z,\widetilde{Z},\widehat{Z},\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$Z$}}}}Z))\right]_{|P=(1,0,0)}=\frac{d}{d\theta}\left(\|F\|,\theta_{F}+c\theta,\Theta_{F}\right)_{P=(1,0,0)}=c\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta},

\displaystyle dF_{Z}\left(V_{\Theta}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\Theta}\left[F(\Theta\cdot(Z,\widetilde{Z},\widehat{Z},\mathrlap{\hskip 2.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.7}[1]{\phantom{$Z$}}}}Z))\right]_{|P=(1,0,0)}=\frac{d}{d\Theta}\left(\|F\|,\theta_{F},\Theta_{F}+d\Theta+d^{\prime}\overline{\Theta}\right)_{P=(1,0,0)}=d\frac{\partial}{\partial\Theta}.

The three vectors generate a complex plane, or a real space with dimension
4
. Therefore, we obtain the existence of 4 linearly independent vectors in the image of
dF_{Z}
.
∎

Notation
.

Henceforth we use the following convention. We always write
d
or
d^{\prime}
if these are not zero. Otherwise, the associated exponentials
e^{\frac{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\Theta}{d}}
or
e^{\frac{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\Theta}}{d}}
are read as constant equal to
1
.

Proposition 5.3
.

The map
F:\mathbb{BC}^{n}\setminus V_{F}\longrightarrow\mathbb{BC}^{*}
is a locally trivial fibration.

Proof.

Define an open cover of
\mathbb{BC}^{*}
given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{0}

\displaystyle=\{Z\in\mathbb{BC}^{*}:\operatorname{Re}\left(\arg_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}Z\right)\neq 0\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\pi}

\displaystyle=\{Z\in\mathbb{BC}^{*}:\operatorname{Re}\left(\arg_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}Z\right)\neq\pi\}.

The following maps and their inverses give the local trivialization:

\displaystyle\tau_{0}:F^{-1}(1)\times\mathcal{U}_{0}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow F^{-1}(\mathcal{U}_{0})

\displaystyle(Z,se^{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta}e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\Theta})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow s^{\frac{1}{a}}e^{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\theta}{c}}e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\Theta}{d}}e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\overline{\Theta}}{d^{\prime}}}\cdot Z

\displaystyle\tau_{0}^{-1}:F^{-1}(\mathcal{U}_{0})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow F^{-1}(1)\times\mathcal{U}_{0}

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle\longmapsto\left(\rho(W)\cdot W,F(W)\right),

where

\rho(W)=\|F(W)\|^{-\frac{1}{a}}e^{-\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\arg(\|F(W)\|_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu})}{c}}e^{-\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\arg_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}(F(W))}{d}}e^{-\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\overline{\arg_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}(F(W))}}{d^{\prime}}}.

Analogously for

and its inverse, both defined by the same expressions as above.
∎

6.
Join theorem

In this section, we prove a join type theorem for bicomplex polar weighted homogeneous polynomials.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
G:\mathbb{BC}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{BC}
and
H:\mathbb{BC}^{m}\longrightarrow\mathbb{BC}
be bicomplex polar weighted homogeneous polynomials. Let
F:\mathbb{BC}^{n}\times\mathbb{BC}^{m}\longrightarrow\mathbb{BC}
be defined by:

\displaystyle F(Z,W)=G(Z)+H(W)

and the fibers

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=F^{-1}(1)\subset\mathbb{BC}^{n+m},

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=G^{-1}(1)\subset\mathbb{BC}^{n},

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=H^{-1}(1)\subset\mathbb{BC}^{m}.

Then, there is a homotopy equivalence
\alpha:X\longrightarrow Y\ast T
, where
Y\ast T
denotes the Join product of
Y
and
T
.

Proof.

The proof is almost identical to that in
[
4
, Theorem 4.1]
. We only remark on how to modify each homotopy in this new context. First, suppose that
G(Z)
and
H(W)
have radial, polar, and complex polar degrees
a,b,c,c^{\prime}
and
d,e,f,f^{\prime}
, respectively.

(1)

Defining
\widetilde{X}
: We define a quotient space
\widetilde{X}
of
X
by the following equivalences. A point
(Z,W)\sim(Z^{\prime},W^{\prime})
if and only if:

(a)

Z=Z^{\prime}
and
H(W)=H(W^{\prime})
, whose class is denoted by
[Z,*]
.

(b)

W=W^{\prime}
and
G(Z)=G(Z^{\prime})
, whose class is denoted by
[*,W]
.

(c)

Z=Z^{\prime}
and
W=W^{\prime}
, where
G(Z)\neq 0,1
, whose class is denote by
[Z,W]
.

Points of type
(3)
could be defined in terms of
H(W)\neq 0,1
, since
G(Z)+H(W)=1
for
(Z,W)\in X
. The topology of
X
is the weakest topology such that the projections on each coordinate of
[Z,W]
are continuous. Notice that this structure is coarser than the quotient one.

(2)

\widetilde{X}
has the homotopy type of
X
: Let
\epsilon>0
and define

\displaystyle N_{1,\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\{(Z,W)\in X:\|H(W)\|\leq\epsilon\},

\displaystyle N_{2,\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\{(Z,W)\in X:\|G(Z)\|\leq\epsilon\}.

Let
\rho(t):\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function such that

a)

\rho(t)
is monotone decreasing on
(\epsilon,2\epsilon)
.

b)

\rho^{-1}(1)=(-\infty,\epsilon)
and
\rho^{-1}(0)=[2\epsilon,\infty)
.

Define
\mathcal{H}:X\times I\longrightarrow X
by:

a)

For
(Z,W)\notin N_{1,2\epsilon}\cup N_{2,\epsilon}
, the homotopy is the identity.

b)

For
(Z,W)\in N_{1,2\epsilon}
,
\mathcal{H}\left([Z,W],t\right)=(Z(t),W(t))
, where

\displaystyle Z(t)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{r_{t}}{r_{0}}\right)^{\frac{1}{a}}\cdot e^{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\theta_{t}}{b}}\cdot e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\Theta_{t}}{c}}e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\overline{\Theta}_{t}}{c^{\prime}}},

\displaystyle W(t)

\displaystyle=\left(1-t\rho\left(H(W)\right)\right)^{\frac{1}{d}},

and

\displaystyle r(t)

\displaystyle=\|1-H(W(t))\|,

\displaystyle\theta_{t}

\displaystyle=\arg\left\|\frac{1-H(W(t))}{1-H(W)}\right\|_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu},

\displaystyle\Theta_{t}

\displaystyle=\arg_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}\left(\frac{1-H(W(t))}{1-H(W)}\right).

c)

For points in
N_{2,2\epsilon}
, we have the analogous definitions interchange the roles of
G(Z)
and
H(W)
.

It follows that
\mathcal{H}
is continuous and preserves
X
. To establish the homotopy between
X
and
\widetilde{X}
, a map
\mathcal{H}_{1}:\widetilde{X}\longrightarrow X
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\circ\pi=\mathcal{H}_{1}.

By the properties of the classes in
\widetilde{X}
and the definition of
\mathcal{H}
, it is well-defined and continuous.

(3)

\widetilde{X}
has the homotopy type of
R\widetilde{X}
: where

\displaystyle R\widetilde{X}=\{[Z,W]\in\widetilde{X}:G(Z)\in\mathbb{R}\}.

Since
G(Z)+H(W)=1
we could define
R\widetilde{X}
in terms of
H(W)\in\mathbb{R}
. Let us introduce a notation. For
G(Z)=G_{1}(Z)+\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0muG_{2}(Z)
, we denote

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}(G(Z))

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}(G_{1}(Z)),

\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}(G(Z))

\displaystyle=1/\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\left(G(Z)-\operatorname{Re}(G(Z))\right).

Also, for
t\in[0,1]
, one has

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{t}(Z)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}(G(Z))+\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu(1-t)\operatorname{Im}(G(Z)),

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{t}(W)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}(H(W))+\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu(1-t)\operatorname{Im}(H(W)).

A deformation
\mathcal{F}:\widetilde{X}\times I\longrightarrow\widetilde{X}
of
\widetilde{X}
to
R\widetilde{X}
is defined by
\mathcal{F}\left([Z,W],t\right)=[Z(t),W(t)]
as:

a)

For points
[Z,*]
or
[*,W]
,
\mathcal{F}
is the identity.

b)

For points
[Z,W]
, it is given by

\displaystyle Z(t)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{r_{t}}{r_{0}}\right)^{\frac{1}{a}}\cdot e^{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\alpha_{t}}{b}}\cdot e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\beta_{t}}{c}}\cdot e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\overline{\beta}_{t}}{c^{\prime}}},

\displaystyle W(t)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{s_{t}}{s_{0}}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}\cdot e^{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\gamma_{t}}{e}}\cdot e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\lambda_{t}}{f}}\cdot e^{\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\overline{\lambda}_{t}}{f^{\prime}}},

where

\displaystyle r_{t}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{G}_{t}(Z)\|,\quad s_{t}=\|\mathbf{H}_{t}(W)\|,

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}

\displaystyle=\arg\|\mathbf{G}_{t}(Z)/G(Z)\|_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu},\quad\gamma_{t}=\arg\|\mathbf{H}_{t}(W)/H(W)\|_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu},

\displaystyle\beta_{t}

\displaystyle=\arg_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}\left(\mathbf{G}_{t}(Z)/G(Z)\right),\quad\lambda_{t}=\arg_{\mathrm{\bf i}\mkern 1.0mu}\left(\mathbf{H}_{t}(W)/H(W)\right).

It is clear that

\displaystyle G(Z(t))=\mathbf{G}_{t}(Z)\quad\text{and}\quad H(W(t))=\mathbf{H}_{t}(W),

which shows that
\mathcal{F}
preserves
R\widetilde{X}
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(Z(t),W(t))

\displaystyle=G(Z(t))+H(W(t))

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}_{t}(Z)+\mathbf{H}_{t}(W)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}(F(Z,W))+\mathrm{\bf j}\mkern 1.0mu(1-t)\operatorname{Im}(F(Z,W))

\displaystyle=1,

this implies that the homotopy
\mathcal{F}
belongs to
\widetilde{X}
.

(4)

Conclusion: The space
R\widetilde{X}
is deformed to

\displaystyle R^{+}\widetilde{X}=\{[Z,W]\in R\widetilde{X}:G(Z),H(W)\geq 0\},

by a homotopy that depends only on the radial homogeneity and thus it is defined in the same manner. This space is proved to be homeomorphic with
Y\ast T
again by maps depending only on this property and the assertion follows.

∎
