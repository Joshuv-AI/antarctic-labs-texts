Leveraging antisolvent-processed β-sitosterol to construct a multifunctional curcumin-loaded oleogel for diabetic wound healing
Authors: Hongyang Guo, Shulin Deng, Caihuan Huang, Jie Zheng, Juanying Ou, Zhenzhen Liu, Shiyi Ou, Fu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe complex microenvironment of diabetic wounds is the primary cause of slow wound healing, manifesting as a vicious cycle of excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and overexpression of inflammatory factors. Regulating this microenvironment by inhibiting wound inflammation, oxidative stress, and bacterial infection is a key challenge in the treatment of diabetic wounds. In this study, β-sitosterol particles (AS-Sit) were obtained via anti-solvent precipitation which solved the problem of poor dispersibility of β-sitosterol and combined them with candelilla wax to develop a novel multifunctional oleogel system capable of loading curcumin. The oleogel system demonstrates notable stability, spreadability, and sustained-release performance. Furthermore, the synergistic effects of β-sitosterol and curcumin resulted in the OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel demonstrating significant efficacy in bacterial elimination, ROS scavenging, fibroblast migration promotion, macrophage M2 polarization induction, and inflammatory factor expression regulation. This multifunctional oleogel represents a simple and effective treatment for promoting the healing of chronic diabetic wounds and holds great potential for future clinical applications.Keywords: Antisolvent, β-sitosterol, Oleogel, Curcumin, Diabetic, Wound healing

Introduction

Diabetes is one of the most prevalent chronic diseases, with an estimated 537 million people worldwide living with diabetes in 2021, a number projected to rise to 783 million by 2045 [1]. Approximately 15-25% of individuals with diabetes suffer from chronic wounds, putting them at risk of amputation and death. These chronic wounds are characterized by persistent inflammation and are prone to bacterial infections, which hinder wound healing. A variety of local pathological changes are evident in the diabetic wound microenvironment, such as bacterial infection, neuropathy, immune cell dysfunction, and a chronic pro-inflammatory state [2,3]. In response to the complexities of the wound microenvironment, oleogels containing bioactive ingredients have emerged as an alternative treatment option for diabetic wounds.

Oleogels are formed by dissolving gel factors in liquid oil and then trapping the liquid oil through self-assembly or crystallization of the oleogel factors [4]. The semi-solid system created by the self-assembled three-dimensional network structure can effectively support fat-soluble drugs and demonstrate good drug permeability through lipid-mediated intracellular matrix diffusion [5]. However, the application of oleogels for loading and releasing bioactive substances may encounter several challenges, including limited oil capacity, poor structural recovery, susceptibility to oxidation of vegetable oils, and the potential risk of oleogel factors surpassing their melting thresholds [6]. Consequently, there is an increasing demand for the development of new lipid-based vectors capable of carrying bioactive ingredients.

Among the various gel factors used to prepare transdermal drug delivery oleogels, β-sitosterol (Sit) stands out for its health benefits, including anti-inflammatory, anti-diabetic, antioxidant, and positive effects on cardiovascular disease [7]. Sit is commonly used in combination with other compounds to form stable oleogels [8]. However, the application of Sit in biomedical materials and bioactive delivery has been largely limited by its high melting point, poor water solubility, and insufficient oil solubility [9]. To address these issues, current research has focused on improving the solubility and dispersibility of phytosterols through various strategies, such as the development of water-soluble derivatives. In our previous study, we demonstrated that water-dispersed phytosterol particles obtained via antisolvent treatment—without the need for additional stabilizers—can serve as excellent candidates for W/O Pickering emulsions [10]. Compared with pristine Sit particles, antisolvent-precipitated Sit (AS-Sit) particles exhibit markedly reduced particle size, altered crystal structure, and significantly improved solubility and dispersibility in both aqueous and oil phases. These distinct physicochemical properties endow AS-Sit with superior performance in oleogel formation. Specifically, AS-Sit particles can form a more homogeneous and stable oleogel network, whereas pristine Sit particles tend to aggregate and sediment in the oil phase, leading to poor gel consistency. Consequently, oleogels prepared from AS-Sit particles show better spreadability, enhanced stability, and improved utilization of lipophilic bioactive ingredients. Thus, the antisolvent precipitation approach provides a simple and effective strategy to overcome the intrinsic limitations of Sit and expand its potential in biomedical applications and bioactive compound delivery. Furthermore, from the perspective of simplicity in preparation, the novel oleogel system can be formed simply by mixing natural oils and phytosterols, which is considerably simpler and more feasible to implement compared with gel systems that rely on complex processes such as piezoelectric polymers, nanomaterial synthesis, or photothermal conversion [11].

Curcumin, a polyphenol extracted from turmeric, is renowned for its diverse physiological and health benefits, including its anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, anticancer, and antioxidant properties. As a result, it is widely utilized in the foods, cosmetics, and pharmaceutical industries [12]. However, the application of curcumin is significantly hindered by its poor water solubility, chemical instability, susceptibility to oxidative degradation, and low bioavailability [13]. Consequently, developing effective drug delivery systems to overcome these challenges is a complex and ongoing process.

To address the practical limitations associated with the application of Sit, this study developed antisolvent-precipitated β-sitosterol (AS-Sit) featuring a distinctive needle-like morphology. The AS-Sit was uniformly dispersed within the oil phase and subsequently combined with candelilla wax (Cw) to construct a stable oleogel system (AS-Sit/Cw). The structural features and mechanical properties of both AS-Sit particles and the resultant AS-Sit/Cw oleogel were systematically characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), polarized light microscopy, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and rheological analysis. Moreover, the maximum biocompatible concentration of curcumin incorporated into the oleogel was determined through in vitro cytocompatibility assays. The therapeutic efficacy and underlying mechanisms of the oleogel in promoting diabetic wound healing were validated using an in vitro high-glucose oxidative stress cell model and an in vivo diabetic mouse wound healing model. This study introduces a novel multifunctional oleogel preparation strategy, offering a potential alternative for the clinical management of diabetic wounds.

Materials and methods

Materials

Materials were sourced from the following suppliers: β-sitosterol from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd.; curcumin, γ-oryzanol, and glyceryl monostearate from Shanghai MacLean Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.; candelilla wax from Shanghai Jizhi Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.; NIH3T3 cells (mouse embryonic fibroblasts) and RAW 264.7 cells (macrophages) from Shanghai Zhongqiao Xinzhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd.; Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC6538), Escherichia coli (ATCC8739), and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (ATCC27853) strains from Shanghai Luwei Technology Co., Ltd.; rabbit blood from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd.; and ELISA kits (Macklin) from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.

Preparation and characterization of antisolvent β-sitosterol particles

Preparation of antisolvent β-sitosterol particles

The original Sit was mixed with anhydrous ethanol at a ratio of 1:100 (g/mL), and the mixture was ultrasonicated until the large particles were completely dissolved. Then, the anhydrous ethanol solution containing β-sitosterol was obtained by using a constant temperature water bath at 45 °C for 5 min until completely dissolved. After cooling, the T25 high-speed homogenizer was used to add the organic phase (anhydrous ethanol) containing β-sitosterol to the aqueous phase (PBS, pH = 7) within 2 min under intense stirring at 8000 rpm. The ratio of the organic phase to the aqueous phase (antisolvent ratio) was set at 1:1. After mixing, the mixture was homogenized for 2 min. Finally, all the ethanol and part of the water were removed using a rotary evaporator. Then, the water dispersion liquid was frozen and dried for 72 h to obtain antisolvent β-sitosterol particles (AS-Sit).

Particle size

The particle size of the particles was measured by laser nano particle size analyzer. Parameter setting: Dispersant is water, refractive index 1.333. Each sample was measured after 2 min of equilibrium, and the average value of 5 measurements was taken.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) can be used to study the microstructure of particles. The sample particles are first placed on a clean mica surface. It was then analyzed using a scanning electron microscope at an acceleration voltage of 10 KV. Finally, the particle size and surface shape were observed and analyzed by scanning electron microscopy.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

The same mass of sit and AS-Sit particles were weighed separately, and each sample was weighed three times. A crucible was used to press the sheet and a differential scanning calorimeter was used to measure the sample. At a nitrogen flow rate of 15 mL/min, the sample was rapidly heated to 170 °C to eliminate thermal history. After cooling, the sample was heated again from 25 °C to 250 °C, 10 °C each time. An empty crucible was used as a reference.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

The variation of crystallinity of Sit and AS-Sit particles can be analyzed by X-ray polycrystalline diffractometer. The sample particles are first laid out on a special utensil and then placed on a work surface. The scanning range is 2° ∼30° and the step size is 0.02°.

Oil dispersion liquid and microscopic

5% (w/v) Sit and AS-Sit particles were dispersed in soybean oil, and Sit oil dispersion was used as the control group to observe the appearance changes and take photos. The crystal structure of different oil dispersions was observed by polarizing microscope. The different groups of oil dispersion drop on the slide, and then gently cover the cover slide for observation.

Preparation of oleogel

5% (w/v) AS-Sit was mixed with gel factors (oryzanol, glycerol monostearate, candelilla wax) in soybean oil at a mass ratio of 1:1. The mixture was placed in a 90 °C water bath and stirred magnetically at 1000 rpm until the solid was completely dissolved. And then cooled to room temperature to form gel. The oleogels prepared by AS-Sit + oryzanol, AS-Sit + glycerol monostearate, and AS-Sit + candelilla wax were labeled as AS-Sit/Ory, AS-Sti/Gm, and AS-Sit/Cw, respectively.

Microstructure of oleogel

The crystal network morphology of the oleogel was observed by polarizing microscope. The oleogel sample was laid in the center of the slide, and the crystal network morphology of the oleogel was observed and photographed under the condition of 20 times magnification after the cover glass was covered.

Gel factor intermolecular interaction

The molecular structure of gel factors and the intermolecular forces of grease gels were analyzed by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR). The spectral acquisition was carried out in total reflection (ATR) mode with a resolution of 4 cm−1. A small number of samples were placed on the crystal surface of the ATR accessory and repeated 32 times in the scanning range of 600∼4000 cm−1. The spectral data were processed using the OPUS Infrared Spectroscopy workstation (Bruker Optics GmbH).

Molecular docking: Docking was completed using Autodock Vina, and the cavity selection was framed according to the principle of full coverage of small molecules. The docked complex results were selected according to the binding energy, and the force analysis and docking position evaluation were performed. The force analysis of the ligand and receptor was completed by the PLIP online server (https://plip-tool.biotec.tu-dresden.de/plip-web), and the 3D conformation of the ligand-receptor was displayed using the Pymol software.

Rheological properties

The rheological properties of the oleogels were evaluated using a stress-controlled HAAKE™ MARS™ rheometer (Thermelfeld, China). The measurement patterns were as follows [14]: (a) Frequency sweep tests were performed at 37 °C to investigate the association between frequency and modulus (energy storage modulus G′ and loss modulus G″) over a range of frequencies from 0.1 Hz to 10 Hz. (b) Strain scanning tests were performed on molded specimens at 37 °C to determine the linear viscoelastic region, with oscillatory strains increasing from 1% to 1000%. (c) Step strain amplitude tests were performed to investigate the self-recovery properties of the oleogels, with oscillatory strains alternating from 1% to 1000% at intervals of 100 s. (d) Temperature recovery characteristics test: To assess the effects of heating and cooling on the oleogel, through a rheometer. The temperature ramp test was carried out. The oleogel sample was laid flat on the plate of the rheometer and balanced for 2 min before testing. The scanning temperature ranges from 10 °C to 60 °C. First, the temperature rise from 10 °C to 60 °C, the time scan test at 60 °C lasted for 2 min, then the temperature slope from 60 °C to 10 °C (cooling) and the time scan at 10 °C lasted for 2 min. The entire cycle is repeated once to assess the reversibility of the oleogel.

Preparation of oleogel loaded with curcumin

A certain amount of soybean oil was preheated at 90 °C, then 5% (w/v) AS-Sit and 7% (w/v) Cw were added to them. They were stirred magnetometry at 1000 rpm for 30 min, and curcumin was added (curcumin concentration was 0.5%, 1.0%, 1.5%, 2.0%, 2.5% w/v). Continue stirring for 5 min until curcumin is completely dissolved. Cool to room temperature. In order to reduce curcumin degradation, the preparation process should avoid light as much as possible. The oleogels with different amounts of curcumin added were labeled as OG/Cur-0.05%, OG/Cu-0.10%, OG/Cur-0.15%, OG/Cur-0.20%, and OG/Cur-0.25%, respectively.

Photochemical stability and thermal stability of curcumin in oleogel

The photochemical stability of curcumin in oleogel was monitored [15,16]. Oleogels containing curcumin were placed in a centrifuge tube and exposed to ultraviolet light (385 nm) at 35 W for, 1, 2, 3 and 4 h. The absorbance of the above samples was measured at 428 nm via the UV-vis spectrophotometer.

Oleogels containing curcumin were placed in the centrifuge tube and heated at 40 °C in the water bath for 1, 2, 3, 4 h, then immediately cooled to 25 °C in the ice water. After the measurement, oleogels with curcumin were mechanically mashed to destroy the oleogels structure. The absorbance of the above samples was measured at 428 nm via the UV-vis spectrophotometer.

In vitro release of curcumin from oleogels

The in vitro release of curcumin from oleogels was evaluated by dialysis bag method [17]. 1 mL of the oleogel was placed into a dialysis bag (molecular weight cut-off MWCO 3000 Da) and immersed in 100 mg of release medium (PBS containing 25 mM glucose, pH 7.4). The dialysis bag was placed in 10 mL of the same release medium and suspended at 37 °C at an oscillating speed of 100 r/min. The dialysis bag was removed every 6 h and placed in a fresh 10 mL release medium. The content of curcumin in the release medium was measured at 428 nm by UV-visible spectrophotometer.

ROS scavenging ability of oleogel on cells damaged by high glucose oxidation

To estimate the intracellular ROS-scavenging abilities of the oleogels, lipopolysaccharides (LPS, 10 μg/mL) and glucose (200 mM) were used to induce cellular oxidative stress in RAW 264.7 cells. Briefly, 1 × 106 RAW 264.7 cells were cultured for 12 h until they adhered to the wall. To mimic the oxidative stress microenvironment of diabetic wounds, RAW 264.7 cells were co-cultured with LPS and LPS + glucose for 12 h. Then, the solution was discarded, and the cells were cocultured with 500 μL of different oleogel immersion solutions. After 24 h of treatment, the cells were covered with DCFH-DA (1:1000) for 30 min. The results were obtained using an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Moreover, for the quantitative analysis, the fluorescence intensity of the cells was determined using flow cytometry (BD Biosciences) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. The resulting data were analyzed using FlowJoc.

In vitro cell migration of cells damaged by high glucose oxidation by oleogel

The cells were then rinsed three times with PBS to remove any floating cells. Subsequently, 500 μL serum-free medium and 500 μL oleogel extract were added to 12 well plates, respectively. The cells were observed under a microscope after 0 and 24 h.

Results and discussion

Characterization and analysis of sit particles prepared by anti-solvent treatment

This study introduces a structural modification of Sit particles through antisolvent precipitation, addressing the challenges of poor dispersibility and solubility in the oil phase and enabling their uniform dispersion.

The formation process of AS-Sit particles is shown in Fig. 1a. Further research revealed that, compared with Sit, AS-Sit particles could be uniformly dispersed in soybean oil (Fig. 1b i and iv). Meanwhile, the structure of AS-Sit changed from spherical (Fig. 1b ii) to long needle-like (Fig. 1b v). This may be due to the high supersaturation during the rapid mixing of the solvent phase and the antisolvent phase. In this case, the solubility of Sit particles decreases rapidly to a supersaturated state, and nucleation grows and aggregates to form AS-Sit particles [22,23]. To further investigate the morphological transformations of the particles, SEM was employed to examine the freeze-dried particles. As depicted in Fig. 1b iii and vi, the Sit particles exhibit a spherical morphology, whereas the AS-Sit particles uniformly display a lamellar structure. This lamellar morphology is a result of the aggregation of needle-like structures that occurred during the freeze-drying process. The particle size analysis detailed in Fig. 1c illustrates a substantial reduction in the particle size of the AS-Sit particles. This may be more conducive to its dispersion in the oil phase.

Schematic diagram of the preparation of AS-Sit particles (a); The crystal structure and appearance of Sit and AS-Sit particles (b); Particle size (c), Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) (d), and X-ray diffraction (XRD) (e) Images of Sit and AS-Sit particles. Sit and AS-Sit are β-sitosterol and antisolvent β-sitosterol, respectively.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) were utilized to analyze the crystallization behavior of the particles. As illustrated in Fig. 1d, both Sit and AS-Sit exhibit a melting point range, suggesting that no phase transition occurs during the heating process. The heat absorption peak corresponds to the melting peak of the crystal, and the temperature at which this peak occurs is the melting point of the crystal. For Sit, a melting peak is observed at 138.33 °C. Following antisolvent treatment the melting peak of AS-Sit is significantly reduced. This means that the crystallinity of the particle is reduced, indicating that the thermodynamic properties have been greatly improved, and it is not easy to precipitate during application, and the solubility has been improved [24]. Additionally, as shown in Fig. 1e, the characteristic peak intensity of AS-Sit particles is diminished, suggesting that the crystal structure of AS-Sit undergoes changes after antisolvent treatment. This alteration in the crystal structure leads to a decrease in crystallinity and a reduction in particle size. From the above experimental results, it can be seen that the AS-Sit particles obtained by anti-solvent precipitation have good dispersibility, solubility and stability, which is essential for the formation of uniform and stable oleogels.

Appearance and mechanical properties characterization of AS-Sit-based oleogel

The macroscopic appearance of the AS-Sit-based oleogel is presented in Fig. 2a. In the absence of additional gelling agents, AS-Sit alone failed to form a stable gel structure. Therefore, in this study, AS-Sit was combined with three gelling agents Oryzanol (Ory), glyceryl monostearate (Gm), and candelilla wax (Cw) to prepare three distinct oleogel system. Significant differences in mechanical properties were observed among these oleogels. Specifically, the AS-Sit/Ory and AS-Sit/Gm oleogels exhibited relatively high hardness (Table S1) and a distinct granular texture upon application (Fig. 2a). In contrast, the AS-Sit/Cw oleogel displayed a softer texture (Table S1), with no noticeable granular sensation and excellent spreadability.

AS-Sit/Ory, AS-Sit/Gm, and AS-Sit/Cw are oleogels formed by the combination of AS-Sit with oryzanol, glyceryl monostearate, and candelilla wax, respectively.

To elucidate the underlying causes of these mechanical differences, the crystal structures of the oleogels were first examined using polarized light microscopy (PLM). PLM analysis revealed that the crystals in AS-Sit/Ory and AS-Sit/Gm oleogels were relatively large, unevenly distributed, and exhibited an aggregated and disordered arrangement. Conversely, the AS-Sit/Cw oleogel featured smaller, uniformly distributed crystals throughout the gel matrix (Fig. S1). This ordered and homogeneous crystal distribution provides a structural foundation for the formation of a stable gel system [25].

Furthermore, the crystal structures of the various oleogels were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD). Compared to pure AS-Sit, the characteristic peaks of AS-Sit-Ory, AS-Sit-Gm, and AS-Sit-Cw oleogels at 2θ = 4.670° disappeared (Fig. 2b), indicating molecular reorganization during gel formation, which altered the crystalline packing pattern. Variations in the lipid hydrogen chain arrangement can also induce differences in crystal polymorphism, typically classified into α, β′, and β forms[26]. Previous studies have reported that α crystals exhibit peaks at 4.15 Å, while β′ crystals appear at 3.80 Å and 4.20 Å. As shown in Fig. 2b, the XRD patterns of AS-Sit-Ory, AS-Sit-Gm, and AS-Sit-Cw oleogels display characteristic peaks at 2θ = 19.72°, 19.58°, and 19.17° (corresponding to d-spacings of 4.50 Å, 4.53 Å, and 4.62 Å), consistent with the β crystal type. At lower temperatures, lipids tend to form α crystals, which are thermodynamically unstable and exhibit the lowest melting point. With increasing temperature, these α crystals gradually transform into β′ and β forms. The β form represents the most stable crystalline state, characterized by a high melting point [27]. Notably, the AS-Sit/Cw oleogel also exhibited a peak at 2θ = 21.40° (d = 4.15 Å), suggesting the coexistence of β′ crystals alongside the dominant β form. The stability of β’ crystals is intermediate between that of α and β crystals, and they typically possess smaller grain sizes [28], which aligns with the PLM observations.

To further investigate intermolecular interactions within the oleogel systems, Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to analyze the characteristic absorption peaks. FTIR analysis of Sit and AS-Sit (Fig. 2c) revealed no significant changes in the main absorption peaks of AS-Sit compared to Sit. Specifically, the stretching vibration peak of the -OH group at 3530 cm−1, the symmetric and asymmetric stretching vibrations of CH3 at 2918 cm−1 and 2851 cm−1, and the C=C stretching vibration at 1643 cm−1 remained unchanged. This indicates that the antisolvent precipitation method did not substantially alter the chemical structure of β-sitosterol particles. As shown in Fig. 2d, in the FTIR spectrum of the AS-Sit/Ory oleogel, the hydroxyl peak shifted from 3533 cm−1 to 3401 cm−1 and broadened, indicating the formation of intermolecular hydrogen bonds within the gel system. This observation is consistent with previous reports by Yang et al. [29], who demonstrated that the hydroxyl group of β-sitosterol can form hydrogen bonds with the carbonyl oxygen of oryzanol. Similar hydrogen bonding interactions were observed in the AS-Sit/Gm and AS-Sit/Cw oleogels (Fig. 2e and f), albeit to a lesser extent. Molecular docking analysis further revealed that the AS-Sit/Ory oleogel exhibited the highest binding energy (5.016 kcal/mol), suggesting the strongest hydrogen bond interactions and gel strength among the tested formulations. Although the binding energies of AS-Sit/Gm and AS-Sit/Cw oleogels were comparable, the molecular docking model indicated that two hydrogen bonds were formed in the AS-Sit/Gm system (Fig. 2e), which may account for its increased hardness.

Rheological properties of AS-Sit-based oleogels

To further elucidate the gelation behavior and mechanical performance of the AS-Sit-based oleogels, a comprehensive rheological analysis was conducted. As shown in the apparent viscosity profile (Fig. S2a), all oleogel formulations exhibited pronounced shear-thinning behavior, characterized by a significant decrease in apparent viscosity with increasing shear rate (0.1–100 s−1). Notably, the AS-Sit-Cw oleogel displayed moderate apparent viscosity, which is clinically advantageous as it minimizes the risk of secondary tissue damage during wound debridement. This observation aligns with the texture profile analysis results presented in Table S1. The yield stress of the AS-Sit-Cw oleogel was determined to be 69.96 Pa (Fig. S2b), indicating that minimal force is required to achieve uniform spreading. Furthermore, the AS-Sit-Cw oleogel demonstrated appreciable deformability and recovery capacity (Fig. S2c). Temperature sweep analysis revealed that, in contrast to the other formulations, the AS-Sit-Cw oleogel maintained consistent gel strength throughout repeated heating and cooling cycles, confirming its superior thermal stability (Fig. S2d).

Oil binding capacity (OBC) and oxidative stability of AS-Sit-based oleogels

The structural differences of oleogels significantly influences their capacity to retain oil, a critical parameter quantified as the oil binding capacity (OBC). As presented in Table S1, the OBC values for AS-Sit/Ory, AS-Sit/Gm, and AS-Sit/Cw oleogels were substantially higher than that of the AS-Sit oleogel, reaching 98.01%, 96.48%, and 93.35%, respectively. This enhancement in OBC is attributed to the formation of a robust three-dimensional network within the oleogels, which effectively immobilizes the oil phase. Beyond oil retention, this stable network structure confers additional functional advantages. Specifically, compared to the AS-Sit oleogel, the AS-Sit/Ory, AS-Sit/Gm, and AS-Sit/Cw formulations demonstrated markedly improved resistance to oil oxidation. For instance, the peroxide value (PV) and anisidine value (AV) of the AS-Sit/Cw oleogel were reduced to 54.15 mmol/kg and 30.37, respectively (Fig. S3). These findings indicate that the well-organized gel network effectively mitigates lipid oxidation and delays the onset of rancidity.

In summary, the AS-Sit/Cw oleogel exhibited the most favorable combination of physicochemical properties among the formulations evaluated. The acicular crystalline structure and appropriateintermolecular interactions within the AS-Sit/Cw oleogel contributed to its appropriate hardness and viscosity, excellent stability and spreadability, and notable lipid antioxidant capacity. These attributes collectively position the AS-Sit/Cw oleogel as a promising candidate for the transdermal delivery of curcumin.

Stability and in vitro release behavior of curcumin in oleogel

The release profile of curcumin is a critical determinant of the biological efficacy of oleogel-based delivery systems. Molecular docking simulations revealed that curcumin primarily interacts with AS-Sit molecules through hydrogen bonding (Fig. 3a). In the AS-Sit/Cw oleogel, the incorporation of Cw enhances the hydrogen bonding network within the system, thereby effectively encapsulating curcumin within the gel matrix. This compact gel network structure significantly mitigates the degradation of curcumin induced by external environmental factors. Photostability and thermostability assays demonstrated that the retention rate of curcumin in the AS-Sit/Cw oleogel exceeded that of the AS-Sit oleogel by more than 50% (Fig. 3b and c). Concurrently, the dense and stable gel network structure retards the release of curcumin, resulting in a sustained release profile (Fig. 3d). The Korsmeyer-Peppas kinetic model fitting results show that the release exponent “n” of the AS-Sit/Cw oleogel is close to 1, indicating that the release process is completely controlled by the erosion or swelling front advancement rate of the polymer matrix, exhibiting zero-order constant-release kinetics. This sustained-release characteristic is highly consistent with the prolonged healing process of diabetic wounds, effectively avoiding the cytotoxicity caused by burst release and the discomfort associated with frequent dressing changes for patients. To elucidate the underlying release mechanism of curcumin from the AS-Sit/Cw oleogel, mean square displacement (MSD) analysis was performed over a 500 ps simulation period. Fig. 3e illustrates the comparative release behaviors of curcumin from the two oleogel systems. Under identical simulation conditions, the release rate of curcumin from the AS-Sit oleogel was markedly higher than that from the AS-Sit/Cw oleogel. The linear release curve derived from MSD analysis further corroborates the accelerated release kinetics of curcumin in the AS-Sit oleogel (Fig. S4), with calculated diffusion coefficients of 1.77 × 10−7 and 5.93 × 10−8, respectively. These computational findings are in strong agreement with the experimental results presented in Fig. 3d.

Antioxidant and antibacterial activities of oleogels with varying curcumin contents

To optimize the therapeutic potential of oleogels for wound healing applications, the influence of curcumin loading levels on their biological activities was systematically evaluated. The antioxidant capacity of curcumin-loaded oleogels was preliminarily assessed using DPPH and hydroxyl free radical scavenging assays. As illustrated in Fig. S5a and b, the antioxidant activity exhibited a concentration-dependent enhancement with increasing curcumin content. This positive correlation underscores the pivotal role of curcumin in augmenting the free radical scavenging capability of the oleogel, which is essential for mitigating oxidative stress in wound microenvironments.

Bacterial infection represents a major impediment to wound healing, often leading to suppuration and delayed tissue repair. To evaluate the antimicrobial efficacy of the oleogels, representative bacterial strains (including Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus, Gram-negative Escherichia coli, and clinically isolated Pseudomonas aeruginosa) were employed as model pathogens [30]. The antimicrobial activity of the oleogels was markedly enhanced by curcumin incorporation. As shown in Fig. 4a, curcumin-loaded oleogels demonstrated significant bactericidal effects against all three tested strains. Notably, the bactericidal activity intensified in a curcumin concentration-dependent manner. At a curcumin concentration of 0.15% (w/v), the oleogel nearly eradicated Pseudomonas aeruginosa, while substantially reducing the colony-forming units of Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus (antibacterial rates >85%, Fig. 4b and c). Taking Escherichia coli as an example, it can be seen from Fig. S6 that OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel disrupts the structure of the bacterial membrane, causing it to become wrinkled and even ruptured, thereby effectively killing the bacteria. Furthermore, the antimicrobial activity of OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel against three types of bacteria was determined using the microbroth dilution method. The results showed that the MBC/MIC ratios were 2, 2.5, and 2 respectively, all of which were ≤4, indicating that this oleogel exhibited bactericidal effects against the three types of bacteria (Table S3). Meanwhile, this study established a time-antibacterial model for OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel, further confirming its inhibitory effect on the three tested bacteria. The results in Fig. S7d showed that light had no significant impact on the antibacterial activity of this oleogel, thereby reducing its application limitations.

These findings suggest that curcumin-loaded oleogels, when formulated at optimal concentrations, can serve as effective antimicrobial agents for preventing and treating wound infections, thereby potentially accelerating the healing process. However, it is crucial to determine the optimal curcumin concentration to maximize therapeutic benefits while minimizing potential adverse effects on wound healing or surrounding healthy tissues.

Biocompatibility and hemocompatibility of oleogels with varying curcumin contents

As illustrated in Fig. 4e, it was observed that when the curcumin addition exceeded 0.15% (w/v), significant precipitation of curcumin occurred within the oleogel. The presence of unencapsulated free curcumin may compromise the biological functionality of the oleogel, as it could impair targeted delivery to the wound site or induce unintended cytotoxic effects. Given that biocompatibility is a critical determinant of the clinical applicability of wound dressings, the cytocompatibility of the oleogels was systematically evaluated using three key cell types involved in wound healing: fibroblasts (NIH-3T3), macrophages (RAW 264.7), and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC). These cell lines collectively represent critical phases of wound repair, including proliferation, inflammation modulation, and angiogenesis.

Live/dead staining assays (Fig. 4f) revealed that oleogels containing curcumin concentrations above 0.15% (w/v) resulted in a marked increase in dead cells, with cell viability dropping below 90% (Fig. S8). This finding suggests that excessive curcumin loading adversely affects cellular proliferation and function, potentially hindering the wound healing process. In contrast, at a curcumin concentration of 0.15% (w/v), cell viability remained above 93% for all tested cell lines, meeting the biocompatibility criteria established in the literature [31]. Thus, 0.15% (w/v) was identified as the optimal curcumin concentration to ensure both therapeutic efficacy and cellular safety.

Blood compatibility is another essential safety parameter for wound dressings, as direct contact with blood may trigger adverse reactions. Hemolysis assays were conducted to evaluate the potential of oleogels to induce red blood cell lysis, a standard indicator of hemocompatibility [32]. As shown in Fig. 4g, exposure to deionized water (positive control) resulted in complete hemolysis, evidenced by a red supernatant due to osmotic shock. In contrast, most oleogel formulations exhibited minimal hemolytic, with hemolysis rates below 5%, which complies with the ISO 10993-4 standard for medical devices. Notably, the OG/Cur-0.25% group showed elevated hemolysis, further supporting the conclusion that curcumin concentrations exceeding 0.15% (w/v) may compromise biocompatibility.

Effects of oleogels on cell migration and proliferation under high-glucose-induced oxidative stress

Cell migration and proliferation are fundamental processes in tissue repair and regeneration, particularly in the context of wound healing [33]. Fibroblasts play a pivotal role in this process by synthesizing collagen and other extracellular matrix (ECM) components essential for wound closure and tissue remodeling.

To evaluate the effects of various oleogel formulations on NIH3T3 fibroblast migration, in vitro scratch assays were performed under both normal and high-glucose-induced oxidative stress conditions. The results demonstrated that the OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel significantly promoted cell migration under normal culture conditions (Fig. 5a), achieving a scratch closure rate of 89.56% (Fig. 5b). This finding indicates that curcumin, at a concentration of 0.15% (w/v), markedly enhances the migratory capacity of fibroblasts, a critical factor in wound healing.

Under high-glucose oxidative stress conditions, which simulate the diabetic wound microenvironment, cell migration was substantially impaired. Nevertheless, the OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel maintained its pro-migratory activity, achieving a scratch closure rate of 75.31% (Fig. 5c and d). This suggests that the formulation retains its efficacy even under pathophysiological conditions relevant to diabetic wound healing.

Furthermore, the impact of oleogels on cell proliferation under high-glucose oxidative stress was assessed. As shown in Fig. 5e–h, the OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel group exhibited a significantly higher proliferation rate compared to the control group under both normal and high-glucose conditions. These results underscore the ability of the oleogel, particularly at 0.15% curcumin, to stimulate cell growth and division, which is indispensable for tissue regeneration.

In summary, the OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel demonstrated the most pronounced effects on both cell migration and proliferation—two processes integral to effective wound repair. These findings highlight the potential of this formulation as a promising therapeutic strategy for diabetic wounds, where enhancing cellular activities in the presence of hyperglycemia and oxidative stress is crucial for successful healing outcomes.

Scavenging effects of oleogels on ROS in cells subjected to high-glucose-induced oxidative stress

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are highly reactive molecules that induce oxidative stress, leading to cellular damage, protein denaturation, and DNA fragmentation [34]. In diabetic patients, excessive ROS accumulation is a key contributor to impaired wound healing and the development of chronic wounds. Antioxidants can neutralize ROS, thereby alleviating oxidative stress and facilitating tissue repair.

To evaluate the antioxidant capacity of the oleogels, the ROS-sensitive probe 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) was employed. This non-fluorescent probe undergoes oxidation by ROS to yield a fluorescent product, enabling the visualization and quantification of intracellular ROS levels. As shown in Fig. 5i and j, curcumin-loaded oleogels significantly reduced ROS levels in cells under both normal and high-glucose-induced oxidative stress conditions. This reduction in ROS levels indicates that the oleogels can protect cells from oxidative damage and foster a microenvironment conducive to wound healing. These findings are consistent with previous in vitro antioxidant assays.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that the incorporation of curcumin into oleogels effectively scavenges ROS, thereby mitigating oxidative stress in diabetic wounds. The antioxidant properties of the oleogels, combined with their ability to enhance cell migration and proliferation, position them as promising candidates for advanced wound dressings aimed at improving healing outcomes in diabetic patients.

Modulatory effects of oleogels on macrophage polarization under high-glucose-induced oxidative stress

The immune response plays a pivotal role in wound healing, wherein the balance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory processes is crucial for effective tissue repair [35]. In chronic wounds, particularly those associated with diabetes, persistent pro-inflammatory responses often disrupt this balance, thereby impeding the healing process [36,37]. Macrophages, as key immune cells, exhibit distinct phenotypes: the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype and the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. M1 macrophages secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines that exacerbate tissue damage, whereas M2 macrophages release anti-inflammatory mediators that facilitate tissue repair and remodeling.

As shown in Fig. 5k, fluorescence staining and flow cytometry analyses were employed to evaluate the effects of curcumin-loaded oleogels on macrophage polarization. In the control group, macrophages stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and high glucose predominantly exhibited the M1 phenotype, characterized by elevated expression of the M1 surface marker CD86. In contrast, co-culture with the oleogel, particularly at a curcumin concentration of 0.15% (w/v), resulted in a significant downregulation of CD86 expression. Concurrently, an upregulation of the M2 marker CD206 was observed, indicating a shift toward the M2 phenotype. This phenotypic transition was further corroborated by cytokine profiling, which revealed a marked reduction in the pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α, alongside significant increases in the anti-inflammatory cytokines TGF-β and IL-10 (Fig. 5l).

These findings demonstrate that curcumin-loaded oleogels can effectively modulate the immune microenvironment at the wound site by promoting M2 macrophage polarization. This transition to an anti-inflammatory state facilitates the resolution of inflammation, minimizes tissue damage, and fosters a regenerative microenvironment conducive to wound healing. By enhancing the expression of anti-inflammatory cytokines while suppressing pro-inflammatory mediators, the oleogel holds significant potential for improving the management of chronic wounds and enhancing overall healing outcomes in diabetic patients.

In vivo evaluation of diabetic wound healing with oleogel treatment

To further investigate the therapeutic potential of the OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel in promoting wound healing in vivo, a streptozotocin (STZ)-induced diabetic mouse model was employed. A full-thickness excisional wound was created on the dorsal surface of each mouse to evaluate the efficacy of different gel treatments (PBS as the blank control, and RMC gel as the commercial control) in promoting diabetic wound repair (Fig. 6a and b). Wound closure kinetics were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software, and the temporal progression of wound healing is presented in Fig. 6c. In the PBS-treated group, ulceration was evident by day 3 and persisted until day 7, suggesting impaired wound healing. In contrast, the OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel-treated group exhibited early scab formation by day 3 without any signs of infection. By day 7, the wound area in the OG/Cur-0.15% group was significantly reduced to 15.01% of the original size, representing the most substantial healing effect among all groups. By the end of the experimental period (day 14), wounds in the OG/Cur-0.15% group were nearly completely healed, with only 5.71% of the original wound area remaining. In comparison, the PBS and RMC gel-treated groups showed residual wound areas of 19.02% and 13.41%, respectively (Fig. 6d). Additionally, colony counts from wound tissues isolated and cultured on day 3 demonstrated that the OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel exhibited significant antibacterial effects (Fig. S9). These results collectively demonstrate the superior efficacy of the OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel in accelerating diabetic wound healing.

Analysis of wound tissue staining in diabetic mice

The healing of chronic wounds is a complex and prolonged process involving several key stages, including the resolution of inflammation, stimulation of cell proliferation, acceleration of angiogenesis, and collagen deposition [38]. In the PBS and RMC groups, AB-PAS staining on day 3 showed abnormally elevated levels of blue (acidic mucosubstances) and purple (neutral mucosubstances/glycogen) in the wound tissue, indicating local mucin overdeposition or glycogen metabolism disorders. This change may form a physical barrier and exacerbate the inflammatory microenvironment, thereby impairing wound healing (as shown in Fig. 6e). In contrast, following treatment with OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel, the AB-PAS staining intensity was significantly reduced, indicating that the accumulated mucosubstances and glycogen were effectively cleared, thereby restoring a wound microenvironment conducive to cell migration and re-epithelialization. To further evaluate the inflammatory response in the wound tissue, Hematoxylin-Eosin (HE) staining was performed on the wound tissue on day 7 (Fig. 7). The results showed that in the PBS group, the wound surface was still heavily infiltrated with numerous inflammatory cells, suggesting the presence of a persistent and severe inflammatory reaction [39]. In contrast, the inflammatory response was alleviated in both the OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel group and the RMC gel group, with a gradual decreasing trend in the number of inflammatory cells. Notably, the OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel treatment group exhibited the mildest degree of inflammation. Concurrently, this group also showed a significant increase in epidermal thickness (Fig. S10a), indicating favorable wound healing and effective re-epithelialization. To assess the formation and deposition of collagen within the wound tissue, Masson staining was employed, with the results presented in Fig. 7. On day 7, compared to the other two groups, the OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel treatment group displayed more abundant granulation tissue and denser collagen deposition (Fig. S10b), suggesting a positive role in promoting tissue remodeling. In summary, these findings collectively highlight the crucial role of the OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel in promoting chronic diabetic wound healing by modulating the wound microenvironment, reducing inflammation, and enhancing re-epithelialization and collagen deposition.

In vivo immunomodulatory mechanisms of oleogel in diabetic wound healing

Chronic inflammation represents a major impediment to the healing of diabetic wounds. To elucidate the therapeutic potential of the OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel, we evaluated macrophage polarization in wound tissues. The results demonstrated that this gel significantly downregulated CD86 expression and promoted M2 macrophage polarization (Fig. 7c–e). As key inflammatory markers, IL-6 and IL-10 reflect the local inflammatory status within the wound microenvironment [40]. Immunofluorescence staining performed on day 3 revealed that the OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel markedly reduced IL-6 expression compared to the PBS and RMC groups, indicating suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokine production and mitigation of wound inflammation (Fig. 7c–f, g). Concurrently, the expression of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 was significantly elevated in the OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel group. Collectively, these findings indicate that the OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel effectively modulates the immune response at the wound site, thereby facilitating the healing process in diabetic wounds.

Angiogenesis, the formation of new blood vessels, is a critical phase in the repair of diabetic wounds [41]. To investigate the underlying mechanism by which the OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel enhances angiogenesis, we employed immunofluorescence staining to evaluate the expression levels of VEGF and CD31. As illustrated in Fig. 7c–h, and i, on day 3 post-treatment, the OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel group exhibited significantly higher expression of both CD31 and VEGF compared to the PBS and RMC groups. These results suggest that the OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel effectively stimulates angiogenesis during the early stages of wound repair by upregulating CD31 and VEGF expression in wound tissue, thereby accelerating the overall wound healing process.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a novel multifunctional oleogel (OG/Cur-0.15%) designed to accelerate the healing of diabetic chronic wounds. OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel is constructed from antisolvent β-sitosterol (AS-Sit) particles and candelilla wax. The excellent dispersibility and fine needle-like crystalline structure of AS-Sit particles endowed the oleogel with superior spreadability and stability. Furthermore, the OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel was demonstrated to enhance the stability of curcumin, facilitate a sustained release profile, and thereby improve its bioavailability. The incorporation of curcumin into the oleogel matrix conferred significant antibacterial activity. Additionally, the OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel effectively scavenged reactive oxygen species (ROS) in an oxidative damage cell model, thereby mitigating oxidative stress in cells within chronic wounds. In vitro scratch and cell proliferation assays further confirmed that the OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel promoted the migration and proliferation of NIH3T3 cells under high-glucose and high-ROS conditions. Meanwhile, the oleogel was shown to induce macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype and modulate the expression of inflammatory cytokines. In vivo experiments demonstrated that the OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel accelerated diabetic wound healing by reducing local blood glucose levels, promoting epithelialization and collagen deposition, regulating immune responses, and enhancing angiogenesis. Consequently, the development of OG/Cur-0.15% oleogel holds promise for improving therapeutic outcomes and quality of life for patients through the accelerated healing of diabetic wounds. Furthermore, considering the variability in wound shape and depth encountered in clinical settings, future studies will focus on the development of 3D printable gel formulations to address a broader spectrum of clinical wound types.
