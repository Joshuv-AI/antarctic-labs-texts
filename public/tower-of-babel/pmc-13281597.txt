School belonging in the preschool period: school counsellors’ views and documented institutional practices
Authors: Miray Dağyar, Gamze Kasalak, Gülnur Özbek, Kübra Köleoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSchool belonging is a key protective factor for socioemotional functioning during the preschool period. Counsellors working in preschool institutions play an important role in developing sense of school belonging by meeting their emotional and social needs and fostering a positive school environment. This study examined the views of school counsellors working in preschool education institutions in Türkiye regarding sense of school belonging and, to support these views and to reveal their reflection at the level of institutional practice, analysed routine guidance and counselling documents to identify how belonging-supportive practices are formally planned, implemented, and recorded over the school year.MethodsA phenomenological qualitative design was employed. Data were collected through structured interviews with school counsellors and complemented by document analysis of counselling artefacts to examine how belonging practices are formally planned, implemented, and documented across the school year. The document corpus comprised 186 written artefacts from 10 kindergartens. Data were analysed using computer-aided content analysis, and a hierarchical code–subcode model was utilised in MAXQDA 2020.ResultsFindings indicated that counsellors conceptualised school belonging as children’s feelings of acceptance and security within the school community and described belonging-related differences in children’s emotions, participation, and peer interactions. Counsellors also highlighted multi-level strategies targeting emotional safety, peer relationships, teacher practices, and family involvement. Complementing these findings, the document analysis yielded five practice-oriented domains through which counselling services may support school belonging: (1) emotional safety and adjustment to school routines, (2) support for peer relationships and social problem-solving, (3) cultivation of a positive classroom/school climate through teacher collaboration, (4) systematic family involvement and parent education, and (5) developmental monitoring and evaluation. Document analysis corroborated counsellors’ reported perspectives by providing practice-level evidence of how belonging-supportive strategies were formally embedded in institutional plans and documented in artefacts.ConclusionsPreschool children’s school belonging is supported through a year-long programme of practice spanning children, teachers, and families. Strengthening preventive counselling services that promote emotional safety, peer-inclusion, and family–school collaboration may improve school belonging in early childhood, as this phenomenon has far-reaching implications for children’s educational trajectories, working lives, and broader societal and democratic engagement.

Introduction

According to the hierarchy of needs developed by Abraham Maslow as a theory of human psychology, people have needs that must be fulfilled, and if any of an individual’s needs is not fulfilled, then the individual is under the pressure of that need in terms of personality development. A person who meets his physiological needs for food, water, sleep, sexuality, etc., wishes to feel safe in terms of body, family, health, etc. These needs are followed by social needs such as belonging and gaining esteem [1]. The preschool period is focused on a developmental process in which children begin to learn to meet their social needs as well as their physiological needs. Kindergartens, which are fundamental in the preschool period, systematise the child’s development with specific learning outcomes and accelerate the child’s process of adaptation to school and life. The psychological counselling service provided in kindergartens also aims to support the child in the adaptation process and to enable a healthier development process. Therefore, the preschool guidance programme aims include preparing children for school and life, developing positive attitudes towards the learning process, and enabling them to get to know themselves and develop their potential [2]. The main aims of guidance services in the preschool period reveal the need for the child to feel that he/she belongs to school. In fact, it is emphasised in the literature that the need for belonging has priority in terms of meeting other social needs and the need for security [3, 4].

The word “belonging” is interpreted as the noun form of the verb “to belong”, while in terms of meaning, the word can be defined as the feeling of being accepted by other people and considering oneself as a part of a whole in the environment where one lives. Belonging is a part of individuals’ social needs and can be regarded as a prerequisite for meeting other social needs. Moreover, a sense of belonging provides people with the social support and approval they need, as well as the feeling of being safe in a safe environment. A sense of belonging leads people to accept themselves as part of a group and to feel safe within the group in which they are accepted. Therefore, a sense of belonging can play an important role in meeting other social and security needs. Recent work on the politics of belonging in early childhood emphasises that belonging is co-constructed through everyday institutional practices and power relations, with direct implications for how inclusion is enacted in early educational settings [5].

Guidance and psychological counselling services are formally embedded within the centrally regulated national education system under the Ministry of National Education (MoNE) in Türkiye. In the preschool period, the Guidance Program adopts a developmental, competency- and strength-based approach and is intended to support children’s adaptation to school and its environment, the development of a sense of school belonging, and their self-knowledge, socialisation, self-confidence, school adjustment, and positive attitudes towards school and learning [6, 7]. Within this framework, preschool guidance services are implemented through planned, preventive, and school-wide practices involving children, teachers, and families, and are categorized through preschool class guidance activities [8, 9]. In official regulations, the school counsellor (referred to as “psychological counsellor” in the Turkish context) is defined as the professional responsible for delivering school guidance and psychological counselling services and for coordinating the implementation of the school guidance and psychological counselling programme. The title “school counsellor” is for personnel with at least a bachelor’s degree in counselling-related fields [7]. Professional practice is also governed by a formal ethics directive, that these services are regulated, institutionally defined, and not left to individual interpretation [10].

It is revealed in the literature that school counsellors play an important role in children’s acquisition of school belonging [11, 12]. Evidence from an early childhood education centre in Türkiye also suggests that limited adult knowledge of a ‘pedagogy of belonging’ may constrain children’s social problem-solving and independence, indicating a concrete area for professional support [13]. In an individual whose needs in Maslow’s hierarchy are fully met, it is accepted that there is no obstacle preventing self-actualisation, which is defined as an individual’s self-development at a high level by being aware of his/her full potential [14]. Therefore, what is expected from psychologists is that they determine which unfulfilled need prevents individuals from reaching the stage of self-actualisation and to help them to meet that need [1]. In fact, the roles that school counsellors fulfil to ensure children’s school belonging can be explained within the scope of cognitive, affective, and behavioural characteristics that should be acquired by the child. School counsellors can improve children’s social skills by helping them develop their skills in communicating with people and helping them communicate effectively within a group. They can help children develop a positive attitude towards school, adapt to school, and be accepted as part of the school. In particular, they can play an effective role in solving the problems that children experience with school belonging, and in increasing their motivation and self-efficacy to perform certain behaviours. While fulfilling all these roles, they utilise certain methods and practices that have been scientifically proven to be effective [15].

Theoretical foundations of school belonging

Numerous studies in the literature examine school belonging [16–19]. Recent child-centred research with 3–8-year-olds using the draw–write–tell method shows that children describe belonging primarily through feeling safe, included, and connected via friendship and a caring environment [20]. Similarly, research with children in their first year of formal schooling links belonging to predictable routines, key school spaces, and social engagement during play, underscoring the role of structured environments and relationships in early belonging [18].

Belonging emerges as children’s emotional needs are met, particularly through developing a sense of attachment, being able to express oneself easily, and feeling safe/at ease/happy within the group [3, 21]. This sense is further supported as children develop being part of a group/feeling affinity with a group, perceive themselves as group members, and experience a sense of ownership/assimilation in their social relationships [21, 22]. The development of belonging is strengthened through children’s interactions and play, which enable them to build connections with one another, as well as through the inclusion of children from diverse cultural backgrounds [3, 23]. At the same time, belonging constitutes a fundamental need for children to feel being accepted and to align with social norms [4].

School functions for children both as an obligatory place where they have to go and as a playground, where they develop independence in an environment where they first become independent and build a social networking site through peer interactions [23–25]. In the development of belonging, it is important that school is experienced as a second home and a trusted place, as well as an environment of discovery where children can explore and be able to express themselves easily [25]. However, school may also be perceived as an unfamiliar house/an unfamiliar environment or even a prison, reflecting its potentially restrictive nature alongside its supportive role [24, 26]. Overall, school represents a complex environment in which children establish social relationships, experience being part of a group/feeling affinity with a group, and learn social norms [4, 23].

Children with a low sense of school belonging may exhibit behaviours such as dropout, inability to make friends, lack of motivation, reluctance to participate in activities, and missing their parents; additionally, stress indicators such as nail biting, wetting themselves, and propensity for violence may also emerge [27, 28]. A lack of belonging is also associated with emotional difficulties, including timidity and introversion, distrust of school, and reluctance to go to school [29, 30]. Moreover, children with low school belonging are more likely to develop negative feelings, particularly anxiety, sadness, anger, and restlessness [27, 30, 31].

Children with a high sense of school belonging tend to feel happy at school, take part in activities, take responsibility, and demonstrate showing interest in school and loving school [12, 32, 33]. Moreover, belonging supports having confidence in school and fosters feeling excitement toward learning processes [32]. School belonging is closely associated with students’ having high life satisfaction and emotional well-being, as well as academic success [34], increasing belonging contributes to being enterprising, enhances motivation, and is linked to the reduction of physiological disorders and negative behaviours [33].

A sense of school belonging has a strong positive effect on academic achievement, while also supporting emotional intelligence, self-confidence, and social skills [12, 33, 35]. Students with a high sense of belonging demonstrate a stronger effect on confidence in their environment, experience enhanced effect on friend relationships, and develop more positive effect on feelings for school, which in turn contributes to improved effect on future school life and future work life [29, 33, 36]. Furthermore, school belonging also yields positive effect on family life and effect on social life, while contributing to broader developmental outcomes such as effect on love of nature and animals and overall well-being [33, 36]. Supporting a sense of belonging in school enhances children’s psychosocial adjustment, strengthens positive developmental trajectories, and reduces behavioural problems while improving mental health outcomes [16].

Strategies used to develop a sense of school belonging include strengthening teacher–student relationships through exhibiting positive teacher behaviours, effective communication with child, showing the child trust and empathy, and patience, all of which constitute the foundation of belonging processes [35, 37]. In addition, interventions such as psychological support/guidance, emotional intelligence club, and other socio-emotional learning practices contribute to enhancing children’s emotional regulation and interpersonal competencies, thereby strengthening belonging [12, 31]. Improving the physical condition of classroom, organising an orientation week, and implementing activities such as playing games/organising activities, picnics, breakfasts, music, and drawing pictures help children feel safe, engaged, and socially connected within the school environment [18, 38].

Family–school collaboration through improving school-parent cooperation plays a critical role in fostering belonging, as active parental involvement and supportive attitudes strengthen children’s attachment to school [12, 31]. Furthermore, according to Self-Determination Theory, belonging is supported by satisfying needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness,thus, practices such as responsibility, allowing child to bring a special item to school, and organising activities contribute to these psychological needs [19, 39].

Emotional regulation strategies such as breathing exercises and systematic desensitization also support children’s adaptation processes by enhancing emotional stability and coping skills, thereby reinforcing belonging [35, 40]. Additionally, activities such as music, playing games/organising activities, emotional intelligence club, and drawing pictures function as socio-emotional learning practices that promote engagement and connection [19, 40]. Family support and active parental involvement are among the strongest predictors of students’ sense of school belonging,in particular, family based emotional support plays a critical role in strengthening belonging [12, 34]. School based factors include fair disciplinary practices, school climate, and leadership support, while teachers’ academic and emotional support also contributes to enhancing belonging [12, 34].

In terms of student based factors, individual characteristics such as personality traits (e.g., extraversion, agreeableness), social participation, and intrinsic motivation play a significant role in shaping belonging [12, 41]. Regarding teacher based factors, the quality of teacher–student relationships strengthens students’ sense of belonging by enabling them to feel valued and secure within the school environment [35, 42]. From a socio-ecological perspective, school belonging is understood as a dynamic outcome of ongoing interactions among students, families, schools, and the broader environment,therefore, it requires multidimensional interventions [26, 43].

Significance of the study

A recent systematic review of 86 studies reaffirmed that school belonging is associated with students’ academic, behavioral, and psychological outcomes, while also showing that the literature still pays limited attention to how predictors of belonging interact across ecological levels and how this knowledge can inform evidence-based interventions [44]. In early childhood research, belonging has been conceptualized as children’s sense of feeling safe and “at home,” but recent cross-national evidence also shows that such experiences cannot be taken for granted, as parents and educators continue to report concerns about exclusion and alienation in early education settings [45]. Indeed, recent child-centered studies have further documented that young children describe belonging through concrete experiences such as happiness, safety, friendship, inclusion, caring relationships, familiarity, play, and connection to people and places in school [18, 20]. In addition, the evidence on family–school relationships suggests that children’s engagement and psychological security are strengthened when families and schools communicate, trust one another, and work together,however, this body of work has more often focused on engagement than on belonging itself [46]. Recent research has pointed to an ongoing theory–practice gap in school belonging research, noting that conceptual and ecological models are not always translated clearly into everyday educational practice [47]. This literature shows that although the field has become clearer about why belonging matters and what experiences support it, less is known about how belonging is understood, planned, and documented in routine preschool guidance and counselling practice. The present study addresses this by examining school counsellors’ views with institutional guidance and counselling documents, thereby focusing on how belonging-related support is categorized into themes and represented through practices in preschool settings.

Studies in Türkiye and internationally that focus on the views of guidance and school counsellors regarding school belonging have generally been conducted using qualitative methods, with some supplemented by document analysis. For example, research examining the perspectives of teachers, counsellors, and other educational stakeholders in the international literature indicates that school belonging is strengthened through emotional support, social participation, and collaborative environments [12, 47]. Studies based on document analysis further clarify the relationship between practice and discourse by revealing how counsellors’ strategies are reflected in formal planning and documentation processes [12].

In the international literature, systematic meta-analyses and expert perspectives on the factors supporting school belonging emphasize the challenges in adapting conceptual frameworks to educational contexts and highlight existing gaps in practice [39, 47]. Practices supporting school belonging in guidance and psychological counselling documents predominantly include strategies aimed at enhancing emotional intelligence, promoting social participation, and supporting well-being,however, the alignment between these documents and counsellors’ expressed views may remain limited due to systemic barriers such as role ambiguity and the lack of standardized procedures [11, 12, 39].

Documents such as annual plans and activity files tend to represent school belonging through structured interventions; nevertheless, they may not fully capture the more nuanced strategies employed by counsellors or the contextual challenges they encounter [11, 47]. Overall, fostering school belonging in early childhood requires integrated efforts that encompass both practices reflected in formal documentation and counsellors’ adaptive practices that address emotional and social dimensions [12, 39, 47].

This study aims to examine the views of school counsellors working in preschool education institutions regarding children’s sense of school belonging and how psychological counselling services classify and organise practices into themes that support preschool children’s school belonging. To provide support for these views and to reveal their reflection at the level of institutional practice to counsellors’ perspectives, a document analysis of routine guidance and counselling artefacts produced in preschool settings was also conducted. Documentary materials were analysed to triangulate the interview findings and to examine how belonging-supportive practices are translated into year-long planning and formally recorded as part of service delivery. Specifically, the documents provided insight into how counsellors’ priorities are classified and organised over time, what is emphasised in annual plans, and how practices are coordinated across the school year. Therefore, the present study demonstrates originality by triangulating school counsellors’ views with document analysis in the context of Türkiye, thereby revealing the link between individual perceptions and institutional practices, and addressing a gap that has not yet been sufficiently explored in the global literature.

Method

Research model

A phenomenological design, which is one of the qualitative research methods, was used in the study. Phenomenology is a type of research that examines social phenomena that affect the general population but about which we do not have in-depth knowledge [48]. In this study, preschool children’s sense of school belonging, considered a social phenomenon, was examined in depth based on the opinions of school counsellors. To provide support for the interview and to reveal its reflection at the level of institutional practice, a document analysis of routine guidance and counselling artefacts produced in preschool settings was conducted. Documentary materials offer insight into how counsellors’ intentions are classified and organised over time, what is prioritised in annual planning, and which practices are formally recorded as part of service delivery.

Document analysis provides institutional and practice-level evidence to triangulate counsellors’ views. This multi-source qualitative approach enabled the convergence and expansion of themes by comparing participants’ accounts with documented service delivery practices over time.

Participants and document corpus

The study group consisted of twenty school counsellors employed in kindergartens affiliated with the Ministry of National Education. Participants were recruited using purposive criterion-based sampling as follows: being familiar with the concept of school belonging prior to the interview, having at least two years of professional experience, being employed as a school counsellor in a preschool institution affiliated with the Ministry of National Education, and volunteering to participate in the study. Demographic characteristics are presented in Table 1. Participants' professional experience ranged from 2 to 26 years, reflecting a diverse range of early-career, mid-career, and senior counsellors. Within the study setting, school counsellors are responsible for delivering school guidance and psychological counselling services and coordinating the school guidance programme, while working with children, teachers, school administrators, and families through developmental and preventive practices [6]. As indicated in official regulations, the title “school counsellor” is reserved for personnel who have completed at least a bachelor’s degree in educational psychological services, psychological counselling and guidance, or an equivalent recognised programme [6]. The participants’ educational backgrounds included bachelor’s and postgraduate degrees in counselling-related fields (Table 1).

Data collection and data sources

Data were collected through two complementary sources, as summarised in Table 3: (a) structured interviews with school counsellors and (b) routine guidance and counselling documents obtained from preschool institutions. The structured interview protocol was developed in alignment with the research questions and informed by the school belonging literature [51, 52]. The interview questions focused on counsellors’ conceptualisations of school belonging, observable behavioural indicators, perceived outcomes for children, strategies used to foster belonging, and contextual factors influencing belonging in preschool settings. Interviews followed a structured format to ensure consistency across participants and to facilitate systematic comparison of responses. Two academics with expertise in Psychological Counselling and Guidance reviewed the interview questions. In addition, pilot testing was conducted with two counsellors prior to the main data collection, and the interview form was finalised as 13 open-ended questions organised under five sub-objectives. Documentary materials were collected as institutional records of practice across the school year (see Table 2). All documents were anonymised prior to analysis, digitised, and prepared for qualitative coding. Using these two data sources enabled triangulation by comparing counsellors’ perspectives with documented practices and service delivery records.

Data analysis

Data analysis drew on two data sources: structured interviews with school counsellors and routine guidance and counselling documents collected from kindergartens. Computer-aided qualitative content analysis was used to analyse the data. Computer-aided analysis enables a more systematic examination of large and voluminous textual datasets [53]. Content analysis involves coding and classification to identify patterns and themes in texts, and a coding form can be used to support this process [54].

Interview data were coded in MAXQDA 2020 using a hierarchical code–subcode model, which facilitated the organisation, visualisation, and cross-referencing of qualitative data. Interview responses were systematically coded, and thematic clustering was used to support theme development.

Codes were quantified through frequency counts of coded meaning units. The coding framework and thematic structure, developed through an iterative analysis process, are presented in Table 3 and under the heading of the thematic analysis process. The thematic analysis process serves as an analytical reference for the themes reported in the findings section. Figures of hierarchical models were presented to provide visual clarity for exploring thematic relationships.

Thematic analysis process

The qualitative data obtained in this study were analyzed using the six-phase thematic analysis framework developed by Braun and Clarke [55]. Thematic analysis is an iterative process that involves familiarization with the data, generating codes, and constructing themes from these codes [55, 56]. The thematic analysis process was structured as follows:

Phase 1. Familiarization with the data

At this stage, the researchers conducted an in-depth reading of the structured interview transcripts obtained from 20 school counsellors and 186 guidance documents (e.g., annual plans, activity files, monitoring forms) collected from 10 independent preschools. During the data immersion process, initial notes and reflective observations were recorded regarding how participants defined belonging and how this concept was concretely represented in documents through practices such as orientation weeks or parental involvement.

Phase 2. Generating initial codes

The dataset was systematically coded using MAXQDA 2020 software. A hybrid approach was adopted, combining deductive codes derived from the literature and the Ministry of National Education (MoNE) guidance framework with inductive codes emerging directly from the data [6, 7, 49]. Code frequency (f) was calculated based on the number of coded meaning units identified across the dataset. Each meaningful segment was counted when it represented a distinct idea, rather than a simple repetition of the same expression. Thus, frequency values indicate the degree to which each code is supported by the data. For example,

P1’s statement, “It means feeling safe in a place,” was coded under the Feeling safe/at ease/happy code, whereas P15’s statement, “It means feeling connected to a place, it is the emotion of feeling happier and safer in that place,” was coded as a separate meaning unit reflecting both Being loved in a group/feeling connected to a group and Feeling safe/at ease/happy dimensions. Similarly, P4’s statement, “It can be adopting that place or that person, finding a part of yourself, thinking the same things, or feeling more comfortable there,” was treated as separate meaning units under relevant codes such as Sense of ownership/assimilation and Being able to express oneself easily. Accordingly, frequency values reflect not superficial repetitions of participants’ statements, but the total number of meaningful and distinct coded data segments within the dataset.

Phase 3. Searching for themes

The generated codes were clustered to form broader patterns of meaning in line with the research questions. Related codes were grouped together to generate potential themes. At this stage, thematic mapping was employed to visualize how codes interacted with or contradicted one another. The visual models presented in Figures were designed not merely as lists of finalized codes but as coherent representations of their logical and analytical relationships.

Phase 4. Reviewing themes

The validity of the candidate themes was evaluated at two levels: first, the coherence between coded data (extracts) and the themes (internal homogeneity); and second, the distinctiveness between themes (external heterogeneity). The alignment of themes with the dataset and their internal consistency were carefully examined. At this stage, triangulation was conducted between interview data and document analysis findings to explore how the data corroborated or extended one another. In brief, document-based themes were compared with interview-based themes to analyze corroboration, expansion, and divergence. This triangulation enhanced the credibility of the interpretations. Inconsistent data were also reported to reduce confirmatory bias.

To ensure reliability, inter-coder agreement was established with an independent coder, yielding a strong level of agreement (Cohen’s kappa κ = 0.85). Although the overall kappa coefficient indicated strong agreement, the analytical validity of each theme and its grounding in the data were further verified in line with the qualitative rigor standards proposed by Ahmed et al. [56]. Clear operational definitions and scope boundaries were established for each theme, ensuring internal homogeneity and external heterogeneity. Evidence supporting the existence of themes was strengthened through triangulation of interview findings with 186 institutional documents, demonstrating that each theme was corroborated across both participant accounts and official records. Coding discrepancies were resolved through iterative discussions and consensus-building cycles among the researchers, in accordance with the principle of reflexivity emphasized in the 16-item checklist proposed by Ahmed et al. [56], ensuring full agreement that the data supported the essence of each theme.

Phase 5. Defining and naming themes

The essence of each theme was clarified, and comprehensive definitions and final names were assigned. Detailed summaries were written for each theme, explaining what it represents and how it contributes to the research question. The scope and boundaries of each theme were explicitly defined, resulting in the following five main themes:

Documented school belonging practices and associations with interview themes

Emotional safety and adjustment to school routines

Documents showed that practices designed to strengthen children’s emotional security and facilitate adjustment to school routines were prioritised, particularly at the beginning of the academic year. Adaptation-week plans and related activities aimed to promote positive perceptions of school and reduce uncertainty during children’s transition into formal schooling. Many documents specifically targeted children’s familiarisation with the physical school environment through guided tours of classrooms, play areas, toilets, gardens, and shared spaces (KS3, Adaptation Week Activity Plan). In addition, relationship-building activities were widely documented, including name games, introductory activities, and dramatic play, aimed at fostering trust among teachers and school staff (KS7, Group Guidance Activity File). Some teacher-oriented information notes also emphasised interactional practices such as maintaining eye contact, using children’s names frequently, and providing positive feedback to support emotional safety (KS1, Teacher Information Notes). A further subtheme concerned reducing separation anxiety among newly enrolled children through emotion-card activities, separation-themed storytelling, and short caregiver-supported adjustment routines (KS5, Social–Emotional Development Activity File). Evaluation records additionally noted decreased crying and increased time spent in the classroom following these practices (KS9, Guidance Services Evaluation Report). Overall, the documents indicate that emotional safety is treated as a foundational component of belonging and is actively supported through structured and recorded adjustment practices.

Support for peer relationships and social problem-solving

A second domain consisted of guidance activities designed to strengthen children’s peer relationships and social participation throughout the school year. Group guidance plans frequently included cooperative, play-based activities focused on sharing, turn-taking, and collaboration, with the aim of increasing children’s classroom interaction and mutual acceptance (KS2, Annual Group Guidance Plan). The activity evaluation forms described these processes as supporting peer interaction and contributing to children’s sense of belonging to the classroom community (KS6, Activity Evaluation Forms). Another prominent practice set involved socioemotional learning through drama activities, which targeted emotion recognition and expression and were intended to increase empathy and supportive peer responses (KS4, Social–Emotional Development Activity File). Interim evaluation reports documented observational notes suggesting that children displayed more understanding and supportive behaviours toward peers following these activities (KS8, Mid-Year Guidance Evaluation Report). The documents also highlighted structured problem-solving and conflict-resolution practices, in which everyday peer conflicts were treated as learning opportunities. These practices aimed to support children in communicating needs, proposing solutions, and developing reconciliation-oriented behaviours (KS10, Guidance Implementation Plan). Teacher feedback forms further suggested that these practices contributed to reduced classroom conflict and more positive peer relationships (KS5, Teacher Feedback Forms). Collectively, documentary records indicate that peer belonging was supported through systematic planning of social competence activities rather than isolated or ad-hoc interventions.

Cultivation of a positive classroom/school climate through teacher collaboration

Documentary materials suggested that school belonging was addressed not only through child-focused activities but also through structured teacher collaboration and teacher-facing guidance. Teacher-oriented presentations and information notes emphasised that children’s perceptions of school are shaped by classroom climate and teacher practices. Key topics included building a climate of respect, acceptance, emotional responsiveness, and reinforcement of positive behaviours (KS2, Teacher Guidance Presentation). Guidance activity reports also noted that positive classroom climate was considered a contributor to children’s willingness to attend school and actively participate in classroom activities (KS6, Guidance Services Activity Report). A further focus involved recognising the emotional needs underlying children’s behaviours. Teacher information notes described behaviours such as withdrawal, crying, aggression, and difficulties with rule compliance as potentially linked to adjustment and belonging processes (KS4, Teacher Information Notes). In addition, documents emphasised inclusive teacher practices, including avoiding exclusionary attitudes and affirming individual differences to support children experiencing adjustment difficulties (KS9, Guidance Service Training Materials). Year-end evaluation reports also documented the view that inclusive classroom practices can strengthen children's sense of belonging among those who face challenges during adaptation (KS1, End-of-Year Guidance Evaluation Report). These findings suggest that belonging is conceptualised institutionally as a process shaped by teacher practices and classroom-level routines.

Systematic family involvement and parent education

A fourth domain reflected structured efforts to involve families as active partners in children’s adjustment and belonging. Annual guidance plans and parent-oriented materials indicated that counselling services were designed to extend beyond the school context and incorporate a home–school partnership perspective. Parent seminar presentations and informational bulletins emphasised that parental attitudes toward school and teachers influence children’s perceptions of school directly (KS3, Parent Education Seminar Presentation). Documents highlighted that presenting school as safe and supportive, and using positive and encouraging language when discussing school at home, were expected to reduce separation anxiety and support adjustment (KS7, Parent Information Bulletin). Annual plans also documented scheduled meetings, individual consultations, and regular communication routines aimed at strengthening family–school collaboration (KS1, Annual Guidance Services Plan). End-of-year evaluation reports included observations suggesting that in contexts where consistent home–school communication was established, children showed increased willingness to attend school and higher participation in classroom activities (KS10, End-of-Year Guidance Evaluation Report). Parent education materials additionally encouraged parents to listen to children’s school experiences, reinforce positive events, and support children in feeling valued within the school context (KS5, Parent Education Materials). Overall, the documents portray belonging as co-constructed across home and school through planned family involvement and structured counselling practices.

Developmental monitoring and evaluation

Finally, documentary records indicated that some institutions implemented systematic monitoring and evaluation practices to support children’s social–emotional development and school adjustment over time. Observation forms included indicators related to participation in classroom activities, peer interactions, relationships with teachers, and emotional responses to school routines (KS6, Student Observation Forms). In several settings, these monitoring tools were completed periodically, and the data were incorporated into institutional monitoring and evaluation reports (KS2, Guidance Monitoring Report). Importantly, monitoring practices were described not only as descriptive but also as preventive, aligning with a preventive guidance approach. Evaluation reports documented that children displaying limited participation or social–emotional adjustment difficulties were identified early and directed to supportive guidance practices (KS9, Guidance Services Evaluation Report). Some documents also indicated that monitoring outcomes were shared with teachers to inform and provide targeted recommendations (KS4, Teacher Feedback Forms). In addition, guidance notes suggested that families were involved in some monitoring processes through consultation records, enabling support strategies to extend into the home environment (KS8, Family Consultation Records). This domain provides evidence that belonging-supportive practices were sustained through ongoing documentation, follow-up, and responsive intervention.

Comparison of document analysis and school counsellors’ perspectives

An examination of the documents included in the study suggests that, rather than a direct contradiction, there are notable differences between school school counsellors’ interview data and institutional document analysis in terms of focus and level of detail. While institutional documents tend to foreground the operational and bureaucratic dimensions of practice, school counsellors more frequently emphasize relational and emotional aspects in their accounts.

Several key differences emerge between the interview data and the document analysis, as well as elements that are clearly present in the documents but not explicitly articulated by school counsellors.

One of the most visible differences concerns systematic monitoring and evaluation cycles. The institutional documents indicate that practices supporting school belonging are not limited to isolated activities; rather, they are embedded within structured processes involving observation forms, periodic tracking tools, and formal institutional reports. These documents reflect a continuous cycle of observation, planning, and follow-up. However, in school counsellors’ narratives, such formalized documentation processes are less explicitly addressed. Instead, school counsellors tend to focus on developing a sense of attachment, feeling safe/at ease/happy, and relational dynamics within their professional practice.

Another important difference relates to the timing and frequency of processes within the planning dimension. Annual plans and orientation week reports provide detailed information about how activities are distributed across the academic year, including their sequencing and frequency. These temporal and organizational aspects are clearly visible in the documents. In contrast, school counsellors tend to place greater emphasis on the nature and quality of practices rather than on how these practices are structured over time.

Preventive intervention and data sharing also represent a distinct area of divergence. The documents include structured tools designed for the early identification of children experiencing adjustment difficulties, as well as institutional mechanisms for sharing this information with teachers. This data-driven preventive guidance approach is prominently reflected in institutional records. However, such bureaucratic and monitoring-oriented processes are less visible in school counsellors’ descriptions of their work.

Finally, differences in institutional and relational language use are also evident. School counsellors generally describe their professional practices using relational and affective terms such as feeling safe/at ease/happy, being accepted, and engaging in trust-based interactions. In contrast, institutional documents tend to articulate similar practices through procedural and bureaucratic terminology, including references to annual activity plans, monitoring reports, and evaluation tools.

Discussion

This study examined how school counsellors conceptualise school belonging in the preschool period, including the associated behaviours, perceived effects, strategies, and influencing factors, and how belonging-supportive practices are represented and organised in guidance and counselling artefacts in ways that align with school counsellors’ reported views and strategies. The present results extend previous established literature by showing how these belonging-related principles are translated into routine counselling practices in preschool institutions. While school counsellors highlighted the centrality of emotional safety, peer inclusion, teacher collaboration, and family engagement in developing school belonging (RQ1), the document analysis (RQ2) provided support for these views and revealed their reflection at the level of institutional practice, illustrating how these intentions are organised into thematic categories and represented through formalised guidance plans and recorded practices across the school year. Specifically, the institutional documents yielded five practice-oriented domains through which counselling services may support children’s school belonging. This integrated pattern indicates that belonging support in preschool settings is not limited to isolated interventions; rather, it is enacted as a coordinated programme of practice distributed across time and across actors (school counsellors, teachers, and families).

This study contributes to the literature by addressing a gap between what recent studies have shown about the meaning and value of belonging in early childhood and what remains limited in routine preschool guidance and counselling practice. Recent studies have documented that young children experience belonging through feeling safe, happy, included, and cared for, as well as through friendships, familiarity, play, and relationships with others [18, 20]. Other work has shown that belonging in early education cannot be assumed, since exclusion and alienation remain concerns for some children and are noticed by both parents and educators [45]. Review studies have also emphasized that stronger family–school relationships can support children’s engagement and psychological security, especially when communication and trust are present between adults in the child’s environment [46]. At a broader level, recent reviews have argued that the field still needs more work to link knowledge about belonging to evidence-based educational practices and interventions [44, 47]. In this context, the present study adds to the literature by showing how school belonging is reflected not only in views and relationships, but also in the routine structures of preschool guidance and counselling, including planning, family communication, teacher collaboration, and documentation practices.

From a triangulation perspective, strong convergence was observed between school counsellors’ views and the documentary evidence, regarding adaptation-week practices, socioemotional activity plans supporting peer interactions, and family involvement practices. At the same time, the documentary corpus expanded the interview findings by providing greater practice-level specificity about sequencing, frequency, and recorded follow-up procedures. In particular, the prominence of monitoring and evaluation documents suggests that belonging-related behaviours are often addressed through systematic observation and documentation cycles, which may be less explicitly emphasised in school counsellors’ narratives but appear central in institutional record-keeping. These results strengthen the interpretation that preschool belonging is simultaneously relational (children’s feelings of safety, acceptance, and connection) and organisational (embedded in planned routines, documented services, and collaborative guidance practices). The data sources did not foreground the same aspects of practice to the same degree. In the interviews, school counsellors more often emphasised the relational and affective dimensions of belonging, such as children’s emotional safety, peer acceptance, teacher sensitivity, and family trust. By contrast, the routine documents more clearly reflected the procedural and institutional side of support, including planned annual activities, observation records, evaluation forms, and follow-up practices. This does not indicate contradiction; rather, it suggests that some belonging-related work may be experienced and described by school counsellors primarily in relational terms, while being documented institutionally through formal planning and monitoring routines. This approach is important because it associates the lived, perceived language of practice with the bureaucratic language through officially recorded practice.

Belonging is the need for individuals to be accepted by other members of society and to feel valued and important [14]. A person’s sense of belonging to a group indicates that the person is a member of that group, and the individual who feels a sense of belonging feels accepted, understood, cared for, and supported by the group he/she is a member of. Similarly, the individual’s sense of belonging to school means that he/she perceive him/herself as a member of the school. School belonging is a feeling that expresses a student’s compliance and commitment to his/her school and to other students, teachers, and staff that form the school community [39, 52, 58].

A sense of belonging leads students to wish to adapt to the school and its goals. Particularly in the pre-school period, children’s sense of school belonging supports them in developing a positive attitude and sense of belonging towards the schools where they will be educated in the following years. If children develop a positive attitude towards school, they are more likely to want to attend school. This can in turn lead to lifelong outcomes, such as children being successful throughout their education life and having better job opportunities [59].

Sense of belonging ensures that children are determined to show effort and willingness to engage in complex and challenging cognitive activities [60] and thus to be academically successful [34]. It also increases their school satisfaction [61], self-efficacy perceptions [51], and motivation [52, 61]. Children with a high sense of belonging feel safe and accepted. While it ensures that the child’s feelings towards school, such as being happy, willing, and loving, are positive, it also ensures that negative emotions, such as uneasiness, sadness, and anxiety, decrease in the child [52]. Consequently, this helps children meet their basic needs for a healthy psychological development. Children with a high sense of belonging communicate better with other children and adults and improve their social skills [62]. While performing the desired behaviours, they are determined and spend time and effort [17]. They have a high tendency to stay on task and complete the given task [63].

School counsellors and teachers can consolidate school belonging by encouraging students’ active participation in school, creating a positive and supportive environment, and commending students on their achievements. Involving students in classroom activities and interacting positively with them, respecting them and making them feel this respect can increase students’ sense of commitment to school [64, 65]. However, students’ personal characteristics and emotional states can affect their sense of school belonging. Factors such as self-esteem, self-confidence, motivation to learn and academic achievement can shape students’ sense of school belonging [66]. Moreover, students’ interests, goals and individual skills can also affect school belonging. Students’ peer relationships and student–teacher interactions are also important factors affecting school belonging. Positive peer relationships can increase students’ sense of commitment to school [67]. The support and interest shown to students by their teachers can also make students feel that they belong to school. A warm, supportive environment created by teachers in the classroom can positively affect school belonging [68]. School culture, management and family support are also among the factors that affect school belonging. If the school provides an environment that encourages tolerance and diversity, then students can increase their sense of belonging. Furthermore, the involvement of families in school processes and a supportive home environment can also strengthen school belonging [64].

As in Türkiye, family involvement emerges as a critical component in education systems with diverse structures, particularly in ensuring children’s emotional security and strengthening school–family continuity [69, 70]. Global child protection policies and emotional well-being strategies prioritize feeling safe/at ease/happy and family involvement as core components of school belonging, aiming to enhance children’s social-emotional development and school adjustment [71, 72].

In particular, emotional security can be fostered through supportive environments that are collaboratively established by teachers, guidance services, and families, enabling children to feel safe/at ease/happy and to develop a stronger sense of attachment [12, 45]. In this regard, emotional security is maintained through supportive school climates that help children feel safe/at ease/happy, while family involvement further strengthens school belonging by encouraging children’s active participation in learning processes [73, 74].

Across different education systems, the implementation of these components requires flexible and inclusive programs that take into account local cultural values and family structures. Such approaches may help reduce the risk of social exclusion and increase the effectiveness of belonging-oriented policies [45, 69]. Indeed, programs implemented in various contexts often adopt collaborative models that integrate families’ cultural experiences and home-based knowledge into school settings, thereby aiming to reduce power imbalances in school–family relationships [74]. Furthermore, enhancing emotional security in schools requires that teachers and guidance services remain responsive to children’s individual needs and develop strategies to address linguistic and cultural barriers [3, 4].

In conclusion, strengthening school belonging in a global context requires holistic approaches that emphasize family involvement and emotional security. These approaches should be aligned with international child protection and well-being policies [45, 70, 71].

Recent early-childhood research also supports Consellors’ emphasis on creating safe, supportive, and relationship-rich preschool environments to foster a sense of belonging. Child-centered studies employing the draw–write–tell approach suggest that the early childhood period is typically characterized by a sense of belonging, fostered through feelings of safety, inclusion, and connection, which are facilitated by friendships and caring adults [20]. Similarly, working with children in their first year of formal schooling involves establishing a sense of belonging through predictable routines, familiar spaces, and social engagement during play, highlighting practical levers such as consistent routines and opportunities for positive peer interaction [18]. At the same time, the “politics of belonging” perspective emphasises that belonging can be shaped by everyday institutional practices and subtle boundary-making that legitimises inclusion or exclusion, reinforcing the need for intentional, equity-oriented classroom routines and adult facilitation [4, 5]. Evidence suggests that strengthening educators’ and school counsellors’ “pedagogy of belonging” knowledge may be especially important for supporting children’s independence and social problem-solving [13].

Document analysis corroborated and organised school counsellors’ views into thematic categories by showing that belonging-supportive practices were distributed across the year, embedded in routine plans, and recorded through monitoring and evaluation artefacts. However, the relatively limited emphasis on meticulous record-keeping and monitoring processes in school counsellors’ narratives suggests that, in practice, such procedures may sometimes be perceived as routine administrative tasks, whereas the actual impact lies in the relational qualities emphasized by school counsellors. This indicates that written policies (theoretical/document-based frameworks) provide a structural foundation, while practitioners in the field interpret and enact these structures primarily through human relationships. The limited reflection of “monitoring and evaluation” processes in school counsellors’ accounts further suggests that these mechanisms need to be transformed into more effective and responsive feedback cycles in practice.

The findings may be considered transferable to contexts in which preschool guidance is organised as a developmental and preventive service and where family–school collaboration is formally embedded in institutional practice, as in this study’s national context. This study’s national regulations define preschool guidance and psychological counselling services in relation to children’s socialisation, self-confidence, school adjustment, and positive attitudes toward learning, and they require planned guidance activities, evaluation processes, and parent-oriented practices within preschool institutions [6, 49]. Even though the themes identified in this study, such as emotional safety, peer relations, teacher support, and family–school trust, are consistent with recent international research and can be generalized to education systems in other countries, specific expressions may vary depending on how counselling services, family participation, and preschool support structures are organized across international systems.

Conclusion

This study examined the views of school counsellors working in preschool institutions regarding children’s sense of school belonging, including how they conceptualize belonging, how they describe the behaviours of children with and without a sense of belonging, the perceived effects of belonging, the strategies used to support it, and the factors influencing it. The findings indicate that participants attribute an important role to school counsellors in supporting children’s sense of school belonging, particularly by addressing children’s emotional and social needs and fostering a positive classroom and school climate.

School belonging was consistently described by participants as being associated with positive behavioural and emotional outcomes in children. In this regard, participants reported implementing a range of practices aimed at supporting children’s sense of belonging, particularly during the initial period of school adjustment, followed by ongoing supportive efforts throughout the school year. These practices appear to reflect an emphasis on both preventive and developmental approaches within school counselling services.

In addition, the document analysis suggests that these practices are not limited to individual perspectives but are also embedded in formal guidance plans and institutional documents. This alignment between reported practices and documented plans points to a structured approach to supporting school belonging within preschool settings. Overall, the findings contribute to a better understanding of how school counsellors conceptualize and support school belonging in early childhood education contexts.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the study group consisted of school counsellors whose prior knowledge of school belonging had been confirmed before data collection. This may have influenced the findings, as participants’ responses largely aligned with the existing literature, potentially limiting the emergence of diverse or contrasting perspectives.

Second, the study relied on self-reported data obtained through interviews. As with all self-report methods, the findings may be affected by response biases, including social desirability. In addition, the absence of observational or longitudinal data limits the ability to draw conclusions about actual practices or the long-term effects of school belonging on children.

Third, the study was conducted with school counsellors working in kindergartens within a specific province. This context-specific focus may limit the transferability of the findings to other regions, school types, or educational contexts.

Finally, the document analysis was based on formal institutional records, which primarily reflect planned and documented practices. Such materials may not fully capture informal, spontaneous, or relational processes that also play a significant role in the development of children’s sense of school belonging. Therefore, potential discrepancies between documented practices and their implementation in everyday school settings should be taken into consideration.

Implications

The findings of this study offer several implications for research and practice. First, future studies may benefit from including participants with varying levels of prior knowledge of school belonging and from employing more diverse sampling strategies. Examining how different levels of knowledge influence professional perspectives and practices may provide a more nuanced understanding of the topic.

Second, although participants’ views were generally consistent with the literature, further research is needed to explore how this knowledge is translated into practice. In this regard, observational or action research designs may provide valuable insights into how school belonging is supported in real classroom contexts, particularly during the initial adjustment period in preschool settings.

Third, the relatively low proportion of participants with postgraduate education suggests the need to further examine the relationship between educational background and professional practice. Exploring this relationship may contribute to the development of more targeted professional development opportunities for school counsellors.

In addition, the document analysis points to several practice-oriented areas that may be relevant for supporting school belonging, such as adaptation processes, emotional safety practices, peer interactions, and family involvement. These areas may serve as reference points for developing preventive and supportive interventions; however, their effectiveness should be examined through further empirical research.

Finally, future research may benefit from integrating multiple data sources, including observations and child-focused measures, in order to better understand how school belonging is experienced and supported in everyday educational settings.
