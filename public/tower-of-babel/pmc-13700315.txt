Prior CABG and native-vessel CTO PCI: lesion complexity, strategy planning, procedural burden, and technical success in a multicenter registry
Authors: Semi Ozturk, Ahmet Seyfeddin Gurbuz, Sefa Sural, Sule Goktekin, Sevket Gorgulu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrior coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) has been associated with greater lesion complexity and lower success rates in chronic total occlusion (CTO) percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). In native-vessel CTO PCI, prior CABG may also indicate greater preprocedural complexity and more advanced strategy planning. We examined whether prior CABG was associated with lesion complexity, strategy selection, procedural burden, and technical success in a contemporary multicenter registry.MethodsWe performed a retrospective analysis of a prospective multicenter CTO PCI registry including 1,800 consecutive native-vessel procedures performed between January 2020 and June 2025. Of these, 205 were performed in patients with prior CABG and 1,595 in patients without prior CABG. The primary endpoint was technical success, defined as final TIMI 3 flow with residual stenosis < 30%. The key secondary endpoint was procedural success, defined as technical success without in-hospital major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events.ResultsPrior-CABG procedures had greater lesion complexity, more frequent planned retrograde/hybrid crossing, longer procedural and fluoroscopy times, higher dose-area product, and greater adjusted contrast use. Technical success was lower in prior-CABG procedures (85.9% vs. 93.3%, p < 0.001), as was procedural success (84.9% vs. 92.4%, p < 0.001). In multivariable analysis, prior CABG was independently associated with technical failure (adjusted OR, 1.94; 95% CI, 1.19–3.18; p = 0.008). Planned retrograde/hybrid strategy was also associated with technical failure (adjusted OR 2.82, 95% CI 1.81–4.40, p < 0.001), a finding interpreted as reflecting case selection and lesion difficulty rather than a causal effect of the strategy. In a companion model, prior CABG was independently associated with the selection of a planned retrograde/hybrid strategy (adjusted OR 2.03, 95% CI 1.40–2.96, p < 0.001).ConclusionsIn native-vessel CTO PCI, prior CABG was associated with more complex lesions, more frequent retrograde/hybrid planning, greater procedural burden, and lower technical success. However, its incremental prognostic value beyond the J-CTO score was limited. Prior CABG should therefore be interpreted primarily as a marker of an unfavorable procedural phenotype rather than as a standalone risk-stratification tool.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06355-x.

Introduction

Chronic total occlusion (CTO) percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) has become a dedicated field within interventional cardiology, supported by contemporary crossing algorithms, specialized devices, and lesion-difficulty scores such as J-CTO, PROGRESS-CTO, and EuroCTO (CASTLE) [1–4]. In experienced centers, these advances have improved success rates despite substantial anatomical complexity [4, 5].

Patients with prior coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) represent a distinct subgroup among those undergoing native-vessel CTO PCI. Previous studies have shown that these procedures are often associated with longer lesions, more calcification, more frequent retrograde treatment, greater procedural burden, and lower overall success [6, 7]. However, the importance of prior CABG may extend beyond surgical history alone. In daily practice, prior CABG may also identify procedures in which greater lesion complexity, more demanding strategy planning, and heavier procedural burden are already apparent before wire escalation.

Clinically, this may matter for preprocedural planning and patient counseling. If prior CABG identifies a more complex native-vessel CTO PCI phenotype, it may help guide case selection, crossing strategy, patient counseling, referral decisions, and expectations regarding radiation and contrast burden. At the same time, strategy-related findings in this setting should be interpreted with caution, because planned retrograde or hybrid use is more likely to reflect anticipated lesion complexity and procedural escalation than a treatment effect alone.

We therefore analyzed a contemporary multicenter native-vessel CTO PCI registry to determine whether prior CABG was associated with lesion complexity, strategy selection, procedural burden, and lower technical success, and to assess whether it provided incremental information beyond the J-CTO score. Technical success was the principal efficacy endpoint; procedural success was examined as a secondary endpoint.

Methods

Study design and population

This study was a retrospective analysis of a prospective, multicenter CTO PCI registry that included consecutive native-vessel procedures performed between January 2020 and June 2025. All procedures included in the present native-vessel analytic cohort were performed in tertiary centers with active CTO PCI programs by dedicated CTO operators. Annual CTO PCI volume exceeded 100 procedures in each participating CTO program, and annual non-CTO PCI volume exceeded 300 procedures; thus, the participating programs exceeded the EuroCTO Club criterion of at least 50 CTO PCI procedures per year for experienced CTO practice [4]. Center/operator identifiers were not included in the exported analytic dataset and therefore could not be used for center- or operator-adjusted modeling. The raw registry export contained 1,856 records. Of these, 56 with non-native or uncodable target-vessel coding were excluded, leaving 1,800 native-vessel CTO PCI procedures in the analytic cohort. Prior CABG was defined as a documented history of surgical coronary revascularization before the index CTO PCI. The registry captured whether the CTO target vessel was within a CABG territory, but graft patency, graft type, number of grafts, and time from CABG to CTO PCI were not systematically available. Using the available RegistryID field, 1,764 unique registry identifiers were present; 36 identifiers contributed two native-vessel CTO PCI procedure records, all on the same procedure date and involving different main CTO target arteries. These records were retained as separate procedure-level observations.

CTO was defined as a complete absence of antegrade flow across the occluded segment, with an estimated or documented occlusion duration of at least 3 months [4]. The target vessel was categorized as left anterior descending (LAD), left circumflex (LCx), or right coronary artery (RCA).

Angiographic variables

Angiographic variables were coded using prespecified registry definitions aligned with the J-CTO framework [1, 4]. The J-CTO score was calculated using the original Morino scoring system [1]. Lesion characteristics evaluated in the present analysis included CTO length, moderate/severe calcification, blunt stump or proximal cap ambiguity, severe proximal tortuosity, distal vessel opacification, and in-CTO bending.

Outcomes and definitions

The primary endpoint was technical success, defined as final TIMI 3 flow with residual stenosis < 30% in the target vessel.

The key secondary endpoint was procedural success, defined as technical success without in-hospital major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (MACCE). In-hospital MACCE was defined as all-cause death, myocardial infarction, stroke, emergency repeat PCI, or emergency CABG during the index hospitalization.

Additional secondary analyses examined planned retrograde/hybrid strategy, actual retrograde involvement, procedural duration, fluoroscopy time, dose-area product (DAP), contrast volume, donor-vessel complication, contrast-associated acute kidney injury (CA-AKI), intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) use, and in-hospital death. Optical coherence tomography was not systematically captured in the registry.

Correct wire crossing was defined according to the site-reported registry coding field as successful guidewire passage through the CTO body into the true distal lumen; independent core-laboratory adjudication was not performed.

CA-AKI was evaluated descriptively when baseline and post-procedure creatinine values were available and was defined as an increase in serum creatinine of at least 0.3 mg/dL during the index hospitalization [8, 9].

Planned and actual strategy variables

Planned crossing strategy was recorded at the start of the procedure as primary antegrade, primary retrograde, antegrade-to-retrograde, or retrograde-to-antegrade. For analysis, the latter three categories were grouped as planned retrograde/hybrid.

Actual retrograde involvement was defined as any documented retrograde microcatheter use, retrograde approach method, or retrograde result. It was analyzed separately to distinguish preprocedural strategy intention from actual procedural conduct. The concordance between planned strategy and actual retrograde involvement is shown in Supplementary Table S1.

Among procedures with actual retrograde involvement, the retrograde pathway was descriptively coded as septal, septoseptal, atrial, epicardial, apical, ipsilateral, via bypass direct, or via bypass collateral when pathway information was available.

Because planned strategy may reflect lesion complexity, operator judgment, and center practice, analyses involving strategy selection were interpreted as descriptive associations rather than causal treatment-effect analyses.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range) or mean ± standard deviation, as appropriate, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Group comparisons used the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous or ordinal variables and the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

To examine the clinical relevance of prior CABG, we evaluated four related domains: baseline lesion characteristics, strategy selection, procedural burden, and success outcomes. First, we compared baseline clinical and angiographic characteristics according to prior CABG status. Second, we modeled the selection of planned retrograde/hybrid strategies using multivariable logistic regression. Third, we examined procedural-burden outcomes using adjusted log-linear models of fluoroscopy time, procedural duration, DAP, and contrast volume. Fourth, we modeled technical failure as the primary adjusted outcome and procedural failure as a key secondary adjusted outcome using multivariable logistic regression.

For technical failure, we evaluated two related multivariable specifications. A clinical/angiographic model included prior CABG, age, sex, diabetes mellitus, baseline creatinine > 1.5 mg/dL, left ventricular ejection fraction ≤ 35%, J-CTO score, good distal vessel opacification, and target vessel. The strategy-inclusive model additionally included planned retrograde/hybrid strategy and served as the primary adjusted model. Covariates were selected according to clinical relevance, availability in the registry, and the need to avoid overfitting; J-CTO score was used as an established composite angiographic complexity measure rather than entering all of its components separately. The primary strategy-inclusive model included 132 technical failure events and 11 covariate terms, corresponding to approximately 12 events per variable. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors, all of which were < 1.35. A companion multivariable model was used to examine the selection of planned retrograde/hybrid strategy. A secondary multivariable model was also fitted for procedural failure. Additional exploratory analyses summarized success metrics according to planned-strategy strata, CABG-territory status among prior-CABG procedures, and retrograde pathway among prior-CABG procedures with actual retrograde involvement.

Missing data were handled using a prespecified variable-level approach. Descriptive analyses were based on available-case denominators for each variable. The primary adjusted models were fitted in the complete-case cohort after excluding procedures with missing planned-approach coding or missing distal-vessel opacification data (N = 1,776). A sensitivity analysis addressing missing planned approach coding is shown in Supplementary Table S2. Variable-level missingness and a comparison of complete-case and excluded procedures are shown in Supplementary Table S9. By contrast, completeness was lower for selected procedural-burden and renal safety variables, particularly dose-area product and creatinine-based CA-AKI assessment; these outcomes were therefore analyzed in the available-case population. To assess the robustness of the radiation findings, supportive sensitivity analyses for missing DAP data were performed (Supplementary Table S3). Model discrimination was summarized using apparent C-statistics. Calibration of the full technical-failure model was assessed using the observed technical-failure rate, the mean predicted risk, and the Brier score. A temporal sensitivity analysis restricted to procedures performed from 2022 to 2025 was conducted to address potential instability associated with the early COVID-19 era. Analyses were performed at the procedure level. Center and operator identifiers were not available in the exported analytic dataset; therefore, center-level random effects, operator-level random effects, or cluster-robust standard errors by center/operator could not be applied. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed in Python using pandas, NumPy, SciPy, and statsmodels.

Results

Study cohort and baseline phenotype

Of 1,856 raw registry records, 56 with non-native or uncodable target-vessel coding were excluded, leaving 1,800 native-vessel CTO PCI procedures in the analytic cohort. Of these, 205 procedures (11.4%) were performed in patients with prior CABG and 1,595 in patients without prior CABG. Review of the available RegistryID field identified 36 duplicate identifiers with two same-day records involving different main CTO target arteries; no delayed repeat attempt on the same main CTO artery was identified.

Missingness was low among variables included in the primary adjusted models, yielding a complete-case cohort of 1,776 procedures. Technical failure was numerically more frequent among the 24 procedures excluded from the primary complete-case model than in the complete-case cohort (4/24 [16.7%] vs. 132/1,776 [7.4%]); however, the excluded sample was small and included only one prior-CABG procedure (Supplementary Table S9). Completeness was lower for selected procedural-burden and renal safety variables, particularly dose-area product and creatinine-based CA-AKI assessment; these outcomes were therefore analyzed using available-case denominators, with supportive sensitivity analyses for DAP reported in the Supplementary Appendix.

Baseline clinical and angiographic characteristics are shown in Table 1. Patients with prior CABG were older and more often had diabetes mellitus. They also had a more complex lesion profile, with longer CTO length, more frequent moderate-to-severe calcification, more frequent in-CTO bending, and a higher proportion of J-CTO scores ≥ 4. Although the median J-CTO score was identical between groups (2 [1–3]), the prior-CABG group had a larger upper tail of highly complex lesions, as reflected by a higher proportion of J-CTO scores ≥ 4 (22.0% vs. 12.4%). The full J-CTO score distribution by prior-CABG status is shown in Supplementary Figure S1. Target-vessel distribution also differed, with fewer LAD CTOs and more LCx and RCA CTOs in procedures performed in patients with prior CABG.

Procedures in patients with prior CABG were also more demanding procedurally. Median procedural duration was longer (103 [71–145] vs. 75 [52–120] min, p < 0.001), fluoroscopy time was longer (48 [29–71] vs. 34 [21–59] min, p < 0.001), and contrast volume was numerically higher in unadjusted analysis (250 [180–350] vs. 200 [150–300] mL, p = 0.065). Among procedures with recorded radiation data, dose-area product was also higher in the prior-CABG group (18,450 [9,606–28,755] vs. 14,320 [7,046–23,903] cGy·cm², p = 0.002). The DAP association was similar in the complete-case and inverse-probability-weighted sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Table S3). Adjusted resource-use models showed consistent associations for fluoroscopy time, procedural duration, DAP, and contrast volume (Supplementary Table S4). In particular, the adjusted contrast-volume association became statistically significant after accounting for lesion complexity, target vessel, and planned strategy. IVUS was used in 151 of 1,595 no-CABG procedures (9.5%) and 15 of 205 prior-CABG procedures (7.3%; p = 0.317). OCT use was not systematically captured.

Selected in-hospital outcomes were infrequent overall.

In-hospital MACCE occurred in 2.4% of prior-CABG procedures and 1.6% of procedures without prior CABG (p = 0.378). Donor-vessel complications were uncommon in both groups (0.0% vs. 1.3%, p = 0.156). In-hospital death was numerically more frequent in prior-CABG procedures (2.0% vs. 0.8%), but this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.099). CA-AKI rates were similar between groups among procedures with available creatinine data (3.5% vs. 4.6%, p = 0.556).

CTO success metrics

CTO success metrics are shown in Table 3; Fig. 2. Correct wire crossing was less frequent in prior-CABG procedures than in procedures without prior CABG (90.7% vs. 95.7%, p = 0.002). Technical success, which was the primary endpoint, was also lower in prior-CABG procedures (85.9% vs. 93.3%, p < 0.001). Procedural success, defined as technical success without in-hospital MACCE, was similarly lower in prior-CABG procedures (84.9% vs. 92.4%, p < 0.001). Within procedures planned for retrograde/hybrid crossing, technical success was similar in patients with and without prior CABG (80.5% vs. 83.4%, p = 0.525; Supplementary Table S5). Additional CABG-territory and retrograde-route analyses are provided in Supplementary Table S6.

Overall, the lower success rate in prior-CABG procedures was evident at the technical stage. Because in-hospital MACCE was infrequent and did not differ significantly between groups, the difference in procedural success was driven primarily by the technical success component.

Adjusted associations

Adjusted associations are shown in Table 4. In the companion multivariable model for planned retrograde/hybrid strategy selection, prior CABG was independently associated with planned retrograde/hybrid strategy (adjusted OR 2.03, 95% CI 1.40–2.96, p < 0.001). Higher J-CTO score and RCA target vessel were also associated with planned retrograde/hybrid selection, whereas LCx target vessel, age, and female sex were inversely associated with planned retrograde/hybrid selection.

In a clinical/angiographic technical-failure model that did not include planned retrograde/hybrid strategy, prior CABG was associated with technical failure (adjusted OR 2.12, 95% CI 1.30–3.45, p = 0.003). After adding planned retrograde/hybrid strategy, prior CABG remained associated with technical failure, although the estimate was attenuated (adjusted OR, 1.94; 95% CI, 1.19–3.18; p = 0.008; Supplementary Table S8). This attenuation is compatible with overlap between prior-CABG status and planning-stage complexity, but this comparison was not intended as a formal mediation analysis. Planned retrograde/hybrid strategy was also associated with technical failure (adjusted OR 2.82, 95% CI 1.81–4.40, p < 0.001), a finding interpreted as reflecting case selection and lesion difficulty rather than a causal effect of the strategy. A higher J-CTO score was also independently associated with technical failure (adjusted OR 1.83 per point, 95% CI 1.51–2.20, p < 0.001), whereas good distal vessel opacification was associated with a lower risk of technical failure (adjusted OR 0.29, 95% CI 0.13–0.64, p = 0.002). The apparent C-statistic was 0.753 for J-CTO alone, 0.762 for J-CTO plus prior CABG, and 0.808 for the full technical failure model. For the full model, the observed technical failure rate and mean predicted risk were both 7.4%, and the Brier score was 0.061 (Supplementary Table S7).

In the secondary multivariable model, prior CABG also remained independently associated with procedural failure (adjusted OR 1.76, 95% CI 1.09–2.83, p = 0.020). Planned retrograde/hybrid strategy (adjusted OR 3.21, 95% CI 2.10–4.89, p < 0.001) and higher J-CTO score (adjusted OR 1.70 per point, 95% CI 1.43–2.03, p < 0.001) were also associated with procedural failure, whereas good distal vessel opacification remained associated with a lower risk (adjusted OR 0.41, 95% CI 0.22–0.79, p = 0.008). In a temporal sensitivity analysis restricted to procedures performed from 2022 to 2025, the association between prior CABG and technical failure remained consistent (adjusted OR 1.96, 95% CI 1.12–3.45, p = 0.019; Supplementary Table S7).

Discussion

In this multicenter registry of native-vessel CTO PCI, prior CABG was associated with more complex angiographic features, more frequent retrograde/hybrid planning, greater procedural burden, and lower technical success. Beyond confirming that prior-CABG CTO PCI is challenging, the present analysis links surgical history to preprocedural anatomy, planned strategy, resource use, and final technical success within the same multicenter cohort.

In the broader context of chronic coronary syndromes, percutaneous revascularization remains an important treatment option in selected patients [10]. Previous studies and meta-analyses have shown that CTO PCI in patients with prior CABG is often associated with longer lesions, more calcification, more frequent retrograde treatment, greater procedural burden, and lower overall success [6, 7, 11]. Our findings are consistent with these reports and suggest that the relevance of prior CABG begins before wire escalation: prior-CABG procedures already had a less favorable lesion profile and were more often assigned to retrograde or hybrid crossing at the planning stage.

Several mechanisms may contribute to this association. Native vessels after bypass surgery may have more diffuse disease, longer occlusion segments, negative remodeling, heavier calcification, and less favorable distal target characteristics. Severe calcification is common in CTO lesions and has been associated with greater procedural complexity, lower technical success, and a higher risk of complications [12, 13]. CCTA-based studies further suggest that calcium morphology and distribution within CTO segments may refine preprocedural assessment beyond binary calcification grading [14, 15]. In selected prior-CABG patients, coronary imaging, including CCTA and intravascular imaging, may help define vessel course, calcification pattern, proximal cap ambiguity, and distal target quality before or during complex CTO PCI [15, 16].

The modest increase in discrimination after adding prior CABG to J-CTO alone clarifies the role of prior CABG. Although prior CABG remained independently associated with technical failure, the C-statistic increased only from 0.753 with J-CTO alone to 0.762 when prior CABG was added. The full model also showed close agreement between the observed technical failure rate and the mean predicted risk in the apparent calibration assessment. Therefore, prior CABG should not be presented as a standalone prediction tool or as substantially improving risk stratification beyond the J-CTO score. Its practical value is mainly contextual: it may prompt more careful angiographic review, procedural planning, and counseling when other features also suggest a difficult CTO PCI case.

The strategy-related findings require caution. In observational CTO PCI data, a retrograde or hybrid plan is usually selected because the lesion is expected to be difficult, not at random. Such selection may reflect proximal cap ambiguity, distal target quality, collateral suitability, and the operator’s expectation of antegrade failure. Therefore, the association between planned retrograde/hybrid strategy and lower technical or procedural success should not be interpreted as evidence that the strategy itself worsens outcomes. The substantial concordance between planned strategy and actual retrograde involvement (κ = 0.755 overall and κ = 0.857 in prior-CABG procedures) suggests that planned strategy generally translated into procedural conduct, particularly in prior-CABG cases. The planned-strategy subgroup analysis supports this interpretation: among procedures already planned for retrograde/hybrid crossing, technical success was similar in patients with and without prior CABG (80.5% vs. 83.4%). In the descriptive route analysis, bypass graft routes had the highest technical success rate (15/17, 88.2%), but the small number of cases and the absence of graft-patency data preclude firm conclusions.

The lower technical success observed in prior-CABG procedures is clinically important because technical success was the principal procedural efficacy endpoint in this study and remains a central endpoint in CTO PCI [4]. In our data, the difference between groups was already present at the wire-crossing stage and remained at the final technical stage. Procedural success, defined as technical success without in-hospital MACCE, was also lower in prior-CABG procedures; however, because in-hospital MACCE was infrequent and not significantly different, this secondary endpoint was driven primarily by the technical success component.

In practice, these results may help with case planning and patient counseling. Prior CABG should not be used to discourage CTO PCI on its own. Rather, it should alert operators to the possibility of greater procedural burden, retrograde or hybrid escalation, use of bypass grafts or collateral routes, and the need for referral to experienced CTO centers when lesion complexity is high.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was an observational registry analysis, and residual confounding remains possible despite multivariable adjustment. Prior CABG may have contextual clinical value but remains an imperfect surrogate for anatomical and procedural complexity when considered in isolation. Second, the planned strategy was not randomized and should be interpreted as a planning-stage marker rather than evidence of treatment effect. Third, the analysis was performed at the procedure level. Review of the available RegistryID field identified 36 duplicate identifiers with two same-day records involving different main CTO target arteries and no delayed repeat attempt on the same main CTO artery. Nevertheless, the analysis did not model within-patient correlation. Fourth, although all participating CTO programs were high-volume programs, center/operator identifiers were not available in the exported analytic dataset; therefore, center-level random effects, operator-level random effects, and cluster-robust standard errors by center or operator could not be applied. This is an important limitation because unmeasured variation in local practice, operator experience, imaging use, radiation practice, retrograde planning, and graft-route selection may have influenced both strategy selection and outcomes. Fifth, prior CABG was defined broadly. Although CABG-territory status of the CTO target vessel was available and analyzed descriptively, graft patency, graft type, number of grafts, complete surgical anatomy, bypass-conduit usability, and time from CABG to CTO PCI were not systematically captured. Therefore, the prior-CABG group likely included heterogeneous surgical and anatomical scenarios. Sixth, retrograde-route analyses were descriptive and limited by small subgroups and missing pathway data in some prior-CABG retrograde procedures. Seventh, missingness was not uniform across registry variables. Although the primary adjusted models were performed in a near-complete cohort and sensitivity analyses supported the DAP findings, selected procedural-burden and renal safety outcomes were available only in subsets of the registry and should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution. Finally, procedural success and correct wire crossing were based on prespecified registry fields and site-reported outcomes without independent core-laboratory adjudication. In-hospital death was infrequent, and the study was not powered to compare mortality. Nevertheless, the observed in-hospital death rate in prior-CABG procedures was numerically higher than that reported in some large contemporary CTO PCI registries, including prior-CABG analyses reporting in-hospital mortality of 0.8% versus 0.3% and 1.0% versus 0.4% in patients with and without prior CABG, respectively [7, 17]. Therefore, this numerical difference should be interpreted cautiously and may reflect differences in case selection, baseline risk, and registry-level event ascertainment.

Conclusion

In native-vessel CTO PCI, prior CABG was associated with a more complex lesion profile, more frequent retrograde/hybrid planning, greater procedural burden, and lower technical success. Procedural success was also lower, but this secondary endpoint was driven primarily by the technical success component. Although prior CABG remained independently associated with technical failure, its incremental prognostic value beyond the J-CTO score was limited. Prior CABG should therefore be interpreted mainly as a marker of an unfavorable procedural phenotype rather than as a standalone risk-stratification tool or evidence that any crossing strategy causes failure.
