A detection method for dense emitters based on a separation and boundary-aware collaborative enhancement detection network
Authors: Ziyi Zhang, Youchen Fan, Shunhu Hou, Maolin Tang, Shengliang Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To address the low detection efficiency of dense emitters in radio environment maps, this paper analyzes two fundamental challenges: feature confusion, which causes merged targets and increases missed detections, and boundary ambiguity, which blurs edges and raises false alarms. Together, they create a precision–recall dilemma. To this end, we propose a Separation and Boundary-Aware Collaborative Enhancement Detection Network, which contains two modules that respectively separate confused features and restore blurred boundaries. Experiments on a dense emitter dataset constructed from RadioMapSeer demonstrate that SBCE-Net achieves an F1 score of 0.988 alongside a recall of 0.991 and a precision of 0.985, outperforming seven existing methods in precision–recall balance. The FPPI–Recall curves show that this advantage holds across a broad range of confidence thresholds, and stratified density tests confirm that the method maintains the highest F1 at every density level, with a variation of only 0.116 from the densest to the sparsest condition. Ablation studies further verify that the two modules contribute complementarily, confirming the effectiveness of the collaborative design.

Introduction

Accurate detection of emitters is fundamental to electromagnetic situational awareness and spectrum security. In military applications, it enables the identification and localization of threat radiation sources such as airborne radars and ground-based jammers, providing critical information for battlefield decision-making1–3. In civilian spectrum management, it supports interference source localization, dynamic spectrum access coordination, and unauthorized emission monitoring, thereby ensuring the reliable operation of communication networks and radio services4–8. In scenarios with densely distributed emitters, detection efficiency is often severely degraded: closely spaced signals are prone to being missed, while the attempt to capture more targets can introduce excessive false alarms. Both missed detections and false alarms carry significant costs—the former creates dangerous blind spots in situational awareness, while the latter wastes limited investigation resources and may mislead subsequent decisions. A detection method that can balance these two risks, i.e., achieve a favorable trade-off between precision and recall, is therefore of considerable practical value.

Existing approaches for emitter detection can be broadly categorized into two groups: signal-processing-based methods and computer-vision-based methods that utilize the radio environment map (REM). Signal-processing techniques rely on parameter extraction and association, such as direction finding and time-difference-of-arrival, but their performance degrades sharply in dense scenarios due to signal aliasing and association ambiguity9–15. The REM transforms discrete signal measurements into a two-dimensional spatial representation of received signal strength, converting the detection problem into an image-based visual analysis task16,17. With advances in REM construction techniques, REM-based visual detection has become an increasingly important research direction. However, when processing densely distributed emitters, these methods face two fundamental challenges: feature confusion and boundary ambiguity. Feature confusion refers to the phenomenon where adjacent emitters appear as merged or overlapping bright spots, causing the detector to misclassify multiple targets as a single one. Boundary ambiguity arises from signal superposition and the progressive downsampling in deep feature extraction, which blurs target edges and degrades localization accuracy. Together, these two issues create a precision-recall dilemma: lowering the detection threshold to improve recall leads to false positives caused by boundary indistinctness, while raising the threshold to maintain precision results in missed detections of confused targets. Existing visual detectors, lacking mechanisms to simultaneously address both challenges, struggle to achieve a satisfactory balance. To this end, this paper constructs a Separation and Boundary-Aware Collaborative Enhancement Detection Network (SBCE-Net). The main contributions are as follows:

Related work

Signal-parameter-based emitter detection methods

Emitter detection methods based on signal parameter estimation and geometric localization have long been the mainstream technical approach. These methods extract parameters such as pulse descriptor words from received signals, perform parameter association across multiple receivers, and compute source locations through direction finding intersection or time-difference-of-arrival positioning18–21. Under sparse signal conditions with favorable signal-to-noise ratios, they can achieve low misdetection rates and high localization accuracy22. To improve parameter extraction quality in non-stationary environments, researchers have incorporated time-frequency analysis techniques such as wavelet transform23,24 as well as radio frequency fingerprinting methods that exploit hardware-specific signal distortions for emitter identification25–28.

However, the fundamental bottleneck of parameter-based methods lies in their heavy reliance on accurate parameter extraction and association. In dense emitter scenarios, multiple signals overlap in time, frequency, and space, rendering individual parameter estimates unreliable and causing the combinatorial complexity of multi-source association to grow rapidly29. The Direct Position Determination approach attempts to bypass explicit parameter association by jointly inferring source locations from raw signals, but incurs high computational cost that hinders practical deployment30–32. Grid-based approximations can reduce this burden, yet introduce quantization errors that fundamentally limit the ability to resolve closely spaced emitters33,34. These methods all operate in the parameter domain, where discriminative capability becomes fragile under signal aliasing: when the parameters of individual signals cannot be reliably estimated, they also cannot be correctly assigned to the corresponding sources. Consequently, parameter-based methods struggle to simultaneously achieve high recall and high precision in dense scenarios.

Detection methods based on REM

The REM aggregates spatially distributed signal measurements into a two-dimensional representation of received signal strength, transforming emitter detection from a parameter association problem into a visual analysis task35. Early research primarily treated REM as a visualization platform36–39. Wang et al.40 applied maximum-value search and weighted centroid localization on reconstructed spectrum maps, but this approach assumes isolated bright spots and is unsuitable for dense multi-source scenarios. Zhao et al.41 employed persistent homology for detection, yet computational complexity becomes prohibitive as emitter density increases.

With breakthroughs in deep learning for object detection, CNN-based methods have been introduced to REM-based emitter detection. Mainstream architectures such as Faster R-CNN42 and the YOLO series establish the end-to-end detection paradigm of feature extraction, feature fusion, box regression, and classification, employing FPN43 to fuse multi-scale features. However, dense emitter detection faces two intertwined challenges: feature confusion and boundary ambiguity. First, feature confusion arises from the spatial proximity of emitters. As spacing decreases, emitters appear as overlapping bright spots in the REM, and the detector struggles to produce sufficient feature differences to distinguish adjacent targets. FPN assigns small objects to shallow feature maps with limited semantic information, and feature discrepancy between non-adjacent layers is progressively diluted during fusion—its essence lies in enhancing target saliency rather than inter-target discrimination, and it does not explicitly recover boundary information lost during hierarchical feature processing43. Multi-scale fusion and attention mechanisms similarly enhance feature responses, improving target saliency to some extent, but do not fundamentally address the target separation problem when features are merged44,45. Second, boundary ambiguity stems from the dual effect of physical signal superposition and internal network downsampling, causing emitter boundaries to be progressively lost in high-level features. SBA46 fuses shallow boundary details with deep semantic information for improved localization, SEAM45 enhances boundary region responses via attention mechanisms, and boundary refinement networks in the SAR domain have been proposed to recover edge details of blurred dense objects41. These methods can sharpen edges and improve localization accuracy, but their design premise is to optimize boundaries on existing feature maps rather than to decouple emitter features already merged at the input level. These two challenges are not independent; they reinforce each other: feature confusion makes it difficult to determine whether a detection region contains one emitter or multiple, while boundary ambiguity means that even when correctly identified, each target cannot be precisely localized. This directly leads to a precision-recall imbalance—lowering the threshold to discover more targets introduces false positives due to blurred boundaries, while raising the threshold to maintain precision causes confused targets to be missed. Existing methods lack a unified collaborative framework that jointly addresses feature discrimination and boundary restoration: the former group focuses on improving target saliency, the latter on edge sharpening, and the two have not been organically combined within a single architecture. Therefore, a detection method that synergistically handles target feature separation and boundary detail recovery is needed to improve the precision-recall balance in dense emitter detection.

Method

Network overview

Fig. 1Architecture of the proposed SBCE-Net.

Architecture of the proposed SBCE-Net.

The proposed SBCE-Net is designed to address two specific challenges in dense emitter detection on REM: feature confusion between adjacent emitters and boundary ambiguity of individual emitters. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the network follows the FPN pipeline and consists of four main components: a backbone, the SDR module, the BADF module, and a detection head.

The YOLOv11 backbone is adopted for feature extraction. It produces multi-scale feature maps P3, P4, and P5 from the input REM, providing hierarchical spatial and semantic information for subsequent processing. To mitigate feature confusion, the SDR module is integrated into the neck of the network. It receives multi-level features from the backbone and performs two operations in parallel: aggregating multi-scale contextual information through cascaded pooling, and actively enhancing inter-target edges through a spatial-domain sharpening mechanism. These operations can improve the feature separability of closely spaced emitters, making adjacent “bright spots” more distinguishable. The detailed structure is described in Section III-B. To mitigate boundary ambiguity, the BADF module is introduced into the feature fusion path. During hierarchical feature extraction and upsampling in FPN, boundary details of small emitters are progressively lost, degrading localization accuracy. The BADF module restores these boundary details through two complementary branches: one that enhances edge components to sharpen emitter boundaries, and another that performs content-aware upsampling to maintain smooth spatial transitions between adjacent regions. The detailed structure is described in Section III-C. The enhanced multi-scale feature maps, after processing by SDR and BADF, are fed into an efficient detection head that predicts bounding boxes and confidence scores.

Experiments

Experimental setup

Fig. 5Schematic diagram of synthetic dense emitter REM based on RadioMapSeer.

Schematic diagram of synthetic dense emitter REM based on RadioMapSeer.

In electromagnetic field theory, the total field intensity at a point in space is the vector superposition of the field intensities from individual emitters. However, phase information for each source is unavailable in the RadioMapSeer dataset. Based on electromagnetic wave propagation characteristics, when multiple emitters have random phases, the power density at a receiving point is proportional to the square of the field intensity. For incoherent sources, the phase difference is a random variable, and the expectation of the cross-term is zero. Therefore, the statistical expectation of the total power equals the linear sum of the individual source powers. Within a Bayesian estimation framework, the linear addition of power conforms to the principle of maximum entropy when phase information is missing, representing the optimal estimation under information constraints. Following this principle, this paper adopts the power linear addition method to construct multi-source REM on the RadioMapSeer dataset, each REM image represents a 256 × 256 grid with 1 m resolution, as summarized in Table 1. This method preserves the spatial distribution characteristics of single sources while ensuring the theoretical correctness of multi-source synthesis through rigorous physical principles.

It should be noted that during dataset construction, it was found that severe aliasing occurs when the distance between adjacent emitters is less than 3 pixels. Under the constraints of unavailable raw time-frequency data and the REM resolution of 1 m/pixel, severely aliased sources cannot be separated. Therefore, when constructing dense source scenarios, the minimum spacing is set to start from 3 pixels.

Training Configuration: The input resolution is 256 × 256, with a batch size of 32, and training is conducted for 200 epochs. The SGD optimizer (initial learning rate 0.01) is used, accompanied by a 3-epoch learning rate warm up. Data augmentation includes Mosaic, random flipping, and RandAugment. To enhance training effectiveness in dense scenarios, a curriculum learning strategy is adopted: the model first learns basic detection capabilities on the train set of data1 and then undergoes fine-tuning on the train set of data2. All comparative experiments are performed under this unified configuration to ensure fairness.

Evaluation Metrics: Four metrics are adopted to evaluate the detection performance from complementary perspectives: precision, recall, F1 score, and the FPPI-Recall curve.

Precision and recall are defined as37\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:P=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}\:，R=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}$$\end{document}

where TP, FP, and FN denote true positives, false positives, and false negatives, respectively. Precision measures the proportion of detected emitters that are correct, reflecting the ability to control false alarms. Recall measures the proportion of actual emitters that are successfully detected, reflecting the ability to control missed detections. In dense emitter detection, precision and recall are inherently in tension—improving one often sacrifices the other—so neither alone is sufficient to characterize overall detection efficiency.

The F1 score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall:38\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:F1=2\times\:\frac{P\times\:R}{P+R}$$\end{document}

It provides a single metric that balances the two, reflecting the overall detection efficiency at a given confidence threshold. A higher F1 score indicates a more favorable trade-off between reducing missed detections and controlling false alarms.

The FPPI-Recall curve (False Positives Per Image vs. Recall) evaluates performance across all possible confidence thresholds. Unlike precision, recall, and F1 score, which are measured at a single operating point, the FPPI-Recall curve traverses the entire range of thresholds, characterizing the inherent trade-off between recall and false-alarm rate. A curve closer to the upper-left corner indicates that the model maintains high recall at low FPPI across a wide operating regime, demonstrating robust balance independent of any specific threshold setting. Together with F1 score, these metrics provide a comprehensive assessment of both the optimal-point and the global precision-recall balance.

Conclusion

This paper addressed the problem of low detection efficiency for dense emitters in REM, analyzed the mechanism by which feature confusion and boundary ambiguity jointly cause a precision-recall imbalance, and constructed an SBCE-Net. In this network, the Spatial Detail Refinement module separates confused emitter features through multi-scale context aggregation and edge enhancement, while the Boundary-Aware Detail Fusion module recovers boundary details lost during hierarchical processing via learnable adaptive filtering, with all operations performed in the spatial domain. The two modules work collaboratively: SDR ensures that adjacent emitters can be distinguished as independent targets, and BADF ensures that each target retains sharp boundaries for accurate localization.

Experimental results consistently validate the improvement in detection efficiency brought by this collaborative design from multiple dimensions. The ablation study shows that neither module alone achieves a satisfactory precision-recall balance: SDR improves recall but reduces precision, while BADF enhances localization yet cannot separate already confused features. Their combination achieves the highest F1 score of 0.987, proving that feature separation and boundary restoration are complementary and both necessary. In comparison with seven existing methods, SBCE-Net attains the highest F1 score of 0.988, along with a recall of 0.991 and a precision of 0.985, indicating that the method alleviates the imbalance from both the precision and recall sides. The FPPI-Recall curves further confirm this finding across all possible confidence thresholds, with SBCE-Net maintaining the highest recall over a wide FPPI range. In stratified tests across different densities, SBCE-Net achieves the highest F1 at every density level, with an F1 variation of only 0.116 from the densest to the sparsest condition, showing that its balanced performance does not depend on large increases in emitter spacing. The radar chart comparison under two extreme densities shows that SBCE-Net maintains the largest coverage area and symmetry across precision, recall, and F1, indicating no sharp performance decline under drastic density variation.

Several limitations of this study should be noted. The computational cost of 17.7 GFlops is moderate among the compared methods, which may constrain deployment under stringent real-time requirements47,48. The method belongs to the supervised learning paradigm and relies on accurately annotated REM data; acquiring such annotations in real electromagnetic environments is difficult and costly49. The evaluation is based on synthetic REM data, and its generalization to real-world conditions with complex propagation and dynamic interference remains to be verified. Future work will pursue three directions: optimizing the dual-module architecture to reduce computational overhead, exploring unsupervised or weakly supervised detection methods to alleviate the annotation burden, and validating on measured REM data, thereby advancing toward practical deployment for electromagnetic spectrum situational awareness50–52.
