Knowledge of cervical cancer and uptake of HPV vaccine among in-school adolescent girls in Kwara State, Nigeria

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCervical cancer ranks as the second most common malignancy among Nigerian women, contributing heavily to preventable mortality, particularly in low-resource settings. HPV infection is the primary cause, and vaccination provides an effective prevention strategy. However, coverage in Nigeria remains critically low. Understanding knowledge and barriers among adolescent girls is imperative for designing interventions. This study explored these determinants among in-school girls in Kwara State.MethodsThis cross-sectional study surveyed 628 adolescent girls aged 9–14 years across rural, semi urban and urban LGAs in Kwara State. Multistage sampling included, random LGA selection, random school selection (7 public, 5 private per LGA), and systematic student sampling. Data were collected via comprehensive interviewer-administered questionnaires covering demographics, cervical cancer/HPV knowledge, barriers, and vaccine uptake. Analysis used STATA v18 with descriptive statistics and logistic regression with robust standard errors to identify predictors.ResultsMean age was 12.4 years (SD:1.7); 68.0% were in junior high school and 53.3% attended public schools. Overall knowledge levels were notably low: only 3.2% had good knowledge, 36.8% fair, and 60.0% poor. None of the participants had received the HPV vaccine. Major barriers included lack of awareness (82.2%), cost (76.0%), fear of side effects (71.2%), limited facility access (71.7%), and unawareness of vaccination sites (70.5%). Encouragingly, willingness to receive the vaccine was expressed by 71.2%. Fully adjusted logistic regression revealed that Muslim respondents had higher intention versus Christians (aOR = 1.61, 95%CI:1.12–2.33, p = 0.01). Location, age, school type, education level, and risk perception were not found to be significant predictors.ConclusionThis study exposes a critical disconnect between high willingness to vaccinate and profound knowledge deficits alongside substantial structural barriers. The complete absence of prior vaccinations underscores missed healthcare opportunities. To bridge this gap, targeted school-based health education leveraging teachers and healthcare workers is urgently needed to dispel myths about side effects and disseminate accurate information. Simultaneously, policy-driven strategies such as subsidizing vaccine costs, integrating vaccination into routine school health programs, and deploying mobile outreach to remote communities are essential to overcome financial and geographical obstacles. Introduction

Cervical cancer remains a significant public health issue globally, with a notable impact on women’s health in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) like Nigeria [1]. Globally, cervical cancer is the fourth most common cancer among women, with an estimated 660,000 new cases and 350,000 deaths in 2022 [1]. Approximately 90% of new cases and deaths occur in LMICs, where access to prevention, screening, and treatment services is limited [1]. In sub-Saharan Africa, cervical cancer is the second most common cancer among women and the leading cause of cancer-related deaths in many countries [2]. In Nigeria specifically, cervical cancer is the second most common cancer among women, with an estimated 14,089 new cases and 8,240 deaths annually [2].

According to the World Health Organization [1], cervical cancer is predominantly caused by persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) types, which can be prevented through vaccination and early screening [1]. The WHO recommends HPV vaccination for girls aged 9–14 years [3]. HPV is known to affect adults and children alike, and to date, studies have identified over 100 different strains of HPV, highlighting its diverse and complex nature [4]. Vaccinating pre-adolescents against oncogenic HPV is a crucial preventive measure against developing HPV-related cancers later in life.

Despite the availability of effective vaccines, the uptake of HPV vaccination remains suboptimal in many regions [2], including Kwara State, Nigeria. The HPV vaccine was licensed and introduced in Nigeria in 2009 [4], yet more than a decade later, the knowledge of HPV as the causative agent of cervical cancer, and HPV vaccination as a preventive measure against cervical cancer, including the uptake of vaccination by the target population of young people, is still abysmally low. Many studies conducted among female students in secondary schools and universities in Nigeria have alluded to this fact [4–8]. Until recently, HPV vaccines were only available in private health facilities, and at a cost that many Nigerians cannot afford [9]. To bridge these gaps, the Nigerian government commenced mass vaccination against HPV in October 2023, aiming to reach 7.7 million girls [10, 11]. This represents the largest single-round HPV vaccination campaign in the African region. Due to a global vaccine shortage, the rollout was phased to ensure equitable access across the 36 states and the Federal Capital Territory. The criteria for phasing included the level of risk of cervical cancer disease, cold chain capacity sufficiency, and Penta 3 coverage.

Eligible girls within the age range of 9–14 years were reached through a multi-age cohort campaign (MAC) for one week in the introducing states. After the campaign, the HPV vaccine was integrated into the country’s routine immunization schedule and made available in all government health facilities for girls aged nine years from 2024. The HPV vaccine was delivered to eligible girls through three approaches: Fixed Posts (Primary Health Centers and Teaching Hospitals), School Outreach (Public and Private Schools, non-conventional and Faith-based schools), and Community Outreach (Markets, Religious houses, and other social gatherings) [10].

In Nigeria, various socio-cultural factors, including myths, misconceptions, and limited access to accurate information, contribute to the low uptake of HPV vaccines [12]. Studies have indicated that many adolescent girls have limited knowledge about cervical cancer and the preventive role of HPV vaccination, which poses a barrier to vaccine acceptance and uptake [13–15]. The high prevalence of HPV and the associated risk of cervical cancer in Nigeria underscore the need for effective prevention strategies. The introduction of the HPV vaccine offers a significant opportunity to reduce the burden of cervical cancer; however, the low uptake of the vaccine remains a major challenge, and factors contributing to this include lack of awareness, misinformation, and socio-cultural barriers [16]. A study conducted in Gombe State, Nigeria, revealed that only 3.7% of adolescent girls had received at least one dose of the HPV vaccine, despite 73.1% having heard about the HPV vaccine [17]. This gap between awareness and uptake highlights the need for targeted educational programs that address specific barriers to vaccination. Additionally, the role of parents and guardians in influencing vaccine uptake cannot be overlooked. Studies have shown that parental consent and support are crucial determinants of vaccination rates among adolescents [18].

While several studies have assessed HPV vaccine knowledge and uptake among adolescent girls in various Nigerian states [4–8], there remains a notable gap in evidence from Kwara State, a north-central state targeted for the national HPV mass vaccination campaign in April, 2024. Reports have examined aspects of HPV vaccination in Kwara State, including stakeholder perspectives and news reports on the vaccine rollout [10, 11]. However, to the best of our knowledge, no comprehensive quantitative study has assessed baseline knowledge of cervical cancer, HPV vaccine awareness, uptake, and barriers among the 9-14-year age group in Kwara State prior to the mass vaccination campaign. This evidence gap is particularly significant given that Kwara State was scheduled for the multi-age cohort campaign in April 2024, making baseline data essential for evaluating the campaign’s impact, identifying priority areas for intervention, and designing targeted strategies to improve vaccine acceptance and uptake. Conducting our research before this campaign allowed us to establish baseline data on adolescents’ knowledge of cervical cancer and their willingness to receive the HPV vaccine.

The present study aimed to assess the level of knowledge about cervical cancer and HPV, awareness and uptake of the HPV vaccine, barriers to vaccination, and factors influencing vaccine acceptance among in-school adolescent girls aged 9–14 years in Kwara State. By understanding the current knowledge gaps, risk perception, and factors influencing vaccine acceptance, targeted strategies can be developed to improve vaccination rates and reduce the incidence of cervical cancer in this region. This study is particularly pertinent given the Nigerian government’s efforts to integrate HPV vaccination into the national immunization program to achieve the target of vaccinating 7.7 million girls against the leading cause of cervical cancer. The study will also contribute to the ongoing global push to eliminate cervical cancer as a public health problem by 2030 [19].

Methods

Study design, study area, and study population

A descriptive cross-sectional design was adopted for this study to determine the knowledge of cervical cancer and HPV vaccination uptake among in-school adolescent girls. This study was conducted in selected Local Government Areas of Kwara state located in the north-central region of Nigeria. The study was also conducted in selected high schools in both the rural and urban locations of the state. The schools were selected among private and public schools. The study population consisted of in-school adolescent girls aged 9 to 14 years, this population was of interest because they represent the age group eligible for the multi-age cohort campaign (MAC) HPV vaccination in Nigeria. Although the national rollout began in October 2023, implementation was phased across states. Kwara State was scheduled for the multi-age cohort campaign (MAC) in April 2024, and our data collection (February-March 2024) was completed prior to the commencement of any vaccination activities or awareness campaigns in the state. Therefore, respondents had not been exposed to any state-level HPV vaccination campaign activities at the time of data collection. This timing allowed us to establish genuine baseline data on cervical cancer knowledge and HPV vaccine uptake before the introduction of the vaccine in Kwara State.

The study initially targeted 410 participants. However, during data collection, the high availability and willingness of eligible students across the selected schools resulted in a larger sample. To maintain proportional representation across clusters and ensure adequate statistical power for subgroup analyses, we included all eligible and consenting students, resulting in a final sample of 628 respondents. This sample size exceeds the minimum required and provides sufficient power for the planned analyses.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Adolescent girls within the age range of 9 to 14 years were included in the study, while girls below 9 years or 15 years and above, adolescent girls who were not willing or unable to participate in the study, and adolescent girls with physical or mental conditions that could impact their effective responses were excluded from the study.

Sampling technique

A multistage sampling approach was used: (a) Kwara State was chosen due to its status as one of the north-central Nigerian states lacking prior studies on cervical cancer education and HPV vaccine uptake among in-school adolescents. Moreover, it was targeted by the government for a mass vaccination campaign aimed at adolescent girls aged 9–14 years. Conducting our research before this campaign allowed us to establish baseline data on adolescents’ knowledge of cervical cancer and their willingness to receive the HPV vaccine. (b) Cluster sampling to select Local Government Areas (LGAs): the LGAs in the state were classified as urban, semi-urban/suburban, or rural based on the National Population Commission classification [22]. Two LGAs, Ilorin South and Ilorin East, were randomly selected from the urban category using simple random sampling by balloting. Similarly, one LGA each from the semi-urban/suburban and rural categories, Edu and Patigi respectively, was randomly selected using the same technique. (c) Selection of Schools: we obtained a comprehensive list of all secondary schools (both public and private) from the Kwara State Ministry of Education for each selected LGA. Schools were stratified into public and private categories. From each stratum, schools were selected using simple random sampling (balloting) to ensure a 6:4 ratio of public to private schools in the final sample. This ratio was chosen to reflect the approximate distribution of public and private schools in the state. A total of 12 schools were selected across the four LGAs (3 schools per LGA), comprising 7 public schools and 5 private schools, and (d) Selection of Respondents: within each selected school, we obtained the total number of adolescent girls aged 9–14 years from school records. The sample size was proportionally allocated to each school based on the number of eligible girls in that school relative to the total eligible population across all selected schools. Systematic sampling was then used to select respondents from the list of eligible girls in each school: a sampling interval (k) was calculated by dividing the total number of eligible girls in the school by the number of girls to be sampled from that school. A random starting point was selected between 1 and k, and every kth girl was subsequently selected until the required sample size for that school was achieved. Instrument for data collection

A semi-structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire was used to collect data, addressing each study objective. The full questionnaire and consent/assent forms are provided as additional file. The use of trained interviewers was deemed appropriate given the age group of respondents (9–14 years) to ensure proper comprehension of questions and to provide clarification when needed. Each interview was conducted in a private setting within the school premises to ensure confidentiality and minimize external influence. The items in the questionnaire were written in English and translated into Yoruba, the two predominant languages in Kwara State, with the Yoruba version having been translated and back-translated to ensure accuracy.

The questionnaire had six sections, each addressing a variable of interest. (1) Socio-demographic variables such as age, religion, educational level, school name, school type, Local Government Area, and dwelling location were assessed. (2) Awareness of the HPV vaccine was assessed, including whether respondents had received information about it and the source of that information. (3) Knowledge of cervical cancer and HPV was assessed, covering cervical cancer risk factors, symptoms, prevention, screening, and curability, as well as HPV’s role as a causative agent. (4) Risk perception to cervical cancer infection was assessed, including perceived susceptibility, severity, and barriers to prevention. (5) HPV vaccine and cervical cancer screening uptake was assessed, including whether respondents had received the vaccine, their age at vaccination, reasons for vaccination, and history of cervical cancer screening. (6) Barriers to HPV vaccination were assessed, considering factors like fear of pain, lack of awareness, cost, fear of side effects, access issues, and parental support.

The scale used for each section was adapted from an existing questionnaire previously used and validated by various authors [13, 23, 24]. To ensure the questionnaire was valid for the current study, the various sections were thoroughly reviewed by subject experts and languages to be considered ambiguous for the study population were rephrased. Additional items were also added to some of the scales to comprehensively address the scope of the study. The questionnaire was revalidated to ensure they are valid for the study. Data was collected from February to March 2024, with quality ensured during collection, entry, and analysis.

Validity and reliability

Measures were taken to ensure the validity and reliability of the instrument. Content validity was achieved through literature consultation and alignment with study objectives. Peer and expert reviews led to necessary corrections. For reliability, a pre-test evaluation was conducted with 41 adolescents, administering the questionnaire twice within a two-week interval to assess response consistency. The reliability was determined using Cronbach Alpha measurement and the reliability co-efficient measure was carried out on the pre-test questionnaire to ascertain the reliability of the instrument. For this study, the Cronbach Alpha was co-efficient 0.74 which was considered to be reliable. Findings informed further corrections to ensure the relevance, appropriateness, and adequacy of the instrument’s items.

Data management and analysis

The questionnaires were checked for errors and numbered serially by the researcher for easy identification and recall. Completed questionnaires were sorted, edited, and coded with the use of a coding guide and entered into STATA version 18. The socio-demographic information was described using descriptive statistics by showing frequency in frequency distribution tables and charts. Knowledge of cervical cancer and HPV was assessed using 30 items covering four domains: (1) awareness of cervical cancer and HPV (4 items), (2) knowledge of risk factors for cervical cancer (8 items), (3) knowledge of symptoms of cervical cancer (8 items), and (4) knowledge of prevention, screening, and curability of cervical cancer (10 items). Each correct response was scored 1 point, and incorrect responses were scored 0, giving a maximum possible score of 30. The total knowledge score was categorized as follows: 0–10 points (poor knowledge), 11–20 points (fair knowledge), and 21–30 points (good knowledge). These categories were chosen based on Bloom’s cut-off points (score < 60% = poor, 60–79% = fair, ≥ 80% = good) [25], which have been widely used and validated in similar knowledge assessment studies in Nigeria [4, 5]. Risk perception to cervical cancer infection was assessed using an 11-item scale adapted from validated instruments used in previous studies on cervical cancer risk perception among adolescent girls in sub-Saharan Africa [13, 24]. Each item was scored as 1 for ‘Yes’ and 0 for ‘No’. The total score ranged from 0 to 11. A score of ≤ 5.5 was categorized as low-risk perception, and a score of > 5.5 was categorized as high-risk perception. The midpoint split (≤ 5.5 and > 5.5) was used as the cut-off point following the methodology of previous studies that have used similar risk perception scales in Nigerian and African populations [7, 13, 26]. This approach allows meaningful dichotomization of risk perception for analytical purposes and has been demonstrated to have adequate construct validity in comparable study populations. The level of uptake of the HPV vaccine and barriers to uptake were presented using descriptive statistics. Inferential statistics was done, and the association between categorical variables was measured using Fisher’s exact and the chi-square. For the logistic regression analysis, unadjusted models were fitted for variables including age (continuous), education level (categorical), religion (categorical), school type (categorical), location of residence (categorical), perception of risk (categorized as high/low), and level of knowledge about cervical cancer (categorized as poor/fair/good). For the adjusted model, variables with unadjusted p-values < 0.1 were included, in addition to risk perception and level of cervical cancer knowledge, based on a priori considerations of their potential confounding effects. Strength of association was quantified using Odds Ratio (OR) with 95% Confidence Interval (95% CI). The level of significance was set at p-value ≤ 0.05.

Ethical and cultural considerations

This study was conducted in Kwara State and received ethical approval from the Ethics Research Committee of the Kwara State Ministry of Health, which is licensed by the National Health Research Ethics Committee (NHREC) of Nigeria. The reference number for this approval is ERC/MOH/2023/09/151. The study strictly adhered to the ethical guidelines outlined in the Helsinki Declaration for research involving human subjects. Before the research commenced, the participants were informed of the study’s purpose and process. Furthermore, time was spent properly educating respondents about the purpose of the study, the procedures, the risks, the benefits, and their rights. Written informed consents were obtained from all participants, and their confidentiality was assured with the agreement that their identities would remain undisclosed. Also, written informed consent was obtained from the parent or legal guardian of respondents under 15, and assent was obtained from each participant to secure their consent to participate in the study. To further safeguard their confidentiality, no personal identifiers were collected from the respondents. In addition, the study did not involve any invasive procedures, so the respondents were not subjected to any physical harm. Participants were allowed to answer the questions in a comfortable environment without public interference. The participants were also informed that they had the right to withdraw from the study at any time without any prejudice.

Results

More than two-thirds of the respondents (68.0%) were in junior high school, slightly above half (53.3%) attended public school, and 3.7% lived in semi-urban areas. Awareness of HPV vaccine

A majority of the respondents (88.9%) had never received information on the HPV vaccine, and for those that had ever received vaccine information, health workers (4.6%) were the major source of information for respondents. The church (0.5%) was the least reported source of information on the HPV vaccine. Other reported sources of HPV information in this study include Mass media (1.9%), social media (3.7%), family members (3.2%), classroom teaching (1.3%), friends (1.1%), and school assembly (1.8%). Parents were the main family members who provided information to them on HPV vaccine, and such information did not include if they had previously taken the HPV vaccine in the past. Knowledge of cervical cancer and HPV

The knowledge level of the study respondents was assessed, and more than half (60.0%) had poor knowledge about cervical cancer and HPV. Some of the respondents (39.8%) stated that cervical cancer is a sexually transmitted infection. Other risk factors identified by some of the respondents include smoking (36.8%), poor dietary intake (27.7%), early sexual activity (31.7%), family history of cervical cancer (25.2%), and having multiple sexual partners (38.7%). About one-third of the respondents (33.9%) stated that HPV is a causative agent of cervical cancer, 10.7% indicated that cervical cancer is caused by evil spirits. Common symptoms of cervical cancer identified by some of the respondents include Intermenstrual bleeding (37.1%), pelvic pain (29.8%), infertility (21.3%), foul-smelling vaginal discharge (36.9%), lengthy and heavier menstruation (32.0%), pain during intercourse (23.4%), persistent diarrhea (22.9%), and post-coital bleeding (18.6%). Some of the respondents (19.9%) believed that cervical cancer is not curable, and 15.6% believed that cervical cancer is not preventable. Almost all the respondents (98.4%) had never heard about pap smear tests. See Table 2 for the knowledge of cervical cancer and HPV.

Level of knowledge, uptake of HPV vaccine and cervical cancer screening

Many of the respondents (60.0%) have poor knowledge about HPV and cervical cancer, some (36.8%) have fair knowledge, and 3.2% have good knowledge about HPV and cervical cancer. None of the respondents have ever received the HPV vaccine, and none have ever been screened against cervical cancer see Fig. 3.

Risk perception and beliefs regarding cervical cancer

The overall risk perception of cervical cancer among the respondents was low with 80.3% perceiving their risk as low, while 19.7% had a high-risk perception of contracting cervical cancer. A total of 125 (19.9%) respondents perceived that they are likely to get cervical cancer in the future, while many (55.6%) believe that their chance of getting cervical cancer in the next few years is low. The thought of cervical cancer scares many of the respondents (53.8%), some (41.9%) believed that cervical cancer does not have a cure while some (39.5%) believed that cervical cancer is not as common as projected. Almost half of the respondents (43.6%) assumed they were not at risk because no one ever had cervical cancer in their family, 19.4% were afraid because they had a family history of cervical cancer, while 15.6% trusted they would not be at risk if they are vaccinated. Barriers and willingness to HPV vaccination

Many barriers to HPV vaccination were highlighted in this study. Most of the respondents (82.2%) identified the lack of awareness about the HPV vaccine as one of the barriers to the HPV vaccine, and most (76.0%) identified the cost of the vaccine as another barrier to HPV vaccination. Ways young girls can be motivated to take the HPV vaccine

Many ways were suggested by the respondents to motivate the uptake of the HPV vaccine, and the creation of awareness was suggested by many of the respondents (69.1%). Some of the respondents (18.9%) highlighted more encouragement as one of the ways to motivate girls to take the HPV vaccine. About 4.1% highlighted that increased accessibility to reduce distance barriers will motivate girls to take the HPV vaccine. Smaller proportions recommended making vaccines painless (2.9%), maintaining good hygiene (1.2%), vaccine promotion (2.5%), and parental support (1.3%) will motivate girls to take the HPV vaccine.

Association between the Socio-demographic characteristics and knowledge of cervical cancer

The chi-square analysis revealed that there was a statistically significant association between the type of religion and knowledge about cervical cancer (χ2 = 14.913, p = 0.001). There was also a statistically significant association between the dwelling location of the respondents and their knowledge of cervical cancer (χ2 = 120.494, p < 0.001). However, the result revealed that there was no statistically significant association between the type of school attended and the knowledge about cervical cancer (χ2 = 0.948, p > 0.05). Discussion

The study assessed the knowledge of cervical cancer and uptake of HPV vaccine among in-school adolescent girls in Kwara State, Nigeria. The findings of the study uncovered that a considerable portion of the respondents lack adequate knowledge of cervical cancer and its risk factors. For instance, the study shows that over a third of respondents (40.0%) had heard about cervical cancer, with health workers being the commonest source of the information. A comparable level of awareness was observed across various subject groups in a study conducted in Lagos State, Nigeria [27] while higher levels of awareness have been reported in other studies conducted in different parts of Nigeria [28, 29]. Only 39.8% of the respondents recognized cervical cancer as a sexually transmitted infection, and 36.8% knew that smoking could be a risk factor while 33.9% stated that HPV is a causative agent of cervical cancer. These notable figures reflect the broader trend observed in other parts of Nigeria. In a study conducted in an urban slum in Lagos, Nigeria [26], it was found that only 22.4% of the respondents were aware that HPV is a causative agent of cervical cancer, indicating a widespread lack of knowledge about the disease and its transmission [26]. These differences would be attributed to the time difference between the studies and the specific subject groups of respondents included in the studies.

The low level of awareness about the HPV vaccine, as seen in our study, where 88.9% of respondents had never received information on the vaccine, and the concomitant poor uptake mirrors findings in other regions of Nigeria. For instance, a study conducted in Lagos found that awareness of the HPV vaccine among adolescent girls was very low, with only 9.7% of respondents having heard about it [29]. This lack of awareness is also a significant barrier to vaccine uptake. The poor knowledge of cervical cancer and HPV vaccine has direct implications for the uptake of preventive measures, as reported in this study where none of the respondents had ever received the HPV vaccine or been screened for cervical cancer. This is consistent with a study conducted in Benin, Nigeria that found less than 1% of the respondents had received the HPV vaccine [30]. This is a significant public health concern, as the HPV vaccine is a critical tool in the prevention of cervical cancer. The lack of vaccination can be attributed to several barriers, including fear of pain, lack of awareness, cost, lack of access to nearby facilities, lack of knowledge of where vaccines can be received, and mistrust in the healthcare system; all these are major determinants of vaccine uptake. These barriers are consistent with findings from other studies across Nigeria, which have identified similar challenges. For example, a study of urban women in Lagos reported that misconceptions about the vaccine and its side effects were major deterrents to HPV vaccination among adolescent girls and women [31]. Also, Ubah et al. [32], in a study of barriers to HPV vaccine conducted in an urban community in Anambra State Nigeria, found a great majority (64.6%) of their respondents claimed that they do not know how often they should go for the screening, did not know at what age this test is necessary, and did not also know when and where to do the screening [32]. It is, therefore, necessary that the government should address HPV vaccine accessibility by making it available across all primary healthcare facilities. The mass campaign and introduction as part of routine immunization is also seen as a bold step in addressing accessibility.

Nevertheless, the willingness to receive the HPV vaccine was relatively high at 71.2%, suggesting that with proper education and removal of barriers, uptake could significantly improve. This finding is supported by previous studies, such as a cross-sectional study among in-school adolescent girls in South-south Nigeria, which found that when adolescents were adequately informed about the benefits of the HPV vaccine, their willingness to be vaccinated increased significantly [21]. Furthermore, the overall risk perception of cervical cancer among the respondents, as shown in this present study, was low, with 80.3% perceiving their risk as low. This is of concern as it indicates a disconnect between knowledge of the disease and the perceived need for preventive action. This low-risk perception is also reported in other Nigerian studies. For instance, a study conducted among female adolescents in selected secondary schools in Ibadan reported that many female adolescents do not perceive themselves to be at risk of cervical cancer, which contributes to the low uptake of HPV vaccination [33].

Our study uncovered some substantial misconceptions about cervical cancer. Over 10% of participants believed that cervical cancer is caused by evil spirits. Others held misguided views, such as thinking that every woman, regardless of age or sexual experience, should undergo screening. Some participants believed that a cervical cancer diagnosis was essentially a death sentence, with survival unlikely beyond five years. Others thought that having multiple sexual partners didn’t increase the risk of cervical cancer or that the disease was less common than projected. These common misconceptions stem from a lack of understanding about cervical cancer and HPV. Our findings highlight the urgent need for targeted education and awareness initiatives to dispel these harmful misconceptions and promote accurate knowledge.

Finally, the study found that those practicing the Islamic religion were more likely to have the intention to uptake the HPV vaccine compared to being Christian. This is contrary to a similar study conducted in Kenya, where being Muslim was found to reduce the odds of HPV vaccine uptake [34]. However, a recent scoping review by Kisa and Kisa [35] found that religious beliefs and practices toward HPV vaccine acceptance in Islamic countries are highly heterogeneous and influenced more by geographical and local religious interpretations than by the religion itself [35]. In the Nigerian context, the observed association may be explained by differential exposure to health messaging, cultural norms, or healthcare-seeking behaviors that vary across religious groups. Given that this is a cross-sectional study, we cannot establish causality, and this finding should be interpreted with caution until further research confirms or refutes it in the Nigerian population.

Contrary to our hypothesis and previous studies [36], we found no statistically significant association between knowledge level and intention to uptake the HPV vaccine in the adjusted model. This finding may be explained by several factors. First, the overall level of knowledge among respondents was very low, with only 3.2% having good knowledge and 60% having poor knowledge. The limited variation in knowledge scores may have reduced the power to detect an association. Second, intention to uptake the vaccine may be influenced more by perceived social norms, trust in healthcare providers, or parental influence than by factual knowledge about cervical cancer and HPV. Previous studies have shown that vaccine acceptance decisions are often driven by emotional and social factors rather than purely knowledge-based considerations [13, 14]. Third, the measurement of knowledge using a dichotomous scale may not fully capture the nuanced understanding that influences vaccine decision-making. These findings suggest that improving knowledge alone may be insufficient to increase vaccine uptake, and interventions should address multiple determinants including access, cultural barriers, and perceived risks.

The findings of this study paint a sobering picture of the preparedness of adolescent girls in Kwara State to benefit from HPV vaccination prior to the 2024 mass vaccination campaign. While willingness to vaccinate was encouraging, it existed largely in a vacuum of accurate knowledge, adequate risk perception, and practical access. These gaps are not insurmountable. Schools and healthcare workers emerged as trusted and potentially effective channels for health communication in this population, and any future intervention that leverages these touchpoints, utilizing messages that are culturally grounded, age-appropriate, and myth-correcting, stands a strong chance of converting expressed willingness into actual vaccine uptake. The baseline data from this study provide a valuable benchmark against which the impact of Nigeria’s national HPV vaccination program can be measured in Kwara State and beyond.

Study limitation

This study is not without limitations. While we used a multistage sampling approach to select LGAs, schools, and respondents, we only selected two urban and two rural LGAs from the state, which may not fully represent the diversity of all LGAs in Kwara State. The exclusion of other LGAs may limit the generalizability of our findings to the entire state. However, efforts were made to ensure representation from both urban, sub-urban, and rural areas, and the selected LGAs were broadly representative of the state’s socio-demographic characteristics. The descriptive cross-sectional design used in this study is prone to certain biases, including recall bias and social desirability bias. Although we used trained interviewers and assured confidentiality to minimize these biases, we could not eliminate them entirely. The cross-sectional design also precludes establishing causal relationships between variables, and the associations identified should be interpreted as correlations rather than causal effects. The study relied on self-reported data, which may not always accurately reflect true behaviors, particularly regarding sensitive topics such as sexual activity and vaccine uptake. We attempted to mitigate this by ensuring confidentiality and using trained interviewers who built rapport with respondents. While our sample size exceeded the minimum required, the low proportion of respondents with good knowledge (3.2%) may have limited our power to detect associations between knowledge and other variables. This also highlights the urgent need for interventions to improve knowledge in this population. It was difficult to verify the authenticity of the expressed views or opinions in this study. The research team could only rely on what the interviewees disclosed or reported during data collection. Therefore, any freely expressed views or opinions by respondents were assumed to be true. This study has not been previously conducted among high school adolescents in the state; hence, the students were not too familiar with some of the terminologies used in this study, which required additional explanation from the research assistants. Adolescent girls above the age of 14 were willing to be part of the study; hence, they openly expressed bias in their exclusion, not minding the reasons behind the priority population of 9–14 years. Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable baseline data on cervical cancer knowledge and HPV vaccine uptake among in-school adolescent girls in Kwara State, and the findings can inform targeted interventions to improve vaccine acceptance and uptake.

Conclusion

The study highlighted critical gaps in knowledge and awareness of cervical cancer and HPV vaccination among adolescents in Kwara State, Nigeria. Various barriers were also identified, which seriously impinged on access to cervical cancer prevention services. Addressing these gaps through targeted health education, addressing the various barriers to accessing HPV vaccination, and dispelling myths can significantly improve both intention to uptake and actual uptake of HPV vaccine. Future interventions should focus on leveraging schools and healthcare workers as primary information sources and ensuring that educational campaigns are culturally and contextually appropriate.
