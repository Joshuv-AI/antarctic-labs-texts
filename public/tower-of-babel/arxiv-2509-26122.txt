Trustworthy AI in numerics: On verification algorithms for neural network-based PDE solvers
Emil Haugen †, Alexei Stepanenko ‡, Anders C. Hansen ‡,†

Abstract
. We present new algorithms for a posteriori verification of neural networks (NNs) approximating solutions to PDEs. These verification algorithms compute accurate estimates of L p L^{p} norms of NNs and their derivatives. When combined with residual bounds for specific PDEs, the algorithms provide guarantees of ε \varepsilon -accuracy (in a suitable norm) with respect to the true, but unknown, solution of the PDE – for arbitrary ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 . In particular, if the NN fails to meet the desired accuracy, our algorithms will detect that and reject it, whereas any NN that passes the verification algorithms is certified to be ε \varepsilon -accurate. This framework enables trustworthy algorithms for NN-based PDE solvers, regardless of how the NN is initially computed. Such a posteriori verification is essential, since a priori error bounds in general cannot guarantee the accuracy of computed solutions, due to algorithmic undecidability of the optimization problems used to train NNs.

1.1.
Necessity of a posteriori error bounds – Undecidability in optimization

Recent theoretical results identify scenarios where optimal NNs can be proven to exist mathematically, yet no algorithm is capable of computing them reliably
[
3
,
6
,
1
,
35
,
21
,
47
,
13
]
. This paradox arises from the following phenomenon. Let
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be convex and consider the computational problem of finding

(1.1)

\tilde{\theta}\in\mathrm{argmin}_{\theta\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{L}(\theta),

where
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is some convex set. For basic objective functions
\mathcal{L}
and constraints
\mathcal{C}
such as linear programming, LASSO and basis pursuit, there are phase transitions of computability/decidability
[
3
,
4
,
21
,
35
,
7
]
(see in particular Problem 5 in
[
34
]
(J. Lagarias)). This occurs when the input is given approximately – yet with arbitrary precision. Hence, it happens in all cases where floating-point arithmetic, which is the basis of scientific computing, is used.
More precisely, the phenomenon can be described as follows: for an approximation threshold
\varepsilon_{1}>0
, an
\varepsilon
-approximation to a minimizer of (
1.1
) can be efficiently computed for
\varepsilon>\varepsilon_{1}
, but becomes undecidable (no algorithm exists to compute it) for
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{1}
.
This undecidability/non-computability phenomenon will of course also occur in the non-convex case when computing NNs
[
6
]
.

Remark 1.1

(Non-computability even in the randomized case)
.

Note that the undecidability or non-computability results above
[
4
,
21
,
35
,
7
,
34
,
5
]
also hold in the randomized case. In particular, when
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{1}
there is no randomized algorithm that can compute an
\varepsilon
-approximation with probability higher than
1/2
. This is crucial, as most optimization algorithms used in modern AI are random. Moreover, the approximation threshold
\varepsilon_{1}
can be in the whole interval
(0,1)
, i.e., for any
\varepsilon_{1}\in(0,1)
, there is a problem class (of LASSO problems, say) that has a phase transition at
\varepsilon_{1}
.

Thus, a priori error bounds for NNs applied to PDEs – whether stated as general existence results or as characterizations of the minimizers of optimization problems – do not, in themselves, guarantee accuracy of the computed network. Such guarantees require bounds that incorporate a detailed analysis of the entire optimization procedure. However, this level of analysis is only feasible in very special cases, since the problem is, in general, non-computable. This leaves one option: a posteriori error bounds. The a posteriori approach is well-established in the classical PDE literature
[
75
,
18
,
19
,
14
]
, but a general framework for its effective application to DL-based solutions is currently lacking.

Suppose now that we are interested in measuring the error of a given approximation
v
(the NN) to the true solution
u
of a PDE in some normed space
X
with norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{X}
. Ideally, we would like to be able to answer the following question.

Question 1.2
.

Given a tolerance
\varepsilon>0
and an approximation
v
of the true, but unknown, solution
u
, is
\lVert u-v\rVert_{X}<\varepsilon
?

However, in general, rigorously estimating the desired norm
\lVert u-v\rVert_{X}
directly will not be feasible. To resolve this, our residual-based approach relies on constructing inequalities of the form

(1.2)

\lVert u-v\rVert_{X}\leq C_{1}\,\lVert\phi[v]\rVert_{Y}\leq C_{2}\,\lVert u-v\rVert_{Z},

where
Y,Z
are (semi)normed spaces
and
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is some given functional arising from the PDE (see
Section

2.2
for the setup we adopt here). Instead of the generally infeasible
1.2
, we then consider the following weaker question.

Question 1.3
.

Can one construct an algorithm with the following property? Suppose
\{v_{m}\}
is a sequence of NNs containing a subsequence converging to the unknown true solution
u
of a given PDE in the normed space
X
. Given
\varepsilon>0
, the algorithm determines an approximation
v_{m}
such that
\lVert u-v_{m}\rVert_{X}<\varepsilon
.

As we show in this paper,
1.3
can be answered affirmatively in many cases by applying the framework (
1.2
).

Remark 1.2
.

In practice one is not given a sequence as in
1.3
, but this formalizes the idea that iterative training procedures yield better and better approximations. This is similar to classical approaches such as finite element or finite difference methods, where the convergence analysis typically requires some parameter
h
to tend to zero, which of course does not happen in practice. Our verification algorithms will show that for an approximation
v
we can guarantee that
\lVert u-v\rVert_{X}<\varepsilon_{1}
, which is a true, but often pessimistic estimate. Indeed, typically
\lVert u-v\rVert_{X}<\varepsilon_{2}
and
\varepsilon_{2}\leq\varepsilon_{1}
, but we will never know
\varepsilon_{2}
.

A program on trustworthiness of AI methods in PDEs – Undecidability included:

Residual bounds for PDEs – leading to error bounds for approximate solutions as in (

However, caution is warranted. Just as undecidability plays a role in optimization and construction of NNs, undecidability of the location of solutions to PDEs (see Tao’s program on embedding of universal Turing machines into flows of differential equations and undecidability in PDEs

Neural networks and rigorous verification

Fix

throughout and let

act on an input vector

(2.1)

\begin{split}z^{1}(x)&\coloneqq W^{1}x+b^{1},\,\qquad a^{1}(x)\coloneqq\mu(z^{1}(x)),\\
z^{2}(x)&\coloneqq W^{2}a^{1}(x)+b^{2},\quad a^{2}(x)\coloneqq\mu(z^{2}(x)),\\
&\quad\vdots\\
z^{L}(x)&\coloneqq W^{L}a^{L-1}(x)+b^{L-1},\quad a^{L}(x)\coloneqq\mu(z^{L}(x))\\
f(x)&\coloneqq W^{L+1}a^{L}(x)+b^{L}.\end{split}

The

and

\mathrm{ReLU}(t)=\max\{0,t\},\quad\mathrm{RePU}(t)=\max\{0,t\}^{n},

Let

with a RePU activation function of order

.

Notation 2.1

For

we denote by

Let

(a\odot b)_{j}=a_{j}b_{j}

2.1.
Approximation error through residual bounds in PDEs

The idea of using residual bounds to estimate the accuracy of an approximate solution to a PDE is straightforward. One substitutes the approximation into the PDE to obtain residual functions, and in many cases these residuals can be related directly to the error of the approximation. We illustrate the approach with two classical examples: the heat equation and the wave equation.

The heat equation – Deriving approximation error

For any
d\geq 2
, let
U\coloneqq[-1,1]^{d-1}
and consider the heat equation,

(2.2)

\begin{cases}\hfill(\partial_{t}-\kappa\Delta)u=0\quad&\mathrm{in}\,\,U\times(0,T]\\
\hfill u=g\quad&\mathrm{on}\,\,U\times\{t=0\}\\
\hfill u=0\quad&\mathrm{on}\,\,\partial U\times[0,T].\\
\end{cases}

where
\Delta
denotes the Laplacian in space,

T,\kappa>0
are the time horizon and diffusivity constant, respectively, and
g\colon U\to\mathbb{R}
represents the initial condition.
Let
u\colon U\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
denote the unique weak solution to (
2.2
)
[
31
]
and suppose that we are given an approximation
v\colon U\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
. Under mild regularity assumptions and the requirement that
v|_{\partial U\times[0,T]}\equiv 0
, the approximation error is bounded by the following
energy estimate
(see
Theorem

4.1
for the precise statement):

(2.3)

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\lVert u-v\rVert_{L^{2}(U)}\leq C\left\lparen\lVert(\partial_{t}-\kappa\Delta)v\rVert_{L^{2}((U\times[0,T]))}+\lVert(v(\cdot,0)-g)\rVert_{L^{2}(U)}\right\rparen,

for an explicit constant
C>0
. Observe that the right hand side of (
2.3
) depends on known functions
v
and
g
but not on the (unknown) true solution
u
. Thus, a posteriori error estimates can be provided if one can compute accurate estimates of the right hand side of (
2.3
).

The wave equation – Deriving approximation error

Next, consider the wave equation

(2.4)

\begin{cases}\hfill(\partial_{t}^{2}-\Delta)u=0\quad&\mathrm{in}\,\,\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T]\\
\hfill u=g\quad&\mathrm{on}\,\,\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{t=0\}\\
\hfill\partial_{t}u=0\quad&\mathrm{on}\,\,\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{t=0\}.\\
\end{cases}

Suppose that
u
solves (
2.4
)
and that
v
is a proposed approximation of
u
.
In this case,
[
71
, eqn. 6]
can be used as a residual bound estimating the error
e\coloneqq v-u
. In particular, the error is bounded for
1<p<\infty
and
0<t<T
by

(2.5)

\lVert e(\cdot,t)\rVert_{L^{p}_{x}}\leq C\left\lparen\lVert v(\cdot,0)-g\rVert_{W^{k,p}}+\lVert\partial_{t}v(\cdot,0)\rVert_{W^{k-1,p}}+\lVert(\partial^{2}_{t}-\Delta)v\rVert_{L^{1}([0,t];W^{k-1,p})}\right\rparen

for any integer
k\geq(d-1)\lvert\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}\rvert
, where the constant
C
only depends on
p,d,T
, and
W^{k,p}
denotes the standard Sobolev spaces. Moreover these bounds are
sharp

[
69
,
60
]
, underscoring the need for rigorous and computable Sobolev norm estimates for NNs in order to make trustworthy approximations using e.g. PINNs.

2.2.
The general numerical problem and choosing the quadrature

The inequalities (
2.3
) and (
2.5
) demonstrate that to obtain a posteriori error bounds for NNs in PDEs, we need methods to estimate
L^{p}
norms given by

(2.6)

\lVert\phi[f]\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}=\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\phi[f](x)\right\rvert^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x

over some bounded domain
\Omega
, where the function
\phi[f]
is a (for the purposes of the current paper) linear combination of derivatives of
f
up to some given order
m\in\mathbb{N}
,

(2.7)

\phi[f](x)=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}c_{\alpha}\,\partial^{\alpha}f(x),\quad c_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}.

For example,

\phi[f]=(\partial_{t}-\kappa\Delta)f

in the case of the heat equation.
We will assume that
\Omega
can be written as a finite union of rectangles:

(2.8)

\Omega=\bigcup_{k}\mathcal{B}_{k},\qquad\mathcal{B}_{k}\coloneqq\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon_{k}}(y_{k})\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\lVert x-y_{k}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon_{k}/2\},

for some
y_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\varepsilon_{k}>0
, so that the
\mathcal{B}_{k}
have pairwise disjoint interiors with volumes
|\mathcal{B}_{k}|=\varepsilon_{k}^{d}
. Given (
2.8
), we shall employ a midpoint quadrature to estimate (
2.6
),

(2.9)

\lVert\phi[f]\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}\approx I_{p}(\phi[f])\coloneqq\sum_{k}|\mathcal{B}_{k}|\,\left\lvert\phi[f](y_{k})\right\rvert^{p}.

Since we are interested in integrands on the form of (
2.7
), we first focus on:

General numerical problem: estimate
\lVert\partial^{\alpha}f\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}
given some multi-index
\alpha
.

To this end we shall use the following local error estimate for midpoint quadrature.

Proposition 2.1

(Local quadrature error)
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha
be a multi-index such that
\partial^{\alpha}f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\varepsilon>0
and define
\mathcal{B}\coloneqq\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(y)
as in (
2.8
). Then the following estimate holds.

(2.10)

\begin{split}\left|\int_{\mathcal{B}}\left\lvert\partial^{\alpha}f(x)\right\rvert^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x-|\mathcal{B}|\,\left\lvert\partial^{\alpha}f(y)\right\rvert^{p}\right|&\leq p\frac{\varepsilon^{d+1}\sqrt{d}}{2}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{B}}\lVert\partial^{\alpha}f(x)^{p-1}\,\nabla(\partial^{\alpha}f)(x)\rVert.\end{split}

Proof.

Noting that

\left\lvert|\partial^{\alpha}f(x)|^{p}-|\partial^{\alpha}f(y)|^{p}\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert\partial^{\alpha}f(x)^{p}-\partial^{\alpha}f(y)^{p}\right\rvert
, the inequality follows from a standard application of Taylor’s formula and the chain rule for
(\partial^{\alpha}f)^{p}
.
∎

The above bound shows that what is needed to rigorously estimate norms
\lVert\partial^{\alpha}f\rVert_{L^{p}}
are (local) bounds for the derivatives of
f
, which have previously not been available (see
Remark

3.1
). Such bounds are the main technical contribution of the present paper.

Remark 2.2

(On choice of quadrature)
.

As we shall see, bounding derivatives of NNs becomes harder as the order increases.
Proposition

2.1
shows that the midpoint error estimate only requires bounds on one extra order of derivatives, so this is a natural choice of quadrature. Our methods can also be used for
\phi
which are non-linear in
f
, but we focus on the linear case in this work.
For conciseness we will construct estimates for
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L^{p}}^{p}
. A simple Taylor’s formula argument for
t\mapsto t^{1/p}
then provides the estimate for the
L^{p}
norm.

Remark 2.3

(Operator learning)
.

We will work under the assumption that the approximation is essentially an NN. However, the methods developed in this paper can also potentially be applied to approximations constructed via operator learning. For instance, an approximation
v
can be given by (see e.g.
[
45
, Section 6.1]
)

v(x)=\int_{D}\kappa(x,y)\,f(y)dy

where
D
is a bounded domain,
\kappa
is a learned NN and
f
is some function given by the data from the PDE. Then derivative bounds for
v
follow e.g. from

\displaystyle|\partial_{i}v(x)|\leq\int_{D}|\partial_{x_{i}}\kappa(x,y)f(y)|dy\leq\lVert\partial_{i}\kappa(x,\cdot)\rVert_{L^{2}(D)}\,\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(D)}

The first
L^{2}
norm can be bounded using the methods developed in this paper. The second norm must be bounded using assumptions on the data
f
. Once these derivative bounds are computed, the residual-based approach may be employed identically. This is an avenue for further work.

3.
Algorithms for derivative estimates of neural networks

This section provides our main technical contributions, namely algorithms for derivative estimates of NNs needed for the construction of verification algorithms. From (
2.10
) we see that what is needed to obtain quadrature error estimates is a method to compute, given
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\varepsilon>0
, upper bounds for

\left\lvert\partial^{\alpha}f(x)\right\rvert\text{ and }\lVert\nabla\partial^{\alpha}f(x)\rVert\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(y).

To achieve this, we shall construct algorithms
\mathrm{Alg}^{k},k\in\mathbb{N}
which take as inputs a multi-index
\alpha
,
f
(via point samples of
f
and its activation function
\mu
) a point in
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a radius
\varepsilon>0
, whose output satisfies

(3.1)

\left\lvert\partial^{\alpha}f(x)-\partial^{\alpha}f(y)\right\rvert\leq\mathrm{Alg}^{|\alpha|}(\alpha,f,y,\varepsilon)\,\text{ for all }\,x\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(y).

Remark 3.1
.

Computing Lipschitz-constants for NNs is a well-studied problem in the ML literature, see e.g.
[
33
]
. However, computing similar estimates for higher order derivatives has not attracted the same attention, as pointed out in
[
67
]
.

Our approach is based on exact formulas for partial derivatives of NNs. Obtaining such a formula for any
\partial^{\alpha}f
for
f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{L}
with
L
and
\alpha
arbitrary is difficult. However, they can be constructed inductively. If
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
and
f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{1}
then from (
2.1
), observing that
\partial_{i}z^{1}_{k}(x)=W^{1}_{ki}
, we have

\partial_{i}f(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{n_{1}}W^{2}_{k}\,\mu^{\prime}(z^{1}_{k}(x))\,W^{1}_{ki}.

Iteratively differentiating the above equation and writing the sum as a matrix-vector product, for a general multi-index
\alpha
and
f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{1}
this gives (recall
2.1
),

(3.2)

\begin{split}\partial^{\alpha}f(x)&=W^{2}\left\lparen\mu^{(|\alpha|)}(z^{1}(x))\odot F(\alpha)\right\rparen,\\
F(\alpha)&\coloneqq\bigodot_{k=1}^{d}(W^{1}e_{k})^{\alpha_{k}}.\qquad\text{(Element-wise exponent.)}\end{split}

Now let
f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{L}
with arbitrary
L\in\mathbb{N}
. We use (
3.2
) as a base case of a recursive procedure for computing
\partial^{\alpha}f(x)
. By (
2.1
) we have

(3.3)

\partial^{\alpha}f(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{n_{L}}W^{L+1}_{k}\partial^{\alpha}(\mu(z^{L}_{k}(x)).

By the multivariate Faà di Bruno’s formula
[
39
, Proposition 5]
we have

(3.4)

\partial^{\alpha}(\mu(z^{L}_{k}(x)))=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{|\alpha|}}\mu^{(|\alpha|)}(z^{L}_{k}(x))\prod_{B\in\pi}\frac{\partial^{|B|}(z^{L}_{k}(x))}{\prod_{j\in B}\partial x_{j}}.

Here
\mathcal{P}_{m}
is the set of all partitions of the set
\{1,\dots,m\}
for
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
B\in\pi
means that
B
runs through all the subsets in the partition
\pi
.
Since
z^{L}_{k}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{L-1}
and we have the explicit (
3.2
) for
L=1
, inserting (
3.4
) into (
3.3
) gives an iterative procedure for computing
\partial^{\alpha}f(x)
.

The size of
\mathcal{P}_{m}
is known as the
Bell number

B_{m}
, and these increase exponentially,
B_{2}=2,B_{3}=5,B_{4}=15,B_{5}=52,B_{6}=203\dots
. Simultaneously, one recursive step is needed for each hidden layer. Thus using exact formulas for large
|\alpha|
and
L
poses a significant computational challenge. Therefore, in
Section

3.1
we focus on the case
|\alpha|=1
for general
L\in\mathbb{N}
, and in
Section

3.2
we consider
|\alpha|\in\{2,3\}
for
L=2
.

3.1.
First order derivative bounds

Throughout we fix
f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{L},y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\varepsilon>0
and
x\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(y)
. For
l=1,\dots,d
, letting
\alpha\coloneqq e_{l}
we shall construct the right hand side of (
3.1
).
Our analysis hinges on the consideration of the vectors (recall (
2.1
))

D^{k}(x,y)\coloneqq\mu^{\prime}(z^{k}(x))-\mu^{\prime}(z^{k}(y)),\quad k\in\{1,\dots,L\},

where the (weak) derivative
\mu^{\prime}
of the activation function is applied component-wise.
In the case of a ReLU activation function, these vectors represent the change in
activation status
of the neurons in the
k^{\mathrm{th}}
hidden layer as the input changes from
y
to
x
. In particular, our algorithm shall compute non-negative numbers
Q^{k}_{j}
for
k\in\{1,\dots,L\},\,j\in\{1,\dots,N_{k}\}
which satisfy

(3.5)

|\mu^{\prime}(z^{k}_{j}(x))-\mu^{\prime}(z^{k}_{j}(y))|\leq Q^{k}_{j}.

We arrange the numbers
\{Q^{k}_{j}\}_{j}
into vectors
Q^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}
and refer to inequalities (
3.5
) as
activation bounds
. For later notational convenience we define,

(3.6)

\xi^{k}\coloneqq\mu^{\prime}(z^{k}(x)),\quad\xi_{0}^{k}\coloneqq\mu^{\prime}(z^{k}(y)),\quad D^{k}\coloneqq\xi^{k}-\xi^{k}_{0},\quad k=1,\dots,L.

Supposing that we have computed vectors
Q^{1},\dots,Q^{L}
such that the activation bounds (
3.5
) hold for the NN
f
, we shall show in
Section

3.1.2
how to compute a function
\mathrm{bound}_{L}^{(l)}
satisfying

(3.7)

|\partial_{l}f(x)-\partial_{l}f(y)|\leq\mathrm{bound}_{L}^{(l)}(f;Q^{1},\dots,Q^{L}).

3.1.1.
Computing activation bounds

The computation of the
Q^{1},\dots,Q^{L}
above is performed sequentially starting at
Q^{1}
by alternating between the following two inequalities.

1.

Given the activation function
\mu
there exist computable functions

\widehat{Q}^{j,k}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+},\qquad k\in\{1,\dots,L\},\quad j\in\{1,\dots,N_{k}\},

such that given numbers
E^{k}_{j}>0
satisfying

\lVert\nabla z_{j}^{k}(x)\rVert\leq E^{k}_{j}
, we have

|\mu^{\prime}(z^{k}_{j}(x))-\mu^{\prime}(z^{k}_{j}(y))|\leq\widehat{Q}^{j,k}(E^{k}_{j}).

We therefore set
Q^{k}_{j}\coloneqq\widehat{Q}^{j,k}(E^{k}_{j})
.
See (
3.8
), where such functions
\widehat{Q}^{j,k}
are constructed explicitly for the
\mathrm{ReLU}
and
\mathrm{RePU}
activations.

2.

Let
k\in\{2,\dots,L\}
.
Suppose we have computed
Q^{1},\dots,Q^{k-1}
satisfying (
3.5
).
Notice that for each
j\in\{1,\dots,N_{k}\}
, we have

z^{k}_{j}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1}.

Hence, we may apply inequality (
3.7
), which readily implies that

\lVert\nabla z_{j}^{k}(x)\rVert\leq\left\lparen\sum_{m=1}^{d}\left\lparen|\partial_{m}z_{j}^{k}(y)|+\mathrm{bound}^{(m)}_{k-1}(z_{j}^{k};Q^{1},\dots,Q^{k-1})\right\rparen^{2}\right\rparen^{\frac{1}{2}}

We therefore set
E^{k}_{j}
to be the right hand side of the above inequality.

Recalling that
n
is the order of the RePU activation function, set

(3.8)

\begin{split}\widehat{Q}^{(j,k)}_{\mathrm{ReLU}}(X)&\coloneqq\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\varepsilon X<|z_{j}^{k}(y)|\\
1&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},\quad X>0.\\
\widehat{Q}^{(j,k,n)}_{\mathrm{RePU}}(X)&\coloneqq\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\frac{(\varepsilon X)^{m}}{m!}|\mu^{(m+1)}(z_{j}^{k}(y))|+n(\varepsilon X)^{n-1}\widehat{Q}^{(j,k)}_{\mathrm{ReLU}}(X),\quad X>0.\end{split}

The following lemma allows us to compute the activation bounds (
3.5
) and is proved in
Section

A.1.1
. The corresponding statement for
\mu=\mathrm{RePU}
is postponed until
Lemma

3.3
, where we also deal with higher order derivatives.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
k\in\{1,\dots,L\}
and
j\in\{1,\dots,N_{k}\}
.
If
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\varepsilon>0
and
E^{k}_{j}\geq 0
satisfies
\lVert\nabla z_{j}^{k}(x)\rVert\leq E^{k}_{j}
for
x\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(y)
then,

|\mu^{\prime}(z^{k}_{j}(x))-\mu^{\prime}(z^{k}_{j}(y))|\leq\widehat{Q}^{(j,k)}_{\mathrm{ReLU}}(E^{k}_{j}),\quad x\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(y).

3.1.2.
From activation bounds to first derivative bounds

We now explain how to get from activation bounds (
3.5
) to the derivative bounds (
3.7
), i.e., how to construct the functions
\mathrm{Bound}^{(l)}_{L}
. The starting observation is that for
l=1,\dots,d
, (recall the notation (
3.6
))

(3.9)

\partial_{l}f(x)=W^{L+1}\xi^{L}\odot\cdots\odot W^{2}\xi^{1}\odot W^{1}e_{l}.

Replacing
\xi^{k}
with
\xi^{k}_{0}
yields
\partial_{l}f(y)
. To keep the exposition concise, we give the details for the case of
L=2
hidden layers, but the method for first derivatives can be extended to any arbitrary
L\in\mathbb{N}
(see
Remark

3.2
), and this is implemented in
[
40
]
. Combining the formula (
3.9
) with the activation bounds readily gives a uniform bound on the
difference
of derivatives.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose
f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{2}
. Let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\varepsilon>0
and
x\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(y)
. If the
Q^{k}_{j}
satisfy (
3.5
), then for
l=1,\dots,d
,

(3.10)

\begin{split}\left\lvert\partial_{l}f(x)-\partial_{l}f(y)\right\rvert\leq\mathrm{Bound}_{2}^{(l)}(f;Q^{1},Q^{2})&\coloneqq\left\lvert W^{3}\right\rvert\Big[Q^{2}\odot\left\lvert W^{2}\right\rvert Q^{1}\odot\left\lvert W^{1}e_{l}\right\rvert+\\
Q^{2}\odot\left\lvert W^{2}\xi^{1}_{0}\odot W^{1}e_{l}\right\rvert&\,\,\,+\lvert\xi^{2}_{0}\rvert\odot\left\lvert W^{2}\right\rvert Q^{1}\odot\left\lvert W^{1}e_{l}\right\rvert\Big]\end{split}

Proof.

Substituting
\xi^{k}=D^{k}+\xi^{k}_{0}
into (
3.9
), we have

(3.11)

\begin{split}\partial_{l}f(x)-\partial_{l}f(y)=W^{3}\Big[&(D^{2}+\xi^{2}_{0})\odot W^{2}(D^{1}+\xi^{1}_{0})-\xi^{2}_{0}\odot W^{2}\xi^{1}_{0}\Big]\odot W^{1}e_{l}\end{split}

Observe that when distributing the products the term with only
\xi^{k}_{0}
cancels out and we are left with three terms on the RHS of (
3.11
).
The activation bounds (
3.5
) say precisely that
Q^{k}\geq\lvert D^{k}\rvert
element-wise and so (
3.10
) follows.
∎

Remark 3.2

(More than two hidden layers)
.

Lemma

3.2
constructs

on the RHS of (

) for

. We omit the details of this procedure since they do not provide any important insights, and refer to
[

]
for the implementation of

.

4.
A posteriori verification via
L^{p}
norms of neural network derivatives

In this section we apply the algorithms from
Section

3
to construct estimates for
L^{p}
norms of NNs and their derivatives as suggested by (

). These estimates in turn result in rigorous verifications of NNs solving the heat equation (

4.1.
Energy estimate for the heat equation and the residual framework

Recall that (
2.2
) has a unique, weak solution
[
31
, Section 6.2]
. The following energy estimate allows us to bound the error by a quantity only depending on the approximation and not on the unknown solution. This establishes the framework (
1.2
) for the heat equation.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose
u\in C([0,T],L^{2}(U))
is the weak solution of (
2.2
) for some
T,\kappa>0
and
g\in C^{1}(U)
with
g|_{\partial U}=0
.
Let
v\in L^{2}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(U))
. Then, it holds for some constant
C>0
that

(4.1)

\begin{split}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\lVert u-v\rVert_{L^{2}(U)}&\leq\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\pi\sqrt{\kappa}}\lVert(\partial_{t}-\kappa\Delta)v\rVert_{L^{2}(U_{T})}+\lVert v(\cdot,0)-g\rVert_{L^{2}(U)}\\
&\leq C\lVert u-v\rVert_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(U))}\end{split}

Proof.

Consider
Proposition

A.1
with
e\coloneqq v-u,f\coloneqq(\partial_{t}-\kappa\Delta)v
and
h\coloneqq v(\cdot,0)-g
.
Using
\varepsilon=\kappa\pi^{2}/d
, taking supremum over
0\leq t\leq T
on both sides and taking square roots before using
\sqrt{a+b}\leq\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}
on the RHS yields the first inequality. Since
u
is the weak solution to (
2.2
), we have
g\equiv u(\cdot,0)
as well as

\langle(\partial_{t}-\kappa\Delta)u,w\rangle_{L^{2}(U)}=0
for all
w\in H_{0}^{1}(U)
and almost all
0\leq t\leq T
, and so the second inequality follows in similar manner.
∎

Remark 4.1
.

More sophisticated estimates than (
4.1
) for more general PDEs are certainly available in the literature, see e.g.
[
31
, Section 7.2]
, or
[
20
,
9
,
30
]
. However, to keep the demonstration of our methodology succinct,
Theorem

4.1
will suffice.

We shall now estimate the two
L^{2}
norms on the RHS of (
4.1
) with
v\coloneqq\chi f,f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{2}^{(n)},n\geq 3
, and
\chi|_{\partial U\times[0,T]}=0
. For these two norms, associated with the
PDE residual
and the
initial data
respectively, we define

\phi_{\mathcal{P}}[f](x)\coloneqq(\partial_{t}-\kappa\Delta)v(x),\quad\phi_{0}[f](x)\coloneqq v(\cdot,0)-g.

For readability we suppress
f
in the above notation going forward. We estimate the
squared

L^{2}
norms, c.f.
Remark

2.2
.

Notation and assumptions

The operator
\Delta
acts on the spatial variables and
\partial_{t}=\partial_{d}
. The function used to enforce the boundary conditions is

\chi(x)\coloneqq(1-x_{1}^{2})\cdots(1-x_{d-1}^{2})
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Assumption 4.1
.

We are given
\varepsilon>0
and a finite set
Y_{\mathrm{grid}}=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{N}\}\subset U_{T}
so that letting

Y_{0}\coloneqq\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\colon(y,\varepsilon/2)\in Y_{\mathrm{grid}}\},
then
U_{T}
and
U
can be written as:

(4.2)

\begin{split}U_{T}&=\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(y_{k}),\quad\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(y_{k})\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\lVert x-y_{k}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon/2\},\\
U&=\bigcup_{y\in Y_{0}}\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(y),\quad\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(y)\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\colon\lVert x-y\rVert_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon/2\},\end{split}

where the rectangles have pairwise disjoint interiors. We also make a standard assumption on the derivatives of
g
, namely that we have
uniform
bounds
\mathcal{G}^{i}
satisfying

(4.3)

\left\lvert\partial_{i}g(x)\right\rvert\leq\mathcal{G}^{i},\quad x\in U.

4.2.
Bounding the residual norms

Setup complete, we now estimate the two
L^{2}
norms on the right hand side of (
4.1
), corresponding to the
Y
norm in (
1.2
).

4.2.1.
Estimating the
L^{2}
norm of

\phi_{\mathcal{P}}

As indicated in (
2.9
), we use the midpoint quadrature estimate

(4.4)

\lVert\phi_{\mathcal{P}}\rVert_{L^{2}(U_{T})}^{2}\approx I_{2}(\phi_{\mathcal{P}})\coloneqq\sum_{y\in Y_{\mathrm{grid}}}\varepsilon^{d}\phi_{\mathcal{P}}(y)^{2}.

As demonstrated by (
2.10
), we require local bounds for
|\phi_{\mathcal{P}}|
and
\lVert\nabla\phi_{\mathcal{P}}\rVert
. The presence of the function
\chi
simply introduces some extra terms via the product rule. The desired bounds follow from the below lemma, which is proved in
Section

A.3.1
using the derivative bounds from
Section

3
to construct the key function
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{2}^{(n)},y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\varepsilon>0
and
\phi_{\mathcal{P}}=(\partial_{t}-\kappa\Delta)(\chi f)
be as above.
There exists a computable function
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

satisfying,

(4.5)

\lVert\nabla\phi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)\rVert\leq\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(y,\varepsilon),\quad x\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(y).

By
4.1
,
Proposition

4.1
readily implies a global quadrature error bound.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
U_{T}
be given by (
4.2
) and
f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{2}^{(n)}
,
I_{2}(\phi_{\mathcal{P}})
defined as in (
4.4
).
Then the global error for the quadrature estimate (
4.4
) satisfies

(4.6)

\bigg|\lVert\phi_{\mathcal{P}}\rVert_{L^{2}(U_{T})}^{2}-I_{2}(\phi_{\mathcal{P}})\bigg|\leq\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{P}}\coloneqq\sum_{y\in Y_{\mathrm{grid}}}R_{\mathcal{P}}(y,\varepsilon)\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}0,

where
R_{\mathcal{P}}(y,\varepsilon)\coloneqq\varepsilon^{d+1}\sqrt{d}\,\left\lparen\left\lvert\phi_{\mathcal{P}}(y)\right\rvert+\frac{\varepsilon\sqrt{d}}{2}\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(y,\varepsilon)\right\rparen\,\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(y,\varepsilon)
.

Proof.

Let
y\in Y_{\mathrm{grid}}
be an arbitrary quadrature point as in
4.1
and let
\varepsilon>0,x\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(y)
. Noting that
\lvert\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(y)\rvert=\varepsilon^{d}
, using (
2.10
) with
p=2
we have

(4.7)

\begin{split}\left|\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(y)}\phi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x-\varepsilon^{d}\phi_{\mathcal{P}}(y)^{2}\right|&\leq\varepsilon^{d+1}\sqrt{d}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(y)}\lVert\phi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)\nabla\phi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)\rVert.\end{split}

By
Proposition

4.1
and Taylor’s formula for
\phi_{\mathcal{P}}
,

\left\lvert\phi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert\phi_{\mathcal{P}}(y)\right\rvert+\frac{\varepsilon\sqrt{d}}{2}\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(y,\varepsilon).

Since
U_{T}
is given by (
4.2
),
inserting this bound and (
4.5
) into (
4.7
) and summing over
y\in Y_{\mathrm{grid}}
, inequality (
4.6
) follows.

The derivative bounds
\mathcal{E}^{(\alpha)}
are constructed via finite linear combinations and compositions of continuous or bounded functions on a bounded domain, and hence
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}
is bounded on say
U_{T}\times[0,1]
. Since the number of points in

is proportional to

, we have
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{P}}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
∎

\lVert\phi_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}(U)}^{2}\approx I_{2}(\phi_{0})\coloneqq\sum_{y\in Y_{0}}\varepsilon^{d-1}\phi_{0}(y)^{2}.

Let

4.3.
Verification algorithms

We give an informal “local” verification algorithm and a “global” one which together provide an affirmative answer to
1.3
.

Local verification algorithm

Given
v=\chi f
and a desired accuracy
\varepsilon_{0}>0
, we want to verify that
v
is within
\varepsilon_{0}
of the unknown
u
in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(U))
.

(1)

input:
v=\chi f
,
\varepsilon_{0}>0
, bounds
\mathcal{G}^{i}
as in (
4.3
).

(2)

initialize
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
(e.g. take largest) so that
\exists N_{x},N_{T}\in\mathbb{N},N_{T}\varepsilon=T,N_{x}\varepsilon=1
and construct the corresponding grid of
U_{T}
(and of
U
) as in
4.1
.

(3)

compute quadrature estimates
I_{2}(\phi_{i})
and error bounds
\mathcal{R}_{i},i\in\{\mathcal{P},0\}
as in
Section

4.2
over the grids using the algorithms from
Section

3
. By Taylor’s formula for
t\mapsto\sqrt{t}
,

(4.11)

\left\lvert\lVert\phi_{i}\rVert-I_{2}(\phi_{i})\right\rvert\leq\mathcal{R}_{i}|I_{2}(\phi_{i})-\mathcal{R}_{i}|^{-1/2}\eqqcolon B_{i}(\varepsilon).

(4)

if

(4.12)

\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\pi\sqrt{\kappa}}\left\lparen I_{2}(\phi_{\mathcal{P}})+B_{\mathcal{P}}(\varepsilon)\right\rparen+\left\lparen I_{2}(\phi_{0})+B_{0}(\varepsilon)\right\rparen<\varepsilon_{0},

halt and return YES.
Otherwise set
\varepsilon\leftarrow\varepsilon/2
and start over from point 3.

Even though
\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\lVert u-v\rVert_{L^{2}(U)}<\varepsilon_{0}
there is in general no guarantee that the local verification algorithm will ever halt. This motivates the global algorithm.

Global verification algorithm

Given the local verification algorithm we can finally formulate the global algorithm which affirmatively answers
1.3
. We have given the explicit construction for the heat equation, but the same procedure works for any PDE which has residual bounds of the form (
4.1
) (containing up to two derivatives given our algorithms in
Section

3
).

(1)

input: tolerance
\varepsilon_{0}>0
, sequence
\{v_{m}=\chi f_{m}\},f_{m}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{2}^{(n)},n\geq 3
s.t.
\exists
subsequence
v_{m_{k}}\to u
in
L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(U))
. Bounds
\mathcal{G}^{i}
as in (
4.3
).

(2)

iteratively start running the local algorithm on each pair
(v_{m},\varepsilon_{0})
.

(3)

output the first index
m
where the local algorithm halts.

The global algorithm will halt after finitely many steps, because there exists a smallest
m\in\mathbb{N}
so that
\lVert u-v_{m}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(U))}<\varepsilon_{0}/C
with
C
as in (
4.1
). Thus when running the local algorithm on
(v_{m},\varepsilon_{0})
we have,

\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\pi\sqrt{\kappa}}\lVert\phi_{\mathcal{P}}\rVert+\lVert\phi_{0}\rVert<\varepsilon_{0}.

Since
\mathcal{R}_{i}\to 0
and hence
B_{i}(\varepsilon)\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
for
i\in\{\mathcal{P},0\}
, (
4.11
) implies that after finitely many iterations in the local verification algorithm, the criterion (
4.12
) will be satisfied and the global algorithm halts.

and

A.1.1.
Proof of
Lemma

3.1
for
\mathrm{ReLU}
activation functions

Let
\mu=\mathrm{ReLU}
and
x\in B_{\varepsilon}(y)
.
By assumption
\lVert\nabla z_{j}^{k}(x)\rVert\leq E^{k}_{j}
, and
\lVert x-y\rVert\leq\varepsilon\sqrt{d}/2
, hence Taylor’s theorem yields

(A.1)

|z^{k}_{j}(x)-z^{k}_{j}(y)|\leq\varepsilon\sqrt{d}E^{k}_{j}/2.

Observe that (since
\mathrm{ReLU}^{\prime}
only changes if the input changes sign)

\mu^{\prime}(z^{k}_{j}(x))=\mu^{\prime}(z^{k}_{j}(y))\qquad\text{if}\qquad|z^{k}_{j}(x)-z^{k}_{j}(y)|<|z^{k}_{j}(y)|.

so by (
A.1
),

\mu^{\prime}(z^{k}_{j}(x))-\mu^{\prime}(z^{k}_{j}(y))=0

if

\varepsilon\sqrt{d}E^{k}_{j}<2|z^{k}_{j}(y)|.

Therefore,

|\mu^{\prime}(z^{k}_{j}(x))-\mu^{\prime}(z^{k}_{j}(y))|\leq\widehat{Q}^{(j,k)}_{\mathrm{ReLU}}(E^{k}_{j})\coloneqq\begin{cases}0&\text{if}\quad\varepsilon\sqrt{d}|E^{k}_{j}|<2|z^{k}_{j}(y)|\\
1&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

A.1.2.
Proof of
Lemma

3.3
for
\mathrm{RePU}
activation functions

Let
\mu=\mathrm{RePU}
and
x\in B_{\varepsilon}(y)
.
By Taylor’s theorem with integral form for the remainder, we have

\mu^{(i)}(s)=\sum_{m=0}^{n-i-1}\frac{(s-s_{0})^{m}}{m!}\mu^{(m+i)}(s_{0})+\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\frac{\mu^{(n)}(t)}{(n-i-1)!}(s-t)^{n-i-1}\,\,\mathrm{d}t,\quad s,s_{0}\in\mathbb{R}.

Rearranging terms,

\mu^{(i)}(s)-\mu^{(i)}(s_{0})=\sum_{m=1}^{n-i}\frac{(s-s_{0})^{m}}{m!}\mu^{(m+i)}(s_{0})+\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\frac{\mu^{(n)}(t)-\mu^{(n)}(s_{0})}{(n-i-1)!}(s-t)^{n-i-1}\,\,\mathrm{d}t.

Substituting in
z_{j}^{k}(x)
for
s
,
z_{j}^{k}(y)
for
s_{0}
and since (
A.1
) holds also here, we obtain

|\mu^{(i)}(z^{k}_{j}(x))-\mu^{(i)}(z^{k}_{j}(y))|\\
\leq\sum_{m=1}^{n-i}\frac{(\varepsilon\sqrt{d}E^{k}_{j}/2)^{m}}{m!}|\mu^{(m+i)}(z_{j}^{k}(y))|+\frac{n!}{(n-i)!}(\varepsilon\sqrt{d}E^{k}_{j}/2)^{n-i}a_{j}^{(k)},

where

a_{j}^{(k)}\coloneqq\sup_{w\in B_{\varepsilon}(y)}\frac{1}{n!}\left\lvert\mu^{(n)}(z_{j}^{k}(w))-\mu^{(n)}(z_{j}^{k}(y))\right\rvert.

It is easily seen that
\mathrm{RePU}^{(n)}=n!\mathrm{ReLU}^{\prime}
, and so we have

a_{j}^{(k)}\leq\widehat{Q}^{(j,k)}_{\mathrm{ReLU}}(E^{k}_{j}),

hence

|\mu^{(i)}(z^{k}_{j}(x))-\mu^{(i)}(z^{k}_{j}(y))|\leq\widehat{Q}^{(j,k,n,i)}_{\mathrm{RePU}}(E^{k}_{j}).

A.4.
Energy estimate needed for the proof of
Theorem

4.1

Proposition A.1
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(U)),h\in H_{0}^{1}(U)
and
U
be as in (
2.2
),
T,\kappa>0
. Suppose that
e\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(U))
is the weak solution to

\begin{cases}\hfill(\partial_{t}-\kappa\Delta)e=f\quad&\mathrm{in}\,\,U\times(0,T]\\
\hfill e=h\quad&\mathrm{on}\,\,U\times\{t=0\}\\
\hfill e=0\quad&\mathrm{on}\,\,\partial U\times[0,T].\\
\end{cases}

Then for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\lVert e(\cdot,t)\rVert^{2}\leq\exp{[(\varepsilon-2\kappa\pi^{2}/d)t}]\,\left\lparen\lVert h\rVert^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}\lVert f(\cdot,s)\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\right\rparen.

Proof.

Multiplying both sides of the main equation with
e
and integrating,

(A.3)

\int_{U}e(\partial_{t}-\kappa\Delta)e\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{U}f\,e\,\mathrm{d}x.

On the LHS above we have, using
[
31
, Section 5.9]
and the boundary conditions,

\begin{split}\int_{U}e\,\partial_{t}e\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\lVert e(\cdot,t)\rVert_{L^{2}(U)}^{2},\quad-\kappa\int_{U}e\,\Delta e\,\mathrm{d}x=\kappa\int_{U}\left\lVert\nabla e\right\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\geq 0.\end{split}

Thus applying Young’s inequality with
\varepsilon>0
, on the RHS of (
A.3
) as well as Poincaré’s inequality with constant
\sqrt{\text{diam}(U)}/\pi=\sqrt{d}/\pi
on the LHS,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\lVert e(\cdot,t)\rVert^{2}\leq(\varepsilon-2\kappa\pi^{2}/d)\lVert e(\cdot,t)\rVert^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\lVert f(\cdot,t)\rVert^{2}.

The result then follows immediately from Grönwall’s inequality since
e(\cdot,0)\equiv h
.
