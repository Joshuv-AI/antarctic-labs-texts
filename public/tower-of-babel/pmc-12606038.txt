“Hughes‐Stovin Syndrome: A Comprehensive Narrative Review”
Authors: Muhammad Mustafa Imran, Abdul Rehman Shahid Khan, Muhammad Usama Younas, Yashika Gupta, Roma Bai, Ali Gohar, Zohaib Iftikhar, Masab Ali, Muhammad Husnain Ahmad, Maryam Saeed, Fida Hussain, Muhammad Haseeb
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsHughes‐Stovin Syndrome (HSS) is an extremely rare vasculitic disorder characterized by a combination of pulmonary artery aneurysms and deep venous thrombosis (DVT). Often considered a variant or incomplete form of Behçet's disease (BD), it presents diagnostic and therapeutic challenges due to its overlap with other conditions and the lack of standardized criteria. This narrative review aims to describe the epidemiology, pathophysiology, clinical presentation, diagnostic process, treatment strategies, and clinical outcomes associated with HSS.MethodsA narrative review of the literature was conducted using PubMed and Google Scholar. The search included the terms “HSS” OR “pulmonary artery aneurysm” AND “Behçet's disease.” Relevant literature was selected for inclusion based on thematic relevance and clinical importance. Only English‐language articles focusing on human subjects were included, with an emphasis on case reports, series, reviews, and expert opinions. Literature on Behçet's disease was also reviewed for comparative purposes. The reference lists of key articles were manually searched for additional sources. No publication date restrictions were applied. The final search was completed on April 6, 2025.ResultsFewer than 100 cases of HSS have been published in the English‐language literature to date. It primarily affects young adult males and typically presents with hemoptysis, chest pain, and venous thrombosis. Diagnosis is clinical and radiological, with CTPA being the preferred imaging modality. Immunosuppressive therapy remains the cornerstone of management, though there is no consensus on treatment protocols. The use of anticoagulation is controversial due to the risk of aneurysmal rupture.ConclusionsHSS presents significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges due to its rarity and clinical overlap with other vasculitides. Bridging the current gaps in clinical knowledge will require global collaboration to establish consistent diagnostic standards, deepen our understanding of its underlying mechanisms, and develop evidence‐based treatment protocols.

Introduction

Hughes‐Stovin syndrome (HSS), a rare condition, is named after two British doctors, John Patterson Hughes and Peter George Ingle Stovin, who first identified it in 1959. They described the syndrome in four male patients who had a combination of deep venous thrombosis and localized pulmonary artery aneurysms (PAAs) [1]. HSS is characterized by signs and symptoms of thrombophlebitis, along with multiple pulmonary or bronchial aneurysms [2]. This rare syndrome is often confused with Behçet's disease (BD), which presents similarly with a combination of aneurysms and thrombosis, making differentiation between the two challenging. In contrast to HSS, BD also includes typical skin‐mucosal findings such as iritis, hypopyon, and orogenital ulcers, as described by H. Behçet in 1937 [3]. In a patient exhibiting aneurysms and thrombosis, after excluding alternative etiologies, the differential diagnosis typically includes HSS or BD. However, if the patient does not present with the distinctive features of BD, it can be ruled out.

While HSS is an exceptionally rare condition, with fewer than 100 cases documented in English medical literature till date. A comprehensive analysis by the HSS International Study Group in 2021 reviewed 57 (fewer than 100 cases) documented cases, highlighting the syndrome's rarity [4]. This review aims to compile existing research on the syndrome and identify key areas where improvements can be made to enhance outcomes for patients with HSS.

Methodology

Our aim was to narratively describe HSS with respect to its epidemiology, pathophysiology, clinical presentation, diagnostic challenges, management strategies, and clinical outcomes. We conducted a comprehensive literature review using PubMed and Google Scholar with the search terms “HSS” AND “pulmonary artery aneurysm.”

We included only articles written in English and focusing on human subjects of all age groups, with a specific emphasis on case reports, case series, reviews, and expert opinion pieces that discussed HSS in detail. Articles discussing Behçet's disease were also reviewed where relevant to draw comparisons and explore overlaps in clinical features.

Additional relevant articles were identified by manually screening the reference lists of selected papers. No restriction on publication year was applied to ensure comprehensive coverage of the available literature. The final search was concluded on the 6th of April, 2025.

Epidemiology

HSS is an extremely rare condition. Consequently, epidemiological data remain limited. Fewer than 100 cases have been documented in English‐language medical literature so far, so it's hard to get a clear picture of its actual prevalence [5, 6].

Available literature indicates, HSS mostly affects young adult males, usually between the ages of 12 and 48, and there appears to be a noticeable male predominance [7]. There isn't any well‐established racial or regional trend, but interestingly, many reported cases have come from areas where Behçet's disease is also more common, like the Middle East and the Mediterranean region [8, 9].

The overlap with Behçet's disease has led some researchers to think HSS might actually be a rare or incomplete form of it [10]. However, many patients with HSS don't show the full set of symptoms seen in Behçet's. This distinction remains controversial [11].

As the majority of available data derives from individual case reports and small case series, comprehensive epidemiological insights remain limited. There's a critical need for more collective data and research to better define how this disease behaves and who it tends to affect.

Pathophysiology

The exact cause and etiology of HSS are not yet understood. Multiple theories have been proposed to explain the symptoms of this rare condition. It is widely agreed that vasculitis, is the main pathological process responsible for HSS [12]. Consensus is that the potential manifestations of Behçet's Syndrome play a role in this condition. Several scholars suggest that HSS may represent a partial expression of BD, due to the similar findings of vasculitis associated with PAAs [4, 10, 13, 14]. Due to its association with BD Immunogenetics might also play role in the mechanism of disease development as BD has been associated with the human leukocyte antigen B51. Other hypothesis regarding HSS pathophysiology include, infections, such as septic embolisms and abscesses and angiodysplasia of bronchial arteries [14, 15, 16, 17].

Clinical Presentation

HSS is a rare condition that primarily affects the vascular system, leading to complications such as pulmonary artery aneurysms and arterial thrombosis. Pulmonary symptoms are the most common, with patients often presenting with hemoptysis (coughing up blood), dyspnea (shortness of breath), and chest pain due to aneurysms in the pulmonary arteries [18]. Additionally, deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and other thrombotic events are frequently seen, which may affect multiple organs. Neurological symptoms may also develop, typically as a result of embolic events affecting the brain [19]

Kechida et al. (2022) emphasize that systemic inflammatory symptoms, such as fever, fatigue, and weight loss, are also commonly reported in HSS patients, complicating the diagnosis. These nonspecific symptoms overlap with those seen in conditions like Behçet's disease, making differential diagnosis challenging [20].

While oral ulcers and other mucocutaneous symptoms have been observed in some patients, these are much less pronounced than those seen in Behçet's disease [21]. The diverse range of clinical manifestations and the overlap with other conditions make HSS difficult to diagnose (Figure 2), and some researchers debate whether it represents a milder form of Behçet's disease [11]. Diagnosis

Due to the rarity of this syndrome, there is no formal diagnostic criterion. Hughes–Stovin Syndrome (HSS) is diagnosed based on a combination of characteristic clinical features, imaging findings, and exclusion of other causes. The characteristic findings in HSS are recurrent, widespread vascular occlusions with evidence of pulmonary vasculitis and a normal coagulation profile.

Laboratory investigations may show elevated inflammatory markers such as C‐Reactive Protein and Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate. Thrombophilia screens are typically unremarkable and are done to rule out other conditions.

Imaging is central to diagnosis. Computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) is the gold standard and may reveal single or multiple Pulmonary Artery Aneurysms (PAA) with or without in‐situ thrombosis and aneurysmal wall enhancement. A comprehensive CTPA atlas developed by the Hughes–Stovin Syndrome International Study Group (HSSISG) illustrates the broad radiological spectrum [22]. Additional modalities such as fiberoptic bronchoscopy, are used in cases of hemoptysis to detect bronchial artery aneurysm [23]. Chest x‐rays may reveal hilar enlargement [18]. Management

There is currently no standardized treatment protocol for Hughes–Stovin Syndrome (HSS). Therapeutic strategies are largely derived from the management of Behçet's disease and other systemic vasculitides. Immunosuppressive therapy is the cornerstone of HSS treatment. High‐dose corticosteroids are typically initiated, often in combination with cytotoxic agents such as cyclophosphamide. Alternative immunosuppressants, including mycophenolate mofetil, colchicine, cyclosporine, and azathioprine, have also been employed in clinical practice [25]. In cases refractory to conventional therapies, anti‐TNF‐α agents such as infliximab have demonstrated effectiveness [26]. The use of anticoagulants in HSS remains controversial due to the potential risk of aneurysmal rupture and life‐threatening hemorrhage. Anticoagulation may be cautiously considered in patients with significant thrombotic burden and no evidence of unstable aneurysms, following a thorough risk–benefit assessment. Clinical Gaps in Research

The gaps in understanding HSS may be attributed to its rare occurrence. According to the latest data, approximately 90 cases have been reported to date [31].

There are multiple theories regarding the pathogenesis of the disease. Most studies suggest it represents a vasculitic manifestation of Behçet's disease. However, other proposed mechanisms include angiodysplasia of large and medium‐sized arteries, abnormalities in thrombotic pathways, and an autoimmune or infectious etiology.

Currently, there are no standardized diagnostic criteria for HSS. Clinicians primarily rely on clinical findings [30]. Its complex presentation—including thrombosis, hemoptysis, pulmonary artery aneurysms, and superficial thrombophlebitis—overlaps significantly with Behçet's disease, complicating clinical diagnosis [4]. For the assessment of pulmonary aneurysms, multiple diagnostic modalities such as bronchoscopy, helical CT, and CT pulmonary angiography are being utilized. However, reliance on these tools may lead to diagnostic delays [20].

Despite the high fatality rate, an appropriate treatment strategy remains under discussion. One study suggested that early diagnosis and administration of low‐molecular‐weight heparin (LMWH) may prevent pulmonary embolism [32]. Another reported improved outcome with the use of glucocorticoids in combination with single or multiple immunosuppressive agents [30]. Although anticoagulation is typically the first line of therapy for thrombosis, it may be hazardous in HSS due to the risk of fatal hemorrhage from ruptured pulmonary vessels [20]. Interventional and surgical approaches, especially during disease remission, should also be considered to prevent recurrence [33].

Conclusion

HSS presents significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges due to its rarity and clinical overlap with other vasculitides, particularly Behçet's disease. Addressing the existing clinical gaps requires international collaborative efforts to establish standardized diagnostic criteria, clarify pathophysiological mechanisms, and develop evidence‐based treatment guidelines. Future research should focus on multicenter studies to gather larger patient cohorts, thereby enhancing our understanding of this enigmatic syndrome and improving patient outcomes.
