Dentin Autograft as a Pulp Capping Material: A Systematic Review
Authors: Wasifoddin A. Chaudhari, Sameer K. Jadhav, Manu Bansal, Shweta Jadhav, Surajsinh U. Chouhan, Shweta Bhayde
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Vital pulp therapy focuses on maintaining pulp vitality and stimulating reparative dentinogenesis following pulp exposure. While conventional materials such as calcium hydroxide and mineral trioxide aggregate remain established options, there is growing interest in bioactive autograft and xenograft dentin-derived materials. This review evaluated five experimental studies across rat, canine, swine, and human models assessing the regenerative performance of demineralized bone matrix, treated dentin matrix hydrogel, xenogeneic demineralized dentin paste, and autologous demineralized dentin matrix. Key outcomes examined included inflammatory response, pulp tissue organization, dentin bridge formation, odontogenic marker expression, biodegradation characteristics, and the quality of regenerated dentin. All studies demonstrated preservation of pulp vitality and clear evidence of reparative dentin formation. Demineralized bone matrix induced organized dentinogenesis with reduced inflammation compared to calcium hydroxide. Treated dentin matrix hydrogel produced complete, thick dentin bridges with well-aligned odontoblast layers and biodegradation synchronized with new dentin deposition. Xenogeneic dentin paste formed physiologic dentin bridges within 4–8 weeks in canine models, closely mimicking natural dentin structure. Autologous dentin matrix in swine supported high-quality reparative dentin comparable to or better than Bioglass® and calcium hydroxide. Across studies, dentin-derived materials showed favorable biocompatibility, minimal inflammation, and strong inductive potential. These findings highlight their promise as regenerative alternatives to conventional pulp capping agents, though further standardized clinical trials are required to validate long-term effectiveness.

INTRODUCTION

Vital pulp therapy (VPT) aims to preserve and maintain the vitality and function of the dental pulp following exposure due to caries, trauma, or iatrogenic causes. A critical aspect of VPT is the selection of a pulp capping material that can both protect the exposed pulp and stimulate reparative dentinogenesis.[12]

Historically, calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)2) has been the benchmark pulp capping agent due to its antibacterial activity and ability to stimulate dentin bridge formation. However, limitations include tunnel defects in the dentin bridge, poor sealing ability, and long-term solubility. Mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA) has addressed many of these limitations, showing improved sealing and more consistent dentin bridge quality, but is hindered by long setting times, discoloration potential, and high cost.[34]

Recently, there has been growing interest in biologically derived materials, especially dentin autograft and related derivatives such as demineralized dentin matrix (DDM), treated dentin matrix hydrogel (TDMH), and xenogeneic demineralized dentin paste (DDP). These materials are rich in type I collagen, non-collagenous proteins, and bioactive growth factors, such as transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) and bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs), which may enhance odontoblast differentiation and physiologic reparative dentin formation.[12345]

This review synthesizes evidence from five studies evaluating dentin autograft derivatives and compares their biological performance to conventional materials.

METHODS

A comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library from inception to [30/7/2025] using key terms such as dentin autograft, autogenous dentin, pulp capping, vital pulp therapy, dentinogenesis, calcium hydroxide, MTA, and Biodentine. Studies in English that reported histological and/or clinical outcomes of pulp healing and dentin bridge formation with dentin autograft, and those comparing it with conventional materials (Ca(OH)2, MTA, Biodentine), were included, while reviews, case reports, and studies without primary data were excluded. Screening and data extraction were performed independently by two reviewers, with disagreements resolved by consensus. Extracted data covered study design, sample type, material formulation, controls, follow-up, and outcomes. Owing to heterogeneity in methodologies and incomplete datasets, a meta-analysis was not feasible, and findings were synthesized narratively.

RESULTS

The findings from five experimental and clinical studies involving animal (rats, beagle dogs, miniature swine) and human tooth models (n = ~166 teeth), dentin-based materials demonstrated consistent potential for pulp healing and dentin regeneration [Table 1]. It was observed that dentin-derived materials show promising regenerative potential as pulp capping agents. DBM reduced inflammation and promoted regular dentin formation in rat models, while TDMH in human premolars produced thicker bridges and synchronized biodegradation with dentin formation. In dogs, DDP supported tubular dentin formation, and in swine, DDM generated dentin closest to natural tissue, outperforming conventional controls. Overall, DBM, TDMH, DDP, and DDM appear effective biocompatible alternatives to traditional materials in vital pulp therapy.

DISCUSSION

Dentin-derived materials, whether autologous, xenogeneic, or in hydrogel form, consistently demonstrated bioactivity in stimulating odontoblast differentiation and reparative dentinogenesis across diverse models.[12]

These materials provide a natural collagenous scaffold enriched with growth factors such as BMPs and TGF-β, which are known to trigger odontoblast-like cell differentiation and matrix deposition. The presence of dentinal tubules in the regenerated tissue, as seen with DDP and DDM, suggests a closer mimicry of natural dentin than the osteodentin typically formed under Ca(OH)2 or MTA.[13]

Dentin autograft offers several advantages over conventional pulp capping materials, primarily due to its biological composition, which closely resembles native dentin and provides essential bioactive cues for pulp healing and regeneration.[34] Histological findings from experimental studies have demonstrated its potential to induce the formation of organized tubular dentin rather than irregular osteodentin, indicating superior regenerative quality.[25] Moreover, with modified forms, such as treated dentin matrix hydrogel (TDMH), its biodegradation rate can be synchronized with new dentin formation, ensuring a balanced scaffold resorption process that supports tissue integration.[124]

However, despite these promising attributes, most available evidence remains limited to preclinical studies, with only a few human trials conducted to date. Standardization in material preparation, sterilization techniques, and handling protocols is still required to ensure reproducibility and safety. Additionally, there is a need for long-term clinical evaluations to confirm pulp vitality maintenance and assess the functional success of dentin autograft in real-world settings.

CONCLUSION

Evidence from animal and limited human studies supports the potential of dentin autografts and derivatives as effective pulp capping materials. Their biological similarity to dentin, capacity to release odontogenic growth factors, and ability to form structurally and functionally natural dentin make them promising alternatives to conventional agents. Further controlled clinical trials are essential before routine clinical adoption.
