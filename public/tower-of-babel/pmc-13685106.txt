Valorization of sorghum stalk lignocellulose for xylooligosaccharide production: process optimization and chromatographic characterization
Authors: Nisha Nisha, Sumit Arora, Richa Singh, Ashis Kumar Samanta, Nepolean Thirunavukkarasu, Tara C Satyavathi, Sachin Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSorghum stalk (Sorghum bicolor) represents an abundant and underutilized lignocellulosic biomass with significant potential for the generation of value-added bioproducts within biorefinery systems. In the present study, sorghum stalk (SS) biomass was valorized for the production of xylooligosaccharides (XOS) through alkaline extraction of xylan, followed by enzymatic hydrolysis. SS was treated with alkali to extract high-purity xylan, where increasing NaOH concentrations significantly (p < 0.05) enhanced xylan recovery. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy indicated that xylan extracted using 3 M NaOH contained minimal lignin and represented a higher recovery (87.17 ± 0.32%). Enzymatic hydrolysis of extracted xylan, as optimized using Design-Expert software, resulted in maximum XOS production at pH 5.5, 45.45 °C, 24 h incubation, and an enzyme dose of 20 IU per 200 mg xylan. Analytical characterization revealed that the hydrolysates predominantly contained XOS with a degree of polymerization ranging from 2 to 6, primarily composed of xylohexaose. The produced XOS exhibited promising prebiotic potential for Lactobacillus salivarius, as evidenced by a higher prebiotic index (1.74 ± 0.04) than galacto-oligosaccharides (1.12 ± 0.03) and inulin (1.27 ± 0.08), together with the highest prebiotic activity score (13.42 ± 0.27) under the experimental conditions evaluated. The XOS also demonstrated concentration-dependent gradual increase in DPPH scavenging capacity, ranging from 23.02% at 0.125 mg/mL to 75.83% at 4 mg/mL. Furthermore, the XOS showed a higher resistance to simulated gastric conditions (pH 1–5), with less than 6% hydrolysis after 6 h. Overall, the findings highlight SS as a promising lignocellulosic biomass for the sustainable production of XOS and support their potential as high-value bioproducts derived from agricultural residues.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13068-026-02798-8.Keywords: Lignocellulosic biomass, Sorghum stalks, Xylan extraction, Xylooligosaccharides, Biomass valorization, Bioproducts

Introduction

The strain on agricultural production system is continuously increasing due to population growth, leading to bumper production of primary produce, i.e. grains and cereals along with enormous quantities of agricultural wastes. Agro-based waste not only results in economic losses for a nation, but also contributes towards environmental pollution, release of greenhouse gases, breeding ground for insects and pests, social consequences, and health-related issues [47]. Utilization of underutilized agricultural waste is a key approach for achieving the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) of the United Nations by 2030. In line with modern biorefinery concepts, agricultural residues are increasingly viewed as renewable feedstocks for producing multiple value-added products, including biofuels, platform chemicals, biomaterials, and functional biomolecules. Such integrated biomass valorization strategies improve resource utilization efficiency, enhance economic feasibility, and support the transition towards a circular bioeconomy [38]. The economic and environmental benefits of biomass valorization can be further enhanced through integrated biorefinery strategies that simultaneously convert cellulose-, hemicellulose-, and lignin-rich fractions into multiple value-added products. Recent work has demonstrated the production of butanol-rich solvents, furfural, and lignin-derived compounds from lignocellulosic biomass within a single biorefinery framework, highlighting the importance of maximizing the utilization of all biomass components [39].

Efficient fractionation of lignocellulosic biomass is a critical step in biomass valorization, as it enables the selective recovery of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin for subsequent conversion into value-added products. Various biomass fractionation approaches, including acid, alkaline, organosolv, ionic liquid, and deep eutectic solvent pretreatments, have been explored for lignocellulosic biomass valorization, each offering distinct advantages and limitations in terms of component recovery, inhibitor formation, process efficiency, and economic feasibility [54]. Recent studies have highlighted hydrothermal, autohydrolysis, and other green pretreatment technologies as environmentally friendly alternatives for XOS production due to their reduced chemical requirements and lower wastewater generation [29, 83]. However, when the objective is to recover a xylan-rich fraction for subsequent enzymatic conversion, alkali-assisted extraction remains an effective approach due to the stability and the ease of recovering xylan from the alkali-dissolved fraction [30, 36, 62]. Additionally, this technique eliminates uronic acid substitutions and acetyl groups from hemicelluloses, increasing the accessibility of carbohydrates for enzymatic hydrolysis [85] and lowering the requirement for auxiliary enzymes [19]. Moreover, the alkaline extraction requires milder operational conditions than other pretreatment techniques [7].

The functional food market has grown significantly in recent years, particularly for products containing prebiotics. According to the available market intelligence report, the global prebiotic market is valued at USD 5.797 billion in 2025 and is projected to grow at a CAGR of approximately 12.61% to reach USD 10.498 billion by 2030. This growth is largely attributed to increased consumer awareness of gut health, as consumers seek foods that not only meet the nutritional needs but also contribute to overall well-being.

The most prevalent agro-based waste, i.e. lignocellulosic biomass, holds a significant potential to yield functional ingredients for the food sector [13, 16]. Of these, xylooligosaccharides (XOS) are particularly noteworthy [58]. Evidently, xylan acts as a precursor to XOS which consist of 2–10 xylose monomers linked by β-1,4 xylosidic linkage [59]. These functional oligomers are attracting increasing attention for their potential health benefits, including prebiotic action, antidiabetic, anticarcinogenic, antioxidant, and lipid-lowering effects [69, 84]. The growing market for XOS makes its production from lignocellulosic biomass an interesting option since the hemicellulose portion of biomass is currently underused. The XOS could be produced chemically, hydrothermally (auto hydrolysis), or enzymatically [49]. Currently, the enzymatic hydrolysis is the preferred method for producing the XOS in the food industry because it is environmentally friendly and avoids harmful by-products [1]. Xylanase is the key enzyme for catalysing the breakdown of the xylan, producing XOS with lower DP (2–6) and without any undesired side products [71].

Sorghum (Sorghum bicolor) is the fifth most cultivated cereal crop in the world, with an estimated global production of 58.7 million tonnes in 2020 [8]. In India, sorghum production reached 4.15 million tonnes in 2022 [20]. The leftover residue after the crop harvest released approximately 1.31 kilo-tonnes of nitrous oxide emissions [20], a potent greenhouse gas that exacerbates serious environmental concerns and waste management issues. Therefore, the valorization of sorghum stalks into high-value functional ingredients such as xylooligosaccharides (XOS) represents a sustainable strategy for agricultural waste management and circular bioeconomy development. Despite their relatively high xylan content and widespread availability, sorghum stalks remain largely unexplored as a feedstock for XOS production [8].

Previous studies have demonstrated the production of XOS from various lignocellulosic biomasses using alkaline extraction followed by enzymatic hydrolysis. However, limited information is available regarding the integrated valorization of sorghum stalks, particularly with respect to optimizing xylan recovery, producing XOS with a broader degree of polymerization (DP2–6), and comprehensively evaluating their functional properties. Based on this knowledge gap, we hypothesized that recovering a high-purity xylan fraction from sorghum stalks through alkali-assisted extraction, followed by optimization of enzymatic hydrolysis, would enhance both the yield and DP distribution of XOS while preserving desirable biological properties. Therefore, the present study aimed to (i) optimize alkaline extraction of xylan from sorghum stalks; (ii) optimize enzymatic hydrolysis for enhanced production of XOS; (iii) characterize the resulting XOS with respect to composition and DP distribution; and (iv) evaluate their prebiotic potential, antioxidant activity, and resistance to simulated gastric conditions. The novelty of this work lies in combining feedstock valorization, process optimization, detailed product characterization, and functional validation in a single integrated study, thereby demonstrating the feasibility of sorghum stalks as a sustainable feedstock for the production of high-value prebiotic XOS within a lignocellulosic biorefinery framework.

Materials and methods

Raw materials and chemicals

Sorghum stalks (Sorghum bicolor, Swati variety) were procured from the Central Sheep and Wool Research Institute (CSWRI), Rajasthan, India, and milled to a particle size of < 1 mm. The XOS standards were procured from M/S Megazyme International, Bray, Ireland. These included xylobiose (DP2; Catalogue no. O-XBI), xylotriose (DP3; Catalogue no. O-XTR), xylotetraose (DP4; Catalogue no. O-XTE), xylopentaose (DP5; Catalogue no. O-XPE), and xylohexaose (DP6; Catalogue no. O-XHE). Standard xylan from oat spelt (Catalogue no. X0627), xylanase recombinantly expressed in Aspergillus oryzae (2.5 IU/mg; Catalogue no. X2753-10G) and standard xylose (Catalogue no. X1500) were procured from Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA. Autospan® Glucose, glucose oxidase–peroxidase (GOD-POD), Endpoint assay kit was procured from ARKRAY Healthcare Private Limited, Surat, Gujarat, India. Silica gel 60 F254 TLC plates were purchased from Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany, for use in TLC analysis. Inulin was procured from MP Biomedicals, Solon, OH, USA. Galacto-oligosaccharide (GOS) powder (Vivinal GOS Powder) was obtained from Friesland Campina DOMO, Zwolle, The Netherlands. Ligilactobacillus salivarius RBL 22, a probiotic strain, and Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 were obtained from the Rumen Biotechnology Lab, Animal Nutrition Division, and the Molecular Biology Unit, Dairy Microbiology Division of ICAR-National Dairy Research Institute, Karnal, respectively. All other reagents used were of analytical grade.

Compositional analysis of sorghum stalks (SS) and extraction of xylan

The powdered SS were analysed for cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin, crude protein, organic matter, and total ash content. Cell wall constituents were analysed using the method proposed by Van Soest et al. [73]. The crude protein content was estimated using a manual Kjeldahl apparatus [5]. Organic matter and total ash content in the stalks were also determined [4].

Extraction of xylan from SS was carried out at room temperature (~ 25 °C) using sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solutions of concentration ranging from 1 to 4 M, following the protocol of Samanta et al. [57]. The alkaline extraction of xylan involved three intermediate steps (Supplementary Fig. 1): (i) treatment of SS with NaOH, (ii) acidification of the alkali-solubilized fraction (ASF) to pH 6–6.5 using glacial acetic acid, a weak organic acid, non-toxic, and a greener alternative to strong mineral acids [42, 81], and (iii) precipitation of ASF with ice-cold ethanol (95%, v/v), an effective antisolvent for separating and purifying hemicellulose polysaccharides [21, 63], and environmentally friendly compared to other fractionation agents [21, 44]. Optimization of the process for xylan extraction and FTIR analysis

The extraction process was optimized based on both recovery of xylan from SS and FTIR-based assessment of xylan purity since extraction yield alone may be influenced by co-extraction of lignin. Therefore, xylan samples exhibiting higher recoveries were subjected to FTIR analysis (Nicolet Summit LITE FTIR Spectrometer, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) to determine the ideal alkali concentration (3, 3.5, and 4 M NaOH) that would yield a higher recovery of xylan with the least amount of lignin contamination. The analysis was conducted in transmission mode, at a resolution of 0.9 cm−1 and in the spectral range of 400–4000 cm−1. Pellets were prepared by grinding 5 mg of the sample with 95 mg of KBr using a pestle and mortar. The standard xylan was analysed alongside samples for comparison and interpretation of results.

Characterization of xylan extracted using optimized alkali concentration

Xylan extracted using the optimized alkali concentration was evaluated for total carbohydrates [18] and reducing sugars [64]. The glucose content was measured using a glucose monitoring kit. Additionally, the structural properties of the xylan were studied using FTIR spectroscopy.

Optimization of enzymatic hydrolysis applying response surface methodology (RSM)

The enzymatic hydrolysis process was optimized using Central Composite Design (CCD) under RSM. The effects of four independent variables, pH, temperature, enzyme dose, and incubation time, were evaluated to maximize the yield of XOS. The design consisted of 30 experimental runs (Supplementary Table 1), generated using Design-Expert software (version 13, Stat-Ease Inc., USA), which included 16 factorial points, 8 axial points, and 6 replicated centre points.

Production, qualitative and quantitative analysis of XOS

The xylan was hydrolysed into XOS using xylanase [57]. Briefly, 10 mL solution of 2% (w/v) xylan in 0.1 M citrate phosphate buffer was incubated in a shaker incubator (OLSC-109-1, Ocean Life Science Corporation, Firozabad, Uttar Pradesh, India) for hydrolysis. The sample was collected post-hydrolysis, filtered through a 0.22 μm cellulose nitrate membrane filter, and subjected to further analysis. The extent of xylan hydrolysis was estimated based on quantification of reducing sugars [64] released during the hydrolysis.

Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) analysis of XOS

The presence of oligosaccharides and their DP was detected using TLC according to Gomez de Segura and Fevre [23] with a minor modification. The mobile phase consisted of ethyl acetate, acetic acid, and water in a 2:2:1 (v/v/v) ratio rather than a 2:1:1 (v/v/v) ratio. The developed plates were sprayed with α-naphthol reagent (0.5% w/v in a 95:5 methanol and sulfuric acid mixture) as described by Khaire et al. [33], instead of aniline diphenylamine reagent, and heated at 100 °C for 5 min to visualize the spots.

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis of XOS

The quantification of XOS and xylose was carried out using HPLC (Waters HPLC 515, Milford, Massachusetts, USA), equipped with a refractive index (RI) detector (Waters 2414 RI detector) and a Waters Spherisorb Amino column, with 63% acetonitrile in HPLC water as the mobile phase [57]. Analysis was completed in 30 min at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min and a temperature of 30 °C for both column and detector. The quantification was done by injecting 20 μL of filtered samples into the system and by using calibration curves for xylose and XOS (DP2-6). The total XOS content was calculated as the sum of DP2-6 and reported as mg XOS per mL of xylan solution. Prebiotic utilization assay of XOS

The XOS-rich hydrolysate produced from enzymatic hydrolysis of xylan was concentrated using a rotary evaporator (RE100-PRO, Spark systems, Hyderabad, India) at 55–60 ºC to one-third volume, then freeze-dried (Labconco 7753030 Freezone 6 L Console Freeze Dryer, Labconco Corporation, Kansas City, Missouri, USA) to obtain XOS powder. The selected probiotic and pathogenic bacteria were cultured in MRS broth at 37 ºC anaerobically for 18–24 h. A carbohydrate-free basal media (CFBM) was prepared with the following composition (per litre): proteose peptone, 1.00 g; beef extract, 1.00 g; yeast extract, 0.50 g; polysorbate 80, 0.10 g; triammonium citrate, 0.20 g; sodium acetate, 0.50 g; magnesium sulphate, 0.01 g; manganese sulphate, 0.005 g; and dipotassium hydrogen phosphate, 0.20 g. The final pH of the medium was adjusted to 6.5 ± 0.2 using 0.1 N hydrochloric acid (HCl). The prepared CFBM was then supplemented with 1% of XOS, glucose, GOS, and inulin for test, control, and reference media, respectively. Each medium was inoculated with 1% (v/v) of bacterial culture under aseptic conditions, followed by incubation of all the inoculated tubes anaerobically at 37 ºC for 24 h. The samples were subsequently poured onto MRS agar plates and incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 24 h. The prebiotic potential of XOS was assessed by measuring bacterial growth as log colony-forming units (CFU) per mL at 0 and 24 h [55].

DPPH radical-scavenging ability of XOS mixture

Then 2 mL of a 0.2 mmol/L DPPH solution in 95% ethanol was mixed with 1 mL of the XOS mixture, followed by the addition of 2 mL of 95% ethanol. The mixture was vortexed vigorously and then incubated in the dark at 25 °C for 30 min. Its absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-2700, 230V) against a blank, and the data were presented as a graph of percentage radical inhibition versus XOS concentration. Ascorbic acid was used as a positive control. The experiment was conducted in triplicate, and the mean results were recorded.

Resistance of XOS mixture to gastric juice

Simulated gastric juice was prepared as an HCl buffer [77] by dissolving 8 g NaCl, 0.2 g KCl, 8.25 g Na2HPO4·2H2O, 14.35 g NaHPO4, 0.1 g CaCl2·2H2O, 0.18 g MgCl2·6H2O per L. The pH of the juice was varied from 1 to 5 using 5 N HCl. The freeze-dried XOS mixture was dissolved in reverse osmosis (RO) water to prepare a 1% (w/v) solution. Equal volumes (5 mL) of the simulated gastric buffer at each pH and the sample solution were mixed and incubated at 37 °C for 6 h. Statistical analysis

All results were expressed as the mean of three independent replicates ± standard error (SE). One-way ANOVA was performed using SPSS 25.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA), and the level of significance (p < 0.05) among the treatments was determined using Tukey’s honestly significant difference test. The optimization data for XOS production were analysed separately using RSM in Design-Expert software (version 13, Stat-Ease Inc., USA).

Results and discussion

Chemical composition of SS

The stalks contained 95.05 ± 0.15% organic matter, 3.16 ± 0.29% crude protein, and 4.95 ± 0.15% total ash content. Notably, the concentrations of cell wall structural constituents were significantly higher with cellulose (37.77 ± 0.88%) being the most prominent, followed by hemicellulose (26.96 ± 0.20%) and lignin (9.81 ± 0.71%). These findings aligned with the observations of Sathesh-Prabu and Murugesan [60], who evaluated the cellular components of sorghum stalks. The hemicellulose content was higher than that reported by Sun et al. [68] and Wan et al. [75], whereas the lignin content was found to be lower. The stalks were found to be an ideal candidate for xylan extraction since xylan is the principal hemicellulose in plants [59]. Furthermore, the stalks exhibited a higher xylan-to-lignin ratio, indicating their suitability for the production of XOS, as LCBs with greater xylan content and lower lignin levels are preferred [3, 82]. Additionally, alkaline delignification proved more effective when the raw material contained lower lignin content [35].

Optimization of the xylan extraction process

Xylan extraction was optimized based on two criteria: maximizing the recovery of xylan from SS and confirming the purity of the extracted xylan using FTIR spectroscopy.

Recovery of xylan extracted using different concentrations of alkali

The recovery of xylan was 36.67 ± 0.45, 52.33 ± 0.4, 67.49 ± 0.43, 78.99 ± 0.33, 87.17 ± 0.32, 98.48 ± 0.84, and 110.56 ± 1.09% for NaOH concentrations of 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, and 4 M, respectively. Samanta et al. [56, 57] also reported an increase in the recovery of xylan with increasing concentration of NaOH and KOH. As a strong catalyst, NaOH effectively cleaves the ether and ester bonds between lignin and hemicellulose within the lignocellulosic matrix by forming a sodium phenolate salt, thus facilitating greater lignin removal [35, 52]. This soluble salt enters the alkali-solubilized fraction (ASF) along with hemicellulose, thereby separating cellulose [52]. Acidification of ASF (pH 6–6.5) precipitated some of the xylan fractions and acid-insoluble lignin (AIL). Further, ethanol precipitation of the acidified ASF resulted in the co-precipitation of xylan (both soluble and precipitated fractions) along with AIL. The acid-soluble lignin (ASL) remained in the supernatant since ethanol precipitates xylan, but does not precipitate lignin [63], enabling effective separation of ASL (Supplementary Fig. 1). However, at higher alkali concentration, the xylan was also susceptible to contamination with lignin, which reduced the true recovery of xylan. In the case of xylan extraction with 4 M NaOH, recovery greater than 100% indicated potential contamination of the extracted xylan with lignin. As the concentration of NaOH increased, more lignin might have been solubilized into ASF. Upon acidification, this solubilized lignin precipitated, which consequently increased the lignin content in the final ethanol-precipitated mass. Sobri et al. [63] extracted lignin and xylan subfractions from alkaline black liquor obtained from oil palm fronds, using 6 M HCl and 95% ethanol, respectively. Lower lignin content is beneficial for XOS production as the presence of lignin hinders xylanase activity due to non-specific binding of the enzyme to the lignin surface [65]. Beyond serving as a substrate for XOS production, efficient recovery of xylan from lignocellulosic biomass is also important from an integrated biorefinery perspective, where the remaining cellulose- and lignin-rich fractions can be further valorized into additional value-added products. Recent studies have demonstrated the co-production of XOS, fermentable sugars, and lignin-derived products from lignocellulosic feedstocks, highlighting the potential of comprehensive biomass utilization strategies [37].

FTIR analysis of xylan extracted using 3, 3.5, and 4 M NaOH

The recovery values exceeding 100% at higher alkali concentrations suggested co-extraction of lignin with xylan. Therefore, extraction yield alone might not accurately reflect xylan purity. To further assess the purity of the extracted xylan fractions, FTIR spectra of xylan extracted using 3, 3.5, and 4 M NaOH were compared with that of standard xylan (Fig. 1). The results indicated similarity in overall structure; however, they differed in intensity response, especially in the 1300–1600 cm−1 region. The presence of bands around 1300–1350 cm−1 and 1500–1600 cm−1 corresponds with the presence of lignin in xylan [12]. Standard xylan displayed very weak lignin-associated bands, indicating its high purity and minimal lignin content. FTIR analysis revealed more intense lignin-associated vibrational bands in xylan extracted using 3.5 and 4 M NaOH than in xylan extracted using 3 M NaOH, suggesting increased co-extraction of lignin at higher alkali concentrations. Xylan extracted using 4 M NaOH exhibited the most intense lignin peaks within the highlighted region (Fig. 1). The progressive increase in the intensity of lignin-associated bands with increasing alkali concentration further supported the likelihood of lignin contamination contributing to the apparent increase in xylan recovery. Similar observations were reported by Buslov et al. [12], who observed stronger aromatic skeletal vibrations in beechwood xylan due to higher lignin residues compared with xylan from oats and birchwood. Likewise, Sun et al. [66] reported that increasing peak intensity within lignin-specific regions of hemicellulosic fractions extracted from corn cobs corresponded to increasing lignin content. Although direct quantification of residual lignin would provide additional confirmation of purity, FTIR analysis offered useful qualitative evidence regarding the relative extent of lignin contamination among the extracted samples.

Selection of the optimum level of alkali for xylan extraction and its characterization

Xylan extracted with 3 M NaOH was selected for further analysis, considering its high recovery and minimal lignin presence.

Non-reducing carbohydrate or xylan content in the extract

Xylan extracted using 3 M NaOH contained 98.03% (± 1.05) total carbohydrates, 0.71% (± 0.02) reducing sugars, and 0.55% glucose on a dry weight basis. The extract consisted of 97.23% (± 1.03) non-reducing carbohydrate extract, identified as xylan, calculated by subtracting the reducing sugar content from the total carbohydrate content. The high non-reducing carbohydrate content (97.23%) further supported the effectiveness of the selected extraction conditions in obtaining a xylan-rich fraction with minimal non-carbohydrate impurities. This high carbohydrate purity, together with the low reducing sugar content (0.71%), indicated negligible contamination by low-molecular-weight sugars and confirmed the suitability of the extracted xylan for subsequent enzymatic hydrolysis and XOS production. A similar approach was used by Chikkerur et al. [14] to determine the actual inulin content in chicory roots by subtracting reducing sugars from total carbohydrates. Their room-temperature extraction yielded approximately 80% pure inulin, which they found to be highly suitable for subsequent bioconversion into short-chain fructo-oligosaccharides.

Reducing sugars and glucose content of the extracted xylan

Xylan possessed a very negligible quantity of reducing sugars (0.71%), which might have originated from soluble xylan or resulted from minor hydrolysis of the xylan backbone during the extraction process [80]. Glucose detected in the extracted xylan likely originated from the hydrolysis of cellulose or the xylan backbone, as both contain glucose residues to varying extents [2, 50, 72]. The presence of a small amount of glucose in the extract further indicated that the method employed for xylan extraction was able to separate cellulose and selectively extract xylan without its extensive hydrolysis [34].

FTIR analysis of xylan

FTIR analysis of the xylan extracted using 3 M NaOH was conducted to evaluate its structural and conformational properties. The spectral profile of xylan displayed features typical of xylan structure (Supplementary Fig. 2); consistent with earlier findings of Jamaldheen et al. [27] and Batog et al. [9]. The broad absorption band at 3464.49 cm−1 and sharp bands at 2930.79 and 2864.74 cm−1 represented stretching of hydrogen-bonded hydroxyl groups and methylene groups, respectively, present in the main chain of sorghum xylan. Xylan contained adsorbed water as indicated by a band at 1656.55 cm−1. Weak signal bands at 1570.74, 1423.32, and 1324 cm−1 corresponded to the aromatic skeletal vibrations of lignin, indicating the extract contained a small amount of bound lignin in its structure [12]. The band at 1047 cm−1 is characteristic of xylan with β-(1–4) backbone, and additional bands at 655.2 and 521.17 cm−1 might be present due to C–C–H or C–O–C stretching or bending in xylan structure [31]. A weak signal band at 815.29 cm−1 may be due to the presence of alpha anomers in side chains, characteristic of typical xylan structures [57, 67]. The extraction methodology optimized was able to completely remove ester and acetyl bonds formed between xylan and lignin, as indicated by the absence of absorption bands in the 1730 cm−1 range [45]. According to Kacurakova et al. [31], the absence of a signal band at 1520 cm−1 indicated the absence of pectin from the xylan structure.

Enzymatic hydrolysis of xylan

The yield and DP of the enzymatic hydrolysates depend on various hydrolysis conditions, including incubation time, pH, temperature, and enzyme dose [41, 48]. Hence, these four key process variables were selected for XOS production [32, 57]. The reducing sugar and XOS contents for each hydrolysis condition are shown in Table 1. The highest release of reducing sugars was observed at pH 5.5, 50 °C, 24 h incubation time, and 20 IU enzyme dose per 200 mg of xylan (Run 6). The results indicated that lower pH, longer incubation time, higher temperature, and greater enzyme dose favour xylan breakdown. However, since reducing sugars also include xylose, a monomer, this serves as an indirect measure of XOS content. Additionally, reducing sugar levels ranged from 4.61 to 18.155 mg/mL, which was higher than the XOS content (4.51 ± 0.11 to 17.79 ± 0.14 mg/mL) across all runs, confirming the presence of xylose alongside oligosaccharides in the hydrolysate. Samanta et al. [57] also noted that the reducing sugars after xylan hydrolysis consisted of xylose and XOS (DP2-6). Therefore, quantifying XOS using HPLC is an appropriate approach for analysis with RSM as compared to measurement of reducing sugars concentration alone.

Qualitative and quantitative analysis of XOS

TLC analysis revealed the presence of XOS fractions ranging from DP2-6 in the hydrolysates (Fig. 2). Additional bands migrating below the DP6 band were also observed, suggesting the possible presence of higher-degree oligomers (DP7–8). However, because standards were available only up to DP6, the identity of these higher-molecular-weight bands could not be conclusively established by TLC. Moreover, Fig. 2 displays a very faint xylose spot and comparatively darker XOS spots, indicating limited xylose formation and successful conversion of xylan into XOS. Ravichandra et al. [50] worked with xylanase-hydrolysed sorghum xylan and identified XOS with DP2-7 along with negligible quantities of xylose on TLC plates. Dhaver et al. [17] also hydrolysed xylan in chicken feed with recombinant xylanase, and the TLC analysis indicated the production of XOS with DP2–6 in the enzymatic hydrolysates. HPLC analysis showed the presence of xylose and XOS components (DP2-6) in the hydrolysed samples, as XOS with DP2-6 are the preferred products of xylanase action [22] and are reported to exhibit excellent prebiotic effects [25, 53]. Minor chromatographic responses were present after DP6 peak suggesting the presence of oligomers with DP greater than 6. Nevertheless, due to the lack of calibration standards beyond DP6, these components could not be reliably identified or quantified. Therefore, subsequent quantitative analysis and discussion were restricted to XOS fractions from DP2-6. The HPLC profiling further revealed xylohexaose (DP6) as the predominant fraction in all runs, followed by xylobiose (DP2) (Supplementary Fig. 3). The short-chain oligosaccharides are known to be utilized faster by probiotic bacteria, and higher doses of the prebiotic could lead to abdominal bloating and diarrhoea [84]. However, higher DP XOS contributes to sustained fermentation in the colon, and thereby favours longer duration gut health caring response [25].

TLC analysis of XOS samples where DP2, DP3, DP4, DP5, and DP6 are xylobiose, xylotriose, xylotetraose, xylopentaose, and xylohexaose, respectively, and 1, 2, 3, and 4 are XOS samples generated under different experimental conditions

Xylose content was notably lower across all hydrolysis combinations (Fig. 3), as also evident from TLC analysis, suggesting that hydrolysis favoured the XOS production, while minimizing the release of xylose. This highlighted the preferential endo-cleaving action and limited exo-xylanolytic activity of the experimental xylanase, which would otherwise result in higher xylose production due to excessive depolymerization of higher DP XOS [6, 58]. Vieira et al. [74] also reported minimal production of xylose with XOS (DP2-6) in peach palm waste due to limited exo-xylanolytic activity of xylanase. The excessive release of xylose is undesirable as it can reduce the prebiotic selectivity of the oligosaccharide mixture [70].

Effect of process variables on XOS yield

Effect of pH

The individual effects of selected process variables on XOS production were evaluated using both linear and quadratic terms in the regression equation. Higher XOS yields were obtained at lower pH values compared to elevated pH, as evident particularly in runs 3 and 22, respectively. The XOS yield was observed to be higher at lower pH due to the ionization or deionization of functional groups within the enzyme's active site under these conditions [22], aligning with the optimal pH for xylanase activity (i.e. pH 5–6). Yang et al. [79] also obtained a maximal XOS content at pH 5.4, when pH values ranging from 3.6 to 6 were explored for XOS production. Samanta et al. [56] observed that higher DP2 and DP3 content were accumulated at lower pH values, especially at pH 5.

Effect of temperature

An increase in temperature resulted in a favourable but less pronounced increase in the XOS production, which may be attributed to the lower thermal stability of the enzyme at higher temperatures [50, 79]. Furthermore, xylanase action led to a greater release of xylose at higher temperatures, thereby reducing the XOS yield as evident from Run 16. Samanta et al. [56] also observed a reduction in XOS content and an increase in xylose content with the increase in temperature from 30 to 50 °C. Polizeli et al. [46] reported that a greater amount of xylose accumulated at higher temperatures, which reduced the XOS yield owing to the inhibitory effect of xylose on xylanase.

Effect of incubation time

Incubation time exhibited the most substantial positive linear effect on XOS yield, indicating that the XOS yield increased with longer incubation durations. However, the XOS content decreased with extended incubation time (i.e. 32 h) as evident from Run 4, with a corresponding increase in xylose content. This might be attributed to the extensive breakdown of the XOS by the enzyme after 24 h, leading to the release of higher amounts of xylose (0.685 mg/mL; Run 4) and a decrease in XOS content (15.117 mg/mL; Run 4). Samanta et al. [57] and Wang et al. [76] also recorded an increase in xylose content and a decline in XOS content with longer incubation time. The decrease in XOS content under an extended incubation period might be due to the presence of higher amounts of xylose, which acts as an inhibitor of xylanase action [24, 46].

Effect of enzyme dose

It is evident from the results obtained that exceeding the enzyme dose beyond 20 IU (i.e. 25 IU; Run 28) resulted in a non-significant increase in XOS release. Yang et al. [79], Wang et al. [76], and Ravichandra et al. [50] also observed that the XOS content increased sharply with the enzyme dose, but plateaued afterwards. Ravichandra et al. [50] and Wang et al. [76] noted no significant improvement beyond 50 IU/mL and 120 IU/g, respectively. Yang et al. [79] also found a non-significant increase as enzyme dose increased from 25 to 35 IU/g. This may be due to end-product inhibition of the enzyme, leading to a decline in XOS production, especially at higher enzyme doses [10]. Moreover, Boraston et al. [11] highlighted that enzyme's binding capacity becomes saturated once a specific enzyme-to-xylan ratio is reached.

Response surface analysis of process parameters affecting XOS yield

3D surface response plots were generated to evaluate the effect of key process variables on the XOS yield and to identify the optimal conditions for maximizing XOS yield. Each plot (Fig. 4a–f) depicts the interaction between two variables, while holding the other two variables at their central levels.

3D surface response plots showing the interactive effect of process parameters on XOS yield (%): a pH (A) vs temperature (B) (C and D held constant); b pH (A) vs incubation time (C) (B and D held constant); c pH (A) vs enzyme dose (D) (B and C held constant); d temperature (B) vs incubation time (C) (A and D held constant); e temperature (B) vs enzyme dose (D) (A and C held constant); f incubation time (C) vs enzyme dose (D) (A and B held constant)

The surface plot illustrating the combined effect of pH (A) and temperature (B) on the XOS yield (Fig. 4a) appeared to be mildly curved and relatively flat, suggesting that pH and temperature do not strongly influence the XOS yield. The XOS yield was detected to increase moderately with rising temperatures (~ 45–47 °C), while the effect of pH was less pronounced, showing a slight increase in yield at lower pH values (~ 5.5–5.6). The plot showing pH (A) and incubation time (C) (Fig. 4b) revealed that extended incubation enhanced the XOS yield, particularly under mildly acidic conditions. The surface was almost flat along the pH axis with a slight decline in yield at higher pH values (~ 6.3–6.5), suggesting that the lower pH (~ 5.5–5.8) was marginally more favourable for the XOS yield. The interaction between pH (A) and enzyme dose (D) (Fig. 4c) indicated that the enzyme dose was a key driver in the XOS production process. A very mild curvature was observed for pH, with the yield decreasing slightly as pH increased from 5.5 to 6.5. This suggested that a higher enzyme dose (20 IU) at acidic pH (5.5) increased the XOS production. Higher yields were achieved at longer incubation durations (24 h) and moderate temperatures (~ 45 °C) (Fig. 4d). This suggested that an adequate time is required for the enzyme to act efficiently, especially under thermally favourable conditions. A consistent increase in XOS yield was observed with increasing enzyme dose (Fig. 4e), as more enzyme molecules led to enhanced xylan hydrolysis. The plot revealed a moderate positive effect of temperature on the XOS yield, particularly at mid-range values. However, beyond that, the yield remains nearly constant (plateau), suggesting that higher temperatures might not proportionally enhance enzyme efficiency further and might lead to a negative relation of temperature and enzyme dose with the XOS production. Kathiresan et al. [32] also reported that temperature and enzyme dose together were the driving factors affecting the XOS production. Moreover, Mazlan et al. [40] highlighted that using a higher enzyme dosage and maintaining a lower temperature increased XOS yield, supporting the present findings. Lastly, the interaction between incubation time and enzyme dose (Fig. 4f) exhibited the most prominent effect on yield. The highest yield was observed at the upper end of both incubation time and enzyme dose, emphasizing the need for a longer incubation duration (20 h) and a higher enzyme dose (20 IU) for effective xylan hydrolysis. The plots highlighted that lower pH, moderate temperature, prolonged incubation, and higher enzyme dose increased XOS yield, thereby helping in the selection of optimal conditions for maximizing the response.

Optimized conditions and yield of XOS

Based on the regression model and response surface analysis, the predicted optimal conditions for achieving maximum XOS yield were: pH 5.5, temperature 45.45 °C, incubation time 24 h, and enzyme dose 20 IU per 200 mg xylan, with a desirability score of 0.978 (close to 1). The optimal conditions for maximal production of XOS from sugarcane bagasse were pH 4.75, temperature 45 °C, enzyme concentration 4 U/mL, and incubation time 16 h [28] . Samanta et al. [57] reported maximum XOS production from pigeon pea stalks at pH 4.91, temperature 48.11 °C, enzyme dose 11.01 U/mL, and 15.65 h incubation time.

The yield of XOS obtained under the optimized conditions was found to be 85.26% (w/w) in the present investigation using xylanase from Aspergillus oryzae. Vieira et al. [74] produced XOS from peach palm heart wastes, including inner sheath and peel, using xylanase from the same source after xylan was extracted from both using alkali. The yield of XOS was found to be 50.1% and 48.8% from inner sheath and peel xylan, respectively, at pH 5.3, enzyme loading 30 IU/g substrate, temperature 40 °C, and incubation time of 48 h. They also hydrolysed standard xylan from beechwood, oat spelt, and birchwood, and the XOS yields were found to be 80.0%, 77.5%, and 76.5%, respectively. The yield of XOS in the current work was higher than the 50.3% reported previously for SS using acidic hydrolysis [15], highlighting that enzymatic approach is more efficient for increasing the yield of SS-derived XOS. Ruzene et al. [51] reported that XOS yield is influenced by the structural properties of the xylan source and the enzymatic conditions. Furthermore, the substrate's higher water solubility enhanced accessibility to enzymatic action, thereby increasing XOS yield [78]. Hence, the yield of the XOS obtained from SS could be influenced by the type and structure of raw material used and the hydrolysis conditions employed.

Prebiotic utilization assay of XOS

PI assesses the growth of probiotic bacteria on the test substrate (XOS) compared to a reference carbohydrate (glucose). The PI value for Lactobacillus salivarius was found to be 1.74 ± 0.04 for XOS, 1.12 ± 0.03 for GOS, and 1.27 ± 0.08 for inulin, expressed as the mean ± SE from three replicates. A PI value greater than 1 indicates that the prebiotic oligosaccharide has a stronger growth-stimulating effect on probiotic bacteria than the control carbohydrate (glucose) [43]. The XOS showed the highest PI, suggesting greater prebiotic potential and better support for the growth of selected probiotic bacteria as compared to GOS and inulin. The results obtained were in consonance with the findings of Kathiresan et al. [32], who reported PI values ranging from 0.21 to 1.93 for XOS derived from sugarcane bagasse, evaluated using five different probiotic bacterial strains: Lactobacillus plantarum, Lactobacillus fermentum, Bacillus amyloliquefaciens, Bacillus clausii, and Enterococcus faecium.

PAS provides a quantitative measure of the differential growth rates of probiotic and pathogenic strains on a given substrate. PAS was calculated to evaluate the ability of test prebiotics XOS, GOS, and inulin to enhance the growth of a probiotic strain over a pathogenic strain. Among the three tested prebiotics, XOS demonstrated the highest PAS value (13.42 ± 0.27), indicating a superior ability to selectively promote the growth of Lactobacillus salivarius while simultaneously suppressing the growth of Escherichia coli. Inulin showed a moderate effect, with a PAS value of 1.32 ± 0.04, while GOS was less effective in supporting the probiotic relative to pathogenic bacteria, with a PAS value of 0.71 ± 0.06. A PAS value below 1 suggests that the substrate might also support the growth of non-beneficial strains, reducing its specificity as a prebiotic [26]. The PAS value of XOS was significantly higher than that of inulin and GOS, demonstrating superior performance in the prebiotic activity assay employed in this study. Kathiresan et al. [32] reported PAS values of 12.43 and 17.29 for the XOS derived from sugarcane bagasse, tested against Lactobacillus plantarum and Lactobacillus fermentum, respectively, supporting the present findings.

The produced XOS exhibited encouraging prebiotic properties, as reflected by the PI and PAS values obtained in this study. However, these findings should be considered preliminary, since the evaluation was conducted using only one probiotic strain and one non-beneficial bacterial strain. While this approach provides valuable insight into the selective utilization of XOS, it does not fully capture the complexity of microbial interactions within the human gut ecosystem. Therefore, future studies involving a wider range of probiotic and pathogenic microorganisms, as well as more representative in vitro gut fermentation models, would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the prebiotic efficacy of the produced XOS.

DPPH radical-scavenging ability

The antioxidant potential of XOS from SS was evaluated using the DPPH radical-scavenging assay. Results are shown as percentage DPPH radical inhibition relative to concentration (Fig. 5). The XOS exhibited a concentration-dependent increase in DPPH scavenging activity up to 1 mg/mL, where the maximum inhibition (78.54%) was observed. Further increases in concentration (2–4 mg/mL) did not enhance the scavenging activity and resulted in a slight decline, with inhibition remaining 75.83% at the highest concentration tested (4 mg/mL). This behaviour suggested saturation of the radical-scavenging mechanism at higher XOS concentrations. Although the activity was always lower than that of ascorbic acid (positive control), the relatively small difference indicated appreciable antioxidant power of the produced oligosaccharides. This might be attributed to the higher DP6 concentration in our XOS mixture, indicating the presence of more free hydroxyl groups and reducing ends, which facilitate the scavenging of DPPH radicals, since higher DP XOS are known to possess greater antioxidant potential than lower DP XOS [61]. The concentration-dependent response observed in this study is consistent with the previous reports on hemicellulose-derived XOS [48, 74]. The highest scavenging activity was observed at 1.25 mg/mL for XOS enzymatically derived from maize straw xylan [48], supporting the present findings and confirming the excellent antioxidant potential of the XOS even at lower concentrations.

Resistance of XOS mixture to gastric juice

The resistance of the XOS to acidic conditions was evaluated by monitoring its hydrolysis behaviour under simulated gastric conditions at pH 1–5 for up to 6 h. The influence of pH and hydrolysis time on the degree of hydrolysis of XOS is shown in Fig. 6. A gradual increase in hydrolysis over time was observed at all pH values. At pH 1 and 2, the hydrolysis increased to 5.58% and 5.39%, respectively, indicating that highly acidic conditions promote greater hydrolysis of the XOS. Under moderately acidic conditions (pH 3), the hydrolysis reached 3.67%, whereas at pH 4 and 5, the degradation was lower, at 3.50% and 3.00%, respectively, after 6 h. Overall, hydrolysis remained below 6% across all experimental conditions, indicating limited XOS breakdown in simulated gastric environments. Kathiresan et al. [32] reported that the degree of hydrolysis of XOS derived from sugarcane bagasse ranged from 5.3%, 3.9%, 3.89%, 2.43%, 2%, and 1.4% after 6 h in the varied pH range (1–6). Wichienchot et al. [77] also highlighted that the maximum hydrolysis (4.07%) occurred after 2 h of incubation at pH 1 for pitaya oligosaccharides. Thus, the present findings are consistent with the previous reports that the XOS resists upper gastrointestinal digestion, a key requirement for qualifying a molecule as prebiotic.

Conclusions

This current study examined the XOS production from SS via the enzymatic route. Analysis of 3 M NaOH-extracted xylan showed high purity. The XOS yield of 85.26% was achieved under the optimized conditions: pH 5.5, 45.45 °C, 20 IU enzyme dosage/200 mg xylan, and 24 h incubation. The process produced significantly more XOS than similar biomasses. The main oligosaccharide fractions were DP2-6, with DP6 being the most predominant, followed by DP2. These fractions are known for stronger prebiotic activity, making them commercially valuable. XOS exhibited favourable prebiotic potential compared with the commercial prebiotics evaluated under the experimental conditions. In addition, the XOS showed appreciable antioxidant activity and retained substantial stability under simulated gastric conditions, indicating their potential to reach the lower gastrointestinal tract, where prebiotic effects are exerted. The results support SS as an effective feedstock for XOS production. Combining alkali pretreatment and enzymatic hydrolysis under RSM conditions proved scalable and efficient. Further, this approach offers a sustainable way to valorize agricultural residues into high-value prebiotics for food, feed, and nutraceutical uses.
