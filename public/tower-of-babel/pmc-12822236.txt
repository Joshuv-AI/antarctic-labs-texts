Association of inter-delivery interval and the success of vaginal birth after cesarean delivery in high-risk women: a population-based study in the US
Authors: Ziwen Xu, Meng Ni, Xiayi Wang, Weiwei Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCompared to a repeat cesarean section, vaginal birth after cesarean (VBAC) offers potential health benefits. While inter-delivery interval (IDI) may influence VBAC success, its specific impact in high-risk women remains unclear.MethodsThis population-based cross-sectional study utilized data from the US National Vital Statistics System from 2016 to 2018, including women with a prior cesarean and a current singleton live birth. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed.ResultsAmong 673,005 high-risk women, 96,479 underwent a trial of labor after cesarean (TOLAC), with 64,167 (66.5%) achieving VBAC. In adjusted analysis, each additional month of IDI was associated with a 0.3% higher odds of VBAC failure (aOR 1.003, 95%CI 1.002–1.003). Using categorical intervals, IDI of 24–59 months and ≥ 60 months were associated with 6.1% (aOR 1.061, 95%CI 1.016–1.108) and 25.9% (aOR 1.259, 95%CI 1.202–1.319) higher odds of failure, respectively, compared to 18–23 months. IDI ≤ 5 or 6–11 months showed no significant association.ConclusionsA longer IDI, particularly ≥ 60 months, is an independent risk factor for VBAC failure among high-risk women.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08326-0.

Background

Between 1970 and 2016, cesarean delivery rates in the United States (US) rose significantly from 5% to 31.9% [1]. The growth of cesarean delivery has led to increased interest in trial of labor after cesarean (TOLAC), as achieving vaginal birth after cesarean (VBAC) offers several potential health benefits [2–5]. These include short-term advantages such as reduced risk of transfusion and infection, and long-term benefits such as lower risks of hysterectomy, bowel or bladder injury, and abnormal placentation associated with multiple cesarean deliveries [4–6]. However, many TOLAC attempts failed due to various factors, including previous breech presentation, prior vaginal delivery, advanced maternal age, birth weight > 4 kg, and use of augmentation or induction [7, 8]. Understanding these predictors is crucial for patient selection and counseling. In addition to these factors, optimal birth spacing is another crucial aspect of postpartum family planning [9].

The inter-delivery interval (IDI), defined as the date of the last live birth delivery from the date of the current pregnancy delivery, is a modifiable risk factor for adverse neonatal and maternal outcomes [10, 11]. Overall, the association between IDI and pregnancy outcomes demonstrates a U-shaped pattern: short IDI (< 18 months) is linked to increased risks of maternal infection, preterm birth, low birth weight, and neonatal mortality [12, 13], whereas long IDI (≥ 60 months) is associated with higher risks of preeclampsia, gestational diabetes, and stillbirth [14, 15]. To minimize adverse events, the World Health Organization guidelines recommend waiting at least 2 years after a live birth before conceiving again [16]. This recommendation highlights the importance of birth spacing, which may also be relevant for women attempting VBAC.

High-risk pregnancies—those complicated by conditions such as pre-pregnancy hypertension, diabetes, or obesity—are particularly susceptible to surgical complications following cesarean delivery, including wound infection, separation, or venous thromboembolism [17–19]. In the US, the prevalence of pre-pregnancy obesity is 29%, with pre-pregnancy hypertension and pre-pregnancy diabetes rising to 3.7% and 2%, respectively [20–22]. Currently, there are no specific recommendations regarding optimal IDI for high-risk women attempting VBAC [23]. Therefore, identifying factors associated with successful VBAC in this population is crucial. This study aims to investigate the association between IDI and VBAC success in high-risk women.

Materials and methods

Study population

This prospective, population-based cohort study used data from the US National Vital Statistics System (NVSS), including 11,622,400 live births between January 1, 2016, and December 31, 2018.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) singleton pregnancy; (2) previous cesarean delivery; (3) presence of at least one high-risk factor (pre-pregnancy hypertension, pre-pregnancy diabetes, or pre-pregnancy Body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m²); and (4) availability of maternal and neonatal outcome data. Exclusion criteria were: (1) primipara; (2) multiple births; (3) missing information on IDI or method of delivery; (4) received planned cesarean.

There were 11,622,400 pregnant women with live births recorded in NVSS from 2016 to 2018 (Fig. 1). After the exclusion of 4,404,375 (37.9%) primipara, multiple births this time, previous eutocia, or missing information on parity or IDI. A total of 1,672,625 (25.1%) women with previous cesarean remained. Then, individuals received planned cesarean or without information on the method of delivery were excluded. Finally, a total of 96,479 high-risk pregnancies with TOLAC for the index pregnancy were available for further analysis (Fig. 2).Fig. 1Study flowchart of patient enrollmentFig. 2Characteristics of high-risk factor distribution in the population

This study was exempt from informed consent by the International Peace Maternal and Child Hospital Institutional Review Board as it used publicly available data. Reporting follows the STROBE guidelines.

Exposure and outcomes

The primary outcome was successful VBAC. IDI was calculated as the time between the previous live birth and current delivery, analyzed both continuously and categorically: ≤ 5, 6–11, 12–17, 18–23 (as reference), 24–59, and ≥ 60 months.

Outcomes were divided into maternal surgical complications, neonatal outcomes, and neonatal interventions. Maternal surgical complications included maternal transfusion, perineal laceration, ruptured uterus, unplanned hysterectomy, and admission to the intensive care unit (ICU). Neonatal outcomes included birth weight, gestational age, Apgar score in 5 min, and occurrence of seizures or serious neurologic dysfunction. Birth weight was further categorized as very low birth weight (< 1,500 g), low birth weight (< 2,500 g), non–low birth weight(≥ 2,500 g), and unknown. Gestational age was categorized as extremely preterm (< 28 weeks), very preterm (28–31+6 weeks), middle preterm (32–33+6 weeks), late preterm (34–36+6 weeks), term (37–41+6 weeks), or over term (≥ 42 weeks). Apgar score in 5 min was categorized as 0–3, 4–6, 7–8, 9–10, and unknown, based on commonly used cutoffs [24]. Neonatal interventions included admission to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), assisted ventilation immediately after delivery, assisted ventilation for more than 6 h, use of antibiotics for suspected neonatal sepsis, and administration of surfactant replacement therapy.

Covariates

The study considered several maternal variables for analysis, including maternal age, race and ethnicity, education, marital status, parity, pre-pregnancy BMI, weight gain, gestational diabetes, gestational hypertension, eclampsia, history of preterm birth, mode of conception, neonatal sex, and prenatal visit.

Maternal age was determined as the age at the time of birth and categorized into groups: < 20, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, and ≥ 35 years [25]. Maternal race and ethnicity were classified as White, Black, Asian, American Indian or Alaska Native (AIAN), Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander (NHOPI), people of more than one race, Hispanic, and unknown or unstated racial/ethnic origin. Maternal educational levels were recorded as completion of 8th grade or lower, completion of 9th −12th grade without a diploma, completion of high school, college or above degree, and unknown. Marital status was categorized as married, unmarried, and unknown. Parity, defined as the total number of live births excluding the current delivery, was classified as one, two, three, or four and above. Maternal pre-pregnancy BMI was categorized as < 18.5, 18.5–24.9, 25.0–29.9, 30.0–34.9, 35.0–39.9, ≥ 40.0 kg/m², or unknown [26]. Maternal weight gain was categorized as < 11, 11–20, 21–30, 31–40, ≥ 41 pounds, or unknown [27]. The presence of gestational diabetes, gestational hypertension, eclampsia, and history of preterm birth was recorded as "yes" or "no". Mode of conception was classified as natural, assisted reproductive technology, and fertility-enhancing drugs. Neonatal sex was divided into female and male. Prenatal visits were categorized as "no", 1–6, 7–10, 11–18, > 18, or unknown.

These variables were obtained from the facility worksheet of the US Standard Certificate of Live Birth. Diagnostic criteria for these conditions were documented in the worksheet. In cases where data were missing for any of these covariates, a separate category for missing values was included.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD, categorical variables as n (%). Group comparisons used t-tests, Kruskal–Wallis, chi-square, or rank-sum tests as appropriate.

Univariable logistic regression was conducted to identify potential confounders for VBAC. Significant variables were included in multivariable logistic regression. All selected covariates—including maternal age, race and ethnicity, education, marital status, pre-pregnancy BMI, weight gain, parity, number of prenatal visits, mode of conception, and neonatal sex—were retained in the final model due to clinical relevance. Results are reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). (Supplemental Table 1)

All statistical analyses were performed using R 4.0.2 software (e.g., rms package and glm package). Two-sided tests were conducted, and a P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Among 96,479 high-risk pregnancies attempting TOLAC, 64,167 (66.5%) women succeeded in VBAC, while 32,312 (33.5%) failed the trial. Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the study population are presented in Table 1. Among the VBAC group, the maternal age distribution was comparable between the VBAC group and the failed TOLAC group. White (41.2% vs. 39.3%) and Hispanic women (28.3% vs. 25.6%) were more likely to achieve VBAC, whereas Black women were more likely to experience failed attempts. Interestingly, women in the VBAC group were of lower education levels (52.7% vs. 56.0%). Besides, failed groups were often with one parity (38.6% vs. 26.7%), and they had higher pre-pregnancy BMI, greater weight gain, and were more likely to develop gestational diabetes (11.4% vs. 10.0%), gestational hypertension (10.0% vs. 6.8%), and eclampsia (0.5% vs. 0.3%).

As reported in Table 2, in terms of maternal outcomes, the failed group had a higher chance of maternal transfusion (0.8% vs. 0.4%), uterine rupture (0.8% vs. 0.0%), and unplanned hysterectomy (0.2% vs. 0.1%). In comparison, the VBAC group has higher perineal lacerations rate (1.0% vs. 0.0%). Regarding neonatal outcomes, neonates of the VBAC group were with a higher rate of very low birth weight (2.2% vs. 1.5%), fewer preterm births (11.6% vs. 13.0%) but higher extremely preterm birth (< 28 weeks, 1.8% vs. 0.7%). In addition, newborns in VBAC group were less likely to require assisted ventilation (1.6% vs. 2.8%) or antibiotic use (2.9% vs. 4.2%).

Using recommended time interval, the unadjusted model demonstrated that in high-risk pregnant women with IDI between 24 and 59 months and ≥ 60 months, the odds of failing VBAC increased by 12.8% and 55.0%, respectively (OR 1.128, 95%CI 1.080 to 1.179 for 24–59 months; OR 1.550, 95%CI 1.481 to 1.623 for ≥ 60 months). After adjusting for covariates, the strength of IDI between 24 and 59 months and ≥ 60 months attenuated, but maintained a dose-response effect (6.1% for 24–59 months; 25.9% for ≥ 60 months). However, IDI ≤ 5 months or IDI between 6 and 11 months manifested a protective effect with no statistical significance.

Other risk factors include male offspring, maternal race of Black or Asian descent, education level above middle school, unmarried status, more weight gain, prenatal visits > 18, and less parity; the details are listed in Supplemental Table 2.

Then, we explored the relationship in three high-risk pregnancies separately (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2, pre-pregnancy hypertension, pre-pregnancy diabetes) (Supplemental Table 3). In women with obesity, longer IDI showed a modest but statistically significant increase in VBAC failure risk when analyzed as a continuous variable (OR 1.004, 95%CI 1.004 to 1.005). This continuous relationship translated into meaningful clinical effects in categorical analysis, where extended intervals became significant risk factors for VBAC failure: 24–59 months (OR 1.09, 95%CI 1.04 to 1.14) and ≥ 60 months (OR 1.42, 95%CI 1.35 to 1.49), with the longest interval category showing the highest risk. In women with pre-pregnancy hypertension, continuous analysis showed a minimal but statistically significant increase in VBAC failure risk with longer intervals (OR 1.00, 95%CI 1.00 to 1.01), though the effect size was negligible clinically. Categorical analysis revealed that only the longest interval category (≥ 60 months) showed a significant association with increased VBAC failure risk (OR 1.44, 95%CI 1.03 to 2.01). Women with pre-pregnancy diabetes demonstrated no significant association between IDI and VBAC success due to small sample size. Nevertheless, given that pre-pregnancy hypertension or pre-pregnancy diabetes are often comorbid with obesity [28, 29], as well as in our study, our findings remain meaningful.

A clear dose-response relationship was observed between parity and the strength of association between IDI and VBAC failure. The OR progressively increased with higher parity: parity 1 (OR 1.016, 95%CI 1.008 to 1.026), parity 2 (OR 1.045, 95%CI 1.036 to 1.055), parity 3 (OR 1.058, 95%CI 1.046 to 1.070), and parity ≥ 4 (OR 1.091, 95%CI 1.081 to 1.102). This trend suggested that the positive effect of longer IDI on VBAC failure became more pronounced in multiparous women. Significant racial and ethnic differences were observed in the association strength. Most racial/ethnic groups demonstrated statistically significant positive associations between IDI and VBAC failure. Notably, NHOPI women showed no significant association between IDI and VBAC failure (OR 0.995, 95%CI 0.913 to 1.084, P = 0.910). Maternal age showed variable effects, with the strongest associations in women aged 25–34 years. Educational attainment and marital status showed relatively consistent associations across subgroups.

Discussion

This large population-based study demonstrates that longer IDI, particularly ≥ 60 months, is independently associated with increased risk of VBAC failure in high-risk women. Short IDI (< 18 months) is not significantly associated with failure, contrasting with findings in general obstetric populations [30, 31].

Consistent with previous studies in similar cohorts, the VBAC success rate in this study is relatively high at 66.5%. Despite this, the success rate is lower than the approximately 75% observed in general populations [23, 32–34]. This discrepancy reflects the compound challenges posed by underlying maternal comorbidities including pre-pregnancy hypertension, diabetes, and obesity. More than 80% of our high-risk cohort experienced planned repeat cesarean delivery, with the remainder attempting TOLAC.

Previous studies have identified numerous factors for VBAC failure, which can be categorized into demographic factors (ethnicity of Hispanic, race of African-American, advanced maternal age), maternal factors (obesity, short stature, previous preterm caesarean birth), and fetal factors (male neonatal sex, estimated fetal weight > 4 kg) [7, 34–36]. Our analysis confirms several of these associations while identifying additional factors, including education level above middle school, unmarried status, and more than 18 prenatal visits.

The definition of short IDI usually raging from < 3 months to < 18 months in different literature, while an interval of ≥ 60 months is often referred to as a long IDI [37–39]. The relationship between IDI and VBAC success has been controversial in existing literature. By analyzing 129 studies, Ni et al. demonstrated that a long interval (≥ 60 months) increases the risk of cesarean delivery [40]. Similar to our results, Knight et al. conducted a cohort study of 75,086 women attempting VBAC and found that a birth interval of 3 years or more reduced the likelihood of successful VBAC [14]. Meanwhile, Lauterbach et al. stratified patients with prior cesarean delivery and attempted TOLAC by IDI (< 12 to > 24 months) and found that short IDI (≤ 24 months) was not associated with TOLAC failure or adverse obstetric outcomes. Both studies focused solely on women with one prior birth and included both uncomplicated pregnancies and those with high risks. This approach may limit the applicability of the results. Whereas our analysis included multiparous women across various parity levels. Our parity-stratified analysis revealed a complex relationship: VBAC failure risk increased with IDI in higher-parity women, despite higher parity generally improving VBAC success rates. This apparent paradox may reflect the interaction between advancing maternal age and longer intervals. As IDI lengthens, maternal age increases, and women of advanced maternal age (≥ 35 years) have higher cesarean delivery rates than younger women [41–43]. The physiological basis for this relationship involves the decline in physiological and anatomical adaptations of the reproductive system over extended intervals [44]. These adaptations, including increased uterine blood flow and enhanced contractility, may reverse over time, causing maternal physiological characteristics to revert to those resembling primiparous states and potentially increasing cesarean rates [45, 46].

In high-risk populations, additional pathophysiological mechanisms may contribute to VBAC failure with prolonged IDI. Long-standing hypertension, diabetes, and obesity contribute to maternal vascular malperfusion, thereby elevating the risk of placental abruption as well as increasing the likelihood of cesarean delivery [47–50]. The progressive accumulation of these underlying pathological processes over longer intervals may predispose high-risk women to VBAC failure while compounding the baseline risks associated with other maternal comorbidities.

The absence of significant associations with short IDI in our high-risk population contrasts with findings in general populations [30, 31]. We speculate that the adverse effects typically associated with short IDI may be masked by other factors prevalent in high-risk pregnancies, such as poor uterine contractility, dystocia, and fetal distress [51–53]. However, this explanation requires further investigation with more detailed clinical data.

Unexpectedly, NHOPI women appeared more resilient to the adverse impact of a long IDI on VBAC failure. This resilience may be attributed to comparatively lower rates of obstetric trauma during vaginal delivery and other delivery-related complications in Native Hawaiians [54]. Future studies should differentiate between Native Hawaiian and other Pacific Islander populations and conduct targeted research.

Our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The NVSS database lacks specific clinical information, including oxytocin use, labor induction details, indications for previous cesarean sections, and cervical dilatation achieved before prior cesarean delivery. These factors can provide important insights into the mechanisms underlying our observations. Additionally, our study population was restricted to women with pre-pregnancy hypertension, diabetes, or a BMI > 30 kg/m², which may limit generalizability to other high-risk conditions, such as multifetal gestations. The heterogeneous and interrelated etiology of these three conditions may have influenced our results in complex ways that warranting separate analysis in future studies.

In conclusion, our large-scale analysis of NVSS data reveals that the impact of IDI on VBAC success varies between high-risk and general obstetric populations. As TOLAC attempts and success rates continue to increase annually [55], to minimize complications accompanied by emergency cesarean delivery and achieve VBAC [56], clinicians should incorporate IDI into their risk assessment discussions. Particularly emphasizing that, while short intervals may not pose additional risks in high-risk pregnancies, intervals ≥ 60 months significantly increase the likelihood of VBAC failure.

Our findings provide valuable evidence to guide clinical decision-making and counseling for high-risk women planning subsequent pregnancies. Future research should focus on prospective studies with detailed clinical data collection to better understand the mechanisms underlying these associations. Studies should also investigate whether these patterns hold across different types of high-risk conditions and examine the role of interventions that might mitigate the increased risks associated with long IDI.

Conclusions

In this study, we observed a moderately high success rate of VBAC among high-risk women, making it a relatively safe and viable option. Compared to an interval between 18 and 23, advanced IDI, especially ≥ 60 months, is an independent risk factor for VBAC failure. Thus, caution is advised in deciding to perform TOLAC on high-risk populations with a longer IDI.
