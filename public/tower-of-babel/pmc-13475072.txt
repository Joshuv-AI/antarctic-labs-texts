Socioeconomic, fertility and contraceptive determinants of unintended pregnancy among married women in Nepal: evidence from the 2022 NDHS
Authors: Ashraful Alam Siddique, Towhida Nasrin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGlobally, nearly half of pregnancies are unintended, particularly in low- and middle-income countries like Nepal, contributing to unsafe abortions and maternal mortality. Despite progress, Nepal continues to face high abortion rates linked to unintended pregnancies. This study investigates socioeconomic, fertility and contraceptive factors associated with unintended pregnancy to inform policies and interventions for Nepalese women.MethodsUsing Nepal Demographic and Health Survey 2022 data from 2992 married Nepalese women aged 15–49, we assessed factors associated with unintended pregnancy through descriptive statistics, χ2 tests and multivariable logistic regression accounting for complex survey design. Primary factors included age, education, wealth, fertility intentions and contraceptive use before last pregnancy. Unmet need was described but excluded as a predictor due to outcome overlap.ResultsThe prevalence of unintended pregnancy was 27.9%, while unmet need for contraception stood at 29.4%. Highest rates were observed among adolescents (15–19), women aged 35–49 and those with lower education and wealth. Multivariable logistic regression revealed that not desiring more children (adjusted OR (aOR)=2.90, 95% CI 2.36 to 3.57), contraceptive use before the last pregnancy (aOR=1.26, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.52), belonging to the poorest wealth quintile (aOR=1.69, 95% CI 1.21 to 2.37) and having a husband with only primary education (aOR=1.63, 95% CI 1.11 to 2.40) were significantly associated with unintended pregnancy. Regional disparities were notable, with Karnali Province reporting the highest unintended pregnancy rate at 38%.ConclusionThese findings underscore the urgent need to expand access to family planning services and address structural barriers, particularly for vulnerable groups such as adolescents, older women and the economically disadvantaged. Tailored interventions that promote consistent contraceptive use and support informed reproductive decision-making are essential to reducing unintended pregnancies and improving maternal and reproductive health outcomes in Nepal. A comprehensive, equity-focused strategy is critical to ensuring all women can exercise control over their reproductive lives.

Background

Approximately half of all pregnancies are unintended across the world,1 with a higher proportion occurring in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).2 Unintended pregnancies present a major public health challenge, often leading to unsafe abortion, maternal mortality and long-term health and economic burden.1 According to recent evidence, almost 30% of all pregnancies are terminated, a number that rises beyond an alarming 60% threshold when looking at unintended pregnancies. Alarmingly, half of these abortions are unsafe, leading to serious health complications and contributing substantially to maternal mortality.3 Each year, around 7 million women require hospitalisation due to complications from these procedures, and this contributes to 5–13% of all maternal deaths, making abortion a primary reason for maternal mortality. On the other hand, the economic burden of treating complications from unsafe abortions in developing countries is estimated to be US$553 million per year.1 Between 2015 and 2019, unintended pregnancy rates showed significant regional variations. In Europe and North America, the annual unintended pregnancy rate was reported to be 35 per 1000 women aged between 15 and 49 years. In contrast, Central and Southern Asia experienced a higher rate of 64 unintended pregnancies per 1000 women. Sub-Saharan Africa faced the highest incidence, with 91 unintended pregnancies annually per 1000 women in the same age group.1 Reducing unintended pregnancy directly contributes to Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.7 (universal access to sexual and reproductive health services) and SDG 5.6 (reproductive rights and autonomy).4 Beyond health harms, unintended pregnancy perpetuates poverty by interrupting education, limiting women’s labour force participation and straining household resources, with estimated annual economic costs of unsafe abortion complications alone reaching US$553 million in developing countries.1 Furthermore, unintended pregnancies have profound consequences for maternal, child and parental health, including increased risks of adverse maternal and child health outcomes, delayed prenatal care and poor child development.56

Meanwhile, Nepal, one of the poorest and slowest-growing economies in South Asia, has seen significant changes in its unintended pregnancy rates.7 Between 2015 and 2019, unintended pregnancy rates showed wide regional variation: 35 per 1000 women in Europe and North America, 64 in Central and Southern Asia and 91 in sub-Saharan Africa. In Nepal, unintended pregnancy rates have declined by 45% between 1990–1994 and 2015–2019, but the abortion rate for unintended pregnancies increased dramatically from 34% to 69% during the same period, which is a major concern.8 One of the key goals of the agenda is ensuring gender equality, as it is considered a pillar of human development. An indicator of gender equality is stated to be ‘the proportion of women aged 15 to 49 years who make their own informed decisions regarding sexual relations, contraceptive use, and reproductive health care’.4 The most recent data on this (SDG 5.6.1), which looked at partnered women of reproductive age in 64 countries, found that 23% are unable to refuse sex, 24% are unable to make choices regarding their own healthcare and 8% are unable to make decisions specifically about contraception, indicating that a significant proportion of women are unable to exercise decision-making power over their sexual and reproductive health and rights.1

Nepal has made important commitments toward improving reproductive health through the Safe Motherhood and Reproductive Health Rights Act9 and its Family Planning (FP)-203010 agenda. Despite these initiatives, substantial disparities in access to family planning services persist across provinces, particularly in geographically remote and socioeconomically disadvantaged regions such as Karnali and Sudurpashchim. Sociocultural norms, limited male involvement, misconceptions regarding contraceptive side effects and restricted autonomy among women continue to influence contraceptive uptake and reproductive decision-making in Nepal.

To address this issue, it is essential to identify the factors correlated with unintended pregnancy.11 Several such factors contributing to unintended pregnancies have been identified, including limited access to reproductive health services, high rates of contraceptive discontinuation, uncertainty about fertility goals and instances of gender-based violence.1213 While several socioeconomic and fertility-related factors are known to influence unintended pregnancy, evidence from the most recent Nepal Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS 2022)14 requires updating to inform current policy and programming. Despite efforts over the past two decades to improve reproductive health outcomes, Nepal has a maternal mortality rate of 151 per 100 000 live births.15 While the unmet need for contraception has shown a slight decrease from 24.7% in 2006 to 23.7% in 2016,16 the unintended pregnancy rate remains high at 68 per 1000 women of reproductive age.17 Evidence on the association between contraceptive practices, fertility preferences, and unintended pregnancies in the context of Nepal requires updating with recent data.

This study aimed to bridge the gap in current research by exploring how contraceptive use prior to pregnancy, along with socioeconomic and fertility-related factors, influences unintended pregnancies among Nepalese women. Insights from this analysis can help policymakers and healthcare providers develop targeted strategies to improve reproductive health outcomes and support informed family planning decisions in Nepal.

Methods

Data source, study design, and study population

This study was conducted using the dataset of NDHS 2022, carried out by the Department of Health Services, Ministry of Health and Population in Nepal.14 A cross-sectional design was implemented to survey married women between the ages of 15 and 49, providing a nationally representative sample. A total of 14 845 women from that age group were successfully interviewed in 14 845 households from 476 enumeration areas (EAs) (248 in urban and 228 in rural areas) in all seven Nepalese provinces. Provinces were stratified into urban and rural areas, with EAs selected using probability proportional to size. Response rates were 97% overall and 95% for the specific group. The study population was 2992 women who were extracted from 14 845 based on the study criterion. Details of the survey were publicly available on ‘The DHS Program’ website, with technical support provided by UNICEF, ICF International through the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) Program and the WHO.

Measurement of variables

Outcome variable

In this study, we recorded the pregnancy intention status of women regarding their most recent pregnancy as the outcome variable. For the analysis, we considered only the most recently completed pregnancy within the preceding 5 years. Currently pregnant women were excluded because their pregnancy outcome (intended or unintended) was not yet complete, and recall of intentions during an ongoing pregnancy may differ from retrospective assessment after delivery. The participants were asked if their last successful pregnancy was wanted, and they could answer with wanted, wanted later and not wanted. For this analysis, we considered wanted later and not wanted as the negative response (unintended) while wanting the pregnancy obviously indicated an affirmative response (intended).

Exploratory variables

The primary explanatory variables in the multivariable model included age, educational attainment (respondent and husband), wealth index, residence, desire for more children, history of terminated pregnancy and contraceptive use prior to the last pregnancy. Unmet need for contraception was calculated descriptively using the DHS-endorsed Bradley algorithm18 but was not included as a predictor in the multivariable logistic regression model due to conceptual overlap with both contraceptive use and fertility intentions. To avoid multicollinearity, we conducted a variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis; all variables retained in the final model had VIF values <2.5. Contraceptive use was measured as use of any method in the 3 months immediately preceding the last pregnancy, extracted from the NDHS reproductive calendar to establish temporal precedence.

For descriptive purposes (table 1), we reported current contraceptive use at the time of survey and unmet need for contraception. For bivariate analysis (table 2), we examined the association of these variables with unintended pregnancy using χ2 tests. For multivariable logistic regression (table 3), we replaced current contraceptive use with contraceptive use in the 3 months preceding the last pregnancy, derived from the NDHS reproductive calendar, to establish temporal precedence. Based on the relevant literature review, we selected some covariates for this analysis. We categorised the age of the study population into five groups as follows: 15–19, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34 and 35–49. Residency was classified as either urban or rural. Education levels were categorised as ‘No Education’, ‘Primary’, ‘Secondary’ or ‘Higher’. The husband’s education was similarly classified. Participants’ religion was recorded as Hindu, Buddhist, Muslim or Other. The household head was identified as either male or female. Employment status was noted as currently working (yes) or not working (no). The respondent’s occupation was categorised into ‘Not working’, ‘Agriculture’, ‘Services’, ‘Sales’, ‘Skilled’ or ‘Unskilled’. The desire for more children was recorded as yes or no. Decision-making authority regarding the respondent’s healthcare was also noted, categorised as ‘Yes’ or ‘No’. Whether the respondent had ever experienced a terminated pregnancy was recorded as ‘Yes’ or ‘No’. The wealth index or socioeconomic status in the DHS was determined using principal component analysis into five groups: poorest, poor, middle, rich and richest based on household assets and ownership (online supplemental table S1).

Data analysis

In this study, data were analysed using Stata V.16. All analyses accounted for the complex survey design of NDHS 2022, including survey weights (v005), stratification (v023), and primary sampling units (v021) using Stata’s svy prefix. We performed descriptive, bivariate and multivariate analyses. Descriptive analysis allowed for the exploration of sociodemographic characteristics and their distributions. Bivariate analysis (χ2 test) was used to identify the association between unintended pregnancy and exploratory variables. Multicollinearity was checked for the exploratory variables using VIF. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed only with variables that showed statistically significant association (p<0.05) in the χ2 test. Results were presented as ORs with 95% CIs. All tests were considered significant at the 5% level (p<0.05).

Results

The prevalence of unintended pregnancy among women in the study was 27.9%, while 29.4% of women reported an unmet need for contraception (figure 1).

Prevalence of unintended pregnancy and unmet need for contraception among women in Nepal, 2022.

Table 1 presents the sociodemographic and reproductive characteristics of the 2992 women of reproductive age included in this analysis, based on weighted data from the 2022 NDHS. The majority of participants were aged between 20 and 29 years, with about one-third (33.9%) aged between 20 and 24 and another 31.8% aged between 25 and 29. Two-thirds (65.9%) of the women lived in urban areas. Most had received at least secondary education, and over 70% of their husbands had completed secondary or higher education. Nearly 70% of households were headed by men. About half of the women were currently working, with the largest group engaged in agriculture. More than half of the participants (56.6%) said they did not want any more children, while just under half (51.0%) were using some form of contraception. Around 30% had experienced a terminated pregnancy.

Notably the regional differences reveal in pregnancy intention across Nepal’s provinces. Karnali Province reported the highest proportion of unintended pregnancies (38%), followed by Gandaki (31%) and Lumbini (30%). In contrast, Madhesh and Koshi provinces had the lowest rates, at 25% and 26%, respectively (figure 2).

Regional variation in pregnancy intention among women in Nepal, 2022.

Karnali Province had the highest proportion of women who reported wanting no more children (20%), followed by Gandaki (18%) and Lumbini (13%). In contrast, Madhesh had the lowest proportion of women expressing a desire to stop childbearing (8%), despite having a relatively high percentage (17%) of women who wanted to delay their next pregnancy (online supplemental figure 1).

Online supplemental figure 1. Type of unintended pregnancy by province among married women in Nepal, 2022

Table 2 outlines the relationship between unintended pregnancy and various demographic and socioeconomic variables. The data indicate significant age-related differences, with the highest levels of unintended pregnancies among women aged 35–49 (47.4%) and adolescents aged 15–19 (31.6%) (p<0.001), suggesting that both younger and older women are particularly at risk. Education appears to play a protective role. Women with higher education had the lowest proportion of unintended pregnancies (24.2%), whereas those with no formal education experienced higher rates (31.1%) (p=0.009). This inverse relationship also holds for the education level of husbands, where unintended pregnancy rates were lower among women whose husbands had higher education (p=0.002). Economic status, as measured by the wealth index, was also significantly associated with unintended pregnancy. Women in the poorest quintile reported a higher prevalence (34.5%) compared with those in the richest group (22.7%) (p=0.001). Additionally, women engaged in paid work had a greater incidence (30.7%) than those not currently working (24.9%) (p=0.042), possibly pointing to work-related stressors or limited access to reproductive health services. Strong associations were also found between reproductive history and unintended pregnancy. Women who expressed a desire to have no more children were more likely to experience an unintended pregnancy (36.3%) than those who still wanted children (19.7%) (p<0.001). Likewise, women who had ever had a pregnancy termination (45.5%) or reported unmet need for contraception (38.1%) were more likely to report their current or recent pregnancy as unintended (p<0.001 for both), suggesting unmet needs in reproductive planning and contraceptive coverage.

The data indicate a clear inverse relationship between wealth status and the prevalence of unintended pregnancy. Women in the poorest wealth quintile experienced the highest rate of unintended pregnancy at 35%, while those in the richest quintile had the lowest rate at 23%. The prevalence gradually decreased across the wealth categories, with poorer, middle and richer groups reporting rates of 27%, 28% and 26%, respectively (online supplemental figure 2).

Online supplemental figure 2. Unintended pregnancy by household wealth.

Table 3 presents the factors associated with unintended pregnancy. In the adjusted model, adolescents aged 15–19 years had higher odds of unintended pregnancy (adjusted OR (aOR)=1.58, 95% CI 1.02 to 2.46, p=0.044), while women aged 25–34 years had lower odds. Husband’s primary education (aOR=1.63, 95% CI 1.11 to 2.40, p=0.012) and poorest wealth quintile (aOR=1.69, 95% CI 1.21 to 2.37, p=0.002) were associated with higher odds. Women who did not desire more children had nearly three times higher odds (aOR=2.90, 95% CI 2.36 to 3.57, p<0.001), and those with no history of terminated pregnancy were 74% less likely to experience unintended pregnancy (aOR=0.26, 95% CI 0.22 to 0.32, p<0.001). Contraceptive use before the last pregnancy was associated with 26% higher odds (aOR=1.26, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.52, p=0.022), reflecting possible contraceptive failure or inconsistent use (table 3).

Discussion

The present study examines the relationship between the unmet need for contraception and unintended pregnancy in Nepal. Despite a mounting trend in contraceptive use among young Nepalese women over the past decade,19 this study found a high occurrence of unintended pregnancy (27.9%) and an unmet need for contraception (29.4%) in Nepal. Comparing across NDHS cycles, unintended pregnancy has decreased modestly from 30% in NDHS 2016 to 27.9% in 2022, while unmet need for contraception has remained nearly stagnant at approximately 29%. This suggests that while some progress has been made, structural barriers including supply chain gaps, geographical inaccessibility and persistent sociocultural norms continue to hinder further reductions.

An important finding of this study is that women who reported using contraception in the 3 months prior to their last pregnancy had 26% higher odds of that pregnancy being unintended (aOR=1.26, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.52). This counterintuitive result likely reflects contraceptive failure (particularly with typical use of methods such as condoms or periodic abstinence), inconsistent or incorrect use or discontinuation due to side effects or access barriers.20–22 This finding should not be misinterpreted as contraception causing pregnancy; rather, it highlights gaps in contraceptive effectiveness, counselling quality and method matching to individual needs. These results align with studies conducted in other LMICs like Bangladesh, Ethiopia and Angola, which have documented high rates of contraceptive failure and discontinuation contributing to unintended pregnancies.23–27 Furthermore, links have been established between unintended pregnancies and an elevated risk of abortion and maternal health complications.28 Women burdened with unintended pregnancies are more likely to resort to unsafe abortion practices, which can lead to severe health consequences and potential life-threatening complications.29

Women who did not desire any more children had nearly three times higher odds of unintended pregnancy (aOR=2.90, 95% CI 2.36 to 3.57) compared with those who wanted more children. This finding is consistent with studies in Ethiopia, which demonstrated that the lower the desired number of children, the higher the risk of unintended pregnancy.3031 The reason for this could be that every pregnancy is unintended for a woman who does not want additional children.30 Discordance in fertility intentions, where husbands desire more children than their wives, often results in pressure on women to continue childbearing. When such discordance coincides with inconsistent or non-use of contraceptives—whether due to limited autonomy, inadequate spousal communication or structural barriers—the likelihood of unintended pregnancy among women who have already achieved their desired family size becomes substantially higher.32

Adolescents aged 15–19 years had significantly higher odds of unintended pregnancy (aOR=1.58, 95% CI 1.02 to 2.46) compared to women aged 35–49 years. This finding is similar to previous studies2533 but does not match several studies conducted in African settings.3031 This could be due to a lack of comprehensive knowledge regarding contraceptive methods, combined with restricted access to sexual education stemming from sociocultural norms, as well as inadequate availability of contraceptive services for young women in Nepal. Additionally, early marriage or premature initiation of sexual activity can result in a prolonged period of risk for pregnancy, thereby elevating the chances of becoming pregnant.33 Identifying preferred contraceptive methods, coupled with the incapacity to negotiate safer sex practices within sexual relationships, might be an additional challenge for this age group. Women in the poorest wealth quintile had 69% higher odds of unintended pregnancy (aOR=1.69, 95% CI 1.21 to 2.37) compared to those in the richest quintile. This economic disparity reflects barriers to accessing effective contraceptive methods and quality reproductive health services. Women whose husbands had only primary education also had higher odds (aOR=1.63, 95% CI 1.11 to 2.40) compared to those whose husbands had no education. For women whose husbands have a low level of education, the inability to independently make reproductive health decisions,34 coupled with the husband’s disapproval or fear of decreased sexual pleasure,21 can also lead to unintended pregnancies. Women who had never experienced a terminated pregnancy were 74% less likely to report an unintended pregnancy (aOR=0.26, 95% CI 0.22 to 0.32) compared to those with a history of termination. This strong association may reflect that women with unintended pregnancies are more likely to seek abortion, or alternatively, that women with prior terminations have different reproductive intentions and behaviours.

The elevated unintended pregnancy rate in Karnali (38%) aligns with prior findings that remote regions face systemic barriers, including limited healthcare infrastructure and lower contraceptive uptake.17 Targeted interventions, such as mobile clinics and culturally tailored counselling, may help reduce this disparity.35 These regional variations underscore the need for geographically sensitive reproductive health strategies. Provinces with higher rates of unintended pregnancy and completed fertility preferences may benefit from expanded access to long-term contraceptive methods and counselling services. Meanwhile, regions with greater demand for birth spacing require robust support for short-term methods and community-based education. Addressing these disparities is essential for improving reproductive autonomy and aligning Nepal’s family planning efforts with its FP-2030 commitments and SDGs.11273637

Conclusion

This study underscores the strong link between contraceptive practices, fertility preferences and unintended pregnancies among married women in Nepal. Women who did not desire more children had nearly three times higher odds of unintended pregnancy, and those who used contraception before their last pregnancy had 26% higher odds, reflecting possible contraceptive failure or inconsistent use. Younger age, lower household wealth, limited partner education was associated with higher odds. These findings highlight the importance of improving access to contraceptive services and tailoring interventions to the needs of vulnerable subgroups such as adolescents and the economically disadvantaged. Addressing gaps in effective contraceptive coverage and promoting informed reproductive choices are essential for reducing unintended pregnancies and their associated health risks. A comprehensive, equity-focused approach that integrates individual, relational and systemic factors is vital to advancing reproductive health outcomes in Nepal.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences; therefore, we report association rather than causal relationship. Second, self-reported pregnancy intention may introduce recall bias. Third, the desire for more children was measured after pregnancy, potentially introducing rationalisation bias. Fourth, the exact timing of contraceptive discontinuation relative to conception could not be precisely determined. Fifth, the study did not incorporate qualitative approaches to explore deeper cultural, behavioural or interpersonal factors (eg, husband’s disapproval, fear of side effects, religious taboos), limiting understanding of the complex social dynamics underlying unmet need and contraceptive behaviour.

Recommendation

To reduce unintended pregnancies and address gaps in effective contraceptive use, several strategies should be prioritised. First, culturally responsive counselling programmes that align with local beliefs, norms and misconceptions are essential; community health workers can play a vital role by engaging families to address concerns about side effects and religious taboos related to contraceptive use. Second, reproductive health education should be integrated into school curricula and youth platforms, with a special focus on adolescents aged 15–19, who face higher risks of unintended pregnancy. Third, promoting male involvement through couple-based counselling can encourage shared decision-making and help dispel myths about contraception’s impact on sexual satisfaction. Fourth, ensuring consistent availability of modern contraceptive methods in both urban and rural health facilities is critical, alongside outreach services that reach remote and underserved communities. Finally, empowering women to make independent reproductive choices through education, economic opportunities and health literacy initiatives will strengthen their autonomy and improve reproductive health outcomes.
