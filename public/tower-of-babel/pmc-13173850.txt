Construction of an integrated curriculum for Medical English based on questionnaire surveys
Authors: Changyou Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntegrated curriculum design plays a crucial role in accelerating talent development and has attracted considerable attention from scholars both domestically and internationally. However, current research primarily focuses on the integration of professional courses, while studies on the integration of public courses remain relatively limited. Medical English, as the universal language for international medical communication, has been highly valued by medical schools in both English-speaking and non-English-speaking countries. Consequently, exploring the integration of Medical English as a public course holds significant theoretical and practical value.MethodsTo thoroughly investigate the current status and needs of Medical English courses, this study employed a random sampling approach in the summer of 2025. A total of 23 medical schools that offered Medical English courses, were selected from different regions of China, primarily focusing on “Double Non” universities with a supplementary selection of “211” universities. Three surveys were administered to postgraduates and their supervisors. The surveys were distributed and collected via the Questionnaire Star platform, with objective question data automatically processed by the platform. Subjective responses were coded with the aid of Deepseek V3. The reliability of the questionnaires was assessed using Cronbach’s α coefficient, while coding consistency was evaluated with Cohen’s κ coefficient. A random 15% subsample was independently reviewed to validate the coding reliability. Group differences were analyzed using chi-square tests.ResultsChi-square tests indicated no statistically significant differences in overall support for the integrated Medical English curriculum across respondent groups (p > 0.05), suggesting broadly consistent perceptions regarding its necessity among teachers and students. Cronbach’s α coefficients ranged from 0.74 to 0.83, reflecting acceptable to good internal consistency across all constructs. Cohen’s κ coefficients ranged from 0.77 to 0.82, indicating a substantial level of coding agreement. A manual review of a randomly selected 15% subsample showed that fewer than 6% of AI-generated codes required correction, demonstrating high overall consistency with manual coding. Descriptive statistics revealed three main findings: First, current Medical English courses were perceived to lack coherence (71.67%), with significant content repetition (73.93%). Second, there was a strong demand for integrated Medical English teaching, with 94.01% of teachers and 92.21% of postgraduates supporting this approach. Third, based on the differentiated course needs of teachers and postgraduates at the undergraduate, master’s, and doctoral levels, an integrated Medical English curriculum framework was developed, incorporating both vertical continuity and horizontal integration.ConclusionThe integrated Medical English curriculum framework, developed based on the survey results, appears to offer a promising approach to addressing the current lack of systematization in Medical English courses. It may contribute to the development of internationally competent medical professionals. Furthermore, it is in alignment with national educational policies and may serve as a valuable reference for the design of Medical English curricula in other medical schools.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09109-7.Keywords: Questionnaire survey, Medical English, Integrated curriculum, Construction framework

Introduction

The “integrated” curriculum is widely recognized as an effective approach for accelerating talent development. With regard to the concept of “integration,” some scholars define it as a sequence of educational stages—from bachelor’s to master’s, from master’s to doctoral, or spanning the full continuum from bachelor’s through doctoral study [1], whereas others view it as a natural linkage connecting undergraduate, master’s, and doctoral training programs [2]. Although in medical education the term “integration” is frequently associated with interdisciplinary or problem-based curriculum structures, in the context of this study, it specifically refers to the vertical alignment of Medical English courses across successive educational stages (undergraduate, master’s, and doctoral). More precisely, this study emphasizes the cross-stage continuity and coordination of Medical English as a public course, aiming to facilitate coherent progression of course content across educational levels and thereby enhance overall teaching effectiveness.

In recent years, scholars have conducted extensive research on the integrated teaching of undergraduate, master’s, and doctoral programs to optimize talent development. In developed countries, talent cultivation under an integrated curriculum has become nearly universal and is widely recognized and referenced internationally [3]. Drawing on these foreign experiences, an increasing number of universities in China have placed emphasis on the design of integrated curricula spanning undergraduate, master’s, and doctoral programs. Given the extended duration of medical education, integrated talent cultivation in China was first implemented in medical schools [4] and was subsequently extended to other disciplines. Overall, existing studies have predominantly focused on the integrated cultivation of professional courses.

Medical English, as a universal language for global medical communication and a tool for tracking developments in the medical field, has long been highly valued by medical schools in both English-speaking and non-English-speaking countries. In terms of curriculum development, foreign institutions typically place greater emphasis on the design of course content. For example, countries such as the United States, the United Kingdom, France, and Australia generally offer courses on Medical English lexicology and writing, including case reports, prescriptions, diagnostic certificates, and research papers [5], whereas Tokyo Medical University primarily offers courses on Medical English vocabulary, medical conversation, reading, and writing [6]. In China, research has predominantly focused on teacher training and instructional models. For example, Wang [7] proposed establishing a teacher community composed of “language teachers + medical teachers” to provide targeted professional development, while Cao [8] suggested a teaching model based on the characteristics of medical schools, combining “general English + Medical English.” Nevertheless, studies on the construction of an integrated Medical English curriculum remain limited. Most existing research is confined to a single educational stage (either undergraduate or master’s), lacking a comprehensive framework spanning the undergraduate, master’s, and doctoral levels. This limitation has led to problems such as fragmented curriculum design, repetitive content, and low teaching efficiency.

To address these research gaps, this study was guided by relevant theoretical and policy frameworks in the development of the questionnaires. The questionnaires were distributed to postgraduates and supervisors across 23 medical schools in China. The study aimed to systematically assess the demand for Medical English instruction at different educational stages. Based on empirical analyses, the study identified the appropriate curriculum content for each stage and proposed a vertically integrated Medical English curriculum framework spanning undergraduate, master’s, and doctoral programs. This framework could not only address critical gaps in the literature but also provide practical guidance for the development and optimization of Medical English curricula in higher education.

Methods

Participants

This study primarily focused on universities classified as “Double Non” (i.e., institutions that are neither part of the “985” nor the “211” initiatives), with a small number of “211” universities also included. A total of 23 medical schools offering Medical English courses were randomly selected from various regions across China, encompassing 886 postgraduates. These institutions included Shandong First Medical University, Anhui Medical University, Harbin Medical University, Hainan Medical University, Xinjiang Medical University, Guangxi Medical University, and Guizhou Medical University, among others. Utilizing a faculty network established at prior national Medical English conferences, the researcher invited teachers participating in the survey to distribute the questionnaire to their students via WeChat groups. Owing to difficulties in accessing medical faculty at other universities, the supervisor sample was primarily drawn from Yanbian University, comprising 103 participants (30 basic supervisors and 73 clinical supervisors). The questionnaires for the supervisors were distributed through separate WeChat groups for basic and clinical faculty. The final response rates were 56.29% (886/1574) for postgraduates and 68.21% (103/151) for supervisors.

Questionnaire rationale

Policy foundation

This study was guided by the requirements for integrated teaching outlined in the following policy documents: the Guidelines for Accelerating the “Double First-Class” Construction in Higher Education [9], the Opinions on Accelerating the Reform and Development of Graduate Education in the New Era [10], and the Opinions on Pilot Reforms in Basic Discipline Enrollment at Some Universities [11]. These documents provided the national policy context for curriculum design and implementation.

Theoretical foundation

The integrated curriculum framework was developed based on a range of educational theories and instructional concepts, including the Spiral Curriculum [12], the modular teaching approach, Needs Analysis Theory, Medical English as a Lingua Franca [13], and the principles of English for Specific Purposes (ESP). These theoretical foundations provided a robust basis for the design of the survey.

Drawing on these policy and theoretical foundations, the questionnaires were administered to the participants described above in the summer of 2025 using Questionnaire Star [14], a widely adopted survey platform in China, for both distribution and data collection.

Questionnaire development

The questionnaire was developed based on a comprehensive review of the literature on Medical English curriculum design and the author’s teaching experience, comprising 35 items each for basic and clinical supervisors and 31 items for postgraduates. Building on this foundation, the author conducted consultations with experts in the field to further refine the questionnaire constructs. The questionnaire was primarily structured around 12 constructs, each represented by two to six parallel items across the three versions of the questionnaire.

Prior to final distribution, a pilot test was conducted with 10 postgraduates to ensure that the wording of the items was clear and easily comprehensible. Based on their feedback, revisions were made to the wording of several items to eliminate potential ambiguities. The reliability of the questionnaire was assessed using Cronbach’s α, content validity was ensured through expert review, and construct validity was supported through triangulation of data collected from the three respondent groups.

Data processing

The responses to the objective questions in the questionnaire were processed using Questionnaire Star. Responses to the subjective (open-ended) questions were first exported and cleaned to remove incomplete or irrelevant responses. The cleaned textual data were then analyzed using Deepseek V3 for preliminary thematic extraction.

Based on the extracted themes and the research objectives, three coding categories were established: support for integrated Medical English teaching, support for discipline-centered instruction, and support for teaching by a relatively fixed group of instructors. To implement the coding process, a keyword-based coding framework was developed to identify expressions indicating positive attitudes (e.g., “support”, “agree”, “necessary”, “recommend”). Each response was coded according to whether it expressed a positive, neutral, or negative attitude toward the corresponding theme.

To ensure reliability, 15% of the responses were randomly selected and independently coded by the researcher and the coder. Inter-coder reliability was assessed using Cohen’s κ coefficient, and disagreements were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached.

Chi-square tests were conducted to examine potential differences between respondent groups, including postgraduates and supervisors, as well as between basic and clinical supervisors. These analyses aimed to determine whether perceptions regarding the necessity of integrated Medical English teaching differed significantly across groups. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Yanbian University of Medical Sciences (Ethics code: 20250182). To ensure voluntary participation, questionnaire participants were informed that their involvement was entirely optional. All participants completed the surveys anonymously and no names were recorded.

Results

Among the valid responses, 886 were from postgraduates, 30 from basic supervisors, and 73 from clinical supervisors. Data were analyzed using descriptive and inferential methods. Descriptive statistics, presented as frequencies and percentages, summarized overall response distributions. Chi-square tests indicated no significant differences between groups (p > 0.05), suggesting broadly consistent perceptions of the course’s necessity. Cronbach’s α ranged from 0.74 to 0.83, reflecting acceptable to good internal consistency. Inter-coder reliability (Cohen’s κ) ranged from 0.77 to 0.82. Manual review of a subsample showed that fewer than 6% of AI-generated codes required correction, indicating close alignment with human coding. These results provide empirical support for the design of the integrated Medical English curriculum framework.

On connection and repetition of medical English courses

A majority of postgraduate respondents perceive that the current Medical English curriculum lacks coherence and contains repetitive content. Specifically, 71.67% (635 out of 886) report poor curricular continuity, while 73.93% (655 out of 886) indicate that course content is repetitive across different educational stages (Fig. 4). These findings suggest the need for a more coherent and integrated curriculum structure.

These findings indicate that both teachers and students demonstrate broadly consistent attitudes toward the integration of Medical English curricula.

Subjective questions

Based on the qualitative coding of open-ended responses described in the Methods section, answers expressing positive attitudes toward integrated Medical English teaching (e.g., “support,” “agree,” “necessary”) were categorized as approval. The results show that the average approval rate among supervisors and postgraduates was 69.48% (80% for postgraduates, 69.86% for clinical supervisors, and 58.58% for basic supervisors). This rate is lower than the 94.01% approval rate derived from objective single-choice questions (92.21% for postgraduates, 93.15% for clinical supervisors, and 96.67% for basic supervisors). The discrepancy may be attributed to the differences between subjective and objective questions. While objective questions provide clear, predefined options, subjective responses allow for a broader range of expressions. Nevertheless, the subjective responses still indicate strong support for integrated Medical English teaching.

Further analysis revealed that, on average, 75.28% of clinical supervisors and postgraduates supported discipline-centered teaching (69.87% for supervisors and 80.68% for postgraduates), while 73.23% of basic supervisors favored instruction by a relatively fixed group of instructors at comprehensive universities. These findings provide valuable insights for guiding future curriculum design and research.

Discussion

Based on the above demands for integrated curriculum, the proposed curriculum framework is outlined as follows.

Course construction based on demand

Vertical construction: teaching semester, teaching hours and course attributes

For undergraduate students, Medical English courses are traditionally introduced around the fifth semester, after most foundational medical courses have been completed. At this stage, students generally have a better understanding of medical concepts and terminology, which may facilitate the learning of Medical English. However, in later semesters, the increasing workload of professional courses may reduce students’ engagement with language courses unless they are offered as compulsory subjects.

According to the survey results, 55.34% (57 out of 103) of medical supervisors suggested that Medical English courses could be introduced earlier, between the first and fourth semesters. In addition, students reported a need to improve their general English proficiency during the first semester and expressed strong interest in Medical English, while also indicating challenges in developing competence in the language. Based on these findings, it is recommended that the curriculum be structured to begin in the second semester. Specifically, Medical Humanities may be offered in the second semester as an elective course, followed by Medical Vocabulary in the third semester and Medical Popular Science Reading in the fourth semester as compulsory courses. This progressive arrangement aims to support the gradual development of Medical English competence.

For master’s students, given that an average of 63.20% of supervisors and postgraduates (625 out of 989) supported offering medical courses in the first two semesters, Medical Literature Reading (compulsory) and Medical English Conversation (elective) may be scheduled for the first semester, while Medical Writing (elective) may be offered in the second semester. Considering their distinct educational objectives, academic master’s students primarily focus on academic contexts, whereas professional master’s students emphasize workplace applications. According to Hutchinson and Waters (1987), English for Occupational Purposes (EOP) refers to courses designed to meet the specific linguistic needs of individuals in professional environments, whereas English for Academic Purposes (EAP) refers to courses aimed at equipping students with the language skills required for academic study [15]. Accordingly, the master’s curriculum may be structured to accommodate these distinct objectives, a design approach supported by 65.1% of surveyed supervisors (67 of 103). For example, in the Medical English Conversation course, professional master’s students concentrate on hospital-based communication (EOP), while academic master’s students focus on academic exchanges (EAP). Similarly, in the Medical Writing course, professional master’s students emphasize medical practical writing (EOP), whereas academic master’s students focus on thesis writing (EAP). Horizontal construction: teaching methods and teaching contents

In the context of internationalization, collaborative teaching and the integration of internationalized course content may help to enhance the overall quality of the current curriculum design. Accordingly, it is recommended that these elements be incorporated horizontally within the vertical curriculum structure to further enrich the educational framework. Although these recommendations are not directly derived from the survey data, they are presented as forward-looking guidance. Future research should evaluate the feasibility and effectiveness of these approaches within the local context.

Firstly, collaborative teaching.

Collaboration can be categorized into two forms. The first involves domestic interdisciplinary or inter-college cooperation. Effective Medical English teaching requires proficiency in both language and professional medical knowledge. Given the limitations of disciplinary expertise, neither medical teachers nor English teachers can independently deliver Medical English courses. Considering that 60.00% (18 of 30) of basic teachers and 64.38% (47 of 73) of clinical teachers expressed willingness to participate in cooperative teaching, it is recommended that universities take the lead in establishing formal cooperative teaching teams, comprising both medical and English instructors, to collaboratively design the Medical English curriculum.

The second form involves cross-border collaboration with international counterparts. Within the context of higher education internationalization, integrating domestic and international academic resources through a joint mentorship system may broaden students’ academic horizons and enhance their research capabilities. Previous research indicates that postgraduates supervised by dual mentors could publish more papers than those guided by a single mentor [16]. Accordingly, opportunities for cross-border collaboration should be actively promoted.

Secondly, internationalized course content.

In Chinese medical schools, platforms such as Web of Science, PubMed [17], Scopus, and Mayo Clinic [18] are widely accessible through library subscriptions or open-access resources. These platforms are commonly used by postgraduates and faculty for literature retrieval, academic writing, and research communication. In addition, platforms such as Complete Anatomy [19], edX [20], Overleaf [21], and Zoom Events & Webinars [22] are increasingly being integrated into medical education and online academic collaboration. The accessibility and growing adoption of these digital platforms provide feasibility for Chinese medical schools and offer effective channels for accessing internationalized course content. Consequently, curriculum content at all educational stages may leverage these platforms to align more closely with international standards.

At the undergraduate level, students could use Complete Anatomy and the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) [23] to master core medical vocabulary, learn medical ethics and medical humanities courses through edX, and read medical popular science materials from Mayo Clinic.

At the postgraduate level, students may use core academic databases such as PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science to learn internationally recognized standards for academic writing. They could practice extracting key points and composing papers through Overleaf, and participate in Medical English conversations and international academic conferences via Zoom Events & Webinars.

Curriculum content at all educational stages should be updated regularly to keep pace with contemporary developments and academic advancements.

The correlation between the vertical and horizontal dimensions

Drawing on Needs Analysis Theory alongside instructional concepts, the conceptual framework of the integrated curriculum could be further articulated through the metaphor of a “big tree,” as illustrated in Fig. 8. The vertical dimension (the trunk) may represent a demand-driven and stage-progressive curriculum structure spanning the undergraduate, master’s, and doctoral levels, reflecting the continuity and progressive development of knowledge and competencies. The horizontal dimension (the branches and leaves) could represent an expansion layer supported by collaborative teaching and internationalized course content, which provides essential enrichment and support for the vertical curriculum structure. These outcomes could be operationalized through clearly defined learning objectives, competency mappings, and assessment methods presented in Table 5. These outcomes could be aligned with the China Standards of English (CSE) and the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages (CEFR), and could be assessed through diversified evaluation approaches, including both formative and summative assessments. Through the dynamic interaction of vertical and horizontal integration, the overall framework of an integrated Medical English curriculum could be established.

Feasibility and implementation considerations.

Although the proposed vertical curriculum model could provide a coherent framework for the progressive development of Medical English competence, its practical implementation requires careful consideration of several institutional constraints.

First, student credit load should be taken into account. Medical students typically face intensive professional coursework, and the addition of new language courses may increase their academic burden if not appropriately scheduled. Therefore, the proposed courses should be integrated into existing credit structures or offered as flexible elective modules when possible.

Second, the availability of qualified teaching staff may represent another potential constraint. Medical English teaching requires interdisciplinary expertise in both language education and medical knowledge. Institutions may therefore need to establish collaborative teaching teams consisting of English instructors and medical faculty to ensure effective course delivery.

Third, the implementation of the proposed curriculum should align with institutional policies and administrative regulations, including curriculum approval procedures and program planning requirements. Universities may need to adapt the framework according to their own institutional context and teaching resources.

Considering these factors, the vertical curriculum model should be viewed as a flexible framework rather than a rigid prescription. Pilot implementation in selected institutions may help further evaluate its feasibility and effectiveness in practice.

Limitations

Firstly, among the 77 public undergraduate medical schools in China [24], 886 postgraduates from 23 universities participated in the survey. However, as noted in the Methods section, faculty from institutions beyond the home university could not be contacted, leading to the inclusion of supervisors from only one institution. This institutional concentration introduces potential bias, limiting the generalizability and representativeness of the findings.

Secondly, due to the constraints of the current teaching and training program, there was insufficient time allocated to test and optimize the integrated curriculum framework.

Thirdly, while doctoral students were not directly surveyed in this study, many participating supervisors were responsible for mentoring doctoral candidates. Consequently, the recommendations regarding the doctoral-level curriculum were primarily informed by these supervisors’ perspectives and teaching experiences within the vertically integrated curriculum framework.

Furthermore, the data in this study were primarily derived from self-reported questionnaires. As participation was voluntary, response bias may have been introduced, with individuals possessing a greater interest in the topic or more favorable attitudes toward the curriculum being more likely to participate. The large sample size and broad coverage of students also influenced response rates, which may have further affected the representativeness of the sample. Additionally, because the data were self-reported, responses may have been subject to biases such as social desirability, recall bias, or personal perceptions, potentially leading to discrepancies between reported and actual teaching or learning behaviors.

Lastly, some qualitative responses were processed using DeepSeek V3 for thematic extraction. Although the researcher manually verified and revised the AI-generated results, the algorithm may not have fully captured subtle nuances in participants’ expressions, introducing the potential for interpretation bias (AI-assisted coding bias). Future research could mitigate these limitations by combining AI-assisted analysis with cross-validation from multiple data sources and strengthening manual verification during coding, thus enhancing the rigor and robustness of the study’s findings.

Future research

First, following the completion of the current teaching and training program, several medical schools will be selected to pilot and further optimize the integrated curriculum framework. In addition, future studies may incorporate direct feedback from doctoral students to further validate and refine the proposed curriculum at the doctoral level.

Second, given that 94.24% of postgraduates (835 of 886) and an average of 90.29% of supervisors (93 of 103) regularly engage with literature related to their major, discipline-centered teaching could be adopted in the future to make instruction more targeted and practically relevant.

Finally, as 66.67% of basic medical supervisors (20 of 30) advocate for teaching tailored to the needs of individual medical colleges, it is recommended that college English instruction in the future be designed according to the specific needs of each institution rather than implemented uniformly. Such an approach would facilitate the effective implementation of English teaching reforms.

Conclusion

The integrated curriculum framework, developed based on needs analysis, has the potential to enhance the systematization and coherence of Medical English education. It may also support the cultivation of internationally competent medical professionals and strengthen their global competitiveness.

This framework could address the learning needs of medical students. It aligns closely with national education reform policies, which emphasize the integration of teaching across different educational stages. Specifically, these policies advocate strengthening the integration of courses and instruction at the undergraduate, master’s, and doctoral levels, facilitating seamless transitions between master’s and doctoral education, and establishing an integrated curriculum model that spans all three stages.

The findings of this study, particularly the strong support for integrated Medical English teaching (94.01% among supervisors and 92.21% among postgraduates), could suggest that this approach is broadly consistent with national educational objectives. Alignment between the curriculum and these policies could be expected to accelerate the training of medical professionals, improve public health outcomes, and contribute to the goals of the “Healthy China” strategy.
