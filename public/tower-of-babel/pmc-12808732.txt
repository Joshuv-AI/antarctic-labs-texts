A comprehensive dataset of riverine levee overtopping events for advancing risk assessment
Authors: Stefan Flynn, Farshid Vahedifard, David Schaaf
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Earthen levees are the primary flood protection infrastructure worldwide, with overtopping recognized as the most prevalent mode of their failure. Despite its significance, detailed field data on overtopping events are often limited and fragmented. Here we present the Levee Loading and Incident Dataset - Overtopping (LLID-OT) version 1.0, a curated dataset of 487 overtopping events in U.S. riverine levees compiled over the past 15 years. Data were collected from diverse sources, including U.S. Army Corps of Engineers reports, aerial imagery, hydrologic data repositories, and eyewitness accounts, and include detailed information on hydraulic loading, soil and foundation classifications, construction/maintenance characteristics, geometry, and breach dimensions. Quality control included cross-referencing sources and verifying key parameters to ensure dataset consistency and reliability. Analysis reveals consistent relationships between breach characteristics and variables such as soil type, overtopping depth and duration, and levee geometry. The LLID-OT supports the development and calibration of data-driven and physics-based models, advancing levee risk assessment, design, and flood management.

Background & Summary

Most earthen levees are engineered embankments constructed along rivers and coasts to reduce flood risk by containing or diverting water during high-flow events. They are among the oldest and most widely used forms of flood protection infrastructure worldwide. Archaeological records suggest levee construction began as early as 6,500 years ago1. Natural and human-made levees (often referred to as dikes in some countries) now exist on nearly every continent2, with many nations establishing agencies to manage the extensive networks of this critical infrastructure and its associated risks3–6. Recent estimates indicate that there are over 500,000 kilometers (km) of levees globally, protecting nearly 10% of the world’s population7. In several regions, the construction of levees has also contributed to increased development within floodplains, an outcome commonly referred to as the “levee effect”8,9. For example, in the U.S., levee construction has been associated with a 62% increase in floodplain urbanization, more than double the national county-wide average of 29%, highlighting a substantial shift in development patterns following levee construction10. In this context, levees serve not only as protective flood barriers but also as drivers of increased systemic exposure. Compounding the growing global flood risk, both current observations and future projections indicate an increase in the frequency and magnitude of flood events across many regions of the world11–13, alongside rising populations residing in leveed areas14. These trends highlight the increasing pressure on levee systems and underscore the urgent need for an improved understanding of their performance under evolving environmental and societal conditions.

Among the various causes of levee failure, breach due to overtopping is widely recognized as the most prevalent mechanism15,16. Widespread levee overtopping events have occurred in the past decade across the globe, notably in the United States in 201917, Central and Western Europe in 202118, and Italy in 202319. Despite its criticality, the complex phenomenon of levee breach due to overtopping is rarely captured in detail at the field reconnaissance level; therefore, real event data is limited and often fragmented in existing literature20,21. Studies are available that detail proposed parametric or physics-based evaluation of levee breach initiation and continuation based on laboratory22,23 and field-scale experimentation24,25. These datasets have been utilized to develop an array of parametric and physics-informed models for both dams and levees26–28 requiring various levels and qualities of input data. However, these models are frequently constrained by limited boundary conditions and the narrow scope of parameters captured during experimental investigations. In response, more recent efforts20,29 have aimed to compile broader datasets on levee performance, including many overtopping events that resulted in breach, to overcome these limitations and improve model realism.

Here, we present the Levee Loading and Incident Dataset - Overtopping (LLID-OT) version 1.0, a comprehensive dataset of 487 overtopping events in U.S. riverine levees compiled over the past 15 years. Because LLID-OT version 1.0 focuses exclusively on riverine overtopping events, the data presented herein represent static overflow conditions and are not directly applicable to modeling wave-induced overtopping. The presented LLID-OT includes detailed information on hydraulic loading, embankment and foundation soil classifications, construction characteristics, levee geometry, and breach dimensions. This effort builds upon post-Hurricane Katrina reforms, when catastrophic flooding and widespread levee failures30,31 prompted the establishment of the USACE Levee Safety Program in 200632 and subsequent authorization under the Water Resources Development Act of 200733. These efforts led to the development of the Levee Screening Tool (LST)34, whose performance metrics are supported by data from the LLID, which has been developed and maintained since 2012 to inform base failure rates and contributing factors using real-world data35,36. The dataset presented herein serves as a valuable research tool for understanding the initiation and progression of levee overtopping breaches. Rigorous quality control measures, including cross-referencing multiple data sources and verifying key parameters, were implemented to promote consistency and reliability. A preliminary analysis is conducted to examine the relationships between breach characteristics and variables such as soil type, overtopping depth and duration, and levee geometry.

The LLID-OT provides a foundational resource for advancing data-driven and physics-based levee breach modelling, improving flood risk management strategies, and guiding future infrastructure design and policy decisions under a changing climate. While the LLID-OT primarily focuses on the physical and hydraulic parameters that govern levee overtopping and breach, these data form the basis for quantitative fragility and system response modeling, key components of an end-to-end flood risk assessment. In this way, the dataset serves as an enabling resource for integrating process-level understanding of levee performance into broader frameworks that quantify hazard, exposure, and consequence.

Methods

Data collection

Over the past 15 years, data for the LLID-OT version 1.0 have been primarily collected from USACE construction documentation, flood fight reports, post-event repair records, river gauge information, and publicly available sources, including the National Levee Database (NLD) and aerial imagery. In some cases, additional details were inferred or elicited from eyewitness accounts or individuals familiar with specific levee overtopping events. Eyewitness accounts were used only to verify or refine the timing of breach initiation. As a result, certain temporal data points, specifically the timing of the breach, may rely on human recollection and subjective judgment. This information was only included if it could be reasonably verified through cross-referencing with other reliable sources. An earlier categorical version of the LLID overtopping dataset was published along with Flynn et al.37 and served as a foundation for the development of a logistic regression statistical model that predicts the probability of levee breach given overtopping. The current dataset represents a substantial expansion of that initial effort, incorporating additional overtopping events, refined hydraulic data, newly categorized embankment and foundation material properties, and verified levee system information, including the year of construction and protected area extent.

These new data elements were compiled through continued analysis of USACE project documentation and supplemented with hydraulic data from Rivergages38, USGS Water Data39, the National Water Prediction Service40, the NLD3, and the USACE LST 2.041. Systems and incident data

The levee system categorization includes the levee segment name, NLD segment identification number3, year of construction, responsible construction entity, total leveed area (km2), and the characterization of development within the leveed area (i.e., setting). All fields were manually populated using data derived from the NLD3, except for the levee setting. The setting (urban, rural, or mixed) was qualitatively assessed based on aerial imagery and the USACE LST41.

Spatiotemporal data associated with each overtopping incident include the event identification number, the year of levee overtopping occurrence, and a binary indicator for whether a breach occurred during the overtopping event. The timing of each hydraulic loading event was confirmed using stream gauge data38–40, while the timing of the breach, when available, was further refined through flood fight notes and archived media coverage.

Geometric data

Geometry-related data include embankment crest width, riverside slope ratio, landside slope ratio, embankment height above the landside toe, foundation surface layer thickness, final breach top width along the alignment of the levee, final breach bottom width, and maximum breach depth. The embankment (levee) height is defined as the vertical distance from the landside toe elevation (i.e., datum) to the crest of the levee at the breach location. It is noted that subsidence may alter absolute crest elevation over time, but levee height as defined (toe-to-crest) reflects the structural height of the embankment rather than an absolute geodetic elevation.

All breaches included assume flow into the system; therefore, no breaches are included that were determined to be caused by the outflow of floodwaters. These values were obtained from project plans, as-built drawings, inspection reports, scaled aerial imagery, and LST41 or NLD3 databases. When possible, values were cross-verified against multiple sources to promote accuracy. Slope ratios are expressed as whole numbers, with a ratio of 3, for example, indicating a 3-to-1 horizontal-to-vertical slope. Embankment height was recorded at the breach location when available; otherwise, representative and/or average height values from the NLD or project documentation are used. Foundation dimensions were derived from geotechnical investigation reports or LST41 documentation. Breach depth represents the maximum observed scour depth within the breach, typically occurring at the point of initial overtopping.

Geotechnical data

Geotechnical parameters include qualitative descriptions and estimated or known classifications of embankment and foundation materials based on the Unified Soil Classification System (USCS)42. These data were primarily sourced from project-specific geotechnical investigations and LST screenings. The historical use of qualitative descriptors enabled categorization into broad erosion resistance classes43. By incorporating estimated USCS classifications, the LLID-OT achieves greater granularity in defining parameters that influence erosion susceptibility. This level of detail enhances insight into the relationship between soil erodibility and breach progression during overtopping events.

Precise measurement of overtopping depth at the moment of breach is often infeasible due to the lack of localized instrumentation. As a result, the estimated overtopping depth is used as a proxy, allowing reproduction in future modeling efforts. In this dataset, overtopping depth is calculated as the difference between the maximum estimated water surface elevation (from the nearest hydrograph) and the estimated top-of-levee elevation at the breach location, presenting a best estimate. When multiple flood crests occurred during an event, the one most likely responsible for the breach is used. In non-breach cases, the highest recorded elevation during the event is applied.

Overtopping duration refers to the total period during which water levels exceeded the known or estimated crest elevation of the levee, based on the event hydrograph. In some instances, eyewitness accounts or detailed documentation provide timing of the breach, enabling point estimates for the duration of overtopping before failure.

The time required for the hydrograph to fall to two-thirds of the levee height after overtopping begins is included as a potential proxy for the period during which flow contributes meaningfully to breach initiation and widening. It is assumed that, once water levels drop below this threshold, flow becomes subcritical and erosion potential decreases significantly27,44.

Data quality control

Robust data quality control measures were implemented to promote accuracy and consistency. Data fields were entered individually while concealing adjacent rows and columns to minimize cognitive bias during manual assessments. Each event record underwent a detailed review, with special attention given to identifying and resolving potential outliers. Incomplete or non-descriptive entries were excluded from the final dataset to improve overall data integrity and reliability.

Technical Validation

Previous database use for model development

An early version of this dataset was previously used to develop a data-driven screening tool for predicting the probability of initiating a riverine levee breach due to overtopping. The model, based on five categorical input variables, achieved an accuracy of approximately 73% in hindcasting breach outcomes37. This earlier application demonstrates the utility of this data in supporting predictive modelling. The LLID-OT is designed to facilitate more refined and robust estimations of breach processes by incorporating additional variables, higher-resolution data, and broader event coverage.

Erosion properties

A key strength of the presented LLID-OT is the inclusion of detailed breach dimension data, which enables analysis of global erosion trends. The time to develop the breach is assumed as the difference in time between breach initiation and the point where the hydrograph recedes to two-thirds of the levee height, or tp,critical. As shown in Fig. 5, global erosion rates for coarse-grained soils tend to be higher than those for fine-grained soils, and values across both soil types span several orders of magnitude. Representativeness of national levee database

To assess the representativeness of the LLID relative to the broader U.S. levee inventory, two categorical variables were compared against the NLD: levee construction entity (federal vs. non-federal) and year of construction. The NLD reports approximately 75% of levees as being constructed under non-federal authority and 25% under federal authority3. In comparison, the LLID-OT dataset reflects a 70% non-federal and 30% federal split, indicating strong alignment.

Construction year data were also analyzed and are illustrated in Fig. 7 using both stacked bar charts and kernel density estimations. The distributions show good overall agreement, though the LLID is slightly skewed toward older construction dates. This is likely due to the focus on historical overtopping events, as older levees tend to lack modern design standards and are therefore more prone to breach given overtopping. This temporal skew is important to consider when extrapolating results, but it also highlights the critical need to document legacy infrastructure performance.Fig. 7Construction history of levee segments in LLID-OT version 1.0 compared to the broader National Levee Database (NLD). Usage Notes

This LLID-OT is intended to support research and development efforts aimed at understanding the rate and extent of levee breach due to overtopping. It is particularly suited for use in data-driven and physics-based model development, calibration, and compatibility testing. While the dataset captures a broad range of variables related to levee geometry, material properties, and hydraulic loading, users should note that data precision varies. Due to the retrospective nature of post-event assessments and the challenges of field documentation during flood emergencies, many values represent the best estimates available. As such, careful consideration should be given to data quality and source reliability when using the LLID-OT in high-resolution modelling or quantitative analyses.

Integration with systemic risk assessment frameworks

The LLID-OT dataset provides a foundational resource for advancing systemic risk assessment of levee networks under both current and evolving climate conditions. By compiling standardized, event-based information on riverine overtopping, breach mechanisms, hydraulic loading, and geotechnical characteristics, the dataset captures the physical response of levee systems to specific stressors. These data can be applied across multiple spatial scales, from structural-scale analyses that assess individual levee segment fragility and failure mechanisms to regional-scale studies that evaluate the interdependencies and cumulative behavior of levee systems within larger river basins. Because each record includes spatial identifiers and consistent parameterization, LLID-OT supports the development of multi-scale models that represent not only localized breach processes but also their cascading and network-wide effects on flood protection systems.

In addition, LLID-OT serves as an empirical foundation for exploring systemic flood risk in a non-stationary climate context, where the frequency, magnitude, and duration of extreme events are expected to change over time. The dataset documents how levee systems have historically performed under varying hydraulic loading conditions, providing the empirical basis for stressor–response relationships that can be extended through modeling to future climate scenarios. When integrated within systemic risk assessment frameworks, LLID-OT enables evaluation of how climate-driven shifts in flood regimes could modify the probability, spatial extent, and cascading impacts of levee failures. Consequently, the dataset functions not only as a comprehensive record of past performance but also as a critical component for developing integrated, climate-informed models of infrastructure resilience and systemic flood risk at both structural and regional scales.

Limiting assumptions

The authors emphasize the following assumptions and limitations:Multiple Breaches per Segment: In some events, multiple breaches are reported for a single levee segment. However, the sequence and timing of each breach are often undocumented. Typically, only the first breach is temporally defined. Users should account for this uncertainty in applications involving breach chronology or progression.Foundation Conditions: Subsurface conditions are based on best estimates, typically inferred from available documentation or regional geological information. In data-sparse areas, conservative assumptions were applied. These values should be used with caution, especially in mechanistic modeling.Overtopping Duration and Breach Timing: While overtopping duration is reported for many events, only ~25% include sufficient detail to estimate the duration of overtopping prior to breach. This limits the ability to estimate breach initiation timing and widening rates for most events.Hydraulic Data Interpretation: Hydraulic variables describe the general flood hydrograph and loading characteristics but may not capture precise conditions at the breach location or moment of failure. Potential dataset improvements

LLID-OT version 1.0 provides a comprehensive dataset designed to improve understanding of levee overtopping and breach risk, with particular emphasis on system response and fragility. Future enhancements could expand the dataset’s scope to better support holistic risk assessment and more detailed event characterization. Potential areas for improvement include:Inundation Data: Incorporation of measured inundation extent and/or depth for each event to quantify the spatial and temporal footprint of flooding.Consequence Data: Addition of post-flood impact information, including evacuation statistics, documented life loss, and economic damage estimates, to enable consequence-based risk analyses.Hydrologic and Meteorological Parameters: Expansion of hydraulic loading and forcing data fields to include design and event-specific annual exceedance probabilities, distance to the nearest gauge used to estimate overtopping depth, forecast lead time, on-site monitoring activities, and meteorological drivers of the flood event.System-Level Characteristics: Inclusion of qualitative system attributes such as the levee’s position within the river network (e.g., mainstem, tributary, confluence), evidence of cascading or sequential breaches, and documentation of known maintenance or operational issues. These additions would facilitate systemic risk modeling and interdependency analyses.Geotechnical Parameters: Integration of additional soil index and strength properties (e.g., shear strength, plasticity index, compaction data) where available to improve the dataset’s value for numerical and constitutive modeling.Surface Cover Characteristics: Documentation of levee surface cover (e.g., vegetation, armoring, erosion protection) where consistently available. Although such data are currently excluded due to inconsistent reporting, future work may incorporate this factor using standardized remote sensing or inspection-based classification methods.
