Multimodal AI and single-cell transcriptomics integrate to construct a histopathological prognostic model for bladder cancer, revealing the RTN3-glycolysis axis in chemoresistance
Authors: Shunli Yu, Yingjian Wang, Dapeng Feng, Xinglai Dai, Yongbo Luo, Jianing Wang, Zihe Wang, Fuxiang Chen, Haoran Wang, Fengyan Tian, Xuejia Zhai, Chaohui Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBladder cancer is a common malignant tumor of the urinary system, and gemcitabine-based chemotherapy often fails because of drug resistance. Tumor heterogeneity, particularly copy number variations (CNVs) heterogeneity at the genomic level, is a core factor that drives disease progression and drug resistance. However, the relationship between CNV heterogeneity at the single-cell level and the histomorphological features that are relied upon in a routine pathological diagnosis remains unclear, limiting its application in clinical prognostic assessments and precision therapy.MethodsThis study integrated six public single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets as discovery cohorts to systematically analyze the cellular landscape of bladder cancer. The inferCNV algorithm was used to infer CNVs at the single-cell level and identify subpopulations with high CNV (HCNV). A multimodal artificial intelligence (AI) strategy combining deep learning and image analysis was employed to construct a prognostic model based on routine H&E-stained pathological sections, with HCNV activity serving as a biological anchor for pathology feature selection. Hub genes were screened using multiomics data, and in vitro and in vivo functional experiments using bladder cancer cell lines were conducted to validate the functions and mechanisms of these hub genes.ResultsThe single-cell analysis revealed that HCNV cells constitute a malignant subpopulation with a unique molecular phenotype characterized by widespread activation of oncogenic pathways (such as TGF-β, JAK–STAT and PI3K), active communication with the tumor microenvironment (e.g., cancer-associated fibroblasts and M2 macrophages), and a poor prognosis for patients. The multimodal AI-based pathological imaging model effectively predicted patient survival. The model-derived risk score showed a significant but modest correlation with transcriptomic HCNV activity. Through multi-algorithm feature selection, we identified RTN3 as the core gene driving the HCNV phenotype. Our study confirms that RTN3 not only orchestrates an immunosuppressive microenvironment but also directly mediates resistance to gemcitabine. Mechanistically, RTN3 activates the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway, which transcriptionally upregulates key glycolytic enzymes (PKM2, GLUT1, and LDHA), thereby driving glycolytic metabolic reprogramming and ultimately conferring gemcitabine resistance in bladder tumors.ConclusionsThis study reveals a malignant cell subtype defined by HCNV in bladder cancer at the single-cell level and elucidates the molecular and microenvironmental basis of the poor prognosis of bladder cancer. A clinically translatable multimodal AI pathological prediction model was successfully developed. Furthermore, our findings establish the RTN3/JAK2/STAT3/glycolysis axis as a critical mechanism of gemcitabine resistance, highlighting RTN3 as a promising therapeutic target for overcoming chemoresistance in bladder cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40164-026-00825-w.Keywords: Single-cell transcriptomics, Pathomics, Multimodal artificial intelligence, Copy number variation, Bladder cancer, Chemoresistance

Introduction

Bladder cancer is one of the most common urological malignancies worldwide and poses a significant threat to human health [1]. Although gemcitabine serves as a cornerstone chemotherapy for muscle-invasive and metastatic bladder cancer, the high incidence of gemcitabine resistance remains a major cause of clinical treatment failure and a poor prognosis for patients [2–4]. Tumor heterogeneity is a core factor driving malignant progression and therapeutic resistance [5]. This heterogeneity exists not only among different patients (intertumoral heterogeneity) but also, more profoundly, within the same tumor (intratumoral heterogeneity), spanning the genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and microenvironmental levels [6].

At the genomic level, somatic copy number variations (CNVs) represent one of the most frequent genetic alterations in bladder cancer and serve as direct indicators of chromosomal instability [7]. Previous large-scale sequencing studies have classified bladder cancer into distinct molecular subtypes, some characterized by high-frequency CNV events and associated with specific clinicopathological features and the prognosis [8, 9]. However, these analyses, which are based on averaged signals from bulk cell populations, cannot resolve the intercellular heterogeneity of CNV states within tumors. They are even less capable of elucidating how specific CNV patterns dynamically shape the tumor’s cellular ecosystem, drive particular biological behaviors, and ultimately influence the therapeutic response.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) enables the high-resolution dissection of tumor heterogeneity and the tumor microenvironment (TME), providing critical insights into the cellular composition, functional states, and interaction networks [10]. Integrating this technology with CNV inference algorithms enables researchers to delineate the genomic heterogeneity of tumor cells and their concomitant transcriptional features at the single-cell level [11]. In addition, artificial intelligence (AI) has profoundly transformed the analysis of tissue morphology. Deep learning can not only extract morphological patterns related to the prognosis from routine H&E-stained sections [12, 13] but also propel the development of multimodal AI technologies designed to integrate multisource data, such as imaging and genomics data [14, 15]. Through algorithmic models, these technologies mine complementary correlations between data modalities, thereby offering a powerful framework for constructing more comprehensive models of disease biology.

Within this context, the present study is designed to comprehensively investigate the multifaceted features and clinical relevance of CNV heterogeneity in bladder cancer. We utilized multiple public single-cell transcriptomic datasets as discovery cohorts to identify a malignant cell subpopulation characterized by high CNV (HCNV). We then applied a multimodal learning strategy to associate these molecular features with routine pathological sections. This approach constructed and validated an intelligent prognostic model. HCNV activity served as a biological anchor to identify pathological features reflective of the HCNV phenotype. The final model predicts patient prognosis from morphological images. Finally, through integrated multi-algorithm screening, we identified RTN3 as the core hub gene driving the HCNV phenotype. We systematically investigated the role of RTN3 in vitro and in vivo, demonstrating that it not only fosters an immunosuppressive TME but actively drives gemcitabine resistance. Finally, we mechanistically elucidated that RTN3 confers this resistance by activating the JAK2/STAT3 signaling cascade to upregulate essential glycolytic enzymes. This study provides not only a deeper understanding of the biological consequences of genomic heterogeneity in bladder cancer but also novel targets and strategies for overcoming chemotherapy resistance and achieving precision diagnosis and treatment.

Methods

Data collection and processing

The scRNA-seq data utilized in this research were sourced from six public scRNA-seq datasets as discovery cohorts, including four datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (accession numbers: GSE135337 [16], GSE222315 [17], GSE130001 [18], and GSE211388 [19]) and datasets from published studies (Horowitz and PRJNA662018) [20, 21]. Additionally, one independent scRNA-seq dataset (GSE277524 [22]) was employed as an external validation cohort. Please refer to Supplementary Table S1 for a detailed overview of all enrolled datasets.

We obtained bulk RNA sequencing (bulk RNA-seq) data and matched clinical information for bladder cancer and normal paired tissues from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) Bladder Urothelial Carcinoma (BLCA) project. The whole-slide images (WSIs) used in model training and validation were also downloaded from this database. Please refer to Supplementary Table S1 for a detailed overview of the datasets.

Specifically, scRNA-seq data were processed primarily using the Seurat package (v4.4.0) [10, 23]. We implemented stringent quality control for all the data to ensure analytical reliability.

We applied the following filtering criteria: cells were removed if the mitochondrial gene content exceeded 20% or if fewer than 200 genes were detected; genes expressed in at least 3 cells were retained. We then performed data integration and batch effect correction using the Harmony algorithm [24]. Next, we conducted normalization and principal component analysis (PCA). Using the top 30 principal components, we constructed a K-nearest neighbor graph with the FindNeighbors function. Finally, we performed unsupervised cell clustering using the FindClusters function, which is based on the Louvain algorithm with the resolution set to 0.3.

Analysis of spatial transcriptomic data

Spatial transcriptomic data were processed and visualized using the Seurat R package. The data were normalized using the SCTransform (SCT) method. During data integration, the functions PrepSCTIntegration, FindIntegrationAnchors, SelectIntegrationFeatures, and IntegrateData were sequentially applied. Afterward, unsupervised clustering was applied to group the ST data with similar patterns. Cell populations were subsequently annotated by integrating information from H&E-stained histological sections and the highly variable genes specific to each cluster. The localization and visualization of the expression of specific genes in the ST data were achieved by constructing spatial dimension distribution plots and spatial feature expression plots. To visualize the spatial distribution of HCNV activity, we applied AddModuleScore (Seurat) using the 54-gene HCNV activity signature to ST data, generating per-spot HCNV activity scores that were mapped onto histological sections.

CNV inference and grouping

Somatic CNVs in tumor epithelial cells were inferred using the R package “inferCNV” (v1.8.1). Normal epithelial cells or nonepithelial cells (e.g., fibroblasts and immune cells) annotated from the same sample were used as the reference background. Large-scale copy number gains or losses were identified by smoothing changes in gene expression along chromosomal positions. The total CNV score for each cell was calculated by quantifying the change in its expression from the reference set. Based on the distribution of CNV scores across all malignant epithelial cells, we objectively divided them into three groups using tertiles to capture the continuous spectrum of intratumoral CNV heterogeneity: the low CNV group (LCNV, score ≤ 33rd percentile), the medium CNV group (MCNV, score between the 33rd and 66th percentiles), and the HCNV group (score ≥ 66th percentile).

Pseudotemporal transcriptional ordering analysis

The pseudotemporal ordering of malignant epithelial cells was inferred using Monocle2 [25]. The cell trajectory was generated from differentially expressed genes, with cells arranged along the pseudotime axis to model the transcriptional continuum across cells with different CNV levels.

Analysis of cell‒cell communication

The intercellular communication network was analyzed using the CellChat R package (v2.1.2). Leveraging established ligand‒receptor interaction databases, this tool quantitatively infers the probability and strength of communication between tumor cells from different CNV groups (LCNV, MCNV, and HCNV) and various cell types in the TME. Differentially activated signaling pathways and key ligand–receptor pairs were identified by constructing circular network diagrams and bubble plots.

Weighted gene coexpression network analysis

We detected gene coexpression modules linked to CNV levels by extracting the expression matrix of all malignant epithelial cells from the scRNA-seq data and analyzed it using the high-dimensional weighted gene coexpression network analysis (hdWGCNA) R package (v0.4.01). A scale-free topology fitting approach was applied to select an appropriate soft-thresholding power. Afterward, an adjacency matrix and a topological overlap matrix (TOM) were generated to represent gene‒gene interactions. Coexpressed modules were detected via dynamic tree cutting. Finally, we evaluated correlations between module eigengenes (MEs) and CNV scores to identify modules with significant associations.

Digital pathological feature extraction and integration

WSIs of H&E stained sections downloaded from TCGA database were used for pathological feature extraction. We employed the following multimodal feature extraction strategy:

Deep Learning Feature Extraction—A ResNet-50 model pretrained on ImageNet was used. WSIs were tiled into 224 × 224 pixel patches, and background patches with a low tissue content were filtered out. Qualified patches were input into ResNet-50, and the activated features before the global average pooling layer were extracted as a 2048-dimensional deep feature vector, representing high-level tissue morphological patterns.

Deep Learning Feature Extraction—A ResNet-50 model pretrained on ImageNet was used. WSIs were tiled into 224 × 224 pixel patches, and background patches with a low tissue content were filtered out. Qualified patches were input into ResNet-50, and the activated features before the global average pooling layer were extracted as a 2048-dimensional deep feature vector, representing high-level tissue morphological patterns.

Traditional Morphological Feature Extraction and Quantitative Analysis—A quantitative analysis of the images at the cellular level was performed using CellProfiler software (v4.2.8). The images underwent color deconvolution to separate the hematoxylin (nuclear) channel, followed by nuclear segmentation. Hundreds of quantitative features related to the nuclear morphology, intensity, texture, and spatial distribution were extracted.

Traditional Morphological Feature Extraction and Quantitative Analysis—A quantitative analysis of the images at the cellular level was performed using CellProfiler software (v4.2.8). The images underwent color deconvolution to separate the hematoxylin (nuclear) channel, followed by nuclear segmentation. Hundreds of quantitative features related to the nuclear morphology, intensity, texture, and spatial distribution were extracted.

HCNV activity score construction

To derive a continuous index of HCNV transcriptional burden, we first constructed an HCNV-specific gene signature. The top 50 hub genes (ranked by kME) from each of the nine HCNV-enriched hdWGCNA modules were intersected with differentially expressed genes between HCNV and LCNV malignant epithelial cells (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, |log2FC|> 1, Bonferroni-adjusted p < 0.05), yielding 220 genes (Supplementary Table S6). From this core set, we further screened for prognosis-associated genes using univariate Cox regression, resulting in a final 54-gene HCNV activity signature (Supplementary Table S7). For each TCGA-BLCA sample, we applied single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) using the GSVA R package (v1.46.0, kcdf = “Gaussian”, min.sz = 10, max.sz = 500, mx.diff = TRUE) to bulk RNA-seq data, with the 54-gene signature as input. The resulting enrichment scores were standardized to Z-scores across all TCGA-BLCA samples, generating a continuous score representing the relative enrichment of the HCNV transcriptional program. For survival analyses and group comparisons, patients were stratified into high and low HCNV activity groups using the median Z-score as the cutoff.

Construction and validation of the prognostic risk model

First, a correlation analysis (Spearman’s correlation analysis) was performed between the extracted deep features/traditional morphological features and the “HCNV activity” score defined from the transcriptomic data to screen for significantly correlated features. A univariate Cox proportional hazards regression model was subsequently applied to TCGA-BLCA training cohort (comprising 70% of the samples) to evaluate the relationships between individual pathological features and overall survival (OS). Model development and selection were performed using the MIME framework, which incorporates 101 machine learning algorithms [26]. This framework integrates ten core methods, such as random survival forest (RSF), elastic net (Enet), stepwise Cox regression (StepCox), CoxBoost, gradient boosting machine (GBM), and survival support vector machine (Survival-SVM). By combining different variable selection strategies, including Lasso, StepCox, CoxBoost, and RSF, MIME generated 101 model variants. Model performance was compared through 5-fold cross-validation, and the optimal model was chosen based on the concordance index (C-index) to derive a composite risk score (RiskScore). The ability of the selected model to stratify the patient prognosis, prediction accuracy, and net clinical benefit was subsequently assessed in an independent TCGA validation set (30% of samples).

Analysis of the immune microenvironment

We characterized the tumor immune microenvironment using multiple complementary approaches. For immune cell infiltration and signature scoring, we applied the IOBR R package to bulk transcriptomic data from TCGA-BLCA. To cross-validate immune cell proportions, we performed integrated deconvolution using multiple algorithms including EPIC, MCPcounter, Quantiseq, TIMER, and ssGSEA (Fig. S7C). ESTIMATE was used to calculate immune and stromal scores. To assess immune pathway activities, we performed ssGSEA using gene sets related to immune exclusion, T-cell-mediated inflammation, antigen presentation, and other immune-related pathways. This pathway-level ssGSEA is distinct from the ssGSEA employed as a deconvolution method in Fig. S7C. For immunotherapy response prediction, we applied the TIDE algorithm to estimate the likelihood of immune escape across risk groups, and the SubMap algorithm to infer probable responses to ICB by matching molecular profiles of each risk group with known ICB responders. Finally, we systematically analyzed immune-related gene expression across risk groups and identified group-specific molecular patterns through hierarchical clustering.

Drug sensitivity prediction

Drug sensitivity was predicted using the oncoPredict package (v1.2) trained on the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer 2 (GDSC2). Preprocessing included removal of low-variance genes (removeLowVaryingGenes = 0.2), exclusion of genes expressed in <10 samples (minNumSamples = 10), power transformation of IC50 (powerTransformPhenotype = TRUE), and empirical Bayes batch correction (batchCorrect = 'eb'). Ridge regression (calcPhenotype function) was applied to TCGA-BLCA RNA-seq data (log2(TPM+1)) to predict IC50 values. Independent validation used CTRP v2.0 and PRISM Repurposing datasets via pRRophetic (v0.5); primary findings are based on GDSC2-trained oncoPredict results. Correlations between predicted IC50 and HCNV activity scores or RTN3 expression were analyzed.

Histopathological attention mapping

Patch-level interpretability was achieved through Grad-CAM, which produced attention heatmaps emphasizing image regions that most strongly influenced the model's decision-making process. For WSIs reconstruction, predicted patch labels were spatially registered to their corresponding coordinates within the original slide. The resulting spatial prediction maps were rendered using Matplotlib to illustrate the model's output distribution across the entire tissue section.

Model interpretation via SHAP

To evaluate the contribution of individual features within the final prognostic model, we employed Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP). SHAP values were derived by applying the finalized model to the same feature matrix and normalization parameters utilized during the training phase, thereby maintaining methodological coherence between model development and post-hoc interpretation. Feature importance was determined by ordering variables according to their mean absolute SHAP values, enabling the prioritization of the most prognostically relevant predictors. Model transparency was enhanced through multiple visualization strategies: waterfall and force plots were used to demonstrate instance-specific feature contributions, whereas beeswarm and dependency plots were employed to characterize the overall magnitude and directional effects of features across the entire cohort. This interpretability framework elucidated how transcriptomic signatures and tumor microenvironmental characteristics jointly modulated survival risk, thereby improving the model's clinical interpretability and translational potential.

Cell lines and cell culture

The human bladder urothelial carcinoma cell lines T24 and UMUC3 were acquired from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Cells were cultured under standard conditions in RPMI-1640 medium or MEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. Gemcitabine (HY-17026), oligomycin (HY-N6782) and Stattic (HY-13818) were obtained from MedChemExpress (MCE).

Construction of gemcitabine-resistant cell lines

A stepwise drug concentration escalation method was employed. Parental cells were treated with a low concentration of gemcitabine, with drug pressure maintained across passages. As cell growth recovered, the drug concentration was gradually increased, with continuous treatment administered until the cells could stably proliferate in the presence of a relatively high concentration of gemcitabine (5 µg/mL). Previous reports indicate that established gemcitabine-resistant cell lines exhibit a 5-fold or even greater than 10-fold increase in the IC50 compared with the corresponding wild-type cells [27]. In this study, we successfully established the gemcitabine-resistant cell lines T24-R and UMUC3-R, whose IC50 values for gemcitabine were increased by more than 10-fold compared with those of their respective parental cells.

Gene knockdown

Two specific shRNA sequences were designed to knock down the human RTN3 gene and cloned and inserted into a lentiviral vector. The recombinant vector was then cotransfected with packaging plasmids into HEK293T cells to produce lentiviral particles. Viral supernatant was harvested and used to transduce both parental (T24 and UMUC3) and resistant cell lines. Stably transfected cell pools were subsequently selected using puromycin. The knockdown efficiency was verified by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) at the mRNA level and, correspondingly, by Western blotting at the protein level. The corresponding shRNA sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Construction of RTN3-overexpressing and rescue lentiviruses

Two distinct lentiviral constructs were generated using the same backbone (pLVX-IRES-Puro). For overexpression in parental cells, the full-length human RTN3 coding sequence (RefSeq: NM_001265589) was amplified by qRT-PCR from T24 cells and cloned directly into the vector (RTN3-WT). For rescue experiments in shRTN3-knockdown cells, a shRNA-resistant full-length RTN3 mutant (RTN3-rescue) was generated by introducing multiple silent mutations into the shRTN3-targeted regions via site-directed mutagenesis, which disrupt shRNA recognition without altering the amino acid sequence (Supplementary Table S2). Recombinant lentiviruses were produced by co-transfecting each construct with packaging plasmids into HEK293T cells. Viral supernatants were collected and used to infect parental cells (RTN3-WT) or RTN3-knockdown resistant cells (RTN3-rescue), respectively. Stable pools were selected with puromycin (2 μg/mL) and validated by qRT-PCR and Western blotting.

Construction of constitutively active STAT3 (STAT3-C)

Two point mutations (A662C and N664C) were introduced into the human STAT3 coding sequence to generate a constitutively active STAT3 mutant (STAT3-C). This mutation locks STAT3 in a constitutively dimerized and transcriptionally active state through the formation of an intermolecular disulfide bond, independent of phosphorylation at Tyr705. The mutant fragment was subcloned into a lentiviral expression vector and verified by Sanger sequencing. For rescue experiments, STAT3-C lentivirus or empty vector control was transduced into RTN3-knockdown T24-R and UMUC3-R cells, and stable pools were selected with puromycin. Protein expression and phosphorylation status were confirmed by Western blotting.

Cell proliferation assay

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates. At different time points (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 days), CCK-8 reagent (CT0001, sparkjade) was added, the samples were incubated, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm to plot the growth curves.

Colony formation assay

Cells were plated at a low density in 6-well plates and cultured for 10–14 days. Colonies were fixed with methanol and stained with crystal violet, and colonies containing more than 50 cells were counted.

Wound healing assay

A linear scratch was made in a confluent monolayer. After the cells were washed with PBS, serum-free medium was added. Microscopy images were captured at 0 h and after 24 h, after which the width of the scratch was measured.

Cell migration assay

Cells in serum-free medium were placed in the upper chamber of the Transwell, with medium containing 10% FBS placed in the lower chamber. After 24 h, nonmigrated cells were removed. The cells that migrated to the lower membrane surface were fixed with methanol, stained with crystal violet, and counted under a microscope.

Preparation of conditioned medium and functional assays

Preparation of conditioned medium (CM)

To determine whether RTN3 remodels the TME via secreted soluble factors, we performed CM transfer experiments. Stably transfected T24 and UMUC3 cells (RTN3-overexpressing, RTN3-knockdown, and their respective controls) were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well. After 24 h of adhesion, the medium was replaced with serum-free RPMI-1640, and the cells were cultured for an additional 24 h. The culture supernatants were collected, centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris, and filtered through a 0.22 μm syringe filter to obtain sterile tumor cell CM, which was aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C until use.

T cell activation and indirect co-culture

Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated, and primary T cells were subsequently purified using a magnetic bead sorting method. The T cells were seeded in culture plates pre-coated with anti-CD3 (5 μg/mL) and anti-CD28 (2 μg/mL) antibodies for activation. The filtered tumor cell-derived CM was added to the T cell culture system, and the cells were co-incubated for 48 h. The co-culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min to remove suspended cells. The concentrations of secreted pro-inflammatory cytokines (IFN-γ and IL-2) in the supernatants were quantified using specific enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits according to the manufacturer's instructions. IFN-γ and IL-2 were measured by ELISA (Elabscience, #E-EL-H0108 and #E-EL-H0099) per the manufacturer's instructions.

Macrophage polarization and CM treatment

Human monocytic THP-1 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at 2 × 105 cells per well and cultured in RPMI-1640 complete medium supplemented with 100 nM PMA for 24 h to induce differentiation into adherent M0 macrophages. After removing the medium and washing with PBS three times, the cells were cultured for 48 h in a 1:1 mixture of tumor cell CM and fresh serum-free RPMI-1640. Cells were then collected for Western blot and qRT-PCR analysis of M2 polarization markers (CD206, CD163, Arg1).

Fibroblast activation assay

Human bladder cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs, HyCyte, catalog no. PSHX-C221) were seeded into 6-well plates at 3 × 105 cells per well and cultured in DMEM/F12 complete medium until 80% confluence. The original medium was discarded, cells were washed with PBS three times, and a 1:1 mixture of tumor cell CM and serum-free DMEM/F12 was added. After 48 h of culture, cells were harvested for Western blot and qRT-PCR detection of CAF activation markers (α-SMA, FAP).

Drug sensitivity (IC50) assay

Cells were treated with increasing concentrations of gemcitabine for 24 h. Viability was determined using the CCK-8 assay. The IC50 was calculated from a dose‒response curve.

Glucose uptake and lactate production

Glucose consumption and lactate accumulation were measured using a fluorescence-based glucose assay kit (BC2500, Solarbio) and a lactate assay kit (BC22300, Solarbio), respectively, according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

Western blot

Cellular proteins were isolated, and their concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Following separation via SDS‒PAGE, the proteins were electrotransferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with BSA and subsequently incubated with specific primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After being washed, the membranes were treated with DyLight 800-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature. Fluorescent signals were captured and analyzed using an Odyssey CLx Imaging System. The primary antibodies used included RTN3 (Proteintech, 68215-1-1g), PKM2 (Proteintech, 60268-1-1g), GLUT1 (Proteintech, 66290-1-1g), LDHA (Proteintech, 84198-2-RR), CD206 (Proteintech, 18704-1-AP), CD163 (Proteintech, 16646-1-AP), ARG1 (Proteintech, 16001-1-AP), FAP (Proteintech, 11779-1-AP), α-SMA (Proteintech, 14395-1-AP), JAK2 (ABclonal, A11497), STAT3 (Proteintech, 60199-1-lg), pJAK2 (ABclonal, AP0531), pSTAT3 (Proteintech, 39,595), and β-actin (Proteintech, 536-1-AP) antibodies.

RNA Preparation and qRT-PCR

RNA was isolated using a commercial RNA extraction kit (AC0205, sparkjade). cDNA synthesis was subsequently performed with a dedicated reverse transcription kit (R433-01, Vazyme). Gene expression levels were quantified by qRT‒PCR with a universal, dye-based high-sensitivity detection system (Q7111-02, Vazyme). The corresponding primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Animal model

Four- to six-week-old female BALB/c nude mice were used. T24-R cells (3 × 106) suspended in a PBS/Matrigel mixture were inoculated subcutaneously into the right flank of each mouse. The tumor dimensions (length L and width W) were recorded at three-day intervals, and the tumor volume was computed as (L × W2)/2. Body weight was monitored regularly. At the experimental endpoint, nine mice were euthanized, and the tumors were dissected and weighed. A portion of each tumor was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and processed for subsequent hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemistry (IHC).

Histology and IHC

Paraffin sections were dewaxed, hydrated through a graded series of alcohol solutions, and subjected to antigen retrieval. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% H2O2, followed by blocking with serum. The sections were probed with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After being washed, the sections were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies and a DAB chromogen substrate for color development. Finally, the nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin, and the sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. The primary antibodies used were anti-caspase 3 (25128-1-AP, Proteintech) and anti-Ki67 (27309-1-AP, Proteintech).

Statistical analysis and data processing

R software (version 4.3.3) served as the platform for all the statistical computations and visualization of the data in this study. Analyses of the correlations between continuous variables were performed by calculating Spearman’s correlation coefficients. Categorical data were analyzed using the chi-square test for comparisons between two groups, whereas continuous variables were assessed using either the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or Student’s t test, depending on the distribution of the data. Comparisons among three or more groups were analyzed with the Kruskal‒Wallis H test. For experimental designs involving multiple factors, two-way ANOVA was employed to model and evaluate the effects of interactions. Unless specified otherwise, the default settings were used for all R packages. A two-sided p value of less than 0.05 was defined as the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

Identification of an HCNV tumor cell subpopulation associated with malignant progression at the single-cell level

In this study, a systematic analysis of bladder cancer and normal tissues was performed using scRNA-seq. Following quality control and batch effect adjustment, the downstream analysis was performed using the default workflow in the Seurat package (v4.4.0). UMAP dimensionality reduction and clustering revealed a total of 14 cell subpopulations (0–13) (Fig. 1A). These subpopulations were classified according to the expression of established marker genes and epithelial cells, T/NK cells, myeloid cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, B cells, mast cells, plasma cells, and proliferative cells were ultimately identified. The Figure Simultaneously displays the information on the tumor/normal tissue origin and cell cycle status of the cells (Fig. 1B). To validate the annotation results, a dot plot was constructed to show the expression patterns of characteristic genes for each cell type and their expression levels across cell subpopulations (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, representative marker genes were selected, and their expression across different cell groups was visually presented as expression density distributions in UMAP space (Fig. 1D). We integrated multiple public datasets (including PRJNA662018, Horowitz, GSE222315, GSE211388, GSE135337, and GSE130001) as discovery cohorts to further investigate systematic changes in the TME. The UMAP plot of the integrated data is shown in Fig. S1A. A comparison of cellular composition between tumor and normal tissue samples revealed marked shifts in immune and stromal cell populations within the TME (Fig. 1E). We quantified these changes, which revealed the differences in the proportions myeloid cells, epithelial cells, fibroblasts, T/NK cells, endothelial cells, B cells, mast cells, and plasma cells between tumor and normal tissues, thereby more comprehensively revealing the remodeling of the cellular composition of the TME (Fig. 1F). Based on this analysis of the bladder cancer single-cell landscape, an analysis of the cellular composition integrating multicohort data revealed that the proportion of epithelial cells was markedly increased in tumor tissues compared with normal tissues (Fig. 1E, F).

Additionally, given that bladder cancer is an epithelial-origin tumor, its malignant progression is closely related to changes in the heterogeneity and abundance of epithelial cells [28]. Therefore, for an in-depth analysis of the intrinsic cellular features driving tumorigenesis and progression, we subsequently focused on the epithelial cell population to systematically analyze its CNV landscape and associated biological significance. First, based on non-malignant reference cells—including immune cells, stromal cells, and normal epithelial cells annotated from the same sample—the inferCNV (v1.8.1) algorithm was applied to infer CNVs in epithelial cells and identify malignant cells, which allowed us to classify and annotate all cells (Fig. S2A) and calculate the total CNV score for each cell type (Fig. S2B). This score showed a heterogeneous distribution in the UMAP plot of epithelial cells (Figs. 2A, S2C). Based on CNV levels, malignant epithelial cells were categorized into three subgroups: LCNV, MCNV, and HCNV. This classification showed a clear distribution pattern in the dimensionality reduction space (Fig. 2B), indicating significant transcriptomic differences among cells with different CNV levels. We explored the pseudotemporal ordering of tumor cells using Monocle2 (v2.22.0) to chart the transcriptional continuum of HCNV, MCNV, and LCNV cells (Figs. 2C, S2D). The changes in the coordinates of HCNV cells along Component 1 and Component 2 were more pronounced than those in LCNV and MCNV cells, suggesting that their transcriptional state exhibits greater heterogeneity across the pseudotime axis (Fig. S2E, colored by CNV level; Fig. S2F, colored by sample type). A dynamic analysis of gene expression along the pseudotemporal axis revealed differential expression patterns of key genes across the CNV spectrum. The enrichment analysis of these genes revealed their significant involvement in tumor-related biological processes, including epithelial cell proliferation and growth regulation (Fig. 2D). Considering the heterogeneity of tissue origin in the single-cell data, we further employed a Ro/e analysis to assess the distribution of cell subpopulations defined by different CNVs across anatomical origins (normal tissue vs. tumor tissue). The Ro/e analysis (Fig. 2E) showed that the HCNV cell population was significantly enriched in bladder cancer tissues, whereas the OR analysis (Fig. 2F) statistically confirmed the distribution of HCNV in tumor tissues (OR > 1, p < 0.05). Together, these analyses consistently demonstrated that cells with an HCNV burden are markedly enriched in bladder cancer tissues, suggesting a significant association between CNV accumulation and bladder cancer progression at the cellular population distribution level.

The TME is critically involved in tumor initiation, progression, and therapeutic resistance [29]. We analyzed the molecular functional characteristics and intercellular communication networks of bladder cancer cells in depth to further elucidate how these cells with different CNV levels shape and adapt to the microenvironment. An evaluation of key cancer-associated signaling pathway activities in tumor cells with different CNV levels showed that HCNV cells were significantly enriched in multiple pathways closely related to tumor progression, therapeutic resistance, and a poor prognosis, such as the TGF-β/JAK–STAT, PI3K, and VEGF pathways. In contrast, LCNV cells generally exhibited lower activity of these pathways and were enriched in tumor suppressor pathways such as p53 signaling (Fig. 2G). Furthermore, the HCNV group was significantly enriched in pathways driving cell proliferation, anabolism, and growth (e.g., DNA replication, cell cycle, and ether lipid metabolism), indicating active anabolic and proliferation-driven features. Conversely, several classical tumor suppressor pathways (e.g., efferocytosis, FoxO signaling, p53 signaling, cellular senescence, and the Hippo signaling pathway) were notably enriched in the LCNV group (Fig. 2H). These findings suggest that higher CNV levels are significantly associated with the widespread activation of oncogenic signaling pathways, suggesting that HCNV cells possess a greater degree of malignancy. The analysis of cell‒cell interactions revealed that HCNV cells had both a greater number and stronger intensity of interactions with other cell types (e.g., fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and immune cells) (Fig. 2I), further supporting the stronger capacity of HCNV cells to regulate the tumor microenvironment [30]. Figure S3A presents a panoramic network view of outgoing signals from each cell type to other cell types based on the analysis of ligand‒receptor pairs, from which HCNV-specific outgoing and incoming signal patterns were identified (Fig. 2J). Specifically, compared with LCNV cells, HCNV cells, which act as signal senders (ligands), exhibited significantly enhanced interactions with CAFs, M2-type macrophages, and endothelial cells (Fig. 2K). Moreover, as signal receivers (receptors), HCNV cells were more active in receiving signals from immunosuppressive cells such as CAFs, regulatory T cells (Tregs), and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) (Fig. 2L). A comprehensive evaluation of the strength of signals exchanged across cell types revealed that HCNV cells were the most active in both sending and receiving signals (Fig. S3B). The analysis of metabolic pathways showed that compared with LCNV cells, HCNV cells exhibited greater enrichment in anabolic pathways and efficient energy production pathways for amino acids, nucleotides, and fatty acids, supporting their rapid proliferation and adaptation. Moreover, metabolites (e.g., kynurenine) can suppress immunity, driving a highly malignant phenotype [31] (Fig. S3C).

In summary, high-CNV tumor cells not only display stronger intrinsic signaling pathway activity but also engage in more frequent and closer communication with the TME, collectively shaping their highly malignant phenotype.

We conducted systematic gene expression and functional analyses to further elucidate the molecular features and clinical significance of HCNV cells. A Disease Ontology (DO) enrichment analysis of these core genes revealed that they were not only highly related to bladder cancer but also significantly associated with various solid tumors, such as lung, pancreatic, and breast cancers (Fig. 4C), suggesting that the HCNV-associated gene program may represent a universal malignant feature across cancer types. We constructed an 'HCNV activity' score based on these characteristic genes. This score was used as a molecular anchor to identify pathological features reflective of the HCNV phenotype for subsequent model building. Validation in tissue samples demonstrated markedly increased HCNV activity in tumor samples compared with normal tissue samples (Fig. 4D). A correlation analysis with the clinical stage showed that HCNV activity was positively correlated with clinical stage, with patients with stage IV disease having significantly higher HCNV activity than patients with stage I+II and III disease (Fig. 4E). The analysis of correlations with clinicopathological features showed that HCNV activity was positively correlated with the lymph node metastasis status (N stage), with patients with an N2+N3 status having significantly higher HCNV activity than patients with an N0 status (Fig. 4F). In TCGA BLCA cohort, OS was significantly shorter among patients who exhibited high HCNV activity (Fig. 4G). The spatial transcriptomic analysis revealed a heterogeneous distribution of HCNV activity within tumor tissue (Fig. 4H). The single-cell analysis indicated that epithelial cells (especially malignant epithelial cells) had the highest HCNV activity, while immune and stromal cells in the TME also exhibited varying degrees of activity (Fig. 4I), and this activity was significantly enriched in tumor epithelial cells (Fig. 4J, K). Finally, we validated the general upregulation of HCNV activity in tumor tissues across multiple independent scRNA-seq datasets (GSE222315, GSE135337, PRJNA662018, and the integrated cohort) (Fig. 4L).

To further substantiate the genomic basis of HCNV activity with orthogonal DNA-level evidence, we leveraged bulk copy-number alteration (CNA) profiles from the TCGA-BLCA cohort. Patients classified as having high HCNV activity displayed a significantly elevated fraction of genome altered (FGA) compared with those in the low-activity group (Fig. S4A). Moreover, both the fraction of genome gained (FGG) and the fraction of genome lost (FGL) were concurrently increased in the high-HCNV-activity group (Fig. S4B). These results demonstrate that the transcriptome-derived HCNV activity score faithfully mirrors underlying chromosomal instability at the DNA level, thereby providing robust orthogonal validation for the single-cell inferCNV-based HCNV classification.

In summary, HCNV activity is a stable molecular feature with prognostic value. Its universality across cell types and datasets underscores its core role in the malignant progression of bladder cancer.

Construction of a multimodal AI pathology model for prognostic prediction

We developed a prognostic risk model based on HCNV-associated pathological features. A multimodal AI strategy integrating deep learning and image analysis extracted complementary information from digital pathology images. This model predicts patient survival. HCNV activity served as a biological anchor to screen for pathological features correlated with the HCNV phenotype. Our analytical framework is depicted in Fig. 5A. First, the CellProfiler image analysis and ResNet-50 deep learning methods were integrated to quantitatively extract morphological and deep features from WSIs, and the associations of these features with the transcriptomically defined HCNV activity score were assessed. A total of 730 imaging features were significantly correlated with the HCNV score, including 144 pathological features extracted by CellProfiler and 586 deep learning features extracted by ResNet-50 (Supplementary Table S8). By performing a univariate Cox regression analysis of all the imaging features, we screened 20 features that were significantly associated with the patient prognosis (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Table S9). We systematically compared the performance of 101 machine learning algorithms (including RSF, CoxNet, and StepCox) to construct the optimal prognostic prediction model. The clinical profiles of patient samples within the training and validation sets are outlined in Supplementary Table S10. The final selection of the modeling strategy was based on its superior average C-index performance in the validation set (Fig. 5C). A risk score model was developed in TCGA training set using the selected features and the optimal algorithm. This model yielded a clear stratification of patients into high- and low-risk categories, with a significant difference in survival (log-rank p < 0.0001) (Fig. 5D) and good discrimination in 1–5-year survival predictions (AUC 0.67–0.78) (Fig. 5E). In the independent TCGA validation set, the survival outcomes of the high-risk group were significantly worse (log-rank p = 0.032) (Fig. 5F), confirming the prognostic validity of the model. The consistency of the predictions was further supported by the time-dependent ROC curves (Fig. 5G). To validate generalizability beyond TCGA, we prospectively collected an independent in-house cohort (ZZU-BLCA, n = 58) from patients who underwent radical cystectomy between 2021 and 2023. Clinical characteristics are summarized in Supplementary Table S11, and representative H&E whole-slide images are shown in Fig. S5A. The risk score model significantly stratified OS (log-rank p = 0.00076) (Fig. 5H), with time-dependent ROC curves demonstrating predictive accuracy across 1–5 years (Fig. 5I). Importantly, the image-derived risk score showed a significant but modest correlation with transcriptomic HCNV activity (Spearman R = 0.308, p = 0.0359) (Fig. 5J).

Moreover, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses demonstrated that the imaging-based risk score was significantly associated with overall survival (univariate HR = 2.14, 95% CI 1.61–2.84, p < 0.001; multivariate HR = 2.18, 95% CI 1.58–3.03, p < 0.001) and remained an independent prognostic factor beyond conventional clinicopathological variables (Fig. S5B–C). We combined this score with key clinical parameters (e.g., TNM stage) to construct a visual nomogram for predicting patient 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year OS rates to promote its clinical translation (Fig. S5D). The model displayed good discrimination (Fig. S5E) and calibration (Fig. S5F), and the predicted survival rates were highly concordant with the actual observed survival rates (Fig. S5F). Decision curve analysis further indicated that using this model for clinical decision-making provides a significant net benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities (Fig. S5G). A correlation network diagram was constructed to reveal the patterns of associations between all HCNV characteristic genes and the key pathological imaging features used for modeling and to parse the biological basis of model construction (Fig. S5H). In summary, we successfully established and validated an imaging-based prognostic biomarker built from HCNV-anchored pathological features in routine sections, laying the foundation for its clinical translational application.

To enhance model interpretability and delineate the biological basis of the multimodal AI predictions, we conducted comprehensive SHAP and Grad-CAM analyses. Ranking by mean absolute SHAP values identified the six most influential features: five deep learning features (ResNet129, ResNet393, ResNet187, ResNet28, and ResNet242) and one traditional CellProfiler feature (Total_Intensity_PercentMaximal_OrigGray) (Fig. S6A). The SHAP summary plot revealed distinct feature distributions across patients, showing that elevated ResNet129, ResNet393, and ResNet187 values were predominantly associated with increased risk scores, whereas higher ResNet242 and Total_Intensity_PercentMaximal_OrigGray values correlated with reduced risk (Fig. S6B). SHAP dependence plots further revealed the relationship between feature values and their SHAP contributions for these six features, uncovering potential interactions with other covariates, indicating that the model integrates complementary morphological information from deep learning-based representations and classical cellular metrics (Fig. S6C). At the individual patient level, SHAP decision plots for three representative high-risk and three low-risk patients demonstrated that cumulative positive contributions from ResNet129 and ResNet393 drove high-risk predictions, while negative contributions from ResNet187, ResNet129, ResNet393, and ResNet28 underpinned low-risk classifications (Fig. S6D). Finally, Grad-CAM heatmap overlays on representative H&E patches localized the morphological regions driving model decisions: high-risk cases exhibited prominent activation in areas with dense, pleomorphic nuclei and disorganized tissue architecture, whereas low-risk cases showed activation in regions with organized urothelial architecture and uniform nuclear morphology (Fig. S6E). Collectively, these interpretability analyses confirm that the model predictions are grounded in biologically meaningful histopathological patterns rather than opaque black-box computations, thereby facilitating clinical trust and translational application.

Clinical translational potential of the risk score model: predicting the treatment response and the immune microenvironment

We assessed the clinical relevance of the established prognostic model by investigating its ability to predict the bladder cancer treatment response. A drug sensitivity analysis was conducted using the oncoPredict R package (v1.2). The predicted IC50 values for gemcitabine (p = 0.0005) and mitoxantrone (p = 0.0162) were significantly higher in tumors with high-risk scores, suggesting that this score may be associated with resistance to these treatments (Fig. 6A, B). Additionally, the high-risk score was significantly correlated with sensitivity to certain targeted drugs. An analysis of the Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP) database revealed a negative correlation between high-risk scores and sensitivity to two compounds: the PI3K/mTOR inhibitor PI-103 and BRD9647 (Figs. 6C, S7A). A screen of the Profiling Relative Inhibition Simultaneously in Mixtures (PRISM) database further revealed four candidate compounds that were significantly negatively correlated with a high-risk score, namely, teniposide, atorvastatin, mitoxantrone, and anguidine (Figs. 6D, S7B). These computational predictions suggest that a high-risk score may identify tumors with a characteristic drug response signature, representing a hypothesis-generating biomarker for personalized therapy.

We investigated the relationships between the risk score and both the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) and immunotherapy response. We employed the IOBR R package to evaluate immune-related signatures and cell infiltration patterns (Fig. 6E, F). Immune exclusion profiling indicated that a high-risk score was associated with a pronounced enrichment of immunosuppressive components, such as CAFs and M2-polarized macrophages (Fig. 6E). A more granular analysis of immune cell subsets further revealed that the high-risk group exhibited increased levels of immunosuppressive cell types, including regulatory T cells (Tregs), M2 macrophages, specific myofibroblastic CAFs (e.g., ecm_myCAFs), and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and a relative decrease in the number of cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cells (Teffs) (Fig. 6F). To cross-validate immune cell proportions, we performed integrated deconvolution using multiple algorithms including EPIC, MCPcounter, Quantiseq, TIMER, and ssGSEA (Fig. S7C).

We subsequently evaluated the potential influence of the TIME on immunotherapy efficacy. Computational profiling using the SubMap algorithm suggested that patients in the low-risk group may be more likely to respond to anti-PD-1 therapy, whereas those in the high-risk group had a reduced probability of a clinical benefit (Fig. 6G). An analysis of pathological sections visually demonstrated that tumor tissues with high-risk scores had markedly reduced lymphocyte infiltration (the “absent” pattern), whereas tumors with low risk scores showed abundant lymphocyte infiltration (the “lymphocyte” pattern) (Fig. 6H). Finally, we further mapped the bladder cancer immune checkpoint interaction landscape and analyzed its relationships with the risk score, gene expression, methylation, and CNV (Fig. 6I).

In summary, a high-risk score is associated with a “cold” TME dominated by immune exclusion and suppression. These findings generate the hypothesis that the risk score may serve as a biomarker for predicting immunotherapy response, pending validation in ICB-treated cohorts.

Among the top 10 genes most positively correlated with the risk score, RTN3 exhibited the strongest correlation (Fig. 7B). To adjudicate between these two candidates, we performed parallel clinicopathological analyses of DPM3 (Fig. S8).

Although DPM3 was upregulated in tumor tissues compared with normal tissues (Fig. S8A, B), its high expression was paradoxically associated with favorable OS and disease-specific survival (Fig. S8D–E), indicating that its tumor elevation is a concomitant phenomenon rather than a driver of malignant progression [32]. Moreover, DPM3 showed no significant correlation with T stage or overall clinical stage, and only a weak association with N stage (Fig. S8F–I). These findings suggest that DPM3 represents a “passenger” gene, whereas RTN3—which exhibited comprehensive prognostic consistency and significant positive correlations with all major clinicopathological stages (T, N, M, and clinical stage)—fulfills the criteria of a bona fide driver gene. Therefore, we selected RTN3 as the definitive hub gene.

Analyses of TCGA-BLCA data confirmed that RTN3 expression was significantly elevated in bladder cancer tissues in both unpaired and paired comparisons (Fig. 7C, D). Correlation network analysis revealed that RTN3 occupied a central node linking pathological imaging features with HCNV characteristic genes (Fig. 7E). Clinically, high RTN3 expression was significantly associated with shorter OS, disease-specific survival, and progression-free survival (Fig. 7F–H), and was positively correlated with advanced T stage, N stage, M stage, and overall clinical stage (Fig. 7I–L).

To independently validate the enrichment of RTN3 in HCNV cells and its association with pathological features, we performed correlation network analysis and external single-cell validation. Correlation analysis revealed significant associations between key pathological imaging features and HCNV characteristic genes, with RTN3 occupying a central node (Fig. S9A, B). We then analyzed an external single-cell RNA-seq cohort (GSE277524) comprising primary and recurrent bladder cancer samples. UMAP visualization confirmed distinct cell populations by sample source, tissue type, and annotated cell types (Fig. S9C–E), and InferCNV analysis successfully classified epithelial cells into LCNV, MCNV, and HCNV subgroups (Fig. S9F). In this independent validation cohort, RTN3 was significantly enriched in HCNV cells and exhibited excellent discriminatory power for HCNV versus LCNV (AUC = 0.893; Fig. S9G–H). Consistently, in the integrated discovery cohort, RTN3 also showed marked HCNV enrichment with strong diagnostic accuracy (AUC = 0.878; Fig. S9I–J). This HCNV-specific enrichment was reproducible across all six individual discovery datasets, with robust diagnostic performance confirmed by corresponding ROC curves (Fig. S9K–L).

To further functionally validate RTN3, we established stable knockdown (shRTN3) and overexpression (RTN3-OE) cell lines in T24 and UMUC3 cells, confirming efficient knockdown and overexpression at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 7M–P). Given the bioinformatics evidence that HCNV cells actively shape an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, we investigated whether RTN3 functionally contributes to this phenotype. CM from RTN3-overexpressing cells significantly suppressed IFN-γ and IL-2 secretion from co-cultured T cells (Fig. 7Q, R), whereas CM from RTN3-knockdown cells markedly enhanced T-cell cytokine production (Fig. 7S, T), indicating that tumor-intrinsic RTN3 directly impairs T-cell effector function.

We further examined whether RTN3 influences immunosuppressive stromal and myeloid cells using both loss-of-function and gain-of-function approaches across both cell lines. Macrophages treated with CM from RTN3-knockdown T24 cells exhibited reduced protein and mRNA levels of M2-polarization markers (CD206, CD163, and Arg1) (Fig. 7U, W), and these findings were reproducible in the UMUC3 system (Fig. S10A–B). Conversely, CM from RTN3-overexpressing T24 and UMUC3 cells significantly upregulated M2-polarization markers in macrophages at both protein and mRNA levels (Fig. S10E–H). Similarly, fibroblasts exposed to CM from RTN3-knockdown T24 cells showed downregulated expression of CAFs activation markers (α-SMA and FAP) at both protein and mRNA levels (Fig. 7V, X), and consistent results were obtained in the UMUC3 knockdown system (Fig. S10C, D). In contrast, CM from RTN3-overexpressing T24 and UMUC3 cells markedly enhanced CAF activation markers in fibroblasts (Fig. S10I–L). Collectively, these bidirectional and cross-cell-line findings establish that RTN3 is not merely a marker but a functional driver of the HCNV-associated malignant phenotype, simultaneously orchestrating an immunosuppressive microenvironment by inhibiting T-cell function, facilitating M2 macrophage polarization, and promoting CAF activation.

To functionally validate RTN3 as a driver of the HCNV malignant phenotype, we established stable knockdown and overexpression cell lines in both T24 and UMUC3 cells. Bidirectional cell function assays demonstrated that RTN3 knockdown significantly inhibited proliferation, colony formation, migration, and invasion, whereas RTN3 overexpression markedly enhanced these malignant phenotypes (Fig. S11). To exclude off-target effects and establish causality, we performed rescue experiments by reintroducing RTN3 into shRTN3-knockdown cells; the re-expression of RTN3 successfully reversed the inhibitory effects on all malignant phenotypes (Fig. S12). Collectively, these loss-of-function, gain-of-function, and rescue experiments confirm that RTN3 is not merely a correlative marker but a bona fide driver of bladder cancer malignant progression.

RTN3 mediates resistance to gemcitabine chemotherapy in bladder cancer cells

Based on the results described above, we found that a high-risk score could predict bladder cancer cell resistance to gemcitabine (Fig. 6), and gemcitabine is a first-line chemotherapy drug used to treat bladder cancer in the clinic. Concurrently, RTN3 was identified as the hub gene driving the malignant phenotype associated with a high-risk score (Fig. 7). Therefore, we hypothesized that RTN3 might directly mediate gemcitabine resistance in bladder cancer. We tested this hypothesis by conducting functional studies at the cellular level. First, we successfully constructed the gemcitabine-resistant cell lines T24-R and UMUC3-R through long-term treatment with gradient concentrations of gemcitabine. Their IC50s were significantly higher than those of their respective parental cells (Fig. 8A, B). Notably, RTN3 protein expression was progressively increased in the gemcitabine-resistant cell lines after treatment with different concentrations of gemcitabine (Fig. 8C). Given that endogenous RTN3 was markedly upregulated in resistant cells, we next performed ectopic overexpression of RTN3 in parental cells for subsequent studies. We knocked down RTN3 in resistant cells to validate its function in maintaining the resistant phenotype (Fig. 8D) and found that RTN3 significantly reduced the IC50 value of gemcitabine, partially reversing resistance (Fig. 8E, F). Further combination treatment experiments showed that compared with treatment with gemcitabine alone or RTN3 knockdown alone, the combination of both more effectively reduced the viability (Fig. 8G, H) and clonogenic ability (Fig. 8I) of the resistant cells.

To further investigate whether elevated RTN3 promotes chemoresistance, we overexpressed RTN3 in parental T24 and UMUC3 cells. RTN3 overexpression significantly increased the IC50 values for gemcitabine compared with vector controls (Fig. S13A, B). To further validate the dependence of the resistant phenotype on RTN3, we performed rescue experiments in the resistant background: RTN3 knockdown inhibited proliferation and colony formation in T24-R and UMUC3-R cells, whereas re-expression of RTN3 successfully rescued these phenotypes (Fig. S13C–F).

These results indicate that high RTN3 expression is an important factor that enables bladder cancer cells to acquire and maintain gemcitabine resistance. Thus, targeting RTN3 may become a potential strategy to reverse chemotherapy resistance.

We further validated the synergistic therapeutic effects of RTN3 knockdown and gemcitabine in vivo by establishing a mouse subcutaneous xenograft model using T24-R cells. Compared with the control treatment, RTN3 knockdown alone partially inhibited tumor growth, whereas the combination treatment (RTN3 knockdown + gemcitabine) inhibited tumor growth to the greatest extent (Fig. 9A, C). The body weights of the mice in all the groups remained stable, indicating that the treatments did not cause significant toxicity (Fig. 9B). Endpoint tumor weight statistics further confirmed the most significant effect of the combination treatment (Fig. 9D). Immunohistochemical staining indicated that the combination treatment most effectively induced tumor cell apoptosis (highest caspase−3 expression, Fig. 9E, F) and inhibited proliferation (lowest Ki67 expression, Fig. 9E, G). These results confirm that targeting RTN3 in vivo can effectively increase the sensitivity of resistant bladder cancer cells to gemcitabine, and the two treatments clearly exert a synergistic effect on inhibiting tumor growth, providing a strong rationale for clinical combination therapy.

RTN3 drives gemcitabine resistance via JAK2/STAT3-mediated glycolytic reprogramming

We further elucidated the metabolic mechanism by which RTN3 mediates chemotherapy resistance, and the gene set enrichment analysis revealed significantly increased activity of the glycolytic pathway in cells with high RTN3 expression (Fig. 10A). Therefore, we focused our investigation on a key feature of tumor metabolic reprogramming—glycolysis [33]. At the molecular level, RTN3 knockdown in gemcitabine-resistant cells led to the downregulation of key glycolytic proteins PKM2, GLUT1, and LDHA (Fig. 10B), with concordant reductions in mRNA levels (Fig. S14A, B). Conversely, RTN3 overexpression in parental cells markedly upregulated these enzymes at both protein and mRNA levels (Fig. S14C–F). Furthermore, in the resistant background, rescue experiments showed that re-expression of RTN3 restored the expression of PKM2, GLUT1, and LDHA that had been suppressed by knockdown (Fig. S14G, H).

Functionally, RTN3 knockdown significantly reduced lactate production and glucose uptake in resistant cells under gemcitabine pressure (Fig. 10C, D). These metabolic alterations were partially reversed by RTN3 rescue (Fig. S14I–L). We verified the necessity of glycolytic activity in RTN3-regulated resistance by administering the glycolysis inhibitor oligomycin to RTN3-knockdown resistant cells. The results showed that oligomycin treatment partially reversed the increase in gemcitabine sensitivity caused by RTN3 knockdown, which specifically manifested as a recovery of cell proliferation (Fig. 10E–F) and colony formation (Fig. 10G). These results reveal that RTN3 drives glycolytic metabolic flux in bladder cancer cells by maintaining the high expression and activity of key glycolytic proteins, thereby supporting their survival and proliferation under chemotherapy pressure to ultimately lead to gemcitabine resistance.

We established a subcutaneous xenograft model in mice using T24-R cells to further validate the necessity of glycolytic metabolism in RTN3-mediated gemcitabine resistance in vivo. RTN3 knockdown significantly inhibited tumor growth, whereas the combination of RTN3 knockdown and oligomycin treatment partially reversed the tumor growth inhibition caused by RTN3 knockdown (Fig. 11A, C). The body weights of the mice in all the treatment groups remained stable, indicating that the treatments were not significantly toxic (Fig. 11B). Endpoint tumor weight statistics also supported this trend (Fig. 11D). Immunohistochemical staining further showed that oligomycin treatment partially antagonized the increase in apoptosis (relatively lower Caspase-3 expression; Fig. 11E, F) and alleviated the inhibition of proliferation (relative recovery of Ki67 expression; Fig. 11E, G) resulting from RTN3 knockdown. The results of these in vivo experiments indicate that inhibiting glycolysis can reverse the increase in gemcitabine efficacy due to RTN3 loss, thereby establishing that at the whole-animal level, glycolytic metabolism is the key downstream effector pathway through which RTN3 drives chemotherapy resistance in bladder cancer.

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which RTN3 regulates glycolytic enzyme expression, we sought to identify the upstream signaling hub responsible for this transcriptional control. Our single-cell bioinformatic analysis revealed that the JAK2/STAT3 pathway is significantly activated in HCNV cells (Fig. 2G), suggesting a potential link between this axis and the HCNV-associated metabolic phenotype. Moreover, previous studies have established STAT3 as a key transcription factor that directly binds to the promoter regions of glycolytic genes—including PKM2, GLUT1, and LDHA—to drive their expression [34–36]. Therefore, we hypothesized that RTN3 might promote glycolytic reprogramming by activating the JAK2/STAT3 pathway.

We first confirmed that RTN3 overexpression in parental T24 and UMUC3 cells markedly increased the phosphorylation levels of JAK and STAT3, without altering their total protein expression (Fig. 12A, B). Conversely, RTN3 knockdown in gemcitabine-resistant T24-R and UMUC3-R cells significantly reduced p-JAK and p-STAT3 levels (Fig. 12C, D), establishing RTN3 as an upstream activator of JAK2/STAT3 signaling.

We next determined whether this activation is the causal mediator linking RTN3 to glycolytic reprogramming. In RTN3-overexpressing T24 and UMUC3 cells, treatment with the selective STAT3 inhibitor Stattic abolished the upregulation of PKM2, GLUT1, and LDHA at both protein and mRNA levels (Fig. 12E–H). To confirm this axis in the resistant background, we introduced a constitutively active STAT3 construct (STAT3-C) into RTN3-knockdown T24-R and UMUC3-R cells. Forced activation of STAT3 successfully rescued the expression of PKM2, GLUT1, and LDHA that had been suppressed by RTN3 knockdown (Fig. 12I–L). Functionally, Stattic treatment also reversed the RTN3-overexpression-induced increase in colony formation (Fig. 12M, N) and cell proliferation (Fig. 12O, P). In summary, we have established a clear mechanistic chain: RTN3 activates the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway, which transcriptionally upregulates PKM2, GLUT1, and LDHA, thereby driving glycolytic metabolic reprogramming and ultimately leading to gemcitabine resistance in bladder cancer.

Discussion

Through the integration of multiomics data and systematic functional experiments, a multidimensional overview of CNV heterogeneity in bladder cancer was systematically delineated in this study. We discovered that HCNV tumor cells constitute a malignant subpopulation with a unique molecular phenotype. This subpopulation not only is associated with widespread activation of oncogenic pathways, active remodeling of the TME, and immunosuppressive features but also significantly correlates with a poor clinical prognosis. Building on these findings, we used transcriptomic HCNV activity as a biological anchor to select HCNV-associated pathological features from routine sections and constructed a multimodal AI prognostic model. This model provides a generalizable tool for the noninvasive prognostic stratification of patients with this molecular subtype. Further analysis employed six independent feature selection algorithms for joint screening of candidate genes. Among these, RTN3 was identified as a core hub molecule driving the HCNV-associated malignant phenotype. Mechanistically, RTN3 mediates gemcitabine resistance by activating the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway and subsequently upregulating key glycolytic enzymes. Moreover, through bidirectional genetic manipulation and in vitro functional assays, we found that RTN3-overexpressing tumor cells can simultaneously suppress T-cell function, promote M2 macrophage polarization, and activate CAFs in a paracrine manner, suggesting an important role for RTN3 in orchestrating the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. The multimodal AI model constructed in this study was validated for its robustness in an independent in-house cohort (ZZU-BLCA). This study organically links genomic instability, transcriptional reprogramming, metabolic adaptation, microenvironment interactions, and clinical outcomes, providing a new framework for a deeper understanding of bladder cancer heterogeneity and treatment resistance.

Our findings directly link genomic instability in bladder cancer to observable molecular and functional phenotypes. The widespread activation of key pathways such as DNA replication and TGF-β, JAK–STAT, PI3K, and VEGF signaling in HCNV tumor cells provides intrinsic drivers for their high proliferation, strong survival, and invasive capabilities [37, 38]. This finding is consistent with those of previous studies of breast and ovarian cancers, where chromosomal instability can directly drive tumor progression by activating oncogenic signaling pathways [39]. More importantly, our intercellular communication analysis predicted that HCNV cells closely interact with immunosuppressive cells, including CAFs and M2-polarized macrophages [40–42]. Cancer-associated fibroblasts play pivotal roles in cancer development and therapy, contributing to stromal remodeling and therapeutic resistance [43, 44], while tissue-resident macrophages serve critical functions in both health and cancer, influencing tumor progression and immune evasion [45]. To test whether HCNV cells directly remodel the immune microenvironment through their core driver genes, we performed functional studies targeting RTN3. The results showed that CM from RTN3-knockdown tumor cells significantly enhanced the secretion of IFN-γ and IL-2 by T cells, suppressed M2 macrophage polarization, and reduced CAF activation markers. Conversely, RTN3 overexpression produced the opposite effects. These findings functionally establish RTN3 as a molecular executor of a "triple immunosuppressive hit" exerted by HCNV cells—simultaneously suppressing adaptive immunity, remodeling myeloid cells, and activating stromal fibroblasts.

RTN3 significantly enhances glycolytic flux (Warburg effect) in tumor cells by upregulating the expression of key proteins such as PKM2, GLUT1, and LDHA [46, 47]. Glycolysis provides energy and biosynthetic precursors for rapid proliferation, and its metabolites (e.g., lactate) can further acidify the microenvironment, inhibit immune cell function, and promote therapeutic resistance [48, 49]. The metabolic dialogue within the tumor microenvironment, from immune suppression to precision cancer therapies, underscores how metabolic reprogramming fundamentally alters drug sensitivity and therapeutic responses [50]. Mechanistically, RTN3 hyperactivates the JAK2/STAT3 cascade to transcriptionally upregulate key glycolytic factors (GLUT1, PKM2, and LDHA). Bidirectional rescue experiments confirmed the necessity of this pathway: STAT3 inhibition via Stattic blocked metabolic reprogramming and restored chemosensitivity, whereas forced STAT3 activation (STAT3-C) or compensatory glycolysis (oligomycin) in RTN3-knockdown cells effectively re-induced the chemoresistant phenotype. This clearly establishes the “RTN3–JAK2/STAT3–glycolysis” axis as a primary driver of gemcitabine resistance. Building upon the established transcriptional role of STAT3 on these metabolic genes, our study identifies RTN3 as the definitive upstream “switch” of this network. This finding successfully bridges genomic heterogeneity with metabolic plasticity, providing a robust rationale for targeting the RTN3/JAK2/STAT3 axis to overcome clinical chemoresistance.

In terms of translational medicine, the digital pathological prediction model based on multimodal AI that we constructed holds significant clinical potential. The core innovation of this model is the use of a multimodal AI framework that integrates abstract morphological patterns extracted by deep learning with cellular morphological features quantified by a traditional image analysis, achieving a more robust characterization of HCNV-associated morphological patterns. Traditionally, a pathological diagnosis relies heavily on the subjective experience of physicians. In recent years, deep learning (especially convolutional neural networks) has enabled the automated extraction of morphological features, realizing objective, quantitative predictions of tumor characteristics [12, 13]. The application of AI in cancer diagnosis has also expanded into other clinical domains. For example, genetic factors, risk prediction, and AI applications have advanced the field of thrombotic diseases [51], while large language models with image analysis capabilities have demonstrated potential in diagnosing osteosarcoma from X-ray images [52]. Our work extends these AI-driven diagnostic advances to the specific context of bladder cancer pathology. Currently, the forefront of this field focuses on two main multimodal AI strategies. The first is cross-scale multiple instance learning, which integrates morphological features at different scales (from nuclei to cells to the tissue architecture) within a single imaging modality, fusing information from different magnification levels through multibranch networks to comprehensively capture complex biological patterns [53]. The second is the deep integration of pathology–multiomics data, which constitutes a typical multimodal AI integration paradigm aiming to correlate and fuse deep pathological features with multidimensional data such as genomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics at the patient level, thereby building more powerful prediction models, revealing the molecular mechanism underlying morphological phenotypes, and achieving “form–function” mapping [15]. Therefore, through a multimodal AI framework, this study both integrated multiscale morphological features and successfully established a “form–function” map between pathological morphology and RTN3 expression.

We acknowledge that spatial transcriptomics revealed intratumoral heterogeneity in HCNV activity. Our pathological model generates a single global risk score per WSI, analogous to bulk transcriptomic profiling that provides an averaged molecular signature. This global score integrates aggregate HCNV-associated morphological patterns across the entire tissue section and remains prognostically informative, as demonstrated by its significant correlation with patient survival in both the TCGA and independent ZZU-BLCA cohorts. Future integration of spatially resolved multimodal AI frameworks that explicitly account for regional HCNV heterogeneity may further refine predictive accuracy and biological interpretation.

This study has several limitations. First, the conclusions are primarily based on retrospective data and preclinical models. The value of RTN3 as a biomarker and therapeutic target needs further validation in prospective clinical trials. Second, these immunotherapy and chemosensitivity predictions are strictly computational and require prospective clinical validation; they are presented here as hypothesis-generating biomarkers rather than definitive clinical conclusions. Computational predictions of immunotherapy response and drug sensitivity require validation in real patient cohorts. Third, single-cell transcriptomic data cannot fully resolve the spatial localization of cellular interactions, necessitating future validation using spatial transcriptomics or multiplex immunofluorescence staining [54]. Fourth, animal models do not fully recapitulate the complex human immune system. The immunosuppressive microenvironment remodeling by RTN3 was demonstrated through in vitro CM assays; in vivo validation in immunocompetent models is warranted. Moreover, the efficacy and safety of combination therapies must be evaluated in models more closely related to humans [55]. Fifth, the public scRNA-seq datasets employed in this study served as discovery and supportive cohorts to construct the HCNV activity signature and establish the HCNV-RTN3 association. They do not constitute rigorous independent validation of the multimodal AI pathological prognostic model, which was exclusively validated in the prospective ZZU-BLCA cohort. Sixth, the ZZU-BLCA external validation cohort (n = 58) was from a single institution, and its relatively modest sample size necessitates further validation in larger, multi-center cohorts.

In the future, we suggest the following experiments: First, the predictive value of HCNV features and RTN3 expression for the treatment response should be prospectively validated in large cohorts of bladder cancer patients receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy or immunotherapy. Second, small-molecule inhibitors or monoclonal antibodies targeting RTN3 should be actively developed, and combination treatment regimens with gemcitabine, immune checkpoint inhibitors, or metabolic inhibitors should be explored. Third, more advanced cross-scale multiple-instance learning frameworks should be developed to optimize our pathological model and explore pathological–genomic–transcriptomic multimodal fusion models to identify the HCNV subtype and predict its biological behavior more accurately. Fourth, the universality of HCNV features in other solid tumors (e.g., lung cancer and gastric cancer) should be explored to determine whether it represents a cross-cancer, common malignant subtype driven by genomic instability.

Conclusions

In summary, this study systematically revealed a malignant bladder cancer subtype defined by HCNV and successfully developed a clinically translatable prognostic model. We further elucidated the core mechanism by which RTN3 drives the progression and resistance of this subtype through the reprogramming of glycolytic metabolism. These findings not only deepen the understanding of bladder cancer heterogeneity but also provide new biomarkers and intervention strategies for achieving its precise diagnosis and targeted treatment.
