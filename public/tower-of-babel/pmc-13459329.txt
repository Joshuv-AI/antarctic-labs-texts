Age-dependent starch–sucrose metabolic transition during bulb development of Fritillaria unibracteata
Authors: Mengyu Li, Hanxiao Zheng, Lie Meng, Jingzhe Pu, Chong Hu, Lingli Chen, Yazhong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe bulb of Fritillaria unibracteata is a valuable traditional Chinese medicinal material whose bioactive compound accumulation is closely linked to carbon metabolism. As a perennial herb, its bulbs undergo multi-year developmental cycles, with starch and sucrose serving as key storage and mobilizable carbohydrates, respectively. However, the age-dependent dynamics of starch–sucrose interconversion during bulb development and its expression patterns remain poorly understood, limiting our comprehension of the carbon allocation dynamics that support biomass accumulation and medicinal metabolite production.ResultsStarch content increased progressively from the first to fourth year, peaked in the fourth year, and then significantly declined in the fifth year. By contrast, sucrose levels exhibited an opposite trend, decreasing in the fourth year and rising sharply in the fifth year, indicating a marked shift in carbohydrate allocation during the late developmental stage. Transcriptome analysis identified 21,964 differentially expressed genes, among which the starch and sucrose metabolic pathway was significantly enriched. Notably, key genes involved in starch biosynthesis, including ADP-glucose pyrophosphorylase and soluble starch synthase/granule-bound starch synthase, showed their highest expression levels in the fourth year, coinciding with maximum starch accumulation. Conversely, the expression of genes associated with sucrose biosynthesis, particularly sucrose phosphate synthase, was significantly upregulated from the fourth to fifth year, corresponding to the observed sucrose increase. Enzyme activity assays mirrored these transcriptional patterns. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis further identified modules associated with starch–sucrose metabolic traits, providing evidence for coordinated transcriptional networks in carbon allocation. Collectively, these findings reveal a pronounced metabolic transition from starch accumulation to sucrose synthesis during bulb development.ConclusionThe transition from the fourth to fifth year represents a critical developmental stage, during which the carbon allocation pattern shifts from starch-dominated storage to enhanced sucrose accumulation and mobilization. Background

Fritillaria unibracteata Hsiao et K. C. Hsia is a perennial herbaceous plant belonging to the family Liliaceae, predominantly distributed in northwestern Sichuan and southeastern Qinghai Provinces, China, where it grows in alpine grasslands at elevations ranging from 3200 to 4500 m [1]. As one of the botanical origins of Fritillariae Cirrhosae Bulbus recorded in the Pharmacopoeia of the People’s Republic of China (2025 edition), its dried bulbs are widely used in traditional medicine [2–4].

Phytochemical studies have shown that the bulbs of F. unibracteata are rich in polysaccharides and other bioactive constituents [5–7], yet wild resource depletion has made artificial cultivation essential for sustainable utilization [8]. Growth age is a critical determinant of yield and quality during cultivation [9, 10]. As the primary underground storage organ, the bulb undergoes pronounced age-dependent morphological differentiation synchronized with enlargement [11], and plays a central role in carbon allocation [12], with starch and sucrose serving as the key storage and transport forms of carbohydrate, respectively [13]. However, systematic investigations integrating developmental morphology, carbohydrate accumulation dynamics, and associated transcriptional patterns in this species remain limited. This study therefore aimed to characterize the age-dependent patterns of starch and sucrose accumulation in F. unibracteata bulbs and to explore the underlying transcriptional coordination through integrated transcriptomic and enzyme activity analyses.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Bulb samples of Fritillaria unibracteata Hsiao et K. C. Hsia were collected from the Shenhe Agricultural Base in Aba Prefecture, Sichuan Province, China (33°31′ N, 100°28′ E; altitude 3466 m). The collection was conducted with permission from the management of the Shenhe Agricultural Base. Plants of five growth ages (1–5 years) were cultivated under uniform field management in the same experimental plot with consistent soil type, natural light conditions, and ambient temperature, ensuring that chronological age was the primary variable. All samples were harvested at a single time point in early July 2025, approximately 120 days after sprouting, during the late vegetative growth stage when aboveground growth had stabilized. This time point was selected to ensure that plants of all ages were at a comparable developmental phase, thereby minimizing confounding effects from seasonal or circannual variations. For each age group, five independent biological replicates were collected, resulting in a total of 25 samples. Voucher specimens have been deposited in the Traditional Chinese Medicine Herbarium of Anhui Provincial Institute for Food and Drug Control (specimen numbers: [AHFD 2025–011 to 025]), and the species identification was confirmed by Mr. Yazhong Zhang, Chief Pharmacist of the institute.

Measurement of morphological parameters

Major morphological parameters of F. unibracteata bulbs at different growth ages were determined. Fresh weight, dry weight, transverse diameter, longitudinal diameter of bulbs, and plant height were measured using an electronic balance and a digital vernier caliper. Fresh weight was measured immediately after harvest using an electronic balance. For dry weight determination, bulb samples were oven-dried at 50 °C for 48 h to constant weight before weighing. Transverse and longitudinal diameters were measured at the widest and longest points of each bulb using a digital vernier caliper. Plant height was measured from the soil surface to the tip of the highest leaf. All measurements were performed using five independent biological replicates per developmental stage. These morphological data were used as a basis for characterizing age-dependent growth traits and subsequently analyzing biomass accumulation and developmental progression.

Dynamic changes in microstructural characteristics

To investigate the relationship between bulb structural development and carbohydrate accumulation, bulbs from 1- to 5-year-old plants were subjected to paraffin sectioning, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) following previously reported methods [14–16].

For paraffin sectioning, bulb tissues were fixed in formalin-aceto-alcohol solution, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and then stained in accordance with standard histological procedures. Sections were observed under a light microscope, and images were captured under identical optical settings to ensure comparability among developmental stages. For the analysis of starch granules, images of the selected fields of view were processed using the HALO image analysis platform (Indica Labs, USA). Automated image segmentation was performed to identify starch granules based on color and morphological characteristics, and the recognized regions were highlighted in red for visualization.

For SEM, the samples were fixed, dehydrated, and dried using standard protocols, then sputter-coated with gold and observed under a scanning electron microscope. For TEM, the samples were processed by routine fixation, dehydration, embedding, and ultrathin sectioning, and subsequently examined under a transmission electron microscope. The long diameter of starch granules in SEM images was measured using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 (Media Cybernetics, USA) based on the calibrated scale bar. All organelles observed in TEM images were annotated to facilitate structural identification. Microstructural observations across all three assays were used to characterize tissue organization and starch granule features at different developmental stages, providing morphological evidence for carbohydrate metabolism analysis during bulb enlargement.

Determination of starch and sucrose contents

To analyze starch accumulation dynamics in F. unibracteata bulbs of different growth ages, 0.5 g of bulb powder (passed through a No. 5 sieve) was accurately weighed. Lipids were removed by Soxhlet extraction with 100 mL of petroleum ether (60–90 °C) under reflux at 85 °C for 6 h. The defatted residue was extracted with 10 mL of 80% ethanol under ultrasonication (50 W, 40 kHz) for 30 min, allowed to cool, and then centrifuged at 3,500 rpm for 5 min. After discarding the supernatant, the extraction was repeated once to completely remove soluble sugars. The resulting residue was mixed with 10 mL of water, heated in a boiling water bath for 15 min, immediately cooled on ice, and centrifuged. The precipitate was washed with water and dehydrated with anhydrous ethanol to obtain gelatinized starch. The gelatinized sample was then sequentially digested with α-amylase at 95 °C for 15 min and amyloglucosidase at 60 °C for 1 h to achieve complete hydrolysis into glucose. The hydrolysate was centrifuged, and the supernatant was transferred to a 50 mL volumetric flask and brought to volume with water. The glucose content derived from starch hydrolysis was quantified using a U3000 high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system equipped with an Alltech ELSD 6000 evaporative light scattering detector (ELSD). Separation was performed on an Alltech Prevail Carbohydrate ES column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm). The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile–water (75:25, v/v) at a flow rate of 1.0 mL·min⁻1. The column temperature was maintained at 35 °C, and the injection volume was 5 μL. ELSD parameters were set as follows: drift tube temperature of 110 °C and nebulizer gas flow rate of 2.5 L·min⁻1 [17].

To simultaneously characterize sucrose accumulation, 0.5 g of bulb powder from the same batch was extracted with 25 mL of 60% ethanol under ultrasonication (50 W, 40 kHz) for 30 min. After cooling, the weight loss was replenished with 60% ethanol, and the mixture was filtered. The filtrate was used for analysis. Sucrose content was determined under the same HPLC conditions as described above [18].

Standard calibration curves were generated using sucrose and D-glucose reference standards. The sucrose standard solution was prepared at a concentration of 0.5058 mg·mL⁻1 (R2 = 0.9995), and the glucose standard solution was prepared at 1.0072 mg·mL⁻1 (R2 = 0.9988). Quantification was performed using the external standard method based on the corresponding calibration curves. Comparative analysis of starch and sucrose contents at different growth ages was conducted to elucidate carbon allocation and transport patterns in developing bulbs.

Transcriptome sequencing and data analysis

To explore the transcriptional patterns associated with carbohydrate metabolism in F. unibracteata bulbs, gene expression was analyzed across different growth ages by transcriptome sequencing. RNA sequencing was conducted by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) using samples collected from the Shenhe Agricultural Base. For each developmental stage, three independent biological replicates were included. Full-length transcriptome sequencing was performed using the SMRT Link Iso-Seq protocol, generating high-quality full-length transcripts for downstream analyses. Paired-end sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq™ platform with an average read length of 150 bp [19]. Raw sequencing data were generated in FASTQ format (.fastq.gz). As F. unibracteata is a non-model species with no publicly available reference genome, the full-length transcripts obtained from Iso-Seq were used as the reference transcriptome for subsequent read mapping and expression level quantification.

Functional annotation of the identified genes was carried out using multiple public databases, including the NCBI non-redundant protein database (NR), nucleotide database (NT), Swiss-Prot, protein family database (Pfam), conserved domain database (CDD), Gene Ontology (GO), and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [20, 21]. Gene annotation against the NR, NT, Swiss-Prot, Pfam, CDD, and GO databases was performed using NCBI BLAST + v2.6.0 [22–24]. KEGG pathway annotation was conducted using KAAS v2.1 (KEGG Automatic Annotation Server), and genes were assigned to metabolic pathways based on KEGG Orthology (KO) classification.

Gene expression levels were quantified using the transcripts per million (TPM) method. TPM represents the proportion of a given transcript in the total RNA pool. Assay of key enzymes involved in carbohydrate metabolism

Based on transcriptomic results, enzyme activity assays were further performed to investigate the expression patterns of carbohydrate metabolism in F. unibracteata bulbs. Activities of key carbohydrate metabolism-related enzymes, including ADP-glucose pyrophosphorylase (AGPase; AKSU029M), granule-bound starch synthase (GBSS; AKSU028M), soluble starch synthase (SSS; AKSU027M), starch branching enzyme (SBE; AKSU032M), sucrose synthase (SS; AKSU021M), β-amylase (BMY; AKSU031M), starch phosphorylase (SP; AKSU082M), sucrose phosphate synthase (SPS; AKSU022M), and debranching enzyme (DBE; AKSU033M), were determined using commercial assay kits (Boxbio, China).

Samples were processed at a ratio of tissue fresh weight (g) to extraction buffer volume (mL) of 1:5–10 (w/v). Tissues were homogenized under ice-cold conditions and then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and stored on ice for subsequent enzyme activity assays. Enzyme activities were determined using a microplate reader in a 200 μL reaction system. Absorbance was recorded at the specified wavelength (e.g., 540 nm) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Enzyme activity was calculated based on a standard curve generated using known concentrations of reducing sugar and expressed as μmol of reducing sugar produced per minute per gram of fresh weight (μmol·min⁻1·g⁻1 FW). One unit (U) of enzyme activity was defined as the amount of enzyme that catalyzes the production of 1 μmol of reducing sugar per minute under the assay conditions.

Statistical analysis

One-way analysis of variance was performed in GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 (GraphPad Software, USA) to evaluate the effects of different growth stages on morphological traits, paraffin section parameters, carbohydrate contents, and metabolic enzyme activities. Post-hoc comparisons between group means were conducted using Tukey’s multiple comparison test. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed using ImageGP to identify core gene modules and candidate genes associated with starch–sucrose metabolic traits. Pearson correlation analysis was performed using SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corp., USA) to evaluate relationships among morphological traits, carbohydrate content, gene expression levels, and enzyme activity. All data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from five biological replicates. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 in all analyses.

Results

Dynamic characteristics of bulb development and carbohydrate accumulation

To characterize age-dependent morphological development and carbohydrate accumulation in F. unibracteata, key morphological parameters were measured in plants aged 1–5 years (Figs. 1 and 2). Plant height, bulb fresh weight, and bulb dry weight increased progressively with growth age (Fig. 2A–C). In addition, bulb enlargement occurred in both transverse and longitudinal dimensions, indicating relatively balanced radial and axial expansion during development (Fig. 2D, E). Despite the continuous increase in biomass, bulb water content did not differ significantly among the different growth ages (Fig. 2F). This observation suggests that the enlargement of F. unibracteata bulbs is primarily attributable to the sustained accumulation of dry matter rather than changes in tissue water status. These phenotypic characteristics provide a quantitative basis for subsequent analyses of starch and sucrose metabolism during bulb development.

Morphological basis of tissue development and starch distribution

To elucidate the cytological basis of bulb enlargement and carbohydrate accumulation, multi-level microscopic observations were integrated to characterize the continuous developmental progression of F. unibracteata bulbs from structural establishment to efficient storage and subsequent mobilization.

Paraffin section analysis revealed pronounced structural changes in bulb tissues with increasing growth age. Bulbs from 1-year-old plants exhibited a relatively loose tissue organization, whereas those from older plants displayed progressively denser cell arrangements with well-developed storage parenchyma cells (Fig. 3A–E). Quantitative analysis further showed that the number of vascular bundles continuously increased from 1- to 5-year-old bulbs (Fig. 3F), which is consistent with enhanced capacity for nutrient transport. By contrast, the cross-sectional area of vascular bundles reached a maximum in the fourth year and slightly declined in the fifth year (Fig. 3G), indicating a shift in developmental emphasis during later stages. Histochemical staining provided direct evidence for dynamic starch accumulation in bulb tissues. The starch-positive area transiently decreased from the first to second year, markedly increased from the second to fourth year, peaked in the fourth year, and subsequently declined in the fifth year (Fig. 3H). This quantitative pattern identifies year four as a critical stage for starch accumulation in F. unibracteata bulbs.

Ultrastructural observations further supported these findings. SEM and TEM analyses showed that the number and density of starch granules substantially increased from the second to fourth year, with parenchyma cells being largely occupied by starch granules. Measurements of starch granule long diameter in SEM images revealed variation across developmental stages, with mean values of 17.24, 22.85, 20.60, 25.06, and 22.70 μm from the first to fifth year, respectively. Statistical analysis of these morphological data is presented in Supplementary Fig. S1. In addition, starch granules were frequently observed in close proximity to energy-related organelles such as mitochondria, suggesting active starch biosynthesis during this period. By contrast, bulbs in the fifth year exhibited a less compact arrangement of starch granules (Figs. 4 and 5), consistent with the initiation of starch degradation and mobilization.

Overall, bulb development in F. unibracteata represents a dynamic process characterized by coordinated structural and functional changes. Early developmental stages are associated with tissue differentiation and efficient starch synthesis and storage, whereas later stages exhibit features consistent with starch breakdown and reutilization. These morphological and cytological observations provide structural evidence for age-dependent shifts in carbon storage and mobilization during bulb development.

Dynamic changes in starch and sucrose contents during bulb development

To investigate the carbon metabolic basis associated with bulb enlargement, the contents of starch and sucrose in F. unibracteata bulbs at different developmental stages were quantitatively analyzed. The results revealed a pronounced stage-dependent and complementary accumulation pattern between starch and sucrose (Fig. 6). Starch content steadily increased from the first to fourth year and peaked in the fourth year, indicating that during this period, photosynthetically derived carbon was predominantly converted into storage starch, concomitant with rapid bulb enlargement. By contrast, starch content significantly decreased in the fifth year, suggesting the initiation of starch mobilization at later developmental stages.

Changes in starch and sucrose contents in Fritillaria unibracteata bulbs at different growth ages. A Starch content. B Sucrose content. Different lowercase letters indicate statistically significant differences among growth ages (P < 0.05, Tukey’s test). Sample size was n = 5 for each growth stage

Conversely, sucrose content declined in the fourth year, consistent with its utilization as a substrate for starch biosynthesis. As starch content decreased in the fifth year, sucrose levels markedly increased, indicating a metabolic shift toward the degradation of starch and regeneration of sucrose as a transportable carbon form. Together, these results demonstrate a coordinated temporal transition between starch accumulation and sucrose availability during bulb development, providing physiological evidence for dynamic carbon allocation in F. unibracteata bulbs.

Transcriptomic differentiation among samples and developmental stage characteristics

To elucidate the transcriptional patterns associated with dynamic changes in carbohydrate metabolism during bulb development, transcriptome sequencing was performed on F. unibracteata bulbs collected from plants aged 1–5 years, with three biological replicates per group. A total of 84.8 Gb of high-quality clean reads was obtained after quality control, with Q30 values exceeding 97% and an average GC content of 51.82% (detailed information is provided in Supplementary Table S1). In total, 194,994 transcripts were annotated across all samples (Fig. 7A), including alternative splice variants, of which 181,715 transcripts (93.19%) were successfully annotated in at least one public database. Detailed annotations and expression levels of all identified transcripts are provided in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3. Annotation coverage was highest in the NR database (92.27%), followed by Swiss-Prot (79.71%), GO (52.17%), and KEGG (34.71%).

To assess gene expression differences among growth ages, clustering analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was conducted, and the results were visualized using a heatmap (Fig. 7B), which clearly distinguished expression patterns among developmental stages. Notably, pronounced transcriptional divergence was observed during the transition from the second to third year (Fig. 7C). Quantitative comparison of DEG numbers among age groups showed that the Y2 vs. Y3 comparison yielded the largest number of DEGs (4023), whereas the Y1 vs. Y2 comparison exhibited the fewest DEGs (928) (Fig. 7D).

Venn diagram analysis revealed a limited overlap of DEGs among different pairwise comparisons, with no common core DEGs shared across all groups (Fig. 7E), indicating a high degree of stage-specific transcriptional dynamics during bulb development. Comparisons involving key developmental stages, such as Y2 vs. Y3 (1631 DEGs) and Y1 vs. Y3 (1522 DEGs), displayed a relatively large number of unique DEGs. The large number of unique DEGs identified in the Y2 vs. Y3 comparison indicates that the transition from the second to third year represents a phase of extensive transcriptional change in bulb development. This phase coincides with rapid bulb enlargement, accelerated starch accumulation, and significant structural differentiation. Consequently, the extensive transcriptional changes observed during this stage likely reflect coordinated shifts in the expression of multiple genes associated with enhanced uptake capacity, tissue development, and reserve accumulation. These stage-specific DEGs are associated with this critical developmental transition. Complete lists of DEGs for each pairwise comparison among developmental years are provided in Supplementary Table S4.

GO enrichment analysis further showed that DEGs were mainly distributed across the categories of biological process, cellular component, and molecular function, with enrichment in terms related to growth and development, cellular structural organization, and metabolic processes (Supplementary Fig. S2). To further explore the involvement of DEGs in carbohydrate metabolism, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed. The starch–sucrose metabolic pathway was significantly enriched (Fig. 8), indicating that dynamic changes in the expression of genes associated with starch and sucrose metabolism are closely associated with age-dependent variations in carbohydrate content during bulb development.

Transcriptional patterns of the starch–sucrose metabolic pathway

The starch–sucrose metabolic pathway represents a central metabolic framework for carbon metabolism in F. unibracteata bulbs, in which directional carbon flux is closely associated with age-dependent accumulation patterns of starch and sucrose. Figure 9 illustrates the core nodes of the starch–sucrose metabolic pathway and the expression profiles of key genes across bulbs from 1- to 5-year-old plants.

Metabolic pathway map of differentially expressed genes involved in starch–sucrose metabolism in Fritillaria unibracteata bulbs. Boxes represent individual genes. Red and blue boxes indicate upregulated and downregulated genes, respectively. Genes associated with the starch biosynthesis pathway include AGPase (ADP-glucose pyrophosphorylase), GBSS (granule-bound starch synthase), SSS (soluble starch synthase), and SBE (starch branching enzyme). Genes involved in the starch degradation pathway include ISA (isoamylase), AMY (α-amylase), and BMY (β-amylase). Genes associated with sucrose synthesis and cleavage pathways include SPS (sucrose phosphate synthase), SUT (sucrose transport protein), TPS (trehalose 6-phosphate synthase), otsB (trehalose 6-phosphate phosphatase), SS (sucrose synthase), INV (invertase), FrK (fructokinase), and HK (hexokinase)

During the rapid starch accumulation phase from the first to fourth year, genes involved in the sucrose cleavage branch exhibited elevated expression levels. In particular, the expression levels of SS and invertase (INV) were significantly upregulated, contributing to the conversion of sucrose into UDP-glucose and hexoses, thereby providing substrates for starch biosynthesis. Concurrently, genes associated with the starch biosynthetic branch, including AGPase, SSS, and GBSS, showed coordinated upregulation. This expression pattern is consistent with the enhanced production of ADP-glucose and synthesis of amylose and amylopectin, corresponding to substantial starch accumulation during this developmental period.

By contrast, genes associated with sucrose biosynthesis and transport displayed marked expression changes in the fifth year. The expression of SPS was significantly upregulated, accompanied by altered expression of sucrose transporters, indicating a shift toward sucrose synthesis and translocation. Meanwhile, the expression levels of key starch biosynthetic genes such as SSS and GBSS were downregulated, whereas those of genes involved in starch degradation, including AMY and BMY, were variable. Together, these transcriptional changes are consistent with a transition from starch storage toward sucrose re-accumulation during later stages of bulb development.

Overall, the pathway-level expression patterns demonstrate a close correspondence between age-dependent transcriptional changes of key metabolic genes and directional shifts in carbon flux within the starch–sucrose metabolic network. These results indicate that stage-specific expression patterns of enzymes within this pathway are closely associated with the dynamic redistribution of carbon resources during F. unibracteata bulb development.

Changes in enzyme activities related to starch and sucrose biosynthesis

To examine the activity patterns of key enzymes in the starch–sucrose metabolic pathway across developmental stages, the activities of key enzymes involved in starch and sucrose metabolism were determined across different developmental stages (Fig. 10). Overall, the trends in enzyme activities were largely consistent with the corresponding transcriptional expression patterns, supporting age-dependent shifts in carbon storage strategies during bulb development.

Changes in activities of enzymes related to starch and sucrose metabolism in Fritillaria unibracteata bulbs at different growth ages. A Activity of AGPase (ADP-glucose pyrophosphorylase). B Activity of GBSS (granule-bound starch synthase). C Activity of SSS (soluble starch synthase). D Activity of SBE (starch branching enzyme). E Activity of SS (sucrose synthase). F Activity of BMY (β-amylase). G Activity of SP (starch phosphorylase). H Activity of SPS (sucrose phosphate synthase). I Activity of DBE (debranching enzyme). Different lowercase letters indicate statistically significant differences among growth ages (P < 0.05, Tukey’s test). Sample size was n = 5 for each growth stage

In the first to fourth year, the activity of AGPase, a rate-limiting enzyme in starch biosynthesis, significantly increased and reached its maximum in the fourth year (Fig. 10A), providing essential substrates for starch synthesis. In parallel, the activities of GBSS, SSS, and SBE were also elevated (Fig. 10B–D). Among these enzymes, GBSS and SSS exhibited peak activities in the fourth year, whereas SBE activity peaked in the third year. During the same period, SS activity continuously increased (Fig. 10F), consistent with the enhanced sucrose cleavage and carbon flux toward starch accumulation.

In the fifth year, the activity of the starch biosynthetic enzyme GBSS markedly declined, accompanied by a significant increase in BMY activity (Fig. 10I), indicating the initiation of stored starch degradation. In addition, the activity of SP exhibited noticeable fluctuations (Fig. 10H), suggesting its involvement in carbon release during starch breakdown. Concurrently, SPS activity increased (Fig. 10G), consistent with the enhanced sucrose re-synthesis at later developmental stages. The activity of DBE also concomitantly increased (Fig. 10E), suggesting a potential role in the efficient utilization of starch degradation products.

Taken together, these stage-specific changes in enzyme activities closely correspond to the transcriptional patterns of key genes within the starch–sucrose metabolic pathway. Early to mid-developmental stages were characterized by elevated activities of starch biosynthetic enzymes, whereas the fifth year stage exhibited increased activities of starch-degrading enzymes and sucrose biosynthetic enzymes, reflecting a transition in carbon allocation during bulb development.

WGCNA analysis of genes associated with the starch–sucrose metabolism pathway

To explore the co-expression patterns of starch–sucrose metabolism during bulb development in F. unibracteata, WGCNA was performed using 2,730 genes (TPM > 5 in three biological replicates per group) across 15 transcriptome samples. Based on the constructed scale-free co-expression network, eight co-expression modules were identified. As shown in Fig. 11A, these modules were represented by distinct colors. Clustering of module eigengenes (Fig. 11B) grouped the modules into three clusters with similar expression patterns: (i) MEpink; (ii) MEred and MEturquoise; and (iii) MEblack, MEbrown, MEgreen, MEblue, and MEyellow.

Module-trait correlation analysis was performed using the activities of nine key starch–sucrose metabolic enzymes as phenotypic traits (Fig. 11C). MEred and MEturquoise showed the strongest associations with starch metabolism, suggesting that these modules are associated with carbon metabolism during bulb development.

To further explore the internal connectivity of the starch-associated modules, network visualization was performed for MEred and MEturquoise (edge weight threshold > 0.5). Under this criterion, no prominent hub genes were identified in the MEred and MEturquoise modules. This observation indicates that the co-expression networks within these modules exhibit a distributed connectivity structure, rather than being dominated by a small number of highly connected hub genes. Notably, no transcripts significantly associated with SP activity were identified in these two modules, indicating that SP-related transcripts may be distributed across other co-expression modules or exhibit more complex expression patterns. Complete gene lists for all modules are provided in Supplementary Table S5.

Coordinated changes among bulb morphology, carbon metabolites, key gene expression, and enzyme activities

To further elucidate the relationships between bulb enlargement, carbon metabolism, and gene expression patterns, Pearson correlation analysis was performed among morphological traits, carbohydrate contents, key gene expression levels, and enzyme activities (Fig. 12; full correlation data in Supplementary Table S6).

Pearson correlation analysis of bulb-related traits, carbon metabolites, key gene expression, and enzyme activity in Fritillaria unibracteata bulbs. Heatmap displaying Pearson correlation coefficients among fresh weight, dry weight, morphological parameters, starch and sucrose contents, and activities of key enzymes involved in starch and sucrose metabolism. Color gradient from blue to red represents correlation strength and direction, with red indicating positive correlation and blue indicating negative correlation. Asterisks (*) indicate statistically significant correlations at P < 0.05

Core bulb morphological traits (fresh weight, dry weight, transverse/longitudinal diameter, vascular bundle number) showed extremely significant positive correlations (r > 0.95, P < 0.01), indicating synchronous biomass accumulation and structural development. Starch and sucrose contents had strong positive correlations with these morphological parameters (P < 0.01). Notably, AGPase activity (rate limiting for starch biosynthesis) showed the strongest positive correlation with starch content (r = 0.965, P < 0.01); SPS activity was highly correlated with sucrose content (r = 0.788, P < 0.01).

By contrast, key starch-degrading enzymes (BMY, SP) had extremely significant negative correlations with starch content and bulb morphological traits (r = −0.770, −0.948, P < 0.01). Overall, bulb enlargement is closely associated with coordinated increases in starch/sucrose biosynthesis and decreased activities of starch degradation.

Discussion

Morphological and structural coordination during bulb development

Bulb enlargement in plants relies on the coordinated processes of cell division and expansion, dry matter accumulation, and tissue differentiation [25]. In the present study, bulb enlargement of F. unibracteata was primarily associated with dry matter accumulation rather than changes in water content (Fig. 2F), which is consistent with developmental patterns reported in other bulbous species such as onion and lily [26, 27]. Microscopic observations further demonstrated that during the 1- to 4-year stages, bulb enlargement was supported by the proliferation and densification of storage parenchyma cells, together with the progressive development of vascular bundles, providing structural capacity for assimilate transport and storage. Notably, vascular tissue progressively expanded from the first to fourth year (Fig. 3G), coinciding with rapid starch accumulation and elevated starch biosynthetic enzyme activity (Figs. 6 and 10). This suggests that enhanced transport capacity and tissue differentiation may support the influx of assimilates and the deposition of reserves during the storage accumulation phase.

After the fourth year, a reduction in vascular bundle area and a loosening of amyloplast structure were observed (Fig. 3G; Figs. 4–5), indicating a developmental transition from structural establishment and reserve accumulation toward material mobilization and redistribution. Such a shift may represent an adaptive strategy to meet increased carbon demands during reproductive growth. A reduction in vascular bundle area may alter transport efficiency and facilitate the mobilization of stored reserves, thereby supporting subsequent carbon reallocation. This developmental pattern is consistent with reported carbon redistribution strategies in Fritillaria species during later growth stages [28–30].

Stage-specific balance of starch–sucrose metabolism and carbon allocation

Directional allocation of carbon metabolites represents a central process in organ development, and the dynamic balance between starch and sucrose directly influences bulb enlargement and reserve accumulation [31]. In many bulbous plants, stored starch is converted into soluble sugars after flowering and subsequently transported to aerial organs to support reproductive development. Previous studies have shown that starch content markedly decreases after flowering, whereas soluble sugar levels significantly increase [32, 33], reflecting carbon mobilization and redistribution during reproductive growth.

Consistent with these observations, the present study revealed a pronounced complementary accumulation pattern between starch and sucrose in F. unibracteata bulbs. From the first to fourth year, starch content continuously increased and reached a maximum in the fourth year, while sucrose content concomitantly decreased. By contrast, in the fifth year, starch content declined while sucrose levels increased (Fig. 6), suggesting mobilization of stored carbon reserves to meet developmental demands. This carbon allocation strategy, characterized by a transition from reserve synthesis to reserve utilization, resembles metabolic patterns reported in other storage organs such as potato tubers and garlic bulbs [34–36]. The starch accumulation inflection point observed in the fourth year may be related to species-specific developmental rhythms and increasing demands for secondary metabolism during later stages [37].

Coordinated transcriptional and enzymatic patterns of the starch–sucrose metabolic pathway

Stage-dependent transitions in starch–sucrose metabolism are associated with coordinated changes in the expression and activity of key metabolic genes and enzymes [38]. From the first to fourth year, the upregulated expression of sucrose cleavage-related genes (e.g., SS, INV) and starch biosynthetic genes (e.g., AGPase, SSS, GBSS), together with the enhanced activities of their corresponding enzymes, collectively promoted the conversion of sucrose into starch. These patterns suggest a potential contribution to the conversion of sucrose into starch during bulb enlargement. This trend is consistent with patterns reported for starch accumulation in cereal crops such as rice and wheat [39, 40].

Integration of transcript expression patterns (Fig. 9) with enzyme activities (Fig. 10) revealed that for certain genes, transcript levels and enzyme activities both peaked in the fourth year, coinciding with maximum starch accumulation (Fig. 6). This parallel variation suggests that transcriptional changes are linked to increased flux toward starch deposition during the bulb enlargement phase [41]. However, correlation analysis indicated that the overall strength of associations between transcript levels and the measured phenotypic and biochemical parameters was limited (Fig. 12), demonstrating that transcriptional abundance alone cannot fully account for the observed variation in enzyme activities and carbohydrate accumulation. The transcriptional patterns reported here should therefore be viewed as one component of a broader network of processes that likely involves post‑transcriptional and post‑translational contributions. Consistent with this interpretation, WGCNA identified co‑expression modules (MEred and MEturquoise) that were correlated with the activities of several starch–sucrose metabolic enzymes, and analysis of internal module connectivity revealed a distributed network structure with no dominant hub genes (edge weight threshold > 0.5).

Interestingly, the positive correlation between SS expression and starch accumulation in F. unibracteata bulbs differs from observations in Arabidopsis leaves [42]. This difference may reflect organ-specific metabolic strategies. Unlike autotrophic leaves, bulb tissues function as heterotrophic storage organs, where SS may preferentially channel sucrose toward storage metabolism. Functional differentiation among SS isoforms may also exist. These interpretations are based on correlative evidence and highlight the need for future experimental validation.

In the fifth year, the expression levels and activities of starch biosynthetic components declined, whereas those of starch degradation-related genes (AMY, BMY) and sucrose biosynthetic genes (SPS) increased. These coordinated changes were accompanied by a redistribution of carbon flux from starch storage toward sucrose re-accumulation (Figs. 9 and 10), a pattern commonly observed during carbon remobilization in plant storage organs [43]. Collectively, these findings indicate that transcriptional changes are associated with enzyme activity patterns during bulb development, although the limited overall correlations suggest that additional processes likely participate in shaping carbon allocation dynamics. Direct functional validation remains necessary to clarify specific gene roles.

Limitations and future perspectives

Although this study provides integrated morphological, physiological, and transcriptomic evidence for the age-dependent patterns of starch–sucrose metabolism during bulb development in F. unibracteata, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, gene expression analyses were conducted only at the transcriptional level, and post-transcriptional and post-translational processes were not examined, which likely contribute to the modulation of enzyme activities and carbon allocation. In addition, metabolomic profiling was not included, which limits precise quantification of intermediate carbon flux. Future studies integrating functional genomics, enzyme kinetics, and multi-omics approaches are necessary to clarify the transcriptional networks associated with carbon allocation and provide deeper insights into bulb developmental physiology.

Conclusion

Bulb development in F. unibracteata exhibits clear age-dependent shifts in structure and carbon metabolism. From the first to fourth year, bulb growth is characterized by tissue differentiation, vascular development, and starch accumulation, with the fourth year representing the key stage of starch deposition. By contrast, the fifth year marks a transition from carbon storage to remobilization, accompanied by sucrose re-accumulation and starch degradation. These metabolic transitions are associated with coordinated transcriptional and enzymatic patterns in the starch–sucrose pathway. WGCNA further identified co-expression modules linked to starch–sucrose metabolic traits, revealing transcriptional patterns associated with carbon allocation. Together, these findings provide integrated physiological and molecular insights into carbon allocation dynamics during bulb development and contribute to a better understanding of growth-stage patterns in medicinal Fritillaria species.
