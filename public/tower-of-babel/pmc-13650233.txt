Detection rate of PSMA PET MRI (18 F-Flotufolastat) at the onset of biochemical recurrence following radical prostatectomy

Introduction

Biochemical disease recurrence (BCR) following Radical Prostatectomy (RP) is typically defined by two consecutive PSA levels > 0.2 ng/ml (Cookson et al. 2007). Biochemical disease recurrence 8 years following RP occurs in approximately 10%, 25%, 50%, 65% and 80% men with GG 1,2,3,4 and 5, respectively (Epstein et al. 2016). In these men, the diagnostic challenge is to determine if the disease recurrence is localized to the prostatic bed, lymph nodes (LN), skeletal system or solid organs. Positron emission tomography (PET) is the imaging tool with the highest detection rate for identifying disease recurrence following RP (Lawhn-Heath et al. 2021). Several Gallium-68 and Flourine-18 PET imaging ligands targeting prostate specific membrane antigen (PSMA) are commercially available for localization of disease recurrence following RP. Cyclotron fluorinated ligands have improved imaging resolution due to lower positron energy higher positron yield and potentially increased diagnostic accuracy due to a longer half-life decay compared to generator produced gallium ligands and will likely emerge as the preferred source of PSMA PET ligands (Werner et al. 2020).

The optimal timing to obtain the initial PET-PSMA imaging study when disease recurrence is suspected following RP is controversial. Salvage radiation therapy for disease recurrence following RP is most effective if initiated early (Stish et al. 2016). The unique aspect of the present study is to determine the detection rates of PSMA PET imaging at the immediate onset of BCR for men following RP. A secondary objective is to assess timing of subsequent PSMA PET imaging following an initial negative study.

Materials and methods

Subjects

Men undergoing open or robotic RP at our institution are followed longitudinally with serial PSA testing. Only these men were eligible for the present study. All men with new onset of BCR were invited to participate in the present study providing their PSA within the prior year was < 0.2 ng/ml. BCR following RP was defined as a PSA > 0.2 ng/ml with a confirmatory PSA test.

Protocol

All men meeting the eligibility were invited to participate in the present study. The study protocol was designed to enroll 24 subjects. Twenty-four men meeting the eligibility criteria signed informed consent to participate in the study with IRB # 22–00547 or ClinicalTrials.gov study number NCT05678322. The initial 18F-Flotufolastat PSMA PET MRI (study #1) was performed within 2 months of signing informed consent. Salvage treatment was offered only if site(s) of disease recurrence were identified by study #1. The specific salvage treatment was at the discretion of the multi-disciplinary prostate cancer providers at our institution. The protocol for those subjects with a negative study #1 was PSA monitoring at 6 and 12 months. A second 18F-Flotufolastat PSMA PET MRI (study #2) was performed 1 year following Study 1 or sooner if the 6 months PSA > 0.5 ng/ml. The PSA doubling time between studies # 1 and 2 was calculated as follows: Prostate-specific antigen doubling time (PSADT) for those untreated subjects with a negative study #1 was calculated as follows: ln(2) / x, where x = slope of the relationship between ln(PSA, ng/mL) and time in months of PSA measurement ln(PSA2) - ln(PSA1) / time between PSA2 and PSA1, where PSA1 is the PSA at study enrollment and PSA2 is the PSA immediately prior to study 2 (Patel et al. 1997).

Salvage treatments following study #2 was at the discretion of the multi-disciplinary prostate cancer providers at our institution.

Protocol for performing and interpreting PSMA PET MRI studies

PET/MR was performed in a dedicated scanner (Biograph mMR, Siemens Technologies, Germany) which allows simultaneous acquisition of PET and MR images. PET images were acquired from the top of the skull to the mid thighs approximately 60 min following the IV administration of 8 mCi of 18 F-Flotufolastat (18 F-rH PSMA, Blue Earth Diagnostics, UK). PET data was acquired in 3D mode for 2–3 min per bed position in flow technique. Multiplanar T1, T2 and diffusion weighted images were acquired for PET attenuation correction and anatomic localization and characterization.

All PET/MR exams were interpreted by a board-certified nuclear medicine physician or radiologist with expertise in molecular imaging. Radiopharmaceutical accumulation higher than the surrounding background and not attributable to physiological uptake was interpreted as positive for prostate cancer lesions.

Statistical methods

Continuous variables were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges, and categorical variables were summarized using counts and proportions. We compared continuous features using the Mann-Whitney-U test and categorical features using the Fisher’s exact test.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using Python (version 3.12.7), including packages from scipy, numpy, and pandas.

Discussion

PSMA PET imaging is superior to conventional body imaging and bone scintigraphy for detecting sites of disease recurrence following RP (Lawhn-Heath et al. 2021). Therefore, PSMA PET imaging is guideline recommended for localizing site(s) of disease recurrence in men with BCR following RP (Tilki et al. 2024, Morgan et al. 2024). In the present study, we investigated the ligand18F-Flotufolastat (Posluma) which is FDA approved for monitoring disease recurrence following RP (Heo and Flotufolastat 2023). A potential advantageous property of 18F-Flotufolastat is its delayed excretion in the urine which may improve detection of recurrent disease at the base of the bladder (Tolvanen et al. 2021). In the present study PSMA PET was coupled with MRI imaging. There is evidence that PSMA PET MRI may be superior to PSMA PET CT for detecting disease recurrence following RP (Hoffmann et al. 2019).

The performance of 18-flurinated PSMA PET imaging has been reported for men with PSA levels < 0.5 following RP, which is generally considered a low level of PSA (Mojsak et al. 2023, Treglia et al. 2019). The challenge for urologists is to identify the site(s) of disease recurrence at the onset of BCR when the PSA initially exceeds 0.2 ng/ml. The present study is the first to assess the detection rate of PSMA PET imaging at the onset of BCR following RP. Only men who had a PSA < 0.2 in the year following development of BCR were eligible for the present study.

In the present study, a PSMA PET MRI study was performed at the onset of BCR (study 1). The site of disease recurrence at this time point was observed in 25% of subjects. Disease recurrence was observed in 2, 2, and 2 within the prostatic bed only, regional LN only and both prostatic bed and regional LN, respectively. We feel the 25% diagnostic yield is clinically significant and based on our findings we will continue to obtain PSMA PET MRI imaging at the onset of BCR. Our multi-disciplinary group of urologists, radiation oncologists and medical oncologists recommended salvage RT to the prostatic bed and regional LN together with ADT independent on site(s) of recurrence.

The protocol recommended active surveillance for the 18 subjects with negative study #1. This provided the opportunity to compare the diagnostic yield of PSMA PET MRI imaging at onset of BCR with a second PSMA PET one year later or if the PSA > 0.5 ng/ml. There were 3 protocol violations who received salvage RT with a negative study 1. Of the 15 untreated men with a negative study #1, study #2 was positive in 6 (40%). The overall detection rate for the 21 subjects compliant with protocol was 57%. Our detection rate is consistent with a meta-analysis reporting a detection rate of PSMA PET imaging of 61.5% for studies performed for PSA < 0.5 ng/ml (Treglia et al. 2019). The median PSA doubling time for subjects demonstrating disease recurrence on only study #2 was 12.8 months. The prolonged PSA doubling time was 75% greater in those subjects with a negative study #2 compared to those with a positive study #2. The difference in PSA doubling time was not statistically significant due to the relatively small sample size. This finding is consistent with other reports in the literature showing PSA doubling time is associated with positive PSMA imaging for men with BCR following RP. Only 2 of the 6 subjects with a positive study #2 had an increase in PSA > 0.5 within 6 months. Four and 2 subjects with a positive study #2 were found to have disease localized to the prostatic bed only and pelvic LN, respectively.

Pathological grade, stage and marginal status did not predict positive PSMA PET MRI imaging at the onset of BCR.

Based on the present study, we obtain a PSMA PET MRI at the onset of BCR and manage any disease recurrence with radiation therapy to the prostatic bed and lymph nodes and neoadjuvant ADT. We generally withhold salvage treatment if the initial PSMA PET MRI does not identify site(s) of disease. We repeat the PSMA PET when the PSA > 0.5 ng/ml, if the PSA doubling time is greater than 1 year, or within 2 years. There is no consensus amongst our multi-disciplinary team how to manage men with BCR and negative PSMA PET MRI imaging.

A limitation of the present study is its small sample size since we only included men at the immediate onset of BCR who were followed longitudinally at our institution willing to enroll in a clinical trial. Despite the small sample size, the prospective design of the present study enables offering only preliminary evidence-based recommendation about the detection rates of PSMA PET MRI imaging at the onset of BCR. While we feel the sample size is adequate to assess detection rates at the imaging time points, it is not adequate for group comparisons.

A general limitation of PSMA PET imaging studies is that a positive scan may not represent true disease recurrence. It is often technically challenging to biopsy positive PSMA PET findings in the prostatic bed or lymph nodes due to small size of the lesions especially at the onset of biochemical recurrence. Because of these technical challenges, false negative biopsy results may be due to tissue sampling limitations. In the present study, we assumed positive findings reflected disease recurrence and treated accordingly.

If the objective of PSMA PET testing is to detect sites of disease recurrence, then the timing of imaging studies should be influenced by detection rates. The 25% disease detection and localizations justify performing PSMA PET MRI imaging at the onset of BCR. If the initial PSMA PET MRI does not identify site(s) of disease, our study supports repeating a PSMA PET study when the PSA is > 0.5 ng/ml or if the PSA doubling time is < 1 year.
