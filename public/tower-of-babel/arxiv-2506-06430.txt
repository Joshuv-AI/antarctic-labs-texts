Constraint instabilities in first-order viscous relativistic fluids
Delfina Fantini 1, Marcelo E. Rubio 2,3

Abstract
Relativistic hydrodynamics provides a solid framework for evolving matter and energy in a wide variety of phenomena. Nevertheless, the inclusion of dissipative effects in realistic scenarios through causal, stable, and well-posed theories still constitutes an open problem. In this paper, we point out that the first-order reduction originally proposed by Bemfica, Disconzi, Noronha and Kovtun (BDNK) for proving the local well-posedness of conformally-invariant viscous fluids in Sobolev spaces, admits a set of differential constraints which do not propagate along evolution. To see so, we first show analytically that this is the case for uniform-velocity configurations. Motivated by this result, we perform numerical simulations of the BDNK first-order reduction restricted to plane-symmetric configurations in Minkowski spacetime. We report on initial data sets which satisfy the constraints, but display exponential grow at early times. Thus, although the principal part of their reduction is diagonalizable with real eigenvalues –as proved by BDNK–, the corresponding differential constraints appearing from it are not conserved along evolution. This result suggests that an alternative path is needed to prove the strong-hyperbolicity for the conformal version of the theory.

Relativistic hydrodynamics is perhaps the most suitable workhorse for describing the macroscopic behavior of matter in extreme conditions, with a scale range running from heavy-ion collisions

The mathematical formulation of any theory aiming to describe fluid dynamics is based on conservation laws, provided an equation of state which accounts for microscopic effects of the fluid elements

;
batchelor2000introduction
;
Rezzolla:2013dea
. The simplest approach is the ideal fluid, which describes a macroscopic, homogeneous and isotropic system in thermal equilibrium, and whose dynamics is governed by the Euler’s equations. While this formulation results effective as a first approximation to the phenomenology of fluids, it is not entirely accurate for modeling scenarios involving energy transport through heat and viscosity. This necessarily motivates the inclusion of various viscous effects, according to the scales of the problem at hand. A classic example is constituted by the Navier-Stokes equations, which allow a more accurate evolution of macroscopic phenomena, and where the ideal approximation renders the description of the system inconsistent

;

Nonetheless, the Navier-Stokes equations are effective in a non-relativistic framework. Indeed, they result insufficient for the study of astrophysical processes where either the characteristic velocities are close to the speed of light –such as the modeling of astrophysical jets

;

;

The first covariant formulations attempting to account for viscous effects in the description of relativistic fluids date back to Eckart, in 1940

, and Landau-Lifshitz, in 1953

Although both of them reached a fully-covariant formulation by means of constitutive relations depending on first gradients of the thermodynamic variables, they failed to be effective due to generic linear instabilities, thus predicting unbounded fluid modes in the high-frequency limit. This seminal study was carefully carried out by Hiscock and Lindblom in 1984

, suggesting that first-order theories are unsuitable for physical predictions through numerical simulations. To address these shortcomings, Müller, Israel and Stewart proposed going beyond first-order theories, and considered instead second-order contributions for modeling viscous effects

By introducing additional evolution equations for the dissipative fields, the authors were able to restore the causality and stability of the theory, allowing to perform the first numerical simulations on viscous fluids in stronger regimes. However, their approach relied on a truncated expansion of non-equilibrium effects, introducing additional complexity and new parameters in the equations

;

;

.

More recently, Bemfica, Disconzi, Noronha and Kovtun (BDNK) suggested an interesting alternative framework for treating viscous fluids which, even assuming first-order dissipative contributions, it surprisingly gives rise to causal and linearly stable theories, provided some restrictions on the parameter space

Well-posedness is a local concept which means that, for any given initial data set in certain functional space, there must exist (locally) a unique solution. Additionally, the map between the space of initial data and the space of solutions must be continuous, with respect to the topology considered on each functional space
Hadamard1902
. For physically-motivated systems of quasi-linear first-order equations, it is usual to look for well-posedness in Sobolev spaces
Friedrichs54
;
friedrichs1971systems
;
Reula04
. If the system has no differential constraints, the standard way to achieve this goal is by studying the algebraic properties of the principal part of the equations (i.e., the terms with highest order in derivatives). The latter contains all the information about the propagation speeds of the theory, and the well-posedness is elucidated through the concept of
hyperbolicity

geroch2001hyperbolic
, a set of algebraic conditions the principal part must satisfy. In particular, a system whose principal part is diagonalizable with real eigenvalues is referred as to be
strongly-hyperbolic

kreiss
;
Kreiss70
;
Kreiss89
. It can be shown that, for first-order quasi-linear partial differential equations, strong-hyperbolicity is equivalent to local well-posedness in Sobolev spaces
friedrichs1971systems
;
Choquet-Bruhat52fr
;
Choquet82
. Finally, if the system admits differential constraints (i.e., equations without time derivatives that must be satisfied along evolution), the hyperbolicity criteria can be addressed in an similar way, provided the correct propagation of the constraints. The latter means that, if the constraints are initially satisfied, then, as a consequence of the evolution equations, they must be also satisfied for further times. The strong hyperbolicity of general covariant systems with differential constraints is currently an active open problem, and we refer the reader to Refs.
Abalos:2017jtt
;
Abalos:2021rqs
;
Abalos:2024auy
, for further details.

The well-posedness of BDNK theory was first proved in a weaker sense, for which existence and uniqueness of solutions was guaranteed in certain Gevrey spaces. This was achieved by means of the Leray hyperbolicity criteria

;

, which consists of studying specific properties of the characteristic polynomial of the principal part. Shortly after, the authors considered the general problem of well-posedness in Sobolev spaces

The authors proved that the principal part of such a reduction turns out to be diagonalizable with real eigenvalues, concluding that the theory is strongly-hyperbolic. Nevertheless, the inspection of the constraint evolution seems not completely addressed.

In this work we perform an analytic study and a numerical exploration of the behavior of the differential constraints emerging from the BDNK reduction displayed in the work

In order to assess the validity of our numerical scheme, we performed several convergence tests, checking out the accuracy of the methods here used. Indeed, we displayed convergence tests both in time and space domains. As it was pointed out in the previous sections, for the time integration we implemented a fourth-order Runge-Kutta scheme, adding artificial viscosity for avoiding spurious oscillations near eventual shock fronts. Instead, for the spatial discretization, we implemented the method of lines by means of centered finite differences which were chosen to be second-order accurate. On top of this, our finite differences satisfy the property of summation by parts
Calabrese:2003vx
, allowing semi-discrete energy estimates for the corresponding initial-boundary value problem, thus guaranteeing numerical stability.

If the numerical scheme is of order
p
, we can assess its correct operation by performing three runs at different resolutions; say
r
,
r/2
and
r/4
, and then define the (logarithmic) convergence ratio
KreissOrtiz2014

Q(t)\equiv\log_{2}\left(\frac{\|\Phi_{r}(t,x)-\Phi_{r/2}(t,x)\|}{\|\Phi_{r/2}(t,x)-\Phi_{r/4}(t,x)\|}\right),

(40)

where
\Phi_{r}
,
\Phi_{r/2}
and
\Phi_{r/4}
represent the evolutions obtained at resolutions
r
(low),
r/2

r/4
(high), respectively, while

is some adequate norm taken over the whole spatial domain. It can be shown that if the method is order

. This standard way to test the accuracy of a numerical scheme is useful for (at least) three important reasons: (i) the convergence factor
Q(t)

Of course, it is usually performed along the whole time evolution, for monitoring that the convergence is not being lost for physical reasons. Nonetheless, an adequate norm needs to be chosen. For our purposes, as we are dealing with smooth evolutions, the

Thus, this tests allow us to trust in the robustness of our results, as well as to draw solid conclusions on the propagation of the constraints.

VI
Final remarks

In this work, we investigated the evolution and stability of the differential constraints introduced by BDNK for proving the well-posedness of the initial-value problem for their theory of viscous fluids. We restricted our analysis to the conformal class by means of a pure-radiation equation of state. For this theory, first-order constitutive relations were suggested for the corrections of the energy, pressure and stresses in terms of first gradients of the temperature and four-velocity. Although the evolution equations obtained from the local conservation of the energy-momentum tensor are second-order in derivatives, the authors introduced a set of differential constraints that allow, together with said constitutive relations, to obtain a fully first-order evolution system. This fact not only restricts the allowed initial data for evolving the system, but also requires these constraints to be satisfied along evolution, as a pure consequence of the dynamical equations. Although the authors used this reduction for showing that, under certain conditions in the parameter space, the conformal system is algebraically diagonalizable admitting purely real eigenvalues, the constraint propagation has not been studied, to the best of our knowledge, which constitutes a crucial step for the strong-hyperbolicity proof. In this work, we reviewed the set of differential constraints proposed by BDNK and showed that there exist initial configurations that develop a smooth evolution, but violate them.

As a first step of our investigation, we analytically found a simple and stable configuration of the conformal fluid for which the corresponding constraints are not satisfied. This motivated a deeper study of these relations for more general configurations. For doing so, we developed a numerical code in one spatial dimension, and with planar symmetry, that allowed us to find smooth evolutions of the fluid, starting from initial data that satisfy the constraints. We saw that if the initial data is sufficiently uniform (almost constant, or close to zero), the differential constraints are satisfied quite well (more specifically, their
L^{2}
and
L^{\infty}
norms remain bounded along evolution). However, we reported on smooth initial data which initially satisfy such constraints, but develop exponential growth at very early evolution times, violating them by several orders of magnitude. This behavior occurs in configurations where all the dynamical fields exhibit smooth and bounded evolutions, with finite propagation velocities, for which stability of the constraints is expected.

In light of our results, several issues should be pointed out. Firstly, it is well known that the equations describing fluid dynamics are generally highly nonlinear, enabling the formation of shocks during evolution. Nevertheless, the reduction proposed by BDNK is written in variables which are not conservative, for which shock-capturing methods cannot be implemented for the numerical evolution of such reduction. Nevertheless, the solutions found in this work display smooth behavior during the whole evolution time. In fact, our results display the expected convergence in both time and space, thus validating the robustness of the scheme. Secondly, it is important to recall that our results on constraint instabilities
does not imply
that BDNK theory admits an ill-posed initial-value problem: what we are pointing out here is that the suggested first-order reduction does not seem to be the appropriate one for showing strong-hyperbolicity in Sobolev spaces.

Finally, and as a future perspective, it would be interesting to look for a suitable first-order reduction rendering the system strongly-hyperbolic, and involving constraints that propagate in the right way during evolution. Alternatively, it should be possible to tackle the well-posedness of the theory by means of algebraic criteria applied directly to the original, second-order dynamical equations; i.e., without requiring a first-order reduction, for instance by using similar techniques to the ones developed in
Kreiss:2001cu
;
Nagy:2004td
(see also
Hilditch:2014naa
). Intriguingly, in all the previous attempts for numerically evolving the BDNK theory, the second order formulation has been implemented, showing stable evolutions. This is why we believe that the theory may admit a well-posed formulation, for which an intense study should still be done.
