Minimal Flavor Violation (MFV) provides a compelling framework for exploring physics beyond the Standard Model, in which new QCD-singlet fields transforming under the global
\mathrm{SU}(3)^{3}
quark flavor symmetry can naturally be stable and act as dark matter (DM) candidates.
We show that the DM–MFV framework naturally accommodates the excess in either
K^{+}\to\pi^{+}\nu\bar{\nu}
or
B^{+}\to K^{+}\nu\bar{\nu}
, while a unified explanation of both channels simultaneously cannot be achieved within a minimal setup containing only a single dark matter multiplet with nearly degenerate masses.
Overall, our findings underscore the intricate interplay between MFV-based model building, flavored dark matter scenarios, and precision flavor experiments, highlighting flavored dark matter as a framework that is both theoretically robust and experimentally testable.

Introduction

Rare flavor-changing neutral current (FCNC) processes such as those involving
s\to d
and
b\to s
transitions are among the most sensitive probes of physics beyond the Standard Model (SM). Their theoretical cleanliness—stemming from the dominance of short-distance dynamics, and the absence of large hadronic uncertainties—makes them ideal for testing new physics scenarios.

Experimental efforts by NA62
[

[

[

]
. Even more strikingly, the latest Belle II results in the

2
Dark Matter within Minimal Flavor Violation

The SM exhibits a large global flavor symmetry
{\rm U}(3)^{5}={\rm U}(3)_{q}\times{\rm U}(3)_{u}\times{\rm U}(3)_{d}\times{\rm U}(3)_{\ell}\times{\rm U}(3)_{e}
in the gauge sector.
This symmetry is explicitly broken by the Yukawa interactions of quarks and leptons to the doublet Higgs field
H
, and flavor violating processes show specific patterns controlled by the structure of the Yukawa interaction matrices
Y_{u,d,e}
.

New interactions from physics beyond the SM can introduce independent sources of flavor violation, generally at very high scales much above the TeV scale. The MFV hypothesis
[
44
,
67
,
33
,
57
]
requires that new physics interactions also respect the
{\rm U}(3)^{5}
flavor symmetry, with breaking controlled solely by the Yukawa matrices.
Formally, MFV is implemented by promoting the Yukawa matrices to spurion fields which transform like

Y_{u}\sim({3},\bar{{3}},{1},{1},{1})\,,\quad Y_{d}\sim({3},{1},\bar{{3}},{1},{1})\,,\quad Y_{e}\sim({1},{1},{1},{3},\bar{{3}})\,,

(2.1)

under the
{\rm U}(3)^{5}
flavor group.

In this formalism, for any operator
{\cal O}_{ij\dots}
with flavor indices
i,j,\dots
, its coupling
C_{ij\dots}
is expanded in Yukawa spurions to ensure invariance under flavor transformations.
More concretely,
C_{ij}
is expressed for
{\cal O}_{ij}\sim({1},{3}\otimes\bar{3},{1},{1},{1})
as

C_{ij}=c_{0}\,\delta_{ij}+\epsilon\,c_{1}(Y_{u}^{\dagger}Y_{u})_{ij}+\epsilon^{2}\Big[c_{2}(Y_{u}^{\dagger}Y_{u}Y_{u}^{\dagger}Y_{u})_{ij}+c_{2}^{\prime}(Y_{u}^{\dagger}Y_{d}Y_{d}^{\dagger}Y_{u})_{ij}\Big]+\dots\,,

(2.2)

where the ellipsis represents further spurion insertions, which are suppressed by additional powers of Yukawa couplings, CKM off-diagonal elements, and a potentially small MFV expansion parameter
\epsilon
.

Remarkably, when new QCD-singlet particles are added to the SM, the principle of MFV can also ensure the stability of some of them, allowing these particles to serve as dark matter (DM) candidates.
Consider a new QCD-singlet field
\chi
, transforming like
\chi\sim(n_{q},m_{q})\times(n_{u},m_{u})\times(n_{d},m_{d})
under
G_{q}={\rm SU}(3)_{q}\times{\rm SU}(3)_{u}\times{\rm SU}(3)_{d}
.
One can show that the lightest state of
\chi
is absolutely stable when
(n_{q}+n_{u}+n_{d}-m_{q}-m_{u}-m_{d})\,{\rm mod}\,3\neq 0
is satisfied
[
15
,
14
]
.
This state,
\chi
, is thus an excellent DM candidate if electrically neutral. Table
1
lists the lowest-dimensional representations of
G_{q}
that are stable according to the above conditions.

. Depending on their electroweak quantum numbers, these multiplets

In this framework, interactions between quarks and dark matter particles determine the phenomenology in the dark sector: e.g. dark matter production in the early universe, for (in)direct searches, and decays of the heavy dark states.
The traditional WIMP parameter space, where
\chi
is heavier than
B
mesons, was comprehensively studied in
[
15
,
83
]
, with implications ranging from freeze-out cosmology to collider signals and FCNC processes.
The possibility of multi-component dark matter was investigated in
[
87
]
.
If instead
\chi
is lighter than
B
mesons, some operators can induce dark matter emission from heavy quark decays, such as
d_{i}\to d_{j}\chi_{i}\bar{\chi}_{j}
.
Since
\chi
must be effectively unobservable with current detectors, these new decay modes mimic the FCNC decays,
d_{i}\to d_{j}\nu\bar{\nu}
.

In this section, we classify the leading effective operators contributing to
d_{i}\to d_{j}\chi_{i}\bar{\chi}_{j}
, with a focus on the lowest-dimensional representations with
(n,m)=(1,0)
.
In this work, we consider a flavored dark matter multiplet
\chi=S
(scalar) or
\psi
(fermion) as the only new light degrees of freedom below the electroweak scale.
Their interactions with the SM quarks are described by higher‑dimensional operators whose flavor structure is fixed by the MFV hypothesis. In this framework, the fields
\chi
are degenerate at leading order in the MFV expansion, and mass splittings arise only from insertions of the Yukawa spurions at higher order:

(m_{S}^{2})_{ij}=m_{S}^{2}\left[\delta_{ij}+c_{S}\,\epsilon\,(Y_{q}^{\dagger}Y_{q})_{ij}+\dots\right]

(2.3)

for a scalar multiplet
S
, or

(m_{\psi})_{ij}=m_{\psi}\left[\delta_{ij}+c_{\psi}\,\epsilon\,(Y_{q}^{\dagger}Y_{q})_{ij}+\dots\right]

(2.4)

for a fermion multiplet
\psi
.
Here,
c_{S,\psi}
are unknown coefficients of order unity, and
\epsilon\leq 1
counts the number of Yukawa insertions in the MFV expansion. The matrix
Y_{q}
denotes either
Y_{u}
or
Y_{d}
, depending on the flavor representation of the new fields.
Throughout this paper, we ignore small corrections of
{\cal O}(\epsilon)
and only keep the leading order terms. Consequently, the
\chi
fields are degenerate at this order.

2.1
Scalar DM

First, we focus on the case of scalar DM
S
. The relevant interaction Lagrangian is composed of dimension-6 operators involving two quark fields and two dark matter fields,

\mathscr{L}^{\rm eff}_{S}=\frac{1}{\Lambda^{2}}\sum_{I}\sum_{i,j,k,l}C_{ijkl}^{I}{\cal O}_{ijkl}^{I}\,,

(2.5)

where
i,j,k,l=1,2,3
are quark flavor indices and a full set of the operators for scalar DM is given by

\displaystyle{\cal O}^{1}_{ijkl}

\displaystyle=(\bar{q}_{Li}\gamma^{\mu}q_{Lj})(S^{\dagger}_{k}i\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\partial_{\mu}}S_{l})\,,

(2.6)

\displaystyle{\cal O}^{2}_{ijkl}

\displaystyle=(\bar{u}_{Ri}\gamma^{\mu}u_{Rj})(S^{\dagger}_{k}i\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\partial_{\mu}}S_{l})\,,

(2.7)

\displaystyle{\cal O}^{3}_{ijkl}

\displaystyle=(\bar{d}_{Ri}\gamma^{\mu}d_{Rj})(S^{\dagger}_{k}i\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\partial_{\mu}}S_{l})\,,

(2.8)

\displaystyle{\cal O}^{4}_{ijkl}=(\bar{q}_{Li}\widetilde{H}u_{Rj})(S^{\dagger}_{k}S_{l})

\displaystyle\,,\quad{\cal O}^{\overline{4}}_{ijkl}=(\bar{u}_{Ri}\widetilde{H}^{\dagger}q_{Lj})(S^{\dagger}_{k}S_{l})=\left[{\cal O}^{4}_{jilk}\right]^{\dagger}\,,

(2.9)

\displaystyle{\cal O}^{5}_{ijkl}=(\bar{q}_{Li}Hd_{Rj})(S^{\dagger}_{k}S_{l})

\displaystyle\,,\quad{\cal O}^{\overline{5}}_{ijkl}=(\bar{d}_{Ri}H^{\dagger}q_{Lj})(S^{\dagger}_{k}S_{l})=\left[{\cal O}^{5}_{jilk}\right]^{\dagger}\,,

(2.10)

where
A\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\partial_{\mu}}B=A(\partial_{\mu}B)-(\partial_{\mu}A)B
.
Hermiticity requires that

\begin{split}C_{ijkl}^{1}=\left[C_{jilk}^{1}\right]^{*}\,,\quad C_{ijkl}^{2}&=\left[C_{jilk}^{2}\right]^{*}\,,\quad C_{ijkl}^{3}=\left[C_{jilk}^{3}\right]^{*}\,,\\
C_{ijkl}^{4}=\left[C_{jilk}^{\overline{4}}\right]^{*}&\,,\quad C_{ijkl}^{5}=\left[C_{jilk}^{\overline{5}}\right]^{*}\,.\end{split}

(2.11)

Of these operators,
{\cal O}^{1}_{ijkl}
,
{\cal O}^{3}_{ijkl}
and
{\cal O}^{5}_{ijkl}
provide flavor violating interactions to the down quarks at the tree level, so that hereafter we only consider these three operators.

Under MFV, the coefficients
C_{ijkl}^{I}
are expanded in terms of the quark Yukawa matrices and their concrete expressions depend on the
G_{q}
representation of the DM field
S_{i}
.

2.1.1
S\sim({\textbf{3}},{\textbf{1}},{\textbf{1}})

The leading coefficients are given by

\displaystyle C_{ijkl}^{1}

\displaystyle=c^{1}_{1}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+c^{1}_{2}\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}+\dots\ ,

(2.12)

\displaystyle C_{ijkl}^{3}

\displaystyle=c^{3}_{1}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\dots\ ,

(2.13)

\displaystyle C_{ijkl}^{5}

\displaystyle=c^{5}_{1}(Y_{d})_{ij}\delta_{kl}+c^{5}_{2}(Y_{d})_{kj}\delta_{il}+\dots\ ,

(2.14)

where the ellipses indicate further Yukawa matrix insertions and
c^{n}_{m}
are taken to be real.

Without loss of generality, one can take
(Y_{d})_{ij}=y_{d}^{i}\delta_{ij}
and
Y_{u}=V^{\dagger}\hat{Y}_{u}
, with
V
being the CKM matrix and
(\hat{Y}_{u})_{ij}=y_{u}^{i}\delta_{ij}
.
In the unitary gauge
\langle H\rangle=(0,v/\sqrt{2})^{T}
and
in the quark mass basis (
u_{L}\to V^{\dagger}u_{L}
), we find

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}^{\rm eff}_{S}\supset\frac{1}{\Lambda^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\Bigg\{

\displaystyle c^{1}_{2}(\bar{d}_{Li}\gamma^{\mu}d_{Lj})(S^{\dagger}_{j}i\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\partial_{\mu}}S_{i})+c^{1}_{2}\sum_{k,l}V_{il}V^{*}_{jk}(\bar{u}_{Li}\gamma^{\mu}u_{Lj})(S^{\dagger}_{k}i\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\partial_{\mu}}S_{l})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle c^{5}_{2}(m_{d}^{j}+m_{d}^{i})(\bar{d}_{i}d_{j})(S^{\dagger}_{j}S_{i})+c^{5}_{2}(m_{d}^{j}-m_{d}^{i})(\bar{d}_{i}\gamma_{5}d_{j})(S^{\dagger}_{j}S_{i})\Bigg\}\,,

(2.15)

where
m_{d}^{i}=y_{d}^{i}v/\sqrt{2}
.
Note that in the last equation both quarks and DM fields are in the mass basis.

2.1.2
S\sim({\textbf{1}},{\textbf{3}},{\textbf{1}})

The leading coefficients are given by

\displaystyle C_{ijkl}^{1}

\displaystyle=c^{1}_{1}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\dots\ ,

(2.16)

\displaystyle C_{ijkl}^{3}

\displaystyle=c^{3}_{1}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\dots\ ,

(2.17)

\displaystyle C_{ijkl}^{5}

\displaystyle=c^{5}_{1}(Y_{d})_{ij}+\dots\ ,

(2.18)

where the ellipses indicate further Yukawa matrix insertions.
There is no flavor violating interaction in the down sector, thus
S\sim(1,3,1)
is not a suitable representation to address the Belle II and NA62 excesses.

2.1.3
S\sim({\textbf{1}},{\textbf{1}},{\textbf{3}})

The leading coefficients are given by

\displaystyle C_{ijkl}^{1}

\displaystyle=c^{1}_{1}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\dots\ ,

(2.19)

\displaystyle C_{ijkl}^{3}

\displaystyle=c^{3}_{1}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+c^{3}_{2}\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}+\dots\ ,

(2.20)

\displaystyle C_{ijkl}^{5}

\displaystyle=c^{5}_{1}(Y_{d})_{ij}\delta_{kl}+c^{5}_{2}(Y_{d})_{il}\delta_{jk}+\dots\ ,

(2.21)

where the ellipses indicate further Yukawa matrix insertions and
c^{n}_{m}
are taken to be real.
The flavor violating interactions arise only from
{\cal O}^{3}_{ijkl}
and
{\cal O}^{5}_{ijkl}
, which are in the form

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}^{\rm eff}_{S}\simeq\frac{1}{\Lambda^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\Bigg\{

\displaystyle c^{3}_{2}\left(\bar{d}_{Li}\gamma^{\mu}d_{Lj}\right)(S^{\dagger}_{j}i\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\partial_{\mu}}S_{i})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle c^{5}_{2}(m_{d}^{i}+m_{d}^{j})(\bar{d}_{i}d_{j})(S^{\dagger}_{j}S_{i})+c^{5}_{2}(m_{d}^{i}-m_{d}^{j})(\bar{d}_{i}\gamma_{5}d_{j})(S^{\dagger}_{j}S_{i})\Bigg\}\,,

(2.22)

where we take the unitary gauge and the quark mass basis. Note that the
d_{i}\to d_{j}
operators of this Lagrangian are identical to those of Eq. (
2.15
) with
c^{1}_{2}\to c^{3}_{2}
.

2.2
Fermion DM

Next, we focus on the case of fermion DM
\psi
.
The effective Lagrangian consists of a set of higher dimensional operators,

\mathscr{L}^{{\rm eff}}_{F}=\sum_{I}\frac{1}{\Lambda^{d-4}}\,C_{ijkl}^{I}{\cal O}_{ijkl}^{I}\,,

(2.23)

where
d
denotes the dimension of the operator
{\cal O}^{I}_{ijkl}
.
The dimension-6 operators for fermion DM are given by

\displaystyle{\cal O}^{1V}_{ijkl}

\displaystyle=(\bar{q}_{Li}\gamma^{\mu}q_{Lj})(\bar{\psi}_{k}\gamma_{\mu}\psi_{l})\,,\quad{\cal O}^{1A}_{ijkl}=(\bar{q}_{Li}\gamma^{\mu}q_{Lj})(\bar{\psi}_{k}\gamma_{\mu}\gamma_{5}\psi_{l})\,,

(2.24)

\displaystyle{\cal O}^{2V}_{ijkl}

\displaystyle=(\bar{u}_{Ri}\gamma^{\mu}u_{Rj})(\bar{\psi}_{k}\gamma_{\mu}\psi_{l})\,,\quad{\cal O}^{2A}_{ijkl}=(\bar{u}_{Ri}\gamma^{\mu}u_{Rj})(\bar{\psi}_{k}\gamma_{\mu}\gamma_{5}\psi_{l})\,,

(2.25)

\displaystyle{\cal O}^{3V}_{ijkl}

\displaystyle=(\bar{d}_{Ri}\gamma^{\mu}d_{Rj})(\bar{\psi}_{k}\gamma_{\mu}\psi_{l})\,,\quad{\cal O}^{3A}_{ijkl}=(\bar{d}_{Ri}\gamma^{\mu}d_{Rj})(\bar{\psi}_{k}\gamma_{\mu}\gamma_{5}\psi_{l})\,.

(2.26)

The scalar-scalar operators are now dimension-7, and are given by

\displaystyle{\cal O}^{4S}_{ijkl}=(\bar{q}_{Li}\widetilde{H}u_{Rj})(\bar{\psi}_{k}\psi_{l})\,,

\displaystyle\quad{\cal O}^{\overline{4S}}_{ijkl}=(\bar{u}_{Ri}\widetilde{H}^{\dagger}q_{Lj})(\bar{\psi}_{k}\psi_{l})=\left[{\cal O}^{4S}_{jilk}\right]^{\dagger}\,,

(2.27)

\displaystyle{\cal O}^{4P}_{ijkl}=(\bar{q}_{Li}\widetilde{H}u_{Rj})(\bar{\psi}_{k}i\gamma_{5}\psi_{l})\,,

\displaystyle\quad{\cal O}^{\overline{4P}}_{ijkl}=(\bar{u}_{Ri}\widetilde{H}^{\dagger}q_{Lj})(\bar{\psi}_{k}i\gamma_{5}\psi_{l})=\left[{\cal O}^{4P}_{jilk}\right]^{\dagger}\,,

(2.28)

\displaystyle{\cal O}^{5S}_{ijkl}=(\bar{q}_{Li}Hd_{Rj})(\bar{\psi}_{k}\psi_{l})\,,

\displaystyle\quad{\cal O}^{\overline{5S}}_{ijkl}=(\bar{d}_{Ri}H^{\dagger}q_{Lj})(\bar{\psi}_{k}\psi_{l})=\left[{\cal O}^{5S}_{jilk}\right]^{\dagger}\,,

(2.29)

\displaystyle{\cal O}^{5P}_{ijkl}=(\bar{q}_{Li}Hd_{Rj})(\bar{\psi}_{k}i\gamma_{5}\psi_{l})\,,

\displaystyle\quad{\cal O}^{\overline{5P}}_{ijkl}=(\bar{d}_{Ri}H^{\dagger}q_{Lj})(\bar{\psi}_{k}i\gamma_{5}\psi_{l})=\left[{\cal O}^{5P}_{jilk}\right]^{\dagger}\,.

(2.30)

For simplicity, we neglect tensor operators.
Hermiticity requires that

\begin{split}C_{ijkl}^{1V}=\left[C_{jilk}^{1V}\right]^{*}\,,&\,C_{ijkl}^{1A}=\left[C_{jilk}^{1A}\right]^{*}\,,\,C_{ijkl}^{2V}=\left[C_{jilk}^{2V}\right]^{*}\,,\,C_{ijkl}^{2A}=\left[C_{jilk}^{2A}\right]^{*}\,,\\
C_{ijkl}^{3V}=\left[C_{jilk}^{3V}\right]^{*}\,,&\,C_{ijkl}^{3A}=\left[C_{jilk}^{3A}\right]^{*}\,,\,C_{ijkl}^{4S}=\left[C_{jilk}^{\overline{4S}}\right]^{*}\,,\,C_{ijkl}^{4P}=\left[C_{jilk}^{\overline{4P}}\right]^{*}\,,\\
C_{ijkl}^{5S}=\left[C_{jilk}^{\overline{5S}}\right]^{*}\,,&\,C_{ijkl}^{5P}=\left[C_{jilk}^{\overline{5P}}\right]^{*}\,.\end{split}

(2.31)

Of these operators,
{\cal O}^{1V,1A}_{ijkl}
,
{\cal O}^{3V,3A}_{ijkl}
and
{\cal O}^{5S,5P}_{ijkl}
provide flavor violating interactions to the down quarks at the tree level, so that hereafter we only consider these three operators.

2.2.1
\psi\sim({\textbf{3}},{\textbf{1}},{\textbf{1}})

The leading coefficients are given by

\displaystyle C_{ijkl}^{1V,1A}

\displaystyle=c^{1V,1A}_{1}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+c^{1V,1A}_{2}\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}+\dots\ ,

(2.32)

\displaystyle C_{ijkl}^{3V,3A}

\displaystyle=c^{3V,3A}_{1}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\dots\ ,

(2.33)

\displaystyle C_{ijkl}^{5S,5P}

\displaystyle=c^{5S,5P}_{1}(Y_{d})_{ij}\delta_{kl}+c^{5S,5P}_{2}(Y_{d})_{kj}\delta_{il}+\dots\ ,

(2.34)

where the ellipses indicate further Yukawa matrix insertions and
c^{n}_{m}
are taken to be real.

Again, we take
(Y_{d})_{ij}=y_{u}^{i}\delta_{ij}
and
Y_{u}=V^{\dagger}\hat{Y}_{u}
without loss of generality.
In the unitary gauge
\langle H\rangle=(0,v/\sqrt{2})^{T}
and
in the quark mass basis (

In this section we present the results of our analysis.
In each case, we evaluate the improvement of our model’s description compared to the SM.
To do so, we construct a likelihood
\mathcal{L}_{\text{BSM}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{c})
as described in Appendix
A
, where
\boldsymbol{\theta}

The improvement is then measured by the test statistic

\Delta\equiv-2\ln\frac{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{BSM}}}{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{SM}}}\ ,

(4.1)

where a hat indicates the value of a likelihood at the best-fit point of the data.
Negative values of

2
2

2

When the usual regularity conditions are satisfied,

5
Conclusions

Current rare-decay programs, in particular at NA62 and Belle II, provide an exceptional window into missing-energy signatures potentially linked to dark matter emission.
Within this context, we have investigated flavored dark matter in the MFV framework, focusing on the processes
d_{i}\to d_{j}+\not{E}
.
The MFV hypothesis offers a theoretically robust and predictive structure for incorporating new sources of flavor violation and naturally stabilizes the
lightest component of a new flavored multiplet, making it an appealing setting for dark matter model building.

Our results show that the MFV setup can account for the observed excesses in either
K^{+}\to\pi^{+}\nu\bar{\nu}
or
B^{+}\to K^{+}\nu\bar{\nu}
individually, providing descriptions that are favored with respect to the SM by
\sim 3\sigma
. However, a satisfactory simultaneous explanation of both channels cannot be achieved within a minimal construction containing only a single dark matter multiplet with degenerate components.
Reconciling both excesses may require either introducing an additional multiplet with a different mass for each flavor, or generating sizeable mass splittings within a single multiplet.
While such extensions remain fully consistent with MFV, they move away from strict minimality and may appear less economical from a theoretical point of view. Therefore, we do not pursue these non‑minimal possibilities further in this work.

Despite these structural limitations, our findings highlight the rich interplay between flavored dark matter, MFV-based model building, and precision flavor experiments.
They demonstrate that flavored dark matter remains a compelling and testable paradigm, capable of addressing both collider-scale anomalies and the cosmological dark matter abundance.

Our analysis relies on a likelihood model built from available experimental data on
d_{i}\to d_{j}+\not{E}
channels, revisiting old datasets and incorporating the latest results to provide a comprehensive and up-to-date phenomenological assessment of the MFV DM scenario.

Likelihood function

In this section we present the details of the likelihood constructed for the analysis of this work.
The total likelihood is given by the product of the likelihood of each experimental measurement times a likelihood term for the SM prediction of the di-neutrino modes, i.e.

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{Belle~II}}\ \mathcal{L}_{\text{BaBar}}\ \mathcal{L}_{\text{Belle}}\ \mathcal{L}_{\text{NA62}}\ \mathcal{L}_{\text{KOTO}}\ \mathcal{L}_{\nu\bar{\nu}}\ .

(A.1)

Each term is presented and explained below.

A.1
Belle II

We construct the likelihood term for the Belle II measurement based on the public data of Fig. 17 (ITA) and Fig. 20 (HTA) from Ref.
[
4
]
, similarly to Ref.
[
3
]
. The likelihood function is constructed as the product of two contributions, i.e.

{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{Belle~II}}={\mathcal{L}}_{\text{Belle~II}}^{(1)}{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{Belle~II}}^{(2)}\ .

(A.2)

The first and main term in the likelihood function represents a Poisson distribution
P
for each bin, labeled by an index
i
. Assuming the bins to be uncorrelated, we get

\mathcal{L}_{\text{Belle~II}}^{(1)}=\prod_{i}^{n_{\rm bin}}P(n^{i}_{\rm obs};\lambda^{i})\ .

(A.3)

Here
n^{i}_{\rm obs}
is the number of observed events in bin
i
, defined by
q^{2}\in[q^{2}_{i},q^{2}_{i+1})
, and
\lambda^{i}
is the corresponding expected number of events

\begin{split}\lambda^{i}~=~n^{i}_{\text{DM}}+n^{i}_{\nu\bar{\nu}}+(1+\sigma^{\text{bkg}}\,\theta)\,n^{i}_{\text{bkg}}~.\end{split}

(A.4)

This expression applies to either the ITA or HTA dataset, where
n^{i}_{\text{DM}}
,
n^{i}_{\nu\bar{\nu}}
, and
n^{i}_{\rm bkg}
are the yields in the
i^{\rm th}
bin for the signal, the
B^{+}\to K^{+}\nu\bar{\nu}
decay, and the background, respectively. Within the two methods, the first two components on the r.h.s. of Eq. (
A.4
) are defined as

\displaystyle n^{i}_{X,\text{ITA}}=

\displaystyle N_{B}\int\limits_{\text{$i^{\text{th}}$ bin}}dq^{2}_{{\rm rec}}\int dq^{2}\,f_{q^{2}_{{\rm rec}}}(q^{2})\,\epsilon_{\text{ITA}}(q^{2})\,\frac{d{\cal B}_{X}}{dq^{2}}~,

(A.5)

\displaystyle n^{i}_{X,\text{HTA}}=

\displaystyle N_{B}\int\limits_{\text{$i^{\text{th}}$ bin}}dq^{2}\,\epsilon_{\text{HTA}}(q^{2})\,\frac{d{\cal B}_{X}}{dq^{2}}~.

(A.6)

In order to get an estimate of the uncertainty on the total background normalisation for ITA and HTA, we identify from Figs. 17 and 20 of Ref.
[
4
]

\text{pull}=\frac{|n^{i}_{\rm dat}-n^{i}_{\rm hst}|}{\sqrt{(\sigma^{i}_{\rm dat})^{2}+(\sigma^{i}_{\rm hst})^{2}}}\ ,

(A.7)

where the subscripts ‘dat’ and ‘hst’ denote respectively the data and the value of the stacked histograms, and
\sigma^{i}_{\rm dat}
the uncertainty in the data point. By inverting the pull distribution, we get
\sigma_{\text{ITA}}^{\text{bkg}}\approx 1\%
and
\sigma_{\text{HTA}}^{\text{bkg}}\approx 13\%
.
Thus, we introduced two nuisance parameters, i.e.
\theta_{\text{ITA}}
and
\theta_{\text{HTA}}
, both constrained by a normal Gaussian distribution in the likelihood as

{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{Belle~II}}^{(2)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\theta^{2}_{\text{ITA}}/2}\times\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\theta^{2}_{\text{HTA}}/2}\,.

(A.8)

Ref.
[
3
]
has shown that this procedure applied to the
B^{+}\to K^{+}\nu\bar{\nu}
analysis produces results in good agreement with those of Belle II.

A.3
Belle

In the case of Belle search, we have no access to the kinematic distribution of the events and, additionally, their measurements only put upper limits on the decay rates.
Therefore we construct a likelihood term that constraints only the total BR of the DM signal such as

{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{BaBar,k}}^{(2)}\propto\prod_{k}\text{exp}\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{{\cal B}_{\text{DM}}}{\sigma_{{\cal B}_{\text{DM}}}}\right)^{2}\right\}

(A.14)

where
k
labels the decay modes and we estimate

\sigma_{{\cal B}_{\text{DM}}}\approx\frac{{\cal B}_{\text{exp}}-{\cal B}_{\nu\bar{\nu}}}{\sqrt{\chi^{2}(90\%,1\text{dof})}}\ ,

(A.15)

with
{\cal B}_{\text{exp}}
the experimental limit in Eqs. (
3.6
)-(
3.9
) and
\chi^{2}(90\%,1\text{dof})
the value of a chi-squared variable at 90%CL with 1 degree of freedom.

From Ref.
[
16
]
, the SM predictions of the neutrino decay modes are

\displaystyle{\cal B}(B^{+}\to\pi^{+}\nu\bar{\nu})

\displaystyle=1.2\times 10^{-7}\ ,

(A.16)

\displaystyle{\cal B}(B^{0}\to\pi^{0}\nu\bar{\nu})

\displaystyle=5.6\times 10^{-8}\ ,

(A.17)

\displaystyle{\cal B}(B^{+}\to\rho^{+}\nu\bar{\nu})

\displaystyle=4.9\times 10^{-7}\ ,

(A.18)

\displaystyle{\cal B}(B^{0}\to\rho^{0}\nu\bar{\nu})

\displaystyle=2.3\times 10^{-7}\ .

(A.19)

A.4
NA62

To construct the likelihood for NA62, we follow a variation of the procedure of Ref.
[
48
]
similarly to Ref.
[
64
]
.
We perform a fully frequentist hypothesis test using a shape analysis on the invisible invariant mass, and an unbinned profile likelihood ratio test statistic, based solely on public data. The likelihood function is constructed as the product of three contributions, i.e.

\mathcal{L}_{\text{NA62}}={\cal L}_{\text{NA62}}^{(1)}\ {\cal L}_{\text{NA62}}^{(2)}\ {\cal L}_{\text{NA62}}^{(3)}\ .

(A.20)

The first term is a Poisson distribution for the total number of events
n_{\text{tot}}
with mean value equal to the total number of observed events, which reads

\mathcal{L}_{\text{NA62}}^{(1)}=P(n_{\rm obs};n_{\text{tot}})\ .

(A.21)

As reported in Ref.
[
49
]
, NA62 observed a total number of events equal to
n_{\text{obs}}=51
in the full 2016-2022 dataset.
The variable
n_{\text{tot}}
can be decomposed as
n_{\text{tot}}=n_{b}+n_{\nu\bar{\nu}}+n_{\text{DM}}
, which are the number of background, neutrino and DM events, respectively.
The numbers of events
n_{\nu\bar{\nu}}
and
n_{\text{DM}}
are obtained from the BR by integrating Eq. (
3.2
) over the signal region.

Given
n_{\text{tot}}
, its components are distributed as a function of the invisible invariant mass squared
m_{{\rm inv}}^{2}
according to a multinomial likelihood, yielding
{\cal L}_{\text{NA62}}^{(2)}
as

{\cal L}_{\text{NA62}}^{(2)}=\prod_{j=1}^{n_{\text{obs}}}\left[\frac{n_{b}}{n_{\text{tot}}}g_{b}(m_{j}^{2})+\frac{n_{\nu\bar{\nu}}}{n_{\text{tot}}}g_{\nu\bar{\nu}}(m_{j}^{2})+\frac{n_{\text{DM}}}{n_{\text{tot}}}g_{\text{DM}}(m_{j}^{2})\right]\ ,

(A.22)

where
g_{b}(m_{\rm inv}^{2})
is a function reproducing the distribution of the background events, and normalized to unit integral over the signal regions. We took this function from published data, i.e. from the right panel of Fig. 10 in Ref.
[
49
]
.
Conversely,
g_{\nu\bar{\nu},\text{DM}}(m_{\rm inv}^{2})
is the BR shape normalized to unit integral over the signal regions.
Finally, the last likelihood factor is a Poisson-distributed constraint term for the number of background events

\mathcal{L}_{\text{NA62}}^{(3)}=\frac{(\tau n_{b})^{n_{\text{off}}}}{n_{\text{off}}!}e^{-\tau n_{b}}\ ,

(A.23)

with
\tau=\mu_{b}/\sigma_{b}^{2}
and
n_{\text{off}}=(\mu_{b}/\sigma_{b})^{2}

[
51
]
obtained from the estimated mean value
\mu_{b}
and uncertainty
\sigma_{b}
of the number of background events. From Ref
[
49
]
we take
\mu_{b}=18.0
and
\sigma_{b}=2.5
, with the latter obtained by averaging over the asymmetric uncertainty.

A.5
KOTO

The likelihood for the KOTO measurement is constructed similarly to the one for NA62. In this case, acpording to Ref.
[
5
]
, we set
n_{\text{obs}}=0
,
\mu_{b}=0.252
and
\sigma_{b}=0.08
, where the latter is obtained by averaging over the asymmetric statistical uncertainty and then adding in quadrature the statistical and systematic uncertainties.
