Variational Principles for the Helmholtz equation: application to Finite Element and Neural Network approximations


Abstract
In this paper, we investigate whether Variational Principles can be associated with the Helmholtz equation subject to impedance (absorbing) boundary conditions. This model has been extensively studied in the literature from both mathematical and computational perspectives. It is classical with wide applications, yet accurate approximation at high wavenumbers remains challenging. We address the question of whether there exist energy functionals with a clear physical interpretation whose stationary points , the zeros of their first variation, correspond to solutions of the Helmholtz problem. Starting from Hamilton’s principle for the wave equation, we derive time-harmonic energies. The resulting functionals are generally indefinite . As a next step, we construct strongly coercive augmentations of these indefinite functionals that preserve their physical interpretation. Finally, we show how these variational principles lead to practical numerical methods based on finite element spaces and neural network architectures.

Introduction

1.1
Helmholtz equation and Variational Principles

We consider the Helmholtz equation with an impedance (absorbing) boundary condition and the associated boundary-value problem. We seek a complex-valued function
u
such that

\left\{\begin{aligned} -&\Delta u-k^{2}u=f\quad&&\text{in}&&\varOmega\\[2.0pt]
&\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u=iku\quad&&\text{on }&&\partial\varOmega\end{aligned}\right.

(1)

where
u:\varOmega\to\mathbb{C}
,
\varOmega\subset\mathbb{R}^{\nu}
is a bounded Lipschitz domain,
f\in L^{2}(\varOmega)
is a complex-valued function and
k>0
is the wavenumber.
We use the compact notation
{\mathscr{L}}u:=-\Delta u-k^{2}u
.
Equation (
) is the time-harmonic wave equation with an impedance boundary condition. While this model is classical and broadly applicable, the design of numerical algorithms capable of accurately approximating it at high wavenumbers remains a significant challenge.

In this work, we investigate whether
Variational Principles
can be associated with (
). Specifically, we ask whether there exist energies with a clear physical interpretation whose
stationary points
, the zeros of their first variation, coincide with solutions of (
). To the best of our knowledge, such principles have not been systematically developed for (
). Starting from Hamilton’s principle for the wave equation, we derive time-harmonic energies that are generally
indefinite
. We then construct strongly coercive augmentations of these energies whose unique minimiser coincides with the solution of (
). These regularised energies are carefully designed modifications of the original indefinite functionals and preserve their physical relevance, since the regularisation terms vanish on the solution manifold
{\mathscr{L}}u=f
.

Apart from its theoretical appeal, the derivation of new variational principles has significant practical implications. Modern methods that utilise neural-network-based discrete spaces typically rely on discrete minimisation problems, where the loss functional is constructed either by approximating a continuous energy associated with the problem or by using the
L^{2}
residual of the differential operator
[
44
]
. While the residual-based approach is straightforward, it may be ineffective for PDEs with highly oscillatory behaviour such as (
). In contrast, employing a variational principle with strong coercivity provides a more robust foundation for efficient and reliable methods. We show that the proposed variational principle yields an effective neural-network formulation for (
). We also present initial evidence that the same principle leads to efficient finite element formulations and outline how its properties motivate discretisations with improved characteristics. Strongly coercive bilinear forms for the Helmholtz equation were previously developed in
[
38
]
, leading to finite element methods that, in general, produce non-symmetric matrices. While we employ similar analytical tools, namely the Rellich and Morawetz identities, which are effective in the analysis of (
), our motivation and design strategy differ substantially, see Remark
and Section 4.1.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows. We derive variational principles for the Helmholtz equation (
) from first principles, using Hamilton’s principle (least action) and a Lagrangian formulation of the wave equation to motivate the associated energy functionals. As expected, these functionals are generally indefinite; we next construct regularised versions that are strongly coercive while preserving their physical relevance.
Our main idea is to perturb the physical Lagrangian by adding a term of the form

\gamma\|{\mathscr{L}}u-f\|^{2}.
This regularisation enhances the stability of the variational principle, though in a nontrivial way. It turns out that, by choosing
\gamma
suitably, one can obtain a coercive functional with respect to an appropriately strengthened norm. The resulting coercivity arises from a combination of the original physical Lagrangian and suitable Morawetz identities, which are employed to derive lower bounds first for the bulk energy terms and subsequently for the boundary contributions.

The resulting energies form the basis for well-posed minimisation problems whose unique minimisers coincide with solutions of the Helmholtz equation.

Finally, we show how these variational principles yield discrete approximation schemes. We present a unified framework that encompasses classical conforming finite elements and neural-network discretisations. The primary focus is the mathematical analysis underpinning the design of the new variational principles, but we also include prototype formulations for both finite elements and neural networks to illustrate their potential for computing approximations to (
). It is worth emphasising that neural network approximations depend on appropriate minimisation principles, and our approach constitutes the only known alternative so far to standard least-squares PINN formulations. Preliminary results suggest several desirable properties, a detailed numerical investigation is deferred to future work.

2
Energy principles

2.1
The basic model: Transverse vibrations of a string

In this section, we explain how the wave and the Helmholtz equations can be derived from Hamilton’s principle. Such a derivation provides us with a natural Lagrangian, which in turn arises in the standard variational reformulation of certain boundary value problems for the Helmholtz equation. For simplicity, we restrict the discussion to the case
\nu=1
, and we consider the transverse motion of a string occupying the interval
\Omega=(\alpha,\beta)
along the
x
-axis. Analogous results can be derived for
\nu=2
or
\nu=3
, corresponding to the transverse motion of a membrane or to sound waves in an ideal compressible fluid, respectively.
We assume that the mass density (per unit length) of the string is
\rho(x)
and that the string is subjected to tension by opposite forces of strength
p
at its ends. Then, the transverse deflection
U(x,t)
satisfies the wave equation

\frac{1}{c^{2}(x)}\frac{\partial^{2}U}{\partial t^{2}}=\frac{\partial^{2}U}{\partial x^{2}}{-\frac{F(x,t)}{p}},\ \ t>0\ ,\ \ \alpha<x<\beta,

(2)

where
c(x)=\left(p/\rho(x)\right)^{1/2}
is the phase velocity of the waves propagating along the string, and
F(x,t)
denotes an external transverse load per unit length (the sign convention in (
) is immaterial for what follows). We assume that
c
and
F
are sufficiently smooth.
Various combinations of
U(x,t)
or
\partial U/\partial x(x,t)
at the ends
x=\alpha,\beta
for all
t>0
, and also initial data for
U(x,0)
and
\partial U/\partial t(x,0)
for all
\alpha<x<\beta
, must be prescribed to determine a unique solution of the problem.

In many applications, as well as for fundamental theoretical reasons in scattering theory, it is necessary to study the corresponding time-harmonic problem, and, in particular, the associated Green’s function. To this end, we consider the motion of a string that starts from rest at time
t=0
, subject to a transverse force distribution which varies periodically in time with frequency
\omega
. Such a force is represented by the distribution
F(x,t)=\Re\bigl(e^{-i\omega t}f(x)\bigr)H(t)
, where, typically,
f(x)
is a localised function and
H(t)
is the Heaviside function. If the Limiting Amplitude Principle applies, then for sufficiently large
t
the motion becomes time-harmonic of the form
U(x,t)=\Re\bigl(u(x)e^{-i\omega t}\bigr)
, where the complex amplitude
u(x)
satisfies the Helmholtz equation

u^{\prime\prime}(x)+k^{2}(x)u(x)=\frac{f(x)}{p},\qquad k(x)=\frac{\omega}{c(x)},\quad\alpha<x<\beta\ ,

(3)

and certain boundary conditions dictated by
U(x,t)
and
\partial U/\partial x(x,t)
at
x=\alpha,\beta
. Whenever
\alpha=-\infty
and/or
\beta=+\infty
, the boundary conditions must be replaced by outgoing (Sommerfeld-type) radiation conditions to ensure uniqueness of the solution.

2.2
Variational derivation of the wave equation

Let

\mathcal{T}(x,t)=\frac{1}{2}\rho(x)\Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial t}\Bigr)^{2}\ ,

(4)

be the
kinetic energy density
(that is, energy per unit length), and

\mathcal{V}(x,t)={\frac{1}{2}p\Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}\Bigr)^{2}}

(5)

be the
potential energy density
of the string; the external loading enters through the work term in the Lagrangian rather than as stored potential energy.

Now, it can be shown that the wave equation (

DI[U]=0
, where the action

(6)

is the space-time integral of the
Lagrangian density

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}\Bigl(x,{U},\frac{\partial U}{\partial t},\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}\Bigr)&=\mathcal{T}(x,t)-\mathcal{V}(x,t){-F(x,t)U(x,t)}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\rho(x)\Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial t}\Bigr)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}p\Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}\Bigr)^{2}{-F(x,t)U(x,t)}\ ,\end{split}

(7)

for any time interval
0<t<T
,
T<\infty

[
27
,
16
]
.
In fact, the Euler-Lagrange equation,
[
13
, Section 8.1]
,

{DI[U]=0}

(8)

implies the wave equation

\frac{1}{c^{2}(x)}\frac{\partial^{2}U}{\partial t^{2}}-\frac{\partial^{2}U}{\partial x^{2}}={-\frac{F}{p}}.

(9)

Indeed, (
) implies for any test function with compact support
\eta=\eta(x,t)
that

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\Bigl\{\rho(x)\frac{\partial U}{\partial t}\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}-p\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x}{-F(x,t)\eta}\Bigr\}{\mathrm{d}}x{\mathrm{d}}t=0\ ,

(10)

i.e. (

2.3
Variational derivation of the Helmholtz equation

The Helmholtz equation can be derived from the time-harmonic version of Hamilton’s principle. We choose
T=\frac{2\pi}{\omega}
, the period of the time-harmonic wave, and define the stationary (time-averaged) density
\ell
as

\ell\Bigl(x,u,\tfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}\Bigr):=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{L}\Bigl(x,{U},\frac{\partial U}{\partial t},\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}\Bigr){\mathrm{d}}t,

(11)

the time mean of the Lagrangian density
\mathcal{L}
.

Substituting the time-harmonic ansatz
U(x,t)=\Re\bigl(u(x)e^{-i\omega t}\bigr)
into (
) and averaging over one period yields

\ell\Bigl(x,u,\tfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}\Bigr)={\frac{p}{4}\Bigl(k(x)^{2}|u|^{2}-\bigl|\tfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}\bigr|^{2}\Bigr)-\frac{1}{2}\Re\bigl(f(x)\overline{u(x)}\bigr),}\qquad k(x)=\frac{\omega}{c(x)}.

(12)

(Any positive constant multiple of
\ell
produces the same stationary points, but the coefficients in (
) match the time average of (
).)

We now derive the Helmholtz equation from the stationary variational principle

{D\mathcal{I}_{S}[u]=0,}

(13)

where

\mathcal{I}_{S}[u]:=\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\ell\Bigl(x,u,\tfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}\Bigr){\mathrm{d}}x.

(14)

This principle arises naturally by rewriting the action
I
in (
) using the time average,

\displaystyle I=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\mathcal{L}\Bigl(x,{U},\frac{\partial U}{\partial t},\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}\Bigr){\mathrm{d}}x{\mathrm{d}}t=\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\ell\Bigl(x,u,\tfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}\Bigr){\mathrm{d}}x.

(15)

With variations taken with respect to the real inner product on
H^{1}((\alpha,\beta);\mathbb{C})
, the Euler-Lagrange equation for (
) is precisely the Helmholtz equation in the sense of distributions. See Section
for the detailed computation and
[
39
]
for an analogous variational derivation of the Schrödinger equation from a complex Lagrangian.

2.4
Impedance boundary conditions

The
total energy density
(per unit length) of the string at time
t
is

\mathcal{E}(x,t)=\mathcal{T}(x,t)+\mathcal{V}(x,t).

(16)

For
F\equiv 0
, the total energy
E(t)=\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\mathcal{E}(x,t){\mathrm{d}}x
is constant for suitable choices of initial and boundary conditions. In fact, by direct calculation and using (
),

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}E(t)}{{\mathrm{d}}t}=\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\Biggl(\frac{1}{2}\rho(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial t}\Bigr)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}p\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}\Bigr)^{2}\Biggr){\mathrm{d}}x=p\Biggl[\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}\frac{\partial U}{\partial t}\Biggr]_{x=\alpha}^{x=\beta}.

(17)

If, for example, either the deflection
U
or the slope
\partial U/\partial x
at the endpoints is zero for all
t>0
, then the last term in (
) vanishes.
For an infinite string, where
\alpha=-\infty
and
\beta=+\infty
, the derivative
\partial U/\partial t
vanishes at both ends due to the finite speed of propagation.

To model absorbing (dissipative) endpoints, the impedance law must be consistent with the
outward
unit normal at each end. In one dimension the outward normal is
+1
at
x=\beta
and
-1
at
x=\alpha
. Accordingly, we prescribe

\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}=-\mu\frac{\partial U}{\partial t}\quad\text{at }x=\beta,\qquad\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}=+\mu\frac{\partial U}{\partial t}\quad\text{at }x=\alpha,

(18)

where
\mu>0
. Substituting (
) into (
) gives

{\frac{{\mathrm{d}}E(t)}{{\mathrm{d}}t}=p\bigl(-\mu(\partial_{t}U)^{2}(\beta,t)-\mu(\partial_{t}U)^{2}(\alpha,t)\bigr)\leq 0,}

with strict decay unless
\partial_{t}U(\alpha,t)=\partial_{t}U(\beta,t)=0
; thus (
) is dissipative.

For the time-harmonic problem, the boundary conditions (
) imply the following conditions for the Helmholtz equation:

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}u}{{\mathrm{d}}x}=i\sigma_{\beta}k_{\beta}u\quad\text{at }x=\beta,\qquad\frac{{\mathrm{d}}u}{{\mathrm{d}}x}=-i\sigma_{\alpha}k_{\alpha}u\quad\text{at }x=\alpha,

(19)

where
k_{\alpha}=\omega/c(\alpha)
,
k_{\beta}=\omega/c(\beta)
, and
\sigma_{\alpha}=\mu c(\alpha)
,
\sigma_{\beta}=\mu c(\beta)
. These signs reflect the outward normals at the two ends. In higher dimensions the analogous law is the Robin (impedance) condition

{\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u=i\sigma ku\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,}

with
\mathbf{n}
the outward unit normal and
\sigma>0
. The canonical choice
\sigma\equiv 1
yields the boundary condition used in (
).

Regularised energy for Helmholtz

The physical energy
\mathcal{E}_{P}
derived in Section
provides a variational characterisation of the Helmholtz
equation with clear physical meaning. However, it is indefinite and lacks coercivity, which presents
difficulties for both analysis and numerical approximation. To address this, we introduce a regularised energy functional that penalises the residual of the Helmholtz operator in a least-squares
sense. While this regularised energy differs from the physical one, its stationary point coincides with the solution of (
) and it admits a coercivity theory that makes it suitable for minimisation over appropriate function spaces.
Specifically, we consider the following regularised energy associated with the Helmholtz equation:

\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}(v)=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(|\nabla v|^{2}-k^{2}|v|^{2}\Bigr){\mathrm{d}}x-{\Re\int_{\Omega}f\overline{v}{\mathrm{d}}x}+\gamma\int_{\Omega}\bigl|{\mathscr{L}}v-f\bigr|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}x.

(29)

The added least-squares term vanishes exactly on the solution manifold
{\mathscr{L}}v=f.

We will show that for
\gamma\geq\gamma_{0}{>0}
the functional
\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}
is strongly coercive over
{\mathscr{V}}_{BC}
, the subspace of
{\mathscr{V}}
consisting of functions satisfying the boundary condition

\partial_{\mathbf{n}}v=ikv\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.

In particular, the quadratic part of
\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}
(the case
f=0
) controls the norm
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathscr{V}}_{BC}}
introduced earlier.

Consider the energy
\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}
defined in (

(30)

4
Proofs for strongly coercive variational principles

4.1
Design strategy

Our construction of the enhanced energy functionals is intrinsically connected to the analysis establishing their coercivity.
The key idea is to observe that the energy
\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}(u)
contains the indefinite term

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\!\left({\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}-k^{2}\left|u\right|^{2}}\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,,

(46)

and at the same time a standard Rellich identity, see (
), can be written as

\int_{\Omega}\!\left({(\nu-2)\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}-\nu k^{2}\left|u\right|^{2}}\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\\
+\int_{\Omega}2\Re\!\left({\!\left({\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\nabla u}\right)\overline{{\mathscr{L}}u}}\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}x+B_{R}(u)=0,

(47)

where
B_{R}(u)
accounts for the boundary terms in (
).
Then , an appropriate linear combination of the energy for
f=0
(see (
)) and the identity (
)
has two key advantages: the energy changes only by a constant factor, since the contribution of (
) is zero,
and the coefficients of
\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}
and
k^{2}\left|u\right|^{2}
can both be made constants of the same sign.
The least-squares regularisation term in the energy functional makes it possible to control the second term in (
).
Moreover, the boundary term in (
) can also be controlled, particularly through the use of another
lower-order Morawetz identity — a fact that is not immediately apparent. It is important to emphasise that, within this framework, the structure
of the regularised energy is preserved, and coercivity is ensured for all
\gamma\geq\gamma_{0}
, where the constant
\gamma_{0}

can be determined explicitly.

4.1.1
Rellich and Morawetz Identities

Let
{\mathscr{L}}u=-\Delta u-k^{2}u
be the Helmholtz operator applied to a
sufficiently smooth function
u\colon\mathbb{R}^{\nu}\to\mathbb{C}
. Then the following identity holds:

\displaystyle\!\left({\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\overline{\nabla u}}\right){\mathscr{L}}u+\!\left({\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\nabla u}\right)\overline{{\mathscr{L}}u}

\displaystyle=-\text{\rm div}\!\left({(\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\overline{\nabla u})\nabla u+(\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\nabla u)\overline{\nabla u}-\boldsymbol{x}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}+k^{2}\boldsymbol{x}\left|u\right|^{2}}\right)-(\nu-2)\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}+\nu k^{2}\left|u\right|^{2},

(48)

where
\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\nabla u=\sum_{{j=1}}^{\nu}x_{j}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{j}}
is the directional derivative of
u
along
\boldsymbol{x}

Although this identity is well known
[
9

51
,
38
]
as the simplest form of a Morawetz (or Rellich) identity, we briefly outline the main steps of its derivation to highlight the role of the Morawetz multiplier.

To begin, let
\Phi
denote the left-hand side of identity (
4.1.1
). Using the definition of the Helmholtz operator
{\mathscr{L}}
, we have

\Phi=\!\left({\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\overline{\nabla u}}\right)\!\left({-\Delta u-k^{2}u}\right)+\!\left({\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\nabla u}\right)\!\left({-\Delta\overline{u}-k^{2}\overline{u}}\right).

(49)

Expanding each term gives

\Phi=-\!\left({\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\overline{\nabla u}}\right)\Delta u-k^{2}\!\left({\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\overline{\nabla u}}\right)u-\!\left({\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\nabla u}\right)\Delta\overline{u}-k^{2}\!\left({\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\nabla u}\right)\overline{u}.

(50)

The terms involving the Laplacian expand as follows:

-\!\left({\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\overline{\nabla u}}\right)\Delta u-\!\left({\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\nabla u}\right)\Delta\overline{u}=-\text{\rm div}\!\left({\!\left({\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\overline{\nabla u}}\right)\nabla u+\!\left({\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\nabla u}\right)\overline{\nabla u}{-\boldsymbol{x}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}}}\right){-(\nu-2)}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2},

(51)

where we have used that

\text{\rm div}\!\left({\boldsymbol{x}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}}\right)={\nu}\,\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}+\!\left({\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\nabla}\right)\!\left({\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}}\right).

(52)

For the remaining two terms, we observe that they can be written in the form

-k^{2}\!\left({\!\left({\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\overline{\nabla u}}\right)u+\!\left({\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\nabla u}\right)\overline{u}}\right)=-k^{2}\!\left({\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\nabla\!\left({\left|u\right|^{2}}\right)}\right).

(53)

Rewriting this in divergence form, we obtain

-k^{2}\!\left({\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\nabla\!\left({\left|u\right|^{2}}\right)}\right)=-\text{\rm div}\!\left({k^{2}\boldsymbol{x}\left|u\right|^{2}}\right)+\nu k^{2}\left|u\right|^{2}.

(54)

Combining all the contributions derived above, we recover identity (
4.1.1

Integration over
\Omega

[

51

38

yields the integrated form of identity (

Proposition 5
(Rellich/Morawetz Identity)

\int_{\Omega}2\Re\!\left({\!\left({\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\nabla u}\right)\overline{{\mathscr{L}}u}}\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}x+\int_{\Omega}\!\left({(\nu-2)\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}-\nu k^{2}\left|u\right|^{2}}\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\\
+\int_{\partial\Omega}\!\left({2\Re\!\left({\!\left({\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\overline{\nabla u}}\right)\!\left({\nabla u\cdot\boldsymbol{n}}\right)}\right)-\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}\!\left({\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}}\right)+k^{2}\left|u\right|^{2}\!\left({\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}}\right)}\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}S=0,

To control the mixed volume term in (

Lemma 12
(Control of Weighted Gradient Norm)

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{\nu}
be a bounded domain and let
L=\mathrm{diam}(\Omega)
. Then, for any sufficiently smooth function

{\int_{\Omega}\big|(\boldsymbol{x}-x_{0})\cdot\nabla u\big|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}x\leq L^{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}x.}

(64)

Proof

By definition of the diameter,
\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{x}-x_{0}\|\leq L
for any
x_{0}\in\overline{\Omega}

{|(\boldsymbol{x}-x_{0})\cdot\nabla u|\leq\|\boldsymbol{x}-x_{0}\|\|\nabla u\|\leq L\|\nabla u\|,}

and the stated inequality follows upon squaring and integrating over
\Omega

Proposition 13
(Coercivity of Bulk Terms)

Let

Proof

We bound the last term in the bulk energy,

E_{\gamma,\Omega}(\nu,\alpha;u)=\nu\gamma\left\|{\mathscr{L}}u\right\|^{2}+\!\left({\frac{\nu}{2}-\alpha(\nu-2)}\right)\left\|\nabla u\right\|^{2}+\!\left({\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\nu k^{2}\left\|u\right\|^{2}-\alpha\int_{\Omega}2\Re\!\left({(\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\nabla u)\overline{{\mathscr{L}}u}}\right){\mathrm{d}}x.

By Young’s inequality, for any
\varepsilon_{2}>0

\bigg|\int_{\Omega}2\Re\!\left({(\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\nabla u)\overline{{\mathscr{L}}u}}\right){\mathrm{d}}x\bigg|\leq\varepsilon_{2}\int_{\Omega}\left|\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\nabla u\right|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}x+\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{2}}\int_{\Omega}\left|{\mathscr{L}}u\right|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}x.

Using Lemma

\bigg|\int_{\Omega}2\Re\!\left({(\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\nabla u)\overline{{\mathscr{L}}u}}\right){\mathrm{d}}x\bigg|\leq\varepsilon_{1}\left\|\nabla u\right\|^{2}+\frac{L^{2}}{\varepsilon_{1}}\left\|{\mathscr{L}}u\right\|^{2}.

Hence

-\alpha\int_{\Omega}2\Re\!\left({(\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\nabla u)\overline{{\mathscr{L}}u}}\right){\mathrm{d}}x\geq-|\alpha|\Big(\varepsilon_{1}\left\|\nabla u\right\|^{2}+\frac{L^{2}}{\varepsilon_{1}}\left\|{\mathscr{L}}u\right\|^{2}\Big)\geq-\varepsilon_{1}\left\|\nabla u\right\|^{2}-\frac{\alpha^{2}L^{2}}{\varepsilon_{1}}\left\|{\mathscr{L}}u\right\|^{2},

4.2.1

We now consider the case where

(75)

{\big(\nu\gamma_{2}-\tfrac{\alpha}{\varepsilon_{3}}\big)\|\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u-iku\|^{2}_{\partial\Omega}.}

[Coercivity with weakly enforced impedance]

Remark 16
(Explicit calculation of the constants)

A convenient choice of the constants appearing in front of the boundary norms is

\varepsilon_{2}=\frac{L_{0}}{4},\qquad\varepsilon_{3}=\frac{L_{0}}{4L^{2}}.

\displaystyle\alpha L_{0}-\alpha L^{2}\varepsilon_{3}-\alpha\varepsilon_{2}=\frac{\alpha L_{0}}{2},

\displaystyle\nu\gamma_{2}-\frac{\alpha}{\varepsilon_{3}}=\nu\gamma_{2}-\frac{4\alpha L^{2}}{L_{0}}.

\beta>\frac{\alpha L}{2}+\frac{2\alpha L^{2}}{L_{0}},\qquad\gamma_{2}>\frac{4\alpha L^{2}}{\nu L_{0}}.

Other choices are possible; the corresponding constants in the coercivity bounds can be traced explicitly from the preceding estimates.

Variational Discretisation Methods

We now describe two classes of methods for approximating solutions of the Helmholtz equation (

5.1
Conforming Galerkin method

We now formulate a conforming Galerkin discretisation of the regularised variational principle associated with
\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}
. Since the functional involves second-order derivatives through the residual term
\|{\mathscr{L}}v-f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
, we need to ensure that the finite element space we work with is a subspace of
{\mathscr{V}}
. A sufficient condition is to take
V_{h}\subset H^{2}(\Omega)
, which guarantees
{\mathscr{L}}v_{h}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}v_{h}\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
so that all terms are well defined.

Let
V_{h}\subset H^{2}(\Omega)
be a conforming finite element space, for instance based on Argyris elements or other
C^{1}
-continuous constructions. Then the conforming Galerkin approximation is defined by the minimisation problem:

u_{h}\in V_{h}\quad\text{such that}\quad\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}(u_{h})=\min_{v_{h}\in V_{h}}\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}(v_{h}).

(78)

Given that
V_{h}
is a linear space, the main properties of the minimisation problem are preserved. In particular,
this problem is well posed and strictly convex for
\gamma\geq\gamma_{0}
, and the unique minimiser
u_{h}\in V_{h}

solves the Hermitian discrete variational formulation

\mathcal{A}_{WBC}(u_{h},v_{h})=\int_{\Omega}f\overline{\big(v_{h}+2\gamma_{1}{\mathscr{L}}v_{h}\big)}{\mathrm{d}}x,\qquad\text{for all }v_{h}\in V_{h},

(79)

where
\mathcal{A}_{WBC}
is defined by

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}_{WBC}(u,v)&:=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\overline{v}{\mathrm{d}}x-k^{2}\int_{\Omega}u\overline{v}{\mathrm{d}}x+2\gamma_{1}\int_{\Omega}({\mathscr{L}}u)\overline{{\mathscr{L}}v}{\mathrm{d}}x\\
&\quad+2\gamma_{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}\Bigl(\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u-iku\Bigr)\overline{\Bigl(\partial_{\mathbf{n}}v-ikv\Bigr)}{\mathrm{d}}S.\end{split}

(80)

As before, well posedness follows from
[
11
, Section 1, Chapter VII]
. The use of
H^{2}
-conforming elements ensures that all terms in the energy and in

\mathcal{A}_{WBC}(u,v)

are well defined, including the penalty terms for residuals and boundary mismatch.

\Phi(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
with
D=2PR
.

The model used in the experiments has two parts. The first part is a linear plane-wave field
\Phi(x)W
with
W\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times 2}
mapping features to
(u_{R},u_{I})
. The second part is a shallow nonlinear correction applied to feature-wise gains. We write

u_{W,\eta}(x)=\underbrace{\Phi(x)W}_{\text{linear plane-wave field}}+\underbrace{\mathcal{M}_{\eta}\big(\,\Phi(x)\odot g_{\eta}(x)\,\big)}_{\text{nonlinear correction}}\,,

where
g_{\eta}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
produces
per-feature
bounded gains and
\mathcal{M}_{\eta}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
mixes the modulated features into
(u_{R},u_{I})
. Concretely, with a smooth SiLU nonlinearity
\sigma
and a final
\tanh
cap for bounded outputs,

\begin{split}g_{\eta}(x)&=\mathbf{1}+\alpha_{g}\,\tanh\Big(G_{2}\,\sigma(G_{1}x+c_{1})+c_{2}\Big)\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\\
\mathcal{M}_{\eta}(z)&=M_{2}\,\sigma(M_{1}z+b_{1})+b_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\end{split}

(84)

with trainable matrices
G_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{h_{g}\times 2}
,
G_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times h_{g}}
,
M_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{h_{m}\times D}
,
M_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times h_{m}}
and vectors
c_{1},c_{2},b_{1},b_{2}
;
\alpha_{g}>0
is a small scale so that
g_{\eta}\approx\mathbf{1}
initially. The on-shell ring (
r=0
) represents the homogeneous component while off-shell rings (
r\geq 1
) provide degrees of freedom for the particular solution
(-\Delta-k^{2})u=f
.

The coefficient
W
is initialised by a streamed least-squares solve that couples the interior off-shell residual with the impedance boundary condition written in real/imaginary form, including the trace/normal-derivative cross terms. This initialisation uses a boundary weight
\varpi/h
consistent with the finite element scaling and a small Tikhonov ridge for stability.

Starting from this initial
W
, we then train all parameters
(W,\eta)
by stochastic minimisation. At each iteration we draw fresh interior points
\widetilde{X}_{\Omega}\subset\Omega
and boundary points
\widetilde{X}_{\partial}\subset\partial\Omega
and minimise the Monte-Carlo objective

\widehat{\mathcal{J}}(W,\eta)=\gamma_{1}\,\frac{1}{\widetilde{N}}\sum_{x\in\widetilde{X}_{\Omega}}\big|(-\Delta-k^{2})u(x)-f(x)\big|^{2}+\gamma_{\mathrm{bnd}}\,\frac{1}{\widetilde{M}}\sum_{y\in\widetilde{X}_{\partial}}\big|\partial_{n}u(y)-ik\,u(y)\big|^{2}

\hskip 55.00008pt+\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{1}{\widetilde{N}}\sum_{x\in\widetilde{X}_{\Omega}}\big(|\nabla u|^{2}-k^{2}|u|^{2}\big)(x)-\Re\left(\frac{1}{\widetilde{N}}\sum_{x\in\widetilde{X}_{\Omega}}f(x)\,\overline{u(x)}\right),\qquad u=u_{W,\eta}.

In the experiments we take
\gamma_{\mathrm{bnd}}=50
, use SiLU activations, and train with Adam and cosine annealing, followed by a short L-BFGS pass on the mixer. All integrals are thus replaced by random quadrature, consistent with the variational formulation and the discrete objective remains coercive on the finite-dimensional class defined above.

6
Conclusion

We have developed a new class of variational principles for the Helmholtz equation based on a Lagrangian formulation of the underlying wave dynamics. Introducing regularised energies that are strongly coercive allows for stable minimisation formulations that naturally accommodate both finite element and neural network approximations. The theoretical framework guarantees well-posedness and robustness independent of the wave number and we demonstrated its practical effectiveness through a set of illustrative numerical experiments.

The current implementation employs simplified numerical schemes to provide a proof of concept for the potential of the proposed formulation.

In future work, we will develop more efficient discretisations based on flexible Galerkin methods and alternative neural network approaches that preserve the coercivity properties of the regularised functional. We also plan to extend the numerical analysis of the discrete schemes, establishing rigorous error bounds and a stability theory within the neural network framework.
