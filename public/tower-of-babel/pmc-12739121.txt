High-resolution climate reconstruction from historical Chinese weather records using optimized natural language processing
Authors: Tang Li, Tao Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Reconstructing high-resolution climate data from historical documents is hindered by subjectivity and a lack of standardization. This study develops and validates a novel framework to overcome these challenges. In this paper, a historical weather classification lexicon is constructed by optimizing natural language processing (NLP) techniques. Leveraging semantic clustering and dynamic expansion, this lexicon effectively captures the linguistic diversity associated with weather events across different regions and intensity levels. Building on this lexicon, we propose a multi-dimensional index system to quantify historical weather grades. This system includes indicators such as weather intensity, agricultural impact, economic impact, social impact, and population casualties. For each indicator, scientific and objective weights are assigned using the entropy method combined with expert judgment. To validate the effectiveness of our approach, we extracted low-temperature weather records from historical documents of Guangdong and Hebei provinces in China. The results show that the overall trend of low-temperature weather in these two provinces is consistent with existing research on climate change during the Qing Dynasty. Moreover, the provincial trend maps reveal not only synchronous change patterns but also significant regional differences. A Random Forest model was employed to validate our index, achieving a classification accuracy of 94.0%, with Area Under the Curve(AUC) scores exceeding 0.98 for low-grade events. This data-driven methodology offers a replicable and scalable tool for converting qualitative historical narratives into high-resolution quantitative climate data, thereby enhancing our understanding of past climate variability and its societal impacts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-28006-5.

Introduction

The intensification of global climate change has significantly increased the frequency1 and intensity of extreme weather events2. In this context, high-resolution historical climate reconstruction3 has become a crucial tool for understanding the driving forces of climate change, while also providing a scientific foundation for modern societies to address climate risks and achieve sustainable development. Recent years have made significant progress in high-resolution climate reconstruction research4, yet it continues to grapple with multifaceted challenges in data sources and methodology.

Firstly, regarding data sources, the diversity of existing data is accompanied by significant limitations. Natural proxy data5,6, such as tree rings, ice cores, and lake sediments, offer long-term and high-resolution climate information but suffer from uneven spatial coverage. In contrast, historical documents, serving as humanity’s climate diary, offer a unique perspective for regional climate reconstruction7. They are particularly suitable for filling temporal and spatial gaps where natural proxy data are sparse. However, historical document data are characterized by high subjectivity, semantic ambiguity, and structural inconsistency, which hinder the feasibility of systematic processing and standardized analysis. Furthermore, while traditional text analysis methods excel at extracting climate signals, they often struggle to capture subtle or nonlinear signals within complex climate systems. China, due to its abundant historical literature, provides invaluable research materials for climate reconstruction. Researchers have successfully reconstructed climate change trends in North China during the Qing Dynasty by analyzing local chronicles and other documents8. With the advancement of natural language processing (NLP) technology, particularly the advent of deep learning, information extraction and classification have transitioned from shallow models to deep learning models. Shallow learning models predominantly encompass techniques such as TF-IDF9, N-gram10, and Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA)11. In contrast, deep learning models leverage an end-to-end learning framework, automatically learning data feature representations through multi-hidden-layer neural networks. Key methodologies within this domain include convolutional neural network (CNN)-based feature extraction models, such as TextCNN12 and Char-CNN13, as well as context mechanism models like TextRNN 14 and R-CNN15 and attention models like BERT16 (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) and HAN17. The work of Hu Duanmu18 exemplifies the integration of the BERT-CNN model to efficiently identify and classify meteorological disaster events, such as droughts and floods, from both historical documents and modern network data. Their research primarily focuses on event extraction, specifically determining the timing, location, and type of disasters, and has achieved a notably high F1 score. This pioneering research has significantly contributed to the automated processing of massive texts and the construction of spatio-temporal databases for disaster events. However, these cutting-edge event extraction methods predominantly address the binary question of “whether a disaster has occurred”. For historical climate reconstruction and disaster history studies, a more fundamental and challenging issue is quantifying “how severe this disaster was”19. The social and economic impacts of a “severe drought” versus a “severe drought” characterized by “thousands of miles of barren land” are vastly different, and traditional event classification models struggle to differentiate between them. However, such studies primarily rely on manual analysis20, leading to inefficiencies and a lack of detailed standardization and quantitative analysis methods.

To address the aforementioned challenges and fill the existing research gap, this study proposes a systematic quantitative analysis framework. The core innovations and academic contributions of this framework are primarily manifested in the following three aspects:1)An Optimized NLP Lexicon Construction Method for Classical Chinese Texts: Addressing the unique grammatical and lexical challenges posed by historical documents in Classical Chinese, this study moves beyond generic NLP tools to develop a customized lexicon construction workflow. This process integrates a TextRank(TR) algorithm based on semantic clustering with a novel “exact matching” strategy. Through dynamic iteration and optimization of seed words, it significantly enhances the precision of graded extraction of weather-descriptive vocabulary within the context of classical texts, thereby overcoming the limitations of standard models when applied to historical literature.2) An Objective and Multi-dimensional Quantitative Index System: This study introduces the Entropy Weight Method into the quantitative analysis of historical climate literature for the first time. We have constructed a multi-dimensional impact index system that encompasses weather intensity, agricultural, economic, social, and casualty impacts. By employing the entropy method, we objectively calculate the weight of each indicator, thereby replacing the reliance on subjective judgment or single-variable analysis that is common in previous research. This establishes a transparent and replicable standardized process for assessing the severity of historical weather events.3) A Robust “Construct-then-Validate” Research Paradigm: To address the challenge of the lack of large-scale, pre-labeled data in the field of historical research, this study proposes a comprehensive “construct-then-validate” paradigm. We first constructed a highly interpretable multi-dimensional index system and then utilized a machine learning model (Random Forest) to validate the system’s effectiveness and explanatory power, rather than employing it for direct prediction. For qualitative, data-sparse domains such as history, this paradigm proves to be more robust, transparent, and reliable compared to traditional supervised learning or end-to-end “black box” models.

This paper focuses on Chinese historical documents and selects historical low-temperature events in Guangdong Province and Hebei Province as case studies. Using the hierarchical lexicon and quantitative framework developed in this paper, it empirically analyzes the low-temperature weather records of these two regions21. The results demonstrate that the constructed system significantly improves the accuracy of weather event classification and index factor analysis, refining regional climate change trends and accurately identifying extreme weather events. Incorporating NLP technology, this study has achieved a qualitative-to-quantitative transformation in the field of historical climate reconstruction. It not only offers an innovative solution for extracting climate information from Chinese historical documents but also demonstrates its broad applicability, offering high-resolution tools for constructing historical weather databases with broader coverage and more varied weather types.

Results

Optimization of lexicon construction

Through iterative optimization, the number of seed words increased from the initial 24 to 67, significantly enhancing the coverage of the lexicon (Fig. 1). Thirteen new seed words were added in the first to second iterations, with a fast expansion speed. However, from the third iteration onwards, the number of newly added seed words gradually decreased, indicating a decline in the ability of the TR algorithm to mine new words in the later stages. At the same time, the accuracy of the model increased from the initial 0.45 to 0.71, with the greatest contribution from the first to third iterations (from 0.45 to 0.68). In the third to fourth iterations, the accuracy improvement was only 0.03, showing a decreasing marginal effect. Overall, there is a positive correlation between the number of seed words and the accuracy of the model, but it tends to saturate in the later stages, and the optimization effect of adding seed words on model performance is limited. Therefore, the TR algorithm extension terminates after the fourth iteration.Fig. 1Seed words optimization and model performance.

Validation of the indicator system

Temporal trend analysis

The core scientific hypothesis of this study is that through improved natural language processing (NLP) techniques and multidimensional quantitative indicator systems, a high-precision and widely applicable historical weather grade system can be constructed to achieve standardized classification and accurate quantification of historical weather events. To verify this hypothesis, we used a time trend chart for analysis and conducted multi-level validation using a random forest classification model. The results indicate that the system performs excellently in terms of classification accuracy and applicability.

We selected representative provinces in the north and south of China for analysis. Guangdong Province is selected in the south, whose subtropical monsoon climate features frequent extreme weather such as typhoons and rainstorm, and has a significant impact on economy and society. Hebei Province is selected in the north, whose temperate monsoon climate characteristics result in frequent occurrences of extreme weather such as cold waves, blizzards, and droughts, which have a significant impact on agricultural production and economic development.

We extracted 73027 (Data extracted from《Three Thousand Years of China》) historical low-temperature weather events for quantification and compared the results with historical literature records and existing research. By analyzing the time trend line charts of five weather grades from 1645 to 1860, we found that the distribution of different weather grades exhibited significant temporal variations (Fig. 3a and fig. 3b). Specifically, the grades of 4 and 5 significantly increased during the Maunder Minimum from the mid-17th century to the early 18th century and from the early 19th century 28–31 to the mid-19th century. In combination with Fig. 3a and b, the spatial extent of high-grade events was relatively large during the cold phase of the Little Ice Age. However, this extent gradually diminished from the mid-18th century through the early 19th century and into the mid-to-late 19th century. It is evident that low-temperature events during the cold period of the early Qing Dynasty were more concentrated in both time and space, with higher grades, whereas low-temperature events during the cold period of the 19th century exhibited greater persistence over longer durations. These findings indicate that the application of this indicator system demonstrates favorable temporal adaptability.Fig. 3(a) Time trend chart during 1645 to 1864, (b) Stacking chart during 1645 to 1864. Since the same county may experience low-temperature events of varying intensities across different years, for the purpose of facilitating display and highlighting key points, this paper only retains high-level low-temperature event data recorded in the history of each county for mapping. This method can intuitively reflect the spatial distribution characteristics of low-temperature weather events while simplifying the complexity of map presentation (maps and place names adhere to China’s current administrative divisions).

Through Fig. 5a and b, it is evident that different counties and cities correspond to varying levels of low-temperature events and their spatial distribution characteristics. Additionally, the two maps also provide insights into the geographical and economic features of the regions. The dataset pertaining to Hebei Province comprises a substantial volume of records, totaling 490 entries. After undergoing rigorous data cleaning procedures, it encompasses information on 146 counties within Hebei Province (historically referred to as Zhili Province) during the Qing Dynasty. According to the “Geography of the Draft History of Qing,” Zhili Province consisted of a total of 152 prefectures and counties during that era, yielding a spatial coverage rate of 96.05%. On average, each county is linked to 3.32 recorded events. Nevertheless, a certain degree of spatial deviation is evident, with the density of records being significantly higher in the Beijing-Tianjin region compared to the mountainous areas in northern Hebei. The dataset for Guangdong Province, following deduplication, includes 78 valid county-level administrative units, covering a diverse range of administrative divisions such as counties, scattered prefectures, and directly governed prefectures. Based on the 80 county-level units that constituted Guangdong Province during the Qing Dynasty (excluding cross-provincial and non-county-level units), the dataset achieves a spatial coverage rate of approximately 97.5%, encompassing all 78 county-level units.

This analysis confirms that our methodology achieves high-resolution, county-level spatial coverage and successfully identifies historically significant agricultural and economic zones vulnerable to climate events.

Discussion

Historical weather grading system: Advances, challenges, and future outlook

The core objective of this study is to respond to a fundamental challenge in the field of historical climatology: how to scientifically and effectively extract information from complex, scattered and subjective historical documents, and minimize the uncertainty of reconstruction results. As Yang Yuda and others33 have summarized, the research progress over the past few decades has always revolved around this core issue.

This study developed a scientifically robust and high-resolution historical weather grading system by optimizing NLP technology to construct a hierarchical word bank and employing the entropy weight method to determine the weights of multi-dimensional indicators. The system’s effectiveness in spatio-temporal applicability was validated through empirical analysis. Research findings indicate that the system can accurately capture climate fluctuation patterns during the early Qing cold period and the 19th-century cold period, and it can precisely identify extreme weather events at the county and city levels. The frequent or high-grade occurrences of cold weather not only reflect regional climate vulnerability and environmental adaptability but also reveal the agricultural economy’s high dependence on climatic conditions and the profound impact of cold events on social stability and national disaster management capabilities. By conducting a comprehensive analysis of multi-dimensional indicators such as weather intensity, agricultural impact, economic impact, social impact, and population casualties, the system effectively quantifies the overall impact of cold events on the region, thereby verifying the scientific validity and practical applicability of the historical weather grading index system. For instance, historical records of Hebei and Shandong, the frequent occurrence of high-grade cold events aligns with historical literature records, further demonstrating the system’s ability to accurately capture the spatial distribution characteristics of cold events and their multiple impacts on socio-economic systems.

In terms of methodology, early pioneering works, such as the “Atlas of Drought and Flood Distribution in the Past Five Hundred Years”, laid the foundation for large-scale quantitative research. However, as Chen Yexin (2020) pointed out, these studies mainly rely on manual interpretation and relatively simple single-dimensional classification (such as drought/flood), which is insufficient for in-depth quantitative research from the perspective of climate grades. Our research has deepened and innovated traditional methods in the following key aspects: Firstly, in terms of the accuracy of information extraction, compared with traditional manual organization, the natural language processing (NLP) technology and dynamically optimized word bank introduced in this study can handle massive texts more systematically and precisely, directly responding to the challenge of “improving the efficiency of information extraction” proposed by Yang Yuda et al. Secondly, in terms of the dimensions of the evaluation system, our multi-dimensional indicator system (covering weather intensity, agriculture, economy, society and casualty impacts) and the objective entropy weight method represent a fundamental leap over the traditional single-variable analysis approach. It no longer merely measures how dry or cold a place is, but comprehensively assesses the overall impact of a climate event on society, providing a more comprehensive perspective for understanding climate-social interactions during historical periods.

Finally, our “construction-validation” paradigm provides quantitative accuracy assessment through machine learning models (with a classification accuracy rate of 94.0%), offering credibility testing for the classification of historical disaster levels. This addresses the key issue of “reducing uncertainty” emphasized by Yang Yuda et al.

Despite the notable advancements achieved in this study, several limitations persist and require attention: The scarcity and uneven distribution of historical documents hinder the completeness of the data, resulting in significant gaps across numerous regions or time periods. Furthermore, the subjective nature of historical records and the ambiguities in language pose challenges for the precise quantification of climate information, particularly when dealing with polysemous words and semantic ambiguities. Additionally, while the Random Forest classification model validates the system’s applicability, some confusion persists in the classification of medium-to-high-grade events. This may be attributed to imbalances in the training data or overlapping features. Furthermore, a pivotal methodological concern pertains to the potential for spatial bias, which may emerge when applying weights derived from Shanxi’s data to the case studies of Hebei and Guangdong. Economic structure, and even historical recording practices could engender variations in the optimal weighting of indicators. For instance, the socio-economic repercussions of a severe cold event might be weighted differently within the agrarian-based economy of historical Hebei, in contrast to Guangdong, which is characterized by its distinctive subtropical climate and maritime trade. Recognizing this limitation, our current model should be regarded as a robust, first-order approximation of historical weather impacts, rather than a definitive, locally-calibrated measure.

Despite this limitation, our framework incorporates several features aimed at bolstering its universality and adaptability. Firstly, the entropy weight method is intrinsically data-driven and objective, capturing the relative informational value of each indicator within a diverse dataset. This approach effectively mitigates subjective regional assumptions at the initial stage. Secondly, the final weights are a synthesis of this objective calculation and expert judgment, enabling adjustments that transcend the specificities of any single region.

Most crucially, the indicator system itself is designed to be adaptable. As demonstrated by the concluding formula, the weights (WI, AI, etc.) can be recalibrated for different provinces or time periods, provided that sufficient local data becomes available in future research endeavors,. This adaptability ensures that the framework is not static but can evolve in tandem with the digitization of more historical records, thereby establishing it as a valuable and scalable tool for historical climate reconstruction.

Given these limitations, future research endeavors can further enhance our comprehension of vague language, particularly in identifying polysemous words and low-frequency terms. By broadening the scope of historical document sources, we can mitigate the imbalance in training data, thereby improving the model’s capacity to discern medium-to-high-grade events. Incorporating sociological and economic data into the research process will enable a deeper exploration of the intricate relationship between climate change and social interactions, offering support for multi-domain studies on climate adaptability. Future research endeavors should prioritize the development of regionally-tailored weighting systems. As an increasing number of local chronicles from various provinces undergo digitization, a comparative analysis can be undertaken to investigate how socio-economic and climatic factors influence the weights assigned to disaster-impact indicators. Furthermore, conducting a sensitivity analysis—by systematically adjusting the weights—would facilitate the quantification of the extent to which the classification of historical weather events remains robust under different weighting schemes. This approach would yield profound insights into the stability and reliability of our proposed system across a wide array of historical contexts.

Consequently, it provides an objective and replicable analytical tool for historical climate reconstruction.

Methods

The historical weather data utilized in this study were sourced from classical Chinese texts (‘Wen yan wen’), which are characterized by their unique grammar, concise vocabulary and implicit expression. To address these linguistic challenges, we developed an optimized NLP35 model specifically designed for the unique features of classical Chinese. During the translation of results into English, meticulous attention was paid to preserving semantic accuracy, although certain nuances, such as distinctions between active and passive phrase, may not be fully captured.

Data sources and preprocessing

The data sources utilized for constructing the vocabulary in this paper include: 1) Abstracts of Climate Impact Data from the Qing Dynasty Chronicles (The historical records are all from the county records of Qing Dynasty in Shanxi Province.) ; 2) A Comprehensive Collection of Meteorological Records in China Over Three Thousand Years36,The data content, recorded in classical Chinese, stems from official historical records. Rigorous preprocessing measures were implemented on the literature data in this study. Specifically, irrelevant information and outliers were meticulously removed. Furthermore, a combination of stopword lists and a custom stopword list tailored for this study was utilized to eliminate meaningless high-frequency words.

Word segmentation and seed word optimization

We devised a three-step process for word segmentation. Initially, jieba word segmentation was employed for preliminary segmentation. Subsequently, an independently designed exact matching algorithm was introduced to ensure precise recognition of common weather description terms.

This study compares two commonly used word segmentation tools: Jiayan and Jieba. Jiayan37 is specifically designed for classical Chinese. It handles ambiguities through a dictionary-based maximum matching method and combines statistical learning approaches to predict the boundaries of new words, adapting to linguistic changes. Jieba, often employed for modern Chinese, can also be adapted to the word segmentation needs of classical Chinese through custom dictionaries. Its openness and flexibility enable it to quickly accommodate texts from multiple domains.

Our “exact matching” strategy was developed to overcome the limitations of baseline tokenizers by specifically addressing false positives and false negatives.Mitigating False Positives: False positives arise when non-target words are incorrectly classified as keywords. For example, a standard tokenizer may flag the solar term “霜降” (Shuāngjiàng, “Frost’s Descent”) as a weather event because it contains the character “霜” (frost). Our curated, lexicon-based approach prevents such errors by exclusively matching predefined disaster-related terms, thereby enhancing precision.Mitigating False Negatives: False negatives occur when meaningful compound terms are erroneously segmented, leading to information loss. The term “霜雪” (shuāngxuě), describing a composite frost-and-snow event, could be split into individual characters (“霜”and “雪”), losing the term’s specific semantic weight. Our strategy prevents this fragmentation by matching the complete term as a single entity, thus improving recall.

In addition, this study further assesses other standard methods, such as TF-IDF. Classical Chinese, characterized by its concise language, flexible grammatical structures, and frequent employment of fixed expressions or historical allusions, presents substantial challenges to conventional keyword extraction techniques like TF-IDF. The TF-IDF method hinges on term frequency statistics to gauge keyword significance. Nevertheless, in Classical Chinese texts, numerous critical disaster-related terms may manifest as low-frequency words, whereas high-frequency words often lack substantive semantic content. Moreover, TF-IDF is incapable of capturing contextual relationships between terms, which further constrains its efficacy in reflecting the thematic relevance of meteorological disasters. In contrast, the TR algorithm constructs a graph depicting relationships between terms, enabling it to scrutinize co-occurrence relationships within the term context. This facilitates more precise extraction of disaster-related keywords. Unlike TF-IDF, the TR algorithm does not depend on term frequency statistics; even if certain terms appear infrequently, they can still be identified as important provided they maintain strong connections with other high-weight terms. Consequently, in the keyword extraction tasks of this study, the TR algorithm surpasses TF-IDF by more accurately capturing the core vocabulary of disaster-related themes. Although neural models like BERT have demonstrated remarkable success, they necessitate large-scale, high-quality annotated datasets for fine-tuning. Lexicon construction

In alignment with the research objectives aimed at classifying and grading weather-related vocabulary, this study utilizes the TextRank (TR) algorithm to establish an initial lexicon. This lexicon is iteratively expanded by incorporating seed words. Based on the TR ranking scores, high-frequency words with a high degree of semantic relevance to the initial seed words are extracted40.

While TR-based keyword extraction provides a foundational framework for constructing the classified and graded lexicon, it is imperative to strike a balance between the subjective descriptions found in historical materials and the scientific rigor of contemporary indicators. This balance is necessary due to historical period changes, socio-economic contexts, and discrepancies in recording standards compared to present-day practices. To address this challenge, this study introduces a lexicon construction method that integrates algorithmic extraction with expert adjustment. Issues necessitating expert intervention primarily manifest as ambiguous semantic attributions or deviations in word frequency usage from actual conditions. Expert adjustment methods encompass modifications aligned with modern meteorological standards and optimizations drawing from existing research knowledge. The classification of weather intensity levels is determined by the intensity and impact described by the keywords, employing the Semantic Differential Method41 to determine these levels.

Historical weather grade indicator system

Drawing upon climate system theory and aligning with international assessment frameworks such as the IPCC(Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change) and SDGs(Sustainable Development Goals), this study constructs a historical weather indicator system from the perspective of the bidirectional interplay between climate and the econom46. This system aims to uncover the ramifications of climate change on economic activities and mirror the characteristics of climate change through variations in economic endeavors47,48.

The construction of this indicator system adheres to five fundamental principles: 1) Diversity, ensuring a balance between the natural attributes and societal repercussions of meteorological events to reflect regional weather patterns and socio-economic characteristics; 2) Comparability, selecting universally applicable and clearly delineated indicators to facilitate cross-regional and cross-temporal comparative analyses; 3) Hierarchy, utilizing a layered structure spanning from macro to micro levels, delineating the general nature of meteorological events while emphasizing crucial local details; 4) Independence, maintaining relative independence among indicators to preclude conceptual and extensional overlaps; and 5) Operability, devising an indicator system conducive to practical application and adaptable to the analytical requirements of intricate historical materials. Within this framework, “weather intensity” describes the physical characteristics of weather phenomena, whereas “weather severity impact” focuses on its socio-economic ramifications, serving as a pivotal basis for grading.

To ascertain the weights of each indicator objectively, this paper incorporates the entropy method leveraging the information entropy of indicators to mirror their degree of variation. Specifically, 807 weather data(Shanxi Prefectural Chronicles) entries from Shanxi Province during the Qing Dynasty are selected in this paper. To establish a fundamental weighting framework, this study leverages 807 historical weather records from Shanxi Province (covering the period from 1645–1864) to compute the initial indicator weights. The selection of Shanxi as a starting point is intentional and fulfills several critical functions for constructing a generalizable model. Firstly, as a province situated in central China, Shanxi encompasses a wide array of climatic conditions, including droughts, floods, and low-temperature events. Consequently, it serves as a microcosm of the diverse meteorological challenges encountered across broader regions of China. This climatic diversity furnishes a well-balanced dataset, which is essential for the entropy method, as it leverages data variability to assign objective weights. Secondly, Shanxi’s historical records are of high quality, characterized by comprehensive temporal and spatial coverage, thereby ensuring the reliability of the baseline model.

Crucially, by developing the weights on a dataset distinct from those of our validation provinces (Hebei and Guangdong), we mitigate the risk of overfitting and enhance our capacity to rigorously assess the model’s spatial applicability. We posit that this baseline model, which encapsulates fundamental socio-economic responses to climate shocks (e.g., impacts on agriculture, economy, and population), provides a robust and objective foundation prior to its application in other regions.

Instead of being assigned arbitrarily, the severity grade thresholds49 were methodically established through a two-stage process that integrates statistical analysis with heuristic refinement. Initially, a quantile-based method (quintiles) was applied to the score distribution of the 807-record dataset from Shanxi to establish objective, data-driven boundaries. This approach ensures comparable sample sizes across grades and minimizes subjective bias. Subsequently, these preliminary thresholds underwent refinement via a heuristic approach to enhance interpretability. This refinement process involved adjusting boundaries to distinct integer values for operational clarity and, crucially, verifying their semantic consistency with the qualitative descriptions embedded in the historical source materials. Consequently, the final grading criteria strike a robust balance between statistical validity and domain-specific interpretability, reflecting both the intrinsic data distribution and the historical context.

From the analysis of entropy and weight, it is evident that meteorological intensity and population casualties are core indicators in system evaluation. Despite their high entropy values and relatively uniform distribution, these factors exert a particularly significant direct impact on the system. Agricultural and economic influences exhibit intermediate levels of entropy and weight, underscoring their crucial role in maintaining system balance. In contrast, social impact has the lowest entropy, indicating substantial data variability, yet its weight is also low, possibly due to limited short-term quantitative effects or incomplete historical records. It is important to note that the weights derived from the entropy method are based solely on the quantitative analysis of data distribution characteristics and do not fully incorporate practical significance or domain-specific knowledge. The grading score is directly proportional to the grade level; for instance, Level 1 corresponds to 10 points, and Level 2 to 20 points. Different weather phenomena are classified into distinct grades based on their specific characteristics. For example, the grading scale for storm surges has a narrower range, while that for high temperatures and droughts exhibits a broader spectrum due to more detailed descriptions and greater differentiation. Finally, the grades are determined based on the grade scores. This paper classifies low-temperature scores into five grades: 0–6, 7–12, 13–18, 19–24, and 25 or higher. Depending on the study area, the index weights and grade score intervals can be adjusted according to actual application requirements.

The severity grade thresholds (0–6, 7–12, 13–18, 19–24, and ≥25) were not arbitrarily assigned but were systematically determined through a two-stage process combining statistical analysis and heuristic refinement. Initially, a quantile-based method (quintiles) was applied to the score distribution of the 807-record dataset from Shanxi to establish objective, data-driven boundaries, ensuring comparable sample sizes across grades and minimizing subjective bias. Subsequently, these preliminary thresholds were fine-tuned using a heuristic approach to enhance interpretability. This refinement involved adjusting boundaries to clear integer values for operational clarity and, crucially, verifying their semantic consistency with the qualitative descriptions found in the historical source materials.
