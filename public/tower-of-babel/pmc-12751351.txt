Risk stratification of incidental parathyroidectomy in patients undergoing thyroidectomy for nodular goiter: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Can Akgün, Mehmet Alperen Avcı
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIncidental parathyroidectomy (IPT) is a potential complication of thyroidectomy and may result in significant postoperative morbidity, including hypocalcemia and hypoparathyroidism. Despite improvements in surgical techniques, IPT remains a frequent finding in pathological specimens, and its risk factors are not fully elucidated. This study aimed to determine the incidence of IPT among patients undergoing thyroidectomy for nodular goiter and to identify independent risk factors contributing to its occurrence.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 558 patients who underwent thyroidectomy at a tertiary center between January 2021 and January 2024. Patient demographics, ultrasonographic findings, surgical data, and postoperative pathology reports were reviewed. IPT was defined as the presence of parathyroid tissue in the surgical specimen. Hypocalcemia and hypoparathyroidism were assessed through biochemical parameters. Statistical analyses included univariate and multivariate logistic regression models to determine independent predictors of IPT.ResultsAmong 558 patients, the overall incidence of IPT was 19.4%. IPT was more frequently observed in female patients (p = 0.011). A significant inverse correlation was found between mediolateral nodule size and IPT incidence (p = 0.008). In multivariate analysis, female sex (OR: 2.225, 95% CI: 1.21–4.32, p = 0.011) and smaller mediolateral nodule diameter (OR: 0.969, 95% CI: 0.939–0.999, p = 0.046) were independently associated with IPT.ConclusionsFemale patients and those with smaller thyroid nodules appear to be at higher risk for unintentional parathyroid removal. Preoperative awareness of these risk factors may support more meticulous dissection techniques and improve intraoperative decision-making.Keywords: Parathyroidectomy, Thyroidectomy, Risk factors, Thyroid nodule, Prevention

Introduction

Thyroidectomy is the most frequently performed procedure among endocrine surgeries. Standardization of surgical techniques and advancements in perioperative management have significantly reduced morbidity and mortality rates. Today, the complication rate has declined to below 5% [1–3].

Because of their intimate anatomical proximity and common vascular sources with the thyroid gland, the parathyroid glands are vulnerable to damage or unintended removal in the course of thyroidectomy. Their resemblance to thyroid and lymph nodes, along with their small size, makes it challenging to distinguish them from other cervical tissues [4].

Incidental parathyroidectomy (IPT), defined as the presence of parathyroid tissue in thyroidectomy specimens, remains a common complication even in high-volume centers, with reported rates ranging from 2.9% to 31% [1, 5, 6]. Despite extensive research, its biochemical consequences, risk factors, and predictors are still debated. Hypocalcemia and hypoparathyroidism related to IPT can lead to serious outcomes including prolonged hospitalization, neuromuscular symptoms, cardiac issues, nephrolithiasis, and increased healthcare costs [7]. These complications may arise not only from the removal of the glands but also due to perfusion problems of the parathyroid glands caused by surgical intervention. Numerous studies have investigated the effects of IPT on transient and permanent hypocalcemia and hypoparathyroidism, as well as the associated risk factors. However, a definitive conclusion has yet to be reached [8, 9].

Recent literature has increasingly explored the anatomical and surgical variables associated with IPT, yet the role of thyroid nodule characteristics remains underexamined. We hypothesized that nodule size, particularly smaller mediolateral dimensions, might be associated with higher IPT risk due to their proximity to the thyroid capsule and potential difficulty in intraoperative dissection. This anatomical consideration formed the basis for investigating the relationship between nodule size and IPT in our cohort.

Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the predictive significance of gender, imaging, and pathological findings for incidental parathyroidectomy in patients who underwent thyroidectomy due to nodular goiter.

Materials and methods

In this retrospective study, the data of patients who underwent thyroidectomy at a tertiary center hospital between January 2021 and January 2024 were analyzed. Prior to the analysis, approval was obtained from the local ethics committee (No: 2025/4/8). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and no personal patient information was disclosed. Informed consent was not obtained individually from patients, as the institutional ethics committee waived the requirement due to the retrospective and anonymized nature of the study.

The surgical interventions were planned based on the 2015 American Thyroid Association guidelines, considering clinical evaluation or fine-needle aspiration biopsy findings [9]. A total of 874 thyroidectomy procedures were performed by five surgeons during the study period (January 2021–January 2024), of which 558 cases met the inclusion criteria. Based on institutional surgical records, each surgeon performed approximately 50–60 thyroidectomies per year on average. Patients underwent standardized procedures using a classic Kocher incision. Excessive dissection was avoided to prevent unnecessary exploration of undetectable parathyroid glands. In cases where parathyroid glands were identified in the specimen, they were preserved in cold saline solution, subsequently sectioned into 1 × 1 mm fragments, and autotransplanted into the ipsilateral sternocleidomastoid muscle.

Data collection

Patient data, including age, sex, type of surgery, laboratory results, ultrasonographic findings, and pathology reports, were retrieved from the hospital archive system.

The presence of parathyroid tissue in the pathology report was defined as IPT positivity. Calcium and parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels were categorized into preoperative, postoperative 8th hour, and postoperative 6th month measurements.

Postoperative calcium and parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels were analyzed only in patients with histopathologically confirmed incidental parathyroidectomy. The first calcium and albumin measurements were performed at the postoperative 8th hour, and calcium levels were adjusted based on albumin levels. Corrected calcium levels (normal range: 8.8–10.2 mg/dL) below 8.8 mg/dL and/or the presence of symptoms were classified as hypocalcemia, while PTH levels < 12 pg/mL were defined as hypoparathyroidism (normal range:12–65 pg/mL).

Patients diagnosed with symptomatic hypocalcemia were treated with oral calcium and vitamin D supplementation along with intravenous calcium gluconate when necessary.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 20 software. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, median, and interquartile range) were calculated for quantitative variables, while frequency tables and percentage distributions were presented for categorical variables. The Chi-square test was used to compare the proportions of categorical variables between two or more groups.

For comparisons of numerical variables between groups, normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. If the normality assumption was met, the Independent Samples T-test was applied; otherwise, the Mann-Whitney U test was used. Additionally, phi coefficient and Cramer’s V were utilized to analyze the association between unordered categorical variables.

Potential confounders were adjusted for using multivariable logistic regression, including variables such as age, sex, surgical extent, and histopathological diagnosis. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. The goodness of fit of the model was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow Chi-square test, where p > 0.05 was considered indicative of an adequate model fit.

A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all analyses. The results were reported in tabular format along with descriptive statistics.

Results

A total of 558 patients were included in the study, of whom 438 (78.5%) were female and 120 (21.5%) were male, with a mean age of 49.2 years.

A total of 88 patients (15.8%) had preoperative FNAB results reported as Bethesda V (suspicious for malignancy) or Bethesda VI (malignant), although none of these patients underwent central or lateral neck dissection.

Regarding the type of surgical procedure, total thyroidectomy was performed in 463 patients (83%), and lobectomy in 95 patients (17%). Incidental parathyroid tissue was identified in the pathological specimens of 124 patients (22%). Among these, 16 patients (12.9%) had intrathyroidal parathyroid glands, as reported by the pathology department. These cases were excluded from the main analysis due to the non-modifiable nature of this anatomical variant. A single parathyroid gland was excised in 91 patients (84.3%), two glands in 15 patients (13.9%), and three glands in 2 patients (1.9%) (Table 1). No significant association was identified between patient age, anteroposterior dimension of the nodule, ultrasonographic features, tumor size in malignant nodules and the incidence of incidental parathyroidectomy. Specifically, the rate of incidental parathyroidectomy did not differ significantly among patients with benign pathology, follicular carcinoma, and papillary carcinoma (p = 0.66; Tables 3 and 4). Additionally, type of surgery (unilateral vs. bilateral thyroidectomy) was included in the univariate analysis, and no statistically significant association with IPT was observed (Table 3).

In the logistic regression, female sex (p = 0.011, OR: 2.2, 95% CI: 1.2–4.3) and a smaller mediolateral nodule dimension (p = 0.046 OR: 0.969, 95% CI: 0.93–0.99) were identified as independent risk factors for IPT. Additionally, a one-unit (mm) increase in the ML diameter of the nodule was associated with a 3.2% reduction in IPT risk (Table 5).

Discussion

Thyroid diseases are the second most common endocrine disorders after diabetes. Correspondingly, thyroidectomy is one of the most frequently performed endocrine surgeries worldwide, especially in regions with iodine deficiency. Despite advances in surgical technique and intraoperative technologies, incidental parathyroidectomy (IPT) remains a common postoperative finding with potential clinical consequences such as hypocalcemia and hypoparathyroidism.

Preservation of parathyroid glands during thyroidectomy can often be achieved through careful dissection along the thyroid capsule and meticulous identification of parathyroid tissue. However, even experienced surgeons encounter IPT, underscoring the importance of identifying contributing risk factors to mitigate this complication. Given that this complication can have important implications for postoperative calcium management, identifying its risk factors could facilitate the implementation of preventive strategies to reduce the likelihood of IPT. Several studies in the literature have investigated risk factors associated with IPT [10–17]. However, to the best of our knowledge, the number of studies analyzing the relationship between thyroid nodule size and IPT risk remains limited.

Previous studies have reported IPT rates ranging from 5.8% to 29% [16, 18]. Even when central neck dissection is excluded, the incidence may range between 16.2% and 22.4% [10, 11]. The presence of intrathyroidal parathyroid glands, which are often difficult to detect intraoperatively, may partially account for this frequency. However, to focus on potentially preventable surgical factors, these cases were excluded from our analysis. This exclusion is supported by literature noting that intrathyroidal parathyroid glands may account for up to 15% of IPTs and are often considered non-preventable due to their anatomical location [19]. In our study, this rate was determined as 12.9%, which is consistent with findings in the literature.

The literature includes conflicting findings regarding the relationship between IPT and variables such as age, sex, gland volume, and nodule size. These inconsistencies may stem from differences in study populations, definitions of IPT, intraoperative practices, and statistical modeling strategies.

The relationship between IPT incidence, age, and sex remains a subject of debate. Privitera et al. reported a significantly higher incidence of IPT in patients younger than 40 years [20]. Similarly, Prazenica et al. identified younger age as a risk factor for IPT [21]. In contrast, Melot et al. [14], Manatakis et al. [6], and Mencio et al. [15] found no significant association between age and IPT incidence. Consistent with these findings, our study also did not reveal a significant correlation between age and IPT incidence.

The association between sex and IPT incidence has also produced conflicting results in the literature. Melot et al. [14], Privitera et al. [20], and Manatakis et al. [6] reported no significant relationship between sex and IPT incidence. However, some studies have suggested that IPT is more prevalent in female patients [1, 14]. In our study, 88% of patients with IPT were female, reflecting the overall sex distribution of the cohort. However, in both univariate and multivariate models, female sex was independently associated with a significantly increased risk of IPT.

Our analysis did not reveal a significant difference in IPT incidence between unilateral and bilateral thyroidectomy, despite the assumption that bilateral procedures may involve a higher risk due to wider dissection. This may reflect relative surgical consistency in our cohort or limited variability in operative complexity.

A review of the literature reveals multiple studies investigating the relationship between thyroid gland weight, volume, and nodular characteristics with IPT incidence. While some studies found no significant correlation between thyroid dimensions or weight and IPT risk [1, 6, 12], others reported inverse associations. Granata et al. [13] observed that IPT incidence decreases as thyroid volume increases. Similarly, other studies suggested that larger thyroid gland weight is associated with a lower rate of IPT [14, 17]. In the study by Mencio et al., a larger dominant nodule was linked to a reduced IPT incidence [15]. Consistently, Zhou et al. proposed that smaller thyroid glands may position the parathyroid glands closer to the thyroid capsule, reducing their intraoperative detectability and thereby increasing the risk of IPT [17].

One of the notable aspects of this study was the identification of nodule dimensions, particularly smaller mediolateral diameter as a potential risk factor for IPT. This observation may suggest that flatter or narrower nodules pose a greater challenge for dissection, possibly increasing the likelihood of parathyroid tissue being inadvertently removed with the thyroid capsule. To the best of our knowledge, few studies in the current literature have specifically focused on the relationship between nodule mediolateral diameter and IPT risk, making this a potentially novel contribution.

Moreover, our study contributes methodologically by emphasizing preoperative and intraoperative variables, such as ultrasonographic and surgical factors in predicting IPT. By deliberately excluding central and lateral neck dissections and minimizing postoperative confounders, we sought to isolate risk factors relevant to routine total thyroidectomy. The multivariable model primarily focused on preoperative and intraoperative variables, but also included histopathological diagnosis due to its potential clinical relevance. Although this postoperative variable did not show a significant association with IPT in our data, its inclusion is acknowledged as a methodological limitation.

Contrary to some studies reporting a higher IPT rate in patients with thyroid malignancy [21], our findings did not show a significant association between histopathological diagnosis and IPT. This discrepancy may be explained by our exclusion of cases with central or lateral neck dissection, where more extensive surgical manipulation could increase the likelihood of parathyroid removal. Our study design focused on patients undergoing total thyroidectomy without lymph node dissection, aiming to reduce variability and assess risk factors more specific to routine thyroid surgery.

In addition, differences in inclusion criteria across studies (such as whether lymph node dissection was performed), variation in how IPT is defined (histopathological versus clinical criteria), and statistical modeling approaches may all contribute to the inconsistency of reported risk factors in the literature. By limiting our cohort to thyroidectomy procedures without lymph node dissection (including both total and lobar thyroidectomies), we aimed to reduce surgical variability and ensure a more objective assessment of outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective and single-center design may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, although the average annual case volume per surgeon was relatively high (~ 58 cases/year), it did not meet the ≥ 100 cases/year threshold used in some studies to define high-volume surgeons [22]. Third, the inclusion of final pathological diagnosis, a postoperative variable in the multivariable analysis may have introduced bias, although no significant association was found between histopathological diagnosis and incidental parathyroidectomy in our data.

Additionally, while the type of surgery (unilateral vs. bilateral thyroidectomy) was assessed and showed no significant association with IPT, residual confounding related to surgical extent cannot be entirely excluded due to the retrospective nature of the study. Finally, thyroid functional status (e.g., hypo- or hyperthyroidism) was not recorded, which may be a limitation, as hyperthyroid states could increase dissection difficulty and potentially affect the risk of incidental parathyroidectomy.

Conclusion

Female sex and a smaller mediolateral dimension of the thyroid nodule were identified as independent risk factors for incidental parathyroidectomy. Awareness of these risk factors may assist surgeons in preoperative planning and intraoperative decision-making. However, further prospective studies are needed to support these findings.
