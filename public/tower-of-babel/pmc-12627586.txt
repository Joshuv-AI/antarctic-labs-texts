MAFB regulates hematopoietic stem cell proliferation and maintenance
Authors: Saki Asano, Ching-Wei Liao, Yurina Matsunaga, Hyojung Jeon, Kyoko Sawaguchi, Keigo Asano, Manabu Kusakabe, Zeynab Javanfekr Shahri, Natalia Gogoleva, Tomomasa Yokomizo, Satoru Takahashi, Michito Hamada
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
MAFB, a transcription factor of the large Maf family, is expressed in both fetal liver (FL) and bone marrow (BM) hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs). However, its stage-specific roles remain elusive. Here, we reveal that MAFB plays distinct roles in FL and BM HSCs. Using Mafb-deficient and Mafb-GFP knock-in mouse models, we demonstrate that Mafb deletion enhances proliferation, cell cycle entry, and myeloid differentiation of FL HSCs, leading to enhanced chimerism rate in transplantation assays. However, Mafb-deficient BM HSCs exhibit impaired long-term reconstitution and progressive exhaustion, supported by serial transplantation and reduced colony-forming capacity. HSCs from Mafbf/f::Tie2-Cre mouse (Mafb cKO) further revealed a significant decline in long-term HSC (LT-HSC) populations and multilineage differentiation potential. Together, our findings suggest a stage-dependent role of MAFB as a regulator of HSC proliferation during fetal development and a critical factor for HSC maintenance during adulthood, providing insights into the stage-specific regulation of HSC function linked to cell cycle control and long-term repopulation capacity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24389-7.

Introduction

MAFB, a member of the large Maf family of transcription factors, including v-MAF, c-MAF, MAFA, and NRL, plays crucial roles in fetal development and hematopoiesis. These bZip proteins form homo- or heterodimers and bind to Maf recognition elements (MAREs) in DNA to regulate target gene expression1. In the blood system, MAFB was reported to regulate erythroid cell differentiation2. While numerous studies have focused on the role of Mafb in monocytes and macrophages, highlighting its involvement in macrophage polarization and homeostatic function3–5. Previously, Sarrazin et al. demonstrated that Mafb is expressed in hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) and contributes to an early myeloid lineage expansion in fetal HSCs. While previous studies suggested a role for Mafb in fetal HSC differentiation bias, its specific contributions to the regulation and maintenance of adult HSC functionality remain largely unexplored.

The hematopoietic system undergoes significant changes during development, with HSCs originating in the aorta-gonad-mesonephros (AGM) region, migrating to the fetal liver (FL), and ultimately residing in the bone marrow (BM) in adults. These different niches impart distinct characteristics to HSCs. In the FL, HSCs undergo massive expansion to support embryonic hematopoiesis, exhibiting higher proliferation rates and different metabolic characteristics. In contrast, HSCs in the adult BM largely remain quiescent; mostly activated to maintain blood system when facing blood loss or infection. Gene expression analyses have suggested the molecular differences between HSCs at various developmental stages, highlighting the importance of understanding stage-specific HSC regulation6. Notably, MAFB is expressed at higher levels in FL HSCs compared to BM HSCs, suggesting different roles in HSCs at different stages7. However, the detailed mechanisms and precise role of Mafb in FL HSCs and BM HSCs remain elusive.

In this study, we aim to find the stage-specific roles of MAFB in HSC function by using Mafb-deficient and Mafb-GFP knock-in mouse models. Through gene expression analysis, evaluation of cell cycle activity, and transplantation assays, we investigate the differences in the roles of MAFB between FL HSCs and BM HSCs. Our results reveal that Mafb deficiency increases cell cycle activity, proliferation, and differentiation in FL HSCs, whereas it leads to functional decline and exhaustion in BM HSCs. These findings provide new insights into the mechanisms governing the stage-specific regulation of HSCs by MAFB.

Methods

Mice

Mafb-GFP knock-in (MafbGFP/+), Mafb-null (Mafb−/−), and conditional Mafb-floxed (Mafbf/f) mice were previously described8–10. Mafbf/f mice were crossed with Tie2-Cre knock-in mice11 to generate endothelial and hematopoietic-specific deletion (Mafb cKO). All lines were maintained on a C57BL/6J background. All animal procedures were approved by the University of Tsukuba Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol No. 24-125) and followed institutional guidelines. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Genetic phenotypes of embryos were identified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using genomic DNA extracted from tails as templates. Primer sequences were as follows.

Mafb-forward: 5’ - TGAGCATGGGGCAAGAGCTG -3’

Mafb-reverse: 5’ - CCATCCAGTACAGGTCCTCG -3’

Gene expression analysis

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen), and cDNA was synthesized with QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (Qiagen). Quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Takara) on a Thermal Cycler Dice Real Time System (Takara). Gene expression was normalized to Hprt and calculated using the ΔΔCt method.

Primer sequences were as follows.

Hprt-forward: 5’-TTGTTGTTGGATATGCCCTTGACTA-3’

Hprt-reverse: 5’-AGGCAGATGGCCACAGGACTA-3’

Mafb-forward: 5’-TGAATTTGCTGGCACTGCTG-3’

Mafb-reverse: 5’-AAGCACCATGCGGTTCATACA-3’

RNA-seq analysis

RNA-seq analysis was performed in R. Public GEO data set (GSE190011 and GSE165695) were used, and sample tables were converted to gene-level matrices by summing rows that mapped to the same gene within each sample. In GSE165695, gene identifiers were harmonized to official mouse symbols using org.Mm.eg.db, after which counts were aggregated by symbol. Values for each sample were transformed as log2 (value+1) to place the data on a common scale. For the target genes replicate measurements on the log scale were summarized with the mean and standard deviation. Figures were generated with ggplot2 to visualize the dataset.

Flow cytometry and cell sorting

BM cells were harvested from femoral bones, tibial bones and pelvises of adult mice; FL cells from E14.5 embryos. Tissues were flushed with PBS using 23G needles and filtered through 40 μm strainers. For enrichment of c-Kit+ cells, samples were incubated with anti-c-Kit-PE antibody (eBioscience) for 30 minutes at 4 °C, followed by magnetic labeling with anti-PE MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec) for 15 minutes. After washing, labeled cells were passed through LS columns (Miltenyi Biotec) and c-Kit+ cells were collected. Enriched c-Kit+ cells were then incubated with biotin-conjugated Lineage cocktail [CD4, CD8a, B220, Mac-1, Gr-1, Ter119] (eBioscience) for 30 minutes at 4 °C. After washing, cells were stained with the following antibodies for 90 minutes at 4 °C: Sca-1-PE/Cy7 (BioLegend), Streptavidin-PE-Texas Red (BD Biosciences), and c-Kit-PE (eBioscience). For subset analysis, BM samples were further stained with CD34-eFluor® 660 (eBioscience), while FL samples were stained with CD150-PE/Cy5 (BioLegend) and CD48-APC (eBioscience). All antibody incubations and wash steps were performed in PBS supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum. Final cell suspensions were adjusted to 1×10⁷ cells/mL.

FCM analysis

Peripheral blood from competitively transplanted mice was collected every 4 weeks post-transplantation via the cheek vein using an 18G needle under isoflurane anesthesia. For serially transplanted mice, the same procedure was performed at 8 and 16 weeks after transplantation. Red blood cells were lysed using ammonium chloride hemolysis buffer, and the remaining leukocytes were washed in PBS. For competitively transplanted mice, cells were stained for 20 minutes at 4 °C with the following antibodies: B220-PE (BioLegend), B220-biotin (eBioscience), CD3ε-PE (BioLegend), CD45.2-PE/Cy7 (BioLegend), CD45.1-APC (Tonbo Biosciences), and CD11b-biotin (eBioscience). Streptavidin-APC/Cy7 (BioLegend) was subsequently added to detect biotin-labeled markers. For serially transplanted mice, peripheral blood samples were similarly prepared and stained with CD45.2-PE/Cy7 (BioLegend) and CD45.1-APC (Tonbo Biosciences) to assess donor chimerism.

For the analysis of HSC populations in Mafbf/f::Tie2-Cre mice and wild-type controls (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 10), BM cells were first stained with antibodies against c-Kit, Sca-1, CD34, CD150, and CD48, and were then fixed with 0.5% paraformaldehyde (PFA).

All samples were analyzed on either a Gallios flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter) or a BD LSR instrument (BD Biosciences). All data were processed using FlowJo or equivalent software.

Cell cycle analysis (BrdU staining)

Pregnant Mafb+/− females were injected intraperitoneally with 3 mg BrdU (Sigma) at E13.5. Embryonic FLs were harvested 2 hours later, flushed with PBS, and filtered through 40 µm strainers. After counting, cells were first labeled with Lineage-biotin cocktail for 30 minutes at 4 °C, then stained with c-Kit-PE, Sca-1-PE/Cy7, Streptavidin-PE-Texas Red, CD150-PE/Cy5, and CD48-APC for 30 minutes. After washing, cells were fixed and permeabilized using BrdU Staining Buffer Set (eBioscience) and incubated overnight at room temperature. Samples were treated with DNase I for 1 hour at 37 °C, followed by staining with BrdU-eFluor®450 (eBioscience). Final suspensions were prepared in Flow Cytometry Staining Buffer for acquisition.

Fetal liver HSC competitive transplantation

200 FL KSL cells (Lin−c-Kit+Sca-1+) were sorted from Ly5.1 WT or Mafb−/− E14.5 embryos directly into 96-well plates coated with FBS. Sorted FL cells (test cells, CD45.1+) were transplanted via intravenous injection into lethally irradiated (3.5 Gy ×2, 4 hours apart) Ly5.2 WT recipient mice along with 2×105 whole BM competitor cells from Ly5.1/5.2 WT mice (CD45.1+CD45.2+). BD FACSAriaTM II (BD Biosciences) was used for cell sorting.

Bone marrow isolation from mice transplanted with E14.5 WT or Mafb−/− fetal liver cells

BM-derived KSL, CD34+ KSL, or CD34− KSL cells (test cells, CD45.1+) were harvested from the Mafb−/− hematopoietic chimeric mice described above (generated by transplanting Ly5.1 Mafb−/− E14.5 FL cells into Ly5.2 WT recipients) or from control WT chimeric mice, typically 8-16 weeks after initial reconstitution. Specifically, 500 BM KSL cells, 200 BM CD34+ KSL cells, or 20 BM CD34− KSL cells were sorted. These test cells were transplanted via intravenous injection into lethally irradiated (3.5 Gy ×2, 4 hours apart) Ly5.2 WT recipient mice along with 2×105 whole BM competitor cells from Ly5.2 WT mice (CD45.2+).

Serial transplantation

At first transplantation, E14.5 FLs were collected from Ly5.1 Mafb−/− or WT embryos and flushed by using 23G needles and 2.5 ml syringes. 2×105 of those FL cells were transplanted into the tail veins of lethally X-ray irradiated (7 Gy) WT mice. At the second, third and fourth transplantation, 2×105 reconstituted BM cells from previous transplanted mice were transplanted into the tail veins of new lethally X-ray irradiated (7 Gy) WT mice.

Bone marrow–derived macrophages (BMDMs)

BM cells from 10-week-old Mafbf/f (WT) and Mafbf/f::Tie2-Cre (cKO) mice (n = 3 per genotype) were cultured in vitro to generate BMDMs as previously described10. Briefly, cells were plated at a density of 1×106 cells/mL in complete DMEM supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 10 ng/mL recombinant mouse M-CSF. Medium was replaced every 2 days, and after 6 days of culture, adherent macrophages were harvested for RNA extraction and quantitative RT-PCR analysis.

Colony assay

BM cells were flushed from femora of adult Mafbf/f::Tie2-Cre (Mafb cKO) mice with ice-cold PBS using a 20-G needle, passed through a 40-µm strainer, centrifuged (340×g, 4 min, 4 °C), washed twice, and counted by Trypan blue exclusion. A total of 1 × 105 viable cells were suspended in 1 mL MethoCult™ M3434 (STEMCELL Technologies) containing 1% penicillin–streptomycin, mixed gently, and 330 µL aliquots were plated in duplicate into a 4-well dish; the unused well contained PBS to maintain humidity. Cultures were incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2, and colonies were scored at 48 h (CFU-E) and day 7 (BFU-E, CFU-GM, CFU-Mix). Results are reported as the mean of duplicates

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (http://www.R-project.org). Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homoscedasticity or heteroscedasticity was evaluated with the F-test, Ansari–Bradley test, or Mood test. For parametric data, Welch’s t-test was applied, depending on the homoscedasticity. For non-parametric data, the Mann–Whitney U-test or Permuted Brunner–Munzel test was used, depending on the homoscedasticity. Survival curves were compared using the log-rank test.

GFP expression was also observed in progenitor populations, including common myeloid progenitors (CMPs) and granulocyte–macrophage progenitors (GMPs) (Supplementary Fig. 2B, C), further confirming that Mafb is expressed across subsets of BM HSPCs.

In the fetal liver (FL), FCM confirmed a similar expression pattern, with a higher proportion of GFP-positive KSL and LT-HSCs in Mafb+/− mice compared to WT (Fig. 1C; Supplementary Fig. 2D). To investigate potential functional redundancy among large Maf family members, we re-analyzed public RNA-seq datasets from both BM and FL LT-HSCs. This analysis revealed that while both Mafb and c-Maf are expressed, the expression of Mafa and Nrl was negligible in both HSC populations (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Collectively, these findings indicate that Mafb and c-Maf are the main large Maf family members expressed in both fetal and adult HSCs. In the present study, we focused on Mafb to delineate its specific functional role.

Next, we performed BrdU incorporation assays to directly measure cell cycle entry. Representative FCM analysis revealed a marked increase in BrdU-positive cells within the LT-HSC population of Mafb−/− embryos (64.7%) compared to WT controls (20.6%). Mafb deficiency impairs LT-HSC reconstitution in serial transplantation assays

Following transplantation with FL grafts, FCM analysis of BM from recipient mice reconstituted with WT or Mafb−/− FL cells confirmed that the frequency of KSL cells was unchanged in Mafb−/− recipients (Supplementary Fig. 7). This indicates that Mafb deficiency does not affect the size of the phenotypically defined HSPC compartment but may influence the functional capacity of HSCs. Therefore, to further evaluate the functional capacity of Mafb−/− HSCs, serial transplantation assays were performed using FL cells as donor cells. Donor cells from embryonic day 14.5 (E14.5) Ly5.1 WT or Mafb−/− embryos were transplanted into lethally irradiated Ly5.2 recipient mice. Following primary transplantation, serial transplantations were conducted approximately 6 months (25-28 weeks) to assess the long-term reconstitution potential (Fig. 4A). PB chimerism was analyzed at 8, 12, 16, and 20 weeks following the third transplantation (Fig. 4B). WT controls maintained chimerism levels above 95%, whereas Mafb−/− transplanted mice exhibited a slight decline at 16 and 20 weeks (p < 0.01). In the fourth transplantation, survival analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in the survival rate of mice receiving Mafb−/− donor cells compared to WT controls (Fig. 4C, p < 0.001). Most recipients transplanted with Mafb−/− cells failed to survive beyond 30 weeks, whereas WT donor-transplanted recipient mice maintained survival rates above 90%. Further analysis of BM HSC populations after the fourth transplantation (31 weeks) revealed a significant reduction in the proportion of CD34−CD150+KSL LT-HSCs in Mafb−/− recipients compared to WT controls (Fig. 4D, p < 0.05). FCM analysis confirmed this decline, reinforcing the notion of HSC exhaustion. Together, these results support the view that MAFB is important for maintaining the long-term self-renewal capacity of HSCs, and its deficiency is associated with progressive functional exhaustion under replicative stress.

Representative FCM plots at 16 weeks post-transplantation demonstrated the presence of both test and competitor cell populations in recipient mice (Fig. 5B). Longitudinal analysis revealed that Mafb−/− KSL test cells exhibited a significant reduction in engraftment from 8 weeks post-transplantation compared to WT, with differences persisting till 16 weeks and a decline in trend at 20 weeks (Fig. 5C). Similarly, Mafb−/− CD34− KSL test cells showed a progressive decline in chimerism from 8 weeks onward, with chimerism levels dropping to less than half of those observed in WT at 8 and 12 weeks (p < 0.01) and remaining relatively decreased compared to WT at later time points (Fig. 5D).

In contrast, the chimerism of the CD34+ KSL test cell population remained stable over time, with no significant differences between Mafb−/− and WT test cells at any time point (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, lineage differentiation into B cells, T cells, and myeloid cells was comparable between WT and Mafb−/− test cells across all analyzed subpopulations (Fig. 5F, Supplementary Fig. 8). These results suggest that Mafb deficiency selectively impairs the long-term engraftment capacity of KSL and CD34− KSL HSCs, potentially reflecting increased cell cycle activity and premature exhaustion.

Discussion

MAFB was reported as a pivotal regulator of HSC fate, intricately controlling differentiation processes with a notable preference towards myeloid lineage without affecting lymphoid or erythroid populations12. On the other hand, MAFB was also found to be important for self-renewal in functional macrophages13. However, the role of its function in HSC remains elusive. Our study has found the stage-specific roles of MAFB in HSCs. Mafb deficiency promotes increased proliferation, cell cycle entry and early expansion in myeloid lineage in FL HSCs (Fig. 2C, D and Fig. 3). However, in adult BM HSCs, this deficiency appears to impair long-term regenerative capacity, potentially contributing to the observed stem cell exhaustion (Figs. 4, Fig. 5C,D). This early myeloid expansion is directionally consistent with previous findings (Fig. 2D), although the tendency was less pronounced in our experimental system. Such differences might be attributed to variations in experimental conditions or analytical approaches.

The increased BrdU incorporation in Mafb−/− FL LT-HSCs indicates accelerated cell-cycle entry (Fig. 3B). Bernitz et al. recently demonstrated that HSCs possess the ability to "count and remember" self-renewal divisions, which influences their functional maintenance over time14. We proposed that higher frequency of cell cycle in Mafb deficient HSCs (Fig. 3B) leads to excessive proliferation. As a result, these cells may prematurely reach their self-renewal limit and eventually lose the ability to divide (Figs. 4 and 5). Our conditional Mafb deletion experiments also supported this hypothesis, which also revealed abnormalities in the maintenance of quiescence that appears essential for long-term HSC preservation (Fig. 6C, D). Comparable phenotypes are seen in p21−/− HSCs, where loss of p21 increases cycling and leads to exhaustion15. While Cdk6 deficiency or kinase-dead knock-in impaired exit from quiescence and enhanced long-term HSC maintenance16,17, indicating that p21 maintains HSC quiescence by inhibiting CDK6 activity, while CDK6 drives HSCs out of quiescence. In addition, the disruption of the lncRNA-MAF:MAFB network reduces CDKN1A (p21) expression during differentiation in human keratinocytes, indicating that Mafb could regulate p2118. Together with our data, this supports the importance of MAFB in regulating HSC cell cycle and maintenance across developmental stages.

The phenotype of Mafb-deficient HSCs parallels that of several known quiescence regulators, including CD150, CD81, osteopontin (OPN), HIF1α19–21, TGF-β, CD38, CD201 and Neogenin-1 (NEO1). In mice, CD150+ HSCs are largely quiescent with durable self-renewal potential, whereas CD150− HSCs exhibit higher proliferative and lymphoid-biased activity but retain the capacity to generate CD150+ HSCs, supporting a dynamic transition between proliferative and dormant states22,23. Similarly, CD81 deficiency disrupts HSC quiescence, leading to increased HSC cycling and eventual exhaustion, while OPN deletion expands HSCs but impairs their long-term repopulating ability. HIF1α protects HSCs from oxidative stress in hypoxic niches, thereby maintaining their functional integrity. TGF-β signaling preserves the dormancy of adult CD34−CD150+ LT-HSCs, with neutralization inducing cell cycle entry and exposure reinforcing hibernation24. In parallel, CD38 has been placed within a dormancy network that engages c-Fos and p57 (Cdkn1c) to sustain HSC quiescence25. MAFB itself can form heterodimers with Fos family proteins, providing a biochemical link to AP-1–dependent transcription relevant for HSC activation1. For niche-derived regulation, NEO1 maintains LT-HSC quiescence through netrin-1 signaling, and loss of NEO1 function leads to transient HSC expansion followed by premature exhaustion26. In Mpl-deficient mice, enforced CD201 expression accelerates the re-entry of HSCs into quiescence after transplantation, accompanied by Bcl-xL upregulation, highlighting CD201’s role in facilitating the return to a dormant state27. Collectively, these regulators converge on maintaining the balance between dormancy and activation, though they operate through distinct mechanisms: CD150, CD81, and CD201 mainly demarcate or reinforce quiescent states; OPN and TGF-β act via niche-derived cues; HIF1α provides metabolic adaptation; CD38 and Mafb connect quiescence to transcriptional programs; and NEO1 integrates niche signaling with long-term self-renewal. These shared and divergent features suggest that Mafb may function together with this regulatory network, and further studies are needed to define its precise positioning in the hierarchy governing HSC fate.

It has been reported that FL HSCs and BM HSCs present different characteristics. Fetal HSCs exhibit remarkably high proliferative activity to establish the nascent blood system, demonstrating up to a 38-fold expansion in FL28,29. Compared to high cell cycle in fetal HSCs, adult HSCs primarily reside in the BM in a quiescent G0 state, preserving their stemness and mitigating exhaustion30. Notably, Mafb is expressed at significantly higher levels during embryogenesis than in adulthood, which suggests MAFB plays roles in fetal HSCs31. In line with this, our study showed that Mafb deficiency in FL HSCs markedly augmented proliferative activity and cell cycle progression, indicating that MAFB could be a negative regulator controlling proliferation during embryonic hematopoiesis (Fig. 3). In contrast, BM HSCs with Mafb deficiency showed decreased chimerism rate after serial transplantation and reduced colony-forming potential (Fig. 4B-D, Fig. 6E). This may result from increased cell cycle entry in Mafb-deficient HSCs, leading to exhaustion during adulthood. However, more evidence is needed to decipher the underlying mechanism of how Mafb deficiency affects the function of BM HSCs. Retinoic acid (RA) signaling, a well-characterized downstream pathway of vitamin A, modulates Mafb expression through retinoic acid receptors (RARs)32. In macrophages, RA induces Mafb expression33, and in hematopoietic contexts, RA activity promotes HSC formation during development and maintains quiescence in adults34. Vitamin A deficiency, conversely, leads to excessive proliferation and impaired long-term function35. In agreement with these findings, our data show that Mafb deficiency enhances cell cycle entry in FL HSCs, supporting the view that MAFB may act downstream of RA signaling to preserve HSC dormancy. GSK3, a serine/threonine kinase, has been shown to enhance MAFB activity through phosphorylation36. Notably, transient inhibition of GSK3 promotes HSC proliferation but eventually depletes stem cell pools37. This transient expansion followed by exhaustion closely mirrors the phenotype of Mafb-deficient FL HSCs observed in our transplantation assays. c-MYB, a transcription factor central to hematopoietic regulation, suppresses MAFB transactivation through SUMOylation in macrophages38. Loss of c-Myb in HSCs leads to increased proliferationf with reduced self-renewal capacity39,40, and even partial loss (Myb+/−) produces a phenotype strikingly similar to that seen in Mafb-deficient HSCs41. Although a direct regulatory link between c-MYB and MAFB in HSCs remains to be established, these parallels suggest a functional intersection worth investigating. In addition, our prior work showed that PGE2 stimulates Mafb expression in macrophages via EP4 receptor signaling5. Since PGE2 was known to support HSC survival and expansion42, it is plausible that MAFB may participate in a negative feedback loop, counteracting PGE2-induced proliferation to maintain HSC homeostasis. Further studies are required to explore this regulatory axis in detail. GATA2 is indispensable for adult HSC maintenance and has been reported to downregulate Mafb in dendritic cell progenitors43,44. While GATA2 loss rapidly depletes HSCs, our data show that Mafb deficiency results in progressive rather than acute exhaustion. This indicates that GATA2 likely governs HSC function through both Mafb-dependent and -independent pathways. β-adrenergic signaling, through β-agonists such as isoproterenol, increases Mafb expression in hematopoietic progenitors and skews differentiation toward myeloid lineages45. In our study, Mafb-deficient FL HSCs showed an early myeloid expansion, suggesting that MAFB may act downstream of β-adrenergic signaling to fine-tune lineage output during development. HOXA10, a homeobox transcription factor, has been reported to upregulate Mafb and promote expansion of hematopoietic progenitors46,47. Overexpression of HOXA10 in human CD34+ cells increase self-renewal-associated gene expression48. In contrast, loss of Mafb in our model leads to increased proliferation but reduced long-term reconstitution. This discrepancy may reflect differences between stem and progenitor populations or species-specific regulatory networks. Taken together, these upstream factors converge on MAFB as a key effector modulating HSC proliferation, quiescence, and lineage balance. Our findings not only align with several of these regulatory pathways but also emphasize the developmental context in which MAFB exerts its distinct functions.

Our findings share common ground with those reported by Sarrazin et al., particularly regarding the role of MAFB in restraining HSC proliferation and promoting a myeloid-biased differentiation12. However, our study reveals a notable difference in long-term HSC function. While Sarrazin et al. observed sustained self-renewal in Mafb-deficient adult HSCs, we found that FL-derived Mafb−/− HSCs underwent progressive exhaustion after serial transplantation. This discrepancy likely arises from developmental stage differences and experimental strategies. Whereas their study used polyI:C-inducible Mx1-Cre in adult mice, we employed direct transplantation of E14.5 fetal HSCs from germline-deficient embryos into irradiated recipients. These developmental and contextual differences, including the stress of irradiation, differential niche environments, and epigenetic memory of fetal HSCs, may account for the divergence in long-term repopulation outcomes. Our results highlight the importance of considering developmental timing and transplantation context when interpreting MAFB function in HSC regulation.

The use of a Mafb-GFP knock-in model also introduces the possibility of gene dosage effects. Notably, MafbGFP/+ mice occasionally exhibited intermediate phenotypes compared to wild-type and knockout littermates, suggesting that partial reduction in Mafb expression may influence HSC behavior independently of complete gene deletion. Although we did not perform quantitative comparisons across genotypes in this study, future experiments directly comparing homozygous and heterozygous animals under identical conditions will help determine whether gene dosage contributes to the observed phenotypes. On the other hand, a limitation of our BrdU analyses is the absence of doublet discrimination and live-dead exclusion, due to fixation/permeabilization and instrument constraints. Because all samples were processed identically, any bias would have been systematic rather than genotype specific. The finding of increased cycling in Mafb−/− HSCs is further supported by transplantation assays, providing complementary evidence that the phenotype reflects a biological effect rather than a technical artifact. While our transplantation-based approaches were comprehensive, they may not fully reflect steady-state hematopoiesis. The stress induced by lethal irradiation and subsequent transplantation creates a regenerative context that differs from physiological homeostasis, potentially altering HSC behavior, cycling kinetics, or lineage commitment49. Additional studies under unperturbed conditions will be needed to confirm the generalizability of these findings. We also acknowledge that the Tie2-Cre system may induce recombination not only in hematopoietic cells but also in endothelial and lymphatic endothelial compartments50. This raises the possibility that some observed phenotypes may reflect non-cell-autonomous effects. Since MAFB is known to play a role in lymphatic vessel development51, we cannot exclude the potential contribution of altered vascular or lymphatic microenvironments to HSC regulation. However, the phenotypic abnormalities observed in our study, particularly the reduced LT-HSC pool and impaired colony-forming ability, were primarily confined to the hematopoietic compartment. These findings are consistent with a cell-intrinsic contribution of MAFB, although non-cell-autonomous effects cannot be excluded. Nevertheless, to definitively address this question, future studies employing HSC-specific Cre drivers such as Vav-iCre or Scl-CreERT2 will be essential.

Altogether, our study identifies MAFB as a critical factor for maintaining long-term HSC function in a developmental stage-dependent manner. Beyond its known influence on lineage bias, MAFB emerges as a key regulator of HSC exhaustion under both intrinsic and niche-influenced conditions.
