Transport and inhibition mechanisms of human glycine transporter 2
Authors: Wenming Ji, Yuanzhi Yu, Zirui Liu, Junwen Su, Qianyu Wang, Mulin Lai, Jing-Xiang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neuronal human glycine transporter 2 (hGlyT2) plays a critical role in maintaining glycinergic neurotransmission via the reuptake of glycine into presynaptic neurons by using the driving force of sodium and chloride ion gradients. hGlyT2 represents an important drug target for analgesic purpose. However, its structure and the molecular mechanisms remain elusive. Here, we report structures of hGlyT2 in three functional states, including the apo state, the substrate glycine-bound state, and the inhibitor-bound states. The apo state of hGlyT2 adopts an inward conformation. The substrate glycine binds at the central pocket of hGlyT2 in its occluded conformation. Both inhibitors, ORG25543 and opiranserin, bind to an allosteric site, which is vertical to the extracellular tunnel, buried under the extracellular loop 4 (EL4) and near to the transmembrane helix 1b (TM1b). These inhibitors act as wedges to prevent the inward movement of TM1b and closure of the extracellular gate. Further structural analysis reveals both global and local conformational changes associated with the ions and glycine binding and release. These structures define the mechanisms governing transport and allosteric inhibition in hGlyT2, providing a blueprint for further development of non-opioid analgesics targeting hGlyT2.

Introduction

Glycine is a critically important neurotransmitter with diverse functions within the central nervous system. Its primary role is as an inhibitory neurotransmitter, particularly in the brainstem and spinal cord, where it modulates excitatory tone in the ascending pain pathways to prevent excessive nociceptive signaling1–5. The synaptic concentration of glycine is tightly regulated by two sodium- and chloride-dependent transporters: hGlyT1 (SLC6A9) and hGlyT2 (SLC6A5)6,7. These transporters exhibit complementary distribution and functional specialization within the nervous system. hGlyT1 is broadly expressed throughout the central nervous system and primarily influences glycine availability in astrocytes and glutamatergic neurons, thereby modulating N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) activity8,9. Conversely, hGlyT2 is predominantly localized in presynaptic glycinergic inhibitory neurons, where it facilitates the rapid removal of glycine from synaptic clefts and recycles it into synaptic vesicles for subsequent release, thus maintaining inhibitory neurotransmission10–12.

Genetic and pharmacological studies underscore the essential role of hGlyT2 in inhibitory signaling. hGlyT2 knockout mice display markedly reduced glycinergic inhibition and develop severe motor disturbances, often resulting in death shortly after birth13. In humans, mutations in the SLC6A5 gene are associated with hyperekplexia—a neurological disorder characterized by exaggerated startle responses involving defects in presynaptic glycinergic transmission14. Conversely, partial knockdown of hGlyT2 using small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) has demonstrated a reduction in allodynia in rat models of neuropathic pain without observable adverse effects on motor or respiratory functions15. These findings emphasize hGlyT2’s pivotal role in glycine-mediated inhibition and suggest that selective inhibition of hGlyT2 could serve as a therapeutic strategy to alleviate neuropathic pain with minimal side effects.

Neuropathic pain, resulting from nerve injury or disease, is highly prevalent and significantly diminishes patients’ quality of life16. The critical involvement of hGlyT2 in pain processing has spurred the development of hGlyT2 inhibitors as potential analgesic agents. One of the earliest hGlyT2 inhibitors, ORG25543, has been extensively employed as a pharmacological tool in both in vitro and in vivo studies17. ORG25543 exhibits high selectivity for hGlyT2 and effectively alleviates hyperalgesia across various pain models15,18,19. However, owing to its pseudo-irreversible binding characteristic, by virtue of a slow off-rate, ORG25543 can induce tremors and mortality with prolonged exposure or at high doses20,21. Irreversible hGlyT2 inhibitors tend to cause sustained increases in cytosolic glycine concentrations, which can lead to long-term alterations in neuronal function and heightened motor side effects—phenotypically resembling hGlyT2 knockouts13,15. To mitigate these adverse effects, a series of ORG25543-based derivatives with reversible binding properties have been developed, successfully providing analgesia in rodent models of neuropathic pain without significant side effects—phenotypically resembling partial hGlyT2 knockdowns15,21–23. Among these, opiranserin (VVZ-149), a structural analogue of ORG25543 crafted by Vivozon, Inc., demonstrates moderate hGlyT2 inhibitory potency23. Notably, opiranserin effectively reduces postoperative pain and diminishes opioid consumption, with no reported serious adverse events24,25. In 2024, opiranserin achieved marketing approval in South Korea, representing a significant advancement in the field of analgesic therapeutics. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms underlying the differences in affinity and dissociation kinetics between ORG25543 and opiranserin remain poorly understood, posing a challenge to the rational design of this new class of pain relief drugs and impeding further clinical development.

In this study, we successfully deciphered the structures of hGlyT2 in the apo, substrate-bound, ORG25543-bound and opiranserin-bound states at resolutions of 3.13 Å, 3.10 Å, 2.73 Å, and 3.20 Å, respectively. These structures were respectively stabilized within three functional conformations: outward (ORG25543- and opiranserin-bound states), occluded (substrate-bound state), and inward (apo state) conformation, which propels our comprehensive understanding of the substrate and ions translocation mechanism of hGlyT2. In addition, by comparing the structures of hGlyT2 and hGlyT1 bound to different inhibitors, we have uncovered the structural basis of their differences in selectivity, affinity, and dissociation rates of inhibitors. Consequently, this work has established a solid structural basis for the development of drugs that target these transporters.

Results and discussion

Structure determination and architecture of hGlyT2

To elucidate the structural mechanisms of substrate recognition and inhibition in hGlyT2, we engineered a full-length construct with a C-terminal GFP-MBP fusion to assess protein homogeneity and stability via fluorescence-detection size-exclusion chromatography (FSEC). Given the essential roles of Na+ and Cl- in driving substrate transport, the protein was extracted and purified in a buffer containing 150 mM NaCl, and stabilized by lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (L-MNG) and cholesteryl hemisuccinate (CHS) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Subsequent cryo-EM data collection and image processing yielded high-resolution density maps of hGlyT2 in four distinct states: the apo state, the glycine-bound state, and the inhibitor-bound states (bound to ORG25543 or opiranserin), at resolutions of 3.13 Å, 3.10 Å, 3.20 Å, and 2.73 Å, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 2–5 and Supplementary Table 1).

The sharpened maps permitted the atomic modeling of hGlyT2, with well-resolved side-chain density across twelve transmembrane helices (TM1–TM12) (Fig. 1a-c and Supplementary Fig. 6). The arrangement of these helices conforms to the classic LeuT fold26–31, featuring a pseudo-twofold symmetric core (TM1-5 and TM6-10) where unwound segments in TM1 and TM6 provide the structural plasticity requisite for transport (Fig. 1b, c). While most connecting loops were unambiguously modeled, the extensive N-terminus (residues 1–194), C-terminus (788-797) and a segment of EL2 (residues 333–363)—encompassing the predicted N-glycosylation sites at N343, N353, and N358 were not resolved, presumably due to their intrinsically disordered nature (Supplementary Fig. 7). In addition, residues 417–421 in TM5 were omitted from the model due to weak and discontinuous density. To delineate the substrate-transport pathway, we utilized the HOLE algorithm32 to evaluate the pore dimensions in the apo state. Our analysis revealed a prominent extracellular cavity at the entrance of the transporter (Fig. 1d). However, upon progressing toward the substrate-binding site, the extracellular vestibule was significantly obstructed, with a radius less than 1.4 Å. Glycine binding pocket of hGlyT2

To assess the transport activity of hGlyT2, we performed the [3H]glycine uptake assays33. hGlyT2 exhibited glycine transport with a Km of 369.5 µM (Fig. 2a), consistent with the similar value (Km = 271 µM) measured previously34. To investigate the molecular basis of glycine recognition, we determined the structure of hGlyT2 in complex with glycine at 3.10 Å (Supplementary Fig. 3). The map shows an unambiguous density in substrate-binding pocket that perfectly accommodates the glycine molecule (Fig. 2b). The glycine-bound structure reveals a canonical occluded conformation, where the substrate resides in a cavity midway through the membrane bilayer (~20 Å from both surfaces) (Figs. 1f, 2c, d). Within this site, glycine is anchored by a sophisticated hydrogen-bonding network involving TMs 1, 3, 6 and 8. Specifically, residues A208, G210, G212, Y287, F476 and S479 coordinate the carboxyl and amino groups of glycine, which is validated by the significantly impaired uptake activity of hGlyT2 in the corresponding mutants (Fig. 2d,e). Additionally, W482 stabilizes the amino group of glycine via cation-π interactions, which are further reinforced by T-shaped π-π stacking with F476 and hydrogen bonding with S479 (Fig. 2d-f). These observations align with the known loss of function in W482L/F/Y variants and the pathogenic W482R mutation identified in hyperekplexia patients35–37. Notably, W482L and W482F mutations have been reported to broaden substrate specificity, enabling the transport of larger amino acids such as alanine, leucine, and methionine35. In the glycine-bound hGlyT2 structure, the amino group of glycine is oriented toward S479, which participates in a stabilizing network through hydrogen bonds with residues Y207, F476 and W482 (Fig. 2f). Comparative analysis reveals that isoform-specific substitutions at F476 and S479 (corresponding to Y316 and G319 in hGlyT1) significantly constrict the binding pocket of hGlyT2 (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Figs. 7, 8a). Specifically, the Cα distances from these residues to the opposing G210TM1 are shortened to 9.8 Å and 9.5 Å, compared to 10.6 Å and 10.3 Å in hGlyT1, respectively (Fig. 2g). Unlike hGlyT1, hGlyT2 fails to transport the sarcosine, and this narrowed pocket architecture likely dictates the exclusive substrate specificity of hGlyT2. Based on the sarcosine-bound hGlyT1, we reasoned that the N-methyl group of sarcosine would sterically clash with the larger side chain of S479 in hGlyT2 (Supplementary Fig. 8b). To test this hypothesis, we performed sarcosine-d3 uptake assays and [3H]glycine competitive inhibition experiments. The results showed that, compared with wild-type hGlyT2, the F476Y&S479G double mutant exhibits markedly enhanced recognition and transport of sarcosine (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Fig. 8c). Thus, the compact binding environment defined by F476 and S479 is a key determinant for hGlyT2’s strict selectivity for glycine over sarcosine.

Allosteric inhibition of hGlyT2 by ORG25543 and opiranserin

The selective hGlyT2 inhibitors ORG25543 and opiranserin dose-dependently inhibited the transport activity of hGlyT2, with IC50 values of approximately 18.4 nM and 1.1 μM, respectively (Fig. 3a). These results are in close agreement with the high affinity of ORG25543 (IC50 = 16 nM) and the low affinity of opiranserin (IC50 = 0.86 μM) reported previously17,23. We determined the structures of hGlyT2 in complex with ORG25543 and opiranserin at resolutions of 3.20 Å and 2.73 Å, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 4, 5 and Supplementary Table 1). Analysis of the density maps reveals that the central substrate-binding site is accessible to the bulk solvent through an open extracellular vestibule, whereas the intracellular vestibule remains occluded (Fig. 3b). Detailed structural analysis of the cryo-EM maps reveals that both ORG25543 and opiranserin inhibitors are sandwiched in an allosteric pocket defined by TMs 1b, 3, 7, 8, 10 and EL4. Both inhibitors penetrate deeply into the hGlyT2 scaffold and are buried beneath EL4, with only a minimal surface area exposed to the extracellular vestibule (Fig. 3b-d). Specifically, the terminal dimethylamino group (G1, Fig. 3a) is solvent-exposed and forms hydrogen bonds with residues N213TM1b and D633TM10 (subsite I, Fig. 3c-e). The adjacent moiety (G2, Fig. 3a) inserts into a hydrophobic pocket formed by residues L211, G212, Y286, Y287, I290, P543, F629 and D633 (subsite II, Fig. 3c-e). Within this subsite, both inhibitors engage in nearly identical interactions, despite that the tetrahydropyran ring of opiranserin, containing an additional polar oxygen is larger than the five-membered cyclopentane ring of ORG25543. Notably, this hydrophobic subsite does not form favorable interactions with the polar oxygen of opiranserin. Instead, the increased polar volume may lower the energetic barrier for dissociation from this subsite (Fig. 3d). The central dimethoxybenzamide moiety (G3, Fig. 3a) is sandwiched between TM1b and EL4 (subsite III), establishing a robust interaction network with hGlyT2 (Fig. 3c-e). This includes π-π stacking interactions with both W215TM1b and P543EL4, complemented by a hydrogen bond and a salt-bridge interaction with R216TM1b. Finally, the hydrophobic G4 (Fig. 3a) group is deeply buried in a hydrophobic cavity (subsite IV) formed by a cluster of residues from TM1b, EL4 and TM8 (Fig. 3c-e). The phenoxy group of ORG25543 establishes extensive hydrophobic contacts with V519EL4, I520EL4 and M570TM8, while its phenyl ring participates in multiple T-shaped π-π interactions with W215TM1b, Y550EL4 and F567TM8 (Fig. 3c). In striking contrast, these interactions are substantially attenuated in the opiranserin-bound structure (Fig. 3c-e). The smaller 3-carbon butyl group of opiranserin not only eliminates the hydrophobic contacts with the side chains of V519, Y550 and M570, but also abolishes the π-π interactions with W215, Y550 and F567 (Fig. 3c-e). These structural divergences provide a compelling molecular rationale for the ~60-fold difference in IC50 values (18.4 nM for ORG25543 versus 1.1 μM for opiranserin) and may help alleviate the slow dissociation and associated liabilities of ORG25543. To validate this hypothesis, we conducted site-directed mutagenesis. While the Y550L mutation has been reported to mildly impair the inhibitory effect of ORG25543 on hGlyT238, we further engineered V519A and M570A variants. Although these mutations preserved the transport function of hGlyT2, they exhibited markedly diminished sensitivity to ORG25543, with IC50 values shifted to 115.1 nM and 40.2 nM, respectively. Conversely, their sensitivity to opiranserin remained largely unaffected (IC50 of 0.67 μM and 0.80 μM, respectively) (Fig. 3f-g). These findings demonstrate that V519 and M570 are indispensable for the high-affinity inhibition of ORG25543, whereas they play a negligible role in opiranserin binding.

The closely related hGlyT1 is less sensitive to the inhibition of transport activity by ORG25543 or opiranserin39,40. Sequence alignment analysis shows that most hGlyT2 residues interacting with ORG25543 or opiranserin are conserved except for I290 (V145 in hGlyT1) and F629 (L470 in hGlyT1) at the subsite II and I545 (L145 in hGlyT1) at the subsite IV (Supplementary Fig. 7). Notably, previously characterized structures of hGlyT1 in outward conformation (bound to SSR504734 or PF-03463275) exhibit high architectural similarity to the hGlyT2-inhibitor complexes (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). Structural superposition indicates that portions of SSR504734 and PF-03463275 align with ORG25543 and insert into the subsite II (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). Intriguingly, while these hGlyT1 inhibitors occupy a similar space within subsite II, their larger chemical entities—the trifluoromethyl-substituted phenyl ring in SSR504734 and the 3-chloro-4-fluoro moiety in PF-03463275, directly engage the smaller V145 and L470 residues in hGlyT1, but encounter steric clashes with the larger side chains of I290 and F629 in hGlyT2 (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). Consequently, we hypothesized that the substitution of smaller sidechain residues at the subsite II (V145 and L470) in hGlyT1 enlarges the binding pocket, thereby diminishing the residues’ capacity for contact with inhibitors and reducing binding affinity of ORG25543 and opiranserin. To validate this speculation, we engineered I290V and F629L mutants and evaluated their impact on inhibitor potency. Both mutations resulted in markedly diminished sensitivity to ORG25543, with IC50 values shifted to 177.0 nM and 93.5 nM, approximately 10 and 5 times higher than those of the wild-type hGlyT2, respectively (Fig. 3g). Similarly, the IC50 of opiranserin increased significantly from 1.1 μM in the wild-type hGlyT2 to 5.8 μM for the F629L mutant (Fig. 3g). These findings demonstrate that the bulky side chains of I290 and F629 contribute to the increased susceptibility of hGlyT2 to ORG25543 and opiranserin.

Strikingly, the spatial dimensions of the ORG25543-binding site in hGlyT2 closely mirror the allosteric pocket occupied by MRS7292 in the human dopamine transporter (hDAT). Sequence conservation analysis confirms that most residues interacting with ORG25543 are largely conserved in hDAT, with only minor substitutions involving similar hydrophobic residues: Y286 (F155hDAT), I520 (V364hDAT), I545 (L389hDAT) and A546 (I390hDAT) (Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Fig. 9c). However, compared to MRS7292-bound hDAT, the binding of ORG25543 to hGlyT2 induces a pronounced conformational readjustment, displacing TM1b and EL4a by 2.5 Å and 4.4 Å, and rotating them by 9.0° and 3.2°, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 9c). This structural remodeling of TM1b, EL4a, and the EL4a-EL4b turn effectively tailors the pocket to accommodate the unique G1-G4 moieties of ORG25543. These observations suggest that ORG25543 and opiranserin binding induce the conformational changes of hGlyT2 via an induced-fit mechanism, analogous to that observed for MRS7292 binding to hDAT. Taken together, our results suggest that this allosteric regulatory site may represent a conserved yet functionally specialized feature across the SLC6 transporters, capable of binding diverse, scaffold-specific inhibitors.

Ion binding of the hGlyT2 in three different conformations

hGlyT2 leverages the Na+/Cl− gradient to drive glycine uptake41–44. The ~3 Å resolution cryo-EM maps allow us to identify the binding sites for these ions. In the occluded conformation, the Na1 site is coordinated by the carboxyl oxygen of glycine, residues A208TM1, N213TM1 and N509TM7, the side chain hydroxyl oxygen of S477TM6a, and the main chain nitrogen of S478TM6a (Fig. 4a). The Na2 site is coordinated by the main-chain carbonyls of G206TM1, V209TM1 and L574TM8, along with the side chain hydroxyl oxygen of T578TM8 (Fig. 4b). The Cl− site is defined by a cluster of residues including N213TM1a, Y233TM1b, Q473TM6a, S473TM6, N509TM7 and S513TM7 (Fig. 4c). However, no convincing isolated, spherical density characteristic of a Na⁺ ion was observed in our maps at the proposed Na3 site41. Given the limited resolution, definitive assignment of a third sodium site lies beyond the scope of the current structural data. In the outward conformation, while densities corresponding to the Na1 and Cl− sites remain visible, the Na2 site lacks discernible density (Fig. 4d-f). Superimposition of the local structural elements forming the Na2 site (K197-A208 of TM1a and S560-D593 of TM8) reveals that its geometric organization in the outward conformation is distinctly modified compared to the occluded conformation. Specifically, G206 undergoes a 1.1 Å displacement toward the Na2 site, which probably precludes sodium occupancy by disrupting the required coordination geometry (Fig. 4b, e). In the inward conformation, no discernible density is observed at the Na1 and Na2 sites (Fig. 4g, h), whereas the Cl− site remains occupied (Fig. 4i). The disruption of the ion-binding sites in the inward conformation is primarily driven by a substantial translocation of TM1a. Specifically, the carbonyl groups of the sodium-coordinating residues A208TM1a and G206TM1a shift by 4.3 Å and 4.9 Å, respectively, away from the Na1 and Na2 sites (Fig. 4a, b, g, h). Integrating these findings with previous studies reveals a coordinated mechanism: ORG25543 or opiranserin binding is sodium-independent and can stabilize hGlyT2 in the outward conformation38,39. Upon transition to the occluded conformation, hGlyT2 chelates the Na+ and Cl− ions to facilitate substrate binding. Subsequently, the transition to the inward conformation involves the dramatic displacement of TM1a, which disrupts the Na1 and Na2 sites, thereby promoting the release of sodium ions and substrate into the cytoplasm.

Conformational transition of hGlyT2

The structures of hGlyT2 in the outward, occluded, and inward conformations provide a comprehensive framework for elucidating the conformational transitions during the transport cycle. To delineate the transition from the outward conformation to the occluded conformation, we superimposed the opiranserin-bound outward structure with the glycine-bound occluded structure, yielding a root-mean-square deviation (R.M.S.D.) value of 2.43 Å over 543 Cα atoms. This transition is characterized by a pronounced inward movement of the extracellular segments of TM1b and TM6a, which tilt by ~18° and 27°, respectively (Fig. 5a). These rotations drive the assembly of the extracellular gate (EC-gate). In the outward conformation, the Cα-Cα distances between key EC-gate residue pairs R216TM1b/D633TM10 and Y286TM3/ F476TM6a are 13.8 Å and 16.2 Å, respectively (Fig. 5b). In this conformation, R216 and D633 are engaged in stabilizing the inhibitors, forming hydrogen bonds with the carbonyl oxygen (G3) and a salt bridge with the amino nitrogen (G1), respectively (Fig. 3c-e). Upon transition to the occluded conformation, these residues undergo a dramatic reorganization: Y286TM3 directly contacts F476TM6a, while R216TM1b forms a salt bridge and a hydrogen bond with D633TM10 (Fig. 5b). This gate closure is further reinforced by a network of polar contacts involving Y219TM1b and Q473TM6a, effectively obstructing the extracellular pathway (Fig. 5b). Notably, structural comparison of TM1 reveals that the N-terminal segment of TM1b (N213-W215) undergoes a remarkable ~180° rotation during this transition (Fig. 5b). Opiranserin binding sterically precludes this rotation, as the inhibitor would directly clash with the inward-positioned TM1b of the occluded conformation, thereby locking hGlyT2 in the outward conformation (Fig. 5b). Beyond TM1b and TM6a, substantial structural remodeling occurs in TM2, EL3 and EL4 (Fig. 5c). The extracellular end of TM2 in the outward conformation tilts by 28° and undergoes a 6.4 Å shift (Cα − Cα distance of F229) towards its corresponding position in the occluded conformation (Fig. 5c, d). Simultaneously, EL3a and EL3b move toward the scaffold domain—the relatively rigid core region that provides a stable structural framework—by 3.1 Å (Cα − Cα distance of I454) and 6.8 Å (Cα − Cα distance of T462), respectively (Fig. 5d). Furthermore, the end of EL4a (Cα − Cα distance of P543) and EL4b tilts (Cα − Cα distance of R556) upward by 3.4 Å (Fig. 5d). To understand the occluded-to-inward transition, we compared the glycine-bound structure with the apo structure. These two structures are well superimposed with an R.M.S.D. value of 1.51 Å over 553 Cα atoms (Fig. 5e). Notably, TM1a exhibits the most significant conformational change between these two conformations (Fig. 5e, f). In the occluded conformation, three critical intracellular gate (IC-gate) residues on the N-terminal loop form interactions to seal off the substrate permeation tunnel to the cytosol. R191 not only establishes a salt bridge with D592TM8, but also forms a hydrogen bond with Y491TM6 (Fig. 5f). Moreover, K197 forms a salt bridge with D501TM7, and W194 forms a π-π stacking and a hydrogen bond with Y491TM6(Fig. 5f). Upon transition to the inward conformation, TM1a undergoes a dramatic 8.6 Å translocation, accompanied by an ~20.5° rotation towards the membrane (Fig. 5e, f). Concurrently, the Cα distance between Y207 and S479 increases from 7.6 Å to 10.0 Å, and the side chain of the conserved residue Y207 flips by 90° away from the intracellular tunnel, abolishing its hydrogen bond with S479 (Fig. 5f). Additionally, K197 also shifts by 8.6 Å, disrupting the IC-gate interaction network and effectively breaking the cytosolic seal (Fig. 5f). Collectively, these structural changes lead to the full opening of the intracellular tunnel, facilitating the release of glycine and co-transported ions into the cytosol.

In summary, our structural characterization of hGlyT2 across three distinct conformations: the benzamide-based inhibitor-bound outward, the substrate-bound occluded, and the apo inward conformations, provides a comprehensive mechanistic blueprint for the hGlyT2 transport cycle (Fig. 1f and Fig. 6). In the outward conformation, an extracellular tunnel defined by TMs 1, 3, 6, and 10 facilitates substrate access to the central binding site (Fig. 6). Benzamide-related chemotype inhibitors, ORG25543 and opiranserin, bind at an allosteric site formed by TM1b, TM6a, EL4, and TM10, effectively immobilizing TM1b and TM6a to prevent conformational transition to the occluded conformation (Fig. 6). Notably, opiranserin possesses a relatively polar G2 and a linear three-carbon butyl G4 group, resulting in fewer interactions at subsite IV and yielding lower affinity and higher reversibility compared to ORG25543 for hGlyT2. The transition to the occluded conformation is orchestrated by the coordinated inward shift of TM1b and TM6a toward TM3 and TM10, leading to tight EC-gate closure through R216-D633 and Y286-F476 interactions (Fig. 5a, b). Simultaneously, the IC-gate is maintained by an extensive interaction network (R191-D592, K197-D501, W194-Y491), sequestering the substrate within its unique binding pocket (Fig. 5f). The substrate-bound structure highlights the uniqueness of the glycine binding pocket of hGlyT2, which is even distinct from the closely related hGlyT1. When transitioning to the inward conformation, TM1a shifts and bends towards the membrane to open the intracellular tunnel, facilitating the release of glycine and ions into the cytoplasm (Fig. 5e, f). Methods

Cell culture for protein expression

The hGlyT2 was expressed in Freestyle 293 F suspension cells, cultured in FreeStyle 293 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 1% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (PS) solution supplemented with 1% PS at 37 °C with an atmosphere of 5% CO2 and 70% humidity. Sf9 insect cells were grown in SIM SF medium (Sino Biological) at 27 °C. Hi Expi 293 cells were cultured in 293 F Hi Expi medium (Shanghai OPM Bioscience) supplemented with 1% PS at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Expression constructs and FSEC

A plasmid including the complementary DNA (cDNA) of human GlyT2 (1–797; UniProt: Q9Y345) was purchased from HedgehogBio Science and Technology Ltd (catalog number: HH-gene-1124). Wild-type of hGlyT2 and its mutants were cloned into a modified BacMam expression vector45 with all primer sequences provided in the Supplementary Table 2. And a C-terminal GFP tag engineered to carry a dual Strep-tag II affinity tag was appended for purification and fluorescence-detection size-exclusion chromatography (FSEC)46. The FSEC was performed using a Shimadzu HPLC system (LC-20AT) equipped with a SuperoseTM 6 Increase 5/150 GL column (Cytiva). The column was equilibrated with TBS buffer containing 0.025% DDM and 0.0025% CHS. For protein extraction, cells were solubilized in TBS buffer supplemented with 1% (w/v) LMNG and 0.1% (w/v) CHS. The FSEC column was equilibrated with TBS buffer containing 0.025% (w/v) DDM and 0.0025% (w/v) CHS. The GFP fluorescence was monitored at excitation and emission wavelengths of 488 nm and 520 nm, respectively.

Protein expression and purification

FreeStyle 293 F cells at a density of 2.5–3.0 × 106 cells/mL were infected for 60 h with P2 virus produced with the Bac-to-Bac system using Sf9 insect cells, and sodium butyrate was added 8–12 h post-infection with a final concentration of 10 mM. Cells were collected by centrifugation at 3500 × g for 15 min and washed once with TBS buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl). For protein purification, cell pellets expressing hGlyT2 were thawed and resuspended in TBS buffer with 2 µg/mL aprotinin, 2 µg/mL pepstatin, 2 µg/mL leupeptin and 1 mM phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). The suspension was solubilized by 1% (w/v) L-MNG and 0.1% (w/v) CHS at 4 °C for 1.5 h. Insoluble material was removed by centrifugation at 3500 × g for 15 min, followed by ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 1 h. The supernatant was loaded onto 5 ml of Strep-Tactin 4FF resin (Smart-Lifesciences). After loading, the resin was subsequently washed by TBS buffer containing 0.004% L-MNG and 0.0004% CHS to remove those impurity proteins, and the target protein was finally eluted with wash buffer supplemented with 2.5 mM desthiobiotin (IBA Lifesciences). In order to remove aggregates and heat shock proteins, 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 10 mM MgCl2 and 2 mM adenosine 5′-triphosphate disodium salt hydrate (ATP) were added to the eluate. The protein was then concentrated using a 100 kDa cut-off centrifugal filter unit (Millipore) and further purified via size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) using the SuperoseTM 6 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) equilibrated with TBS buffer containing 0.004% L-MNG and 0.0004% CHS. The peak fractions related to the target protein were pooled and concentrated for cryo-EM sample preparation.

[3H]glycine uptake assay

Hi Expi 293 cells were transfected with plasmids containing C-terminal GFP tag at a density of 3 × 106 cells/mL and maintained in an incubator at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 and 95% air. After being transfected for 36 h, cells were washed twice with HBSSG buffer, consisting of 136 mM NaCl, 1.26 mM CaCl2, 0.40 mM MgSO4, 0.33 mM Na2HPO4, 0.44 mM KH2PO4, 5.4 mM KCl, 5.55 mM glucose, and 4.17 mM NaHCO3 in distilled water, adjusted to pH 7.4. Saturation experiments were conducted with seven concentrations of [3H]glycine ranging from 18.875 µM to 1200 µM. For inhibition experiments, ORG25543 or opiranserin was pre-incubated for 20 min before uptake buffer added, which contained 50 µM [3H]glycine and different concentrations of these two antagonists. Uptake was terminated after 15 s incubation at 25 °C by washing the plates twice with cold HBSSG buffer. Then cells were lysed with 1% Triton-HBSSG buffer at RT for at least 15 min, followed by scintillation counting (Optiphase Supermix; Hidex 300 SL counter; 20 s/tube). For sarcosine (2 mM) competitive inhibition assays, the uptake time was extended to 10 min to enhance the signal-to-noise ratio for the F476Y&S479G double mutant, while all other conditions remained identical to those described above.

All data were derived from three biological replicates and analyzed in GraphPad Prism 9 by fitting substrate concentration curves or inhibition concentration curves. In the saturation or inhibition experiment, specific uptake was calculated as the difference between total and nonspecific uptake.

Sarcosine-d3 uptake assay

Hi Expi 293 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 2.0 × 106 cells/mL and cultured for 12 h. Cells were infected with hGlyT2 and related point mutation baculoviruses, followed by addition of 10 mM sodium butyrate after 12 h to enhance expression. After 12 h of induction, cells were resuspended in fresh 293 F Hi Expi medium at 1.5 × 10⁶ cells/mL and transferred to 96-well plates. Following 12 h of expression, cells were washed twice with 100 μL HBSSG buffer and then equilibrated in 50 μL HBSSG buffer.

Uptake assays was initiated by adding 200 μM sarcosine-d3 and incubating at 37 °C for 1, 2, 5 and 10 min, respectively. Reactions were terminated by addition of ice-cold PBS buffer (137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na₂HPO₄, 1.8 mM KH₂PO₄), followed by three wash cycles with ice-cold PBS buffer. Cells were lysed by adding 100 μL lysis buffer (10% water + 90% acetonitrile, v/v) containing 1 μM sarcosine-¹⁵N as the internal standard, followed by incubation at 4 °C for 2 h. The lysate was then transferred to 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes, mixed with 500 μL 90% acetonitrile, and incubated at −20 °C for 30 min to precipitate denatured proteins. Samples were centrifuged at 18,757 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected and dried under vacuum. Dried extracts were reconstituted in 100 μL of 90% acetonitrile containing 1 μM sarcosine-15N and clarified by centrifugation at 18,757 × g for 15 min at 4 °C prior to LC–MS/MS analysis.

LC-MS/MS was performed on an ACQUITY UPLC I-Class (Waters, USA) coupled with a triple-quadrupole mass spectrum (Xevo TQ-S micro, Waters, USA). 10 µL clarified supernatant was injected per run. The chromatographic separation was performed using a XBridge BEH Amide HILIC column (4.6 × 100 mm, 3.5 µm, Waters) maintained at 40°C at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. Mobile phase A consisted of water containing 4 mM ammonium formate and 0.08% formic acid, and mobile phase B was acetonitrile. The gradient procedure was set as follows: 0 min, 100% B; 1.3 min, 1% B; 2.1–9.0 min, 100% B. The MS was operated in a positive electrospray ionization (ESI) mode using the multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) method. The m/z values were 92.9116 → 46.9248 (cone voltage: 30 V; collision energy: 4 V) and 90.8321 → 44.9035 (cone voltage: 30 V; collision energy: 8 V) for sarcosine-d3 and the internal standard sarcosine-15N, respectively. The raw data was acquired using MassLynx 4.2 software (Waters, USA).

All raw data were derived from three biological replicates and verified using MassLynx 4.2 software (Waters, USA) with the following settings: peak identification threshold was set to S/N ≥ 3, and peak purity was verified by comparing the MRM transition ratio with the standard sample. Data analysis was performed by normalizing the peak area of sarcosine-d3 to that of the internal standard sarcosine-15N, and statistical significance was determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with GraphPad Prism 9.0 software.

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data acquisition

The purified hGlyT2 protein was concentrated to about 12.6 mg/mL to prepare the apo samples and incubated at 4 °C for 2 h with 4 mM glycine, 1 mM opiranserin, or 1 mM ORG25543 separately to prepare substrate-bound samples and inhibitor-bound samples. Then, 2.5 µl of the sample was added to holey carbon grids (GIG-Au 300 mesh, R 1/1) glow-discharged for 2 min using 25% O2 and 75% Ar by the Solarus advanced plasma system (Gatan). The sampled grids were blotted for 4 s with a blot force of 2 after a 5 s incubation on the grids at 4 °C under 100% humidity and then flash-frozen in liquid ethane cooled by liquid nitrogen using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

A Talos Arctica electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operating at 200 kV and equipped with a K2 camera (Gatan) was first used to screen the quality of samples on cryo-grids. High-resolution data collection was subsequently conducted on a Titan Krios electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operating at 300 kV, equipped with a K3 Summit direct electron detector (Gatan) mounted after a quantum energy filter set to a slit width of 20 eV.

All movie stacks were collected using EPU software in super-resolution mode at a magnification of ×81,000 with a calibrated pixel size of 0.5335 Å and a defocus range of −1.5 µm to −1.8 µm. Each movie stack consisting of 32 frames was exposed for 2.56 s, yielding a total dose of about 50 e- Å−2 with a dose rate of 22.36 e− pixel−1 s−1.

Cryo-EM data processing

The data-processing workflows are shown in Supplementary Figs. 2–5 and Supplementary Table 1. A total of 5674 multi-frame movies of glycine-bound hGlyT2 were collected. The beam-induced motion was corrected and twofold binned to a pixel size of 1.067 Å with dose weighting applied using MotionCor247. Micrographs were manually curated to exclude those exhibiting significant ice contamination or containing only carbon support film without discernible particles. The remaining micrographs were then imported into cryoSPARC48, and contrast transfer function (CTF) parameters were estimated using patch-CTF49. Blob picker was used for initial particle selection, followed by 4 rounds of 2D classification, resulting in the selection of high-quality particles, which were used to generate a good initial model with obvious secondary structure features by ab initio reconstruction. Using this model as a template, particles were re-picked using Topaz50, and seed-facilitated 3D classification51 was performed to improve resolution. After seed-facilitated 3D classification and multi-reference 3D classification, the final map reached a resolution of 3.10 Å in occluded conformation using non-uniform refinement (NU refinement) followed by local refinement with the soft mask covering hGlyT2.

For the apo state, opiranserin-bound and ORG25543-bound hGlyT2 datasets, 6,604, 9,552, and 5,409 super-resolution movie stacks were collected, corrected, and twofold binned to a pixel size of 1.067 Å with dose weighting applied using MotionCor2. Micrographs showing contamination or carbon support film without discernible particles were manually excluded. The remaining micrographs were then imported into cryoSPARC, and CTF parameters were estimated using patch-CTF. After being picked by the blob picker, 11,380,545 particles of apo state were selected for 4 rounds of 2D classification to generate a good initial model with obvious secondary structure features by ab initio reconstruction. Seed-facilitated 3D classification and multi-reference 3D classification were performed to further improve the resolution to 3.13 Å in inward conformation. For the inhibitor-bound states, initial particle picking and classification followed a similar workflow to generate high-quality model with resolution of 3.8 Å and 3.4 Å for ORG25543-bound and opiranserin-bound states, respectively. Particles were then re-picked using Topaz, processed for seed-facilitated 3D classification and multi-reference 3D classification to improve the map quality, and finally reached a resolution of 3.20 Å and 2.73 Å in outward conformation, respectively.

Model building and refinement

The initial atomic model of hGlyT2 was computationally generated using AlphaFold352 and then fitted into the cryo-EM map using UCSF Chimera X53 and rebuilt manually using Coot54. Phenix.real_space_refine and phenix.validation_cryoem tools in PHENIX55 were used to further refine and validate models to obtain final structures. The model-related metrics, including MolProbity scores, clash scores, rotamer outliers, and statistics of the Ramachandran plots, were summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Figures were displayed with ChimeraX.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Global resolution estimations of cryo-EM density maps are based on the 0.143 Fourier shell correlation criterion. The local resolution was estimated using cryoSPARC. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes. The cell lines were routinely checked to be negative for mycoplasma contamination but have not been authenticated.
