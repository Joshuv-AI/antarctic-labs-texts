Sustainable synthesis and optimization of aluminium matrix composites reinforced with laboratory waste borosilicate glass for resource sustainability circularity
Authors: A. Bhowmik, Raman Kumar, T. Ramachandran, Hrushikesh Sarangi, Priyaranjan Samal, Jeewan Singh, Rajender Kumar, Nagaraj Ashok
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study presents the synthesis and optimization of AA8011 aluminium matrix composites reinforced with waste borosilicate glass particles using stir casting. Reinforcement levels of 0–15 wt.% were evaluated for tribological performance under varying operating conditions. A Taguchi L16 design integrated with Grey Relational Analysis (GRA) was employed to optimize wear rate, friction force, and coefficient of friction. The results show that the incorporation of borosilicate glass reduces wear by approximately 30–35% compared to the base alloy. Optimal performance was achieved at 5 wt.% reinforcement, 60 N load, 1.5 m/s sliding speed, and 2250 m sliding distance, corresponding to a maximum Grey Relational Grade of 0.8210. ANOVA indicates that applied load is the most influential factor (56.56%), followed by sliding distance (17.20%) and sliding speed (14.41%). The prediction error between experimental and model results was 3.47%, confirming the robustness of the optimization approach. The use of waste borosilicate glass contributes to material reuse and improved component life, supporting sustainable material development. In general, the resulting composites have considerable potential in terms of sustainable and energy-efficient use in the automotive and industrial engineering segments.

Introduction

The very fast development of composite materials has contributed to the transformative revolution in the modern-day engineering practice, particularly in the aerospace, automotive, and marine industries in which the materials are required to provide high strength, lower weight, and extended durability1,2. The technological improvements in fabrication technologies in the past decades have given further momentum to the use of composites by their economic feasibility and applications in various fields. These have included metal matrix composites (MMCs) which have turned into a category of highly influential materials by making available features which far surpass those of traditional metals in both mechanical strength and tribological performance. It is common knowledge that MMCs possess high wear resistance, low density, increased stiffness, and better thermal and electrical conductivities, which render them quite appropriate in the stressful engineering conditions3–5. The fabrication route used heavily relies on the performance of MMCs. Among the different techniques available, the stir casting has become most notable because of its simplicity, scalability, and cost effectiveness. It is particularly preferred in the manufacturing of aluminium-based composite because it allows the successful integration of reinforcement particles in the molten material and therefore, tailoring of material properties6–8. Although it has its benefits, one of the biggest problems in stir casting is the attainment of homogeneous reinforcement particle distribution, which is very important in the attainment of homogenous mechanical performance. The high price of MMC production is one of the reasons that obstruct the wider industrial application, as noted by J. Hashim9, and enhancement of the dispersion of the particles is one of the measures that can be used to counter this problem. The consistency of the reinforcement distribution is extremely dependent on such process parameters like the stirring speed, melting temperature, and the duration of the stirring, which have to be carefully optimized. The effects of these parameters on composite behavior have been explored by a number of studies. As an example, Benal and Shivanand10 investigated the thermal expansion characteristics of fibre-reinforced hybrid Al6061 composites incorporating E-glass and silicon carbide under both as-cast and heat-treated conditions. The study revealed that mismatches in the thermal expansion coefficients of the different phases generate residual stresses, which tend to build up over time and can ultimately compromise dimensional stability. Similarly, Mallikarjana et al.11 explored aluminium 4046-based hybrid composites reinforced with fly ash and S-glass. Their results indicated that increasing the reinforcement content significantly improved the impact strength, primarily due to enhanced energy absorption and dissipation mechanisms introduced by the reinforcements.

Aluminium matrix composites have gained a lot of attention because they have a mix of being lightweight and having good mechanical properties compared to regular alloys. Studies have shown that adding reinforcements like SiO₂, SiC and Al₂O₃ makes them much harder, stronger and more resistant to wear12,13. This is because the reinforcements facilitate load transfer and hinder dislocation motion within the grains. These composites are also stiffer and capable of withstanding elevated temperatures, which makes them good for structural and tribological applications. However, when they get stronger, they often become less flexible and less able to withstand impact14,15. This is mainly because the reinforcements are brittle and can create areas where stress builds up. There are also challenges like particles clumping not spreading out evenly and reacting with each other at the interface. If these issues aren’t controlled during processing they can hurt the performance of the composites. So, finding a balance, between strength and flexibility is crucial when designing and developing aluminium matrix composites16,17.

Various research has been done on various reinforcement methods to enhance the performance of aluminium-based composites. As an example, the authors of Hamedan and Shahmiri18 used a high-end stir casting method in the case of A356 aluminium combined with SiC nanoparticles. Their study revealed that proper management of stirring parameters especially temperature and rotational velocity is important in ensuring the homogeneous distribution of nanoparticles. They have stated that temperatures of 650 to 800 °C and stirring speed of 450–950 rpm have recorded significant improvement in mechanical performance and the importance of process parameter adjustment is therefore important. In a different study, YC and Sankar19 concentrated on the absorbing materials with Al6061 composites that had been reinforced with glass powder in different particle sizes and weight fractions. Their outcomes exhibited that the reinforcement level of 9 wt.% gave the best balance of strength and hardness signifying the optimum level of reinforcement used to improve the property. In line with this observation, Rao and Parmar20 also expressed a similar finding whereby hardness and tensile strength were on the rise with addition of glass powder in aluminium matrices. On the same finding, Wessley et al.21 produced AA6063-based composites that were reinforced with fly ash and borosilicate glass. Although reinforcement content tended to decrease ductility as more content was added, which is a typical tradeoff in composite systems, they had higher strength and stiffness, which are more useful in load-bearing materials. On the same note, Anupama Hiremath22 reported the fabrication of hybrid aluminium matrix composites reinforced with fly ash and borosilicate glass, demonstrating a simultaneous enhancement in both tensile strength and ductility. Subsequently, Rathnararaj and Sathish23 were able to point out that borosilicate glass is an effective reinforcement material, especially when it is evenly spread throughout the matrix. Their experiment portrayed significant increases in the hardness and tensile strength highlighting its possible application in aerospace-related projects. Bulei et al.24 reported that metal matrix composites, particularly fibre-reinforced aluminium systems, offer superior properties but face cost and manufacturing limitations. Consequently, attention shifted to discontinuously reinforced aluminium composites, emphasizing recyclability, material reuse, and broader industrial applicability with improved structural performance. Petrova et al.25 examined oxidation kinetics of aluminium alloys, highlighting protective oxide layers, temperature-dependent behavior, and water vapour effects. They reported that Fe, Mg, and Sb contents influence oxidation intensity, exponent values, and promote a two-stage oxidation mechanism in aluminium ribbons. Vencl et al.26 reported that A356 Al–Si composites reinforced via compocasting with SiC, Al₂O₃, and graphite improved tribological performance; SiC enhanced wear resistance, while hybrid SiC–graphite minimized friction and wear, with adhesion as the dominant wear mechanism under dry sliding conditions. Miladinović et al.15 applied ANN and Taguchi–grey optimization to hypereutectic Al–Si composites, predicting wear rate and friction with high accuracy. Optimal conditions involved Al₂O₃ micro/nano reinforcement at 100 N load, with wear dominated by mild abrasion and adhesion mechanisms. Usca et al.27 investigated hot-pressed Al-4Cu/B4C–SiC hybrid composites, reporting enhanced hardness, density, and bending strength. Improved wear resistance with reduced mass loss was observed, governed by plastic deformation and oxidation, highlighting the effectiveness of hybrid reinforcements in tribological and mechanical performance. Şap et al.28 developed Al–5Cu/Al₂O₃–SiC hybrid composites via powder metallurgy and hot pressing, reporting enhanced density, hardness, and flexural strength. Their tribological study showed improved wear resistance with a minimum SWR of 2.09 × 10−6 mm3/N·m, dominated by adhesion and plastic deformation. Şap et al.29 reported that Al–3Gr hybrid composites reinforced with SiC and BN, fabricated via powder metallurgy, exhibited enhanced hardness and tribological performance; hybrid reinforcements outperformed single systems, achieving minimum wear loss and reduced friction, with properties strongly influenced by applied load and reinforcement content. Usca et al.30 investigated Cu–Gr composites reinforced with SiC and WC via powder metallurgy. Increasing reinforcement improved hardness, while optimal ratios enhanced flexural strength and wear resistance. Microstructural analyses confirmed uniform particle distribution, strong interfacial bonding, and absence of detrimental interfacial reactions. Earlier studies31–34 demonstrate that borosilicate glass is a cost effective and an environmentally friendly reinforcement material of aluminium matrix composites.

The main contribution of the present study lies in the development and systematic evaluation of Al 6061-based functionally graded composites reinforced with alumina, with particular emphasis on their tribological performance and sustainability aspects. Unlike conventional studies that primarily focus on homogeneous aluminium matrix composites, this work introduces a graded reinforcement architecture to achieve improved wear resistance and mechanical stability across varying operating conditions. The novelty of this research is further strengthened by establishing a clear correlation between compositional gradation, microstructural features, and dominant wear mechanisms. In addition, the study incorporates a sustainability perspective by highlighting how enhanced wear resistance can contribute to prolonged component life, reduced material consumption, and improved resource efficiency. Therefore, this work offers both scientific insight and practical relevance, making it a meaningful contribution to the advancement of functionally graded composite materials and their engineering applications.

Despite several studies on glass-reinforced aluminium composites, limited research has focused on the tribological optimization of AA8011 composites reinforced with laboratory waste borosilicate glass using a systematic multi-response statistical framework. Moreover, previous investigations primarily emphasized mechanical characterization, whereas comprehensive tribological optimization integrated with sustainability considerations remains insufficiently explored. The present work therefore aims to develop sustainable AA8011-based composites utilizing discarded laboratory borosilicate glass and to optimize their wear behavior using Taguchi-based Grey Relational Analysis.

Material selection

AA8011 was selected as the matrix material due to its wide range of industrial applications. It is also an ideal material in structural parts since it offers a good strength-to-weight ratio and its appearance is also a factor in balance. In addition, the alloy facilitates the production of complex geometries with smooth quality surface finishes which are most beneficial in anodizing applications. Borosilicate glass is silica-based glass with boron trioxide; it is known to have low coefficient of thermal expansion and this gives it high thermal shock resistance in comparison to the standard glass. In India and other countries, glass waste recycling and management remain to be a problem, thus the necessity of finding alternative ways of using it. In that connection, glass can also be considered as the possible reinforcement material, and it can serve as the alternative to the old reinforcement types, carbide and oxide. Borosilicate glass, also used as a laboratory material, is considerably cheaper than traditional soda-lime glass—about one-third of the price—and much lower in coefficient of thermal expansion. The property reduces the effects of thermally generated stresses, so it is compatible with applications where temperature changes are present. In this experiment the borosilicate glass discarded in laboratories was washed, crushed and ground into fine sand and then sieved to sort the grain size between 38–53 μm. Due to the low density (2.3 g/cc) of the borosilicate glass relative to AA8011 aluminium alloy (2.7 g/cc), the addition of the borosilicate glass helps to decrease the over-all weight of the developed composite. Aluminium matrix was strengthened using different contents of borosilicate glass powder, namely 0, 5, 10 and 15 percent of weight. The selection of reinforcement weight fractions (0, 5, 10, and 15 wt.%) was based on prior literature reports and practical considerations in stir casting, where reinforcement beyond ~ 15 wt.% often leads to agglomeration and poor dispersion. These levels were further structured within the Taguchi design to enable systematic optimization of tribological performance.

In terms of economic considerations, stir casting is significantly more economical than powder metallurgy and other advanced processing techniques due to lower energy consumption, minimal material loss, and reduced processing steps. This makes it particularly attractive for large-scale and commercial applications.

Experimentation

With an induction heating system, aluminium alloy 8011 was first melted in a graphite crucible. In order to reduce defects caused by oxidation process, the borosilicate glass reinforcement particles were initially preheated at 450 °C in a muffle furnace. At the same time, the permanent mould was kept at 400 °C in order to minimize the chance of casting problems like shrinkage and cracking. The molten alloy was added with 2 wt.% magnesium to improve the wettability between the aluminium matrix and borosilicate glass reinforcement. Magnesium reduces the surface tension of molten aluminium and enhances interfacial adhesion by promoting the formation of a stable interface, thereby facilitating effective load transfer between matrix and reinforcement. This addition is particularly important for silica-based reinforcements, which otherwise exhibit poor wettability with molten aluminium, leading to particle rejection and clustering. Composite fabrication was done using stir casting, which is known to be simple to operate, economical and efficient in terms of producing homogeneous dispersion. In Fig. 1, the borosilicate glass particles were gradually introduced into the molten alloy at a stirring speed of 300 rpm for 10 min. Stir casting was specifically selected in this study due to its demonstrated capability in processing discontinuously reinforced aluminium matrix composites with acceptable particle incorporation efficiency. Compared to alternative methods such as powder metallurgy or squeeze casting, stir casting enables liquid-state mixing, which facilitates better particle wetting and distribution when appropriate process parameters are maintained. This makes it particularly suitable for incorporating low-density ceramic or glass particulates such as borosilicate glass in a cost-effective and scalable manner. The distribution of reinforcement particles within the aluminium matrix was observed to be reasonably uniform, which can be attributed to adequate control of stirring parameters such as speed, temperature, and duration. However, slight particle agglomeration was noted at higher reinforcement content, which is a common limitation of stir casting. Overall, the microstructural analysis confirms effective particle incorporation with acceptable dispersion characteristics.Fig. 1Schematic illustration depicting the sequential stages involved in the casting procedure and subsequent specimen preparation.

Dry sliding wear behaviour was evaluated using a pin-on-disc tribometer, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Cylindrical test samples measuring 40 mm in length and 6 mm in diameter were prepared and tested in accordance with ASTM G99 standards. Each specimen was slid against a hardened EN31 steel disc with a hardness of 62 HRC. Prior to experimentation, both the pin and disc surfaces were thoroughly cleaned with acetone to eliminate any surface contaminants. Based on insights from earlier studies, normal loads of 15, 30, 45, and 60 N were selected to examine their influence on wear characteristics, while maintaining a constant sliding velocity of 2 m/s and a total sliding distance of 2000 m. Each experimental condition was repeated three times, and the average values together with the corresponding standard deviations were considered for statistical analysis to ensure repeatability and reliability of the tribological measurements.Fig. 2Pin–disc tribological testing setup for assessing wear behavior.

Statistical analysis

Grey Relational Analysis (GRA) was adopted in this study because the tribological performance evaluation involved simultaneous optimization of multiple conflicting responses, namely wear rate, friction force, and coefficient of friction. Conventional single-response optimization techniques are inadequate for such problems; therefore, GRA was employed to transform the multi-response problem into a single Grey Relational Grade (GRG), enabling effective ranking and optimization of process parameters under the Taguchi L16 orthogonal array.

Figure 7 presents the residual diagnostics of the signal-to-noise (S/N) ratios of the signal of the proposed statistical model, the Grey Fuzzy Grade (GFG) which gives an overall evaluation of the power and adequacy of the proposed statistical model. The assessment is done using four standard diagnostic plots, including the normal probability plot, residuals versus fitted values, residual histogram, and residuals verses run order. The normal probability plot indicates that the residuals follow the reference line, and this indicates that the distribution is approximately normal. This fact confirms the assumption of normality needed to make the regression and ANOVA analysis valid as there are minor deviations at the ends which can be explained by the uncertainties associated with the experiment and the lack of a large sample to analyze. The plot of the residual values versus the fitted values has an irregular distribution of points around the zero line with no apparent pattern or systematic structure. This indicates the presence of homoscedastic residuals and validates the existence of sufficient model weaknesses. In the same way, the histogram of the residuals shows a very symmetric distribution with a mean value of zero which supports the assumption of normality. Even though, there is slight asymmetry, it is not significant enough to undermine the predictive performance or the accuracy of the model. Moreover, the plot of the residuals versus the order of observation does not demonstrate any noticeable trends, periodicity, and clustering. This validates that there is no correlation between the residuals and shows that there are no effects of time or order to the experimental data.Fig. 7Residual graph for GFG.

Residual graph for GFG.

The steep gradient observed for applied load confirms its dominant influence, where increased load intensifies asperity interaction and material removal. In contrast, moderate slopes for sliding speed and reinforcement indicate secondary yet synergistic effects, particularly in promoting tribofilm formation and load transfer.

Residual diagnostics were further examined to validate the adequacy of the developed Taguchi-GRA model. The approximately normal distribution of residuals, random scatter around zero, and absence of trend with run order confirm that the assumptions of normality, homoscedasticity, and independence were reasonably satisfied, thereby supporting the reliability of the statistical conclusions.

The absence of systematic structure in residual plots confirms that no higher-order interactions or unmodeled nonlinearities significantly affect the response, thereby validating the robustness of the Taguchi–GRA model for predictive optimization.

Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was performed on the Grey Relational Grade to statistically determine the relative significance of each process parameter and quantify their percentage contributions toward overall tribological performance. ANOVA was selected because it enables decomposition of total variability into contributions from individual factors, thereby identifying the dominant control parameters affecting wear behaviour. The statistical significance was assessed based on F-values, p-values, and percentage contribution ratios.

According to the ANOVA results, applied load (p = 0.009) was statistically significant at a confidence level of 95%, whereas reinforcement content (p = 0.092) and sliding speed (p = 0.059) were statistically insignificant at α = 0.05, despite contributing to overall tribological behavior.

The obtained results are consistent with previously reported studies on aluminium matrix composites reinforced with ceramic and glass-based particulates. For instance, Vencl et al.20 reported improved wear resistance in Al–Si composites reinforced with SiC and Al₂O₃ due to enhanced load-bearing capacity and formation of protective tribolayers, which aligns with the present findings of reduced wear rate and friction. Similarly, Miladinović et al.21 demonstrated effective prediction and optimization of tribological behavior using Taguchi–Grey methods, supporting the reliability of the current optimization approach with a low prediction error of 3.47%.

Furthermore, studies by Rao and Parmar14 and YC and Sankar13 indicated that glass particle reinforcement improves hardness and strength up to an optimal content, beyond which performance may decline due to particle agglomeration. This trend is in agreement with the present study, where optimal performance was observed at 5 wt.% reinforcement, while higher contents showed marginal reduction in efficiency due to clustering effects.

Compared to hybrid and powder metallurgy-based composites reported by Usca et al.22 and Şap et al.23, the current work demonstrates comparable tribological improvements using a cost-effective stir casting route and waste-derived reinforcement, thereby offering an additional advantage in terms of sustainability and resource efficiency.

The proposed wear mechanisms involving mechanically mixed layers (MML), oxidative tribofilms, and abrasive interactions are inferred from tribological response trends and previously reported literature, since direct SEM/EDS characterization of worn surfaces was not performed in the present study.

At lower to moderate applied loads, this tribolayer remains relatively stable and adherent, thereby minimizing direct metal-to-metal contact and reducing material removal. The presence of borosilicate glass particles contributes to this mechanism by enhancing load-bearing capacity and promoting the formation of a compacted protective layer. However, at higher loads (60 N), the increased contact stress can lead to rupture and delamination of the tribolayer, resulting in higher wear rates and transition toward severe wear regimes.

Similarly, the influence of sliding speed can be attributed to frictional heating and oxidation effects. At moderate sliding speeds, the temperature rise at the interface facilitates the formation of oxide-rich tribofilms, which improve surface stability and reduce adhesion. In contrast, prolonged sliding distance leads to progressive degradation of this layer due to repeated shear, exposing fresh surfaces and increasing wear.

These interpretations are consistent with reported studies on aluminium-based composites, where wear mechanisms transition from mild oxidative and abrasive wear to severe adhesive wear depending on operating conditions. Therefore, the improved tribological performance observed in this work can be attributed to the synergistic effects of reinforcement-induced load sharing and the formation of a transient but protective tribolayer.

Contour plot analyses

Figure 8 shows a sequence of contour plots drawn with the help of MINITAB software in order to show the interaction effects of process parameters constituting the interaction on the GRG. Parameters that are taken into account are wt.% of glass powder reinforcement, applied load, sliding speed and distance. These contour plots are a quantitative visualization of the overall effect of combined parameters variation on the tribological performance of the developed composite. Figure 8a is a contour plot of the dependent variables (wt.% of the glass powder (usually between a low level to a higher reinforcement level) and dependent variables (applied load (usually between 20–60 N) where it can be seen that the GRG values are between a low of approximately below 0.55, and a high of approximately above 0.80. The peak of the GRG (> 0.75–0.80) is reached in the case of moderate reinforcement (some 6.9 wt.%,) and lower applied loads (20–30 N). At a load more than 50 N, the GRG value becomes significantly small (less than 0.60), meaning that the performance worsens, as the frictional force and removal of the material become more pronounced. This implies that optimum reinforcing with reduced load is more beneficial in the case of wear resistance. Figure 8b depicts the correlation of wt.% glass powder and sliding velocity (between 1 and 4 m/s). GRG values vary between 0.58 and 0.82. The increased GRG values (above 0.78) are observed in moderate to high speeds of sliding (approximately 2.5–4 m/s) and optimum reinforcement content (approximately 6–10 wt.%). At reduced slide speeds (1 − 1.5 m/s), the GRG values reduce to less than 0.65 whether reinforced or not, which is evidence that there is no adequate formation of a protective tribolayer. It means that the faster the sliding velocity is increased within the controlled range, the better tribological performance is. Figure 8c contour plot between wt. percent of glass powder and sliding distance (usually 1000–3000 m) shows that the values of GRG are higher than 0.60 and lower than 0.80. The greater GRG material (> 0.75) is observed at moderate reinforcing levels and less length of sliding (~ 1000–1500 m). GRG declines (< 0.65) at a sliding distance exceeding 2500 m, which implies that cumulative performance effects dominate performance. This underscores the fact that material efficiency is minimized with prolonged sliding even with material reinforcement. Figure 8d shows the correlation between the load applied and the speed of sliding. GRG values are between 0.55 and 0.82. The most suitable region (> 0.78) is noted to occur at decreasing loads (2030 N) and increasing speeds of the sliding object (34 m/s). On the other hand, the lowest GRG (less than 0.60) is at high loads (more than 50 N) and low velocities (approximately 1 m/s). This is a clear indication that the effect of load is dominated by a negative effect whereas the speed is beneficial in stabilizing contact surface by formation of tribofilms. The contour plot in Fig. 8e of load versus sliding distance demonstrated that the GRG value was between 0.57 and 0.79. The greatest GRG (> 0.75) is obtained at low loads (2030 N) and fewer distances of sliding (approximately 1000–1500 m). GRG decreases dramatically (< 0.62), as both load and distance increase at the same rate, indicating harsh conditions of wear because of long-term exposure to high loads. This mixture increases the rate of degradation of materials. Lastly, Fig. 8f shows how the distance of the slide and speed of the slide interact. GRG values range between 0.60 and 0.81. GRG regions increase (> 0.77) with increased sliding speed (34 m/s) and reduced sliding distances (1000–1500 m). GRG is below 0.65 at lower speeds and longer distances, which means poor tribological performance. This is an indication that increased speeds assist in preserving the integrity of surfaces, and long-periods of sliding will still decrease performance. Although the Taguchi optimization identified 60 N as the optimum parameter level within the discrete experimental combinations of the L16 orthogonal array, the contour plots indicate a broader trend of decreasing GRG beyond approximately 50 N. This difference arises because Taguchi ranking identifies the best discrete experimental condition, whereas contour plots represent interpolated global response trends across the parameter space. All in all, it is evident in the contour plots in Fig. 8 that the best tribological behaviours (greater GRG) are obtained at moderate amounts of reinforcements, lower loads applied, faster sliding speeds, and reduced sliding distances. Of all the parameters, applied load and sliding distance demonstrate a negative impact to a large extent when raised, but on the other hand, the sliding speed has a positive effect to a given limit. Sustainability assessment

The sustainability feature of developed AA8011-based composites reinforced with waste borosilicate glass may be well comprehended via the material usage of the composites as well as the material performance. However, the sustainability assessment presented in this work remains qualitative in nature, and a complete life-cycle assessment (LCA) is required for rigorous quantification of environmental benefits and carbon-footprint reduction. Sustainability Framework of AA8011 Composites Reinforced with Waste Borosilicate Glass as illustrated in Fig. 9. The addition of laboratory waste borosilicate glass as a reinforcer is a feasible way of waste valorization, which can decrease the amount of landfills and encourage the use of the recycled materials. The replacement of conventional ceramic reinforcements with waste-derived borosilicate glass is also expected to reduce raw-material processing energy consumption compared with energy-intensive virgin ceramic production routes. However, rather than using the traditional ceramic reinforcement like SiC or Al2O3, the discarded glass has very low cost of raw material and energy is saved in the extraction and processing of the virgin materials. Based on the results of the experiment, it is clearly seen that the addition of the glass reinforcement enhances the tribological performance through the reduction of the rate of wear, friction force, and coefficient of friction. This improvement also leads to sustainability directly due to the extension of the service life of the components, which will reduce the rate of replacement and the material used. The optimal parameter combination that was determined using the grey relational analysis will also provide efficiency in the work conditions of moderate loads and higher sliding speed that will reduce the energy losses associated with friction. Additionally, the stir casting method used in this work is energy efficient in nature and can be applied in large scale production hence is a sustainable manufacturing pathway. The comparatively low processing temperature and the minimized equipment needs lower overall carbon footprint than powder metallurgy or modern methods of fabrication. The contour plot analysis also reveals that the best performance is maintained at moderate reinforcement (about 6–9 wt.%) to reduce unwarranted usage of material and at the same time achieve high wear resistance. This performance/material efficiency is essential to sustainable design. On the whole, the current work proves that the incorporation of waste-based borosilicate glass does not only improve the performance of the material but also corresponds with the most important sustainability criteria, such as resource savings, decreased amount of waste, cost-effectiveness, and energy-efficient functioning. These results demonstrate the possibility of using such composites in engineering systems that are environmentally friendly especially in car and industrial parts where durability and efficiency are of great importance.Fig. 9Sustainability framework for AA8011 composites reinforced with waste borosilicate glass.

Sustainability framework for AA8011 composites reinforced with waste borosilicate glass.

To ensure a systematic sustainability evaluation, the present study adopts a performance-linked assessment framework incorporating: (i) material substitution impact (waste glass replacing virgin ceramic reinforcements), (ii) tribological performance improvement as a proxy for service life enhancement, and (iii) qualitative energy comparison of processing routes. The reduction in wear rate (≈30–35%) is used as an indirect indicator of increased component lifespan, which contributes to lower material consumption over time. Furthermore, the use of waste-derived borosilicate glass eliminates the need for energy-intensive synthesis of conventional reinforcements such as SiC and Al₂O₃.
