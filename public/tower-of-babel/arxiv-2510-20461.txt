Long time behaviour of one facilitated kinetically constrained models: results and open problems
Fabio Martinelli, Assaf Shapira, Cristina Toninelli

Abstract
. Kinetically constrained models (KCMs) are interacting particle systems introduced in the ‘80s by physicists to have accessible stochastic models with glassy-type dynamics. The key mechanism behind the complex evolution of these otherwise simple models is the so-called dynamical facilitation, a feature embedded into the models via appropriate kinetic constraints. KCMs are reversible with respect to a Bernoulli product measure, and the analysis of their stationary evolution has witnessed significant progress in the last decade. Unfortunately, in the interesting regime when the equilibrium density of the facilitating vertices is small, many fundamental questions concerning the non-stationary evolution of even the simplest models remain unsolved. In this paper, we discuss some of these questions, along with partial new results and conjectures, for the one facilitated model and its variants, as well as for the biased annihilating branching process.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
State of the art and some conjectures

Fredrickson–Andersen 1-spin facilitated model (FA-1f) is an interacting
particle system that belongs to the class of kinetically constrained models (KCMs) (see
HT
for a recent review). KCMs are Glauber-type Markov processes on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
(or on suitable other graphs) informally defined as follows. Call a vertex
x
infected if its
state is “0” and healthy if “1”. Then each vertex becomes infected at rate
q
and healthy at rate
1-q
provided a certain local
constraint
is satisfied. The parameter
q\in(0,1)
is called the
infection density
.

The key feature shared by all KCMs is that the local constraint at each vertex
x
depends only on the current state of a suitable neighborhood of
x
and not on the state of
x
itself. A key example is the FA-1f model, whose constraint simply checks if there is an infection among the nearest neighbors of
x
.
It can be easily verified that KCMs are reversible with respect to the Bernoulli product measure
\pi
with density
q
for the infected vertices. Despite this trivial equilibrium measure, establishing the long-time behavior of KCMs poses very challenging and interesting problems.
Indeed, though major progress has been made in the last 20 years towards a full and rigorous understanding of the large-time behavior of the stationary process (see
(
HT
, chapters 4-6)
), robust tools to analyze FA-1f and general KCMs out of equilibrium are still lacking and several beautiful questions remain open (see
(
HT
, chapter 7)
). For clarity of exposition, in the sequel, unless otherwise explicitly stated, we will use the one-dimensional lattice
\mathbb{Z}
as the underlying graph.

The first natural question concerns the set of stationary measures for the process. It can be proved by entropy production methods (see Corollary
2.9
) that any stationary measure is a convex combination of the completely healthy configuration and
\pi
. In higher dimensions, the same holds for translation-invariant stationary measures.

A second basic question is to determine under which conditions on
q
and on the initial distribution
\nu
, the law of the process at time
t
converges to the equilibrium measure
\pi
as
t\to\infty
. Natural choices for
\nu
are the Bernoulli product measure
\mu_{q_{0}}
with
q_{0}>0,q_{0}\neq q
or the Dirac mass on the configuration with a single infection at the origin. For FA-
1
, it is natural to conjecture the following.

Conjecture 1
.

For any
q\in(0,1)
and any function
f
depending on the state of finitely many vertices

(1.1)

\lim_{t\to\infty}|{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu}(f(\eta(t)))-\pi(f)|=0,

provided that
\nu(\exists\text{ an infected vertex})=1.

The next obvious question concerns the speed of relaxation to the reversible measure
\pi
under some condition on
\nu
stronger than just the a.s. existence of some infection.

Conjecture 2
.

If there exists
\kappa>0
such that
\nu(\text{no infected vertices in $[-\ell,\ell]$})=O(e^{-\kappa\ell})
for all
\ell>0
large enough then for all
q>0
the convergence in (
1.1
) is exponentially fast.

Unfortunately, robust tools to prove Conjectures
1
and
2
are not yet available, and the results are limited to
q
larger than a certain threshold
BCRT
;
Ertul
;
Mareche
. For general KCM on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
, there are results for high values of
q

HTout
based on comparisons with suitable contact processes.
The only model for which the convergence to equilibrium is well understood for all
q>0
and all
d\geq 1
is the East model (see
(
HT
, Section 7.2)
and references therein).

The first and foremost reason for the failure in proving the above conjectures for any
q>0
is the fact that the process is not attractive (that is, the product partial order is not preserved by the semi-group of the process, see
(
Liggett05
, Sections II.2 and III.2)
for background). This is due to the fact that the presence of more infected sites may make certain constraints satisfied and therefore allow certain infected sites to become healthy. Consequently, many of the powerful techniques (e.g. censoring or coupling arguments) which have been developed for the study of other Glauber dynamics (e.g. the contact process and stochastic Ising model, see
Liggett05
;
Liggett99
;
Martinelli99
), fail here.

To make things worse, the usual Holley-Stroock strategy
Holley89
to attack questions of this kind, for example, stochastic Ising models, does not apply here. Indeed, this approach utilizes the finiteness of the logarithmic Sobolev constant, which implies, in turn, the hypercontractivity of the semigroup. However, due to the presence of constraints, the logarithmic Sobolev constant is either infinite for the process on the whole lattice, or very large (i.e.
\Theta(n)
) for the chain on the finite set
\Lambda_{n}=\{0,1,\dots,n\}
with either appropriate infected boundary conditions ensuring irreducibility or conditioned on having at least one infection. In the latter setting, the mixing time of the chain scales linearly with
n
and one expects the
cutoff phenomenon
(see e.g.
(
Peresbook
, Chapter 18)
) to occur for all
q>0
. The cutoff has been proven in
Ertul
for
q
not too small.

Finally, a related question in higher dimensions is the scaling with
n
of the mixing time of the (ergodic) chain on
\Lambda^{d}_{n}
. The conjecture is a linear scaling for all values of
q>0
, but, once more, that has been proved only for
q
large enough
HTout
.

The goal of this review is to present some progress and past results around the above two conjectures for FA-1f and three other closely related models on
\mathbb{Z}
: the

East-polluted FA-1f
, the
Weakly unoriented East process (or
\delta
-West model)
, and the

Biased annihilating branching process (BABP)
. The paper is organized as follows. In Section
1.2
, we fix some notation and define the four models under consideration precisely. In Section
2
we present a general result on the stationary measures, while Sections 3,4, and 5 are devoted to results concerning the East polluted FA-1f model, the
\delta
-West model, and BABP. In Section 6, we present some preliminary results for FA-1f starting from a finite number of vacancies.

Sometimes we need to work on subsets

and

for the

of

\Lambda

Given two disjoint sets

we write
\eta^{(1)}\cdot\eta^{(2)}\in\Omega_{\Lambda_{1}\cup\Lambda_{2}}
for the configuration such that

(1.2)

(\eta^{(1)}\cdot\eta^{(2)})_{x}=\begin{cases}\eta^{(1)}_{x}&\text{if }x\in\Lambda_{1},\\
\eta^{(2)}_{x}&\text{if }x\in\Lambda_{2}.\end{cases}

We denote the fully occupied (resp. empty) configurations in

\eta^{x}_{y}=\begin{cases}\eta_{y}&y\neq x,\\

1.3.
The Markov process

The Markov process that will be the object of this paper can be constructed via their self-adjoint Markov
semigroup

P_{t}:=e^{t\mathcal{L}}
on
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\pi)
, where the
generator

\mathcal{L}
is a non-negative self-adjoint operator with domain
\mathrm{Dom}(\mathcal{L})
that can be constructed in a standard way (see e.g.
(
Liggett05
, Sections I.3, IV.4)
) starting from its action on
local functions
(i.e. functions depending on the occupancy variables on a finite number sites):

(1.4)

\mathcal{L}f=\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}}c_{x}\cdot\left(\pi_{x}(f)-f\right).

where each update rate
c_{x}(\cdot)
is a local function specific to the model, which depends
only
on the state of the neighbors of
x
and
not
on the state of
x
itself, and which can be zero.
Spelling out the definition of
\pi_{x}
, the generator can be equivalently rewritten as

(1.5)

\mathcal{L}f(\eta)=\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}c_{x}(\eta)\left((1-\eta_{x})(1-q)+\eta_{x}q\right)(f(\eta^{x})-f(\eta)).

We further introduce the Dirichlet form

\mathcal{D}:{\mbox{Dom}}(\mathcal{L})\to\mathbb{R}
defined as

(1.6)

\mathcal{D}(f)=-\pi(f\cdot\mathcal{L}f)=\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\pi\left(c_{x}\operatorname{Var}_{x}(f)\right).

Using the formulation (
1.5
) it is not hard to verify that

\mathcal{D}(f)=\frac{1}{2}\int\pi(\mathrm{d}\eta)\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}}c_{x}(\eta)\left((1-\eta_{x})(1-q)+\eta_{x}q\right)\left(f(\eta^{x})-f(\eta)\right)^{2}.

When the initial distribution at time
t=0
is the probability measure
\nu
on
\Omega
, the law and expectation of the KCM process on the Skorokhod space
D([0,\infty),\Omega)
of càdlàg functions are denoted by
{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu}
and
{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu}
respectively (see
(
Billingsley99
, Chapter III)
for background). If
\nu
is concentrated over a single configuration
\eta
we
write simply
{\mathbb{P}}_{\eta}
and
{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}
for
{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu}
and
{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu}
, while if
\nu=\pi
, we simply write
{\mathbb{P}}
and
{\mathbb{E}}
. We use
\eta(t)
to denote the state of the KCM at time
t\geq 0
.

1.4.
One dimensional examples

Here we briefly present the models that will be analyzed in this paper.

1.4.1.
The one facilitated process (FA-1f )

For the FA-1f process the constraint satisfies

(1.7)

c_{x}(\eta)=\mathbbm{1}_{\{\eta_{x-1}+\eta_{x+1}\neq 2\}}.

Informally, FA-1f can be described
via its so-called
graphical representation
as follows:
each vertex
{x}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
is equipped with a unit intensity Poisson process, whose atoms
t_{x,k}
for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}

. We are further given independent Bernoulli random variables

with parameter

. At the clock ring

x

x
to
s_{x,k}
. Such updates are called
legal

FA-
1

\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}
(we write
\Lambda\Subset{\mathbb{Z}}
when we assume that
\Lambda
is finite). In this case, the most natural choice is to imagine that the configuration is defined also outside
\Lambda
, where it is frozen and equal to some reference configuration
\sigma\in\Omega_{{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\Lambda}
, the
boundary condition
. Then, for
x\in\Lambda
,
\eta\in\Omega_{\Lambda}
, the constraint is defined as

(1.8)

c^{\sigma}_{x}(\eta)=c_{x}(\eta\cdot\sigma)

(recall (

) and (

The generator and Dirichlet form of the process on
\Omega_{\Lambda}
with boundary condition

and

) and (

) to sites in
\Lambda
and substituting

with

. We similarly denote by
{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu}^{\sigma}
and
{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu}^{\sigma}

the law and expectation of the process with initial distribution

and by

is reversible for this process.

1.4.2.
The biased annihilating branching process (BABP)

The BABP constraints are now defined as

(1.9)

c_{x}=2-\eta_{x-1}-\eta_{x+1}.

Namely, if
x
has two empty nearest neighbours, it is updated at rate
2
; if it has one empty nearest neighbour, it is updated at rate

The constraints in this case are set to

1-\eta_{x-1}&\text{ if $x\sim E$}.\end{cases}

In other words, vertices of type FA-1f are updated as in the FA-1f process (cf. (

1.4.4.
The weakly unoriented East process (or
\delta
-West process)

Fix
\delta\in[0,1]

(1.11)

c_{x}=(1-\eta_{x-1})+\delta(1-\eta_{x+1})

Notice that for

2.
A general result on the stationary measures

In this part, we consider a general KCM on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, with constraint
c_{x}(\eta)
which depends on the occupation of sites at a distance at most
\operatorname{r}
from
x
. In the sequel for an integer
N
, we will write
\Lambda_{N}
for the box
\{-N,\dots,N\}^{d}
.
It is not hard to verify that, since the constraint
c_{x}(\eta)
does not depend on
\eta_{x}
, the dynamics of all the models we defined satisfy detailed balance w.r.t. the product measure
\pi
.
Therefore,
\pi
is reversible (i.e.
\pi(f\cdot P_{t}g)=\pi(g\cdot P_{t}f)
for all
f,g\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\pi)
and
t\geq 0
) and therefore it is an invariant measure for the process (i.e.
\pi P_{t}=\pi
for all
t\geq 0
). However,
\pi
is
not
the unique invariant measure; indeed, the Dirac measure on the fully healthy configuration is also invariant.

Reference
SudburyNeuhauser93
characterizes the stationary measures of BABP. Here we present a general argument which we apply, in addition to the models illustrated above, also to the East model and to the FA2f model in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d},d\geq 2
, for which the constraints require at least two infections among the nearest neighbors. We note that the same argument used for the East model can be applied to the North-East model, reproducing a result from
KordzakhiaLalley
.

In order to understand the stationary measures of a KCM, we need two main ingredients: (i)the identification of the ergodic components; (ii) a detailed balance equation describing how probabilities change within an ergodic component.

To understand the ergodic component of a KCM, we need to introduce an intimately related deterministic process,

bootstrap percolation
.

Definition 2.1
.

The bootstrap percolation is a deterministic dynamics in discrete
time, where at each step, sites for which the KCM constraint is satisfied
becomes empty. This is a monotone process; hence, starting from any
configuration
\eta
, it converges to a limiting configuration that
we denote
\operatorname{BP}(\eta)
.
We say that a configuration
\eta
is
stable
for the bootstrap
percolation if
\operatorname{BP}(\eta)=\eta
, and denote the set of stable configurations
by
\mathcal{E}
. Note that the constant configurations
\underline{{\mathbf{0}}}

and
\underline{{\mathbf{1}}}
always belong to
\mathcal{E}
.
Finally, we may consider the bootstrap percolation restricted to a
set
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, with filled boundary conditions. It that
case, we denote the final configuration by
\operatorname{BP}^{\Lambda}(\eta)
.

The stable configurations for the bootstrap percolation characterize
the ergodic components of the KCM, as described in the following result
(
HT
, Corollary 3.7)
:

Theorem 2.2
.

Two configurations
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}
belong to the same ergodic
component (i.e., for any finite
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
there is a
sequence of legal flips bringing
\eta
to a configuration which
agrees with
\eta^{\prime}
on
\Lambda
) if and only if
\operatorname{BP}(\eta)=\operatorname{BP}(\eta^{\prime})
.

Inside an ergodic component, the stationary measures will be described
by detailed balance.

Definition 2.3
.

We say that
\mu
satisfies detailed balance if for any finite set
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
and any
x
such that
x+[-\operatorname{r},\operatorname{r}]^{d}\in\Lambda

(2.1)

c_{x}(\eta_{\Lambda})\pi(\eta_{\Lambda}^{x})\mu(\eta_{\Lambda})=c_{x}(\eta^{x}_{\Lambda})\pi(\eta_{\Lambda})\mu(\eta_{\Lambda}^{x})=c_{x}(\eta_{\Lambda})\pi(\eta_{\Lambda})\mu(\eta_{\Lambda}^{x}).

Theorem 2.4
.

(
Holley
) Assume that
\mu
is stationary and translation invariant.
Then, detailed balance (
2.1
) holds.

Actually, translation invariance is not needed in one dimension. We will indeed prove the following result:

Theorem 2.5
.

Consider a one dimensional model,
and assume that
\mu
is stationary. Then detailed balance (
2.1
)
holds.

We combine these two ingredients in two different mechanisms: when
the bootstrap percolation spreads out from within a box, and when
it invades a box from the outside.

Definition 2.6
.

Fix a configuration
\eta
, a stable configuration
\beta\in\mathcal{E}
,
and a finite box
\Lambda
. We say that
\eta

\beta
-spans

\Lambda
internally
if
\operatorname{BP}^{\Lambda}(\eta)_{\Lambda}=\beta_{\Lambda}
,
i.e., if
\operatorname{BP}^{\Lambda}(\eta)
agrees with
\beta
on
\Lambda
.

Definition 2.7
.

Fix a configuration
\eta
, a stable configuration
\beta\in\mathcal{E}
,
and two finite boxes
\Lambda\subset\Lambda^{\prime}
. We say that
\eta

\beta
-spans
\Lambda
externally
from
\Lambda^{\prime}
if
\operatorname{BP}^{\Lambda^{\prime}}(\eta_{\Lambda^{\prime}\setminus\Lambda}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda})_{\Lambda}=\beta_{\Lambda}
.
We denote the
set of configurations that
\beta
-span
\Lambda
externally from

\Lambda^{\prime}
by
E_{\beta}(\Lambda^{\prime},\Lambda)
.

Under the assumption of detailed balance (
2.1
), we can exploit the above mechanisms to try identifying the stationary measure.
For the internal spanning one, we have:

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\mu,\mu^{\prime}
be two measures
satisfying detailed balance (
2.1
), such
that
\mu^{\prime}
is a product measure. Assume that for some
\beta\in\mathcal{E}
,
both measures are supported on the ergodic component associated with

and
that

(2.2)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mu\left(\operatorname{BP}^{\Lambda_{N}}(\eta)=\beta_{\Lambda_{N}}\right)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mu^{\prime}\left(\operatorname{BP}^{\Lambda_{N}}(\eta)=\beta_{\Lambda_{N}}\right)=1.

Then
\mu=\mu^{\prime}
.

As an immediate corollary, in the FA-1f model, if we write a stationary

as the sum
\mu=\mu(\operatorname{BP}(\eta)=\mathbf{0})\mu\left(\cdot|\operatorname{BP}(\eta)=\mathbf{0}\right)+\mu(\operatorname{BP}(\eta)=\mathbf{1})\mu\left(\cdot|\operatorname{BP}(\eta)=\mathbf{1}\right)

Let

for some

.

Another consequence of Lemma

Theorem 2.10

Consider a KCM such that
\pi(\operatorname{BP}^{\Lambda_{N}}(\eta)=\mathbf{0}_{\Lambda_{N}})\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}1

),

.

The next lemma helps describe the stationary reversible measures for external

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
\mu,\mu^{\prime}
be two measures

), such that
that

.

Let

(2.4)

\mu=\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}}\lambda_{i}\mu^{(i)},

for some positive sequence

,

The tools developed in this section are limited to either one-dimensional models or translation-invariant measures. However, special properties of the East model allow us to obtain a general result in any dimension.

For
x,y\in({\mathbb{Z}}\cup\{\pm\infty\})^{d}
, we say that
y
precedes
x
if each coordinate of
y
is not larger than the corresponding coordinate of
x
.
We also write
\mathcal{P}^{-}_{x}
for the set of vertices which strictly precede
x
, and
\mathcal{P}^{+}_{x}
for the set of vertices strictly preceded by
x
. With this notation, the constraint
c_{x}
of the
d
-dimensional East model requires at least one vacancy among the neighbors of
x
in
\mathcal{P}^{-}_{x}
. In particular, under the corresponding bootstrap percolation, a single vacancy at
x
is able to empty
\mathcal{P}^{+}_{x}
. Therefore, the
0
’s of any

to conclude that
\lim_{t\to+\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}\big(f(\eta)\big)=\pi(f)
. Suppose now that
\beta
is different from
\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1}
and recall the definition of
I_{\beta}\subset({\mathbb{Z}}\cup\{\pm\infty\})^{d}
. For simplicity we assume that
I_{\beta}\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, the general case being covered by the sequel combined with the argument used for
\beta=\mathbf{0}
. By construction
I
is infected in
\beta
, whereas
\cup_{x\in I}\mathcal{P}^{-}_{x}
is healthy. Necessarily, the same is true for
\eta
. Fix a function
f
depending on finitely many variables in
\cup_{x\in I_{\beta}}\mathcal{P}^{+}_{x},
and choose a finite subset
I
of
I_{\beta}
and
M>0
such that
\Lambda=\cup_{x\in I}\mathcal{P}^{+}_{x}\cap\{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}:\|x\|_{\infty}\leq M\}
contains the support of
f
. Because of the orientation of the East constraints, one immediately verifies that the marginal in
\Omega_{\Lambda}
of the law of the East process with initial condition
\eta
coincides with the law of the East chain in
\Omega_{\Lambda}
with empty boundary condition at the vertices of
I
and healthy elsewhere, and initial condition
\eta_{\Lambda}
. Such a chain is ergodic, with reversible measure
\pi_{\Lambda},
and therefore
\lim_{t\to+\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}\big(f(\eta_{t})\big)=\pi(f)
.
∎

3.
Results for the East polluted FA1
\rm f
model

In this section, we consider the East polluted FA-1f model (see Section
1.4.3
). Recall that we assume that there are infinitely many vertices of type East to the left and to the right of the origin. Note first that the set
\mathcal{E}
of stable configurations
under the bootstrap transformation consists of the configurations
\mathbf{1},\mathbf{0},
and of those configurations which, for a given
x\sim E
, are infected exactly at any
y\geq x
and at
\{x-k,x-k+1,\dots,x-1\}
if these sites form a maximal sequence of sites of type F to the left of
x
. The same proof of Theorem
2.12
gives that any stationary measure is the convex combination of
\pi
, the measure
\delta_{\mathbf{1}}
, and the following collection of probability measures
\hat{\mu}^{(i)},i\sim E
. Let
j(i)=\max\{k\geq 0:i-1,\dots,i-k\text{ are all of type F}\}
. Then
\hat{\mu}^{(i)}
is the product measure of the Dirac mass on the singleton all healthy sites to the left of
i-k
and
\pi
to the right of
i-k-1
conditioned to have at least one infection in
[i-k,\dots,i]
.

Let
\nu
be a probability measure on
\Omega
and let
\nu_{t}
be the law of the process at time
t>0
when the initial distribution is
\nu
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume that
\nu
-a.s. there exist infinitely many infected sites to the left of the origin. Then
\lim_{t\to\infty}\nu_{t}=\pi
.

Proof.

We begin with a result about the persistence of vacancies. W.l.o.g., we assume that the origin is of type East, and we write

x^{(n)}
for the
n^{th}
-East site to the left of the origin.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let

p_{n}=\sup_{x^{(1)}<x\leq 0}\sup_{\eta:\,\eta_{x}=0}{\mathbb{P}}_{\eta}(\eta_{y}(t)=1\ \forall\,y\in[x^{(n)},0]).

Then

p_{n}\leq p^{n}
.

Proof.

Let us fix

t>0,x\in[x^{(1)}+1,0]
and an initial configuration
\eta
such that
\eta(x)=0
. Recall the graphical construction, denote the rings of the Poisson clock at a generic site
y
by
\{t_{y,i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
, and for each such ring denote by
\xi_{y,i}\in\{0,1\}
the result of the corresponding coin toss. By construction the variables
\{\xi_{y,i}\}_{y\in{\mathbb{Z}},i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
are i.i.d. Bernoulli(p).

Define now
\alpha^{(k)}
as the
last
legal ring before time
t
at
x^{(k)}+1
with an infection at
x^{(k)}
. More formally,

\alpha^{(k)}=\max\left\{i:\ t_{x^{(k)}+1,i}<t\text{ and }\eta_{x^{(k)}}(t_{x^{(k)}+1,i})=0\right\},

with the convention that
\max\emptyset=0
.
We now observe that having at least one infection at
x\in(x^{(1)},0]
at time
t=0
and no infection at time
t
in the interval
[x^{(n)},0]
implies that each of the sites
x^{(1)}+1,\dots,x^{(n)}+1
must have had at least one legal ring before
t
occurring with an infection at their left neighbor and with the corresponding coin toss equal to one. Indeed, if for some
k
there is no such ring, the infection cannot leave the interval
[x^{(k)},0]
. In other words,
\alpha^{(k)}\geq 1
and
\xi_{x^{(k)}+1,\alpha^{(k)}}=1
for
k=1,\dots,n
. Therefore,

p_{n}\leq\sup_{x^{(1)}<x\leq 0}\sup_{\eta:\,\eta_{x}=0}\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{\infty}\dots\sum_{i_{n}=1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}(\prod_{k=1}^{n}{\mathbf{1}}_{\{\alpha^{(k)}=i_{k}\}}\,\xi_{x^{(k)}+1,i_{k}}).

Since
x^{(1)}
is an East site, the coin tosses in
[x^{(1)}+1,\infty)
do not affect the process on
(-\infty,x^{(1)}]
. In particular
\xi_{x^{(1)}+1,i_{1}}
is independent of
\{\alpha^{(k)}=i_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
. Therefore,

\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{\infty}\dots\sum_{i_{n}=1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}(\prod_{k=1}^{n}{\mathbf{1}}_{\{\tau^{(k)}=i_{k}\}}\,\xi_{x^{(k)}+1,i_{k}})\leq p\times\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{\infty}\dots\sum_{i_{n}=1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}(\prod_{k=2}^{n}{\mathbf{1}}_{\{\tau^{(k)}=i_{k}\}}\,\xi_{x^{(k)}+1,i_{k}}),

where we used
\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{\infty}{\mathbf{1}}_{\alpha^{(1)}=i_{1}}\leq 1
. Iterating the argument, we get

\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{\infty}\dots\sum_{i_{n}=1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}(\prod_{k=1}^{n}{\mathbf{1}}_{\{\tau^{(k)}=i_{k}\}}\,\xi_{x^{(k)}+1,i_{k}})\leq p^{n},

finishing the proof.
∎

Choose now a configuration
\eta
with infinitely many infected sites to the left of the origin and let
z
be one of them. W.l.o.g., we can assume that the first vertex of type East greater than or equal to
z
is the origin. Thanks to the lemma, any limit point
\mu
of the law of
\eta_{t}
as
t\to\infty
satisfies

\mu(\eta_{x}=1\ \forall x\in[x^{(n)},0])\leq p^{n}.

By
(
Mountford95
, Theorem 1)

\mu
is stationary, hence using the arbitrariness of
z
and
n
together with the description of the stationary measures, we conclude that
\mu
coincides with
\pi
.
∎

4.
Results for the
\delta
-West process

The generator of the
\delta
-West process can be written as
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}^{E}+\delta\mathcal{L}^{W},\delta\in(0,1),
where

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{E}f(\eta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}}(1-\eta_{x-1})\left(\pi_{x}(f)-f\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{W}f(\eta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}}(1-\eta_{x+1})\left(\pi_{x}(f)-f\right).

As for the FA-1f process, any stationary measure is the convex combination of
\delta_{\mathbf{1}}
and
\pi
. Our main result reads as follows.

Theorem 4.1
.

For any
q\in(0,1)
there exists
0<\delta_{0}<1
such that the following holds. Let
\nu
be the initial law of the process, which is either a non-trivial product Bernoulli measure or, for some constant
M>0
, it is supported on configurations
\eta
with at least one infection every
M
vertices. Then, for any local function

\lim_{t\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu}(f(\eta(t))=\pi(f)

and the convergence is exponentially fast.

Proof.

The proof follows the general scheme adopted in
(
BCRT
, Section 3)
for the FA-1f process with
q>1/2
, which we summarize briefly here into three main steps. In the sequel, for simplicity, we assume that the local function is simply
f(\eta)=\eta_{0}
and that
\nu
is concentrated on a single configuration
\eta
that satisfies the theorem’s requirement.

Step 1
. Using finite speed of propagation
{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}(f(\eta_{t}))
is approximated by the same average for the finite
\delta
-West chain in the interval
\Lambda_{t}=[-2t,2t]\cap{\mathbb{Z}}
with infected boundary conditions at
\pm(2t+1)
and initial condition the restriction of
\eta
to
\Lambda_{t}
.

Step 2
. Given a small positive constant
\varepsilon
,
\Lambda_{t}
is partitioned into consecutive intervals
\Lambda_{i}
, each one with
\varepsilon t
vertices. One then defines
\hat{\Omega}_{\Lambda_{t}}
as the set of those configurations in
\{0,1\}^{\Lambda_{t}}
that have at least one infection in each interval
\Lambda_{i},
and one compares the average of
f(\eta_{t})
for the
\delta
-West chain in
\Lambda_{t}
to the same average w.r.t.
\delta
-West chain in
\Lambda_{t}

restricted
to
\hat{\Omega}_{\Lambda_{t}}
. The assumption on
\eta
ensures that, for
t
large enough, each interval
\Lambda_{i}
is infected at time
t=0
. Hence, using the key Corollary
4.7
, for
\delta
small enough and some constant
c>0
, with probability
1-O(e^{-ct})
the unrestricted chain will coincide with the restricted one up to time
t
.

Step 3
. In the last step, for
\varepsilon
small enough, one successfully compares the average of
f(\eta_{t})
for the restricted chain to its equilibrium value using bounds on the logarithmic Sobolev constant of the restricted chain. Here, the key input (cf. Lemma
4.8
) is that the growth of the logarithmic Sobolev constant of the restricted chain as
t\to\infty
is
O(\varepsilon t),
to be compared with the
\Theta(t)
growth for the
unrestricted
chain.

We now turn to the analysis of the two key inputs that are needed for the above approach to be successful: (i) persistence of initial infections and (ii) a bound on the logarithmic Sobolev constant of the restricted chain.
For (i), it is convenient to consider the following graphical construction of the
\delta
-West process.

Each vertex carries two independent Poisson clocks, an East clock and a West clock, of rate one and
\delta
respectively. As usual, independence is assumed through the lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}
, and when the East clock at
x
rings, the site
x-1
is queried. Iff
x-1
is infected, then the ring is called
legal
and the state of
x
is resampled from the Bernoulli(p) measure. Similarly, for the West clock rings, but in this case, the queried site is
x+1
.
In the sequel we will denote by
\{t^{E}_{x,i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty},\,\{t^{W}_{x,i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
the rings of the East and West clock at
x
respectively, and by
\{\xi^{E}_{x,i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty},\,\{\xi^{W}_{x,i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
the corresponding Bernoulli(p)-variables.

Definition 4.2
.

(a)

Consider the space-time
S={\mathbb{Z}}\times[0,+\infty)
and fix an integer
\ell
. A broken line
\Gamma^{+}
in
{\mathbb{R}}\times[0,+\infty)
connecting
{\mathbb{Z}}\times 0
to
{\mathbb{Z}}\times t
by going only North or East (up or right) and with corners, if any, occurring only at the vertices of
{\mathbb{Z}}\times\ell{\mathbb{N}}
is called a
NE-barrier
. Similarly, we define a
NW-gate

\Gamma^{-}
. We write
\Gamma^{\pm}(0)\in{\mathbb{Z}}
for the starting point of
\Gamma^{\pm}
and
\Gamma^{\pm}(s),s>0,s\notin\ell{\mathbb{N}},
for the spatial coordinate of the point
\Gamma^{\pm}\cap\{{\mathbb{Z}}\times s\}\in S
. If
s
is a multiple of
\ell
we let
\Gamma^{\pm}(s):=\lim_{s^{\prime}\to s^{-}}\Gamma^{\pm}(s)
.

(b)

Given two NE-barrier
\Gamma^{+}_{1},\Gamma^{+}_{2}
we say that
\Gamma^{+}_{2}
is to the right of
\Gamma^{+}_{1}
if
\Gamma^{+}_{2}(s)>\Gamma^{+}_{1}(s)
for any
s\leq t
. Similarly for the NW-gates.

(c)

Finally, a NE-barrier or a NW-gate is good if there is no West clock ring along its vertical parts.

Remark 4.3
.

(a)

For a.a. realizations of the Poisson clocks in the interval
[0,t]
, there exist infinitely many good NE-barriers and good NW-gates connecting
{\mathbb{Z}}\times 0
to
{\mathbb{Z}}\times t
. Moreover, NE-barriers and NW-gates can be horizontal only at times multiple of
\ell
. Hence, a.s. any ring of the Poisson clocks along them can only occur inside their open vertical segments.

(b)

Suppose that
I=i\times(k_{1}\ell,k_{2}\ell),
with
k_{1}<k_{2}\in{\mathbb{N}}
, is a (open) vertical stretch of a NE-barrier or of a NW-gate between two consecutive turns. In order that there is no West clock ring along
I
it is sufficient that all points
\{(i,j),j=k_{1},\dots,k_{2}-1\}
are good, where
(i,j)\in{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{N}}
is
good
iff the West clock at
i
does not ring during the time interval
[j\ell,(j+1)\ell)
. Clearly
the events
\{(i,j)\text{ is good})\}_{(i,j)\in{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{N}}}
are independent and
{\mathbb{P}}((i,j)\text{ is good})=e^{-\delta\ell}
.

The main properties of good NE-barriers and NW-gates are summarized in the next claim.

Claim 4.4
.

(1)

Suppose that we have a good NE-barrier
\Gamma^{+}
starting at time
t=0
at some positive site and a good NW-gate
\Gamma^{-}
starting at some negative site. If at time
t=0
the origin is infected and at time
t\notin\ell{\mathbb{N}}
there is no infection in
\{\Gamma^{-}(t)+1,\dots,\Gamma^{+}(t)\}
then there exists
s<t,s\notin\ell{\mathbb{N}},
such that:

(i)

s
is a East-ring for
\Gamma^{-}(s)+1
with coin toss variable equal to one;

(ii)

the sites
\Gamma^{-}(s)+2,\dots,\Gamma^{+}(s)
are healthy while the sites
\Gamma^{-}(s)
and
\Gamma^{-}(s)+1
are infected.

(2)

Let
\Gamma^{-}
be a good NW-gate starting at
t=0
and assume to know all the clock rings together with their corresponding coin tosses in the region
\cup_{0\leq s\leq t}\,\left[\{x\leq\Gamma^{-}(s)\}\times s\right]
. Then, given
\{\eta_{x}(0)\}_{x\leq\Gamma^{-}(0)},
the variables
\cup_{s\leq t}\cup_{x\leq\Gamma^{-}(s)}\{\eta_{x}(s)\}
are known.

Proof of Claim
4.4
.

(1)

For simplicity write
\Lambda(s)=[\Gamma^{-}(s)+1,\Gamma^{+}(s)]\cap{\mathbb{Z}}
and observe that
\Gamma^{+}(s)>0
and
\Gamma^{-}(s)<0
for all
s\geq 0
.
Let
\tau=\inf\{s\in[0,t):\eta(s)\restriction_{\Lambda(s)}=1\}.

By construction, at time
\tau
there is a unique vacancy in
\Lambda(\tau)
at either
\Gamma^{+}(\tau)
or
\Gamma^{-}(\tau)+1
which disappears because of a legal ring at its location. We can exclude the first option because a ring at
\Gamma^{+}(\tau)
can only be an East ring, which, in order to be legal, needs a vacancy at
\Gamma^{+}(\tau)-1\in\Lambda(\tau)
.

(2)

It follows immediately from the fact that there are no West rings on
\Gamma^{-}
and no rings at times multiple of
\ell
.

∎

Write now
\Gamma^{+}
for the leftmost good NE-barrier starting to the right of the origin,
\Gamma^{-}_{1}
for the rightmost good NW-gate starting to the left of the origin,
\Gamma^{-}_{2}
for the rightmost good NW-gate to the left of
\Gamma^{-}_{1}
, etc. For any
s\in[0,t]
also let
\Lambda_{n}(s)=\{\Gamma_{n}^{-}(s)+1,\dots,\Gamma^{+}(s)\}
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Fix
\eta
such that
\eta_{0}=0
and call
\mathcal{H}_{n}(t)
the event that
\Lambda_{n}(t)
is healthy. Then
{\mathbb{P}}_{\eta}(\mathcal{H}_{n}(t))\leq p^{n}
.

Proof of the lemma.

In analogy with the proof of Lemma
3.2
, we set

T_{k}=\max\{s\leq t:\ \eta_{\Gamma^{-}_{k}(s)}(s)=0\text{ and }s=t_{\Gamma^{-}_{k}(s)+1,i}\text{ for some $i$}\},

and call
\gamma_{k}=\Gamma^{-}_{k}(T_{k})
and
\alpha_{k}
the label
i
of the clock ring at
\gamma_{k}+1
such that
T_{k}=t_{\gamma_{k}+1,i}
.
The same arguments used to prove part 1 of the previous claim, show that if
\Lambda_{1}(0)
is infected while
\Lambda_{n}(t)
is healthy, then necessarily
\{T_{k},\gamma_{k},\alpha_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
are well defined and the corresponding coin tosses
\{\xi_{\gamma_{k}+1,\alpha_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{n}
are all equal to one.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{t}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by all the Poisson clocks up to time
t
. By construction, a.s.

{\mathbb{P}}_{\eta}(\mathcal{H}_{n}(t)|\mathcal{F}_{t})\leq\sum_{y_{n}<\dots<y_{1}=-1}^{\infty}\dots\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{n}=1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}(\prod_{k=1}^{n}{\mathbf{1}}_{\{\gamma_{k}=y_{k}\}}{\mathbf{1}}_{\{\alpha_{k}=i_{k}\}}\xi_{y_{k}+1,i_{k}}|\mathcal{F}_{t}).

Since there is no West ring along
\Gamma^{-}_{1}
, the coin tosses associated to any clock ring in the space-time region
\cup_{s=0}^{t}[[\Gamma^{-}_{1}(s)+1),\infty)\times s]
do not affect the evolution in the region
\cup_{s\leq t}[(-\infty,\Gamma^{-}_{1}(s)]\times s.
Hence the variable
\xi_{y_{1}+1,i_{1}}
is independent of the variables
\{\gamma_{k},\alpha_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
and

\displaystyle\sum_{y_{n}<\dots<y_{1}=-1}^{\infty}\dots\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{n}=1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}(\prod_{k=1}^{n}{\mathbf{1}}_{\{\gamma_{k}=y_{k}\}}{\mathbf{1}}_{\{\alpha_{k}=i_{k}\}}\xi_{y_{k}+1,i_{k}}|\mathcal{F}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{y_{n}<\dots<y_{2}=-1}^{\infty}\dots\sum_{i_{2},\dots,i_{n}=1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}(\prod_{k=2}^{n}{\mathbf{1}}_{\{\gamma_{k}=y_{k}\}}{\mathbf{1}}_{\{\alpha_{k}=i_{k}\}}\xi_{y_{k}+1,i_{k}}|\mathcal{F}_{t})\times p.

By iterating the above argument, we conclude that, a.s.,
{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{H}_{n}(t)|\mathcal{F}(t))\leq p^{n}
.
∎

We now exploit the lemma to show that for
\delta
small enough, an initial infection at the origin is likely to survive not too far from the origin.

Lemma 4.6
.

For any
\epsilon>0
there exists
\delta_{0}\in(0,1)
and
c>0
such that for any
0<\delta\leq\delta_{0}
and any
t>0

\sup_{\eta:\,\eta_{0}=0}{\mathbb{P}}_{\eta}([-\epsilon t,\varepsilon t]\text{ is healthy at time $t$ })\leq e^{-c\varepsilon t}.

Proof of the lemma.

Fix
\kappa>0
in such a way that oriented (NE or NW) site percolation in
{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{N}}
(cf. e.g.
Durrett
) with parameter
e^{-\kappa}
is supercritical. In particular (see e.g.
(
KobAndersen
, Proposition 3.1)
), for any
n
large enough with probability greater than
1-e^{-\Theta(n)}
there exist
\Theta(n)
disjoint occupied NE-oriented paths connecting
{\mathbb{Z}}\times 0
to
{\mathbb{Z}}\times n
and all contained in
\cup_{j=0}^{n}\{j,j+1,\dots,j+n\}
.

Recall now part (b) of Remark
4.3
. If we choose the free parameter
\ell
of Definition
4.2
equal to
\lfloor\kappa/\delta\rfloor
, we get that the good sites of
{\mathbb{Z}}\times\ell{\mathbb{N}}\subset S
perform a supercritical oriented percolation with slope
\Theta(\kappa/\delta)
. Given an oriented (NE or NW) good path
\gamma
in
{\mathbb{Z}}\times\ell{\mathbb{N}}\subset S
connecting
{\mathbb{Z}}\times 0
to
{\mathbb{Z}}\times N
, we can form a good (NE-barrier or NW-gate) by joining one vertex of
\gamma
to the next one in increasing order w.r.t. the time coordinate. Notice that in this construction of a good NE-barrier or of a good NW-gate, also the points lying on a horizontal part of the line are good.

In conclusion, if we choose
\delta<O(\kappa\varepsilon)
we get that there exist positive constants
\lambda
and
c
depending only on
\kappa
such that with probability greater that
1-e^{-c\varepsilon t}
there exists at least
\lambda\varepsilon t
good NW-gates in the space-time window
[-\varepsilon t,0]\times[0,t]
and one good NE-barrier in the space-time window
[0,\varepsilon t]\times[0,t]
joining
{\mathbb{Z}}\times 0
to
{\mathbb{Z}}\times t
. The proof is finished using Lemma
4.5
with
n=\lceil\lambda\varepsilon t\rceil
.
∎

Corollary 4.7
.

In the same assumption of Lemma
4.6
there exists
c^{\prime}>0
such that for any
t>0

\sup_{\eta:\,\eta_{0}=0}{\mathbb{P}}_{\eta}(\exists s\leq t:\,[-\epsilon t,\varepsilon t]\text{ is healthy at time $s$ })\leq e^{-c^{\prime}et}.

Proof of the corollary.

It follows immediately from a union bound over the possible Poisson clocks rings in
[-\varepsilon t,\varepsilon t]\times[0,t],
standard large deviations bounds for the Poisson process, and the argument given in the proof of Lemma
4.6
.
∎

We now turn to the second key input behind the approach described at the beginning of the proof.

Fix
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
and let
\Lambda_{N}
be the union of the first
N
consecutive discrete intervals
\Lambda_{i}=\{i(\ell+1)-\ell,i(\ell+1)\}
. Let
\hat{\Omega}_{N,\ell}
consists of those configurations of
\{0,1\}^{\Lambda_{N}}
that have at least one infection in each interval
\Lambda_{i},i=1,\dots,N
. We then consider the East+
\delta
-West chain in
\Lambda_{N}
restricted to
\hat{\Omega}_{N,\ell}
and with infected boundary conditions at the origin and at
x=N(L\ell+1)+1
. This restricted chain is ergodic and reversible w.r.t.
\hat{\pi}_{\Lambda_{N}}(\cdot)=\otimes_{i}\hat{\pi}_{\Lambda_{i}},\ \hat{\pi}_{\Lambda_{i}}(\cdot):=\pi_{\Lambda_{i}}(\cdot|\Lambda_{i}\text{ infected})
, with a positive spectral gap and a finite logarithmic Sobolev constant (see
Diaconis
and
(
BCRT
, Section 3.1)
). The key point is that the logarithmic Sobolev constant is
O(\ell)
. More precisely,

Lemma 4.8
.

There exists a constant
c=c(q)
such that for any
\delta\geq 0
and any
N
, the logarithmic Sobolev constant of the restricted chain is bounded from above by
c\ell
.

Proof of the lemma.

We first recall the following result
(
Martinelli99
, Proposition 3.10)
. For any discrete probability measure
\mu
with finite
\mu^{*}:=\min_{\eta}\mu(\eta)
it holds that, for any
f
,

\displaystyle{\rm Ent}_{\mu}(f^{2})

\displaystyle:=\mu(f^{2}\log(f^{2}))-\mu(f^{2})\log(\mu(f^{2})))

(4.1)

\displaystyle\leq 2(4+2|\log(\mu^{*})|)\operatorname{Var}_{\mu}(f).

Using the fact that
\hat{\pi}_{\Lambda_{N}}=\otimes_{i}\hat{\pi}_{\Lambda_{i}}

it follows immediately from (
4
) that for any
f:\Omega_{N,\ell}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}

{\rm Ent}_{\hat{\pi}_{\Lambda_{N}}}(f^{2})\leq c_{q}\ell\sum_{i}\hat{\pi}_{\Lambda_{N}}\big(\operatorname{Var}_{\hat{\pi}_{\Lambda_{i}}}(f)\big).

For each interval
\Lambda_{i},\;i\geq 2,
the previous block
\Lambda_{i-1}
has a leftmost infection. The required infection for the interval
\Lambda_{1}
is simply represented by the infected boundary condition at the origin. We can then use the standard enlargement trick
(
HT
, Section 4.2.2.2 equation 4.13)
to extend
\operatorname{Var}_{\hat{\pi}_{\Lambda_{i}}}(f)
to a larger interval whose left boundary borders the leftmost infection in
\Lambda_{i-1}
. For the enlarged variance we can now use the Poincaré inequality and the spectral gap bound for the East model (i.e. with
\delta=0
) to conclude that the r.h.s above is bounded from above by
c_{q}\times\ell\times{\rm gap}_{\rm East}^{-1}\times\hat{}\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda_{N}}(f)
, where
\hat{}\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda_{N}}(f)
is the restricted chain Dirichlet form of
f
.
∎

The proof of the theorem is complete.
∎

5.
Results for BABP

All the results concerning BABP are based on a remarkable self-duality property and on its quasi-duality with the
Double Flip Process (DFP)

Loyd-Sudbury97
, an interacting particle system in which the state of each
edge of
{\mathbb{Z}}
, independently across
{\mathbb{Z}}
, is updated according to the rule:

\displaystyle(0,0)\rightarrow(1,1)

\displaystyle\text{ with rate }(\sqrt{1+\lambda}+1)^{2}/2,

\displaystyle(1,1)\rightarrow(0,0)

\displaystyle\text{ with rate }(\sqrt{1+\lambda}-1)^{2}/2,

\displaystyle(1,0)\leftrightarrow(0,1)

\displaystyle\text{ with rate }\lambda/2,

where
\lambda=q/p
.

Remark 5.1
.

If one considers DFP on
\{1,\dots,n\}
it has two ergodic components because the parity of the number of infections is preserved.

It is easy to check that DFP is reversible w.r.t. product Bernoulli measure
\hat{\pi}
of parameter
\hat{p}=\frac{\sqrt{1+\lambda}+1}{2\sqrt{1+\lambda}},
with
\hat{q}:=1-\hat{p}\simeq q/4
as
q\to 0
. To avoid confusion, we will use
{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}(\cdot)
and
\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}(\cdot)
in the sequel to denote the averages with respect to the BABP and the DFP, respectively, with initial state
\eta
.
Our main result for DFP is the following.

Theorem 5.2
.

For any
\lambda>0
, there exists
m>0
, and for any function
f
depending on the state of finitely many vertices, there exists a constant
C_{f}>0
such that

(5.1)

\sup_{\eta}|\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}(f(\eta(t)))-\hat{\pi}(f)|\leq C_{f}e^{-mt}.

Before proving the theorem, we first present the self-duality of BABP and its quasi-duality with DFP, and show how to combine them to derive two interesting consequences for BABP. We emphasize that the main ideas presented here originate from
Sudbury99
;
Loyd-Sudbury97
, and that our primary new contribution is the proof of exponential ergodicity for DFP in
{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Call
y=\sqrt{\lambda+1}
, and fix three subsets
B,B^{\prime},D
of
{\mathbb{Z}}
. We consider three independent processes
\{B(t)\}_{t\geq 0},\{B^{\prime}(t)\}_{t\geq 0},
and
\{D(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
in
{\mathbb{Z}}
defined as follows. The set
B(t)
represents the set of infections at time
t
of BABP with
B(0)=B
. Similarly for
B^{\prime}(t)
with
B
replaced by
B^{\prime}
. The set
D(t)
represents instead the set of infections at time
t
of DFP with
D(t)=D
. The initial sets
B,B^{\prime},D
could be random according to a certain joint law, and the symbol
{\mathbb{E}}(\cdot)
will denote the global average over the three processes including their initial randomness.

If
|B|
denotes the cardinality of the set
B
, then
the
self-duality
of BABP and the
quasi-duality
between BABP and DFP read as follows.

(5.2)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big((-\frac{1}{\lambda})^{|B^{\prime}(t)\cap B|}\big)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\big((-\frac{1}{\lambda})^{|B(t)\cap B^{\prime}|}\big),

(5.3)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big(\big(\frac{1}{y+1}\big)^{|D\cap B(t)|}\big(\frac{-1}{y-1}\big)^{|D^{c}\cap B(t)|}\Big)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\Big(\big(\frac{1}{y+1}\big)^{|B\cap D(t)|}\big(\frac{-1}{y-1}\big)^{|B\cap D^{c}(t)|}\Big),

whenever the l.h.s and the r.h.s. make sense.

Remark 5.3
.

It is not difficult to verify that, if the r.h.s. of (
5.2
) tends to zero as
t\to\infty
for any finite set
B^{\prime},
then the law of
B(t)
tends to
\pi
as
t\to\infty
.

Application 1

(Linear growth of
B(t)
starting from finitely many infections)
.

Take for simplicity
B=\{0\}
(any finite set would do as well). Finite speed of propagation implies that a.s.
|B(t)|\leq O(t)
as
t\to\infty
.
If
D={\mathbb{Z}},
then (
5.3
) together with Theorem
5.2
implies that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big(\big(\frac{1}{y+1}\big)^{|B(t)|}\big)

\displaystyle=\big(\frac{1}{y+1}+\frac{1}{y-1}\big){\mathbb{P}}(D(t)\ni\{0\})-\frac{1}{y-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{y-1}\Big(\frac{2y}{y+1}{\mathbb{P}}(D(t)\ni\{0\})-1\Big)=O(e^{-mt}).

In particular,

{\mathbb{P}}(|B(t)|\leq\delta mt)\leq O(e^{-mt/2}),

for some
\delta\in(0,1)
small enough. The a.s. linear growth in
t
of
|B(t)|
as
t\to\infty
follows easily.

Remark 5.4
.

In
(
Mountford93
, Corollary 2.3)
, it was proved that if
\lambda>1/3,
then the BABP process starting from finitely many infections converges to
\pi
. The condition
\lambda>1/3
was later improved to
\lambda>0.0347
in
(
Sudbury99
, Theorem 7)
.

Application 2

(Convergence of BABP to
\pi
when the initial measure is a Bernoulli product measure)
.

Let
B
be a finite set, let
\eta^{\prime}
be distributed according to
\nu_{\alpha}
, the Bernoulli product measure with parameter
\alpha,
and let
B^{\prime}
be the set of infections of
\eta^{\prime}
. We distinguish between two cases for
\alpha
.

(1)

0<1-\alpha<\frac{2\lambda}{\lambda+1}
. In this case self-duality (
5.2
) gives

(5.4)

{\mathbb{E}}\big((-\frac{1}{\lambda})^{|B^{\prime}(t)\cap B|}\big)={\mathbb{E}}\big((\alpha-(1-\alpha)/\lambda)^{|B(t)|}\big).

Notice that
|\alpha-(1-\alpha)/\lambda|<1
so that
a.s. the r.h.s. of (
5.4
) decreases exponentially fast in
t
because
|B(t)|
grows a.s. linearly in
t
.
Using Remark
5.3
, we conclude that the law of BABP with initial measure
\nu_{\alpha}
converges to
\pi
exponentially fast.

(2)

\frac{2\lambda}{\lambda+1}\leq 1-\alpha\leq 1
.
Let
\beta=\frac{1}{2}\big(1+\alpha y\big).
Then
\beta\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
and

\alpha-(1-\alpha)/\lambda=\frac{\beta}{y+1}-\frac{1-\beta}{y-1}.

Thus, if
D\sim\nu_{\beta},
the r.h.s. of (
5.2
) coincides with the l.h.s. of (
5.3
). On the other hand, the r.h.s. of (
5.3
) is
O(e^{-mt})
for some constant
m>0
because of Theorem
5.2
. In conclusion, the l.h.s. of (
5.2
) is exponentially small in
t
and the conclusion is as in the previous case.

Remark 5.5
.

The fact that BABP started from
\nu_{\alpha},\ \alpha\leq 1/y,
converges exponentially fast to
\pi
could also be derived from the quasi-thinning relation between BABP and DFP
Loyd-Sudbury97
. In this case, the law of BABP at time
t
can be mapped into the law of DFP at time
t
with initial distribution
\nu_{1-\beta}
. The same conclusion holds if we start BABP from an inhomogeneous Bernoulli product measure
\otimes_{x}\nu_{\alpha_{x}}
with
\alpha_{x}\leq 1/y
for all
x
.

5.1.
Proof of Theorem
5.2

To fix ideas one can imagine that the local function
f
is just the state of the origin,
\eta_{0}
. If
\Lambda=\Lambda_{t}=[-Mt,Mt],
finite speed of propagation implies that we can choose
M=M(\lambda)>0
such that, uniformly in
\eta
,

\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}(f(\eta(t)))=\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta^{\Lambda}}(f(\eta_{\Lambda}(t)))+O(e^{-t}),

where
\eta^{\Lambda}(t)
is the DFP continuous time chain in
\Lambda
. In the sequel, for lightness of notation, we will drop the superscript
\Lambda
from the notation, and we will write
\hat{P}_{t}
for the semigroup of the DFP in
\Lambda
.
We now analyze
\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta^{\Lambda}}(f(\eta_{\Lambda}(t))\equiv(\hat{P}_{t}f)(\eta)
.

As explained in Remark
5.1
, the parity
\mathcal{P}(\eta)\in\{+,-\}
of a configuration
\eta
, i.e., whether it has an even or odd number of infections, is preserved by the DFP chain. In particular, if
\hat{\pi}^{\star}
denote the product Bernoulli(
\hat{p}
) measure in
\Lambda
conditioned to
\mathcal{P}=\star
, then
(\hat{P}_{t}f)(\eta)
will converge to
\hat{\pi}^{\star}
as
t\to\infty
for all
\eta
with
\mathcal{P}(\eta)=\star
. It is easy to verify that there exists
c=c(\hat{p})>0
such that for all
t>0

|\hat{\pi}^{+}(f)-\hat{\pi}^{-}(f)|\leq O(e^{-ct}),

and therefore the theorem follows if we can prove that there exists
m>0
such that, for all
t
large enough,

(5.5)

\max_{\star}\max_{\eta\sim\star}|(\hat{P}_{t}f)(\eta)-\hat{\pi}^{\star}(f)|\leq e^{-mt}.

The key to proving (
5.5
) is the following result, whose proof is postponed to the end of the section.

Consider the DFP in the interval
I_{n}=\{1,\dots,n\}
restricted to configurations with
\mathcal{P}=\star
and with reversible measure
\hat{\pi}^{\star}_{n},
the product Bernoulli
(\hat{p})
measure in
I_{n}
conditioned to
\mathcal{P}=\star
. Write
c_{sob}(n,\star)
for its logarithmic Sobolev constant, i.e. the best constant
c
in the Logarithmic Sobolev inequality

{\rm Ent}_{\hat{\pi}^{\star}_{n}}(f^{2})\leq c\mathcal{D}^{\star}_{n}(f,f),\quad\forall f:\Omega_{I_{n}}^{\star}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}},

where
{\rm Ent}_{\hat{\pi}^{\star}_{n}}(f^{2})=\hat{\pi}_{n}^{\star}(f^{2}\log(\frac{f^{2}}{\hat{\pi}^{\star}_{n}(f^{2})}))
,
\Omega_{I_{n}}^{\star}=\{\eta\in\Omega_{I_{n}}:\ \mathcal{P}(\eta)=\star\},
and
\mathcal{D}^{\star}_{n}(f,f)
is the Dirichlet form of the chain.

Proposition 5.6
.

c_{sob}:=\sup_{n,\star}c_{sob}(n,\star)<+\infty.

Back to the proof of (
5.5
), let
g_{t-s}=\hat{P}_{t-s}f-\hat{\pi}^{\star}(f),\,s=\log(t)^{2}
.
Then, for any
\eta
with
\mathcal{P}(\eta)=\star
and
a=1+e^{2s/c_{sob}}
,

\displaystyle|(\hat{P}_{t}f)(\eta)-\hat{\pi}^{\star}(f)|

\displaystyle=|(\hat{P}_{s}g_{t-s})(\eta)|

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{\hat{\pi}^{*}\big(|\hat{P}_{s}g_{t-s}|^{a}\big)}{\hat{\pi}^{*}(\eta)}\Big)^{1/a}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{1}{\hat{\pi}^{*}(\eta)}\Big)^{1/a}\hat{\pi}^{*}\big(g^{2}_{t-s}\big)^{1/2},

where we used hypercontractivity of the chain in the last inequality
Diaconis
. The choice of
s,a
guarantees that
\Big(\frac{1}{\hat{\pi}^{*}(\eta)}\Big)^{1/a}=O(1)
as
t\to\infty
, while the boundedness of
c_{sob}
and the fact that
\hat{\pi}^{\star}(g_{t-s})=0
imply that

\hat{\pi}^{*}\big(g^{2}_{t-s}\big)^{1/2}\leq e^{-(t-s)/c_{sob}}\operatorname{Var}_{\hat{\pi}^{\star}}(f)^{1/2},

and (
5.5
) follows.

Proof of Proposition
5.6
.

Let
\gamma_{k}=\max_{n\leq 2^{k}+\sqrt{2^{k}}}\max_{\star}c_{sob}(n,\star)
. We will prove recursively that for all
k
large enough

(5.6)

\gamma_{k+1}\leq(1+\varepsilon_{k})\gamma_{k},\qquad\varepsilon_{k}=O(e^{-c^{\prime}k}),

for some
c^{\prime}>0
.
Clearly (
5.6
) shows that
\sup_{k}\gamma_{k}<+\infty
, i.e.
c_{sob}<+\infty
.

Fix an integer
n\in(2^{k}+\sqrt{2^{k}},2^{k+1}+\sqrt{2^{k+1}}]
and cover the interval
I_{n}
with
\Lambda_{1}=\{1,\dots,n_{1}\}
and
\Lambda_{2}=\{n_{2},\dots,n\}
, where
n_{1}=\lfloor\frac{n}{2}+n^{1/3}\rfloor
and
n_{2}=\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
. Finally, denote by
\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2}
the
\sigma
-algebras generated by the variables
\{\eta_{x}\}_{x\in\{1,\dots,n_{2}-1\}}
and
\{\eta_{x}\}_{x\in\{n_{1}+1,\dots,n\}}
respectively.

Claim 5.7
.

There exists a constant
c>0
such that, for any function
g
measurable w.r.t
\mathcal{F}_{1}
and any parity
\star
,

(5.7)

\|\hat{\pi}_{n}^{\star}(g|\mathcal{F}_{2})-\hat{\pi}_{n}^{\star}(g)\|_{\infty}\leq e^{-cn^{1/3}}\,\hat{\pi}_{n}^{\star}(|g|).

Proof of the claim.

The conditional expectation
\hat{\pi}_{n}^{\star}(g|\mathcal{F}_{2})
depends on the variables generating
\mathcal{F}_{2}
only through their parity. Moreover,
g
depends only on the first
n_{2}-1
variables in
\Lambda_{1}
. Hence, (
5.7
) follows immediately by using the product structure of
\hat{\pi}
together with the well known computation
{\mathbb{P}}(\text{Bin}(m,\hat{p})\text{ is even })=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}(1-2\hat{p})^{m}
.
∎

If we combine (
5.7
) together with
(
Cesi
, Proposition 2.1)
, we get the quasi-factorization of
{\rm Ent}_{\hat{\pi}^{\star}_{n}}(f^{2}),
namely

(5.8)

{\rm Ent}_{\hat{\pi}^{\star}_{n}}(f^{2})\leq(1+O(\delta_{n}))\hat{\pi}_{n}^{\star}\Big({\rm Ent}_{\hat{\pi}^{\star}_{n}(\cdot|\mathcal{F}_{2})}(f^{2})+{\rm Ent}_{\hat{\pi}^{\star}_{n}(\cdot|\mathcal{F}_{1})}(f^{2})\Big),

where
\delta_{n}=e^{-c\,n^{1/3}}
.

In order to bound the r.h.s. above, we observe that, for all
k
large enough, the cardinality of
\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}
is smaller than
2^{k}+\sqrt{2^{k}}
. Hence, the logarithmic Sobolev constant of the DFP in
\Lambda_{i},i=1,2
with arbitrary parity is bounded from above by
\gamma_{k}
. By applying the logarithmic Sobolev inequality to each term
\hat{\pi}_{n}^{\star}\Big({\rm Ent}_{\hat{\pi}^{\star}_{n}(\cdot|\mathcal{F}_{i})}(f^{2})\Big),i=1,2,
we conclude that

(5.9)

\hat{\pi}_{n}^{\star}\Big({\rm Ent}_{\hat{\pi}^{\star}_{n}(\cdot|\mathcal{F}_{2})}(f^{2})+{\rm Ent}_{\hat{\pi}^{\star}_{n}(\cdot|\mathcal{F}_{1})}(f^{2})\Big)\leq\gamma_{k}\mathcal{D}^{\star}_{n}(f,f)+\gamma_{k}\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda_{1}\cap\Lambda_{2}}^{\star}(f,f),

where
\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda_{1}\cap\Lambda_{2}}^{\star}(f,f)
is the contribution to the total Dirichlet form
\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\star}(f,f)
of the updates of nearest neighbor pairs contained in
\Lambda_{1}\cap\Lambda_{2}
. Hence,

(5.10)

{\rm Ent}_{\hat{\pi}^{\star}_{n}}(f^{2})\leq(1+O(\delta_{n}))\gamma_{k}\Big(\mathcal{D}^{\star}_{n}(f,f)+\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda_{1}\cap\Lambda_{2}}^{\star}(f,f)\Big)

The trick to overcome the double counting in the r.h.s. above is to spread it out over many similar choices of the sets
\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}
(see
(
Martinelli99
, Proof of Theorem 4.5)
). More precisely, let
N=\lfloor n^{\frac{1}{7}}\rfloor,
and let
\Lambda_{1}^{(j)}=\{1,\dots,n_{1}^{(j)}\},\Lambda_{2}^{(j)}=\{n_{2}^{(j)},\dots,n\},j=1,\dots,N,
, where
n_{1}^{(j)}=\lfloor\frac{n}{2}+jn^{1/3}\rfloor
and
n_{2}^{(j)}=n_{1}^{(j-1)}+1
. For any
k
large enough, the cardinality of each of the above sets is still at most
2^{k}+\sqrt{2^{k}}
and
\Lambda_{1}^{(j)}\cap\Lambda_{2}^{(j)}
are disjoint sets as
j
varies. We can then apply (
5.10
) to each pair
\Lambda_{1}^{(j)},\Lambda_{2}^{(j)}
and average the resulting inequality over
j
to get

(5.11)

\displaystyle{\rm Ent}_{\hat{\pi}^{\star}_{n}}(f^{2})

\displaystyle\leq(1+O(\delta_{n}))\gamma_{k}\Big(\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\star}(f,f)+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda^{(j)}_{1}\cap\Lambda^{(j)}_{2}}^{\star}(f,f)\Big)

(5.12)

\displaystyle\leq(1+O(\delta_{n}))(1+\frac{1}{N})\gamma_{k}\,\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\star}(f,f).

In conclusion, for any
2^{k}+\sqrt{2^{k}}<n\leq 2^{k+1}+\sqrt{2^{k+1}},

c_{sob}(n,\star)\leq(1+O(\delta_{n}))(1+\frac{1}{\lfloor n^{\frac{1}{7}}\rfloor})\gamma_{k}\leq(1+O(e^{-c^{\prime}k}))\gamma_{k}

for a suitable
c^{\prime}>0
. (
5.6
) is now established.
∎

5.1.1.
Extension to higher dimensions

We claim that the two main results presented in Applications 1 and 2 hold in any dimension.

The self-duality of BABP and its quasi-duality with DFP also hold for the processes defined in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
d\geq 2
. Theorem
5.2
also extends to higher dimensions because the logarithmic Sobolev constant of DFP in dimension
d
can be bounded from above by the same constant in dimension
d=1
.
To see that, suppose w.l.o.g.
d=2
and delete from
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
all the vertical edges. On the new graph, consider the auxiliary process defined as the product of one-dimensional DFP, one for every horizontal copy of
{\mathbb{Z}}
. The auxiliary process is still reversible w.r.t.
\hat{\pi}
, and its Dirichlet form is smaller than the full DFP Dirichlet form because the contribution coming from the vertical edges is missing. Moreover, the logarithmic Sobolev constant of the auxiliary process is equal to that of the one-dimensional DFP because of the well-known tensorization property of the logarithmic Sobolev inequality
Diaconis
.

6.
FA1
{\rm f}
with finitely many infections

In this section, we present some simple results for the FA-1f process, starting with a finite number of infections.

Given
\eta\in\Omega
let
X^{+}(\eta)=\sup\{x:\ \eta_{x}=0\},X^{-}(\eta)=\inf\{x:\ \eta_{x}=0\}
be the position of the rightmost/leftmost infection of
\eta
and set

Y(\eta)=\max(|X^{+}(\eta)|,|X^{-}(\eta)|),\quad D(\eta)=X^{+}(\eta)-X^{-}(\eta).

Let
\eta(t)
be the FA-1f process starting from
\eta
and write
X^{\pm}(t),Y(t),D(t)
for the quantities
X^{\pm}(\eta(t)),Y(\eta(t)),D(\eta(t))
.
Before stating the main result on the long-time behaviour of
Y(t),D(t)
when they are both finite, let us make some easy observations.

Claim 6.1
.

(i)

\limsup_{t\to+\infty}X^{+}(t)
and
\liminf_{t\to+\infty}X^{-}(t)
belong to
\{-\infty,+\infty\}
a.s.

(ii)

If
\eta
has a single vacancy at the origin then
{\mathbb{P}}_{\eta}(\limsup_{t\to+\infty}X^{+}(t)=+\infty)\geq 1/2
.

(iii)

If
Y(\eta)<+\infty
then
{\mathbb{P}}_{\eta}(\limsup_{t\to+\infty}X^{+}(t)=+\infty)>0
.

Proof.

(i)

Suppose by contradiction that
\limsup_{t\to+\infty}X^{+}(t)
is finite, say equal to
x
for some
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}.
Then, every time that
X^{+}(t)=x
there is a positive chance to advance to
x+1
contradicting the hypothesis.

(ii)

Let
\beta={\mathbb{P}}_{\eta}(\limsup_{t\to+\infty}X^{+}(t)=-\infty)
. Then
{\mathbb{P}}_{\eta}(\liminf_{t\to+\infty}X^{-}(t)=-\infty)\geq\beta
and symmetry implies that
1-\beta={\mathbb{P}}_{\eta}(\limsup_{t\to+\infty}X^{+}(t)=+\infty)\geq\beta
, i.e.
\beta\leq 1/2.

(iii)

If
\eta
has finitely many infections, with positive probability the process can reach the configuration with a single infection at time
t=1
. Hence, the statement follows from part (ii).

∎

Theorem 6.2
.

For any
q\in(0,1]
there exist two positive constants
b,c
such that, for any
\eta
with finitely many infections, a.s. as
t\to+\infty

(6.1)

\displaystyle b^{-1}t\leq Y(t)

\displaystyle\leq ct

(6.2)

\displaystyle c^{-1}t\leq D(t)

\displaystyle\leq ct.

Remark 6.3
.

Notice that for BABP, the result follows at once from the linear growth in time of the cardinality of the infection proved in the first application.

Proof of (
6.1
).

We introduce the hitting times
\tau_{n}=\inf\{t:Y(t)=n\}
and the return times
\sigma_{n}=\inf\{t\geq\tau_{n}:Y(t)=0\}
. In the sequel, we say that the process has an
n
-excursion if
\sigma_{n}<\tau_{n+1}
and we write
p_{n}
for its probability. The duration of an
n
-excursion is
\sigma_{n}
.

Lemma 6.4
.

There exist positive constants
\kappa_{-},\kappa_{+},c
such that, for any large enough
n,

{\mathbb{P}}_{\eta}\big(\tau_{n}\notin(\kappa_{-}n,\kappa_{+}n)\big)\leq e^{-n}
and
p_{n}\leq e^{-c^{\prime}n}.

Proof of the lemma.

For simplicity and without loss of generality, we assume that
\eta
has a single infection at the origin.

The fact that
{\mathbb{P}}_{\eta}(\tau_{n}\leq n/3)\leq\frac{1}{2}e^{-n}
for any
n
large enough follows from the usual finite speed of propagation argument (see e.g.
HT
). Hence, we can choose
\kappa_{-}=1/3
.

Next, we observe that, by construction,
\tau_{n}
coincides with the same hitting time for the finite FA-1f
chain
on the interval
\Lambda_{n}=[-n,n]
with healthy boundary condition at
\pm(n+1).
For the latter, by applying a general result on hitting times for Markov chains
(
Aldous-Fill
, Proposition 3.21)
we get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{\eta}(\tau_{n}>t)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{p^{2n}q}{\mathbb{P}}_{\pi_{\Lambda_{n}}}(\tau_{n}\geq t)\leq\frac{1}{p^{2n}q}e^{-q\gamma_{n}t},

where
\gamma_{n}\geq\gamma_{\infty}>0
is the spectral gap of the chain on
\Lambda_{n}
with healthy boundary conditions. Hence, we can choose
\kappa_{+}
large enough such that for all
n
large enough
{\mathbb{P}}_{\eta}(\tau_{n}>\kappa_{+}\,n)\leq\frac{1}{2}e^{-n}
.

In order to bound from above the probability of a
n
-excursion we observe that
the event
\{\tau_{n+1}\geq\kappa_{+}(n+1)\}
is implied by the event that the process has
m=\lceil\frac{\kappa_{+}+1}{\kappa_{-}}\rceil

n
-excursions and each one of them has duration at least
\kappa_{-}n
. Call
\mathcal{B}
the latter event and observe that at the end of an
n
-excursion the process starts afresh from
\eta
. In particular, the probability of
m

n
-excursions is
p_{n}^{m}
.
Using the first part of the lemma, we get

\displaystyle e^{-(n+1)}

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{P}}_{\eta}(\tau_{n+1}\geq\kappa_{+}(n+1))\geq{\mathbb{P}}_{\eta}(\mathcal{B})\geq p_{n}^{m}-me^{-n},

where
me^{-n}
is a union bound of the probability that one
n
-excursion among the
m
ones has a duration less than
k_{-}n
. In conclusion

p_{n}\leq\Big(e^{-(n+1)}+me^{-n}\Big)^{1/m}.

∎

Next, we show that, once
Y(t)
reaches level
n
, it is unlikely that it will retrace to level
\varepsilon n
before reaching level
n+1
.

Lemma 6.5
.

There exists
0<\varepsilon_{0}<1
and
c>0
such that for all
0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
and all
n
large enough the following holds. Let
\tau_{\varepsilon n}:=\inf\{t\geq\tau_{n}:Y(t)=\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor\}
.

{\mathbb{P}}_{\eta}(\tau_{\varepsilon n}<\tau_{n+1})\leq e^{-cn}.

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary configuration
\xi
in the interval
I_{\varepsilon,n}=[-\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor,\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor]
with at least one infection at the end points of
I_{\varepsilon,n}
and let

p_{\xi,\epsilon,n}(t)
be the probability that the FA-1f chain in
I_{\varepsilon,n}
, with healthy boundary conditions at
\pm(\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor+1)
and initial state
\xi,
has a single infection at the origin at time
t
. Using that the mixing time of the chain is
O(\varepsilon n)
, we can choose
\lambda=\lambda(q)
large enough such that the variation distance of the law of the chain at time
t=\lambda\varepsilon n
from
\pi_{I_{\varepsilon,n}}
is smaller than
\frac{1}{2}qp^{2\varepsilon n}
. Hence,

\min_{\xi}p_{\xi,\epsilon n}(\lambda\varepsilon n)\geq\frac{1}{2}qp^{2\varepsilon n}.

Back to the FA-1f process on the whole lattice, suppose now that
\tau_{\varepsilon n}<\tau_{n+1}
and that the clocks at
\pm(\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor+1)
never ring between
\tau_{\varepsilon n}
and
\tau_{\varepsilon n}+\lambda\varepsilon n
. Then the previous computation together with the strong Markov property prove that the process, after the hitting time
\tau_{\varepsilon n}
has probability at least
\frac{1}{2}qp^{2\varepsilon n}e^{-2\lambda\varepsilon n}
to go back to the configuration with a single infection at the origin before
\tau_{n+1}
. Using Lemma
4.6
, we conclude that for all
n
large enough

\frac{1}{2}qp^{2\varepsilon n}e^{-2\lambda\varepsilon n}\times{\mathbb{P}}_{\eta}(\tau_{\varepsilon n}<\tau_{n+1})\leq p_{n}\leq e^{-c^{\prime}n},

and the statement follows by taking
\varepsilon
small enough.
∎

A simple Borel-Cantelli argument together with Lemma
6.4
and
6.5
now proves the a.s. linear growth (
6.1
) of
Y(t)
.
∎

Proof of (
6.2
).

To prove the linear growth of
D(t):=D(\eta(t))
, we proceed as follows. Let
\tau_{n}=\inf\{t:D(t)=n\}
and let

S_{n}
be the discrete circle with
2n
points which we label from
0
to
2n-1
in clockwise order starting e.g., from the North Pole. On
S_{n}
, consider the FA-1f chain
\{\hat{\eta}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
starting with exactly one vacancy at the origin, and let
\hat{D}(t)
be the largest distance on
S_{n}
between two vacancies of
\hat{\eta}(t)
. Let also
\hat{\tau}_{n}=\inf\{t>0:\ \hat{D}(t)=n\}
.

Claim 6.6
.

{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{n}>t)\leq{\mathbb{P}}(\hat{\tau}_{n}>t)
.

Proof of the claim.

If
\tau_{n}>t
then for any
s<t
we can map the process on
{\mathbb{Z}}
,
\eta(s),
into a legal configuration
\phi(\eta(s))
of the FA-1f chain on
S_{n}
as follows. Suppose that
X^{+}(\eta(s))=k\times 2n+m,
with
m=[0,2n-1]
and
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Then we set
\phi(\eta(s))_{m-j}=\eta(s)_{k\times 2n+m-j},j=0,\dots,n,
and
\phi(\eta(s))_{x}=1
everywhere else, where
m-j\in S_{n}
is the vertex reached in
j
counterclockwise steps from
m
. Starting with a single vacancy at the origin of
{\mathbb{Z}}
, the evolution of
\phi(\eta(s))
is the correct one for the FA-1f chain on
S_{n}
up to time
\tau_{n}
.
∎

Using the claim and the fact that the FA-1f chain on
S_{n}
restricted to the set of configurations with at least one vacancy has a spectral gap that is uniformly positive in
n
, we obtain the analog of Lemma
6.4
for
\tau_{n}
.
Next, we define the
n
-excursion for the process
D(\cdot)
.

As before, we let
\sigma_{n}=\inf\{t>\tau_{n}:\ D(t)=0\},
we say that there is a
n
-excursion if
\sigma_{n}<\tau_{n+1},
and we write
p_{n}
for the probability of the latter event. Notice that if the process on
{\mathbb{Z}}
had
k

n
-excursions before
\tau_{n+1}
then so does the chain on the torus
S_{n+2}
. Moreover, at the end of each excursion, both processes have a single vacancy somewhere, and they start afresh. Since each excursion lasts
\Omega(n)
w.h.p. because of the finite speed of propagation, we easily get the analog of Lemma
6.4
and

6.5
and from there the linear growth of
D(t)
.
∎
