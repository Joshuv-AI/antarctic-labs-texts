Minimally invasive tubular retractor implantation of a paddle electrode stimulator for the trigeminal nucleus caudalis: illustrative case
Authors: Melod Mehdipour, Muhammad Sulman, Vanshit Thakkar, Arash Ghaffari-Rafi, Stephano Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDPaddle electrode stimulation of the trigeminal nucleus caudalis (TNC) for refractory craniofacial pain syndromes provides improved coverage and has lower migration rates than cylindrical leads; however, paddle implantation requires open surgery, which can cause significant pain and limit use in high-risk patients. Herein, the authors present a minimally invasive (MIS) approach, adapting the use of tubular retractors for MIS spine surgery and therefore minimizing the surgical morbidity of paddle electrode implantation at the craniocervical junction.OBSERVATIONSAn 81-year-old man with postherpetic trigeminal neuropathy in the left V1 and V2 distribution underwent paddle lead implantation via a 2.5-cm occipitocervical incision and a tubular retractor system. The patient experienced a reduction in pain (from a visual analog scale score of 9/10 to 2/10), without any postoperative complications.LESSONSThis technical report demonstrates the feasibility of implanting paddle electrodes via an MIS tubular retractor system for stimulating the TNC. The approach minimizes soft tissue disruption, reduces postoperative pain, and offers a viable alternative to open surgery, which is especially useful for medically complex patients requiring neuromodulation for craniofacial pain. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE25721

Neuromodulation of the trigeminal nucleus caudalis (TNC) has emerged as a promising strategy for managing refractory craniofacial pain syndromes that are unresponsive to medical management or therapies geared toward the trigeminal nerve.1,2 Specifically, spinal cord stimulation (SCS) of the upper cervical dorsal columns is hypothesized to modulate nociceptive input to the TNC, enabling treatment of refractory trigeminal neuropathy.3,4 However, the widely used options for SCS implantation at this level, namely percutaneous implantation of wire leads or open surgical implantation of paddle electrodes, each present with some limitations, highlighting a need for innovation in surgical approach.

For instance, cylindrical wire electrodes have the advantage of being implanted percutaneously, but are more prone to migration, apply stimulation both toward and away from the neuraxis, and may not follow the unique anatomy of the TNC.5,6 On the other hand, although paddle electrodes are less likely to migrate and allow for broader lateral coverage to capture the TNC, placement requires an open surgery, with more postoperative pain, disruption of posterior structural elements, and perioperative complications.7 To address these limitations, we expand the use of the tubular retractor system as a minimally invasive (MIS) technique for paddle electrode implantation at the TNC, preserving the advantages of paddle leads while enabling precise placement through a small, muscle-sparing corridor.

Illustrative Case

An 81-year-old man presented with a 2-year history of constant, dull, and burning left V1/V2 pain following an episode of herpes zoster ophthalmicus. The severe pain was unremitting and aggravated by light touch. He was treated with antiviral medications, gabapentin (causing hallucinations), and Lyrica (causing suicidal ideation). Botox injections, amitriptyline, opioids, supraorbital nerve blocks, and Gamma Knife radiosurgery targeting the trigeminal nerve ganglion did not yield significant or durable relief. He denied numbness or sensory loss after radiosurgery.

His medical history is significant for hypertension, obstructive sleep apnea (unable to tolerate CPAP [continuous positive airway pressurecontinuous positive airway pressure]continuous positive airway pressurecontinuous positive airway pressure due to facial pain), coronary artery disease, and prior lumbar fusion. He lived independently with his spouse and was functionally limited due to persistent pain. Examination was notable for allodynia and hyperesthesia in the left V1/V2 distribution without motor reflex abnormalities.

Visual analog scale (VAS) pain scores were persistently 9/10 prior to intervention. Preoperative imaging did not reveal structural lesions or ongoing inflammation. On psychological evaluation, he was cleared for neuromodulation. His diagnosis is consistent with trigeminal neuropathic pain (type B per the Burchiel classification) with a postherpetic etiology.

Procedure

After general endotracheal intubation, the Mayfield head clamp is applied in the same manner as it is for standard craniocervical junction cases prior to flipping the patient prone, to allow for careful flexion and posterior translation at the craniocervical junction, to facilitate an approach to the C1 arch that is approximately 45° from the horizontal.

The procedure is performed in the midline through the avascular nuchal plane. Lateral fluoroscopy is used to identify an appropriate trajectory (approximately 45° from horizontal) to the C1 arch, and the 2.5-cm skin incision is centered on this midline trajectory. The skin incision is extended into the nuchal fascia, so that a Penfield No. 1 can be used to palpate the occiput, followed by gently walking the instrument down toward the foramen magnum. The arch of C1 is palpated, identified, and visually confirmed under fluoroscopic control, with the posterior atlanto-occipital membrane left intact to protect the cord. Skipping the K-wire, the initial dilator is carefully advanced to the caudal portion of the occiput under direct visualization and confirmed with fluoroscopy. The angle of approach here is closer to perpendicular to allow for safe dilation of tubes over the caudal occiput without risking plunging toward the cord. Once the 22-mm-diameter tubular retractor is in place over the caudal occiput, the trajectory of the retractor is gently pivoted caudally toward the arch of C1 (Fig. 2), taking care not to push the retractor downward, and is fixed in place with the retractor holder. The key here is to make small careful movements under fluoroscopic control, and the final result is that the tubular retractor should float just over the arch of C1 without placing any significant downward force on it at any point. The angle here should be approximately 45° from the horizontal to facilitate advancement of the paddle electrode into the epidural space. Under the microscope, the midportion of the arch of C1 is identified and palpated with a Penfield No. 1 and carefully exposed, avoiding going beyond 10 mm laterally on either side of the midline to prevent encountering the vertebral arteries. The posterior atlanto-occipital membrane is carefully dissected off the rostral aspect of the C1 arch and opened in the midline to expose the epidural space. Kerrison rongeurs can be used to remove a small amount of bone from the rostral aspect of the C1 arch to widen this opening into the epidural space. A Penfield No. 3 is then gently inserted into this space, again being careful not to exert any downward force into the spinal canal, to ensure it is open and free of ligament or adhesions before advancing the paddle electrode into the space. The paddle electrode should be inserted into the epidural space (Fig. 3) without any resistance, and care must be taken to apply the bayoneted forceps distally on the paddle electrode to control its insertion into the epidural space and ensure it does not buckle at any point during this step. Anterior-posterior and lateral fluoroscopy are used to confirm midline electrode positioning, typically from the C2 lamina to the occiput. Placement of the paddle electrode is confirmed on the lateral fluoroscopic view (Fig. 4). Intraoperative monitoring (somatosensory evoked potentials and motor evoked potentials) can be used to monitor long tract integrity during the procedure. Ideal positioning was confirmed via anterior-posterior and lateral fluoroscopy with contacts centered over the superolateral cervical cord. A dog bone plate affixed to the occiput was used to anchor the paddle (Fig. 4). In subsequent patients, we have begun anchoring the paddle using the silicone anchoring sleeve that comes with the device and suturing it to the paraspinal fascia in order to minimize the risk of fracturing the lead. The electrodes were tunneled down to the flank region, where they were connected to temporary percutaneous extension leads exiting out the contralateral flank for the 2-week stimulation trial. Postoperative Course

No postoperative complications were observed, and the patient was discharged home later the same day. Trial stimulation was initiated postoperatively and maintained for 2 weeks. The patient reported a reduction in VAS score from 9/10 to 2/10 with marked improvement in allodynia, facial sensitivity, and sleep. He elected to proceed with permanent implantation, and the 1-month follow-up visit demonstrated the durability of efficacy.

Discussion

Observations

This case report describes the first reported application of an MIS tubular retractor system for paddle electrode implantation specifically targeting the TNC. While MIS tubular retractor systems for SCS paddle leads have been previously described at cervical and thoracic levels, our case uniquely adapts these principles to the craniocervical junction to stimulate the TNC. This adaptation leverages the natural corridor between the occiput and C1, typically requiring minimal bone drilling due to the available anatomical space, but this approach might be limited in patients with a small space between the occiput and C1 arch.

Facial pain syndromes with neuropathic origin such as postherpetic neuralgia are notoriously difficult to treat. Different interventional approaches have been described to address postherpetic neuralgia. Peripheral nerve stimulation targeting the supraorbital or infraorbital nerves has shown promise in select cases but is limited in coverage and unsuitable in patients without residual sensation.8–11 Central neuromodulation approaches including motor cortex stimulation have also been explored but require craniotomy and risk seizure and surgical morbidity.12–16

The TNC represents a convergence zone for trigeminal nociceptive input and is a promising target for neuromodulation. Compared with destructive lesioning techniques such as dorsal root entry zone lesioning or TNC lesioning, TNC stimulation offers reversibility, programmability, and reduced procedural morbidity.2,17–19 In a single-center series, patients with refractory trigeminal neuropathy experienced a median pain reduction of 57.1% following upper cervical SCS, with coverage of the pain zone averaging 72%.3 Reviews of neuromodulation for trigeminal neuropathic pain further support the role of SCS and other device-based therapies as promising options for refractory facial pain, although they also highlight the need for more robust clinical evidence.4 Therefore, neuromodulation of the TNC has emerged as a promising treatment for refractory craniofacial pain syndromes, particularly in patients who have failed conservative and pharmacological therapies.1 The TNC serves as the primary relay for nociceptive input from the trigeminal nerve and extends caudally into the upper cervical spinal cord, allowing for dorsal column access through a cervical approach.20 The stimulation of TNC with a cylindrical percutaneous lead is limited due to its narrow surface coverage and risk of migration, which has been reported to be as high as 11.7%.6,21 Therefore, the paddle electrodes are preferred for neuromodulation in this scenario, as they offer broader lateral coverage to accommodate the specific anatomy of the TNC.6,22 However, their placement requires an open surgical approach, which is accompanied by significant paraspinal muscle dissection, increased operative time, blood loss, and risk of spinal instability, particularly at the upper cervical levels.23

To overcome such surgical morbidities, the tubular retractor system has been used for SCS as an MIS technique. Conventional spine procedures using MIS tubular retractor systems preserve musculature, reduce intraoperative blood loss, minimize postoperative pain, and support early discharge home. Patients who underwent MIS spinal cord paddle lead implantation experienced less perioperative back pain, shorter procedure durations, and had earlier initiation of neurostimulation trials. Lead placement remained precise and complication rates did not rise.24,25 By adapting this approach for TNC stimulation, we demonstrate that it is possible to achieve precise paddle placement with significantly reduced morbidity. Our approach shares core principles with established MIS cervical decompression and paddle lead implantation techniques, including sequential dilation, tubular access, and minimal soft tissue disruption.26 By applying these principles to the craniocervical junction, this technique seeks to preserve musculature, reduce perioperative pain, and broaden access to paddle-based neuromodulation for medically complex patients. However, it should be viewed as a proof of concept requiring validation in larger, prospective studies.

Our experience supports the anatomical feasibility and procedural safety of this method. The patient tolerated the procedure well, experienced reduced postoperative pain, and achieved pain control during trial stimulation. These findings suggest that MIS-assisted paddle electrode implantation is a viable alternative to open laminectomy for neuromodulation of the TNC. Importantly, the use of a tubular retractor system for lead implantation for TNC stimulation may broaden the eligibility of patients for paddle-based trials, especially in those with comorbidities or anatomical constraints that contraindicate open surgery. This approach may also offer a platform for broader adoption of TNC-targeted neuromodulation in clinical practice. Given the increasing interest in neuromodulation for craniofacial pain, further studies are warranted to evaluate long-term outcomes and comparative efficacy against traditional wire electrodes.

Limitations

This report describes a single-patient experience with application of an MIS tubular retractor system for paddle electrode implantation at the TNC. As such, its findings are preliminary and hypothesis generating rather than conclusive. The absence of a control group precludes definitive comparisons of procedural morbidity or efficacy relative to open laminectomy or other approaches. Our 1-month follow-up is insufficient to assess long-term outcomes such as lead migration, fracture, hardware failure, or durability of pain relief. Patient-specific anatomical factors, including a congenitally small or bifid posterior arch of C1, congenital defect of the posterior arch of C1, or prior instrumentation, may also limit generalizability and technical feasibility. Furthermore, the use of VAS alone provides only a unidimensional measure of pain intensity; future investigations should use validated multidimensional scales and quality of life indices to better characterize facial pain outcomes. Prospective studies with larger cohorts and extended follow-up are necessary to evaluate the long-term safety, stability, and efficacy of this approach.

Lessons

Craniocervical neuromodulation targeting the TNC via a tubular retractor system presents a promising MIS technique for refractory craniofacial neuropathic pain. Our case demonstrates that it is a potentially safe and effective alternative to open or ablative procedures. This is especially evident in medically complex patients with prior treatment failures. We evaluated our single patient over a relatively short follow-up period. The anatomy of the TNC is such that the center of the face corresponds to the most rostral aspect of the TNC, which is typically the most difficult to achieve good coverage with neuromodulation. Thus, patients with pain focused exclusively in the center of their face may be the most difficult patients to treat using this approach. Therefore, further prospective studies are warranted to evaluate long-term efficacy, stimulation parameters, and patient selection criteria.
