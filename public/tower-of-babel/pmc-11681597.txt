Effect of Combination Antihypertensive Pills on Blood Pressure Control
Authors: Joseph Heaton, Anmol Johal, Abbas Alshami, Alexis Okoh, Ndausung Udongwo, Matthew Schoenfeld, Matthew Saybolt, Jesus Almendral, Brett Sealove
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypertension is a global health issue causing increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. Adherence to prescribed medication is a crucial factor in obtaining targeted outcomes, and fixed‐dose combined antihypertensive pills (FCAPs) have been shown to help combat difficulties associated with polypharmacy management. This study investigated the influence of combination antihypertensives on blood pressure (BP) management.Methods and ResultsData from the 2013 to 2020 NHANES (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey) were analyzed. Participants were included if between 18 and 79 years of age and were prescribed 2 antihypertensive classes. BP was deemed controlled if <140/<90 mm Hg. Examiner documentation and questionnaire data determined prescription antihypertensive medication usage and the presence of an FCAP. Descriptive statistics and multivariate regression analyses were used to compare the 2 groups. Subgroup analysis was performed for stricter BP goals of <130/<80 mm Hg. A total of 15 927 747 weighted participants met the inclusion criteria, 32.7% of whom were undergoing management with an FCAP. Participants with an FCAP were 1.78 (95% CI, 1.28–2.47, P=0.001) times more likely to have controlled BP (76.4% versus 67.3%) than those without an FCAP. Subgroup analysis revealed that FCAPs were associated with stricter BP goals (odds ratio [OR], 1.65, P=0.008; 87.6% versus 71.2%) compared with those without.ConclusionsParticipants with an FCAP were more likely to exhibit controlled BP, including participants with clinical atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and those targeting stricter control. Clinicians can immediately and meaningfully affect their patient's BP by opting for FCAPs.

Hypertension is a widespread health concern marked by consistently high blood pressure (BP) linked to an increased risk of heart attacks, strokes, and organ failure. Effective management of hypertension has been shown to decrease overall mortality and cardiovascular‐related deaths. 1 , 2 The American College of Cardiology and the American Heart Association 2017 guidelines emphasized the importance of preventing, detecting, evaluating, and managing hypertension in adults. 3 These guidelines introduced updated BP targets to reduce morbidity and mortality further compared with previous recommendations. 4

Despite significant efforts from health care providers, achieving optimal targets in hypertension management remains below par. 5 , 6 The World Health Organization has identified 5 main reasons for suboptimal adherence: socioeconomic factors, patient‐related issues, therapy‐related challenges, condition‐related aspects, and health care‐related barriers. 7 Poor compliance and early treatment discontinuation have been identified as therapy‐related challenges. 8 Up to 70% of patients with hypertension fail to reach their BP goals with a single medication, 9 and for certain individuals, initiation of 2‐drug combinations may be more suitable. 10 Consequently, barriers to adherence increase as patients are often managed with 2 or more antihypertensive medications. 11 , 12

Combination pills have shown promise in improving adherence, including those combining multiple antihypertensives and an antihypertensive with a nonantihypertensive medication. 13 , 14 However, data on their effectiveness remain constrained by limitations from single‐regimen trials and retrospective analyses restricted to specific populations. 15 , 16 , 17 The impact of all regimen combinations on varying BP targets within a nationally representative US population remains underexplored. 18 Our study aims to investigate the influence of all‐formulation fixed‐dose combination antihypertensive pills (FCAPs) on achieving target BP goals in a US nationally representative sample.

METHODS

We used the 2013 to 2020 prepandemic NHANES (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey), a nationally representative survey of noninstitutionalized US citizens. NHANES employs stratified, multistage probability sampling to identify individuals from the population of the United States who reflect the diversity and characteristics of the broader population. This method provides the US nationally representative prevalence estimates from a limited sample of observations when properly weighted. The 2013 to 2020 prepandemic survey used in this study is a data set of 35 706 individuals, representing a population of 317 612 204 with weighting.

We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guideline for cohort studies. All questionnaire data, surveyor techniques, physical exam, laboratory, and analytic guidelines are publicly available from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's National Center for Health Statistics website at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm and Akinbami et al. 20 As NHANES data are publicly available, individual consent for this specific analysis was not required, and the research was exempt from institutional review board approval.

Participants were selected for inclusion if they were between the ages of 18 and 79 years, reported taking medication for hypertension, and were prescribed 2 classes of antihypertensive medications. Participants were excluded if they had 3 or more classes present to prevent underpowered subgroup analysis. 19 Participants were excluded if any of the following were present: pregnant or unable to determine pregnancy status; missing demographics (age, sex, race), laboratory (cholesterol, hemoglobin A1c, renal function), physical exam (systolic or diastolic BP, height, weight), or relevant questionnaire data (prescribed antihypertensive medication, smoking status); either a single or ≥3 antihypertensive classes identified in provided medications. The presence of an FCAP then stratified qualifying participants.

BP was determined using the mean of at least 3 measurements provided. Participants provided medications to surveyors, who personally identified and logged the prescriptions. Antihypertensive medications were identified as such if listed in the American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology's 2017 guidelines. 3 FCAPs were identified if a single pill was listed with 2 antihypertensive medications from the 2017 guidelines. Clinical atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) was identified if participants reported a previous myocardial infarction, stroke, or known coronary artery disease.

The primary analysis was predictors of BP control, defined as an average BP <140/90 mm Hg. Secondary analysis was conducted for predictors of BP control in individual cohorts of participants with clinical ASCVD, the influence on stricter BP control, defined as an average BP <130/80 mm Hg, and factors influencing an FCAP prescription. Confounding factors accounted for included age, race, sex, the presence of chronic kidney disease, diabetes, smoking cigarettes, familial history of heart disease, the overuse of alcohol, activity level, body mass index, average hours of sleep, poverty level, educational achievement, marital status, primary insurance, quantity of health care visits in the past year, total medications taking, and the survey cycle.

Statistical Analysis

We present frequency and proportions for demographic and clinical characteristics of US adults in each group. Multivariate logistic regression was used to identify odds ratios (ORs) for predictors of controlled BP. Complex survey methodology with sample weights was used in accordance with NHANES analytic guidelines to attain US national representative prevalence estimates. Data management and analysis were conducted using Stata version 18 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX). Missingness of variables was assessed, and zero participants who reported taking hypertension medications were missing antihypertensive prescriptions upon interviewer inspection.

RESULTS

Clinical ASCVD was present in 2 721 445 (unweighted n=277) participants, of whom 12.8% (349 552 weighted, 27 unweighted) were prescribed an FCAP. Target BP was achieved in 87.6% with an FCAP versus 71.2% without an FCAP. Logistic regression revealed FCAPs were associated with a 3.82 (95% CI, 1.09–13.34, P=0.036) times higher odds of achieving goal BP compared with those without an FCAP (Table S2).

Strict BP control of 130/80 mm Hg was noted in 50.2% of participants with an FCAP and 41.9% of those without an FCAP. Logistic regression revealed FCAPs were associated with a 1.65 (95% CI, 1.15–2.37, P=0.008) times higher odds of achieving strict BP control compared with those without an FCAP (Table S3).

FCAPs were more likely to be prescribed in participants with diabetes (OR, 2.77 [95% CI, 1.14–6.70], P=0.025) and less likely in Black participants (OR, 0.36 [95% CI, 0.15–0.87] P=0.24) and with an increasing number of total prescriptions (OR, 0.57 [95% CI, 0.47–0.69], P<0.001). FCAP prescriptions were not associated with income, insurance, or education (P>0.05) (Table S4). Formulation frequencies for FCAP and free combinations are presented in Table S5.

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the first study to analyze the influence of dual class all‐formulation FCAPs on hypertension management, including new stricter goals, in a nationally representative population. An FCAP was associated with significantly higher rates of controlled BP, accounting for differences in age, sex, insurance coverage, poverty level, and common clinical factors. FCAPs were also more associated with BP control in higher‐risk cohorts with clinical ASCVD and at stricter BP goals. Notably, there was no statistical difference in age groups, sex, race, or access to health care between those with and without FPACs. A significant difference was noted in the prevalence of FCAP prescriptions between poverty levels, though regression analysis showed no significant association between issuance of a prescription among poverty levels. Clinical ASCVD was more prevalent in those without an FPAC.

Recent estimates from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention have shown only 1 in 4 adults with hypertension has controlled BP. 21 Two antihypertensives have shown better control and may be beneficial for initiation in qualifying patients. 3 , 10 Our study showed that 70.3% of participants had controlled BP on 2 antihypertensives. However, there was a significant discrepancy associated with the use of an FCAP, as the combination pill was associated with 9.1% more (76.4% versus 67.3%) participants achieving target BP. Despite multiple studies showing increased control, 16 , 22 including ours, only about one third of participants were prescribed an FCAP. FCAPs were also associated with the achievement of target BP in patients with clinical ASCVD and in all patients targeting a stricter BP goal. The greater influence of FCAPs in these higher‐risk subgroups may be related to reducing pill burden in participants with significantly higher quantities of prescribed medications. 23

FCAPs were more common in participants with higher incomes. However, regression analysis revealed that FCAP prescriptions were not statistically associated with varying poverty levels. Combination pills are typically cost equivalent to their solo counterparts 19 unless recently brought to market, 22 and may be associated with lower costs compared with purchasing separate pills. 24 , 25 Furthermore, FCAPs are associated with increased quality‐adjusted life‐years and may help reduce the future health care cost burden. 26

Numerous obstacles hinder the attainment of controlled BP, spanning from intricate lifestyle adjustments to rectifying systemic health care inequalities, presenting challenges for both clinicians and patients. Alternative nonpharmacologic interventions, such as renal denervation, can help lower BP as an adjuvant antihypertensive therapy. 27 However, FCAPs offer a simple, noninvasive option without the costs or complications, and were associated with a lower systolic BP in our study. This presents clinicians with a pivotal opportunity to safely foster substantial improvements in their patient's health by advocating for FCAP prescriptions. By opting for this approach, patients may consume fewer pills, witness enhanced outcomes, and potentially decrease future health care expenditures.

The benefits of FCAPs are not limited to current practice, though. The success of the polypill, a combination of aspirin, statin, and ACE‐inhibitor, has been documented across the globe, especially in poverty‐stricken and underserved areas. 15 , 28 FCAPs can further support this success by providing multiple additional medications without significantly increasing pill burden. True success, however, would be achieved by augmenting the polypill with nonoverlapping FPACs to create a single pill with 2 or more antihypertensive medications. The benefits of these derived ASCVD super pills could summate the success of polypills and FPACs and potentially exhibit a compound effect.

Limitations

This study is limited by its retrospective nature and all inherent limitations of the NHANES database. Establishing a patient's mean BP is a constant process; thus, assessing BP at 2 or more visits and considering ambulatory BP monitoring is recommended. The effect of combination pills versus free pill combinations on BP management could not be examined due to the nature of cross‐sectional sampling. This study examined FCAPs only if they contained 2 antihypertensive medications, excluding combinations of antihypertensives with nonantihypertensive treatments such as statins. Participants with heart failure on multiple guideline‐directed medical therapies were inherently excluded. Antihypertensive medications not listed in the 2017 guidelines, including timolol, reserpine, ethacrynic acid, bendroflumethiazide, chlorothiazide, hydroflumethiazide, methyclothiazide, and trichlormethiazide, were excluded from the analysis, as they were present in <0.5% of the sampled population.

CONCLUSIONS

In this first nationally representative survey of all‐regimen fixed‐dose combination antihypertensive pills, the FCAP has shown significant associations with the achievement of controlled BP. These improvements were associated regardless of pill formulation and were consistently associated with increased BP control in high‐risk populations. Clinicians can make an immediate and meaningful impact on their patient's BP control by opting for FCAPs when available. The future holds an opportunity to combine polypills and FPACs to create an all‐in‐one solution, compounding their success to achieve a significantly reduced global ASCVD burden.
