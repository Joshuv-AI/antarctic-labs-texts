Quantifying robustness and locality of Majorana bound states in interacting systems
William Samuelson, Juan Daniel Torres Luna, Sebastian Miles, A. Mert Bozkurt, Martin Leijnse, Michael Wimmer, Viktor Svensson

Abstract
Protecting qubits from perturbations is a central challenge in quantum computing. Topological superconductors with separated Majorana bound states (MBSs) provide a strong form of protection that only depends on the locality of perturbations. While the link between MBS separation, robust degeneracy, and protected braiding is well understood in non-interacting systems, recent experimental progress in short quantum-dot-based Kitaev chains highlights the need to establish these connections rigorously for interacting systems. We do this by defining MBSs from many-body ground states and show how their locality constrains their coupling to an environment. This, in turn, quantifies the protection of the energy degeneracy and the feasibility of non-abelian braiding.

Introduction

Robust degeneracies in quantum systems are of fundamental importance and offer potential for quantum technologies.
Topological protection provides a particularly strong form of robustness, where the degeneracy is insensitive to local perturbations.
Topological superconductors realize this protection as a result of separated Majorana bound states (MBSs): quasiparticles with non-abelian exchange statistics and potential for fault-tolerant quantum computation

In non-interacting models, the spatial separation of MBSs implies both protected braiding protocols and robustness of the ground-state degeneracy against local perturbations.
Extensions of these concepts to interacting systems include the classification of topological phases

II
MBSs in interacting systems

In non-interacting systems, single-particle MBSs are obtained by diagonalizing the Bogoliubov-de-Gennes Hamiltonian. These operators act on the full Hilbert space. However, in adiabatic braiding protocols, only the low-energy ground-state sector is relevant. Motivated by this fact, we consider a fermionic system
S
with two low-energy states of opposite fermion parity,
|e\rangle
and
|o\rangle
, separated from all excited states by an energy gap.

As an operator basis for this space, we define

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=|e\rangle\langle o|+|o\rangle\langle e|,

(1a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}

\displaystyle=i(|e\rangle\langle o|-|o\rangle\langle e|),

(1b)

\displaystyle i\gamma\tilde{\gamma}

\displaystyle=|e\rangle\langle e|-|o\rangle\langle o|,

(1c)

which are analogous to the Pauli operators. They are hermitian and square to identity in the space of ground states

\displaystyle\gamma^{2}=\tilde{\gamma}^{2}=|e\rangle\langle e|+|o\rangle\langle o|.

(2)

A crucial difference with spin operators is that
\gamma
and
\tilde{\gamma}
are fermionic. We refer to them as ground-state MBSs, and their product as their parity. These operators describe the low-energy physics of
S
, and can be defined in both interacting and non-interacting systems.

The ground-state MBSs are ’weak’ in the sense that they only map between the opposite parity states in the ground-state sector
Alicea and Fendley 2016
;
Wada et al. 2021
. This is in contrast to ’strong’ MBSs, which map between the parity sectors in the full Hilbert space
Fendley 2016
;
Kells 2015a
;
Kells 2015b
;
Kells et al. 2018
;
Behrends et al. 2019
;
Chepiga and Laflorencie 2023
;
Bozkurt et al. 2025
. Whether or not a strong MBS exists is irrelevant here—our focus is solely on the ground-state sector.

Unlike single-particle MBSs,
\gamma
and
\tilde{\gamma}
are non-local and generally act non-trivially on the entire system. Nevertheless, when coupling to them in a region
R
, only the local part plays a role.
This is captured by the fermionic partial trace, which defines the reduced MBSs in region
R
as

\displaystyle\gamma_{R}=\mathrm{Tr}_{\overline{R}}[\gamma],

(3)

where
\bar{R}
is the complement of the region
R
. For fermionic systems, partial traces and tensor products need to be slightly modified to ensure consistency with the anti-commutation relations, and we follow Ref.
Szalay et al. 2021
closely. These technicalities are not important for the conceptual understanding of our results, so we have saved them for the proofs in the Appendix.

Due to fermionic superselection, the relative phase between
|e\rangle
and
|o\rangle
is an unphysical gauge freedom. A rotation of this phase corresponds to mixing the two ground-state MBSs, and it is important to choose the phase to maximize the separation of
\gamma
and
\tilde{\gamma}
in the coupling region
R
. This leads to a many-body generalization of the Majorana polarization, see
Section

V
.

III
Bounding couplings by locality

To see how the localization of the ground-state MBSs implies protection from perturbations, we couple the system
S
to an environment
B
. The full Hamiltonian reads

H=H_{S}+H_{RB}+H_{B},

(4)

where
H_{RB}
is assumed to act only in a subregion
R
of
S
and
H_{B}
is arbitrary. The coupling can be split into odd and even terms,

H_{RB}=H_{RB}^{e}+H_{RB}^{o}.

(5)

H_{RB}^{e}
has terms with even fermion parity in both
R
and
B
, such as local chemical potential shifts within
R
or Coulomb interaction between
R
and
B
, while
H_{RB}^{o}
collects terms with odd parity in both
R
and
B
, such as single-particle hopping between them.

Projecting out the excited states in
S
, we can write an effective Hamiltonian in terms of the operators in
Eq.

1
. It takes the form

H_{\text{eff}}=(\varepsilon+G)i\gamma\tilde{\gamma}/2+\gamma F/2+\tilde{\gamma}\tilde{F}/2+H_{B},

(6)

where
F,\tilde{F}
, and
G
are operators acting in the environment
1
1

1

To simplify notation in the main text, we do not write explicitly on which space operators act. The convention we use is that it acts on the smallest space possible allowed in the context. When two operators in different spaces are multiplied or added, they are implicitly extended to a joint space by a fermionic tensor product.
, and
\varepsilon
is the energy difference between the low-energy states of
H_{S}
. To relate the effective operators to the full Hamiltonian, note that
F=\Tr_{S}[\gamma H_{\text{eff}}]
, and since
\gamma
projects to the ground-state sector, we can replace
H_{\text{eff}}
with
H
.

Therefore, the effective operators can be expressed as

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=\Tr_{S}{\left[\gamma H_{RB}^{o}\right]}=\Tr_{R}{\left[\gamma_{R}H_{RB}^{o}\right]},

(7a)

\displaystyle\tilde{F}

\displaystyle=\Tr_{S}{\left[\tilde{\gamma}H_{RB}^{o}\right]}=\Tr_{R}{\left[\tilde{\gamma}_{R}H_{RB}^{o}\right]},

(7b)

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=\Tr_{S}{\left[i\gamma\tilde{\gamma}H_{RB}^{e}\right]}=\Tr_{R}{\left[(i\gamma\tilde{\gamma})_{R}H_{RB}^{e}\right]},

(7c)

where
\gamma_{R}
is the reduced ground-state MBS defined in
Eq.

3
.
If the norms of the reduced MBSs are small, the effective operators will be small.
In
Appendix

A
, we prove that

\displaystyle\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert H_{RB}^{o}\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert\gamma_{R}\right\rVert_{q},

(8a)

\displaystyle\lVert{\tilde{F}\rVert}_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert H_{RB}^{o}\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert\tilde{\gamma}_{R}\right\rVert_{q},

(8b)

\displaystyle\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert H_{RB}^{e}\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert(i\gamma\tilde{\gamma})_{R}\right\rVert_{q},

(8c)

where
1/q+1/p=1
, and we use the Schatten norm defined by

\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{p}=\Tr\left[\sqrt{A^{\dagger}A}^{p}\right]^{1/p}.

(9)

These bounds are sharp. For example, for
q=2
(the Frobenius norm), the bound for
F
is saturated if

H_{RB}^{o}=\gamma_{R}h_{B},

(10)

for any operator
h_{B}
in the environment.

These bounds constrain how much an environment can couple to a pair of MBSs and their parity.
We use these to derive bounds on the energy spectrum in
Section

IV
and discuss consequences for braiding in
Section

VII
.

IV
Energy splitting bound

A useful property of MBSs is that the energy spectrum is protected if they commute with a perturbation. We derive an inequality connecting the protection to the localization of the ground-state MBSs, see
Appendix

B
for all the details. While the case of most interest is when
S
is degenerate (
\varepsilon=0
), we formulate the more general result with non-zero initial splitting
\varepsilon
. These bounds only hold in the low-energy subspace—they can be violated if the coupling is comparable to the excitation gap in
S
.

Let
|\mathcal{O}\rangle
and
|\mathcal{E}\rangle
be eigenstates of
H_{\text{eff}}
, with odd and even parity, respectively. Their energy difference is

\delta E=\langle\mathcal{E}|H_{\text{eff}}|\mathcal{E}\rangle-\langle\mathcal{O}|H_{\text{eff}}|\mathcal{O}\rangle=\frac{\langle\mathcal{O}|[\gamma,H_{\text{eff}}]|\mathcal{E}\rangle}{\langle\mathcal{O}|\gamma|\mathcal{E}\rangle}.

(11)

The commutator of the effective Hamiltonian and the ground-state MBS
\gamma
is

[\gamma,H_{\text{eff}}]=\varepsilon i\tilde{\gamma}+i\tilde{\gamma}G+F.

(12)

Using
Eqs.

8
,
11
and
12
, we obtain

|\delta E\langle\mathcal{O}|\gamma|\mathcal{E}\rangle-\varepsilon\langle\mathcal{O}|i\tilde{\gamma}|\mathcal{E}\rangle|\leq\left\lVert(i\gamma\tilde{\gamma})_{R}\right\rVert_{q}\left\lVert H_{RB}^{e}\right\rVert_{p}\\
+\left\lVert\gamma_{R}\right\rVert_{q}\left\lVert H_{RB}^{o}\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert(|\mathcal{E}\rangle\langle\mathcal{O}|)_{B}\right\rVert_{q}.

(13)

The operator
|\mathcal{E}\rangle\langle\mathcal{O}|
can be thought of as a fermion that switches between the two states, and
\left\lVert(|\mathcal{E}\rangle\langle\mathcal{O}|)_{B}\right\rVert
quantifies how much of that fermion is contained in the environment.

We can specify additional bounds on the three quantities
|\langle\mathcal{O}|\gamma|\mathcal{E}\rangle|,|\langle\mathcal{O}|i\tilde{\gamma}|\mathcal{E}\rangle|
and
\left\lVert(|\mathcal{E}\rangle\langle\mathcal{O}|)_{B}\right\rVert_{q}
.
In
Appendix

B
, we derive several bounds applicable in different regimes set by the ratios of the energy scales
\varepsilon
,
\left\lVert H_{RB}\right\rVert
, and the level spacings in
B
.

Here, we assume that
\left\lVert H_{RB}\right\rVert
may be larger than
\varepsilon
and any level spacings in
B
, but still smaller than the excitation gap in
S
. If there are
d_{B}
states in
B
, we find the result:
For any eigenstate of
H_{\text{eff}}
, there exists another eigenstate with opposite parity such that their energy difference is bounded by

|\delta E|\leq\sqrt{d_{B}}(\left\lVert(i\gamma\tilde{\gamma})_{R}\right\rVert_{q}\left\lVert H_{RB}^{e}\right\rVert_{p}+\left\lVert\gamma_{R}\right\rVert_{q}\left\lVert H_{RB}^{o}\right\rVert_{p}+|\varepsilon|).

(14)

There is a straightforward generalization to the case where the coupling is written as a sum of terms

H_{c}=\sum_{n}H_{R_{n}B}

(15)

acting in different regions
R_{n}
, see
Appendix

E
. While the above bound can be used with
R=\bigcup_{n}R_{n}
, a more fine-grained bound is

|\delta E|\leq\sqrt{d_{B}}\left(Q_{e}\left\lVert H_{c}^{e}\right\rVert_{2}+Q_{o}\left\lVert H_{c}^{o}\right\rVert_{2}+|\varepsilon|\right),

(16)

where

\displaystyle Q_{o}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{n}\left\lVert\gamma_{R_{n}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}},

(17a)

\displaystyle Q_{e}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{n}\left\lVert(i\gamma\tilde{\gamma})_{R_{n}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}},

(17b)

which quantify protection from odd and even couplings, respectively.

V
Quality measures, gauge choice and Majorana polarization

Here, we consider how the measures depend on the relative phase between
|e\rangle
and
|o\rangle
, which is a gauge freedom due to superselection. The gauge rotates the ground-state MBSs into each other, but their parity,
i\gamma\tilde{\gamma}
, is invariant. The local parity can be written in terms of the reduced density matrices of the odd (
\rho_{R}^{o}
) and even (
\rho_{R}^{e}
) ground states as

\left\lVert(i\gamma\tilde{\gamma})_{R}\right\rVert_{q}=\left\lVert\rho_{R}^{e}-\rho_{R}^{o}\right\rVert_{q},

(18)

and thus measures the local distinguishability of the two ground states in
R
, as discussed in Ref.
Svensson and Leijnse 2024
.

The ground-state MBSs, the couplings
F
and
\tilde{F}
, and
Q_{o}
are gauge-dependent. Some gauges may give better bounds than others. To get the strongest bound in
Eq.

16
, we minimize
Q_{o}
.
Defining the ground-state fermion
c=(\gamma+i\tilde{\gamma})/\sqrt{2}
,
we find the minimum to be

\displaystyle\min_{\text{gauges}}\sum_{n}\left\lVert\gamma_{R_{n}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\sum_{n}\Tr[c^{\dagger}_{R_{n}}c_{R_{n}}]-\Bigl\lvert\sum_{n}\Tr[c_{R_{n}}c_{R_{n}}]\Bigr\rvert,

(19)

see
Appendix

F
for details.
The first term can be interpreted as a fermion density, and the second as a Majorana density. We will see that the ratio of these,

M=\frac{\Bigl\lvert\sum_{n}\Tr[c_{R_{n}}c_{R_{n}}]\Bigr\rvert}{\sum_{n}\Tr[c^{\dagger}_{R_{n}}c_{R_{n}}]},

(20)

is a generalization of the Majorana polarization, a commonly used measure of MBS quality. This quantity depends on the two ground states and on the set of regions
\{R_{n}\}
the coupling acts on.

The maximum value of
M
is 1, in which case
Q_{o}=0
and one can only couple to a single MBS in this set of subregions.
The phase of
\Tr[c_{R_{n}}c_{R_{n}}]
determines the optimal gauge for the subregion
R_{n}
. There is only one gauge parameter, so if it varies, we cannot optimize each term simultaneously, and the value of
M
decreases.

If
H_{S}
is real, the optimal gauge will either be when the eigenstates are real (
\gamma
is the smallest) or purely imaginary (
\tilde{\gamma}
is the smallest).

V.1
Comparison with existing quality measures

V.1.1
Interacting systems

Here, we consider a special case where the general expression in
Eq.

20
reduces to an expression often used to quantify MBS quality in interacting systems. Let
f_{j}
be a standard fermion and define the Majorana basis operators

\displaystyle\Gamma_{j}^{+}

\displaystyle=f^{\dagger}_{j}+f_{j},

(21a)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{j}^{-}

\displaystyle=i(f^{\dagger}_{j}-f_{j}).

(21b)

Consider an odd coupling which can be written as a sum over single-mode subregions
R_{j}
and let
R=\cup_{j}R_{j}
.
The coupling takes the form

H_{c}^{o}=\sum_{j\in R}t_{j}f_{j}^{\dagger}F_{j}+\text{h.c.},

(22)

where
F_{j}
is any odd operator in the environment. This corresponds to tunneling of
f
-particles in and out of the system. For single-mode subregions, the reduced MBSs are fully defined by the matrix elements
\langle o|\Gamma_{j}^{s}|e\rangle
, and
Eq.

20
becomes

M=\frac{\left|\sum_{s,j\in R}\langle o|\Gamma_{j}^{s}|e\rangle^{2}\right|}{\sum_{s,j\in R}|\langle o|\Gamma_{j}^{s}|e\rangle|^{2}},

(23)

which has been used before
Tsintzis et al. 2024
;
Aksenov et al. 2020
;
Tsintzis et al. 2022
;
Souto et al. 2023
;
Samuelson et al. 2024
;
Torres Luna et al. 2024
. This puts the use of
Eq.

23
on a firm footing as it bounds the energy splitting coming from the most physically relevant odd couplings. The only case where it does not apply is when the odd coupling has many-body terms in
S
. The single-mode subregions do not have to be purely spatial: in spinful systems, they refer to a single spin at one site.

V.1.2
Non-interacting systems

If the system is non-interacting,
one can derive bounds without projecting to the ground-state sector (see
Appendix

G
).
These bounds involve the single-particle MBSs
\Gamma
and
\tilde{\Gamma}
obtained by diagonalizing the BdG-Hamiltonian. Note that even in non-interacting systems, these are very different from the ground-state MBSs
\gamma
and
\tilde{\gamma}
, see
Appendix

H
for an extended discussion.

The derivation is analogous to the interacting case, yielding similar bounds for the couplings and energy splitting. In this case, we will use a single region
R
, because the single-particle structure will allow us to split the region into individual fermionic modes anyway. The quality measures are then
Q_{o}=\left\lVert\Gamma_{R}\right\rVert_{2}
, which bounds odd couplings, and
Q_{e}=\lVert(i\Gamma\tilde{\Gamma})_{R}\rVert_{2}
, which bounds the even couplings. There is an analogous gauge freedom here, corresponding to a Bogoliubov rotation of the two single-particle MBSs. Again,
Q_{o}
is gauge-dependent, but
Q_{e}
is not.
By optimizing the gauge, we find

\min_{\mathrm{gauges}}\left\lVert\Gamma_{R}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\Tr[C^{\dagger}_{R}C_{R}](1-M),

(24)

where
C=(\Gamma+i\tilde{\Gamma})/\sqrt{2}
and

M=\frac{\bigl\lvert\Tr[C_{R}C_{R}]\bigr\rvert}{\Tr[C^{\dagger}_{R}C_{R}]}

(25)

is the Majorana polarization from
Eq.

20
when using a single region
R
and the single-particle fermion
C
. In contrast to the interacting case, the single-particle structure of the MBSs can be used to simplify the local parity as

\left\lVert(i\Gamma\tilde{\Gamma})_{R}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\frac{\Tr[C^{\dagger}_{R}C_{R}]^{2}}{d_{R}d_{\bar{R}}^{2}}\left(1-M^{2}\right).

(26)

So in the non-interacting case, the Majorana polarization can be used to bound the energy splitting for both odd and even couplings.

We can rewrite
Eq.

25
in terms of the single-particle wavefunction. Writing the fermion as

C=\sum_{j}u_{j}f_{j}+v_{j}f_{j}^{\dagger},

(27)

and letting
\mathbf{u}_{R}
and
\mathbf{v}_{R}
be vectors containing the coefficients belonging to operators acting on the subregion
R
, the normalized Majorana polarization is

M=\frac{2|\mathbf{v}_{R}^{T}\mathbf{u}_{R}|}{\mathbf{u}_{R}^{\dagger}\mathbf{u}_{R}+\mathbf{v}_{R}^{\dagger}\mathbf{v}_{R}}.

(28)

Originally, the complex number
2v_{j}u_{j}
was named Majorana polarization
Sedlmayr and Bena 2015
;
Sedlmayr et al. 2016
;
Bena 2017
, where it was stressed that the variation of the phase implies lower quality MBSs. The same feature occurs here in the many-body generalization
\Tr[c_{R}c_{R}]
, where the phase variation means that the optimal gauge is different in different regions.

Equations

23
,
28
and
25
are equivalent in non-interacting systems.
Equation

23
can therefore be used to bound the energy splitting in both non-interacting and interacting systems, though for the latter only in a special (but physically relevant) case and only for the odd coupling.

VI
Example: interacting Kitaev chain

To illustrate our results, we take the interacting Kitaev chain
Gergs et al. 2016
;
Katsura et al. 2015
;
Miao et al. 2017
;
Kells et al. 2018

H_{K}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mu_{j}n_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}(tf_{j}^{\dagger}f_{j+1}+\Delta f_{j}^{\dagger}f_{j+1}^{\dagger}+\text{h.c.})+Un_{j}n_{j+1},

(29)

where
n_{j}=f_{j}^{\dagger}f_{j}
,
\mu_{j}
is the chemical potential at site
j
,
t
and
\Delta
are the hopping and pairing amplitudes (which we take to be real), and
U
is the nearest-neighbor Coulomb interaction.

First, we analyze
H_{K}
analytically at a fine-tuned sweet spot where MBSs are perfectly localized on the outermost sites. Second, we study the isolated chain away from the sweet spot and demonstrate the decay of the MBSs away from the edges. Third, we couple the chain to a quantum dot (the environment) and compare the bounds derived in
Section

IV
with the actual ground-state energy splitting.
We form ground-state MBSs using the real eigenstates.

VI.1
Ground-state MBSs at the sweet spot

At the point

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=t+U/2,

(30a)

\displaystyle\mu_{j}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-U,&1<j<N\\
-U/2,&j=1,N\end{cases},

(30b)

the ground states of
H_{K}
are degenerate and take the same form as the ground states in the non-interacting Kitaev chain (
U=0
) at
\mu_{j}=0,t=\Delta

Katsura et al. 2015
.
In the non-interacting limit, the standard, single-particle MBSs are localized at the edges. The situation is similar for the ground-state MBSs in the interacting case, although with additional subtleties.

As a concrete example, we take
N=3
. Then, in terms of the single-particle Majorana basis
\Gamma_{j}^{s}
defined in
Eq.

21
,
we find

\gamma=\Gamma_{1}^{+}(I-i\Gamma_{1}^{-}\Gamma_{2}^{+})(I-i\Gamma_{2}^{-}\Gamma_{3}^{+})/4.

(31)

This operator has support on the full system, but the partial traces to single sites are

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}

\displaystyle=\Tr_{\{2,3\}}[\gamma]=\Gamma_{1}^{+},

(32a)

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}

\displaystyle=\Tr_{\{1,3\}}[\gamma]=0,

(32b)

\displaystyle\gamma_{3}

\displaystyle=\Tr_{\{1,2\}}[\gamma]=0.

(32c)

The other ground-state MBS also has support on the full system, but its partial traces are nonzero only for regions that include the third site. Therefore, one can only couple to
\gamma
at the first site, and to
\tilde{\gamma}
on the third site. This example highlights that the localization of these operators is subtle. For further discussion of these points, see
Appendix

H
.

of the chemical potential. The exact sweet spot occurs along the whole
x
-axis where
\delta\mu=0
. In the dark region, the chain is robust against arbitrary perturbations and couplings acting on the left half of the system.

VI.3
Energy bounds when coupled to a quantum dot

To test the bounds on the energy splitting, we couple the end of the chain (
R=\{N\}
) to a spinless quantum dot, acting as the environment,
B
. Depending on the degree of MBS separation, the ground-state degeneracy splits when tuning the dot level to resonance
Prada et al. 2017
;
Clarke 2017
;
Deng et al. 2016
;
Souto et al. 2023
.
The idea is analogous to the steps leading to our bounds in
Section

IV
: the ground-state degeneracy splits if the dot couples to more than one MBS.
This protocol was recently tested experimentally in a quantum-dot-based Kitaev chain
Bordin et al. 2025b
.

The Hamiltonian of the coupled chain-dot system is

H=H_{K}+(t_{c}f_{N}f_{B}^{\dagger}+\Delta_{c}f_{N}f_{B}+\text{h.c.})+U_{c}n_{N}n_{B}+\varepsilon_{d}n_{B},

(33)

where
f_{B}
is the annihilation operator on the dot, which costs an energy
\varepsilon_{d}
to occupy. The coupling consists of tunneling (
t_{c}
), pairing (
\Delta_{c}
), and Coulomb interaction (
U_{c}
) between the dot and the end of the chain. Here, the
t_{c}
and
\Delta_{c}
terms correspond to the odd coupling
H_{RB}^{o}
and the
U_{c}
term provides an even coupling. However, parts of the
U_{c}
term act only in
B
(renormalizing
\varepsilon_{d}
), which we move to
H_{B}
to minimize the norm of the coupling. See
Appendix

D
for details on handling ambiguities of
H_{RB}
.

To parametrize
t_{c}
,
\Delta_{c}
, and
U_{c}
, we introduce an overall coupling amplitude
\lambda
and an angle
\phi
that tunes between tunneling- and pairing-dominated coupling. In terms of these parameters, we set

\displaystyle t_{c}

\displaystyle=\lambda\cos{\phi},

(34a)

\displaystyle\Delta_{c}

\displaystyle=\lambda\sin{\phi},

(34b)

\displaystyle U_{c}

\displaystyle=\lambda.

(34c)

This way, we can express
\left\lVert H_{RB}^{o}\right\rVert_{p}
and
\left\lVert H_{RB}^{e}\right\rVert_{p}
in terms of
\lambda
and
\phi
. In particular, for
q=p=2
, they only depend on
\lambda
. We find
\left\lVert H_{RB}^{e}\right\rVert_{2}=\lambda/\sqrt{2}
, and
\left\lVert H_{RB}^{o}\right\rVert_{2}=\sqrt{2}\lambda
.

\left\lVert H_{RB}^{e}\right\rVert_{2}
and
\left\lVert H_{RB}^{o}\right\rVert_{2}

(36)

where we used
\left\lVert(|\mathcal{E}\rangle\langle\mathcal{O}|)_{B}\right\rVert_{2}\leq 1
and
|\langle\mathcal{O}|\gamma|\mathcal{E}\rangle|\geq 1/\sqrt{2}
. We select
|\mathcal{E}\rangle
and
|\mathcal{O}\rangle
as the ground states of the coupled system.

In

We have projected

VII
Braiding ground-state MBSs

By coupling several systems with MBSs, see
Fig.

4
, one can perform coupling-based braiding protocols
Alicea et al. 2011
;
Sau et al. 2011
;
Beenakker 2020
to realize topological quantum computation
Nayak et al. 2008
. In such protocols, certain couplings between the MBSs must remain small to suppress errors
Tsintzis et al. 2024
;
Nitsch et al. 2025
;
Miles et al. 2025
, while others must be large to enable the exchanges. In this section, we use our methods to generalize these statements to an interacting setting. We couple three interacting systems and derive an effective Hamiltonian in terms of ground-state MBSs. We then show how the locality of the MBSs bounds unwanted couplings in the braiding protocol.

Figure 4:

We put bounds on the undesired couplings and specify how to maximize the desired ones.

\gamma^{A},\tilde{\gamma}^{A}
and
\gamma^{B},\tilde{\gamma}^{B}
. We assume that the couplings act only between a subregion
R\subset S
and subregions
R_{A}\subset A
and
R_{B}\subset B
, see
Fig.

. The coupling Hamiltonian for this setup is

H_{c}=H_{RR_{A}}+H_{RR_{B}}.

(37)

We consider only the odd part of
H_{c}
here, and refer to
Appendix

I

\displaystyle H^{o}_{c,\text{eff}}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{X=A,B}\Delta_{X}i\tilde{\gamma}\tilde{\gamma}^{X}+\tau_{X}i\gamma\gamma^{X}+\tilde{\tau}_{X}i\gamma\tilde{\gamma}^{X},

(38)

where we have picked the gauges of
A
and
B
to minimize the number of couplings.
If the coupling varies in time, the effective couplings become time-dependent. A successful braiding protocol can then be performed if the desirable couplings
\Delta_{X}
are tunable while the undesirable couplings
\tau_{X}
and
\tilde{\tau}_{X}
are small.
The latter ones are bounded by

\displaystyle|\tau_{X}|

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert H_{RR_{X}}^{o}\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert\gamma_{R}\right\rVert_{q}\lVert{(\gamma^{X})_{R_{X}}}\rVert_{q},

(39a)

\displaystyle|\tilde{\tau}_{X}|

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert H_{RR_{X}}^{o}\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert\gamma_{R}\right\rVert_{q}\lVert{(\tilde{\gamma}^{X})_{R_{X}}}\rVert_{q}.

(39b)

For the desired couplings to be large, there needs to be MBSs in the subregions that are coupled, i.e.,
\left\lVert\tilde{\gamma}_{R}\right\rVert
should be large, and similarly for the other systems.
Furthermore,
H_{RR_{A}}
and
H_{RR_{B}}
need to be chosen such that the
\Delta
-terms do not vanish. The
q=2
bound for
\Delta_{X}
is saturated if

H_{RR_{X}}\propto\tilde{\gamma}_{R}(\tilde{\gamma}^{X})_{R_{X}},

(40)

which is therefore optimal to maximize the desired effective couplings. In an experiment, it may not be feasible to tune the coupling to exactly match the optimal one. However, an imperfect coupling does not ruin the protocol. The desired couplings would be smaller, which increases adiabatic errors, but this can be compensated for by increasing the operation time.

These results provide simple criteria for assessing whether a system is suitable for use in a coupling-based braiding setup: if
\left\lVert\gamma_{R}\right\rVert_{q}
and
\left\lVert(i\gamma\tilde{\gamma})_{R}\right\rVert_{q}
are small, and
\left\lVert\tilde{\gamma}_{R}\right\rVert_{q}
is large, then three copies of the system can be used to implement non-abelian braiding.

VIII
Conclusions

For non-interacting systems, the spatial separation of MBSs is an established tool to understand the robustness of the energy spectrum and non-abelian braiding. This concept relies on single-particle wave functions, which are not available in the interacting case.
In this work, we developed a formalism valid in both interacting and non-interacting systems, based on the partial trace of ground-state MBSs, allowing us to use the concept of MBS localization in any system with two low-energy ground states of different fermionic parity. We proved rigorous bounds on the protection of the energy spectrum, and discussed the implications for braiding protocols.
To illustrate our results, we studied the interacting Kitaev chain and found that some of the intuition gained from single-particle MBSs carries over to ground-state MBSs.

The partial trace is usually used to study individual states via the reduced density matrix. We point out that when considering multiple states, such as in a topological theory with several (nearly) degenerate ground states, the reduced transition operators are also meaningful and can reveal topological edge states. It would be interesting to understand their structure in more detail. In particular, how the properties of the two ground states (such as their entanglement) influence the properties of the ground-state MBSs.

Our bounds involve quality measures that generalize those previously used for MBSs, namely the Majorana polarization and the local distinguishability. We clarified the conditions under which previously used formulas remain valid, and when they need to be generalized.
The construction of ground-state MBSs and the quality measures rely only on the ground states, which means our methods can be used even in large interacting systems using, e.g., density matrix renormalization group (DMRG) methods. Thus, our results open up a new way to study strongly interacting topological systems.

Appendix A
Proof of coupling bounds

Consider a bipartite system with regions
X,\bar{X}
. We use
\mathfrak{i}_{\bar{X}}(\cdot)
to denote the fermionic embedding of an operator in
X
into the full space
X\cup\bar{X}
, see Ref.
Szalay et al. 2021
. We denote the fermionic partial trace of an operator
A
acting in
X\cup\bar{X}
by
A_{X}=\Tr_{\bar{X}}[A]
.

The main facts we need to prove the bounds on couplings are

1.

The Schatten norm satisfies

\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{p}=\sup_{\left\lVert C\right\rVert_{q}=1}{|\Tr[BC]|}.

(41)

2.

The fermionic partial trace is the adjoint of the fermionic embedding
Szalay et al. 2021
, i.e.,
\Tr[\mathfrak{i}_{\bar{X}}(A)C]=\Tr[A\Tr_{\bar{X}}[C]]=\Tr[AC_{X}]
.

3.

|\Tr{AB}|\leq\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{q}
for any two matrices
A,B
.

4.

If
A_{X}
has a specific parity, then

\mathfrak{i}_{X}(A)\mathfrak{i}_{\bar{X}}(B)
is unitarily equivalent to
A_{X}\otimes B_{\bar{X}}
and therefore their Schatten norms are the same. The case when both
A
and
B
are even is proved in
Szalay et al. 2021
by constructing a unitary
U
. This can be extended to the more general case. Assume that
A_{X}
is even; otherwise multiply it by an odd unitary. In this case, the unitary that does the job is
U^{\prime}=(-1)^{N_{X}N_{\bar{X}}}U
, where
N_{X}
is the number operator in region
X
.

Let
H
be an operator in the full system,
A
an operator in
X
with definite parity, and
B
any operator in
\bar{X}
. We can bound the trace of their product by

\begin{split}|\Tr{\left[\mathfrak{i}_{\bar{X}}(A)\mathfrak{i}_{X}(B)H\right]}|&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\leq}}\left\lVert H\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert\mathfrak{i}_{\bar{X}}(A)\mathfrak{i}_{X}(B)\right\rVert_{q}\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 4}}{{=}}\left\lVert H\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert A\otimes B\right\rVert_{q}\\
&=\left\lVert H\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{q}\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{q}.\end{split}

(42)

This bound applies to the couplings of gapped systems in
Section

VII
and
Appendix

I
. It is saturated when

H=\mathfrak{i}_{X}((B^{\dagger}B)^{q/2-1}B^{\dagger})\mathfrak{i}_{\bar{X}}((A^{\dagger}A)^{q/2-1}A^{\dagger}).

(43)

If
q/2<1
and the matrices are not invertible, one can use the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse.

Next, we derive the bound used for the effective couplings in
Section

III
. Let
H
be an operator in the full system,
A
an operator in
X
with definite parity and

B_{\bar{X}}\equiv\Tr_{X}\left[\mathfrak{i}_{\bar{X}}(A)H\right].

(44)

The effective operators in the main text have this form. We will prove that

\left\lVert B_{\bar{X}}\right\rVert_{p}\leq\left\lVert H\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{q},

(45)

where
1/p+1/q=1
. The proof is

\begin{split}\left\lVert B_{\bar{X}}\right\rVert_{p}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{=}}\sup_{\left\lVert C\right\rVert_{q}=1}{|\Tr[BC]|}\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref{eq:B_definition}}}{{=}}\sup_{\left\lVert C\right\rVert_{q}=1}{|\Tr[\Tr_{X}\left[\mathfrak{i}_{\bar{X}}(A)H\right]C]|}\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{=}}\sup_{\left\lVert C\right\rVert_{q}=1}{|\Tr[\mathfrak{i}_{\bar{X}}(A)H\mathfrak{i}_{X}(C)]|}\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\leq}}\left\lVert H\right\rVert_{p}\sup_{\left\lVert C\right\rVert_{q}=1}{\left\lVert\mathfrak{i}_{\bar{X}}(A)\mathfrak{i}_{X}(C)\right\rVert_{q}}\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref{eq:proof_gapped_bound}}}{{\leq}}\left\lVert H\right\rVert_{p}\sup_{\left\lVert C\right\rVert_{q}=1}{\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{q}\left\lVert C\right\rVert_{q}}\\
&=\left\lVert H\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{q}.\end{split}

(46)

The bound is saturated when

H=\mathfrak{i}_{X}(h_{\bar{X}})\mathfrak{i}_{\bar{X}}((A^{\dagger}A)^{q/2-1}A^{\dagger}),

(47)

for any operator
h_{\bar{X}}
in
\bar{X}
.

Appendix B
Proof of energy splitting bounds

This section contains the details on the bound for the energy splitting. The idea is to consider the quantity

\mathcal{C}\equiv[\mathfrak{i}_{B}(\gamma),H_{\text{eff}}]-i\varepsilon\mathfrak{i}_{B}(\tilde{\gamma}),

(48)

which, because of the commutator, can be related to energy differences, and can be bounded because
H_{\text{eff}}
can be expressed in terms of the effective couplings.

Let
|\mathcal{E}\rangle,|\mathcal{O}\rangle
be two eigenstates of
H_{\text{eff}}
of even and odd parity. The matrix element

|\langle\mathcal{O}|\mathcal{C}|\mathcal{E}\rangle|=|\langle\mathcal{O}|[\mathfrak{i}_{B}(\gamma),H]-i\varepsilon\mathfrak{i}_{B}(\tilde{\gamma})|\mathcal{E}\rangle|\\
=|\delta E\langle\mathcal{O}|\mathfrak{i}_{B}(\gamma)|\mathcal{E}\rangle-i\varepsilon\langle\mathcal{O}|\mathfrak{i}_{B}(\tilde{\gamma})|\mathcal{E}\rangle|

(49)

provides us with the energy difference
\delta E
between the eigenstates. Next,
\mathcal{C}
can also be expressed in terms of the effective couplings as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=\mathfrak{i}_{S}(G)\mathfrak{i}_{B}(i\tilde{\gamma})+\mathfrak{i}_{S}(F).

(50)

First, we use the triangle inequality

|\langle\mathcal{O}|\mathcal{C}|\mathcal{E}\rangle|\leq|\langle\mathcal{O}|\mathfrak{i}_{B}(\gamma)\mathfrak{i}_{S}(G)|\mathcal{E}\rangle|+|\langle\mathcal{O}|\mathfrak{i}_{S}(F)|\mathcal{E}\rangle|,

(51)

and then we will bound the two terms separately. In addition to the properties used in the previous section, we will use that

5.

The Schatten-1 norm gets smaller under the partial trace
Rastegin 2012
.

6.

For any two matrices
A
and
B
and
1/p+1/q=1/r
, we have that
\left\lVert AB\right\rVert_{r}\leq\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{q}
.

7.

\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{p}\leq\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{q}
for
p>q
.

The
G
-term can be bounded as

\begin{split}|\langle\mathcal{O}|\mathfrak{i}_{B}(\gamma)\mathfrak{i}_{S}(G)|\mathcal{E}\rangle|&=|\Tr[\mathfrak{i}_{S}(G)\mathfrak{i}_{B}(\gamma)|\mathcal{E}\rangle\langle\mathcal{O}|]|\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{=}}|\Tr[G(\mathfrak{i}_{B}(\gamma)|\mathcal{E}\rangle\langle\mathcal{O}|)_{B}]|\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\leq}}\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{\infty}\left\lVert(\mathfrak{i}_{B}(\gamma)|\mathcal{E}\rangle\langle\mathcal{O}|)_{B}\right\rVert_{1}\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 5}}{{\leq}}\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{\infty}\left\lVert\mathfrak{i}_{B}(\gamma)|\mathcal{E}\rangle\langle\mathcal{O}|\right\rVert_{1}\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 6}}{{\leq}}\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{\infty}\left\lVert\mathfrak{i}_{B}(\gamma)\right\rVert_{\infty}\left\lVert|\mathcal{E}\rangle\langle\mathcal{O}|\right\rVert_{1}\\
&=\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{\infty}\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 7}}{{\leq}}\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{p}.\end{split}

(52)

The
F
-term is bounded by

\begin{split}|\langle\mathcal{O}|\mathfrak{i}_{S}(F)|\mathcal{E}\rangle|&\leq\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert(|\mathcal{E}\rangle\langle\mathcal{O}|)_{B}\right\rVert_{q},\end{split}

(53)

by using properties 2 and 3.

Combining these statements, we end up with the general bound in the main paper

\begin{split}&|\delta E\langle\mathcal{O}|\mathfrak{i}_{B}(\gamma)|\mathcal{E}\rangle-i\varepsilon\langle\mathcal{O}|\mathfrak{i}_{B}(\tilde{\gamma})|\mathcal{E}\rangle|\\
&\leq\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{p}+\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert(|\mathcal{E}\rangle\langle\mathcal{O}|)_{B}\right\rVert_{q}\\
&\leq\left\lVert(i\gamma\tilde{\gamma})_{R}\right\rVert_{q}\left\lVert H_{RB}^{e}\right\rVert_{p}+\left\lVert\gamma_{R}\right\rVert_{q}\left\lVert H_{RB}^{o}\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert(|\mathcal{E}\rangle\langle\mathcal{O}|)_{B}\right\rVert_{q}.\end{split}

(54)

B.1
Simplifying the bound

Assume that when the systems are uncoupled, the environment has a cluster of
d_{B}
eigenstates which are separated from other eigenstates by an energy gap
E_{B}
. We consider the case
\left\lVert H_{RB}\right\rVert\ll E_{B}
so that we can neglect any mixing with other states. We assume that the cluster consists of
d_{B}^{e}
states with even parity and
d_{B}^{o}
states with odd parity, and denote them by
|\psi_{j}^{e}\rangle
and
|\psi_{j}^{o}\rangle
.

When the coupling between the system and the bath is turned on, the eigenstates can be expressed as

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{B}^{e}}a_{ij}|e\rangle|\psi_{j}^{e}\rangle+\sum_{j=1}^{d_{B}^{o}}b_{ij}|o\rangle|\psi_{j}^{o}\rangle,

(55)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{O}_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{B}^{e}}c_{ij}|o\rangle|\psi_{j}^{e}\rangle+\sum_{j=1}^{d_{B}^{o}}d_{ij}|e\rangle|\psi_{j}^{o}\rangle.

(56)

B.1.1
No parity mixing

In general, the states in
Eq.

55
mix states with different parities in
S
and
B
. If that does not happen, we can simplify things considerably. This simplification happens, for example, if
\varepsilon\gg\left\lVert H_{RB}\right\rVert
or if either
d_{B}^{e}=0
or
d_{B}^{o}=0
. Then one can treat the different parity sectors in
B
independently. Here, we consider the even parity states of the environment. The eigenstates take the form

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{B}^{e}}a_{ij}|e\rangle|\psi_{j}^{e}\rangle

(57)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{O}_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{B}^{e}}c_{ij}|o\rangle|\psi_{j}^{e}\rangle.

(58)

It follows that

\displaystyle\left\lVert(|\mathcal{E}_{i}\rangle\langle\mathcal{O}_{j}|)_{B}\right\rVert_{q}=0

(59)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O}_{i}|\gamma|\mathcal{E}_{j}\rangle=i\langle\mathcal{O}_{i}|\tilde{\gamma}|\mathcal{E}_{j}\rangle

(60)

for all pairs. The bound simplifies to

\left|\delta E-\varepsilon\right|\left|\langle\mathcal{O}|\gamma|\mathcal{E}\rangle\right|\leq\left\lVert(i\gamma\tilde{\gamma})_{R}\right\rVert_{q}\left\lVert H_{RB}^{e}\right\rVert_{p}.

(61)

Next, we want a lower bound on
\left|\langle\mathcal{O}|\gamma|\mathcal{E}\rangle\right|
. Since
\gamma|\mathcal{E}_{i}\rangle
is a normalized state which can be expanded in the basis
|\mathcal{O}_{j}\rangle
, it must overlap with at least one of those states, and that overlap must be larger than
\sqrt{1/d_{B}^{e}}
. We arrive at the following statement: for all eigenstates in this set, there exists another eigenstate such that their energy difference is bounded by

\lvert\delta E-\varepsilon\rvert\leq\left\lVert(i\gamma\tilde{\gamma})_{R}\right\rVert_{q}\left\lVert H_{RB}^{e}\right\rVert_{p}\sqrt{d_{B}^{e}}.

(62)

An analogous bound holds when considering odd states of the environment.

B.1.2
Parity mixing

In this case, the bound is weaker. Use the triangle inequality on the left-hand side to separate
\delta E
and
\varepsilon
and then

\displaystyle\left\lVert(|\mathcal{E}_{i}\rangle\langle\mathcal{O}_{j}|)_{B}\right\rVert_{q}\leq 1,

(63)

\displaystyle\left|\langle\mathcal{O}_{i}|i\tilde{\gamma}|\mathcal{E}_{j}\rangle\right|\leq 1,

(64)

\displaystyle\left|\langle\mathcal{O}_{i}|\gamma|\mathcal{E}_{j}\rangle\right|\geq\sqrt{1/d_{B}},

(65)

to bound the unknown quantities.
The result is:
For each eigenstate, there exists another eigenstate with different parity, and their energy difference is bounded by

|\delta E|\leq\sqrt{d_{B}}(\left\lVert(i\gamma\tilde{\gamma})_{R}\right\rVert_{q}\left\lVert H_{RB}^{e}\right\rVert_{p}+\left\lVert\gamma_{R}\right\rVert_{q}\left\lVert H_{RB}^{o}\right\rVert_{p}+|\varepsilon|).

(66)

B.2
Uncoupled eigenstates

A simpler bound is obtained by considering the original uncoupled eigenstates. The energy difference between
|e\rangle|\psi\rangle
and
|o\rangle|\psi\rangle
, where
|\psi\rangle
is any state in the environment is

\delta E\equiv\langle\psi|\langle e|H_{\text{eff}}|e\rangle|\psi\rangle-\langle\psi|\langle o|H_{\text{eff}}|o\rangle|\psi\rangle\\
=\langle\psi|\langle o|[\gamma,H_{\text{eff}}]|e\rangle|\psi\rangle.

(67)

Together with
Eqs.

12
and
8
it is straightforward to show that

|\delta E-\varepsilon|\leq\left\lVert(i\gamma\tilde{\gamma})_{R}\right\rVert_{q}\left\lVert H_{RB}^{e}\right\rVert_{p},

(68)

which says that the perturbation of the energy difference only changes if the MBSs overlap in the region
R
. This generalizes a bound from Ref.
Svensson and Leijnse 2024
.

Appendix C
Choice of norms

Throughout this article, we use the Schatten norm and express the bounds for general
p,q
, as different choices have different advantages. The two most interesting cases are
q=1,p=\infty
(trace and operator norm), and
p=q=2
(Frobenius norm).

The choice
q=1
has the advantage that the partial trace is contractive in the trace norm, i.e.,

R\subset R^{\prime}\Rightarrow\left\lVert\gamma_{R}\right\rVert_{1}\leq\left\lVert\gamma_{R^{\prime}}\right\rVert_{1},

(69)

which intuitively says that a region contained in another region must contain less of the MBS. This is not necessarily the case for other norms.
Additionally, if the environment is expanded
B\rightarrow B\cup B^{\prime}
trivially as
H_{RBB^{\prime}}=H_{RB}\otimes I_{B^{\prime}}
, then the norm is
\left\lVert H_{RB^{\prime}}\right\rVert_{p}=d_{B^{\prime}}^{1/p}\left\lVert H_{RB}\right\rVert_{p}
. Choosing
p=\infty
makes the norm insensitive to this trivial extension.

Finally, for
q=1
, we have that

\left\lVert(i\gamma\tilde{\gamma})_{R}\right\rVert_{1}=\left\lVert\rho^{e}_{R}-\rho^{o}_{R}\right\rVert_{1},

(70)

which is proportional to the trace distance between the reduced density matrix of the even (odd) ground states
\rho_{R}^{e(o)}
. This distance measure has an operational meaning in that it gives maximum probability to distinguish between the states by a measurement
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
.

On the other hand, choosing the Frobenius norm
p=q=2
has the advantage that it is easier to evaluate analytically, and optimizing with this norm provides a natural gauge for defining MBSs. While the partial trace in this norm is not necessarily contractive, it satisfies

\left\lVert\gamma_{R}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}+\left\lVert\gamma_{\bar{R}}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\leq 2\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert^{2}_{2}=4,

(71)

see Ref.
Rico 2025
.

Appendix D
Ambiguities of the coupling Hamiltonian

Writing the coupled Hamiltonian as

H=H_{S}+H_{RB}+H_{B},

(72)

introduces some ambiguities, as

1.

terms acting only in
R
can be in either
H_{S}
or
H_{RB}
, and

2.

terms acting only in
B
can be in either
H_{B}
or
H_{RB}
.

To minimize the norm of
H_{RB}
, one can put the second type of terms in
B
. In other words,
\Tr_{R}[H_{RB}]=0
. One can also put the first type of terms in
H_{S}
for the same reason, but note that this will influence the eigenstates of
S
and its ground-state MBS. In
Section

VI
, such terms arise from the Coulomb interaction between
R
and
B
, and we choose to keep them in
H_{RB}
to keep the MBSs constant and the degeneracy
\varepsilon=0
.

Appendix E
Coupling to several subregions

Here, we consider the case where the coupling Hamiltonian consists of a sum of terms, each acting in a region
R_{n}
. These regions may overlap. One can directly use the bounds in the main paper with the region
R=\bigcup_{n}R_{n}
. However, one can also treat each region independently. We do so here, defining effective operators for each region, bounding them, and generalizing the bound for the energy spectrum.

Let the coupling Hamiltonian be

H_{SB}=\sum_{n}H_{R_{n}B}.

(73)

The effective Hamiltonian can be written

H_{\text{eff}}=\varepsilon i\gamma\tilde{\gamma}/2+\sum_{n}G_{n}i\gamma\tilde{\gamma}/2+\gamma F_{n}/2+\tilde{\gamma}\tilde{F}_{n}/2+H_{B},

(74)

The effective operators are

\displaystyle G_{n}

\displaystyle=\Tr_{S}{\left[i\gamma\tilde{\gamma}H_{R_{n}B}^{e}\right]}=\Tr_{R}{\left[(i\gamma\tilde{\gamma})_{R_{n}}H_{R_{n}B}^{e}\right]},

(75a)

\displaystyle F_{n}

\displaystyle=\Tr_{S}{\left[\gamma H_{R_{n}B}^{o}\right]}=\Tr_{R}{\left[\gamma_{R_{n}}H_{R_{n}B}^{o}\right]},

(75b)

\displaystyle\tilde{F}_{n}

\displaystyle=\Tr_{S}{\left[\tilde{\gamma}H_{R_{n}B}^{o}\right]}=\Tr_{R}{\left[\tilde{\gamma}_{R_{n}}H_{R_{n}B}^{o}\right]}

(75c)

and they are bounded as

\displaystyle\left\lVert F_{n}\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert H_{R_{n}B}^{o}\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert\gamma_{R_{n}}\right\rVert_{q},

(76a)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\tilde{F}_{n}\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert H_{R_{n}B}^{o}\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert\tilde{\gamma}_{R_{n}}\right\rVert_{q},

(76b)

\displaystyle\left\lVert G_{n}\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert H_{R_{n}B}^{e}\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert(i\gamma\tilde{\gamma})_{R_{n}}\right\rVert_{q}.

(76c)

The commutator is

[\gamma,H_{\text{eff}}]-\varepsilon i\tilde{\gamma}=\sum_{n}i\tilde{\gamma}G_{n}+F_{n}.

(77)

The generalization of the energy bound is

|\delta E\langle\mathcal{O}|\gamma|\mathcal{E}\rangle-\varepsilon\langle\mathcal{O}|i\tilde{\gamma}|\mathcal{E}\rangle|\leq\sum_{n}\left\lVert(i\gamma\tilde{\gamma})_{R_{n}}\right\rVert_{q}\left\lVert H_{R_{n}B}^{e}\right\rVert_{p}\\
+\left\lVert\gamma_{R_{n}}\right\rVert_{q}\left\lVert H_{R_{n}B}^{o}\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert(|\mathcal{E}\rangle\langle\mathcal{O}|)_{B}\right\rVert_{q}.

(78)

We make the same simplifications as in
Appendix

B
, and also use Cauchy-Schwartz for the sum on the right-hand side to find

|\delta E|\leq\sqrt{d_{B}}\left(Q_{e}h_{e}+Q_{o}h_{o}+|\varepsilon|\right),

(79)

where

\displaystyle Q_{o}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{n}\left\lVert\gamma_{R_{n}}\right\rVert_{q}^{2}},

(80a)

\displaystyle Q_{e}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{n}\left\lVert(i\gamma\tilde{\gamma})_{R_{n}}\right\rVert_{q}^{2}},

(80b)

\displaystyle h_{o}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\left\lVert H_{R_{n}B}^{o}\right\rVert_{p}^{2},

(80c)

\displaystyle h_{e}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\left\lVert H_{R_{n}B}^{e}\right\rVert_{p}^{2}.

(80d)

Q
is an MBS quality factor which only depends on the subregions in which the coupling acts, and
h
measures the size of the coupling.

Now we specialize to
q=p=2
. As in
Appendix

D
, there is an ambiguity in the coupling Hamiltonian since some terms could belong to different
H_{R_{n}B}
. But one can always partition terms such that different couplings
H_{R_{n}B}
are orthogonal. Then, we find

|\delta E|\leq\sqrt{d_{B}}\left(Q_{e}\left\lVert H_{RB}^{e}\right\rVert_{2}+Q_{o}\left\lVert H_{RB}^{o}\right\rVert_{2}+|\varepsilon|\right).

(81)

Note that we have only a single gauge parameter to choose, and we cannot necessarily pick one gauge that is optimal for all subregions simultaneously.

Appendix F
Details on gauge choices and Majorana polarization

A shift
\theta
of the relative phase between
|e\rangle
and
|o\rangle
results in a rotation of the ground-state MBSs as

\displaystyle\gamma^{\theta}

\displaystyle=\cos{\theta}\gamma+\sin{\theta}\tilde{\gamma},

(82a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}^{\theta}

\displaystyle=-\sin{\theta}\gamma+\cos{\theta}\tilde{\gamma}.

(82b)

In the Frobenius norm (
q=2
), we can analytically derive the optimal gauge that minimizes the norm of one of the MBSs in a region. Another useful feature of the Frobenius norm is that the other MBS has a maximum in that gauge (this is not true in general for other norms). Furthermore, for a real Hamiltonian, the gauge with either real (or imaginary) states is optimal.

It is convenient to work with the ground-state fermion

c=(\gamma+i\tilde{\gamma})/\sqrt{2},

(83)

which transforms as
c\rightarrow e^{i\theta}c
under the gauge. The minimum can then be determined as

\begin{split}\min_{\theta}\sum_{n}\left\lVert\gamma^{\theta}_{R_{n}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}&=\min_{\theta}\sum_{n}\left\lVert e^{i\theta}c_{R_{n}}+e^{-i\theta}c_{R_{n}}^{\dagger}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}/2\\
&=\min_{\theta}\sum_{n}\Tr[c^{\dagger}_{R_{n}}c_{R_{n}}]+\\
&\qquad\Re\left(e^{2i\theta}\sum_{n}\Tr[c_{R_{n}}c_{R_{n}}]\right)\\
&=\sum_{n}\Tr[c^{\dagger}_{R_{n}}c_{R_{n}}]-\left|\sum_{n}\Tr[c_{R_{n}}c_{R_{n}}]\right|,\end{split}

(84)

and the optimal phase is negative half the phase of the number
\sum_{n}\Tr[c_{R_{n}}c_{R_{n}}]
. The first term in
Eq.

84
can be interpreted as a fermion density, and the second term as a Majorana density. This can also be written in the form

\min_{\theta}\sum_{n}\left\lVert\gamma^{\theta}_{R_{n}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\left(\sum_{n}\left\lVert c_{R_{n}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)(1-M),

(85)

where

M=\max_{\theta}\frac{\sum_{n}\left\lVert\gamma_{R_{n}}^{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\left\lVert\tilde{\gamma}_{R_{n}}^{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\sum_{n}\left\lVert\gamma_{R_{n}}^{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert\tilde{\gamma}^{\theta}_{R_{n}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}=\frac{\left|\sum_{n}\Tr[c_{R_{n}}c_{R_{n}}]\right|}{\sum_{n}\Tr[c_{R_{n}}^{\dagger}c_{R_{n}}]}.

(86)

In the case of a single region
R
,
Eq.

85
shows that
\min_{\theta}\lVert\gamma^{\theta}_{R}\rVert_{2}
vanishes if
R
contains no MBS at all (
\left\lVert c_{R}\right\rVert_{2}=0
), or if it contains exactly one MBS (
M=1
).

The Majorana polarization can be calculated using MBSs defined in any gauge using the expression

M=\frac{\sqrt{\left(\sum_{n}\left\lVert\gamma_{R_{n}}^{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\left\lVert\tilde{\gamma}^{\theta}_{R_{n}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)^{2}+4\left\lvert\sum_{n}\Tr[\gamma_{R}^{\theta}\tilde{\gamma}_{R_{n}}^{\theta}]\right\rvert^{2}}}{\sum_{n}\left\lVert\gamma_{R_{n}}^{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert\tilde{\gamma}_{R_{n}}^{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}.

(87)

Comparing this expression with
Eq.

86
, we see that
\sum_{n}\Tr[\gamma^{\theta}_{R_{n}}\tilde{\gamma}_{R_{n}}^{\theta}]=0
in the optimal gauge.

Appendix G
Bounds for non-interacting systems

G.1
Couplings

Now we assume that all terms which act non-trivially on the system
S
are at most quadratic in the fermions of
S
. The environment is allowed to be interacting. In this case, we can work in the full Hilbert space rather than at the ground-state level. Begin by diagonalizing
H_{S}
in terms of standard Majoranas
\Gamma
,
\tilde{\Gamma}
and
\{\chi_{n},\tilde{\chi}_{n}\}
as

H_{S}=i\varepsilon\Gamma\tilde{\Gamma}/2+\sum_{n=1}^{N_{S}-1}i\varepsilon_{n}\chi_{n}\tilde{\chi}_{n}/2,

(88)

where
N_{S}
is the number of fermionic modes in
S
, and
\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{n}\geq 0
are energies corresponding to a pair of Majoranas. In writing
Eq.

88
, we have picked out a particular pair of Majoranas
\Gamma
and
\tilde{\Gamma}
. These Majoranas could, for instance, be the pair with the lowest energy. Then,
\varepsilon
is the energy splitting of the ground states in the isolated system
S
, analogously to the previous section.

The coupling can be decomposed as

H_{RB}=H_{RB}^{e}+H_{RB}^{o},

(89)

where
H_{RB}^{e}
is quadratic in
R
and
H_{RB}^{o}
has terms with one fermion in
R
. The full Hamiltonian can be written

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=i(\varepsilon/2+G)\Gamma\tilde{\Gamma}+\Gamma F+\sum_{n=1}^{N_{S}-1}(iG_{n}\Gamma\chi_{n}+i\tilde{G}_{n}\Gamma\tilde{\chi}_{n})

(90)

\displaystyle+\text{terms commuting with $\Gamma$},

where
F,G,G_{n},\tilde{G_{n}}
are operators acting on the environment, given by

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=\Tr_{R}[\Gamma_{R}H_{RB}^{o}]/d_{S},

(91a)

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=\Tr_{R}[i[\Gamma_{R},\tilde{\Gamma}_{R}]H^{e}_{RB}]/(2d_{\bar{R}}d_{S}),

(91b)

\displaystyle G_{n}

\displaystyle=\Tr_{R}[i[\Gamma_{R},\chi_{nR}]H^{e}_{RB}]/(2d_{\bar{R}}d_{S}),

(91c)

and similarly for
\tilde{G}_{n}
.
Here we have used that for two anti-commuting single-particle operators,
(\Gamma\chi)_{R}=[\Gamma_{R},\chi_{R}]/(2d_{\bar{R}})
. If the reduced Majorana
\Gamma_{R}
is small, the effective operators will be small. More precisely,

\displaystyle\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert H^{o}_{RB}\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert\Gamma_{R}\right\rVert_{q}/d_{S},

(92a)

\displaystyle\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert H^{e}_{RB}\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert(i\Gamma\tilde{\Gamma})_{R}\right\rVert_{q}/(d_{S})

(92b)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert H^{e}_{RB}\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert[\Gamma_{R},\tilde{\Gamma}_{R}]\right\rVert_{q}/(2d_{\bar{R}}d_{S}),

\displaystyle\left\lVert G_{n}\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert H^{e}_{RB}\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert[\Gamma_{R},\chi_{nR}]\right\rVert_{q}/(2d_{\bar{R}}d_{S})

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert H^{e}_{RB}\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert\Gamma_{R}\right\rVert_{q}d_{R}^{1/q}/d_{S},

(92c)

and similarly for
\tilde{G}_{n}
.

Optimizing the gauge to minimize
\left\lVert\Gamma\right\rVert_{q}
, we have

\min_{\text{gauges}}\left\lVert\Gamma_{R}\right\rVert_{q}=\left\lVert C_{R}\right\rVert_{q}\sqrt{1-M},

(93)

where
C=(\Gamma+i\tilde{\Gamma})/\sqrt{2}
and

M=\frac{\bigl\lvert\Tr[C_{R}C_{R}]\bigr\rvert}{\Tr[C^{\dagger}_{R}C_{R}]}.

(94)

We can also write
\left\lVert[\Gamma_{R},\tilde{\Gamma}_{R}]\right\rVert_{q}
in a similar form. It is gauge independent, so we choose the gauge where
\Gamma_{R}
and
\tilde{\Gamma}_{R}
anticommute. Then one finds that

\left\lVert[\Gamma_{R},\tilde{\Gamma}_{R}]\right\rVert_{q}=2\frac{\left\lVert C_{R}\right\rVert^{2}_{q}}{d_{R}^{1/q}}\sqrt{1-M^{2}}

(95)

G.2
Energy bounds

We will bound the energy difference between two arbitrary eigenstates
|\mathcal{E}\rangle,|\mathcal{O}\rangle
of the full Hamiltonian. As in the interacting case, the strategy is to consider the quantity

\displaystyle\left[\Gamma,H\right]-i\varepsilon\tilde{\Gamma}=\mathcal{C},

(96)

where
\mathcal{C}=2iG\tilde{\Gamma}+2F+2i\sum_{n}G_{n}\chi_{n}+\tilde{G}_{n}\tilde{\chi}_{n}
. All terms in
\mathcal{C}
are small if
\left\lVert\Gamma_{R}\right\rVert
is small. Repeating the derivation for the energy bound in the interacting case, we find

\begin{split}&|\delta E\langle\mathcal{O}|\Gamma|\mathcal{E}\rangle-i\varepsilon\langle\mathcal{O}|\tilde{\Gamma}|\mathcal{E}\rangle|/2\\
&\leq\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{p}+\sum_{n=1}^{N_{S}-1}\left\lVert G_{n}\right\rVert_{p}+\lVert{\tilde{G}_{n}\rVert}_{p}+\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert(|\mathcal{E}\rangle\langle\mathcal{O}|)_{B}\right\rVert_{q}.\end{split}

(97)

Using the optimal gauge and the coupling bounds, this becomes

\begin{split}&|\delta E\langle\mathcal{O}|\Gamma|\mathcal{E}\rangle-i\varepsilon\langle\mathcal{O}|\tilde{\Gamma}|\mathcal{E}\rangle|/2\\
&\leq\frac{\left\lVert C_{R}\right\rVert_{q}}{d_{S}}\sqrt{1-M}\Big[\left\lVert H_{RB}^{o}\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert(|\mathcal{E}\rangle\langle\mathcal{O}|)_{B}\right\rVert_{q}\\
&\qquad+\left\lVert H_{RB}^{e}\right\rVert_{p}\Big(2(N_{S}-1)d_{R}^{1/q}+\frac{\left\lVert C_{R}\right\rVert_{q}}{d_{\bar{R}}d_{R}^{1/q}}\sqrt{1+M}\Big)\Big].\end{split}

(98)

This expression still contains quantities which are hard to calculate, namely
|\langle\mathcal{O}|\Gamma|\mathcal{E}\rangle|,|\langle\mathcal{O}|\tilde{\Gamma}|\mathcal{E}\rangle|
and
\left\lVert(|\mathcal{E}\rangle\langle\mathcal{O}|)_{B}\right\rVert_{q}
. Bounding these is more complicated than for the effective theory of the ground-state sector, as we have to deal with the full Hilbert space of the system. On the other hand, these bounds hold for arbitrarily large coupling. The main takeaway from this equation is the prefactor
\left\lVert C_{R}\right\rVert_{q}\sqrt{1-M}
, which characterizes the MBS quality.

G.3
Majorana polarization

So far, we have worked with the single-particle MBSs as operators in Hilbert space. Since they are single-particle operators, it is also useful to represent them in a single-particle operator basis and study their wavefunction.

We need some notation for converting single-particle operators to their wavefunction. For an operator expanded in a basis of single-particle Majorana operators

\displaystyle\chi=\sum_{js}a_{js}\Gamma_{j}^{s},

(99)

we define
|\Gamma)=\vec{a}
to be the vector of the coefficients. By
|\Gamma)_{R}
we mean the restriction to only those components
\Gamma_{j}^{s}
that live in the region
R
. It is related to the partial trace by
|\Gamma_{R})=d_{\bar{R}}|\Gamma)_{R}
. The inner product of two single-particle operators in the Hilbert space is related to the inner product of the wavefunctions as

\displaystyle\Tr[\chi_{1}^{\dagger}\chi_{2}]

\displaystyle=(\chi_{1}|\chi_{2})d_{H}

(100)

\displaystyle\{\chi_{1},\chi_{2}\}

\displaystyle=2(\chi_{1}|\chi_{2})I.

(101)

The Majorana polarization
Eq.

87
is

M=\frac{\sqrt{\left(({\Gamma}|\Gamma)_{R}-(\tilde{\Gamma}|\tilde{\Gamma})_{R}\right)^{2}+4(\tilde{\Gamma}|\Gamma)_{R}^{2}}}{({\Gamma}|\Gamma)_{R}+(\tilde{\Gamma}|\tilde{\Gamma})_{R}}

(102)

Consider two states
|e\rangle,|o\rangle
such that
\Gamma|e\rangle=|o\rangle
and
\tilde{\Gamma}|e\rangle=i|o\rangle
. Correlations of these states define the wavefunctions as

\displaystyle(\Gamma|\Gamma)_{R}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s,j\in R}\Re[\langle o|\Gamma_{j}^{s}|e\rangle]^{2}

(103)

\displaystyle(\tilde{\Gamma}|\tilde{\Gamma})_{R}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s,j\in R}\Im[\langle o|\Gamma_{j}^{s}|e\rangle]^{2}

(104)

\displaystyle(\tilde{\Gamma}|{\Gamma})_{R}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s,j\in R}\Re[\langle o|\Gamma_{j}^{s}|e\rangle]\Im[\langle o|\Gamma_{j}^{s}|e\rangle]

(105)

and then the Majorana polarization can be expressed as

\displaystyle M=\frac{\left|\sum_{s,j\in R}\langle o|\Gamma_{j}^{s}|e\rangle^{2}\right|}{\sum_{s,j\in R}|\langle o|\Gamma_{j}^{s}|e\rangle|^{2}}.

(106)

The structure of these equations becomes clearer if we define the vector
\mathbf{z}_{R}
with elements

(\mathbf{z}_{R})_{js}=\langle o|\Gamma_{j}^{s}|e\rangle,

(107)

where
j\in R
,
s=\pm
. The real and imaginary parts of this complex vector give the coefficients of the two MBSs, and the Majorana polarization is

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=\frac{|\mathbf{z}_{R}^{T}\mathbf{z}_{R}|}{\mathbf{z}_{R}^{\dagger}\mathbf{z}_{R}}

(108)

The two quantities
\mathbf{z}_{R}^{\dagger}\mathbf{z}_{R}
and
|\mathbf{z}_{R}^{T}\mathbf{z}_{R}|
are in fact the only two gauge-invariant quantities that can be written down for a region
R
in a non-interacting theory. Such a quantity must be independent of the basis
\{\Gamma_{i}^{s}\}
and of the relative phase between the odd and even states. A change of basis corresponds to a Bogoliubov rotation of
\mathbf{z}_{R}
by a real orthogonal matrix, and the relative phase multiplies
\mathbf{z}_{R}
by a phase. The only two invariants of these transformations are
\mathbf{z}_{R}^{\dagger}\mathbf{z}_{R}
and
|\mathbf{z}_{R}^{T}\mathbf{z}_{R}|
.

Appendix H
Many-body description of Majorana bound states

The ground-state MBSs
\gamma
and
\tilde{\gamma}
defined in
Eq.

1
are very different from the standard, single-particle MBSs
\Gamma
and
\tilde{\Gamma}
from non-interacting systems. In this section, we contrast the structure and locality of the two types of MBSs, and for non-interacting systems, we demonstrate their relationship. Unlike single-particle MBSs,
\gamma
and
\tilde{\gamma}
are non-local and act non-trivially on the entire system. Nevertheless, when coupling to them in a region
R
, they behave effectively as local operators, captured by
\gamma_{R}
and
\tilde{\gamma}_{R}
.

In the non-interacting case, we diagonalize
H_{S}
in terms of single-particle Majoranas as in
Eq.

88
. Focusing on the low-energy MBS
\Gamma
, we expand it in a position basis as

\Gamma=\sum_{j,s}a_{js}\Gamma_{j}^{s},

(109)

where
a_{js}\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\Gamma_{j}^{s}
form the single-particle Majorana basis defined in
Eq.

21
.

Filling the mode defined by
\Gamma
and
\tilde{\Gamma}
always costs an energy
\varepsilon
, since without interactions it is independent of the filling of the other modes. Therefore, the many-body spectrum is ”strong” in the sense that every state in the even sector is paired up with a state in the odd sector separated by
\varepsilon
.
\Gamma
and
\tilde{\Gamma}
map between the odd and even states throughout the spectrum and can be represented in the energy basis as

\Gamma=|e\rangle\langle o|+\sum_{i}|e_{i}\rangle\langle o_{i}|+\text{h.c.},

(110)

where
i
indexes the excited states. It is important to choose the relative phases between different sectors correctly to get a single-particle operator
Kells 2015b
.

Unlike the non-interacting case, where degenerate ground states imply degeneracy between excited states as well, the spectrum in interacting systems is, in general, weak—the ground states could be degenerate while excited states are not. This fact, along with our focus on adiabatic protocols, further motivates our definition of MBSs acting only in the ground-state sector

\gamma=|e\rangle\langle o|+\text{h.c.}

(111)

While
Eq.

110
and
Eq.

111
have a similar form in the energy basis, the positional representation of
\gamma
is more complicated than that of
\Gamma
, as the former contains many-body terms. In general, we can write it as

\gamma=\sum_{I}a_{I}\Upsilon_{I},

(112)

where
I=(j_{1}s_{1},j_{2}s_{2},\dots,j_{|I|}s_{|I|})
is a multi-index and

\Upsilon_{I}=i^{|I|(|I|-1)/2}\prod_{js\in I}\Gamma_{j}^{s}

(113)

is a Hermitian
|I|
-body product of single-particle Majoranas. Note that
|I|
is odd since
\gamma
is fermionic and therefore contains only terms with an odd number of fermions.

In a non-interacting system, we can relate the single-particle and ground-state MBSs by

\gamma=\Gamma Q,

(114)

where
Q
is the projector on the ground-state sector. It can be expressed in terms of the other Bogoliubov quasiparticles
\chi_{n},\tilde{\chi}_{n}
(see
Eq.

88
) as

Q=\prod_{n=1}^{N_{S}-1}\frac{(I-i\chi_{n}\tilde{\chi}_{n})}{2}.

(115)

Plugging
Eq.

115
into
Eq.

114
, we get

\displaystyle\gamma=\frac{1}{2^{N_{S}-1}}(\Gamma-i\Gamma\chi_{1}\tilde{\chi}_{1}+\dots)=\frac{\Gamma}{2^{N_{S}-1}}+M,

(116)

where
M
is an operator containing three-body terms and higher.
The single-particle content of
\gamma
is completely determined by
\Gamma
while the many-body content depends on all the quasiparticles. Note that
\gamma
has non-zero many-body content, even in the non-interacting case.

The two ground-state MBSs are not independent, as they are related by

\tilde{\gamma}=iP\gamma,

(117)

where
P=\prod_{j}i\Gamma_{j}^{+}\Gamma_{j}^{-}
is the total parity operator. This is different for the single-particle MBSs whose wavefunctions are independent.

Turning to the localization properties of
\Gamma
and
\gamma
, we first define a bipartition
R,\bar{R}
of
S
. Then, in the non-interacting system,
Eq.

109
can be split into two parts

\Gamma=\sum_{s,j\in R}a_{js}\Gamma_{j}^{s}+\sum_{s,j\in\bar{R}}a_{js}\Gamma_{j}^{s},

(118)

where each term lives either in
R
or
\bar{R}
. The reduced operator is simply

\Gamma_{R}=d_{\bar{R}}\sum_{s,j\in R}a_{js}\Gamma_{j}^{s}.

(119)

In the interacting case, we have to split
Eq.

112
into three parts

\gamma=\sum_{I\in R}a_{I}\Upsilon_{I}+\sum_{I\in\bar{R}}a_{I}\Upsilon_{I}+\sum_{I\in R\bar{R}}a_{I}\Upsilon_{I},

(120)

where for the sum over
I\in R\bar{R}
, we require that
I
contains indices in both regions. The last term is a non-local term, which makes the locality of the operator less obvious.

Although
\gamma
is generally non-local, its effective action within a region
R
can still be made precise using the partial trace. Under the partial trace to
R
, all terms that are non-trivial in
\bar{R}
vanish as they are traceless there. Only the first term in
Eq.

120
survives the partial trace, and the reduced operator becomes

\gamma_{R}=d_{\bar{R}}\sum_{I\in R}a_{I}\Upsilon_{I},

(121)

which captures the part of
\gamma
that an odd coupling contained in
R
would effectively probe.

Finally, we comment on another notion of Majorana locality discussed in the literature, namely, commutation properties
Bozkurt et al. 2025
;
O’Brien and Wright 2015
. For a single-particle Majorana operator, if
\Gamma_{R}=0
then it commutes with all even operators
A_{R}
in
R
(
[A_{R},\Gamma]=0
), and anti-commutes with all odd operators
F_{R}
in
R
