Proportion of alcohol use disorder diagnoses in electronic health records documented with stigmatized descriptors: a comparison across race, ethnicity, and sex
Authors: Robert L Ellis, Kevin A Hallgren, Emily C Williams, Courtney R Lyles, Joseph E Glass, Isaac C Rhew, Malia M Oliver, Katharine A Bradley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesIn a sample of patients with diagnosed alcohol use disorder (AUD), we examined the prevalence of both stigmatizing (eg, “alcohol abuse”) and highly stigmatizing, non-medical descriptors (eg, “alcoholic”) documented in electronic health records (EHRs). We also assessed whether the use of these terms—entered by clinicians when documenting AUD using search tools and picklists—varied across patient subgroups within a regional health system.Study DesignCross-sectional study.MeasurementsThis study used EHR data from AUD diagnoses documented (January 3, 2015-May 5, 2023) for adult primary care patients identified as Asian, Black, Latine, or White. Four researchers classified AUD descriptors used to diagnose AUD as “stigmatizing” or “highly stigmatizing.” Stigmatizing AUD descriptors were terms that carry negative connotations, imply blame, moral failing, or character flaws. Highly stigmatizing AUD descriptors were non-standard medical terms with strong negative connotations.ResultsAmong 61 886 AUD diagnoses (18 068 patients: 3.5% Asian, 5.0% Black, 5.9% Latine, 85.6% White; 40.5% women), stigmatizing descriptors were used in 88.5% and highly stigmatizing in 18.6% of diagnoses. Differences across intersectional subgroups were minimal.ConclusionMost AUD descriptors were stigmatizing (1 in 5 highly stigmatizing), with men showing slightly higher prevalence than women.

Introduction

Stigma—defined as labeling, stereotyping, separation, status loss, and discrimination against individuals or groups based on certain characteristics, conditions, or behaviors—remains deeply entrenched in society and contributes to healthcare inequalities.1–3 Addressing stigma is essential for improving health outcomes and advancing health equity. Analyzing descriptors for diagnosing and documenting alcohol use disorder (AUD) in electronic health records (EHRs) may offer valuable insight into how stigma appears in real-world clinical care settings. These descriptors—entered by clinicians using search tools and picklists that incorporate autocomplete, predictive typing, and structured terminology technology—may reflect implicit attitudes and potential biases across patient demographics, including intersectional identities.4,5

Stigma generally originates from societal structures, such as laws and policies, which advantage or disadvantage people based on personal (eg, identity characteristics) or clinical characteristics (eg, substance use disorders). For instance, structural racism has played a significant role in perpetuating substance use stigma in Black populations through discriminatory policies and practices such as the War on Drugs, mandatory sentencing, and disproportionately higher alcohol outlet density in Black neighborhoods.6–9 Also due to structures, such as structural racism and other forms of discrimination, stigma may differentially impact individuals as a result of their other lived experiences. For instance, Intersectionality Theory, rooted in Black Feminist scholarship, highlights how multiple forms of discrimination—such as racism and sexism—interact to shape individuals’ experiences in ways that cannot be understood by examining each factor in isolation.10–13 This likely applies to AUD, as prior research suggests disparities in diagnosis and treatment of AUD across groups based on intersectional racialized, ethnic, and gender identities.14 For instance, Vickers-Smith et al. (2023) found that Black and Latine Veterans were less likely than White VA patients to receive an AUD diagnosis despite similar clinical indicators, and Williams et al. (2016) reported that Black and Latine VA patients had higher rates of clinically recognized AUD than White VA patients yet were less often offered specialty addiction care.15,16 Gender-based disparities are also evident: women with AUD often experience longer delays before entering treatment and face heightened stigma related to caregiving roles.17

Health technologies may exacerbate (or potentially reduce) stigma. For instance, EHRs document medical assessments, diagnoses, and treatments using multiple descriptors that can carry different levels of stigma. For example, consistent with stereotypes portrayed by the media in the United States, Black patients are 2.5 times more likely than White patients to have negative descriptors like “aggressive” or “non-compliant” in EHR progress notes.18–20 Specific to AUD, stigmatizing descriptors such as “abuser” and “habit” found in EHRs reinforce harmful notions about people with AUD, especially minoritized patients, portraying them as solely to blame for their addiction rather than addressing systemic barriers to care.21–23 While stigmatizing language is not the only factor contributing to the stigma surrounding AUD, it is a modifiable element within healthcare systems. However, research on stigmatizing language in structured EHR data elements and search fields is limited.

Further, stigmatizing language—though definitions may change over time and are not universally agreed upon—has been shown to result in increased perceptions that individuals deserve punishment, blame, and that substance use is under personal control.24,25 Stigmatizing language may lead clinicians to adopt more punitive treatment approaches or be less sympathetic toward some patients.24,26 In contrast, using scientifically accurate, person-centered language that affirms self-worth positively influences patients’ healthcare experience.26

This study examines stigmatizing language used to document AUD in EHRs. We focused on text descriptors entered and selected by clinicians from EHR search tools and picklists that incorporate autocomplete, predictive typing, and structured terminology. Specifically, we analyzed terms chosen by clinicians at the time of AUD diagnosis within a large regional health system to: (1) identify stigmatizing and highly stigmatizing AUD descriptors and (2) describe patterns of stigmatizing language, overall and across subgroups based on intersections of race, ethnicity, and sex.

Methods

Study setting

This cross-sectional study used secondary Epic EHR data from Kaiser Permanente Washington (KPWA), an integrated health system that provides insurance for about 350 000 patients and medical care through its 33 primary care sites in Washington State. Further, as part of integrated behavioral health care within primary care, KPWA has implemented annual population-based alcohol screening.27 This study was approved by KPWA Health Research Institute’s Review Board, with waivers of consent and HIPAA authorization for use of existing EHR data.

Sample

The sample includes all patients who were 18 or older and whose race or ethnicity in the EHR was reported as Asian, Black, Latine, or White (Figure 1) with AUD diagnoses documented during primary care visits (January 3, 2015-May 5, 2023). Patients could have one or more AUD diagnoses over the study period, and each AUD diagnosis was considered an observation. Diagnoses assigned to patients with race or ethnicity documented as Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander (NH/PI) and American Indian/Alaska Native (AI/AN), Other, or Unknown were excluded due to small numbers, and for subsgroup analyses to have clinical relevance.

Flow diagram illustrating inclusion and exclusion criteria for the analytic sample of primary care patients with documented alcohol use disorder diagnoses.

Measures

Race or ethnicity

Race and ethnicity were extracted from the EHR and combined into a single variable with the following categories: Latine, non-Latine Asian, non-Latine Black, or non-Latine White (hereafter Asian, Black, Latine, and White, respectively). Patients who reported Latine were assigned to the Latine category regardless of other reported races, and patients reporting multiple races were assigned to their reported race group with the smallest count within the study sample, consistent with other epidemiological studies.28,29 We assigned individuals to a single race category to permit analysis of 8 intersectional groups (described below).

Sex

Sex was extracted from the EHR and typically represented sex as identified on legal forms.

Groups representing intersections of race, ethnicity, and sex

Race, ethnicity, and sex were combined to create a composite variable for 8 intersectional groups: Asian women/men, Black women/men, Latine women/men, and White women/men. These 8 groups were constructed as a proxy for the distinct lived experiences patients may encounter both within he healthcare system and in broader sociopolitical contexts, consistent with Intersectionality Theory.12,13,30

Primary outcome: stigmatizing AUD descriptors

The primary outcome was the presence of stigmatizing text descriptors—terms or phrases in medical documentation that convey negative connotations, reinforce stereotypes, or imply moral failing or personal blame related to a patient’s condition—used by clinicians when documenting AUD diagnoses. At KPWA, clinicians document diagnoses by entering free-text (eg, “alcohol”) into Epic’s EHR search box, which uses autocomplete and predictive typing to display a structured “picklist” of diagnostic text descriptors linked to International Classification of Diseases, Ninth or Tenth Revision (ICD-9 or ICD-10) codes supplied by a third-party vendor. Each ICD code may correspond to multiple text descriptors (Table S1), which vary in their use of stigmatizing vs non-stigmatizing language. These descriptors are visible to all clinicians with access to the patient’s record and may also appear in billing documentation. Although patients can view their diagnosis displayed as the ICD name (eg, “Alcohol use disorder, mild”) in their after-visit summary, they cannot see the exact text descriptor—including potentially stigmatizing terms—used by the clinician to select the ICD diagnosis. These text descriptors reflect a spectrum from stigmatizing to non-stigmatizing language used in clinical practice and are not unique to this specific healthcare setting or EHR platform.

To classify text descriptors selected by clinicians as stigmatizing or non-stigmatizing we used a 4-coder review process, guided by educational resources from the National Institute on Drug Abuse31 and Shatterproof, a national non-profit organization aimed at ending addiction stigma32 (Table S2). First, 4 researchers with expertise in AUD (K.A.H., E.C.W., J.E.G., K.A.B.) independently coded 25 most commonly used AUD diagnostic text descriptors as either stigmatizing or non-stigmatizing. The lead author summarized the coders’ decisions, reviewed them with the co-authors, and applied a majority-rule coding framework with the following guidelines.

Descriptors were coded as stigmatizing if they used terms such as abuse, alcoholic/alcoholism, slang, or dependence. For example, “alcohol abuse” and “alcohol dependence” were stigmatizing despite being used in ICD-10 and DSM-IV terminology.33

Next, the researchers independently coded the remaining diagnostic descriptors using these rules. Discrepancies were resolved through masked consensus review, resulting in a final binary classification of each descriptor as stigmatizing or non-stigmatizing (Table S3A and B).

Secondary outcome: highly stigmatizing AUD descriptors

Because stigmatizing language remains embedded in standard medical terminology—for example, the DSM-IV includes diagnoses such as “alcohol abuse”—we used the same coding process described above to create a secondary subsetted measure that identified highly stigmatizing language. However, for this measure, we restricted inclusion to terminology that falls outside of standard medical classifications (Tables S3C and D). Therefore, highly stigmatizing AUD descriptors are non-standard medical terms or phrases that carry negative connotations, reinforce stereotypes, or imply blame, moral failing, or character flaws related to a patient’s condition.

Analyses

Descriptive statistics

We described the demographic and clinical characteristics of patients with documented AUD, clustering by AUD diagnosis at the patient level, both overall and across racialized or ethnic subgroups. Chi-square tests were used to test for differences in age, sex, and insurance type across subgroups.

Primary analyses

All analyses were conducted at the observation (AUD diagnosis) level. We estimated the unadjusted proportion of documented stigmatizing AUD descriptors among AUD diagnoses using a generalized estimating equation (GEE) model with a logit link and robust “sandwich” standard error estimation to account for repeated diagnoses nested within patients. We first identified the proportion (95% CIs) of stigmatizing descriptors in the full sample of AUD diagnoses. We then identified these metrics across subgroups based on the intersection of race, ethnicity, and race. This intersectional approach allowed us to capture (in proxy) structural inequities and social dynamics that are often missed in analyses that consider race or sex independently.30 Marginal standardization provided point estimates and 95% CIs, with Wald tests for statistical comparisons across groups. Secondary analyses repeated the main analyses but assessed “highly stigmatizing” AUD descriptors as the outcome of interest.

We performed a priori power analyses to estimate the precision of estimates based on anticipated 95% CIs, assuming a stigmatizing descriptor of 30%34 that showed within the estimated size of the smallest minoritized subgroup (N = 217; expected 95% CI: 24.3%-36.4%) and the largest minoritized subgroup (N = 614; expected 95% CI: 26.5%-33.7%), our sample size provides adequate precision to support comparisons across intersectional groups.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

The sample included 18 068 primary care patients with documented AUD; 3.5% were Asian, 5.0% were Black, 5.9% were Latine, 85.6% were White, and 40.5% were women. While sex distributions varied across racialized or ethnic subgroups, men were the majority in all subgroups. White patients were slightly older and had the highest proportion of Medicare insurance compared to other groups (Table 1).

Stigmatizing AUD descriptors

Among 61 886 documented AUD diagnoses, 88.5% (95% CI: 88.2-88.8) had documented stigmatizing AUD descriptors. Diagnoses assigned to men had a slightly higher proportion of stigmatized descriptors at 88.9% (95% CI: 88.5-89.3) compared to those assigned to women at 88.0% (95% CI: 87.5-88.4, P < .001; Table 2: Model of Primary Outcome). There were no significant differences across racialized or ethnic groups or in subgroups representing intersections of race, ethnicity, and sex. While the point prevalence of stigmatized descriptors used for diagnoses assigned to Asian women, Asian men, and Black women (87.1%, 87.3%, and 87.7%, respectively), was lower compared to Black men (90.5%), these differences were not significantly different (Table 2: Model of Primary Outcome).

Highly stigmatizing AUD descriptors

Of 61 886 documented AUD diagnoses, 18.6% (95% CI: 18.2-19.0) were documented with highly stigmatizing descriptors. Diagnoses assigned to men had a higher proportion, 19.2% (95% CI: 18.7-19.7), compared to women, 17.8% (95% CI: 17.3-18.4; P < .001; Table 2: Model of Secondary Outcome). Patterns across intersectional groups were consistent with results from the primary outcome.

Discussion

In a large primary care sample, this study identified and described the prevalence of stigmatizing AUD descriptors used to document AUD diagnosis in EHRs, both overall and across intersections of race, ethnicity, and sex. Overall, 88.5% of AUD text descriptors used to document diagnoses were stigmatizing, including 18.6% (nearly 1 in 5 AUD diagnoses) that were documented with a highly stigmatizing descriptor. These findings highlight a prevalent problem in documentation that may perpetuate stigma toward individuals with AUD.

While not statistically significant, there were nominal differences in stigmatizing and highly stigmatizing AUD descriptors across intersectional identities. For instance, Asian women, Asian men, and Black women had the lowest estimated proportions of stigmatizing descriptors. These findings align with prior research among a nationally representative sample of adults with AUD, which shows that Black and Latine individuals are more likely than White to experience perceived stigma.35 Stigma creates barriers to mental health and substance use services, particularly among Black and Latine women who face heightened stigma due to cultural and intersectional factors.10,36–38

Despite prior evidence that women face greater substance use stigma, especially around motherhood,17 and national surveys show higher substance use-related judgment and personal blame,39 the current study found a small difference between men and women, such that men were more frequently described with stigmatizing descriptors. Since this study occurred in primary care settings, various factors—such as societal gender norms, perceived dangerousness, and gendered expectations of healthcare providers—may have contributed to these findings.1,17,39

Taken together, our findings suggest that stigmatizing language is commonly used to document AUD in primary care patients. Addressing stigma in medical language likely requires attention from organizations responsible for disease classifications, informatics organizations (eg, EHR vendors), and data purveyors (eg, contractors providing text descriptors for diagnoses in EHRs). Each of these entities contributes to the persistence of stigmatizing language in clinical documentation, and all must be engaged if we are to meaningfully reduce AUD-related stigma in EHRs. Encouragingly, there are ongoing efforts to reduce alcohol-related stigma.40 For instance, the DSM-5 made significant changes to reduce stigmatizing language compared to DSM-IV, particularly with the elimination of “abuse” and “dependence” labels26 in 2013, 2 year prior to this study.

While broader systemic efforts are needed, our findings also point to opportunities for organizational-level reforms within healthcare systems. The widespread use of stigmatizing descriptors—nearly 9 in 10 AUD diagnoses—signals a persistent issue within EHR infrastructure and clinical workflows, even in the absence of major differences across intersectional subgroups. Addressing this problem may require coordinated efforts across informatics, provider training, and documentation standards to update language consistent with updated terms for AUD and related conditions. Specifically, health systems and EHR vendors may consider auditing diagnostic picklists to ensure that terms are consistent with current DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for AUD, which no longer use abuse or dependence since 2013,41 as well as recent updates in alcohol-associated liver disease terminology.42 Removing outdated terms and implementing person-centered alternatives aligned with current clinical guidelines would further reduce stigma.41,43 Complementary strategies might include clinician education and organizational policies that support respectful, non-stigmatizing language. This study provides foundational evidence to guide these reforms and promote more patient-centered care for individuals with AUD.

Limitations

This study has important limitations. First, stigmatizing AUD descriptors were defined by coding from 4 alcohol researchers, with 69.9% of stigmatizing descriptors coming from existing DSM-IV terms (“alcohol abuse” and “alcohol dependence”). Second, AUD text descriptors used by providers in EHRs are drawn from pre-generated lists, frequently arranged alphabetically or based on provider preferences, which may impact which text descriptors providers choose. Third, patients in this study could have multiple AUD diagnoses documented in their EHR, but the study was not designed to evaluate whether those with more diagnoses are more likely to have AUD documented with stigmatizing or highly stigmatizing descriptors. Fourth, ∼85% of documented AUD diagnoses in our sample involved White patients, reflecting the state where it is located. Although precision was lower in the smallest subgroups (eg, ∼12% points for Asian women), our analyses showed that subgroup sample sizes were sufficient to support comparisons, with narrower intervals observed in the larger subgroups (eg, ∼7% points for Latine men, ∼2% points for White men). Additionally, the sample excluded 3 smaller minoritized racial groups (NH/PI, AI/AN, and multiracial), preventing us from exploring stigmatizing AUD descriptors in these vulnerable populations. Fifth, relying solely on EHR data poses challenges, as EHR data on sex, race, and ethnicity do not fully capture the complex factors contributing to structural discrimination and intersecting identities. Further, diagnostic descriptors are typically not shown directly to patients, their presence in provider-facing sections of the EHR may still influence clinician perceptions, communication, and decision-making. Thus, even when patients do not view the language, stigma can persist through provider behavior and documentation practices. Although our study did not assess these downstream effects, future research should examine how exposure to stigmatizing terminology affects both patient experience and provider behavior. Lastly, we were unable to account for individual provider characteristics or clinic-level variation, despite the fact that there were overall high rates of stigmatizing language in the dataset. As such, we cannot disentangle whether differences in stigmatizing languages are primarily driven by patient demographics or contextual or provider-level factors. Future work should explore how provider demographics, clinical role, and setting may influence the use of stigmatizing language in documentation, particularly within diverse patient populations.

Despite these limitations, the study has several strengths. It innovatively used EHR data to assess potential provider bias in documenting AUD diagnoses and is novel in evaluating stigma within text descriptors selected by providers when assigning diagnoses in routine practice. Future research should further explore the use of stigmatizing language in EHRs. For example, the use of stigmatizing descriptors could vary by patient age or co-occurring medical conditions. The persistence of stigmatizing descriptors could also reflect factors such as the duration of an AUD diagnosis, the frequency with which it is documented, or the number of treatment episodes. Finally, it will be important to examine whether changes in terminology in DSM-5 and other alcohol diagnoses alone, whether changes in documentation processes, or whether broader structural interventions—including modifications to EHR systems and clinician education—are needed to meaningfully reduce stigmatizing language in clinical care.

Conclusion

In this study, almost 90% of AUD text descriptors used when documenting AUD diagnoses were stigmatizing, and nearly 1 in 5 were highly stigmatizing. Men had a significantly higher prevalence of stigmatizing descriptors compared to women, but the magnitude of the difference was small and largely reflected the large sample sizes. Only nominal differences across intersectional subgroups were observed. Given the high prevalence of stigmatized language in EHRs, this study emphasizes the need to reduce stigmatizing language associated with AUD and highlights an opportunity for EHR improvements to reduce stigma by removing stigmatizing descriptors.
