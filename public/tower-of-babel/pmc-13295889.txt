Effects of different numbers of micro-osteoperforations on acceleration of orthodontic tooth movement and alveolar bone remodeling in rats
Authors: Yingyu Chen, Mao Liu, Fan Yang, Yi Lu, Di Jiang, Bin Wu, Bin Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract‌BackgroundMicro-osteoperforations (MOPs) have been confirmed to accelerate orthodontic tooth movement (OTM). However, the optimal number of perforations and the associated changes in alveolar bone structure, composition, and biomechanical properties under different numbers of perforations remain unclear.MethodsSeventy-two Sprague-Dawley rats undergoing OTM were divided into four groups: OTM (0 MOPs), 2MOP, 3MOP, or 4MOP. Rats were euthanized on Days 3, 7, or 14, and maxillary samples were analyzed using micro-CT, Raman spectroscopy, and nanoindentation to assess bone morphology, composition, and biomechanical properties.ResultsMOPs accelerated OTM, with 3MOP and 4MOP showing superior efficacy to 2MOP. Alveolar bone in 3MOP and 4MOP groups exhibited initial resorption followed by regeneration from Days 3–14. Mineral-to-matrix ratios decreased then increased from Days 3–14 across all groups, while carbonate substitution levels showed the opposite trend. Elastic modulus and hardness followed a similar decreasing-increasing pattern. No consistent pattern of intergroup differences was observed in bone microstructure, chemical composition, or biomechanical properties.ConclusionsMOPs can accelerate OTM, with three MOPs potentially offering a more effective balance between therapeutic efficacy and surgical intervention. No additional acceleration effect was observed in the four-perforation group. Under higher trauma levels (≥3 MOPs), alveolar bone demonstrated a time-dependent transition (initial resorption followed by regeneration) across microstructural, compositional, and biomechanical parameters.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08498-x.Keywords: Orthodontic tooth movement, Micro-osteoperforations, Alveolar bone, Biomechanical properties

Introduction

Traditional orthodontic treatment usually takes about two years to complete [1], whereas extended treatment duration can lead to several adverse effects, including enamel demineralization, dental caries, and root resorption [2–4]. Therefore, accelerating tooth movement and shortening treatment time have become a common goal among both orthodontists and patients.

Micro-osteoperforation is a minimally invasive surgical technique used to accelerate OTM, which uses micro screw perforations that can be performed independently by the orthodontist, with minimal postoperative discomfort and mild pain [5].

The effect of MOPs in accelerating OTM can be influenced by multiple factors. A study evaluating the impact of MOP intervals (4, 8, and 12 weeks) on OTM demonstrated that frequent repetition of MOPs throughout treatment significantly enhanced the tooth movement rate [6]. Bavikati et al. [7] also suggested that performing MOPs at 6–8 week intervals may optimize the acceleration effect.‌ Additionally, other researchers explored varying perforation depths and found that three MOPs at a depth of 4 mm were clinically effective in increasing the rate of tooth movement [8]. Furthermore, the mechanism underlying MOP-accelerated tooth movement may be associated with the regional acceleratory phenomenon (RAP) theory proposed by Frost [9], which indicates that surgical disruption of bone tissue reduces localized bone density, while traumatic stimulation activates cellular regulatory mechanisms, thereby accelerating bone remodeling and enhancing the rate of tooth movement.‌ Based on previous studies, we hypothesized that alterations in MOP intervals and depth result in changes in the cumulative trauma volume, which can ultimately influence the acceleration effect of MOPs on OTM. As a way to regulate trauma accumulation, changing the number of MOPs may also be a potential strategy to regulate the acceleration of tooth movement. However, few studies have been conducted in this area.

Previous research on the mechanisms of micro-osteoperforations in accelerating OTM has predominantly focused on the cellular and molecular mechanisms. Studies have demonstrated that MOPs upregulate inflammatory cytokine expression, promote osteoclastogenesis, and stimulate periodontal ligament cell activity [10, 11]. However, tooth movement is closely associated with alveolar bone remodeling. Most current studies on bone remodeling during OTM primarily rely on single-scale morphological assessments—typically based on micro-CT and often lack evaluation of alveolar bone composition and mechanical properties. Importantly, chemical and micromechanical parameters may change before significant microstructural remodeling occurs. These changes can be captured using Raman spectroscopy and nanoindentation techniques [12]. Therefore, there is a compelling need to perform multi-scale investigations integrating multimodal experimental data to comprehensively characterize alveolar bone morphology, composition, and mechanical behavior, thereby elucidating bone remodeling during MOPs-accelerated tooth movement.

Studies have shown that microdamage acts as a biomechanically functional component of bone architecture. Specifically, even limited microdamage accumulation in cancellous bone may significantly compromise the biomechanical integrity of bone [13]. As traumatic procedures, MOPs may also influence the material properties of alveolar bone. ‌Experimental evidence from mouse models indicates that higher MOP frequency is associated with enhanced OTM, accompanied by reduced bone mineral density and bone mass near the target dentition [14]. Additionally, other studies have analyzed the degree of MOP-induced intraosseous effects by mechanical testing to quantify the damage and short-term bone adaptation occurring in and around the site of MOPs [15]. However, the impact of MOPs on the biomechanical properties of alveolar bone during OTM remains unclear.

Moreover, biochemical and structural modifications in bone composition have been demonstrated to significantly alter its biomechanical properties [16, 17]. The mechanical behavior of bone is directly influenced by its chemical constituents, particularly through variations in organic matrix components (e.g., collagen) and mineral content, both of which contribute to overall skeletal strength [18]. Based on the above findings, we hypothesize that in the process of OTM, MOPs may induce alterations in the chemical composition of alveolar bone. These changes, combined with modifications in the biomechanical properties of the bone, may underlie the mechanism by which MOPs enhance OTM. However, there is currently no research directly linking changes in chemical composition to adaptations in biomechanical properties within MOP-treated alveolar bone.

In the research on the compositional analysis of bone tissue, Raman spectroscopy has been widely utilized ‌as it can‌ generate composite images integrating both molecular and morphological information [19]. Additionally, in investigations into biomechanical properties of bone tissue, nanoindentation can probe mechanical properties in tissue volumes as small as individual lamellae, allowing analysis of local variations in bone material properties [20]. The integration of these two techniques provides a comprehensive approach to assess bone tissue quality [21, 22]. Therefore, this study employed these two techniques to analyze changes in the chemical and mechanical properties of alveolar bone. Combined with micro-CT-derived morphological data, these data enabled a comprehensive evaluation of MOPs on OTM and alveolar bone remodeling in rats.

In this study, we hypothesized that increasing the number of MOPs exacerbates trauma-induced remodeling of alveolar bone, subsequently modifying the biomechanical properties and biochemical composition of alveolar bone. To test this hypothesis, we created a gradient of bone trauma by varying the number of MOPs and investigated their effects on OTM and bone tissue in rats. This study represents the first simultaneous quantification of alveolar bone structure, composition, and local material properties under varying numbers of MOPs. It examined, within a certain range, the relationship between the number of micro-osteoperforations and the efficiency gain in tooth movement. This work aimed to provide a more in-depth understanding of how MOPs accelerate tooth movement and to offer preliminary evidence for determining the optimal number of MOPs required to enhance the efficiency of orthodontic treatment.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

All the animal procedures described in the study were approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Nanjing Medical University (Approval ID: IACUC-2205040).

Experimental group

This study used 72 male Sprague-Dawley rats (240–260 g; 8 weeks old; purchased from Zhejiang Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd.). The rats were randomly assigned to four groups (18 rats/group) based on the number of MOPs applied: OTM + 0 MOPs (OTM group), OTM + 2 MOPs (2MOP group), OTM + 3 MOPs (3MOP group), and OTM + 4 MOPs (4MOP group). During modeling, the rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 3% sodium pentobarbital (1.5 mL/kg). On 3, 7, and 14 days post-orthodontic force application, 6 rats per group at each time point were sacrificed for subsequent micro-CT imaging, Raman spectroscopy, and nanoindentation analyses. Based on previous research [14], the region of interest (ROI) for this study were defined. Figure 2 illustrates the protocol for rat micro-osteoperforations modeling used in this study.

Modeling of rat tooth movement and micro-osteoperforations

Animals were maintained under standardized conditions (23–25 °C, 50–65% humidity, 12-h light/dark cycle). After a 7-day acclimatization feeding, OTM modeling was performed in all rats. The left maxillary first molars were ligated to the maxillary incisors using 0.25 mm nickel-titanium (NiTi) orthodontic springs inducing 50 g of force. For MOPs groups, micro screw implant (Protect, China) with a spherical resin stop (3M, USA) was employed to create standardized perforations with depth controlled at 1 mm and maximum perforation diameter at approximately 0.5 mm (Fig. 2, A). Previous studies have demonstrated that palatal micro-osteoperforations around the first molar effectively accelerate tooth movement and induce periradicular bone remodeling [10, 14], based on this evidence, this study adopted palatal perforation to enhance operational feasibility and improve control over perforation site localization and depth. The detailed MOP protocol is illustrated in Fig. 2, B. After surgery, the orthodontic appliance was checked daily to ensure proper fixation and prevent dislodgement. To minimize postoperative discomfort and prevent the hard food from interfering with the appliance, a soft diet was provided daily.

Micro-CT scanning and radiographic data analysis

High-resolution micro-CT scanning (Scanco Medical, Switzerland) was performed on the harvested maxillae (55 kV, 72 µA; voxel size: 15.6 μm). The tooth movement distance was quantified using Dataviewer software (Bruker, USA, Version 1.5.4.0) by measuring the linear displacement between the distal crown contour of the first molar and mesial contour of the second molar, with the two teeth being initially in contact (0 mm displacement) [23]. The velocity of OTM was calculated by dividing the total displacement by the duration of force application (days).

Bone morphometric analysis was performed using CTan software (Bruker, USA, Version 1.15.4.0). A uniform region of interest (ROI) was defined as follows: it was located in the furcation area of the rat’s first molar. Horizontally, the ROI was positioned in the central region of the five roots. Vertically, its upper boundary was set at the plane 1 mm apical to the cemento-enamel junction (CEJ), from this plane, it extended 35 consecutive micro-CT slices further apically to define the lower boundary. Within these boundaries, a cubic region measuring 35 × 35 × 35 voxels (total height: 0.546 mm, given a voxel size of 15.6 μm) was delineated (Fig. 1, D-E). This three-dimensionally defined region constituted the final ROI and served as the volumetric basis for subsequent bone morphometric analyses. This study employed a multimodal complementary design, to maintain colocalization of the testing regions, the areas analyzed by Raman spectroscopy and nanoindentation were kept consistent with the region of interest defined by micro-CT. The evaluated parameters included bone volume fraction (BV/TV, bone volume/tissue volume), total porosity (Po(tot)), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), and trabecular thickness (Tb.Th). Three-dimensional reconstruction of tooth movement was conducted using Mimics software (Materialise, Belgium, Version 21.0). Micro-CT datasets were imported into the software, followed by segmentation of dental structures and alveolar bone through manual masking to generate three-dimensional models.

Raman spectroscopy and nanoindentation sample preparation

For each group at each time point (n = 6), the specimens were first subjected to Raman spectroscopy analysis, followed by nanoindentation testing in the same region.

For Raman analysis, specimens were sectioned using an IsoMet low-speed saw (Buehler, USA) equipped with a diamond blade (Shenyang Cogent, China) positioned 1 mm apical to the CEJ and parallel to the occlusal plane. During sectioning, the cutting speed was strictly controlled while maintaining adequate irrigation with chilled water to prevent thermally-induced chemical damage to tissue integrity. Following completion of the sectioning protocol, transverse maxillary specimens containing intact crown segments were preserved for subsequent analysis (Fig. 1, C).

Raman spectroscopy testing

Following specimen preparation, compositional analysis of the alveolar bone ROI was performed using a confocal micro-laser Raman spectrometer (HORIBA Scientific, France). Initial localization of ROI was conducted under 10× objective magnification, then the lens was switched to a 50× objective lens with focal length adjusted. Systematic interval sampling along the mesiodistal orientation was implemented within the demarcated region, with three discrete sampling locations selected for each specimen. The analytical protocol utilized a 785 nm excitation wavelength [24], with operational parameters set as follows: laser power at 50% of maximum output, integration duration of 15 s, spectral resolution of 3 cm⁻¹, and spectral acquisition range of 200–2000 cm⁻¹. All Raman spectroscopic measurements were conducted under controlled isothermal conditions (25 °C) within a light-attenuated environment. Upon completion of spectroscopic measurements, the spectral data were subjected to baseline correction and smoothing using Labspec software (HORIBA Scientific, France, Version 7.1.0.1). Two biomechanically relevant parameters were quantified according to established bone tissue protocols: (1) the mineral-to-matrix ratio, defined as the integrated intensity ratio of the ν₂PO₄³⁻ band (430 cm⁻¹) to the sum of amide III bands (1242 cm⁻¹ and 1272 cm⁻¹); and (2) the carbonate substitution index, calculated as the ν₁CO₃²⁻/ν₁PO₄³⁻ peak area ratio (1070 cm⁻¹ to 959 cm⁻¹) [25–28].

Nanoindentation testing

For nanoindentation testing, the specimens were embedded using metallographic cold-mounting resin (Ji’an Metallographic, China). During embedding, the tissue-exposed surfaces of specimens were positioned facing the bottoms of mounting molds (Zhejiang Lixie, China). After complete resin polymerization, the embedded specimens were removed from molds and subjected to surface grinding and polishing using a fully automatic grinding and polishing system (Maige Instruments, China). The specific procedures were conducted as follows: Initially, 600-grit abrasive paper was employed for grinding to remove irregular edges and protruding tissues for 30 s, creating a preliminary flattened surface. Subsequently, sequential grinding was performed with 800-, 1200-, and 2400-grit abrasive papers for 60 s each to achieve progressive surface refinement. This was followed by 60 s polishing with alumina polishing slurry-impregnated red velvet cloth. Throughout the polishing process, the test surface was maintained parallel to the horizontal plane. Finally, the specimens underwent ultrasonic cleaning for 5 min at 37 °C in a dedicated cleaner to complete the sample preparation (Fig. 1, C).

The polished specimens were subjected to biomechanical characterization using a nanoindentation instrument (NanoForce, USA). The nanoindentation test sites were selected in accordance with the locations used in the Raman spectroscopic analysis. ‌Indentation parameters were set as follows:‌ A Berkovich indenter (MicroStar, USA) was used, with indentation points located ≥ 50 μm from the sample boundary and spaced ≥ 3× the contact radius apart. The strain-controlled mode was selected, with a maximum indentation depth of 500 nm, a loading strain rate of 0.05 s⁻¹, a holding time of 30 s, and a Poisson’s ratio of 0.3 [29]. Multiple random points were selected within the region of interest to account for material heterogeneity. Prior to testing, low-load test indentations were performed, followed by microscopic surface inspection. If drag marks or debris were observed, the sample was re-polished. This methodology enabled the determination of both elastic modulus and hardness for the alveolar bone ROI. Subsequent data visualization, including the generation of load-displacement curves, was performed using Origin software (OriginLab, USA, Version 9.9).

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were analyzed and visualized using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad, USA, version 9.0.0). Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons were conducted using two-way analysis of variance (two-way ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Before performing two-way ANOVA, the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were evaluated. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was adopted for all statistical tests.

Results

MOPs increased both the distance and rate of OTM

MOPs significantly enhanced the mean OTM distance in rats (Table 1). Across different stages of tooth movement, the average distance of tooth movement gradually increased from Days 3 to 7 and from Days 7 to 14 with a larger increase observed during the Days 7 to 14 phase. In terms of intergroup differences, the average tooth movement distance in all three MOP groups was greater than that in the OTM group. Additionally, the average tooth movement distances of the 3MOP and 4MOP groups were close to each other on Days 3, 7, and 14, and both exceeded those of the 2MOP group. (Fig. 3, A). On Day 3, no statistically significant difference in mean OTM distance was observed between the OTM group and the 2MOP group (p = 0.5480). However, the OTM group exhibited a significantly lower value compared to the 3MOP (p = 0.0005) and 4MOP groups (p = 0.0011). Similarly, the 2MOP group also showed significantly lower values compared to the 3MOP (p = 0.0022) and 4MOP groups (p = 0.0039), while no difference was detected between the 3MOP and 4MOP groups (p = 0.9856). By Day 7, the OTM and 2MOP groups remained statistically comparable (p = 0.0889), but both groups showed significantly lower OTM distances than the 3MOP (p = 0.0029) and 4MOP groups (p = 0.0033). The 2MOP group also exhibited smaller OTM distances than the 3MOP (p = 0.0233) and 4MOP groups (p = 0.0223), with no difference between the 3MOP and 4MOP groups (p = 0.9955). On Day 14, the OTM group displayed significantly smaller OTM distances than the 2MOP (p = 0.0044), 3MOP (p = 0.0010), and 4MOP groups (p = 0.0010). The 2MOP group also showed reduced distances compared to the 3MOP (p = 0.0240) and 4MOP groups (p = 0.0549), while the 3MOP and 4MOP groups remained statistically equivalent (p = 0.8020). Micro-osteoperforations similarly increased the mean tooth movement velocity in rats (Supplementary Table S2). During different phases of tooth movement, the average rate of tooth movement was highest during the Days 8 to 14. In terms of intergroup differences, the average rates in all three MOP groups exceeded those in the OTM group. Specifically, the 3MOP and 4MOP groups exhibited comparable average rates, both surpassing the 2MOP group (Fig. 3, B). During Days 0 to 3, no significant difference in mean OTM velocity was observed between the OTM and 2MOP groups (p = 0.5485). However, both groups exhibited significantly lower velocities than the 3MOP (p = 0.0005) and 4MOP groups (p = 0.0011), with the 2MOP group also demonstrating reduced velocities relative to the 3MOP (p = 0.0022) and 4MOP groups (p = 0.0039). No difference was detected between the 3MOP and 4MOP groups (p = 0.9856).

During Days 4 to 7, the OTM and 2MOP groups remained comparable (p = 0.0887), but both showed lower velocities than the 3MOP (p = 0.0028) and 4MOP groups (p = 0.0033). The 2MOP group also showed reduced velocities compared to the 3MOP (p = 0.0233) and 4MOP groups (p = 0.0223), while the 3MOP and 4MOP groups were statistically equivalent (p = 0.9954). From Days 8 to 14, the OTM group had significantly lower velocities than the 2MOP (p = 0.0044), 3MOP (p = 0.0010), and 4MOP groups (p = 0.0010). The 2MOP group showed reduced velocities relative to the 3MOP group (p = 0.0240) but not the 4MOP group (p = 0.0549), and no difference was observed between the 3MOP and 4MOP groups (p = 0.8017). Superimposed 3D reconstructions of tooth movement on Day 14 (vs. baseline) are shown in Fig. 3, C.

Effects of different numbers of MOPs on alveolar bone micro-architecture‌

Varying numbers of MOPs were found to differentially affect alveolar bone microstructure (Supplementary Tables S3–S6). Figure 4, A presents the morphology of the alveolar bone structure within the ROI for each group at different time points. On Day 3, a wider periodontal ligament space was observed in the micro-osteoperforation groups compared to the OTM group. On Day 7, bone resorption persisted, with similar resorption patterns across all groups. By Day 14, bone remodeling was evident, characterized by increased trabecular bone volume in the furcation region, decreased porosity, and narrowing of the periodontal ligament space. Figures 4 B-C and 5 A-B show the bone microstructural parameters of each group at different time points. BV/TV showed no intergroup differences on Day 3. By Day 7, the 3MOP group exhibited significantly lower BV/TV than the OTM (p = 0.0330). On Day 14, the 2MOP group demonstrated reduced BV/TV compared to the 3MOP (p = 0.0106) and 4MOP groups (p = 0.0238) (Fig. 4, B). Po(tot) showed no differences on Days 3 and 7. However, by Day 14, the 2MOP group exhibited significantly higher Po(tot) than the 3MOP (p = 0.0122) and 4MOP groups (p = 0.0266) (Fig. 4, C). On Days 3, 7, and 14, the differences in Tb.Sp among the groups were not statistically significant (Fig. 5, A). Tb.Th in the 4MOP group was significantly higher than in the 3MOP group on Day 3 (p = 0.0230), but no differences were observed on Days 7 and 14 (Fig. 5, B).

Effects of different numbers of MOPs on the chemical composition of alveolar bone

Temporal changes in the chemical composition of alveolar bone exhibited distinct patterns across different stages of OTM, but the influence of varying numbers of MOPs on the chemical composition of rat alveolar bone showed minimal variations (Supplementary Tables S7–S8). Representative Raman spectra are presented in Fig. 6, A. The mineral-to-matrix ratio decreased markedly from Days 3 to 7, followed by recovery from Days 7 to 14. Notably, the 3MOP and 4MOP groups showed pronounced decreases on Day 7 and subsequent increases on Day 14. Although there were some differences in the mineral-to-matrix ratio among the groups at different time points, no clear pattern was observed (Fig. 6, B). Carbonate substitution levels appeared to increase in all groups on Day 7, but similarly, no clear pattern of intergroup differences was found at any time point (Fig. 6, C).

Effects of different numbers of MOPs on the biomechanics of alveolar bone

Biomechanical properties of alveolar bone showed time-dependent changes, with minimal intergroup variation (Supplementary Tables S9–S10). Representative load-displacement curves are presented in Fig. 7, A. Elastic modulus and hardness both exhibited a trend of initial decrease (from Day 3 to 7) followed by recovery (from Day 7 to 14) (Fig. 7, B-C). Overall, the 3MOP and 4MOP groups showed a more pronounced decrease in mechanical properties on Day 7, while by Day 14, the increase in these two groups was more substantial. No consistent pattern of intergroup differences was observed at any time point. In addition, correlation analysis demonstrated a consistent parallel trend between bone chemical composition (mineral-to-matrix ratio) and biomechanical properties (elastic modulus and hardness). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table S11, mineral-to-matrix ratio was positively correlated with both elastic modulus (r = 0.514–0.843) and hardness (r = 0.465–0.871) across all four experimental groups.

Discussion

This study compared the effects of varying numbers of MOPs on OTM and alveolar bone remodeling in rats. Results showed that two perforations moderately accelerated tooth movement, while three and four MOPs achieved superior acceleration, with no additional benefit observed in the 4MOP group. Given that the 3MOP and 4MOP groups demonstrated statistically equivalent acceleration of OTM, and considering that increasing perforation number inherently increases the degree of surgical intervention, applying three MOPs appears to offer the most efficient therapeutic effect in this model. While a direct histological assessment of tissue trauma was not performed in this study, the principle of achieving maximal effect with the least necessary intervention is a cornerstone of clinical practice. These findings indicated that the acceleration effect was not directly proportional to the number of MOPs within the tested range. Notably, the 3MOP and 4MOP groups exhibited remarkably higher movement velocity on Day 3 compared to other groups and their own Day 7 measurements, which was potentially attributable to microdamage-induced reductions in alveolar bone resistance [15]. Consistent with these findings, finite element analyses have corroborated that MOPs enhance initial tooth displacement through modified stress distribution patterns in supporting bone, aligning with our observations of greater initial movement with multiple perforations [30].

As a minimally invasive procedure, the biomechanical acceleration mechanism of MOPs has been partially elucidated through molecular biology perspectives, including inflammatory cytokine upregulation and periodontal ligament cell modulation [31–33]. To further explore the underlying mechanisms of MOP number-dependent effects, this study employed a multiscale experimental strategy to evaluate alveolar bone microstructure, chemical composition, and mechanical properties. Previous studies have demonstrated accelerated tooth movement rates in osteoporotic animal models [34, 35]. Osteoporotic bone is associated with reduced skeletal strength, which pathophysiologically manifests as trabecular microstructural defects and compromised intrinsic material properties of bone tissue [36]. Prior research has shown that bone microperforation leads to decreased alveolar bone density and increased porosity [14, 37]. To further investigate the relationship between bone microperforation and the material properties of alveolar bone, nanoindentation testing was conducted to measure elastic modulus and hardness. The nanoindentation results revealed a consistent pattern in elastic modulus and hardness from Days 3 to 14: elastic modulus decreased on Day 7 and recovered by Day 14 across all groups. However, bone composition is complex, and its strength also depends on bone quality, where minerals and collagen within bone tissue serve as critical determinants of bone quality [38]. Raman spectroscopy revealed time-dependent variations in mineral-to-matrix ratio (v2PO43−/Amide III) and carbonate substitution level (v1CO32−/v1PO43−): mineral-to-matrix ratio decreased on Day 7 followed by recovery on Day 14, with carbonate substitution levels showing inverse trends. This pattern suggests bone resorption dominance on Day 7 (characterized by mineral loss and deposition imbalance) transitioning to reparative remodeling by Day 14, likely driven by inflammatory resolution. In addition, a positive correlation was observed between the mineral-to-matrix ratio and biomechanical properties, indicating that bone chemical composition is associated with its mechanical performance. The findings of this study suggest that alveolar bone tissue undergoes remodeling and reconstruction during distinct phases of tooth movement, thereby inducing alterations in its chemical composition and biomechanical properties, which can be detected through Raman spectroscopy and nanoindentation techniques. The results establish a direct link between the differences in tooth movement rates during different time periods and the composition and biomechanical properties of alveolar bone tissue.

Although this study identified significant intergroup differences in OTM velocity, no consistent patterns were observed in alveolar bone structure, composition, or mechanical properties across groups at the same time points. This may be attributable to the relatively modest differences in the number of MOPs within the experimental design or limitations in measurement sensitivity. Notably, in the 3MOP and 4MOP groups, alveolar bone exhibited a general trend of initial resorption followed by subsequent repair from Days 3 to 14, with changes in chemical composition and biomechanical properties roughly aligning with those in bone microstructure. Specifically, mineral content showed an initial decrease followed by an increase, while elastic modulus and hardness followed a similar trend of reduction and subsequent recovery. This observation, to some extent, suggests a potential correlation among bone morphology, composition, and biomechanical performance. However, this trend was less evident in the OTM and 2MOP groups. Based on previous findings that different numbers of perforations result in varying expression levels of inflammatory factors [14], we speculate that the OTM and 2MOP groups may not have induced sufficient local inflammation, thereby leading to a less pronounced correlation among these parameters. Furthermore, the 3MOP and 4MOP groups exhibited a more pronounced pattern of initial decrease followed by an increase in both mineral-to-matrix ratio and mechanical properties, with more significant bone repair observed by Day 14. This may be associated with MOP-enhanced bone metabolism and new bone formation [33]. Additionally, we observed a substantial increase in tooth movement distance from Day 8 to Day 14. Given that bone repair had already occurred by Day 14, it is inferred that the peak tooth movement rate occurred during this phase, potentially followed by a decline thereafter.

In conclusion, MOP-induced local trauma alters cellular microenvironments, yet this alone does not fully explain the underlying acceleration mechanism. Bone quality, encompassing structural, compositional, and biomechanical characteristics, provides a more comprehensive framework for understanding MOP effects [39, 40]. Our multiscale analysis revealed synchronized temporal patterns of microstructural, compositional, and mechanical changes in 3MOP and 4MOP groups, though intergroup differences at specific phases were not statistically significant. However, in this study, because of the differences in anatomical structure and bone metabolism between rats and humans [41], the conclusions of this study regarding the effects of different numbers of MOPs on accelerating OTM are applicable only to the rat model and cannot be directly extrapolated as clinical recommendations for humans. From a biological research perspective, however, alveolar bone remodeling and osteocyte density in rats are similar to those in humans, and the sequence of bone remodeling events is likewise identical [42–45]. Based on our attempt to characterize the impact of MOPs on alveolar bone remodeling through multiscale experimental approaches, we posit that the rat model retains a certain degree of similarity to humans in this context. Nevertheless, due to differences in skeletal scale between rats and humans, future studies may consider using animal models with anatomical scales closer to humans, such as dogs or pigs [46].

Additional limitations of this study should also be acknowledged. First, the observation period was limited to 14 days, which did not cover the entire process of tooth movement and bone remodeling and may not fully capture the long-term effects of MOPs or potential adverse outcomes such as root resorption. Second, only a single orthodontic force magnitude (50 g) was applied, leaving the potential interaction between the number of MOPs and force level unexplored. Finally, although our multimodal approach characterized MOP-induced bone remodeling at the structural, compositional, and mechanical levels, the underlying molecular mechanisms have not yet been validated. Future studies integrating molecular and cellular analyses are needed to establish a causal relationship among MOPs, changes in bone quality, and acceleration of orthodontic tooth movement.

The present study provides a novel evaluation of the impacts of MOP numbers on OTM by examining bone quality parameters and proposes an optimal number of MOPs for the rat model and offers new insights into chemical and biomechanical remodeling processes. These findings advance our understanding of minimally invasive surgical adjuncts in orthodontics, and the multiscale findings may serve as a theoretical foundation for optimizing acceleration protocols and informing clinical practice in orthodontics.

Conclusions

This study confirmed that MOPs can accelerate tooth movement and demonstrated differential effects of MOP numbers on OTM. In this rat model, both three and four MOPs produced a similarly enhanced acceleration of tooth movement compared with two MOPs or orthodontic force alone, suggesting that within a certain range, the rate of tooth movement may not increase linearly with the number of MOPs. This finding provides new evidence for optimizing and standardizing MOP parameters. As no statistically significant additional benefit in the rate of tooth movement was observed with four MOPs, our findings suggest that three MOPs may strike a more efficient balance between achieving the desired therapeutic efficacy and minimizing surgical intervention. However, this conclusion represents a trade-off between therapeutic efficacy and surgical intervention within a specific animal model, rather than a direct clinical recommendation. Furthermore, at higher perforation numbers (3 and 4 MOPs), alveolar bone exhibited temporally coordinated remodeling across its microstructural, chemical, and biomechanical properties from Days 3 to 14, suggesting that trauma-related multiscale responses of bone tissue may be associated with the extent of the applied trauma.
