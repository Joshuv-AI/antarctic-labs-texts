Characterization of Plasmodium falciparum infections among people living with HIV under antiretroviral and co-trimoxazole chemoprophylaxis in Brazzaville, Republic of Congo
Authors: Jean Claude Djontu, Marcel Tapsou Baina, Jacques Dollon Mbama Ntabi, Claujens Chastel Mfoutou Mapanguy, Armel Landry Batchi-Bouyou, Viny Andzi Elenga, Franck Yannis Kouikani, Arcel Christ Massamba Ndala, Alain Maxime Mouanga, Francine Ntoumi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough interactions between human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and Plasmodium falciparum infections may lead to an exponential rise in their harmful impact on human health, few data exist on the prevalence of Plamodium species, the multiplicity of infection and molecular markers of antimalarial drug resistance, as well as clinical presentation of malaria infections in Congolese people living with HIV (PLWH). The aim of this study was to characterize malaria infection among PLWH receiving antiretroviral treatment (ART) and co-trimoxazole prophylaxis in Brazzaville, Republic of Congo.MethodAs part of follow-up activities, blood samples, socio-demographic and clinical data were collected from 683 PLWH aged from 1 month to 84 years (sex ratio male/female of 0.4) under ART and co-trimoxazole prophylaxis. This heath facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted from January to September 2022 at the Outpatient Treatment Center of the National HIV Program, located in Brazzaville. Malaria infection was diagnosed by microscopy and nested PCR. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNP) were detected by RFLP-PCR for the Pfdhfr, Pfdhps, Pfcrt genes and by sequencing for the PfK13 and Pfmdr1 genes. Plasmodium falciparum genetic diversity was investigated by nested PCR using gene markers (msp1, msp2 and glurp).ResultsAll the Plasmodium infected participants were asymptomatic. By microscopy, 1.2% (8/683) were found to be infected, whereas 9.2% (62/675) carried submicroscopic infections detected by PCR. P. falciparum mono-infection was the most common (69/70, 98.6%), but single separate cases of P. malariae alone, and of co-infection, P. falciparum with P. ovale, were also detected. The mean multiplicity of P. falciparum infection was 2.1 ± 1.09, with 68.1% of infections being polyclonal. Overall, a high prevalence of mutations was found within the Pfdhfr gene: 51I (78.2%), 59R (84.2%), 108N (84.0%); Pfdhps gene: 437G (72.0%, 540E (30.8%), 581G (48.5%); Pfcrt gene: 76T (46.9%) and Pfmdr1gene: 184F (42.1%). None of the Pfmdr1 86Y, 1034C, 1042D, and 1246Y mutant alleles or of the 12 significant mutations known to confer artemisinin partial resistance, were detected.ConclusionThe results of this study show a very low prevalence of malaria infection and a comparatively low genetic diversity of P. falciparum in isolates from PLWH under antiretroviral therapy and co-trimoxazole-based chemoprophylaxis in Brazzaville.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12000-3.

Background

Malaria and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection are two of the world’s major public health concerns, with overlapping distributions in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) where the transmission of both diseases is common. In 2023, there were an estimated 263 million malaria cases and 597,000 related deaths globally. SSA alone accounted for about 246 million cases (95%), and 569,000 (96%) of the total malaria deaths [1]. Currently, 39.9 million people are living with HIV worldwide, with 65% of them living in SSA [2]. Out of 1.3 million newly diagnosed HIV cases in 2023, Western and Central Africa accounted for 190, 000 [2]. AIDS-related diseases caused approximately 630,000 deaths in 2023 globally, with 13% occurring in Western and Central Africa [2].

With a population estimated at 6 million inhabitants in 2023, the Republic of Congo hosts about 140,000 PLWH (26.4% under ART), with the infection rate being higher in women aged 15–49 years (4.5%) compared to men for the same age group (1.9%) [3]. Co-trimoxazole prophylaxis has been added to the HIV care package of the country since 2013 in accordance with 2013 WHO guidelines on the use of antiretroviral drugs for treating and preventing HIV infection, the prevention, screening and management of common co-infections [4].

Malaria was responsible for 56% medical consultations, 43% hospital admissions and 34% of deaths in the Republic of Congo in 2023 [3]. A recent community-based study conducted in Southern Brazzaville reported 20% prevalence of microscopic Plasmodium infection and 73% of submicroscopic infections [5], with a mean multiplicity of P. falciparum infections (MOI) of 2.4 and 2.0 for microscopic and submicroscopic infections, respectively [6]. Other studies, in health facilities in the same setting, revealed a prevalence of microscopic infection ranging from 9.2% to 41.6% [7], and MOI ranging from 2.55 to 3.23 [7, 8]. In addition, P. falciparum isolates circulating in Brazzaville have been shown to harbour a high prevalence of mutations associated with SP resistance [9], and a low prevalence of mutations related to lumefantrine, chloroquine, amodiaquine and quinine resistance [10]. To date, no validated mutations for artemisinin resistance have been reported in this setting [10, 11]. All the above data were obtained from HIV-uninfected individuals exclusively and are thus not generalizable to PLWH.

Malaria and HIV infections can interact synergistically, and this may lead to an exponential rise in their harmful impact on human health [12]. Malaria infection is associated with a temporary decrease in the circulating CD4 + T cell count [13] but strong CD4 + T cell activation and increased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, conditions that provide an ideal micro-environment for HIV viral replication. This could result in increased viral load, worsening the clinical outcome of HIV infection, and promoting HIV transmission [14]. HIV has been shown to contribute to a higher incidence and severity of P. falciparum malaria as CD4 counts decline [15–17]. HIV infection (with CD4 + T cell counts ˂350 cells /µL) can impair immune responses to malaria parasites, and lead to an inability to control parasite clearance and increasing risk of malaria transmission [15]. In fact, malaria risk has been reported to be three-fold higher in HIV-infected individuals with CD4 cell counts of 200–499/µl compared to HIV-uninfected individuals, while HIV-infected individuals with CD4 cell counts less than 200/µL had a five-fold increased risk regardless of the level of malaria endemicity [18].

To reduce the burden of opportunistic diseases (malaria and bacterial infections), co-trimoxazole prophylaxis has been recommended in resource-limited settings since 2006 as an integral component of HIV chronic care packages [19]. WHO 2013 guidelines recommend a daily dose of co-trimoxazole prophylaxis to all PLWH (adults, adolescents and children) in settings where malaria and/or severe bacterial infections are highly prevalent, regardless of their CD4 cell count [1]. Co-trimoxazole is a combination of sulfamethoxazole and trimethoprim with a broad-spectrum antimicrobial agent that targets a range of aerobic gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria, as well as fungi and protozoa such as malaria parasites. A systematic review identified two randomized trials, which found co-trimoxazole prophylaxis to be non-inferior to sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP) for intermittent preventive treatment of malaria in pregnancy, with respect to infant mortality, low birth weight, placental malaria, maternal death and treatment-limiting adverse events [20].

HIV can impair malaria treatment, with a potential selection of general antimalarial-resistant parasites [21, 22], while daily co-trimoxazole prophylaxis may represent a pressure for SP-resistant P. falciparum strains selection. However, three studies have found that co-trimoxazole prophylaxis is associated with a reduction in the risk of malaria in HIV-infected adults and children [23–25]. This study sought to characterize malaria infections in isolates from PLWH receiving ART and co-trimoxazole prophylaxis in Brazzaville, Republic of Congo. The aim is to generate the baseline clinical and epidemiological data which will contribute to improve the management and control of both diseases.

Methods

Study site

This cross-sectional study was carried out from January to September in 2022 in the Outpatient Treatment Center of the National HIV Program, situated in Brazzaville, and that is responsible for ~ 6,000 PLWH. Brazzaville is the political and administrative capital of the Republic of Congo, located along the Congo River, in the south of the country. Since the city has an equatorial climate, it is consistently humid year-round, with an average daily temperature of 25 °C, and between 16 and 21 °C at night. The average yearly rainfall in the city is estimated at 1100 mm. The rainy season, which lasts 9 months, has two maximum periods: one in March-May and another in September-November. Malaria transmission in Brazzaville is perennial with P. falciparum being responsible for 96% malaria cases, and Anopheles gambiae sensus lato, representing 91% of Anopheles mosquito vectors. The entomological inoculation rate in the area was estimated at 47.5 infective bites/person/year in 2022, with an overall sporozoite infection rate of 22.3% [5].

Study population and sample collection

PLWH with well-controlled HIV infection (CD4 count within the last week ≥ 800/mm3, viral load undetectable within the last week, under co-trimoxazole treatment and regularly taking Highly Active Antiretroviral Therapy for at least 6 months), were randomly enrolled on a daily basis from January to September 2022 at the Outpatient Treatment Center of the National HIV Program in Brazzaville. The others inclusion criteria were (1) good adherence to ART therapy and co-trimoxazole prophylaxis, (2) residing in Brazzaville, (3) signing an informed consent form or having the parents or guardians consent for minors (participants under the age of 18 years). Upon obtaining informed consent, information on each participant’s age, residence, sex, use of bed net, malaria symptoms such as fever (temperature above 37.5 °C), vomiting, convulsion, headache and/or loss of appetite, were recorded using a standard questionnaire (Suplementary file). Peripheral blood samples (2–4 mL) were collected in EDTA tubes from each participant. A portion of the blood was used to prepare a thick smear for microscopy, and to measure haemoglobin levels. The remainder was stored at -20 °C until used for molecular analyses. Asymptomatic individuals were defined as those with an axillary temperature below 37.5 °C, no history of fever for the past 48 h and who were infected with plasmodial parasites as determined by microscopy or PCR. Participants with parasites undetected by microscopy but with a positive PCR for Plasmodium spp were defined as having submicroscopic infections.

Diagnosis of malaria and anaemia

Thick and thin blood smears were prepared from each participant’s blood sample and read by two qualified microscopists to determine the presence or absence of plasmodial parasites and parasitaemia as previously described [9]. Haemoglobin level was determined using a hematological analyzer (Cypress Diagnostic, Hulshout, Belgium) and was used for the diagnosis of anaemia as previously described [9, 26, 27].

Assessment of P.falciparum genetic diversity and multiplicity of infection

Parasite genomic DNA was extracted from whole blood using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Cat. No. 51306) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Eluted DNA was stored at -20 °C until used for molecular analysis. Nested PCR targeting the gene encoding 18 S rRNA was performed to identify the different Plasmodium species as previously described by Snounou et al. [28]. The protocol has been described with more details in our previous study [5].

Genotyping of P. falciparum msp-1, msp-2 and glurp genes

Nested PCR of the polymorphic regions of msp-1 (block 2), msp-2 (block 3) and glurp (R2 region) genes were performed as previously described [29, 30]. A primary reaction was performed to amplify the entire block 2, block 3 and R2 region of the msp-1, msp-2 and glurp genes respectively, using specific primers (Table S1). The products generated in the primary amplification were used as templates in seven separate nested PCR reactions, using separate primer pairs (Table S1), targeting the respective allelic families of msp-1 (K1, MAD20 and R033), msp-2 (MSA2, 3D7 and FC27) and glurp. The primary and secondary PCR mix reactions contained 500 nM of each primer, 2.5 mM MgCl2, 100 µM of each dNTP and 0.05 units of Taq DNA polymerase (Qiagen). Amplification conditions for msp-1, msp-2 and glurp are described on Table S1. Positive and negative controls (DNA of P.falciparum wild-type 3D7, and mutant-type Dd2 and HB3 laboratory stains) were included in each series of reactions for quality control as previously reported [6, 8]. Amplicons were separated on agarose gel (Agarose UltraPure™ 500 g): 2.5% (msp-1), 2% (msp-2) and 1.5% (glurp). DNA bands were stained with SYBR Green and visualized by UV transillumination (Gel Doc™ EZ, Bio-Rad), and automatically analyzed by Image Lab Software Version 6.0 (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc, USA). Fragment (allele) sizes were estimated by comparison to DNA ladder (100 bp DNA Ladder, New England). Alleles were considered identical if the fragment sizes were within the range of 10 bp for msp-1 alleles as reported previously [31–35], 20 bp for msp-2 alleles [34, 35], and 40 bp for glurp alleles.

The mean MOI was defined as the mean number of P. falciparum alleles (genotypes) per infected subject, estimated by dividing the total number of genotypes detected using msp-1, msp-2 and glurp gene markers by the number of samples positive for the different genes [8, 34]. Isolates carrying a single P. falciparum genotype at one of three loci (msp-1, msp-2 and glurp) were considered to correspond to a monoclonal infection, while the presence of more than one genotype was considered to indicate a polyclonal infection [36].

Genotyping of P. falciparum Pfdhfr, Pfdhps and Pfcrt genes

Nested PCR followed by the allele-specific restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) analysis were used for the detection of single nucleotide polymorphisms within Pfdfr, Pfdhps and Pfcrt genes as described previously [10, 37–39]. The primary PCR was designed to amplify the region of the Pfdhfr, Pfdhps and Pfcrt genes surrounding the codons of interest, using specific primers (M1 and M5), (R2 and R/) and (Crt1 and Crt2) (Table S2). For the first-round amplification of Pfdhfr and Pfdhps genes, 3 µl of genomic DNA (16.0-96.1ng/µL) were added to a reaction mixture containing 2.5µL of 1mM dNTP, 2.5 µL of each primer (5 µM), and 2.5 µL of MgCl2 (25 mM), 0 0.125 µL for 5 U/µL of Taq DNA polymerase for a final volume reaction of 25 µL. The amplification was performed according to the conditions described in Table S2. The second round of PCR was performed using the specific primers for each of the codons 51, 59, 108 and 164 of Pfdhfr gene, and 437, 540 and 581 of Pfdhps gene as previously described [37, 39](Table S4). Briefly, 1 µL of amplicons from the primary PCR was added to a reaction mixture containing 2.5 µL of 1 µM dNTP, 0.5 µL of each primer (5 µM), 2.5 µL of MgCl2 (25 mM), 0.125 µL for 5 units of Taq DNA polymerase for a final volume reaction of 25 µL. Amplification was performed using a thermocycler (Eppendorf, Germany) following the conditions described in Table S2.

For the first-round amplification of the Pfcrt gene, 2µL of DNA extract (16.0-96.1ng/µL) was added to a reaction mixture (total volume of 25µL) containing 500 nM of each primer, 2.5 mM MgCl2, 100 µM dNTP, and 0.05 units of Taq DNA polymerase (QIAGEN). The same conditions were used for the second round of PCR reaction, except the volume of amplicon that was reduced to 1µL and primers which were specific for the codons 76 of the gene. Amplification was performed according to the conditions described in Table S2. Genomic DNA from mutant laboratory strains (HB3, and Dd2) and wild-type strains (3D7) were included in each PCR round for quality control as reported previously [10, 37, 39, 40]. Digestion reactions were set at a final volume of 50 µL containing 8 µL (56.0-255 ng/µL) of the nested PCR product, enzyme buffer and 0.4 units of the appropriate restriction enzyme (Mnlc, Xmn I, Alu I, DraI, Bstu I, Fok I, Ava II or Apo I (New England Biolabs). Mixtures were digested overnight at 37 ˚C for all the restriction enzymes except Bstu I whose incubation temperature was 60 °C, as recommended by the manufacturer. The amplified products were separated on a 2.5% agarose gel (Agarose UltraPure™ 100 g). After migration, the SYBR Green stained DNA fragments were visualized by UV transilluminator (Gel Doc™ EZ, Biorad). Restriction enzymes and expected fragment sizes for each point mutation are presented in Table S2.

Genotyping of P. falciparum mdr1 and k13 genes

The codons region (80–1300) of the Pfmdr1 gene, flanking mutations N86Y, Y184F S1034C, N1042D, and D1246Y, and the codons region (470–630) of the PfK13 gene, flanking mutations T474I, M476I, A481V, Y493H, T508N, P527T, G533S, N537I, R539T, I543T, P553L, R561H, V568G, P574L, A578S, C580Y, R622I, and A675V, were amplified by nested PCR as respectively described [41, 42], and obtained amplified products were sequenced using Oxford Nanopore technology as described below. In general, the region of the Pfmdr1, and PfK13 genes containing the codons of interest was amplified using the respective primer pairs (A1, A3, F1 and R1), and (K13-1, K13-4) for the first round PCR and (A2, A4, F2 and R2), and (K13-F, K13-R) respectively for the second round PCR (Table S3). For the first reaction, 2 µl of genomic DNA (16.0-96.1 ng/µL) extract was added to a reaction mixture (total volume of 25µL) containing 500 nM of each primer, 2.5 mM MgCl2, 100 µM dNTP, and 0.05 units of Taq DNA polymerase (QIAGEN). The same conditions were used for the second round of PCR reaction. Amplification was performed according to the conditions described in Table S3. Genomic DNA from mutant and wild-type strains (HB3, 3D7, and Dd2) was included in each PCR reaction for quality control. After confirmation of amplification by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis, PCR products were sequenced.

For sequencing, amplicons from PCR were first individually purified using the magnet beads method; the eluted DNA was then quantified with the Qubit (Thermo Fisher, BR DNA kit), and 100 ng of DNA in 7.5 µL was mixed with 2.5 µL of barcodes from the SQK-RBK110.96 kit (Oxford Nanopore). The mixture was incubated, the thermal cycling included 30 °C for 2 min, 80 °C for 2 min, and 4 °C for 1 min. samples were pooled purified using Ampure XP magnetic beads, and eluted in 11 µL. A 1 µL volume of RAP adapter was added to form the final sequencing library. For flow cell loading, 37.5 µL of sequencing buffer and 25.5 µL of loading beads were added. Sequencing was performed on an ONT GridION platform following the manufacturer’s Rapid Barcoding protocol (SQK-RBK110.96).

Bioinformatics analysis

On the Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) sequencing platform, raw data in FAST5 files were systematically converted to FASTQ by Guppy software. The quality control of data in FASTQ format was performed with FastQC to filter out low-quality reads (Q score < 7) [43]. Adapter sequences were trimmed using Porechop [44], and filtered reads were aligned to the P. falciparum 3D7 reference genome (GenBank: PF3D7_0523000 for Pfmdr1, PF3D7_1343700 for PfK13) using Minimap2 (v2.24) [45]. BAM files were sorted and indexed using SAMtools (v1.15) [46], and coverage statistics were calculated with Bedtools [47]. Variant calling was conducted using Medaka (v1.5.0), a consensus calling tool optimized for Oxford Nanopore data. SNPs were annotated using SnpEff with a P. falciparum-specific database [48]. Only variants with ≥ 20× coverage and ≥ 80% allele frequency were retained for downstream analysis. For transparency and reproducibility, all bioinformatics workflows were run using the Galaxy web platform (https://usegalaxy.org) or in a Dockerized Ubuntu 20.04 environment. The complete workflow including tool names, versions, and parameters is available upon request. Sequences were published on GenBank with accession numbers: Pfmdr1 (86–184) (PQ537870 to PQ537904); Pfmdr1(1034–1246) (PQ537891 to PQ537904), and Pfk13 (PQ537851to PQ537869). To identify potential single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), all sequences with a coverage greater than 80% were aligned against their respective reference sequences using MEGA 11 software [49].

Statistical data analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software (version 8). Continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges. The Gaussian distribution of the data was assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk normality test, and variables that passed the normality test were expressed with mean ± standard deviation (SD). Otherwise, the variable was expressed with median. The χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare proportions. Statistical significance was defined by p values < 0.05.

Results

General characteristics of the study population

The socio-demographic and clinical features of the participants in this study are summarized in Table 1. Of 683 PLWH enrolled, 483 (70.7%) [67.2–74.0] were female. Participants’ ages ranged from 1 to 84 years with a median age of 48 years, and 88.6% [85.9–90.8] of individuals aged over 15 years. The median parasitaemia was 5,770 parasites/µL with an interquartile range of 498 − 19,083 parasites/µL. No case of fever was reported, but 67.1% [63.4–70.5] of the participants had anaemia. All participants reported sleeping under a bed net, and were receiving co-trimoxazole and ART, but none of them reported having taken antimalarial drugs prior to enrolment.

Prevalence of malaria parasite infection among study population

All malaria infected participants were asymptomatic. The prevalence of plasmodial infection detected by microscopy was 1.2% [0.6–2.3] (8/683) compared to 9.2% [7.2–11.6] (62/675) with submicroscopic infections detected by PCR (Fig. 1A). P. falciparum mono-infection was the most common, 69/70 (98.6%) [92.3–99.9], but one sample was positive for P. malariae, and one other harboured both P. falciparum and P. ovale. No Plasmodium infection was found in children ˂ 5 years. In addition, no statistically significant difference in terms of the prevalence of microscopic and submicroscopic malaria infections was observed between individuals aged 5–15 years and individuals aged over 15 years (Fig. 1B).

Genetic diversity of P. falciparum in isolates from the study population

Of the 70 P. falciparum positive samples (diagnosed by 18 S rRNA gene nested PCR), 29, 23 and 70 samples were successfully amplified for msp-1, msp-2 and glurp genes respectively (Table 2). Overall, 34 different alleles, ranging in size from 120 to 390 Pb, were detected for msp-1 gene, among which 17 identified as K1, 13 as MAD20, and 4 as RO33 allelic families respectively. In addition, 28 different alleles of the msp-2 gene ranging in size from 200 to 760 bp were also detected (11 and 17 alleles for the FC27 and 3D7 allelic families respectively). A total of 11 alleles ranging in size from 400 to 960 bp were identified for glurp gene.

The frequency of polyclonal infection was 67.6% [55.5–77.0], while the mean multiplicity of infection (MOI) was 2.1 ± 1.1. Among the msp-1 gene allelic families, K1 (48.6%) [31.4–65.6] was more frequent compared to MAD20 (25.7%) [12.7–43.4] and RO33 (25.7%) [12.7–43.4]. For the msp-2 gene, the frequency of the FC27 allelic family (49.0%) [29.2–67.0] was similar to that of 3D7 (51.0%) [32.9–70.8]. Multiple infections with K1 + R033 (34.5%) [19.9–52.6] were the most common among the analyzed isolates, followed by K1 + MAD20 (20.7%) [9.8–38.4], K1 + MAD20 + R033 (17.2%) [7.6–34.5], R033 + MA20 (3.5%) [0.2–17.2]. Also, 43.5% [25.6–63.2] of samples harbored infection with FC27 + 3D7 (Fig. 2).

Mutations on antimalarial drug resistance genes Pfcrt, Pfmdr1, Pfdhfr, Pfdhps and Pfk13 of P. falciparum

Out of the 70 P. falciparum positive isolates, 31 and 19 were successfully genotyped for Pfcrt K76T and Pfmdr1 Y184F single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) respectively while 20 isolates were successfully genotyped for each of Pfmdr1 N86Y, S1034C, N1042D, and D1246Y SNPs. The frequency of Pfcrt and Pfmdr1 alleles is presented in Table 3. The frequencies of Pfcrt K76 wild type and Pfcrt 76T mutant type were 51.6% (16/31) [34.8–68.0] Frequency (ratio) [95 CI uncertainty] and 25.8% (8/31) [13.7–43.2] respectively while mixed Pfcrt alleles (Pfcrt K76 + Pfcrt 76 T) were identified in 7/31 (22.6%) [11.4–39.8] of isolates. The frequency of Pfmdr1 Y184 wild type was 58.0% (11/19) [36.3–76.9] while the Pfmdr1 184F mutant allele was found in 8/19 (42.2%) [23.1–63.7] of the isolates. Discussion

This study aimed to characterize plasmodial infection among PLWH receiving antiretroviral and co-trimoxazole prophylaxis in Brazzaville, Republic of Congo. Our results indicate a very low prevalence of microscopic (1.2%) and submicroscopic (9.2%) Plasmodium infection among this PLWH-population compared to the prevalence previously reported in HIV-uninfected individuals from the same setting (23.5% and 73.0% for microscopic and submicroscopic infections respectively) [5, 7]. This difference could be explained by the care-seeker behaviour of PLWH and daily co-trimoxazole prophylaxis, a fixed-dose combination of trimethoprim plus sulfamethoxazole, used to prevent opportunistic infections in PLWH, which also has antimalarial properties [19]. Although it has been reported to be non-inferior to sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP) for intermittent preventive treatment of malaria in pregnancy [20], daily co-trimoxazole prophylaxis might not completely prevent malaria infection among PLWH, but could help in reducing the prevalence of the infection and maintaining parasite density sufficiently low to prevent any clinical infection. In fact, all Plasmodium infected participants in this study were asymptomatic, harbouring mostly (88.6%) submicroscopic parasitaemias. These asymptomatic PLWH with low parasitaemia often remain undetected, forming a reservoir for malaria transmission and spread. Thus, in addition to daily co-trimoxazole prophylaxis, PLWH can be given ACT at predefined intervals to eliminate the residual malaria parasite.

Although children are at highest risk of developing malaria, no case of Plasmodium infection was detected in under 5-year-old participants in this study, likely due to the very small sample size. In fact, only 12% (n = 14) of the study population were aged under 5 years, one of the age groups found least affected by HIV in SSA, since most HIV-infected children in SSA die very young. Previous studies carried out in Brazzaville reported P. falciparum as the cause of 90%-97% malaria infections [5, 8]. This is in line with the findings from this study reporting 97.2% (69/70) of participants infected by P. falciparum, with only one (1.4%) participant being infected by P. malariae and one (1.4%) participant co-infected with P. falciparum and P. ovale.

P. falciparum is the most virulent and widespread Plasmodium species in sub-Saharan Africa. Despite its crucial roles in malaria severity and drug resistance, comprehensive data on its genetic diversity and multiplicity of infection (MOI) are scarce and not available in PLWH in the Republic of Congo. In the present study, the highly polymorphic regions of P. falciparum msp-1, msp-2 and glurp genes have been genotyped by nested PCR. The results reveal a low genotyping success for the msp-1 (41.4%) and msp-2 (32.8%). These findings contrast with our recent report in HIV-uninfected individuals in southern Brazzaville showing msp-1 and msp-2 genotyping success ranging from 95% to 100%. The difference is likely due to the lower parasitemia present in samples used in this study, since the same method was used for both studies. In general, the degree of genetic diversity (33 different alleles), the mean number of parasite clones per infection (2.1) as well as the prevalence of polyclonal infection (67.6%) were significantly lower in isolates from this study population compared to our previous report from an HIV-uninfected population in the same setting, showing 57 different alleles, 2.9 parasite clones per infection, and 90% polyclonal infection [8]. In addition, multiple infections with K1 + MAD20 + RO33 (17.2%) alleles and 3D7 + FC27 (43.5%) alleles were less frequent in the present study compared to the previous one which reported 46.6% and 51.8% for K1 + MAD20 + RO33 isolates and 3D7 + FC27 isolates respectively. Since both studies were carried out using the same methodological approach, and samples were collected in the same time period, the observed differences could thus be explained by the fact that participants from this study had asymptomatic infection with a higher proportion of submicrocopic infection (reflecting the effectiveness of daily co-trimoxazole prophylaxis) compared to HIV-uninfected participants from our previous study who harbored microscopic parasitaemia and were symptomatic.

The variability in parasite genetic diversity infection profiles has implications for treatment strategies, as well as the efficacy of antimalarial drugs. Thus, the spread of Plasmodium parasites resistant to antimalarial drugs remains an obstacle to managing and eradicating malaria. P. falciparum isolates from HIV-uninfected individuals in Brazzaville have been shown to harbour a high prevalence of mutations associated to SP resistance [9], a low prevalence of mutations related to lumefantrine, chloroquine, amodiaquine and quinine resistance [10], and no validated mutations for artemisinin resistance [10, 11]. A similar trend was observed in this study which used isolates from PLWH under daily co-trimoxazole prophylaxis. Co-trimoxazole prophylaxis may play a dual role in malaria-HIV interactions. Its anti-parasitic activity may lead to a reduction in the risk of malaria while it may also accelerate the spread of resistance to antifolate drugs, rendering sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP), the only antimalarial drug currently recommended for prophylaxis in pregnancy, less effective. Consistent with this, we were expecting SP resistance-related mutations to be higher in this study population as the result of selection due to daily co-trimoxazole prophylaxis which is in line with reduced in parasite diversity reported above. Even though it was not investigated in this study due to low sample size of genotyped isolates, a previous study has not found any association between P. falciparum msp1, and msp2 genetic diversity and levels of moleclar markers of the parasite resistance to antimalarial drugs. In fact, the frequency of SP resistance-related mutations even tended to decrease in this study compared to previous studies carried out in HIV-uninfected individuals [9], suggesting that the co-trimoxazole prophylaxis might not significantly influence the selection of SP-resistant P. falciparum strains in this setting. This could be explained by the fact that co-trimoxazole is used by a very small number of people compared to the overall population. Thus, even in the current context of relatively high prevalence of SP resistant molecular markers in P. falciparum isolates in sub-Saharan Africa, co-trimoxazole prophylaxis might remain efficient in reducing mortality and morbidity in PLWH in malaria endemic areas.

The present study has several limitations. Firstly, the sample size of genotyped samples was small due to the high proportion of samples with low parasitaemia (submicroscopic parasitaemia) which limited the rate of genotyping success of different analyzed genes. Another limitation arises from significant size variations among msp-1, msp-2, and glurp alleles, potentially introducing bias in amplification efficiency. In cases of multiclonal infections, this bias may result in the preferential amplification of shorter fragments, leading to the loss of longer alleles [50, 51]. This emphasizes the importance of employing advanced tools such as microsatellite analysis and whole-genome sequencing for accurately assessing P. falciparum genetic diversity. The finding of no evidence for selection of dhfr and dhps mutations is reassuring. However, this conclusion is based on genotyping of only 25 to 33 isolates, which may be insufficient particularly in the absence of a comparator. As with the prevalence findings, further investigation including more appropriate control group would help substantiate this conclusion. Additionally, some recent Pfdhps mutations such as I431V, not yet clearly proven to be involved in SP resistance, were not investigated in this study even though it is widely reported in Western Africa and in Cameroon [52].

Conclusion

The results of this study reveal a very low prevalence of malaria infection and a low multiplicity of P. falciparum infection among isolates from PLWH under antiretroviral therapy and co-trimoxazole based chemoprophylaxis in Brazzaville. The frequency of investigated molecular markers of antimalarial drug resistance was not significantly different to that previously reported in HIV-uninfected individuals from the same setting.
