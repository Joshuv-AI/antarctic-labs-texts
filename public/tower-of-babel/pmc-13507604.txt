Multicomponent Peptide‐MXene‐Hyaluronic Acid Hydrogel as a Conductive Scaffold for Enhanced Fibroblast Proliferation and Migration Under Electrical Stimulation
Authors: Offir Loboda, Dana Cohen‐Gerassi, Dor Aaron Goldstein, Or Messer, Bar Favelukis, Ehud Gazit, Maxim Sokol, Lihi Adler‐Abramovich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChronic and acute skin wounds remain a major clinical challenge due to impaired angiogenesis, persistent inflammation, reduced cell proliferation and migration, and a high risk of infection. These pathological conditions highlight the need for advanced biomaterial platforms capable of actively modulating cell behavior through biochemical, mechanical, and electrical cues relevant to the skin microenvironment. In this study, a multicomponent electrically conductive hydrogel scaffold composed of the self‐assembling dipeptide Fluorenylmethoxycarbonyl diphenylalanine (FmocFF), Ti3C2Tx MXene nanosheets, and hyaluronic acid (HA) is presented. The FmocFF/MXene/HA hydrogel integrates structural, electrical, and biochemical functionalities together with a self‐healing capability within a single platform, providing an extracellular matrix‐like fibrous architecture, stable electrical conductivity for controlled stimulation, and HA‐mediated bioactivity to support cellular responses. The MXene nanosheets enhanced the mechanical stiffness of the hydrogel, and formed percolated conductive pathways, while the peptide matrix protected MXene from oxidation. In vitro studies confirmed excellent cytocompatibility and cell attachment. Importantly, electrical stimulation at physiologically relevant voltages, consistent with the endogenous transepithelial potential of the skin, significantly enhanced fibroblast proliferation and accelerated migration across cell‐free regions, demonstrating the scaffold's ability to support electrically stimulated cell activity. These findings establish the FmocFF/MXene/HA composite hydrogel as a promising platform for electrically assisted tissue regeneration.Keywords: biomaterial, biomedical engineering, cell growth, electrical stimulations, fibroblast, hyaluronic acid, mxenes, scaffold, self‐assembling peptide

Introduction

Chronic and acute wounds represent a major clinical challenge, particularly in patients suffering from diabetes, vascular disorders, or compromised immunity [1]. Conventional wound dressings primarily function as passive barriers, preventing infection and maintaining moisture, yet they lack the ability to actively accelerate tissue regeneration [2]. Critically, these materials do not modulate the wound microenvironment and thus do not promote essential processes such as angiogenesis, cell proliferation, and migration [3]. Consequently, there is a growing demand for advanced materials that provide bioactive and multifunctional cues capable of stimulating cellular activity, guiding tissue remodeling, and enhancing the natural repair process [4, 5].

Biomaterial‐based scaffolds are designed to mimic the extracellular matrix (ECM), providing both mechanical support and biochemical cues that regulate cell adhesion, migration, and proliferation [6]. Among these, hydrogels have received significant attention due to their high water content, tunable mechanical properties, and capacity to encapsulate bioactive molecules [7]. However, many conventional hydrogels are mechanically fragile and biologically inert, which limits their regenerative effectiveness in complex wound environments [8].

Self‐assembling peptides have emerged as a powerful class of biomaterials capable of forming supramolecular nanostructures that mimic the ECM [9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Their ability to spontaneously organize into β‐sheet‐rich fibrillar networks makes them highly attractive for tissue‐engineering and regenerative medicine applications [14]. Among these systems, fluorenylmethoxycarbonyl diphenylalanine (FmocFF) is one of the most extensively studied low‐molecular‐weight peptide hydrogelators, known for forming nanofibrillar networks stabilized by π–π stacking and hydrogen bonding. The resulting hydrogels are biocompatible, structurally robust, and can be precisely tuned to meet specific biological and mechanical requirements [15, 16, 17, 18]. A widely used strategy to further tailor the properties of FmocFF‐based hydrogels involves coassembly with other peptides or polysaccharides, enabling modulation of mechanical behavior, biological functionality, and antimicrobial performance [19, 20, 21]. Consequently, peptide‐based hydrogels and scaffolds have been extensively explored across a broad spectrum of biomedical applications, including soft‐tissue regeneration, wound healing, antibacterial activity, and tissue engineering [22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27]. In addition, self‐assembling peptide systems have also been developed for hard‐tissue repair, such as bone and cartilage regeneration, demonstrating their versatility across musculoskeletal applications [28, 29, 30]. Beyond structural and regenerative roles, peptide‐based hydrogels have been engineered for biosensing and bioelectronic applications, where their dynamic and adaptive properties are particularly advantageous [31, 32]. While self‐assembling peptide systems typically form anisotropic fibrillar networks, additional strategies enabling controlled isotropic organization could further expand their applicability toward tissues requiring guided cellular alignment and maturation [33].

Hyaluronic acid (HA), a natural polysaccharide abundantly present in the ECM, plays a central role in tissue repair by regulating hydration, cell migration, cell proliferation, and inflammation [34]. Its incorporation into wound dressings has been shown to accelerate healing in a variety of contexts, including burns, and acute and chronic wounds, due to its ability to maintain a moist environment and promote various cell activities [34]. Building on these advantages, recent strategies combining HA with many types of hydrogels have been explored [35, 36]. Previous studies demonstrated that integrating HA with the FmocFF peptide matrix enhances the hydrogel's mechanical properties while maintaining its biocompatibility [37]. Furthermore, it was shown that FmocFF/HA fibrous hydrogel exerted immunomodulatory effects and promoted bone regeneration in vivo [29].

Under homeostatic conditions, the skin maintains a transepithelial potential generated by ion transport across tight junctions, typically in the order of 10–60 mV [38]. When the epithelial barrier is disrupted following injury, this potential collapses at the wound site, creating lateral endogenous electric fields that drive cell proliferation and directed cell migration, a process known as galvanotaxis [39]. These wound‐induced currents play a critical role in coordinating keratinocyte and fibroblast orientation, accelerating re‐epithelialization, and guiding tissue repair [38]. Harnessing or mimicking these bioelectric cues through electrically conductive scaffolds and controlled stimulation, therefore, provides a rational strategy to enhance wound healing. Applying external electrical stimulation to conductive scaffolds has been shown to accelerate wound closure, enhance re‐epithelialization, and promote angiogenesis [40, 41, 42]. Electrically conductive scaffolds, therefore, can serve a dual role, providing an ECM‐mimicking structure while transmitting bioelectric signals that actively regulate cell behavior [43]. While these approaches show promise, challenges such as cytotoxicity, poor biodegradability, and long‐term instability in physiological environments limit their clinical translation [8]. This has led to increasing interest in developing conductive scaffolds that balance high conductivity with biocompatibility, bioactivity, and structural tunability.

Among emerging conductive nanomaterials for biomedical applications, MXenes, a family of 2D transition metal carbides and nitrides, stand out due to their excellent electrical conductivity, hydrophilicity, and biocompatibility [44]. They comprise a general chemical composition of Mn+1XnTx, where M represents a transition metal, X represents carbon (C) and/or nitrogen (N), T denotes surface functional groups (─O, ─OH, or ─F), and n is an integer. Since their initial synthesis by exfoliation of Ti3AlC2 into Ti3C2Tx nanosheets [45], MXenes have been explored across a broad spectrum of applications, including biomedical engineering [46, 47, 48], energy storage [49], sensing and biosensing [50, 51], ceramic nanocomposites [52], optoelectronics [53], and environmental remediation [54]. Recently, we developed a conductive peptide‐based MXene hydrogel as a piezoresistive pressure sensor with high sensitivity [31], demonstrating the potential of peptides to self‐assemble with MXene nanosheets to create electroconductive and biocompatible hydrogels. We furthermore integrated Ti3C2Tx MXene into a bioelectronic scaffold platform enabling real‐time, label‐free monitoring of cell adhesion and proliferation via electrical impedance spectroscopy, establishing a quantitative correlation between impedance parameters and biological activity [55]. Taken together, MXenes represent multifunctional nanomaterials that bridge the gap between conductivity, bioactivity, and structural support, making them ideal candidates for incorporation into wound healing scaffolds.

Here, we aimed to develop a multifunctional, electrically conductive peptide‐based hydrogel scaffold, capable of supporting cell viability and proliferation while enabling controlled electrical stimulation, for potential wound‐healing applications. For this purpose, we combined the FmocFF peptide, 2D MXene Ti3C2Tx nanosheets, and HA into a single hydrogel using self‐assembly. The composite hydrogel integrates an ECM‐like nanofibrous network formed by the self‐assembled peptides, electrical conductivity conferred by the MXene nanosheets to enable electrical stimulation, and biochemical cues derived from the HA component to promote cellular activities. We systematically evaluate the effects of the MXene concentration and HA molecular weight on the gel homogeneity, mechanical properties, electrical conductivity, and biocompatibility. Furthermore, we demonstrate that the scaffold supports cell viability, proliferation, and migration, and that the application of physiologically relevant electrical stimulation in vitro further enhances regenerative outcomes.

Results and Discussion

Fabrication and Structural Characterization of the Multicomponent Hydrogels

Three complementary materials were integrated into a single supramolecular scaffold using the solvent‐switch method: FmocFF peptide (Figure 1a), HA (Figure 1b), and Ti3C2Tx MXene nanosheets (Figure 1c). Briefly, HA was premixed with an aqueous Ti3C2Tx MXene dispersion, followed by adding FmocFF peptide to trigger the self‐assembly into a stable, 3D, self‐supporting hydrogel (Figure 1d). Ti3C2Tx MXene was produced following a previously described protocol [56], yielding 2D Ti3C2Tx MXene nanosheets with an average lateral size of approximately 1 µm. High‐resolution SEM imaging was employed to investigate the morphology of the different hydrogel formulations, each containing a different composition of the building blocks (Figure 1e–h). Both FmocFF and FmocFF/HA hydrogels displayed highly entangled and continuous nanofibrous networks, with similar morphology detected (Figure 1e–f). The average fibril diameter in FmocFF and FmocFF/HA formulations was measured to be 55 ± 12 nm (N = 50) and 57 ± 12 nm (N = 50), respectively (Figure S1a,b). In contrast, the incorporation of the MXene nanosheets produced a slightly looser network, visible in both the FmocFF/MXene (66 ± 16 nm, N = 50) and FmocFF/MXene/HA hydrogels (68 ± 18 nm, N = 50) (Figures 1g,h and S1c,d). Distinct plate‐like MXene sheets were clearly observed to be embedded within the peptide matrix (Figure 1g,h), indicating successful dispersion of the nanosheets throughout the fibrous scaffold. Overall, the ECM‐like fibrous architecture was preserved across all samples, demonstrating that both HA and Mxene were well integrated without disrupting the nanofibrillar organization.

Backscattered electron (BSE) imaging and Energy Dispersive X‐ray Spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping were performed to evaluate the spatial distribution of MXene nanosheets within the scaffold network (Figures 1i–l and S2). The BSE micrograph (Figure 1i) revealed the characteristic fibrous scaffold morphology together with brighter sheet‐like structures, attributed to the higher atomic number contrast of titanium‐containing MXene nanosheets relative to the surrounding organic matrix, confirming successful MXene incorporation within the scaffold architecture.

EDS elemental mapping further demonstrated homogeneous titanium distribution throughout the scaffold, indicating well‐dispersed MXene nanosheets without significant aggregation or localized accumulation (Figures 1j and S2). Such uniform distribution is important for maintaining continuous conductive pathways while preserving scaffold structural uniformity and favorable cellular interactions. Notably, scaffolds containing 0.3% and 0.5% MXene exhibited a more continuous and overlapping titanium distribution compared to the 0.1% formulation, suggesting enhanced nanosheet interconnectivity at higher MXene concentrations.

Powder X‐ray diffraction pattern of pristine Ti3C2Tx MXene showed a distinct (002) reflection at ≈ 6° (2θ), confirming its layered structure (Figure S3). The (002) peak was absent in powder X‐ray diffraction patterns of FmocFF/MXene, FmocFF/HA, and FmocFF/MXene/HA composite hydrogels, suggesting a successful embedding of MXene nanosheets within the peptide matrix, yielding an homogeneously distributed network.

Interfacial Interactions Analysis of the Multicomponent Hydrogel

One limitation of MXene is its tendency to undergo surface oxidation, which compromises its electrical conductivity and diminishes its functional properties over time [57]. To examine the oxidation of the Ti3C2Tx in the multicomponent hydrogel, high‐resolution X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra of C 1s, O 1s, and Ti 2p were collected for both pristine MXene and the FmocFF/MXene/HA hydrogel after 14 days of their formation. For the C 1s, the pristine MXene spectrum (Figure 2a) displayed characteristic contributions from C─M (C─Ti, ∼281 eV), C─C/C─H (∼284 eV), and minor components from C─O (∼286 eV) and COO (∼288 eV), consistent with the partially oxidized surface termination of Ti3C2Tx [58]. In the composite hydrogel (Figure 2e), the signals from C─O and COO were also detected, corresponding to contributions from HA and peptide groups. The C─M peak remained visible, indicating that the MXene's structural integrity had been preserved within the hydrogel. In addition, a weak satellite feature was detected at 292 eV, assigned to a π–π* shake‐up transition. This feature arises from π–π* electronic excitations in the aromatic groups of FmocFF and confirms the presence of intact conjugated π‐systems [59]. The detection of this π–π* peak demonstrated that after incorporation with HA and MXene, the aromatic moieties of FmocFF had retained their delocalized electronic structure and stacking interactions, which are essential for peptide nanofiber self‐assembly and contribute to the structural stability of the hydrogel network.

(b) Ti2p spectra of pristine MXene. (c) O1s spectra of pristine MXene. (d) Ti2p spectra of pristine MXene over 4 weeks. (e) C1s spectra of FmocFF/MXene/HA hydrogel. (f) Ti2p spectra of FmocFF/MXene/HA hydrogel. (g) O1s spectra of FmocFF/MXene/HA hydrogel. (h) Ti2p spectra of FmocFF/MXene/HA hydrogel over 4 weeks.

In the Ti 2p region, pristine MXene (Figure 2b) showed Ti─C (∼455 eV, ∼461 eV), Ti─C─X (∼457 eV, ∼462 eV), and oxidized TiO2 (∼459 eV). In the composite hydrogel (Figure 2f), Ti─C and Ti─C─X remained detectable, but the TiO2 peak was absent, indicating oxygenation protection of MXene surfaces by the organic matrix.

To further investigate the long‐term stability of MXene within the hydrogel matrix, pristine MXene and FmocFF/MXene/HA hydrogel scaffolds were analyzed by XPS for up to four weeks following fabrication. As shown in the Ti 2p spectra, pristine MXene (Figure 2d) exhibited a progressively increased TiO2 signal around ∼459 eV over time, indicative of ongoing oxidation. In contrast, the FmocFF/MXene/HA hydrogel (Figure 2h) displayed a markedly reduced TiO2 peak intensity throughout the examined period, even after four weeks of storage under ambient conditions. These results suggest that confining MXene within the peptide‐HA network significantly suppresses oxidation and improves the material's long‐term stability.

In the O 1s spectra, pristine MXene (Figure 1c) displayed strong M─O (∼530 eV) and C─O (∼531 eV) features, reflecting surface oxidation. In contrast, the hydrogel spectrum (Figure 1g) showed increased intensity of C═O/C─O (∼524 eV) from peptide and HA functionalities, with no M─O signal, consistent with stabilization of MXene nanosheets within the hydrogel.

Incorporation of MXene into the FmocFF/HA hydrogel matrix provides a protective microenvironment that reduces direct exposure to oxygen and aqueous oxidative conditions. By stabilizing the nanosheets within the supramolecular peptide‐polysaccharide network, the hydrogel effectively mitigates MXene degradation, in agreement with our previous findings [60].

Mechanical Properties and Self‐Healing Behavior of the Multicomponent Hydrogels

Next, we examined the contribution of each component to the mechanical properties of the FmocFF/MXene/HA hydrogel by performing strain‐sweep rheological measurements to assess the storage (G′) and loss (G″) moduli (Figure 3a–c). First, we evaluated the effect of MXene concentration, ranging from 0.1% to 0.7% (w/v), while keeping the HA molecular weight (1000–1250 kDa) and FmocFF/HA ratio (3:1) constant (Figure 3a). All formulations displayed gel‐like behavior within the linear viscoelastic region (LVR), with G′ > G″ at low strains. As the MXene concentration increased, the hydrogel exhibited a gradual increase in the storage modulus (G′), indicating greater rigidity. Although the 0.7% MXene formulation showed the highest modulus, it gelled almost instantaneously, limiting its applicability. Thus, the 0.5% MXene concentration was chosen for further analysis.

(b) Strain‐sweep measurements of hydrogels containing HA at various molecular weights. (c) Strain‐sweep measurements of FmocFF, FmocFF/HA, FmocFF/MXene, and FmocFF/MXene/HA. (d) Continuous step‐strain measurement of FmocFF/MXene/HA demonstrates reversible G′ recovery under alternating low (0.1%) and high (100%) strain, confirming self‐healing capability. (e) Visual demonstration of the self‐healing of the FmocFF/MXene/HA and FmocFF/HA hydrogels.

Next, the effect of HA molecular weight, ranging from 500–750 kDa to 2000–2200 kDa, was examined while keeping the same MXene concentration (0.5%) and FmocFF/HA ratio (3:1) constant (Figure 3b). All hydrogels exhibited solid‐like behavior within the LVR, with G′ consistently exceeding G″ at low strains. Samples containing low (500–750 kDa) and high (1000–1250 kDa) molecular weight HA showed comparable G′ values and similar LVR of up to ∼5 Pa and ∼7% strain, respectively, with G′ and G′′ crossover at ∼10%, indicating that HA molecular weight within this range has a minimal effect on network rigidity. In contrast, hydrogels containing very high molecular weight HA (2000–2200 kDa) displayed a slightly higher initial G′, reflecting increased rigidity (∼6 kPa), but exhibited an earlier decrease in G′ and a crossover with G″ at ∼4% strain, suggesting reduced strain tolerance. Collectively, these results indicate that very high‐molecular‐weight HA enhances rigidity but compromises elasticity, whereas HA in the 1000–1250 kDa range provides an optimal balance between mechanical strength and resilience. This formulation was thus selected for subsequent experiments.

After identifying the optimal MXene concentration (0.5%) and HA molecular weight (1000–1250 kDa), we next compared the mechanical properties of the individual and composite hydrogels: FmocFF, FmocFF/MXene, FmocFF/HA, and FmocFF/MXene/HA (Figure 3c). The FmocFF/MXene hydrogel exhibited the highest storage modulus (∼6 kPa) and a broad LVR extending up to ∼10% strain, indicating a strong, solid‐like network that withstands deformation. The addition of HA (FmocFF/MXene/HA) produced slightly lower G′ (∼5 kPa) but maintained a broad LVR, suggesting that the ternary composite retains robust gel properties while still incorporating the expected bioactive polysaccharide functionality. In contrast, the FmocFF (∼2.5 kPa) and FmocFF/HA (∼2 kPa) hydrogels displayed significantly lower moduli, reflecting weaker and less deformation‐resistant networks.

The pronounced increase in storage modules observed upon incorporation of MXene can be attributed to strong interfacial interactions between the MXene nanosheets and the peptide nanofiber network. MXenes may provide additional physical reinforcement points via hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions with peptide and polysaccharide functional groups, thereby strengthening the hydrogel matrix. Furthermore, the large aspect ratio and high stiffness of MXene nanosheets enable efficient load transfer within the network, thereby improving mechanical strength and resistance to deformation. The fact that the FmocFF/MXene/HA composite retains a relatively high G′ despite the presence of HA suggests that MXene plays a dominant role in reinforcing the structure, while HA could provide bioactive cues and hydration without severely compromising mechanical integrity. From a biological perspective, scaffold rigidity plays a crucial role in regulating fibroblast behavior []. Fibroblasts are mechanosensitive cells that respond to the elastic properties of their substrate, with higher stiffness generally promoting cell adhesion, spreading, and proliferation [62].

To explore the self‐healing abilities of the multifunctional scaffold, a thixotropic step‐strain assay was performed on the FmocFF/MXene/HA hydrogel by alternately applying 0.1% strain (low strain) and 100% strain (high strain) (Figure 3d). At 0.1% strain, the hydrogel exhibited solid‐like behavior with G′ ≫ G″ (G′ in the 5–6 kPa range), indicating a well‐formed elastic network. Switching to 100% strain led to an abrupt modulus drop and G′ < G″, consistent with transient network rupture and a quasi‐sol state. Importantly, upon returning to 0.1% strain, the G′ rapidly recovered to its initial plateau in each cycle, again surpassing G″, and this recovery was reproducible over multiple intervals. The rapid, repeatable G′ restoration underscores robust thixotropy and self‐healing properties, attributable to reversible noncovalent interactions (π–π stacking of Fmoc groups, H‐bonding, and electrostatic interactions among HA and MXene terminations [63]) that reform the fibrillar network after large deformations. Such strain‐responsive resilience is advantageous for injectable and load‐bearing wound dressings that must withstand handling and physiological motion while maintaining function.

In addition, the self‐healing ability of the hydrogels was demonstrated by bringing two separate blocks of FmocFF/MXene/HA and FmocFF/HA hydrogels into contact (Figure 3e). After a few minutes of contact, the two pieces adhered and formed a stable junction, allowing the composite structure to be manipulated as a single unit.

Electrical Performance of the Multicomponent Hydrogels

The electrical properties of the FmocFF/MXene/HA composite hydrogels containing different MXene concentrations were evaluated using current‐voltage (I‐V) measurements and resistance analysis (Figure 4a,b). Pristine MXene exhibited the steepest I‐V slope and the lowest resistance (∼20 Ω), reflecting its intrinsic high conductivity, while the FmocFF hydrogel showed no current response, confirming it acts as an insulator [31]. Incorporation of MXene into the FmocFF/HA network significantly enhanced conductivity in a concentration‐dependent manner. The 0.1% MXene hydrogel showed a measurable current response but maintained a relatively higher resistance (∼160 Ω), indicating reduced conductivity compared to higher MXene loadings. In contrast, hydrogels containing 0.3%, 0.5%, and 0.7% MXene displayed lower resistances (∼60–80 Ω) and steeper I‐V slopes, with no statistically significant differences between these concentrations. This behavior may suggest that above ∼0.3% MXene, more extensive conductive interconnections begin to form throughout the hydrogel, resulting in a conductivity plateau where additional MXene incorporation provides only limited further improvement in charge transport efficiency, in accordance with electrical percolation [64].

To better understand the effect of HA on the conductivity, hydrogels incorporating MXene (0.5 wt %, FmocFF:HA ratio of 3:1) and HA of various molecular weights (500–750 kDa, 1000–1250 and 2000–2200 kDa) were evaluated using I‐V measurements and resistance analysis (Figure 4c,d). FmocFF/HA gels in the absence of MXene exhibited no change in current when the voltage was varied, thereby confirming a typical insulator. Incorporation of MXene resulted in a marked increase in current response across all HA molecular weights, yielding resistance values of ∼55–65 Ω. Notably, no statistically significant differences were observed between hydrogels containing 500–750 (low Mw), 1000–1250 (high Mw), or 2000–2200 (very high) kDa HA, indicating that electrical performance is determined by the percolated MXene network rather than by HA chain length. The FmocFF/MXene gel without HA displayed a slightly lower resistance compared to HA‐containing composites, suggesting that the polysaccharide may slightly influence nanosheet spacing, but overall conductive pathways remained intact. These results highlight that HA molecular weight does not affect the electrical conductivity of the peptide/MXene/HA composite network.

The long‐term electrical stability of pristine MXene under physiological conditions was evaluated over 24 days using I‐V measurements and resistance analysis (Figure 4e–h). Freshly prepared MXene exhibited a steep linear I‐V response and low resistance (∼20 Ω), consistent with its intrinsically high conductivity (Figure 4e). However, upon incubation in cell culture medium (DMEM) at 37°C, MXene rapidly lost electrical conductivity, as evidenced by a dramatic increase in resistance over time (Figure 4f). Already after 3 days, the resistance increased by several orders of magnitude to ∼104 Ω, indicating substantial disruption of the conductivity. Prolonged incubation resulted in a further conductivity decline, reaching resistance values approaching ∼106 Ω by day 24. This rapid electrical degradation is consistent with the known instability of Ti3C2TX MXene in aqueous oxygen‐containing physiological environments, where oxidation and hydrolytic degradation progressively convert the conductive MXene nanosheets into poorly conductive titanium oxide‐rich species, thereby disrupting electron transport [65]. In contrast, the FmocFF/MXene/HA composite hydrogel exhibited substantially improved long‐term electrical stability under the same physiological conditions (DMEM cell culture medium, 37°C). The composite maintained linear ohmic I‐V behavior throughout the incubation period, with only moderate changes in resistance during the first 15 days (∼50–100 Ω), indicating preservation of continuous conductive pathways within the hydrogel network (Figure 4g). Although a gradual increase in resistance was observed after prolonged incubation, particularly after day 18, the scaffold retained measurable electrical conductivity throughout the 24‐day study (Figure 4h). By day 24, the composite hydrogel displayed noticeably increased resistance (∼103–104 Ω), indicating that the scaffold became less electrically conductive over time. Nevertheless, its resistance remained several orders of magnitude lower than that of pristine MXene incubated under identical conditions, demonstrating the ability of the hydrogel matrix to significantly delay MXene degradation and preserve charge transport for extended periods.

In a proof‐of‐concept demonstration of practical conductivity, each leg of a light‐emitting diode (LED) was independently inserted into a separate FmocFF/MXene/HA hydrogel bulk, and electrical connection was completed by gently contacting each hydrogel bulk with platinum electrodes that were connected to a power source (Figure S4). Under these conditions, the hydrogel segments collectively served as the conductive medium, allowing current to flow and the LED to illuminate. Disconnecting the electrodes immediately turned off the LED, confirming that charge transport occurred through the hydrogel network itself.

Biocompatibility of the Multicomponent Hydrogels

The biocompatibility of the different hydrogels was evaluated using live/dead staining. NIH‐3T3 fibroblasts cultured on the different gels exhibited distinct differences in cell viability and morphology depending on the formulation (Figure 5a). FmocFF and FmocFF/HA supported modest cell densities with occasional dead cells. On these scaffolds, fibroblasts predominantly exhibited a round morphology, indicative of limited adhesion [66]. By contrast, the FmocFF/MXene and the FmocFF/MXene/HA hydrogels displayed markedly higher fractions of viable cells, with fibroblasts appearing more spread, consistent with stronger substrate attachment. The improvement in cell viability on MXene‐containing gels is attributed to the intrinsic biocompatibility of MXene and its contribution to increased scaffold rigidity, which together enhance fibroblast adhesion and spreading [62]. Consistent with the mechanical properties results, MXene reinforcement elevates the storage modulus (G′) into a more favorable regime for fibroblast attachment, while HA incorporation further improves the cellular microenvironment. Among all groups, FmocFF/MXene/HA produced the highest cell viability with well‐spread fibroblasts.

In addition, cell viability on different hydrogels with varying MXene concentrations and various HA molecular weights was quantitatively evaluated using an Alamar Blue assay of NIH‐3T3 fibroblasts (Figure 5b). All formulations showed higher fibroblast viability than the FmocFF hydrogel control, confirming the favorable biological profile of the hybrid scaffolds.

Nevertheless, several formulations were excluded from further experiments based on combined cell viability (Figure 5b) and electrical considerations (Figure 4). 0.1% wt MXene hydrogel obtained low electrical conductivity and was therefore not tested biologically. 0.3% wt MXene hydrogel yielded lower cell viability than the 0.5% formulations, while 0.7% wt MXene hydrogels displayed acceptable viability, particularly with 2000–2200 kDa HA, but rapid gelation and poor homogeneity precluded further analysis. Notably, the 0.5% wt MXene composites consistently exhibited the highest fibroblast viability across all HA molecular weights, leading to the selection of the 1000–1250 kDa HA formulation as optimal. This HA range and above is considered high molecular weight, which is associated with pro‐regenerative and wound healing functions [34]. Although the 2000–2200 kDa HA composites also supported good cell viability, they gelated too quickly, produced less homogeneous structures, and were less convenient to handle than the 1000–1250 kDa HA formulations. The 500–750 kDa HA is considered to have a low‐to‐medium molecular weight and is therefore pro‐inflammatory [34]. Indeed, this formulation did not increase cell viability compared to high molecular weight HA (1000 kDa and above).

The combination of 1000–1250 kDa HA and 0.5% MXene combined high mechanical (Figure 3a) and conductive properties (Figure 4a), robust biocompatibility (Figure 5b), homogeneous gelation, and practical handling, establishing it as the most suitable scaffold candidate for subsequent studies.

Additional Alamar Blue assays further confirmed the positive effect of MXene and HA incorporation on fibroblast viability over prolonged culture periods (Figure 5c). Cell viability values were normalized to the FmocFF hydrogel cell viability on day 2, which served as the baseline control for all biological comparisons in this experiment. Cells cultured on pure FmocFF hydrogels exhibited relatively low viability throughout the study, with a slight but statistically significant decrease observed over time, indicating limited support for long‐term fibroblast proliferation. Similarly, incorporation of HA alone into the peptide matrix (FmocFF/HA) resulted in only a modest improvement in viability, with no substantial increase in metabolic activity during the 11‐day culture period. Notably, both the FmocFF and FmocFF/HA hydrogels gradually degraded and lost structural integrity over time under cell culture conditions, which likely negatively affected long‐term cell attachment and proliferation. In contrast, hydrogels containing MXene demonstrated markedly enhanced cellular viability and proliferation. The FmocFF/MXene scaffold exhibited a significant time‐dependent increase in cell viability, reaching approximately 200% viability by day 11, indicating that incorporation of conductive MXene nanosheets substantially improves the cellular microenvironment. Importantly, unlike the pristine peptide‐based hydrogels, the MXene‐containing scaffolds maintained their structural integrity throughout the culture period without noticeable degradation, further supporting the role of MXene in reinforcing scaffold stiffness and durability under prolonged physiological conditions. The FmocFF/MXene/HA composite hydrogel displayed the highest overall cell viability among all tested groups, with viability progressively increasing throughout the culture period and reaching ∼220%–230% by day 11. The cell viability enhancement may originate from the improved mechanical reinforcement and bioelectrical properties of the scaffold, which support fibroblast adhesion, spreading, and proliferation [67, 68]. Notably, the combined incorporation of MXene and HA resulted in significantly higher viability compared to pristine FmocFF and HA‐containing scaffolds without MXene, demonstrating a synergistic effect between the conductive nanosheets and the polysaccharide component.

It should be noted that the simultaneous incorporation of MXene and HA modulates several key scaffold properties, including electrical conductivity, mechanical stiffness, and microstructural organization. These parameters are inherently interdependent in multicomponent self‐assembling systems, making it challenging to attribute the observed cellular responses to a single factor. Indeed, fibroblast adhesion, spreading, and proliferation are known to be regulated by a combination of mechanical and bioelectrical cues, as well as by matrix architecture. Therefore, the enhanced cellular activity observed in the FmocFF/MXene/HA hydrogels is likely governed by a synergistic interplay among these factors, rather than by the isolated effect of either factor.

Optimization of Electrical Stimulation Parameters for Enhanced Cell Viability and Migration

To evaluate the influence of electrical stimulation on cell behavior, a custom‐made in vitro stimulation system was utilized (Figure 6a). Hydrogels were placed in 24‐well plates fitted with customized lids containing two aligned openings designed to accommodate platinum wire electrodes (Figure S5). The electrodes were connected to a programmable power supply, enabling precise control of the applied voltage and stimulation duration for each hydrogel sample. In contrast to pulsed electrical stimulation commonly used for electrically excitable tissues such as cardiac muscle, Direct current (DC) stimulation is more physiologically relevant for wound healing applications and fibroblast‐guided tissue regeneration. Accordingly, continuous DC electrical stimulation was selected to mimic the endogenous transepithelial electric fields naturally present at skin wound sites, which are known to regulate fibroblast migration and wound closure through galvanotaxis [39]. The hydrogels were subjected to daily electrical stimulation (1 h per day) at voltages ranging from 0 (control) to 2000 mV (DC). Prior to assessing cell migration, stimulation parameters were optimized to ensure the most efficient stimulation. Electrical stimulation induced a voltage‐dependent effect on fibroblast viability (Figure 6b). Stimulation at 100 and 200 mV significantly enhanced cell viability, with increases of ∼30%–40% relative to unstimulated controls, whereas higher voltages (500–2000 mV) resulted in reduced or unchanged viability. The strong response observed at 100–200 mV is particularly noteworthy, as this range corresponds to the endogenous wound transepithelial potential of skin epithelia [39]. This electric field is known to guide keratinocyte and fibroblast migration and proliferation at wound sites, and its replication in vitro provides a physiologically relevant cue for regeneration. By contrast, stimulation at 2000 mV markedly reduced cell viability, probably due to extensive electrolysis within the hydrogel scaffold, leading to its structural degradation, while simultaneously exerting a direct damaging effect on the cells. These findings demonstrate that conductive scaffolds deliver a maximal regenerative benefit when stimulated within physiologically relevant voltage ranges, whereas excessive voltages destabilize the system and impair biocompatibility.

To further explore the effect of the stimulation regimen, FmocFF/MXene/HA scaffolds were cultured with fibroblasts and stimulated daily with 100 mV (DC), while varying the stimulation duration from 0 (control) to 3 h per day (Figure 6c). Cell viability, as measured by Alamar Blue, increased with stimulation times from 30 min to 1 h per day, yielding significantly higher metabolic activity than unstimulated controls. Notably, even 30 min of stimulation per day was sufficient to enhance cell viability, which is advantageous compared to most reports in the literature where ≥ 1 h of stimulation per day is typically required [40, 41, 42]. This finding suggests that our hydrogel system is more efficient in transmitting bioelectrical cues, likely due to its optimized conductive and bioactive network. Extending the stimulation to 2–3 h per day resulted in a major decline in cell viability compared to 30 min or 1 h per day, suggesting that prolonged exposure to external voltage may impose stress on the system. These results reinforce that short, daily pulses of electrical stimulation at physiological voltages are sufficient to maximize fibroblast activity, while excessive stimulation durations provide no added value.

Fibroblast migration was then assessed under the optimized stimulation conditions using a scratch assay performed directly on the hydrogel surface, enabling evaluation of the scaffold's capacity to enhance in vitro wound healing (Figure 6d,e). Fibroblasts that stably express green fluorescent protein (GFP) were seeded on the scaffolds to allow direct visualization of migration. At time = 0, a clear cell‐free zone (≈ 500 µm in width) was present across all scaffolds following the scratch assay.

Incorporation of HA (FmocFF/MXene/HA) produced a slight increase in migration, resulting in 26.2% ± 4.5% closure. Electrical stimulation (1 h at 100 mV DC) on FmocFF/MXene, without HA, further accelerated migration, yielding 32.1% ± 4.8% closure. The enhanced performance of HA‐containing scaffolds is consistent with the established role of high molecular weight HA in wound healing, where it promotes fibroblast motility by creating a hydrated extracellular environment, supporting provisional matrix formation, and activating CD44 signaling pathways [6]. In addition, as mentioned above, electrical stimulation is known to enhance fibroblast motility by recreating endogenous wound electric fields, which direct cell orientation and promote cytoskeletal remodeling, thereby increasing migration rates. This highlights the synergistic action of HA‐mediated bioactivity and electrical stimulation in promoting fibroblast migration, effectively recapitulating key cues of the regenerative wound environment.

Conclusions

In this work, we developed and systematically evaluated an electrically conductive and bioactive hydrogel scaffold based on the self‐assembling dipeptide FmocFF, reinforced with MXene nanosheets, and supplemented with HA. Structural characterization revealed a well‐defined nanofibrous network with uniformly embedded MXene nanosheets. Mechanical characterization demonstrated that MXene incorporation significantly enhanced mechanical stiffness, while HA moderately softened the matrix, enabling tunable mechanical properties. Electrical measurements confirmed that MXene formed stable percolated conductive networks, with conductivity reaching a plateau above 0.3% wt MXene, thereby enabling efficient and reproducible electrical signal transmission.

Biocompatibility assays demonstrated that MXene‐containing scaffolds strongly supported fibroblast adhesion, spreading, and proliferation, owing to the MXene's intrinsic biocompatibility and the beneficial effect of increased stiffness on cell‐scaffold interactions. Among the tested conditions, 0.5% MXene with high‐molecular‐weight HA (1000–1250 kDa) emerged as the optimal composition, combining uniform gel formation, stable conductivity, and maximal cell viability. The choice of high‐molecular‐weight HA is further supported by its well‐established role in promoting cell migration and tissue regeneration during wound healing [34].

Electrical stimulation experiments highlighted the scaffold's ability to effectively transduce physiologically relevant electrical cues. Stimulation at 100–200 mV significantly enhanced fibroblast viability, whereas excessive voltages resulted in reduced cell viability. Importantly, even stimulation durations as short as 30 min per day were sufficient to elicit a positive cellular response, underscoring the efficiency of this system compared to most reported platforms that require at least 1 h of daily stimulation [40, 41, 42]. This efficiency reflects the optimized conductive and bioactive architecture of the scaffold, enabling effective cell stimulation with minimal electrical input.

Finally, cell migration assays demonstrated that both electrical stimulation and HA presence in the scaffold acted synergistically to enhance fibroblast migration, with the highest wound closure observed on the FmocFF/MXene/HA scaffold upon electrical stimulation (39.4% ± 5.4 closure after 12 h). Electrical stimulation provided directional galvanotactic cues that guided migration, while HA improved the local microenvironment by facilitating cell motility and matrix interactions. Together, these findings demonstrate that the synergistic integration of MXene‐mediated stiffness and conductivity, HA‐mediated bioactivity, and exogenous electrical stimulation recapitulates key biochemical and biophysical features of the native wound healing environment.

Beyond its functional performance, the FmocFF/MXene/HA scaffold also offers two key design advantages. First, incorporation of MXene into the FmocFF/HA supramolecular matrix mitigates MXene oxidation, thereby preserving electrical conductivity and extending functional lifetime, addressing a major limitation of MXene‐based biomaterials [69]. Second, the hydrogel is fabricated via a simple, crosslinker‐free process with high reproducibility, supporting scalability and translational feasibility.

While the present study focuses on fibroblast proliferation and migration in the context of wound healing, the conductive and bioactive properties of the FmocFF/MXene/HA scaffold may also be relevant for other electrically responsive tissues. Future studies could explore the interaction of these scaffolds with additional cell types, including skeletal muscle cells or hiPSC‐derived cardiomyocytes, as well as evaluate their performance in relevant in vivo regeneration models.

Overall, this study establishes a comprehensive design framework for electrically active, biocompatible hydrogel scaffolds with the optimized FmocFF/MXene/HA system representing a promising platform for advanced wound‐healing applications.

Materials and Methods

Ti3AlC2 MAX and Ti3C2Tx MXene Synthesis

The Ti3AlC2 MAX precursor was synthesized by mixing titanium carbide (Alfa Aesar, 99.5%, 2 µm), aluminum (Alfa Aesar, 99.5%, 325 mesh), and titanium (Alfa Aesar, 99.5%, 325 mesh) powders at a 2:1.15:1 molar ratio, respectively [56]. The elementary powders were mixed in a tumbler mixer at 150 rpm for 24 h until a homogeneous mixture was obtained. The mixed powders were then heat‐treated in a tube furnace at 1400°C at a heating rate of 5°C min−1 for 3 h under a flowing Ar atmosphere (100 sccm). Finally, the resulting Ti3AlC2 MAX phase was milled to a fine powder using a planetary ball mill and sieved through a 400‐mesh screen (<38 µm) to attain a homogeneous powder.

Ti3C2Tx MXene was synthesized by selectively etching the metallic Al layer of the Ti3AlC2 MAX precursor. The Ti3AlC2 powder was etched in an HCl and LiF solution, which reacted to form in situ HF. Initially, 1.6 g of LiF (Alfa Aesar, 99.5%, 325 mesh) was dissolved in 20 mL of 32% HCl (Fisher Scientific). This was followed by the slow addition of 1 g of the Ti3AlC2 powder to prevent a violent reaction, and the solution was then stirred for 24 h at 45°C and 300 rpm. The etching was performed in an HDPE container with a vent to allow the release of hydrogen produced during the reaction. The black slurry was transferred to a 50‐mL centrifuge vial, and type I deionized (DI) water was added to fill the vial. The slurry was centrifuged at 2300 RCF for 2 min, and the resulting clear supernatant was decanted while retaining the bottom sediment. To remove the excess LiF, the etched material was washed twice with 1 M HCl solution and centrifuged after each wash. After the HCl washing, the material was washed and centrifuged with the above DI water several more times, until the pH of the solution reached ∼6. This step yielded a multilayered Ti3C2Tx MXene. To exfoliate the MXene to a single layer, the mixture was sonicated under a bubbling Ar flow for 1 h, while maintaining the temperature of the sonication bath below 10°C. The solution was then centrifuged for 30 min at 3500 RPM, and only the colloid solution was collected.

Preparation of Hydrogels

A stock solution of hyaluronic acid (HA) (Contripo, Czech Republic) was prepared by dissolving sodium hyaluronate powder (Contripo) in double‐distilled water (ddH2O) to a concentration of 10 mg mL−1 under gentle stirring overnight. Separately, a stock solution of FmocFF powder (GL Biochem) was prepared at 150 mg mL−1 in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma‐Aldrich) by vortexing until a transparent solution was obtained.

Four hydrogel formulations were prepared: FmocFF, FmocFF/HA, FmocFF/MXene, and FmocFF/MXene/HA. The FmocFF hydrogel formulation was prepared by mixing a portion of the FmocFF stock solution with ddH2O to yield a final concentration of 3.75 mg mL−1. The FmocFF/HA hydrogel contained 3.75 mg mL−1 FmocFF and 1.25 mg mL−1 HA, prepared by combining aliquots of HA stock solution with ddH2O prior to peptide addition. For the FmocFF/MXene hydrogel, colloidal MXene suspension was diluted to the desired concentration with ddH2O, then mixed with FmocFF stock solution. Finally, the ternary FmocFF/MXene/HA hydrogel was produced by mixing HA stock solution with MXene suspension and ddH2O, followed by addition of peptide stock, resulting in a final composition of 3.75 mg mL−1 FmocFF, 1.25 mg mL−1 HA, and MXene according to the desired concentration. The weight ratio of FmocFF:HA was 3:1 for all formulation unless noted otherwise.

High‐Resolution Scanning Electron Microscopy (HRSEM)

HRSEM was performed using a Zeiss Gemini 300 in high‐vacuum mode, with a work distance (WD) ≈ 10 mm and a voltage of 3 kV. Hydrogel scaffolds were fabricated as outlined above, and after overnight of gelation time, the scaffolds were washed three times with PBS and fixed overnight at 4°C using 2.5% (v/v) glutaraldehyde (Sigma‐Aldrich) in PBS. Post‐fixation, samples were washed once with PBS and subjected to a graded ethanol dehydration series (50%, 75%, and 100% in PBS), with each step lasting 30 min. Subsequently, the samples were maintained in 100% ethanol before critical‐point drying (CPD300, Leica), gold sputter coating, and HRSEM imaging. HRSEM images were analyzed using ImageJ to determine the average fibril diameter. Elemental analysis (EDS) was conducted using an X‐Flash 6/60 detector (Bruker) under an accelerating voltage of 7 kV and a probe current of 1.6 nA.

X‐ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)

XPS measurements were performed using an ESCALAB QXi (Thermo Scientific) system. The samples were irradiated with monochromatic Al K𝛼 radiation. High‐resolution spectra of C1s, O1s, and Ti2p were collected with a pass energy of 40 eV with a 650‐micron spot size. The samples were stored in sealed vials at room temperature under ambient conditions prior to analysis (up to 4 weeks).

X‐ray Diffraction (XRD)

The diffraction spectra of pristine MXene and the different hydrogel formulations were collected using a PANalytical AERIS research Edition diffractometer with CuK𝛼 radiation (40 kV and 15 mA), a step size of ≈0.0108° 2𝜃, and scan speed ≈0.0557° sec−1 in the range of 5–60° 2𝜃.

Mechanical Properties Analysis

The mechanical properties of the hydrogels were evaluated using an AR‐G2 rheometer (TA Instruments) equipped with an 8 mm stainless steel parallel‐plate geometry. Hydrogels were first cast in silicone molds (9 mm diameter) and allowed to gelate for 2 h at room temperature prior to testing. Discs of each gel (110 µL) were then loaded onto the rheometer, yielding a gap size of 0.6 mm, and measurements were performed at 25°C. Oscillatory strain sweeps (0.01%–100%) were conducted to determine the linear viscoelastic region and flow behavior.

To assess shear‐responsive behavior and printability of the FmocFF/MXene/HA hydrogel (0.375/0.5/0.125 wt%, respectively), step‐strain experiments were performed in which the hydrogels were subjected to seven alternating cycles of low (0.1%, 3 min) and high (100%, 1 min) strain during time sweep measurements. At high strain, the hydrogels transitioned into a quasi‐liquid (sol) state, as indicated by the inversion of the storage and loss moduli (G′ < G″). When the strain was reduced back to 0.1%, the gels rapidly reformed, with G′ exceeding G″, demonstrating recovery of the elastic network.

Electrical Properties Analysis

The hydrogels were drop‐cast on prefabricated field‐effect transistor substrates (Ossila) with an electrode gap of 6 µm and dried under ambient conditions. Current‐voltage (I‐V) measurements were performed with a MultiPalmSense 4 (PalmSense). All measurements were taken from ‐2 to 2 V with 0.05 V steps. Resistance values were extracted from the slope of the linear curves and plotted in a bar graph. The measurements were repeated for at least three independent samples to ensure reproducibility. For the long‐term conductivity measurements under physiological conditions, pristine MXene and FmocFF/MXene/HA hydrogels deposited on the transistor substrates were incubated in cell culture medium at 37°C for 24 days. Every 3 days, the samples were removed from the medium, gently dried under ambient conditions, and subjected to I‐V measurements using the same parameters described above. Resistance values at each time point were extracted from the linear region of the I‐V curves to evaluate changes in electrical conductivity over time.

Live/Dead Viability Assay

NIH 3T3 fibroblast cells were purchased from ATCC and cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM) (Gibco) supplemented with 10 wt% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% Penicillin‐Streptomycin (Sigma‐Aldrich), and 1% L‐Glutamine (200 mM) (Sartorius) (complete DMEM). The cells were cultured in a petri dish at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

For the live/dead cell viability assay, hydrogel scaffolds were cast onto coverslips in a 24‐well cell‐repellent plate and seeded with fibroblast cells. After 2 days of incubation, the hydrogels were incubated with fluorescein diacetate (FDA, 6.6 µg mL−1, Sigma‐Aldrich) to stain viable cells (green) and propidium iodide (PI, 5 µg mL−1, Sigma‐ Aldrich) to stain nonviable cells (red). The samples were incubated with the staining solution for 5 min at 37°C in 5% CO2, rinsed once with PBS, and immediately imaged using a ZEISS LSM 900 confocal microscope (ZEISS). To overcome light interference caused by the dark color of MXene‐containing gels, the coverslips were carefully flipped face down before imaging to improve visualization of the stained cells.

Alamar Blue Viability Assay

The biocompatibility of the hydrogel formulations was evaluated using an Alamar Blue assay (Bio‐Rad). FmocFF hydrogels (FmocFF/HA 0.375%/0.125% wt% respectively) that contained different MXene concentrations (0.3%, 0.5%, 0.7% wt%) and HA molecular weights (500–750, 1000–1250, 2000–2200 kDa) were cast in cell‐repellent 24‐well plates at a volume of 350 µL per well and allowed to gelate overnight. Following gelation, the hydrogels were sterilized by UV irradiation and washed five times with 1 mL DMEM per well, with each wash lasting 30 min, to remove residual FmocFF monomers. NIH 3T3 fibroblasts were harvested by trypsinization at approximately 90% confluency and were seeded at a density of 50,000 cells per hydrogel scaffold in 1 mL DMEM. After 3 days of culture, cell metabolic activity was assessed by replacing the incubation medium with Alamar Blue solution diluted 1:9 in DMEM and incubating for 3 h (37°C, 5% CO2). To avoid interference from the dark hydrogel background, the Alamar Blue solution was carefully collected and transferred to a new plate, where absorbance was measured using a Tecan Spark Infinite M200 PRO plate reader at λ = 570 nm and λ = 600 nm.

Electrical Stimulation

FmocFF/MXene/HA hydrogels (0.375%/0.5%/0.125% wt% respectively, 350 µL per well) were cast in cell‐repellent 24‐well plates and allowed to gelate overnight. Following gelation, scaffolds were sterilized by UV irradiation and washed five times with 1 mL DMEM for 30 min each, as described above. NIH 3T3 fibroblasts were harvested at ∼90% confluency and seeded at a density of 50,000 cells per well in 1 mL complete DMEM. For electrical stimulation, a custom lid fabricated from Perspex was prepared, with two holes drilled for each well. Platinum wire electrodes were inserted through the holes to reach the hydrogel surface and were connected via alligator clips to a DC power supply. The entire setup, including the wired plate, was placed in a cell culture incubator (37°C, 5% CO2). On the following day, scaffolds were subjected to a daily DC stimulation for 3 consecutive days (including the first day after cell seeding). The distance between the electrodes was fixed at 1 cm, resulting in applied electric fields of 50, 100, 200, 500, 1000, and 2000 mV cm−1 for the corresponding applied voltages. In the voltage‐dependent study, gels were stimulated for 1 h/day at different voltages (50–2000 mV cm−1). In the time‐dependent study, stimulation was applied at a constant 100 mV for 0.5, 1, 2, or 3 h/day. At the end of the 3‐day stimulation regimen, cell metabolic activity was quantified using an Alamar Blue assay (1:9 dilution in DMEM, 3 h incubation), and results were compared across conditions relative to unstimulated, cell‐seeded scaffold controls.

Cell Migration

NIH 3T3 mouse fibroblasts were genetically modified to express green fluorescent protein (GFP) and maintained in complete DMEM supplemented with 6 µg mL−1 puromycin for the growth of specific GFP‐containing cells. Cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 until they reached ∼90% confluency, then harvested by trypsinization for seeding, as described below.

FmocFF/MXene/HA hydrogels (0.375%/0.5%/0.125% wt%) and FmocFF/MXene hydrogels (0.375%/0.5% wt%) were cast directly on glass coverslips placed in cell‐repellent 24‐well plates and allowed to gelate overnight, followed by sterilization using UV irradiation and five washes with 1 mL DMEM for 30 min each, as described above. A removable silicone dual‐chamber insert was positioned on top of the hydrogel surface, and 40,000 GFP‐expressing fibroblasts were seeded per chamber in 70 µL of complete DMEM. The constructs were incubated overnight to allow cell attachment. On the following day, the silicone insert was carefully removed to create a cell‐free gap (∼500 µm) across the hydrogel surface. Samples were gently rinsed once with DMEM to remove nonadherent cells and immediately imaged using a fluorescence microscope to record the baseline (t = 0 h). After this initial imaging step, the hydrogels were subjected to electrical stimulation at 100 mV DC for 1 h using platinum wire electrodes inserted through the custom‐made Perspex lid described above (see Electrical Stimulation). The entire setup was maintained inside a cell culture incubator during stimulation. Migration was subsequently monitored by imaging every 2 h for a total of 12 h. Because the dark color of MXene interfered with fluorescence detection, the hydrogels were flipped face‐down onto the coverslip prior to each imaging step to enable accurate visualization of the GFP‐positive fibroblasts.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Prism version 10.4.2 (GraphPad). Data is presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), based on at least three independent experiments. Details of specific statistical tests and comparisons are provided in the respective figure legends.
