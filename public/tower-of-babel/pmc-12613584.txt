Multi-class brain tumor MRI segmentation and classification using deep learning and machine learning approaches
Authors: Aqib Ali, Xinde Li, Wali Khan Mashwani, Mohammad Abiad, Faten Khalid Karim, Samih M Mostafa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBrain tumor classification using Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is crucial for diagnosis and treatment planning. The differentiation between malignant and benign brain tumors and their subtypes remains a challenging task that can benefit from advanced computational techniques.PurposeThis study uses an MRI dataset to explore the effectiveness of deep learning (DL) and machine learning (ML) approaches for classifying brain tumors.Materials and methodsA dataset comprising 1200 DICOM brain tumor MRI images, representing malignant and benign tumors with six subtypes, was prepared. Each image was converted to a 512 × 512-pixel digital format, selecting 200 images per tumor class. Image quality was enhanced using sharpening algorithms and mean filtering. The proposed edge refined binary histogram segmentation (ER-BHS) was applied to extract hybrid features from the regions of interest. Feature optimization through a correlation-based method reduced the dataset to 11 key features. Multiple classifiers, including DL, neural networks, and ML models, were evaluated on the optimized dataset using 10-fold cross-validation.ResultsAmong the tested models, the random committee (RC) classifier demonstrated superior performance, achieving an accuracy of 98.61% on the optimized hybrid brain tumor MRI dataset. Overall, DL and ML methods effectively automated brain tumor classification.ConclusionThe promising results affirm the potential of DL and ML approaches to enhance medical image analysis and improve diagnostic accuracy in brain tumor classification, potentially revolutionizing clinical workflows.Keywords: Brain tumor, Deep learning, Machine learning, ER-BHS, MRI

Introduction

Every organ in the body depends on the brain because the brain controls all body processes. The brain helps us negotiate and react to our surroundings, changing our behaviors, decisions, views, emotions, and body enzymes [1]. Many illnesses, including cancer, require early detection for optimal treatment. Brain cancer is among the most severe and frightening disorders. According to World Health Organization (WHO) data, it is the second leading cause of mortality globally. Based on their characteristics, over 120 brain tumors might be benign and malignant [2]. Non-malignant cells in benign tumors are unlikely to spread to other organs and tissues, making them surgically treatable. Malignant tumors are grade three or four in hospitals, while benign tumors are grade one or two. While gliomas are malignant, adenomas and meningiomas are frequently benign [3].

Brain gliomas usually form in neurons, blood vessels, and basic tissue. Adenomas surround the cranium, while meningiomas encompass the Central Nervous System (CNS). Radiologists and neurologists can start treatment immediately for early brain tumors. Brain tumors can only be detected by Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), Computed Tomography (CT), and Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) [4]. Neurologists and radiologists are needed for this intricate and time-consuming operation. Brain tumors can complicate biopsy-validated treatment. Brain tumor patients have hazardous biopsies during surgery, which stands out among bodily tumors. This subjective process is time-consuming and intellectually taxing. Hence, automated brain tumor categorization is essential. This method improves radiologists’ brain tumor detection, saving lives.

Multiple image processing algorithms have been presented for brain tumor classification [5]. The methods include shape, texture, and machine learning categorization. MR image patterns can be used to categorize cancers utilizing texture analysis. The research considers intensity and pixel distribution. Tumor classification and research benefit from tumor size and shape analysis. Deep Learning (DL) and Machine Learning (ML) are used to classify brain tumors. ML classification, texture analysis, and form analysis are examples. Tumor dimensions and structures enhance shape analysis and malignancy classification [6].

These algorithms successfully classify tumors into numerous types using MRI data. Image processing makes brain tumor classification difficult since tumor appearance varies by patient and imaging technology [7]. Researchers have created multimodal brain tumor classification methods to solve this difficulty. These technologies combine multimodal imaging data. However, many tumors are tiny, making detection and categorization more difficult. Many approaches have been taken to address this issue. Tumor segmentation, MRI boundary identification, and classification property extraction come under this area [8]. Accurate brain tumor detection improves patient outcomes, reduces unnecessary, intrusive surgeries, and helps choose the optimal treatment approach for each patient. Brain tumor classification relies on image processing to decrease radiologists’ visual judgment variability of MR images. Processing MR images may assist in constructing tumor detection ML and DL models. This procedure eliminates visual inspection subjectivity and ambiguity, making more reliable diagnoses [7–9].

Related studies

The Reference [10] proposed an automated three-module brain tumor classification method using MRI data. The first module extracts high-level features from MRI scans using pre-trained DenseNet121 and ResNet101 models to characterize brain tumor characteristics fully. The second module decreases feature dimensionality using PCA. The third module’s Random Forest ML classifier predicts cancer using minimal features. An excellent accuracy above 90% was achieved using five MRI datasets to evaluate the approach. Reference [11] explains that explainable artificial intelligence, semantic segmentation, and Bayesian machine learning can find and classify brain cancers in MRI images. Hyperparameter tuning, an enhanced radial movement optimization approach, and 97.75% accuracy made the new model far better than previous models. The report suggests multimodal data and model optimization research for better clinical usage. Reference [12] Automatic brain tumor detection and classification utilizing MRI images with feature fusion and machine learning (ML). Gabor wavelet features, histograms of oriented gradients (HOG), local binary patterns (LBP), and segmentation-based fractal texture analysis (SFTA) are combined to create a single feature vector for classification using a Random Forest (RF) classifier. Lesion enhancement using five clusters, program matching, and unsupervised segmentation aids tumor region detection. With 95.5% accuracy on BRATS 2015, the suggested strategy outperformed current methods on five benchmark datasets. Accurate and consistent tumor segmentation and classification from feature fusion improve early diagnosis and therapy planning.

The Reference [13] introduced two neural network designs: a 2D CNN, a convolutional auto-encoder network, and six traditional machine learning classifiers to identify brain tumors in MRI images. Four types of brain MRI images are in the collection. The networks had 96.47% training accuracy for the 2D CNN and 95.63% for the auto-encoder. Its high accuracy and fast execution make it a viable brain tumor detection tool. These algorithms can help radiologists make clinical decisions quickly and consistently. Reference [14] presented new MRI-based brain tumor detection technology. The adaptive contrast enhancement technique and median filtering were used to reduce noise in pre-processed MRIs. GLCM feature extraction and fuzzy C-means (FCM) segmentation split the images. An Ensemble Deep Neural Support Vector Machine (EDN-SVM) classifier finds malignancies. The model performs well with 97.93% accuracy, 92% sensitivity, and 98% specificity. A new CNN model for feature extraction and ML classification approaches was introduced [15]. Bayesian optimization raises ML algorithm hyperparameters, improving classification accuracy to 80%. Experimental results of the hybrid model show 97.15% classification accuracy.

The Reference [16] proposed a CNN-based transfer learning model to classify brain tumor MRI images. The model is tuned on many MRI images pre-processed with rotation, shifting, and contrast enhancement to improve dataset diversity and model performance. The EfficientNetB0 architecture gets the model with 97% classification accuracy. Dropouts and many callbacks maximize training and prevent overfitting in the study. Reference [17] presented ML-based brain tumor detection using MRI segmentation. It employs 2D and 3D-UNet to extract image texture and shape information, then classifies them using a hybrid ML model. Soft voting combines K-nearest neighbor (KNN) and gradient boosting classifier (GBC) to improve accuracy in the hybrid model. 3D-UNet segmentation improves performance, with 2D-UNet characteristics achieving 71% accuracy versus 64%. KNN and GBC are combined in the hybrid model to classify brain cancer better than standard approaches. Reference [18] used ML and DL on MRI and CT scan images to classify brain cancers. The approach compares six classical ML classifiers with the SVM, which performs best in multiple stages. Second, CNN models like VGG16 improve categorization. The experiment had the highest accuracy of 97.86%. To provide a comprehensive summary of the discussed literature, Table 1 presents a summary of these works.

Materials and methods

This study collected magnetic resonance (MR) images to evaluate and classify brain tumors into six unique classes. The dataset classified brain tumors into two main categories: benign and malignant, which include meningioma, schwannoma, neurofibromatosis, glioma, chondrosarcoma, and chordoma. These categories demonstrate the different forms of benign and malignant tumors that can affect the brain. A detailed and informative visual depiction of these six types of brain tumors is shown in Fig. 1.

Sample of MRI dataset with different type of brain tumors

This study gathered a dataset comprising individuals diagnosed with brain tumors. This research aims to develop a multi-class classification model capable of distinguishing six different types of brain tumors, addressing a gap in the current literature where most studies focus only on binary classification (i.e., tumor vs. non-tumor). The MRI dataset is constructed through collaboration with two publicly accessible repositories. The MRI dataset of two tumor types (Meningioma and Glioma) was acquired at Nanfang Hospital, Guangzhou, China, and General Hospital, Tianjin Medical University, China, using spin-echo-weighted images with a 512 × 512 matrix. The pixel dimensions of the images are 0.49 × 0.49 mm², the slice thickness is 6 mm, the slice gap is 1 mm, and the Gd dose was 0.1 mmol/kg at a rate of 2 ml/s [19]. Secondly, four additional tumor types (Schwannoma, Neurofibromatosis, Chondrosarcoma, and Chordoma) were obtained from the Radiopaedia [20], a brain tumor MRI database. A total of 200 patients were meticulously chosen for each distinct category of brain tumor, culminating in a dataset of 1200 MR images (200 patients × 6 categories).

Proposed methodology for MRI based multi brain tumor classification

Most previous research was done on binary classification, per the literature review. Firstly, we collected an MRI in DICOM format and converted it into a digital image to form an image dataset. After that, the images are transformed into a grey-scale image to reduce complexity while preserving the information. The edges must be enhanced to get the most information from an image, so we deployed sharpening algorithms. Further, a mean filter was used to smooth the image and reduce noise, which can standardize the data, eliminate discrepancies from various technical aspects, and ensure uniformity in the data. Segmentation is a primary step in assessing the early diagnosis of a brain tumor. If segmentation is done at early stages, it becomes beneficial to identify the exact location of tumors. Currently, various kinds of segmentation approaches are available in this field. Some methods are dependent on human consideration, and some approaches work automatically. In the study, we used edge-refined binary histogram segmentation (ER-BHS), a robust segmentation technique that isolates the tumor area from the surrounding healthy area. After the segmentation process was completed, the hybrid feature extraction process was carried out from the segmented area; for this purpose, we selected first-order features that describe pixel intensity values and second-order features that describe the texture patterns within the image. In the feature extraction process, we extracted a very advanced feature, such as the co-occurrence matrix, which evaluates the pixel’s intensity and the spatial relationship. The wavelet transform-based features were selected, indicating that the image’s frequency is analyzed in detail and its structure is explained. The histogram feature, which tells about the pixel intensity, was extracted and generated to provide statistical insights into the image. Lastly, spectral features were extracted, which tell about the edge and shape information of the image.

Edge refined binary histogram segmentation (ER-BHS)

Various approaches can extract regions of interest (ROI) from medical images, some of which are automated and some semi-automated. Automated ROI depends on image segmentation techniques that are acceptable worldwide. Semi-automated approaches need expert and human-based suggestions, and both methods have advantages [23]. To overcome these challenges, the edge refined binary histogram segmentation (ER-BHS) technique was applied to the brain tumor MRI digital image dataset. ER-BHS converts the image into a natural binary format and uses neighboring seed groups to establish different regions of interest. These seeds are irregular polygons that vary from the image center, which increases the possibility of grouping seeds from the exact location. This approach allows for any ROI size, dimension, and shape. The ER-BHS method is applied to the enhanced regions in the post-processing stage. Feature optimization

Feature optimization is critical in machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) workflows. It involves identifying the most relevant features in a dataset while eliminating redundant and irrelevant ones, which helps improve model efficiency, speed, and generalization [28]. In this study, feature optimization was used to enhance the classification of brain tumors from MRI images by reducing the complexity of the data without compromising its intrinsic characteristics. The dataset used had an exceptionally high Feature Vector Size (FVS) of 237,600 (66 × 3,600), representing extracted features across multiple MRI slices and sequences. Handling such a high-dimensional dataset poses significant computational and overfitting challenges, making dimensionality reduction essential. The objective was to select only the most informative features that contribute to accurate tumor classification while preserving the original structure and variability of the dataset.

To achieve this, Correlation-Based Feature Selection (CFS) with Best First Search (BFS) approach [29] was employed. CFS is a filter-based method grounded in information theory. It evaluates feature subsets rather than individual features, selecting those that are highly correlated with the target class (i.e., tumor type) but minimally correlated with each other. This approach ensures that the selected features are predictive and non-redundant. The CFS method uses entropy and information gain to assess feature relevance. Classification

The research employed a combination of advanced classifiers to achieve optimal results, utilizing a range of classifiers, including deep learning (DL), neural network (NN), and machine learning (ML) models. The DL classifiers included deep learning 4j (DL4J) and multilayer perceptron (MLP). The NN classifiers include the resilient backpropagation neural network (RNN) and the weightless neural network (WiSARD). Furthermore, four ML Classifiers were employed, such as random committee (RC), random forest (RF), J48, and BayesNet (BN). The MLP [30] classifiers are a class of feedforward artificial neural networks used for classification tasks. They consist of multiple interconnected neurons: an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer. Each neuron applies a weighted sum of its inputs, followed by a non-linear activation function, allowing the network to learn complex patterns and decision boundaries in data. MLP classifiers are trained using backpropagation to minimize classification error and are widely used for problems where relationships between features and classes are non-linear. In noisy and sparse datasets, the RNN [31] is an adapted version of the traditional backpropagation algorithm that adjusts weights according to the sign of the gradient rather than its magnitude, thereby obviating the necessity for a learning rate and accelerating convergence. Supported architectures encompass DNNs, CNNs, RNNs, and LSTMs. The DL4J [32] is an open-source deep learning framework for Java that integrates with Weka, enabling users to expand computations using Apache Spark and Hadoop for extensive datasets, utilizing GPU acceleration for rapid training. WiSARD (Weightless Neural Network) [33] operates on a winner-takes-all mechanism for classification, eliminating the necessity for traditional weight adjustments via backpropagation. This weightless neural network employs associative memory to store patterns and link them to output classes, making it rapid and efficient for small datasets and straightforward pattern recognition tasks. The Random Committee [34] is an ensemble learning method that typically comprises multiple classifiers, often decision trees, which aggregate their outputs through voting to improve performance, especially when individual classifiers are susceptible to overfitting. Random Forest is an ensemble method that employs bootstrap aggregating (bagging) and random feature selection to mitigate overfitting and improve performance in classification and regression tasks by generating several decision trees. J48, typically employed for interpretable classification tasks, implements the C4.5 decision tree algorithm that recursively partitions data according to the most optimal attribute, employs pruning to mitigate overfitting, and accommodates missing values. It is advantageous for classification and regression tasks involving uncertainty, absent data, and intricate probabilistic relationships. BayesNet is a probabilistic graphical model representing conditional dependencies among variables through a Bayesian network. Each classifier, ranging from decision trees to probabilistic models, is suited for distinct issue types and offers various solutions for machine learning applications. To ensure the reliability and robustness of the classification results, careful attention was given to the dataset distribution, preprocessing pipeline, and validation strategy. The dataset consisted of MRI scans distributed across multiple tumor classes, and class balance was verified before training. Feature extraction and optimization steps were conducted only on the training set, preventing information leakage into the test set. Specifically, the dataset was first split into 10-fold cross-validation subsets, and all preprocessing, including normalization and feature selection using BFS-CFS, was strictly applied to the training data. The same transformation parameters were then applied to the test set. K-fold cross-validation (with k = 10) was employed on the training data to validate model robustness. This approach allowed reliable estimation of model performance across different data splits and helped minimize the risk of overfitting. The final results reported were averaged across folds to ensure consistency and generalizability.

Results and discussion

In this study, we employed four DL and NN classifiers, namely MLP, RNN, DL4J, and WiSARD, alongside four robust ML classifiers, RC, RF, J48, and BN, to effectively classify multi-brain tumors using an optimized hybrid features dataset. We generated these comprehensive hybrid features by combining multiple features such as histograms, wavelets, co-occurrences, and spectral datasets from MR images. These eight classifiers demonstrated exceptional accuracy and promising speed performance among the DL, NN, and ML classifiers. We initially conducted a 10-fold cross-validation on the hybrid feature dataset derived from MR images to assess the classifier’s performance. The overall classification accuracy achieved by DL classifiers, MLP, RNN, DL4J, and WiSARD, was 98.05%, 97.86%, 97.47%, and 97.30%, respectively. This study applied DL, NN, and ML classifiers to classify brain tumors from MRI datasets. While these approaches showed promising results, they exhibited distinct performance and computational efficiency characteristics. DL, NN classifiers, such as RNN, DL4J, WiSARD, and MLP, demonstrated high classification accuracy, with MLP achieving the highest accuracy of 98.05% on the pre-optimized dataset. DL classifiers generally performed well in precision and recall, achieving consistently high scores. However, these models are computationally intensive and take longer to train, particularly on large datasets, as demonstrated by the relatively high training times observed with MLP (54.85 s).

On the other hand, ML classifiers, including RC, RF, J48, and BN, also performed exceptionally well, with RC achieving the highest accuracy of 98.61% on the optimized dataset. While DL is slightly less accurate than its ML counterparts in this study, DL classifiers were more efficient regarding computational cost and training time. For instance, RC took only 0.07 s to train on the optimized dataset, making it a faster option than DL classifiers. ML models are typically better suited for tasks with limited data and are often faster to implement, especially when the dataset size is not enormous. To evaluate the effectiveness of the feature optimization process, an ablation study was conducted comparing the performance of the classification model before and after feature selection. Initially, the model was trained using the complete feature set of higher dimensions. In the second phase, the same classifier was trained using the optimized subset of features selected through the Correlation-Based Feature Selection (CFS) method combined with Best First Search (BFS). The comparison focused on classification accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. Results indicated that the optimized feature set improved the classification performance and significantly reduced training time and computational cost. Additionally, the model trained with the reduced feature set showed better generalization and lower overfitting risk, confirming feature optimization’s positive impact on the overall system.

The DL approaches excel in classification accuracy, particularly when handling binary and complex datasets, whereas ML classifiers offer a more efficient and faster solution while dealing with multiple classes. The choice between DL and ML depends on the task’s specific requirements, including the dataset’s size, computational resources, and the need for accuracy versus efficiency.

Conclusion

This research study classifies six types of brain tumors, namely chondrosarcoma, chordoma, glioma, meningioma, neurofibromatosis, and schwannoma. The classification processes involved image pre-processing approaches such as sharpening algorithms and mean filters for data standardization. The auto single thresholding-based region growing segmentation approach is deployed to obtain the exact tumor location and creates an automated ROI. Hybrid features were extracted and refined using a correlation-based features optimization approach, and an optimized hybrid feature dataset was generated for this research. The four DL classifiers, MLP, RNN, DL4J, and WiSARD, achieved 98.05%, 97.86%, 97.47%, and 97.30% accuracy, respectively. Secondly, Four Machine learning classifiers, RC, RF, J48, and BN, deployed on pre-optimized datasets obtained 98.47%, 97.83%, 97.79%, and 96.88% accuracy, respectively. It has been observed that a large dataset size and a pre-optimized dataset take a long time to build a classification model. To resolve this issue, the optimized hybrid features dataset used to classify the DL classifiers RNN, WiSARD, DL4J, and MLP shows 97.69%, 97.44%, 95.97%, and 97.25% accuracy. Similarly, the ML classifiers RC, RF, J48, and BN give 98.61%, 97.94%, 98.11%, and 96.91% accuracy, respectively. Optimized hybrid datasets improve classification accuracy and the time to build a model. The ML classifier RC shows promising results and gives a higher accuracy of 98.61% in the overall classification process. The RC classifier exhibited the following individual accuracies for each class: chondrosarcoma (98.66%), chordoma (98.33%), glioma (98.33%), meningioma (99.16%), neurofibromatosis (99.33%), and schwannoma (97.83%). Lastly, we will compare the ML and DL approaches and conclude that this research design proposed technique is robust and efficient, can reduce human error, and ensures that the proposed system performs efficiently when presented with MR images of new patients.

Future work

A hybrid model for classifying brain tumors could be developed in the future. The hybrid classification method involves extracting features through DL algorithms and applying ML classifiers for classification tasks. Advanced performance across various challenges has been attained through applying DL algorithms for feature extraction, a primary advantage being their capacity to handle large and complex datasets. However, it demands significant training resources and requires considerable data and computational power. This approach could enhance precision, and the study would be strengthened by utilizing a comprehensive dataset of imaging techniques, such as CT and X-rays.
