Diagnosis and Management of Inferior Alveolar Nerve Injury Caused by Calcium Hydroxide Extrusion during Root Canal Treatment: Case Report
Authors: Manal Almaslamani, Suliman Alhassawi, Prathibha Prasad, Maher Alshayeb
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis report highlights a rare occurrence of inferior alveolar nerve (IAN) injury resulting from calcium hydroxide extrusion during endodontic treatment. The patient experienced severe pain and paresthesia in the lower right lip following root canal treatment due to the inadvertent overflow of calcium hydroxide into the IAN canal. No surgical intervention was performed, and the patient received non-steroidal anti-inflammatory analgesics and vitamin B complex injections to manage the symptoms. At 6 months, complete resorption of the root canal material occurred, resulting in further reduction in paresthesia. After a 2-year follow-up, the complete disappearance of paresthesia occurred. The importance of the preoperative radiographs is highlighted. The lack of radiographic assessment at the patient’s request led to calcium hydroxide overflow.

INTRODUCTION

Calcium hydroxide, commonly used as an intracanal medication, has antimicrobial properties and aids in the disinfection and healing of root canals.[12] However, when improperly applied, it can cause tissue damage, including nerve injury.[34567891011121314] When calcium hydroxide applied to the root canal and inadvertently overflowed into the inferior alveolar nerve (IAN) canal, it may lead to postoperative paresthesia for the patient.[4567891011121314]

In cases where calcium hydroxide is overextended, severe necrosis of the gingival and oral mucosa can occur.[15] This can result in pain, inflammation, ulceration, and delayed healing of the affected tissues. Several case reports are discussed, highlighting the complications associated with overextending calcium hydroxide into surrounding tissues, including necrosis and nerve injury. De Bruyne et al.[15] described cases of overextending a calcium hydroxide intracanal dressing into the periradicular and soft tissue after iatrogenic perforation of maxillary incisors. The result was severe necrosis of the gingival and oral mucosa. Sharma et al.[16] described two cases in which calcium hydroxide was injected into an artery through the root canal system of maxillary canals.

The importance of proper technique and precautions during the placement of calcium hydroxide is emphasized to avoid such complications. Various techniques can be used for its placement in the root canal system, including the lentulo spiral technique, syringe and needle technique, and paper points or absorbent points technique. The chosen technique should ensure proper placement and coverage of the canal walls without overfilling or extrusion.[1718]

Accidental injury to the IAN during endodontic treatment is a rare but significant complication. This case report presents the clinical findings, diagnosis, management, and follow-up of a patient who experienced IAN injury due to the extrusion of calcium hydroxide during endodontic treatment. The management strategies and considerations for this complication are discussed based on general principles of endodontic care and clinical experience.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 45-year-old, medically healthy male patient was initially referred to the endodontic clinic for the completion of root canal treatment on the lower right second molar (#47). The patient presented to the endodontic clinic with severe pain in the lower right quadrant, specifically pointing to tooth #47, described as sharp spontaneous pain that worsened upon biting and disrupted sleep. Additionally, the patient reported severe electrical shooting pain, numbness, and tingling (paresthesia) in the right side of the lower lip.

History of the chief complaint

The patient had experienced symptoms of symptomatic irreversible pulpitis from the tooth #47 2 days prior. The general dentist performed pulp extirpation, placed non-setting calcium hydroxide, and a temporary filling. However, the patient’s pain intensified after the initial visit, and analgesics provided no relief. Concerned about the potential side effects, the patient was initially resistant to taking preoperative radiographs.

After 2 days of complaining of the severe pain, the general dentist referred the patient to the endodontist to complete the randomized controlled trial (RCT), thinking there might be a missed canal or unusual anatomy. No radiographs were associated with the referral letter.

Clinical findings

Upon examination, tooth #47 had a class II temporary filling (IRM) and exhibited a negative response to pulp sensibility testing. Tenderness to percussion was observed. The patient’s resistance to preoperative radiographs was addressed, emphasizing the necessity for diagnosis and legal requirements. The patient agreed to minimal radiographs.

Radiographic findings

A periapical radiograph of tooth #47 revealed a class II temporary filling, fused roots in close proximity to the inferior dental nerve (IDN) canal, and the presence of white radiopaque material in the IDN canal. A panoramic radiograph confirmed the extrusion of non-setting calcium hydroxide into the IDN canal [Figures 1 and 2].

A periapical radiograph showing the lower right quadrant. Tooth #47 had class II temporary filling, fused roots, and extrusion of the non-setting calcium hydroxide intra-canal medicament into the IAND canal

A panoramic radiograph showing the extrusion of non-setting calcium hydroxide medicament into the IAN canal

Diagnosis

A diagnosis of previously initiated root canal treatment with symptomatic apical periodontitis and extrusion of the intracanal medicament into the IDN canal, causing irritation of the IDN, was made.

Management

After explaining the condition to the patient, non-steroidal inflammatory analgesic was prescribed (400 mg three times daily). Vitamin B complex intramuscular (2 ml/IM) injection was prescribed one injection per day for 2 weeks.

Follow-up

After 2 weeks, the patient reported no pain, the absence of percussion tenderness, but persistent tingling and electrical sensations in the lower lip. The patient refused to have a follow-up periapical radiograph. Due to financial reasons, the patient chose to complete the endodontic treatment with the general dentist for cost purposes [Figures 3 and 4].

A periapical master cone radiograph for the tooth #47, showing the disappearance of non-setting calcium hydroxide in the IAN canal

One-month follow-up preapical radiograph of the tooth #47 showing dissolution of the non-setting calcium hydroxide in the IADN canal

Six months later, tooth #47 remained asymptomatic, although the tingling sensation in the lower lip persisted but with reduced severity. At the 1-year follow-up, the tooth continued to be asymptomatic, and the patient described near-normal sensation in the lower lip. Finally, at the 2-year follow-up, the tooth remained asymptomatic, and the complete disappearance of lip paresthesia was achieved.

The informed written consent form was obtained from the patient for the treatment as well as the publication.

DISCUSSION

This case report presents a situation where non-setting calcium hydroxide was accidentally injected into the IDN canal during root canal treatment, resulting in significant pain and paresthesia of the right lower lip.

Although specific clinical studies on the management of non-setting calcium hydroxide extrusion into the IDN canal are lacking, the proposed strategies align with general principles of managing nerve-related complications in endodontics and are supported by clinical expertise.[19] The management involves several considerations to alleviate symptoms and promote healing.[20] Communication with the patient and reassurance regarding the situation are important to address any concerns and alleviate anxiety.

Symptomatic relief can be achieved by prescribing non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs to manage pain and discomfort associated with nerve irritation. The choice of medication and dosage should be personalized based on the patient’s needs and medical history.[1920]

Vitamin B complex supplementation, administering vitamin B complex injections or oral supplements, may help promote nerve healing and reduce symptoms of paresthesia. While evidence specific to calcium hydroxide extrusion is limited, vitamin B supplementation has been suggested for nerve-related symptoms in various clinical scenarios.[1920]

Regular follow-up and monitoring of the patient’s symptoms and healing progress are important to ensure appropriate management and detect any changes or complications.[1718]

In severe cases or when symptoms persist despite conservative management, referral to an oral and maxillofacial surgeon or a neurologist may be necessary for further evaluation and potential surgical interventions, such as nerve decompression.[14151619]

In this case, the patient had reversible damage to IDN, in comparison with other case reports in the literature where the patient had to go through major surgery to remove the extrusive material.[4567891011121314]

There are no specific guidelines dedicated solely to the placement of calcium hydroxide into the root canal.[1718] However, there are general recommendations and techniques followed by endodontists for the proper placement of calcium hydroxide in the canal.

Regardless of which technique was used, the aim is carefully introducing the carrier into the canal while avoiding overfilling or extrusion beyond the apical foramen. The goal is to completely cover the canal walls with calcium hydroxide.[1718] The specific technique used may depend on factors, such as the consistency of the calcium hydroxide paste, the size and shape of the canal, and the clinician’s preference and experience.

The patient chose to be treated by the general practitioner (GP) as he was concerned about the cost of the treatment. Communication between colleagues is essential, and the endodontist who contacted the GP explained the importance of taking intraoperative radiographs to avoid any other material(s) extrusion into the IDN canal.

In this particular case, it is imperative to underscore the importance of acquiring preoperative periapical radiographs to precisely assess the length of the root canals and the proximity to the IDN canal before the administration of medicaments. By performing preoperative radiographs, the estimated length of the root canals can be assessed, which is essential in preventing accidental extrusion of materials into critical anatomical structures, including the IDN canal.

In this case report, the patient’s request of not having a preoperative radiograph was followed, but ended up with more severe postoperative pain and more costly management. More effort is needed by both physicians and dentists to educate the patients about the importance of taking X-rays for the dental treatments particularly root canal treatment.

The radiation dose associated with intraoral periapical radiographs can vary depending on several factors, including the type of X-ray machine used, technique settings, film or digital sensor sensitivity, and patient anatomy.[21] The effective dose of a single intraoral periapical radiograph typically ranges from 2 to 6 μSv for film-based radiographs and 3 to 9 μSv for digital radiographs (1 microsievert = 1 μSv).[21] These values may vary depending on the specific equipment and techniques used. The effective dose of a panoramic radiograph typically ranges from 2 to 23 μSv, with an average dose of approximately 10 μSv.[22] It is important to note that this is a rough estimate, and doses can vary depending on the specific machine and settings used. Panoramic radiographs generally have a higher dose compared to intraoral radiographs due to the larger area of exposure. To put it into perspective, this dose is comparable to the background radiation received during a few days to a few months of normal environmental exposure.[2324] The radiation dose associated with limited-field dental cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans can vary depending on the field of view (FOV) and scanning parameters. The effective dose for a dental CBCT scan typically ranges from 5 to 100 μSv, with an average dose of approximately 30 μSv. The effective dose for larger FOVs or higher resolution scans can be higher.[25] This range is comparable to several days to several months of background radiation exposure. It is important to note that doses can vary significantly depending on the specific CBCT machine, protocols, and patient characteristics.[25]

It is essential for dental professionals to adhere to the As Low As Reasonably Achievable (ALARA) principle when performing radiographic examinations, ensuring that the benefits of obtaining radiographic information outweigh the associated risks of radiation exposure. Dentists should follow proper techniques, use appropriate equipment, and adhere to national or international guidelines on radiation protection in dental radiology.[2425]

Small FOV CBCT can be considered an effective radiographic diagnostic device when endodontic-related IAN or mental foramen paresthesia is suspected. CBCT is able to provide detailed three-dimensional images of the tooth, the root canal system, and the surrounding tissue. Unfortunately, in this case, the patient refused to take CBCT for diagnosis and follow-up to assess the presence of the material in the IAN canal.[25]

The determination of whether calcium hydroxide extrusion is considered legally dentist negligence can vary depending on the specific circumstances and jurisdiction. Generally, in legal matters, negligence is assessed based on whether a dentist failed to meet the standard of care expected of a reasonably skilled and competent practitioner in the same field under similar circumstances.[1718]

In the legal context, it is generally accepted that dentists and endodontists have a professional obligation to take preoperative radiographs and at least one intraoperative radiograph during root canal treatment. These radiographs serve as a baseline assessment and provide essential information about the tooth’s anatomy, canal morphology, and any existing pathology. They also help in identifying potential complications, such as calcified canals or anatomical variations. Failure to obtain the necessary radiographs during root canal treatment may be seen as a deviation from the standard of care expected of a reasonably skilled and competent practitioner. In the event of adverse outcomes or complications, the absence of radiographs may contribute to allegations of negligence.[1718]

CONCLUSION

This case highlights the occurrence of IAN injury due to calcium hydroxide extrusion during endodontic treatment. Prompt diagnosis, effective communication with the patient, and appropriate management contribute to successful outcomes in managing such complications. Long-term follow-up demonstrates the potential for gradual resolution and restoration of normal sensory function in affected patients.
