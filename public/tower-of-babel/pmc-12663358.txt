Stress hyperglycemia ratio and albumin predict in-hospital outcomes in patients with acute myocardial infarction: a cohort study
Authors: Yuxiu Yang, Fei Gao, Xiaoteng Ma, Lixia Yang, Zhijian Wang, Yujie Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractElevated stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) is a putative risk indicator for acute myocardial infarction (AMI). However, the relationship between SHR, albumin, and subsequent cardiovascular adverse outcomes is less well-established. We consecutively included patients from a multi-center, prospective registry (NCT05337319) from January 2022 to October 2023 and analyzed their baseline SHR and albumin levels. Low and high SHR were determined using the optimal cutoff value. The primary outcome was in-hospital major adverse cardiovascular events, which were composed of all-cause mortality, cardiac shock, acute congestive heart failure (ACHF), reinfarction, and stroke. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to estimate albumin-stratified associations between SHR and outcomes. A total of 5269 patients presenting with AMI were included, of which 27.7% were high-SHR and 19.5% had hypoalbuminemia. Low-SHR normalbuminemia had the lowest in-hospital MACEs (1.4%), with which as the reference, high-SHR hypoalbuminemia had the highest increase in MACEs (adjusted OR, 6.800; 95% CI, 3.883–11.907; P < 0.001), followed by low-SHR hypoalbuminemia (adjusted OR, 3.033; 95% CI, 1.775–5.182, P < 0.001), and high-SHR normalbuminemia (adjusted OR, 2.375; 95% CI, 1.335–4.226, P = 0.003). High-SHR was associated with increased cardiovascular risk regardless of baseline albumin level for MACEs (normalbuminemia: adjusted OR, 2.325; 95% CI, 1.307–4.136, P = 0.004; hypoalbuminemia: adjusted OR, 2.213; 95% CI, 1.343–3.647, P = 0.002; P = 0.866 for interaction), and cardiac shock or ACHF. Higher SHR is prevalent among patients with AMI, especially in those with hypoalbuminemia. Introduction

The stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR), which represents the adjusted acute increase in admission glucose concentration for background glycemia estimated from glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), has been considered as a potential indicator concerning the severity of illness in critically ill individuals1,2. Mounting evidence suggests an association between elevated SHR and atherosclerotic cerebro-cardiovascular disease, including coronary artery disease and ischemic stroke3–7. Hypoalbuminemia is a surrogate to the pathobiology of infection, inflammation, and malnutrition, fostering an increased burden through the vicious cycle of sarcopenia, organic dysfunction, and cachexia, and it’s also a modifiable risk factor on which physicians could act8–10.

Despite both SHR and hypoalbuminemia independently predicting adverse outcomes in acute myocardial infarction (AMI)11–15, whether their combination improves risk stratification, and whether SHR’s prognostic impact persists regardless of albumin status, remains unknown. Crucially, these biomarkers reflect distinct pathophysiological pathways: SHR captures acute metabolic stress response, while albumin signifies chronic inflammatory/nutritional status1,9. Their interaction may identify a high-risk phenotype where metabolic derangement synergizes with systemic vulnerability—a hypothesis untested in prior studies focusing on single biomarkers.

To close the gap, we investigated the combined effect and interplay between SHR and hypoalbuminemia in a large, comprehensive data set of patients hospitalized with AMI, hypothesizing that elevated SHR is associated with increased clinical cardiovascular endpoints regardless of baseline albumin level.

Methods

Study design and population

We retrospectively examined patient data from the Prospective Registry of the Current Status of Care for Patients with Coronary Artery Disease (NCT05337319) initiated by the National Clinical Research Center for Cardiovascular Diseases, Beijing Anzhen Hospital; the ongoing registry includes 7772 patients with a final diagnosis of AMI between January 2022 and October 2023. Patients in the current study were 18 years or older, presenting with AMI. Patients with missing data on serum albumin, fasting blood glucose (FBG) measured within the first 24 h of hospital admission, and HbA1c were excluded (Fig. 1). The ultimate sample consisted of 5269 participants presented with AMI. The ethics committee of Beijing Anzhen Hospital, Capital Medical University, China, granted institutional review board approval for this research. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination of this research.

Flow chart of the study population enrollment and stratification. Abbreviations: AMI, acute myocardial infarction; FBG, fasting blood glucose; HbA1c, glycated hemoglobin; SHR, stress hyperglycemia ratio.

The SHR was calculated as follows: admission blood glucose (mmol/L)/ [1.59 × HbA1c (%) − 2.59]1. Patients were stratified into two groups (low-SHR and high-SHR) based on the optimal cutoff value of 0.92 with the results obtained from the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Hypoalbuminemia was defined by serum albumin levels ≤ 35 g/L. Patients were stratified into four groups by their SHR and albumin levels as low-SHR normalbuminemia, high-SHR normalbuminemia, low-SHR hypoalbuminemia, and high-SHR hypoalbuminemia.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was in-hospital MACEs, defined as a composite endpoint of all-cause death, cardiac shock or acute congestive heart failure (ACHF), and reinfarction or stroke during hospitalization.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages; numerical variables were summarized as the mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range), depending on the data distribution. Categorical variables were compared with the χ2 test, and continuous variables with Mann–Whitney non-parametric tests. The associations between the variables and the binary study outcomes were assessed using multivariable logistic regression models with an odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) in the total population and by stratified groups. Potential covariates were identified by reviewing existing literature and clinician consensus; the following variables were extracted from the baseline assessment data: demographic factors (age and sex); clinical characteristics (heart rate, systolic blood pressure, body mass index, hypertension, diabetes, previous AMI, previous ischemic stroke, diagnosis of ST-elevation myocardial infarction [STEMI], hemoglobin, leukocyte, platelet, triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL-C], estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate [eGFR], left ventricular ejection fraction[LVEF], left main coronary artery lesion [LMCAD] or prior left anterior descending [LAD] lesion, and multivessel coronary artery disease [CAD]); in-hospital management (percutaneous coronary intervention [PCI] and dual-antiplatelet therapy[DAPT]); their correlation with in-hospital mortality and total MACEs was displayed in Table S1. The assumption of regression analysis was checked by examining the interaction term between the SHR-albumin status. A two-sided P-value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS version 24 (SPSS Statistics, IBM Corp, Armonk, United States).

Results

A total of 5269 AMI patients were included, with 27.7% classified as high-SHR and 19.5% as hypoalbuminemia. The high-SHR/hypoalbuminemia group (6.3%) exhibited the highest-risk profile: predominant STEMI presentation (50.4%), lowest LVEF (median 48%), and frequent multivessel disease (56.1%). During hospitalization (median 5 days), 252 MACEs occurred (4.7%) (Table 1).

Discussion

Among 5269 individuals presenting with AMI from an ongoing comprehensive prospective registry, we for the first time demonstrate that higher SHR accompanied by hypoalbuminemia marks a severely unfavorable prognosis. Moreover, higher SHR is associated with cardiovascular risk regardless of baseline albumin value, which represents a large proportion of the individuals seen in clinical practice.

Stress hyperglycemia is characterized by a reactive increase in blood glucose levels triggered by acute stress, largely attributed to the activated nervous, endocrine, and immune mechanisms, including the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis and the sympathetic-adrenomedullary axis16–19. Mounting investigations have evidenced an association between higher SHR and adverse outcomes among patients with cardiovascular diseases. Yang et al. reported a U-shaped or J-shaped nonlinear correlation between SHR and early and late adverse cardiovascular events among acute coronary syndrome (ACS) patients treated with drug-eluting stent implantation20. Zhang et al. demonstrated a J-shaped association between the SHR and the risk of cardiovascular mortality in ACS patients with triple-vessel disease, especially those with existing diabetes; it also provided an incremental value over the standard SYNTAX II model5. Gao et al found that SHR was closely related to in-hospital MACEs in STEMI patients regardless of diabetic status21. Mohammed et al. confirmed that elevated SHR was independently associated with an increased risk for MACEs among patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction22. Stress hyperglycemia is a common occurrence among patients grappling with trauma and critical illness, which was observed in nearly 10% of patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU)23,24, and the higher SHR was reported to be approximately 20% among patients with ACS or AMI in prior studies5,20,25. The SHR has evolved as a robust risk indicator providing a reliable discrimination ability for clinicians in the diagnosis and treatment of critically ill ACS patients.

There is biological plausibility suggesting a possible synergistic effect between stress hyperglycemia and hypoalbuminemia on cardiovascular risk. Critically ill patients are prone to have low serum albumin due to capillary leakage into interstitial fluid, inhibition of albumin synthesis, poor nutritional status, excessive loss of albumin, and increased catabolism26,27. The increased likelihood of poor outcomes such as cachexia, mortality, morbidity, and prolonged ICU and hospital stay in critically ill patients with hypoalbuminemia is well recognized25–27. Hypoalbuminemia is frequent in ACS patients and the prevalence is estimated between 30 and 50% across the whole spectrum of population28–30. Pan et al. demonstrated that low serum albumin level was an independent predictor of longer hospital stays in patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention for ACS, with shorter stays observed for increasing serum albumin levels31. Plakht et al. reported that decreased albumin levels on admission are significantly associated with long-term all-cause mortality in hospital survivors of AMI with a “dose–response” type association32. Hypoalbuminemia is also proved to be associated with poor reperfusion at the epicardial and tissue level after PCI33. These findings emphasize the important role of albumin in cardiovascular diseases and suggest its potential application value in disease management, risk assessment, and treatment.

Using a large-scale comprehensive AMI patient dataset, the present study provides the first evidence that the double burden of stress hyperglycemia and hypoalbuminemia is not uncommon in AMI patients and clarifies a strong unfavorable in-hospital prognostic implication; besides, the SHR is associated with post-infarct in-hospital cardiovascular risk independently and regardless of baseline albumin level. Patients presented with both stress hyperglycemia and hypoalbuminemia prevailed in 6.3% of our AMI cohort, who displayed the highest proportion of comorbidities, including hypertension, diabetes, prior ischemic stroke, renal insufficiency, and STEMI diagnosis out of the total subgroups. The synergistic detriment is profound: patients with both high-SHR and hypoalbuminemia had a 6.8-fold higher MACE risk, substantially exceeding risks from isolated high-SHR or hypoalbuminemia. The high-SHR/hypoalbuminemia group exhibited 26.4% MACE incidence, 19-fold higher than the low-risk group and 6.4-fold higher than high-SHR alone. Crucially, the absence of interaction underscores that SHR and albumin may operate through distinct pathophysiological pathways, which makes their combination biologically non-redundant and clinically additive. This dual-biomarker approach uniquely enables early identification of high-risk phenotypes, offering a practical advantage by leveraging routinely available tests for immediate implementation even in resource-constrained settings. Clinicians need to remain vigilant for the identification of the various SHR and albumin phenotypes, particularly in the at-risk population; tailored secondary prevention strategies can help improve the post-infarct care bundle with dynamic biochemical monitoring, nutritional supplements, risk stratification, and management strategies. Further validation should assess whether integrating SHR and albumin provides incremental value to established AMI risk models, potentially refining dynamic risk assessment protocols.

However, this study has its limitations. As an observational study, we couldn’t conclude direct causality or account for other residual confounding factors. Although several other risk factors or biomarkers were examined, data were lacking on some covariates (e.g., family history of premature coronary artery disease, lipoprotein(a), and malignancy); additionally, information on socioeconomic or psychosocial status was missing. Since only in-hospital data were available, we couldn’t access post-discharge or long-term survival information, and analysis of potential time-dependent effects was not applicable. Besides, although we adjusted for diabetes status and glycemic markers (e.g., HbA1c, fasting glucose), we did not evaluate insulin resistance (IR) using gold standard methods (e.g., hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp) or surrogate indices such as the Triglyceride-Glucose index11; future studies should incorporate IR metrics to elucidate further its interaction with stress hyperglycemia and hypoalbuminemia in AMI outcomes. Moreover, while we employed complete-case analysis for covariates with low missingness, sensitivity analyses were not performed; future studies should incorporate these methods to validate our findings. Finally, as this study is based on a Chinese cohort, the results should be interpreted cautiously and not directly generalized to other ethnicities. Future prospective studies are warranted to assess whether targeted interventions for patients with combined SHR-albumin abnormalities (e.g., protocolized glycemic control and nutritional support) can improve clinical outcomes, thereby advancing personalized AMI management.

Conclusion

In a large-scale comprehensive registry of AMI patients in China, stress hyperglycemia is prevalent, especially among those with hypoalbuminemia. Higher SHR is associated with in-hospital cardiovascular risk independently and regardless of serum albumin level. As routinely measurable biomarkers, screening for SHR and albumin status provides a practical tool for immediate risk stratification and personalized secondary prevention, enabling clinicians to prioritize intensive management for high-risk patients to aid in individualized AMI care.
