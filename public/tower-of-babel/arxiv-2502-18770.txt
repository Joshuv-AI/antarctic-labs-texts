Reward Shaping to Mitigate Reward Hacking in RLHF
Jiayi Fu, Xuandong Zhao, Chengyuan Yao, Heng Wang, Qi Han, Yanghua Xiao, Yuxia Wang

Abstract
Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) is widely used to align large language models (LLMs) with human preferences. However, RLHF remains vulnerable to reward hacking , whereby a policy exploits imperfections in the reward function instead of learning the intended behavior, thereby undermining alignment. Although reward shaping can stabilize RLHF training and partially mitigate reward hacking, shaping methods and their underlying design principles have not been systematically investigated. To address this gap, we conduct a comprehensive study of prevalent reward-shaping techniques. Our analysis identifies two key design principles: (1) the reinforcement-learning reward should be bounded, and (2) it should grow rapidly at first and then gradually saturate. Motivated by these principles, we propose Preference as Reward (PAR), a novel method that uses the latent preferences encoded in the reward model as the reinforcement-learning signal. We further show that PAR possesses two variance-reduction properties that stabilize RLHF training and substantially widen the practical window for early stopping. Our evaluation consists of two parts. First, we compare PAR with several other reward-shaping strategies using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) as the reinforcement-learning algorithm and Gemma2-2B as the base model. Second, we compare PAR with the vanilla baseline (i.e., unshaped reward) across four base models and four reinforcement-learning algorithms. In the first set of experiments, PAR consistently outperforms other reward-shaping methods and also reflects high data efficiency and robustness. The second set of experiments shows that PAR is particularly effective for actor-critic RL algorithms when value estimates become unstable and demonstrates its effectiveness across different base models. The code is available at https://github.com/PorUna-byte/PAR.



1
Introduction

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) is widely used to align large language models (LLMs) with human intentions and improve their capabilities
(
Ouyang et al., 2022
;
OpenAI, 2024
;
Bai et al., 2022
;
Guo et al., 2024
)
. However, RLHF remains vulnerable to
reward hacking
, whereby a policy exploits weaknesses in the reward model to obtain high rewards without genuinely improving alignment or response quality
(
Amodei et al., 2016
;
Gao et al., 2023
;
Singhal et al., 2023
)
. This may lead to undesirable behaviors such as repetitive or unnecessarily verbose responses.

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
(
Schulman et al., 2017
)
is widely adopted in RLHF
(
Ouyang et al., 2022
)
, yet it is particularly susceptible to reward hacking
(
Gao et al., 2023
)
. Existing mitigation strategies often reshape proxy rewards through clipping or rescaling
(
Dai et al., 2024
;
Wang et al., 2024b
)
. Nevertheless, reward-shaping methods have not been systematically compared, and their underlying design principles remain unclear.

To address this gap, we systematically study reward shaping for RLHF. Our analysis suggests that excessively large rewards can become decoupled from true response quality and destabilize critic learning, motivating our first design principle:
(1) the RL reward should be bounded
. We further find that optimization is more stable in low-reward regions, leading to our second principle:
(2) the RL reward should grow rapidly at first and then gradually saturate
.

Figure 1
:
RLHF training with reward shaping. The reward model assigns proxy rewards to policy responses, which are then optionally transformed using reference rewards (dashed box) before policy optimization. PAR applies a sigmoid transformation to the centered proxy reward.

Guided by these principles, we introduce
Preference as Reward
(PAR), a reward-shaping method illustrated in Figure
1
. PAR applies a sigmoid function to the centered proxy reward:
r_{\text{RL}}=\operatorname{sigmoid}(r-r_{\text{ref}})
,
where
r
and
r_{\text{ref}}
denote the rewards of the policy and reference responses, respectively. Because the policy is typically initialized from the reference model, the centered reward is initially close to zero, where the sigmoid has its steepest slope and thus facilitates rapid learning. As training progresses, the sigmoid gradually saturates, limiting extreme rewards and improving stability.

PAR is also closely related to the Bradley–Terry preference model
(
Bradley and Terry, 1952
)
. When exponentiated proxy rewards are viewed as latent preference strengths, analogous to Elo ratings
(
Elo, 1978
)
,
r_{\text{RL}}
represents the reward model’s preference for the policy response over the reference response. PAR therefore converts absolute proxy scores into bounded and interpretable preference signals.
We further show that PAR reduces the variances of both accumulated returns and policy-gradient estimates, two factors that are critical to stable RLHF training. Although reward shaping cannot eliminate reward hacking under sufficiently long training, PAR delays its onset and widens the practical window for early stopping.

Our experiment consists of two parts. First, we compare PAR with several other reward-shaping methods using Gemma2-2B
(
Gemma Team, 2024
)
, UltraFeedback Binarized
(
Cui et al., 2023
)
, and PPO. PAR achieves the strongest performance on both the in-domain test set and out-of-domain benchmarks like AlpacaEval 2.0
(
Li et al., 2023
)
and MT-Bench
(
Zheng et al., 2023a
)
. It also requires only a single reference reward to achieve high performance and remains robust to reward hacking after two training epochs.
Second, we evaluate PAR across multiple base models and optimization algorithms on HH-RLHF
(
Bai et al., 2022
)
. PAR consistently outperforms the unshaped-reward baseline under PPO and Advantage Actor-Critic (A2C) algorithms, mitigating reward hacking and stabilizing critic learning. Its effect is smaller under Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), supporting our hypothesis that critic stability is an important source of PAR’s benefits. We also find that Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is already highly effective on preference datasets.

Our main contributions are as follows:

•

We identify two principles for effective reward shaping: boundedness and rapid initial growth followed by gradual saturation.

•

We introduce PAR, connect it to the preferences encoded by the reward model, and establish its variance-reduction properties.

•

We empirically verify that PAR mitigates reward hacking, stabilizes training, and generalizes across models, datasets, and optimization algorithms.

2
Related Work

Characterizing reward hacking.

Reward hacking occurs when optimization exploits discrepancies between a proxy reward and the intended objective
(
Amodei et al., 2016
)
. In a synthetic setting,
Gao et al. (2023)
show that gold reward can decline as proxy-reward optimization intensifies, while
Singhal et al. (2023)
identify response length as a major driver of apparent RLHF improvements. KL regularization constrains policy drift but provides no guarantee under heavy-tailed reward misspecification
(
Kwa et al., 2024
)
. RLHF can also make incorrect responses more convincing
(
Wen et al., 2025
)
, and outcome-only supervision can reward invalid reasoning that reaches correct answers
(
Yuan et al., 2026
)
. These findings motivate interventions throughout the RLHF pipeline.

Robust reward modeling.

Reward-model ensembles mitigate overoptimization, although correlated errors may persist
(
Coste et al., 2024
;
Eisenstein et al., 2024
)
; WARM improves efficiency by averaging reward models in weight space
(
Rame et al., 2024
)
. Other approaches include information-bottleneck reward modeling
(
Miao et al., 2024
)
, hidden-state regularization
(
Yang et al., 2024
)
, and causal data augmentation to separate contextual preferences from spurious artifacts
(
Liu et al., 2025
)
. Length-specific defenses use disentangled reward heads
(
Chen et al., 2024
)
, context-adaptive length modeling
(
Bu et al., 2025
)
, post-hoc calibration
(
Huang et al., 2025
)
, or nonlinear bias fitting
(
Zhao et al., 2026
)
. For reasoning tasks, rubric-based rewards evaluate intermediate steps rather than only final answers
(
Yuan et al., 2026
)
.

Policy-side mitigation.

Constrained RLHF restricts reward optimization to regions where reward models remain reliable
(
Moskovitz et al., 2024
)
. AdvPO incorporates lightweight reward uncertainty into a distributionally robust objective
(
Zhang et al., 2024
)
, while BSPO regularizes out-of-distribution value estimates
(
Dai et al., 2025
)
. EPPO penalizes excessive final-layer energy loss
(
Miao et al., 2025
)
, and PF-PPO filters samples with unreliable rewards
(
Zhang et al., 2025
)
. These policy-side methods are complementary to post-hoc reward transformation.

Reward transformation.

Reward transformation modifies the optimization signal without retraining the reward model.
Wang et al. (2024b)
derive a log-sigmoid-centered transformation of Bradley–Terry rewards, while
Shen et al. (2024)
construct contrastive rewards relative to baseline responses. Related inference-time work regularizes best-of-
N
selection using minimum Bayes risk
(
Jinnai et al., 2024
)
. PAR instead uses the Bradley–Terry preference probability itself as the RL reward relative to a reference response, yielding a bounded signal in
[0,1]
that can be combined with reward-model and policy-side defenses.

3
Method

3.1
Design Principles

As detailed in Section
1
, we restate our two design principles here: (1) RL reward should be bounded, (2) RL reward should grow rapidly at first and then gradually saturate. To elucidate the rationale behind these principles, we examine the PPO policy and critic loss functions for prompt
x
and response
y
(notation detailed in Table
3
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{policy}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x)}\bigg[\min\bigg(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})}\cdot A_{t},\text{clip}\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon\right)\cdot A_{t}\bigg)\bigg],

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{critic}}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x)}\big[||V_{\alpha}(x,y_{<t})-G_{t}||_{2}^{2}\big].

For the policy loss,
A_{t}=\sum_{l=t}^{T}(\gamma\lambda)^{l-t}\delta_{l}
represents the generalized advantage estimation (GAE) at token
t
, where
\delta_{t}=r_{t}+\gamma V_{\alpha_{\text{old}}}(s_{t+1})-V_{\alpha_{\text{old}}}(s_{t})
is the temporal difference (TD) error.
\pi_{\theta}
denotes the current policy model, and
\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}
refers to the policy model from the previous iteration.

V_{\alpha_{\text{old}}}
is the critic’s value function from the previous iteration. For the critic loss,
G_{t}=\sum_{l=t}^{T}\gamma^{l-t}r_{l}
represents the return, defined as the discounted sum of per-token rewards.
The per-token reward at position
t
, denoted as
r_{t}
, is defined as:

r_{t}=\begin{cases}r_{\text{RL}}-\eta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})}&\text{if }t=T\\
-\eta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})}&\text{if }t<T\end{cases}

Figure 2
:
Loss curves from PPO training show that PAR exhibits greater stability, particularly in critic loss, compared to Vanilla training. This stability is attributed to PAR’s bounded RL reward.

The first principle, advocating for bounded RL rewards, is crucial for stabilizing critic training. Excessively large rewards can hinder the critic model’s ability to accurately learn the value function, as illustrated in Figure
2
. We hypothesize that this issue arises from the nature of the regression loss used in the critic model. Specifically, large RL rewards
r_{\text{RL}}
lead to large variance of returns
G_{t}
(see Theorem
3.1
), making the critic loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{critic}}(\alpha)
more challenging to optimize. Furthermore, this effect propagates to the excessive advantage estimate
A_{t}
, rendering it unstable and leading to overly aggressive policy updates.
The second principle aims to encourage the model to learn more aggressively in the low-reward region, which we find to be relatively safer to optimize. In contrast, the high-reward region is more susceptible to reward hacking. Therefore, we design the RL reward to converge gradually in later stages of training.

We investigate several candidate functions that satisfy the two design principles, with a primary focus on sigmoid-like functions. This choice is motivated by the theoretical result that the sigmoid function minimizes the variance of the policy gradient among unbiased shaping functions under logistic preference noise (see Theorem
3.2
). The corresponding curves for these candidate functions are illustrated in Figure
4(b)
.

3.2
Preference as Reward

After careful consideration and empirical evaluation, we recommend using the sigmoid function applied to centered rewards as the reward shaping method. The sigmoid function is bounded, has the steepest slope at the initial point (zero), and converges gradually to its upper bound of one. This property makes it particularly suitable for stabilizing the RL training process.
Furthermore, our analysis reveals that this shaping approach is intrinsically linked to the hidden preferences encoded within the reward model. Given a reward model
r_{\phi}
, the hidden preference between two responses
y
and
y^{\prime}
to a prompt
x
can be expressed as:

\mathcal{P}_{\phi}(y\succ y^{\prime}|x)=\sigma(r_{\phi}(x,y)-r_{\phi}(x,y^{\prime}))
.
This formulation shows that applying the sigmoid function to centered rewards corresponds precisely to the preference score of the policy response over the reference response. Consequently, we term this method
Preference As Reward (PAR)
. To enhance stability, we use
M
reference rewards:

r_{\text{RL}}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sigma(r-r_{\text{ref}}^{m})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathcal{P}_{\phi}(y\succ y_{\text{ref}}^{m})
. The pseudo-code for the reward shaping procedure under PAR is presented in Algorithm
12
. The overall reward shaping pipeline is illustrated separately in Figure
1
.

3.3
Theoretical Analysis

We further establish that our proposed PAR method satisfies two key theoretical properties: (1) The return variance is upper bounded, and (2) Policy gradient variance is minimized among unbiased shaping functions under logistic preference noise. A rigorous derivation of these guarantees is provided in Appendix
F
. These theoretical foundations are essential for ensuring the robustness and reliability of PAR in practical applications.

Theorem 3.1

(Bounded rewards reduce return variance)
.

Let
\gamma\in[0,1)
and define the discounted return

G_{t}\;=\;\sum_{l=t}^{T}\gamma^{\,l-t}r_{l},

where the per-step reward satisfies
|r_{l}|<1
,
then

\text{Var}[G_{t}]\ \leq\ \frac{1}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}
.

Theorem 3.2

(Sigmoid is the minimum-variance unbiased shaping under logistic preference noise)
.

Sample a prompt
x\in\mathcal{D},y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x),y_{\mathrm{ref}}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot|x)
, let

z(x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}})=r_{\phi}(x,y)-r_{\phi}(x,y_{\rm ref})
.

Define the REINFORCE estimator
\tilde{g}=\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\,\tilde{r}
.
where
\tilde{r}
may use any additional randomness but must satisfy the
pointwise unbiasedness constraint

\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}\mid x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}}]=\sigma(z(x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}})).

Then the unique minimum-variance estimator in this class is

g_{\sigma}:=\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\,\sigma(z(x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}})),

and for every estimator
\tilde{g}
,

\text{Var}\!\left(\tilde{g}\right)\ \geq\ \text{Var}\!\left(g_{\sigma}\right).

Theorem
3.1
addresses the stability of critic model training, while Theorem
3.2
focuses on stabilizing policy model training. Together, these variance reduction properties help mitigate the reward hacking problem by enabling a prolonged time window during which the policy model maintains high performance.

4
Comparison of Reward-Hacking Mitigation Methods

This first set of experiments evaluates the two proposed reward-shaping principles, compares PAR with existing reward-hacking mitigation methods, and examines the data efficiency and robustness of PAR.

4.1
Experimental Setting

Datasets and models.

We use UltraFeedback Binarized
(
Cui et al., 2023
)
as the training dataset, Gemma2-2B
(
Gemma Team, 2024
)
as the base model, and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) as the RL algorithm.

Mitigation baselines.

We compare PAR with seven reward-hacking mitigation methods:

•

WARM

(
Rame et al., 2024
)
averages the weights of multiple reward models and uses the resulting model to provide rewards during RL training.

•

ODIN

(
Chen et al., 2024
)
jointly trains length-correlated and length-decorrelated reward heads. Only the length-decorrelated quality head is used during RL training.

•

Reg

(
Dai et al., 2024
)
adds an
\ell_{2}
regularization term to the reward-model training objective:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reward}}=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim D}\left[-\log\sigma\!\left(r_{\phi}(x,y_{w})-r_{\phi}(x,y_{l})\right)+\beta\lVert r_{\phi}(x,y_{w})\rVert_{2}^{2}+\beta\lVert r_{\phi}(x,y_{l})\rVert_{2}^{2}\right].

•

Meanstd
standardizes the reward using its running mean and standard deviation:

r_{\mathrm{RL}}=\frac{r-\mu}{s},

where
\mu
and
s
are the running mean and standard deviation, respectively.

•

Clip
clips the reward:

r_{\mathrm{RL}}=\operatorname{clip}(r,\mu-s,\mu+s).

•

Minmax
rescales the reward using its running minimum and maximum:

r_{\mathrm{RL}}=\frac{r-r_{\min}}{r_{\max}-r_{\min}},

where
r_{\min}
and
r_{\max}
are the running minimum and maximum, respectively.

•

LSC

(
Wang et al., 2024b
)
applies the log-sigmoid-centered transformation

r_{\mathrm{RL}}=\log\sigma\!\left(r-r_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(0.85)}\right),

where
r_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(0.85)}
is the
85
th percentile of the Gaussian distribution fitted using the mean and variance of the reference rewards.

Evaluation metrics.

We monitor two test-set metrics during training: the
proxy reward
and the
win rate
. The win rate measures how often the policy response is preferred to the SFT response by DeepSeek-V3
(
DeepSeek-AI, 2024
)
. The horizontal axis denotes the cumulative number of training examples.
For AlpacaEval 2.0
(
Li et al., 2023
)
, we report the length-controlled (LC) win rate, raw win rate, and average response length. Both win rates are computed against the SFT model using DeepSeek-V3 as the judge. For MT-Bench
(
Zheng et al., 2023a
)
, DeepSeek-V3 evaluates the first-turn, second-turn, and overall performance.

Training details.

After removing noisy examples from UltraFeedback Binarized, we train the SFT model on
the chosen responses for two epochs and the reward model—the base model with a linear
reward head—for one epoch, both at a learning rate of
5\times 10^{-6}
. The policy is
initialized from the SFT model and trained for one epoch at
3\times 10^{-7}
, and the
critic from the reward model at
5\times 10^{-6}
. All stages use a linear schedule with
warm-up over the first
0.1
epoch. We evaluate every
0.1
epoch, yielding ten
checkpoints per mitigation method. Full details are in
Appendix
B.1
.

Principle 1: Bounded Rewards

To evaluate the principle that
the RL reward should be bounded
, we vary the KL-penalty coefficient and impose different upper bounds on the reward during PPO training. As shown in Figure
3
, restricting excessive reward optimization substantially mitigates reward hacking. Increasing the KL coefficient from
0.01
to
0.1
lowers the proxy reward while improving the win rate. Imposing a smaller reward ceiling produces a similar effect, providing direct evidence that limiting reward magnitude improves training stability.

We next evaluate the principle that
the RL reward should grow rapidly at first and then gradually saturate
. We compare several sigmoid-like functions and their centered and uncentered variants, as shown in Figure
4(a)
.
Centered transformations consistently achieve higher win rates than their uncentered counterparts. Because the policy is initialized from the reference model, centered rewards are concentrated near zero at the beginning of training, where sigmoid-like functions have their largest gradients. This facilitates rapid early learning. By contrast, uncentered rewards may lie in regions with weaker or poorly controlled gradients. The right-shifted Slow-Grow-Fast-Converge (SgFc) function further illustrates this effect: its small initial gradient yields lower early win rates, while its abrupt transition and saturation are followed by reward hacking later in training (Figure
5
). These results support the need for rapid initial growth followed by gradual saturation.

Comparison of Mitigation Methods

In this setting, WARM, Minmax, and PAR mitigate the decline in win rate, with PAR achieving the highest win rate at the end of training.

Test-set performance.

We compare PAR with methods that either reshape the reward or modify the reward model. Figure

shows that vanilla PPO, which directly optimizes the unshaped proxy reward, exhibits severe reward hacking: the proxy reward continues to increase while the win rate declines. In this setting, ODIN, Reg, Meanstd, Clip, and LSC do not prevent this divergence. WARM, Minmax, and PAR provide varying degrees of mitigation over one epoch, with PAR achieving the highest final win rate.

Benchmark performance.

To evaluate generalization beyond the training distribution, we select the checkpoint obtained after one epoch for each reward hacking mitigation method and evaluate it on AlpacaEval 2.0 and MT-Bench. As shown in Table

, the benchmark results broadly agree with the test-set performance in Figure

. Vanilla PPO suffers severe performance degradation. PAR and Minmax perform strongest overall, while WARM also yields substantial improvements over vanilla PPO. PAR achieves the highest LC and raw win rates on AlpacaEval 2.0 and the highest first-turn, second-turn, and overall scores on MT-Bench.

Abalation study

We also do some ablation studies about the data efficiency, robustness, and calibration. The result shows that PAR is highly data-efficient, requires only one reference reward to achieve good performance (i.e., m=1), and is robust against prolonged training to 2 epochs. Validation of PAR across Base Models and Optimization Algorithms

The preceding experiments focus on a single base model and RL algorithm. In this section, we extend our evaluation to multiple base models and optimization algorithms.

Experimental Setting

We use the HH-RLHF dataset
(

)

6
Human Evaluation

To reduce reliance on a single LLM judge and introduce greater diversity in automated evaluation, we use DeepSeek-V3 for the Part-1 experiments and GPT-5-nano for the Part-2 experiments, and additionally conduct a small-scale human evaluation to assess the reliability of the LLM-as-a-judge results.

7
Limitations

Although PAR effectively mitigates reward hacking and stabilizes RLHF training, it does not substantially improve peak performance, as measured by the win rate of the best checkpoint. Moreover, the dynamics of reward shaping—including the optimal rate of initial growth and the appropriate pace of saturation—remain insufficiently understood. Also, the datasets are all preference dialogue datasets where the simple and effective DPO already performs very well; a further study on the reasoning datasets remains as future research. And on the Qwen3-4B-Base model, the effectiveness is not obvious, indicating that PAR can work on some base models, not all.

8
Conclusion

We identify two principles for designing effective reward-shaping methods: the RL reward should be bounded, and it should exhibit rapid initial growth followed by gradual saturation. Guided by these principles, we introduce
Preference as Reward
(PAR) and establish two variance-reduction properties that contribute to stable RLHF training.
Under the setting of the first set of experiments, across a broad range of reward-hacking mitigation methods, PAR achieves the strongest performance after one training epoch and maintains a high win rate after two epochs, demonstrating robustness under prolonged optimization. PAR is also highly data-efficient, requiring only a single reference reward to achieve strong performance.
Under the setting of the second set of experiments, PAR demonstrates effectiveness across different base models and Critic-based RL algorithms.

Dataset preprocessing.

We conduct the Part-1 experiments on the UltraFeedback Binarized

Compute and optimization schedule.

Appendix C
Evaluation

C.1
Test-Set Win Rate

We use DeepSeek-V3 and GPT-5-nano as pairwise judges to compare responses generated by the SFT and policy models on the test set. The complete system prompt and user-input format are provided in Figure
8
.

To mitigate positional bias
(
Wang et al., 2024a
)
, each response pair is evaluated twice with the presentation order reversed. Given responses A and B, the judge first evaluates them in the order A–B and then in the order B–A. In each evaluation, the preferred response receives a score of 1, the non-preferred response receives 0, and each response receives 0.5 in the case of a tie. We then aggregate the scores across the two orderings. The response with the higher aggregate score is considered the winner; if the aggregate scores are equal, each response receives one-half of a win. The policy win rate is computed by averaging its win count across all test examples.

C.2
Benchmark Evaluation

We additionally evaluate the models on AlpacaEval 2.0 and MT-Bench, using DeepSeek-V3 as the judge. The reported metrics are defined below.

AlpacaEval 2.0.

•

LC Win Rate
: The length-controlled win rate estimates how often the policy response is preferred to the baseline response while adjusting for the influence of response length.

•

Win Rate
: The raw win rate measures the proportion of pairwise comparisons in which the policy response is preferred to the baseline response.

•

Length
: The average response length, as computed by the evaluation pipeline, provides an indication of model verbosity.

MT-Bench.

•

T1
: The first-turn score evaluates the relevance, coherence, and informativeness of the model’s initial response on a 0–10 scale.

•

T2
: The second-turn score evaluates the model’s ability to preserve conversational context and provide a consistent, high-quality follow-up response on the same scale.

•

Overall
: The overall score is the average of the first- and second-turn scores.

Appendix E
Comparison of Some Reward Shaping Techniques

Previous work has explored various approaches to reward transformation in RLHF.
Shen et al. (2024)
proposed contrastive rewards that share similarities with our method. Their approach employs a reference model to generate multiple baseline responses (
y_{\text{ref}}^{m}
), computing rewards as:

r_{\text{RL}}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left[r(x,y)-r(x,y_{\text{ref}}^{m})\right].

This method primarily focuses on enhancing PPO through contrastive learning against reference responses.

Ahmadian et al. (2024)
revisited the REINFORCE algorithm for RLHF, adopting a response-centric approach analogous to GRPO. Their formulation centers on rewards using multiple sampled responses:

g=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left[r(x,y_{i})-\frac{1}{M-1}\sum_{j\neq i}r(x,y_{j})\right]\nabla\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}|x),

where
y_{1},\ldots,y_{M}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)
. This work emphasizes algorithmic simplicity and stability by replacing PPO with REINFORCE.

Wang et al. (2024b)
introduced a log-sigmoid reward transformation for multi-reward integration:

r_{\text{RL}}=\text{log-sigmoid}\left[r(x,y)-r(x,y_{\text{ref}}^{.85})\right].

Where the
r_{\text{ref}}^{.85}
denotes the 85th percentile of the normal distribution, built from the mean and variance of the reference rewards.
Their method specifically addresses the challenge of effectively combining multiple reward signals during training.

In this work, we present Preference As Reward (PAR), which applies a sigmoid transformation to mitigate reward hacking:

r_{\text{RL}}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\text{sigmoid}\left(r(x,y)-r(x,y_{\text{ref}}^{m})\right).

Our approach specifically targets reward hacking while maintaining stable policy optimization.

We give a principled justification for
Preference As Reward
(PAR) here.

F.1

PAR has the key property
|r_{l}|<1,\forall l

\eta
in Section
3
; here
r_{l}

(2)

F.2
The Justification of the Sigmoid Function

Probability space and notation.

Fix policy parameters
\theta
.
A single stochastic gradient sample is generated by the following random process:

(i) draw a prompt
x\sim\mathcal{D}
, and a reference response
y_{\mathrm{ref}}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot|x)
;

(ii) draw a response
y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)
;

Denote

z(x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}}):=r_{\phi}(x,y)-r_{\phi}(x,y_{\mathrm{ref}})

We allow an alternative shaped reward
\tilde{r}
that may further depend on
additional randomness
\omega
(e.g., sampling noise), i.e.,
\tilde{r}=\tilde{r}(x,y,\omega)
.
Unless otherwise specified, all expectations and variances,

\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
and
\mathrm{Var}(\cdot)
, are taken over the joint randomness of

x\sim\mathcal{D},\quad y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x),\quad y_{\mathrm{ref}}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x),\quad\omega

With
\theta
treated as fixed.
We use
\mathbb{E}[\cdot\mid x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}}]
and
\mathrm{Var}(\cdot\mid x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}})
to denote conditional
expectation/variance where
x
,
y
and
y_{\mathrm{ref}}
are fixed and the remaining randomness is
over
\omega
only.

Theorem F.2

(Sigmoid is the minimum-variance unbiased shaping under logistic preference noise)
.

Define the REINFORCE estimator
\tilde{g}=\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\,\tilde{r}
.

where
\tilde{r}
may use any additional randomness but must satisfy the
pointwise unbiasedness constraint

\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}\mid x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}}]=\sigma(z(x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}})).

Then the unique minimum-variance estimator in this class is

g_{\sigma}:=\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\,\sigma(z(x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}})),

and for every estimator
\tilde{g}
,

\text{Var}\!\left(\tilde{g}\right)\ \geq\ \text{Var}\!\left(g_{\sigma}\right).

Proof.

Condition on
(x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}})
. Then
S:=\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)
is deterministic,
while all randomness in
\tilde{g}
comes from
\tilde{r}
.
We apply the law of total variance:

\text{Var}(\tilde{g})=\text{Var}\!\big(\mathbb{E}[\tilde{g}\mid x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}}]\big)+\mathbb{E}\!\big[\text{Var}(\tilde{g}\mid x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}})\big].

By the unbiasedness constraint,
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}\mid x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}}]=\sigma(z(x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}}))
, hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tilde{g}\mid x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}}]=S\,\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}\mid x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}}]

\displaystyle=S\,\sigma(z(x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}}))=\mathbb{E}[g_{\sigma}\mid x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}}].

Therefore,

\text{Var}\!\big(\mathbb{E}[\tilde{g}\mid x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}}]\big)=\text{Var}\!\big(\mathbb{E}[g_{\sigma}\mid x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}}]\big)=\text{Var}(g_{\sigma}),

because
g_{\sigma}
is deterministic given
(x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}})
and thus

\text{Var}(g_{\sigma}\mid x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}})=0
.

Moreover, still conditioning on
(x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}})
,

\text{Var}(\tilde{g}\mid x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}})=\text{Var}\big(S\,\tilde{r}\mid x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}}\big)=(S)^{2}\text{Var}(\tilde{r}\mid x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}})\ \geq\ 0.

Putting these together gives

\text{Var}(\tilde{g})=\text{Var}(g_{\sigma})+\mathbb{E}\!\big[(S)^{2}\text{Var}(\tilde{r}\mid x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}})\big]\ \geq\ \text{Var}(g_{\sigma}).

Equality holds iff
\text{Var}(\tilde{r}\mid x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}})=0
almost surely, i.e.,
\tilde{r}
is
deterministic given
(x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}})
. Combined with
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}\mid x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}}]=\sigma(z(x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}}))
,
this forces
\tilde{r}=\sigma(z(x,y,y_{\mathrm{ref}}))
a.s., hence the minimizer is unique.
∎

Theorem
3.2
justifies our selection of sigmoid-like functions for bounding RL rewards, as the sigmoid’s properties minimize policy gradient variance.
