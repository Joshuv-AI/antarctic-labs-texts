Efficacy of rTMS combined with tDCS in patients with major depressive disorder with anxiety: a randomised, double-blind, sham-controlled study
Authors: Deyang Li, Jiaxin Li, Shuochi Wei, Xingxing Li, Junyao Liu, Ruichenxi Luo, Yanli Li, Dongsheng Zhou, Xiangyang Zhang, Dongmei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMajor depressive disorder (MDD) with comorbid anxiety presents a greater treatment challenge and higher relapse risk. Non-invasive brain stimulation techniques such as repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) and transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) have shown efficacy individually, but their combined effect remains unclear.ObjectiveTo assess the efficacy and acceptability of rTMS, tDCS and their combined therapy in alleviating depressive and anxiety symptoms in patients with MDD.MethodsIn this double-blind, randomised, sham-controlled trial, 240 patients with MDD with anxiety symptoms were assigned to four groups: active rTMS+active tDCS, active rTMS+sham tDCS, sham rTMS+active tDCS and sham rTMS+sham tDCS. Participants received 10 sessions over 2 weeks and were followed for an additional 2 weeks. Primary outcomes were changes in Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS) and Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA) scores from baseline to post-treatment. χ² tests and mixed-design analysis of variance were used for group comparisons.FindingsAfter 2 weeks of treatment, the combined rTMS+tDCS group showed significantly greater reductions in HDRS scores than the other three groups. Reductions in HAMA scores were also larger compared with the sham–sham group and remained superior to that of the other groups at the 4-week follow-up. The combined group had the highest anxiety response rate (82.83%, p=0.014) and remission rate (26.17%, p=0.020) at post-treatment. No serious adverse events were reported across groups.ConclusionsCombined rTMS and tDCS were more effective than monotherapy or sham in alleviating depression and anxiety in MDD, with good tolerability and no significant side effects.Clinical implicationsThis combined neuromodulation approach may serve as a promising and well-tolerated non-pharmacological option for patients with MDD with comorbid anxiety. It offers a clinically scalable intervention that could complement current first-line therapies.Trial registration numberChiCTR2100052122.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) and transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) are both non-invasive brain stimulation techniques shown to be effective in treating major depressive disorder (MDD). However, their combined therapeutic effect and safety in patients with MDD with comorbid anxiety symptoms have not been systematically evaluated in randomised controlled trials.

Background

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a psychiatric disorder defined by marked and persistent depressed mood, delayed thinking, diminished motivation and physical symptoms.1 The WHO reports that approximately 3.8% of the global population suffers from depression.2 Depression is frequently accompanied by symptoms of anxiety. For example, a recent study shows that around 75% of patients with MDD also suffer from generalised anxiety disorder.3 In clinical practice, depression and concomitant anxiety symptoms are primarily treated with antidepressants, but patients may experience treatment resistance and increased side effects.4 Patients with comorbid anxiety and depression (anxious depression) respond poorly to standard antidepressants, resulting in a longer course of illness and poorer prognosis.5 Anxiolytic augmentation strategies such as benzodiazepines may carry risks of dependence and cognitive impairment, limiting their long-term utility.6 The neurobiological mechanisms of anxious depression involve abnormalities in brain structure, brain activation and hemispheric asymmetry, all of which may contribute to its poor response to medication.5 Given these challenges, there is growing interest in non-pharmacological treatments that target the underlying neural circuitry for anxious depression.

Over the past decade, non-invasive brain stimulation (NIBS) has been progressively applied in the clinic, and its efficacy and safety have led to an increasing interest in such therapies in the clinical neuropsychiatric field. Among all NIBS techniques, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) and transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) are the two most commonly used therapies. rTMS is a neuromodulation technique using magnetic pulses to generate electrical currents in the brain and has been employed to treat various mental disorders, such as obsessive-compulsive disorder, anxiety disorders, schizophrenia and MDD.7 Some research has found that rTMS improves anxiety symptoms more significantly in patients with depression than medication or sham stimulation alone.8 tDCS applies a continuous weak direct current (DC) to patients’ scalp through electrodes, and the current passes through the skull to stimulate the cerebral cortex and modulate the plasticity of cortical neurons. It has been used extensively in the rehabilitation of depression, schizophrenia, addictions, anxiety disorders and other psychiatric diseases.9 One study found that tDCS treatment for patients with MDD and bipolar disorder resulted in improvements in both depressive and anxiety symptoms.10 Therefore, both forms of brain stimulation may be important for treating patients with anxious depression.

It is hypothesised that rTMS combined with tDCS is more effective than either rTMS or tDCS as monotherapy. People with Parkinson’s disease experience a more sustained increase in cortical excitability following tDCS-pretreated rTMS stimulation.11 Similarly, in patients who had a stroke, the combination of cathodal tDCS with high-frequency rTMS has been shown to yield greater improvements in motor performance compared with rTMS alone.12 rTMS paired with tDCS stimulation of the contralateral M1 led to significant improvement in the overall efficiency of the interhemispheric connections, bilateral hemispheric connections and motor networks, while no significant change was found in the single rTMS group.13 Overall, combination therapies are considered to have better efficacy for various disorders than monotherapy.

In addition, several studies have shown that combined stimulation has a positive effect on healthy individuals. Anodal tDCS on the left M1 and rTMS on the right M1 induced interhemispheric modulation and plasticity changes that modulated cortical excitability and motor function.14 In healthy individuals, simultaneous rTMS and tDCS, as well as rTMS following tDCS, induce significantly higher motor-evoked potentials (MEPs) than sham measurements.15 An increasing body of evidence suggests that rTMS combined with tDCS therapy is superior for both healthy people and patients.

It is worth mentioning that the combination of rTMS and tDCS has been used primarily to stimulate the motor cortex to promote motor recovery in neurological disorders, but its use as a therapeutic approach for psychiatric conditions remains limited. Emerging evidence suggests its potential in this field. For instance, combining navigated rTMS with tDCS targeting the V1 region has been shown to alleviate depressive symptoms and cognitive impairment in patients with bipolar disorder, accompanied by neuroplastic changes in the visual cortex.16 A case report further indicated that a short, intensive combined neuromodulation protocol was both effective and well tolerated in an elderly patient with depression and generalised anxiety.17 In line with these findings, our recent studies demonstrated that the combination of rTMS and tDCS was more effective than either intervention alone in addressing depressive symptoms and cognitive deficits in patients with MDD.18 However, to our knowledge, no clinical trial has used this combined therapy in patients with MDD to investigate its efficacy on anxiety symptoms.

Objective

In this study, we specifically recruited patients with MDD with anxiety symptoms to conduct a randomised, double-blind, sham-controlled trial. The aims of the present study were: (1) to explore the efficacy of rTMS, tDCS and their combined therapy on alleviating depression and anxiety symptoms in patients with MDD, and (2) to examine the response and remission rates of anxiety symptoms post-treatment and to evaluate the safety and potential side effects of each therapy.

Methods

Participants

Figure 1 illustrates the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials flow diagram of the study. From November 2021 to December 2023, a total of 569 potential participants were contacted and screened. 240 patients were recruited and randomly assigned to the four groups. Sample size calculation is detailed in online supplemental methods S1. All participants were exclusively recruited from inpatient units of three hospitals (Ningbo Kangning Hospital, Taizhou Second People’s Hospital and Lishui Second People’s Hospital). Criteria for selecting participants were: (1) meeting diagnosis of MDD and generalised anxiety disorder in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-V; (2) aged between 18 and 65 years; (3) an overall score above 20 on the 24-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS-24); (4) an overall score above 7 on the 14-item Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA-14); and (5) not having received electrotherapy or magnetotherapy, such as rTMS, tDCS or electroconvulsive therapy, in the past 3 months. For the existing medications of participants, please see online supplemental methods S2. The neuromodulation protocols in this study were applied as add-on treatments to existing pharmacotherapy.

The CONSORT diagram of the experimental design and procedure. Flow chart illustrating the enrolment, treatment and follow-up of patients with MDD across the four study groups. CONSORT, Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials; ITT, intention to treat; MDD, major depressive disorder; rTMS, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation; tDCS, transcranial direct current stimulation.

The exclusion criteria were: (1) current or previous comorbidity with other psychiatric disorders (eg, mania or obsessive-compulsive disorder); (2) participation in other ongoing clinical trials; and (3) certain contraindications to rTMS, including epilepsy, brain tumours or a history of trauma. The criteria for loss to follow-up were: (1) repeated unwillingness to undergo treatment; (2) serious side effects requiring appropriate interventions; and (3) change of medication or additional antipsychotic medications during the trial.

This trial was registered on the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry, a WHO Primary Registry (https://www.chictr.org.cn/, ChiCTR2100052122), before patients were formally recruited. All trial procedures followed the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants voluntarily enrolled in this study and did not receive monetary compensation or transportation reimbursement. All clinical assessments were provided free of charge during hospitalisation, and no additional expenses were incurred. All participants agreed to join the study and provided informed consent after the researcher explained the study in easy-to-understand language.

Randomisation and rTMS or tDCS treatment and blinding

This study consists of a 2-week intervention and a 2-week follow-up. Patients were randomly assigned to the four groups in a 1:1:1:1 ratio using random numbers generated by computer: group A (active rTMS plus active tDCS), group B (active rTMS plus sham tDCS), group C (sham rTMS plus active tDCS) and group D (sham rTMS plus sham tDCS). The randomisation sequence was generated by an impartial third party who was not involved in recruitment, assessment or treatment procedures. Allocation was concealed using sequentially numbered, sealed opaque envelopes. Both participants and the researchers were kept blinded to group assignment throughout the study.

In this study, tDCS was administered prior to rTMS within each treatment session. This sequence was chosen based on previous evidence suggesting that tDCS-induced modulation of cortical excitability may precondition the targeted brain regions and enhance the neuromodulatory effects of subsequent rTMS.19

The tDCS was connected to two 5×5 pieces of rubber with two sponges on top and was supplied with a constant 2 mA DC stimulation (Foc.us, London, UK). A trained nurse moistened the sponges with saline. The anode was placed on the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), with the cathode positioned on the right DLPFC. Each treatment was given once a day for 20 min, five times per week, for 10 treatments over 2 weeks (with weekends off). Sham stimulation was performed in sham mode. The current was felt only during the first 30 s, and 30 s before the end of stimulation; for the rest of the time, only the parameters were displayed without stimulation.

Before the rTMS section, the resting motor threshold (RMT) was established by identifying the minimum intensity of MEPs >50 mV generated in five consecutive single pulses in the primary motor cortex. This study used a Magstim Rapid 2 stimulator (Magstim, Carmarthenshire, UK) with a figure-of-eight coil measuring 70 mm in diameter. The left DLPFC was selected as the stimulated target, positioned according to the Montreal Neurological Institute coordinates of x,y,z=−44,40,29 using the Brainsight neuronavigation system (Rogue Research, Montreal, Canada). Groups receiving rTMS treatment were administered 1600 pulses per day (4 s on, 26 s off, 40 repetitions). The rTMS treatment was performed at a frequency of 10 Hz with 100% RMT intensity, over a 2-week period, five times a week. It was delivered at the end of the tDCS treatment for 30–60 min. For the sham rTMS, a sham magnetic coil that matched the appearance, sound and sensation of the active coil was used. During this period, patients in both groups continued to receive conventional medications.

At the conclusion of the trial, blinding was evaluated by asking both participants and psychiatrists to identify their presumed group assignment. All individuals involved in the study, including investigators, nurses, raters and patients, were blinded to the group allocations.

Clinical assessment and outcomes

All participants in the scale assessments were psychiatrists trained prior to the study. Their intrarater correlation coefficients were maintained over 0.8, ensuring the accuracy and reliability of the scale ratings.

Efficacy was assessed at three timepoints: baseline, end of treatment (2 weeks) and follow-up (4 weeks). From baseline to week 4, the primary efficacy of these patients with MDD was assessed for depressive and anxiety symptoms using the HDRS-24 and HAMA-14 scales, respectively. The HDRS-24 was used to measure patients’ current depressive episodes.20 The HAMA-14, a validated measure of anxiety severity, consists of the psychological subscale and somatic subscale.21 A total score above 7 on the HAMA-14 scale indicates that a person may have symptoms of anxiety. In addition, the Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview was used to determine the presence of anxiety.22

The coprimary outcomes were changes in HDRS and HAMA scores from baseline to week 2. The secondary outcomes were (1) changes in HDRS and HAMA scores from baseline to week 4; (2) response rate, defined as a reduction of at least 50% in the HAMA score from baseline to week 223; (3) remission rate, defined as a post-treatment HAMA score less than 724; and (4) documented adverse events, including probable dizziness, burns, seizures and headaches, to assess the safety of the four interventions.

Statistical analysis

Means and SDs were used to summarise continuous demographic and clinical variables, while categorical variables were reported as percentages. The primary efficacy analyses were conducted under the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, including all randomised participants (n=240). Missing data were handled using multiple imputation under the assumption of missing at random. 20 imputed datasets were generated using chained equations, incorporating baseline, demographic and clinical variables as predictors. All subsequent analyses of HDRS and HAMA scores were conducted on the pooled imputed datasets using Rubin’s rules to combine estimates. As a sensitivity analysis, we additionally performed completer analyses (n=200) to examine the robustness of our findings. For eligible continuous variables, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed, while categorical variables were analysed using the χ² test. To compare the impact of four different interventions on anxiety and depression, a mixed-design ANOVA was used to examine changes in HDRS and HAMA scores across three timepoints (baseline, week 2 and week 4), with group as the between-subjects factor. Sphericity was tested using Mauchly’s test, and Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied when the sphericity assumption was violated. If a significant time×group interaction was observed, post hoc analysis controlling for baseline scores was conducted to investigate between-group effects at weeks 2 and 4. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was used to control for multiple group comparisons within each timepoint. No additional adjustment was made across timepoints or between coprimary endpoints. If the interaction was not significant, no further analysis was required. We also conducted a χ2 test to assess differences in clinical remission rates and the proportion of responding patients. All statistical analyses and multiple imputation were conducted using RStudio V.4.2.0, with a two-tailed significance level set at p<0.05. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism V.9.0.

Efficacy of anxiety symptoms in the four groups

For the HAMA scores, Mauchly’s test indicated that the assumption of sphericity was violated (χ²=21.391, p<0.001). Therefore, the df were corrected using the Greenhouse-Geisser estimate (ε=0.906). After multiple imputation, the ITT mixed ANOVA revealed a non-significant main effect of group (F=1.93, p=0.125), a significant main effect of time (F=890.20, p<0.001) and a significant group×time interaction (F=3.20, p=0.004) (table 2 and online supplemental figure S1).

At week 2, baseline-adjusted post hoc analysis showed that group A had significantly lower HAMA scores compared with group D (FDR-adjusted p=0.003), and HAMA scores in group B (FDR-adjusted p=0.023) and group C (FDR-adjusted p=0.022) were also significantly lower than group D (figure 2 and online supplemental table S3). Other contrasts were not significant. At week 4, group A had significantly lower HAMA scores than group D (FDR-adjusted p<0.001). The differences between group A and groups B (FDR-adjusted p=0.024) and C (FDR-adjusted p=0.024) also approached significance.

Furthermore, ITT analysis showed significant differences in 2-week response rate (χ2=12.738, p=0.014) and remission rate (χ2=10.654, p=0.020) (online supplemental table S4). The results from completer analyses were consistent with those from the ITT sample (online supplemental table S5). Online supplemental tables S6 and S7 illustrated the risk differences (RD) of response rates and remission rates among the four groups. Compared with group A (82.83%), the response rates were significantly lower in group C (RD=21.08%, 95% CI 2.97% to 39.20%, p=0.023) and group D (RD=29.17%, 95% CI 10.82% to 47.52%, p=0.002), and marginally lower in group B (RD=17.92%, 95% CI –0.04% to 35.87%, p=0.051). In addition, group A achieved a remission rate of 26.17%, which was significantly higher than that of group D (RD=20.50%, 95% CI 6.96% to 34.04%, p=0.003). In contrast, none of the other three groups demonstrated superiority over group D. At week 4, the χ2 test results revealed a consistent significant difference among the four groups in remission rate (χ2=16.339, p=0.002). Specifically, group A (RD=34.17%, 95% CI 15.92% to 52.41%, p<0.001), group B (RD=21.92%, 95% CI 4.17% to 39.67%, p=0.016) and group C (RD=27.08%, 95% CI 8.82% to 45.35%, p=0.004) all exhibited higher remission rates than group D. However, no significant difference was found in response rates at week 4.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this trial is the first to investigate the combined use of rTMS and tDCS for alleviating anxiety symptoms in patients with MDD, while also systematically evaluating its safety, feasibility and efficacy. The main findings are as follows: (1) the rTMS+tDCS group exhibited the greatest reduction in HDRS scores post-treatment, and the greatest reduction in HAMA scores during the follow-up, compared with the other three groups; (2) compared with the sham group, the combined therapy significantly improved both depressive and anxiety symptoms after treatment and maintained these improvements over the follow-up period; and (3) among all four groups, the combined therapy yielded the highest response rate for anxiety symptoms at the 2-week treatment.

Our study found that rTMS combined with tDCS regimen resulted in a more pronounced relief of depressive symptoms than other groups. These findings align with previous studies that demonstrate the effectiveness of rTMS and tDCS in treating depression.25 rTMS can generate neuronal action potentials and adjust the functional connectivity between the DLPFC and the subgenual anterior cingulate cortex (sgACC), thereby reducing depressive symptoms.26 tDCS can alter the rate of neurotransmitter release by affecting the propagation of action potentials.27 Based on these mechanisms, better efficacy may be achieved by applying tDCS as prestimulation followed by rTMS.

Furthermore, our results suggested that the combined therapy showed its superior efficacy in treating anxiety symptoms in patients with MDD. Studies using rTMS for the treatment of anxious depression have demonstrated improvements in both depressive and anxiety symptoms.8 The mechanisms by which rTMS improves anxiety symptoms mainly involve changes in neurotransmitter release and cortical plasticity.28 rTMS targeting the left DLPFC may enhance dopaminergic and serotonergic transmission in the cortex, which is crucial for the regulation of fear and anxiety.29 In addition, rTMS treatment may improve anxiety symptoms by modulating connectivity between different brain regions. A longitudinal brain development study showed that decreased activity between the anterior cingulate cortex and the DLPFC was associated with increased internalising behaviours (eg, anxiety and depression) later in life.30 rTMS therapy corrects the abnormal activity of the DLPFC and sgACC in patients with depression and modulates the strength of the DLPFC-sgACC functional connectivity.31 Therefore, rTMS is a vital tool for treating patients with MDD with anxiety symptoms.

More importantly, after a 2-week treatment followed by a 2-week follow-up, the combined therapy group demonstrated a significant improvement in HAMA scores compared with the other three groups. Meanwhile, the combined therapy group had the highest response rate for anxiety symptoms, with 82.83% at week 2 and 96.08% at week 4. Both single rTMS and tDCS treatments have anxiolytic effects. Patients receiving the combination treatment extended the duration of active treatment and increased the number of stimulation sites per day. A recent study found that patients with medication-resistant depression who underwent intermittent theta burst stimulation treatment multiple times per day experienced a rapid reduction in depressive symptoms over a short period.32 This finding indicates that the frequency, overall duration and dosage of daily treatment might influence the effectiveness of NIBS. In the present study, the treatment regimen was rTMS once a day plus tDCS once a day. Although different from the aforementioned studies, the combined therapy involved a longer treatment duration and more daily sessions, resulting in a greater reduction of anxiety symptoms in a shorter period. In addition, participants in the combined treatment group received cathodal tDCS in the right DLPFC compared with the single rTMS group, which may improve connectivity and functional regulation between the bilateral hemispheres. Compared with the single tDCS group, participants in the combined treatment group received additional high-frequency rTMS pulses in the left DLPFC. The hypofunctional left DLPFC received improved and more stimulation, thus showing better results.

Another hypothesis for the superior effectiveness of combined therapy in treating anxiety symptoms in patients with MDD is the preconditioning effect of tDCS. In this study, we applied anodal tDCS over the left DLPFC prior to rTMS to leverage a state-dependent modulation of synaptic plasticity. The DLPFC plays a central role in top-down emotion regulation, particularly through its interactions with limbic structures such as the striatum and anterior cingulate cortex.33 Network-based models of mood and anxiety disorders highlight dysregulation in these frontolimbic circuits, characterised by impaired interaction between the DLPFC and subcortical regions like the amygdala and hippocampus.34 tDCS and rTMS have been shown to modulate the excitability and functional connectivity of these networks. Direct action potentials are generated by rTMS in cortical neurons, leading to long-term potentiation and changes in brain plasticity. However, tDCS causes cortical excitation and depolarisation of neurons on the anodic side of the brain.9 Prior evidence suggests that preconditioning with tDCS can shift the excitability threshold of cortical neurons, potentially enhancing the neuromodulation impact of subsequent stimulation.19 This sequential approach may augment therapeutic efficacy by amplifying rTMS-induced neuroplastic changes and cortical excitability improvement in emotion regulation circuits. Therefore, we supposed that preconditioning tDCS on the left DLPFC enhanced the efficacy of rTMS and expedited the alleviation of anxiety symptoms in patients with MDD. Future studies need to incorporate neurophysiological assessments such as electroencephalogram, functional MRI or molecular biomarkers to explore the underlying mechanisms of combined neuromodulation and validate its neural targets.

The present study has several limitations. First off, a longer stabilisation period could further ensure pharmacological steady state and minimise potential confounding effects of recent medication adjustments, especially for participants using fluoxetine in this study. During the trial, although participants were maintained on stable antidepressants, we acknowledge that medication use remains a potential confounding factor. Given the small sample sizes within each medication category, subgroup analyses were not performed to avoid underpowered and potentially misleading comparisons. Second, we did not collect detailed information on participants’ medical comorbidities and the duration of prior antidepressant use, which may limit the interpretability of treatment effects and potential interactions with individual clinical histories. Third, although FDR correction was applied to post hoc tests within each timepoint, no adjustment was made across timepoints or between coprimary outcomes. Thus, interpretations of week-4 results and comparisons between HDRS and HAMA should be considered exploratory. Fourth, the concurrent inpatient pharmacotherapy may result in a ceiling effect, which made the 4-week response rate very high in group A and less sensitive to differentiate between different groups. Fifth, all participants in this study were recruited from inpatient psychiatric units. While this ensured a high level of diagnostic rigour and treatment adherence, it may limit the external validity of the findings. Future research should replicate these findings in more diverse samples, including those recruited from outpatient clinics and community settings. Finally, since we did not investigate the neurophysiological mechanisms in our study, we were unable to determine the exact mechanism of the combined therapy. Further study will consider exploring combined electrophysiology, neuroimaging and blood markers.

Clinical implications

This study presents the first evidence that rTMS combined with tDCS therapy is a novel NIBS protocol with comparable safety that improves comorbid anxiety symptoms in patients with MDD and is more effective than any single stimulation. Future research is essential to investigate the underlying mechanisms of this combined therapy. In addition, the optimal settings for various stimulation parameters (including frequency, current and electrode units) for rTMS combined with tDCS protocol in treating comorbid anxiety symptoms in patients with MDD need to be further explored.
