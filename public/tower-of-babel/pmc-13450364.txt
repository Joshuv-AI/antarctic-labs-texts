Modified langendorff-free method for isolating cardiomyocytes and non-cardiomyocytes from adult mice
Authors: Runhua Li, Jia Shen, Shuang Li, Yingchao Shi, Wenting Zhao, Chenqiu Wang, Lu Chen, Jiamian Chen, Xiaolei Cheng, Junyue Xing, Zhen Li, Tongguang Shi, Chuanyu Gao, Hao Tang, Liguo Jian, Dongdong Jian, Datun Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdult murine cardiomyocytes serve as a pivotal model for cardiovascular research. High-quality isolation is essential for studying disease mechanisms and drug screening. Current methods, however, are limited by reliance on specialized equipment and inconsistent efficiency, necessitating a more convenient and reliable protocol.Methods and resultsWe developed an optimized, Langendorff-free method for isolating cardiomyocytes and cardiac fibroblasts from adult mice. Following anesthesia, an enzymatic digestion solution was infused directly via the aortic root, utilizing the coronary circulation to achieve efficient tissue dissociation. This approach yielded adult mouse cardiomyocytes with high viability, intact morphology, and suitability for long-term culture, comparable to the standard Langendorff technique. Functionally, the isolated cardiomyocytes retained robust responsiveness to pathological stimuli, including hypoxia and adrenergic agonists (isoprenaline, norepinephrine, phenylephrine), demonstrated by characteristic alterations in contractile function, relaxation kinetics, and calcium handling. Furthermore, cardiac fibroblasts co-isolated using this protocol could be successfully activated by TGF-β, showing significant upregulation of myofibroblast markers (e.g., α-smooth muscle actin) and pro-fibrotic signaling pathways.ConclusionThis study establishes a novel, efficient, and accessible method for the simultaneous isolation of functional cardiomyocytes and cardiac fibroblasts from adult mice. The protocol overcomes key limitations of existing techniques and provides a reliable platform for in vitro mechanistic studies and drug discovery in cardiovascular disease.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12896-026-01174-6.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases are among the leading causes of death worldwide [1]. Cardiomyocytes are a primary focus of research on heart-related diseases. In vitro culture of cardiomyocytes is essential for simulating cardiac physiological or pathological states, as well as for studying single-cell structures and functions in a variety of settings, including detailed analyses following specific molecular biology manipulations [2]. Adult mammalian cardiomyocytes are closely connected with adjacent cells and the extracellular matrix and have intercalated discs between adjacent cells, where the cell membrane is folded and differentiated to form desmosomes that maintain strong connections [3, 4]. This structural organization makes isolating intact adult cardiomyocytes challenging. Adult cardiomyocytes are also vulnerable to mechanical disturbance, osmolarity changes, enzymatic damage, hypoxia, nutrient limitations, and fluctuations in pH and ions during isolation, often resulting in isolation failure or reduced viability [5, 6].

In 1898, Oscar Langendorff developed the heart perfusion method to isolate adult mouse cardiomyocytes [7]. The excised heart is mounted on a Langendorff apparatus, and an proteolytic enzymes-containing solution is perfused into the heart at constant pressure or flow through a cannula inserted into the ascending aorta. The Langendorff method enables efficient and stable isolation of adult mouse cardiomyocytes by infusing proteolytic enzymes-containing digestion solution into the coronary circulation through the aortic root [8]. However, it requires specialized instruments and expertise, creating financial, logistical, and technical barriers. In addition, equipment based on the Langendorff perfusion protocol has difficulty meeting sterility requirements, which hinders subsequent cardiomyocyte culture. This method also typically requires pre-injection of anticoagulants such as heparin, which may interfere with downstream polymerase chain reaction experiments [9].

Recently, Roger S. Foo and colleagues developed a Langendorff-free enzymatic digestion method for isolating adult mouse cardiomyocytes [10]. In this approach, the ascending aorta is clamped and the enzyme solution is injected into the left ventricle, passing anterogradely through the aortic valve into the coronary circulation. This method is simpler, does not require Langendorff equipment or extensive training, can be performed in a sterile laminar flow cabinet, and yields cardiomyocytes suitable for long-term culture, functional assessment, and pathological model induction. However, repeated puncturing of the left ventricle with a syringe creates an enlarging hole, causing pressure loss that reduces perfusion efficiency and prolongs digestion time.

Here we present a method combining both approaches with two key modifications. First, buffers were injected through a cannula placed in the ascending aorta. Second, cardiomyocytes are collected by low-speed centrifugation (100 g × 3 min) before calcium reintroduction to shorten handling time. This method achieves efficient coronary perfusion without a Langendorff apparatus and shortens enzymatic digestion to 10–15 min. Relatively pure cardiomyocytes can be collected within approximately 10 min before calcium reintroduction, and then viable cardiomyocytes with normal function can be obtained by gradually increasing calcium concentration. Cardiomyocytes isolated using this method can be cultured long term, maintain normal contractile and relaxation function, respond to ISO stimulation, and support adenovirus transfection. Furthermore, this method allows simultaneous isolation of cardiomyocytes and cardiac fibroblasts from a single mouse heart, providing a useful protocol for in vitro studies of cardiovascular diseases.

Materials and methods

Mouse models

All animal experiments complied with the ARRIVE Guidelines and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH, 8th edition, 2011) and were approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Central China Fuwai Hospital of Zhengzhou University (Permit No. FZX-IACUC-2024020). Male C57BL/6J mice (8–10 weeks old, 20–25 g) from SPF Biotechnology (Beijing) were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility under standard conditions (temperature:23 ± 1 °C; humidity: 55 ± 5% 12-h light/dark cycle) with free access to food and water. All surgical procedures were performed under anesthesia induced by 3% isoflurane inhalation, followed by cervical dislocation in accordance with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals.

Buffer preparation

The preparation of buffers required for cell culture followed previously reported protocols with minor modifications [10]. The buffer solution formulation is provided in the Supplementary Table S3. Base buffer, EDTA buffer, Perfusion buffer are pre-prepared solutions that can be stored at 4 °C for 2 weeks and filter the solution through a 0.2 μm filter before use to ensure sterility. Enzymatic digestion buffer, Stop buffer, Plating medium, Culture medium, Calcium reintroduction buffers and Fibroblast growth medium are freshly prepared solutions require ensured sterility and filter the solution through a 0.2 μm filter before use to ensure sterility. All buffers were pre-oxygenated for 20 min prior to use.

Mouse heart dissociation

C57BL/6J mice (8–10 weeks old) were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane inhalation followed by cervical dislocation. Subsequently, the thoracic cavity was opened to expose the heart, followed by transection of the descending aorta. 5 mL EDTA buffer was injected through the right ventricle to perfuse the entire heart, preventing blood coagulation and initially flushing the blood in the coronary circulation. Transect across the ascending aorta (retaining as much length of the aorta as possible to facilitate subsequent cannulation), then remove the intact heart and lungs and place them in a 6 cm petri dish containing EDTA buffer. Resect the lung tissue, isolate the ascending aorta, and make an incision using micro-scissors. Subsequently, a 7-gauge lumbar puncture needle (Outer diameter of approximately 0.7 mm; Cat No.20172152143, KYUAN, Yangzhou, Jiangsu, China) was manually trimmed to retain a 3-cm needle shaft length with the stylet protruding about 2 mm beyond the needle shaft to facilitate subsequent intubation operations (In our Online Resource 1, the procedure of intubation was demonstrated beside the biosafety cabinet for clearer visualization and recording. Once proficient with the operation, this step can be fully performed inside the biosafety cabinet to maintain optimal aseptic conditions). After inserting the puncture needle into the aortic root, it was initially secured with a vascular clamp. Further, use 6 − 0 silk suture to securely ligate along the lower edge of the vascular clamp, thereby fixing the heart to the lumbar puncture needle. Remove the needle core, connect a syringe prefilled with 5 mL of pre-warmed EDTA buffer, and initiate slow injection (1.5–2.5 mL/min) of the EDTA buffer to fully clear residual blood from the coronary circulation. Next, use a 10 mL syringe filled with pre-warmed perfusion buffer and inject at the same rate (1.5–2.5 mL/min) for approximately 3 min to clear EDTA from the heart chambers and circulation. Finally, replace with a 10 mL syringe filled with enzymatic digestion buffer, and slowly perfuse at a rate of 1.0-1.5 mL/min for digestion. After each buffer replenishment, tap the syringe barrel and push the plunger to expel any air bubbles (During slow infusion of the perfusate, fine forceps can be gently applied to compress the heart to facilitate the removal of possible air bubbles within the coronary circulation). The entire perfusion digestion process usually takes about 10 to 15 min. This procedure can also be performed using a syringe pump (ISPLab01-Pro) set at a flow rate of 1.5 mL/min. Before use, flush the tubing with 75% ethanol and pre-remove air bubbles with EDTA buffer. Signs of complete digestion include: loss of the heart’s shape, elasticity, and firmness; paler color on the heart surface.

Upon completion of digestion, transfer the heart to a 6 cm Petri dish and gently dissect away the cardiac vessels and atrial tissue. Subsequently, move the remaining ventricular tissue to a new 6 cm Petri dish filled with approximately 3 mL of preheated enzymatic digestion buffer. Using microforceps, tease the ventricular tissue into small fragments with a rough size of 1 mm × 1 mm. Transfer the minced tissue into a 50 mL centrifuge tube and incubate in a 37 °C water bath for 5 min. Gently triturate the tissue/cell suspension 20 times using a 3 mL Pasteur pipette—the tissue should be fully digested at this point. Add 7 mL of stop buffer pre-cooled to 4 °C to terminate the digestion, and gently triturate the suspension another 5 times. Centrifuge at 100 × g for 3 min and collect the supernatant for subsequent isolation of cardiac fibroblasts. Resuspend the cell pellet in fresh stop buffer and centrifuge again. Repeat this washing step twice to obtain relatively pure cardiomyocytes. Resuspend the cells sequentially in the three calcium reintroduction buffers ([1, 2], and [3]), allowing the cells to settle for 15 min after each resuspension, and collect the supernatant each time for later isolation of cardiac fibroblasts. Resuspend the final cardiomyocyte pellet in 3–5 mL of culture medium and seed onto culture dishes coated with 0.1 mg/mL Matrigel at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells/cm². Allow the cells to adhere for 1 h, then replace the medium with Culture medium. Combine all collected supernatants containing non-myocytes, centrifuge at 300 × g for 5 min, resuspend the pellet in Fibroblast medium, plate onto culture dishes, and transfer to the incubator. Change the medium after 6–24 h of culture, and then every 48 h thereafter.

Calcium re-introduction, as well as the culture of cardiomyocytes and cardiac fibroblasts, was performed in accordance with the method described by Roger S. Foo [10].

Transverse aortic constriction (TAC) model of pressure-overload induced hypertrophy

According to previously established protocols, transverse aortic constriction (TAC) was performed on 6-week-old male C57BL/6J mice. The procedure was briefly conducted as follows: after anesthetizing the mice, intubation and connection to a ventilator were carried out. A midline sternotomy was performed to expose the aortic arch. At the junction between the aortic arch and the distal segment of the brachiocephalic artery, a ligation was performed using a prolene suture threaded through a 27-gauge needle. The needle was immediately withdrawn, and the thoracic cavity and skin were subsequently sutured. Sham-operated control mice underwent the same procedure without ligation. 8 weeks later, the mice were euthanized for cardiomyocyte isolation.

Hypoxia treatment of cardiomyocytes

Freshly isolated cardiomyocytes were cultured under normal conditions in a 37 °C, 5% CO₂ incubator for 24 h, then transferred to a hypoxic incubator (5% CO₂, 0.2% O₂) for an additional 24 h of hypoxia treatment. Subsequently, cellular RNA was collected to analyze the expression levels of hypoxia-inducible genes.

TGF-β induction of fibroblasts

Isolated cardiac fibroblasts were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ until reaching approximately 60% confluence. Subsequently, the cells were treated for 48 h with fibroblast growth medium containing 5% FBS and supplemented with 10 ng/mL TGF-β. Following treatment, cells were collected for the detection of myofibroblast-related gene and protein expression.

Western blot and qPCR

For Western blotting, cells were lysed and centrifuged to obtain supernatants for protein quantification. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, and incubated with primary and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Antibody information is provided in Supplementary Table S2. Signals were detected by chemiluminescence. For qPCR, total RNA was extracted with TRIzol, reverse-transcribed into cDNA, and amplified using SYBR Green mix on a CFX Connect system. Gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method normalized to GAPDH, with primer sequences provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Ca²⁺ transients, contraction and relaxation analysis

Cardiomyocyte intracellular Ca²⁺ concentration, Ca²⁺ transients and sarcomere contraction and relaxation analysis were assessed as previously described [11]. Briefly, cardiomyocytes were isolated from mouse hearts using the Modified method described above. Rod-shaped, quiescent myocytes with clear striations that responded to field stimulation (1 Hz, 15 V, 4 ms; MyoPacer EP, IonOptix) were randomly selected. Intracellular Ca²⁺ was monitored at 35 ± 2 °C with Fura-2AM under an inverted microscope. Dual-excitation fluorescence (340/380 nm) was acquired using a photomultiplier system (IonOptix). Background fluorescence (10 non-loaded cells) was subtracted to generate 340/380 ratios for each single-cell Ca²⁺ transient. Sarcomere dynamics were tracked in a 15–20 sarcomere region of interest using a variable-rate CCD camera (MyoCam-S, IonOptix) mounted on an Olympus IX71 microscope. Sarcomere length changes were computed via Fast Fourier Transform during electrically evoked twitches; 20–30 twitches were averaged per cell. Data were collected from > 60 myocytes per group (n = 6 mice) and analyzed using IonWizard 6.5 (IonOptix).

Immunofluorescence and phalloidin staining

Cells grown on coverslips were fixed with 4% PFA, permeabilized with Triton X-100, and blocked with BSA. For immunofluorescence, samples were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C followed by species-specific fluorescent secondary antibodies (1:1000). For F-actin staining, fixed cardiomyocytes were directly incubated with CoraLite®594-phalloidin (1:200). All nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a Leica Stellaris 5 microscope and analyzed with LAS X and ImageJ software, with the lateral surface area of cardiomyocytes measured in immunofluorescence samples.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.2, San Diego, CA, USA). Comparisons between two groups were made using unpaired Student’s t test or Mann–Whitney U tests, depending on data distribution. For multiple group comparisons, one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Results

Modified method successfully isolated adult mouse cardiomyocytes

The two widely used methods for isolating cardiomyocytes from adult mouse hearts are the Langendorff method and the Foo method. Our modified method combines the advantages of both techniques by using a syringe, rather than the Langendorff apparatus, to slowly infuse the dissociation buffer into the myocardium via aortic cannulation and the coronary vasculature, thereby achieving the same hydrodynamic effect through a simplified procedure (Fig. 1). A summary of the methodology is shown in Fig. 2. For the detailed operation video, please refer to Online Resource 1.

To determine whether our Modified method can isolate normal rod-shaped adult mouse cardiomyocytes as effectively as the Langendorff and Foo methods, we quantified the total number and percentage of viable rod-shaped myocytes obtained using all three methods. As shown in Fig. 3A, the cardiomyocytes isolated using our Modified method were morphologically consistent with those obtained using the other two methods. After reintroduction of calcium, the cardiomyocytes that adhered to Matrigel-coated culture surfaces exhibited characteristic angular morphologies and clearly organized sarcomeric striations. Furthermore, the total number and percentage of rod‑shaped myocytes obtained with our modified method were comparable to those obtained with the Langendorff method, and slightly higher than those obtained with the Foo method (Fig. 3B-C). No significant difference was observed in the yield of fibroblasts among the three methods (Fig. 3D). In addition, we have summarized the cell yield and viability of the different isolation methods in Supplementary Table S1 to facilitate clear comparison. These results confirm that our Modified method can isolate adult mouse cardiomyocytes with normal morphology.

Morphology and culture of isolated cardiomyocytes

Due to the large size of the Langendorff apparatus, it is often placed outside the biosafety cabinet in most laboratories, making complete sterile operation extremely difficult and preventing the isolated cells from being used for sterile culture. In contrast, cardiomyocytes isolated by the Modified method and the Foo method can meet the requirements for sterile culture. Therefore, we performed a 21‑day long‑term culture experiment using cardiomyocytes isolated by these two methods. The results showed no significant difference in cell viability during culture between cardiomyocytes isolated by the two methods (Fig. 3E).

Previous studies have indicated that adult cardiomyocytes undergo gradual morphological and electrophysiological remodeling during in vitro culture [10]. We observed a similar pattern in cardiomyocytes isolated by both the Modified method and the Foo method. For example, after 24 h of culture, the cardiomyocytes still maintained an adult ventricular morphology with intact sarcomere organization. By day 7, however, the cell edges began to round, and the originally well-organized sarcomeres became disorganized or even lost. Between days 14 and 21, the cardiomyocytes gradually adopted a polygonal structure and developed pseudopodia, seemingly re-establishing a morphology resembling the sarcomeric arrangement of normal cardiomyocytes (Fig. 3F). These dynamic morphological changes are consistent with previously reported findings. In subsequent functional validation experiments using adult mouse cardiomyocytes, all assays were completed within 7 days post-isolation to ensure the reliability of the results.

Cultured cardiomyocytes can respond to pathological stimuli

To evaluate and compare the suitability of the isolated cardiomyocytes for routine experiments, we performed adherent culture of freshly isolated cells from the Modified and Foo methods, followed by various pathological stimulations, with the specific experimental design illustrated in Fig. 4A. After 24 h of hypoxia treatment, qPCR results showed that cardiomyocytes isolated by both methods significantly upregulated the expression of hypoxia-inducible genes, including Nppa, Nppb, Myh7, Hk2, Slc2a1, and Hif-1a, under hypoxic conditions (Fig. 4B), demonstrating their capacity to respond to hypoxic stimulation.

Next, to evaluate the suitability the suitability of the isolated cardiomyocytes for biochemical signaling studies, adherent cardiomyocytes were stimulated with ISO or NE. After 30 min, phosphorylation levels of AKT and phospholamban (PLN) were significantly increased (Fig. 4C-D, Figure S1 A-F). To induce a hypertrophic response, cells were treated with 100 µM phenylephrine (PE) for 48 h. qPCR analysis showed that PE stimulation significantly upregulated the mRNA levels of hypertrophy‑related genes (Nppa, Nppb, Myh7) in cardiomyocytes isolated by both the Modified and Foo methods (Fig. 4E). Consistently, protein levels were also markedly elevated (Fig. 4F-G).

These results indicate that cardiomyocytes isolated by the Modified method retained the capacity to respond to multiple pathological stimuli during in vitro culture, making them suitable for modeling a variety of diseases.

Cultured Cardiomyocytes possess calcium handling capacity and contractile/relaxation functions responsive to pathological stimuli

The calcium handling ability and contraction/relaxation functions of cardiomyocytes are among their most important physiological properties [12, 13]. To validate whether cardiomyocytes isolated by the Modified method retain normal calcium handling, contractile, and relaxation functions, and to assess their responsiveness to pathological stimuli, we measured changes in contractility and relaxation in freshly isolated cardiomyocytes after 30 min of stimulation with 0.1 µM ISO and compared the results with those from cardiomyocytes isolated using the Langendorff method.

The results shown that ISO significantly enhanced the contractile function of cardiomyocytes isolated by the Modified method and Langendorff method, as evidenced by an increased sarcomere response (Fig. 5A-B), increased fractional shortening (Fig. 5C), decreased contraction and relaxation times (Fig. 5D-E), and increased contraction/relaxation rates (Fig. 5F-G). Correspondingly, ISO also significantly increased the amplitudes of Ca²⁺ transients (Fig. 5H–I), manifested as increased pacing-induced Ca²⁺ transient amplitude (Fig. 5J), reduced time to peak (Fig. 5K), reduced time to half decay (Fig. 5L), and shortened tau of Ca²⁺ transient decay (Fig. 5M). The changes of the contractility and Ca²⁺ transients of the two methods were not significantly different under ISO stimulation. These results show that our isolated cardiomyocytes exhibit normal calcium handling and contractile/relaxation functions can make appropriate adjustments in response to pathological stimuli.

Simultaneous isolation of cardiomyocytes and cardiac fibroblasts from a single mouse heart

The adult mammalian heart contains many non-myocyte cells that play important roles in regulating cardiac physiology and pathological remodeling. Cardiac fibroblasts, in particular, are abundant and functionally important. Since various reported cardiomyocyte isolation methods allow for the simultaneous isolation of cardiac fibroblasts [14–18], here we tested whether the Modified method could achieve this.

We characterized the phenotype and function of the cardiac fibroblasts isolated using the Modified method. Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed that after passage 2, the isolated non-cardiomyocytes highly expressed the fibroblast marker Vimentin (VIM), while showing negligible expression of the endothelial marker CD31 (Fig. 6A). qPCR further confirmed that cardiac fibroblasts highly expressed Pdgfra and Vimentin, but barely expressed the cardiomyocyte marker cardiac troponin T (Tnnt2), and slightly expressed the endothelial marker Pecam1 (Fig. 6B). These results demonstrate that the Modified method can isolate cardiomyocytes while simultaneously obtaining nearly pure cardiac fibroblasts.

A key feature of cardiac fibroblasts during pathological remodeling is their transformation into myofibroblasts and their pro-fibrotic effects, a process that can be partially mimicked by TGF-β stimulation. In this study, ffibroblasts isolated by the Modified method were treated with TGF-β for 48 h. Western blot analysis revealed significantly elevated protein levels of ACTA2 and Col1a1, along with enhanced phosphorylation of ERK and SMAD3 (Fig. 6C-D), indicating activation of the pro‑fibrotic signaling pathways (SMAD2/3 and ERK). Furthermore, qPCR analysis showed significant upregulation of myofibroblast marker genes, including ACTA2, Col1a1, Fibronectin, and Pdgfra (Fig. 6E). These results confirm that the Modified method can isolate cardiac fibroblasts that are capable of recapitulating the pathological remodeling phenotype in vitro.

Discussion

Cardiovascular disease is a leading cause of global mortality, driving the need for reliable in vitro cardiac cell models to study disease mechanisms and therapeutic strategies [19, 20]. An isolation and culture system for adult mouse cardiomyocytes and non-myocytes (e.g., fibroblasts) has therefore become an essential tool for investigating the pathophysiological processes underlying heart disorders [21]. While the classic Langendorff perfusion method yields cells with high viability and yield, its reliance on specialized equipment and operational complexity limit its widespread application in standard laboratories [22]. In recent years, researchers such as Roger S. Foo have developed a non-Langendorff enzymatic digestion method, which replaces retrograde arterial perfusion with left ventricular enzyme injection and reduces the need for complex equipment [10]. However, it still faces issues such as puncture-induced injury, high ventricular pressure, and inconsistent digestion efficiency. To address these limitations, this study developed a Modified method for isolating cardiac cells from adult mice by integrating the advantages and drawbacks of both the Langendorff and Foo methods. By perfusing enzymatic digestion solution through a cannula inserted into the ascending aorta, our method mimics the coronary circulation pathway of the Langendorff approach, achieving efficient and uniform heart digestion without the need for complex perfusion systems. Compared to the Foo method, which delivers solutions into the entire coronary arterial system via the aortic root in an anterograde manner, our Modified method employs aortic cannulation in a retrograde fashion, a technique that shares the same principle as the classic Langendorff apparatus. In our approach, the infused digestive enzyme solution is driven by external pressure and enters the coronary network retrogradely through the aortic root, thereby ensuring uniform distribution of the enzymes throughout all regions of the myocardium. Simultaneously, closure of the aortic valve prevents partial reflux of the solution into the ventricle, which reduces intraventricular pressure and may mitigate myocardial injury, which might correspond to the enhanced viability. A limitation is that it still requires aortic cannulation, demanding a certain level of surgical skill from the experimenter. In terms of cell yield, the Langendorff method remains superior, but the Modified method also showed no significant disadvantage. Furthermore, we adopted Foo method and used EDTA as the anticoagulant instead of heparin, thereby avoiding potential interference from heparin in subsequent PCR reactions.

Functionally, cardiomyocytes isolated using our method retain intact morphology and exhibit normal physiological properties, including contractile and relaxation dynamics, positive inotropic responses to stimulants such as ISO, NE, and PE, and hypoxia-induced upregulation of genes associated with pathological remodeling. These findings confirm that the isolated cardiomyocytes maintain strong responsiveness to external pathological stimuli, making them suitable for modeling heart disease and conducting mechanistic studies. Notably, we also achieved efficient isolation of cardiac fibroblasts from the same single mouse heart using the same workflow. Upon induction with transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), these fibroblasts were successfully activated into a myofibroblast phenotype, characterized by increased expression of ACTA2, Col1a1, and fibronectin, and activation of the ERK/SMAD signaling pathway. This “one-protocol-for-two-cell-types” approach provides a consistent model for investigating cardiomyocyte–fibroblast interactions, an important yet understudied aspect of cardiovascular research.

Our method also demonstrates strong practicality and scalability. The entire procedure can be performed in a biological safety cabinet, ensuring sterility and compatibility with diverse in vitro experimental settings. It does not require expensive automated perfusion pumps or constant-temperature perfusion systems, relying only on standard surgical and centrifugation equipment. Moreover, the short digestion time (10–15 min) and high cell recovery rate increase experimental throughput while preserving cell viability, which is particularly advantageous for large-scale drug screening and omics studies.

We also preliminarily explored the application of our Modified method for cardiomyocytes isolation from aged mice (20-months-old). Both our Modified method and Foo method enabled efficient isolation of cardiomyocytes from aged mice. Meanwhile, the proportion of viable rod-shaped cardiomyocytes harvested via the Modified method was slightly higher than that obtained using Foo method (Supplementary Fig. S2A). Western blot analysis revealed markedly elevated expression of senescence-associated proteins in cardiomyocytes isolated by both methods, demonstrating that the harvested cardiomyocytes are suitable for the validation of senescence-related markers (Supplementary Fig. S2B). During the isolation, we observed that compared with 8-week-old mice, hearts from 20-months-old mice required appropriately extended digestion time, as indicated by fading surface color and loss of tissue firmness. This is likely related to increased cardiac fibrosis with aging. In light of this, we suggest that in disease models inducing pathological cardiac remodeling—such as angiotensin II infusion or transverse aortic constriction—digestion time should be empirically adjusted based on the observed state of the heart during isolation.

Nevertheless, this study has some limitations. First, we have not systematically evaluated the consistency of isolation efficiency across mice of different strains, ages, or disease models. Second, this protocol has not yet been tested for isolating cardiac cells from larger animals such as rats, which will be important for validating its translational potential. Additionally, cardiomyocyte dedifferentiation was observed to commence at approximately day 7 of long-term culture using both our Modified method and Foo method. Nevertheless, the underlying molecular mechanisms governing this dedifferentiation process remain largely unelucidated. This phenomenon may be associated with the absence of in vivo extracellular matrix support under in vitro culture conditions, electrical remodeling resulting from the loss of cardiomyocyte electrical activity, as well as the deficiency of certain components in the culture medium that sustain the differentiated phenotype of cardiomyocytes. Further in-depth studies are warranted to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms, and subsequently develop strategies to inhibit or promote cardiomyocyte dedifferentiation.

Conclusions

This study establishes an efficient, stable, and user-friendly method for the simultaneous isolation of adult mouse cardiomyocytes and cardiac fibroblasts. It integrates the high-efficiency perfusion advantage of the Langendorff method with the equipment independence and operational simplicity of non-Langendorff techniques. By overcoming the reliance on complex instrumentation and the difficulty of maintaining aseptic conditions, this protocol yields high-viability, functionally mature cardiac cells. It provides a reliable and scalable platform for mechanistic studies, drug screening, and the development of cardiac co-culture systems, addressing a critical gap in current cardiac research methodologies.
