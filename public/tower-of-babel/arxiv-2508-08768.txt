Seok-Jun Chang, Max Gronke, Jorryt Matthee, Charlotte Mason

Abstract
Little Red Dots (LRDs) are compact sources at z > 5 z>5 discovered through JWST spectroscopy. Their spectra exhibit broad Balmer emission lines ( ≳ 1000 ​ km ​ s − 1 \gtrsim 1000\rm~km~s^{-1} ), alongside absorption features and a pronounced Balmer break – evidence for a dense, neutral hydrogen medium with the n = 2 n=2 state. When interpreted as arising from AGN broad-line regions, inferred black hole masses from local scaling relations exceed expectations given their stellar masses, challenging models of early black hole–galaxy co-evolution. However, radiative transfer effects in dense media may also impact the formation of hydrogen emission lines. We model three scattering processes shaping hydrogen line profiles: resonance scattering by hydrogen in the n = 2 n=2 state, Raman scattering of UV radiation by ground-state hydrogen, and Thomson scattering by free electrons. Using 3D Monte Carlo radiative transfer simulations with multi-branching resonance transitions, we examine their imprint on line shapes and ratios. Resonance scattering produces strong deviations from Case B flux ratios, clear differences between H α \alpha and H β \beta , and encodes gas kinematics in line profiles but cannot broaden H β \beta due to conversion to Pa α \alpha . While Raman scattering can yield broad wings, scattering of UV continuum is disfavored given the absence of strong FWHM variations across transitions. Raman scattering of higher Lyman-series emission can produce H α \alpha /H β \beta wing width ratios of ≳ 1.28 \gtrsim 1.28 , agreeing with observations. Thomson scattering can reproduce the observed ≳ 1000 ​ km ​ s − 1 \gtrsim 1000~\rm km\,s^{-1} wings under plausible conditions – e.g., T e ∼ 10 4 T_{\rm e}\sim 10^{4} K and N e ∼ 10 24 ​ cm − 2 N_{\rm e}\sim 10^{24}\rm~cm^{-2} – and lead to black hole mass overestimates by factors ≳ 10 \gtrsim 10 . Our results provide a framework for interpreting hydrogen lines in LRDs and similar systems.

Method

To understand the formation of Balmer line profiles in the Little Red Dots (LRDs), we conduct Monte Carlo radiative transfer (RT) simulations focusing on three scattering processes: (i) resonance scattering of Balmer lines, H
\alpha
and H
\beta
, by hydrogen atoms in the metastable
2s
state, (ii) Raman scattering of UV continuum photons by ground-state hydrogen atoms, and (iii) Thomson scattering of Balmer line photons by free electrons. Each mechanism can contribute to the broadening and shaping of emission lines, producing features such as P-Cygni profiles, double peaks, or broad wings in the spectra.

In order to explore the impact of scattering processes on observables, our simulations adopt a simplified spherical geometry, where a central point source is embedded within a uniform scattering medium. For each process, we use appropriate Monte Carlo radiative transfer codes to track photon propagation and scattering interactions, accounting for the specific cross-sections, branching ratios, and physical conditions relevant to each scattering mechanism. The following sections describe the setup, physics, and simulation methods for each scattering process in detail.

2.2.1
Physical Mechanism

Resonance scattering of Balmer lines occurs when hydrogen atoms in the
2s
state absorb photons near the H
\alpha
and H
\beta
transition energies, exciting electrons to the
n=3
or
n=4
states, respectively. The subsequent de-excitation of the electron can result in the re-emission of a photon at the same wavelength or, through branching, produce photons in other lines such as Pa
\alpha
. This process is analogous to Ly
\alpha

Dijkstra et al. 2019

The key role of the
2s

np

\sim 10^{-9}
s). This long lifetime enables a significant population of hydrogen atoms to accumulate in the
2s

H(a,x)=\frac{a}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-y^{2}}}{(x-y)^{2}+a^{2}}dy.

(2)

Here,
a=\Gamma/(4\pi\Delta\nu_{D})
is the natural width parameter, and
x=(\nu-\nu_{0})/\Delta\nu_{D}
is the dimensionless frequency offset. The thermal Doppler width is
\Delta\nu_{D}=\nu_{0}v_{\mathrm{th}}/c

1.

Define the spherical geometry characterized by a
H
i

{N}_{\rm HI,2s}

\sigma_{\rm R}

2.

Generate isotropic H

\sigma_{\rm Src}

3.

For each photon, determine the scattering location. Using a dimensionless free path of the photon,
\tau_{f}=\ln{r}
, where

is a random number drawn uniformly in
[0,1]

is given by
\tau_{f}/\sigma_{\nu}
;
\sigma_{\nu}
is a scattering cross section (Eq.
1

is adopted as the frequency in the rest frame of the cell, where the photon penetrates.

4.

Determine the scattering outcome:

•

For H

•

For H
\beta

0.739

5.

Repeat steps (iii)–(v) until the photon escapes the grid. When collecting photons, the photon’s frequency is converted to the frequency in the observed frame, taking into account the kinematics at the last scattering location.

Raman scattering generates extremely broad wings around Balmer lines, due to the inelastic scattering of UV photons near Lyman-series wavelengths by neutral hydrogen. While this process requires strong UV radiation fields and large
{N}_{\rm HI}
, both conditions are plausible in LRDs, which show V-shaped SEDs and deep Balmer breaks. If Lyman emission lines or a strong UV continuum exist, Raman scattering can significantly reshape the Balmer line profiles and even mimic AGN-like broad components.

2.3.1
Physical Mechanism

Raman scattering with
H
i
atom is an inelastic process in which a UV photon near the hydrogen Lyman series is scattered by a ground-state hydrogen atom, producing an optical or infrared photon through de-excitation to
n>1
states. If the electron returns to the ground state (
n=1
) after scattering, the process is elastic, and the photon retains its original energy; this is known as Rayleigh scattering. In contrast, if the electron de-excites to higher energy levels (
n>1
), the scattered photon undergoes an energy shift corresponding to the difference between the Lyman transition energy and the new state. Specifically, UV continuum photons near Ly
\beta
form scattered features around H
\alpha
through Raman scattering to the
n=2
state. Fig.
3
illustrates how Raman scattering of a Ly
\gamma
photon to
n=2
and
n=3
states results in the conversion of the UV photon to H
\alpha
and Pa
\alpha
, respectively.

Computing the probability of Raman scattering occurring as a function of a wavelength in the far-UV regime is non-trivial.
While the scattering probability for Rayleigh and Raman scatterings is given by the total cross section, the individual transition probabilities are given by the Branching ratios, which are the ratio between the cross section for Raman scattering divided by the total scattering cross section.
Thus, the sum of the branching ratios to the
n=1
state (Rayleigh scattering) and
n>1
states (Raman scattering) is unity.
Fig.
17
shows the total scattering cross section and branching ratios to each
n
state.

This process involves an energy shift corresponding to the Lyman transition energy, given by

E_{\mathrm{inc}}=E_{\mathrm{sc}}+E_{\mathrm{Lyman}},

(5)

where
E_{\mathrm{inc}}
is the energy of the incident UV photon,
E_{\mathrm{sc}}
is the energy of the scattered photon, and
E_{\mathrm{Lyman}}
is the energy of the corresponding Lyman transition. For instance, following the example to Pa
\alpha
depicted in Fig.
3
(light blue to red arrow),
E_{\rm inc}\approx E_{\rm Ly\gamma}
,
E_{\rm Lyman}=E_{\rm Ly\beta}
, and
E_{\rm sc}\approx E_{\rm Pa\alpha}
.

The scattered wavelength is computed correspondingly as

\frac{1}{\lambda_{\mathrm{sc}}}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\mathrm{inc}}}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{\mathrm{Lyman}}},

(6)

where
\lambda_{\mathrm{Lyman}}
is the wavelength of the relevant Lyman transition (e.g., Ly
\beta
, Ly
\gamma
).

This scattering process leads to an emergent broad line. To first order, the emergent line shape follows the corresponding cross section (cf. Fig.
17
). However, the broadening of Raman-scattered features arises not only from the natural cross-section dependence but also from the energy shift. This can be illustrated by the differential relation between the incident and scattered wavelengths, which is given by

d\lambda_{\mathrm{sc}}=\left(\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{sc}}}{\lambda_{\mathrm{inc}}}\right)^{2}d\lambda_{\mathrm{inc}},

(7)

where the factor
\left(\lambda_{\mathrm{inc}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{sc}}\right)^{2}
represents the additional broadening from the Raman scattering process. As the derivative of the velocity is given by
dv=d\lambda/\lambda
, this factor becomes
\lambda_{\mathrm{sc}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{inc}}
in velocity space, which is
\sim 6.4
and
5
for H
\alpha
and H
\beta
, respectively.

Due to the cross-section dependence, the overall broadening of Raman features depends primarily on the total
H
i
column density
{N}_{\rm HI}
, with higher
{N}_{\rm HI}
leading to broader features with widths exceeding several thousand  km s
-1

(
Lee & Yun 1998
;
Chang et al. 2015
;
Chang et al. 2018b
;
Kokubo 2024
)
. Unlike resonance scattering, the effects of the bulk velocity
v_{\rm exp}
or the random motion
\sigma_{\rm R}
are negligible compared to the intrinsic broadening from the energy shift, assuming the continuum radiation as an input source.
However, if UV emission lines, such as He II lines at 1025, 972, and 949 Å  and O VI resonance lines at 1032 and 1038 Å(rather than the continuum) undergo Raman scattering, the kinematics of the
H
i
region and the profile of input radiation
(
Schmid 1989
;
Harries & Howarth 1996
;
Choi et al. 2020
;
Chang & Lee 2020
;
Heo et al. 2021
;
Chang et al. 2023b
)
can affect the formation of Raman-scattered features.

We will show results of Raman scattered continuum photons in Section
3.2
and discuss Raman-scattered features of the emission lines of the higher Lyman series (Ly
\beta
and Ly
\gamma
) in Appendix
B
.

2.3.2
Monte Carlo Simulation

The Raman scattering simulations are performed using the Monte Carlo code
STaRS

(
Chang & Lee 2020
)
, following these steps:

1.

Define the spherical geometry with uniform
H
i
column density
{N}_{\rm HI}
.

2.

Generate a flat UV continuum between 940–1060 Å from the central source or Gaussian emission of Lyman series.

3.

For each photon, determine the scattering location using the total scattering cross section (the black solid line in Fig.
17
).
The details to calculate a free path are described in (iii) of Section
2.2.3
.

4.

Select the de-excitation channel (i.e., final state of the electron) based on branching ratios computed from the Kramers–Heisenberg formula. If the random number
r
from 0 to 1 is smaller than the branching ratio to the
n>1
state, the photon Raman scatters and converts to an optical or IR photon (e.g., H
\alpha
from Ly
\beta
, and H
\beta
and Pa
\alpha
from Ly
\gamma
). Otherwise, the photon undergoes Rayleigh scattering and remains a UV photon.

5.

Compute the scattered wavelength
\lambda_{\mathrm{sc}}
using Eq.
6
, and generate the new photon direction following the Thomson scattering phase function (Eq.
9
) as Raman scattering is off-resonance scattering
(
Karzas & Latter 1961
)
. If the photon Raman scatters, it is considered to escape the grid immediately.

6.

Repeat steps (iii)–(v) until the photon escapes the grid or undergoes Raman scattering.

7.

Back to a step (ii) until the total number of intrinsic photons becomes
10^{8}
.

The detailed Monte-Carlo method of
STaRS
is described in Section 2 of
Chang & Lee 2020
.
We will investigate the formation of the broad wings around hydrogen lines by Raman scattering and estimate their widths in Section

2.4
Thomson Scattering

Thomson scattering is particularly relevant in the presence of ionized gas in LRDs. The exponential wings observed in many Balmer profiles suggest the influence of electron scattering, as proposed in previous work
(
Laor 2006
;
Rusakov et al. 2025
)
. Importantly, Thomson scattering produces symmetric broadening across all lines when the emission region is embedded within the scattering medium. Unlike resonance or Raman processes, Thomson scattering can introduce significant line broadening without requiring large gas velocities. This poses a challenge for interpreting Balmer line widths as purely virial in origin, motivating detailed modeling of this mechanism in the LRD context.

2.4.1
Physical Mechanism

Thomson scattering is an elastic process in which photons scatter with free electrons, without any change in photon energy in the electron’s rest frame. The scattering cross section is independent of wavelength
(
Rybicki & Lightman 1986
)
and given by

\sigma_{\mathrm{Th}}\approx 6.65\times 10^{-25}\,\mathrm{cm}^{2}.

(8)

The angular distribution of scattered photons is described by the Thomson phase function:

P(\theta)=\frac{3}{16\pi}(1+\cos^{2}\theta),

(9)

where
\theta
is the scattering angle between the incident and scattered directions. With this scattering phase function, the probability of forward and backward scattering is greater than that of scattering in the perpendicular direction.

The broadening of the scattered photon spectrum arises from the thermal motion of electrons. The thermal speed of electrons is

v_{\mathrm{th,e}}=\sqrt{\frac{2kT_{e}}{m_{e}}}\approx 548\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\,\left(\frac{T_{e}}{10^{4}\,\mathrm{K}}\right)^{1/2},

(10)

where
T_{e}
is the electron temperature. Since the scattering cross section is wavelength-independent, the resulting broad features from Thomson scattering appear around any emission lines from the same emission region with a similar width in velocity space, if these lines are emitted from the same region.

2.4.2
Monte Carlo Simulation

Since Thomson scattering is an elastic process in the electron’s rest frame, like resonance scattering, the Thomson scattering simulations are performed using the Monte Carlo code
RT-scat

(
Chang & Gronke 2024
)
with the wavelength-independent cross section, following these steps:

1.

Define the spherical geometry with uniform electron density
n_{e}
, electron temperature
T_{e}
, and compute the Thomson optical depth
\tau_{\mathrm{Th}}=\sigma_{\mathrm{Th}}n_{e}R
.

2.

Generate isotropic photons from the central source, with an intrinsic Gaussian width
\sigma_{\rm Src}
.
Due to the wavelength independence of the Thomson scattering cross section, the generated photon represents any emission from the central source (e.g., H
\alpha
and H
\beta
).

3.

For each photon, determine the scattering location using the Thomson cross section
\sigma_{\rm Th}
in Eq. (
8
) (details are described in (iii) of Section
2.2.3
).

4.

Compute the new photon direction using the Thomson phase function. The scattered wavelength is identical to the incident one in the rest frame of the electron. However, the scattered wavelengths vary in the observed frame. With a 3D velocity from a Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution
{\bf v_{e}}=v_{\rm th,e}\hat{\bf v}
at an electron temperature
T_{e}
, a scattered wavelength in the observed frame
\lambda_{s}
is given by

\lambda_{s}\approx\lambda_{i}\left[1+\frac{v_{\rm th,e}}{c}\hat{\bf v}\cdot(\hat{\bf k_{i}}-\hat{\bf k_{s}})\right],

(11)

where
\hat{\bf k_{i}}
and
\hat{\bf k_{s}}
are unit vectors of incident and scattered photons, and

\lambda_{i}
is an incident wavelength in the observed frame; the range of
\hat{\bf v}\cdot(\hat{\bf k_{i}}-\hat{\bf k_{s}})
term is
[-1,1]
.

5.

Repeat steps (iii)–(iv) until the photon escapes the grid.

6.

Back to a step (ii) until the total number of intrinsic photons becomes
10^{8}
.

The detailed Monte-Carlo method of
RT-scat
is described in Section 2.5 of
Chang & Gronke 2024
(also see
Laor 2006
;
Kim et al. 2007
;
Chang et al. 2018b
;
Choe & Lee 2023
).
We will explore Thomson-scattered features for various electron temperatures
T_{e}
and optical depths
\tau_{\mathrm{Th}}
, and investigate which environment forms an exponential profile.

\mathcal{C}_{\rm H\beta\to Pa\alpha}=\frac{\Phi_{\rm H\beta\to Pa\alpha}}{\Phi_{\rm H\beta}},

(12)

where
\Phi_{\rm H\beta}
is the number of escaping H
\beta

\Phi_{\rm H\beta\to Pa\alpha}
is the number of escaping Pa
\alpha
photons generated by H
\beta
scattering. Fig.
4
shows
\mathcal{C}_{\rm H\beta\to Pa\alpha}
as a function of
{N}_{\rm HI,2s}
.

\mathcal{C}_{\rm H\beta\to Pa\alpha}
increases with increasing
{N}_{\rm HI,2s}
in Fig.
4
because higher optical depths lead to more scatterings, providing more opportunities for H
\beta
photons to convert to Pa
\alpha
and H
\alpha

4p\to 3s/3d

The effective optical depth for the
n=3

{\rm BR3}\,\tau_{\rm 0,H\beta}
, where BR3 is the branching ratio to the n = 3 state,
\sim 0.26
. The number of escaping H
\beta

2s

n=3

\beta

\Phi_{\rm H\beta}=\Phi_{\rm in}\,e^{-{\rm BR3}\,\tau_{\rm 0,H\beta}},

(13)

where
\Phi_{\rm in}
is the total number of input photons. The number of Pa
\alpha

\Phi_{\rm in}-\Phi_{\rm H\beta}
, i.e.,

\Phi_{\rm H\beta\to Pa\alpha}=\Phi_{\rm in}\left(1-e^{-{\rm BR3}\,\tau_{\rm 0,H\beta}}\right).

(14)

Thus, the conversion rate is

\mathcal{C}_{\rm H\beta\to Pa\alpha}=\frac{\Phi_{\rm H\beta\to Pa\alpha}}{\Phi_{\rm H\beta}}=\frac{1}{e^{{\rm BR3}\,\tau_{\rm 0,H\beta}}-1}.

(15)

Fig.
4
shows that the analytic solution and the simulation results match well. For
\tau_{\rm 0,H\beta}>0.5

4.3
Detection of Raman Broad Wing

Raman-scattered wings around H
\alpha
and H
\beta
have been observed in a variety of astrophysical environments, such as symbiotic stars
(
Nussbaumer et al. 1989
;
Chang et al. 2018b
;
Lee & Hyung 2018
)
, planetary nebulae
(
Lee & Hyung 2000
;
Lee et al. 2006
;
Miranda et al. 2022
)
, and
H
ii
regions
(
Dopita et al. 2016
;
Henney 2021
)
. Theoretical studies have also predicted Raman broad wings in AGNs
(
Chang et al. 2015
;
Kokubo & Harikane 2024
)
.
The strength of the Raman scattering wings depends on the input UV radiation. If all incident UV photons are fully Raman scattered, the resulting scattered features have strengths of approximately
\sim 10^{-3}
relative to the input UV flux (see Fig.
10
) due to less energetic scattered photons (Eq. (
5
)) and Raman broadening (Eq. (
7
)). Therefore, environments where Raman scattering is observed typically require both strong UV radiation and an optically thick
H
i
medium.

LRDs can provide such conditions, given their strong Balmer breaks and V-shaped SEDs
(
Kocevski et al. 2023
;
Matthee et al. 2024
;
Greene et al. 2024
;
Taylor et al. 2025b
;
Akins et al. 2025
)
. However, the broad wings around H
\alpha
and H
\beta
observed in LRDs exhibit similar widths over 1000  km s
-1

(
D’Eugenio et al. 2025a
;
D’Eugenio et al. 2025b
;
Juodžbalis et al. 2025
;
Brazzini et al. 2025
)
, which contrasts with the basic Raman scattering expectation (see Fig.
11
) which predicts, e.g., a FWHM ratio of
\sim 3
between H
\alpha
and H
\beta
.

Thus, it seems that Raman scattering is unlikely to be important for LRDs. However, it is important to note that Raman scattering to the
n=2
state, specifically the transition
1s\to np\to 2s
, leaves an electron in the metastable 2s state and can enhance the
n=2
population, increasing the optical depth for resonance scattering of Balmer lines.
Therefore, although it likely does not shape the emergent spectrum, Raman scattering of continuum photons can still affect the radiative transfer dynamics in this manner.

In addition, as shown in Appendix
B
, Raman scattering of emission lines from the Lyman series can, in fact, produce broad wings of comparable width for both H
\alpha
and H
\beta
. Assuming the same width of Ly
\beta
and Ly
\gamma
emissions, the widths of Raman scattered H
\alpha
and H
\beta
are
\approx 6.4
and
5.0
(cf. Eq. (
7
) and Section
2.3
) times broader than those of the Lyman series, resulting in the width ratio of H
\alpha
and H
\beta

\approx 1.28
. This is because the Raman-scattered profiles reflect the velocity distribution of the source emission rather than that of the Raman cross-section.
This implies that for observed widths of
\sim 1000{\rm\,km\,s^{-1}}
for LRDs in H
\alpha
, the width of Ly
\beta
needs to be
\sim 150{\rm\,km\,s^{-1}}
, which is well within the observed narrow component of LRDs.

In this case, to form the broad feature with 10% of H
\alpha
(H
\beta
) flux by Raman scattering, the flux ratio of Ly
\beta
/H
\alpha

\approx 0.64
(Ly
\gamma
/H
\beta

\approx 0.5
) is required, assuming Ly
\beta
(Ly
\gamma

{N}_{\rm HI}
. Although a higher Lyman series emission, such as Ly

/H

\approx 5

Consequently, without strong emission in the higher Lyman series, Raman scattering cannot be the dominant process responsible for the broad wings around Balmer lines in LRDs. However, if Ly
\beta
and Ly
\gamma

\alpha
to H
\beta
of
\gtrsim 1.28
(cf. Fig.

and Appendix

). Future higher signal-to-noise observations of larger LRD samples may be able to detect such subtle line width differences.
Additionally, while higher Lyman-series photons are converted to Balmer lines due to Raman scattering, in practice a fraction of Ly
\beta
and Ly
\gamma

Tripodi et al. 2025

If such Lyman emission features are observed, comparing their measured spectral widths with those required for Raman broadening could serve as a test of this scenario. As discussed above, Raman scattering can explain H

\sim 1000

\sim 150

4.4
Combining Thomson and Resonance Scattering

The spectra of hydrogen emission lines in LRDs exhibit broad components of similar width in both Balmer and Paschen lines (ranging from 1000 to 2000  km s
-1
;
Juodžbalis et al. 2025
;
Brazzini et al. 2025
), along with absorption-like features near the line center, but only for the Balmer lines. As shown in Section
3.1
, resonance scattering of H
\alpha
and H
\beta
produces P-Cygni-like or symmetric double-peaked profiles in Balmer lines, while Thomson scattering contributes similarly broad wings to both Balmer and Paschen lines (Section
3.3
). These effects are consistent with several spectral features observed in LRDs.
Therefore, in this section, we explore how the combination of these two scattering mechanisms shapes the emergent hydrogen line profiles.

We adopt a scattering geometry with a central emission source and a spherical scattering medium of radius
R
. The central source emits hydrogen lines – H
\alpha
, H
\beta
, and Pa
\alpha
with intrinsic widths of
{\sigma_{\rm Src}}=200{\rm\,km\,s^{-1}}
and flux ratios under Case B recombination. We assume that the intrinsic width is less than the electron thermal motion,
v_{\rm e,th}
(
\approx 500{\rm\,km\,s^{-1}}
at
10^{4}\,\rm K

v_{\rm e,th}
; we will discuss the dependence on the width of the Thomson broadening in Section
4.6

The scattering medium is composed of two layers: an inner ionized region responsible for Thomson scattering and an outer neutral region causing resonance scattering. The inner region (within
0.01R

T_{e}
and Thomson optical depth
\tau_{\rm Th}

H

{N}_{\rm HI,2s}

v_{\rm exp}
, and turbulent velocity
\sigma_{\rm R}

H

H
ii

\alpha
and H
\beta
line profiles depends on the radius of the
H
ii
region relevant for Thomson scattering (see Appendix
C
).

Intrinsic photons emitted from the central source first undergo Thomson scattering in the inner
H
ii
region. Then, H
\alpha
and H
\beta
photons experience additional resonance scattering in the outer
H
i
layer. In contrast, Pa
\alpha
photons do not experience resonance scattering, as the
n=3
level has a much shorter lifetime than the
n=2
state (see Fig.
2
) and hence is unlikely to be overpopulated.

4.5
Formation of Hydrogen Balmer Lines in Optically Thick, Dense Gas

Although our simulations only consider resonance scattering of two Balmer lines, combining Thomson and resonance scattering already produces a wide diversity of line profiles. Under the Case B recombination assumption (on-the-spot approximation), higher Lyman series lines (Ly
\beta
, Ly
\gamma
, etc.) are assumed to be optically thick and to convert into lower-series lines via interaction with neutral hydrogen.
If the
H
i
gas has a high
n=2
population and is optically thick to all Balmer transitions (i.e., on-the-spot approximation for Balmer lines), only H
\alpha
photons can escape, due to the metastable nature of the 2s state. This modifies the Balmer line ratios. Therefore, various H
\alpha
/H
\beta
ratios observed in LRDs
(
Taylor et al. 2025b
;
Brooks et al. 2025
)
may be due to radiative transfer effects, rather than dust extinction.

The collisional process in a dense gas, such as the broad line region in AGN and stellar atmosphere, affects the formation of Balmer lines
(
Drake & Ulrich 1980
;
Collin-Souffrin et al. 1982
;
Korista & Goad 2004
;
Ferland et al. 2017
)
.
At a high electron number density (
n_{\rm e}>10^{8}\,\rm cm^{-3}
), the collisional de-excitation is not negligible, thus electrons in the
n=4
states are easier de-excited than in the
n=3
state.
It causes a difference between intrinsic H
\alpha
and H
\beta
profiles, and their emission region is not identical.
Thus, if the Thomson scattering originates from a dense gas (e.g,
Begelman & Dexter 2025
), H
\alpha
and H
\beta
emission experience Thomson scattering with different optical depth, resulting in a difference in strength of the broad component around the hydrogen emission lines
(
Brazzini et al. 2025
)
.

This extreme gas condition may be relevant not only for LRDs and AGN, but also for other objects where H
\alpha
shows strong emission while higher Balmer lines show an absorption-dominated feature. In future studies, we will investigate the formation of Balmer lines under these conditions, including the effects of scattering on higher-order Balmer lines such as H
\gamma
and H
\delta
.

Thomson-dominated case

Thomson-scattered broad line region case

In the second scenario – the Thomson-scattered BLR case – we consider a geometry similar to the first, with a central emission region surrounded by an

4.6.3
Implications for Line Profiles and
M_{\rm BH}
Estimates

In summary, this section investigates the effect of Thomson scattering on black hole mass measurements.
We explored two scenarios: (1) a Thomson-dominated wing and (2) a Thomson-scattered BLR. In both cases, we estimated
M_{\rm BH}
using the standard scaling relation calibrated from reverberation mapping of local AGN.
In the first case, we showed that pure Thomson scattering can generate broad wings around Balmer lines, resulting in fictitious black hole masses exceeding
10^{6}M_{\odot}
when
T_{e}\geq 10^{4}\,\rm K
and
\tau_{\rm Th}\geq 0.1
.
In the second case, we demonstrated that scattering of BLR emission broadens the observed profile and leads to overestimation of
M_{\rm BH}
, particularly when
\tau_{\rm Th}\geq 1
.
Although Thomson scattering has a weaker impact on
M_{\rm BH}
estimates in the optically thin regime (
\tau_{\rm Th}<1
), it still produces exponential wings in the far line wings, as seen in local AGNs
(
Laor 2006
, e.g.,)
.

The role of Thomson scattering in LRDs remains debated.
Rusakov et al. 2025
proposed that the broad H
\alpha
features in LRDs are dominated by Thomson scattering, suggesting that intrinsically narrow emission lines with widths
\sim 100-200{\rm\,km\,s^{-1}}
are broadened through scattering in dense, ionized gas, which is similar to our first scenario. In contrast,
Brazzini et al. 2025
argued that the broad hydrogen line features in LRDs are inconsistent with this Thomson-dominated scenario. They suggest that if scattering dominates, the H
\alpha
scattering fraction should be higher than that of H
\beta
due to dust extinction. However, as discussed in Section
4.5
, the Thomson optical depth of H
\beta
can be higher than that of H
\alpha
in dense environments, complicating this interpretation.
These contrasting views highlight the need for detailed radiative transfer modeling to understand the formation of broad emission features in LRDs.

Our scenarios have important implications for LRDs, where Balmer absorption features suggest dense gas environments. Thomson scattering can naturally produce exponential wings and apparent line broadening, potentially biasing
M_{\rm BH}
estimates. In addition, if the bulk velocity of the scattering medium is faster than the electron thermal speed, Thomson scattering can provide asymmetric wings on emission lines, depending on the velocity structure of the gas. However, line profile analysis alone is insufficient to distinguish between kinematic and scattering origins of broad wings. Since Thomson scattering induces strong polarization
(
Lee 1999
;
Kim et al. 2007
)
, future spectropolarimetric observations of LRDs are a potential avenue to test this hypothesis.

5
Conclusion

In this study, we explored the physical origin of the hydrogen line profiles observed in Little Red Dots, which are compact, high-redshift sources identified through JWST spectroscopy. These systems exhibit broad wings (FWHM
\gtrsim 1000
km s
-1
) in Balmer and helium emission lines, along with Balmer absorption features and a strong Balmer break, indicating a dense, neutral hydrogen medium in the
n=2
state.
Under such conditions, radiative transfer effects may affect the formation of hydrogen lines.

We performed 3D Monte Carlo radiative transfer simulations to examine three key scattering mechanisms that shape hydrogen line profiles: (1) resonance scattering by hydrogen in the
n=2
state, (2) Raman scattering of UV photons by ground-state hydrogen (
n=1
), and (3) Thomson scattering by free electrons. For H
\beta
, we explicitly include multi-branching transitions in the resonance scattering process, accounting for both
n=4\rightarrow 2
and
n=4\rightarrow 3
decay channels. These pathways allow H
\beta
photons to convert into Pa
\alpha
and H
\alpha
, significantly suppressing the emergent H
\beta
flux. For Raman scattering, we adopt wavelength-dependent cross sections and branching ratios near the Lyman series. For Thomson scattering, we explore a range of electron temperatures (
T_{e}
) and optical depths (
\tau_{\rm Th}
).

Our results from each scattering process show that:

•

Resonance scattering explains the distinct profile differences between H
\alpha
, H
\beta
, and Pa
\alpha
and leads to photon conversion from H
\beta
to Pa
\alpha
and H
\alpha
, altering flux ratios (i.e., leading to large deviations from the value under the case B recombination, see Fig.
5
) and absorption depths. We have demonstrated how these effects imprint the gas geometry and kinematics on the emergent line shapes (Section
3.1
), and will explore their diagnostic power systematically in future work.

•

While resonant scattering can significantly alter (and for very large optical depths) widen the emergent H
\alpha
spectrum significantly, this is not the case for H
\beta
due to the efficient conversion to Pa
\alpha
(Section
4.2
).

•

Raman scattering can produce extremely broad wings (up to
\sim 10^{4}
km s
-1
), with widths scaling approximately as
{\rm FWHM}\propto\sqrt{N_{\rm HI}}
and different degrees of broadening for each Balmer line (Fig.
11
and Section
3.2
).
We show that the observed Balmer line widths are not consistent with these continuum Raman scattered features. In addition, the UV continuum in LRDs is likely too faint, effectively ruling out Raman scattered continuum photons to be the origin of the broad Balmer lines observed (Section
4.3
).

•

However, we also show that if higher Lyman series with the width
\sim 150{\rm\,km\,s^{-1}}
are fully Raman scattered, the width of the Raman wing around H
\alpha
and H
\beta
is over 1000  km s
-1
, with a FWHM ratio of H
\alpha
and H
\beta
is
\approx 1.28
(Fig.
19
and Appendix
B
).

•

Thomson scattering produces symmetric broad wings with similar widths across all hydrogen lines. In the optically thin regime, the wings exhibit exponential profiles, with widths scaling as
{\rm FWHM}\propto\sqrt{T_{e}}
(Figs.
11
and
12
). At higher optical depths (
\tau_{\rm Th}\gtrsim 2
), the wings deviate from the exponential shape due to multiple scatterings, and their width further increases with
\tau_{\rm Th}
(Figs.
12
and
13
).

We show that a combination of Thomson and resonant scattering can explain most spectral features observed in LRDs, specifically the broad wings and the central asymmetric double peak (Section
4.4
).
However, to understand different absorption features and broad wings of Balmer lines, the additional radiative transfer effects, such as on-the-spot approximation of Balmer lines, collisional excitation, and collisional de-excitation, are required (Section
4.5
).

Importantly, we demonstrate that if the broad component of Balmer lines is shaped by Thomson scattering rather than intrinsic broad emission, then black hole masses estimated using methods for local AGNs can be significantly overestimated (Section
4.6
). For optically thick cases (
\tau_{\rm Th}\geq 2
), the overestimation can exceed a factor of 10 (Fig.
16
).

Our results show the importance of detailed radiative transfer modeling when interpreting hydrogen line profiles, not only for LRDs and AGN, but also for other astrophysical systems exhibiting broad hydrogen wings and absorption features. In future work, we will apply our simulation library to analyze observed spectra of LRDs and expand our models to include more complex environments, such as those combining resonance and Thomson scattering in multi-phase media.
