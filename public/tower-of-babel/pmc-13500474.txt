Deep knowledge-driven multi-modal fusion for diagnosis and prognosis of SI-ARDS
Authors: Hongyi Chen, Yang Gu, Guangwei Zhang, Yan Zhang, Xiaohui Duan, Ziying Li, Jiaqi Lin, Xiaoling Yi, Mansheng Chen, Tianqi Yang, Leqi Zheng, Xuanqi Huang, Guoqiong Zeng, Huijun Hu, Riyu Han, Hao Wu, Phei Er Saw, Peiyuan Lai, Li Li, Changdong Wang, Yunfang Yu, Tao Yu, Mohsen Guizani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSepsis-Induced Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (SI-ARDS) presents significant diagnostic and prognostic challenges due to its complex clinical manifestations and high mortality rate.MethodsWe developed a deep Knowledge-Driven multi-Modal Fusion (KDMF) framework for the accurate diagnosis and prognosis of SI-ARDS. The model leverages multi-modal data, including CT images, CT reports, and laboratory indicators, alongside a disease-specific knowledge graph.ResultsKDMF achieves superior performance in predicting SI-ARDS incidence (AUC 0.930) and time to 28-day mortality (AUC 0.843, C-index 0.833). Comprehensive error analysis and ablation studies demonstrate the critical contributions of each data modality and the integrated knowledge graph.ConclusionsThe results highlight the potential of KDMF to enhance early intervention and treatment strategies, underscoring the robustness and interpretability of the framework in clinical applications.

Introduction

Sepsis, a life-threatening organ dysfunction triggered by a dysregulated host response to infection, presents a substantial global burden, implicated in at least 20% of all deaths with an estimated incidence of 508.4 per 100,000 person-years1. Its progression to Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS)—characterized by persistent hypoxemia2—defines sepsis-induced ARDS (SI-ARDS), a major clinical challenge accounting for 10.4% of ICU admissions where sepsis is the leading cause in up to 75% of cases3 and mortality reaches approximately 40%4,5. Consequently, early identification is critical yet profoundly difficult due to variable presentation, leading to frequent underdiagnosis and undertreatment6. This challenge is exacerbated by ICU workload and information overload7,8, with studies quantifying the gap: clinician recognition of ARDS can be as low as 51.3% for mild cases3, and manual screening misses approximately 73% of acute lung injury patients compared to automated surveillance8. The absence of timely intervention for these missed patients worsens9–12, thereby underscoring an urgent need for intelligent tools to support early SI-ARDS detection.

Historically, research concentrated on early ARDS detection through a combination of demographic data, clinical indicators, and laboratory parameters. For instance, while the Lung Injury Prediction Score (LIPS) was the first ARDS risk score system with high sensitivity, its specificity in predicting ARDS remained subpar13. The advent of Artificial Intelligence (AI) has considerably bolstered the precision of ARDS prediction models14–17. Yet, many AI models still primarily employ the traditional machine learning techniques like Random Forest (RF) or Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), achieving the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) values between 0.75 and 0.8318–21. In the medical deep learning space, contemporary approaches increasingly leverage multimodal clinical data, such as chest radiographs, ventilator waveforms, and radiology reports processed with natural language processing (NLP)14,22,23. By incorporating clinical uncertainty metrics and iterative algorithmic optimization, model performance has been significantly enhanced, with some externally validated models achieving AUROC values up to 0.9314,22. However, further performance improvements remain constrained by the reliance on high-quality annotated data and limited generalizability across diverse populations and clinical settings24,25, while the lack of interpretability in model decision-making continues to affect clinical trust and adoption26,27. Notably, medical practitioners typically assess organ dysfunction in sepsis patients by amalgamating data from diverse dimensions. By integrating medical domain knowledge with AI models, one can harness information from multiple data sources, enhancing predictive accuracy.

To address these limitations, this study develops a Knowledge-Driven Multi-modal Fusion (KDMF) deep learning framework. The model integrates thin-section CT images, radiology reports, and structured medical records with laboratory indicators, further enhanced by a domain-specific knowledge graph. In our evaluation, KDMF demonstrates a strong discriminative ability for SI-ARDS diagnosis, achieving an AUC of 0.930. For the prediction of 28-day mortality, it attains an AUC of 0.843 and a C-index of 0.833. Comprehensive ablation and error analyses confirm the critical contribution of each data modality and the integrated knowledge graph to the model’s performance and interpretability. These findings highlight the potential of the KDMF framework to serve as a robust clinical tool for early risk stratification and informed decision-making in patients with sepsis.

Methods

The details of the proposed method will be introduced in this section. Overall, the proposed method mainly contains four modules, namely CT image feature extraction module, CT report feature extraction module, laboratory indicator feature extraction module and multi-modal adaptive fusion module. The method takes three modalities of medical data as input, namely Image, Text and Index, representing CT images, the corresponding CT reports, and structured medical records and laboratory indicators, respectively. The data of each modality is processed through its own feature extraction module to obtain modality-specific features. Then, these modality-specific features are fused by multi-modal adaptive fusion module to obtain the fused features. Finally, the fused features are fed into the classification head and regression head for ARDS diagnosis and prognosis prediction, respectively. For the prognosis prediction, we treate it as a survival analysis task with right-censoring for patients who were alive at day 28. The details of each module are as follows.

The KDMF framework for SI-ARDS diagnosis and prognosis, depicted in Fig. 1(a), involves several stages: (a) Data representations are extracted in their specific ways, and a middle-term fusion (or joint fusion) method28 is utilized to integrate the multi-modal data. (b) A domain knowledge graph, anchored on the structural medical records and laboratory indicators, is constructed. (c) A multi-task model, including predictions for SI-ARDS onset and survival, is developed. This utilizes 3D convolutional neural networks29–31, bidirectional encoder representations from transformers (BERT)32 and graph attention networks33,34. CT image feature extraction module

To fully utilize the information from raw data, we input the entire sequence of CT images for each sample into the module. Specifically, we reconstruct the CT sequence into a 3D image. To account for varying lengths and slice dimensions among samples, interpolation techniques are employed to adjust the 3D image to a fixed size. The fixed-size images are then fed into CT image feature extraction module which consists of five 3D convolutional layers. Risk score computation and application

To quantitatively assess individual mortality risk within the 28-day observation window, a continuous risk score was defined based on survival duration. For each patient i, let ni denote the number of days survived before death or censoring, and let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T}_{\max }$$\end{document}Tmax denote the fixed observation horizon. The risk score was formulated as: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{i}=1-\frac{{n}_{i}}{{T}_{\max }}$$\end{document}Ri=1−niTmax

This formulation assigns higher risk scores to patients with shorter survival times and provides a normalized representation of mortality tendency aligned with the model’s probabilistic output.

Evaluation of the prognosis model

The prognosis task was formulated as a time-to-event (survival) analysis, with the event defined as death within 28 days after ICU admission. For patients who were alive at day 28 or lost to follow-up before death, their observations were treated as right-censored. The prognostic model outputs a risk score, which was used within a Cox proportional hazards framework to predict the time-to-event outcome.

The performance of the prognosis model was assessed using three complementary metrics suitable for censored survival data: 1) Harrell’s Concordance Index (C-index) to evaluate the overall ranking consistency of predicted risks; 2) Time-dependent Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) at specific time points (7, 14, and 28 days), calculated using the cumulative/dynamic estimator proposed by Heagerty et al. (Heagerty & Zheng, 2005); and 3) the Integrated Brier Score (IBS), which measures the average squared difference between the observed survival status and the predicted survival probability across the entire 28-day observation period, providing a measure of overall prediction accuracy and calibration; 4) The optimal risk score cut-off for stratifying patients into high- and low-risk groups was determined by maximizing the Youden index (J = sensitivity + specificity - 1) from the time-dependent ROC curve at day 28 in the training cohort, and this cut-off was subsequently applied unchanged to the validation cohort for all survival analyses. All survival analyses and metric calculations were performed using the survival, timeROC, and survex packages in R.

Results

Characteristics of patients

A total of 307 patients were enrolled from Sun Yat-Sen Memorial Hospital from March 2018 to October 2022. Among the 307 enrolled sepsis patients, 146 (47.6%) developed SI-ARDS within 28 days, while 161 (52.4%) did not. The number of deaths and survivors at day 7, day 14, and day 28 is provided in Fig. S1. The workflow of patient enrollment and group classification is presented in Fig. 1(c). For each patient, three data modalities were collected, including CT images (refer to as image data), textual content from CT reports (refer to as text data), and structured medical records and numerical laboratory indicators (together refer to as index data). Out of these, 246 patients (representing 80% of the cohort) were allocated to the training dataset for the development of the ARDS model. The baseline characteristics and clinical characteristics of sepsis patients in the training and validation groups are listed in Tables 1 and 2, respectively.

Multi-modal model for SI-ARDS diagnosis

Four primary data sources were integrated: CT images, text from CT reports, structured medical records and numerical laboratory indicators. These data, acquired during early admission, were further augmented with a domain-specific knowledge graph (DKG) constructed from the ARDS-related indicators. To obtain robust estimates of the model’s performance, we employed bootstrap resampling techniques. This involved randomly sampling the training and validation dataset ten times, resulting in ten different training and validation datasets, respectively. Then we plotted an average receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. This was achieved by first identifying the unique false positive rates (FPRs) from all iterations and then interpolating the corresponding true positive rates (TPRs) at these FPR values. The mean TPR values at these unique FPRs were then computed, resulting in an average ROC curve. The average AUC value was determined by calculating the area under the average ROC curve. This metric reflects the model’s performance under 10-fold stratified cross-validation, where the dataset was partitioned into ten non-overlapping subsets, and each subset was used exactly once as the validation set while the remaining nine were used for training. The reported AUC represents the mean value across all folds, with variability quantified by the standard deviation (shown as error bars in figures). A lung window thin-section CT sequence was chosen from 307 sepsis patients who had definite survival outcomes within 28 days and documented ARDS status. This cohort was comprised of 161 patients with sepsis who did not manifest ARDS and 146 patients diagnosed with sepsis who developed ARDS within the 28-day window. Given the vast volume of image data, prior studies typically employed a 2D approach for model design. In this method, each image from a CT sequence is viewed as a singular input to predict classification or regression outcomes. Contrarily, the methodology adopted in this research utilized 3D convolution. Here, each input is a 3D reconstructed comprehensive CT image of the patient’s lung. This approach offers the advantage of enabling the model to capture feature information in the third dimension, namely depth.

Each CT report included a segment titled “imaging findings”. The text descriptions from this segment were extracted, sub-coded, and processed through BERT32,47, converting each patient’s text report into a 768-dimensional feature vector.

The numerical values selected from blood sampling laboratory tests underwent z-score normalization, while categorical variables (0/1) derived from baseline characteristics (Table 1) and clinical manifestations (Table 2) were one-hot encoded. These processed data were then input into graph attention network (GAT)33,34 to derive feature vectors. It’s important to note that while z-score is applied to numerical values, one-hot encoding is utilized for categorical variables. Node values in the knowledge graph were randomly initialized and then fed into GAT to obtain the topological structure information of DKG. Finally, we concatenated these updated node values with processed laboratory indicators and structured medical records.

Multi-modal model for 28-day mortality prediction

In the prediction of 28-day mortality, the KDMF model demonstrated robust performance. The model achieved a C-index of 0.833 (95% CI: 0.804–0.863) and AUC of 0.843 in the validation set, indicating good discriminative ability for ranking patient risks (Fig. 2(b), Table 4). To evaluate time-specific discrimination, we computed time-dependent AUCs, which were 0.824, 0.843, and 0.843 at days 7, 14, and 28, respectively (Fig. 2(d)). Furthermore, the model attained an Integrated Brier Score (IBS) of 0.085 in the validation set, reflecting good overall prediction accuracy and calibration across the 28-day timeframe (Fig. S2).

Patients were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups based on a risk score cut-off derived from the training cohort (via the Youden index from the time-dependent ROC at day 28). This pre-specified cut-off was then applied consistently to both the training and validation cohorts. Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated that sepsis patients with higher risk scores had significantly worse survival in the discovery cohort. This finding was validated in an independent cohort using the same pre-specified cut-off, where the high-risk group likewise exhibited significantly poorer survival (log-rank p = 0.032) (Fig. S3). These results confirm that the prognostic model is well-equipped to predict the survival outcomes of sepsis patients and can potentially identify high-risk patients in advance to allow for early intervention.

Ablation study for model evaluation

To clarify the underlying mechanics of multi-modal models, feature extraction was conducted separately for each modality, and the resultant predictive capabilities were then assessed. To gain insight into the overall performance across the ten iterations, for each of these datasets, we calculated metrics such as AUC, sensitivity, specificity, etc. Finally, these calculated values of different datasets were then used to compute the mean value and its associated 95% confidence interval. The mean value serves as a representative performance measure, while the confidence interval provides a measure of the uncertainty in this estimate. When extracting features exclusively from CT images (labelled as “Image” in Table 3) and predicting using a deep learning model, the classification task for SI-ARDS diagnosis attained an AUC of 0.766, and the regression task for predicting survival days in sepsis patients achieved a C-index of 0.746 (Table 4). Ablation study on the prognosis task

To further dissect the fusion mechanisms and determine the unique contributions of each modality to ARDS prediction and survival estimation, ablation experiments were executed, omitting one modality during each iteration. For predicting SI-ARDS incidence, the standout combination was the dual-modal fusion of image and report, producing an AUC of 0.855 (Fig. 2(g)). In predicting 28-day survival, the combination of report and index was most effective, yielding a C-index of 0.811, but the low sensitivity of report+index fusion may reflect textual noise and missing indicators (Table 4). Nevertheless, no combination of just two modalities matched the performance of the KDMF model that integrated all three data modalities for both tasks.

Knowledge graph impact analysis

Symptoms, laboratory tests, and vital signs associated with SI-ARDS were integrated into the domain knowledge graph. The relationships among the nodes drew upon extensive open medical knowledge graphs and the most recent clinical guidelines from the emergency department. To ascertain the impact of DKG on the KDMF model, an ablation experiment was conducted without DKG (addressed as “Image+Report+Index” in Table 3 and Table 4). This exclusion led to reduced accuracy in the classification task (AUC = 0.825 [95% CI: 0.793–0.856] vs. 0.930 [0.913–0.946] with DKG, an absolute improvement of +0.105), and diminished performance in the regression task (C-index = 0.770 [0.730–0.811] vs. 0.833 [0.804–0.863] with DKG, an absolute improvement of +0.063). The non-overlapping 95% confidence intervals between the models with and without DKG suggest a meaningful contribution of the knowledge graph to both diagnostic and prognostic performance. Utilizing the weight matrix, the pivotal entities within the SI-ARDS DKG were identified (Fig. 3(b)). For a patient with sepsis, the top five symptoms or vital signs crucial for ARDS prediction included UTI, diabetes, abdominal infection, bloodstream infection, and lactate dehydrogenase, as shown in Fig. 3(b). In Fig. 3(c), the nodes of greater importance were represented with an enlarged size and a more profound hue.

Error analysis

In this study, the multi-modal model produced 614 computational outputs. An error analysis was performed to assess the model’s performance and investigate the origins of incorrect results, distinguishing four error categories: false positives and negatives in both diagnostic and prognostic tasks. For each erroneous prediction, the contribution of each modality was quantified as its normalized attention weight from the adaptive fusion module—defined as the ratio of a modality’s attention score to the sum of all three modalities’ scores—and averaged across cases within each error category.

Analysis of the model’s design revealed that the contributions of the three modalities (image, report, and index) to errors were generally comparable. Notably, however, the imaging modality was more influential, particularly in prognostic false positives, where it contributed to over 50% of errors (Table 5). These findings highlight the image modality’s significant role in the model’s performance, suggesting a focus on image processing improvements could enhance overall accuracy. Discussion

ARDS is a prevalent clinical disorder in critically ill patients, characterized by significant morbidity and mortality. While early diagnosis and treatment of SI-ARDS are pivotal for enhancing patient outcomes, current diagnostic tools often fall short in terms of both accuracy and efficiency6,48. Although artificial intelligence algorithms for ARDS diagnosis and management have been developed in recent years49,50, their widespread application has been hindered by issues such as data source heterogeneity, limited interpretability, and a lack of prospective validation. This research presents an innovative approach for the accurate diagnosis and prognosis prediction of SI-ARDS through a data-knowledge dual-driven AI model. Such accuracy paves the way for timely medical interventions, potentially ameliorating patient outcomes. Furthermore, the inclusion of an ablation study enabled the discernment of the most influential data modalities on model accuracy. By pinpointing these crucial data inputs, the model can be fine-tuned and made more accessible for real-world clinical applications.

In clinical decision-making, while objective patient examinations and medical histories are crucial, the role of expert knowledge and consensus in disease diagnosis and prognosis prediction remains indispensable34,51. In recent years, integrating medical knowledge graphs and updated clinical guidelines into predictive models has emerged as a significant frontier. Research indicates that leveraging the implicit judgments of clinical experts to construct an independent gold standard can effectively circumvent the “circularity problem” that arises when the same criteria are used to define both labels and features, thereby enhancing the validity and discoverability of predictive models52. Furthermore, structurally integrating knowledge graph information encompassing multiple organ systems—through deep learning networks such as bidirectional LSTMs—enables real-time and accurate prediction of sepsis severity53. The use of prompt engineering driven by large language models offers a paradigm for efficiently and accurately constructing specialized knowledge graphs from multicenter clinical data, thereby expanding and organizing expert knowledge54. This study hypothesizes that supplementing models with such structured external expert knowledge can improve their performance and generalizability. Experimental validation confirms that incorporating representation vectors extracted from the topological structure of the disease knowledge graph led to significant improvements: the AUC for the SI-ARDS diagnostic task increased from 0.825 to 0.930, and for the sepsis prognosis task, from 0.775 to 0.843 (Fig. 3(a)). Despite variations in patient populations across healthcare institutions, expert insights and core medical knowledge possess global commonality. Beyond CT images, CT reports, and laboratory indicators are routinely collected for patients in emergency settings. In clinical scenarios, physicians often synthesize information from various sources rather than relying solely on one modality, such as images or lab results. Nevertheless, the integration and processing of such multi-modal data present challenges for deep learning diagnostic models. Historically, clinical studies using knowledge and data-driven methods haven’t integrated both image and text data. This study employed a medium-term fusion strategy, channeling medical data from three modalities (image, report, and index) into an attention-based network. This approach sought to emulate the holistic decision-making process typical of physicians in real-world practice and yielded promising prediction outcomes.

In the analysis of false positives and false negatives, it has been found that imaging consistently contributes the most errors of various types. However, considering the clinical reality and the unique nature of ARDS pathology, the unclear characteristics of lung CT images present challenges. The current model is unable to surpass the existing cognitive features related to ARDS imaging. Therefore, we emphasize the importance of a multi-modal approach. To improve imaging effects and enhance prediction capabilities, future enhancements may involve incorporating large-scale medical imaging models55. However, it is important to note that mature medical imaging models have not yet emerged, which offers a promising direction for future exploration.

It is worth noting that a performance gap exists between the training and validation sets across the tested models (Table 3). This discrepancy is likely attributable to the relatively limited sample size (N = 307) inherent to this specialized dataset, which may predispose high-capacity deep learning models to a degree of overfitting. However, several observations suggest that the proposed KDMF framework learns generalizable clinical features rather than fitting noise. First, the KDMF model maintains a high AUC of 0.930 on the validation set, significantly outperforming the single-modal baselines. Second, the integration of the Domain Knowledge Graph (DKG) notably reduced the generalization gap compared to the ’Image + Report + Index’ model (Validation AUC improvement from 0.825 to 0.930), indicating that domain knowledge effectively regularizes the learning process. Finally, the implementation of bootstrap resampling (10 iterations) further ensures that the reported performance is robust and stable across different data distributions.
