Climate change health risks and workplace protective strategies for construction workers
Authors: Suyon Baek, Eun-Hi Choi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Climate change increasingly threatens occupational health, with construction workers facing elevated risks due to prolonged outdoor exposure. Although individual risk factors are well documented, workplace-level protective strategies integrating multi-stakeholder perspectives remain limited. To develop an integrated framework identifying climate change factors threatening construction workers’ health, examine associated health impacts, and propose evidence-based workplace protective strategies. Three focus group interviews were conducted with 23 construction sector experts, including eight government officials specializing in outdoor labor conditions and fifteen industry health managers with extensive experience in climate-related occupational health management. Data were collected between January and March 2021 and analyzed using systematic thematic analysis following established qualitative frameworks. Analysis revealed four interconnected themes comprising 24 categories: (1) climate factors affecting construction workers (heat waves, humidity, cold waves, fine particulate matter); (2) health problems encompassing direct physiological effects (heat stroke, cardiovascular events, respiratory dysfunction) and indirect outcomes (musculoskeletal disorders, increased accident rates); (3) systemic and organizational barriers including inadequate rest facilities, insufficient budgets, inflexible schedules, and limited managerial awareness; and (4) protective strategies requiring policy integration, including climate-specific regulatory standards, weather-contingent scheduling, multi-level education programs, enhanced health manager capacity, mandated rest facilities, and advanced protective technologies. This study presents the first comprehensive practitioner-informed framework integrating climate risks, health impacts, and protective strategies for construction workers. Effective protection demands coordinated policy action, organizational investment, and technological innovation rather than fragmented safety measures. The framework provides actionable guidance for policymakers and industry stakeholders globally. As findings reflect Korean construction sector practitioners’ perspectives, future research should include climate scientists and validate findings across diverse settings.

Introduction

Climate change is occurring worldwide, with wide-ranging effects on human health that require interventions and adaptations to mitigate their impact1. Increases in ambient temperature, air pollution, ultraviolet radiation exposure, and extreme weather events are some of the categories of climate change that impact human health2. Climate change has also been linked to adverse human health impacts, such as heat-related mortality3.

Outdoor workers are experiencing adverse health impacts due to climate change. These individuals perform their work activities outside traditional office settings and may be employed in various industries, including construction, agriculture, landscaping, and transportation. For these workers, the primary health risks associated with climate change include heat-related illnesses4,5, cardiovascular disease6, and an increased risk of accidents7.

The construction industry is a prime example of outdoor work and is characterized by a complex network organization, stemming from the numerous project participants and their distributed responsibilities8. It is broadly categorized into building construction and civil construction. Building construction includes driving piles into the ground, excavating to the designated depth, and blasting rock when necessary. During underground excavations, the soil must be capped to prevent collapse, and formwork is utilized to support the concrete. Tasks such as reinforcement, steelwork, concrete laying, masonry, plastering, and extrusion are also performed9. Civil construction similarly involves earthwork, structural foundation work, and material installation in outdoor environments. Critically, both construction sectors share a common occupational feature: the majority of work is conducted outdoors under direct environmental exposure, making workers across all construction domains vulnerable to climate-related health risks.

Climate change exacerbates the exposure to physicochemical factors in construction workplaces, as exemplified by increased levels of fine particulate matter and air pollutants during extreme weather events, leading to heightened respiratory risks for outdoor workers10. Musculoskeletal disorders are common among construction workers due to the physical demands of their work11. Extreme weather conditions, such as heat waves or cold waves caused by climate change, can exacerbate these disorders by increasing muscle fatigue and joint discomfort. High temperatures during heat waves can accelerate physical exhaustion, while cold waves can lead to muscle stiffness and reduced flexibility, both of which increase the risk of musculoskeletal problems12.

Previous studies have demonstrated that outdoor workers exhibit signs of heat stress, including elevated core body temperatures, abnormally high sweat rates, dehydration, and impaired renal health13. Research has explored the effects of high ambient temperatures on physiological heat stress among outdoor construction workers, revealing that stress levels rise with increasing temperatures14. Additionally, workers in the construction industry face considerable risks from the impacts of climate change, which exacerbate existing occupational health and safety hazards15. These risks are intensified by climate change hazards that accelerate health threats in conjunction with the inherent dangers of construction work.

To protect construction workers from the impacts of climate change, several studies have recommended various measures. These include encouraging frequent fluid intake, resting in shaded or cooled areas, adjusting work hours, and increasing electrolyte consumption16. Additional strategies suggest optimizing work-rest schedules and allowing adequate time for workers to acclimate to extreme conditions5.

Existing research provides valuable insights into the impacts of climate change on workers; however, there is a notable gap in studies addressing the practical implementation of workplace strategies to safeguard construction workers. This study seeks to bridge this gap by exploring fundamental workplace strategies to protect outdoor workers in the construction industry from health risks associated with climate change. The research questions guiding this study include: “What climate change hazards threaten workers at construction sites, typically outdoor environments?”, “How do these hazards endanger workers’ health?”, “Are current workplace measures adequate to protect workers from climate change hazards?”, and “What strategies should be implemented to protect construction workers from these hazards?”

To address these questions, the study aims to generate essential data for developing strategies and policies to safeguard the health of construction workers against climate change. The specific objectives of this study were as follows.

First, to identify climate change factors that threaten construction workers.

Second, to examine health problems caused by these climate change factors.

Third, to investigate workplace barriers that hinder the implementation of protective measures.

Fourth, to propose practical workplace strategies to protect construction workers from health issues related to climate change.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative study aimed to identify factors associated with climate change that threaten the safety and health of outdoor construction workers and to explore strategies for managing their health. The study design was grounded in the National Research Council (NRC) climate–health risk framework2,17,18, which conceptualizes climate impacts through exposure pathways, health outcomes, and vulnerability modifiers. Building on this foundation, we incorporated a fourth analytical domain—systemic and organizational barriers—to account for workplace infrastructure and managerial constraints that hinder the implementation of protective measures19. This modified framework provided the overall analytical structure for examining climate hazards, health impacts, systemic barriers, and protective strategies, and served as the conceptual basis for the study’s qualitative analysis.

Recruitment of study participants and ethical considerations

Twenty-three construction-sector experts participated in three focus group interviews conducted between January and March 2021. Participants were recruited through purposive snowball sampling using explicit eligibility criteria: (1) a minimum of three years of professional experience in construction-industry occupational health management or governmental oversight of outdoor workplace safety; (2) direct involvement in climate-related health risk assessment or intervention implementation (e.g., heat illness prevention, extreme weather protocols); and (3) the ability to articulate workplace challenges and strategies in a group discussion format.

Five initial “seed” participants representing diverse organizational contexts (three governmental agencies and two construction firms) were identified through the research team’s professional networks. These individuals recommended colleagues who met the eligibility criteria. To maintain researcher control and ensure standardized communication, the research team directly contacted all nominated professionals (n = 27), verified eligibility, explained study objectives, and obtained informed consent. Twenty-three consented to participate (85.2% response rate); four declined due to scheduling conflicts (n = 3) or self-perceived lack of climate-specific expertise (n = 1). No systematic demographic or organizational differences were observed between participants and decliners.

To mitigate potential homogeneity bias in snowball sampling, two diversification strategies were implemented: (1) seed participants were instructed to nominate professionals from different organizational settings and roles (governmental vs. industrial contexts); and (2) the research team continuously monitored emerging sample composition and solicited additional nominations to enhance heterogeneity across organization type and experience level. The final sample represented six governmental agencies and nine construction companies, with work experience ranging from 3 to 18 years (Table 1), supporting the transferability of findings across diverse contexts.

Three focus groups comprising 7–8 participants each were deliberately composed to include both government officials and industry health managers, promoting cross-perspective dialogue. Each session was facilitated by an experienced qualitative researcher using explicit ground rules emphasizing equal participation and respect for diverse perspectives. Facilitation techniques to ensure balanced engagement included: (1) direct invitations for less vocal participants to contribute; (2) sequential “round-robin” questioning on key topics; and (3) real-time monitoring of speaking turns to prevent dominance. Transcript analysis confirmed that no single participant contributed more than 20% of total speaking turns, indicating equitable participation.

All participants were informed of the study’s purpose, duration, and procedures before the sessions, and written consent was obtained. The consent form specified that participation was voluntary and that individuals could withdraw at any time without consequence. Each participant received ₩200,000 (approximately USD $150) as compensation for professional time (90–120 min plus travel), consistent with institutional guidelines for expert consultation. This amount acknowledged participants’ expertise without constituting undue inducement. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Eulji University (EU21-050).

Development of the questionnaire

The researcher posed the following questions: “What are the health problems caused by climate change for outdoor workers?” and “What are the intervention measures when health problems occur due to climate change?” We reformulated these questions for expert consultation, adhering to the principles outlined in the Focus Group Question Guide20. The revised set comprised four questions: “What climate factors affect the safety and health of outdoor workers in construction?” “Please describe the environment at your workplace.” “What health problems arise for outdoor workers due to climate change?” “What are the difficulties in managing the health of outdoor workers in response to climate change?” and “What are your workplace strategies to protect the health of outdoor workers?” To augment the face validity of the questionnaire, feedback was obtained from health experts in the construction industry and professionals experienced in assessing face validity. These experts reviewed the questionnaire and provided suggestions for improvement, ensuring its relevance and clarity for the study objectives.

Procedure of focus group interviews

The focus group sessions were held in a private lounge area to ensure a comfortable and neutral environment. Participants were seated around a square table to facilitate equal visual access, reduce hierarchical seating effects, and promote open dialogue between governmental and industry representatives. Scheduling accommodated participants’ availability to maximize attendance and engagement.

Each session was facilitated by the lead researcher, an experienced qualitative investigator with over ten years of occupational health research and focus group moderation experience. The facilitator obtained informed consent prior to each session and managed group dynamics throughout the discussions. To ensure balanced participation, several facilitation strategies were employed: (1) establishing explicit ground rules emphasizing equal opportunity to speak; (2) directly inviting quieter participants by name to share their views; (3) using a round-robin approach for key questions before opening general discussion; and (4) monitoring speaking time distribution to prevent dominance by any single participant. Post-session transcript review confirmed equitable participation, with no individual contributing more than 20% of total speaking turns in any session.

After each question, the facilitator summarized the discussion and invited additional or contrasting viewpoints. Following each focus group, transcripts were produced immediately and analyzed for meaningful statements. Insights from one session informed the subsequent session through refined probing questions and follow-up on emerging themes. Data saturation was assessed using two criteria: (1) thematic exhaustion, when no new codes appeared in the final group transcript, and (2) informational redundancy, when participants in the last session reiterated existing themes without introducing novel perspectives. Consistency of themes across all groups and the absence of emergent codes confirmed that data saturation had been achieved.

Data analysis

The research team transcribed all focus group interviews verbatim immediately following each session, producing 84 pages of transcript data. Transcriptions were systematically verified against debriefing notes and field observations documented by the facilitator to ensure accuracy and completeness. Data were analyzed using Morse and Field’s latent content analysis method21, which involves iterative reading and interpretation to identify underlying meanings within textual data. This approach was appropriate for exploring practitioners’ implicit understandings of climate-related health issues in construction settings.

Analysis followed a hybrid deductive–inductive approach structured by the modified National Research Council (NRC) climate–health framework (Fig. 1). Four a priori analytical domains—climate hazards, health impacts, systemic barriers, and protective strategies—derived from the climate–health literature17–19 guided the initial coding process. Within these domains, specific categories and subcategories were identified inductively through repeated engagement with the transcripts (see Table 2, Results section).

Two researchers collaboratively reviewed and coded the transcripts. When differences in interpretation arose, they re-examined the relevant sections together and revised the coding until agreement was reached. To enhance analytical rigor, the following verification procedures were implemented: (1) A preliminary codebook specifying code definitions and exemplar quotations was collaboratively developed during initial coding. (2) A subset of transcripts (about 30%) was independently double-coded by both analysts; discrepancies were discussed until interpretive agreement was achieved. (3) After themes were identified, the research team reviewed and refined coding consistency across transcripts, confirming that interpretations were stable across sessions. (4) Concise summaries of each theme were then shared with several participants to confirm that the findings accurately reflected their perspectives (member checking). Feedback from these participants was used to adjust the wording and contextual descriptions of themes.

Trustworthiness and rigor

To ensure methodological rigor and enhance confidence in the study findings, verification procedures were evaluated against Sandelowski’s22 four criteria for trustworthiness in qualitative research: credibility, fittingness, auditability, and confirmability.

Credibility was established through several convergent strategies. Interviews were conducted at times and in formats preferred by participants, encouraging open expression without constraint. At the end of each session, facilitators confirmed that summaries accurately reflected participants’ views and clarified any interpretive ambiguities. Member checking (Section “Conclusion”) enabled participants to validate that thematic interpretations authentically represented their experiences. In addition, two external workplace health managers—experts in construction-sector occupational health but independent of the study—reviewed all derived themes to assess interpretive accuracy and phenomenological authenticity. This external validation provided an objective check ensuring that interpretations reflected workplace realities rather than researcher assumptions.

Fittingness was supported through purposive sampling designed to capture diverse professional perspectives. Participants included both senior (≥ 10 years) and early-career (3–5 years) professionals from six governmental agencies and nine construction companies, encompassing regulatory and operational viewpoints. Data collection across three focus groups continued until thematic saturation was reached, evidenced by convergence of key themes and the absence of new categories. This heterogeneity of experience enhances the transferability of findings to comparable construction-industry contexts.

Auditability was achieved through transparent documentation that allows reconstruction of analytical decisions. The preliminary codebook (Section “Conclusion”) provided explicit coding definitions and exemplar quotations used for thematic categorization. In addition, verbatim participant quotations are presented in the Results (Table 2), enabling readers to evaluate the alignment between raw data and interpretive claims. This transparency supports external appraisal of the analytical process.

Confirmability was reinforced through reflexive consideration of researcher bias and multiple triangulation strategies. The analytical team included an occupational health nursing faculty member and a researcher trained in qualitative methods. They reflected collaboratively on how their professional perspectives could shape data interpretation. Investigator triangulation (dual independent coding and consensus), data-source triangulation (three focus groups representing diverse organizations), and external expert validation collectively strengthened the neutrality of interpretations. The convergence of findings across these verification sources—internal analytical consensus, external expert review, and participant confirmation—provides assurance that derived themes reflect participant meanings rather than researcher preconceptions.

Collectively, these integrated verification procedures demonstrate systematic attention to trustworthiness criteria and establish methodological transparency, providing confidence that the study findings authentically represent the phenomenon under investigation.

All subjects provided written informed consent prior to their participation in the study. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Eulji University (IRB No. EU21-050) before data collection.

① Progressive heat-related physiological strain: heat stress, elevated core temperature, thermal fatigue, and heat stroke.

Construction workers experience a continuum of heat-related physiological impacts that escalate progressively with exposure intensity and duration. Health managers documented observable elevations in core body temperature during active work periods, particularly under direct solar radiation.

“When we measured workers’ body temperature as they worked, it was 39.2 degrees Celsius. But all the workers there had temperatures like 39.2 degrees or 38.3 degrees when measured. After resting, their temperatures dropped.” (Expert 4).

“When we measured workers’ body temperature as they worked, it was 39.2 degrees Celsius. But all the workers there had temperatures like 39.2 degrees or 38.3 degrees when measured. After resting, their temperatures dropped.” (Expert 4).

Sustained heat exposure amplifies physical exhaustion beyond normal occupational fatigue, particularly when radiant heat from construction materials—such as asphalt surfaces exceeding 60 °C or steel structures under direct sunlight—compounds ambient thermal stress.

“ Standing outside for an hour, in general, is very different from standing at a construction site. Standing on asphalt for an hour makes you feel like you’re melting. If you measure it with a thermometer or something, it’s like 70 or 90 degrees Celsius.” (Expert 3).

“ Standing outside for an hour, in general, is very different from standing at a construction site. Standing on asphalt for an hour makes you feel like you’re melting. If you measure it with a thermometer or something, it’s like 70 or 90 degrees Celsius.” (Expert 3).

In extreme scenarios, prolonged exposure under high-temperature high-humidity conditions can overwhelm thermoregulatory capacity, elevating acute heat stroke risk—a medical emergency characterized by core temperatures exceeding 40 °C and potential multi-organ dysfunction.

“ If workers engage in moderate to heavy work in high temperatures and humidity during summer, the effectiveness of lowering body temperature through sweating diminishes. As a result, the core body temperature of the worker rises, increasing the risk of developing acute heat stroke.” (Expert 9).

“ If workers engage in moderate to heavy work in high temperatures and humidity during summer, the effectiveness of lowering body temperature through sweating diminishes. As a result, the core body temperature of the worker rises, increasing the risk of developing acute heat stroke.” (Expert 9).

② Increased risk of acute cardiovascular events due to cold exposure and early-morning work conditions.

Cold wave exposure combined with demanding early-morning work schedules creates conditions conducive to acute cardiovascular events among construction workers. Health managers identified that winter construction practices impose compounded physiological stressors.

“When it’s cold outside, it’s common for workers to start their shifts before 5 a.m. They often skip breakfast after starting work, smoke, and drink coffee. Due to the cold temperatures, there’s a risk of hypothermia, which can increase the likelihood of cardiovascular diseases.” (Expert 16).

“When it’s cold outside, it’s common for workers to start their shifts before 5 a.m. They often skip breakfast after starting work, smoke, and drink coffee. Due to the cold temperatures, there’s a risk of hypothermia, which can increase the likelihood of cardiovascular diseases.” (Expert 16).

③ Accelerated decline in lung function due to fine particulate matter.

Outdoor workers frequently experience respiratory problems due to dust exposure at construction sites. Participants noted that dust levels become particularly severe during spring because dry weather and strong winds disperse soil and construction debris.

“In spring, construction sites are vulnerable to respiratory issues due to fine and mineral dust. Workers often neglect wearing protective gear due to discomfort. As a result, it’s common to find many workers with respiratory abnormalities during health screenings.” (Expert 11).

“In spring, construction sites are vulnerable to respiratory issues due to fine and mineral dust. Workers often neglect wearing protective gear due to discomfort. As a result, it’s common to find many workers with respiratory abnormalities during health screenings.” (Expert 11).

Indirect impacts.

① Increase in musculoskeletal disorders: exacerbated by heat-induced muscle fatigue and cold-induced tissue stiffness.

During the summer, workers often collapse and sleep wherever they can during their breaks after engaging in physically demanding tasks. However, this behavior can exacerbate muscle fatigue. Accumulated fatigue in the muscles can lead to a higher incidence of musculoskeletal diseases.

“When moving heavy steel bars, the relaxed muscles accumulate fatigue due to high temperatures, increasing the risk of musculoskeletal disorders.” (Expert 8).

“When moving heavy steel bars, the relaxed muscles accumulate fatigue due to high temperatures, increasing the risk of musculoskeletal disorders.” (Expert 8).

“People who exert themselves often just collapse and sleep wherever they can during their breaks. This itself contributes to the accumulation of fatigue, which leads to musculoskeletal disorders occurring 2–3 times more frequently.” (Expert 1).

“People who exert themselves often just collapse and sleep wherever they can during their breaks. This itself contributes to the accumulation of fatigue, which leads to musculoskeletal disorders occurring 2–3 times more frequently.” (Expert 1).

Conversely, cold wave exposure induces biomechanical risks through a different mechanism. Low temperatures cause peripheral vasoconstriction and connective tissue stiffness, reducing joint flexibility and grip strength—critical biomechanical factors when manipulating heavy construction materials.

“Workers typically use their hands and bodies extensively. However, in cold weather, tasks like handling steel bars become difficult. It’s hard to grip objects, and working becomes challenging when hands freeze.” (Expert 3).

“Workers typically use their hands and bodies extensively. However, in cold weather, tasks like handling steel bars become difficult. It’s hard to grip objects, and working becomes challenging when hands freeze.” (Expert 3).

② Risk of target organ damage from heat-enhanced volatilization of organic compounds: hepatic, renal, and pulmonary toxicity.

Participants identified that heat waves accelerate the volatilization of organic solvents and compounds used in painting, coating, and waterproofing applications. This thermally enhanced volatilization increases airborne concentrations of chemicals at construction sites, creating compounded health risks beyond direct heat stress.

“Painters working on exterior wall painting often engage in tasks such as walking along a very narrow platform while painting, and they face an increased risk of heat-related illnesses during heat waves. Moreover, the volatilization of organic compounds can accelerate adverse health effects, including the liver, kidneys, and lungs.” (Expert 8).

“Painters working on exterior wall painting often engage in tasks such as walking along a very narrow platform while painting, and they face an increased risk of heat-related illnesses during heat waves. Moreover, the volatilization of organic compounds can accelerate adverse health effects, including the liver, kidneys, and lungs.” (Expert 8).

③ Increased cold-related asphyxiation mortality: associated with winter heating practices during concrete curing.

Participants reported a winter-specific occupational hazard linked to concrete curing operations during cold weather. Concrete setting is a temperature-dependent process that proceeds inadequately under cold conditions. To maintain construction schedules, workers commonly use combustion of lignite or similar materials to provide supplemental heat for concrete curing.

“Concrete and steel have the same coefficient of thermal expansion. They don’t set in winter, so we can’t do the overlay work. So, in the winter, we burn lignite or other materials. Workers have to stay overnight for this job, but there are no proper rest conditions. Therefore, in the cold weather, there are many cases of suffocation accidents due to lignite.” (Expert 3).

“Concrete and steel have the same coefficient of thermal expansion. They don’t set in winter, so we can’t do the overlay work. So, in the winter, we burn lignite or other materials. Discussion

This qualitative study analyzed interviews with health professionals in the construction industry to identify workplace strategies for protecting outdoor workers from climate change-induced health problems. The analysis yielded four themes and 24 categories, detailed in Table 2; Fig. 2, with a primary focus on Fig. 2.

The health risks associated with climate change include heat waves, cold waves, fine particulate matter, strong winds, and ultraviolet rays. Heat waves are particularly significant, causing heat-related illnesses13, rapid increases in body temperature, and thermal fatigue without prior symptoms. Workers reported fatigue, pain, and heat strain during high temperatures, worsened by humidity23. This reduced work efficiency and increased musculoskeletal disorders, aligning with studies linking heat stress to joint pain and musculoskeletal issues24,25. Humidity amplifies heat strain by limiting sweat evaporation, which is why combined indices such as WBGT are standard in occupational screening26,27. While combined heat-humidity can approach human tolerance limits28, epidemiological effects vary by setting and metric29,30, positioning humidity as a conditional amplification factor.

Heat waves indirectly increase accidents by inducing careless actions among workers. A 5-year time-series analysis in Italy and a similar study in Australia both found higher occupational accident rates with rising temperatures31,32. In construction, accidents are linked to unsafe behavior influenced by factors like age, sleep quality33, and increased psychological strain34. High temperatures may impair cognition, judgment, and posture, heightening psychological stress and accident risks. Heat waves can also intensify chemical hazards, such as the volatilization of organic compounds (VOCs), potentially leading to target organ damage35. Workplaces must address these risks, including fatigue-induced musculoskeletal disorders, safety accidents, and chemical exposure.

The study identified health risks linked to climate change, including cerebrovascular diseases triggered by cold waves. A meta-analysis revealed that cardiovascular mortality increases with each 1 °C drop in temperature, particularly affecting coronary heart disease and aortic aneurysms36. Experimental studies have suggested that lower temperatures may elevate blood pressure, indicating vascular effects37. Additionally, cold waves indirectly increase suffocation incidents, particularly at South Korean construction sites where 9.7% of asphyxiation accidents were linked to winter practices such as using lignite for concrete curing38.

This study identified fine particulate matter (PM2.5) as a significant climate-related health risk that is particularly acute for construction workers due to combined exposure pathways. Atmospheric PM2.5 levels increase during spring due to climatic factors such as dry weather and reduced precipitation39. Simultaneously, construction activities such as excavation and drilling generate occupational dust40. Participants reported that respiratory problems become particularly severe during spring, when elevated atmospheric PM2.5 coincides with construction-generated dust, creating compounded exposure. Workers frequently reported throat and bronchial discomfort after shifts during this season41,42. Prolonged exposure to such combined particulate matter can impair lung function and increase the risk of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease41. Future climate projections indicate continued increases in PM2.5 levels39, further elevating health risks for outdoor workers who face both environmental and occupational particulate exposure simultaneously.

This study identified three main factors hindering workplace responses to health risks posed by climate change. The first is the lack of facilities, budgets, data at construction sites to protect outdoor workers’ health. Facilities such as restrooms and showers provide essential shade, hydration, and rest, reducing heat stress and improving physical stability during heat waves43–47. Restrooms also contribute to better health, reduced healthcare utilization, and increased job satisfaction44,45. However, their availability is influenced by the construction industry’s status, managers’ perceptions, and associated costs48. In Korea, Article 72 of the Occupational Safety and Health Act allocates a portion of construction costs to safety and health management49, but it excludes expenses for break room installation and maintenance, which fall on companies. Developing improved funding models for occupational safety and health management has been proposed to address this gap50,51. Mitigating climate change risks requires corporate and regulatory support for investing in health protection facilities for outdoor workers.

This study identified several infrastructure deficiencies, including the lack of workplace-specific data measurement and the need for advanced technologies to protect workers. For outdoor workers, perceived body temperature increases with age due to the combined effects of ambient air temperature and radiant heat from asphalt and construction materials52. Currently, Korean workplaces rely on general heat warnings and intensity levels issued by the Korea Meteorological Administration to address heat waves and cold waves. However, few sites measure the wet bulb globe temperature (WBGT), which accounts for temperature, humidity, and workload53. The absence of site-specific WBGT measurements limits the ability to gather accurate data, which is particularly important as many construction workers are older54. Regular WBGT measurements at worksites could provide critical data to improve health protection strategies over time. Furthermore, protective gear such as cooling hats and vests can help mitigate heat stress by circulating cold air around the body55. However, participants reported that such equipment is often heavy, uncomfortable, and costly, limiting its use during regular work. These findings indicate the need to develop lightweight, comfortable, and affordable protective technologies that can be readily adopted in construction settings. Improving usability and comfort will be critical to increasing worker compliance and ensuring sustained protection under changing climatic conditions.

A second barrier to protecting outdoor workers’ health identified in this study was the limited awareness among both workers and managers regarding the health impacts of climate change. Several participants noted that many site supervisors still regarded heat illness or cold-related symptoms as personal endurance issues rather than occupational risks. This perception gap was especially evident among older and temporary workers who often prioritized immediate income over preventive actions, consistent with previous reports of limited safety awareness among aging construction workforces54,56. Participants also emphasized that existing safety education rarely addresses climate change–related risks, focusing mainly on conventional accident prevention. This finding aligns with prior studies showing that safety training in the construction industry remains largely compliance-oriented and insufficient to address emerging environmental hazards57. Therefore, managerial support and regular education are critical for fostering a stronger safety culture and improving workers’ adaptive behaviors58,59. Furthermore, as participants indicated, time pressure and tight schedules often discourage the suspension of outdoor work during extreme weather events, which corresponds with earlier findings that compressed project timelines undermine the implementation of safety measures60. Introducing contractual flexibility to extend construction periods under extreme climate conditions may thus be an effective strategy to protect worker health and safety61.

Health managers play a critical role in safeguarding outdoor construction workers from climate change. Despite most construction accidents being safety-related, health factors have often been overlooked. Since 2015, South Korea has required health managers in construction, recognizing that the benefits of protecting workers’ health outweigh the associated costs62. This regulation, introduced decades later than in manufacturing (1982), has led health managers to focus on gaining site-specific knowledge and developing tailored guidelines63. Collaboration and information sharing among health managers are vital to enhancing their impact on workers’ health64.

Finally, while this study was conducted within the Korean construction sector, its findings have broader relevance for outdoor industries in other regions facing similar climate-related challenges. The framework proposed in this study presents general management principles for protecting workers’ health from climate hazards; however, specific strategies—such as rest breaks, workload adjustment, and environmental monitoring—should be adapted to each country’s climatic conditions, industrial practices, and regulatory systems. Because there is no universal international standard for managing heat or cold stress, future research and policy development should focus on creating context-specific guidelines that reflect local climate realities and labor environments.

The primary limitation of this study is its qualitative nature, meaning that its findings may not be generalizable across all construction settings or climates. Furthermore, the reliance on focus group interviews means that the findings are based on the subjective experiences and perceptions of a limited number of participants. Despite these limitations, this research adds to the existing body of knowledge by identifying specific climate change factors that pose threats to the health of construction workers and proposing actionable strategies to mitigate these risks. It provides a foundational framework for the development of policies and interventions designed to protect workers from climate-induced health hazards. Additionally, the study underscores the critical need for the industry-wide adoption of advanced protective technologies and ongoing education to ensure the safety and well-being of construction workers in a changing climate.

Conclusion

This qualitative study interviewed 23 construction-sector experts (8 government officials, 15 industry health managers) to identify strategies for protecting construction workers from climate change hazards. Analysis revealed four interconnected themes comprising 24 categories: climate.

factors, health impacts, systemic barriers, and protective strategies.

Key findings demonstrate that climate change poses both direct health risks (heat stroke, cardiovascular events, respiratory dysfunction) and indirect risks (musculoskeletal disorders, accidents) to construction workers. Critical systemic barriers include inadequate rest facilities, insufficient budgets, inflexible schedules, and limited managerial awareness of climate-related health risks.

Based on these findings, we recommend four integrated approaches: First, develop comprehensive workplace response plans addressing not only direct climatic impacts but also secondary effects on workers’ physical and mental health and workplace chemical exposures. Second, implement proactive education programs to enhance climate health awareness among both managers and workers. Third, establish mandatory government policies including extended construction periods during extreme weather and dedicated budgets for climate adaptation measures. Fourth, develop lightweight, user-friendly protective technologies with biorhythm monitoring capabilities to improve worker compliance.

This study provides the first practitioner-informed framework integrating climate risks, health impacts, and protective strategies for construction workers. The framework offers actionable guidance for policymakers and industry stakeholders, emphasizing that effective protection requires.

coordinated policy action, organizational investment, and technological innovation rather than fragmented measures.

However, limitations should be acknowledged. Participants were health management practitioners rather than climate scientists, and findings reflect the Korean construction context. Transferability to other countries, sectors, or expert groups may be limited. Future research should triangulate these practitioner perspectives with climate science expertise and validate findings through quantitative and mixed-methods studies across diverse occupational settings.

Despite these limitations, this research contributes essential knowledge for developing climate-adaptive occupational health policies tailored to outdoor work realities, particularly relevant for rapidly developing economies confronting similar challenges.
