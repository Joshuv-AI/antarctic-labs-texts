Asymptotics of high-codimensional area-minimizing currents in hyperbolic space
Xumin Jiang, Jiongduo Xie

Abstract
. We investigate the asymptotic behavior of high-codimensional area-minimizing locally rectifiable currents in hyperbolic space, addressing a problem posed by F.H. Lin [ 23 ] and establishing “boundary regularity at infinity” results for such currents near their asymptotic boundaries under the standard Euclidean metric. Intrinsic obstructions to high-order regularity arise for odd-dimensional minimal surfaces, revealing a constraint dependent on the geometry of the asymptotic boundary. Our work advances the asymptotic theory of high-codimensional minimal surfaces in hyperbolic space.



1.
Introduction

In the upper half-plane model, the
\mathfrak{m}
-dimensional hyperbolic space is given by the set

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}=\bigl\{(x^{\prime},x^{\mathfrak{m}})\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}-1}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\bigr\},\qquad\mathfrak{m}\geqslant 2.

(1.1)

Set
\mathfrak{m}=n+k
, where
n\geqslant 2
and
k\geqslant 1
are natural numbers. Let
\Gamma
be a closed
C^{1,\alpha}
submanifold of dimension
n-1
in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}-1}\times\{0\}
for some constant
\alpha\in(0,1)
. In
[
2
]
, M. Anderson proved that there exists an area-minimizing, locally rectifiable
n
-current
T
, which is complete, without boundary, and asymptotic to
\Gamma
at infinity. See also
[
3
]
.

Hardt and Lin
[
14
]
investigated the hypersurface case (
k=1
) and established the “boundary regularity at infinity” result: for any such hyperbolic-area-minimizing current
T
, the union of the support of
T
(denoted
M
) and
\Gamma
—when endowed with the Euclidean metric—is a finite union of
C^{1,\alpha}
hypersurfaces with boundary
\Gamma
in a neighborhood of
\Gamma
. These hypersurfaces intersect
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\{0\}
orthogonally along
\Gamma
, and all interior singularities of
M
are confined to a bounded region of
\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}
.
Lin
[
24
]
further established a higher-order boundary regularity result: if
\Gamma
is a
C^{l,\alpha}
submanifold for
l=2,3,\dots,n
, then
M\cup\Gamma
is a
C^{l,\alpha}
smooth hypersurface with boundary
\Gamma
near
\Gamma
(in the Euclidean metric). See also
[
27
]
and
[
25
]
.

If
\Gamma
is a
C^{l,\alpha}
submanifold for some integer
l\geqslant n+1
, then there exists a geometric obstruction that prevents
M\cup\Gamma
from being a
C^{l,\alpha}
hypersurface with boundary
\Gamma
in a neighborhood of
\Gamma
. As a prototypical example, when
n=3
, the union
M\cup\Gamma
is a
C^{\infty}
hypersurface of dimension
3
with boundary
\Gamma
near
\Gamma
if and only if
\Gamma
is a Willmore surface. By definition, this requires the mean curvature
H
and the Gaussian curvature
K
of
\Gamma
to satisfy the Willmore equation

\displaystyle\Delta H+2H(H^{2}-K)=0.

(1.2)

Han and Jiang
[
8
]
analyzed such geometric obstructions to high-order regularity and established several associated regularity results.
In a subsequent work, Han and Jiang
[
10
]
proved a convergence theorem under the additional assumption that
\Gamma
is analytic. For related results, we refer the reader to
[
6
,
9
,
13
,
15
,
17
]
. An important application of such precise asymptotic behavior lies in the gluing program developed by Fu, Hein and Jiang
[
7
]
. See also
[
16
]
.

In this paper, we focus on the high codimension setting, namely
k\geqslant 2
. Federer
[
4
]
constructed explicit examples showing that the interior of minimal surfaces in this regime admits codimension-two singularities. A canonical illustration is provided by complex submanifolds in
\mathbb{C}^{2n}
: these objects are all locally absolutely area-minimizing minimal surfaces, whose singularities consist precisely of real codimension-two branching points.
By the classical interior regularity theory established by F. Almgren
[
1
]
, the support of any hyperbolic-area-minimizing locally rectifiable
n
-current is a relatively closed subset of
\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}
, and forms a real analytic submanifold off a relatively closed singular set whose Hausdorff dimension is bounded above by
n-2
.
Lin
[
23
]
established the existence theorem for area-minimizing locally rectifiable
n
-currents and area-minimizing flat chains modulo
p
with
p\geqslant 2
in hyperbolic space. In the same work, Lin derived the “boundary regularity at infinity” result for area-minimizing flat chains modulo
2
.

The primary aim of this work is to resolve the open problems raised by Lin in
[
23
]
regarding whether
\operatorname{supp}(T)\cup\Gamma
is smooth near
\Gamma
in the Euclidean metric. In particular, Lin
[
23
]
formulated the following assumption.

Assumption 1.1
.

Let
T
be an area-minimizing locally rectifiable
n
-current in
\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}
. Assume that
T
is a normal current with respect to the standard Euclidean metric, and satisfies
\partial T=[\Gamma]
.

Under the foregoing assumption, we provide a positive solution to Problem 3 posed by Lin in
[
23
]
.

Theorem 1.2

(From locally rectifiable to
C^{1,\alpha}
regularity)
.

Let
T
be an area-minimizing locally rectifiable
n
-current in
\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}
, and let
\Gamma
be a closed

\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}-1}\times\{0\}

, for some

. Assume that

is a normal current with respect to the standard Euclidean metric and satisfies

. Then there exists a constant
\rho_{\Gamma}>0
such that, in the Euclidean metric, the restricted current

T\mathbin{\llcorner}\bigl\{(x^{\prime},x^{\mathfrak{m}})\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}:x^{\mathfrak{m}}<\rho_{\Gamma}\bigr\}

n
-dimensional

\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}
up to the boundary
\Gamma
.

Moreover, at every point

, the Euclidean tangent plane of

at

is vertical, meaning that it is orthogonal to the hyperplane
\{x^{\mathfrak{m}}=0\}
.

We also establish a local version of Theorem
1.2
. For any point
Q\in\Gamma
, let
T_{Q}\Gamma
denote the
n
-dimensional tangent plane of
\Gamma
at

, where

\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}-1}\times\{0\}

\displaystyle H^{+}=\bigl\{(x^{\prime},x^{\mathfrak{m}})\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}:(x^{\prime},0)\in T_{Q}\Gamma,\ x^{\mathfrak{m}}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\bigr\}.

(1.3)

In Assumption
1.3
below, we normalize coordinates by setting

\displaystyle Q=(\mathbf{0},0),

(1.4)

and fix the associated half-plane
H^{+}
. To be precise, we impose the coordinate condition on

that

\displaystyle x^{n}=x^{n+1}=\cdots=x^{\mathfrak{m}-1}=0.

(1.5)

For any
R>0

\displaystyle G_{R}

\displaystyle=\bigl\{(x^{\prime},x^{\mathfrak{m}})\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}:|x^{\prime}|<R,\ 0<x^{\mathfrak{m}}<R\bigr\},

(1.6)

and the corresponding restricted domain in

by

\displaystyle B_{R}^{+}

\displaystyle=G_{R}\cap H^{+}.

(1.7)

Assumption 1.3
.

Let
T
be an area-minimizing locally rectifiable
n
-current in
\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}
that is asymptotic to
\Gamma
, which is a closed
(n-1)
-dimensional
C^{1}
submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}-1}\times\{0\}
and

\displaystyle\Gamma\cap\overline{G_{R}}\in C^{1,\alpha},

(1.8)

for some
0<\alpha\leqslant 1
.
Let
H^{+},G_{R},B_{R}^{+}
be defined as in (
1.3
), (
1.6
), and (
1.7
), respectively, with the coordinate condition (
1.5
) satisfied on
H^{+}
. There exists a constant
R>0
such that the following hold:

(1) For a single
r\in(0,R)
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Proj}\bigl(T\mathbin{\llcorner}G_{r}\bigr)=[B^{+}_{r}],

(1.9)

where
\operatorname{Proj}\bigl(T\mathbin{\llcorner}G_{r}\bigr)
denotes the Euclidean orthogonal projection of

onto
H^{+}
;

(2) For a fixed small constant
c_{0}=c_{0}(\mathfrak{m},\Gamma)>0
, and for all
P\in G_{R}
, the hyperbolic mass satisfies

\displaystyle M_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}\bigl(T\mathbin{\llcorner}B_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(P,2)\bigr)<e^{c_{0}\bigl(x^{\mathfrak{m}}(P)\bigr)^{-\alpha}}.

(1.10)

We note that Assumption
1.3
is weaker than Assumption
1.1
. By the Constancy Theorem (4.1.7,
[
4
]
), there exists a small constant
\rho_{\Gamma}>0
depending on

such that the validity of (

) for a single

is equivalent to its validity for all

. Equation (

) enforces the multiplicity one condition; this serves to exclude singularities, such as branch points, which occur in higher multiplicity scenarios.

As

decays exponentially with respect to the geodesic distance function on
\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}
as the latter tends to infinity, the term

\displaystyle e^{c_{0}\bigl(x^{\mathfrak{m}}(\cdot)\bigr)^{-\alpha}}

(1.11)

) grows

with respect to the geodesic distance function on
\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}
. A key insight in our work is that the doubly exponential mass growth condition implies a uniform upper bound for the local mass near the asymptotic boundary.

Theorem 1.4
.

There exists a constant
\rho_{\Gamma}>0
such that if Assumption
1.3
holds for some
R\in(0,\rho_{\Gamma}]
, then for any
r\in(0,R)
,
\operatorname{supp}(T)\cap G_{r}
is an
n
-dimensional analytic submanifold of
G_{r}
in the Euclidean metric. This submanifold extends continuously to
\Gamma\cap\overline{G_{r}}
and is of class
C^{1,\alpha}
up to this boundary.

Moreover, at every point
P\in\Gamma\cap\overline{G_{r}}
, the Euclidean tangent plane of
T
at
P
is vertical, meaning it is orthogonal to the hyperplane
\{x^{\mathfrak{m}}=0\}
.

We next introduce the corresponding system of equations and proceed to investigate its regularity theory; in general, however, the analysis of such regularity theory poses substantial challenges owing to the absence of a maximum principle.
Examples demonstrate that solutions of the system of minimal surface equations may fail to exist, be non-unique, or lack stability; for instance, even when the domain is a 4-dimensional ball and the boundary values are analytic, Lipschitz solutions to the minimal surface system generally do not exist. While Lawson and Osserman
[
22
]
established the existence of solutions to the minimal surface system when the domain is 2-dimensional, they also constructed examples showing that such solutions are generally neither unique nor stable.
Within the hyperbolic setting, we have rigorously derived an elegant system of equations governing minimal surfaces near
\Gamma
, which we present as follows.

Locally in a neighborhood of
Q\in\Gamma
, we introduce the Euclidean coordinate chart

\displaystyle y=(y^{\prime},y^{n})=(y^{1},\cdots,y^{n-1},y^{n})=(x^{1},\cdots,x^{n-1},x^{\mathfrak{m}})

(1.12)

on
H^{+}
. By virtue of (
1.4
),
Q
corresponds to the origin in the
y
-coordinate system.

Lemma 1.5
.

Let
T
be an area-minimizing locally rectifiable current in
\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}
. Suppose that near
Q\in\Gamma
,
T
can be represented as the graph of a
C^{1}
mapping

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(y)=\bigl(u_{1}(y),\cdots,u_{\mathfrak{m}-n}(y)\bigr)

(1.13)

on some domain
\Omega\subseteq H^{+}
. Then
\mathbf{u}
is real-analytic and satisfies the system of equations

\displaystyle g^{ij}\frac{\partial^{2}u_{s}}{\partial y^{i}\partial y^{j}}-\frac{n}{y^{n}}\frac{\partial u_{s}}{\partial y^{n}}=0\quad\text{in }\Omega,

(1.14)

for
s=1,\cdots,\mathfrak{m}-n
. Here, the coefficients
g_{ij}
are defined by

\displaystyle g_{ij}:=\delta_{ij}+\sum_{l=1}^{\mathfrak{m}-n}\frac{\partial u_{l}}{\partial y^{i}}\frac{\partial u_{l}}{\partial y^{j}},

(1.15)

and
(g^{ij})
denotes the inverse matrix of
(g_{ij})
.

The regularity analysis of
\mathbf{u}
yields the following theorems, which settle the PDE aspects of the asymptotics for area-minimizing locally rectifiable currents in hyperbolic space.

Theorem 1.6

(From
C^{1,\alpha}
to
C^{n,\alpha}
)
.

There exists a constant
\rho_{\Gamma}>0
such that if Assumption
1.3
holds and

\displaystyle\Gamma\cap\overline{G_{R}}\in C^{n,\alpha},

(1.16)

for some
R\in(0,\rho_{\Gamma}]
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, then for any
r\in(0,R)
,
\operatorname{supp}(T)\cap G_{r}
is an
n
-dimensional analytic submanifold of
G_{r}
in the Euclidean metric. This submanifold extends continuously to
\Gamma\cap\overline{G_{r}}
and is of class
C^{n,\alpha}
up to this boundary.

Theorem 1.7

(Boundary Regularity Theorem I)
.

There exists a constant
\rho_{\Gamma}>0
such that if Assumption
1.3
holds and

\displaystyle\Gamma\cap\overline{G_{R}}\in C^{\infty},

(1.17)

for some
R\in(0,\rho_{\Gamma}]
, then for any
r\in(0,R)
,
\operatorname{supp}(T)\cap G_{r}
is the graph of an analytic
(\mathfrak{m}-n)
-valued function
\mathbf{u}
defined on
B^{+}_{r}
in the Euclidean metric. Moreover,
\mathbf{u}
can be regarded as a smooth function of

,

, and

on the closed domain

\displaystyle\bigl\{(y^{\prime},y^{n},y^{n}\log(y^{n})):|y^{\prime}|\leqslant r,\ 0\leqslant y^{n}\leqslant r,\ 0\leqslant y^{n}|\log(y^{n})|\leqslant r\bigr\}.

(1.18)

If in addition

\mathbf{u}
is of class
C^{\infty}
in

on
\overline{B^{+}_{r}}
.

By Theorem

with respect to

,

, and

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=\varphi(y^{\prime})+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\mathbf{c}_{i}(y^{\prime})(y^{n})^{i}+\sum_{i=n+1}^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{i-1}{n}\rfloor}\mathbf{c}_{i,j}(y^{\prime})(y^{n})^{i}(\log(y^{n}))^{j}+\mathbf{R}_{k},

2.
Preliminary

In this paper, we say that
T
is an area-minimizing locally rectifiable
n
-current in
\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}
if, for every bounded domain
U\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}
, the restricted current
T\,\mathbin{\llcorner}\,U
is absolutely area-minimizing. Precisely, for any rectifiable current
S
in
\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}
satisfying
\partial S=\partial(T\,\mathbin{\llcorner}\,U)
, the mass inequality

\displaystyle M(T\mathbin{\llcorner}U)\leqslant M(S)

(2.1)

holds.

Throughout this paper, we make the standing assumption that
\Gamma
is an oriented closed
C^{1,\alpha}
submanifold of the hyperplane
\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}-1}\times\{0\}
at infinity, for some
0<\alpha\leqslant 1
. We say that
T
is asymptotic to
\Gamma
if
\partial T=0
in the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}
, and the boundary of
\operatorname{supp}(T)
coincides with
\Gamma
in the Euclidean metric.

Let
\nu_{\Gamma}
denote a unit normal vector field on
\Gamma
in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}-1}
. For any
Q\in\Gamma
and
r>0
, we define

\displaystyle\delta(Q,r)=\min\bigl\{\operatorname{dist}\bigl(Q+r\nu_{\Gamma}(Q),\Gamma\bigr),\operatorname{dist}\bigl(Q-r\nu_{\Gamma}(Q),\Gamma\bigr)\bigr\}.

(2.2)

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}-1}\times\{0\}
, we set

\displaystyle d(x)=\operatorname{dist}(x,\Gamma).

(2.3)

The minimal surface
\operatorname{supp}(T)
is contained in the set

\displaystyle W:=\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}-1}\times(0,\infty)\setminus\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(x)>0,\\
x^{\mathfrak{m}}=0\end{subarray}}B_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(x,d(x)).

(2.4)

For a multi-valued function
\mathbf{w}(x)=\bigl(\dots,w_{s}(x),\dots\bigr)
, we adopt the notation

\displaystyle w_{s,i}=\frac{\partial w_{s}}{\partial x^{i}},\quad w_{s,ij}=\frac{\partial^{2}w_{s}}{\partial x^{i}\partial x^{j}}.

(2.5)

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be of class
C^{1,\alpha}
for some
0<\alpha\leqslant 1
and let
W
be defined as in (
2.4
). Then there exists a small constant
\rho_{\Gamma}>0
such that for any fixed
Q\in\Gamma
, if

\displaystyle x=(x^{\prime},x^{\mathfrak{m}})\in W\cap\{x^{\mathfrak{m}}<\rho_{\Gamma}\}

(2.6)

and

\displaystyle(x^{\prime},0)=Q+r\nu_{\Gamma}(Q)

(2.7)

for some
r>0
and some unit normal vector
\nu_{\Gamma}(Q)
to
\Gamma
at
Q
, then

\displaystyle r<C(x^{\mathfrak{m}})^{1+\alpha},

(2.8)

where
C=C(\mathfrak{m},n,\Gamma)
is a constant independent of
Q
and
x
.

Proof.

Translate
Q
to the origin and adopt the coordinate conventions from (
1.3
) to (
1.5
). By a suitable rotation of coordinates, we may set

\displaystyle\nu_{\Gamma}(Q)=(x^{1},\dots,x^{n-1},x^{n},x^{n+1},\dots,x^{\mathfrak{m}})=(0,\dots,0,1,0,\dots,0).

(2.9)

For any point
P\in\Gamma
of the form

\displaystyle(\tilde{x},\varphi(\tilde{x}),0):=(x^{1},\dots,x^{n-1},\varphi(x^{1},\dots,x^{n-1}),0),

(2.10)

the Euclidean distance between
s\nu_{\Gamma}(Q)
and
P
is given by

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}(s\nu_{\Gamma}(Q),P)=\sqrt{|\tilde{x}|^{2}+(\varphi_{1}(\tilde{x})-s)^{2}+\sum_{\beta=2}^{\mathfrak{m}-n}\varphi_{\beta}^{2}(\tilde{x})},

(2.11)

where
\varphi_{i}
denotes the
i
-th component of
\varphi
. At a minimizer of this distance, we have for all
i=1,\dots,n-1
that

\displaystyle x_{i}+(\varphi_{1}-s)\varphi_{1,i}+\sum_{\beta=2}^{\mathfrak{m}-n}\varphi_{\beta}\varphi_{\beta,i}=0.

(2.12)

Trivially,

\displaystyle\min_{P\in\Gamma}\operatorname{dist}(s\nu_{\Gamma}(Q),P)\leqslant s.

(2.13)

There exists a small constant
\delta=\delta(\Gamma)>0
such that for all
s\in(0,\delta]
, the distance between
s\nu_{\Gamma}(Q)
and
\Gamma
is attained by
\operatorname{dist}(s\nu_{\Gamma}(Q),P)
for some
P\in\Gamma
of the form (
2.10
) satisfying the critical condition (
2.12
). By the
C^{1,\alpha}
regularity of
\Gamma
, we have the estimates

\displaystyle|\varphi(\tilde{x})|\leqslant C|\tilde{x}|^{1+\alpha},\quad|D\varphi(\tilde{x})|\leqslant C|\tilde{x}|^{\alpha}.

(2.14)

Condition (
2.12
) then implies

\displaystyle|\tilde{x}+(\varphi_{1}(\tilde{x})-s)D\varphi_{1}(\tilde{x})|\leqslant C|\tilde{x}|^{1+2\alpha}.

(2.15)

Combining this with the bound

\displaystyle|\varphi_{1}(\tilde{x})D\varphi_{1}(\tilde{x})|\leqslant C|\tilde{x}|^{1+2\alpha},

(2.16)

we deduce

\displaystyle|\tilde{x}|\leqslant Cs|D\varphi_{1}(\tilde{x})|.

(2.17)

From (
2.14
) and (
2.17
), it follows that

\displaystyle s\geqslant C^{-1}|\tilde{x}|^{1-\alpha}.

(2.18)

In the special case
\alpha=1
, by shrinking
\delta
if necessary, (
2.14
) and (
2.17
) force
\tilde{x}=0
and hence
\operatorname{dist}(s\nu_{\Gamma}(Q),P)=s
.

By the definition of
W
, for each
s>0
we subtract a ball of radius
\operatorname{dist}(s\nu_{\Gamma}(Q),\Gamma)
from the upper half-space. If

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}(s\nu_{\Gamma}(Q),\Gamma)=\operatorname{dist}(s\nu_{\Gamma}(Q),P)\geqslant x^{\mathfrak{m}},

(2.19)

then algebraic manipulation yields

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle<s-\sqrt{\operatorname{dist}(s\nu_{\Gamma}(Q),P)^{2}-(x^{\mathfrak{m}})^{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant s-\sqrt{|\tilde{x}|^{2}+(\varphi_{1}(\tilde{x})-s)^{2}+\sum_{\beta=2}^{\mathfrak{m}-n}\varphi_{\beta}^{2}(\tilde{x})-(x^{\mathfrak{m}})^{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2\varphi_{1}(\tilde{x})s+(x^{\mathfrak{m}})^{2}}{s+\sqrt{|\tilde{x}|^{2}+(\varphi_{1}(\tilde{x})-s)^{2}+\sum_{\beta=2}^{\mathfrak{m}-n}\varphi_{\beta}^{2}(\tilde{x})-(x^{\mathfrak{m}})^{2}}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2\varphi_{1}(\tilde{x})s+(x^{\mathfrak{m}})^{2}}{s}.

(2.20)

For
\alpha=1
, we have
\tilde{x}=0
. Setting
s=\delta
gives

\displaystyle r<\delta^{-1}(x^{\mathfrak{m}})^{2}.

(2.21)

For
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we set

\displaystyle s=(x^{\mathfrak{m}})^{1-\alpha}.

(2.22)

There exists a small constant
\rho_{\Gamma}>0
such that
x^{\mathfrak{m}}\in(0,\rho_{\Gamma}]
implies
s\in(0,\delta]
. By (
2.18
) and (
2.22
), we obtain the key bound

\displaystyle|\tilde{x}|\leqslant Cx^{\mathfrak{m}}.

(2.23)

Combining (
2.14
), (
2.23
), and (
2.20
), we conclude

\displaystyle r<C(x^{\mathfrak{m}})^{1+\alpha}.

(2.24)

Since
\Gamma
is closed, we may enlarge
C
if necessary to ensure it is independent of the choice of
Q\in\Gamma
.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

From the proof of Lemma
2.1
, we deduce that for
\alpha\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\sup_{Q\in\Gamma}\left(1-r^{-1}\delta(Q,r)\right)\leqslant C_{\Gamma}r^{\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}},

(2.25)

or equivalently,

\displaystyle r-C_{\Gamma}r^{\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\leqslant\delta(Q,r)\leqslant r.

(2.26)

If
\alpha=1
, then
\delta(Q,r)=r
. See also Section 1 of
[
14
]
.

3.
From Local Rectifiability to
C^{1,\alpha}
Regularity

For
r>0
, we define the domain

\displaystyle D_{r}=\bigl\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}:(x^{1})^{2}+\cdots+(x^{n})^{2}<r^{2},\ x^{n+1}=\cdots=x^{\mathfrak{m}}=0\bigr\}.

(3.1)

Lemma 3.1

(Mass Bound I)
.

Let
T
be an absolutely area-minimizing rectifiable
n
-current in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}
, and suppose that

\displaystyle\partial T\mathbin{\llcorner}B_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(2)=0,

(3.2)

where
B_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(2)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}
denotes the Euclidean ball of radius
2
centered at the origin. If in addition the following two conditions hold:

(1) there exists a small constant
\delta>0
such that the Gromov–Hausdorff distance between

\displaystyle\operatorname{supp}\bigl(T\mathbin{\llcorner}B_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(2)\bigr)

(3.3)

and
D_{2}
is bounded above by
\delta
;

(2) the projection identity

\displaystyle\operatorname{Proj}(T)\mathbin{\llcorner}D_{2-\delta}=[D_{2-\delta}]

(3.4)

holds, where
\operatorname{Proj}(T)
denotes the orthogonal projection of
T\mathbin{\llcorner}B_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(2)
onto
D_{3}
;

then there exists a positive constant
c_{0}=c_{0}(\mathfrak{m},n)
such that

\displaystyle M_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}\bigl(T\mathbin{\llcorner}B_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(1)\bigr)

\displaystyle\leqslant e^{-\frac{1}{c_{0}\delta}}M_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}\bigl(T\mathbin{\llcorner}B_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(2)\bigr)+C(n).

(3.5)

Proof.

For
r\in[1,2]
, we set

\displaystyle f(r)=M(T_{r}):=M\bigl(T\mathbin{\llcorner}B_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(r)\bigr).

(3.6)

For almost every
r\in[1,2]
, the boundary current
\partial\bigl(T\mathbin{\llcorner}B_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(r)\bigr)
is rectifiable. For each fixed such
r
, the support of
\partial\bigl(T\mathbin{\llcorner}B_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(r)\bigr)
is contained in the union of a countable collection of
(n-1)
-dimensional Lipschitz submanifolds
\{S_{i}\}_{i\geqslant 1}
and a set
S_{0}
of
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
-measure zero. In addition, by partitioning the support into sufficiently fine pieces
\{S_{i}\}
(modulo a set of
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
-measure zero), we ensure the multiplicity is constant on each
S_{i}
, and also

\displaystyle M\bigl(\partial\bigl(T\mathbin{\llcorner}B_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(r)\bigr)\bigr)=\sum_{i}\theta_{i}\cdot\operatorname{Area}(S_{i}),

(3.7)

where
\theta_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
is the constant multiplicity of the current restricted to
S_{i}
.
For each
S_{i}
, we connect points on
S_{i}
to the flat disk
D_{r-\delta}
via shortest line segments. This construction defines a rectifiable
n
-current
R
satisfying

\displaystyle R\mathbin{\llcorner}D_{r-\delta}=[D_{r-\delta}],\,\,\partial R=\partial\bigl(T_{r}).

(3.8)

Moreover, there exists a positive constant
c_{0}>0
such that

\displaystyle M(T_{r})\leqslant M(R)\leqslant c_{0}\delta M(\partial T_{r})+C(n),

(3.9)

where
C(n)
denotes the mass of
D_{2}
. Following the argument in the proof of Lemma 1.3 in
[
23
]
, slicing theory—with
u(r)
denoting the distance function to the center of
D_{2}
—yields

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(r)\geqslant M\langle T_{r},u,r^{+}\rangle=M(\partial T_{r}).

(3.10)

This implies the differential inequality

\displaystyle f(r)\leqslant c_{0}\delta f^{\prime}(r)+C(n),

(3.11)

from which we deduce that for all
r\in[1,2]
,

\displaystyle f(r)\leqslant e^{\frac{r-2}{c_{0}\delta}}f(2)+C(n).

(3.12)

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

The next is a hyperbolic version of the above lemma.

Lemma 3.2

(Mass bound II)
.

Let
H^{+}
be defined as in (
1.3
) and let
\{\tilde{D}_{r}\}
be a family of
n
-dimensional hyperbolic disks of radius
r>0
in
H^{+}
, sharing a common center
P
. Let
T
be an area-minimizing locally rectifiable
n
-current in
\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}
, and suppose that

\displaystyle x^{\mathfrak{m}}(P)=1,\quad\partial T\mathbin{\llcorner}B_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(P,2)=0.

(3.13)

If in addition the following hold:

(1) there exists a small constant
\delta>0
such that the Gromov–Hausdorff distance between

\displaystyle\operatorname{supp}\bigl(T\mathbin{\llcorner}B_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(P,2)\bigr)

(3.14)

and
\tilde{D}_{2}
is bounded by
\delta
;

(2)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Proj}(T)\mathbin{\llcorner}\tilde{D}_{2-\delta}=[\tilde{D}_{2-\delta}],

(3.15)

where
\operatorname{Proj}(T)
denotes the Euclidean orthogonal projection of
T\mathbin{\llcorner}B_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(P,2)
onto
H^{+}
;

then there exists a positive constant
c_{0}=c_{0}(\mathfrak{m},n)
such that

\displaystyle M_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}\bigl(T\mathbin{\llcorner}B_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(P,1)\bigr)

\displaystyle\leqslant e^{-\frac{1}{c_{0}\delta}}M_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}\bigl(T\mathbin{\llcorner}B_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(P,2)\bigr)+C(n).

(3.16)

Proof.

According to the proof of Lemma 1.3 in Lin
[
23
]
, a hyperbolic area may be described by the parametric integrand

\displaystyle\Phi(x,\xi)=|x^{\mathfrak{m}}|^{-n}|\xi|.

(3.17)

In
B_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(P,2)
, this integrand is (with respect to the Euclidean metric) elliptic with an ellipticity bound
e^{-2n}
.

Similarly as in the proof of Lemma
3.1
, we construct a rectifiable
n
-current
R
, whose Euclidean and hyperbolic masses are bounded by
c_{0}\delta M(\partial(T\,\Large\llcorner\normalsize\,B_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(P,r)))+C(n)
.
The rest of the proof is the same as that of Lemma
3.1
.
∎

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\Gamma
be a
C^{1,\alpha}
submanifold of the hyperplane at infinity, let
\rho_{\Gamma}
be defined as in Section
2
and let
P
be a point in
G_{r}
with

\displaystyle r<\tfrac{1}{2}\rho_{\Gamma}.

(3.18)

Assume that
T
is an area-minimizing locally rectifiable
n
-current in
\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}
, and
T
is a normal current in the standard Euclidean metric with
\partial T=[\Gamma]
. Then

\displaystyle M_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}\bigl(T\mathbin{\llcorner}B_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(P,1)\bigr)<C\bigl(M_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(T)+1\bigr),

(3.19)

where the constant
C=C(\mathfrak{m},\Gamma)
is independent of the choice of
P
.

Proof.

Let
\rho_{\Gamma}
be defined as in Section
2
. We prove the theorem without applying Anderson’s monotonicity theorem in
[
2
]
.

First, we establish (
3.15
). Consider the current
S
represented by the set

\displaystyle\bigl\{(x^{\prime},x^{\mathfrak{m}})\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}:(x^{\prime},0)\in\Gamma,\,x^{\mathfrak{m}}\leqslant 0\bigr\},

(3.20)

satisfying
\partial S=-[\Gamma]
. By our assumptions, we have

\displaystyle\partial(T+S)=0

(3.21)

in the standard Euclidean metric, and for every compact domain
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}
, the restricted current

\displaystyle(T+S)\mathbin{\llcorner}K

(3.22)

is a normal current in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}
. We fix a constant

\displaystyle\varepsilon=\tfrac{1}{2}\rho_{\Gamma}.

(3.23)

For a fixed point
Q\in\Gamma
and the associated half-space
H^{+}
, we define the restricted current

\displaystyle T_{\varepsilon}:=(T+S)\mathbin{\llcorner}B_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(Q,\varepsilon).

(3.24)

By the Constancy Theorem (4.1.7 in
[
4
]
) and the structural property of the set
W
in (
2.4
), the Euclidean orthogonal projection of
T_{\varepsilon}
onto
H^{+}
satisfies

\displaystyle\operatorname{Proj}(T_{\varepsilon})\mathbin{\llcorner}\bigl(B_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(Q,\varepsilon/2)\cap H^{+}\bigr)=k\bigl[B_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(Q,\varepsilon/2)\cap H^{+}\bigr],

(3.25)

for some integer
k\in\mathbb{N}
. It is immediate that
k=1
by the definition of
S
, which yields (
3.15
).

Secondly, we derive a mass bound for
T\mathbin{\llcorner}B_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(P,2)
. It is immediate that

\displaystyle M_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}\bigl(T\mathbin{\llcorner}B_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(P,2)\bigr)\leqslant M_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(T).

(3.26)

Within
B_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(P,2)
, we have
x^{\mathfrak{m}}\geqslant e^{-2}\cdot x^{\mathfrak{m}}(P)
, which implies the estimate

\displaystyle M_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}\bigl(T\mathbin{\llcorner}B_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(P,2)\bigr)\leqslant e^{2n}\cdot\bigl(x^{\mathfrak{m}}(P)\bigr)^{-n}M_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(T).

(3.27)

Lastly, we set

\displaystyle\tilde{\delta}=\bigl(e^{2}x^{\mathfrak{m}}(P)\bigr)^{1+\alpha}.

(3.28)

Then by (
2.8
), the Gromov–Hausdorff distance between the support of
T\mathbin{\llcorner}B_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(P,2)
and
\tilde{D}_{2}
is bounded by
\tilde{\delta}
. We define an isometric map
\Phi
by

\displaystyle\Phi(x^{\prime},x^{\mathfrak{m}})=\bigl(x^{\mathfrak{m}}(P)\bigr)^{-1}\bigl(x^{\prime}-x^{\prime}(P),x^{\mathfrak{m}}\bigr),

(3.29)

which maps
B_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(P,2)
bijectively onto
B_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(\Phi(P),2)
with
x^{\mathfrak{m}}(\Phi(P))=1
. We deduce that the Gromov–Hausdorff distance between the support of
(\Phi_{\#}T)\mathbin{\llcorner}B_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(\Phi(P),2)
and
\Phi_{\#}\tilde{D}_{2}
is bounded by

\displaystyle\delta=e^{2+2\alpha}\bigl(x^{\mathfrak{m}}(P)\bigr)^{\alpha}.

(3.30)

Therefore, by Lemma
3.2
, together with (
3.27
) and (
3.30
), we obtain the chain of inequalities

\displaystyle M_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}\bigl(T\mathbin{\llcorner}B_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(P,1)\bigr)

\displaystyle=M_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}\bigl((\Phi_{\#}T)\mathbin{\llcorner}B_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(\Phi(P),1)\bigr)

\displaystyle\leqslant e^{-\frac{1}{c_{0}\delta}}M_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}\bigl((\Phi_{\#}T)\mathbin{\llcorner}B_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(\Phi(P),2)\bigr)+C(\mathfrak{m})

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{1}{c_{0}\delta}}M_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}\bigl(T\mathbin{\llcorner}B_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(P,2)\bigr)+C(\mathfrak{m})

\displaystyle\leqslant e^{-\frac{1}{c_{0}\delta}}e^{2n}\cdot\bigl(x^{\mathfrak{m}}(P)\bigr)^{-n}M_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(T)+C(\mathfrak{m})

\displaystyle\leqslant C\bigl(M_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}(T)+1\bigr),

(3.31)

where
C
in (
3.31
) depends on
\mathfrak{m}
and
\Gamma
.
∎

4.
System of Minimal Surface Equations

Let
Q\in\Gamma
and let
H^{+}
be defined as in (
1.3
). Without loss of generality, we assume that for all
(x^{\prime},x^{\mathfrak{m}})\in H^{+}
,

\displaystyle x^{i}=0\quad\text{if}\quad n\leqslant i<\mathfrak{m}.

(4.1)

We define the domain
G_{R}
as in
(
1.6
).

By Theorem
1.4
, the area-minimizing rectifiable current
T
admits a graphical representation near
Q
by the vector-valued function
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\cdots,u_{\mathfrak{m}-n}):G_{r}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}-n}
, in the form

\displaystyle(x^{1},\cdots,x^{n-1},x^{\mathfrak{m}})\mapsto\bigl(x^{1},\cdots,x^{n-1},u_{1},\cdots,u_{\mathfrak{m}-n},x^{\mathfrak{m}}\bigr).

(4.2)

Setting
y^{1}=x^{1},\cdots,y^{n-1}=x^{n-1},y^{n}=x^{\mathfrak{m}}
as in (
1.12
), we may rewrite this graph mapping as

\displaystyle(y^{1},\cdots,y^{n})\mapsto\bigl(y^{1},\cdots,y^{n},u_{1},\cdots,u_{\mathfrak{m}-n}\bigr).

(4.3)

For the remainder of this section, we adopt the coordinate system
y
in place of
x
.

Let
\mathbf{e}_{i}
denote the
i
-th standard basis vector of
H^{+}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}
. The corresponding
i
-th tangent vector to the graph of
\mathbf{u}
is given by

\displaystyle(\mathbf{e}_{i},\mathbf{u}_{,i}):=\bigl(\mathbf{e}_{i},(u_{1})_{i},\cdots,(u_{\mathfrak{m}-n})_{i}\bigr).

(4.4)

The graph of
\mathbf{u}
admits
\mathfrak{m}-n
distinct normal vectors, which take the form

\displaystyle\nu_{s}=\bigl(D_{y}u_{s},-\mathbf{e}_{s}\bigr)=\bigl((u_{s})_{1},\cdots,(u_{s})_{n},-\mathbf{e}_{s}\bigr),

(4.5)

for
s=1,\cdots,\mathfrak{m}-n
.

We define the graph mapping

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}(y)=(y,\mathbf{u}(y)).

(4.6)

For convenience, we extend the coordinate
y
on
H^{+}
to a system of Euclidean coordinates in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}
, where

\displaystyle y^{n+1}=x^{n},\quad y^{n+2}=x^{n+1},\quad\cdots,\quad y^{\mathfrak{m}}=x^{\mathfrak{m}-1}.

(4.7)

Then, for indices satisfying
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
and
n+1\leqslant l\leqslant\mathfrak{m}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{i}=y^{i},\quad\mathbf{F}_{l}=\mathbf{u}_{l}.

(4.8)

Let
M
denote the graph of
\mathbf{u}
(i.e.,
M=\mathbf{F}(G_{r})
). The mean curvature of
M
with respect to the normal vector
\nu_{s}
is given by

\displaystyle H_{s}=g^{ij}_{M}\bigl\langle\mathbf{F}_{,ij},\nu_{s}\bigr\rangle_{g_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}}=\bigl\langle\Delta_{g_{M}}\mathbf{F},\nu_{s}\bigr\rangle_{g_{\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}}}.

(4.9)

If all mean curvatures
H_{s}
vanish, then
\Delta_{g_{M}}\mathbf{F}=0
. To establish this, we start with the covariant derivative expansion of
\mathbf{F}_{,ij}
: for
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n,

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{,ij}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\mathbf{F}_{*}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{i}}}\left(\mathbf{F}_{*}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{j}}\right)

(4.10)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\frac{\partial\mathbf{F}_{l}}{\partial y^{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{l}}}\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{F}_{k}}{\partial y^{j}}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{k}}\right)

(4.11)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}\mathbf{F}_{k}}{\partial y^{i}\partial y^{j}}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{k}}+\frac{\partial\mathbf{F}_{l}}{\partial y^{i}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{F}_{k}}{\partial y^{j}}\cdot\nabla_{\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{l}}}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{k}},

(4.12)

where
\nabla
denotes the Levi-Civita connection of
\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}
.
Contracting with the inverse metric
g_{M}^{ij}
yields

\displaystyle\Delta_{g_{M}}\mathbf{F}=g_{M}^{ij}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}\mathbf{F}_{k}}{\partial y^{i}\partial y^{j}}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{k}}+\frac{\partial\mathbf{F}_{l}}{\partial y^{i}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{F}_{k}}{\partial y^{j}}\cdot\nabla_{\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{l}}}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{k}}\right).

(4.13)

Recall that
y^{n}
is the height coordinate in the hyperbolic space; the connection coefficients satisfy the explicit formula

\displaystyle\nabla_{\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{l}}}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{k}}=-\frac{1}{y^{n}}\left(\delta_{ln}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{k}}+\delta_{kn}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{l}}-\delta_{lk}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{n}}\right).

(4.14)

We compute the quadratic term in the Laplacian expansion. For simplicity, we suppress the Einstein summation convention in this calculation, and obtain

g_{M}^{ij}\frac{\partial\mathbf{F}_{l}}{\partial y^{i}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{F}_{k}}{\partial y^{j}}\cdot\nabla_{\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{l}}}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{k}}=\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{y^{n}}g_{M}^{lk}\left(\delta_{ln}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{k}}+\delta_{kn}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{l}}-\delta_{lk}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{n}}\right),&1\leqslant l,k\leqslant n;\\
-\frac{1}{y^{n}}\frac{\partial u_{l-n}}{\partial y^{i}}\cdot\delta_{kn}g_{M}^{ik}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{l}},&n+1\leqslant l\leqslant\mathfrak{m}\text{ and }1\leqslant k\leqslant n;\\
\frac{1}{y^{n}}\frac{\partial u_{l-n}}{\partial y^{i}}\frac{\partial u_{k-n}}{\partial y^{j}}\cdot\delta_{lk}g_{M}^{ij}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{n}},&n+1\leqslant l,k\leqslant\mathfrak{m}.\end{cases}

(4.15)

Combining (
4.5
), (
4.9
), and (
4.15
), together with the metric representation

\displaystyle g_{ij}^{M}=(y^{n})^{-2}\left(\delta_{ij}+\sum_{l=1}^{\mathfrak{m}-n}\frac{\partial u_{l}}{\partial y^{i}}\frac{\partial u_{l}}{\partial y^{j}}\right),

(4.16)

the vanishing mean curvature condition
H_{s}=0
implies the following system of PDEs. Defining the rescaled metric

g_{ij}=(y^{n})^{2}g_{ij}^{M},

which coincides with the metric defined in (
1.15
), we have

\displaystyle g^{ij}\frac{\partial^{2}u_{s}}{\partial y^{i}\partial y^{j}}-\frac{n}{y^{n}}\frac{\partial u_{s}}{\partial y^{n}}=0,

(4.17)

for
s=1,\cdots,\mathfrak{m}-n
. This is the primary system of minimal surface equations investigated in the present paper. In summary, we have established Lemma
1.5
as well as the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

If the graph of
\mathbf{u}\in C^{1}
in the form given by (
4.3
), defined over a domain
\Omega\subseteq H^{+}
, is an
n
-dimensional minimal submanifold of
\mathbb{H}^{\mathfrak{m}}
(i.e., all mean curvatures vanish), then the PDE system (
4.17
) holds.

We may also derive the system (
4.17
) via the method of variations.
Consider an arbitrary smooth map defined on
G_{r}
, given in the form

\displaystyle\Phi:y\mapsto(\phi(y),\eta(y)),

(4.18)

where
\phi=(\phi_{1},\cdots,\phi_{n})
and
\eta=(\eta_{1},\cdots,\eta_{\mathfrak{m}-n})
. For the graph mapping
\mathbf{F}
defined in (
4.6
), we write the induced metric on the graph of
\mathbf{u}
as

\displaystyle h_{ij}=g_{ij}/(y^{n})^{2},

(4.19)

where
(g_{ij})
is given by (
1.15
).
Let
g=\det(g_{ij})
and
h=\det(h_{ij})
denote the determinants of the respective metric tensors. Then, for any compact domain
\Omega\subseteq G_{r}
, the area functional of the graph is given by

\displaystyle A(\mathbf{F}):=\mathrm{Area}(\mathbf{F}|_{\Omega})=\int_{\Omega}\sqrt{h}\,dy=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{(y^{n})^{n}}\sqrt{g}\,dy.

(4.20)

For simplicity, we suppress the volume element
dy
in the calculation and compute the first variation of the area functional:

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}A(\mathbf{F}+t\Phi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\frac{1}{(y^{n}+t\phi_{n})^{n}}\sqrt{\det\left(\left\langle(\mathbf{F}+t\Phi)_{y^{i}},(\mathbf{F}+t\Phi)_{y^{j}}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}\right)}

(4.21)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\frac{n}{(y^{n})^{n+1}}\phi_{n}\sqrt{g}+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{(y^{n})^{n}}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{g}}\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\det\left(\left\langle(\mathbf{F}+t\Phi)_{y^{i}},(\mathbf{F}+t\Phi)_{y^{j}}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\frac{n}{(y^{n})^{n+1}}\phi_{n}\sqrt{g}+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{(y^{n})^{n}}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{g}}\left(\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\left\langle(\mathbf{F}+t\Phi)_{y^{i}},(\mathbf{F}+t\Phi)_{y^{j}}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}\right)gg^{ij}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\frac{n}{(y^{n})^{n+1}}\phi_{n}\sqrt{g}+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{(y^{n})^{n}}\sqrt{g}g^{ij}\left\langle\mathbf{F}_{y^{j}},\Phi_{y^{i}}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}.

Next, we select suitable test functions. Let
\zeta\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
and set

\displaystyle\zeta_{i}=\frac{\partial\zeta}{\partial y^{i}}.

(4.22)

We denote by
\mathbf{e}_{i}
the standard basis vector of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}
whose
i
-th component is
1
and all other components are
0
.

•

Take

\displaystyle\phi(y)=\zeta(y)\mathbf{e}_{\alpha},\quad\eta(y)=\mathbf{0},

(4.23)

for
\alpha=1,\cdots,n-1
. Substituting this into the first variation formula yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{(y^{n})^{n}}\zeta_{i}\delta_{j\alpha}\sqrt{g}\,g^{ij}=0.

(4.24)

Hence, we deduce the identity

\left(\frac{1}{(y^{n})^{n}}\sqrt{g}\,g^{i\alpha}\right)_{,i}=0\quad\text{for }\alpha=1,\cdots,n-1.

(4.25)

•

Take

\displaystyle\phi(y)=\zeta(y)\mathbf{e}_{n},\quad\eta(y)=\mathbf{0}.

(4.26)

Substituting this test function into the variation formula gives

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\frac{n}{(y^{n})^{n+1}}\zeta\sqrt{g}+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{(y^{n})^{n}}\zeta_{i}\delta_{jn}\sqrt{g}\,g^{ij}=0.

(4.27)

Hence, we obtain the divergence identity

\left(\frac{1}{(y^{n})^{n}}\sqrt{g}\,g^{in}\right)_{,i}=-\frac{n}{(y^{n})^{n+1}}\sqrt{g}.

(4.28)

•

Take

\displaystyle\phi(y)=\mathbf{0},\quad\eta(y)=\zeta(y)\mathbf{e}_{s},

(4.29)

for
s=1,\cdots,\mathfrak{m}-n
. This leads to the integral identity

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{(y^{n})^{n}}\sqrt{g}\,g^{ij}(u_{s})_{,j}\zeta_{,i}=0.

(4.30)

Combining the identities (
4.25
), (
4.28
), and (
4.30
), we compute the divergence of the relevant expression:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{(y^{n})^{n}}\sqrt{g}\,g^{ij}(u_{s})_{,j}\right)_{,i}

(4.31)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{(y^{n})^{n}}\sqrt{g}\,g^{ij}\right)_{,i}(u_{s})_{,j}+\frac{1}{(y^{n})^{n}}\sqrt{g}\,g^{ij}(u_{s})_{,ij}

\displaystyle=-\frac{n}{(y^{n})^{n+1}}\sqrt{g}\,(u_{s})_{,n}+\frac{1}{(y^{n})^{n}}\sqrt{g}\,g^{ij}(u_{s})_{,ij}.

Dividing both sides by
\frac{1}{(y^{n})^{n}}\sqrt{g}
(which is non-vanishing), we conclude that

g^{ij}(u_{s})_{,ij}-\frac{n}{y^{n}}(u_{s})_{,n}=0,\quad\text{for }s=1,\cdots,\mathfrak{m}-n,

(4.32)

which coincides with the minimal surface system (
4.17
).

5.
Higher Order Regularity Theorems

We investigate the minimal surface system (
4.17
), where the vector-valued function
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\cdots,u_{\mathfrak{m}-n})
is defined on the domain
B^{+}_{r}
specified by (
1.7
). The matrix
(g^{ij})
appearing in the system denotes the inverse of the metric tensor

\displaystyle g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+\sum_{l=1}^{\mathfrak{m}-n}\frac{\partial u_{l}}{\partial y^{i}}\frac{\partial u_{l}}{\partial y^{j}}.

(5.1)

Theorem
1.4
guarantees that the solution
\mathbf{u}
is of class
C^{1,\alpha}
up to the boundary. We introduce the tangential domain

\displaystyle B_{r}^{\prime}=\{y^{\prime}=(y^{1},\cdots,y^{n-1}):|y^{\prime}|<r\}.

(5.2)

By virtue of the coordinate transformations (
1.4
)–(
1.5
), we may impose the following normalized boundary conditions:

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(y^{\prime},0)=\varphi(y^{\prime}),\quad\text{for }y^{\prime}\in B_{r}^{\prime},

(5.3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{0},0)=\varphi(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0},\quad D\varphi({0})=\mathbf{0},

(5.4)

where
\varphi
is the
(\mathfrak{m}-n)
-valued local defining function of the boundary manifold
\Gamma
, and
\varphi\in C^{1,\alpha}(B_{r}^{\prime})
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1],r>0
be given constants, and let
\mathbf{u}\in C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{B^{+}_{r}})\cap C^{2}(B^{+}_{r})
be a solution to the minimal surface system (
4.17
) in
B^{+}_{r}
, satisfying the boundary conditions (
5.3
)–(
5.4
). Suppose further that the closure of the graph of
\mathbf{u}
, denoted by
\mathcal{C}(\operatorname{graph}(\mathbf{u}))\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}
, admits a vertical tangent plane at every point
P
of the form

\displaystyle P=(y^{\prime},0)\in\mathcal{C}(\operatorname{graph}(\mathbf{u})),

(5.5)

with respect to the Euclidean metric of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}
. Then the following asymptotic expansion holds:

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(y)=\varphi(y^{\prime})+O\bigl((y^{n})^{1+\alpha}\bigr),

(5.6)

where the remainder term
O\bigl((y^{n})^{1+\alpha}\bigr)
is itself a
(\mathfrak{m}-n)
-valued
C^{1,\alpha}
function.

Proof.

By the Taylor expansion theorem for vector-valued functions, we have the expansion

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(y)=\varphi(y^{\prime})+\mathbf{c}_{1}(y^{\prime})y^{n}+O\bigl((y^{n})^{1+\alpha}\bigr),

(5.7)

where
\mathbf{c}_{1}
is an
(\mathfrak{m}-n)
-valued function on
B_{r}^{\prime}
. Substituting this expansion into the definition of the metric component
g_{nn}
, we obtain

\displaystyle g_{nn}(y^{\prime},0)=1+\bigl|\mathbf{c}_{1}(y^{\prime})\bigr|^{2}.

(5.8)

The vertical tangent plane assumption implies that
g_{nn}(y^{\prime},0)=1
, which immediately yields
\mathbf{c}_{1}=\mathbf{0}
. This completes the proof.
∎

By virtue of Lemma
5.1
, we may assume the following asymptotic expansion for the metric tensor components:

\displaystyle g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+\sum_{l=1}^{\mathfrak{m}-n}\frac{\partial\varphi_{l}}{\partial y^{i}}\frac{\partial\varphi_{l}}{\partial y^{j}}+O\bigl((y^{n})^{\alpha}\bigr),

(5.9)

where we adopt the convention that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\varphi_{l}}{\partial y^{n}}=0.

(5.10)

We deduce that for
\beta,\gamma=1,\cdots,n-1
, the tangential metric inverse
(g^{\beta\gamma})
is the inverse of the matrix

\displaystyle g_{\beta\gamma}=\delta_{\beta\gamma}+\sum_{l=1}^{\mathfrak{m}-n}\frac{\partial\varphi_{l}}{\partial y^{\beta}}\frac{\partial\varphi_{l}}{\partial y^{\gamma}}+O\bigl((y^{n})^{\alpha}\bigr),

(5.11)

and the remaining metric inverse components satisfy

\displaystyle g^{nn}=1+O\bigl((y^{n})^{\alpha}\bigr),\quad g^{\beta n}=O\bigl((y^{n})^{\alpha}\bigr).

(5.12)

Here, we note that
\alpha=1
in the case where
\mathbf{u}\in C^{1,1}(\overline{B^{+}_{r}})
.

From the minimal surface system (
4.17
), the
(\mathfrak{m}-n)
-valued function defined by

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u}-\varphi

(5.13)

satisfies, for each
s=1,\cdots,\mathfrak{m}-n
, the PDE

\displaystyle(y^{n})^{2}g^{ij}(v_{s})_{,ij}-ny^{n}(v_{s})_{,n}=-(y^{n})^{2}g^{ij}(\varphi_{s})_{,ij}.

(5.14)

This equation is uniformly elliptic after rescaling in a neighborhood of any point
P\in B^{+}_{r/2}
. Precisely, under the rescaled coordinate transformation

\displaystyle z=(y^{n}(P))^{-1}(x-x(P)),

(5.15)

equation (
5.14
) is uniformly elliptic on the rescaled domain

\displaystyle\left\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|z|<\frac{1}{2}\right\}.

(5.16)

Using the auxiliary metric
h
defined in (
4.19
), we apply the Schauder estimates and the maximum principle to establish the following lemma.

Lemma 5.2

(Tangential Smoothness)
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1],R>0
be given constants, and let
\mathbf{u}\in C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{B^{+}_{R}})\cap C^{2}(B^{+}_{R})
be a solution to the minimal surface system (
4.17
) in
B^{+}_{R}
. Suppose further that there exists a smooth function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(B_{R}^{\prime})
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}-\varphi=O\bigl((y^{n})^{1+\alpha}\bigr).

(5.17)

Then for any
r\in(0,R/2)
, any
P\in G_{r}^{+}
, and any integer
l\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a positive constant
C_{l}>0
independent of
P
such that

\displaystyle\|D_{y^{\prime}}^{l}(\mathbf{u}-\varphi)\|_{C^{2,\alpha}_{h}\bigl(B_{h}(P,\tfrac{1}{2})\bigr)}\leqslant C_{l}(y^{n})^{2}.

(5.18)

Proof.

By means of the rescaled coordinate transformation (
5.15
), combined with the PDE (
5.14
), the asymptotic condition (
5.17
), and the Schauder estimates for elliptic equations, we immediately obtain the a priori estimate

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}-\varphi\|_{C^{2,\alpha}_{h}\bigl(B_{h}(P,\tfrac{1}{2})\bigr)}\leqslant C(y^{n})^{1+\alpha}.

(5.19)

We denote by
D_{y^{\prime}}^{l}
an arbitrary
l
-th order tangential differential operator, which takes the form
D_{y^{\prime}}^{\beta}
where
\beta
is a multi-index satisfying
|\beta|=l
. Applying the tangential differential operator
D_{y^{\prime}}^{l}
to both sides of (
5.14
), we derive the differentiated PDE

\displaystyle g^{ij}(D_{y^{\prime}}^{l}v_{s})_{,ij}-n\frac{(D_{y^{\prime}}^{l}v_{s})_{,n}}{y^{n}}=-D_{y^{\prime}}^{l}\bigl(g^{ij}(\varphi_{s})_{,ij}\bigr)-\sum_{m=0}^{l-1}\binom{l}{m}\bigl(D_{y^{\prime}}^{l-m}g^{ij}\bigr)(D_{y^{\prime}}^{m}v_{s})_{,ij},

(5.20)

where for any
r\in(0,R/2)
and any
P\in G_{r}^{+}
, the
C^{\alpha}_{h}\bigl(B_{h}(P,\frac{1}{2})\bigr)
-norm of the right-hand side is bounded by a constant depending only on

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m},\,n,\,l,\,\|\mathbf{u}\|_{C^{1,\alpha}(G_{R})},\,\|\varphi\|_{C^{l+2,\alpha}(B_{R}^{\prime})}

(5.21)

and the constants

\displaystyle C_{0},\,C_{1},\,\cdots,\,C_{l-1}

(5.22)

appearing in the estimate (
5.18
) for lower orders. Note that the constants in (
5.22
) are redundant when
l=0
.

For any fixed
\delta\in(0,r)
and any
y_{0}^{\prime}\in B_{r-\delta}^{\prime}
, we introduce the barrier function

\displaystyle M(y^{\prime},y^{n})=a|y^{\prime}-y_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}+b(y^{n})^{2},

(5.23)

where
a,b>0
are to be determined. Following the argument in the proof of Theorem 3.1 in
[
8
]
, we apply the maximum principle for elliptic PDEs to establish the
L^{\infty}
-estimate

\displaystyle\|D_{y^{\prime}}^{l}(\mathbf{u}-\varphi)\|_{L^{\infty}(G_{r-\delta})}\leqslant C(y^{n})^{2}.

(5.24)

Finally, by shrinking the radius
r
if necessary, we complete the proof by invoking the rescaling argument once again.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

The estimate (
5.18
) can be improved to be, for any
q\in\mathbb{N},

\displaystyle\|D_{y^{\prime}}^{l}(\mathbf{u}-\varphi)\|_{C^{q,\alpha}_{h}(B_{h}(P,\frac{1}{2}))}\leqslant C_{q,l}(y^{n})^{2}.

(5.25)

Meanwhile, the minimal surface system (
4.17
) can be recast in the following form for each
s=1,\cdots,\mathfrak{m}-n
:

\displaystyle(u_{s})_{,nn}-\frac{n}{y^{n}}(u_{s})_{,n}=(u_{s})_{,nn}-g^{ij}(u_{s})_{,ij},

(5.26)

where the right-hand side contains only second-order tangential derivatives and mixed derivatives of
u_{s}
, provided that we neglect the derivatives of
\mathbf{u}
appearing in the metric coefficients
g^{ij}
.

For any
(\mathfrak{m}-n)
-valued function
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\cdots,w_{\mathfrak{m}-n})
defined on
G_{R}
, we define the differential operator
\mathcal{Q}[\mathbf{w}]
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}[\mathbf{w}]:=g^{ij}[\mathbf{w}]\frac{\partial^{2}w_{s}}{\partial y^{i}\partial y^{j}}-\frac{n}{y^{n}}\frac{\partial w_{s}}{\partial y^{n}},

(5.27)

where
g^{ij}[\mathbf{w}]
denotes the inverse of the metric tensor

\displaystyle g_{ij}[\mathbf{w}]=\delta_{ij}+\sum_{l=1}^{\mathfrak{m}-n}\frac{\partial w_{l}}{\partial y^{i}}\frac{\partial w_{l}}{\partial y^{j}}.

(5.28)

By means of the metric inverse estimates (
5.11
)–(
5.12
), we establish the following lemma on formal series solutions.

Lemma 5.4

(Formal Series Solution)
.

Let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(B_{r}^{\prime})
be a smooth function. Then there exist smooth
(\mathfrak{m}-n)
-valued functions
\mathbf{c}_{i}
and
\mathbf{c}_{i,j}
on
B_{r}^{\prime}
(with
(i,j)\neq(n+1,0)
) that depend on
\varphi
and the first global coefficient
\mathbf{c}_{n+1,0}
such that, for any given smooth
(\mathfrak{m}-n)
-valued function
\mathbf{c}_{n+1,0}
on
B_{r}^{\prime}
, there exists a unique formal series of the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=\varphi+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\mathbf{c}_{i}(y^{\prime})(y^{n})^{i}+\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{i-1}{n}\right\rfloor}\mathbf{c}_{i,j}(y^{\prime})(y^{n})^{i}\bigl(\log(y^{n})\bigr)^{j}\quad\text{in }G_{r},

(5.29)

which formally solves the minimal surface system (
4.17
). Here
\mathbf{c}_{i}(y^{\prime})=\mathbf{0}
whenever
i\leqslant n
is an even integer.

In addition, we define the partial sum
\mathbf{T}_{k}
by

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{k}=\varphi+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=2\\
i\ \text{even}\end{subarray}}^{2\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor}\mathbf{c}_{i}(y^{\prime})(y^{n})^{i}+\sum_{i=n+1}^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{i-1}{n}\right\rfloor}\mathbf{c}_{i,j}(y^{\prime})(y^{n})^{i}\bigl(\log(y^{n})\bigr)^{j}.

(5.30)

This partial sum satisfies the asymptotic estimate

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{T}_{k})=O\Bigl((y^{n})^{k}\bigl(\log(y^{n})\bigr)^{\left\lfloor\frac{k-1}{n}\right\rfloor}\Bigr).

(5.31)

Remark 5.5
.

When
n
is even, we have the identity

\displaystyle 2\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor=n,

(5.32)

in which case all logarithmic terms in the series expansion (
5.29
) vanish identically.

Proof of Lemma
5.4
.

By (
2.5
), we recast equation (
5.26
) in the following equivalent form:

\displaystyle(y^{n})^{2}\mathbf{u}_{,nn}-ny^{n}\mathbf{u}_{,n}=(y^{n})^{2}\bigl(\mathbf{u}_{,nn}-g^{ij}\mathbf{u}_{,ij}\bigr).

(5.33)

We initialize the coefficient recursion by setting
\mathbf{c}_{0}=\varphi
, and we proceed by induction: assume that we have formally determined the partial sum
\mathbf{T}_{i-1}
for some integer
i\geqslant 2
.

Consider a general term
\mathbf{c}_{i,j}(y^{n})^{i}\bigl(\log(y^{n})\bigr)^{j}
appearing in the series expansion (
5.29
) of
\mathbf{u}
. Substituting this term into the left-hand side of (
5.33
), we compute its leading contribution, which is given by

\displaystyle i\bigl(i-(n+1)\bigr)\mathbf{c}_{i,j}(y^{n})^{i}\bigl(\log(y^{n})\bigr)^{j}.

(5.34)

Next, we expand the right-hand side of (
5.33
) as a power series (with logarithmic terms) and isolate the terms involving
\mathbf{c}_{i,j}
. A key observation is that all such terms carry a factor of
(y^{n})^{i+1}
(or higher order, if logarithmic factors are disregarded).

Therefore, whenever

\displaystyle i\neq n+1,

(5.35)

we can uniquely solve for the coefficient
\mathbf{c}_{i,j}
by equating the coefficients of the term
(y^{n})^{i}\bigl(\log(y^{n})\bigr)^{j}
on both sides of (
5.33
). The resulting expression for
\mathbf{c}_{i,j}
depends only on the earlier coefficients
\mathbf{c}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
with
i^{\prime}<i
.

For
k\leqslant n
, we first establish that
\mathbf{T}_{k}
contains only even powers of
y^{n}
via mathematical induction. We initialize the base case with
\mathbf{T}_{2}=\varphi+\mathbf{c}_{2}(y^{\prime})(y^{n})^{2}
, which trivially consists of even powers of
y^{n}
alone. Assume the inductive hypothesis holds: for any even integer
k\in[2,n-1]
, the partial sum
\mathbf{T}_{k}
contains only even powers of
y^{n}
.

We substitute
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{T}_{k}
into the right-hand side of (
5.33
) and analyze the metric coefficient expansions. A key observation is that for
\beta,\gamma=1,\cdots,n-1
, the expansion of
g^{\beta n}[\mathbf{T}_{k}]
comprises only odd powers of
y^{n}
, while the expansion of
g^{\beta\gamma}[\mathbf{T}_{k}]
comprises only even powers of
y^{n}
. Combining these parity properties, we conclude that the entire right-hand side of (
5.33
) is a sum of even powers of
y^{n}
.

By substituting
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{T}_{k+1}
into (
5.33
) and invoking the earlier analysis of the leading term formula (
5.34
), we uniquely deduce that
\mathbf{c}_{k+1}=\mathbf{0}
. This verifies the inductive step, and the claim follows by the principle of mathematical induction.

Next, we consider the critical case
i=n+1
. For
j\geqslant 1
, the leading term involving
\mathbf{c}_{i,j}
on the left-hand side of (
5.33
) simplifies to

\displaystyle(2i-1-n)j\mathbf{c}_{i,j}(y^{n})^{i}\bigl(\log(y^{n})\bigr)^{j-1}=(n+1)j\mathbf{c}_{n+1,j}(y^{n})^{n+1}\bigl(\log(y^{n})\bigr)^{j-1}.

(5.36)

Following the identical coefficient-matching procedure as above, we can uniquely solve for the coefficients
\mathbf{c}_{n+1,j}
for all
j\geqslant 1
.

Finally, we note that the coefficient
\mathbf{c}_{n+1,0}
cannot be determined by this recursive process—it serves as a free parameter. If we prescribe a smooth
(\mathfrak{m}-n)
-valued function
\mathbf{c}_{n+1,0}
on
B_{r}^{\prime}
, then the entire recursive scheme yields a unique formal series solution of the form (
5.29
).
∎

For notational simplicity, we set
t=y^{n}
throughout the subsequent discussion.

Definition 5.6
.

A function
\mathbf{w}
(which may be single-valued or
(\mathfrak{m}-n)
-valued) is said to admit an expansion of order
t^{k}
in
B^{+}_{r}
if there exist smooth functions
\mathbf{c}_{i,j}(y^{\prime})
on
B_{r}^{\prime}
, positive integers
N_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
, and a constant
\varepsilon>0
such that the decomposition

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{T}_{k}+\mathbf{R}_{k}:=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{N_{i}}\mathbf{c}_{i,j}t^{i}(\log t)^{j}+\mathbf{R}_{k}

(5.37)

holds in
\overline{B^{+}_{r}}
. Here, the remainder term
\mathbf{R}_{k}
satisfies two key conditions:

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{k}\in O(t^{k+\varepsilon})\cap C^{k,\varepsilon}(\overline{G_{r}}),

(5.38)

and for all nonnegative integers
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
, the tangential derivative estimate

\displaystyle\|D_{y^{\prime}}^{p}\mathbf{R}_{k}\|_{C^{q,\alpha}_{h}(G_{r})}\leqslant C_{p,q,k}\,t^{k+\varepsilon}

(5.39)

is valid, where
C_{p,q,k}
denotes a constant independent of
t
and
y^{\prime}
.

We refer to the decomposition (
5.37
) as a Taylor expansion with logarithmic terms of
\mathbf{w}
.

By virtue of Lemma
5.2
and Remark
5.3
, we immediately conclude that the solution
\mathbf{u}
admits an expansion of order
t
.

To derive the full Taylor expansion of
\mathbf{u}
with logarithmic terms, we proceed by iteratively applying the following lemma.

Lemma 5.7
.

Assume the same hypotheses as in Lemma
5.2
. For any integer
k\geqslant 1
, if
\mathbf{u}
admits an expansion of order
t^{k}
in
B^{+}_{R}
, then
\mathbf{u}
admits an expansion of order
t^{k+1}
in
B^{+}_{r}
for every
r\in(0,R)
.

Proof.

We split the proof into two key steps.

First, we prove that if
\mathbf{u}
admits an expansion of order
t^{k}
in
G_{r}
, then the nonlinear forcing term defined by

\displaystyle F_{s}(y,\mathbf{u}):=t^{2}(u_{s})_{,tt}-t^{2}g^{ij}(u_{s})_{,ij}

(5.40)

(cf. equation (
5.33
)) admits an expansion of order
t^{k+1}
in
G_{r}
.

By the inductive hypothesis,
\mathbf{u}
satisfies the decomposition (
5.37
) with remainder
\mathbf{R}_{k}
of order
t^{k+\varepsilon}
. Substituting this expansion into (
5.40
), we observe that the remainder contribution
\mathbf{R}_{k}
acquires an additional factor of
t
after differentiation and multiplication by
t^{2}
. This claim follows directly from formal differentiation and substitution; for a detailed computation, we refer the reader to the proof of Lemma 5.2 in
[
15
]
.

Next, we show that if
F_{s}
admits an expansion of order
t^{k+1}
, then so does
u_{s}
. The key tool is the integral representation of the solution to the ODE derived from (
5.26
). For
t\in(0,r)
, we have

\displaystyle u_{s}(y^{\prime},t)=

\displaystyle\left[u_{s}(y^{\prime},r)r^{-\overline{m}}-\frac{r^{\underline{m}-\overline{m}}}{\overline{m}-\underline{m}}\int_{0}^{r}\zeta^{-1-\underline{m}}F_{s}(y^{\prime},\mathbf{u}(y^{\prime},\zeta))\,d\zeta\right]t^{\overline{m}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{t^{\underline{m}}}{\overline{m}-\underline{m}}\int_{0}^{t}\zeta^{-1-\underline{m}}F_{s}(y^{\prime},\mathbf{u}(y^{\prime},\zeta))\,d\zeta

For simplicity, we set

For the solution

By the argument presented in the proof of Theorem

Combining the minimal surface system (

) and the derivative formula (

of

satisfy the following PDE for

\displaystyle g^{ij}[\mathbf{u}]v_{s,ij}-\frac{n}{t}v_{s,t}=-t^{2}G_{1}(y)+\bigl(g^{ij}\bigl[\varphi+\mathbf{c}_{2}t^{2}\bigr]-g^{ij}[\mathbf{u}]\bigr)\bigl(\varphi_{s}+c_{2,s}t^{2}\bigr)_{ij},

(6.3)

where the metric difference term admits the integral representation

\displaystyle g^{ij}\bigl[\varphi+\mathbf{c}_{2}t^{2}\bigr]-g^{ij}[\mathbf{u}]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}g^{ij}\bigl[\varphi+\mathbf{c}_{2}t^{2}+\zeta\mathbf{v}\bigr]d\zeta

(6.4)

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{0}^{1}g^{il}\bigl[\varphi+\mathbf{c}_{2}t^{2}+\zeta\mathbf{v}\bigr]\cdot g^{mj}\bigl[\varphi+\mathbf{c}_{2}t^{2}+\zeta\mathbf{v}\bigr]d\zeta\right)

(6.5)

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot\left(\mathbf{v}_{l}\cdot\bigl(\varphi+\mathbf{c}_{2}t^{2}\bigr)_{m}+\mathbf{v}_{m}\cdot\bigl(\varphi+\mathbf{c}_{2}t^{2}\bigr)_{l}\right).

(6.6)

This metric difference term is smooth with respect to the derivatives of

Dividing both sides of (

\displaystyle v_{s,tt}-\frac{n}{t}v_{s,t}=\bigl(g^{nn}\bigr)^{-1}\left(\bigl(g^{nn}v_{s,tt}-g^{ij}v_{s,ij}\bigr)-\frac{n\bigl(g^{nn}-1\bigr)}{t}v_{s,t}-G_{2}\right),

(6.7)

\displaystyle g^{nn}v_{s,tt}-g^{ij}v_{s,ij}

contains only mixed and tangential second-order derivatives of

. Let

\displaystyle g^{nn}-1=\frac{\det(g_{\alpha\beta})-\det(g_{ij})}{\det(g_{ij})}=\frac{\sum_{\beta=1}^{n-1}g_{n\beta}\bigl(g^{*}\bigr)^{\beta n}}{\det(g_{ij})},
