Association of Urinary Soluble CD163 With Response to Immunosuppressive Therapy and Renal Relapse in IgAN
Authors: Hao Zhao, Zishu Zhan, Tong Lin, Yongyi Pan, Shuling Yuan, Jingxin Huang, Ling Jia, Di Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe absence of a dynamic, noninvasive biomarker to monitor intrarenal immunologic activity during and after immunosuppressive therapy limits patient-specific risk stratification and timely treatment decisions in IgA nephropathy (IgAN). The clinical utility of urinary soluble CD163 (sCD163) for predicting response to immunosuppressive therapy and detecting renal relapse remains to be fully elucidated.MethodsThis prospective cohort study included 281 patients with IgAN who were at high risk of progression. All the patients received immunosuppressive therapy for a median of 18 months. Urinary sCD163 levels were measured and normalized to creatinine. Treatment response to immunosuppression was defined as a reduction in urinary protein excretion with stable renal function within 12 months of immunosuppression. Relapse was defined as an increase in urinary protein excretion after remission.ResultsBaseline urinary sCD163 correlated strongly with glomerular and tubulointerstitial CD163-positive macrophage density on kidney biopsy. Patients in the highest tertile of baseline urinary sCD163 had 8-fold greater odds of treatment response (adjusted odds ratio: 8.04, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 3.88–16.67) than those in the lowest tertile. Adding urinary sCD163 to a clinical-histological model significantly improved the discriminatory ability for treatment response, increasing the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) to 0.82. Longitudinal urinary sCD163 was significantly associated with time to relapse, with an adjusted hazard ratio of 1.38 (95% CI: 1.21–1.56).ConclusionUrinary sCD163 is independently associated with response to immunosuppressive therapy and may serve as a potential biomarker for impending relapse in high-risk IgAN, which could help personalize therapeutic management.

IgAN is the most prevalent primary glomerulonephritis worldwide, and approximately 50% of patients progress to end-stage kidney disease within 10 to 20 years of diagnosis.1 Because its pathogenesis is driven by mesangial deposition of IgA-containing immune complexes and complement activation, targeting the underlying immune dysregulation represents a rational therapeutic strategy.2 The most widely used immunosuppressive therapies for IgAN, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, remain glucocorticoids and mycophenolate mofetil. Recent studies have shown that these treatments can reduce proteinuria and slow the decline in kidney function.3, 4, 5 However, the response rates are only 50% to 60%, underscoring the need to identify patients early in the disease course who are most likely to benefit from immunosuppressive therapy. Such early identification could help guide personalized treatment decisions and avoid unnecessary exposure to immunosuppression.

Infiltration and activation of macrophages within the glomeruli and tubulointerstitium contribute significantly to the pathogenesis of IgAN.6,7 Our previous work demonstrated that the intensity of glomerular macrophage infiltration predicts response to immunosuppressive therapy in IgAN.8 However, given the dynamic nature of immunologic activity, the disease activity index from the initial diagnostic kidney biopsy may not accurately reflect intrarenal disease activity after long-term immunosuppressive treatment. Therefore, a noninvasive urinary biomarker that dynamically reflects intrarenal immunologic activity is needed to better identify patients most likely to benefit from immunosuppressive therapy.

sCD163 is cleaved from the surface of alternatively activated macrophages and is thus detectable in urine during active kidney inflammation.9 Several studies have demonstrated an association between urinary sCD163 and disease activity in antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody–associated glomerulonephritis and lupus nephritis.10, 11, 12 In addition, urinary sCD163 correlates with histologic injury, renal function decline, and poor prognosis in IgAN,13,14 suggesting its potential as a biomarker of kidney inflammation across immune-mediated kidney diseases.

To determine whether urinary sCD163 is associated with treatment response to immunosuppressive therapy and risk of renal relapse in IgAN, we conducted a prospective cohort study of patients with IgAN who were at high risk of disease progression and treated with immunosuppressive therapy. Longitudinal measurement of urinary sCD163 may help identify renal relapse in patients who achieved remission after immunosuppressive treatment.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This single-center, prospective cohort study was conducted at Nanfang Hospital, a tertiary medical center in southern China. Among 679 patients with biopsy-proven IgAN between February 2022 and June 2024, we screened 328 patients who met the high-risk criteria for inclusion. High risk for progression was defined as persistent proteinuria ≥ 1.0 g/d despite ≥3 months of optimized supportive care, which included maximally tolerated renin-angiotensin system inhibitor therapy,15 blood pressure control, and lifestyle modifications; sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors were added when appropriate.16 Of note, some patients had already initiated optimized supportive care before kidney biopsy; therefore, the 3-month period of supportive care was not necessarily measured from the time of biopsy. The major exclusion criteria were the following: (i) secondary IgAN (e.g., due to cirrhosis or autoimmune diseases), (ii) use of any systemic immunosuppressive agents within 6 months before biopsy, (iii) estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 30 ml/min per 1.73 m2 at biopsy, and (iv) concurrent acute kidney injury at biopsy. All eligible patients received immunosuppressive therapy in addition to renin-angiotensin system inhibitor therapy for ≥6 months and were followed up at 3-month intervals. The specific immunosuppressive regimen was determined by the treating physicians according to standard clinical practice. Of the 328 patients screened, 281 completed ≥6 months of immunosuppressive therapy and were included in the final analysis (Figure 1). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Definitions

Treatment response to immunosuppression was defined as a reduction in urinary protein excretion to <0.5 g/d accompanied by a <25% decline in eGFR from baseline. Remission required confirmation by a repeat measurement of proteinuria and eGFR after a 30-day interval. Patients who achieved remission within 12 months after the initiation of immunosuppressive therapy were classified as responders. Patients who did not achieve remission by 12 months were classified as poor responders. Relapse was defined as an increase in urinary protein excretion to ≥1.0 g/d after remission, confirmed by a repeat measurement after a 30-day interval.

Laboratory Measurements

All urine samples were collected as morning spot urine, centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 minutes, and the supernatants were immediately stored at −80 °C until analysis. Urinary sCD163 was measured using a commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (DuoSet DY1607; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN). Urinary sCD163 concentrations were normalized to urinary creatinine and expressed as ng/mg creatinine (ng/mg Cr). Assay precision was evaluated by determining intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation. Specifically, intraassay coefficient of variation was assessed by testing 3 samples 10 times on a single plate, and interassay coefficient of variation was assessed across 5 independent assays. The intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were 2.5% and 5.3%, respectively.

Quantification of CD163 Infiltration in Renal Tissue

CD163-positive cells were identified using immunohistochemistry in 20 kidney biopsy samples, as described previously.8,17 Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded kidney sections (4 μm-thick) were incubated with a monoclonal anti-CD163 primary antibody (ab182422; Abcam, Cambridge, UK). The slides were then incubated with the secondary antibody and visualized using a DAKO Envision+ System Kit (K5007; DAKO). Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin and scanned using a Leica Digital Pathology Slide Scanner (Wetzlar, Germany). Positively stained cells were automatically counted in all nonglobally sclerotic glomeruli and in the tubulointerstitium at 400× magnification using Aperio eSlide Manager (Leica). The automated quantification was validated against manual counts performed by an experienced renal pathologist in 20 randomly selected biopsies, showing strong correlations for both glomerular (r = 0.94) and tubulointerstitial (r = 0.92) CD163-positive cell densities. For glomerular analysis, all nonglobally sclerotic glomeruli were evaluated. Glomerular CD163+ cell density was calculated as the total number of positive cells divided by the number of glomeruli assessed. In the tubulointerstitial compartment, positively stained cells were automatically counted and expressed as the average number per 100mm2 of tubulointerstitium.

Assessment of Renal Histologic Lesions and Renal Function

All kidney biopsy specimens were evaluated by 2 independent pathologists who were blinded to the clinical data, urinary sCD163 levels, and clinical outcomes. Renal histological lesions were scored according to the Oxford Classification (MEST-C score).18 Urinary protein excretion was quantified from 24-hour urine collections. Urinary and serum creatinine were measured using an enzymatic method (Roche Diagnostics). eGFR was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration 2021 creatinine equation.19

Statistical Analyses

All patients included in the final analysis had complete data. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± SD or median with interquartile range, as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as number (percentage). Between-group differences for continuous variables were assessed using the t test, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, or 1-way analysis of variance, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test.

Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to assess the relationship between urinary sCD163 levels and CD163-positive cell infiltration in glomeruli and tubulointerstitium. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate the association between the tertiles of baseline urinary sCD163 and response to immunosuppressive therapy, with and without adjustment for age, sex, baseline eGFR, 24-hour urinary protein excretion (log-transformed), mean arterial pressure, MEST-C score, and the type of immunosuppressive therapy. Crude and adjusted odds ratios with corresponding 95% CIs were reported. In addition, restricted cubic spline analyses were applied to estimate the potential nonlinear relationship between baseline urinary sCD163 and the treatment response. Subgroup analyses were performed by stratifying patients according to baseline characteristics: age (< 35 vs. ≥ 35 years), sex, eGFR (< 60 vs. ≥ 60 ml/min per 1.73 m2), urinary protein excretion (< 2.5 vs. ≥ 2.5 g/d), Oxford T-score (T0 vs. T1 + T2), Oxford C-score (C0 vs. C1 + T2), and type of immunosuppressive therapy. Furthermore, to assess the robustness of the primary findings, we performed 3 sensitivity analyses as follows: (i) restricting the analysis to patients who initiated immunosuppressive therapy within 1 month after kidney biopsy, (ii) additionally adjusting for baseline sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor use in the multivariable logistic regression model, and (iii) redefining treatment response as a ≥50% reduction in proteinuria from baseline within 12 months.

The predictive performance of different logistic regression models for treatment response was evaluated using the AUC in the overall cohort and further assessed using 10-fold cross-validation. The following prediction models were compared: a univariable model with baseline urinary sCD163 alone, a clinical model containing baseline clinical variables and the MEST-C score, and a full model combining urinary sCD163 with the clinical variables and MEST-C score. The difference between AUCs was determined using the DeLong test. Calibration of the prediction models was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Reclassification performance was evaluated using the continuous Net Reclassification Improvement and Integrated Discrimination Improvement, with the model containing clinical data only as the reference.

A joint model combining longitudinal log-transformed urinary sCD163 and time to renal relapse was fitted using a linear mixed-effects submodel (with fixed effects for time and baseline proteinuria, and random intercepts and slopes for each patient) and a Cox proportional hazards submodel (with baseline eGFR and proteinuria as covariates). The 2 submodels were linked by a shared random effects structure assuming a Weibull proportional hazards association. The median lead time from a detectable rise in urinary sCD163 to clinical relapse was derived from the joint model predictions.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing). A 2-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Urinary sCD163 levels at biopsy were significantly elevated in patients with severe proteinuria (urinary protein excretion ≥ 2.5 g/d) than in those with mild proteinuria (< 2.5 g/d) (Figure 2d). Although urinary sCD163 levels tended to be higher in patients with reduced kidney function (eGFR < 60 ml/min per 1.73 m2), this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.051; Figure 2c). Compared with those with lower levels, patients with higher urinary sCD163 levels had greater proteinuria; lower eGFR and serum albumin; and a higher prevalence of the Oxford histological lesions, including endocapillary hypercellularity (E), segmental glomerulosclerosis (S), and crescents (C).

Association Between Urinary sCD163 and Response to Immunosuppressive Therapy

After adjusting for age, sex, eGFR, urinary protein excretion, mean arterial pressure, and the Oxford MEST-C score, higher baseline urinary sCD163 levels remained independently associated with a favorable response to immunosuppressive therapy. Compared with the lowest tertile, the highest tertile of urinary sCD163 at the time of biopsy was associated with higher odds of response to immunosuppressive therapy (adjusted odds ratio: 8.04; 95% CI: 3.88–16.67; Table 2). When urinary sCD163 levels were analyzed as a continuous variable, each SD increase in urinary sCD163 was associated with increased odds of treatment response (adjusted odds ratio: 2.73; 95% CI: 1.91–3.89; Table 2). Restricted cubic spline analyses showed an approximately linear, positive association between urinary sCD163 and response to immunosuppressive therapy (Supplementary Figure S1). The association between urinary sCD163 and response to immunosuppressive therapy was consistent across all prespecified subgroups, including those stratified by age, sex, eGFR, urinary protein excretion, Oxford T score, Oxford C-score, and immunosuppressive strategies (Supplementary Table S5). Performance of Urinary sCD163 in Predicting Response to Immunosuppressive Therapy

Baseline urinary sCD163 demonstrated good discrimination for predicting treatment response, with an AUC of 0.76 (95% CI: 0.71–0.82) (Supplementary Figure S2). Multivariable models containing only clinical variables and the MEST-C score showed limited discrimination (AUC: 0.65, 95% CI: 0.60–0.66). Incorporation of urinary sCD163 into a baseline model containing clinical variables and the MEST-C score significantly improved the prediction of treatment response (AUC: 0.82, 95% CI: 0.76–0.88; Supplementary Figure S2A). This discriminative performance was confirmed by internal validation using 10-fold cross-validation (Supplementary Figure S2B). As shown in Supplementary Table S9, adding urinary sCD163 to a clinical-histological model significantly improved risk reclassification compared with the model containing clinical data only. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test showed close agreement between predicted and observed response to immunosuppression (P = 0.16).

Urinary sCD163 as an Early Indicator of Relapse During Treatment

We next evaluated longitudinal changes in urinary sCD163 levels over 12 months after initiation of immunosuppressive therapy, stratified by clinical response (Figure 3). In responders who remained relapse-free, urinary sCD163 levels decreased in parallel with proteinuria and remained low throughout follow-up (Figure 3a). In contrast, poor responders showed no significant change from baseline in either urinary sCD163 or proteinuria (Figure 3b). Among responders who subsequently relapsed, urinary sCD163 and proteinuria levels initially declined after treatment but increased again near the time of relapse. Notably, the rise in urinary sCD163 preceded the recurrence of proteinuria, becoming detectable as early as month 3. To formally quantify the predictive ability of serial urinary sCD163 measurements for renal relapse, a joint modeling analysis of longitudinal and time-to-event data was performed. In the joint model, longitudinal urinary sCD163 levels were significantly associated with time to relapse, with an adjusted hazard ratio of 1.38 (95% CI: 1.21–1.56). The median lead time from a detectable increase in urinary sCD163 to clinical proteinuria recurrence was 2.8 (1.8–3.7) months.

Discussion

In this prospective cohort of patients with IgAN, urinary sCD163 levels correlated positively with proteinuria and inversely correlated with eGFR. Furthermore, elevated urinary sCD163 levels at the time of biopsy were independently associated with a favorable response to immunosuppressive therapy. Incorporation of urinary sCD163 into a baseline model containing clinical variables and the MEST-C score improved the model's discriminative ability for treatment response. Finally, longitudinal monitoring of urinary sCD163 in treatment responders enabled earlier detection of disease relapse than conventional clinical markers, often identifying impending relapse months in advance.

In our previous study, patients who responded to immunosuppression had a higher density of CD206-positive macrophages (M2 type macrophages) infiltration in the renal biopsies. Moreover, the intensity of glomerular CD206-positive macrophage infiltration was identified as an independent predictor of response to immunosuppression in IgAN.8 Here, we extend these findings to a noninvasive biomarker, urinary sCD163. Consistent with this, responders had significantly higher urinary sCD163 levels at biopsy than poor responders. In addition, urinary sCD163 levels correlated strongly with the glomerular and tubulointerstitial density of CD163-positive macrophages, another marker of M2 polarization. Patients in the highest tertile of urinary sCD163 at biopsy had an 8-fold greater probability of responding to immunosuppression than those in the lowest tertile. This association was consistent across all prespecified subgroups. Consistent with our earlier findings, other variables, including clinical variables and the MEST-C score failed to accurately predict treatment response when used alone.

Macrophage infiltration is generally considered a histological marker of disease activity in many proliferative forms of glomerulonephritis,20 including IgAN.21 Although CD163-positive macrophages are thought to infiltrate inflamed tissues primarily during the healing phase of acute inflammation,22 several studies have linked them to active inflammation in kidney diseases such as lupus nephritis and active renal vasculitis.12,23, 24, 25 Urinary sCD163 derives from the cleavage of the surface receptor on CD163-positive macrophages and serves as a surrogate marker reflecting renal CD163-positive macrophage infiltration.26,27 In line with this, we found that patients with elevated urinary sCD163 had a higher prevalence of both active and chronic histological lesions than those with lower levels, consistent with prior reports in IgAN. Therefore, the inflammatory activity indicated by elevated urinary sCD163 likely represents a key therapeutic target, potentially explain its strong association with a favorable response to immunosuppression in our cohort.

IgAN encompasses a wide spectrum of histological lesions, which can be active and potentially responsive to immunosuppression, or chronic and irreversible with current therapies,28 making treatment decisions at the time of biopsy particularly challenging. Our previous study suggested that the number of glomerular macrophages could predict the response to immunosuppressive therapy, thereby helping to restrict immunomodulatory treatment to patients with a more favorable benefit-risk ratio. An ideal biomarker of disease activity would inform therapeutic decisions at the time of biopsy. Relying solely on histology to assess activity would necessitate serial kidney biopsies, which is neither practical nor feasible in clinical practice. Therefore, a noninvasive urinary biomarker that dynamically reflects intrarenal immunologic activity throughout the course of immunosuppressive treatment would be invaluable. Our findings address this need by demonstrating that urinary sCD163 is a noninvasive correlate of renal CD163-positive macrophage infiltration. It thus offers a practical alternative to relying on histology for this specific aspect of disease activity, providing dynamic and repeated assessments across the clinical spectrum of IgAN.

Accurately identifying patients with relapsing or persistent disease activity is essential for optimizing therapeutic management and minimizing the risks associated with prolonged or unnecessary immunosuppression. In IgAN, proteinuria is an imperfect biomarker because not only can it not reliably differentiate active inflammation from chronic hyperfiltration but also, even when in remission, it does not necessarily signify the resolution of underlying kidney inflammation.29 In this prospective cohort, we demonstrate that serial measurement of urinary sCD163 after achieving clinical remission can identify patients at heightened risk for subsequent relapse. In responders, urinary sCD163 levels generally decreased with therapy and remained low during the 12-month follow-up. In contrast, among responders who later relapsed, urinary sCD163 levels began to rise prior to the recurrence of proteinuria, with increases detectable as early as 3 months before clinical relapse. These data support the notion that urinary sCD163 reflects dynamic disease activity and serves as an early indicator of impending relapse in IgAN. Consequently, serial monitoring of urinary sCD163 could facilitate earlier patient stratification and guide timely therapeutic adjustments.

The strengths of this study include its prospective design and the enrollment of patients at high risk for progression, as defined by contemporary guidelines. This approach minimizes selection bias and enhances the clinical relevance of the cohort. Furthermore, the longitudinal serial measurement of urinary sCD163 and proteinuria from treatment initiation allowed us to assess urinary sCD163 for 3 purposes: reflecting concurrent disease activity, evaluating treatment response, and predicting impending clinical relapse.

Our study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center study conducted in a Chinese population. Therefore, the generalizability of our findings requires external validation in other populations. Second, the study cohort did not include patients receiving newer targeted therapies (e.g., BAFF/APRIL inhibitors or anti-CD38 inhibitors), because these were not yet approved for IgAN in China during the enrollment period. Consequently, the predictive value of urinary sCD163 in patients receiving these newer therapies remains to be established. Future studies in ethnically diverse populations and in patients treated with these novel agents are needed to validate and extend our findings. Third, the limited number of relapse events in our cohort may affect the stability of the estimates from the joint modeling analysis. Therefore, external validation in larger cohorts with more events is warranted.

In conclusion, urinary sCD163 serves as both an independent predictor of response to immunosuppressive therapy and a promising biomarker for detecting renal relapse in high-risk IgAN. These findings could therefore facilitate personalized management in IgAN by informing treatment decisions at the time of biopsy and guiding subsequent surveillance strategies.
