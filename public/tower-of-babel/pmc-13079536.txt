A differentiated thyroid carcinoma-specific graded prognostic assessment for brain metastases: derivation in a 10,306-patient cohort and external validation
Authors: Hamza A. Salim, Sarimar Agosto, Heba Al Qudah, Kim Learned, Mark Zafereo, Jennifer Wang, Mimi I. Hu, Ahmed Msherghi, Naifa Busaidy, F. Eymen Ucisik, Sarah Hamidi, Hussein Tawbi, Rami W. Eldaya, Jing Li, Max Wintermark, Maria E. Cabanillas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBrain metastases from differentiated thyroid carcinoma (DTC) are rare but associated with significantly worse prognosis compared to extracranial metastatic sites. Existing prognostic models for brain metastases are not tailored to DTC, limiting risk stratification and therapeutic guidance in this population.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of 10,306 patients with DTC treated at a single tertiary cancer center from 2011 to 2025. Patients with radiologically or pathologically confirmed brain metastases were included. Multivariable Cox regression was used to identify independent prognostic factors for overall survival (OS), defined from the time of Brain metastases diagnosis. Based on these factors, we developed the Differentiated Thyroid Cancer Graded Prognostic Assessment (DTC-GPA) and evaluated its discriminatory performance using Harrell’s C-index. External validation was performed using individual-level data from published case series identified via systematic review.ResultsOf 10,306 patients with DTC, 146 (1.4%) were found to have brain metastases. The median OS after Brain metastases diagnosis was 20.4 months. ECOG performance status ≥ 2 (HR, 2.67; 95% CI, 1.65–4.30; p < 0.001), presence of multiple brain metastases (HR, 2.30; 95% CI, 1.39–3.82; p = 0.002), and interval ≥ 3 years between primary DTC and Brain metastases diagnosis (HR, 1.84; 95% CI, 1.03–3.27; p = 0.03) were independently associated with worse OS. These three factors formed the DTC-GPA, assigning one point for each favorable variable (range: 0–3). Median OS by score group was 7.7 months (score 0), 22.9 months (score 1), and 61.3 months (score 2–3). The C-index was 0.70 in the derivation cohort and 0.68 in the external validation cohort of 83 patients.ConclusionsThe DTC-GPA is the first validated, disease-specific prognostic tool for patients with brain metastases from DTC. This simple, three-point score offers clinically meaningful risk stratification to guide treatment decisions and prognosis estimation in a rare and heterogeneous patient population.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11060-026-05507-5.

Introduction

Differentiated thyroid carcinoma (DTC), is generally considered an indolent malignancy with excellent long-term survival when managed appropriately with surgery, radioactive iodine therapy, and thyroid hormone suppression [1–3]. However, in a small subset of patients, DTC progresses to advanced disease with distant metastases, most commonly affecting the lungs and bones [4]. Brain metastases from DTC are rare, with an estimated incidence of 0.1–1.3% among all patients with thyroid cancer [2–5], but their presence portends a significantly poorer prognosis [6]. Reported median overall survival (OS) after brain metastases diagnosis ranges widely, from 3 to 60 months, depending on patient and disease characteristics, as well as therapeutic interventions [7–27].

In an effort to guide clinical decision-making for patients with brain metastases, several prognostic systems have been developed across various primary cancers. In 1997, Gaspar et al. introduced a recursive partitioning analysis (RPA) of patients with brain metastases enrolled in Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) studies, identifying performance status, primary tumor control, age, and presence of extracranial metastases as key predictors of survival [28]. Subsequently, the Graded Prognostic Assessment (GPA) was introduced and refined, incorporating variables such as performance status, number of brain metastases, extracranial disease burden, and molecular markers [29]. Diagnosis-specific GPA (DS-GPA) models are now widely used in cancers such as lung, breast, melanoma, and renal cell carcinoma [30–32]. However, no DS-GPA has been validated specifically for thyroid cancer, a gap largely attributable to the rarity of brain metastases in this disease.

The limited incidence of brain metastases in DTC, combined with wide heterogeneity in clinical presentation and treatment strategies, has restricted the availability of robust prognostic data. Current evidence is predominantly derived from small retrospective series with variable follow-up and inconsistent reporting of clinical, imaging, and molecular factors [3, 4, 7–27, 33]. As a result, survival prediction and treatment selection for DTC patients with brain metastases remain highly individualized and lack standardization.

To address these critical gaps, we conducted a large, single-institution retrospective cohort study across 10,306 patients with DTC to systematically evaluate prognostic factors in patients with DTC-related brain metastases and to develop a novel, simple and validated score, the Differentiated Thyroid Cancer GPA (DTC-GPA), to facilitate risk stratification and guide clinical management.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

We conducted a retrospective cohort study at The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center, a tertiary oncology institution. Institutional Review Board approval was obtained, with informed consent waived due to the study’s retrospective design. We included follicular cell–derived thyroid carcinomas on the differentiated-to-poorly differentiated spectrum (papillary, follicular, oncocytic/Hürthle cell, and poorly differentiated thyroid carcinoma). Medullary and anaplastic thyroid carcinomas were excluded due to distinct cellular origin and clinical behavior [34, 35]. Patients were excluded if they had (1) anaplastic or medullary thyroid carcinoma; (2) coexistent non-thyroid malignancies unless there was pathological confirmation that the brain metastasis originated from thyroid cancer; or (3) no radiologic or pathologic confirmation of brain metastasis. In all included cases, brain metastases were deemed attributable to thyroid cancer based on the treating multidisciplinary team’s assessment. Leptomeningeal disease without discrete intracranial lesions was not included.

Clinical data collection

Data were retrospectively extracted from electronic medical records. Collected variables included age at diagnosis of brain metastases, dichotomized at ≥55 years per AJCC 8th edition criteria; sex; and race/ethnicity. Performance status was documented using Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) scores and categorized as 0–1 versus ≥2. Tumor histology subtypes included papillary, follicular, Oncocytic (Hürthle Cell), and poorly differentiated thyroid carcinoma. The interval from thyroid cancer diagnosis to brain metastasis was assessed continuously and dichotomized at 3 years, informed by Osborne JR et. al study [36]. The presence and specific locations of extracranial metastases at the time of brain metastasis diagnosis were documented, including lung, liver, bone, mediastinum, adrenal glands, and other sites.

Imaging data collection

Imaging characteristics at the time of initial diagnosis of brain metastasis were retrospectively documented based on routine clinical MRI interpretations by board-certified neuroradiologists. Imaging features systematically recorded included the number of brain metastases (classified as single or multiple), maximum diameter of the largest lesion, and lesion characteristics such as T1/T2/FLAIR signal intensity, the presence of hemorrhage (defined by hyperintensity on T1-weighted imaging or susceptibility artifact on GRE/SWI), perilesional edema (hyperintensity on FLAIR or T2-weighted images), cystic components (non-enhancing fluid-filled regions), enhancement pattern, mass effect, midline shift (≥2 mm), herniation (subfalcine, uncal, or tonsillar), and hydrocephalus. The anatomical locations of brain lesions were documented by specific brain regions, including frontal, parietal, temporal, occipital lobes, cerebellum, basal ganglia/thalamus, brainstem, pituitary gland, intraventricular/choroid plexus, and dural-based/extra-axial sites.

Molecular profiling

Molecular profiling was conducted as part of routine clinical care using next-generation sequencing (NGS) assays. The analysis incorporated both primary tumor and metastatic tissue specimens when available. For survival analysis, genetic alterations were categorized as binary variables. Mutations in the BRAF gene were considered positive if they included known activating alterations such as V600E or K601E [37, 38]. TERT promoter mutations [39] and TP53 mutations encompassed any non-synonymous alterations, including both missense and truncating variants [40]. A composite variable for BRAF and TERT co-mutation was created to explore potential synergistic effects [40].

Outcomes and follow-up

The primary outcome measure was overall survival (OS), defined as the interval from brain metastasis diagnosis to death from any cause. Patients alive at the last clinical follow-up were censored. Survival status and dates of last contact or death were determined through both passive and active follow-up mechanisms maintained by the institutional cancer registry.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies with percentages and compared using the Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) and compared using the Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test.

Associations between clinical, imaging, and molecular variables and OS were first evaluated using univariable Cox proportional hazards regression models. Variables associated with OS at a 2-sided p value < 0.1 in univariable analysis were subsequently included in multivariable Cox regression models to identify independent prognostic factors. To evaluate the association between brain metastasis anatomical location and OS, additional Cox models were constructed adjusting for the number of brain metastases to account for the potential confounding effect of lesion number.

Statistical significance was defined as a 2-sided p value < 0.05. All analyses were performed using R statistical software, version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), with RStudio, version 2023.03.0 + 386.

Prognostic score development

A prognostic scoring system, termed the Differentiated Thyroid Cancer Graded Prognostic Assessment (DTC-GPA), was developed based on independent prognostic variables identified in multivariable analysis. The score assigned 1 point for each of the following favorable factors: ECOG performance status of 0–1, an interval of <3 years from initial thyroid cancer diagnosis to brain metastasis diagnosis, and presence of a solitary brain metastasis. Total DTC-GPA scores ranged from 0 to 3. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated to compare OS across DTC-GPA score groups, with OS probabilities at 12, 24, and 36 months reported. DTC-GPA score discrimination was assessed using the concordance index (Harrell C statistic). Time-dependent discrimination was summarized using the mean time-dependent AUC (mAUC) across 12, 24, and 36 months. Overall prediction error was summarized using the integrated Brier score (IBS) over 0–36 months.

External validation

To externally validate the DTC-GPA, we used individual published patient-level data extracted from published studies identified through a systematic review of the literature on thyroid cancer brain metastases. A comprehensive search of PubMed, Scopus, MEDLINE, and Web of Science (from inception to January 1, 2025) was performed to identify eligible studies reporting clinical characteristics and outcomes of patients with thyroid cancer and brain metastases. For the purpose of external validation in the present study, only cases with sufficient individual-level data to calculate the DTC-GPA score (including ECOG performance status, interval from primary thyroid cancer diagnosis to brain metastasis diagnosis, and number of brain metastases) were included. The score was calculated for each patient, and the concordance index (Harrell C statistic) was used to assess the discriminative performance of the DTC-GPA in the external validation cohort. Details about the external validation data are documented in Supplementary Appendix 1.

After exclusion of 45 cases attributed to a non-thyroid primary tumor, the final analytic cohort comprised 146 patients (Fig. 1). Variation by histologic subtype

Poorly differentiated carcinoma had the highest rate of multiple brain metastases (78%), whereas 80% of Hurthle-cell cases were solitary (p = 0.09). Bone metastases were universal in follicular carcinoma (100%) versus lower rates in papillary (49%), Oncocytic thyroid carcinoma (40%), and poorly differentiated (67%) disease (p < 0.001). Liver metastases were more common in follicular (24%) and poorly differentiated (28%) than in Hurthle-cell (0%) or papillary (8.8%) cases (p = 0.05). Papillary carcinoma exhibited higher rate of hemorrhagic brain metastases (30%) while follicular carcinoma had the lowest rate (4.8%; p = 0.03). (Table 2)

Overall survival and prognostic factors

The median OS for the entire cohort was 20.4 months. On univariable Cox regression analysis, factors associated with OS included ECOG ≥ 2 (HR, 2.69; 95% CI, 1.70–4.26; p < 0.001), presence of multiple brain metastases (HR, 2.58; 95% CI, 1.57–4.24; p < 0.001), and DTC to brain metastases interval ≥ 3 years (HR, 1.58; 95% CI, 0.91–2.76; p = 0.09). In multivariable analysis, ECOG ≥ 2 (HR, 2.67; 95% CI, 1.65–4.30; p < 0.001), multiple brain metastases (HR, 2.30; 95% CI, 1.39–3.82; p = 0.002), and DTC to brain metastases interval ≥ 3 years (HR, 1.84; 95% CI, 1.03–3.27; p = 0.03) remained independently associated with worse OS. Molecular alterations, including BRAF, TERT promoter, and TP53 mutations, were not significantly associated with OS (Table 3).

Among extracranial metastatic sites, metastases to the lungs (HR, 3.05; 95% CI, 1.11–8.36; p = 0.03) and bone (HR, 1.84; 95% CI, 1.14–2.97; p = 0.013) were independently associated with worse OS (Supplementary Table 1).

Regarding brain lesion location, metastases involving the pituitary gland (HR, 3.75; 95% CI, 1.33–10.6; p = 0.012) and the frontal lobe (HR, 1.59; 95% CI, 1.01–2.52; p = 0.046) were associated with poorer OS after adjusting for number of brain lesions (Supplementary Table 2).

External validation

External validation of the DTC-GPA was performed in an independent cohort of 83 patients. Discrimination remained consistent (Harrell C-index, 0.68; mAUC [12, 24, and 36 months], 0.71), with an IBS (0–36 months) of 0.23.

Discussion

The objective of this study was to identify clinical, radiographic, and molecular factors associated with OS in patients with brain metastases from DTC, and to develop and externally validate a DTC-GPA for prognostic risk stratification. This score is prognostic (survival estimation) rather than predictive of treatment benefit. Among over 10,000 DTC patients screened, 146 were found to have Brain metastases. We identified three independent factors associated with OS: performance status (ECOG), time from initial DTC diagnosis to Brain metastases, and number of brain lesions. These three factors formed the basis of the simple 3-point DTC-GPA score. The score stratified patients into distinct prognostic groups: those with a score of 2–3 (good ECOG, solitary Brain metastases, short interval) had a median OS of ~ 61 months, compared to only ~8 months in the poorest prognostic group (score 0). The DTC-GPA demonstrated strong prognostic discrimination in both derivation (C-index 0.70) and external validation cohorts (C-index 0.68).

These results both reinforce and extend prior observations from smaller studies. Performance status has consistently emerged as one of the strongest predictors of outcome in brain metastases across cancers [4, 36]. We confirmed that an ECOG ≥ 2 is associated with significantly lower survival, mirroring Henriques et al.’s report that DTC patients with a poor performance survived a median of only 3 months versus 27 months for those with good performance status [11, 36]. Additionally, patients with poor performance status often lack the physiological resilience to tolerate intensive treatments, such as neurosurgical resection [25] or SRS [41]. placing them at higher risk for perioperative complications [42]. Consequently, they are less likely to receive or benefit from potentially life-prolonging interventions.

Likewise, the number of brain metastases is a well-established prognostic factor [4, 36]. Choi et al., for example, identified ≤ 3 brain metastases as a good prognostic factor in DTC, alongside performance status and age [4]. In our study the number of brain metastases was dichotomized as single vs multiple, consistent with prognostic indices (GPA) and clinical guidelines, where single lesions often prompt local therapies and multiple lesions typically require systemic or palliative approaches [1].

The presence of extracranial metastatic disease has also been linked to worse survival in brain metastasis patients [4]. Consistently, we found that virtually all patients in our series had other distant metastases (92%), and those with lung or bone metastases had significantly shorter survival. This suggests that brain metastases in DTC usually signify disseminated, and aggressive disease and the mere presence of additional systemic metastases may be less prognostically informative. It is also possible that the near-ubiquity of extracranial metastases in our series limited our ability to discriminate based on this variable.

Interestingly, the time interval from initial thyroid cancer diagnosis to brain metastasis showed inverse association with OS, a counterintuitive finding also reported by Osborne et al. Patients in our study who developed brain metastases earlier (within 3 years of the primary cancer) actually survived longer after the metastasis than those with a very late-occurring brain lesion. Osborne et al. observed similarly that a shorter DTC-to-Brain metastases interval (<3 years) correlated with prolonged post-Brain metastases survival [36]. One hypothesis is that patients with rapid metastasis may have been younger or eligible for more aggressive therapies (or had vigilant surveillance leading to earlier intervention), whereas those with very delayed brain metastases might represent a subset with indolent but ultimately therapy-resistant disease, older age, or cumulative comorbidities, leading to worse outcomes once metastasis finally occurs. Additionally, our finding that metastases in the pituitary gland and frontal lobe independently predicted higher mortality is a novel observation. Prior studies have not specifically highlighted anatomical site of brain metastasis as a prognostic factor, likely due to limited sample sizes. It is plausible that pituitary involvement reflects particularly aggressive biology or causes endocrinologic and neurologic derangements that complicate treatment, prior studies across solid tumors show that pituitary metastases commonly result in clinically significant endocrine deficits and portend dismal prognosis (median survival often < 12 months) [43, 44]. Moreover, frontal lobe lesions may cause early cognitive decline affecting care.

To our knowledge, this is the largest and most detailed investigation of brain metastases across all subtypes of DTC, including Oncocytic thyroid carcinoma, a subgroup often minimally included in prior analyses. The high prevalence of extracranial metastases among patients who eventually developed Brain metastases suggests a potential role for more targeted CNS screening. Notably, all follicular thyroid carcinoma patients with brain metastases in our cohort had bone metastases prior to CNS involvement. This observation raises the possibility that bone metastases may serve as indicator for CNS spread in follicular thyroid carcinoma, warranting consideration of brain imaging in this subgroup. More broadly, DTC patients with any extracranial metastases, especially to bone or lung, may benefit from earlier CNS screening. While routine CNS imaging is not currently standard in DTC, our data support the development of risk-adapted screening approaches in high-risk individuals.

The DTC-GPA score offers a practical tool for stratifying patients into distinct prognostic categories. Patients with a score of 0 (ECOG ≥ 2, multiple BMs, and DTC-Brain metastases interval ≥ 3 years) had a median OS of only 7.7 months, while those with a score of 2–3 had a median OS exceeding 5 years. This wide survival gradient highlights the clinical utility of the score for guiding treatment intensity, informing discussions with patients and families, and may help in selecting patients for aggressive interventions such as neurosurgery, radiosurgery, or enrollment in clinical trials.

An important secondary finding of our study is that common molecular alterations in DTC, including BRAF mutations, TERT promoter mutations, and TP53 mutations, showed no significant association with OS. At first glance, this may seem surprising because BRAF V600E and TERT mutations are known to confer a more aggressive biology and poorer prognosis in primary thyroid cancer. However, our results align with those of Osborne et al., who found that while most DTC patients with brain metastases had a TERT promoter mutation, mutational status was not an independent predictor of survival [36]. There are several possible explanations. First, by the time a DTC has progressed to brain metastasis, it is typically refractory to radioactive iodine and has likely accumulated multiple genetic alterations, so any single mutation’s prognostic impact may be blunted. Second, the advent of targeted therapies may obscure the natural prognostic effect of these mutations. Patients with BRAF-mutant tumors, for instance, might receive BRAF/MEK inhibitors or multitarget TKIs, which can substantially alter outcomes and potentially improve survival regardless of the baseline mutation-driven risk. Another consideration, molecular testing was not uniformly available across all cases, potentially limiting statistical power for these analyses.

Limitations

We must acknowledge several limitations of our study. First, its retrospective design and single-center setting may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability. Our institution is a tertiary cancer center, which likely enriched our cohort with more advanced or refractory DTC cases; thus, the observed 1.4–1.8% prevalence of brain metastases (higher than the ~0.3–1% reported in general series) [3, 45] and the longer median survival (20 months, whereas older reports noted ~7–15 months) [45] might not directly translate to all practice settings. Patients treated here had broad access to MRI surveillance, high-quality neurosurgical care, SRS, and systemic therapy, which could improve outcomes compared to less-resourced settings. Second, despite the large base population, the absolute number of brain metastasis cases was still modest given the rarity of this condition. Third, our analysis of molecular data was constrained by the fact that not all patients underwent comprehensive genomic testing. Finally, although we performed an external validation of the DTC-GPA with published cases, the literature-derived data may be subject to reporting biases and variations in patient selection across studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study presents the first validated DTC-GPA for patients with brain metastases from differentiated thyroid carcinoma. The DTC-GPA score is a robust, simple tool for estimating survival and guiding treatment decisions. Our findings highlight the need for heightened clinical vigilance and potentially earlier brain imaging in DTC patients with high-risk features, such as lung or bone metastases. This work addresses a longstanding gap in the literature and provides a foundation for future research focused on early detection, tailored surveillance, and personalized treatment strategies in this rare but impactful manifestation of thyroid cancer.
