Trends of in vitro pharmacological potency and in vivo pharmacokinetics parameters of modern drugs: Can the therapeutic/subtherapeutic dose be estimated from in vitro K i and pharmacokinetic parameters?
Authors: Toshiaki Tsuchitani, Motohiro Kato, Atsuko Tomaru, Yasunori Aoki, Yuichi Sugiyama
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn drug discovery and development, estimating therapeutic and subtherapeutic doses is crucial for designing early‐phase clinical trials, particularly first‐in‐human (FIH) studies. While increasing the in vitro pharmacological potency (lowering K i) of a compound to its target is expected to decrease the therapeutic dose, its benefit is not necessarily clarified. We analyzed in vitro K i, human in vivo pharmacokinetics (PK) parameters, and therapeutics doses of 144 oral small‐molecule drugs approved in Japan (2010–2021). The data on classic drugs were obtained from Goodman and Gilman's textbook, 9th ed. (published in 1996). Modern drugs had lower K i (2.5 nM) and daily doses (88 μmol/day) than classic drugs (33 nM and 313 μmol/day), but 3.6‐fold higher intrinsic clearance (CLint; 171 vs. 47 L/h), suggest that increasing potency over the years has not resulted in a reduction in dosage as expected. Estimating therapeutic doses using target receptor occupancy (tRO)‐optimized approach improved estimation accuracy (63% within 10‐fold of observed values) compared with tRO‐fixed approaches. Subtherapeutic dose estimations revealed a risk of overdosing in FIH trials, indicating that these estimates are not necessarily as conservative as is generally understood. Notably, the unbound average concentration‐to‐K i ratio (C ave,u/K i) varied among drugs and correlated negatively with K i, suggesting the possible necessity of incorporating it into dose estimation methods. This study provides insights into the relationship between in vitro K i, in vivo PK parameters, and therapeutic dose of modern drugs, proposing strategies and revealing the risk for dose estimation and drug development in the era of highly potent small molecules.

Study Highlights WHAT IS THE CURRENT KNOWLEDGE ON THE TOPIC? Over the past decades, there has been a notable decrease in both in vitro K i values of low‐molecular weight drugs to the pharmacological target and its clinical doses. In the early stage of drug development, in vitro K i is often employed to estimate the range of doses for clinical trials, which should cover subtherapeutic and therapeutic doses. WHAT QUESTION DID THIS STUDY ADDRESS? What is the relationship between in vitro K i to the target, in vivo PK parameters, and therapeutic doses of low‐molecular weight drugs, and how much has increased potency contributed to lowering the dose? How well can we estimate the therapeutic and subtherapeutic doses from in vitro K i? WHAT DOES THIS STUDY ADD TO OUR KNOWLEDGE? Compared with classic drugs, modern drugs have high pharmacological potency and lower dose; however, lower biological stability in the human body. We are not genuinely benefitting from increasing potency in terms of dose; K i and therapeutic dose are not completely proportional, therefore, potentially a similar dose can be obtained for a drug with a higher K i. The unbound average concentration‐to‐K i ratio (C ave,u/K i) varied among drugs and correlated negatively with K i, indicating that it may need to be incorporated into dose estimation methods. The therapeutic dose can be roughly estimated from in vitro K i using our tRO‐optimized approach; however, we must be conscious of the over‐dosing risk, especially for subtherapeutic dose estimations which are necessary to design FIH or microdose studies. HOW MIGHT THIS CHANGE CLINICAL PHARMACOLOGY OR TRANSLATIONAL SCIENCE? Our matched dataset of in vitro K i and in vivo PK parameters may be utilized for the target product profile since increasing the potency over a certain extent is not necessarily beneficial in terms of decreasing the dose. Our dose estimation methods and results may facilitate efficient early‐phase clinical trial design by providing empirical evidence of dose estimation accuracy and safety factors.

INTRODUCTION

In the typical drug discovery and development process for small‐molecule drugs, the earliest parameters obtained in preclinical studies are the pharmacological potency parameter of compounds against their targets measured in vitro, such as dissociation constant (K d), inhibitory constant (K i) and 50% inhibition concentration (IC50) (hereafter collectively referred to as K i). It is generally understood that the higher the in vitro potency (the lower the K i), the greater the chance of avoiding off‐target effects since there will be less possibility of interacting with other targets. Furthermore, it is generally accepted that the drug concentration (particularly unbound concentration) approximately four times the K i is sufficient to achieve 80% target occupancy and exert adequate pharmacological effects. 1 Therefore, compounds with lower K i values are expected to have lower efficacious plasma concentrations (especially unbound concentrations), and ultimately, require lower therapeutic dose. Advances in drug design technologies such as quantitative structure–activity relationship models and target structure‐based drug design must have contributed to designing drugs with higher affinity. 2 , 3 In fact, Leeson et al. reported that both K i and dose of low‐molecular weight drugs have decreased over time. 4 Particularly for oral administration, increasing in vitro potency and reducing dose is an important strategy to avoid limitations in dose due to gastrointestinal solubility and to avoid potential off‐target toxicities such as gastrointestinal and hepatic toxicities.

The therapeutic and subtherapeutic doses must be estimated to design the dosage range for the first‐in‐human (FIH) trial (phase I), in which the ascending dose should start with subtherapeutic doses, and proof‐of‐concept (phase II) clinical trials where therapeutic dose must be given. Predicting the dose from in vitro potency data is one of the major approaches in the industry 5 ; therefore, it is our interest to investigate the accuracy of therapeutic/subtherapeutic dose estimation using in vitro potency data. Jansson et al. investigated the relationship between in vitro K i, average unbound plasma concentration (C ave,u), and dose for 164 small‐molecule drugs approved between 2010 and 2017. 6 They found that although in vitro K i correlated to some extent with C ave,u and dose, the ratio of K i to C ave,u or dose varied widely among drugs, making it difficult to predict them from K i. Kotani & Ito conducted a similar study in 2018, focusing on non‐cytotoxic anticancer drugs. 7 They found that for hematological malignancy drugs, there was a moderate correlation between C ave,u and K i. For anti‐solid tumor drugs, although the correlation was not as clear as for hematological malignancy drugs, there was still a moderate correlation between C ave,u and K i, with C ave,u tending to be significantly higher than K i. Based on these studies and the general theory of clinical pharmacology, it is quite reasonable to assume that the ratio of C ave,u to K i varies depending on the target and the indication.

These previous studies have only examined the correlation between K i and C ave,u or therapeutic dose. Even with the same K i, the ideal C ave,u/K i ratio may differ depending on the indication, and the dose should not be the same if the pharmacokinetic (PK) characteristics differ. Additionally, the potential to estimate subtherapeutic doses, which is critical in FIH trial design where over‐dosing must be avoided, has not been investigated. In this study, we first created a dataset with matched in vitro K i and in vivo PK of orally administered low‐molecular weight drugs recently approved in Japan (2010–2021), and the trends in the parameters were analyzed. Thereafter, therapeutic and subtherapeutic doses were estimated based on in vitro K i using a predictive and clinical pharmacology theory‐based approach.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data collection, parameter calculations, and data analysis

Orally administered small‐molecule drugs containing new active compounds approved in Japan between 2010 and 2021 were first collected. The data source was the “List of Approved Products” published by PMDA. 8 The following drugs were excluded: combination drugs, drugs with unclear target molecules, cytotoxic anticancer agents, prodrugs, endogenous compounds, non‐absorbable drugs, drugs for diagnosis, neutralizing or prophylactic purposes, drugs involving radiation irradiation, compounds for trace element supplementation, and anesthesia. Information on the selected drugs was collected from Interview Forms (IFs) issued by the manufacturer. The following information was collected from the IFs: logD (if not shown, logP), in vitro pharmacologic parameter to the target, therapeutic dose and number of doses per day, area under the plasma concentration–time curve from time zero to infinity (AUC) and maximum plasma concentration (C max) after a single dose administration to healthy adults in the fasted state (patients if not conducted in healthy adults), elimination half‐life (t 1/2), unbound fraction in plasma (f p), volume of distribution (V d), bioavailability (F), and fraction of unchanged drug excreted in urine (A e,urine). Average plasma concentration (C ave) was calculated as the AUC per dosing interval.

Oral clearance (CLpo), total body clearance (CLtot), renal clearance (CLr), hepatic clearance (CLh), hepatic intrinsic clearance (CLint, assuming well‐stirred model), protein concentration over dissociation constant (nPt/K d), and accumulation coefficient (R) were calculated from the following equations. (1) CLpo=Dose/AUC (2) CLtot=CLpo*F (3) CLr=Ae,urine/AUC (4) CLh=CLtot–CLr (5) CLint=QhCLh/Qh–CLh/fp (6) nPt/Kd=1–fp/fp (7) R=1/1−0.5τ/t1/2 where τ is the dosing interval time. Hepatic blood flow (Q h) and blood‐to‐plasma concentration ratio were assumed to be 96.6 L/h/body 9 and 1, respectively.

Therapeutic dose was defined as the dose before dose adjustment or the maintenance dose if any adjustment or escalation were instructed. In case when AUC and C max at the therapeutic dose were not evaluated, the AUC and C max data from the dose closest to the therapeutic dose was utilized and normalized to the therapeutic dose, assuming linear kinetics. The median value was used for f p when it was described as a range. Out of the in vitro pharmacological parameter (K d, K i, IC50, half maximal effective concentration (EC50), or minimal inhibitory concentrations against 50% (MIC50)) values described in the IFs, the lowest value was used. These parameters are treated without distinction and presented as K i in this article. The PK parameters (CLint, CLr/f p, dose, nPt/K d, V d/f p) were compared with the parameters of the drugs summarized in Goodman and Gilman's textbook, The Pharmacological Basis of Therapeutics, 9th ed. 10 (published in 1996). The distribution and correlation of each collected parameter to the K i were evaluated. The list of drugs collected, and their parameters are provided in the Supplemental Materials.

The collected drugs were classified into anticancer, antivirus (including hepatitis and HIV treatment drugs), agonist, and antibiotics (antimicrobial drugs) based on their indication and mechanism of action. Inhibitors that could not be classified into these groups were classified as “Antagonist”. The distribution of the collected parameters in each drug group and the correlation to K i were examined. The drugs were also classified based on the site of action and target protein as follows: antibiotics, blood (target in the systemic blood), cancer, central nerve system (CNS), dipeptidyl peptidase (DPP)‐4, factor Xa (FXa), histamine 1 receptor (H1), hypoxia‐inducible factor prolyl hydroxylase (HIF‐PH), urine (in case the drug was accessible to the target from the urine), and virus. Drugs that could not be classified into these groups were classified as others (intracellular/extracellular), depending on whether the drug can access the target from the intracellular space or extracellular space.

The receptor occupancy (RO) was approximated as the following equation, assuming the indicated K i refers to the unbound concentration. (8) RO=1/Ki/Cave,u+1

Distribution of RO in each drug group (antagonist, anticancer, antivirus, agonist, antibiotics) as well as in each category based on the target profile were compared.

Therapeutic and subtherapeutic dose estimation from in vitro K i

The estimated daily dose (Doseest) was determined from the estimated average therapeutic unbound concentration (C ave,u,est), which refers to the daily AUC (Equation 9). (9) Doseest=Cave,u,est/fp*24*CLpo C ave,u,est was back‐calculated from the K i and the target RO (tRO), the same relationship as presented in Equation (8). Observed values were utilized as the CLpo and fp.

The daily dose (μmol/day) was estimated for therapeutic dose estimation. The estimation accuracy was compared with the method of setting the tRO uniformly to 0.5 (Method A) or 0.8 (Method B), to the tRO‐optimized method (Method C). For method C, tRO for each drug category was selected by comparing the performance from tRO of 0.5, 0.8, 0.95, and 0.99. The tRO that yielded the highest proportion of estimates within 10‐fold of the observed values was selected for each drug group. For each estimation method, the number of drugs that fell within 3‐fold and 10‐fold error ranges, average fold error (AFE), and root mean squared error (RMSE) were compared. AFE and RMSE were calculated as the following Equations (10) and (11). (10) AFE=101n∑logDoseEstDoseObs (11) RMSE=1n∑logDoseEst−logDoseObs2 where DoseObs is the observed daily dose (μmol/day).

Two estimations were investigated for subtherapeutic dose estimation: microdose and minimum anticipated biological effect level (MABEL) dose. As for microdose, it is defined as 1/100 of the therapeutic dose or 100 μg if 1/100 of the therapeutic dose is above 100 μg. 11 The microdose was estimated from the therapeutic dose estimated by method C. The mass per dosing (mg per administration), calculated from Doseest (μmol/day) and the number of doses per day, was used as the reference of the microdose. The estimated microdose as a percentage of the actual therapeutic dose was compared with the observed value. The MABEL dose was estimated as well since it is an index for the dose range of FIH trials and it is also applied to low‐molecular weight drugs, although the background comes from safe dosing of biologics. MABEL dose was estimated as the daily dose which accomplish tRO = 0.1 (Equations 8 and 9), since 10% or below or 11%–20% of the maximum effect is often defined as the MABEL dose in the industry. 5 The percentage of the estimated MABEL dose to the actual dose was calculated and its distribution was examined.

RESULTS

A total of 144 drugs were collected in this study. The drugs were classified into antagonist (n = 76), anticancer (n = 47), antivirus (n = 6), agonist (n = 11), and antibiotics (n = 4). All collected parameters were distributed in log‐normal distribution (Figures S1 and S2). The mean and mean ± SD range (log distribution) of MW, K i, and daily dose of all drugs were 407 [296–559], 2.51 [0.167–37.6] (nM), and 87.6 [10.9–710] (μmol/day), respectively (Figure 1, Table S1). Anticancer, antivirus, and antibiotics drugs tended to have higher MW, logD, daily dose, and C ave,u/K i, and lower K i than other drug groups. Most drug groups had an average f pCLint of around 10 L/h, and 80% of the drugs were eliminated predominantly from the liver (Figure S3, Table S1). Compared with the PK parameters of the classic drugs presented in Goodman and Gilman's textbook, drugs collected in our study (modern drugs) tended to have higher CLint, lower dose, higher protein binding (nPt/K d), and higher V d/f p (Figure 2, Table S1). Plotting the C ave/C max ratio against t 1/2 showed a clear proportional relationship (Figure S4A). When the t 1/2 was classified based on the number of doses per day, the median t 1/2 was ~8 h and 9 h for drugs administered twice and thrice daily, respectively, while the median half‐life for drugs administered once daily was around 12 h (Figure S4B).

K i values significantly correlated with MW, logD, dose, C ave,u, nPt/K d, V d/f p, CLint, and CLpo/f p (Figure 3, Table S2). The slope of the regression line between K i and C ave,u was 0.512, which is closer to unity compared with the slope with C ave (0.298). The slope between K i and daily dose was 0.186. More than 69% of the drugs had an RO equal to or greater than 0.5 (Figure 4), with the mean ± SD of C ave,u/K i to be 4.15 [0.225–76.8], corresponding to RO of 0.806 [0.184–0.987] (Table S1). In the agonist group, only 36% of drugs had an RO of 0.5 or more, while more than 62% of drugs in the other groups had an RO of 0.5 or more. The anticancer and antivirus groups had a higher proportion of drugs with an RO of 0.9 or greater. Notably, there was a significant correlation between K i and Cave,u/K i (Figure 5a, Table S2). When the distribution of RO was compared based on the range of K i (less than 2 nM, 2 to 20 nM, and above 20 nM), the RO peaks were above 0.9 for Ki <2 nM and 2 ≤ Ki ≤ 20 nM (Figure 5b). In the group with Ki < 2 nM, 75% of the drugs had an RO above 0.7. Similarly, in the group with 2 ≤ Ki ≤ 20 nM, 62% of the drugs had an RO above 0.7. However, the distribution of RO for drugs with Ki > 20 nM was broad with no clear peak. When the C ave,u/K i were compared based on their targets, more than half of the drugs in the categories other than H1, HIF‐PH, and agonists had a C ave,u/K i greater than 1 (RO > 0.5). In contrast, more than half of the drugs targeting cancer, DPP‐4, FXa, and viruses had a C ave,u/K i greater than 4 (RO > 0.8). There was no clear difference between the target categories of others (intracell) and others (extracell) in terms of the distribution of C ave,u/K i (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we collected and analyzed the in vitro pharmacological potency and pharmacokinetic parameters of low‐molecular weight oral drugs approved in Japan over the past decade. It has long been recognized that increasing a compound's affinity to its target leads to higher MW and lipophilicity, 4 , 12 and that higher lipophilicity leads to higher CLint, protein binding, and V d/f p 13 , 14 Our survey also observed such tendencies (Figures 2 and 3, Tables S1 and S2). The relationship of these parameters is perhaps a paradox in drug development, that is, efforts to lower the K i lead to higher lipophilicity, which inevitably causes biological instability and worsens pharmacokinetic properties. Based on our data, a simple quantitative understanding is that the K i has decreased 13‐fold from 33 to 2.5 nM over the years (Table S1), which should contribute in reducing the efficacious concentration and dose 13‐fold as well if the C ave,u/K i and PK properties were similar. However, the dose has decreased only 3.6‐fold (classic drugs 313 vs. modern drugs 88 μmol/day; Figure 2). This gap may be explained by, at least in part, that the CLint increased 3.6‐fold (classic drugs 47 vs. modern drugs 171 L/h). In the context of compound optimization in drug development, since the relationship between K i and dosage is not strictly proportional, we cannot expect to “genuinely benefit” a proportional decrease in dosage by lowering the K i during compound optimization. These findings may facilitate compound selection strategies and optimization, for example, it may not be necessary to forcibly design or select compounds with affinity beyond a certain extent.

The common understanding that lipophilic compounds are more biologically unstable is just a general tendency with a large variability among drugs. 15 , 16 It is far too simplistic to conclude that as drug lipophilicity has increased over time, drugs have become more easily metabolized, leading to an increase in CLint, especially considering the progress in designing metabolically stable compounds. 17 Rather that metabolic stability, protein binding may be a confounding factor to the relationship between the K i and CLint since highly protein‐bound drugs possess albumin‐mediated uptake effect. This is a phenomenon in which the albumin–drug complex contributes to the drug uptake, therefore, the uptake clearance into the liver does not follow the free‐drug hypothesis. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 We may overcome this paradox of modern drug development by designing a drug with lower protein binding with the same affinity to the target. If not, we must search for an efficient balance of affinity and biological stability.

The properties of the drugs collected in this study remarkably followed the principles of pharmacokinetics and clinical pharmacology. K i showed a significant correlation with C ave,u, with a slope relatively close to unity, which was not the case for K i and dose (Figure 3, Table S2). This can be attributed to the relationship that drugs with lower K i tend to have higher CLint, requiring a higher dose than expected. Plotting the t 1/2 against C ave/C max revealed a clear correlation, and drugs with shorter t 1/2 to be administered more frequently (Figure S4). Several drugs with very short t 1/2 have been observed, where 23 drugs had a t 1/2 of 5 h or less. Such drugs would not accumulate after repetitive dosing and may even lack efficacy before the next dose. Looking in detail at the 23 drugs, 9 had a sufficiently high C ave,u/K i to maintain adequate trough concentrations, 4 had irreversible inhibition or active metabolites that may prolong the pharmacological effect beyond the plasma concentration, 3 were sleeping pills where somewhat short t 1/2 is preferable, and 1 was a PK booster. Most of the drugs had a justifiable reason for their short t 1/2 to be viable as a medication.

Looking at the distribution of RO in each drug group, it was found that 76.6% of anticancer drugs had an RO of 0.75 or higher, and all antiviral drugs had an RO of 0.75 or higher, clearly indicating a trend of higher RO and C ave,u/K i in these drug groups compared with others (Figure 4, Table 1). This is likely due to the disease characteristics, where viral infections (such as HIV and HBV) require constant and complete suppression of viral replication, regardless of PK variability among individuals. For anticancer agents, the maximum tolerable dose (MTD) is often set as the clinical dose, 22 and RO may not necessarily be used as an indicator for dose determination. Moreover, most anticancer drugs are multi‐kinase inhibitors, therefore, it is uncertain whether the most potently inhibited target is the “key target” for therapeutic efficacy. Additionally, dose reduction criteria are often set based on the severity of adverse effects. In fact, more than half of the anticancer drugs collected in this study allow for a dose reduction of 50% or more. This implies that a plasma concentration sufficient to occupy the target to a certain extent is necessary, even with dose reduction. Therefore, the relatively high RO observed in anticancer drugs was an expected result as well. Recently, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) announced Project Optimus, which sets forth a guideline to move away from MTD‐based dosing to exposure‐efficacy relationship‐based dosing for anticancer drugs. 23 If the core target kinase and the K i to those targets can be identified, our tRO‐optimized method to estimate the therapeutic dose may facilitate dose estimations and determinations. In actual practice of anticancer drug development, the difference in PK properties between healthy and patients must be considered since some phase I studies are conducted in healthy volunteers. 24 For agonists, more than half of the drugs had an RO of 0.5 or less, which is consistent with the general understanding that agonists have a mild RO rate.

When the drugs were classified based on the distribution of their target molecules and the RO was compared, RO was particularly skewed at 0.8 or higher for DPP‐4 and FXa inhibitors (Table 1). Both targets are circulating enzymes in the blood, and for the drugs classified into these categories, the enzyme activity in the blood after drug administration has been measured. For example, plasma DPP‐4 activity in human plasma after administration of linagliptin 25 and alogliptin 26 was observed to be above 80%, which is consistent with the RO approximated in this study. In the case of FXa inhibitors, there is a large discrepancy between the IC50 measured in the human plasma and in vitro using purified FXa; for apixaban, edoxaban, and rivaroxaban, the in vitro K i was 0.08, 0.561, and 0.4 nM, whereas the IC50 measured in plasma was 138, 394, and 100 nM (corrected to free concentration), respectively. 27 The complex cascade of blood coagulation, involving the interaction of numerous coagulation factors to each other 28 may be contributing to this discrepancy, however, the mechanism behind this is unclear. When examining whether there was a difference in RO based on whether the target molecule was located intracellularly or extracellularly, no clear difference in the distribution was found. This is consistent with the pharmacokinetic theory that the unbound drug concentration is equivalent among inside and outside the cell (with exceptions such as the liver) and suggests that the location of the target molecule, whether intracellular or extracellular, may generally not be an important factor.

Notably, when C ave,u/K i was plotted against K i, a negative correlation was observed (Figure 5a, Table S2). When the RO was organized as a histogram for each range of K i values, as shown in Figure 5b, drugs with K i ≤ 20 nM are skewed around RO = 0.9, while drugs with K i > 20 nM have a broad distribution with no peak in RO. This does not follow the basic principles of clinical pharmacology; the C ave,u/K i required for efficacy should remain constant regardless of the K i value. Several possibilities can be considered as factors contributing to this negative correlation. One is that drugs with larger K i are indications which lower RO is more ideal for treatment. Other factors such as dose limitation due to on‐target or off‐target toxicity or PK limitations such as gastrointestinal solubility may be related to the low C ave,u/K i for these drugs. Conversely, the high C ave,u/K i for drugs with low K i may be attributed to the advantage of avoiding off‐target effects due to high selectivity. The third possible factor is that the dose may be designed based on C max. Assuming linear pharmacokinetics, maximum unbound concentration at steady‐state (C max,u,SS) was approximated as the product of the C max,u after single dose and the accumulation factor, R. Comparing the RO estimated from C max,u,SS to those estimated from C ave,u, there were approximately 10 drugs where the RO estimated from C ave,u was below 0.5, but above 0.5 in case it was estimated from C max,u,SS (Figure S6). Therefore, the possibility that some dosages are designed based on peak concentration at steady‐state is quite plausible.

We attempted to estimate the clinical dose using the in vitro K i in a clinical pharmacology theory‐based approach. When the dose was estimated by setting the tRO individually for each drug group (method C), 63% of the total drugs had their dose estimated within a 10‐fold range of the observed values, improving the prediction accuracy compared with method A or B (Figure 6b, Table S3). Although method C is somewhat efficient for rough dose estimation, it will give greater dose estimation errors in actual practice since the PK parameters utilized in this study were all observed data in humans. In our dose estimation approach, we did not distinguish between the in vitro parameters. The various in vitro parameters (K d, K i, IC50, EC50, and MIC50) are not necessarily equivalent, and the value may depend on the experimental conditions or systems (e.g., IC50 value is not equal to K i when the ligand concentration is relatively high; EC50 may differ among cells due to different signal stimuli, etc.). Such simplification may be one of the reasons for the large variability in the dose estimation errors. However, establishing more specific prediction models for each parameter would be far more resource‐demanding and complex. It is worth noting here that CLint tends to underpredict especially for highly protein‐bound drugs, 29 which is a common feature of modern drugs. The result of method C also demonstrates the risk of this approach, as some drugs had their estimated dose to be 100‐ or 1000‐fold higher than the actual therapeutic dose. In our approach, we did not consider the correlation between C ave,u/K i and K i since there is no rational theory to incorporate it. Exploring and incorporating this factor may improve the dose estimation accuracy.

Microdose and intra‐target microdosing studies are conducted in subtherapeutic doses that should not exert pharmacological effects derived from systemic exposure. 11 , 30 If the therapeutic dose is 10 mg or exceeds 10 mg, the estimated microdose will not exceed 0.1 mg or 1% of the therapeutic dose. However, for drugs with a therapeutic dose of less than 10 mg, the estimated microdose have the risk of exerting pharmacological effects (Figure 6c). As modern drugs tend to have both lower K i and dosage, sufficient risk management is necessary when conducting microdose/intra‐target microdosing trials for new drug candidates with high‐affinity and unknown therapeutic doses. Ultimately, we may need a novel criterion to determine the microdose for such drugs. The same can be said for estimating the MABEL dose. MABEL is the minimum dose that exerts pharmacological effects, however, 17% of the drugs had a MABEL dose that was 35% or more of the therapeutic dose (Figure 6d). This does not align with the background that the MABEL approach aims to protect subjects from excessive exposure, or the general understanding that MABEL is a conservative approach. 5 , 31 Although MABEL is not the only approach for dose determination, this risk cannot be ignored since one‐third of MABEL dose predictions rely on in vitro parameters. In actual practice, safety factors may be applied to the MABEL method, therefore, our results may be used to determine the safety factor that is not too conservative.

In conclusion, in this study, we have collected and analyzed the in vitro potency of the target and in vivo PK parameters of recently approved orally administered low‐molecular weight drugs. If a method can be established to lower the K i while keeping CLint (and protein‐binding) below a certain level, it may be possible to create drugs that genuinely benefit from lowering K i. Our tRO‐optimized method was somewhat efficient for semi‐quantitative dose estimation; however, it highlighted the risk of over‐dosing even for subtherapeutic doses. Although there was very large variability among drugs in Cave,u/K i, notably, there was a significant correlation with K i. Incorporating such phenomenon into therapeutic dose estimation may facilitate more reliable dose estimation and efficient design of early‐phase clinical trials.
