Nanoparticle-Mediated Brain-Targeted Drug Delivery for Methamphetamine Use Disorder: Advances, Challenges and Translational Perspectives
Authors: Xinjie Zhang, Ruike Xu, Min Wang, Yan Du, Miao Zhang, Lihua Li, Genmeng Yang, Shijun Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMethamphetamine use disorder (MUD) poses a significant global health challenge, lacking FDA-approved pharmacotherapies. Despite extensive exploration of therapeutic targets and interventions, effective treatment remains scarce due to challenges in delivering therapeutic agents to relevant brain regions at effective concentrations. Nanoparticle-mediated delivery offers a promising approach for transporting various therapeutic cargos to the central nervous system, including small molecules, nucleic acids, peptides, proteins, and gene-editing systems. However, the application of nanomedicine strategies in addressing the unique pathophysiological characteristics of MUD has not been systematically assessed. This review presents a problem-focused analysis of how lipid-based, polymeric, inorganic, biological, and hybrid nanocarriers tackle successive delivery obstacles such as systemic stability, blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration, brain region-specific distribution, and controlled release of intracellular cargo. We compare preclinical nanotherapeutic approaches encompassing small-molecule neuroprotective agents, anti-METH vaccines, gene-silencing RNAs, neurotrophic proteins, and emerging gene-editing methods, outlining their respective benefits, limitations, and design considerations. Additionally, we explore key factors influencing nanomedicine performance in MUD, including protein corona formation, the impact of METH-related BBB dysfunction and neuroinflammation on nanoparticle distribution, and the disparity between acute animal models and chronic human MUD with relapse tendencies. In conclusion, we present a systematic framework for aligning nanocarrier characteristics with distinct delivery obstacles and therapeutic needs. We also emphasize forthcoming avenues such as disease-triggered nanocarriers, targeted delivery to specific regions, combined nanomedical approaches, theranostic strategies, and standardized assessments of biodistribution, effectiveness, safety, and translational potential.

Introduction

Methamphetamine use disorder (MUD) remains one of the most devastating and therapeutically neglected neuropsychiatric conditions worldwide in the 21st century. According to the 2024 UNODC World Drug Report, approximately 33 million individuals worldwide suffer from MUD, with a staggering >60% relapse rate within 1 year of abstinence.1 This chronic relapsing disorder lead to persistent neurobiological changes, neurotoxic effects, and neuropsychiatric disorders, while also imposing a significant socioeconomic burden.2–4 Despite decades of research identifying potential therapeutic targets, including dopamine receptors,5 dopamine transporters (DATs),6 vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2),7 serotonin receptors,8 and the identification of numerous promising candidate compounds, no pharmacotherapies have been specifically approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for MUD. Current treatment relies largely on behavioral interventions and off-label pharmacological approaches, with limited and variable efficacy in reducing methamphetamine (METH) use and craving.9–11 However, poor patient adherence, dose-limiting systemic toxicity, and the inability of most therapeutic molecules to cross the intact blood-brain barrier (BBB) render many otherwise effective preclinical candidates clinically unusable, improving the delivery of therapeutic agents to the brain, while maintaining adequate exposure and minimizing off-target effects, represents an important consideration in the development of new treatments for MUD.12–15

Efficient delivery of drugs to the central nervous system (CNS) involves a series of interconnected biological processes, rather than a singular barrier-crossing occurrence. Upon systemic introduction, drugs need to uphold chemical stability and systemic bioavailability to withstand metabolic breakdown and non-specific tissue retention.16,17 Subsequently, these drugs gather in the cerebral microvascular bed through blood circulation and eventually traverse the BBB to reach brain tissue. The BBB, formed by tight junction-sealed brain endothelial cells, pericytes, and astrocytic endfeet, constitutes the most formidable physiological barrier to neurotherapeutics, excluding >98% of small-molecule drugs and virtually all macromolecules from reaching therapeutic concentrations in the brain parenchyma.18,19 The mechanisms of drug passage across the BBB are dictated by their physicochemical characteristics, encompassing passive diffusion, carrier-mediated transport, receptor-mediated transcytosis, adsorption-mediated transport, and other modalities.20 Upon entering the brain tissue, drugs must further navigate the brain parenchyma and diffuse through the extracellular matrix to access the targeted anatomical sites.21 At the cellular level, successful delivery to the desired cell population relies on interactions with the surrounding cellular environment, cellular recognition and uptake, and subsequent intracellular trafficking. To achieve therapeutically effective concentrations in specific brain regions and cell types, drugs must exhibit appropriate distribution and favorable retention within local tissues to ensure adequate pharmacological efficacy.

Nevertheless, the BBB within the blood-brain barrier should not be viewed as a fixed obstacle. METH exposure has the potential to compromise BBB integrity and impact neurovascular function. Prolonged exposure is linked to enduring neuroinflammatory reactions characterized by activated microglia and reactive astrocytes.22,23 These pathological alterations can have intricate and situation-specific implications for drug delivery. While heightened BBB permeability might enhance the penetration of certain therapeutic agents into the brain, modifications in endothelial function, shifts in transport mechanisms, vascular inflammation, and changes in clearance pathways could concurrently affect their distribution and retention. Increased BBB permeability does not necessarily enhance the efficiency or precision of brain targeting.24 Furthermore, MUD is characterized by pathological alterations across distributed and interconnected neural circuits, including the nucleus accumbens, medial prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, and other areas implicated in reward processing, executive function, memory, and relapse-related behaviors.25–27 Variations in BBB properties, vascular characteristics, extracellular matrix composition, receptor expression, metabolic state, and inflammatory activity among these regions may lead to heterogeneous drug distribution and therapeutic responses. The persistent activation of microglia and astrocytes, along with altered cytokine signaling, oxidative stress, and modifications in phagocytic and lysosomal activity, may further influence the local processing of therapeutic agents once they penetrate the brain.28,29 These disease-related changes underscore the necessity of considering the pathological brain environment when designing and assessing targeted drug delivery strategies for MUD.

The intrinsic characteristics of therapeutic candidates can impede their delivery process. Peptides, proteins, and nucleic acids may degrade rapidly due to enzymes and have limited systemic stability. Conversely, some small-molecule candidates may suffer from poor aqueous solubility, quick metabolism, limited bioavailability, or insufficient CNS exposure.30,31 These constraints might necessitate frequent or high-dose administration, potentially heightening systemic adverse effects and compromising treatment adherence. Traditional systemic administration also offers limited control over the spatial and temporal distribution of therapeutic agents, making it challenging to selectively enhance drug exposure in disease-relevant brain regions while minimizing exposure elsewhere. Although viral vectors are commonly utilized for experimental circuit manipulation and gene delivery, they encounter obstacles for systemic therapeutic applications, such as constrained passage across the BBB under normal conditions, immunogenicity, cargo limitations, and difficulties with controlled and repeated administration.32,33 These challenges have sparked significant interest in utilizing nanomaterials as alternative delivery platforms for addressing neuropsychiatric disorders.

Nanoparticle (NP) delivery systems offer customizable physicochemical properties such as particle size, surface charge, composition, morphology, and surface functionality, enabling the encapsulation, conjugation, or transportation of various therapeutic cargos.34–36 These properties can be tailored to overcome specific challenges in drug delivery, such as systemic instability, limited BBB transport, suboptimal brain distribution, inadequate cellular uptake, and premature intracellular degradation or release. Nanocarriers also present opportunities to integrate therapeutic cargos with targeting or responsive features, enhancing the spatial, temporal, or pharmacokinetic aspects of drug delivery (Figure 1). Increasing preclinical evidence indicates that strategically engineered nanoparticle platforms can enhance the delivery and pharmacological efficacy of therapeutic agents in the CNS and influence disease-related characteristics in various substance use disorders (SUDs), including MUD (Table 1). It is crucial to note that the benefits of nanomedicine extend beyond improved BBB penetration. The therapeutic effectiveness of a nanocarrier relies on how well its physicochemical and biological attributes address the pertinent barriers throughout the delivery process and how these attributes interact with the pathological milieu of MUD. Core challenges in conventional methamphetamine use disorder (MUD) treatment, limitations of viral vector systems, and transformative advantages of nanoparticle-mediated delivery for MUD therapy. Conventional therapies are hindered by a restrictive blood‒brain barrier (BBB), lack of spatial/cellular specificity, and poor pharmacokinetic properties, whereas viral vectors suffer from the inability to cross the intact BBB, transient transgene expression, and immune responses. Nanoparticle systems overcome these barriers through intact BBB traversal, universal cargo protection, spatiotemporally controlled drug release, precise targeting, and favorable biocompatibility.

Despite increasing interest in nanomedicine for MUD, the current literature on nanotherapeutics for MUD remains largely descriptive and incremental: most studies merely catalog which nanocarrier was used with which drug and whether a single behavioral endpoint was improved. Missing from nearly all reports are critical comparative analyses that are essential for clinical translation: which delivery platform is most suited for a given therapeutic modality? How does METH-induced pathology quantitatively alter the fraction of the injected dose that reaches target cells? Can long-term biosafety be guaranteed in a brain already compromised by chronic substance abuse? These fundamental gaps have prevented the field from identifying clear frontrunners for clinical development.

This review provides a systematic and critical overview of state-of-the-art brain-targeted nanotechnologies for MUD treatment. We first compare the design principles, performance characteristics, and preclinical efficacy of five major nanocarrier classes: lipid, polymeric, inorganic, biologically derived, and hybrid NPs. We then discuss the diverse therapeutic modalities enabled by nanotechnology, including small-molecule neuroprotection, nanoparticle vaccines, nucleic acid gene silencing, peptide/protein replacement, and CRISPR-based gene regulation. Finally, we dissect the critical challenges hindering clinical translation and propose actionable future directions to accelerate the development of effective nanotherapeutics for MUD.

Brain-Targeted Nanoparticles

The efficacy of a brain-targeted nanocarrier relies not on a single physicochemical factor but on the interplay of various attributes, encompassing particle size, composition, morphology, surface charge, surface chemistry, cargo-loading properties, and release kinetics (Figure 2). These attributes impact circulation stability, interactions with the neurovascular unit, BBB permeation, extracellular diffusion, cellular internalization, intracellular trafficking, and eventual cargo liberation. It is crucial to differentiate between the pathways through which nanoparticles access the brain and the therapeutic outcomes that indirectly imply successful CNS delivery. Direct proof, such as quantitative brain accumulation, regional distribution within the brain, or cellular localization, offers more robust evidence of brain targeting compared to mere behavioral or molecular enhancements. Presently, due to the limited availability of direct comparative studies among different nanocarrier categories in models of MUD, the subsequent sections will contrast platforms based on their physicochemical attributes, proposed or validated mechanisms for brain entry, targeting efficacy, compatibility with therapeutic payloads, and relevance to MUD. Lipid Nanoparticles

Lipid-based nanoparticles constitute a well-established and rapidly expanding class of biomimetic nanocarriers, defined by their lipid-dominant composition and characteristic size range of 10–1000 nm.48 Including liposomes, solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs), and nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs) are among the most extensively investigated nanocarrier systems for CNS drug delivery.49–51 Adjusting physicochemical properties of lipid nanoparticles can be achieved by modifying lipid composition, particle size, surface charge, and incorporating functional lipids, enabling customization for different therapeutic cargos. These nanoparticles are versatile, accommodating both lipophilic and hydrophilic compounds while safeguarding encapsulated cargos from premature degradation.52 The fusion-competent lipid bilayer facilitates cellular uptake and endosomal escape, with the efficacy of the latter heavily reliant on formulation composition.53 Lipid nanoparticles for brain delivery can traverse the BBB through various mechanisms, such as interactions with BBB endothelium, transcellular transport, and, when necessary, ligand-mediated transport post surface modification. Hence, surface functionalization serves as a crucial design strategy rather than an inherent trait of all lipid nanoparticles. Integration of targeting ligands or peptides can enhance specific interactions with BBB-associated receptors or cellular subsets. In the realm of MUD, these attributes are particularly beneficial for enhancing the transport of poorly soluble small molecules or biologically active cargos (eg, mRNA vaccines) with limited systemic stability or brain penetrance. Nonetheless, lipid-based systems may exhibit instability in biological fluids depending on the formulation, and loading efficiency can significantly fluctuate based on the physicochemical properties of the cargo.54 Therefore, the potential advantages of lipid nanoparticles in MUD necessitate comprehensive evaluation alongside empirical evidence of actual brain accumulation and, if applicable, regional or cellular localization.

Polymeric Nanoparticles

Synthetic polymeric systems, including polyethyleneimine (PEI), polylactic acid (PLA), polyethylene glycol (PEG)-containing formulations, poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), polycaprolactone (PCL), and dendrimers, offer substantial flexibility in controlling particle composition, degradation behavior, surface chemistry, and cargo encapsulation.55,56 These attributes are especially beneficial for cargoes necessitating sustained protection or controlled release. PLGA, an FDA-approved biodegradable polymer for parenteral applications, stands out as a primary platform for CNS delivery. The ability of polymeric nanoparticles to penetrate the BBB is contingent upon factors such as particle size, surface characteristics, formulation composition, and route of administration. Enhancing transcytosis is significantly achieved through surface modification with cell-penetrating peptides (eg, TAT) or BBB receptor ligands (eg, transferrin).19,57–59 However, the mere presence of a targeting ligand should not be equated with successful BBB penetration; direct measurements of brain transport or accumulation are imperative to validate targeting effectiveness. A notable drawback of systemically administered polymeric nanoparticles is their sequestration by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPs) and the reticuloendothelial system (REs) during circulation, leading to a decrease in the fraction of the administered dose available for brain delivery.60–63 Therefore, surface engineering approaches, aimed at mitigating nonspecific protein adsorption or enhancing circulation stability, play a crucial role in augmenting brain exposure. In the context of METH-associated pathological conditions, the interplay between prolonged circulation, BBB permeation, biodegradability, and systemic clearance warrants further investigation to determine their optimal balance.

Inorganic Nanoparticles

Inorganic nanoparticles, including gold nanoparticles (AuNPs),64,65 superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs),66 and carbon nanotubes,67 exhibit unique optical, magnetic, and electronic properties that significantly differ from those of lipid and polymeric systems.68,69 Their relatively stable inorganic cores and modifiable surfaces facilitate high-density functionalization with targeting ligands or therapeutic agents.70 In certain formulations, factors such as particle size, surface charge, and surface chemistry can affect interactions with the BBB and cellular uptake, AuNPs have been shown to demonstrate size-and charge-dependent brain transport.71 A notable potential advantage of inorganic systems lies in their capacity to combine therapeutic delivery with imaging or externally controlled functionalities. For instance, magnetic nanoparticles can be manipulated using external magnetic fields, while other inorganic materials offer optical or imaging-related capabilities. Nonetheless, these functional benefits must be weighed against concerns regarding biodegradation, tissue retention, and long-term toxicity.72 Such considerations are particularly pertinent to MUD, as therapeutic interventions may necessitate repeated administration. Furthermore, it is essential to differentiate evidence of brain accumulation from therapeutic outcomes, especially when evaluating inorganic nanomaterials in models where behavioral improvement serves as the primary endpoint.

Biologically Derived Nanocarriers

Biologically derived nanocarriers, such as exosomes and cell membrane-coated nanoparticles, offer an alternative strategy for brain delivery.73 These carriers contain biological membrane components that can enhance interactions with target cells and, depending on their source and surface properties, facilitate passage across biological barriers. Exosomes derived from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) harbor intrinsic neuroprotective cargo and can be modified to exhibit specific targeting molecules.46,47 Their natural origin may provide benefits in terms of biocompatibility and cellular affinity; however, these attributes come with significant challenges in terms of manufacturing, purification, cargo incorporation, characterization, and consistency between production batches.47,74–76 In the context of MUD, exosome-based platforms have demonstrated promise in transporting compounds like cannabidiol, yet the existing body of evidence is limited and varies significantly in terms of administration method, cargo loading efficiency, and assessment of brain distribution. Therefore, while acknowledging their potential advantages, it is essential to also address the necessity for standardized manufacturing processes and direct assessment of brain-targeting capabilities.

Multicomponent Hybrid Nanoparticles

Hybrid nanoparticles, which combine two or more material components, integrate complementary physicochemical and biological properties in a single delivery system. Instead of being inherently better than single-component systems, hybrid formulations offer added design flexibility by merging features like structural stability, cargo loading, membrane interaction, immune evasion, and surface functionalization. This strategy is especially relevant in drug delivery when optimizing systemic stability, blood-brain barrier transport, and controlled cargo release simultaneously is necessary. An instance is the Ang-RBCm@RSV biomimetic composite platform, embedding resveratrol in a PCL-PEG core and utilizing an erythrocyte membrane modified with angiopep-2.40 The membrane coating aims to decrease nonspecific clearance, while angiopep-2 facilitates interaction with LRP1-associated blood-brain barrier transport pathways.74 In a METH-induced CPP mouse model, this formulation led to a reported 4.2-fold increase in brain accumulation compared to free resveratrol, resulting in reduced CPP scores and enhanced synaptic plasticity.34 This research offers compelling evidence linking nanocarrier engineering to increased brain accumulation and therapeutic outcomes, yet it does not assert the universal superiority of hybrid nanoparticles over other nanocarrier types. Instead, it demonstrates the integration of multiple engineering functions in a unified platform. Further investigations employing standardized drug delivery models and direct comparisons with alternative nanocarrier systems are essential to ascertain whether such multifunctional designs consistently offer an advantage.

Natural Small-Molecule Drugs in Preclinical Methamphetamine Use Disorder (MUD) Studies

Resveratrol (RSV)

Resveratrol is a polyphenolic compound with well-documented antioxidant and neuroprotective properties. Free resveratrol has extremely low bioavailability (<1%) because of its poor solubility and rapid metabolism.114–116 Encapsulation in the Ang-RBCm@RSV hybrid system led to a 4.2-fold increase in brain accumulation compared to free resveratrol, correlating with a reduction in METH-induced CPP.40 This study, however, did not quantify the proportion of the administered dose internalized by distinct brain cell subtypes or elucidate the connection between intracellular release and treatment efficacy. Therefore, enhanced brain accumulation indicates enhanced central nervous system exposure but does not alone confirm targeted delivery to specific cells.

Cannabidiol (CBD)

Cannabidiol has emerged as the most extensively studied natural small molecule for MUD treatment. Its unique pharmacological profile—including anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective, and anxiolytic properties-makes it particularly well suited for addressing the multifactorial pathology of METH addiction.117,118 Preclinical studies have consistently demonstrated that CBD reduces METH seeking and relapse by modulating neuroinflammatory pathways and restoring synaptic homeostasis.80,119 Despite these promising effects, the clinical translation of CBD is hindered by its poor pharmacokinetic properties: oral bioavailability is only ~6% because of extensive hepatic metabolism, and its lipophilic nature limits BBB penetration.120 Compared with free CBD, the exosomal formulation HP-Exo-CBD-TAT, administered intranasally, increased brain CBD levels 7.3-fold compared to free CBD and reduced METH-induced neuroinflammation and sensitization. The hypoxic exosome carrier also contributes to intrinsic anti-inflammatory activity, providing synergistic effects.47 Yet, exosome loading efficiency for lipophilic CBD was modest (~15%), and intranasal delivery, while noninvasive delivery, introduces significant interanimal variability in brain deposition.

Curcumin

Curcumin, another widely studied natural antioxidant, has also benefitted from nanoformulation. While free curcumin has been shown to be effective in preclinical models of various neurological disorders,121–124 its poor solubility and rapid metabolism limit its delivery to the CNS. Encapsulation in SLNs significantly improved the BBB penetration ability and neuroprotective efficacy of curcumin. With respect to MUD, titanium dioxide nanowire-mediated curcumin delivery not only protected against METH-induced BBB disruption and neuronal apoptosis but also restored dopamine and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) levels in the nucleus accumbens, resulting in therapeutic outcomes superior to those of free curcumin.41 The reported therapeutic effects are consistent with improved CNS delivery, but whether the formulation preferentially accumulated in METH-affected regions requires direct regional biodistribution studies.

“Drug-Free” Nanomedicine

Aggregated single-walled carbon nanotubes (aSWNTs), by binding and oxidizing extracellular dopamine, directly suppressed METH-induced dopamine elevation and drug-seeking behavior.39 While innovative, the intracerebroventricular route used precludes clinical translation, and the long-term fate of aSWNTs in brain tissue remains unaddressed.

Collectively, these studies demonstrate that nanocarriers can overcome the inherent pharmacokinetic limitations of natural compounds. However, a systematic head-to-head comparison of different delivery systems-evaluating brain biodistribution, target cell uptake, and dose-response in the same MUD model-is urgently needed to identify optimal formulations for clinical development.

Nanovaccine Immunotherapy

Nanovaccine strategies are unique applications of nanotechnology as they primarily rely on peripheral immune responses instead of directly delivering therapeutic cargo to the brain. The development of METH vaccines has faced challenges related to the immunogenicity and structural features of the METH hapten, as well as the complexity of eliciting robust and long-lasting antibody responses.125–127 Utilizing nanoparticle formulations could enhance vaccine efficacy by enabling multivalent antigen presentation, precise spatial arrangement of hapten-carrier conjugates, co-administration of adjuvants, and prolonged antigen stimulation.128,129

Enhanced Hapten Density and Spatial Control

Multivalent display on liposomes or PLGA nanoparticles enables high-density, properly oriented METH epitopes that drive robust B-cell receptor clustering.126,130,131 This directly increases antibody affinity and specificity.

Codelivery of Adjuvants

Toll like receptor (TLR) agonists can be coencapsulated within the same nanoparticle, ensuring synchronized activation of antigen-presenting cells and a Th1-biased response.132,133

Sustained Release

PLGA matrices provide antigen depot effects, mimicking natural infection and promoting germinal center formation and memory B-cell generation.134 A single injection could provide protective antibody titers for months.

These characteristics highlight a significant benefit of nanotechnology beyond BBB penetration: nanoparticles have the capability to manipulate the pharmacological and immunological environment for presenting a therapeutic antigen. Nevertheless, the advancement of METH nanovaccine is currently limited to preclinical phases. The translation of heightened antibody reactions into substantial protection against recurring or intense METH exposure is still ambiguous. Additionally, the applicability of vaccination approaches to individuals with multiple substance use disorders, as well as the enduring effectiveness and safety of the immune response elicited, necessitate additional assessment.

Nucleic Acid

Nucleic acid therapeutics, such as siRNA, miRNA, mRNA, and related gene-regulatory strategies, offer precise modulation of disease-associated gene expression.135 Nonetheless, unmodified nucleic acids typically undergo quick degradation and have restricted tissue penetration, posing a significant obstacle for CNS applications. While modulation of dopaminergic and addiction-related gene networks in MUD has demonstrated therapeutic promise in preclinical investigations, achieving effective and targeted delivery to specific brain regions remains a primary constraint.

Gold Nanorods

Bonoiu et al and Ignatowski et aldemonstrated that DARPP-32 siRNA complexed with gold nanorods induced prolonged gene silencing in the rat brain and reduced morphine withdrawal symptoms.38,136 This landmark study highlights the ability of inorganic carriers to protect siRNA and mediate endosomal escape via the proton-sponge effect. However, these studies do not establish that systemically administered nanoparticles can selectively reach MUD-relevant brain regions and the heavy metal core remains a long-term toxicity concern for human use.

Polymeric and Lipid Nanoparticles

Polymeric and lipid nanoparticles can provide protection from nucleases and offer opportunities for surface functionalization. Peptide-functionalized (RVG, Tet1) nanoparticles have been used to target neurons via nicotinic receptors,137 although the cited proof-of-concept evidence is largely derived from models outside MUD.138 In MUD, where the target is discrete reward circuitry, achieving region-specific delivery following systemic administration is still an unsolved challenge.

Exosomes

Native exosomes carry their own miRNA cargo, offering a unique natural delivery mechanism, but engineering them to carry specific therapeutic nucleic acids while maintaining their ability to penetrate the BBB is still in its infancy.139

Nanocarriers collectively present a significant technological opportunity for nucleic acid therapeutics through the integration of cargo protection, surface engineering, and intracellular delivery. However, there is limited direct evidence supporting systemic, region-selective, and cell-selective nucleic acid delivery in MUD.

Peptides/Proteins

Peptide and protein therapeutics have emerged as a promising class of biologics for the treatment of MUD. These molecules, which include neuropeptides and neurotrophic factors, directly modulate neuronal signaling and promote neural repair. Notably, oxytocin (OT),140 glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF),141 and BDNF142 have been shown to reverse METH-induced neuronal damage, attenuate neuroinflammation, and modulate addiction-related behaviors. However, their clinical potential remains largely untapped because of two fundamental limitations: rapid enzymatic degradation in the systemic circulation and inability to cross the intact BBB. Nanoparticle can potentially address these limitations by protecting biologics during circulation, prolonging exposure, and facilitating brain delivery.

Oxytocin(OT)

OT modulates activity in the mesocorticolimbic reward circuit and amygdala, reducing METH craving, withdrawal symptoms, and relapse vulnerability.143–145 The therapeutic potential of OT has been validated in two clinical trials, where adjunctive OT treatment combined with group psychotherapy improved treatment retention and significantly reduced craving and depression scores in MUD patients.146,147 Despite these promising results, the clinical utility of native OT is severely limited by its short plasma half-life (~3 minutes) and poor BBB permeability. To address these challenges, a dual-targeted BSA nanoparticle system functionalized with transferrin and RVG peptides was developed, which result in a 5.8-fold greater accumulation of OT in the brain than free OT did.148 This discovery offers conclusive proof that the manipulation of nanocarriers can enhance the brain’s exposure to a therapeutic peptide. Nevertheless, further investigation is needed to elucidate whether this heightened exposure is primarily due to BBB transcytosis, modified pharmacokinetics, or other delivery mechanisms, and the supporting data for targeted delivery to specific regions or cells is currently scarce.

Neurotrophic Factors

Neurotrophic factors represent another important class of protein therapeutics for MUD. GDNF and BDNF play essential roles in neuronal survival, differentiation, and synaptic plasticity, and their expression is dysregulated following chronic METH exposure.149 While viral vector-mediated delivery of GDNF to the striatum has been shown to reduce METH-induced drug-seeking behavior,150,151 the risk of insertional mutagenesis and immune responses associated with viral vectors has limited its clinical translation. In contrast, nanoparticle-based delivery systems offer safer alternatives. Compared with free BDNF, the conjugation of BDNF to magnetic nanoparticles enables efficient BBB penetration in vitro and enhances dendritic spine formation.152 Similarly, maghemite nanoparticle-mediated GDNF delivery to the striatum significantly reduced cocaine self-administration in rats without altering natural reward behaviors,37 demonstrating the specificity and safety of this approach. However, in vivo, the applied magnetic fields required for brain accumulation have limited tissue penetration, restricting this approach to superficial brain structures unless combined with focused ultrasound or other physical BBB modulations.

In summary, nanoparticle carriers address the core limitations of peptide and protein therapeutics by protecting them from degradation, prolonging their circulation, and enabling targeted delivery to the brain. These advances have opened new avenues for the development of effective biologic therapies for MUD, while the extent of systemic brain targeting in MUD remains to be established.

Gene Editing Technology

Gene-editing techniques hold promise for altering molecular pathways associated with addiction, necessitating the delivery of large and intricate nucleic acid or protein components for therapeutic purposes. While AAV-based methods are commonly utilized for experimental gene editing, their application for CNS delivery is limited by challenges such as BBB penetration, cargo capacity, and safety concerns.153–155 Nanoparticle-mediated delivery presents a potential solution, as nanocarriers can safeguard nucleic acids or ribonucleoprotein payloads and be tailored for intracellular release. For instance, CRISPR-Gold facilitated mGluR5 modification through intracranial injection, leading to behavioral changes in addiction models.156 Nevertheless, this approach has not demonstrated systemic BBB traversal or region-specific targeting. Similarly, the use of lipid nanoparticles for systemically administering CRISPRa/i to distinct addiction-related neuronal subtypes is still an evolving rather than established method.157 Thus, the primary advantage of nanotechnology in gene editing lies not only in protecting cargos but also in orchestrating BBB transport, tissue dispersion, cellular absorption, endosomal liberation, and regulated intracellular release. Progressing through these stages systematically in relevant disease models is imperative to evaluate the therapeutic viability of systemic nanoparticle-mediated gene editing.

Critical Translational Bottlenecks and MUD-Specific Determinants

The Translational Model Disconnect

Most preclinical studies on nanodelivery for MUD typically utilize acute or short-term METH paradigms, such as binge-like exposure and CPP models.158 In contrast, human MUD involves repeated cycles of exposure, abstinence, craving, and relapse,159 potentially impacting various factors like BBB integrity, inflammatory response, vascular function, receptor expression, and drug kinetics, which could in turn affect the behavior of nanoparticles. Additionally, polysubstance abuse is prevalent among individuals with substance use disorders, whereas preclinical nanodelivery research often focuses on single-drug scenarios.160 Therefore, it is imperative to incorporate more clinically relevant models, such as chronic self-administration, withdrawal, relapse, and polysubstance paradigms, to assess the durability of nanocarrier efficacy under conditions that more closely mimic human MUD.

METH-Associated BBB Dysfunction and Nanoparticle Delivery

Dysfunction of the BBB induced by METH is a crucial yet not fully understood factor affecting the delivery of nanomedicine. METH exposure in the short term can compromise BBB integrity,161 leading to increased permeability that may enhance the penetration of certain therapeutic agents or nanoparticles. However, heightened permeability does not guarantee efficient or specific brain targeting. Changes in endothelial function, tight-junction arrangement, transcytosis, vascular inflammation, extracellular matrix characteristics, and clearance pathways collectively impact the transportation and retention of nanoparticles.162,163 Consequently, the outcomes could be two-fold. While increased permeability might elevate the proportion of circulating substances reaching the brain, alterations in the neurovascular unit due to pathological conditions could result in non-uniform distribution or increased uptake by non-target cells.164 Simultaneously, activated astrocytes and microglia could influence the uptake, breakdown, and elimination of nanoparticles.165,166 The overall impact on the bioavailable therapeutic fraction in the brain remains unclear. This ambiguity directly influences the design of carriers. Instead of solely focusing on enhancing BBB permeability, forthcoming systems should strive for regulated transport that ensures predictable regional spread and suitable retention. Chronic METH exposure models will be especially crucial in assessing whether targeting approaches developed in healthy animals remain efficacious in the METH-affected neurovascular milieu.

Regional Heterogeneity and Spatial Distribution

Addiction-related brain circuits involve interconnected regions such as the nucleus accumbens, prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, and striatum.167 Disparities in vascular, extracellular, metabolic, and inflammatory conditions among these areas may result in non-uniform effects of METH exposure. Therefore, assessing whole-brain nanoparticle accumulation may not accurately indicate if a therapeutic payload reaches the specific brain region expressing its molecular target. It is crucial to differentiate regional delivery from overall brain permeation to ensure targeted drug distribution. Enhancing total brain exposure does not guarantee selective accumulation in key regions like the nucleus accumbens, prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, or striatum. Likewise, regional accumulation does not confirm delivery to the pertinent cellular populations. Future studies on MUD nanomedicine should incorporate quantitative regional biodistribution analysis, along with cellular localization when applicable, as a standard practice. This becomes especially critical for treatments targeting specific molecular entities or neural pathways. Evaluation of nanocarrier design should focus on whether its physical and chemical attributes, as well as targeting approaches, achieve adequate exposure at the desired site while minimizing exposure to irrelevant brain areas and peripheral tissues.

Chronic Neuroinflammation and Nanoparticle Fate

Chronic neuroinflammation can alter the behavior of nanoparticles upon entering the brain. Sustained activation of microglia and astrocytes can impact various cellular processes such as phagocytosis, lysosomal function, inflammatory pathways, and tissue metabolism.168 These alterations may affect how nanoparticles are absorbed, transported inside cells, broken down, and eliminated, ultimately influencing the concentration of therapeutic substances reaching the target site. It is crucial to recognize that nanoparticle dynamics in an inflamed brain may deviate from those in a healthy one. Surface modifications that reduce nonspecific uptake in normal conditions may respond differently in an inflammatory milieu due to changes in plasma proteins, extracellular matrix components, and cellular responses that can modify nanoparticle interactions.169,170 Therefore, the inflammatory environment associated with neurodegenerative disorders should be viewed not only as a consequence of disease but also as a factor influencing the effectiveness of nanocarriers. Currently, there is limited direct evidence linking chronic exposure to methamphetamine, neuroinflammation, nanoparticle distribution, and processing of therapeutic cargo. Research combining nanoparticle tracking with assessments of microglial and astrocytic uptake, spatial dispersion, and availability of therapeutic cargo is necessary to elucidate these connections.

The Protein Corona and Targeting Performance

Following systemic administration, nanoparticles swiftly engage with plasma proteins to establish a protein corona. The composition and dynamics of this corona can modify nanoparticle size, surface characteristics, cellular interactions, and the availability of targeting ligands. This corona may obscure targeting ligands (eg, transferrin and angiopep-2), negate receptor-mediated transport across the BBB, and redirect particles to MPs organs.171–173 As a result, a ligand that demonstrates receptor-mediated transport under controlled experimental conditions may exhibit altered behavior in vivo following corona formation. This concern is particularly pertinent in the context of MUD, where chronic substance exposure and inflammation can influence systemic physiology and the plasma proteome. Nonetheless, the composition and functional implications of the protein corona surrounding brain-targeted nanoparticles in models exposed to METH remain largely unexamined. Investigating whether disease-associated alterations in the corona affect BBB transport, cellular uptake, or clearance could elucidate a crucial mechanistic connection between systemic disease states and nanoparticle efficacy.

Long-Term Safety and Repeated Administration

Long-term safety is a critical consideration for MUD nanomedicine, particularly because relapse prevention may necessitate repeated treatments. Concerns regarding tissue retention and long-term accumulation arise with non-biodegradable inorganic materials, such as certain gold-based and carbon-based nanomaterials.174,175 Although biodegradable systems may present advantages in this regard, the evaluation of biodegradation products, repeated exposure, and formulation-specific inflammatory responses remains essential. Consequently, safety assessments should extend beyond acute toxicity. Chronic studies must investigate neuroinflammation, activation of microglia and astrocytes, synaptic integrity, behavioral effects, peripheral organ toxicity, nanoparticle clearance, and tissue accumulation over clinically relevant time frames. Importantly, these evaluations should be performed in chronic METH exposure models, as the safety profile of a nanocarrier in a healthy brain may not accurately predict its behavior in a chronically altered neurobiological environment.

Future Directions: Toward Disease-Informed and Evidence-Based Nanomedicine for MUD

Matching Nanocarrier Properties to Specific Delivery Barriers

Future development should transition from the empirical selection of nanocarriers to a rational alignment of nanocarrier properties with specific delivery requirements. Factors such as particle size, surface charge, morphology, composition, cargo-loading capacity, and release characteristics can significantly influence various stages of the delivery cascade.176 For instance, systems designed for poorly soluble small molecules may emphasize solubilization and pharmacokinetic enhancement, while nucleic acid and protein therapeutics necessitate effective cargo protection and intracellular delivery.177 Additionally, ligands targeting the BBB may be advantageous when receptor-mediated transport is sought,58 however, their efficacy must be assessed in light of protein corona formation and disease-related alterations in the BBB. Rather than seeking a universally superior nanocarrier, future research should delineate which platform characteristics are most suitable for a specific therapeutic cargo, administration route, brain-delivery mechanism, and the pathological context associated with the MUD.

Region- and Cell-Selective Delivery

Given that MUD involves distributed yet functionally distinct neural circuits, future nanomedicines must increasingly account for spatial specificity. Whole-organ brain accumulation may prove inadequate when the therapeutic target is localized to a specific region. Consequently, strategies that utilize BBB receptor targeting, neuronal or glial recognition ligands, or regionally controlled delivery may enhance specificity.178,179 Nonetheless, assertions of region- or cell-selective targeting should be substantiated by direct biodistribution and cellular localization studies, rather than being inferred solely from behavioral improvements. This distinction will be crucial for translating nanomedicine strategies from proof-of-concept studies to clinically relevant interventions.

Disease-Responsive and Combination Nanomedicine

The pathological conditions within MUD present potential opportunities for disease-responsive drug delivery. Changes in oxidative stress, inflammatory signaling, extracellular enzymes, or other local characteristics could be harnessed to induce cargo release specifically at sites of pathological processes. For instance, pH-responsive polymers could release cargo in the acidic neuroinflammatory environment,180 while ROS-cleavable linkers could activate prodrugs in regions of oxidative stress.181 However, these strategies should be based on experimentally confirmed disease-related signals rather than assumed to be exclusive to MUD. Additionally, the development of multivalent nanoparticle vaccines containing epitopes for METH, opioids, and cocaine, along with small-molecule neuroprotectants, could effectively target the complex clinical scenario of polysubstance abuse. Nanocarriers also have the potential to enable combination therapies by co-delivering drugs with complementary mechanisms, which is pertinent to the multifaceted nature of MUD involving neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, synaptic dysfunction, and addiction-related circuit irregularities. Nonetheless, the implementation of multi-component drug delivery systems needs thorough assessment for formulation intricacy, optimal dosing, pharmacokinetic compatibility, and long-term safety considerations.

Integrating Physical BBB Modulation and Theranostics

Focused ultrasound can transiently and regionally disrupt the BBB precisely by millimeters, enabling the entry of nanoparticles into specific brain regions such as the nucleus accumbens while minimizing systemic exposure.182,183 These approaches have the potential to enhance spatial precision when conventional pharmacological targeting alone is inadequate, although their safety, reproducibility, and suitability for repeated treatments necessitate further investigation.

Theranostic nanoplatforms offer an alternative avenue by combining therapeutic delivery with imaging or monitoring capabilities. Nanoparticles that can provide insights into brain distribution, BBB integrity, payload release, or target interaction could help identify whether therapeutic inefficacy stems from insufficient brain exposure, suboptimal intracellular delivery, or inadequate pharmacological efficacy. In the context of MUD, such data could be especially valuable for optimizing treatment across individuals with diverse disease conditions.

Regulatory and Manufacturing Standards

Translation will ultimately necessitate standardized characterization and evaluation across nanocarrier platforms. At a minimum, studies should report particle size and distribution, composition, surface properties, cargo loading, route of administration, dosage, release characteristics, mechanisms for crossing the BBB or brain entry, direct evidence of brain accumulation, regional distribution, and cellular localization where applicable, as well as therapeutic outcomes and the experimental model employed.

Standardized reporting would facilitate meaningful evidence-based comparisons across platforms, even in the absence of direct head-to-head studies. Additionally, long-term safety, batch-to-batch reproducibility, scalability, sterility, degradation, and manufacturing consistency should be integrated into preclinical development. Importantly, future studies should progressively incorporate chronic METH exposure, relapse-related paradigms, and clinically relevant treatment schedules to assess whether the advantages of nanocarriers observed in experimental settings remain robust under conditions that more accurately reflect human MUD.

Conclusion

Utilizing nanoparticles for drug delivery shows promise in addressing pharmacological and delivery challenges hindering therapeutic advancements for MUD. However, effective brain delivery encompasses more than just BBB penetration or overall brain accumulation; it necessitates a seamless transition from systemic stability and BBB transport to parenchymal diffusion, localized dispersion, cellular targeting, intracellular payload release, and eventual pharmacological effectiveness.

The suitability of platforms for treating MUD varies depending on the context. Factors such as particle physicochemical properties, surface functionality, cargo characteristics, and release behavior interact with the disease-altered neurovascular and brain microenvironment. This includes METH-associated BBB dysfunction, regional heterogeneity, persistent neuroinflammation, and altered glial activity, impacting transport, biodistribution, cellular processing, and therapeutic responses. There is no universally optimal nanoparticle platform; instead, development should follow a disease-informed approach that aligns nanocarrier properties with the specific biological barriers, brain regions, target cells, cargoes, and exposure durations required for each intervention. Despite advancements, there are still translational gaps. Evidence of regional and cell-specific brain delivery is scarce, improvements in behavioral or molecular outcomes do not always indicate successful intracellular cargo delivery, and many studies are based on acute or short-term models that do not capture the chronic, relapsing nature of human MUD. Future research should encompass quantitative biodistribution, regional and cellular localization, intracellular cargo availability, pharmacodynamics, therapeutic effectiveness, and long-term safety using clinically relevant MUD models. A disease-informed, mechanism-based framework that connects nanoparticle properties with BBB penetration, brain targeting, efficacy, and translational potential provides a logical foundation for progressing nanotherapeutics for MUD.
