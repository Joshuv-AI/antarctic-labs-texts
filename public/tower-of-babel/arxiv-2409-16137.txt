General vacuum stability of orbifold gauge breaking and application to asymptotic grand unification
Giacomo Cacciapaglia, Alan S. Cornell, Aldo Deandrea, Wanda Isnard, Roman Pasechnik, Anca Preda, Zhi-Wei Wang

Abstract
We examine the vacuum stability of gauge symmetry breaking in five dimensions, compactified on the S 1 / ( ℤ 2 × ℤ 2 ′ ) S_{1}/(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\prime}) orbifold. We consider SU ​ ( N ) \text{SU}(N) , Sp ​ ( N ) \text{Sp}(N) , SO ​ ( 2 ​ N ) \text{SO}(2N) and SO ​ ( 2 ​ N + 1 ) \text{SO}(2N+1) theories in the bulk, and provide an exhaustive classification of possible parity assignments that lead to stable orbifolds and of the corresponding symmetry breaking patterns. We use these results in the search for viable asymptotic grand unification theories (aGUT), testing the stability criteria on models based on SU ​ ( 6 ) \text{SU}(6) and SU ​ ( 8 ) \text{SU}(8) . As a result, we identify two viable aGUTs: a unique SU ​ ( 6 ) \text{SU}(6) pathway down to the Standard Model, and one SU ​ ( 8 ) \text{SU}(8) model leading to an intermediate Pati-Salam partial unification.

In all cases where gauge fields propagate in the bulk of the extra dimensions, a compactification based on an “orbifold” is required in order to consistently break the bulk gauge symmetry (when necessary) and ensure a chiral spectrum for the massless fermionic modes. An orbifold is defined as the covering of the extra space

It is well known that special values of the VEV exist, where the unbroken symmetry is enhanced to match that of an orbifold breaking. In other words, it may occur that one starts with an orbifold theory breaking the bulk gauge symmetry

In this work, we systematically investigate the general conditions leading to the stability or instability of a gauge symmetry breaking orbifold in five dimensions, for bulk gauge groups

\text{SO}(N)

We are particularly interested in applying these criteria to models of asymptotic grand unification (aGUT)

We will see this in practice within some concrete examples. We remark that unstable orbifolds may still be of phenomenological interest if bulk fermions and/or scalars overwhelm the gauge contribution to the potential, as may be the case for large multiplicities

2
Methodology and classification

We consider a five dimensional gauge theory where the extra spatial dimension
x^{5}
is compactified on a flat
S^{1}/(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\prime})
orbifold. This orbifold is defined as the covering of the infinite one dimensional line
\mathbb{R}^{1}
by two mirror parities
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
, centred on the two boundaries of the interval, which we conventionally indicate as
x^{5}=0
and
x^{5}=\pi R
, respectively. On the 5D gauge fields, the parities act as follows:

A_{\mu}(r_{k}(x^{5}))=P_{k}\cdot A_{\mu}(x^{5})\cdot P_{k}\,,\quad A_{5}(r_{k}(x^{5}))=-P_{k}\cdot A_{5}(x^{5})\cdot P_{k}\,,

(1)

for
k=1,2
, and where
P_{k}
are two independent parity matrices generated by the bulk gauge group
G
.
The two mirror parities commute, i.e.

r_{1}\ast r_{2}=r_{2}\ast r_{1}\,,

(2)

hence the parity matrices
P_{k}
also commute. For any bulk gauge group
G
, therefore, the parity matrices
P_{k}
are generated by elements of the Cartan subalgebra of
G
, while satisfying the condition
P_{k}^{2}=\mathbb{1}
. In general, we can define

\Omega(\theta_{i})=\prod_{i=1}^{N_{C}}\exp(i \theta_i X_i^C)\,,

(3)

where
X_{i}^{C}
are the
N_{C}
Cartan generators. Then, the most general parity matrix
P
can be written as:

P(\theta_{0},\theta_{i})=e^{i\theta_{0}}\Omega(\theta_{i})

(4)

for a choice of
\theta_{i}
,
i=0,\dots N_{C}
that satisfies
P\cdot P=\mathbb{1}
. Note that the overall sign of
P
is unphysical, c.f. Eq. (
1
).

If at least one of the parity matrices is non-trivial, i.e.
P_{k}\neq\mathbb{1}
, the bulk gauge group is broken. More specifically, each parity breaks
G\to H_{k}
, while the unbroken 4D gauge symmetry is the intersection
H=H_{1}\cap H_{2}
. In practice, the
G
gauge multiplet can be decomposed under
H
, and each component is assigned a well defined parity
\pm 1
under
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
. Hence, the components can be classified in terms of a pair of parities, where conventionally we will list those of the vector
A_{\mu}
, as those for
A_{5}
having opposite signs. The components with parities
(+,+)
contain spin-1 zero modes, matching the unbroken generators of
H
. Instead, components with parities
(-,-)
correspond to massless gauge-scalars, arising from
A_{5}
. Once the latter develops a VEV, the effective potential determining its value is computed by properly applying the one-loop Coleman-Weinberg formalism
[
29
]
, which depends on the spectrum as a function of the VEVs.

In general, the spectrum will have two kinds of contributions
[
41
]
:

\displaystyle m_{n,i}=\frac{n+a_{i}}{R}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\Delta V_{\rm eff}\propto\mathcal{F}^{+}(a_{i})=\frac{3}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\cos 2\pi na_{i}}{n^{5}}\,,

(5)

\displaystyle m_{n,j}=\frac{n+1/2+a_{j}}{R}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\Delta V_{\rm eff}\propto\mathcal{F}^{-}(a_{j})=\frac{3}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1^{n})\frac{\cos 2\pi na_{j}}{n^{5}}\,,

(6)

where
a_{i}
and
a_{j}
are the component-specific functions of the VEVs normalised to the inverse compactification scale
R^{-1}
. Contributions of type
(
5
)
stem from components with parity
(+,+)
and
(-,-)
, while contributions of type (
6
) stem from parities
(+,-)
and
(-,+)
. The latter feature no zero modes. Hence, the most general potential can be written schematically as
[
49
,
41
]

V_{\rm eff}=C\ \left[-3\ \mathcal{V}_{R_{G}}+4\sum_{f}\mathcal{V}_{R_{f}}-\sum_{s}x_{s}\mathcal{V}_{R_{s}}\right]\,,

(7)

where
R_{G}=Adj
for the gauge contribution, and
f
and
s
count the fermion and scalar bulk fields in the representations
R_{f}
and
R_{s}
, respectively, with
x_{s}=1
for real and
x_{s}=2
for complex scalar fields. The overall normalisation factor reads

C=\frac{1}{32\pi^{6}R^{4}}\,.

(8)

The functions
\mathcal{V}_{R}
contain the contribution of the modes in each representation
R
, giving

\mathcal{V}_{R}=\sum_{i}\mathcal{F}^{+}(a_{i})+\sum_{j}\mathcal{F}^{-}(a_{j})\,,

(9)

where
i
and
j
count components with parity
(\pm,\pm)
and
(\pm,\mp)
, respectively.

The functions
\mathcal{F}^{\pm}
have notable properties that will be important for the stability analyses. First of all, we find that

\mathcal{F}^{+}(a+1/2)=\mathcal{F}^{-}(a)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{F}^{-}(a+1/2)=\mathcal{F}^{+}(a)\,.

(10)

Furthermore,
\mathcal{F}^{-}
can be expressed in terms of
\mathcal{F}^{+}
as follows:

\mathcal{F}^{-}(a)=-\mathcal{F}^{+}(a)+\frac{1}{16}\mathcal{F}^{+}(2a)\,.

(11)

Both functions are periodic,
\mathcal{F}^{\pm}(a+n)=\mathcal{F}^{\pm}(a)
, and acquire an extremal value, i.e. they have minima or maxima, at integer,
a=n
, or semi-integer,
a=1/2+n
, values of the argument. At such points, the unbroken gauge group is maximal and it corresponds to an orbifold breaking. The starting orbifold is considered
stable
if all VEVs occur at
a=0
, while it is
unstable
and needs to be replaced by a different orbifold if some VEVs correspond to
a=1/2
(maximal VEV configuration).
Hence, it suffices to evaluate the potential at
a=0
and
a=1/2
and check which configuration gives the smallest value. At such points, we have

\mathcal{F}^{+}(0)=\frac{3}{2}\zeta(5)>0\,,

(12)

while

\mathcal{F}^{-}(0)=\mathcal{F}^{+}(1/2)=-\frac{15}{16}\mathcal{F}^{+}(0)\,,\quad\mathcal{F}^{-}(1/2)=\mathcal{F}^{+}(0)\,.

(13)

From the signs above, one establishes that components with parities
(\pm,\pm)
tend to stabilise the starting orbifold, while components with parities
(\pm,\mp)
destabilise it.

We now define the orbifold parities and classify the stability of symmetry breaking patterns for different bulk gauge groups:
\text{SU}(N)
,
\text{Sp}(2N)
,
\text{SO}(2N)
and
\text{SO}(2N+1)
. Whenever massless gauge-scalar modes appear, we determine the conditions ensuring that the effective potential generated by the gauge multiplet prefers zero VEVs. When the orbifold parities do not allow massless gauge-scalars to appear, the orbifold is automatically stable. We leave exceptional groups for further investigation.

2.1
Special unitary groups:
\text{SU}(N)
case

We first consider a
\text{SU}(N)
gauge group in the bulk. The most general symmetry breaking patterns and gauge-scalar potentials have been studied in Ref.
[
49
]
, here we re-derive the main results and use them to probe the stability condition for this class of orbifolds.

Using the definition of the parity matrix in Eq. (
4
), the most general
\text{SU}(N)
parity matrix is diagonal with
\pm 1
entries:

P_{\text{SU}(N)}={\rm diag}(\underbrace{1,\cdots,1}_{A},\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{N-A})

(14)

where
A\in[\![1,N]\!]
. More details on the derivation of this matrix can be found in Appendix
A
. This parity breaks the gauge group in the bulk as it contains two sub-blocks with different signs, the first with
A
diagonal entries and the second with
N-A
:

\text{SU}(N)\to\text{SU}(A)\times\text{SU}(N-A)\times\text{U}(1)\,,

(15)

while for
A=1
and
A=N
, the group is unbroken.

For the orbifold
S^{1}/(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\prime})
, the breaking pattern is determined by the relative alignment of the
\pm 1
entries in the two parity matrices. The most general scenario entails four different blocks with distinct combinations of parities
[
49
]
:

\displaystyle P_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm diag}(+1,\cdots,+1,+1,\cdots,+1,-1,\cdots,-1,-1,\cdots,-1),

\displaystyle P_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm diag}(\underbrace{+1,\cdots,+1}_{p},\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{q},\underbrace{+1,\cdots,+1}_{r},\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{s})\,,

(16)

where
p+q+r+s=N
, and each block dimension
p,q,r,s\in[\![0,N]\!]
. These parities leave the following 4D gauge group invariant:

\text{SU}(N)\to\frac{\text{U}(p)\times\text{U}(q)\times\text{U}(r)\times\text{U}(s)}{\text{U}(1)}\,,

(17)

where
\text{U}(0)\equiv\emptyset
and the
N
-dimensional identity matrix generator is removed (hence, maximally three
\text{U}(1)
factors can arise). Note that the overall sign of the parity matrix is irrelevant, as only relative signs matter. Hence, there are equivalences among various configurations of
(p,q,r,s)
, as follows:

\displaystyle P_{1}\to-P_{1}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle p\leftrightarrow r,\;\;q\leftrightarrow s\,,

(18)

\displaystyle P_{2}\to-P_{2}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle p\leftrightarrow q,\;\;r\leftrightarrow s\,,

(19)

and flipping the sign of both parities (
i=1
and
i=2
):

P_{i}\to-P_{i}\Rightarrow p\leftrightarrow s,\;\;q\leftrightarrow r\,.

(20)

It is also equivalent (in flat space) to exchange the two parities:

P_{1}\leftrightarrow P_{2}\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;q\leftrightarrow r\,.\\

(21)

Using these relations, one can show that any configuration can be recast into one where
p\geq r
and
q\geq s
, and we will assume this condition in the rest of this section. The parities of the gauge fields read:

(P_{1},P_{2})(A_{\mu})=\begin{pNiceMatrix}p&q&r&s&\\
(+,+)&(+,-)&(-,+)&(-,-)&p\\
(+,-)&(+,+)&(-,-)&(-,+)&q\\
(-,+)&(-,-)&(+,+)&(+,-)&r\\
(-,-)&(-,+)&(+,-)&(+,+)&s\\
\end{pNiceMatrix}\;\;\equiv\;\Xi\,,

(22)

where the signs apply to the corresponding block.
The parities for
A_{5}
can be easily deduced by flipping all the signs in Eq. (
22
). In general, two massless gauge-scalars are present, corresponding to the
(-,-)
blocks, and they transform as bi-fundamental representations of two pairs of unbroken
\text{SU}(K)
factors:

\varphi_{ps}=(F,1,1,\bar{F})\,,\qquad\varphi_{qr}=(1,F,\bar{F},1)\,.

(23)

In the previous equation
F
stands for the fundamental representation and the position in the list refers, respectively, to the
\text{SU}(p)
,
\text{SU}(q)
,
\text{SU}(r)
,
\text{SU}(s)
subgroups.
Gauge-scalar VEVs will further break the corresponding 4D gauge groups.

As already mentioned, there exist values of the VEVs that entail an enhanced symmetry, which corresponds to a rank-preserving orbifold breaking
[
50
]
. For instance, if one component of the
\varphi_{ps}
gauge-scalar is concerned, this is equivalent to flipping the parity signs in the corresponding positions of the parity matrices. Henceforth, a maximal VEV would correspond to a new parity configuration:

\varphi_{ps}:\;\;\;(p,q,r,s)\to(p-1,q+1,r+1,s-1)\,.

(24)

Similarly, a maximal VEV for one component of
\varphi_{qr}
corresponds to

\varphi_{qr}:\;\;\;(p,q,r,s)\to(p+1,q-1,r-1,s+1)\,.

(25)

It is straightforward to show that, if one maximal VEV is preferred, then the largest number of VEVs in the gauge-scalar multiplet will also prefer to be maximal: this is
\text{min}(p,s)=s
for
\varphi_{ps}
and
\text{min}(q,r)=r
for
\varphi_{qr}
.

Before studying the stability conditions, it is worth reminding the reader that, depending on the parity and their alignment, a 4D unbroken group can have various numbers of
\text{SU}(K)
factors and that a gauge-scalar is not always present. We distinguish the following cases:

1.

One-block case occurs when three blocks are trivial (e.g.,
q=r=s=0
), hence
\text{SU}(N)
remains unbroken and no gauge-scalars arise.

2.

Two-block case occurs when two blocks are trivial, leading to

\text{SU}(N)\to\text{SU}(A)\times\text{SU}(N-A)\times U(1)\,.

(26)

A gauge scalar can be present (for instance, if
q=r=0
) or not (if
r=s=0
), depending on which blocks are trivial.

3.

Three-block case occurs when one block is trivial, and it always contains a gauge-scalar. Using the sign equivalences listed in Eq. (
18
) to (
20
), the most general case can be parameterised by
r=0
, leading to

\text{SU}(N)\to\text{SU}(p)\times\text{SU}(q)\times\text{SU}(s)\times\text{U}(1)^{2}\,,

(27)

where, by convention, one gauge-scalar is in the bi-fundamental of
\text{SU}(p)\times\text{SU}(s)
and
p\geq s
.

4.

Four-block case is the most general one with
p,q,r,s\geq 1
and two gauge-scalars.

In the following, we will always order the
\text{SU}(K)
factors according to the conventions established above.

For the stability analysis, we will consider the most general four-block case, where the other subcases can be obtained trivially by setting to zero the appropriate blocks. As two gauge-scalars are present, the maximal VEV can occur in either one’s direction, or in both, leading to the identification of three orbifolds that are connected to the original one:

•

If the maximal VEV is in
\varphi_{ps}
, the equivalent orbifold is given by the parities:

\displaystyle P_{1}={\rm diag}(+1\cdots,+1,+1,\cdots,+1,-1,\cdots,-1),

\displaystyle P_{2}={\rm diag}(\underbrace{+1,\cdots,+1}_{p-s},\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{q+s},\underbrace{+1,\cdots,+1}_{r+s})\,.

(28)

Hence, we end up with a three-block case:

\text{SU}(N)\to\text{SU}(q+s)\times\text{SU}(p-s)\times\text{SU}(r+s)\times\text{U}(1)^{2}\,,

(29)

with a gauge-scalar in the bi-fundamental representation of
\text{SU}(q+s)\times\text{SU}(r+s)
.

•

If the maximal VEV is in
\varphi_{qr}
, the equivalent orbifold is:

\displaystyle P_{1}={\rm diag}(+1\cdots,+1,+1,\cdots,+1,-1,\cdots,-1),

\displaystyle P_{2}={\rm diag}(\underbrace{+1,\cdots,+1}_{p+r},\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{q-r},\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{r+s})\,,

(30)

also corresponding to a three-block case:

\text{SU}(N)\to\text{SU}(p+r)\times\text{SU}(q-r)\times\text{SU}(r+s)\times\text{U}(1)^{2}\,,

(31)

with gauge-scalar in the bi-fundamental representation of
\text{SU}(p+r)\times\text{SU}(r+s)
. Notably, a maximal VEV on this gauge-scalar connects this orbifold to the previous three-block one, and vice versa.

•

If both gauge scalar VEVs are maximal, it yields:

\displaystyle P_{1}={\rm diag}(+1\cdots,+1,+1,\cdots,+1,-1,\cdots,-1,-1\cdots,-1),

(32)

\displaystyle P_{2}={\rm diag}(\underbrace{+1,\cdots,+1}_{p-s+r},\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{q-r+s},\underbrace{+1,\cdots,+1}_{s},\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{r}).

We can check that this orbifold is connected to the previous three-block cases we saw appearing before, in a similar fashion.

\text{SU}(N)
orbifolds connected by maximal VEVs of the gauge-scalars. The four-block case in the bottom-right corner can reduce to a three-block one if
r=q+s

s=p+r
, while the three-block case can reduce to a two-block one for
q=r

p=s

Henceforth, there are four orbifolds that are connected to each other by the generation of maximal VEVs for their gauge-scalars, as illustrated in Fig.
1
. To check which one is preferred, we need to study the potential generated at one loop by the gauge multiplets in the bulk.

To compute the potential
[
41
,
49
]
, we normalise the gauge-scalar VEVs in such a way that the shifts
a_{i}
in the spectrum always appear in the form of an integer times a parameter proportional to the VEV times the radius
R
. We call the latter
a
as
\varphi_{ps}
and
b
as
\varphi_{qr}
, where we recall that all
s
and
r
VEVs are assumed to be equal. Up to the normalisation, see Eq. (
7
), the gauge potential reads
[
49
]
:

\displaystyle\left.{\mathcal{V}_{Adj}}\right|_{\text{SU}(N)}(a,b)=\left[s^{2}\mathcal{F}^{+}(2a)+r^{2}\mathcal{F}^{+}(2b)+2rs\left(\mathcal{F}^{-}(a+b)+\mathcal{F}^{-}(a-b)\right)+\right.

\displaystyle\left.2(p-s)\left(s\mathcal{F}^{+}(a)+r\mathcal{F}^{-}(b)\right)+2(q-r)\left(r\mathcal{F}^{+}(b)+s\mathcal{F}^{-}(a)\right)\right]\,,

(33)

where
V_{\rm eff}=-3C\ \mathcal{V}_{Adj}
. We then check the orbifold stability by computing the differences between the possible minima of the effective potential and the origin. We recall that there are three different possibilities: a maximal VEV in
\phi_{ps}
such that
(a=1/2,b=0)
, in
\phi_{qr}
leading to
(a=0,b=1/2)
, or both
(a=1/2,b=1/2)
:

\displaystyle-\Delta\mathcal{V}_{(1/2,0)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{93}{16}\zeta(5)s(2p-N)\,,

(34)

\displaystyle-\Delta\mathcal{V}_{(0,1/2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{93}{16}\zeta(5)r(2q-N)\,,

(35)

\displaystyle-\Delta\mathcal{V}_{(1/2,1/2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{93}{16}\zeta(5)(s-r)(p+r-q-s)\,.

(36)

The original orbifold is, therefore, stable if all
\Delta\mathcal{V}<0
. For the first two, corresponding to the three-block orbifolds, at least one gives positive
\Delta\mathcal{V}
, signalling that four-block orbifolds are always unstable and must be described in terms of an orbifold with three or less blocks.

The next step, therefore, is to check the stability of the three-block cases. Without loss of generality, we consider the general potential in Eq. (
33
) with
r=0
. In which case

-\Delta\mathcal{V}_{(1/2)}=-\Delta\mathcal{V}_{(1/2,0)}\propto s(2p-N)\,.

(37)

Hence, the orbifold is stable only if
p\geq N/2
. For
p=N/2
, the two orbifolds are equivalent as they lead to the same symmetry breaking pattern. If the starting three-block orbifold is unstable,
p<N/2
, we can construct the one corresponding to the maximal VEV, which has
p^{\prime}=q+s
,
q^{\prime}=p-s
and
s^{\prime}=s
. We can check that the latter orbifold is stable as

p^{\prime}=N-p\geq\frac{N}{2}\,.

(38)

We then notice that, among the two three-block orbifolds connected to a four-block one, one is always stable: they have
p^{\prime}=q+s
and
p^{\prime}=p+r
, respectively, and as
N=p+r+q+s
, at least one of these two is greater than
N/2
.

A two-block orbifold with gauge-scalars corresponds to
r=q=0
. The study of the minima of the potential is similar to the three-block case:
\Delta\mathcal{V}
has the same expression as the three-block case but we always have
p>N/2
as
p+s=N
.

In summary, we have shown that:

•

Four-block orbifolds are
always unstable
.

•

Three-block orbifolds are
stable only if

p\geq N/2
. The gauge scalar is always in the bi-fundamental representation of the largest subgroup,
\text{SU}(p)
, and another
\text{SU}(K)
factor.

•

Two-block orbifolds are
always stable
.

2.2
Symplectic groups:
\text{Sp}(2N)
case

Symplectic groups
\text{Sp}(2N)
are defined by a set of generators, being unitary matrices
X
that satisfy:

X\cdot E+E\cdot X^{T}=0\,,\quad\mbox{with}\;\;E=\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbb{1}_{N}\\
-\mathbb{1}_{N}&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(39)

The generators can be written in terms of
N\times N
blocks as follows:

X=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&-A^{T}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(40)

with
B
and
C
being symmetric matrices, while
A
is a unitary matrix.
Following this convention, the computation of the parity matrices can be found in Appendix
A
.
There are two inequivalent ways to define them, and we denote them by
P^{\rm I}
and
P^{\rm II}
. They have the following expressions (in the same block form as the generators):

P^{\rm I}_{\text{Sp}(2N)}={\rm diag}(\underbrace{1,\cdots,1}_{A},\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{N-A})\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}=P_{\text{SU}(N)}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\,,

(41)

P^{\rm II}_{\text{Sp}(2N)}={\rm diag}(\underbrace{1,\cdots,1}_{N})\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\,.

(42)

They lead to two different breaking patterns:

\begin{split}P^{\rm I}:&~~\text{Sp}(2N)\to\text{Sp}(2A)\times\text{Sp}(2(N-A))\,,\\
P^{\rm II}:&~~\text{Sp}(2N)\to\text{SU}(N)\times\text{U}(1)\,.\end{split}

(43)

Having two types of parity matrices, the orbifold
S_{1}/(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\prime})
features three general types of symmetry breaking patterns:

I+I :

The first type is constructed out of two parities of type I, with the following general alignment:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}P_{1}&=&{\rm diag}(+1,\cdots,+1,+1,\cdots,+1,-1,\cdots,-1,-1,\cdots,-1)\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\,,\\
\\
P_{2}&=&{\rm diag}(\underbrace{+1,\cdots,+1}_{p},\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{q},\underbrace{+1,\cdots,+1}_{r},\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{s})\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\,.\end{array}

(44)

The parities of the adjoint components can be given in the block scheme of Eq. (
40
) in terms of the
\Xi
pattern defined in Eq. (
22
):

(P_{1},P_{2})(A_{\mu})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\Xi&\Xi\\
\hline\cr\Xi&\Xi\end{array}\right)\,.

(45)

This leads to the following symmetry breaking pattern:

\text{Sp}(2N)\to\text{Sp}(2p)\times\text{Sp}(2q)\times\text{Sp}(2r)\times\text{Sp}(2s)\,,

(46)

with two massless gauge scalars in the bi-fundamental representation,

\varphi_{ps}=(F,1,1,F)\,,\qquad\varphi_{qr}=(1,F,F,1)\,.

(47)

The potential for the gauge-scalar VEVs, computed in Appendix
B
, is the same as that of the
\text{SU}(N)
case in Eq. (
33
), up to a factor of 2. Therefore, we can immediately conclude that the vacuum structure will be analogous to that of the
\text{SU}(N)
case. The general conclusion is that the only stable orbifolds have either two blocks, or three blocks with
p\geq N/2
:

A)

\text{Sp}(2N)\to\text{Sp}(2p)\times\text{Sp}(2(N-p))
, with or without gauge-scalar in
(F,F)
-representation of the unbroken gauge group,

B)

\text{Sp}(2N)\to\text{Sp}(2p)\times\text{Sp}(2q)\times\text{Sp}(2(N-p-q))
, with
p\geq N/2
and the gauge-scalar in
(F,1,F)
-representation of the unbroken gauge group.

II+II :

The second type is constructed out of two parities of type II. The misalignment occurs by exchanging the components with a
\pm 1
in the blocks, leading to the most general scenario:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}P_{1}&=&{\rm diag}(+1,\cdots,+1,+1,\cdots,+1)\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\,,\\
\\
P_{2}&=&{\rm diag}(\underbrace{+1,\cdots,+1}_{p},\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{q})\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\,,\end{array}

(48)

and leading to the parity scheme

(P_{1},P_{2})(A_{\mu})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\begin{array}[]{cc}{(+,+)}&(+,-)\\
(+,-)&{(+,+)}\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{cc}{(-,-)}&(-,+)\\
(-,+)&{(-,-)}\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{cc}{(-,-)}&(-,+)\\
(-,+)&{(-,-)}\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{cc}{(+,+)}&(+,-)\\
(+,-)&{(+,+)}\end{array}\end{array}\right)\,.

(49)

The definite parities apply to sub-blocks of size
p
or
q
(where
p+q=N
).
The corresponding symmetry breaking pattern is

\text{Sp}(2N)\to\text{U}(p)\times\text{U}(q)\,,

(50)

with two gauge scalars transforming as the symmetric representation
S
of the two gauge factors:

\varphi_{p}=(S,1)\,,\qquad\varphi_{q}=(1,S)\,.

(51)

The computation of the effective potential can be found in Appendix
B
. Evaluating for equal values of all VEV components, up to the normalisation, the potential reads

\left.\mathcal{V}^{\rm II+II}_{Adj}\right|_{\text{Sp}(2N)}(a,b)=p^{2}\mathcal{F}^{+}(2a)+q^{2}\mathcal{F}^{+}(2b)+2pq\left(\mathcal{F}^{-}(a+b)+\mathcal{F}^{-}(a-b)\right)\,,

(52)

where
a
and
b
stem from the symmetry of
\text{SU}(p)
and
\text{SU}(q)
, respectively. As the potential difference is only given by
\mathcal{F}^{-}
functions, we see that the global minimum is never at
(a,b)=(0,0)
for any value of
p
and
q
. Performing the gauge transformation to remove the maximal VEVs, we find that the only stable orbifold must have aligned parities
(P_{1},P_{1})
, leading to the only stable pattern:

\text{Sp}(2N)\to\text{U}(N)\,,

(53)

without gauge-scalars.

II+I :

The third type has one parity of each type. We can always define the type-II parity as in Eq. (
42
), and misalign the parity of type-I, leading to the general case:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}P_{1}&=&{\rm diag}(+1,\cdots,+1,+1,\cdots,+1)\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\,,\\
\\
P_{2}&=&{\rm diag}(\underbrace{+1,\cdots,+1}_{p},\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{q})\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\,.\end{array}

(54)

The parity scheme on the adjoint components is

(P_{1},P_{2})(A_{\mu})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\begin{array}[]{cc}{(+,+)}&(+,-)\\
(+,-)&{(+,+)}\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{cc}(-,+)&{(-,-)}\\
{(-,-)}&(-,+)\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{cc}(-,+)&{(-,-)}\\
{(-,-)}&(-,+)\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{cc}{(+,+)}&(+,-)\\
(+,-)&{(+,+)}\end{array}\end{array}\right)\,,

(55)

leading to the following symmetry breaking pattern:

\text{Sp}(2N)\to\text{U}(p)\times\text{U}(q)\,.

(56)

The massless gauge-scalar is in the bi-fundamental representation of
\text{SU}(p)\times\text{SU}(q)
:

\varphi_{pq}=(F,\bar{F})\,.

(57)

The potential, also computed in the Appendix
B
, up to the normalisation, reads:

\displaystyle\left.V^{\rm I+II}_{Adj}\right|_{\text{Sp}(2N)}(a)=\frac{1}{16}\left[q^{2}\mathcal{F}^{+}(4a)+2q(p-q)\mathcal{F}^{+}(2a)\right]\,,

(58)

where we assumed
p>q
without loss of generality. Containing only functions
\mathcal{F}^{+}
, it always has a minimum at zero VEV, hence, all such configurations are stable.

2.3
Special orthogonal groups:
\text{SO}(2N)
case

Special orthogonal groups in even dimension share features similar to the symplectic case. The generators can be written in an analogous block form as follows:

X=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
-B^{T}&D\end{pmatrix}\,,

(59)

where
A
and
D
are antisymmetric matrices and
B
is a unitary matrix.
The general form of the parity matrices has been computed in Appendix
A
, and two types are present,
P^{\rm I}
and
P^{\rm II}
. In block form, the parity matrices can be written in terms of the
\text{SU}(N)
parity matrix as follows:

P_{\text{SO}(2N)}^{\rm I}=P_{\text{SU}(N)}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\,,

(60)

P_{\text{SO}(2N)}^{\rm II}=P_{\text{SU}(N)}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(61)

They also lead to different breaking patterns:

\begin{split}P^{\rm I}:&~~\text{SO}(2N)\to\text{SO}(2A)\times\text{SO}(2(N-A))\,,\\
P^{\rm II}:&~~\text{SO}(2N)\to\text{SU}(N)\times\text{U}(1)\,.\end{split}

(62)

As before, the two parity types can be combined in three cases, leading to three different symmetry breaking patterns:

I+I :

If both parities are of type I, then the situation is very similar to the
\text{Sp}(2N)
case:

\begin{array}[]{rll}P_{1}&=&{\rm diag}(+1,\cdots,+1,+1,\cdots,+1,-1,\cdots,-1,-1,\cdots,-1)\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\,,\\
\\
P_{2}&=&{\rm diag}(\underbrace{+1,\cdots,+1}_{p},\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{q},\underbrace{+1,\cdots,+1}_{r},\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{s})\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\,.\end{array}

(63)

As a consequence, the adjoint components will receive the same block form parity scheme as in Eq. (
45
), in terms of the
\Xi
patterns of Eq. (
22
). This corresponds to the breaking pattern

\text{SO}(2N)\to\text{SO}(2p)\times\text{SO}(2q)\times\text{SO}(2r)\times\text{SO}(2s)\,,

(64)

with two zero mode gauge-scalars:

\varphi_{ps}=(F,1,1,F)\,,\qquad\varphi_{qr}=(1,F,F,1)\,.

(65)

The effective potential is computed in Appendix
B
and, like for the symplectic case, it gives the same expression as in the
\text{SU}(N)
case up to a factor of 2. As a result, the vacuum structure will be the same as in the
\text{SU}(N)
case. The stable orbifolds are:

A)

\text{SO}(2N)\to\text{SO}(2p)\times\text{SO}(2(N-p))
with or without gauge-scalar in
(F,F)
-representation of the unbroken gauge group,

B)

\text{SO}(2N)\to\text{SO}(2p)\times\text{SO}(2q)\times\text{SO}(2(N-p-q))
with
p\geq N/2
and the gauge-scalar in
(F,1,F)
-representation of the unbroken gauge group.

II+II :

This case differs slightly from the symplectic case due to the off-diagonal nature of the parity matrix in Eq. (
61
). To identify generators with definite parities, we split the general form into two sets, as follows:

X=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}A_{+}/2&B_{s}\\
-B_{s}&A_{+}/2\end{pmatrix}}_{X_{+}}+\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}A_{-}/2&B_{a}\\
B_{a}&-A_{-}/2\end{pmatrix}}_{X_{-}}\,,

(66)

where
A_{\pm}=A\pm D
and the
B_{a/s}
refer to the antisymmetric and symmetric parts of
B
, respectively. The parities of the
X_{\pm}
generators are opposite, with the action of the parities on
X_{+}
given by:

\begin{array}[]{rll}P_{1}&=&{\rm diag}(+1,\cdots,+1,+1,\cdots,+1)\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\,,\\
\\
P_{2}&=&{\rm diag}(\underbrace{+1,\cdots,+1}_{p},\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{q})\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}\,.\end{array}

(67)

Hence, the parity scheme on the components of the adjoint will be

(P_{1},P_{2})(A_{\mu}^{+})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\begin{array}[]{cc}{(+,+)}&(+,-)\\
(+,-)&{(+,+)}\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{cc}{(+,+)}&(+,-)\\
(+,-)&{(+,+)}\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{cc}{(+,+)}&(+,-)\\
(+,-)&{(+,+)}\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{cc}{(+,+)}&(+,-)\\
(+,-)&{(+,+)}\end{array}\end{array}\right)\,,

(68)

(P_{1},P_{2})(A_{\mu}^{-})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\begin{array}[]{cc}{(-,-)}&(-,+)\\
(-,+)&{(-,-)}\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{cc}{(-,-)}&(-,+)\\
(-,+)&{(-,-)}\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{cc}{(-,-)}&(-,+)\\
(-,+)&{(-,-)}\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{cc}{(-,-)}&(-,+)\\
(-,+)&{(-,-)}\end{array}\end{array}\right)\,,

(69)

where
A^{\pm}_{\mu}
corresponds to the generators
X_{\pm}
. The breaking pattern is

\text{SO}(2N)\to\text{U}(p)\times\text{U}(q)\,,

(70)

with two gauge scalars transforming as an antisymmetric representation
A
of the two gauge factors:

\varphi_{p}=(A,1)\,,\qquad\varphi_{q}=(1,A)\,.

(71)

The potential for this scenario can be found in Appendix
B
, and it gives, up to the normalisation,

\left.\mathcal{V}_{Adj}^{\rm II+II}\right|_{\text{SO}(2N)}=\frac{3}{2}p^{2}\mathcal{F}^{+}(2a)+\frac{3}{2}q^{2}\mathcal{F}^{+}(2b)+4pq\left(\mathcal{F}^{-}(a+b)+\mathcal{F}^{-}(a-b)\right)\,.

(72)

Similar to the symplectic case, the potential differences are only determined by
\mathcal{F}^{-}
functions, hence the orbifold is never stable. The only stable case has
p=0
or
q=0
, leading to aligned parities and the symmetry breaking pattern,

\text{SO}(2N)\to\text{U}(N)\,,

(73)

without gauge-scalars.

I+II :

This scenario is similar to the II+II combination discussed before, except that the
X_{\pm}
generators have opposite parities only under the type-II parity. For
X_{+}
, we have the same parity definitions as in Eq. (
67
).
Henceforth, while the gauge generators
A_{+}
will have a parity scheme like in Eq. (
68
), for
A_{-}
a sign needs to be flipped as compared to Eq. (
69
). If we consider that the type-I parity is the second one, it leads to:

(P_{1},P_{2})(A^{-}_{\mu})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\begin{array}[]{cc}{(-,+)}&(-,-)\\
(-,-)&{(-,+)}\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{cc}{(-,+)}&(-,-)\\
(-,-)&{(-,+)}\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{cc}{(-,+)}&(-,-)\\
(-,-)&{(-,+)}\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{cc}{(-,+)}&(-,-)\\
(-,-)&{(-,+)}\end{array}\end{array}\right).

(74)

The resulting breaking pattern is:

\text{SO}(2N)\to\text{U}(p)\times\text{U}(q)\,,

(75)

with a massless gauge-scalar in the following representation:

\varphi_{pq}=(F,\bar{F})\,.

(76)

The potential for this case is computed in Appendix
B
, and it gives, up to the normalisation:

\left.\mathcal{V}_{Adj}^{\rm I+II}\right|_{\text{SO}(2N)}(a)=\frac{q^{2}}{16}\mathcal{F}^{+}(4a)+\frac{q(p-q)}{8}\mathcal{F}^{+}(2a)+q^{2}\mathcal{F}^{+}(2a)\,.

(77)

As such, the minimum is always at
a=0
, thus making this scenario stable for all values of
p
and
q
.

2.4
Special orthogonal groups:
\text{SO}(2N+1)
case

In the case of odd-dimensional
\text{SO}(2N+1)
groups, the Cartan algebra has the same number of elements as that of
\text{SO}(2N)
. Due to the presence of an additional dimension, it is only possible to define a parity matrix of type-I, analogous to
P^{\rm I}_{\text{SO}(2N)}
in Eq. (
60
), see Appendix
A
for more details. Hence, the most general parity is a diagonal matrix with
2A+1
plus signs and
2(N-A)
minus signs, breaking the symmetry as

\text{SO}(2N+1)\to\text{SO}(2A+1)\times\text{SO}(2(N-A))\,.

(78)

As a result, only one combination of parities can be formed:

\begin{array}[]{rll}P_{1}&=&{\rm diag}(+1,\cdots,+1,+1,\cdots,+1,-1,\cdots,-1,-1,\cdots,-1)\,,\\
P_{2}&=&{\rm diag}(\underbrace{+1,\cdots,+1}_{2p+1},\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{2q},\underbrace{+1,\cdots,+1}_{2r},\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{2s})\,,\end{array}

(79)

which generates the symmetry breaking pattern

\text{SO}(2N+1)\to\text{SO}(2p+1)\times\text{SO}(2q)\times\text{SO}(2r)\times\text{SO}(2s)\,.

(80)

The parities of the gauge fields read

(P_{1},P_{2})(A_{\mu})=\begin{pNiceMatrix}2p+1&2q&2r&2s&\\
(+,+)&(+,-)&(-,+)&(-,-)&2p+1\\
(+,-)&(+,+)&(-,-)&(-,+)&2q\\
(-,+)&(-,-)&(+,+)&(+,-)&2r\\
(-,-)&(-,+)&(+,-)&(+,+)&2s\\
\end{pNiceMatrix}\,.

(81)

The two zero mode gauge-scalars are in the
ps
and
qr
sectors, such that

\varphi_{ps}=(F,1,1,F)\,,\qquad\varphi_{qr}=(1,F,F,1)\,,

(82)

where the notation is similar to the one used in the previous sections. From Eq. (
81
), we see that this case is very similar to the
\text{SO}(2N)
one with two type-I parity matrices, hence, we can apply the same results here.

From the previous stability analysis, we see that the four-block case is always unstable. The only peculiarity of the
\text{SO}(2N+1)
case is that one of the block dimensions is always odd, while all the others are even. From the previous analysis, we can identify the following stable orbifolds:

A)

\text{SO}(2N+1)\to\text{SO}(2p+1)\times\text{SO}(2q)\times\text{SO}(2s)
with
2p+1>N
;

B)

\text{SO}(2N+1)\to\text{SO}(2p)\times\text{SO}(2q+1)\times\text{SO}(2s)
with
2p>N
;

C)

\text{SO}(2N+1)\to\text{SO}(2p)\times\text{SO}(2q)\times\text{SO}(2s+1)
with
2p>N
;

D)

All two block cases, with and without a gauge-scalar.

For the three-block cases, the gauge-scalar is a bi-fundamental representation of the first and the last
\text{SO}(K)
factors.

3
Minimal aGUT models

Models of asymptotic Grand Unification (or aGUTs) offer an alternative to the traditional unification paradigm. In their renormalisation group evolution, the couplings flow towards the same fixed point in the ultra-violet regime
[
47
]
. Instead of acquiring the same value at a finite energy scale as in traditional GUTs, they only reach unification asymptotically and the unified symmetry is only restored approximately when the theory approaches the fixed point. Gauge theories in five dimensions may feature such ultra-violet fixed points, depending on the matter content of the model
[
27
,
26
]
. The presence of a fixed point for the gauge couplings, as well as for the Yukawa ones, imposes severe limitations on the multiplicity and representation of the fermions in the bulk, and a general procedure for the identification of models has been established
[
46
]
.

In this work, we include the requirement that the 5D aGUT models should be based on stable orbifolds, as determined by the gauge-scalar potential from the gauge multiplet. In aGUTs, the fermions in the bulk are limited in number and can only appear in small-rank representations of the bulk gauge group, else the gauge fixed point is lost. As we will see in some examples below, their contribution to the gauge scalar potential can at most be comparable to that of the gauge multiplet, and they tend to move the minimum towards the breaking of the 4D gauge symmetry preserved by the orbifold. This feature could be useful in some cases. As a starter, we can inspect the orbifolds identified in Table
1
to find aGUT candidates. Exceptional groups can also be employed, with a candidate model identified in Ref.
[
45
]
, and we leave their systematic study for a future work.

In this work, we will consider two options regarding the unbroken 4D gauge group:

A)

SM route, where the 4D unbroken group
H=\text{SU}(3)_{c}\times\text{SU}(2)_{L}\times U(1)_{Y}\times X
, with
X
being an additional factorised (non-simple) group.

B)

Pati-Salam (PS)
[
51
]
route, where
H=\text{SU}(4)_{\rm PS}\times\text{SU}(2)_{L}\times\text{SU}(2)_{R}\times X
.

In principle,
X
could be any group, however here we will only consider
X=\text{U}(1)
or
\text{U}(1)^{2}
for various reasons: non-Abelian groups are typically incompatible with the fermion embedding
[
46
]
and such a case would typically require a parity scheme with more than three blocks, which is unstable. The candidate models are listed in Table
2
.

Before analysing the three remaining aGUTs, we want to recap how the ultra-violet fixed points are determined
[
46
]
, as this computation will be presented for the
\text{SU}(8)
model in the following subsections. At energies above the compactification scale
1/R
, where the 5D regime kicks in, any bulk coupling
h
can be expressed in terms of an effective ’t Hooft coupling, which takes into account the number of Kaluza-Klein (KK) modes below the renormalisation scale
\mu
:

\tilde{\alpha}_{h}=\frac{h^{2}}{4\pi}\mu R\,.

(83)

For a gauge coupling
g
, the renormalisation group equation at one loop order reads

2\pi\ \frac{d\tilde{\alpha}_{g}}{d\ln\mu}=2\pi\ \tilde{\alpha}_{g}-b_{5}\ \tilde{\alpha}_{g}^{2}\,,

(84)

where
b_{5}
is the 5D beta-function for gauge coupling evolution
[
46
]
. The fixed point exists if
b_{5}>0
, and it reads

\tilde{\alpha}_{g}^{\ast}=\frac{2\pi}{b_{5}}\,.

(85)

For a model with a set of bulk Yukawa couplings
y
, one obtains coupled differential equations in the form

2\pi\ \frac{d\tilde{\alpha}_{y}}{d\ln\mu}=\left(2\pi+\sum_{y^{\prime}}c_{yy^{\prime}}\tilde{\alpha}_{y^{\prime}}-d_{y}\tilde{\alpha}_{g}\right)\tilde{\alpha}_{y}\,,

(86)

where the coefficients
d_{y}
and
c_{yy^{\prime}}
are computed at one loop order. In this case, the fixed points read

\tilde{\alpha}_{y}^{\ast}=\sum_{y^{\prime}}c_{yy^{\prime}}^{-1}\left(d_{y}\tilde{\alpha}_{g}^{\ast}-2\pi\right)\,,

(87)

where all values must be positive. We do not consider here the evolution of scalar quartic couplings.

In the remainder of this section, we will analyse the three models based on stable orbifolds, compute the potential for the gauge-scalars and their mass, and determine their viability. For
\text{SU}(6)
and
\text{SU}(8)
, we will also provide a comparison with models based on unstable orbifolds.

3.1
Stable
\text{SU}(6)\to\text{SU}(3)\times\text{SU}(2)\times\text{U}(1)^{2}
model

The minimal stable
\text{SU}(6)
model in Table
2
has been identified as model 6A’ in Ref.
[
46
]
. The orbifold is based on the parities:

\displaystyle P_{1}={\rm diag}(+1\cdots,+1,+1,\cdots,+1,-1,\cdots,-1)\,,

\displaystyle P_{2}={\rm diag}(\underbrace{+1,\cdots,+1}_{p=3},\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{q=2},\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{s=1})\,,

(88)

hence leading to a stable three-block pattern. Contrary to the gauge multiplets, where the parities are uniquely determined by the parity matrices, for matter fields (i.e. bulk fermions and scalars) an overall parity can be assigned,
\eta_{1,2}=\pm 1
. Hence, one can introduce fields in the same representation and with different parity assignments. However, relative parities of the components inside each field cannot be changed
[
46
]
. The SM fermions, therefore, can be obtained as zero modes of the following two fermion fields, both in the antisymmetric representation
{\bf 15}
of
\text{SU}(6)
:

\displaystyle\Psi_{\bf 15}^{(+,-)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle({\bf 3,2})_{1/6,1}^{(+,+)}\oplus({\bf 1,2})_{1/2,-2}^{(-,-)}\oplus({\bf\bar{3},1})_{-2/3,1}^{(+,-)}\oplus({\bf 1,1})_{1,1}^{(+,-)}\oplus({\bf 3,1})_{-1/3,-2}^{(-,+)}\,,

(89)

\displaystyle\Psi_{\overline{\bf 15}}^{(-,-)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle({\bf 3,1})_{2/3,-1}^{(-,-)}\oplus({\bf 1,1})_{-1,-1}^{(-,-)}\oplus({\bf\bar{3},1})_{1/3,2}^{(+,+)}\oplus({\bf\bar{3},2})_{-1/6,-1}^{(-,+)}\oplus({\bf 1,2})_{-1/2,2}^{(+,-)}\,,

(90)

where the components are labelled in terms of their quantum numbers as follows:

(\text{SU}(3)_{c},\text{SU}(2)_{L})_{\text{U}(1)_{Y},\text{U}(1)_{Z}}\,,

(91)

where
\text{U}(1)_{Z}
is the additional non-SM charge symmetry.
The parities
(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
are indicated on the
\Psi
fields. For the components, we recall that
(+,+)
entails a left-handed zero mode, while
(-,-)
entails a right-handed one. Hence,
\Psi_{\bf 15}^{(+,-)}\supset q_{L}+l_{L}^{c}
and
\Psi_{\bf\bar{15}}^{(-,-)}\supset u_{R}+e_{R}+d_{R}^{c}
, where
{X}^{c}
indicates the charge conjugate of a field
X
.
Since the gauge-scalar
\varphi_{0}=({\bf 3},{\bf 1})_{-1/3,3}
is a colour triplet, it must not acquire a VEV. To generate SM fermion masses, a bulk scalar
{\bf 15}
is added

\Phi_{\bf 15}^{(-,+)}=({\bf 1,2})_{1/2,-2}^{(+,+)}\oplus({\bf 3,2})_{1/6,1}^{(-,-)}\oplus({\bf\bar{3},1})_{-2/3,1}^{(-,+)}\oplus({\bf 1,1})_{1,1}^{(-,+)}\oplus({\bf 3,1})_{-1/3,-2}^{(+,-)}\,,

(92)

which contains the SM Higgs doublet zero mode living in the
(+,+)
component. To generate neutrino masses and introduce a viable Indalo dark matter candidate
[
43
]
, two singlets shall also be added:

\Psi_{\bf 1}^{(-,-)}=({\bf 1},{\bf 1})_{0,0}^{(-,-)}\,,\qquad\Psi_{\bf 1^{\prime}}^{(+,-)}=({\bf 1},{\bf 1})_{0,0}^{(+,-)}\,.

(93)

Hence, one can write bulk Yukawa couplings for up-type quarks and for the two singlets as follows:

\mathcal{L}_{\rm Yuk}=-Y_{u}\ \overline{\Psi}_{\bf\overline{15}}\Psi_{\bf 15}\Phi_{\bf 15}-Y_{\nu}\ \overline{\Psi}_{\bf 15}\Psi_{\bf 1}\Phi_{\bf 15}-Y_{\chi}\ \overline{\Psi}_{\bf\overline{15}}\Psi_{\bf 1^{\prime}}\Phi_{\bf 15}\,.

(94)

These bulk Yukawas give mass to the up-type fermions of the SM, while the down-type ones remain massless and must receive a mass from other mechanisms (for instance, via localised Yukawa couplings). The existence of fixed points for both gauge and Yukawa couplings requires
n_{g}=3
(see result for model 6A with the singlet Yukawas in Ref.
[
46
]
).

The total effective potential can be written in terms of the contribution of vector, fermion and scalar bulk fields as in Eq. (
7
).
The contributions of the adjoint and antisymmetric representations read

\mathcal{V}_{Adj}(a)=\frac{5}{4}\mathcal{F}^{+}(2a)\,,\qquad\mathcal{V}_{A}(a)=\frac{1}{8}\mathcal{F}^{+}(2a)\,,

(95)

respectively, where the latter does not depend on the parities. Hence,

V_{\rm eff}(a)=C\left(n_{g}-4\right)\mathcal{F}^{+}(2a)=-C\ \mathcal{F}^{+}(2a)\,,

(96)

where we fixed
n_{g}=3
. In general, the stability of the orbifold requires
n_{g}\leq 3
, consistently with the fixed points.
The profile of the potential for
n_{g}=3
is plotted in Fig.
2
, where we can see that the minimum remains at
a=0
, as expected. Notably, in this case the second minimum at maximal value
a=1/2
, degenerate with the
a=0
one, corresponds to a completely equivalent orbifold. From Eq. (
96
), we computed the mass of the gauge scalar at the minimum
a=0
, leading to

m_{\varphi}^{2}=\left.\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial a^{2}}V_{\rm total}(a)\right|_{a=0}\,\frac{R^{2}}{2}=\frac{3}{16}\zeta(3)\frac{1}{\pi^{4}\ R^{2}}\,,

(97)

where the factor
R^{2}/2
comes from the relation between
a
and the properly normalised gauge scalar field,
a=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}R\varphi_{0}

[
41
]
. Via the bulk gauge interactions of
\Psi_{\bf 15}^{(+,-)}
, the gauge scalar couples to the quark and lepton doublets
\bar{q}_{L}l_{L}^{c}
, hence it will decay like a scalar leptoquark. Searches for leptoquarks at the Large Hadron Collider have been performed by both ATLAS
[
55
]
and CMS
1
1

1

https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/bin/view/CMSPublic/SummaryPlotsEXO13TeV
, providing bounds on the masses in the ballpark of
2
TeV for the QCD-induced pair-production channel. Hence, we can estimate a lower bound on the compactification scale in this
\text{SU}(6)
aGUT model,

\frac{1}{R}=m_{\rm KK}\gtrsim 4\pi^{2}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{3\zeta(3)}}}\times 2~\mbox{TeV}\sim 50~\mbox{TeV}\,,

(98)

which acts as a lower bound on the mass of the KK modes.

A variant of this model can be constructed by replacing
\Psi_{\bf 15}
with the symmetric
\Psi_{\bf 21}
, at the price of embedding the Higgs boson into a bulk
{\bf 105}
representation of
\text{SU}(6)
, which contains multiple scalar zero modes.

3.2
Unstable
\text{SU}(6)\to\text{SU}(3)\times\text{SU}(2)\times\text{U}(1)^{2}

For comparison, we also discuss the case of an unstable orbifold of
\text{SU}(6)
, which was identified in Ref.
[
46
]
as the source of very appealing aGUT models, dubbed 6A, 6S and 6Aflip. In such models, one Higgs doublet arises as a gauge scalar, while a second one is in the bulk.
The parity scheme is given by

\displaystyle P_{1}={\rm diag}(+1\cdots,+1,+1,\cdots,+1,-1,\cdots,-1)\,,

(99)

\displaystyle P_{2}={\rm diag}(\underbrace{+1,\cdots,+1}_{q=3},\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{p=2},\underbrace{+1,\cdots,+1}_{s=1})\,,

which is unstable as
p=2<3
. The model 6A has the same bulk representations as model 6A’ up to different parities. In the model 6Aflip, the fermions are embedded in two fundamental and one 3-index antisymmetric representations of
\text{SU}(6)
:

\displaystyle\Psi_{\bf 20}^{(-,-)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle({\bf\bar{3},2})_{-1/6,3/2}^{(-,+)}\oplus({\bf 3,1})_{2/3,3/2}^{(-,-)}\oplus({\bf 1,1})_{-1,3/2}^{(-,-)}\oplus({\bf 3,2})_{1/6,-3/2}^{(+,+)}

(100)

\displaystyle\oplus({\bf\bar{3},1})_{-2/3,-3/2}^{(+,-)}\oplus({\bf 1,1})_{1,-3/2}^{(+,-)}\,,

\displaystyle\Psi_{\bf 6}^{(+,+)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle({\bf 3,1})_{-1/3,1/2}^{(+,+)}\oplus({\bf 1,2})_{1/2,1/2}^{(+,-)}\oplus({\bf 1,1})_{0,-5/2}^{(-,+)}\,,

(101)

\displaystyle\Psi_{\bf\bar{6}}^{(+,-)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle({\bf\bar{3},1})_{1/3,-1/2}^{(+,-)}\oplus({\bf 1,2})_{-1/2,-1/2}^{(+,+)}\oplus({\bf 1,1})_{0,5/2}^{(-,-)}\,.

(102)

The bulk scalar field is the same in the two models and belongs to the antisymmetric
\bf 15
representation.

As before, the effective potential for the gauge-scalar can be computed from contributions of relevant representations found as

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{Adj}(a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{+}(2a)+2\mathcal{F}^{+}(a)+6\mathcal{F}^{-}(a)\,,

(103)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{F}(a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{-}(a)\,,

(104)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{A}(a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{+}(a)+3\mathcal{F}^{-}(a)\,,

(105)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{A_{3}}(a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 3(\mathcal{F}^{+}(a)+\mathcal{F}^{-}(a))\,,

(106)

where the fermion and scalar contributions refer to fields with parities
(\pm,\pm)
. For parities
(\pm,\mp)
, it is enough to exchange
\mathcal{F}^{+}\leftrightarrow\mathcal{F}^{-}
.
As model 6A contains a pair of
\Psi_{\bf 15}^{(+,-)}
and
\Psi_{\bf\bar{15}}^{(-,-)}
per generation, the fermionic contribution reads

\mathcal{V}_{\rm fermions}^{\rm 6A}(a)=n_{g}\ \frac{1}{4}\mathcal{F}^{+}(2a)\,.

(107)

Model 6Aflip contains different matter content, consisting of two fundamental representations with flipped parity and a
3
index antisymmetric representation
A_{3}
. The fermionic contributions to the effective potential can be computed in a similar way:

\mathcal{V}_{\rm fermions}^{\rm 6Aflip}(a)=n_{g}\ 4(\mathcal{F}^{+}(a)+\mathcal{F}^{-}(a))=n_{g}\frac{1}{4}\mathcal{F}^{+}(2a)\,,

(108)

which is accidentally the same as model 6A.
Hence, both models generate the same effective potential for the gauge-scalar, giving

V_{\rm eff}(a)=C\left[(n_{g}-3)\mathcal{F}^{+}(2a)-8(\mathcal{F}^{+}(a)+3\mathcal{F}^{-}(a))\right]\,.

(109)

The profile of the effective potential for
n_{g}=3
is plotted in Fig.
3
. We see that the gauge contribution determines the global minimum at
a=1/2
, which corresponds to an orbifold with a three-block symmetry breaking

\text{SU}(6)\to\text{SU}(4)\times\text{U}(1)\times\text{U}(1)\,,

(110)

incompatible with the SM. Reducing the number of bulk generations to
n_{g}=1
or
2
, as is possible in model 6Aflip, does not affect the global minimum, however, it will induce a metastable local minimum at
a=0
.

3.3
\text{SO}(10)\to\text{SU}(4)\times\text{SU}(2)\times\text{SU}(2)

This model has been studied in detail in Ref.
[
44
]
, and we will recap here the main features. The orbifold parity scheme reads

\begin{array}[]{rll}P_{1}&=&{\rm diag}(+1,\cdots,+1,+1,\cdots,+1)\\
P_{2}&=&{\rm diag}(\underbrace{+1,\cdots,+1}_{p},\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{q})\end{array}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\,,

(111)

leading to the breaking pattern

\text{SO}(10)\to\text{SO}(6)\times\text{SO}(4)\equiv\text{SU}(4)\times\text{SU}(2)\times\text{SU}(2)\,

(112)

without a massless gauge-scalar. The SM fermions in PS transform as a left-handed
({\bf 4,2,1})
and a right-handed
({\bf 4,1,2})
, which can be obtained as zero modes of a
\bf 16
and
\bf\overline{16}
in the bulk
[
44
]
. A bulk Yukawa, therefore, requires the addition of a real scalar
\bf 10
. Using the results in Ref.
[
46
]
, combined with the computations of the coupling running in Ref.
[
44
]
, we find the following renormalisation coefficients:

b_{5}=\frac{167-32n_{g}}{6}\,,\qquad d_{y}=\frac{171}{8}\,,\qquad c_{yy}=90\,.

(113)

Hence, we determine the two fixed points to be

\tilde{\alpha}^{\ast}_{g}=\frac{12\pi}{167-32n_{g}}\,,\qquad\tilde{\alpha}_{y}^{\ast}=\frac{(128n_{g}-155)\pi}{180(167-32n_{g})}\,.

(114)

We can deduce that a gauge fixed point exists for a number of generations up to
5
, and that the existence of a fixed point for the Yukawas excludes the case of
n_{g}=1
. We are therefore left with
2\leq n_{g}\leq 5
.

While this model is apparently viable, one issue highlighted in Ref.
[
44
]
is that, for three bulk generations, the value of the fixed point seems incompatible with the low energy value of the top Yukawa. Henceforth we deem the
\text{SO}(10)
aGUT to be disfavoured.

3.4
Stable
\text{SU}(8)\to\text{SU}(4)\times\text{SU}(2)\times\text{SU}(2)\times\text{U}(1)^{2}
model

A PS model can also be obtained from a bulk
\text{SU}(8)
via the following parity scheme:

\displaystyle P_{1}=(+1\cdots,+1,+1,\cdots,+1,-1,\cdots,-1)\,,

(115)

\displaystyle P_{2}=(\underbrace{+1,\cdots,+1}_{p=4},\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{q=2},\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{s=2})\,,

which also leaves two unbroken
\text{U}(1)
charges and features one massless gauge-scalar in the representation

\varphi_{0}=({\bf 4,1,2})_{1,0}+({\bf\bar{4},1,2})_{-1,0}\,,

(116)

where the notation indicates the quantum numbers in the form

(\text{SU}(4),\text{SU}(2),\text{SU}(2))_{\text{U}(1)_{1},\text{U}(1)_{2}}\,.

(117)

It is convenient to identify the second
\text{SU}(2)
as
\text{SU}(2)_{R}
, so that the gauge-scalar may be used to break the PS symmetry, if it develops a non-trivial VEV.

The SM fermions, transforming as left-handed
({\bf 4,2,1})
and right-handed
({\bf 4,1,2})
ones, can be minimally embedded in a two-index antisymmetric
\bf 28
representation, which decomposes as

\mathbf{28}=({\bf 6,1,1})_{2,0}^{(+,+)}+({\bf 1,1,1})_{-2,2}^{(+,+)}+({\bf 1,1,1})_{-2,-2}^{(+,+)}+({\bf 4,2,1})_{0,1}^{(+,-)}+({\bf 4,1,2})_{0,-1}^{(-,-)}+({\bf 1,2,2})_{-2,0}^{(-,+)}\,.

(118)

For each component, we indicate the parities given by the parity matrix. Henceforth, the left-handed multiplet can be obtained from a
\Psi_{{\bf 28}{\rm L}}^{(+,-)}
, while the right-handed ones from
\Psi_{{\bf 28}{\rm R}}^{(+,+)}
. As one can deduce from Eq. (
118
), both fields contain additional zero modes, which are in real or pseudo-real representations of the PS group, hence they can be given a mass via the breaking of the two
\text{U}(1)
’s. This case is similar to what we encountered in the exceptional aGUT based on E
6

[
45
]
and, while non-minimal, we will consider it as a viable model.

The unified PS Yukawa can only be written down via a Higgs field embedded in the adjoint of
\text{SU}(8)
, whose intrinsic parities are:

\mathbf{63}=({\bf 15,1,1})_{0,0}^{(+,+)}+({\bf 1,3,1})_{0,0}^{(+,+)}+({\bf 1,1,3})_{0,0}^{(+,+)}+({\bf 1,1,1})_{0,0}^{(+,+)}+({\bf 1,1,1})_{0,0}^{(+,+)}+\\
\left[({\bf 4,2,1})_{1,-2}^{(+,-)}+({\bf 4,1,2})_{1,0}^{(-,-)}+({\bf 1,2,2})_{0,2}^{(-,+)}+\mbox{c.c.}\right]\,,

(119)

where c.c. indicates the conjugate representations with the same parities. Hence, we add a real bulk scalar field
\Phi_{\bf 63}^{(-,+)}
, which only contains a complex bi-doublet of
\text{SU}(2)_{L}\times\text{SU}(2)_{R}
at the zero mode level. The bulk Yukawa will, therefore, be given by:

\mathcal{L}_{\rm Yuk}\supset-Y_{\rm bulk}\overline{\Psi}_{{\bf 28}{\rm L}}\Phi_{\bf 63}\Psi_{{\bf 28}{\rm R}}+\mbox{h.c.}\,.

(120)

To check if a fixed point for both gauge and Yukawa couplings exist, we computed the 5D running coefficients, obtaining

b_{5}=\frac{8(10-3n_{g})}{3}\,,\;\;d_{y}=\frac{157}{4}\,,\;\;c_{yy}=\frac{1}{8}\,.

(121)

Hence, a UV fixed point for the gauge,
b_{5}>0
, exists only for
n_{g}\leq 3
. The same occurs for the Yukawa, leading to the fixed point values

\tilde{\alpha}_{g}^{\ast}=\frac{3\pi}{4(10-3n_{g})}\,,\qquad\tilde{\alpha}_{y}^{\ast}=\frac{(96n_{g}+151)\pi}{2(10-3n_{g})}\,.

(122)

Note that for
n_{g}=3
, the Yukawa fixed point is non-perturbative, so in a realistic model one may need to localise one or two generations to the orbifold boundary.

Finally, we computed the effective potential for the gauge-scalar. Being in a
({\bf 4,1,2})
representation of the PS gauge group, it can have two independent VEVs, which we label
a
and
b
. The contribution of the relevant representations gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{Adj}(a,b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{5}{4}\left(\mathcal{F}^{+}(2a)+\mathcal{F}^{+}(2b)\right)+2\left(\mathcal{F}^{+}(a+b)+\mathcal{F}^{+}(a-b)\right)\,,

(123)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\rm fermions}(a,b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\left(\mathcal{F}^{+}(2a)+\mathcal{F}^{+}(2b)\right)+\frac{1}{16}\left(\mathcal{F}^{+}(2a+2b)+\mathcal{F}^{+}(2a-2b)\right)\,,

(124)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\rm scalar}(a,b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4}\left(\mathcal{F}^{+}(2a)+\mathcal{F}^{+}(2b)\right)+\mathcal{F}^{-}(2a)+\mathcal{F}^{-}(2b)+

(125)

\displaystyle+2\left(\mathcal{F}^{-}(a+b)+\mathcal{F}^{-}(a-b)\right)\,,

where we have included the two
\bf 28
representations for the fermion contribution, and the adjoint with appropriate parities for the scalar one. The total potential from Eq. (
7
) reads, for
n_{g}=3
:

V_{\rm eff}(a,b)=-C\left[4\left(\mathcal{F}^{+}(a+b)+\mathcal{F}^{+}(a-b)\right)+\frac{1}{16}\left(\mathcal{F}^{+}(4a)+\mathcal{F}^{+}(4b)\right)\right.\\
\left.-\frac{5}{8}\left(\mathcal{F}^{+}(2a+2b)+\mathcal{F}^{+}(2a-2b)\right)\right]\,.

(126)

As shown in Fig.
4
, this potential has two degenerate minima for
a=b=0
and
a=b=1/2
, which correspond to the same orbifold and are also preferred by the gauge contribution to the potential. The same structure remains for fewer bulk generations. This result confirms the stability analysis from the previous section.

3.5
Unstable
\text{SU}(8)\to\text{SU}(4)\times\text{SU}(2)\times\text{SU}(2)\times\text{U}(1)^{2}

An interesting aGUT PS model can also be obtained by changing the parities to

\displaystyle P_{1}=(+1\cdots,+1,+1,\cdots,+1,-1,\cdots,-1),

(127)

\displaystyle P_{2}=(\underbrace{+1,\cdots,+1}_{p=4},\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{q=2},\underbrace{+1,\cdots,+1}_{r=2})\,,

which leads to an unstable orbifold, with a gauge-scalar transforming as
({\bf 1,2,2})
, i.e. the SM Higgs doublet.
In fact, the intrinsic parities of the adjoint read:

\mathbf{63}=({\bf 15,1,1})_{0,0}^{(+,+)}+({\bf 1,3,1})_{0,0}^{(+,+)}+({\bf 1,1,3})_{0,0}^{(+,+)}+({\bf 1,1,1})_{0,0}^{(+,+)}+({\bf 1,1,1})_{0,0}^{(+,+)}+\\
\left[({\bf 4,2,1})_{1,-2}^{(+,-)}+({\bf 4,1,2})_{1,0}^{(-,+)}+({\bf 1,2,2})_{0,2}^{(-,-)}+\mbox{c.c.}\right]\,.

(128)

As the intrinsic parities of the antisymmetric
\bf 28
representation now read

\mathbf{28}=({\bf 6,1,1})_{2,0}^{(+,+)}+({\bf 1,1,1})_{-2,2}^{(+,+)}+({\bf 1,1,1})_{-2,-2}^{(+,+)}+({\bf 4,2,1})_{0,1}^{(+,-)}+({\bf 4,1,2})_{0,-1}^{(-,+)}+({\bf 1,2,2})_{-2,0}^{(-,-)}\,,

(129)

a SM generation can be obtained from a single bulk field
\Psi_{\bf 28}^{(+,-)}
without any extra zero modes. Furthermore, all Yukawa couplings stem from the gauge interactions, guaranteeing the presence of an attractive fixed point for both gauge and Yukawa couplings
[
46
]
. The gauge coupling running has

b_{5}=28-4n_{g}\,.

(130)

Hence a fixed point exists for
n_{g}\leq 6
.

The simplest version of this model, however, suffers from the instability issue. The contribution of the gauge and one fermion to the gauge-scalar potential reads:

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{Adj}(a,b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{+}(2a)+\mathcal{F}^{+}(2b)+2\mathcal{F}^{+}(a+b)+2\mathcal{F}^{+}(a-b)

(131)

\displaystyle+8\left(\mathcal{F}^{-}(a)+\mathcal{F}^{-}(b)\right)\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{28,(+,-)}(a,b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{-}(a+b)+\mathcal{F}^{-}(a-b)+4\left(\mathcal{F}^{+}(a)+\mathcal{F}^{+}(b)\right)\,,

(132)

where
a
and
b
are the normalised VEVs of the two doublets in the gauge-scalar. For a number of bulk generations satisfying
n_{g}\leq 6
, the potential has a minimum at the value preferred by the gauge contribution,
a=b=1/2
, which corresponds to a different orbifold with breaking

\text{SU}(8)\to\text{SU}(6)\times\text{SU}(2)\times\text{U}(1)\,,

(133)

with gauge-scalar in the bi-fundamental representation.
This conclusion may be changed once the breaking of the PS group is included, which could occur at a scale much larger than
m_{KK}
. Hence this model remains of interest while requiring some engineering to be feasible and realistic.

Another possibility arises by adding supersymmetry. The main advantage is that the loop-induced potential for the gauge-scalar vanishes, and it can only be generated by supersymmetry breaking terms. Furthermore, the running of the gauge coupling is also modified
[
46
]
, leading to

b_{5}^{\rm susy}=\frac{\pi}{2}(8-3n_{g})\,.

(134)

This allows a fixed point for up to 2 bulk families (at least one must remain localised at one boundary of the orbifold). We leave a detailed investigation of this model for future studies.

4
Summary and conclusions

Orbifolds can be used to efficiently reduce extra-dimensional theories to four-dimensional ones that resemble the Standard Model, in particular, achieving a chiral spectrum of massless fermions and breaking the large gauge symmetry groups. However, the presence of massless scalar modes from the gauge multiplet may signal an instability in the orbifold configuration. We systematically studied the stability of orbifolds based on the most general (flat) interval
S^{1}/(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\prime})
, which breaks gauge groups
\text{SU}(N)
,
\text{Sp}(2N)
and
\text{SO}(N)
. The summary of stable configurations and symmetry breaking patterns is presented in Table
1
. In particular, we find that the bulk group can only be broken to, at most, three non-Abelian subgroups. The classification we present here can be used as a starting point for the construction of models in five dimensions, especially for scenarios with a limited number of bulk matter fields, which could act as stabilising agents.

We apply our results to the case of asymptotic Grand Unification models, where the fermion content in the bulk is limited by the requirement of fixed points for the gauge and Yukawa couplings. Henceforth, we have shown that the stability criterion is an effective way to skim off unrealistic models, and it complements the classification procedure delineated in Ref.
[
46
]
. As a consequence, from all possible aGUTs based on
\text{SU}(N)
,
\text{Sp}(2N)
or
\text{SO}(N)
, we identify only two feasible minimal models:

A)

An
\text{SU}(6)
model leading to the Standard Model at low energies;

B)

An
\text{SU}(8)
model leading to Pati-Salam at low energies.

Both models are based on a stable orbifold, and feature fixed points for both gauge and bulk Yukawa couplings. For the
\text{SU}(6)
model, the number of bulk generations is fixed to be 3, while the
\text{SU}(8)
model allows up to 3 generations in the bulk. An
\text{SO}(10)
model leading to PS also seems viable, however, the Yukawa fixed point predicted for three generations is at odds with the low energy value of the top Yukawa. Exceptional groups will be studied in a forthcoming publication.

Our results show that constraining requirements allow us to pin down
unique
candidates for aGUT models. Note, however, that we based our analysis on minimality. Some models based on unstable orbifolds may be salvaged if the potential for the massless gauge-scalar is modified, for instance via the inclusion of strong localised interactions. This leaves the door open for the construction of
lemon aGUT
models, based on the inclusion of ad-hoc non-minimal terms. Finally, some models may benefit from the addition of supersymmetry, which ensures the vanishing of the gauge-scalar potential. Hence, we identified a second
\text{SU}(8)
model, where supersymmetry allows us to render the orbifold stable and the scalar potential is generated by a supersymmetry breaking mechanism.

Appendices

Appendix A
Parity definition on orbifolds

In this Appendix, we will derive the general form of the orbifold parities depending on the gauge group we consider in the bulk. The parity matrices are elements of the Cartan subalgebra of the gauge group with an overall phase

P=e^{i\theta_{0}}\ \Omega(\theta_{j})\,,

(135)

to which the additional condition
P\cdot P=\mathbb{1}
is imposed.

A.1
\text{SU}(N)

The Cartan subalgebra is generated by the diagonal generators

X_{j}^{C}=\mbox{diag}(0,\dots 0,1,0,\dots-1)\,,\;\;j=1,\dots N-1\,,

(136)

where the “1” is in the
j
-th position. Hence,

\Omega_{\text{SU}(N)}=\mbox{diag}(e^{i\theta_{1}},\ \dots,e^{i\theta_{N-1}},\prod_{j}e^{-i\theta_{i}})\,.

(137)

This implies that the most general parity matrix will be diagonal, and can only have
\pm 1
entries. To obtain the most general combination, one can choose
\theta_{j}=-\theta_{0}
or
\pi-\theta_{0}
, so that

P\left(\theta_{0},\theta_{i}\in\{-\theta_{0},\pi-\theta_{0}\}\right)={\rm diag}(\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{\alpha},\underbrace{+1,\cdots,+1}_{N-1-\alpha},(-1)^{\alpha}e^{iN\theta_{0}})\,,\quad\alpha\in[\![1,N-1]\!]

(138)

where we can choose
N\theta_{0}=\pi
or
0
. In summary, the most general
\text{SU}(N)
parity matrix reads

P_{\text{SU}(N)}={\rm diag}(\underbrace{1,\cdots,1}_{A},\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{N-A})\,,

(139)

with
A\in[\![1,N]\!]
. This parity yields the breaking pattern

\text{SU}(N)\to\text{SU}(A)\times\text{SU}(N-A)\times\text{U}(1)\,.

(140)

A.2
\text{Sp}(2N)

The generators of the Cartan subalgebra are given by:

X_{j}=\mbox{diag}(0,\dots 0,1,0,\dots 0)\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\,,\;\;j=1,\dots N\,,

(141)

where the “1” is in the j-th position. Hence, a general element of the Cartan subalgebra reads

\Omega_{\text{Sp}(2N)}=\mbox{diag}(e^{i\theta_{1}}\,,\dots e^{i\theta_{N}},e^{-i\theta_{1}},\dots e^{-i\theta_{N}})\,.

(142)

There are two independent choices for the definition of a parity:

I)

One can choose
\theta_{0}=0
and
\theta_{i}=0
or
\pi
, thus obtaining:

P^{\rm I}_{\text{Sp}(2N)}={\rm diag}(\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{A},\underbrace{+1,\cdots,+1}_{N-A})\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}=P_{\text{SU}(N)}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\,,

(143)

which corresponds to the breaking

\text{Sp}(2N)\to\text{Sp}(2A)\times\text{Sp}(2(N-A))\,.

(144)

II)

One can choose
\theta_{0}=\pi/2
and
\theta_{i}=\pm\pi/2
, thus obtaining

P^{\rm II}_{\text{Sp}(2N)}={\rm diag}(\underbrace{-1,\cdots,-1}_{A},\underbrace{+1,\cdots,+1}_{N-A})\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}=P_{\text{SU}(N)}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\,,

(145)

yielding the pattern

\text{Sp}(2N)\to\text{SU}(N)\times\text{U}(1)\,.

(146)

The fact that the breaking does not depend on
A
means that one can always flip signs in the parity, leading to:

P^{\rm II}_{\text{Sp}(2N)}={\rm diag}(\underbrace{1,\cdots,1}_{N})\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\,.

(147)

Hence, the
\text{Sp}(2N)
case features two qualitatively different parity matrices.

A.3
\text{SO}(2N)

The Cartan subalgebra is generated by the following
N
anti-symmetric matrices:

X_{j}=\sigma_{2}\otimes\mbox{diag}(0,\dots 0,1,0\dots 0)\,,\;\;j=1,\dots N\,,

(148)

where, again, the “1” is in the j-th position. It is more convenient to rewrite it in a form resembling the structure for
\text{Sp}(2N)
, so that

\tilde{X}_{j}=\mbox{diag}(0,\dots 0,1,0\dots 0)\otimes\sigma_{2}\,,\;\;j=1,\dots N\,.

(149)

The generators can be written in terms of four
N\times N
blocks as

X=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
-B^{T}&D\end{pmatrix}\,,

(150)

where
A^{T}=-A
and
D^{T}=-D
. We can construct the
\text{SO}(2N)
transformation,

\Omega_{\text{SO}(2N)}=\sum_{i}\mbox{diag}(0,\dots 0,1,0\dots 0)\otimes e^{i\theta_{j}\sigma_{2}}\,,

(151)

where

e^{i\theta_{i}\sigma_{2}}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta_{i}&\sin\theta_{i}\\
-\sin\theta_{i}&\cos\theta_{i}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(152)

We notice that
\Omega_{\text{SO}(2N)}\cdot\Omega_{\text{SO}(2N)}
is made of blocks proportional to
e^{i2\theta_{j}\sigma_{2}}
. There are two choices of
\theta_{j}
to make it proportional to the identity:

e^{i2\theta_{j}\sigma_{2}}=-\mathbb{1}_{2}\;\;\mbox{for}\;\;\theta_{j}=\frac{\pi}{2},\ \frac{3\pi}{2}\,,\qquad e^{i2\theta_{j}\sigma_{2}}=\mathbb{1}_{2}\;\;\mbox{for}\;\;\theta_{j}=0,\ \pi\,.

(153)

Hence, there are two ways to construct a parity matrix:

I)

By choosing
\theta_{j}=0
or
\pi
, we can build diagonal matrices as follows:

P_{\text{SO}(2N)}^{\rm I}=\Omega_{\text{SO}(2N)}=P_{\text{SU}(N)}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\,,

(154)

which gives rise to the breaking

\text{SO}(2N)\to\text{SO}(2A)\times\text{SO}(2(N-A))\,.

(155)

II)

By choosing
\theta_{j}=\pi/2
or
3\pi/2
, we define

P_{\text{SO}(2N)}^{\rm II}=i\Omega_{\text{SO}(2N)}=P_{\text{SU}(N)}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix}\,,

(156)

which triggers the breaking

\text{SO}(2N)\to\text{SU}(N)\times\text{U}(1)\,.

(157)

Note, however, that this parity will act non-trivially on the four blocks of the generators. In fact, we can write explicitly

P^{\rm II}_{\text{SO}(2N)}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-iP_{\text{SU}(N)}\\
iP_{\text{SU}(N)}&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(158)

Hence,

P^{\rm II}_{\text{SO}(2N)}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
-B^{T}&D\end{pmatrix}\cdot P^{\rm II}_{\text{SO}(2N)}=\begin{pmatrix}P_{\text{SU}(N)}\cdot D\cdot P_{\text{SU}(N)}&-P_{\text{SU}(N)}\cdot B^{T}\cdot P_{\text{SU}(N)}\\
P_{\text{SU}(N)}\cdot B\cdot P_{\text{SU}(N)}&P_{\text{SU}(N)}\cdot A\cdot P_{\text{SU}(N)}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(159)

One can define combinations of the matrices
A
,
B
and
D
with definite parities as follows:

A_{\pm}=A\pm D\,,\qquad B=B_{a}+B_{s}\,,

(160)

where the subscripts indicate the symmetric and antisymmetric components of
B
(i.e.,
B_{s}^{T}=B_{s}
and
B_{a}^{T}=-B_{a}
), such that

\displaystyle A_{\pm}

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\pm P_{\text{SU}(N)}\cdot A_{\pm}\cdot P_{\text{SU}(N)}\,,

(161)

\displaystyle B_{a/s}

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\pm P_{\text{SU}(N)}\cdot B_{a/s}\cdot P_{\text{SU}(N)}\,.

(162)

In cases where this type of parity is employed, it is better to use
A_{\pm}
and
B_{a/s}
as defining blocks for the generators. Note also that this is compatible with the other type of parity, for which all blocks will receive the same parity assignments. Thus, we will use the notations

X=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}A_{+}/2&B_{s}\\
-B_{s}&A_{+}/2\end{pmatrix}}_{X_{+}}+\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}A_{-}/2&B_{a}\\
B_{a}&-A_{-}/2\end{pmatrix}}_{X_{-}},

(163)

where
X_{\pm}
schematically transform under
P^{\rm II}
as

\displaystyle X_{+}

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle P_{\text{SU}(N)}\cdot X_{+}\cdot P_{\text{SU}(N)}\,,

(164)

\displaystyle X_{-}

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle-P_{\text{SU}(N)}\cdot X_{-}\cdot P_{\text{SU}(N)}\,.

(165)

The projection matrices corresponding to the action of
P^{\rm II}
on
X_{\pm}
are

\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}\>\>\>\text{and}\>\>\>\begin{pmatrix}-1&-1\\
-1&-1\end{pmatrix}\,.

(166)

Also, the adjoint Adj will decompose as

\mbox{Adj}\to\mbox{Adj}\oplus S_{x}\oplus\bar{S}_{-x}\,.

(167)

A.4
\text{SO}(2N+1)

For odd-dimension orthogonal groups, the Cartan subalgebra has the same dimension as the corresponding even
\text{SO}(2N)
group. Hence, the generators have the same form, with an additional entry that remains empty. Because of the odd dimension, one can only define parities of type
P^{\rm I}_{\text{SO}}
similar to the even case.

Appendix B
General formulas for the effective potential

In this Appendix, we will detail the computation of the Coleman-Weinberg effective potential for each gauge group in the bulk.

B.1
\text{SU}(N)

We consider here the most general parity assignment, leading to a four-block symmetry breaking pattern
[
49
]
. From Eq. (
22
), we can deduce that the gauge-scalar zero modes always transform as bi-fundamental representations. Without loss of generality, their VEVs can be expressed in diagonal block form, with dimensions
n_{ps}=\mbox{min}(p,s)
and
n_{qr}=\mbox{min}(q,r)
, respectively. As a starter, we can consider a matrix restricted to only rows and columns where a VEV appears

(P_{1},P_{2})(A_{\mu}^{*})=\begin{pNiceMatrix}n_{ps}&n_{qr}&n_{qr}&n_{ps}&\\
(+,+)&(+,-)&(-,+)&(-,-)&n_{ps}\\
(+,-)&(+,+)&(-,-)&(-,+)&n_{qr}\\
(-,+)&(-,-)&(+,+)&(+,-)&n_{qr}\\
(-,-)&(-,+)&(+,-)&(+,+)&n_{ps}\\
\end{pNiceMatrix}\,.

(168)

The VEVs can be written in terms of a ‘charge’ matrix

Q=\begin{pNiceMatrix}n_{ps}&n_{qr}&n_{qr}&n_{ps}&\\
0&0&0&\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]&n_{ps}\\
0&0&\mbox{diag}[b_{j}]&0&n_{qr}\\
0&\mbox{diag}[b_{j}]&0&0&n_{qr}\\
\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]&0&0&0&n_{ps}\\
\end{pNiceMatrix}\,.

(169)

This block matrix can be diagonalised via

V=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{1}_{n_{ps}}&0&0&-\mathbb{1}_{n_{ps}}\\
0&\mathbb{1}_{n_{qr}}&-\mathbb{1}_{n_{qr}}&0\\
0&\mathbb{1}_{n_{qr}}&\mathbb{1}_{n_{qr}}&0\\
\mathbb{1}_{n_{ps}}&0&0&\mathbb{1}_{n_{ps}}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(170)

so that

Q^{\mbox{diag}}=V\cdot Q\cdot V^{T}=\begin{pmatrix}-\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]&0&0&0\\
0&-\mbox{diag}[b_{j}]&0&0\\
0&0&\mbox{diag}[b_{j}]&0\\
0&0&0&\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]\end{pmatrix}\,.

(171)

Note that, once applied to
A_{\mu}
, the rotation
V
only mixes states with the same KK mass, as
(+,+)
and
(-,-)
have KK mass
n/R
, while
(+,-)
and
(-,+)
have KK mass
(n+1/2)/R
. In the diagonal basis, the KK spectrum coming from the various blocks can be derived by computing the VEV ‘charge’ of the various components:

[Q,A_{\mu}^{\ast}]\to\begin{pNiceMatrix}n_{ps}&n_{qr}&n_{qr}&n_{ps}&\\
n-a_{i}+a_{k}&n+\frac{1}{2}-a_{i}+b_{l}&n+\frac{1}{2}-a_{i}-b_{l}&n-a_{i}-a_{k}&n_{ps}\\
n+\frac{1}{2}-b_{j}+a_{k}&n-b_{j}+b_{l}&n-b_{j}-b_{l}&n+\frac{1}{2}-b_{j}-a_{k}&n_{qr}\\
n+\frac{1}{2}+b_{j}+a_{k}&n+b_{j}+b_{l}&n+b_{j}-b_{l}&n+\frac{1}{2}+b_{j}-a_{k}&n_{qr}\\
n+a_{i}+a_{k}&n+\frac{1}{2}+a_{i}+b_{l}&n+\frac{1}{2}+a_{i}-b_{l}&n+a_{i}-a_{k}&n_{ps}\\
\end{pNiceMatrix}\,.

(172)

The components we discarded also receive a mass contribution from the VEV ‘charges’. If we consider the first row of the matrix, the
(+,+)
block has
|p-s|
off-diagonal components that receive charges
a_{i}
, while the
(+,-)
block has
|q-r|
components receiving charges
a_{i}
. Similarly, in the second row, the
(+,+)
block has
|q-r|
components receiving charges
b_{j}
and the
(-,+)
block has
|p-s|
components receiving charges
b_{j}
.

Putting all the results together, the most general potential from the adjoint representation reads

\displaystyle\left.\mathcal{V}_{Adj}\right|_{\text{SU}(N)}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,k=1}^{n_{ps}}\left(\mathcal{F}^{+}(a_{i}+a_{k})+\mathcal{F}^{+}(a_{i}-a_{k})\right)+\sum_{j,l=1}^{n_{qr}}\left(\mathcal{F}^{+}(b_{j}+b_{l})+\mathcal{F}^{+}(b_{j}-b_{l})\right)

\displaystyle+2\sum_{i=1}^{n_{ps}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{qr}}\left(\mathcal{F}^{-}(a_{i}+b_{j})+\mathcal{F}^{-}(a_{i}-b_{j})\right)

\displaystyle+2|p-s|\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{ps}}\mathcal{F}^{+}(a_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{qr}}\mathcal{F}^{-}(b_{j})\right)+2|q-r|\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{ps}}\mathcal{F}^{-}(a_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{qr}}\mathcal{F}^{+}(b_{j})\right)\,,

(173)

in agreement with Ref.
[
49
]
. We will use the same VEV ‘charge’ technique for the other general cases.

B.2
\text{Sp}(2N)

We need to distinguish three cases, depending on the type of parity applied on the two boundaries: I+I, I+II, II+II.

B.2.1
Case I+I

In this case, the most general configuration breaks
\text{Sp}(2N)
into four subgroups. The most general parities for the gauge multiplet read

(P_{1},P_{2})(A_{\mu})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\begin{array}[]{cccc}{(+,+)}&(+,-)&(-,+)&{(-,-)}\\
(+,-)&{(+,+)}&{(-,-)}&(-,+)\\
(-,+)&{(-,-)}&{(+,+)}&(+,-)\\
{(-,-)}&(-,+)&(+,-)&{(+,+)}\\
\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{cccc}{(+,+)}&(+,-)&(-,+)&{(-,-)}\\
(+,-)&{(+,+)}&{(-,-)}&(-,+)\\
(-,+)&{(-,-)}&{(+,+)}&(+,-)\\
{(-,-)}&(-,+)&(+,-)&{(+,+)}\\
\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{cccc}{(+,+)}&(+,-)&(-,+)&{(-,-)}\\
(+,-)&{(+,+)}&{(-,-)}&(-,+)\\
(-,+)&{(-,-)}&{(+,+)}&(+,-)\\
{(-,-)}&(-,+)&(+,-)&{(+,+)}\\
\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{cccc}{(+,+)}&(+,-)&(-,+)&{(-,-)}\\
(+,-)&{(+,+)}&{(-,-)}&(-,+)\\
(-,+)&{(-,-)}&{(+,+)}&(+,-)\\
{(-,-)}&(-,+)&(+,-)&{(+,+)}\\
\end{array}\end{array}\right)\,.

(174)

The gauge-scalars transform as bi-fundamental representations of
\text{Sp}(2p)\times\text{Sp}(2s)
and of
\text{Sp}(2q)\times\text{Sp}(2r)
. We recall that the structure of the generators is as follows

X_{\text{Sp}(2N)}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}A&B\\
\hline\cr C&-A^{T}\end{array}\right)\,,

(175)

where
B
and
C
are symmetric matrices.
Each gauge-scalar should allow for
n_{ps}
and
n_{qr}
independent VEVs

Q=i\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc|cccc}0&0&0&{\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]}&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&{\mbox{diag}[b_{j}]}&0&0&0&0&0\\
0&{-\mbox{diag}[b_{j}]}&0&0&0&0&0&0\\
{-\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]}&0&0&0&0&0&0&0\\
\hline\cr 0&0&0&0&0&0&0&{\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]}\\
0&0&0&0&0&0&{\mbox{diag}[b_{j}]}&0\\
0&0&0&0&0&{-\mbox{diag}[b_{j}]}&0&0\\
0&0&0&0&{-\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]}&0&0&0\\
\end{array}\right)\,.

(176)

The complex VEV is chosen for convenience, and we can check that
a_{i}=a
and
b_{j}=b
preserves a
\text{Sp}(2n_{ps})\times\text{Sp}(2n_{qr})
subgroup, hence, it is the most general maximally breaking VEV structure. The above charge can be diagonalised via a block matrix:

V=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\mathcal{V}&0\\
\hline\cr 0&\mathcal{V}\end{array}\right)\qquad\mbox{with}\quad\mathcal{V}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{1}_{n_{ps}}&0&0&-i\mathbb{1}_{n_{ps}}\\
0&\mathbb{1}_{n_{qr}}&-i\mathbb{1}_{n_{qr}}&0\\
0&-i\mathbb{1}_{n_{qr}}&\mathbb{1}_{n_{qr}}&0\\
-i\mathbb{1}_{n_{ps}}&0&0&\mathbb{1}_{n_{ps}}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(177)

The diagonal VEV ‘charge’ matrix reads:

V\cdot Q\cdot V^{\dagger}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc|cccc}{-\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]}&0&0&0&0&0&0&0\\
0&{-\mbox{diag}[b_{j}]}&0&0&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&{\mbox{diag}[b_{j}]}&0&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&{\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]}&0&0&0&0\\
\hline\cr 0&0&0&0&{-\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]}&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&0&0&{-\mbox{diag}[b_{j}]}&0&0\\
0&0&0&0&0&0&{\mbox{diag}[b_{j}]}&0\\
0&0&0&0&0&0&0&{\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]}\\
\end{array}\right)\,.

(178)

The structure of the VEV ‘charges’ is the same as for the
\text{SU}(N)
case. We can then conclude that block
A
provides the same contribution to the potential as the adjoint of
\text{SU}(N)
and each
B
and
C
blocks give half the contribution of the adjoint of
\text{SU}(N)
. This can be deduced by the fact that diagonal elements have zero charge, so only the off-diagonal ones contribute. Thus the symmetric part of the
\text{SU}(N)
adjoint constitute half of the total effective potential. Summing the contribution of
A
,
B
and

\left.\mathcal{V}_{Adj}^{\rm I+I}\right|_{\text{Sp}(2N)}=2\ \left.\mathcal{V}_{Adj}\right|_{\text{SU}(N)}\,.

(179)

B.2.2
Case I+II

In this case, two
\text{U}(n)
symmetries are preserved. The most general parity configuration reads

(P_{1},P_{2})(A_{\mu})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\begin{array}[]{cc}{(+,+)}&(+,-)\\
(+,-)&{(+,+)}\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{cc}(-,+)&{(-,-)}\\
{(-,-)}&(-,+)\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{cc}(-,+)&{(-,-)}\\
{(-,-)}&(-,+)\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{cc}{(+,+)}&(+,-)\\
(+,-)&{(+,+)}\end{array}\end{array}\right)\,,

(180)

hence, the most general VEV structure is as follows

Q=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc|cc}0&0&0&\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]\\
0&0&\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]&0\\
\hline\cr 0&\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]&0&0\\
\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]&0&0&0\\
\end{array}\right)\,.

(181)

This VEV ‘charge’ matrix can be diagonalised to

Q^{\rm diag}=V\cdot Q\cdot V^{T}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc|cc}-\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]&0&0&0\\
0&\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]&0&0\\
\hline\cr 0&0&-\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]&0\\
0&0&0&\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]\\
\end{array}\right)\,.

(182)

We can immediately infer that the
A
block of the
\text{Sp}(2N)
adjoint will contribute as the adjoint of
\text{SU}(N)
with only one type of VEV. The
B
and
C
blocks together contribute as an
\text{SU}(N)
adjoint with one parity flipped. Concretely, the effective potential is the
\text{SU}(N)
one with the exchange of
\mathcal{F}^{+}
and
\mathcal{F}^{-}
. All in all, we find:

\displaystyle\left.\mathcal{V}^{\rm I+II}_{Adj}\right|_{\text{Sp}(2N)}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{16}\left[\sum_{m,n=1}^{n_{pq}}\left(\mathcal{F}^{+}(2a_{m}+2a_{n})+\mathcal{F}^{+}(2a_{m}-2a_{n})\right)+2|p-q|\;\sum_{m=1}^{n_{pq}}\mathcal{F}^{+}(2a_{m})\right]\,,

(183)

where
n_{pq}=\mbox{min}[p,q]
.

B.2.3
Case II+II

We have seen that the most general configurations for the parities lead to:

(P_{1},P_{2})(A_{\mu})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\begin{array}[]{cc}{(+,+)}&(+,-)\\
(+,-)&{(+,+)}\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{cc}{(-,-)}&(-,+)\\
(-,+)&{(-,-)}\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{cc}{(-,-)}&(-,+)\\
(-,+)&{(-,-)}\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{cc}{(+,+)}&(+,-)\\
(+,-)&{(+,+)}\end{array}\end{array}\right)\,,

(184)

such that the gauge-scalars
\varphi_{p}
and
\varphi_{q}
can be found in the symmetric representations of
\text{U}(p)
and
\text{U}(q)
, respectively. Without loss of generality, we can define the VEVs of the gauge-scalars in the following way:

\langle\varphi_{p}\rangle={\rm diag}[a_{i}]\,,~~~~~\langle\varphi_{q}\rangle={\rm diag}[b_{j}]\,.

(185)

Thus the charge matrix reads

Q=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc|cc}0&0&\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]&0\\
0&0&0&\mbox{diag}[b_{j}]\\
\hline\cr\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]&0&0&0\\
0&\mbox{diag}[b_{j}]&0&0\\
\end{array}\right)\,.

(186)

This VEV ‘charge’ matrix can also be diagonalised to simplify the computation of the contributions to the effective potential. We end up with a very similar result as in
\text{SU}(N)
, except that the off-diagonal terms are not there as we are working with symmetric representations. The effective potential is then

\begin{split}\left.\mathcal{V}^{{\rm II+II}}_{Adj}\right|_{\text{Sp}(2N)}=&\sum_{i,k=1}^{p}\left(\mathcal{F}^{+}(a_{i}+a_{k})+\mathcal{F}^{+}(a_{i}-a_{k})\right)+\sum_{j,l=1}^{q}\left(\mathcal{F}^{+}(b_{j}+b_{l})+\mathcal{F}^{+}(b_{j}-b_{l})\right)\\
&+2\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sum_{j=1}^{q}\left(\mathcal{F}^{-}(a_{i}+b_{j})+\mathcal{F}^{-}(a_{i}-b_{j})\right)\,.\end{split}

(187)

B.3
\text{SO}(2N)

As already described for the
\text{Sp}(2N)
scenario, there are three independent cases based on the parity types.

B.3.1
Case I+I

The parity assignments of the adjoint representation are the same as in the
\text{Sp}(2N)
case. We note that the gauge-scalars transform as bi-fundamentals of
\text{SO}(2p)\times\text{SO}(2s)
and of
\text{SO}(2q)\times\text{SO}(2r)
and we can choose the VEV in the off-diagonal blocks, such that

Q=i\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc|cccc}0&0&0&0&0&0&0&{\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]}\\
0&0&0&0&0&0&{\mbox{diag}[b_{j}]}&0\\
0&0&0&0&0&{\mbox{diag}[b_{j}]}&0&0\\
0&0&0&0&{\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]}&0&0&0\\
\hline\cr 0&0&0&{-\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]}&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&{-\mbox{diag}[b_{j}]}&0&0&0&0&0\\
0&{-\mbox{diag}[b_{j}]}&0&0&0&0&0&0\\
{-\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]}&0&0&0&0&0&0&0\\
\end{array}\right)\,.

(188)

To find the potential, we first need to diagonalise the VEV ‘charge’ via

V=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccccc}\mathbb{1}_{n_{ps}}&0&0&0&0&0&0&-i\mathbb{1}_{n_{ps}}\\
0&\mathbb{1}_{n_{qr}}&0&0&0&0&-i\mathbb{1}_{n_{qr}}&0\\
0&0&\mathbb{1}_{n_{qr}}&0&0&-i\mathbb{1}_{n_{qr}}&0&0\\
0&0&0&\mathbb{1}_{n_{qr}}&-i\mathbb{1}_{n_{ps}}&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&-i\mathbb{1}_{n_{ps}}&\mathbb{1}_{n_{ps}}&0&0&0\\
0&0&-i\mathbb{1}_{n_{qr}}&0&0&\mathbb{1}_{n_{qr}}&0&0\\
0&-i\mathbb{1}_{n_{qr}}&0&0&0&0&\mathbb{1}_{n_{qr}}&0\\
-i\mathbb{1}_{n_{ps}}&0&0&0&0&0&0&\mathbb{1}_{n_{ps}}\\
\end{array}\right)\,,

(189)

which gives the diagonal matrix

Q^{\rm diag}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc|cccc}{\ -\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]}&0&0&0&0&0&0&0\\
0&{-\ \mbox{diag}[b_{j}]}&0&0&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&{-\ \mbox{diag}[b_{j}]}&0&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&{-\ \mbox{diag}[a_{i}]}&0&0&0&0\\
\hline\cr 0&0&0&0&{\ \mbox{diag}[a_{i}]}&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&0&0&{\ \mbox{diag}[b_{j}]}&0&0\\
0&0&0&0&0&0&{\ \mbox{diag}[b_{j}]}&0\\
0&0&0&0&0&0&0&{\ \mbox{diag}[a_{i}]}\\
\end{array}\right)\,.

(190)

The potential is then computed in a similar way as in the
\text{Sp}(2N)
case. The off-diagonal block gives the same contribution as one
\text{SU}(N)
adjoint, while the diagonal blocks give a half of an
\text{SU}(N)
contribution due to their antisymmetric nature. The final result becomes

\left.\mathcal{V}^{{\rm I+I}}_{Adj}\right|_{\text{SO}(2N)}=2\ \left.\mathcal{V}_{Adj}\right|_{\text{SU}(N)}\,.

(191)

B.3.2
Case I+II

We will consider
P_{1}
to be the type II parity and
P_{2}
to be of the type I. Defining
A_{\mu}^{+}
and
A_{\mu}^{-}
as the components of the adjoint associated to
X_{+}
and
X_{-}
, we find the following parity assignments:

(P_{1},P_{2})(A^{+}_{\mu})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\begin{array}[]{cc}{(+,+)}&(+,-)\\
(+,-)&{(+,+)}\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{cc}{(+,+)}&(+,-)\\
(+,-)&{(+,+)}\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{cc}{(+,+)}&(+,-)\\
(+,-)&{(+,+)}\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{cc}{(+,+)}&(+,-)\\
(+,-)&{(+,+)}\end{array}\end{array}\right)\,,

(192)

(P_{1},P_{2})(A^{-}_{\mu})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\begin{array}[]{cc}{(-,+)}&(-,-)\\
(-,-)&{(-,+)}\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{cc}{(-,+)}&(-,-)\\
(-,-)&{(-,+)}\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{cc}{(-,+)}&(-,-)\\
(-,-)&{(-,+)}\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{cc}{(-,+)}&(-,-)\\
(-,-)&{(-,+)}\end{array}\end{array}\right)\,.

(193)

We notice here that only
A_{\mu}^{-}
contains gauge-scalar zero modes, living in the bi-fundamental representation of
\text{U}(p)\times\text{U}(q)
. Hence, the most general structure for the VEV of
A_{5}
is

Q=i\left(\begin{array}[]{cc|cc}0&0&0&\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]\\
0&0&-\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]&0\\
\hline\cr 0&\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]&0&0\\
-\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]&0&0&0\\
\end{array}\right)\,.

(194)

Once again we can diagonalise the VEV ‘charge’, resulting in

Q^{\rm diag}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc|cc}-\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]&0&0&0\\
0&-\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]&0&0\\
\hline\cr 0&0&\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]&0\\
0&0&0&\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]\\
\end{array}\right)\,.

(195)

The generated spectrum for
A^{\pm}_{\mu}
will then be computed using
[Q^{\rm diag},A^{\pm}_{\mu}]

[Q^{\rm diag},A^{+}_{\mu}]=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\begin{array}[]{cc}0&n+\frac{1}{2}-a_{i}+a_{j}\\
n+\frac{1}{2}-a_{i}+a_{j}&0\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{cc}n-a_{i}-a_{j}&n+\frac{1}{2}-a_{i}-a_{j}\\
n+\frac{1}{2}-a_{i}-a_{j}&n-a_{i}-a_{j}\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{cc}n+a_{i}+a_{j}&n+\frac{1}{2}+a_{i}+a_{j}\\
n+\frac{1}{2}+a_{i}+a_{j}&n+a_{i}+a_{j}\\
\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{cc}0&n+\frac{1}{2}+a_{i}-a_{j}\\
n+\frac{1}{2}+a_{i}-a_{j}&0\end{array}\end{array}\right)\,,

(196)

[Q^{\rm diag},A^{-}_{\mu}]=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\begin{array}[]{cc}0&n-a_{i}+a_{j}\\
n-a_{i}+a_{j}&0\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{cc}0&n-a_{i}-a_{j}\\
n-a_{i}-a_{j}&0\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{cc}0&n+a_{i}+a_{j}\\
n+a_{i}+a_{j}&0\\
\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{cc}0&n+a_{i}-a_{j}\\
n+a_{i}-a_{j}&0\end{array}\end{array}\right)\,.

(197)

Given these spectra, we notice that the contribution from
A^{+}_{\mu}
and
A^{-}_{\mu}
are not the same under the exchange of
\mathcal{F}^{+}
and
\mathcal{F}^{-}
, which is consistent with the fact that they contain a different number of fields. To compute the potential we need to look at the substructure of Eqs. (
196
)-(
197
).
For
A^{+}_{\mu}
, the diagonal blocks give half of an
\text{SU}(N)
contribution with one type of VEV, while the off-diagonal blocks contribute schematically as:

\frac{1}{2}\left.\mathcal{V}_{Adj}\right|_{\text{SU}(N)}+[\text{diagonal elements}]\,.

(198)

For
A^{-}_{\mu}
each of the diagonal/off-diagonal blocks will give half a contribution of
\text{SU}(N)
with one parity flipped. The total potential then becomes

\begin{split}\left.\mathcal{V}^{{\rm I+II}}_{Adj}\right|_{\text{SO}(2N)}=&\sum_{i,j=1}^{n_{pq}}\left[\mathcal{F}^{+}(a_{i}+a_{j})+\mathcal{F}^{+}(a_{i}-a_{j})\right]+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n_{pq}}\left[\mathcal{F}^{-}(a_{i}+a_{j})+\mathcal{F}^{-}(a_{i}-a_{j})\right]+\\
&+2|p-q|\sum_{i,j=1}^{n_{pq}}\mathcal{F}^{+}(a_{i})+2|p-q|\sum_{i,j=1}^{n_{pq}}\mathcal{F}^{-}(a_{i})+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n_{pq}}\mathcal{F}^{+}(a_{i}+a_{j})\,.\end{split}

(199)

B.3.3
Case II+II

The last case we can look at is when both parities are of the type II. Once again, we split the components of the gauge fields depending on the
X^{+}
and
X^{-}
generators. The parity assignments are

(P_{1},P_{2})(A_{\mu}^{+})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\begin{array}[]{cc}{(+,+)}&(+,-)\\
(+,-)&{(+,+)}\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{cc}{(+,+)}&(+,-)\\
(+,-)&{(+,+)}\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{cc}{(+,+)}&(+,-)\\
(+,-)&{(+,+)}\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{cc}{(+,+)}&(+,-)\\
(+,-)&{(+,+)}\end{array}\end{array}\right)\,,

(200)

(P_{1},P_{2})(A_{\mu}^{-})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\begin{array}[]{cc}{(-,-)}&(-,+)\\
(-,+)&{(-,-)}\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{cc}{(-,-)}&(-,+)\\
(-,+)&{(-,-)}\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{cc}{(-,-)}&(-,+)\\
(-,+)&{(-,-)}\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{cc}{(-,-)}&(-,+)\\
(-,+)&{(-,-)}\end{array}\end{array}\right)\,.

(201)

The gauge-scalar zero modes can also be found in
A_{\mu}^{-}
, but this time they are in the antisymmetric representations of
\text{U}(p)
and
\text{U}(q)
. Without loss of generality, we can define a VEV for
A_{5}
in the following way:

Q=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc|cc}\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]&0&0&0\\
0&\mbox{diag}[b_{j}]&0&0\\
\hline\cr 0&0&\mbox{diag}[a_{i}]&0\\
0&0&0&\mbox{diag}[b_{j}]\\
\end{array}\right)\otimes\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(202)

This charge can be diagonalised in a similar fashion. We can compute again the spectrum for
A_{\mu}^{\pm}
. As with the case with parities I+II, some contributions in
A_{\mu}^{+}
are not found in
A_{\mu}^{-}
as the number of degrees of freedom is not the same. As we are working with antisymmetric representations, there is no off-diagonal blocks. We then get the following potential:

\begin{split}\left.\mathcal{V}^{{\rm II+II}}_{Adj}\right|_{\text{SO}(2N)}&=\frac{3}{2}\sum_{i,k=1}^{p}\left[\mathcal{F}^{+}(a_{i}+a_{k})+\mathcal{F}^{+}(a_{i}-a_{k})\right]+\frac{3}{2}\sum^{q}_{j,l=1}\left[\mathcal{F}^{+}(b_{j}+b_{l})+\mathcal{F}^{+}(b_{j}-b_{l})\right]\\
&+4\sum_{i}^{p}\sum_{j}^{q}\left[\mathcal{F}^{-}(a_{i}+b_{j})+\mathcal{F}^{-}(a_{i}-b_{j})\right]\,.\end{split}

(203)

Appendix C
Stable
\text{SO}(10)/\text{Sp}(10)\to\text{SU}(3)\times\text{SU}(2)\times\text{U}(1)^{2}

As we have seen, both
\text{Sp}(10)
and
\text{SO}(10)
can be broken to
\text{U}(3)\times\text{U}(2)
by use of a combination of parity types I and II. The parity of type II breaks the group to
\text{SU}(5)\times\text{U}(1)
, while the additional breaking of
\text{SU}(5)
can be obtained via a misalignment in the parity of type I, encoded by an
\text{SU}(5)
parity matrix
P_{5}=\mbox{diag}(+1,+1,+1,-1,-1)
. In both cases, therefore, it will be convenient to characterise the representations in terms of
\text{SU}(5)
components and their parities.

C.1
\text{Sp}(10)
model

The two most promising representations are the fundamental
\bf 10
and the antisymmetric
\bf 45
. They decompose under
\text{SU}(5)\times\text{U}(1)
as

{\bf 10}\to 5_{1}\oplus\overline{5}_{-1}\,,\quad{\bf 45}\to 10_{2}\oplus\overline{10}_{-2}\oplus 24_{0}\oplus 1_{0}\,.

(204)

Hence, the SM can be embedded inside the
\text{SU}(5)
group, broken by
P_{5}
.
The parities of the components under the SM can, therefore, be expressed in terms of the diagonal entries of
P_{5}
. For the fundamental
\bf 10
representation,

5_{1}\to(P_{5},+)\,,\qquad\overline{5}_{-1}\to(-P_{5},-)\,.

(205)

These parities match those of the minimal
\text{SU}(5)
aGUT
[
43
]
, therefore, the
\bf 10
representation can be arranged to contain the lepton doublets and the right-handed down quarks. However, for the
\bf 45
representation, we obtain

10_{2}\to(P_{5},+)\,,\quad\overline{10}_{2}\to(P_{5},+)\,,\quad 25_{0}\oplus 1_{0}\to(-P_{5},-)\,.

(206)

As the two
10
have the same parities, one cannot use them to embed the remaining SM fermions. For this reason, the
\text{Sp}(10)
case is ruled out.

C.2
\text{SO}(10)
model

A similar construction can be applied to the
\text{SO}(10)
bulk gauge group. As for
\text{Sp}(10)
, the fundamental and antisymmetric representations do not contain the SM fermions for very similar reasons. However, orthogonal groups also allow for spinorial representations, which are in fact used in the traditional
\text{SO}(10)
GUTs. Contrary to the other representations, the spinorial
\bf 16
representation of
\text{SO}(10)
is complex. Note that this orbifold has been considered in Ref.
[
52
]
.

As for the previous case, we can decompose the spinorial in terms of
\text{SU}(5)
components

{\bf 16}\to 10_{1}\oplus\overline{5}_{-3}\oplus 1_{5}\,,

(207)

with parities,

10_{1}\to(-P_{5},-)\,,\quad\overline{5}_{-3}\to(P_{5},+)\,,\quad 1_{5}\to(-,+)\,.

(208)

Comparing with the analysis in Ref.
[
43
]
, one can immediately see that the relative parities between the
10
and
\overline{5}
do not allow to embed the SM fermions. Hence, this
\text{SO}(10)
model needs to be discarded as well.
