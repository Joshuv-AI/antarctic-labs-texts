Letter to Editor: Gut‐Microbiome and Nutritional Analysis Reveals Food Intake as a Key Factor in the Incidence and Prevention of Colon Polyps: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Sundas Adnan Butt
Published: 2025

To the Editor,

I read the article, “Gut‐Microbiome and Nutritional Analysis Reveals Food Intake as a Key Factor in the Incidence and Prevention of Colon Polyps: A Cross‐Sectional Study” [1] with great interest. The article discusses a relevant concern in the public health domain because it addresses how eating habits affect the microbiota of the gut and, consequently, the risk of developing colon polyps. Colon polyps are precursors of colorectal cancer, and the presence of modifiable dietary and microbial determinants is an important advancement towards prevention. By comparing nutritional data with the microbial profiling, the authors contribute to the existing evidence that illustrates the interactions between host diet, gut microbial composition, and colorectal health. Nevertheless, despite the value and timeliness of the study, certain limitations must be addressed to improve the clinical and translational contribution of future studies in the area.

First, the cross‐sectional nature of the study design used in this study is valid in identifying associations, but it limits causal inference. It is not clear whether the microbial changes were determined by diet or whether the existing differences in microbiome determined food preference and polyp development. This shortcoming raises the risk of misinterpretation, which may result in an exaggeration of the prophylactic effect of particular dietary patterns. Similar research in Chinese cohorts has demonstrated how dietary and metabolic tracking of change over time can provide more powerful information on causality and the progression of the disease [2]. Using a one‐time‐point measure, the current study could confound correlation and causation, limiting its proper use in informing preventive interventions. In future research, prospective cohort studies or randomized intervention studies should be considered to establish the temporal and causal relationships between diet, microbiota, and colon polyp incidence. Second, systemic metabolic biomarkers, including plasma lipid and fatty acid profiles, which are increasingly being cited as central mediators of colorectal neoplasia risk, are absent in the study. Long‐chain omega‐6 fatty acids stimulate inflammation and have been associated with the development of adenoma independent of gut microbiota. This omission of systemic biochemical contributors may simplify the relationship between diet and microbiome, and the effect of metabolic inflammation underestimated in the study. Studies done on plasma phospholipid fatty acids before have shown the relevance of incorporating systemic lipid metabolism information in the evaluation of adenoma risk [3]. The inclusion of metabolomic and lipidomic profiling in future research would provide a more detailed view of the interaction between various dietary intakes and host metabolism and microbial composition to effect colon health. Third, the research does not consider the interference of the circadian rhythm due to abnormal eating habits. New data show that it is not only the type of food a person eats that has a strong effect on microbiota composition and the risk of carcinogenesis, but also the timing of food intake. Alterations in circadian regulation, microbial rhythms, and tumorigenesis in the colon are mediated by deviant dietary practices, such as eating at night or eating at odd hours. There is experimental evidence that circadian perturbation enhances alcohol‐induced carcinogenesis and manipulates the microbial communities in a way conducive to tumorigenesis [4]. Failing to consider circadian‐related eating behaviors, the research might fail to rank an influential behavioral variable with regard to polyp risk. Future research should also evaluate the timing, frequency, and circadian synchronization of meals to provide a more detailed description of how temporal dietary factors interact with the microbiome to facilitate colorectal effects. Fourth, although the inclusion of microbiome and dietary data is one of the strengths, the research did not incorporate into a more multi‐biomarker‐focused study. There is growing evidence that a combination of dietary habits, microbial evidence, inflammatory evidence, lipid measures, and tumor evidence would increase diagnostic performance and predictive ability in colorectal polyps. The complexity of polyp development is typically too complex to be described using single‐dimensional models. Multi‐marker studies using case‐control designs [5] have demonstrated that microbial data in combination with serum and inflammatory markers have a greater sensitivity and specificity in identifying precancerous lesions. The current study can also be limited in terms of leveraging the opportunities to construct clinically applicable predictive models because it focuses on a very narrow range of factors and treats microbiota as a key predictor of diet. A multi‐dimensional biomarker approach should be adapted in future studies to achieve a translation effect.

To summarize, the study offers valuable insights into the interrelation between food intake, gut microbiome, and the presence of colon polyps. However, its cross‐sectional design, and failure to use systemic bio‐markers in addition to circadian meal patterns and multi‐markers technologies constrain the extrapolative power and clinical relevance of the results. Sealing these gaps in future research would provide more definitive evidence as to causal pathways, expand the understanding of systemic and temporal intermediates of polyp risk, and reinforce diagnostic and preventive paradigms.
