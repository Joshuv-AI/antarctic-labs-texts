Perceptions of barriers and facilitators to healthy eating: a photovoice study in a Colombian Caribbean community with diabetes risk factors
Authors: Daniela Daza Juvinao, Rafael Tuesca Molina, Bree Hemingway, Tania Acosta Vergara, J E Rod
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetes is a rising but preventable health concern in Latin America and the Caribbean, where healthy eating research and behavioral interventions are crucial to reducing its global health burden. This study aimed to explore perceived barriers and facilitators of healthy eating, explain their underlying psychosocial mechanisms with the Theoretical Domains Framework and identify relevant behavioral interventions.MethodsA multimethod qualitative Photovoice study was conducted to identify themes related to healthy eating barriers and facilitators. The Theoretical Domains Framework was used to explain the psychosocial mechanisms explaining barrier and facilitator themes, and identify behavior change techniques.ResultsThirteen participants contributed 48 photos for analysis. The multimethod triangulation between thematic and content analysis highlighted that barriers are concentrated around structural factors such as the socioeconomic context, while facilitator’s themes are less concentrated and have a higher code frequency supporting skills, knowledge and personal factors. The environmental context, social role and influences were core TDF domains explaining barriers. The TDF domains explaining facilitators were more heterogeneous with social influences and environmental context, knowledge and belief about consequences being the core psychosocial mechanisms explaining facilitators. Restructuring the physical and social environment where the main behavioral interventions relevant to the overall sample. Additionally, modelling the desired behavior and simultaneously providing instruction on how to perform the behaviour, especially by peers in the social network, were also relevant to the sample.ConclusionThese findings unique to Latin America and the Caribbean reveal the potential of participatory research to provide insights into local healthy eating behavioral etiology to inform small-scale interventions and personalized behavioral interventions. Furthermore, primary health care teams conducting home visits could use this information to employ behavioral change techniques beyond providing or discussing information to community members.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25153-2.Keywords: Diabetes prevention, Healthy eating behavior, Participatory research, Photovoice study, Theoretical domains framework (TDF), Behavioral change techniques (BCTs)

Background

Diabetes is among the top 10 global causes of death. By 2045, it could affect 700 million people [1]. In 2019, it cost healthcare systems over 760 billion USD, and this is expected to increase by approximately 21% to 845 billion USD by 2045 [2]. South, Central, North America and the Caribbean accounted for 17% of people living with diabetes in 2019 with South and Central America projected to experience a 60% growth in prevalence compared with a 37% prevalence increase in North America and the Caribbean [3]. Although some of this increase is related to overall population growth, rapid urbanization and changing lifestyle indicates the proportion of patients with diabetes is expected to grow faster than the overall population growth in LMICS [3].

Diabetes is a feasible target for community-based interventions relative to other diet related disorders due to its clear diagnostic criteria, which rely on simple blood tests like HbA1c. In contrast, diagnosing conditions like non-alcoholic fatty liver disease or metabolic syndrome is more complex, requiring expensive invasive tests or establishing the presence of multiple risk factors, which is challenging in low-resource settings. In addition, when people reflect upon risk factors like obesity, the direct consequences of a disease such as diabetes are often more immediate and visible in the public consciousness. For instance, qualitative research supports that people with diabetes in low resource settings see obesity as sickness, but people without diabetes perceive it as a sign of success [4].

Latin American has one of the fastest-growing diabetic populations in the world [5]. However, effective prevention interventions have been mainly implemented in high-income countries, with less than a handful of successful replications in Latin America and the Caribbean (LACs) [5, 6]. Healthy eating is essential to diabetes prevention and management. Nevertheless, behavioral interventions focusing solely on individual-level behavior have shown short-lived improvements and temporal sustainability [6]. Psychosocial factors shape individuals’ capacity to make health-conscious decisions sustainably [7, 8] with cognitive, affective, and environmental demands reducing self-regulation [9, 10] and affecting the implementation, and the scalability of behavioral interventions [11]. For instance, there is a lack of research focusing on structural barriers of facilitators to healthy eating in most countries in Latin America with 61% of the research concentrated in Brazil. In Colombia, the discussion has mainly focused on food labeling without much consideration to other aspects of how the food environment (Food retail, provision, pricing, location) influence healthy eating [12].

Qualitative community-based participatory research (CPBR) such as Photovoice offers a detailed view of the complex psychosocial factors that constituted the etiology of health behaviors [13–16] by enabling the understanding of participants’ worldviews through sharing photographs of their everyday realities. This approach results in additional insights compared to other qualitative research methods [17, 18]. For instance, in a community-based study with adolescent girls at risk of iron deficiency anemia in Mozambique, photovoice and group discussions showed that their nutrition is hampered by seasonal food scarcity, unequal distribution of household responsibilities compared to boys, and public stigma to taking iron supplements. The findings helped create a nutrition curriculum and a discreet, at-home supplement delivery system [19]. The use of Photovoice in this context not only uncovered the multifaceted determinants of their dietary habits but also empowered the girls by giving them a platform to voice their challenges, resulting in solutions that were directly tailored to their needs and preferences. Other benefits include its ability to build critical consciousness by encouraging participants to reflect on their own lives, and its value as a powerful tool for advocacy and social change by presenting compelling visual evidence to the community.

The value of Photovoice in the context of eating related disorders is evident when considering the growing interest in employing the method to study diet in populations at risk of diet-related diseases due to its potential to discover specific actionable insights from the participants´perspectives [20–23]. For instance, photovoice has been employed as part of interventions to help people manage diabetes and hypertension by becoming more physically active. Participants took and discussed photos, identified barriers and facilitators of physical activity, reflected on their own habits, and created action plans [21]. The literature on the use of Photovoice to understand diabetes focuses on self-management, treatment adherence and physical activity among patients with an already-existing diagnosis of diabetes and in high-income countries [21]. However, to the best of our knowledge, we do not know of any studies employing Photovoice to study healthy eating on patients at risk of specifically developing Type II diabetes (T2D). Furthermore, the literature suggests the psychosocial perspectives of patients at risk of diabetes are understudied [24].

The evidence on effective diabetes prevention in LACs for patients at risk of diabetes is scarce, with only a handful of studies [5, 25]. Even more concerning is the lack of research about the local etiology of health behaviors, such as healthy eating, that inform the design of contextualized behavioral interventions. This is likely complicated by the prolific number of psychosocial theories and constructs informing interventions. For instance, a systematic review of behavior change interventions in LACs found that there was limited researchers’ knowledge and implementation of behavior change theory [26]. In addition, out of 29 studies implementing interventions, only 15 were related to the promotion of healthy eating [26] and only 10 of these interventions explicitly identify behavioral change techniques (BCTs) [26].

BCTs are effective because they address the psychosocial factors, the barriers and facilitators, that influence behavior. By classifying these factors into standardized domains [27], researchers and designers can better tailor interventions. This is especially useful for patients at risk of diabetes, as it allows for the selection of specific BCTs that target the psychosocial mechanisms of action that either hinder or promote healthy eating. Instead of offering generic advice, interventions could become precisely targeted. Additionally, understanding the complexity of the local context could also help identify potential dissemination strategies for local interventions. Therefore, the present study aimed to (i) explore core perceived barriers and facilitators of healthy eating, (ii) explore participants concept of healthy eating any other relevant themes related to healthy eating (iii) identify psychosocial mechanism explaining the existence of the barriers and facilitators and (iv) ascertain suitable BCTs for the identified psychosocial domains.

Methods

Theoretical considerations

Perceived behavioral barriers and facilitators

There are at least 128 known psychosocial constructs for behavioral change design, a few aim to capture factors such as environmental triggers/context including barriers and facilitators [27]. Barriers are defined as factors that hinder the acquisition or maintenance of healthy behaviors, while perceived facilitators are elements that support the desired behavior [28]. The same element can be perceived as a barrier or a facilitator [29].

Persons at risk of developing diabetes

At a population level, persons without known risk factors for diabetes can be considered to have a baseline risk of T2D [30, 31] with a set of parameters determining the risk level [32, 33]. However, in the Caribbean communities of Colombia most of the population is not risk-stratified [34, 35]. The present study considered a patient at risk of diabetes as those with at least one of the following self-reported conditions: Body mass index greater than or equal to 25 (calculated with self-reported weight and height) [36, 37], family history of diabetes in first and second-degree relatives [36, 38], tobacco use [38, 39] a diagnosis of dislipidemias [40], sedentary behavior (more than 6 h of sitting/lying per day) [41] and physical inactivity (less than 30 min of moderate to vigorous exercise at least 5/week) [41].

The theoretical domains framework (TDF)

The TDF is an existing framework synthesizing concepts from 33 behavior change theories through expert consensus and has been extensively applied in behavior change research due to its usefulness as a tool for coding psychosocial mechanisms that explain behavior [42]. The Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) offers a comprehensive approach to understanding the drivers of health behaviors by organizing them into 14 distinct domains. These domains allow practitioners to reflect about the complex interaction of different psychosocial domains that jointly influence a person’s actions [43]. For instance, Knowledge relates to awareness of procedures or new evidence, Skills involves the ability acquired through practice. Social/Professional Role and Identity highlights how a person’s position in society influences their behavior. Beliefs about Capabilities refers to a person’s confidence in their own ability to succeed at a task. Optimism refers to the inner confidence that goals will be attained. Beliefs about Consequences captures what a person thinks will happen if they perform the behavior. Reinforcement refers to rewards, incentives and sanctions. Intentions, refers to the conscious decision to perform a behavior. Goals capture the prioritizing of goals, planning and implementation expectations. Memory, Attention and Decision Processes is the ability to retain information, maintain attention and make informed decisions. Environmental Context and Resources refer to awareness of environmental stressors and organizational culture. Social Influences focuses on the impact of social norms and powerplay. Emotion recognizes that feelings, such as fear or anxiety, can be powerful drivers of behavior. Finally, Behavioral Regulation involves a person’s ability to self-monitor and manage their own actions.

Behavioral change techniques and links to psychosocial domains

The Behavior Change Techniques Taxonomy version 1 (BCTTv1) is a standardized and widely used classification system for linking behavior change interventions to the domains of the TDF, it can be thought of as a tool for linking mechanisms of action through which BCTs have their effects [27, 44]. In this context, a BCT is a specific, actionable component of an intervention that is designed to directly influence a person’s behavior. Previous research has linked specific BCTs that target specific TDF domains [45]. For example, the BCT of providing instruction about how to perform a behaviour works through improving kwnoledge and skills, the psychosocial mechanisms of actions. BCTs can influence behaviour throgh single or multiple mechanisms. For instance “Goal Setting,” which is linked exclusively to one domain, while “Problem Solving,” influence various domains [45].

Critical realism as interpretative framework

The main goal of critical realism is to identify key underlying mechanisms that go beyond surface level observations of participants. This implies going beyond induction or deduction to abduction (placing original ideas in the best possible social theories), retroduction (answering with established theories what properties must exist for X to be what X is? What mechanisms are related to these structures?) and retrodiction (explore the relationship between various exploratory mechanisms) [46].

Study design and setting

This is a multimethod qualitative participatory action research study using Photovoice in collaboration with community members from Gaira, a 66,548 inhabitants’ neighborhood of Santa Marta City (Colombia) where diabetes mellitus mortality rate was 36% higher than the national rate in 2020 [47]. Before the study, participants did not know the researchers, and were recruited by convenience sampling through house-to-house visits and snowball sampling. DY visited 10 homes. 15 individuals were interested in participating; they were aware that the research was conducted by a female nutritionist pursuing a master´s degree in public health (DY). Before starting the study, DY successfully completed all the master related coursework, including two qualitative research modules. Data collection took place in March and April 2023. Participants’ inclusion criteria included (i) reporting at least one risk factor for developing the disease, (ii) attend all group sessions, and (iii) signing informed consent. Exclusion criteria included (i) having a diagnosis of diabetes (ii) having any impediment to handling digital cameras or mobile phones with an integrated camera.

A total of 15 participants were invited to an initial meeting facilitated by DY. At the initial meeting, all the 15 participants completed the consent form. Once the informed consent was signed, participants completed a short sociodemographic survey and received instructions on the photovoice process. The instruction included information about technical aspects of photography, along with the definition of barriers and facilitators. After completing the initial training, participants had two weeks to collect photos. During this time, they were asked to take at least 4 photos. Participants were encouraged to take photos of aspects of their environment they perceived as barriers or facilitators to healthy eating. During the two weeks, the primary author followed up with participants through a WhatsApp group and calls, to answer questions and remind them of the second meeting. After two weeks 2 participants dropped out of the study due to time constraints and 13 participants attended the second meeting where DY facilitated two focus groups alone in two of participants homes (n1 = six and n2 = seven) where participants were able to reflect and analyze the photos. Given that the main aim was to discover core barriers and facilitators of healthy eating, two focus groups were considered sufficient. Empirical research suggests that 64% of themes can be generated by 60–90 min one focus group, and two focus groups can reach up to 80% theme saturation, including the most prevalent themes [48]. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Health Research Ethics Committee of the Universidad del Norte with the record N°2302–4957. Photos with participant faces were cropped, blurred and present in silhouettes to protect participants anonymity.

Data collection

DY, TA, RT developed a baseline form to collect data on participants’ demographics and diabetes risk factors during the first meeting. For the second meeting a focus group guide was developed by DY and JR based on the SHOWED method [17], a structured questionnaire promoting reflection about the photos. Two additional questions were included: Why do you believe this photograph illustrates a facilitator (or barrier) to healthy eating? Does someone else in the group relate to this photograph? Additionally, DY asked six structured questions about participants’ perceptions of their healthy eating concept. For example, Among the food available in “tiendas” or supermarkets, which ones do you consider “healthy” or beneficial for your health? Why do you consider them healthy? Both focus groups lasted approximately 120 min, sessions were audio recorded and transcribed. All focus group discussions were facilitated by DY.

Data analysis

Critical realism análisis steps

The data were analyzed in multiple stages using a critical realism interpretive framework following Parras’ adaptation of Danemark, Ekström and Karlson critical realism guideline for explanatory qualitative research [46]. This framework involves 5 stages starting from the concrete and moving to the abstract and then back to the concrete: (i) Collecting data on concrete descriptions, (ii) the analytical resolution prompt researchers to create initial abstractions to delineate features and the level of analysis, (iii) abduction is the process of employing psychosocial theories to hypothesize the existence of underlying causal mechanisms, (iv) the retroduction in this study was focus on finding potential intervention mechanisms (BCTs) related to the previous causal mechanisms, and (v) retrodiction, where the explanatory power of the mechanism is compared between identified structures.

Descriptions

Participant descriptions (photos and transcriptions of the focus groups) gave us an understanding of participants’ understanding of the core barriers and facilitators of healthy eating.

Analytical resolution

Critical realisms embrace method triangulation to obtain a richer understanding of the causal mechanisms behind events. Thus, both thematic analysis of focus group transcripts and content analysis of photographs completed the process of analytical resolution [46, 49, 50]. The thematic analysis was conducted first following Clarke and Baun 2012 steps of analysis [51]. First, DY listened to and transcribed the audio verbatim. DY then read the transcripts multiple times, extracted principal ideas, and started a preliminary coding book, which she iteratively reviewed with JR [51]. DY and JR collated the codes into sub-themes and themes [51]. The analysis employed both a deductive latent approach to identify and categorize codes into barriers and facilitators, healthy earing concept and an inductive approach to identify emerging themes DY and JR verified the themes aligned with the participants’ narrative, resolving any discrepancies in consultation with the other authors. A final thematic map was created, and the frequency of codes associated with each theme was reported.

Following this, a manifest content analysis was performed on the photos. DY and JR, allowed the data to be both qualitatively analyzed and quantified following Bengtsson steps as a guideline for the analysis [52]. An initial codebook was developed deductively from systematic reviews of published photovoice research related to healthy eating [29, 53, 54]. This codebook was then adapted to include new, inductive codes generated from the photographs themselves. DY and JR collaboratively developed and refined this codebook, resolving any discrepancies in consultation with the other authors. Using this codebook, the data was categorized into codes, sub-themes, and themes, which were then compiled. The two methods together provided a comprehensive approach to analyzing both the qualitative narratives and the visual data.

Abduction

To complete the process of abduction, the TDF framework was employed as a theory to classify the barriers and facilitator’s themes based on psychosocial mechanisms. For the text data, the mapping of the TDF domains was done manually, deductively and employing latent analysis on each of the original participants quotes that was connected to a theme of barriers or facilitators. In the case of the photos, the mapping of the TDF domains was done manually, deductively and employing latent analysis on the codes linked to theme. DY and JER created a matrix to present the cross tabulation of barrier and facilitator themes and the presence (present/absent as a mechanism it at least one participant quote/photo´s code) of a TDF domain explaining the theme.

Retroduction

In the retroduction stage, the BCTTv1 was employed to link the TDF mechanisms to specific Behavior Change Techniques (BCTs). DY and JR considered two criteria to link the BCT to the TDF domain: (i) the three BCTs with the strongest evidence of a relationship based on previous research [24, 45] and (ii) from those three, the BCT(s) most relevant for the participants based on the text/photo data and the thematic/content analysis results. Thereby identifying the potential “causal powers” needed to address barriers and facilitate behavior change particularly relevant to the sample of participants.

Retrodiction

A matrix was created to present the potential explanatory power of different mechanisms based on the relative frequency of mapping the TDF domains to barrier and facilitator themes (for each time a domain was present in a quote/photo). In addition, to visually represent the final relative explanatory power of the (i) TDF domain relation to a theme, and (ii) the BCTs relation to barriers and facilitators, three chord diagrams were created. Chord diagrams are circular visualizations that shows relationships and their strength between different data. All analyses were conducted using a shared matrix in Microsoft Excel, drawing thematic maps in PowerPoint, and the chord diagram was created in RawGraphs 2.0.

Multimethod analytical resolution

The deductive thematic analysis led to the identification of five themes related to the healthy eating concept: 1) Food, 2) Effect on health, 3) Economic, social, and market context, 4) skills and knowledge, and 5) Personal factors. Five themes for the barriers: 1) Economic, social, and market context, 2) Personal factors 3) Processed food, 4) Lack of skills and knowledge, and 5) negative impact of consumption. For facilitators, seven themes were identified: 1) Social and economic context, 2) skills and knowledge, 3) Personal factors, 4) Fresh food, 5) Positive effect of physical activity, 6) positive impact of consumption, and 7) Technology use.

In the inductive analysis, a category emerged that the researchers called “Preventive behavior”. Within this category, four themes were identified: 1) Exposure reduction, 2) Meal planning, 3) Alternative food, and 4) Technology use. In Fig. 1 we present a thematic map with the relative frequency of codes supporting each theme. Participants’ discussions about their concept of healthy eating focused mainly on the characteristics of the foods.

Retroduction and retrodiction of BCT-TDF links

In Fig. 3 we can see to the right the most frequent TDF mechanisms explaining barriers and facilitators according to Table 2. In addition, the most frequently linked BCTs to TDF domains according to the academic literature are presented in blue color (retroduction). In addition, among these BCTs, the most relevant to our sample of participants is presented in dark blue (retrodiction). For the TDF domain of environmental context, the evidence from participants quotes suggest that the potential interventions to restructure the physical infrastructure can be divided in private physical environments and public physical environments when the physical elements in the environment are barriers to healthy eating. For instances, participant 5 reporting:

Chord diagram presenting the retroduction (Most frequent BCTs linked to TDF domains based on Carey et al. 2018) and retrodiction (most relevant BCTs based on quotes/picture codes of the thematic and content analysis results)

“I love bread and cake too. …as long as I don’t see it or buy it, I’m fine. But as soon as I see it, and if someone brings it to me, I’ll eat it right away. I might put it in the fridge and eat it over time, but I’ll eat it”.

Reorganizing the private physical environment of this participant family home so that the bread or pudding is stored in a specific cabinet or a bread holder away from sight, might reduce the possibility of binge eating carbohydrates. For the public environment, the data provided more opportunities for intervention. For example, limiting the number of places to sell processed food (specially tiendas) or limiting the visual display of processed food to particular hours or days relative to the total number of processed food establishments in the neighborhood of Gaira could be a potential intervention. This is evident by participant 6 and 11 comments respectively:

“yes, that’s a barrier—having a tienda nearby—because it’s so tempting”.

“Or what happens to me at the produce stand… sometimes it’s tough because I go for vegetables and all that, but then I see a huge display case of fried foods (laughs). That also distracts me… it makes me hesitate, you know? I might arrive wanting to eat a fried potato and know I can’t, but I end up eating it anyway”.

The above mentioned quote also supports that adding prompt or cues to redirect attention when seeing processing food is the most relevant target for the TDF domain of memory attention and decision.

In the case of facilitators, there are fewer examples on how restructuring the physical environment can aid healthy eating, but there are few cases and they were restricted to the private physical environment. One example is visually displaying healthy bases or condiments that reduce the likelihood of using unhealthy sauces as participant 7 comments.

“Because it adds so much flavor and aroma to my dishes, it helps me avoid using dressings or sauces like mustard and ketchup. It keeps me from consuming them and makes my food taste delicious. So, I use them on proteins, like meat, as well as in rice dishes and soups. They have a lot of flavor and smell, so it gives you a real sense of peace when you’re preparing your food”.

Implicit considerations based on the relationship between the physical environment with other domains is how the physical environment promotes a distributed social learning experience that facilitate the intergenerational transfer of healthy eating habits and a culture to effectively organize time. This is evident when considering the TDF domain of the social influences, which was particularly strong as a facilitator of healthy eating. This highlights the BCTs of encouraging the social network of the participants to provide social support. Examples of quotes supporting this is that of participant 5, 9 and 12 commenting:

“Well, I have a sister and a niece who are nutritionists, and I have another sister who isn't a nutritionist but she also knows a lot about cooking. They're always talking to me about it, and I've seen the meals they make, so that's how I've learned to do it”

“My mom always has lunch ready for us on time. The meal is always well-prepared with everything: the salad, the protein, everything you need”

“She was a stay-at-home mom, but she was one who educated herself on how we should be eating. In a way, she gradually got us into it. I used to detest liver and now I love it—I mean, I was forced to love it. I was forced to love lentils, liver, salads, vegetables, and so many other things. Now, if there aren’t any vegetables at lunch, it feels like something is missing”.

The above presented quotes reflect how the sight of peers conducting a behaviour motivates participants to engage in healthy eating. Thus, for the TDF domain of knowledge, the BCT of provide visual instruction on how to perform the behaviour as the most relevant for the sample. In addition, the environmental barriers encounter support that participants have information about positive and negative consequences of food consumption, but the information alone is not a strong behaviour change technique, it need the support of other techniques. For example, eliciting participants to reflect on the information about pros and cons to target the mechanisms of beliefs about consequences. This also supports why for the TDF domain of skill, behaviour practice/rehearsal was the selected BCT.

Other BCTs such as perform action planning for improving behavioral regulation were based on the results of the thematic/content analysis were participants expressed already engaging in meal planning activities and exposure reduction strategies. Finally, the BCT of present information from credible sources was selected for the information of social role given participants constraints on time due to work related activities. Here the intention is to find information from credible sources to help participants manage their time commitments related to work.

Retrodiction of TDF themes classification

The relative importance of TDF domains explaining themes is presented in Table 2 (frequency of quotes/photos codes) and Fig. 4 (width of the chord linking domains and themes). The analysis found a higher number of TDF domains explaining facilitators than with barriers. In addition, the explanatory power of the TDF domains is more concentrated in fewer domains for barriers than for facilitators. When considering the psychosocial domains explaining the most important barriers, beliefs about capabilities was the main mechanism of action explaining the personal factors theme as a barrier. Barriers were primarily explained by external factors (environmental context, social role and influences) and to a less extend by memory-attention-decision which mainly influenced the social and economic context theme. Social influences and the environmental context were the main mechanism of action explaining the socioeconomic context theme as a barrier. In general, facilitators were explained more frequently by knowledge, environmental context, social influences domains and beliefs about consequences.

The only mechanism of action from the TDF domain identified in this step without a linked BCT (in Fig. 3) is belief about capabilities. For this domain, the three most frequent BCTs are: Problem solving, demonstration of a behaviour and behavioral rehearsal. The rationale was based on the previous quote of participant 5 above and the following comment from participant 8:

Discussion

Previous studies on psychosocial factors of healthy eating usually do not target diabetes [53, 54]. The present study focused on how multiple psychosocial domains can explain barriers and facilitators of healthy eating in patients at risk of developing diabetes in LACs. This provides actionable insights for researchers and practitioners to prioritize psychosocial domains when designing interventions. More than 80% of participants were women. Previous research supports that women are often more likely than men to participate in diabetes research [14, 55]. Males in LAC could have less interest or resources to devote to diabetes prevention despite having a higher prevalence of the disease [56]. Another explanation could be that females frequently assume roles related to shopping for groceries, cooking, and taking responsibility for family nutrition [57, 58]. There needs to be a focused strategy to recruit and maintain male participants in diabetes prevention research in LAC. For instance, the inclusion of recreational soccer as a prevention intervention could attract more male participants [59].

The previous and the following recommendations in this section are important for the Colombian context, but the methodological approach of the study can inform research and small-scale community interventions in the region and globally to discover BCTs relevant to communities. In addition, the study’s methodology could be adapted to identify relevant BCTs for a particular patient. Then, the n-of-1 trial design could be used to determine the most effective BCT for healthy eating in an individual, moving beyond population-level averages to provide personalized care. N of 1 trials are within-patient crossover trials that assess the effects of different treatments by having a single patient switch between treatments and measuring the outcomes [60]. For contexts in the Caribbean region such as Colombia, where laws that seek to redirect health care spending towards primary health services are in place, Community Primary Health Teams (CPHT) could collect Photovoice and interview data. Then, local authorities in collaboration with qualitative researchers could centralize the critical realism analysis steps, and later provide relevant BCTs recommendations back to the CPHT to implement at the community level. The CPHT in Colombia usually includes a psychologist and mainly focus on the BCT of providing/discussing information [61, 62]. In the particular case of the city of Santa Marta, the Health Ministry of Colombia has reported the operation of 32 CPHT [63].

Healthy eating concept

Participants’ concept of healthy eating was heavily influenced by a biomedical model of health [64], meaning they primarily associated it with the body’s physical needs, rather than with psychosocial dimensions. Some participants provided concrete negative biological outcomes such as increased cholesterol and triglycerides. However, the discussion was mostly centered around general rules of thumb, about what is “bad” and “good” for the body (See barriers quotes in Fig. 1). The participants perspective accurately reflects the goals of the Pan American Health Organization [65], suggesting a healthy diet is based on the consumption of foods that have undergone minimal industrial processing, with a simultaneous restriction on the use of ultra-processed foods [65]. The findings support that participants are aware of the healthy eating recommendations outlined the Colombian Ministry of Health, asserting that a healthy diet is one that aids the proper functioning of the body [66].

A previous report in children aged 8 to 12 years in El Salvador suggested that linking food with biological outcomes begins at a young age [67]. This could be considered as a baseline skill to understand healthy eating. Our findings indicate that this baseline skill is susceptible to be improved to a more complex view of food, especially as complex health conditions are associated with health information seeking behaviour [68, 69]. Few participants show signs of thinking about food in complex terms such as frequency, quantity, food chemical composition and the chemical transformation of food and relative to a deliberate mental or physical health goal. CPHT could promote BCTs such as perform action planning and behavioral rehearsal to encourage participants to think beyond a dichotomy of food negative/positive effects to biomedical health, to a more deliberate social, mental and biological health goal based on community values.

Barriers

The socioeconomic context and personal factors were the most important barriers with the social role, social influences, the environmental context and memory-attention-decision being the main mechanisms explaining the themes. However, the code frequency supports the socioeconomic context as a more important barrier, suggesting structural factors play a bigger role as barriers to healthy eating in Gaira. This highlights the role of the local authorities in designing targeted policies to regulate these structural factors. Additionally, it suggests that CPHT could have a limited effect on removing the main barriers to healthy eating through individual BCTs. However, regulatory approaches from the government could employ the presented insights to enact rules that employ the BCTs of restructuring the physical environment. For instance, limiting the number of places to sell processed food (specially tiendas) or limiting the visual display of processed food to specific time periods (hours, days, weeks, months) relative to the total number of processed food establishments in the neighborhood of Gaira could be a potential intervention.

For the CPHT, the TDF domains contain multiple psychological constructs under an umbrella term, for instance the role of the constructs of subjective norms is an important social influence, which involves the perceived social pressure from others for a person to act in a certain way and their willingness to align with other people’s expectations [70]. Participants felt particularly susceptible to the marketing strategies employed by the food industry in partnership with local “tiendas”. While “tiendas” sell a variety of products (including healthy food), participants emphasized that marketing practices within these stores often promote the consumption of unhealthy foods, especially when customers are influenced by seeing others eating these products on site. There seems to be a normalized behaviour in the community of people ordering while they wait to be served by the staff of the tienda (See Fig. 1). In addition, most photos collected in public locations showed that the commercial food offering was concentrated on fast food, bakery products and processed snacks, in which a similar social pressure could be present. specially if the owners of establishments are neighbors with close relationships to the community.

Researchers in behavioral etiology and CPHT should consider the interactions among different forms of subjective norms, such as the influence of marketing, the behavior of patrons in tiendas, and a social obligation to contribute to a community member entrepreneurial activity. Our finding align with other work that shows that food availability, marketing and media are important factors that impact healthy eating [71], as well as research highlighting the role of local food establishments such “tiendas” as influential food environments due to their proximity and the availability of food [67]. Moreover, encourage interventions that include the BCT of social support could be supported by suggesting participants to ask friends/family that successfully handle marketing strategies, to offer company when commuting through locations where there are tiendas. More importantly, “tiendas” present as an important stakeholder in behavior change in LACs. This knowledge could be leveraged to favor the dissemination of research and interventions and research strategies to productively engage “tiendas” in diabetes prevention.

Previous research suggests that the main social negative influence on healthy eating are partners/spouses, with friends and especially mothers being a positive influence on healthy eating [71]. In contrast, the present study suggests that for the sample selected from the Gaira community, an important social influence was friends’ being entrepreneurs/owners of food establishments that sold unhealthy food, making it harder for participants to eat healthy. The social role of friendship could be associated with a social obligation to support the friend business, in this case presenting information from a credible source for managing expectation with friends could aid members of the community manage a healthy separation of their friendship and consumer roles. In addition, the local government could incentivize other types of entrepreneurial activity different to food establishments. The most important psychosocial mechanism explaining personal factors as a barrier was belief about personal capabilities. In this case, in their rutinary visits to homes, CPHT could identify community members that successfully employ TDF mechanisms that remove barriers or enhance facilitators. With community members permission, they pair community members so that one community member models the behaviour to other community members and act as a good positive influence.

Other personal factors can be explained through the TDF domain of lack of behavioral regulation, where participants reported as having a lack of “will power”. This is similar to Mabry et al. [72], reporting personal factors such as perceptions of lack of self-control, and emotional eating. Notably, some participants reported that a previous health condition requires personal resources (such as mental effort) that hinder their self-control. Patients with an increased risk of diabetes might have a lower baseline for self-control. The mental effort required to manage risk factors with limited resources could reduce the capacity to exercise will power [73]. In this case, BCTs such as rehearsing behavior and identifying particular cues that prompt behavior in mental exhaustion states could aid participants in managing this specific scenario. However, given the lack of time reported by participants and the existence of some preventive behaviors like exposure reduction and meal planning, it is likely that the most relevant BCT for the community to target behavioral regulation is perform action planning.

Facilitators

The social and economic context was the most important facilitator in our study. In the social aspect, there were the positive impacts of family support in the social context. Previous studies suggest family members can be a source of motivation, information and provide emotional and financial support [74]. This is especially important for Latino populations [5]. The presence of a cooperative partner or supportive family member contributes to the ease of adopting and sustaining a healthy dietary practices [75]. Mother-to-offspring relationships were important for transfer of knowledge, habits and skills, whereas son-to-mother relationships offered financial support towards healthy eating.

In other social contexts, participants expressed lack of time due to work related commitments. This highlights that primary health care strategies should go beyond the community and engage the Safety and Health at Work Management System (SG-SST) in Colombia. Each organization in Colombia designates a SG-SST leader to coordinate health risk stratification and promotion activities within the organization. These leaders could consider intervening diabetes risk factors such as unhealthy eating in the workplace. However, there are not strong structural incentives to bring BCTs to influence healthy eating at the workplace as diabetes is not considered a labor related disease. The influence of the work social network on healthy eating is understudied [76], this is concerning given that frequent celebrations at work often involve unhealthy foods [75].

In the broader social and environmental context participants also reported that the increase in prices of unhealthy food and the availability of vegetables on “Tiendas” presented as a facilitator of healthy eating. This is consistent with previous research supporting that the economics of food can have vast implications for global health outcomes. Unfortunately, the global cost of a healthy diet increased by 6.7% from 2019 to 2021. Notably, Africa, Asia, Latin America, the Caribbean, and Oceania experienced over 5% increases. Increasing the cost of unhealthy foods and subsidizing the costs of healthy food could aid in the promotion of healthier diets [77]. Health behavior intervention designers should consider ways in which the cost of healthy food could be reduced to potential participants.

Preventive behavior

It might be possible to conceived behaviour as a facilitator or other behaviours. However, given that most of the themes related to facilitators were not participant actions but factors or consequences of behaviours we groupped several themes under this separate category. The most important preventive behaviors was exposure reduction. Being around food triggers cravings, encourages people to consuming greater food quantities [78]. Meal planning was also reported as an active preventive behavior, with evidence supporting it to be associated with lower odds of being overweight (OR 0.92) and Obese (0.79) [79]. These highlight that CPHT could target this already preventive behaviours in some community members to enhance their skills and promote social interaction with community members who can promote these capabilities.

Strengths and limitations

To date, most studies focused on barriers and facilitators of healthy eating have been focused on rural women of childbearing age [72], youth from 8 to 12 years [67], and 13 to 18 years [80] university students [53], rural older women [81], and low-income families during the pandemic [54]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study reporting barriers and facilitators of healthy eating in patients at a higher risk of diabetes. While some previous studies have explored perceived barriers and facilitators of healthy eating ” [54, 67, 72, 81], our research is unique in its focus on patients at higher risk of diabetes. We employed a transparent and systematic multimethod photovoice study following critical realism, thematic and content analysis guidelines. Few studies in the literature on barriers and facilitators of healthy eating report underlying philosophical assumptions and interpretative frameworks for qualitative research [80]. In the present study, formal steps were followed for following a critical realism perspective and conducting both content and thematic qualitative analysis. A limitation is the under-representation of male participants, which may restrict the transferability of findings, given the higher prevalence of diabetes among men. However, this highlights the need for future studies to actively recruit more male participants to better capture their perspectives. Another limitation is the use of only two focus groups; nevertheless, the literature suggests that two focus groups could be enough to reach between 82% and 84% theme saturation [48]. A final limitation is that the results of the analysis were only discussed with three participants and only up to the analytical resolution step of analysis.

Conclusion

The experience of health behavior change programs targeting diabetes in the Colombian Caribbean is extremely limited, not only due to the absence of routine stratification of patients at risk of diabetes [82], but also due to the lack of qualitative and quantitative research about the local etiology of healthy eating behaviors. The present qualitative research showed that participatory research could be employed to identify relevant barriers, facilitators and associated psychosocial domains and link it to identify relevant behavioral change techniques. Based on this information, local researchers, intervention designers, CPTHs and health promoters in work environments have actionable insights from where to further conduct research or have initial evidence to inform behavioral interventions that target healthy eating in small groups. Furthermore, there are other primary health care initiatives in the Caribbean and South America that could replicate the study for communities and individuals to find relevant BCTs that go beyond providing information to community members. However, further qualitative research is needed to explore the cultural acceptance of communities about different BCTs and the feasibility of CPTHs to collect photovoice data, conduct focus groups and introduce BCTs to intervene patients at risk of diabetes.

Healthy eating is associated with weight reduction, which could contribute to a reversion from hyperglycemic to normoglycemic states [82], improve the quality of life people in LAC and the Caribbean [83] and contribute towards improving global health by reducing the diabetes burden of disease.
