Coexistence of Erythrodermic Psoriasis and Bullous Pemphigoid: A Case Report
Authors: Menglin Li, Tao Guo, Junling Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundErythrodermic psoriasis (EP) represents a severe inflammatory subtype of psoriasis. Bullous pemphigoid (BP), the most common autoimmune subepidermal blistering disorder in clinical practice, predominantly affects elderly populations. Comorbidity of these two diseases is uncommon, and BP secondary to EP in young-adult patients is particularly rare.Case PresentationA 33-year-old male with a 14-year relapsing-remitting history of psoriasis was admitted for erythrodermic psoriasis flare. Tense soybean-sized bullae with clear fluid appeared on bilateral lower extremities on the 5th hospital day. Skin biopsy demonstrated subepidermal blister formation with psoriasiform epidermal alterations. Direct immunofluorescence revealed basement-membrane-zone (BMZ) IgG (±) and C3 (+). Serum serology revealed markedly elevated levels of both anti-BP180 and anti-BP230 antibodies, whereas anti-desmoglein-1 and anti-desmoglein-3 antibodies were negative, supporting the diagnosis of coexisting EP and BP. The patient was treated with oral acitretin combined with high-potency topical halometasone cream, no new bullae occurred subsequently. Both psoriatic erythrodermic lesions and bullous lesions achieved marked improvement over the 6-month follow-up period.ConclusionThis rare case of BP secondary to EP in a young adult underscores that clinicians should maintain high suspicion for BP when tense bullae develop de novo in patients with EP. Timely performance of histopathology, perilesional direct immunofluorescence, and pemphigoid-targeted serological testing is critical for establishing a definitive diagnosis.

Introduction

Erythrodermic psoriasis (EP) is a severe, uncommon variant of psoriasis characterized by diffuse cutaneous erythema, infiltration, and abundant furfuraceous scaling, frequently accompanied by systemic inflammatory symptoms. Bullous pemphigoid (BP) is the most prevalent autoimmune subepidermal blistering disease, typically affecting elderly individuals, defined clinically by widespread tense bullae and immunopathologically by linear basement-membrane-zone (BMZ) deposition of IgG and C3 together with circulating autoantibodies targeting BP180 and/or BP230 antigens. An epidemiological association between psoriasis and BP has been well-documented in retrospective case series, in which BP represents the most frequent autoimmune blistering disease complicating psoriasis; psoriasis precedes BP onset in the vast majority of patients.1 Nevertheless, concomitant EP and BP remains uncommon. And most published BP-EP cases involve older adults, whereas BP in patients younger than 40 years is unusual.2,3 New-onset blisters in patients with EP may result from a variety of disorders. A comprehensive assessment integrating clinical manifestations, histopathology, direct immunofluorescence, serological autoantibody testing and other relevant laboratory investigations is required for definitive diagnosis. We herein report a rare case of isolated tense bullae arising on the skin lesions of a 33-year-old male patient with EP. Histopathology demonstrated subepidermal blister formation. Direct immunofluorescence revealed BMZ IgG (±) and C3 (+). Markedly elevated anti-BP180 and anti-BP230 antibodies were also detected, consistent with the coexistence of EP and BP. This case documents the rare simultaneous occurrence of these two disorders in a young-adult patient. Combined with published literature, we analyze the potential association between the two diseases and summarize key diagnostic points and therapeutic regimens. This report aims to deepen clinicians’ understanding of this uncommon clinical entity and possesses considerable practical instructional value.

Case Presentation

The patient was a 33-year-old male who presented with a 14-year history of generalized erythema, papules, and scaly pruritic skin lesions, which had deteriorated over the preceding 10 days. Fourteen years prior, scaly erythematous papules initially developed on the scalp and subsequently generalized to the whole body. He was diagnosed with psoriasis at a local hospital and received sequential treatments including medicated baths, phototherapy, and oral acitretin capsules. The patient also self-administered folk remedies and topical “Shennong Baicao Gao” ointment intermittently during the disease course. Over the 14-year disease duration, the cutaneous lesions relapsed repeatedly with variable severity. Ten days before admission, his condition deteriorated again, characterized by diffuse bright-red erythema and papules involving the face, scalp, trunk, and extremities, accompanied by focal dry desquamation and mild limb edema. The patient was admitted to our department with a clinical diagnosis of erythrodermic psoriasis.

After admission, relevant laboratory investigations were completed. Compound amino-peptide tablets and acitretin capsules at a daily dose of 40 mg were administered to regulate epidermal cell differentiation. On hospital day 5, several isolated soybean-sized tense vesicles containing clear fluid developed on the patient’s bilateral lower extremities (Figure 1A and B). A skin biopsy was obtained from one of the blisters. Skin histopathological biopsy showed epidermal hyperkeratosis, parakeratosis, focal neutrophil aggregation in the stratum corneum, acanthosis, and subepidermal blister formation, with numerous lymphocytes and neutrophils in the blister cavity, as well as perivascular infiltration of abundant lymphocytes and sparse eosinophils in the dermis (Figure 2). Direct immunofluorescence demonstrated BMZ IgG (±), IgA (−), and C3 (+). Pemphigus-related autoantibody testing revealed anti-BP180 antibody 69.8 U/mL (elevated), anti-BP230 antibody 47.4 RU/mL (elevated), while anti-desmoglein-1 and anti-desmoglein-3 antibodies were both negative. The patient’s past medical history included old myocardial infarction with coronary stent implantation four years previously; cardiac medications had been withheld for 2 years. He denied other comorbidities, drug allergies, and family history.

Dermatological Examination

Diffuse bright-red erythema and papules were observed over the face, scalp, trunk, and extremities, with focal dry scaling. Mild edema of the extremities was present. Several isolated soybean-sized tense vesicles containing clear fluid were scattered over both lower limbs, some distributed along scratch marks. Nikolsky’s sign was negative (Figure 1A and B). The body-surface-area (BSA) involvement was 97%, the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) score was 38.4, and the Bullous Pemphigoid Disease Area Index (BPDAI) score was 26.

Final Diagnosis

1. Erythrodermic psoriasis 2. Bullous pemphigoid

Therapeutic Regimen

The patient was treated with oral acitretin combined with high-potency topical halometasone cream, no new bullae occurred subsequently. Both psoriatic erythrodermic lesions and bullous lesions achieved marked improvement over the 6-month follow-up period.

Discussion

EP is a severe inflammatory subtype of psoriasis, while BP is the most prevalent autoimmune sub-epidermal blistering disorder predominantly affecting elderly populations. Both EP and BP are severe cutaneous diseases, and their concurrence in the same patient is extremely rare. Ohata et al retrospectively analyzed data from 145 patients diagnosed with psoriasis complicated with autoimmune blistering diseases (AIBD) between January 1, 1996 and July 31, 2013.1 Their results demonstrated that psoriasis vulgaris was documented in 84.1% of cases and pustular psoriasis in 9%, while only 7 cases were erythrodermic psoriasis. Among these patients, BP was the most common blistering disease comorbid with psoriasis (63.4%), followed by anti-laminin γ-1 (p200) pemphigoid (37.2%). In the majority of cases, psoriasis preceded the onset of BP, whereas blistering disease occurred prior to psoriasis in merely four patients. To date, there have been scarce case reports describing BP secondary to EP in young adults. The clinical course of our patient, who developed BP after a 14-year history of psoriasis, is consistent with the above-mentioned studies.

The exact pathogenesis of psoriasis complicated with BP remains incompletely elucidated. A two-sample bidirectional Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis leveraging publicly available genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics demonstrated that individuals with genetic susceptibility to psoriasis have a significantly elevated risk of developing BP, which may be triggered by in-vivo and in-vitro predisposing factors.4 Chronic cutaneous inflammation in psoriasis may contribute to BP development through a mechanism known as epitope spreading. Persistent skin inflammation disrupts the BMZ architecture, sequestered autoantigens such as BP180 and BP230 become exposed, triggering a secondary autoimmune response targeting the BMZ and ultimately resulting in subepidermal blister formation.5 Meanwhile, accelerated epithelial cell turnover, accompanied by persistent inflammatory alterations, robust recruitment of immune cells, and degradation of BMZ components, may alter BMZ antigenicity, enabling auto-reactive T-cells to access antigens and produce autoantibodies.6 Furthermore, pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-1, IL-17 and IL-23) as well as matrix-degrading neutrophils may exert shared pathogenic effects in both psoriasis and BP.7 Certain therapeutic modalities for psoriasis, including phototherapy and biological agents, may also precipitate BP. Ultraviolet radiation can induce basal cell injury, conformational modification of BMZ antigens, or altered antigenicity of the BMZ, leading to exposure or release of modified antigens and subsequent stimulation of auto-reactive T-cells or autoantibody production.6 To date, drug-induced BP has been reported in psoriasis patients treated with multiple biologics, including efalizumab, adalimumab, etanercept, secukinumab, guselkumab and ustekinumab.8 Husein-ElAhmed et al proposed that BP onset during biologic therapy is more likely attributable to disturbed immune homeostasis rather than specific effects on cytokine signaling pathway.9

New-onset vesicles or bullae arising on the background of EP pose a diagnostic challenge for clinicians. A variety of heterogeneous disorders can induce blistering lesions in the setting of generalized erythroderma, mainly including BP, generalized pustular psoriasis, acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis, drug-induced BP, and other severe cutaneous adverse drug reactions. In addition, patients with EP have severely impaired skin barrier and are susceptible to secondary viral infection such as Kaposi varicelliform eruption, which presents with disseminated monomorphic vesicles.10 Traumatic blisters caused by severe epidermal edema, friction and scratching under erythrodermic status also represent non-autoimmune conditions requiring differential diagnosis. Differentiation of these disorders relies on comprehensive assessment incorporating clinical manifestations, skin histopathology, perilesional direct immunofluorescence, indirect immunofluorescence, salt-split skin assay, and serological autoantibody detection. Anchoring bias, whereby new blisters are simply attributed to psoriasis flare-up, may readily result in missed diagnosis and delayed management. Accordingly, multimodal immunopathological evaluation should be promptly performed when tense bullae newly develop in patients with EP.11

The management of EP complicated with BP poses practical clinical dilemmas. In their review focusing on the treatment of BP concomitant with psoriasis, Hsieh et al recommended that physicians should evaluate the severity of BP and identify if there is any modifiable trigger factor, such as UV or biologics.12 After trigger-factor elimination, topical corticosteroids may be prioritized for patients with mild BP. For more extensive disease, systemic immunosuppressants including corticosteroids and methotrexate are the most commonly adopted options, followed by azathioprine and cyclosporine; nevertheless, medications should be tapered gradually to avoid flare-ups of either psoriasis or BP. In our case, oral acitretin was administered to regulate epidermal proliferation, combined with high-potency halometasone cream for local suppression of autoimmune-mediated inflammation. No new blisters developed after treatment and both erythrodermic psoriatic lesions and bullous lesions improved markedly during the 6-month follow-up period. Furthermore, existing literature has reported that JAK inhibitors, such as tofacitinib, baricitinib and upadacitinib, are safe and effective for patients with concomitant psoriasis and bullous pemphigoid.13,14

Limitations

There are several limitations in the present case. First, the exact cause underlying the co-occurrence of EP and BP in our patient remains unclear. As a single-case report, it cannot establish a causal relationship between previous exposure factors and the onset of BP. Second, representative direct immunofluorescence photographs were not available for this case to support the diagnosis. Third, indirect immunofluorescence and salt-split skin assay were not performed; therefore, the exact location of immune-complex deposition could not be further precisely determined. Fourth, serial follow-up data of anti-BP180 and anti-BP230 antibody titers were unavailable to monitor disease progression.

Conclusion

This case describes a rare presentation of BP complicating EP in a young-adult male. Although its precise pathogenesis remains unclear, epitope spreading driven by chronic psoriatic inflammation, genetic susceptibility and exogenous triggers may contribute to this comorbidity. New-onset tense bullae in patients with EP constitute an important diagnostic pitfall; comprehensive assessment including histopathology, immunofluorescence and serological autoantibody testing is essential to distinguish BP from pustular psoriasis, drug-induced eruptions, viral infection and traumatic blisters. Clinicians should maintain high vigilance for concurrent BP when tense bullae arise in EP, so as to facilitate timely diagnosis and appropriate individualized therapy.
