Efgartigimod-responsive autoimmune peripheral neuropathy with concurrent anti-GM2 IgG and anti-contactin-1 IgG antibodies: a rare case report
Authors: Ju Huang, Dan Li, Ruyang He, Jun Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune-mediated peripheral neuropathies arise from dysregulated immune responses targeting peripheral nerves. Contactin-1 (CNTN1) antibodies, typically of the IgG4 subclass, are associated with autoimmune nodo/paranodopathies and often respond poorly to intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). Background

Immune-mediated peripheral neuropathies comprise a heterogeneous group of disorders driven by immune responses against myelin, axons [1], or specialized peripheral nerve structures, including the node of Ranvier and paranode [2, 3]. Autoimmune nodo/paranodopathies are increasingly recognized as a distinct subgroup characterized by antibodies against paranodal proteins such as neurofascin-155, contactin-associated protein 1, and CNTN1 [4]. Anti-CNTN1 antibodies are predominantly of the IgG4 subclass and disrupt axoglial adhesion without complement activation, which may partly explain their reduced responsiveness to intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) [5, 6]. In contrast, ganglioside antibodies such as anti-GM2 IgG are uncommon [7] and are typically associated with acute immune-mediated neuropathies [8]. Their pathogenic effects are thought to involve complement activation and membrane attack complex formation [9], contributing to axonal or Schwann cell injury. Although nodo/paranodal antibodies and ganglioside antibodies have been reported individually, their coexistence has not, to our knowledge, been previously described in the literature. Here, we report a patient with concurrent anti-GM2 IgG and anti-CNTN1 antibodies who showed marked clinical improvement after FcRn blockade, and we discuss implications for disease mechanisms and targeted immunotherapy.

Case presentation

A 73-year-old man with a history of long-standing gout (over 30 years), coronary artery disease with prior coronary stent implantation for myocardial infarction, and gastric and colonic ulcer disease was admitted for progressive low back pain with bilateral lower-limb pain for more than one month. He had no history of diabetes, hypertension, liver disease, tuberculosis, toxin exposure, recent vaccination, or family history of neurological disease. One to two months before admission, the patient developed distal limb numbness and gait instability that gradually worsened. Lumbar magnetic resonance imaging revealed L4/5 spondylolisthesis with spinal canal stenosis, and posterior decompression with fusion was performed. The lack of postoperative improvement suggested that the symptoms were unlikely to be fully explained by compressive lumbar radiculopathy alone.

Four days after surgery, he acutely developed impaired consciousness and bilateral upper-limb weakness, which rapidly progressed to flaccid quadriparesis. Although central nervous system involvement could not be fully excluded due to the lack of contrast-enhanced MRI and EEG, his impaired consciousness was attributed mainly to hypoxemia. Subsequent arterial blood gas analysis showed hypoxemia with reduced oxygen saturation accompanied by somnolence, which partially improved after airway suctioning. However, recurrent hypoxemia occurred shortly thereafter (PaO₂ 62 mmHg), necessitating mechanical ventilation. Neurological examination showed generalized hypotonia, severe asymmetric limb weakness, and absence of deep tendon reflexes. Cranial nerve examination suggested bulbar involvement with weakened pharyngeal and cough reflexes. Sensory examination was limited by poor cooperation. Brain and spinal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed no evidence of acute ischemic stroke or other spinal cord abnormalities, apart from postoperative changes associated with L4/5 spondylolisthesis and spinal canal stenosis (Fig. 1). Electrophysiological studies showed widespread but non-uniform involvement: prolonged median distal motor latencies, reduced F-wave persistence across multiple nerves, and markedly decreased peroneal compound muscle action potential amplitudes (right > left). Sensory studies showed slowed median conduction velocities, while superficial peroneal responses were relatively preserved. Repetitive nerve stimulation did not indicate neuromuscular junction transmission failure. These findings collectively suggest a mixed demyelinating and axonal peripheral neuropathy, predominantly affecting the median, tibial, and peroneal nerves in a non-uniform pattern, without evidence of neuromuscular junction dysfunction (Table 1). Serological testing identified dual positivity for anti-GM2 IgG antibodies and anti-CNTN1 antibodies (titer 1:100), accompanied by a serum IgG4 level of 1.81 g/L (within reference range) (Table 2). Screening for other ganglioside antibodies, autoimmune markers, vasculitis-related indices, and monoclonal gammopathy was negative. Given the reported association between anti-CNTN1 antibodies and glomerulonephritis [10], we performed a focused evaluation of the patient’s renal function. No significant abnormalities were identified before surgery, prior to efgartigimod treatment, or during follow-up. Cerebrospinal fluid examination was not performed due to postoperative constraints. Based on the acute clinical course, electrophysiological abnormalities, and antibody profile, an immune-mediated peripheral neuropathy with Guillain–Barré syndrome–like features was considered.

Initial IVIG was given at 22.5 g/day (0.4 g/kg/day) for 5 days [11]. Despite completion of the treatment course, no early clinical improvement was observed, and the therapeutic response could not yet be adequately assessed. Meanwhile, the patient’s condition continued to deteriorate, progressing to ventilator dependence. Given the rapidly progressive and life-threatening course, treatment escalation was deemed necessary. Plasma exchange was discussed as a potential rescue therapy but was not performed because the family declined the procedure. In this context, therapy was escalated to efgartigimod (800 mg weekly for four cycles) combined with methylprednisolone (40 mg daily). Neurological improvement was observed shortly after initiation of FcRn blockade and was temporally associated with efgartigimod treatment. He was successfully weaned from mechanical ventilation, resumed oral intake, and showed gradual recovery of limb strength. At discharge, he was able to ambulate with assistance, with lower-limb strength graded as Medical Research Council grade 4. At telephone follow-up two weeks later, he could walk independently with near-complete recovery and no evidence of relapse. The patient completed all four planned cycles of efgartigimod without interruption, and treatment adherence was ensured through inpatient administration and medical record verification. No infusion-related reactions or other adverse events were observed during efgartigimod treatment or the subsequent hospitalization.

Discussion and conclusions

Clinical reports of anti-CNTN1–associated nodo/paranodopathies typically describe rapidly progressive neuropathy with prominent motor impairment and limited responsiveness to intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) [5, 12], with improvement reported after escalation to corticosteroids, rituximab, or plasma exchange in selected cases [4, 13]. In contrast, anti-GM2 IgG–associated neuropathies are rare, often acute, and frequently linked to infection-related contexts, with heterogeneous electrophysiological features and treatment responses [7, 8]. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of concurrent anti-GM2 IgG and anti-CNTN1 IgG positivity in a single patient, expanding the spectrum of antibody-mediated peripheral neuropathies and providing insight into how mixed immune mechanisms may influence disease phenotype and treatment response.

The coexistence of paranodal and ganglioside antibodies may indicate a potential hybrid pathogenic mechanism, involving both nodal/paranodal dysfunction and complement-mediated axonal injury. Although anti-CNTN1 antibodies are typically of the IgG4 subclass [5], subclass determination was not performed in this case. Anti-CNTN1 antibodies are frequently associated with the IgG4 subclass and may disrupt axoglial adhesion without complement activation, which may contribute to reduced responsiveness to IVIG [6], whereas antiganglioside antibodies such as anti-GM2 are more likely to engage complement-mediated injury [9]. Nevertheless, the possibility of antibody cross-reactivity should also be considered. Dual seropositivity does not necessarily establish independent pathogenicity of both antibodies, particularly in the absence of confirmatory functional assays or serial antibody testing. Therefore, this dual positivity should be interpreted cautiously as reflecting overlapping immune mechanisms, broader dysregulation, or cross-reactivity. Surgery-related immune activation may have acted as a nonspecific trigger, although causality cannot be established. The electrophysiological findings align with this integrated mechanism. Nodal or paranodal dysfunction in CNTN1-associated disease has been linked to conduction failure [5, 12] and characteristic electrophysiological abnormalities, including prolonged distal motor latencies and reduced F-wave persistence [4, 5]. Meanwhile, the markedly reduced peroneal compound muscle action potential amplitudes indicate concomitant axonal involvement [14], a feature observed in immune-mediated neuropathies [13] and antiganglioside-associated nerve injury [15]. In addition, the relative preservation of superficial peroneal sensory responses suggests widespread but non-uniform nerve involvement, a pattern more consistent with nodo/paranodopathies [2, 4] than with classic length-dependent axonopathy [14].

The limited early clinical improvement following IVIG in this case is broadly consistent with previous observations in autoimmune nodo/paranodopathies [4, 12]. However, IVIG response is typically assessed over 2–4 weeks, and early lack of improvement does not necessarily indicate treatment failure [11]. Given the patient’s rapid clinical deterioration, treatment escalation was initiated before a full evaluation of IVIG efficacy could be completed.

Neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) blockade represents a mechanism-based therapeutic approach by accelerating IgG catabolism and lowering circulating IgG levels, including pathogenic IgG4 subclasses [16, 17]. In this patient, efgartigimod treatment was temporally associated with rapid clinical improvement. Although rituximab is used for autoimmune nodopathies [18]; its slower onset made it less suitable for this rapidly progressive case. As a B-cell–depleting therapy, rituximab generally requires weeks to months to achieve clinical effects [19]. In contrast, FcRn antagonists act more distally by inhibiting IgG recycling, leading to a more rapid decline in circulating IgG levels [20]. From a pharmacodynamic perspective, this difference may offer a potential advantage in scenarios requiring prompt immunomodulation, although comparative efficacy remains to be established. Although concomitant corticosteroid therapy may have contributed, the regimen used (methylprednisolone 40 mg/day) was lower than the moderate- to high-dose regimens typically employed in immune-mediated neuropathies. Moreover, corticosteroid-induced clinical improvement in CIDP is generally delayed, with responses often emerging over several weeks to months [21]. Therefore, the rapid neurological improvement observed within three days is more temporally consistent with FcRn blockade, although a contributory effect of corticosteroids cannot be excluded. Emerging evidence suggests that FcRn blockade with efgartigimod may enable rapid improvement in antibody-mediated autoimmune nodopathies, including anti-CNTN1–associated disease. In a recent prospective study, early neurological improvement was observed within two weeks in several patients treated with weekly efgartigimod, including those with anti-CNTN1 antibodies [22]. These findings are consistent with FcRn biology and may help explain the rapid clinical response observed in this case. However, given the single-case nature and concomitant therapies, definitive conclusions regarding treatment efficacy cannot be established.

Clinically, this case highlights the importance of early and comprehensive antibody profiling in rapidly progressive or treatment-refractory immune-mediated peripheral neuropathies. Identification of specific antibody subtypes may inform prognosis, anticipate poor responsiveness to IVIG, and facilitate timely escalation to targeted immunotherapy [4, 6, 12]. Further studies are required to determine the prevalence, optimal management strategies, and long-term outcomes of rare antibody combinations.

In conclusion, this report expands the spectrum of antibody-mediated peripheral neuropathies by describing the coexistence of anti-GM2 IgG and anti-CNTN1 antibodies. This case suggests that FcRn blockade may be considered as a potential therapeutic option in selected patients with severe or treatment-refractory antibody-associated peripheral neuropathy.

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. This report describes a single patient, which limits the generalizability of the findings and precludes causal inference regarding the interaction between anti-GM2 IgG and anti-CNTN1 antibodies. Due to postoperative constraints, cerebrospinal fluid examination was not performed, preventing the assessment of typical findings such as albuminocytologic dissociation. Additionally, preoperative electrophysiological studies were not performed, limiting our ability to definitively distinguish compressive radiculopathy from an evolving peripheral neuropathy and to clarify their temporal relationship. Furthermore, the patient’s rapidly progressive respiratory deterioration and altered consciousness precluded a comprehensive neurological examination immediately before mechanical ventilation, including formal assessment of neck flexor strength. The assessment of IVIG non-response in this case should also be interpreted with caution. Although no early clinical improvement was observed following treatment, therapeutic response to IVIG is typically evaluated over a longer time frame, often within 2–4 weeks post-therapy. Given the rapidly progressive course, escalation occurred before full IVIG evaluation, so IVIG ineffectiveness cannot be definitively concluded. Furthermore, serial post-treatment antibody titers were unavailable, limiting the assessment of dynamic immunological changes following FcRn blockade. Long-term follow-up was limited, and the lack of follow-up electromyography precluded the evaluation of electrophysiological recovery and the durability of clinical remission. Despite the presence of confusion, contrast-enhanced MRI and electroencephalography were not performed, which hindered the evaluation of potential central nervous system involvement. Additionally, brachial plexus MRI was not conducted during the disease course, restricting the anatomical assessment of proximal peripheral nerve involvement. Finally, while rituximab represents a potential alternative therapy for autoimmune nodopathies, further studies are necessary to compare the efficacy and optimal timing of different treatment strategies in acute clinical settings.

Patient perspective

The patient reported that after a period of profound weakness and dependence on mechanical ventilation, he experienced noticeable improvement in breathing and limb strength shortly after initiation of the new treatment. He expressed satisfaction with regaining the ability to eat orally and walk independently and indicated willingness to continue follow-up as recommended.
