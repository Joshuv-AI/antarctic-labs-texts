Acquisition time/dose reduction in pediatric PET imaging using patch-based deep learning
Authors: Chenyang Han, Andrew T Trout, Andi Li, Joseph G Meier, Vinicius P V Alves, Joseph MacLean, Nadeen Abu Ata, Yinan Li, Susan E Sharp, Christopher G Anton, Jing Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDeep learning (DL)-based denoising methods have shown promise for reducing radiation dose and/or acquisition time in pediatric PET imaging. However, conventional DL approaches typically require large and diverse training datasets to achieve generalizability. We propose a patch-based DL (PDL) approach that learns local structural representations from a single high-quality examination, with the goal of improving image quality and preserving quantitative accuracy of reduced-count pediatric whole-body PET while minimizing training burden.MethodsThis retrospective study included PET/CT datasets acquired on a GE Discovery MI Gen 2 scanner. A single pediatric examination with high structural conspicuity and low noise was used for training. Testing was performed using a NEMA phantom and 88 clinical pediatric examinations (age < 12 years) acquired with routine pediatric FDG dosing and a 90‑s‑per‑bed protocol. Clinical list‑mode data were truncated to 20 s per bed to simulate reduced‑count acquisitions; training and phantom datasets were downsampled to match this noise level. The PDL model was trained using paired full‑count and reduced‑count image patches and applied to denoise reduced‑count phantom and patient images. Phantom evaluations assessed spatial resolution, contrast recovery, and noise. Clinical evaluation included a blinded observer study and quantitative SUV analysis, followed by assessment of the noise–bias tradeoff.ResultsPDL preserved spatial resolution and contrast while achieving substantial noise reduction in phantom experiments. In the observer study, PDL‑enhanced 20‑s‑per‑bed images were rated as equivalent or superior in overall image quality and noise relative to standard 90‑s‑per‑bed images in more than 85% of cases. Small negative relative bias in SUVmax and SUVmean was observed in the liver, mediastinal blood pool, and lesions, with no statistically significant differences compared with standard‑count images. Compared with highly regularized reconstruction, PDL achieved greater noise reduction with reduced bias.ConclusionsPDL enhancement for pediatric whole-body PET imaging may enable a reduction in acquisition time to 20 s per bed or a reduction in administered activity to approximately 22% of current clinical practice, while maintaining spatial resolution, contrast, quantitative accuracy, and diagnostic image quality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40658-026-00876-2.Keywords: Pediatric PET imaging, Dose reduction, Patch-based deep learning

Introduction

Fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) positron emission tomography (PET) is a cornerstone in diagnosing and managing pediatric patients with known or suspected malignancies because of its unique ability to characterize and quantify metabolic processes in vivo [1, 2]. Despite these advantages, pediatric PET imaging is constrained by two major factors: ionizing radiation exposure and prolonged scan durations.

Among pediatric diagnostic imaging modalities, PET imparts one of the highest radiation doses [3–5]. Children are inherently more radiosensitive than adults and have longer life expectancies, increasing their susceptibility to stochastic effects of radiation [6, 7]. Consequently, minimizing radiation exposure is a critical priority. PET dose can vary substantially depending on factors such as patient age, body weight, and administered radiotracer activity, which further underscores the need for strategies that reliably reduce dose without compromising diagnostic quality. Similarly, reducing scan duration is desirable, as current PET protocols often necessitate sedation or anesthesia in younger patients. Shorter scans could reduce or eliminate this need, thereby lowering associated risks and costs [8, 9].

However, lowering injected activity or shortening acquisition time reduces detected coincidence events, leading to increased image noise and degraded image quality, which can compromise quantitative accuracy and diagnostic reliability [10]. Recent advances in deep learning (DL) have shown promise in mitigating these limitations by effectively reducing noise while preserving spatial resolution, enabling low-dose or rapid PET imaging [11]. Various DL architectures have been explored, including convolutional neural networks (CNNs) [12], conditional generative adversarial networks (cGANs) [13], transfer learning approaches [14], and, most recently, denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs) [15]. Despite these developments, DL-based denoising for pediatric whole-body PET remains underexplored due to several challenges: (1) Data scarcity and variability: Pediatric imaging datasets are limited and exhibit substantial variability in disease states, body habitus, and physiology, complicating model generalization [16, 17]. (2) Dependence on high-quality labels: Supervised DL methods require high-count reference images, which are difficult to obtain clinically due to ethical and logistical constraints [18, 19]. (3) Intra-patient uptake heterogeneity: Whole-body PET exhibits pronounced regional uptake differences (e.g., high 18F-FDG accumulation in the brain and bladder), challenging model robustness across anatomical regions [20].

Patch-based image processing offers a potential solution by leveraging local geometric similarities to capture fine image details. This approach has demonstrated success in denoising tasks for natural images [21, 22], CT [23], and MRI [24, 25]. Building on these principles, we previously developed patch-based DL (PDL) methods for brain and cardiac PET denoising [26–28], achieving strong generalization from single-dataset training.

In this study, we extend PDL to pediatric whole-body PET imaging. Specifically, we (1) Propose a PDL model for denoising reduced-count pediatric whole-body 18F-FDG PET images; (2) Demonstrate the advantage of training on a dataset with high structural conspicuity and low noise; and (3) Validate the method through phantom and patient studies.

Methods

Study datasets

This retrospective HIPAA-compliant study was conducted at Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Medical Center and approved by the institutional review board. A waiver of written informed consent was granted. All 18F-FDG PET/CT datasets were acquired using a 30-cm axial field-of-view digital PET/CT scanner (Discovery MI Gen 2, GE Healthcare, Waukesha, WI).

Phantom data

A NEMA IEC body phantom containing spheres with diameters of 10, 13, 17, 22, 28, and 37 mm was prepared with 89.2 MBq 18F-FDG, maintaining a 4:1 activity ratio between the spheres and background. The phantom was scanned for 1,800 s, beginning 86 min after preparation.

Patient data

A total of 89 clinical 18F-FDG patient PET/CT examinations were included. All were acquired with weight-based FDG dosing of 4.44 MBq/kg for patients ≥ 20 kg and 3.7 MBq/kg for those < 20 kg and a 90-s-per-bed acquisition protocol [10, 29].

Training Dataset: One examination of a 17-year-old male patient was selected for model training based on high structural conspicuity and low image noise (Figure S1). Selection was performed by AT, a board-certified radiologist with subspecialty qualification in nuclear radiology and pediatric radiology and 11 years of post-fellowship experience.

Testing Datasets: The remaining 88 examinations, acquired between August 2022 and March 2023, were from 64 unique patients under 12 years of age (adolescents and young adults were excluded). These datasets were exclusively for model testing.

Data preparation

Image reconstruction for phantom and patient datasets was performed offline using a dedicated toolbox (Duetto; GE HealthCare, Waukesha, WI) with two algorithms: (1) Ordered Subset Expectation Maximization (OSEM): 2 iterations, 34 subsets; and (2) Regularized Reconstruction (Q.Clear; GE Healthcare) with a regularization parameter β. Both algorithms incorporated corrections for normalization, CT-based attenuation, scatter, randoms, time-of-flight (TOF), and point spread function modeling. Transaxial image dimensions were 256 × 256, with the slice numbers varying by patient height. Voxel size was 2.73 × 2.73 × 2.80 mm3.

Reduced count: To match the noise levels of reduced-count patient images, reconstructions used the first 10 million counts of each noise realization. These were reconstructed using OSEM and regularized reconstruction with β = 700 and β = 1,300 (the latter produces lower-noise images for comparison).

Training patient data

The clinically acquired 90-s-per-bed list-mode data for the training patient were reconstructed with β = 700 to obtain a standard-count image. To simulate reduced-count conditions, the list-mode data were truncated to the first 10 s per bed (Table 2) and reconstructed using OSEM and β = 700.

Test patient data

For each test patient dataset, clinically acquired 90-s-per-bed list-mode data were reconstructed using regularized reconstruction with β = 700 to generate standard-count images. Reduced-count images were created by truncating the list-mode data to the first 20 s per bed position, simulating a shortened acquisition. This truncation was chosen to enable a complete PET/CT examination in under 5 min for most pediatric patients. Reduced-count datasets were reconstructed using OSEM, and regularized reconstruction with β = 700 and β = 1,300, with the higher β value applied to produce lower-noise images for comparison.

Patch-based deep learning model

A schematic of the proposed PDL architecture is shown in Fig. 1. The model is a fully connected, patch-based deep neural network comprising three hidden layers. Unlike convolutional architectures, fully connected layers ensure that each output voxel depends on all input voxels without requiring spatial downsampling or expansion of receptive fields. This design preserves spatial resolution—an especial consideration for PET imaging, where resolution is inherently limited by physical and reconstruction constraints.

Architecture of the patch-based deep learning model during training. The network input consists of location-matched 4 × 4 × 4 patches extracted from reduced-count PET images reconstructed using OSEM and regularized reconstruction (β = 700). The target patch is the corresponding 4 × 4 × 4 voxel patch from the standard-count image reconstructed with β = 700

Each training input consisted of two patches extracted from the same spatial location: one patch from the OSEM-reconstructed reduced-count image, which provided higher spatial resolution and contrast under the reconstruction settings used in this study, and another patch from the regularized reconstruction β = 700, which contributed features with reduced noise. The corresponding label patch was extracted from the 90-s-per-bed standard-count image reconstructed with β = 700. To assess the contribution of the less noisy features, we also trained a comparative model using only the OSEM-reconstructed reduced-count patch as input.

By using maximum overlap between adjacent patches—implemented as a one-voxel shift in a single direction—we extracted 4 × 4 × 4 image patches from the training images after cropping to reduce background dominance. This approach ensured full spatial coverage, provided diverse contextual neighborhoods for each voxel, and minimized boundary effects. To limit computational cost, only a subset of the extracted patches was used for network training. Half of the patches were selected based on variance to enrich the dataset with anatomically informative and lesion-related structures, while the remaining half were randomly sampled to maintain unbiased spatial coverage [28]. Although overlapping introduces some redundancy in high-variance patches, it stabilizes training for fully connected networks, which rely on large and diverse example sets. The size of the training subset was determined by training models with varying numbers of patches while keeping the patch selection strategy fixed.

Whole-body PET images exhibit substantial intra-patient variability in radiotracer uptake. Regions with very high activity but limited diagnostic relevance, such as the bladder, tend to dominate network attention compared with lesions and organs with lower uptake. To mitigate this effect, PET images were intensity-truncated before patch generation by applying a maximum threshold of 100 kBq/mL. Any voxel exceeding this threshold was set to 100 kBq/mL, and the entire image was subsequently normalized to a range of 0–1.

The PDL network was trained for 1,000 epochs, each comprising 1,400 iterations, with a batch size of 1,000. An L2 loss function and the Adam optimizer were employed, with an initial learning rate of 5 × 10–4, and network weights were randomly initialized. The implementation was performed in PyTorch 2.0 and executed on an NVIDIA A100 GPU.

After training, the model was applied to enhance reduced-count phantom and patient images, generating PDL-enhanced reconstructions. Similar to the training process, reduced-count images reconstructed using OSEM and β = 700 were intensity-truncated and normalized. Image patches were then extracted with maximum overlap and processed through the trained network. The enhanced patches were reassembled and denormalized to obtain the final PDL-enhanced reduced-count images.

To evaluate the impact of training with a low-noise dataset versus an average-noise dataset, we randomly selected a test examination with an average noise level and trained an additional PDL model using patch pairs from 20-s-per-bed and 90-s-per-bed images. This model was then applied to generate enhanced reduced-count phantom images, which were compared with those produced by the model trained on the low-noise dataset.

Phantom data evaluation

Circular regions of interest (ROIs) were placed on the transaxial PET slice intersecting the centers of the 10, 13, 17, and 22 mm hot spheres, guided by the corresponding CT image of the phantom. Additionally, twelve non-overlapping 3-cm spherical ROIs were defined in the background.

Spatial Resolution: Full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) was calculated from line profiles drawn along the left–right (x) axis intersecting the highest activity within each hot sphere (13, 17, and 22 mm) for full-count, standard-count, and reduced-count images, both without and with PDL enhancement [30]. In accordance with NEMA standards, the 10 mm sphere was excluded because its FWHM (7.9 mm) was smaller than the size of three voxels (8.19 mm) [31].

Patient data evaluation

Qualitative analysis

A human observer study was conducted by five board-certified pediatric radiologists who regularly interpret pediatric PET at Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Medical Center (NA, YL, CA, SS, and AT, with 1, 5, 25, 16, and 13 years of post-fellowship experience, respectively). Observers compared image quality and image noise between standard-count images reconstructed with β = 700 and PDL-enhanced reduced-count images for each test examination.

For each comparison, the two images were displayed side by side in random left–right order, with the arrangement blinded to all readers [32]. Images were presented using MIM Encore (MIM Software Inc., Cleveland, OH) to replicate a clinical reading environment. Radiologists were asked to indicate their preference for one image over the other based on two criteria: (1) overall structural conspicuity and diagnostic efficacy, and (2) image noise. Inter-reader agreement was assessed using weighted Cohen’s kappa (κw).

Quantitative analysis

Following the blinded qualitative assessment, one reader (AT) placed a 3-cm spherical ROI in the liver and a 1.5-cm spherical ROI in the mediastinal blood pool on each image set. The same reader also contoured up to five of the most FDG-avid lesions per examination using the PET Edge tool (MIM Software), resulting in 164 hypermetabolic lesions from 73 examinations. Lesions were categorized by anatomical regions: 48 in the head and neck, 31 in the chest, 63 in the abdomen and pelvis, and 22 in the extremities.

For each ROI, SUVmean and SUVmax were measured. Regression analysis and relative difference plots were used to compare SUVs from PDL-enhanced reduced-count images against those from standard-count images. Paired t-tests were performed to assess systematic difference, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05.

A separate observer (CH) drew multiple non-overlapping 3-cm ROIs in homogeneous liver regions under guidance by VA and AT. These liver ROIs, combined with lesion ROIs drawn by AT, were used for noise-bias evaluation. Figure 6a summarizes reader preferences between standard-count images reconstructed with β = 700 and PDL-enhanced reduced-count images, categorized by observers. For overall image quality, preferences varied among observers, with an overall weighted Cohen’s kappa (κw) of 0.34 (95% CI 0.29–0.39). Three readers preferred most PDL-enhanced reduced-count images over standard-count images, while two readers expressed no overall preference. Across all readers, PDL-enhanced reduced-count images were rated as better than (46.4 ± 38.3%) or no different from (40.2 ± 38.3%) the standard-count images in an average of 86.6 ± 10.6% of cases. Notably, no reader expressed an overall preference for standard-count images over PDL-enhanced reduced-count images. For image noise, there was a clear preference for the PDL-enhanced reduced-count images, with an overall weighted κw of 0.55 (95% CI 0.43–0.67). Across five readers, these images were rated as better than the standard-count images in 89.8 ± 2.1% of cases and as no different in 2.0 ± 3.4% of cases, for a combined mean of 91.8 ± 2.5%.

Discussion

In this study, we present a PDL method for enhancing reduced-count pediatric 18F-FDG PET images using training derived from a single low-noise patient dataset. In phantom experiments, PDL enhancement achieved spatial resolution and image contrast comparable to a full-count (1,800 s) reference image, while reducing noise relative to both standard-count and reduced-count images reconstructed with regularization. In the patient study, PDL-enhanced 20-s-per-bed reduced-count images were subjectively rated as comparable to—or in many cases better than—90-s-per-bed standard-count images in overall image quality and noise. Although minor quantitative biases in SUVmax and SUVmean ​ were observed, these differences were not statistically significant and are unlikely to affect clinical interpretation. Importantly, blinded observer ratings confirmed that PDL-enhanced images maintain high subjective image quality and diagnostic confidence, which is critical for clinical applicability. Furthermore, PDL enhancement achieved a noise–bias trade-off unattainable by regularized reconstruction, which typically reduces noise at the expense of spatial resolution through strong regularization [26].

A PDL model can be trained with any examination; however, extracting patches from a high-quality image to serve as training labels is expected to improve model performance when applied to test datasets. As demonstrated by our phantom study (Table 3), a model trained with an examination exhibiting high structure conspicuity and low noise achieved better spatial resolution, improved contrast, and greater noise reduction compared with a model trained on a dataset with average noise levels. We downsampled the training inputs to a 10‑s acquisition duration so that their noise characteristics matched the 88 test datasets, consistent with the strategy described by Liu et al. [18]. Unmatched training and testing noise/dose levels can otherwise lead to different trade-offs between denoising and resolution preservation [34].

For this study, the PDL model was trained using 1.4 million patches extracted from a single low-noise PET scan. This dataset size lies within the performance saturation range (≥ 0.5 million patches) and ensures that the ratio of training samples to network parameters (~ 106 k) remains within the empirically recommended range of 10–20 (13 in this study), helping mitigate overfitting [35]. Because structural patterns such as edges, textures, smooth regions, and noise are sufficient to reconstruct a wide range of image appearances [36], training on a large and diverse patch set enabled effective generalization to pediatric cases (n = 88; < 12 years; height 62–157 cm), despite substantial differences from the single training subject (n = 1; 17 years; height 192 cm). Patient age exhibited no observable effect on reader preference. Moreover, quantitative SUV measurements were not appreciably influenced by patient age or lesion location, providing additional evidence that local patch-level image features capture sufficient structural information to ensure robust model performance across patient populations. These findings align with our previous research [26–28].

Our results echo prior literature demonstrating the ability of DL-based enhancement to enable whole-body PET imaging with reduced dose or acquisition time. Representative studies have reported that 12.5% [37], 33% [38], 10% [39], and 66% [40] of the standard dose/acquisition time can be achieved without noticeable differences in image quality or quantitative measures. Despite variations in DL models, scanners, imaging protocols, and evaluation metrics, these studies relied on large training datasets (> 100 exams). In contrast, our PDL model eliminates the need for such large volumes of training data.

Several factors may influence the performance of the proposed PDL method. In the patient study, sub-centimeter structures were more prone to blurring, as indicated by consistent negative bias in SUVmean for lesions < 1 mL and in SUVmax. This likely occurs because the PDL model minimizes differences across all training patches, creating an average mapping between the inputs and targets. Consequently, tiny structures may merge with the background, leading to loss of detail. Reducing voxel size, which increases the relative size of sub-centimeter structures within a patch, has been suggested as a potential solution [41], as demonstrated in prior studies on sub-centimeter hot spheres [30, 42]. However, smaller voxel sizes increase image noise, complicating denoising [43]. Optimizing voxel size to balance structural preservation and effective denoising remains an area of interest.

Age dependence was observed in a small subset of cases: in four of 22 examinations from patients < 3 years of age, a majority of observers (≥ 3) preferred the standard-count images for image quality. However, this preference was not consistent across all examinations in this age group, and no unanimous preference was observed. No majority preference for standard-count images occurred in patients ≥ 3 years of age. These findings may reflect differences in FDG physiology in the youngest patients [17] or the small-structure blurring effect discussed earlier. Nevertheless, the overall rare preference for standard-count images and absence of unanimous preference indicates that broad applicability of the proposed method across pediatric populations.

Another consideration is the truncation threshold (100 kBq/mL), which was selected based on our routine injected dose. For protocols using substantially lower activities, this threshold may need proportional adjustment to maintain consistent voxel-value ranges. Our study focused on a simulated 2/9 dose-reduction setting and demonstrated that the model tolerated variability in image noise within this range; however, extension to substantially different dose levels (e.g., full or standard counts) was not assessed and would likely require retraining to ensure optimal performance. Finally, applicability to PET examinations using radiopharmaceuticals other than 18F-FDG or on different scanner platforms has not yet been tested. These factors constrain generalizability, but the minimal training data requirement and straightforward implementation of the PDL method simplify adaptation to other tracers, scanners, and dose levels.

Conclusions

We developed and validated a PDL model to enhance pediatric 18F-FDG whole-body PET images acquired using our routine injected dose with a shortened acquisition time of 20 s per bed—approximately 22% of the clinically used 90-s protocol. Compared with standard clinical images, the PDL-enhanced images demonstrated comparable spatial resolution, contrast, and diagnostic image quality. Unlike conventional supervised deep learning approaches, our method requires only a single dataset for training and demonstrates strong generalizability across diverse patient conditions. Furthermore, we highlight the advantage of using high structural conspicuity and low-noise images as training labels. For pediatric whole-body 18F-FDG PET imaging on a GE Discovery MI Gen 2 scanner, this technique may enable total acquisition times under 5 min using our routine injected dose. Alternatively, when using the standard 90-s-per-bed acquisition protocol, it offers the potential for up to a 78% reduction in administered radiotracer dose for pediatric patients.
