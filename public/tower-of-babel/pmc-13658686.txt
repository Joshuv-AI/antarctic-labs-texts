Burden of Invasive Meningococcal Disease: Evolving Epidemiology and Implications for Prevention Policy
Authors: Mary Bausch-Jurken, Sean Leng, Jana Shaw, Stephen Pelton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Invasive meningococcal disease (IMD), caused by Neisseria meningitidis, remains a rare but devastating condition characterized by rapid clinical progression, high case fatality, and long-term morbidity. Despite effective conjugate (or alternative) vaccine availability, sporadic cases and outbreaks persist across the age spectrum in the USA and globally. Contemporary surveillance data reveal a dynamic epidemiologic landscape, shifting serogroup dominance, evolving outbreak patterns involving community-based and socially connected groups, and growing recognition of disease burden among adults. Adolescents serve as key reservoirs of nasopharyngeal carriage; however, despite IMD incidence and case-fatality rates being highest among infants and older adults, respectively, both populations lack routine age-based vaccination recommendations. Prevention strategies in the USA remain anchored to historical incidence peaks and narrowly defined risk groups, leaving substantial gaps in protection, as highlighted by the sharp contrast between high adolescent quadrivalent meningococcal conjugate vaccine coverage and low serogroup B vaccine uptake under shared clinical decision-making. This narrative review synthesizes contemporary evidence on age-specific IMD burden, serogroup dynamics, and long-term sequelae, and critically examines how existing vaccination policies align with infection risk across the lifespan, with emphasis on US vaccination policy. Additional country-specific studies were included to contextualize age-specific epidemiology and provide policy comparators. A life-course-based prevention framework integrating age-related vulnerability, severity, and evolving transmission dynamics is needed to mitigate the burden of IMD.

Introduction

Invasive meningococcal disease (IMD), caused by the Gram-negative, polysaccharide-encapsulated diplococcus Neisseria meningitidis, represents a significant global public health threat due to its rapid clinical progression, high associated mortality, and substantial long-term sequelae [1–5]. Although rare in absolute terms, with an estimated annual incidence of 0.2–0.4 per 100,000 population in the USA, IMD carries case fatality rates (CFRs) of 8–15%, even with optimal medical management [5–8].

IMD most commonly manifests as meningitis or meningococcemia (meningococcal septicemia), either alone or in combination; less common presentations include pneumonia, septic arthritis, and pericarditis [9, 10]. Meningococcal septicemia can progress from nonspecific symptoms to death within 24 h, imposing a burden on families, healthcare systems, and communities that is disproportionate to its incidence [11, 12]. For families, the burden is devastating; the disease can be fatal, and up to 50% of survivors will experience permanent sequelae including limb amputation, sensorineural hearing loss, and neurodevelopmental impairment [2, 6, 11–13].

The epidemiology of IMD is dynamic, shaped by interactions among pathogen biology, host immunity, population behavior, and vaccination programs [12, 14–16]. Six serogroups (A, B, C, W, X, and Y) account for 95% of invasive disease, although their relative predominance varies by geographic region, age group, and over time [6, 15, 17, 18]. Disease incidence varies considerably across the lifespan, with infants younger than 1 year having the highest age-specific incidence rates. A second peak occurs among adolescents and young adults aged 16–23 years; this age group serves as the principal reservoir for N. meningitidis, which peaks at approximately 20–25% due to social behaviors that facilitate transmission and confer a disproportionate role in community transmission [19]. Adults and older adults account for a growing proportion of cases and experience the highest CFRs [6–8, 15]. Globally, the burden remains geographically heterogeneous, varying between regions from persistent endemic disease to periodic epidemics and explosive outbreaks. The African meningitis belt, a region spanning sub-Saharan Africa from Senegal to Ethiopia, is characterized by recurrent large-scale meningococcal epidemics [6, 15, 20–23]. Post-coronavirus disease of 2019 (COVID-19) shifts in transmission patterns and delayed clinical recognition have further complicated the epidemiologic landscape [24, 25].

Meningococcal vaccination programs in the USA have been guided since 1972 by recommendations from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) [26, 27]. There are three types of routine ACIP recommendation: age-based, risk-based, and catch-up. Routine age- and risk-based recommendations pertain to everyone in a particular age group or risk category, respectively, while catch-up recommendations relate to missed doses. In 2019, shared clinical decision-making (SCDM) was introduced into the ACIP framework as “Category B” or “permissive recommendation” to address vaccines that provide individual-level benefit but lack sufficient evidence of broad population-level impact [26]. SCDM relies on a discussion between patient/caregiver and health care provider (HCP) and is recommended for specific vaccines, including MenB [26]. Despite long-standing vaccination programs, current US meningococcal prevention policy is predominantly anchored to historical incidence peaks in adolescents and narrowly defined high-risk groups.

In the US, the introduction of quadrivalent meningococcal conjugate vaccines (MenACWY) in 2005 contributed to the substantial reduction in serogroup C and Y disease among adolescents, with serogroup B emerging as the leading cause of sporadic (residual) disease in young adults. In adults and older adults, serogroups W and Y have become important drivers of disease, due in part to the expansion of hyperinvasive clonal complexes such as serogroup Y sequence type (ST)−1466 [8, 15, 22, 28–30]. The 2023–2024 National Immunization Survey-Teen—USA reported adolescent coverage of > 90% for receipt of at least one dose of a MenACWY vaccine (Menveo [Novartis/GSK], Menactra [Sanofi Pasteur], or MenQuadfi [Sanofi Pasteur]); however, completion rates for the two dose series drop to 61.1% [31]. Serogroup B meningococcal vaccines (MenB; Bexsero [GSK] and Trumenba [Pfizer]), which were first licensed in the USA in 2014–2015 and recommended under the ACIP SCDM framework for individuals aged 16–23 years, have substantially lower uptake [32, 33]. Among adolescents, only 20–33% initiate the vaccine series within 3 years of becoming eligible, and of those who start, only 34–61% complete the series within 15 months [34, 35]. Coverage among medically high-risk adults is similarly suboptimal [36–38]. Several factors contribute to these protection gaps, including HCP uncertainty regarding the strength of the SCDM and risk-based recommendations, lack of a standardized approach and logistical barriers to HCP–patient SCDM consultations, insurance coverage and reimbursement variability (depending upon whether it is delivered under ACIP risk-based or SCDM recommendations), and the absence of high-school or college-entry mandates for meningitis B vaccination [26, 39–42]. While there is a strong rationale for the focus on individuals entering college or school dormitories, it cannot be forgotten that outbreaks of IMD do occur in other settings, and there are large sections of society who are also vulnerable, particularly those in socially connected or overcrowded settings, often where there are low rates of vaccination [28, 43]. These challenges are further compounded by gaps in adult immunization infrastructure, such as fragmented vaccine delivery systems, unclear responsibility across medical specialties, and incomplete immunization registries [44, 45]. Notably, although older adults experience the highest CFRs of any age group (estimated 15–30%) and are recognized by the CDC as an at-risk population, there is no routine age-based meningococcal vaccination recommendation for older adults [7, 8, 46–48]. Several countries have implemented routine infant meningococcal vaccination programs and broader adult vaccination strategies, demonstrating effective alternatives and underscoring policy variability, epidemiologic differences, and unmet needs [49, 50]. The recent licensure of pentavalent conjugated meningitis ABCWY (MenABCWY) vaccines (Penbraya [Pfizer]; Penmenvy [GSK]) and accumulating evidence on the consequences of SCDM frameworks for vaccine uptake, especially in light of the recent shifts in MenACWY recommendation, add further urgency to reevaluation of prevention strategies across all age groups [42, 51–53].

This narrative review aims to synthesize contemporary evidence on age-specific IMD burden, evolving serogroup dynamics, long-term sequelae, and prevention and vaccination strategies across the lifespan to critically examine the extent to which current US vaccination strategies align, with risk across the lifespan. We consider the epidemiologic, clinical, and policy landscape across age groups and medically vulnerable populations. We conclude by identifying strategic gaps in prevention policy and proposing a framework for life-course-based meningococcal prevention.

Methods

We conducted a narrative, nonsystematic literature review with targeted searches in PubMed/MEDLINE using terms including “Neisseria meningitidis,” “invasive meningococcal disease,” “meningococcal epidemiology,” “meningococcal vaccine,” and “serogroup,” combined with age-specific and burden-related terms using Boolean operators. We focused primarily on English-language publications from January 2015 through June 2026, with inclusion of landmark studies published before 2015. Authoritative surveillance data, policy documents, regulatory guidance, and reports from the World Health Organization, European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, US CDC, and ACIP were included. Reference lists of key articles and recent clinical practice guidelines were also reviewed. Included studies addressed global epidemiology, with emphasis on high-income settings including the USA, age-specific burden, and risk-based populations. The vaccination policy analysis focused primarily on the USA; CDC and ACIP sources were prioritized for current recommendations, vaccine coverage, and implementation, while World Health Organization, European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, and country-specific studies were used to contextualize age-specific epidemiology and serve as policy comparators. Case reports, small case series without broader epidemiologic relevance, and nonhuman or purely laboratory-based studies not directly linked to disease burden or prevention were excluded. Key information was extracted qualitatively, including epidemiologic trends, serogroup distribution, age-specific incidence and mortality, sequelae, and evidence on vaccine impact and programmatic outcomes. Findings were synthesized descriptively; no formal risk-of-bias assessment was performed, consistent with narrative review methodology.

Ethical Approval

This is a literature review and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors. Therefore, ethics committee approval was not required.

Results

Age-Specific Epidemiology

The burden of IMD in terms of both incidence and severity varies substantially across the lifespan, creating a rationale for examining epidemiologic patterns by age group (Table 1 and Fig. 1). Infants younger than 1 year consistently experience the highest age-specific incidence rates across virtually all geographic settings (~6.5 cases per 100,000 population), followed by a second peak among adolescents and young adults aged 15–24 years (~0.8 per 100,000 population) [5, 6, 29]. Although overall incidence is lower among working-age adults (24–64 years) and older adults (≥ 65 years), these groups account for a growing proportion of cases and bear a disproportionately high CFR [5, 6, 8, 29, 30, 54]. Infants and Young Children (0–5 Years)

Infants and young children bear the highest incidence of IMD across virtually all geographic settings, with infants younger than 1 year consistently experiencing rates substantially higher than any other age group (0.9 [USA] and 6.5 [Europe] per 100,000 population) [5, 6, 47]. Infant and early-childhood IMD carries a high CFR and substantial risk of permanent sequelae, including neurodevelopmental impairment and sensorineural hearing loss, that impose a lifelong disability burden [2, 47, 55–58]. Infants have an immature adaptive immune system; in the context of IMD, infants and neonates younger than 2 years can raise only a very limited type 2 thymus-independent immune response, rendering them largely unable to generate antibodies to encapsulated bacterial polysaccharides [59, 60]. Even in the minority of infants where maternal antibody is passively transferred across the placenta at protective levels, rapid waning renders young infants particularly susceptible to infection with N. meningitidis, with disease in this age group often presenting as fulminant meningitis or septicemia with rapid progression [56, 57, 61]. Asymptomatic adults and adolescents are the greatest source of N. meningitidis transmission to young children [56]. Therefore, infants are particularly vulnerable due to both their limited ability to fight bacterial infections and their relatively high exposure risk in household and daycare environments [62, 63]. Although incidence declines through childhood, children from birth through 4 years of age continue to experience rates greater than population averages and remain at substantial risk for severe outcomes [5, 6, 47].

In high-income settings, most infections among infants and young children are caused by serogroup B, reflecting the intrinsic pathogenicity of hyperinvasive serogroup B clonal complexes [5, 6, 64–66]. In addition, evidence to date indicates that vaccination with a serogroup B vaccine (e.g., MenB), while effective at preventing IMD, does not halt nasopharyngeal carriage of N. meningitidis B, and thus does not appear to confer effective herd immunity [67–69]. Interpretation of data from other studies is limited by confounders, including the variable timing of vaccine initiation and the proportion of participants who did not complete the full vaccine series [68, 69]. Serogroups C, W, and Y contribute variably by geographic region and over time, while serogroup X, an emerging serogroup of increasing public health concern, remains geographically localized, primarily within the African meningitis belt, although, even there, the incidence remains approximately 1–2% [5, 6, 70–72].

Adolescents and Young Adults (16–23 Years)

A second incidence peak occurs among adolescents and young adults aged 16–23 years (estimated incidence per 100,000 population, 0.1–0.2 [USA] and 0.8 [Europe]), driven by the confluence of high nasopharyngeal carriage rates, social mixing behaviors, and congregate living environments [5, 6, 8, 16]. This age group serves as the principal reservoir for meningococcal transmission, with nasopharyngeal carriage prevalence peaking in late adolescence and early adulthood [19, 73, 74]. In developed countries, carriage rates increase during childhood, peaking in adolescents and young adults at around 23.7% [19], but can reach as high as 56% in certain congregate settings [75]. Adolescents and young adults thus play a disproportionate role in sustaining carriage and precipitating outbreaks [14, 19, 67, 76, 77]. In this population, IMD most commonly presents as meningitis or meningococcemia, often with rapid onset. The classic clinical triad of fever, neck stiffness, and petechial or purpuric rash is more reliably present in this age group than in younger children or older adults [78, 79]. Disease is caused primarily by serogroup B, which predominates in sporadic disease and campus-associated outbreaks. Serogroups C, W, and Y may also cause infections, depending on regional epidemiology [68]. Serogroup X disease is almost nonexistent in developed countries, and serogroup A has disappeared from US epidemiology [6, 8, 80, 81]. Historically, the risk paradigm for meningococcal disease in young adults has been shaped by college attendance as a proxy for congregate living [68]. However, contemporary data demonstrate that IMD outbreaks increasingly occur outside university settings, including among community-based and socially connected groups [28, 43]. Underserved populations include those who live in high-contact environments, such as those in shared or crowded housing (with a high crowding index), particularly where there is passive smoking, and people who frequently attend community-based events or other crowded social venues (concerts, nightclubs, pubs), among others [28, 82–85]. Several of these factors intersect with broader social determinants of health, including socioeconomic disadvantage and lower educational attainment, which independently contribute to IMD risk through pathways such as higher prevalence of chronic underlying illnesses. Furthermore, many of these factors are disproportionately associated with certain ethnicities/races, including Māori, Pacifica, Black or African American, and Hispanic populations, and with low rates of vaccine uptake, highlighting underlying health inequities [82, 83, 85, 86]. Black or African American people in the USA bear the highest burden for IMD; in a recent outbreak largely associated with N. meningitidis serogroup Y in the USA, 63% of affected individuals were Black or African American [29].

Older Adults (≥ 65 Years)

Older adults represent a paradox in meningococcal disease: they experience the lowest incidence (25%) among adult age groups [87] but bear the highest CFRs, estimated at 15−20% in individuals ≥ 65 years, and up to 28−33% at age ≥ 80 years [7, 8, 47, 87, 88]. Globally, serogroups C, W, and Y account for the majority of cases in this population; serogroup B is uncommon [5, 6, 80]. The disproportionate mortality is likely due to age-related immunosenescence, a higher prevalence of comorbid conditions, and atypical clinical presentations that can lead to delayed diagnosis of IMD.

IMD in older adults frequently diverges from the classic triad of fever, neck stiffness, and petechial rash. Meningococcal pneumonia, gastrointestinal presentations, and septic arthritis may predominate, which can lead to misdiagnosis and delayed therapy [89]. Serogroup Y has a well-described tropism for the respiratory tract and is a recognized cause of primary meningococcal pneumonia in this population, a presentation that may be mistaken for community-acquired bacterial pneumonia of other etiology [90, 91]. The challenge of diagnosing IMD in older adults is compounded by frequent initiation of empiric antibiotics prior to sample culture collection, which can reduce culture yield and delay or prevent microbiologic confirmation [92]. Syndromic multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) panels offer the potential to improve diagnostic sensitivity even after antibiotic treatment, enhancing both surveillance accuracy and clinical recognition of IMD in older adults and other underrecognized populations [92, 93].

Medically Vulnerable and Other At-Risk Populations

Adults aged 24–64 years occupy an epidemiologic space characterized by lower overall incidence compared with the pediatric population; however, IMD in this population does present a substantial and underrecognized burden, with a CFR of approximately 10–14% and the greatest economic productivity losses of any group [7, 47, 94]. Recent US and European surveillance data document an increasing proportion of IMD cases in adults aged 24–64 years, with serogroups C, W, and particularly Y contributing to a growing share of adult disease [5, 6, 28, 29, 95, 96]. Behavioral factors (tobacco and alcohol use) and environmental or occupational exposures (e.g., overcrowding, laboratory workers who are routinely exposed to meningococci) in this population also increase risk [23, 84, 97, 98]. As in older adults, atypical clinical presentations (e.g., fever, nausea, vomiting) in working-age adults can contribute to delayed diagnosis and worse outcomes [9, 10, 92]. CFRs in adults exceed those observed in adolescents, and survivors face substantial economic burden (approximately USD 700,000 per case), including lost work productivity, nonmarket losses (unpaid household, caregiving activities), and prolonged rehabilitation [7, 47, 94, 99–101]. The growing adult IMD burden challenges age-restricted prevention paradigms and highlights gaps in both surveillance sensitivity and clinician awareness of meningococcal disease beyond pediatric populations.

Certain medical conditions are associated with a substantially elevated risk of IMD, regardless of age. Individuals with complement component deficiencies or receiving complement inhibitor therapies (e.g., eculizumab, ravulizumab), people with anatomical or functional asplenia, and those living with chronic renal disease or human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) are at markedly increased risk of IMD [4, 23, 84, 97, 98, 102, 103].

Community-based and occupational outbreaks involving multiple serogroups underscore the limitations of narrowly defined risk-based frameworks. Outbreaks involving serogroups C, Y, and B have been documented among men who have sex with men, people experiencing homelessness, and other socially connected groups [43, 68, 104–106]. A community-based outbreak attributable to serogroup Y in Virginia (USA) between 2022 and 2024 affected 36 individuals. This outbreak, which had a CFR of 19.4%, predominantly affected individuals aged 30–60 years (64%; not usually high risk for IMD). A disproportionately high proportion of affected individuals were African American (78% of cases); 14% of cases occurred in individuals who had HIV (people for whom there are currently meningococcal vaccine recommendations [37]), and all cases required hospitalization. Only one of the affected individuals had evidence of previous vaccination against N. meningitidis (16 years prior) [107]. In a community outbreak in Chicago (IL, USA) that was declared over in March 2026, ten individuals were diagnosed with IMD between mid-January and February 2026; two patients died (CFR, 20%). Patients in this outbreak were aged 30–80 years; four of the cases were associated with a community of people experiencing homelessness. It is reported that older adults, African Americans, and Hispanic/Latino people were disproportionately affected. Several of the affected individuals had preexisting conditions, including oral cancer, kidney failure, renal transplant, cirrhosis, chronic heart failure, and valvular heart disease. No cases were reported in individuals with HIV [108–110].

Long-Term Sequelae and Societal Burden

The burden of IMD extends well beyond acute illness. Approximately 10–50% of survivors develop one or more permanent sequelae, with risk varying by age at presentation, clinical syndrome, serogroup, and timeliness of treatment [2, 13, 55, 111–113]. Age is a strong independent predictor of both mortality and long-term disability. Older adults suffering from IMD experience the highest fatality, while children and adolescents face a higher cumulative lifetime burden of disability owing to longer survival with sequelae [2, 7, 13, 87, 114]. In children, as many as 30–49% of IMD survivors will experience at least one of the sequelae, most commonly sensorineural hearing loss, cognitive deficits, and behavioral or emotional problems. Other IMD-associated sequelae include neurodevelopmental impairment, seizures, motor deficits, and limb loss [115]. The prevalence and severity of individual conditions vary widely [115]; however, these conditions can impose lifelong disability [2, 58, 114, 115]. Among adolescents and young adults, psychological and cognitive sequelae, including posttraumatic stress, anxiety, and executive dysfunction, may disrupt educational and vocational trajectories [2]. In adults, chronic fatigue, cognitive impairment, and reduced work capacity contribute to an “invisible disability” that is frequently underestimated, while older adult survivors face accelerated functional decline, loss of independence, and exacerbation of preexisting comorbidities [11, 100, 111, 116].

The societal impact of IMD extends to caregivers, family members, and households, who may experience mental health effects, financial strain, and impaired workforce participation [11, 13, 112]. Total (direct and indirect) healthcare costs for IMD survivors with sequelae are estimated to be approximately two-fold higher than for those without, driven largely by direct costs associated with rehospitalization risk and ongoing specialist resource utilization [99, 117–119]. With regard to indirect healthcare costs, lifetime productivity losses for individuals diagnosed with IMD in the USA are estimated at approximately $700,000 per case [94]. However, these indirect nonhealthcare costs, including productivity losses and informal caregiving, are frequently excluded from economic analyses, leading to systematic underestimation of the true societal burden [94, 120, 121]. Contemporary data on long-term outcomes are particularly limited for older adults and for associations between burden of sequelae and specific serogroups or clinical management [87]. These evidence gaps underscore the importance of incorporating broader societal outcomes into prevention evaluations.

Prevention and Vaccination Strategies Across the Lifespan

In most countries, IMD serogroups A, B, C, W, and Y are vaccine preventable, and vaccination offers the most effective strategy to reduce both acute disease and potential lifelong IMD-associated disability. However, meningococcal vaccination policy in the USA is structured around age-based and risk-based recommendations rather than a comprehensive lifespan strategy (Fig. 1 and Table 2). The Department of Health and Human Services/ACIP and American Academy of Pediatrics currently recommend routine meningococcal vaccination for adolescents using the quadrivalent conjugate vaccine MenACWY, administered as a two-dose series, with the first dose at age 11–12 years, and a booster dose at age 16 years (designed to sustain protection through the period of highest carriage and transmission risk) [46, 53]. In the adolescent population, MenACWY vaccination achieves high coverage; however, a gap remains in two-dose series completion; in 2024 approximately 90% received the first dose and 60% received the second [31]. MenB vaccines were made available for individuals aged 16–23 years under the SCDM framework, introduced by the ACIP in 2015 with the aim of permitting individualized assessment of vaccination risk and benefit [46, 53]. Clinical trials have demonstrated the safety and tolerability of MenACWY vaccines in older adults when administered either as a booster or a primary dose (participants aged ≥ 56 years, with study populations ranging from 56–103 years of age) [123–125]. After implementation of additional amendments in April 2025, the ACIP now recommends vaccination with the pentavalent conjugate vaccine covering all five serogroups, MenABCWY (Penbraya and Penmenvy, both currently licensed for use in persons aged 10–25 years [51, 52]) for individuals aged ≥ 10 years who are eligible to receive concurrent quadrivalent MenACWY and monovalent MenB vaccines, and for individuals aged ≥ 10 years who are at increased risk for meningococcal disease and who had received either the quadrivalent or monovalent formulations at least 6 months earlier (aligned with the MenB formulation received at the initial dose: MenB-FHbp or MenB-4C) [53]. In terms of a public-health benefit, the pentavalent vaccine may offer the opportunity to reduce the number of required vaccines for broader coverage and improve series completion rates (although the latter has not been confirmed in clinical trials). However, at the current time, the recommendation is only for visits at which both the MenACWY and MenB vaccines are being administered; therefore, there is no recommendation for use at the 11–12-year visit, when only MenACWY is scheduled [126]. In October 2024, the ACIP updated its recommendations for the MenB-4C vaccine based on new immunogenicity data, alignment with the updated US Food and Drug Administration label, and harmonization with the dosing schedules of other MenB vaccines. For healthy adolescents and young adults aged 16–23 years, the recommended dosing interval was updated to 6 months between doses. For individuals aged ≥ 10 years who are at increased risk for meningococcal disease, ACIP now recommends a three-dose series administered at 0, 1–2, and 6 months [127].

Conjugate vaccines confer both direct protection and indirect (herd) effects that benefit unvaccinated age groups, particularly infants, and elicit immunologic memory that supports booster responsiveness [128]. By contrast, MenB vaccines provide protection that is more short-lived and individually directed, with no consistent evidence of herd effect or reduced carriage. Compared with MenACWY, uptake of MenB vaccination under the SCDM framework remains substantially lower among adolescents and young adults; of the 20–30% of these individuals who initiate vaccination, it is estimated that less than 50% will complete the series [42, 43, 67, 129]. The SCDM designation has been associated with reduced vaccine delivery compared with routinely recommended vaccines, raising the question of whether this framework adequately serves a population at measurable risk [41, 42, 46, 130]. Contributing factors may include lack of HCP awareness around the underlying need for meningococcal vaccination, uncertainty regarding the strength of the recommendation, differences in recommendations for MenB versus MenACWY, insurance coverage variability, and logistical barriers to a multidose series [39, 41, 131, 132]. The gap between MenACWY and MenB series initiation (90% versus 37% in 2024) and completion (61% versus 16%) among adolescents and young adults in one study highlights the impact of recommendation category on vaccine uptake [31].

In the USA, most cases of IMD occur sporadically (approximately 95%), with only a small proportion (~5%) linked to outbreaks. Data from 2009 to 2013 indicate that serogroup B was the predominant cause of outbreaks associated with organized settings, including most university outbreaks, whereas serogroup C was more frequently identified in community-based outbreaks. Among outbreaks with available information, nearly half involved the implementation of vaccination and/or expanded chemoprophylaxis interventions [43]. The reactive deployment of vaccination during outbreaks, while necessary, underscores gaps in proactive prevention [68]. Moreover, the continued focus on historically emphasized high-risk settings (college/university attendance) may fail to capture contemporary epidemiologic dynamics (community-based social groups) and carries equity implications. The crowding, tobacco use, and social mixing patterns that elevate meningococcal risk may disproportionately affect noncollege-bound and socioeconomically disadvantaged populations who are less likely to be reached by campus-centric vaccination strategies [80, 85, 121, 133].

At the time of writing (June 2026), there are no routine meningococcal vaccination recommendations in the USA for healthy infants, working-age adults outside defined risk groups, or older adults, despite measurable disease burden and, in the case of older adults, the highest CFRs of any age group [7, 8, 46, 47]. By comparison, several European and other high-income countries have implemented routine infant MenB vaccination programs, with demonstrated impact on serogroup B disease [46, 80, 87, 134, 135]. In some countries, including Australia, the Czech Republic, Finland, France, and Malta, MenACWY vaccination programs have been extended to include additional age groups, reflecting different cost-effectiveness thresholds and policy philosophies [80, 135]. This policy divergence reflects ongoing debate regarding the cost-effectiveness and programmatic feasibility of direct infant protection versus reliance on indirect effects from adolescent vaccination [136, 137]. As demonstrated in studies in the UK, while routine MenACWY vaccination of older children and adolescents has yielded measurable indirect protection for young children, resulting in approximately 25–35% efficacy against carriage and 65% decrease in carriage prevalence, there remains a persistent residual disease burden [138–140]. However, these data must be considered in the light of the inherent methodologic challenges associated with carriage studies, including cross-sectional designs, sampling variability, and difficulty measuring carriage acquisition and duration, which limit precise quantification of indirect vaccine effects on transmission [138–140]. With that caveat, the data available to date suggest that herd protection alone is insufficient protection against IMD for younger individuals [138, 140, 141]. These findings underscore the need for continued evaluation of vaccination approaches to improve protection in this highly vulnerable population.

Structural barriers also affect protection against IMD in adults and older populations. Unlike pediatric immunization, which benefits from well-established infrastructure with robust registries and school-entry mandates, adult vaccination in the USA suffers from fragmented delivery, unclear ownership across medical specialties, and incomplete immunization registries [45, 142]. Adult immunization tracking varies widely across the USA due to differences in state registry policies and provider participation. Some systems require adult opt-in or have inconsistent reporting requirements, leading to incomplete data capture. In addition, limited interoperability across systems further hinders accurate tracking and coordinated vaccination efforts [143].

Primary care physicians, infectious disease specialists, and pharmacists may each assume that this is the others’ responsibility, resulting in missed vaccination opportunities. Low clinician awareness of IMD risk beyond childhood and adolescence compounds these barriers [3, 40, 46, 132]. Therefore, despite measurable severity and mortality, at the time of writing there are no age-based meningococcal vaccination recommendations for older adults in the USA [46, 80, 87]. Similarly, no other high-income country currently implements routine age-based meningococcal vaccination for older adults in its national immunization schedule [19, 80, 87]. This stands in contrast with the approach to other vaccine-preventable diseases in this age group, including pneumococcal disease, influenza, respiratory syncytial virus, and herpes zoster, for which routine age-based vaccination is standard [46, 144]. The absence of a proactive vaccination strategy for older adults constitutes a notable difference in current prevention policy.

ACIP recommendations also include vaccination with MenACWY and MenB in medically vulnerable and high-risk groups [46]. The expanding use of complement-targeted biologics and other immunomodulatory therapies across a growing range of indications has created an increasing population of immunocompromised patients who may require, but may not consistently receive, appropriate meningococcal prophylaxis [97, 103, 145–147]. Vaccination is also recommended for travelers to hyperendemic regions (including mandatory MenACWY vaccination for Hajj and Umrah pilgrims) and for laboratory workers who are routinely exposed to N. meningitidis isolates and certain other healthcare workers [23, 46, 97, 148]. Community-based and occupational outbreaks involving multiple serogroups underscore the limitations of narrowly defined risk-based frameworks. These patterns reinforce the need for serogroup-inclusive prevention strategies and suggest that current risk-group definitions incompletely capture populations at elevated risk. Clearer and more consistently implemented guidance is needed to reach high-risk groups, including people living with HIV, for whom meningococcal vaccination recommendations exist but uptake data remain limited (10.6% and 16.3% at 1 and 2 years after initial diagnosis, respectively) [37, 102, 122].

The complexity and fragmentation of current meningococcal vaccination recommendations, with multiple vaccine products targeting different serogroup combinations, varying dose schedules for different risk groups, and the distinction between routine and SCDM recommendations, create a landscape that is challenging for HCPs to navigate and for patients to understand [82, 85]. While use of separate MenACWY and MenB vaccines may offer greater flexibility to optimize MenB vaccination around periods of highest risk, particularly given the relatively short duration of MenB protection, pentavalent meningococcal vaccines nonetheless present important programmatic advantages. In the current setting, use of pentavalent formulations would still require stocking multiple vaccine products; however, these vaccines offer an opportunity to simplify vaccination schedules and broaden serogroup coverage with fewer vaccination encounters, potentially improving uptake and enhancing the breadth of protection across the lifespan [16, 42, 51–53]. Leveraging pentavalent vaccines within a more streamlined and inclusive recommendation framework, characterized by clearer, harmonized, and risk-informed yet age-inclusive guidance, may help address persistent gaps in meningococcal protection. Strengthening surveillance infrastructure, establishing coherent policy, providing opportunities to educate the public and HCPs on IMD and vaccination strategies, and improving adult vaccination delivery and HCP accountability are complementary priorities [16, 80, 87].

Discussion

Several evidence gaps and emerging opportunities merit attention as the field moves toward a more comprehensive approach to meningococcal prevention. Although modeling studies demonstrate a substantial public health impact associated with newer MenABCWY and MenACWY vaccination, real-world data on the durability and breadth of protection conferred by newer pentavalent vaccines across age groups are needed, as are data on their impact on nasopharyngeal carriage and indirect protection [13, 67, 149]. The post-COVID-19 period has been characterized by altered respiratory pathogen transmission dynamics, with US cases surpassing 2019 levels driven by serogroup Y strain ST-1466 [29]; whether these shifts have durably affected meningococcal epidemiology, including carriage prevalence, serogroup distribution, and outbreak patterns, remains an active area of investigation requiring continued surveillance [24, 29].

In January 2026, the CDC signed a policy to transition MenACWY from a routine recommendation to SCDM; however, this action was later invalidated following a federal court ruling [150–152]. Should it be confirmed, such a policy shift would warrant particular attention since the onus of protection would transition from public-health expertise to the individual, an approach that historically has been associated with health disparities in marginalized populations [151]. Moreover, this planned policy shift and its reversal could potentially create uncertainty, confuse the public and HCPs, and undermine confidence in vaccines by incorrectly implying a change in the underlying evidence [153]. Clear communication and education for both the public and HCPs are needed to counteract any perceived misinformation and limit any negative impact on vaccine uptake. Improved data on implementation effectiveness of SCDM, vaccine uptake and outcomes in high-risk and socially marginalized populations, and the comparative effectiveness of routine versus permissive recommendation frameworks would help inform future policy decisions [39, 42].

Advances in molecular diagnostics, particularly syndromic multiplex PCR panels, have the potential to improve IMD recognition where atypical presentations predominate, including those among older adults and patients who have received empiric antibiotics prior to culture collection [92, 154]. Broader deployment of these tools may enhance surveillance accuracy and inform more precise estimates of age-specific burden.

Long-term outcome studies spanning the full age spectrum, incorporating health-related quality of life, economic impact, and caregiver burden, are needed to provide a complete account of the societal cost of IMD. Such data are critical for health economic analyses that inform vaccination policy and for demonstrating that prevention strategies averting even small numbers of cases may yield substantial long-term benefit [13, 94, 99, 118]. International experiences with infant meningococcal vaccination programs and broader adult vaccination strategies provide informative comparators and may offer lessons for US policy development [49, 155]. Moreover, risk-based strategies alone may prove insufficient in the context of expanding use of immunomodulatory therapies and aging populations with increasing comorbidity, further supporting a move toward age-inclusive vaccination frameworks [3, 16].

Strengths and Limitations

This targeted literature review enabled collation of data on current IMD vaccination recommendations and their implementation in the USA from articles published since the 2015 introduction of the MenB SCDM framework. Certain limitations of these studies should be considered when interpreting the findings. This was a nonsystematic literature review, and as such was not bound by established methodological and reporting guidelines (e.g., Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses [156]). In addition, no risk-of-bias assessment was performed. While the resulting high risk of selection bias limits the robustness of the findings [157], which should be considered qualitative, the review has provided a broad and contemporary overview of IMD epidemiology and meningococcal vaccination practices and coverage in the USA. We have been able to identify significant gaps in vaccination profiles not only among key targets of vaccination recommendations in the USA, such as adolescents and young adults, but also across several vulnerable populations. These insights may help to inform evidence-based decision making and future IMD prevention policy across the lifespan in the USA, including for currently underserved groups.

Conclusions

IMD remains a rare but devastating disease, the full burden of which is underestimated when measured solely by incidence and acute mortality. Contemporary epidemiologic data reveal a dynamic landscape characterized by shifting serogroup dominance, outbreak patterns that extend beyond traditional settings, and increasing recognition of disease in adults and older populations. The highest CFRs occur in older adults, yet this group lacks routine age-based vaccination recommendations. Current US prevention strategies, largely anchored to historical pediatric and adolescent incidence peaks and narrowly defined risk groups, leave substantial coverage gaps across the lifespan.

The evidence reviewed herein supports reorientation toward a lifespan-based prevention framework that integrates age-related vulnerability, evolving transmission dynamics, and severity and outcome disparities. Adolescents remain a critical target for transmission control, but protection strategies should extend beyond college-centric models recognizing that all teenagers and young adults (16–23 years of age) are a peak risk age group for meningococcal disease. Adults and older adults represent an underrecognized prevention gap, with high CFRs, atypical clinical presentations, and no routine age-based vaccination recommendations despite measurable burden. The expanding use of immunomodulatory therapies, together with the growth of aging populations with a higher burden of comorbidities, further challenges the effectiveness of risk-based strategies alone. The availability of pentavalent vaccines, improved diagnostics, and accumulating data on the lifetime burden of sequelae create both the tools and the all-important rationale for a more coherent, inclusive, and evidence-informed approach to meningococcal disease prevention.
