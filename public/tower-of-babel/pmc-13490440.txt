Climatic transferability and regional validation of a PAT-based mechanistic model for apple scab ascospore maturation under temperate Himalayan conditions
Authors: Arif Bashir, Shakeel Ahmad Mir, Tariq Rasool, Bhagyashree Dhekale, Shashank Shekar, Shabir Ahmad Mir, Masood Saleem Mir, Shabir Ahmad Wani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe seasonal availability of airborne ascospores of Venturia inaequalis is a key determinant of primary apple scab infections, and mechanistic models describing ascospore maturation are widely used in disease forecasting systems. However, the applicability of such models under climatic conditions different from those for which they were originally developed remains insufficiently evaluated. This study presents a field evaluation of the climatic transferability of the Proportion of Ascospores capable of becoming Airborne (PAT) based ascospore maturation model proposed by Rossi et al. in 2000, under the temperate Himalayan conditions of Jammu and Kashmir, India. The original model structure and equations were retained and applied using hourly meteorological data (air temperature, rainfall, relative humidity and leaf wetness) collected over six consecutive seasons (2020–2025). Model outputs describing ascospore maturation and discharge dynamics were evaluated against independent field observations of airborne V. inaequalis ascospores obtained using a volumetric spore trap. Validation was conducted using event-based biological validation and statistical goodness-of-fit metrics. The model consistently reproduced the timing of ascospore availability, the accelerated maturation phase, and the exhaustion of the primary ascospore pool. Approximately 90% of observed ascospore release events occurred within model-predicted release-favourable windows. Statistical evaluation of maturation trajectories showed excellent agreement between predicted and observed dynamics (R2 ≈ 0.99; CCC = 0.96 -1.00). These results demonstrate that the PAT-based ascospore maturation model retains its predictive capability under temperate Himalayan conditions with little parameter calibration and is suitable for supporting regionally adapted apple scab disease warning systems.Keywords: Model development, Apple scab, Venturia inaequalis, Ascospore release, Spore maturation, Temperate Himalayas, EpidemiologySubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Microbiology

Introduction

Apple scab, caused by Venturia inaequalis (Cooke) Wint., is the most important disease of apple (Malus domestica Borkh.) in temperate regions worldwide1,2. Primary infections originate from ascospores released from pseudothecia developing in overwintered leaf litter during spring, and the seasonal availability of these ascospores largely determines epidemic development3,4. Ascospore maturation and discharge are strongly regulated by environmental conditions, particularly temperature, rainfall, and leaf wetness5–7. Early epidemiological studies led to the development of empirical infection criteria, most notably the Mills infection period tables8, which were later revised after experimental evidence showed that infection and ascospore discharge may occur under a wider range of conditions than originally assumed, especially at low temperatures9,10. To overcome the limitations of empirical approaches, mechanistic models were developed to explicitly describe ascospore maturation as a function of weather variables. In particular, Rossi et al., proposed a model based on the Proportion of Ascospores capable of becoming Airborne (PAT), describing the seasonal pattern of ascospore maturation using a logistic function of cumulative degree-day accumulation under wet conditions11. This modelling approach has been successfully applied to characterize airborne ascospore dynamics and was subsequently incorporated into the A-scab simulation framework for estimating primary infection risk12.

Ascospore maturation and discharge processes are, however, highly sensitive to local climatic conditions. As a result, models developed and parameterized under one environment may not be directly applicable under different climatic regimes without evaluation. Rossi et al.11,13, demonstrated that ascospore maturation models calibrated under North American conditions did not adequately reproduce airborne ascospore patterns in northern Italy without accounting for local environmental constraints. Similarly, experimental studies showed that low temperatures can significantly delay ascospore discharge and reduce airborne inoculum availability, influencing epidemic development beyond the assumptions of generalized infection criteria10. In India, apple cultivation is concentrated in the temperate Himalayan region, particularly in Jammu and Kashmir, where prolonged winter dormancy, frequent low-temperature wetting events, and variable spring precipitation prevail14–16. These agro-climatic conditions differ substantially from those under which the PAT-based ascospore maturation model was originally developed, and its applicability under temperate Himalayan conditions has not been previously evaluated.

Apple scab remains the most economically important disease affecting apple production in Jammu and Kashmir, where temperate climatic conditions characterized by prolonged spring wetness, intermittent rainfall events, and moderate temperatures are highly conducive to epidemic development. In commercial orchards, disease management largely depends on repeated fungicide applications during the primary infection period, often involving calendar-based spray schedules with limited consideration of actual infection risk. Such approaches increase production costs, environmental concerns, and the potential risk of fungicide resistance development. Therefore, the development and regional validation of biologically based forecasting systems capable of accurately identifying primary infection risk periods are essential for improving disease management efficiency and supporting sustainable apple production under temperate Himalayan conditions.

The aim of this study was therefore to evaluate the climatic transferability and regional adaptation of the mechanistic PAT-based ascospore maturation model proposed by Rossi et al.11, under temperate Himalayan conditions. Specifically, the study assessed whether the model could reproduce the seasonal dynamics of ascospore maturation and discharge of V. inaequalis when applied using locally recorded meteorological data, and whether it is suitable for supporting regionally adapted apple scab disease warning systems.

Materials and methods

Study site and data collection

The study was conducted in a commercial apple orchard located at Shalimar, Sher-e-Kashmir University of Agricultural Sciences and Technology of Kashmir (SKUAST-K), Jammu and Kashmir, India (34.1471° N, 74.8790° E), representative of temperate Himalayan apple-growing conditions. The region is characterized by cool winters, moderate spring temperatures, and frequent rainfall events during the apple growing season, conditions favorable for the development of apple scab epidemics. Compared with the temperate conditions of Northern Italy, where the original PAT model was developed, the study area exhibits broadly similar seasonal trends in temperature and moisture regimes, although with relatively prolonged winter chilling and variable spring rainfall distribution.

The experiments were conducted in a 10-year-old high-density apple orchard planted at a spacing of 1 × 3 m. The orchard consisted primarily of scab-susceptible cultivars, including Red Delicious strains, Gala, Fuji, and other locally cultivated cultivars. The orchard floor maintained natural grass cover throughout the study period. Standard orchard management practices were followed, including fungicide applications during the cropping season according to local recommendations.

Apple scab occurred naturally every year during the study period. No artificial inoculum was introduced, as abundant natural inoculum was available from infected overwintered leaf litter present within the orchard.. At the end of each growing season, fungicide applications were generally discontinued after harvest, resulting in substantial scab incidence on fallen leaves, often exceeding 50%, thereby providing sufficient overwintering inoculum for subsequent seasons. In addition to apple scab, Alternaria leaf blotch and powdery mildew were occasionally observed during the investigation period.

Meteorological observations and spore monitoring

Hourly meteorological data, including air temperature (°C), rainfall (mm), relative humidity (%), wind speed, wind direction, soil temperature, and leaf wetness, were recorded continuously using an automated weather station (Advance Tech, India) installed within the orchard during six consecutive seasons (2020–2025). Rainfall measurements were obtained using a RainWise rain gauge, wind speed and wind direction were monitored using DEVIS sensors, leaf wetness duration was recorded using a DEVIS electronic leaf wetness sensor, and soil temperature was monitored using OMRON sensors. Meteorological observations were recorded at hourly intervals using an automated data logging system.

To ensure consistency and reliability of observations, recorded weather data were routinely cross-validated with meteorological observations from the India Meteorological Department (IMD), Meteoblue weather services (subscription-based access), and the agrometeorological weather station installed at SKUAST-K. Particular attention was given to rainfall, relative humidity, and leaf wetness observations because of their epidemiological importance in apple scab development.

Airborne ascospores of Venturia inaequalis were monitored continuously using a Burkard Volumetric Spore Trap (Burkard Manufacturing Co., UK), a Hirst-type air sampler widely used in aerobiological studies. The trap operated at a constant airflow rate of 10 L min⁻1 and collected airborne spores on lubricated microscope slides using a 24-h continuous sampling system, enabling daily and hourly assessment of spore discharge dynamics. The trap was installed at the centre of the orchard on a fixed stand with the intake slit positioned approximately 2 m above ground level at the mid-canopy height of the apple trees. Microscope slides from the volumetric spore trap were replaced daily and examined under a compound microscope at appropriate magnification for ascospore identification and counting. Venturia inaequalis ascospores were identified based on their characteristic morphological features following standard aerobiological procedures.

Meteorological data collected during 2020–2025 were used for model fitting, whereas independent ascospore observation data from the 2024–2025 season were used for model validation.

Ascospore maturation model

Ascospore maturation was simulated using the mechanistic Proportion of Ascospores capable of becoming Airborne (PAT) model proposed by Rossi et al.11. PAT represents the cumulative fraction of the seasonal ascospore population potentially available for discharge on day i. Cumulative degree-days (CDD) were initiated from the green tip stage of apple development and calculated by summing hourly air temperatures above 0 °C. Degree-days (DD) were accumulated hourly whenever temperature exceeded 0 °C. Leaf wetness was not included in DD calculations and was treated as an independent variable/condition in the model. Ascospore discharge simulation

Ascospore discharge events were simulated as rainfall-driven processes conditional on ascospore maturity. Discharge was permitted only during rainfall events ≥ 0.2 mm h⁻112. Daytime rainfall resulted in immediate discharge, whereas nighttime rainfall resulted in delayed discharge at sunset, unless ascospore maturity exceeded PAT ≥ 0.80 or the proportion of ascospores released during the event exceeded 0.3011,12, Rossi et al. 2005. Results

Ascospore maturation dynamics

Ascospore maturation was quantified using the Proportion of Ascospores capable of becoming Airborne (PAT) and its confidence limits (PAT′ and PAT″) for six consecutive seasons (2020–25). In all years, the temporal progression of PAT consistently delineated four biologically distinct phases: no-risk, beginning of risk, accelerated maturation (maximum risk), and completion of maturation (Table 1 and Fig. 1).

During the no-risk phase (PAT′ ≤ 0.016), pseudothecia remained largely immature and biologically meaningful ascospore availability was absent. This phase was sustained until late February or early March for all seasons. The onset of risk (PAT′ ≥ 0.016) was initiated within a limited temporal window. Specifically, the onset of ascospore availability was observed on 05 March 2020 (CDD 147), 05 March 2021 (CDD 154), 08 March 2022 (CDD 149), 27 February 2023 (CDD 151), 27 February 2024 (CDD 167), and 24 February 2025 (CDD 154). At this stage, only 1–2% ((approximately) of the seasonal ascospore pool had matured.

The accelerated phase of maturation was considered the main risk phase with notable ascospore production and availability. This phase was from the time PAT values rose above approximately 0.20 until values neared unity. This period was from late March to mid-April for all seasons. In 2020, this phase extended from 07 April (CDD 423) to 22 April (CDD 620); in 2021 from 03 April (CDD 428) to 21 April (CDD 636); in 2022 from 30 March (CDD 426) to 11 April (CDD 636); in 2023 from 28 March (CDD 420) to 13 April (CDD 634); in 2024 from 12 March (CDD 400) to 15 April (CDD 635); and in 2025 from 31 March (CDD 431) to 14 April (CDD 631). During this interval, approximately 50–94% of the seasonal ascospore population matured.

Completion of maturation (PAT ≥ 0.99) occurred between late April and mid-May. Specifically, maturation was completed on 11 May 2020 (CDD 948), 07 May 2021 (CDD 905), 30 April 2022 (CDD 968), 06 May 2023 (CDD 964), 14 May 2024 (CDD 1005), and 03 May 2025 (CDD 963), indicating exhaustion of the primary ascospore reservoir.

Ascospore discharge events

The discharge events were related to rainfall occurrence when there was sufficient maturity of ascospores, defined as PAT′ ≥ 0.016. Overall, across all seasons, the first discharge events occurred shortly after the beginning of the beginning of risk period, around mid-March (Fig. 1). The discharge events observed during early season had low values of PAT, around 0.02–0.05, and low proportions of the seasonal ascospore pool released, SRAdis around 0.02–0.06. A significant increase in discharge frequency and intensity was consistently observed during the accelerated maturation phase. The highest discharge events occurred when values of PAT varied between around 0.50 and 0.80, when a majority of pseudothecia contained mature asci. The first discharge was observed on 21 March 2020 (Fig. 1F), when CDD was 285 and PAT was approximately 0.04, while the highest discharge events occurred on 14–15 April, when CDD was between 520 and 545, and PAT varied between 0.65 and 0.72, releasing approximately 31% of the seasonal ascospore pool each time. In 2021 (Fig. 1E), the initial discharge was observed on 24 March (CDD ≈ 300, PAT ≈ 0.05). The maximum discharge was observed on 12 April (CDD ≈ 560, PAT ≈ 0.70). The discharge accounted for 35% of the ascospores in the season. The discharge patterns were consistent in subsequent years. In 2022 (Fig. 1D), the maximum discharge was observed on 09 April (CDD ≈ 545, PAT ≈ 0.68). The discharge accounted for 30% of the ascospores in the season. In 2023 (Fig. 1C), the maximum discharge was observed on 08 April (CDD ≈ 540, PAT ≈ 0.66). The discharge accounted for 33% of the ascospores in the season. In 2024 (Fig. 1B), the maximum discharge was observed on 10 April (CDD ≈ 530, PAT ≈ 0.62). The discharge accounted for 28% of the ascospores in the season. In 2025 (Fig. 1A), the maximum discharge was observed on 11 April (CDD ≈ 560, PAT ≈ 0.70). The discharge accounted for 32% of the ascospores in the season. The final discharge events were observed when PAT was close to 0.94–0.97. At this time, there was no ascospore release. The discharge events were consistent in all seasons.

Statistical characteristics of the maturation curves

The logistic models of the curves of PAT, PAT′, and PAT″ showed significant agreement with the actual maturation curves. In all cases, the coefficients of determination (R2) were 0.99 or higher, and the root-mean-squared errors (RMSE) were all 0.91 or lower. The concordance correlation coefficients ranged from 0.96 to 1.00, indicating a very high level of agreement between the predicted maturation curves and the actual ascospore dynamics.

Validation

Model validation was conducted to assess whether the observed seasonal pattern of airborne Venturia inaequalis ascospores could be reproduced by the PAT-based ascospore maturation model when applied under temperate Himalayan conditions. Validation was performed using independent field observations obtained from a continuously operating volumetric spore trap installed in the orchard. Validation was carried out at two complementary levels:

Biological (event-based) validation

Biological validation was performed by comparing model-simulated ascospore maturation dynamics (PAT, PAT′, and PAT″) with observed ascospore release (Table 2, Fig. 2 and 3) events across multiple seasons. The model consistently predicted the onset of ascospore availability (PAT′ ≥ 0.016) during late February to early March, which closely corresponded with the timing of the first observed ascospore release events detected by the spore trap. For example, in 2020 the model predicted the beginning of ascospore availability on 05 March (CDD ≈ 147), while the first observed ascospore release was recorded on 21 March, when PAT had increased to approximately 0.02 and rainfall conditions were favourable for discharge. In 2021, the start of maturation was predicted on 05 March (CDD ≈ 154), and the first discharge event was recorded on 16 March at PAT ≈ 0.02. The most significant correlation between model predictions and actual discharge was recorded when the accelerated maturation (maximum-risk) phase was active, characterized by a PAT value higher than 0.5. In 2020, the model predicted the main maturation period from 07 to 22 April (CDD 423–620), matching the highest discharge events of ascospores, especially on 14–15 April when the PAT was between 0.58 and 0.62 and the highest spore trap counts were recorded. This was also the case in 2021 when the maximum-risk period predicted by the model (03–21 April; PAT ≈ 0.55–0.81) matched the highest actual airborne concentrations of ascospores recorded from 11 to 18 April. Similar trends were recorded in 2022–2025 when the highest discharge events were recorded when the model predicted rapid increases in PAT and discharge potential was high. Throughout the seasons, on average, about 90% of observed ascospore release events were contained within model-predicted release-favourable windows, i.e., when both ascospore maturity (PAT ≥ threshold values) and rainfall were present. However, once PAT exceeded ≥ 0.99, the model indicated that the primary ascospore population had been depleted, and observed spore trap counts were low despite the occurrence of additional rainfall events. For example, in 2020, no additional significant ascospore release events were observed after 11 May when PAT was approximately equal to 1.00; similar terminations of release activity were also observed after early May in the subsequent seasons.

Statistical validation

Statistical validation was conducted in order to quantify the agreement between the model-simulated trajectories of ascospore maturation curves and the observed ascospore dynamics. The coefficients of determination (R2) for the logistic models developed for PAT, PAT′, and PAT″ were found to be ≥ 0.99, the values of the root-mean-square error (RMSE) were < 0.01, while the concordance correlation coefficients ranged between 0.96 and 1.00. Although these statistical values are a measure of the internal consistency of the maturation component of the model, they are considered here in conjunction with the results of the biological validation. The close correspondence between the predicted maturation phases and observed ascospore release events provides a second validation that the model is reproducing adequately the timing and seasonal progression of primary inoculum availability under temperate Himalayan conditions.

Discussion

The PAT model was able to identify all four biologically relevant phases in ascospore maturation in all six seasons, with R2 values ≥ 0.99, RMSE < 0.91, and concordance correlation coefficients between 0.96 and 1.00, thereby indicating excellent agreement between simulated and observed ascospore dynamics. These performance criteria are consistent with those presented in Rossi et al.12 in validation studies of the A-scab framework in different European environmental conditions. These results therefore demonstrate that the mechanistic structure of the PAT model is sufficiently robust to accommodate the agro-climatic conditions of the temperate Himalayas without requiring structural modification. Despite differences in winter chilling duration and spring rainfall distribution between the temperate Himalayas and Northern Italy, the model retained strong predictive performance, further supporting the climatic transferability of the PAT framework.

The period of ascospore availability, defined by when PAT′ ≥ 0.016, consistently occurred between 24 February and 08 March across all seasons, equivalent to a cumulative degree-day range of 147 to 167, suggesting that the thermal threshold for initial pseudothecial maturation is relatively stable across years. Similar temporal stability in pseudothecial maturation was also reported for central Poland, where first ascospore release dates varied between 12 March and 19 April across ten seasons, while inter-annual variability was attributed to variations in moisture availability and temperature accumulation17. The period of rapid maturation, when 50 to 94% of the seasonal ascospore population matured, consistently occurred between late March and mid-April, equivalent to a cumulative degree-day range of 400 to 431, representing the main period of primary inoculum availability. The peak airborne concentrations of ascospores, as demonstrated by Rossi et al., in 2003, are related to rainfall events during periods of rapid pseudothecial maturation, as observed in this study18,19. Completion of maturation (PAT ≥ 0.99) occurred between late April and mid-May (CDD 905-1005), earlier than the late May to mid-June termination reported for the cooler continental climate of central Poland17, reflecting the accelerated thermal time accumulation characteristic of the Himalayan foothills.

Ascospore discharge events were consistently associated with rainfall coinciding with sufficient ascospore maturity, with the first detectable releases occurring in mid-March across all seasons. The model incorporated the biologically established constraint of delayed discharge for nighttime rainfall events20, and the minimum rainfall threshold of 0.2 mm h⁻1 used to trigger discharge is consistent with published values20, Jankowski & Masny, 2019). Early-season events were characterized by low PAT values (0.02–0.05) and small proportional releases, while the largest individual discharge events (28–35% of the seasonal pool) occurred when PAT ranged between 0.50 and 0.80, consistent with the mechanistic expectation that discharge magnitude reflects the increment in PAT since the preceding event Rossi et al.12. The cessation of discharge activity at PAT values between 0.94 and 0.97, despite subsequent rainfall, reinforced biological exhaustion of the primary ascospore reservoir in all seasons, thereby validating the termination criteria incorporated in the PAT model Rossi et al.12 as well as the pragmatic advice to cease fungicide control for primary infections when PAT values approach unity.

The moisture-conditioning degree day accumulation method used in the PAT model is supported by the well-established relationship between pseudothecial maturation and the free moisture availability6,12,17. Jankowski and Masny found positive correlations between the rate of ascospore maturation, precipitation, and leaf wetness, reinforcing the fact that pseudothecial maturation is driven mainly by precipitation17. It is interesting to note that the authors found that all the tested pseudothecial maturation models had very high and unsatisfactory ranges of uncertainty when applied to the Polish data, suggesting that the limitation of the tested models was the failure of the models to properly account for the local moisture conditions. In contrast, the PAT model performed satisfactorily under temperate Himalayan conditions when applied to the Himalayan data, possibly because of the generally high spring precipitation of the Himalayan region, which ensures that moisture conditions are never limiting during the main risk period. Under such conditions, the thermal component of the model is possibly sufficient to account for the main pseudothecial maturation process without the need to incorporate any additional moisture-weighting parameter12.

Around 90% of the observed ascospore release events were found to have occurred within the model-predicted release-favourable windows, with a sharp decline in the spore trap counts after the model-predicted exhaustion of the pool, indicating good biological validation of the PAT model. This is supported by the transferability of the model, as indicated by Rossi et al., who found that the outputs of the A-scab model correlated well with the daily spore count and disease onset without any need for correction or calibration, using multiple locations of apple-growing regions12. Any differences between the model predictions and the observations could be due to the spatial variation of leaf litter distribution or wind-borne dispersal from the adjacent orchards, not taken into account in the PAT model. Minor discrepancies between predicted and observed early-season discharge events may also be associated with very low initial ascospore concentrations remaining below the practical detection threshold of the volumetric spore trap during the earliest stages of seasonal ascospore maturation. The integration of the deposition model of Rossi et al. with consideration of the meteorological conditions and the horticultural characteristics of the orchard could be beneficial for the estimation of the primary infection risk, considering the Himalayan conditions19. Although disease symptom onset was not systematically monitored in the present study, the primary objective was to evaluate ascospore maturation and discharge dynamics under temperate Himalayan conditions. Future studies integrating symptom development and infection severity assessments may further strengthen epidemiological validation of the model.

The successful validation of the PAT model under temperate Himalayan conditions demonstrates its practical utility for apple scab forecasting in Jammu and Kashmir, where apple scab remains the major disease constraint to apple production. The model consistently identified the beginning of risk (late February to early March), the maximum-risk period (late March to mid-April), and the termination of primary infection risk (late April to mid-May), thereby providing a biologically meaningful framework for optimizing fungicide application timing. Integration of the PAT model within region-specific disease warning systems, such as the A-scab framework proposed by Rossi et al.12, could contribute to reducing unnecessary fungicide applications, improving disease management efficiency, lowering production costs, and supporting integrated pest management strategies under temperate Himalayan conditions.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that the PAT-based mechanistic model for ascospore maturation of Venturia inaequalis is climatically transferable to temperate Himalayan conditions and can successfully identify biologically meaningful periods of primary apple scab infection risk under the agro-climatic conditions of Jammu and Kashmir. The model consistently reproduced the seasonal dynamics of ascospore maturation and discharge, including the onset, peak, and termination of primary inoculum availability, with strong agreement between predicted and observed ascospore release events.

The results further indicate that the major period of primary infection risk occurs during spring, when rapid ascospore maturation and discharge coincide with favourable environmental conditions. The successful validation of the model under temperate Himalayan conditions supports its applicability as a biologically based forecasting tool for apple scab management.

Integration of the PAT framework into region-specific disease warning systems could improve fungicide application timing, reduce unnecessary spray applications, lower production costs, and support sustainable integrated disease management strategies for apple production in the Himalayan region.
