On the arithmetic of power monoids
Laura Cossu, Salvatore Tringali

Abstract
. Given a monoid H H (written multiplicatively), the family 𝒫 fin , 1 ​ ( H ) \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H) of all non-empty finite subsets of H H containing the identity element 1 H 1_{H} is itself a monoid, called the reduced finitary power monoid of H H , under the operation of setwise multiplication induced by H H . We investigate the arithmetic of 𝒫 fin , 1 ​ ( H ) \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H) from the perspective of minimal factorizations into irreducibles, paying particular attention to the potential presence of non-trivial idempotents. Among other results, we provide necessary and sufficient conditions on H H for 𝒫 fin , 1 ​ ( H ) \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H) to admit unique minimal factorizations. Our results generalize and shed new light on recent developments on the topic.

1.
Introduction

Roughly speaking, factorization theory is the study of a broad spectrum of phenomena arising from the possibility or impossibility of extending the fundamental theorem of arithmetic from the integers to more abstract settings where a set comes endowed with a (binary) operation.

The theory has traditionally focused on domains and cancellative monoids, primarily in the commutative setting
[
15
,
2
]
. It is only in the past few years that researchers have made first significant steps towards a systematic study of factorizations in non-cancellative (and non-commutative) settings; see
[
4
, Section 2.4]
for a review of some older “scattered results” along the same lines. The new trend has partly originated from a renewed interest in
power monoids

[
13
,
4
]
, a class of “highly non-cancellative” monoids intensively studied by semigroup theorists and computer scientists during the 1980s and 1990s, and featuring a dense interplay between algebra and combinatorics (see
[
14
, Section 1]
,
[
20
, Section 1]
, and references therein for a more extensive historical overview).

Power monoids exhibit a rich arithmetic, making them an ideal testing ground for the ongoing development of a “unifying theory of factorization”
[
21
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
8
]
that extends far beyond the boundaries of the classical theory. Moreover, they provide an algebraic framework for a variety of interesting problems in additive combinatorics and closely related areas
[
22
,
5
,
6
,
23
,
14
]
, notably including Sárközy’s conjecture
[
19
, Conjecture 1.6]
on the “additive irreducibility” of the set of [non-zero] squares in a finite field of prime order.
Further recent contributions to the topic are due to Gotti and his students
[
16
,
1
,
12
]
, who have investigated the arithmetic of power monoids from the perspective of algebraic combinatorics; and to Reinhart
[
18
]
, who has taken the first significant step in years towards the proof of a challenging conjecture of Fan and Tringali
[
13
, Sect. 5]
.

In more detail, let
H
be a (multiplicatively written) monoid, often referred to in this context as the
ground monoid
. Equipped with the operation of setwise multiplication

(X,Y)\mapsto XY:=\{xy\colon x\in X,\kern 1.00006pty\in Y\},

the family of all non-empty finite subsets of
H
containing the identity
1_{H}
forms a monoid in its own turn, herein denoted by
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
and called the
reduced
(
finitary
)
power monoid
of
H
.
It is a natural problem to understand if, and how, a set
X\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
can be decomposed into a product of certain other sets regarded as the “building blocks” of the decomposition process.

In the classical theory, the building blocks are typically the atoms of the monoid under consideration, an atom being a non-unit that does not factor as a product of two non-units. However, atoms become less appealing in non-cancellative contexts, where the existence itself of atomic decompositions is not guaranteed even in monoids with “smooth properties”.
Inspired by Anderson and Valdes-Leon’s work
[
3
]
in the commutative setting, this has led Tringali
[
21
]
to the idea of refining the notion of atom with the weaker notion of
irreducible
(Section
2.1
). It is worth stressing from the outset that, while the terms “atom” and “irreducible” are used
interchangeably in the classical theory, they assume distinct meanings in our framework: the two concepts coincide, say, in a cancellative commutative monoid (see Remark
2.1
(1)
), but the latter is significantly more versatile than the former and better suited for a more general version of the theory (see, for instance,
[
21
, Theorem 3.10]
,
[
9
, Theorem 5.19]
, and
[
10
, Theorems 4.7 and 5.1]
).

In fact, there is more to the story, as another problem is inherent to the
non-cancellative setting and also arises in the non-commutative (cancellative)
scenario: in many situations (e.g., in the presence of idempotents other than the identity), factorizations blow up in a predictable fashion, causing most of the invariants studied in the classical theory to lose their significance.
This prompted Antoniou and Tringali
[
4
, Section 4]
, followed by Cossu and Tringali
[
10
]
, to introduce and develop the notion of
minimal factorization
(Section
2.2
) as a natural refinement of classical factorizations capable of countering the “blow-up phenomenon”.

In the present paper, we add to this line of research. The goal is to study minimal factorizations into irreducibles in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
.
In Section
2
, after recalling basic ideas and definitions, we introduce the notion of (minimal) factorization in an arbitrary monoid
M
, describing its building blocks and related properties. Next, we specialize these general concepts to the case of reduced power monoids. In particular, Proposition
2.3
characterizes the irreducibles of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
that are not atoms. In Propositions
2.7
and
2.8
, we gather a series of finiteness results on (minimal) factorizations that hold for arbitrary reduced power monoids. In Section
3
, we investigate conditions on
H
under which
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is UmF, i.e., it admits “unique” minimal factorizations. The characterization provided by the main Theorem
3.6
implies that the UmF-ness of reduced power monoids can be reduced to the UmF-ness of reduced power monoids over a particular class of monoids we refer to as “almost-breakable” (Definition
3.1
). Section
4
deals with reduced power monoids of almost-breakable monoids. Most notably, we obtain a complete characterization of UmF-ness when
H
contains at least one non-idempotent non-unit (Corollary
3.7
) or is commutative (Theorem
4.8
).
Our results generalize those in
[
4
]
(which only deal with factorizations into atoms) and make it possible to avoid imposing unnatural conditions to guarantee the existence of factorizations.

2.
Irreducibles and factorization

Through this section, we introduce the notions of factorization we are interested in and specialize them to the class of monoids we aim to study.
We address the reader to Clifford and Preston’s classical monograph
[
7
]
for generalities on semigroups and monoids.

Notation and terminology, if not explained upon first use, are standard or should be clear from the context. In particular, we denote by

the non-negative integers and, given

, we refer to

from

. Also, as a rule of thumb, we use the letter

for an arbitrary monoid, and

2.1.
Irreducibles, atoms, and quarks

Let
M
be a (multiplicatively written) monoid; we use
1_{M}
for the
identity
of
M
. We call an element

a

if
xy=yx=1_{M}
for some

; otherwise,

is a

. We denote by

the set of units of

. It is a basic fact that

is a subgroup of
M
. We call
M

reduced
if
M^{\times}=\{1_{M}\}
, and
Dedekind-finite
if
xy=1_{M}
for some
x,y\in M
implies

.

We denote by
\mid_{M}
the binary relation on

(read as “

in
M
” or “

is a divisor of

in
M

. It is straightforward that
\mid_{M}
is reflexive and transitive, hence a (partial) preorder. Accordingly, we refer to
\mid_{M}
as the
divisibility preorder
on
M
. Two elements
x,y\in M
are
associated
(in
M
), written
x\simeq_{M}y
, if they divide each other. Moreover,

is a

of
y
(or, equivalently,
x
properly divides

) if
x
divides but is not associated to

(i.e.,

and
x\notin MyM
), and is a

(resp., a

) if

. Later, we will simply write
\mid
instead of
\mid_{M}
and
\simeq

\simeq_{M}
whenever the “ambient monoid”

is clear from the context.

Following
[
9
, Section 3]
, we let an
irreducible
(of
M
) be a non-unit-divisor

such that
a\neq xy
for all non-unit-divisors

that properly divide

. An

is, on the other hand, a non-unit-divisor that does not factor as a product of two non-unit-divisors, while a
quark
is a non-unit-divisor which is not properly divided by any non-unit-divisor.

Remarks 2.1
.

(1)

In a monoid
M
, every atom is an irreducible, and so is every quark. The converse, however, is false in general (see
[
21
, Example 4.8]
and Proposition
2.3
below); but it holds, for instance, when
M
is
acyclic

[
21
, Definition 4.2 and Corollary 4.4]
, meaning that
x\neq\allowbreak uxv
for all
u,v,x\in M
such that
u
or
v
is a non-unit (e.g., a cancellative commutative monoid is acyclic). Note, in addition, that if
M
is Dedekind-finite, then the unit-divisors of
M
coincide with its units. In this case, an atom of
M
is precisely a non-unit that cannot be expressed as a product of two non-units, in line with the definition given in the introduction. Moreover, every acyclic monoid is Dedekind-finite
[
21
, Corollary 4.4]
.

(2)

The notion of
irreducible
used in the present work is actually a special case of a much more abstract concept (see
[
21
, Definition 3.6]
and
[
9
, Definition 3.1]
), whose definition is based on the idea of equipping a monoid

with a preorder
\preceq
(in our case,
\preceq
is the divisibility preorder
\mid_{M}
). From this perspective, the identity
1_{M}
plays a particularly distinguished role, as one first defines a
\preceq
-unit
as
an element
u\in M
such that
1_{M}\preceq u\preceq 1_{M}
, and a
\preceq
-non-unit as an element
u\in M
that is not a
\preceq
-unit. Accordingly, a
\preceq
-irreducible

is a
\preceq
-non-unit
a\in M
such that
a\neq xy
for all
\preceq
-non-units

x,y\in M
with
x\prec a
and
y\prec a
, where
u\prec v
means
u\preceq v\not\preceq u
. Likewise, a
\preceq
-atom
is a
\preceq
-non-unit
a\in M

such that
a\neq xy
for all
\preceq
-non-units
x,y\in M
; and a
\preceq
-quark
is a
\preceq
-non-unit

such that if

then
b
is a
\preceq
-unit.

We aim to characterize the irreducibles, atoms, and quarks of reduced power monoids. To this end, our first lemma collects some basic properties that will come in handy later.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
H
be a monoid and let
X,Y,Z\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
. The following hold:

(i)

If
X
is a divisor of
Y
in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
, then
X\subseteq Y
.

(ii)

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is a reduced, Dedekind-finite monoid.

(iii)

X
and
Y
are associated in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
if and only if
X=Y
.

(iv)

X
is irreducible in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
if and only if
X\neq\{1_{H}\}
and
X\neq YZ
for all
Y,Z\subsetneq X
.

(v)

X
is irreducible in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
if and only if it is a quark.

(vi)

If
X
is irreducible in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
but not an atom, then
X^{2}=X
.

Proof.

As for
(i)
, it is enough to note that, if
X
is a divisor of
Y
in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
, then
Y=UXV
for some
U,V\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
and hence
X=\{1_{H}\}X\{1_{H}\}\subseteq UXV=Y
.
(ii)
is now immediate, because
XY=\{1_{H}\}
implies by
(i)
that
X\cup Y\subseteq\{1_{H}\}
and hence
X=Y=\{1_{H}\}
.
On the other hand,
(iv)
is straightforward from
(i)
–
(iii)
, and
(v)
follows from
[
21
, Proposition 4.11(iii)]
. So, we focus on
(iii)
and
(vi)
.

(iii)
We prove the equivalent statement:
X
is a proper divisor of
Y
if and only if
X\mid Y
and
X\subsetneq Y
. Assume that
X
is a proper divisor of
Y
in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
. If
X=Y
, then
Y=\{1_{H}\}X
, and consequently
Y\mid X
, a contradiction. Thus,
X\neq Y
. Now assume that
X\mid Y
and
X\neq Y
. By item
(i)
,
X\subseteq Y
and
Y\nmid X
, otherwise
Y\subseteq X
, yielding
X=Y
.

(vi)
Assume that
X
is irreducible, but it is not an atom. Then, by item
(ii)
,
X=YZ
for some non-identity elements
Y,Z\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
. This implies that
Y\mid X
and
Z\mid X
, but we know from
(v)
that
X
is a quark, and this forces
Y=Z=X
.
∎

In supplement to items
(v)
and
(vi)
of Lemma
2.2
, we continue with a characterization of the irreducibles of a reduced power monoid that are not atoms.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
H
be a monoid. A set
X\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is irreducible but not an atom if and only if
X=\{1_{H},x\}
for some
x\in H
such that
x^{2}=1_{H}
or
x^{2}=x
.

Proof.

To begin, assume
X
is irreducible but not an atom. By items
(ii)
and
(vi)
of Lemma
2.2
, this yields
X^{2}=X\neq\{1_{H}\}
. Accordingly,
X=\{1_{H},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
for some
x_{1},\allowbreak\ldots,\allowbreak x_{n}\in\allowbreak H\smallsetminus\{1_{H}\}
. Then
X\subseteq\allowbreak X\{1_{H},x_{1}\}\subseteq X^{2}=X
and hence
\{1_{H},x_{1}\}\mid X\{1_{H},x_{1}\}=X
. Since
X
is a quark by Lemma
2.2
(v)
, it follows that
X=\{1_{H},x_{1}\}
and
\{1_{H},x_{1}\}^{2}=\{1_{H},x_{1}\}
, which is only possible if
x_{1}^{2}=x_{1}
or
x_{1}=1_{H}
.

Conversely, suppose that
X=\{1_{H},x\}
for some
x\in H
such that
x^{2}=x
or
x^{2}=1_{H}
. It is then clear that
X
is an idempotent. Thus we are done, because every
2
-element set in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is irreducible (e.g., by item
(iv)
of Lemma
2.2
) and it is obvious that, in any monoid, an atom is not an idempotent.
∎

As one might expect, a comprehensive characterization of the irreducibles of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is going to critically depend on specific properties of the monoids
H
. Nevertheless, there are “constructive results” that hold in full generality, as with the following.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
H
be a monoid and
A\subseteq H\smallsetminus\{1_{H}\}
be a non-empty finite
\mid_{H}
-antichain
, meaning that
a\nmid_{H}b
for all
a,b\in A
with
a\neq b
. The set
\{1_{H}\}\cup\allowbreak A
is then irreducible in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
.

Proof.

The set
A^{\prime}:=\{1_{H}\}\cup A
is a non-unit of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
, because
A
is a non-empty finite subset of
H\smallsetminus\{1_{H}\}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is reduced by Lemma
2.2
(ii)
. Suppose for a contradiction that
A^{\prime}
is not irreducible. There then exist
proper
subsets
X
and
Y
of
A^{\prime}
, both containing the identity
1_{H}
, such that
A^{\prime}=\allowbreak XY
, and this can only happen if
|A|\geq 2
. Consequently, we can find
a,b\in A
with
a\notin X
and
b\notin Y
; moreover, each element in
A
is a non-unit-divisor, or else
A
would not be a
\mid_{H}
-antichain (which is absurd) since a unit-divisor divides any other element in
H
(and
A
has at least two elements). It follows that
b\in X
and hence
a\neq b
(if
b\notin X
, then
b\in XY
and
b\notin Y
would yield

for certain
c,d\in A\smallsetminus\{1_{H},b\}
, contradicting that
A
is a
\mid_{H}
-antichain). In a similar way,
a\in Y
. Putting the pieces together, we can thus conclude that
1_{H}\neq ba\in A
and hence
ba=c
for some
c\in A
, which is impossible as it implies that either
a\mid_{H}c
and
a\neq c
, or
b\mid_{H}c
and
b\neq c
(again contradicting that
A
is a
\mid_{H}
-antichain).
∎

2.2.
Factorizations and minimal factorizations

We write
\mathscr{F}(X)
for the free monoid over a set
X
. We refer to the elements of
\mathscr{F}(X)
as
X
-words
and to its identity as the
empty word
.

Given a monoid
M
and an element
x\in M
, we denote by
\mathscr{I}(M)
the set of irreducibles of
M
and define a
factorization
(
into irreducibles
) of
x
as an
\mathscr{I}(M)
-word
\mathfrak{a}
such that
\pi_{M}(\mathfrak{a})=x
. Here,
\pi_{M}
is the
factorization homomorphism
of
M
, namely, the unique extension of the identity map on
M
to a monoid homomorphism
\mathscr{F}(M)\to M
. Following
[
10
, Section 3]
, we define
\sqsubseteq_{M}
as the binary relation on
\mathscr{F}(M)
whose graph consists of all pairs
(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b})
of
M
-words such that
\mathfrak{a}
is, up to associatedness of letters, a subword of some permutation of
\mathfrak{b}
(it is readily checked that
\sqsubseteq_{M}
is a preorder). Two
M
-words
\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathfrak{b}
are
equivalent
if
\mathfrak{a}\sqsubseteq_{M}\mathfrak{b}\sqsubseteq_{M}\mathfrak{a}
; they are
inequivalent
otherwise. Also, a factorization (into irreducibles) of
x
is a
minimal factorization
if there is no factorization
\mathfrak{b}
of
x
with
\mathfrak{b}\sqsubseteq_{M}\mathfrak{a}\not\sqsubseteq_{M}\mathfrak{b}
.

We say that
M
is
factorable
if every non-unit-divisor of
M
factors as a product of irreducibles;
BF
(or a
bounded factorization monoid
) if
M
is factorable and the set of all factorization lengths of any fixed element is bounded (the length of a factorization
\mathfrak{a}
being the length of
\mathfrak{a}
as a word in the free monoid over
M
);
HF
(or a
half-factorial monoid
) if
M
is factorable and the factorizations of any element have all the same length;
FF
(or a
finite factorization monoid
) if it is factorable and each element has finitely many inequivalent factorizations; and
UF
(or a
unique factorization monoid
) if
M
is factorable and any two irreducible factorizations of an element are equivalent.

Replacing factorizations with minimal factorizations in the definition of BF, HF, FF, and UF monoid results in the notions of
BmF
,
HmF
,
FmF
, and
UmF monoid
, resp. Note that, if
M
is a factorable monoid, then every non-unit-divisor
x\in M
has at least one minimal factorization, i.e., there exists a factorization
\mathfrak{a}
of
x
with the property that
\mathfrak{b}\not\sqsubseteq_{M}\mathfrak{a}
for any
\mathscr{I}(M)
-word
\mathfrak{b}\in\pi_{M}^{-1}(x)
.

If we shift our perspective to regard atoms (rather than irreducibles) as the “building blocks” of the decompositions of interest, we can modify the above definitions accordingly. This results in the notions of
atomic factorization
,
atomic
monoid,
BF-atomic
monoid, and so on.

Remark 2.5
.

In an arbitrary monoid
M
, the only factorization (into irreducibles) of the identity
1_{M}
is the empty word. In fact, if
1_{M}=x_{1}\cdots x_{n}
for some
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in M
(with
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
), then each of the
x_{i}
’s is a unit-divisor and hence cannot be an irreducible of
M
.

Suppose, on the other hand, that
M
has the property that every irreducible is a quark. If
a\in\mathscr{I}(M)
and
a=x_{1}\cdots x_{n}
for some non-unit-divisors
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in M
, then
x_{i}\mid_{M}a
for each
i\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
. Since
a
is a quark, it follows that
a
and each of
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
are associated. This implies
a\sqsubseteq_{M}x_{1}\ast\cdots\ast x_{n}
, and the inequality is strict unless
n=1
. So, the only factorization of
a
(into irreducibles) is the
\mathscr{I}(M)
-word
a
.

In the remainder of the section, we will focus our attention on (minimal) factorizations in reduced power monoids. Before proceeding, another remark is in order.

Remark 2.6
.

Let
H
be an arbitrary monoid.
By Lemma
2.2
(iii)
, two
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
-words are equivalent if and only if they are a permutation of each other. Thus, a non-empty minimal factorization of a set
X\in\allowbreak\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is nothing else than a non-empty word
A_{1}\ast\cdots\ast A_{n}
in the free monoid over
\mathscr{I}(\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H))
such that
X\neq\allowbreak A_{\sigma(1)}\cdots\allowbreak A_{\sigma(k)}
for all
k\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket
and every permutation
\sigma
of
\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
. In addition, it is not difficult to see that a minimal factorization of
X
must have length
\leq|X|-1
. The proof mirrors the one of
[
4
, Proposition 4.12]
, after noticing that, by Remark
2.5
and Lemma
2.2
(v)
, the only minimal factorization of an irreducible
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is the
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
-word
A
(we omit further details).

As mentioned in Section
1
, minimal factorizations were first introduced by Antoniou and Tringali
[
4
, Section 4.1]
, who, however, used atoms (rather than irreducibles) as their building blocks. In particular, this led them to prove in
[
4
, Theorem 3.9]
that the atomicity of the reduced power monoid
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
of a monoid
H
is equivalent to the condition that
1_{H}\neq x^{2}\neq x
for all non-identity elements
x\in H
. The next results are complementary to Antoniou and Tringali’s theorem and help complete the picture.

Proposition 2.7
.

The following are equivalent for a monoid
H
:

(a)

H
is aperiodic (i.e., every non-identity element of
H
generates an infinite submonoid).

(b)

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is BF-atomic.

(c)

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is BF.

(d)

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is FF.

(e)

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is FF-atomic.

Proof.

For the equivalence
(a)

\Leftrightarrow

(b)
, see
[
4
, Theorem 3.11(ii)]
, where the term “torsion-free” is used in place of “aperiodic” (but with the same meaning). On the other hand, if
H
is aperiodic, then
1_{H}\neq x^{2}\neq\allowbreak x
for every
x\in H\smallsetminus\{1_{H}\}
, and this ensures by Proposition
2.3
that every irreducible of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is an atom. It is thus clear that
(a)

\Leftrightarrow

(b)

\Rightarrow

(c)
. Also, it is obvious that any FF-atomic monoid is BF-atomic (cf. Remark 3.3(2) in
[
10
]
), with the result that
(e)

\Rightarrow

(b)
.
So, it remains to show that
(c)

\Rightarrow

(d)

\Rightarrow

(e)
.

(c)

\Rightarrow

(d)
. Let
X\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
and suppose that there exists an integer
k\geq 0
such that the length of any factorization of
X
(into irreducibles) is bounded above by
k
. We need to show that the number
n_{X}
of factorizations of
X
in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is finite. Indeed, since any divisor of
X
in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is contained in
X
and any term in a factorization of
X
is a divisor of the same set,
n_{X}
is bounded above by
|X|^{k}
and hence finite (recall from Remark
2.5
that the only factorization of the identity is the empty word).

(d)

\Rightarrow

(e)
. By Lemma
2.2
(ii)
,
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is Dedekind-finite. Since any FF monoid is BF (cf., again, Remark 3.3(2) in
[
10
]
), it follows by
[
10
, Example 5.4(6)]
that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is acyclic. So, every irreducible of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is an atom (Remark
2.1
(1)
) and hence
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is BF-atomic (by the fact that it is BF).
∎

The limitations inherent in Proposition
2.7
highlight once again that atomic factorizations and, more generally, “unrestricted factorizations” into irreducibles are not the best choice possible when it comes to monoids that are not “nearly cancellative”. The next proposition shows how, at least in the case of reduced power monoids, the paradigm of minimal factorizations allows us to overcome these limitations.

Proposition 2.8
.

The following hold for a monoid
H
:

(i)

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is FmF.

(ii)

BmF-ness and FmF-ness are equivalent properties for
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
.

Proof.

This is straightforward from
[
10
, Theorem 4.11]
(see also
[
10
, Remark 5.5]
).
∎

Note that, by
[
4
, Theorem 4.13(a)–(d)]
, the reduced power monoid of a monoid
H
is FmF-atomic if and only if it is BmF-atomic, if and only if
1_{H}\neq x^{2}\neq x
for every non-identity element
x\in H
. This is clearly a consequence of Propositions
2.3
and
2.8
.

3.
Power monoids with unique minimal factorizations

In the following, we discuss certain conditions for a reduced power monoid to be HmF or UmF. Most notably, we obtain a complete characterization of UmF-ness when the ground monoid is commutative or cancellative. A key role in this direction will be played by the following:

Definition 3.1
.

A (multiplicatively written) semigroup (resp., monoid)
S
is
almost-breakable
if, for every
x,y\in S
, either
xy\in\allowbreak\{x,y\}
or
yx\in\{x,y\}
; and it is
breakable
if
xy\in\{x,y\}
for all
x,y\in S
.

Breakable semigroups seem to have been first considered by L. Rédei in
[
17
, Section 27]
, and they recently played a role in the Bienvenu-Geroldinger isomorphism problem for power monoids
[
22
, Example 1.2(2)]
. We have not been able to find any references to almost-breakable semigroups in the literature; consequently, a few elementary remarks may be appropriate before proceeding.

Remarks 3.2
.

(1)

Every breakable semigroup is, of course, almost-breakable. The converse need not be true. For instance, let
S
be the
3
-element magma described by the following (Cayley) table:

\begin{array}[]{l|*{3}{l}}&x&y&z\\
\hline\cr x&x&x&x\\
y&x&y&x\\
z&z&z&z\end{array}

It is easily checked that
S
is an almost-breakable semigroup. Yet,
S
is not breakable, since
yz\notin\{y,z\}
.

(2)

It is clear from the definition that any almost-breakable monoid
H
is idempotent, i.e.,
x^{2}=x
for every
x\in H
. It is then easy to check that
H
is Dedekind-finite and reduced: In fact, if
xy=1_{H}
for some
x,y\in H
, then
y=xy^{2}=xy=1_{H}
and hence
x=y=1_{H}
.

We will also need a special case of the general construction of
ideal extensions

[
7
, Section 4.4]
. More precisely, let
H
and
K
be disjoint semigroups (written multiplicatively). It is then possible to define a “joint extension” of the operations of
H
and
K
to a binary operation on
H\cup K
by taking
xy=yx:=y
for all
x\in H
and
y\in K
. We denote the magma obtained in this way by
H\oslash K
and call it the
trivial ideal extension of
K
by
H
. The next remark highlights some features of this construction.

Remark 3.3
.

It is easily seen that the trivial ideal extension of a semigroup
K
by a semigroup
H
is still a semigroup. In particular,
H\oslash K
is a monoid if and only if
H
is, in which case the identity (resp., the group of units) of
H\oslash K
is the same as the identity (resp., the group of units) of
H
. It follows that
H\oslash K
is a unit-cancellative monoid if and only if
H
is a group and
K
is a cancellative semigroup. By letting
H
be a non-trivial group and
K
be a cancellative semigroup, this offers a systematic method for generating unit-cancellative monoids that are not cancellative, which may be interesting in itself when considering that most of the recent work on factorization in a non-cancellative setting has focused on the unit-cancellative case (see, e.g.,
[
13
]
,
[
21
, Sections 4.1 and 4.3]
, and
[
11
]
). Here, we say that a monoid
M
is
unit-cancellative
if
xy\neq\allowbreak x\neq yx
for all
x,y\in M
such that
y
is a non-unit, whence a commutative monoid is unit-cancellative if and only if it is acyclic.

With these preliminaries in place, we are about to prove a couple of lemmas that provide stringent
necessary
conditions on the structure of a monoid
H
whose reduced power monoid is either HmF or UmF. To this end, we recall that the
order
of an element
x\in H
is the size of the submonoid generated by the element itself (with the result that the order is either a positive integer or
\infty
).

Lemma 3.4
.

The following hold for a monoid
H
:

(i)

If
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is HmF, then every element of
H
has order
\leq 3
.

(ii)

If
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is UmF, then every element of
H
has order
\leq 2
.

In either case,
H
is periodic (i.e., any element of
H
has finite order) and hence Dedekind-finite.

Proof.

The “In particular” part of the statement is straightforward from items
(i)
and
(ii)
, given that every periodic monoid is Dedekind-finite
[
21
, Remark 4.9(3)]
. Therefore, we focus on the rest.

(i)
Let
H
be non-trivial (otherwise there is nothing to do), suppose
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is HmF, and denote by
\kappa
the order of a non-identity
x\in H
. We have to show that
\kappa\leq 3
.

Assume to the contrary that
\kappa\geq 4
and set
X:=\{1_{H},x,x^{2},x^{3}\}
. It is clear that
x^{3}\notin\{1_{H},x,x^{2}\}
, or else
x^{n}\in\{1_{H},x,x^{2}\}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
(by a routine induction) and hence
\kappa\leq 3
(which is absurd). So, we gather from Proposition
2.4
that the words
\mathfrak{a}:=\{1_{H},x\}^{\ast 3}
and
\mathfrak{b}:=\{1_{H},x\}\ast\{1_{H},x^{2}\}
are both minimal factorizations of
X
in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
, with the result that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is not HmF (a contradiction).

(ii)
Since every UmF monoid is, obviously, HmF (cf. Remark 3.3(2) in
[
10
]
), we gather from
(i)
that any element of
H
has order
\leq 3
, and it only remains to check that this inequality is strict. Suppose to the contrary that
H
has an element,
x
, of order
3
. Then, similarly as in the proof of the previous item,
x^{2}\notin\{1_{H},x\}
. Therefore, we get from Proposition
2.4
that the words
\{1_{H},\allowbreak x\}\ast\allowbreak\{1_{H},x\}
and
\{1_{H},x\}\ast\{1_{H},x^{2}\}
are inequivalent minimal factorizations of
\{1_{H},x,x^{2}\}
, contradicting that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is a UmF monoid.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
H
be a monoid and suppose that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is UmF. The following hold:

(i)

The group of units of
H
has order
\leq 2
.

(ii)

uy=yu=y
for all
u\in H^{\times}
and
y\in H\smallsetminus H^{\times}
.

(iii)

H\smallsetminus H^{\times}
is an almost-breakable semigroup.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is UmF, we gather from Lemma
3.4
(ii)
that, for every
x\in H
, either
x^{2}=1_{H}
or
x^{2}=x
. In particular,
x\in H^{\times}
if and only if
x^{2}=1_{H}
. Besides that, we will use without further comment that any two-element set in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is, by Proposition
2.4
, irreducible.

(i)
Assume by way of contradiction that there exist
u,v\in H^{\times}
with
u\neq v
and
u\neq 1_{H}\neq v
. Then
uv\notin\{u,v\}
, or else
u=1_{H}
or
v=1_{H}
. Recalling from the above that
u^{2}=1_{H}
, it follows that

\{1_{H},u,v,uv\}=\{1_{H},u\}\{1_{H},v\}=\{1_{H},u\}\{1_{H},uv\},

in contradiction to the hypothesis that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is UmF. So,
H^{\times}
must have at most two elements.

(ii)
Let
u\in H^{\times}
and
y\in H\smallsetminus H^{\times}
. We may assume that
H^{\times}
is non-trivial, otherwise there is nothing to prove. Then we get from item
(i)
that
H^{\times}=\{1_{H},u\}
for some
u\in H
with
u^{2}=1_{H}\neq u
. If
uy=1_{H}
, then
y=uy^{2}=uy=1_{H}
(which is absurd); and if
uy=u
, then
u^{2}y=y=u^{2}=1_{H}
(which is again absurd). Therefore,
uy
is a non-unit. Considering that

\{1_{H},u\}\{1_{H},y\}=\{1_{H},u,y,uy\}=\{1_{H},u,y,u^{2}y\}=\{1_{H},u\}\{1_{H},uy\},

it follows that
\{1_{H},u\}\ast\{1_{H},y\}
and
\{1_{H},u\}\ast\{1_{H},uy\}
are both minimal factorizations of
\{1_{H},u,y,uy\}
. This, however, is only possible if
uy=y
, since
uy\neq y
would contradict the UmF-ness of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
.

(iii)
Since
H
is Dedekind-finite by Lemma
3.4
, then
H\smallsetminus H^{\times}
is a semigroup. Assume for a contradiction that there exist
x,y\in H\smallsetminus H^{\times}
such that
xy,yx\notin\{x,y\}
. By Lemma
3.4
(ii)
,
x
and
y
are idempotent. So, it is clear that
x\neq y
and
xy\neq 1_{H}\neq yx
. Moreover, we gather from Lemma
2.2
(iv)
that the set
X:=\{1_{H},x,y\}
is irreducible in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
. In fact,
\{1_{H},x\}
and
\{1_{H},y\}
are the only proper divisors of
X
, and we have from the above that

\{1_{H},x\}\{1_{H},y\}=\{1_{H},x,y,xy\}\neq X\neq\{1_{H},x,y,yx\}=\{1_{H},y\}\{1_{H},x\}.

Thus, the words
\{1_{H},x\}\ast\{1_{H},y\}
and
\{1_{H},x,y\}\ast\{1_{H},y\}
are, by Proposition
2.4
, inequivalent minimal factorizations of
\{1_{H},x,y,xy\}
, in contradiction with the UmF-ness of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
.
∎

We are finally ready to prove the first main result of the paper, that is, a
necessary and sufficient
condition for a reduced power monoid to be UmF.

Theorem 3.6
.

The following are equivalent for a monoid
H
:

(a)

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is UmF.

(b)

H\smallsetminus H^{\times}
is an almost-breakable subsemigroup of
H
, the group of units of
H
has order
\leq 2
,
H
is the trivial ideal extension of
H\smallsetminus H^{\times}
by
H^{\times}
, and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}((H\smallsetminus H^{\times})\cup\{1_{H}\})
is UmF.

Proof.

(a)

\Rightarrow

(b)
. Since
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is UmF (by hypothesis), we get from Lemma
3.4
that
H
is Dedekind-finite and hence
K:=(H\smallsetminus H^{\times})\cup\{1_{H}\}
is a submonoid of
H
. It follows from
[
4
, Proposition 3.2(iii)]
that

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
is a divisor-closed submonoid of

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
, and then from

[
10
, Proposition 3.5(iii)]
that

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
is UmF. By
Lemma
3.5
, this suffices to complete the
first half of the proof.

(b)

\Rightarrow

(a)
. Let
H\smallsetminus H^{\times}
be an almost-breakable subsemigroup of
H
and set
K:=(H\smallsetminus H^{\times})\cup\{1_{H}\}
. If
H^{\times}
is trivial, then we have nothing to do. Thus, for the remainder of
the proof, we suppose that

H^{\times}=\{1_{H},u\},\qquad\text{for some }u\in H\text{ with }u^{2}=1_{H}\neq u.

By definition of a trivial ideal extension, we have that

\{1_{H},u\}Y=Y\{1_{H},u\}=Y\cup\{u\},\qquad\text{for every }Y\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H).

(1)

Consequently, any set of the form
\{1_{H},u,y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}
with
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\in H\smallsetminus H^{\times}
(and
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
) is not irreducible in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
, because it factors as
\{1_{H},u\}\allowbreak\{1_{H},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}
. In other words,

\text{if }A\text{ is an irreducible of }\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)\text{ with }u\in A,\text{ then }A=\{1_{H},u\}.

(2)

Let
X
be an arbitrary element of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
. If
u\notin X
, then

X
lies in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
and, by the hypothesis that

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
is UmF, it has an essentially unique minimal
factorization in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
. So, assume
u\in X
and let

\mathfrak{a}=Y_{1}\ast\cdots\ast Y_{n}
be a minimal factorization of
X
. Since the product of any two non-units of
H
is still a non-unit (by the hypothesis that
H\smallsetminus H^{\times}
is a subsemigroup of
H
), it is clear that
u\in Y_{i}
for some
i\in\allowbreak\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
. It then follows from Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
) that, without loss of generality,
Y_{1}=\{1_{H},u\}
(recall that two factorizations in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
are equivalent if and only if they differ in the order of the factors).

If
Y_{j}=\{1_{H},u\}
for some
j\in\llbracket 2,n\rrbracket
, then
Y_{1}Y_{j}=\{1_{H},u\}^{2}=\{1_{H},u\}=Y_{1}
, which, again by Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
), would contradict the minimality of
\mathfrak{a}
. Therefore,
Y_{j}\neq\{1_{H},u\}
for any
j\in\llbracket 2,n\rrbracket
, and
\mathfrak{a}=\{1_{H},u\}\ast\allowbreak Y_{2}\ast\cdots\ast Y_{n}
with
Y_{2},\dots,Y_{n}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
.
As a result,
X=(Y_{2}\cdots Y_{n})\cup\{u\}
. Since
u\notin Y_{2}\cdots Y_{n}
, it follows that
Y_{2}\cdots Y_{n}=X\smallsetminus\{u\}
. Moreover,
\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}:=Y_{2}\ast\cdots\ast Y_{n}
is a factorization of
X\smallsetminus\{u\}
.

We claim that
\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}
is actually a minimal factorization of
X\smallsetminus\{u\}
.
Otherwise, there exists a permutation
\mathfrak{b}
of a
proper
subword of
\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}

such that
\pi(\mathfrak{b})=\pi(\mathfrak{a}^{\prime})=X\smallsetminus\{u\}
, where
\pi
denotes the
factorization homomorphism of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
. But then

\pi(\{1_{H},u\}\ast\mathfrak{b})=X=\pi(\mathfrak{a})
, contradicting the minimality of
\mathfrak{a}
.

It remains to show that, up to a permutation of its letters,
\mathfrak{a}
is the unique
minimal factorization of
X
. From the above, every minimal factorization of
X
is of the
form
\{1_{H},u\}\ast\mathfrak{z}
, where
\mathfrak{z}
is a minimal factorization of

X\smallsetminus\{u\}
in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
. Since
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
is UmF
(by hypothesis), it follows that
\mathfrak{z}
and
\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}
are equivalent (that is, a permutation of each other), and
thus
\mathfrak{a}
is essentially unique.
∎

A straightforward consequence of Theorem
3.6
is the following characterization,
which applies in particular to cancellative and acyclic monoids.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
H
be either a group or a monoid whose non-units do not form an almost-breakable semigroup. Then, the reduced power monoid of a monoid
H
is UmF if and only if
H
is either trivial or isomorphic to the integers modulo
2
under addition.

Note that, by
[
4
, Corollary 4.15]
, a reduced power monoid is UmF-atomic if and only if its ground monoid, say
H
, is trivial. This is a special case of Corollary
3.7
. In fact,
[
4
, Theorem 4.13]
shows that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is atomic if and only if
x^{2}\notin\{1_{H},x\}
for every non-identity
x\in H
, and the latter condition, in turn, entails that every irreducible of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is an atom (Proposition
2.3
),
H\smallsetminus H^{\times}
is not an almost-breakable semigroup, and
H
is not isomorphic to the additive group of integers modulo
2
.

4.
Focus on the almost-breakable case

By Theorem
3.6
, characterizing the UmF-ness of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
comes down to the case when the ground monoid
H
is almost-breakable. Through the present section, we thus restrict our attention to this case. Before getting to the punchline, we prove a series of preliminary results. We recall from Section
2.1
that we write
x\simeq_{H}y
to mean that two elements
x
and
y
in a monoid
H
are
associated
, i.e.,
HxH=HyH
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
H
be an almost-breakable semigroup and let
x,y\in H
. The following hold:

(i)

The principal (two-sided) ideals of
H
form a chain under set inclusion.

(ii)

If
HxH\subsetneq HyH
, then
xyx=x
and
xy\simeq_{H}yx\simeq_{H}x
.

(iii)

If
x\simeq_{H}y
, then
\{xy,yx\}=\{x,y\}
.

(iv)

There are no elements
x,y,z\in H
with
HxH\subsetneq HyH
and
HxH\subsetneq HzH
such that
xy\neq x\neq zx
.

(v)

If
HxH\subsetneq HyH
,
yx\neq x
, and
x\simeq_{H}x^{\prime}
for some
x^{\prime}\in H
, then
x^{\prime}y=x^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Recall from Remark
3.2
(2)
that every almost-breakable semigroup is idempotent, and note that the opposite monoid of an almost-breakable monoid is itself almost-breakable. We will freely use these simple facts in the remainder without further comment.

(i)
Since
H
is almost-breakable, either
xy\in\{x,y\}
or
yx\in\{x,y\}
. Consequently, either
x\in\{xy,yx\}
or
y\in\{xy,yx\}
. In the former case,
HxH\subseteq HyH
; and in the latter,
HyH\subseteq HxH
.

(ii)
By the almost-breakability of
H
, either
xy\in\{x,y\}
or
yx\in\allowbreak\{x,y\}
. Assume
xy\in\{x,y\}
(the other case is analogous). Then,
xy=x
(otherwise
y\in HxH
), and so
xyx=x
. It follows that
x\in HxyH
and
x\in\allowbreak HyxH
, so
HxH\subseteq HxyH
and
HxH\subseteq HyxH
. The reverse inclusions are trivial.

(iii)
Assume without loss of generality that
xy=x
(otherwise, flip the roles of
x
and
y
, or work in the opposite monoid of
H
). Then
xyx=x^{2}=x
and, being
HxH=HyH
,
y=uxv
for some
u,v\in H
. It follows that
yv=yxv=y
and so
yxy=yxyv=yxv=y
. Thus,
y=yxy=yx
.

(iv)
Assume the contrary. We know from
(i)
that
HyH\subseteq HzH
or
HzH\subseteq HyH
. So, we get from
(ii)
and
(iii)
that
yz\in HyH\cup HzH\supsetneq HxH
. Moreover, the hypotheses imply by
(ii)
that
xy\simeq_{H}x\simeq_{H}zx
. Thus, we conclude from
(iii)
that
\{xy,zx\}\ni(xy)(zx)=x(yz)x=x
, contradicting that
xy\neq x\neq zx
.

(v)
Assume to the contrary that there exists
x^{\prime}\in H
such that
HxH=Hx^{\prime}H
and
x^{\prime}y\neq x^{\prime}
. It is then clear that
x^{\prime}y\neq y
, or else
HxH\subsetneq HyH\subseteq Hx^{\prime}H=HxH
(which is absurd). So, by the almost-breakability of
H
, we have by
(ii)
that
yx^{\prime}=x^{\prime}
. Analogously, we get from the hypothesis that
xy=x
. Thus

∎

Next, we characterize the irreducibles of the reduced power monoid of an almost-breakable monoid and describe an interesting property of its factorizations.

Proposition 4.2
.

The following hold for an almost-breakable monoid

(i)

The irreducibles of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
are precisely the
2
-element subsets of
H
containing the identity.

(ii)

Every element of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
admits a square-free factorization into irreducibles.

Proof.

(i)
We may restrict our attention on the “only” part of the statement, because we already know from Proposition
2.4
that any
2

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is irreducible.

Fix

with

4.1
(i)
(and the fact that

for every

that

Accordingly, we define the following subsets of

•

•

It is clear that

(3)

We will distinguish two cases, depending on whether

or

Case 1:

. If

(4)

with

. There exist

(6)

where

Corollary 4.3
.

If the reduced power monoid of an almost-breakable monoid is UmF, then the minimal factorizations of its elements are square-free.

Proof.

Let
H
be an almost-breakable monoid. If
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is UmF, then the minimal factorizations of a set
X\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
have all the same factors (by Remark
2.6
). So, it remains to see that
X
has a square-free minimal factorization. For, we know from Proposition
4.2
(ii)
that
X
admits a square-free factorization
\mathfrak{a}
. If
\mathfrak{a}
is minimal, then we are done. Otherwise, there is a subword
\mathfrak{b}
of
\mathfrak{a}
which is a minimal factorization of
X
, and it is obvious that a subword of a square-free word is itself square-free.
∎

We introduce the following definitions to streamline the sequel of the presentation.

Definition 4.4
.

We let a
balanced pair
of a monoid
H
be an ordered pair
(x,y)\in H\times H
such that
xy\in\{x,y\}
; otherwise, the pair is
unbalanced
. Accordingly, the monoid
H
is
twisted
if there exist unbalanced pairs
(x,y)
and
(z,w)
of
H
with
\{x,y\}\cap\{z,w\}=\varnothing
such that
xy\in\{z,w\}
and
zw\in\{x,y\}
; and
bridged
if there exist unbalanced pairs
(x_{1},x_{2})
,
(x_{2},x_{3})
, and
(x_{1},x_{3})
of
H
such that
x_{1}x_{3}\notin\{x_{1}x_{2},x_{2}x_{3}\}
.
If
H
is not twisted (resp., bridged), then we call it
untwisted
(resp.,
unbridged
).

Our interest in these concepts arises from the next proposition, in which we show that the ground monoid being almost-breakable is not
sufficient
for a reduced power monoid to be UmF. More precisely, we are going to see that, if
H
is an almost-breakable monoid, then a
necessary
condition for
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
to be UmF is that
H
is also untwisted
and
unbridged. To this end, we will often use without further mention that, if
H
is almost-breakable and
(x,y)
is an unbalanced pair of
H
, then the pair
(y,x)
is balanced.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
H
be an almost-breakable monoid. If
H
is either twisted or bridged, then the reduced power monoid of
H
is not UmF.

Proof.

Assume first that
H
is twisted. Accordingly, let
(x,y)
and
(z,w)
be two unbalanced pairs of
H
with
\{x,y\}\cap\{z,w\}=\varnothing
such that
xy\in\{z,w\}
and
zw\in\{x,y\}
. We claim that the set
X:=\{1_{H},x,y,z,w\}
has two inequivalent minimal factorizations (and hence
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is not UmF). To this end, we distinguish two cases that cover all possible configurations.

Case 1:

xy=z
and
zw=x
.
Under these assumptions,
(z,x)
,
(x,z)
,
(z,y)
,
(y,z)
,
(x,w)
,
(w,x)
,
(y,x)
,
(w,z)
are balanced pairs. Moreover, by the hypothesis of almost-breakability, either
(y,w)
or
(w,y)
is balanced. Say
yw\in\{y,w\}
(the other case is similar). Then,

\{1,x\}\ast\{1,y\}\ast\{1,w\}
and
\{1,y\}\ast\{1,z\}\ast\{1,w\}
are inequivalent minimal factorizations of
X
. The minimality follows from a purely combinatorial argument, since the product of two
2
-element sets contains at most
4
elements while
|X|=5
.

Case 2:

xy=z
and
zw=y
.
Under these assumptions,
(z,x)
,
(x,z)
,
(z,y)
,
(y,z)
,
(y,w)
,
(w,y)
,
(y,x)
,
(w,z)
are balanced pairs. Moreover, by almost-breakability, either
(x,w)
or
(w,x)
is also balanced. Say
wx\in\{x,w\}
(the other case is analogous). Then,
\{1,x\}\ast\{1,z\}\ast\{1,w\}
and
\{1,w\}\ast\{1,x\}\ast\{1,y\}
are inequivalent minimal factorizations of
X
.

Assume now that
H
is bridged. Let
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in H
be such such that
(x_{1},x_{2})
,
(x_{2},x_{3})
, and
(x_{1},x_{3})
are unbalanced pairs and
x_{1}x_{2}\neq x_{1}x_{3}\neq x_{2}x_{3}
. We claim that

\mathfrak{a}:=\{1_{H},x_{2}\}\ast\{1_{H},x_{3}\}\ast\{1_{H},x_{1}\}\ast\{1_{H},x_{2}\}

is a minimal factorization of the set

Y:=\{1_{H},x_{1},\allowbreak x_{2},\allowbreak x_{3},\allowbreak x_{1}x_{2},x_{2}x_{3}\}.

By Corollary
4.3
, this would imply that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is not UmF. In light of items
(i)
and
(iii)
of Lemma
4.1
, the two-sided ideals
Hx_{i}H
are ordered with respect to the strict inclusion
\subsetneq
. Thus, by Lemma
4.1
(ii)
,
x_{2}x_{3}\neq x_{1}
and
x_{1}x_{2}\neq x_{3}
. Moreover,
x_{1}x_{2}\neq x_{2}x_{3}
. In fact, by Lemma
4.1
(iv)
, if
x_{1}x_{2}=x_{2}x_{3}
, then it must be either
Hx_{1}H\subsetneq Hx_{2}H
or
Hx_{3}H\subsetneq Hx_{2}H
. If, e.g.,
Hx_{1}H\subsetneq Hx_{2}H
(the other case works analogously), then
x_{1}x_{2}\simeq_{H}x_{1}
but
x_{2}x_{3}=x_{1}x_{2}
is associated to
x_{2}
or
x_{3}
, a contradiction. It then follows that
|Y|=6
. We first observe that

Y=\{1_{H},x_{2}\}\{1_{H},x_{3}\}\{1_{H},x_{1}\}\{1_{H},x_{2}\},

i.e.,
\mathfrak{a}
is a factorization of
Y
. In fact, since
H
is almost-breakable, then
(x_{2},x_{1}),(x_{3},x_{2}),(x_{3},x_{1})
are balanced pairs and, as a consequence,
x_{2}x_{1}x_{2}\in\{x_{1}x_{2},x_{2}\}
,
x_{2}x_{3}x_{2}\in\{x_{2}x_{3},x_{2}\}
,
x_{2}x_{3}x_{1}\in\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{2}x_{3}\}
,
x_{3}x_{1}x_{2}\in\{x_{2},x_{3},x_{1}x_{2}\}
, and
x_{2}x_{3}x_{1}x_{2}\in\{x_{2},x_{1}x_{2},x_{2}x_{3}\}
. It remains to prove that
\mathfrak{a}
is minimal. Note that
\{1_{H},x_{1}\},\{1_{H},x_{2}\},\{1_{H},x_{3}\}
must appear in all possible factorizations of
Y
since it is impossible to recover one of the
x_{i}
’s as a product of the other two. Moreover, the factor
\{1_{H},x_{3}\}
must always precede
\{1_{H},x_{1}\}
, otherwise the element
x_{1}x_{3}\notin Y
would appear in the product. Therefore, the only subwords of
\mathfrak{a}
that can be eligible as minimal factorizations of
Y
are

\mathfrak{b}:=\{1_{H},x_{2}\}\ast\{1_{H},x_{3}\}\ast\{1_{H},x_{1}\},\quad\mathfrak{c}:=\allowbreak\{1_{H},\allowbreak x_{3}\}\ast\{1_{H},x_{2}\}\ast\{1_{H},x_{1}\},

\text{and }\mathfrak{d}:=\{1_{H},x_{3}\}\ast\{1_{H},x_{1}\}\ast\{1_{H},x_{2}\}.

However, it is immediate to verify by a direct computation that
\pi(\mathfrak{b})
and
\pi(\mathfrak{c})
do not contain
x_{1}x_{2}
, while
\pi(\mathfrak{d})
does not contain
x_{2}x_{3}
. This shows that
\mathfrak{a}
is minimal and concludes the proof.
∎

.

(1)

Let

Let

The next proposition shows that, while the monoid
H
being almost-breakable is not a sufficient condition for
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
to be UmF,

being breakable is.

Proposition 4.7
.

The reduced power monoid of a breakable monoid is UmF.

Proof.

Let
H
be a breakable monoid and fix
X\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
. We get from Proposition
2.8
(i)
that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is FmF (and hence factorable), so it only remains to check that
X
has an essentially unique minimal factorization (into irreducibles). For, assume
k:=|X|-1\neq 0
and write
X=\{1_{H},x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}
, where the
x_{i}
’s are non-identity elements of
H
(if
k=0
, then
X=\{1_{H}\}
and we are done, see Remark
2.5
).

If
A
is an irreducible divisor of
X
, then it follows from Proposition
4.2
(ii)
and Lemma
2.2
(ii)
that
A=\allowbreak\{1_{H},x_{i}\}
for some
i\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
. So, we gather from Remark
2.6
that a minimal factorization of
X
must be a
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
-word of the form
\mathfrak{a}=\{1_{H},x_{i_{1}}\}\ast\cdots\ast\{1_{H},x_{i_{n}}\}
with
n\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
and
i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
. To conclude, it is therefore enough to check that
n
equals
k
and the
i_{j}
’s are pairwise distinct.

To begin, denote by
\pi
the factorization homomorphism of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
and suppose to the contrary that
n<k
. There then exists an index
i\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
such that
i\notin I:=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\}
. Since
x_{i}\in X\smallsetminus\{1_{H}\}
and

, we thus find that
x_{i}=x_{j_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{r}}
for some

and
j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\in I
. But this is impossible, because
H
being breakable implies
x_{j_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{r}}\in\{x_{j_{1}},\ldots,x_{j_{r}}\}\subseteq X\smallsetminus\{x_{i}\}
.

Next, assume for a contradiction that there exist

with

such that
x_{i_{s}}=x_{i_{t}}
. Accordingly, set
A:=\pi(\mathfrak{z}_{1})
and

, where the
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
-words
\mathfrak{z}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{z}_{2}
are defined by

\mathfrak{z}_{1}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\{1_{H},x_{i_{1}}\}\ast\cdots\ast\{1_{H},x_{i_{s-1}}\}&\text{if }s\neq 1,\\
1_{\mathscr{F}(H)}&\text{if }s=1,\end{array}\right.\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathfrak{z}_{2}:=\{1_{H},x_{i_{s+1}}\}\ast\allowbreak\cdots\ast\{1_{H},x_{i_{n}}\}.

Then
\pi(\mathfrak{a})=A\{1,x_{s}\}B=AB\cup Ax_{s}B=AB=\pi(\mathfrak{z}_{1}\ast\mathfrak{z}_{2})
and this contradicts the minimality of
\mathfrak{a}
.
∎

We finish with a characterization of UmF-ness in the reduced power monoid of a commutative monoid.

Theorem 4.8
.

The following are equivalent for a commutative monoid
H
:

(a)

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is UmF.

(b)

H\smallsetminus H^{\times}
is a breakable subsemigroup of
H
, the group of units of
H
has order
\leq 2
, and
H
is the trivial ideal extension of
H\smallsetminus H^{\times}
by
H^{\times}
.

Proof.

A commutative monoid
H
is almost-breakable if and only it is breakable. The claim is therefore an immediate consequence of Theorem
3.6
and Proposition
4.7
.
∎
