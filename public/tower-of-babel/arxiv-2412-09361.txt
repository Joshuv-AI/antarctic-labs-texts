Arithmetic localisation and completion of spectra
N. P. Strickland

Abstract
. This is an exposition of facts about p p -local spectra, p p -complete spectra and modules over the p p -complete sphere spectrum, including homological criteria for finiteness. Most things are well-known to the experts, with a couple of potential exceptions explained in the introduction.



1.
Introduction

Let
{\mathcal{B}}
be Boardman’s stable homotopy category of spectra
(see
[
Mar83
]
, for example). Given a set
Q
of primes, we can
define a localised subcategory
{\mathcal{B}}[Q^{-1}]
, as will be recalled in
Section
4
. Given a single prime
p
, we have a category

{\mathcal{B}}_{(p)}={\mathcal{B}}[(\{\text{primes}\}\setminus\{p\})^{-1}]
of
p
-local
spectra, and we can also define a subcategory
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{p}
of
p
-complete
spectra, as will be recalled in Section
5
. This
contains the
p
-complete sphere spectrum
S_{p}
. One can also
construct
S_{p}
as a strictly commutative ring spectrum and then
consider the homotopy category of
S_{p}
-modules, which we call

{\mathcal{M}}_{p}
; this will be recalled in Section
6
. (Although
we will not give details in this note, there are a number of
applications that work more smoothly if we use
{\mathcal{M}}_{p}
as the ambient
category rather than
{\mathcal{B}}
or
{\mathcal{B}}_{(p)}
or
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{p}
. For example,
this applies to various questions related to Freyd’s Generating
Hypothesis, or to the Chromatic Splitting Conjecture.)

All of these are connective stable homotopy categories in the sense
of
[
HPS97
]
, such that the localising subcategory generated
by the unit of the symmetric monoidal product is the whole category.

Remark 1.1
.

We will refer to
[
HPS97
]
for a number of things, but we
will use different notation. There, coproducts are denoted by

\bigvee_{i}X_{i}
, the monoidal product of
X
and
Y
is denoted by

X\wedge Y
, and the corresponding internal function object is
denoted by
F(X,Y)
. Here we will follow more recent conventions and
use the notations
\bigoplus_{i}X_{i}
and
X\otimes Y
and
\underline{\operatorname{Hom}}(X,Y)
.
The unit of the symmetric monoidal product will generically be
denoted by
S
, but by some other symbol such as
S[Q^{-1}]
in some
particular cases. The suspension functor will be denoted by

\Sigma
, and we write
S^{i}
for
\Sigma^{i}S
.

There are two standard finiteness conditions to consider in this
context.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a stable homotopy category.

(a)

We say that an object
X\in{\mathcal{C}}
is
compact
(or

small
) if the functor
{\mathcal{C}}(X,-)
preserves all coproducts.

(b)

We say that an object
X\in{\mathcal{C}}
is
strongly
dualisable
if the natural map
\underline{\operatorname{Hom}}(X,S)\otimes X\to\underline{\operatorname{Hom}}(X,X)
is an
isomorphism.

In this note we will develop some general theory of the categories

{\mathcal{B}}[Q^{-1}]
,
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{p}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{p}
, with emphasis on the above
finiteness conditions and their relationship with various homological
criteria. Almost all of the results explained here are well-known to
the experts, and many of them are treated in
[
Mar83
, Chapters 8 and
9]
. Other useful classical references include
[
Bou79
]

and
[
BK72
]
. Nonetheless, we hope that a consolidated and
updated treatment will be useful. We will need various facts about
the algebraic theory of abelian groups, and for these we will mostly
refer to
[
Str20
]
, which is again a consolidated exposition of
facts that are mostly well-known.

One fact proved here that is not quite as standard is that any
strongly dualisable object in
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{p}
or
{\mathcal{M}}_{p}
is equivalent to the

p
-completion of a finite spectrum. We have not seen this in the
literature, and we found that it required more work than expected. It
appears here as part of Proposition
5.31
. We
will also prove as Theorem
6.19
that
{\mathcal{M}}_{p}
has Brown
representability for homology. The corresponding facts for

{\mathcal{B}}[Q^{-1}]
and
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{p}
appear in the literature, but for
{\mathcal{M}}_{p}

we need to adapt the method used in
[
Nee97
]
rather than quoting
the results proved there.

2.
Global spectra

Definition 2.1
.

Let
X
be a bounded below spectrum. By a
CW structure
on
X
we
mean a diagram

\dotsb X_{-2}\to X_{-1}\to X_{0}\to X_{1}\to X_{2}\to\dotsb\to X

such that

(a)

X_{i}=0
for
i\ll 0
.

(b)

The cofibre
X_{i}/X_{i-1}
is (equivalent to) a spectrum
of the form
(T_{i})_{+}\otimes S^{i}
, for some set
T_{i}
.

(c)

The cofibre
X/X_{i}
has
\pi_{j}(X/X_{i})=0
for
j\leq i
.

We say that the structure is of
finite type
if each set
T_{i}

is finite. We say that the structure is
finite
if in addition
we have
T_{i}=\emptyset
for almost all
i
.

We will often produce CW structures (and other similar structures)
using the following construction.

Construction 2.2
.

Suppose we have spectra
X^{i}
for all
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}
together with
morphisms
f^{i}\colon X^{i}\to X^{i+1}
that are identities for
i\ll 0
.
Let
W_{i}
be the fibre of
f^{i}
(so
W_{i}=0
for
i\ll 0
). Let

X^{-\infty}
be the value of
X^{i}
for
i\ll 0
, and let

X^{\infty}
be the sequential colimit (or telescope) of the sequence.
Put
X_{i}=\operatorname{fib}(X_{-\infty}\to X^{i+1})
. The octahedral
axiom provides a diagram as follows, in which the rows and columns
are cofibre sequences, and the middle square is a homotopy pullback:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces W_{i}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces W_{i}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{-\infty}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{i}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Sigma X_{i-1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Sigma X^{-\infty}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{-\infty}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{i+1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Sigma X_{i}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Sigma X^{-\infty}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Sigma W_{i}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Sigma W_{i}}

In other words, we have a chain of maps

\dotsb\to X_{i-2}\xrightarrow{g_{i-2}}X_{i-1}\xrightarrow{g_{i-1}}X_{i}\xrightarrow{g_{i}}X_{i+1}\to\dotsb

with
X_{i}=0
for
i\ll 0
, and
X_{i}/X_{i-1}=W_{i}
. It follows that

X_{i}
lies in the thick subcategory generated by
\{W_{j}\;|\;j\leq i\}
. Now let
X_{\infty}
be the homotopy colimit of the spectra

X_{i}
. We find that this is the fibre of the map
X^{-\infty}\to X^{\infty}
, and that it lies in the localising subcategory
generated by
\{W_{i}\;|\;i\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
X
be a bounded below spectrum.

(a)

If
H_{*}(X)=0
then
X
is contractible.

(b)

If
i
is the smallest integer such that
H_{i}(X)\neq 0
,
then the Hurewicz map
\pi_{i}(X)\to H_{i}(X)
is an isomorphism.

Proof.

First suppose that
H_{*}(X)=0
. By assumption
X
is bounded below, so
there exists
n
such that
\pi_{j}(X)=0
for all
j<n
. The Hurewicz
theorem then tells us that
\pi_{n}(X)\simeq H_{n}(X)=0
. This allows us
to apply the Hurewicz theorem one dimension higher, so

\pi_{n+1}(X)\simeq H_{n+1}(X)=0
. We can continue this inductively
to see that
\pi_{i}(X)=0
for all
X
, so
X
is contractible.
The proof for (b) is essentially the same.
∎

Construction 2.4
.

Let
X
be a spectrum with
\pi_{i}(X)=0
for
i<b
(so

H_{b}(X)\simeq\pi_{b}(X)
by the Hurewicz Theorem). If
\pi_{b}(X)
is a
free abelian group, we choose a family of elements
(u_{t})_{t\in T}

in
\pi_{b}(X)
that form a basis. If
\pi_{b}(X)
is not a free abelian
group, we choose a family of elements
(u_{t})_{t\in T}
in
\pi_{b}(X)

that generates
\pi_{b}(X)
and has cardinality as small as possible.
The maps
u_{t}\colon S^{b}\to X
can be assembled to give a map
u\colon W\to X
,
where
W=\bigoplus_{t\in T}S^{b}=T_{+}\otimes S^{b}
, and we write
Y
for
the cofibre of
u
.

Lemma 2.5
.

With notation as in Construction
2.4
:

(a)

\pi_{i}(Y)=H_{i}(Y)=0
for
i\leq b
.

(b)

H_{i}(Y)=H_{i}(X)
for
i>b+1
.

(c)

If
\pi_{b}(X)
is free abelian then
H_{i}(Y)=H_{i}(X)
for

i\geq b+1
.

(d)

If
H_{b+1}(X)
is free abelian then so is
H_{b+1}(Y)
.

(e)

If the groups
H_{b}(X)=\pi_{b}(X)
and
H_{b+1}(X)
are both
finitely generated, then so is
H_{b+1}(Y)
.

Proof.

First, we have exact sequences

\pi_{i}(W)\to\pi_{i}(X)\to\pi_{i}(Y)\to\pi_{i-1}(W)
with

\pi_{i}(W)=\pi_{i}(X)=0
for
i<b
and
\pi_{b}(W)=\bigoplus_{t\in T}{\mathbb{Z}}
,
and the map
\pi_{b}(W)\to\pi_{b}(X)
is surjective by construction. It
follows easily that
\pi_{i}(Y)=0
for
i\leq b
, and so
H_{i}(Y)=0
for

i\leq b
by the Hurewicz Theorem. This proves (a).

Next, we have exact sequences

H_{i}(W)\to H_{i}(X)\to H_{i}(Y)\to H_{i-1}(W)
. If
i\not\in\{b,b+1\}

then
H_{i}(W)=H_{i-1}(W)=0
so
H_{i}(Y)=H_{i}(X)
, which proves (b). The
more interesting part of the sequence has the form

H_{b+1}(X)\rightarrowtail H_{b+1}(Y)\to H_{b}(W)\xrightarrow{u_{*}}H_{b}(X)\twoheadrightarrow H_{b}(Y).

The map
u_{*}
is surjective by construction, so
H_{b}(Y)=0
, as we
already saw in (a). If
H_{b}(X)
is free abelian then
u_{*}
is an
isomorphism by construction and so
H_{b+1}(Y)=H_{b+1}(X)
; this
proves (c). In general, let
F
be the kernel of
u_{*}
. This is a
subgroup of the free abelian group
\pi_{b}(W)
, so it is again a free
abelian group. (This standard fact is proved
as
[
Str20
, Theorem 6.6]
, for example.) It follows that the
short exact sequence
H_{b+1}(X)\to H_{b+1}(Y)\to F
must split,
giving
H_{b+1}(Y)=H_{b+1}(X)\oplus F
. Claim (d) is clear from this.
Moreover, if
\pi_{b}(X)
is finitely generated then the same is true
of
\pi_{b}(W)
and
F
, so if
H_{b+1}(X)
is also finitely generated
then
H_{b+1}(Y)
has the same property. This proves (e).
∎

Proposition 2.6
.

Any bounded below spectrum
X
admits a CW structure

\{X_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
(with
X_{n}/X_{n-1}=(T_{n})_{+}\otimes S^{n}
say) such
that the following additional properties are satisfied:

(a)

If
\pi_{i}(X)=0
for all
i<b
, then
X_{i}=0
and

T_{i}=\emptyset
for all
i<b
.

(b)

If
H_{t}(X)
is free abelian and
H_{i}(X)=0
for all
i>t

then
X_{i}=X
for
i\geq t
, and
T_{i}=\emptyset

for
i>t
. (In particular, this works if
H_{i}(X)=0
for all

i\geq t
.)

(c)

If
H_{i}(X)
is finitely generated for all
i
then
T_{i}

is finite for all
i
.

(d)

If
\bigoplus_{i}H_{i}(X)
is finitely generated, then

\coprod_{i}T_{i}
is finite.

Proof.

If
X=0
then everything is clear (with
X_{n}=0
and
T_{n}=\emptyset

for all
n
). Otherwise, let
b
be the smallest integer such that

\pi_{b}(X)\neq 0
. Put
X^{k}=X
for
k\leq b
. Then apply
Construction
2.5
repeatedly to give cofibrations

W_{k}\to X^{k}\to X^{k+1}
where
W_{k}=(T_{k})_{+}\otimes S^{k}
. These will have
properties as follows:

(a)

\pi_{i}(X^{k+1})=H_{i}(X^{k+1})=0
for
i\leq k
.

(b)

H_{i}(X^{k+1})=H_{i}(X^{k})
for
i>k+1
.

(c)

If
\pi_{k}(X^{k})
is free abelian then

H_{i}(X^{k+1})=H_{i}(X^{k})
for
i\geq k+1
.

(d)

If
H_{k+1}(X^{k})
is free abelian then so is
H_{k+1}(X^{k+1})
.

(e)

If the groups
H_{k}(X^{k})=\pi_{k}(X^{k})
and
H_{k+1}(X^{k})
are both
finitely generated, then so is
H_{k+1}(X^{k+1})
.

Let
X^{\infty}
be the homotopy colimit of the spectra
X^{k}
. Then

\pi_{i}(X^{\infty})
is the colimit of the groups
\pi_{i}(X^{k})
, which
are zero for
k>i
, so
\pi_{i}(X^{\infty})=0
for all
i
, so

X^{\infty}=0
. Now put
X_{i}=\operatorname{fib}(X\to X^{i+1})
as in
Construction
2.2
. The homotopy colimit of the spectra

X_{i}
is then
\operatorname{fib}(X^{-\infty}\to X^{\infty})=\operatorname{fib}(X\to 0)=X
, and

X_{i}/X_{i-1}=W_{i}
. It follows that this gives a CW structure.
Property (a) is clear by construction. Now suppose that
H_{t}(X)
is
free abelian and
H_{i}(X)=0
for
i>t
. Using Lemma
2.5

we see that when
k<t
we have
H_{i}(X^{k})=H_{i}(X)
for all
i\geq t
,
so
H_{t}(X^{k})
is free abelian and
H_{i}(X^{k})=0
for
i>t
. We then
find that
H_{t}(X^{t})
may be different from
H_{t}(X)
, but it is still
free abelian by Lemma
2.5
(d). Thus, in the next
application of Construction
2.4
we will get

H_{*}(X^{t+1})=0
, but
X^{t+1}
is bounded below, so
X^{t+1}=0
.
This means that
X_{t}=\operatorname{fib}(X\to X^{t+1})=X
, which implies claim (b).
Claim (c) is clear from the finiteness clauses in
Lemma
2.5
, and claim (d) follows from (b) and (c).
∎

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
X
be a bounded below spectrum. Then the following are equivalent:

(a)

X
admits a finite CW structure.

(b)

X
lies in the thick subcategory generated by the sphere

S=S^{0}
.

(c)

X
is compact

(d)

X
is strongly dualisable

(e)

\bigoplus_{i}H_{*}(X)
is finitely generated over
{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Proof.

It is clear that condition (a) implies (b). It is standard that
S

is compact in
{\mathcal{B}}
, so
{\mathcal{B}}
is monogenic as defined
in
[
HPS97
]
. We therefore see using
[
HPS97
, Theorem
2.1.3(d)]
that conditions (b), (c) and (d) are all
equivalent. (Moreover, this does not require the hypothesis that
X

is bounded below.) Put

{\mathcal{C}}=\{X\;|\;H_{*}(X)\text{ is finitely generated }\}
, and note that
this is preserved by suspension and desuspension. Suppose we have a
cofibration
X\xrightarrow{f}Y\xrightarrow{g}Z\xrightarrow{h}\Sigma X
with
X,Z\in{\mathcal{C}}
. We
then have a long exact sequence
H_{*}(X)\to H_{*}(Y)\to H_{*}(Z)
with

H_{*}(X)
and
H_{*}(Z)
finitely generated. This in turn gives a short
exact sequence
A_{*}\to H_{*}(Y)\to B_{*}
where
A_{*}
is a quotient of

H_{*}(X)
and
B_{*}
is a subgroup of
H_{*}(Z)
, so both
A_{*}
and
B_{*}

are finitely generated. This implies that
H_{*}(Y)
is finitely
generated, so
Y\in{\mathcal{C}}
. Similarly, if
X
is a retract of
Y
and

Y\in{\mathcal{C}}
then
H_{*}(X)
is a retract of the finitely generated group

H_{*}(Y)
, so it is also finitely generated, so
X\in{\mathcal{C}}
. This
proves that
{\mathcal{C}}
is a thick subcategory and it clearly contains
S
.
Using this we see that (b) implies (e). Finally,
Proposition
2.6
implies that (e) implies (a).
∎

3.
Moore spectra

Definition 3.1
.

A
Moore spectrum
is a spectrum
M
with
\pi_{k}M=0
for
k<0

(so
\pi_{0}(M)=H_{0}(M)
by the Hurewicz theorem) and
H_{k}(M)=0
for
k>0
.
We say that a Moore spectrum
M
is
free
if
H_{0}(M)
is a free
abelian group (and similarly for other adjectives that can be applied
to abelian groups, such as
finitely generated
). Given an
abelian group
A
, we write
SA
for any Moore spectrum
M
equipped
with an isomorphism
A\to\pi_{0}(M)
.

Example 3.2
.

For any
n>0
we define
S/n
to be the cofibre of
n.1_{S}\colon S\to S
;
this is a Moore spectrum with
\pi_{0}(S/n)={\mathbb{Z}}/n
.

Note that for any spectra
X
and
Y
we have a natural map

\pi_{0}\colon[X,Y]\to\operatorname{Hom}(\pi_{0}X,\pi_{0}Y).

If
X
is a Moore spectrum then this will often be an isomorphism; we
will give various results in this direction below.

Lemma 3.3
.

If
f\colon X\to Y
is a map of Moore spectra and

\pi_{0}(f)\colon\pi_{0}(X)\to\pi_{0}(Y)
is an isomorphism then
f
is an
equivalence.

Proof.

The assumption can be rephrased as saying that
H_{0}(f)
is an
isomorphism, so
H_{*}(\operatorname{cof}(f))=0
. For
i<0
we also have

\pi_{i}(X)=\pi_{i}(Y)=0
and so
\pi_{i}(\operatorname{cof}(f))=0
. It follows by the
Hurewicz Theorem that
\operatorname{cof}(f)=0
, so
f
is an equivalence.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

If
M
and
N
are Moore spectra with
\operatorname{Tor}(\pi_{0}(M),\pi_{0}(N))=0
,
then the Künneth theorem shows that
M\otimes N
is a Moore spectrum
with
\pi_{0}(M\otimes N)=\pi_{0}(M)\otimes\pi_{0}(N)
. In particular, this
works if either
\pi_{0}(M)
or
\pi_{0}(N)
is torsion free.

Remark 3.5
.

Given an abelian group
A
, we can construct a Moore spectrum
SA
as
follows. We first choose a free abelian group

Q_{0}=\bigoplus_{j\in J}{\mathbb{Z}}
and a surjective
homomorphism
e_{0}\colon Q_{0}\to A
. Then
\ker(e_{0})
is a subgroup of a
free abelian group and so is free abelian, so we can choose a group

P_{0}=\bigoplus_{i\in I}{\mathbb{Z}}
and an isomorphism
P_{0}\simeq\ker(e_{0})
,
giving a short exact sequence
P_{0}\xrightarrow{f_{0}}Q_{0}\xrightarrow{e_{0}}A
. Put

P=\bigoplus_{i\in I}S
and
Q=\bigoplus_{j\in J}S
so
\pi_{0}(P)=P_{0}

and
\pi_{0}(Q)=Q_{0}
, so we have a natural map

\pi_{0}\colon[P,Q]\to\operatorname{Hom}(\pi_{0}(P),\pi_{0}(Q))=\operatorname{Hom}(P_{0},Q_{0}).

It is easy to see that this is an isomorphism, so there is a unique
map
f\colon P\to Q
with
\pi_{0}(f)=f_{0}
. If we let
M
be the cofibre of

f
, it is easy to check that
M
is a Moore spectrum of type
SA
.

Lemma 3.6
.

With
SA=M
as constructed above, for any spectrum
X
we have a
short exact sequence

\operatorname{Ext}(A,\pi_{i+1}X)\rightarrowtail\pi_{i}\underline{\operatorname{Hom}}(SA,X)\twoheadrightarrow\operatorname{Hom}(A,\pi_{i}X),

which is natural in
X
.

Proof.

The cofibration
P\xrightarrow{f}Q\to SA
gives a fibration

\underline{\operatorname{Hom}}(\Sigma Q,X)\to\underline{\operatorname{Hom}}(\Sigma P,X)\to\underline{\operatorname{Hom}}(SA,X)\to\underline{\operatorname{Hom}}(Q,X)\to\underline{\operatorname{Hom}}(P,X).

By applying
\pi_{i}
we get an exact sequence of abelian groups.
We have
\pi_{i}\underline{\operatorname{Hom}}(Q,X)=[Q,\Sigma^{-i}X]
and this has a natural map to

\operatorname{Hom}(\pi_{0}Q,\pi_{0}\Sigma^{-i}X)=\operatorname{Hom}(Q_{0},\pi_{i}X)
. As
Q
is just a
coproduct of copies of
S
, this map is an isomorphism. By
identifying the other terms in a similar way, we get an exact
sequence

\operatorname{Hom}(Q,\pi_{i+1}X)\xrightarrow{f_{0}^{*}}\operatorname{Hom}(P,\pi_{i+1}X)\to\pi_{i}\underline{\operatorname{Hom}}(SA,X)\to\operatorname{Hom}(Q,\pi_{i}X)\xrightarrow{f^{*}}\operatorname{Hom}(P,\pi_{i}X).

The cokernel of the first map is (essentially by definition)

\operatorname{Ext}(A,\pi_{i+1}X)
, and the kernel of the last map is

\operatorname{Hom}(A,\pi_{i}X)
, so we have an induced short exact sequence as
claimed.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

We can apply the above when
X
is another Moore spectrum of type

SA
constructed in some other way, and we can conclude that

X\simeq SA
, so all Moore spectra of type
SA
are equivalent. It
also follows that the map
\pi_{0}\colon[SA,SB]\to\operatorname{Hom}(A,B)
is surjective,
with kernel
\operatorname{Ext}(A,\pi_{1}SB)
. This is often zero but not always,
because of the fact that
\pi_{1}(S)={\mathbb{Z}}/2\neq 0
. Thus, it is almost
correct but not quite correct to think of
SA
as being functorial in

A
.

Example 3.8
.

Let
p
be a prime. It is a standard fact that
\pi_{1}(S)={\mathbb{Z}}/2
, and
it follows using the cofibration
S\xrightarrow{p}S\to S/p
that

\pi_{1}(S/p)=0
except when
p=2
in which case
\pi_{1}(S/2)={\mathbb{Z}}/2
.
Lemma
3.6
gives a short exact sequence

\operatorname{Ext}({\mathbb{Z}}/p,\pi_{1}(S/p))\rightarrowtail[S/p,S/p]\twoheadrightarrow\operatorname{Hom}({\mathbb{Z}}/p,{\mathbb{Z}}/p).

This gives
[S/p,S/p]={\mathbb{Z}}/p
when
p
is odd, and
|[S/2,S/2]|=4
. In
particular we have
p^{2}.1_{S/p}=0
in all cases. (It is known that

[S/2,S/2]={\mathbb{Z}}/4
, but we will not need that.)

Remark 3.9
.

As a special case of Remark
3.5
, suppose we have a
commutative ring
P_{0}
which is free as an abelian group, together
with an element
x\in P_{0}
which is not a zero divisor. We can the
form a spectrum
P
of the form
\bigoplus_{i\in I}S
with

\pi_{0}(P)=P_{0}
. We then find that

with
\pi_{0}(P\otimes P)=P_{0}\otimes P_{0}

\mu\colon P\otimes P\to P

ring spectrum. There is also a unique map
\mu_{x}\colon P\to P

\pi_{0}(\mu_{x})(a)=ax
for all

of

\pi_{0}(P/x)=P_{0}/x

can be seen as an example of

Definition 3.10
.

Let
Q
be a set of prime numbers, and let
\langle Q\rangle
be the set of
positive integers that can be expressed as a product of primes in

Q
. As usual, given an abelian group

we write

for
the group of fractions

a_{0}/m_{0}=a_{1}/m_{1}

[

In Section

we will need a Moore spectrum for

(which will be denoted by

Construction 3.11
.

Let
Q
be a set of primes. Any integer

can be factored as

n_{0}n_{1}
, where
n_{0}
is a product of primes in
Q
and
n_{1}
is a
product of primes not in
Q
. We write
n_{Q}
for
n_{0}
, and call
this the
Q
-factor
of
n
.

Now let
m_{r}
be the
Q
-factor of
r!
, and let
m_{r,s}
be the

Q
-factor of
\binom{r+s}{r}
, so
m_{0}=m_{1}=1
and

m_{r+s}=m_{r}m_{s}m_{r,s}
. Let
DP_{0}(Q)
be the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-linear span of the

t_{r}=t^{r}/m_{r}\in{\mathbb{Q}}[t]
. These satisfy

t_{r}t_{s}=m_{r,s}t_{r+s}

\phi\colon DP_{0}(Q)\to{\mathbb{Z}}[Q^{-1}]

.
This is not a zero divisor, and it satisfies

an induced surjective homomorphism
\overline{\phi}\colon DP_{0}(Q)/x\to{\mathbb{Z}}[Q^{-1}]

In

Thus, we can construct a ring spectrum
DP(Q)=\bigoplus_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}S

with

\mu_{x}\colon DP(Q)\to DP(Q)

is a Moore spectrum for

.

We could use the above construction to make a Moore spectrum for the
ring
{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]
. However, in this special case it is more convenient

Definition 3.12
.

We write
S[t]
for
\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}S
, and let

be the

Construction 3.13
.

Now fix a prime
p

Q
-local spectra

Definition 4.1
.

Again let
Q
be a set of primes, so
\langle Q\rangle
be the set of positive
integers that can be expressed as a product of primes in
Q
.

(a)

We say that an abelian group
A
is
Q
-local if for all

q\in Q
, the map
q.1_{A}\colon A\to A
is an isomorphism.

(b)

We say
A
is
Q
-torsion if for
all
a\in A
there exists
m\in\langle Q\rangle
with
ma=0

(c)

We say
A
is uniformly
Q
-torsion if there exists

m\in\langle Q\rangle
with
mA=0

(d)

We say that a spectrum
X
is
Q
-local if each homotopy group

\pi_{i}(X)
is
Q
-local.

(e)

We say that
X
is
Q
-torsion if each homotopy group

\pi_{i}(X)
is
Q
-torsion.

(f)

We say that
X
is uniformly
Q
-torsion if there exists

m\in\langle Q\rangle
with
m.1_{X}=0
.

(g)

We write
{\mathcal{B}}[Q^{-1}]
for the full subcategory of

Q
-local spectra.

Remark 4.2
.

An abelian group
A
is
\{p\}
-local if and only if
p.1_{A}
is an
isomorphism. On the other hand,
A
is said to be
p
-local if it is

Q
-local, where
Q
is the set of primes different from
p
. This
terminology is unfortunate, but well-established.

Remark 4.3
.

Recall that
f\colon X\to Y
is an equivalence iff

\pi_{*}(f)\colon\pi_{*}(X)\to\pi_{*}(Y)
is an isomorphism. It follows that

X
is
Q
-local iff
q.1_{X}\colon X\to X
is an equivalence for all

q\in Q
. If so, we see that each homology group
H_{i}(X)
is

Q
-local. Conversely, if
X
is bounded below and all homology
groups are
Q
-local, then we can use Lemma
2.3
to
see that
X
is
Q
-local.

Remark 4.4
.

If
Q
is the set of all primes then
{\mathbb{Z}}[Q^{-1}]={\mathbb{Q}}
. We use the
notation
A{\mathbb{Q}}
for
A[Q^{-1}]
and
{\mathcal{B}}{\mathbb{Q}}
for
{\mathcal{B}}[Q^{-1}]
.

Now suppose instead that we fix a prime
p
and let
Q
be the set of
all other primes. We then use the notation
A_{(p)}
for
A[Q^{-1}]

and
{\mathcal{B}}_{(p)}
for
{\mathcal{B}}[Q^{-1}]
.

Definition 4.5
.

We also use the notation
L_{Q}S
for
S[Q^{-1}]
, and we define
C_{Q}S

to be the fibre of the unit map
S\to L_{Q}S
. Given an arbitrary
spectrum
X
we write
L_{Q}X
or
X[Q^{-1}]
for the spectrum

L_{Q}S\otimes X=S[Q^{-1}]\otimes X
, and we write
C_{Q}X
for

C_{Q}S\otimes X
. The fibration
C_{Q}S\xrightarrow{\zeta}S\xrightarrow{\eta}L_{Q}S
gives
a natural fibration
C_{Q}X\xrightarrow{\zeta}X\xrightarrow{\eta}L_{Q}X
.

Proposition 4.6
.

(a)

L_{Q}X
is always
Q
-local, with

\pi_{*}(L_{Q}X)=\pi_{*}(X)[Q^{-1}]
.

(b)

C_{Q}X
is always
Q
-torsion.

(c)

A spectrum
X
is
Q
-local iff
C_{Q}X=0
iff the map

\eta\colon X\to L_{Q}X
is an equivalence.

(d)

A spectrum
X
is
Q
-torsion iff
L_{Q}X=0
iff the map

\zeta\colon C_{Q}X\to X
is an equivalence.

Proof.

We will prove (a) then (c) then (d) then (b).

(a)

Consider an element
q\in Q
, so
q.1_{S[Q^{-1}]}
is an
equivalence, so
(q.1_{S[Q^{-1}]})\otimes 1_{X}
is an equivalence.
As all relevant constructions are additive functors, this is the
same as
q.1_{S[Q^{-1}]\otimes X}=q.1_{L_{Q}X}
. It follows that

L_{Q}X
is
Q
-local, so each homotopy group
\pi_{i}(L_{Q}X)
is

Q
-local. It follows that the map
\eta_{*}\colon\pi_{i}(X)\to\pi_{i}(L_{Q}X)

extends uniquely to give a map

\eta^{\prime}\colon\pi_{i}(X)[Q^{-1}]={\mathbb{Z}}[Q^{-1}]\otimes\pi_{i}(X)\to\pi_{i}(L_{Q}X)
. As

{\mathbb{Z}}[Q^{-1}]
is torsion-free, the functor
{\mathbb{Z}}[Q^{-1}]\otimes(-)
is
exact, so both
{\mathbb{Z}}[Q^{-1}]\otimes\pi_{*}(X)
and
\pi_{*}(L_{Q}X)
are
homology theories. The map
\eta^{\prime}
is a morphism of homology
theories that is an isomorphism when
X=S
, so it is always an
isomorphism, so
\pi_{*}(L_{Q}X)=\pi_{*}(X)[Q^{-1}]
.

(c)

Given an abelian group
A
, it is
standard
[
Str20
, Proposition 9.14]
that
A
is

Q
-local if and only if the natural map
A\to A[Q^{-1}]
is an
isomorphism. Thus, a spectrum
X
is
Q
-local if and only if the
natural map
\pi_{*}(X)\to\pi_{*}(X)[Q^{-1}]=\pi_{*}(L_{Q}X)
is an
isomorphism, if and only if the map
X\to L_{Q}X
is an equivalence,
if and only if
C_{Q}X=0
.

(d)

It is standard that an abelian group
A
is
Q
-torsion
if and only if
A[Q^{-1}]=0
. Thus a spectrum
X
is
Q
-torsion
if and only if the graded group
\pi_{*}(X)[Q^{-1}]=\pi_{*}(L_{Q}X)
is
trivial, if and only if the spectrum
L_{Q}X
is zero, if and only if
the map
\zeta\colon C_{Q}X\to X
is an equivalence.

(b)

As
L_{Q}X
is local we have
C_{Q}L_{Q}X=0
by (c). However,

C_{Q}L_{Q}X=C_{Q}S\otimes L_{Q}S\otimes X\simeq L_{Q}S\otimes C_{Q}S\otimes X=L_{Q}C_{Q}X,

so
L_{Q}C_{Q}X=0
. Using (d) we conclude that
C_{Q}X
is
Q
-torsion.

∎

Corollary 4.7
.

(a)

If
X
is
Q
-torsion and
Y
is
Q
-local then
[X,Y]=0

(b)

Conversely, if
X
is such that
[X,Y]=0
for all

Q
-local
Y
, then
X
is
Q
-torsion.

(c)

Similarly, if
Y
is such that
[X,Y]=0
for all

Q
-torsion

, then
Y
is
Q
-local.

(d)

If
X\in{\mathcal{B}}
and
Y\in{\mathcal{B}}[Q^{-1}]
then the map

\eta^{*}\colon[L_{Q}X,Y]\to[X,Y]
is an isomorphism.

(e)

Thus,
{\mathcal{B}}[Q^{-1}]
is the localisation of
{\mathcal{B}}
with
respect to
S[Q^{-1}]
(in the sense of Bousfield
[
Bou79
]
,
also explained in
[
HPS97
, Section 3]
). The
corresponding localisation functor is
L_{Q}
, and the corresponding
acyclisation functor is
C_{Q}
.

Proof.

(a)

Suppose we have

where
X
is
X
-torsion and

Y
is
Q
-local, so
L_{Q}X=0
and the map
Y\to L_{Q}Y
is an
equivalence. We have a commutative diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L_{Q}X=0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L_{Q}Y,}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\eta}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L_{Q}f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\simeq}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\eta}

from which it is clear that

.

(b)

If
X
is as described, then in particular the natural
map
X\to L_{Q}X
must be zero, so the fibre
C_{Q}X\to X
is a split
epimorphism, so
X
is a retract of the
Q
-torsion spectrum

, so

is
Q
-torsion.

(c)

If
Y
is as described, then in particular the natural
map
C_{Q}Y\to Y
must be zero, so the cofibre
X\to L_{Q}X
is a split
monomorphism, so
X
is a retract of the
Q
-local spectrum

L_{Q}X
, so
X
is
Q
-local.

(d)

The cofibration
C_{Q}X\to X\xrightarrow{\eta}L_{Q}X
gives an exact
sequence
[\Sigma C_{Q}X,Y]\to[L_{Q}X,Y]\xrightarrow{\eta^{*}}[X,Y]\to[C_{Q}X,Y]
in which
the first and last terms are zero by (a), so

is an isomorphism.

(e)

The required axioms are precisely what we have proved.

Remark 4.8
.

The Eilenberg-MacLane spectrum
H
has
\pi_{0}(H)={\mathbb{Z}}
and
\pi_{i}(H)=0

for
i\neq 0
. It follows that the spectrum

H[Q^{-1}]=H\otimes S[Q^{-1}]
has
\pi_{0}(H[Q^{-1}])={\mathbb{Z}}[Q^{-1}]
and

\pi_{i}(H[Q^{-1}])=0
for
i\neq 0
. In other words,
H[Q^{-1}]
is
the Eilenberg-MacLane spectrum for the ring
{\mathbb{Z}}[Q^{-1}]
. It follows
that

H_{*}(X)[Q^{-1}]=\pi_{*}(H\otimes X)[Q^{-1}]=\pi_{*}(H\otimes X\otimes S[Q^{-1}])=\pi_{*}(H[Q^{-1}]\otimes X)=H_{*}(X;{\mathbb{Z}}[Q^{-1}]).

Definition 4.9
.

Let
X
be a bounded below
Q
-local spectrum. By a
Q
-local
CW structure
on

we mean a diagram

\dotsb X_{-2}\to X_{-1}\to X_{0}\to X_{1}\to X_{2}\to\dotsb\to X

(a)

X_{i}=0
for

.

(b)

The cofibre

\Sigma^{i}(T_{i})_{+}\otimes S[Q^{-1}]

.

(c)

.

Construction 4.10
.

Let
X
be a spectrum such that the group
\pi_{i}(X)[Q^{-1}]=0
for

i<b
. This means that the spectrum
X[Q^{-1}]
has

\pi_{i}(X[Q^{-1}])=0
for

, so we can apply the Hurewicz theorem
to see that
H_{i}(X[Q^{-1}])=0
for
i<b
and

, or equivalently

H_{b}(X)[Q^{-1}]=\pi_{b}(X)[Q^{-1}]
. If
\pi_{b}(X)[Q^{-1}]
is a free
module over
{\mathbb{Z}}[Q^{-1}]
, we choose a family of elements

(u^{\prime}_{t})_{t\in T}
in
\pi_{b}(X)[Q^{-1}]
that form a basis. If

\pi_{b}(X)[Q^{-1}]
is not a free module, we choose a family of
elements
(u^{\prime}_{t})_{t\in T}
in
\pi_{b}(X)[Q^{-1}]
that generates

\pi_{b}(X)[Q^{-1}]
and has cardinality as small as possible. Each

can be expressed as
u_{t}/m_{t}
for some element

and

, and the images of these elements

will again form a basis or generating set.

The maps
u_{t}\colon S^{b}\to X
can be assembled to give a map
u\colon W\to X
,
where
W=\bigoplus_{t\in T}S^{b}=T_{+}\otimes S^{b}
, and we write
Y
for
the cofibre of

.

Lemma 4.11
.

With notation as in Construction
4.10
:

(a)

For
i\leq b
we have

\pi_{i}(Y)[Q^{-1}]=H_{i}(Y)[Q^{-1}]=0
.

(b)

For
i>b+1
we have
H_{i}(Y)[Q^{-1}]=H_{i}(X)[Q^{-1}]
.

(c)

If
\pi_{b}(X)[Q^{-1}]
is a free module over
{\mathbb{Z}}[Q^{-1}]

then
H_{i}(Y)[Q^{-1}]=H_{i}(X)[Q^{-1}]
for
i\geq b+1
.

(d)

If
H_{b+1}(X)[Q^{-1}]
is a free module over

{\mathbb{Z}}[Q^{-1}]
then so is
H_{b+1}(Y)[Q^{-1}]
.

(e)

If the modules
H_{b}(X)[Q^{-1}]=\pi_{b}(X)[Q^{-1}]
and

are both finitely generated over

.

Proof.

The cofibration
W\to X\to Y
gives a cofibration

W[Q^{-1}]\to X[Q^{-1}]\to Y[Q^{-1}]
, which gives exact sequences

\pi_{i}(W)[Q^{-1}]\to\pi_{i}(X)[Q^{-1}]\to\pi_{i}(Y)[Q^{-1}]\to\pi_{i-1}(W)[Q^{-1}]

and

H_{i}(W)[Q^{-1}]\to H_{i}(X)[Q^{-1}]\to H_{i}(Y)[Q^{-1}]\to H_{i-1}(W)[Q^{-1}]
.
These can be used to prove the stated claims, in the same way as in
Lemma
2.5
. (We need to use the fact that any submodule
of a free
{\mathbb{Z}}[Q^{-1}]
-module is again free, which holds because

{\mathbb{Z}}[Q^{-1}]
is a principal ideal domain.)

Proposition 4.14
.

Let

(b)

(c)

(d)

(e)

\bigoplus_{i}H_{*}(X)

.

(f)

Remark 4.15
.

There are various different frameworks in which one can construct a
more geometric (rather than homotopical) model for
S[Q^{-1}]
as a
strictly commutative ring spectrum, and then consider the homotopy
category of strict
S[Q^{-1}]
-modules, which we will call

{\mathcal{M}}[Q^{-1}]
. For example, one can
use
[
EKMM96
, Chapter III and Theorem VIII.2.1]
or

[
Lur17
, Section 4.5 and Theorem 7.5.0.6]
. We will see
that this does not give anything new in the present case, but we will
spell out some details for comparison with the
p
-complete case to
be considered later.

The properties that we expect from such a framework are encapsulated
by the definition below.

Definition 4.16
.

A
strict module category
for
S[Q^{-1}]
consists of a
monogenic stable homotopy category
{\mathcal{M}}[Q^{-1}]
together with an
exact, strongly symmetric monoidal functor
P\colon{\mathcal{B}}\to{\mathcal{M}}[Q^{-1}]
and
a right adjoint
U\colon{\mathcal{M}}[Q^{-1}]\to{\mathcal{B}}
such that
UPX
is naturally
isomorphic to
S[Q^{-1}]\otimes X
.

For the rest of this section we assume that we have a strict module
category as above.

Lemma 4.17
.

If
M\in{\mathcal{M}}[Q^{-1}]
with
UM=0
, then
M=0
. Thus, if
f\colon M\to N

in
{\mathcal{M}}[Q^{-1}]
and
U(f)
is an isomorphism then
f
is an
isomorphism.

Proof.

we have
{\mathcal{M}}[Q^{-1}](\Sigma^{i}PS,M)={\mathcal{B}}(S^{i},UM)=0
for all
i
. Also,

P
is a strongly symmetric monoidal functor, so
PS
is the unit
object of
{\mathcal{M}}[Q^{-1}]
, and
{\mathcal{M}}[Q^{-1}]
is assumed to be
monogenic, so from
{\mathcal{M}}[Q^{-1}](\Sigma^{*}PS,M)=0
we can deduce that

M=0
as claimed. The second claim follows by applying the first to

\operatorname{cof}(f)
.
∎

Lemma 4.18
.

Suppose we have a functor
F\colon{\mathcal{C}}\to{\mathcal{D}}
with a right adjoint

G\colon{\mathcal{D}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
such that the unit map
\eta\colon X\to G(F(X))
is an
isomorphism for all
X\in{\mathcal{C}}
. Suppose also that
G
reflects
isomorphisms (so if
g\in{\mathcal{D}}(Y_{0},Y_{1})
and
U(g)
is an isomorphism
then
g
is an isomorphism). Then the counit maps

\epsilon_{Y}\colon F(G(Y))\to Y
are also isomorphisms, so
F
and
G
give an
adjoint equivalence between
{\mathcal{C}}
and
{\mathcal{D}}
.

Proof.

By the axioms for an adjunction we have a triangular identity

G(\epsilon_{Y})\circ\eta_{G(Y)}=1\colon G(Y)\to G(Y)
. As
\eta_{G(Y)}
is an
isomorphism we conclude that
G(\epsilon_{Y})
is an isomorphism. As
G

reflects isomorphisms we conclude that
\epsilon_{Y}
is an isomorphism.
∎

Proposition 4.19
.

The functor
U
takes values in
{\mathcal{B}}[Q^{-1}]
and the restricted
functors
{\mathcal{B}}[Q^{-1}]\xrightarrow{P_{1}}{\mathcal{M}}[Q^{-1}]\xrightarrow{U_{1}}{\mathcal{B}}[Q^{-1}]
give
an adjoint equivalence.

This could be seen as an instance
of
[
EKMM96
, Theorem VIII.3.3]
, but we will prove it directly.

Proof.

We have

{\mathcal{M}}[Q^{-1}](P(S),P(S))={\mathcal{B}}(S,U(P(S)))={\mathcal{B}}(S,S[Q^{-1}])={\mathbb{Z}}[Q^{-1}]
.
Thus, for every
q\in Q
, multiplication by
q
is an isomorphism on

P(S)
. For any
M\in{\mathcal{M}}[Q^{-1}]
we have

\pi_{i}(U(M))={\mathcal{B}}(S^{i},U(M))={\mathcal{M}}[Q^{-1}](\Sigma^{i}P(S),M)
, and it follows
that multiplication by
q
is also an isomorphism on this group. Thus,
the functor
U
takes values in
{\mathcal{B}}[Q^{-1}]
. For

X\in{\mathcal{B}}[Q^{-1}]
we have

U(P(X))=S[Q^{-1}]\otimes X=X[Q^{-1}]\simeq X
, so we see that the unit

\eta_{X}\colon X\to U(P(X))
is an isomorphism. As
U
reflects
isomorphisms (by Lemma
4.17
), we can apply
Lemma
4.18
to complete the proof.
∎

5.
p
-complete spectra

In this section we fix a prime number
p
. We will need various ideas
about
p
-completion and derived
p
-completion of abelian groups,
for which we will follow the treatment in
[
Str20
, Section 12]
.
The basic definitions are as follows.

Definition 5.1
.

(a)

We say that an abelian group
A
is

\operatorname{Ext}
-
p
-complete
if
\operatorname{Hom}({\mathbb{Z}}[1/p],A)=\operatorname{Ext}({\mathbb{Z}}[1/p],A)=0
.

(b)

We define
L_{0}(A)=\operatorname{Ext}({\mathbb{Z}}/p^{\infty},A)
and

L_{1}(A)=\operatorname{Hom}({\mathbb{Z}}/p^{\infty},A)
.

Lemma 5.2
.

For any abelian group
A
, there is a natural exact sequence

L_{1}(A)\rightarrowtail\operatorname{Hom}({\mathbb{Z}}[1/p],A)\to A\to L_{0}(A)\twoheadrightarrow\operatorname{Ext}({\mathbb{Z}}[1/p],A).

Thus, there is a natural map
\xi_{A}\colon A\to L_{0}(A)
, and
A
is

\operatorname{Ext}
-
p
-complete if and only
L_{1}(A)=0
and
\xi_{A}
is an
isomorphism.

Proof.

The sequence is the standard six term exact sequence of
\operatorname{Hom}
and

\operatorname{Ext}
groups derived from the short exact sequence

{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]\to{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{\infty}
, bearing in mind that
\operatorname{Hom}({\mathbb{Z}},A)=A
and

\operatorname{Ext}({\mathbb{Z}},A)=0
. The second claim is immediate from the exactness of
the sequence.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

The groups
L_{0}(A)=\operatorname{Ext}({\mathbb{Z}}/p^{\infty},A)
and

L_{1}(A)=\operatorname{Hom}({\mathbb{Z}}/p^{\infty},A)
are naturally modules over the ring

\operatorname{End}({\mathbb{Z}}/p^{\infty})
, which can be identified with the ring
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
of

p
-adic integers. If
A
is
\operatorname{Ext}
-
p
-complete then the map

\eta\colon A\to L_{0}A
is an isomorphism, so
A
inherits a module
structure over
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
. Naturality of
\eta
ensures that every
abelian group homomorphism between
\operatorname{Ext}
-
p
-complete groups is
actually a morphism of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
-modules. Similarly, for any short
exact sequence
A\to B\to C
and any abelian group
T
we have a
connecting map
\delta\colon\operatorname{Hom}(T,C)\to\operatorname{Ext}(T,A)
which is natural in
T

and thus compatible with endomorphisms of
T
. By taking

T={\mathbb{Z}}/p^{\infty}
we see that the connecting map
L_{1}C\to L_{0}A
is also

{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
-linear.

Remark 5.4
.

The category
{\mathcal{A}}_{p}
of
\operatorname{Ext}
-
p
-complete groups is not closed
under infinite direct sums. Nonetheless, any family of groups

A_{i}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{p}
has a coproduct in
{\mathcal{A}}_{p}
, namely the group

L_{0}\left(\bigoplus_{i}A_{i}\right)
. As an example, put

P=\bigoplus_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
, the group of sequences
(a_{n})
in

\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
such that
a_{n}=0
for
n\gg 0
. Let
Q
be the
larger group of sequences
a
that converge to zero
p
-adically, so
for all
k
there exists
n
with
a_{i}=0\pmod{p^{k}}
when
i\geq n
.
It is not hard to identify
L_{0}(P)
with
Q
.

Lemma 5.5
.

If
\alpha\colon A\to B
is a morphism of
\operatorname{Ext}
-
p
-complete groups and the
induced map
A/p\to B/p
is surjective, then
\alpha
is surjective.

Proof.

Let
C
be the cokernel of
\alpha
. The functor
A\mapsto A/p

is right exact, so
C/p=0
, so
L_{0}(C)=0

by
[
Str20
, Proposition 12.29]
. However, we also know
from
[
Str20
, Proposition 12.22(c)]
that
C
is

\operatorname{Ext}
-
p
-complete, so
C=L_{0}(C)=0
, so
\alpha
is surjective.
∎

Lemma 5.6
.

If
p^{e}A=0
for some
e\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then
A
is
\operatorname{Ext}
-
p
-complete, so

L_{1}(A)=0
and
L_{0}(A)=A
.

Proof.

Multiplication by
p^{e}
is an isomorphism on
{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]
, so it is also
an isomorphism on
\operatorname{Hom}({\mathbb{Z}}[1/p],A)
and
\operatorname{Ext}({\mathbb{Z}}[1/p],A)
. On the
other hand, multiplication by
p^{e}
is zero on
A
, so it is also
zero on
\operatorname{Hom}({\mathbb{Z}}[1/p],A)
and
\operatorname{Ext}({\mathbb{Z}}[1/p],A)
. These facts can
only be compatible if
\operatorname{Hom}({\mathbb{Z}}[1/p],A)=\operatorname{Ext}({\mathbb{Z}}[1/p],A)=0
.
∎

Definition 5.7
.

We write

Definition 5.9
.

(a)

We say that a spectrum

Lemma 5.10
.

If

-complete.

Definition 5.11
.

We write

For any spectrum

The first two sequences are special cases of

.

(a)

\widehat{L}_{p}X

(d)

(a)

Lemma 5.24
.

Suppose we have an exact sequence
A\to B\xrightarrow{f}C\to D
in which

and

is finitely generated over
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}

is finitely generated over
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
, and

L_{1}(C)=0
.

We have short exact sequences
\ker(f)\rightarrowtail B\twoheadrightarrow\operatorname{img}(f)
and

\operatorname{img}(f)\rightarrowtail C\twoheadrightarrow\operatorname{cok}(f)
in which
\ker(f)
is a quotient of
A

and
\operatorname{cok}(f)
is a subgroup of
D
, so both are finite. If
U
is
finite then it is easy to see that
L_{1}(U)=0
and
L_{0}(U)
is the

p
-torsion summand in

. We have a six term exact sequence

L_{1}(\ker(f))\to L_{1}(B)\to L_{1}(\operatorname{img}(f))\to L_{0}(\ker(f))\to L_{0}(B)\to L_{0}(\operatorname{img}(f)).

We have
L_{1}(B)=0
by assumption, so
L_{1}(\operatorname{img}(f))
embeds in the
finite
p

. However,

L_{1}(\operatorname{img}(f))=\operatorname{Hom}({\mathbb{Z}}/p^{\infty},\operatorname{img}(f))

p

p
is injective

We also see from the above sequence that

is a quotient
of

. Next, we have

L_{1}(\operatorname{img}(f))\rightarrowtail L_{1}(C)\to L_{1}(\operatorname{cok}(f))\to L_{0}(\operatorname{img}(f))\to L_{0}(C)\to L_{0}(\operatorname{cok}(f)).

that

, so we have
L_{1}(C)=0
. Thus, the above

L_{0}(\operatorname{img}(f))
is
finitely generated over
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
and

is a finite abelian

p

is finitely generated over

{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}

Remark 5.30
.

Just as in Remark

, the subcategory
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{p}\subseteq{\mathcal{B}}
is
not closed under infinite coproducts, but
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{p}
has its own
coproducts, given by taking the coproduct in

and then applying
the functor
\widehat{L}_{p}
. An object

is (by definition)

sends these adjusted
coproducts to direct sums. Similarly,
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{p}
has a closed symmetric
monoidal structure, with the monoidal unit being

, monoidal

and the internal
mapping objects being the same as in
{\mathcal{B}}
. Thus, an object

is strongly dualisable if and only if the natural map

\widehat{L}_{p}(\underline{\operatorname{Hom}}(X,S_{p})\otimes X)\to\underline{\operatorname{Hom}}(X,X)

Proposition 5.32
.

Let
X
be a bounded below
p
-complete spectrum. Then the following
are equivalent:

(p)

X
lies in the thick subcategory generated by
S/p

(q)

X

(r)

\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{p}

Proof.

If we have a cofibration

, Definition 4.1.4]
.

Our proof, which will be given after some preparatory results, is
closely related to the approach of Neeman
[
Nee97
]
. He considers
a triangulated category
{\mathcal{S}}
with only countably many morphisms, and
proves a representability result in that context. In our case the
relevant category is
{\mathcal{M}}_{p}^{d}
. Every object here is isomorphic to

FX
for some finite spectrum
X
, which implies that there are
only countably many isomorphism classes. Morphism sets may be
uncountable, but they are always finitely generated modules over

{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
. We will show that this is enough to replace one step in
Neeman’s argument, and the other steps can be used without further
changes, although we will present them in a somewhat different way.

Note that homology functors on
{\mathcal{M}}_{p}
are equivalent to exact
functors
{\mathcal{M}}_{p}^{d}\to\operatorname{Ab}
, by
[
HPS97
, Corollary 2.3.11]
.
Moreover, the duality functor
D(X)=\underline{\operatorname{Hom}}(X,S_{p})
gives an equivalence

({\mathcal{M}}_{p}^{d})^{\operatorname{op}}\to{\mathcal{M}}_{p}^{d}
. Thus, everything can be reinterpreted in
terms of exact functors
({\mathcal{M}}_{p}^{d})^{\operatorname{op}}\to\operatorname{Ab}
.

Definition 6.20
.

(a)

We write
{\mathcal{A}}
for the category of additive functors

({\mathcal{M}}_{p}^{d})^{\operatorname{op}}\to\operatorname{Ab}
.

(b)

For each
U\in{\mathcal{M}}_{p}
we define
H^{U}\in{\mathcal{A}}

by
H^{U}(X)=[X,U]={\mathcal{M}}_{p}(X,U)=H_{U}(D(X))
.

(c)

We say that an object
P\in{\mathcal{M}}_{p}
is
free
if it
can be expressed as a coproduct
\bigoplus_{i\in I}U_{i}
with

U_{i}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{p}^{d}
, and note that in this case we have

H^{P}\simeq\bigoplus_{i\in I}H^{U_{i}}
. We say that a functor

F\in{\mathcal{A}}
is
free
if it is isomorphic to
H^{P}
for some free
object
P
. We write
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}
for the full subcategory of free
objects in
{\mathcal{M}}_{p}
, and
{\mathcal{P}}
for the category of free functors.

(d)

We write
{\mathcal{H}}
for the full subcategory of exact functors
in
{\mathcal{A}}
.

(e)

We write
{\mathcal{P}}_{\omega}
for the full
subcategory of
{\mathcal{P}}
consisting of objects that can be expressed as

\bigoplus_{i\in I}H^{U_{i}}
for some countable set
I
.

(f)

We say that a functor
F\in{\mathcal{A}}
is countably generated if it
admits an epimorphism from some object in
{\mathcal{P}}_{\omega}
, and we write

{\mathcal{A}}_{\omega}
for the category of countably generated functors.

Lemma 6.21
.

If
P\in{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}_{p}
and
X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{p}
then the natural map

{\mathcal{M}}_{p}(P,X)\to{\mathcal{A}}(H^{P},H^{X})
is an isomorphism. Thus the functor

P\mapsto H^{P}
gives an equivalence
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}\to{\mathcal{P}}
.

Proof.

We can express
P
as
\bigoplus_{i\in I}U_{i}
with
U_{i}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{p}^{d}
,
and then
{\mathcal{M}}_{p}(P,X)=\prod_{i}{\mathcal{M}}_{p}(U_{i},X)
and

H^{P}=\bigoplus_{i}H^{U_{i}}
so
{\mathcal{A}}(H^{P},H^{X})=\prod_{i}{\mathcal{A}}(H^{U_{i}},H^{X})
.
Moreover, the Yoneda Lemma gives

{\mathcal{A}}(H^{U_{i}},H^{X})=H^{X}(U_{i})={\mathcal{M}}_{p}(U_{i},X)
. The claim is clear from
this.
∎

Lemma 6.22
.

Given a short exact sequence
F\rightarrowtail G\twoheadrightarrow H
in
{\mathcal{A}}
, if two of the
functors
F
,
G
and
H
lie in
{\mathcal{H}}
, then so does the third.

Proof.

Given a cofibre sequence
X=(X_{0}\to X_{1}\to X_{2}\to\Sigma X)
we can
extend the sequence infinitely in both directions in the usual way
and then apply
F
to get a chain complex which we call
F(X)
. By
definition,
F
lies in
{\mathcal{H}}
iff
F(X)
is acyclic for all
X
. A
short exact seequence of functors
F\rightarrowtail G\twoheadrightarrow H
gives a short exact
sequence
F(X)\rightarrowtail G(X)\twoheadrightarrow H(X)
of chain complexes and thus a long
exact sequence of homology groups, using which we see that if two of

F(X)
,
G(X)
and
H(X)
is acyclic, then the third is also. The
claim is clear from this.
∎

.

Definition 6.24
.

Proposition
6.17
tells us that every object in

{\mathcal{M}}_{p}^{d}
is isomorphic to
P(X)
for some finite spectrum
X
, and
it follows that
{\mathcal{M}}_{p}^{d}
has only countably many isomorphism
classes. We choose a list
T_{0},T_{1},\dotsc
containing precisely one
representative of each isomorphism class, starting with
T_{0}=0
and

T_{1}=S_{p}
. We write
{\mathcal{S}}
for the full subcategory of
{\mathcal{M}}_{p}^{d}
with
objects
\{T_{i}\;|\;i\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
, so the inclusion
{\mathcal{S}}\to{\mathcal{M}}_{p}^{d}
is an
equivalence.

Definition 6.25
.

Consider a functor
H\in{\mathcal{H}}
. We define
\operatorname{el}(H)
to be the category
of pairs
(X,x)
with
X\in{\mathcal{S}}
and
x\in H(X)
. The morphisms from

(X,x)
to
(Y,y)
are the maps
f\in{\mathcal{S}}(X,Y)
with
f^{*}(y)=x
.
Recall that a subcategory
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\operatorname{el}(H)
is said to be

filtered
if it has properties (a) to (c) below; we will say
that it is
well-filtered
if it also has property (d).

(a)

{\mathcal{X}}
is not empty

(b)

For all
P_{0},P_{1}\in{\mathcal{X}}
there is an object
P_{2}\in{\mathcal{X}}

and morphisms
P_{0}\xrightarrow{}P_{2}\xleftarrow{}P_{1}
.

(c)

For all pairs of parallel morphisms
f,g\colon P_{0}\to P_{1}
in

{\mathcal{X}}
there is a morphism
h\colon P_{1}\to P_{2}
in
{\mathcal{X}}
with
hf=hg

(d)

For all
(X,x)\in{\mathcal{X}}
and
W\in{\mathcal{S}}
and

f\colon W\to X
with
f^{*}(x)=0
, there exists
g\colon(X,x)\to(Y,y)
in

{\mathcal{X}}
with
gf=0
.

Proposition 6.26
.

Any countable subcategory of
\operatorname{el}(H)
is contained in a countable
well-filtered subcategory.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}_{0}
be a countable subcategory. We define an increasing
sequence of countable subcategories
{\mathcal{X}}_{n}
as follows. Given

{\mathcal{X}}_{n}
, we define
{\mathcal{X}}_{n+1}
by starting with
{\mathcal{X}}_{n}
and adding
extra objects and morphisms:

(a)

For each pair of objects
(X_{0},x_{0})
and
(X_{1},x_{1})
in

{\mathcal{X}}_{n}
, we choose a coproduct diagram

X_{0}\xrightarrow{i_{0}}X_{2}\xleftarrow{i_{1}}X_{1}
in
{\mathcal{S}}
. As
H
is
cohomological, there is a unique element
x_{2}\in H(X_{2})
with

i_{0}^{*}(x_{2})=x_{0}
and
i_{1}^{*}(x_{2})=x_{1}
. We add the object

(X_{2},x_{2})
and the morphisms

(X_{0},x_{0})\xrightarrow{i_{0}}(X_{2},x_{2})\xleftarrow{i_{1}}(X_{1},x_{1})
to
{\mathcal{X}}_{n+1}
.

(b)

For each pair of objects
parallel morphisms
f,g\colon(X_{0},x_{0})\to(X_{1},x_{1})
in
{\mathcal{X}}_{n}
, we
choose a cofibre
h\colon X_{1}\to X_{2}
for
g-f
in
{\mathcal{S}}
. As
H

is cohomological, we can choose
x_{2}\in H(X_{2})
with

h^{*}(x_{2})=x_{1}
. We add the object
(X_{2},x_{2})
and the morphism

h\colon(X_{1},x_{1})\to(X_{2},x_{2})
to
{\mathcal{X}}_{n+1}
.

(c)

For each
W\in{\mathcal{S}}
and
(X,x)\in{\mathcal{X}}_{n}
, we note
that
[W,X]
is a finitely generated
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
-module, so the same is
true for the submodule
\{m\in[W,X]\;|\;m^{*}(x)=0\}
. We choose
generators
m_{0},\dotsc,m_{r}
for this submodule, and combine them to
give a morphism
m\colon\bigoplus_{i<r}W\to X
with
m^{*}(x)=0
. We
then choose a cofibre
f\colon X\to Y
in
{\mathcal{S}}
, and an element

y\in H(Y)
with
f^{*}(y)=x
. We add the object
(Y,y)
and the
morphism
f\colon(X,x)\to(Y,y)
to
{\mathcal{X}}_{n+1}
.

(d)

Finally, we add in all composites of composable chains of
morphisms that have already been included.

Standard arguments show that
{\mathcal{X}}_{n+1}
is still countable, as is
the union
{\mathcal{X}}_{\infty}=\bigcup_{n}{\mathcal{X}}_{n}
, and
{\mathcal{X}}_{\infty}
is also
well-filtered.
∎

Corollary 6.27
.

If
H\in{\mathcal{H}}
and
F\leq H
is countably generated, then there exists

G
with
F\leq G\leq H
and a short exact sequence

0\to P\xrightarrow{d}P\xrightarrow{f}G\to 0
with
P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\omega}
. In particular,

G
lies in
{\mathcal{H}}
and is representable.

Proof.

Choose a countable family
{\mathcal{X}}
of objects
(X,x)\in\operatorname{el}(F)\subseteq\operatorname{el}(H)

such that the resulting maps
H^{X}\to F
are jointly surjective.
Consider this as a category with only identity morphisms, and enlarge
it to a countable well-filtered subcategory
{\mathcal{Y}}\subseteq\operatorname{el}(H)
. This is
in particular filtered, so we can choose a cofinal sequence

(X_{0},x_{0})\to(X_{1},x_{1})\to(X_{2},x_{2})\to\dotsb
. Put

P=\bigoplus_{n}H^{X_{n}}
and let
f\colon P\to H
be the map given by
x_{n}

on the
n
th summand. Let
d\colon P\to P
be the identity minus the
shift, so
fd=0
, so
f
factors through a map
f\colon\operatorname{cok}(d)\to H

(with
\operatorname{cok}(d)=\operatornamewithlimits{\underset{\longrightarrow}{lim}}_{n}H^{X_{n}}
). Let
G
be the image of
f
. As
our sequence is cofinal and the category
{\mathcal{Y}}
contains
{\mathcal{X}}
, we see
that
F\leq G
. All that is left is to show that the map

\operatorname{cok}(d)\to H
is injective. Suppose we have
W\in{\mathcal{S}}
and

m\in\operatorname{cok}(d)(W)
mapping to zero in
H(W)
. The element
m
can be
represented by a map
m\colon W\to X_{n}
for some
n
, and the image of
this in
H(W)
is
m^{*}(x_{n})
, so
m^{*}(x_{n})=0
. As
{\mathcal{Y}}
is
well-filtered, there is a morphism
p\colon(X_{n},x_{n})\to(Y,y)
in
{\mathcal{Y}}

with
pm=0
. As our sequence is cofinal, there is a morphism from

(Y,y)
to
(X_{k},x_{k})
for some
k
such that the composite

(X_{n},x_{n})\to(Y,y)\to(X_{k},x_{k})
is just the given map in the
sequence. This shows that
m
maps to zero in the group

\operatorname{cok}(d)(W)
, as required.
∎

Proposition 6.28
.

For any
H\in{\mathcal{H}}
there is a short exact sequence

0\to Q\xrightarrow{d}P\xrightarrow{f}H\to 0
with
P,Q\in{\mathcal{P}}
.

Proof.

We can choose an ordinal
\kappa
and a family of objects

U_{\alpha}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{p}^{d}
and elements
u_{\alpha}\in H(U_{\alpha})
for
\alpha<\kappa
such
that the resulting map
\bigoplus_{\alpha<\kappa}H^{U_{\alpha}}\to H
is an
epimorphism. We aim to construct the following:

•

For all
\alpha<\kappa
, objects
Q_{\alpha},P_{\alpha}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\omega}
,
which in turn give objects
Q_{<\alpha}=\bigoplus_{\beta<\alpha}Q_{\beta}

and
P_{<\alpha}=\bigoplus_{\beta<\alpha}P_{\beta}
.

•

Monomorphisms
d_{\alpha}\colon Q_{<\alpha}\to P_{<\alpha}
for all
\alpha<\kappa
,
with
d_{\alpha}|_{Q_{<\beta}}=d_{\beta}
for all
\beta\leq\alpha
.

•

Maps

for all

, such that

, and

for all

.

Suppose we have constructed all this for all

. If
\lambda
is
a limit ordinal, then

is the colimit of the objects

for

, and similarly for

, so there is a

and

. Suppose instead

. As

is exact. As
\operatorname{img}(f_{\mu})

and

are exact, it follows that the quotient

is also exact. Using

we can choose a left exact sequence

. As

is an epimorphism and

is projective in

we can choose

. As

we see
