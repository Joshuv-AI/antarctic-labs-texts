Exploring the role of high-fidelity simulation for pre-foundation assistantship students to improve preparedness for practice
Authors: Amber Jennifer Sara Moore, Kallpana Dhas, Dilroshini Karunaratne, Krishna Dasigan, Ailie Brennan-Davies, Ankita Sahni, James Patrick, Lisa Saleh Rasool, Syeda Hridi, Sef Carter, Shiras Patel, Lianne Fakes, Mary Kelada, Ammar Shohayeb, Ana Baptista, Brent Bartholomew
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn the UK, new medical graduates frequently feel underprepared in managing acutely unwell patients. Clinical experiential learning gained, can be opportunistic and variable. Simulation-based medical education (SBME) has previously been shown to improve preparedness for practice. At Imperial College London, a half day high-fidelity simulation course for final year students during their pre-foundation assistantship (PFA) placement was set up focusing on the assessment and management of acutely unwell patients including appropriate escalation and use of the adult life support algorithm.MethodsUsing student self-reported questionnaires, this study aimed to explore if there was any added value in implementing a high-fidelity simulation in preparing PFA students for Foundation Year 1 (FY1) training and to identify factors influencing effectiveness.ResultsPre-session and post-session questionnaires completed by students demonstrated an improvement in self-reported confidence and preparedness for practice. Median confidence in diagnosing and managing medical emergencies and in using the Advanced Life Support algorithm improved by 1 point on a 5-point Likert scale. Students also felt more prepared for Foundation training and managing an acute medical situation with more distant supervision, with median preparedness and distance of supervision respectively improving by 1 point. Key themes from student questionnaires suggested hands-on learning, debriefing and faculty continuity were beneficial aspects of the course.ConclusionsHigh fidelity simulation delivered in the pre-foundation period improved the self-reported confidence and preparedness of students for practice, aligning with previous research supporting the effectiveness of SBME to improve preparedness for practice. It adds to existing literature regarding the added value of SBME incorporation during the pre-foundation period and which features optimise the simulation learning environment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08477-w.

Background

The transition from medical school to clinical practice represents a critical juncture in a doctor’s career. For newly qualified resident doctors, this shift involves navigating increased responsibility, high-pressure situations, and the need to provide safe and effective patient care [1]. In the United Kingdom (UK), the persistent rise in the number of emergency admissions has added to the current pressures faced by the National Health Service (NHS). Between August 2011 to August 2019, the number of emergency admissions via A&E increased by 20%. Although the number of admissions fell slightly during the COVID-19 pandemic, numbers have now returned to pre-pandemic levels [2]. This reality underscores the urgency of preparing medical students and pre-Foundation doctors for this transition, ensuring they are both competent and confident in their abilities.

Preparedness for clinical practice is a multi-faceted concept encompassing capability and competence (incorporating knowledge and skills), as well as professionalism, resilience and self-confidence [3, 4]. Outcomes for Graduates, published by the General Medical Council (GMC), provides a set of expectations for Foundation Year 1 (FY1) doctors. Within this document, they state that recognising a deteriorating patient, diagnosing and managing acute clinical presentations and making appropriate clinical judgements are all essential skills for new doctors [5]. However, many FY1s feel underprepared in these key areas [6].

Imperial College London is a top-ranking university for medicine and its School of Medicine (ICSM) has approximately 2200 students, therefore providing a substantial cohort of resident doctors for the NHS workforce [7, 8]. Whilst students from ICSM perform well academically [8], the GMC National Survey in 2021 revealed that in comparison to the national average, ICSM graduates felt less prepared for their first FY1 job. Only 62.7% felt that their skills in the early management of acutely ill patients were adequate, in comparison to the national benchmark of 70.2% [9]. This discrepancy highlighted the need for the medical school to improve preparedness of final year medical students for FY1 training.

Meaningful patient contact in undergraduate education has previously been reported as desired by newly qualified UK doctors to ease the transition from medical student to resident doctor [10]. Greater clinical exposure is a growing desire in other countries (including the United States and Canada) to better prepare medical students in their transition to junior doctors [11]. Since the publication of Tomorrow’s Doctors in 2009 [12], student assistantships have been introduced by many UK medical schools into the period prior to commencing Foundation training to facilitate student participation in junior doctor responsibilities under supervision. At Imperial College London, following final year exams, the pre-foundation assistantship (PFA) clinical placement helps overcome these challenges by focusing on helping students transition to FY1 clinical practice. However, the clinical, experiential learning gained is often opportunistic and variable, with many factors affecting learning outcomes including student confidence in their role, team interactions and integration as well as environment familiarity and demands [13–15]. This can lead to gaps in some students’ perceived preparedness especially in scenarios such as working on-call or managing acutely unwell patients that rely heavily on clinical, experiential learning and place more responsibility on the newly qualified doctor but are also areas where students may receive less frequent exposure [6, 16]. A systematic review performed following the introduction of Tomorrow’s Doctors 2009 supported this. It reported that students felt underprepared in safe prescribing, clinical reasoning and managing acutely unwell patients [15]. Simulation-based medical education (SBME) has emerged as a promising intervention to help address these challenges, bridging the gap between theoretical knowledge and practical application. By recreating clinical scenarios, simulation allows learners to develop diagnostic reasoning, refine communication and teamwork, and practice technical skills within a controllable learning environment [17]. These interventions not only foster competence but also provide a safe space for building confidence and adaptability in the face of clinical complexities [18, 19]. Self-confidence is an essential aspect of perceived preparedness for practice [4]. It can influence behaviour and subsequently learning [20]. Recent studies have also shown that high-fidelity simulation enhances learners’ confidence, satisfaction and clinical performance across different nursing and medical training scenarios [21, 22]. This holds true in research conducted in multiple countries - for example, research from Palestine demonstrated significant improvements in students’ self-confidence and practical skills following simulation-based training in general nursing, mental health, paediatrics and airway management [23–25]. These findings further support the growing evidence that simulation provides a safe and effective learning environment that helps bridge the gap between theory and clinical practice.

Simulation courses designed for both final-year medical students and pre-foundation doctors have gained traction as a strategy to enhance preparedness for clinical roles. These courses simulate essential aspects of FY1 responsibilities, such as prescribing, managing acute scenarios, and patient handovers, offering participants an opportunity to preview and prepare for the challenges ahead [26–32]. A recent meta-analysis showed improved confidence, knowledge and simulated performance of final year medical students and newly qualified doctors following a simulation of a deteriorating patient [22]. However, despite the growing popularity of pre-foundation placements, evidence of effectiveness and added value of a simulation course in improving perceptions of preparedness during this immediate pre-foundation period remains limited. Many simulation courses have been undertaken during the final year of medical school or during Foundation training [22, 26, 27, 29, 30, 32] and simulation during the pre-foundation period has mainly focused on prescribing, ward round tasks and handovers [28, 31]. However, managing acutely unwell patients is a key area of reported lack of preparedness for newly qualified doctors and also needs to be addressed as a safety critical task [15, 33, 34]. Simulation addressing managing the deteriorating patient during the pre-foundation period needs greater exploration as this may provide an ideal time to consolidate medical school knowledge prior to starting Foundation training. Medical students at this stage have fewer clinical demands and less mental exhaustion which may improve learning from simulation opportunities [35, 36]. There is also unclear evidence regarding which aspects of simulation create an optimal learning environment in this setting [22]. Exploring the student voices on the impact of simulation and its key aspects, can give useful insight into the learning process and how to further support students’ self-confidence and preparedness for practice [37].

This study thus intended to further investigate the role of a high-fidelity simulation course described below on preparedness for practice and the factors influencing the effectiveness of high-fidelity simulation educational activities, investigated by students’ self-reported voices.

The ‘acute simulation and life support’ course

All final year medical students (n = 301) from ICSM were asked to take part in the ‘acute simulation and life support’ course which took place during their PFA. The multi-station simulation course was held within the Undergraduate Department at Chelsea and Westminster Hospital between April and June 2023. There were 13 half-day sessions in total. Students were allocated into groups of five to six, with each student attending one half-day session. Allocations were based on student availability and students from the same PFA base hospital were grouped together if feasible.

Course design

Students received a pre-course handbook which included the presentation, investigation and management plan for 20 common acute conditions. These conditions were identified in an audit of the top 20 acute admissions across Chelsea and Westminster Hospital NHS Foundation Trust (CWHNFT) within the previous year (Williams M, de Rohan C, McLaughlin E, Khinder S, Moore A, Karunaratne D, et al. Targeting simulation teaching for final year medical students through identifying the 20 most common acute conditions and presentations that lead to hospital admission. 2022, Unpublished) to improve relevance, engagement and therefore learning from the content [38, 39]. This covered the content to be discussed on the acute simulation course.

The course itself included four stations: Status Epilepticus, Non-ST Elevation Myocardial Infarction (NSTEMI), Infective Exacerbation of Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (IECOPD) and Sepsis with Acute Kidney Injury and Hyperkalaemia. These scenarios were chosen to each focus on different parts of the Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure (ABCDE) assessment and all except Status Epilepticus were part of the 20 commonest reasons for acute admission within the Trust. Status Epilepticus was included due to the time-critical nature of management [40] and ongoing variability in management in UK hospitals with little recent improvement [41]. Facilitators and training

Faculty included undergraduate teaching fellows from the students’ base hospitals as well as resident and consultant doctors from CWHNFT. Facilitators introduced and guided each scenario, and led each post-simulation debrief. Facilitators rotated through each scenario with their allocated group of students. Simulated patients remained in the same scenario throughout.

Facilitators and simulated patients underwent pre-course training including documents to review and a 30-minute briefing on the day of the course.

Station setup

For each station, students were presented with a situation and then 2–4 students would enter the scenario, assessing and initiating appropriate management for the patient, including appropriate escalation (see Fig. 1). Each student had a pre-allocated role including leading, history taking, ABCDE examining, or undertaking interventions. To aid the subsequent debrief, 1–2 students would observe and complete a checklist of observed tasks. Students were not graded during the scenarios. After 15 minutes, the simulated patient would deteriorate to the point of cardiac arrest, prompting students to initiate the ALS algorithm [43]. Patient actors would switch with a cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) manikin [44] for students to practice chest compressions and defibrillation. Quality of CPR was recorded via the qCPR app [45]. All stations were immediately followed by a 15-minute debrief with the facilitator and simulated patient.

The scenario setup aimed to improve the fidelity of the simulation, enhance authenticity and student emersion in the scenario [38]. This brings the scenarios closer to experiential learning. Although simulation is not a classical example of experiential learning, it provides a psychologically safe environment to practice, reflect, debrief and learn [38, 46]. Despite the use of CPR manikins with no real-time feedback lowering physical fidelity during ALS management, it was felt that the use of simulated patients for the rest of the scenario, real-time observations and attempts to re-create a clinical environment had a high psychological fidelity and would greatly improve engagement, arguably more important for learning [47, 48].

The primary aim for this study was to explore if there was any added value in implementing a high-fidelity simulation in preparing PFA students for FY1. As a secondary aim, the factors influencing the effectiveness of high-fidelity simulation were explored. To explore these aims, using the Kirkpatrick model [49], the following data was initially collected:

Questionnaire design, validity and reliability

To ensure content validity, the questionnaire format and some items were drawn from previously validated questionnaires given to final year medical students and newly qualified doctors to investigate the impact of simulation courses on preparedness for practice [26, 52]. Some questions differed in terms of reference to self-reported preparedness or confidence, however there is some evidence to suggest medical students may interpret preparedness as confidence [53]. Content was also derived from the PFA curriculum and the GMC’s Outcomes for Graduates [5]. To determine face validity, following development, our questionnaire was scrutinised by undergraduate medical educators from Imperial College London and lay individuals. The questionnaire was found to be satisfactory in assessing perceived preparedness for practice. Internal reliability was tested using Cronbach’s alpha following data collection (see Results section).

Participant recruitment and procedure

All ICSM PFA students attending the course were eligible to take part in this study. Students were informed of the study in a pre-course email briefing and invited to provide voluntary, informed consent at the beginning of the course. Students were informed that they could voluntarily withdraw from the study at any point and of the process for doing this.

The simulation scenarios, as presented, were run for the first time at the time of this study. Therefore, no power calculations were carried out. Measures were taken to facilitate a high response rate (as detailed above). All data was pseudoanonymised and transcribed into Microsoft Excel by researchers with identifiers stored securely and separately from the data. Verification of data integrity was undertaken by a separate researcher.

Data analysis

Missing data was managed by the deletion of the relevant case from the data set only for the specific analysis where data was missing, therefore maximising the data available for each specific analysis. Quantitative data analysis was undertaken using ‘IBM SPSS Statistics’, version 30 [54]. Data are presented as medians with inter-quartile ranges where applicable. Questionnaire data were analysed using Wilcoxon signed-rank test to compare pre-course and post-course scores. p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Standardised effect size (r) was also calculated.

Qualitative data were analysed using thematic analysis [55] to identify key themes relating to students’ learning experiences from the course, their preparedness for practice and course factors affecting this. An inductive approach was used to identify themes. Four authors coded all qualitative data. To minimise bias, regular research meetings were held to ensure consistency in data interpretation. During meetings, samples of independent coding and sub-theme development would be reviewed by all authors present and disagreements would be resolved through discussion. Two of these authors subsequently reviewed all codes and developed the initial themes which were reviewed by all authors. A consensual approach from all authors was used in regards to the narrative explored.

Results

Respondent profile

301 students undertook the PFA placement with 256 students (85%) attending the simulation course. 255 students consented to taking part in the project. In general, there was reasonable spread in attendance from the different hospital sites and across different course dates (see Appendix C).

Questionnaires: quantitative analysis

252 paired pre-course and post-course questionnaires were collected (98.4% response rate, representing 83.7% of the PFA placement cohort). Cronbach’s alpha was 0.855, yielding a high level of internal consistency.

Students varied in their previous clinical and simulated experience in managing acutely unwell patients (see Appendix C) with a median number of previous clinical and simulated experiences being 1 and 3 respectively. This is useful to staff members to understand what constitutes students’ previous exposure to certain clinical situations.

Student self-reported confidence in different scenarios was compared before and after the simulation course and significance was assessed using the Wilcoxon Signed Rank Test. In all scenarios, there was a significant increase in student confidence in managing acute clinical scenarios following the simulation course. There remained a significant difference in student confidence when analysis was undertaken with outliers removed from the dataset.

In general, students also reported that they were satisfied and engaged by the pre-course material, and would recommend the material to a friend.

Questionnaires: qualitative analysis

Thematic analysis of the students’ post-simulation free-text responses to open-ended questionnaire questions revealed several key themes highlighting their perceptions of the simulation course and its impact on their preparedness for clinical practice. Below, the most prominent themes are outlined, supported by representative quotes from students.

Usefulness & effectiveness of the simulation course

Students widely regarded the simulation as beneficial for their learning, with many noting that the structured approach reinforced key concepts. Participants found the pre-course materials, particularly the handbook and videos, to be valuable resources in consolidating theoretical knowledge before engaging in practical scenarios. One participant commented:

“The handbook was very well-structured, providing clear summaries of conditions we need to know.”

“The handbook was very well-structured, providing clear summaries of conditions we need to know.”

Similarly, the practical component of the simulation was commended for enhancing clinical reasoning and decision-making skills, with a student remarking:

“It was great to apply theoretical knowledge to real-world scenarios in a safe environment.”

“It was great to apply theoretical knowledge to real-world scenarios in a safe environment.”

Increased confidence & preparedness

Many students expressed that they felt more comfortable managing unwell patients after the course. One participant stated:

“I now feel much more confident assessing and escalating deteriorating patients, something I found quite intimidating before.”

“I now feel much more confident assessing and escalating deteriorating patients, something I found quite intimidating before.”

Another student highlighted the direct relevance of the simulation to FY1 responsibilities:

“This was the most useful session we had before starting FY1—finally, something that actually prepares us for the job.”

“This was the most useful session we had before starting FY1—finally, something that actually prepares us for the job.”

Realistic simulation & hands-on learning

The realism of the scenarios was highly praised, with students stating that the cases closely mirrored real-world clinical encounters. Many felt that the hands-on practice was invaluable, particularly in applying the ABCDE approach:

“It was so helpful practicing the ABCDE assessment under pressure—it made me realise how important it is to stay structured in an emergency.”

“It was so helpful practicing the ABCDE assessment under pressure—it made me realise how important it is to stay structured in an emergency.”

“The scenarios felt real, which made me more aware of the challenges I might face in actual clinical practice.”

“The scenarios felt real, which made me more aware of the challenges I might face in actual clinical practice.”

However, a small number of students suggested that greater fidelity in equipment or patient deterioration cues could further enhance immersion.

Importance of feedback & debriefing

Participants valued the structured debriefing sessions, and felt they provided an opportunity for reflection and learning. Many emphasised that constructive feedback was instrumental in identifying areas for improvement. One student noted:

“The debrief was just as important as the scenario itself. It made me reflect on my thought process and highlighted what I need to work on.”

“The debrief was just as important as the scenario itself. It made me reflect on my thought process and highlighted what I need to work on.”

Others mentioned that peer discussions during debriefing facilitated insightful learning experiences, with one stating:

“Hearing others’ approaches to the same scenario made me realise there’s more than one way to manage a situation.”

“Hearing others’ approaches to the same scenario made me realise there’s more than one way to manage a situation.”

Facilitator continuity & teaching quality

Some students appreciated having the same facilitator throughout different scenarios, as it allowed for personalised feedback and progressive skill development. One student stated:

“Having the same facilitator across different scenarios helped them see our progression and give more tailored feedback.”

“Having the same facilitator across different scenarios helped them see our progression and give more tailored feedback.”

Facilitators who were engaging and supportive were frequently mentioned as enhancing the learning environment:

Discussion

The findings of this study provide evidence to suggest the added value of a standardised pre-foundation high-fidelity simulation course in improving self-confidence and preparedness of final year medical students for FY1 Training. The existing literature supports the integration of SBME into medical students’ clinical rotations [19, 22, 56]. For studies focusing on preparedness for practice, it is difficult to directly compare the magnitude of perceived preparedness between studies due to differing methodologies and the stage of training when simulation interventions were implemented [22]. However, the previous limited evidence for SBME in the immediate pre-foundation period means that this study adds to the literature, demonstrating the benefit of simulation on improving self-confidence and preparedness for practice even during the period just prior to FY1 training.

However, similar to many previous studies [22], this study focuses on self-reported outcomes. Exploring the students’ voices is essential to optimising their learning opportunities [37]. Self-confidence is an important aspect of preparedness for practice, and students’ own perceptions will have an impact on their behaviours and learning [4, 20]. However, self-confidence can’t be extrapolated consistently to competence, and improved perceived confidence and preparedness doesn’t consistently demonstrate improved simulated or real-life competence [19, 57, 58]. Previous reviews have demonstrated a limited ability of physician or student self-assessment to accurately correspond with objective competence, though, it should be noted that differing methodology of the studies included in these reviews may have contributed to inconclusive conclusions [57, 58]. Moreover, the interaction between subjective confidence and objective competence can be complex. In a study of 251 medical students in Brazil undertaking a multiple-choice examination, over-confidence was frequently seen in students with lower scores, whilst under-confidence was frequently seen in high-achievers [59]. Although some studies have been able to demonstrate the benefit in simulation through assessment of knowledge and simulated skills, very few studies have been able to demonstrate an improvement in the learner’s performance within the workplace [22] due to the presence of many confounding factors (a common challenge in SBME [17, 22]). For example, mini-CEX data would likely have been confounded by additional experiential learning from the PFA placement. A sample of students taken from the same medical school during a 3-week PFA placement in 2020 (prior to the introduction of this simulation) identified 79% felt confident in managing an acutely unwell patient after the assistantship compared to 18% before the assistantship [60]. A similar interim Foundation Year 1 placement at a South East England teaching hospital also showed an improved confidence of 0.32 (on a 5-point scale) in managing an acutely unwell patient [61].

In this study, a comparison group of students who didn’t take part in the simulation would have improved validity of this study’s findings, and may have provided further evidence that simulation may improve preparedness for practice in the pre-foundation period. This was unfortunately not possible due to the ethical need to allow equal learning opportunities for students in the cohort and therefore equal access to the simulation course. Only 15% of PFA students chose not to attend the simulation course and so recruiting an adequate comparison group may have been challenging [62].

Even if additional data was incorporated, this study would still only provide a short-term view of preparedness for practice. Preparedness is not unidirectional nor linear [15] and although it is important that students feel prepared prior to starting post-graduate clinical training, their reflections with hindsight following starting FY1 is also invaluable. This has been explored previously with other acute care simulation interventions [26, 27, 32, 53]. Additionally, although some demographic data was collected, there is some evidence to suggest ethnicity and personality traits may influence student perceived preparedness and this may also have influenced results [15]. There is some evidence to suggest gender doesn’t affect perceived preparedness [15].

Authentic, clinical experience is invaluable in developing contextual professional attributes and helping students become more central (rather than peripheral) participants within their clinical community [13, 14, 63]. However, the variable occurrence of some clinical learning opportunities can result in students feeling underprepared for scenarios which they are less frequently exposured to and where greater responsibility may be required such as managing acutely unwell patients [6, 16]. Therefore, the consistent learning environment of simulation can supplement clinical experiences in improving perceived preparedness for practice [22, 64].

Hands-on learning, debriefing and faculty continuity were highlighted in this study as factors influencing the effectiveness of the simulation course. In 2005, a systematic review covering articles involving high-fidelity simulation from 1969 to 2003 identified the provision of feedback as the most important factor highlighted in 47% of journals, with repeated practice and incorporation into the curriculum being identified as other key features. However, out of the articles reviewed, research quality was generally poor and only 20% stated clear, likely true outcomes [18]. Hands-on learning, in the context of experiential learning is explicitly emphasised in individual articles such as a UK based study reviewing the effect of bleep and ward-based simulation courses focusing on preparedness for practice [26]. However, it is frequently only implied in recent review articles [18, 65]. A recent review article attempted to identify the best features of effective simulation for preparing final-year students and FY1 doctors for managing acutely unwell patients [22]. Although favoured features could be identified, the inconsistent methodologies between included articles contributed to full analysis not taking place. Faculty continuity is less explicitly highlighted in similar simulation courses [18, 22, 38, 65] which adds to previous research regarding features to optimise the learning environment in high-fidelity simulations for preparedness for practice.

Hands-on learning praised by students likely related to the practicality and authenticity of a scenario providing an immersive learning experience that closely mimicked real-life clinical scenarios. Authenticity in simulation enhances the fidelity of the training environment, improving engagement and potentially making it easier for students to transfer the skills learned in simulation to actual clinical practice [38, 46]. By simulating the pressures and complexities of actual clinical practice, students can gain confidence and competence in handling emergency situations and routine procedures through experience [18, 26, 56, 64]. Furthermore, the authenticity of a simulator helps establish a “fiction contract”, where participants agree to engage with the simulation as if it were real, which, combined with pre-briefing elements such as respect for learners and clear expectations, enhances participant engagement and creates a safe context for learning [66]. This psychologically safe environment allows students to make and learn from mistakes without the fear of harming real patients, which is essential for building confidence and competence [46].

Structured feedback and debriefing sessions help students reflect on their performance, identify areas for improvement and consolidate their knowledge as demonstrated in the qualitative responses from post-simulation questionnaires. This aligns with the literature emphasising the importance of debriefing in simulation-based learning. It has previously been demonstrated to promote transparent communication, explore decision-making and actions, as well as evaluate what went well and what requires improvement [67].

Students appreciated the continuity of facilitators throughout the course since it likely further enhanced the effectiveness of the feedback. Having the same facilitator across various simulation stations provides consistency and could foster personalised, cohesive feedback through a deeper understanding of each student’s strengths and areas of improvement, leading to a more integrated and comprehensive educational experience [65]. This continuity seems to promote a trusting relationship between students and faculty, creating a safe learning environment which has been shown to enhance student satisfaction and learning [18, 68].

These insights suggest that integrating simulation-based learning for pre-foundation doctors may help with their preparedness for practice, with particular emphasis on ensuring there is hands-on learning, effective debriefs and facilitator continuity. Simulation could be used to supplement pre-foundation clinical placements, potentially helping students to become more confident clinicians who feel better prepared by the time they transition to their roles as resident doctors.

Limitations

The single-centre design of this study involving students from one single medical school does limit its generalisability – different curricula, patient demographics and teaching cultures between medical schools and their associated teaching hospitals may influence their students’ self-confidence and self-perceived preparedness for starting FY1. Our students had limited pre-existing exposure to simulation, which may not be the case in different curricula and may influence how students approach simulation. The addition of a comparison group who did not receive this simulation would be useful to help separate the specific influence of this simulation and improve the validity of our findings.

This study also relied on self-reported measures of confidence and preparedness which, as discussed, does not necessarily translate to improved real-world competence. The latter can be challenging to assess under the best of conditions, but the significant transition point from medical student to Foundation doctor involves many confounding factors meaning that assessing the influence of one intervention may be difficult. There is also the potential for social desirability bias to influence these results – particularly as these students are at a time of transition between identifying as a student or doctor. The students’ expected improvement in confidence and psychological effect of attending a course designed to prepare them for FY1 may also influence these results.

Future research should consider employing a multi-centred approach, incorporating objective measures of student performance (for greater triangulation) and an attempt to evaluate the impact of simulation on clinical practice and behaviours.

Conclusion

A pre-foundation high-fidelity simulation course can improve the perceived preparedness of final year medical students for FY1 Training, creating a psychologically safe environment, where students can make and learn from mistakes without the fear of harming real patients, which is essential for building confidence. It provides consistent additional learning opportunities to supplement clinical, experiential learning. This study also highlights that hands-on learning, debriefing and facilitator continuity are likely highly valued components of an effective simulation course. This adds to previous research by indicating the added value of SBME during the pre-foundation period and which features optimise the simulation learning environment.
