Williams’ and Graded Equivalence Conjectures for small graphs
Roozbeh Hazrat, Elizabeth Pacheco

Abstract
. We prove what might have been expected: The Williams Conjecture in symbolic dynamics and Graded Classification Conjecture for Leavitt/ C ∗ C^{*} -graph algebras hold for “small graphs”, i.e., connected graphs with three vertices, no parallel edges, no sinks and with no trivial hereditary and saturated subsets. Namely, two small graphs are shift equivalent if and only if they are strong shift equivalent if and only if their Leavitt/ C ∗ C^{*} -graph algebras are graded/equivariant Morita equivalent.



1.
introduction

One of the fundamental objects of study in symbolic dynamics is called a
shift of finite type
(SFT), which consists of sequences (often indexed by

\mathbb{Z}
) of symbols chosen from a finite set, that do not include certain “forbidden” finite sequences, equipped with a
shift map
which creates a dynamical behaviour. The applications abound, from topological quantum field theory, ergodic
theory, and statistical mechanics to coding and information theory
[
27
,
29
]
.

The fundamental work of Williams in the 1970’s
[
33
]
shows that understanding these dynamical systems reduces to understanding certain relations between matrices: two shifts of finite type are topologically “conjugate” if and only if their underlying matrices (or graphs) are “strong shift equivalent”. Williams also introduced the notion of “shift equivalent” which were originally thought to be the same as strong shift equivalent but rather more tractable. However, it took 20 years to establish that they are not the same
[
28
]
and after 50 years the extremely subtle distinction between shift equivalence and strong shift equivalence still remains elusive.

It was already noticed by Cuntz and Krieger in the 1980’s
[
17
]
that the notion of shifts of finite type and their equivalences can be related to invariances of certain graph
C^{*}
-algebras. This line of research was pursued and substantially developed by Matsumoto and others (see
[
30
,
18
]
and the references there). Leavitt path algebras, which are the discrete version of graph
C^{*}
-algebras, were introduced in 2005 and the classification of these algebras and their relationships to shifts of finite type became an active line of research
[
3
,
16
]
.
As they are purely algebraic objects, they facilitate connections with other areas of algebra such as representation theory and even chip firing
[
5
]
.
One of the main conjectures that would relate these topics is the Graded Morita Classification Conjecture: Two square matrices (with no zero rows) are shift equivalent if and only if their Leavitt path algebras are “graded Morita equivalent” if and only if their graph
C^{*}
-algebras are “equivariant Morita equivalent”.
Reformulating this in terms of
K
-theory enables us to eliminate the no-sink assumption from the conjecture:
for two finite graphs
E
and
F
, the Leavitt path algebra
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
is graded Morita equivalent to
L_{\mathsf{k}}(F)
if and only if there is an order-preserving
\mathbb{Z}[x,x^{-1}]
-module isomorphism
K_{0}^{\operatorname{gr}}(L_{\mathsf{k}}(E))\cong K_{0}^{\operatorname{gr}}(L_{\mathsf{k}}(F))
. A similar conjecture can be written in the analytic setting
[
16
]
: The graph
C^{*}
-algebras
C^{*}(E)
and
C^{*}(F)
are equivariant Morita equivalent if and only if
K_{0}^{\mathbb{T}}(C^{*}(E))\cong K_{0}^{\mathbb{T}}(C^{*}(F))
as order-preserving
\mathbb{Z}[x,x^{-1}]
-modules. If under these isomorphisms the order units map to each other, then conjectures assert that these algebras are graded/equivariant isomorphic (
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
). We refer the reader to
[
4
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
14
,
19
,
34
]
for works on the graded classification conjecture and
[
16
]
for a comprehensive survey.

2.1.
Graded algebras

Throughout the paper rings will have identities and modules are unitary in the sense that the identity of the ring acts as the identity operator on the module. We refer the reader to
[
21
]
for the theory of graded rings and the standard terminologies. We provide some definitions for the sake of notation.

Let
\Gamma
be an abelian group.
A ring
R
is
\Gamma
-graded
if
R
decomposes as a direct sum
\bigoplus_{\gamma\in\Gamma}R_{\gamma}
, where each
R_{\gamma}
is an additive subgroup of
R
and
R_{\gamma}R_{\eta}\subseteq R_{\gamma+\eta}
, for all
\gamma,\eta\in\Gamma
.

Let
R
be a
\Gamma
-graded ring.
A right
R
-module
M
is
\Gamma
-graded
if
M
decomposes as a direct sum
\bigoplus_{\gamma\in\Gamma}M_{\gamma}
, where each
M_{\gamma}
is an additive subgroup of
M
and
M_{\gamma}R_{\eta}\subseteq M_{\gamma+\eta}
, for all
\gamma,\eta\in\Gamma
.
A graded left
R
-module is defined analogously.
If
R
and
S
are
\Gamma
-graded rings, then an
R
-
S
bimodule
M
is
\Gamma
-graded if
M=\bigoplus_{\gamma\in\Gamma}M_{\gamma}
is both a graded left
R
-module and a graded right
S
-module.
In particular,
R_{\gamma}M_{\eta}S_{\zeta}\subseteq M_{\gamma+\eta+\zeta}
, for all
\gamma,\eta,\zeta\in\Gamma
.
If
M
is a
\Gamma
-graded
R
-module and
\gamma\in\Gamma
, then the
\gamma
-shifted graded right
R
-module is

M(\gamma):=\bigoplus_{\eta\in\Gamma}M(\gamma)_{\eta},\text{ where }M(\gamma)_{\eta}=M_{\gamma+\eta},\text{ for all }\eta\in\Gamma.

For a
\Gamma
-graded ring
R
, there is a graded isomorphism of rings given by the regular representation
\eta:R\to\operatorname{End}_{R}(R)

which sends
r\in R
to
\eta_{r}
where
\eta_{r}(x)=rx
, for all
x\in R
, see e.g.
[
21
, proof of Theorem 2.3.7]
.

We write
\operatorname{Mod-}R
for the category of unitary right
R
-modules and module homomorphisms. If
R
is
\Gamma
-graded, we denote by
{\operatorname{{Gr}-}}R
the category of unitary graded right
R
-modules with morphisms preserving the grading. For
\Gamma
-graded rings
R
and

, a functor
\mathcal{F}:{\operatorname{{Gr}-}}R\to{\operatorname{{Gr}-}}S

\mathcal{F}(M(\gamma))=\mathcal{F}(M)(\gamma)
for all graded
R
-modules
M
and all

\gamma\in\Gamma
. For
\gamma\in\Gamma
, the
shift functor

(1)

\mathcal{T}_{\gamma}:{\operatorname{{Gr}-}}R\longrightarrow{\operatorname{{Gr}-}}R,\quad M\mapsto M(\gamma)

is an isomorphism with the property that
\mathcal{T}_{\gamma}\mathcal{T}_{\eta}=\mathcal{T}_{\gamma+\eta}
, for
\gamma,\eta\in\Gamma
.

\Gamma
be an abelian group, and let

\Gamma

A

For an

Let

2.2.
Leavitt path algebras

We refer the reader to
[
3
]
for the definition of Leavitt path algebras and the standard terminologies. We provide some definitions for the sake of notation.

Let
E=(E^{0},E^{1},r,s)
be a row-finite directed graph and
\mathsf{k}
a field. Throughout the paper, we assume
E
is a finite graph, i.e., has finite number of vertices and edges. The
Leavitt path algebra

L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
is the universal
\mathsf{k}
-algebra generated by formal elements
\{v\ |\ v\in E^{0}\}
and
\{e,e^{*}\ |\ e\in E^{1}\}
subject to the relations

(1)

v^{2}=v
, and
vw=0
if
v\neq w
are in
E^{0}

(2)

s(e)e=er(e)=e,\ r(e)e^{*}=e^{*}s(e)=e^{*}
, for all
e\in E^{0}

(3)

e^{*}e=r(e)
, for
e\in E^{1}

(4)

v=\sum_{s(e)=v}ee^{*}
, for
v
which is not a sink.

The main invariant used in this paper is the
graded Grothendieck group

K^{\operatorname{gr}}_{0}(L_{\mathsf{k}}(E))
, for the graph
E
and its positive cone
K_{0}^{\operatorname{gr}}(L_{\mathsf{k}}(E))^{+}
.
Using the deep work of George Bergman on realisation of conical monoids
[
3
]
, the monoid
K_{0}^{\operatorname{gr}}(L_{\mathsf{k}}(E))^{+}
can be described directly from the graph
E
. Namely, there is a
\mathbb{Z}
-monoid isomorphism

K_{0}^{\operatorname{gr}}(L_{\mathsf{k}}(E))^{+}\cong T_{E}
, where
T_{E}
is the so-called
talented monoid
of the graph
E
:

T_{E}=\Big\langle\,v(i),v\in E^{0},i\in\mathbb{Z}\,\,\Big|\,\,v(i)=\sum_{e\in s^{-1}(v)}r(e)(i+1)\ \Big\rangle.

Here the relations in the monoid are defined for those vertices that emit edges. The action of
\mathbb{Z}
on
T_{E}
is defined by
{}^{n}v(i)=v(i+n)
, where
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Under this isomorphism, the class
[L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)]
is sent to
1_{E}:=\sum_{v\in E^{0}}v
.

Throughout the paper, we occasionally use the notation
E

2.3.
Symbolic dynamics

We refer the reader to
[
29
]
for an introduction to symbolic dynamics.

Two square nonnegative integer matrices
A
and
B
are called
elementary shift equivalent
, denoted
A\sim_{\operatorname{ES}}B
, if there are nonnegative integer matrices
R
and
S
such that

A=RS\,\,\,\text{and }\,\,B=SR.\\

Note that
R
and
S
need not be square matrices.

The equivalence relation
\sim_{\operatorname{SS}}
generated by elementary shift equivalence on the set of nonnegative integer square matrices (of any finite size) is called
strong shift equivalence
. That is,
A\sim_{\operatorname{SS}}B
in case there is a sequence of square matrices
A_{i}
and a sequence of elementary shift equivalence
(R_{i},S_{i})
,
1\leq i\leq k
, such that

A=A_{0}\sim_{\operatorname{ES}}A_{1}\sim_{\operatorname{ES}}A_{2}\sim_{\operatorname{ES}}\cdots\sim_{\operatorname{ES}}A_{k}=B.

Here the intermediate matrices are related by
A_{i-1}=R_{i}S_{i}
and
A_{i}=S_{i}R_{i}
.

Next we introduce the notion of shift equivalences. The nonnegative integer square matrices
A
and
B
are called
shift equivalent
, denoted
A\sim_{S}B
, if there exist
k\geq 1
and nonnegative integer matrices
R
and
S
such that

(3)

\displaystyle A^{k}=RS,\ \ \,B^{k}=SR,

\displaystyle AR=RB,\ \ \,SA=BS.

If
A
and
B
are strong shift equivalent via a sequence of elementary shift equivalence
(R_{i},S_{i})
,
1\leq i\leq k
, then
R=R_{1}\dots R_{k}
and
S=S_{k}\dots S_{1}
give (
3
), i.e.,
A
and
B
are shift equivalent
[
29
, Proposition 7.3.2]
.

Next we recall the definition of Krieger’s dimension triple, which is a complete invariant for shift equivalence. Let
A
be an
r\times r
matrix over
\mathbb{N}
.
The
dimension group
of
A
is the inductive limit of the stationary inductive system given by
A
as a linear map on
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
(acting from right), i.e.,

(4)

\mathbb{Z}^{r}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle A}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{Z}^{r}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle A}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{Z}^{r}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle A}}{{\longrightarrow}}\cdots.

As a group, this is isomorphic to

(5)

G_{A}:=(\mathbb{Z}^{r}\times\mathbb{N})/\sim_{A}

where
(v,k)\sim_{A}(w,l)
if
vA^{m-k}=wA^{m-l}
, for some
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Let
[v,k]
denote the equivalence class of
(v,k)
. Clearly
[vA^{n},n+k]=[v,k]
, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, it is not difficult to show that the direct limit of the system (
4
) is the abelian group consists of equivalence classes
[v,k]
,
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, with addition defined by

[v,k]+[w,k^{\prime}]=[vA^{k^{\prime}}+wA^{k},k+k^{\prime}].

The
positive cone
of
G_{A}
is the commutative monoid

G_{A}^{+}:=\big\{[v,k]\in G_{A}\mid v\in\mathbb{N}^{r},k\in\mathbb{N}\big\}.

The multiplication by
A
defines an automorphism
\theta_{A}
on
G_{A}
that preserves the positive cone. This automorphism induces a
\mathbb{Z}[x,x^{-1}]
-module structure on
G_{A}
.
The class of the unit
u_{A}:=[(1,\ldots,1),0]
is an order unit in
G_{A}
. The triple
(G_{A},G_{A}^{+},\theta_{A})
is called
Krieger’s dimension triple of
A
, or
dimension group
of
A
for short.

The dimension group is a complete invariant: two matrices
A
and
B
are shift equivalent if and only if there is a group isomorphism between the dimension groups
G_{A}
and
G_{B}

which preserves the positive cones and the
\mathbb{Z}[x,x^{-1}]
-module structures on the groups (
[
29
, Theorem 7.5.8]
).

A shift equivalence between the matrices
A
and
B
implemented via
(R,S)
(
3
) determines the order-preserving
\mathbb{Z}[x,x^{-1}]
-module isomorphism

(6)

\displaystyle\phi_{R}:G_{A}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow G_{B}

\displaystyle[v,k]

\displaystyle\longmapsto[vR,k].

Finally, we relate the Leavitt path algebra of a graph via the graded Grothendieck group with the dimension group of its adjacency matrix. For finite graphs with no sinks, there is an order-preserving group isomorphism

(7)

\displaystyle\beta_{E}:K_{0}^{\operatorname{gr}}(L_{\mathsf{k}}(E))

\displaystyle\longrightarrow G_{A_{E}}

\displaystyle[L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)]

\displaystyle\longmapsto[(1,\dots,1),0]

which preserves the
\mathbb{Z}[x,x^{-1}]
-module structures on the groups. Combining with (

[

and
[

]

(8)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K_{0}^{\operatorname{gr}}(L_{\mathsf{k}}(E))}

It is easy to see that if an

integral matrix

is invertible in

(9)

\displaystyle G_{A}=(\mathbb{Z}^{r}\times\mathbb{N})/\sim_{A}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1}{\det(A)}\right]^{r}

\displaystyle[v,k]

\displaystyle\longmapsto vA^{-k}.

Combining (

Lemma 2.2

Let

be a finite graph with no sinks and

its associated adjacency matrix. If

, then as a group

K_{0}^{\operatorname{gr}}(L_{\mathsf{k}}(E))\cong\mathbb{Z}^{r}.

Proof.

One can easily show that when

∎

For other cases, even when

When the determinant of an adjacency matrix is not

Let

with positive cone

\Delta_{A}^{+}:=\big\{v\in\Delta_{A}\mid vA^{l}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}\text{, for some }l\in\mathbb{N}\big\}.

The multiplication

\delta_{A}
on

that preserves the positive cone. The automorphism

naturally makes

-module.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let

be an

invertible (over

\Delta_{A}=\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{Z}^{r}A^{-k}.

Proof.

First note that if the matrix

takes the simpler form:

\Delta_{A}=\Big\{v\in\mathbb{Q}^{r}\,|\,vA^{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}\text{, for some }k\geq 0\Big\}.

The statement of the Corollary is now immediate.

\displaystyle v

Recall that a

is a matrix where all its entries are nonnegative. A nonnegative matrix

is

if for each ordered

, there exists some

such that

A strongly connected graph (or its adjacency matrix) has

is the greatest common divisor of the length of its cycles. A graph (or its adjacency matrix) is

if its period

is

. A matrix is

if it is both aperiodic and irreducible. By

is positive. This fact will be used in the paper.

The following code, written in Mathematica, will check whether two matrices
A
and
B
with entries
0
and
1
and of arbitrary sizes, are elementary shift equivalent.

Eshift[A_, B_] := Module[{r = Length[A], c = Length[B], xSet = {}},
d = r * c;
(* all r*c matrices with entries 0 and 1 *)
t = Tuples[Range[0, 1], {r, c}];
s = Tuples[Range[0, 1], {c, r}];
(* Range[0,k] gives matrices with entries between 0 and k *)
Do[
If[
t[[i]] . s[[j]] == A && s[[j]] . t[[i]] == B,
AppendTo[xSet, {t[[i]], s[[j]]}]
],
{i, 1, 2^d}, {j, 1, 2^d}
];
Map[MatrixForm, xSet, {2}]
]
