We now describe the components of these pairs. Starting with
\overline{P}_{n}(N)
,
let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be a finite field of cardinality
q
and
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x,y,x^{-1},y^{-1}]
be the algebra of
two-variable Laurent polynomials with coefficients in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
For any
n\geq 1
define
C_{n}(q)
to be the number of ideals of codimension
n
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x,y,x^{-1},y^{-1}]
;
such ideals are in one-to-one correspondence with the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-points of the Hilbert scheme
of
n
points on the two-dimensional torus.
In
[
9
]
we showed that
C_{n}(q)
is a monic polynomial of degree
2n
with integer coefficients in the variable
q
.
Moreover,
C_{n}(q)
is palindromic and divisible by
(q-1)^{2}
.
We gave explicit formulas for the coefficients of
C_{n}(q)
in
[
10
, Th. 1.1]
.

Since
C_{n}(q)
is divisible by
(q-1)^{2}
we can define the degree
2n-2
polynomial
P_{n}(q)
by
C_{n}(q)=(q-1)^{2}P_{n}(q)
:
it is monic, palindromic and has integer coefficients.
One of the most striking features of
P_{n}(q)
is that its coefficients are all
non-negative
.
Actually we showed that each coefficient of
P_{n}(q)
can be expressed
as the number of divisors of
n
in a certain interval (see
[
10
, Thm. 1.3]
and §
2.1
below).

In
[
8
,
10
]
we also computed the values taken by
P_{n}(q)
when
q
is a complex root of unity of order
1,2,3,4
or
6
,
equivalently when
q+q^{-1}
takes one of the respective values
2,-2,-1,0,1
(see
[
10
, Thm. 1.6]
),
thus recovering well-known arithmetical functions. For instance,
P_{n}(1)=\sigma(n)
is equal to the sum of divisors of
n

and
P_{n}(-1)=r(n)/4
, where
r(n)
is the number of representations of
n
as a sum of squares of two integers.

Actually the Laurent polynomial
{P_{n}(q)}/{q^{n-1}}
takes integer values whenever
q+q^{-1}=N
is an integer.
Computing such integer values is our main objective in this note.
For simplicity we replace the Laurent polynomial
P_{n}(q)/q^{n-1}
in the variable
q

by the degree
n-1
polynomial
\overline{P}_{n}(X)

in the variable
X
, defined by

(1.1)

\overline{P}_{n}(q+q^{-1})=P_{n}(q)/q^{n-1}.

(This is possible since
P_{n}(q)
is palindromic of degree
2n-2
.)
We have thus reduced our problem to computing
\overline{P}_{n}(N)
at integers
N
.

Plugging the integers
\overline{P}_{1}(N),\overline{P}_{2}(N),\ldots,\overline{P}_{8}(N)
for
N=5
into the

On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences

[
12
]

has yielded an “approximate match” by the first coefficients of the formal power series expansion of the rational function

\frac{1+t}{1-5t+t^{2}}.

Replacing
5
by
3
and by
4
in this rational function produced similar approximate matches
for
\overline{P}_{n}(3)
and
\overline{P}_{n}(4)
respectively.
It was then natural to replace
3,4,5
by an indeterminate
X
, which we do next.

The second integer sequence mentioned above consists of integer values at
N
of the polynomials
F_{k}(X)

defined by

(1.2)

\sum_{k\geq 0}\,F_{k}(X)\,t^{k}=\frac{1+t}{1-Xt+t^{2}}\,.

It turns out that each polynomial
F_{k}(X)
is of degree
k-1
, has integer coefficients and we have

(1.3)

F_{k}(X)=1+\sum_{m=1}^{k}\,\overline{T}_{m}(X)

is the sum of monic versions
\overline{T}_{m}(X)
of the Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind
defined in §
3.1
below.

With this notation we establish that each
\overline{P}_{n}(X)
can be approximated by
F_{n-1}(X)
in the sense that
the difference between these two monic polynomials, both of degree
n-1
,
is a polynomial of much lower degree (see Theorem
4.1
for a precise statement).

More interestingly,
\overline{P}_{n}(X)
can be expressed as the following linear combination of
F_{k}(X)
,
whose terms are parameterized by the odd divisors
d
of
n
:

(1.4)

\overline{P}_{n}(X)=\sum_{d|n,\,d\,\mathrm{odd}\atop r_{n}(d)\geq 0}\,F_{r_{n}(d)}(X)-\sum_{d|n,\,d\,\mathrm{odd}\atop r_{n}(d)<0}\,F_{-r_{n}(d)-1}(X),

where
r_{n}(d)={n/d}-{(d+1)/2}
; see Theorem
4.4
.
For
d=1
we have
r_{n}(1)=n-1
, which implies the presence of
F_{n-1}(X)
in (
1.4
) for all
n\geq 1
.

Since the powers of
2
are the only integers with a unique odd divisor, namely
d=1
,
we have
\overline{P}_{n}(X)=F_{n-1}(X)
if and only if
n
is a power of
2
.
This explains the coincidences observed in Table
1
.

We deduce Formula (
1.4
) from the following new expression for
C_{n}(q)
:

(1.5)

C_{n}(q)=q^{n}\,\sum_{d|n,\,d\,\mathrm{odd}}\,\left(q^{\frac{n}{d}-\frac{d-1}{2}}+q^{-\frac{n}{d}+\frac{d-1}{2}}-q^{\frac{n}{d}-\frac{d+1}{2}}-q^{-\frac{n}{d}+\frac{d+1}{2}}\right).

See Theorem
5.3
.

Formula (
1.5
) allows us to express the local zeta function and the Hasse–Weil zeta function
of the Hilbert scheme of
n
points on the two-dimensional torus
as products parameterized by the odd divisors of
n
(see §
5.3
).

2.
The polynomials
\overline{P}_{n}(X)

2.1.
Definition and basic properties

For each
n\geq 1
we define the degree
n-1
polynomial
\overline{P}_{n}(X)
by

(2.1)

\overline{P}_{n}(q+q^{-1})=P_{n}(q)/q^{n-1},

where
P_{n}(q)
was introduced in the introduction.
This makes sense since
P_{n}(q)
is a palindromic polynomial of degree
2n-2
.
Since
P_{n}(q)
has integer coefficients, so has
\overline{P}_{n}(X)
.

As a consequence of
[
9
, Cor. 1.4]
, we have the equality of formal power series

1+(q+q^{-1}-2)\sum_{n\geq 1}\,\frac{P_{n}(q)}{q^{n-1}}\,t^{n}=\prod_{i\geq 1}\,\frac{(1-t^{i})^{2}}{1-(q+q^{-1})t^{i}+t^{2i}}\,.

This translates into the following equality of formal power series:

(2.2)

1+(X-2)\sum_{n\geq 1}\,\overline{P}_{n}(X)\,t^{n}=\prod_{i\geq 1}\,\frac{(1-t^{i})^{2}}{1-Xt^{i}+t^{2i}}\,.

By
[
10
, Thm. 1.3]
each polynomial
P_{n}(q)
is of the form

P_{n}(q)=a_{n,0}\,q^{n-1}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\,a_{n,i}\,\left(q^{n+i-1}+q^{n-i-1}\right),

where the coefficient
a_{n,i}
is equal to the number of divisors
d
of
n
satisfying the inequalities

(2.3)

\frac{i+\sqrt{2n+i^{2}}}{{2}}<d\leq i+\sqrt{2n+i^{2}}.\qquad(0\leq i\leq n-1)

It follows that for each positive integer
n
,

(2.4)

\overline{P}_{n}(X)=a_{n,0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\,a_{n,i}\,\overline{T}_{i}(X),

where
\overline{T}_{i}(X)
is the monic Chebyshev polynomial of degree
i
defined below by (
3.1
).
Since the coefficients
a_{n,i}
are non-negative, each
\overline{P}_{n}(X)
is a
sum
of monic Chebyshev polynomials
\overline{T}_{k}(X)
.

2.2.
Multiplicative values of
|P_{n}(q)|
at roots of unity

The content of this subsection is an addendum to
[
10
, Th. 1.6]
. It concerns the cases when
q+q^{-1}=N
,
where
N
is an integer such that
-2\leq N\leq 2
, namely when
q
is a root of unity of order
d=1,2,3,4
or
6
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
q
be a complex root of unity of order
d=1,2,3
or
4
.
Then the sequence of non-negative integers

\left|P_{n}(q)\right|=\left|P_{n}(q)/q^{n-1}\right|=\left|\overline{P}_{n}(q+q^{-1})\right|

is multiplicative as a function of
n
.

Recall that a function
f(n)
defined on non-negative integers is
multiplicative
if
f(mn)=f(m)f(n)

whenever
m
and
n
are coprime.

Proof.

(i) When
q=1
, by
[
9
, Cor.  1.2]
we have
\overline{P}_{n}(2)=P_{n}(1)=\sigma(n)
, the sum of divisors of
n
,
which is well-known to be multiplicative. See
[
2
, Sect. 2.13]
and
[
12
, A000203]
.

(ii) For
q=-1
we have
\overline{P}_{n}(-2)=P_{n}(-1)=r(n)/4
, where
r(n)
is the number of representations of
n

as a sum of squares of two integers. By
[
12
, A002654]
the sequence
r(n)/4
is multiplicative.
Then so is
P_{n}(-1)
.

(iii) For
q=j
, a root of unity of order
3
we have

\overline{P}_{n}(-1)=P_{n}(j)/j^{n-1}=\lambda(n)
, where
\lambda(n)
is the sequence A113063 in
[
12
]
.
By
[
6
, Sect. 32, p. 79]

\lambda(n)
is multiplicative. See also
[
5
]
.

(iv) Let
i
be a square root of
-1
. It follows again from
[
10
, Th. 1.6]
that we have

|\overline{P}_{n}(0)|=|P_{n}(i)/i^{n-1}|=r^{\prime}(n)/2,

where
r^{\prime}(n)
is the number of representations of
n
as a sum of a
square and twice another square. The sequence
r^{\prime}(n)/2
is the sequence A002325 in
[
12
]
.
It is known to be multiplicative.
∎

The case when
q=\omega
is a root of unity of order
6
is a little more involved.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\omega
be a root of unity of order
6
.
Then the sequence of non-negative integers
|P_{n}(\omega)|=|\overline{P}_{n}(1)|
is almost multiplicative
in the sense that for all pairs
(m,n)
of coprime positive integers,

|P_{m}(\omega)||P_{n}(\omega)|=|P_{mn}(\omega)|\quad\text{when}\;\;(m,n)\equiv(0,1),(1,1)\;\text{or}\;(1,2)\pmod{3},

|P_{m}(\omega)||P_{n}(\omega)|=2\,|P_{mn}(\omega)|\quad\text{when}\;\;(m,n)\equiv(0,2)\pmod{3},

|P_{m}(\omega)||P_{n}(\omega)|=4\,|P_{mn}(\omega)|\quad\text{when}\;\;(m,n)\equiv(2,2)\pmod{3}.

Proof.

It is a consequence of the multiplicativity of sequence
r(n)/4
observed above and of the following reformulation
of
[
8
, Th. 1.1]
or
[
10
, Th. 1.6 (d)]
:

|P_{n}(\omega)|=\begin{cases}\hskip 12.0ptr(n)&\text{if}\;n\equiv 0,\\
\hskip 7.0ptr(n)/4&\text{if}\;n\equiv 1,\quad\pmod{3}\\
\hskip 7.0ptr(n)/2&\text{if}\;n\equiv 2.\end{cases}

∎

We have the following consequence of Propositions
2.1
and
2.2
.

Corollary 2.3
.

Given a pair
(m,n)
of coprime positive integers,
the polynomial
\overline{P}_{mn}(X)^{2}-\overline{P}_{m}(X)^{2}\overline{P}_{n}(X)^{2}
is divisible by
X(X+1)(X^{2}-4)
,
and moreover by
X-1
if
(m,n)\equiv(0,1),(1,1)
or
(1,2)\pmod{3}
.

We may wonder whether there are other integers
N
such that
|\overline{P}_{n}(N)|
is multiplicative.
The answer is no, as witnessed by the following.

Proposition 2.4
.

The function
|\overline{P}_{n}(N)|
is multiplicative if and only if
N=-2,-1,0
or
2
.

Proof.

The “if direction” is a reformulation of Proposition
2.1
. Conversely,
if
|\overline{P}_{n}(N)|
is multiplicative, then we have
|\overline{P}_{6}(N)|=|\overline{P}_{2}(N)||\overline{P}_{3}(N)|
as a special case.
Now
\overline{P}_{6}(X)\pm\overline{P}_{2}(X)\overline{P}_{3}(X)
has the following factorizations:

\displaystyle\overline{P}_{6}(X)-\overline{P}_{2}(X)\overline{P}_{3}(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(X-1)(X+1)^{2}(X-2)(X+2),

\displaystyle\overline{P}_{6}(X)+\overline{P}_{2}(X)\overline{P}_{3}(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X(X-1)^{2}(X+1)(X+2).

This implies that multiplicativity holds only for integers
N
such that
|N|\leq 2
.
We rule out the case
N=1
in view of Proposition
2.2
.
∎

3.1.
Monic Chebyshev polynomials

Given an integer
k\geq 0
the standard Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind
T_{k}(X)
is the degree
k

polynomial defined by

\cos(k\theta)=T_{k}(\cos(\theta)),

equivalently by

\frac{q^{k}+q^{-k}}{2}=T_{k}\left(\frac{q+q^{-1}}{2}\right).

We are interested in the following variant:
let
\overline{T}_{k}(X)
be the degree
k
polynomial defined by

(3.1)

q^{k}+q^{-k}=\overline{T}_{k}(q+q^{-1}).

Both
T_{k}(X)
and
\overline{T}_{k}(X)
have integer coefficients.
They are related by

\overline{T}_{k}(X)=2T_{k}(X/2).

The advantage of
\overline{T}_{k}(X)
over
T_{k}(X)
is that the former is monic for
k\geq 1
.
The
monic Chebyshev polynomials

\overline{T}_{k}(X)
are denoted
C_{k}
in
[
1
]

and

in
[
7
]
, where they are called
Vieta–Lucas polynomials
.
See
[
13
]
for a general reference on Chebyshev polynomials.

We now state a few properties of the polynomials
\overline{T}_{k}(X)
which can be easily derived from well-known properties
of
T_{k}(X)
.
For instance, the polynomials
\overline{T}_{k}(X)
can be defined inductively by

\overline{T}_{0}(X)=2
,
\overline{T}_{1}(X)=X
, and for
k\geq 1
by

(3.2)

\overline{T}_{k+1}(X)=X\overline{T}_{k}(X)-\overline{T}_{k-1}(X).

The previous linear recurrence relation can be written in matrix form as

(3.3)

\begin{pmatrix}\overline{T}_{k+1}(X)\\
\overline{T}_{k}(X)\end{pmatrix}=M\begin{pmatrix}\overline{T}_{k}(X)\\
\overline{T}_{k-1}(X)\end{pmatrix},\;\;\text{where}\;M=\begin{pmatrix}X&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

The generating function of the polynomials
\overline{T}_{k}(X)
is the power series expansion of the
following rational function:

(3.4)

\sum_{m\geq 0}\,\overline{T}_{k}(X)\,t^{k}=\frac{2-Xt}{1-Xt+t^{2}}.

We have the following expression of
\overline{T}_{n}(X)
in terms of powers of
X
:

\overline{T}_{n}(X)=\sum_{m=0}^{[n/2]}\,(-1)^{m}\left\{\binom{n-m}{m}-\binom{n-m-1}{m-1}\right\}X^{n-2m}.

We close our summary on
\overline{T}_{k}(X)
with the following result, which may be of independent interest.

Proposition 3.1
.

For all
k\geq 0
we have
\overline{T}_{k}(X)=\mathrm{tr}(M^{k})
, where
M
is the
2\times 2
-matrix introduced in (
3.3
).

Proof.

First observe that
\mathrm{tr}(M)=X=\overline{T}_{1}(X)
and
\mathrm{tr}(M^{0})=\mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{Id})=2=\overline{T}_{0}(X)
, where
\mathrm{Id}
is the identity matrix.
To prove the equality
\overline{T}_{k}(X)=\mathrm{tr}(M^{k})
, it suffices to show that
the sequence
(\mathrm{tr}(M^{k}))_{k\geq 0}
satisfies the linear recurrence relation (
3.2
).
By the Cayley–Hamilton theorem applied to the matrix
M
, we have

M^{2}=\mathrm{tr}(M)M-\det(M)\mathrm{Id}=XM-\mathrm{Id}.

Now multiply both sides by
M^{k-1}
and take traces.
∎

3.2.
Definition and basic properties of
F_{n}(X)

We now define the sequence of polynomials
(F_{n}(X))_{n\geq 0}
inductively by
F_{0}(X)=1
and

(3.5)

F_{n}(X)=F_{n-1}(X)+\overline{T}_{n}(X)

for
n\geq 1
. In other words,

(3.6)

F_{n}(X)=1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\,\overline{T}_{k}(X).

It is clear that each

has integer coefficients and is monic of degree

Our first result is the following.

Proposition 3.2
.

The generating function of the polynomials
F_{n}(X)
is the power series expansion of the following rational function:

\sum_{n\geq 0}\,F_{n}(X)\,t^{n}=\frac{1+t}{1-Xt+t^{2}}\,.

Proof.

Using (
3.4
), we obtain

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{k\geq 1}\,\overline{T}_{k}(X)\,t^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2-Xt}{1-Xt+t^{2}}+1-\overline{T}_{0}(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2-Xt}{1-Xt+t^{2}}-1=\frac{1-t^{2}}{1-Xt+t^{2}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\,F_{n}(X)\,t^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\,\left(1+\sum_{m=1}^{n}\,\overline{T}_{m}(X)\right)t^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\,t^{\ell}\right)\left(1+\sum_{k\geq 1}\,\overline{T}_{k}(X)\,t^{k}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-t^{2}}{(1-t)(1-Xt+t^{2})}=\frac{1+t}{1-Xt+t^{2}}.

∎

Setting
X=0
in the formula of Proposition
3.2
yields the
constant term
of
F_{n}(X)
:
for all
m\geq 0
we have

F_{2m}(0)=F_{2m+1}(0)=(-1)^{m}.

For the remaining coefficients of
F_{n}(X)
we have the following expression.

Proposition 3.3
.

For
n\geq 1
we have

F_{n}(X)=\sum_{0\leq m\leq n/2}\,(-1)^{m}\binom{n-m}{m}X^{n-2m}\,+\hskip-10.0pt\sum_{0\leq m\leq(n-1)/2}\,(-1)^{m}\binom{n-m-1}{m}X^{n-2m-1}.

Proof.

Expanding the following fraction, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-Xt+t^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{r\geq 1}\,t^{r}(X-t)^{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{r\geq 1}\sum_{s=0}^{r}\,(-1)^{s}\binom{r}{s}X^{r-s}t^{r+s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{n\geq 1}\,\sum_{0\leq m\leq n/2}\,(-1)^{m}\binom{n-m}{m}X^{n-2m}t^{n}.

In view of Proposition
3.2
,
multiplying by
1+t
yields the desired formula for
F_{n}(X)
.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

For
n\geq 5

\displaystyle F_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X^{n}+X^{n-1}-(n-1)X^{n-2}-(n-2)X^{n-3}+

\displaystyle{}+\frac{(n-2)(n-3)}{2}X^{n-4}+\frac{(n-3)(n-4)}{2}X^{n-5}+

\displaystyle{}+\text{monomials of degree}\leq n-6.

In Section

Proposition 3.5
.

We have

F_{k+1}(X)=XF_{k}(X)-F_{k-1}(X)\qquad(k\geq 1).

Proof.

The rational function expressing the generating function in Proposition

has the same denominator
as the rational function in (

Therefore the polynomials

satisfy the same linear recurrence relation
as the polynomials

in (

[

∎

4.1.
Approximating

with

The aim of this subsection is the following result.

Theorem 4.1
.

For all

, the difference
\overline{P}_{n}(X)-F_{n-1}(X)
is a polynomial of degree
<n/2-1
.

Recall that
\overline{P}_{n}(X)
and

are both of degree

.
The theorem states that they coincide in degree
\geq n/2-1
.
The bound on degrees is sharp as can be seen from Table

indeed,
\overline{P}_{9}(X)-F_{8}(X)=-F_{3}(X)+F_{1}(X)
is of degree

;
similarly,
\overline{P}_{11}(X)-F_{10}(X)=-F_{4}(X)
is of degree
4<11/2-1
,
and
\overline{P}_{15}(X)-F_{14}(X)
is of degree
6<15/2-1
.

Together with Corollary
3.4
we derive the following.

Corollary 4.2
.

For all
n\geq 10
we have

\displaystyle\overline{P}_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X^{n-1}+X^{n-2}-(n-2)X^{n-3}-(n-3)X^{n-4}+

\displaystyle{}+\frac{(n-3)(n-4)}{2}X^{n-5}+\frac{(n-4)(n-5)}{2}X^{n-6}+

\displaystyle{}+\text{monomials of degree}\leq n-7.

Recall the (non-negative) coefficients
a_{n,i}
of
\overline{P}_{n}(X)
appearing in (
2.3
)–(
2.4
).
For the proof of Theorem
4.1
we need the following result.

Lemma 4.3
.

We have
a_{n,i}=1
for all
i
such that
n/2-1\leq i\leq n-1
.

Proof.

By
[
10
, Thm. 1.3]
and (
2.3
) above
the integer
a_{n,i}
is the number of divisors of
n
belonging to the half-open interval

I_{n,i}=\left(\frac{i+\sqrt{2n+i^{2}}}{2},i+\sqrt{2n+i^{2}}\right].

It is enough to show that
n
is the only divisor of
n
belonging to
I_{n,i}
. Since any other divisor of
n
is
\leq n/2
,
it suffices to check that
n\leq i+\sqrt{2n+i^{2}}
and
n/2\leq(i+\sqrt{2n+i^{2}})/2
; the latter inequality is equivalent to the former.
They are both equivalent to
n(n-2i)=(n-i)^{2}-i^{2}\leq 2n
, which simplifies into
n-2i\leq 2
hence to
i\geq(n-2)/2=n/2-1
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

By (
3.6
) and (
2.4
) we have

\displaystyle\overline{P}_{n}(X)-F_{n-1}(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(a_{n,0}-1)+\sum_{1\leq i\leq n-1}\,(a_{n,i}-1)\,\overline{T}_{i}(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(a_{n,0}-1)+\sum_{1\leq i<n/2-1}\,(a_{n,i}-1)\,\overline{T}_{i}(X),

the last equality being a consequence of Lemma
4.3
.
Since
\overline{T}_{i}(X)
is of degree
i
, we obtain the desired result.
∎

4.2.
Odd divisors

We now give a complete formula for
\overline{P}_{n}(X)
as a linear combination of the polynomials
F_{k}(X)
, each appearance of
F_{k}(X)

being parameterized by an odd divisor of
n
.

Recall that if we express
n
(
\geq 1
) as the product

n=2^{a}p_{1}^{a_{1}}p_{2}^{a_{2}}\cdots p_{r}^{a_{r}}
,
where
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{r}
are distinct odd primes and
a,a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{r}
are non-negative integers,
then the number of
odd divisors
of
n
(including
1
) is equal to

(a_{1}+1)(a_{2}+1)\cdots(a_{r}+1)\geq 1.

This number is a multiplicative function of
n
(see
[
12
, A001227]
).

Observe that
(a_{1}+1)(a_{2}+1)\cdots(a_{r}+1)=1
if and only if
n
is a power of
2

otherwise, the number of odd divisors of
n
is at least
2
.

For
n\geq 1
and any odd divisor
d
of
n
, define the integer

(4.1)

r_{n}(d)=\frac{n}{d}-\frac{d+1}{2}\,.

When
d
is the trivial divisor

we have
r_{n}(1)=n-1
.

We now state our main result for
\overline{P}_{n}(X)
.

Theorem 4.4
.

For all

\overline{P}_{n}(X)=\sum_{d|n,\,d\,\mathrm{odd}\atop r_{n}(d)\geq 0}\,F_{r_{n}(d)}(X)-\sum_{d|n,\,d\,\mathrm{odd}\atop r_{n}(d)<0}\,F_{-r_{n}(d)-1}(X).

The number of summands
F_{r}(X)
in the previous sum is clearly equal to the number of odd divisors of
n
.
The proof of Theorem
4.4
will be given in Section
6
.

Since
d=1
is an odd divisor for any
n\geq 1
and
r_{n}(1)=n-1\geq 0
, the polynomial
F_{n-1}(X)
always
appears with a positive sign in the previous formula for
\overline{P}_{n}(X)
.

We remarked above that only powers of
2
have a unique odd divisor, namely
d=1
.
This implies the following consequence of Theorem
4.4
, which explains the coincidences

Corollary 4.5

We have
\overline{P}_{n}(X)=F_{n-1}(X)
if and only if

2

5.1.
Increasing sequences

The following material is taken from
[
11
]
.

We call
increasing sequence
any finite non-empty ordered set of consecutive integers
\{a+1,\ldots,a+h\}

with smallest element
a+1
and largest element
a+h
.
Here
a
is any integer (positive, negative or zero) and
h
is a positive integer. We denote this set by
\mathrm{IS}(a,h)
;
it has
h
elements. An increasing sequence
\mathrm{IS}(a,h)
is called
positive
if
a\geq 0

and
negative
if
a<0
.
It is called
odd
if
h
is odd, and
even
if
h
is even.

We say that an integer
n\geq 1
is represented by
\mathrm{IS}(a,h)
, and write
\mathrm{IS}(a,h)\models n
, if
n
is equal to the sum
of all elements of
\mathrm{IS}(a,h)
.

There is an involution
\mathrm{IS}(a,h)\mapsto\mathrm{IS}(\check{a},\check{h})
on increasing sequences determined by

(5.1)

a+\check{a}+1=0\quad\text{and}\quad\check{a}+\check{h}=a+h.

If
\mathrm{IS}(a,h)
represents
n
, so does
\mathrm{IS}(\check{a},\check{h})
.
Since
\check{h}-h=a-\check{a}=2a+1
, we see that
h
and
\check{h}
are of opposite parity, i.e.

(-1)^{h}=-(-1)^{\check{h}}
.
In other words,
\mathrm{IS}(a,h)
is even (resp. odd) if and only if
\mathrm{IS}(\check{a},\check{h})
is odd (resp. even).
Moreover, if
\mathrm{IS}(a,h)
is positive (resp. negative), then
\mathrm{IS}(\check{a},\check{h})
is negative (resp. positive).

Let
n\geq 1
be a positive integer.
To any odd divisor
d
of
n
we associate the odd increasing sequence
\mathrm{IS}(a,h)
, where

(5.2)

a=\frac{n}{d}-\frac{d+1}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad h=d;

it is centered around
n/d
:

(5.3)

\mathrm{IS}(a,h)=\left\{\frac{n}{d}-\frac{d-1}{2},\ldots,\frac{n}{d}-1,\frac{n}{d},\frac{n}{d}+1,\ldots,\frac{n}{d}+\frac{d-1}{2}\right\}.

Depending on
d
, this increasing sequence may be positive or negative. We have
\mathrm{IS}(a,h)\models n
.

From
a=n/d-(d+1)/2
and
h=d
we derive

(5.4)

\check{a}=-\frac{n}{d}+\frac{d-1}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\check{h}=\frac{2n}{d}.

We obtain the even increasing sequence

(5.5)

\mathrm{IS}(\check{a},\check{h})=\left\{-\frac{n}{d}+\frac{d+1}{2},\ldots,\frac{n}{d}+\frac{d-1}{2}\right\}\models n.

Clearly, if
\mathrm{IS}(a,h)
is positive, then
\mathrm{IS}(a,h)\subset\mathrm{IS}(\check{a},\check{h})
and

(5.6)

\mathrm{IS}(\check{a},\check{h})\setminus\mathrm{IS}(a,h)=\left\{-\frac{n}{d}+\frac{d+1}{2},\ldots,\frac{n}{d}-\frac{d+1}{2}\right\}.

If
\mathrm{IS}(a,h)
is negative, then
\mathrm{IS}(\check{a},\check{h})\subset\mathrm{IS}(a,h)
and

(5.7)

\mathrm{IS}(a,h)\setminus\mathrm{IS}(\check{a},\check{h})=\left\{\frac{n}{d}-\frac{d-1}{2},\ldots,-\frac{n}{d}+\frac{d-1}{2}\right\}.

For both pairs
(a,h)
and
(\check{a},\check{h})
we have the same relation, namely

(5.8)

\frac{h(h+2a+1)}{2}=n=\frac{\check{h}(\check{h}+2\check{a}+1)}{2}.

Conversely, starting from an increasing sequence
\mathrm{IS}(a,h)
we obtain an odd divisor
d
of
n
as follows:

d
is equal to the only element of
\{h,\check{h}\}
which is odd.
In this way we have a bijection between the set of odd divisors of
n
and
the set of positive increasing sequences representing
n
,
which is in bijection with the set of negative increasing sequences representing
n
.

Suppose that given
n\geq 1
we have a pair
(a,h)
of integers with
h\geq 1
verifying Equation (
5.8
),
which we rewrite as
h(h+2a+1)=2n
.
If
h
is odd, hence coprime to
2
, then
d=h
is an odd divisor of
n
and
a=n/d-(d+1)/2
, as in (
5.2
).
If
h
is even, then
d=h+2a+1
is odd; being a divisor of
2n
, it is an odd divisor of
n
; in this case

h=2n/d
and
a=-n/d+(d-1)/2
, as in (
5.4
).

Examples 5.1
.

(i) If
n
is a power of
2
, then it has only one odd divisor, namely
d=1
; the corresponding positive increasing sequence
is
\mathrm{IS}(n-1,1)=\{n\}
and the corresponding negative one is

\mathrm{IS}(-n,2n)=\{-(n-1),\ldots,-1,0,1,\ldots,n-1,n\}.

(ii) Any other integer
n
has odd divisors
d\neq 1
, hence more than one positive (or negative) increasing sequences.

Take for instance
n=18
. If
d=3
, then
a=4
,
h=3
,
\check{a}=-5
,
\check{h}=12
and

\mathrm{IS}(4,3)=\{5,6,7\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{IS}(-5,12)=\{-4,-3,\ldots,3,4,5,6,7\}.

If
d=9
, then
a=-3
,
h=9
,
\check{a}=2
,
\check{h}=4
and

\mathrm{IS}(-3,9)=\{-2,-1,0,1,2,3,4,5,6\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{IS}(2,4)=\{3,4,5,6\}.

5.2.
A formula for
C_{n}(q)
in terms of odd divisors of
n

We now turn to
C_{n}(q)
.
In
[
10
, Th. 1.1]
we gave the following explicit expression:

\frac{C_{n}(q)}{q^{n}}=c_{n,0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\,c_{n,i}\,\left(q^{i}+q^{-i}\right),

where

(5.9)

c_{n,0}=2(-1)^{r}

if
n=r(r+1)/2
for some positive integer
r
and
c_{n,0}=0
otherwise.
For the remaining coefficients
c_{n,i}
(
i\geq 1
) we have

(5.10)

c_{n,i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}(-1)^{k}&\text{if}\;\,n=k(k+2i+1)/2\;\;\text{for some integer}\;k\geq 1,\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr(-1)^{k-1}&\text{if}\;\,n=k(k+2i-1)/2\;\;\text{for some integer}\;k\geq 1,\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr 0&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Observe that in (
5.10
) the two first conditions are mutually exclusive, as follows
from
[
10
, Lemma 3.5 (ii)]
. This may also be verified as follows: suppose

k(k+2i+1)=h(h+2i-1)

with
i,h,k\geq 1
; then
k^{2}+2ik+k=h^{2}+2ih-h
, hence

(k-h+1)(k+h)+2i(k-h)=0;

it is readily seen that the left-hand side cannot vanish, neither in the case
k\geq h
, nor in the case
k<h
.

Lemma 5.2
.

We have

\frac{C_{n}(q)}{q^{n}}=\sum\,(-1)^{h}\,\left(q^{a}+q^{-a}\right),

where the summation is over all
h\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
and
a\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
n=h(h+1+2a)/2
.
Moreover, if
n
has such a representation and
|a|
is given, then
a,h
are unique.

Proof.

From (
5.9
) it follows that both sides have the same constant term.

From now on we concentrate on the case when
a
is a non-zero integer.
Let
i\geq 1
.
Suppose that
n=k(k+2i+1)/2
with
k\geq 1
as in the first row of (
5.10
);
then
n=h(h+2a+1)/2
with
h=k\geq 1
,
a=i\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
, and
(-1)^{k}=(-1)^{h}
.

Now suppose that
n=k(k+2i-1)/2
with
k\geq 1
as in the second row of (
5.10
);
then
n=h(h+2a+1)/2
with
h=k+2i-1\geq 1
,
a=-i\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
, and
(-1)^{k-1}=(-1)^{h}
.

Conversely, let
n=h(h+2a+1)/2
with
h\geq 1
,
i\in\mathbb{Z}
and
a\neq 0
.
Then either
a\geq 1
, and then
n=k(k+2i+1)/2
with
k=h
,
i=a\geq 1
, and
(-1)^{k}=(-1)^{h}
;
or
a<0
, and then
n=k(k+2i-1)/2
,
k=h+2a+1\geq 1
,
i=-a>0
, and
(-1)^{k-1}=(-1)^{h}
.

This proves the lemma in view of the observation made just before.
∎

We have the following new formulas for
C_{n}(q)
.

Theorem 5.3
.

(i) For each integer
n\geq 1
we have

\frac{C_{n}(q)}{q^{n}}=\sum\,(-1)^{h}\,\left(q^{a}+q^{-a}\right),

where the summation is taken over all increasing sequences
\mathrm{IS}(a,h)
such that
\mathrm{IS}(a,h)\models n
.
Moreover, for given
|a|
, there is a unique increasing sequence
\mathrm{IS}(a,h)
with
\mathrm{IS}(a,h)\models n
.

(ii) We also have

\frac{C_{n}(q)}{q^{n}}=\sum_{d|n,\,d\,\mathrm{odd}}\,\left(q^{\frac{n}{d}-\frac{d-1}{2}}+q^{-\frac{n}{d}+\frac{d-1}{2}}-q^{\frac{n}{d}-\frac{d+1}{2}}-q^{-\frac{n}{d}+\frac{d+1}{2}}\right).

Moreover, all monomials
\neq q^{0}
in the previous sum are distinct.

Proof.

(i) The first formula follows from Lemma
5.2
since the sum of the elements in
\mathrm{IS}(a,h)
is
equal to

a+1+a+2+\cdots+a+h=ha+h(h+1)/2=h(h+2a+1)/2.

(ii) As in §
5.1
, with each odd divisor
d
of
n
we associate the two increasing
sequences
\mathrm{IS}(a,h)
and
\mathrm{IS}(\check{a},\check{h})
, respectively odd and even, defined by (
5.3
) and (
5.5
).
All increasing sequences representing
n
are obtained in this way.
Item (ii) then follows from Item (i): the first two terms correspond to
\mathrm{IS}(\check{a},\check{h})
and the last two to
\mathrm{IS}(a,h)
.
∎

The previous result immediately implies the following.

Corollary 5.4
.

The sum of the absolute values of the coefficients of
C_{n}(q)
is equal to four times the number of odd divisors of
n
.

5.3.
Zeta functions

We state further consequences of Theorem
5.3
.
Recall the
local zeta function

Z_{\mathcal{H}^{n}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}(t)

of the Hilbert scheme
\mathcal{H}^{n}
of
n
points on the two-dimensional torus
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
mentioned in the introduction.
In
[
10
, Th. 2.1]
, we gave the following formula for it:

Z_{\mathcal{H}^{n}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}(t)=\frac{1}{(1-q^{n}t)^{c_{n,0}}}\,\prod_{i=1}^{n}\,\frac{1}{[(1-q^{n+i}t)(1-q^{n-i}t)]^{c_{n,i}}}\,,

where
c_{n,i}
are the coefficients of
C_{n}(q)
.
Rewriting this formula in view of Theorem
5.3
yields the following statement, where
r_{n}(d)=n/d-(d+1)/2

is the integer introduced in (
4.1
).

Proposition 5.5
.

For
n\geq 1
we have

Z_{\mathcal{H}^{n}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}(t)=\prod_{d|n,\,d\,\mathrm{odd}}\,\frac{(1-q^{n+r_{n}(d)}t)(1-q^{n-r_{n}(d)}t)}{(1-q^{n+r_{n}(d)+1}t)(1-q^{n-r_{n}(d)-1}t)}.

This is a rational function whose numerator and denominator are factorized into degree-one polynomials.
Clearly the number of such factors in the numerator as well as in the denominator is equal to twice
the number of odd divisors of
n
.

Let us display the product above in two examples.
For
n=4
, which has only
1
as an odd divisor, we have
r_{4}(1)=3
. Hence

Z_{\mathcal{H}^{4}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}(t)=\frac{(1-q^{7}t)(1-qt)}{(1-q^{8}t)(1-t)}.

For
n=3
, which has two odd divisors, we have
r_{3}(1)=2
and
r_{3}(3)=-1
. Hence

Z_{\mathcal{H}^{3}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}(t)=\frac{(1-q^{5}t)(1-qt)}{(1-q^{6}t)(1-t)}\cdot\frac{(1-q^{2}t)(1-q^{4}t)}{(1-q^{3}t)^{2}}.

Proposition
5.5
allows us to give a similar formula for the
Hasse–Weil zeta function

\zeta_{\mathcal{H}^{n}}(s)
of the above Hilbert scheme; this zeta function is defined over the complex numbers by

\zeta_{\mathcal{H}^{n}}(s)=\prod_{p\;\text{prime}}\,Z_{{\mathcal{H}^{n}_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}}/\mathbb{F}_{p}}(p^{-s}),\qquad(s\in\mathbb{C})

where the product is taken over all prime integers
p
.
As an immediate consequence of the formula for the local zeta functions
Z_{\mathcal{H}^{n}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}(t)
we have

(5.11)

\zeta_{\mathcal{H}^{n}}(s)=\prod_{d|n,\,d\,\mathrm{odd}}\,\frac{\zeta(s-n-r_{n}(d))\,\zeta(s-n+r_{n}(d))}{\zeta(s-n-r_{n}(d)-1)\,\zeta(s-n+r_{n}(d)+1)}\,,

where
\zeta(s)
is Riemann’s zeta function.
Recall from
[
10
, Sect. 2]
that the Hasse–Weil zeta function satisfies the simple functional equation

(5.12)

\zeta_{\mathcal{H}^{n}}(s)=\zeta_{\mathcal{H}^{n}}(2n-s).

6.
Proof of Theorem
4.4

The following lemma exhibits a crucial link between the polynomials
F_{r}(X)
and
the expression in parentheses in the formula of Theorem
5.3
(ii).

Lemma 6.1
.

For
r\geq 0
we have

q^{r+1}+q^{-r-1}-q^{r}-q^{-r}=q^{-1}(q-1)^{2}F_{r}(q+q^{-1}).

Proof.

The formula can be rephrased in terms of polynomials in the indeterminate
X

as
\overline{T}_{r+1}(X)-\overline{T}_{r}(X)=(X-2)F_{r}(X)
.
By (
3.5
) the latter is equivalent to
(F_{r+1}(X)-F_{r}(X))-(F_{r}(X)-F_{r-1}(X))=XF_{r}(X)-2F_{r}(X)
,
hence to the linear recurrence relation of Proposition
3.5
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.4
.

It follows from Theorem
5.3
(ii) that

\displaystyle q^{-1}(q-1)^{2}\frac{P_{n}(q)}{q^{n-1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{C_{n}(q)}{q^{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{d|n,\,d\,\mathrm{odd}}\,\left(q^{\frac{n}{d}-\frac{d-1}{2}}+q^{-\frac{n}{d}+\frac{d-1}{2}}-q^{\frac{n}{d}-\frac{d+1}{2}}-q^{-\frac{n}{d}+\frac{d+1}{2}}\right).

We partition the previous sum into two sums depending on the sign of
r_{n}(d)=n/d-(d+1)/2
.
We obtain

\displaystyle q^{-1}(q-1)^{2}\frac{P_{n}(q)}{q^{n-1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{d|n,\,d\,\mathrm{odd}\atop r_{n}(d)\geq 0}\,\left(q^{\frac{n}{d}-\frac{d-1}{2}}+q^{-\frac{n}{d}+\frac{d-1}{2}}-q^{\frac{n}{d}-\frac{d+1}{2}}-q^{-\frac{n}{d}+\frac{d+1}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{d|n,\,d\,\mathrm{odd}\atop r_{n}(d)<0}\,\left(q^{\frac{n}{d}-\frac{d-1}{2}}+q^{-\frac{n}{d}+\frac{d-1}{2}}-q^{\frac{n}{d}-\frac{d+1}{2}}-q^{-\frac{n}{d}+\frac{d+1}{2}}\right).

Now replacing
r
by
r_{n}(d)
in Lemma
6.1
, we see that the sum of monomials corresponding to
r_{n}(d)\geq 0

is equal to

q^{-1}(q-1)^{2}\sum_{d|n,\,d\,\mathrm{odd}\atop r_{n}(d)\geq 0}\,F_{r_{n}(d)}(q+q^{-1}).

Replacing
r
by
-r_{n}(d)-1
, we observe that the sum of monomials corresponding to
r_{n}(d)<0
,
equivalently to
-r_{n}(d)-1\geq 0
, is equal to

q^{-1}(q-1)^{2}\sum_{d|n,\,d\,\mathrm{odd}\atop r_{n}(d)<0}\,F_{-r_{n}(d)-1}(q+q^{-1}).

To conclude, it suffices to divide by
q^{-1}(q-1)^{2}

and to remind that
P_{n}(q)/q^{n-1}=\overline{P}_{n}(q+q^{-1})
.
∎

We close this section with another formula for
P_{n}(q)/q^{n-1}
.

Proposition 6.2
.

We have

\frac{P_{n}(q)}{q^{n-1}}=\sum_{d|n,\,d\,\mathrm{odd}\atop r_{n}(d)\geq 0}\;\sum_{i\in\mathrm{IS}(\check{a},\check{h})\setminus\mathrm{IS}(a,h)}\,q^{i}-\sum_{d|n,\,d\,\mathrm{odd}\atop r_{n}(d)<0}\;\sum_{i\in\mathrm{IS}(a,h)\setminus\mathrm{IS}(\check{a},\check{h})}\,q^{i},

where
a=r_{n}(d)
and
\check{a}=-r_{n}(d)-1
, and

\mathrm{IS}(a,h)
and
\mathrm{IS}(\check{a},\check{h})
are defined respectively by (
5.3
) and (
5.5
).

Proof.

It follows from Theorem
4.4
and the explicit descriptions (
5.6
)–(
5.7
).
∎
