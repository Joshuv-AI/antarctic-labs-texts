Demographic factors, knowledge, attitude and perception and their association with nursing students’ intention to use artificial intelligence (AI): a multicentre survey across 10 Arab countries
Authors: Omar Al Omari, Muna Alshammari, Wafa Al Jabri, Asma Al Yahyaei, Khalid Abdullah Aljohani, Hala Mohamed Sanad, Mohammed Baqer Al-Jubouri, Ibrahim Bashayreh, Mirna Fawaz, Mohammed ALBashtawy, Abdullah Alkhawaldeh, Jamal Qaddumi, Samah Anwar Shalaby, Haitham Mokhtar Abdallah, Loai AbuSharour, Mohammad Al Qadire, Maen Aljezawi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) is becoming increasingly important in healthcare, with a significant impact on nursing practice. As future healthcare practitioners, nursing students must be prepared to incorporate AI technologies into their job. This study aimed to explore the associated factors with nursing students’ intention to use AI.MethodsDescriptive cross-sectional multi-centre design was used. A convenience sample of 1713 university nursing students from Bahrain, Egypt, Iraq, Jordan, Kuwait, Lebanon, Oman, Palestine, Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates completed a self-reported online instrument divided into five sections covering: (1) demographic sheet, (2) knowledge, (3) attitude, (4) perception and (5) intention questionnaire.ResultsMost nursing students in Arab countries have moderate levels of knowledge, attitude, perception and intention towards the use of AI. There was a significant positive association between knowledge, attitude, perception and intention towards the use of AI. A multivariate regression analysis revealed that understanding of AI technologies, self-perception as tech-savvy, age, clinical performance in previous semesters and knowledge of AI were significant and positively correlated with intention.ConclusionThe findings highlight the importance of targeted educational interventions and customised strategies to support AI integration within nursing education settings across Arab countries, equipping future nurses with the necessary skills and knowledge to use AI effectively in their practice.Keywords: Knowledge, Attitude, Perception, Nursing students, Intention, Artificial Intelligence

Background

Artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly transforming various healthcare sectors, with applications ranging from diagnostic tools to patient care management. In nursing, AI has the potential to revolutionize processes by assisting with clinical decision-making, personalizing patient education, and enhancing operational efficiency [1]. AI is the simulation of human intelligence in machines, enabling them to perform tasks that typically require human cognitive functions, such as learning, reasoning, and problem-solving [2]. For AI to be effectively incorporated into healthcare, nursing students and future healthcare professionals must be prepared by possessing adequate knowledge, positive attitudes, and clear perceptions of AI’s role in clinical practice. Understanding the factors influencing nursing students’ intentions to use AI in various regions, particularly in Arab countries where AI use in healthcare is emerging, is vital for ensuring a smooth transition into an AI-driven healthcare environment.

Nursing students' intention to use AI in practice is an important predictor of successful integration because it demonstrates their readiness and willingness to adopt AI tools into their future clinical roles. Various factors influence intention to use AI, including knowledge, attitudes, and views of the technology, as well as exposure to AI in educational contexts [3]. However, intention levels vary greatly among students, highlighting the importance of focused educational interventions that address individual factors [4, 5]. Understanding these factors is critical for creating AI-focused curriculum that help nursing students acquire positive attitudes about AI adoption.

Knowledge plays a significant role in influencing students’ intentions to use AI, which encompasses a wide range of applications, including patient education, nurse education, diagnostic tools, predictive analytics [6]. A significant positive association between level of knowledge and intention has been reported in previous literature among nursing students [7, 8]. Nursing students’ knowledge of AI varies greatly, with some having a rudimentary comprehension and others unaware of its uses in healthcare [8, 9], which may have a significant impact on their intention to use AI. Students with a better understanding of educational technologies, including AI-based tools, are more likely to recognise their potential benefits and are more receptive to their integration into their education [10]. Conversely, limited knowledge and awareness of AI have been identified as potential barriers to the adoption of AI among students [7, 10].

The attitude of nursing students towards AI was another key factor that influences nursing students’ intentions to adopt AI [11, 12]. Generally, evidence suggests that healthcare students have a favourable attitude towards AI and intend to use AI technology in their future careers [13]. Nursing students with a positive attitude towards AI are more likely to express a willingness to integrate AI into their practice [3, 14] and incorporate it into their learning experiences [15]. Nursing students’ perceptions were another contributing factor. Their perceptions of the use of AI shape their attitudes towards AI, which, in turn, influence their intention to adopt AI technology [3, 7]. Perception of AI affects students’ views on the usability, ethical implications and impact of AI on patient care. Other factors might influence students’ intentions to incorporate AI into their education, such as perceived support from peers, educators, and professional and institutional policies [16]. Additionally, adherence to social norms was also found to significantly influence student’s intentions to use AI-based tools in healthcare [15].

Perceptions of AI's utility and ethical consequences also influence nursing students' intents. Perception includes ideas regarding AI's potential benefits, ethical problems, and effects on patient care [12]. Students who see AI as a useful tool for improving healthcare efficiency are more likely to use it, whereas concerns about privacy and autonomy may discourage use [4]. Addressing these views is critical to promoting a fair understanding of AI's potential and limitations.

The relationship between intention, knowledge, attitude and perceptions of nursing students towards AI is dynamic in its nature and varies according to the student’s context. Understanding the relationship between knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions is critical for establishing successful teaching initiatives that increase nursing students' desire to use AI. However, there is limited research examining these factors in Arab nursing students, especially across multiple countries. There is also a lack of studies that measure intention across several contexts using standardised unified tools. This multi-centre study aimed to bridge this gap and provide data about nursing students in 10 Arab countries using unified tools, which will enable comparison in future research and offer insights to guide AI-focused curricula that can better prepare students for the realities of AI-driven healthcare.

Methods

Design

A cross-sectional design was used. This design offers efficient, large-scale data gathering over a short period of time and at a cheap cost.

Sample and setting

The study targeted a sample of university nursing students from Bahrain, Egypt, Iraq, Jordan, Kuwait, Lebanon, Oman, Palestine, Saudi Arabia (SA) and the United Arab Emirates (UAE). The inclusion criteria include students currently enrolled in nursing programmes, who have completed of at least one academic year and at least one clinical course, as well as being able to read and write in English, as the survey uses the English language.

Sample size was calculated using G Power, a tool for calculating the necessary sample size to achieve sufficient statistical power. G*Power was used to ensure the study could detect statistically significant associations between variables with an alpha level of 0.05, a small effect size of 0.15, and 80% power, resulting in a suggested minimum sample of 113 participants per country [17]. A convenience sampling technique was employed, targeting nursing students across 10 Arab countries: Bahrain, Egypt, Iraq, Jordan, Kuwait, Lebanon, Oman, Palestine, Saudi Arabia, and the United Arab Emirates. The final sample size for the study was 1713 university nursing students from 10 Arabic countries.

Data collection

Over the course of three months, from May to July 2024, data for this study was collected. An online survey through Google Forms was created, and nursing students received a flyer with a survey link that was posted on social media platforms, such as X, Facebook and Twitter. The flyer outlined the requirements for participation and indicated that clicking the Google link would be taken as agreement to take part in the study. Participants received multiple reminders to complete the survey, and the contact details of the primary investigator were supplied in case they had any questions or required any clarifications while the data was being collected. This made sure that participants received assistance and direction during the research. The use of online surveys through Google Forms allows for efficient distribution, seamless participant engagement and secure data storage.

Research instrument

A structured questionnaire was used as the primary tool to collect and measure data related to the study’s variables: knowledge, attitudes, perceptions, and intention to use AI. This questionnaire was adapted from previously validated instruments in the literature to ensure reliability and relevance for nursing students.

Intention

The questionnaire was adapted from Venkatesh, Morris [18] to assess nursing students’ intentions to use AI technology in their future nursing practice. The questionnaire consists of three questions that measured the intention of nursing students towards the use of AI in the form of a five‐point Likert scale ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”. The total scores were categorised into three groups: low intention (3.00–6.99) – student nurses have significant reservations or reluctance to incorporate AI technologies in their future careers; moderate intention (7.0–10.99) – students have a balanced attitude, showing openness to AI adoption but possibly requiring additional encouragement; and high intention (11.0–15.00) – students have strong enthusiasm and willingness to embrace AI technologies as integral components of their future nursing roles, demonstrating a positive and proactive stance towards AI adoption. The scale has previously been tested in student nurses and found to have acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.69) and outstanding concurrent validity [3, 12]. In the current study Cronbach’s α coefficient was calculated and found to be 0.873.

Knowledge

The questionnaire was adapted from Hamedani, Moradi [19] to assess the knowledge towards AI and its application from the perspective of physicians and nurses. The questionnaire consisted of eight questions that measured physicians’ and nurses’ knowledge about AI in the form of a three‐point Likert scale as follows: “Yes, it is correct (scores = 3),” “No, it is not correct (scores = 2),” and “I don’t know” (score = 1). Obtaining a score of 8–13.5 indicates low knowledge, 13.6–19 indicates average knowledge, and 20–24 indicates high knowledge among physicians and nurses regarding AI. The Survey was in Farsi, however, with the assistance of a professor who is native speaker and speaks English fluently, the researchers translated the survey from Farsi to English, and back translation was conducted by another professor to ensure accuracy of the translation. Cronbach’s alpha in the current study was 0.811, which represents very good reliability.

Perceptions

The questionnaire was adapted from Shinners, Grace [20] to assess the nurses’ perceptions of the use of AI and modified by Labrague, Aguilar-Rosales [3] for nursing students. The questionnaire consisted of 10 questions that measured the perception of nursing students towards the use of AI in the form of a five‐point Likert scale ranging from “totally disagree” to “totally agree”. The total scores were categorised into three groups: low perceptions (10–23) – student nurses exhibit scepticism or apprehension about the role of AI in nursing and may be hesitant to adopt AI technologies; moderate perceptions (24–36) – student nurses are open to the idea of AI in nursing but may have reservations or require more information to fully embrace its potential; high perceptions (37–50) – student nurses have a positive outlook, believe in its benefits and are willing to adopt AI technologies in their future nursing practice. The scale has previously been tested in student nurses and found to have very good reliability and outstanding criterion validity [3]. Cronbach’s alpha in the current study was 0.885, which represents very good reliability.

Attitude

The questionnaire was adapted from Swan [21] and Pinto dos Santos, Giese [22] that assessed nurses’ attitudes towards the use of AI in nursing, and was modified by Labrague, Aguilar-Rosales [3] for nursing students. The questionnaire consisted of three questions that measured the attitude of nursing students towards the use of AI in the form of a five‐point Likert scale ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”. The total scores were categorised into three groups: low attitude (10–23) – nursing students are mainly sceptical or negative about the use of AI in nursing; moderate attitude (24–36) –students are neutral or balanced about the use of AI in nursing; and high attitude (37–50) – students are enthusiastic and positive about the use of AI in nursing practice. The scale has good concurrent and criterion validity [22, 23]. Cronbach’s alpha in the current study was 0.89, which represents very good reliability.

Data analysis plan

Google Forms data were converted into an Excel spreadsheet, which was then imported into SPSS version 23 for analysis. To answer the first research question, the frequencies, means and standard deviations was used. Bivariate analysis – independent t tests and ANOVAs – were conducted to measure the mean differences between groups and to answer the second research question. Furthermore, to answer the third research question, Pearson correlation was performed to measure the association between the variables. In addition to Pearson correlation, multiple linear regression analysis was performed to test the association between the outcome variable (intention to use AI) and independent variables (demographics, knowledge, attitude, perception, and demographics). While Pearson correlation identifies the strength and direction of bivariate relationships, multiple linear regression offers a more comprehensive analysis by enabling us to control for potential confounding variables, thus providing a clearer understanding of each predictor’s unique contribution to the outcome variable. This technique also enables simultaneous assessment of multiple predictors, reducing the risk of biased results and increasing the robustness of findings regarding factors influencing nursing students' intention to adopt AI. Statistical significance was set a priori at p < 0.05.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was secured from the ethics and research committee at the College of Nursing at Sultan Qaboos University, Oman (CON/NF/2023/27), where the principal investigator and coordinating institution were based. Since data was collected using social media across 10 Arab countries, centralized approval from Oman was deemed sufficient, in accordance with international ethical standards. Ethical considerations were paramount throughout the data collection process. Informed consent was obtained from each participant, which emphasised their right to privacy and the anonymity of their responses.

A large proportion of the nursing students were female (69.1%, n = 1183) and were unaware how to use AI in nursing practice (60.7%, n = 1040); however, nearly three-quarters (73.3%; n = 1256) had heard of AI being used in healthcare. Post-hoc analysis revealed a significant difference in mean knowledge scores between the UAE, Iraq and Lebanon compared with the other Arab countries (p < 0.01). For details see Table 4.

Attitude

Mean attitude scores towards AI in nursing practice revealed significant differences based on gender (p = 0.024); males had significantly higher attitude scores (M = 35.59, SD = 9.25) than females (M = 34.51, SD = 9.03). Those who knew about AI in nursing practice had significantly higher attitude scores (M = 36.78, SD = 8.97) compared with those who did not (M = 33.58, SD = 8.99) (p < 0.01).

Nurses who had heard about AI in healthcare also had significantly higher attitude scores (M = 35.32, SD = 8.95) compared with those who had not heard about it (M = 33.54, SD = 9.39) (p < 0.01). Additionally, there intention scores across the countries were significantly different (p = 0.004). The highest mean score was Oman (M = 35.67, SD = 7.27) and the lowest was Bahrin (M = 30.68, SD = 8.66). A post-hoc Tukey test revealed a significant difference in the mean attitude between Bahrin and the rest of Arabic countries except Kuwait (P < 0.01). For further details see Table 4.

Perception

The average scores of perception levels varied significantly based on gender (p = 0.007); male scores were significantly higher (M = 33.76, SD = 8.35) than their female counterparts (M = 32.59, SD = 8.19). Knowledge of AI in nursing practice was associated with significantly higher perception scores (M = 34.80, SD = 8.34) compared with those without knowledge (M = 31.78, SD = 7.98) (p < 0.01). Similarly, previous knowledge of AI was associated with significantly higher perception scores among nursing students (M = 33.45, SD = 8.17) compared with those who had no previous knowledge of AI (M = 31.59, SD = 8.35) (p < 0.01). Country-wise differences were not significant (p > 0.05). For further details see Table 4.

Intention

Intention levels varied significantly between groups. The mean intention score for males was significantly higher (M = 10.51, SD = 3.19) than females (M = 10.15, SD = 3.17), (p = 0.029). There were significant differences in mean intention scores across the countries (p < 0.01). The highest mean score was Oman (M = 10.94, SD = 2.76) and the lowest was for Bahrain (M = 9.15, SD = 3.09) and Jordan (M = 9.88, SD = 3.08). Post-hoc analysis revealed a significant difference in mean intention scores between Bahrin compared with Oman, Kuwait, and SA. There was also a significant difference between Oman compared with Lebanon and Jordan. For further details see Table 4.

Multivariate analysis

Multiple linear regression analysis identified significant predictors of nursing students’ intention to use AI. Perception of AI (B = 0.114, β = 0.295, p < 0.01) significantly influenced AI usage intentions, indicating that positive perception correlates with greater intention to use AI. Understanding AI technologies (B = 0.278, β = 0.092, p < 0.01) also positively affects intention, suggesting that better understanding leads to stronger intention. Attitudes towards AI (B = 0.074, β = 0.211, p < 0.01) and self-perception as being tech-savvy (B = 0.390, β = 0.108, p < 0.01) similarly showed significant positive effects, which suggests that favourable attitudes and tech-savviness boost AI usage intentions. Age (B = 0.066, β = 0.074, p < 0.01) and clinical performance in previous semesters (B = 0.308, β = 0.060, p = 0.003) were also significant, indicating that older students and those with better clinical performance have stronger intentions to use AI. Lastly, knowledge of AI (B = 0.040, β = 0.061, p = 0.005) was positively correlated with intention, emphasising the role of AI knowledge in shaping usage intentions. The final model was significant (F = 126.283, p < 0.001), explaining 34% of the variance in AI usage intentions (R 2 = 0.342) (see Table 6).

Discussion

Our results shed light on the variables affecting nursing students' intentions to use AI, with knowledge, attitude, perception, age, understanding of the technologies used in AI, considering oneself tech-savvy and clinical performance in previous semesters as significant predictors. Nursing students in this study reported weak to moderate intention with none of the students having good intentions of using AI, which are consistent with earlier research that indicates a lack of knowledge and experience with AI in healthcare may stifle interest in incorporating AI into nursing practice [3]. Similarly, a study by Seo and Cho [24] highlights that weak intentions among nursing students are often due to limited structured AI education and inadequate exposure to practical applications, signifying the need for systematic educational programs to improve clinical competency and acceptance of AI. There was also a significant difference in intention scores among the countries in the study. These discrepancies indicate that regional factors and varying educational contexts might contribute to shaping nursing students’ intentions towards the integration of AI in nursing practice.

Despite the excitement around AI, several studies warn that relying on AI may accidentally undermine critical clinical skills and the patient-centred care that distinguishes nursing (Stokes & Palmer, 2020; von Gerich et al., 2022). The rapid growth of AI highlights the importance of establishing clear boundaries for its applications in nursing, ensuring that AI supports rather than replaces nursing responsibilities. Therefore, it is necessary to reconsider which interventions should be AI-driven, and which require human judgment. To do this, enhancing nursing students' and practitioners' critical thinking abilities as they manage AI's integration into patient care may help [25, 26].

The first significant positive factor associated with intention was students’ perception about the integration of AI, which is in line Cho and Seo [4] who reported a positive significant bidirectional relationship between perception and intention to use the AI among nursing students. That is, individuals who perceive AI positively are more likely to incorporate AI into their practice [27]. Studies in various disciplines outside nursing have shown that positive perceptions arise when students view AI as enhance their efficiency of their practice [15, 27]. However, there is a risk that when AI technologies are utilized blindly, they may add biases or inaccuracies if over-relied on, which could significantly impact patient outcomes. Nursing students must be trained to critically evaluate AI outputs and maintain a balanced approach that complements rather than disregards clinical judgment. It is worth noting that regional characteristics had no significant effect on perception, implying that individual experiences and education may have a more significant universal impact on the perception of AI than regional or cultural context [28, 29]. This finding contradicts earlier research that implies cultural factors can influence technology acceptability, indicating a viable field for additional investigation. This brings up an important issue: AI applications may already use in nursing but might not be explicitly recognized as AI. For example, tools for patient monitoring, and electronic health record management may incorporate AI-driven algorithms, yet students might perceive these technologies simply as part of routine practice rather than as examples of AI [30]. This shows that AI exposure in nursing education may occur without explicitly emphasising the underlying AI technology, leading to the under-recognition of AI’s presence in the nursing curricula.

Knowledge also emerged as a strong predictor of intention, with previous studies showing that possessing more knowledge of AI’s significantly enhances students' willingness to adopt it in their future practice. Previous studies indicate that solid knowledge of AI uses and advantages boosts self-esteem, lowers anxiety, and favourably affects students' willingness to incorporate AI into clinical settings [24, 31, 32]. However, lack of exposure to AI in formal nursing curricula may lead to misconceptions about its applications, as nursing students might encounter AI only in a limited or indirect context, leading to an under-recognition of AI’s role in nursing [30].

The knowledge level varied across the Arab countries with almost two-thirds of nursing students reporting weak to moderate knowledge, except in the UAE. Similar results have been reported in different countries – among health sciences students – including the US, Nigeria and England [23, 33, 34]; however, students from Germany and Pakistan had higher levels of knowledge [35, 36]. This indicates that there might be country-specific factors that impact students’ knowledge levels regarding the adoption of AI in nursing education. For example, in this study, male participants demonstrated significantly higher knowledge scores compared with female participants. This suggests a gender disparity in knowledge levels regarding AI in nursing education among the surveyed students. Furthermore, participants who had some familiarity with AI scored higher than those who did not. These results seem to contradict with results from Mohamed, Abed El-Rahman [37], which revealed a significant positive effect in both sexes regarding nursing students knowledge about AI.

Attitude towards AI is another factor significantly associated with intention. Several articles have reported the same significant positive correlation [13, 21]. These findings signify the importance of nursing students having a positive attitude towards incorporating the new technology in their curriculum. The attitude toward AI can also be shaped outside of nursing as nursing students are not living in silos and interact with healthcare providers and can be exposed to general technology in everyday life. Interestingly, in this study, only about one-third of nursing students reported a good attitude towards AI; however, several studies in western countries have reported a more positive attitude among nursing students [3, 4] as well as medical and health sciences students [22]. Similar to the previous factors, attitudes among males towards AI was significantly higher compared with females. This result supports results from Lukić et al., (2022) who reported a higher score for males compared with females, emphasising the importance of considering a gender perspective in education and training programmes with AI. Furthermore, participants in this study who were familiar with AI in nursing practice showed significantly higher attitude scores compared with those who were not familiar with AI. Future studies could examine whether increased exposure to AI within nursing education, through case studies or simulations, may further improve attitudes and intentions.

Overall, our findings emphasize the need for further AI exposure and education to foster nursing students’ intention to adopt AI in clinical settings. Kwak et al., (2022) stressed the importance of studying intention at two levels – first, at a cultural level with contextual factors, and secondly, the level country-specific factors that influence nursing students’ intentions towards integrating AI in healthcare. Nursing faculty members should develop curricula that address AI's ethical and clinical constraints to provide balanced training that promotes students' development of critical judgment and technical abilities.

This study highlights the importance of several factors that shape nursing students’ intentions towards integrating AI into nursing practice to help bridge the current educational gap: students’ attitudes, perceptions, knowledge of AI, clinical performance, prior exposure and country of origin. These results highlight the importance of targeted educational interventions and customised strategies to enhance intentions and preparedness for AI integration within nursing education settings across Arab countries. Leaders in nursing should design tailored educational strategies and policies to facilitate and enhance the intention of the integration of AI in nursing practice. Nurse educators need to provide nursing students with more practical workshops, simulations, case studies, seminars and campaigns to increase their knowledge, improve their perceptions and attitudes towards using AI in nursing, which will significantly reflect in their intentions. This method may lead to more widespread and successful AI applications in healthcare, improving patient outcomes and more efficient nursing procedures. Addressing regional disparities is also crucial; nursing education policymakers should ensure equitable access to AI education across different locations, accounting for regional differences in AI exposure and resources.

This study is not without limitations. Some inherent limitations arise from the study design – the cross-sectional design prevents establishing a temporal relationship between the variables; however, the large sample size and recruitment of heterogeneous samples from different Arab countries increases the external validity of the study. Furthermore, the reliance on self-reported data introduces the potential for response bias.

Conclusion

In conclusion, understanding the nuanced dynamics surrounding intention levels towards AI in nursing practice, considering factors such as knowledge, attitude, perception, clinical performance and regional context can guide the development of strategies aimed at promoting acceptance and successful integration of AI tools in nursing education and practice within the context of Arab countries.

This study's significance stems from its ability to pinpoint factors that nurse educators must focus on to advance AI intention. This study emphasizes the need for focused educational interventions that give nursing students real-world experience, simulations, and ethical conversations about AI by concentrating on elements like perception and knowledge that directly influence intention. These programs could improve students' understanding of AI's uses, give them more self-assurance, and make them more inclined to use AI in therapeutic settings.

Future research should examine the relationship between intention levels and focused AI education, as well as other contextual elements that may impact the adoption of AI in various educational settings. By implementing informed solutions, nursing leaders and educators can bridge existing gaps and ensure that future nurses are equipped to use AI to improve patient care and healthcare outcomes.
