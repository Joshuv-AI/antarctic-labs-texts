Recent progress in boron nanoclusters: from borophene to boron buckminsterfullerene
Authors: Lai-Sheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Boron nanoclusters remain an especially rich arena in chemistry because they continue to reveal unusual structures, novel bonding motifs, aromaticity, and dimensional transformations. Significant experimental progress has been made over the past several years that has substantially reshaped our understanding of these remarkable nanosystems. While the discovery of the B40 borospherene established that all-boron fullerene-like cages can exist, subsequent studies showed that larger boron clusters do not follow a simple fullerene growth path. Instead, B41− and B42− remain quasi-planar with borophene-like vacancy motifs, B48− adopts a bilayer structure, and B46− marks the onset of three-dimensional core–shell structures. The investigation of B56− further revealed the emergence of the B12 icosahedral motif characteristic of bulk boron, providing a direct molecular-scale link to the structural chemistry of elemental boron. Following the discovery of borospherene, metal incorporation was found to be an additional route to create boron cages, in which the metal atoms become integral components of the cage surface. Most recently, photoelectron spectroscopy of B80− has provided compelling evidence for boron buckminsterfullerene, representing a striking culmination of this structural evolution. This perspective discusses these recent developments and highlights how photoelectron spectroscopy in conjunction with theoretical calculations reveals the evolving structural principles that underlie borophene motifs, bulk-like boron units, metallo–borospherenes, and boron buckminsterfullerene.

Introduction

Boron occupies a singular place in the periodic table. Long before it was recognized as an element, boron entered human history through its mineral compounds, particularly borax and related borates, which were valued as fluxes and components of glazes and glasses.1 Thus, boron was first encountered not as an isolated element but as the basis of a wide range of practical materials. This early technological role foreshadowed a recurring theme in boron chemistry: boron rarely behaves as a simple element, but instead reveals its importance through the unusual structures and properties of the compounds and materials it forms.2 The elemental identity of boron emerged only in the early nineteenth century.3 Boron is highly reactive, forms stable chemical compounds with most elements, and is strongly influenced by impurities. As a result, the isolation and structural characterization of elemental boron remained challenging for decades,4 and the chemistry of boron developed along a path quite different from that of its neighbor carbon.

This historical difficulty is closely connected to the fundamental bonding problem that makes boron chemistry so rich. With only three valence electrons, boron cannot satisfy conventional two-center two-electron (2c–2e) bonding requirements, and is known as a “rule breaker”. Instead, boron adopts multicenter bonding schemes, electron-delocalized frameworks, and structural motifs that depart from the simple valence rules familiar in chemistry.5 In the solid state, this gives rise to complex polymorphism and polyhedral building units.6–8 Thus, elemental boron is not merely a less familiar neighbor of carbon; its electron deficiency places it into a distinct structural and bonding landscape. The same features that once made elemental boron difficult to isolate have now made it scientifically compelling. Boron-containing materials have become central to borosilicate glasses, detergents, ceramics, superhard materials, neutron-absorbing compounds, new pharmaceuticals, and emerging low-dimensional boron nanomaterials.2,9–12 These applications span from everyday technologies to advanced materials, yet they share a common origin in the electron-deficient bonding and structural adaptability of boron. Therefore, the history of boron is a progression from mineral utility to elemental complexity to modern materials and medicinal discovery.

Elemental boron clusters provide a particularly powerful playing-field to examine this progression at the molecular level. Unlike bulk boron, where structural complexity and impurities can obscure the fundamental bonding patterns, size-selected clusters allow individual nuclearities, charge states, and compositions to be isolated and interrogated directly. Hence, they serve as molecular-scale models for understanding how boron atoms organize when conventional valence requirements cannot be met. Over the past two decades, we have systematically investigated size-selected boron clusters (Bx−) using photoelectron spectroscopy (PES) and have discovered an extraordinary structural landscape, including planar and quasi-planar clusters,13–16 aromatic and antiaromatic boron networks,16–19 tubular and bilayer motifs,20–24 borophenes,25–27 borospherenes,28–30 and boron clusters with transition-metal-like structures and bonding.31,32 Metal-boride clusters represent an even richer area of studies that has led to a class of transition-metal-centered aromatic borometallic compounds,33–35 lanthanide inverse-sandwich complexes,36–38 metal–borozene complexes,39–42 and novel metal–boron multiple bonds.43–47

Recent progress has propelled the field into a new stage. Boron and boride clusters are no longer viewed only as exotic finite-size species, but as structurally precise fragments that connect molecular bonding, low-dimensional materials, and extended boron nanostructures. The sustained investigation of increasingly large boron clusters has led to the observation of the 2D-to-3D transition, the appearance of bulk-like structural features,48 and most recently the landmark boron buckminsterfullerene.49 This perspective aims to discuss recent progress in the study of elemental boron clusters, with emphasis on how joint PES and theoretical studies illuminate the bonding principles underlying boron's structural diversity and open pathways to new boron-based architectures and materials.

A number of reviews and perspectives have appeared on boron and boride clusters. In addition to several earlier reviews,50,51 a 2013 account by Romanescu et al. discussed the design principles for aromatic borometallic clusters,52 while a 2014 account by Sergeeva et al. focused on the structures and bonding of planar boron clusters.53 My 2016 review comprehensively summarized PES studies of boron clusters from planar structures to borophenes and borospherenes,54 whereas a 2017 perspective by Li et al. focused on borophenes and metallo-borophenes.55 A 2019 account by Pan et al. highlighted the fluxionality of planar boron clusters revealed by IR spectroscopy,56 whereas a 2019 review by Jian et al. summarized progress on boron and doped boron clusters.57 A 2022 review by Barroso et al. emphasized computational studies of structural transformations in boron clusters upon doping by one or two metal atoms,58 while a recent account by Cui et al. presented computational studies of boron clusters doped by alkali and alkaline-earth elements.59

The current perspective primarily focuses on elemental boron clusters and draws mainly on recent experimental progress in the author's lab since the 2019 comprehensive review.57 The article is organized as follows. Following the introduction, the PES technique used in these studies is briefly described in Section 2. Section 3 discusses the structural evolution of elemental boron clusters beyond B40, focusing on planar and bilayer motifs. Section 4 describes the recent observation of 2D-to-3D transitions and the appearance of bulk-like structural features. Section 5 discusses boron cage clusters, from the B40 borospherene to metallo-borospherenes, and Section 6 presents the remarkable recent discovery of the boron buckminsterfullerene. The article concludes with a summary and outlook in Section 7.

Experimental techniques

We have developed two separate PES apparatuses to study size-selected boron and boride clusters: one is equipped with a magnetic-bottle photoelectron analyzer54 and the other with a velocity-map imaging system.60 Both apparatuses consist of a laser vaporization cluster source and a time-of-flight (TOF) mass spectrometer. The photoelectron imaging apparatus has also recently been equipped with a cryogenic ion trap to cool clusters from the laser vaporization source,61 which is essential for high-resolution studies. All the PES studies covered in this perspective were done on the magnetic-bottle apparatus, as shown schematically in Fig. 1 and described briefly here.

Schematics of the magnetic-bottle photoelectron spectroscopy apparatus.

A pulsed laser beam (usually 532 nm) is focused onto a disk target, generating a plasma containing neutral and ionized boron atoms. An intense carrier gas pulse (He or He seeded with Ar) is delivered to quench the plasma and initiate nucleation and cluster growth. Clusters formed in the nozzle are entrained by the carrier gas and cooled via supersonic expansion. After a skimmer, cluster anions from the collimated cluster beam are extracted perpendicularly into a TOF mass spectrometer. Clusters of interest are selected by a mass gate and decelerated before photodetachment by a laser beam. Photoelectrons emitted over nearly 4π steradians are collected by the magnetic-bottle analyzer with a collecting efficiency exceeding 90% and detected after a 3.5 m long electron TOF tube. The kinetic energy (KE) resolution of the magnetic-bottle apparatus is moderate; its main advantages are its high collecting efficiency and its capacity to allow high photon energies. For large boron clusters, the key to obtaining well-resolved spectra is to create cold clusters by controlling the cluster residence time in the nozzle,54 and by using Ar-seeded carrier gas, as demonstrated recently.48,49

Structure evolution beyond the B40 borospherene

Systematic joint PES and theoretical studies showed that boron cluster anions (Bx−) up to 40 atoms are planar or quasi-planar (Fig. 2),13–16,53,54,57 in contrast to bulk boron, which consists of polyhedral building blocks, in particular the B12 icosahedral cages.6–8 An early ion mobility study showed that cationic boron clusters (Bx+) adopted tubular structures for x > 16.22 A recent study on neutral boron clusters found that the B9 cluster has a heptagonal bipyramidal structure.62 IR spectroscopy and theoretical calculations have revealed the fluxional nature of the planar boron clusters.63–65 The observation of the planar hexagonal B36 cluster provided the first experimental evidence for the viability of borophene,25 two-dimensional boron sheets containing hexagonal vacancies, which have been synthesized on inert substrates and are becoming a new class of synthetic 2D materials.12,66,67 While the global minimum of B40− was found to be planar, a fullerene-like cage isomer (borospherene) was also observed experimentally and was shown to be a higher-energy isomer.28 However, for neutral B40, borospherene is overwhelmingly the global minimum with a very large HOMO–LUMO gap. On the other hand, the global minimum B39− cluster was found to be a borospherene cage (Fig. 2).29 A central question was: how do the structures of larger boron clusters evolve? Do they become cages? When does the 2D-to-3D transition occur? When does the bulk-like B12 structural unit appear? Theoretical calculations suggested different structural motifs for larger boron clusters, including planar, cage-like, tubular, bilayer, and 3D core–shell-type structures.24,68–75

Experimentally confirmed structures of Bx− (x = 3–40).

B41− and B42−: persistence of planar borophene motifs beyond B40

Following the discovery of the B40 borospherene, an immediate question was whether larger boron clusters would continue to grow as fullerene-like cages, analogous to the growth pattern of carbon fullerenes, or whether two-dimensional borophene-like motifs would remain favored. The first clusters beyond B40− are B41− and B42−, which provided a direct test of this question. The 193 nm photoelectron spectra of both clusters (Fig. 3) were broad and congested, indicating complicated potential-energy landscapes and the likely coexistence of multiple low-lying isomers.76

For B41−, global-minimum searches revealed three closely spaced quasi-planar isomers related to the planar B40− structure,28 as shown in the insets of Fig. 3a. Although isomer I was calculated to be the lowest-energy structure, the simulated spectrum of isomer II, which contains a double-hexagonal vacancy, gave the best overall agreement with the experimental spectrum (Fig. 3a) and was assigned as the dominant species in the cluster beam. The calculated spectra of isomers I and III accounted for the low-binding-energy tail and other congested features, suggesting that they were also present as minor isomers. Thus, the B41− spectrum reflected an ensemble of closely related planar structures rather than a single isolated species.

The potential-energy landscape of B42− is even more complex. Several quasi-planar isomers with double-hexagonal or heptagonal vacancies were found to be energetically competitive, along with a tubular isomer. Comparison between experiment and simulated spectra showed that isomer II, a quasi-planar structure with a double-hexagonal vacancy, was the major contributor to the observed spectrum (Fig. 3b), while other low-lying isomers, particularly isomer III and possibly I, might also contribute to the broad first band and congested higher-binding-energy features. For both B41− and B42−, fullerene-like cage isomers were located in the global searches, but they were much higher in energy and did not account for the experimental spectra.

These results demonstrated that the structural evolution of boron clusters beyond B40 does not follow a simple fullerene growth path. Instead, B41− and B42− preserve quasi-planar borophene-related motifs, with double-hexagonal vacancies emerging as especially important structural defects. Chemical bonding analyses further showed that the dominant B41− and B42− structures exhibit delocalized π bonding analogous to that in polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons,28 extending the concept of all-boron analogues of aromatic hydrocarbons to larger cluster sizes.16 The B41−/B42− study, therefore, established an important starting point for the post-B40 size regime: even after the discovery of borospherene, the next larger boron clusters remain fundamentally governed by planar borophene-like bonding and vacancy patterns.

Bilayer boron clusters

Beyond B42−, the photoelectron spectra of larger boron clusters generally became increasingly congested and poorly resolved, reflecting the growing complexity of their potential-energy landscapes and the possible coexistence of multiple isomers. In this context, the B48− cluster was unusual because its photoelectron spectrum displayed two relatively well-resolved low-binding-energy features, followed by broad and nearly continuous signals at higher binding energies, as shown in Fig. 4a.23 These distinct low-binding-energy bands provided a useful electronic fingerprint for comparison with theoretical calculations.

(b) Two views of the bilayer structure of B48 (D2h). (c) AdNDP bonding analyses for B48. Reproduced with permission from ref. 23. Copyright 2022, Royal Society of Chemistry.

Previous theoretical work had suggested that several structural motifs, including bilayer, core–shell, planar, and cage-like structures, could compete in this size range.24 Our global-minimum searches for B48− and B48 showed, however, that the bilayer structure was overwhelmingly favored.23 Both the B48− anion and the neutral B48 cluster possess D2h bilayer structures (Fig. 4b), whereas the closest competing tubular, core–shell, planar, and borospherene-like isomers lie substantially higher in energy. The simulated photoelectron spectrum of the D2h bilayer B48− reproduced the experimental spectrum well, including the first detachment band, the more intense second band, the energy gap that follows, and the broad higher-binding-energy features. In contrast, the simulated spectrum of the low-lying tubular isomer did not match the experiment and could be ruled out as a significant contributor.

The B48− cluster represents the first experimentally confirmed bilayer structure for boron clusters. Structurally, it may be viewed as a bilayer B38 hexagonal prism composed of two equivalent B19 layers, joined at the waist by two V-shaped B5 chains. The two B19 layers are significantly inward-buckled, producing two strong interlayer B–B covalent bonds, in addition to bonding around the periphery. Chemical bonding analyses using AdNDP77 revealed a rich bonding pattern, consisting of localized peripheral B–B σ bonds, multicenter σ bonds within and between the layers, and delocalized π bonding distributed over the bilayer surface (Fig. 4c). These interlayer and delocalized bonding interactions account for the high stability of the bilayer structure.

The significance of the B48− study extends beyond the determination of a single cluster structure. It introduced a new structural motif in the evolution of boron nanoclusters. After the persistence of quasi-planar double-hexagonal-vacancy structures in B41− and B42−, the B48− cluster showed that boron clusters can transition into stable bilayer architectures rather than cage structures. Moreover, extension of the B48 bilayer motif leads to bilayer borophenes,23,78 which have been synthesized on Cu and Ag substrates.79,80 Thus, the B48− cluster serves as an important molecular-scale bridge between monolayer and bilayer borophene materials.

2D-to-3D transition and the emergence of bulk-like B12 motifs

The observation of the planar B41−/B42− clusters and the bilayer B48− raised a key question: at exactly what size do elemental boron clusters undergo a true 2D-to-3D transition, and when do the characteristic structural motifs of bulk boron begin to appear? These questions were difficult to answer experimentally, because our preliminary PES experiments showed that Bx− clusters with x > 50 gave rise to featureless spectra. Large boron clusters can contain substantial internal energies due to the strong B–B bonding, and the possible coexistence of multiple low-lying isomers can further obscure clear spectroscopic fingerprints, as shown in the cases of B41− and B42− (Fig. 3). Removing this large internal energy and creating colder clusters became a major challenge in our PES study of large boron clusters.

A critical experimental advance was the use of Ar-seeded helium carrier gas to improve cluster cooling in the laser vaporization source. Because Ar is a more effective collision partner for large clusters, Ar-seeded helium carrier gas enhances thermalization of the nascent clusters in the nozzle and during the supersonic expansion. We found that helium carrier gas seeded with 10–20% Ar was optimal to produce colder boron clusters, yielding resolvable photoelectron spectra, as shown in Fig. 5b for B46− and Fig. 5c for B56−.48 Even under these improved cooling conditions, however, the spectra remained highly congested, indicating that multiple low-lying isomers were likely present in the cluster beam. Thus, other techniques were needed to provide structural or isomeric information. Both ion mobility and IR experiments have been applied to small boron clusters previously.22,65,81 However, large boron clusters are also challenging for these techniques.

It turned out that chemisorption experiments provided the critical complementary information needed to interpret these complex spectra.48,82 As shown in Fig. 6, small planar boron cluster anions were inert toward C2H4, CO, and CO2 under ambient conditions, consistent with their smooth surfaces and strong peripheral B–B bonds. In contrast, B46− and B56− showed distinctive reactivity among the medium-sized clusters. For clusters up to B57−, only B46− and B56− chemisorbed C2H4 and CO, while B56− was uniquely reactive toward CO2 over the broader size range examined up to B68− (Fig. 6a). Size-selected kinetic studies further revealed isomer-specific reactivity: B46− contained at least two isomeric populations, with a major unreactive component and a minor component reactive toward C2H4 and CO (Fig. 6b). B56− was even more complex, containing at least three isomeric populations, including one that chemisorbs CO2 and another minor population that activates CO2 to form B56O− and CO (Fig. 6c). These reactivity patterns indicated the emergence of rugged 3D structures with exposed or interfacial chemisorption sites.

Global-minimum searches and electronic-structure calculations confirmed this interpretation. For B46−, the lowest energy structures were core–shell-type clusters rather than planar, bilayer, or cage-like motifs. At the highest level of theory, two core–shell isomers, C2 B2@B44− and C1 B@B45−, competed for the global minimum, with additional B@B45− core–shell isomers lying nearby in energy (Fig. 5a). The simulated spectra of these low-lying isomers reproduced the major features of the experimental spectrum: the B2@B44− isomer (1) accounted for the principal low-binding-energy detachment feature and the large energy gap observed, whereas the B@B45− isomer (2) accounted for additional intensity within the gap region (Fig. 5b). The coexistence of the two isomers was also consistent with the chemisorption kinetics. Structurally, B46− represents the first experimentally verified core–shell boron cluster. In the B2@B44− structure, two internal boron atoms are embedded within a 44-atom shell, forming a motif reminiscent of the interstitial B2 dumbbells in bulk γ-B28 boron.7 Thus, B46− provides the first clear experimental marker for the 2D-to-3D transition in boron nanoclusters.

The B56− cluster revealed an even more striking development. Its global minimum was found to be a nest-like C1 B12@B44− structure (5), consisting of an icosahedral B12 core partially enclosed by a 44-atom half-shell. The second lowest-lying isomer has a closely related nest-like B12@B44− structure (6), whereas other low-lying isomers include a single-B core–shell B@B55− structure (7) and a bilayer B56− structure (8). The simulated spectra of these four low-lying isomers collectively accounted for the congested experimental spectrum (Fig. 5c), consistent with the chemisorption evidence for multiple isomers. The two nest-like B12@B44− structures and the bilayer isomer are reactive toward C2H4 and CO, whereas the smoother B@B55− isomer (7) is comparatively inert and likely accounts for the unreactive population. Most notably, the bilayer isomer contains a unique interfacial pentagonal site that can activate CO2 and release CO to form B56O−, explaining the minor reactive population observed in the CO2 chemisorption experiment (Fig. 6c).

The appearance of the B12 core in B56− is a major milestone because the B12 icosahedron is the defining structural unit of bulk boron allotropes. As shown in Fig. 7, bonding analyses show that the half-covered B12 core in the nest-like B12@B44− structure follows the n + 1 Wade's rule for a 12-vertex polyhedral framework,83 with 13 delocalized skeletal electron pairs distributed over the B12 core. This bonding pattern is closely related to that in α-rhombohedral boron and the closo-borane B12H122−,84 and it gives rise to spherical aromaticity and enhanced stability of the B12 unit. Therefore, while B46− marks the transition from 2D to 3D boron nanoclusters, B56− marks the onset of the iconic B12-based icosahedral bulk-like structural features.

The B46− and B56− studies established that the structural evolution of boron nanoclusters is not a simple progression from planar sheets to bilayer motifs or cages. Instead, larger clusters access a diverse set of three-dimensional motifs, including core–shell, nest-like, and bilayer structures; their relative stabilities and reactivities depend sensitively on internal cores, surface roughness, and interfacial active sites. This combination of PES, chemisorption kinetics, and theoretical calculations provides a powerful approach for identifying structural transitions in large boron clusters and sets the stage for understanding still larger boron nanoarchitectures.

From borospherene to metallo-borospherenes

The B40 cluster established that all-boron fullerene-like cages can exist, but the structural evolution of larger boron clusters did not follow a simple fullerene growth sequence. As discussed above, B41− and B42− remain quasi-planar, B48− adopts a bilayer structure, and B46−/B56− reveal core–shell and bulk-like motifs. In this regard, the observation of lanthanide-containing metallo-borospherenes was especially notable because it showed that metal incorporation can stabilize a distinct type of curved boron cage that is not accessible in the corresponding bare boron cluster.85

Lanthanide–boron interactions differ substantially from those in transition-metal-doped boron clusters. Whereas transition-metal-doped boron clusters often form aromatic borometallic molecular wheels or metal-centered tubular structures,33–35 lanthanide-doped boron clusters are strongly influenced by charge transfer from the electropositive lanthanide atoms to the boron framework, together with significant Ln 5d–B 2p covalent interactions. This bonding pattern had already been reflected in half-sandwich and inverse-sandwich lanthanide-boron clusters.36–39 In tri-lanthanide-doped boron clusters, however, a new structural motif emerged.85 The photoelectron spectra of La3B18− and Tb3B18− (Fig. 8a) were observed to be relatively simple and well resolved, suggesting highly symmetric structures. The similarity between the La3B18− and Tb3B18− spectra further indicated that these two clusters shared closely related geometries and electronic structures.

(c) Schematics for the construction of the unique curved B18 framework in the La3B18− cage. Reproduced with permission from ref. 85. Copyright 2020, Springer Nature.

Global-minimum searches and simulated photoelectron spectra showed that La3B18− and Tb3B18− are hollow cages with D3h symmetry (Fig. 8b). In these structures, the B18 framework can be viewed as two triangular B6 units connected by three B2 bridges, generating three shared B10 rings (Fig. 8c). Each B10 ring coordinates to one lanthanide atom, such that the three lanthanide atoms complete the cage surface rather than occupying an endohedral position. The resulting geometry is a spherical trihedron, an unusual cage topology in which the metal atoms are integral components of the surface. This distinguishes Ln3B18− from conventional endohedral metallo-fullerenes,86 where metal atoms are trapped inside a pre-existing carbon cage, and from heterofullerenes, where substitution of a cage atom by a metal often induces substantial local distortion and is not favored.87

The stability of the Ln3B18− metallo-borospherenes arises from strong interactions between the lanthanide atoms and the boron framework. Fragment molecular orbital (MO) analyses indicate substantial charge transfer from Ln to the B18 framework, giving an approximate electronic description as (Ln3+)3[B18]10−. At the same time, the diffuse Ln 5d orbitals interact with B 2p-based orbitals of the B18 framework, producing significant d–p covalent bonding between each Ln atom and its associated B10 ring. Thus, the cage is stabilized by both ionic and covalent components of the Ln–B interaction. Chemical bonding analyses further reveal localized B–B σ bonds, multicenter σ and π bonding within the B18 framework, and fully delocalized bonding involving the Ln atoms and the boron cage. The Ln3B18− clusters also exhibit three-dimensional aromatic character, consistent with the high stability of this closed cage.

The broader significance of the Ln3B18− cages is that they expand the concept of borospherenes beyond all-boron clusters. The electron deficiency and structural flexibility of boron allow metal atoms to be part of the cage surface, creating metallo-borospherenes rather than merely metal-doped boron clusters. Calculations suggest that analogous D3h Ln3B18− cages should exist across the lanthanide series, implying a broad family of lanthanide-containing metallo-borospherenes with tunable electronic, magnetic, and chemical properties. Subsequent computational studies have further extended this idea to related metal–boron cage compositions.88–96 Thus, metallo-borospherenes provide an important conceptual breakthrough: boron cages can be stabilized not only by all-boron multicenter bonding, but also by strong metal–boron interactions that reshape the boron cage framework itself.

Boron buckminsterfullerene

The 2D-to-3D transition observed at B46− and the emergence of the bulk-like B12 core in B56− would seem to suggest that core–shell-type structures should be the dominant motifs for still larger boron clusters. This expectation was consistent with prior theoretical studies of B80. Although a fullerene-like B80 structure was originally proposed by filling the 20 hexagons of a soccer-ball-shaped B60 framework with additional boron atoms,97 subsequent global-minimum searches generally found the B80 buckyball to be a high-energy isomer and favored low-symmetry core–shell, cage, or other distorted structures.98–104 Preliminary PES studies of Bx− clusters beyond B56− also produced increasingly congested and poorly resolved spectra, consistent with either low-symmetry structures or the coexistence of multiple isomers. Notably, high-symmetry core–shell-type structures, known as B12-stuffed fullerenes, were proposed for larger clusters,105 starting from B84, which can be viewed as an icosahedral B12 core inside a B72 boron fullerene shell with capped pentagons and Ih symmetry. A number of related core–shell structures were also built around the B84 stuffed fullerene in the size range between B84 and B116.105–107 If the B84− anion maintains the high symmetry as the neutral, it is expected to give a characteristic photoelectron spectrum due to its high electronic degeneracy.

Against this background, we continued the PES studies of larger Bx− clusters using the improved cluster cooling conditions with Ar-seeded helium carrier gas, hoping to find high-symmetry core–shell clusters. However, we were completely shocked when we reached B80−. While we expected a diffuse and congested spectrum as observed using a helium carrier gas, what we observed here was a remarkably simple spectral pattern with three well-resolved bands (X, A, B), as shown in Fig. 9a. This spectrum was completely different from the congested spectra observed for smaller clusters in the size range of x < 80. Band X in the B80− spectrum yielded an adiabatic detachment energy of 3.1 eV and a first vertical detachment energy of 3.2 eV, whereas bands A and B appeared at 4.0 and 4.8 eV, respectively. The X–A separation of about 0.8 eV reflects the HOMO–LUMO gap of the neutral B80 cluster. Such a simple spectrum was completely unexpected for a boron cluster of this size and immediately suggested a highly symmetric and stable structure with significant electronic degeneracy. The spectrum is strikingly reminiscent of the photoelectron spectrum of C60−, which stood out among larger carbon cluster anions because of the high symmetry and the exceptional stability of the fullerene cage.108 This was a “Eureka” moment: we realized that what we had observed must be the B80 buckyball. The challenge was that prior DFT calculations all indicated that the fullerene cage was a high-energy isomer with a distorted symmetry, Th instead of Ih.109–112 The question was, could there be other high-symmetry structures that were missed by previous calculations?

Extensive global-minimum searches were subsequently carried out for the B80− anion;49 nevertheless, they reproduced the theoretical difficulty that had surrounded B80. At the DFT level, numerous low-lying structures were located, including planar, core–shell, tubular, and alternative cage-like isomers. In all DFT calculations, the B80 buckyball in both its anion and neutral forms remained higher in energy than low-symmetry structures, consistent with earlier theoretical studies. However, comparison with experiment provided a decisive test. Simulated photoelectron spectra were generated for representative low-lying isomers, and only the simulated spectrum of the B80− buckyball reproduced the three characteristic experimental bands, as shown in Fig. 9a. Other candidate structures gave congested or qualitatively different spectral patterns. Because significant populations of multiple low-lying isomers would be expected to smear out the spectrum, the clean experimental pattern provides a strong electronic fingerprint for the buckyball structure and its high stability relative to other isomers. The combined PES and theoretical evidence confirmed unequivocally the B80− buckyball structure.

The B80 buckyball also highlights the limitations of standard DFT for highly delocalized electron-deficient boron systems. In fact, DFT calculations suggested that the Ih B80 structure is a saddle point, which distorts to a lower-symmetry form (Th):109–111 some of the hexagon-centered boron atoms move inward and others outward. In contrast, higher-level MP2 calculations recovered the Ih structure as a true minimum,112 with the 20 hexagon-centered boron atoms staying at the centers of the hexagons. These results indicate that delicate electron-correlation effects are crucial for the B80 buckyball and that common DFT methods underestimate its stability. In the B80− anion, the extra electron occupies the triply degenerate LUMO of neutral B80, producing a Jahn–Teller distortion and lowering the symmetry to D5d. Even so, the splitting of the degenerate levels remains small, preserving the characteristic grouping of MO energy levels that gives rise to the simple photoelectron spectrum, as shown in Fig. 9b. The agreement between the MO energy levels and the photoelectron spectrum shown in Fig. 9b demonstrates why well-resolved photoelectron spectra provide the electronic fingerprints for the underlying cluster structures and how high electronic degeneracies in high-symmetry clusters lead to simple spectral patterns. Such one-to-one correspondence between MO energy levels and photoelectron spectra is commonly observed for diatomic molecules, validating the MO theory in its early days of development.113 The agreement for a system as complicated as B80− is truly unprecedented, providing unequivocal confirmation of its high symmetry and high stability.

The relationship between B80 and C60 is deeper than structural resemblance. The B80 buckyball is valence-isoelectronic with C60:114–116 the 60 boron atoms of the fullerene-like framework and the 20 hexagon-centered boron atoms together provide the same total number of valence electrons as C60 (Fig. 10). As in C60, the neutral B80 cluster has a triply degenerate LUMO and a five-fold degenerate HOMO, although the HOMO–LUMO gap of B80 is smaller, as shown in Fig. 10.

Chemical bonding analyses show how the electron-deficient B80 reproduces the bonding pattern of C60 through multicenter bonding, as shown in Fig. 11. In C60, the bonding can be described in terms of 60 single C–C bonds (2c–2e bonds) around the pentagons and 30 CC double bonds shared between adjacent hexagons. In B80, the corresponding bonding pattern consists of 60 three-center two-electron (3c–2e) σ bonds, 30 four-center two-electron (4c–2e) σ bonds, and 30 4c–2e π bonds. The 3c–2e σ bonds correspond to the single C–C bonds around the pentagons of C60, whereas the 4c–2e σ and π bonds together correspond to the CC double bonds between adjacent hexagons. Thus, B80 achieves a C60-like bonding framework not by conventional two-center bonds, but through multicenter delocalization commonly found in electron-deficient boron compounds.5 The twenty hexagon-centered B atoms share their valence electrons via multicenter bonding with the B60 framework to fulfill the C60-like electron counts.

The discovery of the B80 boron buckminsterfullerene closes a long-standing conceptual loop in boron nanoclusters. The B40 borospherene demonstrated that all-boron fullerene-like cages can exist, but the subsequent structural evolution of larger clusters appeared to favor planar, bilayer, core–shell, and bulk-like motifs rather than a fullerene growth path. B80 now shows that a true boron analogue of buckminsterfullerene is nevertheless attainable. In fact, because its 20 hexagons are filled by boron atoms, the B80 structure is closer to the triangulated surface of a geodesic dome than C60 itself. Its diameter is also larger than that of C60, which may provide more space for endohedral chemistry. More broadly, the observation of B80 opens a new frontier in boron fullerene chemistry, including questions of reactivity, doping, endohedral and exohedral functionalization,117–121 and possible bulk synthesis. If B80 or related boron fullerenes can be produced in macroscopic quantities, they could define a new class of boron-based molecular and solid-state materials. Therefore, boron buckminsterfullerene is not only the climax of the structural evolution of size-selected boron clusters pursued systematically over the past two decades, but also a signpost for the next stage of boron nanocluster and boron materials chemistry.

Summary and outlook

While small boron clusters favor planar structures, the recent advances discussed in this perspective reveal how electron-deficient boron organizes itself across different dimensions and length scales as cluster size increases. The structural evolution beyond B40 is especially instructive. The B41− and B42− clusters showed that planar borophene-like motifs persist beyond the first all-boron fullerene. The B48− cluster introduced a bilayer architecture and provided a molecular-scale motif for the viability of bilayer borophenes. The B46− and B56− clusters revealed the onset of true three-dimensional structures and the appearance of bulk-like B12 motifs. Metallo-borospherenes further showed that metal atoms can become integral components of curved boron cages. The recent discovery of the B80 boron buckminsterfullerene demonstrated that a true boron analogue of C60 is attainable, even though its stability and bonding arise from multicenter electron delocalization rather than conventional two-center bonding typical of carbon molecules.

These discoveries reinforce a central theme of boron nanoclusters: the structural evolution is not monotonic. Larger clusters do not simply progress from planar sheets to cages, nor do they follow a single pathway toward bulk boron. Instead, boron accesses a remarkably diverse landscape of planar, bilayer, core–shell, nest-like, and cage-like structures. Small changes in size, charge state, or electron count can redirect the preferred structure. This diversity is a direct consequence of boron's electron deficiency and its ability to distribute electron density through multicenter σ and π bonding. This complex structural landscape also underscores the critical role of close cooperation between experiment and theory. Photoelectron spectroscopy provides electronic fingerprints, while global-minimum searches and higher-level electronic structure calculations are essential for correlating these fingerprints with the structures and bonding of the underlying clusters.

From this perspective, several directions stand out as especially important. First, the structural evolution of still larger boron clusters remains largely unexplored experimentally. The observation of B80− raises the question of whether other high-symmetry boron cages, stuffed fullerenes, or related fullerene-like structures can be found at larger sizes. At the same time, the complexity observed for B46− and B56− suggests that many larger clusters may contain multiple low-lying isomers, making improved cooling, isomer control, and complementary structural probes, such as ion mobility, chemisorption, and IR spectroscopy, increasingly important. Second, neutral boron clusters deserve greater attention. Most experimentally characterized boron clusters have been anions because they are convenient targets for PES interrogation. The global minimum structures of small boron cluster anions and neutrals are generally similar, but there are exceptions. Thus, the study of neutral clusters will allow assessment of how charge state can influence the potential-energy landscape of larger boron clusters. The advent of tunable VUV light sources may finally allow IR-VUV double-resonance spectroscopy to overcome the size-selectivity problem for neutral clusters.81,122

A third major challenge is the translation of gas-phase boron cluster motifs into isolable molecules or materials. Planar boron clusters anticipated borophenes; the bilayer B48− cluster provides a molecular motif for bilayer borophenes; B56− reveals the emergence of bulk-like B12 units; and the B80 buckyball may usher in an era of boron fullerenes. These recent advances raise compelling questions. Can boron-rich aromatic motifs be stabilized by ligands, metal centers, or surfaces? Can B80 or related boron fullerenes be produced in macroscopic quantities analogous to the fullerite?123 Can endohedral, exohedral, or doped boron fullerenes be designed and synthesized with controlled electronic, magnetic, or redox properties? Answering these questions would move the field from structural discovery toward chemical control.

The discovery of boron buckminsterfullerene is a major milestone, but it also highlights how much remains to be discovered in elemental boron clusters. Boron continues to reveal new ways of solving its electron-deficiency problem: through planar delocalization, vacancy formation, bilayer bonding, core–shell growth, spherical aromaticity, metal-assisted cage formation, and multicenter bonding. Boron nanoclusters occupy a unique position between molecular motifs and extended boron materials. The continuing challenge—and opportunity—is to sharpen experimental tools for large clusters, discover new architectures that would be difficult to anticipate from either conventional valence rules or bulk materials alone, and ultimately to isolate selected motifs in bulk form.
