Contrasting pre-vaccine COVID-19 waves in Italy through functional data analysis
Authors: Tobia Boschi, Jacopo Di Iorio, Lorenzo Testa, Marzia A. Cremona, Francesca Chiaromonte
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study analyzes mortality patterns during two pre-vaccine COVID-19 waves in Italy, using data from its 107 provinces. Mortality is examined alongside information on mobility, governmental restrictions, and socio-demographic, infrastructural, and environmental factors using Functional Data Analysis tools. Publicly available differential mortality data and local mobility data from Google are processed with smoothing splines and aligned through landmark registration. The resulting curves are clustered to identify mortality patterns, and regression models are used to evaluate the impact of mobility, restrictions, and other factors on mortality. We find significant differences between the two waves: the first had higher, more concentrated mortality peaks, while the second was more widespread and asynchronous. Our results also support the effectiveness of timely restrictions in curbing mortality, and a strong positive association between local mobility and mortality in both pre-vaccine waves. Despite data quality limitations, our findings strengthen the evidence for the role of government restrictions and mobility controls during the pandemic.

Introduction

On January 30, 2020, Italy recorded the first two cases of SARS-CoV-2; a couple of Chinese tourists visiting Rome. The first non-travel related case was recorded in the municipality of Codogno (Lombardia) on February 19, 2020. With outbreaks quickly emerging in Lombardia and Veneto, local lockdowns were imposed in ten municipalities in the province of Lodi, and in one municipality in the province of Padova – affecting a total of over 50,000 people. As cases and deaths mounted, local and central authorities took progressively more stringent measures to limit mobility and social gatherings, e.g., suspending university activities, educational trips, sporting events, and exhibitions. These restrictions culminated with a general nationwide lockdown (March 9, 2020) and the suspension of all non-essential production activities (March 23, 2020), and were not lifted until May – when free movement was restored within the whole national territory.

After a fairly quiet summer, many countries in Europe, including Italy, witnessed a new rise in COVID-19 cases and deaths, leading to new containment measures. Face masks became mandatory, and gathering places such as gyms, theaters, and cinemas closed again. On November 4, 2020, the Italian government created a classification system whereby regions could be labeled as yellow, orange, or red based on a number of indicators of epidemic spread and stress on the healthcare infrastructure. Each color corresponded to different curfew rules and limitations on mobility. As a precautionary measure, during the holidays, red-zone rules were imposed in all regions – including those where the epidemic was not raging at the time (these were the strictest rules, equivalent to the lockdown of the first wave). In January 2021, as the second wave started to subside, restrictions were loosened again to the pre-holiday color system.

Prior analyses by our group, which employed Functional Data Analysis (FDA) tools1,2 on data from the first COVID-19 wave at the resolution of regions, characterized different epidemic patterns unfolding in different areas of the country, and suggested a relevant role for mobility and some socio-demographic, infrastructural and environmental factors as statistical predictors of mortality3. While our findings aligned with those of other studies4–7, they were potentially affected by signal dilution due to data aggregation. Furthermore, the limited sample size (Italy comprises only 20 regions) constrained our ability to use more complex statistical models without overfitting8. The current study builds upon Boschi et al.3 introducing significant novelty in data, methods and analysis aims. Here, we contrast the first and second wave of the epidemic, analyzing data on the two 150-day intervals from February 25, 2020, to July 23, 2020, and from October 1, 2020, to February 27, 2021. Moreover, we substantially increase spatial resolution considering 107 Italian provinces, as opposed to 20 Italian regions.

The increased resolution allows us to achieve a more granular characterization of the epidemic and its associations with potential predictors. Additionally, while some statistical techniques in this work relate to Boschi et al.3, we employ new methodology and define new proxies (derived from differential mortality curves) to handle shortcomings in public data. Crucially, while very different in terms of restriction regimes imposed on the population and of its mobility behaviors, the first and second waves of COVID-19 in Italy were both pre-vaccine (the vaccination campaign in the European Union, including Italy, started – for healthcare professionals and selected fragile categories – on December 27, 2020). Thus, contrasting them offers an opportunity to refine our understanding of the effectiveness of restrictions and non-vaccine-based interventions.

At the outset of our analyses, and yet among our key findings, is evidence of poor data quality. Specifically, we observe a vast under-counting of deaths in the official reports. This concerned especially the first epidemic wave (see also9,10) but persisted into the second. We also observe alarming discrepancies in case counts. Ignoring information on cases, properly processing information on deaths, and using FDA tools, we find marked differences between the two waves. The first was dramatic and spatially concentrated, hitting particularly hard and with fairly homogeneous timing a handful of provinces in Lombardia. In contrast, the second wave was less dramatic but more widespread, affecting more locations with less homogeneity in timing. Notably, provinces hardest hit during the first wave were among the least affected during the second; the contrast is particularly striking in the case of Bergamo, which experienced the heaviest mortality during the first wave, making headlines around the world11,12, and the lightest during the second. This may be due to the fact that inhabitants of provinces hardest hit during the first wave adhered more diligently to restrictions and safety mandates during the second, to a reduced number of people at risk (as an effect of the large number of deaths among the most vulnerable individuals during the first wave), or possibly to some kind of herd immunity13–15 (as an effect of the large number of cases during the first wave).

To evaluate how the timing of restrictions may affect epidemic unfolding, and more specifically, whether introducing restrictions early enough may help curb mortality, we create an ad-hoc variable measuring, for each province and separately for the two waves, the area under the mortality curve up to the point in time when restrictions were imposed. Such cumulative mortality should capture how much the epidemic had a chance to “build up” in the province prior to the implementation of restriction measures. In our analyses, this variable is far stronger than socio-demographic, infrastructural, and environmental factors as a predictor of the mortality unfolding henceforth – a result consistent with findings in other recent studies16,17. Assessing and comparing the role of mobility during the two waves is less straightforward. Restriction policies were very different in the two waves (uniform across the country in the first, localized and linked to the evolution of epidemic and stress parameters in the second), and so were the behaviors of the population in response to them. Likely as a consequence of these differences, the variability observed within the mobility curves of any given province, as well as the variability observed across such curves, changed markedly between the two waves. Notwithstanding these changes, we detect a strong and significant positive association between mortality and mobility in both waves, highlighting the importance of restrictions imposed specifically on the latter as tools to control the epidemic.

Our study offers some important elements of novelty compared to the vast literature devoted to the analysis of COVID-19 in Italy in the last five years – both in terms of study design and in terms of methodology. Compared to our prior work in Boschi et al.3 we increase resolution (province-level), contrast two pre-vaccine waves and, as we shall see in detail, use additional FDA tools and variables (e.g., landmark registration, concurrent functional regression, differential mortality curves and areas computed under them). Notably, Vinceti et al.15 do provide a province-level comparison of first vs. second-wave mortality, but do so without employing FDA tools or linking mortality to potential predictors. Mobility datasets and early lockdown effects are investigated in Pepe et al.18 and socio-economic drivers of mobility in Gauvin et al.19, but these works do not analyze mortality outcomes or FDA regressions. Ferrante20 examines associations between mobility restrictions and lethality, yet does not integrate additional covariates or FDA methodology. Finally, Scimone et al.21 employ FDA for autoregressive dynamics and spatial clustering of mortality but do not incorporate mobility or policy covariates in their regressions. Thus, through its scope, resolution and use of sophisticated statistical techniques, our study fills a gap not addressed by prior literature.

In the remainder of this article we present our results, provide a discussion of key findings and conclusions, and describe the methodology employed – including details on data retrieval and pre-processing. The data used for our analyses are available at https://github.com/tobiaboschi/fdaCOVID2.

Results

Here we present the salient outcomes of our analyses. First, we document inconsistencies in case and death counts across different sources and geographical resolutions. Next, we characterize mortality patterns during the two waves and evaluate the effects of various potential predictors – including socio-demographic, infrastructural, and environmental factors. Last, we study mobility curves, their dynamics, and their association with mortality curves during the two waves.

Inconsistencies in death and case data

Unlike our previous work on COVID-193, which employed the low spatial resolution of Italian regions, here we target the higher resolution of provinces. To date, Italian authorities (Dipartimento della Protezione Civile; DPC) provide COVID-19 case counts at both the regional and provincial level, and COVID-19 death counts only at the regional level (deaths by province remain undisclosed). However, differential mortality, which can be computed at both the regional and provincial level based on all-cause death counts published by the Italian National Statistical Institute (ISTAT; see Methods for details), is often advocated as an effective proxy when COVID-19 death counts are not available22–24.

A first, critical insight on data quality comes from comparing differential mortality as sourced from ISTAT and COVID-19 mortality as sourced from the DPC at the resolution of regions. These reveal a vast under-counting in the DPC official death records – markedly in the first wave, but also in the second9,10,25. Figure 1A contrasts, for each region, totals over the 150 days comprised in each wave. Overall, ISTAT and DPC data do become more consistent in the second wave, especially in regions such as Emilia Romagna, Lazio, Lombardia, Veneto, and Friuli Venezia Giulia. This, by and large, suggests an improvement in the DPC records – even though they remain much lower than ISTAT differential mortality in regions such as Basilicata, Calabria, and Sardegna. Notably, in Emilia Romagna and Lazio, DPC records were slightly higher than ISTAT differential mortality during the second wave. A second insight on data quality comes from comparing the number of cases reported by the DPC at the resolutions of provinces and regions. These harbor massive and concerning discrepancies. As shown in Fig. 1B, aggregating reported province-level cases by region, one obtains totals that do not match reported region-level cases. This is strikingly demonstrated by Molise and Sicilia, where the sums of the cases reported in the provinces are, respectively, over 12 and around 7 times higher than the cases reported at the regional level.

Due to the strong inconsistencies that still exist in the official Italian COVID-19 data, we ignore cases and focus on mortality alone to characterize the epidemic. Moreover, we do so using a differential mortality proxy instead of recorded COVID-19 deaths. This proxy has its own limitations, as it may indeed comprise some deaths not related, or indirectly related, to COVID-19 – but it is still more likely to accurately reflect mortality than the officially released deaths records and, crucially, it allows us to perform analyses at the spatial resolution of provinces.

Mortality patterns

We smooth daily province-level differential mortality data over the time intervals corresponding to the two epidemic waves – each 150-day long. This generates two sets of 107 curves. Next, within each set (i.e., for each wave) we shift the curves as to align their peaks. Intuitively, this landmark registration synchronizes the curves and facilitates their comparison net of their “horizontal” (i.e., temporal) differences.

Figure 2A shows aligned mortality curves during the first and second wave. Figure S1, in addition, shows the curves before the shifts applied to align their peaks, as well as the shift distributions. Full detail on the processing techniques utilized to produce and align the differential mortality curves is provided in the Methods.

Consistent with prior studies26,27, our curves delineate a dramatic and concentrated first epidemic wave, with similar timing across a small number of very hard-hit provinces, especially in Lombardia28, and a milder, more diffused second wave, with smaller differences and weaker synchronization across provinces. Notably, the largest mortality peaks were much higher in the first wave (around 20 daily deaths per 100,000 inhabitants) than in the second (around 5 daily deaths per 100,000 inhabitants), highlighting the grim death toll Italy withstood during the former. However, such death toll was very concentrated; many curves remained low, and just a handful had very high peaks. In contrast, the mortality peaks of the second wave were less differentiated, indicating an epidemic more evenly spread across the country. As previously documented at the regional level3, in the first wave Italy saw the unfolding of two very different epidemic patterns: a relatively mild one in the majority of the country, and a very strong one affecting the north-western areas. The current analysis, performed at the higher resolution of provinces, affords a more detailed description of this phenomenon: while the epidemic was more severe and more spatially concentrated in the first wave than in the second, both waves harbored two, possibly three different patterns; namely, a flatter, “mild” pattern in the majority of the provinces, a very steep, “exponential” one in a small number of provinces, and an intermediate, still “exponential” but less extreme pattern in the rest of the country.

The provinces that withstood the most dramatic losses during the first wave were Bergamo, Brescia, Cremona, Lodi, and Piacenza. These provinces (in dark red in the upper portion of Fig. 2A and in the left portion of Fig. 2B) are all contiguous, in an area between Lombardia and adjacent Emilia Romagna (the very first cases of COVID-19 in the country were reported in Codogno, a municipality in the province of Lodi). Interestingly, in the second wave, they all belong to the cluster with the lightest mortality (in yellow in the lower portion of Fig. 2A and in the right portion of Fig. 2B). This could be due to several factors. The inhabitants of these areas may have been more diligent in practicing social distancing rules and adhering to restrictions and safety mandates after the dramatic events of the first wave. In addition, the large number of deaths among vulnerable individuals during the first wave may have effectively reduced the number of people at risk in these areas. Finally, the large number of cases during the first wave may have induced some degree of herd immunity30.

In the second wave, the hardest hit provinces were Alessandria, Aosta, Asti, Biella, Cuneo, Udine, and Vercelli. The change in most affected provinces with respect to the first wave, along with the fact that fewer provinces belong to the lightest mortality cluster (in the first wave the yellow, orange, and red clusters, from lightest to hardest hit, comprise 86, 16 and 5 provinces, respectively; these counts for the second wave are 72, 28 and 7), confirm that, while less severe in terms of mortality, the epidemic had a broader spread across the country in the second wave.

We further compare the two waves considering the ranking of the province-level mortality curves based on their peaks (maxima; from smallest to largest) and the differences between such rankings – specifically, ranking in the second wave minus ranking in the first. These differences are color-coded on the Italian map of Fig. 2C, and show very clearly how many among the provinces most affected in the first wave (highest ranks) were among the least affected in the second (lowest ranks). Role of restrictions and socio-demographic, infrastructural and environmental factors

Next, we analyze associations between mortality and six scalar covariates representing a number of potentially relevant socio-demographic, infrastructural, and environmental factors, namely: aging of the population (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\%$$\end{document} Over 65); quality of distributed primary health care (Adults per family doctor); the potential of hospitals (Ave. beds per hospital), schools (Ave. students per classroom) and workplaces (Ave. employees per firm) to act as contagion hubs; and pollution levels (PM10) (see Table 1). The significance of this kind of covariates has been shown in recent studies3,16 related to COVID-19, both focusing on the first wave of the Italian epidemic, and on inter-generational relationships using data from 24 countries31. They could all be retrieved at the province-level from public databases with reasonable data quality. Full detail on their calculation and sourcing is provided in the Methods.

We also consider two additional variables calculated from the mortality curves themselves, which we call area before, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{bef}$$\end{document}, and area after, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{aft}$$\end{document}. These measure the areas under the mortality curve to the left and right, respectively, of the date when restrictions were introduced (March 9, 2020, for the first wave and November 4, 2020, for the second – the calculation is performed before aligning the curves; detail is again provided in the Methods). Hence, they represent the cumulative mortality at the time of restriction and after restriction, respectively. Intuitively, before the introduction of restrictions the epidemic accelerates, with contagions spreading and, metaphorically, filling a pool of “potential deaths.” Restrictions, ideally, stop this process – or rather, in practice, curb it with varying degrees of effectiveness. After their introduction, as the mortality pattern unfolds over time, the pool of potential deaths empties.

We use it as a potential predictor in our regression models, along with socio-demographic, infrastructural, and environmental factors. In a way, it can be thought of as an “intermediate” predictor, subsuming the effects of a multitude of observed and unobserved factors, and influencing the subsequent unfolding of the mortality pattern. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{aft}$$\end{document} is our way to define a scalar summary of this subsequent unfolding, capturing its cumulative severity. Similarly, another study16 pinpointed the onset of the epidemic in an area and showed that the delay in such onset (and thus the shortening of the time between onset and restrictions) is a significant negative predictor of mortality levels.

Mobility patterns

Several studies found evidence that local, short-range mobility worked as a key modulator of COVID-19 spread7,32,33. Here, we produce province-level mobility curves over the time intervals corresponding to the two epidemic waves smoothing daily values of the “Grocery & Pharmacy” and “Workplace” mobility indexes released by Google. These express percentage changes in mobility linked to shopping for necessities and commuting to work with respect to a pre-pandemic reference (the first five weeks of 2020; see Methods).

Association between mortality and mobility

We now turn to the analysis of the association between mortality and mobility curves. To begin, we evaluate the delay between changes in mobility and their potential impact on mortality. For each province in each wave, we compute a lag, defined as the number of days between the date in which mobility restrictions were introduced and the date in which the province experienced its mortality peak. Restricting attention to the provinces which did experience a discernible epidemic in each wave (those in the hard- and medium-hit clusters 2 and 3; see Fig. 2B), Fig. 4B shows a scatterplot of mortality peak values against lags, together with their marginal distributions, for the two waves. For these provinces, lags are much more spread in the second wave than in the first, but their medians are remarkably similar (19 and 17 days, respectively). In contrast, peak values are higher and much more spread in the first wave than in the second; in particular, the first wave peak distribution has a long right tail. Notably, there appears to be a distinct negative relationship between lag and peak size in the first wave, which provides yet more evidence for the importance of restrictions timing. In the provinces most severely affected by the epidemic, restrictions came too late to curb mortality; these provinces reached their very high mortality peaks shortly after the implementation of the national lockdown. In contrast, provinces that, while affected, were spared the worst impacts were those where restrictions came earlier during the epidemic progression; they had smaller peaks occurring at a later stage. Intriguingly, an analogous negative relationship between lags and peaks cannot be traced in the second wave, when even in the worst-hit provinces peak values remained relatively low.

Next, we evaluate the impact of mobility on mortality developing appropriate regression models. We consider as potential predictors of mortality curves both “Grocery & Pharmacy” and “Workplace” mobility curves, as well as two scalar variables from our prior analyses; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{bef}$$\end{document} (which summarizes the progression of mortality up to the introduction of restrictions), and pc1 (which summarizes the factors listed in Table 1). To gauge collinearities, we fit functional regressions of “Grocery & Pharmacy” and “Workplace” on each other, and on each of the scalar variables. Discussion

Notwithstanding the limited availability and at times poor quality of publicly available data, we were able to leverage Functional Data Analysis (FDA) techniques to characterize and compare the initial two waves of the COVID-19 pandemic in Italy. Our analyses were conducted at the province-level; this finer resolution compared to prior studies3,24,40,41 allowed us to shed additional light on critical associations and relationships – avoiding the loss of signal caused by spatial aggregations and enabling the use of more sophisticated statistical models. The two waves span a complete year, from February 2020 to February 2021, and both occurred before the beginning of the vaccination campaign. Therefore, the patterns and associations we observed were not influenced by vaccine availability and the progressive increase of vaccine-related immunity in the country.

Using functional hierarchical clustering, we identified three groups of provinces in both waves; one characterized by a mild mortality pattern, and two characterized by an intermediate strength and a harsh exponential pattern, respectively. Perhaps the most interesting observation obtained by contrasting clustering results for the two waves is that some of the provinces most impacted during the first wave (e.g., Bergamo, Como, and Lodi in Lombardia) were among the least impacted during the second. This could have been due to a reduced number of vulnerable individuals after the deaths in the first wave, to behavioral adaptations that led the population in these areas to adhere more strictly to recommendations on, e.g., social distancing and mask use, and possibly to a degree of herd immunity.

Next, we highlighted the importance of a timely introduction of restrictions. Given the poor quality of data on cases, we created a variable, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{bef}$$\end{document}, that quantifies the escalation of the epidemic prior to the introduction of restrictions based on mortality data alone. Using a number of regression techniques we found \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{bef}$$\end{document} to be a very strong predictor of mortality in both waves – in fact, a stronger and more consistent predictor than the proxies for socio-demographic, infrastructural, and environmental factors at our disposal. Potentially suboptimal proxies, collinearity, and possible confounding effects require that we interpret these results with care. But they do suggest that, by “hitting the brakes” on the exponential dynamics of mortality, restrictions play a pivotal role in curbing the spread of the epidemic and mitigating its impact.

Finally, we explored the relationships between mortality and mobility patterns. During the first wave lockdown, mobility exhibited very pronounced and similar contractions across the country. Following the introduction of the second wave color-coded restriction system, mobility contractions were milder and more geographically differentiated. Despite these differences, through additional functional regression exercises, we were able to identify significant and robust positive associations between mobility and mortality in both waves. We note that, through all our regression exercises, we adopted a minimalistic approach in terms of control covariates – using the first principal component of the variables listed in Table 1 as a single “summary” control. In future work, other, more exhaustive strategies should be explored. For instance, even though publicly available data is often not readily available to create appropriate proxies at province-level resolution, efforts to retrieve and analyze data on additional socio-demographic, infrastructural and environmental factors could provide further insights on the unfolding of the epidemic.

This study, alongside our previous work3, demonstrates the potential of FDA for analyzing epidemiological data. Specifically, FDA tools for smoothing and shifting (more generally, aligning) curves provide an effective way to handle outlying observations and compare phenomena that may occur asynchronously or in different time frames. Moreover, functional regression models can naturally incorporate a time dynamic along with predictors of different types. Also importantly, FDA-based analyses do not rely on the strict assumptions often required by standard epidemiological models, and thus allow a different perspective – and more flexibility – in representing the temporal evolution of the epidemic. FDA tools as a means to handle longitudinal data, or other data collected on structured domains in biomedical and public health research, are gaining in popularity42 but are still under-utilized. Showcasing well-established and recent FDA methods (including those developed by our group43) is one of the contributions of this study. We point out that smoothing, as well as FDA tools applied downstream from it, can be used to analyze spatial or even spatio-temporal data (2 or 3D domains) – provided the measurements are numerous enough21,44–46. Our study treated the temporal, but not the spatial, component functionally; extending functional treatment to the spatial component represents a promising avenue for future work in COVID-19 as well as other epidemiological research.

Methods

Data retrieval and pre-processing

Deaths and cases

Differential mortality was computed using the daily all-cause death counts provided by the Italian National Statistical Institute (ISTAT) for 7270 Italian municipalities (comprising about 93.5% of the Italian population). In particular, we take the difference between daily deaths and the average daily deaths in the five years prior to the pandemic (2015–2019), and divide by the total population as of January 1, 2019. This is done for each province and for each day in the first and the second waves of the epidemic; that is, for the two 150-day intervals from February 25, 2020, to July 23, 2020, and from October 1, 2020, to February 27, 2021, respectively.

Daily province-level differential mortality values are then smoothed into curves using cubic smoothing B-splines2 with knots approximately at each week (21 knots equally spaced over 150 days – the time domain of each wave) and a roughness penalty on the curve second derivative. Employing one knot per week allows us to focus on general trends and smooth out daily fluctuations. Separately for each wave, the smoothing parameter is selected as to minimize the average generalized cross-validation (GCV) error47 across all 107 province-level curves (the same criterion is employed to smooth mobility data; see below). GCV is a well-established, widely used selection criterion in the literature3,48; in Fig. S12 we compare it to alternative criteria and provide a quantitative justification for our choice. Next, we align the differential mortality curves using landmark registration2. Specifically, for each wave, we shift curves horizontally in order to align their peaks. For curves with multiple peaks, we consider the highest peak occurring between day 10 and day 100. The new common peak time is set to be equal to the time of the earliest peak, specifically day 20 (peak of the province of Lodi) for the first wave, and day 33 (peak of the province of Cagliari) for the second wave. Finally, we add (remove) days at the end (beginning) of the domain for the shifted curves, to guarantee the same 150-day length for all curves (curves are smoothed one more time after this “domain integration”, again choosing the smoothing parameter by minimizing the GCV criterion). As a result, the aligned, common time in each wave is the one of the province with the earliest peak. All computations are performed using the R package fda49.

We repeat mortality calculations at the level of regions, in order to compare results with the data provided by the Italian Civil Protection agency (Dipartimento della Protezione Civile; DPC), which releases regional daily counts of recorded COVID-19 deaths since February 2020. We divide both ISTAT differential death counts and DPC death counts by the total population as of January 1, 2019, to obtain ISTAT differential mortality and DPC mortality, respectively. We do not perform any further preprocessing, since we employ these data only for validation purposes.

We also consider two additional variables calculated from our differential mortality curves, which we call area before, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{bef}$$\end{document}, and area after, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{aft}$$\end{document}. For each province, these measure the areas under the curve to the left and the right, respectively, of the date when restrictions were introduced (March 9, 2020, for the first wave, and November 4, 2020, for the second). Note that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{bef}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{aft}$$\end{document} are computed before shifting the mortality curves, and therefore the number of days used to calculate each of them is the same for all provinces. Moreover, since both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{bef}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{aft}$$\end{document} are highly right-skewed, we take their logarithms to normalize them before using them in our regression models (see below).

Local mobility

Daily mobility indexes are provided by Google at the resolution of Italian provinces50. These measure the fractional reduction in mobility with respect to levels registered in the first five weeks of 2020 (January 3 to February 6), and are organized in categories based on mobility aims. We focus on two categories that capture the local, short-range mobility of individuals, which was allowed in all provinces also during mobility restrictions: the “Grocery & Pharmacy” index, which accounts for local trips to grocery and pharmacy stores, and the “Workplace” index, which accounts for trips to and from workplaces. We produce mobility curves by smoothing these daily data with the same procedure used for mortality curves (see above).

Statistical analyses

Clustering and rank comparisons

We perform functional clustering of the shifted province-level mortality curves, separately for each wave. This is done with an agglomerative hierarchical algorithm employing a Euclidean functional distance (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L^2$$\end{document}) and complete linkage. The (squared) \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L^2$$\end{document} distance between two generic curves \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_i(t)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_j(t)$$\end{document} on a generic domain \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[t_1, t_2]$$\end{document} is simply defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{1}{ (t_2 - t_1)} \int _{ t_1}^{ t_2} \left( y_i(t) - y_j(t) \right) ^{2} \, dt$$\end{document}. Starting from the matrix of pair-wise distances \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{d_{i,j}\}$$\end{document}, the agglomerative hierarchical algorithm merges singletons based on their distance, and clusters based on the maximal distance between their members (complete linkage).

To further compare province-level mortality between the two waves, we rank mortality curves based on their peak values (the curve with the lowest peak is ranked first and the one with the highest peak is ranked 107th), separately for each wave. Next, for each province, we compute the difference between its rankings in the second and first wave.

Feature selection for scalar and functional regression

The second model is a function-on-scalar regression, where the full mortality curves (functional response) are modeled as a function of the same scalar predictors. Concurrent function-on-function regression modeling

We formulate both models A and B described in the Results section as “lagged” concurrent function-on-function regressions2,34 where the functional response (mortality) at time t is assumed to depend only on the functional predictor(s) (one or both mobility indexes) at time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t-\ell$$\end{document}; in this formulation the lag \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ell$$\end{document} is an additional model parameter. We shift the mobility curves of each province by the same shift used to align its mortality curves (Fig. S10 shows the mobility curves after this alignment), and we fit the models with 10 different values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ell$$\end{document} ranging from 15 to 24 days. This range is consistent with the observed lag distributions (Fig. 4B), previous findings36, and established epidemiological frameworks37–39. Fitting our models with different lags addresses potential stability pitfalls35, and provides a robust characterization of the relationships under investigation. We use tensor-product terms of type ti to construct the smooth, t-varying coefficient functions.
