Postoperative hospital stay and associated factors among adult surgical patients at a specialized hospital in Ethiopia
Authors: Mihret Kefie, Addisu Getie, Abebe Dilie Afenigus, Baye Tsegaye Amlak, Mengistu Abebe Messelu, Setarg Ayenew Birhanie, Zinaw Beyene
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPostoperative length of hospital stay is a widely recognized indicator of surgical care quality and hospital performance. Extended hospital stays increase the risk of hospital-acquired infections and limit bed availability for new patients. Despite its clinical relevance, there is limited evidence on the length of postoperative stay and its associated factors among adult surgical patients in Ethiopia. This study aimed to assess the duration of postoperative hospital stay and its associated factors among adult patients admitted to the surgical ward of Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Ethiopia.MethodsAn institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted on 515 adult postoperative patients from October 30, 2024, to January 30, 2025, selected via a systematic random sampling method. Prolonged postoperative hospital stay was defined as ≥ 75th percentile of length of stay within each surgical procedure group. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews and chart reviews and analyzed using logistic regression, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.ResultsThis study found that 29.2% of patients experienced prolonged postoperative hospital stays (95% CI: 25.2–33.1), with a median length of stay of 5 days (IQR: 3–8). The median surgery duration was 80 min (IQR: 45–115). Factors associated with prolonged postoperative stay were preoperative anemia (AOR: 3.81, 95% CI: 2.39–6.06), delayed ambulation (AOR: 2.57, 95% CI: 1.66–3.99), hospital-acquired pneumonia (AOR: 3.53, 95% CI: 1.75–7.11), and surgical site infection (AOR: 2.74, 95% CI: 1.38–5.43).ConclusionAlmost one-third of the participants experienced an extended postoperative hospital stay. Key contributing factors were preoperative anemia, delayed ambulation, hospital-acquired pneumonia, and surgical site infections. Interventions such as enhanced infection control, early mobilization, and preoperative anemia screening and management are recommended to reduce length of stay.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03358-x.Keywords: Prolonged hospital stays, Postoperative patients, Ethiopia

Introduction

Length of postoperative hospital stay (LOS) refers to the duration a patient remains hospitalized from the day of surgery to discharge [1, 2]. It is a key indicator of healthcare quality, operative performance, patient care, and hospital resource utilization [3–5], and is essential for optimal surgical patient management [6]. Postoperative complications can prolong LOS [7]. There is no universal cutoff for prolonged LOS (PLOS) [5]; it is generally defined as a stay exceeding the expected duration for a given procedure [3]. Studies use different thresholds, including the median (50th percentile), 75th percentile [2, 8, 9], or 90th percentile [5], with the 75th percentile commonly applied to assess efficiency and patient recovery [2, 8–10].

Globally, 3–25% of hospitalized patients experience prolonged stays due to postoperative complications [11]. Annually, over 143 million surgeries are performed in low- and middle-income countries, straining healthcare systems [12], and the average hospital stay rose nearly 19% from 2019 to 2022 [13]. Reported prevalence of PLOS varies by region: Europe 4.92–12.41% [14–16], Asia 5.4–30.5% [10, 17, 18], and Africa 18.6–63% [6, 8, 9, 19, 20]. Prolonged length of postoperative hospital stays imposes challenges on healthcare services, newly admitted patients, and caregivers [21]. It can increase hospital-acquired infections, healthcare costs, bed shortages, and antibiotic resistance [5, 6, 22].

In Africa, prolonged LOS is associated with comorbidities, older age, female sex, government-funded care, emergency surgery, hospital-acquired pneumonia, surgical site infections, preoperative anemia, referral source, and longer surgery duration [6, 8, 9, 20]. Reducing LOS is important to lower costs, minimize infection risk, and improve patient quality of life [6, 23]. Healthcare systems are adopting strategies to optimize hospital stays, care quality, and costs [24]. In Ethiopia, the Ministry of Health has implemented the World Health Organization (WHO) surgical safety checklist to improve surgical outcomes and reduce hospital stays [25], yet studies indicate that postoperative PLOS remains high [8, 9].

Data on hospital stay durations and their associated factors are limited in low-income countries, particularly in Ethiopia. Although a study in the Amhara region assessed overall hospital stays for surgical patients, no research has specifically examined postoperative stay lengths at Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, a major referral center serving a large catchment area in northwest Ethiopia with high surgical demand. Additionally, prior studies have not explored variations in postoperative stay across specific surgical procedures or used a standardized approach to define prolonged stay. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the length of postoperative hospital stay and its associated factors among adult surgical patients at this institution, using a procedure-specific definition of prolonged stay. Unlike prior studies that applied a uniform cutoff, this study uses a procedure-specific 75th percentile threshold to define prolonged stay, enabling more accurate assessment across varied surgeries.

Methods

Study setting and design

A cross-sectional study design was conducted at Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital between October 30, 2024, and January 30, 2025. The hospital is situated in Debre Markos city administration, within the East Gojjam Zone of the Amhara Regional State. It lies approximately 300 km northwest of Addis Ababa, the capital of Ethiopia, and about 265 km southwest of Bahirdar, the capital of the Amhara region [26]. According to the 2023 third-quarter report from the Human Resource Administration of Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, the facility serves a catchment population exceeding 5 million people. The hospital employs over 522 healthcare professionals, including 240 nurses. Within the surgical ward, there are 44 nurses, 9 surgeons, and 2 Integrated Emergency Surgery and Obstetrics (IESO) practitioners. The ward has a total of 44 beds, and over the past year, approximately 4,500 adult patients underwent surgery and were admitted to the surgical ward.

Population

All adult patients who underwent major surgery and were admitted to the surgical ward at Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital during the study period were included. Patients who underwent major orthopedic surgeries were excluded because these procedures typically require longer and highly variable recovery periods, which could disproportionately influence postoperative length-of-stay estimates and reduce comparability across other surgical groups.

Sample size determination

The sample size (n) was determined using a single population proportion formula, based on the following assumptions: a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), a 5% significance level (α), a 4% margin of error, and a proportion of 26.5% derived from a previous study conducted in the Amhara region [8]. After adding a 10% non-response rate, the final sample size was 515.

Sampling technique and procedure

According to the surgical ward registration book, a total of 4,500 adult patients underwent surgery and were admitted to the surgical ward at Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital over the past year. To determine the average monthly admissions, the total was divided by 12, yielding 375 patients per month. Over the three-month data collection period, this resulted in a study population (N) of 1,125. Dividing this by the desired sample size (n = 515) produced a sampling interval (k) of 2. The first participant was selected using a lottery method, and subsequent participants were enrolled using systematic random sampling at every second interval until the required sample size was reached.

Variable

Dependent variable

Length of postoperative hospital stays.

Independent variables

Sociodemographic factors (age, sex, marital status, education, residence, occupation, health insurance), admission-related factors (type and time of admission, discharge planning, referral source), clinical factors (comorbidities such as diabetes, hypertension, HIV/AIDS, asthma; in-hospital complications including pneumonia, surgical site infection/leakage, DVT, bed sores; surgery duration, preoperative anemia, antibiotic prophylaxis, delayed ambulation, surgery type, BMI, reoperation, number of medications), and behavioral factors (alcohol use and social support).

Operational definition

Prolonged length of postoperative hospital stay was defined as the length of hospital stay in days ≥ 75th percentile of length of stay separately within each group of surgical procedure [2, 8]. This approach addresses heterogeneity in recovery durations across procedures and allows for meaningful comparison while accounting for procedure-specific expectations. A normal postoperative hospital stay is defined as a stay shorter than the 75th percentile of stays within each group of surgical procedure [2, 8, 9]. In-hospital complications are unintended harms from medical care after surgery, such as hospital-acquired pneumonia, surgical site infections, bed sores, and deep vein thrombosis [8, 9]. Delayed ambulation is defined as the act of a patient unable to begin to mobilize either independently or with assistance within 24 h of a surgical procedure [27].

Data collection tools and procedures

Data were collected from postoperative adult patients in the surgical ward using a structured, pretested questionnaire administered via interviews, supplemented by retrospective medical chart reviews and direct measurements. The data collection instruments were adapted from previously published studies [8, 9, 20, 28, 29]. The data collection tool consisted of four sections: socio-demographic information, admission-related details, behavioral factors, and clinical data. To facilitate data collection, three BSc nurses and one MSc nurse (serving as supervisor), all working in the surgical ward, were recruited. A one-day training was provided covering data collection procedures using Kobo Collect software, informed consent protocols, and clarification of ambiguous questions. Data were then collected through interviewer-administered questionnaires using Kobo Collect, with close supervision by the principal investigator and supervisor throughout the process. Patient charts were carefully reviewed to ensure completeness of all key variables. Incomplete data were cross-checked with operative notes, discharge summaries, and nursing records, with exclusions or corrections documented, ensuring an accurate and reliable dataset without affecting study validity.

Data quality control measures

To ensure consistency, the questionnaire was translated into Amharic and then back-translated into English by language experts. Face validity was assessed by three senior surgical clinicians and two researchers. The principal investigator provided a one-day training session for data collectors and the supervisor, covering the study objectives, questionnaire content, data collection procedures using Kobo Collect software, interview techniques, and ethical considerations. The principal investigator and supervisor reviewed the collected data daily for completeness and provided additional guidance as needed. A pretest was conducted on 5% of the total sample to evaluate the clarity and reliability of the tool. Based on the findings, minor revisions were made to improve question wording and formatting before the final data collection, ensuring better comprehension and data quality.

Data processing and analysis

The collected data were exported to SPSS version 27 for cleaning and analysis. Descriptive statistics were summarized using medians with interquartile ranges and/or frequencies with percentages, and results were presented in tables, figures, and narrative text. Associations between dependent and independent variables were examined using a binary logistic regression model. Model fitness was assessed with the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, with a non-significant result (p = 0.53) indicating good fit. Multicollinearity among independent variables was checked using the Variance Inflation Factor (mean VIF = 1.14), with a threshold of VIF < 10 applied to ensure independence. Variables with p < 0.25 in the bivariate analysis, as recommended for variable selection in logistic regression, were included in the multivariable model. Statistical significance was determined at p < 0.05, with adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported [30].

Results

Participant characteristics

Of the 515 participants, 511 completed the study (99.2% response rate); 56.4% were male, with a median age of 40 years (IQR: 30–53) (Table 1). The median surgery duration was 80 min (IQR: 45–115), with 62% lasting 70 min or less; 36.8% ambulated after 24 h, and 19.4% experienced postoperative complications (Table 2). Additionally, 54.4% underwent emergency surgery, 67.1% were admitted during daytime, 65.8% were referred from public facilities, and 53.2% lacked discharge planning information (Table 3). In terms of behavioral characteristics, 9.4% were alcohol-dependent, and 43.2% reported low social support. No one died, and none of the patients were transferred to other hospitals.

Length of postoperative hospital stay

Among 511 postoperative patients, 149 (29.2%; 95% CI: 25.2–33.1) had prolonged hospital stays, defined as ≥ 75th percentile. Laparotomy accounted for the largest share of prolonged stays (52 patients, 35%), followed by appendectomy (40 patients) (Table 4). Factors associated with prolonged length of postoperative hospital stay

In the bivariate analysis, several factors were linked to PLOS, including age, preoperative anemia, delayed ambulation, reoperation, number of medications, admission time, surgery duration, comorbidities, hospital-acquired pneumonia, surgical site infection, and referral source. However, multivariable logistic regression identified only four factors, delayed ambulation, preoperative anemia, hospital-acquired pneumonia, and surgical site infection, as significantly associated with PLOS (p < 0.05, 95% CI). Patients with preoperative anemia had 3.8 times higher odds of prolonged stay compared to those without anemia (AOR = 3.81; 95% CI: 2.39–6.06). Similarly, those with delayed ambulation had 2.57 times greater odds of extended hospitalization than those who ambulated early (AOR = 2.57; 95% CI: 1.66–3.99). Patients who developed hospital-acquired pneumonia had 3.53 times increased odds of prolonged stay (AOR = 3.53; 95% CI: 1.75–7.11), while surgical site infections raised the odds by 2.74 times (AOR = 2.74; 95% CI: 1.38–5.43) (Table 5).

Discussion

The prevalence of prolonged hospital stay after surgery in this study was 29.2% (95% CI: 25.2–33.1%), which is comparable to reports from Oman (30.5%) [10], South Africa (28.1%) [19], USA (27.5%) [2], Amhara region state, Ethiopia (26.5%) [8], Jimma University Medical Center, Ethiopia(25.3%) [9]. However, this prevalence is higher than reports from Poland and Germany (4.9%) [16], Mexico(5.1%) [5], Chicago(18.4%) [31], and Michigan University, America (22.4%) [32]. The observed differences may be attributed to better healthcare systems, advanced infrastructure, and specialized personnel in developed countries, whereas low- and middle-income settings face resource constraints, limited diagnostic capacity, and staff shortages [33]. Differences in sample size and definitions of prolonged stay may also contribute; for example, the Mexico study used the 95th percentile cutoff, whereas the present study applied the 75th percentile [5]. Conversely, the prevalence in this study was lower than in Canada [34], Nigeria (63%) [6], and the Addis Ababa cardiac center, Ethiopia (50.8%) [35]. These discrepancies may relate to differences in study populations, surgical types, and cutoff criteria. The Canadian study focused on lower limb amputations, while the Nigerian and Addis Ababa studies involved more complex or high-risk procedures [6, 35].

Several factors were significantly associated with prolonged postoperative stay. Preoperative anemia increased the likelihood of extended hospitalization, consistent with studies from Singapore, Germany, and Ethiopia [8, 36, 37]. Anemia can impair oxygen delivery, delay wound healing, and increase transfusion requirements [38]. Early detection and correction preoperatively or via postoperative parenteral iron in emergencies may improve outcomes [39]. Delayed ambulation was also associated with prolonged hospital stay, aligning with findings from Chicago and Japan [40, 41]. Immobility increases the risk of pneumonia, deep vein thrombosis, and delayed wound healing [42]. This emphasizes the importance of implementing Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols that promote early mobilization to minimize complications and reduce hospital stay duration. Similarly, hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP) was linked to longer postoperative stays, in line with studies from Ethiopia and Portugal [8, 43]. This may result from prolonged antibiotic therapy and delayed wound healing as the immune system combats infection [44]. Preventive measures such as timely antibiotic prophylaxis and ensuring adequate ventilation in surgical wards are crucial.

Surgical site infection (SSI) was another significant factor, consistent with findings from Germany and Portugal [43, 45]. Surgical site infection delays wound healing and may necessitate additional procedures, thereby extending hospitalization [46].

These findings underscore the need for proactive perioperative management, particularly for high-risk procedures such as laparotomy and appendectomy. In resource-limited settings, hospital-level interventions, including preoperative screening for anemia and comorbidities, implementation of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols, early mobilization, and infection-prevention bundles can reduce prolonged stays, improve patient outcomes, and optimize healthcare resource utilization. At the national level, integrating these strategies into surgical quality improvement initiatives can enhance perioperative care and promote standardization across hospitals.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is its detailed analysis of postoperative hospital stay by specific surgical procedures, providing nuanced, procedure-specific insights. The large sample size enhanced the statistical power and reliability of the findings, while the use of Kobo Collect software ensured accurate, real-time data collection and minimized entry errors. Nonetheless, several limitations should be considered. The exclusion of orthopedic surgeries may limit external validity, potentially underestimate prolonged postoperative stay, and restrict the applicability of identified associated factors to other surgical fields. The single-center design may reduce generalizability to other hospitals with different patient populations or healthcare systems. The cross-sectional nature of the study prevents causal inference, and residual confounding from unmeasured variables cannot be ruled out. The use of self-reported data introduces the possibility of recall bias. Additionally, early discharge pressures in overcrowded wards could have led to underestimation of prolonged stays, and the lack of post-discharge follow-up means that readmissions or complications after discharge were not captured, potentially underrepresenting the total recovery burden. Future studies including multiple surgical specialties, follow-up data, and diverse hospital settings are warranted to enhance generalizability and fully assess postoperative recovery.

Conclusion

The study revealed a high prevalence of prolonged postoperative hospital stays. Key contributing factors included preoperative anemia, delayed ambulation, surgical site infections, and hospital-acquired pneumonia. To address these issues, practical measures tailored to the Ethiopian context are essential. Clinical practices should emphasize routine screening and management of anemia, early postoperative mobilization, and implementation of infection-prevention bundles. Policymakers should support the integration of context-adapted Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols across hospitals to standardize care and reduce unnecessary stays. Future research should explore the cost-effectiveness and scalability of these interventions within resource-limited settings.
