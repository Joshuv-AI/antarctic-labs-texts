No-slip, slip and friction at fluid-solid interfaces: Concept of adsorption layer
Haodong Zhang 1,2, Fei Wang 1,2, Britta Nestler 1,2,3

Abstract
When a fluid contacts solid surfaces, it can spread or slide, and the motion of the contact line involves a complex interplay between hydrodynamic and thermodynamic effects. Hydrodynamic theories, like the Huh & \& Scriven and Cox & \& Voinov models, assume a no-slip boundary condition, neglecting the macroscopic fluid slip at the fluid-solid interface. However, they cannot explain the contact line motion during droplet spreading which in thermodynamic theories is attribute to the microscopic surface diffusion of fluid molecules. To bridge these perspectives, we heed the physical origin of slip phenomenon by employing energy minimization principles to establish a force balance between solid-fluid friction, thermodynamic force, and viscous stress at the fluid-solid contact region. Our analysis reveals that slip is an intrinsic property, just like molecular surface diffusion, is also governed by fluid-solid intermolecular interactions. Based on our model with slip, we extend the classical Huh & \& Scriven and Cox & \& Voinov theories by incorporating friction, enabling a more comprehensive understanding of droplet slide and kinetic induced contact angle hysteresis (CAH). Our results demonstrate that solid-fluid friction plays a vital role in momentum transfer between the substrate and the droplet, therefore, modifies the droplet internal fluid flow during wetting drastically. Under strong friction, classical wetting predictions with CAH are recovered, whereas weak friction suppresses internal fluid motion, leading to the disappearance of CAH. These findings provide new insights into wetting dynamics, highlighting the importance of friction in slip behavior. This work has broad implications for droplet transport, surface engineering, and applications in microfluidics and coatings.

1
Introduction

When fluids are in contact with solid substrates,
various boundary layers emerge at the fluid-solid interface.
In multicomponent fluids, intermolecular interactions with the substrate
can lead to depletion or adsorption layers on the microscopic scale,
(
De Gennes 1981
;
Wang et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2024c
)

which influence macroscopic wetting and dewetting behaviors.
Furthermore, when the fluid flows,
its relative motion is damped by the solid wall,
causing a velocity gradient near the fluid-solid interface
(
Xia 2024
;
Hadjiconstantinou 2024
)
.
This velocity boundary layer is generally categorized into two main types:

•

No-Slip Boundary Condition

(
Lauga et al. 2005
;
Luchini 2013
;
Wen et al. 2022
)
.
From a macroscopic perspective,
numerous experiments support the no-slip boundary condition,

\displaystyle\textbf{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}=\textbf{0}.

(1)

which has been regarded as a fundamental principle in fluid mechanics.
With the advent of computational fluid dynamics, the noNaStlip condition became the standard assumption due to its predictive precision
(
Qian et al. 2008
;
Yue & Feng 2012
;
Krüger et al. 2017
;
Lim et al. 2019
;
Yue 2020
;
Ding & Xia 2023
)
.
However, this assumption leads to a paradox in wetting dynamics: If the velocity at the droplet-substrate interface were strictly zero, the
fluid molecules would remain stationary,
contradicting experimental observations of the droplet spreading and retraction.

Qian et al. 2006
;
Yue et al. 2010
addressed this issue through numerical simulations,
showing that droplet motion arises from molecular diffusion
at the solid-fluid interface rather than classical advection.
This provides a solid explanation for the velocity boundary layer.

•

Slip boundary condition

(
Lauga & Stone 2003
;
Jung et al. 2016
;
Kusumaatmaja et al. 2016
;
Bae et al. 2019
;
Yariv 2023
)
.
Recent experiments at sub-micrometer and nanometer scales
have revealed that slip velocity at the fluid-solid interface does exist
(
Neto et al. 2005
;
Myers 2011
;
Maali & Bhushan 2012
;
Xie et al. 2018
)
.
Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations also confirm that
fluid molecules can slip
(
Qian et al. 2003
;
Qian et al. 2006
;
Voronov et al. 2006
;
Kumar Kannam et al. 2012
;
Ramos-Alvarado et al. 2016
;
Alipour et al. 2019
)
.
These findings challenge the universality of the no-slip condition.
In fact, the concept of slip is not new;
it dates back to the 19th century,
when Navier and Maxwell introduced the slip length
\text{l}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}

to characterize the slip velocity
\textbf{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}

(
Navier 1822
;
Maxwell 1879
;
Coron 1996
)
as

\displaystyle\textbf{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}=\text{l}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}\,\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{n}}\textbf{u}.

(2)

The value of
\text{l}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}
ranges from nanometers to micrometers
in experiments
(
Joseph & Tabeling 2005
;
Muralidhar et al. 2011
)
,
depending on fluid-substrate properties.
Additionally,
\text{l}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{s}}
is highly sensitive to flow geometry;
for instance, slip in carbon nanotubes
(
Secchi et al. 2016
)
is orders of magnitude larger than that observed in macroscopic systems.
However, few fundamental questions remain:
What governs the transition between slip and no-slip conditions?
Why do some fluids slip while others adhere?
Why do nanometer-scale droplets exhibit significant slip while macroscopic drops do not?

A key mechanism for suppressing slip is the solid-fluid friction
(
Petravic & Harrowell 2007
;
Cross et al. 2018
;
Li et al. 2023
)
.
In everyday life, we have experienced that friction allows vehicles to stop when braking.
Similarly, at the fluid-solid interface,
velocity damping occurs due to friction,
which is quantified by the friction coefficient
\lambda
.
In principle, if
\lambda
goes to infinity,
the no-slip condition (
\text{l}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{s}}=0
) in enforced,
while
\lambda=0
corresponds to perfect slip (
\text{l}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{s}}\rightarrow\infty
).
However, the physical magnitude of
\lambda
remains uncertain.
To address this,
Bocquet & Barrat 1994
linked friction
to velocity dissipation in the boundary layer using
linear response theory
.
They related
\lambda
to the dynamic autocorrelation function,
drawing an analogy to transport coefficients via the Green-Kubo relation.
In their framework, friction emerges as a dissipation mechanism for
kinetic energy perturbations
,
consistent with the fluctuation-dissipation theorem.
But, a crucial question is unresolved:
For a partially wetted multicomponent droplet,
where chemical free energy fluctuates and surface tension influences the macroscopic flow motion,
how does friction respond linearly to these complex factors?

In this study, we explore the interplay between solid-fluid friction, surface tension, and viscous stress
from an energy minimization perspective.
We focus on multicomponent fluid systems,
integrating thermodynamic insights
(
Yue et al. 2010
;
Xu et al. 2018
)

with hydrodynamic theories
(
Ren et al. 2015
;
Hadjiconstantinou 2021
)

to develop a more comprehensive understanding of the slip phenomenon.
Furthermore, we examine the molecular origins of friction by analyzing fluid-solid interactions.
Based on this, we formulate a generalized energy dissipation law at the fluid-solid interface,
which plays a pivotal role in determining slip behavior of several conventional flow scenarios,
including Poiseuille flow, Couette flow, wedge flow, droplet slide, and so on.

\text{r}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{c}}

This paper is structured as follows:
Section 2 presents the total energy functional formulation,
including contributions from chemical free energy, potential energy, and kinetic energy.
Section 3 derives evolution equations governing wetting dynamics based on energy minimization principles.
Section 4 discusses the fundamental mechanisms underlying friction at the fluid-solid interface,
including a Green-Kubo relation analysis and a molecular-scale perspective.
Section 5 examines the steady-state behaviors of wetting,
highlighting equilibrium conditions and flow structures near the contact line.
Finally, Section 6 presents case studies on single-phase flow, wedge flow dynamics, and droplet sliding.

2
Total energy functional

Considering the multi-component system with droplets contacting solid substrate,
the
fluid
has the total energy density functional
\mathcal{L}

consisted of three parts, namely,
the chemical free energy functional
\mathcal{F}
, the potential energy
\mathcal{P}
,
and the kinetic energy
\mathcal{K}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{F}+\mathcal{P}+\mathcal{K}.

Additionally, the kinetic energy of the
solid substrate

\mathcal{K}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{w}}

also needs to be considered.
Thus, the total energy density functional of the fluid and substrate system reads,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}=\mathcal{L}+\mathcal{K}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{w}}.

2.1
Chemical free energy functional

The fluid chemical free energy functional
\mathcal{F}

is formulated as the summation of
the bulk free energy functional
in the bulk fluid domain
\Omega
,
and the wall energy functional at the fluid-solid contact surface
L
as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle=\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\Omega}g\,\mathrm{d}\Omega+\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{L}}g_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{w}\,}\delta l\,\mathrm{d}\text{L}.

(3)

Especially, in this work, we specify the thin layer at the fluid-solid contact region
with the thickness
\delta l

and denominate it as the Langmuir adsorption layer (LAL).
This thin layer denotes the region where solid and fluid interactions exist,
resulting in the adsorption and depletion of the fluid molecules.
For different fluid-solid pairs, the magnitude of
\delta l
ranges from several nanometer to micro-meter scales
(
Butt et al. 2018
;
Wang & Nestler 2024b
)
,
which not only depends on the properties of the fluid solid interactions,
but also relies on the size of the molecules.

2.1.1
Chemical free energy in bulk fluids

In Eq. (
3
),
the bulk free energy density functional takes the Cahn-Hilliard formulation
(
Cahn & Hilliard 1958
;
Christianto et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2022
)

\displaystyle g=f(\boldsymbol{c})+{\textstyle\sum}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}\,}(\kappa/2)\big(\nabla c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\big)^{\scriptscriptstyle 2},

in which the spatial-temporal fluid composition is defined
by
\boldsymbol{c}(\mathbf{x},\text{t})=(c_{\scriptscriptstyle 1},c_{\scriptscriptstyle 2},\cdots,c_{\scriptscriptstyle i},\cdots)
.
The bulk free energy density

f
is assigned with the regular solution model, yielding

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{c})=(k_{\scriptscriptstyle B}T/v_{\scriptscriptstyle m}){\textstyle\sum}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\big(c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\ln c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}+{\textstyle\sum}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{j}\,}\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{ij}}\,c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{j}\,}\big).

The parameters
k_{\scriptscriptstyle B},\ T
, and
v_{\scriptscriptstyle m}

represent the Boltzmann constant, temperature,
and lattice volume, respectively.
The molecular interaction between fluid molecules
is quantified by
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{ij}}
,
as
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{ij}}>0(<0)
denotes the repulsion (attraction).
The parameter
\kappa
scales the fluid-fluid interfacial tension.

2.1.2
Chemical free energy in LAL

Figure 2:
(I) 2D illustration of the multicomponent fluid contacting the gray solid substrate.
The mid blue Langmuir adsorption layer (LAL)
with thickness
\delta l
forms
between the gray substrate and the light blue bulk fluid.
(II) The LAL composition
\bar{c}
deviates from the bulk equilibrium composition
c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{eq}}

due to the solid-fluid interactions.

In Eq. (
3
),
the second integrand scales the chemical interaction
between the fluid and the solid substrate,
and is related with the combination of
entropy density
s_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{w}}
,
enthalpy density
h_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{w}}

and van der Waals interaction
f_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{vdW}}

(
Wang et al. 2024
;
De Gennes 1985
)
as,

\displaystyle f_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{w}}=h_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{w}}(\boldsymbol{c})-\,Ts_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{w}}(\boldsymbol{c})+f_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{vdW}}.

Noteworthily,
f_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{vdW}}
between solid and fluid molecules
alters the free energy of fluid molecules
and can be calculated with the Lennard-Jones type of potential energy density as

\displaystyle f_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{vdW}}={\textstyle\sum}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\epsilon\,c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}\,}\big(\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}/\ \text{r}^{\,\scriptscriptstyle m}-\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}/\text{r}^{\,\scriptscriptstyle n}\big),

where
\epsilon
is the depth of the potential well,
and
\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}
is the distance at which the potential energy is zero.
The scaling parameters
m
and
n
are obtained from experiments.
Apparently, we can assume that the total van der Waals force is composition dependent.
In this way,
we use the averaged composition

\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}
of LAL
to express the wall energy density as,

\displaystyle f_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{w}}=(k_{\scriptscriptstyle B}T/v_{\scriptscriptstyle m}){\textstyle\sum}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\big(\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\ln\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}+\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\text{w}}\,\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}+{\textstyle\sum}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{j}\,}\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{ij}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\text{w}}\,\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{j}}\big).

(4)

The fluid molecules in LAL are constraint by
the wall energy density function
f_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{w}}

which differs from the bulk free energy density
f
.
Consequently, when the fluid contacts the solid substrate,

f_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{w}}
and
f

should be balanced in both LAL and bulk fluid.
Resulting from this,
the adsorption/depletion
scenarios appear
with the composition decay/increase from LAL to the bulk region,
as shown in figure
2
(II).
This surface composition variance also lead to the excess energy
which is obtained by the method developed in our previous work
Cai et al. 2024
.
By defining the thickness of LAL
\delta l
as the characteristic length scale
of solid-fluid intermolecular forces,
the total chemical free energy functional Eq. (
3
)
is formulated with

\displaystyle\!\!\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle=\!\!\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\Omega}\Big[f(\boldsymbol{c})+{\textstyle\sum}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}(\kappa/2)\big(\nabla c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\big)^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\Big]\,\mathrm{d}\Omega+\!\!\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{L}}\Big[f_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{w}}(\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}})+{\textstyle\sum}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}(\kappa^{\scriptscriptstyle\,\prime\!}/2)\big(\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle\!\text{s}\,}\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\big)^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\Big]\delta l\,\mathrm{d}\text{L},

(5)

where the surface gradient operator is defined as
\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle\!\text{s}}:=\nabla-\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{n}\cdot\nabla)

with respect to the normal vector of the solid substrate,
\mathbf{n}
.
This chemical free energy functional
is consistent with the dynamical boundary condition models by
Liu et al. 2024
.
The physical meaning of
\kappa^{\scriptscriptstyle\,\prime}
will be elucidated in Sec.
5.1
.

2.2
Potential energy and kinetic energy

For the multi-component fluid flow,
the potential energy of the fluid is represented by

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}=\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\Omega}p\,\mathrm{d}\Omega+\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{L}}\bar{p}\,\delta l\,\mathrm{d}\text{L}.

(6)

Here, the pressure density in bulk and LAL
are labeled by
p
and
\bar{p}
, respectively.
The fluid kinetic energy takes the following formulation
with the macroscopic fluid velocity
\mathbf{u}
inside the bulk region,
and
\bar{\mathbf{u}}
in LAL,

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}=\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\Omega}\frac{\rho\,\mathbf{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle\,2}}{2}\,\mathrm{d}\Omega+\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{L}}\frac{\rho\,\bar{\mathbf{u}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\,2}}{2}\delta l\,\mathrm{d}\text{L},

(7)

where the density
\rho
is assumed to be constant
and the fluid velocity
\bar{\mathbf{u}}
in LAL assigns the average value for
the fluid molecules.

3
Evolution equations by energy minimization

In this section,
we derive the proper evolution equations for the system,
based on the energy minimization method discussed in our previous works
(
Zhang et al. 2024a
;
Wang & Nestler 2024a
)
.
For the sake of brevity,
only the crucial deductions are presented.

3.1
Chemical free energy functional dissipation

Starting from the temporal decay of the chemical free energy functional
\mathcal{F}
,

\displaystyle\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}/\mathrm{d}\text{t}=

\displaystyle\!\!\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\Omega}{\textstyle\sum}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\Big[\Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}}-\kappa\nabla^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\Big)\frac{\mathrm{d}c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}}{\mathrm{d}\text{t}}+\nabla\!\cdot\!\big(\kappa\nabla c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\otimes\nabla c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\big)\cdot\!\mathbf{u}\Big]\mathrm{d}\Omega-\!\!\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\partial\Omega}\!\!{\textstyle\sum}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\big(\kappa\nabla\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\cdot\mathbf{n}\big)\frac{\mathrm{d}\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}}{\mathrm{d}\text{t}\,}\,\mathrm{d}\text{L}

(8)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\!\!\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{L}}{\textstyle\sum}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\Big[\Big(\frac{\partial f_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{w}}}{\partial\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}}-\kappa^{\scriptscriptstyle\prime}\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle\!\text{s}\,}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\Big)\frac{\mathrm{d}\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}}{\mathrm{d}\text{t}\,}+\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle\!\text{s}}\cdot\big(\kappa^{\scriptscriptstyle\prime}\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle\!\text{s}\,}\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\otimes\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle\!\text{s}\,}\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\big)\cdot\bar{\mathbf{u}}\Big]\delta l_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\mathrm{d}\text{L}.

(9)

The second integral in Eq. (
8
) is obtained from the divergence theorem and
the well-known equality of vector calculus,
\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\otimes\nabla c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}})=\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}})\nabla c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}+\kappa\nabla c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\cdot\nabla\nabla c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}
.
Physically, Eqs. (
8
) and (
9
)
describe the chemical free energy dissipation via bulk diffusion
and surface diffusion in LAL, respectively.

3.2
Potential energy dissipation

The potential energy dissipation follows the simple chain rule,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathcal{P}/\mathrm{d}\text{t}=\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\Omega}\frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}{\mathrm{dt}}\,\mathrm{d}\Omega+\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{L}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\bar{p}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}{\mathrm{dt}}\,\delta l\,\mathrm{d}\text{L}=\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\Omega}\nabla p\cdot\mathbf{u}\,\mathrm{d}\Omega+\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{L}}\nabla\bar{p}\cdot\bar{\mathbf{u}}\,\delta l\,\mathrm{d}\text{L}.

(10)

3.3
Kinetic energy dissipation

The kinetic energy dissipation is calculated with the total derivatives as,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathcal{K}/\mathrm{d}\text{t}=

\displaystyle\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\Omega}\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}/\mathrm{d}\text{t}\,\mathrm{d}\Omega+\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{L}}\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\bar{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\mathrm{d}\bar{\mathbf{u}}/\mathrm{d}\text{t}\,\delta l\,\mathrm{d}\text{L}.

(11)

In bulk fluid
\Omega
, we apply the vector calculus
and express the force balance,

\displaystyle\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}/\mathrm{d}\text{t}=\mathbf{f}+\nabla\big(\,\zeta\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}\,\big)+\nabla\cdot\underline{\underline{\text{T}}},

(12)

The force
\mathbf{f}
exerted on the fluid takes the tensor formation

\mathbf{f}=-\nabla\cdot\big(\text{P}\,\mathbf{I}+\underline{\underline{\Theta}})
,
which contains the grand potential
P

and the surface stress tensor
\underline{\underline{\Theta}}
reading

\displaystyle\text{P}=p-g+{\textstyle\sum}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}(\delta g/\delta c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}})_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}},\quad\underline{\underline{\Theta}}=-\big[g-{\textstyle\sum}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}(\delta g/\delta c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}})c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\big]_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\mathbf{I}+{\textstyle\sum}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}\,}\kappa\,\nabla c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\otimes\nabla c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}.

The second term on the right hand side (
rhs
) of Eq. (
12
)
describes the compressibility of the fluid
scaled by the volume viscosity
\zeta
.
In this work, the fluid is incompressible,
so that
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0

becomes an individual equation.
The last term in Eq. (
12
)
denotes the diffusion of fluid velocity
which is actually the viscous dissipation with the viscosity
\eta
.
Due to the angular momentum conservation,
the fluid viscous stress adopts the symmetric tensor as,

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{\text{T}}}

\displaystyle=\eta\,\big(\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle\text{T}}\big).

After simplification,
the Navier-Stokes equations (NaSt) in the bulk fluid are derived,

\begin{gathered}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0,\\
\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}/\mathrm{d}\text{t}=-\nabla\cdot(\text{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\textbf{I}+\underline{\underline{\Theta}})+\nabla\cdot\underline{\underline{\text{T}}}.\end{gathered}

(13)

Substituting NaSt into Eq. (
11
),
the kinetic energy decay is simplified as

\displaystyle\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{K}/\mathrm{d}\text{t}=-

\displaystyle\!\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\Omega}\!\Big[\nabla\cdot\big(\text{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\mathbf{I}+\underline{\underline{\Theta}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\big)\cdot\mathbf{u}+\underline{\underline{\text{T}}}:\nabla\mathbf{u}\Big]\mathrm{d}\Omega-\!\!\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\partial\Omega}\underline{\underline{\text{T}}}\cdot\mathbf{n}\cdot\bar{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathrm{d}\text{L}-\!\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{L}}\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\mathrm{d}\bar{\mathbf{u}}/\mathrm{d}\text{t}\cdot\bar{\mathbf{u}}\,\delta l\,\mathrm{d}\text{L}.

(14)

Here, the second integral in Eq. (
14
) hinges
the viscous stress between bulk and LAL at
\partial\Omega\equiv\text{L}
,
and is obtained by the divergence theorem
with
(\nabla\cdot\underline{\underline{\text{T}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,})\cdot\mathbf{u}=\nabla\cdot(\,\underline{\underline{\text{T}}}\;\mathbf{u}\,)-\underline{\underline{\text{T}}}:\nabla\mathbf{u}
.

3.4
Governing equations in bulk fluids

At this step, we easily obtain the evolution equations by the energy dissipation in bulk fluid.
Taking the Cahn-Hilliard type of material conservation equation with NaSt Eqs. (
13
),
the Cahn-Hilliard-Navier-Stokes model (CHNS), or the so-called model H,
is derived,

\begin{gathered}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0,\\
\partial c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}/\partial\text{t}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}=\nabla\cdot\big(\mathcal{M}\nabla\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\big),\\
\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}/\mathrm{d}\text{t}=-\nabla p-{\textstyle\sum}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\nabla\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}+\nabla\cdot\big[\eta\,\big(\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle\text{T}}\big)\big],\end{gathered}

(15)

in which the bulk chemical potential
is defined, with
\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}=\partial f/\partial c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}-\kappa\nabla^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}
.
The mobility
\mathcal{M}
scales the bulk diffusion
which follows the Onsager’s relation; more details in our previous work
Zhang et al. 2021
.

3.5
Governing equations in LAL

As the chemical free energy fluctuation in LAL can be dissipated via the surface diffusion
(
Yue et al. 2010
)
;
see Eq. (
9
),
we may question:
(I) Why the diffusion process cannot lead to fluid flow in LAL?
(II) If the unmovable molecules in LAL obey the mathematical no-slip boundary condition,
what is the physical origin of it?
(III) Is there a possible kinetic energy dissipation inside LAL
which may violate the no-slip boundary condition?

To answer these crucial questions,
we “forget” the no-slip boundary condition,
and focus on deducing the feasible force balance equation in LAL.
Still, the skeleton key is the total energy minimization.
Adding Eqs. (
9
), (
10
) (
14
),
the total energy functional
\mathcal{L}
decay as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}}{\mathrm{d}\text{t}}=&\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\Omega}\Big[{\textstyle\sum}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}}-\kappa\nabla^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\Big)\frac{\mathrm{d}c_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}}{\mathrm{d}\text{t}}-\underline{\underline{\text{T}}}:\!\nabla\mathbf{u}\Big]\,\mathrm{d}\Omega\ -\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\partial\Omega}\Big[{\textstyle\sum}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\big(\kappa\nabla\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\cdot\mathbf{n}\big)\frac{\mathrm{d}\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}}{\mathrm{d}\text{t}\,}+\underline{\underline{\text{T}}}\cdot\mathbf{n}\cdot\bar{\mathbf{u}}\Big]\,\mathrm{d}\text{L}\\
+&\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{L}}\Big[{\textstyle\sum}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\Big(\frac{\partial f_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{w}}}{\partial\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}}-\kappa^{\scriptscriptstyle\prime}\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle\!\text{s}\,}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\Big)\frac{\mathrm{d}\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}}{\mathrm{d}\text{t}\,}+{\textstyle\sum}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle\!\text{s}}\!\cdot\!\big(\kappa^{\scriptscriptstyle\prime}\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle\!\text{s}\,}\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\otimes\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle\!\text{s}\,}\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\big)\!\cdot\!\bar{\mathbf{u}}+\Big(\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle\!\text{s}\,}\bar{p}+\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\frac{\mathrm{d}\bar{\mathbf{u}}}{\mathrm{d}\text{t}}\Big)\cdot\bar{\mathbf{u}}\Big]\delta l_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\mathrm{d}\text{L}.\end{aligned}

(16)

Substituting CHNS into Eq. (
16
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}}{\mathrm{dt}}=

\displaystyle-\!\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\Omega}\big[{\textstyle\sum}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\mathcal{M}(\nabla\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}})^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}+\eta\nabla\mathbf{u}:\!\nabla\mathbf{u}\big]\,\mathrm{d}\Omega+\!\!\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{L}}\Big[{\textstyle\sum}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\Big(\frac{\partial f_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{w}}}{\partial\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}}-\kappa\nabla\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\cdot\mathbf{n}/\delta l-\kappa^{\scriptscriptstyle\prime}\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle\!\text{s}\,}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\Big)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}}{\mathrm{dt}}\,\Big]\delta l_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\mathrm{d}\text{L}

4
Physical origin of solid-fluid friction

Before heeding the influence of friction on fluid flow,
we need to have a clear definition for the solid-fluid friction
which is vaguely defined in Sec.
3.5.2
.
Every force appearing in the force balance equation of LAL is derived from
the minimization of the corresponding energy,
except for the friction force
\mathbf{F}
.
Just like other previous studies, we simply introduce this force and accept it without further justification.
Given that the fluctuation-dissipation theorem is a universal principle
for materials across scales from galaxies to quantum systems,
how can friction forces lack a corresponding energy?
What is the energy term associated with friction?
What microscopic factors influence the magnitude of friction?

4.1
Green-Kubo relation

A common method to extract the friction magnitude
is through molecular dynamics (MD) simulations,
based on the theory introduced by
Bocquet & Barrat 1994
.
Here, the linear response is assumed to hold under weak fluid flow conditions.
As derived from the energy law in Sec.
3.5.2
,
the friction force
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x},\text{t})
alters the fluid momentum,
leading to dissipation that follows the Green-Kubo relation at equilibrium,

\displaystyle\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{G-K}}=1/(Ak_{\scriptscriptstyle\!B}T)\!\!\int_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}\!\!\!\mathrm{dt}\,\langle\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x},\text{t}),\,\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x},0)\rangle,

(22)

with the friction area denoted by
A
.
If the bulk fluid flow is negligible,
the friction coefficient
\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{G-K}}
tells
an intrinsic property of the solid-fluid interface.
However, as noted by
Bui & Cox 2024
,
the energy dissipation of the bulk fluid due to viscous stress
also contributes to the calculation of Eq. (
22
) in MD simulations,
corresponding to the hydrodynamic coupling of bulk and LAL
by the second integral in Eq. (
14
).
They argue that the Green-Kubo relation
only accounts for the combined effects of solid-liquid intermolecular friction
and fluid viscous effect.

To eliminate the influence of bulk fluids, they introduced the so-called
effective friction coefficient
\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{eff}}
,
which cancels the influence of the bulk liquid flow profile.
Consequently,

\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{eff}}
incorporates
a geometry correction related to the simulation setup.
For example,
for Poiseuille flow in a cylindrical pipe,
\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{eff}}

decays with the pipe radius.
In 2D Poiseuille flow between parallel planes,

\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{eff}}
is inversely related to the plane spacing.
Hence, their studies affirm that friction at the solid-fluid interface
is not purely an intrinsic property of the substrate,
but also depends on the flow setup geometry.

To explain the concept of the effective friction coefficient,
we formulate the Langevin equation for the force balance in LAL
inside one dimensional Poiseuille flow (
\bar{\text{u}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}\!}=\bar{\text{u}}
),

\displaystyle\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\mathrm{d\bar{\text{u}}}/\mathrm{dt}=-\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{x}\,}\bar{p}+\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{n}\,}\bar{\text{u}}/\delta l-\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\bar{\text{u}}+\tilde{\xi}.

The pressure gradient
\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{x}}\bar{p}

and thermal noise
\tilde{\xi}
propels the flow,
while other two dissipative terms counteract the fluid momentum:
the friction
-\lambda\,\bar{\text{u}}

arises from the intrinsic property of the solid-liquid interactions;
the viscous stress
\eta\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{n}}\bar{\text{u}}

describes the drag force exerted by the bulk fluid on the material in LAL,
which is only the intrinsic property of fluid itself.
The Green-Kubo relation therefore describes the equilibrium
of the Langevin equation
where the fluctuation-dissipation in LAL is coupled with the bulk fluid above it.
Therefore, when the flow setup geometry changes,
the velocity profile of the bulk fluid varies accordingly.
As a result, the viscous stress
\eta\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{n}}\bar{\text{u}}

will be modified, altering the steady-state of
\bar{\text{u}}
in LAL.
Consequently, the friction coefficient
\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{G-K}}

obtained via the Green-Kubo relation
certainly absorbs the impact of geometry-dependent bulk fluid flow
and cannot represent the pure property of fluid-solid friction.

4.2
Molecule perspective

Figure 3:
Shear deformation pushes
fluid molecules (blue circles) away
from equilibrium positions (dashed circles)
along the tangential direction of the solid surface (gray).
The displacement
\Delta\textbf{r}=\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}\,}\Delta\text{t}

varies linearly with the slip velocity
\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}
.

Now, we provide our understanding of the physical origin of solid-fluid friction
from a molecular perspective
and attempt to derive an expression for the intrinsic friction coefficient.
Initially, we temporarily name the total solid-fluid interaction energy within the LAL as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}=\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{L}}f_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{w}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{c}},\mathbf{r})\delta l\,\mathrm{d}\text{L}.

Here,
\mathbf{r}(\text{t})
represents the spacing between the fluid and solid molecules;
see figure
3
.
Under small fluctuations, such as minor shear deformations,
the fluid molecules move away from their equilibrium position,
the energy density can be expanded as a Taylor series in terms of displacement
\Delta\mathbf{r}=\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}\,}\Delta\text{t}

(
Cai et al. 2024
)

as,

\displaystyle f_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{w}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{c}},\mathbf{r}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}\pm\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,})=f_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{w}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{c}},\mathbf{r}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0})+\frac{\partial^{\scriptscriptstyle 2\!}f_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{w}}/\partial\Delta\mathbf{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle\,2}}{2}(\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}\,}\Delta\text{t})^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}+\mathcal{O}(\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\,4}\Delta\text{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\,4}),

where
\Delta\text{t}
denotes the characteristic timescale of shear deformation.
Hence, the power of the friction is expressed

\mathbf{F}\cdot\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}=-\mathrm{d}f_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{w}}/\mathrm{dt}

which gives rise to the average friction force,

\mathbf{F}=-(\partial^{\scriptscriptstyle 2\!}f_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{w}}/\partial\Delta\mathbf{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle\,2})\,\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}\,}\Delta\text{t}=-\lambda\,\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}
,
so that the friction coefficient is written as

\displaystyle\lambda(\bar{\boldsymbol{c}})=(\partial^{\scriptscriptstyle 2\!}f_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{w}}/\partial\Delta\mathbf{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle\,2})\Delta\text{t}.

It is noteworthy that the displacement
\Delta\mathbf{r}
denotes the elongation of solid-fluid bond
which is depicted by the red spring-like structure in figure
3
,
representing the physical model of the fluid-solid bond
(
Shimizu et al. 1998
)
.
Thus, the scaling parameter
\partial^{\scriptscriptstyle 2\!}f_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{w}}/\partial\Delta\mathbf{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}

is determined by the magnitudes of the solid-fluid bonding energy.
Due to this reason, the average friction force
\mathbf{F}

takes a formulation analogous to Hooke’s law,
acting as the counter force to stop the relative motion of molecules
and bring them back to their equilibrium position.

Resulting from this, we suggest that the fluid-solid friction indicates
another dissipation mechanism for the wall energy fluctuations.
Conventional theories mainly focus on the dissipation by composition changes via
(I) depletion/adsorption and (II) surface diffusion.
Both account for the variance of
f_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{w}}

with respect to the composition
\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}
.
(III) Fluid flow induced by Korteweg stress and Maxwell stress
amid the fluid phase,
dissipates through fluid intermolecular friction (viscous stress)
(
Zhang et al. 2024b
)
.

In this work,
we highlight a fourth mechanism that is often overlooked.
(IV) Fluid flow induces relative motion between solid-fluid molecules at the interface,
causing variations in bond length and angle.
These molecular deformations alter the corresponding energy landscape,
generating a restorative force that pulls fluid molecules back toward their equilibrium positions,
thereby resisting their departure.
This resistance is the fundamental source of solid-fluid intermolecular friction.
Since the friction force is associated with bond rotation/elongation,
it can neither be neglected nor assigned arbitrary magnitudes.
Moreover, we emphasize that both the friction force
and the depletion/adsorption force originate from the same fundamental mechanism,
namely,
the minimization of wall energy at the atomic scale.
The key distinction between them lies in their relation to molecular motion:
friction force pertains to shear motion, governed by hydrodynamic interactions,
while depletion/adsorption force arises from diffusive motion, driven by thermodynamic effects.
This distinction may help explain why some frying pans are coated with PTFE (polytetrafluoroethylene).
The fluorocarbon compounds exhibit weak van der Waals interactions
due to the low electric polarizability of fluorine, resulting in two valuable properties:
hydrophobicity (low adsorption) and slippery (low friction).

5.1
Chemical equilibrium in LAL and Young’s law

First, we demonstrate the thermodynamic equilibrium

\displaystyle\bar{\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{i}}\,}\delta l

(23)

As illustrated by the sessile droplet in figure

Next, we focus on the steady state of the

The composition of fluid one is denoted as
\bar{c}

1-\bar{c}
.
Without loss of generality, we simplify the fluid flow to the 2-dimensional case
and express the force balance in the x-o-n coordinate system,
where x and n represent the tangential and normal directions of the substrate, respectively.
In x-direction, the force balance is given by:

\displaystyle-\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{x}}\bar{p}-\bar{c}\,\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{x}}\bar{\mu}+\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{x}}(\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{x}}\bar{\text{u}})+(\eta/\delta l)\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{n}}\bar{\text{u}}-\lambda\,\bar{\text{u}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}=0.

(26)

5.2.1
Generalized Navier boundary condition

If no pressure gradient is applied in the x-direction,
as in Couette flow and droplet wetting,
we have
\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{x}}\bar{p}=0
,
and we multiply both sides of Eq. (
26
) by
\delta l
,

\displaystyle(\lambda\,\delta l)_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\bar{\text{u}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}

\displaystyle=-c^{\scriptscriptstyle\text{s}}\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{x}\,}\bar{\mu}+\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{n}}\bar{\text{u}}+\delta l\,\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{x}}(\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{x}}\bar{\text{u}}),

(27)

where
c^{\scriptscriptstyle\text{s}}\!=\bar{c}\,\delta l

represents the fluid surface composition.
Eq. (
27
) bears a clear resemblance to
the generalized Navier boundary condition (GNBC)
presented in the classic paper by
Qian et al. 2006
, with

\displaystyle\beta_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{n}\,}\bar{\text{u}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}=-c^{\scriptscriptstyle\,s}\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{x}\,}\bar{\mu}+\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}(\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{n}}\bar{\text{u}}+\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{x}}\bar{\text{v}}).

(28)

The friction coefficient
\lambda=\beta_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{n}}/\delta l

is related to the slip coefficient
\beta_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{n}}
defined by Navier.
In addition to these similarities, our
Eq. (
27
) also introduces two distinctions:

•

The normal stress term
\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{x}}(\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{x}}\bar{\text{u}})

vanishes in GNBC.
For single-phase flow, this term is always zero.
However, in multiphase flow with an interface,
differences in velocity and viscosity between phases
contribute an additional viscous force term
that cannot be neglected.
The vanishing of this term is
due to the application of the divergence theorem
in the derivation of Eq. (
28
),
where the volumetric functional variation over the entire domain
is automatically transformed into surface normal flux.
Implicitly, the energy functional variation
in the boundary tangential direction is neglected.
Although this tangential functional variation
does not affect the total energy flux
through
the domain boundary,
it can alter the boundary energy functional density and,
consequently, influence the local momentum and composition distributions
at the boundaries.

•

Our equation does not contain
\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{x}}\bar{\text{v}}
, being consistent with Navier boundary condition.

5.2.2
Navier boundary condition

For the single phase flow at the steady state,
the unbalanced thermodynamic force related to
inhomogeneous composition
\bar{c}
is neglected,
Eq. (
27
) becomes

\displaystyle(\lambda\,\delta l)\bar{\text{u}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}

\displaystyle=\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{n}}\bar{\text{u}}+\delta l\,\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{x}}(\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{x}}\bar{\text{u}}).

Considering the flow where the velocity in the n-direction is absent,
i.e.,
\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{n}}\bar{v}=0
,
such as in Couette and Poiseuille flows,
the normal component of the viscous stress

\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{x}}\bar{\text{u}}=0
,
leads to the Navier boundary condition (NBC)
with the so-called Navier slip length is derived as

\displaystyle\text{l}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}=\frac{\eta}{\lambda\,\delta l\,}.

(29)

Moreover, when the friction force dominates
with the coefficient
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
,
the Navier slip length
\text{l}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}=0
recovers the no-slip boundary condition.

Noteworthily, we argue that the Navier slip length
\text{l}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}

is merely a fictitious parameter without the clear physical definition.
This concept is introduced because Navier-Stokes equation
is a second-order PDE
that requires a corresponding mathematical boundary condition for the solution.
To elucidate the physical interpretation of slip length
\text{l}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}
,
we may consider the energy dissipation of the single phase flow (
\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}/\mathrm{dt}=0
)
and easily find that

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{T}}/\mathrm{d}\text{t}=\!\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\partial\Omega}\!\!\big(-\underline{\underline{\bar{\text{T}}}}\!\cdot\bar{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\mathbf{n}+\textbf{F}\cdot\textbf{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{w}\,}\delta l\big)\,\mathrm{dL}-\!\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\Omega}\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\nabla\mathbf{u}:\!\nabla\mathbf{u}\,\mathrm{d}\Omega,

(30)

in which the first and second integrals
represent the viscous dissipation at the boundary
\partial\Omega
and in the bulk
\Omega
, respectively.
Usually, two mathematical approaches on the domain boundary
\partial\Omega

ensure the total energy decay over time, namely,

•

no-slip boundary condition
(BC) with

\bar{\mathbf{u}}=\mathbf{0}
and
\textbf{F}=\textbf{0}
gives rise to

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{T}}/\mathrm{d}\text{t}=-\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\Omega}\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\nabla\mathbf{u}:\!\nabla\mathbf{u}\,\mathrm{d}\Omega\leq 0,

indicating the energy can only be dissipated by the bulk fluid.
The solid-fluid boundary does not dissipate any energy.

•

Navier boundary condition
(NBC) with
\text{l}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}\,}\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{n}}\bar{\mathbf{u}}=\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}

and
\textbf{F}=-(\eta/\text{l}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}})_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}

result in

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{T}}/\mathrm{d}\text{t}=-\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\partial\Omega}(\eta/\text{l}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}})_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\,2}\,\mathrm{dL}-\int_{\scriptscriptstyle\Omega}\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\nabla\mathbf{u}:\!\nabla\mathbf{u}\,\mathrm{d}\Omega\leq 0.

In this case, the kinetic energy dissipation on the solid-fluid boundary
\partial\Omega
also plays a role.
With the increase in the slip length
\text{l}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}
, the dissipation on the boundary becomes weaker.
Still, for a given system, the value of
\text{l}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}
is unknown
and should be obtained by other indirect methods, such as MD simulation.

But the no-slip BC and NBC are not the only two conditions that guarantee the energy minimization.
Based on the above discussion,
we suggest that the no-slip BC, NBC,
and GNBC are merely potential strong constraints equivalent
to the steady states of our force balance equation Eq. (
21
)
in LAL with
\mathrm{d}\bar{\mathbf{u}}/\mathrm{dt}=0
.
In fact, neither the slip velocity
\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}
,
nor the slip length
\text{l}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}
,
should be assigned by the constant values before reaching the steady state.
In other words, the fluid flow in the LAL should develop itself.
Initially, the fluid material in LAL undergoes an acceleration process,
beginning to move along the substrate surface with
\mathrm{d}\bar{\mathbf{u}}/\mathrm{dt}>0
.
For instance, in Couette flow, the initial speed-up results from the solid-fluid friction force
\mathbf{F}
.
In Poiseuille flow, it originates from the pressure gradient
-\nabla p
.
And in droplet wetting, the impulse arises from the unbalanced thermodynamic force

-\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\nabla\bar{\mu}
.
The materials then gradually slow down,
as other forces come into play to dissipate the kinetic energy.
Eventually, the fluid molecules reach the force balance
and keep the constant slip velocity
\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}
at steady states.

Besides, during the development of the slip velocity,
the velocity gradient
\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{n}}\bar{\mathbf{u}}

in the LAL will also vary with time.
Therefore, we suggest that the pre-defined constant Navier slip length
\text{l}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}
in Eq. (
29
)
may be physically plausible.
Even for the MD simulation with Green-Kubo relation, only
\text{l}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}
at equilibrium is obtained.
If
\text{l}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{s}}
is merely a mathematical tool invented to solve the second-order PDE,
why couldn’t we put it aside and use other more generalized ways to define the slip phenomenon
in a more physical manner?
In the following section, we will show that the solid-fluid friction is of great importance
for the slip phenomenon.

6
Case study

In this section, we apply our slip model to several conventional flow configurations,
and select the thickness of the Langmuir adsorption layer (LAL) as the characteristic length scale,
setting
\delta l=1
.
To emphasize the roles of surface tension and viscous stress in micro-droplets,
we adopt water viscosity and water interfacial tension as characteristic values.
Under these conditions, the system’s Reynolds number remains
\text{Re}\ll 1
,
indicating that fluid inertia is negligible.
This enables us to further analyze the steady-state solutions of fluid motion with slip and friction,
and compare our results to classical wetting theories,
including Huh & Scriven and Cox & Voinov, for binary fluid systems.
Since our study mainly focuses on theoretical modeling and analytical solutions,
we do not incorporate numerical simulations in this work,
as they cannot be directly compared to these classical theoretical frameworks.

6.1.1
1D Poiseuille flow

We start with the simplest single phase scenario
and consider the one dimensional Poiseuille flow through
the pipe with radius R.
The velocity field is simplified in the cylindrical coordinates x-o-r,
as demonstrated in figure

The fluid velocity obeys the following force balances in the bulk fluid and LAL, as

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\nabla p=\eta\nabla^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\mathbf{u},&\text{in r}\in(-\text{R}+\delta l,\text{R}-\delta l),\\[3.00003pt]

The solution denotes the steady flow field related with the pipe radius as

\displaystyle\text{u}(\text{r})=\bigg[\dfrac{\text{r}^{2}-(\text{R}-\delta l)^{2}}{4_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\eta}-\dfrac{\text{R}+2\,\delta l}{2\,\lambda\,\delta l}\bigg]\dfrac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{dx}},

\displaystyle l_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{eff}}=\frac{\text{R}+2\,\delta l}{\text{R}-\delta l}\,\text{l}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{s}}.

For the large enough pipe with radius

approaches the Navier slip length
\text{l}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{s}}
of Eq. (
29

which shows no geometry dependence anymore.

6.1.2
2D Poiseuille flow

Similarly, for the two dimensional Poiseuille flow through
two parallel planes with spacing 2H;

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\nabla p=\eta\nabla^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\mathbf{u},&\text{in h}\in(-\text{H}+\delta l,\text{H}-\delta l),\\[5.0pt]
\lambda\,\bar{\text{u}}=\eta\partial\bar{\text{u}}/\partial\text{h}-\mathrm{d}p/\mathrm{dx},&\text{in h}=\pm(\text{H}-\delta l),\end{array}\right.

which denotes the steady flow field as

leading to the geometry dependent effective slip length on the plane spacing

H as

\displaystyle l_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{eff}}=\frac{\text{H}}{\text{H}-\delta l}\,\text{l}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{s}}.

(32)

2D Couette flow

All three cases above indicate that the slip length

and the solid-fluid molecular forces in LAL
but also by the flow setup.
These simple case studies also indicate
the plausible conclusion on the geometry dependent

\lambda
which is lacking concrete physical significance.

Model

We adopt the classical Huh

\phi\in[0,\theta)

\phi\in(\theta,\pi]

\phi=\theta

\alpha-\delta

The stream function
\psi

\displaystyle\nabla^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\nabla^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\psi=0.

(35)

Its general solution in the polar coordinate
(\text{r},\phi)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\!\alpha}=\text{r}(a\sin\phi\hskip 1.60004pt+b\cos\phi\hskip 1.00006pt+\hskip 1.00006ptc\phi\sin\phi\hskip 1.19995pt+d\phi\cos\phi\hskip 1.19995pt),&\text{in phase }\alpha,\\[3.99994pt]
\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\!\delta}=\text{r}(A\sin\phi+B\cos\phi+C\phi\sin\phi+D\phi\cos\phi),&\text{in phase }\delta.\end{array}\right.

(36)

The velocity components in each phase
i
(
i=\alpha,\delta

\displaystyle\text{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{r}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\,i}=-(1/\text{r})_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\partial\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\!i}/\partial\phi,\quad\text{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\phi}^{\scriptscriptstyle\,i}=\partial\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\!i}/\partial\text{r}.

The eight unknown parameter in Eqs. (
36
)
are calculated with the following boundary conditions.
At the boundary of bulk liquid and LAL,
no normal flow exists and the fluid in each phase only slips
along the substrate tangential direction with,

\displaystyle\text{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\phi}^{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}\big\rvert_{\scriptscriptstyle\phi=0}=0,\quad\text{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\phi}^{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}\big\rvert_{\scriptscriptstyle\phi=\pi}=0.

Meanwhile, at the fluid-fluid interface, the situation is similar.
Fluid cannot move across its surface
and the tangential velocity is identical in both phases, yielding

\displaystyle\text{u}_{\,\scriptscriptstyle\text{r}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}\big\rvert_{\scriptscriptstyle\phi=\theta}\!\!=\text{u}_{\,\scriptscriptstyle\text{r}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}\big\rvert_{\scriptscriptstyle\phi=\theta},\quad\text{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\phi}^{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}\big\rvert_{\scriptscriptstyle\phi=\theta}=0,\quad\text{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\phi}^{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}\big\rvert_{\scriptscriptstyle\phi=\theta}=0.

The continuity of the tangential viscous stress at the
\alpha-\delta
interface says

\displaystyle\eta^{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}\partial^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\!\alpha}/\partial\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}=\eta^{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}\partial^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\!\delta}/\partial\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}.

In contrast to the standard Huh
\&
Scriven theory,
we incorporate slip effects at the fluid-solid contact regions.
Since friction magnitudes differ between the two phases,
the corresponding slip velocities, U
{}_{\scriptscriptstyle\!\alpha\text{s}}
and U
{}_{\scriptscriptstyle\!\delta\text{s}}
,
follow distinct formulations,

\displaystyle\text{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{r}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}\big\rvert_{\scriptscriptstyle\phi=0}=\text{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle\!\alpha\text{s}},\quad\text{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{r}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}\big\rvert_{\scriptscriptstyle\phi=\pi}=\text{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle\!\delta\text{s}},

which could be obtained by the following force balance,

\displaystyle\lambda^{\scriptscriptstyle\!\alpha\,}(\text{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle\!\alpha\text{s}}-\text{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0})=\eta^{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}\partial\text{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{r}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}/\partial\phi,\quad\lambda^{\scriptscriptstyle\!\delta\,}(\text{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}-\text{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle\!\delta\text{s}})=\eta^{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}\partial\text{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,\text{r}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}/\partial\phi,

(37)

with
\text{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}
denotes the velocity of the substrate.

These results indicate that the no-slip boundary condition,
as assumed in Cox-Voinov theory,
is not an intrinsic property of the system
but is fundamentally linked to friction.
Friction plays a crucial role in transferring momentum
from substrate molecules to the droplet molecules through intermolecular forces,
thereby inducing internal fluid motion within the droplet.
This flow field generates viscous stress,
modifies the curvature of the droplet-air interface,
and ultimately leads to deviations from Young’s law.

While friction acts as the primary driver of momentum transfer,

In figure

For highly viscous droplets,

leading to rapid energy dissipation
that prevents effective momentum transfer to the bulk droplet.
Conversely, for less viscous droplets,
where friction becomes more dominant,
the Cox-Voinov behavior is recovered,
suggesting an effective equivalence to the no-slip boundary condition.
Thus, slip behavior is not an absolute property
but rather a consequence of the interplay between the friction at droplet-solid
and the viscous effect of droplet moleculars.
For instance, a highly viscous droplet does not necessarily exhibit the no-slip condition;
its interaction with the substrate also plays a determining role.

6.4
Droplet sliding with friction force

We now examine how friction forces affect droplet sliding.
As illustrated in figure
13
(I)(i),
when the substrate moves leftward, the receding contact angle
\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{R}}

forms at the left triple junction
\text{o}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{R}}
,
and the advancing contact angle
\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{A}}

appears at the right triple junction
\text{o}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{A}}
,
both deviate from the equilibrium Young’s angle
\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{Y}}
.

For small capillary number Ca, we adopt the stagnation assumption for the triple junctions,
following the study of
Qian et al. 2006
.
That is, although the triple junctions remain stationary,
an internal flow field develops inside the droplet,
as depicted in figure
13
(I)(ii).
According to Voinov’s theory,
this internal flow induces viscous stresses that must be balanced by surface tension,
leading to contact angle hysteresis.
However, in previous models,
the velocity at the droplet-substrate interface is assumed
to be equal to the substrate velocity
\text{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}
,
corresponding to the no-slip boundary condition.
In this work, we challenge this assumption by arguing that
the fluid flow at the droplet-substrate interface follows a more complex evolution,
governed by the interplay of friction, viscous stresses, and surface tension.
We investigate how this balance influences droplet motion
and how deviations from the no-slip condition impact the overall wetting dynamics.

which is depicted by the solid black lines in figure

However, when incorporating droplet-solid friction in the LAL,
we find that the friction parameter
\lambda
significantly influence CAH.
Notably, the dynamic contact angle converges to Voinov’s theoretical prediction
only under strong droplet-solid friction.
This suggests that friction plays a crucial role in
transferring momentum from the moving substrate to the droplet,
which drives the internal fluid motion.
At
\lambda=0
(i.e., in the absence of friction),
the momentum transfer is negligible,
resulting in an extremely weak internal flow.
Consequently, the viscous stress becomes minimal,
preventing significant curvature variation at the contact line.
As a result, CAH vanishes entirely.

With our theory, we write the force balance for the whole sliding droplet with the mass
\text{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle*}

by considering the friction force at the droplet-solid interface,

\displaystyle\textbf{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{cric}}=\text{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle*}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{U}}{\mathrm{dt}}=2\int_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pi}\!\!\!\int_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\text{r}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{c}}\!\cos\!\xi}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\big[\lambda(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0})-\eta\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{n}}\mathbf{u}-\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{xx}}\mathbf{u}-\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{x}}\bar{\mu}\big]\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\xi.

Letting
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{U}/\mathrm{dt}=0
, the critical friction force

\textbf{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{cric}}
is composed of three parts.
The first contributor is the kinetics induced friction
\textbf{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{kin}}
,

\displaystyle\textbf{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{kin}}

\displaystyle=2\int_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pi}\!\!\!\int_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\text{r}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{c}}\!\cos\!\xi}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\lambda(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0})\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\xi=-2\pi\text{r}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{c}\,}\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\mathbf{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}/\beta,

where the solid-fluid friction is scaled by the contact line length
2\pi\text{r}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{c}}

and propels the droplet motion with the substrate.
The second contributor is the viscous drag force from the bulk fluid above
\textbf{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{drag}}
,

\displaystyle\textbf{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{drag}}

\displaystyle=2\int_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pi}\!\!\!\int_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\text{r}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{c}}\!\cos\!\xi}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\big(-\eta\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{n}}\mathbf{u}-\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{xx}}\mathbf{u}\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\xi=2\pi\text{r}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{c}\,}\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\beta_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\mathbf{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0},

where the solid-fluid friction is scaled by the contact line length
2\pi\text{r}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{c}}

and propels the droplet motion with the substrate.
The last contributor is the capillary induced friction
\textbf{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{cap}}
,

\displaystyle\textbf{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{cap}}

\displaystyle=2\int_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pi}\!\!\!\int_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\text{r}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{c}}\!\cos\!\xi}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!-\bar{c}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{x}}\bar{\mu}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\xi=2\,\text{r}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{c}}\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{LV}}(\cos\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{R}}-\cos\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{A}}),

which is consistent with the Kawasaki-Furmidge equation
(
Kawasaki 1960
;
Furmidge 1962
;
Li et al. 2023
)

and resists the droplet slide.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we investigate the fundamental
physical mechanisms underlying no-slip,
as well as partial-slip boundary conditions
by examining the interplay between hydrodynamics and thermodynamics
in multi-component fluids in contact with solid substrates.
We introduce the Langmuir adsorption layer (LAL)
as a crucial interfacial region
where solid-fluid intermolecular interactions not only
drive depletion/adsorption phenomena, reducing chemical free energy,
but also induce fluid motion to minimize kinetic energy.

Using energy minimization principles,
we derive the force balance framework in LAL,
revealing the critical role of solid-fluid friction in governing slip behavior.
From a molecular perspective,
we propose that friction originates from
the elongation, shortening, and rotation of solid-fluid bonds,
which resist molecular slip.
Consequently, the magnitude of the friction force
F

is intrinsically linked to the solid-fluid bonding energy,
rather than the flow geometry.
LAL thus serves as a crucial transition zone between the solid substrate and bulk fluid,
where energy dissipation occurs through both hydrodynamic and thermodynamic processes.

Extending our framework to classical fluid flow problems,
including single-phase flow and Huh
\&
Scriven wedge flow,
we demonstrate that solid-fluid friction in LAL acts as
a key mechanism for momentum transfer which modifies bulk fluid flow,
making slip behavior an emergent property governed by the interplay
of interfacial tension, solid-fluid friction, and bulk viscosity.
These findings challenge classical mathematical no-slip condition
where steady-state velocity profile is typically predefined
but may not always be physically justified.

Furthermore, by incorporating the Cox-Voinov theory with slip for droplet sliding,
we reveal that solid-fluid friction strongly influences droplet internal flow
near the triple junction and directly contributes to contact angle hysteresis (CAH)
derivation from the standard Cox-Voinov theory:

\displaystyle\theta^{\scriptscriptstyle 3}=\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{app}}^{\scriptscriptstyle 3}+\frac{-2\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{LV}}(\cos\theta-\cos\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{app}})+(\lambda/\beta+\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\beta)\text{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}+\lambda\text{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0\,}\text{r}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{c}}}{4\eta/(\lambda\beta)-\lambda/\beta-\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\beta+\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\text{r}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{c}}}\frac{\ 9\eta\ }{\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{LV}}}\ln\Big(\frac{\ \text{h}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{m}}}{\ \delta l\ }\Big),

where the friction significantly impacts the internal droplet flow,
modifies the viscous stress across the droplet surface,
and deforms the droplet dynamic contact angle
\theta

deviating from the apparent Young’s angle
\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{app}}
.

Starting from the force balance equation in the adsorption layer,
the critical friction force for the droplet slide is deduced
as a combination of:

•

Capillary induced friction

\textbf{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{cap}}\!=\!2\,\text{r}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{c}}\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{LV}}(\cos\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{R}\!}-_{\scriptscriptstyle\!}\cos\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{A}})
related with surface tension
\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{LV}}
;

•

fluid viscous drag force

\textbf{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{drag}}=2\pi\text{r}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{c}\,}\eta\beta_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\mathbf{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}

depending on the fluid viscosity
\eta
;

•

Kinetics induced friction
\textbf{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{kin}}=-2\pi\text{r}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{c}\,}\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\,}\mathbf{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}/\beta
scaled by the contact line length
2\pi\text{r}_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{c}}
.

Future research could further expand our model by incorporating:
temperature gradients (Marangoni effects) to explore thermally induced slip,
electric fields (electrowetting) to study charge-driven wetting behavior,
and more complex multi-scale interactions
across solid, liquid, and gas phases.
Additionally, validations via experiments and molecular dynamics simulations
and advanced imaging techniques would provide deeper insights
into the microscopic origins of slip, friction,
and energy dissipation at fluid-solid interfaces.
By refining our understanding of wetting and interfacial slip,
this work lays the foundation for advancements in microfluidics, coatings,
and droplet-based technologies.
