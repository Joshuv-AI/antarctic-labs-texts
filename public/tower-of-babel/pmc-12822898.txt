Challenges and future directions of gastric cancer prevention in Iran: Perspectives of the key stakeholders
Authors: Maryam Sabouri, Nastaran Keshavarz Mohammadi, Ali Ramazenkhani, Mashhid Namdari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Gastric cancer (GC) is a critical public health concern that demands urgent attention. It is one of the most prevalent cancers in Iran, and it also has the highest mortality rate among all other cancers. Despite being recognized as one of the most preventable types of cancer worldwide, the healthcare system in Iran faces considerable challenges in preventing GC to curb its incidence effectively. This qualitative study explores the perspectives of public health and clinical experts regarding these complexities and explains how more effective preventive measures could be developed.MATERIALS AND METHODS:An exploratory qualitative study was conducted in Iran from December 2023 to June 2024. The data collection involved semistructured, in-depth, face-to-face interviews with 31 experts in various fields related to GC, including education, research, prevention, diagnosis, treatment, and management. These experts are affiliated with organizations such as the Ministry of Health and Medical Education, medical universities, hospitals, health centers, cancer research centers, education and research centers, the Iranian Cancer Association, and The Academy of Medical Sciences of the Islamic Republic of Iran, Tehran. Transcripts of the interviews formed the content of the data for analysis. Conventional content analysis of the data was conducted using MAXQDA 20.RESULTS:The experts’ perspectives were categorized into two main sections: (1) the challenges of GC prevention in Iran, along with the barriers hindering the implementation of prevention, and (2) strategies for enhancing the prevention system. These strategies include fostering a healthier culture, increasing health status and value, empowering individuals regarding cancer prevention, and developing health-supportive environments.CONCLUSION:Given the prevalence and mortality rate of GC in Iran, its prevention requires greater attention. The findings of our study emphasize the importance of GC prevention in Iran, which requires more attention and a shift from a medical approach to a social one. In this regard, the most important effective interventions are building health-related public laws aimed at creating a healthy social and biological environment and health promotional products.

Introduction

Cancer is among the leading causes of mortality worldwide, with nearly 10 million deaths in 2020,[1] and is considered a top public health challenge. Rising cancer incidents and lifestyle changes are responsible for a large proportion of mortality.[2] GC is one of the most lethal malignant tumors and multifactorial disease with a 5-year survival rate of about 20%.[3] GC is reported as the fifth most common cancer and the fourth leading cause of cancer death in the world.[4,5] The highest incidence rates are observed in Eastern Asia, including Mongolia, Japan, and the Republic of Korea, while the highest mortality rate is in West Asian countries of Tajikistan, Kyrgyzstan, and Iran.[6]

Cancer ranks the second most prevalent chronic noncommunicable disease in Iran and the third leading cause of death, following heart diseases, accidental deaths, and natural causes of death.[7] GC is known as the most common cancer among Iranian men, and it is reported to be the third most common cancer, following breast and colorectal cancers in women.[8,9] Hence, GC is the most lethal type of cancer in Iran. The average GC-related mortality rate in Iranian men and women has been reported to be 22.4 and 12.5 per 100,000, respectively.[10] In addition, GC remains responsible for 8965 deaths in a year, which makes it the top leading cause of cancer-related deaths.[8,9] This cancer is highly prevalent in the northwest and north of Iran, especially in the Azeri-speaking provinces, For example, Ardabil reports the highest rates in Iran, with 25.4 men and 49.1 women per 100,000.[11]

Environmental and lifestyle are critical factors in the development of GC.[3] Exposure to risk factors and access to screening services influence cancer incidence.[12,13] In addition, low socioeconomic status, older age, and male gender are among the demographic factors that significantly contribute to increasing GC incidence.[14,15] Hence, the rising average age of the Iranian population can affect the prevalence and prognosis of GC.[8] Cancer prevention has been shown to be a cost-effective, long-term strategy for cancer control.[12] Nevertheless, cancer prevention has received little attention in most countries. Research suggests that both the public and healthcare professionals lack adequate knowledge regarding the potential benefits of cancer prevention.[7] Some studies conducted in Iran indicate inadequate awareness and negative attitudes of the public towards GC. In addition, the participation rate in screening programs is unacceptably low.[16] Raising awareness and addressing modifiable risk factors can significantly affect the rate of GC prevention. This qualitative study aimed to investigate the experts’ perspectives on the challenges of GC prevention and identify the strategies to enhance preventive measures among Iranian adults.

Materials and MethodsStudy design and setting

This qualitative study is part of a research project focused on the development, psychometrics, and application of a GC prevention literacy tool in Iranian adults. This study was conducted using in-depth face-to-face interviews in Iran between December 2023 and June 2024.

Study participants and sampling

The study populations consisted of researchers, managers, or those with clinical experience in GC management according to the World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines, who were willing to participate in the study. These professionals were selected through purposive sampling with maximum diversity. The participants were informed of their voluntary participation and their right to withdraw from the study at any time.

Data collection tool and technique

Semistructured, in-depth interview was used to collect data. The first author (M. S.) a PhD candidate in health education and health promotion who has also been trained in qualitative research, conducted interviews using an interview guide including probing question. Informed consent and permission to record were also obtained from the participants. All the interviews, except two, were conducted at the participants’ workplaces. Only those two exceptions were conducted through a phone call. The interviews lasted between 20 and 75 minutes, during which field notes were taken by the interviewer, and the interviews were transcribed immediately. Each participant was interviewed only once. Data saturation was achieved by interviewing 17 women and 14 men (Additional File 1 contains examples of interview questions).

Data analysis

Data analysis and data collection were conducted simultaneously in five steps: 1) transcribing the entire interview immediately after it was performed, 2) reading the whole text to grasp a general understanding and summary of the content, 3) breaking down the data into meaningful units and initial codes, 4) grouping similar codes under broader categories, and 5) determining the main topic for each category.[17] First, the author, M. S., transcribed the recorded interviews word for word in Persian. Then, they sent the transcription to the participants for their approval or comments. The transcriptions were uploaded in MAXQDA 20 for analysis.[18] Conventional qualitative content analysis was used in the study.[19]

Then, another author (N. K., a qualitative researcher) reviewed the transcript for quality assurance. Research team members, M. S, N. K. and A. R., independently coded the assigned transcripts. Meaning units and initial codes were extracted through reading the transcripts line by line during coding process. Coders discussed any differences in coding to reach a consensus. Afterward, the codes were categorized into more comprehensive categories, considering their similarities and differences. Specifically, similar subcategories were placed within the same category, and similar categories were organized under the same concept. Ultimately, the main topics were identified, including those which are reported in this article: 1) Challenges of GC prevention in Iran, along with the barriers hindering the implementation of prevention, and 2) strategies for enhancing the prevention system. Guba and Lincoln’s criteria were applied to ensure trustworthiness of the study.[20] The principal investigator (M. S.) spent a decent amount of time reviewing the data meticulously over an extended period and then documented all the stages of the research with precision from start to finish. Subsequently, the coded interviews were given to two participants for feedback. Moreover, the credibility of the data can be assured through multiple individuals. The data were analyzed independently by two different researchers to ensure dependability. Confirmability was ensured by having a qualified researcher who was well-versed in qualitative studies and thoroughly reviewed and validated the data analysis process. This researcher was familiar with qualitative studies but was not involved in the research itself. Precise documentation of all the stages and the decisions made along the way, detailed description of the participants, sampling method, time and place of data collection, diversity in research participants contributed to the transferability of the findings.

Ethical consideration

The participants were informed of their confidentiality and voluntary participation before the study. The objectives of the study were also explained to all the participants before the interview. Informed consent was obtained from all the participants. This study has been approved with the ethics code of IR.SBMU.PHNS.REC.1402.117 by the Research Ethics Committee of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences, School of Public Health and Safety, Neuroscience Research Center.

Challenges in the prevention of gastric cancer

Low priority of health and prevention among individuals

Some experts have stated that people, especially men, often neglect their health and preventive care. They believe that this neglect is rooted in living conditions, sometimes a low prioritization of health, and a lack of concern for their well-being.

“The pressures on people, these days people are mentally under pressure due to various reasons. This kind of pressure is either economic or family-related. The truth is that it sometimes makes them neglect their own health, meaning health comes next. They are under so much pressure that they don’t look for ways to prevent GC. People have a limited understanding of the importance of health because of the problems they have.” (Interview No. 27, a 33-year-old female, Ph.D)

Several interviewees believed that health system is treatment-oriented, and treatment is prioritized over prevention. One of the participants mentioned the lack of guidelines for screening GC as an instance or the reason for the frequent neglect of preventive measures. “The problems of the health system, is that it pays little attention to its main mission, which is health promotion and disease prevention, as it is rarely addressed. The primary focus of the health system has always been its human resources, centers, facilities, management, hospital-related policies, and rehabilitation centers. I mean … they overlook the importance of prevention. Anyways, there are the main challenges that exist and must be revised.” (Interview No. 10, a 67-year-old male, M.D)

Barriers to prevention

More than half of the interviewees argued that failure in the effective GC prevention system is not limited to the low priority of prevention among people and the health system. Rather, it is rooted in the existing barriers or missing enabling factors that hinder the adoption of preventive behaviors or actions against GC at the individual, organizational, or environmental levels. These obstacles can be categorized into knowledge, cultural, economic, and environmental barriers.

Many argued that the lack of sufficient knowledge and skills regarding cancer prevention—often described as “low awareness and low health literacy”—is among the obstacles to adopting preventive behaviors against cancer, including GC. In addition, a few interviewees noted that men tend to be indifferent to their health.

Many interviewees mentioned the “financial crisis” among the barriers to prevention. Almost half of the interviewees stated that many individuals are well aware of the importance of prevention, but they are financially stretched to cover the prevention expenses.

Moreover, the interviewees stated that the private sector provides a high share of preventive services and health assessment services at costs that are not covered by insurance. This is also a possible factor preventing people to seek help. Some also argued that busy lives and time pressure create barriers to obtaining the required preventive health services, particularly when healthcare facilities are located far from their homes:

“Many people are swamped, people who are employed cannot. they do not have the time, energy, or resources for checkups or even attend educational classes.” (Interview No. 8, a 45-year-old female, Ph.D).

Strategies for enhancing the prevention system

Some interviewees believed that there are various barriers to cancer prevention that need to be addressed from multiple aspects. In line with the identified barriers to prevention, interviewees introduced numerous individual, organizational, and environmental solutions to improve cancer prevention in Iran.

An analysis of what interviewees stated regarding improving prevention system reveals some insights. That is, their responses vary based on their understanding of different factors including individual, social, and organizational.

Interviewees, especially more experienced ones, suggested several strategies: 1) fostering a culture of health and the significance of well-being, 2) promoting individual empowerment for cancer prevention, and 3) fostering health-supportive environments both at home and within the community.

Fostering a culture of prevention and the significance of well-being

Almost half of the interviewees reported that fostering a culture of prevention and emphasizing the significance of well-being is one of the strategies. For instance, one interviewee stated:

“The way I see it, depends on how much we value life and how much effort we are willing to invest in it.” (Interview No. 31, a 53-year-old female, Ph.D).

“The way I see it, depends on how much we value life and how much effort we are willing to invest in it.” (Interview No. 31, a 53-year-old female, Ph.D).

“The way I see it, depends on how much we value life and how much effort we are willing to invest in it.” (Interview No. 31, a 53-year-old female, Ph.D).

Promoting individual empowerment for cancer prevention

Improving personal development and health literacy for a healthy lifestyle

Nearly half of the interviewees reported that improving self-efficacy, resilience, motivation, life expectancy, fear management, and stress management are all essential for cancer prevention as a means of individual empowerment.

“Motivation and life expectancy matter a great deal. Well, I engage in lots of preventive behaviors.” (Interview No. 16, a 52-year-old female holding a master’s degree).

“Motivation and life expectancy matter a great deal. Well, I engage in lots of preventive behaviors.” (Interview No. 16, a 52-year-old female holding a master’s degree).

“Motivation and life expectancy matter a great deal. Well, I engage in lots of preventive behaviors.” (Interview No. 16, a 52-year-old female holding a master’s degree).

Most interviewees stated that personal empowerment requires improving personal development skills for a healthy lifestyle. Personal development skills are learned through education related to GC, including nutrition and lifestyle modification. Some interviewees believe that education prevents diseases and GC by fostering healthy behaviors and reducing exposures to risk factors. According to all the interviewees, health literacy includes proper nutrition, which everyone should follow. This means avoiding fast foods, foods high in sugar and salt, soft drinks, canned food, processed food, smoked food, and frozen protein for long-term storage. In addition, individuals must be educated and encouraged to consume fresh fruits and vegetables, get tested for Helicobacter pylori, learn stress management techniques, ensure they get enough and regular sleep, engage in regular physical activity, and learn table manners and dining etiquette (chew your food until it becomes soft, eat slowly, avoid swallowing air while eating, sit up straight). The interviewees described healthy eating skills and a healthy lifestyle as follows:

“Given the fact that nutritional risk factors play a critical role in the pathogenesis of an organ like the stomach, one of the most essential things that people should consider is proper eating manners or eating etiquette. Choosing foods in terms of quantity, quality, and variety matters as well.” (Interview No. 10, a 67-year-old male, M.D)

Fostering health-supportive environments at homes and within communities

Creating a supportive social environment for promoting healthy behaviors

Many participants raised the importance of health-supportive environments for health prevention, including homes, neighborhoods, and workplaces. For instance, more than half of the interviewees considered creating a supportive social environment for promoting healthy behaviors. From the perspective of some participants, home, as a social environment, plays a critical role in fostering a culture of prevention and supporting a healthy lifestyle.

In addition, enhancing health and the biological and social appeal of work and living environments, according to some interviewees, may effectively prevent GC.

“People living in a healthy environment—in terms of clean air, water, and soil—which are the prerequisites of health, along with the socially desirable and clean environment, have greater protection against many diseases, including GC.” (Interview No. 31, a 53-year-old female, Ph.D).

These must be identified and, as far as possible, eliminated or replaced with low-risk substances” (Interview No. 18, a 58-year-old male, M.D).

Reorienting health services and systems

Many interviewees suggested that the health system should be overhauled, reoriented, and focused on prevention and health promotion. Free screening services, prioritizing prevention over treatment, overhauling the insurance system, and shifting to preventive care in a new health system are among the proposed changes. Below are some comments that exemplify these suggestions:

“For specific centers such as those for digestive diseases—like hospitals, like health network—there is a need for proactive measures. For example, in cases of common digestive diseases that are persistent or recurrent, or the groups who are prone to digestive diseases, or those who previously experienced a major digestive problem, procedures such as endoscopy should be conducted earlier and on a wider spectrum” (Interview No. 10, 67-year-old man, M.D). and “Primary prevention is definitely better. This approach not only increases costs but also burdens families.” (Interview No. 15, a 37-year-old male holding a master’s degree)

Discussion

GC prevention requires assessing the current situation, challenges, barriers, and opportunities for improvement and empowering strategies. Identifying these leads to designing and implementing more effective primary and secondary prevention strategies. This qualitative study provided a comprehensive perspective on the culture of GC prevention within the public and healthcare system. It also assessed the performance of the healthcare system at the level of cancer prevention and individual, organizational, and social challenges related to cancer prevention. Moreover, this study proposed strategies for improving GC prevention in Iran.

Owing to the qualitative nature of this study, we cannot determine the frequency of these perspectives within the community or generalize them to the entire society. However, the findings provide a deeper understanding of the dimensions and reasons for the current state of GC prevention in Iran. Gaining this understanding can help design interventions to improve the situation. One of the important findings of this study was the classification of the causes of the current situation, the challenges or barriers involved, and strategies at levels, including organizational, social, environmental, and individual.

The interviewees in this study pointed out the poor condition and performance of prevention and the absence of policies for the prevention of GC according to the experts. They believed that the healthcare system needs to shift its focus from diagnosing and treating cancer, including GC, to emphasizing prevention. To achieve this, new programs and policies are required. They believed GC screening programs should be integrated into broader prevention initiatives. Research has shown that collaboration among healthcare policymakers, general practitioners, and the public can enhance the selection and implementation of effective screening programs, ultimately helping to prevent GC and reduce its incidence.[21]

From the interviewees’ perspective, free and comprehensive screening and insurance coverage, an insurance system overhaul, and the need to foster a proactive approach in the healthcare system led to cost-effectiveness. In previous studies regarding screening and eradication of H. pylori infection,[22,23] it has been found that health insurance coverage for the eradication of H. pylori in patients with chronic gastritis, confirmed through endoscopic diagnosis, is beneficial.[24,25] In addition, insurance coverage for a GC prevention program that includes H. pylori screening is considered cost-effective.[26] This also applies to radiographic or gastroscopic screening for Japanese aged 50 years and for endoscopic or radiographic examinations for all Koreans aged 40 years, which should occur every two years.[27]

One of the important challenges is the unavoidable exposure to cancer risk factors such as air pollution or agricultural products, and many interviewees expressed concerns about this issue. Other studies have similarly addressed these challenges as well. For instance, numerous studies have indicated a connection between exposure to arsenic, nitrates, and radiation and the development of cancer, including GC. Although the certainty of the cause-and-effect relationship may vary across different studies and risk factors, it is prudent to take them all seriously.

According to the interviewees, arsenic and its compounds are carcinogenic to humans. These substances can sometimes be integrated through the consumption of certain types of rice. Studies have shown that high mortality rates of bladder, kidney, skin, liver, and colon cancers are prevalent in many arsenic-contaminated areas.[28] Humans are exposed to arsenic through drinking water, rice, and other foods, making it essential to reduce exposure to arsenic through rice consumption.[29]

Nitrates and nitrites are considered risk factors for GC; they are naturally present in fruits and vegetables. However, these substances are also often used as food additives in processed meats such as ham, sausages, and hot dogs.[30] Moreover, exposure to radiation and working in industries such as coal mines, rubber production, and leather tanning all increase the risk of GC.[31,32]

The nature of these challenges indicates that addressing them requires interventions beyond just educational, health, and medical measures, as well as improving environmental conditions.[33] One of the most vital strategies, taking a health promotion approach, is to establish laws that prevent or reduce the production of these risk factors or limit people’s exposure to them.[34] These laws can help create health-supportive or health-promoting living and working environments. Creating health-supportive environments—both physical and social—is the second health promotion strategy based on the Ottawa Charter,[35] which was also frequently mentioned by participants of this study. In addition, implementing prevention-oriented laws and programs focused on environmental sanitation, improving air quality, and ensuring food safety is crucial.

International studies have also reported that physical activity in schools, universities, and workplaces contributes to a healthy and safe environment. Moreover, these activities support the establishment of food subsidies[36] and the provision of food/beverage subsidies, which can have positive health effects in the control of noncommunicable diseases.[37]

Using a socioecological framework can help clarify primary (risk reduction) and secondary (early detection) prevention strategies aimed at reducing the burden of cancer in low- and middle-income countries. This approach is conducted at individual, organizational, social, and political levels.[38] In addition, mycotoxin prevention and control strategies can be implemented through a combination of effective environmental controls, good agricultural practices, proper storage conditions, quality assurance programs, and safe postharvest detoxification methods.[39] All in all, these strategies contribute to creating a supportive environment.

Although the government plays a central role in creating a healthy environment—both physically and socially—the roles of individuals in society in preventing GC cannot be neglected. It is worth noting that adopting and having a healthy lifestyle is the responsibility of individuals. According to the interviewees’ perspective, encouraging and supporting individuals regarding preventive measures and teaching a healthy lifestyle prevents them from being exposed to GC risk factors. Interviewees in the study mentioned institutionalizing prevention through education on lifestyles, nutrition, and methods as an approach to preventing exposure to risk factors. Research also indicates that education affects preventive behaviors against noncommunicable diseases and promotes healthy behaviors.[40,41,42,43,44]

Although exposure to environmental risk factors is often beyond the individual’s control, there are lifestyle choices that they can manage, such as dietary habits, tobacco and alcohol consumption, and sleeping patterns. Environmental risk factors include a lack of access to facilities and resources that would allow individuals to avoid risk factors. Moreover, financial and time constraints are among the inevitable risk factors. For instance, studies have shown that unhealthy diets are associated with the development of GC. Cooking meats at high temperatures—frying and grilling—results in the production of carcinogenic chemicals, such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, which can lead to GC. In addition, artificial fruit juices, smoked and salted fish, hot tea, diets high in sodium, as well as high consumption of processed meats, pickles,[45] moldy food, hot food, overeating sweets, sugar-sweetened beverages, high-fat dairy products, fast foods, and sauces all contribute to the risk of GC.[46,47]

In addition, frequent consumption of canned and hot foods, irregular mealtimes, eating fast and overeating increase the risk of developing GC.[47] Smoking also has a destructive and irreversible effect on stomach tissues and increase the risk of malignant cancer.[48] The considerable risk of GC for individuals who sleep more than 10 hours is a new and potentially modifiable risk factor for stomach cancer.[49,50] Excessive levels of stress hormones contribute to carcinogenesis by damaging DNA and related pathways.[51] However, it is worth noting that the impact of stress hormones on GC cells is not yet apparent, according to the evidence.[52]

The findings of our study are in line with other studies indicating that protective behaviors play a vital role in reducing GC risk; as an illustration, the protective effect of fruits and vegetables on GC,[42,53] testing for Helicobacter pylori,[43] high levels of physical activity,[44] and exposure reduction to risk factors such as tobacco use.[43]

Another study mentioned the results of the primary intervention for GC prevention, which are as follows: dietary changes, such as eating more fruits and vegetables and reducing salt or salt-preserved foods.[54] In addition, lifestyle modifications, including increasing physical activity and quitting smoking, all contribute to GC prevention.[55]

As some experts in this study pointed out, people often face some degree of environmental and economic barriers to adopting a healthy lifestyle. Providing empowering programs encompassing health literacy, stress management skills, personal development, and medical support can be an effective strategy for overcoming these barriers. This approach is crucial for promoting healthier lifestyles and preventing stomach cancer in Iran.

Strengths and limitations

One important strength of this study was incorporating multiple experts’ perspectives, which provided a deeper understanding of GC prevention in Tehran, Iran. Second, we interviewed individuals with diverse majors and fields of expertise. However, patients’ perspectives were not included in this study.

Conclusion

Given the prevalence and lethality of GC in Iran, there is a need for increased focus on cancer prevention. The findings of our study emphasize that preventing GC in Iran requires a shift from a medical approach to a broader emphasis on social health promotion. In this regard, strategies for effective GC prevention include build healthy public laws that foster a healthy social and biological environment and promoting health-supportive products. This study had a novelty in explaining the challenges of gastric cancer prevention and the current situation based on the perspectives of key stakeholders.

The findings of our study can be a starting point for devising national programs aimed at GC prevention in Iran. This study emphasizes that preventing GC requires social and environmental improvement and empowering individuals to adopt healthier lifestyles. These prevention-oriented interventions should be comprehensive, immediate, and at broader levels, including individual, social, and environmental. That is, implementing prevention-oriented health laws and services, providing financial support, combating environmental pollution, and ensuring food safety.

The results of this study provide an opportunity to establish a solid foundation for the design and implementation of national intervention programs with the purpose of preventing and reducing incidence of GC successfully. This research can also be used as a guide for managers and policymakers to develop a prevention program. Future research studies should provide convincing evidence on potential barriers and facilitators of policymaking regarding GC prevention. In addition, efforts should be made to design and implement interventions and programs for at least vulnerable groups, exploring appropriate strategies to improve their implementation in a way that helps intervention planners and implementers achieve maximum effectiveness and sustainability while minimizing challenges.
