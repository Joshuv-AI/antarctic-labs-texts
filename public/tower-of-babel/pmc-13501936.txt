Lung adenocarcinoma dural metastasis at the petroclival region mimicking meningioma: a frozen-section diagnostic pitfall. Illustrative case
Authors: Tomoya Sofue, Yudai Morisaki, Yumi Ko, Kenta Nakase, Shohei Yokoyama, Shuichi Yamada, Ichiro Nakagawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Intracranial dural metastases occur in approximately 9% of patients with advanced cancer and are most commonly derived from breast, prostate, and lung cancers.1 They are most frequently located along the cranial convexity (40.9%), whereas skull base involvement is less common (22.2%).2 Solitary skull base dural metastases may mimic meningiomas because of their homogeneous enhancement, dural attachment, and occasional dural tail sign.3 Intraoperative ­frozen-section diagnosis may also be challenging because crush artifact, limited sampling, and architectural distortion can lead to diagnostic pitfalls in CNS lesions.4 We report an easily misinterpreted case of lung adenocarcinoma presenting as a solitary dural metastasis along the anterior petrous ridge that mimicked meningioma both radiologically and on frozen-section diagnosis.

Illustrative Case

A 74-year-old woman presented with numbness of the left lip and tongue. She also reported diplopia that worsened on left gaze. Neurological examination suggested trochlear nerve palsy and severe dysesthesia in the maxillary division (V2) of the trigeminal nerve. No facial nerve or vestibulocochlear nerve dysfunction was observed.

MRI demonstrated a 21 × 16–mm homogeneously enhancing mass in the left cerebellopontine angle attached to the tentorium along the anterior petrous ridge (Fig. 1A– D). The lesion showed a dural tail sign and compressed the pons dorsomedially. Mild adjacent brainstem T2 hyperintensity suggestive of edema was also observed. Contrast-enhanced CT also showed homogeneous enhancement without obvious calcification (Fig. 1E and F). The patient had a history of pulmonary Mycobacterium avium complex infection treated approximately 10 years earlier and bronchiectasis. A meningioma arising from the tentorium and the anterior edge of the petrous bone was suspected, with extension into Meckel’s cave. Therefore, the anterior transpetrosal approach was chosen to achieve trigeminal nerve decompression and provide early access to the petroclival lesion. FIG. 1.Preoperative images. Intraoperative Findings and Frozen-Section Analysis

After incising the tentorium and posterior fossa dura, the tumor exposed in the cisternal space was submitted for frozen-section analysis (Fig. 2A). The tentorium appeared thickened and infiltrated, findings consistent with meningioma. However, the tumor showed marked adhesion to the trigeminal nerve and brainstem (Fig. 2B and C), and its yellowish appearance in the cisternal space was atypical for meningioma (Fig. 2D). Final Pathological Findings

Permanent histopathological examination revealed infiltrative tumor cells proliferating within the dural connective tissue (Fig. 4A and B). The tumor was composed predominantly of irregular glandular structures with focal solid growth patterns (Fig. 4C). Tumor cells demonstrated enlarged nuclei with prominent nucleoli and moderate to marked nuclear atypia. A focal desmoplastic stromal reaction was also observed. Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated positivity for CK7 (Fig. 4D) and TTF-1 (Fig. 4E) and negativity for somatostatin receptor 2A (SSTR2A) (Fig. 4F), CK20, GATA3, and estrogen receptor. These findings were consistent with dural metastasis of lung adenocarcinoma. FIG. 4.Final pathological findings. Postoperative Course

Gross-total resection of the intracranial lesion was achieved (Fig. 5A and B). The patient’s facial numbness gradually improved, and no new neurological deficits developed. Following the final pathological diagnosis, a systemic evaluation was performed to identify the primary lesion. The imaging evaluation indicated that the 26-mm nodular lesion, which had previously been difficult to evaluate because of atelectasis, represented the primary tumor, while the multiple small nodules in both lungs were considered intrapulmonary metastases. Metastatic lesions were identified in the sacrum and both lungs (Fig. 5C). The patient was subsequently referred for systemic chemotherapy and radiation therapy, which are currently ongoing. The intracranial lesion has been managed with observation alone. FIG. 5.Postoperative findings. A:AxialMR image showing gross-total resection of the tumor. Discussion

Dural-based lesions include a broad spectrum of entities, such as meningioma, metastatic tumors, primary CNS lymphoma, and solitary fibrous tumor/hemangiopericytoma spectrum. Inflammatory conditions, including immunoglobulin G4–related disease, may also present as dural masses. These lesions often show overlapping imaging features such as homogeneous enhancement and dural attachment, making radiological differentiation challenging. Radiologically, dural metastases can also mimic meningiomas. Several reports have described dural metastases presenting with a dural tail sign, as seen in this case.3,5 Some reports have indicated that the dural tail sign itself is not reliable for distinguishing dural metastases from meningiomas.5,6 Intracranial dural metastases are less common than brain parenchymal metastases but occur in approximately 9% of patients with advanced cancer. The most common primary tumors include breast, prostate, and lung cancers.1 According to Alali et al., the cranial convexity is the most common site of dural metastasis (40.9%), whereas skull base involvement is relatively uncommon, accounting for 10.9% at the sphenoid ridge, 8.7% at the tuberculum sellae, and only 2.6% in the petroclival region.2

Tumor-to-tumor metastasis to meningioma is a recognized phenomenon, most commonly arising from lung and breast carcinomas.7,8 Meningiomas are the most frequent intracranial recipient tumors, possibly due to their rich vascular supply, slow growth, and a favorable microenvironment for metastatic seeding.8 In our case, no meningothelial features were identified on hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, and SSTR2A staining (reported to have higher sensitivity and specificity than epithelial membrane antigen [EMA] staining for meningioma) was negative, arguing against tumor-to-meningioma metastasis.

In the present case, preoperative systemic screening with plain CT of the neck, chest, abdomen, and pelvis and tumor marker evaluation showed no significant abnormalities except for small pulmonary nodules, which was initially interpreted as inflammatory change. Furthermore, no imaging findings specific for metastasis were identified, and the lesion was radiologically indistinguishable from meningioma. The patient presented with trigeminal and trochlear nerve symptoms; however, given the tumor location and size, such symptoms could also occur in meningioma, and dural metastasis was therefore not suspected based on the neurological findings alone. Consequently, the presence of primary lung adenocarcinoma was not initially recognized. Given these findings, metastatic disease was not strongly suspected in the preoperative setting. Even if metastasis had been diagnosed intraoperatively, the surgical strategy would likely not have changed because decompression and pathological diagnosis were necessary for symptom relief. However, an intraoperative diagnosis of metastasis may have prompted earlier systemic evaluation and oncological planning. Although the retrosigmoid approach provides an adequate surgical field without temporal lobe retraction, the anterior transpetrosal approach was selected because it allows earlier treatment of the tumor attachment and more sufficient decompression of the trigeminal nerve.

An important lesson in this case was that metastatic lung adenocarcinoma was misinterpreted as meningothelial meningioma on intraoperative frozen-section examination. Permanent histopathological analysis revealed irregular glandular structures with focal solid growth patterns, consistent with lung adenocarcinoma metastasis. Intraoperative interpretation was likely confounded by freezing-related artifact, crush artifact, and architectural distortion during specimen preparation, resulting in a spindle cell–like appearance that can mimic meningothelial lesions. As described by Chand et al.,4 intraoperative diagnostic pitfalls include both technical factors and interpretative limitations, particularly due to overlapping morphological patterns in spindle cell–rich lesions, in which whorl-like or meningothelial-like features are not specific to meningioma. Our pathologist noted that the intraoperative frozen-section findings were not entirely typical of meningioma; however, given the crush artifact, they were still considered compatible with meningioma, even on retrospective review. Previous studies have reported diagnostic accuracy rates for intraoperative consultation in CNS lesions ranging from 83% to 97%, highlighting both the utility and inherent limitations of frozen-section diagnosis.9 Therefore, careful interpretation of frozen-section findings and appropriate use of immunohistochemistry (e.g., EMA and cytokeratin) are essential when considering the differential diagnosis.

Observations

A dural-based cerebellopontine angle mass showed homogeneous enhancement, dural attachment, and a dural tail sign on MRI, suggestive of meningioma. Frozen-section analysis demonstrated spindle cell–like proliferation with whorl-like features; however, freezing-related artifacts, crush artifacts, and architectural distortion obscured glandular differentiation and resulted in a meningioma-like interpretation. Final pathological analysis revealed metastatic lung adenocarcinoma.

Lessons

Frozen-section diagnosis of dural-based lesions may be misleading when freezing-related artifact and crush artifact obscure true tumor architecture, producing meningioma-like morphology. Dural metastasis should be included in the differential diagnosis even when frozen-section analysis suggests meningioma, and final diagnosis should rely on permanent histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses.
