Genetic and molecular factors underlying human longevity and epigenetic aging
Authors: Kai Gai, Wenjian Li, Junpeng Li, Haibin He, Lulu Shi, MiaoMiao Tang, Fang Wang, Tianpeng Chang, Yang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Biological aging is a complex process associated with declining physiological function and increased risk of aging-related diseases. However, its risk factors and molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood. Here, we performed a comprehensive integrative analysis to identify putative risk factors and molecular phenotypes associated with four epigenetic aging acceleration and human longevity. We first investigated the association between aging-related traits and potential risk factors using genome-wide association study (GWAS) data, identifying cholesterol levels, immune cell traits and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF1) as associated with longevity. To investigate the molecular mechanisms, we integrated GWAS summary data for epigenetic aging and longevity with five types of molecular QTL (xQTL) datasets, including gene expression (eQTL), splicing (sQTL), alternative polyadenylation (apaQTL), protein (pQTL), and metabolite QTL (mQTL). We identified 30 genes, 11 splicing events, 5 proteins, 3 alternative polyadenylation events, and 39 metabolites associated with aging-related traits, highlighting key regulatory mechanisms that link genetic variants to epigenetic aging and longevity. Drug-target annotation using DrugBank further prioritized therapeutic candidates, including CASP8, PSRC1 and SORT, as potential intervention targets. These findings provide a comprehensive resource for understanding the molecular architecture of aging and highlight potential novel targets for precision interventions in aging-related diseases.

Introduction

Aging is a complex, multifactorial biological process influenced by the interplay of genetic, environmental, and molecular factors1. Despite advances in understanding its mechanisms, effective strategies to delay or reverse biological aging remain limited. Among available biomarkers, epigenetic clocks have emerged as the most promising indicators of biological age. These clocks show strong correlation with chronological age and other aging-related phenotypes, offering valuable measurements for tracking the biological aging process. Moreover, traits such as healthspan, lifespan, and exceptional longevity are widely considered representative phenotypes for studying the biology of aging. Genetic variation contributes substantially to inter-individual differences in aging-related traits2, with classical heritability estimates ranging from 15 to 33%3,4. However, recent methodological studies indicate that intrinsic genetic influences on aging outcomes may exceed 50% when accounting for biases from extrinsic mortality and other confounding factors5. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified key pathways implicated in longevity, including lipid metabolism6, immune function7, and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF1) signaling8. These pathways are crucial for maintaining metabolic homeostasis, regulating inflammation, promoting tissue repair, and preserving physiological resilience during aging. However, while observational studies suggest associations between these pathways and aging, the causal relationships remain poorly defined.

The increasing availability of large-scale GWAS for aging risk factors presents a unique opportunity to refine the understanding of genetic factors contributing to aging processes. Recent advances in the multivariate GWAS method allow the joint analysis of multiple genetically correlated traits. By integrating univariate GWAS summary statistics, this method effectively increases sample sizes and improves statistical power to identify novel loci9. This approach has been applied to various aging-related phenotypes, such as healthspan, parental lifespan, and exceptional longevity10. Additionally, molecular quantitative trait loci (xQTL) datasets provide multidimensional insight into gene expression, protein levels, and metabolites. However, a unified framework to link molecular signatures to aging-related traits, such as epigenetic aging and longevity, is still lacking. Mendelian Randomization (MR) offers a powerful approach for causal inference by using genetic variants as instrumental variables, thereby reducing confounding and reverse causation biases that commonly affect observational studies11. The generalized summary-data-based MR (GSMR) method further improves traditional MR by accounting for linkage disequilibrium (LD) between instruments, thereby improving power and robustness12. To link the GWAS findings with molecular phenotypes, summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR) is developed to integrate GWAS and xQTL data to identify pleiotropic associations between molecular traits and complex diseases13. Importantly, SMR also includes a heterogeneity test (i.e., HEIDI test) to distinguish pleiotropic associations from spurious associations due to linkage. Building on SMR, the Omics Pleiotropic Association (OPERA) method uses a Bayesian framework that jointly analyzes GWAS and multi-omics xQTL summary statistics to identify colocalized molecular signals across omics layers14. Both SMR and OPERA have been successfully applied across a range of complex traits to prioritize shared causal variants and genetically proxied molecular associations, including genes, proteins, and metabolites, providing mechanistic insights and potential therapeutic targets15,16.

In this study, we present a systematic integration of genetic and multi-omics xQTL data to uncover molecular drivers of epigenetic aging acceleration and longevity. First, we applied GSMR to identify putative causal relationships between candidate risk factors and aging-related traits. We then use SMR and OPERA to detect pleiotropic genes, proteins, and metabolites associated with epigenetic aging and longevity. To interpret these associations, we performed pathway enrichment and functional annotation analyses. Lastly, we assessed the therapeutic potential of these targets by drug-target enrichment analysis with DrugBank, aiming to prioritize candidate targets for precision interventions in aging and longevity.

Results

The effects of risk factors on aging-related traits

An overview of the study design, data source, and analytic framework is presented in Fig. 1. To investigate the influence of biological risk factors on aging-related traits, we analyzed large-scale GWAS summary statistics from multiple sources. To assess potential causal relationships, we applied GSMR using each risk factor as the exposure and aging-related traits as outcomes. After correcting for multiple testing across 78 trait pairs (P < 6.41 × 10−4), we identified 27 significant putative causal effects involving both lipids and immune cell counts (Fig. 2b, Data S3). Specifically, HDL showed a protective effect on longevity (β = 0.03, P = 3.22 × 10−13) (Fig. 2c), whereas LDL exhibited a negative effect (β = −0.06, P = 1.21 × 10−66) (Fig. 2d). Among immune cell counts, white blood cells (β = −0.02, P = 3.41 × 10−11), neutrophils (β = −0.02, P = 1.43 × 10−9) and monocytes (β = −0.01, P = 6.49 × 10−6) were negatively associated with longevity (Fig. S1a–c). These risk factors also showed significant pro-aging effects on epigenetic aging acceleration, particularly on PhenoAge. For example, white blood cells (β = 0.41, P = 3.87 × 10−7), neutrophils (β = 0.50, P = 4.20 × 10−9), and monocytes (β = 0.25, P = 8.90 × 10−5) were significantly associated with accelerated PhenoAge (Fig. 2e and S1d, e). In contrast, IGF1 showed a nominally protective effect, with weak associations with decreased acceleration of PhenoAge (β = −0.19, P = 0.0049) (Fig. 2f), GrimAge (β = −0.148, P = 0.0053) (Fig. S1f), and Hannum (β = −0.104, P = 0.046) (Fig. S1g). Notably, the reverse MR analyses did not yield consistent or significant associations across these relationships (Data S3).

To evaluate the robustness of these putative causal associations identified by GSMR12, we performed sensitivity analysis using nine additional MR methods, including inverse variance weighted (IVW)27, Weighted Median, Robust, Simple Median28, mode-based estimation (Mode)29, MRlap30, mendelian randomization pleiotropy residual sum and outlier (PRESSO)31, robust adjusted profile score (RAPS)32, and Egger33. Across the 17 trait pairs identified by GSMR, most associations (94%) were replicated by at least one alternative method at a nominal significance level (Data S4), and most methods showed consistent effect directions (Fig. S2a–d, Data S4). For instance, the association between LDL and longevity was confirmed by 9 alternative MR methods (Egger, IVW, Mode, MRlap, PRESSO, RAPS, Robust, Simple Median, Weighted Median), reinforcing the robustness of the result (Fig. 2g). Overall, these findings provide strong evidence supporting the causal involvement of lipid metabolism and immune cell biological pathways in shaping aging-related traits. The high concordance across MR methods reduces the likelihood that the observed associations are driven by horizontal pleiotropy or invalid instrumental variables, highlighting the validity and robustness of our causal inferences.

Integrative analysis of eQTL, sQTL, and apaQTL identifies functionally relevant genes for aging-related traits

To identify genes associated with aging-related traits, we performed the SMR analyses13 by integrating GWAS summary statistics with eQTLs, sQTLs, and apaQTLs, respectively (Methods). Using whole blood-derived eQTL data from the eQTLGen study (n = 31,684)34, we identified 15 genes significantly associated with longevity at a genome-wide significance level (PSMR < 3.20 × 10−6). To distinguish pleiotropic associations from those due to LD, we applied the HEIDI test to all significant SMR associations (Methods). Of these 15 associated genes, 11 passed the HEIDI test (PHEIDI > 0.01), suggesting the involvement of shared causal variants influencing both gene expression and longevity (Methods). Several genes, including PSRC1, CASP8, PABPC4, PRMT6, and SKIV2L, showed positive effects on longevity, suggesting potential roles in lifespan extension. In contrast, PSMA4 and IRF4 were negatively associated with longevity, implicating them as potential contributors to accelerated aging (Fig. 3a, Data S5). For epigenetic aging traits, we identified 9 genes each significantly associated with Hannum and IEAA, and only ZMIZ2 was associated with PhenoAge (Fig. S3a–d, Data S6–S9). To validate these findings, we performed replication analyses using independent datasets. We used eQTLs from the INTERVAL study35 and GWAS summary statistics from the mvAge meta-analysis10, which combines data from five aging-related studies, including healthspan, parental lifespan, exceptional longevity, epigenetic age acceleration (EAA), and frailty (Methods). Of the 24 unique genes identified in the discovery analysis, 22 were available for replication analysis. We next performed the sQTL-based SMR analysis for aging-related traits using the brain sQTL data from the BrainMeta study (n = 2865)36, which is the largest available sQTL dataset. For longevity, we identified a single significant sQTL association for PSMA4, which passed both the multiple testing corrected SMR threshold (PSMR < 8.84 × 10−7) and HEIDI test (PHEIDI > 0.01) (Fig. 3b, Data S11). For epigenetic aging traits, we identified two genes (TPMT and SSRP1) for IEAA, two genes (ZNF37A and OPALIN) for Hannum, and two genes (CYP2J2 and TPMT) for PhenoAge (Fig. S4a–d, Data S12–S15). For replication, we used the mvAge GWAS summary data above and the sQTL data from the INTERVAL35. Of the six unique genes identified in the discovery analysis, two were available for replication analysis. Among these, one was replicated at a Bonferroni-corrected threshold of PSMR < 0.0025 (0.05/2) (Data S16).

We also explored the role of alternative polyadenylation by performing the apaQTL-SMR analyses using whole blood apaQTL data from the GTEx (n = 670)37. For longevity, we identified FBXL5 was significantly associated at multiple testing corrected SMR threshold (PSMR < 4.96e-5 and PHEIDI > 0.01), suggesting potential involvement of alternative 3’ UTR regulation in lifespan modulation (Fig. 3c, Data S17). Notably, FBXL5 showed a strong apaQTL association with longevity but lacked corresponding eQTL associations, highlighting the unique regulatory insights provided by alternative polyadenylation analysis (Fig. 3g). For epigenetic aging traits, significant apaQTL signals were identified at multiple testing corrected SMR threshold (PSMR < 4.89e-5 and PHEIDI > 0.01) at the ZMIZ2 locus for PhenoAge and at the PSMB9 locus for GrimAge (Fig. S5a–d, Data S18–S21). Replication analysis was not performed for apaQTL-SMR results due to the unavailability of an independent apaQTL dataset.

Integrative analysis of pQTL and metabolite QTL identifies functionally relevant proteins and metabolites

To identify circulating proteins associated with aging-related traits, we performed SMR13 analyses integrating GWAS summary statistics for aging-related traits with large-scale plasma pQTL data from the deCODE study (n = 35,559)38. In the longevity analysis, we identified 5 proteins (MICB, VARS, HSPA1L, ABO, and ASIP) significantly associated at the multiple testing corrected threshold (PSMR < 2.86 × 10−5 and PHEIDI > 0.01), suggesting shared causal variants influencing both protein levels and longevity (Fig. 3d, Data S22). For epigenetic aging traits, we identified two significant protein associations, MICB with Hannum and RPN1 with GrimAge (Fig. S6a–d, Data S23–S26). To validate these findings, we performed replication analyses using GWAS data from the mvAge meta-analysis and an independent pQTL dataset from the INTERVAL study (n = 3301)39. Among the six identified proteins, four were available for replication analysis, two were replicated at a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold (PSMR < 0.0125, 0.05/4) (Data S27). Notably, the MICB protein demonstrated a robust and consistent association across both pQTL datasets, supporting its potential functional role in aging processes, possibly through immune modulation (Data S27).

To explore the metabolic signatures of aging, we performed SMR analyses using metabolite QTL (mQTL) data from the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging (CLSA) (n = 8299)40. For longevity, we identified 39 metabolites significantly associated at a Bonferroni-corrected threshold (PSMR < 0.00112 and PHEIDI > 0.01) (Fig. 3e, Data S28). These metabolites include various glycerol derivatives (e.g., oleoyl-linoleoyl-glycerol) and fatty acid derivatives (e.g., hexadecanedioate, octadecanedioate), highlighting the potential involvement of lipid metabolism and energy homeostasis in lifespan regulation. In contrast, no metabolites reached statistical significance for the epigenetic aging traits after multiple testing correction (Fig. S7a–d, Data S29–S32), likely reflecting the limited power of GWAS for these traits. Replication analysis was not performed for metabolite SMR results due to the unavailability of an independent metabolite QTL dataset.

Identification of molecular pathways by joint analysis of multi-omics xQTL data

To uncover loci with pleiotropic associations across multiple molecular phenotypes, we applied the OPERA framework14, which integrates GWAS summary statistics with five types of molecular QTL datasets (i.e., eQTL, sQTL, apaQTL, pQTL, and mQTL). OPERA uses a Bayesian colocalization model to compute posterior probability of association (PPA), quantifying the likelihood that multiple molecular phenotypes jointly associate with a complex trait due to shared causal variants14. Using this approach, we identified 44 longevity-associated loci that showed joint association with at least two types of molecular phenotypes (Fig. 4, Data S33). One notable example is the PSRC1 locus (PPA = 0.99, PHEIDI > 0.01), where eQTL and sQTL showed consistent colocalization with the longevity GWAS signal, supporting a shared genetic basis. This aligns with prior findings on PSRC1’s role in lipid metabolism and cardiovascular aging41,42. Another example is the SWAP70 locus, where both gene expression and protein levels colocalized with the longevity GWAS signal (PPA = 0.73, PHEIDI > 0.01), suggesting a functionally coherent link between transcriptional and proteomic regulation in aging. We also identified a strong colocalization signal between the HSD17B12 gene and metabolite (X-17357) (PPA = 0.79, PHEIDI > 0.01), implicating possible transcriptional and metabolic involvement in lifespan modulation. Although the metabolite remains unannotated, the enzyme HSD17B12 is known to participate in fatty acid elongation and estrogen metabolism, pathways previously implicated in longevity43. Importantly, OPERA analysis revealed a colocalization locus with three types of molecular phenotypes at the MAPT locus (PPA > 0.7), involving multiple genes including CRHR1-IT1, LRRC37A2, and MAPT. This region is well known for its association with neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer’s and Parkinson disease44,45, and our findings suggest that its regulatory landscape may also influence longevity and epigenetic aging. Tissue-specific molecular phenotypes associated with aging-related traits

To explore the tissue-specific regulation of aging-related traits, we performed SMR analyses by integrating GWAS summary statistics with multi-tissue eQTL, sQTL, and apaQTL data from the GTEx project37. This analysis identified 331 genes, 299 splicing events, and 71 alternative polyadenylations associated with aging-related traits across tissues (Fig. 5a, Data S34–S36). These findings highlight the widespread influence of genetic regulation on aging phenotypes and reveal substantial tissue-specific heterogeneity. To further prioritize candidate genes with functional relevance, we integrated these results with differential expression analyses comparing aged and young individuals using transcriptomic datasets from the GTEx37. Three genes (i.e., CASP8, CELSR2, and PABPC4) showed consistent associations across both SMR and differential expression analyses (Data S37). Notably, CASP8 was positively associated with longevity in multiple tissues, including the spleen, lung, and heart, and was also significant in aging-related differential expression analyses (Fig. 5b, c and S8a). suggesting a robust and tissue-conserved role in the aging process. Beyond longevity, we identified 8 genes significantly associated with epigenetic aging traits, all of which were supported by the differential expression evidence (Data S38). One notable example is SCGN, which showed a significant positive SMR association with IEAA in adipose and thyroid tissues. Transcriptomic analysis further revealed age-associated downregulation of SCGN expression in both tissues (Fig. 5d, e and S8b), supporting its potential role in age-related physiological decline. We also observed tissue-specificity for gene-trait associations. For example, SORT1 was positively associated with longevity specifically in the liver, but showed no association in whole blood despite a larger sample size (Fig. 5f). Functional and drug target potential of aging-associated molecular phenotypes

To gain insights into the biological roles of genes and proteins associated with aging, we performed functional enrichment analyses using the Functional Mapping and Annotation (FUMA)46. Following multiple testing correction and HEIDI filtering, we retained 2421 non-redundant molecular phenotypes (including genes and proteins) for downstream analysis. The Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed significant enrichment of pathways related to immune regulation (i.e., immune response, inflammatory response, and lymphocyte activation) and metabolic processes (i.e., lipid metabolic process) (Fig. S9a, Data S39). These findings align with the known roles of chronic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation in biological aging. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis further highlighted key signaling cascades enriched for the associated genes, including MTOR signaling pathway, T cell receptor signaling pathway, and P53 signaling pathway (Fig. S9b, Data S39). These pathways are central to cellular aging, immune surveillance, and genomic stability, highlighting the functional relevance of the identified aging-associated genes (see “Discussion” for more details). For aging-associated metabolites, enrichment analysis identified pathways related to lipid and amino acid metabolism. Notably, significant enrichment was observed in the biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids and alpha-linolenic acid metabolism (Fig. S9c, Data S40), emphasizing the potential role of lipid remodeling in lifespan regulation (see “Discussion” for more details).

We next assessed the translational potential of the genes and proteins identified through SMR by mapping them to known drug targets in the DrugBank database. We note several interesting examples below. For example, NFKB1 is a key regulator of inflammation. Aspirin has been shown to suppress NFKB1 expression and inactivate the cAMP signaling pathway to treat atherosclerosis47. Additionally, FES was linked to fostamatinib, a drug used to treat chronic immune thrombocytopenia48. Fostamatinib has been shown to inhibit pathways associated with immune cell signaling and inflammation, potentially modulating the role of FES in these processes, which provides insights into its potential role in colon cancer therapy49. These findings highlight the potential of multi-omics SMR to uncover therapeutically relevant candidates for aging-related traits and support the repurposing of existing compounds for aging interventions.

Discussion

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive integrative analysis to identify putative risk factors and molecular phenotypes underlying aging-related traits by combining large-scale GWAS data with multi-omics xQTL datasets, including eQTLs, sQTLs, apaQTLs, pQTLs, and metabolite QTLs. Using the robust genetic correlation and MR analysis, we highlighted the associations of lipid metabolism, immune regulation, and IGF1 signaling with longevity. In total, we identified 82 molecular phenotypes associated with aging-related traits. Furthermore, functional annotation and drug-target integration revealed three potential therapeutic candidates, i.e., NFKB1, NDUFS5, and FES, providing novel insights into aging mechanisms and avenues for targeted interventions.

Our analyses revealed widespread genetic correlations between aging-related traits and both immune and lipid-related traits. Notably, HDL showed a strong positive genetic correlation with longevity, while LDL showed a negative association. These results likely reflect a shared lipid metabolic pathway influencing aging-related outcomes. This aligns with well-established roles of HDL in promoting reverse cholesterol transport and showing anti-inflammatory effects, which likely contribute to reduced cardiovascular risk and protect against age-related decline50,51. Similarly, multiple immune cell traits, including white blood cells, neutrophils, and monocytes, showed concordant negative associations with longevity. These immune traits are genetically and biologically correlated, and their consistent effects in MR analyses likely represent shared inflammatory and immune regulatory pathways underlying aging, in line with the “inflammaging” hypothesis52. Elevated innate immune cell counts reflect chronic low-grade inflammation, a hallmark of aging and a contributor to age-related diseases such as cardiovascular disease, metabolic disorders, and neurodegeneration53,54. In contrast, higher lymphocyte levels were positively associated with longevity, consistent with prior observations linking immune balance to healthier aging55,56. Regarding IGF1, while our analysis suggested a nominally protective role in longevity, it should be noted that the association did not survive stringent multiple testing correction. IGF1 is known to enhance immune cell function and reduce oxidative stress, supporting its potential anti-inflammatory role57,58. However, further investigation with more robust evidence is needed to confirm IGF1’s contribution to slowing biological aging.

Through SMR analysis, we identified several key genes and metabolites potentially contributing to longevity. One notable gene is CASP8, which showed a positive association with longevity. As a member of the cysteine-aspartic acid protease family, CASP8 plays a crucial role in maintaining cellular homeostasis by eliminating damaged cells, thereby preventing their accumulation and the acceleration of aging59. In addition, CASP8 has been identified as a drug target, with Bardoxolone (an experimental drug) showing efficacy in treating chronic kidney disease60 and other inflammatory disorders61. These findings suggest that pharmacological modulation of CASP8 activity could provide novel strategies for preventing and treating aging-related diseases. Another important gene, PSRC1, was identified in the liver-specific eQTL-based SMR analysis as being positively associated with longevity. PSRC1 is known to regulate serum lipid levels and has been implicated in coronary artery disease41,42. The association was specific to liver tissue, highlighting its likely role in hepatic lipid regulation (Data S34). Previous studies have shown that PSRC1 modulates both cholesterol transport and inflammatory responses, thereby reducing the atherosclerosis risk62. Furthermore, SMR analysis also identified the PSRC1 gene associated with increased HDL level (Fig. S10), supporting its protective function in lipid homeostasis and cardiovascular health. A closely related gene, SORT1, also showed a liver-specific association with longevity, in line with previous findings that it is involved in lipid metabolism63. Notably, the instrument SNP rs12740374 has been shown to enhance SORT1 expression in the liver by creating a C/EBP binding site, subsequently leading to lower LDL levels and reduced coronary artery disease risk64. Given the close correlation between PSRC1 and SORT1 in the liver, they likely act synergistically to influence lipid levels and contribute to increased longevity65. Among the 39 significantly associated metabolites, Oleoyl-linoleoyl-glycerol (18:1/18:2) was the only one negatively associated with longevity. SMR analysis also indicated that elevated levels of this metabolite were associated with both decreased HDL and increased LDL, suggesting a detrimental effect on lipid homeostasis and further a reduction in lifespan (Fig. S11). Previous studies have linked Oleoyl-linoleoyl-glycerol (18:1/18:2) to abdominal aortic aneurysm66, a severe vascular condition characterized by weakening of the aortic wall, which may contribute to its observed negative effect on longevity.

Our study also identified key biological pathways and potential drug targets related to aging through the functional enrichment of identified genes. Notably, significant enrichment in the P53 signaling pathway, which plays a central role in regulating cell cycle arrest, DNA repair, and apoptosis in response to cellular stress67. This pathway is known to be highly activated in segmental progeroid syndromes, which mimic accelerated aging68. Another major pathway identified was MTOR signaling, a well-established regulator of aging that promotes cell growth and protein synthesis while suppressing autophagy. Overactivation of this pathway has been associated with aging-related traits69. Beyond these pathways, GO enrichment analysis also highlighted biological processes related to immune response and lipid metabolism. Metabolomic pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment in the biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, with arachidonate and eicosapentaenoate emerging as key metabolites. Unsaturated fatty acids are critical for maintaining cellular membrane integrity, modulating inflammatory responses, and regulating lipid metabolism70. Arachidonate serves as a precursor for bioactive lipid mediators involved in immune regulation71, while eicosapentaenoate, an omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid, is known for its anti-inflammatory effects72 and cardiovascular protection73, both of which are important contributors to extend healthspan.

We note several limitations of this study. First, the density of current QTL datasets remains limited, which may reduce the power to detect molecular phenotypes with subtle but biologically meaningful effects. Second, the use of stringent multiple-testing corrections, while effective in minimizing false positives, may also limit the detection of molecular phenotypes with modest but relevant associations. In OPERA analysis, the HEIDI test across multiple molecular phenotypes can also be conservative, especially when there is limited overlap between GWAS and QTL variants. Third, xQTL effects can vary depending on tissue, context, and age, and may not fully capture the observed molecular differences related to aging in the studied populations. Therefore, while xQTL integration provides valuable insights, it is not a replacement for direct multi-omics profiling or functional perturbation validation. Fourth, as with any MR framework, unaccounted confounding factors or horizontal pleiotropy may bias the inferred causal relationships. Finally, although our integrative approach provides important insights and candidate targets, experimental validation is essential to confirm their functional roles. Future research incorporating in vitro and in vivo models, as well as single-cell and spatial transcriptomic technologies, will be critical for advancing our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying aging-related traits.

Methods

Ethnics

This study was approved by the Medical Ethical Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University [approval number: 2021 Audit (190)] and conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study utilized only publicly available or previously published GWAS summary statistics and involved no new recruitment or handling of individual-level data. Ethics approval and informed consent were obtained for all original GWAS studies by their respective institutional review boards or ethics committees.

GWAS data used in this study

We used five GWAS summary statistics datasets related to aging-related traits, including IEAA, Hannum, PhenoAge, GrimAge, and multivariate longevity data. All datasets were obtained from the Edinburgh DataShare repository. The Horvath epigenetic clock (i.e., IEAA) estimates biological age based on DNA methylation data from 7844 non-cancer samples across 51 tissues and cell types19, while the Hannum epigenetic clock is trained on whole blood methylation profiles from 482 Caucasian and 174 Hispanic adults18. The PhenoAge integrates 10 clinical characteristics from the NHANES III survey to estimate biological aging20. The GrimAge refines age prediction by incorporating seven DNA methylation-based plasma protein surrogates along with smoking pack-years17. Lastly, the multivariate longevity dataset comprises GWAS summary statistics on healthspan21, parental lifespan22, and extreme longevity23. For replication, we used the mvAge dataset, which includes GWAS summary statistics from five studies that investigate various aspects of aging, including healthspan, parental lifespan, exceptional longevity, EAA, and frailty10. To assess the putative causal risk factors for aging, we used the GWAS summary statistics for HDL and LDL cholesterol levels, derived from 37 studies involving ~100,000 individuals of European ancestry24. We also used the GWAS summary statistics for immune cell counts and IGF1 levels obtained from the UK Biobank (http://www.nealelab.is/uk-biobank) to investigate their potential causal relationship with aging-related traits. Sample overlap exists between the exposure datasets for immune cell counts and IGF-1 and the outcome datasets, with an estimated overlapping proportion of 23%. The details of contributing cohorts and sample sizes are summarized in Data S1.

Molecular QTL summary data used in this study

To identify genes associated with aging-related traits, we performed the SMR analyses by integrating GWAS summary statistics with gene expression QTLs (eQTLs), splicing QTLs (sQTLs), and alternative polyadenylation QTLs (apaQTLs) (Fig. 1). The Blood-derived eQTL data were obtained from the eQTLGen study (n = 31,684)29 and INTERVAL study (n = 4732)35. The sQTL summary data were sourced from the BrainMeta study (n = 2443)31 and the INTERVAL35, while the apaQTL data were derived from whole blood samples in the GTEx project v8 (n = 670)37. All the xQTL effect sizes were standardized. After quality control, we retained 15,785 genes from eQTLGen, 15,265 genes from INTERVAL, 61,603 RNA splicing events from BrainMeta, 21,564 RNA splicing events from INTERVAL, and 1050 alternative polyadenylation events with at least a QTL at a P < 5e-8 for downstream analysis. To further identify the proteins and metabolites associated with aging-related traits, we used the pQTL from the deCODE (n = 35,559)38 and INTERVAL study (n = 3301)39 and the metabolomic QTL dataset (n = 8299) from the CLSA40, respectively. After quality control, we retained 1841 proteins from deCODE, 698 proteins from INTERVAL, and 1108 metabolites with at least a pQTL or metabolite QTL at a P < 5e-8 for downstream analysis.

Genetic correlation analysis using LDSC and High-Definition Likelihood

To assess the shared genetic architecture between aging-related traits and various risk factors, we performed genetic correlation analyses using both the Linkage Disequilibrium Score Regression (LDSC, V1.0.1) (https://github.com/bulik/ldsc)25 and High-Definition Likelihood (V1.4.0) (https://github.com/zhenin/HDL)26. For LDSC, we used LD scores derived from the 1000 Genomes Project reference panel. The GWAS summary statistics were preprocessed to include only high-quality SNPs, filtered by minor allele frequency ≥1% and INFO score ≥0.9, as recommended by the developers. High-Definition Likelihood was employed as a complementary method to obtain higher-resolution estimates of genetic correlation, particularly in polygenic traits. We used the precomputed LD reference panel provided with the High-Definition Likelihood package to ensure consistency in LD modeling26.

Mendelian randomization analysis between risk factors and aging-related traits

We performed MR analysis to investigate the potential causal relationships between aging-related traits and risk factors. We used the GSMR method as it accounts for LD potential horizontal pleiotropy, thus increasing discovery power12. Genetic instruments were clumped using a stringent LD threshold (e.g., P < 5e-8, r² < 0.05 within a 1-Mb window), and outlier variants were excluded using the HEIDI-outlier test. F-statistics were calculated for each instrument, and those with F-values greater than 10 were retained to ensure sufficient instrument strength and avoid weak instrument bias (Data S2). We used the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 European reference panel (European ancestry)74 to estimate LD among SNPs for GSMR analyses.

To validate the robustness of GSMR results, we used 8 additional MR methods: Egger regression33, IVW27, Mode29, MRlap30, PRESSO31, RAPS32, Robust, Simple Median, and Weighted Median28. Concordant effect directions and significance across these methods were used to confirm the reliability of causal inferences and to reduce the likelihood of false-positive findings.

Summary-data-based Mendelian randomization analysis

To identify molecular associations with aging-related traits, we performed the SMR and HEIDI analyses13. These methods integrate GWAS summary statistics for aging-related traits with molecular QTL data to detect pleiotropic associations. We integrated GWAS summary statistics for 5 lifespan-related traits and 13 risk factors with 5 molecular QTL datasets, including eQTL, sQTL, apaQTL, pQTL, and metabolomic QTL. For each molecular QTL dataset, we used the top cis-QTL SNP (i.e., located within 2 Mb of the target molecular phenotype and PQTL < 5 × 10−8) as the genetic instrument variable in the SMR analysis. The significant SMR associations can be caused by linkage, where two variants that one affecting the complex trait and another affecting gene expression level were in LD. We therefore applied the HEIDI test to distinguish pleiotropy (e.g., a causal variant affecting both trait and gene expression level) from the linkage using multiple SNPs in the cis region. Given the large number of tests performed, we applied a stringent Bonferroni correction to control for multiple testing. Only associations passing both the SMR significance threshold and HEIDI test at PHEIDI > 0.01 were retained, ensuring that observed effects are more likely to be due to pleiotropy rather than LD-induced linkage. We used the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 European reference panel (European ancestry)74 to estimate LD among SNPs in SMR analyses.

Joint analysis of multiple molecular QTLs with aging-related traits using OPERA

To further identify the pleiotropic associations between aging-related traits and multiple molecular phenotypes jointly, we performed the Omics Pleiotropic Association (OPERA) analysis. OPERA integrates GWAS summary statistics for aging-related traits with five types of molecular QTL datasets (eQTL, sQTL, apaQTL, pQTL, and mQTL) to jointly assess their associations. The OPERA framework extends the traditional SMR and HEIDI methods by employing a Bayesian framework to model all possible configurations of trait-QTL associations across omics layers. It estimates the global frequency of each pattern and then calculates the PPA for each configuration, allowing for the identification of multi-layered molecular mechanisms underlying complex traits14. We considered associations with PPA > 0.7 as strong candidates. To reduce the risk of false positives due to linkage, we applied a HEIDI test and retained only associations with PHEIDI > 0.01, consistent with evidence for shared causal variants rather than linkage disequilibrium.

Functional and drug target analysis

To explore the biological relevance of molecular phenotypes identified through the SMR analysis, we conducted functional annotation using the FUMA platform (https://fuma.ctglab.nl/)46. Specifically, we performed KEGG pathway analysis and GO enrichment analysis to assess the involvement of these genes in key biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions. In addition, we queried the DrugBank database (https://go.drugbank.com/) to identify known or predicted drug targets among the candidate genes and proteins. This analysis helped determine whether the identified genes are actionable and may inform future therapeutic strategies targeting aging-related mechanisms.
