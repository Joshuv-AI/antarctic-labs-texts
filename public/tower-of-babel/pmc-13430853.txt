Midwives’ perspectives and care practices for pregnant adolescents: a qualitative study in Northern Malawi
Authors: Miyuki Toda, Crystal Patil, Balwani C Mbakaya, Dorkasi L Mwakawanga, Jing Zhou, Yoko Shimpuku
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescent pregnancy remains a significant concern in Malawi, associated with adverse outcomes for both mothers and their infants. Despite the implementation of youth-friendly care programs, there is a paucity of evidence regarding the provision of quality, context-specific care for pregnant adolescents. Midwives, as primary healthcare providers, play a critical role in addressing these challenges. This study aimed to explore midwives’ perspectives and practices concerning adolescent pregnancy care in Malawi. The findings will contribute to the development of contextually grounded, evidence-based interventions to provide quality care for this vulnerable group.MethodsThis study employed a descriptive qualitative design. Six focus group discussions were conducted with 38 midwives, who were purposefully recruited from three hospitals in Northern Malawi, during the period from January to February 2024. Thematic analysis was used to identify key themes related to the midwives’ perspectives and practices.ResultsThree themes emerged from the analysis: (1) perspectives and attitudes related to positive engagement, (2) care practice as holistic care, and (3) envisioning equitable opportunity to receive antenatal care (ANC). Midwives engaged empathetically with adolescents, building trust and providing individualized health education. They collaborated with families and other professionals to support pregnant adolescents. Midwives advocate youth-friendly care training, dedicated antenatal care sites, community engagement, and peer support networks. However, system inadequacies and sociocultural factors continue to be the main challenges to effective maternity care for adolescents.ConclusionThese findings suggest the necessity for a comprehensive, context-specific approach to the care of pregnant adolescents in Malawi, which transcends individual-level support. It is imperative to enhance collaborative care involving families, communities, and multidisciplinary professionals to address the complex needs of pregnant adolescents. Furthermore, it is crucial to strengthen midwifery training in youth-friendly care and to improve the health system infrastructure to facilitate its delivery. These initiatives are vital for improving the quality of care for adolescent pregnancies and for maternal and newborn health outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12978-026-02377-y.Keywords: Adolescent pregnancy, Midwives, Youth-friendly care, Malawi, Antenatal care, Qualitative study

Background

Adolescent pregnancy, defined as pregnancy in girls and young women aged 10–19 years, is a significant global public health concern. The incidence of adolescent pregnancy in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) is 21 million, with 12 million girls giving birth each year, approximately half of whom are unintended [1]. Malawi has one of the highest rates of adolescent pregnancy. According to the Malawi Demographic and Health Survey, the adolescent fertility rate is estimated to be 136 births per 1,000 individuals [2].

Adolescent pregnancies are associated with adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes. Regarding physiological challenges, adolescent pregnancies are at a high risk of anemia, stillbirth, preeclampsia/eclampsia, preterm birth, and low birth weight [3, 4]. The rate of maternal mortality is higher among adolescents than among women aged 20–24 years [5]. Additionally, children born to adolescent mothers have a higher risk of perinatal death than those born to adult mothers [6]. Malawi’s maternal mortality rate was 381 per 100,000 live births, with 16.7% of these cases involving women aged < 19 years [7, 8]. Maternal conditions are the third leading cause of adolescent deaths in Malawi [9]. Unsafe abortions also significantly contribute to the maternal mortality ratio [10]. In addition to these physical risks, adolescent pregnancies face psychosocial challenges such as stigma, social exclusion, social isolation, less social support, and intimate partner violence [11–16]. Additionally, they often experience reduced access to education and a lower socioeconomic status [12, 17]. Depression is a mental health issue among pregnant adolescents and young women in sub-Saharan countries [18]. Thus, pregnant adolescents face diverse context-specific challenges [15, 19].

To achieve SDG Goal 3, it is imperative to not only prevent adolescent pregnancy but also ensure the provision of high-quality evidence-based care for this vulnerable group [19]. However, there remains limited evidence on how to provide quality care that is responsive to their diverse experiences [20]. It has been reported that adolescents who become pregnant are more likely to experience delayed initiation, inadequate access, and substandard provision of antenatal care (ANC) components, despite the well-documented benefits of ANC for the survival and well-being of women and their babies [21–23]. They face multiple barriers to accessing ANC, including individual, social, and health system factors, such as limited education, stigma, financial dependence, long distances to health facilities, and limited availability of adolescent-friendly services [24–26].

In response to these multiple barriers, the concept of youth-friendly health services has been promoted globally to improve adolescents’ access to health services [27]. These services aim to address adolescents’ unique needs, including maintaining privacy and confidentiality [27]. In Malawi, the Youth-Friendly Health Services (YFHS) Program was introduced in 2007 as part of the national response to the challenges and needs of this age group, especially related to sexual and reproductive health [28]. Although the program has expanded over 19 years, ongoing evaluations have led to revisions to the strategy, with the 2022–2030 framework prioritizing key areas, including a competent health workforce, service provision, leadership and governance, community engagement, and financing [28]. However, despite the well-recognized vulnerability of pregnant adolescents, youth-friendly health service strategies have paid limited attention to the care of pregnant adolescents [28].

In Malawi, midwives are the primary healthcare providers of antenatal care [29]. Midwives play a critical role in providing preventive and supportive care that strengthens women’s capabilities through respectful relationships, is tailored to their needs, promotes normal reproductive processes, and provides first-line management of complications [30]. Previous studies have reported mixed ANC experiences among adolescents, with some describing respectful and supportive interactions with midwives, while others have reported discrimination, disrespectful attitudes, and feelings of exclusion [31–33]. At the same time, midwives and other healthcare workers face challenges such as inflexible guidelines, a need for further development skills, and insufficient time to deliver adolescent-responsive care [34–36]. However, to the best of our knowledge, there is a paucity of research exploring how midwives perceive and provide care to pregnant adolescents in Malawi.

Understanding midwives’ perceptions and practices is essential for identifying adolescent-responsive care and improving the quality and accessibility of ANC. Given the vulnerability and complex circumstances of pregnant adolescents, qualitative approaches are valuable for gaining in-depth insights into the realities of care delivery. Therefore, this study aimed to explore midwives’ perceptions and practices in providing antenatal care for pregnant adolescent women in Malawi and to identify opportunities to improve the quality and accessibility of care.

Methods

Study design

This study adopted a qualitative descriptive design that is suitable when a straightforward description of phenomena is desired [37]. To align with this approach, focus group discussions were conducted. This research method is effective in examining participants’ knowledge and experiences, as well as how and why they think in certain ways, which would be less accessible through individual interviews [38]. This approach facilitated a direct description of midwives’ perceptions and practices in providing care for pregnant adolescents through active group interaction.

Setting

This study was conducted at one tertiary referral hospital and two district hospitals in Northern Malawi. To maintain confidentiality, these sites were designated as Facilities A, B, and C. The catchment populations for facilities A, B, and C are approximately 221,272, 187,664, and 1,000,000, respectively. The tertiary referral hospital (Facility A) serves as a referral facility for the surrounding northern districts. The two district hospitals (Facilities B and C) are located in the urban areas of the northern region and provide support to rural hospitals and health centers. All the research facilities were public hospitals that provided care free of charge. Antenatal care for pregnant adolescents is integrated into routine care and is not provided on a designated day at any of the facilities. These facilities frequently encounter challenges, such as staff shortages, limited youth-friendly services, and resource constraints, which affect the quality and accessibility of maternal care for adolescents. Cultural stigma surrounding teenage pregnancy also significantly influences young mothers and midwives’ attitudes.

The northern region of Malawi was selected because of its distinct cultural, socioeconomic, and health system characteristics that affect the perception and management of adolescent pregnancies within health facilities. This region, which includes the third-largest city, is bordered by Zambia and Tanzania. It is home to various tribes, with Tumbuka being the most prominent. The predominant religion is Roman Catholicism, and 11.2% of children aged 0–17 years are orphans [39]. The primary industry in Northern Malawi is agriculture, and some adults work in South Africa, resulting in children living independently or with their guardians. Adolescent pregnancy and motherhood rates remain high, with the northern region exhibiting the highest prevalence at 32.1%, compared to 25.4% in the central region and 31.6% in the Southern region [2]. These rates are often linked to early marriage, poverty, limited access to sexual and reproductive health education, and the prevailing gender norms.

Study participants and recruitment

Recruitment was conducted between January 2024 and February 2024. We recruited midwives using purposive sampling based on the following factors: (1) working in the hospital for at least one year, (2) communicating in English and/or Tumbuka, and (3) direct experience in providing maternal care to pregnant adolescents. First, the researchers contacted the managers of each hospital’s maternity care units, who introduced them to midwives who met the inclusion criteria. Subsequently, each midwife who met the eligibility criteria was contacted by the researchers, informed of the study’s objectives and procedures, and invited to participate in a focus group discussion. A total of 38 midwives participated in six focus group discussions, with each group comprising 5 to 8 participants. Although no participants declined to participate in this study, several measures ensured voluntary participation. Participants were assured that there would be no repercussion if they chose not to engage in the focus group discussion. Additionally, the focus group was organized with consideration of the hierarchical structure based on job positions to mitigate hierarchical influence and facilitate comfortable discussions.

Data collection procedure

Two research assistants (RAs), lecturers in nursing and midwifery in Malawi, with experience in qualitative research, were recruited and trained to collect data. The training involved issues related to informed consent, building rapport and trust with the participants, study objectives, and the research process. These two RAs facilitated focus group discussions (FGDs); one served as the moderator, and the other as the note-taker. The participants provided written informed consent after the RA explained the study objective and used a voice recorder. The second author, BCM, a faculty member in nursing education and a researcher in Malawi, supervised data collection and attended all FGDs. Additionally, MT, a Ph.D. student with experience in qualitative research in Japan during the study period, participated remotely in FGDs via online platforms to maintain direct involvement in qualitative data collection, thereby enabling the capture of contextual nuances. Additionally, the first author had volunteer experience as a community health nurse in Northern Malawi. This experience furnished her with contextual knowledge regarding healthcare conditions and the sociocultural realities of community life in the study area. Based on the participants’ preferences, FGDs were conducted in a mix of Tumbuka and English. The official language of Malawi is English, and all participants could speak Tumbuka, the native language of the study area. The FGD guide was formulated through a comprehensive literature review [15, 21, 32, 36, 40] and consultations with researchers, including midwives BCM, DLM, YS, and anthropologist CP, all of whom had research experience in sub-Saharan African countries. The FGD guide was initially developed in English and subsequently translated into Tumbuka by two RAs and BCM, all of whom were fluent in both languages. Subsequently, a back-translation from Tumbuka to English was conducted. During the RAs’ training, the FGD guide was adapted and used for practice to ensure that the phrasing was culturally sensitive and easily understood by participants. However, no pretesting of the guide was conducted prior to the commencement of data collection. The questions focused on midwives’ perspectives and practices in providing ANC to adolescents, including the challenges and strategies to improve the quality of care. The FGD guide is accessible in Additional File 1. The number of FGDs was determined based on saturation. Data collection was conducted until no additional information was obtained from the participants. This process was monitored through team meetings following each FGD, indicating that data saturation had been achieved [41]. All data were stored in password-protected folders, accessible exclusively to co-researchers.

Focus group discussions

We conducted six FGDs involving 38 midwives. According to Krueger and Casey [42], conducting FGDs with up to 5–8 participants when addressing topics that may be perceived as sensitive or personal, or when the participants possess substantial expertise or experience with the subject matter. Consequently, we included to 5–8 participants in each discussion. The FGDs had homogeneous participants and nurse-midwives from the same hospital who shared the experience of providing care to pregnant adolescent women. Focus group discussions were organized into two distinct categories: Nurse-Midwife Technicians and Nurse-Midwife Officers, and Registered Nurse Midwives. FGDs were conducted in a quiet room with minimal interruptions at each hospital. To ensure confidentiality and anonymity, participants were referred to using numbers during the discussion. The duration of the FGDs ranged from 60 to 90 min. During the FGDs, refreshments, including snacks and juice, were provided to create a comfortable environment for participants to engage in dialogue. Upon the conclusion of the FGDs, each study participant received an equivalent of US$10 for time compensation.

Data analysis

All audio recordings were transcribed verbatim in Tunbuka and then translated into English by the two RAs who conducted the FGDs. BCM checked the adequacy and accuracy of the translation. Data were analyzed using thematic analysis, as outlined by Braun and Clarke (2006) [43]. The researchers engaged in repeated readings of all transcripts and familiarized themselves with the data before coding. A preliminary codebook was developed, which included a list of codes focusing on the perceptions and practices of providing maternity care to pregnant adolescent mothers. These codes were formulated and defined by MT and JZ based on the study objectives. After coding, new emergent codes were added to the codebook. The potential list of codes was examined based on their similarities and differences, and the codes were grouped into sub-themes and themes. Subsequently, the researchers evaluated the themes for their appropriateness by considering whether they should be decomposed or consolidated. The document was shared with the co-author, and discussions were held until a consensus was reached. This process involved refining and defining each theme. Finally, the results were presented through concise quotes that encapsulated participants’ narratives.

Trustworthiness

To ensure the trustworthiness of our study, we adhered to the four criteria : credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability [44, 45]. The credibility of the study was enhanced through triangulation, which involved three research settings and the inclusion of participants from various professional hierarchies. Before commencing the FGDs, we assured the participants that there were no incorrect responses, thereby fostering an environment conducive to open dialogue. Additionally, we included direct quotations from the transcripts to ensure that the findings accurately reflected the participants’ words. Dependability was supported by documenting the research design and data collection procedures. The use of the FGD guide, moderator training, and verbatim transcription followed by cross-checking by independent team members, along with maintaining an audit trail of the analytical process, contributed to the rigor and consistency of data collection and analysis. Confirmability was addressed through the triangulation of data sources and researchers. The data were analyzed inductively by a multidisciplinary team that included midwives and an anthropologist with expertise in African maternal health. We also documented reflexive insights throughout the study. Data were analyzed inductively by a multidisciplinary team that included midwives and an anthropologist with expertise in African maternal health. The transferability of the study was supported by a detailed description of the cultural contexts of the research settings and the healthcare circumstances.

Results

The study participants consisted of 38 midwives (26 females and 12 males. The median age of the participants was 32.8 years, with an age range, 25–51 years). The obstetric midwives were in practice for 1–23 years with a median duration of experience of 7.4 years. The participation rate in the youth-friendly service training was 57.9%.

The thematic analysis generated three key themes that closely aligned with our study’s objectives of understanding midwives’ perspectives and practices. These include the following: (1) perspectives and attitudes related to positive engagement, (2) care practice as holistic care, and (3) envisioning equitable opportunities to receive ANC (see Table 1 for details). Sub-themes and themes of perspectives and practices of midwives caring for pregnant adolescents

Theme 1: Perspectives and attitudes related to positive engagement

This theme highlights the role of midwives in providing positive antenatal care to pregnant adolescents. This theme captures the “inside the head” of care for midwives.

a) Being accepting and empathetic

a) Being accepting and empathetic

Midwives recognized that pregnant adolescents required respect. They also observed that pregnant adolescents exhibited feelings of unworthiness and a lack of confidence. One of the midwives mentions that:

“They may feel that they are not worthy, they may not have confidence in themselves, so that affects even the care of the baby, including attendance at the antenatal clinic is erratic; she would come this month and next month, do not report, yeah (Registered nurse-midwife, Male, Facility A, with 20 years of experience in obstetrics).”

Midwives attributed these feelings to blame and shame imposed on pregnant adolescent women by their families, partners, communities, teachers, and peers.

“Adolescent who is pregnant is pushed too far and they have nothing to do, nowhere to go, so they decide to just hung themselves and even her classmates laugh at her, considered as a child who is in disciplined, and she becomes a reject in the community (Registered nurse-midwife, Male, Facility A, with 20 years of experience in obstetrics)”.

In response, midwives endeavored to accept and avoid judgments, approach their involvement with humility, and use empathetic communication. They engaged with adolescents in a manner consistent with their interactions with women of other age groups. Furthermore, midwives were careful to avoid causing unnecessary fear by using a language that the adolescents could understand and speak in a soft voice. The midwife explained this as follows:

“Instead of playing the role of a judge, we must be compassionate with them, looking at some traumatizing condition they are going through at times. They are already going through a lot; they are traumatized. If we judge them, we are just adding to the trauma. Instead of judging them, we should be supportive and love them to show the pregnant adolescent that someone still believes in them (Registered nurse-midwife, Female, Facility C, with 6 years of experience in obstetrics).”

They also prioritized the privacy and confidentiality of pregnant women. Some midwives indicated that they would refrain from returning to the hospital if their confidential information were not protected. These expressions of empathy and non-judgmental engagement by midwives not only foster a trusting relationship but are essential steps towards improving antenatal attendance and health outcomes among adolescents.

Conversely, some midwives reported negative attitudes towards pregnant adolescents, including maternal attitudes, resorting to verbal abuse, and refusing medical assessment of adolescents. The midwives explained the following:

“Midwives tend to become the parent to this adolescent as if the adolescent is their child (all laughs); they start shouting at the adolescents, complaining that the adolescent has wasted fees, you have the certificate now, your child, what forced you to do (Nurse-Midwife Technician, Female, Facility A with 17 years of experience in obstetrics).”

"Imagine you express yourself to this adolescent like, ‘So, your mother was just seeing you behaving in this way, I cannot tolerate this to my child!’ This scares the adolescent (Nurse-Midwife Technician, Female, Facility B with 9 years of experience in obstetrics)."

b) Building rapport and trust

b) Building rapport and trust

Midwives observed that girls exhibited fear towards them, which impeded effective communication. One midwife mentioned:

“These adolescents have planted in their minds that, most times, nurses will not politely help them. In this vein, every time you talk to them, their understanding is hindered by what is within their minds, hence a communication breakdown. The spirit of fear is always being triggered in their minds (Nurse-Midwife Technician, Female, Facility B with 9 years of experience in obstetrics).”

To address these issues, midwives focused on adolescents, demonstrating open-mindedness, cheerfulness, politeness, tenderness, and warm and welcoming demeanors. Through these actions, midwives believed that adolescents would feel comfortable responding honestly to their inquiries and seeking consultation when faced with challenges. Additionally, they took pride in the fact that the adolescents had opened their minds. The Midwives reported that:

"When they are getting into the examination room, the welcome should be good and would make them attracted to you. Any problem that she would be facing, the adolescent will be able to reach out to you (Nurse-Midwife Technician, Female, Facility A with 10 years of experience in obstetrics)."

"We are also proud when the adolescent has built trust with the midwife, such that she is able to open up and ask all questions honestly, correctly (Nurse-midwife officer, Female, Facility A with 3 years of experience in obstetrics)."

Furthermore, some midwives noted that their experiences of caring for pregnant adolescents underscored the importance of maintaining a positive attitude towards all mothers, regardless of age, rather than focusing solely on pregnancy outcomes. The midwife mentioned the following:

We have been helped much with these teen mothers because we were blind, we do just see the outcomes but from them we learn much and have a good attitude to everyone (Nurse-Midwife Technician, Male, Facility B with 6 years of experience in obstetrics).

c) Instilling hope

c) Instilling hope

Midwives observed that some adolescents exhibited a lack of acceptance of their pregnancies, which impeded access to care. Therefore, repeated counseling sessions were conducted until the adolescents accepted the reality of their pregnancies and children. The midwife stated,

"I think some also have challenges in accepting the baby; they feel like the baby is someone who has brought problems into their life. The baby is the root cause of all problems; to take care of the baby, it becomes difficult, so it takes a lot of counseling for them to accept (Registered nurse-midwife, Female, Facility A with 13 years of experience in obstetrics)."

The midwife anticipated that self-worth and self-esteem would be enhanced through care. Consequently, by employing words of encouragement and hope, they informed adolescents that they would have the opportunity to return to school after giving birth. The midwife mentioned the following:

"I told her that pregnancy should not destroy your future, keep the pregnancy, and you should be coming to the hospital for review. What we want is for you to be healthy and have a healthy baby. Once you deliver, everything ends there, then you can start school (Nurse-Midwife Technician, Female, Facility A with 17 years of experience in obstetrics)."

The midwives were proud when they decided to return to school. The midwife mentioned the following:

"She assured me that she will go back to school and continue with her studies, and I was excited and proud that she made her decision of going back to school and that she will achieve something in life, that pregnancy could not stop her, she can still continue and become someone responsible in her life (Nurse-Midwife Technician, Female, Facility C with 2 years of experience in obstetrics )."

Theme 2: Care practice as holistic care

This theme emphasizes the provision of comprehensive care that addresses the unique needs of pregnant adolescents by incorporating the support from family members and other professionals.

a) Tailored health promotion or education

a) Tailored health promotion or education

Midwives believed that they needed to understand that adolescents’ minds and thoughts were different from those of other age groups. Additionally, the midwife reported that the adolescent had acquired information about her pregnancy from an unspecified source, and was apprehensive because of her lack of knowledge. Based on the foregoing, health education and promotion encompassed the risks associated with abortion and pregnancy management, including potential complications, nutritional advice, and family planning. Health education and promotion were provided to the adolescents either individually or in groups. Some midwives educated maternal knowledge as if adolescents already knew, while others recognized the necessity of repeated instruction to facilitate comprehension, continuing until understanding was achieved. The midwife noted that:

"They need time and issues that you teach them it may seem as if they don’t understand today, but come again tomorrow and teach them you find that they grasp the concepts and then they understand, so I have learned that when you are talking to them you have to keep your time, be patient with them if you have insufficient time the adolescent may not benefit from the service, not two minutes discussing with her then you are done, you need time, time, time(Registered nurse-midwife, Male, Facility A with 12 years of experience in obstetrics)."

By contrast, some midwives expressed that educating girls was boring because they struggled to comprehend the information they conveyed. It was also noted that the opportunity to provide education was constrained if delay-initiated access to ANC occurred, adolescents’ financial constraints hindered the implementation of guidance at home, and there was uncertainty regarding the extent to which teaching was applied at home. The midwives reported the following:

"We need time to share some kind of information, so sometimes we become bored, the adolescent has problems understanding or mastering, yes, sometimes we talk to them, but they do not focus, we just know that what I am communicating, they are not even listening (Registered nurse-midwife, Female, Facility B with 6 years of experience in obstetrics)."

"You find that even when you counsel or talk to an adolescent, she just looks at you and cannot respond anything, they are financially challenges (Nurse-Midwife Technician, Female, Facility A with 17 years of experience in obstetrics)."

b) Building social networks to guarantee access to support

b) Building social networks to guarantee access to support

Midwives observed that some unmarried pregnant adolescents were chased away from their homes and subsequently resided with their relatives. The midwives suggested that the parents of these adolescents experienced feelings of shame or fear and were concerned that the community and church members might perceive them as failing in their parental duties. In another case, the family gave the adolescent two options: either to marry a pregnant man or terminate the pregnancy. In Abortion is legally prohibited in Malawi. Consequently, some adolescents resorted to unsafe abortion practices, which could result in fatal outcomes. The midwife reported the following:

"The girls’ grandmother gave her two options: either to get married to the man in question or else terminate the pregnancy. These girls face such situations, and they are in a dilemma, as they have no say in anything that their parents decide for them. Most families, when such incidents happen, they are the ones who contribute to these adolescents having unsafe abortions (Nurse-Midwife Technician, Female, Facility B with 9 years of experience in obstetrics)."

Midwives actively encourage adolescents to bring their families or guardians to the hospital. In doing so, midwives observed the reactions of these family members, which varied according to their social and cultural backgrounds, financial resources, and educational level. In most cases, when midwives met their parents, a predominant attitude towards condemning adolescents was noted. When blame was directed towards adolescents, midwives pleaded for acceptance and empathy, rather than condemnation. In addition, midwives counseled parents when they looked depressed during their daughters’ pregnancies. The midwife mentioned the following:

"Their guardian does start to shout at them, for example, I thought you did those things for your own. So why are you complaining? And yet we were advising you, but you did not listen; that’s why you are pregnant now. We try to talk to the guardian, not to address the adolescent who is pregnant like that (Nurse-Midwife Technician, Female, Facility C with 4 years of experience in obstetrics)."

They emphasized the importance of parental involvement in understanding midwives’ guidance, urging parents to listen to explanations, and assisting in reinforcing and implementing advice provided during antenatal care at home. Midwives expressed concern that non-compliance with these practices could adversely affect outcomes such as the nutritional status of both the adolescent and her child. The midwife reported that:

"I am educating her because I know that physically she is here and mentally she is somewhere else, so we rely much on the parents that they will get information on behalf of their daughter so that they should be able to keep on reminding the adolescent what to do when they are at home or to guide her at home (Registered nurse-midwife, Male, Facility B, 19 years of experience in obstetrics)."

Regarding adolescent partners, some male partners refused to accept that they had impregnated or denied responsibility. Additionally, midwives observed that some adolescents experienced unintended pregnancies due to socioeconomic deprivation, which suggesting the involvement of an unidentified partner. Midwives were apprehensive that the absence of a partner during antenatal checkups hindered testing for sexually transmitted infections, including syphilis and HIV, thereby increasing the risk of mother-to-child transmission. The midwife stated,

"At the antenatal clinic, the other challenge is that they have to report for antenatal care with the spouse, so that we have to know the HIV status, syphilis, and all these tests have to be done for the couple. But then in most cases the adolescents do not report with their spouses; however, they do have sexual encounters with their partners and yet they do not come to the antenatal clinic with them so for the baby and the mother we are not giving them adequate care that is needed (Registered nurse-midwife, Female, Facility A with 13 years of experience in obstetrics)."

To provide comprehensive support, midwives collaborated with various professionals depending on the circumstances of pregnant adolescents.

"We have a one-stop center, so if we have noted that the adolescent would not be welcomed in her community or would be shouted at, we do send them to a one-stop center. It is our responsibility as midwives to send them to a one-stop center so that they are counseled and given advice until this adolescent delivers properly (Nurse-Midwife Technician, Female, Facility with 23 years of experience in obstetrics)."

However, some adolescents and their parents occasionally misrepresent their age because of concerns regarding the treatment they might receive owing to pregnancy occurring in a minor. Midwives expressed concerns that falsifying age hindered their ability to connect adolescents with necessary services. The midwives reported the following:

“You ask the adolescent how old are you she responds “am’16”, but the guardians would report otherwise, that she is 20 years of age, then as a midwife, you change the whole situation and approach, we many not support the adolescent fully from the One stop center such as to advocate that she continues with her studies, family planning etc., then we withdraw, care changes obviously (Nurse-Midwife Technician, Male, Facility C with 4 years of experience in obstetrics)”.

Theme 3: Envisioning equitable opportunity to receive ANC

This theme highlights midwives’ concerns regarding the adequacy of the current care environment for adolescents, leading to recommendations for improvement.

a) Special site or facility for adolescents

a) Special site or facility for adolescents

Several challenges were identified, including long waiting times, inadequate medical equipment, shortage of medical personnel, and limited time allocated for counseling. Midwives observed that adolescents expressed concern about encountering acquaintances while awaiting medical consultations and felt discomfort in the presence of pregnant women from different age groups. Consequently, midwives recommended that antenatal care for pregnant adolescents be conducted separately from that for other age groups and that pregnant adolescents should be prioritized for health checkups. The midwife mentioned the following:

"They get bored and in addition, this is the facility where so many people are coming so it means she may interact with people that she didn’t want to know that she is pregnant, so, this may affect them, because of the behavior of some of the neighbors, the compound or community would know about this pregnancy… (Nurse-Midwife Technician, Female, Facility A with 17 year of experience in obstetrics)."

By contrast, some midwives provided priority care to pregnant adolescents so that their education could not be interrupted.

Furthermore, midwives expressed concerns regarding the insufficient number of midwives and limited time available for counseling adolescents. The midwife mentioned the following:

We can support them as nurses/midwives, but we are limited because we have so many things to do for an antenatal client, such that counseling may not be adequate, the adolescent could be helped (Registered nurse-midwife, Male, Facility A with 12 years of experience in obstetrics).

b) Expansion of Youth-friendly training and care

b) Expansion of Youth-friendly training and care

Based on midwives’ experiences of systemic and social challenges, they recommend solutions, such as youth-friendly training.

"I also think that youth-friendly health services should reach every youth and also all the providers that deal with youths… even in our syllabus we do not have them like taught to us in details (Nurse-Midwife Technician, Male, Facility C with 4 years of experience in obstetrics)."

Moreover, they were eager to collaborate with community midwives and health surveillance assistants to expand their care beyond antenatal check-ups. They assumed that this collaboration aimed to enable practice within the community, thereby aligning care with an individual’s contextual life circumstances.

"So, conducting a home [visit], as a midwife, you may find that the adolescent is living in a totally different environment, with a lack of food, a poor environment, surrounded by people who may not be caring. They may be getting relief from the midwives more, especially if the midwife is addressing this adolescent in a calm manner; when the adolescent is at home, everything is totally different; we really need to have such services, thus home visiting (Registered nurse-midwife, Male, Facility C with 12 years of experience in obstetrics)."

In addition, they sought to engage with educators at schools attended by pregnant adolescents to encourage them to continue their education.

c) Community engagement

c) Community engagement

Midwives have suggested engaging with the community by offering educational initiatives, including outreach to church members, to promote the understanding and support of pregnant adolescents. They also wanted to encourage these adolescents to attend antenatal clinic and establish peer groups to share their experiences. The midwife aspired to achieve these objectives.

"We need to advocate and conduct civic education and awareness, which can be broadened to other communities, including churches. Most congregants would get the message better as churches command a greater gathering, and they would understand it better because the message had been communicated to them by the church leaders (Registered nurse-midwife, Male, Facility C with 5 years of experience in obstetrics)."

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is one of the few to show how Malawian midwives provide antenatal care to pregnant adolescent women, reflecting the country’s unique social, legal, and systemic conditions. The thematic analysis revealed three major themes: (1) perspectives and attitudes related to positive engagement, (2) care practice as holistic care, and (3) envisioning equitable opportunities to receive ANC. Below, we discuss how each theme relates to prior research and its implications for adolescent health and midwifery.

Our findings show that midwives’ engagement with adolescents, characterized by acceptability and empathy, fosters trust and rapport. This finding is consistent with that of previous studies. Adolescents develop relationships with healthcare workers, considering their role models or friends, because of their positive behavior and attitude, including active listening without judgment, offering targeted advice, and providing support [35, 46]. In many countries, including Malawi, premarital pregnancy is considered taboo, leading to stigma and social exclusion [12, 15, 47–49]. As in this study, earlier research found that midwives recognized adolescents who experienced these pains and acknowledged the need for adolescents to talk to someone who would not judge them [50]. Interactions with healthcare providers who were kind, gentle, and friendly had a positive impact on adolescents’ experiences [47]. On the other hand, the negative attitudes and behaviors of healthcare workers influence adolescent antenatal clinic satisfaction with care and care seeking [33, 51–53]. Additionally, the attitudes, beliefs, and practices of service providers contribute to the distress and marginalization experienced by adolescents [52]. To accept and empathize with adolescents, midwives may need to reflect on their gender and age-related stigma and consider the reasons and circumstances of adolescent pregnancy without judgment. Through a trusting relationship, midwives observed that pregnant adolescents were empowered to discuss their concerns with them and seek assistance voluntarily. A previous study noted that the ability to obtain information from adolescents is an essential skill in antenatal care services [54]. The relationship between midwives and adolescents is crucial for assessing vulnerable mothers and providing effective individualized support [55].

By building trust and rapport, midwives instilled hope in the adolescents. Although Malawian adolescents are legally permitted to return to school after childbirth, their rate of return remains low [56]. Pregnancy was the second main reason for dropping out of school [57]. In Malawi, higher education is linked to better career prospects than no education or only primary education [58]. Similar to midwives’ feelings towards adolescent pregnant women in this study, previous studies have shown that adolescents felt that pregnancy or becoming a mother shattered their dreams [59]. In contrast, as a coping strategy for adolescent pregnancy, some adolescents share their dreams and aspirations for the future [60]. In response to this, having a midwife with whom they have established a trusting relationship, who reassures them that “life does not end after having a child,” and who collaborates with them in contemplating their future might encourage pregnant adolescents. Similarly, previous studies have shown that midwives encourage pregnant adolescents and help cement their aspirations [32]. However, returning to school after childbirth is challenging unless family members care for the child and provide support to the adolescents [52, 61]. Additionally, after re-enrolling in school, adolescents face inadequate support from teachers, the community, and fellow pupils, which disempowers adolescent mothers [11]. Therefore, midwives may need to collaborate not only with pregnant adolescents but also with their families, partners, communities, and school institutions to shape adolescents’ future trajectories.

The second significant aspect of this study was the provision of health promotion and education regarding pregnancy to pregnant adolescents through mixed individual and group sessions. Some midwives reiterated their instructions until they understood them. In a previous study, adolescents reported a knowledge deficit in matters related to health promotion during pregnancy [62]. Moreover, adolescents mentioned that it was challenging to retain the education they received from healthcare providers during ANC [32]. Furthermore, adolescents were concerned about the reaction of an older pregnant woman when they asked questions during group education [31, 32]. Thus, the current midwives’ approach, which tailored health promotion to the context, might have enhanced adolescents’ health and alleviated their anxiety regarding previously unfamiliar maternal topics. Conversely, midwives expressed dissatisfaction with girls’ attitudes during instruction, a sentiment echoed in previous studies [50]. As stated in this study, rather than solely attributing blame to girls to their attitudes, it may be imperative to consider the social, economic, and educational contexts that influence these attitudes and tailor instructions accordingly.

Regarding family care, midwives pleaded with the adolescents’ families to accept and empathize with their daughters and supported them in adhering to the guidance provided at the ANC. The midwives also encouraged adolescent parents when they were disappointed. This finding aligns with previous studies suggesting that healthcare workers believe that pregnant adolescents should receive empathy and support from their parents [40, 50]. However, similar to the present study, previous research has shown that family reactions vary; most cases result in anger towards pregnancy [12, 50, 63]. This exacerbates the strain on adolescent-family relationships during pregnancy [12, 60]. In addition, these strained relationships stress adolescents, cause them to lose their appetite, and attempt self-harm and suicide [12, 40]. Additionally, in Malawi, in the case of a premarital pregnancy, adolescents received pressure from their families to marry, which would save the social status of the young woman and her family [48, 64]. Some forced marriages influenced mental health [64]. However, if adolescents have good relationships with their families, partners, and communities, they receive adequate support, which has a positive influence on their mental health [64]. Therefore, midwives may need to help rebuild the relationships between adolescents and their families. This is important not only because adolescents receive financial support from their families but also because they can receive emotional support.

During family care, midwives faced challenges due to their inability to assess the HIV status of their adolescent partners, as these partners did not accompany the adolescents to ANC. Malawi has an HIV prevalence of 6.8% among adults (age 15 to 49 years) and 5.2% among pregnant women in 2023 [65]. Malawi’s national elimination of Mother-to-Child Transmission (eMTCT) programs, with opt-out HIV testing for pregnant women, have been implemented since 2011 [65]. Despite reduced pediatric HIV infections after eMTCT implementation, 25,000 new infections occurred in 2022 [65]. To eliminate mother-to-child HIV transmission, it is essential to collaborate with pregnant partners [65]. A previous study in Malawi indicated that some newly diagnosed pregnant women with HIV who accessed eMTCT services refrained from telling their husbands about their HIV test results for fear that the stigma associated with HIV would collapse the relationship [66]. Earlier research in South Africa demonstrated that adolescent partners also have positive and negative feelings about pregnancy [67]. In this regard, midwives may need to accept and empathize with their partners’ feelings and provide care when their partners attend ANC. In addition, one suggested approach to address the absent partner issue is through media and radio campaigns aimed at effectively informing men about mother-to-child transmission of HIV and promoting antenatal uptake.

Additionally, midwives engaged in interdisciplinary collaboration, contingent on the specific circumstances of the adolescents involved. Consistent with this, several studies have highlighted the importance of providing multidisciplinary support to pregnant adolescents [50, 58]. Despite the availability of collaborative partners, midwives encounter challenges in situations where family members or adolescents are unable to access the necessary care because of the misrepresentation of age. The family members may harbor concerns that the midwife could report the incident to law enforcement authorities, as Malawian legal statutes prohibit sexual intercourse with girls under the age of 16 [68]. Similar findings have been reported in other studies where parents and relatives frequently refrained from disclosing instances of sexual abuse, particularly in cases involving familial perpetrators, owing to the potential arrest of relatives and the associated stigma, shame, and discrimination that may ensue if such abuse is publicized [69, 70]. Additionally, reluctance to report is noted when the perpetrator serves as the family’s primary financial provider, as their arrest would result in economic hardship, a finding consistent with that of this study [70]. Thus, midwives need to be aware of these socio-structural issues and recognize that while adolescents may wish to reveal the truth, there are cases where they are constrained from doing so.

These experiences inspired midwives to establish opportunities for equitable access to antenatal care for adolescents. Midwives expressed the need for a dedicated antenatal care site, specifically for adolescents. As highlighted in the present study, healthcare workers considered that adolescents might not desire to attend ANC because of the fear of being seen by other people [71]. Additionally, previous research has demonstrated that pregnant adolescents reported inflexible opening hours, lack of specific spaces, inadequate privacy and confidentiality, and inadequate material resources in ANC [31, 51, 72]. These issues contribute to the inadequate access to antenatal care among adolescents. Thus, it may be crucial to consider how pregnant adolescents perceive the circumstances of ANC and innovative ANC environments to meet their unique needs of pregnant adolescents. Additionally, as indicated by the findings of this study, in Malawi, the shortage of midwives negatively affects the maternal care provided to mothers [73–75]. In the present study, midwives wished to spend sufficient time with each pregnant adolescent but felt a dilemma because they were unable to do so due to the lack of midwives. It may be necessary to consider human resource management in order to provide quality care to pregnant adolescents.

Subsequently, midwives demanded enhanced training and expanded care for youth-friendly healthcare services. Similar results have been reported previously [35, 50]. A previous study reported that midwives’ self-efficacy is associated with their intention to provide family planning and maternal and child health services to adolescents [76]. Therefore, gaining confidence through training may be critical. A report from the Malawi Ministry of Health highlighted that regarding the manual on youth-friendly care currently in use in Malawi, the section on service provider attitudes is not sufficiently emphasized [28]. There have been some reports of midwives’ negative behaviors and attitudes in the current study. To address these issues, it may be necessary to update the training manual to include content on enhanced care providers’ positive attitudes and to consider new or alternative training delivery methods from which everyone can access and continue to learn.

In addition, midwives desired to collaborate with community midwives and health-surveillance assistants. To verify whether the teachings imparted at the hospital were implemented at home. Previous studies have documented that adolescents in Malawi feel connected to community nurses and health surveillance assistants through regular visits to their communities [77]. Considering the reported shortage of personnel and inadequate time for each counseling session, collaboration between these two groups would enable sustained support, even in the context of limited resources.

Moreover, the establishment of a sustainable support system for adolescents is suggested to engage the community. As previously mentioned, in societies where cultural norms prioritize family and community connections, deviations from these norms, such as unmarried pregnancies, can lead to social isolation [48]. This study highlights the significance of fostering an understanding among pregnant women’s church leaders. Engaging in dialogue with the community through these leaders may be essential and the community should collaborate to establish a sustainable support system for pregnant women. Midwives also advocated the formation of peer networks, believing that such networks could facilitate the sharing of challenges and support. Prior studies have demonstrated that pregnant adolescents acquire peer support, which leads to emotional support [60]. In a study focusing on pregnant and postpartum adolescent girls and young women living with HIV in Malawi, adolescent participants mentioned wanting to receive psychosocial support from their peers [78]. However, they also expressed concerns about maintaining confidentiality [78]. Therefore, it may be essential to be aware of this point when considering the establishment of peer support.

Through this study, we identified that trust building, provision of holistic care, engagement with adolescents’ families, and addressing systemic barriers are particularly crucial for adolescents. These elements reflect the core values of care and highlight the challenges associated with delivering high-quality care to all women in Malawi. While care for adolescents necessitates increased awareness of circumstances unique to this developmental stage, such as exposure to stigma, heightened restrictions on autonomy, financial constraints, and school dropout, integrating respect and dignity into the care of all women is essential. These principles of respect and dignity should guide the ethical and effective practice of care irrespective of the specific population being served.

Implications for practice

To improve adolescent pregnancy care in Malawi, we recommend investing in youth-friendly care training for all health care providers, adopting a multidisciplinary approach, and engaging families and communities. Additionally, we suggest addressing infrastructure to meet the unique needs of adolescents and reduce accessibility barriers to antenatal care.

Strengths and limitations

Through qualitative interviews, this study elucidated midwives’ perspectives and practices regarding adolescent pregnancy care. The interviews were conducted by researchers from the local nurse-midwifery training faculty who possessed a profound understanding of the local context and were actively involved in the study’s analysis. This approach ensured that the results accurately reflected local realities. While previous studies predominantly focused on youth-friendly services at the policy level, this study is distinct in its focus on midwives’ practices, thereby enabling an examination of how to provide youth-friendly care specifically tailored to pregnant adolescents. However, this study had some limitations. First, the participants voluntarily participated in the interviews. They might have been more motivated or interested in providing youth-friendly care. This sampling bias may indicate that the results do not fully represent the broader views and practices of the midwifery community. Consequently, future research should incorporate adolescents into the research design process to ensure relevance and conduct interviews with pregnant adolescent women and their families. Additionally, utilizing scaled assessment tools, such as women-centered care frameworks, to evaluate gestational care from the perspective of adolescents may be beneficial. Second, this study relied solely on focus group interviews and did not involve individual interviews or direct observations of care practices. The interviewee was a local midwife. As a result, there was a possibility that the positive aspects of care may have been overemphasized, leading to the underreporting of issues due to social desirability bias. Future research should include individual interviews and direct observations of midwives caring for pregnant adolescents, and incorporate objective evaluations to enhance the understanding of this population. Third, due to the interview-based nature of this study, it was not feasible to assess the utilization and frequency of youth-friendly care training. Future research should focus on comparing midwives who have undergone youth-friendly care training with those who have not identified any differences in care delivery. Fourth, given that this study was conducted in public and healthcare facilities in urban areas equipped with specific medical instruments and human resources, its applicability to midwives operating at the health-center level, where such resources may be limited, remains uncertain. Therefore, future research should incorporate facilities at various levels and areas to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the challenges and practices involved in providing care to pregnant adolescents.

Conclusion

Midwives play a critical role in addressing adolescent pregnancies in Malawi. Through daily care, midwives demonstrated sensitivity to adolescents’ unaddressed emotional and social needs. They endeavored to fulfill these needs by providing care characterized by acceptance, empathy, building trust and rapport, and instilling hope. Furthermore, they established relationships with the social networks of pregnant women, enabling them to receive support beyond antenatal care with the assistance of their families and other professionals. Additionally, midwives identified social structural issues, including common systemic inadequacies and sociocultural factors that prevent girls from accessing health checkups fairly and proposed solutions to these issues.
