The internal larval anatomy of two rodent bot fly species: Cuterebra fontinella (Diptera: Oestridae) and Cuterebra americana
Authors: Daniel Martín-Vega, Ezra M Bailey, Agnese Lanzetti, Martin J R Hall, Marco Pezzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpecies within the genus Cuterebra Clark, 1815 (Diptera: Oestridae: Cuterebrinae) are obligate parasites of rodents and other mammals during the larval stage, causing cutaneous myiasis. The internal larval anatomy of Cuterebra species remains unstudied, despite its potential utility for understanding their parasitic adaptations and for comparative studies with other Oestridae. The present study describes the internal larval anatomy of the species Cuterebra fontinella Clark, 1827 and Cuterebra americana (Fabricius, 1775), using micro-computed tomography-based virtual histological sections and 3-dimensional reconstructions. Both species show a broadly similar organization of the internal organs and tissues, with the digestive system being the main system within the hemocoel. The alimentary canal lacks gastric ceca and a crop; a condition shared with other Oestridae subfamilies, but which differs from the larvae of other Oestroidea families. The salivary glands are long and tubular, appearing morphologically distinct from those described in other Oestridae subfamilies thus far. The Malpighian tubules are uniformly thin and lack mineralized concretions, differing from the excretory organs of the oestrid species within the subfamily Oestrinae. The respiratory system shows little variation in relation to the basic tracheal scheme observed in the larvae of other cyclorrhaphous Diptera. Similarities and differences with the larvae of other Oestridae subfamilies and other Oestroidea families are discussed from a functional perspective, highlighting the value of nondestructive imaging techniques in comparative anatomical studies for contributing to a better understanding of the biology, evolution, and pathology of myiasis‑causing Diptera.Keywords: Cuterebrinae, digestive system, excretory system, micro-CT, myiasis

Introduction

Once considered as a separate family (Catts 1982), the Cuterebrinae are 1 of the 4 subfamilies recognized within the dipteran family Oestridae (Hall and Wall 1995, Pape 2001). Like all oestrid species, the Cuterebrinae are obligate parasites during their larval stage, infesting the living tissues of mammals and causing the disease condition known as myiasis (Hall and Wall 1995). Whereas the species within the oestrid subfamilies Gasterophilinae and Oestrinae cause, respectively, gastrointestinal and nasopharyngeal myiasis, the Cuterebrinae and the remaining subfamily, Hypodermatinae, cause subcutaneous, cutaneous, or furuncular myiasis (Hall and Wall 1995, Colwell et al. 2006). Unlike the other Oestridae subfamilies, which contain some widely distributed species that parasitize domestic mammals and have accompanied their hosts into many parts of the world, the Cuterebrinae are restricted to the Nearctic and Neotropical regions (Colwell et al. 2006). Following Pape (2001), this subfamily contains only 2 recognized genera: the Neotropical and monotypic Dermatobia Brauer, 1861, and the Nearctic and Neotropical Cuterebra Clark, 1815. With close to 100 described species, Cuterebra represents by far the most species-rich genus among the Oestridae (Colwell et al. 2006).

Cuterebra species mainly parasitize rodents and lagomorphs, with some species being parasites of Neotropical marsupials and primates (Catts 1982, Colwell et al. 2006). Eggs are typically laid on and around the host habitat to hatch in response to the raised temperature from the presence of a nearby host; then, newly hatched larvae enter the host through natural orifices and migrate to subcutaneous tissues, where they develop forming a characteristic warble with a breathing hole on the surface of the host’s skin (Gingrich 1981, Cogley 1991, Colwell et al. 2006). Once mature, the third instar larva leaves the host through this breathing hole and pupariates in the surrounding substrate, metamorphosing into an adult fly (Catts 1982). Although most Cuterebra species show a high specificity for one or a few close host species, occasional opportunistic infestations of humans and other mammals have been reported (Catts 1982, Park et al. 2021, Aihie et al. 2025). Nonetheless, parasitism by Cuterebra has limited pathology and generally does not seem to have a significant negative impact on the host (Colwell et al. 2006, Timm and Ruedebusch 2022).

In recent years, advanced molecular techniques have provided novel insights into both the evolutionary history of the different oestrid lineages and the evolution of parasitism in the Oestridae and related families like the Calliphoridae, which all together conform the superfamily Oestroidea (eg, Yan et al. 2019, Li et al. 2020, Beza-Beza et al. 2026). However, morphological studies of both the larval and adult stages are still key for reconstructing the phylogenetic relationships within the Oestridae and understanding their parasitic adaptations (Pape 2001, 2006, Pape et al. 2017, Li et al. 2021). In the specific case of the larval stage, morphological analyses enable not only the identification of new phylogenetically informative characters but also of novel adaptations to parasitism (Li et al. 2021, 2025). In fact, the monophyly of the genus Cuterebra is largely supported by larval morphological characters, some of them reflecting adaptations that facilitate their attachment to the host, like the terminal adhesive sack of the first instar larva and the cuticular spines scattered on the entire surface of the third instar larva (Pape 2001, 2006). While external structures have classically been favored in morphological studies of oestrid larvae (Knipling and Brody 1940, Colwell 1986, 2006, Li et al. 2021, 2025), the anatomy of internal organs and systems can also provide new and valuable information on oestrid parasitic adaptations. Novel approaches using noninvasive imaging techniques, like micro-computed tomography (micro-CT), have revealed anatomical features of the larval digestive and excretory organs that differ between oestrids and myiasis-causing Calliphoridae species (Martín-Vega et al. 2025a) and, within the Oestridae, between the subfamilies Oestrinae and Hypodermatinae (Martín-Vega et al. 2021, 2023, 2025b). Those differences are seemingly related to the type of myiasis caused (Martín-Vega et al. 2021, 2023, 2025a, 2025b). In the case of the Cuterebrinae, published research on the larval digestive system of Dermatobia hominis (L. Jr., 1781) suggest some anatomical differences compared with that of the Oestrinae and Hypodermatinae (Evangelista and Leite 2003, 2005, 2007, Martín-Vega et al. 2021), but, to the best of our knowledge, there are no available anatomical studies on the larval internal organs of Cuterebra species.

The present study describes for the first time the internal anatomy of the larva of 2 Cuterebra species that parasitize rodents from North America: Cuterebra fontinella Clark, 1827 and Cuterebra americana (Fabricius, 1775), with a particular focus on the digestive and excretory organs. Cuterebra fontinella parasitizes mice species belonging to the genus Peromyscus Gloger, 1841 (Rodentia: Cricetidae), whereas C. americana parasitizes the eastern wood rat, Neotoma floridana (Ord, 1818) (Rodentia: Cricetidae) (Cogley 1991, Colwell et al. 2006, Slansky 2006, Timm and Ruedebusch 2022). The described anatomical features will be discussed from a functional and comparative perspective, in relation to the biology of Cuterebra and other myiasis-causing Diptera.

Materials and Methods

Both Cuterebra specimens were obtained on loan from the NEON (National Ecological Observatory Network) Biorepository Mammal Endo- and Ectoparasite Collection (NEON: MEEP) as parasites recovered from small mammal vouchers during specimen preparation (Yule et al. 2020, Thibault et al. 2023). The species determinations were made using the combination of collection location and host association as accepted auxiliary taxonomic characters. Given both the paucity of Cuterebra barcoding resources for molecular identification as well as the known difficulty of larval morphological identification, species determination of Cuterebra often takes advantage of the genus’ high host specificity. Identifications were confirmed by the specialist George (Jeff) H. Boettner. The 2 specimens analyzed in the present study are as follows:

Cuterebra fontinella (NEON-MEEP00028, D00000188136) was stored in 95% ethanol at −20 °C and was 22.5 mm in length after preservation. The specimen was recovered from a white-footed mouse, Peromyscus leucopus (Rafinesque, 1818) (NEON01I5P), collected incidentally through the NEON small mammal box trapping protocol on July 18, 2018 at Plot SERC_031, Smithsonian Environmental Research Center Site, Domain 02, Anne Arundel, Maryland, United States, in a deciduous forest habitat at an elevation of 14 m.

Cuterebra americana (NEON-MEEP00712, D00000188128) was stored in 95% ethanol at −20 °C and was 18.0 mm in length after preservation. The specimen was recovered from Neotoma floridana (NEON03JR2) collected incidentally through the NEON small mammal box trapping protocol on July 14, 2020 at Plot KONZ_003, Konza Prairie Biological Station NEON (KONZ), Prairie Peninsula (D06), Riley, Kansas, United States, in a grassland herbaceous habitat at an elevation of 389 to 406 m.

Following the methodology described in previous publications for the preparation of samples for micro-CT scanning (Martín-Vega et al. 2021, 2023, 2025a, 2025b), the specimens were immersed in 0.5 M iodine in aqueous solution and stored at ∼4 °C for 2 wk. Iodine solutions often overstain mineralized structures, but they penetrate the internal tissues rapidly and deeply, providing an excellent contrast for micro-CT scans (Metscher 2009). Twenty-four hours prior to scanning, each specimen was washed with 70% ethanol and then transferred to a centrifuge tube also containing 70% ethanol. Each tube was placed into the Zeiss Versa 520 (ZEISS, Oberkochen, Germany) system at the Imaging and Analysis Center of the Natural History Museum, London, for being subsequently scanned using 0.4 × magnification and low-energy source filter LE4 to reduce beam hardening artifacts. Exposure time was set to 7 s, voltage to 120 kV, and current to 83 μA. A total of 2,034 and 1,878 projections were obtained for C. americana and C. fontinella, respectively. Resulting projections were reconstructed as TIFF stacks using the software Scout-and-Scan Control System and Reconstruction (Carl Zeiss AG, Jena, Germany), with a voxel size of 8.94 μm3 for the C. americana specimen and 16.19 μm3 for the C. fontinella specimen. Then, the reconstructed stacks were imported into (i) the software VG Studio Max 2.2 (Volume Graphics Gmbh, Heidelberg, Germany), where they were rendered, reoriented, and analyzed in cross, horizontal and sagittal anatomical reference planes; and (ii) the software Avizo 3D 2021.2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, United States) for manual segmentation and 3D reconstruction of the digestive, excretory/osmoregulatory and respiratory organs of C. fontinella. Manual segmentation was performed by the first author, and the reconstructed structures were analyzed and validated by all authors. The ‘Material statistics’ module from Avizo 3D 2021.2 software was used for the volumetric measurements of reconstructed organs. Due to the body of the C. americana specimen being somewhat distorted during extraction from the host, manual segmentation and 3D reconstruction of this species were limited to the salivary glands. Moreover, given that the respiratory structures are symmetrical along the midline of the dipteran larval body (Keilin 1944, Tatchell 1960, Ranade 1965), only the right side of the respiratory system of C. fontinella was segmented and 3-dimensionally reconstructed. As in previous studies (Martín-Vega et al. 2021, 2023, 2025a, 2025b), iodine-based contrast staining enabled the unequivocal discrimination of all organ boundaries except for the thinner ramifications of transverse connective and visceral tracheae in the respiratory system, which could not be reconstructed (see “Results and Discussion” section). Terminology follows Tatchell (1960) and Courtney et al. (2000), with the exception of the prothorax, mesothorax, and metathorax, which are referred to here as first, second, and third thoracic segments, respectively.

Results

Both C. fontinella and C. americana larval specimens show the same anatomical configuration of the internal organ systems and structures within the body cavity or hemocoel, delimited by the cuticle and the hypodermal muscles attached to it (Figs 1–4). The characteristic dense covering of cuticular spines of Cuterebra larvae is evident in the scanned specimens, due to a white-saturated coloration resulting from an iodine overstaining of the spines (Figs 1–3). Iodine overstaining can also be observed in the cephalopharyngeal skeleton of the scanned specimens (Figs 1A, 2A, and 3A). Nevertheless, in the 2 species, the main organ system within the hemocoel is the digestive system, particularly the organs that form the alimentary canal (Figs 1–3 and 4A and B). The anterior section of the alimentary canal, the foregut, is a thin, unconvoluted tube, measuring 0.19 mm3 (0.01% of the total body volume) in the analyzed C. fontinella specimen. The foregut progressively widens within the first 2 thoracic segments forming the pharynx (Fig. 4A), then narrows forming the esophagus and runs between the 2 brain hemispheres within the third thoracic segment, entering the cardia or proventriculus within the first abdominal segment (Figs 1C, 2B, C, 3B, C, and 4A). The cardia, distinctly swollen, is the organ responsible for the formation of the peritrophic matrix and is followed by the midgut, which is the longest and largest section of the alimentary canal, measuring 126.15 mm3 (5.48% of the total body volume) in the analyzed C. fontinella specimen. The midgut is considerably thicker than the foregut, lacks gastric ceca, and runs backwards and forwards several times, forming a convoluted and tangled mass that occupies a large portion of the hemocoel within the first 5 abdominal segments (Figs 1, 2D–F, 3, and 4A and B). In the third abdominal segment, within that tangled mass, the midgut is followed by the last section of the alimentary canal, the hindgut, with the Malpighian tubules discharging in the transition between both sections of the canal (Fig. 4C). The hindgut, which is similar in diameter to the midgut, runs anteriorly up to the first abdominal segment, where it turns back to run posteriorly. Then, the hindgut goes around the tangled mass of the midgut, between the second and third abdominal segments, and runs dorsally up to the sixth abdominal segment, where it meanders ventrally to run straight through the last segments before opening into the anus (Figs 2G–H and 4A–C). The volume of the hindgut of the analyzed C. fontinella specimen was 34.26 mm3 (1.49% of the total body volume).

Parallel to the alimentary canal, the salivary duct that discharges into the oral orifice runs ventrally to the foregut and, within the third thoracic segment, it bifurcates, with each derived duct connecting to a thin, convoluted tubular salivary gland (Fig. 4D and E). The pair of salivary glands flank the midgut’s tangled mass, tortuously running up to the fourth abdominal segment (Fig. 4A, B, D, and E). The salivary glands of both C. fontinella and C. americana specimens showed similar volumes: 4.89 mm3 (0.21% of the total body volume) in C. fontinella; 3.97 mm3 (0.25% of the total body volume) in C. americana. On the other hand, the excretory and osmoregulatory organs, the Malpighian tubules, originate as a pair of very thin tubes that discharge into the transition between the midgut and the hindgut within the third abdominal segment. The rightmost tube runs anteriorly amidst the tangled midgut, bifurcating within the third abdominal segment into 2 tubules (the anterior pair of Malpighian tubules; Fig. 4C) that continue running anteriorly up to the first abdominal segment. Conversely, the leftmost tube runs posteriorly, also bifurcating into 2 tubules (the posterior pair of Malpighian tubules; Fig. 4C) that continue running posteriorly; however, this posterior pair of tubules turn back within the fourth abdominal segment to run anteriorly up to the third abdominal segment (Fig. 4A–C). Both the anterior and posterior pair of Malpighian tubules are uniformly thin in diameter, with no enlarged sections observed throughout their length. In the analyzed C. fontinella specimen, the volume of the anterior and posterior pairs of Malpighian tubules was, respectively, 0.44 mm3 (0.02% of the total body volume) and 0.73 mm3 (0.03% of the total body volume).

Another conspicuous organ system within the hemocoel is the respiratory system, due to the numerous observations of tracheae in the virtual histological sections (Fig. 2). This system is amphipneustic in both C. fontinella and C. americana third-instar larva, with a pair of posterior spiracles on the anal division and a pair of anterior spiracles on the first thoracic segment. A pair of thick, main dorsal longitudinal tracheal trunks connect each posterior spiracle to the felt chamber of the corresponding anterior spiracle, which is retracted into a cuticular concavity or furrow (Fig. 1B). In the analyzed C. fontinella specimen, the reconstructed dorsal longitudinal trunk measured 22.41 mm3 (0.97% of the total body volume). Eight transverse connective tracheae branch off of each dorsal longitudinal tracheal trunk, one in each segment from the third thoracic segment to the seventh abdominal segment, from which thinner ramifications subsequently branch off along the corresponding side of the body (Fig. 4F). Those thinner tracheae were hard to track in the virtual histological sections due to lack of contrast with surrounding tissues and hemolymph; hence, manual segmentation and 3-dimensional reconstruction could not be reliably performed beyond their point of origin. In addition, near to the base of each transverse connective—except for that from the first abdominal segment—a visceral trachea branches off (Figs 2E and 4F). The visceral tracheae run anteriorly, increasingly thinner tracheae subsequently branching off them to supply the internal organs. Like for the thin tracheae branching off from the transverse connectives, manual segmentation and 3-dimensional reconstruction could not be reliably performed for the thinner visceral tracheae. In the second thoracic segment, a conspicuous dorsal anastomosis connects both the right and left visceral tracheae that branch off from the corresponding dorsal longitudinal trunk within that segment (Figs 2A and 4F). The 2 dorsal longitudinal trunks are also connected by a very short dorsal anastomosis within the anal division (Fig. 2H). On the other hand, the last visceral trachea (vt7), which branches off within the seventh abdominal segment, is particularly noticeable due to its large diameter and length, running up to the first abdominal segment and branching off, near its base, 3 additional tracheae, one of them of similar diameter to its source visceral trachea vt7 (Figs 1A and B, 3A and B, and 4F).

In addition to sections of the digestive and respiratory organs, the virtual histological sections of the scanned specimens also show a profusion of fat body cells in the hemocoel, particularly within the thoracic and the 3 last abdominal segments, that is, those segments not occupied by the convoluted midgut tube. Within the first to fifth abdominal segments, where the midgut occupies a large portion of the hemocoel, the fat body cells are mostly relegated to the dorsal region and to available spaces within the tangled midgut mass (Figs 1A and 2). The remaining spaces within the hemocoel are filled with connective tissue and with hemolymph, which is pumped by muscular contractions of the tubular heart or dorsal vessel (Rotstein and Paululat 2016), which shows an enlarged heart chamber located dorsally along the sixth to seventh abdominal segments (Figs 1A and 2G).

Discussion

Previous research had shown that, although overall similar to that of other Diptera, the internal larval anatomy of Oestridae shows interesting morphological features, particularly of the digestive organs, that might be related to adaptations to their specific parasitic habits (Martín-Vega et al. 2021, 2023, 2025b). Functionally developed larval digestive organs are key to the successful completion of the life cycle in holometabolous insects, given that the larva is often the main feeding and growing stage (Beutel et al. 2026) and is, therefore, responsible for the final adult size (Hanna et al. 2023). This is especially clear in the Oestridae, as adult flies develop only vestigial mouthparts and digestive organs (Cepeda-Palacios and Scholl 2000, Martín-Vega et al. 2020, 2025b) and cannot feed. The females emerge from the puparium with all the eggs they are capable of producing and ready to mate (Anderson 2006). Like other Oestridae species, the studied larval specimens of both C. fontinella and C. americana show a well-developed alimentary canal with a large midgut section that lacks gastric ceca. Gastric ceca occur in the anterior portion of the larval midgut of other Oestroidea families like Calliphoridae (Caleffe et al. 2024, Martín-Vega et al. 2025a) and Sarcophagidae (Sukontason et al. 2014), and in the larva of many other Diptera as well, including Culicidae (Godoy et al. 2023), Muscidae (Hewitt 1914) and Drosophilidae (Miguel-Aliaga et al. 2018). The function of the gastric ceca has traditionally been considered to be related to the digestion and reabsorption of nutrients and fluids, but recent research suggests that these organs also play a role in osmoregulation (D’Silva and O’Donnell 2018). The physiological implications of the lack of gastric ceca for the studied larvae of Cuterebra and their potential absence in other individuals and in other species within this genus are unknown; nonetheless, considering that they are also absent in the larvae of other Cuterebrinae (Evangelista and Leite 2003) and in the larvae of Gasterophilinae (Roelfstra et al. 2010), Hypodermatinae (Boulard 1969, Martín-Vega et al. 2021), and Oestrinae (Martín-Vega et al. 2021, 2023, 2025b), it might be a common feature of all Oestridae that deserves further investigation.

Another digestive organ that is absent in the studied larval specimens of both C. fontinella and C. americana is the crop, an esophageal diverticulum for food storage that is well-developed in the larvae of both Calliphoridae and Sarcophagidae (Knight 1962, Sukontason et al. 2014, Caleffe et al. 2024, Martín-Vega et al. 2025a). Like the gastric ceca, the crop is absent in the larva of other Cuterebrinae (Evangelista and Leite 2003) and the other Oestridae subfamilies (Boulard 1969, Roelfstra et al. 2010, Martín-Vega et al. 2021, 2023, 2025b). Unlike the gastric ceca, which are found widely, the crop seems to be absent in the larvae of most Diptera (Hewitt 1914, Miguel-Aliaga et al. 2018, Godoy et al. 2023). Therefore, rather than its absence being a potentially distinct character of Oestridae, the larval crop might be a specialized organ only present in some Diptera families like Calliphoridae and Sarcophagidae, enabling the rapid storage of either nutrients from an ephemeral resource like carrion, subject to intense competition in sarcosaprophagous calliphorid and sarcophagid species, or blood in hematophagous calliphorid species (Martín-Vega et al. 2025a). Whether they cause dermal, gastrointestinal, or nasopharyngeal myiasis, oestrid larvae are in a close and continued association with the host tissues during their development; hence, storage of nutrients does not seem to provide any obvious advantage. In fact, the larva of the calliphorid Cordylobia anthropophaga (Blanchard, 1893), an obligate parasite that causes a furuncular type of myiasis somewhat similar to that caused by Cuterebrinae species (Hall and Wall 1995), shows an apparently reduced crop in comparison to other Calliphoridae (Martín-Vega et al. 2025a), suggesting that the storage of large amounts of food is not needed when the contact with the infested tissues of the host is continuous.

While the absence of crop and gastric ceca are features likely shared by all Oestridae that differentiate them from Calliphoridae and Sarcophagidae—and perhaps from other Oestroidea—we found there are also differences in the anatomy of internal larval organs between Oestridae subfamilies. The greatly enlarged anterior region of the midgut observed in the larva of Hypodermatinae species (Martín-Vega et al. 2021) is absent in both the presently analyzed C. fontinella and C. americana specimens, as well as in other Cuterebrinae (Evangelista and Leite 2003) and in Gasterophilinae and Oestrinae species (Keilin 1944, Roelfstra et al. 2010, Martín-Vega et al. 2021, 2023, 2025b). On the other hand, the specific arrangement and great enlargement of both the pair of salivary glands and the distal region of the anterior pair of Malpighian tubules containing mineralized concretions observed in the larvae of Oestrinae (Martín-Vega et al. 2021, 2023, 2025b) might be distinct features of just this subfamily. In both C. fontinella and C. americana analyzed specimens, the anterior and posterior pairs of Malpighian tubules are uniformly thin in diameter with no discernible mineralized concretions in their interior, like in both Gasterophilinae and Hypodermatinae (Keilin 1944, Madel 1970, Martín-Vega et al. 2021). Nonetheless, it should be noted that Evangelista and Leite (2003) provided a camera lucida drawing of the digestive system from a dissected D. hominis third instar where the very distal end of one of the tubules appears somewhat distended. However, those authors made no reference to this issue, which might have been an artifact or a slight squashing of the tubule during dissection and manipulation. No similarly enlarged sections were observed by the same authors in the first instar larva of D. hominis (Evangelista and Leite 2005). In the Oestrinae, a greatly enlarged region of the anterior pair of Malpighian tubules containing calcified concretions is already present in newly hatched first instars (Colwell and Otranto 2021) and occurs through the different instars (Martín-Vega et al. 2021, 2025b). Regarding the salivary glands, their massive size and characteristic arrangement in the Oestrinae—with their posterior ends joining up in some species—certainly seem to be distinctive of that subfamily (Martín-Vega et al. 2021, 2023, 2025b), but the shape and size of that organ may also differ between the other oestrid subfamilies. The salivary glands are short, wide, and sack-like in the Hypodermatinae (Boulard 1969, Madel 1970, Martín-Vega et al. 2021), whereas they are long, thin, tubular and unconvoluted in Gasterophilinae (Keilin 1944, Roelfstra et al. 2010) and in D. hominis (Evangelista and Leite 2003, 2005, 2007). The salivary glands of both C. fontinella and C. americana analyzed specimens are also thin and tubular, but they are convoluted and much longer than those of D. hominis, where the pair of unconvoluted salivary glands only run up to the first abdominal segment (Evangelista and Leite 2005, 2007). Interestingly, the salivary glands of the 2 Cuterebra species studied here are morphologically similar to those of the calliphorid Co. anthropophaga, which also shows a pair of long and convoluted tubular glands that run up to the fourth abdominal segment and, as aforementioned, causes a similar type of myiasis (Martín-Vega et al. 2025a). The shape and size of the salivary glands also differ between calliphorid species that cause different types of myiasis (Martín-Vega et al. 2025a). The salivary glands of myiasis-causing larvae are key in their interactions with the host since their secretions are often the main source of immunogenic antigens (Angulo-Valadez et al. 2010, Pawlas-Opiela et al. 2022). Hence, further studies on the functional morphology of these organs and the characterization of the salivary products may provide new insights into the pathogenesis, management, and control of oestrid myiasis. In the specific case of Cuterebra species, it has been shown that rodents produce antibodies against crude antigens prepared by homogenization of C. fontinella first instars (Pruett and Barrett 1983) and that the sources of immunogenic antigens in the species Cuterebra buccata (Fabricius, 1777) are found in the digestive organs and hemolymph rather than in the cuticle (Weisbroth et al. 1973). However, to the best of our knowledge, no antigens have been characterized yet for any Cuterebra species.

Whereas the larval digestive system shows morphological differences between Oestroidea families and Oestridae subfamilies, the larval respiratory system of the studied Cuterebra specimens shows little variation in comparison with the anatomical configuration observed in the larvae of other cyclorrhaphous Diptera (Whitten 1959, Tatchell 1960, Ranade 1965). Indeed, the tracheal system of cyclorrhaphous Diptera shows a basic anatomical plan that is remarkably stable across different taxa, with constant characters like the broad dorsal anastomosis in the second thoracic segment, the short anastomosis in the anal division or the enlarged last pair of visceral tracheae (Whitten 1959), also observed here in both C. fontinella and C. americana analyzed specimens. Nevertheless, there are some variations of the basic cyclorrhaphous tracheal scheme within the Oestridae: whereas the basic scheme includes one anastomosis per segment (from the second thoracic to the seventh abdominal segment) connecting both dorsal longitudinal trunks (Whitten 1959, Ranade 1965), the Hypodermatinae show a reduction of the number of dorsal anastomoses, which are restricted to the typical broad one within the second thoracic segment and to a thin one within each of the third thoracic, first abdominal, and seventh abdominal segments (Murray 1971). On the other hand, the Gasterophilinae show a unique modification of that basic tracheal scheme, with 4 pairs of conical tracheae within the last abdominal segments, which are connected to modified fat body cells that store hemoglobin (Keilin 1944, Tatchell 1960). Considering the present observations, the tracheal system of Cuterebra larvae might be similar to that of the Hypodermatinae, with an apparent reduction of the dorsal anastomoses to one within the second thoracic segment and another one within the seventh abdominal segment. However, given that thinner ramifications of the transverse connective and visceral tracheae could not be reliably segmented and reconstructed in the present study, due to insufficient contrast with the surrounding tissues and hemolymph, it cannot be stated with certainty that thin dorsal anastomoses are not present in other body segments, like in the Hypodermatinae (Murray 1971). Specific methods for the micro-CT analysis of the insect tracheal system have been developed (Iwan et al. 2015, Alba-Tercedor et al. 2019, Herhold et al. 2023); however, they involve sample fixation and preparation techniques that differ from the approach used here, aimed at studying the soft organs and tissues that form the digestive and excretory systems. Future research should characterize the anatomy of the larval respiratory system of Cuterebra and of other oestrids in further detail, using imaging methods optimized for the visualization of the tracheal system (Iwan et al. 2015, Alba-Tercedor et al. 2019, Herhold et al. 2023). Future research should also extend the present study to additional specimens of C. fontinella and C. americana, as well as to other Cuterebra species. Even if instances of intraspecific anatomical variability have not been found in previous studies based on larger sample sizes from other Oestridae species (Martín-Vega et al. 2021, 2023, 2025b), the analysis of additional larval specimens is required to confirm if the present observations actually reflect distinct anatomical characters of the 2 species studied here and/or of genus Cuterebra, as well as to assess the potential phylogenetic value of those characters. Regrettably, the scarcity of suitable oestrid specimens for internal anatomical studies—particularly of those species that parasitize wild mammals (Colwell and Otranto 2021)—together with potential constraints in the access to micro-CT facilities, are major limitations for analyzing large sample sizes.

The internal larval anatomy of most Diptera taxa remains understudied, perhaps even neglected, under the assumption that their generally simple and uniform external morphology—particularly marked in cyclorrhaphous larvae (Li et al. 2021, Beutel et al. 2026)—is also translated to the morphology of the internal organ systems. However, the larvae of Diptera are extremely diverse in their feeding habits and habitats (Beutel et al. 2026, Beza-Beza et al. 2026); hence, it is expected for some taxa to show specific adaptations in their digestive organs and in other organ systems (Keilin 1944, Colwell 2006). Micro-CT and other novel imaging techniques provide unparalleled opportunities for revealing and analyzing the anatomy of internal organs and structures and performing comparative and functional studies to identify potential evolutionary adaptations and phylogenetically informative characters (Li et al. 2021, 2025, Martín-Vega et al. 2021, 2025b). In the case of the Oestridae—which include several species known only from the larval stage (Colwell and Otranto 2021)—and other myiasis-causing Diptera, those studies may also provide novel insights into their biology, the pathology of myiasis, and the development of management and control measures.
