Explainable artificial intelligence for predicting cardiovascular events in hospitalised COVID-19 patients
Authors: Milena Soriano Marcolino, Isabella Viana Gomes Schettini, Guilherme Fonseca do Nascimento, Leonardo Chaves Dutra da Rocha, Fernanda Cristina Barbosa Lana, Marcos André Gonçalves
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCoronavirus disease (COVID-19) increases the risk of cardiovascular complications, and artificial intelligence (AI) offers promising tools for early risk prediction.ObjectiveTo develop AI models capable of identifying predictors of cardiovascular events in hospitalized COVID-19 patients.MethodologyRetrospective multicentre cohort, which included adult COVID-19 patients from 25 hospitals (March/2021–August/2022). Cardiovascular outcomes, inluding arrhythmia, acute heart failure, myocardial infarction, myocarditis, and pericarditis, were combined into a composite outcome. Two predictive models were developed using the Light gradient-boosting machine (LightGBM): model 1 used 59 variables (demographic, clinical, laboratory, and socioeconomic data) while model 2 used 52 variables (excluding socioeconomic factors). Model performance was assessed using accuracy, macro-F1, recall, precision, and area under the receiving operating characteristic curve (AUROC). Shapley additive explanation (SHAP) values identified the most influencial predictors. To address class imbalance, we applied random oversampling.ResultsAmong 10,700 patients (median age 59 years [interquatile range 48–70]), 5.3% experienced the composite outcome. Both models showed moderate discrimination (AUROC: 0.752 and 0.760) and high accuracy (94.6% and 94.5%). However, class imbalance resulted in low macro-F1 scores (51.2% and 50.7%). F1 scores were high for the majority class (non-events: 97.2%) but very low for the minority class (cardiovascular events: 5.2% and 4.2%). Even after oversampling, performance for the minority class remained limited, with a maximum F1 score of 21.5%, primarily driven by gains in recall. SHAP analysis identified age, urea, platelet count, and oxygen saturation/inspired oxygen fraction (SatO2/FiO2) as key predictors.ConclusionDespite moderate AUROC and high accuracy, both AI models demonstrated limited ability to detect cardiovascular events due to class imbalance. The persistently low F1 score for the minority class underscores this limitation. Traditional rebalancing techniques produced only small gains, mostly improving recall occurring at the expense of precision. Age, urea levels, platelet count, and SatO2/FiO2 were identified as the most relevant predictors of cardiovascular complications in this cohort.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11465-6.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, COVID-19, Cardiovascular diseases, Predictive learning models

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic has profoundly impacted global healthcare systems, not only due to the high burden of respiratory complications but also because of its significant effects on the cardiovascular system [1]. Early in the pandemic, it became evident that SARS-CoV-2 infection was associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular complications, ranging from myocardial injury and arrhythmias to acute heart failure and thromboembolic events [1, 2]. These complications are particularly concerning, as they contribute to worse clinical outcomes, prolonged hospital stays, and increased mortality, especially among patients with preexisting cardiovascular conditions [3–5].

Beyond its direct clinical implications, the COVID-19 pandemic disproportionately burdens healthcare systems worldwide, particularly in regions with limited resources [6]. The lack of hospital infrastructure, healthcare professionals, and access to intensive care contributed to higher mortality and cardiovascular complications related to the virus [7]. This vulnerable situation is even more concerning in Latin America and the Caribbean, where socioeconomic inequalities and fragile healthcare systems may exacerbate the effects of the pandemic [6, 8, 9]. This region also has a high prevalence of comorbidities associated with worse COVID-19 outcomes, such as obesity, diabetes, and hypertension [7], which may have increased the occurrence of cardiovascular complications in hospitalized patients.

While the acute phase of the COVID-19 pandemic has passed, the disease offered a unique opportunity to study how systemic viral infections can trigger cardiovascular complications, particularly in vulnerable populations. Understanding these interactions, especially in the context of socioeconomic determinants, remains valuable not only for retrospective insights but also for strengthening preparedness for future pandemics. By investigating the occurrence of cardiovascular events in hospitalized COVID-19 patients and developing AI models to predict these outcomes, this study contributes to a broader understanding of virus-related cardiovascular risk. Such models may inform early risk stratification and targeted interventions in future outbreaks of COVID-19 variants or novel infectious diseases with similar pathophysiological profiles.Therefore, this study aims to investigate the occurrence of cardiovascular complications, and to develop artificial intelligence (AI) models to identify predictors of cardiovascular events in-hospital COVID-19 patients, providing valuable insights for optimizing clinical care and guiding future research.

Methods

This study is a substudy of the Brazilian COVID-19 Registry, a retrospective multicenter cohort encompassing 41 hospitals across Brazil [10]. It was conducted in 27 public and private hospitals located in 14 cities across five Brazilian states (Bahia, Minas Gerais, Rio Grande do Sul, Santa Catarina and São Paulo). The Brazilian COVID-19 Registry follows a predefined protocol, which has been previously described in detail [10, 11]. This study complies with the strengthening the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [12] (Supplemental file). The central figure summarizes the methods and main results.

Study subjects

This study included consecutive adult patients (aged 18 years or older) with laboratory-confirmed COVID-19 [13] who were admitted to one of the participating hospitals between March 2021 and August 2022. Patients hospitalized for other conditions who developed COVID-19 symptoms during their stay, as well as those transferred to non-participating hospitals, patients in palliative care and pregnant women were excluded. A convenience sampling approach was adopted, and no prior sample size calculation was performed.

Patient data collection and quality assessment

Trained personnel extracted demographic data, underlying comorbidities, clinical characteristics, laboratory findings, and outcomes from medical records [10]. The collected variables have been previously described in detail [11].

Data were recorded using prestructured forms within the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) platform [14], hosted by the Telehealth Center at the Universidade Federal de Minas Gerais [15]. To ensure data accuracy and reliability, periodic audits were conducted to identify outliers or unexpected values. Any discrepancies were sent back to local coordinators for verification and correction.

Hospital data collection

Data on hospital characteristics included funding source (public, private or mixed), accreditation status, academic affiliation (presence of an accredited medical residency program), and the socioeconomic profile of the municipality where each hospital was located, as previously described [16].

To assess the socioeconomic characteristics of the population served by these hospitals, we analyzed the gross domestic product (GDP) per capita and the human development index (HDI) of each municipality. These data were obtained from the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (Instituto Brasileiro de Geografia e Estatística – IBGE) [17].

Outcome

Cardiovascular complications, including arrhythmia, acute heart failure (either new-onset or decompensated chronic), acute myocardial infarction, myocarditis, and pericarditis, were analyzed as a composite outcome. These outcomes were obtained directly from medical records as documented by the attending physicians, without additional clinical evaluation or adjudication. The cardiovascular complications selected for analysis were chosen based on the retrospective design of the study and the availability of consistently documented clinical information in the medical records. Standardized data required to assess cardiovascular death were not available, limiting the evaluation of composite outcomes such as major adverse outcomes (MACE). Therefore, the analysis focused on specific cardiac complications that are well-recognized in the literature as relevant manifestations associated with COVID-19 [1].

Cardiovascular complications were analyzed as a composite outcome due to the low number of cases for several individual events, which limited the feasibility of training separate machine learning models (Supplementary table S1).

Ethics

This study has been approved by the Brazilian National Committee for Research Ethics (Comissão Nacional de Ética em Pesquisa, CONEP, CAAE: 30350820.5.1001.0008). The individual free and informed consent term was exempted, considering the study’s retrospective nature, the pandemic context, and the use of unidentified data. The research was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and Brazilian National Health Council Resolution No. 466/2012.

Data analysis

Descriptive analyses were performed to summarize the variables. Continuous variables were summarized as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR), as they exhibited a non-normal distribution, while categorical variables were represented through counts and percentages.

Variables potentially associated with adverse COVID-19 outcomes were selected based on previous studies. Two predictive models were developed using light gradient-boosting machine (LightGBM): model 1 included 59 variables, including demographic, clinical, laboratory, and socioeconomic data, whereas model 2 included the same data except for socioeconomic variables, resulting in a total of 52 variables (supplementary file; table S2 and S3). LightGBM is a non-linear, tree-based method that excels in effectively modeling non-linear relationships and complex interactions [18]. As it is not affected by missing values, no imputation was required. The development of two distinct predictive models aimed to evaluate the specific contribution of socioeconomic variables to model performance.

Model performance was assessed using accuracy, macro-F1, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), and per class precision, recall and F1-score, with their respective confidence intervals obtained based on the results of the 5-fold cross-validation. Recall represents the proportion of instances belonging to a given class X that were correctly identified by the model as part of that class X. When calculated for the positive class (class 1, cardiovascular event), recall class 1 = true positives/(true positives + false negatives). For the negative class (class 0, no cardiovascular event), recall class 0 = true negatives/(true negatives + false positives). Precision measures the proportion of instances predicted as belonging to a given class X that are correctly predicted. It is calculated for the positive class as precision class 1 = true positives/(true positives + false positives), while for the negative class, it is precision class 0 = true negatives/(true negatives + false negatives). The F1-score, in turn, is a metric that combines both precision and recall of a class into a single value. It is calculated as the harmonic mean of the precision and recall values for that class. For the positive class, F1 class 1 = 2 * (precision class 1 * recall class 1)/(precision class 1 + recall class 1), while for the negative class, F1 class 0 = 2 * (precision class 0 * recall class 0)/(precision class 0 + recall class 0) [19].

Accuracy and macro-F1 are two metrics that aggregate the F1-scores of multiple classes to evaluate the overall performance of a classification model. Accuracy (micro-F1) is obtained by calculating the F1-score globally, while macro-F1 is calculated as the unweighted average of the F1-scores across the classes [19].

The two models were evaluated using 5-fold cross-validation, which consists of dividing the dataset into five equal parts, each maintaining the same class distribution as the entire dataset. Four of these parts are used to train the models, while the remaining one is used for testing. This process is repeated five times (covering all possible combinations), and we report the mean of the results obtained in each iteration along with the corresponding confidence interval. To assess whether there was a statistically significant difference between the performance of models 1 and 2, a Student’s t-test was conducted to compare the accuracy (micro F1) and macro F1 scores.

To identify the most important predictors, we use shapley additive explanation (SHAP) values obtained from a LightGBM model. SHAP is a widely used method in which each variable is assigned a value representing its importance to the model’s final prediction. In the SHAP graph, the Y-axis represents the variables in order of their importance to the model. The X-axis shows the SHAP values, where positive values indicate that the variable contributes to the outcome (cardiovascular event), and negative values suggest a contribution to the opposite outcome (absence of cardiovascular event). Every point in the graph represents an instance and the color represents the high/low variable values, shades of red indicate high values and shades of blue indicate lower values [20].

To evaluate the impact of data imbalance on model performance, we applied a rebalancing technique based on oversampling, which consists of increasing the representation of the minority class (in this case, having the composite outcome). Specifically, we used random oversampling, which randomly duplicates instances of the minority class until it is balanced with the majority class [21].

All source code and documentation used in the analysis are publicly available at: https://github.com/guilherme8426/Predicting-Cardiovascular-Events. This allows other researchers and institutions to replicate, validate, and adapt the models.

Results

Overall, 10,700 patients were included (Fig. 1). The overall median age was 59 (IQR 48 to 70) years old and 54.2% of the participants were men. The most common comorbidities were hypertension (52.7%), diabetes mellitus (26%) and obesity (18.8%). Table 1 summarizes the main clinical and demographic characteristics, as well as patient comorbidities. The dataset exhibited a significant class imbalance, with only 5.3% experiencing any cardiovascular event.

Light gradient-boosting machine models

The LightGBM predictive models for cardiovascular event prediction included all variables listed in Supplementary File (Table S2 and S3). Both models demonstrated moderate discrimination, with an AUROC of 0.752 (0.027) (model 1) and 0.760 (0.026) (model 2).

As shown in Table 2, the performance of the models, considering accuracy, was high: 94.6% for model 1 and 94.5% for model 2. However, the class imbalance posed a significant challenge for accurate prediction of the composite outcome, which was reflected in the lower macro-F1-scores, 51.2% (1.4%) for model 1 and 50.7% (1.5%) for model 2.

For the majority class (no cardiovascular event), both models showed excellent performance. Model 1 achieved a precision of 94.8% (0.1%), recall of 99.8% (0.1%), and an F1-score of 97.2% (0.1%). Model 2 presented similar results, with a precision of 94.8% (0.1%), recall of 99.7% (0.1%), and an F1-score of 97.2% (0.1%). On the other hand, for the minority class (with cardiovascular events), performance was considerably lower, highlighting the models’ limitations in identifying these cases. Model 1 achieved a precision of 40.2% (15.4%), recall of only 2.8% (1.5%), and an F1-score of 5.2% (2.7%), while model 2 achieved a precision of 29.5% (18.6%), recall of 2.3% (1.6%), and an F1-score of 4.2% (2.9%). These results underscore the challenges faced by the models in adequately capturing positive cases (cardiovascular events), primarily due to the strong class imbalance. There was no statistically significant difference in performance between the two models (micro F1 p = 0.3821 and macro F1 p = 0.4531).

The SHAP value analysis identified the variables with the greatest predictive impact in the risk models for cardiovascular events in hospitalized COVID-19 patients. In both models, age, urea, and the SatO₂/FiO₂ emerged as the most influential predictors, reinforcing their clinical relevance in risk stratification (Fig. 2).

With the application of random oversampling, both models showed substantial improvements in identifying the minority class, especially in terms of recall and F1-score (Table 2). In model 1, recall increased to 22.2% (4.3%), and F1-score to 21.5% (4.2%). In model 2, recall increased to 23.1% (4.1%) and F1-score to 22.4% (3.5%). However, this gain in sensitivity came at the cost of a drop in precision: in model 1, precision decreased to 20.7% (4.0%), and in model 2, to 21.8% (3.1%). No statistically significant difference was observed between the models after data balancing (micro F1 p = 0.5632, macro F1 p = 0.7297).

In the SHAP value analysis, applying random oversampling led to minimal changes in the predictive profile of model 2, selected due to its comparable performance to model 1 and its more concise structure. The main predictors remained in nearly the same positions, indicating that data balancing did not substantially alter the patterns learned by the model. Discussion

This study used a non-linear method, based on decision trees (LightGBM), combined with SHAP analysis, to identify predictors of cardiovascular events in hospitalized COVID-19 patients. The models demonstrated good overall predictive performance, although they showed limited sensitivity in detecting such events, likely due to the strong class imbalance. Rebalancing strategies improved effectiveness on detecting these rare events, but this came at a cost: the increase in recall was accompanied by a notable drop in precision. This trade-off means that while the models became better at identifying patients at risk, they also produced more false positives, which could lead to unnecessary anxiety, additional testing, or overtreatment. These findings highlight a central limitation of standard rebalancing techniques in this context, as improving the detection of rare events often compromises the model’s reliability in confirming true cases. In this context, our study illustrates the challenges of using AI to predict cardiovascular events, especially due to issues of class imbalance.

SHAP analysis identified age, urea, SatO₂/FiO₂, and platelet count as the most influential predictors in both models, highlighting their clinical relevance in risk stratification. In addition to these features, other important variables also contributed to the outcomes, such as heart failure, hypertension, and other respiratory parameters. The inclusion of socioeconomic variables did not significantly improve model performance, suggesting that the clinical and demographic factors already captured much of the relevant variability. Our results highlight the applicability of machine learning models for analyzing large volumes of complex clinical data, contributing to a deeper understanding of the factors associated with adverse outcomes in COVID-19 patients. The use of LightGBM, combined with SHAP interpretation, enabled a more robust exploration of the relationships between variables and outcomes, overcoming the limitations of traditional statistical models—such as the assumption of linearity between variables, limited ability to capture complex interactions, reduced flexibility in handling non-normally distributed variables, and sensitivity to missing data. This approach allowed not only the identification of key predictors but also the assessment of their individual influence within each patient’s clinical context. Furthermore, the explainability provided by SHAP values enhances the transparency of predictive models, which is essential for their safe and effective integration into clinical practice [22].

The mechanisms underlying cardiovascular complications related to COVID-19 are multifactorial, involving direct viral invasion of myocardial tissue through the angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptor, systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, coagulation abnormalities, and increased cardiac workload due to acute lung injury. The hyperinflammatory state may exacerbate pre-existing cardiac diseases or even trigger new cardiovascular conditions [1, 23]. Furthermore, the use of corticosteroids in COVID-19 patients may influence cardiovascular outcomes, highlighting the need for careful monitoring and clinical management [24, 25]. A systematic review on acute cardiovascular complications in COVID-19 patients identified pre-existing cardiovascular disease, older age, and severe clinical presentation as possible predictors of these outcomes [1].

Age is a well-known risk factor for adverse events in COVID-19 as well as for cardiovascular outcomes in the general population [19, 20, 23–25]. In the present analysis, age was the most impactful variable on both models. Aging is associated with immunosenescence, the decline of innate and adaptive immune responses, which increases susceptibility to infections and favors a dysregulated inflammatory response, a hallmark of severe COVID-19 [26]. Additionally, the presence of low-grade chronic inflammation (“inflammaging”) may intensify the acute inflammatory process induced by the virus [26–28]. In the cardiovascular system, aging contributes to increased arterial stiffness, endothelial dysfunction, and cardiac remodeling, rendering the organism more susceptible to cardiac injury and cardiovascular complications during infection [27, 28].

Urea may act as an indirect marker of renal dysfunction, dehydration, systemic inflammation, and inadequate tissue perfusion, all of which are closely related to cardiovascular risk [29–31]. COVID-19 can lead to acute kidney injury, particularly in critically ill patients, and renal dysfunction, which in turn increases the likelihood of cardiovascular events such as arrhythmias, myocardial infarction, and acute heart failure, given the kidney’s central role in regulating volume status, blood pressure, and electrolyte balance [29–31]. Furthermore, elevated urea levels may reflect hypoperfusion and increased protein catabolism, both associated with disease severity, placing critically ill patients at greater risk for cardiovascular complications [29, 31]. This pathophysiological rationale supports findings in the literature, including a systematic review that associates elevated urea levels with increased morbidity and mortality in patients with COVID-19 [32].

Reduced platelet count has been shown to be a relevant prognostic marker in patients with COVID-19. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis, which included 29 studies with more than 15,000 patients, demonstrated that platelet levels were significantly lower in critically ill patients. The standardized mean difference in platelet count between groups was − 0.421 (95% CI: −0.659 to −0.182), reinforcing the potential of thrombocytopenia as a prognostic indicator of disease severity. The study highlights the role of platelets not only in hemostasis but also in immune and inflammatory responses during viral infections, supporting their relevance as a simple and accessible biomarker for adverse outcomes in COVID-19 [33]. The prognostic value of thrombocytopenia has also been supported by its inclusion in various scoring systems [34–36]. Although the exact explanation is not yet fully understood, it is believed to be multifactorial, involving mechanisms such as direct infection of bone marrow cells by the virus, leading to impaired hematopoiesis and immune-mediated platelet destruction. Other possible mechanisms include endothelial injury with consequent platelet activation and aggregation, microthrombi formation in the lungs, and abnormal platelet fragmentation processes [37].

In addition to laboratory biomarkers, respiratory parameters also emerged as important predictors. Among them, the SatO₂/FiO₂ ratio was the most influential variable for the outcome, with higher values strongly associated with a lower likelihood of adverse events. This marker, beyond its statistical significance, holds important clinical implications. A reduced SatO₂/FiO₂ ratio is a well-established indicator of impaired gas exchange and hypoxemia, often linked to the need for advanced respiratory support, such as high-flow oxygen therapy or mechanical ventilation [38, 39]. These findings are consistent with the literature, which highlights oxygenation markers, such as pO₂/FiO₂ and SatO₂/FiO₂, as key prognostic tools throughout the pandemic [40, 41]. In the context of cardiovascular complications, a reduced SatO₂/FiO₂ ratio at hospital admission may reflect impaired gas exchange and insufficient tissue oxygenation. Even at a single time point, lower oxygenation can activate molecular pathways involved in endothelial dysfunction, sympathetic nervous system stimulation, pulmonary vasoconstriction and vascular remodeling. Additionally, hypoxia may promote platelet activation through oxidative stress, reduced nitric oxide bioavailability, and upregulation of hypoxia-inducible pathways such as HIF-1α. These mechanisms can contribute to myocardial strain, right ventricular overload, and a pro-thrombotic state, potentially increasing the risk of adverse cardiovascular events [42–44].

It is also important to emphasize the impact of pre-existing cardiac conditions, such as heart failure and hypertension, in increasing the risk of cardiovascular events in patients with COVID-19 [45]. SARS-CoV-2 infection may lead to decompensation of these conditions, facilitating the onset of complications [46]. Additionally, the intense inflammatory response and the hypercoagulable state induced by COVID-19 contribute to myocardial injury, particularly in individuals with reduced cardiac reserve [1, 3, 5, 40]. Therefore, the presence of cardiovascular comorbidities is strongly associated with greater clinical severity and higher mortality [3, 47–49].

During pandemics, pre-existing social inequalities, such as unemployment, social isolation, and mental health disorders, are often exacerbated, resulting in higher exposure to SARS-CoV-2, severe disease, hospitalization, and mortality in socially vulnerable populations [50, 51]. However, incorporating socioeconomic factors into the predictive model did not significantly improve performance compared to the model without these variables. This may suggest that their influence is mediated through variables already captured in the model, such as comorbidities and severity at hospital presentation [52]. It is also possible that limitations in how socioeconomic data were collected or categorized reduced their predictive contribution. Therefore, while social determinants play an important role in shaping health outcomes, their indirect effects may already be reflected in clinical features available at hospital admission. Given the similar performance, the simpler model, excluding socioeconomic variables, may be preferable for practical implementation.

In this context, risk stratification emerges as a strategic tool, particularly in scenarios marked by healthcare system overload, such as during the COVID-19 pandemic. The ability of predictive models to integrate clinical, laboratory, and contextual data in real time offers valuable support for data-driven clinical decision-making [53].

When interpreting the results of machine learning models, such as LightGBM, it is important to note that these models operate differently from traditional regression approaches. Instead of requiring the researcher to pre-specify interactions or confounders, the model learns patterns and relationships directly from the data. In this way, variables such as age, comorbidities, and inflammatory markers can be leveraged in combination to detect high-risk profiles, even when biological mechanisms are not explicitly modeled. Nonetheless, the inclusion of more detailed inflammatory or pathway-specific biomarkers, such as those related to the arachidonic acid cascade, could improve predictive performance and should be considered in future studies [54].

This study contributes to the growing body of evidence demonstrating the utility of artificial intelligence in managing infectious disease crises, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). By applying interpretable machine learning models to real-world hospital data, we provide clinically relevant insights that support early identification of high-risk patients and informed allocation of limited healthcare resources. In addition to reinforcing the value of AI for individual-level risk stratification, our results emphasize the potential of these tools to enhance hospital preparedness and public health planning during global health emergencies.

This study also contributes to the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 3 (Good Health and Well-being) [55], particularly by addressing targets related to the reduction of non-communicable diseases (3.4), strengthening health systems’ responsiveness to public health emergencies (3.d), and supporting equitable access to high-quality healthcare services (3.8). By applying explainable AI to identify predictors of cardiovascular events during a global pandemic, the study offers insights that may inform risk stratification strategies, improve clinical decision-making, and ultimately enhance patient outcomes in resource-constrained settings.

The limitations of this study can be traced back to the information collection, as it is a retrospective study based on patient records’ data collection. We were unable to evaluate MACE as a composite endpoint, due to the lack of reliable data on cardiovascular death in the retrospective medical records. Although stroke data were available, the study was specifically designed to focus on cardiac complications associated with COVID-19 rather than broader cardiovascular outcomes. Additionally, a non-random sampling of the Brazilian hospitals was conducted. An invitation was sent through social media, radio, and through the National Institute of Science and Technology for Health Technology Assessment (Instituto de Avaliação de Tecnologias em Saúde – IATS) website.

However, despite the aforementioned limitations, this study presents important strengths. It constitutes one of the largest multicenter cohorts of hospitalized COVID-19 patients in Brazil, covering different regions of the country and including hospitals with diverse care profiles, which enhances the heterogeneity of the sample and increases the generalizability of the findings. The use of advanced machine learning techniques, combined with SHAP analysis, enabled a robust, transparent, and clinically interpretable predictive approach, overcoming some of the limitations of traditional statistical methods. Moreover, the incorporation of clinical, laboratory, and socioeconomic variables allowed for a broader assessment of factors potentially associated with cardiovascular outcomes. Our main objective was to demonstrate the potential of artificial intelligence models to identify predictors of cardiovascular complications in hospitalized patients with confirmed COVID-19. While the clinical profile of COVID-19 has evolved over time, the methodological contributions of our study remain relevant. These AI modeling strategies can be adapted for use in future outbreaks or other respiratory infections with similar risk profiles. Thus, beyond the context of the pandemic, our findings offer insights for advancing the application of AI in hospital-based risk prediction models.

The low number of positive cases in the dataset is still one of the main challenges to improve the predictive performance of the models. Even with the use of balancing techniques, like random oversampling, the results are still limited, especially when it comes to identifying the less frequent class. This shows how complex the problem is and highlights the importance of using, in future studies, more advanced balancing and modeling strategies that can deal better with data imbalance and outcomes that, even if not frequent, are very important in clinical practice. These results also highlight the relevance of exploring more modern alternative approaches to address class imbalance (as proposed by Fonseca et al. 2025), even though such solutions involve greater technical complexity and higher computational costs [56]. This line of investigation will be considered in future work, as overcoming this challenge, particularly in health-related contexts, requires specific methodological efforts and goes beyond the scope of the present study.

Conclusion

Despite good overall predictive performance in terms of global accuracy and moderate AUROC, both AI models had limited sensitivity in detecting cardiovascular events in hospitalized COVID-19 patients, mostly due to class imbalance. Although the AUROC values were acceptable, they did not fully capture the model’s limitations in identifying patients who actually experienced cardiovascular events, as reflected in the low F1 score for the minority class. Rebalancing techniques modestly improved recall for cardiovascular events, but this came at the expense of reduced precision, highlighting the difficulty of improving sensitivity without increasing false positives. Age, urea levels, platelet count, and oxygen saturation/inspired oxygen fraction were identified as the most relevant predictors in both models, reinforcing their role in cardiovascular risk assessment in COVID-19.
