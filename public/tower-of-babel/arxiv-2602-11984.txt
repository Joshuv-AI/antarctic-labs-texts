Radicals in primitive axial algebras
A. Mamontov, S. Shpectorov, V. Zhelyabin

Abstract




1
Introduction

The class of axial algebras was introduced in
[
HRS
,
HRS1
]
as a broad
generalization of the class of Majorana algebras of Ivanov
[
I
]
. These are
commutative non-associative algebras generated by (primitive) idempotents called axes,
whose adjoint action is semisimple and satisfies a prescribed fusion law. (See the
exact definitions in Section
2
.) Axial algebras is currently a
very active area of research, with many strong results obtained particularly about
two specific classes of primitive axial algebras, the algebras of Monster type

(\alpha,\beta)
, generalizing Majorana algebras, and the more narrow class of algebras of
Jordan type
\eta
including the axial Jordan algebras and the Matsuo algebras
corresponding to
3
-transposition groups. For a recent survey of axial algebras, see

[
MS
]
.

The structure theory of primitive axial algebras was developed in
[
KMS
]
. In
particular, this paper introduced the concept of the radical of an axial algebra
A
,
as the largest ideal
R(A)
not containing any of the generating axes. When
A

is endowed with a Frobenius form
(\cdot,\cdot)
, a bilinear form associating with the algebra
product, the axial radical
R(A)
is contained in the Frobenius form radical

A^{\perp}
, with the equality taking place if and only if all generating axes are
non-singular for the form.

In this note we continue developing the structure theory of primitive axial algebras
by comparing the above two radicals,
R(A)
and
A^{\perp}
, with the Jacobson radical

J(A)
, which we define simply as the intersection of all maximal ideals of
A
.
Our main result is as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

If
A
is a primitive axial algebra with a Frobenius form
(\cdot,\cdot)
then

R(A)\subseteq J(A)\subseteq A^{\perp}
.

Clearly, this means that if all generating axes are non-singular with respect to the
Frobenius form, which happens in the vast majority of known examples, then

R(A)=J(A)=A^{\perp}
.

Note that in this theorem we allow the Frobenius form to be zero, which means our statement covers all primitive axial algebras. When the Frobenius form is zero,
A^{\perp}=A
, but we still have the non-obvious statement
R(A)\subseteq J(A)
.

In the remainder of the text, we discuss the possibility of the three radicals not
being equal. While the equality
J(R)=A^{\perp}
heavily depends on the choice of the
Frobenius form, it is possible that the equality
R(A)=J(A)
is more universal and we
suggest various ideas that may help either to prove or disprove this assertion. We
also show that the hull-kernel topology on the set of maximal ideals of a primitive
axial algebra
A
is discrete. Towards the end, we list some open questions.

2
Axial algebras

In this section we provide the necessary background information on axial algebras.
First of all, all algebras in this note are non-associative, that is, associativity
is not assumed.

Definition 2.1
.

A
fusion law
is a pair
(\mathcal{F},\ast)
, where
\mathcal{F}
is a set (typically a subset
of a field
\mathbb{F}
) and
\ast
is a binary operation on
\mathcal{F}
with values in the set

2^{\mathcal{F}}
of all subsets of
\mathcal{F}
, that is, a map
\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{F}\to 2^{\mathcal{F}}
.

We will usually simply write
\mathcal{F}
for the fusion law
(\mathcal{F},\ast)
, i.e., the operation
\ast
will be tacitly assumed.

If
A
is a commutative algebra over a field
\mathbb{F}
and
a\in A
, we write
\mathrm{ad}_{a}

for the
adjoint action
of
a
on
A
, that is, the linear map
A\to A
given
by
u\mapsto au
. Since
A
is commutative, we do not need to distinguish between the
left and right adjoints, and so we simply speak of the adjoin action.

For
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}
, we write
A_{\lambda}(a)
for the
\lambda
-eigenspace of
\mathrm{ad}_{a}
, that is,

A_{\lambda}(a)=\{u\in A\mid au=\lambda u\}.

Note that
A_{\lambda}(a)
is non-zero if and only if
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
\mathrm{ad}_{a}
.
For a subset
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{F}
, we set

A_{\Lambda}(a)=\oplus_{\lambda\in\Lambda}A_{\lambda}(a).

Definition 2.2
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
is a fusion law. A non-zero idempotent
a\in A
is an

axis
for the fusion law
\mathcal{F}
if

(a)

A=A_{\mathcal{F}}(a)
, and

(b)

A_{\lambda}(a)A_{\mu}(a)\subseteq A_{\lambda\ast\mu}(a)
.

In other words, the adjoint action of
a
must be semisimple with all eigenvalues in

\mathcal{F}
. Furthermore, the operation
\ast
on
\mathcal{F}
controls products of eigenvectors
of
\mathrm{ad}_{a}
.

Note that the above definition requires
\mathcal{F}
to contain
1
. Indeed,

\mathrm{ad}_{a}(a)=a^{2}=a
, which means that
a\in A_{1}(a)
, and so
A_{1}(a)
is non-zero. In
what follows we always assume that
1\in\mathcal{F}
.

Definition 2.3
.

An axis
a\in A
is said to be
primitive
if
A_{1}(a)
is
1
-dimensional,
spanned by
a
.

We are now prepared for the main definition.

Definition 2.4
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
is a fusion law. An axial algebra for the fusion law

\mathcal{F}
is a pair
(A,X)
, where
A
is a commutative algebra over
\mathbb{F}
and
X\subset A
is a set of axes for
\mathcal{F}
generating
A
.

An axial algebra
(A,X)
is said to be
primitive
if all axes in
X
are
primitive.

While non-primitive axial algebras may also be interesting, large parts of the theory
of axial algebras depend on the primitivity condition. Hence below we will always
assume by default that all axial algebras we consider are primitive.

We will often speak of an axial algebra
A
without mentioning the specific set of
generating axes
X
. However,
X
is always assumed, as
A
may in general have many
different generating sets of axes for different fusion laws.

The structure theory for primitive axial algebras was developed in
[
KMS
]
. In
particular, this paper introduced the concept of the radical of a primitive axial
algebra.

Definition 2.5
.

The
radical

R(A)
of a primitive axial algebra
A
is the unique largest
ideal of
A
not containing any axes from
X
.

The existence of the radical follows from Lemma 4.2 in
[
KMS
]
, see the discussion
after Definition 4.3 there. As we consider several different radicals in this note,
we will refer to
R(A)
as the
axial radical
of
A
.

Every ideal of
A
is invariant under the adjoint action of an axis
a
. This leads
to the following result that we will need later. First, we introduce the following
notation and related terminology. Since
A=A_{\mathcal{F}}(a)=\oplus_{\lambda\in\mathcal{F}}A_{\lambda}(a)
,
every
u\in A
can be uniquely decomposed as

u=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{F}}u_{\lambda},

where
u_{\lambda}\in A_{\lambda}(a)
for all
\lambda\in\mathcal{F}
. We will call
u_{\lambda}
the

components
of
u
with respect to the axis
a
.

Lemma 2.6
.

If
I
is an ideal of
A
and
u\in I
then each component
u_{\lambda}
of
u
with
respect to an axis
a\in A
is also contained in
I
.

We will be assuming that
A
is endowed with a bilinear forms associating with the
algebra product.

Definition 2.7
.

A
Frobenius form
on an axial algebra
A
is a bilinear form
(\cdot,\cdot)

such that

(uv,w)=(u,vw)

for all
u,v,w\in A
.

We note that we are really only interested in the non-zero Frobenius forms, and in
most sources this is explicitly assumed. In the current note, for uniformity, it is
convenient to assume that our arbitrary axial algebra admits a Frobenius form, which
can only be guarantied if we allow the form to be zero.

Let us mention some basic facts related to this concept. First of all, it is well
known that a Frobenius form is always symmetric, that is,

(u,v)=(v,u)

for all
u,v\in A
.

Lemma 2.8
.

For an axis
a\in A
and
\lambda,\mu\in\mathcal{F}
,
\lambda\neq\mu
, the eigenspaces
A_{\lambda}(a)

and
A_{\mu}(a)
are orthogonal with respect to the Frobenius form on
A
.

We use the notation
A^{\perp}
for the radical of the Frobenius form. That is,

A^{\perp}=\{u\in A\mid(u,v)=0\mbox{ for all }v\in A\}.

We note that
A^{\perp}
is an ideal of
A
, and it turns out that, under some mild
extra assumptions,
A^{\perp}
coincides with the axial radical
R(A)
. Indeed, Lemma

2.8
implies that
a\in A_{1}(a)
is orthogonal to all of

A_{\mathcal{F}-\{1\}}(a)
. Hence, we have the following.

Lemma 2.9
.

If an axis
a\in A
is primitive then
a\in A^{\perp}
if and only if
(a,a)=0
.

This, in turn, implies the desired claim.

Lemma 2.10
.

If the Frobenius form on a primitive axial algebra
A
has the property that

(a,a)\neq 0
for all
a\in X
then the radical
R(A)
of the algebra coincides with
the radical of the form,
A^{\perp}
.

Finally, let us discuss the direct sum construction for axial algebras. Recall that,
given algebras
A_{j}
,
j\in J
, we can define the direct sum algebra

A=\oplus_{j\in J}A_{j},

where
A_{i}A_{j}=0
for all
i,j\in J
,
i\neq j
.

First let us observe where primitive axes can lie in a direct product algebra.

Lemma 2.11
.

If
a
is a primitive axis in
A=\oplus_{j\in J}A_{j}
then
a\in A_{j}
for some

j\in J
.

Indeed, decompose
a=\sum_{j\in J}a_{j}
, with each
a_{j}\in A_{j}
, and note that each

a_{j}
is contained in the
1
-eigenspace of
\mathrm{ad}_{a}
. Clearly, this means that only
one
a_{j}
is non-zero and so
a=a_{j}\in A_{j}
.

Suppose now that each
A_{j}
is an axial algebra for the same fusion law

\mathcal{F}
, that is, we have a generating set of axes
X_{j}
in each

A_{j}
. Set

X=\cup_{j\in J}X_{j}.

Comparing with Lemma
2.11
, this is the only possible way to introduce on

A
the structure of a primitive axial algebra. Hence, the natural question to ask
is: when is the pair
(A,X)
an axial algebra for the fusion law
\mathcal{F}
?

Lemma 2.12
.

When
|J|\geq 2
, the pair
(A,X)
is an axial algebra for the
fusion law
\mathcal{F}
if and only if
0\in\mathcal{F}
and, additionally,
0\in 0\ast 0
.

Coincidentally, the same condition,
0\in\mathcal{F}
and
0\in 0\ast 0
, is required for

A\not\cong\mathbb{F}
to be unital, i.e., to have a multiplicative identity element.

3
Blocks

In
[
KMS
]
, sum decompositions of primitive axial algebras, slightly generalizing
direct sum decompositions, were investigated. In this note we take a more general
viewpoint.

Definition 3.1
.

For an axial algebra
(A,X)
and an axis
a\in X
, the
block

I_{a}
of
A

corresponding to
a
is defined as the ideal generated by
a
, i.e., the smallest
ideal of
A
containing
a
.

As the sum of ideals is again an ideal, we have the following observation.

Lemma 3.2
.

We have that
A=\sum_{a\in X}I_{a}
.

Clearly, the right side is an ideal, and hence a subalgebra containing all of
X
, so
it must coincide with
A
, as
X
generates
A
.

Definition 3.3
.

We call an axial algebra
decomposable
if it is the sum of its proper ideals.

An indecomposable algebra has a unique maximal ideal, the sum of all its proper
ideals.

In view of Lemma
3.2
, if an axial algebra
A
is indecomposable
then
A=I_{a}
for some
a\in X
. We will now show that in the primitive case the
converse is also true. First, we broadly generalize Lemma
2.11
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose that an algebra
A
is decomposed as a sum of its ideals:

A=\sum_{j\in J}I_{j}.

If
a\in A
is a primitive axis then
a\in I_{j}
for some
j\in J
.

Proof.

Ad absurdum
, suppose that
a\notin I_{j}
for all
j\in J
. Take some
j\in J

and
u\in I_{j}
. Note that by the primitivity of
a
, the component
u_{1}
, taken with
respect to
a
, is a multiple of
a
. In view of Lemma
2.6
,
u_{1}=0
since

a\notin I_{j}
. It follows that
u\in A_{\mathcal{F}-\{1\}}(a)
, and so
I_{j}\subseteq A_{\mathcal{F}-\{1\}}(a)
. Since this is true for all
j\in J
, we conclude that all
I_{j}
are
contained in
A_{\mathcal{F}-\{1\}}(a)
, and so
A=\sum_{j\in J}I_{j}\subseteq A_{\mathcal{F}-\{1\}}(a)
; clearly a contradiction.
∎

Consequently, we have the following.

Corollary 3.5
.

If
A=I_{a}
for some
a\in X
then
A
is indecomposable.

As a word of warning, we cannot claim that each block
I_{a}
is itself an axial
algebra. Indeed, the axes from
X\cap I_{a}
may in principle generate a proper
subalgebra of
I_{a}
. We also cannot claim that every block is indecomposable, as we cannot assume that every ideal of a block is an ideal of the whole algebra
A
.

Can distinct blocks be contained in one another? This kind of questions can be
formulated and investigated using the natural domination relation on the set of axes

X
.

Definition 3.6
.

For axes
a,b\in X
,
a

dominates

b
if
I_{b}\subseteq I_{a}
. Two axes
a
and

b
are
equivalent
if they dominate each other, that is, if
I_{a}=I_{b}
.

Hence, the above question amounts to asking: can dominance be non-symmetric; i.e.,
can it not be an equivalence relation?

The concept of the projection graph was introduced in
[
KMS
]
.

Definition 3.7
.

The
projection digraph
of a primitive axial algebra
(A,X)
has
X
as its set
of vertices. For
a,b\in X
,
a\neq b
, there is an arc from
a
to
b
if and only
if the projection of
a
to
A_{1}(b)
is non-zero. That is,
a_{1}\neq 0
, where
a_{1}

is the
1
-component of
a
with respect to
b
.

As
a_{1}
is a multiple of
b
, since
b
is primitive, we have that
b\in I_{a}
, that
is
a
dominates
b
. As dominance is transitive, we have the following.

Lemma 3.8
.

The axis
a
dominates all axes
b
that can be reached from
a
via oriented paths
in the projection graph.

When two axes are connected by arcs in both directions, we will say that they are
connected by an undirected
edge
. Such two axes are clearly equivalent, and
so we also have the following.

Lemma 3.9
.

If two vertices
a
and
b
are connected in the projection graph via a path
consisting entirely of undirected edges then
a
and
b
are equivalent.

Finally, let us mention what happens in the case where the Frobenius from has the
property that all axes
a\in X
are non-singular, i.e.,
(a,a)\neq 0
for all
a\in X
. In this case, an arc from
a
to
b
exists if and only if
(a,b)\neq 0
. That
is, the projection digraph turns into a
non-orthogonality
graph, as all arcs
are parts of undirected edges. Hence all axes within the same connected component of
the non-orthogonality graph are equivalent. Note that this still leaves the
possibility of non-symmetric domination between axes from different components.

4
Jacobson radical

We start by repeating the definition already stated in the introduction.

Definition 4.1
.

The
Jacobson radical

J(A)
of an axial algebra
A
is the intersection of all
maximal ideals of
A
.

If
A
has no maximal ideals then we simply take
J(A)=A
.

There seems to be no general consensus in the literature how to define the Jacobson
radical for a general non-associative algebra. For the class of Jordan algebras,
closely related to axial algebras, McCrimmon
[
McC
]
defined the quasi-regular
Jacobson radical. Zelmanov introduced the concept of a primitive unital Jordan
algebra and showed that the Jacobson radical is the intersection of primitive
ideals (cores of maximal quadratic ideals)
[
Z
]
. Hogben and McCrimmon

[
HMcC
]
extended this characterization of the Jacobson radical to arbitrary
Jordan algebras. We wonder whether these definitions would simplify under the
axial assumption and be closer to what we consider in this paper.

In the remainder of this section, we prove half of Theorem
1.1
.

Proposition 4.2
.

The axial radical
R(A)
of a primitive axial algebra
A
is contained in
the Jacobson radical
J(A)
.

Proof.

By contradiction, suppose that the axial radical
R=R(A)
of
A
is not contained in

J(A)
. Then
A
has a maximal ideal
I
not containing
R
. Note that
R+I=A
, since

I
is a maximal ideal. According to Lemma
3.4
, every
a\in X
is contained in

R
or in
I
. However, none of them can be in
R
by the definition of the axial
radical. Hence
X\subseteq I
and so also
A\subseteq I
, as
X
generates
A
; a
contradiction.
∎

5
Ideal complement

We now start proving the other half of Theorem
1.1
, concerning the Frobenius
form radical
A^{\perp}
. In this section, we assume that the Frobenius from

(\cdot,\cdot)
on a primitive axial algebra
A
is non-degenerate, that is,

A^{\perp}=0
. By Lemma
2.9
,
(a,a)\neq 0
for all
a\in X
, and this, in turn,
implies, according to Lemma
2.10
, that the axial radical of
A
coincides with

A^{\perp}
, and so it is also trivial.

Suppose
U\neq 0
is an ideal of
A
. Then
U
must contain some axes from
X
, as

R(A)
is trivial. Define
X_{1}=X\cap U
and
X_{2}=X\setminus X_{1}
.

Recall from Lemma
2.6
that, for
a\in X
and
u\in U
, every component of

u
with respect to
a
is also contained in
U
. In particular, if
a\in X_{2}
then
the component
u_{1}
is zero, as it must be a multiple of
a
. Now it follows from
Lemma
2.8
that
(a,u)=0
for all
a\in X_{2}
and
u\in U
.

Consider the magma
M_{i}
of all products of the axes from
X_{i}
,
i=1,2
.

Lemma 5.1
.

For all
m\in M_{2}
, we have that
(U,m)=0
.

Proof.

By contradiction, suppose that we have
m\in M_{2}
that are not orthogonal to
U
.
Select such a product
m
with the smallest possible number of factors. We have seen
that all
a\in X_{2}
are orthogonal to
U
, so
m
cannot be just an axis. Hence

m=m^{\prime}m^{\prime\prime}
for some
m^{\prime},m^{\prime\prime}\in M_{2}
. Therefore,
(U,m^{\prime}m^{\prime\prime})=(Um^{\prime},m^{\prime\prime})=0
since

Um^{\prime}\subseteq U
and
m^{\prime\prime}\in M_{2}
has fewer factors compared to
m
. The
contradiction proves the claim.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

For all
m_{1}\in M_{1}
and
m_{2}\in M_{2}
, we have that
m_{1}m_{2}=0
.

Proof.

Indeed, note that
M_{1}\subseteq U
. Therefore,
(A,m_{1}m_{2})=(Am_{1},m_{2})=0
by Lemma

5.1
since
Am_{1}\subseteq U
. Since the Frobenius form is
non-degenerate, we conclude that
m_{1}m_{2}=0
.
∎

Let
A_{i}
be the subalgebra generated by
X_{i}
,
i=1,2
. Then
A_{i}
is spanned by

M_{i}
.

Proposition 5.3
.

We have that
A=A_{1}+A_{2}=A_{1}\oplus A_{2}
. Furthermore,
U=A_{1}
.

Proof.

Clearly, for
u_{1}\in A_{1}
and
u_{2}\in A_{2}
, we have that
u_{1}u_{2}=0
. This implies
that
A_{1}+A_{2}
is closed for products and so it is a subalgebra of
A
. Since

A_{1}+A_{2}
contains
X=X_{1}\cup X_{2}
, which generates
A
, it follows that
A=A_{1}+A_{2}
.
In particular, both
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are ideals of
A
. This means that
I=A_{1}\cap A_{2}
is also an ideal of
A
. Now note that
I^{2}=II\subseteq A_{1}A_{2}=0
, that is,
I

is a nilpotent ideal. Consequently,
I
contains no non-zero idempotents, and in
particular, it does not contain any axes from
X
. Therefore,
I\subseteq R(A)=0
. We
have shown that
A_{1}\cap A_{2}=0
, that is,
A=A_{1}\oplus A_{2}
.

Finally, if
U
is bigger than
A_{1}
then consider
J=U\cap A_{2}\neq 0
. We again have
that
J
is an ideal and, furthermore,
J
contains no axes from
X_{1}
since

J\subseteq A_{2}
. Also,
J
contains no axes from
X_{2}
since
J\subseteq U
and

X_{2}
is disjoint from
U
. Again, this means that
J
is disjoint from the entire
set of axes
X
, that is,
J
is contained in
R(A)
, which is trivial. The
contradiction proves that
U=A_{1}
, as claimed.
∎

6
Semisimplicity

We continue assuming that
A
is a primitive axial algebra with a non-degenerate
Frobenius from.

Recall that, for
a\in X
, the block
I_{a}
is the ideal generated by
a
.

Lemma 6.1
.

If
b\in X
and
b\in I_{a}
then
I_{b}=I_{a}
.

Proof.

Clearly,
I_{b}\subseteq I_{a}
. By contradiction, suppose that
I_{b}\neq I_{a}
. Then

a\notin I_{b}
. We set
U=I_{b}
and define
X_{i}
,
M_{i}
, and
A_{i}
,
i=1,2
, as in
Section
5
. Then
a\in X_{2}\subset A_{2}
and hence also
I_{a}\subset A_{2}
.
However, this means (see Proposition
5.3
) that
b\in A_{1}\cap A_{2}=0
,
which is a contradiction.
∎

This means that the domination relation, as introduced in section
3
,
is symmetric in our algebra
A
, and hence dominance in
A
is an equivalence
relation. Let
C
be the set of equivalence classes. For
c\in C
, we define
I_{c}

as
I_{a}
for an arbitrary axis
a\in c
.

Proposition 6.2
.

For
c,c^{\prime}\in C
,
c\neq c^{\prime}
, we have that
I_{c}I_{c^{\prime}}=0
. Furthermore, each
(I_{c},c)

is a simple primitive axial algebra and

A=\oplus_{c\in C}I_{c}.

Proof.

The first claim follows from Proposition
5.3
since the axes from
c^{\prime}

are not contained in
U=I_{c}
. Furthermore, again by Proposition
5.3
,

I_{c}=A_{1}
is a primitive axial algebra generated by
X_{1}=I_{c}\cap X=c
. Since
I_{c}
is
a direct summand of
A
, every ideal of
I_{c}
is an ideal of
A
and, clearly, if
this ideal is proper in
I_{c}
then it cannot contain any axes by Lemma
6.1
.
Therefore, such an ideal would be in the radical
R(A)
of
A
, hence trivial. We
have shown that each
I_{c}
is a simple primitive axial algebra.

By Lemma
3.2
, the sum of all
I_{c}
,
c\in C
, is
A
. It
remains to see that this sum is direct. Suppose that we have
0=\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}
,
where
u_{i}\in I_{c_{i}}
for distinct
c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{k}\in C
. Take
U=I_{c_{k}}
.
Then, using the notation from Section
5
,
I_{c_{k}}=A_{1}
and

I_{c_{1}},I_{c_{2}},\ldots,I_{c_{k-1}}\subseteq A_{2}
, as in Proposition
5.3
,
and so
u_{k}=0
, since
A_{1}\cap A_{2}=0
. Clearly, this means that all
u_{i}
are zero,
and this yields the direct sum claim.
∎

We have shown that
A
is a semisimple algebra, a direct sum of simple algebras.
We can now define
J_{c}
as the sum of all blocks
I_{c^{\prime}}
,
c^{\prime}\neq c
. Clearly,

A=I_{c}\oplus J_{c}
, and so
J_{c}
is a maximal ideal, since
A/J_{c}\cong I_{c}
is simple.
We note that the intersection of all the maximal ideals
J_{c}
is trivial, which gives
us the following.

Corollary 6.3
.

We have that
J(A)=0
.

7
Corollaries

Let us now list several applications of Proposition
6.2
and Corollary

6.3
. We start with the general statement completing the proof of
Theorem
1.1
.

Corollary 7.1
.

Suppose that
A
is an axial algebra admitting a Frobenius form. Then
A/A^{\perp}
is
semisimple and so
J(A)\subseteq A^{\perp}
.

Indeed,
A/A^{\perp}
is a primitive axial algebra with a non-degenerate Frobenius
form, and hence the claim follows from Proposition
5.3
and Corollary

6.3
.

An interesting case arises when the fusion law
\mathcal{F}
does not allow direct sums. (See
Lemma
2.12
and the discussion after it.)

Corollary 7.2
.

Suppose that either
0\notin\mathcal{F}
or
0\in\mathcal{F}
but
0\notin 0\ast 0
. If an axial
algebra
A
admits a Frobenius form then
A/A^{\perp}
is a simple algebra.

Clearly, this follows from Corollary
7.1
and Lemma
2.12
.

We have already mentioned in the introduction that, whenever the Frobenius form
satisfies the additional property that
(a,a)\neq 0
for all
a\in X
(i.e., all
generating axes are non-singular for the Frobenius form), the chain of inclusions
from Theorem
1.1
turns into an equality:
R(A)=J(A)=A^{\perp}
. We now mention
two specific classes of axial algebras where this is the case.

One of the most well studied classes of axial algebras is the class of algebras of
Jordan type
\eta
, which are primitive axial algebras for the fusion law

.

. In

8
Hull-kernel topology

The hull-kernel topology is a natural topology on the set of all maximal ideals. It
is defined as follows. Let
\mathcal{M}
be the set of all maximal ideals of
A
.

Definition 8.1
.

For a set
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
, its closure under the hull-kernel topology is

\bar{\mathcal{S}}=\{J\in\mathcal{M}\mid J\supseteq K\},

where
K=\cap_{I\in\mathcal{S}}I
.

For general rings
A
, the hull-kernel topology may be quite non-trivial. We will see
that this is not the case for primitive axial algebras
A
.

We start by reproving the claims in Proposition
6.2
under a weaker
assumption. As in Section
6
, let
C
be the set of equivalence
classes for the mutual dominance equivalence relation on the set of generating axes

X
. Then
C
can be used for indexing all blocks: for an equivalence class
c\in C
,
we define
I_{c}=I_{a}
for an arbitrary axis
a\in c
.

Proposition 8.2
.

Let
A
be a primitive axial algebra with
J(A)=0
. Then

(a)

every block
I_{c}
,
c\in C
, is a simple primitive axial algebra; and

(b)

A=\oplus_{c\in C}I_{c}
.

Proof.

We first establish (a). Let
I=I_{c}
be an arbitrary block. Since
J(A)=0
and
I\neq 0
, there exists a maximal ideal
J
not containing
I
. Then
A=I+J
, since
J
is
maximal. Furthermore,
I/I\cap J\cong I+J/J=A/J
is a simple algebra, which means
that
I\cap J
is a maximal ideal of
I
. If
J^{\prime}
is any other maximal ideal of
A

then either
I\subseteq J^{\prime}
or
I\not\subseteq J^{\prime}
and then, similarly,
I\cap J^{\prime}
is
a maximal ideal of
I
.

We claim that necessarily
I\cap J=I\cap J^{\prime}
. Suppose that this is not the case, that
is,
I\cap J\neq I\cap J^{\prime}
. Then
I=I\cap J+I\cap J^{\prime}
. By Lemma
3.4
,
a\in I\cap J
or
a\in I\cap J^{\prime}
. In either case, we get a contradiction, because
I
is
not the smallest ideal of
A
containing
a
. Thus,
I\cap J=I\cap J^{\prime}
.

We have shown that
I\cap J
is contained in all other maximal ideals
J^{\prime}
of
A
.
Hence,
I\cap J\subseteq J(A)=0
. This gives us that
A=I\oplus J
, since

IJ\subseteq I\cap J=0
. However, this means that every ideal of
I
is an ideal of

A
. If
K\neq 0
is a proper ideal of
I
then
K+J>J
is a proper ideal of
A
; a
contradiction, since
J
is a maximal ideal. This shows that
I
has no proper non-
zero ideals, that is,
I
is simple, proving (a).

For (b), we first note that, for distinct blocks
I_{c}
and
I_{c^{\prime}}
, we have that

I_{c}\cap I_{c}^{\prime}=0
, as it is an ideal in both
I_{c}
and
I_{c^{\prime}}
. Hence also

I_{c}I_{c^{\prime}}\subseteq I_{c}\cap I_{c^{\prime}}=0
.

We already know by Lemma
3.2
that
A=\sum_{c\in C}I_{c}
. To see
that this is a direct sum, suppose we have a non-trivial linear relation

\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}=0
, where
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{k}
are elements of pairwise distinct
blocks
I_{c_{1}},I_{c_{2}},\ldots,I_{c_{k}}
. Assuming that
k
is smallest possible, we
must have that each
u_{i}
is non-zero. Furthermore,
u_{k}=-u_{1}-u_{2}-\ldots-u_{k-1}\in J=I_{c_{1}}+I_{c_{2}}+\ldots+I_{c_{k-1}}
. On the one hand, this means that
I_{c_{k}}\cap J\neq 0
and, since
I_{c_{k}}
is simple, this yields
I_{c_{k}}\subseteq J
. On the
other hand,
I_{c_{k}}J=0
by the previous paragraph. Taking an axis
a\in c_{k}
, we now
see that
a=a^{2}\in I_{c_{k}}J=0
; this contradiction proves (b).
∎

As a consequence, we have the following.

Corollary 8.3
.

If
A
is a primitive axial algebra then
A/J(A)
is a direct sum of simple primitive
axial algebras.

We can now identify the hull-kernel topology of
A
.

Proposition 8.4
.

The hull-kernel topology of a primitive axial algebra
A
is discrete.

This is a consequence of Corollary
8.3
. Clearly, there is a natural
bijective correspondence between the maximal ideals of
A
and of
\bar{A}=A/J(A)
.
Hence we can assume that
J(A)=0
and so
A=\oplus_{c\in C}I_{c}
is a direct sum of
simple algebras. From this, it can be shown that every ideal of
A
is of the form

\oplus_{c\in S}I_{c}
for a subset
S\subseteq C
. Consequently, the lattice of all
ideals of
A
is the same as the lattice of all subsets of
C
, and this implies
in turn that the hull-kernel topology is discrete. Note that this argument is quite
general and not specific to axial algebras. Hence we skip the details.

9
Further questions

In this final section, we discuss our results and mention some related questions
that remain open. Our main resut is that in every primitive axial algebra
(A,X)

admitting a Frobenius form the three radicals, the form radical
A^{\perp}
, the
Jacobson radical
J(A)
and the axial radical
R(A)
are included in one another as
follows:

R(A)\subseteq J(A)\subseteq A^{\perp}.

Furthermore, under a mild and natural extra condition that
(a,a)\neq 0
for all

a\in X
, we have that
R=J(A)=A^{\perp}
. Can the same ultimate conclusion be reached
without this extra condition? Note that
A^{\perp}
can be artificially inflated.
Indeed, the Frobenius form induced on the semisimple factor algebra
A/J(A)
can be
chosen individually on each simple summand. In particular, we can make the Frobenius
form zero on all summands but one, in which case
A^{\perp}
is definitely bigger than

J(A)
as long as
A/J(A)
is not simple. Hence, we do not believe that the equality

J(A)=A^{\perp}
can be shown without the additional condition above.

On the other hand, we do not see any simple tricks allowing to make
R(A)
and
J(A)

distinct. Based on this we pose the following question.

Question 9.1
.

Is it true that the axial radical
R(A)
and the Jacobson radical
J(A)
coincide in
every primitive axial algebra? Equivalently, is it true that
J(A)
never contains
primitive axes?

Note that here we are not assuming that
A
admits a Frobenius form, as it seems
unrelated to the main point of this question. In fact, the existence of a non-zero
Frobenius form seems to be a property of the factor algebra
A/J(A)
. Namely,
A

admits such a Frobenius form if and only if at least one simple summand of
A/J(A)

admits such a form.

Related to the above question are the following questions concerning the structure of
blocks and the domination relation.

Question 9.2
.

Is it true that each axial block is indecomposable?

Question 9.3
.

Can there be non-trivial embeddings between blocks, i.e., can the dominance relation
to be non-symmetric?

We also want to mention the relation of our results and the above questions to the
concept of the non-annihilation graph introduced in
[
KMS
]
.

Definition 9.4
.

Suppose that
(A,X)
is a primitive axial algebra. The
non-annihilation graph

\Delta
has
X
as its set of vertices and distinct
a,b\in X
are adjacent in the
non-annihilation graph if and only if
ab\neq 0
.

The following question was first posed in
[
KMS
]
in the context of Majorana
algebras and then also repeated in a more general seting in the survey
[
MS
]
.

Question 9.5
.

Is it true that in the finest (direct) sum decomposition of a primitive axial algebra
the summands correspond to the connected components of the non-annihilation graph

\Delta
?

When
A
is decomposed as a direct sum of primitive axial algebras then, clearly,
each connected component of
\Delta
is fully connected in one of the summand algebras.
hence the issue is whether an indecomposable primitive axial algebra can have a
disconnected non-annihilation graph
\Delta
.

We have shown that, in the class of Majorana algebras, indecomposable algebras are
simple. Hence the above question is equivalent to the following.

Question 9.6
.

Is it true that the non-annihilation graph
\Delta
of a simple Majorana algebra is
necessarily connected?
