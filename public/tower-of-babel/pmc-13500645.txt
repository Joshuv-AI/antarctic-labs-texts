Mapping gene expression dynamics to developmental phenotypes with information entropy analysis
Authors: Ben Ansbacher, Malachy Guzman, Jordi Garcia-Ojalvo, Arjendu K. Pattanayak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of multicellular organisms entails a deep connection between time-dependent biochemical processes taking place at the subcellular level and the resulting macroscopic phenotypes that arise in populations of up to trillions of cells. Constructing a statistical mechanics of developmental processes would help to understand how microscopic genotypes map onto macroscopic phenotypes, a general goal across biology. Here, we present an attempt in this direction in the context of the fruit fly, Drosophila melanogaster. Applying a variety of information-theoretic measures to public transcriptomics datasets of whole fly embryos during development, we show that the global temporal dynamics of gene expression can be understood as a process that probabilistically guides embryonic dynamics across macroscopic phenotypic stages. In particular, our results suggest signatures of irreversibility in the information complexity of transcriptomic dynamics, as measured mainly by the permutation entropy of indexed ensembles (PI entropy). We also show that the dynamics of PI entropy correlate strongly with developmental stages. Overall, this is a test case in applying information complexity analysis to relate the statistical mechanics of biomarkers to macroscopic developmental dynamics.

Introduction

The development of a living system from a single cell to a fully formed organism can be interpreted as the mapping over time of a large number of microscopic degrees of freedom at the molecular level, the genotype, into a smaller number of macroscopic features at the supracellular level that determine the organism’s form and function, the phenotype. This process is driven by carefully choreographed gene expression in space and time, the details of which have been mostly unraveled by developmental and molecular biologists over the last few decades1. It is now known, for instance, that cell-fate decisions and morphogenesis are two tightly coordinated processes at different scales (cells and tissues, respectively), whose outcome is highly predictable at the scale of the organism2. The specific regulatory mechanisms underlying this coordination are, however, highly contingent on both the developmental stage of the organism and the species to which the organism belongs. The question still remains of whether there are fundamental principles underlying this developmental genotype–phenotype mapping3.

For instance, it remains puzzling how the remarkably consistent organization exhibited by developing organisms emerges from random sources of energy, as well as via biochemical processes that are themselves stochastic4. We argue that one way of attacking this puzzle is through a thermodynamic and statistical physics framework, using concepts connecting dynamical informational complexity to physical entropy. We hypothesize that development proceeds as a time-directed process affecting both the organism and its environment simultaneously, driven by transitions—sometimes smooth and occasionally abrupt—between different kinds of non-equilibrium thermodynamic steady states. Specifically, we consider that the increase in physical entropy production with time in a developing organism, accompanied by an increase in biological organization, allows us to consider both as an irreversible evolution between very different thermodynamic states. This trajectory should be visible in the dynamics of microscopic system properties, thermodynamic properties related to energy usage, as well as macroscopic biological characteristics, all when treated with the appropriate statistical mechanics perspectives5. Thus, appropriate system characteristics should show what we term ‘irreversibility’ or a preferred direction in time.

Techniques and ideas used to characterize this irreversibility in the statistical mechanics of ensembles in physics6–8 should reveal such behavior in information theoretic behavior of ‘microscopic’ biological properties. In the physics context, we seek to characterize the irreversible change from a sharply localized ensemble of initial conditions to an equilibrium or non-equilibrium steady-state (‘thermalization’), and we expect the entropy of the system to increase in time. For developmental biology, on the other hand, while there is also a strong direction in time, it is fundamentally inverted in perspective, which we associate with a growth of correlations and irreversible change of state relative to a final well-defined state9. During development, this means that the appropriate reference state against which dynamics should be measured is the final state, not the initial state9–11. Moreover, our hypothesis implies that the dynamics of these information-theoretic measures should be strongly correlated with macroscopic bulk properties. Below we present a test case for these two sub-hypotheses about irreversibility and correlation between such microscopic and microscopic properties using publicly available bulk transcriptomics data from whole Drosophila melanogaster embryos, measured at multiple stages of development12,13. Drosophila has served as a primary model organism in genetics and development, widely used for over a century1. Below, we use the expression of individual genes as our ensemble, constructed using publicly available bulk gene expression data and characterized with the mean expression and particular information-theoretic constructions: The Shannon entropy, a specialized form of the Kullback-Leibler (KL) entropy, as well as the PI entropy discussed below in more detail. We also use the ensemble-averaged developmental stage (discussed in more detail below) from the same experiments12,13 as our macroscopic phenotypic property. Our results show signatures of irreversibility over the entire period of embryonic development, visible in information-theoretic representations of the gene expression dynamics, which we sketch here and discuss in more detail below.

In particular, the PI entropy8, or Π(t), works by creating a reference distribution of genes indexed by their rank-order in expression value at the final timepoint. At every other timepoint, each gene’s expression value is moved up or down relative to this indexing, resulting in a new distribution with fluctuations relative to the reference one. As described in the sections that follow, Π(t) quantifies the behavior of these fluctuations as a function of time, displaying thermalization behavior. We find that this distribution is initially well-shuffled and wildly fluctuating relative to the final distribution, and settles through continued shuffling as time progresses, resulting in increasing smoothness relative to the final timepoint. In other words, the ensemble relaxes as the shuffling happens on progressively smaller scales along both the expression and indexing axis as the system approaches its ‘final’ state at the end of development.

We also find that the information-theoretic dynamics described above correlate strongly with a phenotypic measure of the macroscopic developmental stage. The correlation is remarkable given the relative crudeness of the data and the degree to which we are using proxies across multiple scales of coarse-graining for both microscopic and macroscopic properties. Taken together, these analyses of gene expression dynamics unveil statistical mechanical trajectories that both show irreversible convergence of the ensemble dynamics of the microscopic determinants of development and correlate with macroscopic properties. The time evolution of these two properties is consistent with the broader idea of development as a sequence of transitions between non-equilibrium steady states. In particular, it seems appropriate to consider expression levels to be playing the role of ‘energy’, while the PI plays the role of the related ‘entropy.’

We also use other ways of filtering the data to probe details of the thermodynamic trajectory. This includes analyzing subsets of genes that display high dynamical variability, using eight groups of tightly co-expressed/co-regulated genes that have been previously identified12. These gene subsets show a variety of expression dynamics within the overall behavior described above: some start high in expression and decrease steadily as time progresses, others start and end at low expression values with a blip of activity in between, and some others start low and end high. We see in these individual subset dynamics, as in the global dynamics, that Π tends to be higher when mean expression values are higher. The results obtained from the analysis of both the global transcriptome and the expression of the gene subsets described above are consistent with more ‘entropy’ being available at higher ‘energy’ levels. The overall irreversibility in the transition between the initial and final developmental states thus proceeds via a settling to lower mean expression (dissipation to lower ’energy’) in gene expression space, and a separate ‘thermalization’ time-scale visible in Π dynamics, where the ensemble expression dynamics is seen to settle down as it approaches the final time, without any overall change in mean expression.

We begin by describing our Methods, including the acquisition and characteristics of the gene expression data itself, as well as our metrics and their interpretation. In Results, we present global irreversibility and correlations between microscopic RNA measurements and macroscopic phenotypes, as well as a focused look at temporally filtered ‘active’ gene subclasses, and what this means for underlying expression dynamics in gene regulatory networks. We also discuss what we learn by filtering by gene identity, and what the overall dynamics say about developmental stages and irreversibility. We conclude with a short summarizing discussion of the interpretations of these results and on prospective work, as well as the broader applicability of information-theoretic techniques in developmental biology.

It is thus possible to state that we see a trajectory in gene information complexity as a function of time during development. Our hypothesis was that development was a transition from one ‘non-equilibrium steady-state’ to another of the organism, and that this gene information complexity should have made a similar quantifiable transition. To be clear, all processes happening at all times are thermodynamically far from equilibrium in the traditional sense, but we are searching for meta-signatures of gene expression complexity that indicate how the expression (‘energy’) budgeting is being prioritized. As a test case of how a gene expression budget that did not have any change in complexity over time behaves under PI entropy, we computed PI entropy curves for randomly generated data under similar constraints as the Drosophila data. Please see Supplementary Fig. 2 for more details. This, combined with our analysis of the effect of indexing on different time points throughout development (also detailed in the Supplementary Fig. 1), allows us to argue that Figs. 2 and 3 show an irreversible change in gene expression complexity over the development of the Drosophila embryo.

Hooper et al.12 qualitatively assign each embryo to one of 17 phenotypic stages at each timepoint. Assuming that each of these stages represents a developmental macrostate, we compute the mean phenotypic stage of the embryo population at each timepoint. This is our macroscopic observable M(t), which we normalize to [0, 1]. Figure 4 shows the progression through phenotypic stages as well as Π(t) as functions of time. We quantify this correlation more concretely, finding that both mean expression and Shannon entropy correlate poorly with phenotypic stage upon qualitative inspection, and achieve relatively low mutual information values of approximately 0.76 and 0.72 nats, respectively (Fig. 5A, B). This is somewhat surprising, as Shannon entropy is among the most common methods for quantifying the complexity of a distribution. AKL and PI entropy correlate more strongly with phenotypic stage both qualitatively and quantitatively (Fig. 5C, D); PI entropy achieves the highest mutual information score of I ≈ 0.98 nats. Note that mutual information does not have a maximum of 1 and is not analogous to a Pearson correlation of 0.98, which would not capture the nonlinear relationships in the data. Rather, we can interpret these mutual information values in relation to their upper bound, given by the lesser Shannon entropy of the two distributions being compared. We find the lowest entropy among the five variables we consider (mean phenotypic stage, mean expression, Shannon entropy of expression, approach Kullback–Leibler, and PI entropy) to be that of mean phenotypic stage, approximately 1.376 nats (computed using the Freedman-Diaconis rule for discretization, see ‘Methods’). Thus I ≤ 1.376 nats for each pair of variables that we consider in Fig. 5. Relative of their maximum value of 1.376 nats, AKL and PI achieve relative mutual information values of 0.67 and 0.71, respectively. On the other hand, mean expression and Shannon entropy have relative mutual information values of 0.55 and 0.52, respectively. Both AKL and PI entropy broadly appear to have a negative exponential relationship with phenotypic stage, but we refrained from explicitly fitting functions to these data due to the current lack of mechanistic understanding that would provide physical meaning to such an equation (see ‘Discussion’). The observed degree of correlation is remarkable considering the relatively coarse-grained nature of our data and the degree to which we are using proxies across multiple scales of coarse-graining. Using a finer-scale microscopic quantity, like single-cell RNAseq with embryo identity retained, we would expect even stronger correlations.

The correlation between the overall dynamics of the microscopic PI and the macroscopic mean phenotypic developmental stage shown in Fig. 5C, D is our central result. This, as well as the form of the time evolution of both these quantities, supports our hypothesis that development is a time-directed process between non-equilibrium thermodynamic states, and that the corresponding thermodynamic trajectory is independently visible in both microscopic system state properties and macroscopic system properties.

A second way of understanding these results is as validating the use of gene expression levels as a microscopic ‘coordinate’ to characterize changes in biological state space. In particular, the behavior above suggests that μ can be thought of as a proxy for a concept such as energy, which is further reinforced by the connection between PI dynamics (indexed along the gene expression axis) and entropy. In other words, we see irreversible convergence of the ensemble dynamics of gene expression levels relative to a final indexed state. These metrics also show broad distinguishability between gene expression states at various stages, as well as correlation with macroscopic dynamics.

This plateau in the data suggests that the developmental program has fixed on a particular temporal trajectory of gene expression, captured by Π. The overall irreversibility in the transition between the initial and final states of this developmental process can be thought of as proceeding first via a dissipation in expression level space, along with a separate diffusive thermalization timescale at that final level.

Interestingly, the point at which this split occurs and the system transitions into the plateau corresponds to the 15th stage of embryogenesis, during which dorsal closure is completed. Dorsal closure is the final morphogenetic process of embryogenesis, when the large gap in the dorsal epithelium produced by germ band retraction is sealed1,15. In real time, this process occurs about halfway through embryogenesis, but is one of the final processes in terms of the 17 stages. The fact that PI entropy rapidly decreases once major morphogenetic movements are complete is further suggestive of the link between these information-theoretic characterizations of the transcriptome and the macroscopic developmental processes the transcriptome drives.

We have also considered gene subsets created by grouping genes by function (Fig. 9), sourcing these functional groups from the GLAD database16. All the curves shown in Fig. 9 share common qualitative features. Interestingly, the overall increase in irreversibility signatures after about the 9th timepoint, visible as a spike in most gene groups, is temporally associated with the beginning of dorsal closure under standard conditions1,15. This is the initiation of a critical transition in cell-fate diversity and a symmetry-breaking event in the developmental process, and PI entropy appears to capture signatures of these events. Each gene class roughly follows the dynamical trend of the whole transcriptome. However, it is worth considering the two outlier gene groups, namely ribosomal genes, which maintain a high PI entropy right until the final reference point, and ion channel genes, which have a low entropy throughout development. It would be tempting to conclude that the high PI levels of ribosomal genes reflect the embryo’s need to maintain high translational flexibility as a downstream method of gene regulation, which has been shown to play an especially important role during development17,18. Similarly, the low PI of genes encoding ion channels could reflect the need for stable ion channel production rates due to the relevance of electrical signaling in development and pathologies associated with abnormal channel expression19. Discussion

This work serves as a test case for the broader statistical biophysics program of correlating dynamics of microscopic biomarkers, broadly construed, with macroscopic phenotypic behavior, and in particular for the hypothesis that such a correlation might be most easily found when thinking of development as a thermodynamic transition between non-equilibrium steady states. Our results suggest a transition along a sequence of such states as the Drosophila embryo develops. Further progress along this direction will require quantifying more carefully the specific functional form of the correlations between different scales, and in particular, working on understanding how these developmental trajectories vary at different scales of description, as discussed earlier. For example, it should be possible to perform such analyses more precisely using finer resolution data such as single-cell RNAseq. Similarly, better resolved phenotypic descriptions should be attainable experimentally, for example, by examining cell-type diversity as a function of time. It would be generally useful to understand how the system’s behavior or properties add or contribute at each scale. Further steps will include investigating the possible universality or individuality of these thermodynamic trajectories across species, taking into account as well variations in environmental conditions. The latter would be helpful in the considerable challenge of understanding how to quantitatively connect these properties to genuine thermodynamic properties. As such, we plan in future work to expand on these findings by applying the approach Kullback-Leibler distance AKL, PI entropy Π, and other such information-theoretic tools to single-cell RNAseq data, which provides higher resolution and can distinguish cell-type-specific patterns, and to possibly connect these two levels of description. More ambitious plans will attempt to integrate the PI entropy approach with specific gene-regulatory network models to quantify the influence of transcriptional regulators on developmental transitions. By correlating the dependence of entropy measures with known gene regulatory networks, we aim to uncover causal relationships between gene expression fluctuations and morphological changes. Ultimately, we seek physically motivated equations relating these microscopic transcriptomic dynamics to macroscopic phenotypic ones, in the spirit of the connection between microscopic statistical mechanics and macroscopic thermodynamics (Fig. 5).

We close by noting that beyond showing the power of information-theoretic techniques applied to temporal transcriptomic trajectories, our study also serves as a test of the concept of PI entropy (Permutation entropy of an Indexed ensemble), recently introduced8 as a framework for efficiently computing a proxy for the dynamics of correlations between members of an ensemble, relying on critical choices of indexing by an observable and using a reference point in time. In this context, it has proved to be useful in quantifying a ‘thermodynamic’ trajectory that makes intuitive sense in the behavior of the dynamical complexity of gene expression during Drosophila embryogenesis, as well as showing strong correlation with a proxy for the macroscopic phenotypic developmental stage. This highlights the utility of applying this sort of information-theoretic approach from non-equilibrium statistical mechanics or ensembles of dynamical systems to biomarker dynamics in developmental biology, with potential applications in other systems ranging from stem cell differentiation to evolutionary developmental biology.

Methods

Data acquisition and processing

We analyze public bulk gene expression data from Drosophila embryos spanning 24 hours of development. Specifically, we use the Whole Genome Drosophila Embryogenesis Time Course dataset, available from the NCBI GEO database under accession number GSE618620. This dataset contains bulk microarray data of Drosophila melanogaster Canton S wild-type embryos, tracking the relative expression levels of 11,456 genes at 30 timepoints covering the entire 24-hour period when the embryos develop into larvae13. Independent embryos were harvested for data collection at each timepoint. The proportion of embryos in a particular embryonic stage (as reported by the dataset) was measured alongside relative gene expression values at each timepoint. For the first 6.5 h of measurements, there are overlapping timepoints, but all measurements after hour 6.5 are taken at 1-h intervals. For example, there is data taken between hour 1 and 2, and as well between hours 1.5 and 2.5; we call the latter ‘half time’ points. Though care was taken to minimize overlap between measurements, in the bulk of our analysis, we remove these ‘half time’ data points and note specifically when they are included.

The gene expression values in the dataset were originally reported as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\log }_{2}$$\end{document}log2-transformed expression ratios (relative expressions) with respect to a common reference sample, in order to make expression levels cross-comparable. This common reference is the median expression for all genes, composed by pooling samples from across the Drosophila lifecycle. More detailed descriptions of data collection and normalization techniques, including more information on the microarray and reference sample preparations, can be found in references12,13. In some of our computations, we reverse the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\log }_{2}$$\end{document}log2 transform on relative expression to show a larger dynamic range and ensure the approach KL metric (AKL) is well-defined.

Since the data do not track the gene dynamics of a collection of the same embryos over time, it is not exactly analogous to trajectories in physics. Instead, the data consist of averages over the collected embryos at each timepoint, normalized to a reference taken over the entire lifetime. In that sense, the data are more aptly characterized as being collected from an ensemble over multiple individual embryo trajectories, yielding the transcriptomic state trajectories averaged over individual genetic variation, throughout development.

Embryos were qualitatively categorized into one of 17 developmental stages (phenotypic stage) and reported as the proportion of embryos in each stage by Hooper et al.12 (see Fig. 4 for our reproduction). Our macroscopic observable, mean phenotypic stage, is the mean stage across the embryos, normalized to [0, 1]. A mean stage of 0 reflects a completely undeveloped embryo population, whereas a mean stage of 1 reflects a fully developed embryo population, on average.

Data were analyzed using the statistical package R (version 4.2.0) and the Python packages NumPy (version 1.26.4) and SciPy (version 13.1). Kernel density estimates were computed with the R package ggplot2 (version 3.5.1), and mutual information values were computed with the R package infotheo (version 1.2.0.1). In order to compute mutual information, we discretized our data according to the Freedman-Diaconis rule for bin width, setting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\rm{Binwidth}}=2\frac{{\rm{IQR}}(x)}{{N}^{1/3}}$$\end{document}Binwidth=2IQR(x)N1/3, where IQR(x) is the interquartile range of the sample distribution x, and N is the number of observations, each for a given metric (e.g., AKL and PI). This estimator better accounts for the generally non-normal distributions of our metrics and provides more robustness to outliers compared to other discretization methods21. Mutual information is computed directly from the discretized (binned) distributions of phenotypic stage and our metrics derived from the expression data.

To quantify the complexity of the shape of the probability distribution of expression values P(μ)dμ, we measure the Shannon entropy H = − ∫P(μ)ln(P(μ))dμ; for our discrete data, this is calculated by defining a histogram of probability of expression levels Pn over the nth neighborhood of μ of width Δμ, i.e., by binning along the expression axis in bins of size Δμ and instead computing the discrete Shannon entropy H = − ∑nPn ln Pn. As described in the previous section, we apply the Freedman-Diaconis rule to set Δμ, which results in a dynamic Δμ. This yields between 53 and 87 bins in the discretized version of each expression distribution, with most timepoints having 70–82 bins. Applying this rule to set Δμ provides a consistent logic between the discretizations of each timepoint’s expression distribution, aiding cross-comparison of H values.

Finally, we also use the Permutation entropy of an Indexed ensemble (PI or Π(t)). This recently proposed measure8 tracks how smoothness in an ensemble evolves as a function of time, in the physics case, typically going from an initially prepared smooth distribution to a complex one as a function of time. The method starts from an ensemble of trajectories that are initially indexed along a coordinate and uses symbolic or ordinal analysis24,25 to compute how this indexing becomes braided over time due to individual trajectory dynamics. The change in smoothness over the ensemble of trajectories as measured by Π(t) was shown to behave as a good proxy for the entropy dynamics of a non-interacting ensemble of chaotic maps. The degree of braiding—relative to a chosen and prepared ‘un-braided’ state—provides both a sense of distance and direction in ensemble space, and in particular a measure of growth or loss of correlations.

In using this technique, we treat the gene expression data as an ensemble of trajectories, and choose the last timepoint as our reference point for ‘smoothness’. This amounts to computing the time development of an information-theoretic distance from this state, as measured by deviation from its referenced smoothness. The coordinate on which we sort the data—that is, choose neighborhoods in which to define smoothness—is the gene expression value μi(tf) at the final time.

To compute the PI entropy of the transcriptome, we start by indexing all genes according to their expression values at the final timepoint (t = tf). The gene with the highest expression at tf is assigned position i = 0, the next most expressed is assigned i = 1, and so on. We then consider the indexed ensemble \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widetilde{\rho }$$\end{document}ρ~ for each timepoint t ∈ {t0, tf}, and at each t compute the degree of ‘braiding’ or shuffling relative to tf by comparing triplets of expression values as we move along i from i = 0, defining ordinal patterns for each of these sequential triplets of gene-expression levels. For example, if μ0 < μ1 < μ2 we record 012, if μ1 < μ0 < μ2 we record 102, etc.

The behaviors of these separate measures add up to tell a more comprehensive story than any one measure by itself. The mean expression level \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{\mu }$$\end{document}μ¯ acts as an equivalent of an energy-like property in physics, which makes intuitive sense, particularly as we consider the energy requirements to express a gene. The Shannon entropy H in turn characterizes the complexity of the shape of the distribution of gene-expression levels when sorted by expression value, crudely measuring the degree to which the expression levels are spread out or localized across genes. This metric, however, does not pay attention to the identity of any gene at a given expression value, thus ignoring ‘exchanges’ in expression levels, for example. The Kullback-Leibler distance metric, AKL, is easy to visualize as resulting from evolution in a high-dimensional vector space, but does not provide an intuition about direction or what a distance means, though it can identify change easily. Finally, the PI entropy Π is subtler to understand but has the advantage of being able to associate a direction in time with the change, as we see below.

We also note that our choice of coordinate for indexing implicitly frames gene expression levels as a proxy of how energy might behave in a physical situation; the PI resulting from this indexing is implicitly a proxy related to physical entropy. We now show how these quantities behave in the context of the developing embryo’s transcriptome.
