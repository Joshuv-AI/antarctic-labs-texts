Interpretable deep learning models for independent fertilizer and crop recommendation
Authors: Stella Mary Venkateswara, Jayashree Padmanaban
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Internet of Things (IoT) technologies is fundamentally transforming agriculture. This integration enables the real-time collection and analysis of critical data such as soil nutrient levels, temperature, humidity, and climatic conditions. A data-driven approach allows for independent fertilizer and crop recommendations, maximizing yield and promoting resource efficiency. Soil properties play a crucial role in crop growth, facilitating the absorption of essential nutrients like Nitrogen (N), Phosphorus (P), and Potassium (K). However, traditional farming practices often lead to nutrient inefficiency and soil health degradation, negatively impacting productivity. While Machine Learning (ML) techniques have emerged for recommending fertilizers and crops, they frequently encounter issues such as inadequate feature selection and class imbalance. These limitations hinder their ability to accurately model the intricate relationships among environmental factors, soil conditions, and crop nutrient requirements. To address these challenges, this study presents a unified smart recommendation system that utilizes TabNet, a deep learning architecture specifically designed for tabular data. The novelty of this work lies in leveraging TabNet’s attention-driven learning to directly discover important patterns from preprocessed IoT-enabled agricultural data for accurate and interpretable crop and fertilizer classifications, without relying on prior feature selection. To enhance transparency, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) is applied at the final stage to provide post hoc interpretability, allowing stakeholders to understand the model’s reasoning behind each recommendation. Evaluated with the Crop and Fertilizer Dataset from Western Maharashtra, TabNet achieves impressive classification accuracies of 95.24% for fertilizer recommendations and 96.21% for crop recommendations, outperforming conventional classifier and existing approach. The study employs robust preprocessing techniques, such as iterative imputation and the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE), to ensure data quality and class balance. The model’s performance and generalizability were rigorously assessed using fivefold cross-validation, consistently maintaining these high accuracy levels across folds.

Introduction

The agriculture industry is an essential contributor to a nation’s economic prosperity. Preserving soil quality is essential for effective crop production. Utilizing advanced technologies for planning, analysis, and production management is essential for improving agricultural productivity, encompassing soil health, plant nutrition, and water quality1,2. As individuals increasingly prioritize health, the cultivation of high-quality crops is essential for contemporary society. Agriculturists use pesticides and incorporate nutrients into the soil to achieve optimal yield. Pesticides are toxic agents employed to exterminate pests, weeds, fungi, and similar organisms, encompassing herbicides, fungicides, and insecticides3. The implementation of IoT in smart farming streamlines agricultural processes by facilitating precise seed sowing and efficient fertilizer application, resulting in increased crop yields4. As the global food demand continues to grow, there is a rising necessity for automated, intelligent, and scalable technologies capable of accurately assessing soil and crop conditions and advising on the most appropriate fertilizers5. An innovative approach for improving agricultural operations is precision agriculture that uses data-driven decision-making to improve agriculture. Fertilizer/crop recommendation is crucial since it boosts agricultural productivity and reduces resource waste and environmental effect. Traditional fertilizer/crop recommendation methods involve soil testing and heuristic-based procedures, which are laborious, time-consuming, and error-prone due to soil composition and environmental conditions6. Integration of smart farming technologies, especially Internet of Things (IoT) in AI based fertilizer/crop recommendation systems can lead advanced revolution in this direction. IoT sensors continuously monitor the soil health by collecting data in real time on the soil moisture, pH, temperature, and nutrient levels7. The integration enhances the scalability of AI-based farming solutions and reduces reliance on conventional soil testing methods8.

Now, recent advancements in Artificial Intelligence (AI) and deep learning for data-driven solutions have transformed agricultural decision-making. The black box nature of deep learning systems has limited its agricultural uptake even though it demonstrates effective performance in farming operations. Technology limitations in deep learning models make it difficult for farmers and agronomists to understand prediction reasoning because these models contain complex non-linear function algorithms9. Users gain better understanding of model behavior and feature importance by applying attention mechanisms alongside TabNet, SHAP, and LIME (Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations)10. The trust and acceptance problems stemming from explainability shortages create particular challenges for important decision domains particularly shown through fertilizer/crop application systems. The development of explainable AI (XAI) approaches provides solutions to this issue through decision-making explanation techniques11.

The implementation of XAI improves AI-powered fertilizer/crop recommendation system transparency, so farmers and specialists will build greater confidence in the solutions12. Systems that recommend fertilizers/crop gain advantages from combining deep learning with XAI because it enhances their performance quality along with their reliability and adaptability. Through AI model interpretability stakeholders obtain the ability to see how input data affects recommendations while detecting biases and implementing recommendations through domain knowledge13. Deep learning algorithms can produce more accurate and context-sensitive fertilizer/crop recommendations by combining this data with satellite imaging and remote sensing methods, thus enhancing nutrient management for diverse soil types and crops. Sustainable agriculture requires humans to pair AI systems with their expertise to create suggestions that help achieve environmental sustainability targets and respect agronomic standards14. The goal of this research aims to determine how deep learning and XAI can create sustainable fertilizer/crop recommendation systems that maintain reliability and scalability and offer interpretability to users. The proposed approach utilizes TabNet along with XAI approaches to enhance nutrient/crop management, precision agriculture methods and sustainably produce food15. Deep learning techniques such as Transformer-based architectures, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) have been shown to be particularly well suited to handle complex and high-dimensional data in agriculture applications such as soil nutrient assessment, disease detection, and yield estimation16. TabNet is a state-of-the-art deep learning architecture, tailored for tabular data, that has shown promise in fertilizer/crop recommendation. Its ability to capture complex interactions between features while remaining interpretable is the main reason for that. Unlike traditional machine learning models, which require extensive feature engineering, TabNet can directly learn feature representations, making it appropriate for heterogeneous agricultural datasets17,18.

TabNet has selected as the core classification model due to its unique architecture tailored for tabular data. Unlike conventional deep learning models that often underperform on tabular datasets, TabNet leverages sequential attention and sparse feature selection to focus on the most relevant input features during each decision step. This allows it to retain interpretability and efficiency—key requirements for agricultural decision-making systems. While other models like NODE (Neural Oblivious Decision Ensembles)19 and FT-Transformer20 have shown competitive performance, TabNet offers the advantage of built-in explainability and superior training speed on structured datasets. These properties made it well-suited for the proposed approach of fertilizer or crop recommendation using static agronomic features.

As a result, the primary goal of this study is to use intelligent technology to recommend effective fertilizer/crop for soil, specifically addressing the key challenges in fertilizer/crop recommendation, namely class imbalance, poor feature selection, and the complexity of soil-climate-crop relationships. Traditional machine learning algorithms suffer from interpretability issues and inadequate feature representation and therefore, the proposed work presents a more sophisticated methodology to tackle the problem at hand. To enhance the interpretability of the model, the proposed system employs SHAP explainability and Tabnet’s interpretability, iterative imputation for missing values, SMOTE for balancing the dataset. Moreover, TabNet is an interpreted deep learning model specifically designed for tabular data that uses attention-based learning and sequential feature selection to learn the correlations among nutrient levels, environmental variables, and soil properties. Hence, TabNet outperforms existing models in terms of predictive ability and interpretability according to experimental data. This research has significant potential for precision agriculture as it provides a precise, interpretable, and computationally efficient tool, allowing agricultural experts to make data-driven fertilizer/crop decisions that augment crop yield and promote sustainable soil management.

Literature review

Smart farming gained significant innovation from the Internet of Things (IoT) because this technology allows precision agriculture together with automation systems and quick decision-making. Production optimization in IoT-based smart farming depends on the core components which include sensors with communication networks as well as data analytics functions. The system proves effective for predicting temperature variations across seasons since it relies on Internet of Things-connected weather stations to monitor weather conditions in real-time5. Javaid et al. performed research about AI technologies which show rising promise for agricultural transformation particularly under climate change conditions. These technologies optimize resource management, improve meteorological predictions, and promote sustainable agricultural practices, ultimately leading to increased crop yields. Their study showed that large climatic datasets utilized for strengthening farming systems against environmental changes by AI driven real-time decision-making6.

Klerkx et al. analyzed that multidisciplinary research becomes vital to address the problems of adoption and ethics as well as policy frameworks in digital agriculture analysis. Big data analytics transforms agricultural supply chains by giving predicted insights together with real-time operational enhancements23. Liakos et al. studied how large data together with advanced computing systems impact the application of machine learning in agriculture. ML successfully manages crops, livestock, water resources and also controls soil quality. Three essential agricultural applications of high importance include yield prediction along with disease detection, weed detection and livestock monitoring. Their work illustrated ML-based solutions improve real time decision making and farming practices7. Tzounis et al. analyzed modern IoT movements in agriculture while focusing on how cloud and fog systems handle big agricultural data collections. The presentation identified data integration requirements for farm productivity enhancement while discussing IoT device interoperability problems24. Youness et al. by introducing a smart irrigation system based on Internet of Things and machine learning. Ray et al. discusses IoT-based solutions and presents several smart farming systems with sensors in various case studies. Their analysis also identified numerous technological challenges that need to be addressed to unlock the full potential of IoT in agriculture, such as network limitations and data security problems25.

The system provides fertilizer recommendations through its module by using real-time soil data to determine suitable nutrient levels which generates results in less than 80 s. Their methodology achieved better accuracy results when compared to standard machine learning approaches including K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) and Support Vector Machine (SVM)9. Dey et al. crafted a machine learning system to guide agricultural and horticultural crop recommendations through combination of environmental factors, soil pH, and nutrient analysis. A research evaluation of the predictive performance involved five machine learning methods: Random Forest, Support Vector Machine, KNN, XGBoost, and Decision Tree. The XGBoost model reached the best accuracy performance thus showing its potential for AI-guided fertilizer recommendations and sustainable farming decisions making10. Helfer et al. conducted soil fertility evaluation through the model uses historical data while the prediction of crop productivity relies on climate event analysis. The research approach showed exceptional effectiveness in forecasting soil organic matter while simultaneously determining clay content through its strong correlation coefficients for fertility assessment in precision agriculture11.

Bhattacharya et al. introduced the PCFRIMDS (Precision Crop and Fertilizer Recommendations Using Integrated Multimodal Data Fusion for Sustainable Agriculture) as an integrated system that performs multimodal data fusion for precision crop and fertilizer recommendations. This system combines NPK values with moisture content, image analysis, and geographical data using a Bidirectional Gated Recurrent Unit (BiGRU) along with an Ant Lion Fuzzy Principal Component Analyzer (ALFPCA). Their solution enhances agricultural recommendations and boosts operational speed to support sustainable farming12. Sims et al. investigated the Australian plantation forest using satellite hyperspectral imaging to detect nutrient status and fertilizer needs. Proposed methodology

The proposed study develops a deep learning-based TabNet architecture that aims to provide independent recommendations for fertilizers and crops while enhancing interpretability, accuracy, and operational efficiency. By utilizing an attention mechanism and sequential feature selection technique, TabNet identifies exceptional relationships among soil, climate, and crop variables, enabling the generation of both precise and explainable recommendations. The transparency of decision-making processes is further enhanced through SHAP explainability and TabNet’s inherent interpretability, which highlight the crucial features influencing these processes.

The performance of the proposed TabNet deep learning approach is evaluated by assessing the predictive model’s accuracy, recall, precision, and F1-score. This evaluation demonstrates that TabNet effectively captures intricate relationships between features, with SHAP explainability contributing to the model’s overall interpretability. The study’s findings confirm that TabNet achieves superior predictive performance and enhanced explainability compared to existing systems. In this work, a single TabNet architecture is employed to separately predict fertilizers and crops. The proposed recommendation system enhances smart farming through the integration of deep learning, preprocessing, and explainability. Unlike traditional models, the interpretable deep learning framework of TabNet, combined with SMOTE-based data balancing, iterative imputation, and SHAP-based explainability, significantly improves prediction accuracy. A structured data pipeline analyzes the correlations between soil, crops, and fertilizers, generating independent recommendations based on soil and weather conditions, all supported by explainable AI insights.

The proposed methodology is intricately organized into several distinct layers, each playing a crucial role in the overall system architecture as illustrated by Fig. 1. At the forefront is the Data Collection Layer, which meticulously gathers a wide array of information pertaining to crops, soil conditions, weather patterns, and types of fertilizers. This foundational data is then processed in the Input Layer, where essential tasks such as data preprocessing, the application of SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique) to address class imbalances, and a series of input operations are carried out to ensure the data is primed for analysis. Moving forward, the TabNet Feature Learning Layer takes center stage, incorporating vital components of the feature learning process. This includes the feature transformer, which extracts meaningful characteristics from the data, the attentive transformer that enhances the model’s focus on relevant features, and the mask mechanism that aids in interpreting the significance of specific input features. Subsequently, in the Classification Layer, the system engages in multi-class classification, skillfully identifying the most suitable fertilizer or crop type through the utilization of softmax activation, which helps to quantify the probabilities of different outcomes. Finally, the Explainability Layer plays a key role in demystifying the model’s operations. It emphasizes SHAP-based explainability, a powerful method for interpreting the impacts of various features on the model’s predictions, along with leveraging TabNet’s interpretability. This layer not only provides insights into how decisions are made but also visualizes the importance of different features, enhancing the user’s understanding of the model’s predictions.Fig. 1Proposed system methodology.

Data collection

At the initial stage, the system collects diverse agricultural data, including soil properties, climate conditions, and crop-specific parameters. These data points can be sourced from smart farming sensor networks, government agricultural repositories, or past research datasets. The dataset typically includes features such as soil pH, nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), potassium (K) content, moisture level, temperature, humidity, and labels like fertilizer and crop.

The recommendation of fertilizers/crops suffers from a severe class imbalance since select fertilizer/crop types appear frequently in the dataset while others appear infrequently. Step 5 Data Splitting

The dataset was divided using an 80:20 split ratio, with 80% allocated for training purposes and the remaining 20% reserved for testing. A fixed random seed was implemented to guarantee the reproducibility of the results. To evaluate the model’s robustness and generalizability, a fivefold cross-validation approach was employed. The dataset was randomly segmented into five equal parts, where in each iteration, four parts were used for training and one part for testing. This process was repeated five times, and the final performance metrics were calculated as the mean ± standard deviation across all five folds.

These preprocessing steps ensured that the dataset was clean, balanced, and adequately prepared for efficient training of the TabNet classifier.

TabNet feature learning layer

The TabNet architecture42 consists of multiple decision steps, where each step processes a D-dimensional feature vector, refining learned representations through successive layers. The primary components of this feature learning process include the Feature Transformer, Attentive Transformer, and Mask Mechanism, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Feature Transformer is a deep feature learning module consists of shared and decision-specific layers. It incorporates batch normalization, fully connected layers, and Gated Linear Units (GLU) to stabilize learning, enhance expressiveness, and prevent overfitting. The feature transformer is responsible for generating informative embeddings that capture the underlying patterns in the dataset. Attentive Transformer and Mask Mechanism are crucial for interpretability and dynamic feature selection at each step. The Attentive Transformer uses the Sparsemax activation function to allocate probabilities to specific features, enhancing feature sparsity. Additionally, the Mask mechanism provides importance scores for features at every decision point, ensuring effective representation learning. The Sparsemax activation function in the Attentive Transformer project feature scores onto a probability simplex to achieve both sparse selection and identification of crucial features. TabNet classification

TabNet generates its final classification output by integrating aggregated transformed feature representations from various decision stages. The classification process involves several key steps. First, each decision step contributes its uniquely weighted feature representations, which are then aggregated into a comprehensive feature vector. This vector encapsulates the information derived from all decision stages, ensuring that diverse perspectives are considered in the final decision-making process. Next, a softmax activation function is employed to convert the aggregated feature vector into class probabilities, reflecting the likelihood of different fertilizer/crop recommendations. Ultimately, the recommendation for the fertilizer/crop type is determined by selecting the class that exhibits the highest probability value, thereby providing a data-driven suggestion for optimal fertilizer use and precise crop selection. This method enhances the accuracy of the recommendations, ensuring they are tailored to the specific needs identified through the feature representations.

A global interpretability method evaluates important features across the whole dataset which generates model confidence with domain expertise capability to confirm recommendations. TabNet’s sequential attention-based feature selection and mask mechanism along with interpretability abilities produce predictable outcomes that base agricultural input optimization on site and environment characteristics. The built-in interpretability of TabNet reveals the crucial features which resulted in specific predictions. Model decisions are trusted through an analysis which includes local interpretability that uses instance explanations with masks and global interpretability showing feature importance across the entire data set.

TabNet is an innovative deep learning architecture specifically crafted for handling tabular data. Its unique approach incorporates a blend of sparse attention mechanisms and sequential decision-making strategies. This allows the model to focus selectively on the most pertinent features at each decision-making step. Dataset description

This study employs the Crop and Fetilrizer dataset51,52, which integrates agricultural sensor data with publicly accessible information regarding soil properties, meteorological conditions, fertilizer and crop varieties. The dataset comprises 5,000 instances across 12 columns: S.No., District_Name, Soil_color, Nitrogen, Phosphorus, Potassium, pH, Rainfall, Temperature, Crop, Fertilizer, and Link. Although the dataset contains 12 columns, only a selected subset of features is utilized for model training, contingent upon the specific objectives of the analysis. In the context of the fertilizer recommendation task, the aim is to identify the optimal type of fertilizer and its application rate suitable for specific agricultural conditions. The features incorporated in this model include Soil_color, Nitrogen, Phosphorus, Potassium, pH, Rainfall, Temperature, and Crop. The target variable in this task is Fertilizer, which is categorical, representing various fertilizer options deemed appropriate based on the provided input conditions.

Conversely, the crop recommendation task seeks to determine the most suitable crop for a designated area of land under particular environmental and soil conditions. The features employed for this model encompass Soil_color, Nitrogen, Phosphorus, Potassium, pH, Rainfall, and Temperature. Here, the target variable is Crop, represented as a categorical label that signifies the best crop recommendation grounded in the input features. For both tasks, the dataset has partitioned into training and testing subsets, with 80% of the data allocated for training and 20% for testing, utilizing Scikit-learn’s train_test_split() function to ensure robustness and reproducibility. Data preprocessing included the imputation of missing values, the application of MinMax scaling for feature normalization, and the implementation of the SMOTE algorithm to mitigate class imbalance, particularly regarding fertilizer/crop labels, by generating synthetic instances from minority classes.

Experimental setup

The experimental framework established for the independent fertilizer and crop recommendation system combines both local and cloud-based computational resources to enhance the efficiency of model training, evaluation, and reproducibility. Initially, data preprocessing and exploratory analysis were conducted on a Windows 10 machine with 8 GB of RAM. Subsequently, model training and validation took place on Google Colab, utilizing T4 GPU acceleration, configured with CUDA 11.2 and cuDNN 8.1.0, to harness hardware acceleration for deep learning applications.

The model development pipeline integrates several crucial Python libraries. For model implementation and training, TensorFlow 2.x and PyTorch were utilized, while Scikit-learn managed data preprocessing, the application of the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE), and performance evaluation. Data handling and visualization were facilitated through Pandas and Matplotlib. To enhance model explainability, the SHAP library was employed to deliver post hoc insights into the contributions of various features.

TabNet was chosen as the principal classification model for both fertilizer and crop recommendation tasks. This deep learning model, featuring an attentive transformer-based architecture, allows for interpretable sequential feature selection while achieving competitive accuracy. Preprocessing steps included label encoding and MinMax scaling of all categorical and numerical features within the crop and fertilizer datasets. To tackle class imbalance, SMOTE was applied, which expanded the fertilizer dataset to 20,729 samples and the crop dataset to 22,000 samples, promoting a more uniform class distribution for effective training.

The TabNet model was configured with empirically optimized hyperparameters: both the decision and attention dimensions were set to 64 (n_d = 64, n_a = 64), and it employed three decision steps (n_steps = 3). A relaxation parameter (gamma = 1.5) and a sparsity regularization coefficient (lambda_sparse = 1e-4) were incorporated to enhance generalization and interpretability. Training utilized the AdamW optimizer, with a batch size of 64 and an initial learning rate of 0.01, which was dynamically adjusted through cosine annealing. These settings were meticulously chosen to balance model complexity, convergence speed, and interpretability.

For training and evaluation purposes, a stratified 80/20 hold-out split was implemented to maintain class distribution. The model was trained for a maximum of 100 epochs using categorical cross-entropy as the loss function. Early stopping techniques were employed to mitigate overfitting by closely monitoring validation loss, and in cases where no improvement occurred over five consecutive epochs, the learning rate was reduced by a factor of 0.1. Performance was evaluated using a comprehensive suite of metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, AUC, and the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC).

To promote interpretability, both SHAP-based feature importance analysis and the built-in attention masks of TabNet were leveraged to assess the influence of input features on model predictions. SHAP also facilitated the optimization of model inputs by identifying key contributing features. For additional validation, a fivefold cross-validation experiment was carried out using the same preprocessing pipeline and model architecture, excluding SHAP-based feature selection due to computational demands. Each fold adhered to an 80/20 stratified split, and the averaged performance metrics (mean ± standard deviation) corroborated TabNet’s robust and consistent performance across various data splits.

Lastly, a Random Forest model was established as a baseline for comparative analysis. This model was trained on the same preprocessed dataset, employing hyperparameters optimized through grid search, specifically: n_estimators = 100, max_depth = 20, min_samples_split = 2, and criterion = ‘gini’. This baseline facilitated a fair evaluation of TabNet’s performance and interpretability advantages.

The SHAP technique enables post-hoc evaluation of feature attributions for model predictions yet TabNet shows how it selects relevant features during its training process through its feature selection system.

Tabnet interpretability

TabNet is a deep learning architecture designed for tabular data, integrating sparse attention mechanisms with sequential decision processes to enhance performance and interpretability. In the crop recommendation task, the model was trained on a balanced dataset of 22,000 samples generated via the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) to rectify initial class imbalances, ensuring equal representation with 1,000 instances per crop class. The dataset was divided into 80% with 17,600 training samples and 20% with 4,400 validation samples. Instead of processing all features simultaneously, TabNet employs an attentive transformer to create sparse masks at each decision step, concentrating on the most informative features. For crop recommendation, this enables the model to prioritize essential attributes such as soil nutrients, pH level, rainfall, and temperature, while reducing the influence of less relevant features. Configured with three decision steps, the model iteratively performs feature selection and transformation, where each step’s feature transformer extracts deep embeddings that are aggregated to yield the final crop prediction. This architecture enhances predictive accuracy while preserving interpretability, making it suitable for real-world agricultural scenarios where understanding model rationale is critical.

The interpretability of the TabNet model in the context of crop recommendation stems from its use of sparse attention masks, which highlight how the model prioritizes different input features at each stage of decision-making, as shown in the TabNet Feature Mask Heatmap in Fig. 20. When examining the visualized attention masks across three decision steps, it becomes evident that TabNet employs a systematic approach to feature selection. In the initial step, the model predominantly concentrates on Nitrogen (Feature 0) and Potassium (Feature 2), underscoring the vital role these nutrients play in plant growth and determining the suitability of crops. These foundational nutrients form the basis for the model’s early decision-making process. As the analysis progresses to Step 2, attention transitions to more contextual factors, such as Soil Color (Feature 6) and Rainfall (Feature 3). This shift indicates that the model incorporates visual and environmental elements to fine-tune its crop recommendations. Finally, in Step 3, the model synthesizes insights gained from earlier steps and places greater significance on Phosphorus (Feature 1) and pH (Feature 4). These factors are crucial as they directly influence nutrient availability and compatibility with various crops. This structured, stepwise reasoning approach mirrors how agronomists assess soil and climatic conditions to determine the most suitable crops, ensuring that the model’s predictions are both informed and understandable.Fig. 20TabNet Feature mask heatmap for crop reccommendation.

Result analysis and discussion

The performance of the TabNet-based independent fertilizer and crop recommendation system clearly shows its advantages over traditional machine learning models, especially when compared to the Random Forest classifier. Random Forest is recognized for its ensemble learning approach, which enhances accuracy by combining the outputs from multiple decision trees. It uses techniques like bagging and random feature selection, which help improve the model’s stability and minimize overfitting. While this makes Random Forest a reliable option, particularly for large datasets with noisy or incomplete values, it often struggles with modeling complex and non-linear relationships among features. When tested on the preprocessed dataset, the Random Forest model achieved a respectable accuracy of 85.34 percent. In contrast, the TabNet model significantly outperformed it, reaching an accuracy of 95.24 percent for fertilizer recommendations. This improvement is evident not just in overall accuracy but also in the model’s capability to provide highly reliable predictions. For instance, TabNet recorded a precision of 95.48 percent, a recall of 95.31 percent, and an F1-score of 95.40 percent. These results indicate that TabNet effectively captures intricate patterns in the data, offering strong generalization and robust recommendation capabilities.

In the domain of crop recommendations, TabNet continued to excel, achieving an impressive accuracy of 96.21 percent. This further illustrates its ability to identify complex patterns regarding crop suitability based on diverse agricultural inputs. The model also maintained high precision and recall scores, both around 95.80 percent, resulting in a consistently strong F1-score. These findings suggest that TabNet can provide reliable and consistent recommendations for both fertilizers and crops. While Random Forest performed reasonably well, its limitations in capturing deeper relationships between features hindered its overall effectiveness. In contrast, the TabNet model thrives due to its advanced learning mechanisms. It stands out by not requiring manual feature engineering; instead, it employs sequential attention to automatically prioritize the most important features during each decision-making step. This capability to learn meaningful interactions from structured tabular data gives TabNet a clear edge over traditional methods.

Another significant advantage of TabNet is its built-in interpretability. Unlike models such as Random Forest that require external tools like SHAP to clarify their predictions, TabNet naturally reveals which features significantly influence each prediction through its masking mechanism. This level of transparency fosters greater trust, especially in sensitive fields like agriculture where accurate and explainable decision support is crucial. The results showcased in Table 5 and Fig. 21, highlight the comparative performance of TabNet against Random Forest and other existing methods. Across all evaluation metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score, TabNet consistently delivers superior outcomes. Conclusion

This study introduces an innovative and independent fertilizer and crop recommendation system that leverages deep learning, specifically the TabNet architecture, while incorporating SHAP for enhanced interpretability. The model examines crucial soil and environmental factors such as nutrient levels, pH, and climatic conditions to provide accurate, data-driven recommendations. Impressively, it achieved an accuracy rate of 99.65 percent for fertilizer suggestions and 96.21 percent for crop recommendations, significantly outperforming traditional classifiers like Random Forest. These results highlight the model’s capacity to learn complex patterns and deliver reliable predictions, which are essential for smart farming initiatives. A key strength of the system is its focus on interpretability. The built-in attention mechanism of TabNet, combined with SHAP visualizations, allows users to understand which factors significantly influence each recommendation. This level of transparency builds trust among users and aids both farmers and agricultural experts in making informed decisions. Additionally, techniques such as SMOTE for data balancing, iterative imputation, and fivefold cross-validation have notably enhanced the model’s reliability and adaptability across different conditions.

Looking ahead, there is a plan to expand the system to cover a wider range of geographical areas and environmental scenarios. Integrating real-time data from IoT sensors for soil and crop monitoring could enable dynamic recommendations at the field level. Adapting TabNet or exploring lighter alternatives could also enhance accessibility and scalability in areas with limited resources. However, some limitations of the TabNet model warrant attention. It can be computationally intensive and often requires GPU acceleration, which may pose challenges for deployment in rural or edge environments. Additionally, the potential for misclassifications exists when fertilizers or crops share similar characteristics, particularly in less represented categories. Since the dataset used primarily focuses on Western Maharashtra, this may restrict the model’s ability to generalize to other regions. Addressing these issues through broader datasets and optimization will be crucial for enhancing real-world effectiveness.
