Introduction

To ensure timely public use of automated external defibrillators (AED) during out-of-hospital cardiac arrests (OHCA), effective signage and global acceptance are essential. Public-access AEDs are crucial for OHCA, but their effectiveness and patient survival depend, in part, on their location and visibility.1, 2, 3 While recognition of AED location is a crucial component in the Chain of Survival, other factors such as public awareness, availability of bystander assistance, and timely activation of emergency services also play vital roles. Early defibrillation is a crucial step in the Chain of Survival, and its success may be facilitated by improved AED visibility through appropriate signage.4 While previous studies examined evidence-based strategies for AED placement, a comprehensive approach regarding sign content, colour, and design—beyond ISO 7010 compliance—is lacking5, 6.

The International Organization for Standardisation (ISO) developed ISO 7010 (safety signs and shape) and ISO 3864–3 (colours and symbols) to standardise safety signs, including those for AEDs, with the goal of creating universally recognisable symbols that enhance public safety. In some regions, recent legislation mandates AED implementation, such as in parts of the United States and Australia. However, many other countries lack such mandates, resulting in significant variation in AED accessibility across different legislative environments7. This has resulted in a plethora of signs being designed by well-meaning individuals and organisations, within the confines of ISO standards, in an attempt to find a sign that may be easily and universally recognisable regardless of distance of competing environmental stimulus. However, studies from the UK6 and Denmark8 have demonstrated that public comprehension and recognition of AED signage are limited, even when following ISO standards6, 8.

To improve public engagement and rapid identification of AEDs in community environments, further research is required. This study seeks to assess the public’s understanding and subsequent impact of the colour coding and iconography used in ISO complicit signage for AEDs and identify preferred alternatives that might improve public recognition and response.

Methods

Participant selection / data collection

An online survey was administered via SurveyMonkey® (Momentive Global Inc., San Mateo, CA, USA). Participants were recruited through Heart of the Nation’s social media platform and Survey Monkey’s own respondents (paid, and independent of Heart of the Nation. Heart of the Nation is an Australian initiative dedicated to increasing awareness and access to Automated External Defibrillators (AEDs) across communities. Founded with a focus on improving out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) survival rates, Heart of the Nation engages with businesses, local organisations, and the general public to ensure that AEDs are readily available and accessible in emergency situations. Their efforts are aimed at building a nationwide network of publicly accessible AEDs and promoting the importance of early defibrillation in the chain of survival. The survey consisted of multiple-choice questions designed to evaluate the recognition and understanding of ISO standards signage colours and the specific AED sign (supplemental information). Baseline demographic data including geographic location (Geographic information was collected at the country level, with additional classification based on state or region in select cases), form of industry employment and prior first aid training was collected. The survey was only administered to individuals fluent in English. The survey questions were developed by the research team in consultation with experts in AED usage and signage design (The experts who contributed to the survey design were co-authors of the study, including electrophysiologists, cardiologists, the CEO of Heart of the Nation, and surgical residents specialising in cardiothoracic and general/trauma surgery). The questions were piloted with a small group of participants to refine wording, clarity, and structure. Feedback from the pilot was incorporated before deploying the full survey via SurveyMonkey.

The survey consisted of three major multiple-choice questions, with one question further broken down into six sub-questions (1–6) to evaluate specific preferences regarding AED signage.

1)Color Recognition:

Color Recognition:

The first question pertained to color symbolism where participants were provided with the ISO category sign color and instructed to match it with the intended meaning (options provided were Danger, Directions, Fire Protection/Equipment, Information, Mandated Actions, Prohibited Actions, Safe Condition, Warning).

2)AED Sign Identification:

AED Sign Identification:

The second question aimed to assess how well the existing ISO and International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR) signs convey the intended message. This was assessed by showing the participants the respective sign for an AED, and asking them to identify its meaning, without any education or information about what an AED is. Participants were asked if the sign indicates the location of:

After they answered the above question, brief education about what an AED is, in case the participant had no prior knowledge or experience of an AED was provided. They were then asked to correctly identify (out of 8 common options seen around the world – Fig. 1), which sign they believe may be the “International Standard” sign for an AED.

Preferred AED Sign

AED sign variants that are used internationally and the number of participants who believed best fulfilled the six criteria (Preferred AED Sign).

Participants were asked to select their top preference from a set of eight AED signs based on each of six criteria (e.g., visibility, recall). The questions were weighted equally, and only the top preference for each sign across the criteria was considered in the final ranking. Analyses

Ethical Statement

As the study did not involve clinical care, patient data, or any intervention, formal ethics approval was not required, nor sought. There was no risk to participants or the community. No identifiable information was collected from participants, there were no plans or attempts to re-identify participants, all reasonable steps were taken to prevent re-identification of data or access of data by those who were not authorised, and data were not shared outside the study investigators.9 This approach was based on the criteria for requiring ethical review and definitions for risk in research outlined in the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research 2023.9 However, the survey was conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines on research involving human participants, and informed consent was obtained from all respondents.

Results

Participants were recruited through Heart of the Nation’s social media platform (n = 244) as well as Survey Monkey’s own respondents (paid, and independent of Heart of the Nation (n = 691). We received 935 responses to the survey. There was geographical diversity, with 511 respondents residing in the US and Canada (54.65 %), 222 in the UK and Europe (23.76 %), 133 in the Asia Pacific (14.22 %), 6 in South America (0.64 %), 2 in the Middle East (0.21 %), and 61 from Other Territories (6.53 %). A total of 455 participants (48.66 %) were first aid trained. AED sign identification

From the array of eight different AED signs used internationally (Fig. 1), only 187 (20 %) participants correctly identified the ISO AED sign as the ‘Standard Sign.’ Further, only 224 (24 %) correctly recognised it as denoting the location of an AED. Meanwhile, 108 (12 %) thought it identified the location of first aid equipment, and 565 (60 %) believed it represented both an AED and first aid equipment. The remaining 37 (4 %) were unsure of its meaning.

The presence of prior first aid training did not skew the results. Of the 455 first aid trained participants, only 118 (26 %) correctly identified the ISO AED sign, comprising 53 % of those able to correctly recognise it.

Preferred AED sign

The sign that best met the six criteria (as per ‘preferred AED sign’), chosen by 252 (27 %) participants, was the yellow sign with a red heart, blue font stating “AED,” and red text reading “NO TRAINING REQUIRED.” The next most popular sign (n = 162, 17 %) was the white sign with a red heart and black font. The ISO sign and ILCOR sign were preferred by only 47 (5 %) and 94 (10 %) respectively, with the ISO sign ranking second last.

Among the 191 participants who identified the ISO sign as the “standardised” AED sign, only 30 (16 %) thought it was the most appropriate overall. In comparison, 37 (19 %) selected the yellow sign and 35 (18 %) the ILCOR sign.

Among healthcare workers, only 32 (21 %) correctly identified the ISO sign as the standard AED sign, with 87 (57 %) mistaking it for a First Aid Kit sign. Among this group, 50 (32 %) preferred the yellow and red sign, compared to 22 (14 %) for the ILCOR sign and 8 (5 %) for the ISO sign. Discussion

The study evaluated both the ISO 7010 AED sign and the ILCOR sign to determine public recognition and preference. The study's results indicate that the current ISO 7010 and 3864 complicit AED signs, are not widely recognised or correctly interpreted by the international public. The green and white colour scheme, while standard, is not widely understood, necessarily, to be related to medical equipment, and could be often confused with other “safe condition” signs, potentially delaying AED access in emergencies of OHCA. Recognition of the ISO AED sign was suboptimal, irrespective of industry employment or prior first aid training. Notably, the healthcare industry, where one might expect higher recognition rates, also demonstrated notable confusion. This confusion could result in critical time being lost when locating an AED, which is counterproductive in time-critical life-threatening situations. This study expands on prior evidence, including research that has attempted to develop 'improved' AED signage by incorporating a wider international sample and by comparing multiple signage options under different criteria. This study extends on prior evidence in the area by providing options that extend beyond the confines of ISO to introduce variations in colour and iconography, as is seen in the yellow and red, Heart of the Nation variant6. Further, the importance of these findings are increasingly pertinent given the recent successful implementation of responder smartphone apps which use global positioning data to enable emergency medical services to alert volunteer responders of nearby potential OHCA10. Volunteer first-responder apps for OHCA, such as the Heartstarter system in Denmark and Sweden, dispatch responders either directly to the victim or to retrieve the nearest AED. In such systems, clear and visible AED signage could significantly enhance the speed and success of AED retrieval, ultimately improving patient outcomes11. Good signage may contribute to the rapid identification of AEDs by making them more visible in public spaces, reducing hesitation by bystanders, and improving overall response time during emergencies. Appropriate signage may also contribute to cost effective AED provision12.

The preference for the yellow sign echoes previous national study of the Australian general population and highlights the importance of visibility and clarity in emergency signage13. This suggests AED signs should not be restricted by ISO 7010 and 3864, but should instead be informed by evidence-based research to maximize effectiveness.

Given AEDs' critical role in saving lives during cardiac arrest, public recognition of these devices is essential. The current ISO signage fails to achieve this, and a shift towards more intuitive, visually distinct signs could improve response times and outcomes.

A growing body of evidence highlights the importance of public awareness and training in improving AED usage during OHCA. Studies have shown that while many laypersons express a willingness to assist during cardiac emergencies, significant barriers remain. A survey in South Carolina indicated that although 69 % of respondents believed they had general knowledge of CPR, only 2.2 % spontaneously mentioned AED use when presented with a hypothetical cardiac arrest scenario, and 63 % admitted they would not know how to locate a public-access AED14. This demonstrates the critical gap between willingness to help and actual preparedness, underscoring the need for more accessible public AED training programs15, 16. Addressing these gaps through targeted public health campaigns and educational outreach is essential to improving AED response rates and, consequently, survival outcomes for OHCA patients.

Regional awareness campaigns have also shown promise in enhancing public readiness to respond to cardiac arrests with AEDs. For instance, a study conducted in Graz, Austria, demonstrated that after a four-year structured awareness campaign focused on “call, compress, shock,” knowledge of basic life support (BLS) actions significantly increased among the lay public17. Prior to the campaign, only 8.5 % of interviewees mentioned complete BLS actions (calling for help, starting chest compressions, and using an AED), which rose to 17.9 % post-campaign. This highlights the impact that sustained, regional initiatives can have on improving public knowledge and willingness to engage in life-saving interventions. However, despite these improvements, large gaps in AED knowledge persist globally, as illustrated by studies in China where awareness of AEDs remains relatively low, and the majority of the public lacks the confidence to perform resuscitation techniques16. These findings suggest that continuous education, supported by both national and regional campaigns, is crucial to fostering a culture where bystanders are not only willing but fully capable of utilising AEDs effectively.

While the survey methodology allowed us to gather broad insights, it is important to acknowledge limitations inherent to this approach. The questions were developed with input from experts and piloted on a small group; however, the range of responses was limited to predefined options, which may not capture the full spectrum of public opinion. Although iterative improvements were made based on pilot feedback, the final set of questions may still reflect inherent biases in survey design. This study recruited participants via the social media platform of 'Heart of the Nation' and SurveyMonkey’s paid respondent pool. These recruitment methods may have introduced a selection bias, potentially limiting the generalisability of our findings. The survey was limited to English-fluent participants, further restricting the diversity of our sample. Although we aimed for an international perspective, the majority of participants came from North America and Europe, which may impact the global applicability of the results. Therefore, caution should be exercised in extrapolating these findings to regions underrepresented in our sample, such as Africa, South America, and the Middle East. The survey also did not capture demographic information such as age, gender, ethnicity, education level, or socioeconomic status. These variables may have influenced participants’ responses to AED signage in an indeterminable manner.

The current study did not assess colour blindness or visual impairment among participants, which may have influenced their ability to interpret and prefer certain AED signage designs. This is an important factor that warrants further investigation, as inclusivity in signage design is critical for ensuring accessibility to life-saving devices such as AEDs. Future research should aim to explore the impact of visual impairments on AED signage recognition and ensure that any new standards accommodate individuals with colour blindness or other visual limitations. This would help create more inclusive signage that improves accessibility for all individuals, regardless of their visual abilities, ensuring that AEDs can be easily located in an emergency.

Conclusion

The findings suggest a need to reconsider the colour and design of AED signs to improve visibility and understanding, ultimately enhancing AED accessibility and usage in OHCAA. The findings highlight the potential benefit of more intuitive and visually distinct signage, but further research is required to validate these recommendations given the study's limitations. The preference for a more distinct and easily recognisable sign, such as the yellow sign identified in this study, indicates a potential direction for future standardisation efforts.
