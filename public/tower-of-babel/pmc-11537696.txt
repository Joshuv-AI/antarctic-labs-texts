Emergency department use before cancer diagnosis in Ontario, Canada: a population-based study
Authors: Keerat Grewal, Andrew Calzavara, Shelley L. McLeod, Antoine Eskander, David W. Savage, Cameron Thompson, Bjug Borgundvaag, Howard Ovens, Sheldon Cheskes, Kerstin de Wit, Jonathan Irish, Monika K. Krzyzanowska, Rachel Walsh, Rohit Mohindra, Venkatesh Thiruganasambandamoorthy, Rinku Sutradhar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough suspicions of cancer may be raised in patients who visit the emergency department, little is known about emergency department use before a cancer diagnosis. We sought to describe emergency department use among patients in Ontario within the 90 days before confirmed cancer diagnosis and to evaluate factors associated with this emergency department use.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, population-based study of patients aged 18 years or older who had a confirmed cancer diagnosis in Ontario from 2014 to 2021 using linked administrative databases. The primary outcome was any emergency department visit within 90 days before the cancer diagnosis date. We used multivariable logistic regression to evaluate factors associated with emergency department use, such as demographics (e.g., age, sex, rurality, Ontario Health region, indicators of marginalization), comorbidities, previous emergency department visits and hospital admissions, continuity of primary care, type of cancer, and year of cancer diagnosis.ResultsWe included 651 071 patients with cancer. Of these, 229 683 (35.3%) had an emergency department visit within 90 days before diagnosis, 51.4% of whom were admitted to hospital from the emergency department. Factors associated with increased odds of emergency department use before cancer diagnosis included rurality (odds ratio [OR] 1.15, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.13–1.17), residence in northern Ontario (North East region OR 1.14, 95% CI 1.10–1.17 and North West region OR 1.27, 95% CI 1.21–1.32, v. Toronto region), and living in the most marginalized areas (material resources OR 1.37, 95% CI 1.35–1.40 and housing OR 1.09, 95% CI 1.06–1.11, v. least marginalized quintile). We observed significant variation in emergency department use by cancer type, with high odds of emergency department use among patients with intracranial, pancreatic, liver or gallbladder, or thoracic cancer.InterpretationEmergency department use is common before cancer diagnosis, with about one-third of patients with cancer in Ontario using the emergency department before diagnosis. Understanding why patients visit the emergency department before cancer diagnosis is important, particularly for patients who live in rural or marginalized areas, or those who have specific cancer types.

The emergency department plays an important role in the diagnosis of cancer for many patients. Several large studies have identified several routes to cancer diagnosis, with the emergency department representing one such route.1,2 Studies have estimated that 16%–50% of cancers are first identified or suspected via the emergency department, depending on the type of cancer.1–5

The emergency department is a distressing environment for patients to receive a possible cancer diagnosis.6–8 Moreover, it is frequently ill-equipped to provide ongoing continuity of care, which can lead patients down a poorly defined diagnostic pathway before receiving a confirmed diagnosis based on tissue and a subsequent treatment plan.9,10 Patients whose cancer is diagnosed via the emergency department have worse outcomes, with a higher stage of cancer at diagnosis and lower overall survival, independent of stage.11–13 Patients are more likely to be older, have less access to primary care, be of lower socioeconomic status, and be from racial and ethnic minority populations than those whose cancer is diagnosed via other pathways,2,14–16 suggesting possible inequities in health care systems.

Relationships between emergency department use before cancer diagnosis and several patient-level characteristics — such as geographic location, immigration, and (given the impact of COVID-19) year of diagnosis — have not been clearly established. Patient populations that disproportionally use the emergency department before cancer diagnosis should be identified. Understanding factors associated with emergency department use before a confirmed cancer diagnosis could help lead to solutions to reduce the need for emergency department use during the diagnostic phase and improve access to cancer care after an emergency department visit. We sought to describe emergency department use before a cancer diagnosis and evaluate factors associated with emergency department use within the 90 days before a cancer diagnosis in Ontario, Canada.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of population-based administrative health data from Jan. 1, 2014, to Dec. 31, 2021, in Ontario, Canada, using province-wide databases held at ICES. Ontario has publicly funded health care coverage for medically necessary care; therefore, these databases capture almost all health care utilization in the province. We followed the Reporting of Studies Conducted Using Observational Routinely Collected Health Data statement to report our study.17

Data sources

We identified adult patients with cancer from the Ontario Cancer Registry, which contains data on all cancers diagnosed in the province (except basal and squamous cell skin cancers).18 The Canadian Institute for Health Information’s National Ambulatory Care Reporting System contains abstracted data on all emergency department visits.19 The Discharge Abstract Database captures information on all acute care hospital admissions and inpatient surgical procedures. The Ontario Health Insurance Plan (OHIP) database contains all physician billings. We used the Immigration, Refugees, and Citizenship Canada database to identify immigration status. The Registered Persons Database contains demographic information and vital statistics, such as deaths for all Ontario residents, including out-of-hospital deaths.20 Appendix 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.240952/tab-related-content, provides further information about data sets. These data sets were linked using unique encoded identifiers and analyzed at ICES.

Study participants

We included all patients aged 18 years or older who had a confirmed primary cancer diagnosis made between Jan. 1, 2014, and Dec. 31, 2021, in the OCR. We examined the first primary cancer diagnosis for each patient during this period. The diagnosis date recorded in the OCR is the pathology-proven specimen collection date. We included patients with a date of death within 90 days before the cancer diagnosis date to allow for postmortem diagnosis or small discrepancies in recorded dates. We excluded patients without a valid OHIP number and patients with a date of death occurring more than 90 days before the cancer diagnosis date.

Outcome

Our outcome of interest was any emergency department visit within 90 days before the first cancer diagnosis date in the OCR, as we assumed that many of these visits were likely related to the cancer diagnosis. We also chose this 90-day period based on discussions with our research team (including oncologists and emergency and family physicians from academic and community areas in Ontario) on time to follow-up and confirmatory tissue diagnosis from various emergency department settings, in addition to previous studies using administrative data that reported median diagnostic periods of less than 90 days.21,22 For patients with more than 1 emergency department visit during this period, we used the visit closest to the cancer diagnosis date as the outcome date.

Exposure variables and covariates

Our exposure variables of interest were those previously reported to be associated with emergency department use before cancer diagnosis and agreed on by the research team a priori. Anchored at the time of diagnosis, factors of interest included patient demographics (age, sex, immigration within previous 5 years, rural residence, Ontario Health region),12 marginalization (area-level material resources and housing or dwelling stability),2,15 previous health care use (hospital admissions and emergency department visits before cancer diagnosis), continuity of medical care (proportion of visits made to the most visited single provider),12 comorbidities,3,12 cancer type,1,2 and diagnosis year.

For previous health care use and continuity of medical care, we evaluated health care use in the 6–30 months before cancer diagnosis to allow for a 6-month washout period to ensure cancer-related health care use was not captured.23 We used components of the Ontario Marginalization Index (ON-Marg) as markers of socioeconomic status. The ON-Marg Index is calculated using the most recent Canadian Census data and based on the dissemination area (400–700 residents) of each patient.24 We used the material resources and household or dwelling components of ON-Marg, categorized as quintiles, with higher quintiles representing the most marginalized areas.

To categorize continuity of care, we used a definition based on the Usual Provider of Care Index, which defines the usual provider of outpatient physician visits (office or clinic or home) for patients with 3 or more visits in the 6–30 months before diagnosis.23,25 We identified patients with a usual provider of care (> 50% of visits were with that provider) who was a general practitioner or family physician; those with a usual provider of care who was a specialist; those with no usual provider of care, but who saw a general practitioner or family physician; those with no usual provider of care and who did not see a general practitioner or family physician. We also defined patients with only 1 or 2 outpatient physician visits and those with no outpatient physician visits. We modified the Usual Provider of Care Index slightly to differentiate between visits to a general practitioner or family physician or to a specialist as the usual provider of care (eMethods in Appendix 1 provides further information about covariate definitions).

Statistical analysis

We compared the distributions of baseline patient characteristics between patients with and without an emergency department visit in the 90 days before cancer diagnosis. We reported frequencies for categorical variables and medians with interquartile ranges for continuous variables. We used standardized differences to assess distributional balance between the 2 groups, where a value less than 0.1 indicated balance.26 For patients with an emergency department visit before diagnosis, we identified the main diagnosis code (from the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision) of the emergency department visit by type of cancer.

To evaluate the association of individual factors with the odds of an emergency department visit before cancer diagnosis, we used multivariable logistic regression where models were adjusted for the covariates. We calculated adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We included observations with missing data in the regression using a separate category for missing or unknown.

We conducted several sensitivity analyses. First, we excluded patients with a death date on or before the date of cancer diagnosis to ensure that possible data discrepancies or postmortem diagnoses did not alter the conclusions. Second, to determine whether the COVID-19 pandemic affected certain factors associated with emergency department use before cancer diagnosis, we stratified our analysis by prepandemic (diagnosis before Mar. 14, 2020) and pandemic periods. Finally, we explored 2 different cut-off points for the outcome of emergency department use before cancer diagnosis by analyzing emergency department use within the 30 days and 180 days before diagnosis.

Ethics approval

ICES is a prescribed entity under Ontario’s Personal Health Information Protection Act (PHIPA). Projects that use data collected by ICES under section 45 of PHIPA, and use no other data, are exempt from ethics board review. The use of the data in this study is authorized under section 45 and was approved by ICES’ Privacy and Legal Office.

Results

After excluding 3936 records without a valid OHIP number, we identified 655 288 unique patients with a diagnosis of cancer made between Jan. 1, 2014, and Dec. 31, 2021. After applying exclusion criteria, we excluded 4066 patients who were younger than 18 years old and 211 patients with a death date more than 90 days before the cancer diagnosis date, leaving 651 071 patients in the study. Of these, 229 683 (35.3%) patients had an emergency department visit in the 90 days before their cancer diagnosis.

Among the patients with an emergency department visit before cancer diagnosis, 178 889 (77.9%) had an emergency department visit 0–30 days before diagnosis, 30 648 (13.3%) had an emergency department visit 31–60 days before diagnosis, and 20 146 (8.8%) had an emergency department visit 61–90 days before diagnosis. Among patients who were seen in the emergency department within 90 days before their cancer diagnosis, 64% had 1 emergency department visit, 23% had 2 visits, and 13% had 3 or more visits.

The most common emergency department diagnoses were usually related to the underlying malignancy (Appendix 1, eTable 1). For example, among patients with colorectal cancer, the most commonly documented emergency department diagnoses were obstruction and abdominal pain. For patients with gastroesophageal cancer, emergency department diagnoses were related to gastrointestinal bleeding, dysphagia, and abdominal pain; for patients with bladder or urinary cancers, emergency department diagnoses were related to hematuria and urinary tract infections.

In the multivariable model of factors associated with emergency department use in the 90 days before cancer diagnosis, a number of factors were significantly associated with an increased odds of an emergency department visit, including older age (age ≥ 75 yr OR 1.54, 95% CI 1.51–1.57 v. 18–55 yr), rurality (OR 1.15, 95% CI 1.13–1.17), residence in northern Ontario (North East region OR 1.14, 95% CI 1.10–1.17 and North West region OR 1.27, 95% CI 1.21–1.32 v. Toronto region), and living in the most marginalized areas (material resource deprivation OR 1.37, 95% CI 1.35–1.40 and household or dwelling stability OR 1.09, 95% CI 1.06–1.11 v. least marginalized area) (Table 2). Patients who were recent immigrants (within previous 5 yr) were less likely to use the emergency department before diagnosis (OR 0.73, 95% CI 0.69–0.76).

Patients with previous hospital admissions (OR 1.07, 95% CI 1.05–1.08) or emergency department use (OR 1.52, 95% CI 1.50–1.53) had increased odds of an emergency department visit before cancer diagnosis. Patients with comorbidities were also more likely to have had an emergency department visit before diagnosis.

Compared with patients with a family physician as their usual provider of care, those who had no outpatient visits (OR 2.09, 95% CI 2.04–2.14) or fewer than 3 outpatient visits (OR 1.41, 95% CI 1.38–1.45) in the 6–30 months before cancer diagnosis were more likely to have an emergency department visit before their cancer diagnosis. Conversely, patients with 3 or more outpatient visits but no usual provider of care were less likely to use the emergency department (OR 0.88, 95% CI 0.82–0.95 if no visits to any family physician; OR 0.87, 95% CI 0.86–0.88 if any visits to any family physician).

We found significant variation in the odds of emergency department use before diagnosis by cancer type, with the highest odds of an emergency department visit among patients with neurologic cancer (OR 38.90, 95% CI 36.49–41.47), pancreatic cancer (OR 14.70, 95% CI 14.10–15.32), liver or gallbladder cancer (OR 10.15, 95% CI 9.74–10.57), thoracic cancer (OR 7.18, 95% CI 6.99–7.37), or gastroesophageal cancer (OR 6.60, 95% CI 6.36–6.85), compared with breast cancer. Cancer diagnoses in 2020 and 2021 were associated with increased odds of emergency department use (OR 1.17, 95% CI 1.14–1.20 in 2020 and OR 1.05, 95% CI 1.03–1.08 in 2021, v. 2014).

We did not find differences in the direction or magnitude of results in the sensitivity analysis that excluded patients who died before or at diagnosis date and the analysis that explored prepandemic and pandemic periods (Appendix 1, eTable 2 and eTable 3). The sensitivity analyses exploring emergency department use in the 30 days and 180 days before cancer diagnosis did not result in substantial differences in the direction of results (Appendix 1, eTable 4). However, when exploring emergency department use in the 30 days before cancer diagnosis, the ORs for rural and northern settings were slightly reduced, which may reflect that 30 days is too short a period to obtain a definitive cancer diagnosis for patients who visited the emergency department in lower-resourced settings.

Interpretation

In this study of more than 650 000 adult patients with cancer diagnosed between 2014 and 2021 in Ontario, more than one-third used the emergency department in the 90 days before cancer diagnosis. Furthermore, a substantial proportion of these patients had more than 1 emergency department visit. Among patients who used the emergency department, more than half were admitted to hospital.

Our results are consistent with studies from the United Kingdom, which reported that 24%–31% of cancer diagnoses involved the emergency department.1,2 In the United States, a recent study from Indiana reported that 11.5% of patients had an emergency department visit in the 6 months before cancer diagnosis,16 whereas in a study of Medicare beneficiaries older than 65 years, 23% were seen in the emergency department in the 30 days before diagnosis.27

Several reasons may explain why patients use the emergency department before diagnosis, including symptoms related to cancer that prompt an emergency department visit that are investigated and eventually diagnosed as cancer, or a visit for an unrelated problem during which clinical examination or investigations surface findings that lead to a cancer diagnosis. Based on the results of our study, the most common emergency department diagnoses were likely related to the underlying type of cancer.

We found several factors associated with emergency department use before cancer diagnosis, including age, marginalization, geographic location, comorbidities, previous health care use, continuity of medical care, type of cancer, and year of diagnosis. Our findings are similar to previous studies that have found older age1,2,13,14,28 and markers of lower socioeconomic status2,16,29 to be associated with cancer diagnosis via the emergency department. We also found that geographic location was associated with emergency department use before cancer diagnosis, which has not previously been well explored. Specifically, patients from rural regions and from northern Ontario had increased odds of emergency department use before cancer diagnosis. The increased use of the emergency department before cancer diagnosis among older adults, patients of lower socioeconomic status, and those from rural or northern areas is likely multifactorial, with various disease-, patient-, and system-level factors associated with these findings.30 Systemic issues such as access to primary care and other types of ambulatory care (e.g., specialists, outpatient diagnostics), as well as lower participation in screening programs, may partially account for the higher odds of emergency department use before cancer diagnosis observed among these patient groups.9,31,32 Poor access to ambulatory care may result in these patients disproportionally requiring the emergency department for their health care needs,9,33–35 or delaying care and presenting to the emergency department once their cancer has become symptomatic, requiring acute medical attention.

We found that patients with fewer than 3 ambulatory visits were more likely to use the emergency department before cancer diagnosis than patients with 3 or more visits to their usual primary care provider. Conversely, patients with 3 or more ambulatory care visits, but who did not have a usual provider of care, were less likely to use the emergency department before diagnosis than those with a usual primary care provider. These results may suggest that patients who do not regularly use ambulatory care seek care in the emergency department setting, possibly because they do not have any other options for timely outpatient care or are unaware of what options exist. In contrast, patients who regularly obtain care in the ambulatory setting, even without a usual provider, may be able to avoid using the emergency department because they have multiple options to access care in ambulatory settings, such as other providers or walk-in clinics.

Patients with neurologic cancers had extremely high odds of emergency department use before cancer diagnosis. This is likely because of the emergent nature of presentation, with acute neurologic symptoms such as weakness, confusion, or seizures, which require urgent assessment. Other studies have also shown that patients with brain cancer were more likely to have emergency department use before diagnosis.1,2 Furthermore, patients with cancers that may initially present with vague symptoms — such as pancreatic, liver, or thoracic cancers — also had high odds of emergency department use before cancer. Future work on how to identify these cancers early is needed. We found that patients with any other cancer had higher odds of emergency department use before diagnosis than those with breast cancer. This may be a result of widespread access and uptake of screening programs for breast cancer,36 meaning that patients who undergo screening have their cancers detected earlier, before symptoms necessitate emergency department use.

Patients with cancer diagnosed in 2020 and 2021 had higher adjusted odds of emergency department use before their diagnosis than those diagnosed in 2014. Although patients whose cancer was diagnosed in 2019 also had an increased odds of emergency department use before diagnosis, the odds were not as high as in 2020. At the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic, in-person health care was extensively restricted. Many in-person physician appointments and outpatient diagnostics were delayed or cancelled.37–39 Because of these restrictions, patients may have relied on the emergency department to see a physician regarding symptoms associated with cancer or to expedite diagnostic imaging or specialist follow-up that was delayed in the outpatient setting, compared with before the pandemic.

The emergency department is not an ideal environment to manage patients with a suspected cancer diagnosis.40 Emergency departments are routinely overcrowded and have limited privacy.9 Receiving a potential cancer diagnosis in this setting has been described by patients as distressing.6,7 Furthermore, emergency departments provide acute episodic care to patients; most emergency departments are not equipped to ensure ongoing care and confirm that a timely cancer workup occurs. Patients with a suspected cancer diagnosis who are discharged from the emergency department have variable access to further diagnostic workup based on the type of cancer and where patients were seen;10 patients may leave the emergency department with uncertainty about their follow-up and prognosis. Although some patients may require hospital admission because of symptoms or complications related to the cancer presentation, relying on admission to hospital to ensure or expedite testing and follow-up from the emergency department is costly and contributes to hospital crowding, especially if these can be done in the outpatient setting.41,42 Given the high rate of emergency department use before cancer diagnosis, the availability of resources for further cancer workup in different emergency department settings across Ontario requires further attention. Equitable access to reliable diagnostic workup from different emergency departments across Ontario should be ensured.

Limitations

We were unable to identify cancer-related emergency department visits; therefore, some included emergency department visits may not have been related to cancer. However, we determined the common emergency department diagnoses by cancer type, and most visits appeared to be related to the cancer diagnosis.

We chose to evaluate emergency department visits in the 90-day period before cancer diagnosis because many were likely related to the cancer or its underlying pathophysiology. However, the time period to examine cancer-related emergency department use before diagnosis is debatable; some patients may wait more than 90 days to receive a cancer diagnosis after an emergency department visit. A recent study used a 6-month period,12 but we chose 90 days to reduce the likelihood of including emergency department visits that were unrelated to the cancer diagnosis. Accordingly, we conducted 2 sensitivity analyses using emergency department visits in the 30 days and 180 days before diagnosis, which did not substantially change our results.

Our results may have been confounded, given the limited variables included in our analysis. We were unable to account for several covariates that may have been related to emergency department use before cancer diagnosis, such as ethnicity, race, and gender, as these data are not reliably captured in the databases. We did not account for primary care provided by nurse practitioners in our variable of usual providers of care.

We evaluated health care use by patients in Ontario, which may limit generalization of our findings to other settings. Our study used administrative data, and misclassification bias from coding errors in the variables used could have been present. A few patients had death dates before diagnosis dates, which may be a result of data discrepancies; however, our sensitivity analysis excluding these patients did not change results. Finally, we included data from 2014 to 2021, which included the start of the COVID-19 pandemic. We included data from only the first 2 years of the pandemic in our analysis, and trends may have changed with subsequent waves of the pandemic.

Conclusion

Use of the emergency department is common before cancer diagnosis, with one-third of patients with cancer having used the emergency department before diagnosis in Ontario. We identified several patient groups that were more likely to visit the emergency department within 90 days before cancer diagnosis, including those who were older, were living in more marginalized areas, were from rural or northern areas, had comorbidities, had several types of cancer, or were diagnosed during the COVID-19 pandemic. The reasons why these patient groups are more likely to use the emergency department before cancer diagnosis must be explored further to improve their care and outcomes.
