Abstract
We prove Hawking’s singularity theorem for spacetime metrics of local Lipschitz regularity. The proof rests on (1) new estimates for the Ricci curvature of regularising smooth metrics that are based upon a quite general Friedrichs-type lemma and (2) the replacement of the usual focusing techniques for timelike geodesics—which in the absence of a classical ODE-theory for the initial value problem are no longer available—by a worldvolume estimate based on a segment-type inequality that allows one to control the volume of the set of points in a spacelike surface that possess long maximisers.

1
Introduction

The singularity theorems form an important body of results in Lorentzian differential geometry that firmly establish the occurrence of spacetime singularities as a generic feature of General Relativity (GR). More precisely they assert causal geodesic incompleteness under different sets of physically reasonable conditions like those associated with complete gravitational collapse
[
50
]
or an expanding universe
[
27
]
. Naturally these results, which appeared roughly in the second half of the 1960-ies, were formulated for smooth spacetimes. However, already Hawking and Ellis in their classic
[
28
]
discussed the issue of regularity: A lack of low-regularity versions of the theorems, that is for spacetime metrics
g
below the
C^{2}
-class, would undermine their physical significance. Indeed, then incompleteness and hence a spacetime singularity in the sense of the standard definition (see e.g.
[
15
, p. 10]
and
[
28
, Sec. 8.1]
for a discussion) could be avoided by a drop in regularity of
g
: If, for example,
g
were in
C^{1,1}
, then via the field equations there would be a finite jump in the matter variables, which hardly could be termed ‘singular’ on physical grounds. Therefore
[
28
, Sec. 8.4]
contains an in-depth discussion of these issues in which the authors argue for a
C^{1,1}
-version of Hawking’s theorem
[
28
, Sec. 8.2, Thm. 4]
and express their expectation that also all the other classical theorems would extend to this regularity. Accepting this for the moment one may observe that given these results, the alternative to incompleteness would now be locally unbounded curvature. This, however, still might be manageable as long as one can make sense of the field equations, which, in particular, is possible if
g
is locally Lipschitz continuous, i.e.,
g\in C^{0,1}
, as again highlighted in
[
28
, Sec. 8.4]
. In particular, this includes very prominent classes of solutions like impulsive gravitational waves (e.g.
[
51
]
,
[
25
, Ch. 20]
,
[
52
]
), thin shells and many matched spacetimes (e.g.
[
29
,
32
,
42
]
) which all exhibit a
\delta
-function like curvature concentrated on a hypersurface. This is precisely the regularity class we deal with in this work, where we extend the validity of the Hawking singularity theorem to spacetime metrics
g\in C^{0,1}
.

While the singularity theorems’ regularity issues have been duly addressed throughout the decades, see e.g.
[
56
, Sec. 6.2]
, real progress only has been made rather recently.
By extending Lorentzian causality theory (mainly) to continuous spacetime metrics
[
14
,
16
,
45
,
53
,
37
]
and to even more general settings
[
46
,
36
]
on the one hand, and by sharpening the analytic tool of approximation via convolution on the other, the classical theorems could be proven for
C^{1,1}
-metrics
[
38
,
39
,
21
]
and later also for
g\in C^{1}

[
20
,
55
,
35
]
. Here we take this endeavour one decisive step further, namely to
g\in C^{0,1}
.

Let us briefly address the added difficulties we have to deal with in this regularity. While for
C^{1}
-metrics the initial value problem for the geodesic equation is still solvable, albeit not uniquely so, we here face the problem that its right hand side is merely locally bounded. This forces us to work with maximising curves rather than with solutions to the ODE and so the ODE-techniques decisively used in the
C^{1}
-results
[
20
, Sec. 2]
to approximate the geodesics of the rough metric by geodesics of approximating metrics are no longer at our disposal. This forces us to significantly modify the analytical core of the proof, which provides the focusing of geodesics under curvature bounds. While we still rely on a regularisation scheme that allows us to exploit the distributional strong energy condition, we will then apply the newly developed worldvolume estimates of
[
22
]
to the smooth regularising metrics. This will allow us to control (the volume of) the set of points on a Cauchy surface that possess long maximisers, but on the other hand forces us to sharpen our estimates on regularised curvature. Indeed, exploiting Friedrichs lemma-type arguments we provide new
L^{p}
-estimates on the Ricci curvature for Lipschitz metrics and their regularisation, which constitute the main analytical advance of our work. Moreover, we also have to deal with the ‘initial condition’ of the theorem, i.e., a bound on the mean curvature of a spacelike hypersurface. We extend the standard condition to Lipschitz regularity by a smearing out of the hypersurface.

Finally, let us address the potential extensions of our methods to the other singularity theorems within Lipschitz regularity. In the Penrose case, where one is concerned with null geodesics emanating from a trapped surface, one would have to replace the usual focusing estimates by a null version of the segment-type inequality. While this seems feasible in principle, it cannot be expected to be a simple variation of the timelike analysis of
[
22
]
. Moreover, the very notion of a trapped surface would have to be adapted in a way similar to the smearing out of the (Cauchy-) hypersurface in the present work. Additionally, the regularisation approach to leverage the distributional null energy condition to gain estimates on the respective approximations is technically more demanding, cf. the
C^{1}
-case in
[
20
,
35
]
—all issues we reserve for future research. Finally, the Hawking-Penrose theorem already classically relies on a more involved focusing analysis providing the existence of conjugate points along a single causal geodesic solely under an energy and a genericity condition which, from our present perspective, seems particularly difficult to extend to the Lipschitz case.

We organise our work as follows. After stating our conventions, we collect some necessary prerequisites in Section
2
. This first concerns causality theory for (Lipschitz)-continuous metrics, where we also detail how for
g\in C^{0,1}
we may exploit results from the more general settings of closed cone structures of
[
47
]
and Lorentzian length spaces
[
36
]
. Then we set up the regularisation scheme to be used throughout and, finally,
introduce the worldvolume estimates of
[
22
]
, which are inspired by the segment inequality of
[
13
]
. In Section
3
we provide regularisation results for distributional curvature. In particular, we establish new
L^{p}
-convergence (
1\leq p<\infty
) as well as
L^{\infty}
-boundedness results for the Ricci curvature of a sequence of metrics approximating
g\in C^{0,1}
(Proposition
3.1
), which are based on the quite general Friedrichs Lemma
3.3
. In Section
4
we will be concerned with the initial condition of the Hawking theorem. We develop a sensible notion of mean curvature of a hypersurface in case
g\in C^{0,1}
and establish its compatibility with the smooth setting. Finally, in Section
5
we formulate and prove our main result, a Hawking singularity theorem for locally Lipschitz continuous spacetimes. As usual we give two formulations, one in the globally hyperbolic case and one supposing just a (converging) compact spacelike hypersurface. Finally, we also comment on the interrelation of our and related
C^{1}
-results to several synthetic versions of the singularity theorems that have appeared recently.

2.1
Causality theory

We base causality notions on locally Lipschitz curves, that is we call a curve
\gamma:I\to M
defined on some arbitrary non-trivial interval
I

timelike, null, causal, future
or
past directed
if it is locally Lipschitz and
\dot{\gamma}
has the respective properties almost everywhere.
Then
p\ll q
(resp.
p\leq q
) means that there exists a future directed timelike (resp. causal) curve from
p
to
q
,
I^{+}(A):=\{q\in M:\,p\ll q\ \mathrm{for\,some}\,p\in A\}
and
J^{+}(A):=\{q\in M:\,p\leq q\ \mathrm{for\,some}\,p\in A\}
.

We call
(M,g)

globally hyperbolic
if it is non-totally imprisoning (no future or past inextendible causal curve is contained in a compact set) and for all pairs of points
p,q\in M
the causal diamonds
J(p,q):=J^{+}(p)\cap J^{-}(q)
are compact. Note that this implies that
M
is strongly causal by
[
53
, Prop. 5.6]
.
A subset
\Sigma\subseteq M
is called a
Cauchy hypersurface
if it is met
exactly once by every inextendible causal curve. It is always a closed acausal topological hypersurface, and
(M,g)
is globally hyperbolic if and only if it possesses a Cauchy hypersurface
\Sigma
, in which case
M
is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}\times\Sigma
,
[
53
, Sec. 5]
.
We define the
Cauchy development
of some set
S
as

D(S):=D^{+}(S)\cup D^{-}(S),

(1)

where
D^{\pm}(S)
are the sets of points
p\in M
such that every future/past-directed future/past inextendible causal curve through
p
meets
S
, cf.
[
14
]
. The interior of the Cauchy development
D(S)^{\circ}
of any acausal set
S
is globally hyperbolic
[
53
, Cor. 5.8]
. Moreover, the Avez-Seifert theorem extends to continuous globally hyperbolic spacetimes, i.e., in such
(M,g)
there is a globally maximising causal curve between any pair of causally related points (cf.
[
53
, Prop. 6.4]
). However, the relation between maximisers and geodesics becomes more subtle here.

Remark 2.1

(Geodesics and maximisers)
.

(i)

For
g\in C^{0,1}
the right hand side of the geodesic equation is merely locally bounded and hence we are outside classical ODE-theory. However, the initial value problem has solutions in the sense of Filippov
[
17
]
which are
C^{1}
-curves
[
57
]
, satisfying a differential inclusion relation for the essential convex hull of the locally bounded right hand side. But solutions of the geodesic equations may fail to be local maximisers already for
g
in the Hölder class
C^{1,\alpha}
for
\alpha<1

[
26
,
54
]
.

(ii)

Conversely, for
g\in C^{0,1}
any maximiser, when parametrized by
g
-arclength is a Filippov-geodesic of regularity
C^{1,1}
, see
[
40
, Thm. 1.1]
and the discussion following it. Also, such maximisers are either timelike or null throughout
[
23
,
40
]
.

For
p,q\in M
the future
time separation
is defined by

\tau(p,q):=\sup(\left\{L(\gamma):\gamma\;\text{is a future directed causal curve from }p\text{ to }q\right\}\cup\{0\}),

(2)

where
L(\gamma)
denotes the Lorentzian arc-length of
\gamma:I\to M
, i.e.,
L(\gamma):=\int_{I}\sqrt{|g(\dot{\gamma}(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))|}dt
. Moreover, one defines the future time separation to a subset
S
by

\tau_{S}(p):=\sup_{q\in S}\tau(q,p).

(3)

While it is known that for continuous metrics basic features of causality theory (such as the push up principle and the openness of
I^{+}
) break down
[
14
,
24
]
, this is not the case for the class of causally plain metrics, to which any
g\in C^{0,1}
belongs
[
14
, Thm. 1.20]
. Moreover, although we will be concerned with the (low regularity) spacetime setting, we will freely use results from causality theory derived in more general settings, in particular the closed cone structures of
[
46
]
and the Lorentzian (pre-)length spaces of
[
36
]
. Here we briefly recall how continuous spacetime metrics
g
fall into these settings.

Remark 2.2

(Cone structures)
.

Causality in cone structures, which generalise Lorentzian causality theory to an order theoretic setting, were introduced by Ettore Minguzzi in the seminal paper
[
46
]
. A
cone structure

(M,C)
is a multivalued map
M\ni p\mapsto C_{p}
, where
C_{p}\subseteq T_{p}M\setminus 0
is a closed sharp convex non-empty cone,
[
46
, Definition 2.2]
. It is called
closed

[
46
, Def. 2.3]
, if it is a closed
subbundle of the slit tangent bundle.

Let

be a locally Lipschitz metric, and
C_{p}:=\{v\in T_{p}M\,\backslash\,\{0\}:g(v,v)\leq 0,v\mbox{ future directed}\}
as in
[
46
, Ex. 2.1]
, then by
[
46
, Prop. 2.4]
the map

is locally Lipschitz and by
[
46
, Prop. 2.3]

having nonempty interior.

Remark 2.3

(Lorentzian length spaces)
.

Lorentzian length spaces were introduced by Kunzinger and Sämann in
[
36
]
as an analogue of metric length spaces. Let
(X,d)
be a metric space endowed with a preorder

as well as a transitive relation

\rho\colon X\times X\to[0,\infty]

, then

is called a

with

The length of a future-directed causal

(i.e.

\gamma(t_{1})\leq\gamma(t_{2})
) is defined as
L_{\rho}(\gamma):=\inf\{\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\rho(\gamma(t_{i}),\gamma(t_{i+1})):a=t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{N}=b,\ N\in\mathbb{N}\}
,
and
(X,\rho)
is called a
Lorentzian length space
if, in addition to some technical assumptions (cf.
[
36
, Def. 3.22]
)
\rho
is intrinsic, i.e.,
\rho(p,q)=\sup\{L_{\rho}(\gamma):\gamma
future-directed causal from
p
to
q\}
.

By
[
36
, Theorem 5.12]
every continuous, strongly causal and causally
plain spacetime (and hence every strongly causal Lipschitz spacetime, cf.
[
14
, Thm. 1.20]
)
is a (strongly localisable) Lorentzian length space (with
\leq,\ll
as usual,
\rho=\tau
, and

).

2.2
Regularisation of distributional tensor fields

A key technical tool throughout this work is regularisation of distributional tensor fields, which we introduce next. Let
(U_{\alpha},\psi_{\alpha})
(
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
) be a countable and locally finite family of relatively compact chart neighbourhoods covering
M
and pick a subordinate partition of unity
(\xi_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
with
\mathrm{supp}(\xi_{\alpha})\subseteq U_{\alpha}
for all
\alpha
. Also, choose a family of cut-off functions
\chi_{\alpha}\in\mathscr{D}(U_{\alpha})
with
\chi_{\alpha}\equiv 1
on a
neighbourhood of
\mathrm{supp}(\xi_{\alpha})
. Let
\rho\in{\mathscr{D}}(B_{1}(0))
be a non-negative test function with unit integral and set,
for
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
,
\rho_{\varepsilon}(x):=\varepsilon^{-n}\rho\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)
. Then denoting by
f_{*}
(resp.
f^{*}
) push-forward (resp. pull-back) of distributions under a diffeomorphism

, for any tensor distribution
\mathcal{T}\in\mathscr{D}^{\prime}\mathcal{T}^{r}_{s}(M)
we set

\mathcal{T}\star_{M}\rho_{\varepsilon}(x):=\sum\limits_{\alpha}\chi_{\alpha}(x)\,(\psi_{\alpha})^{*}\Big[\big((\psi_{\alpha})_{*}(\xi_{\alpha}\cdot\mathcal{T})\big)*\rho_{\varepsilon}\Big](x).

(4)

Here,
(\psi_{\alpha})_{*}(\xi_{\alpha}\cdot\mathcal{T})
is a compactly supported distributional tensor field on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and convolution with
\rho_{\varepsilon}
is understood component-wise, yielding a smooth field on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The cut-off functions
\chi_{\alpha}
ensure that
(\varepsilon,x)\mapsto\mathcal{T}\star_{M}\rho_{\varepsilon}(x)

is a smooth map on
(0,1]\times M
. For any compact set
K\Subset M
there is an
\varepsilon_{K}
such that for all
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{K}
and all
x\in K
, equation (
4
) reduces to a finite sum with all
\chi_{\alpha}\equiv 1
(which can therefore be omitted from the formula), namely when
\varepsilon_{K}
is less than the distance between the support of
\xi_{\alpha}\circ\psi_{\alpha}^{-1}
and the boundary of
\psi_{\alpha}(U_{\alpha})

for all
\alpha
with
U_{\alpha}\cap K\neq\emptyset
.

Since the mollifier
\rho
above is assumed to be nonnegative, it follows that for any nonnegative scalar distribution
u\in{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}(M)
we also have
u\star_{M}\rho_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
.

\ast
will exclusively denote convolutions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, while

\star_{M}
stands for the manifold convolution (
4
). We will write

g_{\varepsilon}:=g\star_{M}\rho_{\varepsilon},

(5)

but to avoid confusion we will otherwise not use the subscript
\varepsilon
to denote quantities derived from

. Instead we will be more explicit and write e.g.
\mathrm{Ric}[g_{\varepsilon}]
for the Ricci curvature derived from the metric

but e.g.
\mathrm{Ric}[g]\star_{M}\rho_{\varepsilon}
for the manifold convolution of the Ricci curvature of

. Finally, for two Lorentzian metrics

on

we write

and say that

has

than

, if for all non-vanishing vectors

\displaystyle g_{1}(X,X)\leq 0\quad\text{implies}\quad g_{2}(X,X)<0.

(6)

Lemma 2.4

(Convergence of approximating metrics)
.

Let
g\in C^{0,1}(M)
be a Lorentzian metric and
let
g_{\varepsilon}
be as in (

). Then there are smooth Lorentzian metrics
\hat{g}_{\varepsilon}
and
\check{g}_{\varepsilon}
on

with the following properties

(i)

\check{g}_{\varepsilon}\prec g\prec\hat{g}_{\varepsilon}
.

(ii)

\check{g}_{\varepsilon}

\hat{g}_{\varepsilon}\to g
, and
(\check{g}_{\varepsilon})^{-1}
,
(\hat{g}_{\varepsilon})^{-1}\to g^{-1}
locally uniformly and in

(iii)

\check{g}_{\varepsilon}-g_{\varepsilon}\to 0
,
\hat{g}_{\varepsilon}-g_{\varepsilon}\to 0
, and

,
(\hat{g}_{\varepsilon})^{-1}-(g_{\varepsilon})^{-1}\to 0

In particular,

(iv)

For any compact subset

2.3
Volume comparison

To formulate the proofs of our main results and to understand the singularity-theorem adjacent result of
[
22
, Thm. 4.1]

which they rely on,
we need to introduce some corresponding notions. Since we will apply these results to smooth regularisations of the rough metric we assume
g
to be smooth throughout this subsection.

We consider a smooth spacelike hypersurface
\Sigma
with unit future normal
\vec{n}
and corresponding mean curvature bounded by
H\leq\beta<0
. Moreover, we assume a lower bound on the Ricci curvature of
(M,g)
in timelike unit directions, i.e.,
\mathrm{Ric}[g](X,X)\geq\kappa n
, where
\kappa
is any negative number (with potentially large modulus). We denote by
\exp_{\Sigma}^{+}:\mathcal{I}^{+}\to M
the future normal exponential map to
\Sigma
. Here
\mathcal{I}^{+}=\{(t,x)\in[0,s^{+}(x))\times\Sigma\}
where

is the maximal domain of definition of the unique geodesic
\gamma_{x}
with initial data
\gamma_{x}(0)=x
and
\dot{\gamma}_{x}(0)=\vec{n}_{x}
.
Further we write
c^{+}_{\Sigma}:\Sigma\to(0,\infty]
for the future cut function of
\Sigma
, i.e.
c^{+}_{\Sigma}(x)=\sup\{t\in[0,s^{+}(x)):\tau_{\Sigma}(\exp_{x}(t\vec{n}_{x}))=t\}
. Then for
T>0
and
B\subseteq\Sigma
we define the future evolution

\Omega^{+}_{T}(B):=\{\exp_{x}(t\vec{n}_{x}):x\in B,\,t\in[0,T]\cap[0,c^{+}_{\Sigma}(x))\}

(7)

and for
\eta>0
(considered small as compared to

) the set of
(T+\eta)
-regular points
\mathrm{Reg}^{+}_{\eta}(T)
of

such that
s^{+}(x)>T+\eta
and
\gamma_{x}
is maximising on
[0,T+\eta]
, i.e.,

\mathrm{Reg}^{+}_{\eta}(T)=(c^{+}_{\Sigma})^{-1}([T+\eta,\infty]).

(8)

Observe that for any
B\subseteq\mathrm{Reg}^{+}_{\eta}(T)
we have that
\Omega^{+}_{T}(B)=\exp_{\Sigma}^{+}([0,T]\times B)
. Now volume comparison techniques lead to the following segment-type inequality (
[
22
, Prop. 3.10]
)
for continuous
f\geq 0
and
B\subseteq\mathrm{Reg}_{\eta}^{+}(T)
with finite volume (i.e.,
0<\sigma(B)<\infty
), where
\sigma
denotes the volume measure on
\Sigma
with respect to the Riemannian metric induced by

\inf_{x\in B}\int_{0}^{\min(T,s^{+}(x))}f(\exp^{+}_{\Sigma}(t,x))\,\mathrm{d}t\ \leq\ \frac{1}{C^{A-}\sigma(B)}\int_{\Omega^{+}_{T}(B)}f\,\mathrm{dvol}_{g}.

(9)

Here the so-called backwards area comparison constant is explicitly given by

C^{A-}(n,\kappa,\eta,T)=\big(\mathrm{sinh}(\eta\sqrt{|\kappa|})/\mathrm{sinh}((T+\eta)\sqrt{|\kappa|})\big)^{n-1}
. Applying inequality (
9
) to the negative part of the Ricci curvature evaluated on the future unit congruence of
\Sigma
, i.e.,
\mathrm{Ric}[g](U,U)_{-}
with

U(p)=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\right|_{s=0}\exp_{x}((t+s)\,\vec{n}_{x})

(10)

for
p=\exp^{+}_{\Sigma}(t,x)
, one has the following result, which is a slightly modified version of
[
22
, Thm. 4.1]
.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
(M,g)
be a smooth globally hyperbolic spacetime with smooth spacelike Cauchy surface

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq|\beta|+\int_{0}^{T}-(n-1)\dot{h}(t)^{2}+h(t)^{2}\mathrm{Ric}(\dot{\gamma}(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))dt=

\displaystyle>|\beta|+\int_{0}^{T}-(n-1)\dot{h}(t)^{2}+h(t)^{2}(n-1)\rho\,\mathrm{d}t-K(\beta,T,\rho)

for any smooth

. Choosing

As indicated above, we will show estimate (

) for

3
Curvature estimates

The main strategy in our regularisation approach is to employ the energy condition for the Lipschitz metric
g
, i.e., the condition that
\mathrm{Ric}[g]
is a non-negative distribution to derive local curvature bounds on the regularised metrics
g_{\varepsilon}
. However, since convergence of
\mathrm{Ric}[g_{\varepsilon}]
(and so by
Lemma

2.4
(iii)
\mathrm{Ric}[\check{g}_{\varepsilon}]
, which is the more relevant quantity in our approach) to
\mathrm{Ric}[g]
is merely distributional, we will exploit the non-negativity of
\mathrm{Ric}[g]\star_{M}\rho_{\varepsilon}
instead. To make use of this property we have to control the difference between the latter quantity and
\mathrm{Ric}[g_{\varepsilon}]
. Deriving the required estimates is the main aim of this section. More precisely we are going to establish the following result:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
(M,g)
be a Lorentzian manifold with a locally Lipschitz metric
g
. Then for
g_{\varepsilon}(=g\star_{M}\rho_{\varepsilon})
we have for any compact
K\Subset M

(i)

\|\mathrm{Ric}[g_{\varepsilon}]-\mathrm{Ric}[g]\star_{M}\rho_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{p}(K)}\rightarrow 0
for all
1\leq p<\infty
, and

(ii)

there exists some
C_{K}>0
such that for
\varepsilon
small enough

\smash{\|\mathrm{Ric}[g_{\varepsilon}]-\mathrm{Ric}[g]\star_{M}\rho_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}\leq C_{K}.}

As a first step towards a proof of Proposition
3.1
, note that explicitly calculating from (
4
) the push-forward under a chart of
g_{\varepsilon}
, the local expressions of the relevant terms in
\mathrm{Ric}[g_{\varepsilon}]-\mathrm{Ric}[g]\star_{M}\rho_{\varepsilon}
containing all second order derivatives of
g
take the form (cf.
[
20
, proof of Lemma 4.6]
) of first order derivatives of

\begin{split}\big[(\psi_{\beta})_{*}g_{\varepsilon}\big]^{ij}\big(\big[\xi\partial_{k}((\psi_{\beta})_{*}g)_{lm}\big]*\rho_{\varepsilon}\big)-\big(\big[(\psi_{\beta})_{*}g\big]^{ij}\xi\partial_{k}((&\psi_{\beta})_{*}g)_{lm}\big)*\rho_{\varepsilon}\\
&=:a_{\varepsilon}(f*\rho_{\varepsilon})-(af)*\rho_{\varepsilon},\end{split}

(15)

where
\psi_{\beta}
is a coordinate chart,
\xi
a cutoff function,
\rho_{\varepsilon}
a standard mollifier, and
*
denotes the usual convolution on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
According to (
4
) and the remarks following it, in a neighbourhood of any
K\Subset M
we can write

(\psi_{\beta})_{*}g_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{\alpha}(\psi_{\alpha}\circ\psi_{\beta}^{-1})^{*}(((\psi_{\alpha})_{*}(\xi_{\alpha}g))*\rho_{\varepsilon}).

(16)

Now set
\tilde{g}_{\alpha}:=(\psi_{\alpha})_{*}(\xi_{\alpha}g)
. Then if each
\tilde{g}_{\alpha}*\rho_{\varepsilon}
converges in
W^{1,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}\cap L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}
, so does
(\psi_{\beta})_{*}g_{\varepsilon}
. Thus for all further calculations in this section we may without loss of generality consider the case
M=\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the single chart
\psi_{\alpha}=\mathrm{id}
and
g_{\varepsilon}=g*\rho_{\varepsilon}
. Then
a_{\varepsilon}
becomes a component of the inverse of the smoothed metric
(g*\rho_{\varepsilon})^{ij}
, and
a
is a component of
g^{ij}
. Also,
f=\xi(\partial_{k}g)_{lm}
is a compactly supported
L^{\infty}
-function. Since
a_{\varepsilon}\neq a*\rho_{\varepsilon}
, we reserve the notation
a_{\varepsilon}
just for that term and write the convolution explicitly for all the others.
Hence, to prove
Proposition

3.1
we have to show that for such
a
,
a_{\varepsilon}
and
f
we have for all
K\Subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

\begin{split}&a_{\varepsilon}(f*\rho_{\varepsilon})-(af)*\rho_{\varepsilon}\to 0\quad\ \text{in $W^{1,p}(K)$ for each $p\in[1,\infty)$, as well as}\\[2.39996pt]
&\|a_{\varepsilon}(f*\rho_{\varepsilon})-(af)*\rho_{\varepsilon}\|_{W^{1,\infty}(K)}\leq C_{K}.\end{split}

(17)

We first collect all relevant properties of
a_{\varepsilon}
and
a
to be used below.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
a_{\varepsilon}
and
a
be components of
(g*\rho_{\varepsilon})^{ij}
and
g^{ij}
, respectively. Then on any compact
K\Subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have

(i)

a
is Lipschitz on
K

(ii)

a_{\varepsilon}
is smooth and Lipschitz on
K
, uniformly in
\varepsilon

(iii)

a_{\varepsilon}\to a\ \text{in}\ W^{1,p}(K)
for
1\leq p<\infty

(iv)

|a_{\varepsilon}(x)-a(x)|\leq C_{K}\varepsilon
.

Proof.

(i) and (iii) follow from the cofactor formula and standard properties of convolution, cf.
Lemma

2.4
(ii).

To prove (ii), recall first that for any locally Lipschitz function
h
we have again by standard properties of the convolution that for all small
\varepsilon

\displaystyle\mathrm{Lip}(h*\rho_{\varepsilon},K)\leq\mathrm{Lip}(h,K^{\prime}),

(18)

where
\mathrm{Lip}(h,K^{\prime})
is the Lipschitz constant of
h
on a suitable compact neighbourhood
K^{\prime}
of
K
. Next we explicitly write out
a_{\varepsilon}
using the cofactor formula

\begin{split}\absolutevalue{a_\varepsilon(x)-a_\varepsilon(z)}&=\absolutevalue{\frac{1}{\mathrm{det}\,(g*\rho_\varepsilon)(x)}((g*\rho_\varepsilon)^{\mathrm{cof}})_{ij}(x)
-\frac{1}{\mathrm{det}\,(g*\rho_\varepsilon)(z)}((g*\rho_\varepsilon)^{\mathrm{cof}})_{ij}(z)}\\
&\leq\absolutevalue{\frac{1}{\mathrm{det}\,(g*\rho_\varepsilon)(x)}}\ \bigg|((g*\rho_{\varepsilon})^{\mathrm{cof}})_{ij}(x)-((g*\rho_{\varepsilon})^{\mathrm{cof}})_{ij}(z)\bigg|\\
&\hskip 85.35826pt+\big|((g*\rho_{\varepsilon})^{\mathrm{cof}})_{ij}(z)\big|\ \bigg|\frac{1}{\det(g*\rho_\varepsilon)(x)}-\frac{1}{\det(g*\rho_\varepsilon)({z})}\bigg|\\
&{\leq}\bigg[\ C_{1}\ \absolutevalue{\frac{1}{\det(g*\rho_\varepsilon)({x})}}+\ C_{2}\ \absolutevalue{\frac{{1}
}{\det(g*\rho_\varepsilon)(z)\det(g*\rho_\varepsilon)(x)}}\bigg]\ \absolutevalue{z-x},\end{split}

(19)

where the constants
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
depend on the Lipschitz constants and the
L^{\infty}
-bounds of the components of
g
on a suitable compact neighbourhood
K^{\prime}

of
K
. Now since
\det g
is uniformly bounded away from zero on
K^{\prime}
and once more by the uniform bounds on
g
we obtain the uniform Lipschitz property on
K
.

Finally, in order to prove (iv) we again write out the cofactor formula and, by inserting and subtracting terms, we obtain a sum of terms which are products of (uniformly in
\varepsilon
) bounded functions with only a single factor being a difference of a component of
g
and its convolution with the mollifier. Since the latter is bounded by a constant times
\varepsilon
the result follows.
∎

We now begin to prove (
17
). Note that the zero order estimates follow easily from
standard properties of the convolution and Lemma
3.2
. In fact, we even have for each
K\Subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

\|a_{\varepsilon}(f*\rho_{\varepsilon})-(af)*\rho_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}\leq C_{K^{\prime}}\ \varepsilon\ \|f\|_{L^{\infty}(K^{\prime})},

(20)

which also implies suitable estimates for any remaining terms in
\mathrm{Ric}[g_{\varepsilon}]-\mathrm{Ric}\star_{M}\rho_{\varepsilon}
containing at most first derivatives of
g
.

The first order estimates are more delicate and we establish them
in the following statement, whose proof follows a layout similar to that of
[
8
, Appendix A]
.

Lemma 3.3

(Friedrichs Lemma)
.

Let
a_{\varepsilon}
,
a
be as in Lemma
3.2
and let
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be compactly supported. Then we have for each
K\Subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and each
1\leq j\leq n

(i)

\left\|\partial_{j}\big(a_{\varepsilon}(f*\rho_{\varepsilon})-(af)*\rho_{\varepsilon}\big)\right\|_{L^{p}(K)}\rightarrow 0
for all
p\in[1,\infty)
, and

(ii)

there is
C_{K}>0
such that
\left\|\partial_{j}\big(a_{\varepsilon}(f*\rho_{\varepsilon})-(af)*\rho_{\varepsilon}\big)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}\leq C_{K}
.

Proof.

To show (i), we start by writing

\displaystyle\partial_{j}\Big(a_{\varepsilon}(f*\rho_{\varepsilon})-(af)*\rho_{\varepsilon}\Big)=\partial_{j}\Big((a_{\varepsilon}-a)(f*\rho_{\varepsilon})\Big)+\partial_{j}\Big(a(f*\rho_{\varepsilon})-(af)*\rho_{\varepsilon}\Big).

(21)

The idea is to write both terms as an integral operator acting on
f
and to study the properties of the corresponding kernels. We start with the latter term on the r.h.s. of (
21
) and find

\begin{split}\partial_{j}\Big(a(f*\rho_{\varepsilon})&-(af)*\rho_{\varepsilon}\Big)(x)\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\partial}{\partial{x^{j}}}\Big(\big(a(x)-a(y)\big)\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\Big)f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=:\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}k_{\varepsilon}(x,y)f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,\end{split}

(22)

so the operator takes the form
K_{\varepsilon}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}k_{\varepsilon}(x,y)f(y)dy
with

k_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=\partial_{x^{j}}\Big(\big(a(x)-a(y)\big)\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\Big).

(23)

Since for fixed
\varepsilon
the kernel
k_{\varepsilon}
is essentially bounded, it gives rise to a bounded operator
K_{\varepsilon}:L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{p}(K)
for all
1\leq p<\infty
.
Moreover, the support of
k_{\varepsilon}
is contained in an
\varepsilon
-neighbourhood of
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}(a)\times\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}(a)
, and
k_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=0
for
|x-y|>\varepsilon
.
Finally, to establish properties of
K_{\varepsilon}
that are uniform in
\varepsilon
we observe that the kernels
k_{\varepsilon}
satisfy:
There is
C>0
such that

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\int_{K}|k_{\varepsilon}(x,y)|\,\mathrm{d}x\leq C\quad\text{for all $y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ and all $\varepsilon>0$,}

(24)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\absolutevalue{k_\varepsilon(x,y)}\,\mathrm{d}y\leq C\quad\text{for all $x\in K$ and all $\varepsilon>0$.}

Indeed writing the kernel as

\displaystyle k_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=\frac{\partial a(x)}{\partial x^{j}}\,\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)+\big(a(x)-a(y)\big)\,\frac{\partial\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)}{\partial x^{j}}

(25)

we obtain the estimate

\displaystyle\int_{K}|k_{\varepsilon}(x,y)|\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\mathrm{Lip}(a,K)+\varepsilon\,\mathrm{Lip}(a,K^{\prime})\,\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int\left|\frac{\partial\rho(z)}{\partial z^{j}}\right|\,\mathrm{d}z,

(26)

were
K^{\prime}
is a compact neighbourhood of
K
. So we obtain (
24
) with
C=(1+\int|\nabla\rho|)\mathrm{Lip}(a,K^{\prime})
and the same reasoning applies to the integral with respect to the
y
-variable.

From
(
24
) we immediately obtain uniform
L^{1}
-boundedness of
K_{\varepsilon}
, and indeed also for
L^{p}
(
1<p<\infty
). In fact, taking advantage of both estimates in (
24
) and Hölder’s inequality we have for
1/p+1/q=1

\displaystyle\|K_{\varepsilon}f\|_{L^{p}(K)}^{p}

\displaystyle=\int_{K}\absolutevalue{\int_{\R^n}k_\varepsilon(x,y)f(y)\,\dint y}^{p}\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{K}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\absolutevalue{k_\varepsilon(x,y)^{\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}}f(y)}\,\mathrm{d}y\bigg)^{p}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{K}\bigg[\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\absolutevalue{k_\varepsilon(x,y)}\absolutevalue{f(y)}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}y\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\absolutevalue{k_\varepsilon(x,y)}\,\mathrm{d}y\bigg)^{\frac{1}{q}}\bigg]^{p}\mathrm{d}x

(27)

\displaystyle\leq C^{\frac{p}{q}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\absolutevalue{f(y)}^{p}\bigg(\int_{K}\absolutevalue{k_\varepsilon(x,y)}\,\mathrm{d}x\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}y\leq C^{p}\ \|f\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Using (
24
) we will now show that
K_{\varepsilon}f\to 0
in
L^{p}(K)
for each
f\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. In fact we would only need to consider
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with compact support. Being a uniformly bounded family of operators it suffices to establish that
\|K_{\varepsilon}f\|_{L^{p}(K)}\to 0
for any test function
f
in the dense subspace

of

, see
Lemma

3.5
, below. While this follows from (
21
) by mostly standard tricks for smoothing by convolution, we include the detailed estimates here for convenience.

So suppose that
f
is a compactly supported smooth function,
then we have

\displaystyle\int_{K}|K_{\varepsilon}f(x)|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{K}\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bigg[\frac{\partial a(x)}{\partial x^{j}}\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)f(y)+\big(a(x)-a(y)\big)\frac{\partial\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)}{\partial x^{j}}f(y)\bigg]\,\mathrm{d}y\bigg|^{p}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{K}\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bigg[\frac{\partial a(x)}{\partial x^{j}}\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(f(y){-f(x)}\big)\,{+}\,\frac{\partial a(x)}{\partial x^{j}}\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y){f(x)}

\displaystyle\hskip 156.49014pt-\big(a(x)-a(y)\big)\frac{\partial\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)}{\partial y^{j}}f(y)\bigg]\,\mathrm{d}y\bigg|^{p}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{K}\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\partial a(x)}{\partial x^{j}}\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(f(y)-f(x)\big)\,\mathrm{d}y

(28)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bigg[\frac{\partial a(x)}{\partial x^{j}}\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)f(x)-\frac{\partial a(y)}{\partial y^{j}}\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)f(y)\bigg]\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big(a(x)-a(y)\big)\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\frac{\partial f(y)}{\partial y^{j}}\,\mathrm{d}y\bigg|^{p}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=:\int_{K}\absolutevalue{(\Box)_1(x)+(\Box)_2(x)+(\Box)_3(x)}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x.

\begin{split}\int_{K}|(\Box)_{1}&(x)|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{K}\absolutevalue{\int_{\R^n}\frac{\partial a(x)}{\partial x^j}\big(f(y)-f(x)\big)\reps(x-y)^{\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}}\,\dint y}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x\\[6.0pt]

(29)

In the same way we estimate the

\displaystyle\int_{K}|(\Box)_{3}(x)|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{K}\absolutevalue{\int_{\R^n}\big(a(x)-a(y)\big)\ \frac{\partial f(y)}{\partial y^j}\ \reps(x-y)\,\dint y}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{K}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\absolutevalue{a(x)-a(y)}^{p}\absolutevalue{\frac{\partial f(y)}{\partial y^j}}^{p}\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{p}\ \mathrm{Lip}(a,K^{\prime})^{p}\ \|\nabla f\|_{L^{\infty}(K^{\prime})}^{p}\ \absolutevalue{K}.

Finally for

\displaystyle\int_{K}|(\Box)_{2}(x)|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{K}\absolutevalue{\int_{\R^n}\bigg[\frac{\partial a(x)}{\partial x^j}f(x)-\frac{\partial a(y)}{\partial y^j}f(y)\bigg]\reps(x-y)\,\dint y}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{K}\absolutevalue{\frac{\partial a(x)}{\partial x^j}f(x)-\int_{\R^n}\frac{\partial a(y)}{\partial y^j}f(y)\reps(x-y)\,\dint y}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\left\|(\partial_{j}a)f-\big((\partial_{j}a)f\big)*\rho_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{p}(K)}^{p},

) and (

for all test functions

Then we have similarly to the above:

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\int_{K}\absolutevalue{h_\varepsilon(x,y)}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq C\quad\text{for all $y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ and all $\varepsilon>0$,}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\absolutevalue{h_\varepsilon(x,y)}\,\mathrm{d}y\leq C\quad\text{for all $x\in K$ and all $\varepsilon>0$.}

Indeed, writing out

explicitly we have

\displaystyle h_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}\big(a_{\varepsilon}(x)-a(x)\big)\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)+\big(a_{\varepsilon}(x)-a(x)\big)\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y),

Now it follows as above that the operators

which goes to zero by Lemma
3.2
(iii). Finally we have

\begin{split}\int_{K}|(\Box)_{5}(x)|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x&\leq\int_{K}\Big|\big(a_{\varepsilon}(x)-a(x)\big)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{j}}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\Big|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x\\[6.0pt]
&\leq\|\nabla f\|_{L^{\infty}(K^{\prime})}^{p}\ \|a_{\varepsilon}-a\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}^{p}\ \absolutevalue{K},\end{split}

(38)

which goes to zero thanks to Lemma
3.2
(iv). Summing up we have
shown that
H_{\varepsilon}f\to 0
in
L^{p}(K)
for all test functions
f
and so we obtain (i).

To prove (ii) we use the decomposition of the kernel
k_{\varepsilon}
given in (

), which for
x\in K
leads to

\displaystyle|K_{\varepsilon}f(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|\frac{\partial a(x)}{\partial x^{j}}\right|\,\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\,|f(y)|\,\mathrm{d}y+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|a(x)-a(y)\right|\,\left|\frac{\partial\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)}{\partial x^{j}}\right|\,|f(y)|\,\mathrm{d}y

(39)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Lip}(a,K)\ \|f\|_{L^{\infty}(K^{\prime})}\ +\ \varepsilon\ \mathrm{Lip}(a,K^{\prime})\ \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ \|\nabla\rho\|_{L^{\infty}(K^{\prime})}\ \|f\|_{L^{\infty}(K^{\prime})}.

(40)

To derive the desired

-bound it only remains to take care of the term
H_{\varepsilon}f
on the r.h.s. of (

). Similarly to the above we have for
x\in K

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{H_\varepsilon f(x)}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\absolutevalue{\frac{\partial}{\partial x^j}\Big(a_\varepsilon(x)-a(x)\Big)}\ \rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\ \absolutevalue{f(y)}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\absolutevalue{a_\varepsilon(x)-a(x)}\absolutevalue{\frac{\partial\reps(x-y)}{\partial x^j}}\absolutevalue{f(y)}\,\mathrm{d}y

(41)

\displaystyle\leq{\tilde{C}\ \|f\|_{L^{\infty}(K^{\prime})}}\ +\ C_{K}\,\varepsilon\ \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ \|\nabla\rho\|_{L^{\infty}(K^{\prime})}\ \|f\|_{L^{\infty}(K^{\prime})},

where we used
Lemma

3.2
(i), (ii) and (iv) in the last inequality.
∎

With an estimate of the form of (

) in mind, we also insert vector fields into the respective Ricci-terms. We now do so in a form directly usable in the course of our main proofs, cf.
Lemma

5.9
below. The proof strategy is the same as in

3.3
.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
X,Y\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
. Then for all
K\Subset M
and any

we have

\displaystyle(\mathrm{Ric}[g]\star_{M}\rho_{\varepsilon})(X,Y)-(\mathrm{Ric}[g](X,Y))\star_{M}\rho_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{in }L^{p}(K),

(42)

as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.

Proof.

As in the discussion following (

), we determine the relevant terms in the Ricci tensor to be locally of the form
\partial(af)
, where
a
is Lipschitz and
f\in L^{\infty}
. This precise structure is not
required for the current proof, so we simply write

in place of

. We use the letter
a
instead to represent terms of the form
X^{i}Y^{j}
by a smooth function in local coordinates. Then proving (

) amounts to showing that

\displaystyle[(\partial_{j}f)*\rho_{\varepsilon}]a-[(\partial_{j}f)a]*\rho_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{in }L^{p}(K).

(43)

Applying

3.3
(i) to the constant net

we obtain that

\displaystyle(\partial_{j}a)(f*\rho_{\varepsilon})+[\partial_{j}(f*\rho_{\varepsilon})]a-[(\partial_{j}a)f]*\rho_{\varepsilon}-[(\partial_{j}f)a]*\rho_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{in }L^{p}(K).

(44)

In this expression,
(\partial_{j}a)(f*\rho_{\varepsilon})-((\partial_{j}a)f)*\rho_{\varepsilon}\to 0
in

, so (

) follows.
∎

Finally, we note the following standard result from functional analysis, which was key in the proof of

.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
K\Subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

. Suppose

in

for each

.

4
Mean curvature for Lipschitz metrics

Regarding the ‘initial condition’ of Hawking’s singularity theorem we need to define what it means to bound the mean curvature of a spacelike hypersurface
\Sigma
. To do so we shall use a ‘thickening’ of the Lebesgue zero set
\Sigma
, to properly extend the classical notion, as follows.

Let
(M,g)
be a spacetime with a Lipschitz metric tensor
g
and let
X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
be smooth and timelike.
Let
\Phi:\mathcal{O}\rightarrow M
be the
flow-out
of
\Sigma
along
X
(cf. e.g.
[
41
, Thm. 9.20]
), where
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\Sigma\times\mathbb{R}
is open.
We say that an open set
\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}\subseteq\mathcal{O}
is
admissible
if
\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}
is an open neighbourhood of
\Sigma\times\{0\}
,
\Phi|_{\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}}
is a diffeomorphism onto its image and the induced metric on each
hypersurface
\Sigma_{t}=\Phi((\Sigma\times\{t\})\cap\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma})
is Riemannian.
We then also call
A_{\Sigma}=\Phi(\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma})
admissible and note that such sets exist by the flow-out theorem
since
\Sigma
is spacelike.

In what follows we
denote by
N
the unique future directed unit vector field which is normal to the leaves
\Sigma_{t}
and note that

N
is locally Lipschitz.
We call a vector field
Y\in\mathfrak{X}(A_{\Sigma})

tangent
to
A_{\Sigma}
and write
Y\in\mathfrak{X}(A_{\Sigma})^{\top}
if
Y(p)\in T_{p}\Sigma_{t}
for any

p\in\Sigma_{t}
for all
t
.

With these conventions, given a smooth timelike vector field
X
we now define the
X
-slab mean curvature

\mathcal{H}^{X}_{\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}}[g]\in L^{\infty}_{\text{loc}}(A_{\Sigma})
of
\Sigma
on an admissible open set
A_{\Sigma}
as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{X}(A_{\Sigma})^{\top}\times\mathfrak{X}(A_{\Sigma})^{\top}\ni(Y,Z)\mapsto-\mathrm{tr}_{g}\left(g(\nabla_{Y}Z,N)\right).

(45)

To be precise, by
\mathrm{tr}_{g}
we mean here the
(n-1)
-dimensional metric trace along the spacelike slices of
A_{\Sigma}
. Thus if
X_{1},\dots,X_{n-1}
form a local frame of tangential vector fields and
G^{ij}
denotes the
inverse of the matrix
(g(X_{i},X_{j}))_{i,j=1}^{n-1}
, then on the common domain of the
X_{i}
’s we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{X}_{\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}}[g]=-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n-1}G^{ij}g(\nabla_{X_{i}}X_{j},N).

(46)

We will then say that the
X
-slab mean curvature of
\Sigma
is bounded above by
b\in\mathbb{R}
if there exists an admissible set
\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}
such that (
46
) is locally essentially bounded, i.e.

\displaystyle\esssup\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}}^{X}[g]<b\qquad\text{ on }A_{\Sigma}.

(47)

In this case, we simply write

\mathcal{H}^{X}[g]<b
.
Note that the property of being admissible is closed under intersections. Also, if there is an
\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}
such that (
47
) holds, then for any other admissible open set
\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}

the bound (
47
) also holds for
\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}\cap\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}
.
We now show that this notion of mean curvature bounds is independent of the choice of
X
.

Lemma 4.1

(Independence of
X
)
.

Let
(M,g)
be a spacetime with a locally Lipschitz metric
g
and let
\Sigma\subseteq M
be a smooth spacelike hypersurface. Assume that
\mathcal{H}^{X}[g]<b
for some timelike
X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
. Then given any other timelike
Y\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
it also holds that
\mathcal{H}^{Y}[g]<b
.

Proof.

We fix a local frame
V_{1},\dots,V_{n-1}\in\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma)
and define
X_{1},\dots,X_{n-1}\in\mathfrak{X}(A_{\Sigma})^{\top}
and
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n-1}\in\mathfrak{X}(B_{\Sigma})^{\top}
by the push-forward of
this frame under the diffeomorphisms
\Phi^{X}
and
\Phi^{Y}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle X_{i}\circ\Phi^{X}(z,t):=T_{(z,t)}\Phi^{X}\left(V_{i}(z),0\right),\qquad Y_{i}\circ\Phi^{Y}(z,t):=T_{(z,t)}\Phi^{Y}\left(V_{i}(z),0\right).

(48)

Then
(X_{i})_{t},(Y_{i})_{t}\rightarrow V_{i}
in
C^{\infty}
as
t\rightarrow 0
.
Denoting by
N^{X}
and
N^{Y}
the corresponding future directed unit normal fields, we note that
(N^{X})_{t}\in\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma^{X}_{t})^{\perp}
and
(N^{Y})_{t}\in\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma^{Y}_{t})^{\perp}
converge locally uniformly to the future-directed unit normal of
\Sigma
as
t\rightarrow 0
.

Let
\Sigma
be compact for the moment. From the assumption
\mathcal{H}^{X}[g]<b
in terms of the essential supremum there exists
\eta>0
such that
\smash{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}}^{X}[g]<b-\eta}
a.e. for some admissible
\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}
.
By the above convergence properties and
the almost everywhere local boundedness of
\Gamma^{i}_{jk}[g]
,
there exists an open neighbourhood
\smash{Z_{\Sigma}\subseteq\Phi^{X}(\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma})\cap\Phi^{Y}(\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma})}
containing
\Sigma
such that

\displaystyle\smash{\left\|\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}}^{X}[g]-\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}}^{Y}[g]\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{\text{loc}}(Z_{\Sigma})}<\frac{\eta}{2}.}

(49)

Consequently,

\displaystyle\smash{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}}^{Y}[g]<b-\frac{\eta}{2}\qquad\mbox{a.e.\ on }Z_{\Sigma}}.

(50)

We construct an admissible open set from
Z_{\Sigma}
as
\mathcal{U}_{\Sigma}:=(\Phi^{Y})^{-1}(Z_{\Sigma})
. Since
\mathcal{H}^{Y}_{\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}}[g]
and
\smash{\mathcal{H}^{Y}_{\mathcal{U}_{\Sigma}}[g]}
coincide on
U_{\Sigma}\subseteq Z_{\Sigma}
, we have that
\smash{\mathcal{H}^{Y}_{\mathcal{U}_{\Sigma}}[g]<b-\frac{\eta}{2}}
a.e. on
U_{\Sigma}
and thereby
\mathcal{H}^{Y}<b
in the sense of (
47
).

For the general case consider an exhaustion of
\Sigma
by compact sets
\{K_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
. Notice that on each
K_{i}
, there exists an
\varepsilon_{i}>0
such that
\Phi^{X}(K_{i}\times(-\varepsilon_{i},\varepsilon_{i}))
and
\Phi^{Y}(K_{i}\times(-\varepsilon_{i},\varepsilon_{i}))
are contained in
\Phi^{X}(\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma})
and
\Phi^{Y}(\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma})
, respectively. By the preceding argument, on each
K_{i}
it holds that
\mathcal{H}^{Y}_{\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}}[g]<b-\frac{\eta}{2}
almost everywhere on an open subset
Z^{i}_{\Sigma}\subseteq M
containing
K_{i}
. Hence we can choose a smooth function
\delta:\Sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that, for each
i
,
\smash{\delta|_{K_{i}}<\varepsilon_{i}}

and
\smash{\Phi^{Y}_{\delta}(K_{i})}
is contained in
Z^{i}_{\Sigma}
, where
\Phi^{Y}_{\delta}:=\Phi^{Y}(\cdot,\delta(\cdot))
. Then
on the admissible open set
\smash{\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}:={(\Phi^{Y})}^{-1}(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}\Phi^{Y}_{\delta}(K_{i}))}
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{Y}_{\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}}[g]<b-\frac{\eta}{2}\qquad\text{a.e.\ on }C_{\Sigma},

(51)

which shows that
\mathcal{H}^{Y}[g]<b
, as claimed.
∎

Thanks to
Lemma

4.1
we can now define
bounds for the slab mean curvature of
\Sigma
independent of the choice of a timelike vector field

X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
.

Definition 4.2

(Mean curvature bounds)
.

Let
(M,g)
be a spacetime with Lipschitz metric
g
. Fix a constant
b\in\mathbb{R}
and let
\Sigma
be a smooth spacelike hypersurface. We say that the slab mean curvature of
\Sigma
is bounded above by
b
and write

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}[g]<b

if there exists a timelike
X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
such that
\mathcal{H}^{X}[g]<b
in the sense of (
47
).

To justify the definition above, we next show that bounds for the slab mean curvature are equivalent to bounds for the usual mean curvature
if
g
is smooth
.

Lemma 4.3

(Equivalence of mean curvature bounds for
g
smooth)
.

Let
(M,g)
be a smooth spacetime. Let
\Sigma
be a smooth spacelike hypersurface with future-directed unit normal vector field
\vec{n}
, and let
H[g]
be the mean curvature of
\Sigma
associated with
\vec{n}
. For
any
b\in\mathbb{R}
, the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

H[g]<b
on
\Sigma
.

(ii)

\mathcal{H}[g]<b
.

Proof.

Note that for
N
as defined above we have
N|_{\Sigma}=\vec{n}
.
The implication (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i) is immediate since
\mathcal{H}^{X}_{\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}}
and
H
agree on
\Sigma
. Conversely, (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) follows by continuity since for any timelike
X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
, the trace
-\smash{\mathrm{tr}_{g}g\left(\nabla_{\,.\,}\,.\,,N^{X}\right)}
is smooth and restricts to
H[g]
on
\Sigma
.
∎

The following final result of this section will allow us to infer mean curvature bounds on regularisations of
g
from those on
g
in the sense of the previous definition.

Lemma 4.4

(Local convergence of the mean curvature)
.

Let
(M,g)
be a spacetime with a locally Lipschitz metric
g
and let
\Sigma
be a smooth spacelike hypersurface.
Let
\tilde{\Sigma}
be open and relatively compact in
\Sigma
and let
\smash{g_{k}:=\check{g}_{\varepsilon_{k}}}
be as in
Lemma

2.4
(iv). Finally, let
X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
be
g
-timelike.
Then there exists some
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and an open neighbourhood
A_{\tilde{\Sigma}}
of
\tilde{\Sigma}
in
M
that is admissible with respect to
X
for
g

and for each
g_{k}
with
k\geq k_{0}
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\smash{\big\|\mathcal{H}^{X}[g_{k}]-\mathcal{H}^{X}[g]\star_{M}\rho_{\varepsilon_{k}}\big\|_{L^{\infty}(A_{\tilde{\Sigma}})}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{ as }k\to\infty.}

(52)

In particular, if
\mathcal{H}[g]<b
for some
b\in\mathbb{R}
, then the
g_{k}
-mean curvature of
\tilde{\Sigma}
satisfies
H_{\tilde{\Sigma}}[g_{k}]<b
for
k
large.

Proof.

Let
A_{\Sigma}
be admissible for
\Sigma
,
X
and
g
and choose an open and relatively compact neighbourhood
A_{\tilde{\Sigma}}\subseteq A_{\Sigma}
of
\tilde{\Sigma}
. Then since
g_{k}\to g
uniformly on
A_{\tilde{\Sigma}}
, there exists
k_{0}
such that the leaves
\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}
are
g_{k}
-spacelike, and thereby
A_{\tilde{\Sigma}}
is
admissible for
\tilde{\Sigma}
,
X
and
g_{k}
, for each
k\geq k_{0}
.

Dropping the subscript indicating the admissible open set for the
X
-slab mean curvature, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{X}[g_{k}]-\mathcal{H}^{X}[g]\star_{M}\rho_{\varepsilon_{k}}=\left(\mathcal{H}^{X}[g_{k}]-\mathcal{H}^{X}[g_{\varepsilon_{k}}]\right)+\left(\mathcal{H}^{X}[g_{\varepsilon_{k}}]-\mathcal{H}^{X}[g]\star_{M}\rho_{\varepsilon_{k}}\right).

(53)

Due to
Equation

46
we can schematically write the local form of the highest order terms of
\mathcal{H}^{X}[g]
in the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{X}[g]\sim g(\partial g)g^{-1}\eta_{i}\xi_{j}\xi_{k}\sim g(\partial g),

(54)

where
\eta_{i}
represents local components of
N^{X}
and
\xi_{j},\xi_{k}
stand for (local derivatives of)
X_{i}
. Here,

and
\eta_{i}
are Lipschitz and the

Combining (
53
) and (
54
) we see that
Lemma

2.4
directly gives convergence (even in

) for the first term on the right-hand side of (

). For the second one, notice that (in a local chart)

\displaystyle g_{\varepsilon}(\partial g_{\varepsilon})-\big(g(\partial g)\big)*\rho_{\varepsilon}=(g*\rho_{\varepsilon})\big((\partial g)*\rho_{\varepsilon}\big)-\big(g(\partial g)\big)*\rho_{\varepsilon}

has the same form as
[

since

is Lipschitz and hence

, so it goes to

locally uniformly, as claimed.

The final claim now follows from Lemmas
4.1
and

.
∎

Remark 4.5

(Comparison with synthetic notions of mean curvature)
.

In the past few
years various synthetic definitions of mean curvature and mean curvature bounds have been proposed,
based on the
needle decomposition
, a tool used to localize (timelike) lower Ricci curvature bounds in the synthetic setting
[
30
,
12
,
7
,
31
,
5
]
.
This notion of localization stems
from convex geometry and serves the purpose of
reducing
a multi-dimensional problem to a one-dimensional one, in order to derive functional and geometric inequalities, in particular
lower Ricci curvature bounds
[
33
,
9
,
11
,
12
]
.

In summary, this construction decomposes the ambient measured Lorentzian pre-length space
(X,\textsf{d},\leq,\ll,\tau,\mathfrak{m})
into maximisers
X_{\alpha}
, where
\alpha
is an index that can be identified with points in the Cauchy hypersurface
\Sigma
, up to a negligible set. Recall that in the Lipschitz case maximisers can be parametrised to be
C^{1,1}
-geodesics in the Filippov sense, see Remark
2.1
(ii).
The so-called
needles

X_{\alpha}
are then viewed as one-dimensional metric measure spaces
(X_{\alpha},\textsf{d}_{\alpha},\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha})
that inherit the curvature properties of the ambient space, cf.
[
12
, Thm. 4.17]
and
[
7
, Thms. 6.37 and A.5]
. As such, these curvature properties yield the absolute continuity of
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}=h_{\alpha}\mathcal{L}^{1}|_{[0,L_{\tau}(X_{\alpha})]}

[
10
, Thm. A.2]
. In the smooth context it follows directly from the definitions that

\displaystyle h_{\alpha}(t)=\psi\cdot\det D\Phi_{(\alpha,t)}|_{T_{\alpha}\Sigma}

(55)

where
\Phi(\alpha,t)=\exp_{\alpha}(-t\nabla\tau_{\Sigma}(\alpha))
is the normal exponential map and
\psi
is a renormalization factor
[
12
, Rem. 5.4]
.

Synthetic mean curvature bounds are then defined via a second-order Taylor expansion of the volume of the region that in the smooth case is spanned by the evolution of
\Sigma
under the map
\Phi
, hence involves
h_{\alpha}
directly (
[
12
, Def. 5.2]
). In particular, just like the notion put forth in
Definition

4.2
, mean curvature bounds in this synthetic sense reproduce the classical bounds in the smooth case (
[
12
, Rem. 5.4]
).
To compare these notions for Lipschitz Lorentzian metrics
it is necessary to study what the right-hand side of (
55
) means in this regularity, where the exponential map is no longer available.
In addition, it requires a study of the needle decomposition in the context of low-regularity metrics,
which is deferred to future work.

5
Hawking’s singularity theorem for Lipschitz metrics

In this section we formulate and prove our main results. As usual we give two versions of the Hawking theorem, the first one providing a global bound on
\tau_{\Sigma}(p)=\sup_{q\in\Sigma}\tau(q,p)
(cf. (
3
)) for a Cauchy surface
\Sigma
, while the second one avoids global hyperbolicity.

5.1
The globally hyperbolic case

In this section we will prove the following general version of Hawking’s singularity theorem:

Theorem 5.1

(
C^{0,1}
-Hawking singularity theorem, I)
.

Let
(M,g)
be a spacetime with a locally Lipschitz metric
g
such that:

(i)

There exists

\rho\in\mathbb{R}

such that
\mathrm{Ric}_{g}(X,X)\geq-(n-1)\rho\,g(X,X)
in the distributional sense for all timelike
X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
.

(ii)

There is a smooth spacelike Cauchy hypersurface
\Sigma
with
\mathcal{H}[g]<\beta<0
.

Then we have

\sup_{M}\tau_{\Sigma}\leq\begin{cases}\frac{n-1}{\absolutevalue{\beta}}&\text{if}\;\rho=0\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\rho|}}\coth^{-1}\Big(\frac{|\beta|}{(n-1)\sqrt{|\rho|}}\Big)&\text{if}\;\rho<0,\text{ provided }|\beta|>(n-1)\sqrt{|\rho|},\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho}}\cot^{-1}\Big(\frac{|\beta|}{(n-1)\sqrt{\rho}}\Big)&\text{if}\;\rho>0.\end{cases}

(56)

Remark 5.2
.

(i)

The case
\rho=0
in (
56
) corresponds to the classical Hawking theorem (cf.
[
49
, Thm. 14.55A]
). Versions of the theorem with
\rho\not=0
have appeared e.g. in
[
4
,
3
]
and in low regularity in
[
19
,
12
]
.

(ii)

Denoting the right hand side of (
56
) by
\alpha(\beta,\rho)
(cf. (
73
) below),
\alpha(\beta,\rho)
is calculated such that
K(\beta,\alpha(\beta,\rho),\rho)=0
, see (
12
). The assumption on
\beta
and
\rho
in
the case
\rho<0
ensures that the argument lies within the domain of
\coth^{-1}
.

(iii)

In the case
\rho>0
the expression on the right hand side of (
56
) is always strictly smaller than
\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{\rho}}
and in fact smaller than
\frac{\sqrt{\rho}}{2\pi}
. This is in accordance with the observations that (in the smooth setting), (a) lower timelike
Ricci curvature bounded below by
(n-1)\rho>0
forces any unit speed timelike geodesic of length
\geq\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{\rho}}
to contain conjugate points (cf.
[
49
, Lem. 10.23]
) and (b) any hypersurface with negative mean curvature (i.e. an initial focussing) develops conjugate points before
\frac{\sqrt{\rho}}{2\pi}
.

Throughout this section, we will generally assume—unless explicitly stated otherwise—that
(M,g)
is globally hyperbolic and that
g
is locally Lipschitz. To prove
Theorem

5.1
some preparations are required.

Lemma 5.3

(Existence of maximisers)
.

Let
\Sigma\subseteq M
be a Cauchy hypersurface of a globally hyperbolic spacetime
(M,g)
with a locally Lipschitz metric
g
. Then

(i)

For any compact set
K\Subset J^{+}(\Sigma)
the set
J^{-}(K)\cap J^{+}(\Sigma)
is compact as well.

(ii)

For any
q\in J^{+}(\Sigma)
there exists a maximising causal curve
\gamma
from
\Sigma
to
q
.

Recall that by Remark
2.1
(ii) the maximiser
\gamma
in (ii) above, when parametrised w.r.t.
g
-arclength, is a
C^{1,1}
-geodesic in the sense of Filippov.

Proof.

(i)
\Sigma
is a closed acausal topological hypersurface. As noted in Remark
2.2
,
C_{p}:=\{v\in T_{p}M\,\backslash\,\{0\}:g(v,v)\leq 0,v\mbox{ future directed}\}

defines a proper cone structure. So, using
J^{+}(\Sigma)=D^{+}(\Sigma)
, we obtain by
[
46
, Thm. 2.44]
that
J^{-}(K)\cap J^{+}(\Sigma)
is compact.

(ii) By Remark
2.3
,
M
is a globally hyperbolic Lorentzian length space and so by
[
36
, Thm. 3.28]
,
\tau
is finite and continuous. Thus
\tau(\cdot,q)
has a maximum on
J^{-}(q)\cap\Sigma
, which is compact by (i).
Hence there is a
p\in J^{-}(q)\cap\Sigma
with
\tau_{\Sigma}(q)=\tau(p,q)
. By the Avez-Seifert theorem (
[
53
, Prop. 6.4]
or
[
36
, Thm. 3.30]
) there exists a maximiser from
p
to
q
, hence from
\Sigma
to
q
.
∎

Lemma 5.4

(Continuity of
\tau_{\Sigma}
)
.

Let
(M,g)
be a globally hyperbolic spacetime with a continuous metric
g
and Cauchy surface
\Sigma
. Then
\tau_{\Sigma}
is continuous.

Proof.

By definition,
\tau_{\Sigma}
is a supremum of continuous functions hence it is lower semicontinuous and we only need to show upper semicontinuity.

Suppose to the contrary that
there is a sequence

q_{n}\to q
and
\delta>0
such that
\tau_{\Sigma}(q_{n})\geq\tau_{\Sigma}(q)+\delta
.
Let
z\in I^{+}(q)
, then w.l.o.g.,
q_{n},q\in J^{-}(z)
. By (the proof of) Lemma
5.3
(ii) for all
n
there is
p_{n}\in\Sigma\cap J^{-}(z)
with
\tau_{\Sigma}(q_{n})=\tau(p_{n},q_{n})
. By compactness, cf. Lemma
5.3
(i), we may assume the
p_{n}
to converge to some
p\in\Sigma\cap J^{-}(z)
and we have

\tau(p_{n},q_{n})=\tau_{\Sigma}(q_{n})\geq\tau_{\Sigma}(q)+\delta\geq\tau(p,q)+\delta

(57)

But by continuity
\tau(p_{n},q_{n})\to\tau(p,q)
, which is a contradiction.
∎

In the following we will make use of the smooth metrics
\check{g}_{\varepsilon}
approximating the rough metric
g
‘from the inside’ as introduced in
Lemma

2.4
. In particular, we use a monotone sequence
\check{g}_{\varepsilon_{k}}
as in item (iv) of that lemma, and for convenience we set

g_{k}:=\check{g}_{\varepsilon_{k}},\quad\tau_{k}:=\tau_{\check{g}_{\varepsilon_{k}}},\quad\text{and}\quad\tau_{\Sigma,k}:=\tau_{\Sigma,\check{g}_{\varepsilon_{k}}}.

(58)

Since the

have narrower light cones
than
g
, each
g_{k}
is globally hyperbolic itself, and any Cauchy surface for
g
is also a Cauchy surface for each

.

Lemma 5.5

(Convergence of time-separations)
.

Let
(M,g)
be a globally hyperbolic spacetime with locally Lipschitz metric
g
and Cauchy surface
\Sigma
. Then we have

(i)

\tau_{k}\rightarrow\tau
locally uniformly, and

(ii)

\tau_{\Sigma,k}\rightarrow\tau_{\Sigma}
locally uniformly.

Remark 5.6
.

In
[
43
, Prop. A.2]
statement (i) is proven
for continuous, causally plain and strongly causal metrics
g
, but for monotonously approximating metrics with
wider
light cones, i.e.,

\tau_{\hat{g}_{\varepsilon_{k}}}\to\tau
locally uniformly. Observe that their proof does not work in our case since it relies on the property
\tau(p,q)\leq\tau_{\hat{g}_{\varepsilon_{k+1}}}(p,q)\leq\tau_{\hat{g}_{\varepsilon_{k}}}(p,q)
, which is reversed for
\tau_{k}:=\tau_{\check{g}_{\varepsilon_{k}}}
, see (
59
) below.

To establish Lemma
5.5
we shall require the following result, which is the analogue of
[
43
, Lem. A.1 (iii) and (iv)]
for metrics
g_{k}=\check{g}_{\varepsilon_{k}}
with narrower light-cones and which can be proven in full analogy.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
(M,g)
be a continuous spacetime and let
g_{k}
be as in (
58
). Then
the sequence
\varepsilon_{k}\searrow 0
can be chosen in such a way that,
for all
g_{k}
-causal
X\in TM
:

(i)

-g_{k}(X,X)<-g(X,X)
, and

(ii)

-g_{k}(X,X)\leq-g_{k+1}(X,X)
.

Proof of
Lemma

5.5
.

(i)
Let
p,q
in some
K\Subset M
and observe first that

\displaystyle\tau_{k}(p,q)\leq\tau_{k+1}(p,q)\leq\tau(p,q).

(59)

Indeed any
g_{k}
-causal
\gamma
from
p
to
q
is also
g_{k+1}
- and
g
-causal. Then from
Lemma

5.7
we have

L_{g_{k}}(\gamma)\leq L_{g_{k+1}}(\gamma)\leq L_{g}(\gamma)
and so

\displaystyle\tau_{k}(p,q)=\sup_{\gamma\,g_{k}\text{-causal}}L_{g_{k}}(\gamma)\leq\sup_{\gamma\,g_{k+1}\text{-causal}}L_{g_{k+1}}(\gamma)\leq\sup_{\gamma\,g\text{-causal}}L_{g}(\gamma)=\tau(p,q).

(60)

We next prove pointwise convergence of
\tau_{k}
to
\tau
, which together with monotonicity (
59
) gives the claim by Dini’s theorem (e.g.
[
2
, Thm. 2.66]
).
In case
\tau(p,q)=0
, again
by (
59
) all
\tau_{k}(p,q)
vanish, and so we only need to consider
\tau(p,q)>0
.
By the Avez-Seifert theorem there is a
g
-maximiser
\gamma
from
p
to
q
, which when parametrised to
g
-unit speed is a

-curve (cf. Remark
2.1
(ii)), implying that
\gamma
is
g_{k}
-timelike for

and we have

. Hence for

and

\displaystyle\tau_{k}(p,q)\geq L_{g_{k}}(\gamma)\geq L_{g}(\gamma)-\delta=\tau(p,q)-\delta,

(61)

and convergence follows by combining this estimate with (

).

(ii) As before, since

-causal curves are
g_{k+1}
- and
g
-causal we have monotonicity, i.e.,
\tau_{\Sigma,k}\leq\tau_{\Sigma,k+1}\leq\tau_{\Sigma}
and again it suffices to show
\tau_{\Sigma,k}(q)\to\tau_{\Sigma}(q)
for all

. Given such

(ii) there is

with

and since by (i) the latter expression equals

we have for any

and all

∎

We next employ the regularisation result

Proposition 5.8

(Lower uniform bound on
{\mathrm{Ric}[g_{k}]}
)
.

Let
(M,g)
be a spacetime with a locally Lipschitz metric
g
. Suppose that there is some
C\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\mathrm{Ric}[g](X,X)\geq C\cdot g(X,X)\quad\text{distributionally for all $g$-timelike $X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)$.}

(63)

Then for any compact
K\Subset M
there is
\tilde{C}\in\mathbb{R}
such that for all
\eta<0

there exists some
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for all
k\geq k_{0}
and all
X\in TM|_{K}
with

\|X\|_{h}\leq D
and
g(X,X)\leq\eta
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ric}[g_{k}](X,X)\geq\tilde{C}\cdot g_{k}(X,X).

(64)

Proof.

We first note that it suffices to show the claim for
g_{\varepsilon_{k}}
instead of
g_{k}=(\check{g}_{\varepsilon_{k}})
. Indeed, suppose that we already know (
64
) for
g_{\varepsilon_{k}}
instead of
g_{k}
. Then due to
Lemma

2.4
, for any
\delta>0
and
k
sufficiently large we
have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ric}[g_{k}](X,X)-\tilde{C}\cdot g_{k}(X,X)

\displaystyle=(\mathrm{Ric}[g_{k}]-\mathrm{Ric}[g_{\varepsilon_{k}}])(X,X)

\displaystyle+(\mathrm{Ric}[g_{\varepsilon_{k}}](X,X)-\tilde{C}g_{\varepsilon_{k}}(X,X))+\tilde{C}(g_{\varepsilon_{k}}(X,X)-g_{k}(X,X))

\displaystyle\geq-\delta\geq\frac{-\delta}{\eta}g(X,X)\geq\frac{-2\delta}{\eta}g_{k}(X,X),

which gives (
64
) with
\tilde{C}-\frac{2\delta}{\eta}
instead of
\tilde{C}
. For the remainder of this proof we therefore may assume that
g_{k}\equiv g_{\varepsilon_{k}}
.

Next, since the claim is local, we may suppose that
M=\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
h
is the standard Euclidean metric, and we can replace
\star_{M}
by the standard convolution
\ast
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(cf. the notational conventions in Section
2.2
).
Since (
63
) is supposed to hold for
g
-timelike vector fields, we may without loss of generality assume that
C>0
.
We will follow the basic layout of the proof of
[
38
, Lem. 3.2]
.

To begin with, by uniform continuity of
g
on
K
there exists some
r>0
such that, for all
p,x\in K
with
\|p-x\|<r
and
any
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\|X\|\leq D
, we have
\|g_{p}(X,X)-g_{x}(X,X)\|<-\eta
. Fixing
p\in K
, it follows that on the open ball
B_{r}(p)
the constant vector field
\tilde{X}:=x\mapsto X
is
g
-timelike. Choose a cut-off function
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\chi\equiv 1
in a neighbourhood of
\overline{B_{r}(p)}
and set, for
1\leq i,j\leq n
,
\tilde{R}_{ij}:=\chi\cdot\mathrm{Ric}[g]_{ij}\in{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Due to (
63
), for any
x\in B_{r-1/k}(p)
we have

\displaystyle(\tilde{R}_{ij}\tilde{X}^{i}\tilde{X}^{j})*\rho_{k}(x)\geq(Cg_{ij}\tilde{X}^{i}\tilde{X}^{j})*\rho_{k}(x)=C(g_{k})_{ij}(x)X^{i}X^{j}.

(65)

Now note that for
1/k<r
we have
(\mathrm{Ric}[g]_{ij}*\rho_{k})(p)=(\tilde{R}_{ij}*\rho_{k})(p)
. Thus for
k>1/r
we obtain,
using the constancy of
\tilde{X}
,

\begin{split}|\mathrm{Ric}[g_{k}]_{ij}(p)X^{i}X^{k}-((\tilde{R}_{ij}\tilde{X}^{i}\tilde{X}^{j})*\rho_{k})(p)|&=|(\mathrm{Ric}[g_{k}]_{ij}(p)-\mathrm{Ric}[g]_{ij}*\rho_{k}(p))X^{i}X^{j}|\\
&\leq D^{2}\;\max_{i,j}\,\sup_{x\in K}|\mathrm{Ric}[g_{k}]_{ij}(x)-\mathrm{Ric}[g]_{ij}*\rho_{k}(x)|.\end{split}

(66)

Using (
65
) we arrive at

\begin{split}\mathrm{Ric}[g_{k}]_{ij}(p)X^{i}X^{j}&=(\tilde{R}_{ij}X^{i}X^{j})*\rho_{k}(p)+(\mathrm{Ric}[g_{k}]_{ij}(p)X^{i}X^{j}-(\tilde{R}_{ij}X^{i}X^{j})*\rho_{k}(p))\\
&\geq C(g_{k})_{ij}(p)X^{i}X^{j}-|\mathrm{Ric}[g_{k}]_{ij}(p)X^{i}X^{j}-((\tilde{R}_{ij}\tilde{X}^{i}\tilde{X}^{j})*\rho_{k})(p)|\\
&=:\Box_{1}+\Box_{2}.\end{split}

(67)

Employing (
66
), together with Proposition
3.1
(ii), we see that

\Box_{2}\geq-C_{K}D^{2}\geq-\frac{C_{K}D^{2}}{\eta}g_{ij}(p)X^{i}X^{j}\geq-\frac{2C_{K}D^{2}}{\eta}(g_{k})_{ij}(p)X^{i}X^{j}

for
k
large since
g_{k}(X,X)\to g(X,X)
uniformly on
K\times\overline{B_{D}(0)}
. Combining this
with (
67
) gives the claim with
\tilde{C}:=C-\frac{2C_{K}D^{2}}{\eta}
.
∎

.

Then by
[
22
, Lem. 3.2 and Rem. 3.3]
and noting that the proofs of these results rely exclusively on the bounds (
80
), (
81
),
we obtain

\begin{split}\mathrm{vol}_{k}({}^{k}\Omega^{+}_{\alpha+2\delta}(B_{k})\cap L)&=\int_{B_{k}}\int_{0}^{\min(\alpha+2\delta,{}^{k}c^{+}_{\Sigma}(x),{}^{k}f_{L}(x))}\mathcal{A}_{k}(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{k}\\
&\leq C\int_{0}^{\alpha+2\delta}\int_{B_{k}}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{k}\,\mathrm{d}t=C\cdot(\alpha+2\delta)\cdot\mathrm{vol}_{\Sigma,k}(B_{k}),\end{split}

(82)

with
C=C(n,\kappa,\beta,\alpha+2\delta)
.
We then conclude (
78
) by combining (
79
) and (
82
).

At this point we want to apply Theorem
2.5
to
(M,g_{k})
for
B_{k}
and the set
{{}^{k}}\Omega^{+}_{\alpha+\delta/2}(B_{k})
for large
k
.
To this end, we first note that, again, inspection of the proof of that result in
[
22
]
shows that the Ricci- resp. mean curvature bounds assumed globally in its formulation can in fact be replaced by (
80
), (
81
) in our setting (as long as
\smash{{{}^{k}}\Omega^{+}_{T+\eta}(B_{k})\subseteq L}
in the notation of Theorem
2.5
).

Next, note that
{{}^{k}}\Omega^{+}_{\alpha+\delta}(B_{k})\subseteq L
, where (
80
) and (
81
) hold with constants
\kappa
and
\beta
satisfying
\smash{\beta\geq-(n-1)\sqrt{|\kappa|}}
. Next we choose
\eta:=\delta/2
, and
T:=\alpha+\eta
.
Since
K(\beta,T,\rho)
from Theorem
2.5
is strictly increasing in
T
we have that
K:=K(\beta,T,\rho)>K(\beta,\alpha(\beta,\rho),\rho)=0
. In case
\rho>0
we may assume
\delta

\sqrt{\rho}T<\frac{\pi}{2}
.

Therefore (i), together with

5.9
implies that the
L^{1}
-norm of the negative part of

{}^{k}U

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathrm{vol}_{\Sigma,k}(B_{k})}\int_{{{}^{k}}\Omega^{+}_{\alpha+\eta}(B_{k})}\Big(\mathrm{Ric}[g_{k}](^{k}U_{p},\,^{k}U_{p})-(n-1)\rho\Big)_{-}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{k}(p)<C^{A-}(n,\kappa,\eta,T)\,K.

(83)

But now Theorem
2.5
gives
B_{k}\not\subseteq{{}^{k}}\mathrm{Reg}^{+}_{\delta/2}(\alpha+\delta/2)={{}^{k}}\mathrm{Reg}^{+}_{\delta}(\alpha)
, which contradicts (
77
).
∎

Remark 5.10

(Comparison with synthetic versions of the Hawking singularity theorem)
.

In
[
12
, Thm. 5.6]
, a synthetic Hawking singularity theorem was proved for globally hyperbolic timelike non-branching Lorentzian geodesic spaces satisfying the strong energy condition in the sense of the timelike measure contraction property
\textsf{TMCP}^{e}(K,N)
. To compare this result, first, to the
C^{1}
-version of
[
20
, Thm. 4.13]
, we note that although
Lemma

4.3
remains true also for
C^{1}
-metrics, the question about what the right-hand side of (
55
) represents still remains. In addition, we need to compare the ways in which the strong energy condition is modelled in each case. In this regularity, it was shown in
[
6
]
that distributional Ricci bounds for
C^{1}
-metrics indeed imply
the bounds used in
[
12
, Thm. 5.6]
.

In the Lipschitz case treated in the current work, apart from what was said on mean curvature bounds in
Remark

4.5
, even the compatibility of the notions of timelike Ricci curvature bounds is still open.
The situation is better in the Riemannian context, where the problem was first studied for the
C^{1}
-case in
[
34
]
, and more recently compatibility was shown in
[
48
]
even down to metrics of regularity
C^{0}\cap W^{1,2}_{\mathrm{loc}}
.

Another very recent synthetic Hawking singularity theorem was shown in
[
7
, Theorem A.7]
. Here, the authors use the same version of mean curvature bounds as in
[
12
]
, and also implement the strong energy condition via the timelike measure contraction property
\textsf{TMCP}^{e}(K,N)
. Thus the same compatibility statements as
detailed above for
[
12
]
apply here as well.
The Hawking theorem itself is then based on integral curvature bounds. Even in the weighted smooth case, this is a new result.

Note that, in any case, since both
[
12
, Thm. 5.6]
and
[
7
, Thm. A.7]
assume an (essential) non-branching condition, even if the curvature bounds turn out to be equivalent in all cases, the synthetic versions of the theorem will not imply our results since the latter do not rely on any non-branching condition.

5.2
The non-globally hyperbolic case

In this final section we are going to state and prove the extension of
[
49
, Thm. 14.55B]
to Lipschitz spacetimes.
Beforehand we establish that maximisers emanating from a spacelike hypersurface
\Sigma
start orthogonal to it, cf.
[
21
, Rem. 6.6(ii)]
for the null case and
g\in C^{1,1}
.

Lemma 5.11

(Maximisers start orthogonally from
\Sigma
)
.

Let
(M,g)
be a spacetime with locally Lipschitz metric tensor
g
and let
\Sigma
be a smooth spacelike hypersurface. Then any maximiser emanating from
\Sigma
starts orthogonally to
\Sigma
.

Proof.

By Remark
2.1
(ii), any maximiser has a parametrization as a
C^{1,1}
-curve.
Let
\gamma_{v}:[0,1]\rightarrow M
be such a curve with
\gamma_{v}(0)=p\in\Sigma
and
\dot{\gamma}_{v}(0)=v
. We suppose by contradiction that
v\not\in T_{p}\Sigma^{\perp}
and construct a variation of
\gamma_{v}
which gives a longer curve from
\Sigma
to
q=\gamma_{v}(1)
.

Since
\gamma_{v}\not\perp\Sigma

there is
y\in T_{p}\Sigma
with
g(y,v)>0
. Let
\alpha:[0,b]\rightarrow\Sigma
be a
C^{2}
-curve with
\alpha(0)=p
and
\dot{\alpha}(0)=y
. Since this is a local issue we may assume
M=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\alpha(0)=p=\gamma_{v}(0)=0
.
Now we define a variation of
\gamma_{v}
by
\sigma:[0,t_{0}]\times[0,s_{0}]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\displaystyle\sigma(t,s)=\gamma_{v}(t)+\left(1-\frac{t}{t_{0}}\right)\alpha(s),

(84)

where we have chosen
t_{0}
and
s_{0}
so small that

\displaystyle\smash{\left\langle y,\dot{\gamma}_{v}(t)\right\rangle_{\sigma(s,t)}>c>0}\qquad\forall(t,s)\in[0,t_{0}]\times[0,s_{0}],

(85)

To show that
\sigma(\cdot,s):[0,t_{0}]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a longer curve from
\alpha(s)\in\Sigma
to
\sigma(t_{0},s)=\gamma_{v}(t_{0})
for
s,t_{0}
small enough, we first Taylor expand
\alpha
to obtain
\alpha(s)=sy+O(s^{2})
. Then we compute

\displaystyle|g(\sigma(t,s))-\smash{g(\underbrace{\gamma_{v}(t)}_{\sigma(t,0)})}|

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Lip}(g)\ |\sigma(t,s)-\sigma(t,0)|

(86)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Lip}(g)\left(1-\frac{t}{t_{0}}\right)\,|\alpha(s)|\leq C\cdot s\left(1-\frac{t}{t_{0}}\right),

for
s
small enough. Moreover, we have
\smash{\partial_{t}\sigma(t,s)=\dot{\gamma}_{v}(t)-\tfrac{1}{t_{0}}\alpha(s)=\dot{\gamma}_{v}(t)-\tfrac{s}{t_{0}}y+O(s^{2})}
and so

\displaystyle\left\langle\partial_{t}\sigma(t,s),\partial_{t}\sigma(t,s)\right\rangle_{\sigma(t,s)}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\dot{\gamma}_{v}(t),\dot{\gamma}_{v}(t)\right\rangle_{\sigma(t,s)}-2\ \frac{s}{t_{0}}\left\langle\dot{\gamma}_{v}(t),y\right\rangle_{\sigma(t,s)}+O(s^{2})

(87)

\displaystyle\leq\left\langle\dot{\gamma}_{v}(t),\dot{\gamma}_{v}(t)\right\rangle_{\gamma_{v}(t)}\ +\ s\left(C\Big(1-\frac{t}{t_{0}}\Big)-2\ \frac{c}{t_{0}}\right)+O(s^{2}).

Now for
s,t_{0}
small enough the sum of the trailing terms on the right hand side becomes negative and so

L(\sigma(\cdot,s))>L(\gamma_{v}|_{[0,t_{0}]})
.
∎

Our second main result can now be stated and proven:

Theorem 5.12

(
C^{0,1}
-Hawking singularity theorem, II)
.

Let
(M,g)
be a spacetime with locally Lipschitz metric tensor
g
such that:

(i)

There exists

\rho\in\mathbb{R}

such that
\mathrm{Ric}_{g}(X,X)\geq-(n-1)\rho\,g(X,X)
in the distributional sense for all timelike
X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
.

(ii)

There is a smooth compact spacelike hypersurface
\Sigma
with
\mathcal{H}[g]<\beta<0
, where in case
\rho<0
we assume
|\beta|>(n-1)\sqrt{|\rho|}
.

Then there exists a timelike future directed geodesic
\gamma
in the sense of Filippov emanating orthogonally from
\Sigma
which is incomplete. More precisely, the length of this
\gamma
is bounded above by the right-hand side of (
56
).

Proof.

Similar to the classical proof (cf.
[
49
, Thm. 14.55B]
) we employ Theorem
5.1
to establish the result. More precisely, our arguments rely on Theorem
5.1
only via the bound on
\tau_{\Sigma}
on the interior of
D_{g}(\Sigma)
. It will therefore suffice to treat the case
\rho=0
, with the understanding that the other cases
follow entirely analogously, simply replacing
\smash{\tfrac{n-1}{\absolutevalue{\beta}}}
by the appropriate bound on the right hand side of (
56
).

Thus, supposing that
\rho=0
, we are going to show that there exists an inextendible future directed timelike Filippov geodesic
\gamma
starting orthogonally from
\Sigma
with length bounded above by
\smash{\tfrac{n-1}{\absolutevalue{\beta}}}
.

To begin with, the covering argument given in
[
49
, Prop. 14.48]
allows us also in the present case to assume, without loss of generality, that
\Sigma
is acausal and connected. By
[
53
, Thm. 5.7]
the interior of the Cauchy development
\mathrm{int}(D_{g}(\Sigma))
is globally hyperbolic and we may apply
Theorem

5.1
to see that
\smash{\tau_{\Sigma}\leq\tfrac{n-1}{\absolutevalue{\beta}}}
on this set.

If the future Cauchy horizon
H^{+}_{g}(\Sigma)=\emptyset
then by
[
46
, Thm. 2.35]
we obtain that
I^{+}(\Sigma)\subseteq D^{+}(\Sigma)
, so the
conclusion follows from
Theorem

5.1
. Thus from now on we assume that

\displaystyle H^{+}_{g}(\Sigma)\neq\emptyset.

(88)

We make the
indirect assumption
that
every inextendible timelike future directed Filippov geodesic starting perpendicularly from
\Sigma

has length strictly greater than
\smash{\tfrac{n-1}{\absolutevalue{\beta}}}
.

Step 1.

Compactness of the horizon.
The set
B
of initial conditions for these geodesics is given by
\{\vec{n}_{p}\mid p\in\Sigma\}
with
\vec{n}
the future directed unit normal to
\Sigma
, hence is compact. Since by assumption all such
\gamma
exist at least on
\smash{[0,\tfrac{n-1}{\absolutevalue{\beta}}]}
, by
[
17
, Thm. 3, p. 79]
there exists
L\Subset M
compact containing all their images.
Thanks to the Avez-Seifert theorem
[
53
, Prop. 6.4]
any point
q\in\mathrm{int}(D^{+}_{g}(\Sigma))
is reached by a maximiser
\gamma
, which by Remark
2.1
(ii) and Lemma
5.11
is a Filippov geodesic emanating orthogonally from
\Sigma
. Moreover, by Theorem
5.1
its length is bounded by
\smash{\tau_{\Sigma}\leq\tfrac{n-1}{\absolutevalue{\beta}}}
,
so we conclude that int
(D^{+}_{g}(\Sigma))\subseteq L
.

Due to the fact that
D^{+}_{g}(\Sigma)\setminus\Sigma
is open by
[
46
, Thm. 2.34]
,
we get
D^{+}_{g}(\Sigma)\setminus\Sigma\subseteq\mathrm{int}(D^{+}_{g}(\Sigma))
, and consequently
\smash{\overline{D^{+}_{g}(\Sigma)}\subseteq L}
.
Hence (again using
[
46
, Thm. 2.35]
)
H^{+}_{g}(\Sigma)\subseteq L
and so it is compact as well.

Step 2.

Any point in
H^{+}_{g}(\Sigma)
can be reached by a maximiser.

Retaining the notation from (
58
) consider an approximating sequence of smooth metrics
g_{k}
with
g_{k}\prec g
. Each
g_{k}
is globally hyperbolic on
\mathrm{int}(D_{g_{k}}(\Sigma))=D_{g_{k}}(\Sigma)
(cf. (the proof of)
[
49
, Lem. 14.43]
).

(1) We claim that for all
k

H_{g}^{+}(\Sigma)\subseteq\overline{D^{+}_{g}(\Sigma)}\subseteq D_{g_{k+1}}^{+}(\Sigma)\subseteq D_{g_{k}}^{+}(\Sigma).

(89)

Indeed, by
[
46
, Thm. 2.36]
we have

\displaystyle\overline{D^{+}_{g}(\Sigma)}=\tilde{D}^{+}_{g}(\Sigma):=\{q\mid\mbox{ every past inextendible timelike curve from }q\mbox{ intersects }\Sigma\}.

(90)

Let
q\in\tilde{D}^{+}_{g}(\Sigma)
and let
\gamma
be
g_{k}
-past directed, past inextendible and causal. Then
\gamma
is also
g
-past directed timelike and hence must intersect
\Sigma
, so
\smash{\overline{D^{+}_{g}(\Sigma)}\subseteq D^{+}_{g_{k}}(\Sigma)}
and (
89
) follows.

(2) Due to the classical Avez-Seifert theorem, given
q\in H^{+}_{g}(\Sigma)
, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a
g_{k}
-maximiser
\gamma_{k}

from
\Sigma
to
q
with

\displaystyle L_{g_{k}}(\gamma_{k})=\tau_{\Sigma,k}(q).

(91)

We assume
\gamma_{k}
to be parametrised with respect to
h
-arclength.
Because
\gamma_{k}
is
g_{k}
-causal it is
g
-timelike and so must be contained in
\smash{\overline{D_{g}^{+}(\Sigma)}}
since it terminates in
H_{g}^{+}(\Sigma)
.
Denoting by
p_{k}
the initial point of
\gamma_{k}
, we may without loss of generality suppose that
p_{k}\to p\in\Sigma
.
Let
\hat{g}_{k}:=\hat{g}_{\varepsilon_{k}}
be a sequence of smooth Lorentzian metrics as in
Lemma

2.4
approximating
g
from the outside.

(3) Fixing
m\in\mathbb{N}
we now want to apply the limit curve theorem
[
44
, Thm. 3.1(2)]
to the sequence
\{\gamma_{k}\}_{k}
of
\hat{g}_{m}
-timelike curves in
(M,\hat{g}_{m})
to obtain a limit curve that connects
p
and
q
. To do so
we have to exclude case (2)(ii) in that theorem. Note that we cannot immediately conclude this from the curves being contained in a compact set since
(M,\hat{g}_{m})
need not be non-totally imprisoning.

Assume, to the contrary, that the
h
-arclengths of the curves
\gamma_{k}
are unbounded, i.e.,
\gamma_{k}:[0,b_{k}]\to M
with
\gamma_{k}(b_{k})=q
and
b_{k}\to\infty
. Then we obtain a future-inextendible
\hat{g}_{m}
-causal limit curve
\gamma:[0,\infty)\to M
emanating from
p
. Note that this limit curve is in fact independent of
m
and is
\hat{g}_{m}
-causal for all
m
, hence
g
-causal.

(4) We claim that for any
t
with
\gamma(t)\in D_{g}^{+}(\Sigma)
,
\gamma
is
g
-maximising from
\Sigma
to
\gamma(t)
. By
Lemma

5.5
(which applies since we are in a globally hyperbolic region) and
Lemma

5.7
(i) we have

\displaystyle\tau_{\Sigma}(\gamma(t))=\lim_{k\to\infty}\tau_{\Sigma,k}(\gamma_{k}(t))=\lim_{k\to\infty}L_{g_{k}}(\gamma_{k}|_{[0,t]})\leq\limsup_{k\to\infty}L_{g}(\gamma_{k}|_{[0,t]}).

Since the
\hat{g}_{m}
approximate
g
from the outside, for each
k
and
m
we
have (using
[
43
, Lem. A.1]
)
L_{g}(\gamma_{k}|_{[0,t]})\leq L_{\hat{g}_{m}}(\gamma_{k}|_{[0,t]})
. Together with
[
44
, Thm. 2.4(b)]
this implies

\limsup_{k}L_{g}(\gamma_{k}|_{[0,t]})\leq\limsup_{k}L_{\hat{g}_{m}}(\gamma_{k}|_{[0,t]})\leq L_{\hat{g}_{m}}(\gamma|_{[0,t]}).

This holds for each
m
, and since
L_{\hat{g}_{m}}(\gamma|_{[0,t]})\to L_{g}(\gamma|_{[0,t]})
as
m\to\infty
,
altogether we arrive at

\tau_{\Sigma}(\gamma(t))\leq L_{g}(\gamma|_{[0,t]}),

proving the claim.

(5) Next we show that
T:=\sup\{t\in[0,\infty)\colon\gamma(t)\in D^{+}_{g}(\Sigma)\}<\infty
. Indeed, if this were not the case then
\gamma\subseteq D^{+}_{g}(\Sigma)
and by the above
\gamma
is always maximising the Lorentzian distance to
\Sigma
. Hence by
Remark

2.1
(ii) and
Lemma

5.11
its re-parametrisation to
g
-unit speed is a Filippov-geodesic starting orthogonally to
\Sigma
. Since
\gamma
is future inextendible and we assume Filippov-geodesic completeness, also its
g
-unit speed parametrisation must be defined on
[0,\infty)
. But then for any
\smash{t>\frac{n-1}{|\beta|}}
we have that
\smash{\tau_{\Sigma}(\gamma(t))=t>\frac{n-1}{|\beta|}}
(where
\gamma
is being parametrized by
g
-unit speed), contradicting
\smash{\tau_{\Sigma}\leq\frac{n-1}{|\beta|}}
on
D^{+}_{g}(\Sigma)
.

(6) Since
D^{+}_{g}(\Sigma)\setminus\Sigma
is open, we have

\smash{\gamma(T)\in\overline{D^{+}_{g}(\Sigma)}\setminus D^{+}_{g}(\Sigma)=H^{+}_{g}(\Sigma)}
(using
[
46
, Thm. 2.35]
).
We now claim that
\gamma(t)\in I_{g}^{+}(H_{g}^{+}(\Sigma))
for all
t>T
.

Fix a globally hyperbolic neighbourhood
U
of
\gamma(T)
and fix a
\delta>0
such that
\gamma([T-\delta,T+\delta])\subseteq U
. By
h
-uniform convergence also
\gamma_{k}([T-\delta,T+\delta])\subseteq U
for large enough
k
. Thus by the same reasoning as in (4)
we conclude that
\gamma|_{[T-\delta,T+\delta]}
is maximising, hence it has a causal character, namely timelike because it is timelike initially.

Since
I^{+}_{g}(H_{g}^{+}(\Sigma))
is open, we also have that
\gamma_{k}(T+1)\in I^{+}_{g}(H_{g}^{+}(\Sigma))
for
k
large, contradicting the fact that
\smash{\gamma_{k}\subseteq\overline{D^{+}_{g}(\Sigma)}}
(cf.
[
46
, Thm. 2.32]
).

Altogether, we have established that case (ii) of
[
44
, Thm. 3.1 (2)]
cannot occur, hence
\gamma:[0,b]\to M
indeed reaches
q=\gamma(b)
. By the same arguments as above,
\gamma|_{[0,b)}\subseteq D_{g}^{+}(\Sigma)
and
\tau_{\Sigma}(\gamma(t))=L_{g}(\gamma|_{[0,t]})
for all
t<b
. Letting
t\nearrow b
and using lower semicontinuity of
\tau_{\Sigma}
we conclude that

\gamma
is maximising from
\Sigma
to
q
, thereby concluding Step 2.

Step 3.

The function
p\mapsto\tau_{\Sigma}(p)
is strictly decreasing on past-pointing generators of
H^{+}_{g}(\Sigma)
.

We
first note that by
[
46
, Thm. 2.32]
every
q\in H^{+}_{g}(\Sigma)
is the future endpoint of a null maximiser
\alpha
, so we fix such a curve and take
s<t
in its domain of definition. By the above there is a past-pointing timelike geodesic
\sigma
from
\alpha(t)
to
\Sigma
such that
L_{g}(\sigma)=\tau_{\Sigma}(\alpha(t))
. But
\alpha
is null and so the curve
\beta
obtained by the concatenation of
\smash{\alpha|_{[s,t]}}
and
\sigma
is not maximising by
[
23
, Thm. 1.1]
. Hence

\displaystyle\tau_{\Sigma}(\alpha(s))>L_{g}(\beta)=L_{g}(\sigma)=\tau_{\Sigma}(\alpha(t)).

(92)

Step 4.

Conclusion.
Since
H^{+}(\Sigma)
is compact the function
p\mapsto\tau_{\Sigma}(p)
attains a minimum at some
q\in H^{+}(\Sigma)
. But this contradicts strict monotonicity of
\tau_{\Sigma}
along a generator starting in
p
, thereby concluding the proof.
∎
