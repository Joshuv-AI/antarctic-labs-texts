Engineered neuronal exosomes mediate α-synuclein clearance to ameliorate Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Lufei Chen, Xiaoling Lin, Mingzhi Fu, Shenglang Chen, Zhexiao Yan, Yuelin Diao, Genyu Chen, Zichen Huang, Yan Sun, Yaxin Lin, Zucheng Ye, Ying Zhou, Qinyong Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractParkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder after Alzheimer’s disease. A hallmark pathological feature of PD is the abnormal aggregation of α-synuclein (αSyn) into insoluble Lewy bodies. Consequently, developing strategies to inhibit αSyn aggregation in the brain has been a major research focus for PD treatment. This study developed a therapeutic approach using engineered neuronal exosomes. These exosomes were modified to extend their blood circulation half-life to 3.8 h and enhance targeting, with a 2.15 ± 0.09% brain signal proportion (vs. 0.78 ± 0.07% for free dye). They were then loaded with a self-developed αSyn aggregation-blocking peptide (sPep) as well as the antioxidant pyrroloquinoline quinone (PQQ). We investigated the therapeutic efficacy of this system in both in vitro and in vivo models of PD. Our experiments confirmed that the screened sPep effectively targeted and blocked αSyn aggregation both in vitro and in vivo. Neuronal exosomes, isolated by ultracentrifugation and hybridization, demonstrated strong abilities to cross the blood-brain barrier. In vivo studies revealed that the treatment significantly improved motor and cognitive functions in PD model mice. The underlying neuroprotective mechanisms included reducing αSyn aggregation, enhancing antioxidant capacity, ameliorating mitochondrial dysfunction, and suppressing cell apoptosis, collectively promoting the survival of dopaminergic neurons. Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD), a major neurodegenerative disorder, is pathologically defined by the progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNpc) and the formation of insoluble Lewy bodies from aggregated α-synuclein (αSyn). This escalating burden is underscored by projections estimating that the global prevalence of PD will exceed 25 million by 2050, with China accounting for half of all cases [1]. Consequently, developing effective interventions that can slow or halt disease progression is an urgent and critical imperative.

αSyn adopts various conformational states. Under physiological conditions, it exists primarily as a monomer and is implicated in certain functions, such as neurotransmission and mitochondrial fission [2]. However, in pathological states, αSyn monomers misfold from their native unfolded state into oligomeric intermediates and amyloid fibrils, ultimately depositing as Lewy bodies [3]. These oligomeric species disrupt crucial neuronal functions and propagate trans-synaptically, disseminating toxic oligomers that cause direct neuronal damage [4]. Furthermore, αSyn accumulation is closely linked to mitochondrial dysfunction and lysosomal alterations [5, 6]. Intracellular αSyn aggregates exacerbate mitochondrial oxidative stress, impair mitochondrial function, and disrupt lysosomal autophagy by reducing lysosomal abundance [7]. Concurrently, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) further propagate mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to the death of dopaminergic neurons, which is a key molecular event in PD pathogenesis [8–10]. Therefore, clearing αSyn aggregates and mitigating excess ROS represent promising potential therapeutic strategies for PD.

In recent years, therapeutic strategies targeting αSyn aggregates have garnered significant attention from researchers. Notable examples of such approaches include monoclonal antibodies such as ABBV-0805, PRX002, and Cinpanemab, which are currently in preclinical studies [11, 12]. However, most antibody-based therapeutics face challenges in achieving desired efficacy endpoints, largely due to their limited blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration efficiency, high immunogenicity, and relatively poor safety profiles [13].

Notably, peptides have emerged as a promising focus in biomedical research owing to their low molecular weight, minimal immunogenicity, ease of modification, and biodegradability [14]. A growing body of studies has demonstrated that peptide-based drugs exert significant positive effects in the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases [15]. Therefore, peptides specifically designed to target αSyn aggregates hold great potential as a powerful strategy for treating diseases associated with αSyn misfolding and aggregation.

Pyrroloquinoline quinone (PQQ), a redox cofactor, plays essential physiological roles, including serving as a nutritional factor and scavenging free radicals. Studies have shown that PQQ can modulate mitochondrial function by targeting respiratory chain complexes I and III. Both in vitro and in vivo, PQQ has been shown to counteract cellular damage induced by complex I inhibition, prevent the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, and help maintain mitochondrial function, as well as exhibiting an excellent safety profile in vivo [16–18]. In Alzheimer’s disease research, PQQ exerts neuroprotection by ameliorating cognitive deficits, regulating genes related to synaptic function and anti-apoptosis, decreasing ROS levels, and activating the SIRT1/CREB pathway [19]. Thus, we propose to leverage the antioxidant properties of PQQ to improve mitochondrial status in dopaminergic neurons in the brain.

The presence of the BBB poses a significant challenge to the efficient accumulation of peptides and PQQ in pathological regions such as the substantia nigra (SN) and striatum. Exosomes, which are nanoscale extracellular vesicles with a lipid bilayer membrane derived from the invagination of the cytoplasmic membrane, offer advantages such as low toxicity, minimal immunogenicity, high plasma stability, and the ability to cross the BBB efficiently [20, 21]. However, the stability of exosomes in systemic circulation is limited. Our recent findings have revealed that hybrid exosome-liposome nanoparticles, formed by combining exosomes with liposomes, can integrate the advantageous properties of both systems and successfully traverse the BBB [22]. Furthermore, the membrane structure of liposomes allows for efficient encapsulation of various types of therapeutics, significantly enhancing drug loading capacity.

This study aims to leverage a specifically designed peptide molecule, obtained through independent high-throughput screening, to bind to abnormal αSyn aggregates, thereby preventing or reversing their aggregation process and reducing neuronal damage caused by these toxic species. Meanwhile, PQQ exerts potent antioxidant and neuroprotective effects to facilitate mitochondrial biogenesis and improve overall mitochondrial quality and function. Previous studies have confirmed that PQQ possesses neuroprotective properties in rotenone-induced PD models, but intraperitoneal injection, the mainstream administration route adopted in existing research, has inherent limitations, including low drug bioavailability and insufficient lesion-targeting ability [16, 18]. Herein, we further validated the mitochondrial protective effects of PQQ in a 6-hydroxydopamine-induced model, which complements previous rotenone-based studies and broadens the research scope of PQQ in PD. In addition, we co-delivered targeted peptides and PQQ via hybrid exosome carriers. This optimized delivery strategy overcomes the poor tissue penetration of free drugs, enables efficient accumulation in lesioned brain regions [23, 24], reduces αSyn oligomer levels, and alleviates oxidative stress, thereby ultimately ameliorating the pathological damage of PD. This approach may provide novel insights and a reference for future PD treatment strategies.

Results

PQQ protects mitochondria from dysfunction

Aggregated αSyn can promote oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, which is another important pathological hallmark of PD. PQQ, emerged as an essential vitamin-like substance, have demonstrated powerful anti-aging properties via promoting mitochondrial biogenesis [17, 25]. To explore the mitochondrial protective effect of PQQ on PD, we applied PQQ to a 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA)-induced PD cell model. First, SH-SY5Y cells were co-incubated with PQQ or 6-OHDA, and then cell viability was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. The results demonstrated that PQQ was non-cytotoxic with excellent biocompatibility (Fig. 1A). 15 µM PQQ effectively rescued cellular damage in the H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress model and significantly alleviated 6-OHDA-induced neurotoxicity (Fig. 1B and S1). Meanwhile, 60 µM 6-OHDA was determined as the optimal concentration for establishing the PD model and was used in subsequent experiments.

To confirm the ability of PQQ to scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS) as an antioxidant, we assessed cytoplasmic ROS and mitochondrial ROS using 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) and MitoSOX assays, respectively. A strong fluorescent signal was observed in the 6-OHDA group, while the fluorescent signal was decreased after PQQ or exosome-liposome hybrids loaded with 15 µM PQQ (EL@PQQ) treatment. The data indicated that PQQ treatment reduced the levels of cytoplasmic and mitochondrial ROS (Fig. 1C, D).

To evaluate the role of PQQ in mitochondrial quality, we used MitoTracker and JC-1 dyes. We found that 6-OHDA treatment decreased mitochondrial quality and membrane potential, while these phenomena were reversed after PQQ or EL@PQQ treatment (Fig. 1E-G). Furthermore, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis revealed that, compared with the 6-OHDA group, PQQ or EL@PQQ treatment ameliorated mitochondrial cristae fragmentation (Fig. 1H, I). These findings demonstrate that PQQ exerts a protective effect on mitochondrial function in the 6-OHDA-induced PD cell model.

In vitro blocking of αSyn aggregation by sPep

Based on the “one-bead-one-compound” (OBOC) approach, we have developed a microarray chip-based high-throughput screening method, in which positive peptides specifically binding to αSyn were screened and isolated from the OBOC library by magnetic bead sorting combined with the biotin-streptavidin system, and finally obtained an attractive peptide targeting αSyn named sPep with the dissociation constants (KD) being 8.72 × 10− 8 mol/L detected using surface plasmon resonance imaging (SPRi) (Fig. S2) [26]. To investigate whether sPep reduces αSyn toxicity by binding to its domains and inducing disaggregation or conformational changes, we first co-incubated sPep with PFFs in vitro. TEM, Thioflavin T (ThT) assay, and dot blot analysis demonstrated that sPep treatment reduced PFFs aggregation in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 2A-C). We then established in vitro PD models by overexpressing either SNCA-WT (wild-type) or SNCA-A53T (mutant) in SH-SY5Y neuronal cells, followed by treatment with PFFs. Immunofluorescence staining for pathological αSyn (p-αSyn) and sPep-Cy5 revealed their co-localization within cells (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, the addition of sPep significantly reduced the levels of endogenous pathological p-αSyn (Fig. 2E and S3) in both WT and A53T cell models. Collectively, these results indicate that sPep effectively inhibits αSyn aggregation in vitro.

Isolation and identification of neuronal exosomes and construction of “exosome-liposome hybrid nanodrug delivery system”

The BBB significantly impedes the efficient delivery of therapeutic peptides and antioxidants, such as sPep and PQQ, to the pathological regions of PD. To address this issue, we developed an exosome-liposome hybrid nanocarrier, which is designed to combine the advantageous properties of both delivery systems for enhanced BBB penetration. We first isolated neuronal exosomes via ultracentrifugation. Exosomes natively carry a variety of membrane proteins and bioactive molecules. To mitigate the potential risks associated with cell-derived exosomes and minimize the potential immunogenicity, we employed an ultrasound-assisted method [27] to remove their components. Compared with the native exosome control group (Exo), the ultrasound-treated exosome group (Exo@S) exhibited a significant reduction in protein contents, alongside with a marked decrease in RNA levels, another major type of components in exosomes (Fig. S4). This approach effectively improves the biosafety of exosomes as delivery vehicles. These exosomes were then fused with liposomes using an ultrasonic method to form exosome-liposome hybrids, which were subsequently co-loaded with sPep and the antioxidant PQQ, creating a novel nanotherapeutic agent termed “EL@sPep/P”. Characterization by TEM revealed the classic cup-shaped morphology of purified Exo, liposomes loaded with sPep/PQQ (Lip@sPep/P), and EL@sPep/P (Fig. 3A). Western blot analysis further indicated that Exo and EL@sPep/P expressed the transmembrane proteins CD63 and CD9 (the two most commonly used exosome markers), with positive expression of endosomal sorting complex-related proteins Alix and TSG101, and negative expression of the endoplasmic reticulum marker Calnexin (Fig. 3B). DLS analysis showed that the particle size of EL@sPep/P was 137.42 nm, indicating that ultrasonication and drug loading did not significantly alter the size of exosomes (Fig. 3C), and the zeta potential was negative (Fig. 3D). Consistently, nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) indicated that the sizes of Exo, Lip@sPep/P, and EL@sPep/P were 125 ± 2.9 nm, 127 ± 5.2 nm, and 131 ± 6.1 nm, respectively. The encapsulation efficiency of EL@sPep/P for sPep was slightly superior to Lip@sPep/P, while the encapsulation efficiency showed no difference for PQQ (Fig. S5). Based on the concentration and our previous results, the therapeutic concentration was adjusted to 5 × 108 particles/µL (5 × 1010 particles per mouse) for injection. The release of sPep and PQQ from EL@sPep/P increased with time (Fig. S6).

To investigate the cellular uptake and BBB-penetrating capacity of EL@sPep/P, we established an in vitro BBB model using bEnd.3 endothelial cells cultured in Transwell inserts. When EL@sPep/P labeled with FITC and Cy5.5 was applied to this model, time-dependent accumulation of fluorescence was observed in the SH-SY5Y cells located in the lower chamber, indicating successful transit across the endothelial barrier (Fig. 3E, F). This functionality was further validated in a PD mouse model. Following tail vein injection of fluorescently labeled EL@sPep/P, in vivo imaging system (IVIS) analysis detected a distinct fluorescence signal in the brain as early as 1 h post-injection, with the EL@sPep/P group exhibiting highly enriched fluorescence signal (Fig. 3G and S7A). At 24 h post-injection, the EL@sPep/P group exhibited significant fluorescent signals in the SN of ex vivo brain tissues (Fig. 3H, I and S7B, C), with an intensity approximately 2.7-fold higher than that of the free Cy5.5 group. Furthermore, kinetic analysis of blood fluorescence revealed that EL@sPep/P had a prolonged circulation half-life of 3.8 h in mice (Fig. 3J), a property that is crucial for its therapeutic delivery to the brain. Ex vivo organ fluorescence imaging confirmed the in vivo distribution profile of EL@sPep/P (Fig. S7D, E). Both free Cy5.5 and Cy5.5-labeled EL@sPep/P primarily accumulated in the liver and kidney, consistent with the physiological role of these organs as the main sites of metabolism and excretion. Notably, EL@sPep/P achieved a significantly higher proportion of brain fluorescence among all major organs compared to free Cy5.5 (2.14 ± 0.09% vs. 0.73 ± 0.06%) (Fig. S7F), demonstrating specific enrichment in the brain. Combined with the favorable biocompatibility verified by HE staining (Fig. S8), these results indicate that EL@sPep/P serves as a highly efficient, low-toxicity brain-targeted nanodelivery system.

EL@sPep/P improves motor behaviors in PD model mice

The pathological features of PD lead to motor dysfunction, and PD animal models (Fig. 4A) were utilized to recapitulate core clinical symptoms of human PD, including bradykinesia and postural instability. To validate the PD model and exclude interference from surgery and empty AAV9 vector, we included a normal control group and an AAV9 empty vector group. Results showed no significant differences in motor ability, the number of tyrosine hydroxylase (TH)-positive neurons in the SN, or TH protein expression between the two groups (Fig. S9). This confirmed that surgery and empty vector injection alone did not induce dopaminergic neuron damage or motor abnormalities, and the pathological changes in the PD model were specifically mediated by SNCA (A53T) overexpression. To evaluate the effects of EL@sPep/P on motor performance, PD model mice were divided into four groups, including PBS (Ctrl), sPep/P, Lip@sPep/P, and EL@sPep/P. After 28 days of tail vein injection (Fig. 4B), the open field test revealed that compared with the control group, the treated groups exhibited increased total movement distance and residence time in the center zone (Fig. 4C-G), indicating improved spontaneous activity and exploratory behavior following treatment. Rotarod and pole climbing tests were used to evaluate the balance and coordination ability of mice. The results showed that mice treated with EL@sPep/P had a prolonged time on the rotarod and a shortened time to climb from the top to the bottom of the pole, demonstrating a significant improvement in the motor balance ability of the mice (Fig. 4H-K). Gait analysis was performed to evaluate stride parameters (brake time, single stance, swing speed, run speed, and stride length). The Ctrl group exhibited typical Parkinsonian features, such as shuffling steps and inconsistent step lengths. In contrast, the treatment groups, especially the EL@sPep/P group, showed a marked improvement in mouse gait (Fig. 4L-O and S10). The above results indicated that EL@sPep/P contributes to the improvement of motor and cognitive functions in PD model mice.

EL@sPep/P exerts neuroprotective effects by degrading αSyn aggregates in PD model mice

To further confirm the in vivo therapeutic effects of EL@sPep/P on PD mice, we detected changes in αSyn aggregates in the SN region of mice using frozen section amyloid ThT fluorescent staining kit and immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 5A, B). The high fluorescence intensity in the control group indicated severe αSyn aggregation. After treatment with sPep/P, the fluorescence intensity slightly decreased. Treatment with Lip@sPep/P and EL@sPep/P further reduced the fluorescence intensity, with the most significant change observed in the EL@sPep/P group. The data indicated that EL@sPep/P effectively reduced αSyn aggregation.

Neuroprotective effects of EL@sPep/P in PD model mice. (A) αSyn aggregation levels detected by Thioflavin T (ThT) staining. (B) αSyn immunofluorescence staining of the mouse SN. (C, D) Western blot analysis of αSyn, TH, and DAT protein expression in the mouse SN and corresponding statistical analysis (n = 3, one-way analysis of variance). (E, F) TH immunofluorescence staining of the mouse SN and corresponding statistical analysis

TH, the rate-limiting enzyme in dopamine (DA) synthesis, is a specific marker of dopaminergic neurons in the SN and is used to evaluate neuronal damage in PD research. Therefore, we performed immunofluorescence staining and western blot analysis of TH in the mouse SN (Fig. 5C-F). Our data showed that the expression of TH was low in the SN of PD model mice, indicating severe death of dopaminergic neurons. After treatment in different groups, the expression of TH increased, with the most significant change in the EL@sPep/P group. We also performed western blot analysis for αSyn and dopamine transporter (DAT) (Fig. 5C, D). The results showed that treatment in different groups could decrease the expression of αSyn, while EL@sPep/P treatment exhibited the most significant restoration of TH expression and also restored DAT expression. These findings demonstrate that EL@sPep/P can reduce the death of dopaminergic neurons in the SN of PD model mice.

EL@sPep/P reduces oxidative stress levels in the SN of PD model mice

Having demonstrated that PQQ reduces ROS levels in vitro, we further investigated whether the in vivo treatment could protect dopaminergic neurons by reducing oxidative stress. RNA sequencing results showed that the expression of antioxidant stress-related genes was upregulated after EL@sPep/P treatment, and GO enrichment analysis indicated a significant enrichment of antioxidant stress-related pathways (Fig. 6A, B and S11). We assessed markers of oxidative stress in the mouse SN using western blot analysis. LRRK2 (leucine-rich repeat kinase 2) and DJ-1 (PARK7) are key genetic risk factors for PD, both of which affect neuronal survival by regulating the oxidative stress pathway. Our results showed that after treatment, LRRK2 expression was decreased, while DJ-1 expression was increased (Fig. 6C, D).

(B) GSVA enrichment scores of GO terms in the SN of mice after EL@sPep/P treatment (P < 0.05). (C) Protein levels of oxidative stress-related markers and JAK2-STAT3 pathway-related indicators in the SN measured by western blot, with quantitative results shown in (D-E). (F) Representative TEM images of mitochondria in the SN

Previous studies have indicated that LRRK2 promotes the degradation of Nrf2, whereas DJ-1 stabilizes Nrf2 expression [28]. As a core regulator of the antioxidant response, Nrf2 activation promotes glutathione (GSH) synthesis and GPX4 expression, enhancing cellular resistance to oxidative damage. Western blot and immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated that treatment with EL@sPep/P upregulated the expression of Nrf2, GSH, and GPX4, while downregulating the expression of cleaved caspase-8 (C-caspase-8), a key protein in apoptosis (Fig. 6C, D and S12). The levels of GSH and GSH/GSSG ratio in mouse plasma were assessed using biochemical assays (Fig. S13). The results indicated that EL@sPep/P activated the antioxidant stress pathway and elevated GSH levels relative to the control group.

RNA-sequencing also showed that after EL@sPep/P treatment, the tyrosine phosphorylation of STAT protein is positively regulated as indicated by Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) (Fig. 6B), which acts as the core execution step and key regulatory node of the JAK2-STAT3 signaling pathway [29]. Previous studies have demonstrated that decreased STAT3 phosphorylation levels in neurons lead to impaired autophagic function and neuronal death [29]. Our results indicated that EL@sPep/P treatment led to the phosphorylation and activation of JAK2 and STAT3, which was accompanied by an increase in the inflammatory cytokine IL-22 and a decrease in the suppressors of cytokine signaling (SOCS) family proteins, including SOCS1 and SOCS3 (Fig. 6C, E and S14, S15). Additionally, increased levels of the inflammatory factors IL-6 and IL-10 in mouse plasma were observed after EL@sPep/P treatment detected by ELISA (Fig. S16).

Furthermore, TEM analysis revealed that, compared with the control group, EL@sPep/P treatment ameliorated mitochondrial cristae fragmentation (Fig. 6F). These findings collectively indicate that EL@sPep/P treatment in vivo enhances antioxidant capacity, reduces cellular damage, protects against mitochondrial dysfunction, and improves neuronal survival in PD model mice (Fig. 7).

Discussion

As the most core pathological feature of PD, the abnormal aggregation of αSyn has long been regarded as a key target for research into PD pathogenesis and the development of therapeutic strategies. αSyn can exert sustained damage on dopaminergic neurons in the SN of the midbrain through multiple pathways, including protein misfolding, Lewy body formation, induction of oxidative stress and neuroinflammation, thereby strongly triggering the typical motor impairments and cognitive decline in PD patients. Studies have demonstrated that the levels of αSyn aggregates in the brain, plasma and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of PD patients are significantly higher than those in healthy controls [30, 31]. In vulnerable brain regions of PD patients such as the SN and striatum, physiologically soluble αSyn monomers misfold into oligomers and further aggregate into fibrillary tangles. These tangles not only serve as the core structural component of Lewy bodies, but also induce neurotoxic stress through multiple mechanisms: First, αSyn inhibits the activation of the JAK2-STAT3 signaling pathway and reduces the phosphorylation level of STAT3, thereby exacerbating microglial dysfunction and oxidative stress-induced neuronal damage [32, 33]. Second, αSyn aggregates disrupt endoplasmic reticulum and protein homeostasis, leading to lysosomal dysfunction and subsequent local neuronal injury [34]. On this basis, the aggregates bind to receptors including LAG3 and RAGE via their C-terminal domains, mediating the prion-like propagation of pathological aggregates and facilitating the spread of lesions throughout the brain. Collectively, these processes result in the progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons [35, 36], which confirms the close association between αSyn aggregation and the pathogenesis of PD.

Based on these well-defined pathogenic mechanisms and core therapeutic targets, substantial progress has been made in therapeutic research targeting αSyn aggregation in recent years. The αSyn-targeting monoclonal antibody prasinezumab, in a 4-year phase Ⅱ clinical trial, specifically binds to aggregated αSyn and inhibits its propagation, slowing the rate of motor function deterioration in patients with early-stage PD by over 40% [37]. The small-molecule inhibitor GSD-16-24 successfully suppresses pathological spread by blocking the interaction between αSyn fibrils and pathogenic receptors [38]. The nanomolar-scale Ig-like binder attenuates dopaminergic neuronal damage via specifically blocking the binding of αSyn fibrils to neuronal surface receptors, as well as inhibiting pathological aggregation and propagation [39]. These studies not only verify the pathogenicity of αSyn aggregation, but also clarify the clinical value of therapeutic strategies aimed at clearing such aggregates.

Oxidative stress plays a central role in the pathological progression of PD. It not only directly damages dopaminergic neurons in the SN, but also promotes the misfolding and aggregation of αSyn, thus accelerating pathological propagation [40]. For instance, oxidative modification of αSyn (e.g., at the Cys106 site) by ROS facilitates its oligomerization and pS129 phosphorylation. In turn, aggregated αSyn further impairs mitochondria, exacerbating ROS release [41, 42]. In addition, epalrestat binds to and degrades KEAP1 to activate the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway, alleviating oxidative stress-induced damage and thereby protecting dopaminergic neurons in PD models [43]. PtCu alloy nanozymes, via their superoxide dismutase (SOD)- and catalase-like activities, scavenge ROS, inhibit the intercellular propagation of phosphorylated αSyn induced by PFFs, prevent the spread of αSyn from the striatum to the SN in vivo, rescue dopaminergic neurons and ameliorate motor functions [44]. These findings highlight the critical importance of antioxidant interventions for PD therapy [44]. Therefore, we adopted a strategy that simultaneously reduces αSyn tangles and alleviates local oxidative stress in a spatiotemporally coordinated manner, aiming to achieve enhanced therapeutic efficacy.

Based on this design rationale, we employed PQQ to reduce oxidative stress levels in PD models. PQQ possesses excellent antioxidant properties, being capable of combating ROS-mediated oxidative stress, delaying senescence, preventing neurodegenerative diseases, and mediating cardiovascular health. For example, PQQ upregulates the activities of endogenous antioxidant enzymes such as SOD and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), scavenges ROS in the brain and mitochondria of mouse models of AD, and alleviates the neurotoxicity induced by oxidative damage [45]. It blocks the pro-inflammatory effects of the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) by targeting heat shock protein HSPA8, thereby ameliorating senescence-related pathological conditions [46]. By activating the AMPK/PGC-1α signaling pathway, PQQ promotes mitochondrial biogenesis in pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells and endothelial cells, enhances mitochondrial respiratory function, repairs mitochondrial structural and functional damage, and ultimately ameliorates the pathological progression of pulmonary arterial hypertension [47]. Numerous studies have found no evidence of toxicity or genotoxicity associated with PQQ, confirming its safety and supporting its classification as a safe nutritional supplement. In animal toxicology studies, for instance, high-dose oral administration of disodium PQQ only caused reversible renal damage in female rats, with no significant genotoxicity observed [48]. Clinical trials in humans have verified that disodium PQQ exhibits significant antioxidant activity; its pharmacokinetic profile is similar to that of B vitamins, with high intestinal absorption and renal excretion efficiency, and no in vivo accumulation detected within 24 h [49]. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved a variety of disodium PQQ dietary supplements, with no reported side effects to date [50].

Notably, the ability of sPep to degrade aggregated αSyn in PD models is a fascinating phenomenon identified in our study, and its specific mechanism awaits further investigation. In a recent study, Hou et al. reported that a self-developed peptide molecule could competitively bind to the self-aggregation sites of tau protein [51]. Specifically, the D-enantiomeric peptide D-TLKIVWC disassembled tau fibrils by binding to tau monomers and relieving conformational stress [51]. It self-assembled into “pseudo-amyloid fibrils” that interacted with tau fibrils isolated from the brains of AD patients, and then disrupted the intermolecular hydrogen bonds of tau through mechanical torque generated by conformational strain release [51]. This process enabled efficient tau depolymerization without the need for additional energy input, enzymatic activity, or external energy sources [51]. This pioneering work provides novel insights for our follow-up mechanistic studies, facilitating the elucidation of the structural and chemical underpinnings of αSyn aggregation, and opening up promising avenues for the early intervention of PD pathogenesis. Further studies have demonstrated that hTau accumulation induces acetylation of STAT1, which subsequently binds to and sequesters STAT3 in the cytoplasm, leading to its inactivation and ultimately contributing to synaptic and cognitive impairments. Overexpression of STAT3 was shown to reverse such damage [52]. In AD patients, the level of activated STAT3 in hippocampal neurons is significantly reduced, with amyloid protein potentially acting as a trigger. Furthermore, levels of activated STAT3 and its activating factors decline with aging, suggesting that STAT3 inactivation is associated with AD pathogenesis [53]. Consistently, our experimental results demonstrate that EL@sPep/P treatment activates the JAK2-STAT3 signaling pathway and exerts neuroprotective effects in PD model mice.

Furthermore, we fully recognize the dual nature of the JAK2-STAT3 pathway: it can both amplify and prolong pathological neuroinflammation, and also trigger anti-inflammatory immune responses in the central nervous system. Its functional outcome is determined by the activation status: transient, moderate activation participates in immune defense and tissue repair, exerting protective effects; while chronic, sustained activation, due to dysregulation of negative feedback mechanisms such as SOCS proteins, shifts to detrimental pathological inflammation [54–56]. The present study demonstrated that EL@sPep/P treatment moderately activated the JAK2-STAT3 pathway and upregulated anti-inflammatory factors such as IL-22, exerting neuroprotective effects on neurons rather than triggering pathological inflammatory responses. Although IL-6 was mildly elevated, no sustained pro-inflammatory feedback loop was formed, and no pathological changes were observed in HE-stained sections of the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, indicating favorable in vivo biosafety of EL@sPep/P. These results indicate that EL@sPep/P can remodel the protective anti-inflammatory effects of the JAK2-STAT3 pathway, providing mechanistic and safety evidence for its application in mouse models of PD. Notably, the neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects of EL@sPep/P are attributed to the synergistic action of PQQ and sPep, rather than the action of either component alone. sPep targets αSyn, blocks upstream pathological events, and reduces pathological activation of the JAK2-STAT3 pathway. PQQ acts directly on this pathway, mediating its moderate activation and upregulating anti-inflammatory factors to exert protective effects. These two components complement each other in upstream inhibition and downstream regulation, illustrating the benefits of multi-target synergistic intervention.

BBB serves as a protective selective barrier for the brain, and the mechanism by which exosomes cross the BBB remains incompletely understood. EL@sPep/P in this study is a nanoscale exosome delivery system with a size far exceeding that of small molecules capable of free diffusion, making it more likely to cross the BBB via active transport. Previous studies have confirmed that specific proteins on the exosome membrane surface (e.g., tetraspanins and integrins) can interact with endothelial cell receptors and deliver cargo into the brain through receptor-mediated transcytosis [57–59], providing a theoretical basis for the brain-targeted delivery of EL@sPep/P. In addition, fusion-mediated transport is a key pathway for exosomes to cross the BBB. Exosomes fuse directly with endothelial cell membranes with the help of phosphatidylserine, cholesterol and sphingomyelin. Their abundant lipid rafts facilitate membrane bending and fusion. This process protects exosomes from endosomal and lysosomal degradation [59, 60]. Beyond efficient brain delivery, the safety of exosome-based systems is equally critical for clinical translation. Exosome therapy is widely used, with safety a key concern. In cynomolgus monkeys, exosome treatment showed no immune or biochemical differences from controls, indicating low immunogenicity and good translational potential [61]. Previous studies demonstrate that ultrasound treatment reduces endogenous enzyme activity in exosomes and causes damage/loss of intravesicular bioactive molecules [62, 63], which supports our strategy of using ultrasound to deplete exosomal contents and lower immunogenicity.

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, the pathogenesis of PD remains poorly defined, and abnormal αSyn aggregation represents a central pathological hallmark and key therapeutic target. Unlike uniform PFFs-induced aggregates in vitro, patient-derived αSyn displays distinct conformational heterogeneity [64]. Although our peptide efficiently disassembled pathological aggregates, the shared features and regulatory mechanisms of endogenous αSyn strains require further exploration. Second, aberrant Lewy body deposition drives the disruption of the cerebral pathological microenvironment [65]. This study only focused on peptide-mediated disaggregation and PQQ-related antioxidative effects. More comprehensive multi-target and time-regulated strategies are needed to better adapt to the complex brain microenvironment. Furthermore, the hybrid exosome-liposome system combines favorable biocompatibility and flexible modification. Nevertheless, cumbersome purification, limited scalable production and intrinsic liposome drawbacks hinder clinical translation. Subsequent optimization will simplify preparation while preserving the brain-homing capacity of exosomes. Finally, although SH-SY5Y-derived exosomes are widely applicable for research [66, 67], their targeting ability toward the SN is still limited. The development of highly nigra-targeted vehicles will facilitate precise PD treatment. Future work will address the above limitations to support the translational application of this therapeutic strategy.

Conclusions

In conclusion, in the present study, the hybrid exosome strategy has improved the in vivo circulation retention of exosomes to a certain extent and enhanced their rigidity, thus exhibiting great potential for clinical application. Current treatments for PD only target a single step in the pathological mechanism: inhibiting upstream αSyn aggregation fails to reverse pre-existing downstream damage, and repairing downstream injury cannot block ongoing upstream damage. Therefore, multi-stage intervention across the complex pathological process is critically needed. Encapsulating PQQ in functional exosomes to simultaneously resolve αSyn aggregation and repair neurons in situ may serve as a more promising therapeutic approach for PD, which is worthy of further in-depth investigation. Zhang constructed engineered exosomes that protect phosphorus dendrimers and quercetin from enzymatic degradation, prolong their in vivo half-life, continuously alleviate oxidative stress-induced damage, activate autophagic pathways to accelerate αSyn degradation, and ultimately achieve dual effects of neuroprotection and pathological clearance [68]. A hybrid system of human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes and nanoliposomes was developed for co-delivering baicalin in PD treatment. This delivery system demonstrates high stability and BBB permeability, and exhibits higher drug internalization efficiency compared with standalone nanoliposomes; it exerts core therapeutic effects by inhibiting αSyn fibrillation and neurotoxicity, thereby ameliorating PD pathology [69]. The aforementioned studies indicate that the multi-modal therapeutic strategy based on hybrid exosome-mediated drug delivery holds distinct advantages in PD treatment. These findings also corroborate that the multi-target therapeutic regimen proposed in our study possesses favorable research prospects and application potential.

Materials and methods

Animals

48 male wild-type mice on a C57BL/6 background, weighing approximately 20 g at 8 weeks of age, were purchased from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. Mice were kept in specific pathogen-free environment under standard conditions. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Fujian Medical University (Approval No. IACUC FJMU 2025-0098).

Cell culture

The human neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cell line (Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Catalog No. SCSP-5014) was cultured in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco). The cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. For the experiments, cells were seeded into 6-well plates and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Subsequently, the SH-SY5Y cells were transfected with the pcSLenti-CMV-SNCA(A53T)-linker-EGFP-3⋅FLAG-PGK-puro-WPRE3 (obtained from Obio Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) lentiviral vector. Following transfection, positive cells were selected using puromycin. Alternatively, cells were seeded in 24-well plates, and intracellular αSyn aggregation was induced via pcDNA3.1-EGFP-hSNCA (obtained from GenePharma Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) plasmid transfection combined with preformed fibrils (PFFs, Arco, ALN-H5214) [70].

Peptide screening

An OBOC peptide library was constructed via the mix-and-split method with Fmoc solid-phase peptide synthesis using TentaGel resin as the support [71]. Positive peptides specifically binding to αSyn were screened and isolated from the OBOC library by magnetic bead sorting combined with the biotin-streptavidin system. The SPRi chip with adsorbed peptides was blocked and subjected to SPRi analysis. At 4 °C, gradient concentrations of αSyn were used as the mobile phase, and association and dissociation signals were recorded following a cyclic protocol: baseline, association, dissociation, and regeneration. The sensorgrams were fitted and analyzed using BIAevaluation software to determine the KD between the peptides and αSyn.

Isolation, purification and characterizations of neuronal-derived exosomes

To isolate exosomes, SH-SY5Y cells were first cultured in exosome-free complete medium for 72 h. The conditioned supernatant was collected and centrifuged sequentially at 800 × g for 5 min and 2,000 × g for 10 min to remove cells and debris. The supernatant was then filtered through a 0.22 μm filter and ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 70 min (Himac CP100NX). The resulting pellet was resuspended in PBS and ultracentrifuged again under identical conditions to obtain the purified exosome fraction. Exosome morphology was visualized using TEM. Particle size distribution and zeta potential were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS).

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)

Exosomes were serially diluted in PBS to ensure the particle concentration was within the linear detection range of the instrument. Detection was carried out at 25 °C, with a 60-second video acquired per run, and the experiment was repeated three times. The particle concentration and size were analyzed using the instrument’s built-in software.

Synthesis of Lip@sPep/P and EL@sPep/P

Liposomes were prepared using the thin-film hydration method. Briefly, 2 mg of DOPE, 1.03 mg of Cholesterol, and 2.34 mg of DSPE-PEG2000 (or DSPE-PEG2000-FITC) were dissolved in 3 mL of chloroform to form a homogeneous mixture. The chloroform was evaporated under vacuum in a rotary evaporator at 45 °C, resulting in the formation of a thin, uniform lipid film on the inner wall of the round-bottom flask. The lipid film was hydrated with 4 mL of PBS containing dissolved 3 mg PQQ and 1 mg sPep at 45 °C for 30 min with agitation to form the loaded liposomal nanoparticles. The resulting liposome suspension was then vortexed for 3 min, followed by 10 min of sonication and a single extrusion through a 0.22 μm polycarbonate membrane. Both PQQ and sPep were encapsulated within the liposomes, which are termed Lip@sPep/P. To prepare the hybrid exosomes, the lipid film and exosomes were mixed at a 9:1 mass ratio. PQQ and sPep in PBS were added to this mixture, which was then processed identically to the subsequent steps as described above. The final product is referred to as EL@sPep/P.

Thioflavin T (ThT) assay

Protein aggregation kinetics were monitored using the ThT fluorescence assay. PFFs were diluted to a final concentration of 10 µM in PBS and mixed with 50 µM sPep. The mixture was then incubated at 37 °C in an orbital shaker (1000 rpm). At different time points, ThT dye was added to the mixture, which was then shaken for 1 h. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a fluorescence microplate reader at an excitation wavelength of 450 nm and an emission wavelength of 482 nm.

Exosome labeling

1 × 1012 particles of exosomes or hybrid exosomes were mixed with Cy5.5 maleimide by gentle pipetting, followed by incubation at room temperature in the dark for 10 min. β‑mercaptoethanol was added to a final concentration of 2 mM to conjugate unreacted free dye. The exosome suspension was then made up with PBS, and ultracentrifugation was performed at 100,000 × g for 70 min at 4 °C to collect the exosome pellet. Finally, the pellet was gently resuspended in PBS to complete the labeling.

BBB permeability in vivo and in vitro

In vivo: Liposomes were labeled with FITC, and exosomes were conjugated with Cy5.5-MAL. Each mouse was intravenously injected with 100 µL of the solution via the tail vein. Immediately following injection, the mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and imaged using a small animal in vivo imaging system (PerkinElmer) for fluorescence detection. After the in vivo imaging, the mice were euthanized, and the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were harvested for ex vivo fluorescence imaging under the same parameters.

In vitro: The mouse brain microvascular endothelial cell line (bEnd.3) was seeded in the upper chamber of polyester Transwell inserts (Corning), while SH-SY5Y cells were cultured in the lower chamber. Following the confirmation of monolayer integrity, the permeability assay was performed using fluorescence-labeled tracer molecules. The subsequent fluorescence imaging analysis of the cell layer in the lower chamber was conducted with a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope.

Measurement of cell viability

The CCK-8 assay (M8180, Solarbio Life Sciences, China) were used to measure cell viability, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, SH-SY5Y cells were seeded in 96-well plates. After 24 h of culture, the medium was replaced, and the following steps were performed. (1) To investigate the cytotoxicity of PQQ, the cells were incubated in fresh medium containing different concentrations of PQQ (0–30 µM) for 48 h. (2) To measure the effects of 6-OHDA (162957, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) on SH-SY5Y cells, the cells were cultured with different concentrations (0-300 µM) of 6-OHDA in fresh medium for 48 h. To determine the protective role of PQQ, cells were co-treated with 6-OHDA in the presence or absence of 15 µM PQQ for the same duration. Lastly, each group of cells was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. The cell viability (%) was calculated as the absorbance of the experimental group/the absorbance of the control group ×100%.

Multiparameter assessment of cytoplasmic ROS and mitochondrial function in live cells

All live-cell staining assays for cytoplasmic ROS (DCFH-DA, MCE, HY-D0940), mitochondrial ROS (MitoSOX Red, MCE, HY-D1055), mitochondrial membrane potential (JC-1, MCE, HY-15534), and mitochondrial mass (MitoTracker, Yeasen, 40742ES50) were performed following a standard base protocol. Briefly, cells seeded in 24-well plates were subjected to stimulation for 48 h prior to subsequent staining. Before staining, the medium was aspirated, and cells were rinsed twice with pre-warmed PBS to remove serum interference. All dyes were diluted to their respective working concentrations in DMEM/F12 medium, and nuclear dyes were added. The mixture was then applied to each well at 500 µL per well and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ environment protected from light. After incubation, unbound dyes were removed by rinsing three times with PBS, and the samples were immediately analyzed to prevent fluorescence quenching.

Western blot

Proteins were separated on 4–12% SDS-PAGE gels (36263ES10, Yeasen) and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore, USA). After blocking with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the indicated primary antibodies, followed by a 1 h incubation at room temperature with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. The signals were then detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) system.

Thioflavin T fluorescent staining of amyloid in frozen sections

For Thioflavine T staining (Kit HPBIO-JM4674), the cryosections were first rinsed with the clearing solution (Reagent A). Following a 10-minute fixation with Reagent B at 4 °C and another rinse with Reagent A, the sections were stained with Reagent C for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. After removal of the staining solution, the sections were incubated with Reagent A for 1 min. The intensification step was then carried out using Reagent D (10 min, room temperature), followed by a final 3-minute incubation with Reagent A. Finally, the sections were mounted with an anti-fade medium and immediately visualized under a (confocal) fluorescence microscope (λ_ex = 440 nm, λ_em = 490 nm).

Stereotactic injections

Stereotactic injection of AAV9-CMV-SNCA(A53T)-P2A-mCherry (Vigenebio Biosciences, Jinan, China) into the SN region was performed in 2-month-old male C57 mice using Nanoliter Microinjection Pump (anteroposterior, -3.1 mm; mediolateral, ± 1.35 mm; dorsoventral, -4.4 mm relative to the bregma) at an infusion rate of 100 nL/min (total amount of virus: 10E13 μg/mL, 500 nL; n = 12 for each group). All surgeries were conducted on animals on a stereotactic frame (RWD Life Science Co., LTD, Guangdong, China) under anesthesia.

Behaviour analysis

Pole test

Pole test

For the pole test, the apparatus consisted of a 50 cm-long wooden rod (10 mm in diameter) wrapped with bandage gauze to provide grip. Prior to the formal test, mice underwent training for two consecutive days, with each training session comprising three trials. On the day of the test, mice were placed on the top of the rod with its head facing down. The total time taken for the mouse to reach the base of the rod was recorded. The maximum cutoff of time to stop the test and recording was 60 s.

(2)Rotarod test

Rotarod test

For the rotarod test, mice were trained for 2 sequential days on the rotarod. Each daily training session involved placing the mice on the rotating rod with an initial speed of 4 rpm and an acceleration of 8 rpm/min up to a maximum speed of 20 rpm, with a maximum duration of 10 min per session. Mice were given three test trials on the test day. The rotating rod speed was adjusted from 4 rpm to a maximum of 40 rpm, with a gradual acceleration of 8 rpm/min throughout each trial. Each trial started and continued for 10 min. The duration of time that the mice remained on the rotating rod was recorded. The results represent the average value of the three trials.

(3)Open field

Open field

For the open field test, each mouse was placed at the center of the bottom of the box, and video recording was conducted simultaneously for 10 min. After completing the test for one mouse, the inner walls and bottom of the box were cleaned thoroughly before replacing it with the next mouse to proceed with the experiment. The total distance traveled by the mice and the time spent in the central zone were quantified for analysis.

(4)Gait analysis experiment

Gait analysis experiment

For the gait test, the walking runway captured by the camera was set to 25 cm in length and 4 cm in width. The green fluorescent footprints of mice were collected using the footprint light refraction technique. The experiment was conducted in a dark and quiet environment to minimize environmental interference with the test itself. Prior to the formal test, each mouse was placed at the entrance of the gait runway and guided to pass through it for acclimation. During the formal experiment, videos were recorded to capture the images of mice walking back and forth on the glass plate. After testing one mouse, the glass plate was wiped with 10% alcohol to prevent any impact on subsequent experiments with other mice. Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD. To assess statistical significance, the data was analyzed by t-test and one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni or Dunnett correction unless otherwise. The differences were considered significant at P < 0.05. Statistical analysis was done using GraphPad Prism.
