Development and cross-amplification of novel SSR markers for population genetic analysis of Kitti’s hog-nosed bat (Craseonycteris thonglongyai) in Thailand
Authors: Supajit Sraphet, Nattaya Srisawad, Nawarat Suksee, Piengtawan Tappiban, Surachit Waengsothorn, Kridsada Chaichoun, Ruangrat Buddhirongawatr, Sarin Suwanpakdee, Siriporn Tungsudjai, Poonyapat Sedwisai, Tatiyanuch Chamsai, Thekhawet Weluwanarak, Nareerat Sangkachai, Sukuman Rittem, Piyarat Parinyapong Chareonsap, Kanokporn Triwitayakorn, Duncan R. Smith
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Kitti’s hog-nosed bat or Craseonycteris thonglongyai is the only species in the family Craseonycteridae genus Craseonycteris and is considered as Endangered on the IUCN Red List of near-threatened vulnerable animals. This study aimed primarily to develop and characterize novel SSR markers specific to C. thonglongyai and secondarily to apply these markers for a preliminary assessment of genetic diversity within the species to develop a strategic conservation management plan. A total of 86 simple sequence repeats (SSR) primer pairs were developed for C. thonglongyai using an enriched-genomic DNA library with (GT)12/(CTG)9 and (AG)12/(CAG)9 probes. Of these, six loci showed polymorphisms within the tested C. thonglongyai samples. Genetic diversity estimates of 57 C. thonglongyai samples were based on 10 polymorphic SSR loci, including 6 newly developed loci from this study and 4 loci previously reported. In total, 48 alleles with an average of 4.8 alleles per locus were identified. The mean observed heterozygosity, expected heterozygosity, and polymorphic information content were 0.506, 0.486, and 0.494, respectively. Although no linkage disequilibrium was observed, three loci showed significant deviations from the Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium. Based on the analysis of 10 polymorphic SSR loci across 57 C. thonglongyai samples, the population in Thailand exhibited a moderate level of genetic diversity. The clustering observed in the phylogenetic tree and principal coordinate analysis did not correspond to the geographic locations of the caves. Moreover, cross-transferability of the polymorphic SSR loci from C. thonglongyai was successful in other bat species. Among the 10 polymorphic SSR loci tested across four bat families, 6 loci successfully amplified in at least one species of the Hipposideridae, Vespertilionidae, and Rhinolophidae, with polymorphic patterns observed in these amplified loci.

Introduction

Kitti’s hog-nosed bat (Craseonycteris thonglongyai), commonly known as the bumblebee bat, is the sole representative of the family Craseonycteridae and is regarded as one of the world’s smallest mammals by body length1. This insectivorous bat is geographically restricted to karst cave habitats in western Thailand (Sai Yok, Kanchanaburi Province) and southeastern Myanmar (Kayin and Mon states)2. Due to its extremely limited distribution, small population size, and ongoing habitat disturbance, C. thonglongyai is currently listed as Near Threatened on the IUCN Red List3.

Recent genetic studies have revealed marked differentiation between Thai and Myanmar populations, supported by microsatellite (SSR) and mitochondrial DNA data4. Distinct echolocation calls frequencies between populations suggest limited gene flow and potential early reproductive isolation. Given this species’ ecological and evolutionary uniqueness, understanding its genetic diversity and population structure is vital for the development of effective conservation strategies4.

Microsatellite markers, or simple sequence repeats (SSRs), are widely used in population genetics due to their codominant inheritance, high polymorphism, and reproducibility5,6. For C. thonglongyai, a set of 16 polymorphic SSR loci was developed and validated, showing moderate levels of genetic variability and population structure7. However, these markers remain underutilized, and further development of additional SSR loci is necessary to improve genetic resolution, particularly across fragmented colonies.

Beyond C. thonglongyai, studies in other bat species have demonstrated the effectiveness of SSRs in characterizing genetic diversity and population structure. For instance, SSRs developed in Miniopterus schreibersii were effective in distinguishing population units across a fragmented landscape and yielded results comparable to next-generation sequencing approaches8. In large-scale comparative studies, genome-wide identification of SSRs in species such as Pteropus vampyrus and Miniopterus natalensis revealed a high abundance and even genomic distribution of these markers, highlighting their broad utility in bat genomics9.

Cross-species amplification of SSR markers is also an efficient strategy for extending marker utility across related taxa. For example, SSR primers derived from Myotis lucifugus successfully amplified loci in 4 bat species, demonstrating the feasibility of cross-transferability in chiropteran studies10. These findings suggest that SSRs developed for C. thonglongyai may also be transferable to other bat families, supporting broader applications in comparative and conservation genetics.

Given the current gaps in SSR marker availability and limited genetic data for C. thonglongyai, this study aimed to isolate and characterize novel SSR markers for C. thonglongyai to enhance population genetic analysis of this endangered species and to evaluate the cross-amplification potential of these markers across four bat families. The resulting markers are expected to provide valuable genetic tools for conservation and to support broader studies on chiropteran biodiversity.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals and DNA extraction

Wildlife in Thailand is protected under the Wildlife Conservation and Protection Act B.E. 2562 (2019), and enforcement is primarily through the Department of National Parks, Wildlife and Plant Conservation. This study obtained appropriate regulatory approval and once the study was permitted, a study protocol was subsequently approved by the Mahidol University, Faculty of Veterinary Science – Animal Care and Use Committee (FVS – ACUC), Protocol No MUVS – 2009–05. The protocol was strictly adhered to, and no adverse incidents were recorded in the sample collection phase. C. thonglongyai was surveyed in caves located in the Sai Yok district of Kanchanaburi Province, Thailand (Lum-Yai Cave: 47P 491279 1592016; Manao-Pee Cave: 47P 493228 1587054; and Pha Cave: 47P 484216 1593100) from 2010 to 2015. In addition, other bat species were also observed, identified and surveyed. The bats were captured with harp traps set at the cave entrances. Wing tissue samples were collected and preserved in 70% alcohol, and the bats were subsequently released back into the wild. Total genomic DNA was extracted from the tissue samples using a genomic DNA extraction kit (tissue) (RBC Bioscience, Taipei, Taiwan), following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA samples were stored at − 20 °C until further analysis. DNA samples of all collected C. thonglongyai were used for evaluation of genetic diversity within the species, while other bat species were used for cross-amplification analysis.

Enriched-genomic DNA library construction

The enriched-genomic DNA library of C. thonglongyai was constructed following Sraphet and colleagues11, with some modifications. DNA was digested with restriction enzymes AluI (Takara, Japan), HaeIII (Takara), and AfaI (Takara). The restricted fragments were purified and ligated with double-stranded linkers (Linker 1: GTTTAGCCTTGTAGCAGAAGC and Linker 2: GCTTCTGCTACAAGGCTAAACAAAA). The ligated fragments were used as templates for PCR using Linker 1 as a primer. Amplified products were hybridized with (GT)12/(CTG)9 and (AG)12/(CAG)9 biotinylated oligonucleotide probes and captured with streptavidin-coated magnetic beads (Invitrogen Dynal AS, Norway). The enriched DNA fragments were PCR amplified using Linker 1. The amplified DNA fragments were run on 1% agarose, and DNA of 0.5–1.0 kb was cut and purified using Wizard® SV Gel and PCR Clean-Up System (Promega, Madison, WI). The purified fragments were cloned using the pGEM®-T Easy Vector System (Promega, Madison, WI). The ligation products were introduced into DH5α-competent Escherichia coli cells through transformation and cultured on Luria-Bertani agar plates, supplemented with ampicillin, X-gal (5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl-D-galactoside), and IPTG (Isopropyl β-D-thiogalactopyranoside). White colonies were amplified using SP6 and T7 primers to check the inserted SSR fragments. Positive clones with an insert SSR motif between 0.5 and 1.0 kb were sequenced by Macrogen Sequencing Service (Macrogen, Seoul, Korea).

SSR identification and primer design

Sequence data were processed to remove vector and linker sequences before being utilized to identify SSR motifs. SSR motifs were identified using the WebSat program12, followed by checking SSR redundancy using the CAP3 sequence assembly program13. To design SSR primers, non-redundant sequences flanking SSR motifs (≥ 12 bp in length) were selected using the WebSat program. The criteria for primer design were as follows: primer lengths between 18 and 25 bp, with an optimal length of 22 bp; GC content between 40% and 60%; annealing temperatures from 57 °C to 68 °C, with an optimal temperature of 55 °C; and expected product sizes ranging from 100 to 500 bp. To ensure primer specificity, potential secondary structures such as hairpin loops, primer-dimers, and self-priming were evaluated using the WebSat program prior to primer synthesis.

SSR analysis

All newly developed SSR primers, as well as 16 additional microsatellite primers previously reported by Puechmaille et al. (2009), were initially tested for amplification on five C. thonglongyai DNA samples to identify loci exhibiting polymorphism. Loci that showed polymorphic patterns in this preliminary screening were subsequently used to analyze all 57 C. thonglongyai samples for genetic diversity assessment. PCR reactions were performed in a 15 µL final volume containing 25 ng DNA, 0.06 µM of each primer, 0.2 mM of each dNTP, 1.5 mM MgCl₂, 1× PCR buffer, and 0.5 U of Taq DNA polymerase (Thermo Scientific, Foster, CA, USA). The PCR cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 2 min, followed by 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 45 s, and 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The PCR products were separated and visualized on 5% denaturing polyacrylamide gels using silver staining14. DNA banding patterns were analyzed and scored to assess genetic diversity.

Data analysis

Genetic diversity parameters, including the number of alleles (A), effective number of alleles (Ne), observed heterozygosity (Ho), expected heterozygosity (He), deviations from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE), polymorphic information content (PIC), and the estimated null allele frequency (FNULL), were calculated using Cervus v3.0.715. The inbreeding coefficient index (FIS), as defined by Weir and Cockerham (1984)14, and the linkage disequilibrium between all loci were determined using the web version of Genepop 4.215,16. The analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA) was performed in GenAlEx v6.517. The genetic differentiation coefficient (PhiPT) was also estimated at 999 permutation.

A phylogenetic tree using UPGMA (unweighted pair group method with arithmetic mean) based on the Euclidean genetic distance with 1,000 bootstraps was generated by the web-server DendroUPGMA (http://genomes.urv.cat/UPGMA)18 and drawn using MegaX software19. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was generated using Past 4.0320. Bayesian clustering was performed using STRUCTURE v2.3.421. The number of genetic clusters (K) was set from 1 to 5. For each K, five independent runs were conducted with a burn-in period of 10,000 iterations followed by 100,000 MCMC iterations. The optimal number of genetic clusters (K) was determined according to the ΔK method of Evanno and colleagues22 in StructureSelector23. Although phylogenetic and principal coordinate analyses are not essential for microsatellite marker characterization, they were performed to demonstrate the applicability of the developed SSR markers in assessing genetic diversity and population structure within C. thonglongyai.

Results

Sample collection

Fifty-seven individual samples of C. thonglongyai (Fig. 1), including 28 males and 29 females, were collected from Lum-Yai (42 samples), Manao-Pee (6 samples), and Pha caves (9 samples) in the Sai Yok district of Kanchanaburi province, Thailand. In addition, other bat species within the genus Hipposideros in the family Hipposideridae (Hipposideros armiger: N = 3: M = 2, F = 1; H. Pomona; N = 1: M = 1), genus Myotis in the family Vespertilionidae (Myotis siligorensis; N = 8: M = 3, F = 5), genus Rhinolophus in the family Rhinolophidae (Rhinolophus affinis: N = 1: M = 1, R. coelophyllus: N = 8: M = 7, F = 1; and R. malayanus: N = 3: M = 2, F = 1), and genus Taphozous in the family Emballonuridae (Taphozous melanopogon; N = 8: M = 3, F = 5) were also found in the study area and samples were taken and used for evaluating possible cross-amplification of SSRs.

Microsatellite characterization and primer design

DNA sequencing analysis of 323 positive clones yielded 253 (78.3%) clones that were successfully sequenced. Of these, 188 (74.3%) sequences contained SSR motifs, including 123 (65.4%) and 65 (34.6%) sequences of non-redundancy and redundancy, respectively. From the non-redundant sequences, two types of microsatellite motifs were observed: dinucleotide comprising 41 sequences of AG (44.6%) and 32 sequences of GT (34.8%), and trinucleotide comprising 11 sequences of CTG (12.0%) and 8 sequences of CAG (8.7%) (Table 1). Based on the flanking sequences of SSR regions, 68 SSR primer pairs (GenBank accession numbers: MK605520-MK605587) were designed and synthesized (Table 2).

Genetic diversity analysis

From 68 SSR primer pairs, 28 (41.18%) pairs successfully amplified the genomic DNA of C. thonglongyai. Of these, 6 (21.43%) and 22 (78.57%) SSR loci showed polymorphism and monomorphism, respectively. An evaluation of the 16 SSRs of C. thonglongyai developed by Puechmaille and colleagues7 revealed that four SSR loci exhibited polymorphic patterns within the C. thonglongyai samples. All 10 identified polymorphic SSR primers were analyzed with 57 samples of C. thonglongyai, yielding 48 alleles (Table 3). The number of alleles per locus ranged from 2 (Ct55, Ct61) to 13 (CTA7), with a mean number of 4.8 alleles per locus. The mean Ne across all loci in C. thonglongyai populations ranged from approximately 2.12 to 2.75, indicating a moderate level of genetic diversity. The Ho of these polymorphic loci ranged from 0.182 (CTA4) to 1.000 (CTA7), with an average of 0.506, and He ranged from 0.172 (CTA4) to 0.848 (CTA7), with an average of 0.486. A significant deviation from the HWE was observed in this study at loci Ct32, Ct52, and CTA7 at P ≤ 0.05. The PIC values for the polymorphic SSRs ranged between 0.165 (CTA4) and 0.820 (CTA7), with an average of 0.494. In addition, tests for linkage disequilibrium (P < 0.001) revealed no linkage disequilibrium among pairs of loci. In the C. thonglongyai population, FNULL varied from − 0.093 (CTA7) to 0.189 (Ct55), averaging at 0.042, and FIS varied from − 0.182 (CTA7) to 0.328 (Ct55), averaging at 0.087.

Phylogenetic tree, PCoA and population structure

The AMOVA based on 10 SSR loci of 57 C. thonglongyai individual samples from three caves (Lum-Yai, Manao-Pee, and Pha) revealed that 13% of the total genetic variation occurred among populations and 87% within populations (PhiPT = 0.128 (P ≤ 0.001); Nm = 1.706), as shown in Table 5. Pairwise PhiPT values showed significant genetic differentiation among all C. thonglongyai populations, with PhiPT values ranging from 0.000 to 0.194 (P ≤ 0.001) (Table 6).

The UPGMA tree of 57 individual C. thonglongyai revealed a mixing of samples from different caves (Fig. 2). The phylogenetic tree also revealed that the samples from the Lum-Yai cave formed a closely related cluster. Most bat samples from the Pha cave grouped together. From the same cave, only three bat samples had less similarity to the main group. The same clustering pattern was not observed in the bat samples from the Manao–Pee cave. The PCoA also showed no clear clustering of the C. thonglongyai samples based on the collection locations (Fig. 3). For the population structure analysis, the highest ΔK was detected at K = 2. However, the bar plot of individual C. thonglongyai at K = 2 showed no clear genetic differentiation among cave populations (Fig. 4).

Discussion

An SSR-enriched library was created using C. thonglongyai from Thailand, and genetic diversity was assessed with samples from 57 individual C. thonglongyai. The number of alleles per locus (mean 3.3, range 2–4 alleles) using new SSR loci (Ct) in the C. thonglongyai population was lower than Thai C. thonglongyai (mean 5.0, range 2–11 alleles) and Myanmar population (mean 3.1, range 1–4 alleles) from a previous study7. By integrating the SSR primers previously developed by Puechmaille and colleagues7 with newly identified SSR loci, a high average number of alleles per locus was achieved (mean 4.8, range 2–13 alleles). This study uncovered a moderate genetic diversity level in the C. thonglongyai population (mean He = 0.550). A similar moderate genetic diversity value was reported from C. thonglongyai in the Thai population with 28 samples (mean He = 0.567)7 and C. thonglongyai in the Myanmar population with 36 samples (mean He = 0.466). The genetic diversity in the Myanmar population was lower than that observed in the present study. Despite the C. thonglongyai population size in the current study being more than twice that used in the previous study, the He value did not markedly decrease when comparing Thai populations. Although an increase in sample size showed a decrease in He, this reduction was not significant once the sample size surpassed 25–30 samples24.

In this study, only 4 of 16 polymorphic SSR loci developed by Puechmaille and colleagues7 could be amplified and showed polymorphism within the Thai population. The low amplification success of several loci from the previous study may be attributed to mutations or mismatches at primer-binding sites among geographically distinct populations, suboptimal PCR conditions, or the presence of null alleles, which are common causes of amplification failure when markers are transferred across studies. In addition, a few newly developed SSR loci also revealed polymorphisms in our population.

This result suggests that the genetic diversity of the C. thonglongyai population is somewhat lower than that observed in the previously studied population. Similar moderate genetic diversity with mean He, about 0.5–0.6, was reported previously for other bat species such as Carollia castanea (mean He = 0.649)25, Corynorhinus townsendii (mean He = 0.593)26, Desmodus rotundus (mean He = 0.668)27, Leptonycteris yerbabuenae (mean He = 0.666)28, and Uroderma bilobatum (mean He = 0.588)29.

Most of the new SSR loci exhibited moderate levels of PIC (0.5 > PIC > 0.25), while the SSR loci from the study7 showed higher levels exceeding 0.5 in this population30. The absence of linkage disequilibrium suggests no genetic linkage between any pair of loci studied. Additionally, a significant deviation from the HWE was noted among the loci, indicating the possible presence of heterozygote deficiency or null alleles31,32. In addition, the average positive FIS value of 0.082, coupled with the mean Ho being less than He, further supports the presence of heterozygote deficiency in this population33,34. The heterozygote deficiency found in the C. thonglongyai population may have primarily resulted from inbreeding rather than null alleles, as the estimated null allele frequencies were low across all loci34,35. The phylogenetic tree and PCoA did not reveal clear clustering of individuals according to cave locations, suggesting a lack of strong population structure within the sampled population. Although FIS values were low, indicating limited inbreeding, continued monitoring is recommended to detect potential changes in population structure and genetic diversity over time. Although the present study revealed no clear clustering among cave populations based on the phylogenetic tree and PCoA, additional analyses such as STRUCTURE and AMOVA using a larger set of informative loci would further clarify the genetic structure of C. thonglongyai populations.

As a caveat we note that the fundamental framework for our analysis is based in the neutral theory of molecular evolution, which suggests that most genetic changes at the molecular level are the result of random genetic changes resulting in neutral or nearly neutral mutations36. As a foundation of this theory, it was assumed that every populations’ genome would continually accrue mutations at a universal rate of mutation and diversity, according to a molecular clock, and this could be used in phylogenic studies (as here). However other authors have disputed this model, and have proposed a “maximum genetic diversity theory”, partly based on the fact that some areas of the genome seem to retain mutations more stably than others37. Thus, application of a different theoretical framework could produce a different phylogenic result.

Given that the genus Craseonycteris (Chiroptera: Craseonycteridae) comprises solely C. thonglongyai, our new polymorphic SSR markers, along with those from Puechmaille and colleagues7, were assessed for cross amplification in other bat families. Cross amplification of polymorphic SSR loci from C. thonglongyai showed high success rates, with 40.0% in Hipposideridae, 30.0% in Rhinolophidae, and 10.0% in Vespertilionidae. These rates exceed previous reports of cross-family amplification in bats, which ranged from 7.1% to 35.7%38–42. The success rate of cross-amplification tends to decrease with increasing taxonomic distance; thus, amplification success is generally higher between species within the same genus, moderate between genera within the same family, and lower when tested across different families39. Similarly, previous studies have reported lower cross-amplification success when SSR markers developed for one bat family were tested in species belonging to other, more distantly related families40,41. Furthermore, a phylogenetic analysis based on cytochrome b showed that Craseonycteridae is more closely related to Hipposideridae than to Rhinolophidae43.

The population of C. thonglongyai, is currently classified as a near-threatened species, and continues to decline44 due to human activities, such as cave exploration, bat guano harvesting, hunting, and religious visits2,44. The observed lower genetic diversity probably resulted from the reduced C. thonglongyai population size. Although the C. thonglongyai population exhibited low FIS values, indicating limited inbreeding, this result may be partly influenced by the small population size and the sampling of multiple individuals from the same cave, which could reduce the apparent level of heterozygosity within the dataset. The observed moderate level of genetic diversity suggests that, despite currently low inbreeding, the restricted population size and limited gene flow among caves could increase inbreeding risk if the population continues to decline. Maintaining genetic diversity is therefore essential to prevent further loss of adaptive potential and reduce extinction risk [44,45]. This reduction of genetic diversity was observed in our study and the previous one7 during 6 years. Therefore, ongoing assessment of C. thonglongyai’s genetic diversity is crucial for the conservation and maintenance of its population in Thailand.

The polymorphic SSR markers identified in this study are valuable for studying the population of C. thonglongyai, and the insights into genetic diversity they provide are essential for formulating conservation strategies for this species in Thailand. In addition, these SSR markers provide potential opportunities to study genetic diversity, phylogenetic relationships, and population structure for other bat species in the family Vespertilionidae, Hipposideridae, and Rhinolophidae.

Our SSR markers developed for C. thonglongyai clearly indicated a moderate genetic diversity of this species. The phylogenetic tree and PCoA did not reveal clear clustering of individuals according to cave locations, suggesting a lack of strong population structure within the sampled population. Although FIS values were low, indicating limited inbreeding, continued monitoring is recommended to detect potential changes in population structure and genetic diversity over time. These newly developed SSR markers were also applied in genetic analysis in other rare bat species. Our findings offer valuable insights into the genetic diversity of C. thonglongyai, aiding in the development of effective conservation strategies for this near-threatened species in Thailand.
