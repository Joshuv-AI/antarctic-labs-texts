Impact of caffeine consumption on headache severity, sleep quality, and psychiatric symptoms in patients with idiopathic intracranial hypertension: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Mohamed Abdelghaffar, Mona Hussein, Rehab Magdy, Anas Elgenidi, Ahmed Dahshan, Nourhan Abdelmohsen Taha, Nahla Merghany, Ahmed Essmat, Amr Hassan, Ramez Reda Moustafa, May M Fayez, Anter Mothenna Abdullah Nasser, Osama Yacoub
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe relationship between caffeine consumption and idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH) remains an area of clinical interest, but with limited research. This work aimed to clarify the impact of caffeine consumption on headache severity, quality of life, psychiatric symptoms, and sleep quality in patients with IIH.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 179 patients diagnosed as having IIH. At the baseline visit, the patients underwent a detailed clinical assessment. They were subsequently asked to complete a set of self-administered questionnaires after one month: the Caffeine Food Frequency questionnaire (C-FFQ), Headache Impact Test-6 (HIT-6), Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-12 (DASS-12), Insomnia Severity Index (ISI), and the Short-Form Health Survey (SF-12). Patients were categorized as heavy caffeine consumers (≥ 400 mg/day), light consumers (< 400 mg/day), or non-consumers.ResultsHeavy caffeine consumers showed significantly higher rates of severe papilledema, elevated CSF pressure, and greater daily dose of acetazolamide compared to light and non-consumers (P < 0.001). Headache burden was significantly greater among heavy consumers compared to light and non-consumers, with higher median monthly headache days (MHDs) [20 vs. 15 vs. 10], visual analogue scale (VAS) pain scores [9 vs. 7 vs. 7], and HIT-6 scores [66 vs. 63 vs. 57] (all P < 0.01). Moreover, heavy consumers reported significantly lower quality of life on SF-12 physical and mental domains, and higher DASS-12 and ISI scores compared to light and non-consumers (P < 0.001).ConclusionIn IIH patients, heavy caffeine consumption was associated with worse headache burden, impaired quality of life, and increased psychiatric and sleep disturbances.Keywords: Idiopathic intracranial hypertension, Caffeine, Quality of life, Psychiatric symptoms, Insomnia

Introduction

Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH) is a neurological disorder characterized by elevated intracranial pressure (ICP) without an identifiable cause. It predominantly affects young obese women in the childbearing period, and its incidence has increased alongside the global obesity epidemic [1]. Patients typically present with headache, bilateral papilledema, double vision, transient visual disturbances (TVOs), tinnitus, and dizziness with a risk of permanent vision loss if left untreated [1]. Nearly half of IIH patients (46%) reported moderate to severe sleep disturbances. Psychological distress is another important aspect of IIH, with high rates of anxiety and depression contributing to reduced quality of life [2].

Caffeine is a widely consumed neurostimulant that may influence several factors relevant to IIH, including headache severity, mood, and sleep quality [3]. However, its role in IIH is not well characterized. Pharmacologically, caffeine is a non-selective adenosine receptor antagonist and cerebral vasoconstrictor [4]. It is known to have analgesic and mood-elevating properties. Consumed in low-to-moderate doses (approximately 50–200 mg, equivalent to 1–2 cups of coffee), caffeine can improve concentration, increase alertness, and reduce fatigue [5]. In headache treatment, adding ~ 100–130 mg of caffeine to standard analgesics has been shown to boost pain relief by about 40% [6]. Clinically, some patients have anecdotally reported temporary relief of headache symptoms after caffeine intake, possibly due to caffeine’s transient vasoconstrictive and ICP-lowering effects observed in experimental models [7].

Although low-to-moderate caffeine intake may confer certain benefits, excessive consumption (typically > 400 mg/day) is frequently associated with adverse effects, including insomnia, palpitations, and heightened anxiety [8]. With sustained use, physiological dependence can develop, and abrupt withdrawal often precipitates rebound headaches, thereby worsening the overall headache burden in patients with chronic disorders such as IIH [9, 10]. In addition, the overuse of caffeine-containing analgesics or energy drinks has been linked to medication overuse headache and the progression to chronic daily headache [11]. Importantly, caffeine’s impact on sleep cannot be ignored, especially considering the high prevalence of sleep disturbances among patients with IIH [12, 13].

These nuanced effects likely explain why IIH management guidelines recommend limiting caffeine consumption as part of a comprehensive lifestyle strategy for IIH patients. By minimizing avoidable triggers (like excessive caffeine use or poor sleep hygiene), patients may gain better control over their headaches and overall well-being [14].

Despite these findings, data on the relationship between caffeine consumption, symptom severity, and quality of life in IIH remain a thorny area, filled with questions that need to be answered. Therefore, this study was designed to investigate the impact of caffeine consumption on headache severity, quality of life, psychiatric symptoms, and sleep quality in IIH patients.

Methods

This is a cross-sectional study conducted at the Neurology Clinics of three university hospitals in Egypt: Cairo, Beni-Suef, and Fayoum University Hospitals. It commenced on May 1, 2025, and continued until August 10, 2025, when the required sample size was achieved.

Patients diagnosed with IIH, according to the modified Dandy Criteria [15], were recruited in a convenience sample. All medically treated patients with a disease duration of at least 6 months are eligible. Exclusion criteria included patients with pre-existing primary headache disorders, patients with severe visual impairment that hinders reading the questions, those having a medical or psychiatric disorder known to impact sleep or the quality of life, or those who would follow a specific diet regimen or change the type or dose of medications during the study period. Patients undergoing active weight-loss interventions were excluded to minimize confounding, as recent weight reduction can independently influence intracranial pressure, headache severity, and quality-of-life measures. Patients who used to take simple analgesics ≥ 15 days per month for more than 3 months were also excluded from the study. Because it is hard to determine if increased medication use caused the worsening of headache (MOH) or if the headache attributed to IIH just worsened independently.

Data collection tools

In the baseline visit, demographic and BMI information were obtained for all patients. Then, they are given a brochure of self-rating questionnaires to be completed over the upcoming month and resubmitted electronically afterwards. The brochure consists of the following:

A caffeine food frequency questionnaire (C-FFQ) [16].It is a self-report of daily caffeine-containing beverages, including energy drinks, soda, coffee, tea, and chocolate, over a one-month period. Participants were requested to select the types of caffeine-containing beverages they consumed daily from a list of currently available products. Then, they asked how often they consumed each brand daily and to select the serving size of each type, such as standard cups, bottles, and containers labelled with the volume in (ml) [16, 17].Accordingly, participants are categorized as either heavy caffeine consumers, defined as those consuming a minimum of 400 mg of caffeine per day, light caffeine consumers, defined as those consuming less than 400 mg of caffeine per day, or non-caffeine consumers [18].

When the answers are submitted at the end of the month, an ophthalmological assessment is conducted to reveal the current grade of papilledema according to Frisén Scale [27]. Lumbar puncture was also performed to measure the CSF opening pressure while the patient was lying in a lateral decubitus position with extended legs using a manometer after at least 1 min relaxation.

Ethics statement

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants included in the study. Data were confidential and anonymous. Ethical approval was obtained from the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Fayoum University.

Sampling

The sample size was calculated using Epi info, version 3.5.1, 2008. Based on a prevalence rate of 3.5/100,000 for IIH [22], a total sample size of at least 90 patients was required to achieve a 99% confidence level.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Version 25. Data were tested for normality of distribution by using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Quantitative data, including age, BMI, disease duration, CSF pressure, daily dose of acetazolamide, MHDs, VAS, HIT-6, SF-12, DASS-12, and ISI, were expressed as median (25th-75th percentile). Categorical data such as sex, grade of papilledema, and IIH symptoms were expressed as frequency & percentage. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare the three groups on quantitative variables, whereas the Chi-squared test was used to compare them on categorical variables. The P-value was adjusted for multiple testing using Bonferroni correction formula.

Results

Demographics and clinical characteristics of IIH patients

This cross-sectional study included 179 patients diagnosed with IIH. Their median age was 33 (27–38) years, with a clear female predominance (91.6%). The median BMI was 32.28 (28.25–35.43) kg/m², and the median disease duration was 1.5 years (0.67–3 years). Papilledema grading revealed that grade II was the most prevalent grade in both the right and left eyes (40.8% and 39.7%, respectively). Median CSF pressure was 305 (270–400) mmH₂O. Symptomatically, blurring of vision was the most frequently reported complaint (90.5%), followed by dizziness (62.6%), tinnitus (58.7%), ocular pain (52%), TVOs (51.4%), and diplopia (45.8%).

Regarding headache frequency and severity, the median number of MHDs was 15 (10–25) days, the median VAS score was 7 (6–9), and the median HIT-6 total score was 64 (57–68). The median daily dose of acetazolamide was 1000 (750–1500) mg (Table 1).

Demographics and clinical characteristics in relation to caffeine consumption

When stratifying patients based on caffeine consumption, no significant differences were observed in age, sex distribution, BMI, disease duration, or IIH symptoms across the three groups (non-, light-, and heavy-caffeine consumers). However, significant differences were noted in papilledema grading, CSF pressure, and daily acetazolamide dose.

Heavy caffeine consumers demonstrated a significantly higher frequency of grade IV papilledema in Rt eye than light and non-consumers (30.8% vs. none vs. 6.5%, P-value < 0.001). The median CSF pressure in heavy caffeine consumers was significantly higher compared to light and non-consumers [400 (350–450) vs. 300 (260–400) vs. 280 (260-302.5), (P-value < 0.001)]. Furthermore, the median daily acetazolamide dose was significantly higher among heavy caffeine consumers compared to light and non-consumers [2000 (1250–2250) vs. 1000 (750–1250) vs. 1000 (687.5–1500), (P-value < 0.001)] (Table 2).

Impact of caffeine consumption on quality of life, psychiatric symptoms, and sleep

Heavy caffeine consumers had significantly lower SF-12 scores, both in physical and mental domains [29.96 (24.55–34.93), 32.12 (27.04–36.14) respectively] compared to non-consumers [45.99 (36.91–55.56), 45.62 (35.16–55.39) respectively] (P-value < 0.001). Similarly, the total SF-12 score was markedly reduced in heavy caffeine consumers compared to light and non-consumers [62.24 (56.92–67.86) vs. 69.61 (60.37–77.01) vs. 96.99 (74.9-101.4), P-value < 0.001].

Regarding psychiatric symptoms, the DASS-12 subscores for depression, anxiety, and stress were significantly higher among heavy caffeine consumers [7 (5–8), 7 (6–9), 8 (7–10) respectively] compared to light [4 (3–8), 5 (3–10), 6 (4–10) respectively] and non-consumers [4 (2.75–5.25), 3 (2–6), 4 (2–7) respectively] (P-value < 0.001). Likewise, the DASS-12 total score was significantly higher among heavy caffeine consumers compared to light and non-consumers [21 (18–26) vs. 17.5 (10.75–25.5) vs. 10.5 (7-16.5), (P-value < 0.001)].

As for insomnia, the total ISI score was most pronounced in heavy caffeine consumers compared with light and non-consumers [21 (17–25) vs. 15.5 (11–21) vs. 9 (5-14.25), (P-value < 0.001)] (Table 4).

Factors associated with elevated CSF pressure in patients with IIH

Multivariate regression analysis was done to identify factors associated with elevated CSF pressure. Selection of independent variables was based on their clinical relevance. Therefore, the following variables were included in the model: age, sex, BMI, disease duration, and heavy caffeine consumption. Caffeine consumption was entered as a categorical variable with heavy consumption considered the independent predictor based on its significant univariate association with CSF pressure. Multicollinearity was evaluated using tolerance and VIF values. All tolerance values were above 0.2 and all VIF values were below 5, indicating that the independent variables were not highly correlated.

Both BMI and high caffeine consumption were significantly associated with elevated CSF pressure (P-value = 0.005, < 0.001 respectively), whereas age, female sex, disease duration were identified as negative predictors of CSF pressure (P-value = 0.038, 0.003, 0.024 respectively) (Table 5).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study demonstrated that heavy caffeine intake was associated with a higher frequency of grade IV papilledema and elevated CSF pressure. In addition, heavy caffeine consumption was linked to a greater daily acetazolamide requirement, higher median MHDs, elevated VAS and HIT-6 scores, as well as more pronounced effects on quality of life, psychiatric symptoms, and sleep disturbance.

Most participants were middle-aged females with elevated BMI, aligning with published literature indicating that the majority of idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH) cases occur in women of childbearing age, with a female-to-male ratio ranging from 4:1 to 15:1. However it is not exclusive to females; it can occur in men and children, albeit at lower rates [28]. Obesity is also a well-established risk factor, affecting between 71% and 94% of patients with IIH [29]. In the present study, multivariate regression analysis demonstrated a significant association between higher BMI and increased CSF pressure.

Caffeine is thought to follow an “effect inversion” theory, whereby long-term use is associated with increased ICP and ventriculomegaly, whereas acute intake has been shown to reduce ICP transiently [30, 31]. The upregulation of A1 adenosine receptors in the choroid plexus with chronic intake can explain increased ICP. Additionally, in animal studies, the development of ventriculomegaly and high Na+, K+-ATPase expression levels were observed with an A1 adenosine receptor agonist and chronic caffeine intake. Chronic consumption seems to cause the body to adjust by boosting the expression of A1 and A2A adenosine receptors in brain tissue and the choroid plexus. This adaptive response increases CSF secretion and Na+/K+-ATPase activity, which may result in long-term increases in ICP [31]. These mechanisms may underlie the observed significant association between elevated CSF pressure and excessive caffeine intake in our study.

Our study showed that patients with chronic caffeine consumption have a higher rate of acetazolamide use. Actually, no direct causal relationship between caffeine and acetazolamide intake was reported in the literature. However, theoretical considerations support the idea that it is a bidirectional interaction rather than a unidirectional causal link. In other words, patients with headache tend to consume more caffeine due to its short-acting analgesic effect; however, chronic consumption may lead to an increase in ICP, thus higher acetazolamide doses are required to achieve adequate pressure control [30, 31].

The acute effects of caffeine may be attributed to its anti-inflammatory properties. As a non-selective antagonist of adenosine receptors (A1, A2A, and A2B) within the central nervous system, caffeine can attenuate the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and suppress microglial activation. Neuroinflammation, in turn, is increasingly recognized as a key factor contributing to altered CSF dynamics and the dysregulation of intracranial pressure [32].

Overall, chronic heavy intake of caffeine is associated with headaches that could be explained through several mechanisms, including the promotion of a pro-nociceptive state of cortical hyperexcitability. Caffeine functions as a competitive inhibitor of adenosine, thereby altering neuronal activity and disturbing blood flow [33].

The occurrence of severe headaches appears to be dose-dependent, with higher caffeine intake associated with an increased incidence of severe headaches. However, this relationship follows an S-shaped curve. In a study by Liao et al., a threshold of 97.5 mg/day was identified, below which each additional 1 mg/day of caffeine intake increased the risk of severe headaches by 0.38%. Notably, this elevated risk was confined to intake levels below 97.5 milligrams per day [34].

Another fundamental issue is that this cross-sectional study can establish only an association between excessive caffeine consumption and increased ICP, but cannot determine the direction of that relationship. It is implausible to definitively determine whether excessive caffeine consumption leads to increased ICP, exacerbation of papilledema, and increased acetazolamide dosage, or whether patients with severe ICP consume more caffeine to manage headaches, fatigue, and impaired alertness. The latter reverse causality is highly credible, given caffeine’s short-term analgesic and psychostimulant effects. Future longitudinal studies are essential to resolve this issue.

Caffeine has well-established psychological benefits, including increased alertness, enhanced cognitive function, and improved mood; however, heavy intake is associated with insomnia, anxiety, agitation, and the exacerbation of psychiatric symptoms, thus leading to a poorer quality of life, which is consistent with our findings [35].

Caffeine was reported to increase the secretion of neurotransmitters like norepinephrine, serotonin, and dopamine, which exacerbate anxiety symptoms and anxiety-like behaviors [36]. Caffeine also alters corticosterone levels by acting on the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and increases the risk of anxiety. This neuroendocrine release may also affect fluid and electrolyte balance, potentially altering CSF dynamics and ICP [37, 38].

It is also known that caffeine may impair sleep quality through its antagonistic action on adenosine receptors, particularly the A1 and A2A subtypes, which play key roles in promoting sleep and regulating arousal. Chronic sleep disruption, in turn, may have important implications on intracranial dynamics. glymphatic clearance, and cerebral autoregulation. These changes may influence CSF pressure and cerebral blood flow, potentially exacerbating IIH symptoms [39, 40].

Notably, the genetic variability and polymorphisms in genes related to adenosine receptor expression and caffeine metabolism have been associated with increased caffeine-induced anxiety, particularly among females, likely due to both hormonal modulation and slower caffeine clearance [41, 42].

In the present study, age, female sex, and disease duration were identified as negative predictors of CSF opening pressure in patients with IIH. These findings are biologically plausible and consistent with established physiological mechanisms. Similar results were reported in a multicenter study of IIH patients diagnosed after age 50, in which older individuals exhibited modestly lower CSF pressure than younger patients. Age-related declines in CSF production and cerebral blood flow, along with increased intracranial compliance secondary to brain atrophy, may collectively contribute to this observation [43].

Regarding female sex, studies examining hormonal profiles in IIH have found that sex hormones, particularly androgen excess, may influence CSF dynamics by upregulating choroid plexus Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activity, thereby enhancing CSF secretion [44].

As for disease duration, prolonged illness may reflect the cumulative effects of medical therapy especially carbonic anhydrase inhibitors and weight reduction, along with adaptive physiological mechanisms that enhance CSF absorption or diminish secretion over time. Consequently, longer disease duration (including treatment exposure) may be associated with lower, rather than persistently elevated, CSF pressures [45].

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the association between caffeine intake and headache severity, sleep quality, and depressive symptoms in patients with IIH. Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size within each caffeine consumption subgroup may limit the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, The cross-sectional design precludes establishing a causal relationship between caffeine intake and the severity of IIH symptoms. Some limitations related to using the C-FFQ are worth noting. Although direct serum analysis of caffeine and its metabolites appears more sensitive than verbal recall using the C-FFQ, the former provides a snapshot in time, which may not accurately reflect overall intake, especially when dosing schedules vary. That’s why using a C-FFQ is a more suitable tool for tracking caffeine intake over a one-month period. Furthermore, the validity and reliability of the C-FFQ have been demonstrated by Watson et al. [16]. The memory bias, a major concern when using the C-FFQ, was mitigated by asking participants to complete the questionnaire prospectively over the next month and resubmit it electronically afterwards. On the other hand, the categorization of caffeine intake relied on one-month recall without accounting for longer-term consumption patterns. Patients may have recently changed their caffeine intake in response to headaches. Finaly, potential confounders such as detailed sleep habits, socioeconomic status, shift work, smoking, physical activity were not were not captured in our data collection. Therefore, Future longitudinal studies should incorporate a broader range of lifestyle and occupational variables to more accurately assess the isolated effect of caffeine consumption.

Conclusion

In patients with IIH, heavy caffeine consumption was associated with more severe headache burden, higher intracranial pressure, and greater papilledema, alongside poorer quality of life, increased psychiatric symptoms, and greater sleep disturbance. Given these observed associations, counseling IIH patients on moderating caffeine intake may represent a simple, feasible component of holistic management. However, prospective trials are warranted before formal recommendations can be established.
