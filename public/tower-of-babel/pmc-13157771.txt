Applying intersectionality theory to health outcomes and healthcare utilisation of ethnic minority populations: a systematic review protocol
Authors: Emma Victoria Marianne Bilney, Emily Tsui Yee Tse, Mazariegos Melanie Laam Chan, Qiman Shi, Junjie Ma, Jane E Parry, David Ka Ki Wong, Luis Gabriel Bernal Pulido, William Chi Wai Wong, Linda Chan, Peter Tanuseputro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIntersectionality, as applied to health, provides a framework for understanding how overlapping social identities, such as ethnicity, gender and socioeconomic status, shape differential health outcomes and healthcare experiences. Individuals who occupy multiple marginalised identities often experience compounded disadvantages. Ethnic minority (EM) populations, defined here as social groups who are numerically smaller and/or socially marginalised within a given national or regional context based on ethnicity, race, culture, language, ancestry, or related heritage (often overlapping with racialised identities), frequently experience systemic exclusion, racism and structural barriers that also contribute to persistent disparities in morbidity, mortality and healthcare access. However, much health research relies on single-axis analyses, which can obscure within-group variation and may inadvertently reinforce inequities. Despite growing recognition of intersectionality, its empirical application to EM health remains limited. This systematic review aims to synthesise evidence on how intersectionality theory has been conceptualised and applied to understand health outcomes and healthcare utilisation among EM populations globally.Methods and analysisThis systematic review will follow Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and the PRISMA-Equity Extension. Comprehensive searches of peer-reviewed databases and grey literature, including MEDLINE, EMBASE, CINAHL Plus, Scopus, Web of Science and ProQuest will identify quantitative, qualitative and mixed-method empirical studies published from 2000 to 2026. Eligible studies must implement intersectionality theory to EM health or healthcare utilisation outcomes. Two independent reviewers will screen titles, abstracts and full-text articles. Data extraction will also be conducted independently and discrepancies will be discussed with input from a third reviewer. Guided by the PROGRESS-Plus equity framework, findings will be synthesised thematically, implementing a mixed-method convergent integrated synthesis design.Ethics and disseminationThis review only analyses previously published data, thus it does not require ethical approval. Findings will be presented at relevant academic or professional conferences and submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed journal.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251248887.

Introduction

Background

Intersectionality, a theoretical framework originally rooted in feminist scholarship, posits that individuals experience overlapping systems of advantage and disadvantage shaped by the interaction of multiple social identities, such as ethnicity, gender, income, sexuality, religion and age.1 2 In healthcare, intersectionality enables a nuanced understanding of how these interacting identities influence health outcomes and healthcare utilisation.3 Healthcare utilisation is a pivotal measure of how the health needs of individuals and populations are addressed by health services. It encompasses access (availability, acceptability, affordability, absence of barriers, service uptake); quality of care (effectiveness, safety, appropriateness of care); and patient experience (communication, satisfaction, patient–physician relationship).47

Evidence shows that people occupying multiple marginalised statuses often experience poorer health outcomes and reduced healthcare access compared with those facing a single axis of inequity (eg, only gender or ethnicity).8 9 Traditional approaches that treat social categories as homogeneous risk obscuring within-group differences and may lead to inequitable policy interventions that reinforce health inequities rather than reduce them.10 Recognising this, the 2020 NIH editorial has called for more robust quantitative and qualitative intersectional research to better capture the compounded effects of multiple axes of marginalisation on health.11

Ethnic minority (EM) populations, defined in this review as social groups who are identified or self-identify as belonging to a distinct ethnic, racial, cultural, linguistic or ancestral heritage that is numerically smaller and/or socially marginalised within their national or regional context, are disproportionately exposed to social exclusion, discrimination and systemic racism within and beyond healthcare systems.12 13 These structural determinants shape differential exposure to disease risks and stressors as well as influence access to, and experiences within, healthcare settings.1417 Understanding how multiple, intersecting social identities operate within EM populations is essential to designing equitable health interventions and policies that address the complex health experiences of marginalised communities.

Rationale and knowledge gaps

Despite growing recognition, the empirical uptake of intersectionality in health research remains inconsistent, limiting its potential to inform equity-focused interventions. Current evidence reveals several gaps:

Limited empirical integration: Few studies employ intersectionality as an analytic, methodological or theoretical framework.

Homogenisation of populations: EM groups are frequently treated as homogeneous categories, masking critical within-group differences.

Narrow focus of intersectional lenses: Many studies focus narrowly on gender and race/ethnicity intersections, overlooking other critical axes such as migration status, employment type, religion, and legal status that can influence access to care.

This systematic review addresses these gaps by synthesising how intersectionality has been applied in EM health research, identifying key intersectional axes shaping health outcomes and care utilisation.

Methods and analysis

The review protocol, following Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA) standards,18 has been registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) prior to data collection (PROSPERO Registration: CRD420251248887). This systematic review will follow the PRISMA19 guidelines and the PRISMA-Equity Extension, given the equity-focused nature of this review.19 20 Any important amendments to the protocol (eg, changes to eligibility criteria, outcomes, search strategy, or analysis plan) will be formally registered as amendments on PROSPERO, including the date, rationale, exact change description, and the version number/date of the updated protocol; minor editorial changes will be tracked separately and included in the final project.

Information sources

A comprehensive, multidatabase search will be conducted across: MEDLINE (via PubMed), EMBASE (via Ovid), CINAHL Plus (via EBSCOhost), Scopus, Web of Science (via Clarivate) and ProQuest (including PsycINFO and ProQuest dissertations and theses). We will conduct further searches of the grey literature through Google Scholar. We will also perform forward and backward citation tracking of identified relevant articles. Due to this review’s focus on the application of intersectionality theory to empirical data, articles with no empirical data (eg, commentaries) will be excluded. We will attempt to contact the authors of relevant studies where additional data may be available. Searches will be carried out from 1 January 2000 to date of search, restricted to English language due to limited translation resources.

Search strategy

Search terms will reflect four main concepts: (1) intersectionality, (2) ethnic minorities, (3) health outcomes and (4) healthcare utilisation. Concepts (3) and (4) will be combined in the search strategy as to be inclusive of all studies looking at health outcomes and/or healthcare utilisation. Search strings will combine controlled vocabulary (eg, MeSH terms) and free-text keywords using Boolean operators, truncation, and proximity operators. Controlled terms will be adapted for each database.

Study records

Data management

Search results will be imported into Covidence for screening purposes. Covidence will document the selection process and the total number of references identified: by each database; after removal of duplicates; via grey literature searches; and selected at each stage of the screening process with reasons for exclusion. The final PRISMA flow diagram will summarise the selection process.

Study selection

Two authors (EVMB and ETYT) will independently screen titles and abstracts for possible inclusion. If identified by at least one author, the article will be included for full-text screening, the second stage of study selection. Two authors (EVMB and ETYT) will independently review full-text versions of articles selected in the screening stage to confirm their eligibility for inclusion. Disagreements will be resolved by consensus or when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer (MMLC).

Data collection

Two reviewers (EVMB and ETYT) will extract the data independently. Data will be entered into a standardised data extraction form, which will be piloted prior to commencing data extraction. After completion of independent data extraction, the two authors will review both sets of extracted data together to check for errors and disagreements. Any disagreements will be resolved by consensus or, if necessary, with the help of an additional author (MMLC). Finalised data will be collated into an Excel spreadsheet. For studies with missing data contact will be made with original authors requesting the raw data if available.

The PROGRESS-Plus framework21 22 will guide data extraction and synthesis. This framework captures key dimensions of structural and social inequality that may influence health outcomes and healthcare access, facilitating consideration of equity-relevant factors across included studies.

The PROGRESS-Plus framework will be applied in a manner that explicitly aligns with intersectionality theory’s emphasis on mutually constitutive, interlocking systems of power and oppression rather than treating categories as independent or merely additive. Extraction and coding will note how studies operationalise multiple PROGRESS-Plus dimensions in interaction (eg, examining joint effects, emergent experiences or compounded disadvantages across categories like ethnicity×gender socioeconomic status, rather than isolated effects). We will also extract how primary studies handle intersections: whether through multi-axis stratification, interaction or effect modification terms, joint consideration of categories, or qualitative exploration linking categories.

To distinguish the operationalisation of intersectionality theory, included studies will be coded according to whether intersectionality is used as (1) a theoretical paradigm guiding its conceptual framing or interpretation of findings (eg, cites intersectionality framework to explain how social identities shape health outcomes); (2) an analytical framework informing study design, data collection, sampling or thematic organisation, but is not necessarily analysed statistically (eg, sampling includes intersecting identities and framework is used descriptively) and/or (3) an analytic method using multiaxis modelling, interaction terms, or intersectional qualitative coding (eg, modelling of intersecting predictors race×gender). Studies may be coded in more than one category if they apply intersectionality across multiple dimensions. This categorisation will enable comparison of conceptual and methodological depth across the included studies.

During synthesis, PROGRESS-Plus categories will serve as a structuring tool to map and compare intersectional axes examined across studies, while reflexive analysis will highlight whether (and how) studies avoid additive assumptions. This will enable identification of patterns of compounded disadvantage and critique of purely additive or single-axis analyses in the literature.

Data synthesis

Given the review’s mixed-method scope (qualitative, quantitative and mixed-methods studies), a convergent integrated mixed-methods synthesis design will be employed.23 Both quantitative and qualitative findings will be analysed in parallel and subsequently integrated using joint display matrices that map statistical associations onto corresponding qualitative explanations to identify areas of convergence, complementarity and dissonance. Quantitative outcomes may be ‘qualitised’ (eg, summarised in the direction and/or magnitude of the effect) to facilitate comparison with qualitative results.23 The synthesis will give equal weight (balanced priority) to quantitative and qualitative evidence, reflecting the convergent integrated approach where neither strand is inherently dominant. This balanced weighting allows quantitative data to provide breadth and patterns of disparity across populations, while qualitative data offer depth, context and explanatory insights into mechanisms of intersecting disadvantage. Due to the expected heterogeneity, synthesis will be narrative and thematic.

Operationalisation of intersectionality will differ across studies; therefore methodological approaches will be compared during synthesis. This comparison will occur at multiple stages: (1) during data extraction, the data item ‘Intersectionality Operationalisation’ will categorise each study’s operationalisation of intersectionality; (2) within separate quantitative and qualitative synthesis (detailed below) and (3) during integration, where findings will be contrasted using different operationalisations of intersectionality, highlighting how methodological choices may have influenced reported health outcomes and healthcare utilisation.

Risk of bias in individual studies

The methodological quality and risk of bias of included studies will be assessed using design-appropriate tools. Quality assessment will guide data synthesis but not exclusion. Quantitative studies (eg, cohort, cross-sectional, case–control or quasi-experimental designs) will be appraised using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklists relevant to each design type.25 Qualitative studies will also be appraised using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research.25 For mixed-methods studies, quantitative and qualitative components will be individually appraised using the appropriate JBI critical appraisal checklist, and overall quality and integration of methodological components will be assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT).26

Two reviewers (EVMB and ETYT) will independently appraise the quality and risk of bias of each included study using the appropriate JBI or MMAT tool. Any disagreements will be resolved through discussion or adjudication by a third reviewer (MMLC). The completed appraisal forms will be recorded in Covidence.

Meta-biases

Risk of bias in data selection

During study selection, search biases or selective inclusion based on study outcomes may occur. To mitigate these risks, a comprehensive and systematic search strategy will be implemented across multiple databases, supplemented by grey literature searches and reference list screening, to reduce search biases, such as publication bias and retrieval bias. Two reviewers (EVMB and ETYT) will independently screen all records in Covidence, using predefined inclusion criteria, with disagreements resolved through discussion or input from a third reviewer (MMLC). Selection decisions and exclusion reasons will be documented and reported in a PRISMA flow diagram.19 These practices will reduce selective inclusion based on study outcomes. Finally, this review will include grey literature to capture evidence from contexts or populations that are likely underrepresented in peer-reviewed research. This will reduce the impact of equity-related publication biases in this review.

Intersectionality theory selection bias

A key methodological choice in this review is the requirement for explicit engagement with intersectionality theory in eligible studies (eg, naming the theory, referencing core principles such as interlocking oppressions, or framing the work through an intersectional lens). This criterion may introduce theoretical selection bias by excluding empirically relevant studies that examine multiple intersecting social identities, demonstrate compounded or unique disadvantages, or conduct multi-axis analyses without using the explicit label ‘intersectionality’ or directly invoking the theory. Such exclusion could arise in contexts where intersectionality is applied implicitly or where terminology varies due to disciplinary or regional differences in adoption of the framework. This may disproportionately affect qualitative work exploring overlapping discriminations narratively without explicit theorisation.

Restricting to explicit applications potentially under-represents the full scope of multi-axis health inequities research among EM populations. This could lead to a narrow portrayal of how intersecting disadvantages operate in practice, reduced generalisability to broader intersectional research, and possible under-identification of gaps in equity-focused health research. Conversely, a more inclusive approach risks diluting the review’s focus on intersectional theory application, introducing heterogeneity in conceptual depth. To mitigate this potential bias within resource constraints, grey literature and reference chaining will help capture less conventional or non-peer-reviewed applications. Additionally, dual independent screening and data extraction, with reflexive documentation of decisions and reviewer assumptions, will enhance transparency around application of the criterion.

Risk of bias in data collection

During data collection, biases may stem from selective data recording. To minimise this risk, independent duplicate data extraction will be conducted by two reviewers (EVMB and ETYT) using a piloted standardised extraction form, ensuring reviewers capture all relevant data. Additionally, reflexive notes will be made throughout data collection to document decisions and assumptions made by each reviewer, ensuring transparency and accountability.

Reporting meta-biases

Potential meta-biases (eg, selective outcome reporting within included studies or under-representation of certain populations) will be narratively assessed and reported in the synthesis. If feasible, patterns of missing or inconsistently reported data will be illustrated within summary tables. Finally, the review team will reflect and report on their sociocultural and disciplinary position, acknowledging how researcher identity can shape inclusion and interpretation of evidence.

Confidence in cumulative evidence

The overall strength and confidence in the body of evidence generated by this review will be systematically assessed using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) tool,27 adapted for non-meta-analytic, narrative synthesis of heterogeneous quantitative, qualitative and mixed-methods studies. The GRADE tool will be applied narratively at the level of each primary outcome domain (intersectional health outcomes and intersectional healthcare utilisation outcomes) to assess the strength of quantitative evidence. Within each domain, where sufficient studies report comparable intersectional patterns (eg, consistent findings on ethnicity×gender effects on a specific outcome, or ethnicity×socioeconomic status gradients), a separate GRADE rating may be assigned to key intersectional patterns or subgroups to reflect variation in confidence across different axes combinations. This flexible application acknowledges the heterogeneous nature of the evidence and the review’s focus on intersectionality.

Based on the results of the JBI25 risk of bias assessments, downgrading will occur if the body of evidence is dominated by studies with serious risk of bias that may affect the reliability of findings. Inconsistency of findings across studies for the same intersectional axes and outcomes, accounting for expected heterogeneity due to population, context and methodological differences, without explanation, will also lead to downgrading. Downgrading may also be applied if studies focus on single-axis analyses, limited PROGRESS-Plus coverage, or populations/settings not representative of broader EM experiences, or if intersectionality is applied superficially. Finally, uncertainty or reliance on few or small studies for key intersectional patterns will warrant downgrading, assessed by number of studies, sample sizes and precision of reported estimates (eg, CIs where available, or whether findings are based on small/underpowered subgroups for specific intersections).

The strength of qualitative and mixed-methods evidence will be assessed using the Confidence in the Evidence from Reviews of Qualitative Research (GRADE-CERQual) tool.28 Each primary outcome will be rated following GRADE or GRADE-CERQual guidance (high, moderate, low, very low) with narrative explanations behind each rating. Additional ratings or confidence statements may be provided for prominent intersectional patterns (eg, ethnicity×gender disparities in mental health, or migration status×socioeconomic status effects on service access) when the evidence base permits meaningful differentiation and when such granularity adds value to interpreting intersectional inequities.

Ratings will consider: the completeness of PROGRESS-Plus reporting; integration of intersectionality theory; and convergence or complementarity or dissonance between quantitative and qualitative strands. Final integration of the graded quantitative, qualitative and mixed-methods studies will provide an overarching interpretive classification on the strength of evidence for each primary outcome domain (health outcomes and healthcare utilisation), PROGRESS-Plus representation, and integration of intersectionality theory. Evidence ratings will be presented in summary tables (eg, GRADE evidence profiles or CERQual summary of findings tables adapted for narrative synthesis), with footnotes explaining domain-specific downgrading decisions in this non-meta-analytic context.

Ethics and dissemination

Since this is a systematic review and only uses previously published publicly available data, it does not require ethical approval. The findings will be disseminated through presentations at professional meetings, relevant scientific or professional conferences, and publication in a peer-reviewed journal.
