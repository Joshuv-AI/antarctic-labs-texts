Local Interaction Autoregressive Model for High Dimension Time Series Data
Jingyang Li, Yang Chen

Abstract
High-dimensional matrix and tensor time series often exhibit local dependency, where each entry interacts mainly with a small neighborhood. Accounting for local interactions in a prediction model can greatly reduce the dimensionality of the parameter space, leading to more efficient inference and more accurate predictions. We propose a Local Interaction Autoregressive ( LIAR ) framework and study Separable LIAR , a variant with shared row and column components, for high-dimensional matrix/tensor time series forecasting problems. We derive a scalable parameter estimation algorithm via parallel least squares with a BIC-type neighborhood selector. Theoretically, we show consistency of neighborhood selection and derive error bounds for kernel and auto-covariance estimation. Numerical simulations show that the BIC selector recovers the true neighborhood with high success rates, the LIAR achieves small estimation errors, and the forecasts outperform matrix time-series baselines. In real data applications, a Total Electron Content (TEC) case study shows the model can identify localized spatio-temporal propagation and improved prediction as compared with non-local time series prediction models.

Further extensions to tensor autoregressive settings capture higher-order interactions and preserve multi-way dependencies in array-valued data

While we can always resort to the well-studied vector time series analysis through vectorization, this would lead to a problem of a significantly larger size and introduce many redundant parameters.
Recent studies, such as the factor autoregressive model
(
4
;
2
)
, have addressed this issue, but the resulting parameters often lack clear interpretability.
Additionally, the vectorization destroys the spatial information that is crucial in geophysics and environmental science.
To address these challenges, several methods based on autoregressive (AR) structures specifically designed for matrix/tensor time series have been developed.
For instance,
3
proposed the following matrix autoregressive (MAR) model with a bilinear form:
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}={\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t-1}{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\top}+{\boldsymbol{E}}_{t}
,
where
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}
is the
M\times N
matrix.
Bayesian method has also been proposed for variable selection in high-dimensional matrix autoregressive models
(
1
)
.
Subsequently,
21
incorporated vector-valued time series data into the matrix autoregression model.
However, tensor time series remains a less explored area.
14
proposed a multi-linear form for tensor time series as a generalization of the matrix case.
Despite these advancements, none of these approaches fully leverage the spatial dependency in the AR coefficient matrices.
In contrast,
11
;
12
took spatial dependency into account by assuming
{\boldsymbol{A}}
and
{\boldsymbol{B}}
to be banded matrices, which significantly decreases the number of parameters.

Although MAR
(
3
)
reduces the number of parameters compared with vectorization, and the MAR-ST (spatio-temporal) model
(
11
)
and banded MAR
(
12
)
further exploits spatial structures for matrix time series, computational cost remains a significant challenge for high-dimensional data.
This high cost is largely because their algorithms are inherently iterative, a requirement that stems directly from the model assumptions being bilinear in the parameters.
Additionally, their model’s underlying constraints can be overly stringent. Specifically, the MAR model can be understood as performing
MN
low-rank matrix regressions, necessitating that the coefficient matrices have a rank of 1 and share identical column subspaces across rows and identical row subspaces across columns. While this structure is manageable for the MAR model due to its adequate number of parameters, it becomes excessively restrictive when banded structures are simultaneously imposed.

In this paper, we propose a general framework for matrix and tensor-variate AR model that is able to incorporate the spatial structures. Our framework is very flexible which covers both MAR
(
3
)
, MAR-ST
(
11
)
, banded MAR
(
12
)
, and tensor AR
(
14
)
.
To incorporate the spatial structure, we specifically focus on the Local Interaction Autoregressive (
LIAR
) model, which applies to both matrix and tensor data.
Our model enjoys a locally adaptive dependency property in that it allows different entries to depend on different sizes of neighborhoods.
In practice, the size of the neighborhood is unknown. We propose a Bayesian information criterion for the selection of the neighborhood.
We show that this criterion leads to consistent neighborhood selection under various asymptotic regimes.
When the neighborhoods are given, we propose parallel least squares (LS) estimators for the coefficients. We also provide asymptotic properties of the parallel LS estimator.
To further reduce the model’s parameters, while also imposing less stringent constraints than the MAR-ST and banded MAR models mentioned previously, we consider a separable structure on the coefficients, which we call Separable-
LIAR
(
SP-LIAR
).

1.1
Our Contributions

Our contributions are summarized as follows. First, we introduce a unified framework for matrix and tensor time series that accommodates high dimensionality and multi-way structure in a coherent setup. It subsumes much of the existing literature as special cases. Specifically, we propose a new model that relaxes the structural constraints of MAR-ST and banded MAR while preserving the same order of parameters. Second, we design algorithms that are both simple to implement and suitable for parallel execution. Our algorithms provide closed-form estimators, eliminating the need for iterative processes. This greatly reduces the computational complexity (see Table
1
for the comparison with existing literature). Third, we establish asymptotic guarantees for our estimators without imposing structural assumptions on the noise, and identifiability poses no obstacle for
LIAR
or
SP-LIAR
because inference is conducted at the equivalence-class level rather than on individual components. Moreover, our neighborhood-selection criterion is consistent under mild conditions across multiple regimes, including jointly growing sample size, ambient dimension, and neighborhood size. Finally, on synthetic data, our criterion selects the true neighborhood with high success rates, kernel and auto-covariance/auto-correlation estimates are accurate, and compared with matrix time-series baselines our method achieves the lowest prediction RMSE with much shorter runtime. On large-scale total electron content (TEC) data, viewed as either matrix or tensor time series, our approach attains slightly better predictive accuracy and markedly faster runtime than existing methods, and the learned neighborhoods exhibit interpretable distributional patterns.

Methodology

Bold lower-case (e.g.,
{\boldsymbol{z}}
) denote vectors, bold capitals (e.g.,
{\boldsymbol{A}},{\boldsymbol{B}}
) denote matrices.
All vectorization operations, denoted by
\text{vec}(\cdot)
, and any implicit index orderings strictly adhere to the column-major convention.
We index matrix location
{\mathcal{S}}=[M]\times[N]
, and write an index
{\boldsymbol{i}}=(i_{1},i_{2})\in{\mathcal{S}}
.
Matrix entries are explicitly denoted with square brackets, as
[{\boldsymbol{A}}]_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}
.
Denote
\|\cdot\|_{\rm{F}}
the Frobenius norm of matrices, and denote
\|\cdot\|_{\ell_{p}}
the
\ell_{p}
-norm of vectors or vectorized tensors for
0\leq p\leq\infty
. Here
\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{\ell_{\infty}}
denotes the largest magnitude of the entries of
{\boldsymbol{v}}
.
The spectral radius of a matrix
{\boldsymbol{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M\times M}
(denoted by
\rho({\boldsymbol{A}})
) is defined to be the maximum modulus of eigenvalues. The operator norm of a matrix
{\boldsymbol{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M\times N}
(denoted by
\|{\boldsymbol{A}}\|
) is defined to be the largest singular value.
We denote
a\vee b=\max\{a,b\},a\wedge b=\min\{a,b\}
.

2.1
Local Interaction Autoregressive (
LIAR
) Model

Let
\{{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathcal{S}}}
be a matrix-valued time series.
For each site
{\boldsymbol{i}}=(i_{1},i_{2})\in{\mathcal{S}}
and lag
p\in[P]
, let
{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}\subset{\mathcal{S}}
be a (location-specific) spatial neighborhood, and let
{\boldsymbol{M}}_{p,{\boldsymbol{i}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathcal{S}}}
satisfy
\textsf{supp}({\boldsymbol{M}}_{p,{\boldsymbol{i}}})={\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}
.
The
LIAR
specification assumes that each entry of
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}
depends only on past values within its neighborhood:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{i}}\in{\mathcal{S}}}\sum_{p\in[P]}\langle{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t-p},{\boldsymbol{M}}_{p,{\boldsymbol{i}}}\rangle\ {\boldsymbol{e}}_{i_{1}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i_{2}}^{\top}+{\boldsymbol{E}}_{t},

(21)

where
{\boldsymbol{E}}_{t}
is a mean-zero innovation independent of
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t-1},{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t-2},\ldots
and
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i_{1}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M},{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i_{2}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
are standard basis.
Both the
shape
and
size
of
{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}
may vary with
{\boldsymbol{i}}
; i.e., neighborhoods need not be rectangular.
Our goal is to recover the neighborhoods
\{{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}\}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}\in{\mathcal{S}}}
and estimate the coefficients
\{{\boldsymbol{M}}_{p,{\boldsymbol{i}}}\}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}\in{\mathcal{S}},p\in[P]}
.

In particular, this formulation admits a VAR representation and includes several familiar special cases:

1.

VAR representation.

Denote
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}=\textsf{vec}({\boldsymbol{X}}_{t})
and
{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{t}=\textsf{vec}({\boldsymbol{E}}_{t})
. Flattening both sides of (
A7
) yields

{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}\;=\;\sum_{p\in[P]}{\boldsymbol{M}}_{p}^{\textsf{vec}}\,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-p}\;+\;{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{t},

where
{\boldsymbol{M}}_{p}^{\textsf{vec}}
has the structured form

{\boldsymbol{M}}_{p}^{\textsf{vec}}\;=\;\sum_{{\boldsymbol{i}}\in{\mathcal{S}}}\big({\boldsymbol{e}}_{i_{2}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i_{1}}\big)\textsf{vec}({\boldsymbol{M}}_{p,{\boldsymbol{i}}})^{\top}.

2.

Banded VAR
(
9
)
.

If
N=1
and
{\mathcal{J}}_{i}=\{u:\,|u-i|\leq K\}
, the model reduces to a banded VAR on a length-
M
vector with bandwidth
K
.

3.

MAR
(
3
)
.

Assume each local coefficient is rank one with shared components:
{\boldsymbol{M}}_{p,{\boldsymbol{i}}}={\boldsymbol{a}}_{p,i_{1}}\,{\boldsymbol{b}}_{p,i_{2}}^{\top}
.
Let
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i_{1}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{A}}_{p}={\boldsymbol{a}}_{p,i_{1}}^{\top}
and
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i_{2}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{B}}_{p}={\boldsymbol{b}}_{p,i_{2}}^{\top}
for
{\boldsymbol{i}}=(i_{1},i_{2})\in{\mathcal{S}}
.
And
{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}={\mathcal{S}}
for all
{\boldsymbol{i}}
, then

{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}\;=\;\sum_{p\in[P]}{\boldsymbol{A}}_{p}\,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t-p}\,{\boldsymbol{B}}_{p}^{\top}\;+\;{\boldsymbol{E}}_{t},

which is the matrix autoregressive (MAR) model.

4.

MAR-ST
(
11
)
or banded MAR
(
12
)
.

Under the same rank-one form
{\boldsymbol{M}}_{p,{\boldsymbol{i}}}={\boldsymbol{a}}_{p,i_{1}}\,{\boldsymbol{b}}_{p,i_{2}}^{\top}
with location-specific rectangular neighborhoods

{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}\;=\;\{(u_{1},u_{2}):\ |u_{1}-i_{1}|\leq K_{1,i_{1}},\ |u_{2}-i_{2}|\leq K_{2,i_{2}}\},

the representation above holds with
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{p}
and
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{p}
banded, yielding the structured MAR.

5.

Separable LIAR (
SP-LIAR
).

To balance number of parameter and expressive power, we impose a low-rank separable structure on each local coefficient. Specifically, let

{\boldsymbol{M}}_{p,{\boldsymbol{i}}}=\sum_{r\in[R]}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{p,r,i_{1}}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{p,r,i_{2}}^{\top}
, with
[{\boldsymbol{a}}_{p,r,i_{1}}]_{u_{1}}=0
if
|i_{1}-u_{1}|>K_{1,i_{1}}
, and
[{\boldsymbol{b}}_{p,r,i_{2}}]_{u_{2}}=0
if
|i_{2}-u_{2}|>K_{2,i_{2}}
, and
R\leq\min_{ij}\{K_{1,i_{1}},K_{2,i_{2}}\}
.
Then the model can be written as

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}=\sum_{p\in[P]}\sum_{r\in[R]}{\boldsymbol{A}}_{p,r}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t-p}{\boldsymbol{B}}_{p,r}^{\top}+{\boldsymbol{E}}_{t},

(22)

where
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i_{1}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{A}}_{p,r}={\boldsymbol{a}}_{p,r,i_{1}}^{\top}
,
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i_{2}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{B}}_{p,r}={\boldsymbol{b}}_{p,r,i_{2}}^{\top}
are banded matrices. Here
R
governs the rank of local interactions:
R=1
recovers MAR-ST or banded MAR, and
SP-LIAR
approaches the unrestricted
LIAR
as
R
increases.

In small-scale settings (e.g., macroeconomic panels), global MAR can be adequate
(
3
)
, but in large-scale spatio–temporal systems it becomes computationally and statistically burdensome due to dense, high-dimensional kernels. MAR-ST
(
11
)
and banded MAR
(
12
)
mitigate this cost by imposing banded structure for matrices. But this rank-1 separable form restriction limits expressiveness and cannot capture location-specific variation.

By contrast,
LIAR
introduces local dependence through neighborhoods
\{{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}\}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}\in{\mathcal{S}}}
that are allowed to vary in shape and size across locations, which captures spatially varying (location-specific) dependence while dramatically reducing the number of parameters compared with MAR. As an intermediate option,
SP-LIAR
imposes a low-rank separable structure within each neighborhood, offering a tunable bridge between structured MAR and the fully flexible
LIAR
: smaller rank yields more parsimonious models, whereas increasing the rank brings
SP-LIAR
closer to unrestricted
LIAR
while preserving locality and interpretability.

Estimating Coefficients

We now present estimation methods for
LIAR

For clarity of exposition, we will focus on

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{i}}\in{\mathcal{S}}}\langle{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t-p},{\boldsymbol{M}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}\rangle\ {\boldsymbol{e}}_{i_{1}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i_{2}}^{\top}+{\boldsymbol{E}}_{t},

(23)

For each
{\boldsymbol{i}}\in{\mathcal{S}}
, we denote
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{({\boldsymbol{i}})}:={\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}({\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}),{\boldsymbol{m}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}={\boldsymbol{M}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}({\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}|}

Here

is the vector that collects the entries

in column-major order for

Then the scalar regression for entry

Stacking over

{\boldsymbol{z}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}={\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}{\boldsymbol{m}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}+{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}},

\begin{split}&{\boldsymbol{z}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}=\begin{bmatrix}[{\boldsymbol{X}}_{2}]_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}&\cdots&[{\boldsymbol{X}}_{T}]_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}\\
\end{bmatrix}^{\top},\quad{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}=\begin{bmatrix}[{\boldsymbol{E}}_{2}]_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}&\cdots&[{\boldsymbol{E}}_{T}]_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}\\

Efficiency and Computational Cost Analysis of Parallel LS.

Due to the spatial locality, our parallel LS is highly efficient. Additionally, the estimator is non-iterative with a closed-form solution.
Let
J=\max_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}|
. Then for each sub-problem, we only need to solve a least squares problem, resulting in a computational cost of order
O(J^{2}T+J^{3})
. It’s worth noting that the computation of coefficients for a single entry is independent of the matrix data’s ambient dimension. The total computational cost is
O\big(MN(J^{2}T+J^{3})\big)

In most applications,
J
is small constant, so the computational cost reduces to
O(MNT)
. Moreover, the parallel LS can be implemented concurrently, further decreasing the algorithm’s runtime.
In contrast, while the spatial structure was utilized in
11
, their iterative least squares algorithm does not fully exploit it, leading to a significantly higher computational cost (see Table
1
).

2.3
Neighborhood Selection

We now discuss how to select the neighborhood
{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}
for
LIAR
.
Ideally, one would hope to select from all subsets of
{\mathcal{S}}
.
However, due to the
non-nested
model structure, this is difficult without refining the candidate class.
We illustrate this with a simple example.

Example (Non-nested ambiguity).

Consider the linear regression problem

y_{t}=\langle{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{M}}\rangle
,
t=1,\cdots,T,
with
{\boldsymbol{M}}
supported on
{\mathcal{J}}=\{(u,v):|u-i_{0}|\leq K_{1},|v-j_{0}|\leq K_{2}\}
.
Consider an alternative candidate
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}=\{(u,v):|u-i_{0}|\leq k_{1},|v-j_{0}|\leq k_{2}\}
for some
k_{1}<K_{1},k_{2}>K_{2}
(so neither rectangle contains the other).
There are natural designs
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}
under which one can construct
\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}
supported on
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}
that reproduces exactly the same responses
y_{t}
as
{\boldsymbol{M}}
. A formal statement and proof are provided in Appendix.

Without nesting, empirical criteria cannot reliably separate such candidates. We therefore restrict the search to a nested sequences indexed by
k
for each location
{\boldsymbol{i}}=(i_{1},i_{2})
:

{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[1]\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[K_{0}]\subset{\mathcal{S}},

where
{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]={\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}
, and
K_{0}
is a prescribed cap.
Write
s_{k}=|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k]|
(depends on
k
, and implicitly on
{\boldsymbol{i}}
).
For any level
k
, let
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}[k]:={\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}\big({\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k]\big)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{s_{k}}
. Then we form the design matrix

{\boldsymbol{Y}}[k]=\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}[k]&\cdots&{\boldsymbol{x}}_{T-1}[k]\\
\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

Also recall
{\boldsymbol{z}}=\begin{bmatrix}[{\boldsymbol{X}}_{2}]_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}&\cdots&[{\boldsymbol{X}}_{T}]_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}\\
\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
.
The residual sum of squares is

\textsf{RSS}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}(k):=\|{\boldsymbol{z}}-{\boldsymbol{Y}}[k]({\boldsymbol{Y}}[k]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}[k])^{-1}{\boldsymbol{Y}}[k]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{z}}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}.

And we use the Bayesian information criterion for the bandwidth selection:

\displaystyle\textsf{BIC}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}(k)=\log\textsf{RSS}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}(k)+D_{0}\,\frac{|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k]|}{T}\,\log(M\vee N\vee T).

(26)

For each

, we select
\widehat{k}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}
by

\widehat{k}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}=\arg\min_{k\leq K_{0}}\ \textsf{BIC}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}(k)
.

Choice of
D_{0}
and
K_{0}
.

We set
D_{0}=\log\log T
when considering the regime where
T,M,N\to\infty
while

remains fixed. The cutoff

can be chosen by
examining the curvature of
\textsf{BIC}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}(k)
. When
J
also diverges, choosing

is more delicate; we defer this case to the next section.

4
Local Interactive Tensor Autoregression Model

In this section, we consider local interactive tensor autoregressive model.
The modeling idea mirrors the matrix case in that each entry evolves from past values in a local neighborhood.
We use calligraphic-font bold-face letters (e.g.,
{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}
) to denote tensors.
An
d
-th order tensor is a
d
-way array, for example,
{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{d}}
means that its
j
-th dimension has size
N_{j}
. We will denote the index set
{\mathcal{S}}_{d}=[N_{1}]\times\cdots[N_{d}]
.
Let
\{{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathcal{S}}_{d}}
be a tensor time series, and

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}_{t}=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{i}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{d}}\langle{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}_{t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}\rangle\cdot{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i_{d}}+{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}_{t},

where
{\boldsymbol{i}}=(i_{1},\cdots,i_{d})\in{\mathcal{S}}_{d}
and
{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}
is supported on a local neighborhood
{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}
of
{\boldsymbol{i}}
.
Vectorizing both sides yields:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}=\underbrace{\sum_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}({\boldsymbol{e}}_{i_{d}}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i_{1}})\textsf{vec}({\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}})^{\top}}_{=:{\boldsymbol{M}}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}+{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{t},

where
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}=\textsf{vec}({\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}_{t}),{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{t}=\textsf{vec}({\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}_{t})
.
And we denote
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{({\boldsymbol{i}})}={\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}_{t}({\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}|},{\boldsymbol{m}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}={\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}({\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}|}
.
Then the scalar regression for entry
{\boldsymbol{i}}
for
t=2,\dots,T
,

[{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}_{t}]_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}=\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}^{({\boldsymbol{i}})},{\boldsymbol{m}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}\rangle+[{\boldsymbol{E}}_{t}]_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}
.
Stacking over
t
gives the standard linear model

{\boldsymbol{z}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}={\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}{\boldsymbol{m}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}+{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}},

where

\begin{split}&{\boldsymbol{z}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}=\begin{bmatrix}[{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}_{2}]_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}&\cdots&[{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}_{T}]_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}\\
\end{bmatrix}^{\top},\quad{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}=\begin{bmatrix}[{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}_{2}]_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}&\cdots&[{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}_{T}]_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}\\
\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T-1},\\
&{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{({\boldsymbol{i}})}&\cdots&{\boldsymbol{x}}_{T-1}^{({\boldsymbol{i}})}\\
\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(T-1)\times|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}|}.\end{split}

And the least square estimator is :

\widehat{\boldsymbol{m}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}=\arg\min_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}\frac{1}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{z}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}-{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}{\boldsymbol{m}}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}=({\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}})^{-1}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{z}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}.

We summarize the procedure in Algorithm
3
.

Algorithm 3

Parallel Least Square for Tensor Autoregression Model

Input:
Local neighborhood
{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}
, data
\{{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}

ParFor

{\boldsymbol{i}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{d}

Collect the covariate
{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}
and response
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}

Solve least square
\widehat{\boldsymbol{m}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}=({\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}})^{-1}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{z}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}

End ParFor

Output:
\widehat{\boldsymbol{m}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}

Theoretical results for coefficient estimation, auto-covariance estimation, and neighborhood selection extend to the tensor setting with the same proof strategy as in the matrix case.
Importantly, tensor auto-regressions may exhibit no locality along some modes; this is naturally accommodated by our framework by choosing
{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}
to span the full range along those dimensions (while remaining local along others).

7
Conclusion

We proposed the
local interaction autoregressive
(
LIAR
) framework, a general principle for modeling high-dimensional matrix and tensor time series via short-range spatio-temporal dependence. In the matrix setting, the proposed
SP-LIAR
serves as a bridge between traditional MAR (or banded MAT-ST) modeling and
LIAR
framework. By restricting each entry’s evolution to a data-driven neighborhood,
LIAR
ensures parsimony and interpretability while maintaining predictive accuracy.

We developed scalable estimation procedures (e.g., parallel least squares, projection-based methods), a BIC-type neighborhood selector, and statistical theory for kernel and auto-covariance estimation. Extensive simulations show
LIAR
achieves lower prediction error and markedly better runtime over competing baselines (e.g., MAR, MAR-ST) across diverse settings. On real-world data,
LIAR
captures salient local dynamics and provides practical forecasting gains.

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}(1+c_{0})^{-1}T\kappa_{1}\|{\boldsymbol{b}}_{2}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}-C_{1}\kappa_{1}^{-1}\mu_{2q}^{4}(1-\delta)^{-4}J_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\square$}}^{2}\log(M\vee N\vee T)

\displaystyle\geq c_{1}\textsf{RSS}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}(k_{0})\frac{\kappa_{1}}{\mu_{2q}^{2}}\|{\boldsymbol{b}}_{2}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}

for some
c_{1}\in(0,1)
. And using
\log(1+x)\geq\frac{1}{2}x
for
x\in(0,2)
, we have

\displaystyle\log\textsf{RSS}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}(k)-\log\textsf{RSS}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}(k_{0})\geq\min\{c_{2}\frac{\kappa_{1}}{\mu_{2q}^{2}}\|{\boldsymbol{b}}_{2}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2},\log 3\}.

Therefore for sufficiently large
T
, we have

\displaystyle\textsf{BIC}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}(k)-\textsf{BIC}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}(k_{0})\geq\min\{c_{2}\frac{\kappa_{1}}{\mu_{2q}^{2}}\|{\boldsymbol{b}}_{2}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2},\log 3\}-D_{0}\frac{|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}|}{T}\log(M\vee N\vee T)>0,

under the margin condition that

\|{\boldsymbol{b}}_{2}\|_{\ell_{2}}=\|{\boldsymbol{M}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}({\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}\backslash{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k])\|_{\rm{F}}\geq\|{\boldsymbol{M}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}({\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}\backslash{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}-1])\|_{\rm{F}}.

Now we show, for all
{\boldsymbol{i}}\in{\mathcal{S}}
and
k>k_{0}
,
{\mathbb{P}}(\textsf{BIC}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}(k)<\textsf{BIC}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}(k_{0}))\rightarrow 0
. We have

\displaystyle\textsf{RSS}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}(k)=\inf_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\|{\boldsymbol{z}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}-{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k]{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}.

We can write
{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k]{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}={\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{1}+{\boldsymbol{S}}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{2}
. Denote

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}=\big(I-{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]\big({\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]\big)^{-1}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}\big){\boldsymbol{S}}.

Then

\displaystyle\textsf{RSS}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}(k)

\displaystyle=\inf_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\|{\boldsymbol{z}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}-{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k]{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}=\inf_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{2}}\|{\boldsymbol{z}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}-{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{S}}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{2}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\inf_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{2}}\|{\boldsymbol{z}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}-{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]\Big({\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{1}+\big({\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]\big)^{-1}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{2}\Big)-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{2}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\inf_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{2}}\|{\boldsymbol{z}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}-{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{1}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{2}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}.

An explicit minimizer is

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{1}=\big({\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]\big)^{-1}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{z}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}},\qquad{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{2}=\big(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}\big)^{-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{z}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\textsf{RSS}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}(k)=\textsf{RSS}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}(k_{0})-\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}\big(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}\big)^{-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}.

Next we bound
\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}})^{-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}
:

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}})^{-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}})^{-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}\leq\lambda_{\max}\big((\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}})^{-1}\big)\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}.

Since
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k]|-|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}|}
, we have

\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\leq\big(|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k]|-|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}|\big)\,\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}\|_{\ell_{\infty}}^{2}.

Recall
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}={\boldsymbol{S}}^{\top}\!\big({\boldsymbol{I}}-{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]({\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}])^{-1}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}\big){\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}
. Let an arbitrary row of
{\boldsymbol{S}}^{\top}
be
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\top}
. Then

\displaystyle\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\top}\!\big({\boldsymbol{I}}-{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]({\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}])^{-1}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}\big){\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{x}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]({\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}])^{-1}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}.

(D15)

From Lemma
2
,
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}=O_{p}\big((T\log(M\vee N\vee T))^{1/2}\mu_{2q}^{2}J_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\square$}}^{1/2}(1-\delta)^{-3/2}\big)
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]({\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}])^{-1}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{x}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]\Big(({\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}])^{-1}-({\mathbb{E}}\,{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}])^{-1}\Big){\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}

\displaystyle\qquad+{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]({\mathbb{E}}\,{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}])^{-1}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}.

Since
\tfrac{1}{T}{\mathbb{E}}\,{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]
is a submatrix of
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}\big(({\mathbb{E}}\,{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}])^{-1}\big)=\lambda_{\min}^{-1}({\mathbb{E}}\,{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}])\leq T^{-1}\kappa_{1}^{-1}.

Therefore, from Lemma
2
and
\max_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{0}]_{{\boldsymbol{i}};{\boldsymbol{i}}}\leq\|[{\boldsymbol{E}}_{t}]_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}\|_{2q}^{2}\leq\mu_{2q}^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\quad|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]({\mathbb{E}}\,{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}])^{-1}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}|

\displaystyle\lesssim(T\log(M\vee N\vee T))^{1/2}\mu_{2q}^{2}\,|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}|\,J_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\square$}}^{1/2}(1-\delta)^{-2}\,\kappa_{1}^{-1}\mu_{2q}^{2}.

Next we bound
\|({\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}])^{-1}-({\mathbb{E}}\,{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}])^{-1}\|
similarly as in (
D14
):

\displaystyle\|({\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}])^{-1}-({\mathbb{E}}\,{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}])^{-1}\|

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\lesssim T^{-1}(T^{-1}\log(M\vee N\vee T))^{1/2}\mu_{2q}^{2}\,|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}|\,J_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\square$}}(1-\delta)^{-4}\kappa_{1}^{-2}.

Thus

\displaystyle\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]\Big(({\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}])^{-1}-({\mathbb{E}}\,{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}])^{-1}\Big){\boldsymbol{Y}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k_{0}]^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim T^{-1}(T^{-1}\log(M\vee N\vee T))^{1/2}\mu_{2q}^{2}\,|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}|\,J_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\square$}}(1-\delta)^{-3}\kappa_{1}^{-2}\cdot|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}|\cdot T\mu_{2q}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt\cdot(T\log(M\vee N\vee T))^{1/2}\mu_{2q}^{2}\,J_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\square$}}^{1/2}(1-\delta)^{-2}

\displaystyle=J_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\square$}}^{3/2}\,|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}|^{2}\,\log(M\vee N\vee T)\,\mu_{2q}^{6}\,\kappa_{1}^{-2}(1-\delta)^{-6}.

Returning to (
D.3
), we conclude

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}\|_{\ell_{\infty}}=O_{p}\big((T\log(M\vee N\vee T))^{1/2}\mu_{2q}^{4}\,|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}|\,J_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\square$}}^{1/2}(1-\delta)^{-2}\cdot\kappa_{1}^{-1}\big).

Hence

\displaystyle\textsf{RSS}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}(k_{0})-\textsf{RSS}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}(k)\lesssim\big(|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k]|-|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}|\big)\,\log(M\vee N\vee T)\,\mu_{2q}^{8}\,|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}|^{2}\,J_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\square$}}(1-\delta)^{-4}\,\kappa_{1}^{-3}.

From Lemma
3
,
\textsf{RSS}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}(k)\gtrsim T\mu_{2q}^{2}
. Therefore

\displaystyle\frac{\textsf{RSS}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}(k_{0})-\textsf{RSS}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}(k)}{\textsf{RSS}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}(k)}\lesssim\frac{|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k]|-|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}|}{T}\,\log(M\vee N\vee T)\,\mu_{2q}^{6}\,|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}|^{2}\,J_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\square$}}(1-\delta)^{-4}\,\kappa_{1}^{-3}.

Using
\log(1+x)\leq x
for
x>0
, we have

\displaystyle\quad\log\textsf{RSS}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}(k_{0})-\log\textsf{RSS}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}(k)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k]|-|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}|}{T}\,\log(M\vee N\vee T)\,\mu_{2q}^{6}\,|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}|^{2}\,J_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\square$}}(1-\delta)^{-4}\,\kappa_{1}^{-3}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\textsf{BIC}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}(k)-\textsf{BIC}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}(k_{0})

\displaystyle\geq D_{0}\,\frac{|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}[k]|-|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}|}{T}\,\log(M\vee N\vee T)

\displaystyle\quad+\log\textsf{RSS}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}(k)-\log\textsf{RSS}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}(k_{0})

\displaystyle>0,

as long as we choose
D_{0}\gtrsim\mu_{2q}^{6}\,|{\mathcal{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}|^{2}\,J_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\square$}}\,(1-\delta)^{-4}\,\kappa_{1}^{-3}
.

∎
