Multi-scale representation of integer sets: application to prime numbers
Mahmoud Melkemi

Abstract
We propose a multi-scale analysis method for studying arithmetic properties of integer sets, such as primality. Our approach organizes information through a hierarchy of nested sequences, where each level enables a hierarchical expression of the studied property by examining patterns at varying levels of granularity. To illustrate the method, we apply it to three cases: the distribution of prime numbers, twin primes, and Mersenne numbers. While this does not claim any new breakthroughs on these classical problems, the approach allows for efficient analysis of the studied property across large integer sequences and reveals new characteristics observable at different scales. This framework provides an alternative perspective for investigating the fundamental arithmetic properties.

Introduction

This article presents a multi-scale representation of natural numbers that enables the analysis of their arithmetic properties at different resolutions. This approach provides a unified framework for simultaneously studying local structures (fine properties of integers) and global structures (general trends).

Multi-scale methods have proven effective across various scientific domains, particularly through multi-resolution analyses based on wavelets
[
1
]
, whose applications in signal and image processing are well established. Building upon this conceptual framework, we adapt these approaches to study fundamental arithmetic properties.

While multi-scale analysis has been applied to prime numbers in previous work
[
2
]
, which connects primes to fractal geometry and numeric accelerators via the Golden Mean, our approach differs fundamentally. Our method applies to general integer properties and, when specialized to prime numbers, reveals a distinct multiscale hierarchical structure.

To demonstrate this framework, we focus on primality - a field that has seen significant theoretical advances
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
. Our goal is not to compete with these significant results but rather to demonstrate the value of a multi-scale representation for studying arithmetic properties through pattern analysis at different scales.

The proposed multi-scale representation allows analysis of integer sets at different resolutions by generating simple integer sequences. In this article, each scale-descriptive sequence is constrained to integers between 0 and 255. Each integer efficiently encodes a given property across large consecutive integers. Analysis of these sequences reveals novel arithmetic properties.

To illustrate the multi-scale representation, let us express it through the example of primality in
\mathbb{N}^{*}
:

Level 1 (fine granularity)

We partition
\mathbb{N}^{*}
into blocks of 8 consecutive integers. Each block is encoded in binary (1=prime, 0=composite), then converted to decimal. For example:
(1,2,...,8)\rightarrow(01101010)_{2}=106
and
(9,...,16)\rightarrow(00101000)_{2}=40
.

The first terms of this sequence are:
106,40,162,10,8,162,8,40,34,130,\ldots

Level 2 (intermediate granularity)

We aggregate terms from level 1 into blocks of 8, encoding their non-nullity (1 if
\neq 0
, 0 otherwise). For example:

(106,40,...,40)\rightarrow(11111111)_{2}=255
. A term like
95=(01011111)_{2}
indicates that 6 of the 8 sub-blocks of 64 integers contain at least one prime.

Level 3 (coarse granularity)

By iterating the process on level 2, we obtain a macroscopic view. The first 366 terms equal
255
, reflecting the high density of primes in these intervals.

Let us now formally describe this multi-scale representation. Let
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a family of indicator functions marking a specific arithmetic property at each scale
k
, where
\phi_{k}(n)=1
if integer
n
possesses the property at scale
k
, and
0
otherwise. The hierarchical construction is performed via the sequences
(f^{(k)})_{k\geq 0}
defined recursively by:

\displaystyle f^{(1)}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{2^{t}-1}\phi_{0}(2^{t}n+1+j)\cdot 2^{2^{t}-1-j},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}

(1)

\displaystyle f^{(k+1)}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{2^{t}-1}\phi_{k}\big(f^{(k)}(2^{t}n+j)\big)\cdot 2^{2^{t}-1-j},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}

In Equation (
1
), the parameter
k
represents the scale, while the fixed value
t>1
determines the pattern size of
2^{t}
bits. Throughout this article, we set
t=3
, resulting in patterns comprising 8 positions.

Specifically, the initial level
f^{(0)}
directly identifies integers satisfying the base property
\phi_{0}
. The first level
f^{(1)}
encodes this property in blocks of 8 consecutive integers
P^{(1)}(n)=\{8n+1,\ldots,8n+8\}
via the binary representation
(\phi_{0}(8n+1),\ldots,\phi_{0}(8n+8))
, converted to a decimal integer equal to
f^{(1)}(n)
, sometimes noted
f^{(1)}(P^{(1)}(n))
.

This construction generalizes to higher scales by setting:

P^{(k)}_{n}=\bigcup_{j=0}^{7}P^{(k-1)}_{8n+j},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},\ k>1

(2)

Definition 1.

We call the
1
-pattern the binary representation

(\phi_{0}(8n+1),\ldots,\phi_{0}(8n+8))
encoded by
f^{(1)}(n)
. More generaly, the
k
-pattern,
k\geq 2
,

is
(\phi_{k-1}(f^{(k-1)}(8n+1)),\ldots,\phi_{k-1}(f^{(k-1)}(8n+8)))
encoded by the decimal number
f^{(k)}(n)
.

The
k
-pattern
is also denoted
f^{(k)}(P^{(k)}_{n})
to emphasizes that the value
f^{(k)}(n)
characterizes the underlying set
P^{(k)}_{n}
of
8^{k}
integers.

In the rest of the article, we also denote
np_{k}(f^{(k)}(n))
the function which counts the number of
1
s in the binary representation, measuring the property density in
k
-pattern.

The study of this model through three concrete examples reveals how this multi-scale decomposition of
\mathbb{N^{*}}
into disjoint partitions enables fine analysis of arithmetic properties. A variant with overlapping partitions could be defined by a minor adaptation of Equation (
1
).

Counting 1-patterns.

The statistical distribution of sequence

is presented in Tables

Statistically, we observe that the number of empty 1-patterns increases more rapidly than that of 1-patterns containing a single prime number, the latter growing slowly. Moreover, the number of 1-patterns containing 2 or 3 prime numbers decreases as
m
increases. Formally, we propose an estimation of these statistics:

Observation 2.

Over
[1,m]
(
m\leq N
), the number of 1-patterns with a single prime and those with multiple primes are expressed as:

\displaystyle N_{1}(m)

\displaystyle\approx\psi(m)\cdot Li(m),

(7)

\displaystyle 2N_{2}(m)+3N_{3}(m)+1

\displaystyle\approx(1-\psi(m))\cdot Li(m)

where

\displaystyle\psi(x)=\frac{x^{0.06}}{1+x^{0.06}}

(8)

\displaystyle Li(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{1}{ln(x)}dx

and
N_{j}(m)
denotes the number of blocks containing
j
prime numbers in the interval
[1,\;m]
.

This observation is verifierd empirically for
m\leq N
. For example when
m=N
we obtain:

\frac{Li(N)}{\pi(N)}=1.0002\;\;\frac{\psi(N)Li(N)}{N_{1}(N)}=1.01439\;\;\frac{2N_{2}(N)+3N_{3}(N)+1}{(1-\psi(N))Li(N)}=1.04124

(9)

Let
\pi(m)
be the number of prime numbers less than
m
. It is known that this number is approximately equal to
Li(m)
. The number of prime numbers is related to

\pi(m)=N_{1}(m)+2N_{2}(m)+3N_{3}(m)+1\approx Li(m)

where

N_{2}(m)=h_{m,1}(10)+h_{m,1}(34)+h_{m,1}(40)+h_{m,1}(130)+h_{m,1}(136)+h_{m,1}(160)

N_{3}(m)=h_{m,1}(138)+h_{m,1}(162)

For large values of

is an open question.

Position of a Prime Number in a 1-Pattern.

Given a sequence of 8 consecutive integers
P^{(1)}(n)
encoded by
f^{(1)}(n)
, whose binary representation is
b_{7}b_{6}b_{5}b_{4}b_{3}b_{2}b_{1}b_{0}

f^{(1)}(n)

Based on statistical analysis over the interval
[1,N]
, there is a
58.99\%
probability that this sequence contains no prime numbers. If it does contain a prime, we adopt the following search strategy: First, check the position
b_{1}
of the sequence, then
b_{3}
, followed by
b_{5}
, and finally
b_{7}
.

Justification.

•

The integer in position
b_{1}
is prime if

f^{(1)}(n)\in S_{1}=\{2,10,34,106,130,138,162\}
.
The associated probability is:

p_{1}=\text{Prob}(\text{prime in }b_{1})=\sum_{j\in S_{1}}h_{1,N}(j)=0.1181526

•

The integer in position
b_{3}
is prime if

f^{(1)}(n)\in S_{2}=\{8,10,40,106,136,138\}

The associated probability is:

p_{3}=\sum_{j\in S_{2}}h_{1,N}(j)=0.1181420
.

•

The integer in position
b_{5}
is prime if

f^{(1)}(n)\in S_{3}=\{32,34,40,106,160,162\}

The associated probability is:

p_{5}=\sum_{j\in S_{3}}h_{1,N}(j)=0.1181155
.

•

The integer in position
b_{7}
is prime if

f^{(1)}(n)\in S_{4}=\{128,130,136,138,160,162\}

The associated probability is:

p_{7}=\sum_{j\in S_{4}}h_{1,N}(j)=0.1180466

We observe that

If, for an interval

Second Level

Prime density.

To analyze the sequence
f^{(2)}(n)
, we are interested in the density of prime numbers in a regular partition of
\mathbb{N}
, although it has been proven that there is no regular partition in which each part contains at least one prime number
[
6
]
. In this context, let us define the density as follows:

Definition 2.

The prime numbers are said to be dense in
[1,m]
, via the partition
P^{(k)}(n)
, if for all
n
, the intersection
(P^{(k)}(n)\cap[1,m])\cap{\cal P}
is non-empty.

Observation 3.

For
m>e^{8^{k}}
, the prime numbers are not dense in
[1,m]
via the partition
P^{(k)}
.

Indeed, we know that
\mathrm{card}(P^{(k)}(n))=8^{k}
, and that the number of prime numbers in
[1,m]
is given by
\pi(m)\approx\frac{m}{\ln(m)}
.
For the prime numbers to be dense in
[1,m]
via
P^{(k)}
, the interval must contain at least
\frac{m}{8^{k}}
prime numbers. However, if

\pi(m)\approx\frac{m}{\ln(m)}<\frac{m}{8^{k}},

then the prime numbers cannot be dense. This inequality is satisfied when
m>e^{8^{k}}
.

The prime numbers are, therefore, not dense in
[1,N]
via the partition
P^{(2)}
, since approximately 1% of the 2-patterns are equal to 0.

6
Conclusion

This work has explored a multi-scale approach to study arithmetic properties of natural numbers. Through three examples - prime numbers, twin primes, and Mersenne numbers - we have shown how this hierarchical representation can reveal interesting structures in integer distributions.

The main aspects of this method include representing arithmetic properties through nested sequences, which allows simultaneous study of local and global behaviors. We have also highlighted combinatorial constraints on possible patterns at different scales, and shown how these multi-scale sequences can serve as measurement tools to quantify certain properties.

Natural extensions of this work could include studying overlapping patterns, adapting to variable block sizes, or applying this multi-scale analysis to other problems in number theory. We hope some of the opened perspectives may inspire future research.
