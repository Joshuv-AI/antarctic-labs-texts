Facilitators and barriers to enhancing cognitive reserve in adults aged ≥ 50 years with HIV-associated mild neurocognitive disorder: a qualitative study
Authors: Xianjun Mao, Jing Cheng, Dan Tan, Wenxi Zhong, Xi Zhang, Li Ma, Jian Tang, Zhaolan Yu, Yanhua Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeople living with HIV (PLWH) aged ≥ 50 years face health challenges, including HIV-associated mild neurocognitive disorder. Cognitive reserve is a crucial neuroprotective mechanism that may help this population benefit from interventions designed to enhance cognitive function. However, the factors influencing cognitive reserve development in this group remain underexplored.ObjectiveThis study aimed to identify facilitators and barriers to enhancing cognitive reserve in individuals aged ≥ 50 with HIV-associated mild neurocognitive disorder, to inform the development of personalized interventions.MethodsPurposive sampling was used to recruit participants from Luzhou, Sichuan. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with 26 PLWH aged ≥ 50 experiencing mild cognitive issues, including 19 men and seven women, with a mean age of 61.1 years. Interviews were recorded, transcribed, and analyzed using NVivo 14 and traditional content analysis.ResultsQualitative analysis identified two primary themes, comprising 12 sub-themes, related to facilitators and barriers to improving cognitive reserve. Key facilitators included perceived cognitive symptoms, compensatory strategies, individualized needs, valued self-efficacy, and external monitoring. Significant barriers included negative perceptions of health status, limited risk perception, concerns about privacy breaches, knowledge deficits, physical limitations, competing priorities, and financial cost.ConclusionThis study highlights key factors influencing cognitive reserve enhancement in PLWH aged ≥ 50 years, emphasizing the need for tailored interventions to address barriers and leverage facilitators. These findings provide a foundation for targeted programs to improve cognitive function in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-26053-1.

Background

The World Health Organization (WHO) refers to individuals aged ≥ 50 years living with HIV as “older people living with HIV (PLWH)” [1]. Globally, this group grew from 8% in 2000 to 24% in 2020 [2]. Mirroring this trend, Luzhou City saw a 15.42-fold rise in new infections among older adults (2011–2022) [3], who face significant comorbidities [4]. HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders (HAND) encompass a spectrum from Asymptomatic Neurocognitive Impairment (ANI) to HIV-associated Dementia (HAD), primarily characterized by memory dysfunction and slowed processing speed [5, 6]. Although antiretroviral therapy (ART) has reduced the incidence of HAD, the prevalence of Mild Neurocognitive Disorder (MND) is rising, particularly among individuals aged ≥ 50 years (43.8%) [7, 8]. A 2024 study in Luzhou found that 75.8% of individuals aged 50 and older had HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders [9]. As a prodromal stage, MND carries a high risk of progressing to HAD [10, 11].This progression, coupled with the fact that MND-associated cognitive deficits often compromise ART adherence and elevate the risk of virological failure [12], significantly exacerbates disease burden and caregiver strain. Therefore, MND represents a pivotal stage for targeted interventions.

Cognitive Reserve (CR) is defined as a neuropsychological resource built over the lifespan through educational experiences, occupational attainment, leisure activities, and lifestyle [13]. It helps individuals cope with damage to brain structure and function [14], and is a key protective factor for cognitive function [15]. A higher level of CR is negatively correlated with the rate of cognitive decline. Recent neuroimaging evidence indicates that the neural basis of CR – encompassing brain network efficiency and compensatory capacity – remains plastic into adulthood and older age [16, 17]. Therefore, cognitive interventions (e.g., cognitive training) may enhance CR by facilitating synaptic repair and reconnection, thereby optimizing neural architecture [18]. Structured cognitive stimulation has been shown to enhance CR in healthy adults [19], while combined training paradigms potentiated CR and neuronal health in aged rats [20]. Consequently, this study defines CR enhancement as the improvement of neural network utilization and compensatory capacity through targeted intervention.

Research shows that older adults with HAND often have a low CR index [21], which may limit their ability to mobilize CR and increase their risk of cognitive decline. Exploring and implementing strategies to enhance CR (e.g., cognitive intervention) is a promising approach to slowing cognitive decline and improving quality of life in this population. Despite growing research on CR, few studies have examined the facilitators and barriers to enhancing CR among PLWH aged ≥ 50 years with MND.

Consequently, this study employed semi-structured interviews to identify facilitators and barriers to CR enhancement in PLWH with MND. The findings aim to inform targeted CR interventions and advance a paradigm shift from passive disease management toward active health promotion among PLWH with MND, contributing to Healthy China goals.

Methods

Aim

To identify and elucidate the key facilitators and barriers associated with the enhancement of cognitive reserve in older adults (aged ≥ 50 years) living with HIV-associated mild neurocognitive disorder.

Design

This study employed a descriptive phenomenological framework, utilizing semi-structured interviews for data collection. Reporting adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [22].

Sample selection

A criterion-based purposive sampling strategy was employed to recruit participants from the ART clinics in Luzhou, between July and September 2024. Eligible individuals were required to meet the following pre-defined criteria: (1) laboratory-confirmed HIV infection per China CDC criteria (2024) [23]; (2) aged ≥ 50 years; and (3) a diagnosis of HIV-associated MND, defined as a Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score of 18–25 after education adjustment. Participants were also required to demonstrate adequate communication capacity – defined as the ability to comprehend and respond to verbal inquiries, articulate personal conditions clearly, and execute simple instructions relevant to the study – and informed consent.

Participants were excluded if they had a history of primary psychiatric disorders with known profound cognitive sequelae (e.g., schizophrenia, bipolar disorder), active psychosis, or language barriers that would invalidate cognitive testing. Common mood disorders (e.g., depressive disorder) were not excluded. Thus, 26 participants were recruited.

Data collection and quality control

Data collection integrated demographic questionnaire, MoCA and semi-structured interviews about enhancing cognitive reserve. A demographic questionnaire developed via systematic literature review and group discussion recording gender, age, educational attainment, current residence (urban/rural), marital status, time since HIV diagnosis (years), monthly income (RMB), and presumed HIV transmission mode.

MoCA was used to screen for MND in PLWH aged ≥ 50 years [24], using education-adjusted scores (< 12 years: +1 point) and diagnostic threshold of 18–25 post-adjustment [25, 26]. Two MoCA-trained postgraduate nursing researchers screened cognition. Comprehensive neuropsychological tests are rarely feasible in resource-limited settings [27] due to prolonged administration time and specialized personnel requirements.

Semi-structured interviews were conducted using a guide developed through a multi-step process. Grounded in a literature review and refined via expert consultation, the guide was piloted to ensure clarity. The pilot led to the replacement of technical jargon with a standardized, layperson-friendly introduction that defines cognitive reserve as a neuropsychological resource built across the lifespan. Participants were recruited from community health centers in Luzhou, China, through referrals from healthcare providers. Individuals meeting initial eligibility criteria completed cognitive screening, and those scoring 18–25 on the education-adjusted MoCA were subsequently invited to take part in semi-structured interviews. Interviews were conducted by postgraduate research assistants who had received certified training in qualitative methodology. All interviews were held in private rooms, lasted approximately 20–40 min, and were initiated only after written informed consent was obtained and the study purpose was explained.

Before beginning the interview guide, interviewers used a standardized script to introduce the construct of cognitive reserve. To ensure participant comprehension, a teach-back approach was employed, requesting that participants restate the concept in their own words. The interview guide consisted of open-ended questions to minimize interviewer influence on participants’ narratives. All interviews were audio-recorded with permission, anonymized during transcription, and accompanied by field notes documenting non-verbal behaviors (e.g., facial expressions, posture).

Each participant completed one interview. Data collection proceeded iteratively and continued until thematic saturation was achieved, defined operationally as the absence of new codes in at least three consecutive interviews [28]. Member checking could not be conducted due to stringent privacy requirements that prohibited collecting participants’ contact information.

Data analysis

Audio recordings were transcribed and independently verified by two researchers within 24 h of each interview. Data were analyzed using conventional content analysis [29] in NVivo 14 following a structured, sequential procedure [30]. A coding framework was developed iteratively through repeated engagement with the transcripts. Initial familiarization involved multiple readings of the data to establish a holistic understanding, followed by line-by-line analysis to identify salient concepts. Text segments were then systematically coded through open coding; whereby raw narrative data were condensed into meaningful units. Emerging codes were compared, grouped according to shared characteristics, and organized into sub-themes. These sub-themes were subsequently integrated into broader, overarching themes through an iterative process of comparing their conceptual relationships.

Findings

Participants characteristics

A total of 26 PLWH aged ≥ 50 years who met criteria for MND were enrolled in the study. Participants were assigned identifiers N1-N26 to ensure confidentiality. The sample comprised 19 men, with a mean age of 61.08 years and an average duration of 5.12 years since HIV diagnosis. Educational attainment varied across the sample, with 13 participants having completed ≤ elementary school, eight having completed middle school, and five having attained ≥ high school education. First, most respondents viewed memory decline as a normal part of aging, which led to passive resignation to this trajectory. This attitudinal barrier hindered the activation of compensatory cognitive reserve mechanisms required to mitigate neurocognitive changes:

“I’m at this age, and my memory is declining in every way. It’s natural for everything to decline; it’s perfectly normal.” (N22).

Additionally, some respondents felt fortunate to be alive after being infected with HIV, which led them to downplay concerns about other health issues:

“I didn’t care about my memory; I didn’t feel anything about it. I had this disease; I just needed to survive, nothing else.” (N11).

“I didn’t care about my memory; I didn’t feel anything about it. I had this disease; I just needed to survive, nothing else.” (N11).

“I mainly just focus on how things are. Thinking too much about it would only get me into trouble. I don’t know what I’m like until I check; I just leave it to fate.” (N25).

Discussion

This study reveals that the pathway to enhancing cognitive reserve in adults aged ≥ 50 with HIV-associated mild neurocognitive disorder is shaped by a critical dynamic: personal facilitators, such as compensatory strategies, are often overshadowed by systemic barriers, particularly negative illness perceptions and limited risk awareness. This imbalance provides a mechanistic explanation for the population’s lower cognitive reserve [21]. To be effective, interventions must shift from merely promoting brain-healthy activities to actively addressing these foundational barriers through psychoeducation and cognitive reframing. Empowering individuals in this manner is crucial to unlocking their motivation and capacity for sustained cognitive reserve enhancement.

Factors supporting cognitive reserve engagement

This study identified five key facilitators of cognitive reserve enhancement in older adults (≥ 50 years) with HIV-associated MND: perception of cognitive symptoms; compensatory strategies; individualized needs; valued self-efficacy; and external supervision. MND threatens cognitive function in PLWH aged ≥ 50 years. Symptom perception – the cognitive-emotional processing of health changes [31] – may influence efforts to enhance cognitive reserve. Our findings align with evidence linking symptom perception to self-management [32] and adaptation to maladaptive illness behaviors [33]. Conversely, in the absence of noticeable symptoms, patients may underestimate disease threats and fail to take protective actions, thereby hindering the enhancement of cognitive reserve [34].

Our findings on compensatory strategies provide crucial qualitative evidence for the active deployment of cognitive reserve in this clinical population. The described behaviors – ranging from explicit alarm-setting to the implicit use of environmental cues – reflect the brain’s attempt to optimize its functional efficiency in response to neural challenges. This aligns with the neurobiological model of CR, which suggests that its value lies not only in passive neural capital but in the dynamic capacity to compensate [35]. By consciously or unconsciously employing these strategies, participants are actively engaging and potentially strengthening the neural networks – particularly those involved in prefrontal executive control and default mode network reallocation [36] – that support CR. This process of strategic substitution and external aid utilization can be viewed as real-world cognitive training, potentially reinforcing synaptic reconnection [37] and enhancing neural network flexibility, thereby exercising the functional potential of the reserve. Consequently, these compensatory behaviors should be considered integral to a malleable CR system, which can be targeted and amplified through intervention. Enhancing cognitive reserve can diverse strategies that promote neuroplasticity [38], such as cognitive training, which optimizes brain network efficiency [39, 40]and regular aerobic exercise, which provides the molecular foundation (e.g., Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor, BDNF) for synaptic repair and regeneration [41, 42].

This study reveals that “valued self-efficacy” plays a pivotal role in enhancing cognitive reserve among PLWH aged 50 and above with MND. Specifically, this psychological mechanism moderates motivational and cognitive processes, enabling participants to find positive meaning in cognitive activities, thereby fostering robust health beliefs and leading to sustained engagement in cognitive reserve enhancement. These findings align with existing research, supporting aging studies that suggest self-efficacy buffers against cognitive decline [43] and providing qualitative validation for Zhang et al.‘s proposed mechanism, in which self-efficacy promotes health behavior engagement by strengthening personal identity [44]. These consistencies further substantiate the universal protective value of self-efficacy in mitigating cognitive decline [45]. Based on these findings, future research should explore strategies that integrate value-restructuring techniques to enhance self-efficacy in PLWH, helping them derive deeper personal meaning from cognitive reserve-enhancement.

External oversight prevents activity discontinuation [46], while peer modeling provides support. These dual facilitators strengthen health beliefs regarding cognitive reserve enhancement. However, group-based cognitive reserve efforts may be avoided by older PLWH with MND due to concerns about HIV status disclosure [47].

Challenges to cognitive reserve enhancement

This study identified several barriers to cognitive reserve enhancement in older PLWH with MND, including negative health perceptions, limited risk awareness, privacy concerns, knowledge deficits, physical health limitations, competing priorities, and financial constraints. Most respondents held negative attitudes toward HIV-associated MND and cognitive reserve enhancement, potentially accelerating cognitive decline. Patients often exhibited low concern [48] and maintained misconceptions, undermining their confidence and motivation to enhance cognitive reserve. In digital environments, participants faced heightened information overload [49], leading to health information burnout or anxiety [50], which deterred engagement in cognitive reserve enhancement activities. Diagnostic shame in PLWH with MND [51] may further exacerbate negative attitudes. Integrating knowledge-practice health education with the ‘Treating Future Disease’ theory [52] is recommended to shift these attitudes and promote a more positive understanding of the condition.

This study revealed a low risk perception of MND among most PLWH aged ≥ 50 years, limiting their intentions to enhance cognitive reserve and hindering intervention implementation [53]. Contributing factors included disease adaptation, which diminished awareness of severity, and knowledge gaps that prevented recognition of risk factors [54]. Multi-channel health education is recommended to strengthen risk perception in this group.

Disease and coping knowledge are crucial for fostering accurate health beliefs and informed medical decisions [55]. Interviews revealed that participants had limited understanding of cognitive reserve enhancement strategies. This knowledge gap arises from: (1) digital disabilities limiting access to information among older southwestern patients [56], particularly regarding online tools [57]; and (2) low cognitive dysfunction literacy [58] and baseline cognitive reserve deficits [59] in older PLWH. As a high-risk group for MND, these barriers hinder engagement in cognitive reserve enhancement. Therefore, improving MND knowledge is essential for enhancing cognitive reserve in this population.

Accelerated physical decline in participants significantly impedes cognitive reserve enhancement. This decline is driven by synergistic aging-HIV effects [60], HIV-related fatigue [61], and ART side effects (e.g., dizziness) [62]. Compared with the general population, this group faces elevated comorbidity risks and often lacks the physical capacity for reserve-building activities [63]. A sedentary lifestyle may become functionally necessary for cognitive preservation [64]. Post-adherence education increases awareness of immune fragility and safety sensitivity, potentially reflecting residual effects of “fear-based HIV education” [65]. Cognitive training, which requires mental engagement without physical limitations, may be crucial to overcoming these challenges.

Strengths and limitations

The key strengths of this study include the use of semi-structured interviews to identify patient-centered barriers and facilitators of cognitive reserve enhancement in high-risk older PLWH with MND. These insights help explain the population’s documented low cognitive reserve indices.

Limitations include: (1) geographic restriction to a single Chinese province, which, coupled with limited diversity in key sociodemographic factors (e.g., education, occupation) within our sample, may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations; (2) cultural and experiential barriers, along with an interview guide that may have prioritized fundamental domains over deeper exploration, potentially limiting the depth of discussions on cognitive reserve; and (3) the possible inclusion of respondents with undetected cognitive decline despite screening criteria, requiring caution in interpretation.

Conclusions

This study identified facilitators and barriers to cognitive reserve enhancement in adults aged ≥ 50 years with HIV-associated mild neurocognitive disorder. The findings highlight the need for tailored interventions targeting modifiable factors (e.g., illness perceptions, disclosure concerns), providing an evidence base for precision strategies to mitigate or delay cognitive decline in this vulnerable population.
