Connections and applications to other fields of mathematics have been an important feature of Ramsey theory from the very beginning. Ramsey himself had an application in mathematical logic in mind when he proved

Today, Ramsey-theoretic theorems and techniques are of fundamental importance in many different fields, including additive number theory, Banach space theory, discrete geometry, ergodic theory, group theory, and theoretical computer science. 2
The Erdős–Szekeres theorem and algorithm

In this section, we prove
Theorem

1.3
(and thus
Theorem

1.1
). This proof is elegant and interesting in its own right, and additionally it contains within it several of the important ideas used in the proof of
Theorem

1.5
. We will actually see three different proofs (or, more precisely, three different ways of viewing the same proof) of
Theorem

1.3
, in each of the next three subsections. Each proof will help introduce some of the key ideas that go into the proof of
Theorem

1.5
.

2.1
Off-diagonal Ramsey numbers

We begin with the original proof of
Erdős & Szekeres (1935)
.
Before proceeding with the proof, we generalize the notion of Ramsey numbers from
Definition

1.2
.
Here and throughout, we denote by
V(K_{N})
and
E(K_{N})
the vertex set and edge set, respectively, of the complete graph
K_{N}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Given integers
k,\ell\geqslant 2
, the
off-diagonal Ramsey number

r(k,\ell)
is the least integer
N
such that every two-coloring of
E(K_{N})
with colors red and blue contains a red
K_{k}
or a blue
K_{\ell}
.

Note that
r(k,\ell)=r(\ell,k)
as the colors play symmetric roles, and that
r(k)=r(k,k)
. The quantity
r(k)
is often called the
diagonal Ramsey number
.

With this terminology, we can prove
Theorem

1.3
. In fact, we will prove the following more precise result.

Theorem 2.2

(
Erdős & Szekeres, 1935
)

For all integers
k,\ell\geqslant 2
, we have

r(k,\ell)\leqslant\binom{k+\ell-2}{k-1}.

In particular,

r(k)\leqslant\binom{2k-2}{k-1}<4^{k}.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
k+\ell
, with the base case
\min\{k,\ell\}=2
being trivial. For the inductive step, the key claim is that the following inequality holds:

r(k,\ell)\leqslant r(k-1,\ell)+r(k,\ell-1).

(2.1)

To prove (
2.1
), fix a red/blue coloring of
E(K_{N})
, where
N=r(k-1,\ell)+r(k,\ell-1)
, and fix some vertex
v\in V(K_{N})
. Suppose for the moment that
v
is incident to at least
r(k-1,\ell)
red edges, and let
R
denote the set of endpoints of these red edges. By definition, as
\lvert R\rvert\geqslant r(k-1,\ell)
, we know that
R
contains a red
K_{k-1}
or a blue
K_{\ell}
. In the latter case we have found a blue
K_{\ell}
(so we are done), and in the former case we can add
v
to this red
K_{k-1}
to obtain a red
K_{k}
(and we are again done).

So we may assume that
v
is incident to fewer than
r(k-1,\ell)
red edges. By the exact same argument, just interchanging the roles of the colors, we may assume that
v
is incident to fewer than
r(k,\ell-1)
blue edges. But then the total number of edges incident to
v
is at most

(r(k-1,\ell)-1)+(r(k,\ell-1)-1)=N-2,

which is impossible, as
v
is adjacent to all
N-1
other vertices. This is a contradiction, proving (
2.1
).

We can now complete the induction. By (
2.1
) and the inductive hypothesis, we find that

\displaystyle r(k,\ell)

\displaystyle\leqslant r(k-1,\ell)+r(k,\ell-1)

\displaystyle\leqslant\binom{(k-1)+\ell-2}{(k-1)-1}+\binom{k+(\ell-1)-2}{k-1}

\displaystyle=\binom{k+\ell-2}{k-1},

where the final equality is Pascal’s identity for binomial coefficients.
∎

2.2
Enter the book

Definition 2.3
.

Let
t,m
be positive integers.
The
book graph

B_{t,m}
consists of a copy of
K_{t}
, plus
m
additional vertices which are adjacent to all vertices of the
K_{t}
, but not adjacent to one another. Equivalently,
B_{t,m}
is obtained from the complete bipartite graph
K_{t,m}
by adding in all the
\binom{t}{2}
possible edges in the side of size
t
. Equivalently,
B_{t,m}
consists of
m
copies of
K_{t+1}
which are glued along a common
K_{t}
.

Note that two important special cases are
m=1
, where
B_{t,1}
is simply the complete graph
K_{t+1}
, and
t=1
, where
B_{1,m}
is simply the star graph
K_{1,m}
, consisting of one vertex joined to
m
others (and no other edges).
The “book” terminology comes from the case
t=2
, in which case
B_{2,m}
consists of
m
triangles sharing an edge, which looks, to some extent, like a book with
m
triangular pages. Continuing this analogy, the
K_{t}
in
B_{t,m}
is called the
spine
, and the
m
additional vertices of
B_{t,m}
are called the
pages
. We will often denote a book as a pair of sets
(A,Y)
, where
A
is the spine and
Y
comprises the pages.

The reason book graphs are important in the study of Ramsey numbers comes down to the following simple observation.

Lemma 2.4

Suppose that a two-coloring of
E(K_{N})
contains a monochromatic red copy of
B_{t,m}
, where
m\geqslant r(k-t,\ell)
. Then this coloring contains a red
K_{k}
or a blue
K_{\ell}
.

Proof.

Let
A
be the spine of the book, and let
Y
be its pages. By assumption,
\lvert Y\rvert=m\geqslant r(k-t,\ell)
, so
Y
contains a blue
K_{\ell}
or a red
K_{k-t}
. In the former case we are done, and in the latter case, we may add
A
to the red
K_{k-t}
to obtain a red
K_{k}
.
∎

This proof should look familiar—we have already encountered the same idea in the proof of
Theorem

2.2
, where we implicitly used the
t=1
case of
Lemma

2.4
. Indeed, in that proof, we showed that if a coloring contains a red star with
r(k-1,\ell)
leaves, then it contains a red
K_{k}
or a blue
K_{\ell}
. The only new idea in
Lemma

2.4
is that we don’t need to consider a single vertex (i.e. the case
t=1
), but may take an arbitrary book.

Although the idea of
Lemma

2.4
basically goes back to the work of
Erdős & Szekeres (1935)
, it was first formulated in essentially this language by
Thomason (1982)
, who used
Lemma

2.4
to propose a natural approach to improving the upper bounds on
r(k)
. Namely, if one can show that every two-coloring of
E(K_{N})
contains a monochromatic
B_{t,m}
, for some appropriate parameters
t
and
m\geqslant r(k-t,k)
, then one can plug this into
Lemma

2.4
and conclude that
r(k)\leqslant N
. Again, this is essentially the approach we used in the proof of
Theorem

2.2
, where a simple argument based on the pigeonhole principle showed that any coloring of
E(K_{N})
contains a large monochromatic star, that is, a monochromatic book with many pages and a spine of size
t=1
. The idea behind Thomason’s program is that perhaps for larger values of
t
, more sophisticated arguments than the pigeonhole principle could yield stronger results, and improve the upper bounds on
r(k)
.

Thomason’s idea has been quite successful. The three prior asymptotic improvements to
Theorem

2.2
, due to
Thomason (1988)
;
Conlon (2009)
;
Sah (2023)
, all used this idea, roughly showing that if some two-coloring of
E(K_{N})
does
not
contain a monochromatic
B_{t,m}
(for some fixed
t,m
), then its structure must be such that the proof of
Theorem

2.2
can be made more efficient. A more precise structural result along these lines is given by
Conlon et al. (2022)
. However, for fundamental technical reasons, none of these techniques seems capable of finding books with spine larger than
t=O(\log k)
, whereas in order to prove a result like
Theorem

1.5
in this way, one would want to take (say)
t=\frac{k}{1000}
. This was where the matter stood for a few years, until the work of
Campos et al. (2023)
.

2.3
The Erdős–Szekeres algorithm

One of the many new ingredients introduced by
Campos et al. (2023)
is the following simple idea: rather than searching for some
specific
book
B_{t,m}
, they define an exploration algorithm for finding
some
book, and then prove that regardless of which book is found, the parameters involved are good enough to plug into
Lemma

2.4
. Although this idea is almost a triviality, this change of perspective is crucial for the proof of
Theorem

1.5
.

Before we define this exploration algorithm—which they termed the
book algorithm
—let us first rephrase the proof of
Theorem

1.3
as an exploration algorithm, the
Erdős–Szekeres algorithm
.
Let us fix a two-coloring of
E(K_{N})
. We assume that this coloring has no monochromatic
K_{k}
, and our goal is to eventually obtain a contradiction if
N
is sufficiently large. For the moment we only seek to get a contradiction if
N>4^{k}
, and thus reprove

1.3
.

For a vertex
v\in V(K_{N})

for the

denotes the
blue neighborhood
of

In the Erdős–Szekeres algorithm, we maintain three disjoint sets

A
and
B
will grow throughout the process, whereas
X
will shrink. The key property we maintain is that
(A,X)
is a red book, and
(B,X)
is a blue book; that is,
A
is completely red,
B
is completely blue, all edges between
A
and
X
are red, and all edges between
B
and
X
are blue. To initialize the process, we set
A=B=\varnothing
and
X=V(K_{N})
. We now repeatedly run the following steps.

Algorithm 2.5
(Erdős–Szekeres algorithm)

1.

If
\lvert X\rvert\leqslant 1
,
\lvert A\rvert\geqslant k
, or
\lvert B\rvert\geqslant k
, stop the process.

2.

Pick a vertex
v\in X
, and check whether
v
has at least
\frac{1}{2}(\lvert X\rvert-1)
red neighbors in
X
.

3.

If yes, move
v
to
A
and shrink
X
to the red neighborhood of
v
. That is, update
A\to A\cup\{v\}
and
X\to X\cap N_{R}(v)
, and keep
B
the same. Call this a
red step
.

4.

If not, then
v
has at least
\frac{1}{2}(\lvert X\rvert-1)
blue neighbors in
X
. We now move
v
to
B
, and shrink
X
to the blue neighborhood of
v
. That is, we update
B\to B\cup\{v\}
and
X\to X\cap N_{B}(v)
, and keep
A
the same. Call this a
blue step
.

5.

Return to step
1
.

By the way we update the sets, we certainly maintain the key property that

and
(B,X)
are red and blue books, respectively, throughout the entire process, since every time we add a vertex

to
A
(resp.

Suppose first that the algorithm terminated because

\alpha(p)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{\varepsilon}{k}&\text{if }p\leqslant\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{k},\\

Again, the point of this is that, if we are at some step of the process where

. The advantage of this is that the amount we

in a density-boost step is itself proportional to

. So if we have already done some number of density-boost steps, such that

, each subsequent density-boost boosts the density even further, at an exponential rate, thus rapidly bringing us closer to the threshold

.

where we plug in our choice of

This proof worked under the assumption that we remain throughout in the range

-\frac{\varepsilon}{k}&\text{when we do a red step,}\\
+\frac{\varepsilon}{k}\cdot\frac{1-\beta_{i}}{\beta_{i}}&\text{when we do the $i$th density-boost step.}\end{cases}

Recall that we had

\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{1-\beta_{i}}{\beta_{i}}\leqslant t+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}=t+o(k),

We have thus proved the desired inequality in the two extreme cases, namely when

throughout the process, and when

throughout the process. Of course, in reality, we may move between these two regimes multiple times during the execution of

4.1

The first new idea is to examine our criterion for deciding whether to do red or blue steps. Recall that, as in

5
The symmetric book algorithm

In this section, we describe the new proof that
r(k)\leqslant(4-\delta)^{k}
, due to
Balister et al. (2024)
. The main new ingredient in their proof is a lemma about the self-correlation of probability distributions in high-dimensional Euclidean space; this lemma can be used to introduce a new kind of density-boost step, which is much simpler to analyze. As such, their proof is also more conceptual, and does not rely on the verification of complicated numerical inequalities. In this section, we discuss their strategy and prove their bound on
r(k)
, while deferring the geometry, and hence the proof of the key lemma, to
Section

6
.

Balister et al. (2024)
introduce a modified book algorithm, which removes the asymmetry that was inherent to
Section

4.1
. Roughly speaking, the fact that the colors play symmetric roles is their new approach is the key reason why this argument generalizes to more than two colors, whereas the earlier one of
Campos et al., 2023
did not. However, for simplicity, we will continue to only work with two colors, as before.

5.1
The steps of the symmetric book algorithm

In the symmetric book algorithm of

, such that
(A,X\cup Y)
is a red book and

is a blue book. Thus, the only difference from the earlier setup is the new set

.
A

Assumption 5.1

At every step of the process, every vertex in

At the beginning of the process, we have

Lemma 5.3

(Refinement lemma)

Let
X,Y,Z
be disjoint sets of vertices in a coloring of
E(K_{N})
, and let
\alpha_{R},\alpha_{B}\in(0,1)
be parameters. There exists a vertex
v\in X
, a subset

, and a real number
\kappa\geqslant 0
with the following properties.

(a)

X^{\prime}
is not much smaller than
X
:
We have
\lvert X^{\prime}\rvert\geqslant c2^{-C\kappa}\lvert X\rvert
, where
C=6
and

are constants.

(b)

No density drop in either color:
Letting
Y^{\prime}=Y\cap N_{R}(v)
and
Z^{\prime}=Z\cap N_{B}(v)
, we have that

d_{R}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\geqslant d_{R}(X,Y)-\alpha_{R}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad d_{B}(X^{\prime},Z^{\prime})\geqslant d_{B}(X,Z)-\alpha_{B}.

(c)

Density boost in one color:
We have that

d_{R}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\geqslant d_{R}(X,Y)+(\kappa^{2}-1)\alpha_{R}\qquad\text{ or }\qquad d_{B}(X^{\prime},Z^{\prime})\geqslant d_{B}(X,Z)+(\kappa^{2}-1)\alpha_{B}.

In the symmetric book algorithm, we apply
Lemma

5.3
at every iteration, before deciding whether to take a red, blue, or density-boost step.
As before, the quantities
\alpha_{R},\alpha_{B}
determine how much we are willing to lose on the red and blue density, respectively. As such, we see that

is extremely helpful, and appears to completely eliminate the need for density-boost steps: every time we take a red or a blue step,

guarantees that the relevant density does not drop by more than our specified amount of
\alpha_{R}
or
\alpha_{B}
.

However, we cannot entirely dispense with density-boost steps. Indeed, every time we apply

5.3
, we shrink
X
to
X^{\prime}
, and we have little control over how much smaller
X^{\prime}
is than
X
: note that, crucially,

is an

is much smaller than

. This is where

comes in: in case

is very large, so that

is much smaller than

and one of

by

. As we will see, the trade-offs in this density-boost step work out in our favor; it is crucial here that

One final difference between the symmetric book algorithm and the book algorithm studied in

The two things that remain are to define

For sufficiently large
k
, we have

s_{R}\kappa_{R}\leqslant\frac{4t}{\kappa_{\mathrm{cutoff}}}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad s_{B}\kappa_{B}\leqslant\frac{4t}{\kappa_{\mathrm{cutoff}}}.

Additionally,

s_{R}\leqslant\frac{4t}{\kappa_{\mathrm{cutoff}}^{2}}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad s_{B}\leqslant\frac{4t}{\kappa_{\mathrm{cutoff}}^{2}}.

Proof.

Recall from (
5.2
) that
t=\eta k
for a fixed constant
\eta
. Hence, for sufficiently large
k
, the
t+o(k)
term in
Lemma

5.8
is at most
2t
. We henceforth assume that
k
is sufficiently large for this to hold. Next, we recall by step
4
of
Section

5.2
that
\kappa_{R,i}\geqslant\kappa_{\mathrm{cutoff}}
for all
i
, and that
\kappa_{\mathrm{cutoff}}\geqslant\sqrt{2}
by our choice in (

). Therefore,

2t\geqslant\sum_{i=1}^{s_{R}}(\kappa_{R,i}^{2}-1)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{s_{R}}\kappa_{R,i}^{2}\geqslant\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{cutoff}}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{s_{R}}\kappa_{R,i}=\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{cutoff}}}{2}\cdot(s_{R}\kappa_{R}),

where we use
Lemma

5.8
in the first inequality, that
x^{2}-1\geqslant\frac{1}{2}x^{2}
for
x\geqslant\sqrt{2}
in the second, that
\kappa_{R,i}\geqslant\kappa_{\mathrm{cutoff}}
in the third, and the definition of
\kappa_{R}
in the fourth. This, together with the analogous result for blue, yields the first claimed result.

The second claimed result then follows immediately if we use once more that
\kappa_{R}\geqslant\kappa_{\mathrm{cutoff}}
, and similarly for blue.
∎

Recall from
Lemma

5.7
that the total contribution of the density-boost steps to
\lvert X\rvert
is
c^{s_{R}+s_{B}}e^{-C(s_{R}\kappa_{R}+s_{B}\kappa_{B})}
. By

5.9
, both of these exponential terms can be bounded as

. This means that if
\kappa_{\mathrm{cutoff}}
is sufficiently large in terms of

and

—and our choice in (

) is indeed sufficiently large—then the contribution of the density-boost steps is negligible compared to the other exponential terms.

It is here that we see why it is crucial to obtain in
Lemma

5.3
a density boost which asymptotically dominates

. It is thanks to this that the proof of
Lemma

5.9
gives us a
\kappa_{\mathrm{cutoff}}
term in the denominator; thanks to this we can make the density-boost steps negligible by a sufficiently large choice of

. That is, if

only gave a density-boost of order

, this argument would not work. Luckily

boosts the density by a quadratic quantity in

Proof of the upper bound on
r(k)

Given our lemmas above on the evolution of the symmetric book algorithm, it is fairly straightforward to prove the following result, which implies that

5.2
always outputs a sufficiently large monochromatic book.

Lemma 5.10

Let

be sufficiently large, and let

for some integer

. Then

5.2
terminates by finding a monochromatic copy of

.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that at the end of the process, we have
\lvert X\rvert>1
and
\lvert Y\rvert,\lvert Z\rvert\geqslant m
. Indeed, if this is the case, then

5.2
can only terminate when one of
A
or

has size

B_{t,m}
with
Y
or

, respectively.

For the second claim, we have by

5.6
that
\lvert Y\rvert\geqslant 2^{-t-s_{R}-o(k)}N
. By

5.9
, we have that

since

by (

at the end of the process proceeds identically.

It remains to prove the lower bound on

5.7
. We estimate each of the quantities appearing in it in turn. First, recalling the definitions of

Next, by

6
High-dimensional geometry and the proof of Lemma
5.3

6.1
Reduction to a geometric statement

All that remains now is to prove
Lemma

5.3
, which is really the heart of the proof presented above. As previously mentioned,
Lemma

5.3
is a consequence of a purely geometric lemma, about probability distributions in high-dimensional Euclidean space. Before stating this geometric lemma, however, let us build up to it by stating a probabilistic strengthening of
Lemma

5.3
.

For a vertex
v\in X
, let us denote by
N_{Y}(v)
the red neighborhood of
v
in
Y
, that is,
N_{Y}(v)\coloneqq Y\cap N_{R}(v)
. Similarly,
N_{Z}(v)\coloneqq Z\cap N_{B}(v)
will denote the blue neighborhood of
v
in
Z
. With this notation, we now state the probabilistic strengthening of
Lemma

5.3
.

Lemma 6.1

Let
X,Y,Z
be disjoint sets of vertices in a coloring of
E(K_{N})
, and let
\alpha_{R},\alpha_{B}\in(0,1)
. Let
p_{R}=d_{R}(X,Y)
and
p_{B}=d_{B}(X,Z)
. Let
\bm{v},\bm{w}
be independent, uniformly random vertices of
X
. There exists a real number
\kappa\geqslant 0
such that

\pr\big(\lvert N_{Y}(\bm{v})\cap N_{Y}(\bm{w})\rvert\geqslant(p_{R}+(\kappa^{2}-1)\alpha_{R})p_{R}\lvert Y\rvert\\
\text{and}\qquad\lvert N_{Z}(\bm{v})\cap N_{Z}(\bm{w})\rvert\geqslant(p_{B}-\alpha_{B})p_{B}\lvert Z\rvert\big)\geqslant c2^{-C\kappa},

(6.1)

or else the same holds upon swapping the roles of red and blue and of
Y
and
Z
. Here,
c=1/8
and
C=6
are the same constants as in
Lemma

5.3
.

Note that
Lemma

6.1
is a more symmetric statement than
Lemma

5.3
: it discusses the common red and blue neighborhoods of a random pair of vertices in
X
, as opposed to isolating a special vertex
v\in X
as well as a special subset
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
. Nonetheless,
Lemma

5.3
follows from
Lemma

6.1
by a simple averaging argument.

Proof of
Lemma

5.3
, assuming
Lemma

6.1
.

Let us suppose that (
6.1
) holds;
the other case, where the roles of the colors are swapped, follows by a symmetric argument. First, by the law of total probability, there exists some fixed
v\in X
such that

\pr\big(\lvert N_{Y}(v)\cap N_{Y}(\bm{w})\rvert\geqslant(p_{R}+(\kappa^{2}-1)\alpha_{R})p_{R}\lvert Y\rvert\\
\text{and}\qquad\lvert N_{Z}(v)\cap N_{Z}(\bm{w})\rvert\geqslant(p_{B}-\alpha_{B})p_{B}\lvert Z\rvert\big)\geqslant c2^{-C\kappa},

(6.2)

This is the same inequality as (
6.1
), except that now only
\bm{w}
is random. As in the statement of
Lemma

5.3
, let
Y^{\prime}=N_{Y}(v)
and
Z^{\prime}=N_{Z}(v)
. Let
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
denote the set of all
w\in X
such that

\lvert N_{Y}(v)\cap N_{Y}(w)\rvert\geqslant(p_{R}+(\kappa^{2}-1)\alpha_{R})p_{R}\lvert Y\rvert\;\;\text{and}\;\;\lvert N_{Z}(v)\cap N_{Z}(w)\rvert\geqslant(p_{B}-\alpha_{B})p_{B}\lvert Z\rvert.

Then (
6.2
) is equivalent to the statement that
\lvert X^{\prime}\rvert\geqslant c2^{-C\kappa}\lvert X\rvert
, proving
5.3(a)
. Next, we have that

e_{R}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=\sum_{w\in X^{\prime}}\lvert N_{R}(w)\cap Y^{\prime}\rvert=\sum_{w\in X^{\prime}}\lvert N_{Y}(v)\cap N_{Y}(w)\rvert\geqslant\lvert X^{\prime}\rvert\cdot(p_{R}+(\kappa^{2}-1)\alpha_{R})p_{R}\lvert Y\rvert,

by the definition of
X^{\prime}
. Recalling that
\lvert Y^{\prime}\rvert=p_{R}\lvert Y\rvert
by
Assumption

5.1
, we find that

d_{R}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=\frac{e_{R}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})}{\lvert X^{\prime}\rvert\lvert Y^{\prime}\rvert}\geqslant p_{R}+(\kappa^{2}-1)\alpha_{R},

which proves
5.3(c)
and also implies the first bound in
5.3(b)
. Similarly, we can compute that

d_{B}(X^{\prime},Z^{\prime})\geqslant p_{B}-\alpha_{B},

which completes the proof of
5.3(b)
.
∎

It thus only remains to prove
Lemma

6.1
, which follows from a geometric argument. In order to convert the statement of
Lemma

6.1
to a geometric one, we simply observe that the size of the intersection of two sets can be encoded in a linear-algebraic way, as the inner product of their indicator vectors. Namely, let us denote by
\bm{1}_{Y}(v)\in\mathbb{R}^{Y}
the indicator vector of the set
N_{Y}(v)
; this is the vector whose
y
th coordinate equals
1
if
y\in N_{Y}(v)
, and
0
otherwise. Then we have the identity

\langle\bm{1}_{Y}(v),\bm{1}_{Y}(w)\rangle=\lvert N_{Y}(v)\cap N_{Y}(w)\rvert,

since the inner product of two binary vectors is precisely the number of coordinates in which they both have a
1
.

In order to precisely arrive at the setup of
Lemma

6.1
, we center and rescale the vectors
\bm{1}_{Y}(v)
. Namely, letting
\bm{1}
denote the all-ones vector, we define

\sigma_{Y}(v)\coloneqq\frac{\bm{1}_{Y}(v)-p_{R}\bm{1}}{\sqrt{\alpha_{R}p_{R}\lvert Y\rvert}}.

(6.3)

Note that by
Assumption

5.1
,
\bm{1}_{Y}(v)
has exactly
p_{R}\lvert Y\rvert
entries equal to
1
, hence by subtracting
p_{R}\bm{1}
we ensure that the sum of the entries in
\sigma_{Y}(v)
is zero. Thus, all the vectors
\{\sigma_{Y}(v):v\in X\}
lie on a sphere of radius
\sqrt{\frac{1-p_{R}}{\alpha_{R}}}
centered at the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{Y}
. Additionally, we can compute that for all
v,w\in X
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\sigma_{Y}(v),\sigma_{Y}(w)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha_{R}p_{R}\lvert Y\rvert}\langle\bm{1}_{Y}(v)-p_{R}\bm{1},\bm{1}_{Y}(w)-p_{R}\bm{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha_{R}p_{R}\lvert Y\rvert}\left(\langle\bm{1}_{Y}(v),\bm{1}_{Y}(w)\rangle-p_{R}\langle\bm{1}_{Y}(v),\bm{1}\rangle-p_{R}\langle\bm{1},\bm{1}_{Y}(w)\rangle+p_{R}^{2}\langle\bm{1},\bm{1}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha_{R}p_{R}\lvert Y\rvert}\left(\lvert N_{Y}(v)\cap N_{Y}(w)\rvert-p_{R}^{2}\lvert Y\rvert\right),

where we used
Assumption

5.1
in the final step, to conclude that
\langle\bm{1}_{Y}(v),\bm{1}\rangle=\langle\bm{1},\bm{1}_{Y}(w)\rangle=p_{R}\lvert Y\rvert
.
Note that if
N_{Y}(v),N_{Y}(w)
were random subsets of
Y
, each of size
p_{R}\lvert Y\rvert
, then their expected intersection size would be exactly
p_{R}^{2}\lvert Y\rvert
, hence the inner product
\langle\sigma_{Y}(v),\sigma_{Y}(w)\rangle
measures (up to scaling) how much their true intersection size deviates from this average value.
In particular, for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, we have that
\langle\sigma_{Y}(v),\sigma_{Y}(w)\rangle\geqslant\lambda
if and only if

\frac{1}{\alpha_{R}p_{R}\lvert Y\rvert}\left(\lvert N_{Y}(v)\cap N_{Y}(w)\rvert-p_{R}^{2}\lvert Y\rvert\right)\geqslant\lambda,

which in turn happens if and only if

\lvert N_{Y}(v)\cap N_{Y}(w)\rvert\geqslant\left(p_{R}+\lambda\alpha_{R}\right)p_{R}\lvert Y\rvert.

This shows that
Lemma

6.1
is a special case of the following geometric result of
Balister et al. (2024)
.

Theorem 6.2

(Geometric lemma)

Let
X,Y,Z
be finite sets, and let
\sigma_{Y}:X\to\mathbb{R}^{Y}
and
\sigma_{Z}:X\to\mathbb{R}^{Z}
be arbitrary functions. Let
\bm{v},\bm{w}
be independent, uniformly random elements of
X
. There exists a real number
\kappa\geqslant 0
such that

\pr\big(\langle\sigma_{Y}(\bm{v}),\sigma_{Y}(\bm{w})\rangle\geqslant\kappa^{2}-1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\langle\sigma_{Z}(\bm{v}),\sigma_{Z}(\bm{w})\rangle\geqslant-1\big)\geqslant c2^{-C\kappa},

(6.4)

or else the same holds upon swapping the roles of
Y
and
Z
.

Indeed, the statement of
Lemma

6.1
is precisely that of
Theorem

6.2
when one defines
\sigma_{Y}
as in (
6.3
) (and analogously for
\sigma_{Z}
). Hence it only remains to prove
Theorem

6.2
.

Before turning to the proof, a few remarks about
Theorem

6.2
are in order. First, note that the sets
Y
and
Z
play no role in the statement: we could equally well take
\sigma_{Y}
and
\sigma_{Z}
to be valued in the same high-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, or alternately in the infinite-dimensional Hilbert space
\ell^{2}
. Second, as we will see, the set
X
also does not really matter: the same statement holds for arbitrary coupled probability distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{Y}\times\mathbb{R}^{Z}
, and not only those distributions that are uniform on a finite support. Finally, and perhaps most importantly, we stress that the statement of
Theorem

6.2
is
not
scale-invariant, unlike many other results about inner products. That is, because the outcome of
Theorem

6.2
involves constants like
-1
, the statement of the lemma changes if we replace, say,
\sigma_{Y}
by
100\sigma_{Y}
: we cannot simply take the same value of
\kappa
upon such a rescaling. In fact, this lack of scale-invariance is crucial in the deduction of
Lemma

6.1
and hence
Lemma

5.3
: recall from (
6.3
) that we chose a careful scaling of
\sigma_{Y}
, in order to incorporate the parameter
\alpha_{R}
.

6.2
The one-color version of
Theorem

6.2

Theorem

6.2
is a “two-color” statement, in that it involves two functions
\sigma_{Y},\sigma_{Z}
, as is necessary for the application to two-color Ramsey numbers.
Balister et al. (2024, Lemma 3.1)
prove a more general result, which gives essentially the same outcome but for an arbitrary number of functions
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{q}
; this is, naturally, necessary for their application to upper bounds on multicolor Ramsey numbers. While we will continue to work in the two-color setting, the proof of
Theorem

6.2
is easier to understand if one begins by proving a simpler statement, which is the
one-color
version of this result.

Proposition 6.3

(One-color geometric lemma)

Let
X,Y
be finite sets, and let
\sigma:X\to\mathbb{R}^{Y}
be an arbitrary function. Let
\bm{v},\bm{w}
be independent, uniformly random elements of
X
. There exists a real number
\kappa\geqslant 0
such that

\pr\left(\langle\sigma(\bm{v}),\sigma(\bm{w})\rangle\geqslant\kappa^{2}-1\right)\geqslant\frac{1}{(\kappa^{2}+2)^{2}}.

Note that the quantitative dependencies in the one-color statement are much better: we obtain a
polynomial
relationship between the lower bound on the inner products and the success probability, rather than the exponential relationship appearing in
Theorem

6.2
. It remains open whether there is such a polynomial strengthening of
Theorem

6.2
; if there is, one would obtain a strengthened version of
Lemma

5.3
, which would in all likelihood lead to better upper bounds on diagonal Ramsey numbers.

In the proof of
Proposition

6.3
, we will use two simple and well-known observations. The first states that the expected inner product of independent, identically distributed random vectors is non-negative.

Lemma 6.4

Let
\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}
be independent, identically distributed random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, sampled from an arbitrary probability distribution. Then

\mathbb{E}[\langle\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}\rangle]\geqslant 0.

Proof.

By linearity of expectation and the independence of
\bm{\sigma}
and
\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}
, we have

\mathbb{E}[\langle\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}\rangle]=\langle\mathbb{E}[\bm{\sigma}],\mathbb{E}[\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}]\rangle.

\mathbb{E}[\bm{\sigma}]
is some fixed vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and as
\bm{\sigma}
and
\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}
are identically distributed, it equals
\mathbb{E}[\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}]
. Hence
\mathbb{E}[\langle\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}\rangle]
is simply the squared norm of some vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which is non-negative.
∎

The second well-known observation we need is an immediate consequence of Fubini’s theorem.

Lemma 6.5

For any random variable
\bm{V}
, we have

\mathbb{E}[\bm{V}]\leqslant\int_{0}^{\infty}\pr(\bm{V}\geqslant\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda.

Proof.

Let the underlying probability space of
\bm{V}
be
(\Omega,\mu)
. Then
\pr(\bm{V}\geqslant\lambda)=\int_{\Omega}\bm{1}_{\bm{V}(\omega)\geqslant\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\omega)
. Additionally, for any fixed real number
V
, we have
\int_{0}^{\infty}\bm{1}_{V\geqslant\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda
equals
V
if
V\geqslant 0
, and equals
0
otherwise. In particular, for all
V
, we have
\int_{0}^{\infty}\bm{1}_{V\geqslant\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\geqslant V
. Therefore, by Fubini’s theorem,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\pr(\bm{V}\geqslant\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}\bm{1}_{\bm{V}(\omega)\geqslant\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{\infty}\bm{1}_{\bm{V}(\omega)\geqslant\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\omega)

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{\Omega}\bm{V}(\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\omega)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\bm{V}].\qed

Proposition

6.3
is now a simple consequence of these two observations.

Proof of
Proposition

6.3
.

Let
\bm{V}=\langle\sigma(\bm{v}),\sigma(\bm{w})\rangle+1
, so that
\mathbb{E}[\bm{V}]\geqslant 1
by
Lemma

6.4
. By
Lemma

6.5
, we find that

1\leqslant\mathbb{E}[\bm{V}]\leqslant\int_{0}^{\infty}\pr(\bm{V}\geqslant\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda=\int_{-1}^{\infty}\pr(\langle\sigma(\bm{v}),\sigma(\bm{w})\rangle\geqslant\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda.

If we had that
\pr(\langle\sigma(\bm{v}),\sigma(\bm{w})\rangle\geqslant\lambda)\leqslant 1/(\lambda+3)^{2}
for all
\lambda\geqslant-1
, then we would conclude that

1\leqslant\int_{-1}^{\infty}\pr(\langle\sigma(\bm{v}),\sigma(\bm{w})\rangle\geqslant\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\leqslant\int_{-1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(\lambda+3)^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda=\frac{1}{2},

a contradiction. Therefore, there exists some
\lambda\geqslant-1
for which
\pr(\langle\sigma(\bm{v}),\sigma(\bm{w})\rangle\geqslant\lambda)\geqslant 1/(\lambda+3)^{2}
.
Substituting
\kappa=\sqrt{\lambda+1}
, we conclude that

\pr(\langle\sigma(\bm{v}),\sigma(\bm{w})\rangle\geqslant\kappa^{2}-1)\geqslant\frac{1}{(\kappa^{2}+2)^{2}},

as claimed.
∎

6.3
Proof of
Theorem

6.2

Although the proof of
Proposition

6.3
was very simple, it is helpful to reconsider it from a high-level view, as a very similar strategy will be used to prove
Theorem

6.2
. In the proof of
Proposition

6.3
, we considered the function
f(x)=x+1
, and then defined a random variable

\bm{V}\coloneqq f(\langle\sigma(\bm{v}),\sigma(\bm{w})\rangle).

The proof of
Proposition

6.3
relies on three important properties of this function
f
, although they are all so simple that they were not explicitly noted in the proof. First,
f
is non-positive on the region
\{x:x\leqslant-1\}
; this was implicitly used to restrict our attention to
\lambda\geqslant-1
, which ends up meaning that we obtain
\kappa\geqslant 0
. Second,
f
does not grow too fast, which was used to obtain a good trade-off between
\lambda
and the probability
\pr(\bm{V}\geqslant\lambda)
. Finally, and most importantly, we had the inequality
\mathbb{E}[\bm{V}]\geqslant 1
, which followed from
Lemma

6.4
.

In the proof of
Theorem

6.2
, we will pick a more complicated function
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, and use it to define a random variable
\bm{V}
by

\bm{V}\coloneqq f(\langle\sigma_{Y}(\bm{v}),\sigma_{Y}(\bm{w})\rangle,\langle\sigma_{Z}(\bm{v}),\sigma_{Z}(\bm{w})\rangle).

(6.5)

In addition to requiring
f
to satisfy analogues of the three properties discussed above, we also want the choice of
f
to allow us
to relate
\pr(\bm{V}\geqslant\lambda)

to the probability we wish to estimate in (
6.4
).
The main difficulty, of course, is that we now want to ensure that two inner products are simultaneously large.

To that end, we define

\bm{M}\coloneqq\max\{\langle\sigma_{Y}(\bm{v}),\sigma_{Y}(\bm{w})\rangle,\langle\sigma_{Z}(\bm{v}),\sigma_{Z}(\bm{w})\rangle\}

(6.6)

and let
\mathcal{E}
be the event

\mathcal{E}\coloneqq\left\{\langle\sigma_{Y}(\bm{v}),\sigma_{Y}(\bm{w})\rangle\geqslant-1\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\langle\sigma_{Z}(\bm{v}),\sigma_{Z}(\bm{w})\rangle\geqslant-1\right\}.

(6.7)

Then our first goal is to obtain a lower bound on
\pr(\{\bm{M}\geqslant\kappa^{2}-1\}\cap\mathcal{E})
, for some
\kappa
; such a lower bound can be directly converted to a lower bound on the probability in (
6.4
) (or on the analogous quantity upon interchanging the roles of
Y
and
Z
). As such, it is natural to try defining the random variable
\bm{V}
as
\bm{M}\bm{1}_{\mathcal{E}}
, since then the probability we are interested in is precisely of the form
\pr(\bm{V}\geqslant\lambda)
. The problem with this choice is that it is not clear how to lower-bound
\mathbb{E}[\bm{V}]
, or indeed why one should expect any non-trivial lower bound to hold. Indeed, while
\mathbb{E}[\bm{M}]\geqslant 0
by
Lemma

6.4
, it is possible that much of the positive contribution to
\mathbb{E}[\bm{M}]
is attained on the complement of
\mathcal{E}
.

Thus, in order for
\bm{V}
to “know about” the event
\mathcal{E}
, we will ensure that
f(y,z)\leqslant 0
if
y\leqslant-1
or
z\leqslant-1
.
This ensures that
\bm{V}
is non-positive on the complement of
\mathcal{E}
, so that bounds on
\pr(\bm{V}\geqslant\lambda)
can be converted to bounds on the quantity we are interested in,
\pr(\{\bm{M}\geqslant\kappa^{2}-1\}\cap\mathcal{E})
. Next, as before, we want a global upper bound on
f(y,z)
in terms of
y
and
z
, and we would like this upper bound to be “reasonable”: we will use this bound to convert information of the form
\bm{V}\geqslant\lambda
to information of form
\bm{M}\geqslant\kappa^{2}-1
, and we want good control on the dependencies between
\kappa
and
\lambda
. Finally, and most importantly, we want that
\mathbb{E}[\bm{V}]\geqslant 1
, so that we can apply the same argument as in the proof of
Proposition

6.3
.

This last condition seems like the most difficult one to satisfy, since we have very little information about the random variables
\langle\sigma_{Y}(\bm{v}),\sigma_{Y}(\bm{w})\rangle
and
\langle\sigma_{Z}(\bm{v}),\sigma_{Z}(\bm{w})\rangle
: if
f
is some complicated function of these variables, why should we expect
\mathbb{E}[\bm{V}]\geqslant 1
?

The way to ensure this is via a very simple but remarkably powerful trick, as follows. First, we will ensure that
f
is analytic with
f(0,0)=1
. We then consider the Taylor series of
f
, that is, we write
f(y,z)=\sum_{a,b\geqslant 0}r_{a,b}y^{a}z^{b}
, and apply analyticity and linearity of expectation to conclude that

\mathbb{E}[\bm{V}]=\sum_{a,b=0}^{\infty}r_{a,b}\mathbb{E}[\langle\sigma_{Y}(\bm{v}),\sigma_{Y}(\bm{w})\rangle^{a}\langle\sigma_{Z}(\bm{v}),\sigma_{Z}(\bm{w})\rangle^{b}].

The utility of this expansion comes from the following simple lemma, a
generalization of
Lemma

6.4
, which states that all the expectations appearing in the expression above are non-negative. Thus, a lower bound on
\mathbb{E}[\bm{V}]
follows immediately, as long as we ensure that
f
has only non-negative Taylor coefficients.

Lemma 6.6

Fix any probability distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{Y}\times\mathbb{R}^{Z}
, and let
(\bm{\sigma}_{Y},\bm{\sigma}_{Z})
and
(\bm{\sigma}_{Y}^{\prime},\bm{\sigma}_{Z}^{\prime})
be independent samples from this distribution. We have

\mathbb{E}[\langle\bm{\sigma}_{Y},\bm{\sigma}_{Y}^{\prime}\rangle^{a}\langle\bm{\sigma}_{Z},\bm{\sigma}_{Z}^{\prime}\rangle^{b}]\geqslant 0

for all non-negative integers
a,b
.

Proof.

Let
\bm{\sigma}
be a random vector in
(\mathbb{R}^{Y})^{\otimes a}\otimes(\mathbb{R}^{Z})^{\otimes b}
defined by

\bm{\sigma}=(\underbrace{\bm{\sigma}_{Y}\otimes\dots\otimes\bm{\sigma}_{Y}}_{a\text{ times}})\otimes(\underbrace{\bm{\sigma}_{Z}\otimes\dots\otimes\bm{\sigma}_{Z}}_{b\text{ times}}),

and define
\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}
analogously. Note that
\bm{\sigma}
and
\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}
are independent and identically distributed, and that

\langle\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}\rangle=\langle\bm{\sigma}_{Y},\bm{\sigma}_{Y}^{\prime}\rangle^{a}\langle\bm{\sigma}_{Z},\bm{\sigma}_{Z}^{\prime}\rangle^{b},

hence the result follows from
Lemma

6.4
.
∎

So all that remains is to pick a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
which is non-positive on the set
\{(y,z):y\leqslant-1\text{ or }z\leqslant-1\}
, which does not grow too quickly, and which is analytic with non-negative Taylor coefficients. The following lemma, whose proof is a simple calculus exercise that we omit, provides such a function.

Lemma 6.7

The function
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

f(y,z)\coloneqq 1+y(2+\cosh\sqrt{2z})+z(2+\cosh\sqrt{2y})

(6.8)

is non-positive on the set
\{(y,z):y\leqslant-1\text{ or }z\leqslant-1\}
, is analytic with non-negative Taylor coefficients, has
f(0,0)=1
, and satisfies

f(y,z)\leqslant e^{3\sqrt{\max\{y,z\}+1}}

(6.9)

for all
y,z\geqslant-1
.

With this, we have all of the ingredients in place to prove
Theorem

6.2
, following the strategy outlined above.

Proof of
Theorem

6.2
.

We define

\bm{V}\coloneqq f(\langle\sigma_{Y}(\bm{v}),\sigma_{Y}(\bm{w})\rangle,\langle\sigma_{Z}(\bm{v}),\sigma_{Z}(\bm{w})\rangle)

as in (
6.5
), where
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by (
6.8
). By
Lemma

6.6
, the fact that
f
is analytic with non-negative Taylor coefficients, and the fact that
f(0,0)=1
, we conclude that
\mathbb{E}[\bm{V}]\geqslant 1
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be the event defined in (
6.7
), and let
\overline{\mathcal{E}}
be its complement.
The fact that
f
is non-positive on the set
\{(y,z):y\leqslant-1\text{ or }z\leqslant-1\}
implies that on the event
\overline{\mathcal{E}}
, the random variable
\bm{V}
is non-positive. Therefore
\mathbb{E}[\bm{V}\bm{1}_{\overline{\mathcal{E}}}]\leqslant 0
, and hence

1\leqslant\mathbb{E}[\bm{V}]=\mathbb{E}[\bm{V}\bm{1}_{\mathcal{E}}]+\mathbb{E}[\bm{V}\bm{1}_{\overline{\mathcal{E}}}]\leqslant\mathbb{E}[\bm{V}\bm{1}_{\mathcal{E}}].

By
Lemma

6.5
, we now have

\displaystyle 1\leqslant\mathbb{E}[\bm{V}\bm{1}_{\mathcal{E}}]

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{0}^{\infty}\pr(\bm{V}\bm{1}_{\mathcal{E}}\geqslant\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda=\int_{0}^{\infty}\pr(\{\bm{V}\geqslant\lambda\}\cap\mathcal{E})\,\mathrm{d}\lambda.

On the interval
[0,1]
, the integrand is at most
\pr(\mathcal{E})
, so

1\leqslant\pr(\mathcal{E})+\int_{1}^{\infty}\pr(\{\bm{V}\geqslant\lambda\}\cap\mathcal{E})\,\mathrm{d}\lambda.

We now recall (
6.9
), which implies that on the event
\mathcal{E}
, we have
\bm{V}\leqslant e^{3\sqrt{\bm{M}+1}}
, hence

1-\pr(\mathcal{E})\leqslant\int_{1}^{\infty}\pr(\{\bm{V}\geqslant\lambda\}\cap\mathcal{E})\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\leqslant\int_{1}^{\infty}\pr\left(\left\{e^{3\sqrt{\bm{M}+1}}\geqslant\lambda\right\}\cap\mathcal{E}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda.

We now change variables to
\lambda=e^{3\kappa}
, so that
e^{3\sqrt{\bm{M}+1}}\geqslant\lambda
if and only if
\bm{M}\geqslant\kappa^{2}-1
, and conclude that

1-\pr(\mathcal{E})\leqslant\int_{0}^{\infty}\pr(\{\bm{M}\geqslant\kappa^{2}-1\}\cap\mathcal{E})\cdot 3e^{3\kappa}\,\mathrm{d}\kappa.

(6.10)

We now claim that for some
\kappa\geqslant 0
, we have

\pr(\{\bm{M}\geqslant\kappa^{2}-1\}\cap\mathcal{E})\geqslant\frac{e^{-4\kappa}}{4}.

(6.11)

Indeed, if this does not hold, then continuing (
6.10
), we conclude that

1-\pr(\mathcal{E})\leqslant\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-4\kappa}}{4}\cdot 3e^{3\kappa}\,\mathrm{d}\kappa=\frac{3}{4}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\kappa}\,\mathrm{d}\kappa=\frac{3}{4},

hence
\pr(\mathcal{E})\geqslant\frac{1}{4}
. But on the event
\mathcal{E}
we have
\bm{M}\geqslant-1
, hence (
6.11
) holds with
\kappa=0
, a contradiction. We conclude that, as claimed, (
6.11
) holds for some
\kappa\geqslant 0
.

Fixing such a
\kappa
, recalling the definitions of
\bm{M}
and
\mathcal{E}
from (
6.6
) and (
6.7
), and applying the union bound, we find that

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-4\kappa}}{4}

\displaystyle\leqslant\pr(\{\bm{M}\geqslant\kappa^{2}-1\}\cap\mathcal{E})

\displaystyle=\pr\big(\max\{\langle\sigma_{Y}(\bm{v}),\sigma_{Y}(\bm{w})\rangle,\langle\sigma_{Z}(\bm{v}),\sigma_{Z}(\bm{w})\rangle\}\geqslant\kappa^{2}-1\text{ and }

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\langle\sigma_{Y}(\bm{v}),\sigma_{Y}(\bm{w})\rangle\geqslant-1\text{ and }\langle\sigma_{Z}(\bm{v}),\sigma_{Z}(\bm{w})\rangle\geqslant-1\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\pr\big(\langle\sigma_{Y}(\bm{v}),\sigma_{Y}(\bm{w})\rangle\geqslant\kappa^{2}-1\text{ and }\langle\sigma_{Z}(\bm{v}),\sigma_{Z}(\bm{w})\rangle\geqslant-1\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt+\pr\big(\langle\sigma_{Z}(\bm{v}),\sigma_{Z}(\bm{w})\rangle\geqslant\kappa^{2}-1\text{ and }\langle\sigma_{Y}(\bm{v}),\sigma_{Y}(\bm{w})\rangle\geqslant-1\big).

Therefore, either

\pr\big(\langle\sigma_{Y}(\bm{v}),\sigma_{Y}(\bm{w})\rangle\geqslant\kappa^{2}-1\text{ and }\langle\sigma_{Z}(\bm{v}),\sigma_{Z}(\bm{w})\rangle\geqslant-1\big)\geqslant\frac{e^{-4\kappa}}{8}

or the same holds upon reversing the roles of
Y
and
Z
. This concludes the proof of
Theorem

6.2
upon recalling that
c=1/8
and
2^{-C}=1/64\leqslant e^{-4}
.
∎

7
Epilogue: Ramsey’s original proof of Theorem
1.1

As mentioned in
Section

1
, there is no known proof of
Theorem

1.1
that does not use book graphs in some way. As a hopefully fitting end to this exposé, let us see the original proof of
Ramsey (1929)
, which uses book graphs in a rather different way from
Lemma

2.4
, yet whose proof shares certain ideas with the ones we have already seen.

Let us denote by
r(B_{t,m})
the least integer
N
such that every two-coloring of
E(K_{N})
contains a monochromatic copy of
B_{t,m}
.
Ramsey (1929)
proved the following upper bound on
r(B_{t,m})
.

Theorem 7.1

(
Ramsey, 1929
)

r(B_{t,m})\leqslant(t+1)!\cdot m
for all
t,m\geqslant 1
.

Note that since
K_{k}=B_{k-1,1}
, this immediately implies the bound
r(k)\leqslant k!
, and hence yields a proof of
Theorem

1.1
.

Proof of
Theorem

7.1
.

We proceed by induction on
t
. For the base case
t=1
, we wish to prove that
r(B_{1,m})\leqslant 2m
, which is immediate: in any two-coloring of
E(K_{2m})
, any vertex is incident to
2m-1
edges, at least
m
of which must have the same color by the pigeonhole principle. This yields a monochromatic copy of
B_{1,m}
.

For the inductive step, suppose the result has been proved for
t-1
, and fix a coloring of
E(K_{N})
where
N=(t+1)!\cdot m=t!\cdot((t+1)m)
. By the inductive hypothesis, this coloring contains a monochromatic copy of
B_{t-1,(t+1)m}
, which we may assume to be red without loss of generality. That is, there exist disjoint sets
A,X\subseteq V(K_{N})
with
\lvert A\rvert=t-1
and
\lvert X\rvert=(t+1)m
, such that all edges inside
A
and between
A
and
X
are red. If there is a vertex
v\in X
with at least
m
red neighbors in
X
, we may perform a “red step” by updating
A\to A\cup\{v\}
and
X\to X\cap N_{R}(v)
, yielding a red
B_{t,m}
. So we may assume that every vertex in
X
has at most
m-1
red neighbors in
X
. Let
v_{1}
be an arbitrary vertex of
X
, and let
X_{1}=N_{B}(v_{1})\cap X
, so that
\lvert X_{1}\rvert\geqslant\lvert X\rvert-m
. Let
v_{2}
be an arbitrary vertex of
X_{1}
, and let
X_{2}=N_{B}(v_{2})\cap X_{1}
, so that
\lvert X_{2}\rvert\geqslant\lvert X_{1}\rvert-m\geqslant\lvert X\rvert-2m
. Continuing in this way, we may build a sequence of vertices
v_{1},\dots,v_{t}
as well as a set
X_{t}
with
\lvert X_{t}\rvert\geqslant\lvert X\rvert-tm=m
, such that each
v_{i}
is adjacent in blue to all
v_{j}
with
j>i
, as well as to all vertices in
X_{t}
. But this precisely means that we have constructed a blue
B_{t,m}
, completing the inductive step.
∎

Given
Theorem

7.1
, it is natural to wonder what the true value of
r(B_{t,m})
is. This question was first explicitly raised by
Erdős et al. (1978)
;
Thomason (1982)
, who independently proved the bounds
(2^{t}-o(1))m\leqslant r(B_{t,m})\leqslant 4^{t}m
. Thomason in particular was motivated by
Lemma

2.4
, as discussed in
Section

2.2
, and made the following bold conjecture.

Conjecture 7.2

(
Thomason, 1982
)

r(B_{t,m})\leqslant 2^{t}(m+t-2)+2
for all
m,t\geqslant 1
.

This conjecture is known to be true (and optimal) for
t\in\{1,2\}
, but it is wide open for
t\geqslant 3
(and may well be false). Moreover,
Conjecture

7.2
is likely to be very difficult: even the
m=1
case would yield
r(k)\leqslant 2^{k+o(k)}
, a bound far stronger than anything currently known. However, a beautiful result of
Conlon (2019)
confirms this conjecture asymptotically for any fixed
t
.

Theorem 7.3

(
Conlon, 2019
)

r(B_{t,m})=(2^{t}+o(1))m
as
m\to\infty
, for any fixed
t
.

Conlon’s result addresses a question that arguably goes back 90 years to the original work of
Ramsey (1929)
, yet uses highly sophisticated tools developed in the interim, such as the regularity lemma of
Szemerédi (1978)
, and this question is also closely related to a famous conjecture of
Burr & Erdős (1975)
, which was recently resolved by
Lee (2017)
. In fact, Ramsey theory has seen a number of recent breakthroughs on old, seemingly intractable problems by the introduction of remarkable new techniques: three other examples from the past two years are the works of
Li (2023)
on explicit constructions, of
Mattheus & Verstraete (2024)
on off-diagonal Ramsey numbers, and of
Reiher & Rödl (2023)
on restricted Ramsey graphs. There is every reason to hope and expect this trend to continue.
