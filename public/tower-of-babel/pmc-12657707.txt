Identification of potential bladder cancer drug targets through Mendelian randomization and molecular docking
Authors: Chengwei Bi, Kexun Li, Yong Yang, Yuanlong Shi, Guoying Zhang, Bing Zhao, Yuanpeng Duan, Yapeng Xing, Wei Luo, Libo Yang, Yu Zhang, Yunchao Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBladder cancer is a common and lethal malignancy with high treatment costs due to frequent recurrence and prolonged therapy. Despite therapeutic advances, many patients develop drug-resistant metastatic disease, underscoring the need for new treatments. Using the STROBE-MR guidelines, we performed Mendelian randomization with cis- expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) from the eQTLGen Consortium (n = 31,684) as exposures. Two independent cohorts served as discovery and replication sets for target identification. Drug prediction and molecular docking were applied to validate the targets. Twelve significant drug targets were identified. Phenome-wide association study revealed additional associations of NDST1 and HMGCR with proteomic traits and health service use, respectively. Molecular docking confirmed strong binding for available protein structures. Our study identified 12 promising drug targets for bladder cancer and suggests that MR-based prioritization may improve clinical trial success and reduce development costs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03929-7.Keywords: Bladder cancer, Mendelian randomization, Drug targets, Genetics

Introduction

Bladder cancer (BC) is the most prevalent malignancy of the urinary tract and ranks as the ninth most commonly diagnosed cancer worldwide. In 2022, approximately 614,000 new cases and 220,000 deaths were reported. The burden and incidence of this disease are significantly higher in males than in females [1]. Urothelial carcinoma accounts for approximately 90% of BC cases and is characterized by a high incidence and postoperative recurrence rate [2]. Current standard therapies often yield unsatisfactory outcomes. First-line platinum-based chemotherapy for advanced or metastatic disease offers only 9–15 months of overall survival and is frequently limited by intolerable side effects [3–5]. For patients diagnosed with muscle-invasive BC, the standard treatment protocol generally includes cisplatin-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by radical cystectomy [6–9]. However, despite this multimodal treatment strategy, patient outcomes remain suboptimal for many individuals. Therefore, identifying novel drug targets for the treatment of BC has important clinical significance and can provide theoretical support for the development of new therapeutic strategies.

Identifying drug targets through genetic methods can significantly enhance the efficiency of drug development while conserving both human and material resources [10, 11]. Proteins encoded by druggable genes have emerged as viable targets for drugs, small molecules, and monoclonal antibodies, and their application has become widespread [12, 13]. Consequently, the development of drug targets informed by genetic evidence represents a more effective strategy for drug discovery.

Mendelian randomization (MR) uses genetic variation as instrumental variables (VIs) to infer causal relationships between exposures (e.g., biomarkers or drug targets) and outcomes [14, 15]. Unlike observational studies, MR reduces confounding from environmental and self-selection biases, as genetic variants are randomly assorted at conception. Variants in drug target gene regions can influence expression and function, thereby mimicking drug effects. Thus, in drug target MR, such variation serves as a proxy for target intervention to estimate its causal effect on disease [16].

In this study, we combined cis-expression quantitative trait loci (cis-eQTL) data with BC risk associations to infer causal effects within an MR framework. Using large-scale genome-wide association study (GWAS) datasets, we identified therapeutic targets for BC and further evaluated their pharmacological potential through drug prediction and molecular docking. This integrative approach leverages genetic causality and computational methods to prioritize targets with improved clinical translatability.

Materials and methods

Exposure data

The eQTLs data were obtained from the eQTLGen Consortium (https://eqtlgen.org/). Methylated quantitative trait loci (mQTL) data were derived from the study by McRae et al., which investigated SNP-CpG associations in the blood of individuals of European ancestry. This dataset included 31,684 individuals, 16,987 genes, and 31,684 cis-eQTLs, predominantly from healthy European participants [17, 18]. The compilation of druggable genes used in this study originated from a survey employing a methodology that integrates extensive GWAS data. This computational approach was designed to identify druggable proteins and link them to established drugs, yielding a total of 4,463 druggable genes [13].

Outcome data

The BC discovery cohort from the GWAS Catalogue (ukb-d-C67) comprised 361,194 European individuals (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/). The validation dataset was derived from the December 2023 release of FinnGen Release 10 (https://www.finngen.fi/en) [19], which included 2,193 cases and 314,193 controls.

Mendelian randomisation analysis

The study was designed according to the STROBE-MR guidelines to ensure that each step of the MR analysis was completed (Table 2). MR analyses were conducted using the R package TwoSampleMR (version 0.5.6) [20]. To ensure the validity of causal estimates, three fundamental assumptions must be satisfied when performing MR the exposure is directly associated with genetic variation. The F-statistic was employed to assess the strength of the IV exposure associations; F-statistics greater than 10 were considered sufficiently robust to minimize weak IV bias [21, 22]. (2) There is no genetic correlation between the exposure and the outcome that could act as a confounding variable; if genetic variation is associated with such confounders, it cannot serve as a valid IV [23]. (3) Genetic variation does not influence the outcome through pathways that are unrelated to the exposure [24]. Several quality-control measures were implemented in this study. First, the exposure and outcome groups consisted almost exclusively of individuals of European ancestry, thereby minimizing potential bias related to population stratification. When genetic associations between SNPs were identified, the reference panel from the 1000 Genomes Project was used to exclude correlated single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) within the thresholds of r2 < 0.2 and 10,000 kb, thereby retaining only the most significant SNPs (those with the smallest p-values) to ensure independence. Finally, genes with SNP variance greater than that of the exposure variant were excluded from the analysis through the application of Steiger filtering.

For genes with multiple instruments in the primary analysis, SNP estimates were analyzed using inverse-variance weighted (IVW), weighted median, and MR-Egger methods. Wald ratio estimates were calculated for each SNP. The IVW method was the most statistically powerful under the assumption that all instruments were valid and was used as the primary estimator when results from multiple MR methods were inconsistent [25]. When the estimation directions were consistent across the three methods, this indicated that the IVW estimates were unlikely to be biased by pleiotropy. For genes with more than two instruments, the MR-Egger intercept test was applied to detect the presence of horizontal pleiotropy, and results were handled consistently across discovery and replication cohorts. Cochran's Q statistic was calculated using both the IVW and MR-Egger methods to assess heterogeneity in Wald ratio estimates, with results applied uniformly to exclude heterogeneous genes in both cohorts. To control for false-positive results due to multiple testing, sensitivity analyses were performed using the Bonferroni correction to adjust significance thresholds. This correction was applied uniformly in the discovery cohort, while a nominal p < 0.05 was required in the replication cohort to confirm exploratory findings and maintain consistency across analyses. applied uniformly in the discovery cohort, while a nominal p < 0.05 was required in the replication cohort to confirm exploratory findings and maintain consistency across analyses.

Phenome-wide association analysis

We conducted a phenome-wide association study (PheWAS) using the AstraZeneca PheWAS portal (https://azphewas.com/) [26] to further investigate the horizontal pleiotropy of potential drug targets and their associated side effects. To mitigate the risk of false positives, multiple testing corrections were applied, and a significance threshold of 5 × 10⁻4 was used..

Enrichment analysis

To investigate the functional characteristics and biological relevance of the identified therapeutic target genes, enrichment analyses were conducted using Gene Ontology (GO) and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) through the R package clusterProfiler [27]. GO includes three categories: biological process, molecular function, and cellular component. KEGG pathways provide insights into metabolic and signaling pathways.

Protein interaction network construction

To obtain a comprehensive understanding of the potential interactions between drug targets, we constructed a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network using the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) online platform(https://string-db.org/). The PPI results were visualized with Cytoscape [28]. Additionally, GeneMANIA (https://genemania.org/) was employed for complementary PPI analysis [29, 30].

Candidate drug prediction and molecular docking

The Drug Signatures Database (DSigDB) is a comprehensive resource comprising 22,527 gene sets and 17,389 distinct compounds, encompassing 19,531 genes. It connects drugs and other chemicals to their corresponding target genes (http://dsigdb.tanlab.org/DSigDBv1.0/) [31]. The identified target genes were uploaded to DSigDB, and drug candidates were predicted to evaluate the pharmacological activity of the targets.

To better characterize the interactions between drug candidates and their targets, molecular docking was performed. Structural data for the drug candidates and target proteins were obtained from the PubChem Compound Database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and the Protein Data Bank (http://www.rcsb.org/) [32], respectively. Prior to docking, all water molecules were removed from the protein and ligand structures, and polar hydrogen atoms were added. AutoDock Tools 1.5.6 was used to generate PDBQT files for docking simulations. Molecular docking was conducted using AutoDock Vina 1.2.2 (http://autodock.scripps.edu/). Results

Two hundred and ten genes causally significantly associated with BC risk during the discovery phase

As illustrated in Fig. 2, the expression of 210 genes in the discovery cohort was causally associated with BC risk. Figure 3 shows the 44 genes that had p-values ≤ 0.01. However, in sensitivity analyses, 13 genes (SERPINE1, TRIB1, MSX2P1, CCHCR1, PSEN1, WFDC3, TTC32, SENP6, PCGF3, OR2A9P, BIN3, B4GALT3, and IDH2) did not show consistent directions of effect across all three methods (Online Resource 1: Table S1). All genes passed horizontal pleiotropy tests (p < 0.05, Online Resource 1: Table S2). Heterogeneity tests (Online Resource 1: Table S3) indicated that CD300E, FAM53A, and MS4A4A SNPs were heterogeneous. These 16 genes were therefore excluded from subsequent analyses.

Replication phase 12 genes remain significant in an independent BC cohorts

In the replication phase, GWAS data from the FinnGen database were used, comprising 2,193 cases and 314,193 controls. MR analyses were conducted in the same manner as in the discovery cohort. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the causal association between genetically predicted expression of 12 genes and BC risk was confirmed using either the Wald ratio or IVW methods. When sensitivity analyses, pleiotropy, and heterogeneity analyses were performed, none of the 12 genes showed inconsistent directions of effect across the three methods or evidence of heterogeneity (Online Resource 1: Table S4–S6). Therefore, all 12 genes were retained for subsequent analyses.

Forest plots displaying the findings from the replication phase for 12 significant genes

PheWAS

To further evaluate whether the 12 potential drug target genes could have beneficial or adverse effects on other traits, and to investigate potential pleiotropy not detected by the MR-Egger intercept test, we analyzed 17,361 dichotomous phenotypes and 1,419 quantitative phenotypes from the AstraZeneca PheWAS Portal at the genetic level. The PheWAS results can be interpreted as associations between genetically determined protein expression and specific diseases or traits. As shown in Online Resource 1 (Figures S1–S24), in addition to continuous traits, NDST1 and HMGCR showed weak associations with OLINK proteomics and health service use, respectively. The remaining 10 genes did not exhibit associations with either continuous or binary traits, and NDST1 and HMGCR were not associated with binary traits. Consequently, all 12 genes were retained for subsequent analyses.

Enrichment analysis

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted to explore the functional roles of the identified target genes. As shown in Fig. 5, the GO analysis revealed significant enrichment in biological processes including isoprenoid metabolic process, regulation of microtubule cytoskeleton organization, skeletal system morphogenesis and regulation of microtubule-based process. For cellular components, the genes were enriched in the trans-Golgi network membrane, Golgi apparatus subcompartment, and synaptic cleft. Molecular functions included oxidoreductase activity acting on the CH–OH group of donors, protein kinase regulator activity, and heparan sulfate sulfotransferase activity.

GO enrichment results for three terms

KEGG pathway analysis (Fig. 6) indicated significant involvement in the AMPK signaling pathway, terpenoid backbone biosynthesis, glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis–heparan sulfate/heparin, human papillomavirus infection, and mucin-type O-glycan biosynthesis. These enriched terms and pathways suggest potential roles for the target genes in cancer-related processes such as metabolism, signal transduction, and extracellular matrix remodeling.

Cell-type specificity expression in the BC tissues

To further investigate the cell type–specific enrichment of the 12 drug target genes, we used single-cell RNA sequencing data to analyze gene expression in BC tissues. All cells were categorized into 14 clusters and subsequently grouped into eight major cellular subsets: B cells, dendritic cells, endothelial cells, epithelial cells, fibroblasts, macrophages, neutrophils, and T cells (Fig. 7a). Eight of the 12 drug target genes were detected in BC tissues. Notably, SYS1, RDH13, NAPG, LAMA5, and HMGCR were predominantly enriched in malignant epithelial cell populations (Fig. 7b).

PPI networks

We imported the 12 drug target genes into the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) to generate a PPI network and visualized the results using Cytoscape. Figure 8 shows the interactions of the 12 drug targets with other proteins in a network comprising 60 nodes and 547 edges.

For the PPI network constructed using GeneMANIA (https://genemania.org/), in addition to the 12 drug targets, 20 additional potentially interacting genes were included, forming 494 interaction links (Fig. 9). Functional enrichment analysis of the network revealed significant involvement in seven biological processes: sterol biosynthetic process, secondary alcohol biosynthetic process, cholesterol metabolic process, sterol metabolic process, secondary alcohol metabolic process, alcohol biosynthetic process, and steroid biosynthetic process.

The results indicated that minocycline and mometasone were the two most significant compounds, associated with DIXDC1 and HMGCR, respectively. Molecular docking

To further evaluate the druggability of the candidate target genes, molecular docking was performed at the atomic level to assess the binding energy and interaction modes between the drug candidates and their target proteins. By analyzing docking results to identify ligands with high binding affinity, drug targets can be prioritized for experimental validation and the design of potential therapeutic compounds can be optimized. In this study, AutoDock Vina 1.2.2 (http://autodock.scripps.edu/), a protein–ligand docking software, was used to dock the two top-ranked compounds to the proteins encoded by the corresponding target genes. Molecular docking revealed that minocycline had binding energies of − 7.5 kcal/mol with both DIXDC1 and HMGCR, and mometasone showed binding energies of − 8.3 kcal/mol with DIXDC1 and –7.7 kcal/mol with HMGCR, indicating highly stable binding (Fig. 10).

Discussion

In this study, a range of MR methods—including the Wald ratio, IVW method, MR-Egger, weighted median method, multiplicity tests, and Cochran’s Q heterogeneity test—were applied to identify 12 drug target genes for BC. Enrichment analyses and PPI networks were performed to elucidate the biological significance of these targets, and PheWAS were conducted to examine potential pleiotropic effects. Finally, candidate drugs corresponding to these targets were predicted and evaluated through molecular docking. Collectively, these findings have important implications for drug discovery in BC and may contribute to the development of new therapeutic strategies.

Research indicates that members of the FAM53 protein family contribute significantly to tumor development, particularly through interactions with transcriptional regulators that influence cell proliferation [33]. LAMA5, a member of the laminin protein family, has been identified as a potential inducer of epithelial–mesenchymal transition markers and the Notch signaling pathway. Furthermore, LAMA5 is closely associated with various tumors, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target in oncology [34, 35]. CCNA1 is a key regulator of the G1/S and G2/M transitions, playing a crucial role in cell cycle progression [36, 37]. Overexpression of CCNA1 has been linked to poor prognosis in multiple cancers, including bladder urothelial carcinoma, ovarian cancer, and head and neck cancer [38–40]. It has also been proposed as a diagnostic marker for thyroid cancer [41]. DIXDC1 functions as a positive regulator of the Wnt signaling pathway, acting downstream of Wnt and upstream of Axin [42]. Given that aberrant Wnt pathway activation is implicated in various cancers, DIXDC1 has emerged as a key focus in oncology research. Several studies have identified DIXDC1 as a potential target for pan-cancer therapies [43–45]. GALNT14, an O-glycosylation–initiating enzyme, has been shown to play an important role in the proliferation, migration, and metastasis of cancer cells [46]. GALNT14 not only regulates multidrug resistance in breast cancer cells but also serves as a prognostic marker for neuroblastoma and non-small-cell lung cancer. Furthermore, it has been proposed as a predictive marker for Apo2L/TRAIL-based anticancer therapy [47, 48]. These results indicate that the drug targets for BC identified in this study are closely associated with the disease, possess therapeutic potential, and hold promise for drug development. Molecular docking provided evidence of strong drug ability, as indicated by low binding energies (–7.5 to − 8.3 kcal/mol, comparable to established benchmarks for stable interactions), likely involving hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic contacts as inferred from protein–ligand interaction maps.

The present study offers several important advantages in the context of emerging MR research in BC. While recent MR studies have identified potential risk factors and circulating biomarkers associated with BC risk [49, 50], our work uniquely focuses on the systematic identification and validation of drug targets, This justifies the use of blood-derived cis-eQTL data as a proxy for bladder tissue dynamics, given shared genetic regulation across tissues and the role of systemic factors such as inflammation in bladder tumorigenesis. Second, the study integrates eQTL and mQTL data to capture both transcriptional and epigenetic regulatory layers relevant to bladder cancer biology, including immune-mediated pathways and methylation-driven gene silencing. Third, this is the first investigation to identify drug targets for BC through MR analysis, leveraging the largest collection of publicly available BC risk GWAS data to date. In addition, we validated the findings using another large cohort dataset. Only genes significant in both GWAS cohorts were considered as drug target genes, thereby confirming the robustness of the results and reducing the likelihood of false positives. The drug target genes identified through this method may facilitate screening for compounds with favorable therapeutic effects, potentially improving clinical trial success rates. We then screened drugs based on these therapeutic targets and identified the top candidates. The final drug prediction results highlighted the therapeutic potential of these genes, and molecular docking analyses demonstrated their strong drug ability, with minocycline and mometasone prioritized for their lowest adjusted p-values. Compounds such as cerebrosterol, which interact with multiple genes, warrant future docking analyses with additional energy comparisons and control benchmarks. This study, encompassing a comprehensive evaluation from genetic identification to drug binding properties, presents 12 drug targets for BC, 10 of which are supported by substantial prior evidence [46, 51–64], PheWAS revealed minimal pleiotropy (e.g., no binary trait associations for 10 genes at p < 5 × 10⁻4), and single-cell RNA sequencing showed enrichment in epithelial cells (e.g., 5 genes predominantly expressed), together supporting functional roles in BC such as cell cycle regulation and immune modulation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged, including the handling of pleiotropy and heterogeneity, which were rigorously addressed through uniform application of MR-Egger and Cochran’s Q tests across cohorts to strengthen robustness. First, the eQTL analyses included individuals of non-European ancestry, whereas the BC cohorts consisted solely of Europeans. Although statistical thresholds were consistently applied to minimize bias, this difference in population background may have introduced potential bias in the estimation of MR effects. Future studies should therefore validate these findings in individuals with diverse genetic backgrounds to enhance their applicability to broader patient populations. Second, although MR provides valuable insights into causality, it cannot fully replicate the outcomes of clinical trials or completely predict drug effects [65]. Future studies should examine regulatory factors beyond cis-eQTLs, as incorporating additional factors may offer a more comprehensive understanding of disease complexity and may identify novel, therapeutically effective targets. Finally, although molecular docking predicts potential interactions between candidate drugs and targets, it does not guarantee their clinical effectiveness. Thus, the feasibility of these interactions should be validated through further in vitro and in vivo experiments.
