Generalization error bound for denoising score matching under relaxed manifold assumption
Konstantin Yakovlev, Nikita Puchkin

Abstract
We examine theoretical properties of the denoising score matching estimate. We model the density of observations with a nonparametric Gaussian mixture. We significantly relax the standard manifold assumption allowing the samples step away from the manifold. At the same time, we are still able to leverage a nice distribution structure. We derive non-asymptotic bounds on the approximation and generalization errors of the denoising score matching estimate. The rates of convergence are determined by the intrinsic dimension. Furthermore, our bounds remain valid even if we allow the ambient dimension grow polynomially with the sample size.

Introduction

Denoising diffusion probabilistic models
(
Song and Ermon, 2019
;
Song et al., 2020
)
provide a state-of-the art tool for generating high-quality
data including images, audio and video synthesis
(
Dhariwal and Nichol, 2021
;
Ho et al., 2022
;
Kong et al., 2021
)
. It is based on an insight from random processes theory that the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process

{\mathrm{d}}X_{t}=-X_{t}\,{\mathrm{d}}t+\sqrt{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}W_{t},\quad 0\leqslant t\leqslant T,

(1)

with an initial condition
X_{0}\sim\mathsf{p}_{0}^{*}
admits an inverse one

{\mathrm{d}}Z_{t}=\left(Z_{t}+2\nabla\log\mathsf{p}_{T-t}^{*}(Z_{t})\right){\mathrm{d}}t+\sqrt{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}B_{t},\quad Z_{0}\sim\mathsf{p}_{T}^{*}.

(2)

Here
W_{t}
and
B_{t}
are independent Wiener processes in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
and
\mathsf{p}_{t}^{*}
is the density of
X_{t}
,
t\in[0,T]
. Since the diffusion (
1
) converges to the standard Gaussian distribution
{\mathcal{N}}(0,I_{D})
quite fast (see, e.g.,
(
Bakry et al., 2014
, Section 4.1)
), one can model the initial density
\mathsf{p}_{0}^{*}
running the inverse process (
2
) with
Z_{0}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,I_{D})
. The problem is that the
score function

s^{*}(y,t)=\nabla\log\mathsf{p}_{t}^{*}(y),\quad(y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\times[0,T],

is unknown and should be estimated from i.i.d. samples
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}\sim\mathsf{p}_{0}^{*}
. In practice, one usually replaces (
2
) with

{\mathrm{d}}\widehat{Z}_{t}=\left(\widehat{Z}_{t}+2\,\widehat{s}(\widehat{Z}_{t},T-t)\right){\mathrm{d}}t+\sqrt{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}B_{t},\quad\widehat{Z}_{0}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,I_{D}),

(3)

where
\widehat{s}(y,t)
is an estimate of
s^{*}(y,t)
. Recently, a lot of researchers (for instance,
Bortoli et al. (2021)
;
De Bortoli (2022)
;
Lee et al. (2023)
;
Chen et al. (2023a)
;
Chen et al. (2023c)
;
Chen et al. (2023d)
;
Benton et al. (2024)
;
Li and Yan (2024)
to name a few) considered generative diffusion models through the lens of Markov processes theory. As one should have expected, the rates of convergence of the density of
\widehat{Z}_{T-t_{0}}
, where
t_{0}>0
is a fixed number referred to as
stopping time
, to the target distribution obtained in those papers heavily depend on the accuracy of estimation of
s^{*}(y,t)
.
For this reason, the problem of score estimation attracted attention of many statisticians.

In
(
Sriperumbudur et al., 2017
;
Wibisono et al., 2024
;
Zhang et al., 2024
)
, the authors tackled this problem via widespread tools from nonparametric statistics. Namely,
Sriperumbudur et al. (2017)
considered an infinite-dimensional exponential family of probability densities, which are parametrized by functions in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space, while
Wibisono et al. (2024)
and
Zhang et al. (2024)
used kernel smoothing. On the other hand,
Oko et al. (2023)
studied theoretical properties of the denoising score matching estimate (see Section
2
for the definition), which is often used in practice. The authors took a class of feed-forward neural networks with ReLU activations as a class of admissible scores. Under the condition that
\mathsf{p}_{0}^{*}
is supported on the cube
[-1,1]^{D}
and bounded away from zero on this set,
Oko et al. (2023)
derived approximation and generalization error bounds. Unfortunately, the rates of convergence in
Sriperumbudur et al. (2017)
;
Wibisono et al. (2024)
;
Zhang et al. (2024)
;
Oko et al. (2023)
deteriorate extremely fast as the dimension
D
grows and are not applicable in real-world scenarios.

At the same time, in various applications the data distribution has a nice low-dimensional structure
(
Bengio et al., 2013
;
Pope et al., 2021
)
despite its high-dimensional representation. For this reason, one can hope for more optimistic rates of convergence depending on the effective dimension rather than on the ambient one. For instance,
Chen et al. (2023b)
attempted to escape the curse of dimensionality imposing additional assumptions on the underlying density
\mathsf{p}_{0}^{*}
. They showed that the risk of the denoising score matching estimate with probability at least
1-1/n
does not exceed

{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{1}{t_{0}}\left(n^{-2/(d+5)}+Dn^{-(d+3)/(d+5)}\right)\mathrm{polylog}(\log D,\log(1/t_{0}),\log n)\right).

Here
t_{0}
is a stopping time and
d
is an intrinsic dimension which may be much smaller than
D
. However, this upper bound estimate (as well as the one in the subsequent work
(
Boffi et al., 2024
)
) was obtained under very restrictive assumption that
\mathsf{p}_{0}^{*}
is supported on a linear
d
-dimensional subspace.
In the recent papers
(
Tang and Yang, 2024
)
and
(
Azangulov et al., 2024
)
the authors considered a much more general and challenging setup when the distribution of
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
is supported on a smooth low-dimensional manifold
{\mathcal{M}}
. They reported that the excess risk of their estimates converges to zero at the rates depending on the manifold smoothness, smoothness of
\mathsf{p}^{*}_{0}
, and the dimension of
{\mathcal{M}}
. However, the works
(
Tang and Yang, 2024
;
Azangulov et al., 2024
)
have several significant issues.

•

In
(
Tang and Yang, 2024
)
the hidden constants in the rates of convergence depend exponentially on the ambient dimension
D
. This makes the derived rates of convergence useless when
D
is of order
\log n
. In practice,
D
is usually much larger than
\log n
.

•

The estimate of
Azangulov et al. (2024)
exploits the fact that
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
lie exactly on the manifold
{\mathcal{M}}
. However, this assumption seems to be unrealistic. In contrary, in several papers
(
Daras et al., 2024a
;
Daras et al., 2024b
;
Kawar et al., 2024
;
Bai et al., 2024
)
the authors note that adding moderate noise to initial samples improves quality of image generation and prevents mode collapse.

•

Both
Tang and Yang (2024)
and
Azangulov et al. (2024)
assume that
{\mathcal{M}}
has no boundary and a positive reach. Furthermore, they suppose that the density
\mathsf{p}_{0}^{*}
(with respect to the volume measure on
{\mathcal{M}}
) is bounded away from zero and infinity. In
(
Zhang et al., 2024
, p. 2)
, the authors write: “The density lower bound greatly simplifies the proof of the score estimation error bound; however, it excludes natural distribution classes, such as multi-modal distributions or mixtures with well-separated components.”

•

Both
Tang and Yang (2024)
and
Azangulov et al. (2024)
refer to
(
Oko et al., 2023
, Theorem C.4)
when derive the estimation error bound. However, Theorem C.4 in
(
Oko et al., 2023
)
has a critical flaw. To be more precise, the last implication in (69) on page 41 does not hold.
Hence, the rates of convergence in all the papers
(
Oko et al., 2023
;
Tang and Yang, 2024
;
Azangulov et al., 2024
)
are incorrect.

Our Contribution.
In this paper, we attempt to overcome the aforementioned drawbacks in the existing results on score estimation.

•

We suggest a statistical model with relaxed manifold assumption. In contrast to
Tang and Yang (2024)
;
Azangulov et al. (2024)
, we do not require the samples
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}\sim\mathsf{p}_{0}^{*}
to lie exactly on a low-dimensional manifold. Instead, we assume that

Y_{i}=g^{*}(U_{i})+\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}\;\xi_{i},\quad 1\leqslant i\leqslant n,

where
\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}\geqslant 0
,
g^{*}:[0,1]^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{D}
is an unknown continuous map and
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}\sim\mathrm{Un}([0,1]^{d})
and
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,I_{D})
are independent random elements. This model admits situations when the image of
g^{*}
has zero reach and when the density
g^{*}(U_{1})
(with respect to the volume measure on the image of
g^{*}
) is not bounded away from zero or infinity.

•

We show that the denoising score matching estimate enjoys a rate of convergence
{\mathcal{O}}(n^{-2\beta/(2\beta+d)})
(up to some logarithmic factors) depending on the intrinsic dimension
d
and the smoothness of
g^{*}
. We also carefully track how the hidden constant behind
{\mathcal{O}}(\cdot)
depends on the ambient dimension
D
and stopping time
t_{0}
.

2
Preliminaries and notations

Denoising score matching.

Score matching approach aims to minimize

\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}}\left\|s(X_{t},t)-s^{*}(X_{t},t)\right\|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}t,

(4)

where
X_{t}
obeys the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process (
1
),
over a class
{\mathcal{S}}
of admissible score functions. The parameter
t_{0}\geqslant 0
is called
stopping time
. Since the score
s^{*}(X_{t},t)
is unknown,
Vincent (2011)
suggested to replace the objective (
4
) by
\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}\ell(s,X_{0})
, where

\ell(s,X_{0})=\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|s(X_{t},t)-\nabla_{X_{t}}\log\mathsf{p}_{t}^{*}(X_{t}\,|\,X_{0})\right\|^{2}\,\big|\,X_{0}\right]{\mathrm{d}}t

and
\mathsf{p}_{t}^{*}(X_{t}\,|\,X_{0})
stands for the conditional density of
X_{t}
given
X_{0}
. In contrast to (
4
), the loss function
\ell(s,X_{0})
admits an explicit expression. Indeed, due to the properties of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, we have that

(X_{t}\,|\,X_{0})\sim{\mathcal{N}}(m_{t}X_{0},\sigma_{t}^{2}I_{D}),\quad\text{where $m_{t}=e^{-t}$ and $\sigma_{t}^{2}=1-e^{-2t}$.}

(5)

Then it is straightforward to observe that

\ell(s,X_{0})=\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|s(X_{t},t)+\frac{X_{t}-m_{t}X_{0}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right\|^{2}\,\Big|\,X_{0}\right]{\mathrm{d}}t.

The intuition of
Vincent (2011)
is based on a simple observation that for any
t>0
and any
s:\mathbb{R}^{D}\times[t_{0},T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
we have

\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}}\|s(X_{t},t)-s^{*}(X_{t},t)\|^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}\,\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}}\left[\|s(X_{t},t)-\nabla_{X_{t}}\log\mathsf{p}_{t}^{*}(X_{t}\,|\,X_{0})\|^{2}\,\big|\,X_{0}\right]+C,

where
C
does not depend on
s
. In particular, this yields that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}}\left\|s(X_{t},t)-s^{*}(X_{t},t)\right\|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}t=\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}\ell(s,X_{0})-\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}\ell(s^{*},X_{0}).

(6)

for all
s:\mathbb{R}^{D}\times[t_{0},T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Given i.i.d. samples
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}\sim\mathsf{p}_{0}^{*}
, the denoising score matching estimate is defined as an empirical risk minimizer

\widehat{s}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}\limits_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\ell(s,Y_{i})\right\}.

(7)

Norms.

Throughout the paper, we denote the Euclidean norm of a vector
v
by
\|v\|
. We also use the notation

\|v\|_{\infty}
for the maximal absolute value of its entries, while
\|v\|_{0}
stands for the number of non-zero entries of
v
.
For a matrix
A
and a tensor
{\mathcal{T}}
of order
k
, their operator norms are defined as

\|A\|=\sup\limits_{\|u\|=\|v\|=1}u^{\top}Av\quad\text{and}\quad\|{\mathcal{T}}\|=\sup\limits_{\|u_{1}\|=\ldots=\|u_{k}\|=1}\left\{\sum\limits_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}{\mathcal{T}}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}u_{1,i_{i}}\dots u_{k,i_{k}}\right\}.

Similarly,
\|A\|_{\infty}
and
\|A\|_{0}
stand for the maximal absolute value of entries of
A
and the number of its non-zero entries, respectively. Finally, for a vector-valued function
f
defined on a set
\Omega
, we denote

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}=\sup_{x\in\Omega}\|f(x)\|\quad\text{and}\quad\|f\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}=\left\{\int_{\Omega}\|f(x)\|^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\right\}^{1/p},\quad p\geqslant 1.

Smoothness classes.

Let
f:\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be an arbitrary function defined on a set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{r}
. For a multi-index
{\mathbf{k}}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{+}
, we define the corresponding partial derivative
\partial^{\mathbf{k}}f
as

\partial^{{\mathbf{k}}}f(x)=\frac{\partial^{|{\mathbf{k}}|}f}{\partial x_{1}^{k_{1}}\ldots\partial x_{r}^{k_{r}}}.

Given
\beta>0
and
H>0
, say that
f
belongs to a Hölder class
{\mathcal{H}}^{\beta}(\Omega,\mathbb{R},H)
if and only if

\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{+}\\
1\leqslant|{\mathbf{k}}|\leqslant\lfloor\beta\rfloor\end{subarray}}\left\|\partial^{\mathbf{k}}f\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leqslant H\quad\text{and}\quad\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{+}\\
|{\mathbf{k}}|=\lfloor\beta\rfloor\end{subarray}}\sup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\Omega\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\;\frac{|\partial^{\mathbf{k}}f(x)-\partial^{\mathbf{k}}f(y)|}{\min\{1,\|x-y\|_{\infty}\}^{\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor}}\leqslant H.

We also say that a vector-valued function
h:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
lies in a Hölder class
{\mathcal{H}}^{\beta}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{m},H)
if and only if every component of
h
is in
{\mathcal{H}}^{\beta}(\Omega,\mathbb{R},H)
.

Neural networks.

In the present paper, we focus on feed-forward neural networks with the activation function
\mathrm{ReLU}(x)=x\vee 0
. For a vector
b=(b_{1},\dots,b_{r})\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
,
we define the shifted activation function
\mathrm{ReLU}_{b}:\mathbb{R}^{r}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{r}
as

\sigma_{b}:(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})\mapsto\bigl(\mathrm{ReLU}(x_{1}-b_{1}),\dots,\mathrm{ReLU}(x_{r}-b_{r})\bigr).

Given a positive integer
L
and a vector
W=(W_{0},W_{1},\dots,W_{L})\in\mathbb{N}^{L+1}
, a neural network of depth
L
and architecture
W
is a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{W_{0}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{W_{L}}
of the form

f(x)=-b_{L}+A_{L}\circ\mathrm{ReLU}_{b_{L-1}}\circ A_{L-1}\circ\mathrm{ReLU}_{b_{L-1}}\circ\ldots\circ A_{2}\circ\mathrm{ReLU}_{b_{1}}\circ A_{1}x,

(8)

where
A_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{W_{j}\times W_{j-1}}
and
b_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{j}}
for all
j\in\{1,\dots,L\}
. The maximum number of neurons in one layer
\|W\|_{\infty}
is referred to as the width of the neural network. In what follows, we consider classes
\mathsf{NN}(L,W,S,B)
of neural networks of the form (
8
) with at most
S
non-zero weights and the weight magnitude
B
:

\displaystyle\mathsf{NN}(L,W,S,B)=\Big\{\text{$f$ is of the form \eqref{eq:nn}}:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=1}^{L}\left(\|A_{j}\|_{0}+\|b_{j}\|_{0}\right)\leqslant S

\displaystyle\text{and }\max\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant L}\left\{\|A_{j}\|_{\infty}\vee\|b_{j}\|_{\infty}\right\}\leqslant B\Big\}.

In our proofs, we will extensively use the results on concatenation and parallel stacking of neural networks described in
(
Nakada and Imaizumi, 2020
, Section B.1.1)
.

3
Main results

In this section, we present main results of our paper. Before we move to upper bounds on the accuracy of score approximation and estimation, let us elaborate on data distribution assumptions.

Assumption 3.1
.

Given a
generator
from the Hölder ball
g^{*}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\beta}([0,1]^{d},\mathbb{R}^{D},H)
,
\|g^{*}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}\leqslant 1
, and
\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}\in[0,1)
, the observed samples
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
are i.i.d. copies of a random element
X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
generated from the model

\displaystyle X_{0}=g^{*}(U)+\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}\;\xi,

where
U\sim\mathrm{Un}([0,1]^{d})
and
\xi\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,I_{D})
are independent.

In the case
\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}>0
,
X_{0}
has a density with respect to the Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
given by

\displaystyle\mathsf{p}_{0}^{*}(y)=(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{\mathrm{data}})^{-D}\int\limits_{[0,1]^{d}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|y-g^{*}(u)\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}^{2}}\right){\mathrm{d}}u,\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{D}.

Assumption
3.1
suggests that the observations occupy a vicinity of a low-dimensional surface
\text{Im}(g^{*})
. However, we allow
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
to slightly deviate from
\text{Im}(g^{*})
adding Gaussian noise
\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}\xi
. This not only reflects common real-world scenarios but also corresponds to the situations when the noise is added manually to move from inherently discrete to absolutely continuous distributions (like in the dequantization trick, see, for example,
(
Dinh et al., 2017
;
Ho et al., 2019
)
).
On the other hand, Assumption
3.1
ensures that the distribution of
Y_{i}
’s has a small entropic dimension (see, for instance,
(
Dudley, 1968
, Section 2)
and
(
Chakraborty and Bartlett, 2024
, Definition 4)
). This is a reason for rates of convergence depending on the intrinsic dimension
d
, rather than on the ambient one (see Theorem
3.4
below).
In addition, Assumption
3.1
encompasses the cases when the data distribution has multiple modes, which is typical to real-world data, supported by empirical studies (see, for example,
Khayatkhoei et al. (2018)
;
Brown et al. (2023)
).
It is worth mentioning that the distribution of
g^{*}(U)
has atoms with respect to the volume measure on the image of
g^{*}
when
g^{*}
is constant on a set of positive measure. This feature alleviates the need for lower and upper bound assumptions on the density of
g^{*}(U)
(with respect to the volume measure) commonly used in several papers (see, for instance,
(
Oko et al., 2023
;
Tang and Yang, 2024
;
Gatmiry et al., 2024
)
).
Furthermore, unlike prior works studying properties of generative diffusion models in the presence of a hidden low-dimensional manifold
(
Tang and Yang, 2024
;
Azangulov et al., 2024
)
, we do not require the image of
g^{*}
to have a positive reach. The difference becomes more evident if one takes into account that
Azangulov et al. (2024)
suppose that the reach of the underlying manifold
{\mathcal{M}}
is not just positive but also large enough (see their Assumption C(iii)). This, together with the condition
{\mathcal{M}}\subset{\mathcal{B}}(0,1)
, puts significant restrictions on the shape of
{\mathcal{M}}
.
Finally, we emphasize that the assumption
\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}\leqslant 1
is reasonable, as it, in conjunction with the bound
\|g^{*}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}\leqslant 1
on generator’s
L^{\infty}
-norm, ensures a well-controlled signal-to-noise ratio, which is crucial for meaningful data analysis.

Assumption
3.1
and the conditional distribution property (
5
) ensure that, for any
t>0
, the density
\mathsf{p}_{t}^{*}
along the forward process (
1
) is expressed as

\mathsf{p}_{t}^{*}(y)=(\sqrt{2\pi}\widetilde{\sigma}_{t})^{-D}\int\limits_{[0,1]^{d}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\|y-m_{t}g^{*}(u)\|^{2}}{2\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\right\}{\mathrm{d}}u,\quad\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}=m_{t}^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}.

(9)

Hence, the corresponding score function is given by

s^{*}(y,t)=\nabla_{y}\log\mathsf{p}_{t}^{*}(y)=-\frac{y}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}+\frac{m_{t}}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}f^{*}(y,t),

(10)

where

\displaystyle f^{*}(y,t)=\frac{\int\limits_{[0,1]^{d}}g^{*}(u)\exp\left(-\frac{\|y-m_{t}g^{*}(u)\|^{2}}{2\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\right){\mathrm{d}}u}{\int\limits_{[0,1]^{d}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|y-m_{t}g^{*}(u)\|^{2}}{2\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\right){\mathrm{d}}u}.

(11)

It is easy to see that
f^{*}(y,t)
is uniformly bounded.
Formally, Assumption
3.1
suggests that for any
t>0

\displaystyle\|f(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}\leqslant\|g^{*}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}\leqslant 1.

These findings about the score function structure motivate us to consider the following class of score estimators.

Definition 3.2

(the class of score estimators)
.

The class of neural score estimators
{\mathcal{S}}(L,W,S,B)
is defined as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}(L,W,S,B)=\biggl\{s(y,t):=-\frac{y}{m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}}+\frac{m_{t}\mathrm{clip}_{2}(f(y,t))}{m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}}:\qquad

\displaystyle f\in\mathsf{NN}(L,W,S,B),\;\sigma\in[0,1)\biggr\},

(12)

where, for any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
,
\mathrm{clip}_{R}(z)
stands for componentwise clipping of
z
at the level
R
:

\mathrm{clip}_{R}(z)=\begin{cases}z,\quad\text{if $\|z\|\leqslant R$},\\
\frac{Rz}{\|z\|},\quad\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The use of componentwise clipping is justified as it does not limit the application of gradient-based learning methods due to the non-differentiable nature of the operation. This is also the case for neural networks that use the ReLU activation function. Distinct from the approach in
(
Chen et al., 2023b
)
, our definition of the score estimator class in Definition
3.2
refrains from imposing extra Lipschitz constraints, which is a significant relaxation in assumptions. Furthermore, unlike
Tang and Yang (2024)
necessitating uniform output boundedness across the entire estimator class, we do not impose such a restriction, thereby enhancing flexibility.

3.1
Score approximation

We move to main results of the paper.
In this section we provide a quantitative expressive power of neural network class from Definition
3.2
to approximate the true score function.

Theorem 3.3

(Approximation of the true score function)
.

Assume that
g^{*}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\beta}([0,1]^{d},\mathbb{R}^{D},H)
and let
s^{*}(y,t)=\nabla\log\mathsf{p}_{t}^{*}(y)
be the corresponding score function. Fix an arbitrary
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
such that

D\varepsilon\sqrt{\log(1/\varepsilon)}\leqslant\widetilde{\sigma}_{t_{0}}^{2},\quad H\varepsilon\sqrt{D}\leqslant\widetilde{\sigma}_{t_{0}},\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{Hd^{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}\varepsilon^{\beta}\sqrt{D}}{\lfloor\beta\rfloor!}\leqslant 1\wedge\widetilde{\sigma}_{t_{0}}.

Then there exists a score function
s\in{\mathcal{S}}(L,W,S,B)
(see Definition
3.2
) with
L\lesssim D^{2}+\log^{4}(1/\varepsilon)
,
\log B\lesssim D+\log^{2}(1/\varepsilon)
, and

\displaystyle\|W\|_{\infty}\lesssim D^{2}\varepsilon^{-d}\left(\frac{1}{t_{0}+\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}^{2}}\vee 1\right)\left(D+\log^{2}(1/\varepsilon)\right)^{3},

\displaystyle S\lesssim D^{2}\varepsilon^{-d}\left(\frac{1}{t_{0}+\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}^{2}}\vee 1\right)\left(D+\log^{2}(1/\varepsilon)\right)^{3}+D\varepsilon^{-d}\left(D+\log^{2}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)^{2\binom{d+\lfloor\beta\rfloor}{d}+5}

such that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}}\left\|s^{*}(X_{t},t)-s(X_{t},t)\right\|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}t\lesssim\frac{D\varepsilon^{2\beta}}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{t_{0}}^{2}}.

(13)

Here
\lesssim
stands for an inequality up to a multiplicative constant depending on
d
and
\beta
.

We provide a rigorous proof of Theorem
3.3
in Appendix
A
and a proof sketch in Section
5.1
. In contrast to the literature on score estimation under the manifold hypothesis, such as
(
Tang and Yang, 2024
;
Azangulov et al., 2024
)
, we do not need the density of
g^{*}(U)
,
U\sim\mathrm{Un}([0,1]^{d})
, (with respect to the volume measure on
\text{Im}(g^{*})
) be bounded away from zero (but, of course, we still have to impose some regularity assumptions on
g^{*}
). This significant difference prevents straightforward adoption of the arguments presented in
(
Oko et al., 2023
;
Tang and Yang, 2024
;
Azangulov et al., 2024
)
, leading to a more intricate proof. Nonetheless, we can exploit properties of the data distribution specified in Assumption
3.1
.

A comparison with the work of
Azangulov et al. (2024)
reveals that an extension of their method to handle positive
\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}
is not straightforward.
This is because the construction of their estimator involves a pre-processing step, which requires the target distribution to be exactly supported on the manifold. Moreover, Assumption D in
(
Azangulov et al., 2024
)
requires the smoothness parameter of the manifold to be large enough. In contrast, the proposed approach addresses these challenges, effectively handling positive
\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}
values and small values of
\beta
without imposing such restrictive conditions.

Another important feature of Theorem
3.3
distinguishing our result from
(
Zhang et al., 2024
;
Tang and Yang, 2024
)

is polynomial dependence on the ambient dimension
D
of both the right-hand side of (

) and the parameters
L
,
W
,
S
, and
\log B
. For instance, the bound of

(Corollary 3.7) explicitly depends on
t_{0}^{-D/2}
. In
(
Tang and Yang, 2024
)
, the exponential dependence on
D
is potentially hidden in the coefficients
a_{lki}
(see pp. 21–22 in
(
Tang and Yang, 2024
)
) as they hide multinomial coefficients which may be as large as
{\mathcal{O}}(e^{D})

Tang and Yang (2024)
do not clarify whether one can get better upper bounds on
a_{lki}

{\mathcal{O}}(e^{D})
. The same concerns the coefficients
a_{l_{1},l_{2},k,s,i}
on page 26 in
(
Tang and Yang, 2024
)
.

3.2
Score estimation

It this section, we present a sample complexity bound for the score estimator derived through the empirical risk minimization (
7
), considering the specified configuration of the score class
{\mathcal{S}}(L,W,S,B)
(see Definition
3.2
).

Theorem 3.4
.

Assume that the conditions of Theorem
3.3
hold.
Let also
T\geqslant 1
and let the sample size be sufficiently large, that is, it fulfils

\displaystyle\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2}n\geqslant\left(\frac{D\sqrt{\log(n\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2})}}{\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2}\sqrt{2\beta+d}}\right)^{2\beta+d}\vee\left(\frac{Hd^{{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}}\sqrt{D}}{{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}!\,\sigma_{t_{0}}}\right)^{2+d/\beta}\vee\left(\frac{H\sqrt{D}}{\sigma_{t_{0}}}\right)^{2\beta+d}.

(14)

Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
(1-\delta)
the excess risk of an empirical risk minimizer (
7
) over the class
{\mathcal{S}}(L,W,S,B)
(see Definition
3.2
) with

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle\lesssim D^{2}\log^{4}n,\quad\|W\|_{\infty}\lesssim t_{0}^{-1}D^{5}(n\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2})^{\frac{d}{2\beta+d}}\log^{6}n,

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\lesssim t_{0}^{-1}D^{6+2\binom{d+{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}}{d}}(n\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2})^{\frac{d}{2\beta+d}}(\log n)^{10+4\binom{d+{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}}{d}},\quad\log B\lesssim D\log^{2}n

satisfies the inequality

\displaystyle\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}}\|\widehat{s}(X_{t},t)-s^{*}(X_{t},t)\|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}t\lesssim\frac{T^{2}D^{12+2\binom{d+{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}}{d}}}{\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2}}(n\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2})^{-\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+d}}L(t_{0},n)\log(4/\delta),

\displaystyle L(t_{0},n)=(\log n)^{20+4\binom{d+{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}}{d}}\log T\log D\log^{3}(1/t_{0}).

The hidden constant behind
\lesssim
depends on
d
and
\beta
only.

We provide a complete proof of Theorem
3.4
in Appendix
B
and its sketch in Section
5.2
.
We would like to emphasize that the requirement that
T\geqslant 1
is mild, since the approximation of the reversed process outlined in (
3
) is tight when
T
is sufficiently large. In the case
\beta\geqslant 1
our rate of convergence
{\mathcal{O}}(n^{-2\beta/(2\beta+d)}\text{polylog}(n))
decays faster than the minimax optimal generalization error bound
{\mathcal{O}}(n^{-2/(4+d)})
of
Wibisono et al. (2024)
(see their Theorems 1 and 3). This is not surprising, since
Wibisono et al. (2024)
studies minimax optimal rates over the class of sub-Gaussian Lipschitz scores
s^{*}
while we consider a more special case.
As we mentioned in the introduction, the concurrent papers
(
Tang and Yang, 2024
;
Azangulov et al., 2024
)
inherit the mistake of
Oko et al. (2023)
in the proof of Theorem C.4 (see eq. (69) on page 41).
In particular, using the bounds
|\ell_{j}(x)-\ell^{\circ}(x)|\leqslant C_{\ell}
and
\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[\ell_{j}(x)-\ell^{\circ}(x)\big]\leqslant r_{j}^{2}
,
Oko, Akiyama, and Suzuki (2023)
mistakenly conclude that

\mathbb{E}_{x}\big(\ell_{j}(x)-\ell^{\circ}(x)\big)^{2}\leqslant C_{\ell}\,r_{j}^{2}.

The last inequality would be true if the difference of losses
\ell_{j}(x)-\ell^{\circ}(x)
was non-negative almost surely. Unfortunately, this is not the case for the denoising score matching loss.
Hence, our paper provides the first upper bound on the generalization error of the denoising score matching estimate under rather general assumptions.
In our proof, we show that the excess losses
\ell(s,X_{0})-\ell(s^{*},X_{0})
,
s\in{\mathcal{S}}
, satisfy the Bernstein condition (see (
68
)):

\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}(\ell(s,X_{0})-\ell(s^{*},X_{0}))^{2}\lesssim\left(\frac{D^{3}T^{2}\log(1/\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2})\log n}{\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2}}\right)\big(\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}[\ell(s,X_{0})-\ell(s^{*},X_{0})]\big)^{1-1/\varkappa},

where
\varkappa=2\vee\big(\log n+\log(\sigma_{t_{0}}^{-2})\big)
. We would like to note that first factor in the right-hand side is

{\mathcal{O}}(\sigma_{t_{0}}^{-2})
, rather than
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
. The linear dependence on
\sigma_{t_{0}}^{-2}
affects the results on distribution estimation in the Kantorovich and total variation distances.
Besides, we carefully track dependence on the ambient dimension
D
(in contrast to
(
Tang and Yang, 2024
)
, where the authors ignore the terms which potentially may be as large as
{\mathcal{O}}(e^{D})
). Finally, our results remain valid under the relaxed manifold assumption (Assumption
3.1
), while the estimate of
Azangulov et al. (2024)
suffers from noise in the observations
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
.

Let us move to the linear case and compare the result of Theorem
3.4
with the one of
Chen et al. (2023b)
.
We set
\delta=n^{-1}
,
\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}=0
,
\beta=1
and take
t_{0}\leqslant 1
, which leads to
\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2}\asymp t_{0}
.
Thus, the generalization error bound from Theorem
3.4
simplifies to

\displaystyle\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}}\|\widehat{s}(X_{t},t)-s^{*}(X_{t},t)\|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}t\lesssim\frac{T^{2}D^{12+2\binom{d+{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}}{d}}}{t_{0}}(n\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2})^{-\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+d}}L(t_{0},n)\log(4/\delta),

with probability at least
1-1/n
.
With the same parameters specified,
Chen et al. (2023b)
claims that, disregarding logarithmic factors in all parameters excluding
n
, with the same confidence level one has

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T-t_{0}}\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}}\|\widehat{s}(X_{t},t)-s^{*}(X_{t},t)\|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}t\lesssim\frac{1}{t_{0}}\left(n^{-\frac{2-2d\log(\log n)/\log n}{d+5}}+Dn^{-\frac{d+3}{d+5}}\right)\log^{3}n,

From the comparison of the rates above we deduce that our estimate enjoys faster rate in terms of the sample size. It is not surprising that the dependence on the ambient dimension and the stopping time is worse, since
Chen et al. (2023b)
impose severe assumptions on the data distribution. This allows them to consider scores of a special kind, while we must deal with a more general class. When comparing our work to that of
Wibisono et al. (2024)
, a critical distinction lies in the strong dependence on the ambient dimension.
Specifically, Theorem 5
(
Wibisono et al., 2024
)
suggests that the score estimation error scales as
(\log n)^{D/2}
.
This exponential-like growth in error with respect to the ambient dimension severely undermines consistency of the estimate when
D\asymp\log n
.
In contrast, our bound given in Theorem
3.4
exhibits a milder dependence on the ambient dimension, successfully addressing the aforementioned issue.

3.3
Distribution Estimation

This section focuses on estimating the true data distribution with density
\mathsf{p}^{*}_{0}
by leveraging the score function
\widehat{s}
learned from data.
For the sake of simplicity, we do not take a discretization error into consideration.
To evaluate a total variation (TV) distance between
X_{0}\sim\mathsf{p}^{*}_{0}
and
\widehat{Z}_{T-t_{0}}
specified in the modified backward process (
3
), we derive the following significant finding, which is an implication of our estimation theory encapsulated in Theorem
3.4
and an auxiliary lemma from
(
Chen et al., 2023d
)
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Assume that
\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}>0
and suppose that the conditions of Theorem
3.4
hold for
t_{0}=\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}^{(2\beta+d)/(3\beta+d)}n^{-\beta/(3\beta+d)}
and

T\asymp\log n
.
Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the following holds:

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}(\widehat{Z}_{T-t_{0}},X_{0})\lesssim D^{6+\binom{d+{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}}{d}}\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}^{-\frac{1}{3\beta+d}}n^{-\frac{\beta}{6\beta+2d}}(\log n)^{25+4\binom{d+{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}}{d}}\log D\log^{1/2}(4/\delta).

The proof of Theorem
3.5
is postponed to Appendix
C
.
Theorem
3.5
establishes that the target distribution can be accurately learned with a polynomial number of samples. This represents a significant enhancement over prior work by
Gatmiry et al. (2024)
, which only guaranteed a quasi-polynomial sample complexity. Notably, while our approach necessitates higher-order smoothness conditions on the support of the distribution of means,
Gatmiry et al. (2024)
achieve their results under more relaxed assumptions without such smoothness constraints. When comparing our result presented in Theorem
3.5
to those of
Wibisono et al. (2024)
;
Zhang et al. (2024)
, a key distinction emerges regarding sensitivity to the ambient dimensionality.
More precisely, Corollary 6 in
(
Wibisono et al., 2024
)
states that the sample complexity scales as
D^{D/2}
.
Comparable limitations are evident in
(
Zhang et al., 2024
, Theorem 3.8)
.
By following their proof, we have found that the hidden degree of the polynomial logarithmic term is proportional to the ambient dimension.
These dependencies impose notable constraints when the dimension is logarithmic in the sample size, a scenario discussed in Section
3.2
.

4
A note on minimax rates of convergence

In conclusion, we would like to briefly discuss minimax optimal rates of convergence for score estimation under Assumption
3.1
. We are going to show that this question is far from being trivial. In Appendix
D
, we prove the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Given an arbitrary
\sigma>0
and
g:[0,1]^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{D}
, let

\mathsf{p}(y)=(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{-D}\int\limits_{[0,1]^{d}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\|y-g(u)\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}{\mathrm{d}}u.

Then, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds that

\sup\limits_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}\left\|\nabla^{k}\left(\log\mathsf{p}(y)-\frac{\|y\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\right\|\leqslant\frac{2^{k-1}(k-1)!}{\sigma^{2k}}\max\limits_{u\in[0,1]^{d}}\|g(u)\|^{k}.

Applying Lemma
4.1
with
g(u)=g^{*}(u)
and
\sigma=\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}
,
t_{0}\leqslant t\leqslant T_{0}
, we observe that the function
\log\mathsf{p}_{t}^{*}(y)
is
(Q,R)
-analytic with
Q\lesssim 1
and
R\lesssim\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{-2}
(see, for example,
(
Belomestny et al., 2023
, Definition 1)
for the definition of
(Q,R)
-analytic functions). Choosing an appropriate system of functions (splines, wavelets, Hermite polynomials, etc.), one can construct an estimate
\widetilde{s}(y,t)
such that

\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}}\left\|\widetilde{s}(X_{t},t)-s^{*}(X_{t},t)\right\|^{2}\lesssim\frac{\mathrm{polylog}(n)}{n}\quad\text{for any $t\in[t_{0},T]$.}

(15)

However, the hidden constant is likely to depend on
D
exponentially, so the obtained upper bound becomes vacuous when
D\gtrsim\log n
. On the other hand, if one tries to prove a minimax lower bound of order
\Omega(n^{-2\beta/(2\beta+d)})
(complementary to the result of Theorem
3.4
), he must take into account that the rate
{\mathcal{O}}(n^{-2\beta/(2\beta+d)})
may be minimax optimal only in the case
D\gtrsim\log n
. Otherwise, one should expect different rates of convergence. For instance, according to (
15
), in the case
D={\mathcal{O}}(1)
one can prove an upper bound
{\mathcal{O}}(\mathrm{polylog}(n)/n)
. This poses a great challenge in deriving optimal rates of convergence in the minimax sense.

5
Proof sketches of main results

In this section we elaborate on main ideas used in the proofs of Theorems
3.3
and
3.4
. A reader can find rigorous derivations in Appendices
A
and
B
, respectively.

5.1
Proof sketch of Theorem
3.3

We split the proof into several steps for convenience.

Step 1: local polynomial approximation.
We begin our analysis by noting that it is sufficient for our purposes to approximate a surrogate score function
s^{\circ}
induced by a local polynomial approximation of
g^{*}
.
This is essential for our subsequent steps.
Our technical findings reveal that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}\sim\mathsf{p}_{t}^{*}}\|s^{\circ}(X_{t},t)-s^{*}(X_{t},t)\|^{2}\leqslant\frac{DH^{2}\varepsilon^{2\beta}}{4(\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}^{2}+e^{2t_{0}}-1)}\left(\frac{d^{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}}{\lfloor\beta\rfloor!}\right)^{2}.

Step 2: reduction to approximation on a compact set.

Representing
s^{\circ}(y,t)=-\frac{y}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}+\frac{m_{t}f^{\circ}(y,t)}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}

and leveraging the property that the distribution
\mathsf{p}_{t}^{*}
is light-tailed, we deduce that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}}\|s^{\circ}(X_{t},t)-s(X_{t},t)\|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}t\leqslant\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{m_{t}^{2}}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{4}}\left(D\varepsilon^{2\beta}+\int\limits_{{\mathcal{K}}_{t}}\|f^{\circ}(y,t)-f(y,t)\|^{2}\mathsf{p}_{t}^{*}(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}t,

where
{\mathcal{K}}_{t}
denotes a compact set containing points that are close to the image of
g^{*}
.

Step 3:
f^{\circ}
is a composition of simpler functions.
We first recall that

f^{\circ}(y,t)={\scriptstyle\left(\sum\limits_{{\mathbf{j}}\in\{1,\dots,N\}^{d}}\;\int\limits_{{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathbf{j}}}g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u)\exp\left\{-\frac{\|y-m_{t}g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u)\|^{2}}{2\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\right\}{\mathrm{d}}u\right)\Big/\left(\sum\limits_{{\mathbf{j}}\in\{1,\dots,N\}^{d}}\;\int\limits_{{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathbf{j}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\|y-m_{t}g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u)\|^{2}}{2\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\right\}{\mathrm{d}}u\right)}.

We next note that the expression under the exponent could be expressed as follows:

\frac{\|y-m_{t}g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u)\|^{2}}{2\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}=V_{{\mathbf{j}},0}(y,t)+{\mathcal{V}}(t)\left\|g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u)-g^{*}(u_{\mathbf{j}})\right\|^{2}+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{+}\\
1\leqslant|{\mathbf{k}}|\leqslant\lfloor\beta\rfloor\end{subarray}}V_{{\mathbf{j}},{\mathbf{k}}}(y,t)\,\frac{(u-u_{\mathbf{j}})^{{\mathbf{k}}}}{{\mathbf{k}}!},

where we introduced intermediate functions

{\mathcal{V}}(t)=\frac{m_{t}^{2}}{2\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}},\qquad V_{{\mathbf{j}},0}(y,t)=\frac{\|y-m_{t}g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u_{\mathbf{j}})\|^{2}}{2\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}},

and

V_{{\mathbf{j}},{\mathbf{k}}}(y,t)=-\frac{m_{t}}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\left.\partial_{u}^{\mathbf{k}}\big((y-m_{t}g^{*}(u_{\mathbf{j}}))^{\top}g^{*}(u)\big)\right|_{u=u_{\mathbf{j}}},\quad{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{+},1\leqslant|{\mathbf{k}}|\leqslant\lfloor\beta\rfloor.

Thus, each term in both the numerator and the denominator of
f^{\circ}
representation is a composition of the introduced functions
with functions of
\binom{d+\lfloor\beta\rfloor}{d}+1
variables.
It is important to note that
\binom{d+\lfloor\beta\rfloor}{d}+1
can be substantially smaller than the ambient dimension
D
,
implying a reduction in the overall complexity of the resulting neural network.

Step 4: approximation of
V_{{\mathbf{j}},0}
,
V_{{\mathbf{j}},{\mathbf{k}}}
and
{\mathcal{V}}
.
We remark that the functions under consideration can be approximated to
\varepsilon
-accuracy with a number of parameters that scales logarithmically with
1/\varepsilon
.
This logarithmic scaling has a positive impact on the complexity of the resulting network architecture.

Step 5: approximation of the composition.
We first approximate

\int\limits_{{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathbf{j}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\|y-m_{t}g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u)\|^{2}}{2\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\right\}{\mathrm{d}}u\quad\text{and}\quad\int\limits_{{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathbf{j}}}g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u)\exp\left\{-\frac{\|y-m_{t}g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u)\|^{2}}{2\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\right\}{\mathrm{d}}u.

The insight from Step 3 regarding the functions with a small number of arguments facilitates the application of a fundamental result on the approximation capabilities of ReLU neural networks
(
Schmidt-Hieber, 2020
, Theorem 5)
.
Second, the approximation of the numerator and denominator in the expression for
f^{\circ}
poses no additional difficulty, as a summation operation introduces an additional linear layer.

Step 6: division approximation.
It remains to approximate the division operation in order to complete the approximation of
f^{\circ}
.
It is crucial to ensure that both the numerator and denominator are approximated with sufficient
relative
precision.
To accomplish this without relying on specific structural assumptions, such as a lower-bounded density (as used in
Tang and Yang (2024)
;
Azangulov et al. (2024)
),
we establish a lower bound for the denominator that is independent of such constraints.
This key step allows us to apply a division approximation result and complete the proof.

5.2
Proof sketch of Theorem
3.4

The proof of Theorem
3.4
consists of several steps.

Step 1: Bernstein’s condition.
We start with the observation that the Bernstein condition for the excess loss class could be easily verified.
Formally, for any
s\in{\mathcal{S}}(L,W,S,B)
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}(\ell(s,X_{0})-\ell(s^{*},X_{0}))^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\frac{D^{2}(T-t_{0})(1+\varkappa)\log(1/\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2})}{\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2}}\right)^{1+1/\varkappa}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}[\ell(s,X_{0})-\ell(s^{*},X_{0})]\right\}^{1-1/\varkappa},

where
\varkappa\geqslant 1
and will be determined later in the proof.
The subsequent proof leverages Bernstein’s inequality in conjunction with the
\varepsilon
-net argument. To enhance clarity, we split the proof into several steps.

Step 1: Bernstein’s large deviation bound.
Given a fixed
s\in{\mathcal{S}}(L,W,S,B)
, we invoke Bernstein’s concentration inequality for unbounded random variables
Lecué and Mitchell (2012)
.
This is feasible due to the fact that
\ell(s,X_{0})-\ell(s^{*},X_{0})
has finite
\psi_{1}
-norm.
Therefore, with probability at least
(1-\delta/2)
,

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}[\ell(s,X_{0})-\ell(s^{*},X_{0})]-\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{X_{0}}[\ell(s,X_{0})-\ell(s^{*},X_{0})]\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{C_{\mathrm{b}}\{\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}[\ell(s,X_{0})-\ell(s^{*},X_{0})]\}^{1-1/\varkappa}\log(4/\delta)}{n}}+\frac{C_{\mathrm{b}}\log(4/\delta)}{n},

where
C_{\mathrm{b}}
denotes the constant in the aforementioned Bernstein-type inequality, given by

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{b}}=\frac{D^{3}T^{2}\log^{2}(\sigma_{t_{0}}^{-2})\log n}{\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2+2/\varkappa}}.

Step 2:
\varepsilon
-net argument and a uniform bound.
For any
\tau>0
we form a set
{\mathcal{S}}_{\tau}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}(L,W,S,B)
, such that

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}(L,W,S,B)}\inf_{s_{\tau}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\tau}}\left\{|\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}[\ell(s,X_{0})-\ell(s_{\tau},X_{0})]|+|\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{X_{0}}[\ell(s,X_{0})-\ell(s_{\tau},X_{0})]|\right\}\leqslant\tau,

with probability at least
(1-\delta)
.
In addition, the following bound holds:

\displaystyle\log|{\mathcal{S}}_{\tau}|\lesssim SL\log(\tau^{-1}L(\|W\|_{\infty}+1)(B\vee 1)DT\sigma_{t_{0}}^{-2}\log(n/\delta)).

This observation yields a uniform bound, guaranteeing that with probability at least
(1-\delta)
,

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}[\ell(s,X_{0})-\ell(s^{*},X_{0})]-\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{X_{0}}[\ell(s,X_{0})-\ell(s^{*},X_{0})]\right|

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim\tau^{1-1/\varkappa}+\sqrt{\frac{C_{\mathrm{b}}\{\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}[\ell(s,X_{0})-\ell(s^{*},X_{0})]\}^{1-1/\varkappa}\log(4|{\mathcal{S}}_{\tau}|/\delta)}{n}}+\frac{C_{\mathrm{b}}\log(4|{\mathcal{S}}_{\tau}|/\delta)}{n}

(16)

holds for all
s\in{\mathcal{S}}(L,W,S,B)
simultaneously.

Step 3: final bound for
\widehat{s}
.

Let
\bar{s}
be the approximation of the true score from Theorem
3.3
formulated for accuracy parameter
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
satisfying the conditions of the theorem.
Then, for the empirical risk minimizer
\widehat{s}
from the uniform bound (
16
), we deduce that with probability at least
(1-\delta)
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{X_{0}}[\ell(\widehat{s},X_{0})-\ell(s^{*},X_{0})]

\displaystyle\leqslant\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{X_{0}}[\ell(\bar{s},X_{0})-\ell(s^{*},X_{0})]

\displaystyle\lesssim\tau^{1-1/\varkappa}+\frac{C_{\mathrm{b}}\log(4|{\mathcal{S}}_{\tau}|/\delta)}{n}+\left(\frac{D\varepsilon^{2\beta}}{\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2}}\right)^{1-1/\varkappa},

Subsequently, combining this result with (
16
) and setting
\tau=\varepsilon^{2\beta}
, we conclude that with probability at least
(1-\delta)
,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}}\|\widehat{s}(X_{t},t)-s^{*}(X_{t},t)\|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}t\lesssim\left(\left(\frac{D\varepsilon^{2\beta}}{\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2}}\right)^{1-1/\varkappa}+\frac{T^{2}\varepsilon^{-d}D^{12+2\binom{d+{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}}{d}}}{t_{0}\cdot(\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2}n)^{1/(1+1/\varkappa)}}\right)L^{\prime}(t_{0},\varepsilon)\log(4/\delta),

where the logarithmic factors are captured in the expression

\displaystyle L^{\prime}(t_{0},\varepsilon)=(\log(1/\varepsilon))^{18+4\binom{d+{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}}{d}}\log T\log D\log^{3}(1/t_{0})\log^{2}n.

Then the choice
\varepsilon=(n\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2\beta+d}}
and
\varkappa=2\vee(\log n+\log(\sigma_{t_{0}}^{-2}))
yields the desired result.

Appendix A
Proof of Theorem
3.3

The proof of Theorem
3.3
is quite technical. For this reason, we split it into several steps.

Step 1: local polynomial approximation.

We start with a simple observation that it is enough to approximate a surrogate score function
s^{\circ}
induced by a local polynomial approximation of
g^{*}
. This will play a crucial role on further steps. Let us introduce
N=\lceil 1/\varepsilon\rceil
, and for any
{\mathbf{j}}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{d})\in\{1,\dots,N\}^{d}
we define

u_{\mathbf{j}}=\frac{{\mathbf{j}}}{N}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathbf{j}}=\left[\frac{j_{1}-1}{N},\frac{j_{1}}{N}\right]\times\left[\frac{j_{2}-1}{N},\frac{j_{2}}{N}\right]\times\dots\times\left[\frac{j_{d}-1}{N},\frac{j_{d}}{N}\right].

Then the local polynomial approximation of
g^{*}
is given by

g^{\circ}(u)=\sum\limits_{{\mathbf{j}}\in\{1,\dots,N\}^{d}}g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u),\quad u\in[0,1]^{d},

(17)

where

g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u)=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{+}\\
|{\mathbf{k}}|\leqslant\lfloor\beta\rfloor\end{subarray}}\frac{\partial^{\mathbf{k}}g^{*}(u_{\mathbf{j}})}{{\mathbf{k}}!}(u-u_{\mathbf{j}})^{{\mathbf{k}}}\;\mathbbm{1}(u\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathbf{j}}),\quad\text{for all ${\mathbf{j}}\in\{1,\dots,N\}^{d}$ and $u\in[0,1]^{d}$.}

(18)

It is straightforward to show that
g^{\circ}
does not differ from
g^{*}
too much. We provide an explicit quantitative bound in the following lemma.

Lemma A.1
.

Let

and let
g^{\circ}
be as defined in (
17
). Then it holds that

\left\|g^{*}-g^{\circ}\right\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}=\max\limits_{u\in[0,1]^{d}}\left\|g^{*}(u)-g^{\circ}(u)\right\|\leqslant\frac{Hd^{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}\varepsilon^{\beta}\sqrt{D}}{\lfloor\beta\rfloor!}.

The proof of Lemma
A.1
is postponed to Appendix
A.1
. Our next goal is to show that the closeness of
g^{*}
and
g^{\circ}
implies the proximity of corresponding score functions. Similarly to
s^{*}(y,t)
(see (
10
)), we denote

s^{\circ}(y,t)=\nabla_{y}\log\int\limits_{[0,1]^{d}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\|y-m_{t}g^{\circ}(u)\|^{2}}{2\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\right\}{\mathrm{d}}u.

Then Proposition
F.1
ensures that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{D}}\|s^{\circ}(y,t)-s^{*}(y,t)\|^{2}\,\mathsf{p}_{t}^{*}(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\,{\mathrm{d}}t

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{DH^{2}\varepsilon^{2\beta}}{4(\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}^{2}+e^{2t_{0}}-1)}\left(\frac{d^{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}}{\lfloor\beta\rfloor!}\right)^{2}.

(19)

In the rest of the proof, we focus on approximation of
s^{\circ}(y,t)

(\sqrt{2\pi}\widetilde{\sigma}_{t})^{-D}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{D}\backslash{\mathcal{K}}_{t}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\|y-m_{t}g(u)\|^{2}}{2\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\right\}\,{\mathrm{d}}y.

Introducing a random vector
Y\sim{\mathcal{N}}(m_{t}g(u),\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}I_{D})
, we note that

(\sqrt{2\pi}\widetilde{\sigma}_{t})^{-D}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{D}\backslash{\mathcal{K}}_{t}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\|y-m_{t}g(u)\|^{2}}{2\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\right\}\,{\mathrm{d}}y=\mathbb{P}\left(Y\notin{\mathcal{K}}_{t}\right)\leqslant\mathbb{P}\left(\|Y-m_{t}g(u)\|\geqslant R_{t}\right).

The probability in the right-hand side is equal to

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\|Y-m_{t}g(u)\|^{2}}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\geqslant\frac{R_{t}^{2}}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\right),\quad\text{where}\quad\frac{\|Y-m_{t}g(u)\|^{2}}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\sim\chi^{2}(D).

Applying the standard concentration bounds for chi-squared random variables (see Proposition
F.3
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|Y-m_{t}g(u)\|\geqslant R_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\|Y-m_{t}g(u)\|^{2}}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\geqslant\frac{R_{t}^{2}}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\|Y-m_{t}g(u)\|^{2}}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\geqslant D+\frac{R_{t}^{2}-D\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{16}\left(\frac{R_{t}^{2}}{D\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\wedge\frac{R_{t}}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}}\right)\right\}.

Hence, it holds that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{D}\backslash{\mathcal{K}}_{t}}\mathsf{p}_{t}^{*}(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{16}\left(\frac{R_{t}^{2}-D\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}{D\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\wedge\frac{\sqrt{R_{t}^{2}-D\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}}\right)\right\}\int\limits_{[0,1]^{d}}{\mathrm{d}}u

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{16}\left(\frac{R_{t}^{2}-D\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}{D\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\wedge\frac{\sqrt{R_{t}^{2}-D\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}}\right)\right\}.

\displaystyle+8\left(\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}|X_{0}=x}\|s(X_{t},t)-s^{\prime}(X_{t},t)\|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}t\right)\cdot\left(\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}|X_{0}=x}\left\|s^{\prime}(X_{t},t)+\frac{X_{t}-m_{t}x}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right\|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}t\right).

Next, according to the definition of
s
and
s^{\prime}
(see (
64
) and (
65
)) it holds that

\displaystyle\|s(X_{t},t)-s^{\prime}(X_{t},t)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2\|X_{t}\|^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}+\frac{4m_{t}^{2}\|h(X_{t},t)\|^{2}}{(m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}+\frac{4m_{t}^{2}\|h^{\prime}(X_{t},t)\|^{2}}{(m_{t}^{2}(\sigma^{\prime})^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{4m_{t}^{2}\|x\|^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}+\frac{4\|X_{t}-m_{t}x\|^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}+\frac{32m_{t}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}.

In the last inequality, we used the fact that both
\|h\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D}\times[t_{0},T])}
and
\|h^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D}\times[t_{0},T])}
do not exceed
2
. Since the conditional distribution of
X_{t}
given
X_{0}=x
is Gaussian
{\mathcal{N}}(m_{t}x,\sigma_{t}^{2}I_{D})
, we obtain that

\mathbb{E}\|X_{t}-m_{t}x\|^{2}=D\sigma_{t}^{2},

and then

\displaystyle\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}|X_{0}=x}\|s(X_{t},t)-s^{\prime}(X_{t},t)\|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}t\leqslant\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\left(\frac{4(\|x\|^{2}+8)m_{t}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}+\frac{4D}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right){\mathrm{d}}t.

One can simplify the expression in the right-hand side evaluating the integrals

\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{2m_{t}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}{\mathrm{d}}t\quad\text{and}\quad\int\limits_{\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2}}^{\sigma_{T}^{2}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}u}{u(1-u)}.

Indeed, substituting
\sigma_{t}^{2}=1-e^{-2t}
with
u
, it is straightforward to check that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{2m_{t}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}{\mathrm{d}}t=\int\limits_{\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2}}^{\sigma_{T}^{2}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}u}{u^{2}}\leqslant\frac{1}{\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{2}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}}t=\int\limits_{\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2}}^{\sigma_{T}^{2}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}u}{u(1-u)}\leqslant\log(1/\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2})+2(T-t_{0}).

(79)

With these bounds at hand, we conclude that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}|X_{0}=x}\|s(X_{t},t)-s^{\prime}(X_{t},t)\|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}t\leqslant\frac{2(\|x\|^{2}+8)}{\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2}}+2D(\log(1/\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2})+2(T-t_{0})).

Hence, we verified the second statement of the Lemma as a byproduct. Nevertheless, the inequality (
66
) will play an important role in future derivations.

It remains to bound

\displaystyle\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}|X_{0}=x}\left\|s^{\prime}(X_{t},t)+\frac{X_{t}-m_{t}x}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right\|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}t

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}|X_{0}=x}\left\|-\frac{X_{t}}{m_{t}^{2}(\sigma^{\prime})^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}}+\frac{m_{t}h^{\prime}(X_{t},t)}{m_{t}^{2}(\sigma^{\prime})^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}}+\frac{X_{t}-m_{t}x}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right\|{\mathrm{d}}t

to finish the proof. Let us introduce
Z=(X_{t}-m_{t}x)/\sigma_{t}
. Note that, conditionally on
X_{0}=x
, the random vector
Z
has a standard Gaussian distribution
{\mathcal{N}}(0,I_{D})
. This allows us to deduce that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}|X_{0}=x}\left\|s^{\prime}(X_{t},t)+\frac{X_{t}-m_{t}x}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right\|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}t

\displaystyle\leqslant\quad 2\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}|X_{0}=x}\frac{m_{t}^{2}\|x-h^{\prime}(X_{t},t)\|^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}{\mathrm{d}}t+2\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\left(\frac{m_{t}^{2}(\sigma^{\prime})^{2}}{m_{t}^{2}(\sigma^{\prime})^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right)^{2}\frac{\sigma_{t}^{2}\mathbb{E}\|Z\|^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}{\mathrm{d}}t

\displaystyle\leqslant 4(\|x\|^{2}+4)\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{m_{t}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}{\mathrm{d}}t+2D\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}t}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}.

The bound (
79
) yields that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}|X_{0}=x}\left\|s^{\prime}(X_{t},t)+\frac{X_{t}-m_{t}x}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right\|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}t\leqslant\frac{2(\|x\|^{2}+4)}{\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2}}+D\log(1/\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2})+2D(T-t_{0}).

The last inequality, combined with (
78
), (
66
), immediately implies that

\displaystyle(\ell(s,x)-\ell(s^{\prime},x))^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 48\left(\frac{\|x\|^{2}+1}{\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2}}+D\log(1/\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2})+D(T-t_{0})\right)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\left(\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}|X_{0}=x}\|s(X_{t},t)-s^{\prime}(X_{t},t)\|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}t\right).

The proof is finished.

B.2
Proof of Lemma
B.2

The proof follows standard techniques. However, before we move to the upper bound on the Orlicz norm of
\ell(s,X_{0})
, we must first elaborate on properties of the loss function
\ell
. For this reason, we split the proof into two steps.

Step 1: upper bound on
\ell(s,X_{0})
.

The goal of this step is to show that
\ell(s,X_{0})
grows as fast as
{\mathcal{O}}(\|X_{0}\|^{2})
and specify the hidden constant. Let us fix an arbitrary
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and recall that

\ell(s,x)=\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\left(\;\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{D}}\left\|s(y,t)-\nabla_{y}\log\mathsf{p}_{t}(y\,|\,x)\right\|^{2}\mathsf{p}_{t}(y\,|\,x){\mathrm{d}}y\right){\mathrm{d}}t,

where

\displaystyle\nabla_{y}\log\mathsf{p}_{t}(y\,|\,x)=-\frac{y-m_{t}x}{\sigma_{t}^{2}},\quad m_{t}=e^{-t},\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{t}^{2}=1-e^{-2t}.

(80)

Since
\mathsf{p}_{t}(y\mid x)
is the density of the Gaussian distribution
{\mathcal{N}}(m_{t}x,\sigma_{t}^{2})
, we have

\ell(s,x)=\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{t}x,\sigma_{t}^{2}I_{D})}\left\|s(Y,t)+\frac{Y-m_{t}x}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right\|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}t.

Due to the conditions of the lemma, the score function
s(y,t)
has a form

s(y,t)=-\frac{y}{m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}}+\frac{m_{t}\,h(y,t)}{m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}},

where
\|h(y,t)\|\leqslant 2
and
\sigma\in[0,1)
.
Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain that

\displaystyle\left\|s(Y,t)+\frac{Y-m_{t}x}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|-\left(\frac{1}{m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right)(Y-m_{t}x)+\frac{m_{t}x}{m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}}+\frac{m_{t}h(Y,t)}{m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 3\left(\frac{1}{m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right)^{2}\|Y-m_{t}x\|^{2}+\frac{3m_{t}^{2}(\|x\|^{2}+\|h(Y,t)\|^{2})}{(m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}},

and then

\displaystyle\ell(s,x)

\displaystyle\leqslant\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{t}x,\sigma_{t}^{2}I_{D})}\left(\frac{3m_{t}^{2}\|h(Y,t)\|^{2}}{(m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}+\frac{3m_{t}^{2}\|x\|^{2}}{(m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}\right){\mathrm{d}}t

\displaystyle\quad+3\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{t}x,\sigma_{t}^{2}I_{D})}\left(\frac{1}{m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right)^{2}\|Y-m_{t}x\|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}t

\displaystyle\leqslant 3\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{t}x,\sigma_{t}^{2}I_{D})}\left(\frac{4m_{t}^{2}}{m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}}+\frac{m_{t}^{2}\|x\|^{2}}{(m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}+\frac{m_{t}^{4}\sigma^{4}\,\|Y-m_{t}x\|^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{4}(m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}\right){\mathrm{d}}t

\displaystyle=12\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{m_{t}^{2}}{m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}}t+3\|x\|^{2}\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{m_{t}^{2}}{(m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}{\mathrm{d}}t+3D\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{m_{t}^{4}\sigma^{4}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}(m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}{\mathrm{d}}t.

The expression in the right-hand side can be simplified if we note that

\frac{m_{t}^{2}}{m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}}\leqslant\frac{m_{t}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}},\quad\frac{m_{t}^{2}}{(m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}\leqslant\frac{m_{t}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{4}},\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{m_{t}^{4}\sigma^{4}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}(m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}\leqslant\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}.

Indeed, it holds that

\displaystyle\ell(s,x)

\displaystyle\leqslant 12\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{m_{t}^{2}}{m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}}t+3\|x\|^{2}\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{m_{t}^{2}}{(m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}{\mathrm{d}}t+3D\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{m_{t}^{4}\sigma^{4}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}(m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}{\mathrm{d}}t

\displaystyle\leqslant 12\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{m_{t}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}}t+3\|x\|^{2}\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{m_{t}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}{\mathrm{d}}t+3D\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}t}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}

\displaystyle=12\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{e^{-2t}{\mathrm{d}}t}{1-e^{-2t}}+3\|x\|^{2}\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{e^{-2t}{\mathrm{d}}t}{(1-e^{-2t})^{2}}+3D\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}t}{1-e^{-2t}}.

Let us elaborate on the integrals in the right-hand side. Substituting
(1-e^{-2t})
with
u
, we observe that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{e^{-2t}{\mathrm{d}}t}{1-e^{-2t}}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{1-e^{-2t_{0}}}^{1-e^{-2T}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}u}{2u}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\log(1-e^{-2T})-\log(1-e^{-2t_{0}})\right)\leqslant-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-e^{-2t_{0}})

\displaystyle\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{e^{-2t}{\mathrm{d}}t}{(1-e^{-2t})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{1-e^{-2t_{0}}}^{1-e^{-2T}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}u}{2u^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{1-e^{-2t_{0}}}-\frac{1}{1-e^{-2T}}\right)\leqslant\frac{1}{2(1-e^{-2t_{0}})}

and

\displaystyle\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}t}{1-e^{-2t}}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{e^{-2t}{\mathrm{d}}t}{e^{-2t}(1-e^{-2t})}=\int\limits_{1-e^{-2t_{0}}}^{1-e^{-2T}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}u}{2u(1-u)}=\int\limits_{1-e^{-2t_{0}}}^{1-e^{-2T}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}u}{2u}+\int\limits_{1-e^{-2t_{0}}}^{1-e^{-2T}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}u}{2(1-u)}

\displaystyle\leqslant-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-e^{-2t_{0}})+(T-t_{0}).

Hence, we obtain that

\displaystyle\ell(s,x)

\displaystyle\leqslant 12\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{e^{-2t}{\mathrm{d}}t}{1-e^{-2t}}+3\|x\|^{2}\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{e^{-2t}{\mathrm{d}}t}{(1-e^{-2t})^{2}}+3D\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}t}{1-e^{-2t}}

\displaystyle\leqslant-6\log(1-e^{-2t_{0}})+\frac{3\|x\|^{2}\,e^{-2t_{0}}}{2(1-e^{-2t_{0}})}-\frac{3D}{2}\log(1-e^{-2t_{0}})+3D(T-t_{0})

\displaystyle=-\frac{3}{2}(4+D)\log(1-e^{-2t_{0}})+\frac{3\|x\|^{2}\,e^{-2t_{0}}}{2(1-e^{-2t_{0}})}+3D(T-t_{0}).

In view of the definition of
\sigma_{t}^{2}
(see (
80
)), we conclude that

\displaystyle\ell(s,x)\leqslant\frac{3}{2}(4+D)\log(\sigma_{t_{0}}^{-2})+\frac{3\|x\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2}}+3D(T-t_{0}),\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}.

(81)

Step 2: upper bound on the Orlicz norm

The current step aims to provide the upper bound for the desired Orlicz norm through the analysis of its exponential moment.
The bound (
81
) yields that for any
\lambda>0
the exponential moment of
\lambda\ell(s,X_{0})
does not exceed

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}\exp\{\lambda\ell(s,X_{0})\}\leqslant\exp\left\{\frac{3\lambda}{2}(4+D)\log(\sigma_{t_{0}}^{-2})+3\lambda D(T-t_{0})\right\}\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}\exp\left\{\frac{3\lambda\|X_{0}\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2}}\right\}.

According to Assumption
3.1
, we have that
X_{0}=g^{*}(U)+\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}Z
, where
U\sim\mathrm{Un}([0,1]^{d})
and
Z\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,I_{D})
are independent.
Moreover, it holds that
\|g^{*}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}\leqslant 1
.
Therefore, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}\exp\left\{\frac{3\lambda\|X_{0}\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2}}\right\}\leqslant\exp\left\{\frac{3\lambda}{\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2}}\right\}\mathbb{E}_{Z}\exp\left\{\frac{3\lambda\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}^{2}\|Z\|^{2}}{\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2}}\right\}.

Since
\|Z\|^{2}\sim\chi^{2}(D)
, it follows from Remark
F.4
that
\|Z\|^{2}
is sub-exponential random variable with parameters
(2\sqrt{D},4)
.
Therefore, using
(
Vershynin, 2018
, Proposition 2.7.1)
, we conclude that there exists an absolute constant
C_{\psi}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Z}\exp\left\{\frac{3\lambda\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}^{2}\|Z\|^{2}}{\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2}}\right\}\leqslant\exp\left\{\frac{\lambda C_{\psi}D\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}^{2}}{\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2}}\right\}

for sufficiently small
\lambda
.
Hence, we obtain that

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}\exp\{\lambda\ell(s,X_{0})\}\leqslant\frac{3\lambda}{2}(4+D)\log(\sigma_{t_{0}}^{-2})+3\lambda D(T-t_{0})+\frac{3\lambda}{\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2}}+\frac{\lambda C_{\psi}D\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}^{2}}{\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2}}.

Taking into account that
\ell(s,X_{0})
is non-negative almost surely and using
(
Vershynin, 2018
, Proposition 2.7.1)
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\|\ell(s,X_{0})\|_{\psi_{1}}\lesssim D\log(\sigma_{t_{0}}^{-2})+D(T-t_{0})+\frac{D\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}^{2}+1}{\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2}},

where the hidden constant does not depend on
s
and the problem parameters.
The proof is complete.

Proof of Lemma

To improve clarity, we divided the proof into several steps.

, for any

(88)

ensures that

\displaystyle\|\ell(s_{1},\cdot)-\ell(s_{2},\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{B}}(0,R_{\text{data}}))}\vee|\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}[\ell(s_{1},X_{0})-\ell(s_{2},X_{0})]|\leqslant\tau/2.

(89)

Step 2: covering number evaluation.
The following result elaborates on the covering number of the

Lemma B.4

(
Suzuki (2019)
, Lemma 3)
.

For any
\tau>0
the covering number of

can be bounded by

\displaystyle\log{\mathcal{N}}(\tau,\mathsf{NN}(L,W,S,B),\|\cdot\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{D})})\lesssim SL\log\left(\tau^{-1}L(\|W\|_{\infty}+1)(B\vee 1)\right).

By leveraging Lemma

, we can infer that multiplying the weight matrix of the initial layer by

\|W\|_{\infty}\lesssim\log(1/\varepsilon)+\log(\widetilde{\sigma}_{t_{0}}^{-2})+\log(1\vee 1/(t_{0}+\Delta_{\sigma})).

It only remains to note that Jensen’s inequality and the definition of
\Delta_{\sigma}
yield that

\displaystyle\log(1\vee 1/(t_{0}+\Delta_{\sigma}))\lesssim\log(1\vee 1/(t_{0}+\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}^{2}))\lesssim\log(\widetilde{\sigma}_{t_{0}}^{-2}).

In other words, the configuration of
\chi_{\gamma,\varepsilon}

has the required form (
96
).
Finally, applying the triangle inequality once again and taking (
97
) and (
98
) into account, we obtain that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[t_{0},T]}\left|\chi_{\gamma,\varepsilon}(t)-\frac{m_{t}^{\gamma}}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\right|\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\sup_{t\in[t_{0},T]}\left|\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}e^{-\gamma t-2k(t+\Delta_{\sigma})}-\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}e^{-2k\Delta_{\sigma}}\phi_{\mathrm{exp}}((\gamma+2k)t)\right|\leqslant\varepsilon.

This concludes the proof.

E.2
Proof of Lemma

Let
\phi_{\mathrm{mult}}(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime})

(99)

In particular, since

Appendix F
Tools from probability theory

This section collects a couple of useful results from probability theory used in the proof of Theorem
3.3
.

Proposition F.1

(
Azangulov et al. (2024)
, Proposition 23)
.

Let
\mathsf{P}
and
\mathsf{Q}
be arbitrary compactly supported measures such that
W_{2}(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Q})<\infty
, where
W_{2}
stands for the Kantorovich distance. Given independent random elements
X\sim\mathsf{P}
,
Y\sim\mathsf{Q}
,
Z\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,I_{D})
in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, let
X_{t}=c_{t}X+\sigma_{t}Z_{D}
and
Y_{t}=c_{t}Y+\sigma_{t}Z_{D}
be two random processes initialized at
X
and
Y
, respectively. For any
t\geqslant 0
, let
\mathsf{p}_{t}
and
\mathsf{q}_{t}
stand for the probability density functions (with respect to the Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
) of
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
, respectively. Then, for any
t_{\max}\geqslant t_{\min}\geqslant 0
, it holds that

\int\limits_{t_{\min}}^{t_{\max}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{D}}\left\|\nabla\log\mathsf{p}_{t}(x)-\nabla\log\mathsf{q}_{t}(x)\right\|^{2}\,\mathsf{p}_{t}(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}t\leqslant W_{2}^{2}(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Q})\,\frac{c_{t_{\min}}^{2}}{4\sigma_{t_{\min}}^{2}}.

Remark F.2
.

In
(
Azangulov et al., 2024
)
, the authors assumed that
c_{t}=e^{-t}
and
\sigma_{t}^{2}=1-e^{-2t}
. However, careful inspection of the proof reveals that Proposition 23 from
(
Azangulov et al., 2024
)
remains valid for arbitrary
c_{t}
and
\sigma_{t}
.

Proposition F.3

(
Wainwright (2019)
, Proposition 2.2)
.

Suppose that
X
is a sub-exponential random variable with parameters
(\nu,b)
, that is

\mathbb{E}e^{\lambda(X-\mathbb{E}X)}\leqslant e^{\nu^{2}\lambda^{2}/2}\quad\text{for all $\lambda$ such that $|\lambda|\leqslant 1/b$.}

Then, for any
t\geqslant 0
, it holds that

\mathbb{P}\left(X\geqslant\mathbb{E}X+t\right)\leqslant\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{t^{2}}{\nu^{2}}\land\frac{t}{b}\right)\right\}.

Remark F.4
.

According to
(
Wainwright, 2019
, Example 2.5)
, chi-squared random variable with
D
degrees of freedom is sub-exponential with parameters
(2\sqrt{D},4)
. This yields that, if
X\sim\chi^{2}(D)
, then

\mathbb{P}\left(X\geqslant D+t\right)\leqslant\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{8}\left(\frac{t^{2}}{D}\land t\right)\right\}\quad\text{for all $t\geqslant 0$.}
