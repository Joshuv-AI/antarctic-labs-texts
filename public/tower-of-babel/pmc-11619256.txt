The epidemiological trends and predictions of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma and gastroesophageal junction carcinoma: an Australian population-based study
Authors: Shalika Bohingamu Mudiyanselage, Dieu Nguyen, Boxiong Tang, Rhys Williams, Mostafa Kamal, Fei-Li Zhao, Lan Gao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGastroesophageal junction carcinoma (GEJC) and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) are increasing in Australia and other Western countries. We aimed to investigate past trends and predict the future direction of GEJC and ESCC cases.MethodsData on GEJC and ESCC were extracted from the Australian Cancer Database, and the National Mortality Database for the Australian states of Victoria, Queensland, Tasmania, and the Australian Capital Territory. The new number of cases were predicted up to 2039 by fitting a least squares linear regression using the Australian incidence rates trend from 2009 to 2018. Past trends were analyzed for prevalence and mortality. Kaplan-Meier curves generated the survival trend for up to 10 years.ResultsBetween 2009 and 2018, the overall incidences of GEJC showed an increasing trend (annual change [β1] = 0.87, P < 0.05). For GEJC, the rates of incidence, prevalence, and mortality were higher in men (β1 = 1.86, 95% CI: 1.29–2.43, P < 0.05; β1 = 14.45, 95% CI: 12.93–15.98, P < 0.05; β1 = 1.04, 95% CI: − 0.68 to 2.76, P = 0.195, respectively) than in women. By 2039, it is estimated that 6 in 100,000 population will be newly diagnosed with GEJC. Notably, the rate of new GEJC cases decreased in women during the study period (β1=–0.08, 95% CI: − 0.39 to 0.24, P = 0.596). For ESCC, the incidence rate increased, albeit at a slower rate compared with GEJC (β1 = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.6–1.15, P < 0.05; β1 = 0.03, 95% CI: − 0.28 to 0.34, P = 0.805, respectively). New cases of ESCC in men declined (β1=–0.03, 95% CI: − 0.46 to 0.4, P = 0.884). By 2039, it is predicted that 2 in 100,000 people will be newly diagnosed. The 10-year survival rate for GEJC and ESCC was low (11% and 20%, respectively), while the survival rate for women was relatively higher than for men.ConclusionThe incidence, prevalence, and mortality would be predicted to increase in 2039 if there were no significant changes in risk factors, diagnosis, treatment, and management compared with 2009–2018. Strategies to identify early signs and enable earlier diagnosis and early and new treatment options are essential in GEJC and ESCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-024-13151-3.

Introduction

Esophageal cancer includes squamous cell carcinoma, adenocarcinoma, sarcomas, small cell carcinomas, lymphomas, and melanomas [1]. Squamous cell carcinoma (begins in the cells that line the esophagus) and adenocarcinoma (begins near the gastroesophageal junction) are the most common esophageal cancers, worldwide [1, 2]. Based on metastatic location and type of cell where cancer originates, adenocarcinomas are referred to as gastroesophageal junction carcinoma (GEJC) and squamous cell carcinoma as esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) [2].

GEJC is more prevalent in developed countries and represents the most common type of esophageal cancer in Australia [3]. In 2020, esophageal cancer ranked 7th globally in incidence and 6th in mortality rate [4]. Although esophageal cancer is rare [3], it is often not diagnosed until it has metastasized [5, 6]. According to Cancer Australia, the age-standardized esophageal cancer incidence rate was 5.4 cases per 100,000 persons in 2018 and is estimated to be 5.1 cases per 100,000 persons in 2022 [7], with the average diagnosis age of 71 years old [3].

Trends of ESCC and GEJC were studied in other countries [8]. A US study found that GEJC was stably increasing from 1992 to 2000 (annual percentage change [β1] = 5.17 [95% CI: 3.28–7.10%]) [8]. Meanwhile, the ESCC rates have steadily declined (β1 = − 0.54% [95% CI: −0.75% to − 0.33%]) [8]. The decreasing incidence of ESCC was observed consistently across age groups, sex, and race/ethnicity, while GEJC rates varied across these subgroups in the USA [8]. A Swedish study revealed (using data from 1970 to 2008) that GEJC has been stabilizing since 2001 [9]. Two Australian-based studies reported the increased trend of GEJC incidence over 1972–2005 and 1982–1993, but did not report the trend in ESCC [10, 11]. A global study (2020) showed a 2.2% (95% CI: 1.5–2.9) increase in the GEJC incidence in Australia between 1994 and 2008 [12]. The global trend in esophageal cancers has indicated that Australia is a higher-risk country among other Western counterparts [13].

Due to the lack of more recent Australian epidemiological studies around esophageal cancer and its subtypes, this study aimed to examine the current epidemiology and investigate the future directions of GEJC and ESCC, particularly (1) quantifying the present (2009–2018) incidence, prevalence, and mortality rates of GEJC and ESCC; (2) predicting the incidence for 20 years (2019–2039) using existing data (2009–2018); and (3) undertaking the survival analysis for GEJC and ESCC in Australia.

Method

Study design and population

This retrospective population study included Australian patients diagnosed with GEJC and ESCC between 2009 and 2018 from 4 Australian states (Victoria, Queensland, Tasmania, and the Australian Capital Territory). The population of these four states is about 50% of the total Australian population.

Data collection

The data were extracted from the Australian Cancer Database, Australian Cancer Registries, and the National Mortality Database by the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare using pre-defined data fields. The pre-defined data fields included the patients’ sex, year of diagnosis, age group at diagnosis, cancer type, diagnosis codes (ICD-O-3 [International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, 3rd Edition] topography code, ICD-O-3 histology code, ICD-10 disease code), age group at death, cause of death, and survival duration (rounded up to the nearest multiple of 5). The registries have followed the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (ICD) guidelines [14] when diagnosing and registering cancer patients. The ICD-10 code for ESCC was C15.0 – C15.9, and for GEJC was C.16. The employed histology code for ESCC was 8050–8089, and for GEJC were 8140–8149, 8160–8162, 8190–8221, 8260–8337, 8350–8551, 8570–8576, 8940–8941, and 8050–8089. We did not have access to data for other territories (Christmas Island, Cocos [Keeling] Islands, Jervis Bay, and Norfolk Island), late registrations, or gender other than male or female. The population size data were extracted from the Australian Bureau of Statistics report containing Australian population data from 2001 to 2021.

Mortality indicators

The mortality in this study represents the all-cause mortality at esophageal cancer diagnosis year. We used the incidence-based mortality [15] (2009–2018) to extract the deaths meeting our mortality based on histological and ICD guidelines confirmed diagnosis (as above). Consistent with previous studies, we did not include deaths that happened in previous years in our mortality analyses, mainly to match the mortality cohort to the incidence cohort [16]. In addition, we used survival as a proxy to measure mortality. Due to the absence of incidence and mortality data before 2009, we only reported trends in mortality and prevalence (based on incidence-based mortality) from 2010 to 2018 and did not predict mortality and prevalence cases for the next 20 years.

Outcome measures

The incidence, prevalence, and mortality rates of ESCC and GEJC were calculated based on the cancer registry data and the specific Australian mid-year population size. Subgroup analysis by sex was conducted for the corresponding incidence, prevalence, and mortality rate. The incidence trends in the next 20 years were predicted using the coefficients identified from least square simple linear regression constructed from the 2009 to 2018 data [17].

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using Microsoft Excel and STATA 17 [18]. The incidence rates were calculated per 1,000,000 person-years, and prevalence and mortality rates were per 1,000,000 population. The P-value of < 0.05 was set as being statistically significant.

The incidence rates were calculated by dividing the “newly diagnosed cancer cases” by the “average mid-year population” [19].

The population and mortalities from 1st July were accounted for the following year. The mortality and prevalence were calculated from 2010 as we did not collect data for 2008 to calculate mid-year case numbers for 2009.

Results

A total of 6488 (68% males) were diagnosed with ESCC (2698, 54% males) and GEJC (3790, 78% males) between 2009 and 2018 (Supplementary Table 1) in Victoria, Queensland, Tasmania, and Australian Capital Territory.

Prevalence

The annual point prevalence of GEJC was generally higher than ESCC between 2010 and 2018 (Fig. 2A; Table 1). Within subtypes, the prevalence of GEJC was higher in males (Fig. 2B) and the prevalence of ESCC was greater in females (Fig. 2C).

Gastroesophageal junction carcinoma

Incidence - GEJC

The overall incidence rate trend of the GEJC was increasing (2009–2018; Fig. 5A). The incidence, prevalence, and mortality were higher for males than for females for GEJC (Fig. 5). The incidence was predicted to rise in the overall population (29.92 to 54.37, an 82% increase over 30 years) and in the male population (44.74 to 97.69, a 118% increase over 30 years; Supplementary Table 2). In males, the average annual growth was higher at 1.86 ([95% CI: 1.29–2.43], P = < 0.05) per 1,000,000 person-years (Table 1). The GEJC in females showed a negative trend (15.26 to 12.35, a 19% decrease over 30 years), with a − 0.08 ([95% CI: − 0.39 to 0.24], P = 0.596) per 1,000,000 person-years average annual decline (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 2).

Incidences, annual point prevalence, and mortality of GEJC. (A) per 1,000,000 person-years, (B) and (C) 1,000,000 population. Abbreviation GEJC, gastroesophageal junction carcinoma

Annual point prevalence - GEJC

The regression analysis showed an average prevalence of 21.66 ([95% CI: 17.06–26.26], P = < 0.05) per 1,000,000 population and an average annual point prevalence change of 8.86 ([95% CI: 8.04–9.67], P = < 0.05) over 2010–2018 (Table 1). The average point prevalence was higher in males with 32.36 ([95% CI: 23.76–40.96], P = < 0.05) per 1,000,000 population, compared with females with 11.01 ([95% CI: 9.59–12.42], P = < 0.05) per 1,000,000 population (Table 1). The prevalence grew rapidly in males, with an annual average increase of 14.45 ([95% CI: 12.93–15.98], P = < 0.05) per 1,000,000 population over the 10-year study period (Fig. 5B; Table 1).

Mortality - GEJC

The average mortality between 2010 and 2018 was 19.70 ([95% CI: 13.84–25.56], P = < 0.05) per 1,000,000 population overall. It was higher in males 20.27 ([95% CI: 20.27–39.6], P = < 0.05) per 1,000,000 population compared with females 9.56 ([95% CI: 6.34–12.78], P = < 0.05) per 1,000,000 population (Fig. 5C; Table 1).

Survival - GEJC

According to Kaplan-Meier survival estimates, males (20%) and females (21%) diagnosed with GEJC had similar survival rates without significant differences between sexes (hazard ratio 1.00 [95% CI: 0.91–1.10], P = 0.931; Fig. 6).

Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma

Incidence - ESCC

The overall ESCC incidence rates showed a slight increase (3%) in 2018 compared with 2009. The incidence increased by 14% from 2009 to 2039 in the overall population (Supplementary Table 1). The incidence, prevalence, and mortality rates in males were higher than in females for ESCC (Fig. 7). However, the average annual growth in males showed a negative trend of − 0.03 ([95% CI: − 0.46 to 0.4], P = 0.884) per 1,000,000 person-years (Table 1), indicating a potential decreasing incidence trend.

Fig. 7Incidences, annual point prevalence, and mortality of ESCC. (A) per 1,000,000 person-years, (B) and (C) 1,000,000 population. Abbreviation ESCC, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma

Incidences, annual point prevalence, and mortality of ESCC. (A) per 1,000,000 person-years, (B) and (C) 1,000,000 population. Abbreviation ESCC, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma

Annual point prevalence - ESCC

The regression analysis conducted for the annual point prevalence showed an average of 18.36 ([95% CI: 17.06–19.65], P < 0.05) per 1,000,000 population and an annual point prevalence change of 4.42 ([95% CI: 4.19–4.65], P < 0.05) per 1,000,000 population over 2010–2018. The average annual point prevalence was slightly higher in males at 17.89 ([95% CI: 12.53–23.25], P < 0.05) per 1,000,000 population compared with females at 16.91 ([95% CI: 15.12–18.71], P < 0.05) per 1,000,000 population (Table 1). Both males and females had similar average annual changes from 2010 to 2018 while continuing to move closer toward 2039 (Fig. 1; Table 1).

Mortality - ESCC

The average mortality across the years 2010–2018 was greater in males (19.55 [95% CI: 11.98–27.12]) per 1,000,000 population when compared with females (14.34 [95% CI: 10.61–18.07]) per 1,000,000 population. However, female ESCC-related mortalities increased rapidly at 0.22 (95% CI: − 0.44 to 0.89) per year per 1,000,000 population, a higher rate than in males at 0.13 ([95% CI:–1.21 to 1.48, P = 0.824]) per year per 1,000,000 population (Fig. 7B).

Survival – ESCC

According to Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, females (16%) had a higher 10-year survival rate than males (9%), with a hazard ratio of 0.77 ([95% CI: 0.70–0.84], P = < 0.05; Fig. 8).

Discussion

Our study predicts that in 2039, 24 in 1,000,000 people will be diagnosed with ESCC and 55 in 1,000,000 people will be diagnosed with GEJC. Our study showed that incidence, annual point prevalence, and mortality in GEJC are rising at a faster rate than for ESCC, especially among men. The trend identified was in line with trends in Australia [11] and other Western countries [10, 12, 20]. Our incidence predictions were slightly different from a prior study that included Australia, in which the incidence for ESCC was 1.52 and GEJC was 3.68 per 100,000 men in 2030 [21]. They also projected higher incidence in the UK and the Netherlands (1 in 100 men would be diagnosed with GEJC) given future changes in risk factors, age structure, and population size in these 2 countries [21]. However, compared with our study, data directly collected from Australian government databases (for 4 states), the prior study used Australian GEJC and ESCC data from the International Agency for Research on Cancer, Cancer Incidences in Five Continents report (all states) [22] and their Australian population data were based on the United Nations World Population Prospects report [23].

Men showed higher average incidence, annual point prevalence, and mortality between 2009 and 2018 for GEJC and ESCC. The incidence of ESCC in men decreased nominally, with a statistically non-significant negative annual average (–0.03 [(95% CI: − 0.46 to 0.40), P = 0.884] per 1,000,000 person-years). In line with an esophagus cancer projection model (1971–2037), they also predicted an overall decrease in ESCC in England in both sexes [24]. The potential downward trend in male ESCC incidence identified in our study may be attributed to heightened awareness of the elevated risk of esophageal cancer in men and changes in risk factors over time. For instance, smoking is a prominent risk factor for ESCC, and data from Australia reveal a 15% decrease in the percentage of regular male smokers between 1995 and 2019 (from 29 to 14%) [25].

Meanwhile, our study showed a potential decreasing trend (average annual change − 0.08 [(95% CI: − 0.39 to 0.24), P = 0.596] per 1,000,000 person-years) in female GEJC incidences. The possible causes for this reduction could be women’s improved risk profiles including diet (daily vegetable consumption increased up to 56% between 1979 and 2017) [26] and reduction in smoking [27], which overlapped well with our study period.

The development of ESCC and GEJC is influenced by various sex-specific risk factors [28, 29]. These factors encompass alcohol intake, tobacco use, obesity, low socioeconomic status, gastroesophageal reflux disease, and Barrett’s esophagus. For instance, Barrett’s esophagus is a significant risk factor for GEJC and is prevalent in Western countries. In particular, men with Barrett’s esophagus were twice as likely to develop GEJC compared with women [30, 31]. The higher incidences of GEJC and ESCC in men may be attributed to their increased risk of Barrett’s esophagus and the prevalence of other risk factors, such as smoking and alcohol consumption, which are more common in men than in women [32, 33].

In our study, the annual point prevalence of ESCC and GEJC rose between 2009 and 2018. The reason could be the higher annual increasing incidence rate (GEJC; β1 = 1.86 [(95% CI: 1.29–2.43), P < 0.05] per 1,000,000 population) compared with mortality rates (GEJC; β1 = 1.04 [(95% CI: − 0.68 to 2.76), P = 0.195] per 1,000,000 population) between 2009 and 2018. This may well account for the growing prevalence of these 2 subtypes.

Our study revealed a concerning pattern of poor survival rates among patients diagnosed with GEJC and ESCC, with no improvements observed over the study period. Late diagnosis appears to be a key contributing factor, as a significant proportion of cases (around 30%) were detected only after metastasis had occurred [34, 35]. The stage at which cancer is initially diagnosed is a crucial determinant of survival outcomes. In Australia, the 5-year survival rate for individuals diagnosed with metastatic-stage disease is alarmingly low at 4%, compared with a more favorable 25% for those diagnosed at localized stages [35]. Notably, early diagnosis of esophageal cancer is associated with higher 3-year survival rates, with GEJC at 56.5% and ESCC at 51.7%, in contrast to substantially lower rates of 9.5% for GEJC and 17.2% for ESCC at advanced stages [36]. In Queensland, Australia, 5-year survival for esophageal cancer increased from 6.7 to 20.4% for GEJC, or 10.1–19.3% for ESCC, between 1982 and 1996 [37] while our results suggested the survival improved slightly between 2009 and 2018. Other factors like sex, age (below and above 50), family history, diagnosis or treatment delay, and cancer location of gastroesophageal junction also influence the survival rates [36]. However, due to the unavailability of specific characteristic variables for further analysis, we were unable to explore these factors in greater detail within our study.

One of the strengths of this study was the utility of the past 10 years of GEJC and ESCC data from the Australian Cancer Database, Australian Cancer Registries, and the National Mortality Database to predict the incidence trends in the next 20 years and examine the trend within the observed period (2009–2018). To our knowledge, this is the latest study that has used the most recent data covering most of Australia (4 out of 8 states) and predicted future GEJC and ESCC trends by sex.

This study is not without limitations. First, although our study used data for 10 years (2009–2018) to predict incidence rates, the mortality and prevalence rates were examined only using 9 years of data due to the absence of data for 2008. Second, future predictions were made assuming no significant changes in risk factors, population characteristics, diagnosis, treatment, and management strategies between 2019 and 2039. Lastly, we did not examine the differences between incidence, prevalence, and mortality by disease-specific characteristics (e.g., stages).

We recommend that future research on the incidence, prevalence, mortality, and survival predictions use all Australian data and consider age groups and other population characteristics (e.g., geographical location, socioeconomic status), risk factors, and cancer stages. Given the increasing incidence and sub-optimal survival following GEJC and ESCC diagnosis, public health policy should be formulated to ensure early access to healthcare defined by the Optimal Care Pathway [38], as well as the information and education to improve prevention and early diagnosis.

Conclusions

GEJC and ESCC are rare cancers in Australia. The incidence, prevalence, and mortality will continue to rise if there are no significant changes in risk factors, diagnosis, treatment, and management compared with 2009–2018. Strategies to identify early signs and enable earlier diagnosis and early treatment with new treatment options are essential in GEJC and ESCC diagnosis and management in Australia. Further research is needed when predicting future incidence, prevalence, and mortality, considering individuals’ age, risk factors, and cancer stages.
