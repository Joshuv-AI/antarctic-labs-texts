Paleogeographic controls on Phanerozoic weathering rates and implications for climate regulation
Authors: R. J. Graham, B. B. Cael, M. Fu, D. S. Abbot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Earth’s climate has remained habitable for billions of years, a stability usually attributed to a negative feedback from silicate weathering – the chemical breakdown of silicate rocks on land, which consumes atmospheric carbon dioxide faster as climate warms. However, whether temperature or hydrology primarily drives this feedback remains uncertain. Here we show the two controls leave geographic fingerprints: in an ensemble of climate simulations with randomized continents, temperature-controlled weathering depends on tropical land fraction, whereas hydrology-controlled weathering depends on continental fragmentation. Stronger weathering removes more carbon dioxide, cooling the planet, so each regime predicts anti-correlation between temperature and its controlling geography. Reconstructed global temperatures spanning 480 million years are anti-correlated with tropical land fraction, except from roughly 295 to 200 million years ago – overlapping the supercontinent Pangea – when they anti-correlate with continental fragmentation. The mechanism stabilizing Earth’s climate may thus have evolved, with temperature control apparently dominant for most of this period.

Introduction

Earth’s climate is regulated on long (105 to 106 year) timescales by stabilizing negative feedbacks within the carbonate-silicate cycle1,2. Silicate weathering reactions on Earth’s surface and seafloor release solutes that enter the ocean and react with oceanic carbon that is in chemical equilibrium with CO2 in Earth’s atmosphere to form carbonate minerals. This process is the primary inorganic sink of CO2 outgassed from Earth’s interior, and a combination of theoretical arguments, laboratory experiments, and observational evidence[e.g., refs. 1,3–5] suggest that weathering reactions accelerate under warmer conditions, providing a feedback mechanism to regulate Earth’s climate: When the planet warms, silicate weathering speeds up, accelerating the removal of CO2 from the atmosphere. This carbon cycle response leads to CO2 drawdown and climatic cooling, which in turn slows the silicate weathering rate until CO2 is being removed from the atmosphere at the same pace that CO2 is added by volcanic outgassing. The same process happens in reverse in response to climatic cooling. Although transient imbalances between CO2 inputs and outputs due to organic carbon interactions6 or limitations in silicate mineral supply7 may have played important roles in Earth’s climate evolution, weathering-based coupling between atmospheric CO2 removal and background climate appears to play a key role in maintaining the quasi-equilibrium in the carbon cycle necessary to avoid frequent excursions to extremely high or low surface temperatures that would have threatened the existence of complex life on Earth during the Phanerozoic8,9.

However, despite a general agreement among researchers about the importance of negative feedback within the carbonate-silicate cycle in sustaining Earth’s habitability, the details of the mechanism(s) mediating this stabilization through Earth's history remain highly uncertain. In this study, we examine a fundamental open question regarding the silicate weathering feedback on land [e.g., refs. 10–16]: Is its coupling to the climate mediated primarily by temperature in the weathering zone, or does hydrology play a stronger role? Many attempts to model Earth’s silicate weathering feedback have based their estimates of the temperature-dependence of Earth’s weathering rate on direct laboratory measurements of the kinetics of silicate dissolution[e.g., ref. 17]. But some recent modeling and theoretical studies have argued that the apparent global temperature-dependence of silicate weathering is significantly weaker than would be expected from laboratory measurements[e.g.4,5,18]. One potential explanation for this discrepancy is that the feedback between climate and weathering may not be rooted directly in the kinetics of silicate dissolution, but is instead a result of the climate-dependence of the flux of water through the weathering zone12,19. Calculations of weathering rates that account for physicochemical interactions between water, dissolving silicates, and the secondary minerals (clays and silica) that precipitate during silicate weathering suggest that, depending on the local lithology, silicate weathering reactions can reach a chemical equilibrium where solutes in runoff are saturated, preventing further net silicate dissolution until unsaturated water flows through–a regime sometimes referred to as “chemostatic” or “runoff-limited” weathering12,20–22. In this regime, climate influences weathering through its influence on hydrologic intensity, with greater fluxes of water leading to proportional increases in silicate weathering, largely independent of local temperature. Since global runoff has a much weaker dependence on temperature than the kinetic temperature dependence of silicate weathering, hydrologically regulated weathering leads to a weaker apparent temperature-dependence and a significantly less effective thermostat compared to silicate weathering based on laboratory measurements of silicate dissolution19.

Although the behavior of silicate weathering and its interaction with the climate is still subject to debate, a substantial volume of work attempts to connect changes in paleogeography during the Phanerozoic Eon to changes in climate and CO2 through the weathering feedback using models of widely varying complexity [e.g., refs. 23–32]. Early models, such as refs. 23,24 assumed a strong temperature control on weathering with no direct runoff-dependence and used bulk estimates of land area and average landmass latitude to predict climate as a function of paleogeography. The GEOCARB and GEOCARBSULF lineage of global-mean box models26 used relationships derived from GCM studies like that of33 to represent the impact of paleogeography on global-mean runoff and temperature, allowing for an effect on climate through a global weathering relationship based on both an assumed strong temperature sensitivity and a moderate runoff sensitivity. The GEOCLIM lineage of models[e.g., refs. 29,30] and successors like SCION31 couple weathering parameterizations with strong temperature-dependence and strong (linear) runoff-dependence to output from GCM simulations in order to calculate spatially resolved weathering fluxes, allowing for investigations of climate and CO2 evolution that directly account for paleogeographic changes in Earth history[e.g., refs. 27,28,34–39]. However, these models all take their chosen weathering feedback strengths for temperature and runoff as given (modulo occasional sensitivity tests), introducing a major source of uncertainty into their results and reconstructions.

Here, to assist in differentiating between signatures of hydrology-controlled and temperature-controlled weathering in Earth history, we analyze a large ensemble of GCM simulations that are identical except for their randomized continental configurations and post-process their runoff and temperature fields to estimate global weathering rates from them. This allows us to demonstrate that these alternative weathering controls lead to alternative predictions for Earth’s climate response to changes in aspects of bulk planetary continental geometry. Then, we statistically compare proxy-based reconstructions of Earth’s climate and land configuration to assess whether the geologic record supports one hypothesis over the other. Ultimately, our calculations are consistent with the hypothesis that Earth’s weathering control mechanism shifted twice in the last 480 Ma, from a temperature-based to a hydrology-based configuration ≈ 300 Ma and then back to a temperature-based configuration ≈ 200 Ma, though this relies on assumptions like weakly varying outgassing, a tight relationship between CO2 and surface temperature, and the accuracy of uncertain paleogeographic reconstructions. Still, the results provide useful new insight into the structure of the carbon cycle which may lead to improved understanding of the evolution of Earth’s climate over the Phanerozoic.

Results

Sensitivity of silicate weathering to tropical land fraction and continental fragmentation

We note for clarity that these weathering values are all calculated with the same background CO2 and global temperature and might therefore be better thought of as values of bulk “global weatherability,” since Earth’s climate would in reality adjust to bring the global weathering rate into balance with the background CO2 outgassing rate if a change to continental geometry were to cause a large transient shift in weathering. In other words, these calculations are not intended to imply that the equilibrium global weathering rate has varied by this magnitude over Earth history (since the equilibrium global rate of weathering is set by outgassing), but instead to illustrate the degree to which changes in continental geometry can act as carbon cycle forcings under different assumptions about global weathering controls41. Several studies have identified changing “weatherability” as a potential major driver behind climate changes during the Phanerozoic Eon due to its influence on the equilibrium point of the carbon cycle and climate system42–46.

To quantify the apparent relationships between weathering and continental geometry, we calculated coefficients of determination between the global weathering rate and TLF and continental fragmentation for our GCM ensemble over a wide range of values for k (temperature-dependence of weathering; see eqn. (2) in Methods) and α (runoff-dependence of weathering; see eqn. (2)), with results shown in Fig. 3. When the weathering feedback is dominated by hydrology instead of temperature (large α, small k), the coefficient of determination between the total global weathering rate (Wtot) and the fragmentation index, R2(Wtot, fragmentation index), can exceed 50% (bottom right corner of top panel in Fig. 3), while R2(Wtot, TLF) falls to nearly zero (bottom right corner of bottom panel in Fig. 3). Conversely, when temperature regulation dominates over hydrology (small α, large k), R2(Wtot, fragmentation index) falls to near zero, while R2(Wtot, TLF) climbs to more than 75%.

The reason for this divergence in response to continental geometry is straightforward. Within the GCM ensemble, global runoff is uncorrelated with TLF (R2 = 0%) but strongly correlated with continental fragmentation (R2 = 54%; see Fig. 4a, b). The lack of correlation between TLF and runoff is due to an increase in land evaporation in high-TLF simulations that compensates for increased land precipitation, similar to an effect observed by ref. 33. This occurs because tropical land intercepts more solar energy, allowing for more efficient recycling of water despite increased overall delivery to the land via precipitation in the wet tropics. Increasing fragmentation does not increase the ease of delivery of solar energy to land, but it does increase delivery of water, so runoff tends to increase as continents break up. So, when weathering is dominated by hydrology, changes to TLF have a minimal effect due to small changes in runoff, but more fragmented configurations produce more runoff, accelerating weathering.Fig. 4Scatter plots of average values of different variables from the global climate model (GCM) ensemble.a shows the lack of correlation between runoff and tropical land fraction (TLF); b shows a relatively strong correlation between runoff and fragmentation; c shows a weak correlation between global-mean surface temperature and TLF; d shows no correlation between global-mean surface temperature and fragmentation; e shows a strong correlation between global-mean land temperature and TLF; and f shows a lack of correlation between global-mean land temperature and fragmentation.

Conversely, although global-mean surface temperature is weakly correlated with TLF (R2 = 16%) and uncorrelated with continental fragmentation (R2 = 0%; see Fig. 4c, d), global-mean land temperature is strongly correlated with TLF (R2 = 71%), while remaining uncorrelated with fragmentation (R2 = 1%; see Fig. 4e, f). So, when temperature is the main driver of weathering, changes to TLF can change the weathering rate by altering the amount of land that experiences higher temperatures near the equator. The small positive relationship between global-mean surface temperature and TLF visible in Fig. 4c is due to an increase in high-albedo land snow in low-TLF simulations where land is colder and closer to the poles, since snow on land has higher albedo than sea ice in these simulations. Warmer global conditions moderate this effect: a smaller ensemble of simulations with 30 of the 120 paleogeographies from this paper and40 with doubled CO2 (2000 ppm) showed a TLF-global temperature correlation with an even weaker R2 of 2% (see Supplementary Fig. 4 in Supplementary Information Section A.4) due to the reduced latitudinal extent of freezing temperatures while preserving a strong TLF-land temperature correlation. In general, the 30 warmer simulations showed the same basic behavior and relationships as the simulations seen in Fig. 4, as did a set of 30 colder simulations (500 ppm CO2) with the same subset of paleogeographies as the warmer simulations (see Supplementary Fig. 4 in Supplementary Information Section A.4).

These distinctive differences in the response of the weathering feedback to continental geometry naturally lead to a hypothesis: If Earth’s carbonate-silicate thermostat is dominated by surface weathering that is primarily sensitive to surface temperatures, then, for a given global outgassing rate, we should observe an anticorrelation between TLF and surface temperature through time, because an increase in TLF would be expected to increase the weathering rate and cause CO2 drawdown and cooling. Similarly, a reduction in TLF would slow weathering and allow CO2 to accumulate, leading to warming. Furthermore, we should not expect there to be a strong correlation between climate and continental fragmentation in this regime. Alternatively, if the silicate weathering response is dominated by Earth’s hydrological response to changes in climate, for a given outgassing rate and with all else being equal, we should expect an anticorrelation between continental fragmentation and climate and little influence from TLF. These opposing predictions provide a geological test that might shed light on the mechanisms that have stabilized Earth’s climate throughout planetary history. In the following section, we investigate Earth’s geologic record in the context of these findings.

Correlations between surface temperature and continental geometry over the Phanerozoic

To differentiate between the hypotheses described in the previous section, we analyzed the statistical relationships between the GMST reconstruction of47 and continental fragmentation and TLF calculated from the paleogeographic reconstruction of48 (see Fig. 5). We find that from 480 Ma to 295 ± 15 Ma (the range of possible transition times is shown as the rightmost error bar in Fig. 5), GMST displays a strong (r < − 0.5) and significant (p < 0.025) anti-correlation with TLF and no significant relationship to fragmentation. Then, at 295 ± 15 Ma, the apparent correlation switches, and subsequently GMST displays a strong, significant anti-correlation with continental fragmentation and no significant relationship to TLF (blue shaded region in Fig. 5) until 200 ± 10 Ma (leftmost error bar range in Fig. 5). Finally, from 200 ± 10 until the present, the relationships between variables revert back to their original behavior, with anti-correlation between GMST and TLF and no significant relationship between GMST and fragmentation. The period of anti-correlation with continental fragmentation notably overlaps with Pangea, which lasted from ≈ 325 Ma to ≈ 200 Ma[colored pink in Fig. 5,49]. The evolution of the relationships between GMST, TLF, and continental fragmentation is consistent with a dynamic scenario in which the main mechanism controlling the coupling between silicate weathering and climate has shifted across the Phanerozoic. Based on our GCM ensemble results, the anti-correlation between GMST and TLF at the beginning and end of the period under examination suggests temperature-controlled weathering, while the anti-correlation between GMST and fragmentation from ≈ 300 Ma to ≈ 200 Ma suggests that hydrology dominated the weathering response during that time span.

Discussion

As a sensitivity test of our results, we repeated the analysis of correlations between surface temperature and exposed continental geometry over the Phanerozoic, replacing the paleogeographic reconstructions from ref. 48 with those of ref. 50. The two sets of reconstructions differ primarily due to different underlying plate tectonic models48, although they are not entirely independent, since ref. 48 relied on some of the same data sources as ref. 50. With the Scotese reconstruction (see Supplementary Fig. 2), our results are qualitatively similar, with a significant negative relationship between TLF and GMST from 480 to 300 Ma (r = − 0.70, p = 2.7 × 10−6) and a significant negative relationship between fragmentation and GMST from 300 Ma to 200 Ma (r = − 0.62, p = 2.7 × 10−3), but the anti-correlation between TLF and GMST from 200 Ma to present is weaker (r = − 0.31) and marginally significant (p = 0.054).

Analyzing reconstructed CO2 may seem like a more direct route for demonstrating a relationship between climate and paleogeography, since changes to silicate weathering fluxes should manifest as changes to CO2 levels. While there do exist a number of CO2 records over the Phanerozoic based on modeling and data compilations51–53, pre-Cenozoic CO2 estimates are poorly constrained and often inconsistent54. Given the large uncertainties associated with CO2 paleo-records and the fact that Phanerozoic temperature variations are thought to mainly reflect CO2 forcing55,56, we choose to focus on GMST in this study. In any case, the reconstructed GMST of47 correlates strongly with (log-transformed) reconstructed CO2 over most of the Phanerozoic [r = 0.77, p < 0.01,47], and our GCM ensemble displayed weak or non-existent relationships between global-mean surface temperature and both TLF and continental fragmentation (Fig. 4c, d). This increases our confidence that it is a reasonable first-order assumption that CO2 and temperature were tightly linked over most of the Phanerozoic and that variations in continental geometry were unlikely to have a large direct (non-carbon-cycle-mediated) effect on surface temperatures, consistent with previous GCM ensemble studies [e.g., refs. 33,57].

It is also important to note that our GCM simulations and weathering calculations are idealized in ways that could impact the results of this study. For example, the land topography is muted compared to Earth, with a maximum elevation of 100 m40. This means there are no mountain ranges, so our results do not include the potentially important impact of orographic precipitation58 or possible interactions between uplift, erosion, and weathering59–62, which may vary systematically with continental fragmentation. We also did not attempt to explicitly represent variations in lithology, which could produce large variations in weathering parameters across the planet at any given time [e.g., refs. 22,63,64]. Additionally, the weathering calculations do not account for the potential biotic acceleration of silicate weathering by land plants [e.g., refs. 65–67], though it is unclear what impact the rise of forested ecosystems ≈ 385 Ma had on chemical weathering68,69. Furthermore, glacial weathering may be an important component of CO2 drawdown70, so since ice extent has varied during the past 485 Ma, glacial cover could provide another co-varying modulator of weathering rates that could impart spurious correlations to our data, with glacial periods during the Ordovician-Silurian, end-Carboniferous, and late Cenozoic71. Seafloor weathering is also absent from our model, despite indications that it may play a crucial role in Earth’s climate stabilization similar to that of silicate weathering on the land2,72,73. Similarly, we do not consider authigenic clay formation ("reverse weathering”) which consumes alkalinity and could impact climate equilibria and the response of the carbon cycle to perturbations [e.g., refs. 74–77].

More generally, given the interconnected and recursive nature of the Earth system, it is difficult to establish causality when analyzing relationships between geological quantities. Although we observe correlations between the variables of interest in this study (i.e., GMST, TLF, and continental fragmentation), it is possible that these relationships are being driven by other mechanisms or variables that are co-varying simultaneously. For example, we excluded variations in CO2 outgassing rate from our study even though such variations could have played a role in dictating the equilibrium weathering rate and climate state at any given time in Earth history. This is justified given the major uncertainty in reconstructions, with large inconsistencies in the magnitude and direction of outgassing changes depending on the set of processes considered and the methodology used for estimation30,31,48,71,78–82. Still, theoretical arguments and some data suggest outgassing may vary systematically across the supercontinent cycle, with periods of enhanced rifting corresponding to increases in the flux of CO2 from Earth’s interior [e.g., refs. 83–85]. If CO2 outgassing increases when continental fragmentation increases, then this may systematically bias the system away from displaying an anti-correlation between the degree of fragmentation and global surface temperature, since increased weathering could be partially or completely offset by increased outgassing. If that is the case, then the imprint of hydrologically controlled weathering on the geologic record might be reduced, biasing our analysis.

One potential geologic test of the hypothesis that the climate stabilization mechanism has shifted from temperature-dependence to hydrology-dependence and back during the Phanerozoic would be to examine global spatial and temporal trends in correlations between paleosol-based precipitation and temperature proxies with weathering proxies like chemical index of alteration86,87 or the weathering index of Parker88. Deng et al.15 used this method (except with direct temperature and precipitation measurements rather than proxies) to infer a strong temperature control on silicate weathering and a weak hydrologic control for the modern Earth, consistent with the results of our study. It is an open question whether geologic proxies would reveal the same pattern on Earth throughout the Phanerozoic across the period our analysis suggests may have displayed hydrologically-dominated weathering. Paleosols89 and GCM simulations29,90 of Earth during Pangea [which existed from ~ 325 Ma to ≈ 200 Ma,49] suggest that this would have been a period of unusually low global precipitation and runoff, which would tend to push weathering zones toward hydrologic control by reducing water fluxes and allowing solutes to build to higher concentrations12,64. This observation suggests that the aridity of the Pangea supercontinent might explain the potential shift to hydrologic control described in this study.

Beyond Earth’s history, if Earth’s thermostat oscillates between temperature control and hydrology control over time due to the supercontinent cycle (or because of some other driver), then in addition to being crucial for understanding Earth’s past, this effect could be important for accurately predicting the future lifespans of various components of Earth’s biosphere91,92. This is because (in the absence of unpredictable biotechnological intervention) Earth’s far future climate state will be determined by the interplay between the gradually brightening Sun and the behavior of atmospheric CO2 in response to this forcing, as dictated by the carbonate-silicate cycle, so the details of silicate weathering processes will be a primary control91–93. Similarly, the classical “circumstellar habitable zone” is typically defined as the band of orbits around a star where rocky exoplanets with CO2-N2-H2O atmospheres can in principle sustain liquid water oceans with the appropriate quantity of CO2 in the atmosphere94,95, and this concept is rooted in the idea that sufficiently Earth-like planets will have carbonate-silicate cycles capable of regulating their CO2 within bounds that allow for liquid water like Earth96. Some even propose to limit the width of the habitable zone based on simulations that suggest that silicate weathering behavior can cause periodic global glaciations for planets receiving somewhat less sunlight than Earth, but the precise details of the weathering parameterization used strongly impact the resulting climate behavior [e.g., refs. 97–99]. Therefore, the question of whether temperature or hydrology controls weathering on Earth directly affects predictions for the climates of potentially habitable exoplanets19,22,100,101.

In this study, we used weathering trends inferred from post-processing a large ensemble of GCM simulations to propose a pair of relationships between components of Earth’s continental geometry and silicate weathering behavior: If silicate weathering is primarily controlled by the local temperature in the weathering zone, then weathering should correlate strongly with tropical land fraction, but if weathering is controlled by hydrology, then it should correlate strongly with continental fragmentation. In turn, those relationships imply that temperature-controlled weathering should result in an anti-correlation between global-mean surface temperature and tropical land fraction, while hydrology-controlled weathering should result in an anti-correlation between global-mean surface temperature and fragmentation. We then analyzed Earth’s geologic record over the past 480 Myr and found that these relationships between continental geometry and climate seem to manifest in distinct periods: From 480 to 295 ± 15 Ma, surface temperature and tropical land fraction are anti-correlated, then from 295 ± 15 to 200 ± 10 Ma surface temperature and continental fragmentation are anti-correlated, and finally from 200 ± 10 Ma to the present surface temperature and tropical land fraction appear anti-correlated once again. The apparent shift in the mechanism stabilizing Earth’s climate overlaps with the existence of Pangea, suggesting a potential relationship between Earth’s dominant weathering mode and the mode of Earth’s supercontinent cycle, perhaps mediated by large changes in continental aridity.

Methods

Climate simulations

We use an ensemble of Community Earth System Model (CESM) global climate model (GCM) simulations originally presented by ref. 40 and available for download at ref. 102 to analyze the sensitivity of silicate weathering rates to tropical land fraction (TLF) and continental fragmentation, represented by the ratio of non-submerged continental perimeter to continental area, which we denote the “continental fragmentation index” following103. The GCM simulations were all carried out at T31 resolution ( ≈ 4∘ by 4∘), and all have an identical top-of-atmosphere subsolar insolation of 1285 W m−2, global land fraction of 30%, atmospheric CO2 concentration of 1000 ppm (parts per million by volume), obliquity of 23.5∘, eccentricity of 0, and lack of vegetation. They differ only in their continental geometries, which are stochastically generated spherical harmonics truncated at degree 12. Baum et al. 40 calculated weathering rates with a fixed set of sensitivity parameters (α = 1 and k = 0.09 K−1), which limits the applicability of their results due to the large uncertainties in temperature- and hydrology-dependence for silicate weathering. We extend their work by varying α across the range α ∈ [0, 1] with α = 0 corresponding to no runoff-dependence for silicate weathering (except if q = 0, we assume weathering shuts off, even if α = 0) and α = 1 corresponding to a linear dependence. We vary k across the range k ∈ [10−2, 10−1] K−1, corresponding to a range of e-folding temperatures Te ∈ [100, 10] K, which encompasses the wide range of weathering temperature sensitivity values that have recently been suggested in the literature4,5,15. This wide range of weathering parameters allows us to probe how variations in strength for different forms of coupling between climate and weathering interact with TLF and fragmentation index, two large-scale aspects of continental geometry that may impact global weathering rates. TLF is defined as the land fraction between 15∘N/S for consistency with40, with results insensitive to variations in latitude range. The fragmentation index is defined as the continental perimeter divided by the continental area. To quantify these relationships, we calculate the coefficient of determination (R2) between weathering rates and both TLF and fragmentation across the GCM simulations in our ensemble as functions of α and k to measure the proportion of variance in weathering rate that is explained by variations in TLF and fragmentation index.

The weathering parameterization we use in this study is based on the common formulation first suggested in ref. 1, sometimes referred to as the WHAK model[e.g., refs. 19,40,111]. An alternative or extension to the WHAK model presented by ref. 12 (MAC) allows for mechanistic transitions between the weathering regimes explored here, with a temperature-controlled regime dominated by silicate dissolution kinetics and a hydrologically regulated regime that occurs when secondary mineral precipitation in the weathering zone prevents solutes from accumulating to higher concentrations. Calculations with the MAC model display very similar patterns to results calculated with WHAK: parameter values with temperature-dependent weathering result in a strong correlation between global weathering and TLF, while hydrology-dependent weathering results in a strong correlation with continental fragmentation (see Supplementary Information Section A.3, Supplementary Fig. 3).

Analysis of reconstructed geologic data

Positive correlations between silicate weathering and TLF or fragmentation index, respectively, would imply, all else being equal, anti-correlations between global temperature and TLF or fragmentation index. This is because with a given CO2 outgassing rate, an increase in global weathering should cause a drawdown in CO2 and a corresponding reduction in temperature until the weathering rate is reduced back to equilibrium with outgassing. Further, the GCM/weathering results imply large differences between weathering regimes controlled by temperature and hydrology in terms of their responses to changes in TLF or changes in continental fragmentation. Therefore, by comparing proxy data for the evolution of global mean surface temperature (GMST) with reconstructed global continental geometry during the Phanerozoic and combining this information with the results from our GCM/weathering experiments, we can use the geologic record to gain insight into the relative strength of temperature vs. hydrology in stabilizing Earth’s climate through time.

To allow for the possibility that the Earth system may have shifted between hydrologically-controlled and temperature-controlled weathering at different times, we calculate the correlation coefficients across all time spans from 485 million years ago (Ma) to the present between a recent data-assimilation-based reconstruction of the Phanerozoic GMST record47 and the TLF and fragmentation index from a recent tectonic reconstruction of exposed land across the Phanerozoic48 using GPlates112. We converted the ShapeFiles from GPlates into NetCDF4 files encoding the exposed land from the48 reconstructions at every 10 Myr interval from 480 Ma to present on a latitude-longitude grid resolution of 1∘ by 1∘. Fragmentation index (total perimeter of exposed land divided by total area) only accounts for the outer boundary of land masses to avoid counting the boundaries of interior lakes and seas.

These calculations help differentiate between the competing hypotheses about the silicate weathering feedback described above by quantifying the statistical relationships between reconstructions of Earth’s paleogeography and surface temperature.
