A rare presentation of a complex Galeazzi fracture-dislocation and a review of literature on complications: dislocation and interposition of the deep flexor of the fifth finger
Authors: Stefania Briano, Federico Vitali, Giacomo Demontis, Emanuela Dapelo, Alessandra Galuppi, Giulia Pachera, Vittoria Mazzola, Matteo Formica
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFractures of the distal third of the radius often involve instability of the distal radioulnar and secondary stabilizers. In some cases, reduction of the distal radioulnar is prevented by the interposition of other dislocated structures. Dislocation and interposition of the deep flexor of the fifth finger is rarely observed in this type of fracture.The authors present a review of the literature and the case of a 36-year-old female athlete who presented to the emergency room after a trauma to the wrist, during a barbell exercise. A first radiographic examination showed a fracture of the distal third of the radius and clinically she presented with an apparent deficit in extension of the fourth and fifth fingers. An initial ultrasound examination, performed in the emergency room, did not show any lesions affecting the ulnar nerve. Following an initial reduction and osteosynthesis of the fracture, in which no instability of the distal radioulnar joint or lesions affecting the ulnar nerve were observed, the patient continued to show an apparent deficit in extension of the IV and V fingers. The patient underwent an orthopaedic and radiological re-evaluation, where a dorsal dislocation of the deep flexor of the V finger was highlighted. This article describes the management and treatment of a rare case of dorsal dislocation of the deep flexor of the V finger following a fracture of the distal third of the radius.Keywords: Wrist, Galeazzi fracture, Tendon lesion, Radio-ulnar joint, Tendon dislocation

Introduction

Fractures of the distal third of the radius can produce instability and dislocation to the distal radioulnar joint and to the secondary stabilizers. These kinds of fractures are commonly referred as Galeazzi fracture-dislocations. Galeazzi fracture-dislocations are rare, accounting approximately 6.8 % of all forearm fractures [1,2]. The typical mechanism of injury is a fall on an outstretched hand with the forearm in pronation. Various patterns have been described, including those by Artiaco et al. [3]. The diagnosis is based on detailed examination and radiographic imaging. Clinical signs include pain, swelling and deformity at the fracture site and potential instability during the forearm rotation. Radiographic evaluation involves imaging of the forearm, wrist and elbow to confirm radial fracture and distal radio-ulnar joint dislocation [4]. Radiographic sign of the distal-radio-ulnar joint include widening of the joint space, shortening of the radius and dislocation of the ulna. In the treatment of a Galeazzi-fracture it is important to start with an open reduction and internal fixation of the radius and then obtain an anatomical reduction of the distal radioulnar joint if necessary, but in some cases the correct reduction of the distal radioulnar joint is prevented by the interposition of other dislocated structures [1,2,5,6] In literature is described the interposition of many structures such as the extensor structures, the extensor carpi ulnaris tendon, the extensor digitiminimi tendon, the extensor digitorum communis tendon of the ring and the little fingers, the extensor retinaculum and the distal radio-ulnar joint capsule [5,6]. However, the dislocation and interposition of a flexor structure such as the deep flexor of the fifth finger is rarely observed in this type of fracture and is not described in literature.

This paper presents a case report of a patient in whom, due to a Galeazzi fracture–dislocation, there has been a dorsal dislocation of the deep flexor of the fifth finger and its interposition within the distal radio ulnar joint. We describe how we discovered, managed and treated this rare case fracture–dislocation.

Case report

A 36-year-old active woman, engaged in CrossFit training, sustained a forearm injury involving hyperextension and hyperpronation and an axial load of her dominant right hand while performing incorrect movements with a barbell.

The same day, she presented to our emergency department. During the initial clinical evaluation, she exhibited pain, swelling, deformity, instability, functional impairment and an apparent deficit in extension of the fourth and fifth fingers with paresthesia on the fourth and fifth finger.

We requested imaging studies as described in literature for wrist, forearm and elbow [4]. Radiological evaluation revealed a fracture of the distal third of the radius with a widening of the joint space of the distal radio-ulnar joint, leading to the suspicion of a Galeazzi-fracture with distal radio ulnar joint dislocation [4,5]. In our clinical practice, with this kind of fractures we usually ask for a CT scan to better study, evaluate and classify the injury. The CT scan was subsequently performed, and we classified the fracture using the AO classification as AO 2R2A2 [7].

Clinically, the patient continued to show a flexed posture of the fourth and fifth fingers, with an ulnar claw hand attitude. We ask for ultrasound imaging, suspecting an ulnar nerve injury at the wrist following the Galeazzi fracture-dislocation. The ultrasound imaging performed in the emergency department, did not reveal any ulnar nerve lesions.

At this point, the patient was discharged from the emergency department with a brachial-metacarpal cast awaiting for the surgery.

Two days later, the patient was taken to the operating room for open reduction and internal fixation of the distal radius fracture. Using a volar approach to the radius as described by Henry et al., we reduced and fixed the fracture with one lag screw and a 3.5 mm and six-hole locking compression plate (LCP). Following reduction and fixation, the intra-operative fluoroscopy revealed good alignment of the distal radioulnar joint (DRUJ). We assessed DRUJ stability using the ballottement test that revealed no signs of instability (Fig. 1).

Postoperatively, the patient continued to exhibit a flexion posture of the fourth and fifth fingers with paresthesia at the fourth and fifth finger. Suspecting ulnar nerve stupor, we discharged the patient with a brachial-metacarpal brace and scheduled a follow-up in 15 days for suture removal.

At the 15-day follow-up, the sutures were removed, but the flexion posture persisted. At this point, we asked for an electroneurography that revealed no lesions or stupor of the ulnar nerve.

From the 16-day after the surgery the patient began a rehabilitation program with a therapist to regain full extension of the fourth and fifth fingers. However, one month after surgery, she continued to display the flexed posture despite utilizing a splint for the affected fingers.

We asked for a second ultrasound evaluation that ruled out any significant ulnar nerve injury but identified a strain of the deep branch of the ulnar nerve. Given the diagnostic uncertainty in contrast with the clinical evaluation, we requested a second US study, which revealed an entrapment of the deep flexor tendon of the fifth finger within the DRUJ. To confirm this, we asked for an MRI, which corroborated the US findings.

The imaging from the US (confirmed by the MRI images) showed that the tendon of the deep flexor tendon of the fifth finger, from distal to proximal, had deviated medially, dorsally, encircling the neck and head of the ulna before returning to its normal volar position. Due to the imaging findings, we understood that in that position and with that trajectory the tendon, could probably act as a stabilizer of the DRUJ, explaining the negative ballottement test in the intraoperative test performed during the surgery (Fig. 2).

Considering this diagnosis, the patient was scheduled for a second surgery to release the entrapped deep flexor tendon of the fifth finger.

During the second surgery, a volar approach was used to open the carpal tunnel. The course of the tendon was traced from distal to proximal, revealing that it had indeed dislocated medially and dorsally. At the level of the DRUJ, the tendon was partially damaged, appearing elongated and thinner. We released the tendon from the DRUJ without opening the joint itself given the previously confirmed DRUJ stability. At this point, the tendon was repositioned and sutured in its original anatomical volar position (Fig. 3).

The patient was discharged with a wrist cast and a splint for the affected fourth and fifth fingers and began rehabilitation with a therapist. One week later, the patient was able to initiate flexion-extension movements of the fourth and fifth fingers (Fig. 4a).

At a two-month follow-up, the patient showed significant improvement of the fourth and fifth finger flexion-extension (Fig. 4b). Three months postoperatively, she demonstrated a full range of motion in the fourth and fifth fingers without paresthesia. An ultrasound revealed a normal structure of the deep flexor tendons of the fourth and fifth fingers (Fig. 4c). Six months after the second surgery, the patient returned to CrossFit training (Fig. 4d-e). After more than one year from the second surgery the patient has returned to the pre-injury conditions and she attended CrossFit competitions.

Discussion

Fractures of the distal third of the radius are often associated with distal radio-ulnar joint injuries and a damage to the secondary stabilizer, such as the extensor carpi ulnaris, the pronator quadratus muscle, the central band of the interosseus membrane, the joint capsule and the ulnocarpal complex. An injury to one of these structures is commonly referred to Galeazzi fracture-dislocations. Galeazzi fracture-dislocations are rare, accounting for approximately 6.8 % of all forearm fractures [1,2]. Various patterns have been described, including those by Artiaco et al. [3].

Typically, these fractures occur due to high-energy trauma, hyperpronation of the forearm combined with axial loading or a fall on outstretched hand with the forearm in pronation [1,2,5]. In the emergency department, any fracture of the distal third of the radius should prompt suspicion of a Galeazzi fracture-dislocation, requiring careful clinical evaluation and radiographs in AP and LL projections, and a careful evaluation of distal radio ulnar joint [5,6].

The distal radio ulnar joint is crucial for the forearm functionality, allowing pronation and supination movements, which enable the hand to rotate the palm up or palm-down. It plays a key role in transmitting forces across the forearm during pronation and supination with a load distribution varying throughout this motion [8].

During the surgery, after the radius reduction and fixation, in this kind of fractures is mandatory to assess the distal radio ulnar joint. In literature to assess the distal radio ulnar joint stability, the ballottement test is commonly reported and used, but several other clinical tests are also recommended such as the piano key test, the ulnar fovea sign, the ulno-meniscus-triquetral dorsal glide test. In addition, if arthroscopy is available, it remains the gold standard for diagnosing and managing intra-articular pathologies of the distal radio-ulnar joint [9,10].

We usually perform the ballottement test to assess the stability of the distal radio-ulnar joint. In case of instability, it is important to reduce it and in case of impossibility in achieving the reduction, it is important to identify the causes. A difficulty in reduction can occur due to the interposition of soft tissues, entrapment of the ulnar head, ligamentous injuries, fracture fragment displacement and muscle spasm of the forearm that can impede an anatomical alignment.

In case of soft tissues interposition, any interposed structure that can make the reduction critical must be identified [4,[10], [11], [12]].

A literature review reveals only a few documented cases of irreducible Galeazzi fracture-dislocations in adults [4,[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]]. According to Yohe et al. and Masayoshi et al., distal radio ulnar joint instability and ulnar styloid fractures are commonly associated with irreducible Galeazzi injuries [1].

Often, in Galeazzi injuries, a correct reduction and synthesis of the radius restores stability to the distal radio-ulnar joint, but sometimes a reduction of the distal radio ulnar joint can be impeded by interposed dislocated structures. The complex anatomy of this area includes numerous structures that can become entrapped [1,3].

Yohe et al. reported that extensor structures, particularly the extensor carpi ulnaris (ECU), are frequently implicated in these cases [1,13], Gunes et Al. also describes the involvement of other extensor structures such as the extensor digiti minimi tendon, the extensor digitorum communis tendon of the ring and the little finger, the extensor retinculum and the distal radio-ulnar joint capsule, the dislocation of the flexor structures. However, is rarely reported in the literature.

In our case, we observed a dislocation of a flexor tendon within the distal radioulnar joint, an extremely rare occurrence of dislocation and interposition of a volar structure with the distal radio ulnar joint.

At the first surgery, after the radius fixation, we didn't find any distal radio-ulnar joint instability. After assessing the stability of the distal radioulnar joint with the ballotment test. We initially attributed the distal radio ulnar joint stability to a correct reduction and fixation of the radius, so we didn't think to any interposed fracture.

However, the persistent flexed posture of the fourth and fifth fingers, combined with the absence of visible ulnar nerve lesion on ultrasound, prompted us to continue searching for the cause of this flexed position. It was after performing an US and MRI that we discovered the interposition of the deep flexor tendon of the fifth finger within the distal radio ulnar joint.

The subsequent diagnosis of deep flexor tendon of the fifth finger dislocation highlighted its role as a “stabilizer” of the DRUJ, explaining the initial negative ballottement test at the time of the first surgery.

During the second surgery, two months after the injury, we choose to release the deep flexor tendon of the fifth finger from the distal radio-ulnar joint by cutting and repositioning it in the correct volar position, avoiding opening the distal radio-ulnar joint, which could have risked causing instability.

Conclusion

This case report highlights the complexity of managing Galeazzi fracture-dislocations. In distal third radius fractures, even in case of apparent stability of the distal radio-ulnar joint, but with discordant clinical sign, it is important to consider the possibility of interposed or dislocated structures. Physical examinations and high-quality diagnostic tools are essential in addressing diagnostic uncertainties. This case teaches us that perseverance in identifying the root cause of persistent symptoms is critical to achieving optimal outcomes.
