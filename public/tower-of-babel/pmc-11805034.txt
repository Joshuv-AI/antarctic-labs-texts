Exfoliated Oral Mucosal Cell Micronucleus Frequency Evaluation in Healthy Individuals, Tobacco Users, and Patients with Potentially Malignant Disorders: An Analytical Comparative Study
Authors: V Kranthi, S Shylaja, Pallavi Kandi, Mohammed Abidullah, Neeharika Soorneedi, Tummakomma P. Latha
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective:Assessing the prevalence of exfoliated oral mucosal cell micronuclei in tobacco smokers, healthy persons, and patients with potentially malignant diseases (PMDs). A critical comparative investigation.Materials and Procedures:The study included 80 participants divided into four groups: healthy persons, tobacco users without any clinically noticeable oral lesions, patients with leukoplakia, and patients with oral submucous fibrosis (OSMF). Each group included 20 participants. Only cases with a verified histological diagnosis of PMDs were considered for evaluating micronuclei (MNi). This included non-smokers with mucosa that seems normal, smokers with mucosa that appears normal, and individuals with leukoplakia and OSMF who were histologically identified.Results:Compared to controls, smokers and patients with PMDs had much higher MN frequencies, with PMD patients having the highest MN frequency. There was a noticeable rise in MN frequency in the H and E and PAP stains from controls to tobacco users and then to PMD patients, showing a stepwise development. While the difference between tobacco users and PMD patients was only significant with the H and E stain, the inter-group comparisons show significant differences in MN frequency between controls and both tobacco users and PMD patients across both staining techniques. Additionally, the comparison of the H and E and PAP stains reveals that whereas MN frequencies in smokers and controls are typically similar, PMD patients have considerably higher MN frequencies in the H and E stains.Conclusion:In summary, the research indicates that tobacco users have a much higher MN frequency than controls, with an even more pronounced in individuals who have PMDs.

INTRODUCTION

A round or oval cytoplasmic chromatin mass that is visible under a microscope and located next to the primary nucleus is known as a micronucleus (MN). It begins with abnormal mitoses that produce eccentric chromosomes, chromatid fragments, or whole chromosomes that are unable to fuse into the daughter nuclei during the mitotic process.[1] The human body’s exposure to different genotoxic agents causes an increase in chromosomal aberrations (such as chromosomal deletions or chromatid anomalies), which can then cause MNi—DNA-containing bodies in the cytoplasm that are structurally unconnected to the main nucleus—to form. The idea is supported by the observation of karyotypic abnormalities and higher deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) content in a range of potentially malignant diseases (PMDs). Chromatid breaking and translocation must occur over an extended period of time before the formation of certain karyotypic abnormalities.[2] The existence and prevalence of MN serve as an “internal dosimeter” to assess the level of risk associated with genotoxicity and determine exposure to different genotoxic chemicals. It has been shown that MN is a significant indicator of genomic damage.[2] Using exfoliative cytology, the MN test is one of the most effective and affordable methods available for measuring the genotoxic effects of carcinogens. It is considered a quantitative indication of genotoxicity.[3] It is described as the microscopic analysis of oral mucosa cells that have been desquamated. It is a non-aggressive method that the patient finds acceptable, and its use in the early detection of cancer has a long history.[4] PMDs in the oral cavity, such as leukoplakia, submucous fibrosis, and erythroplakia, are a reflection of the genotoxic effects of environmental pollutants or any chemical carcinogen that causes genomic instability at an early stage of cancer. The MN test may be used to analyze DNA damage to identify the genotoxic effects and hazards associated with tobacco use and PMD patients.[4567]

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Eighty participants were engaged in the research at the SVS Institute of Dental Sciences in Mahabubnagar. The participants were separated into four groups, each consisting of 20 persons: healthy individuals, tobacco users without oral lesions, patients with oral submucous fibrosis (OSMF), and patients with leukoplakia. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and cases with PMDs verified by histopathology were the only ones evaluated for MNi. Exclusion criteria included patients with systemic illnesses that might impact MN frequency, those diagnosed with squamous cell carcinoma diagnoses, and those who had received PMD treatment in the past or present.

In terms of methodology, subjects were given an oral rinse before having mucosal scrapings taken using a wooden spatula, and the cells spread out onto slides for cytological examination. The smears were stained using precise processes for fixing, staining, and mounting the samples using the Papanicolaou (PAP) and haematoxylin and eosin (H and E) methods. Under a light microscope, the cytological examination was carried out. To determine the frequency, MNi in a thousand cells were counted and screened using the zig-zag technique.

The cytological assessment data were entered into Microsoft Excel, and SPSS version 18 was used for statistical analysis.

DISCUSSION

MNi are tiny nuclei that appear as indicators of chromosomal damage during cell division when chromosome fragments are unable to merge into daughter nuclei. The frequency of MN, which rises with carcinogenic exposure and might signal the development of PMDs into carcinoma, is measured by MN tests on exfoliated oral epithelial cells.[128] The staining technique affects the assay’s sensitivity; DNA-specific stains, such as Feulgen, provide more precise MN detection than non-specific stains, such as H and E and PAP.[34]

The test has been shown to have diagnostic promise in recent investigations. Grover et al.[3] discovered that PMDs had a greater MN frequency than controls and that substantial staining variations affected the precision of identification. Anila et al.[4] found similar results, noting higher MN frequency in individuals with OSMF. There appears to be a relationship between tobacco use and genotoxicity levels based on the progressive rise in MN frequency from controls to tobacco users and OSMF patients.[56]

The need for specificity in chromosomal damage evaluations is shown by a thorough analysis of MN frequency using several staining techniques across different groups (controls, tobacco users, and PMD patients). Due to the potential for inflammatory components to interfere with H and E staining, PAP staining is recommended as a more dependable option for reducing the possibility of misinterpreting MN because of its increased cellular transparency.[7] Further research might corroborate these results and improve MN test procedures.

CONCLUSION

According to the research, the frequency of MNi is much higher in tobacco users than in controls, and it is even higher in patients who have PMDs.
