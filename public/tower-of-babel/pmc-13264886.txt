Continuation of first-line therapy with radiotherapy for oligoprogression versus early switch to second-line therapy in oligoprogressive hepatocellular carcinoma (CROSS): study protocol of a multicentre, randomised, controlled, open-label, phase III trial
Authors: Haohua Wang, Fang Shi, Xiang Zhang, Kunli Zhu, Shumei Jiang, Rui Feng, Xue Dou, Lei Xu, Jinbo Yue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTreatment options remain limited for patients with advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) who experience oligoprogression during first-line systemic therapy (FLST), especially given the modest efficacy and restricted availability of second-line systemic therapy (SLST). This trial aims to evaluate whether continuing FLST combined with radiotherapy (RT) to oligoprogressive lesions can improve progression-free survival (PFS) compared with an early switch to SLST in patients with oligoprogressive HCC while maintaining an acceptable safety profile.Methods and analysisThe continuation of first-line therapy with radiotherapy for oligoprogression versus early switch to second-line therapy in oligoprogressive hepatocellular carcinoma trial is a prospective, multicentre, randomised phase III study that will enrol 132 patients with advanced HCC who experience their first oligoprogression during FLST. Oligoprogression is defined as one to five progressive lesions involving no more than one to three organs. Participants will be randomised (1:1) to either continuation of FLST combined with RT to all oligoprogressive lesions or discontinuation of FLST followed by initiation of SLST. RT will be delivered with a biologically effective dose (linear–quadratic model, α/β=10) of at least 60 Gy whenever feasible. The primary endpoint is PFS. Secondary endpoints include overall survival, objective response rate, disease control rate, duration of response and quality of life. Predefined exploratory analyses include circulating tumour DNA profiling, optional paired tumour biopsies, functional imaging with fibroblast activation protein inhibitor positron emission tomography-CT and longitudinal immune profiling.Ethics and disseminationThis study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Cancer Hospital of Shandong First Medical University (number: SDZLEC2025-025-02) and has been registered in ClinicalTrials. gov (NCT06841172). Background

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is often diagnosed at an advanced stage due to its aggressive nature and lack of early symptoms, making most patients ineligible for curative treatment.1 Approximately 50–60% of patients with HCC are treated with systemic therapies.2 In recent years, novel first-line systemic therapy (FLST) of advanced HCC, especially immunotherapy based combination regimens, have enhanced patient survival outcomes.3 However, the objective response rate (ORR) of these novel FLST remains limited, ranging from approximately 20% to 36%,3 and most patients inevitably develop resistance and experience disease progression during treatment.

Even more concerning is the lack of high-quality evidence to support recommendations for these novel combination regimens. For current second-line systemic therapy (SLST) options, phase III clinical trials—including RESORCE (a phase III trial of regorafenib after sorafenib in patients with advanced hepatocellular carcinoma),4 CELESTIAL (a phase III trial of cabozantinib in previously treated advanced hepatocellular carcinoma)5 and REACH (a phase III trial of ramucirumab after sorafenib in patients with advanced hepatocellular carcinoma)6—have demonstrated the clinical benefit of regorafenib, cabozantinib and ramucirumab, respectively. However, these studies were conducted exclusively in patients with advanced HCC after failure of sorafenib,7 with reported ORRs not exceeding 11%.3

For patients undergoing FLST who develop oligoprogression8 9—defined as the presence of limited progressive lesions while the majority of the disease remains controlled on systemic treatment—the standard approach is typically to switch to SLST. However, the efficacy of SLST remains unsatisfactory,3 10 and with limited options11 available for second-line treatment, there is an urgent need for optimised treatment strategies.

We hypothesise that incorporating local radiotherapy (RT) to target oligoprogressive lesions could help overcome tumour resistance at these specific sites, prevent the emergence of more widespread resistant disease and allow for the continuation of FLST-provided it remains effective for non-progressing lesions. This approach may delay the initiation of SLST and potentially improve both progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS).

This treatment concept has been explored in other malignancies and has demonstrated benefit in several cancers, including prostate cancer, non-small cell lung cancer and renal cell carcinoma.12 13 However, in the field of HCC, even the broader concept of oligometastasis—which includes oligoprogression—is not widely accepted, and the optimal integration of RT with systemic therapy for oligoprogressive or oligometastatic disease remains an area of active investigation. Dr Seong et al14 evaluated the efficacy and safety of stereotactic ablative RT for oligometastatic HCC; however, their study did not specifically focus on the subset of patients with oligoprogression. Recently, our team published a multicentre retrospective study in The Red Journal15 that was the first to report the efficacy of maintaining FLST in combination with RT for oligoprogressive lesions in HCC. The results demonstrated a significant improvement in PFS compared with a direct switch to SLST (median PFS: 8.6 vs 3.1 months), thereby delaying the initiation of SLST.

Subsequently, we conducted a prospective, multicentre, single-arm phase II trial (NCT06261047), which further demonstrated favourable disease control, acceptable safety and preserved quality of life (QoL) with progression-directed RT combined with continued FLST in patients with oligoprogressive HCC.16 Based on this, we have now initiated a randomised, multicentre phase III clinical trial to evaluate whether continuing FLST with the addition of RT to oligoprogressive lesions provides superior efficacy and safety compared with switching directly to SLST in patients with HCC who develop oligoprogression during FLST.

Methods/design

Trial design

The continuation of first-line therapy with radiotherapy for oligoprogression versus early switch to second-line therapy in oligoprogressive hepatocellular carcinoma trial is a prospective, multicentre, randomised, controlled, open-label, phase III study. A total of 132 patients will be enrolled and randomised (1:1) using a stratified randomisation scheme into the experimental group (continuation of FLST with RT for oligoprogression, n=66) and the standard group (early switch to SLST alone, n=66). Randomisation will be stratified according to oligoprogression subtype (repeat vs induced) to ensure balance of this key prognostic factor between treatment arms. Additional clinically relevant baseline variables, including FLST regimen, liver function status and disease distribution, will be prospectively collected and evaluated in predefined subgroup and multivariable analyses to further assess their potential impact on clinical outcomes.

Exploratory endpoints

This study incorporates predefined exploratory and translational research components to characterise molecular, immunological and functional imaging features, aiming to elucidate mechanisms of treatment response, resistance and disease progression, and to identify potential prognostic and predictive biomarkers. CtDNA collection and analysis

Peripheral blood samples will be collected for circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) analysis at predefined time points, including baseline (at the time of oligoprogression before RT), the first post-RT follow-up and subsequent disease progression. ctDNA analysis will be performed to assess dynamic molecular changes over time, including alterations in mutation profiles, variant allele frequency and overall ctDNA burden. Longitudinal changes in ctDNA levels and genomic features, including potential emergence of resistance-associated alterations, will be explored in relation to local tumour control, PFS and OS.

Tumour tissue biopsies and molecular profiling

A subset of patients, in whom tumour biopsy is clinically feasible and deemed safe, will undergo optional tumour tissue sampling. Biopsies may be obtained from primary tumours and oligoprogressive lesions at baseline and, when feasible, again at subsequent disease progression. Tumour tissue will be used for exploratory molecular analyses, including genomic and microenvironmental profiling, to characterise spatial and temporal tumour heterogeneity, clonal evolution, immune microenvironment alterations and potential mechanisms of treatment resistance. Participation in tissue biopsy components is optional and not mandatory for study enrolment.

Functional imaging with FAPI PET-CT

A subset of patients will undergo serial fibroblast activation protein inhibitor (FAPI) positron emission tomography (PET)-CT imaging as part of exploratory functional imaging assessments. FAPI PET-CT scans will be performed at three predefined time points: before study enrolment, at the first follow-up after completion of RT and at subsequent disease progression. Exploratory analyses will evaluate the relationship between quantitative FAPI PET-CT parameters, including standardised uptake values and metabolic tumour burden, and clinical outcomes such as treatment response, PFS and OS.

Peripheral blood immune profiling

Peripheral blood samples will be collected for immune profiling at multiple time points, including baseline, during RT, at the first post-RT follow-up and at subsequent disease progression. Exploratory immune profiling will focus on characterising longitudinal changes in circulating immune cell populations, lymphocyte subsets and selected systemic immune-related biomarkers over the course of treatment. RT-induced immune alterations will be explored in relation to treatment response, disease control and progression patterns.

Definition of oligoprogression following systemic therapy

Oligoprogression is defined as the presence of one to five progressive lesions involving no more than one to three organs. These lesions may correspond to either newly developed metastatic sites or progression of pre-existing lesions occurring after systemic therapy. Oligoprogression may involve intrahepatic disease.

For lymph node involvement, each lymphatic drainage basin is considered a single lesion. In addition, oligoprogressive disease must fulfil one of the two categories defined by the European Society for Radiotherapy and Oncology and the EORTC consensus on oligometastatic disease, namely repeat oligoprogression or induced oligoprogression.9 18

Study intervention/treatment and procedures

Experimental group

In the experimental group, patients will continue the same FLST regimen that was being administered before the diagnosis of oligoprogression. The FLST may include, but is not limited to, combinations such as atezolizumab plus bevacizumab, tremelimumab plus durvalumab or monotherapies such as lenvatinib, sorafenib, tislelizumab, durvalumab or pembrolizumab, at the discretion of the treating physician.

During continuation of FLST, all identified oligoprogressive lesions will be managed with RT. The prescribed radiation dose should achieve a biologically effective dose (BED), calculated using the linear–quadratic model with an α/β ratio of 10, of no less than 60 Gy whenever feasible. This dose threshold is supported by prior clinical evidence indicating that higher BED delivery is associated with improved local tumour control and favourable survival outcomes.19 20

For oligoprogression involving intrahepatic lesions, eligibility for RT must be confirmed through multidisciplinary team (MDT) evaluation. Additional eligibility criteria include adequate residual liver reserve, defined as a non-tumorous liver volume of at least 700 mL. For patients with Child-Pugh class B liver function, dosimetric planning must ensure that the dose delivered to 700 cc of normal liver tissue does not exceed 23 Gy. Acceptable RT techniques include three-dimensional conformal RT, intensity-modulated RT and image-guided RT. The total radiation dose and fractionation schedule will be individualised based on lesion size, anatomical location and proximity to surrounding critical structures. For stereotactic body RT (SBRT), a dose regimen of 7–10 Gy per fraction administered over five fractions is recommended. When non-SBRT techniques are employed, treatment plans should be designed to achieve a BED of at least 60 Gy. For patients presenting with multiple oligoprogressive lesions, concurrent irradiation of all eligible lesions within the same treatment period is encouraged, with the overall duration of RT not exceeding 3 months. In cases where oligoprogressive disease recurs at least 3 months after completion of prior RT, remains oligoprogressive in nature and occurs outside the previously irradiated field, repeat RT may be administered, provided that the patient remains an appropriate candidate for local treatment.

Given the individualised nature of RT planning in this context, it is not feasible to mandate uniform dose constraints for organs at risk (OAR) across all treatment sites. Nevertheless, to promote treatment safety and consistency, dose constraints should be determined with reference to established consensus recommendations, such as those from Quantitative Analyses of Normal Tissue Effects in the Clinic,21 according to lesion location and RT modality. In situations where standard dose prescriptions would result in excessive exposure to OARs, a reduced radiation dose may be applied to ensure compliance with safety constraints.

Control group

Patients randomised to the control arm will discontinue FLST at the time of disease progression and initiate SLST. The selection of second-line therapy will be determined through MDT discussion led by the treating physician, with comprehensive consideration of individual patient characteristics, disease status, prior treatment exposure, drug indications and tolerability.

For patients randomised to the control arm, selection of SLST will be guided by contemporary clinical guidelines, prior systemic treatment exposure, liver function status and MDT evaluation. To reduce treatment heterogeneity across participating centres, all SLST regimens must be approved systemic therapies recommended by current national or international guidelines for advanced HCC. Details of SLST regimens, treatment duration, dose modifications and subsequent systemic therapies will be prospectively recorded for predefined subgroup and exploratory analyses.

Immobilisation, simulation and image acquisition

All patients will undergo RT simulation using CT, with MRI incorporated when clinically indicated for target delineation. A patient-specific immobilisation device will be fabricated to ensure reproducible positioning throughout treatment. For lesions subject to significant respiratory or organ motion, such as pulmonary or adrenal metastases, four-dimensional CT will be employed for motion assessment and management. Planning CT images will be acquired with a slice thickness of no more than 3 mm to support accurate target and OARs delineation. The detailed simulation and immobilisation procedures will be implemented in accordance with institutional standards and are consistent with those applied in our previously published single-arm study.18

Target volume definition

For all treated lesions, the gross tumour volume (GTV) will be defined as the radiographically visible oligoprogressive disease identified on CT and/or MRI, with PET incorporated when available. The clinical target volume (CTV) will encompass the GTV with an additional margin to account for potential microscopic tumour extension, determined at the discretion of the treating radiation oncologist. To compensate for setup uncertainties and organ motion, a uniform expansion of the CTV will be applied to generate the planning target volume, with margins typically ranging from 2 to 5 mm depending on the anatomical site and treatment technique.

To ensure treatment consistency and protocol compliance across participating centres, RT planning and target delineation will be performed according to predefined principles derived from the previously established single-arm protocol,18 while allowing appropriate individualisation based on anatomical and clinical considerations. To improve intercentre consistency, participating radiation oncologists will follow protocol-defined RT planning principles, including target delineation guidance, dose reporting requirements and OAR constraints. RT plans will undergo institutional quality assurance review before treatment delivery.

Follow-up and response assessment

Tumour response will be evaluated locally at each participating centre in accordance with the RECIST, V.1.1. Imaging assessments will be performed according to predefined protocol-specified follow-up intervals across all participating centres to ensure consistency in PFS evaluation. Imaging assessments will be performed at baseline and at 1, 3, 6, 9, 12, 15 and 18 months after initiation of protocol-assigned treatment.

At each follow-up visit, a comprehensive evaluation will be performed, including complete blood count, assessment of hepatic and renal function, coagulation profile and measurement of serum tumour markers. Radiological assessments will consist of contrast-enhanced CT or MRI of the upper abdomen, in combination with chest CT, to assess treatment response. In cases where extrahepatic metastasis is clinically suspected, additional imaging studies, such as PET/CT, may be conducted as clinically indicated to confirm disease status.

Toxicity and quality of life assessment

All AEs occurring during the study will be systematically recorded and monitored by the investigators. The severity of AEs will be graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, V.5.0. Acute toxicities will be assessed on a weekly basis during RT and at 3 months following completion of treatment. Late toxicities occurring beyond 1 month after RT will be evaluated at 3 months, 6 months, 1 year and 3 years post-treatment using the Late Effects of Normal Tissues- Subjective, Objective, Management and Analytic scoring system.

QoL will be assessed at baseline within 1 month after patient enrolment and subsequently at 3, 6 and 9 months. QoL evaluation will be performed using the EORTC QLQ-C30 and QLQ-HCC18 questionnaires,22 with scores calculated according to established scoring manuals to longitudinally monitor patient-reported outcomes.

Statistical analysis

This study is designed as a prospective, randomised, phase III controlled trial and PFS is defined as the primary endpoint of the study. Based on data from prior multicentre retrospective study15 and taking into account potential selection bias inherent to retrospective analyses, a conservative treatment effect was assumed. Although our team’s earlier phase II trial provided prospective evidence supporting the feasibility and activity of this strategy, it was a single-arm study without a concurrent control group and was not designed for direct comparative efficacy estimation. Therefore, the sample size calculation for the current randomised phase III trial was primarily based on comparative effect estimates derived from our prior retrospective cohort study, while adopting a conservative assumed treatment effect. Specifically, the experimental strategy is hypothesised to reduce the risk of disease progression or death by 40% compared with the control arm, corresponding to a HR of 0.60.

The statistical hypotheses are defined as follows: Null hypothesis (H₀): HR=1.0, alternative hypothesis (H₁): HR=0.60; A two-sided significance level of α=0.05 and a statistical power of 80% were applied. The planned accrual period is 24 months, with a total study duration of 36 months. Assuming an annual dropout rate of approximately 3%, sample size estimation was performed using the two-sample log-rank test in Power Analysis and Sample Size V.15. The calculated total sample size required for the study is 132 patients.

All statistical analyses will be conducted using R software (V.4.0.2 or later). The primary efficacy analyses will be performed in the intention-to-treat population, while safety analyses will include all patients who receive at least one study treatment. PFS will be estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and between-group comparisons will be performed using a two-sided log-rank test. HR and corresponding 95% CIs will be calculated using Cox proportional hazards models. Where appropriate, multivariable Cox regression analyses will be conducted to adjust for potential confounding factors.

Continuous variables will be summarised as mean±SD for normally distributed data, or as median (IQR) for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables will be presented as counts and percentages. All statistical tests will be two-sided, and a p value <0.05 will be considered statistically significant.

Data quality assurance and monitoring

In compliance with GCP, a quality assurance and quality control system will be implemented to ensure proper trial conduct and data integrity. Data will be collected, recorded and reported according to the study protocol, GCP standards and applicable regulatory requirements. Regular data review will be performed to ensure accuracy, completeness and consistency.

Discussion

Given the limited options and low efficacy of current SLST, an effective and well-tolerated treatment strategy is needed for patients who develop oligoprogressive HCC during FLST to avoid premature transition to SLST. RT may serve as a ‘1.5 line’ treatment strategy to bridge FLST and SLST for oligoprogressive HCC. To date, no randomised phase III trial has specifically addressed whether local RT combined with continued FLST confers a survival advantage over an early switch to SLST in patients with HCC with FLST-associated oligoprogression. By employing a randomised, multicentre phase III design, this study aims to provide high-level evidence to clarify the role of RT in this clinical context.

Oligoprogression represents a distinct clinical and biological state characterised by limited tumour progression in the context of otherwise sustained systemic disease control. Increasing evidence suggests that oligoprogression reflects spatial tumour heterogeneity and clonal evolution under therapeutic pressure, rather than global treatment resistance.12 13 23 In this setting, oligoprogressive lesions may harbour resistant subclones, while non-progressing lesions remain sensitive to ongoing systemic therapy.

RT offers a rational therapeutic approach for oligoprogressive disease through multiple, potentially complementary mechanisms. First, RT provides durable local control of resistant tumour clones, which may prevent further dissemination and delay the development of widespread systemic resistance.24,26 Second, the continuation of FLST allows sustained suppression of non-progressing lesions, thereby preserving systemic disease control while avoiding the premature initiation of less effective second-line therapies. Third, in the context of immunotherapy-based regimens, RT may exert immunomodulatory effects, including enhanced antigen presentation and modulation of the tumour microenvironment, which could synergise with ongoing systemic treatment.27 Although these systemic immune effects remain exploratory, they provide additional biological plausibility for integrating RT into the management of oligoprogressive HCC.

Another strength of this trial is its translational research exploration, which focuses on two important unresolved issues in the treatment of oligoprogressive disease. First, the current identification of oligoprogression relies predominantly on conventional imaging and RECIST-based criteria, which may incompletely capture the underlying biological complexity of treatment resistance. Radiographical stability or progression does not always accurately reflect clonal dynamics, as subclinical resistant clones may expand before becoming radiographically apparent. FAPI PET-CT28 may provide complementary information beyond conventional imaging modalities, potentially enabling earlier or more precise identification of truly localised progression. ctDNA29 analysis offers a complementary, minimally invasive approach to dynamically monitor clonal evolution, emerging resistance mutations and overall tumour burden over time. In this context, ctDNA may help refine the biological definition of oligoprogression, distinguish true localised resistance from early systemic escape and potentially identify patients most likely to benefit from RT.

Second, although local RT for oligoprogressive lesions has shown encouraging clinical activity, the biological mechanisms underlying its therapeutic benefit remain poorly understood, particularly in HCC. Paired tumour biopsies obtained from oligoprogressive lesions and non-progressive sites provide a unique opportunity to investigate spatial and temporal intrapatient heterogeneity, including differences in genomic alterations, tumour microenvironment composition and immune infiltration patterns. Such analyses may elucidate why certain lesions escape systemic control while others remain sensitive, and how RT reshapes resistant tumour niches. Finally, longitudinal immune profiling of peripheral blood is incorporated to explore systemic immunological effects induced by RT in the setting of ongoing systemic therapy.30 RT has been shown to modulate both innate and adaptive immune responses; however, its impact on systemic immune dynamics in oligoprogressive HCC remains largely unexplored.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Both the FLST regimens and the SLST options encompass a range of guideline-recommended and approved agents and combinations, reflecting contemporary real-world practice in advanced HCC. In particular, the selection of SLST in the control arm is individualised according to prior treatment exposure, liver function status and MDT evaluation, which may introduce treatment heterogeneity and potentially influence interpretation of the comparative treatment effect. Nevertheless, this design allows the treatment strategy under investigation to be evaluated across clinically relevant therapeutic contexts, thereby enhancing the generalisability of the findings and aligning with increasingly individualised clinical decision-making. In addition, because this is an open-label study without blinded central imaging review, potential assessment bias in PFS evaluation cannot be completely excluded, although predefined imaging schedules and RECIST-based assessments are expected to reduce variability across participating centres. Besides, RT techniques and dose fractionation are individualised based on lesion characteristics, anatomical location and OARs constraints. While this approach may contribute to treatment heterogeneity, it reflects routine clinical practice and improves external validity and feasibility across participating centres. Importantly, the randomised, multicentre design, rigorous eligibility criteria and protocol-specified treatment principles are expected to balance baseline patient characteristics and mitigate major confounding, thereby supporting the interpretability and clinical relevance of the study results.

In summary, this phase III trial addresses a clinically relevant yet understudied treatment scenario in advanced HCC. By evaluating the strategy of continuing FLST combined with RT for oligoprogressive lesions, the study has the potential to refine treatment sequencing, inform multidisciplinary decision-making and expand therapeutic options for a patient population with limited alternatives. The rationale for this phase III study is further supported by our recently published prospective phase II trial, which demonstrated encouraging PFS, high DCR and manageable toxicity with this treatment strategy in patients with oligoprogressive HCC.31 If positive, the findings may suggest that, in the interim before more effective and diverse SLST options become widely available, RT could function as a pragmatic ‘1.5-line’ treatment strategy to bridge FLST and SLST in patients with oligoprogressive HCC.
