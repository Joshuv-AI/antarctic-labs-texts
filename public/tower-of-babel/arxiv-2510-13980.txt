Sequential Quantum Measurements and the Instrumental Group Algebra
Christopher S. Jackson

Abstract
Many of the most fundamental observables — position, momentum, phase-point, and spin-direction — cannot be measured by an instrument that obeys the orthogonal projection postulate. Continuous-in-time measurements provide the missing theoretical framework to make physical sense of such observables. The elements of the time-dependent instrument define a group called the instrumental group (IG). Relative to the IG, all of the time-dependence is contained in a certain function called the Kraus-operator density (KOD), which evolves according to a classical Kolmogorov equation. Unlike the Lindblad master equation, the KOD Kolmogorov equation is a direct expression of how the elements of the instrument (not just the total quantum channel) evolve. Shifting from continuous measurements to sequential measurements more generally, the structure of combining instruments in sequence is shown to correspond to the convolution of their KODs. This convolution promotes the IG to an involutive Banach algebra (a structure that goes all the way back to the origins of POVM and C*-algebra theory) which will be called the instrumental group algebra (IGA). The IGA is the true home of the KOD, similar to how the dual of a von Neumann algebra is the true home of the density operator. Operators on the IGA, which play the analogous role for KODs as superoperators play for density operators, are called ultraoperators and various important examples are discussed. Certain ultraoperator-superoperator intertwining relations are also considered throughout, including the relation between the KOD Kolmogorov equation and the Lindblad master equation. The IGA is also shown to have actually two distinct involutions: one respected by the convolution ultraoperators and the other by the quantum channel superoperators. Finally, the KOD Kolmogorov generators are derived for jump processes and more general diffusive processes.

I.1
Prelude

I.1.1
There is a need to get past the idea of instantaneous measurement.

In quantum theory, observables and states are distinct objects, each a certain kind of operator over a Hilbert space.
In particular circumstances, an observable can correspond to directly to a choice of Hermitian operator and the act of observation can be understood to be the random appearance of one of its eigenvalues with a probability that can be calculated by the Born rule.
In this case, the appearance of the eigenvalue is also understood to signify that the state has projected onto the corresponding eigenspace.
This idea of observation, in particular where the post-measurement state is projected, is called a von Neumann measurement or the Luders rule
Von Neumann 1932
;
Busch and Lahti 2009
;
DeBrota and Stacey 2019
.
Useful as it is, the von Neumann-Luders rule fails to make sense of perhaps the most fundamental of observables, the position of a dynamic particle, because the eigendistributions of the position operator are not square-integrable and therefore not sensible state-preparations. (For a more detailed explanation, see the end of section
II.1
.)

Meanwhile, operators do not generally commute because they must also represent physical transformations such as rotations or canonical displacements.
Noncommuting observables therefore become a central feature of quantum measurement theory.
Often it is said that noncommuting observables cannot be measured simultaneously.
Of course, what this refers to is a technical point, that two Hermitian operators cannot be simultaneously diagonalized.
Yet measurements such as optical heterodyne and dual homodyne are (from an experimental point-of-view) obviously able to perform simultaneous measurements of the noncommuting quadratures of a field mode
Leonhardt 1997
;
Shapiro and Wagner 1984
;
Yuen and Lax 1973
;
Personick 1971
.
The von Neumann-Luders rule thus fails again to make sense of the situation.

Despite the abject failure of the von Neumann-Luders rule,
position measurements and simultaneous measurements of noncommuting observables make perfect sense if every von Neumann measurement is not assumed to occur instantaneously, but rather considered to result from a finite-time process or the asymptotics of one.
If a pair of such continuous-in-time processes seperately result in von Neumann measurements of noncommuting observables, it nonetheless also makes sense to run these continuous processes simultaneously, resulting in a new, third measurement that is not of the von Neumann type.

Continuous-in-time processes that result in a von Neumann measurement asymptotically can be seperated into two types, both which involve the concept of an indirect measurement.
The first type is where a single meter interacts with the system observable of interest for a finite amount of time after which the meter is finally measured, registering a single random variable.
Measurements of this first type were also considered by von Neumann in the early 1930s and later revisited by others in the mid 1960s
Von Neumann 1932
;
Arthurs and Kelly 1965
;
She and Heffner 1966
;
Gordon and Louisell 1966
.
In the second type, at every infinitesimal time a new meter weakly interacts with the system observable and is immediately measured, so what is registered overall is a sequence of random variables, one for each infinitesimal increment of time.
Measurements of this second type were originally called forms of
continual monitoring
but these days they tend to be called
continuous measurements

Srinivas and Davies 1981
;
Barchielli et al. 1982
;
Goetsch and Graham 1994
;
Wiseman 1994
;
Silberfarb et al. 2005
;
Jacobs and Steck 2006
;
Barchielli and Gregoratti 2009
;
Wiseman and Milburn 2009
;
Carmichael 2009
;
Cook et al. 2014
;
Gross et al. 2018
.

With continuous measurements, what kinds of instruments are possible?

The kinds of instruments that are produced by measuring noncommuting observables simultaneously are vastly underexplored, with only a handful of fundamental advances, few and far in between, mostly limited to measuring position and momentum

So far, investigations have been focused extensively on two continuous measurements and their

ISM is a simultaneous diffusive measurement of the three orthogonal spin-components, “
J_{x}

J_{y}

J_{z}

ISM stands as the first method discovered that proposes a practical realization of the so-called spin-coherent POVM for arbitrarily large spins because of its

;
Shojaee et al. 2018
;
Jackson and Caves 2022

SPQM is the simultaneous diffusive measurement of the canonical pair of phase space quadrature-components, “
P

SPQM appears to be the first simultaneous diffusive measurement ever considered

;
Barchielli 2006

Both of these measurements have measurement operators (a.k.a. Kraus operators) which generate 7-dimensional Lie groups, respectively called the
instrumental spin group
(
\mathrm{ISpin}(3,\mathbb{C})
) and the
instrumental Weyl-Heisenberg group
(
\mathrm{IWH}

These two groups are examples of what are generally called
instrumental groups
(IGs).
Every simultaneous measurement translates in general to a certain motion or process in the IG.
Put another way, the IG is to be thought of as a static space of possible measurement actions/Kraus operators.
Over time, the possible Kraus operators populate the IG in a specific manner that corresponds collectively to the stochastic motion of a certain distribution.

To analyze and navigate this motion, some sophisticated mathematical tools are required, tools that are not yet familiar to most quantum scientists, hence the recent introduction of the IMP
Jackson and Caves 2023b
.
The need for this sophistication is simple, it is because the IG motions of a continuous simultaneous measurements boil down to analyzing a time-ordered product that generates the finite-time Kraus operators, a problem whose intricacy stems precisely from observable noncommutativity.
For example, if the measurements are diffusive, then these products take the form of a time-ordered exponential, the Kraus operators thus affording the expression

\sqrt{\mathcal{D}\mu[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}]}\;K^{(\vec{L})}\!\left[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}\right]=\sqrt{\mathcal{D}\mu[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}]}\;\mathcal{T}\!\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T-dt}\hat{\updelta}^{(\vec{L})}(d\vec{W}_{t})\right)

\hat{\updelta}^{(\vec{L})}(d\vec{W})\equiv-\hat{Q}_{\vec{L}}\,\kappa dt+\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\hat{L}_{\mu}\sqrt{\kappa}dW^{\mu}\hskip 25.0pt\text{and}\hskip 25.0pt\hat{Q}_{\vec{L}}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\hat{L}_{\mu}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{\mu}+\hat{L}_{\mu}^{2}

(2)

where
T
is the overall time duration of the measurement,
\vec{L}\equiv(L_{1},L_{2},\ldots,L_{n})
is a finite tuple of Lindblad operators (which generalize the Hermitian observable),

is a sequence of Wiener increments (often called the measurement record) representing the raw data output by the instrument,
\mathcal{D}\mu[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}]
is the Wiener measure, and
\hat{X}
denotes a representation of the abstract Lie algebra element
X

(See Appendix
A

\hat{\updelta}^{(L)}

The IG is then the set of all Kraus operators that can result from these time-ordered exponentials and their inverses.
Because the result of the time-ordered exponential is locally determined by the commutator structure of the infinitesimal generators alone, the IG is to be considered as an abstract manifold, the so-called
universal
instrument, which encompasses all possible representations.
What this means mathematically is that the Kraus operators of equation
1
can be considered as representations of a
universal
time-ordered exponential
\gamma\in\text{IG}
, with the
universal property

K^{(\vec{L})}\!\left[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}\right]=K_{\gamma^{(\vec{L})}\!\left[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}\right]}

(3)

where

K_{yx}=K_{y}K_{x}\hskip 25.0pt\text{and}\hskip 25.0pt\gamma^{(\vec{L})}\!\left[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}\right]=\mathcal{T}\!\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T-dt}\updelta^{(\vec{L})}(d\vec{W}_{t})\right).

(4)

The abstracted Lindblad operators,
L_{\mu}
without a hat, have the same commutation relations as the
\hat{L}_{\mu}
but also forget whatever additional spectral information (Hilbert space, highest weights, etc.) may have been included with the
\hat{L}_{\mu}

Q^{\vec{L}}
,
\updelta^{\vec{L}}
, and
\gamma^{\vec{L}}
— are understood to exist in the (closure of the) universal enveloping algebra of the IG Lie algebra.
Technically, we would write
K=\hat{\gamma}
if we wanted to be consistent with the hat notation denoting representation.
However, much of the purpose of this paper is to clarify the distinction between Kraus operators,
K
, and universal trajectories,
\gamma

I.1.3
There is an analytic structure to applying instruments in sequence.

The primary tools of the IMP are the so-called
modified Maurer-Cartan stochastic differential
(MMCSD) and the
Kraus-operator density
(KOD).
With the assistance of any of a set of standard group decompositions, the job of the MMCSD is to transform the raw measured data into a coördinate system that locates the position in the IG.
In this paper, we will pay virtually no attention to the MMCSD.
This is because the instrument structure we are getting at is less about the particular outcomes that would arise in a measurement and more about where these outcomes end up collectively, which is precisely the function of the KOD.

The KOD is a positive function
D_{T}(x)
defined with respect to the left-invariant Haar measure of the IG,

d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,D_{T}(x)=d_{\mathrm{L}}x\int\mathcal{D}\mu[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}]\,\delta_{\text{IG}}\!\left(\gamma[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}]^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}x\right)

(5)

where
d_{\mathrm{L}}x
is the left-invariant Haar measure,
\delta_{\text{IG}}(x)
is the IG delta-function, and the dependence on any Lindblad operators has been dropped (
\gamma=\gamma^{\hat{L}}
).
(See Appendix
C
for details on the delta-function.)
What the KOD essentially does is count the number of measurement records that give the same Kraus operator
x
at any given time
T
, therefore collecting the actions of a continuous measuring instrument.

In the focus on ISM and SPQM, the IMP has thusfar only considered infinite sequences of weak measurements, i.e. diffusive measurements, where the KOD evolves according to a
Kolmogorov forward equation
, a.k.a. a
Fokker-Planck equation
.
In general, the continuous simultaneous measurement of a set of noncommuting observables has an instrument of the form

\mathcal{I}_{T}=\Big\{d_{L}x\,D_{T}(x)\mathcal{O}_{x}\Big\}_{x\in\text{IG}}

(6)

where
T
again is the time duration of the measurement,
d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,
is the left-invariant Haar measure of the IG,
\mathcal{O}_{x}
is the superoperator with action
\mathcal{O}_{x}(\rho)=K_{x}\rho K_{x}^{\dagger}
,
K_{x}
is the Kraus-operator representation (
K_{yx}=K_{y}K_{x}
) of the group element
x\in\text{IG}
, and
D_{T}(x)
is the KOD that satisfies the Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov equation (FPKE)

\frac{1}{\kappa}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}D_{t}(x)=\mathfrak{D}^{\text{\Ankh}}[D_{t}](x)

(7)

with initial condition
D_{0}(x)=\delta_{\text{IG}}(x)
, where
\mathfrak{D}^{\text{\Ankh}}
is the so-called the (Kolmogorov) forward generator
Jackson and Caves 2023b
.
(The
☥
is a type of adjoint which will be explained soon enought, but for the moment please take the symbol
\mathfrak{D}^{\text{\Ankh}}
wholesale.)
By introducing the right-invariant derivative,

\underleftarrow{X}[f](x)\equiv\lim_{h\to\infty}\frac{f(e^{hX}x)-f(x)}{h},

(8)

two examples can be presented which have been studied extensively:
The forward generators of ISM
(
Jackson and Caves 2022
, p. 21)
and SPQM
(
Jackson and Caves 2023a
, p. 14)
,

\mathfrak{D}_{\text{ISM}}^{\text{\Ankh}}=\underleftarrow{\vec{J}^{\,2}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\underleftarrow{J_{x}}\underleftarrow{J_{x}}+\underleftarrow{J_{y}}\underleftarrow{J_{y}}+\underleftarrow{J_{z}}\underleftarrow{J_{z}}\right)\hskip 25.0pt\text{and}\hskip 25.0pt\mathfrak{D}_{\text{SPQM}}^{\text{\Ankh}}=2\underleftarrow{{H_{o}}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\underleftarrow{Q}\underleftarrow{Q}+\underleftarrow{P}\underleftarrow{P}\right)

(9)

where
\vec{J}^{\,2}\equiv J_{x}^{2}+J_{y}^{2}+J_{z}^{2}
is the quadratic Casimir operator for spin and
H_{o}\equiv\frac{1}{2}(Q^{2}+P^{2})
can be recognized as the same operator that would generate simple harmonic motion (if it were instead the Hamiltonian of a unitary process.)

Generators of the KOD FPKE are operators that act on functions of the IG.
To distinguish them from density operators and their superoperators, objects already familiar to quantum scientists, any operator on the space of functions of the IG will be called an
ultraoperator
.
Ultraoperators are generally more analytic in purpose (like differential operators) than their quantum counterparts, so the distinct name should also help to keep this difference in mind.

Again, because the right-invariant derivatives are defined directly in terms of the IG, the KOD FPKE makes sense without any knowledge of the Hilbert space representation in which the state being measured may be and this is precisely why the evolution of the KOD must be understood as
universal
.
Of course, the instrument does not register data without a state to measure.
Nevertheless, when a state is measured, the Born rule probability of registering an element
x
of the IG is of the form

d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,P(x|\rho_{0};T)=d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,D_{T}(x)\tr(\rho_{0}\,K_{x}^{\dagger}K_{x}).

(10)

It is therefore crucial to appreciate that for diffusive measurements, the Born rule consists of three distinct factors: the KOD being the only factor that depends on time, the trace being the only factor that depends on the state, and the Haar measure itself which is neither time- nor state-dependent.

A tool for considering continuous measurement that

familiar to quantum scientists is the so-called Lindblad master equation, the equation that governs the average evolution in time of the state being measured.
In the absense of Hamiltonian system dynamics (in the interaction picture), the Lindblad master equation is

\frac{1}{\kappa}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho_{t}=\mathcal{D}(\rho_{t})

(11)

\frac{1}{\kappa}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathcal{Z}_{t}=\mathcal{D}\circ\mathcal{Z}_{t}

(12)

\rho_{t}=\mathcal{Z}_{t}(\rho_{0})

\mathcal{Z}_{t}
is the channel superoperator for the measuring process,

\mathcal{D}(\rho)=\sum_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{k}\rho+\rho\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{k}\right)+\hat{L}_{k}\rho\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}

(13)

Of course, because the Lindblad master equation takes a state-centric view, it cannot directly address the more fundamental structure of instrument composition, while the KOD FPKE does.

The Lindblad master equation is in fact a direct consequence of the KOD FPKE (equation

Of course, the Lindblad master equation is also a direct consequence of the weak measurement that generates the KOD FPKE to begin with, so the KOD FPKE is by no means required to derive the Lindbladian.
Still, the KOD FPKE has the necessary content for describing the measuring instrument, far more content than the Lindblad master equation.

I.1.5
The structure of sequential measurement is the IG convolution.

The solution to the superoperator Lindblad master equation (equation
12
with initial condition
\mathcal{Z}_{0}(\rho)=\rho
) is obviously the exponential

\mathcal{Z}_{t}=e^{\mathcal{D}\kappa t}

(17)

and has the well-known (one-parameter) group property

\mathcal{Z}_{t+\Delta t}=\mathcal{Z}_{\Delta t}\circ\mathcal{Z}_{t}.

(18)

Just as the Lindblad equation reflects the more elaborate KOD FPKE, this one-parameter group property reflects a similarly more elaborate property enjoyed by the KOD,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 10.0ptD_{t+\Delta t}=D_{\Delta t}*D_{t}\hskip 10.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(19)

where
*
denotes the
IG convolution
,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 10.0ptg*f(z)\equiv\int_{\text{IG}}d_{\mathrm{R}}x\,g(zx^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})f(x)\\
=\int_{\text{IG}}d_{\mathrm{L}}y\,g(y)f(y^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}z).\hskip 10.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(20)

where
d_{\mathbb{R}}x
is the right-invariant Haar measure of the IG.
In terms of the right-invariant measure, this convolution can be recognized as the Chapman-Kolmogorov equation of a right-invariant Markov process.
In terms of the left-invariant measure, this convolution allows the right-invariant Markov process to be converted into simple operator calculus, as will be explained in just a moment.
Right-invariance is the equivalent (in IG language) to the property that the measuring process is nonadaptive, i.e. invariant to the Kraus operator that just preceeds the next step in time.

In fact, this group property extends to any two instruments with a common IG.
If
D(x)
is the KOD of an instrument and we denote it’s total operation superoperator by

\mathcal{Z}_{D}\equiv\int_{\text{IG}}d_{\mathrm{L}}y\,D(y)\mathcal{O}_{y},

\mathcal{Z}_{G}\circ\mathcal{Z}_{D}=\mathcal{Z}_{G*D}

(22)

and the KOD of the composite instrument is
G*D(x)
.
The solution to the superoperator Lindblad master equation is thus a special case of these generalized total operation superoperators,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 10.0pt\mathcal{Z}_{t}=\mathcal{Z}_{D_{t}}.\hskip 10.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

\mathfrak{Z}^{\text{\Ankh}}_{t}=e^{\mathfrak{D}^{\text{\Ankh}}\kappa t}

\mathfrak{Z}^{\text{\Ankh}}_{t+\Delta t}=\mathfrak{Z}^{\text{\Ankh}}_{\Delta t}\circ\mathfrak{Z}^{\text{\Ankh}}_{t}.

The home of the KOD is the IG algebra.

With the IG convolution in hand (equation

I.2
The Commutative Analog

The Instrumental Group (IG), Kraus-Operator Density (KOD), IG convolution, IG Algebra (IGA), convolution ultraoperators, and their relationship to the usual density operators and channel superoperators of standard quantum theory are a lot to swallow for those who have yet to ask for an autonomous, general theory of sequential measurement.
If, however, we consider a commutative IG, then all of these tools and concepts can easily be recognized by anyone familiar with classical real analysis.
To see the forest from the trees, namely the
universal
measuring process from the quantum channels,
it therefore seems quite instructive to go through this.
The baroque notation and strange factors of 2 will look funny, but they are chosen with the intention of making the analogy with the noncommutative case as obvious as possible.
It is very interesting to note how almost all of these key elements seem to stem from Laplace.

Consider the simplest case of diffusion, that of a function
D_{t}(x)
on the real line,

The elements of a generalized measurement are called
Kraus operators
or
measurement operators
.
The index ‘
x
’ is called the
register
and corresponds to a classical piece of data.
The Kraus operators are understood to act on states represented by density operators in the dual of a von Neumann algebra, usually the set of bounded operators
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
of a given Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.
Upon measuring a quantum state with density operator
\rho\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})^{*}
, the event of registering the value ‘
x
’ is understood to be sampled from a probability distribution given by the
generalized Born rule
,

P(x|\rho)=\Tr(\rho\,\Omega_{x}^{\dagger}\Omega_{x})/\Tr(\rho 1),

(63)

on which occasion the state is understood to simultaneously update to
\Omega_{x}\rho\Omega_{x}^{\dagger}
.
Usually the density operator is normalized and renormalized, but this is not necessary and it is often far more convenient to let the normalization wander as it may.
The set of pure states
\{|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|:\psi\in\mathcal{H}\}
is closed under the action of a generalized measurement, in which case one may instead consider the update rule to be
|\psi\rangle\mapsto\Omega_{x}|\psi\rangle
on the event of registering
x
.

Given a generalized measurement
\{\Omega_{x}\}_{x}
, the
positive operator-valued measure
or “
POVM
” is the set of operators

\mathcal{E}=\Big\{\Omega_{x}^{\dagger}\Omega_{x}\Big\}_{x}.

(64)

The POVM is also called a
generalized observable
.
The elements of the POVM are sometimes called
effects
because they are imagined to leave an impression on the observer, but they are more often just called
POVM elements
.
The POVM is all that is needed to calculate the Born rule probability and can be studied in its own right, without reference to a full generalized measurement
Peres 2006
;
Bengtsson and Życzkowski 2006
;
Holevo 2011
;
Kraus 1983
;
Davies and Lewis 1970
;
Jauch and Piron 1967
;
Berberian 1966
;
Sz.-Nagy 1960
;
Stinespring 1955
;
Neumark 1943
, so long as no further, post-measurement observations are made on the system.
Of course, the POVM does not by itself have the information needed to update the state.
However, given a POVM there can exist a generalized measurement with Kraus operators equal to the positive square-roots of the POVM elements, called the
(generalized) Luders measurement
and the assumption to use such Kraus operators is called the
generalized Luders rule

Luders 1952
;
Kirkpatrick 2006
;
Busch and Lahti 2009
;
DeBrota and Stacey 2019
.
The POVM of a position measurement is an essential example where the Luders measurement does not exist, as will be explained later in this subsection.

Operators on the von Neumann algebra or its dual are called
superoperators
.
Associated with generalized measurements, we have already encountered a very fundamental type of superoperator, the so-called
Kraus-rank-one operations

{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}[\Omega]\equiv\Omega\!\odot\!\Omega^{\dagger}

(65)

where
\Omega
is a Kraus operator and the
\odot
is a tensor product with the extra implication that the superoperator acts on operators
\rho
according to

A\!\odot\!B^{\dagger}\,(\rho)\equiv A\rho B^{\dagger}.

(66)

The
instrument
of a generalized measurement is the set of Kraus-rank-one operations,

\mathcal{I}=\Big\{{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}[\Omega_{x}]\Big\}_{x}.

(67)

More generally, one could consider instrument elements that have Kraus-rank higher than one, but we will have no need for this.
In any case, the
instrument elements
also go by the names of conditional operations or selective operations and the sum

\mathcal{Z}\equiv\sum_{x}{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}[\Omega_{x}]

(68)

has been given corresponding names such as the
total operation
, the unconditional operation, or the nonselective operation
Kraus 1983
.
It is also common that the unconditional operation is called
the
quantum operation
Nielsen and Chuang 2000
,
so to avoid confusion, we will mostly reserve the term “operation” for the
total operation
of equation
68
and refer to the elements of equation
67
as the
instrument elements
.
For the instruments considered in this paper, where all instrument elements have Kraus-rank one, the terms “Kraus operator” and “instrument element” will be used interchangably.

In most instances, we will be interested in a continuum of instrument elements.
In such a case each infinitesimal instrument element can be factored into a finite superoperator
{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}[K_{x}]
times an infinitesimal scalar measure
d\mu(x)
,

{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}[\Omega_{x}]=d\mu(x){\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}[K_{x}],

(69)

and we will also call the
{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}[K_{x}]
and
K_{x}
instrument elements and Kraus oepators.
Such an infinitesimal scalar measure is called an
instrument-element distribution
or
Kraus-operator distribution

Jackson and Caves 2023b
.
Often it is also useful to think of the infinitesimal Kraus operators as factoring into the corresponding finite Kraus operator times the square-root of the Kraus-operator distribution,

\Omega_{x}=\sqrt{d\mu(x)}K_{x}.

(70)

In either case, the POVM of equation
64
would therefore be denoted instead as

\mathcal{E}=\{d\mu(x)K_{x}^{\dagger}K_{x}\}_{x}

(71)

and the sum in the total operation (equation
68
) would therefore be denoted instead as

\mathcal{Z}=\int d\mu(x){\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}[K_{x}].

(72)

Without further context, the choice of how the Kraus-operator distribution is factored out of the instrument element is arbitrary or “ostensible”
Warszawski et al. 2020
;
Wiseman 1996
.
In the context of continuous and sequential measurements, however, there is a distinct choice to make as will be explained in section
III.3
.

Any instrument can be “dilated” to a so-called indirect measurement
Kraus 1983
;
Stinespring 1955
;
Von Neumann 1932
where the system of interest interacts with a meter, after which the meter terminates in an eigenvalue measurement of some Hermitian meter observable.
This means the Kraus operators can be expanded into the form

\Omega_{x}=\langle x|\mathcal{U}_{\text{int}}|0\rangle,

(73)

where the meter pointer is prepared in the pure state
|0\rangle
which then couples to the system via the interaction unitary
\mathcal{U}_{\text{int}}
, terminating in the registration of an eigenvalue
x
with linear functional
\langle x|
.
Often the eigenvalue measurement of the meter is considered to be a “von Neumann measurement.”
However, we will more often be interested in when the register
x
corresponds to a continuous position observable, something that does not quite fit into the von Neumann paradigm but is a fundamental tool in the Dirac approach.

To be clear, the basic von Neumann-Dirac tension is only that the eigenfunctions of position are Dirac delta-functions and such functions (more rigorously known today as distributions
De la Madrid 2005
;
Roberts 1966
;
Gelfand and Vilenkin 1966
) are not square-integrable and therefore it is literal nonsense to write
|x\rangle
as a state because it cannot be plugged into the Born rule.
This is an important point and what it reflects is that the preparation of a pure position eigenstate cannot be physical.
Still, this does not exclude position as a legitimate measurement but rather only concludes that the Kraus operators of a position measurement cannot be position eigenstate projection operators.
Instead, the most ideal position measurement could only have Kraus operators of the form
\Omega^{\text{Dirac}}_{x}=\sqrt{dx}|\psi_{x}\rangle\!\langle x|
, where the
|\psi_{x}\rangle
are post-measurement states (with square-integrable wavefunctions) that are not simply specified by the spectrum of the position operator alone.
For this reason a position measurement cannot be considered “von Neumann” while at the same time Dirac’s completeness relation,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx|x\rangle\!\langle x|=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\Omega^{\text{Dirac}{\dagger}}_{x}\Omega^{\text{Dirac}}_{x}=1

(74)

actually makes perfect sense.
Said succinctly, position as a POVM makes rigorous sense but the Luders-von Neumann update rule fails to give it a rigorous instrument.
Regardless, the post-measurement states of a Dirac position measurement are irrelevant to the dilation of the system Kraus operators in equation
73
as it only uses the linear functional
\langle x|
after which no further consideration of the meter is required.

II.2
Total Operations (a.k.a. Channels) and Decoherence

Total operations can also be studied in their own right, without reference to an instrument.
In this case, they are defined as superoperators with two properties, usually referred to as “CP” and “TP”.
A superoperator
\mathcal{Z}
is
completely positive
(CP) if not only does it map positive operators to positive operators, but so too does
\mathcal{Z}\otimes\mathcal{I}d
for any appended Hilbert space with identity superoperator
\mathcal{I}d

Bengtsson and Życzkowski 2006
;
Kitaev et al. 2002
;
Nielsen and Chuang 2002
.
The superoperator is
trace-preserving
(TP) if
\tr(\mathcal{Z}(\rho))=\tr\rho
for all operators
\rho
.
Meanwhile, instrument elements are CP but not TP unless the total operation is the superoperator identity.

These properties can be elegantly captured in terms of a few superoperator involutions
Menicucci 2005
, what we will call the
Hilbert-Schmidt adjoint

\tr\left(Y^{\dagger}\mathcal{Z}^{\ddagger}(X)\right)\equiv\tr\left(\big(\mathcal{Z}(Y)\big)^{\dagger}X\right),

(75)

the
Choi involution

(|a\rangle\!\langle c|\odot|d\rangle\!\langle b|)^{\sharp}\equiv|a\rangle\!\langle b|\odot|d\rangle\!\langle c|,

(76)

and the
Choi-Jamiolkowski quasi-adjoint

{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\ddagger$}}\equiv\sharp\circ{\ddagger}\circ\sharp.

(77)

It is easy to then verify the following useful formulae

(A\odot B^{\dagger})^{\ddagger}\equiv A^{\dagger}\odot B\hskip 25.0pt\text{and therefore}\hskip 25.0pt(A\odot B^{\dagger})^{{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\ddagger$}}}\equiv B\odot A^{\dagger}

(78)

for which it is in turn easy to observe the properties

(\mathcal{Y}\circ\mathcal{X})^{\ddagger}=\mathcal{X}^{\ddagger}\circ\mathcal{Y}^{\ddagger}\hskip 25.0pt\text{and}\hskip 25.0pt(\mathcal{Y}\circ\mathcal{X})^{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\ddagger$}}=\mathcal{Y}^{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\ddagger$}}\circ\mathcal{X}^{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\ddagger$}}

(79)

where it should be noted in particular that the quasi-adjoint
does not
reverse the order of the superoperator composition.
With these involutions, it is easy to see TP is simply the condition
\mathcal{Z}^{\ddagger}(1)=1
.
Further, the
Choi-Jamiolkowski theorem
may be phrased as the statement that
\mathcal{Z}
is CP if and only if
\mathcal{Z}^{\sharp}
is positive, for which it is a corollary that
\mathcal{Z}^{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\ddagger$}}=\mathcal{Z}
.

Of course, the total operation does not supply any information about which register may have been observed or even what kinds of registers are being observed.
In this case that the register becomes a hidden variable, and all the various features of the instrument take on different names because of their change in purpose:
The meter is called the
environment
, the sytem is said to be
open
to the environment, and the CPTP map is called a
(decoherence) channel
.
Meanwhile, every channel can be “dilated” into an instrument like in equation
68
, in which context equation
68
is called a Kraus decomposition, an operator-sum decomposition, or an
unraveling

Wiseman and Milburn 2009
;
Carmichael 2009
;
Kraus 1983
.
In the context of measurement theory and discussions of the observer, one must be careful when using these terms because of how they center the literal discussion around decoherence and dissipative processes, problems for which the instrument itself loses priority because the users of the channel usually don’t know how much of the decoherence is due to the presence of an observer, called the evesdropper.
If the focus is on the measuring process and the observer (which it is for this paper)
one must remember that the value of the register is not hidden
.
Rather, it is quite the opposite.
The registered data is in fact the only thing the observer is able to see directly.

II.3
Continuous-in-Time Decoherence/Dissipation

Infinitesimally generated channels have a particular form called a
Lindbladian
.
Lindbladians include unitary evolution which is generated by superoperators of the form

-\mathrm{ad}[iH]\equiv-iH\odot 1+1\odot iH,

(80)

for any Hermitian operator
H
, called the Hamiltonian.
In addition, there are Lindbladians for dissipative processes, the generators of which are sometimes called
dissipators
and are of the form

\mathcal{D}[\hat{L}]=\hat{L}\odot\hat{L}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}(\hat{L}^{\dagger}\hat{L}\odot 1+1\odot\hat{L}^{\dagger}\hat{L})

(81)

where such an
\hat{L}
is called a
Lindblad operator

Lindblad 1976
.
General Lindbladians are the sum of a Hamiltonian superoperator and any number of dissipators.
Dissipative channels are therefore generated by infinitesimal channels of the form

\mathcal{Z}^{(\hat{L})}_{dt}\equiv e^{\mathcal{D}[\hat{L}]\kappa dt}

(82)

where
dt
is an infinitesimal time and
\kappa
is a rate with units of inverse time so that the Lindblad operators are unitless.
Such dissipative channels can obviously be extend to finite times
dt\rightarrow T
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}^{(\hat{L})}_{T}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathcal{Z}^{(\hat{L})}_{dt}\right)^{\circ T/dt}

(83)

\displaystyle=e^{\mathcal{D}[\hat{L}]\kappa T}

(84)

where
\mathcal{Z}^{\circ n}
denotes the composition of
\mathcal{Z}
with itself
n
times.
Such superoperators are said to be Markovian and satify the
(one-parameter) semigroup property
,

\mathcal{Z}_{t+\Delta t}=\mathcal{Z}_{\Delta t}\circ\mathcal{Z}_{t},

(85)

where the semigroup can be considered to refer to the set of channels
\{\mathcal{Z}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
,
but it often can also refer implicitly to the semigroup of composite instrument elements as will be explained later (in section
III.1
, equation
93
, and section
III.3
, equation
125
).
A state that evolves according to this channel
\rho_{t}=\mathcal{Z}_{t}(\rho_{0})
satisfies a partial differential equation

\frac{1}{\kappa}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho_{t}=\mathcal{D}[\hat{L}](\rho_{t})

(86)

and equations of this type (which more generally include a Hamiltonian and mutiple Lindblad operators) are called
Lindblad master equations
, the term “master” referring to the point that if one knew
\rho_{t}
then they would inherently know the expectation value
\tr(X\rho_{t})
for all observables
X
.

III
Continuous Measurement and Instrumental Groups

With the dimension of time, measurements can be put together in a variety of ways
— continuously (
III.1
), simultaneously (
III.2
), and sequentially (
III.3
) — all of which can be considered to take place in an
instrumental group
(IG).

III.1
The Fundamental Problem of Continuous Measurement: Piling Kraus-Operators (Convolution 1 of 3)

A
continuous measurement
can in principle refer to any infinite sequence of weak measurements.
Applying a sequence of two measuring instruments,
\mathcal{I}_{1}
after
\mathcal{I}_{0}
, defines another instrument given by the
convolution of two sets of operations
,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 10.0pt\mathcal{I}_{1}*\mathcal{I}_{0}\equiv\left\{\mathcal{O}_{1}\circ\mathcal{O}_{0}:\mathcal{O}_{1}\in\mathcal{I}_{1}\text{ and }\mathcal{O}_{0}\in\mathcal{I}_{0}\right\}.\hskip 10.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(87)

Applying an instrument
\mathcal{I}
repeatedly
n
times will thus be denoted as
\mathcal{I}^{*n}
.
The most basic continuous-time instruments are the repeated application of a single weak instrument, also known as continual monitoring.
If
\mathcal{I}_{dt}
denotes the single weak instrument that occurs for a time
dt
, then the continuous instrument
\mathcal{I}_{T}
generated over a time
T
would have a formal expression that parallels equation
83
,

\mathcal{I}_{T}=\mathcal{I}_{dt}^{*T/dt},

(88)

which of course also has the one-parameter semigroup property

\mathcal{I}_{t+\Delta t}=\mathcal{I}_{\Delta t}*\mathcal{I}_{t}.

(89)

Usually, the form of the weak instrument
\mathcal{I}_{dt}
comes effectively in one of two effective types, generating either a jump process or a diffusive process.

The effective weak instrument that generates a jump process has only two elements,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0pt\mathcal{I}^{(\hat{L},\text{Jump})}_{dt}=\left\{(\kappa dt)^{dN}{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}\left[e^{-\frac{1}{2}\hat{L}^{\dagger}\hat{L}\kappa dt}\hat{L}^{dN}\right]\right\}_{dN\in\{0,1\}}\hskip 10.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(90)

and the “jump” refers to the singular binomial event
dN=1
.
For jump processes, the phase of the Lindblad operator is a symmetry of each instrument element,

\mathcal{I}^{(e^{i\phi}\hat{L},\text{Jump})}_{dt}=\mathcal{I}^{(\hat{L},\text{Jump})}_{dt},

(91)

and is therefore an irrelevant degree of freedom.

The effective weak instrument that generates a diffusive process has an infinitesimal continuum of elements,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0pt\mathcal{I}^{(\hat{L},\text{Diff})}_{dt}=\left\{\frac{d(dW)}{\sqrt{2\pi dt}}e^{-\frac{dW^{2}}{2dt}}{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}\left[e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\hat{L}^{\dagger}\hat{L}+\hat{L}^{2})\kappa dt+\hat{L}\sqrt{\kappa}dW}\right]\right\}_{dW\in\mathbb{R}}\hskip 10.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(92)

where the Gaussian random variable
dW
is called a standard Wiener increment and “diffusive” refers to the Brownian motion they generate.
For diffusive processes, the phase actually changes what is being measured.
The examples which make this most obvious are at the extreme:
If the Lindblad operator is Hermitian, then the Kraus operators are all positive (i.e. the instrument is generalized-Luders) and no POVM element is proportional to the identity.
If the Lindblad operator is antiHermitian (i.e. Hermitian times the phase
i=e^{i\pi/2}
) then the Kraus operators are all unitary and every POVM element is proportional to the identity, meaning the registers are completely insensitive to the state preparation.

Either of these instruments can be seen to unravel the infinitesimal dissipative channel in equation
82
.

For the effective system-meter-interaction models that give
90
and
92
, se Appendix
A
.

Every instrument
\mathcal{I}=\{\mathcal{O}_{x}\}
by repetition defines a
convolution semigroup
, the set of all chains of instrument elements,

\tilde{G}_{\mathcal{I}}\equiv\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{I}^{*n}

(93)

where
\mathcal{I}^{*0}
is the instrument with only one element, equal to the identity operation.
As mentioned in section
II
, one should note that the convolution semigroup is quite a bit more elaborate than the one-parameter semigroup of total operations mentioned after equation
85
.
Indeed, the convolution semigroup is usually multi-dimensional and non-commutative.
In practice, the convolution semigroup can often be contained within a group, what will be called the
instrumental group
(IG) and denoted
G_{\mathcal{I}}
.
In section
IV
we will use the IG in a much more systematic way that begins with a more rigorous definition.

Without specifying any commutation relations or the von Neumann algebra on which these instrument elements act,
the elements in
G_{\mathcal{I}}
are by definition distinct and such a group is said to be
free
(of constraints or relations.)
In this case, it is worth noting that the
free product
of two groups (or semigroups)
{G}_{1}
and
{G}_{0}
is precisely the group (or semigroup) of all chains

{G}_{1}*{G}_{0}\equiv\left\{\cdots\circ\mathcal{O}_{2}\circ\mathcal{O}_{1}:\forall k,\mathcal{O}_{k}\in{G}_{1}\cup{G}_{0}\right\}.

(94)

Therefore the IG of a convolution of two instruments is the free product of their individual IGs,

G_{\mathcal{I}_{1}*\mathcal{I}_{0}}=G_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}*G_{\mathcal{I}_{0}}.

(95)

In particular,
G_{\mathcal{I}*\mathcal{I}}=G_{\mathcal{I}}*G_{\mathcal{I}}=G_{\mathcal{I}}
and so for any positive integer
n

G_{\mathcal{I}^{*n}}=G_{\mathcal{I}}.

(96)

If
G_{\mathcal{I}}
is constrained by only commutation relations, the instrument is said to be
universal

Jackson and Caves 2023b
.

The fundamental problem of continuous measurement in general
is whether two sequences of raw registered data,
(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
and
(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n})
drawn from a repeated instrument
\mathcal{I}^{*n}
for arbitrarily large
n
, can be recognized to give the same composite instrument element or not,

\mathcal{O}_{x_{n}}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{O}_{x_{2}}\circ\mathcal{O}_{x_{1}}\overset{?}{=}C\;\mathcal{O}_{y_{n}}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{O}_{y_{2}}\circ\mathcal{O}_{y_{1}}

(97)

up to some positive scalar factor
C\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
(which is added to the Kraus-operator distribution to be explained in section
III.3
.)
This is more commonly known as the
word problem

Rotman 1995
, a topic that was very hot in the ’80s and ’90s, but mostly centered around digital ideas.
Following
Epstein 1992
, a group is said to be
automatic
if two products of group elements can be recognized as equal or distinct by a classical turing machine.
The discussion in
Epstein 1992
;
Gromov 1987
;
Cannon 1984
was specifically interested in the much more complicated problem of finitely-generated groups,
but the concept of an automatic group can just as well be applied to diffusively-generated groups.
Here it will be left open exactly how to define what it means to “recognize” two chains as the same in general.
In our cases of interest, the composite instrument element of every raw chain of events can be put into a certain canonical form that coordinatizes the IG.
The ease in which these coordinatizations work thus suffices to demonstrate that such continuous IGs have the alleged form of automatic pattern recognition.

The universal IGs generated by diffusive processes were recently classified into two groups, depending on the dimension of the IG as calculated via the commutation relations of the Lindblad operators and the quadratic term
Jackson and Caves 2023b
.
If the universal IG is a finite-dimensional Lie group, it is called a
principal instrument
, otherwise, the IG is an infinite-dimensional Lie group and it is called a
chaotic instrument
.
If the IG is finite-dimensional, it can thus be coördinated by applying the MMCSD to any of a standard set of group decompositions
Jackson and Caves 2022
;
Jackson and Caves 2023a
;
Jackson and Caves 2023b
.
Principal instruments are therefore clearly automatic by virtue of the ease with which this coördinatization can be executed.
It may be the case that there are chaotic instruments that are also automatic, but this is an open question.

Of course, the topics of chaos and automation (and their relation to perception, observation, and measurement) are fascinating.
However, they are mostly beyond the current scope of this paper and the expertise of the author.
For some of the original ideas on perception and automation in general, some inspiring references are
Wiener
;
Bellman 1957
;
Sutton and Barto 1998
;
Bennett et al. 1989
;
Dupont et al. 2005
;
Bishop 2006
.

III.2
Simultaneous Measurements of Non-Commuting Observables

Continuous measurements can be done simultaneously, even when their Lindblad operators do not commute
Jackson and Caves 2023b
;
Jackson and Caves 2022
;
Karmakar et al. 2022
.
To see this, consider the weak diffusive Kraus operators with Lindblad operator
\hat{L}
,

K^{\hat{L}}(dW)\equiv e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\hat{L}^{\dagger}\hat{L}+\hat{L}^{2})\kappa dt+\hat{L}\sqrt{\kappa}dW}.

(98)

For two such Lindblad operators
\hat{L}
and
\hat{M}
, the group commutator of one weak diffusive Kraus operator after the other is

\displaystyle\left(K^{\hat{L}}(dW)K^{\hat{M}}(dV)\right)^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}K^{\hat{M}}(dV)K^{\hat{L}}(dW)

\displaystyle=e^{[\hat{M},\hat{L}]\kappa dVdW}

(99)

\displaystyle=1

(100)

because the Kraus-operator distribution is a product of the individual Wiener measures for
dW
and
dV
, and so the Wiener increments are independent.
This means that even if the two Lindblad operators are noncommuting, their weak diffusive Kraus operators still commute.
Thus two diffusive measurements can be done simultaneously, or (perhaps more descriptively) in tandem.
Therefore given any finite number of potentially noncommuting Lindblad operators
\vec{L}=(\hat{L}_{1},\ldots,\hat{L}_{n})
one can define the
Diffusive Measurement of Non-Commuting Observables Simultaneously
(DMNCOS) as the diffusive process generated by the weak instrument

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{(\vec{L},\text{Diff})}_{dt}

\displaystyle\equiv\mathcal{I}^{(\hat{L}_{n},\text{Diff})}_{dt}*\cdots*\mathcal{I}^{(\hat{L}_{2},\text{Diff})}_{dt}*\mathcal{I}^{(\hat{L}_{1},\text{Diff})}_{dt}

(101)

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{d^{n}(d\vec{W})}{(2\pi dt)^{n/2}}e^{-\frac{|d\vec{W}|^{2}}{2dt}}{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}\left[e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\vec{L}^{\dagger}\!\cdot\vec{L}+\vec{L}^{2})\kappa dt+\vec{L}\cdot\sqrt{\kappa}d\vec{W}}\right]\right\}_{d\vec{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}

(102)

where some basic shorthand notation is introduced,

|d\vec{W}|^{2}\equiv\sum_{\mu}(dW^{\mu})^{2}\hskip 25.0pt\text{,}\hskip 25.0pt\vec{L}^{\dagger}\!\cdot\vec{L}+\vec{L}^{2}\equiv\sum_{\mu}\hat{L}_{\mu}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{\mu}+\hat{L}_{\mu}^{2}\hskip 25.0pt\text{, and}\hskip 25.0pt\vec{L}\cdot d\vec{W}\equiv\sum_{\mu}\hat{L}_{\mu}dW^{\mu}.

(103)

The analogous commutativity of weak jump elements is obvious because the product of two jump elements in an infinitesimal time is still negligible, regardless of whether the Lindblad operators of the two jumps commute or not.
One can also define a generating weak instrument for simultaneous jump measurements of non-commuting observables, but this will not be relevant to the current discussion.

If the DMNCOS procedure is carried out for a finite time, the instrument will register finitely many simultaneous sequences of Wiener increments
d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}=\{d\vec{W}_{kdt}\}_{k=0}^{T/dt-1}
called the measurement record, each corresponding to the Kraus operator

\sqrt{\mathcal{D}\mu[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}]}\;K^{(\vec{L})}\!\left[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}\right]=\sqrt{\mathcal{D}\mu[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}]}\;\mathcal{T}\!\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T-dt}\hat{\updelta}^{(\vec{L})}(d\vec{W}_{t})\right)

(104)

where
\mathcal{D}\mu[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}]=\prod_{k=0}^{T/dt-1}\frac{d^{n}(d\vec{W}_{kdt})}{(2\pi dt)^{n/2}}e^{-\frac{|d\vec{W}_{kdt}|^{2}}{2dt}}
is the Wiener measure,
\mathcal{T}\!\exp
denotes the time-ordered exponential, and the infinitesimal generator is

\hat{\updelta}^{(\vec{L})}(d\vec{W})\equiv-\hat{Q}_{\vec{L}}\,\kappa dt+\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\hat{L}_{\mu}\sqrt{\kappa}dW^{\mu}\hskip 25.0pt\text{with}\hskip 25.0pt\hat{Q}_{\vec{L}}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\hat{L}_{\mu}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{\mu}+\hat{L}_{\mu}^{2}.

(105)

The most critical thing to understand about equation
104
is that the time-ordered exponential has a solution that only depends on the commutators of the terms within the infinitesimal generator.
Therefore, the points of the IG and the Brownian motion defined within it by equation
104
can be considered universally, independent of the Hilbert space.
(For further explanation, see
(
Jackson and Caves 2023b
, p. 38)
.)
In effect, this simply means we can take the hats (
\hat{}
) off of all the operators and write an abstract time-ordered exponential,

\gamma^{(\vec{L})}[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}]=\mathcal{T}\!\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T-dt}\updelta^{(\vec{L})}(d\vec{W}_{t})\right)\in\text{IG}

(106)

and consider the Kraus operators as representations of this abstract IG element,

K^{(\vec{L})}\!\left[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}\right]=K_{\gamma^{(\vec{L})}[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}]}

(107)

where
K
with a subscript now denotes a representation of the IG (with the defining property
K_{yx}=K_{y}K_{x}
.)
With this insight, we can consolidate all the registers of the raw instrument (equation
104
) that actually give the same Kraus operator, defining the instrument

\Big\{d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,D_{T}(x){\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}[K_{x}]\Big\}_{x\in IG}

(108)

where
D_{T}(x)
is the so-called Kraus-operator density, defined by

d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,D_{T}(x)=d_{\mathrm{L}}x\int\mathcal{D}\mu[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}]\,\delta_{\text{IG}}\!\left(\gamma[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}]^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}x\right)

(109)

where
\delta_{\text{IG}}(x)
is the delta function of the IG.

III.3
Sequential Measurements: Kraus-Operator Distributions and the Convolution (2 of 3)

A group
G
is
instrumental
if it has a
Kraus-operator representation
,
\{K_{x}\}_{x\in G}
(on some Hilbert space) such that

K_{y}K_{x}=K_{yx},

(110)

with a measure (a.k.a. positive distribution)
d\mu(x)
such that the set

\mathcal{I}\equiv\Big\{d\mu(x){\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}[K_{x}]\Big\}_{x\in G}

(111)

is an instrument, the total operation thus being

\mathcal{Z}_{\mu}=\int_{G}d\mu(x){\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}[K_{x}].

(112)

Such a measure,
d\mu(x)
, will be called the
instrument-element distribution
or
Kraus-operator distribution
.
If the instrumental group (IG) and the Kraus operator representation elements are specified, then the Kraus-operator distribution
d\mu(x)
is fixed and is no longer an ostensible factor of the Kraus operator.
Meanwhile, one also has the corresponding
instrument-element representation
of the IG and we will use the shorthand notation

\mathcal{O}_{x}\equiv{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}[K_{x}]

(113)

for which

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{y}\circ\mathcal{O}_{x}=\mathcal{O}_{yx}.

(114)

It is worth noting that often when the Kraus operator representation
\{K_{x}\}
is irreducible and nonunitary, the tensor product representation
\{\mathcal{O}_{x}\}
is also irreducible.

If two instruments representing the same IG,

\mathcal{I}_{\mu}\equiv\{d\mu(x)\mathcal{O}_{x}\}_{x\in\text{IG}}\hskip 25.0pt\text{and}\hskip 25.0pt\mathcal{I}_{\nu}\equiv\{d\nu(y)\mathcal{O}_{y}\}_{y\in\text{IG}},

(115)

are applied in sequence, then their composite total operation would be

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\nu}\circ\mathcal{Z}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\int_{\text{IG}}d\nu(y)\mathcal{O}_{y}\circ\int_{\text{IG}}d\mu(x)\mathcal{O}_{x}

(116)

\displaystyle=\int_{\text{IG}}d\nu(y)\int_{\text{IG}}d\mu(x)\mathcal{O}_{yx}

(117)

\displaystyle=\int_{\text{IG}}\int_{\text{IG}}d\nu(y)d\mu(y^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}z)\mathcal{O}_{z}

(118)

\displaystyle=\int_{\text{IG}}\int_{\text{IG}}d\nu(zx^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})d\mu(x)\mathcal{O}_{z}

(119)

\displaystyle=\int_{\text{IG}}d(\nu*\mu)(z)\mathcal{O}_{z},

(120)

where the
convolution of two distributions
,
d\nu
after
d\mu
, is

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 10.0ptd(\nu*\mu)(z)\equiv\int_{y\in\text{IG}}d\nu(y)d\mu(y^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}z)\hskip 10.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(121)

\hskip 50.0pt=\int_{x\in\text{IG}}d\nu(zx^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})d\mu(x).

(122)

Therefore, the IG provides the structure to collect all the pairs
(x,y)
that register the same composite instrument element corresponding to the group element
z=yx
.
Sure enough, the convolution of two instruments (equation
87
) with the same IG is an instrument with Kraus-operator distribution equal to the convolution of the two seperate Kraus-operator distributions,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\nu}*\mathcal{I}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{\nu*\mu}

(123)

\displaystyle\equiv\big\{d(\nu*\mu)(z)\mathcal{O}_{z}\big\}_{z\in\text{IG}}

(124)

with total operations that satisfy the
convolution group property
,

\mathcal{Z}_{\nu}\circ\mathcal{Z}_{\mu}=\mathcal{Z}_{\nu*\mu}.

(125)

Equations

and
125
are deeper elaborations of equations
87
and

IV.1
The Haar Measures: Kraus-Operator Densities and the Convolution (3 of 3)

If the IG is locally compact, then it has a distinct pair of measures
d_{\mathrm{L}}y\,
and
d_{\mathrm{R}}x\,

such that for every
g\in
IG

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 10.0ptd_{\mathrm{L}}(gy)=d_{\mathrm{L}}y\,\hskip 10.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}\hskip 25.0pt\text{and}\hskip 25.0ptd_{\mathrm{R}}(xg)=d_{\mathrm{R}}x\,.

(126)

Such a
d_{\mathrm{L}}y\,
is called a
left-invariant Haar measure
and such a
d_{\mathrm{R}}x\,
is called a
right-invariant Haar measure
.
The Haar measures are unique up to normalization and their normalizations may be chosen so that

d_{\mathrm{R}}x\,=d_{\mathrm{L}}(x^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}).

(127)

These Haar measures in turn define the
convolution of two functions
,
g
after
f
, as

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 10.0ptg*f(z)\equiv\int_{y\in\text{IG}}d_{\mathrm{L}}y\,g(y)f(y^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}z)\hskip 10.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(128)

\hskip 35.0pt=\int_{x\in\text{IG}}d_{\mathrm{R}}x\,g(zx^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})f(x).

(129)

The IG is said to be
unimodular
if
d_{\mathrm{R}}x=d_{\mathrm{L}}x
.

Given a Kraus-operator distribution
d\mu(x)
, the Kraus-operator distribution functions or
Kraus-operator densities
(KOD) with respect to either of these Haar measures are the Radon-Nikodym derivatives

\displaystyle\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 10.0ptD^{\mathrm{L}}_{\mu}(x)\equiv\frac{d\mu(x)}{d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,}\hskip 10.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}\hskip 25.0pt\text{and}\hskip 25.0ptD^{\mathrm{R}}_{\mu}(x)\equiv\frac{d\mu(x)}{d_{\mathrm{R}}x\,}.

(130)

Indeed, the KOD of a convolution of two measures is equal to the convolution of the two KODs.
To see this is a simple exercise:
on the one hand

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{L}}z\,D^{\mathrm{L}}_{\nu*\mu}(z)

\displaystyle\equiv d(\nu*\mu)(z)

(131)

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{y\in\text{IG}}d\nu(y)d\mu(y^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}z)

(132)

\displaystyle=\int_{y\in\text{IG}}d_{\mathrm{L}}y\,D^{\mathrm{L}}_{\nu}(y)d_{\mathrm{L}}(y^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}z)D^{\mathrm{L}}_{\mu}(y^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}z)

(133)

\displaystyle=\int_{y\in\text{IG}}d_{\mathrm{L}}y\,D^{\mathrm{L}}_{\nu}(y)d_{\mathrm{L}}z\,D^{\mathrm{L}}_{\mu}(y^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}z)

(134)

\displaystyle=d_{\mathrm{L}}z\,\int_{y\in\text{IG}}d_{\mathrm{L}}y\,D^{\mathrm{L}}_{\nu}(y)D^{\mathrm{L}}_{\mu}(y^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}z)

(135)

\displaystyle\equiv d_{\mathrm{L}}z\,\left(D^{\mathrm{L}}_{\nu}*D^{\mathrm{L}}_{\mu}\right)(z)

(136)

while on the other

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{R}}z\,D^{\mathrm{R}}_{\nu*\mu}(z)

\displaystyle\equiv d(\nu*\mu)(z)

(137)

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in\text{IG}}d\nu(zx^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})d\mu(x)

(138)

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in\text{IG}}d_{\mathrm{R}}(zx^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})D^{\mathrm{R}}_{\nu}(zx^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})d_{\mathrm{R}}x\,D^{\mathrm{R}}_{\mu}(x)

(139)

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in\text{IG}}d_{\mathrm{R}}z\,D^{\mathrm{R}}_{\nu}(zx^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})d_{\mathrm{R}}x\,D^{\mathrm{R}}_{\mu}(x)

(140)

\displaystyle=d_{\mathrm{R}}z\,\int_{x\in\text{IG}}D^{\mathrm{R}}_{\nu}(zx^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})d_{\mathrm{R}}x\,D^{\mathrm{R}}_{\mu}(x)

(141)

\displaystyle=d_{\mathrm{R}}z\,\left(D^{\mathrm{R}}_{\nu}*D^{\mathrm{R}}_{\mu}\right)(z).

(142)

As seen in sections
III.1
and
III.2
and to be explained further in section
IV.4
, the samples of a weak-instrument Kraus-operator distribution are the increments of a continuous invariant Markov process, increments that are locally native to another space beside the IG, or rather tangent to it.
In particular, this means that it is usually easier for small times to work directly with the Kraus-operator distribution of the weak instrument and not the KOD.
Therefore, it is useful to notice that the KOD of a convolution can be computed by directly applying the Kraus-operator distribution of one instrument to the KOD of the other.
There are two such expressions in this regard, one for the KODs with respect to the left-invariant measure,

\displaystyle D^{\mathrm{L}}_{\nu*\mu}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{y\in\text{IG}}d\nu(y)D^{\mathrm{L}}_{\mu}(y^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}z)

(143)

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in\text{IG}}d\nu(zx^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})D^{\mathrm{L}}_{\mu}(x)

(144)

as can be seen from equation
135
,
and the other for the KODs with respect to the right-invariant measure,

\displaystyle D^{\mathrm{R}}_{\nu*\mu}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in\text{IG}}D^{\mathrm{R}}_{\nu}(zx^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})d\mu(x)

(145)

\displaystyle=\int_{y\in\text{IG}}D^{\mathrm{R}}_{\nu}(y)d\mu(y^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}z)

(146)

as can be seen from equation
141
.

As time is considered to march from right to left, it is equation
143
that will be relevant when the instrument sequence is continued forward in time.
Therefore, let it be understood that when the denominator of a KOD is not specified, it is implied to be
the KOD with respect to the left-invariant Haar measure
:

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 10.0ptD_{\mu}(x)\equiv D_{\mu}^{\mathrm{L}}(x).\hskip 10.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(147)

IV.2
Translation Ultraoperators and their Invariant Derivatives

Every element of the IG defines a
left-translation ultraoperator
that acts on the functions of the IG as

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 10.0pt\mathfrak{L}_{g}[f](x)\equiv f(g^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}x).\hskip 10.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(148)

It is easy to see that composition of these ultraoperators is a group homomorphism

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}_{h}[\mathfrak{L}_{g}[f]](x)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}_{g}[f](h^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}x)

(149)

\displaystyle=f(g^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}h^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}x)

(150)

\displaystyle=f\left((hg)^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}x\right)

(151)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}_{hg}[f](x).

(152)

Acting on a given space of functions, the left-translation ultraoperators define the so-called
left-regular representation
of the IG.
With the left-translation ultraoperators, one can extract from the convolution of two functions,

\displaystyle g*f(z)

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{\text{IG}}d_{\mathrm{L}}y\,g(y)f(y^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}z)

(153)

\displaystyle=\int_{\text{IG}}d_{\mathrm{L}}y\,g(y)\mathfrak{L}_{y}[f](z),

(154)

and therefore define for every function
g
the
left-convolution ultraoperator

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Z}^{\mathrm{L}}_{g}

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{\text{IG}}d_{\mathrm{L}}y\,g(y)\mathfrak{L}_{y}

(155)

for which it is easy to see that they form an algebra homomorphism — in particular

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Z}^{\mathrm{L}}_{g}\circ\mathfrak{Z}^{\mathrm{L}}_{f}=\mathfrak{Z}^{\mathrm{L}}_{g*f}.

(156)

We can just as well define a left-convolution ultraoperator for any distribution
\mu
, which in turn means that for KODs (with respect to the left-invariant Haar measure)

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 10.0pt\mathfrak{Z}^{\mathrm{L}}_{\mu}\equiv\mathfrak{Z}^{\mathrm{L}}_{D^{\mathrm{L}}_{\mu}}=\int_{\text{IG}}d\mu(x)\mathfrak{L}_{x}.\hskip 10.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(157)

Of course, there are also
right-translation ultraoperators

\mathfrak{R}_{g}[f](x)\equiv f(xg^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}),

(158)

which instead compose to form a group anti-homomorphism,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{h}[\mathfrak{R}_{g}[f]](x)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{R}_{g}[f](xh^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})

(159)

\displaystyle=f(xh^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}g^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})

(160)

\displaystyle=f(x(gh)^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})

(161)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{R}_{gh}[f](x).

(162)

Although it may be tempting to choose an alternative definition so to make the anti-homomorphism a plain homomorphism, we actually want to define the right-translation ultraoperators in this way because, like the left-translation ultraoperators, they transport a bump localized at the identity to a bump localized at the specified group element.
Here too one can extract from the definition of the convolution

\displaystyle g*f(z)

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{\text{IG}}d_{\mathrm{R}}x\,g(zx^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})f(x)

(163)

\displaystyle=\int_{\text{IG}}d_{\mathrm{R}}x\,\mathfrak{R}_{x}[g](z)f(x)

(164)

and define for every function
f
the
right-convolution ultraoperator

\mathfrak{Z}^{\mathrm{R}}_{f}=\int_{\text{IG}}d_{\mathrm{R}}x\,f(x)\mathfrak{R}_{x}

(165)

for which it is also easy to see they define an algebra anti-homomorphism — specifically

\mathfrak{Z}^{\mathrm{R}}_{g}\circ\mathfrak{Z}^{\mathrm{R}}_{f}=\mathfrak{Z}^{\mathrm{R}}_{f*g}.

(166)

Again, we can just as well define a right-convolution ultraoperator for any distribution
\mu
and for KODs with respect to the right-invariant Haar measure

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Z}^{\mathrm{R}}_{\mu}\equiv\mathfrak{Z}^{\mathrm{R}}_{D^{\mathrm{R}}_{\mu}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\text{IG}}d\mu(x)\mathfrak{R}_{x}.

(167)

We will not have any need for the right-convolution ultraoperators.

Fantasticaly, the left- and right-translation ultraoperators commute,

\mathfrak{R}_{h}\circ\mathfrak{L}_{g}=\mathfrak{L}_{g}\circ\mathfrak{R}_{h}.

(168)

In other words, these translations are invariants of each other.
Because of this, left-translation is also called right-invariant translation and similarly right-translation is also called left-invariant translation.

If an IG is differentiable then it is called an
instrumental Lie group
and has an associated
instrumental Lie algebra
(ILA). In the left-regular representation, the elements of the ILA correspond to the set of invariant differential ultraoperators on the functions of the IG, with the ultraoperator commutator representing the Lie bracket:
For every element
X\in
ILA, one can differentiate a function
f
at the point
x\in
IG by left-translation,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 10.0pt\left.\underleftarrow{X}\right|_{x}f\equiv\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(e^{hX}x)-f(x)}{h}.\hskip 10.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(169)

In turn, one can define differential ultraoperators

\underleftarrow{X}[f](x)\equiv\left.\underleftarrow{X}\right|_{x}f,

(170)

the so-called
right-invariant derivatives
because they have the property

\underleftarrow{X}\circ\mathfrak{R}_{g}=\mathfrak{R}_{g}\circ\underleftarrow{X}\hskip 50.0pt\text{or equivalently}\hskip 50.0pt\left.\underleftarrow{X}\right|_{x}\circ\mathfrak{R}_{g}=\left.\underleftarrow{X}\right|_{xg}.

(171)

Given an IG element
e^{X}
, one can consider a path (a.k.a. one-parameter subgroup or flow line)
e^{tX}
which at
t=0
is the identity and at
t=1
is the given IG element.
Similarly, any function
f
of the IG can be considered as a function over the one-parameter subgroup
\phi^{X}(t)=f(e^{tX}x_{o})
.
On any such one-parameter function one may consider Taylor expansions, the convergences of which correspond to properties of the translation ultraoperator
\phi^{X}(t+\delta)=e^{\delta\frac{d}{dt}}[\phi^{X}](t)
.
By an analytic continuation of Taylor’s theorem on each one-parameter subgroup, one can therefore observe that the right-invariant derivatives generate the left-translation ultraoperators,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 10.0pt\mathfrak{L}_{e^{X}}=e^{-\underleftarrow{X}}.\hskip 10.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(172)

Indeed, one can check that the map
X\mapsto\underleftarrow{X}
is in fact a Lie algebra anti-homomorphism,

[\underleftarrow{X},\underleftarrow{Y}]_{\text{Comm}}=-\underleftarrow{[X,Y]_{\text{Abst}}}

(173)

where
[\mathfrak{U},\mathfrak{V}]_{\text{Comm}}\equiv\mathfrak{U}\circ\mathfrak{V}-\mathfrak{V}\circ\mathfrak{U}
denotes the commutator of two ultraoperators on the space of functions on the IG and
[X,Y]_{\text{Abst}}
denotes the abstract Lie bracket.

Similarly we can define for every element
X\in
ILA the
left-invariant derivative
at the point
x\in
IG of a function
f
as

\displaystyle\left.\underrightarrow{X}\right|_{x}f\equiv\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(xe^{hX})-f(x)}{h}

(174)

and differential ultraoperators

\underrightarrow{X}[f](x)\equiv\left.\underrightarrow{X}\right|_{x}f

(175)

so named because they have the property

\underrightarrow{X}\circ\mathfrak{L}_{g}=\mathfrak{L}_{g}\circ\underrightarrow{X}\hskip 50.0pt\text{or equivalently}\hskip 50.0pt\left.\underrightarrow{X}\right|_{x}\circ\mathfrak{L}_{g}=\left.\underrightarrow{X}\right|_{gx}.

(176)

Sure enough, they too generate (over the vector space of analytic functions) the right-translation IG-ultraoperators

\mathfrak{R}_{e^{tX}}=e^{-t\underrightarrow{X}}

(177)

and the map
X\mapsto\underrightarrow{X}
is a Lie-algebra homomorphism,

[\underrightarrow{X},\underrightarrow{Y}]_{\text{Comm}}=\underrightarrow{[X,Y]_{\text{Abst}}}.

(178)

IV.3
The IG Algebra and Two Involutions: Gelfand-Kolmogorov and Cartan-Schmidt

Remembering that we are ultimately interested in continuous measurements, where time is represented by a product of Kraus operators proceeding from right to left, we focus again on the left-invariant Haar measure.

With the left-invariant Haar measure, one can define various norms and function spaces on the IG.
For any real number
p\geq 1
, the Banach space
L^{p}(\text{IG})
is the space of functions with norm

||f||_{p,\text{IG}}\equiv\int_{\text{IG}}d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,|f(x)|^{p}.

(179)

Of these Banach spaces, only
L^{1}(\text{IG})
is closed under convolution.
3
3

3

The simplest example of this is to consider an IG isomorphic to the real numbers,
\mathbb{R}
, where both Haar measures are equal to the standard differential of the Riemann integral,
d(x+a)=dx
.
For any real number
\epsilon>0
, a bounded function
f
of
\mathbb{R}
with tails that fall off as
f(|x|\gg 1)\sim\frac{1}{|x|^{\frac{1}{p}+\epsilon}}
is in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
.
The convolution of such an
f
with itself would therefore have tails that fall off as
f\!*\!f\,(|x|\gg 1)\sim\frac{1}{|x|^{\frac{2}{p}+2\epsilon-1}}
.
Therefore if
p>1
, then
f*f
is not in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
for
2\epsilon\leq 1-\frac{1}{p}
.

The Banach space
L^{1}(\text{IG})
is therefore a Banach algebra called the group algebra or as we will call it the
IG algebra
(IGA).
The IG algebra is the place where KODs live and closure under IG-convolution is simply a reflection of the fact that two instruments applied in sequence define an instrument.

In addition to norms, one can use the left-invariant measure of the IG to define the
IG-inner product
between two complex-valued functions,

(g,f)_{\text{IG}}\equiv\int_{\text{IG}}d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,\;\overline{g(x)}f(x)

(180)

where
\overline{c}
denotes the complex conjugate of the complex number
c
.
With the IG-inner product, one can therefore define dual spaces of functions.
Most important for us is the space dual to the IGA, the space of bounded functions on the IG, sometimes called
L^{\infty}(\text{IG})
.
Since KODs live in the IGA, the instrument-element representation must consist of bounded superoperators for the total operation to exist.

There are a couple of important subspaces of the IGA that come up.
Although here is not the place to dig into them too much, they are mentioned because the KODs which solve the Kolmogorov equation to be introduced in section
IV.4
are indeed in these subspaces for finite times.
The first important subspace is the closure of the space of infinitely-differentiable functions with compact support, sometimes called the Frechet space
Helgason 2008
.
The dual space of this Frechet space is called the space of distributions.
The term “distribution” here refers to “functions” like Dirac delta-functions and their derivatives which although related are not to be confused with the Kraus-operator distribution, where the KOD is actually in the Frechet space, on the other side of the function duality from the “distributions”.
The second important subspace is the so-called Schwartz space for semisimple IGs, their dual space known as the tempered distributions.
Harish-Chandra 1966

If the KOD is in the Schwartz space, then the corresponding instrument-element representations are examples of the so-called tempered representations.
Knapp 1986

However, for quantum systems the instrument-element representation is generally not a unitary representation as will be explained in just a moment.

The IG-inner product defines an ultraoperator adjoint

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 10.0pt\big(g,\mathfrak{U}^{\text{\Ankh}}[f]\big)_{\text{IG}}\equiv\left(\mathfrak{U}[g],f\right)_{\text{IG}}\hskip 10.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(181)

which will be called the
Kolmogorov (ultraoperator) adjoint
because of its application in section
IV.4
.
The use of an
☥
, called the “Ankh” or “key to life”, is mostly because every obvious choice to denote an adjoint has already been taken —
*
for convolution,
{\dagger}
for Hermitian conjugation,
{\ddagger}
for the Hilbert-Schmidt adjoint (equation
75
) , and

\ddagger

for the Choi-Jamiolkowski quasi-adjoint (equation
77
).
As far as the author is aware, the Ankh does not have any political or historical associations that might make it a distastful symbol to use.
If anything, the Ankh has a fun turn-of-the-twentieth century Western alchemical vibe.

Of particular importance is the Kolmogorov adjoint of the left-translation ultraoperators and their right-invariant derivatives

(\mathfrak{L}_{g})^{\text{\Ankh}}=\mathfrak{L}_{g}^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}\hskip 50.0pt\text{and}\hskip 50.0pt(\underleftarrow{X})^{\text{\Ankh}}=-\underleftarrow{X}.

(182)

One thing this means is that the left-translation ultraoperators are
stochastic
, meaning that they preserve the
L^{1}(\text{IG})
-norm for KODs
D(x)
which are real and non-negative,

\displaystyle||\mathfrak{L}_{g}[D]||_{1,\text{IG}}

\displaystyle=(\mathfrak{L}_{g}[D],1)_{1,\text{IG}}

(183)

\displaystyle=(D,\mathfrak{L}_{g}^{\text{\Ankh}}[1])_{1,\text{IG}}

(184)

\displaystyle=(D,1)_{1,\text{IG}}

(185)

\displaystyle=||D||_{1,\text{IG}}

(186)

where
1
in the right argument of the IG-inner product stands for the constant function equal to unity over all of the IG.
While this also technically means the left-translation ultraoperators are
☥
-unitary in
L^{2}(\text{IG})
, this description should be avoided in this context because for quantum measurement they act on KODs and not wavefunctions (or density operators.)

Meanwhile, the right-translation ultraoperators are
not
stochastic unless the IG is unimodular.
We will have no further use for the right-translation ultraoperators, so this point will not be discussed further.
Remember that the integration has been chosen with respect to the left-invariant Haar measure because the time of the measuring process is always considered to march from right to left, as explained at the end of section
IV.1
.

If the IG is unimodular, then the IGA is also an
involutive Banach algebra
, actually in two different ways.
The first involution is what we will call the
Gelfand involution

f^{\text{\Ankh}}(x)\equiv\overline{f(x^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})}.

(187)

Note that the symbol
☥
has now been given a second definition, for elements of the IGA instead of the first definition for ultraoperators.
With the Gelfand involution, it is easy to see that the left-regular representation with the Kolmogorov ultraoperator adjoint is an
☥
-representation of the IGA, meaning it respects the Gelfand involution,

\displaystyle\left(\mathfrak{Z}^{\mathrm{L}}_{f}\right)^{\text{\Ankh}}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\int d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,f(x)\mathfrak{L}_{x}\right)^{\text{\Ankh}}

(188)

\displaystyle=\int d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,\overline{f(x)}\mathfrak{L}_{x}^{\text{\Ankh}}

(189)

\displaystyle=\int d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,\overline{f(x)}\mathfrak{L}_{x^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}}

(190)

\displaystyle=\int d_{\mathrm{L}}(x^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})\overline{f(x^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})}\mathfrak{L}_{x}

(191)

\displaystyle=\int d_{\mathrm{R}}x\,f^{\text{\Ankh}}(x)\mathfrak{L}_{x}

(192)

\displaystyle=\int d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,f^{\text{\Ankh}}(x)\mathfrak{L}_{x}

(193)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{Z}^{\mathrm{L}}_{f^{\text{\Ankh}}}.

(194)

The IGA with the Gelfand involution is not quite a C*-algebra, or rather a “
\text{C}^{\text{\Ankh}}
-algebra”, because
||f^{\text{\Ankh}}*f||_{1,\text{IG}}\neq||f^{\text{\Ankh}}||_{1,\text{IG}}||f||_{1,\text{IG}}
.
However, this kind of involutive Banach algebra does in general define an enveloping C*-algebra by a standard construction.
Dixmier 1977
;
Dieudonne 1979

In this case, I believe the left-regular representation continues to be an
☥
-representation.
On the other hand, the quantum instrument-element representations equipped with either

or

\ddagger

are
not

☥
-representations.
No deep use is made in this paper of the enveloping C*-algebraic aspects of the IGA, but this connection may nonetheless be important in other contexts where one might be interested in unitary representations of the IGA, such as would be the case in AdS-CFT.
Kitaev 2017

The second involution is called the

,

f^{\ddagger}(x)\equiv\overline{f(x^{\dagger})}

(195)

where
{\dagger}
is a geometric abstraction of the Hermitian conjugate, also called a (type-IV) Cartan involution

;
Jackson and Caves 2022
;
Barut and Raçszka 1986
.
The Kraus-operator representation of the IG is always assumed to be analytic in the sense that the actual Hermitian conjugate for a given representation
K_{x}
respects the type-IV Cartan involution,

(K_{x})^{\dagger}=K_{x^{\dagger}}

(196)

(\mathcal{O}_{x})^{\ddagger}=\mathcal{O}_{x^{\dagger}},

(197)

where recall the

Meanwhile, quantum operations such as equation

\mathcal{Z}_{f}\equiv\int d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,\overline{f(x)}\mathcal{O}_{x}.

(198)

With the Cartan involution, it is then easy to see that quantum instruments with the Hilbert-Schmidt adjoint are
{\ddagger}
-representations of the IGA, meaning they respect the the Cartan involution,

\displaystyle(\mathcal{Z}_{f})^{\ddagger}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\int d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,\overline{f(x)}\mathcal{O}_{x}\right)^{\ddagger}

(199)

\displaystyle=\int d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,f(x)\mathcal{O}_{x}^{\ddagger}

(200)

\displaystyle=\int d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,f(x)\mathcal{O}_{x^{\dagger}}

(201)

\displaystyle=\int d_{\mathrm{L}}(x^{\dagger})f(x^{\dagger})\mathcal{O}_{x}

(202)

\displaystyle=\int d_{\mathrm{R}}x\,\overline{f^{\ddagger}(x)}\mathcal{O}_{x}

(203)

\displaystyle=\int d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,\overline{f^{\ddagger}(x)}\mathcal{O}_{x}

(204)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Z}_{f^{\ddagger}}

(205)

where in particular
d_{\mathrm{L}}(x^{\dagger})=d_{\mathrm{R}}x\,
because the right-invariant measure is unique

Dieudonne 1979
, p. 26)
.
In the same way as was explained for the

The enveloping

Nevertheless, it is a technical perspective that does not seem to appear yet in quantum measurement circles.

The left-convolution ultraoperators are all that we will need to consider, so we will drop the

\mathcal{I}_{dt}
be a weak measurement such as those found in sections

and

be the Kraus-operator distribution of a continuous instrument

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{T}

\displaystyle\equiv\mathcal{I}_{dt}^{*T/dt}

(207)

\displaystyle=\left\{d\mu_{T}(x)\mathcal{O}_{x}\right\}_{x\in\text{IG}}

(208)

with corresponding total operation

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{T}

\displaystyle\equiv\mathcal{Z}_{dt}^{\circ T/dt}

(209)

\displaystyle=\int_{\text{IG}}d\mu_{T}(x)\mathcal{O}_{x}.

(210)

Meanwhile, let the KOD — introduced in section

(see equations
130

147

be denoted as

D_{T}(x)\equiv\frac{d\mu_{T}(x)}{d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,}.

(211)

Recall that not only do the instrument and total operation satisfy the one-parameter group property,

\mathcal{I}_{t+dt}=\mathcal{I}_{dt}*\mathcal{I}_{t}\hskip 50.0pt\text{and}\hskip 50.0pt\mathcal{Z}_{t+dt}=\mathcal{Z}_{dt}\circ\mathcal{Z}_{t}

(212)

as explained around equations
89
and
85
,
but so do the Kraus-operator distribution and its KOD:

\mu_{t+dt}=\mu_{dt}*\mu_{t}\hskip 50.0pt\text{and}\hskip 50.0ptD_{t+dt}=D_{dt}*D_{t}

(213)

which can be thought of as special cases of equations
123
,
125
, and

.
Equations such as

.
Jackson and Caves 2023b
;
Gardiner 1986
;
Gardiner 2019
;
Gardiner 2021

Let
\mathcal{O}_{x}
be an instrument-element representation of the IG (as explained in section
III.3
.)
Observe in particular that they intertwine the left-translation ultraoperators (introduced in section
IV.2
) with left composition by the corresponding superoperator from the instrument-element representation,

\boxed{\hskip 10.0pt\phantom{\Bigg(}\mathfrak{L}_{g}^{\text{\Ankh}}[\mathcal{O}_{x}]=\mathcal{O}_{g}\circ\mathcal{O}_{x}.\phantom{\Bigg)}\hskip 10.0pt}

(214)

Let the weak instrument be now denoted

\mathcal{I}_{dt}=\{d\mu_{dt}(v)\mathcal{O}_{\gamma(v)}\}_{v\in\Omega}

(215)

with local sample space
\Omega
(equal to either

\mathcal{Z}_{dt}\equiv\int_{\Omega}d\mu_{dt}(v)\mathcal{O}_{\gamma(v)}.

By another simple observation,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{dt}\circ\mathcal{O}_{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}d\mu_{dt}(v)\;\mathcal{O}_{\gamma(v)}\circ\mathcal{O}_{x}

(217)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}d\mu_{dt}(v)\;\mathfrak{L}_{\gamma(v)^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}}[\mathcal{O}_{x}]

(218)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{Z}_{dt}[\mathcal{O}_{x}],

(219)

it is clear that the total operation superoperator
\mathcal{Z}_{dt}
acting on the left of the instrument-element representation

by composition is equivalent to the left-convolution ultraoperator (also introduced in section

)

\mathfrak{Z}_{dt}\equiv\int_{\Omega}d\mu_{dt}(v)\;\mathfrak{L}_{\gamma(v)^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}}

(220)

acting on the instrument-element representation as a function of the IG.

Recall that the KOD of the instrument at time

is
D_{t}(x)
, so equation

may also be written as

\mathcal{I}_{t}=\{d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,D_{t}(x)\mathcal{O}_{x}\}_{x\in\text{IG}},

with total operation

Following this with an application of the weak instrument, the composite instrument at time

would be

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{t+dt}

\displaystyle\equiv\mathcal{Z}_{dt}\circ\mathcal{Z}_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{\text{IG}}d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,D_{t}(x)\;\mathcal{Z}_{dt}\circ\mathcal{O}_{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\text{IG}}d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,D_{t}(x)\;\mathfrak{Z}_{dt}[\mathcal{O}_{x}]

\displaystyle D_{t+dt}=\mathfrak{Z}_{dt}^{\text{\Ankh}}[D_{t}]

(227)

where
☥
is the Kolmogorov adjoint over the IG (introduced in section

Meanwhile, the adjoint of the total operation ultraoperator of the weak instrument will have an infinitesimal generator,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Z}_{dt}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}d\mu_{dt}(v)\mathfrak{L}_{\gamma(v)^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}}

\displaystyle=e^{\mathfrak{D}\kappa dt}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{I}+\mathfrak{D}\kappa dt

where

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 10.0pt\frac{1}{\kappa}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}D_{t}=\mathfrak{D}^{\text{\Ankh}}[D_{t}]\hskip 10.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

\mathcal{I}^{(L,\text{Jump})}_{dt}=\left\{(\kappa dt)^{dN}{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}\left[e^{-\frac{1}{2}L^{\dagger}L\kappa dt}L^{dN}\right]\right\}_{dN\in\{0,1\}}

(233)

as introduced in section
III.1
(equations
90
and
92
.)
Simultaneous measurements can also be considered, but for simplicity only one Lindblad operator will be considered at first.
These weak instruments define IGs (also explained in section
III.1
.)
Let the instrument elements of these weak instruments be “minimally” separated into a Kraus-operator distribution and an instrument-element representation in the following way:

\mu_{dt}(dN)\equiv(\kappa dt)^{dN}\hskip 50.0pt\text{and}\hskip 50.0pt\mathcal{O}_{\gamma_{L,\text{Jump}}(dN)}\equiv{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}\left[e^{-\frac{1}{2}\hat{L}^{\dagger}\hat{L}\kappa dt}\hat{L}^{dN}\right]

for jump processes and

(235)

for diffusive processes.

With these in mind, the total operation and Kolmogorov forward-generating ultraoperators may be calculated explicitly.
For the jump process we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Z}_{dt,L,\text{Jump}}^{\text{\Ankh}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{dN}\mu_{dN}\mathfrak{L}_{\gamma_{L,\text{\text{Jump}(dN)}}}

(236)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}_{\gamma_{L,\text{\text{Jump}(0)}}}+\kappa dt\mathfrak{L}_{\gamma_{L,\text{\text{Jump}(1)}}}

(237)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}_{e^{-L^{\dagger}L\frac{1}{2}\kappa dt}}+\kappa dt\mathfrak{L}_{L}

(238)

\displaystyle=e^{\underleftarrow{L^{\dagger}L}\frac{1}{2}\kappa dt}+\kappa dt\mathfrak{L}_{L}

(239)

\displaystyle=1+\kappa dt\left(\frac{1}{2}\underleftarrow{L^{\dagger}L}+\mathfrak{L}_{L}\right)

(240)

and so the infinitesimal generator is

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0pt\mathfrak{D}_{L,\text{Jump}}^{\text{\Ankh}}=\frac{1}{2}\underleftarrow{L^{\dagger}L}+\mathfrak{L}_{L}.\hskip 10.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

For the diffusive process we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Z}_{dt,L,\text{Diff}}^{\text{\Ankh}}

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(dW)\mathfrak{L}_{\gamma_{\text{L,Diff}}(dW)}

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(dW)e^{\underleftarrow{L^{\dagger}L+L^{2}}\frac{1}{2}\kappa dt-\underleftarrow{L}\sqrt{\kappa}dW}

(243)

\displaystyle=e^{\underleftarrow{L^{\dagger}L+L^{2}}\frac{1}{2}\kappa dt}\int d\mu(dW)e^{-\underleftarrow{L}\sqrt{\kappa}dW}

(244)

\displaystyle=e^{\underleftarrow{L^{\dagger}L+L^{2}}\frac{1}{2}\kappa dt}e^{\frac{1}{2}\underleftarrow{L}\underleftarrow{L}\kappa dt}

\displaystyle=e^{(\frac{1}{2}\underleftarrow{L^{\dagger}L+L^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\underleftarrow{L}\underleftarrow{L})\kappa dt}

(246)

and so the infinitesimal generator is

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0pt\mathfrak{D}_{L,\text{Diff}}^{\text{\Ankh}}=\frac{1}{2}\underleftarrow{L^{\dagger}L+L^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\underleftarrow{L}\underleftarrow{L}.\hskip 10.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(247)

\mathfrak{D}_{\vec{L}}^{\text{\Ankh}}=\sum_{i}\mathfrak{D}_{L_{i}}^{\text{\Ankh}}.

V
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, the instrument manifold
4
4

4

“Manifold” is apparently a term from Kantian philosophy, where it is defined as “The sum of the particulars furnished by sense before they have been unified by the synthesis of the understanding”, according to Oxford Languages.

program was further developed by showing that the sequential application of measuring instruments corresponds to the instrumental-group (IG)
convolution
of their Kraus-operator densities (KODs).
The IG and KOD were originally designed to analyze certain continuous measurements, the so-called principal instruments which generate finite-dimensional universal Lie groups
Jackson and Caves 2022
;
Jackson and Caves 2023a
;
Jackson and Caves 2023b
.
In this paper, the perspective was shifted away from continuous measurement and towards the sequential, where by so doing, the presence of the convolution structure was revealed.
With the convolution at hand, it became clear that the space of absolutely integrable functions of the IG, among which KODs are a special case, was in fact an involutive Banach algebra, involutive in two ways, and we called this algebra the instrumental group algebra (IGA).

The convolution was developed in three phases.
We began by identifying how a sequence of two measuring instruments defines a new instrument,
the set of all pairs of elements, one from the first and the other from the second, composed.
This kind of set composition was the first, most basis type of convolution (equation
87
).
Then, the concepts of an instrumental group (IG) and the subsequent Kraus-operation distribution were introduced by recognizing that there is often enough structure to identify whether two distinct pairs of instrument elements produce the same composite instrument element or not,

\Omega_{x}=\sqrt{d\mu(x)}K_{x}\hskip 25.0pt\text{where}\hskip 25.0ptK_{y}K_{x}=K_{yx}.

(260)

This converted the structure of sequential measurement from combining sets of instrument elements to the convolution of their Kraus-operator distributions (equation
121
),

\mathcal{I}_{1}*\mathcal{I}_{0}\hskip 25.0pt\Longrightarrow\hskip 25.0ptd(\mu_{1}*\mu_{0})(z)\equiv\int_{y\in\text{IG}}d\mu_{1}(y)d\mu_{0}(y^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}z).

(261)

Finally, the IG was assumed to be “topological”, meaning that it was assumed to have invariant measures,

d\mu(x)=d_{\mathrm{L}}xD(x)\hskip 25.0pt\text{where}\hskip 25.0ptd_{\mathrm{L}}(gx)=d_{\mathrm{L}}x.

(262)

This converted the Kraus-operator distributions into the more functional Kraus-operator densities (KOD) which could be convolved in a style more typically seen in analysis (equation
128
),

d(\mu_{1}*\mu_{0})(z)\hskip 25.0pt\Longrightarrow\hskip 25.0ptD_{1}*D_{0}(z)\equiv\int_{\text{IG}}d_{\mathrm{L}}y\,D_{1}(y)D_{0}(y^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}z).

(263)

Having identified the convolution, the multiplication of the IGA, it was demonstrated that the Lindblad master equation which governs the evolution of the quantum channel superoperator and the previously discovered Kolmogorov equation which governs the evolution of the KOD,

\frac{1}{\kappa}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{(\lambda)}=\mathcal{D}^{(\lambda)}\circ\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{(\lambda)}\hskip 25.0pt\text{and}\hskip 25.0pt\frac{1}{\kappa}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}D_{t}(x)=\mathfrak{D}^{\text{\Ankh}}[D_{t}](x),

(264)

are in fact intertwined by the instrument element representation of the IG,

\mathfrak{D}[\mathcal{O}_{x}^{(\lambda)}]=\mathcal{D}^{(\lambda)}\circ\mathcal{O}_{x}^{(\lambda)}.

(265)

The one-parameter group property characteristic of the superoperators that solve the Lindblad master equation were seen to lift to a one-parameter group property between KODs, the invariant Chapman-Kolmogorov equation,

\mathcal{Z}^{(\lambda)}_{t+\Delta t}=\mathcal{Z}^{(\lambda)}_{\Delta t}\circ\mathcal{Z}^{(\lambda)}_{t}\hskip 25.0pt\Longrightarrow\hskip 25.0ptD_{t+\Delta t}=D_{\Delta t}*D_{t}.

(266)

Simultaneously, this showed that all quantum channel superoperators are in fact representations of IGA elements,

\mathcal{Z}^{(\lambda)}_{D_{1}}\circ\mathcal{Z}^{(\lambda)}_{D_{0}}=\mathcal{Z}^{(\lambda)}_{D_{1}*D_{0}}\hskip 25.0pt\text{and}\hskip 25.0pt\mathcal{Z}^{(\lambda)}_{D}\equiv\int_{\text{IG}}d_{\mathrm{L}}y\,\overline{D(y)}\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{y},

(267)

which generalized the (one-parameter) group property considerably.
In this way, similar to how the IG has the universal property of lifting the selective time-ordered exponentials off of the system Hilbert space,

K[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}]=K_{\gamma[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}]},

(268)

the IGA has the universal property of lifting the time-dependence of the quantum channel evolution off of the system Hilbert space,

\mathcal{Z}^{(\lambda)}_{t}=\mathcal{Z}^{(\lambda)}_{D_{t}},

(269)

where the
\mathcal{Z}^{(\lambda)}_{t}
on the left denotes the solution to the Lindblad equation and the
\mathcal{Z}^{(\lambda)}_{D}
on the right denotes the representation of the KOD,
D\in\text{IGA}
.
In this way, every quantum channel superoperator becomes the noncommutative analog of a Laplace transform, evaluated at a particular irrep,

\mathcal{Z}:D(x)\longmapsto\mathcal{Z}^{(\lambda)}_{D}.

(270)

In addition to the Kolmogrov generator, further operators on the IGA, generally called ultraoperators, were also considered, allowing the analysis to be completely autonomous from the superoperators that originally represented the measuring process.
Perhaps the most fundamental of the ultraoperators were the right-invariant derivatives and left-translations, denoted
\underleftarrow{X}
and
\mathcal{L}_{x}
.
With these, the left-convolution ultraoperators could be constructed in direct analogy with the quantum channel superoperators,

\mathfrak{Z}^{\text{\Ankh}}_{D_{1}}\circ\mathfrak{Z}^{\text{\Ankh}}_{D_{0}}=\mathfrak{Z}^{\text{\Ankh}}_{D_{1}*D_{0}}\hskip 25.0pt\text{and}\hskip 25.0pt\mathfrak{Z}^{\text{\Ankh}}_{D}\equiv\int_{\text{IG}}d_{\mathrm{L}}y\,D(y)\mathfrak{L}_{y}.

(271)

Unlike the instrument elements
\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{x}
which are not generally trace-preserving, the selective left-translation ultraoperators
\mathfrak{L}_{x}
are always norm-preserving and therefore the left-convolution ultraoperators
\mathfrak{Z}^{\text{\Ankh}}_{D}

Perhaps the most important point about the IG is that because it is universal, it can be considered as a form of memory within the measuring instrument, making it a kind of automaton.
If the IG is principal or automatic, then the measuring process can be simulated with a classical automaton.
This will allow the IMP to take an enormous step:
From the perspective of the IG, it has become clear that the actual reason why a dissipative channel superoperator satisfies the group property is not exactly because the measuring process is Markov.
Rather, it is because in addition, the Markov kernel of the measuring process is right-invariant.

To break right-invariance would mean that the instrument would start to make changes based on its memory — that is, adapt.
If these adaptations are only based on the IG position at that moment in time, then although the time-dependence of the quantum channel superoperator would cease to be Markovian, the instrument evolution would still remain Markov!
From a technical point of view, what this is saying is that if the instrument were to adapt based on the current IG position, then although the Linblad master equation would cease to exist, there would still be a KOD Kolmogorov equation, but now with a Kolmogorov generator that depends on the IG position,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\kappa}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}D_{t}(x)=\mathfrak{D}_{x}^{\text{\Ankh}}[D_{t}](x)!

(276)

Having said all of this, it is crucial to return to the other primary tool of the IMP that was virtually abandoned at the beginning of this paper, the so-called
modified Maurer-Cartan stochastic differential
or MMCSD.
The MMCSD is precisely what an automated measuring instrument would use to update its IG position as it continues to observe.
Further, such automation is only possible if the IG is automatable — that is, itself “automatic” (section
III.1
).

It is therefore quite remarkable that the KOD, its Kolmogorov equation, the IGA, and the ultraoperator formalism, all still manage to exist, regardless of whether or not the IG was automatic to begin with.
Nevertheless, the fundamental interest is still with IGs that are automatic, especially IGs that are universally automatic such for ISM and SPQM
Jackson and Caves 2022
;
Jackson and Caves 2023a
.
Something about this seems to be exactly why classical and generalized phase-space correspondences define realistic, tomographically complete POVMs and also why the theory of quasiprobability manages to provide a completely autonomous, alternaive theory of quantum mechanics for systems such as those with spin or quadrature.

Currently under preparation is a paper that returns to the original, most fundamental of continuous measurements: the indirect single-mode photodetector
Srinivas and Davies 1981
and heterodyne instrument
Goetsch and Graham 1994
;
Wiseman 1994
.
Indeed, these measurements have much simpler IGs than the ones that occur for ISM and SPQM.
By treating them systematically with these more general (but sophisticated) tools (the KOD and the MMCSD), perhaps it will become easier for more scientists to recognize the significance of the IMP.

Appendix A
System-Meter-Interaction Models

The Kraus operators of an instrument can be thought of as the back-actions on the system which are due to an indirect measurement where a meter is strongly measured after it has interacted with the system.
More precisely, a system-meter-interaction model consists of a meter with an pointer state that is measured after interacting with the system of interest.
Because we are interested in weak and continuous measurements, the meter will be modeled as a continuous variable, but one could also use qubits and it would make no difference because the interactions are weak.
(For a treatment with qubit meters, recommended sources are
Strandberg et al.
;
Gross et al. 2018
;
Wiseman 1994
.)
On the other hand, the system is treated generally.

The meter is considered to be a mode of the electromagnetic field, modeled by a standard continuous variable consisting of a pointer in an initial pure state
|0\rangle_{\text{M}}
with quadrature observables
Q_{\text{M}}
and
P_{\text{M}}
, satisfying the canonical commutation relation

[Q_{\text{M}},P_{\text{M}}]=i\hbar 1_{\text{M}}.

(277)

Let
\langle q|_{\text{M}}
be the basis of position quadrature functionals, upon which the actions of the quadrature observables are

\displaystyle\langle q|_{\text{M}}Q_{\text{M}}=q\langle q|_{\text{M}}\hskip 50.0pt\text{and}\hskip 50.0pt\langle q|_{\text{M}}P_{\text{M}}=-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial q}\langle q|_{\text{M}}.

(278)

The initial pointer state is assumed to be vacuum, with Gaussian wavefunction

\displaystyle\sqrt{dq}\,\psi_{\text{M}}(q)

\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{dq}\left\langle q|0\right\rangle_{\text{M}}

(279)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{dq}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}e^{-q^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}}

(280)

and annihilator

a_{\text{M}}=\frac{1}{2\sigma}Q_{\text{M}}+i\frac{\sigma}{\hbar}P_{\text{M}}

(281)

defined by the property

a_{\text{M}}|0\rangle_{\!\text{M}}=0.

(282)

The initial pointer then interacts with the system of interest according to a unitary transformation

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\text{int}}

\displaystyle=e^{-i\sqrt{\kappa dt}\left(X_{\text{S}}\otimes 2\frac{\sigma}{\hbar}P_{\text{M}}-Y_{\text{S}}\otimes\frac{1}{\sigma}Q_{\text{M}}\right)}

(283)

which can be interpreted as the system observables
X_{\text{S}}
and
Y_{\text{S}}
driving a translation (change in position) and a boost (change in momentum) in the meter, considered to occur for a time
dt
at a rate
\kappa
.
The two system observables can be put together to define a Lindblad operator

\displaystyle L_{\text{S}}=X_{\text{S}}+iY_{\text{S}}

(284)

with which the interaction unitary affords the alternative expression

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\text{int}}=e^{\sqrt{\kappa dt}\left(L_{\text{S}}\otimes a_{\text{M}}^{\dagger}-L_{\text{S}}^{\dagger}\otimes a_{\text{M}}\right)}.

(285)

This expression may then be processed into a form which will clarify the action of the system-meter unitary on the initial pointer state:

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\text{int}}

\displaystyle=e^{\sqrt{\kappa dt}(L_{\text{S}}\otimes a_{\text{M}}^{\dagger}-L_{\text{S}}^{\dagger}\otimes a_{\text{M}})}

(286)

\displaystyle=e^{\sqrt{\kappa dt}L_{\text{S}}\otimes a_{\text{M}}^{\dagger}}e^{-\sqrt{\kappa dt}L_{\text{S}}^{\dagger}\otimes a_{\text{M}}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\kappa dt[L_{\text{S}}\otimes a_{\text{M}}^{\dagger},-L_{\text{S}}^{\dagger}\otimes a_{\text{M}}]}

(287)

\displaystyle=e^{\sqrt{\kappa dt}L_{\text{S}}\otimes a_{\text{M}}^{\dagger}}e^{-\sqrt{\kappa dt}L_{\text{S}}^{\dagger}\otimes a_{\text{M}}}e^{\frac{1}{2}\kappa dt\left([L_{\text{S}},L_{\text{S}}^{\dagger}]\otimes a_{\text{M}}^{\dagger}a_{\text{M}}+L_{\text{S}}^{\dagger}L_{\text{S}}\otimes[a_{\text{M}}^{\dagger},a_{\text{M}}]\right)}

(288)

\displaystyle=e^{\sqrt{\kappa dt}L_{\text{S}}\otimes a_{\text{M}}^{\dagger}}e^{-\sqrt{\kappa dt}L_{\text{S}}^{\dagger}\otimes a_{\text{M}}}e^{\frac{1}{2}\kappa dt[L_{\text{S}},L_{\text{S}}^{\dagger}]\otimes a_{\text{M}}^{\dagger}a_{\text{M}}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\kappa dtL_{\text{S}}^{\dagger}L_{\text{S}}\otimes 1_{M}}.

(289)

Acting on the initial pointer state,

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\text{int}}|0\rangle_{\!\text{M}}

\displaystyle=e^{\sqrt{\kappa dt}L_{\text{S}}\otimes a_{\text{M}}^{\dagger}}|0\rangle_{\!\text{M}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\kappa dtL_{\text{S}}^{\dagger}L_{\text{S}}}.

(290)

X_{\text{S}}
and
Y_{\text{S}}

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{d(dW)}{\sqrt{2\pi dt}}e^{-\frac{dW^{2}}{2dt}}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\kappa dt(L_{\text{S}}^{\dagger}L_{\text{S}}+L_{\text{S}}^{2})}e^{L_{\text{S}}\sqrt{\kappa}dW}

\frac{1}{2}\{X_{\text{S}},Y_{\text{S}}\}

Appendix B
Invariant One-Forms, Haar Measures, and the Modular Function

Every finite-dimensional Lie group has two vector-valued one-forms called the so-called Maurer-Cartan (MC) forms.
The left-invariant MC form is

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{L}}(x)

\displaystyle\equiv x^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}dx

(317)

\displaystyle=x^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}g^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}gdx

(318)

\displaystyle=(gx)^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}d(gx)

(319)

\displaystyle=\omega_{\mathrm{L}}(gx)

(320)

and the right-invariant MC form is

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{R}}(x)

\displaystyle\equiv dx\,x^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}

(321)

\displaystyle=dx\,gg^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}x^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}

(322)

\displaystyle=d(xg)\,(xg)^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}

(323)

\displaystyle=\omega_{\mathrm{R}}(xg).

(324)

Fixing a basis
\{X_{\alpha}\}
of the Lie algebra, the differential may be expanded in terms of either their left- or right-invariant derivatives

\displaystyle dx

\displaystyle=\theta_{\mathrm{L}}^{\mu}(x)\underrightarrow{X_{\alpha}}[x]

(325)

\displaystyle=\theta_{\mathrm{L}}^{\mu}(x)xX_{\alpha}

(326)

\displaystyle=\theta_{\mathrm{R}}^{\mu}(x)\underleftarrow{X_{\alpha}}[x]

(327)

\displaystyle=\theta_{\mathrm{R}}^{\mu}(x)X_{\alpha}x

(328)

which means that the left- and right-invariant MC forms can be expanded as

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{L}}(x)=X_{\alpha}\theta^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{L}}(x)\hskip 50.0pt\text{and}\hskip 50.0pt\omega_{\mathrm{R}}(x)=X_{\alpha}\theta^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{R}}(x).

(329)

The one-forms
\theta^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{L}}(x)
and
\theta^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{R}}(x)
, dual to the left- and right-invariant derivatives are also left- and right-invariant respectively,

\displaystyle\theta^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{L}}(gx)=\theta^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{L}}(x)\hskip 50.0pt\text{and}\hskip 50.0pt\theta^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{R}}(xg)=\theta^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{R}}(x).

(330)

These one-forms are examples of what are more generally called solder forms.
The right-invariant MC form is related to the left-invariant MC form by conjugation

\omega_{\mathrm{R}}=x\omega_{\mathrm{L}}x^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}

(331)

which in turn implies the right-invariant solder forms can be expanded simply in terms of the left-invariant solder forms

\theta^{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}(x)={(\mathrm{Ad}_{x})^{\beta}}_{\alpha}\theta^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{L}}(x)

(332)

where
\mathrm{Ad}_{x}
denotes the adjoint representation

xX_{\alpha}x^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}=X_{\beta}{(\mathrm{Ad}_{x})^{\beta}}_{\alpha}.

(333)

The
left-
and
right-invariant Haar measures
are therefore

d_{\mathrm{L}}x\equiv C|\theta^{1}_{\mathrm{L}}(x)\wedge\cdots\wedge\theta^{n}_{\mathrm{L}}(x)|

(334)

and

d_{\mathrm{R}}x\equiv C|\theta^{1}_{\mathrm{R}}(x)\wedge\cdots\wedge\theta^{n}_{\mathrm{R}}(x)|

(335)

where
\wedge
denotes the wedge product,
|\;|
denotes taking an absolute value of the Jacobian determinant, and
C
is an arbitary normalization constant (chosen to be the same for both Haar measures.)
From equation
332
it is clear

d_{\mathrm{R}}x=\Delta(x)d_{\mathrm{L}}x

(336)

where defined is the
modular function

\Delta(x)\equiv|\det\mathrm{Ad}_{x}|.

(337)

Clearly, the modular function is also a representation of the group

\Delta(xy)=\Delta(x)\Delta(y).

(338)

The group is said to be
unimodular
if the modular function is identically equal to one.
Compact groups, Abelian groups, and nilpotent groups are unimodular.

With equation
336
, it is easy to see the Haar measures are quasi-invariant with respect to their opposite translation,

d_{\mathrm{R}}(gx)=\Delta(g)d_{\mathrm{R}}x\hskip 50.0pt\text{and}\hskip 50.0ptd_{\mathrm{L}}(xg)=\frac{d_{\mathrm{L}}x}{\Delta(g)}.

Some further properties of the Haar measures and the group convolution are discussed in Appendix

Appendix C
The
\delta
-Function of a Group

Let
G
be a finite-dimensional Lie group with left- and right-invariant Haar measures
d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,
and
d_{\mathrm{R}}x\,
as defined in section
IV.1
.

The
Dirac delta-distribution

\delta(x)
of a group
G
will first be defined with respect to the left-invariant measure to be the
unique
distribution with the property

\int_{G}d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,\delta(x)f(x)=f(e)

(340)

for all functions
f
over
G
, where
e\in G
denotes the identity group element.
It follows that the
\delta
has the same property with respect to the right-invariant measure as well,

\displaystyle\int_{G}d_{\mathrm{R}}x\,\delta(x)f(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{G}d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,\delta(x)\Delta(x)f(x)

(341)

\displaystyle=\Delta(e)f(e)

(342)

\displaystyle=f(e).

(343)

With these properties, we can imagine the
\delta
as a certain “function” sharply peaked at the identity.
Inverting at the identity reveals

\displaystyle\int_{G}d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,\delta(x^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})f(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{G}d_{\mathrm{L}}(\xi^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})\delta(\xi)f(\xi^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})

(344)

\displaystyle=\int_{G}d_{\mathrm{R}}\xi\,\delta(\xi)f(\xi^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})

(345)

\displaystyle=f(e^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})

(346)

\displaystyle=f(e)

(347)

and so it follows that the delta is symmetric under inversion,

\delta(x^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})=\delta(x).

(348)

Conjugating at the identity reveals

\displaystyle\int_{G}d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,\delta(gxg^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})f(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{G}d_{\mathrm{L}}(g^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}\xi g)\delta(\xi)f(g^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}\xi g)

(349)

\displaystyle=\int_{G}d_{\mathrm{L}}\xi\delta(\xi)\frac{f(g^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}\xi g)}{\Delta(g)}

(350)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(g^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}eg)}{\Delta(g)}

(351)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(e)}{\Delta(g)}

(352)

and so it follows that the delta is only quasi-invariant under conjugation if the group is not unimodular

\delta(gxg^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})=\frac{\delta(x)}{\Delta(g)}.

(353)

In light of this, it is also interesting to observe the Haar measures are equally quasi-invariant under conjugation

d_{\mathrm{R}}(gxg^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})=\Delta(g)d_{\mathrm{R}}x\,\hskip 50.0pt\text{and}\hskip 50.0ptd_{\mathrm{L}}(gxg^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})=\Delta(g)d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,.

(354)

The
\delta
can also be translated appropriately to evaluate the function at any other point,

\displaystyle\int_{G}d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,\delta(x_{0}^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}x)f(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{G}d_{\mathrm{L}}(x_{0}\xi)\delta(\xi)f(x_{0}\xi)

(355)

\displaystyle=\int_{G}d_{\mathrm{L}}\xi\,\delta(\xi)f(x_{0}\xi)

(356)

\displaystyle=f(x_{0}e)

(357)

\displaystyle=f(x_{0})

(358)

and similarly

\displaystyle\int_{G}d_{\mathrm{R}}x\,\delta(xx_{0}^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})f(x)

\displaystyle=f(x_{0}).

(359)

The
\delta
can also be considered as a trikernal of the group convolution,

\displaystyle g*f(z)

\displaystyle\equiv\int d_{\mathrm{L}}y\,d_{\mathrm{R}}x\,\delta\!\left(y^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}zx^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}\right)g(y)f(x)

(360)

\displaystyle=\int d_{\mathrm{L}}y\,d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,\delta\!\left((yx)^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}z\right)g(y)f(x)

(361)

\displaystyle=\int d_{\mathrm{R}}y\,d_{\mathrm{R}}x\,\delta\!\left(z(yx)^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}\right)g(y)f(x).

(362)
