Left ventricular longitudinal function and the hyperkinetic profile: an old echo world to rediscover?
Authors: José Carlos Gasca-Aldama, Karen Harumi López-Rodríguez, Osvaldo Alexis Marché-Fernández, Mario Arturo Carrasco-Flores, Juan Ángel Morales-Ferrer, Marcos Vidals-Sánchez, Eduardo Alberto Gonzáles-Escudero, José Ernesto Juárez-León, Enrique Monares-Zepeda, Arturo Arzate-Ramírez
Published: 2026

To the Editor

We read with interest the study by Cavefors et al. [1] on the feasibility and prognosis of left ventricular (LV) longitudinal systolic function over conventional ejection fraction (LVEF) in critically ill patients. We also commend Gonzalez et al. [2] for highlighting the hyperdynamic LV phenotype (LVEF ≥ 70%) as a maladaptive signature of vasoplegic shock. While Sanfilippo et al. [3] established that global longitudinal strain (GLS) is a powerful, independent predictor of mortality in septic shock where LVEF fails, translating 2D-speckle tracking echocardiography (2D-STE) into a routine bedside tool faces a massive feasibility gap. We believe crucial metrological, pharmacological, and biventricular limitations must be addressed before validating GLS as the universal standard.

First, a subtle methodological asymmetry in the study design of Cavefors et al. warrants scrutiny. The authors averaged MAPSE and tissue Doppler-derived systolic velocity (S’) over three to five cardiac cycles to reduce respiratory and cycle-to-cycle variability but analyzed GLS and Simpson’s LVEF using a single cycle. In patients exhibiting tachycardia, arrhythmias, and patient-ventilator interactions, this metrological discrepancy artificially penalizes GLS precision and feasibility (only 65%). Remarkably, MAPSE demonstrated a robust 90% feasibility and outperformed GLS in Cavefors’ multivariable analysis, showing a stronger independent association with 90-day mortality (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 1.17; 95% CI: 1.06–1.30; p = 0.002 per mm decrease, vs. aOR 1.08; 95% CI: 1.00–1.16; p = 0.048 for GLS). This statistical superiority of a 1D displacement metric over complex 2D software highlights a profound clinical paradox.

Second, while Gonzalez et al. [2] and recently He and Huang [4] discuss the load-dependency of longitudinal markers and translating prognostic association into clinical utility, loading conditions represent absolute confounding factors of contractility assessments. Vasopressor-induced changes in systemic vascular resistance alter left ventricular afterload. This clinical consequence is demonstrated by Dugar et al. [5] in 129 patients with septic shock receiving adjunctive vasopressin. Patients with pre-existing LV dysfunction (LVEF < 45% before vasopressin) showed lower positive hemodynamic responses (37% vs. 61%) and higher 28-day mortality (HR 2.24; 95% CI: 1.08–4.64). Because vasopressin is a vasoconstrictor lacking β1-inotropic activity, its initiation in ventricles without contractile reserve triggers afterload mismatch, decreasing cardiac output. Crucially, Dugar et al. [5] did not analyze advanced deformation or simpler bedside metrics, such as left ventricular GLS or M-mode parameters (fractional shortening [FS], MAPSE, and LV-LWFS), underscoring a major clinical gap: we need highly feasible, bedside metrics to assess contractile reserve before altering loading conditions.

Third, while Gonzalez et al. focus on isolating left ventricular performance, they overlook the reality of biventricular interdependence. As Bowcock et al. [6] demonstrated in sepsis, isolated assessment of right ventricular (RV) metrics is heavily confounded by LV systolic depression due to mechanical ventricular interdependence, whereas pulmonary arterial coupling (TAPSE/PASP ratio) remains independent. An unaddressed analysis of Cavefors et al.’s supplementary cohort data reveals that their cohort exhibited severe biventricular impairment. Patients with normal LV function (MAPSE > 10 mm) had an optimal estimated TAPSE/PASP ratio of 0.73 mm/mmHg (CVP 7 mmHg, TRV 2.4 m/s), whereas those with impaired MAPSE (< 10 mm) demonstrated a collapsed ratio of 0.41 mm/mmHg (CVP 10 mmHg, TRV 2.8 m/s). This exactly crosses the critical mortality threshold of < 0.50 mm/mmHg established by Zhang et al. [7] in septic shock. This tight correlation (Table 1) proves that LV longitudinal impairment and RV-PA uncoupling are clinically inseparable; a single M-mode sweep can characterize biventricular failure without tracking limitations.

Fourth, while we agree that the hyperdynamic LV profile (LVEF ≥ 70%) is a critical marker of high mortality associated with a U-shaped prognosis curve in sepsis [2, 5], we emphasize that “strain” is a physical concept of fractional change in length (ΔL/L0), not a proprietary 2D-STE software technique [8, 9]. Traditional M-mode fractional shortening (FS), evaluated in septic shock by Gutiérrez de la Cruz et al. [10] in the parasternal long-axis (PLAX) view, is a surrogate of radial deformation. Conversely, M-mode mitral annular plane systolic excursion (MAPSE) normalized to LV length in the apical four-chamber view (A4C) yields left ventricular longitudinal wall fractional shortening (LV-LWFS)—a reproducible 1D surrogate of global longitudinal strain [11, 12].

Fifth, why do Gonzalez et al. find the Cavefors dataset so compelling? Because in Cavefors’ mixed ICU cohort, 77% of patients presented with a preserved LVEF (≥ 50%). Indeed, Gonzalez et al. suggest that GLS can unmask subclinical myocardial dysfunction in hyperkinetic profiles. However, this optimistic view must be balanced against the physical limitations of 2D-STE under hyperdynamic and tachycardic conditions. Sepsis accounted for only 31.3% (118 patients) of Cavefors’ cohort. Although Cavefors et al. reported a mean LVEF of 57 ± 13% in this subgroup (with only 22% having LVEF < 50%), specific heart rates and local GLS feasibility remain unreported. Crucially, their supplementary registry data reveals that the mean heart rate was only 80 ± 19 bpm in patients with preserved GLS ( ≤ −16%) and 88 ± 24 bpm in those with impaired GLS (> −16%). While these modest, non-tachycardic heart rates are easily tracked, severe tachycardia is almost universal in early-stage septic shock. This modest heart rate profile explains why Cavefors et al. did not encounter severe tracking limitations but heavily restricts the generalizability of their feasibility findings to hyperdynamic septic shock where tachycardia represents a constant clinical barrier.

Standard GLS has limited feasibility—Johansson Blixt et al. [11] found that only 21% of septic shock patients had apical views suitable for analysis—due to poor acoustic windows (ventilation, high PEEP, dressings). Furthermore, standard 2D-STE (40–80 fps) under septic tachycardia (≥ 120–150 bpm) suffers from speckle decorrelation, degrading tracking accuracy and leading to data dropout [13]. As Sanfilippo himself prophetically warned in his septic-specific meta-analysis [3], this frame-rate-to-heart-rate mismatch represents a critical bottleneck of 2D-STE in hyperdynamic, tachycardic cohorts.

While newer techniques demand cardiac frequency data to avoid tracking errors, simple M-mode fractional shortening (radial PLAX FS and longitudinal LV-LWFS) bypasses this limitation entirely. M-mode’s extraordinary temporal resolution (1000 Hz) captures dimensions with 1-millisecond precision. While longitudinal LV-LWFS is associated with myocardial injury in septic shock [12], Gutiérrez de la Cruz et al. [10] demonstrated that a persistent hyperkinetic radial FS (> 47.8%, ROC-derived, AUROC 0.84) at 48 h is a powerful, independent predictor of 21-day mortality (HR 4.77; 95% CI: 24.2–35.0, p = 0.008). This severe prognostic signal confirms that hyperkinesia represents a maladaptive vasomotor collapse driven by profound vasoplegia.

In conclusion, proposing simpler, highly feasible longitudinal displacement metrics (MAPSE, LV-LWFS) and robust radial surrogates (M-mode PLAX FS)—leveraging M-mode’s superior temporal resolution—under prevailing loading conditions provides a more reliable, bedside-reproducible, and risk-stratified approach, particularly in hyperdynamic septic shock.
