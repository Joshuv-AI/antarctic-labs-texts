Comparative chloroplast genomics of Teucrium species reveals genome evolution, phylogenetic relationships, and candidate molecular markers
Authors: Fatemeh Alsadat Hejazi, Parisa Mohammadi, Aboozar Soorni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The genus Teucrium (Lamiaceae) comprises approximately 250 species with significant medicinal and ecological importance. Despite extensive phytochemical studies, molecular data, particularly on chloroplast (cp.) genomes, remain limited for many species, especially those native to Iran. In this study, we sequenced and analyzed the complete cp. genomes of three Iranian Teucrium species, T. chamaedrys, T. flavum, and T. polium, to investigate genome structure, variation, and phylogenetic relationships within the genus. Using high-throughput sequencing, we assembled and annotated the cp. genomes, revealing typical quadripartite structures with sizes ranging from 149,913 bp to 150,026 bp. Comparative genomic analyses, including IR boundary shifts, codon usage, repeat elements, and nucleotide diversity, highlighted structural variations. Sequence divergence analysis using mVISTA revealed several genes, including rpoC2, ycf1, ycf2, ndhF, rpl32, rps16, and accD, displayed notable sequence variation, making them potential phylogenetic markers. Additionally, highly variable intergenic regions, such as trnH-GUG-psbA, rps16-trnQ-UUG, and petG-trnW-CCA, were identified as potential hotspots for species differentiation. Codon usage analysis revealed a strong preference for A/U-ending codons, while nucleotide diversity (Pi) analysis identified highly variable intergenic regions such as trnH-GUG-psbA and rpl32-trnL-UAG. Phylogenetic reconstruction based on cp. genome sequences placed T. chamaedrys, T. flavum, and T. polium in well-supported clades, clarifying evolutionary relationships within Teucrium. This study provides the first complete cp. genome sequences for these Iranian Teucrium species, expanding genomic resources and offering valuable insights into their genetic diversity, evolutionary history, and medicinal potential.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29339-x.

Introduction

The genus Teucrium (Lamiaceae: Ajugoideae), comprising approximately 250 species, represents a taxonomically complex and ecologically significant lineage. As the second-largest genus within its subfamily, it exhibits a near-cosmopolitan distribution. Phylogenetic studies reveal its paraphyletic status due to the inclusion of Teucridium, Spartothamnella, and Oncinocalyx, with Rubiteucris and Schnabelia identified as sister genera. However, traditional classifications are incongruent with molecular evidence, which highlights calyx morphology as a key diagnostic trait for clade differentiation1,2. Previous research indicates karyotypic evolution within Teucrium is marked by recurrent ploidy shifts in at least two lineages. The genus diversified during the middle to late Miocene, driven by climatic fluctuations and adaptations to Mediterranean aridification, facilitating speciation and dispersal2–7.

Teucrium species are of multidisciplinary interest due to their ecological roles, medicinal applications, and chemically diverse essential oils. These oils, rich in bioactive sesquiterpene hydrocarbons and oxygenated monoterpenes, demonstrate antimicrobial, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory properties8,9. Comparative analyses of T. chamaedrys populations in northern Iran reveal spatial variation in oil composition, with major constituents including α-cadinene and Z-β-farnesene10. Similarly, studies on T. arduini across the Balkans identified two chemotypes: a sesquiterpene-dominated profile (β-caryophyllene, germacrene D) and an oxygenated monoterpene-rich type (pulegone, piperitone oxide), correlating strongly with geographical distribution rather than genetic or morphological divergence11. This chemotypic variability underscores environmental influences on secondary metabolism and the genus’s potential for biotechnological exploitation. Besides, other species of the Teucrium genus, such as T. hircanicum and T. flavum, have been investigated for their essential oil compositions, revealing a rich source of volatile compounds12,13.

Despite the chemical and biochemical data available for these species, molecular information, particularly cp. genomes, remains limited or absent for many of them. Chloroplasts are essential organelles within plant cells, playing a pivotal role in photosynthesis by converting light energy into chemical energy, a process critical for plant growth and development14,15. The cp. genome is a circular, multicopy DNA molecule characterized by a highly conserved quadripartite structure. This structure comprises two inverted repeat (IR) regions (IRa and IRb), which flank a large single-copy (LSC) region and a small single-copy (SSC) region16–18. The cp. genome encodes over 120 genes, which are integral to essential cellular processes such as transcription, translation, photosynthesis, and the biosynthesis of amino acids and fatty acids19. Variations in the size of cp. genomes among species are primarily attributed to the expansion or contraction of the IR regions20. Owing to its uniparental inheritance, low mutation rate, and high conservation, the cp. genome has become a powerful tool for species identification, phylogenetic analysis, and genetic engineering21–25. Specific cpDNA sequences, such as psbA-trnH, matK, and rbcL, are widely utilized as DNA barcodes for species identification. Moreover, the complete cp. genome is increasingly recognized as a “super barcode” for resolving phylogenetic relationships at lower taxonomic levels, offering higher resolution and accuracy26–31. Beyond its applications in phylogenetics, the cp. genome is also employed in genetic transformation and molecular breeding, demonstrating significant potential for improving medicinal and agricultural traits19.

This highlights the importance of expanding research efforts to generate and analyze cp. genome data, which could provide valuable insights into species identification, evolutionary relationships, and the development of genetically enhanced crops with improved traits. In this regard, a limited amount of research has been conducted on cp. genome sequencing of the genus Teucrium, with only seven species currently available in the NCBI database. Khan et al. (2019) sequenced the cp. genomes of T. stocksianum subsp. stenophyllum (150,087 bp), T. stocksianum subsp. stocksianum (150,076 bp), and T. mascatense (150,499 bp), revealing a typical quadripartite structure and variations in inverted repeat (IR) expansions, repeat content, and simple sequence repeats (SSRs). Comparative analyses highlighted high synteny among Teucrium cp. genomes, with notable divergence in coding regions and IR junctions32. Gong (2022) further reported the cp. genome of T. fruticans (150,808 bp), encoding 130 unique genes and phylogenetically clustering with other Teucrium species33.

Despite these advancements, the cp. genomes of several Teucrium species, especially those native to Iran, have not yet been sequenced, leaving a significant gap in our understanding of their genomic architecture and evolutionary relationships. For instance, species such as T. chamaedrys, T. flavum, and T. polium lack complete cp. genome data, which hinders our understanding of their genetic architecture, evolutionary history, and potential for biotechnological applications. Addressing this knowledge gap is essential for advancing research on Teucrium and harnessing its genetic resources for medicinal, agricultural, and conservation purposes. To bridge this gap, we sequenced the complete cp. genomes of three Iranian native Teucrium species, T. chamaedrys, T. flavum, and T. polium, and conducted comprehensive analyses, including genome assembly, annotation, and structural characterization. Comparative genomic analyses were performed to identify conserved and variable regions, such as IRs, LSC, and SSC regions, as well as hypervariable regions that are valuable for species identification and phylogenetic studies. Additionally, we investigated repetitive sequences, codon usage patterns, and RNA editing sites to gain insights into the evolutionary dynamics and functional adaptations of these genomes. Phylogenetic analyses were carried out to elucidate the evolutionary relationships among these species and other members of the Teucrium genus. This study provides foundational genomic data that will facilitate future research on the genetic diversity, conservation, and biotechnological potential of Iranian native Teucrium species. By expanding the availability of cp. genome data, this work contributes to a deeper understanding of the evolutionary biology and functional genomics of this economically and medicinally significant genus.

Results

Features of the Teucrium cp. genomes

The cp. genomes of three Teucrium species, T. chamaedrys, T. flavum, and T. polium, were analyzed, revealing highly conserved structural and genomic features. The cp. genome sizes were nearly identical, with T. chamaedrys at 149,923 bp, T. flavum at 149,913 bp, and T. polium at 150,026 bp (Fig. 1). All three species exhibited the typical quadripartite structure of cp. genomes, consisting of a pair of IR regions, a LSC region, and a SSC region. The IR regions ranged from 25,341 bp (T. flavum) to 25,446 bp (T. polium), while the LSC regions varied slightly from 92,327 bp (T. flavum) to 92,686 bp (T. polium). The SSC regions were highly conserved, with sizes of 17,007 bp (T. chamaedrys), 17,012 bp (T. flavum), and 17,032 bp (T. polium). All three species shared identical gene compositions, with a total of 134 genes, including 89 protein-coding genes, 36 transfer RNA (tRNA) genes, and 8 ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes. This consistency in gene content and structure highlights the high degree of genomic conservation across the Teucrium species.

The cp. genomes of T. chamaedrys, T. flavum, and T. polium exhibited a highly conserved set of protein-coding genes and functional elements. All three species shared identical gene content, including five genes for Photosystem I (psaA, psaB, psaC, psaI, psaJ), fifteen genes for Photosystem II (psbA, psbB, psbC, psbD, psbE, psbF, psbI, psbJ, psbK, psbL, psbM, psbN, psbT, psbZ), and six genes encoding subunits of ATP synthase (atpA, atpB, atpE, atpF, atpH, atpI). Additionally, genes for NADPH dehydrogenase (ndhA, ndhB, ndhC, ndhD, ndhE, ndhF, ndhG, ndhH, ndhI, ndhJ, ndhK), cytochrome b/f complex (petA, petB, petD, petG, petL, petN), and the large subunit of Rubisco (rbcL) were consistently present. Ribosomal RNA genes (rrn16S, rrn23S, rrn4.5 S, rrn5S) and a comprehensive set of transfer RNA genes were also conserved across the species.

Furthermore, genes encoding essential functions such as acetyl-CoA carboxylase (accD), c-type cytochrome synthesis (ccsA), envelope membrane protein (cemA), protease (clpP), maturase (matK), and translation initiation factor (infA) were identified in all three genomes. Hypothetical reading frames (ycf1, ycf2, ycf3, ycf4, ycf15) were also conserved. The high degree of gene conservation across the cp. genomes of T. chamaedrys, T. flavum, and T. polium underscores the stability of cp. genomic architecture within the Teucrium genus.

The characterization of cp. genomes in three Teucrium species also revealed the presence of both cis-splicing and trans-splicing genes. A total of 10 protein-coding genes, including accD, clpD, petD, rpl2, ndhB, ndhA, rps16, atpF, rpoC1, and ycf3 were identified as cis-splicing genes, each containing 1–2 introns in all three species (Fig S1-S3). The positions of these genes and their intron-exon boundaries were conserved across species. Additionally, the rps12 gene was observed to undergo trans-splicing in all three species examined (Fig. 2), with its exons distributed across distinct regions of cp. genome. The 5′-end exon was identified within the LSC region, while the 3′-end exon was located in the IR regions. This spatial distribution of exons highlights the complex organization and processing of the rps12 gene within the cp. genome.

Codon usage analysis

The codon usage patterns of three Teucrium species (T. chamaedrys, T. flavum, and T. polium) were analyzed, revealing both conserved and species-specific trends (Fig S4). The total number of codons ranged from 20,189 in T. polium to 20,197 in T. flavum, indicating minimal variation in coding sequence length among the species. Leucine (Leu) was the most frequently encoded amino acid across all species, with the codon UUA showing the highest RSCU values (ranging from 1.91 to 1.92). In contrast, cysteine (Cys) was among the least frequent, with UGU being the predominant codon (RSCU values ranging from 1.13 to 1.14). The standard initiation codon AUG (Met) was universally conserved across all species, with an RSCU value of 1.0. Stop codon usage also exhibited some variation, with UAA being the most frequently used stop codon in all species (RSCU values ranging from 1.19 to 1.21), while UGA showed slightly elevated usage in T. flavum and T. polium (RSCU = 1.10) compared to T. chamaedrys (RSCU = 1.08). Approximately half of the codons (32 out of 64) showed a usage bias (RSCU > 1.0), with a strong preference for codons ending in adenine (A) or uracil (U). For example, the codons AGA (Arg), GGA (Gly), and UCU (Ser) consistently exhibited high RSCU values across all species, indicating a strong preference for these codons. In contrast, codons such as CGC (Arg) and GGC (Gly) were less preferred, with RSCU values consistently below 1.0. The synonymous codon usage analysis revealed a pronounced bias toward codons ending with A or U at the third position, consistent with the general trend observed in cp. genomes of higher plants. For instance, the codons UUU (Phe), AUU (Ile), and GUU (Val) were highly preferred, with RSCU values exceeding 1.3 in all species. Conversely, codons ending with cytosine (C) or guanine (G), such as UUC (Phe), AUC (Ile), and GUC (Val), were less frequently used, with RSCU values below 1. Among the species, T. chamaedrys displayed slightly distinct codon usage patterns compared to T. flavum and T. polium. For example, the codon UUG (Leu) had a marginally higher RSCU value (1.27) in T. chamaedrys compared to the other two species (1.26). Similarly, the codon CUU (Leu) showed a slightly elevated RSCU value (1.23) in T. chamaedrys, while it ranged between 1.23 and 1.24 in T. flavum and T. polium. These subtle differences suggest potential species-specific adaptations in codon usage, which may reflect evolutionary divergence or environmental adaptations among the Teucrium species.

IR contraction and expansion

Analysis of the boundary regions revealed variations in the length of IR (IRa and IRb) regions among Teucrium species. The newly sequenced cp. genomes exhibited IR lengths of 25,390 bp in T. chamaedrys, 25,341 bp in T. flavum, and 25,446 bp in T. polium. For comparative analysis, these sequences were evaluated alongside previously published cp. genomes, with IR lengths ranging from 22,190 bp in T. fruticans to 25,598 bp in T. stocksianum subsp. stenophyllum. These results indicate a notable IR contraction in T. fruticans, whereas T. stocksianum subsp. stenophyllum displayed a slight expansion. A detailed comparison of IR boundaries at the four junctions (JLB, JSB, JSA, and JLA) between the IR and single-copy regions (LSC and SSC) revealed structural variations (Fig. 3). The rps3 gene, typically located within IRa, shifted 21 bp from IRa into the LSC in T. chamaedrys and T. polium, whereas in T. flavum, it shifted 5 bp from IRa into the LSC. Additionally, in T. chamaedrys and T. polium, rps3 also exhibited a 249 bp shift from IRb into the LSC, whereas in T. flavum, it extended 245 bp into the LSC. The rpl16 gene remained within the LSC but displayed variability in its distance from the IR boundary, particularly in T. fruticans, where it was positioned farther from the junction. The ndhF gene was consistently located at the SSC/IRb boundary in most species, shifting 15 bp into IRb. However, in T. stocksianum subsp. stocksianum, this shift was the most pronounced, extending 38 bp into IRb, indicating a minor IR expansion. In T. fruticans, ndhF extended 10 bp into IRb, while in T. stocksianum subsp. stenophyllum, it was entirely within the SSC, reflecting an IR contraction in this species. The ycf1 gene also spanned the SSC/IRa junction in all species, showing positional variations.

Microsatellite (SSR) and long repeat distribution in Teucrium species

SSR analysis performed on three species of the genus Teucrium revealed a total of 25, 29, and 32 SSRs in T. chamaedrys, T. flavum, and T. polium, respectively. Mononucleotide repeats dominated the SSR profiles, constituting 76% (19/25), 83% (24/29), and 94% (30/32) of the total SSRs in T. chamaedrys, T. flavum, and T. polium, respectively. Dinucleotide repeats were less abundant, with 2, 3, and 2 repeats identified in the three species. Trinucleotide repeats were detected in T. chamaedrys and T. flavum (2 repeats each), while hexanucleotide repeats were exclusively observed in T. chamaedrys (2 repeats). The A/T mononucleotide repeats were the most prevalent, accounting for 68% (17/25), 76% (22/29), and 81% (26/32) of the total SSRs in T. chamaedrys, T. flavum, and T. polium, respectively. Other motifs, including C/G, AT/AT, and AAG/CTT, were also identified but at significantly lower frequencies. Notably, compound microsatellites were detected in all three species, with T. chamaedrys exhibiting the highest number (3 compound SSRs), followed by T. flavum (2) and T. polium (1). Moreover, long repeats in the cp. genomes of Teucrium species were analyzed and classified by type (forward, palindromic, reverse, and complement) and length (30–40 bp, 40–50 bp, and > 50 bp). In T. chamaedrys, 55 long repeats were identified, including 25 forward, 28 palindromic, and 2 reverse with the majority (41 repeats) falling within the 30–40 bp range. Similarly, T. flavum contained 54 long repeats, comprising 23 forward, 27 palindromic, 3 reverse, and 1 complement repeats, of which 42 were 30–40 bp in length. T. polium exhibited the highest number of long repeats (60), with 28 forward, 30 palindromic, and 2 reverse, 37 of these repeats were 30–40 bp in length. Across all three species, palindromic repeats were the most abundant, followed by forward repeats, while reverse and complement repeats were less frequent. Notably, the majority of long repeats (70–75%) were 30–40 bp in length, with fewer repeats in the 40–50 bp and > 50 bp categories. These findings suggest a conserved pattern of long repeat organization in Teucrium cp. genomes, with palindromic and forward repeats likely playing a significant role in genome rearrangement and genetic diversity.

Comparative cp. genomic and Pi analysis

To assess sequence divergence among Teucrium species (T. chamaedrys, T. flavum, T. polium), mVISTA (Fig S5) was used to align their chloroplast genomes with T. mascatense as the reference. The results revealed that LSC and SSC regions exhibited greater sequence variability compared to the more conserved IR regions. Coding regions involved in essential photosynthetic and metabolic functions, such as psbA, matK, rbcL, and atp genes, were highly conserved, underscoring their critical roles in chloroplast function. However, several genes, including rpoC2, ycf1, ycf2, ndhF, rpl32, rps16, and accD, displayed notable sequence variation, making them potential molecular markers for phylogenetic studies. In contrast, non-coding regions, including intergenic spacers (IGS) and introns, displayed significant variability, with several IGS regions identified as highly divergent, such as trnH-GUG-psbA, trnK-UUU-matK, rps16-trnQ-UUG, psbK-psbI, trnS-UGA-psbZ, trnG-GCC-trnM-CAU, and petG-trnW-CCA. These regions exhibited significant sequence variation among Teucrium species, making them potential markers for phylogenetic and evolutionary studies. For instance, the trnH-GUG-psbA spacer, located in the LSC region, showed considerable divergence, consistent with its known use as a DNA barcode in plant phylogenetics. Similarly, the rps16-trnQ-UUG and trnG-GCC-trnM-CAU spacers also displayed notable differences, further emphasizing the importance of non-coding regions in species differentiation within Teucrium.

The analysis of nucleotide diversity across protein-coding genes and IGS regions in the three Teucrium species revealed significant variation in genetic diversity (Fig. 4). Among the genes, trnL-UAG exhibited the highest nucleotide diversity (Pi = 0.025), followed by rpoA (Pi = 0.0190) and rpl20 (Pi = 0.0189). In contrast, genes such as rrn23S (Pi = 0.0012) and ndhB (Pi = 0.0013) showed the lowest levels of diversity, indicating higher conservation. The average nucleotide diversity for genes across the three species was calculated to be 0.0071, reflecting moderate overall genetic variation in coding regions. The IGS regions displayed even greater variability, with trnH-GUG-psbA (Pi = 0.1203) and rps12_1-clpP_3 (Pi = 0.089) exhibiting the highest nucleotide diversity. Other highly diverse IGS regions included ndhD-psaC (Pi = 0.1008) and rpl32-trnL-UAG (Pi = 0.0448). In contrast, regions such as trnV-GAC-rps12_2 (Pi = 0.0021) and rrn5S-trnR-ACG (Pi = 0.0027) were among the most conserved. The average nucleotide diversity for IGS regions was 0.018, which is significantly higher than that of genes, likely due to reduced selective pressure in non-coding regions.

Phylogenetic reconstruction of Teucrium

The phylogenetic analysis of 10 Teucrium species, with A. bracteosa and A. reptans as outgroups, revealed a well-resolved evolutionary relationship among the taxa (Fig. 5). The tree topology indicated strong support for the monophyly of Teucrium, with high bootstrap values (100) at most nodes, reflecting a robust phylogenetic signal in the data. The outgroup species, Ajuga bracteosa and A. reptans, were separated from the Teucrium clade, confirming their distinct evolutionary position relative to the ingroup. Within the Teucrium clade, T. chamaedrys and T. flavum formed a strongly supported sister group (bootstrap value = 100), suggesting a close evolutionary relationship between these two species. The clade contains T. mascatense, T. stocksianum subsp. stenophyllum, and T. stocksianum subsp. stocksianum was also well-supported. Notably, the two subspecies of T. stocksianum were nearly identical genetically, indicating minimal divergence. T. mascatense was closely related to this group, further supporting their shared ancestry. T. polium, one of the species with newly sequenced cp. genomes in this study, was placed within a well-supported clade alongside T. mascatense and the T. stocksianum subspecies, suggesting a close relationship between T. polium and these taxa. Another well-supported clade included T. omeiense and T. simplex. The extremely short branch between T. simplex and its ancestor suggested very recent divergence or conservation of the cp. genome in this lineage. T. ornatum was positioned as a sister group to the clade containing T. omeiense and T. simplex, further resolving its placement within the genus. Finally, T. fruticans was placed as the most divergent species within the Teucrium clade, indicating significant genetic differentiation from the other Teucrium species.

Discussion

The complete chloroplast (cp.) genomes of three Iranian native Teucrium species (T. chamaedrys, T. flavum, and T. polium) were sequenced and analyzed to expand the available genomic resources for the genus. The analysis revealed highly conserved structural features while also identifying minor species-specific variations. These findings add to the growing body of genomic data on Teucrium, particularly for species native to Iran that have been underrepresented in previous studies. The cp. genomes exhibited a typical quadripartite structure, with sizes ranging from 149,913 bp in T. flavum to 150,026 bp in T. polium, consistent with genome sizes reported for other Teucrium species. To date, 13 Teucrium cp. genome records representing 10 unique species are available in the NCBI database, with sequence lengths ranging from 150,076 bp in T. stocksianum subsp. stocksianum to 151,154 bp in T. parvifolium. The sizes reported in this study are also consistent with those of other Teucrium species, such as T. stocksianum subsp. stenophyllum (150,087 bp) and T. mascatense (150,499 bp)32, as well as T. fruticans (150,808 bp)33. This consistency reinforces the stability of cp. genome size across Teucrium species, while minor variations highlight subtle species-specific differences. Previous studies have demonstrated that variations in cp. genome length among species are primarily driven by expansions or contractions of the inverted repeat (IR) regions, differences in intergenic spacers and intron lengths, as well as sequencing and assembly artifacts. These factors collectively contribute to the dynamic evolution of cp. genome architecture. Additionally, structural variations in genes such as ycf1, rpoC2, and accD have been reported to influence genome size differences, highlighting their potential role in shaping Teucrium cp. genome evolution34,35.

The analysis of cp. genomes of T. chamaedrys, T. flavum, and T. polium identified a total of 134 genes, a number that closely aligns with previous reports on Teucrium species. For instance, T. stocksianum subsp. stenophyllum and T. stocksianum subsp. stocksianum were found to contain 135 genes, while T. mascatense contained 136 genes32. This consistency highlights the moderately conserved nature of cp. genome structure within Teucrium, a trend also observed in other Lamiaceae species such as Mentha spicata17, Lavandula angustifolia36, and Perilla frutescens22. The slight variation in gene number between our study and previous reports (e.g., 134 vs. 135–136 genes) may be attributed to differences in annotation methodologies, the presence of pseudogenes, or species-specific evolutionary adaptations. Such variations are commonly observed in cp. genome studies, as seen in Salvia species, where gene losses and gains, particularly in tRNA and protein-coding genes, contribute to genome size differences37–39. Further analysis of intron-containing genes identified 17 such genes across the three Teucrium species, including accD, clpP, petD, rpl2, ndhB, ndhA, rps16, atpF, rpoC1, and ycf3. Notably, clpP and ycf3 contained two introns, a characteristic feature also reported in other angiosperm cp. genomes such as Origanum vulgare40 and S. miltiorrhiza41. Although the intron-exon structure was largely conserved, minor variations were observed in intron lengths among the three species. For instance, in T. chamaedrys, the clpP gene exhibited intron lengths of 723 bp (Intron I) and 627 bp (Intron II), while in T. flavum and T. polium, these introns measured 724 bp and 628 bp, and 726 bp and 626 bp, respectively. Similarly, the ycf3 gene displayed intron lengths of 708 bp (Intron I) and 735 bp (Intron II) in T. chamaedrys, compared to 708 bp and 737 bp in T. flavum, and 708 bp and 739 bp in T. polium. These subtle differences may reflect species-specific evolutionary adaptations or minor discrepancies in genome annotation. When compared to T. stocksianum subsp. stenophyllum, T. stocksianum subsp. stocksianum, and T. mascatense, the intron-containing gene structure in T. chamaedrys, T. flavum, and T. polium appears highly conserved. The previously sequenced Teucrium species also contained 17 intron-bearing genes, with clpP and ycf3 featuring two introns, mirroring the findings in our study32. However, minor variations in intron lengths and exon-intron boundaries were observed. For example, clpP in T. stocksianum subsp. stenophyllum contained introns of 723 bp (Intron I) and 627 bp (Intron II), closely resembling those of T. chamaedrys, whereas T. mascatense exhibited slightly different intron lengths (725 bp and 629 bp). The presence of introns in key genes such as atpF (ATP synthase) and rpoC1 (RNA polymerase) suggests a potential role in gene regulation through mechanisms such as transcript stabilization and alternative splicing42,43.

The analysis of IR contraction and expansion in cp. genomes of Teucrium chamaedrys, T. flavum, and T. polium revealed notable structural variations, consistent with patterns observed in other Teucrium species. The IR lengths in the newly sequenced genomes ranged from 25,341 bp in T. flavum to 25,446 bp in T. polium, falling within the range reported for other Teucrium species, such as T. stocksianum subsp. stenophyllum (25,598 bp) and T. fruticans (22,190 bp). These results highlight a general conservation of IR lengths across the genus, with minor species-specific variations. For instance, T. fruticans exhibited significant IR contraction, while T. stocksianum subsp. stenophyllum showed slight IR expansion32. Such variations in IR boundaries, particularly at the junctions (JLB, JSB, JSA, and JLA), are consistent with findings in other Lamiaceae species, where IR expansions and contractions are often linked to evolutionary adaptations and functional optimization19,44. The positional shifts of genes such as rps3, rpl16, ndhF, and ycf1 relative to the IR boundaries further underscore the dynamic nature of cp. genome evolution. For example, the rps3 gene shifted 21 bp from IRa into the LSC in T. chamaedrys and T. polium, while ndhF extended 15 bp into IRb in most species, with the most pronounced shift (38 bp) observed in T. stocksianum subsp. stocksianum. These findings align with previous studies on Salvia species, where IR boundary shifts were attributed to small insertions, deletions, or gene duplications, reflecting a common evolutionary mechanism within Lamiaceae45,46.

Comparative genomic analysis using mVISTA showed higher sequence variability in the LSC and SSC regions than in the conserved IR regions, consistent with previous studies on Teucrium and other Lamiaceae species32,41. Non-coding regions, such as trnH-GUG-psbA, rps16-trnQ-UUG, and trnG-GCC-trnM-CAU, exhibited high nucleotide diversity, making them valuable phylogenetic markers. In contrast, essential coding genes (psbA, matK, rbcL, and atp) were highly conserved, while rpoC2, ycf1, ycf2, ndhF, rpl32, rps16, and accD showed notable variation, supporting their potential for phylogenetic analysis. The observed patterns of variability, particularly in non-coding regions, align with their established use as DNA barcodes in plant taxonomy26,27. These regions, characterized by high nucleotide diversity, are particularly effective for distinguishing closely related species due to their rapid evolutionary rates and low functional constraints. The utility of these non-coding regions as molecular markers is further supported by their frequent application in phylogenetic studies across diverse plant taxa, including Salvia28. Our study establishes that a higher level of variation was observed in rps16, rpoC1, ycf3, accD, clpP, petB, petD, ycf1, ndhA, and atpF in the three Teucrium species analyzed, as well as in two additional compared species, reinforcing their utility for resolving phylogenetic relationships within the genus. Similarly, Khan et al. (2019) identified ycf1, rpoC2, ndhF, and ycf2 as the most divergent regions in T. stocksianum subsp. stenophyllum, T. stocksianum subsp. stocksianum, and T. mascatense, indicating that these genes exhibit significant variation across multiple Teucrium species32. The conservation of these divergence patterns across different species suggests that these regions may serve as robust molecular markers for clarifying unresolved phylogenetic relationships within Teucrium and the broader Lamiaceae family. Moreover, our findings align with previous studies that have identified ycf1, rpoC1, and ndhA as highly divergent in other angiosperm lineages, including S. miltiorrhiza41 and multiple Asteraceae species47. The identification of these regions as hotspots of genetic variation highlights their importance in phylogenetic inference and evolutionary studies. Furthermore, the presence of conserved but variable coding genes such as rpoC1, accD, clpP, and rpl32 in our study is consistent with their role as some of the most divergent genes in vascular plant cp. genomes, as previously reported48. These findings collectively reinforce the significance of both coding and non-coding cp. genome regions in resolving species relationships and evolutionary divergence within Teucrium.

The phylogenetic analysis of 10 Teucrium species, based on complete chloroplast genome data, revealed a well-resolved evolutionary framework for the genus, with strong support for its monophyly. The clear separation of the outgroup species, A. bracteosa and A. reptans, from the Teucrium clade confirmed their distinct evolutionary positions, consistent with broader phylogenetic relationships within the Lamiaceae family49,50. Within Teucrium, the close sister group relationship between T. chamaedrys and T. flavum suggested shared evolutionary adaptations, possibly linked to similar ecological niches, as previously noted in studies emphasizing the importance of calyx morphology in defining clades2,51. The well-supported clade containing T. mascatense, T. stocksianum subsp. stenophyllum, and T. stocksianum subsp. stocksianum highlighted the minimal genetic divergence between the subspecies, likely reflecting recent speciation or limited genetic drift. Similarly, the placement of T. polium within this clade underscored its close relationship to these taxa, supporting the hypothesis that T. polium shared a common evolutionary history with species adapted to xeric environments52. The extremely short branch length between T. simplex and its ancestor suggested recent divergence or high chloroplast genome conservation, a pattern observed in other Teucrium lineages adapted to specific ecological conditions2. In contrast, the divergent position of T. fruticans within the Teucrium clade indicated significant genetic differentiation, possibly reflecting its adaptation to distinct ecological zones or its role as a relict species preserving ancestral traits. The biogeographic implications of these findings aligned with previous studies suggesting that the Mediterranean and adjacent regions had been important centers of diversification for Teucrium52,53. The close relationship between T. mascatense and the T. stocksianum subspecies pointed to a shared evolutionary history in the Middle East, a region known for its high Teucrium diversity. Meanwhile, the divergent position of T. fruticans may have reflected ancient dispersal events or isolation in refugial areas during climatic fluctuations, consistent with broader patterns of regional endemism in the genus53. These results provided a robust foundation for revising the taxonomy of Teucrium, particularly in clarifying the relationships between closely related species such as T. chamaedrys, T. flavum, and T.fruticans. However, further research was needed to explore the genetic and ecological drivers of speciation in Teucrium, particularly through expanded sampling of underrepresented regions and the integration of nuclear genomic data.

Conclusion

To expand genomic resources for Teucrium genus and elucidate its evolutionary relationships, we sequenced and analyzed the complete cp. genomes of three Teucrium species, T. chamaedrys, T. flavum, and T. polium. The cp. genomes exhibited highly conserved structural features and identical gene compositions. Key findings included the identification of intron-containing genes, with species-specific variations in intron lengths, providing insights into evolutionary adaptations. Codon usage analysis revealed a strong preference for codons ending in adenine or uracil, consistent with trends in other angiosperms, while species-specific variations suggested ecological adaptations. Notably, highly variable non-coding regions, such as trnH-GUG-psbA and rps16-trnQ-UUG, and divergent coding genes, including ycf1 and rpoC2, were identified as valuable molecular markers for phylogenetic and evolutionary studies. Phylogenetic reconstruction strongly supported the monophyly of Teucrium, with high bootstrap values, resolving close relationships between T. chamaedrys and T. flavum, while highlighting the divergent position of T. fruticans. These results significantly enhance our understanding of Teucrium’s evolutionary history and biogeographic patterns, providing a robust foundation for revising its taxonomy and developing molecular tools for species identification, conservation, and phylogenetic studies. The identification of highly variable regions offers practical applications in DNA barcoding and resolving complex evolutionary relationships within the genus and the broader Lamiaceae family.

Materials and methods

Plant material and DNA extraction

Leaf samples were collected from three Teucrium species during their flowering period. These species were gathered from distinct geographical regions across Iran, including T. polium from Fars Province, T. flavum from the National Botanical Garden in Tehran, and T. chamaedrys from Mazandaran Province. No special permits were necessary for the collection of these plant materials, as they were obtained in compliance with local regulations. The taxonomic identification of the collected samples was verified by Dr. Soorni and samples were deposited in the herbarium at the College of Agricultural and Natural Resources, University of Tehran. As the study did not involve the description of a new species, no voucher specimens were preserved or archived in the herbarium. For chloroplast (cp.) genome sequencing, total genomic DNA was isolated from 100 mg of fresh leaf tissue using the DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Germany). The integrity and concentration of the extracted DNA were evaluated using agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA). DNA samples that met the established quality criteria were selected for further processing. Library preparation was carried out in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols, and sequencing was conducted on the Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform (Illumina Inc., USA) with a paired-end 150 bp read configuration, which yielded approximately 37,312,406, 39,312,406, and 37,612,206 million raw read pairs for T. chamaedrys, T. flavum, and T. polium, respectively.

Cp genome assembly and annotation

For each sample, the quality of the raw reads was evaluated using FastQC software version 0.11.9 (available at https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). Reads containing adapter sequences or displaying low-quality regions were filtered and trimmed using Trimmomatic v.0.39 54. The resulting high-quality reads were then assembled into complete cp. genomes using GetOrganelle v.1.7.7.1 55. To ensure the accuracy and completeness of the assemblies, the resulting graphs were visually inspected and validated using Bandage software56. Following assembly, the cp. genomes were annotated using two automated tools: CPGAWAS2 57 and GeSeq 58. Circular diagrams of the cp. genomes were generated using OGDRAW, a specialized tool for visualizing organelle genome maps59. Besides, The gene composition of the cp. genome of species was characterized using CPGView60.

Codon usage and cp. genome boundary analysis in Teucrium species

In this study, synonymous codon usage variation was assessed by calculating relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) values using MEGA6 software61. Furthermore, the boundary regions of cp. genomes were analyzed by comparing the cp. genomes of three Teucrium species with six additional species obtained from the NCBI database, one of which included two subspecies (T. fruticans (NC_061173.1), T. mascatense (NC_044073.1), T. omeiense (MN814871.1), T. ornatum (MN814864.1), T. simplex (MN814872.1), T. stocksianum subsp stenophyllum (MH325131.1), and T. stocksianum subsp stocksianum (MH325133.1)). The contraction and expansion of inverted repeat (IR) regions, including LSC, SSC, IRa, and IRb regions, were visualized using IRScope62. This analysis also included a detailed examination of the four junction points (JLB, JSB, JSA, and JLA) between the IR regions and single-copy regions, offering insights into the structural variations and evolutionary relationships of the cp. genomes.

Comparative genomic analysis and nucleotide variation assessment

A comprehensive comparative analysis of cp. genomes was conducted across three species of the genus Teucrium using advanced bioinformatics methodologies. The study commenced with pairwise sequence alignments, which were executed using the mVISTA online platform, incorporating the Shuffle-LAGAN algorithm63. The annotated cp. genome of T. stocksianum served as the reference sequence for these alignments. Following this, the cp. genome regions of the three Teucrium species were aligned using MUSCLE version v3.8.1551 64. To enhance alignment quality, trimAl v1.4 was employed with specific parameters (“-gt 0.95 -st 0.001”) to eliminate poorly aligned regions and gaps65. To quantify Pi within the cp. genomes, the software DnaSP v6 was utilized66. A sliding window approach was implemented to assess genetic diversity, with a window size of 800 base pairs (bp) and a step size of 200 bp.

Phylogenetic analysis

To explore the phylogenetic relationships within the Teucrium genus, we obtained 71 coding sequences from three Teucrium species sequenced in this study, supplemented with seven additional Teucrium cp. genomes retrieved from the NCBI database. In total, the dataset included 10 cp. genomes representing 10 Teucrium species. To root the phylogenetic tree, A. bracteosa and Ajuga reptans were selected as outgroups. Each gene sequence was aligned using MUSCLE version v3.8.1551 64 with default parameters to generate accurate multiple sequence alignments. The aligned sequences were subsequently refined using trimAl v1.4 with the parameters “-gt 0.95 -st 0.001” 65 to eliminate poorly aligned regions and gaps, ensuring high-quality alignments for further analysis. The trimmed gene alignments were concatenated into a single sequence matrix using SequenceMatrix67, creating a unified dataset for phylogenetic reconstruction. Maximum likelihood analysis was conducted using IQ-TREE68 under the GTR + Gamma model of nucleotide substitution, which was determined to be the best-fit model based on model testing. Branch support was evaluated using 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates to assess the robustness of the inferred tree topology. The resulting phylogenetic tree was visualized and annotated using the iTOL web server69.
