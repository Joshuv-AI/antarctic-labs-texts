Effect of a PEN-3 model-based educational intervention on cigarette smoking in Iranian patients with myocardial infarction
Authors: Nahid Shahabi, Farzaneh Pourjalil, Fatemeh Shafiei Sarvestani, Ali Mouseli, Zahra Hosseini, Teamur Aghamolaei, Marzieh Nikparvar, Sara Dadipoor, Amin Ghanbarnejad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cigarette smoking stands out as a major contributor to cardiovascular diseases (CVD) and myocardial infarction (MI). For patients who have experienced MI, quitting smoking is essential to avoid further complications. This study aimed to assess the efficacy of an educational intervention based on the PEN-3 model in reducing cigarette smoking among MI patients in Bandar Abbas, Iran. This quasi-experimental, interventional study involved 240 inpatients from the cardiac departments of the Payambar A’zam hospital in Bandar Abbas. Simple random sampling was used to choose the participants, who were then divided into two groups: the intervention group and the control group. Data collection was conducted using a questionnaire developed based on the PEN-3 model. It was given both before and three months after the intervention. The intervention group received the educational intervention both in-person and virtually. Data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 25. The intervention group’s mean scores for knowledge, perceptions, enablers, nurturers, and actions significantly increased after the educational intervention as compared to the control group (p < 0.05). A statistically significant difference was observed between daily and weekly cigarette smoking rates in both groups during the posttest (p < 0.001). Covariance analysis revealed a significant difference in posttest behavior scores between the intervention and control groups after adjusting for pretest scores. PEN-3 model-based interventions that enhance knowledge, adjust perceptions, and address social, cultural, and environmental factors of cigarette smoking may effectively reduce cardiovascular illnesses and myocardial infarction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32303-4.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) include a variety of disorders affecting the heart and blood vessels, and are the leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for approximately 17.9 million deaths annually1. CVD is the first cause of mortality in people over 35 years old in Iran and accounts for 39.3% of all mortalities in the country2.

Smoking cigarettes and other tobacco products significantly contribute to coronary vascular diseases and myocardial infarction (MI). 20% of mortalities from CVDs in the United States were caused by cigarette smoking3. The nicotine in cigarettes activates the sympathetic nervous system, leading to arterial constriction, increased heart rate, and elevated blood pressure. Consequently, smoking accelerates heartbeats and raises arterial pressure, damaging vessel walls and increasing the risk of blood clots and arrhythmias4,5. Smoking cigarettes is responsible for 1.62 million or 18% of global mortalities due to CAD6. A meta-analysis indicated that cigarette smoking is linked to a 28% increased risk of developing CVD and a 12% higher risk of mortality from CVD7. Similarly, a 2022 study by Saki et al. involving 9,828 individuals in Iran identified smoking as a significant contributor to CVD prevalence8. Furthermore, research at Shahid Mohammadi Hospital in Bandar Abbas showed that 32% of cardiac inpatients were tobacco users9. Smoking cigarettes is an addictive behavior through time, and the goal of health education is to change one’s harmful habits to establish a healthy health behavior10.

Social culture and norms play an significant role in shaping individual behaviors11. Smoking behavior is influenced by various factors at the individual, familial, and social levels12. Individual factors, such as curiosity about smoking and personal experiences, are often the most significant influences13. Familial factors include the prevalence of cigarette smoking among family members. Social influences encompass the imitation of prominent figures, peer pressure from friends, as well as the impact of compliments and insults related to smoking, which can affect group acceptance14. Moreover, MI patients are often older, and when smoking is common among their family or friends, it can diminish the perceived abnormality of the behavior. As a result, despite the several health risks associated with smoking, these individuals may continue to smoke without feeling discomfort15,16.

Cultural sensitivity is essential in health promotion and behavior change programs. Health education planners must tailor the design, implementation, and evaluation of their programs to align with the cultural context of the community. Recognizing culture as a critical factor in health education and promotion requires a deep understanding of the cultural and political backgrounds that shape people’s health beliefs and behaviors. One effective model that emphasizes culture in promoting preventive behaviors is the PEN-3 model. This theory-based approach to health education has successfully facilitated smoking cessation across various populations17,18. This model consist of three dimensions abbreviated as PEN19. (Fig. 1)

The PEN-3 model comprises three interconnected dimensions. The first dimension, “Cultural Identity,” emphasizes the role of health education within the individual’s immediate and extended family as well as the broader community. The second dimension, “Relationships & Expectations,” involves educational diagnosis by examining perceptions, enablers, and nurturers that shape behavior. The third dimension, “Cultural Empowerment,” underscores how cultural beliefs related to health influence interventions in education and healthcare. This dimension views culture as a dynamic factor affecting individuals’, families’, and communities’ actions—encompassing both positive and negative aspects20–22.

Despite the well-established link between cigarette smoking and heart disease23,24, smoking remains a significant public health issue, particularly among cardiac patients25. Adopting the right life style to maintain health and avoid diseases requires the development of healthy behaviors and implementation of appropriate educational programs to develop these behaviors26,27. Most studies conducted so far on quitting and reducing smoking in cardiac patients have lacked a theoretical framework and mainly focused on psychological counseling28,29. Consequently, this study emphasizes the necessity for a comprehensive approach, such as the PEN-3 model, which incorporates cultural factors to effectively promote smoking reduction behaviors in cardiac patients. By highlighting the central role of culture in health behaviors, the PEN-3 model is particularly well-suited to meet the unique needs of cardiac patients. By integrating cultural elements, PEN-3-based interventions can address both the barriers and facilitators that cardiac patients encounter in their efforts to quit smoking. Bandar Abbas, the capital of Hormozgan province and a major port city in southern Iran, presents a diverse sociocultural population, making it an important setting for this culturally tailored intervention. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the impact of an educational intervention based on the PEN-3 model on reducing cigarette smoking among MI inpatients in the cardiac departments of the Payambar A’zam Hospital in Bandar Abbas.

Methods

Research design and population

The present quasi-experimental study was interventional in design and included both an intervention group and a control group. The intervention was conducted between June 2022 and January 2023 in Bandar Abbas, located in southern Iran. The study population consisted of patients admitted to the Payambar A’zam hospital in Bandar Abbas.

Inclusion criteria

To be eligible for participation, patients were required to have experienced MI within the last six months and be current cigarette smokers (smoking at least one cigarette daily). Additionally, they needed to provide informed consent and have WhatsApp installed on their or a family member’s smartphone. Patients were excluded if they had received any smoking reduction interventions (educational or otherwise) in the past six months or during the study period, experienced recurrent MI that prevented participation in training sessions, missed any training sessions, or used smoking cessation treatments.

Patients in CCU 1 and CCU 2 were included in the intervention group and those in the cardiac ICU and post CCU were assigned to the control group.

To prevent the possible dropout, 5% was added to the sample size and finally the sample size was decided to be 240.

The sampling was simple randomization. To access the sample, firstly, a list was made of cardiac departments of Payambar A’zam hospital in Bandar Abbas. They included CCCU 1, CCCU 2, cardiac ICU, Post CCU, angiography and echocardiography. Among them, four inpatient departments were selected for the study. Two departments (CCU 1 and CCU 2) were considered as the intervention group and the other two (cardiac ICU and post CCU) as the control group.

The two departments of angiography and echocardiography were not selected for the study as they hosted outpatients. Having gained the necessary permission from the manager and head of the selected departments, the relevant departments were visited on a daily basis in the morning shift from 9 :00 to 11:00 a.m. and in the evening shift from 3:00 to 5:00 p.m. using a sampling flowchart as shown in Fig. 2. Thus, upon entering the department, the statistical information about all inpatients was obtained from the head of the relevant department. The patients’ records were reviewed, and the patients who met the inclusion criteria entered the study.

Educational intervention

We designed an educational intervention aimed at influencing the model constructs and promoting the adoption of healthy behaviors. Due to the current COVID-19 pandemic and heightened sensitivity to CVD, it was not feasible to conduct training sessions entirely in person. Instead, during hospitalization, patients and their companions (family or friends) received pamphlets about CVDs and the side effects of cigarette smoking. Following discharge, the intervention continued offline via WhatsApp for participants, their family members, friends, as well as individuals they admired or suggested (their peer group). This involved sharing text messages, pamphlets, and educational clips covering topics such as CVDs, complications of smoking cigarettes, benefits of reducing smoking habits, both positive and negative effects of cigarette smoking on health, and how family members’ influence can help reduce the desire to smoke over a three-month period. The pre-test results indicated that the intervention group scored lower on knowledge and perceptions constructs; therefore, these areas received greater emphasis in the educational intervention.

Face-to-face training sessions facilitate effective social communication between the trainer and patients, providing more opportunities for interviews, discussions, and Q&A sessions. In the present study, we aimed to conduct at least four face-to-face training sessions during patients’ hospitalization in relevant departments.

To build up knowledge, in two sessions, information was provided for the participants and their companions through face-to-face training, presentation of pamphlets, discussion about the threats of continuing to smoke, benefits of reducing smoking, MI and its current status in Iran and the world, relevant factors, risk of heart disease, relationship between cigarette smoking and heart disease, and ways to prevent the heart disease.

For the “perceptions” construct, two face-to-face sessions were conducted where intervention participants and their companions (family, relatives, friends) shared opinions and engaged in discussions about the positive and negative consequences of cigarette smoking behavior. These sessions also explored both positive and negative perceptions associated with smoking.

The “enablers” construct was addressed through five sessions. These included oral discussions on topics such as effective time management—focusing on decision-making skills, task prioritization, delegation techniques—and strategies to manage perfectionism, procrastination, as well as learning how to say no.

Regarding the “nurtures” construct, six WhatsApp sessions were held. Participants received pamphlets and participated in discussions about how media influences cigarette smoking habits, factors contributing to the tendency to smoke cigarettes, and the roles that family members and authorities play in shaping these behaviors. All information was sourced from scientific literature.

The control group did not receive any training or educational content; they were only contacted for the purpose of conducting a survey. After the post-test, the control group had an educational session and received the educational intervention materials. The details of the training program are presented in Table 1.

Data collection

To collect data, the researcher provided participants with necessary explanations about the study’s objectives and the voluntary nature of their participation. After signing an informed consent form, participants were assured that their information would remain confidential and that their responses would be anonymous. The researcher then conducted face-to-face interviews to complete the questionnaire. This method allowed for immediate clarification of any potential misunderstandings or difficulties in reading or comprehending questions. During these interviews, the researcher read each question aloud clearly and recorded interviewees’ responses directly onto the questionnaire.

The questionnaire was completed by participants (patients) in both intervention and control groups at two time points, with a three-month interval between them. Initially, pretest results were used to assess the need for educational content, determine an appropriate intervention method, and decide on the number of educational sessions required. Additionally, contact information—specifically mobile phone numbers—was collected not only from each patient but also from family members, friends, relatives, and other influential individuals in their social circle. This allowed for sharing educational content with these contacts. Three months after implementing the educational intervention, data were collected again from both groups.

The “perceptions” questions evaluated the impact of quitting smoking on reducing the risk of CVD and MI. Responses were scored using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from “strongly agree” to “strongly disagree,” with scores between 17 and 85. An example question was: “I believe that occasional cigarette smoking for fun is not a serious threat.”

The “enablers” section assessed access to recreational venues, smoking cessation centers, and patients’ free time. Questions were rated on a Likert scale from “very high” to “very low,” scoring from 1 to 5. Scores ranged from a minimum of 8 to a maximum of 40. A sample question asked: “How much does the cost of cigarettes influence your decision to smoke?”

The “nurturers” questions examined support levels provided by friends, family members, and religious leaders in promoting smoking cessation. These also used a similar Likert scale ranging from “very high” to “very low.” Scores could range from 7 to 35. An example question was: “To what extent do your friends or colleagues encourage you to reduce cigarette smoking?”

Under the category of “behavior,” one yes/no question asked: “Have you quit smoking cigarettes?” A yes response earned one point; no earned zero points—differentiating between those who had quit versus those who had not. If respondents answered no, they were also asked about their daily and weekly cigarette consumption rates in order to calculate any reduction during the posttest phase.

A panel of experts validated the questionnaire before it was sent for review by professors specializing in health education and health promotion. Their feedback helped enhance its content further. To evaluate reliability, an experimental study using the test-retest method was conducted over two weeks among thirty participants similar in profile to our target population. The questionnaire’s final reliability assessment via Cronbach’s alpha was 0.856.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed in SPSS25. Chi-square test and independent-samples T-test were conducted to compare demographic variables and model constructs in the two groups before and after the intervention. Considering the normality of data, paired-samples T-test and independent-samples T-test were run to check the effect of intervention. Covariance analysis was used to test the effectiveness of model constructs. The significance level was set at 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study received ethical approval (code: IR.HUMS.REC.1400.423) from the Ethics Committee of Hormozgan University of Medical Sciences. Before the study began, all participants were clearly informed about the purpose of the research and what their participation would involve. They were assured that all the information collected would be kept completely confidential. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant. Participation was completely voluntary, and participants were free to leave the study at any time without any consequences. All procedures were carried out following established ethical guidelines and regulations.

Only 25% had a high socioeconomic status and 25% had a low socioeconomic status. 14.46% of participants were unemployed and 23.3% were housewives.

Discussion

This study investigated the impact of an educational intervention grounded in the PEN-3 model on reducing cigarette smoking rates among MI patients at Payambar A’zam Hospital. Following the intervention, significant improvements were observed in the mean scores for knowledge, perceptions, enablers, nurturers, and behavior within the intervention group compared to the control group.

The results indicated that participants’ mean knowledge scores improved post-intervention. Furthermore, there was a statistically significant difference in knowledge scores between the pre- and post-intervention periods across both research groups. This increase in knowledge ultimately translated into desired behavioral outcomes, demonstrating the effectiveness of the intervention. These results are in line with a body of research conducted in Iran31–33. For example, Sarbandi et al34. investigated the effect of an educational intervention based on the trans theoretical model on male smokers’ cessation in pharmaceutical factories. This research showed that knowledge was the most effective predictor of smoking cessation in the participants of intervention group, and that awareness-raising could lead to smoking cessation.

The mean score for perceptions in the intervention group showed a significant increase compared to the control group during the posttest. As perceptions are key predisposing factors influencing both the initiation and continuation of health behaviors, positive perceptions can serve as a crucial asset in efforts to reduce smoking. This finding showed that education had a positive effect on the improvement of patients’ perceived threats of cigarette smoking. Among the possible reasons for the improved perceptions and knowledge scores in the intervention group can be the use of Q&As in social media immediately filled with educational content and increased access to these educational contents. The significant increase in the perceptions score in the intervention group is consistent with a number of studies such as the study of Barfi et al33.. and Salaudeen et al.35. Panahi et al. showed that after the intervention, the mean scores of knowledge and perceived threats of cigarette smoking in the intervention group significantly changed compared to the control group36. Numerous studies showed that belief in one’s own ability (self-efficacy) to quit and a positive attitude towards successful smoking cessation affect the general population of cigarette smokers as well as cardiac patients37,38.

In the present study, for a better impact and effectiveness of the training, the factors affecting two important constructs, nurturers and enablers, were recognized and considered in the intervention. The findings about enablers showed that the mean score of enablers in the intervention group increased significantly compared to the control in the posttest. This finding is consistent with the results of studies conducted by Williams et al.39, Jeihooni et al.40, and Dizaji et al.41, yet inconsistent with the study conducted by Abedi et al.42. Investigation of the effect of an educational intervention on self-care behaviors in Dizaji’s study showed that the mean scores of knowledge, perceptions, behavior, enablers and nurturers increased after the educational intervention41. Abedi et al42. showed that the educational intervention did not increase the enablers score in the intervention group. One reason for the contradictory findings is the time of study, which began at the same time as the national blood pressure control plan by the Ministry of Health in Iran, and therefore, at the same time as the intervention group, the control group also received training and related services through the media.

The present findings showed that the mean score of nurturers in the intervention group increased significantly compared to the control group in the posttest (p < 0.001). In the intervention group, a significant difference was found in terms of having cigarette smoking friends and their encouragement to smoke. Therefore, the educational intervention made in the target group and their families has been effective because it has led to an increase in the scores of nurturers and enablers. One potential reason for the increase in the enablers and nurturers scores in the intervention group can be the active role of the patient’s families and close friends in social networks during the training sessions. Nurturers such as the encouragement or opposition of friends and colleagues, family or media and influential people in smoking or quitting cigarettes can play a significant role. Research has shown that decisions to quit smoking are strongly influenced by social factors such as friends, family and social groups43. In Alboksmaty’s study in the United States, ex-smokers who lived in a household with no smoking member had 60% less relapse than those who lived in households where smoking prevailed43. According to Barrera et al. and Balcazar et al., smoking in family or among friends can reduce the perceived abnormality of behavior, and despite many threats, make the patient smoke without discomfort. Cultural interventions can significantly affect and reduce the health-threatening behaviors of different types in different demographic groups15,16.

The analysis of behavior showed that the mean score of daily and weekly smoking in the intervention group was significantly reduced compared to the control group in the posttest. Baghani Moghadam showed that education was an effective factor in changing smokers’ behavior of reducing consumption and managed to reduce dependence on cigarettes and help quit smoking in patient candidates for a heart surgery44. The results of this study are in line with the present study. Barfi et al. showed that there was a significant difference in smoking behavior among people in the intervention group when comparing the score before and after the intervention33.

In the present study, only a small percentage of patients managed to quit smoking after the intervention, which agrees with the study of Choi et al. on the difference between preventive guidelines and clinical practice among cardiac patients who smoked cigarettes. Within one year, only 52% of patients had quit smoking altogether. Only a small percentage of MI patients adhered to secondary prevention guidelines and a significant proportion failed to quit smoking. These findings emphasized the need for an effective patient education system45. In two studies, the number of patients who continued to quit smoking after the intervention gradually decreased during the follow-up. These findings were consistent with the results of these studies on the effect of minimal intervention in patients with CVDs during46,47.

Strengths and limitations

In this study, the PEN-3 model served as the theoretical framework. It emphasizes culture as a primary determinant of health behavior in public health interventions and health promotion, facilitating preventive or health-promoting behaviors. The educational intervention was delivered both face-to-face and online due to the COVID-19 pandemic, ensuring adaptability to diverse conditions and enhancing its feasibility. Several limitations of this study should be noted. Conducted in a southern province of Iran, the findings may not be representative of the entire Iranian population. However, they can cautiously be generalized to similar cultural contexts in southern cities. The study focused solely on inpatients, which might not accurately reflect all MI patients in the region. Another limitation was that some participants were unable to complete questionnaires due to issues like low literacy or physical disabilities; however, this was mitigated by having interviewers read questions aloud and record responses.

Further direction

Despite these limitations, the positive impact of educational interventions based on the PEN-3 model suggests its potential application in other health promotion areas and behaviors. It is recommended that future studies design and implement similar interventions using the PEN-3 model to address cultural and environmental barriers hindering healthy decision-making across diverse populations.

Conclusion

This study highlighted the effectiveness of the PEN-3 model in reducing smoking behavior among participants. The model facilitated the identification of key perceptual, cultural, and environmental factors that influence desired behaviors. By developing and implementing a targeted intervention program grounded in the PEN-3 model, significant improvements were observed in knowledge, perceptions, enablers, and nurturers—ultimately contributing to a decrease in smoking habits.

Reducing cigarette smoking is essential for preventing CVD and their recurrence. Smoking behavior is influenced by a complex array of personal, familial, social, and cultural factors. The application of the PEN-3 model in interventions reveals that education plays a pivotal role in reducing cigarette and tobacco use among MI patients. Educational efforts can significantly enhance awareness and alter perceptions about smoking’s harmful effects on disease progression. Based on the positive outcomes of this study, it is recommended to adopt this educational intervention approach to prevent and mitigate the exacerbation of heart diseases.
