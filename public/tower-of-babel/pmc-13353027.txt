Revealing symptom-level vulnerabilities between academic involution and anxiety: a network analysis
Authors: Shanshan Xu, Siyang Shao, Ning Yue, Bowen Liu, Yudi Wang, Ze Liang, Junkang Lin, Xinxin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcademic involution—escalating but low-yield academic effort—has become a salient feature of Chinese higher education. Although initial studies suggest a positive association between involution and anxiety, the specific symptom-to-symptom associations remain unclear.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 1,403 undergraduates in Shandong Province, China (mean age = 19.3 years; 34.9% male). Academic involution was assessed with the 24-item Academic Involution Scale, and anxiety symptoms with the 20-item Zung Self-Rating Anxiety Scale. A regularized partial correlation network was estimated using Graphical Gaussian Models with EBIC-LASSO. Central and bridge nodes were identified via expected influence (EI) and bridge expected influence (BEI).ResultsAcademic involution dimensions were positively correlated with anxiety (r = 0.35–0.63, p < 0.001). In the network, the most central anxiety symptoms were dizziness (EI = 1.20), panic (EI = 1.17), and faintness (EI = 1.12). Effort-reward (BEI = 0.38) and self-monitoring (BEI = 0.26) showed the highest bridge expected influence values in the network. Bootstrapping indicated narrow confidence intervals and stable centrality estimates (CS = 0.75).ConclusionsThese findings highlight that academic involution was conditionally associated with anxiety symptoms in specific cognitive–behavioral (effort–reward imbalance, self-monitoring) and physiological (somatic hyperarousal) patterns. Identifying central and bridge symptoms provides a hypothesis‑generating framework for generating more targeted hypotheses for future intervention research on both cognitive evaluations and bodily manifestations of stress.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04720-1.Keywords: Academic involution, Anxiety, Network analysis, Symptom-level associations, Hypothesis generation

Introduction

College students worldwide face intensifying academic competition, amid rapid advances in science and technology, including artificial intelligence, and a tightening labor market. The COVID-19 pandemic further aggravated these pressures through social isolation, disruptions in instruction, rising unemployment risk, and uncertainty about the future [4, 14]. In China, these dynamics intersect with higher-education expansion and limited elite employment opportunities, giving rise to the concept of “academic involution”—a competition climate where repetitive and escalating effort is normalized despite diminishing returns [10, 33, 56, 58]. Comparable dynamics have been observed in other contexts, such as the “school climate of competition” in Western settings [23, 40]. In practice, students may intensify visible but low-yield forms of effort to safeguard their relative academic performance—for instance, by engaging in repetitive practice routines, pursuing parallel test-preparation tracks, accumulating additional credentials, and monitoring peers closely to avoid being outperformed. These patterns are robustly associated with poorer mental health, particularly anxiety [6, 19]. Such contexts underscore the need to examine academic involution as a culturally specific yet globally relevant form of students’ perceived systemic academic competition.

Academic stress and peer pressure have long been recognized in the education and student-mental-health literature. Academic stress is typically conceptualized as an individual-level psychological response to perceived academic demands that exceed coping resources [42]. It reflects emotional and physiological strain arising from workload, examinations, and performance expectations. Peer pressure, by contrast, operates at the interpersonal level, reflecting social comparison processes and perceived expectations from significant others [37, 45, 50]. Academic involution overlaps with these constructs, but it captures a different aspect of students’ academic experience. Rather than focusing on emotional strain itself or on interpersonal influence alone, academic involution refers to students’ perceptions of a structurally intensified competitive academic climate characterized by escalating effort, positional scarcity, and diminishing returns. In this sense, the core of academic involution lies not simply in feeling stressed or pressured, but in perceiving participation in a low-yield competitive system shaped by evaluative norms, positional scarcity, and repeated overinvestment [10]. From a socio-ecological perspective [8], academic involution can be understood as reflecting students’ perceptions of a broader competitive ecology, whereas academic stress and peer pressure focus more directly on subjective strain and interpersonal influence, respectively. Treating academic involution as a distinct construct may therefore deepen understanding of how perceived competitive escalation is associated with student anxiety beyond traditional frameworks centered solely on stress or peer effects.

Anxiety is a state of heightened worry and vigilance toward ambiguous or perceived threats [35] and is a pervasive mental health concern among university populations worldwide [25, 39]. The high prevalence of anxiety among students likely reflects cumulative exposure to contextual stressors characteristic of recent cohorts, including pandemic-related disruptions, the massification of higher education, and intensified competition in the labor market. Previous studies consistently identify that academic stress at the individual level and peer pressure at the interpersonal level are the primary factors triggering anxiety among university students [1, 4, 56]. However, research examining students’ perceptions of academic involution as a competitive academic climate remains insufficient. Yang et al., [57]. Consequently, emerging studies begin to focus on the association between academic involution and anxiety among university students. In a discourse-analytic study of online discussions among Chinese college students, “anxiety” emerged as the most frequent emotion term associated with academic involution [27]. Survey studies with university samples similarly indicate that passive involution shows a stronger positive association with anxiety than other forms [58], and that higher academic involution scores correlate with greater anxiety severity [10]. Despite these indications, most prior work treats academic involution as a unitary construct, without unpacking the shared mechanisms through which involution may relate to anxiety. It therefore remains unclear which facets of academic involution are most closely linked to which anxiety symptoms in Chinese college students.

Importantly, prior research has primarily examined the association between academic involution and anxiety using aggregated total scores, typically via regression-based models. Although such approaches establish overall correlations, they implicitly assume that academic involution exerts a homogeneous effect on anxiety as a unitary construct. This obscures potential heterogeneity across involution dimensions and across anxiety symptoms, leaving unclear whether specific facets of academic involution are differentially associated with particular symptom manifestations. Addressing this gap is essential as diverse involution experiences may activate divergent psychopathological pathways. Without such granular differentiation, the underlying mechanisms linking academic pressure to specific forms of distress remain insufficiently characterized. Consequently, theoretical frameworks remain overly broad, and intervention strategies lack the empirical specificity required for effective clinical targeting and mental health support.

Research has largely focused on aggregate associations, with relatively less attention to mapping dimension–symptom linkages at the structural level. Modeling involution at the dimension level reduces within-scale local dependence while preserving the multidimensional structure of the construct, allowing identification of which specific facets show the strongest bridging associations with anxiety symptoms. From a network perspective, psychopathology can be conceptualized as a system of interrelated components [7]. The present study contributes to the literature by shifting the focus from a global association between academic involution and anxiety to a more nuanced mapping of how specific dimensions relate to individual symptoms. This framework enables the identification of potential cross-domain bridges between particular academic involution dimensions and specific physiological or affective anxiety symptoms. Mapping these discrete linkages may offer a more granular empirical foundation for student well-being initiatives, moving toward symptom-specific management and interventions that are more closely tailored to the nature of a student’s academic stress.

Accordingly, the present study had three aims: (1) to estimate the network structure of associations between academic involution dimensions and anxiety symptoms; (2) to identify the anxiety symptoms occupying the most central positions in the network; and (3) to examine which academic involution dimensions exhibit the strongest bridge associations with anxiety symptoms.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study conducted a cross-sectional survey among college students at a university in Shandong Province, China. Participants were recruited through online survey distribution, and the questionnaire link was disseminated via SoJump. In total, 1,600 students were invited to participate. Of these, 1,413 returned the survey, yielding a response rate of 88.31%. After excluding incomplete questionnaires, duplicate submissions, and responses that failed the attention-check item, the final analytic sample consisted of 1,403 students. Participation was voluntary, and all responses were collected anonymously. Because participants were recruited through online convenience sampling rather than probability-based sampling, the sample should be considered a nonprobability sample, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Of the sample, 34.85% (n = 489) were male, the mean age of the students was 19.33 years (SD = 1.57), and 24.88% (n = 349) reported an urban household registration. In terms of self-rated academic performance: 2.92% (n = 41) rated their performance as “less than ideal”, 48.97% (n = 687) as “average”, 39.70% (n = 557) as “good”, and 8.41% (n = 118) as “excellent”. This study was approved by the Ethic Committee of the university where the researchers were affiliated with.

Measures

Academic involution

The Academic Involution Scale, developed by [10], was used to assess academic involution among college students. The scale comprises 24 items cross five dimensions: emotional arousal (EA), action recognition (AR), self-monitoring (SM), anticipated goal (AG), and effort-reward (ER). Emotional arousal (EA) refers to emotional activation under competitive academic conditions (e.g., “I feel somewhat worried when I see others studying”); action recognition (AR) reflects behavioral manifestations of academic involution (e.g., “I engage in study tasks that are not very helpful to me”); self-monitoring (SM) refers to students’ ongoing monitoring and appraisal of their academic state under such conditions (e.g., “I feel that much of my studying is personally draining”); anticipated goal (AG) reflects the goals or motives underlying involution-related behaviors (e.g., “Involution-style studying is intended to avoid being surpassed by others”); and effort-reward (ER) captures students’ appraisal of the balance between academic effort and return (e.g., “I put in more effort, but my learning has not substantially improved”). Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater academic involution.

In this study, academic involution was operationalized as students’ self-reported perceptions of a competitive academic climate characterized by escalating effort and diminishing returns. This operationalization is consistent with research on organizational and school climate, where broader structural environments are often assessed through individuals’ perceptions of norms and evaluative pressures [49, 53]. Although some items include affective or self-evaluative content, they were conceptualized here as indexing students’ appraisals of competitive escalation, relative standing, and low-yield effort within the perceived academic ecology, rather than a general demand–capacity mismatch or anxiety symptoms per se. Dimensions such as self-monitoring were understood as reflecting how students perceive and navigate this competitive environment, rather than as indicators of anxiety or general academic stress. In this study, the items had good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.959).

Anxiety symptoms

The Self-Rating Anxiety Scale was developed by [60] to assess the severity of anxiety symptoms. The college students were asked to complete 20 items (e.g., “I feel afraid for no reason at all.“) and each one was rated by a 4-point Likert scale from 1 (no or little time) to 4 (most or all time). 5 items were reverse scored and the higher the total score, the higher the anxiety level. In this study, the items had good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.885).

Data analyses

The study analyzed the data using R 4.5.1. Firstly, bruceR package developed by [3] was used to perform descriptive statistical analyses of demographic variables, academic involution, and anxiety symptoms. In addition, correlation analyses were performed on the main continuous variables and differences in demographic variables were analyzed for academic involution and anxiety symptoms.

Second, according to the tutorial on network analysis [17], the network between the academic involution dimensions and the anxiety symptom items was estimated based on the Graphical Gaussian Model (GGM) using the R package qgraph [16]. Specifically, the EBICglasso procedure was used, in which graphical LASSO regularization is applied to the partial correlation matrix to obtain a sparse and interpretable network, and the Extended Bayesian Information Criterion (EBIC) is used to select the tuning parameter. The EBIC hyperparameter γ was set to 0.5, which is commonly used in psychological network analysis as a conventional balance between sparsity and sensitivity, helping to reduce the likelihood of retaining spurious edges while preserving an interpretable network structure [17, 20]. To identify central and bridge nodes in the network, expected influence (EI) and bridge expected influence (BEI) were calculated using the R package networktools [31]. EI was considered more reliable than other node centrality metrics and was the sum of the weight values of the edges connected to a node, reflecting the overall strength of its direct connections within the network [32]. BEI, similarly, was the sum of the edge weight values linking a node to nodes in another community, reflecting the extent to which a node was connected across communities [32].

In the present study, academic involution was modeled at the dimension level, whereas anxiety was modeled at the symptom level. This mixed-level specification was theory-driven. Academic involution is conceptualized as a multidimensional socio-psychological construct reflecting systemic competitive dynamics, with different facets, such as effort-reward imbalance versus emotional arousal, potentially showing different patterns of association with anxiety symptoms. Modeling it at the dimension level allowed us to identify which specific facets of the competitive climate were most strongly associated with the anxiety symptom network and emerged as prominent bridge dimensions [41, 54]. This approach also allowed us to move beyond treating academic involution as a single aggregated construct and to examine whether different dimensions showed distinct links to anxiety symptoms. In contrast, anxiety was modeled at the symptom level in accordance with the network theory, which conceptualizes mental disorders as systems of interacting symptoms [7]. Symptom-level modeling therefore provided a more fine-grained way to examine how academic involution related to individual anxiety symptoms. Additionally, modeling all involution items at the item level could introduce local dependence among highly similar items, potentially inflating within-scale connectivity and obscuring cross-domain bridging patterns [47]. The mixed-level approach was therefore adopted to improve interpretability and reduce this potential concern, although it may obscure the role of specific items within each dimension. This limitation was partly addressed through the supplementary item-level robustness analysis.

Finally, three procedures in the R package bootnet were used to evaluate the stability and accuracy of the network [18]: (1) The accuracy of the edge weights was estimated by randomly resampling the network 2000 times using a non-parametric bootstrap method and calculating confidence intervals (CIs). (2) Network stability was assessed by calculating the correlation stability coefficient (CS-coefficient). The values of the CS-coefficient above 0.25 indicated acceptability, while values above 0.5 indicated preference [15]. (3) Differences in network attributes were assessed by bootstrap difference test [15].

In addition, to examine whether the mixed-level specification influenced the main network findings, we conducted a supplementary robustness analysis by estimating an item-level network that included academic involution items and anxiety symptoms. This analysis was used to assess whether the centrality and bridge patterns observed in the primary mixed-level network were broadly consistent at the item level.

Results

Descriptive statistics

The results of the descriptive statistics and correlation analyses are presented in Table 1. Significant positive correlations were found between anxiety symptoms and the total score and the five dimensions of the academic involution (r = 0.35–0.63, p < 0.001). Notably, effort-reward (r = 0.63) and self-monitoring (r = 0.62) showed stronger associations with anxiety than emotional arousal (r = 0.35), indicating meaningful variation across dimensions. This pattern foreshadows the differential bridging roles observed in the network analysis. The results of the independent samples t-test showed that there were gender differences in the total score of the academic involution (p = 0.012), action recognition (p = 0.031), self-monitoring (p < 0.001), and anticipated goal (p < 0.001), whereas there were no gender differences for the other variables. At the same time, there was no difference in the category of household location for all variables. The ANOVA results showed that there were only differences in the level of anticipated goal (p = 0.040) and effort-reward (p = 0.026).

Network accuracy and stability

The accuracy and stability results of the network are shown in the Appendix (Figure S1, Figure S2, and Figure S3) and in Fig. 3. First, as shown in Figure S1, the bootstrapped 95% CI was very narrow, indicating that the accuracy of the sideline weights was relatively reliable and accurate. Meanwhile, the results of the bootstrap difference test for the sideline weights in Figure S2 showed that a large number of sideline weights were significantly different from each other. These results indicated that the accuracy of sideline weights in network estimation is good. Second, Fig. 3 shows the results of person-dropping stability analysis on the network. And the CS-coefficients of both EI and BEI are 0.75, which was much higher than the threshold of 0.25 at the most basic level [15]. This indicated that the estimates of node EI and BEI were sufficiently stable. Finally, Figure S3 shows the results of the bootstrap difference test for EI and BEI for each node. Significant differences between nodes with high EI and high BEI and the majority of nodes in the network indicated the stability of the network estimates.

Stability of node expected influences and bridge expected influences

Supplementary robustness analysis

To examine whether the mixed-level specification influenced centrality and bridge estimates, we estimated a full item-level network including all academic involution items and anxiety symptoms (44 nodes; see Figure S4). Crucially, an item-level robustness analysis confirmed that the bridging prominence of Effort–Reward and Self-Monitoring was not an artifact of dimension-level aggregation, as items from these dimensions consistently ranked among the highest bridge nodes in the full item-level network (see Figure S5).

The item-level network showed strong convergence with the primary mixed-level model. Specifically, items within the Self-Monitoring and Effort–Reward dimensions consistently ranked among the highest bridge nodes (Figure S5), replicating the bridging prominence observed at the dimension level. This rank-order consistency indicates that the elevated bridge estimates observed in the primary model were not driven by aggregation at the dimension level.

Moreover, the overall network topology was comparable across models. Cross-domain connections between involution components and anxiety symptoms remained distributed rather than concentrated in a single item-level node, suggesting that the bridging effects were not driven by isolated item-level artifacts. Instead, the observed cross-domain associations reflected coherent facet–symptom linkages. Centrality stability in the item-level network remained high (CS = 0.75 for both EI and BEI, see Figure S6), exceeding recommended thresholds [15], further indicating that the identified bridge nodes were robust to specification changes. Taken together, these findings suggest that the bridging prominence of Effort–Reward and Self-Monitoring is not an artifact of aggregation, and that the mixed-level modeling approach did not meaningfully distort network structure or centrality estimates.

Discussion

The present study advances prior research by shifting the focus from global correlations between total scores to a granular, symptom-level mapping of the academic involution-anxiety interface. Rather than treating academic involution as a homogeneous competitive climate, our network analysis identifies heterogeneous patterns of connectivity, revealing how specific facets, notably effort–reward imbalance and self-monitoring, show strong conditional associations with anxiety symptoms and high bridge centrality in the network. Consistent with previous evidence [24, 29, 36], our findings pinpoint somatic symptoms (e.g., dizziness and panic) as central nodes within the anxiety cluster. By elucidating these structural configurations, this research moves beyond general associations and provides a more specific empirical basis for generating hypotheses about which dimensions of academic involution may be most relevant to symptom-level intervention targets in future research.

Cultural context of academic involution in Chinese students

These results are consistent with cross-cultural evidence that somatization is more prevalent in Eastern settings, where psychological distress is often expressed through bodily complaints [46]. Cultural factors, such as the relative social acceptability of reporting physical versus psychological symptoms, along with norms of emotional restraint and indirect self-expression, may be associated with differences in symptom presentation [12]. At the same time, the prominence of somatic symptoms in the present network may also reflect the composition of the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale [60], which includes a relatively high proportion of physiological items such as dizziness, faintness, and tremors. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted in light of both cultural context and measurement characteristics, rather than being attributed to cultural factors alone.

In the Chinese higher education context, academic competition is widely documented as high-stakes [22]. Such institutional arrangements are frequently discussed in relation to sustained academic pressure and heightened peer comparison. Family expectations and prevailing social norms may further contribute to this evaluative climate, as parents often prioritize academic success and students frequently compare themselves with peers [55, 59]. Within such contexts, academic involution can be conceptualized as students’ perceptions of a collective competitive climate rather than merely an individual-level stress response.

The network structure observed in the present study is consistent with these contextual dynamics. The centrality of dizziness and panic, together with the prominent bridge roles of effort-reward (appraising effort–reward balance) and self-monitoring (vigilant checking of one’s status and risks), indicates patterns of co-occurrence between competitive perceptions and anxiety symptoms. The reliability of these findings is supported by high CS-coefficients (0.75) for both EI and BEI, suggesting stable centrality and bridging estimates.

These results underscore the importance of culturally responsive future research and intervention development. This measurement consideration should also be kept in mind when interpreting the apparent centrality of somatic symptoms. Consequently, the scale’s item content may bias the network towards detecting somatic centrality. Future research should use alternative anxiety measures with a broader representation of cognitive and affective symptoms to determine whether the observed somatic prominence reflects genuine phenomenological patterns rather than measurement-driven effects.

Theoretical interpretation: cognitive–behavioral and physiological associations

Cognitive–behavioral associations: effort-reward and self-monitoring

Our results showed that effort-reward and self-monitoring were the academic involution dimensions with the strongest conditional associations with anxiety symptoms in the network. Effort-reward refers to students’ perceptions that their academic efforts are insufficiently rewarded, whereas self-monitoring reflects students’ ongoing monitoring of their academic state within a competitive context. In the network, these two dimensions functioned as bridge nodes, meaning that they were the points at which the academic involution and anxiety clusters showed the strongest cross-domain connections.

At the cognitive level, effort-reward may reflect students’ perceptions of inefficiency within a competitive academic environment, where increased effort does not proportionally translate into valued outcomes. Self-monitoring may be understood as heightened vigilance toward one’s academic state within a competitive context, rather than a direct indicator of emotional response. Importantly, the network showed that effort-reward and self-monitoring had relatively strong bridge connectivity with both somatic (e.g., dizziness, panic) and affective anxiety symptoms. This bridging pattern is broadly consistent with theoretical expectations that appraisals of inefficient effort and excessive self-monitoring of one’s academic standing may be linked to heightened physiological arousal and anxiety-related distress. Although some of these nodes may overlap conceptually with patterns described in the academic stress literature, their prominence in the present network suggests that competitive academic perceptions may be especially salient in this sample.

This bridging pattern is consistent with theoretical models such as the Effort–Reward Imbalance Model [51], which emphasizes that insufficient reward relative to effort is associated with emotional strain, particularly in competitive academic settings [28, 52]. It also aligns with self-regulation theory, which suggests that excessive self-monitoring may be associated with reduced cognitive resources and heightened anxiety [9, 44]. Furthermore, prior research indicates that sustained cognitive monitoring may be associated with increased sensitivity to bodily signals and stronger physiological stress responses [11, 43].

Physiological associations: dizziness and panic

Our findings indicate that dizziness and panic occupy central positions within the anxiety network. This pattern suggests that somatic and autonomic arousal symptoms were relatively high connected within the anxiety symptom structure. From a theoretical perspective, such centrality is consistent with models of autonomic arousal in response to perceived threat in high-pressure academic settings [2, 13, 38]. These patterns are also theoretically compatible with models such as the James-Lange Theory of Emotion [30, 34] and Two-factor Theories of Emotion [48], which emphasize the role of physiological arousal and its cognitive interpretation in the experience of anxiety.

The network pattern showed that academic involution dimensions with higher bridge centrality, particularly effort-reward and self-monitoring, were connected with both somatic and affective anxiety symptoms. This configuration is consistent with theoretical accounts proposing that cognitive appraisals of inefficiency and heightened self-monitoring may be associated with physiological arousal and anxiety-related distress. However, the present cross-sectional network does not establish temporal ordering, mediation, or directional influence between cognitive-evaluative processes and physiological symptoms.

Consistent with cognitive–behavioral theory [5], symptoms such as dizziness or panic may be theoretically compatible with models emphasizing interoceptive attention and health-related worry. These associations are also compatible with theoretical accounts emphasizing ANS reactivity [21] and increased interoceptive sensitivity [26]. The prominence of other physiological nodes (e.g., tremors, body aches, restlessness) further suggests a coherent somatic cluster within the anxiety network. In contexts of academic involution—where students report heightened competitive pressure [33]—such symptom configurations may be particularly salient, although the present cross-sectional design does not permit conclusions regarding directionality or causal amplification.

Significance and practical implications

The present study advances the understanding of student mental health by shifting the focus from global correlations toward a granular, symptom-level mapping of the interface between academic involution and anxiety. Unlike previous research that treats these constructs as homogeneous units, this study deconstructs the multidimensional nature of academic involution to reveal how specific facets—notably effort-reward imbalance and self-monitoring—show conditional associations with distinct anxiety symptoms. This fine-grained specification represents a conceptual advancement by identifying bridge nodes where competitive academic perceptions show the strongest conditional associations with anxiety symptoms. Such findings support a precision mental health framework, suggesting that the “one-size-fits-all” approach to addressing student distress may overlook the heterogeneous associations between specific academic involution dimensions and anxiety symptoms.

Theoretically, these results suggest that perceived competitive dynamics are associated with the broader anxiety network, rather than acting as a single stressor. The relatively strong cross-domain connectivity involving effort–reward imbalance is broadly consistent with the effort–reward imbalance framework [51], particularly insofar as perceptions of low-yield academic effort were linked with anxiety symptoms in the present network. Similarly, the bridge role of self-monitoring reflects self-regulatory processes [9], where excessive surveillance of one’s academic standing may be associated with anxiety symptoms. This structural mapping suggests that these specific cognitive-behavioral features of academic involution are among the nodes showing relatively strong cross-domain connectivity with anxiety symptoms in the present network, offering a more nuanced theoretical basis for understanding how institutional competition is linked to individual-level distress.

From a practical perspective, identifying these bridge nodes, such as effort-reward imbalance and self-monitoring, may help generate hypotheses for future intervention studies to examine whether modifying these perceptions is associated with changes in the academic involution-anxiety network. Although the present cross-sectional findings do not establish causality, they provide a hypothesis-generating framework for designing targeted intervention studies. Rather than relying solely on generalized support programs, future research could examine whether interventions tailored to the specific linkages between academic involution dimensions and anxiety symptoms are associated with different patterns of mental health outcomes. For instance, the central role of somatic symptoms suggests that interventions focusing on physiological regulation (e.g., grounding techniques) may warrant future investigation among students reporting high academic involution. At the individual level, future studies could test whether cognitive-behavioral strategies aimed at reducing maladaptive self-monitoring and reappraising effort-reward perceptions are associated with weaker linkages between involution and emotional distress. At the institutional level, it may also be valuable to examine whether reducing emphasis on purely competitive ranking systems and promoting mastery-oriented assessments is associated with lower perceptions of academic involution.

Although the present cross-sectional design does not establish that modifying specific nodes will alter the broader network structure, the identified bridge configuration provides a structural framework for generating testable hypotheses. These implications should be considered hypothesis-generating and warrant examination in longitudinal or intervention-based studies.

Limitations and future reach

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. The estimated network reflects contemporaneous conditional associations rather than directional or mechanistic processes. Moreover, because the present network was estimated from cross-sectional data, its edges should be interpreted as undirected conditional associations, and the direction of influence between academic involution dimensions and anxiety symptoms cannot be inferred. Although theoretical interpretations were discussed, temporal ordering among academic involution dimensions and anxiety symptoms cannot be determined. Longitudinal or temporal network models are needed to clarify dynamic interactions over time.

Second, because all variables were assessed via self-report measures at a single time point, common method variance may have inflated some of the observed associations, particularly within the academic involution dimensions, where items share similar wording and response formats. Therefore, the strength of certain edges should be interpreted with caution, as they may partly reflect common measurement artifacts rather than purely substantive psychological relationships. In addition, the observed prominence of somatic symptoms may have been partly influenced by the composition of the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS), which includes a substantial number of somatic and physiological items. Future studies using alternative anxiety measures with broader representation of cognitive and affective symptoms, as well as multi-method assessments (e.g., behavioral indicators or physiological measures), would help clarify whether the observed somatic centrality reflects a robust phenomenological pattern or is partly measurement-driven.

Third, the mixed-level network specification—modeling involution at the dimension level and anxiety at the symptom level—represents a theoretically motivated design choice. Although supplementary item-level analyses yielded consistent patterns, aggregation of items into dimension-level nodes may limit direct comparability of centrality magnitude across nodes. Future studies may further examine alternative modeling specifications to assess structural robustness.

Fourth, the sample was drawn from a single university using convenience sampling, limiting generalizability. In addition, the sample was predominantly female, which may further restrict the applicability of the findings to more gender-balanced student populations. Given that gender differences in academic involution and anxiety may exist, the present results should be interpreted with caution when considering their generalizability across student groups. Competitive climates, institutional policies, and broader socioeconomic conditions may vary across regions and educational systems, potentially shaping both perceptions of academic involution and anxiety expression. Multi-site and cross-cultural replication incorporating more demographically balanced samples will be necessary to evaluate the generalizability and contextual specificity of the observed network structure.

Fifth, the present study did not account for several potentially important covariates, such as prior mental health history, family socioeconomic status, and academic performance. These factors may be associated with both perceptions of academic involution and anxiety symptoms, and therefore some of the observed associations should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, the present study focused specifically on anxiety symptoms rather than broader dimensions of psychopathology as a unified factor. Future research could extend this work to examine depressive symptoms or composite psychopathology profiles, as well as their potential interactions with socioeconomic stressors, in order to provide a more comprehensive understanding of student mental health in the context of academic pressures.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that academic involution is closely associated with anxiety symptoms among college students, with somatic symptoms occupying central positions and academic dimensions such as effort-reward and self-monitoring emerging as prominent bridge dimensions. These findings provide a basis for future research to examine whether educational structures and symptom-level processes may represent relevant targets for intervention. Future research may examine whether addressing perceptions of effort and reward, as well as physiological manifestations of anxiety, represents a relevant direction for reducing the emotional and bodily burden associated with academic involution. Longitudinal studies, research in more diverse populations, and intervention-based investigations may help clarify these associations and inform strategies to improve student mental health and well-being.
