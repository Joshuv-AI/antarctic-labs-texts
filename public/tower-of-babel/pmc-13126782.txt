Ethnobotanical survey of medicinal fern species used by communities bordering Kalinzu Central Forest Reserve, Western Uganda
Authors: Herbert Nuwamanya, Grace Kagoro-Rugunda, Raphael Wangalwa, Eunice Apio Olet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAcross the globe, traditional medicine, particularly herbal remedies, is a cornerstone of healthcare. While the medicinal uses of ferns are widely recognized globally, their therapeutic potential in Uganda remains significantly underexplored compared to higher plants. This limited research creates a critical gap in our understanding of the medicinal capacities of ferns, leading to their neglect in drug development. The main aim of this study, therefore, was to document the medicinal fern species and their uses in communities bordering Kalinzu Central Forest Reserve (KCFR).MethodsAn ethnobotanical survey was conducted for 3 months (October, November, and December), 2024 in 20 villages bordering Kalinzu Central Forest Reserve (KCFR) in Kyamuhunga. The study utilized a semi-structured questionnaire and face-to-face interviews with 71 key informants to collect data on the use of ferns in herbal medicine. The questionnaire data was analyzed using various ethnobotanical indices, including Relative Frequency of Citation (RFC), Use Value (UV), Relative Importance (RI), Fidelity Level (FL), Informant Consensus Factor (ICF), and Combination Use Diversity Index (CUDI).ResultsSix fern species were documented for use in herbal medicine by communities around KCFR, and Pteridium capense was the most cited species. The use value (UV) index was highest for P. capense (0.72). The informant consensus factor (ICF) was highest for musculoskeletal illnesses (1.00), and the fidelity level (FL) was highest for Hypolepis sparsisora for treating burns (100%). Fronds were the most used part of the ferns in herbal medicine (76.06%), and dermal (smearing and rubbing) was the most common route of administration (57.75%).ConclusionsMedicinal ferns are an important component of traditional herbal medicine in communities bordering KCFR. The study documented different medicinal fern species for treating various ailments in communities bordering KCFR. Pteridium capense was the most frequently cited species, used to treat various illnesses. These findings highlight the significant ethnobotanical knowledge of the local communities and suggest that further research into the pharmacological properties of these fern species, especially P. capense, is warranted. Further research should characterize and elucidate bioactive compounds responsible for such therapeutic values.

Background

Herbal medicine, a fundamental component of traditional medicine, remains a primary source of health care in developing countries [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately 80% of the population in developing countries rely on herbal medicine [2]. Beyond human health, herbal medicine is utilized within the food industry to control spoilage and pathogen contamination, highlighting the ongoing need for research into herbal remedies [3].

In Uganda specifically, more than 60% of the population depends on traditional medicine due to its affordability [4]. Consequently, herbal medicine has been increasingly integrated into modern Ugandan health care systems by emerging pharmaceutical industries. However, there is a persistent overdependence on only higher plants, often at the expense of neglecting ferns. Despite being overlooked, ferns have a rich medicinal history across Africa, Europe, Asia, and America spanning over 2000 years [5]. Despite their long standing historical use, the medicinal potential of ferns is poorly documented globally, and particularly in Uganda. Worldwide, only 442 fern species have been recorded as medicinal, which represents a mere 5% of extant fern species [6]. This insufficient documentation highlights a significant research gap and an underexplored opportunity for disease treatment on both global and local scales.

Consequently, the neglect of ferns within the modern medical systems has restricted their application primarily to the treatment of diseases in remote areas [7]. However, ferns could contribute as significantly to modern medicine as higher plants do, provided their medicinal values are scientifically established [8]. Ferns have been traditionally employed to treat various ailments, including skin diseases, gonorrhea, epilepsy, and cancer conditions that substantially impact human health [9]. This broad range of applications demonstrates the immense potential of ferns to contribute to modern health care systems. Given this untapped potential, the current research gap necessitates a comprehensive study to document their uses in herbal medicine. Such documentation is crucial for providing a foundation for future drug discovery and development, aimed at identifying new therapeutic agents to combat current health challenges especially in the face of increasing drug resistance. This study shifts the research focus from overexploited higher plants to the untapped medicinal potential of Ugandan ferns. It establishes a framework for pharmacological prioritization, identifying species for screening to bridge the gap between their ancient use and modern scientific neglect.

By validating their therapeutic value, this study provides a critical incentive for the conservation of ferns and their fragile microhabitats, ensuring sustainable harvesting practices. In addition, it revitalizes knowledge transmission by documenting fading indigenous expertise, thereby securing this cultural and scientific heritage for future healthcare integration. We hypothesized that medicinal fern knowledge is concentrated among the older key informants rather than the younger generation, and that P. capense has the highest cultural importance due to availability and multi-ailment use.

Methods

Study area

The study was conducted in Kyamuhunga, a sub-county and town council located in Bushenyi District, Western Uganda (Fig. 1). The study focused specifically on villages bordering KCFR. Kyamuhunga is situated at an average altitude of 1601 m above sea level, with a latitude of 0° 25′ 49′′ S and longitude of 30° 3′ 43′′ E, and borders KCFR to the southeast. This area was chosen based on the pilot study findings, which indicated that a large portion of the population relies on herbal medicine from plants to treat illnesses, of which ferns could be among the medicinal plants they use. This is due to high costs associated with seeking health care at nearby hospitals. Kyamuhunga has a total population of 43,378 people, with 13,785 residing in the town council and 29,593 in the sub-county [10]. The major economic activity in Kyamuhunga is tea growing. Sample size determination

The representative sample was determined through data saturation [12], which was reached when 5 consecutive interviews yielded no new medicinal fern species or therapeutic uses. This point was achieved after interviewing 71 key informants, comprising:

Herbalists (n = 38): Community nominated traditional practitioners with expertise in the preparation and administration of herbal medicine.

Elderly residents (n = 21): Residents aged 50 and above who possess specialized knowledge of traditional medicine.

Key forest resource users (n = 12): Individuals who frequently interact with the forest ecosystem, such as animal trackers, tour guides, and firewood collectors.

Documenting local knowledge on usage of ferns in herbal medicine

Purposive and snowball sampling techniques were used to select the key informants [13]. This approach prioritized individuals with specialized knowledge, specifically herbalists, elderly, and key forest resource users.

Short face-to-face interviews were conducted across 20 villages using a semi-structured questionnaire. On Wednesdays and Saturdays, the designated days for forest resource collection interviews were held at forest entry points. On all other days, interviews were conducted at the key informants’ homes.

The semi-structured questionnaire was developed following the guidelines of Simbo [14] and comprised three sections:

Demographics: Age, gender, education level, and occupation.

Ethnomedicinal Data: Local names of fern species, diseases treated, collection sites and timing, plant parts utilized, preparation methods, additional ingredients, dosage, and administration routes.

Ecological and Safety Data: Alternative uses of ferns, potential risks to the community, and identified threats to fern populations.

Collection and identification of medicinal fern species

The medicinal fern species cited by the respondents were collected. The samples were then identified and accessioned by depositing the specimens at the National Herbarium, Makerere University.

Data analysis

The findings of the ethnobotanical survey were analyzed using various methods to quantify the data. These included frequencies, percentages, and several ethnobotanical indices, such as relative frequency of citation (RFC), use value (UV), relative importance (RI), fidelity level (FL), informant consensus (ICF), and Combination use diversity index (CUDI).

Relative frequency of citation index (RFC)

The RFC represents the usage level of each species. This was determined using the formula below [15]:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{RFC}} = \frac{{{\mathrm{FC}}}}{{{\mathrm{Ni}}}}$$\end{document}RFC=FCNi

Here, FC is the frequency of citation for each species, regardless of the disease category, and Ni is the total number of key informants in the survey.

Use value index (UV)

The UV was calculated using the formula below [16]:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{UV}} = \frac{{\sum {\mathrm{URi}}}}{{{\mathrm{Ni}}}}$$\end{document}UV=∑URiNi

Here, Uri (use reports) is the number of different uses for a given plant species that are mentioned by each key informant, and Ni is the total number of key informants.

Relative importance (RI)

The RI was calculated to determine the importance of each species using the formula below [17]:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{RI}} = \frac{{ \left( {\frac{{{\mathrm{FC}}}}{{{\mathrm{FCMax}}}}} \right) + \left( {\frac{{{\mathrm{RNU}}}}{{{\mathrm{RNUMax}}}}} \right)}}{2}$$\end{document}RI=FCFCMax+RNURNUMax2where FC is the frequency of citation of a particular species, FCMax is the maximum frequency of citation across all species mentioned. RNU is the number of disease categories a species is used for, and RNUMax is the maximum number of disease categories across all the species mentioned.

Fidelity level (FL)

The FL indicates the relative healing ability of a specified plant species against a specified disease. It represents the proportion of key informants who indicate the use of particular plant species to treat a particular illness, and it was determined using the formula described below [18]:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{FL}}\left( \% \right) = \frac{{{\mathrm{Na}}}}{{{\mathrm{Nb}}}} \times 100$$\end{document}FL%=NaNb×100

Here, Na is the number of key informants who use the same species to treat the same main disease, and Nb is the sum of key informants who suggested usage of the same plant species for any significant disease.

Informant consensus factor (ICF)

The ICF shows the level of agreement among herbalists on the plant species used to treat a specified disease. It was determined using the formula below [19]:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{ICF}} = \frac{{\left( {{\mathrm{Nur}} - {\mathrm{Nt}}} \right)}}{{\left( {{\mathrm{Nur}} - 1} \right)}}$$\end{document}ICF=Nur-NtNur-1

Here, Nur is the total number of use reports of each disease category mentioned by different respondents regardless of the species, and Nt is the number of plant species (taxa) mentioned to treat that specified disease. Ailments were classified into five categories based on the physiological systems affected to facilitate the calculation of the ICF:

General health and systemic infections: Malaria, headache, and chickenpox.

Dermatological disorders: Burns, Tinea capitis, Tinea corporis, adult skin rashes, and childhood pimples.

Gastrointestinal diseases: Stomach ache and ulcers.

Injuries and musculoskeletal trauma: Fractures, wounds, and snake bites (venom).

Neurological and psycho-sensory disorders: Epilepsy.

Multi-ailment reports were handled by treating each mention of a fern species for a specific ailment as an individual use report, even when originating from the same key informant. To manage overlaps within disease categories, a species mentioned for multiple ailments but in the same disease category was considered a multiple use report and only counted once.

Combination use diversity index (CUDI)

The combination use diversity (CUDI) was calculated using the following formula [20]:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{CUDI}} = \frac{{\sum {\mathrm{Nsu}} }}{{{\mathrm{Nsp}} \times {\mathrm{Nu}}}}$$\end{document}CUDI=∑NsuNsp×Nuwhere Nus = number of unique “species × use” combinations reported by the group. Nsp = number of species represented in the group. Nu = number of unique uses represented in the group.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

The study population was predominantly female (63.38%) and characterized by limited formal education; over half of the participants (50.70%) were not educated, while only 8.45% reached the secondary level. Economically, the group consisted almost entirely of unemployed peasants (94.37%), with negligible representation in formal employment. The demographics skewed significantly older, with the vast majority of respondents (71.83%) aged 50 or above (Table 1). Usage, collection and threat frequencies of medicinal ferns

The use of ferns in herbal medicine is a deeply rooted tradition, with the majority of respondents having practiced it for over 20 years (49.30%). Medicinal fern collection most frequently occurs along forest-community borders (83.10%) and within the forests themselves (77.46%), primarily during the morning hours (94.37%). While fronds are the most utilized fern parts (76.06%), the use of scales is negligible (1.68%).

The most prevalent preparation method involves burning ferns into ash (47.89%), typically supplemented with jelly as an additive (23.94%). Dermal application, such as smearing and rubbing, is the dominant route of administration (57.75%). Beyond medicinal use, ferns serve primarily as mulches (46.68%) and pastures (28.17%), though they are also widely perceived as agricultural weeds (91.55%) and parasites to crops (88.73%). Regarding environmental impacts, agriculture (92.96%), forest clearing (85.92%), and forest clearing (84.51%) are identified as the most substantial threats to fern populations, whereas pesticides are considered the least significant concern (8.45%) (Table 2). Pteridium capense was the most frequently cited species, used to treat a wide range of conditions, including fractures, skin infections (Tinea capitis, Tinea corporis, and rashes), wounds, burns, and snake venom. It was also noted for relieving headaches, stomach aches, and ulcers. In contrast, O. distenta and H. sparsisora were the least mentioned species. Ptisana fraxinea and Didymochlaena truncatula were the only species mentioned to be used as food (Table 4). Fidelity level (FL) and informant consensus factor (ICF)

Hypolepis sparsisora recorded the highest fidelity level (FL) at 100% for treating burns (Table 6), indicating strong consensus for this use. Conversely, P. capense was the most versatile species, treating 11 different ailments. Its FL ranged from 42% (highest) to 2% for adult skin rashes and childhood pimples, the latter reflecting low consensus. Most ailments were treated by multiple fern species, except those treated exclusively by P. capense (fractures, tinea capitis, wounds, headache, adult skin rashes, and snake venom) or Alsophila mannianum (chicken pox). The informant consensus factor (ICF) was highest for injuries and musculoskeletal ailments (1.00), showing total agreement, and lowest for neurological ailments (0.50). The CUDI value obtained was 0.27, indicating an intermediate level of plant use diversity within the communities bordering KCFR (Table 7). Discussion

Knowledge distribution among informants

The demographic profile of the informants reveals that herbal knowledge is heavily influenced by gender and age. The majority of participants being female support the assertion that women act as the primary "pillars of healthcare" within the family unit and often possess more granular knowledge of local flora for domestic remedies. This aligns well with the findings of Wang et al. [21] and Kacholi [22], who highlight the central and pivotal role of women in traditional medicine. Furthermore, the concentration of knowledge among informants over the age of 50 mirrors the findings of Dagni et al. [23], who noted that profound ethnobotanical expertise typically resides with the elderly. However, since the majority of participants were unemployed and had limited formal education, their reliance on medicinal ferns may be a result of economic necessity; traditional medicine often serves as a low-cost alternative to expensive modern pharmaceuticals for low income populations.

The longevity of fern use in this region is remarkable, with nearly half of the respondents reporting over 20 years of practice. This continuity suggests that the medicinal application of ferns around KCFR is a deeply entrenched cultural legacy, consistent with historical records of fern use dating back to the early 1900s [24].

Collection, preparation modes and administration routes

The preference for collecting ferns along forest-community borders is likely driven by both ecological and logistical factors. Ecologically, these "edge zones" function as transitional habitats (ecotones) that offer higher light intensity, which promotes the growth of many heliophilic fern species. Logistically, the high frequency of morning collection indicates that proximity and ease of access are vital for daily traditional practices. Fronds were identified as the most utilized part, a trend supported by Singh et al. [25], which is likely due to their abundance and the ease with which they can be harvested without destroying the entire plant. The most prevalent preparation method, burning ferns into ash, is particularly noteworthy. When combined with jelly as an additive, this suggests a specialized treatment for skin-related ailments. This correlates directly with the finding that the dermal route (smearing and rubbing) is the dominant administration method, as ash-based topical treatments are frequently used to treat burns and dermatological conditions in areas with limited access to clinical ointments [26].

Diversity of medicinal Ferns and comparison to other regions

This survey revealed that communities bordering the KCFR utilize only six fern species in herbal medicine, suggesting that ferns remain a relatively neglected resource in local ethnobotanical practices. This limited diversity aligns well with findings by dos Santos Reinaldo et al. [8] in Brazil, where a lack of documented data was found to constrain the integration of ferns into herbal medicine. In stark contrast, studies in other regions, such as India, document a much broader pharmacological importance; for instance, Sathiyaraj et al. [27] identified 50 medicinal fern species used for complex conditions, such as renal and diuretic diseases. This disparity suggests that the exploration and application of ferns are significantly more advanced in Asian traditional systems than in Uganda. However, the fact that only six fern species are utilized effectively to treat a broad spectrum of ailments, ranging from skin conditions to internal pains, underscores a high "utility per species" ratio and suggests that much of the local fern herbal knowledge may remain undocumented and may be lost, because it is always transmitted orally.

Food and other uses of ferns

The survey also reveals that ferns provide essential ecosystem services beyond medicine, particularly as mulches and pastures. While food use was reported at a lower percentage, the consumption of Ptisiana fraxinea (rhizomes) and Didymochlaena truncatula (croziers) is significant. These findings are validated by Giri and Uniyal [28], who documented 55 fern species used as food globally, specifically highlighting Ptisiana fraxinea. This multi-purpose utility underscores the importance of ferns to the local economy and food security, even if they are not the primary dietary staple.

Cultural importance and priority species

The high ethnobotanical indices (UV and RI) for Pteridium capense are likely a function of both ecological abundance and phytochemical diversity. Species that thrive in open community habitats are naturally more "visible" and thus more integrated into the local herbal medicine. The versatility of Pteridium capense in treating a broad spectrum of ailments is justified by the presence of secondary metabolites, such as alkaloids, saponins, tannins, and flavonoids, which possess responsible for the cure of diseases in traditional medicine [29]. Conversely, the low citation of forest-interior species suggests that physical barriers and collection effort significantly limit the diversity of the local herbal toolkit.

The Use Reports (UR) for all species were not exceptionally high, as most informants cited only one medicinal fern for a specific disease. This led to a total number of uses only slightly exceeding the total number of informants. Because many respondents cited the same species for the same ailment, the resulting UV and RI indices for all mentioned ferns were relatively low. This pattern indicates a high degree of consensus on specific remedies but also suggests a narrow breadth of medicinal fern diversity currently being utilized around the KCFR. This concentration of use on a few key species highlights both a reliance on proven "staple" remedies and a potential underutilization of other available ferns.

Fidelity Level (FL) serves as a critical indicator of a plant’s perceived power against a specific disease. The maximum FL recorded for H. sparsisora against burns underscores its significant therapeutic capacity for this particular condition, despite its low overall citation rate. The observation that certain species show high fidelity for one ailment but low fidelity for others indicates that these medicinal ferns are highly specialized.

Injuries and musculoskeletal ailments registered the highest ICF values, showing total agreement on the use of ferns for these conditions. In contrast, neurological ailments recorded the lowest ICF showing a lack of consensus among informants which indicates that there is significant disagreement on which fern species are effective for neurological issues, or that multiple species are being used inconsistently. This suggests that while neurological ailments may be present, the traditional knowledge regarding their treatment with ferns is fragmented or less established compared to the highly standardized remedies for physical injuries.

The intermediate CUDI reflects that most ferns are not used as universal cures for the ailments listed except Pteridium capense that treats 11 ailments.

Threats and conservation implications

A significant contradiction exists in the community’s perception of ferns: while valued for medicine, pastures, and mulch, they are overwhelmingly viewed as agricultural weeds and parasites to crops. This negative perception may hinder conservation efforts on private lands. Without formal protection and cultural valuation, these medicinal resources, and the elderly expertise associated with them, risk being lost to rapid agricultural expansion which is identified as the most threatening activity to fern populations. This highlights an urgent need to conserve and document these species, ensuring that essential ecosystem services are maintained for future food and health security.

Conclusions

This study reveals that P. capense is the most important medicinal fern species in communities bordering KCFR. While several illnesses are treated by the cited medicinal fern species, burns remain the largely very well-treated illness. The low number of medicinal fern species cited in this study suggests limited knowledge about the medicinal properties of ferns and low fern distribution and diversity. Therefore, further research should focus on exploring additional medicinal uses of these fern species in communities of Uganda, with specific emphasis on characterizing and elucidating the active secondary metabolites in P. capense. More studies should dive into a comprehensive assessment of diversity and distribution of ferns in Uganda and establish their medicinal uses in other Ugandan communities and forests and the whole world at large.

Limitations of the study

Due to funding limitations, it was not possible to interview all herbalists, elderly residents, and key forest resource users; therefore, a representative sample was selected to serve as key informants. In addition, the study is subject to seasonal bias, because data collection was limited to the months of October through December. This specific time frame may have resulted in an incomplete documentation of fern species, as those with different growth cycles or seasonal requirements were likely excluded.
