Chronic Back Pain Increases Alzheimer’s Disease Risk Independent of Gross Brain Structural Alterations: Genetic Evidence from Two Populations
Authors: Frank F Huang, Yaomei Sun, Yuanzhi Hu, Jeremy R Chang, Zhixing Zhou, Daniel K Y Zheng, Chun Liang Hsu, Dino Samartzis, Jaro Karppinen, Fadi Mohammad Al Zoubi, Siu ngor Fu, Arnold Y L Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObservational studies suggest chronic musculoskeletal pain (CMP) may be a risk factor for dementia, but confounding factors or reverse causation could obscure these findings. Additionally, whether brain structure imaging measurements mediate this relationship remains unclear. We aimed to investigate their causal association using Mendelian randomization (MR).MethodsGenetic instruments derived from genome-wide association studies were employed in a two-sample MR framework. Forward MR analysis examined the genetic effect of CMP on dementia, while reverse MR analysis evaluated the genetic effect of dementia on CMP to assess reverse causation. Primary causal estimates were obtained using the inverse-variance weighted method. Mediation analyses tested brain structural markers as potential mediators. Sensitivity analyses evaluated robustness and horizontal pleiotropy.ResultsPrimary inverse-variance weighted analysis suggested genetically predicted chronic back pain (CBP) is associated with an increased risk of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) (Odds Ratio 2.287, 95% confidence interval: 1.338 to 3.909, P = 0.002). However, this estimate was characterized by wide confidence intervals, conservative SNP heritability estimates, and null results in MR-Egger sensitivity analyses, indicating potential directional pleiotropy cannot be fully ruled out. No significant causal associations were detected for all-cause, frontotemporal, or vascular dementia. Other CMP subtypes showed no significant genetic effect on any dementia type. There was no evidence of reverse causation. While CBP influenced nine brain structures, these did not mediate the CBP-AD link.ConclusionPrimary MR analyses support a directional association between genetically predicted CBP and increased AD risk. However, due to methodological constraints and wide confidence intervals, the large point estimate of the odds ratio should be interpreted with caution. Structural brain alterations do not appear to mediate this relationship. These findings highlight a potential causal pathway between CBP and AD, but require rigorous validation in independent, adequately powered cohorts before clinical inferences can be drawn.Keywords: chronic musculoskeletal pain, chronic back pain, dementia, brain structure imaging measurements, epidemiology, genome-wide association studies, Mendelian randomization

Introduction

Chronic musculoskeletal pain (CMP), affecting approximately 30% of the global population, making it a leading cause of disability worldwide and a major driver of rehabilitation demand.1 CMP encompasses a wide spectrum of conditions driven by etiologies, such as osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, sprains, or fractures.2 The most prevalent presentations include chronic back (CBP), hip (CHP), knee (CKP), and neck or shoulder pain (CNoSP), which collectively burden hundreds of millions of individuals.3 Because these four region-specific pain syndromes account for the majority of CMP-related disability and possess well-characterized, large-scale genetic datasets, they serve as the most robust and clinically relevant representatives of CMP for epidemiological and genetic investigations.3 Importantly, the impact of CMP extends beyond localized discomfort, it is significantly associated with an increased risk of severe systemic conditions, such as cardiovascular disease, cancer, cognitive, and dementia.4,5 Therefore, comprehensive CMP management should go beyond symptom control to include the identification and mitigation of risk factors that predispose these patients to secondary, severe comorbidities.

Dementia is a major public health concern, with global cases projected to increase from 57.4 million in 2019 to 152.8 million by 2050, necessitating extensive treatments worldwide.6 The most common dementia subtypes include Alzheimer’s disease (AD), frontotemporal dementia (FTD), vascular dementia (VD), dementia with Lewy bodies, and unspecified dementia.7 However, the majority of patients lack effective treatment options, and the primary causes remain largely unknown, with only a few familial cases linked to specific genetic mutations.8 A cohort study revealed that 36.7% of the Taiwanese individuals with dementia had CMP-related diagnoses.9 In dementia, neurodegenerative alterations in the anterior cingulate cortex may lead to reduced inhibitory output, which shifts descending pain modulation toward facilitation, resulting in altered pain perception and worsened chronic pain symptoms.10,11 However, the causal association identified in cohort research might be affected by confounders or reverse causation, highlighting the need for further research.

Mendelian randomization (MR) leverages genetic variation to infer causal relationships between exposures and outcomes.12 Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) provide essential data for MR by identifying single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and phenotypic associations without a priori hypotheses.13 Summary data from GWAS approach mitigates confounding factors present in observational studies and allows for a more rigorous examination by switching the exposure and outcome variables.14 Given the diverse clinical presentations of CMP, and the potentially confounding relationship between pain and dementia, it is advisable to employ both univariate and multivariate analyses to disentangle the impacts of CMP on dementia development and to unravel potential reverse causation. Additionally, the Causal Analysis Using Summary Effect Estimates (CAUSE) model is recommended to account for potential correlated and uncorrelated horizontal pleiotropy.15 Currently, no studies have yet utilized the CAUSE model to investigate the causal relationship between CMP and dementia.

Research indicates that CMP can lead to atrophy in hippocampus volume, subsequently resulting in memory decline and dementia.16 To investigate this plausible pathway, we used structural imaging-derived phenotypes in our mediation analyses. These structural imaging data were prioritized because their GWAS summary statistics are derived from substantially larger sample sizes (eg., UK Biobank) compared to functional magnetic resonance imaging, offering greater statistical power and more reliable instrumental variables for two-sample MR mediation analyses.17 Conducting such mediation analyses is warranted, as elucidating the role of specific brain structures could inform targeted prevention and tailored intervention strategies to slow disease progression.18

Our study aimed to investigate the potential bidirectional causal relationships between genetically predisposed CMP and dementia using two-sample MR based on GWAS data, alongside mediation analyses linking GWAS data to brain structure imaging measurements. Specifically, it assessed whether (1) CMP increased the risk of incident dementia and (2) dementia heightened the risk of CMP development, examining how brain structure imaging measurements influenced these relationships. We hypothesized a bidirectional association between CMP and dementia, potentially mediated by specific structural brain changes.

Methods

Study Design

This two-sample MR study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology-Mendelian Randomization Statement (Supplementary Table S1).19 All MR analyses used the latest publicly available data, with SNPs serving as instrumental variables (IVs). Ethical approval for this research was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of The Hong Kong Polytechnic University (reference: HSEARS20260423002).

Data on CMP

Summary data for CMP were sourced from the UKB. This biobank includes approximately 500,000 individuals aged 38 to 73, with data collected between 2006 and 2010 (available at www.ukbiobank.ac.uk). The MRC-IEU UKB GWAS pipeline is optimized for fast, efficient, and standardized GWAS processing of genetic data from all participants. After quality control, the genetic data includes 12,370,749 SNPs and 463,005 individuals.20,21

The UKB dataset includes chronic pain-related GWAS data from 461,857 European participants.22 Chronic pain data were collected through an electronic questionnaire asking, “In the last month, have you experienced any of the following that interfered with your usual activities?” Participants could select from options including headache, facial pain, neck or shoulder pain, back pain, stomach or abdominal pain, hip pain, knee pain, pain all over the body, none of the above, or prefer not to answer. Those selecting a type of pain were then asked if they had experienced it for more than three months.23 Individuals with a specific type of chronic pain lasting over three months were classified as experiencing that type of chronic pain. The GWAS data on exposures used in our research include: (1) CBP (n = 117,404); (2) CHP (n = 51,516); (3) CKP (n = 97,889); and (4) CNoSP (N = 105,396) (Table 1).

Data on Dementia

Summary data for dementia were obtained from GWAS data conducted in the FinnGen R11 database.21 FinnGen database is a public-private partnership that combines newly collected and legacy genotype data from Finnish biobanks with digital health records from the Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare (https://www.finngen.fi/en).21 This project aims to provide new insights into disease genetics using longitudinal health data collected from every Finnish resident since 1969, identifying 30 new associations, mainly low-frequency variants enriched in the Finnish population. This database was based on a prospective cohort design in which patients were screened using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) coding criteria for four subtypes of dementia. The primary outcome was all-cause dementia (N = 388,560), with secondary outcomes including three major dementia phenotypes: (1) AD (n = 227,475); (2) FTD (n = 392,463); and (3) VD (n = 393,024) (Table 1, Supplementary Table S2).

Data on Brain Structure Imaging Measurements

This study utilized GWAS data for 3,935 brain imaging-derived phenotypes. The summary statistics for these phenotypes were sourced from the Oxford Brain Imaging Genetics Serve.17 This dataset comprises genome-wide association analyses of 39,691 brain scans from ~40,000 participants, encompassing measures of regional and tissue volume, signal intensity, and cortical thickness. In the present study, the Desikan-Killiany-Tourville atlas (186 regions) and FreeSurfer’s automated subcortical segmentation (99 regions) were employed for volumetric analysis of cortical and subcortical brain structures, respectively (Supplementary Table S3).24,25

Instrument Selection and Strength Evaluation

Independence Setting by Calculating SNP Heritability

Linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) software was employed to assess SNP heritability, represented by h2 SNP.26 For this purpose, LDSC utilizes the pattern of SNP effects to estimate the variance in the phenotype that can be attributed to the collective influence of all measured SNPs.

Independence Setting by Assessing Weak Instruments and Removing SNPs Associated with Confounders

Screening procedures include assessing weak instruments with the F-statistic (F < 10 indicates weakness), using LDSC and PLINK software to ensure SNP independence [the proportion of exposure variance explained (R2) <0.001, 10000 kb], and removing SNPs associated with confounders (P < 1e-05 for strong exposure-association).27 This significance level for IVs has been used in previous MR research.28 The association values for the corresponding SNPs were obtained from the outcome GWAS summary statistics.

Instrument Strength Evaluation

We assessed R2 by the IVs using the formula for the determination coefficient, where is the test statistic for the ith IV in the exposure trait GWAS, and is the sample size of the exposure trait GWAS.29 Additionally, the F-statistic, , was used to evaluate the strength of the selected IVs. A genetic IV with an F-statistic >10 indicates strong instrument strength, helping mitigate potential bias in MR analysis.30

Assessments of MR Assumptions

There are three assumptions for MR: (1) the IVs are strongly associated with the exposure; (2) the IVs are not related to any confounders; and (3) the IVs influence the outcome only through the exposure rather than through other pathways (no horizontal pleiotropy). To address three assumptions, we selected strong IVs that met the genome-wide significance threshold (F-statistic > 10) for association with the exposure trait.30 For the second MR assumption, inverse-variance weighted (IVW) and CAUSE analyses were used to filter out IVs associated with potential confounders.15,31 The third assumption was evaluated using the IVW sensitivity analysis to identify and account for horizontal pleiotropy.32

Sample Size Calculations

We calculated the magnitude of detectable odds ratios in both forward and reverse MRs, assuming 80% power and a significance level of P <0.05.33 The calculations of sample size can be implemented at https://sb452.shiny apps.io/power/. Additionally, since both the exposure and mediator datasets used for mediation analysis were sourced from the UK Biobank, we assessed potential bias due to sample overlap by calculating the proportion of overlap using the formula proposed by Burgess et al.34

Mediation Analysis

To assess the potential mediating effect of brain structure imaging measurements, we performed a two-step, two-sample MR mediation analysis.18 In the first step, we estimated the causal effect of CMP on brain structure imaging measurements. In the second step, we assessed the causal effect of brain structure imaging measurements on dementia. Finally, the effect of exposure on mediators and the effect of mediators on outcome were estimated.

Statistical Analysis

Forward MR Analyses

The primary analysis employed the IVW method to estimate causal effects by aggregating summary data from multiple SNP variants into a single causal estimate.31 However, this approach may increase the risk of correlated horizontal pleiotropy, which could result in false positives.31 Therefore, the CAUSE analysis was used to minimize false positives.15

Reverse MR Analyses

We also conducted reverse MR analyses to evaluate whether the onset of dementia causally affects four types of CMP.35 Results from both the primary and supplementary analyses were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction, with P < 0.003125 [0.05/(4*4)] considered statistically significant.36

Sensitivity Analyses

Sensitivity analyses including the weighted median method,32 MR-Egger method,37 simple mode method,38 weighted mode method,38 Bayesian Weighted Mendelian Randomization (BWMR) method,39 Cochran’s Q heterogeneity tests,40 MR pleiotropy residual sum and outlier tests,32 and leave-one-out analysis were employed to evaluate the robustness of our results.41 The significance level of the sensitivity analysis is set at 0.05. Results were presented using scatter plots to assess direction and intercept, leave-one-out plots for influence analysis, forest plots for effect size visualization, and funnel plots to evaluate distribution and identify outlier SNPs.

Multivariable Mendelian Randomization Analysis

To estimate the combined effects of multiple exposures on an outcome, Multivariable Mendelian Randomization (MVMR) was applied for multiple genetic instruments.40 For positive results in two-sample MR, MVMR analysis was performed.

Mediation Analysis

The two-step, two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) mediation analysis employed the IVW method, the weighted median method, the MR-Egger method, the simple mode method, and the weighted mode method.31,32,37,38 The significance of the indirect (mediation) effect and standard error were tested using the “product of coefficients method” and the “delta” method.18 The significance level of the sensitivity analysis is set at 0.05 based on previous research.42 All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.4.043 with the “TwoSampleMR” (0.5.7),44 “MRPRESSO” (1.0),32 “ieugwasr” (0.2.2), and “cause” (v1.0.0.0274) package.15

Results

Summary Information on SNPs

The F-statistics for all IVs exceeded the conventional threshold of 10, confirming the strength of the genetic instruments and satisfying the first MR assumption. Specifically, the F-statistics for SNPs associated with CMP and dementia ranged from 20 to 1,820, with corresponding sample sizes varying between 51,516 and 393,024 participants. For individual CMP types, the F-statistics were as follows: 20 to 31 for CBP, 20 to 24 for CHP, 20 to 23 for CKP, and 20 to 29 for CNoSP. In terms of individual dementia types, the F-statistics ranged from 20 to 232 for dementia, 20 to 1,820 for AD, 20 to 257 for FTD, and 20 to 386 for VD. Additionally, SNP heritability (proportion of phenotypic variance explained by genetic variation) was estimated from 0.005 (SE = 0.008) for CMP to 0.002 (SE = 0.001) for dementia (Table 1, Supplementary Tables S4–S13).

Sample Size Calculations

The expected sample sizes of CMP and dementia were estimated to be 10,000 and 20,200, respectively. We compared the actual number of participants in the database with the anticipated sample size required for the study. Given that the actual number of participants in the database exceeds the requirements for the experiment, we can confirm that there is a sufficient sample size to support the research. In the mediation analysis, the exposure sample size for CBP was 117,404, while the outcome sample sizes were 31,968 for seven neuroimaging measurements (right hemisphere superior frontal gyrus volume, global volume of non-white matter hypointensities, global volume of optic chiasm, left hemisphere superior frontal gyrus area, right hemisphere pars opercularis area, right hemisphere superior frontal gyrus area, and global mean intensity of non-white matter hypointensities), and 31,967 for the remaining two measurements (left hemisphere caudal anterior cingulate cortex volume and right hemisphere transverse temporal gyrus area). The proportion of sample overlap between CBP and the structural neuroimaging measurements was 27.2%. The mean F-statistics for the CBP SNPs used as genetic instruments in the mediation Mendelian randomization analyses were 21.48 (Supplementary Table S13), indicating strong instruments. According to the formula proposed by Burgess et al,34 the relative bias attributable to sample overlap was approximately 1.26%, corresponding to an absolute bias of about 0.10. Given that the absolute bias was substantially smaller than the instrumental variable estimate, sample overlap did not represent a major source of bias in this study, and no additional correction was applied.

Forward MR Analyses: Causal Effects of CMP on Dementia

Using the IVW method, we identified a potential causal association between CBP and AD, with an OR of 2.287 (95% CI: 1.338 to 3.909, P = 0.002) (Supplementary Table S14). This association remained statistically significant after Bonferroni correction (P = 0.003125), supporting the robustness of our findings. CAUSE analysis further corroborated this result, yielding comparable effect estimates (gamma = 2.14) for CBP in the same direction and similar magnitude as the IVW analysis (Supplementary Table S15), thus satisfying the second MR assumption.

Conversely, the primary IVW analyses showed that CBP was not significantly associated with all-cause dementia (OR = 1.309, 95% CI: 0.841 to 2.038, P = 0.233), FTD (OR = 1.543, 95% CI: 0.885 to 2.69, P = 0.126), or VD (OR = 0.795, 95% CI: 0.305 to 2.069, P = 0.638) (Supplementary Table S14). Further, genetic liability to CHP, CKP, and CNoSP showed no significant association with any of the four types of dementia (Supplemental Figures S1–S8).

Reverse MR Analyses: Causal Effects of Dementia on CMP

In all reverse MR analyses, the primary IVW analyses produced P-values greater than 0.05, indicating no significant effects of genetic liability for the four types of dementia (all-cause dementia, AD, FTD, and VD) on the onset of the four kinds of CMP (CBP, CHP, CKP, and CNoSP) (Supplementary Table S16). Given these null findings, further validation using the CAUSE method was deemed unnecessary. Therefore, omitting the CAUSE analysis did not compromise the validity or robustness of our results and conclusions (Supplemental Figures S1–S8).

Sensitivity Analysis

In forward MR analyses, no significant causal association between CBP and AD was found using the MR-Egger, weighted median, simple mode, or weighted mode methods. However, the BWMR analysis did reveal a significant causal relationship between CBP and AD (OR = 2.43, 95% CI: 1.362 to 4.336, P = 0.003). Cochran’s Q test showed no heterogeneity (Q = 31, P = 0.364, primary IVW analysis), and MR-Egger intercepts suggested no horizontal pleiotropy (P = 0.091). Furthermore, MR pleiotropy residual sum and outlier tests (P = 0.005 for the main MR result, P = 0.374 for the global test) and leave-one-out analysis supported the main results (Supplemental Table S14).

The reverse MR analyses, using MR-Egger, weighted median, simple mode, weighted mode, and BWMR methods, consistently found no significant causal association between the four types of dementia and the onset of CMP (Supplemental Table S16). Hence, all sensitivity analyses satisfied the third MR assumption, demonstrating the reliability of our findings.

MVMR Analysis

For results of multiple genetic instruments, a statistically significant association between CBP and AD persisted after adjusting for CNoSP (OR = 2.246, 95% CI: 1.149 to 4.389; P = 0.018), CHP (OR = 2.279, 95% CI: 1.238 to 4.196; P = 0.0082), and CKP (OR 2.382, 95% CI: 1.303 to 4.354; P = 0.0048). Moreover, after adjusting for CNoSP, CHP, and CKP, the multivariable estimate indicated statistical significance, as evidenced by the result (OR = 2.138, 95% CI: 1.012 to 4.515; P = 0.0464). CBP had a significant positive causal effect on AD, consistent with that observed in the forward MR analysis. However, when CBP was combined with CNoSP, CHP, and CKP into three pain exposure groups, the lack of SNPs across these pain sites led to insufficient overlap of IVs, resulting in statistically non-significant associations for all three exposure-outcome combinations (Supplementary Table S17).

Mediation Analysis

Considering the significant causal association between CBP and AD, we conducted a mediation analysis to explore brain structure imaging measurements between their relationship. Our findings indicate that CBP leads to increase or atrophy in several cortical brain regions, including the left hemisphere caudal anterior cingulate cortex volume (OR = 1.788, 95% CI: 1.223 to 2.613; P = 0.0027), the right hemisphere superior frontal gyrus volume (OR = 0.615, 95% CI: 0.421 to 0.899; P = 0.0121), the left hemisphere superior frontal gyrus area (OR = 0.599, 95% CI: 0.385 to 0.933; P = 0.0234), the right hemisphere pars opercularis area (OR = 0.641, 95% CI: 0.411 to 0.999; P = 0.0498), the right hemisphere superior frontal gyrus area (OR = 0.579, 95% CI: 0.396 to 0.847; P = 0.0048), and the right hemisphere transverse temporal gyrus area (OR = 0.570, 95% CI: 0.383 to 0.849; P = 0.0057). CBP is associated with atrophy in a specific subcortical brain region, the global volume of optic chiasm (OR = 0.628, 95% CI: 0.429 to 0.917; P = 0.0162). Additionally, CBP correlates with increased measures in two subcortical brain regions: the global volume of non-white matter hypointensities (OR = 1.715, 95% CI: 1.172 to 2.510; P = 0.0055) and the global mean intensity of non-white matter hypointensities (OR = 1.788, 95% CI: 1.223 to 2.613; P = 0.0027). The sensitivity analyses for the association between the primary CBP and cortical brain regions indicate that Cochran’s Q test, MR-Egger intercepts, MR-PRESSO (pleiotropy residual sum and outlier) tests, and leave-one-out analysis all support the main findings (Supplementary Table S18). However, in the second step, none of the nine brain regions mentioned above were significantly casually associated with AD, so we did not proceed with the calculation of indirect effect and standard error (Supplementary Table S18, Supplemental Figures S9–S12).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first MR study to systematically investigate bidirectional causal associations between various CMP conditions and dementia subtypes. Utilizing genetic data from over one million individuals, we identified a significant causal effect of CBP on AD risk, with affected individuals showing 2.3-fold higher odds compared to those without pain. However, given the wide confidence interval and the null results from sensitivity analyses including MR-Egger, this finding warrants cautious interpretation and requires validation in independent cohorts. Furthermore, our research indicates that CBP is associated with alterations in nine cortical and subcortical brain structures. Nevertheless, neuroimaging evidence suggests that these structural changes do not primarily mediate this relationship. In contrast, no causal associations were found between CHP, CKP, and CNoSP and all-cause dementia, AD, FTD, or VD. Additionally, genetic predisposition to four types of dementia showed no significant impact on CMP.

Longitudinal studies indicated that older adults with chronic pain might experience accelerated development of AD, reporting hazard ratios ranging from 1.23 to 2.2, which partially align with our findings.45,46 The underlying mechanisms likely involve multiple pathways: (1) dysfunction of the locus coeruleus-noradrenergic system,16 (2) microglia-mediated neuroinflammation in pain- and cognition-related brain regions,47 and (3) subsequent hippocampal atrophy.16 Peripheral inflammation-induced neuroinflammation can disrupt the blood-brain barrier, activate glial cells linked to systemic immune responses, and affect autonomic nerves through the organ-brain axis, leading to cognitive decline.48 Chronic pain can also lead to physical inactivity, depression, and sleep deprivation, which are known risk factors for cognitive decline.49–51 All these factors contribute to an elevated dementia risk, as also identified by the Rotterdam Study Basic Dementia Risk Model.52

Interestingly, CBP demonstrates a significant causal association with AD, while other CMP types did not significantly increase the risk of the four types of dementia. This difference may be attributed to CBP involving a larger painful area than other types of CMP, similar to multisite pain. A recent prospective cohort study revealed a dose-response relationship between the number of painful sites and dementia risk, suggesting that more painful sites increase the risk of dementia.53 Furthermore, cognitive decline and hippocampal atrophy may interact biologically, potentially explaining the heightened risk of dementia with multisite chronic pain.54

Notably, individuals with CBP demonstrate measurable cognitive deficits, particularly problem-solving, information processing speed, working memory, and delayed memory.55 Among pain-related symptoms, pain interference (ie., the degree to which pain interferes with an individual’s daily activities) and pain tolerance levels were associated with a higher risk of developing dementia and cognitive impairment instead of pain intensity.56–58 However, it remains unclear why CBP specifically raises the risk of AD but no other types of dementia. Given the high prevalence of CBP and its associated risk of cognitive decline, future research should focus on CBP rehabilitation in older individuals to reduce their risk of accelerated cognitive decline.

In our study, we found that genetic predisposition to CHP, CKP, and CNoSP does not increase dementia risk. However, it is noteworthy that environmental factors can influence gene expression. Factors like hearing loss, vision problems, physical inactivity, or social isolation secondary to CMP could increase dementia risk.59–61 Possessing more painful sites might increase the risk of social isolation, depression, sleep problems, or the use of medication like opioids, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, or anticholinergics, which may increase dementia risk alongside the effects of chronic pain on the brain.54,62–65 CMP-related psychosocial issues are also known risk factors for dementia.66 While MR may not be able to address this issue, future research should consider the role of epigenetic factors and multisite chronic pain. Although our MR analyses suggest that CHP, CKP, and CNoSP do not increase the risk of dementia, it is important to effectively prevent and manage these types of CMP. Special attention should be given to older adults, as CMP is common in this population.

Although this study found no significant causal association between dementia and CMP, their association remains noteworthy. In individuals with dementia, pain may significantly impact executive functions and daily life.67 Research indicates that both conditions independently contribute to gray matter atrophy.68 A retrospective cohort study reported that 50% of patients with dementia had at least one pain-related diagnosis, suggesting an interaction between the two conditions.4 Chronic pain significantly affects psychology, mortality, quality of life, and antipsychotic prescriptions in patients with dementia, contributing to behavioral changes among older residents in care facilities.69,70 It is known that chronic pain may serve as a prodromal sign of dementia rather than a direct cause.71 As dementia progresses, these individuals often have diminished pain perception caused by structural changes in prefrontal areas.72 One study indicated that communication barriers in AD patients impede their ability to accurately express pain, which may result in low analgesic use.73 Another explanation for diminished pain sensation in AD patients is dysfunction in pain-processing brain regions and circuits, such as damage to the hypothalamus.74,75 Additionally, an animal study suggested that the absence of toll-like receptor 4 in a specific subset of microglia in a transgenic Alzheimer’s mouse model may activate microglia, release inflammatory mediators, and trigger neuroinflammation, which could impair pain processing and reduce sensitivity to inflammatory pain.76 Therefore, clinicians should pay more attention to the presence of CMP in people with dementia and treat them accordingly.

Our mediation analysis investigated potential neuroanatomical pathways underlying the causal relationship between CBP and AD. We identified several CBP-related structural alterations, including cortical atrophy in regions responsible for executive function and sensory processing. These affected areas encompassed the right pars opercularis (involved in sensorimotor integration), superior frontal gyrus, and transverse temporal cortex (primary auditory cortex), consistent with established patterns of pain-related gray matter reduction.77 Subcortically, CBP correlated with optic chiasm volume reduction, implying visual pathway involvement in chronic pain processing.78 Conversely, anterior cingulate cortex volume increases were observed, potentially indicating neuroinflammatory responses or glial activation.79 Additionally, CBP was associated with an increase in the global volume and mean intensity of non-white matter hypointensities, suggesting underlying vascular pathology such as cerebral microbleeds.80 However, none of these CBP-associated brain changes demonstrated a significant causal effect on AD in the second step of our mediation model. This suggests that the CBP-AD pathway may operate through other mechanisms not captured by structural magnetic resonance imaging. Potential pathways might include chronic neuroinflammation, metabolic dysregulation, functional connectivity changes, or direct influences on protein aggregation processes that preceding macroscopic structural changes.49

This study has several strengths. First, our study combined data from the UK Biobank and the FinnGen dataset, offering a two-sample approach with a large sample size and broader generalizability.21 Second, the CAUSE analysis was used to incorporate information from all genetic variants, not just those most strongly associated with exposures and outcomes, helping to reduce biased estimates.81 Third, multiple sensitivity analyses were conducted to enhance the reliability of the MR findings.

Our study has several limitations. First, while MR estimates are useful for assessing the long-term effects of risk factors across an individual’s lifetime, various epigenetic factors can affect gene expression. However, the UK Biobank and FinnGen do not have data on epigenetic factors. Further, the lack of relevant CMP data in other databases (eg., the Epigenome-Wide Association Study Atlas or the ArrayExpress dataset) prevented us from conducting an Environmental Wide Association Study on CMP and dementia.82,83 This limitation highlights the need for more comprehensive data collection to better understand these complex relationships. Second, due to the absence of data on pain intensity and duration for individuals with CMP, our findings should be interpreted with caution, as higher pain intensity or prolonged chronicity could elevate the risk of developing dementia.84 Third, this MR study relied primarily on data from individuals of European ancestry, which may limit the applicability of the findings to other ancestries. Fourth, the mediation analysis was constrained by the sample size of the available neuroimaging data. Compared to the large-scale GWAS datasets for CMP and dementia, the smaller neuroimaging cohort may have lacked the statistical power required to detect subtle mediating effects.

Conclusion

Our research supports a directional association wherein genetically proxied CBP increases the AD risk, alongside alterations in nine cortical and subcortical brain structures. However, the exact magnitude of this risk should be interpreted with caution due to methodological constraints, and the relationship between CBP and AD does not appear to be predominantly mediated by these macroscopic structural alterations. Although the genetic proxies for CHP, CKP, and CNoSP did not directly increase the risk of dementia in our analyses, it remains crucial to address various CMP-related conditions that could accelerate cognitive decline through non-genetic or environmental pathways. While there is no evidence that dementia causes CMP genetically, individuals with dementia often struggle to articulate their pain, which can be profoundly debilitating. Thus, from a hypothesis-generating perspective, these genetic evidence-informed findings highlight that the cognitive and neurobiological aspects of CMP warrant increased clinical attention and rigorous future longitudinal investigation.
