Understanding Risk in Emergent Reconstructive Flap Surgery
Authors: Aulon Jerliu, Lauren Harrison, Charles Castiglione, Alan Babigian, Robert J. McLoughlin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Flap-based reconstruction is critical for soft tissue defect coverage, but risks in emergency cases are poorly defined. This study compared postoperative outcomes of emergency versus elective myocutaneous and fasciocutaneous flap reconstructions.Methods:We conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study of adults undergoing flap reconstruction from 2012 to 2023 in the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program database. Patients were categorized as emergency or elective. Primary outcomes included 30-day mortality, major complications, surgical site infections, and overall complications. Multivariable logistic regression adjusted for demographics, comorbidities, nutritional status, and physiological markers.Results:Among 12,263 patients, 1570 (12.8%) had emergency reconstructions. Emergency patients more often presented with sepsis, anemia, functional dependence, and American Society of Anesthesiologists class III–V. Unadjusted analysis showed that emergency status was associated with higher rates of mortality (odds ratio [OR] 4.77, 95% confidence interval [CI] 3.01–7.55, P < 0.001), major complications (OR 2.44, 95% CI 1.78–3.35, P < 0.001), and any complication (OR 1.44, 95% CI 1.26–1.64, P < 0.001). Surgical site infection rates were lower (OR 0.83, 95% CI 0.70–1.00, P = 0.048) but not significant after adjustment. After risk adjustment, emergency status remained independently associated with increased mortality (adjusted OR 14.78, 95% CI 2.09–104.35, P = 0.007) and major complications (adjusted OR 3.21, 95% CI 1.25–8.24, P = 0.015) but not with surgical site infection or overall complication rate.Conclusions:Emergency flap reconstruction is independently linked to higher short-term mortality and major complications compared with elective cases, even after accounting for comorbidities and physiological status. These findings underscore the need for careful perioperative planning, triage, and risk mitigation strategies in urgent settings.

Takeaways

Question: How do outcomes of emergent flap-based soft tissue reconstructions compare to elective cases?

Findings: In this large, risk-adjusted national analysis of 12,263 patients undergoing myocutaneous or fasciocutaneous flap reconstruction, emergency status was independently associated with significantly higher 30-day mortality and major complication rates. Fasciocutaneous flaps, although historically less used in emergencies, demonstrated shorter operative times and comparable outcomes to myocutaneous flaps in urgent cases.

Meaning: Emergent flap reconstruction carries an inherently higher risk, even after controlling for comorbidities and physiological compromise. These findings highlight the need for enhanced perioperative planning and suggest that fasciocutaneous flaps may be a viable option in select emergency scenarios.

INTRODUCTION

Flap-based reconstruction addresses soft tissue defects from trauma, oncological resection, chronic wounds, or infections.1,2 The conditions under which these procedures are performed—electively or emergently—may significantly affect recovery, postoperative morbidity, and healthcare resource use.3 Elective flap reconstructions, such as in oncological breast and head and neck cases, have demonstrated high success rates when performed under ideal conditions.

By contrast, emergency flap reconstruction poses substantial clinical challenges. Although emergency cases represent approximately 11% of surgical admissions, they account for up to 50% of surgical mortality.3,4 Emergent operations show nearly a 3-fold increase in perioperative mortality, even after adjustment for comorbidities and patient risk profiles.3,5,6 For example, mortality after emergency hernia repair is reported to be as much as 26 times higher than in elective cases.6 In flap surgery specifically, emergent procedures are often performed in the context of trauma or infection, and carry increased rates of postoperative complications and mortality.7,8

Key modifiable risk factors, including malnutrition, hypoalbuminemia, anemia, and sepsis, have been repeatedly associated with adverse outcomes after flap reconstruction, and these risk factors often cannot be changed before an emergent surgery.9,10 Traditionally, myocutaneous flaps were preferred in contaminated fields for presumed infection resistance, whereas fasciocutaneous flaps were favored electively for aesthetic and donor-site advantages.11,12 However, evidence from comparative studies and meta-analyses challenges this dogma, demonstrating equivalent or superior outcomes with fasciocutaneous flaps in terms of wound durability, reinfection resistance, and reoperation rates.12,13

Despite evolving insights, few large-scale studies compare emergency and elective flap reconstructions across national datasets or control for detailed physiological and nutritional risk factors. The American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (ACS NSQIP) provides a robust, multi-institutional platform for such analyses due to its rigorously audited, risk-adjusted clinical data.14–16

We hypothesized that emergency status is independently associated with significantly increased risk of morbidity and mortality. We predicted that this risk would persist after adjusting for comorbidities, nutritional status (eg, body mass index [BMI], hypoalbuminemia), and physiological derangement (eg, sepsis, anemia).

METHODS

Data Source and Case Selection

We conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study using the ACS NSQIP Participant Use Files from 2012 to 2023. National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (NSQIP) is a validated database of more than 150 perioperative variables from 600+ US hospitals. The dataset includes information on demographics, comorbidities, intraoperative factors, and 30-day postoperative complications.

Cases were identified using the following Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes for myocutaneous and fasciocutaneous flap reconstruction: 15733 (head and neck), 15734 (trunk), 15736 (trochanteric region), 15738 (lower extremity), 15756 (fasciocutaneous free flap), 15757 (free muscle flap), and 15758 (free composite tissue flap). Emergency status was derived from NSQIP variables Case Type and Elective Surgery (ElectSurg), forming a binary classification: “elective” or “urgent/emergent.”

This study used deidentified public data and was therefore exempt from institutional review board oversight. The ACS NSQIP and the hospitals participating in the ACS NSQIP are the sources of the data used herein; they have not been verified and are not responsible for the statistical validity of the data analysis or the conclusions derived by the authors.

Independent and Dependent Variables

Flaps were categorized as myocutaneous or fasciocutaneous using CPT codes. Subgroup analyses evaluated operative time and flap use in both emergency and elective cohorts. Comparisons of mean operative time and proportional use were conducted using χ2 and t tests.

Primary outcomes included 30-day mortality, any postoperative complication (composite endpoint created to reflect the presence of a complication in the 30 d after surgery), major complication (composite endpoint defined as myocardial infarction, cardiac arrest requiring cardiopulmonary resuscitation, stroke or cerebrovascular accident, or pulmonary embolism), surgical site infection (SSI) (composite end point created to include superficial, deep, or organ-space infections), unplanned return to the operating room, and hospital readmission within 30 days. Secondary outcomes included sepsis, pneumonia, urinary tract infection, prolonged mechanical ventilation exceeding 48 hours, total hospital length of stay, and operative time in minutes.

Covariates for risk adjustment included demographics (age, sex, race, ethnicity), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) class (I–V), functional status, diabetes (insulin and non-insulin dependent), dialysis, smoking, steroid use, neurological dysfunction, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), chronic heart failure (within 30 d), bleeding disorders, albumin (<2.5, 2.5–3.5, ≥3.5 g/dL), hematocrit (<30% versus ≥30%), platelet count (<150 versus ≥150 × 10⁹/L), white blood cell count (<4, 4–11, ≥11 × 10⁹ cells/L), alcohol use (>2 drinks/d), and BMI (underweight <18.5 kg/m2, healthy 18.5–24.9 kg/m2, overweight 25.0–29.9 kg/m2, obese ≥30 kg/m2).

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated to compare demographic and clinical characteristics between emergency and elective cases using χ2 tests for categorical variables and Student t tests for continuous variables. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Univariate logistic regression models were first used to evaluate the crude association between emergency case status and each outcome of interest, including 30-day mortality, major adverse events, SSI, and complications. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and P values were reported. Multivariable logistic regression model was used to assess the independent association between emergency status and the following outcomes: 30-day mortality, any adverse event, major complication, and wound infection. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% CIs were reported. The adjusted model controlled for age (continuous), sex, race, ethnicity, more than 2 alcohol drinks per day in 2 weeks before admission, ASA status, smoking, steroid use, cardiac comorbidity, history of COPD, functional status, bleeding disorder, albumin, platelets, hematocrit, neurological dysfunction, ascites, and dialysis. Model discrimination was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, and for specification errors.

RESULTS

Patient Cohort and Demographics

A total of 12,263 patients undergoing myocutaneous or fasciocutaneous flap reconstruction were identified between 2012 and 2023 in the ACS NSQIP database. Of these, 10,693 (87.2%) patients underwent elective procedures, whereas 1570 (12.8%) underwent emergency flap reconstruction. Emergency patients had greater physiological derangement, including higher rates of sepsis, anemia (hematocrit <30%), hypoalbuminemia (<3.5 g/dL), thrombocytopenia, abnormal white blood cell counts, greater functional dependence, and ASA class III or higher. Patients undergoing emergent surgery were slightly older (mean age 57.3 versus 55.8 y, P = 0.001) and were less likely to be woman (38.3% versus 47.1%, P < 0.001). They were more likely to be classified as ASA class III–IV (78.8% versus 60.4%, P < 0.001), and carry a diagnosis of insulin-dependent diabetes (15.6% versus 7.2%, P < 0.001), severe COPD (7.3% versus 4.3%, P < 0.001), congestive heart failure (3.9% versus 1.6%, P < 0.001), dialysis dependence (4.7% versus 0.9%, P < 0.001), and disseminated cancer (5.3% versus 3.1%, P < 0.001) (Table 1). Nutritional compromise and frailty were also more common in the emergency group, with low albumin levels (<3.5 g/dL) present in 44.9% of patients versus 11.4% in the ElectSurg group (P < 0.001). Patients in the emergency surgery group were more likely to be anemic compared with those in the ElectSurg group, with 36.9% versus 7.0% (P < 0.001) having a hemoglobin level less than 30%, respectively (Table 2). They also tended to have an underweight BMI, 4.9% versus 2.9% (P < 0.001), and dependent functional status, 17% versus 9% (P < 0.001). Preoperative systemic inflammation was significantly more prevalent in the emergency cohort, with systemic inflammatory response syndrome, sepsis, and septic shock observed in 7.2%, 5.0%, and 0.5% of patients, respectively, compared with 1.3%, 0.7%, and 0.0% in the elective group (P < 0.001).

Operative and Hospital Course

Emergency cases had shorter operative time (223.7 versus 257.7 min, P < 0.001) but significantly longer hospital stay (17.8 versus 6.5 d, P < 0.001). Time from surgery to discharge was unsurprisingly prolonged in emergency patients (10.4 versus 5.9 d, P < 0.001), and a greater proportion remained hospitalized beyond 30 days (7.2% versus 2.4%, P < 0.001). Additionally, emergency cases had higher rates of return to the operating room (12.5% versus 9.1%, P < 0.001) and 30-day readmission (10.9% versus 8.3%, P = 0.002) (Table 3).

Flap Type and Operative Time

We performed stratified analyses by flap type and case urgency to compare outcomes between elective and emergency procedures, using separate models for myocutaneous and fasciocutaneous reconstructions.

In emergency procedures, myocutaneous flaps were used more often than fasciocutaneous flaps (54.2% versus 45.8%). Fasciocutaneous flaps had shorter operative time (400.9 versus 463.4 min) (Fig. 1). Fasciocutaneous flaps had shorter hospital stays (16.3 versus 19.7 d) and time to discharge (10.9 versus 12.9 d), although not statistically significant (P = 0.0600 and P = 0.0603, respectively). Postoperative complication rates, including return to the operating room (18.1% versus 16.6%) and SSI (11.0% versus 9.2%), were comparable among flap types. Major complications were rare in both groups (0.7% versus 0.0%) (Table 4).

Postoperative Complications

Emergency patients had significantly higher complication rates. Any complication occurred in 21.7% versus 16.2% patients (P < 0.001); major complications occurred in 3.4% versus 1.4% patients (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2). Emergency cases had higher rates of sepsis (5.6% versus 3.0%), pneumonia (2.6% versus 1.8%), prolonged ventilation more than 48 hours (3.4% versus 1.4%), unplanned intubation (2.4% versus 1.0%), deep vein thrombosis (2.0% versus 0.8%), and urinary tract infection (2.5% versus 1.1%) (all P < 0.001 except pneumonia, P = 0.031). SSI occurred more often in emergency cases (7.4% versus 6.0%, P = 0.028), although there was no statistically significant difference for deep and organ-space infections (Table 7).

Mortality and Disposition

The 30-day mortality rate was low but significantly higher in emergency cases compared with elective cases (1.7% versus 0.4%, P < 0.001). Consistent with the increased morbidity, a greater proportion of patients requiring emergency surgery remained hospitalized beyond 30 days (P < 0.001), highlighting the severe and complex nature of these clinical scenarios. Additionally, more patients required return to the operating room in the emergent setting when compared with the elective setting (12.5% versus 9.1%, P < 0.001).

Univariate and Multivariate Logistic Regression Analyses

Unadjusted analyses showed that emergency status increased odds of 30-day mortality (OR 4.77, 95% CI 3.01–7.55, P < 0.001), major complications (OR 2.44), and any complication (OR 1.44) (all P < 0.001) (Fig. 3). SSI was less likely in emergency cases on univariate analysis (OR 0.83, 95% CI 0.69–1.00, P = 0.048).

Forest plot showing unadjusted and adjusted ORs for key outcomes. Both unadjusted and adjusted models highlight the increased risk of adverse events in emergency reconstructions.

After adjustment for relevant covariates, emergency status remained independently associated with both 30-day mortality and major complications (Table 6). Adjusted odds of 30-day mortality were significantly higher in emergency cases than elective cases (aOR 14.78, 95% CI 2.09–104.35, P = 0.007). Similarly, major complications remained elevated (aOR 3.21, 95% CI 1.25–8.24, P = 0.015). However, emergency status was not associated with developing any complication (aOR 1.21, 95% CI 0.84–1.76, P = 0.308) or SSI (aOR 0.76, 95% CI 0.41–1.39, P = 0.370).

DISCUSSION

This study represented the largest risk-adjusted national comparison of emergency versus elective myocutaneous and fasciocutaneous flap reconstruction using NSQIP data to date. We found that emergency case status was independently associated with significantly increased odds of 30-day mortality and major complications, even after controlling for physiological derangement and baseline comorbidities. Because baseline risk profiles differed markedly between elective and emergency cases, our findings should not be interpreted as implying clinical equivalence; rather, they isolate the incremental hazard attributable to emergent timing itself. Unadjusted mortality among emergency patients was 1.7% versus 0.4% for elective cases (OR 4.77), and major complication rates were 3.4% versus 1.4% (OR 2.44). On multivariable analysis, emergency status remained a strong independent predictor of mortality (aOR 14.78, 95% CI 2.09–104.35, P = 0.007) and major complications (aOR 3.21, P = 0.015).

These findings align with previous literature across multiple surgical disciplines demonstrating that emergent procedures are associated with significantly worse perioperative outcomes.3,4 Our results align with NSQIP studies showing operative urgency independently increases morbidity and mortality.5,6 In our cohort, emergency patients exhibited higher rates of preoperative sepsis, anemia, functional dependence, and ASA class III–V status—all known predictors of adverse surgical outcomes.7,9,10,17 These factors likely contributed to the underlying disease processes leading patients to require flap reconstruction of wounds and in turn made their repair of higher risk.

Interestingly, SSI did not remain significantly associated with emergency status in multivariable analysis. Although the raw SSI rate was slightly higher in emergency cases (7.4% versus 6.0%, P = 0.028), the unadjusted OR paradoxically favored lower SSI risk (OR 0.83, P = 0.048). This discrepancy likely reflects baseline clinical differences between groups, and the association disappeared after adjusting for potential confounders (aOR 0.76, P = 0.370). This reinforces previous research suggesting that SSI risk in flap reconstruction is more closely linked to factors such as nutritional status, immunosuppression, and wound classification rather than surgical timing alone.8 Emergency flap-based reconstruction is frequently performed in the setting of trauma or infection, making the wound class contaminated7,8; thus, postoperative infections may be underreported or classified differently than in clean cases. Additionally, the routine use of prophylactic antibiotics and prompt debridement with early coverage may mitigate infection risk in these high-risk emergent settings.11,12

Emergency cases demonstrated a significantly higher rate of return to the operating room compared with elective cases (12.5% versus 9.1%, P < 0.001). Although this likely reflects the greater physiological burden and postoperative complexity in emergency patients, it is important to recognize that not all reoperations necessarily indicate complications. In the emergency setting, return to the operating room may be part of a planned, staged surgical approach—such as serial debridement or secondary closure—which is often required due to the acuity and contamination of the wound at presentation. Because NSQIP does not differentiate between planned and unplanned reoperations, this may lead to overestimation of complication rates in the emergency cohort.

Our flap-specific subgroup analysis revealed among emergency cases alone, fasciocutaneous flaps accounted for approximately 46% of reconstructions and were associated with significantly shorter operative times compared with myocutaneous flaps (400.9 versus 463.4 min, P = 0.0086). This likely reflects case-selection bias: in unstable emergency settings, surgeons favor smaller, technically simpler fasciocutaneous flaps to expedite coverage and minimize anesthetic time. Despite these time differences, complication rates did not significantly differ by flap type, reinforcing growing literature suggesting that fasciocutaneous flaps may be a viable, and underused, option even in urgent settings.11–13 This is especially important, as decreased operative times in an emergent setting are favored. These results underscore the need to reconsider historical dogma that muscle-based flaps are superior in emergency or contaminated scenarios.13

Another key contributor to worse outcomes in emergency patients was the higher prevalence of systemic frailty. Rates of dialysis dependence, disseminated cancer, and functional dependence were all significantly elevated in the emergency group—each of which was independently associated with poor postoperative outcomes.7,10 Frailty has emerged as a powerful predictor of morbidity and mortality than traditional metrics such as age or BMI, particularly when compounded by hypoalbuminemia—an association that has been demonstrated in analyses of flap reconstruction.5,17,18

This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective design. Although the ACS NSQIP database offers a robust and audited national dataset, it lacks granularity on flap-specific technical variables that may influence outcomes—such as flap size, donor-site selection, perforator anatomy, method of inset, degree of tissue contamination, and timing relative to wound debridement.19 Additionally, CPT codes cannot distinguish island, propeller, or other random-pattern designs, nor can they identify adjunct split-thickness skin grafting performed with muscle flaps; these procedures may be grouped under broader codes, limiting subtype precision. Details regarding defect complexity and intraoperative decision-making are also unavailable, limiting our ability to contextualize certain outcome differences. Furthermore, NSQIP lacks operative-note detail, and thus could not determine the specific clinical indication prompting each reconstruction. Surgeon-level factors—such as microsurgical experience and operative setting—could not be controlled for, despite their known influence on outcomes.

Furthermore, NSQIP captures only 30-day postoperative outcomes, precluding analysis of long-term complications such as flap viability, partial flap loss, functional recovery, donor-site morbidity, or chronic wound recurrence. Additionally, although our multivariable models adjusted for a comprehensive set of covariates, residual confounding remains possible due to unmeasured variables, such as timing and duration of antibiotic use, use of negative pressure wound therapy, or degree of preoperative resuscitation in emergency cases. Additionally, incomplete reporting in some variables may confound the clinical picture; for example, preoperative albumin was not reported in approximately 54% of elective cases and 35% of emergency cases. Finally, the definition of “emergency” status in NSQIP was based on surgeon designation at the time of booking and may be applied inconsistently.

Despite these limitations, our study is strengthened by its large size, national scope, and rigorous risk adjustment using covariates. By leveraging over a decade of data and applying multivariable regression techniques, we were able to control for key confounders such as physiological derangement, nutritional status, frailty, and comorbidity burden.20 The inclusion of both myocutaneous and fasciocutaneous flap types across elective and emergency scenarios adds further generalizability. To our knowledge, this represents the largest risk-adjusted analysis to date comparing outcomes of emergency versus elective flap reconstruction at a national level.

As flap-based reconstruction becomes increasingly used in high-acuity settings, our findings provide critical data to guide clinical decision-making. Emergency flap reconstruction should be approached with heightened vigilance, proactive nutritional and physiological optimization, and multidisciplinary coordination. Fasciocutaneous flaps, historically reserved for elective settings, may offer a viable and underused alternative in urgent reconstructions, particularly given their favorable donor-site profile and emerging outcome data.

Future research should aim to validate these findings prospectively and investigate factors driving flap selection in emergency settings, including timing, staging, and functional outcomes. Improved capture of planned versus unplanned reoperations, as well as long-term complication and reconstructive success rates, will be essential to guide flap selection and perioperative optimization strategies.

CONCLUSIONS

Emergency myocutaneous and fasciocutaneous flap reconstructions carry high risk but are essential procedures, independently associated with increased short-term mortality and major complications. Even after adjusting for confounding variables, emergency status remained a strong predictor of adverse outcomes. Our stratified analysis found that fasciocutaneous flaps, used in nearly half of emergency cases, were associated with shorter operative times and similar complication rates compared with myocutaneous flaps, suggesting that they may be a viable and underused option in urgent settings. These findings can guide surgical planning and counseling, and highlight the need for future studies focused on long-term outcomes and targeted strategies to reduce risk in this vulnerable population.
