(Almost) Perfect Discrete Iterative Load Balancing
Petra Berenbrink, Robert Elsässer, Tom Friedetzky, Hamed Hosseinpour, Dominik Kaaser, Peter Kling, Thomas Sauerwald

Abstract
We consider discrete, iterative load balancing via matchings on arbitrary graphs. Initially each node holds a certain number of tokens, defining the load of the node, and the objective is to redistribute the tokens such that eventually each node has approximately the same number of tokens. We present results for a general class of simple local balancing schemes where the tokens are balanced via matchings. In each round the process averages the tokens of any two matched nodes. If the sum of their tokens is odd, the node to receive the one excess token is selected at random. Our class covers three popular models: in the matching model a new matching is generated randomly in each round, in the balancing circuit model a fixed sequence of matchings is applied periodically, and in the asynchronous model the load is balanced over a randomly chosen edge. We measure the quality of a load vector by its discrepancy, defined as the difference between the maximum and minimum load across all nodes. As our main result we show that with high probability our discrete balancing scheme reaches a discrepancy of 3 3 in a number of rounds which asymptotically matches the spectral bound for continuous load balancing with fractional load. This result improves and tightens a long line of previous works, by not only achieving a small constant discrepancy (instead of a non-explicit, large constant) but also holding for arbitrary instead of regular graphs. The result also demonstrates that in the general model we consider, discrete load balancing is no harder than continuous load balancing.

Introduction

In load balancing the goal is to distribute a set of jobs to multiple computing resources. Many large-scale applications use some form of decentralized load balancing, with examples including balancing search engine queries, distributing internet traffic, updating distributed databases, or performing numerical simulations.

In a classical model for parallel and distributed computing,
n

eventually each node has approximately the same number of tokens, thereby minimizing the
discrepancy

Our Results.

We focus on the matching model, as it requires less communication per round than the diffusion model; yet it tends to perform better in theory and practice
[
32

34

In the former model a new matching is generated randomly in each round, while in the latter model a fixed sequence of matchings is applied periodically. We also consider an
“asynchronous”

The very simple load balancing process works as follows.
For each edge in the matching, the two incident nodes average their load as much as possible; in case their total load is odd, the so-called excess token is assigned randomly to either node. This resembles randomized rounding in optimization, which leads to the question of how well the discrete setting can approximate the continuous one with this randomized strategy.
Note that the algorithm considered in this paper is natural and simple, that is, straightforward to implement. Moreover it is strictly distributed in the sense that only local information based on a node and its neighbours are used. Due to the nature of the communication patterns (matchings) there is no multi-port communication, another nod toward real-world constraints.

As is standard in the area of discrete load balancing, we use a very strict notion of smoothness of a load vector called

We will use uppercase letters for random variables and matrices, but lowercase letters for fixed outcomes. We define

To state the next definitions and results more formally, we adopt the notation from

Following

Further, we define the product of all matching matrices from time

as

\mathbf{M}^{[t_{1},t_{2}]}:=\prod_{s=t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathbf{M}^{(s)}
(if

\mathbf{M}^{[t_{1},t_{2}]}
is the identity matrix). For simplicity, we use the same symbol
\mathbf{M}^{(t)}
for both the matching of round

\mathbf{M}
for matchings. Later we also use
\mathbf{M}^{[t_{1}+1,t_{2}]}_{u,D}:=\sum_{v\in D}\mathbf{M}^{[t_{1}+1,t_{2}]}_{u,v}
. Finally,
\mathbf{M}^{[t_{1},t_{2}]}_{u,.}
denotes the row of the matrix

By

Definition 2.1
(
[

).

A fixed sequence of matchings
\left(\mathbf{M}^{(s)}\right)_{s=1}^{t}
,

is called

The expression

Hence the definition above states that the matching sequence is sufficient to balance the load up to

In general the matching sequence can be generated deterministically or randomly, and our main result will cover both cases.
The next definition states the properties of the matchings which we require for our main result.

Definition 2.2
.

A sequence of matchings
\left(\mathbf{M}^{(s)}\right)_{s=1}^{\infty}
is called
(\tau_{\mathrm{global}},\tau_{\mathrm{local}})
-

if

(2.1)

\displaystyle\inf_{t\in\N_{0}}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{u\in V}\left\|\mathbf{M}_{u,\cdot}^{[t+1,t+\tau_{\mathrm{global}}]}-\vec{\frac{1}{n}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n^{7}}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{1}{n^{3}},

(2.2)

\displaystyle\inf_{t\in\N_{0}}\min_{u\in V}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left\|\mathbf{M}_{u,\cdot}^{[t+1,t+\tau_{\mathrm{local}}]}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\log^{10}(n)}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{1}{\log^{11}(n)}.

Since each token is performing a random walk according to the matching sequence (
Lemma

9.1
), we can interpret these events in terms of a distribution of a token performing a time-inhomogeneous random walk and its

-distance to the stationary (i.e, uniform) distribution. The event in

2.1

This basically corresponds to a complete, i.e., “global” mixing of a random walk, since it holds for

With regards to the event in
Eq.

2.2

\tau_{\mathrm{local}}
rounds, and this condition holds only “locally”, i.e., from a specific node

Using standard spectral techniques, for the random matching model we have
\tau_{\mathrm{global}}=O(\log(n)/(1-\lambda))
and
\tau_{\mathrm{local}}=O(\log\log(n)/(1-\lambda))
(assuming

Main Results

Our main result is the following theorem; the proof can be found in
Section

8
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
G
be any undirected, connected graph on
n
nodes and consider any initial load vector
x^{(0)}\in\N_{0}^{n}
with
\discr(x^{(0)})\leq K
.
Assume our process balances the tokens via a
(\tau_{\mathrm{global}},\tau_{\mathrm{local}})
-good sequence of matchings
\left(\mathbf{M}^{(s)}\right)_{s=1}^{\infty}
.
Then there exists a time
\tau
with

\tau=O\left(\frac{\log(Kn)}{\log(n)}\cdot\tau_{\mathrm{global}}+\frac{\log(n)}{\log\log(n)}\cdot\tau_{\mathrm{local}}\right)

such that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\discr\left(X^{(\tau)}\right)\leq 4\,\right]\geq 1-\exp\left(-(1/200)\cdot\frac{\log(n)}{\log\log(n)}\right),

and for any constant
c>0
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\discr\left(X^{(\tau)}\right)\leq 3\,\right]\geq 1-\exp\left(-\log^{1-c}(n)\right).

Note that
Theorem

2.3
does not make any assumption regarding the provenance of the matching sequences; all we use is the abstract property of
(\tau_{\mathrm{global}},\tau_{\mathrm{local}})
-goodness.
We now provide details for three explicit ways to create such sequences, namely the
balancing circuit model
, the
random matching model
and the
asynchronous model

2.4
Comparison of our Results and Techniques with Previous Work

An important novelty of our approach is that our analysis framework seamlessly covers the balancing circuit model, the random matching model and the asynchronous (single edge) model. This is in contrast to previous works
[
32
,
31
,
34
,
22
]
, which either focus only on one specific matching model or provide tailored analyses for each of them. To unify the three models covered here, our analysis is based on a coarse (and local) and a fine (and global) mixing/balancing property of these models.

In comparison with
[
34
]
, the constant factors in both our running time and discrepancy are explicit and small. In addition to our analysis being tighter and simpler, we also obtain a larger success probability. We note that the tool box we use differs substantially from that of
[
34
]
, not least because the pièce de résistance of our analysis, getting from a large constant bound for the discrepancy down to a very small constant is not being done in
[
34
]
at all, and is one of the hardest problems we solve.
Finally, the results of
[
34
]
require regular graphs in the random matching, and a constant
\Delta
for the balancing circuit model. Our results are covering both of these models (and additionally the asynchronous model), and hold without any of these restrictions.

While our proof method yields more general and tighter results, we also believe that our proof is more intuitive and direct. The first and more straightforward step is to carefully bound the number of tokens in each possible subset
S\subseteq V
and then apply a union bound. This is based on a new Hoeffding-type concentration bound, which generalizes and tightens previous bounds
[
22
,
34
]
in that it works for
arbitrary
convex combinations of the load vector. This leaves us with only
O(n)
tokens to balance.

The second part of the analysis makes uses of our new height-sensitive process, which constraints the movement of tokens in such a way that their heights are non-increasing. Despite this restriction, we can prove that their movements are negatively associated. This property, together with the Hoeffding-type concentration, is then used in an involved analysis to show that eventually a discrepancy
4
is reached. An extra iteration of this method finally yields a discrepancy bound of
3
.

An Outline of the Analysis

In this section we give a more detailed outline of our analysis. First, in

These technical tools are crucial for our analysis and their proofs are considerably more challenging than deriving the discrepancy bounds using these tools. Additionally, we believe that some of these tools are of independent interest. At the end of this section, in

3.1
Our Technical Contributions

In order to prove small discrepancy bounds, we need to keep track of the number of tokens at a specific height. Thanks to the height-sensitive process defined earlier, the height of a token is non-increasing over time, and the sequence of locations of a token

4
Summary and Open Problems

In this paper we show that, for the matching model, discrete load balancing is as efficient and effective as continuous load balancing.
We showed that in the discrete setting with integer loads, a discrepancy of
3
is reached in a time that matches the standard spectral bound on the time needed by the continuous setting. In particular, this means that for expanders and a polynomial initial discrepancy, our load balancing schemes achieve a discrepancy of
3
using only
O(\log(n))=O(\operatorname{diam}(G))
rounds, which is optimal not only for a distributed but also a centralized setting.

As an improvement over previous works, our general result holds for a wider class of graphs (e.g., including non-regular graphs) and models (e.g., including the asynchronous model). Also the constants in our runtime as well as in the achieved discrepancy are explicit and small, compared to large and non-explicit constants in
[
34
]
.

At the heart of our analysis lie new association and concentration
results, which we believe to be of independent interest. As the most involved step, showing that
O(n)
tokens can be balanced with maximum load
O(1)
, which can be leveraged to prove a discrepancy of
3
for an arbitrary number of tokens. It should be noted that reaching a discrepancy of
1
needs
\Omega(n)
rounds for any sequence of matchings (see
[
29
]
). Hence one may wonder whether our discrepancy bound could be improved from
3
to
2
. We believe that this might be possible, but it will likely need stronger assumptions on the matchings than just being
(\tau_{\mathrm{global}},\tau_{\mathrm{local}})
-good.

5
Association and Concentration Results

In this section we show two main components our analysis; first, the negative association result about token movements and secondly, the Hoeffding-like concentration inequality.

5.1
Height-Sensitive Negative Association

Lemma 5.1
(Height-Sensitive Negative Association).

Consider any pair of rounds
0\leq t_{1}<t_{2}
, and let
(\mathbf{M}^{(s)})_{s=t_{1}}^{t_{2}}
be an arbitrary but fixed sequence of matchings. Further, let
w^{(t_{1})}=(w_{i})^{(t_{1})}_{i\in\mathcal{T}}
be a fixed location vector in round
t_{1}\geq 0
. Then for any subset of tokens
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
and any subset of nodes
D\subseteq V
, it holds that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\{W_{i}^{(t_{2})}\in D\}~\middle|~w^{(t_{1})}\,\right]\leq\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,W_{i}^{(t_{2})}\in D~\middle|~w^{(t_{1})}\,\right]=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbf{M}_{w_{i}^{(t_{1})},D}^{[t_{1}+1,t_{2}]}.

Proof 1
.

Throughout the proof we always assume that
w^{(t_{1})}
is fixed and we omit the conditioning.
Note that by
Lemma

9.1
, we have
\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,W_{i}^{(t_{2})}\in D\,\right]=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbf{M}_{w_{i}^{(t_{1})},D}^{[t_{1}+1,t_{2}]}
and therefore it only remains to prove the inequality

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\{W_{i}^{(t_{2})}\in D\}\,\right]\leq\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbf{M}_{w_{i}^{(t_{1})},D}^{[t_{1}+1,t_{2}]}.

For every round
t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]
and a token
i\in\mathcal{B}
we define a random variable

(5.1)

Z_{i}^{(t)}:=\sum_{u\in V}\1_{W_{i}^{(t)}=u}\cdot\mathbf{M}_{u,D}^{[t+1,t_{2}]}\qquad\text{and}\qquad Z^{(t)}:=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\ Z_{i}^{(t)}.

Hence, conditional on the token location
W_{i}^{(t)}
,
Z_{i}^{(t)}
is the probability that token
i
starting at time
t
is on a node
w\in D
at time
t_{2}
.
In the following analysis we use the simplified notation

\mathbf{M}_{W_{i}^{(t)},D}^{[t+1,t_{2}]}:=\sum_{u\in V}\1_{W_{i}^{(t)}=u}\cdot\mathbf{M}_{u,D}^{[t+1,t_{2}]}.

Since
\mathbf{M}^{[t_{2}+1,t_{2}]}=\mathbf{I}
,

Z^{(t_{2})}=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbf{M}_{W_{i}^{(t_{2})},D}^{[t_{2}+1,t_{2}]}.

Hence,
Z_{i}^{(t_{2})}
is a random variable that can take on the values zero or one.
Therefore,

\E\left[Z^{(t_{2})}\right]=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Z^{(t_{2})}=1\,\right]=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\left\{Z_{i}^{(t_{2})}=1\right\}\,\right]=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\left\{W_{i}^{(t_{2})}\in D\right\}\,\right].

The key idea is to prove that the sequence
Z^{(t)}
,
t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]
forms a supermartingale with respect to
the filtration
\mathfrak{F}^{(t)}
, which reveals all random decisions of the process between rounds
t_{1}
and
t
(so in particular, it reveals the location vectors
W^{(t)}=\left(W_{1}^{(t)},\ldots,W_{|\mathcal{T}|}^{(t)}\right)
), that is,

(5.2)

\E\left[Z^{(t)}~|~\mathfrak{F}^{(t-1)}\right]\leq Z^{(t-1)},

which would immediately imply

\E\left[Z^{(t_{2})}\right]\leq Z^{(t_{1})}.

It then follows

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\left\{W_{i}^{(t_{2})}\in D\right\}\,\right]=\E\left[Z^{(t_{2})}\right]\leq Z^{(t_{1})}=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbf{M}_{w_{i}^{(t_{1})},D}^{[t_{1}+1,t_{2}]},

finishing the proof.

It remains to prove
Eq.

5.2
.
Without loss of generality we may assume that each matching consists only of one edge. This is because for any sequence of matchings
(\mathbf{M}^{(s)})_{s=t_{1}}^{t_{2}}
in which there is a round
s\in[t_{1}+1,t_{2}]
whose matching includes more than one edge, we can decompose such round
s
into multiple “sub-rounds”, each consisting of exactly one matching. It is clear that this yields the same process, only with a larger number of rounds.

Fix round
t
with
t_{1}\leq t\leq t_{2}
and assume
\{u,v\}
is the single edge in
\mathbf{M}^{(t)}
.
First we compute
Z^{(t-1)}
.
We define
T_{u}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
as the tokens from
\mathcal{B}
which are on
u
and
T_{v}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
as the tokens from
\mathcal{B}
which are on
v
at the beginning of round
t
.
Note that it is possible that there are tokens
i\not\in\mathcal{B}
on those nodes.
We get

\mathbf{M}_{u,D}^{[t,t_{2}]}=\mathbf{M}_{v,D}^{[t,t_{2}]}=\mathbf{M}_{u,v}^{(t)}\cdot\mathbf{M}_{v,D}^{[t+1,t_{2}]}+\mathbf{M}_{u,u}^{(t)}\cdot\mathbf{M}_{u,D}^{[t+1,t_{2}]}=\frac{\mathbf{M}_{u,D}^{[t+1,t_{2}]}+\mathbf{M}_{v,D}^{[t+1,t_{2}]}}{2}.

For ease of presentation we define

p:=\mathbf{M}_{u,D}^{[t+1,t_{2}]}\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad q:=\mathbf{M}_{v,D}^{[t+1,t_{2}]}.

From the definition (
5.1
) it follows that

(5.3)

\displaystyle Z^{(t-1)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}\setminus(T_{u}\cup T_{v})}Z_{i}^{(t-1)}\cdot\prod_{i\in T_{u}\cup T_{v}}Z_{i}^{(t-1)}=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}\setminus(T_{u}\cup T_{v})}Z_{i}^{(t-1)}\cdot\left(\frac{p+q}{2}\right)^{|T_{u}\cup T_{v}|}.

We will continue to analyze
\E[Z^{(t)}~|~\mathfrak{F}^{(t-1)}]
and eventually upper bound it by the right hand side in
Eq.

5.3
, which completes the argument.
For each token
i
located at
\{u,v\}
let
S(i)
be its sibling token on the other node after the moving step of round
t
and before the shuffling step; note that it is possible for a token to have no sibling.
We partition tokens in
T_{u}\cup T_{v}
into three sets:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\{i\in T_{u}\cup T_{v}~|~S(i)\in T_{u}\cup T_{v}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\{i\in T_{u}\cup T_{v}~|~S(i)\in\mathcal{T}\setminus(T_{u}\cup T_{v})\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{3}

\displaystyle=\left\{i\in T_{u}\cup T_{v}~|~\mbox{$S(i)$ does not exist.}\right\}.

Note that the set
\mathcal{B}_{3}
corresponds to the excess token (if there is one), and thus
|\mathcal{B}_{3}|\in\{0,1\}
. We will now apply this partitioning to
\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z^{(t)}~\Big|~\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z^{(t)}~\middle|~\mathfrak{F}^{(t-1)}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\,\prod_{i\in B}Z_{i}^{(t)}~\middle|~\mathfrak{F}^{(t-1)}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\,\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}\setminus(T_{u}\cup T_{v})}Z_{i}^{(t)}\cdot\prod_{i\in T_{u}\cup T_{v}}Z_{i}^{(t)}~\Bigg|~\mathfrak{F}^{(t-1)}\,\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}\setminus(T_{u}\cup T_{v})}Z_{i}^{(t-1)}\cdot\E\left[\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{1}}Z_{i}^{(t)}\cdot\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{2}}Z_{i}^{(t)}\cdot\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{3}}Z_{i}^{(t)}~\Bigg|~\mathfrak{F}^{(t-1)}\right]

(5.4)

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{=}\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}\setminus(T_{u}\cup T_{v})}Z_{i}^{(t-1)}\cdot\E\left[\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{1}}Z_{i}^{(t)}~\Bigg|~\mathfrak{F}^{(t-1)}\right]\cdot\E\left[\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{2}}Z_{i}^{(t)}~\Bigg|~\mathfrak{F}^{(t-1)}\right]\cdot\E\left[\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{3}}Z_{i}^{(t)}~\Bigg|~\mathfrak{F}^{(t-1)}\right],

where
(a)
follows from the fact that for tokens
i\in\mathcal{B}\setminus(T_{u}\cup T_{v})
, we have
Z_{i}^{(t)}=Z_{i}^{(t-1)}
since
W_{i}^{(t)}
is not matched with any node in this round;
(b)
follows since tokens which are in different
\mathcal{B}_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq 3
must be at different heights and therefore move independently in the shuffling phase.

We will now analyze the three different expectations in
Eq.

5.4

Case 1: The expectation over
\mathcal{B}_{1}
.

Using that any token
i\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
only depends on its sibling token
S(i)
we can group all tokens in
\mathcal{B}_{1}
in pairs, and we denote this relation by
\sim
. Further, a token
i
with sibling
j
will take the opposite action in the shuffling step, and therefore

\displaystyle\E\left[\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{1}}Z_{i}^{(t)}~\Bigg|~\mathfrak{F}^{(t-1)}\right]

\displaystyle=\prod_{i<j\in\mathcal{B}_{1}\colon i\sim j}\E\left[Z_{i}^{(t)}\cdot Z_{j}^{(t)}~\Bigg|~\mathfrak{F}^{(t-1)}\right]=\prod_{i<j\in\mathcal{B}_{1}\colon i\sim j}\left(p\cdot q\right).

Case 2: The expectation over
\mathcal{B}_{2}
.

Since each token in
\mathcal{B}_{2}
has no sibling in
\mathcal{B}
(let alone
\mathcal{B}_{2}
), their movements in the shuffling step are independent. Further, each such token is on
u
or
v
with the same probability which yields,

\displaystyle\E\left[\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{2}}Z_{i}^{(t)}~\Bigg|~\mathfrak{F}^{(t-1)}\right]

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{2}}\E\left[Z_{i}^{(t)}~\Bigg|~\mathfrak{F}^{(t-1)}\right]=\prod_{i\in B_{2}}\left(\frac{p+q}{2}\right).

Case 3: The expectation over
\mathcal{B}_{3}
.

This is analogous to Case 2, as a token in
\mathcal{B}_{3}
has no sibling in
\mathcal{B}
(in fact there is not even any other token at this height). Hence,

\displaystyle\E\left[\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{3}}Z_{i}^{(t)}~\Bigg|~\mathfrak{F}^{(t-1)}\right]

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in B_{3}}\left(\frac{p+q}{2}\right).

Aggregating the contributions of all tokens in
\mathcal{B}
in round
t
using the three cases above,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z^{(t)}~\Big|~\mathfrak{F}^{(t-1)}\,\right]

\displaystyle\kern-3.37502pt\mathrel{\mathop{=}\limits^{(\ref{eq:corr})}}\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}\setminus(T_{u}\cup T_{v})}Z_{i}^{(t-1)}\cdot\E\left[\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{1}}Z_{i}^{(t)}~\Bigg|~\mathfrak{F}^{(t-1)}\right]\cdot\E\left[\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{2}}Z_{i}^{(t)}~\Bigg|~\mathfrak{F}^{(t-1)}\right]\cdot\E\left[\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{3}}Z_{i}^{(t)}~\Bigg|~\mathfrak{F}^{(t-1)}\right]\qquad

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}\setminus(T_{u}\cup T_{v})}Z_{i}^{(t-1)}\cdot\prod_{i<j\in\mathcal{B}_{1}\colon i\sim j}\left(p\cdot q\right)\cdot\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{2}}\frac{p+q}{2}\cdot\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{3}}\frac{p+q}{2}

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\leq}\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}\setminus(T_{u}\cup T_{v})}Z_{i}^{(t-1)}\cdot\prod_{i<j\in\mathcal{B}_{1}\colon i\sim j}\left(\frac{p+q}{2}\right)^{2}\cdot\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{2}}\frac{p+q}{2}\cdot\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{3}}\frac{p+q}{2}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{B}\setminus(T_{u}\cup T_{v})}Z_{i}^{(t-1)}\cdot\left(\frac{p+q}{2}\right)^{|T_{u}\cup T_{v}|}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{=}}Z^{(t-1)},

where
(a)
uses Cases 1, 2 and 3 above,
(b)
follows from the fact that
p\cdot q\leq((p+q)/2)^{2}
and
(c)
follows from
Eq.

5.3
.
Hence
\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z^{(t)}~\Big|~\mathfrak{F}^{(t-1)}\,\right]\leq Z^{(t-1)}
,
which is
Eq.

5.2
and, consequently, completes the proof.

5.2
Concentration Inequality for Convex Combinations of Loads

The following “Hoeffding-like” concentration inequality is another key tool in our analysis. We use it frequently to derive (upper) bounds on the number of tokens on a set of nodes.

Lemma 5.2
(Hoeffding Bound for Convex Combinations of Loads).

Consider any load vector
x^{(0)}
with
\discr(x^{(0)})\leq K
and any round
t\geq 1

such that
(\mathbf{M}^{(s)})_{s=1}^{t}
is a
(K,\kappa)
-smoothing sequence of matchings with
K>\kappa
. Then for any stochastic vector
(a_{w})_{w\in V}
, it holds for any
\delta>0
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left|\sum_{w\in V}a_{w}\cdot X_{w}^{(t)}-\overline{x}\right|\geq\delta~\middle|~(\mathbf{M}^{(s)})_{s=1}^{t}\,\right]\leq 2\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{(\delta-\kappa)^{2}}{4\|a\|_{2}^{2}}\right).

As a special case of this concentration inequality, we can take any node
v
, define the unit-vector
a_{w}=\mathbf{1}_{w=v}
(and thus
\|a\|_{2}^{2}=1
), pick
\delta=\sqrt{12\cdot\log(n)}
and then apply a union bound over all nodes
v\in V
. This immediately yields the following bound on the discrepancy.

Corollary 5.3
.

Consider any load vector
x^{(0)}
with
\discr(x^{(0)})\leq K
.
Consider any round
t\geq 1
and let
(\mathbf{M}^{(s)})_{s=1}^{t}
be a fixed
(K,1)
-smoothing sequence of matchings. Then,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\discr(X^{(t)})\leq\sqrt{48\cdot\log(n)}+1\,\right]\geq 1-2\cdot n^{-1}.

The exact same bound was shown in
[
34
, Theorem 3.6]
.

5.3
Proof of the Concentration Inequality (
Lemma

5.2
)

Before giving the proof of
Lemma

5.2
, we need to introduce some notation and auxiliary results.

We first recall a standard formula (e.g.,
[
34
, Equation 2.5]
); for any node
w\in V
,

(5.5)

\displaystyle X_{w}^{(t)}-\xi_{w}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sum_{[u:v]\in\mathbf{M}^{(s)}}E_{u,v}^{(s)}\cdot\left(\mathbf{M}_{u,w}^{[s+1,t]}-\mathbf{M}_{v,w}^{[s+1,t]}\right),

where
\xi_{w}^{(t)}=\sum_{u\in V}x_{u}^{(0)}\cdot\mathbf{M}_{u,w}^{[1,t]}
is the load after
t
rounds in the continuous model where load values can be fractional, and
E_{u,v}^{(t)}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathsf{Odd}\left(X_{u}^{(t-1)}+X_{v}^{(t-1)}\right)\cdot\Phi_{u,v}^{(t)}
is the rounding error; here
\Phi_{u,v}^{(t)}\in\{-1,+1\}
is the random orientation which has Rademacher distribution (that is, it is uniform in
\{-1,+1\}
).
Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{w\in V}a_{w}\cdot\left(X_{w}^{(t)}-\xi_{w}^{(t)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in V}a_{w}\cdot\left(\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sum_{[u:v]\in\mathbf{M}^{(s)}}E_{u,v}^{(s)}\cdot\left(\mathbf{M}_{u,w}^{[s+1,t]}-\mathbf{M}_{v,w}^{[s+1,t]}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sum_{[u:v]\in\mathbf{M}^{(s)}}E_{u,v}^{(s)}\cdot\left[\sum_{w\in V}a_{w}\cdot\left(\mathbf{M}_{u,w}^{[s+1,t]}-\mathbf{M}_{v,w}^{[s+1,t]}\right)\right].

We will first state a Hoeffding-like concentration inequality, applied to last expression above:

Lemma 5.4
(cf.
[
34
, Lemma 3.4]
).

Consider an arbitrary but fixed sequence of matchings
(\mathbf{M}^{(s)})_{s=1}^{t}
for any rounds
t\geq 1
and the
load vector
x^{(0)}
. For any family of numbers
g_{u,v}^{(s)},[u:v]\in\mathbf{M}^{(s)},1\leq s\leq t
, define the random variable

Z:=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sum_{[u:v]\in\mathbf{M}^{(s)}}E_{u,v}^{(s)}\cdot g_{u,v}^{(s)}.

Then,
\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z\,\right]=0
, and for any
\delta>0
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left|Z-\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z\,\right]\right|\geq\delta\,\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sum_{[u:v]\in\mathbf{M}^{(s)}}\left(g_{u,v}^{(s)}\right)^{2}}\right).

The next lemma can be seen as a generalization of the first statement of Lemma 3.2 from
[
34
]
, which considers the fixed vector
a
with
a_{w}:=1
for node
w\in V
and
a_{u}=0
for
u\neq w
. We extend it to arbitrary stochastic vectors
a
, which is crucial to prove
Lemma

5.2
in its full generality.

Lemma 5.5
.

Consider an arbitrary sequence of matchings
\left(\mathbf{M}^{(s)}\right)_{s=1}^{\infty}
.
Let
(a_{k})_{k\in V}
be any stochastic vector. Then:

1.

For any two rounds
0\leq t_{1}\leq t
it holds,

\sum_{s=1}^{t_{1}}\sum_{[u:v]\in\mathbf{M}^{(s)}}\left(\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}\cdot\left(\mathbf{M}_{u,k}^{[s+1,t]}-\mathbf{M}_{v,k}^{[s+1,t]}\right)\right)^{2}\leq 2\cdot\|a\|_{2}^{2}.

2.

For any two rounds
0\leq t_{1}\leq t
it holds,

\sum_{w\in V}\left(\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}\cdot\mathbf{M}_{w,k}^{[t_{1}+1,t]}\right)^{2}\leq\|a\|_{2}^{2}.

Proof 2
.

The proof follows closely the proof of Theorem 3.2 from
[
34
]
.
We define a potential function as

\displaystyle\Psi^{(s)}:=\sum_{w\in V}\left(\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}\mathbf{M}_{w,k}^{[s+1,t]}-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{2},

for any round
1\leq s\leq t
. This is a generalization of the potential function in
[
34
]
, where all
a_{k}:=1
.

Proof of the first statement.

Consider now any round
1\leq s\leq t
, and let
u,v
be nodes with
[u:v]\in\mathbf{M}^{(s)}
.
We have

\mathbf{M}_{u,k}^{[s,t]}=\mathbf{M}_{u,u}^{(s)}\cdot\mathbf{M}_{u,k}^{[s+1,t]}+\mathbf{M}_{u,v}^{(s)}\cdot\mathbf{M}_{v,k}^{[s+1,t]}=\frac{\mathbf{M}_{u,k}^{[s+1,t]}+\mathbf{M}_{v,k}^{[s+1,t]}}{2}.

To simplify notation, define for any two nodes
u,v\in V
,

y_{u,v}:=\mathbf{M}_{u,v}^{[s+1,t]}.

With that notation, the contribution of these nodes to
\Psi^{(s)}-\Psi^{(s-1)}
is

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}\mathbf{M}_{u,k}^{[s+1,t]}-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}\mathbf{M}_{v,k}^{[s+1,t]}-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{2}-\left(\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}\mathbf{M}_{u,k}^{[s,t]}-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{2}-\left(\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}\mathbf{M}_{v,k}^{[s,t]}-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}y_{u,k}-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}y_{v,k}-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-\left(\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}\frac{y_{u,k}+y_{v,k}}{2}-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{2}-\left(\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}\frac{y_{u,k}+y_{v,k}}{2}-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{2}\!\!,

having used that
\mathbf{M}_{u,k}^{[s,t]}=\mathbf{M}_{v,k}^{[s,t]}=\frac{y_{u,k}+y_{v,k}}{2}
. We can further rewrite the last expression as

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}y_{u,k}\right)^{2}-\frac{2}{n}\cdot\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}y_{u,k}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}+\left(\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}y_{v,k}\right)^{2}-\frac{2}{n}\cdot\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}y_{v,k}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}

\displaystyle~~-2\cdot\left(\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}\cdot\frac{y_{u,k}+y_{v,k}}{2}\right)^{2}+\frac{4}{n}\cdot\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}\cdot\frac{y_{u,k}+y_{v,k}}{2}-\frac{2}{n^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}y_{u,k}\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}y_{v,k}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}y_{u,k}+\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}y_{v,k}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle~~+\frac{2}{n}\cdot\underbrace{\left(-\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}y_{u,k}-\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}y_{v,k}+\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}\cdot(y_{u,k}+y_{v,k})\right)}_{=~0}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}y_{u,k}-\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}y_{v,k}\right)^{2},

where in
(a)
we used the fact that
a^{2}+b^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\cdot(a+b)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot(a-b)^{2}
. If a node is not matched in round
s
, then its contribution to
\Psi^{(s)}-\Psi^{(s-1)}
equals zero.
Accumulating the contribution of all nodes yields

(5.6)

\Psi^{(s)}-\Psi^{(s-1)}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sum_{[u:v]\in\mathbf{M}^{(s)}}\left(\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}\cdot\left(\mathbf{M}_{u,k}^{[s+1,t]}-\mathbf{M}_{v,k}^{[s+1,t]}\right)\right)^{2}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\Psi^{(t_{1})}\geq\Psi^{(t_{1})}-\Psi^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{t_{1}}\left(\Psi^{(s)}-\Psi^{(s-1)}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sum_{s=1}^{t_{1}}\sum_{[u:v]\in\mathbf{M}^{(s)}}\left(\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}\cdot\left(\mathbf{M}_{u,k}^{[s+1,t]}-\mathbf{M}_{v,k}^{[s+1,t]}\right)\right)^{2},

and rearranging gives,

\sum_{s=1}^{t_{1}}\sum_{[u:v]\in\mathbf{M}^{(s)}}\left(\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}\cdot\left(\mathbf{M}_{u,k}^{[s+1,t]}-\mathbf{M}_{v,k}^{[s+1,t]}\right)\right)^{2}\leq 2\cdot\Psi^{(t_{1})}\leq 2\cdot\Psi^{(t)},

where the last inequality holds since the potential
\Psi^{(s)}
is non-decreasing over
s
(
Eq.

5.6
).
Moreover, since
\mathbf{M}^{[t+1,t]}
is the identity matrix
\mathbf{I}
, we have
\mathbf{M}^{[t+1,t]}_{w,k}=1
for
k=w
and
\mathbf{M}^{[t+1,t]}_{w,k}=0
for
k\neq w
.
Hence

(5.7)

\Psi^{(t)}=\sum_{w\in V}\left(\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}\mathbf{M}_{w,k}^{[t+1,t]}-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{2}=\sum_{w\in V}\left(a_{w}-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{2}=\|a\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{n}.

Combining the last two statements finishes the proof of the first statement.

Proof of the second statement.

Note that since
t_{1}\leq t
, it follows from
Eq.

5.6
that

\Psi^{(t_{1})}\leq\Psi^{(t)}.

Substituting the definition of
\Psi^{(t_{1})}
and
\Psi^{(t)}
on both sides gives

(5.8)

\sum_{w\in V}\left(\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}\cdot\mathbf{M}_{w,k}^{[t_{1}+1,t]}-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{2}\leq\sum_{w\in V}\left(\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}\cdot\mathbf{M}_{w,k}^{[t+1,t]}-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{2}\overset{\text{Eq.~(\ref{eq:sec:removed})}}{=}\|a\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{n}.

Finally, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{w\in V}\left(\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}\cdot\mathbf{M}_{w,k}^{[t_{1}+1,t]}\right)^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\sum_{w\in V}\left(\sum_{k\in V}a_{k}\cdot\mathbf{M}_{w,k}^{[t_{1}+1,t]}-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\overset{\text{Eq.~(\ref{eq:temp:hh})}}{=}\|a\|_{2}^{2},

where
(a)
follows from
9.13
(since the matrix
\mathbf{M}^{[t_{1}+1,t]}
is doubly stochastic and
\|a\|_{1}=1
).

Proof 3
(Proof of
Lemma

5.2
).

By
Eq.

5.5
,

\displaystyle\sum_{w\in V}a_{w}\cdot\left(X_{w}^{(t)}-\xi_{w}^{(t)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in V}a_{w}\cdot\left(\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sum_{[u:v]\in\mathbf{M}^{(s)}}E_{u,v}^{(s)}\cdot\left(\mathbf{M}_{u,w}^{[s+1,t]}-\mathbf{M}_{v,w}^{[s+1,t]}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sum_{[u:v]\in\mathbf{M}^{(s)}}E_{u,v}^{(s)}\cdot\left[\sum_{w\in V}a_{w}\cdot\left(\mathbf{M}_{u,w}^{[s+1,t]}-\mathbf{M}_{v,w}^{[s+1,t]}\right)\right].

Since the time interval
[1,t]
is
(K,\kappa)
-smoothing, it holds that
-\kappa\leq\xi_{w}^{(t)}-\overline{x}\leq\kappa
for all nodes
w\in V
, and therefore

\sum_{w\in V}a_{w}\cdot\xi_{w}^{(t)}=\overline{x}+B,

where
|B|\leq\kappa
. This implies

(5.9)

\sum_{w\in V}a_{w}\cdot X_{w}^{(t)}-\overline{x}=B+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sum_{[u:v]\in\mathbf{M}^{(s)}}E_{u,v}^{(s)}\cdot\left[\sum_{w\in V}a_{w}\cdot\left(\mathbf{M}_{u,w}^{[s+1,t]}-\mathbf{M}_{v,w}^{[s+1,t]}\right)\right]

Following the notation of
Lemma

5.4
, let us define

Z:=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sum_{[u:v]\in\mathbf{M}^{(s)}}E_{u,v}^{(s)}\cdot\left[\sum_{w\in V}a_{w}\cdot\left(\mathbf{M}_{u,w}^{[s+1,t]}-\mathbf{M}_{v,w}^{[s+1,t]}\right)\right],

and

g_{u,v}^{(s)}:=\sum_{w\in V}a_{w}\cdot\left(\mathbf{M}_{u,w}^{[s+1,t]}-\mathbf{M}_{v,w}^{[s+1,t]}\right).

Since
\mathbf{E}\left[\,E_{u,v}^{(s)}\,\right]=0
, then
\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z\,\right]=0
and from
Lemma

5.4
it follows that,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\lvert Z\rvert\geq\delta\,\right]\leq 2\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sum_{[u:v]\in\mathbf{M}^{(s)}}\left(g_{u,v}^{(s)}\right)^{2}}\right).

From the first statement of
Lemma

5.5

it follows that

\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sum_{[u:v]\in\mathbf{M}^{(s)}}\left(g_{u,v}^{(s)}\right)^{2}\leq 2\cdot\|a\|_{2}^{2}
.
Combining these two gives that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\lvert Z\rvert\geq\delta\,\right]\leq 2\cdot\exp(-{\delta^{2}}/(4\cdot\|a\|_{2}^{2}))
. This together with
Eq.

5.9
implies that,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left|\sum_{w\in V}a_{w}\cdot X_{w}^{(t)}-\overline{x}\right|\geq\delta+\kappa\,\right]\leq 2\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}}{4\cdot\|a\|_{2}^{2}}\right),

and shifting
\delta
by
\kappa
completes the proof.

5.4
Reducing the Number of Tokens to Linear

We will now present an application of
Lemma

5.2
to bound the number of tokens above the average load; this can be regarded as the number of tokens that need to be rearranged to achieve constant discrepancy. The proof relies on the flexibility of
Lemma

5.2
by applying it multiple times for different coefficients
(a_{w})_{w\in V}
in order to prove that there is no large subset in which all nodes have large load.

Lemma 5.6
.

Assume our process applies a sequence of matchings
\left(\mathbf{M}^{(s)}\right)_{s=1}^{t_{1}}
which is

(K,1/(2n))
-smoothing on an arbitrary initial load vector with
\discr(x^{(0)})\leq K
.
Then it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\sum_{w\in V}\max\left\{X_{w}^{(t_{1})}-\overline{x},0\right\}\leq 16\cdot n\,\right]

\displaystyle\geq 1-2\cdot n^{-2}.

Proof 4
.

For any integer
i=1,2,\ldots,2\cdot\sqrt{\log(n)}
, we define the event

\mathcal{E}_{i}:=\biggl\{\left|\left\{u\in V\colon X_{u}^{(t_{1})}-\overline{x}\geq 4\cdot i\right\}\right|\leq n\cdot 2^{-i}\biggr\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{E}_{0}:=\left\{\max_{w\in V}X_{w}^{(t_{1})}-\overline{x}\leq 6\sqrt{\log(n)}\right\}.

From
Lemma

5.2
, second statement (with
\delta:=\sqrt{36\log(n)}
,
a_{w}=1
and
a_{u}=0
for
u\neq w
and union bound over all nodes
w\in V
) we get

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{E}_{0}\,\right]\geq 1-n^{-2}.

It remains to analyze the probability of the other
\mathcal{E}_{i}
. To this end, fix any
1\leq i\leq 2\sqrt{\log(n)}
. By the union bound,

(5.10)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\overline{\mathcal{E}_{i}}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{S\subseteq V\colon|S|=n\cdot 2^{-i}}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{u\in S}\left\{X_{u}^{(t_{1})}\geq\overline{x}+4\cdot i\right\}\,\right].

To calculate this probability we apply
Lemma

5.2
as follows.
We define for a fixed subset
S\subseteq V
a vector
(a_{w})_{w\in V}
by
a_{w}:=1/|S|
for
w\in S
and
a_{w}:=0
otherwise. Then
\sum_{w\in V}a_{w}=1
and
\|a\|_{2}^{2}=|S|\cdot(1/|S|)^{2}=1/|S|
,
\kappa=1/n
, and we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{u\in S}\left\{X_{u}^{(t_{1})}\geq\overline{x}\!+\!4\cdot i\right\}\,\right]\!

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\sum_{u\in S}X_{u}^{(t_{1})}\geq|S|\cdot\left(\overline{x}+4\cdot i\right)\,\right]\leq\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left|\sum_{w\in V}a_{w}\cdot X_{w}^{(t_{1})}-\overline{x}\right|\geq 4\cdot i\,\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}2\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{(3i)^{2}}{4\|a\|_{2}^{2}}\right)\leq 2\cdot\exp\left(-(9/4)\cdot i^{2}\cdot|S|\right)

\displaystyle=2\cdot\exp\left(-(9/4)\cdot i^{2}\cdot n\cdot 2^{-i}\right),

where
(a)
follows from
Lemma

5.2
. Plugging this into
Eq.

5.10
yields

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\overline{\mathcal{E}_{i}}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leq\binom{n}{n\cdot 2^{-i}}\cdot 2\cdot\exp\left(-(9/4)\cdot i^{2}\cdot n\cdot 2^{-i}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\left(e\cdot 2^{i}\right)^{n\cdot 2^{-i}}\cdot 2\cdot\exp\left(-(9/4)\cdot i^{2}\cdot n\cdot 2^{-i}\right)=2\cdot\left(\frac{e\cdot 2^{i}}{e^{(9/4)\cdot i^{2}}}\right)^{n\cdot 2^{-i}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}n^{-3},

where
(a)
used the estimate
\binom{n}{k}\leq(en/k)^{k}
and
(b)
used that
i\in[1,2\sqrt{\log(n)}]
.
By another union bound over
i=0,1,\ldots,2\sqrt{\log(n)}
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{i=0}^{2\sqrt{\log(n)}}\mathcal{E}_{i}\,\right]\geq 1-n^{-2}-2\sqrt{\log(n)}\cdot n^{-3}\geq 1-2\cdot n^{-2}.

We define a sequence
a_{0}:=-\infty
,
a_{i}:=4\cdot i
for
1\leq i\leq 2\sqrt{\log(n)}
and assume the event
\bigcap_{i=0}^{2\sqrt{\log(n)}}\mathcal{E}_{i}
occurs and get

\displaystyle\sum_{w\in V}\max\left\{X_{w}^{(t_{1})}-\overline{x},0\right\}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{2\sqrt{\log(n)}}\left|\left\{w\in V\colon X_{w}^{(t_{1})}-\overline{x}\in(a_{i-1},a_{i}]\right\}\right|\cdot a_{i}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{2\sqrt{\log(n)}}\left|\left\{w\in V\colon X_{w}^{(t_{1})}-\overline{x}\geq a_{i-1}\right\}\right|\cdot a_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(4n\cdot 2^{-i+1}\cdot i\right)\leq 8n\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(i\cdot 2^{-i}\right)=16n.

This completes the proof.

6
Balancing a Linear Number of Tokens

In this section we establish the most challenging step in our analysis.
We consider an initial load vector
x^{(0)}
with at most
(L-\varepsilon)n
tokens for
\frac{4}{\log^{4}(n)}<\varepsilon<L
and integer
L\geq 1
(this is more general than requiring a linear number of tokens, which is what we will need in the final proof).

The main result of this section is given in
Proposition

6.1
, which states that after
O(\tau_{\mathrm{global}}+\tau_{\mathrm{local}}\cdot\log(n)/\log\log(n)
) rounds the maximum load is
L+1
. An illustration of the proof method can be found in
Fig.

3.1
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
t_{2}:=2\cdot\tau_{\mathrm{global}}+{6\cdot\tau_{\mathrm{local}}\cdot\log(n)}/{\log\log(n)}
, let
L
be any integer with
1\leq L\leq\log^{7}(n)
and let
\frac{4}{\log^{4}(n)}\leq\varepsilon<L
(not necessarily constant).
Assume our process applies a
(\tau_{\mathrm{global}},\tau_{\mathrm{local}})
-good sequence of matchings
\left(\mathbf{M}^{(s)}\right)_{s=1}^{\infty}
to an arbitrary initial load vector
x^{(0)}
with at most
(L-\varepsilon)n
tokens.
Then

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max_{w\in V}X_{w}^{(t_{2})}\leq L+1\,\right]\geq 1-\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon\cdot\log(n)}{2\cdot L\cdot\log\log(n)}+8\cdot\log\log(n)\right)-2\cdot n^{-2}.

The proposition makes uses of
Lemma

6.11
and
Lemma

6.12
, which concern Phase 1 and Phase 2, respectively. Both are stated and proved at the end of this section.

Proof 5
(Proof of
Proposition

6.1
).

Phase 1.
Let
t_{1}:=\tau_{\mathrm{global}}+\tau_{\mathrm{local}}\cdot\log(n)/\log\log(n)
and define
Y^{(t_{1})}:=\sum_{u\in V}\max\{X_{u}^{(t_{1})}-L,0\}
as the number of tokens at height at least
L+1
. Define the event

\mathcal{G}_{1}:=\{Y^{(t_{1})}\leq{n}/{\log(n)}\}.

From
Lemma

6.11
(Phase 1 Lemma) it follows that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{G}_{1}\,\right]\geq 1-\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon\cdot\log(n)}{2\cdot L\cdot\log\log(n)}+8\cdot\log\log(n)\right)-n^{-2}.

Phase 2.
For any
t\geq t_{1}
, we define an auxiliary load vector
\widehat{X}^{(t)}
with

\widehat{X}_{u}^{(t)}:=\max\{X_{u}^{(t)}-L,0\}
.
From
9.10
it follows that for any
t\geq t_{1}
,

X_{u}^{(t)}\leq\widehat{X}_{u}^{(t)}+L
.
Hence it is sufficient to show that
\max_{u\in V}\widehat{X}_{u}^{(t_{2})}\leq 1
for some suitable round
t_{2}
. Note that from Phase 1 we get
\sum_{u\in V}\widehat{X}_{u}^{(t_{1})}\leq n/\log(n)
, i.e. the load vector

\widehat{X}^{(t_{1})}
has at most
n/\log(n)
tokens.
This time we define, for any round
t\geq t_{1}
,

\widehat{Y}^{(t)}:=\sum_{u\in V}\max\{\widehat{X}_{u}^{(t)}-1,0\}
,
which is equal to the number of tokens with height at least
2
in
\widehat{X}^{(t)}
. Let
t_{2}:=t_{1}+\tau_{\mathrm{global}}+4\tau_{\mathrm{local}}\cdot\frac{\log(n)}{-\log(\frac{1}{\log(n)}+\frac{2}{\log^{4}(n)})}
.
We define a second event

\mathcal{G}_{2}:=\{\widehat{Y}^{(t_{2})}=0\}
.
From
Lemma

6.12
(Phase 2 Lemma) with
\varepsilon:=1-\frac{1}{\log(n)}
it follows that
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{G}_{2}~|~\mathcal{G}_{1}\,\right]\geq 1-n^{-2},

and by the definition of conditional probability we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{G}_{2}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{G}_{2}~|~\mathcal{G}_{1}\,\right]\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{G}_{1}\,\right]\geq 1-n^{-2}-\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\overline{\mathcal{G}_{1}}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geq 1-\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon\cdot\log(n)}{2\cdot L\cdot\log\log(n)}+8\cdot\log\log(n)\right)-2\cdot n^{-2}.

From the definition of
t_{2}
it follows that

\displaystyle t_{2}

\displaystyle=t_{1}+\tau_{\mathrm{global}}+\frac{4\cdot\log(n)}{-\log\left(\frac{1}{\log(n)}+\frac{2}{\log^{4}(n)}\right)}\cdot\tau_{\mathrm{local}}\leq t_{1}+\tau_{\mathrm{global}}+\frac{5\cdot\log(n)}{\log\log(n)}\cdot\tau_{\mathrm{local}}

\displaystyle=2\cdot\tau_{\mathrm{global}}+\frac{6\cdot\log(n)}{\log\log(n)}\cdot\tau_{\mathrm{local}},

finishing the proof.

In the rest of this section we will prove
Lemma

6.11
and
Lemma

6.12
. To do so, we have to provide several additional definitions and statements, which ultimately leads towards the key lemma (
Lemma

), which proves a drop in the number of tokens with height at least
L+1
within

rounds. From this,

First of all, we define two events corresponding to
\tau_{\mathrm{global}}
(global mixing) and
\tau_{\mathrm{local}}
(local mixing).

Definition 6.2
.

For any round
t\geq\tau_{\mathrm{global}}
we define

\Gamma_{g}^{(t)}:=\left\{\bigcap_{u\in V}\left\|\mathbf{M}_{u,.}^{[1,t]}-\vec{\frac{1}{n}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n^{7}}\right\}.

For any round
t\geq\tau_{\mathrm{local}}
and arbitrary node
u\in V
we define

\Gamma_{\ell}^{(t)}(u):=\left\{\left\|\mathbf{M}_{u,.}^{[t-\tau_{\mathrm{local}}+1,t]}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\log^{10}(n)}\right\}.

Note that the event
\Gamma_{g}^{(t)}
depends on the matching matrices in the time interval
[1,t]
. The event implies that the balancing matrices applied during rounds
[1,t]
are
(n\cdot\log^{7}(n),1/n)
-smoothing
(see
9.9
).
We call that property
globally mixing
, since it implies that a token starting from any node will reach each node in round
t
with probability
(1\pm o(1))/n
.
In contrast, the event
\Gamma_{\ell}^{(t)}(u)
adopts the viewpoint of a single node
u
.
It implies that the matchings chosen during the last
\tau_{\mathrm{local}}
rounds are locally smoothing from the viewpoint
u
.
\Gamma_{\ell}^{(t)}(u)
depends on the matchings applied in the time interval
[t-\tau_{\mathrm{local}}+1,t]
.
We remark that for any round
t\geq 1
, the events
\Gamma_{g}^{(t)}
and
\Gamma_{\ell}^{(t+\tau_{\mathrm{local}})}(u)
are statements over disjoint time-intervals, hence for the random matching model they are independent events.
In the following we first calculate the probabilities that these events occur for a sequence of
(\tau_{\mathrm{global}},\tau_{\mathrm{local}})
-
good
matchings, the proof is a straightforward calculation.

Lemma 6.3
.

For any process generating
(\tau_{\mathrm{global}},\tau_{\mathrm{local}})
-
good
matchings the following holds.

1.

For any round
t\geq\tau_{\mathrm{global}}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gamma_{g}^{(t)}\,\right]\geq 1-1/n^{3}.

2.

For any round
t\geq\tau_{\mathrm{local}}
and arbitrary node
u\in V
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gamma_{\ell}^{(t)}(u)\,\right]\geq 1-1/\log^{11}n.

Proof 6
.

First note that

(6.1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\overline{\Gamma_{g}^{(\tau_{\mathrm{global}})}}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcup_{u\in V}\left\{\left\|\mathbf{M}_{u,.}^{[1,\tau_{\mathrm{global}}]}-\vec{\frac{1}{n}}\right\|_{2}^{2}>\frac{1}{n^{7}}\right\}\,\right].

Applying the definition of
\tau_{\mathrm{global}}
(
Definition

2.2
), we get for

t=\tau_{\mathrm{global}}
that
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\overline{\Gamma_{g}^{(\tau_{\mathrm{global}})}}\,\right]\leq 1/n^{3}
.
The first statement follows from the basic fact that
\left\|\mathbf{M}_{u,.}^{[1,t]}-\vec{\frac{1}{n}}\right\|_{2}^{2}
is non-increasing in

(see

9.14
).
For the second statement, consider an arbitrary round
t\geq\tau_{\mathrm{local}}
and a fixed node
u\in V
. Here, we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\overline{\Gamma_{\ell}^{(t)}(u)}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left\{\left\|\mathbf{M}_{u,.}^{[t-\tau_{\mathrm{local}}+1,t]}\right\|_{2}^{2}>\frac{1}{\log^{10}(n)}\right\}\,\right]\leq\frac{1}{\log^{11}(n)},

where the last inequality follows from the definition of
\tau_{\mathrm{local}}
(
Definition

2.2
). As above, the proof follows from
9.14
.

Recall that Phase 1 starts at round
\tau_{\mathrm{global}}
and each of our two phases is subdivided into

of
\tau_{\mathrm{local}}
many rounds. We refer to the last round of an epoch as

the function

which returns the last round of
k

returns the
k
-th milestone. We define
E(k):=[e(k-1)+1,\ldots,e(k)]
for the interval of rounds constituting the
k
-th epoch.

Let the random variable
Y^{(t)}
denote the number of tokens of height at least
L+1
at round

(6.2)

\displaystyle Y^{(t)}:=\sum_{u\in V}\max\left\{X_{u}^{(t)}-L,0\right\}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{T}}\1_{H_{j}^{(t)}\geq L+1}.

We wish to prove that in each epoch

drops by a constant factor (see

6.8
) such that at the end of Phase 1 the number of tokens of height at least
L+1
is at most

(
Lemma

6.11
). Since the

Let us first focus on the
k

Our goal is to prove that the expected number of tokens that collide with

is smaller than

To this end, we define an indicator random variable

for any token

and any node

as

Z_{i,j}^{(e(k))}(v):=\1_{W_{i}^{(e(k))}=v\ \cap\ W_{j}^{(e(k))}=v}\quad\text{ and }\quad Z_{i}^{(e(k))}:=\sum_{v\in V}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{T}\colon j\neq i}Z_{ij}^{(e(k))}(v),

i.e.,

if and only if tokens

Hence

The next lemma bounds the expected number of collisions.
Note that we assume in the lemma that the applied matrices are fixed. The randomness is due to the random decisions in the shuffling step.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
\left(\mathbf{M}^{(t)}\right)_{t=1}^{e(k)}

For any milestone

.

First we focus on a fixed location vector

Lemma 6.6
.

Assume that the load vector

has at most

Proof 8
.

Consider a node

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{L}^{(e(k))}(u)}

\displaystyle=\left\{\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z_{i}^{(e(k))}~\Big|~W^{(e(k-1))},\Gamma_{\ell}^{(e(k))}(u)\,\right]>L-\varepsilon+\frac{1}{\log^{4}(n)}\right\}\quad\mbox{and}

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{E}^{(e(k))}(u)}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha+\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\overline{\mathcal{L}^{(e(k))}(u)}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\alpha+\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z_{i}^{(e(k))}~\Big|~W^{(e(k-1))},\Gamma_{\ell}^{(e(k))}(u)\,\right]>L-\varepsilon+\frac{1}{\log^{4}(n)}\,\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 47.36096pt\left.\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z_{i}^{(e(k))}~\Big|~W^{(e(k-1))},\Gamma_{\ell}^{(e(k))}(u),\overline{\Gamma_{g}^{(e(k-1))}}\,\right]\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\overline{\Gamma_{g}^{(e(k-1))}}\,\right]>L-\varepsilon+\frac{1}{\log^{4}(n)}\right]

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\overline{\mathcal{E}^{(e(k))}(u)}\,\right]

Lemma 6.8

Using the definition of

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}^{(e(k-1))}\left[\,Y^{(e(k))}\cdot\1_{\cap_{i=0}^{k}\mathcal{E}^{(e(i))}}\,\right]

7.1

38

Here we show that once the load vector consists of only

In this section we derive discrepancy bounds for load vectors where the number of tokens with height above the average load is

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
t^{\star}:=2\cdot\tau_{\mathrm{global}}+\frac{6\log(n)}{\log\log(n)}\cdot\tau_{\mathrm{local}}

Assume our process applies a
(\tau_{\mathrm{global}},\tau_{\mathrm{local}})
-good sequence of matchings
\left(\mathbf{M}^{(s)}\right)_{s=1}^{\infty}

.

For any
t\geq 0

u\in V

\lceil\overline{x}\rceil
tokens from any node (as long as its load is large enough).

\sum_{w\in V}\widetilde{x}^{(0)}_{w}\leq 16\cdot n

Proposition

6.1

\varepsilon:=1/2

9.10

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\min_{w\in V}X_{w}^{(t^{\star})}\geq\lfloor\overline{x}\rfloor-19\,\right]

Note that

\displaystyle t_{2}:

\displaystyle=t_{1}+2\cdot\tau_{\mathrm{global}}+{6\ell}\cdot\tau_{\mathrm{local}}=t_{0}+4\cdot\tau_{\mathrm{global}}+{12\ell}\cdot\tau_{\mathrm{local}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{3\log(2Kn)}{\log(n)}+4\right)\cdot\tau_{\mathrm{global}}+{12\ell}\cdot\tau_{\mathrm{local}},

finishing the proof of the first statement.

Let
c>0
be any constant and
\tau:=t_{2}+2\cdot\tau_{\mathrm{global}}+\lceil 10/c\rceil\cdot\ell\cdot\tau_{\mathrm{local}}

9.1
Basic Properties of the Height-Sensitive Process

Lemma 9.1
.

Consider any pair of rounds
t_{1}<t_{2}
and let
(\mathbf{M}^{(s)})_{s=t_{1}+1}^{t_{2}}
be an arbitrary but fixed sequence of matchings. For any token
i\in\mathcal{T}
it holds that (1)
H_{i}^{(t_{1})}\geq H_{i}^{(t_{2})}
, and (2)

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,W_{i}^{(t_{2})}=v~\middle|~w_{i}^{(t_{1})}=u\,\right]=\mathbf{M}_{u,v}^{[t_{1}+1,t_{2}]}.

Proof 22
.

The height of a token can only change in the moving step of a round
t
. This only happens if the token is on one endpoint of an edge
[u:v]\in\mathbf{M}^{(t)}
with
x_{u}^{(t-1)}\geq x_{v}^{(t-1)}
. Then tokens on node
u
at height
x_{v}^{(t-1)}+\lceil\frac{x_{u}^{(t-1)}-x_{v}^{(t-1)}}{2}\rceil+i
for some
i>0
will decrease their height by
\lceil\frac{x_{u}^{(t-1)}-x_{v}^{(t-1)}}{2}\rceil
and move to node
v
. No other token on
u
or
v
will change its height during the moving step, and hence the first statement follows.

Similar to the proof of
[
34
, Lemma 4.1]
, we prove the second statement by induction over
t\in[t_{1}+1,t_{2}]
, that is, for all
u,v\in V
, we have

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,W_{i}^{(t)}=v~\middle|~w_{i}^{(t_{1})}=u\,\right]=\mathbf{M}_{u,v}^{[t_{1}+1,t]}
. For
t=t_{1}+1
,
\mathbf{M}^{[t_{1}+1,t]}
is the identity matrix, which means that the induction base holds. For the induction hypothesis, consider
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,W_{i}^{(t)}=v~\middle|~w_{i}^{(t_{1})}=u\,\right]
. If
v
is not part of a matching in round
t
, then

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,W_{i}^{(t)}=v~\middle|~w_{i}^{(t_{1})}=u\,\right]=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,W_{i}^{(t-1)}=v~\middle|~w_{i}^{(t_{1})}=u\,\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\star)}}{{=}}\mathbf{M}_{u,v}^{[t_{1}+1,t-1]}=\mathbf{M}_{u,v}^{[t_{1},t]},

where
(\star)
used the induction hypothesis.
If
v
is matched with a node
w
in round
t
, then by definition of the height-sensitive process the token
i
has a probability of
\frac{1}{2}
of reaching
v
from either
v
or
w
, and hence

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,W_{i}^{(t)}=v~\middle|~w_{i}^{(t_{1})}=u\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,W_{i}^{(t-1)}=v~\middle|~w_{i}^{(t_{1})}=u\,\right]\cdot\frac{1}{2}+\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,W_{i}^{(t-1)}=w~\middle|~w_{i}^{(t_{1})}=u\,\right]\cdot\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\star)}}{{=}}\mathbf{M}_{u,v}^{[t_{1},t]}\cdot\frac{1}{2}+\mathbf{M}_{u,w}^{[t_{1},t]}\cdot\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{M}_{u,v}^{[t_{1},t]}\cdot\mathbf{M}_{v,v}^{(t+1)}+\mathbf{M}_{u,w}^{[t_{1},t]}\cdot\mathbf{M}_{w,v}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{M}_{u,v}^{[t_{1},t+1]},

where
(\star)
used the induction hypothesis.

Observation 9.11
