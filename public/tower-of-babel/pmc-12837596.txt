A single-tube RAA-CRISPR-Cas12a diagnostic platform for rapid detection of Chlamydia trachomatis
Authors: Tong Jiang, Pei He, Yong Guo, Hua Liu, Guoping Lu, Zhuo Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chlamydia trachomatis, a pathogen of global public health importance, is prioritized by the World Health Organization as a major sexually transmitted infection. Its continued worldwide spread poses persistent challenges to reproductive health and disease control systems, underscoring the urgent need for rapid and accurate diagnostic methods. While current clinical diagnosis primarily relies on nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs), their dependence on expensive thermal cyclers, complex laboratory setups, and sophisticated procedures limits widespread use in low-resource settings. Recent advances in recombinase-aided amplification (RAA) combined with CRISPR-Cas12a have revolutionized molecular diagnostics. This study established an integrated one-tube CRISPR fluorescence detection system by combining rapid nucleic acid lysis, isothermal amplification, and CRISPR-Cas12a cleavage. The system is coupled with a portable palm-sized 4-channel fluorescence detector and a terminal app for visual readout. Experimental results demonstrated a detection limit of 10¹ copies/mL with no cross-reactivity to common reproductive tract pathogens including Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Ureaplasma urealyticum, Candida albicans, Trichomonas vaginalis, and Gardnerella vaginalis. Clinical validation against qPCR showed 100.00% sensitivity (95% CI: 87.66–100.00%), 98.31% specificity (95% CI: 90.91–99.96), 98.85% overall agreement (95% CI: 93.76– 99.97), 96.55% positive predictive value (95% CI: 80.04–99.49), and 100.00% negative predictive value (95% CI: 93.84–100.00%).This successfully developed CRISPR-Cas12a-based rapid detection method offers simple operation and fast results – complete within 30 min using rapid lysis (5 min nucleic acid release, 20 min RAA amplification, and 5 min CRISPR cleavage) – making it suitable for primary care and field screening, thereby providing a novel strategy for early diagnosis of C. trachomatis infection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13036-025-00591-z.

Introduction

Chlamydia trachomatis (CT), an obligate intracellular pathogen that causes one of the most common bacterial sexually transmitted infections globally, presents significant public health challenges, especially for women of reproductive age [1]. ccording to World Health Organization (WHO) data, the global annual incidence of C. trachomatis among sexually active individuals aged 15–49 is approximately 4.2%, with over 130 million new reported cases each year. However, these figures likely substantially underestimate the true burden due to factors including the completely asymptomatic nature of infection in approximately 70% of women and 50% of men, inadequate surveillance system coverage in developing countries, and lack of effective laboratory diagnostic capacity in many regions [2–4]. Seroepidemiological studies indicate that in some high-prevalence areas, cumulative infection rates among women of reproductive age can reach 40–60% [5–7]. This high prevalence of asymptomatic infection has established C. trachomatis as a “hidden epidemic.” Approximately 70–80% of cervical infections in women and over 50% of urethral infections in men present no noticeable symptoms [8–10]. This “silent epidemic” often results in infected individuals seeking medical care only after severe complications develop. Untreated infections can lead to pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) in women, with approximately 20% of affected women developing tubal factor infertility, a 7–10-fold increased risk of ectopic pregnancy, and chronic pelvic pain occurring in up to 18% of cases [5, 11]. In perinatal transmission, 5–30% of infected mothers transmit the pathogen to their newborns, causing neonatal conjunctivitis (incidence 30–50%) and infant pneumonia (incidence 10–20%) [12–15]. Moreover, studies demonstrate that C. trachomatis infection significantly elevates HIV acquisition risk in seropositive populations [16, 17]. Currently, given the potential for severe sequelae from Chlamydia trachomatis infection, many countries have implemented systematic screening and treatment programs. These initiatives aim to shorten the infection cycle, prevent irreversible fallopian tube damage, and interrupt transmission chains. It is particularly important to emphasize that this prevention strategy holds universal clinical significance: it is crucial not only for asymptomatic individuals (approximately 70% of cases) but equally essential for symptomatic patients. Timely intervention can both alleviate acute clinical symptoms and effectively prevent long-term complications.

However, accurate screening for C. trachomatis still relies on laboratory testing. Currently, nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) are considered the gold standard for C. trachomatis diagnosis. Among these, real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) technology, which targets the ompA gene, 16 S rRNA gene, or cryptic plasmid, can achieve 95–99% sensitivity and specificity [18–20]. Representative domestic enterprises in China, such as Sansure Biotech, DaAn Gene, and Hybribio, have launched various high-performance qPCR kits. Internationally, leading companies like Roche Diagnostics with their cobas® 4800 CT/NG assay, cobas® liat CT/MG/NG assay, and Cepheid with their Xpert CT/NG assay are prominent examples [21, 22].

Nevertheless, the clinical application of these high-performance molecular diagnostics still faces multiple challenges: limitations such as the need for expensive high-throughput fluorescent PCR instruments, matching nucleic acid extraction systems, and strict laboratory zoning mean that over 85% of primary healthcare institutions or resource-limited settings cannot perform such testing. Consequently, developing novel molecular diagnostic technologies that are simple to operate, rapid, sensitive, and low-cost has become an urgent requirement for achieving the WHO’s strategic goal of “eliminating sexually transmitted infections as a public health threat by 2030.” Particularly in resource-limited areas, enabling early diagnosis and treatment will directly contribute to improving global reproductive health outcomes and advancing health equity.

Recent advances in isothermal amplification technologies (e.g., RPA [23], LAMP [24], RAA [25]) and CRISPR-Cas systems have revolutionized molecular diagnostics. The combination of these technologies has established a novel detection paradigm: first, the crRNA guide sequence specifically binds to its complementary target DNA region, activating the Cas12a enzyme in a highly specific manner. Once activated through this precise recognition event, the Cas12a protein unleashes its collateral trans-cleavage activity, nonspecifically degrading nearby single-stranded DNA reporter probes. This dual mechanism—initial target-specific activation followed by nonspecific signal amplification—when coupled with isothermal amplification methods like RAA, creates a powerful detection system that combines exceptional specificity with high sensitivity [25]. Platforms such as HOLMES (one-HOur Low-cost Multipurpose highly Efficient System) [26] and DETECTR (DNA Endonuclease-Targeted CRISPR Trans Reporter) [27] demonstrate the potential of this integrated approach. Compared to conventional qPCR, the CRISPR-RAA system offers distinct advantages: RAA requires only four core components and amplifies target sequences within 30 min at 37–42 °C, eliminating the need for thermal cyclers. The Cas12a/crRNA complex specifically binds target DNA, activating trans-cleavage that degrades fluorescent reporter probes and amplifies detection signals, achieving single-copy sensitivity. Results can be interpreted using portable readers or lateral flow strips, making the system well-suited for point-of-care testing (POCT).

The CRISPR-RAA platform has been successfully implemented for detecting various pathogens, including African swine fever virus, SARS-CoV-2, O157:H7, influenza B Virus and gardnerella vaginalis [28–32]. However, its application to CT detection still requires optimization. For instance, Huang et al. described a CRISPR-Cas13a-based assay coupled with RPA for CT detection [33]. Although this method—inspired by the SHERLOCK platform [34]—demonstrates high sensitivity for RNA targets, its application to CT DNA necessitates an additional reverse transcription step, increasing operational complexity, cost, and contamination risk. To address CT and Neisseria gonorrhoeae (NG) co-infections, Luo et al. developed a dual CRISPR system incorporating both Cas12a and Cas13a [35], adapted from the SHERLOCKv2 platform [36]. This design enables simultaneous detection of DNA (CT) and RNA (NG) targets within a single reaction. Despite this advancement, the requirement for two Cas proteins increases system complexity and cost. Moreover, the persistence of a reverse transcription step and dependence on bulky instrumentation for signal reading limit its suitability for POCT.

Significant progress has also been made in domestic isothermal amplification technologies. For instance, Rendu Biotech’s SAT technology enables viable pathogen identification through RNA detection, while Ustar Biotech’s CPA platform provides a constant-temperature diagnostic solution for genital pathogens. Nevertheless, these approaches present specific limitations. The SAT method imposes stringent operational requirements due to RNA’s susceptibility to ubiquitous RNase degradation, which may lead to false-negative results. The CPA system relies on at least four primers—two cross primers and two displacement primers—that must function cooperatively by binding at multiple sites and forming complex secondary structures (e.g., cruciform configurations) to initiate amplification. This results in considerably greater design complexity and optimization effort compared to PCR or RAA, which typically require only two primers. Although Chen et al. developed a lateral flow assay by integrating LAMP with gold nanoparticles, achieving a sensitivity of 50 copies per test [37], the LAMP method is prone to aerosol contamination and utilizes six primer pairs, increasing its operational complexity. Therefore, optimizing the integration of isothermal amplification with CRISPR technology remains a critical challenge to address.

The “one-pot assay” addresses key limitations of conventional methods by integrating nucleic acid amplification and CRISPR detection within a single sealed reaction tube, effectively minimizing aerosol contamination and streamlining operational procedures. Our preliminary studies on CRISPR-based diagnostic methods for Clostridium difficile and monkeypox virus have shown that spatiotemporal compartmentalization and reaction system optimization significantly enhance the reaction kinetics between isothermal amplification and CRISPR-Cas12a detection. This spatiotemporal separation strategy prevents nonspecific activation of Cas12a during the amplification phase while improving thermal conduction efficiency and mixing homogeneity, collectively enhancing the sensitivity and reproducibility of the single-pot assay [38, 39]. Notably, the application of this one-pot methodology to C. trachomatis diagnosis has not yet been reported.

Based on this foundation, we developed a novel rapid detection system for Chlamydia trachomatis. This system integrates RAA isothermal amplification with CRISPR-Cas12a technology, employing specifically designed primers and crRNA targeting unique genomic regions of the pathogen. Through systematic optimization of reaction conditions and utilizing specially designed “tube-in-tube” reaction vessels, we achieved simultaneous nucleic acid amplification and detection within a single tube. Furthermore, we paired the system with a portable fluorescence detector capable of reading results through a terminal device, establishing a complete portable testing solution from sample to answer.

Preparation of primers and crRNA

The alignment software and primer design software used in this study were SnapGene and Primer Premier 6, respectively. Complete genomic sequence data of clinically prevalent serovars (D-K) of Chlamydia trachomatis were downloaded from the NCBI database. Through multiple sequence alignment analysis, highly conserved regions within the genome were ultimately identified and selected as target regions for subsequent primer design. Amplification primers were designed using primer design software, with the amplicon length precisely constrained to the 100–200 bp range. To ensure primer specificity, all candidate primers were rigorously validated using the Primer-BLAST tool on the NCBI website, confirming that the expected amplification products target only the C. trachomatis genome while excluding potential cross-reactivity with other microbial genomes or the human genome. To meet the specific requirements of the CRISPR-Cas12a detection system, the distribution patterns of Protospacer Adjacent Motif (PAM) sequences (TTTN) within the conserved regions were analyzed. Based on an optimal PAM site selection strategy, specific crRNAs were designed within the 20 bp downstream region of the selected PAM sites. Detailed information for all oligonucleotide sequences is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

RAA amplification procedure

The specific operational procedure is as follows: into a dedicated reaction tube pre-loaded with lyophilized enzyme powder, sequentially add 25 µL of A Buffer, 2.5 µL of B Buffer, 2 µL each of 10 µM forward and reverse primers, 5 µL of DNA template, and 13.5 µL of nuclease-free water to constitute a 50 µL reaction system, followed by vortex mixing. Place the reaction tube in a constant temperature metal bath and incubate precisely at 39 °C for 20 min to complete the isothermal amplification reaction. After amplification, purify the amplification products using a PCR product purification kit. Analyze the purified amplification products by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis, stain with GelRed nucleic acid dye, and observe and record the results under a gel imaging system.

Single-tube RAA amplification protocol

The optimized single-tube RAA reaction procedure is as follows: sequentially add 25 µL of Buffer A and 13.5 µL of nuclease-free water to a specialized reaction tube pre-loaded with lyophilized enzyme powder, and mix thoroughly to dissolve. Transfer 10 µL of the mixed solution to the bottom of a PCR tube, then sequentially add 2 µL of forward primer (10 µM), 2 µL of reverse primer (10 µM), 3 µL of DNA template, and 0.5 µL of Buffer B. After mixing, place the tube in a constant-temperature metal bath and incubate at 39 °C for exactly 20 min to complete the isothermal amplification reaction.

Two-step CRISPR-Cas12a reaction protocol

The two-step CRISPR-Cas12a reaction procedure involves transferring the RAA amplification product into a pre-prepared CRISPR reaction mixture containing 2 µL of 10×HOLMES Buffer, 2 µL of crRNA (200 nM), 1 µL of Cas12a protein (250 nM), 0.5 µL of ssDNA fluorescent probe (10 µM), and 2 µL of the RAA amplification product as the detection template, followed by the addition of 12.5 µL of nuclease-free water to reach a total volume of 20 µL. After preparation, the reaction system is immediately placed in an ABI7500 real-time quantitative PCR instrument, set to a constant temperature of 39 °C, and FAM fluorescence signals are continuously collected at 1-minute intervals for 20 min. Alternatively, by replacing the fluorescent reporter probe with a biotin-modified probe, the results can be read using lateral flow strips. However, the reaction system requires adjustment as follows: 2 µL of 10×HOLMES Buffer, 0.5 µL of 10 µM Cas12a protein, 2 µL of 10 µM crRNA, 2 µL of 1 µM biotin-modified ssDNA probe, and 2 µL of RAA amplification product, with 13 µL of nuclease-free water added to achieve a total volume of 20 µL.

In the presence of a positive target, the trans-cleavage activity of Cas12a is activated, cleaving the biotin probe. The resulting fragments form complexes with gold nanoparticles on the conjugate pad of the strip during lateral flow, which are then captured by immobilized secondary antibodies on the test line (T line), producing a visible signal. For negative targets, the intact biotin probe binds to gold nanoparticles, and the biotin moiety is captured by streptavidin on the control line (C line), resulting in a visible signal.

Construction of a single-tube RAA-CRISPR-Cas12a diagnostic platform

A tube-in-tube structure was adopted to construct an integrated one-pot detection system. Specifically, the pre-formulated RAA amplification mixture (as described previously) was pre-loaded at the bottom of the outer tube, while the CRISPR-Cas12a detection reagents were assembled in the inner tube, consisting of 6 µL of 10× HOLMES Buffer, 7 µL of crRNA (1 µM), 2 µL of Cas12a protein (1 µM), and 6 µL of ssDNA fluorescence probe (1 µM). The total volume in the inner tube was adjusted to 40 µL with nuclease-free water.

The detection procedure was performed as follows. First, the assembled tube-in-tube device was placed in a portable fluorescence detector and incubated precisely at 39 °C for 20 min to complete the isothermal amplification of the target sequence. Subsequently, a piercing rod was used to puncture the bottom membrane of the inner tube, and the contents of both tubes were mixed by brief centrifugation. Finally, the combined reaction system was transferred back to the portable handheld fluorescence detector, and fluorescence kinetic curves were monitored and transmitted through a terminal program for real-time analysis.

Limit of detection and cross-reactivity evaluation

The nucleic acid standard was subjected to 10-fold serial dilution, starting from an initial concentration of 10⁴ copies/mL and sequentially diluted to 10³ copies/mL, 10² copies/mL, 10¹ copies/mL, and 10⁰ copies/mL. The RAA-CRISPR-Cas12a platform was then used to detect the diluted nucleic acids, with the lowest concentration yielding a distinct fluorescence signal defined as the limit of detection.

To evaluate cross-reactivity, DNA extracts from Gardnerella vaginalis (10⁵ copies/mL), Trichomonas vaginalis (10⁴ copies/mL), Mycoplasma genitalium (10⁵ copies/mL), Candida albicans (OD value (A260/A280) measured by Thermo Fisher NanoDrop spectrophotometer: 1.96), Ureaplasma urealyticum (10⁴ copies/mL), Neisseria gonorrhoeae (10⁴ copies/mL), Candida glabrata (OD value (A260/A280): 2.02), and Candida parapsilosis (OD value (A260/A280): 1.78) were used as templates (DNA of C. glabrata, C. parapsilosis, and C. albicans was obtained by direct colony lysis using Takara lysis buffer). Detection with the RAA-CRISPR-Cas12a platform confirmed that only Chlamydia trachomatis yielded a positive result, while all other samples were negative, thus verifying the specificity of the assay.

qPCR reaction protocol

Quantitative PCR validation was performed using the probe-based method with the following reaction system: 10 µL of 2× Probe qPCR Mix, 0.4 µL of forward primer (10 µM), 0.4 µL of reverse primer (10 µM), 0.2 µL of probe (10 µM), and 4 µL of template DNA, supplemented with nuclease-free water to a final volume of 20 µL. Amplification was conducted on an ABI 7500 real-time quantitative PCR instrument under the following conditions: 37 °C for 5 min; 95 °C for 10 min; 45 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s.

Clinical specimen validation

This study strictly adhered to clinical research ethics guidelines and systematically collected 87 discarded vaginal and cervical swab samples from outpatient clinics after routine testing in the affiliated hospital laboratory. To meet POCT application requirements, sample pre-processing was performed using a sample release reagent for rapid nucleic acid release, eliminating the need for complex steps such as high-temperature incubation and high-speed centrifugation (the swab was immersed in the release reagent, rotated multiple times, mixed, and left to stand for 5 min). The methodological comparison followed a double-blind design: (1) The experimental group used a single-tube RAA-CRISPR-Cas12a detection system, with real-time fluorescence monitoring through a supporting portable device and terminal program; (2) The control group used an ABI 7500 real-time quantitative PCR system with a 2× Probe qPCR Mix detection kit, strictly following the manufacturer’s instructions. All test results were independently interpreted by two researchers, and the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value of the single-tube RAA-CRISPR-Cas12a detection platform were calculated.

Results

RAA primer screening and amplification system establishment

Four pairs of RAA amplification primers (designated F1R1, F2R2, F3R3, and F4R4) were designed targeting conserved regions. As shown in Supplementary Figure S1A, only the F1R1 and F4R4 primer pairs successfully amplified the Chlamydia trachomatis genome. Based on comparative analysis of electrophoresis band intensity and clarity, F1R1 was selected as the optimal primer pair for this study. Subsequently, the specificity of the F1R1 primer pair was validated to ensure detection coverage across all serovars. Supplementary Figure S1B demonstrates that F1R1 successfully amplified all targeted serovars.

Development of a two-step RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a-based molecular diagnostic platform for Chlamydia trachomatis

The workflow of the two-step RAA-CRISPR-Cas12a diagnostic platform is illustrated in Fig. 1A. After obtaining nucleic acid DNA from clinical vaginal or cervical swab specimens, isothermal RAA amplification is performed, and the amplification products are transferred to the CRISPR-Cas12a reaction system for subsequent enzymatic cleavage. We first designed C. trachomatis-specific Cas12a-crRNA by identifying PAM sites within the RAA target amplification region. As shown in Fig. 1B, the crRNA structure includes a classic stem-loop region (serving as the binding site for the Cas12a protein) and a target-complementary sequence. To validate the ability of this crRNA to guide Cas12a in exerting trans-cleavage activity, a fluorescent probe was used for detection. Experimental results demonstrated that the designed crRNA successfully guided Cas12a to recognize the target and activate trans-cleavage activity, generating a significant fluorescence signal upon cleavage of the fluorescent probe (Fig. 1C).

To further enhance the convenience and practicality of the detection method, we replaced the fluorescent probe with a biotin-modified probe and adopted lateral flow strips for result interpretation. Using C. trachomatis DNA at an initial concentration of 10⁴ copies/mL as template, the results after amplification and Cas12a-mediated trans-cleavage demonstrated that the test line (T line) successfully captured the cleavage products, resulting in visible coloration. In contrast, for the negative control where the Cas12a system remained inactive, the probe remained intact, thus preventing T line coloration while only the control line (C line) showed visible signal (Fig. 1D).

Subsequently, we optimized the Cas12a reaction system, focusing on the concentration ratio of Cas12a to crRNA. As shown in Supplementary Figure S2A, a series of concentration ratios were tested, and the results indicated that when the Cas12a concentration was 250 nM and the corresponding crRNA concentration was 200 nM, Cas12a exhibited optimal cleavage efficiency.

Finally, we evaluated the LOD and cross-reactivity of the two-step platform. The results demonstrated that the platform’s LOD was 10¹ copies/mL (Fig. 1E, Supplementary Figure S2B). No cross-reactivity was observed with other common reproductive tract pathogens (Fig. 1F, Supplementary Figures S2C, S2D, and S2E).

Development of a single-tube RAA-CRISPR-Cas12a molecular diagnostic platform for Chlamydia trachomatis

To address the complexity of the original two-step operational workflow, we developed a novel single-tube detection platform. We first systematically evaluated the impact of different RAA amplification volumes (5 µL, 10 µL, and 15 µL) on detection performance. Results demonstrated that the CRISPR-Cas12a system exhibited optimal cleavage efficiency with the 10 µL reaction system (Fig. 2A). The corresponding reaction systems are detailed in Supplementary Tables 2–4. Consequently, 10 µL was selected as the optimal reaction volume for subsequent experiments.

Building on this, we validated the feasibility of the single-tube approach by placing the CRISPR system reagents in the tube cap and the RAA reagents at the bottom of the tube, and further optimized the CRISPR system reaction volume. Experiments showed that when the RAA amplification volume was 15.5 µL and the CRISPR reaction volume was 40 µL, the single-tube method achieved ideal detection performance (Fig. 2B and C, and Supplementary Tables 5–10). Evaluation of the LOD revealed that the single-tube platform achieved an LOD of 10¹ copies/mL (Fig. 2D and E, and Supplementary Fig. 3A), comparable to that of the two-step method. In contrast, the LOD of qPCR was only 10² copies/mL (Fig. 2F and G), indicating that the RAA-CRISPR platform exhibits comparable sensitivity to qPCR.

To enhance detection convenience, we also paired the system with a portable fluorescence detection device (Fig. 2H). This instrument integrates four reaction wells with specialized caps, a display screen (featuring temperature control at 37–39 °C and real-time fluorescence data display), a data cable for real-time fluorescence data transmission, and a power cable. Furthermore, to achieve truly convenient and fully enclosed single-tube reactions, we adopted a unique tube-in-tube structure, as illustrated in Fig. 2I. This design consists of an inner tube pre-loaded with RAA amplification reagents and an outer tube containing the CRISPR reaction reagents. After RAA amplification is completed (20 min), a piercing rod is used to puncture the bottom of the inner tube. The top of the rod is then snapped off to seal the outer tube, followed by centrifugation to mix the reagents from both tubes. For positive samples, a distinct curve rise can be observed within 5 min of reaction at 39 °C, whereas negative samples show no such change (Fig. 2I and Supplementary Fig. 3B). This enables rapid on-site detection without reliance on large fluorescence instruments.

Clinical specimen validation

We collected a total of 87 residual reproductive tract swab specimens after routine clinical testing. The results showed that 29 positive and 58 negative samples were detected using the one-pot RAA-CRISPR-Cas12a platform. In comparison, the reference qPCR method detected 28 positive(mean Ct value of the positive clinical samples was 31.20 (range: 25.13–38.96, *n* = 87)) and 59 negative samples. The calculated performance metrics were as follows: sensitivity 100% (95% CI: 87.66% to 100.00%), specificity 98.31% (95% CI: 90.91% to 99.96%), positive predictive value 96.55% (95% CI: 80.04% to 99.49%), negative predictive value 100% (95% CI: 93.84% to 100.00%), and overall agreement 98.85% (95% CI: 93.76% to 99.97%). Notably, for specimens with relatively high Ct values, such as samples #24, #34, #35, and #41, distinct curve escalation trends and discrete fluorescence variations remained observable using the one-pot RAA-CRISPR-Cas12a platform (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Figure S4).

Discussion

Chlamydia trachomatis (CT) is the most prevalent bacterial sexually transmitted infection worldwide, and its epidemiological burden continues to grow. According to data from the U.S. CDC, there were 1.64 million reported cases of CT in 2023, with individuals aged 15–24 accounting for 55.8% of all reported cases [40, 41], underscoring the critical importance of early diagnosis. In this study, we developed and validated a novel detection method that integrates recombinase-aided amplification (RAA) and CRISPR-Cas12a technology into a single-tube platform for the visual, ultra-sensitive, and rapid detection of C. trachomatis. This approach fully leverages the programmability of CRISPR and the high efficiency of isothermal amplification, effectively overcoming the limitations of existing diagnostic techniques.

Our results demonstrate that the finely optimized single-tube RAA-CRISPR-Cas12a platform achieved a limit of detection (LOD) of 10¹ copies/mL for Chlamydia trachomatis standard DNA (Fig. 2D and E), whereas the LOD of qPCR was 10² copies/mL (Fig. 2F and G), indicating that the RAA-CRISPR-Cas12a platform exhibits comparable or even superior sensitivity to qPCR. Cross-reactivity validation confirmed no reactivity with other common urogenital pathogens (Fig. 1G). In a clinical evaluation comprising 87 samples, the method showed 96.55% concordance with the reference qPCR assay (Fig. 3C). Notably, the incorporation of a rapid swab-lysis buffer (specifically applied to clinical samples for immediate nucleic acid release after specimen collection) and a portable fluorescence analyzer significantly enhanced its point-of-care applicability, enabling visual interpretation without compromising performance. Even in samples with high Ct values, the terminal fluorescence curves displayed a distinct rise, clearly distinguishable from negative controls (Fig. 3B).

This system fully leverages the synergistic effect of RAA and Cas12a: RAA enables rapid nucleic acid amplification under isothermal conditions with minimal equipment requirements, while Cas12a provides sequence-specific recognition and signal amplification through its trans-cleavage activity. This dual-stage amplification strategy—where RAA amplifies the target nucleic acid and Cas12a amplifies the detection signal—effectively eliminates the reliance on expensive fluorescence detection instruments, representing the mainstream technical pathway in current CRISPR-based diagnostics. It should be noted that although standalone isothermal amplification techniques (such as LAMP or RPA) could theoretically be read directly via fluorescence or lateral flow strips, their implementation requires stringent conditions: first, specific hybridization probes must be designed, and the experimental design must carefully account for potential dimer formation between probes and primers, as such nonspecific interactions readily lead to false-positive results. Consequently, the screening and optimization of specific probes become a critical technical challenge. Moreover, such methods often necessitate the addition of specialized enzymes such as endonuclease IV (Nfo) to the reagent system, further increasing process complexity.

In contrast, our “basic RAA + CRISPR” strategy offers multiple advantages. First, basic RAA amplification requires no complex probe design, achieving efficient target amplification using only specific primers. Subsequent CRISPR-Cas12a-mediated precise recognition and trans-cleavage effectively avoid non-specific signals common in traditional isothermal methods. Crucially, the single-tube system integrates amplification and detection within a sealed container, fundamentally preventing aerosol contamination. This modular design not only streamlines the workflow but also significantly enhances detection specificity and reliability through CRISPR’s inherent sequence recognition capability. In terms of efficiency, our method completes the entire process in approximately 30 min (including 5 min for nucleic acid release, 20 min for RAA amplification, and 5 min for CRISPR detection), whereas conventional qPCR requires over 40 min for amplification alone. Furthermore, the introduced portable fluorescence detector is compact, integrates a FAM detection channel for real-time fluorescence monitoring, and operates at a constant 39 °C without complex temperature control programs. Result interpretation is intuitive—a distinct curve rise indicates positivity—greatly enhancing practicality.

Looking ahead, the RAA-CRISPR-Cas12a architecture offers excellent extensibility for detecting other pathogens or antimicrobial resistance markers. With appropriate crRNA redesign and validation, the platform can rapidly adapt to emerging infectious disease detection needs. Further end-user usability studies in resource-limited settings will also be crucial for optimizing device design and ensuring operational practicality.

While the present detection system demonstrates favorable analytical performance, certain limitations remain. First, the current platform is designed for a single target, CT. In clinical practice, however, co-infection with Neisseria gonorrhoeae (NG)—another major sexually transmitted pathogen—is notably common; epidemiological studies indicate a global CT/NG co-infection rate as high as 50% [42]. This reality aligns closely with the WHO Target Product Profile (TPP) guidelines for POCT of CT [43, 44], which explicitly recommend that, given the high frequency of co-infection, an ideal platform should be capable of simultaneously detecting both CT and NG to avoid missed diagnoses resulting from single-pathogen testing. Second, the present study employed only one pair of amplification primers targeting a specific region of CT. According to international diagnostic guidelines, a robust diagnostic platform should adopt a dual-target design to mitigate the risk of diagnostic escape due to target-site mutations. Finally, the number of clinically positive samples included in this study remains relatively limited, constrained by the local disease prevalence and the size of the patient population at the study site. Furthermore, all collected samples were residual specimens from routine clinical testing, which had undergone prior processing steps such as centrifugation and washing. This may have led to partial loss of pathogen load and nucleic acid degradation, resulting in a higher proportion of samples with elevated Ct values in qPCR analysis. On the other hand, the clinical cohort also did not contain a sufficient number of low-load samples. Additionally, the workflow itself presents practical limitations, as it requires extra manual steps including brief centrifugation, tube piercing, and transfer to the reader, which substantially diminishes the operational advantage of a single-tube format.

Moving forward, we will address the aforementioned limitations through systematic optimizations: First, we will construct a multiplex pathogen diagnostic platform by integrating crRNA guidance strategies and microfluidic chip technology for simultaneous detection of key reproductive tract pathogens. Second, through vacuum lyophilization optimization of core reaction components, we will ensure long-term reagent stability at ambient temperatures. Additionally, we will upgrade to a multi-channel portable detection device and conduct multi-center clinical validation to achieve an integrated “sample-to-answer” system.
