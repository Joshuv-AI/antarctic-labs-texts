Application of a metatranscriptomics technology, CSI-Dx, for the detection of pathogens associated with prosthetic joint infections
Authors: Justin R Wright, Jeremy R Chen See, Truc T Ly, Vasily Tokarev, Jordan Pellegrino, Logan Peachey, Samantha L C Anderson, Christine Y Walls, Maxwell Hosler, Alexander J Shope, Simmi Gulati, Krista O Toler, Regina Lamendella
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPreoperative identification of causal organism(s) is crucial for effective prosthetic joint infection treatment. Herein, we explore the clinical application of a novel metatranscriptomic (MT) workflow, CSI-Dx, to detect pathogens associated with prosthetic joint infection. MT provides insight into transcriptionally active microbes, overcoming limitations of culture-based and available molecular methods. This study included 340 human synovial fluid specimens subjected to CSI-Dx and traditional culture-based methods. Exploratory analyses were conducted to determine sensitivity and specificity of CSI-Dx for detecting clinically-relevant taxa. Our findings provide insights into the active microbial community composition of synovial fluid from arthroplasty patients and demonstrate the potential clinical utility of CSI-Dx for aiding prosthetic joint infection diagnosis. This approach offers potential for improved sensitivity and acceptable specificity compared to synovial fluid culture, enabling detection of culturable and non-culturable microorganisms. Furthermore, CSI-Dx provides valuable information on antimicrobial resistance gene expression. While further optimization is needed, integrating metatranscriptomic technologies like CSI-Dx into routine clinical practice can revolutionize prosthetic joint infection diagnosis by offering a comprehensive and active snapshot of associated pathogens.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74375-8.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Microbiology, Molecular biology, Bacterial infection

Introduction

Prosthetic joint infection (PJI) represents the most devastating complication associated with joint arthroplasty and is difficult for physicians to diagnose and treat. With an estimated 2.3–2.8% annual incidence and exponentially growing associated costs, developing reliable and cost-effective diagnostic tools and treatments for PJI is critical1,2. PJIs are the second leading cause of revision surgery, behind only aseptic loosening3. Predictions anticipate PJI will eventually account for over 60% of revision surgeries4. With total hip (THA) and knee (TKA) arthroplasty surgeries projected to increase within the next decade, the ability to accurately and timely diagnose PJI will become increasingly vital for healthcare professionals and patients. Aside from the high cost of PJI, the negative impact on patient outcomes is clear. Patients with an active PJI undergoing surgical revision have a two-fold increase in in-hospital mortality compared to patients undergoing aseptic surgical revision5. Additionally, PJI patient long-term mortality rates are significantly higher6. Therefore, accurate knowledge of active PJI is paramount during surgical revision.

Current definitions for diagnosing PJIs depend on culture-based methods to identify microorganisms, despite limitations regarding taxonomic resolution and variable sensitivity (39-70%)7. The advent of untargeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) methods, including shotgun metagenomics (MG) and metatranscriptomics (MT), has shown promise in enabling unbiased identification of microbial species in synovial fluid by circumventing drawbacks associated with culture-based methods and PCR-based techniques8–12. Although rare, PJI can result from slow-growing or difficult-to-culture microorganisms, such as certain mycobacteria13 or anaerobic bacteria14.

Besides culture, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) has also been applied to PJI diagnosis15–17. However, PCR’s ability to detect features of interest is dependent on the primers used, which can lead to false negatives. For example, BioFire’s Joint Infection Panel had an overall sensitivity of 56% in two studies due to causative organisms not being present on its panel16,17. Immune response biomarkers have also been used to diagnose PJI18,19, but relying on such tests alone would not reveal the infecting agent.

MG and MT can identify the causative organism(s) in addition to helping overcome the limitations of traditional culture-based methods by detecting and identifying unculturable or fastidious pathogens directly from a specimen, as well as PCR by virtue of being untargeted. MG targets total DNA, while MT targets RNA. Therefore, although MG cannot differentiate between active and latent microbial populations, MT reveals only transcriptionally active microbial taxa and genes. Consequently, MT could help reveal mechanisms involved in disease pathogenesis and potential therapeutic decisions.

While NGS methods hold excellent promise for pathogen detection, their application to disease diagnosis is an emerging field that requires further optimization for analytical and clinical validation in order to integrate into routine practice20. Additionally, they tend to be more costly than traditional diagnosis methods and do not fully replace some aspects of conventional methods, e.g. phenotypic antibiotic susceptibility testing. Still, these techniques could revolutionize PJI diagnosis by providing a comprehensive and unbiased view of the microbes associated with the infection. The current study seeks to characterize the microbial communities within synovial fluid from patients, 44 of which had a PJI, and 4 of which had native septic arthritis (NSA), based on 2018 ICM criteria21. It presents an exploratory clinical application of a novel MT sequencing workflow (Fig. 1), CSI-Dx, which can simultaneously identify > 100 PJI-associated microorganisms from synovial fluid. The clinical implications of this work will inform the future diagnostic application of NGS methods for PJI.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and preservation

Clinical samples were prospectively collected, and sample status was classified using the 2018 ICM Guidelines21–23 at a central clinical laboratory (CD Laboratories, Towson, MD) specializing in synovial fluid testing for joint infection (Table 1). Institutional Review Board approval of our experimental protocol with waiver of informed consent was granted (WIRB Copernicus Group IRB #201502222) to deidentify remnant samples and data for research. This study was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines/regulations. Hip and knee synovial fluid samples processed for comprehensive native septic arthritis (NSA) or PJI clinical diagnostic testing were deidentified and retained. A total of 340 samples (one per individual) were received within five days of aspiration and characterized into sample cohorts as described in Table 1.

A volume of 750 µL of synovial fluid aspirated from the hip or knee joint was transferred out of initial sample collection tubes into sterile nuclease-free 2 mL conical copolymer polypropylene screw-cap microcentrifuge tubes containing an equal volume of 2X DNA/RNA Shield (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA) for sample preservation. Environmental controls were collected weekly in the sample processing area by exposing a sterile swab to the atmosphere for 20 s and placing it in a sterile 50 mL conical copolymer polypropylene screw-cap centrifuge tube containing 2 mL of 2X DNA/RNA Shield (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA). DNA/RNA Shield was used because it can preserve samples for at least a month at ambient temperature (4oC-25oC), and it inactivates pathogens24. All samples were stored at 4 °C and shipped overnight with ice packs to Contamination Source Identification (CSI, Huntingdon, PA, USA). Upon receipt at CSI, samples were stored at 4 °C for a maximum of 7 days before processing or placed at -80 °C for long-term storage before processing.

Sample preparation for shotgun metatranscriptomic sequencing

Sample preparation, RNA extraction, quantification and library preparation were all performed on a Beckman Coulter Biomek i7 Automated Liquid Handler (Beckman Coulter Life Sciences, Indianapolis, IN, USA) under laminar flow (uniform air flow in the same direction to prevent contamination). Work surfaces were wiped down with 70% ethanol, followed by RNaseZap (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). A positive control (cultured Aliivibrio fischeri cells) and no template control (NTC) (300 µL 2X DNA/RNA Shield and 300 µL sterile nuclease free water) were processed in parallel with samples from extraction through sequencing for each sequencing run. Cultured Priestia megaterium (formerly Bacillus megaterium) cells were added as an internal control to samples and NTCs prior to lysing. P. megaterium and A. fischeri were chosen as controls because they are easy to culture and quantify, and they are not native to human synovial fluid25,26. A volume of 600 µL of sample or control was lysed using ZR BashingBead Lysis Tubes (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA). A total of 200 µL lysed supernatant underwent RNA extraction using the Zymo Research Quick DNA/RNA Pathogen MagBead Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA). RNA was eluted in 65 µL of DNase/RNase-free water and was purified using the RNA Clean and Concentrator kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA). Purified RNA was eluted in 15 µL of DNase/RNase-free water. DNA and RNA concentrations were quantified using the Quant-iT 1X dsDNA HS Assay Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and the Quant-iT RNA Assay Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), respectively. Results of quantification assays were read using the Tecan Infinite 200 PRO Plate Reader (Tecan U.S., Inc., Morrisville, NC, USA).

Concentrated RNA was prepared for shotgun metatranscriptome sequencing using the NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina, following the Section “Protocols for use with Purified mRNA or rRNA Depleted RNA” (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). Depletion of rRNA was not performed on the extracts prior to library preparation. Prepared libraries were quality checked using the Infinite 200 PRO (Tecan U.S., Inc., Morrisville, NC, USA) with a Quant-iT HS dsDNA assay kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), as well as the Agilent 2100 BioAnalyzer with the Agilent High Sensitivity DNA kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Libraries were pooled and purified using AMPure XP SPRI beads (Beckman Coulter, Indianapolis, IN, USA) to size select libraries from 215 to 500 bp. After purification and dilution to optimal loading concentration for sequencing, pooled runs received a 1% PhiX spike-in prior to loading and single-end 150 bp sequencing on the Illumina NextSeq 550 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). A subset of samples (Supplementary Table S1) was subjected to deeper sequencing alone and deeper sequencing in conjunction with CSI’s proprietary pathogen amplification (PPA) technique as described in WO2021042135A220. These samples were chosen based on volume of remaining library and culture-positive status.

Bioinformatic pipeline overview

Demultiplexed FASTQ files were processed with the RAPID-Dx data analysis pipeline as described in patent WO2022183133A127. Briefly, poor quality FASTQ sequences were filtered out, and adapters were trimmed with fastp using sliding window filtration set to a 4-base average Q score (a measure of error probability) of 20 discarding bases in the window and all bases to the right of it28. Reads smaller than 75 basepairs were discarded. Next, Homo sapiens sequences were removed with Kraken2 against a Homo sapiens database constructed from NCBI reference genome (GCF_000001405.40_GRCh38.p14). Host-free reads were then subject to a two-step annotation process. First, reads underwent a fast, k-mer (all possible substrings of the sequences) based, annotation against CSI’s curated database29,30. A k-mer based approach is used for initial annotation mainly due to increased speed compared to traditional alignment-based methods31. Then, in an attempt to improve resolution of clinically relevant taxa, a slow, alignment based, annotation was conducted with a user-defined list of taxa (Supplementary Table S2). All resulting annotation counts were collated into a data frame for further analysis.

Data normalization and pre-processing

Samples with fewer than 5,000,000 sequences remaining after fastp quality filtering and the removal of spurious taxa (taxa that only appeared in a single sample with a count of 1) were excluded from subsequent analysis. Host-associated annotations were also excluded from downstream analysis. Next, the remaining microbial annotation values were subject to counts-per-million (CPM) normalization, in which values were divided by the sum of their respective sample, and the quotient was then multiplied by one million. Resulting CPM values less than 1 were set to zero, and CPM values with a corresponding raw count of less than 3 were also set to zero. CPM values in the NTCs were then used to remove potential contamination by calculating per taxon CPM ratios (CPM-r) as the quotient of CPM values of samples and the respective CPM values of those taxa in the NTC from their corresponding sequencing runs as was done in Miller et al.32. After CPM-r values were calculated, values below one were set to zero. Lastly, all species sharing a genus with the sequencing run positive control (Aliivibrio) or the internal control (Priestia) were removed to counteract potential misclassifications.

Microbial diversity analysis

All calculations and data filtering were performed with the phyloseq R library unless specified otherwise33,34. Ambiguous features (unclassified, root, and other sequences) were removed from the annotation table, and Bray-Curtis distances for Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) were calculated based on CPM-r normalized data. Sample groups with more than ten samples were considered for all alpha and beta diversity statistical analyses. PERMANOVA tests to assess differences in clustering were performed using vegan and pairwiseAdonis R packages35,36. The PERMDISP2 method for analyzing differences in group dispersions was used to evaluate differences within group clustering variation (betadisper function vegan R). The Observed Features metric was calculated as the first step of alpha diversity analysis. Differences among groups were tested using Wilcoxon Rank Sum tests through the rstatix package37. Lastly, all p-values produced by adonis and the Wilcoxon Rank Sum tests were adjusted using the Holm method38 in R.

Differential features analysis

To eliminate spurious taxa, all features that had a CPM-r value of zero also had their raw values set to zero. Individual tables were then summarized at each taxonomic rank (one for phylum, one for class, etc.). MaAsLin239 was run with the default normalization and transformation methods (total sum scaling and log2 respectively) to compare each group pairwise. For each pair, results from all taxonomic levels were combined together, and the Benjamini-Hochberg40 adjusted p-value was recalculated using the rstatix R package. Features with recalculated q-value ≤ 0.25 were considered significant.

Functional annotation

Annotated microbial sequences produced by RAPID-Dx were functionally annotated with eggNOG-mapper v241 using DIAMOND42 on Prodigal43 predicted genes against the eggNOG 5.0.2 database44. Data for samples with at least one sequence corresponding to a KEGG Ortholog associated with antibiotic resistance45–48 were log-transformed and visualized with the ComplexHeatmap R package49.

Accuracy assessment

A list of CRR taxa was determined from culture-positive PJI results provided by CD Laboratories and categorized into groups based on physical characteristics (Supplementary Table S3). Samples with at least 5 million sequences after quality filtering and at least 750 microbial sequences were considered for downstream accuracy assessment. A cutoff of at least 750 microbial sequences resulted in the greatest sensitivity without increasing the false positive rate while retaining at least 75% of culture positive samples (Supplementary Fig. S1). Limit of Blank (LoB) values were calculated according to CLSI guidelines so that the resulting values would correspond to the highest value that 95% of samples would be expected to have for a given taxon if that taxon were truly absent50. LoB was initially calculated using Native as the reference and then again with Aseptic Arthroplasty.

Values ranging from 0 to 20, with a step size of 0.5, were tested for both CPM-r and LoB fold thresholds. Sensitivity at each potential threshold value was determined by dividing the number of culture-positive samples concordant with culture by the total number of culture-positive samples for that CRR taxa group. Similarly, specificity was calculated for each possible threshold value by dividing the number of samples in the group used for LoB calculations that had at least one CRR taxon above the threshold by the total number of samples in that group. Optimal CPM-r and LoB fold thresholds for each taxa group were chosen based on the greatest balanced accuracy obtained for both measurements. In cases when the best-balanced accuracy range included zero, the lowest non-zero value across the taxa groups with overlapping ranges was used as the value for that threshold.

Thresholds were applied to all eight taxa groupings (Supplementary Table S3). Overall sensitivity and specificity were recalculated, and confidence intervals for them were determined using the epiR R package51 with the exact method52. A summary of the global experimental design, data analysis, and per taxon LoB calculation is given in Fig. 1.

Results

Sequencing results

A total of 340 synovial fluid samples collected from knee and hip joints were subjected to MT library preparation and sequencing. These samples represented 11 different cohorts as described in Table 1. A subset of samples yielded low sequence counts, resulting in 328 samples with > 5,000,000 quality-filtered sequences per sample for analysis (Table 1; Fig. 1). In total, this project produced 6,133,196,735 raw sequences and 5,451,066,895 sequences after quality filtering.

Diversity of synovial fluid microbiota

Alpha diversity analysis showed that Native (mean 26.457 ± se 3.398) and Aseptic Arthroplasty samples (mean 25.783 ± se 3.309) had significantly higher diversity compared to Cx + PJI (mean 8.977 ± se 1.775) and Cx- PJI (mean 7.944 ± se 1.876) (Supplementary Fig. S2). The use of CPM-r normalization dramatically reduced overall observed microbial richness (Supplementary Fig. S3). However, a variety of taxa, including several CRR taxa, remained present in multiple samples among the four groups (Supplementary Fig. S4). Beta diversity analysis revealed significant differences in microbial community composition among the sample groups (Adonis, r2 = 0.028, p = 0.001). PCoA demonstrated that while all groups clustered closely towards the left of Axis 1, Aseptic Arthroplasty and Native samples exhibited large variation along Axis 1 (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. S5). Pairwise comparisons detected significant differences in global microbial community composition (Supplementary Table S4). Significant differences in dispersion of microbial communities were observed when comparing Native to Aseptic Arthroplasty and Cx- PJI to Aseptic Arthroplasty (Multivariate homogeneity of groups dispersions, F = 9.02, Holm adjusted p = 0.006) (Supplementary Fig. S6 and Supplementary Table S5). It is worth noting that differences in dispersion have the potential to lead to erroneously significant results, although the Adonis analysis appears to be relatively robust to dispersion effects35. In alignment with the significant Adonis results, MaAsLin2 identified multiple differential taxa (Supplementary Table S6).

Synovial fluid background assessment

LoB (Limit of Blank) values for CRR taxa were calculated twice, using the 95% classical approach method per CLSI50, for two sample groups, Native (N = 81) and Aseptic Arthroplasty (N = 152), respectively. Culture-positive groups (Cx + PJI and Cx + NSA) demonstrated higher CPM-r values for CRR taxa compared to culture-negative groups (Fig. 3). Aseptic Arthroplasty samples that tested positive using CSI-Dx exhibited significantly lower CPM-r values (Wilcoxon rank sum Holm adj. p = 0.000061) compared to culture-positive cohorts (mean 20.151 ± se 5.765 vs. mean 457.616 ± se 216.865). This trend was also consistent for Aseptic Arthroplasty samples that tested negative using CSI-Dx (mean 3.540 ± se 0.318). Since the Aseptic Arthroplasty cohort was larger than Native and consisted of individuals who had previously undergone arthroplasty, LoBs and thresholds were re-established using the Aseptic Arthroplasty group. LoB values derived from Native yielded 5 CRR taxa greater than 0, whereas LoBs from Aseptic Arthroplasty resulted in 10 CRR taxa above 0 (Supplementary Table S7).

Microbial composition of negative and environmental controls

Several taxa within the lineage of the internal control, Priestia megaterium, were among the top 10 most abundant taxa (Supplementary Fig. S7). Most communities (excluding human sequences) from environmental swabs were composed of either unclassified sequences or bacteria. Completely unclassified sequences accounted for an average of 35.85% of the community, while sequences classified as bacteria accounted for 39.05%. The most commonly identified species were Cutibacterium acnes and Priestia megaterium. In environmental swabs, Malassezia restricta ranked as the fifth most abundant feature identified to species level by median expression (0.05%) and the fourth by average abundance (0.25%). C. acnes was present in all swabs with a CPM value of at least 1, while M. restricta and P. megaterium were present in eight of the swabs. NTC samples exhibited a similar microbial composition to environmental swabs. The two most prominent features within the NTCs were Unclassified and Bacteria unclassified, accounting for average abundances of 39.72% and 35.65%, respectively. Similarly, the top three species by median expression in the NTCs were C. acnes, P. megaterium, and M. restricta.

Deep sequencing and PPA quality results

The proportion of samples concordant with culture increased with higher minimum microbial depth thresholds (Supplementary Table S8). Consequently, a subset of samples was retested using either deeper sequencing alone or deeper sequencing combined with CSI’s PPA technique. Retests that passed quality filtering included eight samples subject to PPA and six No-PPA, respectively. Samples that underwent PPA processing exhibited a higher proportion of internal control (IC) sequences compared to the original results (Supplemental Figs. 8–9) as well as an overall increased proportion of microbial sequences (Fig. 4).

Agreement with culture-negative results

A total of 46 samples from culture-negative groups (Aseptic Arthroplasty, Cx- PJI, Native, and PJI Inconclusive) yielded positive results with the CSI-Dx assay (Fig. 5A). CSI-Dx yielded one Cx- PJI and two Possible PJI samples with CRR taxa above thresholds (Supplementary Table S9). A total of 19 out of 81 Native samples that passed QC had at least one CRR taxon (Supplementary Table S9), yielding a specificity of 76.5% (95% CI: 65.8 − 85.2%) if Native samples alone are considered to be the true negative reference. Three taxa (Candida parapsilosis, Staphylococcus arlettae, and Staphylococcus epidermidis) were present in three samples, while Finegoldia magna was present in four samples. Additional CRR taxa (Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Pseudomonas fluorescens) were found in two Native samples each. Two Native samples contained multiple CRR taxa above the defined thresholds.

(c) Bar plot presenting the concordance results by taxa with taxa colored according to their respective groups. In the Aseptic Arthroplasty group, a total of 24 out of 152 samples passed QC and yielded at least one CRR taxon with the CSI-Dx assay, resulting in overall specificity of 84.2% (95% CI: 77.4 − 89.6%). However, if both Aseptic Arthroplasty and Native groups are together considered the true negative reference, specificity would be 81.5% (95% CI: 76.0 − 86.3%). The most prevalent taxon within Aseptic Arthroplasty was Staphylococcus haemolyticus, which was present in five samples. Staphylococcus aureus, Staphylococcus capitis, Candida parapsilosis and Corynebacterium pseudodiphtheriticum were also among the most prevalent taxa, each present in four Aseptic Arthroplasty samples. Additionally, eight other taxa were present above the defined thresholds in at least two Aseptic Arthroplasty samples (Supplementary Table S9). Among the Aseptic Arthroplasty group, ten samples yielded more than one CRR taxon above established thresholds (Supplementary Table S9).

Agreement with culture-positive results

A total of 48 samples with culture-positive results were included in the study. This included four native septic arthritis (NSA) and 44 PJI patients based on the 2018 ICM criteria, with the modification that the biomarker synovial fluid C-reactive protein (CRP) was used for scoring instead of serum CRP. Of these samples, 47 had at least five million sequences remaining after quality filtering, and six samples yielded low microbial sequences (< 750). However, two samples had retests that subsequently yielded enough microbial sequences to be included. Therefore, a total of 43 culture-positive samples were used to evaluate concordance. Of the 43 culture-positive samples, 32 (74.4%; 95% CI: 58.8-86.5%) successfully recaptured all cultured species in at least one variant of the CSI-Dx assay with the established thresholds (Supplementary Figs. S10-S25).

Species-level concordance with culture-positive samples for both original and retest samples remained similar (original: 70.7% (95% CI: 54.5 − 83.9%), best result (original + retests): 74.4% (95% CI: 58.8 − 86.5%)) (Fig. 5B). The CSI-Dx assay was unable to identify Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Staphylococcus epidermidis in culture-positive samples. The failure to recapture both taxa in most cases may be due in part to the use of NTCs for normalization and filtering in this study, as P. aeruginosa was present in all NTCs and S. epidermidis was present in all but two. Notably, S. epidermidis constituted a large portion of the culture-positive samples that passed QC, but it was only recaptured by CSI-Dx in three samples (Fig. 5C). Excluding S. epidermidis from the analysis yields an improved species-level concordance of 87.9% (95% CI: 71.8-96.6%) (Fig. 5D). A per-sample breakdown of concordance is summarized in Table 2.

Antibiotic resistance gene detection

Functional annotation revealed 22 KEGG Orthologs (KOs) containing antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) across 113 samples and controls (Supplementary Table S10). The maximum number of ARG KOs detected from a clinical sample was seven from Native, and a maximum of eight ARG KOs were detected among experimental controls from Environmental Swab samples. For samples and controls that had at least one ARG KO, it was observed that the NTCs and Environmental Swab groups had the highest average number of ARG KOs, with averages of 3.33 and 3.14, respectively. Though, this could have been due to controls generally having much lower proportions of human sequences and consequently yielding greater microbial coverage. Still, all groups with more than 10 samples, i.e., Native, Aseptic Arthroplasty, Cx + PJI, and Cx- PJI, had at least two samples with one or more ARG KOs (Fig. 6). Specifically, the Native group exhibited the highest proportion of samples containing antibiotic resistance, with 35.80% of the samples in this group having at least one ARG KO. Moreover, all PPA samples and half of the No-PPA samples were found to have at least one ARG KO. Despite having fewer samples, the Cx + NSA and Possible PJI groups also had at least one sample with an ARG KO.

Discussion

MT sequencing results presented in this study provide valuable insights into the microbial composition and diagnostic potential of the CSI-Dx assay within synovial fluid samples collected from knee and hip joints. A total of 328 samples yielded nearly six billion quality-filtered RNA sequences, representing the most extensive MT study of PJIs to date.

Differential diversity of native and prosthetic joint infections

Alpha diversity findings revealed Aseptic Arthroplasty and Native samples had higher microbial richness than infected samples. This finding suggests that infected synovial fluid samples are less diverse, and that uninfected synovial fluid may harbor a richer microbial diversity, indicating that a commensal microbial ecosystem may exist in uninfected joints. During infection, the successional changes to the synovial fluid microbial ecosystem could result in reduction of microbial diversity and might be associated with various factors, including disease severity, treatment history, and host immune response11.

Interestingly, pairwise comparisons of beta diversity between all groups detected significant differences in global microbial community compositions, possibly indicating that the microbial composition and clustering patterns within the synovial fluid samples may vary depending on the clinical condition and infection status as supported by previous research11,12. The observed dispersion differences could result from various factors, such as disease heterogeneity, disease progression stages, or variations in patient characteristics53. The significant differences in community composition, diversity, and dispersion patterns may have clinical implications for understanding joint infections and related diseases. Knowing which active taxa drive the clustering of infected versus uninfected samples leads to a better understanding of sentinel taxa that indicate healthy versus diseased synovial fluid ecosystems and PJI pathogenesis. For example, MaAsLin2 analysis identified the genus Staphylococcus as being associated with Cx + PJI compared to the Native cohort, and unclassified Staphylococcus was associated with Cx + PJI compared to Aseptic Arthroplasty (Supplementary Table S6).

Importance of environmental controls in leveraging NGS for clinical diagnostics

The analysis of microbial composition in negative and environmental controls provides critical insights into potential contamination and the presence of non-target organisms in this study54–56. Our findings highlight the importance of NTCs to differentiate true microbial signals from potential contamination or artifacts. We investigated the microbial composition of environmental swabs after excluding human sequences and examined the top 10 most abundant taxa in the negative and environmental controls (Supplementary Fig. S7). Interestingly, a large proportion of the communities in the environmental swabs were either completely unclassified or classified only as bacteria. This observation highlights the potential presence of environmental microbes that are not part of the known human-associated microbiota. Careful consideration and removal of such environmental contaminants are crucial for obtaining accurate results and avoiding false associations between microbial communities and clinical conditions54–56.

Among the identified species, Cutibacterium acnes, a common skin microbe55, was one of the most prevalent taxa in both the environmental swabs and the NTCs. The presence of C. acnes in the controls underscores the potential for contamination from skin-associated microbes, especially during sample collection and processing. Additionally, we identified the fungus Malassezia restricta as a common contaminant in environmental swabs. M. restricta is part of the skin mycobiome and can potentially contaminate samples during collection or processing57. The analysis of microbial composition in negative and environmental controls is crucial for identifying and addressing potential sources of contamination and non-target organisms in the study.

Clinical applicability of metatranscriptomics to PJI diagnostics

To leverage NGS for clinical application, we first sought to determine the Limit of Blank (LoB) for CRR taxa using the Native and Aseptic Arthroplasty groups. The Native cohort, consisting of individuals who had not previously undergone total hip or knee arthroplasty surgery, was first chosen to calculate the LoB of CRR taxa. Notably, samples within the culture-positive groups tended to have higher CPM-r values for CRR taxa. Interestingly, even among Aseptic Arthroplasty samples that tested positive using CSI-Dx, the CPM-r values were significantly lower than those of the culture-positive cohorts (Fig. 3). This finding is expected as Aseptic Arthroplasty consisted of individuals who were being considered for revision surgery and showed no signs of infection based on synovial fluid biomarker testing. Moreover, the sample size of the Aseptic Arthroplasty group was larger than that of the Native group, making it more suitable for calculating LoB and establishing thresholds. Overall, the results suggest that using Aseptic Arthroplasty samples for LoB calculations improved the balanced accuracy of the assay for detecting clinically relevant taxa. Our results demonstrate the importance of selecting appropriate sample cohorts for calculating LoB and establishing thresholds to ensure accurate and reliable detection of clinically relevant taxa in prosthetic joint infections.

Concordance analysis between the CSI-Dx assay and culture results was performed using 43 unique culture-positive samples. If we excluded Staphylococcus epidermidis, as BioFire’s Joint Panel does, likely due to the potential for erroneous results because of its ubiquity17, the overall concordance of the CSI-Dx assay would be 87.9% when only culture positives for other taxa are considered. Notably, S. epidermidis exhibited lower concordance (30%); this could be due to contamination during sample collection, processing steps during either of the culture-based or CSI-Dx processes58, or inherent challenges associated with the degree of human signal recovered compared to that of S. epidermidis in this study. Namely, on average, 13.4% of sequences in the NTCs were identified as human, while an average of 81.1% of sequences in the synovial fluid samples were identified as human, which can lead to instances where the infectious taxa are represented by very few sequences, resulting in false negatives, as observed with S. epidermidis. Specifically, at least one sequence annotated as S. epidermidis was observed in every sample that was culture-positive for it. However, only three S. epidermidis culture-positive samples underwent PPA; thus, further investigation is warranted to improve detection in synovial fluid. In addition, careful consideration of threshold adjustments is necessary to avoid false-positive results. Overall, the CSI-Dx assay demonstrates good agreement with culture-positive results and shows promise as a valuable tool for PJI organism identification.

Agreement with culture-negative results was assessed for the synovial fluid sample groups using the CSI-Dx assay, which demonstrated a specificity of 81.5% based on Aseptic Arthroplasty and Native cohorts (Fig. 5A). However, despite meeting the 2018 ICM criteria to be considered infected, Cx- PJI samples had a very low positivity rate, with only one sample containing CRR taxa above defined thresholds. These findings highlight the importance of refining and modifying the thresholds based on additional samples to achieve consistently high sensitivity and specificity of the CSI-Dx assay. Still, the results demonstrate high concordance between CSI-Dx and culture results for the detected microorganisms in infected synovial fluid specimens, showcasing the promise of MT technology in accurately identifying microorganisms associated with these infections. The specificity and sensitivity of this assay is comparable to that of other commercially available metagenomics assays32. However, in some cases, the assay’s sensitivity may be improved through deeper sequencing and removal of host-nucleic acids. PPA was performed on a subset of samples and resulted in improved concordance with culture (Table 2) by increasing microbial sequencing coverage (Fig. 4). Although more robust testing of PPA is needed, the higher proportion of microbial sequences and enhanced detection of the internal control demonstrates the potential of PPA in improving diagnostic sensitivity. Continued refinement and validation of thresholding and parametrization with larger sample sizes and diverse patient populations are critical in developing reliable and consistent MT technologies in clinical settings.

Active ARGs detected in synovial fluid sample cohorts

CSI-Dx comprehensively detects total RNA sequences; thus, in addition to taxonomic identification, we investigated the expression of ARGs within the synovial fluid samples. Functional annotation revealed the presence of 22 KEGG Orthologs containing ARGs across 113 samples and controls. Native samples and environmental swabs exhibited the highest average number of ARG KOs, suggesting the environment acts as an AMR reservoir and plays a key role in the dissemination of ARGs59,60. Still, the presence of ARGs in synovial fluid samples also emphasizes the importance of understanding antibiotic resistance patterns in joint infections to inform appropriate treatment strategies. Heatmap analysis of antibiotic resistance gene expression highlighted distinct patterns and differences among various sample groups (Fig. 6).

For example, aminoglycoside 3’-phosphotransferase I (aph3-I), was expressed more in infected specimens, and this aminoglycoside resistance gene encodes an enzyme responsible for modifying aminoglycoside antibiotics. This enzyme adds a phosphate group to the 3’-hydroxyl moiety of aminoglycosides, such as kanamycin and tobramycin, rendering them ineffective against bacteria expressing this resistance gene. In the context of PJIs, aminoglycoside resistance genes like aph3’-I can pose significant challenges to effective treatment, as aminoglycoside antibiotics are often used as an antibiotic regimen to treat PJIs61. When bacteria causing PJIs carry aminoglycoside resistance genes, the effectiveness of aminoglycoside antibiotics is compromised, leading to treatment failure, recurrent infections, and chronic PJI cases that are difficult to manage62,63. However, it is important to note that this aminoglycoside resistance gene was primarily found among PPA Cx + PJI samples, in addition to the associated NTC. More work is required to assess whether PPA increased sensitivity of ARG detection or if there is a potential contamination event that might have occurred during processing. Additionally, MT only detects ARGs that are expressed at the time of sampling, meaning other ARGs that could complicate treatment may be missed. Nonetheless, assays like CSI-Dx can help address specific antibiotic resistance mechanisms to determine the most efficacious infectious disease treatments. Further investigation into the functional annotation of the most prominent microbial genes and pathways can provide deeper insights into the genetic basis of antibiotic resistance and disease pathogenesis, which are crucial for developing targeted therapeutic approaches.

Limitations

While MT technologies like CSI-Dx offer potential for detecting transcriptionally active microbial taxa, limitations associated with this technology and the overall study exist. Environmental controls were only collected from the clinical laboratory that processed samples for sequencing. High-touch hospital surfaces have been found to serve as potential reservoirs for pathogen transmission, further highlighting the importance of considering hospital environmental samples in future studies64,65. Still, the presence of clinically relevant taxa, such as C. acnes, S. epidermidis, and P. aeruginosa, in controls (i.e., NTC) suggests contamination could potentially confound pathogen identification. Completely eliminating all contamination is virtually impossible66, but with increased microbial depth, functional annotation could help distinguish between contamination and infection.

Our results depend on the current established thresholds; as more samples are processed, parameters and thresholds may be modified to optimize accuracy metrics. Per sample QC thresholds (sequences remaining after filtering and sequences identified as microbial) were applied to ensure samples with sufficient data were considered for analysis, as most sequences were human. However, the specific threshold for sequences remaining after filtering (5 million sequences) was based on a metagenomics assay that used a different sample matrix32. In this study, larger microbial sequence counts correlated with culture-positive concordance. Consequently, a microbial threshold of 750 sequences was used because it yielded the best agreement with culture positive results while retaining at least 75% of those samples (Supplementary Table S8). The IC (P. megaterium) was not used for downstream quality control due to inconsistent detection among culture positive samples in which CSI-Dx recaptured the cultured taxon. Although microbial coverage was sufficient for taxonomic identification, it was often too low to comprehensively characterize expressed genes for many of the processed samples (Supplementary Table S9). Consequently, future work will continue to investigate methods of improving microbial depth and reducing non-mRNA signal to better comment on ARGs and other clinically relevant functional genes.

Because limited volumes of synovial fluid were collected for NGS processing, only a subset of samples was processed with deeper sequencing or deeper sequencing combined with CSI’s PPA host removal technique. Likewise, the limited number of Cx- PJI samples collected hindered our ability to fully assess the performance of CSI-Dx for infected samples that yielded negative culture results. Lastly, no formal resolution of samples discordant between CSI-Dx and clinical diagnosis was performed due to limitations in sample volume.

Conclusion

The CSI-Dx technology provides valuable information about the microbial composition and active ARGs in synovial fluid samples from knee and hip joints. The results demonstrate the potential of metatranscriptomics as a valuable tool for identification of PJI pathogens and associated active antibiotic resistance mechanisms. However, further analytical, and clinical validation of this assay are needed to comprehensively assess sensitivity and specificity, particularly regarding certain pathogens and ARG detection. These findings contribute to our understanding of the pathogenesis and diagnosis of PJI and may have implications for improved clinical management and treatment strategies.
