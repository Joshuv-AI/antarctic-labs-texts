Antimicrobials for neonates: evaluation and prescribing practices in Australian neonatal units
Authors: Naomi E Spotswood, Erin Grace, Peter A Dargaville, James G Beeson, Leah Hickey, Gabrielle M Haeusler, Penelope A Bryant, Celia Cooper, Amy K Keir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWhile antimicrobials are frequently prescribed in neonatal units, the way in which these prescriptions start, change and cease has had limited evaluation. This study aimed to provide a detailed description of infection evaluation and treatment practices in Australian neonatal healthcare facilities.MethodsA cross-sectional study of infection evaluations and antimicrobial prescriptions was conducted in three Australian neonatal units. All neonatal inpatients were audited across two five-day data capture periods per unit, with extended data collection for any neonate evaluated or treated for suspected or confirmed infection. This included determination of the reason(s) for commencing antimicrobials, the nature and duration of treatment, evaluation of whether infection had occurred, and identification of alternative diagnoses to infection.ResultsThe study captured 401 patient episodes for 388 patients (1256 patient days). Eighty-three evaluations for potential infection were identified, of which 70 (84%) had a course of antimicrobials prescribed. The antimicrobial use rate was 119 antimicrobial days per 1000 patient days. For 12/70 (17%) antimicrobial courses, a bacterial, fungal or viral infection was detected. Twelve groups of reasons were identified for starting antimicrobial therapy, and neonates with higher care complexity more frequently received antimicrobials. Neonates were evaluated for infection both within neonatal units and prior to arriving in neonatal units. Antimicrobial choice change occurred in 33/70 courses (47%). Antimicrobials with a moderate or high potential for antimicrobial resistance (AMR) were prescribed in 30/70 courses (43%), with a practice pattern of adding these agents mid-course. For courses where there was no microbiologically confirmed infection, 42% had an identifiable alternative diagnosis.ConclusionAntimicrobials are prescribed to neonates in varied hospital care contexts, for heterogenous indications, and most often without subsequent microbiological confirmation of infection. Antimicrobials with a propensity for AMR development are commonly prescribed. Renewed and expanded efforts to optimize neonatal antimicrobial prescribing practices are needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06748-z.Keywords: Infant, Newborn, Neonatal Sepsis, Antimicrobial Stewardship, Anti-Infective Agents, Clinical Decision-Making

Introduction

Antimicrobials are the most frequently prescribed medications for neonates who require hospital care [1]. They are lifesaving for neonates who have infections. However, these treatments are not always benign. Antimicrobials are the most common cause of medication errors and adverse drug events in neonatal units [2]. For preterm neonates, they can confer increased risks of mortality and serious morbidities such as necrotising enterocolitis and sepsis particularly where it later becomes apparent that infection is not microbiologically confirmed [3], or antimicrobial courses are prolonged [4, 5]. For neonates born at or near term, antibiotic use can lead to delayed breastfeeding, increased use of formula supplementation, and intravenous extravasations [6–8]. Antimicrobials impact the early life microbiome [9], a potential contributor to later health problems including allergy [10]. Further, human antimicrobial use is one of many drivers for the development of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), a problem of worldwide significance for neonates [11]. The process of infection evaluation and treatment affects entire families: parental anxiety, financial stress and difficulties bonding have all been described [8, 12]. There is an urgent need to optimize neonatal antimicrobial prescribing practices to ensure these vital medications are effectively used where infection is present, with excess antimicrobial exposure reduced.

Point prevalence estimates of antimicrobial prescribing have highlighted this problem of high neonatal antimicrobial exposure in Australia and worldwide across both low/middle and high income countries [13–16]. They show that most prescriptions are given in situations where infection is suspected but not microbiologically confirmed [14–16]. However, they do not fully reveal the heterogeneous patient populations who clinicians evaluate and treat for possible infections in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs), and provide limited insight to the circumstances in which antimicrobials are started, changed and ceased. Further, situations where clinicians consider infection but decide not to prescribe antimicrobials have not been evaluated. To address this gap and to inform future efforts to optimize antimicrobial use, we aimed to investigate (1) patterns of and (2) contributors to neonatal antimicrobial prescribing through detailed analysis of entire courses of antimicrobial therapy.

Methods

Study centers and approvals

Three tertiary-level Australian inpatient neonatal health services were included, located in South Australia, Victoria and Tasmania. Each are metropolitan services per Australia’s Rural, Remote and Metropolitan Area (RRMA) classifications for healthcare [17, 18]. These centers had patient populations which, combined, encompass a broad range of clinical situations encountered by neonatal healthcare facilities: all centres cared for preterm and term neonates, including those requiring surgery, retrieved from other facilities, and presenting from the community (see additional file 1: Table). Two were perinatal centers and one a centre without on-site birthing facilities. Each had antimicrobial prescribing guidelines for early onset neonatal infections, and one for late onset neonatal infections (see additional file 1: Table). All centers provided team-based care, with senior and junior medical staff at all and nurse practitioners in two [19]. Ethics approval was obtained for each center.

Definitions

Neonates were defined by age: <44 weeks corrected gestational age (CGA), with their day of birth designated day 0 [20]. Across the analysis, the term infection encompasses both infections without organ dysfunction, and infections resulting in sepsis with associated organ dysfunction [21, 22]. Microbiologically confirmed infection was defined per the Australian and New Zealand Neonatal Network’s data dictionary for bloodstream, central nervous system and viral infections [23], and by agreement of two investigators for other infection types. Criteria based on a consensus definition for neonatal infections were used to identify possible culture-negative bloodstream infections (see additional file 2: Table) [24]. The time distinction between early onset neonatal infection (EONI) and late onset neonatal infection (LONI) was antimicrobial commencement < 48 h of age or ≥ 48 h of age, respectively, based on the definition of the Australian and New Zealand Neonatal Network, of which each center was a participating unit [23]. Patient days (PD) were defined as inpatient presence at 08:00 on a data capture day. Antimicrobial days (AD) were each calendar day a neonate received one or more enteral, intravenous, or intramuscular antimicrobial(s) to treat a suspected or confirmed infection during a data capture period. The antimicrobial use rate (AUR) was calculated as AD per 1000 PD. The Drug Utilization 90 Index (DU90) comprised the number of antimicrobials that accounted for 90% of prescriptions [25]. An antimicrobial course was defined as the first to final day any antimicrobial agent was administered for the treatment of a suspected or confirmed infection, inclusive of dose and antimicrobial choice changes. Birthweight percentiles were evaluated per Fenton 2013 growth charts [26].

Data collection

Data were collected using the Research Electronic Data Capture platform (REDCap) [27, 28]. Data capture occurred in two five-day periods per center between December 2018 and July 2019. An audit of neonatal inpatients occurred at 08:00 each day across the NICU, neonatal high dependency unit (NHDU) and special care nursery (SCN) in all centers and additionally in birth suites and postnatal wards for the two centers which had an on-site obstetric service (see Table, additional file 1). Neonates in other inpatient areas such as pediatric intensive care or general pediatric wards were excluded. Demographic and clinical data for all admitted neonates were collected at 08:00 on the first day of a data capture period and at 08:00 on each subsequent data capture day for new admissions.

Data on infection evaluation, treatment and clinical outcomes were collected for all neonates who had an existing or newly commenced antimicrobial course during a data capture period. Topical antimicrobials and antimicrobial prescriptions identified as being for infection prophylaxis were excluded. While data capture occurred over five-day periods with AUR calculations limited to this time, all neonates who received antimicrobials had data collection extended to encompass their entire antimicrobial course from the time of initial evaluation for potential infection to the last day an antimicrobial was received, thus including days both before and after the data capture periods. Where antimicrobial courses commenced in peripheral centres data were collected from information and correspondence present within the study centre’s medical record. Where they extended beyond discharge or transfer from a study center, data were obtained from the documented antimicrobial discharge plan.

Data on infection evaluations without treatment were collected if one of the following laboratory tests was performed during a data capture period: full blood examination (FBE: this includes complete blood counts and blood films), C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin or blood culture. If it was unclear whether investigations had been conducted for infection evaluation or another reason (e.g. where an FBE may have been sent to evaluate for anemia rather than potential infection), verbal clarification was sought from the treating clinicians.

A further component to this study, practitioner surveys, occurred concurrent to the above data collection. Their analysis is reported separately [19, 29].

Data analysis

Data were analyzed with Microsoft® Excel and Graphpad Prism (version 10.1.1 for MacOS). Diagrams were created using Graphpad Prism (version 10.1.1 for MacOS) and draw.io (version 24.1.0). Three variables collected as free text (admission diagnosis; summarise the presenting problem(s), sign(s) or history that led to sepsis evaluation; final diagnosis for episode) were categorized by the corresponding author (NES).

For neonates admitted during both data capture periods for a site (n = 13), characteristics without the potential for change (e.g. birth weight) were included once, and data that could change over time (e.g. age, medical supports) were separately recorded. All evaluations for potential infection with or without treatment were included, including where a neonate had two or more occur within or across their centre’s data capture periods (n = 2).

Categorical data were analyzed using Fisher’s exact tests for two groups or Chi-Square tests for multiple groups. Continuous data in two groups were compared using Mann-Whitney tests for two groups and Kruskal-Wallis tests for three or more groups. Antimicrobial choices were evaluated against the Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Healthcare’s (ACSQH) Priority Antibacterial List for AMR containment [30]. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology for Newborn Infection (STROBE-NI) statement informed the preparation of this study [20].

Results

Center populations, infection evaluations and antimicrobial use

This study captured 401 patient episodes for 388 patients, encompassing 1256 patient days. The median gestation was 37 weeks, median birthweight 2900 g, and median age 3 days (Table 1). Some population differences were observed between the centers (see additional files 3 and 4: Tables). These included higher proportions of neonates in Center 1 requiring ventilation and/or central venous access, admission for reasons other than prematurity and a higher proportion of neonates retrieved from other healthcare facilities. Center 2 had a patient cohort that was more preterm than centers 1 or 3.

There were 83 evaluations for potential infection, 70 (84%) with and 13 (16%) without an antimicrobial course prescribed. Neonates evaluated for infection had a median age of 3 days (range 0-101) and median gestation of 37 weeks (range 23 to 41) (Table 1).

The overall AUR was 119 AD per 1000 PD. NICUs had higher AURs, followed by SCNs/NHDUs, then postnatal wards (Table 2). Center 1 had the highest AUR (Table 2) and prescribed a greater range of antimicrobials (n = 20), than centers 2 (n = 7) or 3 (n = 5).

Laboratory investigations

For the 70 antimicrobial courses, FBE, CRP and blood culture were sent for 68/70 (97%), 52/70 (74%), and 66/69 (96%; 1 case with missing data) respectively. Multiple FBEs were sent for 45/70 (64%), multiple CRPs for 29/70 (41%) and multiple blood cultures for 10/69 (14%) (see additional file 5: Figure). Urine and cerebrospinal fluid samples were cultured for 18/70 (26%) and 16/70 (23%), respectively, and 22/70 (31%) had viral pathogen detection tests.

For the 13 infection evaluations without a subsequent antimicrobial course, all had a FBE and most a CRP performed (13/13, 100% and 9/13, 69%, respectively). Procalcitonin was performed once (8%), as was blood culture (8%).

Antimicrobial courses: patient and prescriber characteristics

Neonates who received antimicrobials during a data capture period were more likely than neonates who did not to be admitted to a NICU (Table 1). Of neonates in the higher acuity areas of NICU, NHDU or SCN, those prescribed antimicrobials for potential infection were more likely to also be receiving intensive supports such as ventilation or inotropic infusions, and to have vascular access devices in situ (Table 3).

The median age at antimicrobial commencement was day 1 (interquartile range 0 to 13 days). Treatment commenced prior to 48 h of age for 38/70 courses (54%). Of courses to treat potential LONI, 27/32 (84%) were for neonates already admitted to hospital. Clinical evaluations which led to antimicrobial courses occurred in NICUs (24/70, 34%), SCNs/NHDUs (18/70, 26%), postnatal wards (5/70, 7%), emergency departments (3/70, 4%), birth suite (2/70, 3%), and peripheral hospitals prior to the neonate being transferred to the neonatal unit (18/70, 26%). Evaluations without subsequent antimicrobial treatment correspondingly occurred in NICUs (4/13, 31%), SCNs/NHDUs (5/13, 38%), postnatal wards (3/13, 23%), and an emergency department (1/13, 8%). The role of the practitioner writing the first prescription was identified for 57 antimicrobial courses: 4/57 (7%) were consultants, 8/57 (14%) neonatal nurse practitioners, 15/57 (26%) senior neonatal registrars/fellows and 30/57 (53%) registrars. Most antimicrobial courses commenced either before 8.30 am or after 5.00 pm (63%, 44/70). Twelve groups of reasons for commencing antimicrobials were identified (Fig. 1). Respiratory problems such as respiratory distress, apnea and increased ventilation requirements were the most frequent followed by concern about perinatal risk factors for infection such as prolonged rupture of membranes, preterm birth, concern about maternal chorioamnionitis or fever, Group B Streptococcus colonization status or maternal antimicrobial coverage during labor. More than one reason group was identified for 22/70 (31%).

Antimicrobial courses: choices, doses, duration and changes

A total of 205 prescriptions for 21 different antimicrobials were part of the 70 antimicrobial courses. Eight antimicrobials comprised the DU90,25 of which the most frequently prescribed were gentamicin (63/205; 31%), benzylpenicillin (47/205; 23%) and cefotaxime (25/205, 12%) (Table 4). Most antimicrobials were intravenous or intramuscular (69/70, 99%), of which 7 (10%) transitioned to oral therapy. Antimicrobial course durations ranged from 2 to 27 calendar days (median 3, IQR 3-5.3).

At their outset, 15/70 courses (21%) included one or more antibacterial(s) in Australia’s Priority Antibacterial List for AMR containment’s Review group due to their moderate to high AMR potential [30]. Antimicrobial choice(s) changed in 33/70 courses (47%). Seven (21%) of these were courses for microbiologically confirmed bacterial infections. One or more Review agent(s) were ceased for 6/33 (18%), added for 15/33 (45%), or changed to another Review agent(s) for 4/33 (12%). Of the mid-course Review agent additions, 10 occurred for possible EONI and 5 for possible LONI (10/18, 56% versus 5/15 33%, p = 0.30). Across entire course durations, 30/70 (43%) included one or more Review agent(s).

Microbiologically confirmed infections and alternative diagnoses

Microbiologically confirmed infections were identified during 12/70 antimicrobial courses (17%). Pathogens included Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Klebsiella pneumoniae, coagulase-negative Staphylococcus spp., Candida albicans, enterovirus, cytomegalovirus and human metapneumovirus. Three bacteria were multi-resistant: methicillin resistant S. aureus (one case), and extended-spectrum beta-lactamase producing E. coli (two cases). Infection sites included the bloodstream, central nervous system, urinary tract, respiratory tract, and skin, with some multi-site (see additional file 6: Figure). All aside from two cases with enterovirus received antimicrobial therapy targeted to the identified pathogen(s).

Of antimicrobial courses where the neonate had no microbiologically confirmed infection identified, 20/58 received one or more Review agent(s) [30] (34%) and 23/58 (40%) received four or more calendar days of antimicrobials. From the final diagnoses recorded, a reason other than infection which might have accounted for the clinical circumstances leading to the antimicrobial course was identified for 23/55 (42%; 3 cases insufficient data), most frequently respiratory (13/55, 24%) or neurologic (4/55, 7%) problems. Examples of such alternative diagnoses included hyaline membrane disease, pneumothorax and hypoxic ischaemic encephalopathy. Criteria for a possible culture-negative bloodstream infection were met for 13/57 (23%; one case insufficient data) [24]. Of these, 9 (69%) had an alternative diagnosis to infection identified.

Discussion

Antimicrobials are vital for neonates who have infections. However, they are not ultimately needed for many neonates who receive them, and can cause harm [3, 6, 8]. To better understand drivers for neonatal antimicrobial use we employed a cross-sectional design, with data collection extended beyond the typical timeframe employed in such studies [31]. This approach allowed a comprehensive analysis of the antecedents to antimicrobial use, changes across entire courses of antimicrobial therapy, and diagnostic outcomes. Beyond confirming that antimicrobial exposure amongst hospitalized neonates frequently occurs without microbiologic confirmation of infection [3, 14–16], our study reveals the heterogeneous patient population treated for possible infections in NICUs, and complexities present at both patient and health service levels when antimicrobial decisions are made.

Even with the inclusion of viral infections, not always accounted for in other studies [14–16], 83% of the antimicrobial treatment courses captured in our study had no pathogen identified. Some of these courses may have treated culture-negative infections. To explore this further, we used criteria based on a consensus definition for possible bloodstream infections without microbiologic confirmation [24]. We found many neonates who met these criteria also had an alternative diagnosis to infection. Evaluating how alternative diagnoses are identified was beyond the scope of this analysis. It is possible that they become more apparent with time, or that infection cannot be ruled out as a co-diagnosis. Regardless, this finding speaks to a need to improve the accuracy of the diagnostic process for both infection and its common alternative diagnoses, ideally from the time when antimicrobials are first considered.

We found that neonates who received antimicrobials in this study had a greater likelihood of receiving interventions such as mechanical ventilation and intravascular access than other inpatient neonates. In hospitals which provide higher care complexity, aggregate neonatal antimicrobial use is higher [32]. Our study reflects this finding, but at an individual patient level, with neonates receiving higher levels of support more likely to receive antimicrobials. A neonate who is critically unwell with infection may be expected to need such supports. However, most babies who received antimicrobials in this study, including those receiving intensive care supports, did not have confirmed infection. This pre-existing clinical complexity may complicate decisions to start, change or stop antimicrobials. Further work to understand how clinicians evaluate the possibility of infection amid such diagnostic noise requires further study.

In this study, we elected to reveal and evaluate the wide span of infection evaluation situations encountered by neonatal clinicians to a level of detail not previously reported in Australia [15, 16]. Our data elucidates the breadth of work clinicians in neonatal units undertake, including neonates who were admitted directly following birth, neonates admitted from home and those retrieved from other healthcare facilities. The distribution of these heterogeneous clinical situations can vary between centres, as we observed in this study. We noted one center had a higher AUR, the greatest number of antimicrobials used and most antimicrobial choice changes. This may reflect inter-center practice variations observed for various aspects of neonatal care, including infection management [33, 34], but may also be due to case mix differences as this center included a higher proportion of retrieved patients and patients with higher acuity interventions than the other two centres [32]. An in-depth comparison of the relative contributions of center case mix versus center-specific practices independent of case mix was beyond the scope of this analysis. To better understand these potential contributors to neonatal antimicrobial prescribing, further study on center-specific comparisons of Australian NICUs for neonatal inpatient subpopulations such as retrieved neonates and neonates receiving varying levels of in-hospital supports could be helpful. Nonetheless, both the diversity of neonatal practice and the individual characteristics of a neonatal unit must be considered in antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) efforts. Neonatal AMS employing quality improvement methods, with the centre’s context considered in change processes, have been demonstrated highly effective at reducing antimicrobial use rates in heterogeneous neonatal populations [35–37].

This study delineates both the diversity of patient characteristics and the wide array of healthcare situations in which neonates start antimicrobials. Antimicrobial courses captured in this analysis were for neonates who ranged from extremely preterm to term, across a wide spread of ages, and for a varied assortment of concerns identified in both hospitals and the community. They commenced in hospital areas both within and outside neonatal units, most frequently by medical staff who are not yet specialists, and often outside of regular working hours. We observed longer antimicrobial course durations and more frequent antimicrobial choice changes where evaluation for infection had occurred outside of the neonatal unit team’s care. This observation merits further evaluation in future studies and should be interpreted with caution given one centre had a higher proportion of neonates retrieved than the other two. Nonetheless, our study’s findings call for prescribing guidance that encompasses the diverse population of neonates receiving antimicrobials for suspected infections, is accessible to a range of practitioners, and is applicable to a range of settings. Further, quality improvement efforts to optimize neonatal antimicrobial prescribing must be shared beyond the bounds of specialist neonatal units to include all health service sectors involved in neonatal care [38].

Our study reveals that, while benzylpenicillin and gentamicin are the most frequently prescribed antimicrobials as noted in other studies [15, 16], antimicrobial choice frequently changes. We also identified a greater number of antimicrobials in the DU90 than in a previous Australian analysis, which may be due to our inclusion of antivirals and antifungals in addition to antibacterials [15]. While almost all microbiologically confirmed bacterial infections had antimicrobial changes in their course, most courses with changes were not prescribed for microbiologically confirmed bacterial infections. Further, antimicrobials in the PAL Review group [30] were more likely to have been prescribed by the end of a course than at its outset. This practice pattern is at odds with antimicrobial stewardship guidance which encourages prompt de-escalation of antimicrobials which promote AMR [39], and occurred in courses for both possible EONI and LONI. Drivers for these antimicrobial choice changes and the trend to adding higher-risk agents for AMR mid-course were beyond the scope of this analysis, and merit further attention in future studies. It is possible they reflect the uncertainties clinicians experience when evaluating the progress of an unwell neonate, with treatment changes precipitated by deterioration or lack of improvement in clinical state, particularly if without a clear diagnosis.

Regarding situations where infection is considered without antimicrobials then commencing, this study may not have captured some instances if no laboratory investigations were used for the evaluation. Nonetheless, situations where clinicians considered infection and sent a laboratory test without commencing antimicrobials were more than five times less frequent than those followed by an antimicrobial course. This reflects a practice pattern described in neonatal care where suspicion of infections virtually mandates antimicrobial therapy until sepsis can be ‘ruled out’ [6, 40], and again highlights a need for improved diagnostic strategies at the time of infection evaluation.

Our study appraises infection evaluation and antimicrobial prescribing practices for neonates to a level of detail rarely reported. Due to this study’s intention of collecting depth rather than breadth of data, a small number of centers were included. This is a limitation of our study, as the characteristics of a single center may have influenced the aggregate results, and the practice patterns of other neonatal units may have some differences to the three we evaluated. Further, as we did not include neonates whose care was outside of areas overseen by specialist neonatal teams, further study to evaluate antimicrobial prescribing practices in the wider range of settings where neonates receive care is required.

Conclusion

Antimicrobials are prescribed frequently to hospitalized neonates, most of whom ultimately are not proven to have a microbiologically confirmed infection. They commence in a wide array of situations, both within and outside of specialist neonatal units. Antimicrobials which can potentiate AMR are often prescribed. Improved, early diagnostic clarity for both infection and its alternative diagnoses may substantially assist in reducing neonatal antimicrobial exposure. Renewed and collaborative efforts to optimize antimicrobial prescribing practices for neonates are needed.
