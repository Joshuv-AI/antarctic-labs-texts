Saudi Heart Association Scientific Statement on Cardiovascular–Kidney–Liver-Metabolic (CKLM) Syndrome
Authors: Waleed Alhabeeb, Abdulhalim J. Kinsara, Ahmed Mitwalli, Ali A. Alshehri, Faisal Abaalkhail, Ismail Raslan, Khalid Aljohani, Mohammed Arafah, Muthana Al Sahlawi, Saud Alsifri, Suleiman M. Kharabsheh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular, kidney, metabolic, and liver diseases are closely interconnected and frequently coexist. While the cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) framework provides a unified model, increasing evidence supports a central role for hepatic disease, particularly metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), in this continuum. The Saudi Heart Association (SHA) therefore aimed to introduce the cardiovascular–kidney–liver–metabolic (CKLM) syndrome as an expanded framework and provide expert guidance on its pathophysiology, staging, screening, and management, with relevance to the Saudi population.MethodsA multidisciplinary expert panel convened under the auspices of the SHA conducted a comprehensive, non-systematic literature review and developed consensus-based recommendations through iterative discussion and critical appraisal of the evidence.Results and conclusionsCKLM syndrome is a progressive multisystem disorder driven by shared mechanisms, including insulin resistance, inflammation, and fibrosis, with bidirectional interactions across cardiovascular, kidney, metabolic, and hepatic domains. A stage-based framework is proposed to support risk stratification and guide management. Therapeutic strategies include agents with established cardiovascular, kidney and metabolic benefits, with emerging evidence supporting combination approaches. The high prevalence and coexistence of CKLM-related conditions in Saudi Arabia highlight the need for integrated care. The CKLM framework extends existing models by incorporating hepatic disease into the cardiometabolic–kidney continuum, enabling a more comprehensive approach to risk assessment and management. Multidisciplinary, stage-based strategies are recommended as they are expected to improve outcomes, although further validation is required.

1. Introduction

Cardiovascular, kidney, liver, and metabolic diseases are increasingly recognized as interrelated conditions that share common risk factors and overlapping pathophysiological mechanisms. Historically, these disorders have been described within partially overlapping frameworks, such as metabolic syndrome, cardiometabolic disease, and cardiorenal syndrome, each capturing specific dimensions of a broader, interconnected disease spectrum.

More recently, the American College of Cardiology (ACC) introduced the cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) syndrome as a unified clinical construct, providing the first formal, society-endorsed framework to define and stage the bidirectional interactions among these systems [1]. This model conceptualizes disease progression along a continuum, ranging from early metabolic dysfunction to advanced multiorgan involvement, and underscores the need for integrated approaches to risk assessment and management.

Following this framework, emerging population-level data have highlighted the substantial burden of CKM. Analyses from nationally representative cohorts in the United States and the United Kingdom suggest that nearly 80–90% of adults meet criteria for stage 2 or higher CKM, reflecting a high prevalence of subclinical or overt multisystem disease [2,3]. Concurrently, a growing body of evidence supports a central role for hepatic involvement, particularly metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), in the development and progression of cardiometabolic and kidney disorders [4–6]. MASLD frequently coexists with CKM and is independently associated with increased risks of cardiovascular events, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and all-cause mortality [7]. These observations highlight the liver as both a target organ and an active contributor to systemic metabolic dysregulation, inflammation, and fibrosis.

Taken together, these data provide a strong rationale for expanding the CKM framework to incorporate hepatic disease as a core component of this multisystem continuum. The proposed cardiovascular–kidney–liver–metabolic (CKLM) construct reflects this broader integration, acknowledging the bidirectional interactions between hepatic, metabolic, cardiovascular, and kidney pathways that collectively drive disease progression and adverse outcomes.

This expanded framework is particularly relevant in regions with a high burden of cardiometabolic disease, such as Saudi Arabia, where these conditions frequently coexist and contribute substantially to morbidity and mortality. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of death, with age-standardized mortality rates exceeding global averages [8,9]. The prevalence of metabolic syndrome has been estimated at 31.6%–39.8% [10], while CKD affects a significant proportion of the Saudi population (4.76%) and the Kingdom records some of the highest age-standardized incidence and mortality rates of CKD in the world [11,12]. Moreover, pooled estimates suggest that MASLD affects 16.8% of the general adult population [13].

Importantly, these conditions rarely occur in isolation. Registry data indicate that among patients with chronic heart failure, 52.7% and 66% have concomitant diabetes and hypertension, respectively, and approximately 22% have coexisting CKD or require dialysis [14]. Similarly, over half of patients with CKD have established CVD [15], and MASLD affects 59% of individuals with type 2 diabetes [13]. Moreover, over one-third of MASLD patients meet the criteria for metabolic syndrome [16], and MASLD is itself associated with adverse metabolic and inflammatory profiles which are linked to an increased risk of both CKD and CVD [17].

Collectively, these data underscore the need for a coordinated, multidisciplinary approach to early identification, risk stratification, and integrated management of patients with overlapping cardiovascular, kidney, metabolic, and hepatic disease. In this context, this Saudi Heart Association (SHA) scientific statement aims to provide expert guidance on the screening, prevention, and comprehensive management of CKLM in the Saudi population, with the goal of improving clinical outcomes and reducing the substantial healthcare burden associated with this emerging multisystem syndrome.

2. Methods

2.1. Expert panel and consensus methodology

A multidisciplinary panel of experts in cardiovascular medicine, nephrology, hepatology, endocrinology, and internal medicine was convened under the auspices of the SHA to develop this scientific statement. The panel composition was designed to reflect the multisystem nature of CKLM syndrome and the range of specialties involved in its clinical management.

The development process involved a series of structured virtual meetings during which panel members reviewed and discussed the current evidence base related to the epidemiology, pathophysiology, screening, and management of CKLM and its individual components. Emphasis was placed on integrating evidence across cardiovascular, kidney, metabolic, and hepatic domains to support a unified clinical framework.

Recommendations and key statements were developed through iterative discussion and expert consensus. Differences in opinion were resolved through critical appraisal of the available evidence and structured dialogue. Final statements reflect the collective judgment of the panel and are intended to provide pragmatic, clinically applicable guidance.

Recommendations are expressed using qualitative descriptors (e.g., “recommended,” “should be considered,” “may be considered”, “not recommended”) rather than formal grading systems, consistent with prior SHA scientific and position statements.

Draft sections were circulated among all authors for review and revision to ensure accuracy, coherence, and clinical relevance. Final approval was obtained from all contributors prior to submission.

2.2. Literature review

A comprehensive, non-systematic literature review was conducted to identify relevant studies addressing CKLM-related conditions, including CVD, CKD, metabolic dysfunction, and MASLD. Electronic databases (PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library) were searched without restriction on publication date or language.

Search strategies incorporated combinations of keywords related to CKM/CKLM constructs, cardiometabolic disease, MASLD/MASH, CKD, cardiovascular outcomes, and therapeutic interventions, combinations of controlled vocabulary and free-text terms, including but not limited to: “cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome”, “cardiovascular disease”, “atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease”, “heart failure”, “chronic kidney disease”, “kidney impairment”, “metabolic syndrome”, “insulin resistance”, “type 2 diabetes”, “obesity”, “metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease”, “MASLD”, “hepatic steatosis”, “liver fibrosis”, “cardiorenal syndrome”, “cardiometabolic risk”, “SGLT2 inhibitors”, “GLP-1 receptor agonists”, “tirzepatide”, “finerenone”, “renin–angiotensin system inhibitors”, “combination therapy”, and “Saudi Arabia”.

Additional references were identified through manual review of bibliographies and expert input. Priority was given to high-quality evidence, including randomized controlled trials, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and major international guidelines. Observational and mechanistic studies were also included where relevant to support pathophysiological insights and emerging concepts.

3. Pathophysiology

CKLM syndrome represents a tightly interconnected, multisystem disorder driven by adipose tissue dysfunction and sustained by reciprocal interactions among metabolic, cardiovascular, kidney, and hepatic pathways (Fig. 1). Central to this construct is excess and/or dysfunctional adiposity, particularly visceral and ectopic fat, which acts as both a metabolic reservoir and an active endocrine organ, promoting a chronic pro-inflammatory and pro-oxidative state that drives organ injury and disease progression across systems [18–21]. Adipose tissue dysfunction induces lipotoxicity, impairs insulin signaling, and promotes systemic inflammation through the release of adipokines, cytokines, and reactive oxygen species. Ectopic fat deposition in key organs, including the liver, myocardium, and kidneys, exerts local deleterious effects, while also amplifying systemic metabolic dysregulation [22–25]. Importantly, variability in fat distribution explains discordance between BMI and risk, with visceral adiposity conferring a disproportionate burden of cardiometabolic and organ-specific.

Complications even in individuals with normal BMI [26–31]. A more detailed elaboration of the pathophysiological links between adiposity and the development and progression of CVDs is available in the SHA position statement on obesity [32].

At the core of CKLM pathophysiology lies the metabolic syndrome construct, encompassing abdominal obesity, dysglycemia, dyslipidemia, and hypertension. These factors converge to drive endothelial dysfunction, atherogenesis, thrombosis, myocardial injury, fibrosis, and adverse remodeling, thereby contributing to the full spectrum of CVD as well as kidney and hepatic injury [4,33]. Progression to type 2 diabetes reflects β-cell dysfunction in the setting of chronic insulin resistance and markedly amplifies both vascular and kidney risk.

Mechanistically, CKLM is mediated through overlapping hemodynamic, metabolic, inflammatory, and fibrotic pathways [34–37]. Hyperglycemia induces glomerular hyperfiltration and systemic hypertension, initiating kidney injury, while endothelial dysfunction and shear stress promote both atherosclerosis and glomerulosclerosis [38,39]. At the cellular level, altered glucose and lipid metabolism generates advanced glycation end products, activates protein kinase C and JAK-STAT signaling, and increases oxidative stress, collectively driving persistent inflammation and fibrosis [38–40].

Chronic low-grade inflammation represents another unifying mechanism across CKLM. Activation of key signaling pathways such as NF-κB amplifies systemic inflammation, promoting cardiac remodeling, kidney fibrosis, and hepatic steatosis [41]. In parallel, adipose tissue–mediated insulin resistance, which is driven by ectopic lipid accumulation and inflammatory signaling, accelerates progression to type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia, CKD, and MASLD [42–48].

The cardiovascular system is affected through both direct and indirect mechanisms. Inflammation, oxidative stress, and dyslipidemia promote atherosclerotic plaque formation and instability, while endothelial dysfunction and thrombogenic pathways increase the risk of coronary and cerebrovascular events [49–59]. Hemodynamic stress related to obesity, including increased cardiac output, elevated filling pressures, and myocardial stiffness, drives structural remodeling, left ventricular hypertrophy, and heart failure, particularly heart failure with preserved ejection fraction [60–66]. In addition, ectopic fat deposition and fibrosis contribute to electrical remodeling, increasing susceptibility to atrial fibrillation, ventricular arrhythmias, and sudden cardiac death [23–25,67].

Kidney involvement is both a consequence and amplifier of systemic disease. CKD arises from combined hemodynamic stress (glomerular hyperfiltration, hypertension), metabolic injury (hyperglycemia, lipotoxicity), and both inflammatory and fibrotic pathways. Albuminuria and reduced glomerular filtration rate are strongly associated with increased cardiovascular risk and mortality [68,69]. In turn, CVD, particularly heart failure, can exacerbate kidney dysfunction through reduced perfusion, venous congestion, and neurohormonal activation, establishing a bidirectional cardiovascular-kidney cycle [70,71]. Additional CKD-related factors, including vascular calcification, anemia, and mineral-bone disorders, further compound cardiovascular injury [72,73].

The liver represents another central metabolic hub within CKLM. MASLD arises from insulin resistance, increased fatty acid flux, and hepatic lipid accumulation, leading to lipotoxicity, oxidative stress, and inflammation, which drive progression to steatohepatitis and fibrosis [74]. Beyond being a hepatic manifestation, MASLD contributes to systemic metabolic dysregulation and increases the risk of both CVD and CKD. Although not causal, MASLD is independently associated with CKD and adverse kidney outcomes [75–77], with more advanced disease conferring greater risk [76]. Shared mechanisms, including insulin resistance [78,79], metabolic syndrome [80,81], and chronic inflammation [82,83], underpin the bidirectional liver–kidney interaction.

Collectively, CKLM syndrome reflects a self-reinforcing network in which metabolic dysfunction, adipose tissue pathology, and organ-specific injury are tightly coupled. Metabolic, cardiovascular, kidney, and hepatic systems do not operate in isolation but instead amplify one another through shared pathophysiological pathways. Once established, these processes become progressively autonomous, emphasizing the importance of early, integrated, and mechanism-based intervention targeting upstream drivers, particularly visceral adiposity, insulin resistance, and inflammation, to mitigate downstream multiorgan damage.

4. Definition and staging

4.1. Definition

The SHA adopts the consensus definition of CKM syndrome proposed by the ACC Presidential Advisory [1], while extending its scope to explicitly incorporate liver disease, specifically MASLD, as a central and biologically integral component of the syndrome. This expanded framework, herein termed CKLM syndrome, reflects the growing recognition that hepatic steatosis and fibrosis are not merely comorbid conditions but active contributors to systemic metabolic dysregulation and cardiovascular risk.

Accordingly, CKLM syndrome is defined as: “A systemic disorder arising from the interconnected pathophysiology of cardiovascular, kidney, liver, and metabolic diseases that collectively drive adverse health outcomes.” This expanded definition is grounded in the shared pathobiological pathways linking metabolic dysregulation to progressive multi-organ injury giving rise to CKLM syndrome (detailed in Section 3). Over time, these processes lead to subclinical atherosclerosis, myocardial dysfunction, and progressive decline in kidney and liver function, ultimately increasing the risk of overt cardiovascular events, organ failure, and premature mortality.

4.2. Staging

The SHA adopts the 5-stage (0–4) framework proposed by the ACC, with modification to integrate liver disease severity (see Table 1). It is important to note that MASLD may be present across all stages; however, fibrosis stage provides important prognostic and therapeutic stratification, particularly from ≥F2 onward.

Stage 0: No CKLM risk factors

Stage 0 represents individuals with normal adiposity, normoglycemia, normotension, and a normal lipid profile, without evidence of CKD, MASLD, or subclinical/clinical CVD. This stage represents optimal cardiometabolic, kidney and hepatic health. Accordingly, the primary focus at this stage is the preservation of this low-risk state through primordial prevention strategies, including maintenance of healthy lifestyle behaviors and avoidance of risk factor acquisition. The goal of this approach is preventing the development of metabolic abnormalities and subsequent progression toward CKLM and end-organ involvement.

Stage 1: excess or dysfunctional adiposity

Stage 1 represents individuals with overweight, obesity, central adiposity, or adipose tissue dysfunction in the absence of additional metabolic risk factors, CKD, or MASLD.

As outlined in the recent SHA position statement on the management of obesity in the context of CVD [32], BMI remains a practical starting point for classifying adiposity, but should not be used in isolation given its inability to capture fat distribution or adipose tissue function. Ethnicity-specific considerations are also relevant, as certain populations (e.g., Asian descent) may exhibit increased cardiometabolic risk at lower BMI thresholds. Additional anthropometric measures are therefore recommended to better characterize risk, particularly waist circumference which provides a simple estimate of central adiposity, with sex-specific cutoffs associated with increased cardiometabolic risk (see Table 2).

As discussed in Section 3, excess and dysfunctional adipose tissue are mechanistically linked to CKLM syndrome and contribute to the development of metabolic risk factors, CKD, and MASLD over time. This stage should therefore be viewed as an early disease state rather than an isolated risk factor. From a management perspective, Stage 1 represents a key opportunity to intervene before the onset of overt metabolic or organ involvement. The primary focus is on weight reduction and improvement in adipose tissue function. While lifestyle measures remain the cornerstone, pharmacological therapies and metabolic/bariatric surgery may be appropriate in selected individuals, particularly those at higher risk or with inadequate response to non-pharmacological approaches (see Section 6).

Stage 2: metabolic risk factors and/or early organ involvement

Stage 2 represents individuals with one or more metabolic risk factors (e.g., dyslipidemia, hypertension, metabolic syndrome, or diabetes), with or without low-to moderate-risk CKD and/or MASLD with absent or mild fibrosis (F0–F1). This stage reflects established metabolic dysregulation with or without early target-organ involvement.

At this stage, the interplay between metabolic abnormalities and early organ injury (atherosclerotic changes, progressive nephron injury, and hepatic steatosis) becomes more evident. Although structural damage may still be limited, these pathways collectively accelerate progression toward more advanced CKLM stages. From a clinical perspective, Stage 2 represents a critical inflection point at which risk factor burden is established but potentially reversible. Management is therefore centered on intensive lifestyle modification along-side guideline-directed pharmacological therapy targeting individual risk factors, with the aim of preventing progression to overt CVD and kidney/liver failure.

Stage 3: subclinical CVD or risk equivalents

Stage 3 includes individuals with excess or dysfunctional adiposity and metabolic risk factors, with evidence of subclinical CVD or its risk equivalents. Subclinical atherosclerotic CVD may be identified by coronary artery calcification, coronary computed tomography angiography, carotid ultrasound, or an abnormal ankle–brachial index [91]. Subclinical heart failure may be detected by elevated cardiac biomarkers (e.g., natriuretic peptides or high-sensitivity troponin) and/or structural or functional abnormalities on echocardiography (see Table 2 and [92–94]). Given the association of subclinical CVD and elevated CVD risk [95–97], individuals with high predicted 10-year cardiovascular risk, high- or very high-risk CKD, and MASLD with significant fibrosis (F2–F3) are also considered in Stage 3 as risk- or near-risk-equivalents states that warrant similarly intensive preventive strategies [69,98–101].

Stage 3 reflects the transition from risk factor burden to measurable target-organ injury evident on the level of progression of atherosclerosis, myocardial remodeling, and fibrosis, alongside worsening kidney and liver injury. The presence of fibrosis, whether myocardial, kidney, or hepatic, signals a shift toward more advanced and less reversible disease processes. Clinically, Stage 3 represents a high-risk state requiring intensification of management. Beyond optimization of lifestyle measures, comprehensive and often combination pharmacological strategies are indicated to address all modifiable risk pathways. The primary objective is to halt or delay progression to overt clinical disease, recognizing that individuals at this stage derive substantial absolute benefit from aggressive, multi-domain intervention with benefit across the CKLM spectrum (see Section 7).

Stage 4: clinical CKLM disease

Stage 4 encompasses individuals with established clinical disease, including overt CVD (coronary heart disease, heart failure, stroke, peripheral artery disease, or atrial fibrillation), end-stage CKD, or MASLD with cirrhosis (F4 fibrosis). This stage represents advanced, clinically manifest multiorgan involvement.

At this stage, the cumulative effects of long-standing metabolic dysfunction and organ injury result in irreversible structural damage and functional impairment across one or more organ systems. Atherosclerotic plaque burden, myocardial remodeling, nephron loss, and hepatic fibrosis converge to drive high rates of adverse outcomes, including mortality, a risk that is amplified by the coexistence of cardiac, kidney, and hepatic dysfunction [3,7,102–104].

Stage 4 is further subclassified to reflect disease severity and complexity; Stage 4a represents clinical CVD without end-stage kidney or hepatic disease, while Stage 4 b reflects the most advanced stages characterized with kidney failure and/or cirrhosis. Management at this stage focuses on comprehensive secondary prevention, mitigation of disease progression, and reduction of complications. Care is inherently complex and requires coordinated, multidisciplinary approaches integrating cardiovascular, kidney, and hepatic management strategies, alongside optimization of metabolic control.

5. Screening for CKLM syndrome

Traditionally, the individual components of CKLM syndrome, namely cardiovascular, kidney, liver, and metabolic disorders, have been evaluated in isolation. However, given the shared pathophysiology and frequent coexistence, cross-domain screening approaches are essential to enable earlier detection and more effective prevention strategies. Despite this, guideline-compliant screening for CKD, liver disease, CVD, and metabolic syndrome remains suboptimal in routine clinical practice, across both primary and specialized care settings [105–109]. This gap persists even among high-risk populations and represents a missed opportunity for early identification and intervention across the CKLM spectrum.

Ideally, screening for CKLM syndrome should be conceptualized across the life course, recognizing that risk accumulation begins early and progresses over time. Cardiovascular, liver and kidney diseases develop over decades, with risk factors emerging as early as in utero, influenced by maternal obesity, hypertension, and metabolic health. These risks are further amplified during childhood by obesity, sedentary behavior, and adverse social determinants of health [110–119]. Early-life risk factors frequently track into later stages of life but remain modifiable, with a growing body of evidence across CKLM domains supporting the value of early identification and intervention to alter disease trajectories and improve long-term outcomes [120–124].

Pediatric screening recommendations are out of the scope of this statement and clinicians should refer to dedicated guidelines. In adults, screening typically focuses on the identification and management of modifiable risk factors and early organ involvement. Assessment of hypertension, dysglycemia, and dyslipidemia remains a cornerstone of prevention, as consistently emphasized by regional and international guidelines across cardiovascular, metabolic, hepatic, and kidney diseases. However, this approach is often implemented in a fragmented manner, without fully accounting for the interdependence of organ systems.

The bidirectional relationships between CKLM components further underscore the need for a more integrated approach. CVD increases the risk of CKD, while CKD, particularly reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and albuminuria, is associated with a markedly higher risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and mortality [68,98,125]. Similarly, metabolic syndrome and its components are strongly associated with the development of CVD, MASLD, and CKD, increasing risk by at least 50% and contributing to excess mortality [34,126–130].

Within this framework, CKM syndrome itself has been associated with significantly increased all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in individuals with MASLD, the majority of whom (≈85%) meet criteria for CKM Stage ≈2 [7]. Furthermore, the coexistence of MASLD and CKD confers a particularly high-risk phenotype, independently predicting 10-year mortality and reflecting an advanced state of cardiometabolic multimorbidity [131]. Collectively, these observations reinforce the need for systematic, multi-domain screening rather than organ-specific assessment to enhance CKLM health.

Screening is therefore recommended for all adults, with systematic and periodic reassessment across the CKLM spectrum, especially individuals in earlier stages (Stage 0–3). This longitudinal approach enables timely identification of disease progression and opportunities for intervention before the onset of irreversible organ damage (Stage 4). Screening should be conducted in accordance with local guideline recommendations, as summarized in Table 2, and should include assessment of adiposity, metabolic risk factors, kidney function, liver disease, and subclinical CVD.

Emerging initiatives support this shift toward holistic assessment; for example, structured programs targeting CKD, such as the European ABCDE initiative, highlight the feasibility and clinical value of systematic, risk-based screening incorporating markers of kidney function (albuminuria and eGFR) and metabolic risk factors (blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes) [132]. Validated risk assessment tools (e.g., SCORE2/SCORE2-OP, Pooled Cohort Equations) typically used for cardiovascular risk estimation only incorporate various metabolic and cardiovascular factors. To address this, the American Heart Association Predicting Risk of CVD EVENTs (PREVENT) equations have been developed as sex-specific and race-neutral tools that incorporate kidney function and capture a broader spectrum of cardiovascular outcomes [133]. By integrating these domains, PREVENT aligns more closely with the multisystem nature of CKM syndrome; however, the incremental improvement in discrimination is modest, and the absence of liver-related parameters highlights a persistent gap toward a fully integrated CKLM risk assessment model.

Overall, screening strategies should be comprehensive, leveraging available tools to identify individuals at risk, stratify disease stage, and guide timely intervention across the spectrum of CKLM syndrome. Importantly, screening should not be viewed as a one-time assessment but as a continuous process integrated into longitudinal care.

6. Management of CKLM: SHA recommendations

The SHA proposes a structured, stage-based approach to the management of CKLM syndrome that integrates foundational risk reduction with organ-specific therapeutic intensification across the disease continuum (Fig. 2). Central to this framework is the recognition that CKLM represents a multisystem, interconnected disorder, and therefore requires coordinated management rather than an organ-specific approach.

Across all CKLM stages, and in keeping with clinical guidelines of CKLM syndrome components, first-line management consists of comprehensive lifestyle optimization and aggressive risk factor control, including dyslipidemia, blood pressure, glycemia, and obesity. These measures should be implemented early and maintained longitudinally, alongside systematic screening for cardiovascular, kidney and liver disease, to enable timely identification of subclinical organ involvement.

Stage-specific management is guided by the extent of metabolic dysfunction and target-organ involvement. In early stages, particularly Stage 1, management prioritizes weight-centered interventions, including pharmacologic and procedural strategies where appropriate, to mitigate.

Downstream cardiometabolic risk. Medications currently approved as adjunct to lifestyle changes for chronic weight management of adults are glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists (liraglutide 3.0 mg, semaglutide 2.4 mg) [134–136], dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) and GLP-1 receptor agonist (tirzepatide) [137], naltrexone/bupropion [138], orlistat [139–142], and phentermine-topiramate [143]. Other agents primarily indicated for glycemic control, such as metformin and sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, may also result in modest weight reduction but are not approved specifically for weight management [144,145]. Surgical intervention can be considered in carefully selected patients for the treatment of excess adiposity, particularly obesity, as an adjunct to lifestyle and medical therapy, given its ability to achieve substantial and sustained weight loss and to improve metabolic, cardiovascular, and overall clinical outcomes [146–153].

In Stage 2, characterized by established metabolic risk factors with or without early kidney or liver involvement, treatment should expand to include targeted pharmacotherapy for metabolic disorders (e.g. dyslipidemia, diabetes, hypertension, obesity, metabolic syndrome). Wherever possible, priority should be given to agents demonstrating cardiovascular, kidney and/or hepatic benefit in higher-risk individuals, including renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) inhibitors, SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, and non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs). The evidence supporting these approaches is summarized in Section 7.

In Stage 3, defined by subclinical CVD and/or advanced metabolic organ involvement (including CKD or MASLD with fibrosis stages F2–F3), management requires intensification of both lifestyle and pharmacologic strategies. Statin therapy should be initiated or intensified in the presence of subclinical atherosclerosis in accordance with the recommendations of the corresponding SHA position statement [91], while therapies with proven heart failure benefit should be prioritized in individuals with subclinical cardiac dysfunction. In patients with MASLD/metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH) and clinically significant fibrosis, referral for specialist evaluation and consideration of liver-directed pharmacotherapy is recommended. Available therapeutic options include incretin-based therapies and thyroid hormone receptor-β agonists (e.g., resmetirom), while pioglitazone may also be considered for select patients with no cardiovascular involvement due to concerns regarding weight gain, fluid retention, and heart failure risk.

Stage 4 represents overt CVD with advanced kidney and/or liver involvement, necessitating full implementation of guideline-directed cardiovascular therapies, continued optimization of metabolic risk factors, and coordinated specialist care. At this stage, management priorities extend beyond risk factor modification to include prevention of disease progression, reduction of recurrent events, and preservation of organ function.

Across all stages, patients with persistent or high residual risk should receive therapies with established cardiovascular, kidney and/or hepatic benefit, including SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists or dual incretin therapies, often in combination (Section 7). Importantly, treatment decisions should be individualized based on overall risk profile, comorbidities, and patient preferences, with periodic reassessment to ensure optimal control and to minimize therapeutic inertia.

In this regard, given the complexity and bidirectional interactions inherent to CKLM syndrome, the SHA strongly emphasizes the implementation of multidisciplinary, patient-centered care models (Fig. 3). Management should be coordinated by a designated “owner of care”, preferably a primary care physician, with bidirectional collaboration across cardiology, nephrology, hepatology, and endocrinology. Although primary care infrastructure continues to evolve in some settings in Saudi Arabia, its implication in early screening, risk stratification, and coordination of care pathways is critical. Primary care physicians are essential components of clinical management through their role in reinforcing treatment adherence, management and monitoring of chronic diseases and comorbidities, and the early recognition of clinical deterioration and progression of disease [154–156]. The integration of primary care physician in multidisciplinary care models is not only aligned with the principles of the Saudi Model of Care, which positions primary care as central to chronic disease pathways, but also ensures that the proposed care model is implementable across all regions of Saudi Arabia, including settings with limited specialist availability. To that end, cross-specialty communication and return of care will both be crucial to ensure optimal patient outcomes and addressing fragmentation across health systems [157,158].

The SHA therefore recommends the adoption of structured, multidisciplinary team-based care pathways to address gaps in care delivery and improve outcomes in CKLM syndrome through value- and volume-based approaches. Evidence from integrated care initiatives, including the Cardiometabolic Care Alliance, demonstrates that such approaches can substantially improve risk factor control, increase uptake of guideline-directed therapies, and enhance patient-reported outcomes [159,160].

7. Evidence supporting therapeutic strategies in CKLM syndrome

7.1. Renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) inhibition

Given the central role of the RAAS in vascular, kidney, and metabolic regulation [161], RAAS inhibition remains a well-established therapeutic strategy in cardiovascular and kidney disease with potential translational relevance to CKLM syndrome. Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), and angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors (ARNI) are established agents for addressing metabolic risks and reducing both cardiovascular and kidney morbidity and mortality.

Robust meta-analytic evidence demonstrates that both ACE inhibitors and ARBs produce significant and broadly comparable reductions in major cardiovascular and kidney outcomes [162]. Importantly, observational and clinical data suggest that selected patients with advanced chronic kidney disease (CKD stages 4–5) may still derive benefit from RAAS blockade, particularly in delaying progression to kidney failure requiring replacement therapy [163]. In MASLD, randomized trial data remain limited and inconclusive; however, emerging evidence from observational cohorts suggests that RAAS inhibitors, especially ARBs, may be associated with improved survival and reduced major adverse liver and cardiovascular events [164,165].

ARNI therapy is primarily indicated in patients with heart failure, with or without concomitant CKD. Beyond established benefits in heart failure outcomes, accumulating evidence indicates additional favorable metabolic effects, including improvements in glycemic control and insulin sensitivity. Post-hoc analyses of PARADIGM-HF [166], supported by clinical and experimental studies [167–170], consistently demonstrate reductions in Hemoglobin A1c, decreased initiation of glucose-lowering therapy, and improved metabolic profiles with sacubitril/valsartan compared with ACE inhibitors.

From a kidney perspective, meta-analyses in heart failure populations suggest that ARNI therapy may be associated with reduced kidney impairment, slower eGFR decline, and lower risk of progression to end-stage kidney disease compared with ACE inhibitors or ARBs, particularly in cohorts with reduced ejection fraction [171–173]. In heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, a PARAGON-HF subanalysis demonstrated that sacubitril/valsartan reduced kidney events and attenuated eGFR decline compared with valsartan [174]. Nevertheless, data regarding adverse kidney effects, including worsening kidney function and hyperkalemia, remain heterogeneous, and the magnitude of kidney benefit appears to vary across phenotypes, with more consistent effects observed in heart failure populations. In addition, emerging data suggest potential ARNI benefits on hepatic outcomes, including attenuation of fibrosis progression in steatotic liver disease [175,176].

7.2. SGLT2 inhibitors

SGLT2 inhibitors have demonstrated clinical benefits extending well beyond glycemic control and modest weight reduction, with consistent evidence of cardiovascular and kidney risk reduction across a broad spectrum of patient populations, including those with and without established CVD [177,178].

In patients with established atherosclerotic CVD, large cardiovascular outcomes trials have shown that SGLT2 inhibitors reduce MACE. Specifically, the EMPA-REG OUTCOME and CANVAS trials demonstrated significant MACE reductions with empagliflozin and canagliflozin, respectively [179,180], while the DECLARE-TIMI 58 trial confirmed a significant reduction in hospitalization for heart failure with dapagliflozin [181]. In heart failure populations, SGLT2 inhibitors have consistently demonstrated reductions in cardiovascular mortality and heart failure hospitalizations, alongside improvements in patient-reported outcomes, irrespective of diabetes status or baseline heart failure severity [182–186]. Dedicated heart failure trials, including DAPA-HF and EMPEROR-Reduced, established these benefits in patients with reduced ejection fraction, with subsequent evidence supporting efficacy across the ejection fraction spectrum, including mildly reduced and preserved phenotypes [183,187,188]. In addition, meta-analytic data suggest a reduced risk of atrial fibrillation and flutter, although this outcome has not been evaluated as a primary endpoint in dedicated trials [189].

SGLT2 inhibitors also confer substantial kidney protection, with consistent effects observed in both diabetic and non-diabetic CKD. The DAPA-CKD and CREDENCE trials demonstrated significant reductions in kidney disease progression, end-stage kidney disease, and composite kidney or cardiovascular death with dapagliflozin and canagliflozin, respectively [190,191]. These benefits appear to be independent of baseline kidney function and albuminuria, as confirmed in meta-analyses [192]. Importantly, therapeutic benefit extends to patients with moderate-to-severe CKD (eGFR ≥20 mL/min/1.73 m2), supporting continued use until the initiation of kidney replacement therapy.

Although dedicated randomized trials in MASLD are lacking, meta-analyses of randomized controlled trial data indicate that SGLT2 inhibitors are associated with reductions in hepatic steatosis, along with improvements in liver enzymes and metabolic parameters [193,194]. These findings suggest a potential role for SGLT2 inhibition in addressing hepatic manifestations within the CKLM spectrum, although confirmatory, liver-specific outcomes data remain limited.

7.3. GLP1 receptor agonists

GLP-1 receptor agonists replicate the actions of endogenous incretin hormones, enhancing glucose-dependent insulin secretion and modulating multiple metabolic pathways [195]. In randomized clinical trials, GLP-1 receptor agonists have consistently demonstrated effective glycemic control, accompanied by modest improvements in cardiometabolic risk factors, including reductions in blood pressure, triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and total cholesterol (SUSTAIN-6, PIONEER-6) [196–199]. In addition, these agents induce clinically meaningful and sustained weight loss across diverse populations [197,200,201].

Cardiovascular outcomes trials have established the cardioprotective effects of GLP-1 receptor agonists in patients with type 2 diabetes. Trials including LEADER (liraglutide), SUSTAIN-6 (semaglutide), and REWIND (dulaglutide) demonstrated significant reductions in MACE and cardiovascular mortality compared with placebo [197,202,203], with PIONEER-6 extending these findings to oral semaglutide [204]. While earlier studies suggested neutral effects on heart failure hospitalization, emerging evidence indicates potential benefit in specific phenotypes. In the STEP-HFpEF trial, semaglutide 2.4 mg significantly improved heart failure–related symptoms, physical function, and body weight in patients with obesity-related heart failure with preserved ejection fraction [205]. With respect to arrhythmic risk, meta-analyses encompassing large patient populations have not demonstrated an increased incidence of atrial or ventricular arrhythmias with GLP-1 receptor agonists, although a modest increase in resting heart rate has been observed [206,207].

Kidney benefits have also been consistently reported. GLP-1 receptor agonists reduce the risk of kidney events and slow progression of diabetic kidney disease, as evidenced in SUSTAIN-6, LEADER, and REWIND, which showed reductions in new or worsening nephropathy [197,202,203]. More recently, the FLOW trial confirmed -kidney-protective effects of semaglutide, demonstrating a reduction in kidney failure, sustained eGFR decline, and death from kidney or cardiovascular causes in patients with type 2 diabetes and CKD [208].

In MASLD, accumulating evidence supports a potential therapeutic role for GLP-1 receptor agonists. Meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials indicate improvements in hepatic steatosis, liver enzymes, and cardiometabolic parameters [209]. Furthermore, semaglutide received regulatory approval in 2025 for the treatment of MASH in adults with moderate-to-advanced fibrosis, based on interim results from the phase 3 ESSENCE trial, which demonstrated significant histological improvement at 72 weeks with once-weekly semaglutide 2.4 mg [210].

7.4. Dual GIP/GLP1 receptor agonists

Tirzepatide is a first-in-class dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist that targets complementary incretin pathways, resulting in enhanced glucose-dependent insulin secretion, suppression of glucagon release, delayed gastric emptying, and marked appetite reduction [211,212].

Across the SURPASS program, tirzepatide produced pronounced and dose-dependent reductions in HbA1c, reaching approximately 2.3%–2.6%, and consistently demonstrated superior glycemic efficacy compared with both semaglutide and basal insulin in head-to-head trials (SURPASS-2, -3, and -4) [211–214]. In addition to glycemic effects, tirzepatide is associated with substantial weight loss. Clinical trials in both diabetes and obesity populations have shown greater weight reduction than active comparators, with SURMOUNT-1 reporting mean reductions of approximately 15%–22% and a high proportion of participants achieving clinically meaningful weight loss thresholds (≥10% and ≥20%) [213,215].

Cardiovascular data for tirzepatide are evolving. In individuals with obesity and prediabetes, treatment has been associated with improvements in predicted long-term cardiovascular risk and reduced progression to diabetes [216]. In the SURPASS-CVOT trial, tirzepatide met noninferiority criteria compared with dulaglutide for major cardiovascular events in a high-risk population with established ASCVD [217]. In the context of obesity-related HFpEF, the SUMMIT trial demonstrated a significant reduction in the composite of cardiovascular death or worsening heart failure events, accompanied by improvements in symptoms, functional capacity, and health-related quality of life [218]. These benefits were corroborated in expanded analyses showing consistent improvements across multiple patient-centered domains, including exercise tolerance and overall well-being [219].

Kidney findings are currently derived from secondary and exploratory analyses. Data from SURPASS and SUMMIT indicate favorable effects on albuminuria and markers of kidney function, including eGFR slope, cystatin C, and serum creatinine [218,220]. In SURPASS-CVOT, tirzepatide was associated with a slower rate of eGFR decline compared with dulaglutide in patients with high-risk CKD [217]. However, the absence of dedicated kidney outcomes trials remains an important limitation.

In liver disease, emerging data suggest potential therapeutic effects in MASH. Phase 2 findings from the SYNERGY-NASH trial demonstrated improvements in both steatohepatitis resolution and fibrosis parameters [215]. Imaging-based analyses, including a substudy of SURPASS-3, further support reductions in hepatic fat content with tirzepatide compared with insulin-based therapy [221]. Despite these encouraging signals, regulatory approval for MASH has not yet been established.

7.5. Non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (MRA)

Finerenone is a selective, nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist that mitigates aldosterone-driven sodium retention and exerts antiinflammatory and antifibrotic effects across cardiovascular-kidney tissues [222].

In patients with type 2 diabetes and CKD, finerenone has demonstrated clinically meaningful reductions in both kidney and cardiovascular outcomes. The FIDELIO-DKD trial showed a lower risk of kidney disease progression and kidney failure [223], while FIGARO-DKD demonstrated a reduction in cardiovascular events, driven largely by fewer heart failure hospitalizations and MACE [224]. These findings were reinforced in the FIDELITY pooled analysis, which confirmed consistent benefits across a wide range of CKD severity [225].

More recently, finerenone has shown efficacy in heart failure populations beyond traditional indications for steroidal MRAs. In FINEARTS-HF, treatment reduced the risk of worsening heart failure events and cardiovascular mortality in patients with HFpEF and HFmrEF [226,227]. Similarly, the FINE-HEART pooled analysis demonstrated reductions in all-cause mortality, heart failure hospitalization, and adverse kidney outcomes across nearly 19,000 participants from multiple trials [228].

Evidence regarding hepatic effects remains limited. Analyses from FIDELITY and related datasets evaluating liver enzymes and non-invasive markers of steatosis and fibrosis suggest no consistent or clinically meaningful improvement in liver-specific outcomes [229]. However, the cardiovascular-kidney benefits of finerenone appear to be preserved irrespective of baseline liver-related risk profiles.

Ongoing trials are investigating the broader applicability of finerenone, including in non-diabetic CKD (FIND-CKD) [NCT05047263], acute heart failure settings (REDEFINE-HF, CONFIRMATION-HF) [NCT06008197; NCT06024746], and combination-based approaches in CKD (CAPTIVATE) [NCT06058585].

7.6. Combination therapy

Targeting multiple, complementary biological pathways has emerged as a rational strategy to address the complex, multisystem nature of CKLM-related conditions. Early clinical evidence supports the potential for additive or synergistic effects with combination therapy.

The CONFIDENCE trial evaluated combined treatment with an SGLT2 inhibitor (empagliflozin) and finerenone in patients with type 2 diabetes and CKD, demonstrating a substantially higher proportion of patients achieving clinically meaningful reductions in albuminuria (>30%) compared with either agent alone [230] . Observational and real-world data further suggest improved kidney-related outcomes with combined finerenone and SGLT2 inhibitor therapy relative to monotherapy, although the increased risk of hyperkalemia with finerenone requires careful patient selection and monitoring. A network meta-analysis reported greater cardiovascular risk reduction with combined SGLT2 inhibitor and MRA therapy compared with either treatment alone in patients with type 2 diabetes, irrespective of CKD status [231]. Similarly, pooled analyses of randomized trials indicate that adding an MRA to SGLT2 inhibitor therapy results in greater reductions in albuminuria and systolic blood pressure, albeit with a modest increase in serum potassium levels [232].

More broadly, combination regimens incorporating GLP-1 receptor agonists have also been evaluated. A systematic review integrating randomized and real-world evidence suggests that dual therapy with GLP-1 receptor agonists and either SGLT2 inhibitors or finerenone may enhance cardiovascular-kidney protection beyond that achieved with single agents [233]. Furthermore, analyses of randomized trial data indicate that triple therapy with SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, and nonsteroidal MRAs may provide incremental benefits in reducing cardiovascular and kidney events and improving overall survival in patients with type 2 diabetes and albuminuria [234].

8. Conclusion

CKLM syndrome represents an emerging framework that integrates the complex and bidirectional interactions among cardiovascular, kidney, metabolic, and hepatic diseases. By extending the established CKM construct to incorporate liver involvement, the SHA aims to provide a more comprehensive representation of the multisystem processes and address the limitations of traditional organ-specific approaches to risk assessment and management. Instead, CKLM highlights the need for a unified, continuous stage-based model that emphasizes early identification of risk, recognition of subclinical organ involvement, and timely implementation of targeted interventions. Central to this paradigm is the prioritization of therapies with proven cardiovascular-kidney and metabolic benefit, alongside emerging strategies targeting hepatic disease.

However, important gaps remain, including the need for integrated risk prediction tools incorporating hepatic parameters, dedicated clinical trials in CKLM populations, and evidence-based strategies for combination therapy across disease domains. Future efforts should focus on validating the CKLM framework in diverse populations, refining screening and staging approaches, and evaluating multidisciplinary care models to improve clinical outcomes. In this context, this scientific statement provides a foundation for a more integrated and clinically actionable approach to the management of CKLM, particularly in Saudi Arabia, with the ultimate goal of reducing the substantial burden of cardiometabolic, kidney, and hepatic disease.
