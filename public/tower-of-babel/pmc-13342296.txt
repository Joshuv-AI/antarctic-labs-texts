Genome reshaping by Helitron transposons in Fusarium oxysporum TR4 affects secondary metabolism but not virulence
Authors: Jelmer Dijkstra, Sen Xie, Anouk C van Westerhoven, Yinping Li, Carolina Aguilera-Galvez, Giuliana Nakasato-Tagami, Xiaoqian Shi-Kunne, Desalegn W Etalo, Gert H J Kema
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransposable elements (TEs) are significant drivers of genome evolution, influencing the genome dynamics of clonal fungal pathogens such as those in the Fusarium oxysporum species complex (FOSC) that cause Fusarium wilt in over 100 plant hosts. Among these, Tropical Race 4 (TR4), a clonal lineage within the FOSC, poses a severe threat to global banana production. However, the contribution of TEs to genome variation and functional traits in TR4 remains poorly understood. Here, we investigated Helitron-associated structural variations in a TR4 strain from Mozambique (M1). This revealed two large deletions in core chromosomes associated with an active FoHeli1 Helitron transposon. One of these (464 kb) disrupted 151 genes, including the entire fusaric acid (FA) biosynthetic gene cluster, consequently abolishing FA production, altering secondary metabolite profiles, and increasing sensitivity to exogenous FA. Despite these metabolic changes, infection assays using wild-type, mutant, knock-out, and complemented strains demonstrated that FA production is dispensable for TR4 virulence in banana. Our study highlights the role of FoHeli1 in modulating the genetic and metabolic landscape of TR4, underscoring the broader impact of TEs on fungal genome evolution and functional diversification, especially in clonal lineages.Subject terms: Fungal genomics, Fungal pathogenesis, Fungal biology, Pathogens

Introduction

Transposable elements (TEs) are powerful drivers of genome evolution, shaping genetic diversity and adaptation across the Tree of Life1. These mobile genetic elements can comprise substantial portions of the host genome2 and influence critical processes, such as gene expression, splicing, deletion, recombination, and epigenetic regulation1,3,4. In plant-pathogenic fungi, TEs often associate closely with virulence-related genes and genomic compartments5–8, playing a key role in adaptation and virulence evolution9,10. This association is thought to facilitate fast adaptation in the arms race between pathogenic fungi and their hosts, particularly in clonal populations, by modulating gene stability and expression10–15. However, while beneficial for short-term adaptation, the close association may have long-term negative fitness consequences due to TE accumulation, subsequent genome instability, and deleterious TE insertions10.

The importance of TEs in fungal genome evolution is particularly evident in the Fusarium oxysporum species complex (FOSC). FOSC members can infect over 100 plant hosts16 and possess TE-enriched genome compartments that contribute to their adaptability and virulence5,17–20. Among FOSC lineages, the clonal Tropical Race 4 (TR4) lineage poses a severe threat to global banana production, a crop crucial for food security and local economics21–23, particularly in regions such as the Great Lakes area of Eastern and Central Africa24–26. TR4 is pathogenic to most banana cultivars, including the dominant cultivar Cavendish27, while Race 1 and Race 2 strains continue to threaten locally produced banana varieties25,28. Despite the available genomic data on TR4, the impact TEs have on its genome evolution and adaptability have seen very limited investigation.

Helitrons, a distinct class of DNA transposons, are widespread across eukaryotic lineages and replicate via a rolling circle or peel-and-paste mechanism2,29,30. They have been extensively studied in plants31–33 and animals33,34. Helitrons are also present in fungal genomes, but have seen less investigation compared to plants and animals30,35–37. Here, we address this gap by investigating the recent activity and impact of Helitron element FoHeli1 in the Mozambican TR4 strain M1, providing experimental evidence of large Helitron-driven structural variations.

We identified two major deletions on core chromosomes associated with FoHeli1 transposition, including a 464 kb deletion that eliminates the entire 12-gene biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC) for fusaric acid (FA) production38–40. As the first phytotoxin isolated from infected plants41, FA has been implicated in virulence against tomato, banana, and even mice42,43. As a phytotoxin, FA can cause dose-dependent necrosis in plant tissue and subsequent plant wilting42,44. Moreover, FA plays a role in the rhizosphere microbiome modulation in tomato45. However, its role in virulence appears context-dependent, as FA production is dispensable for Fusarium verticillioides during maize infection39.

Our study highlights the impact of Helitron activity on TR4 genome evolution, demonstrating their potential to reshape the genetic and metabolic landscape. We characterise FoHeli1 dynamics in the M1 strain and the reference TR4 strain II5, revealing extensive copy number variation across banana-infecting Fusarium strains. Furthermore, we assess the impact of the FA BGC loss on growth, metabolism, and pathogenicity, underscoring the broader implications of TE activity-associated genomic changes on fungal biology and host-pathogen interactions.

Results

Helitron activity associated loss of the fusaric acid biosynthetic gene cluster in M1

TR4 has recently been shown to be spreading beyond its initial incursion point in Mozambique46. The TR4 strains from Mozambique were generally found to be highly similar to other globally occurring TR4 strains46. However, whole genome comparisons with the TR4 reference genome reveals two large- scale deletions for strain M1, which was previously used for long-read whole genome sequencing19,46. These two large-scale deletions encompass 184 and 464 kb of the sub-telomeric regions of the M1 core chromosomes 2 and 3, respectively (Fig. 1a), representing approximately 1.25% of the 51 Mb sized genome of II520. Together, the deleted regions comprise 206 putative genes, showing a significant loss of genetic material.

Prediction of BGCs in the reference genome of II5 indicates that the entire FA BGC is located within the borders of the deletion on chromosome 3 (Fig. 1b). Further genomic analysis, BGC prediction, and PCR confirmed that M1 had lost the 12 fub genes comprising the FA BGC and that the cluster was not translocated elsewhere in the genome (Supplementary Fig. 1). Closely related strain M2, also isolated from Mozambique, does not lack the FA BGC (Supplementary Fig. 1). To determine the potential cause of these large-scale deletions, we examined the deletion sites on chromosomes 2 and 3 and found that both deleted sequences were replaced by a short sequence of approximately 6 kb. Further BLAST analysis of this fragment indicated the presence of a FoHeli1 Helitron, a TE that replicates by a rolling-circle mechanism33,37,47. The FoHeli1 copy on chromosome 3 shares 98.6% identity with FoHeli1 in the tomato infecting strain Fol4287 (MF805714.1). We conclude that a total of 648 kb was lost in M1, including the full-length FA BGC, and that this loss is associated with FoHeli1 activity (Fig. 1c).

To check whether loss of the FA BGC has occurred in other strains, we performed a blast analysis of the key biosynthesis gene fub1 (polyketide synthase) across a set of 35 publicly available Fusarium oxysporum genome assemblies48. This analysis shows the loss of the FA BGC in four additional strains (F23, F79, F105, and F109) that are pathogenic to common bean48,49. However, for these strains, no traces of FoHeli1 are present in the deleted region. Instead, in these strains, a large 174 kb region is observed that is absent in II5 (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Based on the large size of this fragment, in the otherwise conserved core chromosomes, we further analysed the genes present in this region for strain F109. Based on the presence of several key genes, we conclude that this large section that is absent in II5 encompasses a Starship element (Supplementary Fig. S2a, b)50. These results indicate that the FA BGC might be lost more commonly through different processes.

FoHeli1 activity in the TR4 lineage is recent and ongoing

The observed deletions indicate that Helitrons play an important role in the genome dynamics of Fusarium oxysporum. To further characterise FoHeli1 dynamics and search for additional copies, all TEs in the chromosomal assemblies of isolates II5 and M1 were predicted using Earl Grey51 (Supplementary Fig. 3), which detected 15 and 16 copies of FoHeli1 TEs in II5 and M1, respectively. Most copies are full-length ( ~ 6 kb), but both isolates contain a single copy of very short length corresponding to approximately the first 230 bases of the full-length FoHeli1 copies. This short-length sequence might represent the remnants of a (non-autonomous) FoHeli1. Similarly, II5 and M1 contain one and two copies of FoHeli1, respectively, with a 5’ truncation of several hundred bases, which may prevent further transposition. Transposable elements tend to accumulate mutations over time. By calculating the Kimura distance between TE sequences within the same family, we obtained a relative indication of when these elements last actively transposed52. FoHeli1 copies have the lowest average weighted Kimura distance in both II5 (0.04) and M1 (0.03) in comparison to all other predicted TEs which have a median average Kimura distance of 11.44 and 12.08 for II5 and M1, respectively, which suggests very recent activity (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 4). To analyse the similarity of FoHeli1 copies in the strains, we compared the sequences of all individual copies. In general, the copies cluster at the strain level, except for two M1 derived copies that cluster along with the II5 derived copies (Fig. 2b). Interestingly, the FoHeli1 copies located on accessory chromosome 12 of II5 formed a distinct cluster, suggesting intrachromosomal transpositions (Fig. 2b). Moreover, we observe that the vast majority of the FoHeli1 copies were found on different chromosomal locations between II5 and M1, further indicating recent activity of this TE (Fig. 2c). For both isolates, accessory chromosome 12 contains the most FoHeli1 copies in line with their generally higher TE density. Another way to determine FoHeli1 activity is to search for copies with multiple 5’ termini, which can arise from self-insertion events37. We used the previously determined FoHeli1 5’ terminal sequence37 and found that, of the 14 longer copies in II5, one copy contains two 5’ termini while another copy contains five 5’ termini. In M1, on the other hand, 5 out of 15 of the longer FoHeli1 copies contain two 5’ termini, while three copies contain three 5’ termini (Supplementary Fig. 5). PCR further confirmed the presence of a circular intermediate of FoHeli1 in both TR4 isolates, while being absent in the other banana-infecting Fusarium strains 36102 and CR1.1, which do not belong to the clonal TR4 lineage and lack any FoHeli1 copies (Fig. 2d). We also tested how recent FoHeli1 activity took place and its propensity to be involved in genomic changes in the TR4 lineage in strains, FocTR4_03242, UKTR4 and HN51, where FoHeli1 also had the lowest average weighted Kimura distance (Supplementary Figs. 6–8) and was, in several cases, located at the border of rearrangements (Supplementary Fig. 9), one of which was independently observed previously53. Around some of these rearrangements, repetitive stretches of sequence with lower levels of GC content are observed, which might have played an additional role in their occurrence (Supplementary Fig. 9). Taken together, we show that FoHeli1 activity in the TR4 lineage is very recent and most likely still ongoing.

FoHeli1 copy number is highly diverse in banana-infecting Fusarium strains

We used DeviaTE54 to estimate the FoHeli1 copy number of 56 Illumina sequenced banana-infecting Fusarium strains19,28. These estimates show that the copy number of FoHeli1 ranges between 9 and 36 in TR4 strains (Fig. 3a). Interestingly, the highest copy numbers (N = 25–36) are observed in a set of TR4 strains which have intrachromosomal duplications of accessory chromosome 12 (Fig. 3a)20. The copy number estimates for isolate II5 by DeviaTE are higher (N = 25) than the copy number obtained from Earl Grey (N = 15). This is caused by intrachromosomal duplications of chromosome 12, that are not resolved in the assembly20. In contrast to TR4, Race 1 and Race 2 strains show a much wider range of FoHeli1 copy number than TR4 (Fig. 3b). Some of the analysed Race 1 strains do not have any FoHeli1 copies, whereas all Race 2 strains contain a high number of FoHeli1 copies (N > 50). It has previously been observed that Race 2 strains have a larger genome size and contain more accessory material19. Comparing the genome sizes of 45 banana-infecting Fusarium strains with their FoHeli1 copy number shows a moderate positive correlation between the two factors (Supplementary Fig. 10). This indicates that the greater number of FoHeli1 elements in Race 2 strains is possibly due to a larger accessory genome size19. This would be in line with the observation in TR4, where FoHeli1 copies were more associated with the accessory genome (Fig. 2c). This wide range of FoHeli1 copy numbers in Race 1 and Race 2 could be indicative of further activity within the wider spectrum of banana-infecting Fusarium strains. Helitrons are known to occasionally capture genes or gene fragments during transposition33,35. Hence, we searched for such events, but identified only a single putative capture event in Race 2 strain C081 (Fig. 3c). In this case, we observe two FoHeli1 copies with several coding fragments in between. The upstream FoHeli1 copy lacks the 3’ hairpin sequence (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 11), which can lead to continued replication until another suitable termination signal is reached33. The downstream FoHeli1 copy instead lacks part of the 5’ hairpin sequence (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 12), which is essential for replication34. The downstream FoHeli1 copy has an intact 3’ hairpin sequence and thus could provide a suitable termination signal for the upstream FoHeli1 copy, leading to capture of the sequence between both FoHeli1 copies33. Although we observed only a single putative gene capture event, such events are likely to be missed in Illumina-sequenced strains due to the limitations of short-read sequencing for structural genomic analyses. Lastly, we observe that FoHeli1 copies are present in all TR4 strains, which all contain chromosome 12, but FoHeli1 is absent in the most closely related strain 36102, which lacks this chromosome19, thus raising questions on the origin of FoHeli1 within the TR4 lineage.

LC-MS analysis of M1 confirms the absence of fusaric acid

To investigate how the large-scale deletions on chromosomes 2 and 3 affect the overall metabolome, particularly FA biosynthesis, we performed untargeted metabolomics profiling on exometabolites of strains II5, M1 and M2 following in vitro cultivation. Firstly, comparison to a commercial FA standard confirmed the presence of FA and the derivative dehydrofusaric acid in II5 and their absence in M1 (Fig. 4a). Thus, the deletion associated with FoHeli1 activity abolished FA production in M1.

Negative fold change features are more abundant in M1; positive fold change features are more abundant in II5.

In total, 1594 metabolite features were detected in positive and negative ionisation modes across the samples, including the growing medium (PDB) (Fig. 4b). We performed Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to examine the variation in the exometabolite profile among strains II5, M1 and M2 and the growing medium. The first and the second principal components explained 46.2% and 15.3% of the variability, respectively, accounting for a cumulative 61.5% of the total variability in the metabolite data (Supplementary Fig. 13). The first principal component underscores the differences in metabolites between the growing medium and the fungal isolates, reflecting the metabolic activity of the fungi. In contrast, the second principal component depicts the variation in metabolite profiles between the three TR4 isolates. Additionally, a pairwise comparison of the individual metabolite features between II5 and M1 revealed that 146 features exhibited significant variation (FC > 1/FC < − 1 and P < 0.05), which included compounds from the alkaloids, amino acid and peptides, carbohydrates, fatty acids, polyketides, shikimate and phenylpropanoids, terpenoid pathways (Fig. 4b). In contrast, pairwise comparison between II5 and M2 showed 107 metabolite features with significant variation (Supplementary Fig. 14a), while 131 metabolite features showed significant variation between M1 and M2 (Supplementary Fig. 14b). Strain M2 did not show a significantly different FA production level compared with strain II5. In particular, comparative metabolomics of M1 with II5 showed either a complete absence or a significant reduction in metabolites belonging to the alkaloid pathway, including fusaric acid, dehydrofusaric acid, and other derivatives (Fig. 4b). Moreover, several metabolites associated with dipeptides showed a significantly higher abundance in II5. These differentially abundant dipeptides and related metabolites are listed in Supplementary Data 1. This differential abundance in dipeptides or other peptide-related metabolites could be due to the loss of additional genes in strain M1, beyond the FA BGC. In fact, some of these genes include aminopeptidases, aminotransferases, and a dipeptidyl peptidase (Supplementary Data 2). A GO term enrichment analysis for all the genes lost in M1 due to FoHeli1 activity does, however, not provide significantly enriched terms (adjusted p-value < 0.05) (Supplementary Data 3). Overall, the loss of the FA BGC and other genes has seemingly affected the abundance of a large number of metabolites of different classes (Supplementary Fig. 15), but did not significantly affect the production of the antimicrobial compounds bikaverin and beauvericin in the single liquid cultures (Supplementary Data 4).

Loss of the fusaric acid biosynthetic gene cluster impacts fungal development

FA has previously been shown to have an antimicrobial effect, including self-inhibition of the producing fungus40,55. Usually, fungal self-protection against FA occurs by the cluster-specific transporter (fub11) and the regulation of derivatives with reduced toxicity (fub12)40. As M1 has lost the entire FA BGC, both functions should be disrupted. In turn, we observed an elevated sensitivity to exogenously applied FA in M1 compared to II5 at concentrations of 100 ppm and higher (Fig. 5a, b). The fub1 knock-out strain grew similarly to II5, while the complementation strain Δfub1c showed a slightly lower FA sensitivity at higher concentrations (Fig. 5a, b). A similarly lower FA sensitivity at higher concentrations was observed for additional complementation lines (Supplementary Fig. 16). For other environmental stress factors, M1 tends to respond similarly to II520. Likewise, FA production has previously been implicated in growth and biomass levels40,55. To assess potential growth effects, we performed growth experiments with II5, M1, the fub1 knock-out strain Δfub1, and the complementation strain Δfub1c. Growth on regular PDA or FA production-promoting Czapek-Dox agar (CDA) showed no significant differences between all tested strains. Only M1 showed slightly higher growth rates on PDA plates, similar to the experiment using exogenous FA application (Fig. 5c). Loss of FA production has been shown previously to affect the production of other secondary metabolites, such as the pigment bikaverin42,56. To test this, we quantified the degree of pigmentation of II5, M1, Δfub1 and Δfub1c using ImageJ (Fig. 5d). Colonies of strain M1 displayed a wide range of pigmentation levels, but significantly more than II5. The knockout mutant, on the other hand, showed slightly more pigmentation, but this difference was not statistically significant. The Δfub1c complementation line showed pigmentation levels similar to those of II5.

Fusaric acid is not required for pathogenicity on banana

To test whether the large deletions affect M1 virulence and if FA contributes to banana infection, Cavendish plants were inoculated with wild-type strains II5, M1 or M2, as well as the newly generated Δfub1 mutant and the complemented strain Δfub1c. The results from the infection assays showed that isolates II5, M1 and M2 caused similar disease severity irrespective of the used spore concentration (Fig. 6a). Comparable to the M1 infection assay, no significant difference was observed between II5, the Δfub1 mutant, and the Δfub1c complementation line at the lower spore concentration (Fig. 6b). In general, a lower spore concentration leads to lower corm necrosis levels and reduced or delayed disease incidence and does not lead to significant differences between the different strains and mutants. No significant difference was observed between II5 and two Δfub1 mutants at the regular spore concentration (106) either (Supplementary Fig. 17). Together, these results indicate that the loss of the major toxin FA, due to deletions associated with FoHeli1 activity or CRISPR/Cas-9 mediated knockout of the core enzyme fub1, does not affect the overall virulence of TR4 on Cavendish plants.

Discussion

Our study reveals the genomic and metabolic dynamics driven by Helitron activity in the banana-infecting clonal TR4 lineage. By characterising large-scale deletions associated with the activity of the FoHeli1 transposon through bioinformatic analysis, metabolomic profiling, growth and pathogenicity assays, our work underscores the significant impact of transposable elements on genome dynamics and the adaptive potential of fungal pathogens. These deletions, notably the loss of the fusaric acid biosynthetic gene cluster, provide new insight into understanding the evolutionary trade-offs inherent in TE-driven genome remodelling.

Helitrons have been implicated to play a role in genomic variation across diverse eukaryotes1,2,30,33. Although Helitrons have seen some investigation within fungi30,35,57, including Fusarium oxysporum37, their impact on genome dynamics in fungal pathogens has remained underexplored, particularly in banana-infecting Fusarium. The recent and likely ongoing activity of FoHeli1 underscores how transposable elements can generate genetic variation in clonally reproducing pathogens. The low sequence diversity among FoHeli1 copies in M1 and II5 strains, their occurrence in distinct loci between the two strains, and the detection of a putative circular intermediate suggest an active phase of transposition that continues to reshape the TR4 genome. This activity aligns with the prior observation of Helitron-mediated genomic changes in other Fusarium strains, such as the loss of an effector gene associated with host specificity58. The loss of this effector AVR1 in the tomato infecting strain Fol4287 is likely the result of recombination between two (near) identical Helitron elements, leading to the excision of the sequence in between the Helitron elements58. Through such events, TEs, including Helitrons, and their activity can result in large-scale genomic changes29,59,60, such as deletions, highlighting their evolutionary significance. Based on the observed activity, the sequence breakpoints and high similarity between FoHeli1 copies, we propose that similar recombination events most parsimoniously explain the deletions observed in strain M1. Alternatively, the deletions could have occurred through other mechanisms, such as following erroneous DNA repair after a double-stranded break61 or by recombination between other TEs. The FoHeli1 copies could then have been inserted after the deletion took place. We, however, observed breakpoints for multiple deletions or rearrangements that align exactly with the FoHeli1 sequence and concluded that FoHeli1 activity must have been very recent, based on sequence diversity, and is likely still ongoing given the presence of circular intermediates. Together, these observations strongly support an association with FoHeli1 activity, although alternative mechanisms cannot be formally excluded. Experimental induction of FoHeli1 activity in a controlled genetic background would provide direct mechanistic validation.

Another indication of ongoing FoHeli1 activity within the TR4 lineage is our observation of multiple 5’ termini in many M1 FoHeli1 copies, which are likely to arise following self-insertion events37. The presence of FoHeli1 copies at the border of genome rearrangements in additional TR4 strains suggests a broader TR4-wide impact of FoHeli1 activity. However, some investigated strains lack FoHeli1 entirely, such as the less virulent strain 36102. This strain is most closely related to the clonal TR4 lineage and lacks accessory chromosome 1219. This raises important questions about the potential horizontal transfer origin of FoHeli1 within the TR4 lineage.

Although Helitrons have also been implicated in the capture and transposition of adjacent genes33,35, we did not observe this within either II5 or M1. Putative captured genes, however, were observed for the Race 2 strain C081. The availability of additional long-read genome assemblies could provide deeper insights into the frequency and mechanisms of gene capture by FoHeli1 and other Helitrons in banana-infecting Fusarium. Furthermore, such assemblies could improve understanding of FoHeli1-related structural genomic changes, their frequency and their mechanism across the Fusarium oxysporum species complex (FOSC) and their evolutionary implications for host-pathogen interactions.

Further estimation of FoHeli1 copy number in 56 sequenced banana-infecting Fusarium strains indicates substantial variation. TR4 strains recently shown to harbour intrachromosomal duplications of accessory chromosome 12 possess the highest number of FoHeli1 copies within the TR4 lineage20. Interestingly, all FoHeli1 copies on chromosome 12 of II5 are highly similar to one another and distinct from those on core chromosomes. These findings indicate a recent local transposition burst, as these copies are not part of any larger segmental duplications. Compared to TR4, Race 1 and Race 2 strains show a much wider range of FoHeli1 copy number variation. Although generally thought to replicate via a peel-and-paste mechanism33, experimental evidence in maize suggests transposition by excision can occur as well62. With the occurrence of FoHeli1 in distinct loci in all investigated TR4 genome assemblies, the degree of copy number variation is more suggestive of predominant transposition by excision rather than a peel-and-paste mechanism.

The loss of the FA BGC challenges the prevailing view of FA as a critical virulence factor in the Fusarium-banana interaction43,44. While FA has been associated with phytotoxicity, self-toxicity, antimicrobial activity, and rhizosphere modulation40,42,45,55,63–65, our infection assays demonstrate that its absence does not compromise TR4 virulence in banana. These findings indicate that FA’s contribution to virulence is likely context-dependent, potentially more significant in competitive microbial environments than in direct pathogenesis. Such flexibility highlights the adaptive resilience of TR4, where the loss of FA might be compensated through other genetic and metabolic pathways, for instance, by increasing production of other antimicrobial compounds, such as beauvericin, bikaverin, and equisetin, to maintain ecological fitness66,67. We did, however, not observe significant differences in beauvericin or bikaverin production between TR4 strains II5 and M1, although this was in single liquid cultures. Whether or not the observed changes in other metabolites, such as the differentially abundant dipeptides, play a role in this remains to be determined. Intriguingly, we also identified the loss of the FA BGC in another four Fusarium strains infecting common bean, suggesting that this cluster may be lost more frequently through diverse evolutionary mechanisms. In addition, FA production has been shown to be dispensable for virulence in other pathosystems, such as the interaction between F. verticillioides and maize39. In general, the levels of FA production between different Fusarium strains can differ significantly, irrespective of virulence against a host68,69. Importantly, our discovery of common bean infecting isolates lacking the FA BGC indicates that the presence of this generally conserved cluster likely does not correlate with virulence in isolates infecting common bean either49. Together, these findings indicate that FA’s role in virulence is likely context-dependent, with its importance varying across the host-pathogen systems and environmental conditions.

Genomic variation because of TE activity, such as FoHeli1, may facilitate adaptation to environmental pressures, including host resistance and agricultural practices9. This capacity for genomic remodelling, while beneficial for the pathogen, poses challenges for disease management, as it can lead to the emergence of new virulence traits or resistance mechanisms11,58,70,71. At the same time unrestricted TE activity and proliferation might have long-term consequences for the pathogen10. Expanding long-read genomic studies across the FOSC will allow for a better assessment of the impact of TEs on structural variation, gene loss and genome evolution.

Furthermore, the loss of FA production raises intriguing questions about the ecological trade-offs faced by TR4. The antimicrobial properties of FA likely confer advantages in niche competition45,63,68,72, yet its self-toxicity and energetic cost may render its production dispensable under certain conditions. The toxicity of FA has been linked to its chelating capacity, modulating the availability of metal ions essential for microbial survival42,73. On the other hand, other microbes have developed several ways to withstand higher concentrations of FA, such as efflux transporters or the increased production of siderophores73,74. Studies showing reduced phytotoxic and antimicrobial effects of FA in the presence of metals like copper, iron and zinc support this hypothesis42,73. This trade-off mirrors the broader evolutionary dynamics of secondary metabolites in fungal pathogens, where ecological versatility often necessitates balancing metabolic costs and adaptive benefits75–77.

In conclusion, this study highlights the dual role of TEs in generating novel genetic variation and affecting genomic instability. In addition, we show that the conserved FA-BGC can be lost in TR4 without affecting virulence on banana. The ongoing activity of FoHeli1 in TR4 exemplifies the dynamic interplay between TE activity, genome evolution and ecological adaptation in fungal pathogens. By elucidating the consequences of TE activity and their associated genomic changes, we advance our understanding of the impact of TEs on genome dynamics and secondary metabolism in a clonally reproducing pathogen.

Methods

Fungal strains, growth conditions and transformation

Fusarium Tropical Race 4 reference strain II5, strains M1 and M2 isolated from Mozambique19,46, and newly generated fub1 knockout strain Δfub1 and fub1c complementation mutants were used in this study. Strains were stored as conidial suspension stocks in 15% glycerol at −80 ˚C. General culturing of the fungal strains was performed using potato dextrose agar (PDA) at 25 ˚C.

The Δfub1 mutant was generated in the II5 background as previously described20. Complementation was performed using the same procedure instead using a DNA fragment containing the fub1 gene and 1 kb of upstream and downstream DNA fused with a hygromycin cassette by overlap extension PCR as a donor fragment. Complementation was done by ectopic integration using the same procedure as in Dijkstra et al. (2025) in the absence of a Cas9 vector. Used primers are listed in Supplementary Data 5. PCR confirmation of the generated mutant and complementation line are shown in Supplementary Figs. S18, S19.

Genome analysis

The previously published whole genome sequences of strains II5 and M1 were used for analysis19. Putative biosynthetic gene clusters were predicted for the genomes of strains II5 and M1 using Antismash version 7.1 (Fungal version) with standard relaxed detection strictness78. The FA BGC cluster and Starship representation were made using Clinker79. Chromosome alignments were made using syntenyPlotteR80. Information on the deleted genes in strain M1 are noted in Supplementary Data 2. Genome assemblies of F. oxysporum f. sp. pisi strains F23, F79, F105 and F109 were obtained from the Joint Genome Institute (JGI) Mycocosm portal81. Blast analysis for the presence or absence of the fub gene cluster and FoHeli1 are noted for 61 isolates in Supplementary Data 6. GO term enrichment analysis was performed using GOATOOLS82.

Transposable element analysis

Initial FoHeli1 identification was done using BLAST83 and comparison to known FoHeli1 sequences37. TEs in long-read sequenced strains II5, M1, FocTR4_0324284, UKTR485 and HN51 (GCA_027920445.1) were annotated and analysed using Earl Grey version 4 with the standard parameters51. Full-length FoHeli1 copies were aligned using Clustal Omega to determine the number of SNPs between each copy86. The FoHeli1 chromosomal location plot was made with the use of MG2C87. FoHeli1 copy numbers were further analysed in 56 short-read sequenced Fusarium strains isolated from banana using DeviaTE version 254. The sequence of II5 translation elongation factor 1-alpha (EF-1 alpha) was used as a single-copy reference gene for copy number estimation. Alignments for highlighting the hairpin sequences were made using ESPript version 3.088. All strains used for DeviaTE and their estimate FoHeli1 copy number are noted in Supplementary Data 7.

DNA from strains II5, M1, 36102, and CR1.1 was extracted from freeze-dried mycelium using the Masterpure™ Yeast DNA Purification Kit (LGC Biosearch™ Technologies, Hoddesdon, United Kingdom). Genomic DNA was used for PCR and subsequent gel electrophoresis to check for FoHeli1 circular intermediates. The primers used are listed in Supplementary Data 5.

Exometabolite extraction

Strains II5, M1 and M2 were grown on PDA plates for 7 days. Five agar plugs were then used to inoculate flasks containing 100 ml PDB supplemented with 5% banana root exudate. Cultures were grown in triplicate at 25 °C and 150 rpm for 7 days. PDB with 5% banana root exudate without Fusarium was used as a control. After growth, the culture filtrates were obtained by filtering the medium through a 0.2 µm filter. Culture filtrates were freeze-dried and afterwards dissolved in 4 mL ethyl acetate for one hour on a rolling shaker. Samples were subsequently dried with nitrogen and dissolved in 5 mL 75% MeOH, 0.1% formic acid. Afterwards, samples were sonicated for one hour. Samples were further centrifuged twice at 13.000 rpm for 10 minutes. HPLC vials were filled with 250 µL supernatant for chromatographic analysis.

Exometabolite analysis

An UltiMate 3000 U-HPLC system (Dionex) was used to create a 45-minute linear gradient of 5–35% (v/v) acetonitrile in 0.1% (v/v) formic acid (FA) in water at a flow rate of 0.19 mL min−1. 5 µL of each extract was injected, and compounds were separated on a Luna C18 column (2.0 × 150 mm, 3 µm; Phenomenex, Torrance, California, USA) maintained at 40 °C89. The detection of compounds eluting from the column was performed with a Q-Exactive Plus Orbitrap FTMS mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific™, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Full scan MS data were generated with electrospray in switching positive/negative ionisation mode at a mass resolution of 35,000 (FWHM at m/z 200) in a range of m/z 95–1350. Subsequent untargeted MS/MS analyses were performed with separate positive or negative electrospray ionisation at a normalised collision energy of 27 and a mass resolution of 17,500. The ionisation voltage was optimised at 3.5 kV for positive mode and 2.5 kV for negative mode; the capillary temperature was set at 250 °C; the auxiliary gas heater temperature was set to 220 °C; sheath gas, auxiliary gas, and the sweep gas flow were optimised at 36, 10 and 1 arbitrary units, respectively. Automatic gain control was set at 3e6 and the injection time at 100 ms. External mass calibration with formic acid clusters was performed in both positive and negative ionisation modes before each sample series.

LCMS data preprocessing and analysis

Mass peak picking and alignment were performed using Metalign software90. Mass features in the resulting peak list were considered real signals if they were detected with an intensity greater than 3 times the noise level and in at least 2 of the three biological replicates for at least one treatment. Mass features originating from the same metabolites were subsequently reconstituted based on their similar retention windows and intensity correlations across all measured samples, using MSClust software91. This resulted in the relative intensity of 725 putative mass features potentially representing individual metabolites across the samples. The raw data were processed using R (version 4.4.1 [2024-06-14])92 for gap-filling and normalisation. Average PDB intensities were calculated for each feature, and these were subtracted from the sample and QC intensities to correct for baseline signals. Negative values were replaced with small positive random values between 90 and 100 to avoid computational errors during subsequent analysis. Logarithmic transformation (log2) was applied to normalised intensities to stabilise variance and normalise distributions. Differentially abundant metabolites identified through statistical analyses were subjected to structural annotation. Representative samples from each treatment were analysed using untargeted positive- and negative-ion MS/MS, generating fragmentation spectra that were processed and exported as.mgf files in MZmine version 4.3.093. These spectra were processed using SIRIUS software for molecular formula prediction and compound annotation based on fragmentation patterns94. MS/MS spectra were matched against public databases such as Biocyc, COCONUT, KEGG, KNApSAcK, LOTUS, GNPS, HMDB, and PubChem:bio and metabolites to enhance confidence in annotation. Annotation confidence levels were categorised following the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) guidelines, with Level 1 corresponding to confirmed identifications through reference standards (only for Fusaric acid) and Levels 2-4 representing varying degrees of confidence based on spectral similarity and predictive algorithms.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

Principal Component Analysis was conducted to visualise variance among the experimental groups. The normalised intensity data were transposed, and PCA was performed using the prcomp function in R with data scaling. The PCA results were visualised using fviz_pca_ind from the factoextra package95 version 1.0.7. Two PCA analyses were performed, including all experimental groups and excluding PDB samples, to assess group-specific variation.

Differential analysis

Differential metabolite abundance between experimental groups (M1 vs. M2, M1 vs. II5, and M2 vs. II5) was evaluated using the limma package in R96 version 3.64.3. For each comparison, a linear model was fitted to the data, and empirical Bayes moderation was applied to improve robustness. The adjusted p-value threshold (false discovery rate, FDR) was set at 0.05. Fold changes greater than ± 2 were considered biologically significant. All annotated features in the comparison between II5 and M1 are noted in Supplementary Data 8. Annotated features in the comparison between II5 and M2 are noted in Supplementary Data 9. Annotated features in the comparison between M1 and M2 are noted in Supplementary Data 10.

Volcano plot visualisation

Volcano plots were generated to highlight significant metabolites for each pairwise comparison. Log fold changes (logFC) and -log10 transformed adjusted p-values were plotted using ggplot297 version 4.0.0. Significant features were colour-coded by pathway categories (e.g., alkaloids, carbohydrates, terpenoids) with a custom colour palette. Threshold lines at logFC ± 1 and p-value < 0.05 were annotated to demarcate significance criteria.

Growth assays

For the determination of colony growth in the presence or absence of fusaric acid, fungal spores were point-inoculated using 2 μL aliquots with 1 × 10⁶ spores/mL on PDA plates containing 0–200 ppm fusaric acid (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, USA). Colony diameter was measured at 4 days post-inoculation (dpi). Colonies were photographed at 4 dpi. For pigmentation quantification, photos were taken at 8 dpi. Pigmentation area was determined using ImageJ98 (version 1.54p). The pigmentation area was isolated using the following parameters in the HSB (Hue, Saturation, Brightness) colour space: Hue (0–10 and 210–255), Saturation (30–255), and Brightness (138–255).

Plant growth conditions and infection assays

Cavendish plants were inoculated and scored as described previously99. In brief, conidia were produced by inoculating flasks of Mung bean medium with agar plugs of the different wild-type Fusarium strains and mutants. The flasks were incubated for 5 days at 150 rpm, 25 °C. Conidia were collected by filtering the medium through two layers of sterile cheesecloth. The conidia were subsequently quantified and diluted to the desired concentration. Plants were inoculated 10 weeks after potting of the in vitro plantlets. Prior to inoculation, Cavendish plants were wounded in their roots twice and inoculated with 200 mL of either 1·106 or 1·104 conidia/ml by pouring. Plants were monitored and scored after 8–10 weeks post-inoculation. To assess virulence levels, plants were cut longitudinally at the corm, and pictures were taken. Finally, the percentage of necrosed corm tissue was determined using ImageJ98 (version 1.54p).

Statistical analyses

To determine statistical significance, P-values were commonly calculated using ANOVA and Tukey’s HSD or in a single case a Spearman correlation test. Values are shown as mean ± SD or box and whiskers plots. Box plots show the median (centre line), the 25th and 75th percentiles (bounds of the box), and the minimum and maximum values (whiskers). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Software (Boston, Massachusetts, USA) (version 10.6.1) or R92. Plots were visualised using GraphPad Software or using ggplot297 version 4.0.0, ggridges100 version 0.5.7, and ggstatsplot101 version 0.13.3. Experiments were performed three times to determine the reproducibility of the results.
