The frontiers of chemical imaging
Authors: Ji-Xin Cheng, Warren S. Warren
Published: 2024

Most of our knowledge about the chemical content in cells, tissues, or materials comes from analysis of fixed samples, but such in vitro assays can only detect the presence of molecules. Spatial and temporal dynamics information of metabolites or biomarkers is often crucial to understanding whether a detected species is the cause or consequence of an activity. Thus, in situ and in vivo molecular analysis (in most cases, without additional labeling), can decipher the function of molecules rather than simply detecting their presence.

The inaugural Gordon Research Conference (GRC) on Chemical Imaging, held at the Stonehill College, Easton, MA, from 31 July to 4 August 2023, brought together experts in engineering, life science, medicine, data science, and industrial leaders to foster the growth of this exciting field. Speakers and attendees have defined the mission of chemical imaging at two levels. The first is mapping of chemical distribution in cells, tissues, and functional materials. The second is imaging of molecular transformations in real time and space. The GRC meeting successfully disseminated some of the innovative advances of the field, educated researchers at all career levels, encouraged and triggered collaborations, and stimulated new avenues for probing molecules in situ and in vivo in their natural environment, either in living systems or in functional materials. Much of this is driven by the maturation of laser and microscopy technologies coupled with an evolving understanding of the many ways nondestructive probes can enhance molecular signatures. The enthusiasm for this rapidly growing field was palpable at the meeting and led to the idea of a sharing these innovations with a wider audience.

This special issue of Science Advances offers a unique opportunity to learn about the frontiers of chemical imaging and a variety of promising chemical imaging modalities. Techniques presented in this issue include stimulated Raman scattering microscopy, cryo-Raman microscopy, ion conductance microscopy, transient absorption microscopy, infrared photothermal microscopy, ultraviolet photoacoustic microscopy, two-photon autofluorescence imaging, three-photon autofluorescence imaging, and quantum chemical sensing. In addition, authors presented polymer dots for super-multiplex molecular imaging, new theory of stimulated Raman scattering, and the use of artificial intelligence in multimodal chemical imaging of clinical samples. We could not cover every aspect of the fast-growing chemical imaging field (for example, the issue does not include papers on imaging mass spectrometry). Overall, we hope that readers can sense the enormous potential of label-free chemical imaging, from high-content single-cell analysis to intraoperative investigation of human patient specimens.
