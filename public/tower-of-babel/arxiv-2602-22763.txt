Towards a microscopic description of 12 C+ 12 C fusion at stellar energies
Pierre Descouvemont

Abstract
I present a fully microscopic description of the 12 C+ 12 C fusion reaction at stellar energies. Utilizing the multichannel Resonating Group Method (RGM), my model explicitly includes 12 C+ 12 C and α + 20 \alpha+^{20} Ne reaction channels (with excited states). This approach provides a consistent, simultaneous, description of fusion, elastic scattering, and Mg 24 {}^{24}\mathrm{Mg} spectroscopy. Results for 12 C+ 12 C elastic scattering show excellent agreement with experimental data, significantly improving the single-channel approximations. Spectroscopic analysis reveals that Mg 24 {}^{24}\mathrm{Mg} states and resonances are highly mixed configurations, contradicting the concept of pure ”molecular states.” The calculated fusion S S -factor is consistent with available experimental data and predicts both narrow and broad resonances near the Coulomb barrier. Main resonance widths originate primarily from the α + 20 \alpha+^{20} Ne exit channels. The S S -factor exhibits a decrease at low energies, providing a microscopic support for the hypothesis of fusion hindrance. This work is a first step towards a reliable theoretical extrapolation of the 12 C+ 12 C reaction to deep stellar burning temperatures. Future works should include the neutron and proton channels to provide a complete description of the 12 C+ 12 C fusion.



I
Introduction

The
12
C+
12
C
fusion reaction plays an important role in stellar nucleosynthesis
Wallerstein et al. 1997
;
Bennett et al. 2012
. It appears to be the main carbon-burning phase in massive stars, and triggers the production of heavy elements at high temperatures
Back et al. 2014
;
Wiescher et al. 2025
;
Chieffi et al. 2025
. Determining the
12
C+
12
C
fusion cross section at stellar energies (typically
\approx 2.4
MeV at
T=10^{9}
K) is extremely difficult. The common problem in low-energy reactions is the smallness of the cross sections, as a consequence of the Coulomb repulsion. This problem is even more severe in heavy-ion reactions where Coulomb effects are further enhanced, compared to
(p,\gamma)
or
(\alpha,\gamma)
reactions.

The literature on
12
C+
12
C
fusion is extremely vast, and I refer to Refs.
Wiescher et al. 2025
;
Chieffi et al. 2025
for recent reviews. The
12
C+
12
C
reaction presents two specificities which make extrapolation very uncertain. The presence of resonances in
12
C+
12
C
scattering is well established for decades
Bromley et al. 1960
. The fusion data around the Gamow energy also present resonances
Jiang et al. 2013
, the origin of which remains unclear. A recent experiment, using the Trojan Horse method
Tumino et al. 2018
, claimed to find several resonances at low energies, which would have significant implications on massive-star evolution. This result, however, was subsequently challenged in Ref.
Mukhamedzhanov et al. 2019
;
Beck et al. 2020
where the authors reanalyzed the data with an improved theoretical model. Resonances at low energies were also suggested by Spillane
et al.

Spillane et al. 2007
, but the large error bars do not permit a definite conclusion.

Another issue under debate is the existence (or absence) of a
12
C+
12
C
fusion hindrance at astrophysical energies. This phenomenon was first observed for heavier systems
Jiang et al. 2002
. Lighter systems such as
12
C+
12
C
were then analyzed by Jiang
et al.

Jiang et al. 2007
who concluded that fusion hindrance may also occur in reactions such as
12
C+
12
C
. This effect would have important consequences in nuclear astrophysics
Cooper et al. 2009
. The current data, however, do not allow to conclude on the hindrance
effect since they do not cover energies sufficiently low. A
recent work of Uzawa and Hagino
Uzawa and Hagino 2025
investigates the hindrance
possibility by fitting the experimental cross sections with more flexibility.

Both physical issues discussed above, the presence of
12
C+
12
C
resonances
at low energies and the fusion hindrance, are difficult to address
by theory. Most calculations are performed within the optical
model and its multi-channel extensions
Gasques et al. 2005
;
Assunção and Descouvemont 2013
;
Chien et al. 2018
;
Gasques et al. 2022
. In this approach, the fusion process is either simulated by an imaginary part in
the optical potential
Assunção and Descouvemont 2013
;
Gasques et al. 2022
, or determined through a barrier penetration model of quantum tunneling
Gasques et al. 2005
;
Chien et al. 2018
. The standard
methodology is to fit the available data, and to extrapolate the theory
down to stellar energies. The optical model, however, is unable to reproduce bound or resonant states of
the system. Although they do not play a direct role in fusion reactions,
these states provide useful tests of the model.
Other approaches, such as the time-dependent Hartree-Fock method
Simenel and Umar 2018
;
Close et al. 2025
or the time-dependent wave packet method
Diaz-Torres and Wiescher 2018
, have been developed. These techniques, however, are limited to the fusion component of the
12
C+
12
C
reaction, and cannot be applied to the
24
Mg spectroscopy.

These difficulties can be addressed by microscopic models, where the nucleon-nucleon interaction is the only input
Wildermuth and Tang 1977
. One of the main advantages of microscopic models is to provide a simultaneous description of fusion, of elastic scattering and of the
24
Mg spectroscopy. However,
solving a many-body problem (i.e.,
A\gtrsim 6
) is an extremely difficult
task, especially for scattering problems. Recent advances in
ab initio
methods
Hergert 2020
;
Quaglioni et al. 2025
consider
\alpha+p

Quaglioni and Navrátil 2009
or
\alpha+d

Navrátil and Quaglioni 2011
scattering, but extending these methods to heavier nuclei is currently not possible.

An efficient technique to overcome this limitation is the cluster approximation
Wildermuth and Tang 1977
. In the Resonating Group Method (RGM, see Ref.
Horiuchi 1977
), the wave function is obtained from cluster wave functions defined in the
shell model. Early calculations focused on simple systems,
such as
\alpha+p
, but the development of computer capabilities
allowed to go far beyond this limit (see, for example, Ref.
Timofeyuk and Descouvemont 2010
).

A microscopic approach to fusion has been considered previously. In Ref.
Descouvemont 1989

a single-channel model of
12
C+
12
C
was used, with a phenomenological
imaginary part fitted to the data. In Ref.
Taniguchi and Kimura 2024
the authors used the
Antisymmetrized Molecular Dynamics (AMD) model, to investigate the

{}^{24}\text{Mg}
nucleus above the
12
C+
12
C
threshold. This approach, however, is based on
the bound-state approximation. The authors determine the widths of resonances from the reduced-width amplitudes, and use the Breit-Wigner approximation for each resonance. This method, however, does not include scattering conditions, and neglects the interference between the resonances.

In the present work, I perform a fully microscopic calculation of
12
C+
12
C

fusion, by including the dominant
\alpha+^{20}
Ne
channels (
{}^{20}\mathrm{Ne}
in the ground state and in several excited states). With the inclusion of

channels, the main component of the fusion cross section is explicitly taken into account. No phenomenological imaginary potential is necessary. The
{}^{12}\mathrm{C}
and
{}^{20}\mathrm{Ne}

clusters are described in the shell model, by considering all
p
-shell and

sd
-shell configurations, respectively. This accurate description leads to very large
bases:
12
C+
12
C
involves
225^{2}=50625
Slater determinants. A large number
of
{}^{12}\text{C}
and
{}^{20}\text{Ne}
states are included in a consistent way. This approach simultaneously provides
{}^{24}\text{Mg}
properties and
12
C+
12
C
elastic
scattering and fusion. In this exploratory study, I omit the
{}^{23}\text{Na}+p

and
{}^{23}\text{Mg}+n
configurations which involve even larger numbers of Slater determinants,
and are beyond the scope of the present work.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, I present the microscopic
cluster model, and the calculation of the fusion cross section. Section
III
is
dedicated to
{}^{12}\text{C}
and
{}^{20}\text{Ne}
in the shell model, and Sec.

to the 24-nucleon wave functions. I present various tests of

.

II
The Microscopic Model

My goal is to describe the
12
C+
12
C
fusion reaction within a fully microscopic approach. This means that the cross sections are derived from a nucleon-nucleon interaction, and that the asymptotic boundary conditions are taken into account explicitly. The
A
-nucleon Hamiltonian (
A=24
here) is given by

\displaystyle H=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{A}t_{\alpha}+\sum_{\alpha<\beta=1}^{A}v_{\alpha\beta},

(1)

where
t_{\alpha}
is the kinetic energy of nucleon
{\alpha}
, and
v_{\alpha\beta}
is a nucleon-nucleon interaction [notice that three-body terms are neglected in Eq. (
1
)].

Solving the Schrödinger equation associated with (
1
) is an extremely complicated problem, which can only be treated approximately. In the present work, I use the multichannel Resonating Group Method (RGM, see Ref.
Wildermuth and Tang 1977
) which is equivalent to the Generator Coordinate Method (GCM, see Ref.
Horiuchi 1977
). In addition to
12
C+
12
C
channels, some
\alpha+^{20}
Ne
channels are open at the threshold, and play a crucial role in the fusion process. They must be included in the model to obtain a realistic description of the fusion process.

Schematically, the RGM wave functions for angular momentum
J
are therefore given by two components written as

\displaystyle\Psi^{J}=\Psi_{\rm{C+C}}^{J}+\Psi_{\alpha+\rm{Ne}}^{J},

(2)

which are associated with the
12
C+
12
C
and
\alpha+^{20}
Ne
partitions, respectively (notice that I only consider positive-parity states owing to the symmetry of the entrance channel). The RGM is based on the cluster approximation which provides, for the symmetrized
12
C+
12
C
component,

\displaystyle\Psi_{\rm{C+C}}^{J}=\mathcal{A}\sum_{ij}g_{ij}^{J}(\rho)\bigl(\Phi_{{\rm C}(i)}\Phi_{{\rm C}(j)}+\Phi_{{\rm C}(j)}\Phi_{{\rm C}(i)}\bigr)/(1+\delta_{ij}),

(3)

where
\mathcal{A}
is the
A
-nucleon antisymmetrizer,
\Phi_{{\rm C}(i)}
are
{}^{12}\mathrm{C}
wave functions, defined in the shell model, and
g_{ij}^{J}(\rho)
are radial functions depending on the
12
C+
12
C
relative coordinate
\rho
. The summation over indices
i
and
j
corresponds to different
12
C states.

For the sake of simplicity, I neglect angular momentum couplings in the definition (
3
).
In practice, the spins of both
{}^{12}\mathrm{C}
nuclei are coupled to form the channel spin
I
, which in turn is coupled to the relative orbital momentum
\ell
. Channels with
i=j
are symmetrized by keeping only even values of
I
.

Similarly, the second component in Eq. (
2
) is defined by

\displaystyle\Psi_{\alpha+{\rm Ne}}^{J}=\mathcal{A}\sum_{k}\tilde{g}_{k}^{J}(\tilde{\rho})\Phi_{\alpha}\Phi_{{\rm Ne}(k)},

(4)

where
\Phi_{\alpha}
is a
(0s)^{4}
shell-model wave function of the
\alpha
particle, and where
\Phi_{\rm{Ne}(k)}
are
20
Ne wave functions, defined with 2 neutrons and 2 protons in the
sd
shell-model space. The relative functions
\tilde{g}_{k}^{J}(\tilde{\rho})
, depending on the
\alpha+^{20}
Ne
relative coordinate
\tilde{\rho}
are determined from the Schrödinger equation in the same way as in Eq. (
3
).

A decisive advantage of cluster models is that the RGM wave functions (
3
) and (
4
) are consistently adapted to scattering problems, and to spectroscopy. The various cross sections (elastic and inelastic scattering, fusion, etc.) are defined from the asymptotic part of the radial wave functions. Assuming that the entrance channel is

{}^{12}\rm{C}(g.s.)+^{12}\rm{C}(g.s.)
, referred to as ”channel 1”, the asymptotic form of the scattering wave function reads

\displaystyle g^{J}_{1}(\rho)\rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{ij}\Bigl[I_{J}(k_{1}\rho)\delta_{i1}\delta_{j1}-U_{1,ij}^{J}O_{J}(k_{ij}\rho)\Bigr]\Phi_{{\rm C}(i)}\Phi_{{\rm C}(j)}

\displaystyle-\sum_{k}U_{1,k}^{J}O_{J}(\tilde{k}_{k}\tilde{\rho})\Phi_{\alpha}\Phi_{\rm{Ne}(k)}.

(5)

In this definition,
I_{J}(x)
and
O_{J}(x)
are the incoming and outgoing Coulomb functions, and
k_{ij}
and
\tilde{k}_{k}
are the wave numbers in the
12
C+
12
C
and
\alpha+^{20}
Ne
channels.
Note that definition (
5
) assumes that all channels are open, which is not true at low energies. However, the extension is fairly simple: for closed channels, the Coulomb functions are replaced by appropriate Whittaker functions. As mentioned before, definition (
5
) is schematic, for the sake of clarity. In actual calculations, the cluster spins are coupled to each other, and the resulting channel spin is coupled to the relative orbital momentum
Descouvemont and Dufour 2012
.

The important quantity in reaction calculations is the scattering matrix
\boldsymbol{U}^{J}
, which appears in Eq. (
5
). Elements
U_{1,1}^{J}
are associated with elastic scattering and are used with standard formulae to determine the elastic cross sections. Inelastic scattering (
i\neq 1
and/or
j\neq 1
) is provided by
U_{1,ij}^{J}
. Elements
U_{1,k}^{J}
which appear in the second term of Eq. (
5
) are associated with the transfer to the
\alpha+^{20}
Ne
channels, i.e. to the fusion process.

Ideally, the RGM wave function Eq. (
2
) should contain
p+^{23}
Na and
n+^{23}
Mg channels, which also play a role in the fusion process. However, owing to the extremely large number of Slater determinants (
C^{12}_{4}\times C^{12}_{3}=108900
for
23
Na and
23
Mg), introducing these channels is beyond the scope of this work. I therefore compute the
\alpha
component of the fusion cross section, which is defined as

\displaystyle\sigma_{F}=\frac{2\pi}{k_{\mathrm{c.m.}}^{2}}\sum_{J\ {\rm even}}(2J+1)\sum_{k}|U_{1,k}^{J}|^{2},

(6)

where
k_{\mathrm{c.m.}}
is the wave number in the
12
C+
12
C
entrance channel. The factor 2 and the limitation to even
J
values stem from the symmetry of the entrance channel.

Microscopic cluster calculations of the
12
C+
12
C
system have been performed for many years, but are limited to a single-channel approximation and do not consider fusion
Baye and Pecher 1982
;
Baye and Descouvemont 1984
;
Suzuki and Hecht 1982
. My work goes far beyond these limitations: it involves all
p
-shell states of
{}^{12}\mathrm{C}
, and paves the way to include more channels since the main inelastic and transfer channels (
\alpha+^{20}
Ne
) are included. In the next section, I discuss the numerical issues of the method and show that these calculations are computationally challenging.

IV
GCM wave functions

IV.1
GCM kernels

The
{}^{12}\mathrm{C}
and
{}^{20}\mathrm{Ne}
shell-model wave functions are then used to describe the
24
-nucleon system. In the GCM, the total
12
C+
12
C
wave function (
2
) is expressed as a combination of Slater determinants involving cluster wave functions as

\displaystyle\Phi_{ij}(\boldsymbol{R})=\mathcal{A}\varPhi_{i}\biggl(-\frac{\boldsymbol{R}}{2}\biggr)\varPhi_{j}\biggl(\frac{\boldsymbol{R}}{2}\biggr),

(9)

where
\boldsymbol{R}
is the generator coordinate. The indices
i
and
j
run from 1 to 225, making the basis size
225^{2}=50625
Slater determinants. Notice that I use a common oscillator parameter (
b=1.6
fm) to exactly factorize the center-of-mass motion.

The main part of the numerical calculation is to compute the Hamiltonian and overlap kernels between basis states (
9
),

\displaystyle H_{ij,kl}(\boldsymbol{R},\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\langle\Phi_{ij}(\boldsymbol{R})|H|\Phi_{kl}(\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle N_{ij,kl}(\boldsymbol{R},\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\langle\Phi_{ij}(\boldsymbol{R})|\Phi_{kl}(\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime})\rangle,

(10)

which is computationally demanding (
50625^{2}
matrix elements). In practice, these matrices are split into pieces, allowing for efficient parallelization of the code. The matrix elements are computed with standard formulae
Brink 1966
. The linear combinations defined in Eqs. (
7
,
8
) are subsequently performed. The same procedure is applied to the
\alpha+^{20}
Ne
channel, and to the couplings between these channels.

The next step in the calculation is to perform the angular momentum projection mentioned in Sec.
II
. The angular-momentum projection is carried out from integrals over the angle between
\boldsymbol{R}
and
\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}
(see Ref.
Descouvemont and Dufour 2012
for detail).

IV.2
Relative functions

The relative functions in (
3
) and (
4
) can be expanded as a superposition of several generator coordinates as

\displaystyle g_{ij}^{J}(\rho)\approx\sum_{n}F_{ij}^{J}(R_{n})\Gamma(\rho,R_{n}),

(11)

where
\Gamma(\rho,R)
are a Gaussian functions centered at
R
, and where
F_{ij}^{J}(R)
is the generator function (see Refs.
Horiuchi 1977
;
Descouvemont and Dufour 2012
for details). A similar expansion is applied to the
\alpha+^{20}
Ne
partition.

The GCM technique is well suited to numerical calculations since it involves Slater determinants. However, the long-range behaviour of expansion (
11
) is Gaussian and does not correspond to the physical Coulomb function (
5
). The problem is solved with the microscopic
R
-matrix method
Baye et al. 1977
;
Descouvemont and Baye 2010
which provides the scattering matrices
\boldsymbol{U}^{J}
as well as the generator functions
F_{ij}^{J}(R)
.

The radial wave functions
g^{J}_{\gamma}(\rho)
are defined with the antisymmetrizer operator
\mathcal{A}
, defined in Eqs. (
3
,
4
) [index
\gamma
stands for
\gamma=(i,j)
]. These functions contain the so-called forbidden states
Saito 1969
, and cannot be interpreted without the operator
\mathcal{A}
. It is, however, possible to transform these relative functions in order to derive approximations neglecting the antisymmetrizer
Horiuchi 1977
;
Varga and Lovas 1988
.

The method is based on the RGM overlap kernel, defined as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}_{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})=\langle\delta(\rho-r)\Phi_{i}\Phi_{j}|\mathcal{A}|\delta(\rho-r^{\prime})\Phi_{k}\Phi_{l}\rangle,

(12)

where
\Phi_{i},\Phi_{j},\Phi_{k},\Phi_{l}
are
{}^{12}\mathrm{C}
,
\alpha
or
{}^{20}\mathrm{Ne}
internal wave functions. As shown in Ref.
Varga and Lovas 1988
, the RGM and GCM kernels (
12
) and (
10
) are connected to each other by integral transforms.

From the overlap kernel (
12
), I define

\displaystyle{\hat{g}}_{\gamma}(\rho)=\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}}\int{\mathcal{N}}^{1/2}_{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})g_{\gamma^{\prime}}(\rho^{\prime})d\rho^{\prime},

\displaystyle{\tilde{g}}_{\gamma}(\rho)=\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}}\int{\mathcal{N}}_{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})g_{\gamma^{\prime}}(\rho^{\prime})d\rho^{\prime},

(13)

which are normalized as

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}}{\mathcal{N}}_{\gamma}=1,\quad{\mathcal{N}}_{\gamma}=\int({\hat{g}}_{\gamma}(\rho))^{2}d\rho,

(14)

and

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma}{\mathcal{S}}_{\gamma}={\mathcal{S}},\quad{\mathcal{S}}_{\gamma}=\int({\tilde{g}}_{\gamma}(\rho))^{2}d\rho.

(15)

In Eq. (
15
),
{\mathcal{S}}_{\gamma}
is the spectroscopic factor in channel
\gamma
. Functions
{\tilde{g}}_{\gamma}(\rho)
are also referred to as the reduced-width amplitudes. The total spectroscopic factor
{\mathcal{S}}
is different from unity, owing to the presence of the antisymmetrizer. The numbers
{\mathcal{N}}_{\gamma}
and
{\mathcal{S}}_{\gamma}
provide information on the structure of a state. At large distance, functions
{\hat{g}}_{\gamma}(\rho)
and
{\tilde{g}}_{\gamma}(\rho)
are identical since antisymmetrization effects are negligible. Additional information about these transformations can be found in Refs.
Varga and Lovas 1988
;
Descouvemont 2023
.

V.1
Conditions of the calculation

The microscopic cluster calculation is based on an effective
nucleon-nucleon interaction
v_{\alpha\beta}
[see Eq. (
1
)]. An important constraint
on the fusion cross section is to reproduce the threshold energy of
the
\alpha+^{20}
Ne
channel (
Q=4.63
MeV). I use the Volkov V2 interaction

Volkov 1965
complemented by Bartlett and Heisenberg terms (with
b=-h
).
This modified interaction provides more flexibility than the original
V2 force since it involves an additional parameter
Dufour and Descouvemont 2005
. The interaction
contains the exact Coulomb force and a zero-range spin-orbit term
(with a commonly used amplitude
S_{0}=30\ \text{MeV}.\text{fm}^{5}
). The oscillator parameter, common to all clusters, is 1.6 fm. The generator coordinate values
R_{n}
range from 1.5 fm to 10.5 fm with a step of 1 fm.

The strategy to determine the adjustable parameters (Majorana
m
, and
Bartlett
b
) is to optimize the ground-state energy while keeping the
threshold at the experimental value. With the multichannel model,

b=0

The present model, using the same calculation conditions, can be used to study elastic scattering. This process is not directly related to fusion, but is based on the same scattering wave functions. Elastic scattering is therefore an excellent test of the model.

I present elastic cross sections (divided by the Mott cross section) in Fig.
4
, and compare them to experiment
Treu et al. 1980
. Below the Coulomb barrier, and even more at stellar energies, this ratio is close to unity. Consequently, I have selected c.m. energies slightly above the Coulomb barrier (6, 8, and 10 MeV). I compare the single-channel calculation (dotted lines) with the multichannel model (solid lines). At
6
MeV, the nuclear effects are minimal, and the cross section weakly depends on the angle. When the energy increases (up to
10
MeV), however, the cross section significantly deviates from the Mott cross section.

The multichannel calculation accurately reproduces the oscillations observed in the experimental data. Clearly, the single-channel approximation is rather far from experiment, and this disagreement increases with energy. The excellent results obtained with the multichannel calculation suggests that absorption effects are well simulated by
12
C+
12
C
and
\alpha+^{20}
Ne
channels, even if many channels are closed (see Fig.
1
). These virtual off-shell couplings obviously help to improve the description of
12
C+
12
C
elastic scattering. The application of this model to the fusion process is therefore expected to be reliable.

In the literature, there is a debate about the possible fusion hindrance in the
12
C+
12
C
reaction at low energies
Jiang et al. 2007
. Some calculations predict a steep decrease of the
S
factor at energies well below the Coulomb barrier (see the discussion in Ref.
Uzawa and Hagino 2025
). This effect is expected below
E\lesssim 2
MeV, but the cross section is too small to be measured in the laboratory, and no definite conclusion can be drawn.

In optical-model calculations, hindrance effects are analyzed through the function

\displaystyle L(E)=\frac{d\ln(\sigma E)}{dE}

(17)

which is parameterized by

\displaystyle L(E)\simeq A_{0}+B_{0}/E^{n},

(18)

with
n=1.5
(this choice, however is questioned in Ref.
Uzawa and Hagino 2025
). Coefficients
A_{0}
and
B_{0}
are fitted, and depend on the potential. In my calculation, the existence of resonances makes the parametrization (
18
) non applicable. However, the GCM
S
-factor is consistent with fusion hindrance, as a decrease of the
S
-factor is found at low energies. Of course this conclusion is limited to the
\alpha
channel, and could be affected by the proton and neutron channels. Calculations with more configurations are necessary before drawing definite conclusions about fusion hindrance.

VII
Conclusion and outlook

The present work paves the way for a fully microscopic approach to fusion reactions. The
12
C+
12
C
reaction is extremely important in nuclear astrophysics, and raises several open issues, such as the presence of resonances or the fusion hindrance below the Coulomb barrier. A microscopic theory, with an explicit treatment of the continuum and of the various reaction channels, is a numerical challenge. However, this approach provides a consistent description of fusion, elastic and inelastic scattering and of the
24
Mg spectroscopy. All physical quantities are based on a nucleon-nucleon interaction only. Data on elastic scattering are well described, and I have shown that the multichannel model strongly improves the single-channel calculation.

My RGM-GCM calculation goes far beyond previous
12
C+
12
C
microscopic calculations, that used a single-channel approximation
Baye and Pecher 1982
;
Suzuki and Hecht 1982
;
Baye and Descouvemont 1984
. I include several
{}^{12}\mathrm{C}
states, as well as
\alpha+^{20}
Ne
channels. One of the consequences is that the so-called “molecular states” found in single-channel calculation, are not present. These states are expected to have a dominant
12
C+
12
C
structure. However, my analysis of
24
Mg bound states and resonances shows that the wave functions are spread over many channels. In the multichannel calculation, I do not find bound states or resonances with a dominant
\text{C}(0^{+}_{1})+\text{C}(0^{+}_{1})
configuration.

The fusion cross section is consistent with experiment, even though experimental uncertainties remain, even around the Coulomb barrier. Extrapolating the data down to stellar energies is a delicate problem. I predict resonances, in particular
0^{+}
and
2^{+}
broad states near the Coulomb barrier. Additionally, narrow
0^{+}
and
4^{+}
resonances are present in the GCM cross section. The model could be improved by including the proton and neutron channels, which are neglected in this exploratory study. Introducing negative-parity states of
20
Ne could also play a role. The inclusion of these channels represents a challenge for future calculations.
