Topological dynamics for the endograph metric II: Extremely radical properties


Abstract
Given a dynamical system ( X , f ) (X,f) we investigate several topological dynamical properties for its Zadeh extension ( ℱ ⁡ ( X ) , f ^ ) (\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f}) endowed with the endograph metric d E d_{E} . In particular, we prove that for some contractive and expansive properties, for chain recurrence, chain transitivity and chain mixing, and for the shadowing property, the endograph metric behaves in an extremely radical way. Our results not only resolve certain open questions in the existing literature, but also yield completely new outcomes concerning the chain-type notions considered and the shadowing property.



by

Antoni López-Martínez
†
†

: 37B02, 37B05, 37B65, 54A40, 54B20.

Key words and phrases
: Topological dynamics, Fuzzy dynamical systems, Expansive properties, Chains, Shadowing.

Introduction

This paper is a continuation of
[
27
]
, where a
dynamical system
is a pair
(X,f)
formed by a continuous map
f:X\longrightarrow X
acting on a metric space
(X,d)
. As in
[
27
]
, the main objective of this paper is comparing the dynamical properties presented by a dynamical system
(X,f)
and those presented by the induced systems
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
, where
\overline{f}
is the usual
hyperextension
of
f
acting on the space
\mathcal{K}(X)
formed by the non-empty compact subsets of
X
, and where
\hat{f}
is the so-called
Zadeh extension
or
fuzzification
of
f
acting on the space
\mathcal{F}(X)
formed by the normal fuzzy sets of
X
.

Looking for the dynamical properties of
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
can be considered as classical topic (see
[
3
,
7
,
32
]
), but the system
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
has appeared much more recently in the literature and many questions and problems remain open for it (see
[
1
,
5
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
30
]
). In addition, although the space
\mathcal{K}(X)
is usually only endowed with the
Hausdorff metric

d_{H}
, the space
\mathcal{F}(X)
has been endowed with several different metrics such as the
supremum metric

d_{\infty}
, the
Skorokhod metric

d_{0}
, and the
sendograph
and
endograph metrics

d_{S}
and
d_{E}
. Following
[
18
,
19
,
21
,
27
]
, for each metric
\rho\in\{d_{\infty},d_{0},d_{S},d_{E}\}
we will denote the metric space
(\mathcal{F}(X),\rho)
by
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X)
,
\mathcal{F}_{0}(X)
,
\mathcal{F}_{S}(X)
and
\mathcal{F}_{E}(X)
respectively, and the symbols
\tau_{\infty}
,
\tau_{0}
,
\tau_{S}
and
\tau_{E}
will stand for the respective induced topologies on
\mathcal{F}(X)
.

In the previous work
[
27
]
we proved that, for several dynamical notions related to topological transitivity, topological recurrence, point-transitivity, Devaney chaos and the specification property, then the systems
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
behave exactly in the same way: if
\mathcal{P}
is one of the above mentioned properties, then
all
of these dynamical systems present such a property
\mathcal{P}
whenever
any
of them does. Actually, the implication

–

“
if the system
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
has property
\mathcal{P}
, then
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
has property
\mathcal{P}
”,

was already proved in the literature for most of the properties
\mathcal{P}
considered in
[
27
]
. Then, since each property
\mathcal{P}
considered in
[
27
]
depends on “
certain conditions that
all open subsets
fulfill
”, and since the inclusions
\tau_{E}\subset\tau_{S}\subset\tau_{0}\subset\tau_{\infty}
always hold, what we did in
[
27
]
was proving the left implication

–

“
if the system
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
has property
\mathcal{P}
, then
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
has property
\mathcal{P}
”.

Contrary to the situation that we had in
[
27
]
, in this paper we focus on dynamical properties for which the system
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
exhibits an extremely radical behaviour. In particular, we address the
contractive
,
expansive
,
expanding
and
positively expansive
properties (see
[
18
,
20
,
37
]
), as well as the notions of
chain recurrence
,
chain transitivity
and
chain mixing
(see
[
24
]
), and the
finite
and
full
versions of the so-called
shadowing property
(see
[
5
]
). The definitions of these properties seem to rely much more on the specific metric used than on the topology that such a metric generates. This is probably why these properties behave in an extremely pronounced manner for
d_{E}
, as we summarize in the following list of main results obtained:

–

When a metric space
(X,d)
has more than one point then the corresponding space
\mathcal{F}_{E}(X)
has no isolated points, regardless of whether
(X,d)
itself has isolated points; see Lemma
2.6
below. We consider that this is an extremely radical behaviour since, as we also prove below, the rest of metric spaces
(\mathcal{K}(X),d_{H})
,
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X)
,
\mathcal{F}_{0}(X)
and
\mathcal{F}_{S}(X)
have isolated points if and only if so does
(X,d)
.

–

Given a dynamical system
(X,f)
, then the systems
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
are contractive if and only if the map
f
is constant; see statement (a) of Theorem
3.1
below. This result reproves
[
37
, Example 7]
in a shorter way, it generalizes
[
37
, Theorem 8]
, and it completely solves both
[
20
, Problems 5.8 and 5.12]
. In particular, such a result exhibits an extremely radical behaviour for the endograph metric (the dynamics of a constant map are far from being interesting).

–

Given a dynamical system
(X,f)
, then the systems
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
are expansive, expanding and positively expansive if and only if the set
X
has exactly one point; see statement (b) of Theorem
3.1
below. This result solves both
[
18
, Problems 3.6 and 4.3]
, reproves in a shorter way
[
18
, Examples 3.5, 3.9 and 4.6]
, and shows that some of the implications proved in
[
18
, Theorems 3.1 and 4.1]
, but also the results
[
18
, Propositions 3.8, 4.4 and 4.5]
, are practically void (such results only apply when
X
is a singleton, which is rarely assumed).

–

Given a dynamical system
(X,f)
, then the system
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
is chain recurrent, chain transitive and chain mixing if and only if the map
f
has dense range (see Theorem
4.3
below). Again, this seems an extreme behaviour because having dense range is a commonly assumed dynamical property.

–

Given a dynamical system
(X,f)
for which
f
has dense range, then
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
can not have the finite shadowing property when
(X,f)
is not topologically mixing (see Theorem
5.4
). This result can be used to show that
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
does not necessarily have the finite shadowing property when the system
(X,f)
is contractive (see Example
5.2
), but
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
does have the full shadowing property when
(X,f)
is contractive and
f^{k}(X)
is bounded for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
(see Theorem
5.6
).

2
Notation and general background

In this section we introduce the notation used along the paper, which will be completely similar to that used in
[
27
]
. In particular, we recall the definition of the extensions
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
, for a given dynamical system
(X,f)
, and we prove Lemma
2.6
mentioned above. From now on let
\mathbb{N}
be the set of strictly positive integers, set
\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, and let
\mathbb{I}
be the unit interval
[0,1]
.

2.1
The extended systems
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})

Given a metric space
(X,d)
we will denote by
\mathcal{B}_{d}(x,\varepsilon)\subset X
the open
d
-ball centred at
x\in X
and of radius
\varepsilon>0
. Moreover, we will consider the spaces
\mathcal{C}(X):=\left\{C\subset X\ ;\ C\text{ is a non-empty closed set}\right\}
and
\mathcal{K}(X):=\left\{K\subset X\ ;\ K\text{ is a non-empty compact set}\right\}
. It is clear that
\mathcal{K}(X)\subset\mathcal{C}(X)
. Given two sets
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathcal{C}(X)
, the
Hausdorff distance
between them is

d_{H}(C_{1},C_{2}):=\max\left\{\sup_{x_{1}\in C_{1}}d(x_{1},C_{2}),\sup_{x_{2}\in C_{2}}d(x_{2},C_{1})\right\}.

This value may be infinite if either
C_{1}
or
C_{2}
are not compact. However, in
\mathcal{K}(X)
we have that the map
d_{H}:\mathcal{K}(X)\times\mathcal{K}(X)\longrightarrow[0,+\infty[
defined as above is a metric, called the
Hausdorff metric
, and every continuous map
f:X\longrightarrow X
induces a
d_{H}
-continuos map
\overline{f}:\mathcal{K}(X)\longrightarrow\mathcal{K}(X)
, defined as
\overline{f}(K):=f(K)=\{f(x)\ ;\ x\in K\}
for each
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
. Thus, given a dynamical system
(X,f)
we have reached the first extended system that we will consider through this paper, namely
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
.

We will denote by
\mathcal{B}_{H}(K,\varepsilon)\subset\mathcal{K}(X)
the open
d_{H}
-ball centred at
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
and of radius
\varepsilon>0
. The collection of these balls provide a basis for the topology induced by
d_{H}
. This topology is known to coincide with the so-called
Vietoris topology
, whose basic open sets are the sets of the form

\mathcal{V}(U_{1},U_{2},...,U_{N}):=\left\{K\in\mathcal{K}(X)\ ;\ K\subset\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}U_{j}\text{ and }K\cap U_{j}\neq\varnothing\text{ for all }1\leq j\leq N\right\},

where
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
U_{1},U_{2}...,U_{N}
are non-empty open subsets of
X
. Given
Y\subset X
and
\varepsilon\geq 0
we will write
Y+\varepsilon:=\{x\in X\ ;\ d(x,y)\leq\varepsilon\text{ for some }y\in Y\}
, and the following are well-known facts:

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space,
\varepsilon\geq 0
, and let
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},C_{4}\in\mathcal{C}(X)
. Then:

(a)

We have that
d_{H}(C_{1},C_{2})\leq\varepsilon
if and only if
C_{1}\subset C_{2}+\varepsilon
and
C_{2}\subset C_{1}+\varepsilon
.

(b)

We always have that
d_{H}(C_{1}\cup C_{2},C_{3}\cup C_{4})\leq\max\{d_{H}(C_{1},C_{3}),d_{H}(C_{2},C_{4})\}
.

We refer the reader to
[
17
]
for a detailed study of
\mathcal{C}(X)
and
\mathcal{K}(X)
, but let us now move into the fuzzy content: a
fuzzy set
on the metric space
(X,d)
will be a function
u:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{I}
, where the value
u(x)\in\mathbb{I}
denotes the degree of membership of
x
in
u
. For such a
u
, its
\alpha
-level
is the set

u_{\alpha}:=\{x\in X\ ;\ u(x)\geq\alpha\}\ \text{ for each }\alpha\in\ ]0,1]\quad\text{ and }\quad u_{0}:=\overline{\{x\in X\ ;\ u(x)>0\}}.

We will denote by
\mathcal{F}(X)
the space of normal fuzzy sets of
(X,d)
, i.e. the space formed by the fuzzy sets
u
that are upper-semicontinuous functions and such that
u_{0}
is compact and
u_{1}
is non-empty. For each subset
Y\subset X
we denote by
\chi_{Y}:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{I}
the characteristic function on
Y
, which belongs to the space
\mathcal{F}(X)
if and only if
Y
is compact. The piecewise-constant function
u:=\max_{1\leq l\leq N}(\alpha_{l}\cdot\chi_{K_{l}})
also belongs to
\mathcal{F}(X)
whenever
0<\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<...<\alpha_{N}=1
and
K_{1},K_{2},...,K_{N}\in\mathcal{K}(X)
. Note that
u=\max_{1\leq l\leq N}(\alpha_{l}\cdot\chi_{K_{l}})
fulfills that
u_{\alpha}=\bigcup\left\{K_{l}\ ;\ 1\leq l\leq N\text{ with }\alpha_{l}\geq\alpha\right\}
for each
\alpha\in[0,1]
, and that

u_{\alpha_{l}}=\bigcup_{j\geq l}K_{j}\quad\text{ for each }1\leq l\leq N.

The next result will allow to get piecewise-constant functions near every fuzzy set (see
[
20
,
21
,
30
]
):

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. Given any set
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
and
\varepsilon>0
there exist numbers
0=\alpha_{0}<\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<...<\alpha_{N}=1
such that
d_{H}(u_{\alpha},u_{\alpha_{l+1}})<\varepsilon
for all
\alpha\in\ ]\alpha_{l},\alpha_{l+1}]
and
0\leq l\leq N-1
. In particular, since
d_{H}(u_{\alpha},u_{\alpha_{1}})<\varepsilon
for every
\alpha\in\ ]0,\alpha_{1}]
, we also have that
d_{H}(u_{0},u_{\alpha_{1}})\leq\varepsilon
.

Given a dynamical system
(X,f)
, it follows from
[
20
, Propositions 3.1 and 4.9]
that there exists a well-defined map
\hat{f}:\mathcal{F}(X)\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}(X)
, called the
Zadeh extension
of
f
, which maps each normal fuzzy set
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
to the following normal fuzzy set:

\hat{f}(u):X\longrightarrow\mathbb{I}\quad\text{ where }\quad\left[\hat{f}(u)\right](x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcc}\sup\{u(y)\ ;\ y\in f^{-1}(\{x\})\},&\text{ if }f^{-1}(\{x\})\neq\varnothing,\\[5.0pt]
0,&\text{ if }f^{-1}(\{x\})=\varnothing.\end{array}\right.

Moreover, the following properties are well-known (see
[
20
,
21
,
36
]
for details):

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
f:X\longrightarrow X
be a continuous map acting on a metric space
(X,d)
,
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{I}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
. Then:

(a)

\left[\hat{f}(u)\right]_{\alpha}=f(u_{\alpha})=\overline{f}(u_{\alpha})
, i.e. the
\alpha
-level of the
\hat{f}
-image coincides with the
\overline{f}
-image of the
\alpha
-level;

(b)

\left(\hat{f}\right)^{n}=\widehat{f^{n}}
, i.e. the composition of
\hat{f}
with itself
n
times coincides with the fuzzification of
f^{n}
;

(c)

\hat{f}\left(\chi_{K}\right)=\chi_{f(K)}=\chi_{\overline{f}(K)}
, i.e. the fuzzification
\hat{f}
is an extension of the hyperextension
\overline{f}
.

To talk about the continuity of the Zadeh extension
\hat{f}:\mathcal{F}(X)\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}(X)
, we will endow the space of normal fuzzy sets
\mathcal{F}(X)
with the four different metrics mentioned at the Introduction.

2.2
Four metrics for the space
\mathcal{F}(X)

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. Thus, the
supremum metric

d_{\infty}:\mathcal{F}(X)\times\mathcal{F}(X)\longrightarrow[0,+\infty[
is defined for each pair
u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
as

d_{\infty}(u,v):=\sup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{I}}d_{H}(u_{\alpha},v_{\alpha}),

where
d_{H}
is the Hausdorff metric on
\mathcal{K}(X)
. Such a metric has also been called the
level-wise metric
in many references (see
[
26
]
and the references included there). In the second place we will consider the
Skorokhod metric

d_{0}:\mathcal{F}(X)\times\mathcal{F}(X)\longrightarrow[0,+\infty[
, which is defined for each pair
u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
as

d_{0}(u,v):=\inf\left\{\varepsilon>0\ ;\ \text{there is }\xi\in\mathcal{T}\text{ such that }\sup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{I}}|\xi(\alpha)-\alpha|\leq\varepsilon\text{ and }d_{\infty}(u,\xi\circ v)\leq\varepsilon\right\},

where
\mathcal{T}
is the set of strictly increasing homeomorphisms of the unit interval
\xi:\mathbb{I}\longrightarrow\mathbb{I}
. This metric was introduced, for the case in which
(X,d)
is an arbitrary metric space, in the 2020 paper
[
20
]
.

To talk about the
endograph
and
sendograph metrics
we need to consider an auxiliary metric
\overline{d}
on the product space
X\times\mathbb{I}
, which is defined as follows:

\overline{d}\left((x,\alpha),(y,\beta)\right):=\max\left\{d(x,y),|\alpha-\beta|\right\}\quad\text{ for each pair }(x,\alpha),(y,\beta)\in X\times\mathbb{I}.

Given now any fuzzy set
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
, the
endograph of
u
is defined as the set

\operatorname{end}(u):=\left\{(x,\alpha)\in X\times\mathbb{I}\ ;\ u(x)\geq\alpha\right\},

and the
sendograph of
u
is defined as
\operatorname{send}(u):=\operatorname{end}(u)\cap(u_{0}\times\mathbb{I})
. Then, for each pair
u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
the
endograph metric

d_{E}(u,v)
on
\mathcal{F}(X)
is the Hausdorff distance
\overline{d}_{H}(\operatorname{end}(u),\operatorname{end}(u))
on
\mathcal{C}(X\times\mathbb{I})
, and the
sendograph metric

d_{S}(u,v)
on
\mathcal{F}(X)
is the Hausdorff metric
\overline{d}_{H}(\operatorname{send}(u),\operatorname{send}(v))
on
\mathcal{K}(X\times\mathbb{I})
. Although
d_{E}
is defined as a distance of closed but not necessarily compact sets on
X\times\mathbb{I}
, the fact that the supports of the fuzzy sets
u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
are compact implies that
d_{E}(u,v)
is well-defined.

Following
[
27
]
we will denote by
\mathcal{B}_{\infty}(u,\varepsilon)
,
\mathcal{B}_{0}(u,\varepsilon)
,
\mathcal{B}_{S}(u,\varepsilon)
and
\mathcal{B}_{E}(u,\varepsilon)
the open balls centred at
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
and of radius
\varepsilon>0
for each of the metrics
d_{\infty}
,
d_{0}
,
d_{S}
and
d_{E}
. Moreover, and as mentioned in the Introduction, for each metric
\rho\in\{d_{\infty},d_{0},d_{S},d_{E}\}
we will denote the metric space
(\mathcal{F}(X),\rho)
by
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X)
,
\mathcal{F}_{0}(X)
,
\mathcal{F}_{S}(X)
and
\mathcal{F}_{E}(X)
respectively, and
\tau_{\infty}
,
\tau_{0}
,
\tau_{S}
and
\tau_{E}
will stand for the respective induced topologies on
\mathcal{F}(X)
. These metrics come from the general theory of Spaces of Fuzzy Sets and each of them has its own role and importance (see
[
27
, Section 2]
). The study of the relationships between these metrics has been an important topic addressed in the literature and we recall here the main relations among them (see
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
27
]
and the references therein for more details):

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space,
u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
,
K,L\in\mathcal{K}(X)
and
x\in X
. Then:

(a)

d_{E}(u,v)\leq d_{S}(u,v)\leq d_{0}(u,v)\leq d_{\infty}(u,v)
and hence
\tau_{E}\subset\tau_{S}\subset\tau_{0}\subset\tau_{\infty}
.

(b)

d_{S}(\chi_{\{x\}},u)=d_{0}(\chi_{\{x\}},u)=d_{\infty}(\chi_{\{x\}},u)=d_{H}(\{x\},u_{0})=\max\{d(x,y)\ ;\ y\in u_{0}\}
.

(c)

d_{0}(\chi_{K},u)=d_{\infty}(\chi_{K},u)=\max\{d_{H}(K,u_{0}),d_{H}(K,u_{1})\}
.

(d)

d_{E}(\chi_{K},\chi_{L})=\min\{d_{H}(K,L),1\}
while
d_{S}(\chi_{K},\chi_{L})=d_{0}(\chi_{K},\chi_{L})=d_{\infty}(\chi_{K},\chi_{L})=d_{H}(K,L)
.

(e)

d_{H}(u_{0},v_{0})\leq d_{S}(u,v)
and also
\max\{d_{H}(u_{0},v_{0}),d_{H}(u_{1},v_{1})\}\leq d_{0}(u,v)
.

The next lemma was the key fact used in
[
27
]
to get most of the results obtained there, but we will also use it here in Sections
4
and
5
:

Lemma 2.5

(
[
27
, Lemma 2.4]
)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space, and assume that the sets
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
and
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
fulfill that
\delta:=d_{E}(\chi_{K},u)<\tfrac{1}{2}
. Then
d_{H}(K,u_{\alpha})\leq\delta
for every
\alpha\in\ ]\delta,1-\delta]
.

Given a dynamical system
(X,f)
, the continuity of the extension
\hat{f}:(\mathcal{F}(X),\rho)\longrightarrow(\mathcal{F}(X),\rho)
for every metric
\rho\in\{d_{\infty},d_{0},d_{S},d_{E}\}
is also well-known (see
[
20
]
and
[
26
]
). Thus, we have reached the second extended system that we will consider through this paper, namely
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
. However, before studying the dynamical properties of such a system, let us close this section by proving that the endograph metric
d_{E}
presents an extreme behaviour with respect to the existence of isolated points:

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. Hence:

(a)

The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

the set
X
is a singleton;

(ii)

the set
\mathcal{K}(X)
is a singleton;

(iii)

the set
\mathcal{F}(X)
is a singleton.

(b)

The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

the metric space
(X,d)
has at least one isolated point;

(ii)

the metric space
(\mathcal{K}(X),d_{H})
has at least one isolated point;

(iii)

the metric space
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X)
has at least one isolated point;

(iv)

the metric space
\mathcal{F}_{0}(X)
has at least one isolated point;

(v)

the metric space
\mathcal{F}_{S}(X)
has at least one isolated point.

(c)

If the set
X
is not a singleton, then the metric space
\mathcal{F}_{E}(X)
has no isolated points.

Proof.

(a): It is not hard to check that
X
is a singleton, say
X=\{x\}
, if and only if
\mathcal{K}(X)=\{\{x\}\}
, but also if and only if
\mathcal{F}(X)=\{\chi_{\{x\}}\}
.

(b): The equivalence (i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii) is well-known (see
[
17
, Proposition 8.3]
). To prove (i)
\Rightarrow
(v), assume that
x\in X
is an isolated point for
(X,d)
so that there is some
\delta>0
for which
\{x\}+\delta=\{x\}
. Thus, given any fuzzy set
u\in\mathcal{B}_{S}(\chi_{\{x\}},\delta)
we have that
d_{H}(\{x\},u_{0})=d_{S}(\chi_{\{x\}},u)\leq\delta
, but then we have that
u_{0}\subset\{x\}+\delta=\{x\}
, so that
u_{0}=\{x\}
and hence
u=\chi_{\{x\}}
. It follows that the fuzzy set
\chi_{\{x\}}
is an isolated point for
\mathcal{F}_{S}(X)
, which proves (v). The implications (v)
\Rightarrow
(iv)
\Rightarrow
(iii) follow from the inclusions
\tau_{S}\subset\tau_{0}\subset\tau_{\infty}
. Finally, to prove (iii)
\Rightarrow
(i), assume that
(X,d)
has no isolated points and let us check that, given any
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
and any
\varepsilon>0
, the set
\mathcal{B}_{\infty}(u,\varepsilon)\setminus\{u\}
is non-empty. Indeed, choose any
x\in u_{1}
and consider the open
d
-ball
\mathcal{B}_{d}(x,\varepsilon)
. We thus have two possibilities:

–

Case 1
:
There exists some point
y\in\mathcal{B}_{d}(x,\varepsilon)\setminus u_{1}
. In this case we can consider the function

v:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{I}\quad\text{ with }\quad v:=\max\{u,\chi_{\{y\}}\}.

It is not hard to check that
v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
, that
u\neq v
, and that
v\in\mathcal{B}_{\infty}(u,\varepsilon)\setminus\{u\}
.

–

Case 2
:
We have the inclusion
\mathcal{B}_{d}(x,\varepsilon)\subset u_{1}
. In this case pick any positive value
0<\delta<\varepsilon
fulfilling that
X\setminus\mathcal{B}_{d}(x,\delta)\neq\varnothing
, which can be done because we are assuming that
(X,d)
has no isolated points so that
X
is not a singleton, and consider the function

w:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{I}\quad\text{ with }\quad w:=u\cdot\chi_{X\setminus\mathcal{B}_{d}(x,\delta)}.

It is not hard to check that
w\in\mathcal{F}(X)
, that
u\neq w
, and that
w\in\mathcal{B}_{\infty}(u,\varepsilon)\setminus\{u\}
.

(c): Assume that the set
X
is not a singleton and, given any fuzzy set
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
and any
\varepsilon>0
, let us prove that
\mathcal{B}_{E}(u,\varepsilon)\setminus\{u\}
is non-empty. We have two possibilities:

–

Case 1
:
We have that
u_{1}\neq X
. In this case pick any point
x\in X\setminus u_{1}
, pick any positive value
0<\delta<\varepsilon
and let
\alpha:=\min\{u(x)+\delta,1\}
. Thus, we can consider the function

v:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{I}\quad\text{ with }\quad v:=\max\{u,\alpha\cdot\chi_{\{x\}}\}.

It is not hard to check that
v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
, that
u\neq v
, and that
v\in\mathcal{B}_{E}(u,\varepsilon)\setminus\{u\}
.

–

Case 2
:
We have that
u_{1}=X
. In this case pick any point
x\in X=u_{1}
and pick any positive value
0<\delta<\varepsilon
fulfilling that
X\setminus\mathcal{B}_{d}(x,\delta)\neq\varnothing
, which can be done because we are assuming that
X
is not a singleton. Thus, we can consider the function

w:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{I}\quad\text{ with }\quad w:=u\cdot\chi_{X\setminus\mathcal{B}_{d}(x,\delta)}.

It is not hard to check that
w\in\mathcal{F}(X)
, that
u\neq w
, and that
w\in\mathcal{B}_{\infty}(u,\varepsilon)\setminus\{u\}\subset\mathcal{B}_{E}(u,\varepsilon)\setminus\{u\}
.∎

Remark 2.7
.

A result stronger than statement (c) of Lemma
2.6
holds:
for any metric space
(X,d)
, the respective metric space
\mathcal{F}_{E}(X)
is path-connected
. We do not use this fact along the paper, but let us briefly justify it. Actually, to avoid trivialities, let us assume that
X
and hence the set
\mathcal{F}(X)
have more than one point. Thus, given two distinct fuzzy sets
u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
we can find a continuous path from
u
to
v
by concatenating four paths: pick
x\in u_{1}
and
y\in v_{1}
, assume that
x\neq y
since otherwise we can avoid paths
\gamma_{2}
and
\gamma_{3}
, and let
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}:[0,1]\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}(X)
be defined for each
t\in[0,1]
as
\gamma_{1}(t):=\max\{\chi_{\{x\}},(1-t)\cdot u\}
,
\gamma_{2}(t):=\chi_{\{x\}}+t\cdot\chi_{\{y\}}
,
\gamma_{3}(t):=\chi_{\{y\}}+(1-t)\cdot\chi_{\{x\}}
and
\gamma_{4}(t):=\max\{\chi_{\{y\}},t\cdot v\}
. It can be checked that
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3}
and
\gamma_{4}
are continuous precisely when
\mathcal{F}(X)
is endowed with the endograph metric
d_{E}
. Moreover, we have that
\gamma_{1}(0)=u
,
\gamma_{1}(1)=\chi_{\{x\}}=\gamma_{2}(0)
,
\gamma_{2}(1)=\chi_{\{x,y\}}=\gamma_{3}(0)
,
\gamma_{3}(1)=\chi_{\{y\}}=\gamma_{4}(0)
and
\gamma_{4}(1)=v
. The stated result follows.

3
Contractions and expansive properties

In this section we focus on the contractive and expansive-type properties considered in
[
18
,
20
]
. Our results solve
[
18
, Problems 3.6, 3.7 and 4.3]
and
[
20
, Problems 5.8 and 5.12]
. We have divided the section into three parts: first we contextualize and state our main result (see Theorem
3.1
); then we prove it using Lemma
3.3
; and we finally solve
[
18
, Problem 3.7]
in the negative with Example
3.4
.

3.1
Definitions and main result

Following
[
18
,
20
]
, given a continuous map
f:X\longrightarrow X
acting on a metric space
(X,d)
, we will say that the corresponding dynamical system
(X,f)
is:

–

contractive
if there exists
\lambda\in[0,1[
such that
d(f(x),f(y))\leq\lambda d(x,y)
for every
x,y\in X
;

–

expansive
if there exists
\lambda>1
such that
d(f(x),f(y))\geq\lambda d(x,y)
for every
x,y\in X
.

These properties come from the theory of Dynamical Systems and they have played a very important role in such a field. Actually, very well-known results such as the Banach fixed-point theorem, or the so-called Walter topologically-stable theorem, justify the importance of the contractive and expansive dynamical systems. Moreover, several variations of the expansive property have been considered in the literature and, following
[
18
,
33
]
, we will say that a dynamical system
(X,f)
is:

–

expanding
if there exist positive values
\varepsilon>0
and
\lambda>1
such that
d(f(x),f(y))>\lambda d(x,y)
for every pair of points
x,y\in X
fulfilling that
0<d(x,y)<\varepsilon
;

–

positively expansive
if there exists a constant
\delta>0
such that for any pair of distinct points
x\neq y
in
X
there is a non-negative integer
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
fulfilling that
d(f^{n}(x),f^{n}(y))>\delta
.

It is known that every
expansive
system is
expanding
, and the notion of
positive expansiveness
has been considered on dynamical systems over compact manifolds with boundary. For instance, it was proved in
[
15
, Theorem 1]
that no compact connected manifold with boundary admits a positively expansive map, and this kind of negative result is similar to the ones we obtain here. Let us recall that, for the systems
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
, the next facts have been proved in
[
4
,
18
,
20
,
29
,
35
]
:

–

a dynamical system
(X,f)
is contractive if and only if so is
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
, but also if and only if so is the fuzzy extension
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
, see
[
4
, Section III.7]
and
[
20
, Proposition 5.7]
;

–

a dynamical system
(X,f)
is expansive if and only if so is
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
, but also if and only if so is the fuzzy extension
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
, see
[
35
, Proposition 3]
and
[
29
, Theorem 5]
;

–

the system
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
is expanding (resp. positively expansive) if and only if so is
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
, and in that case
(X,f)
is expanding (resp. positively expansive)
, see
[
18
, Theorems 3.1 and 4.1]
;

–

there is a expanding (resp. positively expansive) dynamical system
(X,f)
for which
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
and hence
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
are not expanding (resp. positively expansive)
, see
[
18
, Examples 3.2 and 4.2]
.

In this context, it was asked in
[
18
,
20
]
about the possible contractive, expansive, expanding and positively expansive behaviour of
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
, whenever
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
presents any of these properties (see
[
18
, Problems 3.6 and 4.3]
and
[
20
, Problems 5.8 and 5.12]
). The main result of this section shows that it occurs only when the original system
(X,f)
is trivial:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
f:X\longrightarrow X
be a continuous map on a metric space
(X,d)
. Hence:

(a)

The extended dynamical systems
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
are contractive if and only if the map
f
is constant.

(b)

The extended dynamical systems
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
are expansive, expanding and positively expansive if and only if the set
X
is a singleton.

Remark 3.2
.

The reader should note that:

(1)

Statement (a) of Theorem
3.1
ca be used to reprove
[
37
, Example 7]
in a shorter way, it generalizes
[
37
, Theorem 8]
, and it completely solves both
[
20
, Problems 5.8 and 5.12]
by characterizing the systems
(X,f)
whose extensions
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
are contractive.

(2)

Statement (b) of Theorem
3.1
solves both
[
18
, Problems 3.6 and 4.3]
, it can be used to reprove in a shorter way
[
18
, Examples 3.5, 3.9 and 4.6]
, and it shows that the implications “(iv)
\Rightarrow
(iii)” proved in
[
18
, Theorems 3.1 and 4.1]
, but also the results
[
18
, Propositions 3.8, 4.4 and 4.5]
, are practically void (such results only apply when
X
is a singleton, which is rarely assumed).

3.2
Proof of Theorem
3.1

To prove Theorem
3.1
we will need an auxiliary lemma. From now on, given a metric space
(X,d)
and a subset
Y\subset X
we will denote by
\operatorname{diam}_{d}(Y):=\sup\{d(x,y)\ ;\ x,y\in Y\}
the
d
-diameter
of
Y
. Note that the set
X
is a singleton if and only if
\operatorname{diam}_{d}(X)=0
, and that given any map
f:X\longrightarrow X
we have that
f
is constant if and only if
\operatorname{diam}_{d}(f(X))=0
. With this notation we have the following:

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
f:X\longrightarrow X
be a continuous map on a metric space
(X,d)
. Hence:

(a)

If we have that
\operatorname{diam}_{d}(f(X))>0
, then for each integer
k>\max\{2,\operatorname{diam}_{d}(f(X))^{-1}\}
there exist two normal fuzzy sets
u,u^{k}\in\mathcal{F}(X)
fulfilling that

d_{E}(u,u^{k})\leq d_{S}(u,u^{k})\leq d_{0}(u,u^{k})\leq\tfrac{1}{k}

and

d_{E}(\hat{f}(u),\hat{f}(u^{k}))=d_{S}(\hat{f}(u),\hat{f}(u^{k}))=d_{0}(\hat{f}(u),\hat{f}(u^{k}))=\tfrac{1}{k}.

(b)

If we have that
\operatorname{diam}_{d}(X)>0
, then for each integer
k>\max\{2,\operatorname{diam}_{d}(X)^{-1}\}
there exist two normal fuzzy sets
u,u^{k}\in\mathcal{F}(X)
fulfilling that

d_{E}(u,u^{k})=d_{S}(u,u^{k})=d_{0}(u,u^{k})=\tfrac{1}{k}

and

d_{E}(\hat{f}^{n}(u),\hat{f}^{n}(u^{k}))\leq d_{S}(\hat{f}^{n}(u),\hat{f}^{n}(u^{k}))\leq d_{0}(\hat{f}^{n}(u),\hat{f}^{n}(u^{k}))\leq\tfrac{1}{k}\quad\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

In the statement of Lemma
3.3
we could have that
\operatorname{diam}_{d}(f(X))=\infty
or that
\operatorname{diam}_{d}(X)=\infty
, respectively. In each of this cases, the value
\operatorname{diam}_{d}(f(X))^{-1}
or
\operatorname{diam}_{d}(X)^{-1}
would be taken equal to
0
, so that the only limitation of the respective statement would be considering
k>2
.

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.

In both parts (a) and (b) of the statement we are going to fix some integer
k>2
and two distinct points
x,y\in X
, and then we will consider the normal fuzzy sets

u:=\chi_{\{x\}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\chi_{\{y\}}\quad\text{ and }\quad u^{k}:=\chi_{\{x\}}+\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{k}\right)\chi_{\{y\}}.

(3.1)

Moreover, for each integer
k>2
we will consider the 2-piecewise-linear map
\xi_{k}:\mathbb{I}\longrightarrow\mathbb{I}
defined as

\xi_{k}(\alpha):=\begin{cases}\tfrac{k}{k-2}\alpha&\text{ if }0\leq\alpha\leq\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{k},\\[5.0pt]
\tfrac{k}{k+2}\alpha+\tfrac{2}{k+2}&\text{ if }\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{k}<\alpha\leq 1,\end{cases}

(3.2)

which fulfills that
\xi_{k}(0)=0
, that
\xi_{k}(\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{k})=\tfrac{1}{2}
and that
\xi_{k}(1)=1
. It is not hard to check that

\sup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{I}}|\xi_{k}(\alpha)-\alpha|=\left|\xi_{k}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{k}\right)-\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{k}\right)\right|=\left|\tfrac{1}{2}-\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{k}\right)\right|=\tfrac{1}{k},

(3.3)

and that
\xi_{k}^{-1}(\tfrac{1}{2})=\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{k}
. We are now ready to proceed with the proof.

(a): Assume that
\operatorname{diam}_{d}(f(X))>0
. Then, fixed any integer
k>\max\{2,\operatorname{diam}_{d}(f(X))^{-1}\}
we can find points
x,y\in X
such that
\tfrac{1}{k}\leq d(f(x),f(y))\leq\operatorname{diam}_{d}(f(X))
. It follows that
f(x)\neq f(y)
, so that
x
and
y
are necessarily distinct and we can consider the sets
u,u^{k}\in\mathcal{F}(X)
as defined in (
3.1
). Now, since
d_{E}(v,w)\leq d_{S}(v,w)\leq d_{0}(v,w)
holds for every
v,w\in\mathcal{F}(X)
as stated in Proposition
2.4
, to show that
u
and
u^{k}
fulfill the required conditions we only need to prove the next two inequalities:

–

Inequality 1
:
We have that
d_{0}(\hat{f}^{n}(u),\hat{f}^{n}(u^{k}))\leq\tfrac{1}{k}
for
n\in\{0,1\}
. Fix
n\in\{0,1\}
and note that

\hat{f}^{n}(u)=\chi_{\{f^{n}(x)\}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\chi_{\{f^{n}(y)\}}=\begin{cases}\chi_{\{x\}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\chi_{\{y\}}&\text{ if }n=0,\\[5.0pt]
\chi_{\{f(x)\}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\chi_{\{f(y)\}}&\text{ if }n=1,\end{cases}

but also that

\hat{f}^{n}(u^{k})=\chi_{\{f^{n}(x)\}}+\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{k}\right)\chi_{\{f^{n}(y)\}}=\begin{cases}\chi_{\{x\}}+\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{k}\right)\chi_{\{y\}}&\text{ if }n=0,\\[5.0pt]
\chi_{\{f(x)\}}+\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{k}\right)\chi_{\{f(y)\}}&\text{ if }n=1.\end{cases}

Considering the strictly increasing homeomorphism
\xi_{k}:\mathbb{I}\longrightarrow\mathbb{I}
as defined in (
3.2
) we have that

\left[\xi_{k}\circ\left(\hat{f}^{n}(u^{k})\right)\right]_{\alpha}=\left[\hat{f}^{n}(u^{k})\right]_{\xi_{k}^{-1}(\alpha)}=\begin{cases}\{f^{n}(x),f^{n}(y)\}&\text{ if }0\leq\alpha\leq\tfrac{1}{2},\\[5.0pt]
\{f^{n}(x)\}&\text{ if }\tfrac{1}{2}<\alpha\leq 1.\end{cases}

This implies that
\hat{f}^{n}(u)=\xi_{k}\circ\hat{f}^{n}(u^{k})
and hence
d_{\infty}(\hat{f}^{n}(u),\xi_{k}\circ\hat{f}^{n}(u^{k}))=0
. We finally deduce that

d_{0}(\hat{f}^{n}(u),\hat{f}^{n}(u^{k}))\leq\max\left\{\sup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{I}}|\xi_{k}(\alpha)-\alpha|\ ,\ d_{\infty}(\hat{f}^{n}(u),\xi_{k}\circ\hat{f}^{n}(u^{k}))\right\}\overset{\eqref{eq:xi_k=1/k}}{=}\tfrac{1}{k}.

–

Inequality 2
:
We have that
d_{E}(\hat{f}(u),\hat{f}(u^{k}))\geq\tfrac{1}{k}
. We use statement (a) of Proposition
2.1
: fixed any
0\leq\varepsilon<\tfrac{1}{k}
we will check that
\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u))\not\subset\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u^{k}))+\varepsilon
. In fact,
(f(y),\tfrac{1}{2})\in\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u))
,

\inf_{(z,\alpha)\in\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u^{k}))}\overline{d}((f(y),\tfrac{1}{2}),(z,\alpha))=\inf_{(z,\alpha)\in\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u^{k}))}\max\{d(f(y),z),|\tfrac{1}{2}-\alpha|\},

and for each
(z,\alpha)\in\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u^{k}))
we have that

\displaystyle\max\{d(f(y),z),|\tfrac{1}{2}-\alpha|\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\max\{d(f(y),z),\tfrac{1}{2}\}&\text{ if }\alpha=0\text{ and }z\in X,\\[5.0pt]
\max\{d(f(y),f(y)),|\tfrac{1}{2}-\alpha|\}&\text{ if }0<\alpha\leq\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{k}\text{ and }z=f(y),\\[5.0pt]
\max\{d(f(y),f(x)),|\tfrac{1}{2}-\alpha|\}&\text{ if }0<\alpha\leq 1\text{ and }z=f(x),\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\tfrac{1}{2}&\text{ if }\alpha=0\text{ and }z\in X,\\[5.0pt]
\tfrac{1}{k}&\text{ if }0<\alpha\leq\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{k}\text{ and }z=f(y),\\[5.0pt]
d(f(x),f(y))&\text{ if }0<\alpha\leq 1\text{ and }z=f(x),\end{array}\right\}\geq\tfrac{1}{k}>\varepsilon.

We get that
d_{E}(\hat{f}(u),\hat{f}(u^{k}))>\varepsilon
and the arbitrariness of
0\leq\varepsilon<\tfrac{1}{k}
shows that
d_{E}(\hat{f}(u),\hat{f}(u^{k}))\geq\tfrac{1}{k}
.

(b): Assume now that
\operatorname{diam}_{d}(X)>0
. Then, fixed any integer
k>\max\{2,\operatorname{diam}_{d}(X)^{-1}\}
we can find points
x,y\in X
such that
\tfrac{1}{k}\leq d(x,y)\leq\operatorname{diam}_{d}(X)
. It follows that
x
and
y
are distinct and we can consider the sets
u,u^{k}\in\mathcal{F}(X)
as defined in (
3.1
). Using again that
d_{E}(v,w)\leq d_{S}(v,w)\leq d_{0}(v,w)
holds for every
v,w\in\mathcal{F}(X)
, we only need to prove the next two inequalities:

–

Inequality 1
:
We have that
d_{E}(u,u^{k})\geq\tfrac{1}{k}
. As above, to check this inequality we use statement (a) of Proposition
2.1
: fixed any
0\leq\varepsilon<\tfrac{1}{k}
we will check that
\operatorname{end}(u)\not\subset\operatorname{end}(u^{k})+\varepsilon
. In fact, similarly to the previous case, the point
(y,\tfrac{1}{2})\in\operatorname{end}(u)
fulfills that

\inf_{(z,\alpha)\in\operatorname{end}(u^{k})}\overline{d}((y,\tfrac{1}{2}),(z,\alpha))=\inf_{(z,\alpha)\in\operatorname{end}(u^{k})}\max\{d(y,z),|\tfrac{1}{2}-\alpha|\},

and for each
(z,\alpha)\in\operatorname{end}(u^{k})
we have that

\displaystyle\max\{d(y,z),|\tfrac{1}{2}-\alpha|\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\max\{d(y,z),\tfrac{1}{2}\}&\text{ if }\alpha=0\text{ and }z\in X,\\[5.0pt]
\max\{d(y,y),|\tfrac{1}{2}-\alpha|\}&\text{ if }0<\alpha\leq\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{k}\text{ and }z=y,\\[5.0pt]
\max\{d(y,x),|\tfrac{1}{2}-\alpha|\}&\text{ if }0<\alpha\leq 1\text{ and }z=x,\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\tfrac{1}{2}&\text{ if }\alpha=0\text{ and }z\in X,\\[5.0pt]
\tfrac{1}{k}&\text{ if }0<\alpha\leq\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{k}\text{ and }z=y,\\[5.0pt]
d(x,y)&\text{ if }0<\alpha\leq 1\text{ and }z=x,\end{array}\right\}\geq\tfrac{1}{k}>\varepsilon.

We get that
d_{E}(u,u^{k})>\varepsilon
and the arbitrariness of
0\leq\varepsilon<\tfrac{1}{k}
shows that
d_{E}(u,u^{k})\geq\tfrac{1}{k}
.

–

Inequality 2
:
We have that
d_{0}(\hat{f}^{n}(u),\hat{f}^{n}(u^{k}))\leq\tfrac{1}{k}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Fix any
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and note that

\hat{f}^{n}(u)=\begin{cases}\chi_{\{f^{n}(x)\}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\chi_{\{f^{n}(y)\}}&\text{ if }f^{n}(x)\neq f^{n}(y),\\[5.0pt]
\chi_{\{f^{n}(x)\}}&\text{ if }f^{n}(x)=f^{n}(y),\end{cases}

but also that

\hat{f}^{n}(u^{k})=\begin{cases}\chi_{\{f^{n}(x)\}}+\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{k}\right)\chi_{\{f^{n}(y)\}}&\text{ if }f^{n}(x)\neq f^{n}(y),\\[5.0pt]
\chi_{\{f^{n}(x)\}}&\text{ if }f^{n}(x)=f^{n}(y).\end{cases}

If
f^{n}(x)=f^{n}(y)
then we would have that
\hat{f}^{n}(u)=\hat{f}^{n}(u^{k})
and
d_{0}(\hat{f}^{n}(u),\hat{f}^{n}(u^{k}))=0\leq\tfrac{1}{k}
, which would finish the proof in this case. Assume now that
f^{n}(x)\neq f^{n}(y)
. Considering then the strictly increasing homeomorphism
\xi_{k}:\mathbb{I}\longrightarrow\mathbb{I}
as defined in (
3.2
) we have that

\left[\xi_{k}\circ\left(\hat{f}^{n}(u^{k})\right)\right]_{\alpha}=\left[\hat{f}^{n}(u^{k})\right]_{\xi_{k}^{-1}(\alpha)}=\begin{cases}\{f^{n}(x),f^{n}(y)\}&\text{ if }0\leq\alpha\leq\tfrac{1}{2},\\[5.0pt]
\{f^{n}(x)\}&\text{ if }\tfrac{1}{2}<\alpha\leq 1.\end{cases}

This implies that
\hat{f}^{n}(u)=\xi_{k}\circ\hat{f}(u^{k})
and hence
d_{\infty}(\hat{f}^{n}(u),\xi_{k}\circ\hat{f}^{n}(u^{k}))=0
. We finally deduce that

d_{0}(\hat{f}^{n}(u),\hat{f}^{n}(u^{k}))\leq\max\left\{\sup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{I}}|\xi_{k}(\alpha)-\alpha|\ ,\ d_{\infty}(\hat{f}^{n}(u),\xi_{k}\circ\hat{f}^{n}(u^{k}))\right\}\overset{\eqref{eq:xi_k=1/k}}{=}\tfrac{1}{k}.\qed

The proof of Lemma
3.3
is now complete. Note that the choice of the fuzzy sets
u
and
u^{k}
considered in the previous proof admits several variations: for instance, adapting the requirement
k>2
, one could have taken
u=\chi_{\{x\}}+\alpha\chi_{\{y\}}
for any
\alpha\in\ ]0,1[
together with
u^{k}=\chi_{\{x\}}+(\alpha\pm\tfrac{1}{k})\chi_{\{y\}}
instead than the sets
u
and
u^{k}
considered in (
3.1
). In any case, we are now ready to prove Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

(a): Assume first that the map
f:X\longrightarrow X
is constant, so that there exists some point
x^{*}\in X
fulfilling that
f(x)=x^{*}
for all
x\in X
. In this case, it is not hard to check that

\hat{f}(u)=\chi_{\{x^{*}\}}\quad\text{ for all }u\in\mathcal{F}(X).

The system
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
is then contractive for
\lambda=0
, regardless of the topology considered on
\mathcal{F}(X)
.

Conversely, if the map
f:X\longrightarrow X
is non-constant then we have that
\operatorname{diam}_{d}(f(X))>0
. Fix any metric
\rho\in\{d_{0},d_{S},d_{E}\}
and, by contradiction, assume that the dynamical system
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
is contractive when the space
\mathcal{F}(X)
is endowed with the metric
\rho
. In that case there exists a real value
\lambda\in[0,1[
such that
\rho(\hat{f}(v),\hat{f}(w))\leq\lambda\rho(v,w)
for every pair of sets
v,w\in\mathcal{F}(X)
. Applying statement (a) of Lemma
3.3
for any
k>\max\{2,\operatorname{diam}_{d}(f(X))^{-1}\}
we get
u,u^{k}\in\mathcal{F}(X)
for which

\tfrac{1}{k}=\rho(\hat{f}(u),\hat{f}(u^{k}))\leq\lambda\rho(u,u^{k})\leq\lambda\tfrac{1}{k}<\tfrac{1}{k},

which is a contradiction.

(b): Assume first that the set
X
is a singleton. In this case, Lemma
2.6
implies that
\mathcal{F}(X)
is also a singleton, so that
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
reduces to a dynamical system consisting of a single fixed point. It is straightforward to verify that such a system trivially satisfies the definitions of being expansive, expanding, and positively expansive, regardless of the topology considered on
\mathcal{F}(X)
.

Conversely, if the set
X
is not a singleton then we have that
\operatorname{diam}_{d}(X)>0
. As in part (a), let us fix any metric
\rho\in\{d_{0},d_{S},d_{E}\}
and, by contradiction:

–

Assume that the dynamical system
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
is expansive when the space
\mathcal{F}(X)
is endowed with the metric
\rho
. Then there exists a positive value
\lambda>1
such that
\rho(\hat{f}(v),\hat{f}(w))\geq\lambda\rho(v,w)
for every pair of points
v,w\in\mathcal{F}(X)
. Applying now statement (b) of Lemma
3.3
for any
k>\max\{2,\operatorname{diam}_{d}(X)^{-1}\}
we get two fuzzy sets
u,u^{k}\in\mathcal{F}(X)
fulfilling that

\tfrac{1}{k}\geq\rho(\hat{f}(u),\hat{f}(u^{k}))\geq\lambda\rho(u,u^{k})=\lambda\tfrac{1}{k}>\tfrac{1}{k},

which is a contradiction.

–

Assume that the dynamical system
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
is expanding when the space
\mathcal{F}(X)
is endowed with the metric
\rho
. Then there exist
\varepsilon>0
and
\lambda>1
such that
\rho(\hat{f}(v),\hat{f}(w))>\lambda\rho(v,w)
for every pair of normal fuzzy sets
v,w\in\mathcal{F}(X)
fulfilling that
0<\rho(v,w)<\varepsilon
. Applying now statement (b) of Lemma
3.3
for any
k>\max\{2,\operatorname{diam}_{d}(X)^{-1},\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\}
we get two normal fuzzy sets
u,u^{k}\in\mathcal{F}(X)
fulfilling that
0<\rho(u,u^{k})=\tfrac{1}{k}<\varepsilon
and also that

\tfrac{1}{k}\geq\rho(\hat{f}(u),\hat{f}(u^{k}))>\lambda\rho(u,u^{k})=\lambda\tfrac{1}{k}>\tfrac{1}{k},

which is a contradiction.

–

Assume that the system
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
is positively expansive when the space of fuzzy sets
\mathcal{F}(X)
is endowed with the metric
\rho
. Then there exists a constant
\delta>0
such that for any pair of distinct sets
v\neq w
in
\mathcal{F}(X)
there is some
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
fulfilling that
\rho(\hat{f}^{n}(v),\hat{f}^{n}(w))>\delta
. Applying once more statement (b) of Lemma
3.3
for any
k>\max\{2,\operatorname{diam}_{d}(X)^{-1},\tfrac{1}{\delta}\}
, we finally get two distinct fuzzy sets
u\neq u^{k}
in
\mathcal{F}(X)
fulfilling that

\delta>\tfrac{1}{k}\geq\rho(\hat{f}^{n}(u),\hat{f}^{n}(u^{k}))\quad\text{ for every }n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

which is a contradiction.∎

The proof of Theorem
3.1
is now complete. To finish Section
3
we will solve
[
18
, Problem 3.7]
in the negative. This is the only problem, from
[
18
, Problems 3.6, 3.7 and 4.3]
and
[
20
, Problems 5.8 and 5.12]
, that can not be directly solved via Theorem
3.1
(see Remark
3.2
for more details).

3.3
The solution to an expanding problem and a useful lemma

In
[
18
]
the authors also left
[
18
, Problem 3.7]
as an open question:
Let
f
be a continuous map from a metric space
(X,d)
into itself and assume that
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
is expanding. Is it true that there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
d_{0}(\hat{f}(u),\hat{f}(v))\geq d_{0}(u,v)
for all
u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
fulfilling that
d_{0}(u,v)<\varepsilon
?
We will solve this problem in the negative with a counterexample. We also use such example of dynamical system to show that the inequality “
d_{0}(u,u^{k})\leq\tfrac{1}{k}
” in statement (b) of Lemma
3.3
can be strict:

Example 3.4
.

There exists a system
(X,f)
such that
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
is expanding while for each
\varepsilon>0
there exists
u^{\varepsilon},v^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{F}(X)
fulfilling that
\rho(\hat{f}(u^{\varepsilon}),\hat{f}(v^{\varepsilon}))<\rho(u^{\varepsilon},v^{\varepsilon})<\varepsilon
for all
\rho\in\{d_{0},d_{S},d_{E}\}
.

Proof.

Let
X=\{a,b\}
with the usual discrete metric, i.e. given
x,y\in X
we have that
d(x,y)=0
when
x=y
and
d(x,y)=1
when
x\neq y
. Let
f:X\longrightarrow X
with
f(a)=a
and
f(b)=a
. Since both metric spaces
(X,d)
and
(\mathcal{K}(X),d_{H})
are discrete, it is not hard to see that
(X,f)
and
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
are expanding dynamical systems by picking any positive value
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
and any
\lambda>1
. Now, let
\varepsilon>0
and fix an integer
k>\max\{2,\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\}
. Similar to what we did in (
3.1
), we can consider the fuzzy sets
u^{\varepsilon}:=\chi_{\{a\}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\chi_{\{b\}}
and
v^{\varepsilon}:=\chi_{\{a\}}+\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{k}\right)\chi_{\{b\}}
. Since
\operatorname{diam}_{d}(X)=1
and
k>\max\{2,\operatorname{diam}_{d}(X)^{-1}\}
, the proof of part (b) of Lemma
3.3
shows that
\rho(u^{\varepsilon},v^{\varepsilon})=\tfrac{1}{k}<\varepsilon
. Noticing that

\hat{f}(u^{\varepsilon})=\max\{\chi_{\{f(a)\}},\tfrac{1}{2}\chi_{\{f(b)\}}\}=\chi_{\{a\}}=\max\{\chi_{\{f(a)\}},\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{k}\right)\chi_{\{f(b)\}}\}=\hat{f}(v^{\varepsilon}),

we have that
\rho(\hat{f}(u^{\varepsilon}),\hat{f}(v^{\varepsilon}))=0<\rho(u^{\varepsilon},v^{\varepsilon})=\tfrac{1}{k}<\varepsilon
, finishing the counterexample.
∎

The system
(X,f)
from Example
3.4
admits several variations; for instance, its disjoint union with any expansive system is again a counterexample to
[
18
, Problem 3.7]
for which the expanding property of
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
is not necessarily trivial (the details are left to the reader). Following
[
18
,
37
]
we will end this section proving that, although Theorem
3.1
prevents
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
from being contractive or expansive in general, these systems may still behave in an “almost” contractive or expansive manner whenever
(X,f)
itself has such a property.

Lemma 3.5
.

For a continuous map
f:X\longrightarrow X
on a metric space
(X,d)
and any
\rho\in\{d_{0},d_{S},d_{E}\}
:

(a)

If
(X,f)
is a contractive dynamical system, then
\rho(\hat{f}(u),\hat{f}(v))\leq\rho(u,v)
for all
u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
.

(b)

If
(X,f)
is an expansive dynamical system, then
\rho(\hat{f}(u),\hat{f}(v))\geq\rho(u,v)
for all
u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
.

Proof.

(a): The case
\rho=d_{0}
was proved in
[
37
, Lemma 6]
. Thus, let
u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
and note that, by statement (a) of Proposition
2.1
, the definition of
d_{S}
and
d_{E}
, and their symmetry, it suffices to show that
\operatorname{send}(\hat{f}(u))\subset\operatorname{send}(\hat{f}(v))+\varepsilon
or that
\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u))\subset\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(v))+\varepsilon
whenever
\operatorname{send}(u)\subset\operatorname{send}(v)+\varepsilon
or
\operatorname{end}(u)\subset\operatorname{end}(v)+\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon>0
. We show the endograph case, the sendograph one being simpler, i.e. assume that
\operatorname{end}(u)\subset\operatorname{end}(v)+\varepsilon
. Let
(z,\alpha)\in\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u))\cap([\hat{f}(u)]_{0}\times\mathbb{I})=\operatorname{send}(\hat{f}(u))
, so that
z\in[\hat{f}(u)]_{\alpha}
. Then there exist
x\in u_{\alpha}
with
f(x)=z
, so that
(x,\alpha)\in\operatorname{end}(u)
and by assumption there is some
(y,\beta)\in\operatorname{end}(v)
with
\max\{d(x,y),|\alpha-\beta|\}=\overline{d}((x,\alpha),(y,\beta))\leq\varepsilon
. Since
(X,f)
is contractive,

\overline{d}((z,\alpha),(f(y),\beta))=\max\{d(f(x),f(y)),|\alpha-\beta|\}\leq\max\{d(x,y),|\alpha-\beta|\}\leq\varepsilon,

so that
(z,\alpha)\in\{(f(y),\beta)\}+\varepsilon\subset\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(v))+\varepsilon
. For the case
(z,\alpha)\in\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u))\setminus([\hat{f}(u)]_{0}\times\mathbb{I})
we have that
\alpha=0
and hence that
(z,\alpha)\in\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(v))\subset\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(v))+\varepsilon
, finishing the proof.

(b): This was proved in
[
18
, Propositions 3.3 and 3.4]
.
∎

We end Section
3
here, but contractive dynamical systems will be revisited in Subsection
5.2
in connection with the shadowing property, and Lemma
3.5
will be crucial (see Theorem
5.6
).

4
Chain recurrence, chain transitivity and chain mixing

In this section we study the notions of chain recurrence, chain transitivity and chain mixing. We begin by proving Theorem
4.1
, which asserts that each of these properties is presented by
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
if and only if
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
do as well. These equivalences are, to the best of our knowledge, completely new results. We then show that the system
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
is chain recurrent, chain transitive and chain mixing if and only if the underlying map
f:(X,d)\longrightarrow(X,d)
has dense range (see Theorem
4.3
). This exhibits, again, an extreme behaviour for the endograph metric
d_{E}
.

Along this section, given any system
(X,f)
on a metric space
(X,d)
and any positive integer
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we will denote by
f_{(N)}:X^{N}\longrightarrow X^{N}
the
N
-fold direct product
of
f
with itself. That is, the pair denoted by
(X^{N},f_{(N)})
will be the dynamical system

f_{(N)}:=\underbrace{f\times\cdots\times f}_{N}:\underbrace{X\times\cdots\times X}_{N}\longrightarrow\underbrace{X\times\cdots\times X}_{N},

where
X^{N}:=X\times\cdots\times X
is the
N
-fold direct product
of
X
, and where the respective
N
-fold direct map
is defined as
f_{(N)}\left((x_{1},...,x_{N})\right):=(f(x_{1}),...,f(x_{N}))
for each
N
-tuple
(x_{1},...,x_{N})\in X^{N}
. In addition, the space
X^{N}
will be endowed with the metric
d_{(N)}:X^{N}\times X^{N}\longrightarrow[0,\infty[
defined as

d_{(N)}\left((x_{1},...,x_{N}),(y_{1},...,y_{N})\right):=\max_{1\leq l\leq N}d(x_{l},y_{l})\quad\text{ for each pair }(x_{1},...,x_{N}),(y_{1},...,y_{N})\in X^{N}.

4.1
Chain-type properties for the supremum, Skorokhod and sendograph metrics

Let
f:X\longrightarrow X
be a continuous map acting on a metric space
(X,d)
. Then:

–

given a positive value
\delta>0
and a positive integer
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we say that a finite sequence
(x_{j})_{j=0}^{n}
of points in
X
is a
d
-
\delta
-chain
(from
x_{0}
to
x_{n}
, of length
n
, and for the map
f
) if we have that

d(f(x_{j}),x_{j+1})<\delta\quad\text{ for all }0\leq j\leq n-1;

–

a point
x\in X
is called
chain recurrent
for the map
f
if for each positive value
\delta>0
there exists a
d
-
\delta
-chain from
x
to itself for the map
f
; and the system
(X,f)
is said to be
chain recurrent
if every point of the underlying space
X
is chain recurrent for the map
f
;

–

the system
(X,f)
is called
chain transitive
if for each pair of points
x,y\in X
and each
\delta>0
there exists a
d
-
\delta
-chain from
x
to
y
for the map
f
;

–

the system
(X,f)
is called
chain weakly-mixing
if the product system
(X^{2},f_{(2)})
is chain transitive;

–

and the system
(X,f)
is called
chain mixing
if for each pair of points
x,y\in X
and each
\delta>0
there is a positive integer
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every integer
n\geq n_{0}
there exists a
d
-
\delta
-chain from
x
to
y
of length
n
for the map
f
.

From now on we will usually omit the words “
for the map
f
”. These properties generalize the notions of
topological recurrence
(or
non-wandering
, see
[
1
, Remark 2.1]
),
topological transitivity
,
topological weak-mixing
and
topological mixing
respectively (see
[
34
, Section 2]
). The “
\delta
-chains
” were introduced by Conley
[
10
]
, and then further developed by Conley
[
11
]
and Bowen
[
9
]
, together with the notion of
pseudo-trajectory
(see Section
5
below). Such concepts have had a great impact in the qualitative theory of Dynamical Systems, and they have recently been studied in Linear Dynamics (see
[
8
,
28
]
).

In the context of the compact and fuzzy extensions
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
, these chain-type properties have been considered in
[
2
,
13
,
24
]
, albeit under additional assumptions. In all three works, the underlying metric space
(X,d)
is assumed to be compact, a condition that is repeatedly used in their arguments. Moreover, in
[
24
]
, which is the only one of these works in which
\hat{f}
is considered, the dynamics of this map are studied in a space larger than that of normal fuzzy sets: they do not require their fuzzy sets to attain the value
1
and, consequently, their system can not be chain transitive nor chain mixing. As we work in general, not necessarily compact metric spaces, we will briefly reprove the implications established in
[
2
,
13
,
24
]
in the following theorem, which is the first main result of this section. To the best of our knowledge, the fuzzy part of our next result is completely new.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
f:X\longrightarrow X
be a continuous map on a metric space
(X,d)
. Then, the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

(X,f)
is chain recurrent (resp. chain weakly-mixing or chain mixing);

(ii)

(X^{N},f_{(N)})
is chain recurrent (resp. chain transitive or chain mixing) for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
;

(iii)

(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
is chain recurrent (resp. chain transitive or chain mixing);

(iv)

(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
is chain recurrent (resp. chain transitive or chain mixing);

(v)

(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
is chain recurrent (resp. chain transitive or chain mixing);

(vi)

(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
is chain recurrent (resp. chain transitive or chain mixing).

Before proving Theorem
4.1
let us mention that condition (i) can not be relaxed for the case of chain transitivity. In fact, let
X=\{a,b\}
with the discrete metric described in Example
3.4
, and let
f(a)=b
and
f(b)=a
. Thus, the dynamical system
(X,f)
is chain transitive, but for the map
\overline{f}
there is no
d_{H}
-
\delta
-chain in
\mathcal{K}(X)
from
\{a,b\}
to
\{a\}
whenever
0<\delta<1
. An example without isolated points is given in
[
34
, Example 2]
, but it is, in spirit, completely analogous to the example exhibited here.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii): Assume first that
(X,f)
is chain recurrent, let
N\in\mathbb{N}
, fix any point
(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{N})\in X^{N}
and consider any
\delta>0
. Thus, for each
1\leq l\leq N
there exists a
d
-
\delta
-chain from
x_{l}
to itself and of length
n_{l}\in\mathbb{N}
, denoted by
(x_{j}^{l})_{j=0}^{n_{l}}
. Letting
n:=\prod_{j=1}^{N}n_{j}
and concatenating the chain
(x_{j}^{l})_{j=0}^{n_{l}}
with itself
n/n_{l}
-times we get, for each
1\leq l\leq N
, a
d
-
\delta
-chain
(y_{j}^{l})_{j=0}^{n}
from
x_{l}
to itself and of length
n
. Thus, the sequence
((y^{1}_{j},y^{2}_{j},...,y^{N}_{j}))_{j=0}^{n}
is a
d_{(N)}
-
\delta
-chain from
(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{N})
to itself.

Let now
(X,f)
be chain weakly-mixing. It easily follows that
(X,f)
itself is chain transitive, so that the map
f:(X,d)\longrightarrow(X,d)
has dense range. Now we will complete the proof by induction: assume that
(X^{N},f_{(N)})
is chain transitive for some integer
N\geq 2
and let us prove that in this case the system
(X^{N+1},f_{(N+1)})
is also chain transitive. In fact, given two points
(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{N},x_{N+1})
and
(y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{N},y_{N+1})
in
X^{N+1}
and any
\delta>0
we can use that
(X^{N},f_{(N)})
is chain transitive to find two
d_{(N)}
-
\delta
-chains: the first one
((x^{1}_{j},x^{2}_{j},...,x^{N}_{j}))_{j=0}^{n_{1}}
from
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},...,x_{N})
to
(x_{1},x_{1},x_{3},...,x_{N})
, and the second one
((y^{1}_{j},y^{2}_{j},...,y^{N}_{j}))_{j=0}^{n_{2}}
from
(y_{1},y_{1},y_{3},...,y_{N})
to
(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},...,y_{N})
. Using that
f^{n_{2}}
has dense range because
f
does, we can then choose a point
z\in f^{-n_{2}}(\mathcal{B}_{d}(y_{N+1},\delta))
. Again, since
(X^{N},f_{(N)})
is chain transitive we can find a
d_{(N)}
-
\delta
-chain
((z^{1}_{j},z^{2}_{j},...,z^{N}_{j}))_{j=0}^{n_{3}}
from
(x_{1},x_{3},...,x_{N},f^{n_{1}}(x_{N+1}))
to
(y_{1},y_{3},...,y_{N},z)
. Concatenating the following finite sequences of
(N+1)
-tuples

((x^{1}_{j},x^{2}_{j},...,x^{N}_{j},f^{j}(x_{N+1})))_{j=0}^{n_{1}},\quad((z^{1}_{j},z^{1}_{j},z^{2}_{j},...,z^{N}_{j}))_{j=0}^{n_{3}},\quad\text{ and }((y^{1}_{j},y^{2}_{j},...,y^{N}_{j},f^{j}(z)))_{j=0}^{n_{2}},

we get a
d_{(N+1)}
-
\delta
-chain from
(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{N},x_{N+1})
to
(y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{N},f^{n_{2}}(z))
. Since
d(f^{n_{2}}(z),y_{N+1})<\delta
, replacing
f^{n_{2}}(z)
with
y_{N+1}
we get a
d_{(N+1)}
-
\delta
-chain from
(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{N},x_{N+1})
to
(y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{N},y_{N+1})
.

Finally, assume that
(X,f)
is chain mixing, let
N\in\mathbb{N}
, fix any pair of points
(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{N})
and
(y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{N})
in
X^{N}
and consider any
\delta>0
. Using the chain mixing condition on
x_{l}
and
y_{l}
for every
1\leq l\leq N
we can find some positive integer
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for any
n\geq n_{0}
and any
1\leq l\leq N
, there exists a
d
-
\delta
-chain
(x^{l}_{j})_{j=0}^{n}
from
x_{l}
to
y_{l}
of length
n
. Hence, the sequence
((x^{1}_{j},x^{2}_{j},...,x^{N}_{j}))_{j=0}^{n}
is a
d_{(N)}
-
\delta
-chain from
(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{N})
to

As we advanced at the Introduction and at the beginning of Section

Theorem 4.3

be a continuous map on a metric space

(iii)

d_{E}
-dense range by Lemma
4.4
, and let us prove the following strengthened version of (iv):

–

for each
\delta>0
there exists a positive integer
n_{\delta}\in\mathbb{N}
such that, given any pair of sets
u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
and any integer
n\geq 2n_{\delta}
, then there exists a
d_{E}
-
\delta
-chain from
u
to
v
of length
n
for the map
\hat{f}
.

To do so fix any
\delta>0
and let
n_{\delta}:=\lfloor\tfrac{1}{\delta}\rfloor+1
, where
\lfloor\tfrac{1}{\delta}\rfloor
denotes the floor of
\tfrac{1}{\delta}
. In addition, let us fix the positive value
\varepsilon:=\tfrac{1}{n_{\delta}}
, which clearly fulfills that
0<\varepsilon<\delta
. Now, given any pair of arbitrary but fixed normal fuzzy sets
u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
we can:

–

fix a point
x\in u_{1}
, which exists because
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
and hence
u_{1}=u^{-1}(\{1\})\neq\varnothing
;

–

and fixing a fuzzy set
w\in\mathcal{F}(X)
fulfilling that
\hat{f}^{n}(w)\in\mathcal{B}_{E}(v,\delta-\varepsilon)
, which exists since the map
\hat{f}^{n}
has
d_{E}
-dense range because
\hat{f}
does by assumption and Lemma
4.4
.

Then, consider the finite sequence of functions
(u^{j}:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{I})_{j=0}^{n}
formed by

u^{j}:=\max\{\chi_{\{f^{j}(x)\}},(1-j\varepsilon)\cdot\hat{f}^{j}(u),j\varepsilon\cdot\hat{f}^{j}(w)\}\quad\text{ for each }0\leq j\leq n_{\delta},

u^{j}:=\max\{\hat{f}^{j}(w),(1-(j-n_{\delta})\varepsilon)\cdot\chi_{\{f^{j}(x)\}}\}\quad\text{ for each }n_{\delta}+1\leq j\leq 2n_{\delta},

and

u^{j}:=\hat{f}^{j}(w)\quad\text{ for each }2n_{\delta}+1\leq j\leq n\text{ whenever }n>2n_{\delta}.

We start by noticing that all these functions are normal fuzzy sets: actually, the maximum of finitely many upper-semicontinuous functions is again an upper-semicontinuous function; all of these functions clearly have compact support; and these functions attain the value
1
by definition. Now, given any integer
0\leq j\leq n-1
we will check that
d_{E}(\hat{f}(u^{j}),u^{j+1})=\overline{d}_{H}(\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u^{j}),\operatorname{end}(u^{j+1}))\leq\varepsilon<\delta
. In fact:

–

Case 1
:
When
0\leq j\leq n_{\delta}-1
. In this case the reader can easily check that

\hat{f}(u^{j})=\max\{\chi_{\{f^{j+1}(x)\}},(1-j\varepsilon)\cdot\hat{f}^{j+1}(u),j\varepsilon\cdot\hat{f}^{j+1}(w)\}.

Thus, if for each
\beta\in[0,1]
we write

\displaystyle U_{\beta}

\displaystyle:=\{(t,\alpha)\in X\times\mathbb{I}\ ;\ \beta\cdot[\hat{f}^{j+1}(u)](t)\geq\alpha\},

\displaystyle W_{\beta}

\displaystyle:=\{(t,\alpha)\in X\times\mathbb{I}\ ;\ \beta\cdot[\hat{f}^{j+1}(w)](t)\geq\alpha\},

we than have that

\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u^{j}))=\{f^{j+1}(x)\}\times\mathbb{I}\ \cup\ U_{1-j\varepsilon}\ \cup\ W_{j\varepsilon},

while

\operatorname{end}(u^{j+1})=\{f^{j+1}(x)\}\times\mathbb{I}\ \cup\ U_{1-(j+1)\varepsilon}\ \cup\ W_{(j+1)\varepsilon}.

Since in the space of closed sets
\mathcal{C}(X\times\mathbb{I})
with the distance
\overline{d}_{H}
one can trivially check that

U_{1-(j+1)\varepsilon}\subset U_{1-j\varepsilon}\subset U_{1-(j+1)\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\quad\text{ and that }\quad W_{j\varepsilon}\subset W_{(j+1)\varepsilon}\subset W_{j\varepsilon}+\varepsilon,

it follows that
\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u^{j}))\subset\operatorname{end}(u^{j+1})+\varepsilon
but also that
\operatorname{end}(u^{j+1})\subset\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u^{j}))+\varepsilon
. Hence, using statement (a) of Proposition
2.1
we get that
d_{E}(\hat{f}(u^{j}),u^{j+1})=\overline{d}_{H}(\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u^{j}),\operatorname{end}(u^{j+1}))\leq\varepsilon<\delta
.

–

Case 2
:
When
n_{\delta}\leq j\leq 2n_{\delta}-1
. In this case the reader can easily check that

\hat{f}(u^{j})=\max\{\hat{f}^{j+1}(w),(1-(j-n_{\delta})\varepsilon)\cdot\chi_{\{f^{j+1}(x)\}}\}.

Thus, we have that

\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u^{j}))=\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{j+1}(w))\ \cup\ \{f^{j+1}(x)\}\times[0,1-(j-n_{\delta})\varepsilon],

while

\operatorname{end}(u^{j+1})=\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{j+1}(w))\ \cup\ \{f^{j+1}(x)\}\times[0,1-((j+1)-n_{\delta})\varepsilon].

Note that
\operatorname{end}(u^{j+1})\subset\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u^{j}))
. Moreover, since in the space of closed sets
\mathcal{C}(X\times\mathbb{I})
with the distance
\overline{d}_{H}
one can trivially check that

\{f^{j+1}(x)\}\times[0,1-(j-n_{\delta})\varepsilon]\subset\{f^{j+1}(x)\}\times[0,1-((j+1)-n_{\delta})\varepsilon]+\varepsilon,

it follows that
\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u^{j}))\subset\operatorname{end}(u^{j+1})+\varepsilon
. Hence, using statement (a) of Proposition
2.1
we obtain that
d_{E}(\hat{f}(u^{j}),u^{j+1})=\overline{d}_{H}(\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u^{j}),\operatorname{end}(u^{j+1}))\leq\varepsilon<\delta
.

–

Case 3
:
When
n>2n_{\delta}
and
2n_{\delta}\leq j<n-1
. This case is completely trivial since we have the equalities
\hat{f}(u^{j})=\hat{f}^{j+1}(w)=u^{j+1}
, so that
d_{E}(\hat{f}(u^{j}),u^{j+1})=0\leq\varepsilon<\delta
.

Since
u^{0}=u
and
u^{n}=\hat{f}^{n}(w)
, we have thus constructed a
d_{E}
-
\delta
-chain
(u^{j})_{j=0}^{n}
from
u
to
\hat{f}^{n}(w)
of length
n
fulfilling that
d_{E}(\hat{f}(u^{n-1}),u^{n})\leq\varepsilon
. Hence, since
d_{E}(\hat{f}^{n}(w),v)<\delta-\varepsilon
we have that

d_{E}(\hat{f}(u^{n-1}),v)\leq d_{E}(\hat{f}(u^{n-1}),u^{n})+d_{E}(u^{n},v)\leq\varepsilon+d_{E}(\hat{f}^{n}(w),v)<\delta,

and replacing
u^{n}
with
v
in
(u^{j})_{j=0}^{n}
we obtain a
d_{E}
-
\delta
-chain from
u
to
v
of length
n
for the map
\hat{f}
.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

Let us continue the comparison of “
chain recurrence
” with “
topological recurrence
”, of the notion of “
chain transitivity
” with that of “
topological transitivity
” and of the property of being “
chain mixing
” with that of being “
topologically mixing
” mentioned at Subsection
4.1
. Actually, the reader may find interesting to compare Theorems
4.1
and
4.3
, that we have proved for the respective chain-type properties, with
[
27
, Theorems 3.1 and 3.3]
, where it was showed that the system
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
is topologically recurrent, topologically transitive or topologically mixing respectively, if and only if so do
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
. These results provide, once again, an example of how the endograph metric can behave in an extremely radical way: while it aligns well with the topological notions, its behaviour changes dramatically for chains and
\delta
-perturbations.

5
The shadowing property

In this section we focus on the shadowing and finite shadowing properties. These dynamical notions were considered in
[
5
]
for the fuzzy dynamical systems
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
. However, their main result on shadowing
[
5
, Theorem 5]
is not completely valid. We therefore have divided this section into two parts: first we provide two counterexamples together with a corrected version of the statement of such a result (see Examples
5.1
and
5.2
and Theorem
5.3
); and then we prove that
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
can not have the finite shadowing property when the map
f
has dense range and
(X,f)
is not topologically mixing, but that
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
has the full shadowing property when
(X,f)
is contractive and
f^{k}(X)
is bounded for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
(see Theorems
5.4
and
5.6
). The reader may observe along this section that, as it happens in Section
3
with the contractive and expansive notions, the metrics
d_{0}
and
d_{S}
together with their respective systems
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
exhibit, for the shadowing property, a behaviour similar to that of
d_{E}
and its corresponding dynamical system
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
.

5.1
Definitions, counterexamples and equivalences

Shadowing forms part of the so-called “orbit tracing properties” in Topological Dynamics. In fact, this dynamical notion was motivated by questions such as if an approximate trajectory of a dynamical system can be fitted or not by a real trajectory of the system. Thus, let us start by recalling what an “approximate trajectory” is. Given a continuous map
f:X\longrightarrow X
acting on a metric space
(X,d)
, two positive numbers
\varepsilon,\delta>0
, and an infinite sequence
(x_{j})_{j=0}^{\infty}
in
X
, we say that:

–

the sequence
(x_{j})_{j=0}^{\infty}
is a
d
-
\delta
-pseudo-trajectory
for the map
f
if

d(f(x_{j}),x_{j+1})<\delta\quad\text{ for all }0\leq j<\infty;

–

the sequence
(x_{j})_{j=0}^{\infty}
is
d
-
\varepsilon
-shadowed
by the
f
-orbit of some point
x\in X
if

d(f^{j}(x),x_{j})<\varepsilon\quad\text{ for all }0\leq j<\infty.

A
\delta
-pseudo-trajectory
can be seen as an infinite
\delta
-chain
from those considered in Section
4
, and we will say that a finite sequence
(x_{j})_{j=0}^{n}
of points in
X
is
d
-
\varepsilon
-shadowed
by the
f
-orbit of some point
x\in X
whenever
d(f^{j}(x),x_{j})<\varepsilon
for all
0\leq j\leq n
. Following
[
5
,
12
,
14
]
we will say that:

–

a dynamical system
(X,f)
on a metric space
(X,d)
has the (resp.
finite
)
shadowing property
if for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists some
\delta_{\varepsilon}>0
such that each
d
-
\delta_{\varepsilon}
-pseudo-trajectory (resp.
d
-
\delta_{\varepsilon}
-chain) for the map
f
is
d
-
\varepsilon
-shadowed by the
f
-orbit of some point in
X
.

The reader should note that every dynamical system with the “full” shadowing property has the finite shadowing property. The converse does not hold in general (see
[
8
, Section 2]
), but it does for compact metric spaces (see
[
6
, Remark 1]
). Moreover, it was proved in
[
12
, Theorem 3.4]
that
when
f:X\longrightarrow X
is a continuous map on a
compact
metric space
(X,d)
, then the system
(X,f)
has the shadowing property if and only if so does
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
. If the
compactness
assumption is dropped, then one still gets the equivalence for the
finite shadowing property
. In fact, the proof of
[
12
, Theorem 3.4]
follows from three results: first we have
[
12
, Lemma 3.1]
, where compactness can be dropped by properly using continuity rather than uniform continuity; and then we have
[
12
, Theorems 3.2 and 3.3]
, which remain valid exactly with the same proof when “shadowing” is replaced with “finite shadowing”.

For the fuzzy extension
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
, it was claimed in
[
5
, Theorem 5]
that
(X,f)
and
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
have the finite shadowing property if and only if the systems
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
do as well. As we shall reformulate in Theorem
5.3
below, this assertion is only partially correct: while the equivalence holds for
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
, it fails for
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
, as the following counterexamples exhibit.

Example 5.1

(
A discrete counterexample
)
.

There exists a system
(X,f)
having the shadowing property for which
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
do not have the finite shadowing property.

Proof.

Let
X=\{a,b\}
with the discrete metric described in Example
3.4
, and let
f:X\longrightarrow X
be the identity map, that is,
f(a)=a
and
f(b)=b
. It is clear that
(X,f)
has the shadowing property by taking
\delta_{\varepsilon}:=\varepsilon
for any
\varepsilon>0
. Let us now prove that there is an
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for any positive value
\delta>0
there exists a
d_{0}
-
\delta
-chain that is not
d_{E}
-
\varepsilon_{0}
-shadowed. This fact will be enough to conclude the counterexample: actually, since the inequalities
d_{E}(u,v)\leq d_{S}(u,v)\leq d_{0}(u,v)
hold for every pair
u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
as stated in Proposition
2.4
, we then have that every
d_{0}
-
\delta
-chain is also a
d_{S}
-
\delta
-chain and hence a
d_{E}
-
\delta
-chain, but also if a finite sequence can not be
d_{E}
-
\varepsilon_{0}
-shadowed by an
\hat{f}
-orbit in
\mathcal{F}(X)
then such a finite sequence can not be
d_{S}
-
\varepsilon_{0}
-shadowed, nor
d_{0}
-
\varepsilon_{0}
-shadowed, by an
\hat{f}
-orbit in
\mathcal{F}(X)
.

We start by fixing any positive value
0<\varepsilon_{0}<\tfrac{1}{4}
and any
\delta>0
. Now, let
k\in 2\mathbb{N}
be an even integer fulfilling that
\tfrac{1}{k}<\min\{\delta,\tfrac{1}{4}-\varepsilon_{0}\}
, let
n=\tfrac{k}{2}-1
and consider the sequence
(u^{j})_{j=0}^{n}
of length
n
with

u^{j}:=\chi_{\{a\}}+(\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{j}{k})\chi_{\{b\}}\quad\text{ for each }0\leq j\leq n.

Note that we have the equalities
u^{0}=\chi_{\{a\}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\chi_{\{b\}}
and
u^{n}=\chi_{\{a\}}+(1-\tfrac{1}{k})\chi_{\{b\}}
. Let us now check:

–

Fact 1
:
The sequence
(u^{j})_{j=0}^{n}
is a
d_{0}
-
\delta
-chain from
u^{0}
to
u^{n}
. Since
\hat{f}
we is the identity map in
\mathcal{F}(X)
, we only have to check that
d_{0}(u^{j},u^{j+1})<\delta
for all
0\leq j\leq n-1
. We argue as in Lemma
3.3
: fix any
0\leq j\leq n-1
and consider the 2-piecewise-linear map

\xi_{j}(\alpha):=\begin{cases}\tfrac{k+2j}{k+2(j+1)}\alpha&\text{ if }0\leq\alpha\leq\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{j+1}{k},\\[5.0pt]
\tfrac{k-2j}{k-2(j+1)}\alpha-\tfrac{2}{k-2(j+1)}&\text{ if }\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{j+1}{k}<\alpha\leq 1,\end{cases}

(5.1)

which fulfills that
\xi_{j}(0)=0
, that
\xi_{j}(\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{j+1}{k})=\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{j}{k}
and that
\xi_{j}(1)=1
. It is not hard to see that

\sup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{I}}|\xi_{j}(\alpha)-\alpha|=\left|\xi_{j}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{j+1}{k}\right)-\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{j+1}{k}\right)\right|=\left|\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{j}{k}\right)-\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{j+1}{k}\right)\right|=\tfrac{1}{k},

(5.2)

and that
\xi_{k}^{-1}(\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{j}{k})=\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{j+1}{k}
. It follows that

\left[\xi_{j}\circ u^{j+1}\right]_{\alpha}=\left[u^{j+1}\right]_{\xi_{j}^{-1}(\alpha)}=\begin{cases}\{a,b\}&\text{ if }0\leq\alpha\leq\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{j}{k},\\[5.0pt]
\{a\}&\text{ if }\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{j}{k}<\alpha\leq 1.\end{cases}

This implies that
u^{j}=\xi_{j}\circ u^{j+1}
and hence
d_{\infty}(u^{j},\xi_{j}\circ u^{j+1})=0
. We finally deduce that

d_{0}(u^{j},u^{j+1})\leq\max\left\{\sup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{I}}|\xi_{j}(\alpha)-\alpha|\ ,\ d_{\infty}(u^{j},\xi_{j}\circ u^{j+1})\right\}\overset{\eqref{eq:xi_j=1/k}}{=}\tfrac{1}{k}<\delta.

–

Fact 2
:
The
d_{0}
-
\delta
-chain
(u^{j})_{j=0}^{n}
is not
d_{E}
-
\varepsilon_{0}
-shadowed by any
\hat{f}
-orbit in
\mathcal{F}(X)
. By contradiction, assume that such a sequence is
d_{E}
-
\varepsilon_{0}
-shadowed by the orbit of some
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
, which means that

d_{E}(u,u^{j})=d_{E}(\hat{f}^{j}(u),u^{j})<\varepsilon_{0}\quad\text{ for all }0\leq j\leq n,

because
\hat{f}
is the identity map. Hence, from the fact that
\overline{d}_{H}(\operatorname{end}(u),\operatorname{end}(u^{0}))=d_{E}(u,u^{0})<\varepsilon_{0}<\tfrac{1}{4}
we have that
\operatorname{end}(u)\subset\operatorname{end}(u^{0})+\varepsilon_{0}\subset\operatorname{end}(u^{0})+\tfrac{1}{4}
, where

\operatorname{end}(u^{0})+\tfrac{1}{4}=\{a\}\times[0,1]\ \cup\ \{b\}\times[0,\tfrac{3}{4}].

This necessarily implies that
u(a)=1
and that
u(b)\in[0,\tfrac{3}{4}]
. Hence, although we also have that
\overline{d}_{H}(\operatorname{end}(u),\operatorname{end}(u^{n}))=d_{E}(u,u^{n})<\varepsilon_{0}
, we can now check that
\operatorname{end}(u^{n})\not\subset\operatorname{end}(u)+\varepsilon_{0}
, reaching a contradiction since then
\overline{d}_{H}(\operatorname{end}(u),\operatorname{end}(u^{n}))>\varepsilon_{0}
by statement (a) of Proposition
2.1
. In fact, note that the point
(b,1-\tfrac{1}{k})\in\operatorname{end}(u^{n})
fulfills that

\inf_{(x,\alpha)\in\operatorname{end}(u)}\overline{d}((b,1-\tfrac{1}{k}),(x,\alpha))\geq\inf_{(x,\alpha)\in\operatorname{end}(u^{0})+\tfrac{1}{4}}\max\{d(b,x),|(1-\tfrac{1}{k})-\alpha|\},

and for each
(x,\alpha)\in\operatorname{end}(u^{0})+\tfrac{1}{4}
we have that

\displaystyle\max\{d(b,x),|(1-\tfrac{1}{k})-\alpha|\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\max\{d(b,a),|(1-\tfrac{1}{k})-\alpha|\}&\text{ if }0\leq\alpha\leq 1\text{ and }x=a,\\[5.0pt]
\max\{d(b,b),|(1-\tfrac{1}{k})-\alpha|\}&\text{ if }0\leq\alpha\leq\tfrac{3}{4}\text{ and }x=b,\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\text{ if }0\leq\alpha\leq 1\text{ and }x=a,\\[5.0pt]
|(1-\tfrac{1}{k})-\tfrac{3}{4}|&\text{ if }0\leq\alpha\leq\tfrac{3}{4}\text{ and }x=b,\end{array}\right\}\geq\tfrac{1}{4}-\tfrac{1}{k}>\varepsilon_{0}.\qed

The idea behind Example
5.1
, and the reason why we have taken the identity map, was to keep the arguments as simple as possible so that the reader can easily see why the shadowing property fails for the metrics
d_{0}
,
d_{S}
and
d_{E}
. In fact, the construction of the system in Example
5.1
admits several variations: one may take
f:\{a,b\}\longrightarrow\{a,b\}
with
f(a)=b
and
f(b)=a
, but one could also consider a discrete space with
N\geq 3
points,
X=\{a_{1},a_{2},...,a_{N}\}
, together with the map
f(a_{j})=a_{j+1\ (\text{mod }N)}
, among other plausible choices. In all these cases the same arguments apply. Since the reader might suspect that this phenomenon relies on the discreteness of the underlying metric space, we also provide a counterexample on a connected space, where completely analogous arguments hold.

Example 5.2

(
A connected counterexample
)
.

There exists a contractive dynamical system
(X,f)
for which
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
do not have the finite shadowing property.

Proof.

Consider the set of real numbers
X=\mathbb{R}
with the usual metric
d(x,y)=|x-y|
for each pair of points
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
f:X\longrightarrow X
be the contractive linear map with
f(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}x
for each
x\in\mathbb{R}
. It is a classical fact that the dynamical system
(X,f)
has the shadowing property. One way to see this is by invoking the Linear Shadowing Lemma, which states that every hyperbolic invertible operator on a Banach space has the shadowing property (see
[
31
]
). Another approach is noticing that every contractive dynamical system has the shadowing property (see Proposition
5.5
). As in Example
5.1
we are going to prove that there is an
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for any
\delta>0
there exists a
d_{0}
-
\delta
-chain that is not
d_{E}
-
\varepsilon_{0}
-shadowed, which will complete the counterexample. To do so, we again fix any positive value
0<\varepsilon_{0}<\tfrac{1}{4}
and any
\delta>0
. Now, let
k\in 2\mathbb{N}
be an even integer fulfilling that
\tfrac{1}{k}<\min\{\delta,\tfrac{1}{4}-\varepsilon_{0}\}
, let
n=\tfrac{k}{2}-1
and consider the sequence
(u^{j})_{j=0}^{n}
of length
n
with

u^{j}:=\chi_{\{0\}}+(\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{j}{k})\chi_{\{2^{n-j}\}}\quad\text{ for each }0\leq j\leq n.

It can be checked that we have the equalities
u^{0}=\chi_{\{0\}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\chi_{\{2^{n}\}}
,
\hat{f}(u^{j})=\chi_{\{0\}}+(\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{j}{k})\chi_{\{2^{n-(j+1)}\}}
for each
0\leq j\leq n-1
, and
u^{n}=\chi_{\{0\}}+(1-\tfrac{1}{k})\chi_{\{1\}}
. Let us now check:

–

Fact 1
:
The sequence
(u^{j})_{j=0}^{n}
is a
d_{0}
-
\delta
-chain from
u^{0}
to
u^{n}
. Actually, arguing as in Example
5.1
, fix any
0\leq j\leq n-1
and consider the 2-piecewise-linear map
\xi_{j}
as defined in (
5.1
). It follows that

\left[\xi_{j}\circ u^{j+1}\right]_{\alpha}=\left[u^{j+1}\right]_{\xi_{j}^{-1}(\alpha)}=\begin{cases}\{0,2^{n-(j+1)}\}&\text{ if }0\leq\alpha\leq\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{j}{k},\\[5.0pt]
\{0\}&\text{ if }\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{j}{k}<\alpha\leq 1.\end{cases}

Thus,
\hat{f}(u^{j})=\xi_{j}\circ u^{j+1}
so that
d_{0}(\hat{f}(u^{j}),u^{j+1})\leq\tfrac{1}{k}<\delta
by (
5.2
), exactly as in Example
5.1
.

–

Fact 2
:
The
d_{0}
-
\delta
-chain
(u^{j})_{j=0}^{n}
is not
d_{E}
-
\varepsilon_{0}
-shadowed by any
\hat{f}
-orbit in
\mathcal{F}(X)
. By contradiction, assume that there exists some
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
fulfilling that
d_{E}(\hat{f}^{j}(u),u^{j})<\varepsilon_{0}
for all
0\leq j\leq n
. Hence, since
\overline{d}_{H}(\operatorname{end}(u),\operatorname{end}(u^{0}))=d_{E}(u,u^{0})<\varepsilon_{0}<\tfrac{1}{4}
we have that

\operatorname{end}(u)\subset\operatorname{end}(u^{0})+\tfrac{1}{4}=[-\tfrac{1}{4},\tfrac{1}{4}]\times[0,1]\ \cup\ [2^{n}-\tfrac{1}{4},2^{n}+\tfrac{1}{4}]\times[0,\tfrac{3}{4}]\ \cup\ \mathbb{R}\times[0,\tfrac{1}{4}].

Applying
n
times the map
\hat{f}
it is not hard to check that then

\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{n}(u))\subset[-\tfrac{1}{4\cdot 2^{n}},\tfrac{1}{4\cdot 2^{n}}]\times[0,1]\ \cup\ [1-\tfrac{1}{4\cdot 2^{n}},1+\tfrac{1}{4\cdot 2^{n}}]\times[0,\tfrac{3}{4}]\ \cup\ \mathbb{R}\times[0,\tfrac{1}{4}].

(5.3)

Hence, although we also have that
\overline{d}_{H}(\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{n}(u)),\operatorname{end}(u^{n}))=d_{E}(\hat{f}^{n}(u),u^{n})<\varepsilon_{0}
, we can now check that
\operatorname{end}(u^{n})\not\subset\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{n}(u))+\varepsilon_{0}
, reaching a contradiction with statement (a) of Proposition
2.1
. In fact, using that
\varepsilon_{0}<\tfrac{1}{4}-\tfrac{1}{k}<\tfrac{1}{4}
by assumption and hence that
\tfrac{1}{4\cdot 2^{n}}+\varepsilon_{0}<\tfrac{1}{2}
, the point
(1,1-\tfrac{1}{k})
belongs to the set
\operatorname{end}(u^{n})
while by (
5.3
) we have that
(1,1-\tfrac{1}{k})\notin\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{n}(u))
because

\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{n}(u))+\varepsilon_{0}\subset[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}]\times[0,1]\ \cup\ [\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{3}{2}]\times[0,1-\tfrac{1}{k}[\ \cup\ \mathbb{R}\times[0,\tfrac{1}{2}].\qed

In Example
5.2
we could equally have considered the expansive map
f(x)=2x
on the same metric space
(\mathbb{R},d)
, which also provides a counterexample where similar arguments apply (this system also has the shadowing property by the Linear Shadowing Lemma
[
31
]
). Nevertheless, we chose to work with the contractive map
f(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}x
, since contractions will play a central role in Subsection
5.2
below. Having established the previous counterexamples we are now in a position to adapt the ideas originally employed in
[
5
, Theorem 5]
, and we can formulate the next corrected version of it:

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
f:X\longrightarrow X
be a continuous map on a metric space
(X,d)
. Hence:

(a)

The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

(X,f)
has the finite shadowing property;

(ii)

(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
has the finite shadowing property;

(iii)

(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
has the finite shadowing property.

Moreover, if
(X,d)
is a compact metric space, then statements (i) and (ii) can be replaced by:

(i’)

(X,f)
has the shadowing property;

(ii’)

(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
has the shadowing property.

(b)

If the system
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
has the shadowing property, the so does the system
(X,f)
.

(c)

If any of the systems
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
or
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
has the (resp. finite) shadowing property, then so does the system
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
.

Proof.

(a): The equivalence (i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii) follows from the arguments exhibited in
[
12
, Theorem 3.4]
, even if
(X,d)
is not a compact space (see the comments at the beginning of Subsection
5.1
). For the particular case in which
(X,d)
is compact, then
(\mathcal{K}(X),d_{H})
is compact by
[
17
, Theorem 3.5]
and the equivalences of statements (i)-(ii) with (i’)-(ii’) follow from
[
6
, Remark 1]
. The implication (ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii) was proved in
[
5
, Theorem 5]
for the case in which
(X,d)
is a compact metric space, but their proof does not use compactness. We prove the implication (iii)
\Rightarrow
(ii) in part (c) of this theorem.

(b): This was proved in
[
12
, Theorem 3.2]
for the case in which
(X,d)
is compact, but without using compactness. Thus, the proof given there is valid to conclude our statement.

(c): Fix
\rho\in\{d_{\infty},d_{0},d_{S},d_{E}\}
and assume that
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
has the (resp. finite) shadowing property when
\mathcal{F}(X)
is endowed with
\rho
. Thus, given
\varepsilon>0
, which can be assumed to fulfill that
0<\varepsilon\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
without loss of generality, let
\delta_{\varepsilon}>0
such that every finite
\rho
-
\delta_{\varepsilon}
-chain for
\hat{f}
can be
\rho
-
\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}
-shadowed by the
\hat{f}
-orbit of some fuzzy set in
\mathcal{F}(X)
. Consider now a
d_{H}
-
\delta_{\varepsilon}
-pseudo-trajectory (resp.
d_{H}
-
\delta_{\varepsilon}
-chain) of compact sets
(K_{j})_{j=0}^{n}
with
n=\infty
(resp.
n\in\mathbb{N}
). Letting
u^{j}:=\chi_{K_{j}}
for each possible
j
we deduce that
(u^{j})_{j=0}^{n}
is a
\rho
-
\delta_{\varepsilon}
-pseudo-trajectory (resp.
\rho
-
\delta_{\varepsilon}
-chain). In fact, by Proposition
2.4
for
d_{\infty}
,

\rho(\hat{f}(u^{j}),u^{j+1})\leq d_{\infty}(\hat{f}(u^{j}),u^{j+1})=d_{H}(\overline{f}(K_{j}),K_{j+1})<\delta_{\varepsilon}\quad\text{ for all }0\leq j<n.

By assumption there exists
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
such that
\rho(\hat{f}^{j}(u),u^{j})<\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}<\varepsilon\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
for all possible
j
. Using again Proposition
2.4
, but this time for
d_{E}
, we have that

\eta:=\sup_{j}d_{E}(\hat{f}^{j}(u),u^{j})\leq\sup_{j}\rho(\hat{f}^{j}(u),u^{j})\leq\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}<\varepsilon\leq\tfrac{1}{2}.

Picking now any
\alpha\in\ ]\eta,1-\eta]
, an application of Lemma
2.5
shows that

d_{H}(\overline{f}^{j}(u_{\alpha}),K_{j})=d_{H}([\hat{f}^{j}(u)]_{\alpha},K_{j})\leq\eta<\varepsilon\quad\text{ for every possible }j.

We deduce that
(K_{j})_{j=0}^{n}
is
d_{H}
-
\varepsilon
-shadowed by the
\overline{f}
-orbit of
u_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{K}(X)
, which completes the proof.
∎

5.2
Shadowing for the Skorokhod, sendograph and endograph metrics

In view of Theorem
3.1
and given that the arguments employed in Lemma
3.3
closely parallel those used in Examples
5.1
and
5.2
, it is natural to ask whether
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
or
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
can exhibit the shadowing property whenever the set
X
contains more than one point and the map
f
is non-constant. We begin with a negative result for the endograph metric, derived from the theory developed in Section
4
. Recall that a system
(X,f)
is called
topologically mixing
if for any pair of non-empty open subsets
U,V\subset X
there exists some
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{n}(U)\cup V\neq\varnothing
for all
n\geq n_{0}
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
f:X\longrightarrow X
be a continuous map on a metric space
(X,d)
and assume that the map
f
has dense range in
(X,d)
. If the dynamical system
(X,f)
is not topologically mixing, then the extended dynamical system
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
can not have the finite shadowing property.

Proof.

Assume that
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
has the finite shadowing property and let us prove that then
(X,f)
is topologically mixing. By
[
27
, Theorem 3.1]
this is equivalent to prove that
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
is topologically mixing. Moreover, since every
d_{E}
-open set contains an open
d_{E}
-ball, it is enough to prove that for any pair
u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
and any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
fulfilling that

\hat{f}^{n}(\mathcal{B}_{E}(u,\varepsilon))\cap\mathcal{B}_{E}(v,\varepsilon)\neq\varnothing\quad\text{ for all }n\geq n_{0}.

Actually, let
\delta_{\varepsilon}>0
so that every
d_{E}
-
\delta_{\varepsilon}
-chain is
d_{E}
-
\varepsilon
-shadowed by some
\hat{f}
-orbit in
\mathcal{F}(X)
. Since the map
f:(X,d)\longrightarrow(X,d)
has dense range, Theorem
4.3
shows that
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
is chain mixing. Thus, there is a positive integer
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
n\geq n_{0}
there exists a
d_{E}
-
\delta
-chain
(u^{j,n})_{j=0}^{n}
from
u
to
v
of length
n
. By assumption, for each
n\geq n_{0}
there exists a fuzzy set
w^{n}\in\mathcal{F}(X)
fulfilling that

d_{E}(\hat{f}^{j}(w^{n}),u^{j,n})<\varepsilon\quad\text{ for all }0\leq j\leq n.

Thus,
d_{E}(w^{n},u)=d_{E}(\hat{f}^{0}(w^{n}),u^{0,n})<\varepsilon
,
d_{E}(\hat{f}^{n}(w^{n}),v)=d_{E}(\hat{f}^{n}(w^{n}),u^{n,n})<\varepsilon
and hence

\hat{f}^{n}(w^{n})\in\hat{f}^{n}(\mathcal{B}_{E}(u,\varepsilon))\cap\mathcal{B}_{E}(v,\varepsilon)\quad\text{ for each }n\geq n_{0}.\qed

We can use Theorem
5.4
to reprove that
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
does not have the finite shadowing property for the systems
(X,f)
considered in Examples
5.1
and
5.2
, but also for the expansive map
f(x)=2x
defined in
X=\mathbb{R}
with the usual
|\cdot|
-metric: the maps of these systems have dense range but they are not topologically mixing. However, the systems
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
will have the shadowing property whenever
(X,f)
is “contractive enough” (see Theorem
5.6
below). We start by recalling why contractions have the shadowing property:

Proposition 5.5
.

If a dynamical system
(X,f)
, or any of its extensions
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
or
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
, is contractive, then
(X,f)
,
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
have the shadowing property.

Proof.

It is folklore that if
(X,f)
is a contractive dynamical system for
\lambda\in[0,1[
, then
(X,f)
has the shadowing property by taking
\delta_{\varepsilon}:=(1-\lambda)\varepsilon
for each
\varepsilon>0
. For the sake of completeness we will include its brief proof. Actually, given any
d
-
\delta_{\varepsilon}
-pseudo-trajectory
(x_{j})_{j=0}^{\infty}
one can consider the point
x:=x_{0}
, so that we have
d(f^{0}(x),x_{0})=d(x_{0},x_{0})=0=(1-\lambda^{0})\varepsilon
. Arguing inductively, if we now assume that
d(f^{j}(x),x_{j})<(1-\lambda^{j})\varepsilon
for some non-negative integer
j\geq 0
, then we have that

\displaystyle d(f^{j+1}(x),x_{j+1})

\displaystyle\leq d(f^{j+1}(x),f(x_{j}))+d(f(x_{j}),x_{j+1})\leq\lambda d(f^{j}(x),x_{j})+\delta_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda(1-\lambda^{j})\varepsilon+(1-\lambda)\varepsilon=(1-\lambda^{j+1})\varepsilon<\varepsilon.

The whole result follows from the fact that
(X,f)
is contractive if and only if so is
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
, but also if and only if so is
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
, as proved in
[
4
, Section III.7]
and
[
20
, Proposition 5.7]
.
∎

Since the system
(X,f)
considered in Example
5.2
is contractive, it is clear that Proposition
5.5
can not be obtained for
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
in general. Adding a particular boundedness assumption we reach our desired result:

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
f:X\longrightarrow X
be a continuous map acting on a metric space
(X,d)
. If
(X,f)
is a contractive system and the set
f^{k}(X)
is bounded in
(X,d)
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, then the extended dynamical systems
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
have the shadowing property. In particular, the previous conclusion holds whenever
(X,f)
is a contractive system and
(X,d)
is bounded itself.

Proof.

Assume first that
(X,d)
is a complete space. Thus, the Banach fixed-point theorem tells us that there exists a unique point, namely
x^{*}\in X
, fulfilling that
f(x^{*})=x^{*}
. Let now
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary but fixed. Since the system
(X,f)
is contractive and
f(x^{*})=x^{*}
, we can easily find two small enough positive values
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}>0
fulfilling that
0<\varepsilon_{2}\leq\varepsilon_{1}<\tfrac{\varepsilon}{4}
but also that

f(\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}))\subset\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1}).

(5.4)

Using again that
(X,f)
is contractive, that
f(x^{*})=x^{*}
, and this time that
f^{k}(X)
is bounded for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, i.e. that
\operatorname{diam}_{d}(f^{k}(X))<\infty
, we can easily find some positive integer
N\in\mathbb{N}
fulfilling that

f^{j}(X)\subset\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1})\quad\text{ for all }j\geq N.

(5.5)

Let
\rho\in\{d_{0},d_{S},d_{E}\}
, fix
\delta_{\varepsilon}:=\tfrac{\varepsilon_{2}}{N}
, and consider any
\rho
-
\delta_{\varepsilon}
-pseudo-trajectory
(u^{j})_{j=0}^{\infty}
for
\hat{f}
in
\mathcal{F}(X)
. We will check that
(u^{j})_{j=0}^{\infty}
is
\rho
-
\varepsilon
-shadowed by the
\hat{f}
-orbit of
u:=u^{0}
. We start by claiming that

\rho(\hat{f}^{j}(u),u^{j})<j\delta_{\varepsilon}\leq\varepsilon_{2}<\varepsilon\quad\text{ for each }0\leq j\leq N.

(5.6)

Actually, we have that
\rho(\hat{f}^{0}(u),u^{0})=\rho(u^{0},u^{0})=0
. Arguing inductively, if we now assume that the inequality
\rho(\hat{f}^{j}(u),u^{j})<j\delta_{\varepsilon}
holds for some
0\leq j<N
, by statement (a) of Lemma
3.5
we have that

\displaystyle\rho(\hat{f}^{j+1}(u),u^{j+1})

\displaystyle\leq\rho(\hat{f}^{j+1}(u),\hat{f}(u^{j}))+\rho(\hat{f}(u^{j}),u^{j+1})\leq\rho(\hat{f}^{j}(u),u^{j})+\rho(\hat{f}(u^{j}),u^{j+1})

\displaystyle<j\delta_{\varepsilon}+\delta_{\varepsilon}=(j+1)\delta_{\varepsilon}\leq\varepsilon_{2}\leq\varepsilon_{1}<\tfrac{\varepsilon}{4}<\varepsilon.

With (
5.6
) being proved, we now divide the proof in two cases to check that

\rho(\hat{f}^{j+1}(u),u^{j+1})<\varepsilon\quad\text{ for all }j\geq N.

(5.7)

–

Case 1
:
We have that
\rho\in\{d_{0},d_{S}\}
. In this case we start by claiming that

u^{j}_{0}\subset\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})\quad\text{ for all }j\geq N.

(5.8)

Actually, for
j=N
and since
[\hat{f}^{N}(u)]_{0}=f^{N}(u_{0})\subset\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1})
by (
5.5
), using (
5.6
) together with statement (e) of Proposition
2.4
we have that

d_{H}([\hat{f}^{N}(u)]_{0},u^{N}_{0})\leq\rho(\hat{f}^{N}(u),u^{N})<N\delta_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon_{2},

so that
u^{N}_{0}\subset[\hat{f}^{N}(u)]_{0}+\varepsilon_{2}\subset\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1})+\varepsilon_{2}\subset\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})
. Arguing inductively, if we now assume that
u^{j}_{0}\subset\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})
for some
j\geq N
, then by (
5.4
) we have that

[\hat{f}(u^{j})]_{0}=\overline{f}(u^{j}_{0})=f(u^{j}_{0})\subset f(\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}))\subset\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1}).

Using again Proposition
2.4
we obtain that
d_{H}([\hat{f}(u^{j})]_{0},u^{j+1}_{0})\leq\rho(\hat{f}(u^{j}),u^{j+1})<\delta_{\varepsilon}\leq\varepsilon_{2}
and hence

u^{j+1}_{0}\subset[\hat{f}(u^{j})]_{0}+\varepsilon_{2}\subset\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1})+\varepsilon_{2}\subset\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}).

With (
5.8
) being proved we can complete
Case 1
as follows. For each
j\geq N
pick any pair of points
x_{j+1}\in[\hat{f}^{j+1}(u)]_{1}
and
y_{j+1}\in u^{j+1}_{1}
. It follows that
x_{j+1}\in[\hat{f}^{j+1}(u)]_{\alpha}\subset[\hat{f}^{j+1}(u)]_{0}=f^{j+1}(u_{0})
and that
y_{j+1}\in u^{j+1}_{\alpha}\subset u^{j+1}_{0}
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{I}
, which implies, in particular, that
x_{j+1},y_{j+1}\in\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})
by (
5.5
) and (
5.8
) respectively. In addition, note that given any level
\alpha\in\mathbb{I}
, then for every point
x\in[\hat{f}^{j+1}(u)]_{\alpha}\subset f^{j+1}(u_{0})
we have that
x\in\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})
by (
5.5
) and hence that

d(x,u^{j+1}_{\alpha})=\inf_{z\in u^{j+1}_{\alpha}}d(x,z)\leq d(x,y_{j+1})\leq d(x,x^{*})+d(x^{*},y_{j+1})<2(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}),

but also for every point
y\in u^{j+1}_{\alpha}\subset u^{j+1}_{0}
we have that
y\in\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})
by (
5.8
) and hence that

d(y,[\hat{f}^{j+1}(u)]_{\alpha})=\inf_{z\in[\hat{f}^{j+1}(u)]_{\alpha}}d(y,z)\leq d(y,x_{j+1})\leq d(y,x^{*})+d(x^{*},x_{j+1})<2(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}).

Since we had the inequalities
0<\varepsilon_{2}\leq\varepsilon_{1}<\tfrac{\varepsilon}{4}
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\rho(\hat{f}^{j+1}(u),u^{j+1})

\displaystyle\leq d_{\infty}(\hat{f}^{j+1}(u),u^{j+1})=\sup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{I}}d_{H}([\hat{f}^{j+1}(u)]_{\alpha},u^{j+1}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{\sup_{x\in[\hat{f}^{j+1}(u)]_{\alpha}}d(x,u^{j+1}_{\alpha}),\sup_{y\in u^{j+1}_{\alpha}}d(y,[\hat{f}^{j+1}(u)]_{\alpha})\right\}\leq 2(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})<\varepsilon,

which implies that (
5.7
) holds when
\rho\in\{d_{0},d_{S}\}
and we have finished the proof for
Case 1
.

–

Case 2
:
We have that
\rho=d_{E}
. For this case, and without loss of generality, we will assume that we have chosen
\varepsilon_{2}<1
. In addition, in the sequel we will repeatedly use the fact that

f(Y+\delta)\subset f(Y)+\delta\quad\text{ for every subset }Y\subset X\text{ and every positive value }\delta>0,

(5.9)

which is a direct consequence of the fact that
(X,f)
is contractive. We start by claiming that

\operatorname{end}(u^{j})\subset\left(f^{j}(u_{0})+j\delta_{\varepsilon}\right)\times\mathbb{I}\ \cup\bigcup_{0\leq l\leq j-1}\left(f^{l}(X)+l\delta_{\varepsilon}\right)\times[0,(l+1)\delta_{\varepsilon}]\quad\text{ for all }1\leq j\leq N.

(5.10)

Actually, since
\operatorname{end}(u^{0})=\operatorname{end}(u)=\operatorname{send}(u)\ \cup\ X\times\{0\}\subset u_{0}\times\mathbb{I}\ \cup\ X\times\{0\}
, applying the map
\hat{f}
it is not hard to check the inclusion
\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u^{0}))\subset f(u_{0})\times\mathbb{I}\ \cup\ X\times\{0\}
. Thus, using that
\overline{d}_{H}(\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u^{0})),\operatorname{end}(u^{1}))=d_{E}(\hat{f}(u^{0}),u^{1})<\delta_{\varepsilon}
by assumption, it follows that

\operatorname{end}(u^{1})\subset\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u^{0}))+\delta_{\varepsilon}\subset\left(f(u_{0})+\delta_{\varepsilon}\right)\times\mathbb{I}\ \cup\ X\times[0,\delta_{\varepsilon}].

This proves (
5.10
) for
j=1
. Arguing inductively, if we now assume that (
5.10
) holds for some positive integer
1\leq j<N
, applying the map
\hat{f}
to such formula it is not hard to check that

\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u^{j}))\subset f\left(f^{j}(u_{0})+j\delta_{\varepsilon}\right)\times\mathbb{I}\ \cup\bigcup_{0\leq l\leq j-1}f\left(f^{l}(X)+l\delta_{\varepsilon}\right)\times[0,(l+1)\delta_{\varepsilon}]\ \cup\ X\times\{0\}.

Applying (
5.9
) for the corresponding sets and positive values on the previous formula we get that

\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u^{j}))\subset\left(f^{j+1}(u_{0})+j\delta_{\varepsilon}\right)\times\mathbb{I}\ \cup\bigcup_{0\leq l\leq j-1}\left(f^{l+1}(X)+l\delta_{\varepsilon}\right)\times[0,(l+1)\delta_{\varepsilon}]\ \cup\ X\times\{0\}.

Finally, since
\overline{d}_{H}(\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u^{j})),\operatorname{end}(u^{j+1}))=d_{E}(\hat{f}(u^{j}),u^{j+1})<\delta_{\varepsilon}
by assumption, we have that

\operatorname{end}(u^{j+1})\subset\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u^{j}))+\delta_{\varepsilon}\subset\left(f^{j+1}(u_{0})+(j+1)\delta_{\varepsilon}\right)\times\mathbb{I}\ \cup\bigcup_{0\leq l\leq j}\left(f^{l}(X)+l\delta_{\varepsilon}\right)\times[0,(l+1)\delta_{\varepsilon}].

With (
5.10
) being proved, we now claim that

\operatorname{end}(u^{j})\subset\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})\times\mathbb{I}\ \cup\bigcup_{0\leq l\leq N-1}\left(f^{l}(X)+l\delta_{\varepsilon}\right)\times[0,(l+1)\delta_{\varepsilon}]\quad\text{ for all }j\geq N.

(5.11)

Actually, using that
f^{N}(u_{0})\subset\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1})
by (
5.5
) and that
N\delta_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon_{2}
, for
j=N
we have that

\displaystyle\operatorname{end}(u^{N})

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:end.subset.1}}{\subset}\left(f^{N}(u_{0})+N\delta_{\varepsilon}\right)\times\mathbb{I}\ \cup\bigcup_{0\leq l\leq N-1}\left(f^{l}(X)+l\delta_{\varepsilon}\right)\times[0,(l+1)\delta_{\varepsilon}]

\displaystyle\ \ \subset\ \ \mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})\times\mathbb{I}\ \cup\bigcup_{0\leq l\leq N-1}\left(f^{l}(X)+l\delta_{\varepsilon}\right)\times[0,(l+1)\delta_{\varepsilon}].

Arguing inductively, if we now assume that (
5.11
) holds for some
j\geq N
, applying the map
\hat{f}
to such formula it is not hard to check that

\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u^{j}))\subset f\left(\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})\right)\times\mathbb{I}\ \cup\bigcup_{0\leq l\leq N-1}f\left(f^{l}(X)+l\delta_{\varepsilon}\right)\times[0,(l+1)\delta_{\varepsilon}]\ \cup\ X\times\{0\}.

Using (
5.4
) and applying
N
times (
5.9
) we thus have that

\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u^{j}))\subset\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1})\times\mathbb{I}\ \cup\bigcup_{0\leq l\leq N-1}\left(f^{l+1}(X)+l\delta_{\varepsilon}\right)\times[0,(l+1)\delta_{\varepsilon}]\ \cup\ X\times\{0\},

where the term
f^{N}(X)+(N-1)\delta_{\varepsilon}
, corresponding to the element
l=N-1
in the previous big union, fulfills that
f^{N}(X)\subset\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1})
by (
5.5
). Hence,
f^{N}(X)+(N-1)\delta_{\varepsilon}\subset\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1}+(N-1)\delta_{\varepsilon})
, and the previous inclusion can be rewritten as follows

\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u^{j}))\subset\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1}+(N-1)\delta_{\varepsilon})\times\mathbb{I}\ \cup\bigcup_{0\leq l\leq N-1}\left(f^{l}(X)+(l-1)\delta_{\varepsilon}\right)\times[0,l\delta_{\varepsilon}].

Finally, since
\overline{d}_{H}(\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u^{j})),\operatorname{end}(u^{j+1}))=d_{E}(\hat{f}(u^{j}),u^{j+1})<\delta_{\varepsilon}
and
N\delta_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon_{2}
, we have that

\displaystyle\operatorname{end}(u^{j+1})

\displaystyle\subset\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}(u^{j}))+\delta_{\varepsilon}\subset\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1}+N\delta_{\varepsilon})\times\mathbb{I}\ \cup\bigcup_{0\leq l\leq N-1}\left(f^{l}(X)+l\delta_{\varepsilon}\right)\times[0,(l+1)\delta_{\varepsilon}]

\displaystyle\subset\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})\times\mathbb{I}\ \cup\bigcup_{0\leq l\leq N-1}\left(f^{l}(X)+l\delta_{\varepsilon}\right)\times[0,(l+1)\delta_{\varepsilon}],

With (
5.11
) being proved we can complete
Case 2
as follows. For each
j\geq N
pick any pair of points
x_{j+1}\in[\hat{f}^{j+1}(u)]_{1}
and
y_{j+1}\in u^{j+1}_{1}
. Since
x_{j+1}\in[\hat{f}^{j+1}(u)]_{1}\subset[\hat{f}^{j+1}(u)]_{0}=f^{j+1}(u_{0})
, using fact (
5.5
) we have that
x_{j+1}\in\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})
, that

\{x_{j+1}\}\times\mathbb{I}\ \cup\ X\times\{0\}\subset\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{j+1}(u))\subset\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1})\times\mathbb{I}\ \cup\ X\times\{0\},

and that
\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})\subset\{x_{j+1}\}+2(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})
because
d(z,x_{j+1})\leq d(z,x^{*})+d(x^{*},x_{j+1})<2(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})
for each
z\in\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})
. In its turn, since
(l+1)N\delta_{\varepsilon}\leq N\delta_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon_{2}
for all
0\leq l\leq N-1
and
\varepsilon_{2}<1
by assumption in
Case 2
, using (
5.11
) we necessarily have that
y_{j+1}\in\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})
, that

\{y_{j+1}\}\times\mathbb{I}\ \cup\ X\times\{0\}\subset\operatorname{end}(u^{j+1})\subset\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})\times\mathbb{I}\ \cup\ X\times[0,\varepsilon_{2}],

and that
\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})\subset\{y_{j+1}\}+2(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})
because
d(z,y_{j+1})\leq d(z,x^{*})+d(x^{*},y_{j+1})<2(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})
for all
z\in\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})
. From the previous inclusions, it follows that

\displaystyle\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{j}(u))

\displaystyle\subset\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})\times\mathbb{I}\ \cup\ X\times\{0\}\subset\left(\{y_{j+1}\}+2(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})\right)\times\mathbb{I}\ \cup\ X\times\{0\}

\displaystyle\subset\left(\{y_{j+1}\}\times\mathbb{I}\ \cup\ X\times\{0\}\right)+2(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})\subset\operatorname{end}(u^{j+1})+2(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}),

and that

\displaystyle\operatorname{end}(u^{j+1})

\displaystyle\subset\mathcal{B}_{d}(x^{*},\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})\times\mathbb{I}\ \cup\ X\times[0,\varepsilon_{2}]\subset\left(\{x_{j+1}\}+2(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})\right)\times\mathbb{I}\ \cup X\times[0,2(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})]

\displaystyle\subset\left(\{x_{j+1}\}\times\mathbb{I}\ \cup\ X\times\{0\}\right)+2(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})\subset\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{j}(u))+2(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}).

By statement (a) of Proposition
2.1
we finally deduce that

d_{E}(f^{j+1}(u),u^{j+1})=\overline{d}_{H}(\operatorname{end}(f^{j+1}(u)),\operatorname{end}(u^{j+1}))\leq 2(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})<\varepsilon,

which implies that (
5.7
) holds for
\rho=d_{E}
and we have finished the proof for
Case 2
.

Assume now that
(X,d)
is bounded but not a complete space. Consider its completion
(X^{*},d^{*})
and identify
(X,d)
as a proper dense subspace of
(X^{*},d^{*})
. Since
(X,d)
is isometrically and densely embedded in
(X^{*},d^{*})
, it follows that
(X^{*},d^{*})
is bounded. Now, let
f^{*}:(X^{*},d^{*})\longrightarrow(X^{*},d^{*})
be the unique continuous extension of
f:(X,d)\longrightarrow(X,d)
into
(X^{*},d^{*})
. Thus, the system
(X,f)
is a subsystem of
(X^{*},f^{*})
, which can be checked to be contractive (recall that the extension of a Lipschitz map from a dense subspace is again Lipschitz with the same constant). As for
(X,d)
and
(X^{*},d^{*})
, we can consider
\mathcal{F}(X)
as a subset of
\mathcal{F}(X^{*})
, and
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
as a subsystem of
(\mathcal{F}(X^{*}),\hat{f^{*}})
. By the first part of the proof we know that, given any
\rho^{*}\in\{d_{0}^{*},d_{S}^{*},d_{E}^{*}\}
and any
\varepsilon>0
we can find
\delta_{\varepsilon}>0
such that every
\rho^{*}
-
\delta_{\varepsilon}
-pseudo-trajectory
(u^{j})_{j=0}^{\infty}
for
\hat{f^{*}}
is
\rho^{*}
-
\varepsilon
-shadowed by the
\hat{f^{*}}
-orbit of
u^{0}
. Noticing that the metric space
(\mathcal{F}(X),\rho)
is isometrically embedded in
(\mathcal{F}(X^{*}),\rho^{*})
, where we are letting
\rho=\rho^{*}\arrowvert_{\mathcal{F}(X)}
, if we now choose a
\rho
-
\delta_{\varepsilon}
-pseudo-trajectory
(u^{j})_{j=0}^{\infty}
in
\mathcal{F}(X)
we have that

\rho^{*}(\hat{f^{*}}(u^{j}),u^{j+1})=\rho^{*}(\hat{f}(u^{j}),u^{j+1})=\rho(\hat{f}(u^{j}),u^{j+1})<\delta_{\varepsilon}\quad\text{ for all }0\leq j<\infty,

and we achieve that

\rho(\hat{f}^{j}(u^{0}),u^{j})=\rho^{*}(\hat{f}^{j}(u^{0}),u^{j})=\rho^{*}(\hat{f^{*}}^{j}(u^{0}),u^{j})<\varepsilon\quad\text{ for all }0\leq j<\infty.

Noticing that
d_{0}^{*}\arrowvert_{\mathcal{F}(X)}=d_{0}
, that
d_{S}^{*}\arrowvert_{\mathcal{F}(X)}=d_{S}
and that
d_{E}^{*}\arrowvert_{\mathcal{F}(X)}=d_{E}
, the proof is finished.
∎

Remark 5.7
.

Let us include two applications of Theorem
5.6
:

(1)

Let
(\mathbb{R},f)
be the system considered in Example
5.2
, where
\mathbb{R}
is endowed with the usual
|\cdot|
-metric and
f(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}x
for each
x\in\mathbb{R}
. We proved there that
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(\mathbb{R}),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(\mathbb{R}),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(\mathbb{R}),\hat{f})
do not have the finite shadowing property. However, let
X\subset\mathbb{R}
be any
|\cdot|
-bounded set fulfilling that
f(X)\subset X
. It follows that the dynamical systems
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
have the shadowing property by Theorem
5.6
. In particular, given any positive value
r>0
we could consider
X=\{0\}
,
X=]-r,0[
,
X=[-r,0[
,
X=]0,r[
,
X=]0,r]
,
X=\mathbb{Q}\ \cap\ [-r,r]\setminus\{0\}
, or we could combine them by considering unions and intersections of these sets.

(2)

Let
\mathbb{C}
be the set of complex numbers and let
|\cdot|
denote the usual modulus there. Fix any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and, denoting by
i
the imaginary constant and by
\exp(z)
the exponential function, consider the subset
X=\{0\}\cup\{r\exp(\tfrac{j\pi}{k}i)\ ;\ r>0\text{ and }1\leq j\leq k\}
. In addition, define
d:X\times X\longrightarrow[0,\infty[
as
d(z_{1},z_{2})=|z_{1}-z_{2}|
when
\arg(z_{1})=\arg(z_{2})
and
d(z_{1},z_{2})=|z_{1}|+|z_{2}|
when
\arg(z_{1})\neq\arg(z_{2})
for every pair
z_{1},z_{2}\in X
, where
\arg(0)=0
. Consider the map
f:X\longrightarrow X
as
f(0)=0
,
f(r\exp(\tfrac{j\pi}{k}))=\tfrac{r}{2}\exp(\tfrac{(j+1)\pi}{k}i)
for all
r>0
and
1\leq j\leq k-1
, and
f(-r)=-\tfrac{r}{2}
when
r\in\ ]0,1]
and
f(-r)=-(1-\tfrac{3}{2^{l+2}})-\tfrac{r}{2^{2l+2}}
when
r\in\ ]2^{l},2^{l+1}]
for some
l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then, it is not hard to check that
(X,d)
is an unbounded metric space, that
(X,f)
is a contractive system, and that
f^{j}(X)
is an unbounded set for
1\leq j\leq k-1
while
f^{k}(X)
is a bounded set in
(X,d)
. By Theorem
5.6
, the corresponding systems
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
have the shadowing property.

In view of Theorems
3.1
,
5.4
and
5.6
, we end the section posing a natural question:

Problem 5.8
.

Characterize the dynamical systems
(X,f)
for which any of the extended dynamical systems
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
or
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
have the (resp. finite) shadowing property.

6
Conclusions

In this paper, we have examined the interaction between several dynamical properties of a discrete system
(X,f)
and its extension
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
on the space of normal fuzzy sets
\mathcal{F}(X)
and with respect to the supremum, Skorokhod, sendograph and endograph metrics. In particular, we have dealt with the notions of
contractive
,
expansive
,
expanding
and
positively expansive
systems, as well as with the notions of
chain recurrence
,
chain transitivity
,
chain mixing
and the
shadowing property
. Throughout this paper we have solved open problems posed in
[
18
,
20
]
, provided counterexamples to
[
5
, Theorem 5]
, and established new results concerning the previous chain-type notions and the shadowing property.

We recall that this paper is a continuation of
[
27
]
, where, within the same fuzzy framework, we examined in depth several dynamical properties related to
transitivity
,
point-transitivity
,
recurrence
, and the notions of
Devaney chaos
and the
specification property
. Since our original motivation was to address all the dynamical properties considered in
[
1
,
5
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
30
]
for the system
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
with respect to the endograph metric
d_{E}
, it only remains to investigate the notions of
Li-Yorke
and
distributional chaos
considered in
[
30
]
, and the notions of
sensitivity
studied in
[
19
]
. We will address these dynamical properties in a forthcoming work, in collaboration with other authors.

We would like to stress that our study focuses on the space
\mathcal{F}(X)
of normal fuzzy sets, rather than on other commonly used fuzzy frameworks, since this is precisely the setting adopted in our main references
[
1
,
5
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
30
]
mentioned above. For further details on the rationale behind this choice, we refer the reader to
[
27
, Section 5]
and to the comments preceding Theorem
4.1
. Moreover, and as noted in
[
27
]
, possible directions for future research include studying the dynamical properties of the system
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
when
\mathcal{F}(X)
is endowed with other metrics (see
[
16
]
), as well as extending the analysis to the case where
X
is a (not necessarily metrizable) uniform space (see
[
22
,
23
]
).
