Performance Optimization of Novel Laser Hybrid Ultrasonic Vibration Assisted Micro Milling for Inconel 718
Authors: Ahmad Waqar Tehami, Muhammad Rizwan ul Haq, Syed Hussain Imran Jaffery, Muhammad Salman Khan, Shahid Ikramullah Butt, Ghulam Hussain, Muhammad Bilal Khan, Mohammed Alkahtani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Miniaturization drives advanced manufacturing essential for next-generation systems using superalloys in aerospace, biomedical and microelectronics. Hybrid machining combines distinct processes to harness their individual strengths and overcome limitations. This experimental research explored the machinability of Inconel 718 through novel laser hybrid ultrasonic vibration-assisted micro-milling (L-UVAMM), utilizing fiber laser for preparing slots followed by micro-milling on the corresponding laser-generated pilot slots with uncoated and three different coated tools. Cutting speed, feed, depth of cut, tool coatings and amplitude considered as input variables across four discrete levels to design experiments using Taguchi L-16 orthogonal array to investigate their effect on key responses including surface roughness, tool wear, and burr formation. Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) and Grey Relational Analysis (GRA) identified the most significant parameters and determined the best input combinations. Uncoated tools displayed the least tool wear, TiSiN minimized burr height while TiAlN improved surface roughness and reduced burr width. ANOVA for the regression model revealed the tool type as the most crucial factor influencing responses with a contribution ratio of 45.76%. Response surface method optimized the regression model for optimum conditions, resulted in 43.96%, 27.18%, and 19.65% average reduction of surface roughness, tool wear, and burr formation, respectively.

Introduction

Miniaturization is the driving force behind modern technological innovations, fueled by the mandate for more compact, efficient, and affordable solutions. It pointedly impacts on performance enhancement in various industries including aerospace, automotive and biomedical as well as electronics by providing compact designs for more functionality and integrating new features1. Mechanical micromachining can produce complex microstructures on a variety of materials, especially nickel-based alloys, with the advantages of high machining accuracy and surface integrity while diminishing tool wear. With small tool diameters, the issues associated with cutting edge geometry and tool rigidity influence workpiece accuracy and surface finish2,3. Challenging environmental conditions shaped by high temperature, mechanical stress and corrosive elements sturdily need durable superalloy4. In high performance systems, nickel-based alloys are especially imperative for aircraft turbines, rocket and submarine engines operating reliably at temperatures ranging from 450 to 700 °C5. Superalloy Inconel 718 is recognized for its unprecedented combination of high strength at elevated temperatures and excellent corrosion resistance, which guarantees structural integrity in extreme service settings6. Due to its excellent resistance to heat and corrosion, it is preferred choice in sectors such as power generation, gas turbines and nuclear energy systems7,8. Inconel 718 is frequently alloyed with aluminum to produce lightweight and heat-resistant structural components9,10. Numerous studies have focused on the viability of employing various energy field machining techniques to augment the machinability of these alloys11. Vibration-assisted machining converts continuous contact into instantaneous, intermittent and reciprocating by applying low-frequency or ultrasonic vibrations to the tool or workpiece12,13. Ultrasonic machining, characterized by vibrations above 16 kHz14, enhances the machinability of high-strength alloys by generating vibrations in one or multiple cutting directions. For instance, axial vibration is promising for ultrasonic assisted grinding of Inconel 71815. Vibration in machining facilitates chip removal, preventing re-engagement and reducing cutting forces, tool wear and burr formation16. The material’s physical characteristics alter, softens the material, enabling smoother cutting and better surface finish17,18. Ultrasonic Vibration-Assisted Micro-Milling (UVAMM) enhances chip breaking, inhibits burr formation, reduces friction, mitigates size and plowing effects and extends tool life while ensuring precision19,20. While effectively used in turning, drilling and grinding, K. M. Li and S. L. Wang believe that its potential in micro-milling remains unexplored and the influence of tool wear on surface roughness and burr formation remains uncertain2.

Ultrasonic machining offers significant advantages for machining Inconel 71821. Integrating ultrasonic vibrations with traditional milling offers various advantages unique to Inconel 718 by breaking chips into smaller pieces, enabling faster evacuation from the cutting zone. Vibrations minimize tool-workpiece contact, improving surface finish and reducing imperfections without extensive post-processing22,23. It allows precise machining of turbine blades with superior surface quality and dimensional accuracy in aerospace10,24. Experimental investigations by M. Xu et al.25 show that machinability of Inconel 718 can be improved because both cutting force and temperature significantly decreased in ultrasonic machining. H. Zhang et al. concluded that unlike one-dimensional tools that vibrate in a single either tangential or axial direction, two-dimensional ultrasonic tools produce elliptical or circular motion trajectory for enhanced cutting performance26. Rotary ultrasonic elliptical milling which applies transverse ultrasonic vibrations, has demonstrated superior machinability for cast Ni-based superalloys27,28. Laser ultrasonic elliptical machining was conducted by M. Khajehzadeh et al., revealing the effect of vibration amplitude and feed rate on cutting forces and surface roughness29. Lianxing et al. proposed a novel approach using an anticlockwise vibration direction to limit the surface roughness30. N. Ngah et al. believe that innovative approaches like hybrid machining methods which combine ultrasonic milling with other technologies, could provide even more precision and efficiency gains10.

Hybrid manufacturing techniques, like additive-subtractive hybrid manufacturing based on Laser-Directed Energy Deposition, integrate additive and subtractive processes for fabricating complex Inconel 718 structural components, with reduced surface plastic deformation31. H. Zhou et al. employed ultrasonic vibration for the first time to assist Laser-Directed Energy Deposition milling hybrid process in manufacturing thin-walled parts of GH4169 and found that it exhibited varying surface roughness32. Laser beam machining alone cannot produce finished products which can be used for variety of purposes due to limitations like uncontrolled dimensions and tapered surfaces33. Emerging novel hybrid approaches are effective for creating micro-scale parts in difficult-to-cut materials. These methods incorporate non-conventional energy-assisted processes such as vibration, laser and electricity by considering both structural and machining characteristics of the material to improve machinability while minimizing mechanical stress during fabrication34. Y. Du et al. proposed a pulsed laser-assisted ultrasonic elliptical vibration cutting technique, where both processes act synergistically to improve machinability35. Hybrid micro-machining combines two or more processes either sequentially or in assisted configuration to enhance overall performance while minimizing drawbacks36,37. In the sequential approach, each process is performed one after another on separate systems offering greater flexibility and simpler integration in selecting and customizing operations38. Conversely, assisted way involves simultaneous execution, where one process supports the other, requiring precise synchronization and specialized equipment with higher initial costs. Gupta et al. reported that hybrid techniques are applied in fabricating micro-features such as micro-holes, channels and complex 3D structures in challenging materials like metals, non-metals and superalloys39,40.

Laser Assisted Machining or ablation enhances machinability by using a laser beam to locally soften or modify material with negligible damage, forming a metamorphic layer while mechanical machining removes the heat affected zone41,42. Studies on laser assisted micro-milling of Inconel 718 by H. Zhang et al. reveal moderate reduction in cutting forces43. Similarly, it has been applied to Niobium alloy (C-103) and concluded that laser has a substantial impact on response variables44. J. Dominguez-Caballero et al. found that laser ultrasonic machining process improves machinability of Ti-6Al-4 V during turning over diverse speeds and depths of cut23. C. Zhang et al. also did turning by integrating ultrasonic vibration with laser energy to boost tungsten carbide cutting, lifespan of CBN tools and precision machining45. Cemented carbide was machined using laser ultrasonic assisted milling by C. Zhang et al., improving micro-morphology while minimizing cutting forces, tool wear and surface roughness46.

Despite extensive independent investigations on laser-assisted machining and ultrasonic vibration-assisted micromachining, a significant research gap remains concerning their combined influence on the micro-machinability of Inconel 718. Existing laser-assisted milling investigations predominantly employ simultaneous laser heating during cutting to achieve thermal softening and cutting force reduction, while ultrasonic vibration-assisted milling is known to induce high-frequency intermittent tool–workpiece contact, improving chip formation, mitigating adhesion-driven wear, and suppressing burr formation effects that are particularly critical at the micro-scale where minimum uncut chip thickness and ploughing dominate cutting mechanics. In contrast to these single-assistance approaches, the present study introduces and experimentally evaluates a novel sequential hybrid strategy for micromachining Inconel 718, in which controlled laser ablation is first applied to condition the near-surface material state, followed by ultrasonic vibration-assisted micro-milling using both uncoated and coated micro-tools. This intentional sequence establishes modified engagement conditions that enhance intermittent cutting stability and chip-formation behavior during subsequent micro-milling. Unlike conventional simultaneous laser-assisted machining approaches primarily aimed at transient thermal softening during cutting, the proposed L-UVAMM framework employs a sequential hybrid strategy in which laser pre-conditioning establishes pilot-slot geometry in a separate stage prior to ultrasonic vibration-assisted micro-milling. Consequently, the subsequent ultrasonic stage operates on a laser-conditioned surface rather than untreated bulk material, thereby creating a distinct sequential hybrid material-removal pathway for micro-machining of Inconel 718.

This hybrid strategy synergistically combines the geometric flexibility of laser ablation with accuracy, and low thermal damage of mechanical micro-milling. Notably, the combined effects of this sequential hybrid process together with different tool coatings on surface roughness, tool wear, and burr formation have not previously examined within a single experimental framework. Bridging this gap is of considerable industrial importance for improving performance, precision, and productivity in micromachining of Inconel 718. The present work systematically addresses this gap by analyzing the effects of cutting speed, feed per tooth, depth of cut, and vibration amplitude under the proposed L-UVAMM framework. A structured Taguchi L16 orthogonal design (five factors, four levels) is employed, followed by multi-response parametric optimization and Response Surface Methodology, to establish optimal machining conditions and provide practical parameter-ranking guidance for high-quality and reliable micromachining of Inconel 718. Materials and methods

An experimental investigation was conducted on hybrid technique that integrates two distinct processes for machining a workpiece. This method employed a two-step approach. The first step involved creating pilot slots using a fiber laser, followed by ultrasonic vibration-assisted mechanical micro-milling on these laser-generated pilot slots in the second step to achieve the final geometry. During UVAMM in second step, the tool is subjected to high frequency and small vibration amplitudes. The basic concept involved integration of ultrasonic vibrations into the standard milling operation. By delivering high-frequency vibrations of 20 kHz, the machine creates oscillations at micro-scale in cutting tool, which leads to chips breakup and lessen the cutting forces16,47. Ultrasonic vibration-assisted micro-milling improves machining performance primarily through intermittent tool–workpiece contact, which reduces friction, stabilizes chip formation, and suppresses burr formation in Inconel 71819,48. However, under micro-scale cutting conditions, further performance improvement through parameter optimization alone becomes increasingly constrained by ploughing-dominated deformation, unstable chip evacuation, adhesion-driven wear, and severe full-engagement conditions during initial tool entry. In the proposed sequential framework, laser-generated pilot slots act as a pre-conditioning stage by selectively removing material along the intended cutting path through controlled laser ablation43. Unlike conventional simultaneous laser-assisted machining approaches that rely on continuous laser coupling for localized thermal softening during cutting which requires complex synchronization49, the present approach employs laser processing as an independent pre-conditioning stage prior to ultrasonic vibration-assisted micro-milling. This pre-removal reduces the burden of full material engagement along the predefined cutting path, thereby lowering edge loading on the micro-tool and improving engagement stability during subsequent machining.

Taguchi L-16 experimental design is employed to systematically evaluate machining parameters including speed, feed, cutting depth and amplitude. Cutting tools, both coated (TiAlN, TiSiN, nACo) and uncoated, are gauged based on the response variables. An integrated multi-objective optimization framework combining Taguchi Design of Experiments (DOE), Grey Relational Analysis (GRA), and Response Surface Methodology (RSM) is adopted to achieve robust experimental design, simultaneous optimization of multiple machining responses, and predictive modeling of process behavior. Taguchi DOE enables statistically balanced exploration of the machining parameter space with a limited number of experiments, while GRA converts multiple responses into a single Grey Relational Grade (GRG) for unified evaluation. Subsequently, RSM is applied to the computed GRG to establish predictive response models and identify optimum machining conditions within the continuous design space. The combined framework integrates experimental efficiency, multi-response evaluation, and predictive optimization within a unified methodology50–52. ANOVA and regression modeling are used to enumerate the influence of individual factors separately, leading to the determination of the best and worst machining conditions. Finally, validation experiments confirm the optimized settings, ensuring repeatability and reliability of the machining process for high-precision applications.

Selection of material

Unique properties of Inconel 718 have made it the material where conventional materials cannot withstand the rigors of complex, harsh and extreme environments. Seel and aluminum lose their strength in high-temperature and corrosive applications, whereas Inconel 718 keeps its strength over a broad temperature range. Due to its exceptional oxidation resistance and fatigue durability, Inconel 718 operates efficiently at 450–700 °C.

Cutting tool specifications

Selecting appropriate tools for UVAMM of Inconel 718 requires considering material properties, machining conditions, and tool design. Abrasive and heat-resistant nature of Inconel 718 leads to speedy tool wear under intensive forces and temperatures7,55. Polycrystalline diamond and ceramic coatings for cutting tools have been created exclusively for ultrasonic machining of Inconel 718. Improved tool geometries enhance efficiency by reducing cutting forces and aiding chip evacuation56,57.

Ultrasonic vibration assisted micro-milling was performed using 500 μm diameter, two-flute tungsten carbide end mills with a helix angle of 30°. All cutting tools employed in this study were supplied by the same manufacturer “Changzhou North Carbide Tool Co., Ltd.” and possessed identical geometrical characteristics to ensure consistent cutting conditions across all experiments. Workpiece preparation

The Inconel 718 workpiece, measuring 10 mm×10 mm×50 mm was clamped in a vice, and its surface was leveled using a 12 mm carbide square end mill. This leveled surface served as the reference for laser-machined pilot slots. After laser machining, the workpiece remained in the same setup and was transferred to the milling center for UVAMM. Tests were performed with a 10 mm cutting length of slot and 3 mm gap between each slot, as illustrated in Fig. 3.

In laser processing of Inconel 718 and related nickel-based alloys, scanning speed and input laser energy into the workpiece contribute greatly to slot geometry, ablation efficiency and surface quality while pulse frequency dictates the pulse overlap and local heat accumulation with subsequent impact on integrity of materials surface and the formation of recast layer49,64. In addition, multi-pass (loop-based) laser strategies usually employed for controlled and reproducible slot depth, enabling progressive material removal while reducing excessive recast, debris redeposition and surface instability65. Guided by these established principles and supported preliminary trials for stable slot formation, the selected combination of laser parameters guarantees reproducible slot geometry, controlled depth evolution, and sufficient surface conditions for subsequent Ultrasonic Vibration Assisted Micro-Milling (UVAMM).

The laser-generated slots in this study serve as pilot slots in the first phase of a sequential laser hybrid mechanical micro-milling process, while the final slot geometry is produced during the subsequent ultrasonic vibration assisted micro-milling operation. Accordingly, the depth of the laser-machined pilot slots was intentionally maintained approximately 10 μm shallower than the final machined depth. Figure 5 presents representative optical micrographs of laser-generated pilot slots corresponding to different depth levels, together with the laser parameter combinations at which these depths were obtained, as identified through preliminary trials aimed at achieving stable and repeatable pilot slot formation at the targeted depth. The images illustrate the pilot slot geometry and the visible laser-induced thermal influence region adjacent to the slot walls, which is sufficient for evaluating the dimensional constraints of the laser pre-conditioning step within the proposed sequential hybrid machining framework. Detailed microstructural and compositional characterization of the laser-generated slots using SEM and EDS techniques was not undertaken, as the laser-affected material is subsequently removed during the ultrasonic vibration assisted micro-milling stage and does not contribute to the final machined surface condition.

Milling machine - ultrasonic setup

After engraving pilot slots, work-piece was then machined to the final depth on YDPM Model MV-1060, 3-axis vertical milling center shown in Fig. 6a. The milling tool was subjected to ultrasonic vibrations using an Acrow Machinery tool holder, operating at 20 kHz with amplitudes between 0 and 10 μm shown in Fig. 6b. The experimental setup includes an ultrasonic transducer as the primary source of high-frequency vibrations7,66. High-frequency electrical signals were fed to a transducer that converts this energy into vibrations. These vibrations were subsequently transmitted through an energy-focusing component, such as the horn/tool assembly, inducing high-frequency oscillations along the longitudinal axis. This technique has been employed in numerous works for micro machining of various superalloys including nickel and titanium alloys67. Design of experiments

Vibration-assisted machining improves cutting performance, but improper parameter selection can shorten tool life compared to conventional machining. Optimizing milling parameters, including vibration amplitude, frequency and cutting conditions, is essential for achieving stable and predictable machining outcomes10. Each process parameter was investigated at four discrete levels, shown in Table 6, to ensure sufficient coverage of the operating domain and to capture potential nonlinear and transition behaviors characteristic of micro-milling of Inconel 718. The input ranges of the process parameters: spindle speed (6–10.5 m/min), feed per tooth (0.25–1 μm/tooth), and depth of cut (30–90 μm), were selected to span the micro-milling scale where minimum uncut chip thickness, ploughing–shearing transition, tool–workpiece contact mechanics, burr formation sensitivity, and surface integrity evolve significantly. Similar parameter windows have been reported in prior micro-milling studies on Inconel 718 and comparable high-strength alloys with comparable cutting tools and scale. Ultrasonic vibration amplitudes up to 9 μm are consistent with vibration-assisted milling literature, emphasizing improved intermittency and chip breaking. This four-level scheme offers adequate resolution to meaningful identification of trends and robust optimization, while maintaining feasible experimental cost68,69.

Taguchi method uses orthogonal arrays to optimize factor levels, eliminating the need for full factorial experiments while minimizing the number of trials. By focusing on critical experimental runs, it enhances efficiency in analysis and widely applied to optimize machining, minimizes variations and improves process robustness against environmental and component fluctuations65. An experimental layout based on Taguchi L16 orthogonal array was developed which analyzes 4 input parameters at 4 levels each, to achieve a practical balance between experimental economy and multi-factor coverage. Full factorial designs (45 = 1024 runs) are impractical in hybrid micro-milling due to time and cost constraints, whereas the selected L16 array reduces this requirement to just 16 experiments and allows robust estimation of main effects, which is sufficient for screening and optimization under controlled experimental conditions with confirmation tests to validate the attained results. Although Taguchi L16 orthogonal array is inherently limited in resolving all interaction effects due to aliasing/confounding, this design is well established for screening and optimization in machining research. Therefore, interaction-related interpretations are treated cautiously, and the discussion emphasizes statistically supported main effects validated by confirmation tests, following established practice in machining optimization literature54,70–72. Surface roughness, tool wear and burr formation were marked as the response (output) variables. ANOVA was employed to examine the contribution of input parameters to these responses.

Results and discussions

Each experimental condition was conducted twice under identical conditions using a new tool to eliminate wear effects. Table 7 displays the response values of these experimental runs. Due to the high sensitivity of micromachining, even minor errors and noise significantly impact outcomes. Variations between initial and subsequent runs were attributed to human error, tool quality, and machine noise. Measured data was analyzed using ANOVA and the contribution ratios using a lower-the-better approach were considered to determine parameter significance73. Before conducting an analysis of variance (ANOVA), the underlying assumptions of the method were carefully examined, including normality of residuals, homogeneity of variance among factor levels and independence of observations74,75. Normality was verified through residual distribution analysis and normal probability plot, while variance homogeneity was challenged by the residual-versus-fit plots. The experimental design ensured independence of observations through controlled and randomized experimental trials. The diagnostics confirmed the data suitability for applying ANOVA, thereby ensuring the statistical validity and reliability of the reported F-values, p-values, and contribution ratios. Parameters yielding p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant at the 95% confidence level44,76. ANOVA results for best and worst machining conditions were validated using confirmatory tests. A thorough understanding of material behavior, tool alternatives and process parameters is imperative to optimize the UVAMM of Inconel 7187.

The mechanisms governing tool wear and burr formation during laser hybrid ultrasonic micro-milling of Inconel 718 are explained by systematically measured physical responses and validated parametric trends. Tool wear, burr sizes, and surface roughness were measured quantitatively on the machined slots using high resolution digital microscope, while representative micrographs in subsequent sections are provided to describe the measurement approach and typical observed features. The correlations between these physical responses and the governing parameters forms the basis for interpreting the underlying machining behavior. Such an interpretation framework, in which machining behavior is inferred from measured physical responses and their dependence on process parameters, is widely adopted in micro-milling research for nickel-based superalloys, including Inconel 71877,78.

Compared with conventional micro-milling of Inconel 718, ultrasonic vibration-assisted micro-milling alters the cutting kinematics by superimposing high-frequency oscillations on the tool motion, resulting in intermittent tool–workpiece contact. This intermittent engagement reduces effective contact time and frictional sliding, promotes chip segmentation, and suppresses ploughing-dominated deformation at the micro-scale, leading to improved surface finish and reduced burr formation19,48. The same mechanism lowers average cutting forces by reducing frictional resistance and effective shear deformation per engagement, which in turn moderates tool wear progression. Thermal behavior is also moderated, as reduced frictional work and periodic tool–work separation decrease heat generation and facilitate improved heat dissipation from the cutting zone79–81.

Within the proposed L-UVAMM framework, the laser-generated pilot slots establish a pre-conditioned engagement path prior to ultrasonic vibration-assisted micro-milling. Consequently, the ultrasonic stage operates under modified engagement conditions rather than directly on untreated bulk material, thereby improving intermittent cutting stability and chip-removal behavior during subsequent machining. The combined interaction between laser pre-conditioning and ultrasonic intermittent cutting therefore establishes a modified hybrid cutting environment fundamentally different from conventional simultaneous laser-assisted machining approaches primarily based on transient thermal softening during cutting. These interaction-driven effects indicate that the proposed L-UVAMM framework influences not only machining performance, but also the underlying chip-formation stability, burr evolution behavior, and tool–workpiece interaction mechanisms governing material removal during micro-milling of Inconel 718.

Surface roughness analysis

Surface roughness is main challenge in micro-milling, affecting the precision and service life of miniaturized components82. Conventional machining often struggles to achieve low surface roughness due to interrupted force phenomenon, particularly in aircraft component manufacturing. Roughness is prejudiced by tool geometry, machine accuracy, cooling conditions, undeformed chip thickness and tool wear83,84. Studies indicate that ultrasonic vibration cutting with coated carbide tools improves surface roughness, extends tool life and reduces wear85. Surface roughness was quantified using the areal arithmetic mean height (Sa), which provides a more representative characterization of micro-milled surfaces by capturing height variations across the measured area rather than along a single profile. Areal parameters are particularly suitable under micro-milling conditions where ploughing and shearing mechanisms coexist86. Representative areal surface map together with the selected evaluation area used for Sa calculation which corroborates the reported roughness values, are illustrated in Fig. 7b.

Key process parameters such as cutting speed, feed rate, depth of cut and vibration frequency significantly impact surface finish7,47. Surface roughness while machining hard materials like Inconel 718, increases with greater feed and depth of cut but decreases at higher speeds87,88. However, using a lower feed rate with smaller cutting edge radius result reduced residual height of the machined surface83. ANOVA results on surface roughness, as presented in Table 8, identifies cutting speed as the most influential factor, accounting for 26.14% of the overall variability. According to Platt et al.89, this impact is primarily due to temperature variations in the cutting zone at higher speeds. Feed rate emerges as the second most significant factor, contributing 20.14% to the variability, closely aligning with the 19.88% impact of tool coatings. In contrast, vibration amplitude and depth of cut exhibit comparatively smaller effects, contributing 12.87% and 12.39%, respectively. As depicted in Fig. 8, the main effect plots indicate that cutting speed and feed has nonlinear relationship with surface roughness. Initially, surface roughness rises during the transition when speed increases from 6 to 7.5 m/min. However, as speed advances to the higher range of 7.5 to 10.5 m/min, a downward trend emerges, indicating an improvement in surface quality. Lower values of roughness were observed at higher feed rates. Previous studies suggest that the impact of depth of cut on surface roughness remains unpredictable and challenging to assess due to inconsistent findings90. Increasing trend in roughness has been observed with more cutting depths. Deeper milling depths induce microcracks due to overheating, further degrading surface quality46. Research confirms that increasing vibration amplitude91 and frequency can enhance surface morphology and reduce roughness as also illustrated in Fig. 8. Researchers found that reducing tool wear leads to notable improvements in surface quality92.

Tool wear analysis

Understanding the tool wear mechanisms is important for optimizing machining performance, tool longevity, part quality and cost efficiency93. Flank wear is the most important among different wear types because it leads to cutting edge stability and dimensional accuracy, which are due to mechanical, thermal and chemical stresses94. Vibration-assisted machining provides a promising approach to solve this problem by introducing cyclic tool-workpiece separation, improved chip removal and reducing friction and wear95. However, control of improper parameters in ultrasonic machining of Inconel 718 increase tool wear22,25. Studies have demonstrated the advantages of ultrasonic machining over conventional machining as it enhances surface quality and minimizes tool wear96, while similar reductions in cutting forces and cutting-zone temperatures have also been reported for titanium alloys97. Both vibration frequency and amplitude significantly influence tool-workpiece separation. Increasing vibration amplitude reduces cutting forces even up to 48% and extends tool life98,99. Comparisons between ultrasonic vibration-assisted and conventional micro-milling further indicate that vibration reduces cutting edge wear and prevents built-up edge formation, thereby enhancing tool longevity and surface quality. Further investigations are essential to fully optimize the advantages of ultrasonic machining in mitigating tool wear100.

The depth of cut plays a critical part in the ultrasonic machining of Inconel 718, directly influencing cutting forces, material removal rate, heat generation and tool wear. A deeper cut increases cutting pressure and tool wear while accelerating material removal, whereas a shallower cut reduces cutting forces and tool wear but slows down machining20,107. Optimal determination of cutting depth is important for balancing material characteristics, cutting tool capacity and material removal rate to obtain optimal surface quality and tool life7,85. Prior studies verified that with increasing cutting depth, the tool wear rises because of larger area of shear plane resulting in high material removal rate and temperature in cutting zone, which intensifies resistance to tool. This pattern has been well documented by researchers such as Sui et al.108. Figure 11e illustrates a decline in tool wear as the amplitude increases, with highest wear observed under conventional machining condition at 0 μm amplitude. It displays a 24.74% and 19.46% decrease in flank and face wear, respectively, at an amplitude of 9 μm as compared to conventional machining condition73. This reduction is attributed to intermittent tool workpiece interaction in ultrasonic machining, which reduces cutting time, minimizing heat buildup resulting better cooling. Comparable results have been reported by Feng et al.109 and Liu et al.110. Previous research highlights that the ultrasonic vibration cutting method is most effective at low speeds and with high frequency and amplitude104. Optimizing these parameters is essential for improved chip breaking, reduced cutting forces and enhanced material removal rates in Inconel 718 machining111.

Although coated tools generally exhibit superior wear resistance under conventional machining conditions due to their enhanced hardness and thermal stability, the comparatively lower wear observed for uncoated tools in the present study appears closely associated with the thermo-mechanical interaction conditions established under the proposed L-UVAMM framework. Within the proposed sequential hybrid approach, laser-generated pilot slots reduce severe full-engagement cutting conditions, while ultrasonic vibration-assisted micro-milling introduces repetitive intermittent contact and cyclic thermal interaction during material removal25,98. Under such conditions, coated tools become increasingly susceptible to localized delamination, edge chipping, micro-fracture, and coating-interface degradation at the micro-scale55,96. Furthermore, coating deposition modifies the effective cutting-edge geometry, which becomes increasingly significant when coating thickness represents a comparatively larger proportion of the cutting-edge radius2,103. In contrast, uncoated carbide tools maintain comparatively sharper effective cutting edges and avoid coating-interface failure mechanisms under the proposed hybrid intermittent cutting environment. These observations suggest that the comparatively improved wear performance of uncoated tools is closely associated with the intermittent thermo-mechanical interaction conditions established under the proposed L-UVAMM framework. Tool-wear progression therefore appears strongly influenced by the interaction between intermittent cutting behavior, cyclic thermo-mechanical loading, and coating integrity at the micro-scale.

Burr formation analysis

Inconel 718 tends to form large burrs during micro-milling due to its high plasticity, adhesion and chip-breaking challenges, affecting part accuracy and performance. Burr formation is affected by cutting parameters and tool geometry69,112. Burrs reduce dimensional precision and require costly post-processing operations, sometimes introducing residual stress especially for micro-components as small size makes them highly susceptible to damage during machining92. UVAMM enhances chip breaking, reduces burr formation and improves surface quality while minimizing tool wear, particularly at low speeds, high amplitudes and large feeds2,69.

Burr formation is more prominent towards the down milling side as compared to the up-milling side, with larger burr width and height. This occurs because the localized cutting-edge velocity on the up-milling side is consistently higher than on the down milling side, leading to increased burr formation in down milling. Previous studies have reported similar findings in micro-milling research113. To consider the worst-case scenario, top burrs on the down milling side were analyzed only by measuring the maximum burr width and height identified for each run. The measurement approach for both burr width and burr height is illustrated in Fig. 12a, where the reference directions and measurement locations are explicitly annotated. Burr width was measured as the horizontal extension of the burr relative to the slot wall,, as shown in Fig. 12b, whereas burr height was determined as the vertical protrusion of the burr above the surface following established measurement practice114.

Multi-objective optimization using GRA

Multi-objective optimization (MOO) is vital and widely used decision making technique in situations where multiple conflicting objectives need to be addressed simultaneously, when single-objective optimization methods are inadequate to yield satisfactory solutions. Analysis presented in Table 11 is based on main effect plots shown in Figs. 10 and 13, reveals that the optimization of any individual output response is dependent on different input parametric conditions. An integrated optimization strategy based on Taguchi Design of Experiments (DOE), Grey Relational Analysis (GRA) and Response Surface Methodology (RSM) was adopted to perform productive multi-response optimization within the laser hybrid ultrasonic micro-milling framework51,52,117. The Taguchi DOE is initially used to systemically organize the experimental configuration and achieve orthogonal coverage of the predetermined multi-factor space so that main effects of each response can be efficiently estimated and evaluated for individual response with minimum number of runs. This criterion balances parametric exploration and statistical reliability for practical experimental constraints. Hence, the Grey Relational Analysis (GRA) is applied to transition multiple individual responses into a single integrated performance metric, namely the Grey Relational Grade (GRG). Taking the use of weight factors into consideration to suitably handle conflicted objectives; GRA, which originates from Grey System Theory, is well established choice for addressing machining optimization, especially when responses have different scales or units as well as their desired trends.

Last, Response Surface Methodology (RSM) is implemented on the computed ‘GRG’ to formulate continuous predictive models over their design space. RSM makes it possible to produce response surfaces, contour plots and regression equations that allow for interpolation, determination of optimum conditions and interpretation of parameter sensitivities. Therefore, the developed integrated Taguchi-GRA-RSM approach complements effective design, multi-response analysis and predictive modeling in a statistically rigorous yet computationally feasible framework for enhancement of performance of hybrid micro-milling processes. Unlike other complex multi-objective optimization techniques, GRA is simple, computationally efficient, and easy to implement. The approach for multi-objective optimization is explained in Fig. 14. The process of implementing GRA in multi objective optimization follows a systematic approach that includes preprocessing and normalization of data, computation of Grey Relational Coefficients (GRCs) and Grey Relational Grades (GRGs). GRA, based on the Taguchi method, reduces a multi-response problem to a single unique function118,119.

GRA-based optimum represents the best observed experimental condition, whereas the RSM-based optimum represents the model-predicted optimum in the continuous design space. Minor discrepancies between these two solutions are expected and reflect the complementary nature of the two optimization approaches rather than methodological conflict. For practical application, the GRA solution provides a reliable experimentally validated parameter set, while the RSM solution serves as a refined optimization guide for further process improvement.

Meta-heuristic optimization techniques such as Genetic Algorithms, Particle Swarm Optimization, Non-Dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II, and Grey Wolf Optimizer are also widely applied for solving complex, nonlinear, and multi-objective optimization problems in machining and manufacturing120–122, and have been shown to effectively explore global optima in large search spaces. However, these approaches typically require a large number of function evaluations or iterations to converge and extensive computational or surrogate modeling, which are particularly effective in simulation-based studies but impose substantial experimental burden associated with physical machining trials and cost constraints when applied on high-cost alloys such as Inconel 718123,124.

Response surface methodology (RSM)

RSM is a powerful analytical approach for optimizing processes influenced by multiple variables and their interactions. This methodology enables continuous refinement and enhancement of experimental processes by leveraging statistical and mathematical modeling125. Unlike the Taguchi Method, RSM integrates experimental design, statistical analysis, and data-fitting techniques to achieve more precise and effective process optimization.

Regression analysis

Regression analysis is a statistical technique used to establish the relationship between multiple dependent and independent factors simultaneously. It is particularly useful in multi-objective optimization, where multiple conflicting objectives must be balanced to achieve the best possible solution. The regression-based optimization involved regression modeling of multi-objective function, ANOVA for model validation and Optimization using RSM.

Conclusions

Successful experimental investigation has been conducted herein to bridge the identified gap by scrutinizing a set of combination levels of machining parameters in conjunction with various tool coatings for laser hybrid ultrasonic vibration assisted mechanical micro-milling of super alloy Inconel 718. ANOVA and GRA were adopted to analyze the measured outcomes to pattern the impact of each input parameter on surface roughness, tool wear, and burr formation. The findings of this study as summarized below offer a substantial contribution by elucidating the intricate interdependencies among machining parameters, tool coatings and their profound influence on surface integrity, tool degradation, and burr control. These insights emphasize the need for judicious tool coating selection and meticulous parametric optimization to augment surface finish, enhance tool efficiency, and promote operational sustainability.

This direction is expected to broaden the applicability and optimization capability of machining strategies for difficult-to-machine materials.

Integration of meta-heuristic optimization techniques, such as Genetic Algorithms and NSGA-II, with hybrid modeling frameworks to enable global multi-objective optimization of micro-milling processes. Such approaches would facilitate efficient exploration of complex and highly nonlinear design spaces, complement the present statistical framework, and further enhance optimization robustness. This direction is expected to broaden the applicability and optimization capability of machining strategies for difficult-to-machine materials.
