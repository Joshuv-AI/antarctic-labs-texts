Response to “Refining CT Use Before Active Surveillance in Papillary Thyroid Microcarcinoma”
Authors: Young Hun Jeon, Ji Ye Lee
Published: 2026

We sincerely thank Sun et al. [1] for their thoughtful comments regarding our study evaluating the added value of CT in detecting macroscopic lymph node metastasis (LNM) in papillary thyroid microcarcinoma [2]. We appreciate the opportunity to further clarify the intended clinical implications of our findings.

We agree that the incremental detection provided by CT should not be directly interpreted as evidence of improved active surveillance (AS) outcomes. As appropriately highlighted by the authors, our retrospective study was designed to evaluate diagnostic performance, rather than longitudinal oncological outcomes, such as progression during AS, conversion surgery, recurrence, or disease-specific morbidity. Therefore, we concur that CT-detected, ultrasound (US)-occult nodal disease should not automatically be regarded as an absolute contraindication to AS. Rather, our intention was to demonstrate that complementary CT might provide additional staging information that could refine individualized risk stratification and shared decision-making before initiation of AS.

We also appreciate the important discussion regarding the definition of macroscopic LNM. In our study, we adopted the >2 mm cutoff based on prior prognostic literature [3,4,5,6,7,8,9] distinguishing microscopic from macroscopic metastatic disease. However, we acknowledge that nodal burden exists along a biological continuum and that small-volume central compartment metastases may not have the same clinical implications as bulky, multifocal, lateral neck, or extranodal disease [7]. Indeed, as noted by the authors, most US-occult macroscopic metastases identified in our cohort were small-volume lesions located in the central compartment. We agree that future studies incorporating nodal number, compartment, extranodal extension, progression kinetics, and long-term outcomes, would better define the subsets of CT-detected disease that are truly clinically actionable in the context of AS.

We further agree that CT findings should be interpreted within a multimodal and selective diagnostic framework [8,10]. In our study, although the addition of CT substantially improved sensitivity for detecting macroscopic LNM, it was accompanied by a modest reduction in specificity, and the possibility of overstaging. Importantly, our institutional workflow does not rely solely on CT findings for surgical decision-making. Suspicious lymph nodes identified on CT routinely undergo second-look US evaluation and US-guided biopsy with fine-needle aspiration/core needle biopsy and thyroglobulin measurement, whenever feasible, consistent with the current Korean Society of Thyroid Radiology recommendations [10]. Therefore, we agree that CT should primarily serve as a complementary staging tool rather than as an isolated determinant for immediate surgical escalation.

Finally, we fully agree that prospective longitudinal studies are required to determine whether the additional nodal disease detected by CT translates into clinically meaningful improvements in patient outcomes, including progression during AS, extent of delayed surgery, risk of recurrence, quality of life, and cost-effectiveness. We believe our study provides an important diagnostic foundation for future studies.

We again thank the authors for their insightful comments and valuable perspective on this evolving clinical issue.
