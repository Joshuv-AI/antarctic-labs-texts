Tiny but mighty: Insects can drive carrion decomposition in the absence of facultative vertebrate scavengers
Authors: Christopher Fust, Patrick B Finnerty, James Vandersteen, Stefanie Bonat, Emma E Spencer, Mathew S Crowther, Rhys J Cairncross, Philip S Barton, Thomas M Newsome
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractScavengers play a key role in recycling animal biomass, yet the relative contributions of insects and vertebrates to this process remain poorly understood. Moreover, understanding whether one scavenger group can compensate for the absence of another is critical for predicting how carrion decomposition may respond to changes in community composition or seasonal conditions. To address this, we assessed the capacity of scavenging insects and facultative vertebrate scavengers to recycle animal biomass in alpine Australia. We measured carcass persistence (decay rates) of 64 eastern gray kangaroos (Macropus giganteus; 1709.6 kg of total biomass) across four seasons and experimental scenarios of (1) full vertebrate and insect scavenger access, and (2) vertebrate exclusion but insect access. In warmer seasons, insects were the primary consumers of carrion, with only occasional vertebrate scavenging observed. There was almost a complete lack of carrion removal in colder seasons when insects were less active, even where vertebrate scavenging rates were highest. Moreover, the presence of vertebrates did not influence the number of flies or beetles at carrion in any season, suggesting that vertebrates were not suppressing these specialist insect scavengers by rapidly consuming carrion. Our results indicate that carrion decomposition of a relatively large carcass in this alpine ecosystem is primarily driven by insects. Indeed, insect scavengers compensated for the absence of vertebrates, resulting in similar carcass removal rates across treatments. However, vertebrate scavengers did not compensate where insect scavengers were functionally absent during cooler months, with carcasses persisting for prolonged periods. These findings stand in contrast to many studies globally that demonstrate a dominant role of vertebrate scavengers in removing carcass biomass or outcompeting insects, even during colder seasons. Understanding whether insects can compensate for the absence of vertebrates to accelerate carcass biomass removal and vice versa—a form of functional redundancy—is crucial for informing when efficient carcass removal occurs and in turn how ecosystems might respond to high carcass loads during different times of the year.Keywords: carcass use, community ecology, ecosystem resilience, functional redundancy, scavenger

INTRODUCTION

A diverse array of organisms scavenge dead animal biomass (i.e., carrion), playing a crucial role in nutrient recycling within ecosystems (Barton et al., 2013). Yet human‐driven environmental change is threatening scavenger communities, with areas of high human activity showing reduced vertebrate scavenger richness and diversity (Sebastián‐González et al., 2019). The loss of scavengers from ecosystems can disrupt carrion removal rates and other ecological processes linked to decomposition (Barton et al., 2013; Newsome et al., 2021). However, ecosystem resilience depends on the ability to sustain these processes even when scavenger community composition changes (Huijbers et al., 2016), particularly under conditions of stress or disturbance (Biggs et al., 2020). As such, this resilience can hinge on functional overlap among scavenger species—known as functional redundancy—whereby the loss of one species or group can be offset by others fulfilling similar ecological roles (Lawton & Brown, 1993; Rosenfeld, 2002).

Although vertebrate scavengers can dominate carrion biomass removal (Cunningham et al., 2018; O'Bryan et al., 2019; Sebastián‐González et al., 2016), insects also play a critical role in carcass decomposition (Barton & Evans, 2017). These two scavenger guilds typically operate in tandem, and compete with one another for resources, thus facilitating rapid decomposition processes (Benbow et al., 2019; DeVault et al., 2004). However, whether one group can compensate for the absence of the other in removing carrion biomass remains poorly understood (Biggs et al., 2020; Sugiura et al., 2013; Sugiura & Hayashi, 2018). Although limited, emerging research suggests that insect scavengers may have the capacity to sustain carrion removal in the absence of vertebrates. Indeed, on Japan's Oki Islands, vertebrates dominated carcass removal where present, but insects maintained decomposition rates on islands without them (Sugiura & Hayashi, 2018). Similarly, in central Japan, the relative contribution of each guild varied with forest cover, indicating that the expression of functional redundancy is context‐dependent (Sugiura et al., 2013).

Scavenging rates and carcass persistence are also strongly influenced by abiotic factors such as temperature, humidity, and moisture levels (Carter et al., 2008; Payne, 1965). These environmental conditions influence microbial and insect activity levels, thereby affecting overall decomposition rates (Barton & Bump, 2019; Matuszewski et al., 2010; Turner et al., 2017). In cooler months, reduced insect activity can slow carcass decay—particularly when vertebrate scavenger use is infrequent (Spencer & Newsome, 2021). However, vertebrate scavenging is also impacted by temperature and can increase during colder periods, likely due to heightened energetic demands and reduced availability of alternative food sources. For example, in Poland, scavenging by common ravens (Corvus corax), red foxes (Vulpes vulpes), and pine martens (Martes martes) increased at lower temperatures and under deeper snow cover (Selva et al., 2005). Similarly, in Australia, higher scavenging rates by mesoscavengers such as brushtail possums (Trichosurus vulpecula) and red foxes have been observed during cooler months (Finnerty et al., 2024; Vandersteen et al., 2023). Understanding how seasonal and environmental factors influence carcass use is therefore essential for assessing the timing and functional roles of both vertebrate and insect scavengers (Newsome et al., 2021).

Mainland Australia hosts a diverse assemblage of vertebrate and insect scavengers that utilize carrion as a key food resource (Forsyth et al., 2014; Spencer et al., 2020). Insects such as flies, beetles, and ants can significantly accelerate decomposition, doubling biomass loss from small vertebrate carcasses (Barton & Evans, 2017). Apex scavengers like dingoes (Canis dingo) also contribute to carcass decomposition, though their impact is most pronounced when scavenging occurs frequently (Spencer & Newsome, 2021). However, unlike many northern hemisphere systems, which include obligate vertebrate scavengers or multiple large‐bodied scavengers, Australia's mainland vertebrate scavenger guild is dominated by small‐ to medium‐sized facultative scavengers (Finnerty et al., 2024; Vandersteen et al., 2023). The functional implications of this unique guild structure remain poorly understood yet may offer a preview of ecological dynamics in regions globally that contain only facultative scavengers or systems that have lost apex or obligate scavengers (Spencer & Newsome, 2021).

Here we examined the capacity of vertebrate and insect scavengers to facilitate carrion decomposition in an alpine environment in southeastern Australia. We monitored carcass persistence across four seasons under two experimental treatments: (1) full access to both vertebrate and insect scavengers, and (2) exclusion of vertebrates while allowing insect access. This design enabled us to assess how the removal of one scavenger guild affects carcass decomposition rates and whether functional overlap exists between these scavenger groups, in terms of their ability to recycle carrion. First, we predicted that carcass persistence would be longest where vertebrates are excluded and shortest where both groups had access, reflecting complementary scavenging roles that accelerate biomass loss (DeVault et al., 2004). Second, we predicted carcass persistence to increase during the colder seasons of autumn and winter due to reduced insect and microbial activity, leaving only facultative vertebrate scavengers to drive decomposition (Spencer & Newsome, 2021). In doing so, we evaluate the extent of functional redundancy between scavenger guilds by testing whether insects and/or vertebrates can independently facilitate carrion decomposition when one group is absent, and how this capacity varies across seasons.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study site

This study was conducted in Kosciuszko National Park, in Australian alpine and subalpine ecosystems, centered around 148.489 E, 36.30346 S (Figure 1A). Vegetation is dominated by Eucalyptus spp., particularly snow gum (Eucalyptus pauciflora). The study site has a mean daily temperature range of 3.7–18.7°C with mean annual precipitation of 1275.4 mm and snow cover between June and October (Bureau of Meteorology, 2020). Monitoring periods coincided with the four seasons: March–May 2020 (autumn, average min/max: 1°C/13°C); July–September 2020 (winter, average min/max: −3°C/8°C); September–November 2020 (spring, average min/max: 2°C/15°C); January–March 2021 (summer, average min/max: 6°C/21°C) (Appendix S1: Figure S1).

Previous studies have identified 34 vertebrate species at carcass sites within the study site, nine of which scavenged; the most common were brushtail possums and ravens (Corvus spp.), with larger species like dingoes and wedge‐tailed eagles (Aquila audax) occasionally scavenging (Vandersteen et al., 2023). Overall vertebrate scavenging rates were also higher in winter compared to other seasons (Vandersteen et al., 2023). Scavenging insect communities are active on carrion in warmer seasons in the study site and are dominated by flies (Diptera), beetles (Coleoptera), wasps, and ants (Hymenoptera) (Cairncross et al., 2022; Spencer et al., 2020). Carrion biomass estimates are not known in any Australian ecosystem but native (e.g., kangaroos/wallabies) and introduced (e.g., deer, feral horses, and pigs) herbivores are common in the study site and culling of the latter group can lead to unnaturally high background rates of carrion availability (Newsome et al., 2024).

Carcass placements

Sixty‐four eastern gray kangaroo (Macropus giganteus) carcasses (average mass 26.7 ± 1.07 [SE] kg) were monitored between March 2020 and April 2021 across four distinct seasons (autumn, winter, spring, and summer; 16 carcasses per season) (Appendix S1: Figure S2). Carcasses were sourced nearby from licensed harvesters. Carcasses were presented intact with exception of entry and exit wounds from firearm discharge during collection. During each season, carcasses were deployed at eight monitoring sites with similar canopy cover (~50% cover). Monitoring sites were 2 km from each other during each season, and a minimum of 500 m and 3 months delay from preceding replicates. This distance ensured a level of spatial independence between carcasses and prevented habituation of scavengers to a carrion source location (Inagaki et al., 2020; Spencer & Newsome, 2021). This distance also aimed to avoid abnormal carcass removal rates by scavengers, due to “scavenger swamping,” or the saturation of the study area with carcasses (as per Bernardino et al., 2020; Costantini et al., 2017; Smallwood, 2007). Moreover, these distances were selected to mitigate any olfactory cues between carcasses and to mitigate any remaining olfactory cues from previously monitored carcasses (Finnerty et al., 2022). Additionally, a minimum distance of 500 m between carcasses across seasons reduced the likelihood that insects could influence subsequent trials by potentially persisting underground at previously placed carcasses.

In every season, each monitoring site consisted of two carcass treatments: open (no scavenger exclusion, Figure 1B) and caged (vertebrate exclusion, Figure 1C). Carcasses were monitored for 100 days or until carcasses had reached 10% biomass remaining. Within the national park, monitoring sites were no less than 100 m from a seldom used fire trail with the paired treatments, open and caged carcasses, 50 m apart (Figure 1A). The caged treatments involved placing carcasses inside steel mesh walls (5 cm aperture, 2.5 mm gauge), and chicken wire roof (5 cm aperture, 1 mm gauge) with cubic dimensions (2 × 1 × 1 m) (Figure 1C). To ensure maximum visibility of vertebrate behavior at open treatments, carcasses were secured to the ground using two star‐pickets (60 cm) and tie‐wire, a common approach used in scavenging studies (Newsome & Spencer, 2021; Spencer & Newsome, 2021).

Estimating vertebrate and insect scavenger activity

Vertebrate scavenger activity on open treatment carcasses was monitored with a Reconyx PC800 Hyperfire camera 3 m away on a star picket. Cameras were calibrated to take photographs continuously (approximately one image per second) when triggered by thermal movement (i.e., rapid‐fire, no wait period). Image sequences were tagged with the individual species visible in the frame and if the animal was feeding. A feeding event was defined as when an animal was observed actively touching the carcass with its mouth, with feeding events considered “new” if occurring ≥10 min from the previous visitation event by the same species or if a different species began feeding after another, as per other studies (Bragato et al., 2022; Cairncross et al., 2024; Finnerty et al., 2024, 2025; Newsome & Spencer, 2021; Spencer & Newsome, 2021; Vandersteen et al., 2023).

Insects were collected in two 120 mL pitfall traps at each open and caged carcass treatment across all study seasons. Pitfall traps were filled half‐way with ethylene glycol as a preservative and were buried flush with the ground, approximately 20 cm from the head of the carcass and at the base of the tail (Barton & Evans, 2017; Spencer et al., 2020). We sampled insects for 3 days across all four seasons, capturing a peak period of insect activity at carrion during the early stages of decomposition (Barton & Evans, 2017; Cairncross et al., 2022). This 3‐day dataset was used for the main analyses and to test our predictions; however, insects were also sampled for 12 days after carcass placements in summer, with replacement of the vials every third day. We used this dataset as a supplementary test to confirm that any observed trends for the first 3 days can manifest over longer time periods, including during a period when insects are highly active (summer). All insects collected were sorted into the following taxonomic groups: ants (Formicidae), beetles (Coleoptera), flies (Diptera), and European wasps ( Vespula germanica ), and the individual numbers were counted. Formal statistical analyses focused on flies and beetles because of their well‐established interactions with carrion (Barton & Evans, 2017; Spencer et al., 2020). Insects identified that did not have a close life history with carrion or were captured opportunistically were assigned to an “other” category. Insects were identified with an Olympus stereo SZ40 model (0.67×–5×) microscope, with the focal groups (flies and beetles) identified using available reference guides (Couri, 2010; Hangay & Zborowski, 2010; Kavazos et al., 2016; Wallman, 2001).

Carcass persistence

To quantify carcass persistence rates, we weighed carcass remains at the end of the monitoring period and calculated the proportion of carcass body mass removed by subtracting final weights from the starting weight. From this, we were able to generate an estimated daily rate of the proportion of carcass biomass removed by dividing this by the number of days since carcass deployment (proportion removed/day). We did this to standardize across different carcass weights in our analysis.

Statistical analysis

Total counts of vertebrate scavenger feeding events were compared across seasons with a generalized linear model (GLM) using the “MASS” package (Ripley et al., 2021). Data were over‐dispersed, so we fitted a negative binomial distribution. For insects, since flies are a key driver of carcass decomposition within the insect scavenger community (Meehan et al., 2005), we comparatively modeled fly counts between seasons (from the first 3 days of sampling), the two carcass treatments (open and caged), and the interaction between these two variables with a GLM, also using the “MASS” package. Data were over‐dispersed, so we again fitted the model with a negative binomial distribution. We also extended our analysis to include the other core group of insect scavengers that attend terrestrial carrion, these being beetles. We followed the same analytical approach as for flies, modeling beetle counts between seasons (from the first 3 days of sampling), our carcass treatments, and their interaction, with a GLM fitted to a negative binomial distribution.

Rates of carcass biomass removal were modeled using GLMs fitted with beta distributions, from the package “glmmTMB” (Brooks et al., 2017). A beta distribution was used as data were proportions between 0 and 1. Such distributions cannot handle perfect 0 or 1 values; accordingly, a single data point was transformed from 0 to 0.0001 to account for this. We compared overall seasonal differences in carcass biomass removal by constructing a GLM that fitted season as a sole predictor variable. For our comparison between carcass treatments (open and caged), we modeled cool (autumn and winter), and warm (spring and summer) seasons separately to improve model computation. This was undertaken because carcasses deployed in warm seasons decayed much more quickly than cooler seasons. Carcass treatment (open and caged) and season were interacted as predictor variables in these models. Models were validated with the package “performance” (Lüdecke et al., 2021). All statistical analyses were performed in R (R Development Core Team, 2021).

RESULTS

Scavenger visitations

From March 2020 through April 2021, 64 carcasses (1709.6 kg total biomass, mean = 26.71 ± 1.07 (SE) kg per carcass) were monitored across four seasons for a total of 391 days. There was one camera failure on one carcass site in summer, but all other vertebrate data was retrieved. In total, 939,636 photographs were taken, including 454,701 photographs of vertebrate scavengers feeding on carcasses and 2132 distinct feeding events (Figure 2). Of these photographs, 52% were feeding events by brushtail possums and 34% were feeding events by corvids (Figure 2). Winter had the greatest vertebrate scavenger feeding events (42%), followed by spring (22%), autumn (18%), and then summer (17%) (Figure 2; Appendix S1: Figure S3). There was a significant difference in total vertebrate scavenger feeding events between autumn (reference level) and winter (Z = 2.01, p = 0.04), but not between autumn and the other seasons (Appendix S1: Table S1).

Vertebrate species that appeared at carcass sites in low numbers that displayed feeding behavior were grouped into the “other” category and include the brown goshawk (Accipiter fasciatus), laughing kookaburra (Dacelo novaeguineae), superb lyrebird (Menura novaehollandiae), and blue‐tongue lizard (Tiliqua sp.). Asterisk (*) marked species are those not native to Australia.

In the first 3 days of insect monitoring, we collected a total of 8233 insects (all flies, beetles, ants, European wasps, and “other”) at both open and caged carcass treatments (Appendix S1: Table S2, Figure S4). In winter, one carcass site (open and cage) was inaccessible for insect vial retrieval due to poor weather. All other vials were retrieved intact. The insect taxa during the early decay stage was dominated by 6363 ants (Formicidae), whereas there were only 432 were flies (mostly Calliphoridae, Sarcophagidae, Muscidae) and 581 were beetles (mostly Staphylinidae, Silphidae, Histeridae, and some Scarabaeidae and Carabidae). Among the most abundant beetles were Creophilus sp. (Staphylinidae) and Ptomaphila lacrymosa (Silphidae). Fly counts during the first 3 days of sampling across all carcass treatments were higher in autumn compared to winter (Z = −4.590, p < 0.001) and spring (Z = −1.975, p = 0.048, Figure 3). However, we found no difference between summer and autumn (Z = −1.538, p = 0.124), and no effect of carcass treatment on fly counts across all seasons (Z = −0.920, p = 0.357), nor for any interaction terms (Appendix S1: Table S1, Figure S4). Similarly, beetle counts during the first 3 days of sampling were lower in summer (Z = −2.904, p = 0.004) and winter (Z = −2.767, p = 0.006) than in autumn, but not different between autumn and spring (Appendix S1: Table S1; Figure 3). We also found no effect of carcass treatment, or any interaction terms, on the counts of beetles at carcasses (Appendix S1: Table S1). Our supplementary analysis also found no effect of carcass treatment, or any interaction terms, on the counts of flies and beetles at carcasses over the 12‐day sampling period in summer (Appendix S1: Figure S5, Figure S6, Table S3).

Carcass persistence

Carcasses across treatments were removed much faster in spring (n = 16 carcasses) (β = 1.01, SE = 0.18, Z = 5.53, p < 0.001) and summer (n = 16 carcasses) (β = 1.33, SE = 0.18, Z = 7.57, p < 0.001) than the reference level (autumn) (n = 16 carcasses). In comparison, there was no difference between winter (n = 16 carcasses) and the reference level (autumn) (β = 0.13, SE = 0.21, Z = 0.64, p = 0.52). Mean daily proportion (%) of carcass biomass removed in autumn was 0.4 ± 0.3 (SD) and 0.5 ± 0.3 in winter. Average daily removal rates were nearly twice as fast in spring (0.8 ± 0.2) and three times faster in summer (1.1 ± 0.04).

For the two warm seasons, an interaction between the summer season and open carcass treatment was detected (β = 0.04, SE = 0.02, Z = 2.00, p = 0.047). The main effect term of summer also showed a statistical effect, with carcasses in summer decomposing faster than in spring (β = 0.33, SE = 0.01, Z = 22.90, p < 0.001). Similarly, there was an interaction between the winter season and open carcass treatment among the two cool seasons (β = −1.12, SE = 0.49, Z = −2.27, p = 0.02). There was no difference between the main effect of the winter season and the reference level of autumn (β = 0.66, SE = 0.36, Z = 1.85, p = 0.07). Plotting the results (Figure 4) indicated that there was no difference in removal rates between open and caged carcass treatments within the spring, summer, or winter seasons. However, average daily removal rates at open carcass treatments (0.5 ± 0.3 [SD]) were slightly faster than caged carcass treatments (0.2 ± 0.1) in autumn, though there was significant variability in this result.

DISCUSSION

A functionally intact ecosystem typically includes a diverse scavenger guild that facilitates the rapid recycling of carcass nutrients (Newsome et al., 2021). In many ecosystems, this process is facilitated by a scavenger guild that is dominated by obligate vertebrate scavengers or large apex facultative scavengers that can rapidly remove carcass biomass, including bones (Cunningham et al., 2018; Mateo‐Tomás et al., 2017). By contrast, Australia's mainland scavenger community is dominated by small‐ to mid‐sized facultative vertebrates, yet their relative contribution to carrion removal remains poorly understood (Finnerty et al., 2024; Vandersteen et al., 2023). Our results show that vertebrate scavengers in an Australian alpine ecosystem have limited capacity to rapidly remove carrion during the colder seasons when insects are less active. This is despite vertebrate scavenging rates being almost twice as high in winter compared to the other seasons. Our results further reveal that the combination of both vertebrate and insect scavengers at carcass sites, compared to just insects, did not accelerate carcass removal in all seasons, and that the presence of vertebrate scavengers at carcass sites did not affect numbers of flies or beetles in any season. Collectively, these results suggest that scavenging efficiency in the alpine study system is predominantly linked to the presence of insects rather than vertebrate scavengers. This finding has important implications for understanding the level and direction of functional redundancy in the scavenger guild, suggesting that in the absence of large‐bodied vertebrate scavengers, insects may play a disproportionately critical role in maintaining carrion recycling processes.

To test the influence of vertebrate scavengers on carcass decomposition, we used an experimental treatment that excluded vertebrates from a subset of carcasses. We predicted that carcass persistence would be longest when vertebrates were excluded, and shortest when both insect and vertebrate scavengers had access—based on prior work suggesting these guilds act complementarily to accelerate carrion decomposition (DeVault et al., 2004). Contrary to this prediction, although some form of vertebrate scavenging occurred on every open carcass we monitored, decomposition rates did not significantly differ between open and caged (vertebrate‐excluded) carcass treatments in summer, winter, or spring, with only a slight increase in open carcass treatments observed in autumn (Figure 4). The fastest decomposition rates in open carcass treatments were also observed in summer—the season with the lowest vertebrate scavenging rates (Figure 2). However, it is possible that even low‐intensity vertebrate scavenging may indirectly enhance insect access by exposing soft tissues without disturbing them too greatly (Flint et al., 2022; Munoz‐Lozano et al., 2019; Selva et al., 2005). Indeed, predation and partial scavenging of sockeye salmon (Oncorhynchus nerka) carcasses by brown bears (Ursus arctos) in southwestern Alaska facilitated scavenging by maggots, and in turn accelerated carcass biomass loss (Meehan et al., 2005). Moreover, warmer conditions may also have driven faster rates of microbial carcass decay (Metcalf et al., 2016).

Although fly abundance at carcasses was highest in autumn compared to other seasons, their potential contribution to carrion decomposition was likely suppressed by the extremely high abundance of ants at carcasses during this season, such as the behaviorally dominant meat ant (Iridomyrmex purpureus) (Appendix S1: Figure S4). Some ants may be extremely active or abundant during autumn (Fellers, 1989; Suwabe et al., 2009), and via predating on (Cairncross et al., 2022; Spencer et al., 2020) or disturbing flies (Barton & Evans, 2017), ants could have disrupted oviposition and the successful development of maggot masses. As a result, the potential for synergistic effects between scavengers in accelerating carrion biomass loss was likely reduced during autumn. Such dynamics are seen elsewhere when ants dominate among insect scavengers (McNatty et al., 2009). This may also account for why autumn exhibited the only increase in carcass removal rates at open compared to caged carcass treatments. Indeed, ant numbers in autumn were substantially lower in open compared to caged carcass treatments, so their effects on flies would have been diminished at open carcass sites. As wholly terrestrial fauna, lower ant numbers in open carcass sites also reflect that ants may be subject to disturbance from vertebrate scavengers (Munoz‐Lozano et al., 2019).

As expected, vertebrate scavenging rates were higher during the cooler months of autumn, winter, and spring compared to summer, likely due to increased energetic demands or reduced availability of alternative food resources during these periods (Finnerty et al., 2024; Selva et al., 2005; Vandersteen et al., 2023). Given the ability of vertebrate scavengers to play key roles in removing carcass biomass (Cunningham et al., 2018), it was anticipated that increased scavenging would lead to faster decomposition in the open carcass treatments. However, most vertebrate scavenging across these seasons was by corvids and brushtail possums (Figure 2). While high scavenging rates by corvids are common both in Australia and globally (Bragato et al., 2022; Mateo‐Tomás et al., 2017), their small size limits their capacity to remove substantial carcass biomass or bones. Similarly, the brushtail possum—though increasingly documented as a scavenger (Finnerty et al., 2024; Vandersteen et al., 2023)—is unlikely to match the functional impact of larger scavengers such as dingoes (Spencer & Newsome, 2021) or wedge‐tailed eagles (Meisuria et al., 2024; Read & Wilson, 2004). As such, it is unsurprising that the dominance of small‐bodied, facultative scavengers led to no consistent significant differences in carcass decomposition between open and caged (vertebrate‐excluded) carcass treatments.

Insect taxa observed were consistent with those documented in previous and nearby studies (Barton & Evans, 2017; Stone et al., 2023), suggesting that our exclusion cages did not inadvertently alter insect access. However, we found notable seasonal differences in insect abundance, with the highest numbers recorded in autumn, followed by summer and spring, and the lowest in winter—patterns largely reflecting the thermal dependency of insect activity (Benbow et al., 2013). Importantly, we found no evidence that vertebrate scavengers influenced fly or beetle abundance, including for up to 12 days post carcass placement in summer. This suggests that vertebrates in this system did not monopolize carcasses or consume biomass at a rate that interfered with access or function of these insects. This indicates that vertebrates were not interfering with oviposition opportunities for flies (Sawyer et al., 2022) or the capacity of beetles to access and consume carrion. This is potentially due to fly and beetle scavengers being able to locate carrion over vast distances (Busse et al., 2025; Evans et al., 2020), thereby enabling them to colonize carcasses efficiently, including before vertebrates arrive. But even after vertebrate arrival, carcass biomass was readily available, reflecting the inability of the vertebrate scavenger guild to rapidly consume the meat and bones, and thus influence insect access. This stands in contrast to systems where apex scavengers can dominate carrion use, and in turn influence insect use of carrion (Bartel et al., 2025).

Although we predicted that carcass persistence would increase in winter due to reduced insect activity, the magnitude of seasonal differences was still striking (Figure 4). In summer, rates of biomass removal were 1.4‐times faster than in spring. Contrastingly, average rates of biomass removal were 2.2‐times less in winter and nearly 3‐times less in autumn than summer (Figure 4). These results indicate that carcasses remained relatively intact in the cooler seasons, despite higher rates of vertebrate scavenging. This pattern contrasts with findings from ecosystems where vertebrates play a more dominant role in winter carrion removal. For example, in a forest in South Carolina, USA, vertebrate scavengers removed carrion more rapidly during winter due to reduced competition from insects and slower microbial decay, enabling vertebrates to capitalize on carcass availability (DeVault et al., 2004). The fact there were no significant differences in carcass persistence between open and caged (vertebrate‐excluded) carcass treatments in winter, summer, and spring further suggests that insects alone can facilitate carrion decomposition, even in the absence of vertebrate scavengers.

Our results have important implications for understanding ecosystem resilience. When multiple species use the same resource, the loss of one can sometimes be buffered by others, helping maintain ecosystem function and stability (Hooper et al., 2005). However, previous research has shown that the loss of obligate or dominant facultative scavengers—such as vultures—often cannot be compensated for by the remaining vertebrates (Hill et al., 2018; Olson et al., 2012). Similarly, limited functional redundancy among vertebrate scavengers has been observed in disturbed ecosystems within Australia where key scavenging raptor species are absent (Huijbers et al., 2015). Yet far less is known about whether vertebrates can compensate for the loss of insect scavengers, or vice versa. Indeed, a recent study in Japan found that while both vertebrates and insects contributed to carrion removal when present, only insects were able to compensate when vertebrate diversity was low (Sugiura & Hayashi, 2018), aligning with our results.

The dominant role of insect scavengers in our study underscores their critical importance in facilitating carrion decomposition. While vertebrate scavenger loss has been linked to increased risks of disease and pest outbreaks (O'Bryan et al., 2018), the equally important roles of insect scavengers are often overlooked (Olea et al., 2019)—despite evidence of widespread declines in insect abundance globally (Wagner et al., 2021). This oversight is especially concerning in ecosystems where vertebrate scavenger communities have already been disrupted, such as through the decline or persecution of apex or obligate scavenging species. In such systems, the additional loss or suppression of insect scavengers may significantly undermine the capacity for carrion biomass recycling, particularly during periods of stress or mass carcass inputs (Barton et al., 2023). Understanding the functional contributions of both insect and vertebrate scavengers is therefore essential, not only for assessing ecosystem resilience but also for informing conservation and management strategies.

For instance, in our study, carcass decomposition was relatively rapid during summer—a period likely to coincide with peak insect activity. Management interventions that generate high carcass loads, such as large‐scale culling of introduced herbivores, may be best timed to coincide with this seasonal window. Conversely, during cooler months when insect activity is low, strategies to bolster the functional role of vertebrate scavengers may be needed to avoid carcass accumulation. Such strategies could include supporting the conservation or recovery of large‐bodied apex scavengers—like dingoes and wedge‐tailed eagles—who can remove substantial carrion biomass. Dingoes, in particular, remain persecuted or excluded from many Australian landscapes, yet when present and actively scavenging, they can significantly accelerate carcass biomass loss (Spencer & Newsome, 2021). Incorporating scavenger functional roles into wildlife and land management planning may therefore enhance ecosystem functioning and help mitigate unintended consequences arising from shifts in scavenger community composition.
