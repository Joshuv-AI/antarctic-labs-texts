Brief Review: Rethinking Colonic Redundancy in Gastroenterology
Authors: Richard Kellermayer, Réka G Szigeti, Xiao Jing Wang, Robert J Shulman, Eamonn M M Quigley, Michael Camilleri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDolichocolon (DC), or colonic redundancy, is an elongated and tortuous colon described as early as 1820, yet it remains underrecognized in clinical gastroenterology. Advances in imaging and motility assessment offer new insights into its prevalence, mechanisms, and clinical implications.AimsTo summarize current evidence on the anatomy, epidemiology, and potential clinical significance of DC and to explore possible pathophysiological mechanisms linking this variant to gastrointestinal disorders.MethodsA targeted literature review of studies published between 1900 and 2024 was conducted using PubMed and Scopus with search terms including dolichocolon, colonic redundancy, and redundant colon. Publications addressing anatomy, motility, symptom associations, and disease relevance were included.ResultsThough epidemiological data are limited, it has been estimated that DC affects 10–20% of the population and is associated with constipation, volvulus, and, possibly, inflammatory bowel disease. Proposed mechanisms include segmental stasis and ischemia in redundant loops, altered neuromuscular signaling, and increased mucosal surface area promoting immune–microbiota interactions. Despite its potential importance, DC is rarely noted in modern radiology reports, contributing to under-recognition in clinical practice.ConclusionsColonic redundancy represents a common anatomic variant with potentially overlooked clinical implications. Focused Review of Dolichocolon in Gastroenterology

Dolichocolon (DC), a diagnostic descriptor of an elongated and redundant colon (an accepted diagnosis in International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision; ICD-10: Q43.8), was first described in 1820 (reviewed in [1]) and highlighted in The Lancet as early as 1926 [2]. Yet, its potential significance remains underexplored. Consensus on the definition of DC, though evolving, includes three subtypes: sigmoid loop displaced cranially relative to the iliac crests (Type 1); transverse colon caudal to the iliac crests (Type 2); and redundant loops at the hepatic or splenic flexure (Type 3) [1, 3]. A universally agreed consensus definition of DC or elongated/redundant colon is a prerequisite to defining the clinical significance of this anatomic variant in contemporary medicine.

In current practice, radiology reports rarely describe the overall configuration of the colon, resulting in limited clinical awareness of colonic redundancy and its possible consequences. Notably, even comprehensive reviews on constipation often omit discussion of colonic configuration, despite depicting regional redundancy in their figures (see Fig. 13A in [4]). In our institutional review of trauma-related abdominal CT scans [5], we found DC in approximately 15% of cases—aligning with population estimates [1], but no comment was made on the radiology reports that DC was present. DC is observed in asymptomatic individuals within the general population, albeit at a substantially lower prevalence (see later) than in patients with functional constipation, suggesting its incomplete penetrance in terms of symptoms and uncertainty regarding their causation. DC, however, is known to predispose to acute colonic volvulus, most commonly in the sigmoid and cecum (as described in clinical case series [6], and emphasized in practice guidelines [7]), but may generate symptoms even without acute torsion. Interestingly, lay public discussions of DC appear in social media (e.g., Reddit, Instagram), highlighting the need for data on the true clinical relevance of this condition beyond the occurrence of acute volvulus.

The goal of this review is to examine the potential clinical significance of DC by summarizing prior literature that implicates its role in various gastrointestinal disorders and by proposing hypotheses on how it may contribute to related symptoms and conditions.

In addition to colonic segmental volvulus, DC is associated with constipation, occurring in more than half of adults with functional constipation [3], and over 70% of infants and toddlers evaluated for constipation or defecation disorders [8], including dyschezia [9]. We recognized the cause vs. effect enigma in our recent publication [8] when considering DC as either the primary origin or a secondary consequence of an increase in distal colonic pressure. A murine model of slow-transit constipation demonstrated that partial outlet obstruction leads to colonic elongation, altered motility patterns, and activation of inhibitory reflex pathways [10]. These rodent findings lend mechanistic support for a reverse causal pathway in which dolichocolon may arise as a secondary adaptation. Rodent colon and murine biology may, however, not capture the complexity of the human large intestine. Additionally, clinical observations in infancy suggest a congenital origin and a plausibly causative role in constipation [8] and dyschezia [9] in this context, at least in young children. Consequently, the age-dependent prevalence variation of DC postnatally has been theorized to occur by both primary and secondary modes of development [8], suggesting that height and body surface area may also impact the changes in the relative length of the colon during growth and aging.

Constipation is considered a hallmark symptom of DC, and often is accompanied by lower abdominal pain, gas, and bloating [1]. Redundancy of the transverse colon (Type 2 DC) is particularly associated with prolonged transverse colon and overall transit delay, both of which are significantly associated with transverse colon length [11]. Supporting this association, Yik et al. [12] reported that transverse colon elongation was significantly more prevalent in children with slow-transit constipation than in those with normal or accelerated transit. Similarly, Vattimo and colleagues [13] demonstrated, using serial scintigraphy, that all constipated children with DC in their cohort had significantly prolonged total and segmental colonic transit times compared with 44% of children with clinical constipation but normal colonic morphology. Collectively, these findings suggest that anatomical variations in colonic length and configuration may complicate or contribute to the development of constipation. We acknowledge, however, that in most cases of constipation—particularly among adults—the distinction between DC as a causative factor or as a secondary consequence remains unclear [8]. Given that DC exists in asymptomatic, healthy individuals, we propose that it is unlikely to be independently causative in most patients but may function as a risk-modifying factor for constipation and potentially other gastrointestinal disorders.

Recent case reports have highlighted DC not only in functional constipation disorders but also in acute abdominal pain mimicking appendicitis in a young adult [14]; obstructive nephropathy in a 19-day-old neonate [15]; recurrent episodes of transient pseudo-obstruction manifesting as post-exertional right upper quadrant pain associated with non-bloody diarrhea in a 42 year old [16]; cecal volvulus in a 14-year-old patient with acute spasmodic periumbilical pain, followed by bilious emesis [17]; a 56-year-old patient with chronic cough and abdominal pain, attributed to gastroesophageal reflux disease refractory to medical management [18]. Partial large intestinal resection—performed in all but the infant—led to resolution of symptoms, supporting the role of DC in dysfunctions leading to these diverse clinical presentations. Emerging data also hint that DC is more common in children with ulcerative colitis than in Crohn’s disease and non-inflammatory bowel disease controls and may influence the anatomic localization and persistence of ulcerative colitis [5]. A recent study also proposed that Type 1 DC (rectosigmoid redundancy) may increase distal rectosigmoid pressure and serve as an underrecognized anatomic co-morbidity in pediatric perianal Crohn’s disease [19].

Developmental Origins of Colon Length Variation: An Integrative Model

It is currently believed that colon length is primarily genetically determined during fetal development, with ethnic variation established in utero and modulated postnatally by maternal factors, the microbiome, dietary fiber intake, sex hormones, and body habitus. Connective tissue integrity likely influences not only colonic length but also mesenteric laxity and colonic wall compliance, though direct measurement of colon length in connective tissue disorder populations remains a significant gap in the literature. How Might Dolichocolon Contribute to Symptoms?

Several non-mutually exclusive, potentially synergistic, hypothesis-generating mechanisms may link DC with disordered gut–brain interaction and neuromuscular dysfunction (Fig. 1). These include (i) intermittent regional ischemic stress, (ii) altered viscero-visceral reflex modulation (including cologastric inhibitory pathways), and (iii) perturbations of epithelial barrier function.

Redundant colonic loops may intermittently angulate or transiently kink, particularly at vascular “watershed” regions [20], predisposing to low-grade, localized hypoperfusion and repetitive subclinical ischemic stress (Fig. 1B). Indeed, increased colonic length has been associated with kinking and high-curvature (< 90°) angulation. Hanson et al.[21] compared CT colonography (CTC) findings in 100 patients with incomplete colonoscopy versus 100 with complete colonoscopy and found significantly more acute-angle flexures, greater total colorectal length (mean 210.8 vs. 167.0 cm), and longer sigmoid and transverse segments and advanced diverticular disease in the incomplete group. Hochstein et al.[22] used 3D virtual reality reconstruction and found that technically failed colonoscopies had significantly more colonic angles (17.7 ± 3.2 vs. 12.7 ± 2.4, p = 0.008) and longer sigmoid colons (71 vs. 35 cm, p = 0.01). Weber et al.[23] developed automated software to quantify colonic tortuosity on CTC, defining it as the number of high-curvature points < 90°. The sigmoid and transverse segments were the most tortuous (mean 3.39 and 2.75 high-curvature points, respectively), with high inter-patient variability. However, no specific threshold for the number of such flexures has been validated as a clinical cutoff for predicting either procedural failure or symptom generation. Nevertheless, in parallel to kinking, mechanical distension and altered wall tension may modify afferent signaling and central integration of viscero-visceral reflexes, enhancing inhibitory cologastric and enterogastric pathways and thereby slowing gastric emptying and proximal small intestinal transit. These effects may contribute to postprandial fullness, bloating, and dyspeptic symptoms (Fig. 1A). Consistent with this biological plausibility, a statistically significant—although quantitatively modest—difference in 4-h gastric emptying has been reported in patients with constipation and DC compared with those without redundancy [11]. While not establishing causality, this observation supports the concept of altered gut–brain–axis signaling in this anatomical context.

Repetitive ischemic stress may, in principle, promote epithelial barrier dysfunction, increasing paracellular permeability and facilitating translocation of microbial products [24]. In addition, an expanded and tortuous mucosal surface area (Fig. 1C) could alter spatial and temporal dynamics of host–microbiota interactions [25], thereby modulating mucosal immune tone and inflammatory signaling thresholds.

Importantly, these mechanisms should be viewed within the framework of biological stochasticity and inter-individual variability that characterize human gastrointestinal physiology [26, 27]. Neural circuit excitability, smooth muscle responsiveness, immune activation thresholds, epithelial barrier integrity, and microbial community structure all exhibit probabilistic, non-deterministic behavior across individuals and over time [28, 29]. Within this systems-biology context, DC is conceptualized as a risk-modifying anatomical substrate that interacts with stochastic epigenetic, neuroimmune, and microbiome-driven processes to influence symptom emergence, rather than as a deterministic cause of disease.

Spiegel recently proposed that the downward force of gravity can adversely affect gastrointestinal function and may be a contributor to irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) [30], and conceivably to the documented prolongation of transverse colon retention, and overall colonic transit delay [11]. He has emphasized that an elongated, redundant, and tortuous mesenteric-visceral complex might predispose to dysmotility, kinking, stasis, altered microbiome, and thus IBS symptoms. In line with this concept, transverse colon length, given its known relationship with storage time and constipation [31], was greatest in those with functional constipation or IBS constipation [32].

Practical Clinical Research Studies to Test Proposed Mechanisms

To move these hypotheses toward testable biology, pragmatic clinical study designs could be applied (Fig. 2). Intermittent regional ischemic stress could be explored by correlating colonic configuration on routine abdominal CT or MRI with segmental perfusion patterns using contrast-enhanced imaging or near-infrared spectroscopy surrogates. Altered viscero-visceral reflex modulation could be assessed by paired gastric emptying and colonic transit studies, combined with high-resolution antro-duodenal and colonic manometry to evaluate reflex timing and strength. Perturbations of epithelial barrier function could be examined through non-invasive permeability assays (e.g., lactulose–mannitol tests), fecal biomarkers of barrier disruption (calprotectin, zonulin), and microbiome profiling, stratified by colonic length and redundancy. These approachable, low-risk clinical research frameworks may allow hypothesis-driven exploration of how normal anatomic variability in colonic configuration acts as a probabilistic modifier of gut–brain and neuromuscular function rather than a deterministic disease state.

Practical Clinical Considerations

We acknowledge that DC is widely regarded as a clinically insignificant consequence of constipation [33, 34] rather than a contributing etiologic factor. However, significant knowledge gaps persist, and this work calls attention to the need for systematic, preferably prospective, investigation. The following points merit consideration:

Defining and Reporting DC

Objective, age- and body-size-adjusted definitions of DC are needed before its clinical relevance can be established. Once standardized, colonic configuration should be systematically reported in clinical radiology. Notably, the most reliable assessments of colon length and redundancy require contrast-enema-delivered studies (CE, CTC), which cannot be ethically performed in healthy asymptomatic controls—particularly children. Despite this inherent limitation, contrast enema studies in patients with constipation or other disorders of gut–brain interaction (DGBIs) may aid colonoscopy planning when technical difficulty reaching the cecum is anticipated.

Asymptomatic Carriers Do Not Negate Clinical Relevance

The presence of DC in asymptomatic individuals [33] does not exclude a role for DC in predisposing to constipation. An instructive parallel is HLA-DQ2/DQ8 in celiac disease: these alleles are carried by 30–45% of Western populations, yet only 1–3% develop celiac disease. Most carriers remain healthy, but no one disputes HLA-DQ2/DQ8 as the most important genetic determinant of celiac disease. Disease expression requires additional factors beyond the predisposing variant. By the same logic, DC in asymptomatic individuals is consistent with a contributory role for DC in which dietary, autonomic, and pelvic floor factors determine clinical expression.

An Understudied Entity

Since 1946, only 120 publications indexed in PubMed contain the term “dolichocolon,” underscoring how little systematic attention this entity has received. The question of cause versus effect remains unresolved and warrants investigation rather than dismissal. Even if DC is a consequence of constipation or pelvic dyssynergia, it may serve as a self-reinforcing complication against which targeted bowel regimens, gravity-based interventions, or surgical approaches could prove effective. In our pediatric practice, direct visualization of colon redundancy on CE has demonstrably improved family compliance with stool softeners, stimulant laxatives, and dietary modifications—a clinically meaningful outcome regardless of mechanism.

Unmet Burden of DGBIs

Approximately 40% of the global population affected by DGBIs continue to experience inadequately managed symptoms [35]. DC should, at the very least, be considered as a possible contributor to symptoms in DGBIs.

Conclusions

In summary, while DC is commonly identified in patients with functional gastrointestinal disorders, its role—whether as a passive anatomical variant or an active contributor to symptom generation—remains to be established. The associations and conceptual framework presented here are intended to encourage further investigation rather than to assert causality. Prospective studies incorporating standardized imaging criteria and functional assessments will be essential to clarify the clinical significance of DC.
