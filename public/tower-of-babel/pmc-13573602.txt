Single-cell-level perturbation-induced and condition-related signal estimation with batch effect removal using NDreamer
Authors: Xiao Xiao, Hongyu Zhao, Zuoheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdvances in sequencing technologies and the growing volume of single-cell data have created unprecedented opportunities for uncovering gene expression patterns causally induced by experimental perturbations or statistically associated, but not necessarily causal, with disease conditions. However, current analytical methods inadequately account for batch effects and data sparsity or fail to capture the inherent non-linearity in single-cell data, leading to biased estimation. To address these limitations, we developed NDreamer that combines neural discrete representation learning and matching to remove batch effects and estimate perturbation-induced or condition-associated signals at single-cell resolution. NDreamer outperformed existing methods by using mutual information loss on discrete latent variables to disentangle cells’ intrinsic features from conditions or batch effects, while preserving both global and local variance within batches and conditions via triplet and local neighborhood loss. We applied NDreamer to multiple datasets across platforms, organs, and species and validated and benchmarked its performance in removing batch effects and estimating perturbation-induced or condition-associated signals. In particular, we applied NDreamer to an Alzheimer’s disease cohort, revealing biologically relevant gene expression patterns that distinguish dementia patients from controls.

Introduction

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has become a cornerstone technology for studying cellular heterogeneity and detecting gene expression patterns at cell-type level. Research has been conducted to identify gene expression changes induced by perturbations, including genetic modifications [1], chemical treatments [2], and environmental stimuli [3]. Advanced technologies such as perturb-seq [1, 4] have further enabled us to study cellular responses to modifications in disease-related risk genes across diverse cell types.

In single-cell studies, accurately quantifying the influence of perturbations on individual cells and studying how these responses vary across cellular populations remain challenging, since resolving this heterogeneity is essential for uncovering causal mechanisms of cellular regulation and for enabling context-specific therapeutic interventions. It is well known that cells with distinct intrinsic features, such as cell type, subtype, cell cycle stage, microenvironment, chromatin accessibility, and other pre-existing states, respond differently to perturbations. Here, we define the perturbation-induced effect in each cell as the individual treatment effect (ITE) and its intrinsic features as effect modifiers in the causal inference framework [5]. Since we only observe the perturbation label and gene expression of each cell, it is difficult to dissect intrinsic variability from perturbation-induced effects without direct information on intrinsic features. Estimating ITE is further complicated by the fact that we only observe a cell’s transcriptome under one perturbation condition and do not observe its counterfactual state. Counterfactual causal inference approaches are helpful for estimating ITE in perturbation studies.

Early approaches rely on aggregating single-cell data to pseudo-bulk data and compare the expression of cells in the perturbed condition with cells in the control condition. They average the expression of cells of the same type in each sample, failing to capture fine-grained heterogeneity and can produce different results depending on the chosen aggregation resolutions. For example, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells respond differently to CD28 costimulation, where CD4+ T cells sustain proliferation, whereas CD8+ T cells show a limited response [6]. Thus, aggregating all T cells could mask these subtype-specific responses, leading to insignificant results in the analysis of T cell responses. Later methods estimate single-cell-level ITE using single-cell digital twins and generative models [7, 8] or counterfactual matching based on perturbation-independent factors [3, 9]. While these models have advanced the single-cell ITE estimation by identifying and disentangling perturbation-independent factors and isolating perturbation-related ITE responses from the gene expression matrix, they have several limitations. First, the unknown or subtle bias [10, 11] in the expression profile generated by the generative model [7, 8] can impact the ITE estimation and downstream analysis. Second, the inherent non-linearity in single-cell data makes linear decomposition [12] not suitable for analysis. Third, independence measured by correlation coefficients does not guarantee independence, and the discriminator in adversarial training to establish independence may not rigorously lead to independence under various hyperparameter settings. Lastly, it is unknown if the distance in the perturbation-independent factor space is distorted, thus, the causal weighting or matching in the downstream analysis may not be valid.

In single-cell studies with multiple batches, the estimation of ITE can also be biased due to batch effects which previous ITE estimation methods did not account for. A large number of methods have been developed to remove batch effects [12, 13]. Although many existing methods effectively learn cell embeddings that are largely insensitive to batch effects, most of them do not output denoised gene expression profiles. Here, denoised expression refers to reconstructed or corrected expression values in which technical noise and batch-associated variation are reduced while condition-related biological signals are preserved. This distinction is important because batch-invariant embeddings can support downstream integration, but they do not provide directly interpretable gene-level expression estimates. Moreover, some methods may overcorrect the data, removing condition-related signals together with unwanted batch effects [12].

Moreover, large-scale scRNA-seq studies collected on human samples comparing disease patients and healthy controls hold great promise for identifying disease (condition)-associated gene programs and biological pathways across cell types, shedding light on disease mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets. However, estimating single-cell-level condition-related signals (CRS) still remains challenging. Here, CRS can be viewed as ITE when comparing between disease and control conditions, but can only be interpreted as correlations without causal relationships.

To address these challenges, we propose NDreamer (Neural Discrete learning for decomposing condition-Related or perturbation-induced signals, Effect modifiers, And Measurement ERror in scRNA-seq), to remove batch effects and estimate perturbation-induced or condition-associated signals at single-cell resolution. NDreamer can be applied to single-cell experiments, including multiple perturbations or condition labels with or without batches, and estimate the ITE/CRS for each cell and the conditional average treatment effect (CATE) or conditional average condition-related signals (CACRS) for specific cell populations (Fig. 1a). We benchmarked NDreamer against existing single-cell-level condition-related signal estimation methods as well as batch effect-denoising methods and showed that NDreamer outperformed all current methods. Applied to datasets across sequencing platforms, organs, diseases, and species, NDreamer effectively identified heterogeneous responses of cells to genetic editing, chemical treatments, and disease-associated gene expression patterns across conditions. We also applied NDreamer to an Alzheimer’s disease snRNA-seq dataset and uncovered disease-related expression patterns across cell types in the middle temporal gyrus (MTG).

Overview of the NDreamer deep learning model

Effectively extracting batch- and condition-independent effect modifiers [3] while preserving within-batch and within-condition biological variation, without introducing distortions, remains challenging. Moreover, to make sure that the transformation of the condition- and batch-independent effect modifier embedding is not an arbitrary transformation, we assume that the effect modifier space should contain the information of within-batch and within-condition between-cell variance, where the within-batch and within-condition local and global distance structures should be retained in the effect modifier embeddings. To address this, we employed neural discrete representation learning [14, 15], which generates categorical latent variables from the input expression profiles. Following discretization, a subsequent neural network transformation was applied to produce the final effect modifier embedding as a continuous vector. To constrain the embedding space and reduce distance distortions, we applied the variational inference techniques [16] to the final effect modifier embedding and used a neural network decoder to reconstruct the expression (Fig. 2a).

Then a decoder is used to reconstruct the expression. For the categorical latent variables, we apply the independent and dependent loss using normalized mutual information. For the effect modifier embedding, we applied the triplet loss and the local neighborhood loss to preserve biological conservation. (b) Graphical illustration of the triplet loss function. To ensure that the effect modifier embedding is independent of both batch and condition so that it can be used for matching and estimating ITE or CRS [3], we first minimize the normalized mutual information between the categorical latent variable and the batch and condition (Fig. 2a). By decoupling the categorical latent variable from the batch and condition, the transformation of the categorical latent variable, the final effect modifier embedding, also becomes independent of these variables. Compared to the correlation-coefficient-based independence measurements as well as discriminator-based independence constraints [17], the mutual information-based approach can more robustly capture complex independence patterns (Supplementary Notes S3).

After establishing independence through mutual information loss, we applied three loss functions to preserve within-batch and within-condition biological variation while preventing distortions. Firstly, the selection of the categorical latent variable was designed to depend on the unsupervised clustering patterns observed in the raw expression space within each condition and batch. Secondly, we employed a triplet loss to ensure that the distance between cells within the same high-resolution unsupervised cluster remained consistently smaller than the distance between cells from different low-resolution unsupervised clusters [18]. Together, these two loss functions safeguard the global biological structure. Beyond preserving global structure, we implemented a local neighborhood loss to maintain local structure, ensuring that the local neighborhood structures in the effect modifier space mirrored those in the raw expression space (Fig. 2c).

Results

NDreamer demonstrates robustly improved performance across experimental perturbation datasets

To systematically evaluate the performance of NDreamer with existing single-cell-level methods for perturbation-effect analysis, we conducted comprehensive benchmarking across diverse datasets across different sequencing platforms, species, and organs. Our comparison involves a set of currently state-of-the-art models, including CINEMA-OT [3], Mixscape [9], scCAPE [17] (code was downloaded from pip on 17th November 2024), and scGen [7] (implemented by pertpy [2], code was downloaded on 13th December 2024). We applied the default hyperparameter settings provided by their GitHub tutorial page for these methods across all datasets. We treated the perturbation- and batch-free embeddings for previous methods as their estimated effect modifier embeddings. Since the ground truth of ITE is absent in real datasets, our comparison is based on two categories of metrics calculated from the effect modifier embedding, which are also used in the evaluation in previous studies [3, 9]:

Condition label-mixing performance in the effect modifier space. Given that the intrinsic characteristics of cells prior to any perturbation are independent of the perturbation label, the extracted effect modifier embeddings for cells in the two conditions (perturbation versus control), if plotted together, should be well-mixed. Of note that here we refer the perturbation and control as conditions. Borrowing the ideas from the systematic batch-effect removal benchmarking papers [13, 20], this can be evaluated by batch-mixing metrics, where different perturbations are considered as different batches here. Specifically, we use the widely used metrics of average silhouette width (ASW_batch) [20], k-nearest-neighbor batch-effect test (kBET) [21], batch local inverse Simpson’s index (bLISI) [22], and True Positive Rate [23] for batch-mixing evaluation.

Conservation of biological information for the effect modifier space. The extracted effect modifier embeddings should preserve the intrinsic features and within-perturbation variations of cells, which can be characterized by cell type annotation. Therefore, we employed the classical label conservation metrics to evaluate local neighborhoods (1 minus cell-type LISI, cLISI) [22], global cluster alignment (Adjusted Rand Index (ARI), geometric normalized mutual information (NMI)), and relative distances (cell-type ASW) [13].

In addition to these metrics, we computed an overall score by linearly scaling the values of each metric to a range of 0 to 1 and then averaging the scaled values across all metrics. We used five publicly available datasets for the benchmarking: (1) The peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) dataset, including 16, 893 cells from IFN-beta-stimulated and control conditions [24]. (2) The rhinovirus infection (virus) dataset where we compared the 12, 255 cells between the mock condition and the exposure to 2% cigarette-smoke extract perturbation [3]. (3) A small PBMC stimulation (PBMC1) dataset, which contains 5027 cells from the stimulated and control conditions [3]. (4) The ECCITE dataset, containing 20 729 cells of three annotated cell cycle phases (G1, G2M, S) obtained from three technical replicates measured using ECCITE-seq with 25 target genes generated from the stimulated THP-1 cell line [9]. (5) The autism spectrum disorder (ASD) dataset containing brain cells with 35 neurodevelopmental delay (ND) risk gene mutations from 18 batches measured by Perturb-Seq [1].

We begin with the PBMC dataset. As illustrated in Fig. 3a, NDreamer achieves the highest overall score, far ahead of other methods, and provides the most satisfactory uniform manifold approximation and projection [25] (UMAP) visualization with clear cell type separation and condition-mixing (Fig. S1). It demonstrates the clearest cell type separation and the most effective mixing of cells from two different conditions. Notably, NDreamer is the only method that successfully captures the variation between dendritic cells and other cell types. Among the other methods, CINEMA-OT achieves the second-best performance in mixing cells across conditions; however, it incorrectly splits a single cluster of CD4 T cells into two subgroups and fails to clearly distinguish between CD14+ Mono, FCGR3A+ Mono, and dendritic cells. The remaining three methods, while achieving relatively good cell type separation, fail to integrate cells from the two conditions effectively.

We then compared NDreamer to other methods using the virus and PBMC1 datasets. As shown in Fig. 3b and c, Supplementary Figures S2 and S3, NDreamer consistently outperformed the other methods. When averaging biological conservation and condition-mixing performance across all benchmarked methods on the three datasets, NDreamer emerged as the only method that achieved strong performance in both aspects (Fig. 3d). In contrast, Mixscape demonstrated limited condition-mixing capability, while the other three methods showed inadequate performance in preserving biological conservation.

However, in some single-cell experimental perturbation studies, multiple batches are present, and batch effects can significantly influence analysis results. This challenge is further amplified in genetic perturbation studies, where datasets often contain tens of genetic mutations. Previous methods, which only support comparisons between two conditions and lack batch-effect removal functionality, are less applicable in such scenarios. We illustrate this using the ECCITE and ASD datasets.

As shown in Fig. S4a, the raw data of the ECCITE dataset exhibit pronounced batch effects, with clear separation between replication 1 and the other replications. We compared NDreamer (with batch-effect removal enabled) against four other methods in comparing genetically perturbed and unperturbed cells. In Fig. 3g, CINEMA-OT fails to mitigate batch effects, displaying a clear batch-related separation in the effect modifier space.

To systematically evaluate these methods when batch effects exist, we computed condition mixing, biological conservation, and batch-effect removal performance with the same metrics for condition mixing but applied to batches instead. As shown in Fig. 3e and f, NDreamer outperformed all other methods when these three metrics were combined, delivering embeddings with excellent condition-mixing and batch-mixing performance (Fig. S4 and S5). In addition, we also benchmarked these methods by treating each genetic mutation as a condition and enabling batch-effect removal, if applicable, where we found that NDreamer still outperformed all other methods (Fig. S6). The detailed results of the experimental perturbation study benchmarking are available in Supplementary Table S1.

Systematic evaluation of NDreamer: Does it produce condition-related signal-preserved expression with batch effects removed?

Evaluation metrics

In addition to extracting effect modifiers and estimating ITE in experimental perturbation studies, NDreamer can also be applied to observational studies to denoise condition-related expressions from raw expressions influenced by batch effects and estimate CRS. To systematically evaluate its performance, we benchmarked NDreamer against current state-of-the-art models, including CellANOVA [12], scDisInFact [26], AIF [27], and scDML [28], using the default hyperparameter settings for these methods. To systematically evaluate which method can produce denoised expressions that are both free from batch effects and preserve condition-related signals, drawing inspiration from previous studies [12], we conducted benchmarking based on four evaluation aspects (Fig. 4a):

Condition-related signal preservation for denoised expression. In single-cell studies, cells from different conditions (e.g. disease versus healthy control) are expected to exhibit distinct differences, even when they belong to the same cell type or share similar intrinsic features. Capturing these cross-condition differences is crucial for understanding condition-specific biological variation. To assess whether these differences are preserved after integration, same as CellANOVA, we first identify each cell’s 30 out-of-batch nearest neighbors (oobNNs) within the integrated embedding. Out-of-batch nearest neighbors are defined as cells from other batches (or samples) that are closest to a given cell in the integrated space. This approach ensures that comparisons are not confounded by batch-specific biases. For each cell, we then calculate the proportion of its oobNNs that belong to the same condition as the cell itself. If a cell represents a state with condition-specific differences, a higher proportion of its oobNNs is expected to come from the same condition. For quantitative comparison, we aggregate these proportions across cells and stratify them by condition, visualizing their density distributions. Effective integration is indicated by a higher proportion of neighbors belonging to the same condition as the central cell, reflecting the successful recovery of condition-specific signals. Conversely, failure to capture these signals results in uniform distributions, lacking enrichment for same-condition neighbors. To formalize this, we construct a neighboring cell condition versus central cell condition matrix. The value at the i-th row and j-th column in this matrix represents the average proportion of oobNNs from the j-th condition for cells in the i-th condition. The trace of this matrix, normalized by the number of rows, serves as a performance measure: a value closer to 1 indicates that the matrix closely resembles an identity matrix, signifying better recovery of condition-specific signals.

Data distortion in the denoised expression. While denoised data inevitably differ from raw expression data, it is critical that denoising does not distort major biological differences already present within each condition and batch. Because cell-type-specific expression patterns are expected to be largely preserved after appropriate denoising, we used differentially expressed gene (DEG) concordance between raw and denoised data as a data-distortion check. For example, a marker gene for a given cell type in the raw data should remain a marker for the same cell type after denoising, rather than being attenuated, lost, or spuriously assigned to another cell type. Specifically, DEGs between one cell type and all other cells within each condition-batch combination (CBC) should remain as consistent as possible between raw and denoised expressions. To ensure a fair comparison, raw and denoised expressions were analyzed using the same DEG pipeline, including the same cell labels, CBC stratification, gene set, statistical test, and Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) adjustment. We computed the BH-adjusted P-values for each gene in each cell-type-by-CBC comparison for both raw and denoised data. The Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) between the logits of adjusted P-values from raw and denoised data was calculated for each comparison, with the mean PCC across all CBCs serving as the first metric. In addition, because a method could preserve DEG ranking while changing the scale of statistical significance, we evaluated DEG classification consistency using an adjusted P-value cutoff of 0.05. Using DEGs identified from raw expression data as the reference and those from denoised data as predictions, we calculated prediction accuracy and F1 score as the second metric.

Real datasets

Besides the above-mentioned ECCITE and ASD datasets, we also used three additional large datasets with obvious batch effects (Fig. S6) to evaluate the batch-effect-removal and condition-related-signal-preservation performance: (1) The type 1 diabetes (T1D) dataset that contains 69,645 cells from 24 batches under three conditions (11 healthy individuals, 5 individuals with T1D, and 8 individuals with no clinical presentation of T1D but positive for β-cell autoantibodies (AAB)) [29]. (2) The human kidney multiomics atlas (kidney) dataset containing 282,610 cells from 36 donors and 47 batches with 24 ‘Control’ batches and 23 ‘Disease’ batches, where 29 of them are scRNA-seq data, and 18 of them are snRNA-seq data. The analysis of this dataset includes multi-modal data integration [30]. (3) The mouse radiation therapy (mouse) dataset containing 91,752 cells from the epithelial and lamina propria layers of the intestinal segments sequenced at 4 distinct times by two separate technical groups from mice under the control and radiation-treated conditions [12]. We will focus on illustrating the T1D dataset in the following paragraph (Fig. S7).

We first evaluated the condition-related signal preservation. As shown in Fig. 4a and b, the oobNNs for NDreamer’s denoised expression are predominantly from the same condition, resulting in a neighboring cell condition versus central cell condition matrix that closely resembles an identity matrix, with a trace similarity of 0.973. In contrast, the other two methods exhibit less effective separation between conditions, with CellANOVA achieving a trace similarity of 0.442 and scDisInFact achieving a trace similarity of 0.624.

Next, we evaluated the extent of data distortion. We began by visualizing the distribution of PCCs between the adjusted P-values of genes in the raw and denoised expressions for each cell type and CBC. As shown in Fig. 4c, NDreamer achieved the highest mean and median PCC values, outperforming both CellANOVA and scDisInFact.

We further benchmarked NDreamer against previous methods using the metrics of batch-mixing performance within each condition for denoised expression, batch-condition-mixing performance for the effect modifier embeddings, and biological conservation for the effect modifier embeddings. The results are presented in Fig. 4d. NDreamer outperforms all prior methods across all evaluation metrics, with the sole exception of a marginally lower biological conservation score than CellANOVA for the effect-modifier embeddings.

We further benchmarked NDreamer against CellANOVA, scDisInFact, and AIF using additional datasets, including the ECCITE, ASD, kidney, and mouse datasets, and calculated the overall scores. Due to the absence of fine-grained cell-type annotations for the mouse dataset, biological conservation metrics were not evaluated for this dataset. Across all datasets, NDreamer consistently outperformed the other methods with the highest overall scores (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Table S2).

Simulation dataset

Besides real datasets, we also performed a simulation study where we know the ground truth ITE/CRS and the ground truth denoised expression to benchmark NDreamer with previous studies. To generate the simulation data, we first used Symsim [31] to generate the baseline gene expression matrix, where cells are designed to come from different cell types. We then randomly assign cells to six batches: three assigned to the control condition and three assigned to the perturbed condition. Then, conditioning on the cell type information, perturbation-induced effects are added, where 60% of the genes are randomly set to be up- or down-regulated with three levels of regulation. The perturbation-induced effects are then added to the baseline expression for cells in the perturbation group. After that, batch effects are added to the expression matrix using Symsim, and the raw counts are generated through negative-binomial sampling.

We compare NDreamer with CellANOVA, AIF, scDML, scDisInFact, scCAPE, Mixscape, scGen, and CINEMA-OT on the simulation dataset using the five evaluation metrics illustrated above, if applicable. As shown in Fig. 4f, NDreamer outperforms all previous methods across nearly all metrics, with only moderately strong performance on biological conservation in the effect modifier space, and achieves the highest overall score. We then compared the predicted denoised expression with the ground truth using the mean gene-wise PCC. For each gene, we computed the PCC between the predicted and ground-truth denoised expression vectors across all cells, and then averaged the PCCs across genes. A higher average PCC indicates a more accurate estimation. As shown in Fig. 4g, NDreamer achieved the highest average PCC compared with CellANOVA, scDisInFact, and AIF. For the estimated single-cell-level perturbation-induced signal, we compared the estimated values with the ground truth using mean squared error, where a lower MSE indicates better estimation. NDreamer also achieved the lowest MSE compared with CINEMA-OT and Mixscape.

Testing for skewness

A critical requirement for the estimated ITE is that it should not be skewed, where the ITE values for one gene for cells in a certain group should be symmetric around 0 when there is no systematic biological difference in the expression level of this gene in this cell group between two conditions. To test this, we compared the ITEs estimated by NDreamer to those of CINEMA-OT, an innovative and powerful method for estimating single-cell-level ITE causal effect, calculating BH-adjusted P-values for the ITEs of CD8T cells using a nonparametric method to test if ITEs are significantly larger or smaller than 0 using the above-mentioned method. As shown in Fig. 6b, NDreamer identified only 25 genes with adjusted P-values less than 0.05. However, CINEMA-OT reported 1971 out of 2000 highly variable genes as significant. This implausibly high number likely reflects skewness in CINEMA-OT's ITE estimation. We then visualized the two most significant genes with the lowest adjusted P-values (Fig. 6c) by plotting the UMAP colored by the expression level of these genes and the distribution of ITEs for these genes in CD8T cells. Genes identified by NDreamer exhibited clear differences between the two conditions, while those identified by CINEMA-OT appeared to lack meaningful significance, likely arising from skewness. To further investigate, we randomly permuted condition labels for each cell and re-ran both methods. In this scenario, where significant P-values should be rare and driven only by random chance, NDreamer identified only two genes with adjusted P-values below 0.05 out of 2000 HVGs. In contrast, CINEMA-OT still reported 1977 genes as significant (Fig. 6d). This finding strongly suggests that the ITEs estimated by CINEMA-OT may be skewed towards negative.

Nearest neighbor matching is all you need

Nearest neighbor matching, optimal transport matching, and k-nearest neighbor weighting are three widely used methods for covariate adjustment in estimating ITEs in causal inference. However, optimal transport and k-nearest neighbor weighting methods may introduce additional skewness due to the sparsity and high proportion of zeros in single-cell data. Calculating counterfactual cells using (weighted) averages of multiple cells can result in a loss of sparsity in the counterfactual cells, potentially introducing skewness when subtracting the counterfactual expression from the reference cell expression. As a result, theoretically, any weighting method, if not one-to-one matching, would introduce the skewness towards the negative direction in the ITE estimation in scRNA-seq data, as shown in the ITEs of CINEMA-OT, where most ITEs are negative. A detailed proof of why weighting-based methods are skewed is provided in Supplementary Notes S4. In addition, since cells from different conditions and batches are well-mixed in the NDreamer-produced effect modifier space, there is no need to use optimal transport to adjust for the imperfect condition mixing performance as in previous methods.

Ablation study

To identify the key components of NDreamer’s deep learning model, we conducted an ablation study using the PBMC dataset (Fig. 6e, Supplementary Figure S10). The study shows that each loss function contributes to either condition-mixing performance or biological conservation. Specifically, removing the independent loss function (No_Independent) significantly decreases condition-mixing performance, demonstrating that the mutual information-based independent loss effectively identifies condition-independent effect modifiers. Similarly, eliminating the triplet loss (No_Triplet), local neighborhood loss (No_Neighbor), dependent loss (No_Dependent), or all three structure-related loss functions (No_Structure-Related) results in a notable reduction in biological conservation metrics. This highlights the critical role of these losses in preserving local and global biological structure. Moreover, comparing NDreamer to the original VQ-VAE model reveals a dramatic improvement across all metrics. This demonstrates that the inclusion of these loss functions successfully adapts NDreamer for causal inference in single-cell data. We also tested the sensitivity of NDreamer to the number of neighbors in the local neighborhood loss. As shown in Supplementary Table S5, the model’s performance is not sensitive to the change of the number of neighbors, while when it is set to 20, it achieves the highest average score of NMI and kBET.

Discussion

Advances in single-cell sequencing techniques and the growing volume of single-cell data have created unprecedented opportunities for uncovering statistically significant gene expression patterns induced by perturbations or associated with specific disease conditions. However, existing analytical approaches face significant limitations. Many methods for estimating experimental effects at the single-cell level are either skewed or ineffective at extracting condition-independent effect modifiers while preserving biological variation. Moreover, they struggle to scale for large experimental studies involving multiple batches with batch effects. Similarly, current batch-effect removal methods often overlook the non-linear characteristics of single-cell data, fail to achieve adequate batch- and condition-mixing performance, or remove the condition-related signals while correcting for batch.

To address these challenges, we developed NDreamer, a robust framework designed to denoise expression profiles while preserving condition-related signals and effectively estimating individual treatment effects at the single-cell level. Through the combination of loss functions that ensure batch- and condition-independent while preserving global and local biological structure, NDreamer outperformed existing methods across all datasets. Additionally, with optimized tensor computation designs for the mutual information loss and the triplet loss calculations, NDreamer achieves exceptional computational efficiency, finishing model training on the PBMC dataset (2 conditions, 16,893 cells) in under one minute and on the large SEA-AD dataset (1 378 211 cells, 89 batches) in 3837 seconds (Supplementary Table S6) with GPU memory less than 8GB when the batch size is set to 2048. The detailed summary of the running time and GPU usage for all datasets analyzed in the manuscript is provided in Supplementary Table S6. The GPU memory increases linearly with batch size, and the computation time linearly increases with the number of batches, the number of conditions, and the number of cells in the dataset.

However, it is essential to carefully examine the data before applying NDreamer. For causal interpretation in experimental perturbation studies, NDreamer relies on an effect-modifier representation that is minimally dependent on condition and batch. In observational disease-control studies, however, the estimated CRS should be interpreted as condition-associated rather than causal. In some cases, however, the true effect modifier may not be entirely independent of these factors, where NDreamer may not be ideal in such cases. For instance, in the ASD dataset, all cells of the "Vascular" cell type are unperturbed, making the condition inherently linked to the intrinsic features of the cell. Similarly, in the kidney dataset, certain cell types are common in the scRNA-seq modality but are rare in the snRNA-seq modality. Without additional information beyond the expression matrix, condition annotations, and batch annotations, these dependencies cannot be fully resolved based on information theory principles. We also acknowledge that, in the comparison of scRNA-seq data between patients and healthy controls, the effect modifier may not be fully independent of the condition or batch (e.g. there might be cell type composition differences between patients and healthy controls). In those cases, the three loss functions in NDreamer are designed to retain biological conservation, which will help correct this unwanted correlation. The model can still effectively extract the effect modifiers while preserving biological conservation, but the biological conservation metrics may inevitably experience a slight decrease. A practical suggestion in these situations is to exclude cells that inherently carry batch or condition information. For example, in the ASD dataset, if a cell is identified as belonging to the "Vascular" cell type, it can be excluded since it directly indicates the negative control condition. This preprocessing step can help mitigate potential confounding and enhance the reliability of downstream analyses.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to integrate neural discrete representation learning with mutual information loss to establish independence, demonstrating the practical applicability of mutual information loss beyond its theoretical justification. Unlike previous methods that rely on adversarial training and discriminators to enforce independence, our approach is significantly more efficient, especially when the encoder is large (e.g. Transformers), as it reduces the need for multiple backpropagation steps required for discriminator training. Additionally, neural discrete representation learning offers distinct advantages over conventional VAEs, where the latent representations are continuous vectors, particularly when auxiliary discrete-related objective functions, such as the mutual information loss, are involved.

Finally, our theoretical analysis and empirical results suggest that one-to-one matching can avoid the negative skew introduced by weighting-based approaches under the sparse single-cell settings considered here and in experimental perturbation studies; otherwise, weighting may introduce the skewness towards the negative direction for the estimated ITE due to the sparsity of single-cell data. Although there is no formal theoretical guarantee for the nearest neighbor matching, it still performed the best among the approaches considered here.
