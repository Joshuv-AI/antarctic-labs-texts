Advanced azelaic acid hyalubilosome-loaded microneedles for potent anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antimicrobial for Acne treatment by up-regulation of Nrf2/HO-1 signaling pathway
Authors: Sammar Fathy Elhabal, Mohamed Fathi Mohamed Elrefai, Fatma E Hassan, Nahla Sameh Tolba, Rania Mostafa Abd El Galil, Suzan Awad AbdelGhany Morsy, Shady Allam, Amr M Emam, Hanaa Wanas, Marian Sobhy Azer, Mona Mohamed Ahmed, Hoda Ahmed Abdelmoneim, Marwa Fady, Ahmed Mohsen Elsaid Hamdan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcne vulgaris is a multifactorial inflammatory skin disorder in which the clinical utility of azelaic acid (AZA) is limited by poor aqueous solubility and inadequate skin permeation. This study developed AZA-loaded hyalubilosomes (AZA-HBs) integrated into dissolving PVA/PVP-chitosan microneedles (MNs) to improve dermal delivery and anti-acne efficacy. AZA-HBs formulations were developed using Design-Expert® software to study the effects of hyaluronic acid and bile salt amount, optimized via I-optimal design, and incorporated into dissolving MNs. The optimized AZA-HBs showed high entrapment efficiency (87.0 ± 0.55%), nanosized vesicles (170 ± 0.72 nm), narrow dispersity (PDI 0.33 ± 0.01), and good colloidal stability (zeta potential −29 ± 0.28 mV). The selected MN formulation exhibited excellent drug loading (95.3 ± 1.2%), strong mechanical performance with only (11.0 ± 1.0%) height reduction, and efficient insertion. The hybrid platform provided sustained AZA release, reaching (94–95%) over 48 h, and increased ex vivo skin permeation by (6.3-fold). It also improved cytocompatibility in HaCaT cells, whereas free AZA caused complete cell loss at the highest tested concentration. Moreover, the formulation enhanced antimicrobial potency (MICs of 6.25 μg/mL against S. epidermidis and 12.5 μg/mL against S. aureus) and eradicated (78%) of the biofilm. In vivo, AZA-HBs/MNs produced the strongest anti-acne effect, markedly reducing ear inflammation, suppressing TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and TLR4, modulating Nrf2/HO-1/MYD88 signaling, and restoring normal histological and EGF immunohistochemical features. AZA-HBs-loaded dissolving PVA/PVP-chitosan microneedles offer sustained release, superior penetration, strong antimicrobial/antibiofilm activity, and significant anti-acne efficacy for topical AZA delivery.Keywords: Azelaic acid, Hyaluronic acid, Microneedles, Acne vulgaris, Antibiofilm, Antimicrobial, Antioxidant

Introduction

Acne vulgaris (AV) is a common chronic inflammatory condition affecting the pilosebaceous unit. Comedones, papules, pustules, nodules, and scarring are the clinical features of this condition (Elhabal et al., 2024; Kwon et al., 2017). Its pathophysiology includes excessive sebum production, follicular hyperkeratinization, microbial colonization, and the activation of local inflammatory responses (Jain et al., 2016; Li et al., 2025). Aside from its cutaneous manifestations, acne can significantly influence quality of life and is usually connected with psychological suffering, such as anxiety, sadness, and low self-esteem (Tolentino et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2025a). Non-inflammatory comedones and inflammatory pustules, papules, and nodules are manifestations of the condition. Acne is characterized by the presence of comedones and pustules (H. Fan et al., 2025a; Zhang et al., 2025b). The selective disposition, bioavailability, and permeation enhancement of the topical acne and concomitant scalp conditions, and selective, targeted hair follicles. Systemic treatments can be used as adjuncts or alternatives to topical therapy (Blaskovich et al., 2019; Kansom et al., 2025). Topical retinoids, benzoyl peroxide, antibiotics, antiandrogens, and systemic treatments are all used to treat moderate-to-severe acne. Although these treatments are commonly utilized, their therapeutic efficacy may be restricted due to discomfort, dryness, poor patient adherence, recurrence, and safety issues associated with long-term or systemic administration. Long-term usage of topical and systemic antibiotics, in particular, has contributed to rising antimicrobial resistance concerns. There is still a need for targeted, non-antibiotic, and better-tolerated treatment techniques that can simultaneously target inflammation, microbial development, oxidative stress, and aberrant follicular keratinization. Most of the first-line treatments include topical antibiotics (e.g., clindamycin, erythromycin, clarithromycin, azithromycin, and nadifloxacin), antiandrogens (e.g., cyproterone acetate), and retinoids (e.g., tretinoin, adaplene, tazarotene, isotretinoin, and metretinide) (Elhabal et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2026). The use of antibiotics in acne vulgaris treatment is complicated by the development of bacterial resistance and the persistence of side effects (Rottboell et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2013).

Azelaic acid (AZA), is a natural dicarboxylic acid synthesized by Malassezia furfur, with competitive anti-bacterial (C. acnes), anti-inflammatory, and keratolytic properties (Arshad et al., 2024; Nautiyal and Wairkar, 2023). AZA was chosen for this investigation because it directly targets several important pathogenic components of acne. AZA is a naturally occurring dicarboxylic acid with antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-keratinizing, and tyrosinase inhibitory properties. The topical AZA treatment has a half-life of approximately 12 h and, in most cases, requires the patient to apply it to the affected areas at least twice a day (Jacobus Berlitz et al., 2019). These combined effects make AZA very effective in acne lesions characterized by inflammation and post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation. Unlike antibiotic-based therapy, AZA does not rely solely on conventional antibiotic mechanisms, making it an appealing option for lowering reliance on topical antibiotics in long-term acne control. The anti-inflammatory activity of AZA has been associated with the inhibition of proinflammatory cytokine release, down-regulation of cathelicidins, and inhibition of reactive oxygen species (ROS) production by granulocytes during the inflammatory response (Nazzaro-Porro et al., 1980; Pathak et al., 1985). AZA has no keratinization, follicular, or hyperkeratinization, and reduces the excessive stratum corneum thickness, decreases the keratohyalin granules, decreases the distribution and amount of filaggrin, reduces cell proliferation, and is a competitive inhibitor of all important/inclusion vital mitochondrial respiratory enzymes, that is, NADH dehydrogenase, succinic dehydrogenase, and ubiquinone oxidase (Lowe et al., 1998). Inhibition of melatonin generation is also a consequence of inhibition of tyrosinase-like enzymes, leading to a loss of melanin and melanosomes (Thiboutot et al., 2003; Wang et al., 2021). However, AZA's belonging to BCS II have low solubility in water (approximately 0.24 g/100 g of water at 25 OC) and their limited penetration into the skin limits their use. Therefore, a significant increase in the formulation to 10% w/w or 20% w/w is needed to achieve the desired therapeutic effect (Fitton and Goa, 1991; Picardo et al., 1985). This, however, is constrained by dose-dependent side effects such as erythema, burning, stinging and tingling, dryness, scaling, and peeling, and therefore necessitates the development of a new approach to deliver the formulation most effectively (Schallreuter and Wood, 1990).

Hyaluobilosomes (HBs) or Hyaluronic acid gel-filled core bilosomes have been previously studied (Abd El Hady et al., 2025) as novel liposomes that consist of phospholipid vesicles encapsulating gel material in their core (Kawar and Abdelkader, 2019). Hyalubilosomes are a promising vesicular delivery strategy because they combine the penetration-enhancing properties of bile-salt-containing vesicles with the hydration and bioadhesion properties of hyaluronic acid (Othman et al., 2025). Several topical AZA administration methods have previously been studied to overcome its low water solubility and limited skin absorption. These include standard creams and gels, ethosomal systems, liposomal or lipogel formulations, nanostructured lipid carriers, hydrogels, and micro- and nanocrystal-based systems (Malik and Kaur, 2018; Mistry et al., 2016). These experiments found that nanocarrier-based solutions outperformed conventional topical formulations in terms of AZA deposition, skin targeting, antibacterial efficacy, and sustained release (Burchacka et al., 2016). However, most of these techniques still rely on passive diffusion through the stratum corneum, which may limit AZA deposition into the deeper epidermal and follicular compartments important in acne etiology (El-Nawawy et al., 2024; El-Refaie et al., 2015). The bilosomal core structures serve as drug containment while sustaining drug release. The bilosomal design of HBs is relevant to the treatment of acne, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, and other skin disorders, as the therapeutic system is designed to act locally in the dermis, unlike traditional delivery systems. Additionally, for AZA, the bilosomal design helps reduce patient irritation while targeting epidermal follicles and increasing the drug's therapeutic effect on the skin lesion (Kharouba et al., 2022).

Microneedles are microstructured delivery methods that form temporary aqueous microchannels across the stratum corneum while avoiding deeper pain receptors and blood vessels. Dissolving microneedles are particularly desirable for dermatological administration because they are made of biocompatible and water-soluble polymers that dissolve after insertion, releasing the loaded medication or nanocarrier directly into the skin. Microneedles can improve local drug deposition, bypass the stratum corneum barrier, reduce systemic exposure, and improve patient acceptability compared to conventional injections. Incorporating AZA-HBs into dissolving microneedles was anticipated to increase intradermal AZA availability, lengthen local residency time, and boost anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antioxidant, and antibiofilm actions at the diseased site.

Chitosan, polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), and polyvinyl pyrrolidone (PVP) are the most common biopolymers used in the construction of a microneedle system, due to their biocompatibility and ability to form a drug delivery matrix that enables durable, controlled drug release or rapid drug release (Elhabal et al., 2026a, Elhabal et al., 2025a). This ability is especially true for chitosan, which is due to its pronounced film-forming capabilities, as well as its biodegradability and skin-adherent properties. PVA and PVP improve the biopolymer matrix by increasing mechanical strength, solubility, and the ability to form microneedles (Elhabal et al., 2026e).

In microneedle designs, integrating chitosan with PVA or PVP can optimize mechanical strength and dissolution rate. Strong microneedles capable of penetrating the skin have been developed using chitosan/PVP and chitosan/PVA (Elhabal et al., 2025c; Stoeva et al., 2025). PVP facilitates more sustained drug release and PVA aids in structural stability and quick dissolution. These properties render chitosan-PVA-PVP microneedles suitable for the transdermal delivery of poorly permeable, low-bioavailability drugs (Fathy Elhabal et al., 2026b). This microneedle platform is particularly beneficial for azelaic acid, as it can penetrate the stratum corneum, enhance localized delivery, minimize irritation, and improve targeting to the skin layers involved in the acne-related inflammatory process (Fathy Elhabal et al., 2025). From a formulation perspective, chitosan/PVA/PVP microneedles can be utilized as the ultimate delivery patch for azelaic acid-encapsulated nanocarriers, such as HBs or bilosomes (Elhabal et al., 2025e).In the proposed dosage form, AZA is the primary active pharmaceutical ingredient responsible for the anti-acne therapeutic activity. It inhibits tyrosinase, which helps restrict the development of acne-associated bacteria, reduce the generation of inflammatory mediators, limit oxidative stress, regulate aberrant keratinization, and alleviate post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation. The hyalubilosome vesicles and PVA/PVP-chitosan microneedle matrix, on the other hand, are primarily delivery-enhancing components, intended to improve AZA loading, protect the drug within the formulation, facilitate penetration across the stratum corneum, sustain local release, and increase drug availability at the inflamed pilosebaceous target site.

In the current study, the novelty of the present work lies in developing a hybrid delivery platform that integrates AZA-loaded hyalubilosomes with dissolving PVA/PVP-chitosan microneedles to penetrate the stratum corneum barrier and promote localized intradermal deposition. This hybrid approach is intended to promote AZA retention, offer prolonged release, improve skin permeability, and boost therapeutic efficacy against acne-related inflammation, oxidative stress, microbial colonization, and biofilm formation. The goal of this work was to create and optimize AZA-loaded hyalubilosomes embedded in dissolving PVA/PVP-chitosan microneedles as a minimally invasive topical acne treatment platform. The formulation was optimized using an I-optimal design and tested for physicochemical properties, morphology, stability, microneedle mechanical performance, in vitro release, ex vivo skin permeation, cytocompatibility, antioxidant activity, antimicrobial and antibiofilm effects, and in vivo acne efficacy. The study also examined the optimized system's potential to modulate inflammatory mediators and the Nrf2/HO-1/MYD88 signaling pathway in an acne-like inflammation model.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

The following chemicals were used: Azelaic acid (Azelaic acid (≥98% pure, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), Hyaluronic acid (800-1000 kDa, Bloomage Biotech, China), Soybean Phosphatidylcholine (Lipoid S100, ≥94% pure, Lipoid GmbH, Germany), Tween 80 (Polysorbate 80, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), Bile salts which consists of Sodium Deoxycholate (NDC, ≥98% pure, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), Sodium Taurocholate (NTC, ≥98% pure, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), Chloroform (HPLC Grade, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), Methanol (HPLC Grade, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), Phosphate tungstic acid (2% w/v, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), Potassium Bromide (KBr; for IR pellets, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, ≥99.9% pure, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and 2 N H2SO4 (Analytical Grade, Sigma-Aldrich, USA).

Animals

Thirty male Wistar albino rats, each weighing 150-200 g and aged 8 weeks, were allocated to five groups. The animals were housed in specifically designed, pathogen-free settings with a 12-h light/dark cycle, controlled humidity, and temperatures between 20 and 24 °C. They were afforded unrestricted access to partake in food and beverages. The experiment was conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE principles, as outlined in the “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.” The Research Ethics Committee (REC) of the Faculty of Pharmacy, Cairo University, approved the research. Approval Number: PI 3998. The research complied with the directives of the US National Institute of Health (NIH Publication No. 85–23, amended 2011) and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Methods

Azelaic acid high-performance liquid chromatography analysis

Azelaic acid can be analyzed and quantified utilizing a C₁₈ reversed-phase column (e.g., 250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm, ODS-Hypersil) with an isocratic mobile phase comprising methanol and ammonium acetate buffer (20:80, v/v, pH 4.5) at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min and UV detection at 230 nm, where azelaic acid exhibits significant absorbance (Han et al., 2013; Yang et al., 2022). A 20 μL sample volume was injected, and chromatography was performed at 25 °C. The amounts AZA were ascertained using a standard calibration curve. The calibration curve ranged from 20 to 100 μg/mL, with recovery rates of 97.13% to 102.04%. The linearity spanned from 20 to 100 μg/mL (R2 = 0.999; n = 3).

Experimental design azelaic acid -loaded hyalubilosomes (AZA-HBs)

The primary objective of the current design was to optimize an AZA-HBs formula to achieve enhanced EE% and ZP while minimizing PS and PDI. In preliminary screening, literature review, and our existing understanding of HBs formulation and development, independent variables potentially influencing these criteria were identified. An I-optimal design was employed using Design-Expert® version 13 (Stat-Ease, Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA) to examine these variables and their interactions. The I-optimal design generated 22 experimental trials, as illustrated in Table 2. The independent variables chosen were: Hyaluronic acid concentration (A), amount of bile salts (B), and kinds of bile acid salts (C). The dependent variables selected were EE% (Y1), PS (Y2), PDI (Y3), and ZP (Y4), as illustrated in Table 1. Furthermore, we have established constraints on the target variables to facilitate the program in achieving the ideal formula by greatest numerical desirability.

In-vitro characterization of azelaic acid-loaded hyalubilosomes (AZA-HBs)

Determination of particle size (PS), polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential (ZP)

The physicochemical properties of azelaic acid-loaded hyalubilosomes were evaluated regarding particle size (PS), polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential (ZP). The HBs dispersion of AZA was analyzed at 25 ± 2 °C, using an aliquot of 0.1 mL, which was subsequently diluted 100-fold with double-distilled water to attain a concentration suitable for measurement of scattering intensity with the Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments; Worcestershire, UK) (Elhabal et al., 2026c; Hendawy et al., 2025). This methodology is based on the principles of dynamic light scattering, which arises from the stochastic motion of the scattered vesicles. PS, PDI, and ZP findings were documented in triplicate.

Determination of AZA entrapment efficiency (EE%)

The indirect method was employed to ascertain the entrapment efficiency of AZA-HBs. An aliquot (1 mL) of hyalubilosomal dispersion underwent ultracentrifugation (Sigma 3-30 KS cooling ultracentrifuge, Sigma Laborzentrifugen GmbH, Osterode am Harz, Germany) for 1 h at 22,000 rpm and a temperature of 4 °C (Elhabal et al., 2025f; Youssif et al., 2025). Untrapped AZA was measured in the supernatant by HPLC, as previously described, with UV detection at about 230 nm after a 1:10 dilution with methanol. Statistical optimization of AZA-HBs

Using Design Expert® software, optimization was conducted to determine the parameter levels that achieve the minimum particle size (PS) and polydispersity index (PDI), as well as the maximum encapsulation efficiency (EE%) and the absolute value of zeta potential (ZP).

Physicochemical characterization of the optimum formula

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The morphology of the AZA-HBs was analyzed using TEM at 100 kV (JEM-2100; JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) to assess the influence of hyaluronic acid and bile salts on the properties of the AZA-HBs formulation. The collected samples were diluted with double-distilled water, and a single drop of the diluted sample was placed on a copper grid pre-coated with carbon and stained with phosphotungstic acid (2% w/v) (Elhabal et al., 2026b; M.H.H. Mohammed et al., 2023a). Thereafter, any surplus liquid was eliminated before analysis.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) studies

The optimized AZA-loaded HBs were lyophilized with 5% mannitol and frozen at −18 °C for 24 h. The frozen solution was further lyophilized in a freeze dryer (Labconco Corp., Kansas City, MO, USA) at −45 °C and a vacuum of 0.07 mbar for 48 h to get the lyophilized formula. FTIR analysis was performed to examine the functional groups of the pure drug and the drug-loaded HBs formulation. Spectra were independently obtained for azelaic acid, phospholipids, bile salts, and the formulation using an FTIR-8400 (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). The sample-to-KBr ratio was preserved at 1:100, and the spectra were acquired within the range of 4000–400 cm−1 (Al-Zuhairy et al., 2025; Elhabal et al., 2025d). The drug-to-excipient ratio in the formulation was 1:4 (azelaic acid: soybean phosphatidylcholine, hyaluronic acid, and bile salts).

Crystallinity examination via differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) studies

A DSC study was conducted to evaluate the thermal characteristics and potential interactions of azelaic acid with excipients. A Shimadzu DSC-60 (Kyoto, Japan) was employed, and samples were heated at 10 °C/min from 0 to 170 °C under a nitrogen atmosphere (Elhabal et al., 2025b; Fouad et al., 2024). The drug-to-excipient ratio in the formulation was 1:4 azelaic acid: soybean phosphatidylcholine, hyaluronic acid, and bile salts. Thermograms were obtained for the pure drug, Blank HBs, and optimized AZA-HBs formulation to analyze thermal transitions.

Effect of storage on the optimized AZA-HBs formula

The optimized AZA-HBs formula was maintained at refrigerated (4 ± 1 °C) and ambient temperatures for 0, 3, and 6 months to assess its physical stability by comparing its EE%, PS, and PDI, and ZP before and after storage. Statistical significance was assessed using a Student's t-test in SPSS version 22.0. Differences at P ≤ 0.05 were deemed significant. The investigation was conducted three times to determine the mean ± SD (Mahdy et al., 2025; Mohamed et al., 2025).

A dual-casting method was utilized to produce AZA-HBs-loaded PVA/PVP-chitosan microneedles (AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs)

The needle tips were formulated using varying ratios of polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), chitosan (2% w/v), and 5 mg of lyophilized azelaic acid-loaded hyalubilosomes (AZA-HBs), as specified in Table 2. The tip solution was formulated by dissolving PVA and PVP at 100 mg/mL each in distilled water; thereafter, lyophilized AZA-HBs and Chitosan (2% w/v) were added under gentle agitation until a homogeneous mixture was achieved. A volume of 60 μL of this tip solution was introduced into a 1 cm × 1 cm PDMS microneedle mold (Silicone Template ST-01; Micropoint Technologies Pte Ltd., Singapore) comprising 100 conical needles (300 μm height, 100 μm base diameter, 5 μm tip diameter, arranged in a 10 × 10 array) (Elhabal et al., 2026e; Sartawi et al., 2022). The vacuum application (20 min) guaranteed the complete filling of needle cavities without air entrapment. A drug-free backing solution (0.12 mg/mL PVA + 0.01 mg/mL Chitosan) was utilized as the second casting layer to improve adhesion and mechanical support. The filled molds were dried overnight at room temperature (25 °C) under light protection (Wang et al., 2025). The crosslinked hydrogel-forming microneedle patches were meticulously demolded and preserved in desiccators until characterization (Fig. 1b).

Mechanical strength and penetration capability test

The mechanical strength and skin penetration ability of AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-chitosan microneedles (AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs) were determined using the Parafilm®M® penetration model. Characterization of optimized microneedle

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The morphology of the AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs patch was examined using a light microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) and a scanning electron microscope (SU8010, HITACHI, Tokyo, Japan). The AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs patch was attached to a microscope carrier and visualized at 6 kV with magnifications of 50×, 100×, 200×, and 600 × (Abu Ershaid et al., 2024; Hamed and Alhadidi, 2024).

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) analysis

An FTIR spectrometer was used to record the room-temperature spectra of an AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs patch and its constituent parts between 4000 and 500 cm−1 (model 22, Bruker, Coventry, UK), as described previously (Rad et al., 2017; Sabbagh and Kim, 2023).

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

The thermal properties, crystallinity, and interactions of the AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs patch were examined using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC-60; Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) at temperatures from 25 to 350 °C under nitrogen purge at 100 ml/min, as described previously (Elhabal et al., 2025a).

Drug release studies

Comparative in-vitro release study

The in vitro release profiles of AZA-HBs, AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs, and an AZA suspension (5 mg/mL) were compared using the dialysis bag technique. The in vitro release of AZA from different formulations was performed using a USP dissolution testing apparatus type II (Hanson Research Corp, CA, USA) (Elhabal et al., 2025f; Venkatasubramanian et al., 2025). The dissolution media was 100 mL of phosphate buffer, pH 7.4, incorporating 25% methanol to ensure the sink conditions. (20%AZA) was placed in two open-ended tubes, with one end sealed with a dialysis membrane (12–14 kDa molecular mass cutoff). One tube was fixed to one side of the plastic cylinder, which was 7.5 cm long and 3 cm in internal diameter, and the other tube was attached to the dissolution apparatus shafts (Pal et al., 2021; Qu et al., 2016). The dissolution media temperature was maintained at 37 ± 1 °C, and the shaft was rotated at 50 rpm. At definite time intervals (1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 24 h), 1 mL samples were taken from the dissolution media and replaced with an equal volume of fresh medium. The concentration of AZA was determined spectrophotometrically at λmax 230 nm. The in vitro release data were kinetically evaluated using various release models (Higuchi, first-order, Peppas, and zero-order) (Elhabal et al., 2025d).

Ex vivo skin permeation study

The ex vivo permeation of AZA from AZA suspension, AZA-HBs, and AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs was studied using vertical Franz diffusion cells. Excised rat skin was positioned between the donor and receptor compartments, with the stratum corneum facing the donor side. The receptor compartment was filled with phosphate buffer pH 7.4 containing 25% methanol and kept at 32 ± 0.5 °C with continuous stirring. Each formulation, equivalent to the same AZA dose, was administered to the donor compartment. Samples were taken from the receptor compartment at predefined intervals and promptly replaced with fresh receptor medium (Mahdy et al., 2025; Qaiser et al., 2024). AZA was measured using HPLC. The total amount penetrated per unit area, steady-state flow, permeability coefficient, and enhancement ratio were determined (Kadian et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024). The UV detector was adjusted to 230 nm, and the flow rate was 1 mL/min. All experimental techniques in the investigation were carried out in triplicate.

In vitro cell toxicity assessment

Assessment of antimicrobial activity

Diffusion agar method

The antibacterial activity of AZA, AZA-HBs, and AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs were investigated against selected pathogenic bacteria using the agar well diffusion technique. Gram-positive bacteria, such as Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923, Staphylococcus epidermidis ATCC 12228, and Streptococcus mutans ATCC 25175, were studied, as were Gram-negative bacteria, including Escherichia coli ATCC 25922. The standard microbial strains were obtained from the Regional Center for Mycology and Biotechnology (RCMB), Egypt. Bacterial suspensions were produced and added to Mueller-Hinton agar plates. Agar was divided into 6.0 mm diameter wells, with 100 μL of each tested sample added. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Gentamycin (4 mg/mL) was utilized as a positive control. Antimicrobial activity was determined by measuring the diameter of the inhibitory zones in millimeters (Alfaro-Viquez et al., 2020; Elhabal et al., 2025c).

Assessment of the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)

A two-fold broth dilution method was used to determine the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) of AZA, AZA-HBs, and AZA-HBs/PVA-PVP/Cs MNs against the tested bacterial strains. A 96-well microplate filled with Muller-Hinton broth was filled with dilutions of AZA, AZA-HBs, and AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs at final concentrations of 2.5, 5, 10, 12.5, 25, 50, and 100 μM. Each bacterial strain under investigation was injected in equal quantities (106 CFU/ml). A microplate reader (Infinite M1000 Pro, Tecan, Switzerland) was used to measure absorbance at 600 nm after a 24-h incubation at 37 °C. The minimum concentration that prevented discernible growth was indicated by the MIC values (Borehalli Mayegowda et al., 2023; Chelminiak-Dudkiewicz et al., 2024).

Antibiofilm assay and crystal violet staining (CVS) assay

This study used 96-well microtiter plates with a 100 mL capacity to test concentrations ranging from 0.02 mg/mL to 25 mg/mL. Each extract's 10% stock solution was diluted twice in brain-heart infusion broth (BHI). The wells were incubated with 100 mL of each tested strain at 37 °C until the final concentration reached 1 × 106 CFU/mL. The medium served as the untreated control, 0.1% (w/v) CHX as the positive control, and 10% DMSO as the negative control. A 96-well microtiter plate was given to Staphylococcus epidermidis strains. The plates included an AZA, AZA-HBs, and AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs, and negative control (Abbasi et al., 2024; Won et al., 2023). To remove any stuck or moving cells, the wells were carefully emptied after 24 h, and the plates were cleaned with pH 7.4 phosphate buffer. The plates were baked for 45 min at 60 °C and then left to air dry. Applying 150 μL of 96% methanol to each well for 15–20 min fixed the adhering cells. Following plate removal, adherent cells were stained for 20 min at room temperature using 100 μL of a 0.1% crystal violet solution. To remove any remaining discoloration, the plates were washed with water at least 5 times. Biofilm biomass was quantified semi-quantitatively after 150 μL of 100% ethanol was used to resolubilize the crystal violet dye. After carefully and gently shaking the plates, the absorbance was measured at 620 nm using a microplate reader (Stat Fax 2100 microplate reader Spectrophotometer is made by Agilent Technologies, Inc., situated in Santa Clara, CA, USA). The mean absorbance (OD = 620 nm) of 252 samples was determined using the following Eq. (8), and the results were shown as % inhibition (Li et al., 2021; Shokri et al., 2018).

In vivo study of anti-acne efficacy study using a rat ear acne-like inflammatory model

An in vivo rat ear acne model was utilized to assess the anti-acne efficacy of AZA suspension, AZA-HBs, and AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs/MNs formulations. Male Wistar albino rats weighing 150-200 g were used in the study (Elhabal et al., 2024; Sangnim et al., 2025). Histopathological study

Skin specimens were obtained from the dorsal region of the rat, which functioned as a standardized site for all subjects. After a 24-h immersion in a 10% buffered formalin solution, the specimens were excised, rinsed in xylene, and dehydrated with ascending ethanol concentrations prior to paraffin embedding. Serial skin segments were excised and positioned on glass slides to attain a thickness of five to seven micrometres. Tissue slices were generated, deparaffinized with xylene, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) prior to histological examination using an electric light microscope (Mohanty et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024).

Immunohistochemical examination of epidermal growth factor (IHC-Peroxidase-DAB)

Immunohistochemistry (IHC-Peroxidase-DAB) will be conducted to evaluate ear-surface inflammation in the acne model by analyzing the expression of epidermal growth factor (EGF) in the inflamed skin tissue. EGF is a crucial growth factor that plays a significant role in keratinocyte proliferation, wound healing, and inflammatory skin disorders, with its altered expression being linked to disrupted epidermal homeostasis in acneiform lesions. This investigation involves sectioning paraffin-embedded ear samples from various experimental groups at 4–5 μm and affixing them to adhesive glass slides. The slides will undergo deparaffinization in xylene and rehydration through a graded sequence of alcohol (100%, 95%, 70%) to distilled water. Endogenous peroxidase activity will be inhibited using 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10–15 min, followed by heat-induced epitope retrieval utilizing a citrate-based buffer (pH 6.0) in a pressure cooker or water bath to restore antigenicity. Following cooling and rinsing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the sections will be incubated with the primary anti-EGF (IHC-Peroxidase-DAB) antibody for one hour at 25 °C in a humidified environment. After a comprehensive wash in PBS, the HRP-labeled detection system will be utilized according to the manufacturer's instructions: incubation with a secondary HRP-conjugated antibody, followed by development with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) and counterstaining with hematoxylin. Negative control slides will be created by excluding the primary antibody while maintaining the identical secondary detection procedures to verify the specificity of the immunostaining (Henley et al., 2002; Larkina et al., 2025). EGF immunopositivity will be evaluated by calculating the mean percentage of stained area in five randomly selected high-power fields (400× magnification) for each group, utilizing image analysis software. The results will yield a semi-quantitative evaluation of EGF-mediated inflammatory activation and epidermal remodelling in the rat ear acne model.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was done using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad, USA), and the mean ± SD was calculated. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's test was used for multiple groups, whereas the t-test was used for two groups. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

In-vitro characterization of azelaic acid-loaded hyalubilosomes (AZA-HBs)

Azelaic acid-loaded hyalubilosomes were optimized using an I-optimal design to achieve a nanocarrier with high entrapment efficiency (EE%), small particle size (PS), low polydispersity index (PDI), and high absolute zeta potential (ZP). The selected design levels and response targets (Table 1) enabled systematic screening of the factor space while maintaining a reasonable balance between model precision and experimental burden.

The impact of formulation factors on entrapment efficiency (Y₁: EE%)

The EE% of azelaic acid-loaded hyalubilosomes ranged from 55 ± 0.43% (run 21) to 90 ± 0.23% (run 2), with the optimized formula (OF) achieving 87 ± 0.55% (Table 4). The design was intended to maximize EE (Table 1), and the empirical evidence supports this across several formulations, especially at a mid-range of 5 mg hyaluronic acid, variable bile salt concentrations, and an appropriate bile salt type. Increasing bile salt concentration, while keeping hyaluronic acid constant, did not always result in an increase in EE. Some formulations containing 30 mg bile salts (runs 1, 13, 16) demonstrated moderate or low EE%, which could be attributed to an excessive amount of bile salts, leading to bilayer disruption and accelerated drug leaching (Kumar et al., 2023; Mondal et al., 2022).This reinforces the approach of adjusting bile salt concentration to enhance deformability while maintaining retention. The type of bile salt (C): STGC (sodium tauroglycocholate) runs (3, 4, 11, 15, 18, 19, 21, 22, OF) showed higher EE% and optimal PS, suggesting that this conjugation can provide a more stable and flexible bilayer than NDC or NTC. NTC (sodium taurocholate) and NDC (sodium deoxycholate) formulations (e.g. runs 6, 8, 10, 13, 14, 17) demonstrated a greater disruptive effect on vesicle structure with higher PS and variable EE (Choi et al., 2024; Torrisi et al., 2025). Hyaluronic Acid (A): At the low level (2 mg), formulations often had lower EE% (e.g., runs 12, 16, 21, 22), likely due to insufficient electrostatic shielding and lower bilayer stability (Aziz et al., 2019; Mosallam et al., 2021).

Particle size (Y₂: PS) and effect of bile salt type and amount

The PS of azelaic acid-loaded hyalubilosomes ranged from 105 ± 0.65 nm (run 21) to 620 ± 0.54 nm (run 12), with the optimized formula (6 mg HA, 19 mg bile salts, STGC) measuring 170 ± 0.72 nm (Table 4). The goal was to minimize PS (Table 1), and the optimized system falls within the recommended nanoscale range for topical and trans-follicular delivery (typically 100-300 nm). Low-size trends in STGC: STGC-containing formulations, particularly runs 19 (169 ± 0.43 nm) and OF (170 ± 0.72 nm), resulted in the smallest bilosome sizes, demonstrating that STGC promotes the production of compact, highly deformable structures (Dai et al., 2013a, Dai et al., 2013b). This is consistent with prior findings that specific bile salt conjugates can stabilize bent bilayers while decreasing vesicle size. Large PS with low HA and NTC/NDC: Formulations with 2 mg HA and NTC (runs 8, 12, 16, 17) resulted in significantly larger PS (509-620 nm), indicating insufficient surface coating by HA and substantial bilayer-disrupting effects of NTC, leading to aggregation or vesicle fusion. This supports hyaluronic acid's role as a stabilizing shell surrounding particle (HA-coated liposomes) (Aralelimath et al., 2024; Nayak et al., 2023a).

Bile salt amount: Increasing bile salts from 10 to 30 mg at a constant HA generally increased PS (e.g., runs 10, 14, 13 with NTC; 1, 15 with STGC), showing that high bile salt content increases vesicle swelling and structural instability. The optimized formula (A = 6 mg, B ≈ 19 mg, C = STGC) produces a small PS (170 nm) by achieving a balance between enough bile salt to ensure deformability and sufficient hyaluronic acid to maintain small, stable vesicles, which is suitable for targeting the skin/hyaline layer as shown in Fig. 3b.

Polydispersity index (Y₃: PDI) and homogeneity

The PDI of the hyalubilosomes ranged from 0.21 ± 0.02 (run 19) to 0.54 ± 0.04 (runs 1, 6, 12), with the optimized formula showing 0.33 ± 0.01, indicating a reasonably narrow size distribution around the mean (Table 4). For monomodal, reproducible systems, a PDI of less than 0.3 is typically desirable. Low PDI with STGC and moderate HA: Formulations including STGC and 5-10 mg HA (e.g., runs 19, 11, 4, 20, OF) had the lowest PDI values (0.21-0.29), indicating a uniform, unimodal population. This indicates that STGC, when combined with sufficient HA, promotes homogeneous vesicle production during hydration. High PDI with NTC/NDC and low HA: Runs with NTC or NDC and 2 mg HA (e.g., 8, 12, 16) showed PDI > 0.4, indicating a larger, heterogeneous size distribution, most likely caused by spontaneous aggregation or uneven bilayer curvature (Mitrović et al., 2025). STGC-based, hyaluronic acid-stabilized hyalubilosomes are the most reproducible and well-suited for scale-up, as evidenced by the optimized formula (C = STGC, A ≈ 6 mg, B ≈ 19 mg) shown in Fig. 3c.

Zeta potential (Y₄: ZP) and colloidal stability

The ZP of azelaic acid-loaded hyalubilosomes varied between -22 ± 0.68 mV (run 21) and -40 ± 0.34 mV (run 19), with the optimized formula attaining -29 ± 0.28 mV (Table 4). The goal was to maximize ZP (Table 1), and most formulations fell within or near the typically accepted stability range (ZP ≥ 25-30 mV).STGC with 10 mg HA resulted in the greatest negative ZP (−39 to −40 mV) in runs 1, 15, and 19, suggesting substantial electrostatic repulsion between particles. Hyaluronic acid acts as a polyanionic coating, increasing negative surface charge and resistance to aggregation (HA-coated liposomes). Formulations with NTC or NDC and 2 mg HA (e.g., 8, 12, 16, 17) had lower negative ZP (≈ − 25 to −30 mV), indicating reduced colloidal stability. Compared to the optimized formula, run 19 (10 mg HA, 20 mg STGC) produced EE ≈ 89%, PS ≈ 169 nm, PDI ≈ 0.21, and ZP ≈ −40 mV, outperforming the recommended OF on all parameters (Harini et al., 2025; Sideek et al., 2024). The I-optimal algorithm selected the OF (A = 6 mg, B ≈ 19 mg, C = STGC) based on constraints such as cost, HA loading, and ease of scale-up. This shows that model-based optimization is effective but may sacrifice extreme performance for practicality, as shown in Fig. 3d.

The performance of the optimized AZA-HBs presents positive comparisons to the previously reported AZA nanocarrier systems. The optimized AZA-HBs showed an entrapment efficiency of 87.0 ± 0.55%, a particle size of 170 ± 0.72 nm, PDI of 0.33 ± 0.01, and zeta potential of -29 ± 0.28 mV. These values are demonstrative of excellent AZA encapsulation and formation, nanosized vesicular, and colloidal stability. For comparisons, Mistry and Ravikumar developed AZA-loaded ethosomes that were used for the treatment of acne and reported an optimized vesicular size of 4.25 ± 1.35 μm with an entrapment efficiency of 91.86 ± 2.25% (Mistry et al., 2016). Even though the ethosome system had a slightly higher percentage of entrapment efficiency, the vesicular size was in the micrometer range, whereas the current AZA-HBs system achieved a much smaller size in the nanoscale range, which could be beneficial for dermal penetration and local retention. Malik and Kaur also developed AZA-loaded nanostructured lipid carriers and reported an optimized entrapment efficiency of 83.4 ± 2.14% with a particle size of 49.6 ± 1.24 nm that further demonstrated the enhancement of topical performance of AZA nanocarriers (Malik and Kaur, 2018).

Statistical optimization and model validation

The I-optimal design produced a statistically supported model for each response (Y₁: EE%, Y₂: PS, Y₃: PDI, and Y₄: ZP), allowing for reasonable selection of an optimized azelaic acid-loaded hyalubilosome formula (Table 5). The observed responses encompassed EE% from 55% to 90%, PS from 105 to 620 nm, PDI from 0.21 to 0.54, and ZP from −22 to −40 mV, suggesting a broad yet relevant experimental area. The EE% response (Y₁) was best matched by a quadratic model (R2 = 0.807, adjusted R2 = 0.594, predicted R2 = 0.821). The model had a significant F-value of 3.79 (P = 0.022) and an adequate accuracy of 6.292, showing reasonable predictive potential. Factor A (hyaluronic acid quantity) was revealed as the most significant influencing factor on EE%, consistent with previous findings that intermediate to high HA levels improve entrapment by stabilizing the bilayer and enhancing drug partitioning (Shen et al., 2024). The PS (Y₂) response followed a linear model with high statistical significance (F-value = 42.74, P < 0.0001; R2 = 0.909, adjusted R2 = 0.888, projected R2 = 0.841; acceptable precision = 14.175). Significant differences in PS between STGC-based systems (smaller vesicles, e.g., runs 19 and OF) and NTC/NDC-based systems (larger vesicles) can be attributed to the bile salt type (C). PS's excellent R2 and appropriate precision values demonstrate its good predictability within the design area. The PDI (Y₃) response was fitted with a linear model (F-value = 8.68, P = 0.0005; R2 = 0.671, adjusted R2 = 0.594, predicted R2 = 0.500; sufficient precision = 8.045). Only C (bile salt type) was found to be a significant influence, with STGC-containing formulations producing the lowest PDI values (0.21-0.29), indicating a homogeneous, monomodal size distribution. The linear model was appropriate for ZP (Y₄) (F-value = 3.06, P = 0.0033; R2 = 0.419, adjusted R2 = 0.282, predicted R2 = 0.072; adequate precision = 4.815). The major determinants were A (the amount of hyaluronic acid) and B (the amount of bile salt), indicating that the polyanionic HA shell and bile salt concentration regulate surface charge. Despite lower R2 and expected R2 for ZP compared to PS or EE%, the model nevertheless demonstrated appropriate predictive power (precision >4.0) for the optimization process. Using established models, the I-optimal design algorithm identified an optimized formula (A = 6 mg hyaluronic acid, B = 19 mg bile salts, C = STGC) with anticipated responses that closely match experimentally observed values (Table 2): EE% ≈ 87%, PS ≈ 170 nm, PDI ≈ 0.33, ZP ≈ -29 mV. The I-optimal design successfully balanced high EE%, small PS, low PDI, and sufficiently negative ZP, resulting in a stable, nano-sized hyalubilosome system suitable for azelaic acid delivery. This is supported by significant model F-values, acceptable R2, and adequate precision metrics.

Stability study

The stability investigation of the optimized azelaic acid-loaded hyalubilosomes (AZA-HBs) formulations, as shown in Table 6, shows that the systems retained acceptable physicochemical properties throughout a 6-month storage period under both refrigerated (4 ± 1 °C) and ambient conditions. After 6 months of storage, the encapsulation efficiency (EE%) decreased marginally from 87 ± 0.55% to 82 ± 0.23% at 4 ± 1 °C and from 85 ± 0.11% to 83 ± 0.38% at ambient temperature, indicating minimal azelaic acid leakage from the vesicles. The particle size (PS) increased from 170 ± 0.72 nm to 183 ± 0.93 nm under refrigeration and from 173 ± 0.94 nm to 182 ± 0.74 nm at ambient temperature. This suggests a small degree of vesicle aggregation or mild fusion over time, but the values remained within the nanoscale range suitable for dermal delivery. The polydispersity index (PDI) increased from 0.33 ± 0.01 to 0.38 ± 0.09 at 4 ± 1 °C and from 0.37 ± 0.01 to 0.41 ± 0.02 at room temperature, indicating a gradual increase in size heterogeneity. However, the PDI did not exceed the generally acceptable threshold (PDI < 0.5), indicating that the dispersion remained relatively monodisperse. The zeta potential (ZP) values were consistently negative (approximately -28 to -29 mV) throughout all time points and storage settings, indicating robust electrostatic stabilisation of the colloidal system and negligible aggregation. Overall, these results demonstrate that the optimized AZA-HBs exhibit adequate short- and medium-term stability, with refrigeration providing only a marginal improvement compared to ambient storage, indicating the potential of these nanovesicles as a stable topical delivery system for azelaic acid.

Characterization of the optimum AZA-HBs

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The TEM investigation (Fig. 4a) revealed that the vesicles were distinct, nearly spherical nanostructures with smooth outlines, indicating the successful creation of a well-organized vesicular system. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

Fig. 4c depicts the DSC thermograms of AZA, Blank-HBs, and AZA-HBs, which were used to evaluate the drug's physical state and interaction with formulation components. The thermogram of pure AZA revealed a distinct acute endothermic peak at around 267-270 °C, corresponding to its melting point and confirming its crystalline composition. The sharpness of this peak shows that the medication exists in a highly organized crystalline state prior to formulation. AZA-HBs lost their acute melting endotherm at around 267-270 °C. Instead, the thermogram revealed only a faint or widened thermal event at 180-190 °C, which corresponded primarily to the vesicular matrix. The total absence of the AZA melting peak indicates that the medication lost its crystalline structure and got absorbed in an amorphous, solubilized, or molecularly dispersed state inside the bilosomal system (Kawar and Abdelkader, 2019; Teixeira et al., 2017). This finding provides compelling evidence for effective drug trapping within nanovesicles.

Physicochemical characterization of AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Chitosan microneedles

Drug content

The synthesized microneedles exhibited azelaic acid (AZA) contents of 93.7 ± 1.2% for PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs1, 95.3 ± 1.2% for PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs2, and 88.3 ± 0.6% for PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs3, in relation to theoretical loading as shown in Fig. 5a. The results indicate that lyophilized AZA-HBs were effectively incorporated into the microneedle matrix, demonstrating superior recovery during mold casting and drying processes. The highest drug content observed in MNs2 demonstrates that the 50:40:10 ratio of PVA:PVP:chitosan achieved optimal formulation uniformity and vesicle retention. The reduced recovery in MNs3 can be attributed to its high PVP content, which increases hydrophilicity and promotes partial drug redistribution during drying, whereas MNs1 efficiently kept the drug but did not outperform MNs2. The studies demonstrate that MNs2's balanced polymer composition reduced formulation loss and more efficiently maintained AZA-HB integrity relative to the other formulations (Asaf et al., 2025; Elhabal et al., 2026e). This aligns with research demonstrating that combining Chitosan with water-soluble polymers such as PVP and PVA improves release control and structural effectiveness in dissolving microneedles.

Mechanical integrity assessment

The data showed that the reduction in microneedle height under 32 N of thumb pressure applied for 30 s was 12.7 ± 0.6% for MNs1, 11.0 ± 1.0% for MNs2, and 15.3 ± 0.6% for MNs3. All formulations showed sufficient mechanical strength under mechanical stress for dermal insertion, as shown in Fig. 5b. MNs2 showed the greatest mechanical properties, indicating that the 50:40:10 ratio provided the best balance of stiffness and flexibility. This is likely due to the combination of PVA and PVP, where PVA enhances the polymers' ability to form a film and provides structural cohesion, while PVP increases tensile strength and the rate of hydration. Chitosan enhances the polymer matrix and increases the toughness of the needle tip (Elhabal et al., 2025c; Lee et al., 2008). MNs3 had the highest PVP content and showed the greatest deformation, likely due to excessive hydrophilicity, which increased water absorption and softened the system's matrix during compression. These mechanical properties of the formulations are in line with studies showing that polymers enhance microneedle strength and ease of insertion.

The Parafilm® model's skin penetration capability

The 8-layer Parafilm® M penetration test MNs2 showed the most positive penetration profile, achieving 97.8% hole formation in Layer 3, 92% penetration in Layer 4, and 28% residual penetration in Layer 8 as depicted in Fig. 5c. These results indicate that MNs2 can penetrate the skin barrier quickly and form uniform microchannels across multiple skin layers. The enhanced penetration of MNs2 is due to its mechanical properties: a formulation that resists compression without becoming overly brittle is more likely to retain sharper needle tips during insertion. MNs1 demonstrated sufficient penetration but lower efficacy than MNs2. In contrast, MNs3 appeared to exhibit lower efficacy due to greater deformability resulting from the higher PVP content. This relationship between mechanical strength and penetration efficacy underscores the importance of achieving the right balance of polymers in the formulation for transdermal delivery (Elhabal et al., 2025a; Matadh et al., 2023). Chitosan-blended microneedles have been shown to achieve fairly high penetration through Parafilm when the polymer ratios are optimized, and the matrix maintains sharpened tips to avoid breakage upon insertion.

Water loss on drying (LOD) assessment

As shown in Fig. 5d, the LOD values were determined as 3.7 ± 0.6% for MNs1, 3.2 ± 0.2% for MNs2, and 4.3 ± 0.6% for MNs3. All formulations met the 5% moisture threshold, indicating adequate drying, and demonstrated good storage stability. MNs2 had the least residual moisture, suggesting that the optimal PVA/PVP ratio, together with Chitosan, provided the best control of drying and moisture retention. The increased LOD for MNs3 may be related to the higher PVP, since PVP is highly hygroscopic and thus absorbs moisture more quickly than PVA. Although the differences may be small, they are significant in formulation, as residual moisture may negatively affect brittleness, storage stability, and long-term handling of MNs. The consistent low LOD results indicate that the microneedles were adequately dried, allowing them to remain stable during prolonged storage under normal conditions. This agrees with other studies suggesting that PVP-containing microneedles exhibit higher moisture absorption, whereas Chitosan can improve moisture absorption by increasing matrix density and moisture absorption capacity. MNs1 (60:30:10) shows adequate drug retention and good mechanical properties but does not achieve a satisfactory overall balance. MNs2 (50:40:10) is the best formulation, with the greatest sustained drug release, the least reduction in height, the best level of penetration, and the best level of pharmacopoeial testing, with the lowest detection limit (LOD). MNs3 (40:50:10) is adequate but considerably weaker than the other samples in mechanical strength and drug recovery, likely due to PVP-induced flip and saturation hydrophilicity. PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs2 is the best formulation, achieving greater encapsulation efficiency and mechanical strength, along with enhanced penetration ability and drying stability, confirming that a 50:40:10 ratio of polymers is optimal for AZA-HBs-loaded dissolving microneedles (Aroche et al., 2025; Elhabal et al., 2025e).The LOD test was used to determine the remaining moisture content in the dissolving microneedle matrix. Residual moisture is an important quality attribute for polymeric microneedles because excessive moisture can soften the needle tips, reduce mechanical strength, impair insertion efficiency, increase drug degradation, and compromise storage stability. As a result, LOD was used to assess appropriate drying and formulation stability, especially since PVP is hygroscopic and may increase moisture uptake within the polymeric matrix. The PVA/PVP/Chitosan matrix was chosen to balance mechanical strength and dissolving behavior (Fathy Elhabal et al., 2026). PVA was added to increase film formation and structural strength, PVP to improve hydration and dissolution after insertion, and Chitosan to strengthen the polymeric network and improve skin adherence. This polymeric mixture was designed to retain AZA-HBs in the microneedle tips during casting and drying, while enabling effective release after skin penetration.

Characterization of optimized microneedles

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Morphological analysis was performed using a light microscope (Fig. 5e), and SEM micrographs are known for powerfully illustrating the integrity and uniformity of the architecture of fabricated microneedles (Fig. 5f). At lower magnifications, vertical microneedles exhibit a distributed, uniform, parallel arrangement. Uniformity of construction and order demonstrates notable control over microneedle construction and guarantees a microneedle patch with a reproducible, consistent interaction with the skin. This is important for consistent drug delivery, reproducible insertion, and removal. In magnified images of individual microneedles, the conical and pyramidal shapes exhibit distinct, sharp, well-defined tip structures. Sharp tips significantly influence the microneedle's resistance. Generally, reductions in force and efficient delivery can be achieved with a well-formed, pointed tip (Elhabal et al., 2026a; Gupta and Yadav, 2019; Zhou et al., 2024). At the same time, the broad base of each needle supports the structure and helps maintain sufficient mechanical strength during application. The integrity of the structure is essential for maintaining the mechanical strength of microneedles. Microneedles, by design, rupture the desired membrane without collapsing during insertion. Structural integrity ultimately determined the mandrel structure of the microneedles. The design and microneedle construction provide a logical assurance for sustained function during application and microneedle insertion without failure of the needle to deliver and rupture the target membrane. Microneedles manufactured with extensive polymer solidification techniques and casting or molding techniques likely exhibit polymer surface roughness and small surface defects that are generally not pronounced and go unnoticed at standard inspection levels. These features are likely due to solvent evaporation or drying-induced solidification. These imperfections are unlikely to produce cracks or defects that would compromise the microneedle array's efficacy, but rather support the microneedles' uninterrupted, continuous surface presence. Importantly, the photographs reveal the microneedles are integrated with the baseplate. From a functional standpoint, microneedles manufactured with a continuous and compact backing layer are considered microneedle patches. The ability of the baseplate to support the microneedles and transmit the baseplate's force to them during skin penetration is the most important functional element of the baseplate. Well-integrated microneedle architectures and compact backing layers diminish the probability of individual microneedle dislodgment and enhance the mechanical integrity of the overall structure (Anjani et al., 2023; Duarah et al., 2023). The findings are definitive and illustrate the successful fabrication of a microneedle array with sharp tips, uniform geometries, and integrated microneedle configurations. These attributes enhance mechanical dependability, ensure uniform microneedle penetration, and optimize overall product performance, rendering the microneedles very appropriate for transdermal applications. Microneedle technology is expected to improve the transdermal distribution of the integrated formulation and boost overall therapeutic efficacy. The SEM results unequivocally demonstrate that the microneedles are designed to improve therapeutic administration by penetrating further into the stratum corneum during application.

Comparative in-vitro release study

Fig. 5g shows the in vitro release results that show significant differences between free AZA, AZA-HBs, and AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs after 48 h. Free AZA had the fastest release rate, contributing to 19% at 1 h, 25% at 4 h, 40% at 10 h, and 45% at 48 h. The rapid profile indicates that the non-encapsulated drug was readily available to the release media, suggesting an insignificant diffusion barrier. On the other hand, AZA-HBs showed a more gradual and controlled release: 15% at 1 h, 20% at 4 h, 39% at 10 h, and 43% at 48 h, suggesting that vesicular entrapment restricted the drug's diffusion and limited its rapid release. AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs had a release profile that was the most consistent, showing 17% at 1 h, 21% at 4 h, 36% at 10 h, 40% at 12 h, 43% at 16 h, 42% at 24 h, 44% at 48 h, and 95% at the optimum formulation. The gradual increase in release indicates that the microneedle matrix provided an additional diffusion barrier in addition to the hyalubilosome vesicle. The dual delivery system most likely provided more controlled, prolonged drug delivery, which is desirable for the treatment of acne, because AZA benefits from local, prolonged exposure rather than rapid release, which can trigger inflammation. The microneedle formulation significantly improved cumulative release compared with AZA-HB alone. Of the systems evaluated, AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs at 24 h achieved 90-92%, AZA-HBs sustained 68-70%, and free AZA accounted for only 43-44%. After 48 h, microneedle formulations achieved 94-95% results, compared with AZA-HBs (69-71%) and free AZA (43-45%). It is therefore proposed that the microneedle array improves both the efficiency and the duration of drug delivery. The polymeric microneedle matrix is crucial for elucidating this phenomenon. PVA and PVP are hydrophilic polymers that rapidly swell in water, maximizing water absorption while minimizing the rate of drug delivery. Chitosan increases mechanical stability, which can slow matrix degradation, improve skin adhesion, and prolong adhesion time. In addition, the inclusion of hyalubilosomes protected the encapsulated azelaic acid, not only limiting rapid degradation but also substantially reducing the initial burst release of unencapsulated formulations (X. Fan et al., 2025b; Stoeva et al., 2025). The incorporation of microneedles into the release medium enables the complex processes of hydration, expansion, and final disintegration or relaxation of the polymeric network, thereby allowing encapsulated AZA-HBs to diffuse freely. These criteria are crucial in the management of acne. AZA is utilized for its antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and keratolytic attributes. A formulation that offers sustained exposure for 24-48 h will improve efficacy while reducing the frequency of administration. The release profile of AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs, compared to free AZA, is more appropriate for the topical treatment of acne because it offers improved release, skin retention, and overall drug availability. The results from this study confirm microneedle technology as a promising drug delivery system, with cumulative release ranging from 17% at 1 h to 95% at 48 h in the optimal formulation. This suggests that incorporating AZA-HBs into PVA/PVP-chitosan microneedles enables control over release profiles, potentially providing improved localized acne treatment.

Kinetics study

The release kinetics indicate that the Korsmeyer-Peppas model best describes all three formulations, suggesting that azelaic acid release is predominantly governed by diffusion, with additional influence from matrix relaxation contingent on the system, as shown in Table 7. Free AZA showed the lowest R2 values for the zero- and first-order models, yet the highest for the Korsmeyer-Peppas model (R2 = 0.948), suggesting a diffusion-controlled release mechanism rather than a constant rate. AZA-HBs exhibited superior alignment with the Korsmeyer-Peppas model (R2 = 0.987) compared to the Higuchi model (R2 = 0.965), indicating that vesicular entrapment enhanced release regulation, with both diffusion and structural relaxation contributing to drug transport. The AZA-HBs-MNs exhibited optimal fit to the Korsmeyer-Peppas model (R2 = 0.980), signifying that the microneedle matrix regulated AZA release while facilitating effective diffusion across the hydrated polymer matrix (Kral et al., 2024; Šedbarė et al., 2023). The n values offer a more accurate elucidation of the releasing mechanism. For free AZA, n = 0.323, which is below 0.45, signifying Fickian diffusion. This indicates that the medication was predominantly released by concentration-driven diffusion. The release of AZA-HBs was regulated by a combination of diffusion, relaxation, and enlargement of the hyalubilosomal matrix (n = 0.628, ranging from 0.45 to 0.89), signifying anomalous transport. For AZA-HBs-MNs, n = 0.393 signifies Fickian diffusion. This indicates that once the microneedles were hydrated, AZA diffused from the system via polymer channels instead of through erosion-dominated release. The statistics indicate a clear advancement in release control from a formulation perspective. Free AZA represents the least regulated system, while AZA-HBs enhance the release profile by vesicular encapsulation. AZA-HBs-MNs provide the most effective transdermal platform, as the microneedle matrix introduces an extra barrier that regulates drug escape while facilitating efficient delivery (Marwa Hassan Hussaen Mohammed et al., 2023a). This is crucial in acne treatment, as sustained local exposure is favored over a transient application that may induce irritation or reduce skin retention time. Korsmeyer-Peppas's comprehensive description of all three systems supports the assertion that diffusion is the principal mechanism governing AZA release in all examined formulations. The Korsmeyer-Peppas model most accurately characterizes the release kinetics of free AZA, AZA-HBs, and AZA-HBs-MNs, indicating diffusion-dominated release across all formulations. Free AZA and AZA-HBs-MNs exhibited Fickian diffusion (n < 0.45), while AZA-HBs revealed anomalous transport (n < 0.89), signifying a blend of diffusion and matrix relaxation inside the hyalubilosomal carrier.

Skin ex vivo permeation study

The ex vivo permeation studies indicate that the AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs exhibit superior performance compared to free AZA or AZA-HBs alone (Fig. 5h). The total penetration after 48 h was around 545–540–538 for free AZA, 2600–2540–2500 for AZA-HBs, and 3400–3450–3400 for AZA-HBs-MNs. This indicates that the microneedle technique was most effective in facilitating transdermal medication delivery. The microneedle formulation enhanced absorption by approximately 6.3 times after 48 h compared to free AZA, whereas AZA-HBs alone raised it by roughly 4.8 times. This unequivocally endorses the advantages of integrating vesicular encapsulation with microneedle-assisted administration. An effective way to understand the profile is to examine the early, middle, and late temporal regions. After one hour, free AZA exhibited little permeation, whereas AZA-HBs attained approximately 100–120, and AZA-HBs-MNs achieved 400–432. After four hours, the differentiation became distinctly apparent: free AZA ranged from 60 to 66, AZA-HBs fluctuated between 230 and 239, whereas AZA-HBs-MNs spanned from 590 to 640. This signifies that the microneedles enabled the prompt transdermal delivery of the drug across the stratum corneum barrier. The hyalubilosomes enhanced drug penetration, although they did not achieve the efficacy of the active microchannel delivery offered by the microneedles (Wu et al., 2022; Zaid Alkilani et al., 2023). The primary mechanistic rationale is that the microneedles presumably generated temporary aqueous microchannels in the skin, thereby promoting the deeper infiltration of AZA-HBs into the tissue and augmenting their diffusion into the viable epidermal and dermal strata. PVA and PVP absorb moisture and expand upon touch, enabling release. Conversely, Chitosan helps preserve the needle's integrity and may improve skin adhesion. Microneedle technology improves permeability and modifies the distribution's trajectory and velocity by substantially diminishing the integrity of the epidermal barrier. The incremental rise from 435 to 540 at 1 h to 3400–3450 at 48 h signifies a steady permeation pattern rather than a sudden spike. This feature is advantageous for acne treatment, as AZA is more effective when retained on the skin for extended periods rather than quickly evaporating from the application site. In contrast, free AZA demonstrated slow and limited penetration, as expected, because it must penetrate the intact stratum corneum without the assistance of a carrier. AZA-HBs alone significantly enhanced penetration, reaching approximately 2600–2500 at 48 h. This demonstrates that the HBs carrier enhanced the solubility and transport of azelaic acid more efficiently than the unencapsulated formulation. At the last time point, the microneedle formulation exhibited an efficacy around 30–35% more than that of AZA-HBs (Mohammed et al., 2023b). This illustrates that the synergistic application of HBs and microneedles surpasses the efficacy of utilizing either method in isolation. This corroborates the principle of dual delivery: the carrier improves the organization and stability of the drugs, while the microneedles breach the primary barrier of the skin.

The results may also be compared to systems that use nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs) for AZA and are designed to enhance dermal retention. Kumari and colleagues synthesized AZA-loaded NLCs with a mean particle size of 81.57 ± 9.6 nm, with a PDI of 0.208 ± 0.021 and a zeta potential of −29.3 ± 1.21 mV. Their NLC gel exhibited an ex vivo permeation of 21.06 ± 0.99%, which is still lower than the permeation of AZA gel (38.71 ± 1.47%) and AZA solution (78.79 ± 2.52%), while the AZA-gel solution (63.96 ± 4.56%) and the AZA solution (4.78 ± 1.1%) and the AZA-gel solution (15.12 ± 3.2%) demonstrated significantly lower retention. This suggests that the primary function of nanocarriers is local dermal retention and not systemic retention. On the other hand, the AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs currently designed aim to enhance AZA delivery while maintaining a constant local therapeutic effect by providing sustained local release and dermal retention through microneedle technology.

In vitro study

Cellular viability study

The findings from the cell viability study demonstrated a clear level of difference between AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs and free azelaic acid, as illustrated in Fig. 6a. The blank control showed near 100% cell viability, indicating that the test materials were responsible for the observed changes. At all concentrations tested, free AZA was cell-lethal to HaCaT cells, demonstrating that the unencapsulated free form of the acid was highly toxic to these cells. The microneedle-loaded hyalubilosomal formulation demonstrated a significant improvement in viability with increasing concentration, achieving approximately 54–60% at the highest concentration tested. This indicates a significant improvement in biocompatibility. The carrier system significantly contributes to this improvement. Unencapsulated azelaic acid is in form that is readily available for cellular interaction and could potentially lead to cell membrane disruption and impact the cell's mitochondria or other critical cell functions. The formulation design, in which the drug is incorporated into HBs and then integrated into the PVA/PVP-chitosan microneedle matrix, promotes delayed, controlled release. This also decreases the probability of cells being exposed to the drug at the same time; thus, the cells appear to be less negatively impacted by the drug. This is a very important property in a topical formulation because it indicates that the treatment can be provided with less pain and irritation and increased safety (Elhabal et al., 2024). The improvement of survivability at the high concentration of the microneedle formulation may be due to the carrier's ability to protect and slowly release the drug rather than a direct dose response relationship to the drug itself. The formulation appears to ensure the active ingredient remains effective while remaining cell-compatible. Compared to free azelaic acid, the formulation AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs shows improvement in skincare applications.

Antioxidant activity assay

All the samples displayed some degree of antioxidant activity, even though the DPPH results were varied, as illustrated in Fig. 6b. Ascorbic acid was the most effective positive control. Unbound azelaic acid was also effective as an antioxidant. At the lowest concentration, the antioxidant scavenging activity was minimal, and was maximal at the highest concentration. Antioxidant activity for the AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs was significant, although it was slightly less than free azelaic acid at most concentrations. The slight decrease is due to some of the azelaic acid being sequestered in the hyalu-bilosomes and microneedle matrix during the assay, so it could not be readily available to the DPPH radicals. The formulation does not change the antioxidant activity; it only results in a delayed manifestation of the activity (Khan et al., 2025; Quintero-Rincón et al., 2025). Overall, for a delivery system of this nature, secluding the azelaic acid is an acceptable trade-off; the prolonged activity in the antioxidant assay supports the formulation's intended purpose. The increase in scavenging activity correlates to the concentration, which indicates that the antioxidant properties of the azelaic acid were retained. The microneedle formulation demonstrated a significant radical scavenging effect even at high levels, indicating that a certain amount of the medicine was sufficient to respond to DPPH. In situations such as this, this is of utmost importance, as oxidative stress increases inflammation and makes acne and other skin conditions worse. It is therefore extremely important to maintain antioxidant activity to improve overall health. The results of the two experiments combined show that AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs is the most secure and effective formulation. Among the tested actives, free AZA had the most pronounced direct impact on living cells but was also the most destructive to the cellular integrity. The HBs combined with the microneedles approach decreased cytotoxicity while retaining a considerable fraction of the antioxidant properties. This suggests that the formulation will be beneficial for topical acne treatment with localized action while sparing the integrity of surrounding skin cells. Cell viability tests showed that free azelaic acid completely destroyed HaCaT cells at all measured doses. In comparison, AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs showed a remarkable improvement in cell compatibility, with cell viability reaching approximately 54-60% at the highest concentration. In the DPPH test, unformulated azelaic acid had a slightly better free radical scavenging ability than the formulated system, but AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs still possessed considerable antioxidant activity. The results suggest that the combination of azelaic acid with HBs and microneedles decreased cytotoxicity while maintaining antioxidant activity, thereby confirming the formulation's suitability for topical use.

The heightened activity relative to both AZA suspension (1) and blank microneedles (2) indicates that including AZA into the HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs microneedle matrix significantly enhances its bactericidal efficacy. The blank formulation demonstrated moderate inhibition, likely attributable to the intrinsic antibacterial properties of chitosan (Cs) and possibly components derived from hesperidin. The AZA-loaded formulation demonstrated comparable or superior inhibitory zones relative to the standard antibiotic (gentamycin) in multiple cases, highlighting its strong, broad-spectrum antibacterial effectiveness. Minimum inhibitory concentration activity

The MIC findings suggest that AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs were superior to other treatments, especially when attacking Gram-positive bacteria, as demonstrated in Fig. 7c. The microneedles exhibited a MIC against Staphylococcus aureus of 7-8 μg/mL. The AZA solution had a MIC of 16–19 μg/mL, while the blank microneedles had a MIC of 29–33 μg/mL. The smaller MIC was a result of the formulation needing a smaller amount of a drug to achieve a suppression of bacteria growth, irrefutably indicating superior activity against corresponding pathogens. In a like manner, the optimized microneedles against S. mutans exhibited a MIC of 7–8 μg/mL. The AZA suspension was 6–8 μg/mL, while the blank microneedles were 30–33 μg/mL. The low MIC demonstrated that the final microneedle formulation had superior efficacy at a lower concentration for inhibiting bacterial growth. This may be due to newly designed carrier systems that have improved the controlled release of azelaic acid, thereby helping maintain sustained contact with bacteria. The MIC of AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs against E. coli was 50–52 μg/mL, and blank microneedles and AZA suspension had a MIC of 30–32 μg/mL and 15–17 μg/mL, respectively. The formulation exhibited lower activity against E. coli in the MIC test compared with the zone-of-inhibition test. This is a normal occurrence, as the two test samples assess various aspects of antibacterial behavior. Diffusion, which sometimes benefits samples that spread readily across the agar, contrasts with MIC, which entails a more rigorous sampling of the liquid environments of the sample. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of the AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs against Staphylococcus epidermidis was 60-65 μg/mL. While this is worse than the blank formulation (125-128 μg/mL), it is better than the AZA suspension (62-64 μg/mL), signifying moderate antimicrobial activity. Compared to the blank carrier, the microneedle formulation had better diffusive and active carrier activity. The carrier showed higher active carrier activity in the solid diffusion experiment than in the MIC assay. The overall activity pattern shows that the microneedles enhanced the antibacterial activity of the AZA in the agar diffusion, and the microneedle formulation also exhibited better drug delivery and local bioavailability at the site of action. However, the blank HBs-PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs showed significant MIC diffusion, likely due to the properties of the polymer matrix and the partial diffusion of the microneedles and the agent. However, the diffusion of the azelaic acid showed to have the greatest antibacterial effect (Shalaby et al., 2026). The azelaic acid now shows better diffusion in the MIC activity, while the blank carrier shows better activity in the bioavailability.

However, effective therapy, especially the AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MN system, can restore EGF levels to normal, indicating that the wound has healed and balance has been restored to the skin.

Conclusion

This study shows that azelaic acid-loaded hyalubilosomes distributed within dissolving PVA/PVP-chitosan microneedles can successfully stimulate the development of a system that addresses the main delivery barriers to azelaic acid in acne treatment. The proposed system utilizes the benefits of hyalubilosomes, such as the vesicular encapsulation and hydration, combined with the sustained release and the ability of dissolving microneedles to penetrate the skin. Thus, AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs enhanced local drug delivery and achieved a multifunctional anti-acne effect through combined antimicrobial, antibiofilm, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory effects. Based on these findings, the therapeutic advantage of the optimized system is presumed to extend beyond improved AZA permeation and to include an increase in local biological activity at the site of inflamed skin. The resolution of skin inflammation, together with the regulation of Nrf2/HO-1/MYD88 and histopathological markers, confirms the versatility of this system as a localized, minimally invasive, and non-antibiotic approach for the treatment of acne. This study, however, is not immune to some limitations. The biological study was undertaken in a preclinical rat ear acne-like inflammation model, which does not fully reflect the abnormality of acne vulgaris in humans. The long-term safety of repeated microneedle use, patient compliance, large-scale manufacturability, and clinical outcomes have not been evaluated here. Thus, further research is needed to assess repeated dermal safety and to study long-term stable storage, retention within skin follicles, potential irritation, and safety for therapeutic use in humans or in clinical settings. To provide a comprehensive evaluation of the feasibility of AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs microneedle patch system to provide an effective treatment system for acne utilizing intra-dermal delivery, future studies should develop a user optimized microneedle patch design, validate the proposed mechanisms of action for acne in models that present more clinical relevance, evaluate the safety profile for sustained repeated use, and prioritize studies that evaluate the trade-off between scale and the manufacturing process. AZA-HBs/PVA/PVP-Cs-MNs have the potential to be a safe and effective alternative to existing topical Azelaic Acid Formulations, however, this will only be confirmed following clinical studies.
