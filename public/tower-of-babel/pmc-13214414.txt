Helicobacter pylori infection, a risk factors for pulmonary tuberculosis: prevalence, associated factors and clinical profile among treatment-naive tuberculosis patients in Yaounde-Cameroon
Authors: Caporal Mih Kahabiné, Claire Françoise Bitchong Ekono, Laure Brigitte Kouitcheu Mabeku
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe persistence of gastric inflammation in Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection profoundly alters gastric immune response and predispose to gastric hypochlorhydria that may be a risk factor for other infections. This study sought to determine potential risk factors and clinical profile associated with prevalence of H. pylori among newly diagnosed pulmonary tuberculosis (TB) patients in Cameroon.MethodsThis was a cross–sectional study conducted from June 2024 to March 2025 at Jamot Hospital of Yaoundé-Cameroon. A total of 305 newly diagnosed pulmonary tuberculosis patients were enrolled. The study was approved by Ethical Committees. Each subject gave a written consent. For each patient, body mass index (BMI) and direct inquiry about respiratory diseases symptoms were done. Sputum samples were tested for the presence of Mycobacterium tuberculosis using TB LAMP assay. H. pylori antigen in human stool samples ( Evancare medical, China) was used for H. pylori detection. IBM SPSS Statistics 22 was used for data analysis.ResultsThe overall prevalence of H. pylori infection and TB was of 65.90% (n = 201/305) and 58.36% (n = 178/305) respectively. Among TB-positive patients, the prevalence of H. pylori co-infection was 60.67% (n = 108/178). Furthermore, patients with H. pylori infected were 1.77 time more likely to be TB-positive (OR = 1.77; 95%IC = 1.081–2.908; p = 0.02). Among studied variables, being married (p = 0.0096), having low-income level (p = 0.0422), alcohol consumption (p ˂ 0.0001), and low BMI (p = 0.0068) were significantly associated to TB/H.pylori co-infection. The strength of the association between TB and nausea, TB and vomiting increases with co-infection. The OR between TB and night sweats in tuberculosis alone was 39.63-time lower in case of co-infection.ConclusionOur data reveals a significant high prevalence of H. pylori infection among TB patients. Specific factor such as low-income level, being married, alcohol consumption, and underweight are risk factor for H. pylori-TB co-infection. Introduction

Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) is a Gram-negative spiral-shaped bacterium that exhibits microaerophilic growth and exclusively colonizes the human stomach [1]. H. pylori infection is a significant global health burden, affecting around 50% of the worldwide population [2]. Epidemiological studies have reported lower prevalence in Northern European (34.7% in France) and higher prevalence in Eastern European countries such as Portugal and Poland [3, 4]. In Asia, the prevalence of H. pylori infection remains relatively low in certain countries, 37.6% in Japan and 53% in China [5]. In Africa, the overall prevalence is estimated to be around 62.03%, with maximum value estimated to be 97.0% [6]. Central Africa has a mean prevalence of 63.38% [6], with prevalence of 91% in Congo [7], 77.8% in Democratic Republic of Congo [8], 64.39% in Cameroon [9], and 70.8% in Burundi [10].

H. pylori infection is strongly correlated with the development of gastroduodenal disorders, such as inflammation in the stomach lining, gastric ulcer with mucosal erosion, and gastric adenocarcinoma. Additionally, H. pylori has been linked to various extragastric diseases, including iron-deficiency anemia, idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura, and vitamin B12 deficiency [11, 12]. Moreover, both chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [13, 14] and pulmonary tuberculosis are more prevalent in peptic ulcer patients than in the general population [15].

Tuberculosis (TB) is a chronic bacterial infection caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, which affects the lungs. TB is a major public health concern, as it is the leading cause of mortality attributed to antimicrobial resistance. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately one-third of the global population is infected with tuberculosis, with an annual prevalence of 6–7 million new cases [16, 17]. Africa countries report a significant burden of tuberculosis, with an estimated prevalence of 24% [18]. Both TB and H. pylori infection are more common among individuals from low socioeconomic status and those living in overcrowded condition, presumably because M. tuberculosis is transmitted through airborne, from person to person, while H. pylori is transmitted mainly via the oral-oral or fecal-oral contact, often within households.

While epidemiological similarities exist between the two infections, to date, no clear association has been established between H. pylori and TB infection [19]. However, a prior cross-sectional study found an association between prevalence of H. pylori infection and having had clinical TB, suggesting the two infections may be related [20]. Also, a study conducted in Peru shown high prevalence rates of H. pylori infection of 63% among tuberculosis patients [21]. These findings led to the hypothesis that: “H pylori might be associated with TB and play a role in developing the disease”. In contrast, a case-control study found no difference in seroprevalence of H. pylori infection between patients with and without clinical TB [22]. Furthermore, most available studies exploring the relationship between infection by the two bacteria are limited to case-control studies based on seroprevalence data with small sample sizes. Because of the conflicting reported results, there is a need to investigated on the association between the two diseases.

With a prevalence of 69% among TB symptomatic patient attending Laquintinie Hospital in Douala, Cameroon has one of the highest reported TB prevalence rates [23]. Concerning H. pylori infection, the country also has one of the highest reported prevalence rates in Central Africa with a prevalence of 64.39% among subjects aged more than 35 years old [9], 91.03% among asymptomatic subjects [24] and 26.0% among children from birth to 5 years old [25]. Neverthless, no data on the prevalence of co-infection between H. pylori and TB has been investigated in the country. Therefore, we have performed this study to investigate H. pylori active infection in TB patients, the clinical profile and potential factors associated with this co-morbidity.

Method

Study setting

This study was conducted at the Jamot Hospital Yaoundé in Cameroon. The Jamot Hospital Yaoundé is a Category 2 hospital in the Cameroonian’ healthcare system and the reference hospital for the management of respiratory diseases in Cameroon, located in the Centre region of the country. This hospital comprises more than six (06) clinical service units including pulmonology, psychiatry, physiotherapy, neurology, cardiology and care for people living with HIV/AIDS, which are fully operational. It receives between 10,000 and 15,000 patients per year and generally records between 1,500 and 2,500 cases of tuberculosis per year. Although decentralization efforts are underway to treat patients in local centres, the Jamot Hospital Yaoundé remains the main hub health facility in the management of TB, where cases of treatment failure or relapse (complex cases of multidrug-resistant tuberculosis or HIV co-infection) around the country are transferred. As illustration, Jamot Hospital alone accounts for approximately 25% to 35% of tuberculosis patients admitted in the Centre region among the 25,000 cases nationwide in 2022. It has advanced technical facilities for the management of TB (such as GeneXpert molecular tests, Truenat molecular test and TB LAMP molecular test) that are not found in others centres.

Study population

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the Jamot Hospital Yaoundé, the reference hospital for the management of respiratory diseases in Cameroon, located in the Centre region of the country. From June 2024 to March 2025, all patients aged 15 years or older with the symptoms of respiratory diseases (cough for more than two weeks, night sweats, night fever, fatigue, loss of appetite, weight loss, vomiting, Hematemesis, nausea) attended the selected health Centre were referred for diagnosis of H. pylori infection and tuberculosis. A written consent was obtained from all eligible patients before inclusion. For patients aged 15 years old, we obtained written consent from their parental or guardian prior to inclusion in the study.

A consecutive sampling method was used for data collection from all volunteer eligible patients. Exclusion criteria were: patient under anti-TB therapy, immunocompromised patients, patient with previous personal history of tuberculosis. Pregnant and breastfeeding women were also excluded from the study. The number of patients enrolled was not based on a pre-selected statistical power.

Variables

A structured questionnaire developed for this study (Supplementary file) was used to collect sociodemographic data (age, sex) and potential factors associated with Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection. This included information on income level, tobacco and alcohol consumption, family history of tuberculosis, community living or overcrowding living condition, medical history (Diseases leading to a weakening of the immune system such as diabetes mellitus, HIV/AIDS, autoimmune diseases, .), medication history (anti-TB and anti-H pylori therapy).

For all participants, direct inquiry about respiratory diseases symptoms (cough > 2 weeks, night sweats, night fever, fatigue, unintentional weight loss, vomiting, hematemesis, nausea, maleana) was done by a resident pneumologist and the clinical characteristics were recorded. Height and weight were measured for each participant, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight by height squared (kg/m²). The body mass index (BMI) of participants was classified according to World Health Organization (WHO) criteria: underweight (< 18.5), normal weight (18.5–24.9), overweight (25.0–29.9) and obese (≥ 30.0) [26].

Sample collection

Stool sample collection

To obtain reliable results with the Evancare/OEM antigen test, participants were given a sterile stool collection container with appropriate instructions on how to deposit the sample without any contamination. Appropriate instructions included; (1) Patients must not collect the sample directly from the toilet bowl to avoid the risk of contamination by water, urine or cleaning products, (2) Patients must use a clean, dry container or place plastic wrap over the toilet bowl to collect the stool before transferring it to the container, (3) Patient should avoid to mix the stool with urine or toilet paper, (4) The container should be closed immediately after collection, (5) The stool should be bring to the laboratory within 2 h of collection, (6) The sample size must be approximately 5 g or 1 to 2 ml if the stool is liquid.

Sputum sample

Each individual was requested for a sputum sample. Study participants were given a sterile sputum collection container after the consultation with appropriate instructions on how to collect it. Sputum samples were collected at 5:00 a.m. after 8 h-fasting according to local programmatic protocols from the National Tuberculosis Control Program (NTCP) in Cameroon. The early morning sputum typically contains the highest concentration of M. tuberculosis.

Sample analysis

H. pylori detection in stool sample

A rapid antigen test was performed to detect H. pylori in stool sample using the Evancare/OEM commercial kit (One step rapid test, China). The test was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cassette was labelled with the participant identification number. Then 50 mg of stool was collected with a specimen applicator and applied to specimen collection tube containing 2 ml of the dilution buffer. Specimen tube was then shaken vigorously to mix specimen and buffer and was allowed to stand for 2 min. Next, three drops of the mixture were applied to the test nitrocellulose membrane according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reading and recording of the results were done after at 5, 10 and 15 min. Positive samples demonstrated two red lines [one line in the control region (C) and another one in the test region (T)], while negative samples had just one red line in the control region (C) test strip. The test was considered as invalid in case of the absence of line on the test strip. The test had a sensitivity of 90–95% and a specificity of >9.9% compare to urease, urea breath test and culture methods as described in the manual procedure provided with the kit [27].

TB-LAMP assay

The TB detection was performed using TB-LAMP (Eiken Chemical Company Ltd, Tokyo, Japan). TB-LAMP is a manual molecular test with LAMP for the detection of Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC) [28]. Eiken TB-LAMP detects MTBC by targeting IS6110 and gyrB with a limit of detection of 156 CFU/ml [29]. The LAMP-TB test was performed using a sputum processing kit (Loopamp PURE DNA extraction kit; Eiken, Tokyo, Japan) and a Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) amplification detection kit (Loopamp MTBC detection kit; Eiken).

Briefly, 60 µl of sputum sample was transferred into heating tubes using a pipette. The contents of the tube were homogenized by shaking, and the tube was incubated in the HUmaloop heating unit at 90 °C for 5 min for DNA extraction using Loopamp PURE DNA extraction kit. The heating tube was fitted into an absorbent tube, then 30 µl of DNA was eluted from the absorbent tube and transferred into injection caps, where it was mixed with lyophilized reagents (MTB amplification detection kit) and incubated at 67 °C for 40 min. The final result of the TB LAMP assay was obtained using ultraviolet fluorescence detection [30]. A positive result was indicated by the appearance of green fluorescence, while a negative result shows no fluorescence. TBLAMP has a sensitivity of 89.8% and a specificity of 97.1% compare to PCR.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS software version 27 (IBM Corp., Chicago, USA). The quantitative variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and qualitative variables as number and percentage. Comparison between qualitative variables was performed using the Chi-square test and Fisher’s exact probability test. The odds ratio (OR) and 95% corresponding confidence interval were used to summarize the strength of the association. For continuous variables, comparison between groups was performed using Unpaired t-test and the corresponding p value was determined. A logistic regression analysis was done to confirm the impact of exposure variables on the relationship between H pylori/TB co-infection. Significance was accepted at the level of p < 0.05.

Demographic, exposure factors and TB clinical signs of the study population

Regarding lifestyle and socioeconomic status, the majority of the study population belonged to the middle-income class (n = 181, 59.3%), followed by those from low-income (n = 88, 28.9%) and high-income groups (n = 36, 11.8%). Most of the participants resided in urban areas (n = 276, 90.49%) or lived in Community (n = 163, 53.4%). Few participants were smokers (25.57%, n = 78) or had family history of tuberculosis (13.8%, n = 42), while the majority were alcohol consumers (84.3%, n = 257). Regarding BMI values, 59.7% (n = 182) of participants were with a normal weight, 28.9% (n = 88) were overweight or obese, and 11.5% (n = 35) were underweight (Table 1).

Clinical signs of tuberculosis in the study population

Regarding clinical symptoms, 99.0% of the study population had a cough lasting more than two weeks. For the other detected symptoms, weight loss, fatigue, night sweats, nausea and night fever were found in the rate of 97.4%, 59.3%, 58.03%, 55.73%, and 57.37%, respectively. Gastrointestinal symptoms, such as nausea and vomiting were also recorded in the rate of 57.37 and 38.03%, while hematemesis and melena were less common (Table 1).

Factors associated with H. pylori infection

The overall prevalence of H. pylori infection among the 305 participants with suspected tuberculosis was 65.9% (n = 201). Significant higher rates of H. pylori infection were recorded among participants from middle and low-income levels (71.8% and 64.8%, respectively; p = 0.001), smokers (78.2%; p = 0.008), and alcohol consumers (65.8%; p = 0.001) (Table 2).

Concerning exposure factors, low-income level (OR = 18.30 [4.32–77.57], p < 0.001), alcohol consumption (OR = 8.32 [3.86–17.96], p < 0.001), and community living (OR = 2.02 [1.27–3.21], p = 0.003) were significantly associated with an increased likelihood to TB. Similarly, underweight participants (BMI < 18.5) were significantly more likely to be TB-positive compared to those with a normal BMI (OR = 4.91 [1.85–13.03], p < 0.001), (Table 3).

Analysis of clinical signs in relation to TB status revealed that cough > 2 weeks was present in nearly all participants, suggesting its lacks of specificity for TB diagnosis in this cohort. However, night sweats (OR = 44.91, p < 0.0001), nausea (OR = 1.98, p = 0.004), and night fever (OR = 1.62, p = 0.04) were significantly associated with TB infection. Melena showed a significant low frequency among TB patients (OR = 0.0976, p < 0.0001), suggesting a multifactorial origin of this symptom rather than a direct link to TB status alone (Table 3).

Tuberculosis/H. pylori co-infection in the study population

When examining TB status in participants in relation to H. pylori infection, we found that 60.67% (108/178) of TB positive patients were H. pylori infected, whereas 39.3% of TB positive patients were H. pylori non-infected. This difference was significant (X2 = 5.178, p = 0.0226) (Table 3; Fig. 2). The strength of the association between TB positivity and H. pylori infection shows that H. pylori infected participants were 1.77 (1.081–2.908) times more likely to be TB positive with significant p-value (p = 0.02) (Table 3).

Marital status was significantly associated with co-infection, with married participants more affected to co-infection compared to single participants. This association persisted after multivariate adjustment (adjusted OR = 2.26; 95% CI: 1.12–6.30; p = 0.02).

Similarly, participants with low income were significantly more likely to have TB/H. pylori co-infection (p = 0.02), while none of the participants with high income level were found within TB/H. pylori negative group.

Alcohol consumption appeared to be one of the factors strongly associated with co-infection. More than 93% of co-infected patients consumed alcohol regularly, with a high risk in both univariate and multivariate analyses (adjusted OR = 40.32; 95% CI: 12.45–130.63; p < 0.001).

Regarding body mass index, all the underweight patients were co-infected. Underweight status was strongly associated with co-infection, with the highest point estimate observed in the co-infected group [OR = 15.98 (0.94–271.8), p = 0.0068], (Table 4). Moreover, the mean BMI value was significantly lower in TB/H. pylori co-infected patients compared to non-co-infected patients (21.97 ± 5.18 vs. 24.85 ± 2.67; p = 0.002).

Impact of H. pylori infection on tuberculosis clinical profile

Regarding clinical signs, classic TB symptoms such as night sweats, nausea, and night fever were significantly associated with TB infection (Table 3).

When comparing H. pylori/TB co-infected and H. pylori/TB non-co-infected participants in relation to clinical signs of TB, we found that symptoms such as night sweats (p = 0.8445), night fever (p = 0.5556), and nausea (p = 0.1148) were no longer significantly associated to TB. In contrast, symptoms such as malaena (p˂ 0.0001) and vomiting (p = 0.00287) have become significantly associated to TB, with melaena and vomiting most prevalent among non-co-infected (4.62 vs. 32.35%) and co-infected patients (38.88 vs. 19.44%), respectively (Table 5; Fig. 5).

Discussion

The TB-LAMP recommended by WHO’s 2016 as a replacement test for sputum smear microscopy and as a follow-on test for sputum smear-negative specimens in adults [17, 28], was used to assess the TB status in this study population with respiratory diseases symptoms.

Among the 305 participants with respiratory diseases symptoms enrolled, 178 were TB positive, given an overall TB prevalence of 58.36%. This prevalence is comparable to the prevalence of 52% of tuberculosis cases reported in Cameroon according to WHO [32], the prevalence of 57% found in Yaoundé and Douala Metropolis [33], in the North and Far-North Regions of Cameroon [34]. Compared with the prevalence from others Africa country, the present prevalence of TB in patients with respiratory diseases symptoms seems to be low than the 69% rate reported in Ivory Coast [32], the 66.2% in Chad [35], and the 67.42% in Senegal [36]. The entire mentioned reports on TB prevalence in Africa countries as well as in the present study showed that TB remains a common health problem in Africa [37], despite the efforts of the government and its health partners to eradicate the disease.

Approximately all (99.98%) the TB positive patients were aged between 25 and 55 years old. Similarly, a highest prevalence of TB was recorded in age group 26–55 years old seven years ago in Yaoundé [38], in age group 21–50 years old with rate of 72.4% [39] and 86.6% [40] in Mali. Moreover, the mean age of 41.28 ± 14.11 compared to 39.68 ± 13.09 years old was recorded among TB positive and negative patients respectively. Closely mean ages in TB positive patients were observed in Cameroon; 41.87 ± 12 years from Dschang [41] and 40.6 ± 13.4 years from Yaoundé [38]. All these findings are on line with reports from WHO stating that age group 15–50 years accounted for 75% of tuberculosis patients [37]. The high prevalence of tuberculosis is among people aged 15 to 50 years can be explained by several factors related to this age group. In fact, people from this age group are more social and professional active, more subject to migration and exposure to factors contributing to tuberculosis contagion.

Male were more subjected to TB than female, even if the difference was not significant (p = 0.47). Approximately 80% (70.78%) versus 29.21% of TB positive patients were male, given sex ratio of infection of 2.24 in favor of males. Dialo [39] and Dicko [42], which reported a sex ratio of 3.57 in favor of male respectively in Mali, found a similar trend. Also, a TB rate among male of 92% was found in Senegal [43], and a rate of 66.1% in Chad [44]. Such observation can be explained by the fact that male, who make up the majority of the productive workforce, are more exposed to risk factors of TB. Another reason could be the fact that male’s daily activities take place outside the house, which increases the chance of contact with extra-familial contagious cases.

Participant from marital status were 3.57 [(2.12–5.88), p = 0.001] time more prone to develop TB infection, suggesting that being married is associated with an increased risk of tuberculosis. Also, patients living in community and those having family history of TB were 2.020 [(1.272–3.207), 0.0027] and 1.44 [(0.80–2.59), p = 0.21]) time at higher risk of TB infection, respectively. This confirms that marital status, living in community and having family history, which favor close contact with individual, facilitates transmission of tuberculosis. In fact, household crowding and living in community are strong and independent risk factors for person-to-person transmitted diseases [28, 45, 46]. In addition, TB usually spreads even without physical contact because of its airborne transmission occurrence.

Our finding also showed that patients from urban zone were 3.803 [(1.409–10.26), p = 0.0051] time at a high risk to develop TB. The majority of the TB patients were from urban areas (90.49%), a finding which is closely to that observed in Kigali (Rwanda), where 87% of their TB patients were from urban areas [47]. Also, Nguepy et al. found that the majority of their TB patients (64.9%) were from urban areas [41].

The mean living standard of the TB patients was 49.07%. A higher TB rate was noticed in low or poorly skilled class, suggesting a dreadful trend of TB infectivity due to poor socioeconomic status of participants (OR = 18.30 [(4.317–77.57), p ˂ 0.0001]. Similarly, a previous study reported that 77.97% of their TB patients were with poor living standard [41]. Poor socioeconomic and sanitary conditions are favorable factors for TB contagion. People from low or poorly skilled class usually live in overcrowded condition, with poorly ventilated rooms and are more likely prone to airborne transmission occurs diseases. Moreover, individuals exposed to precarious socioeconomic conditions due to poverty were often subjected to malnutrition. In fact, poverty enhanced level of transmission due to malnutrition, poor hygiene and unaffordable heath care. Our data demonstrated that high BMI is negatively associated with an increased incidence of TB infection, an expected result since malnutrition is recognized as a risk factor for tuberculosis.

The low frequency of TB positive in smokers may simply reflected their lower size in our population. Most of the TB patients were alcohol consumers (84.26%), which is similar to the data from Dlinga in N’Djamena, Chad [44].

Considering TB infection and clinical symptoms among the study population, our data showed significant positive association between TB and nausea (p = 0.004), night sweats (p ˂ 0.0001), and night fever (p = 0.0399), indicating these symptoms as significant suggestive of TB infection in the study population. Hematemesis and melaena were commonly found in non-TB patients than TB positive patients. Such observation may suggest that hematemesis and melaena may have a multifactorial cause. However, the lower rates of hematemesis and melaena in the TB infected patients simply reflect the lower number of these specific symptoms in the study population (Hematemesis n = 92, Melaena n = 34) (Table 1).

H. pylori infection was detected in 65.90% of the 305 suspected tuberculosis patients. None of the evaluated sociodemographic factors affected H. pylori infection with significant p-value. However, H. pylori infection was more prevalent among single patients (62.18 vs. 37.81%), among male (68.15 vs. 31.84%) and in age group 25 to 35 years old (31.34%).

Among the exposure factors evaluated, income level (p = 0.001), tobacco consumption (p = 0.008), and alcohol consumption (p = 0.001) significantly affected H. pylori infection prevalence, with a higher prevalence among patients from low (64.77%) and middle (71.82%) income level, tobacco consumers (78.20%), and alcohol consumers (65.75%). The present results agree with previous studies reporting that H. pylori infection increase with increasing dose of nicotine and alcohol [48].

As TB and H. pylori co-infection is concerned, a significant positive association was found between tuberculosis and H. pylori infection, with TB positive participants 1.77(1.08–2.90) time more likely to be H. pylori infected (p = 0.02). The current result regarding tuberculosis-H. pylori co-infection is consistent with previous reports; a study conducted in Athens (Greece) [49], a large cross-sectional study conducted in a southern China population [50], a recent study assessing the prevalence of tuberculin skin test positivity in newly hospitalized patients [51]. Furthermore, studies conducted by Sharon et al. [52] and Roussos [21] also found a positive association between H. pylori infection and TB in their sample population. On the others hands, a study highlights the immunological influence of the concomitant presence of H. pylori on the severity of tuberculosis. In fact, it has been demonstrated in mice that colonization with Helicobacter pylori alters the gut microbiota and compromises the immune system’s ability to control the growth of Mycobacterium tuberculosis, leading to more severe pulmonary tuberculosis [53]. Authors incriminated the presence of common host genetic inductor factors as the reason of the susceptibility to both H. pylori and Mycobacterium tuberculosis. In fact, it has been revealed that HLA-DQ serotype which increases susceptibility to H. pylori infection [54, 55] may enhanced mycobacterial survival and replication [56]. In contrast, study conducted by Torres et al. in Lima (Peru) found no association between tuberculosis and H. pylori infection [19]. Also, Sanaka et al. did not found a difference in H. pylori seroprevalence in patients on anti-tuberculosis chemotherapy for less or more than three months compared to control subjects [22, 57].

The distribution of TB/H. pylori co-infection in relation to the investigated variables was also evaluated. As demographic factors is concerned; gender, education level, and age of participants did not show any significant association in both TB infection alone or in co-infection with H. pylori. Similarly, Perry et al. reported that these factors have little impact on the prevalence of tuberculosis alone or in co-infection [52].

Regarding potential exposure factors investigated, married patients compared to single, patients from urban zone compared to those from rural zone were 3.57 [(2.12–5.88), p = 0.001] and 3.803 [(1.409–10.26), p = 0.0051] at higher risk to develop TB/H. pylori co-infection. This positive relationship persists even after adjustment with confounders, but with significant p-value only against marital status. The present high ORs of tuberculosis infection alone and tuberculosis-H. pylori co-infection in relation to marital status is consistent with the literature emphasizing the role of social status in exposure and vulnerability to tuberculosis [58] and underlining the impact of social determinants on microbial interactions [53]. Similar to our finding, a higher rate of co-infection in married people and participant from urban areas has also been reported in some settings, suggesting that these environments cause complex bacterial interactions through exposure to unfavorable health conditions [59].

None of the participant from higher income class were recorded among those with both TB and H. pylori infection. Patients from low-income class were 8.938[(0.5149–155.1), p = 0.04] at higher risk to develop co-infection. Various epidemiological studies in sub-Saharan Africa have highlighted the impact of socio-economic factors, particularly precariousness on the prevalence of tuberculosis and frequent bacterial co-infections such as H. pylori [37, 60].

Alcohol consumers were 34.63[(11.95–100.3), p ˂ 0.0001] and 40.32[(12.45-130.63), p < 0.001] at higher risk to develop co-infection before and after adjustment with confounders. Such observation confirms the evidence that alcohol permanently impairs immunity and promotes various infections [61].

Low mean BMI values were frequently observed among co-infected patients in this study. Moreover, none of the participant with underweight were recorded among patients from TB/H. pylori non-infected group. This supports the notion that co-infections and nutritional factors interact to modulate the susceptibility and severity of infectious diseases. In fact, malnutrition and low BMI are well recognized as aggravating factors for tuberculosis and have also been described as affecting the dynamics of gastric infections [37, 61]. The positive significant relationship between low body weight, low income and co-infection strongly suggest that a low socio-economic level, precarious hygiene conditions, malnutrition appear as the main risk factors for infections [9, 62].

Clinical signs of tuberculosis such as nausea, night sweats, and night fever were highly suggestive of TB infection in the study population, confirming their clinical relevance in TB diagnostic suspicion in our milieu. This finding is in line with reports describing nausea, and night sweats as typical clinical signs of tuberculosis [63] or as common and suggestive symptoms of active tuberculosis [32].

The association between TB and these suggestive clinical signs were less marked or modified in co-infected patients compared to non-co-infection ones, highlighting a possible clinical change of the classic clinical picture of tuberculosis with co-infection. In fact, our data showed that while night sweats (p = 0.8445), night fever (p = 0.5556), and nausea (p = 0.1148) were no longer significantly associated to TB with co-infection, malaena (p˂ 0.0001) and vomiting (p = 0.00287) have become significantly associated, with melaena most prevalent among non-co-infected and vomiting most prevalent among co-infected patients (Table 5). Moreover, vomiting has a significant higher OR value in co-infection than in case of TB alone (1.979(1.234–3.174), 0.004), suggesting an additional intensification of vomiting as TB symptom in the presence of H. pylori. This result confirms that clinical signs such as night fever or night sweats are specific of TB, unlike nausea and vomiting which are more commonly associated with digestive disorders [63] such as H. pylori infection. Thus, nausea and vomiting appear to be of little relevance in characterizing tuberculosis alone. The intensification of vomiting and nausea in TB patients may possibly related to an altered digestive inflammatory response in the presence of H. pylori.

When examining the impact of H. pylori on the TB clinical signs, we found that night fever was less frequent in the presence of H. pylori (p = 0.032 and p = 0.03) (Table 6). Such observation suggests that the presence of H. pylori reduces the frequency of night fever in TB patients, thereby influencing the manifestation of classic signs of tuberculosis. The literature also describes the modification of the symptomatic profile in the presence of co-infections, where the presentation may be more atypical or masked, complicating the clinical diagnosis [63].

Limitations

Despite the clinical relevance of our findings, several limitations must be acknowledged. Firstly, the cross-sectional design of the study limits our ability to establish a causal relationship between H. pylori infection and the clinical modification of TB infection. Secondly, the relatively small sample size in certain subgroups, such as non-alcohol consumers or patients with high income would have limited the estimation of the strength of the association between TB/H. pylori co-infection and these variables. Thirdly, the inclusion of a control group with no suspected TB symptoms would have enabled us to understand the effect of TB on the variables under investigation.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates a significant positive association between TB infection and H. pylori infection, with a notably high prevalence of H. pylori infection among patients with confirmed tuberculosis. Low-income status, close contact, alcohol consumption, and underweight status were found as associate factors to TB and H. pylori co-infection.

Our findings also revealed that night sweats and night fever are highly suggestive of TB infection and that the presence of H. pylori lead to an additional intensification of vomiting and a reduction of night fever as TB symptoms in our milieu. These observations highlight the importance of considering potential microbial associations when studying the dynamics of TB infection. Further longitudinal research is required to elucidate the underlying pathogenic mechanisms of the potential association between H. pylori infection and TB infection.
