Cyber physical solutions for aquatic monitoring using YOLO with BCP loss for intelligent underwater camouflaged object detection
Authors: Huynh Nguyen-Ngoc, Choonsung Shin, Sunghee Hong, Hieyong Jeong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEfficient fish detection in underwater environments is crucial for monitoring marine biodiversity, yet it poses significant challenges due to low visibility, complex backdrops, and diverse fish morphologies. This study presents a vision-based cyber-physical solution leveraging an enhanced YOLO architecture with a novel Balanced Coverage and Penalization (BCP) loss function. Our key contributions included a novel multi-head detection strategy. This unique approach not only improved the model’s adaptability to diverse fish morphologies but also led to the development of the BCP metric, which reduced prediction-ground truth discrepancies, thereby enhancing detection accuracy and robustness. Experiments on the COD10K and Halibut datasets show consistent gains in precision and stability compared with existing methods. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of the BCP loss in refining spatial coverage and highlight its potential for scalable applications in smart aquaculture, automated fisheries management, and marine environmental monitoring.Keywords: Cyber-physical vision systems, Deep learning for industrial applications, YOLOv8 optimization, Explainable AI, Smart marine monitoringSubject terms: Climate sciences, Ocean sciences

Introduction

The Sixth Assessment Report of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) highlights the urgent need for global action, as human-induced climate change has already caused substantial shifts in weather patterns and an increase in extreme climatic events worldwide1. In 2023, the global average temperature reached 14.98 °C, exceeding the previous record from 2016 by 0.17 °C2. The World Meteorological Organization (WMO) warns of an 80% likelihood that global temperatures will exceed 1.5 °C above pre-industrial levels in at least one year by 20283. This projection underscores the necessity for immediate, coordinated efforts across all sectors to achieve the Paris Agreement’s target of limiting global warming to 1.5 °C4.

Rising sea temperatures in the Northeast Asian region have accelerated the northward migration of the Tsushima Current, causing marked changes in the marine environment5,6. These thermal variations are reshaping marine ecosystems, altering species distributions and biomass dynamics, and leading to seasonal fluctuations in fish stocks7. Notably, some fish species are now more abundant in winter than in the past. The proliferation of gelatinous zooplankton—particularly jellyfish—and the expansion of subtropical species such as giant pelagic rays exemplify these changes. Such ecological transformations reflect the adaptive responses of marine life but also raise serious concerns about the stability of fisheries and the overall balance of coastal ecosystems8.

Practical ocean observation is critical for addressing climate change challenges9,10. Traditional ship-based observation methods are limited in their ability to provide continuous data. Consequently, unmanned ocean observation technologies, such as AUV (Autonomous Underwater Vehicle) and ocean gliders, are gaining prominence11,12. These autonomous platforms offer superior efficiency and data accuracy compared to conventional human-crewed vessels and stationary buoy systems.

These devices are integral to the marine weather observation network, facilitating improved monitoring of ocean heat capacity and enhancements in typhoon forecasting. They are equipped with various IoT sensors that collect extensive marine environmental data, which is then meticulously analyzed by marine scientists13. Additionally, video data capture through integrated cameras provides an intuitive means of information acquisition, making it accessible even to those without advanced technical expertise, a factor that has led to a preference for such methodologies among marine scientists. The work of these scientists in interpreting and analyzing the data is crucial for understanding the state of our marine ecosystems and the impacts of climate change.

However, it is essential to note that many marine organisms exhibit adaptive camouflage, enabling them to blend with their surroundings for predation or protection14,15. This challenges marine scientists when interpreting video data, as camouflaged species may go unrecognized. Therefore, there is a clear need for advanced technical support to enhance the accuracy of video-based analysis in marine observations.

This study introduces a novel deep-learning architecture called CAM-YOLO (Camouflaged YOLO), explicitly optimized for detecting camouflaged fish in underwater environments. CAM-YOLO addresses the limitations of existing methodologies by incorporating two key advancements, offering valuable contributions to both marine biology and computer vision.

First, we meticulously designed an enhanced YOLOv8 version tailored for aquatic scenarios through a distinctive four-head detection framework. This design captures multi-scale features, vital for identifying subtle variations in camouflaged fish against complex and variable underwater backgrounds. Additionally, the model’s neck structure has been fine-tuned to improve the integration of multi-scale features, thereby preserving essential information across diverse aquatic conditions.

The second critical issue lies in the limitation of the Complete Intersection over Union (CIoU) loss function, which is still used in modern detectors such as YOLOv8. Although CIoU incorporates overlap area, center distance, and aspect ratio, it struggles to distinguish between bounding boxes when objects vary greatly in scale or are partially occluded, as commonly seen in underwater environments. In particular, different bounding boxes can receive identical loss values despite having significantly different areas as depicted in Fig. 1, leading to poor boundary localization and missed detections for small, camouflaged targets.

To overcome this limitation, we propose an innovative loss function called the Balanced Coverage and Penalization (BCP) metric, which replaces traditional regression losses in YOLO models. The proposed formulation is specifically designed to enhance boundary localization precision and reduce errors in bounding box placement, particularly in low-contrast and cluttered underwater scenes where fish are often concealed or partially occluded. By directly addressing the shortcomings of existing loss formulations, this metric significantly improves detection robustness and IoU performance, ensuring more reliable results even under the most challenging conditions.

Related works

The detection of marine organisms in underwater ecosystems has gained importance due to advancements in deep learning16, the need for sustainable aquaculture, and biodiversity monitoring17. The role as marine biologists, ecologists, and researchers in AI is crucial in this field. Research is crucial in overcoming challenges such as limited visibility and species camouflage. Current studies mainly focus on improving datasets for training AI models18 and optimizing object detection architectures19,20. However, deep learning frameworks designed explicitly for detecting camouflaged marine life are still needed, which is vital for enhancing marine biodiversity assessments and sustainable aquaculture practices.

Detecting camouflaged objects in complex backgrounds is not just a research challenge but a significant one that underscores the importance of our work. The difficulty in distinguishing items that blend into their environments is a universal issue, arising in terrestrial, marine, and underwater settings, and making traditional object detection models less effective.

Deng-Ping Fan et al. introduced COD10K, a comprehensive dataset that establishes benchmarks for evaluating camouflaged object detection methods. They called for deeper investigations into this domain and suggested future research directions such as exploring RGB-D camouflage detection, utilizing weakly supervised and zero-shot learning, and applying variational autoencoders for better feature representation. They also proposed implementing multi-scale backbones, which are networks that can process images at different scales, to enhance detection across varying complexities.

Building upon, recent research has increasingly explored the use of transformer architectures and attention mechanisms to further improve camouflaged object detection performance. Recently, several advanced approaches have been proposed for camouflaged object detection (COD), leveraging transformer architectures and attention mechanisms to better model complex object-background relationships. For instance, UGTR21 introduces a unified global transformer reasoning framework that captures long-range dependencies for more accurate COD. Similarly, SINet22 improves detection by employing selective integrative attention modules to highlight subtle differences between objects and backgrounds. ZoomNet23 adopts a zoom-aware strategy combined with hierarchical attention to progressively refine features for camouflaged object localization. These methods demonstrate the effectiveness of attention and transformer-based designs in handling the high ambiguity and subtle appearance of camouflaged objects, inspiring the design of more powerful COD models.

A critical barrier in camouflaged object detection is the urgent need for domain-specific advancements. Current methods often need to be revised across settings like underwater environments and wildlife habitats due to insufficient consideration of contextual variables. Notable datasets, including COD10K and FishNet24, lack targeted annotations for specific applications. Addressing these gaps through specialized datasets and diverse input modalities could significantly enhance the effectiveness and resilience of camouflaged object detection systems.

The YOLO (You Only Look Once) framework, a leading algorithm in object detection, is known for its balance of speed and accuracy. Its evolution has improved performance, yet it faces unique challenges in underwater fish detection due to conditions like low visibility, camouflage, and varying lighting. We understand these challenges and are working towards solutions. In underwater environments, differentiating fish from their chaotic surroundings proves difficult, leading to higher detection failures and inflated bounding boxes. YOLO models depend on high-contrast features, which struggle against fish camouflage, resulting in false positives and missed detections. Moreover, the grid-based architecture and reliance on predefined anchor boxes hinder accurate localization, especially with occluded or distant fish. Adaptations like multi-scale detection techniques show promise in improving performance in these challenging scenarios.

In summary, while YOLO models perform well across various applications, ongoing research and adaptations are essential to address localization precision and bounding box calibration in underwater environments. The commitment to enhancing these models is crucial for advancing underwater fish detection efforts and is greatly valued in the field.

While YOLO’s standard loss function, which combines bounding box loss (), classification loss (), and objectness loss (), has improved object detection, it faces limitations, particularly in underwater fish detection. The component often results in oversized boxes that need to accurately represent fish size, especially in murky waters where fish may be occluded or camouflaged. This issue arises from YOLO’s use of grid-based predictions and fixed anchor boxes, which need help to adapt to the diverse characteristics of fish. Additionally, the and components find it challenging to correctly identify fish amidst cluttered backgrounds, leading to increased false positives and missed detections. The rigidity of anchor boxes and inadequate occlusion handling further hinder localization accuracy.

Recent innovations, such as IoU-based loss, aim to enhance bounding box localization but still encounter challenges with occlusion and scale variability typical in underwater environments. IoU-based loss prioritizes overlap over precise alignment with fish contours, limiting its effectiveness.

To overcome these issues, current research is focused on adaptive loss functions that adjust the importance of various loss components based on the detection context, along with spatially aware loss functions that use contextual information to improve the detection of small, camouflaged, or occluded fish. These advancements hold promise for enhancing fish detection in practical applications like aquaculture.

Additional networks in baseline model

The YOLOv8 model is a state-of-the-art object detection system that has shown promising results in various applications, including fish detection in underwater environments. To further enhance its capabilities in this specific domain, we have made substantial modifications to its network architecture, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Key adjustments include the adoption of a four-head detection mechanism and the substitution of the C2f (Cross Stage Partial) module in the neck with the CSPStage (Cross Stage Partial Stage)26. These alterations aim to enable deeper feature extraction and bolster the model’s proficiency in tackling challenges such as high contrast, camouflaged fish, and complex background scenarios. Figure 3 illustrated the CSPStage architecture which addresses this challenge by creating a bifurcated feature map architecture. One pathway preserves fine-grained, detailed features by bypassing transformations, while the alternate path applies convolutions to distil more abstract, high-level representations. This dual- path approach, with its emphasis on capturing local textures and gathering broader contextual information, enables CSPStage to effectively differentiate objects within visually complex environments. Consequently, CSPStage achieves a robust multi-scale feature representation, significantly enhancing the capacity to differentiate objects within visually complex environments.

The enhanced neck component plays a crucial role in capturing and storing the extracted information from the inputs effectively. This improvement is particularly important in the context of making predictions in a noisy environment. We strategically chose a more advanced model architecture that is better suited for information retention and differentiating between foreground and background elements. In the final processing stage, we opted for ECAA over CSPStage, as it maintains a balanced depth and facilitates the direct transmission of information from the backbone.

Given the intricacies of the problem, particularly the presence of complex backgrounds, the model’s ability to leverage the initially extracted features throughout the prediction process is of utmost importance. The primary function of ECAA27 is to use a kernel size of 1 to extract features, as illustrated in Fig. 4, where the module considers each channel and its k neighbors to capture local cross-channel interactions following channel-wise global average pooling (GAP) without dimensionality reduction. Effectiveness and efficiency are guaranteed by such a system. Remember that ECAA can be effectively implemented using a fast 1D convolution of size k. The coverage of local cross-channel interaction, or the number of neighbors that participate in one channel’s attention prediction, is represented by kernel size k. This approach is a local cross-channel interaction strategy that can be effectively performed using 1D convolution without dimensionality reduction. However, this method is crucial for telling the model to prioritize certain channels, which improves feature representation and overall model performance. In scenarios involving intricate backgrounds with camouflaged or low-contrast objects, the ability to discern subtle variations becomes vital.

Experimental conditions

We assess the effectiveness of the proposed method across two datasets: the large-scale COD10K dataset22, and an underwater halibut dataset featuring a complex background28. The COD10K dataset includes 5 super classes (terrestrial, atmobios, aquatic, amphibian, others). In object detection, mean Average Precision (mAP) and BCP are essential metrics for evaluating system performance. Average Precision (AP) provides insights into a model’s ability to identify true positives by assessing precision at various recall levels. Precision indicates the reliability of optimistic predictions, while recall measures the completeness of detecting ground truth positives. The IoU is crucial for evaluating localization accuracy and measuring the overlap between predicted and ground truth bounding boxes. AP is derived by averaging precision values across different IoU thresholds, and mAP consolidates these across classes, making IoU a key factor, especially in the COCO dataset. Moreover, the BCP, as defined in Eq. (1), is a strategic metric designed to optimize detection by ensuring coverage of ground truth instances while penalizing redundant predictions. It is vital in enhancing detection effectiveness and should not be overlooked in any robust object detection framework.

Performance on COD10K

The COD10K dataset has explicitly been established to assess the detection performance of algorithms on camouflaged objects, which present unique challenges due to their subtle boundaries that often blend seamlessly with complex backgrounds. Our proposed model, utilizing BCP loss, shows a remarkable improvement, achieving an excess score of 0.194 significantly better than the previous score of 0.345 obtained with CIoU. This reduction in excess underscores the model’s enhanced and reassuring ability to accurately confine bounding boxes around camouflaged objects, thereby minimizing encroachment into adjacent areas that typically result in false positives.

Despite the emphasis on minimizing excess, the model achieves a robust mean Average Precision (mAP) score of 0.831, indicating that enhanced precision in boundary delineations does not detract from detection capabilities. This consistent mAP score is critical for the COD10K benchmark, which requires a delicate balance of precision and reliability when identifying camouflaged objects. By effectively reducing excess while maintaining mAP, our model is well-suited for the challenging scenarios presented in COD10K. It excels in differentiating camouflaged objects from intricate backgrounds, providing reassurance about its performance.

Conclusion and discussion

This study underscores the effectiveness of the proposed loss function in enhancing the performance of a modified YOLO model for underwater object detection. The experimental results from two demanding datasets reveal that the Balanced Coverage and Penalization scheme consistently outperforms CIoU loss, achieving superior accuracy and more precise bounding box delineation while significantly reducing false-positive rates. These quantitative metrics and qualitative evaluations provide compelling evidence of the new formulation’s ability to navigate the intricacies of underwater environments, where object visibility is often compromised due to factors like camouflage and intricate background clutter, thereby underscoring the unique challenges that this approach effectively addresses.

The improved boundary precision and reduced bounding box surplus made possible by the proposed loss function are particularly crucial for underwater applications. Fluctuating lighting conditions, water turbidity, and variable object textures often pose challenges in these environments, adversely affecting detection accuracy. Therefore, this is also a future challenge with various scenarios (varying water turbidity, lighting conditions, camouflage types). The Balanced Coverage and Penalization scheme’s effective handling of these obstacles strengthens the model’s robustness, making it an invaluable asset for practical applications in aquaculture, marine research, and underwater monitoring, thereby demonstrating the real-world impact of the study.

Our research identifies several key avenues for the future optimization of BCP. A primary focus should be refining its architecture to support real-time applications, ensuring the model remains lightweight and efficient enough to deploy edge devices such as autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs) and remote sensors. We highlight key challenges, including hardware limitations, power constraints, communication issues, and the need for robust performance under dynamic and low-visibility conditions. To address these, we outline potential solutions such as lightweight model compression, hardware-aware optimization, and real-time image enhancement techniques suitable for embedded systems. We will also note complementary strategies such as pruning and compression for deployment, and outline a roadmap for edge deployment that begins with architectural streamlining (e.g., attention reduction and lightweight modules), followed by hardware-aware optimization, and finally domain-specific fine-tuning. These efforts aim to advance the proposed loss function toward practical real-time and edge deployment in underwater environments. However, comparing individual scenarios remains challenging due to limited available data. Detailed scenario information, such as varying turbidity and lighting conditions, is not explicitly provided in the published datasets. Addressing this limitation is part of our ongoing work, focusing on analyzing and predicting image quality under diverse environmental conditions to further strengthen BCP’s robustness in complex underwater scenes.

In summary, the modifications made to the YOLO model by integrating the BCP loss function signify substantial advancements in underwater object detection. These enhancements improve detection accuracy and precision and facilitate the development of more resilient AI solutions tailored to tackle the complex challenges underwater environments pose. This progress is poised to contribute significantly to sustainable aquaculture and the management of marine ecosystems.
