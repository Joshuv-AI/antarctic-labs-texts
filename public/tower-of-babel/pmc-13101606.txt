Multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) in people living with HIV: Clinical issues, therapeutic challenges and perspectives in sub-Saharan Africa. A narrative review
Authors: Christian Tague
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•MDR-TB/HIV coinfection results in mortality often exceeding 40% in sub-Saharan Africa.•Atypical clinical forms and low sensitivity of tests complicate diagnosis in HIV-positive people.•Interactions between second-line anti-tuberculosis drugs and ARVs constitute a major therapeutic obstacle.•Shorter, oral regimens (BPaL) show promising efficacy but are poorly available.•Strengthening diagnostic capacity and equitable access to innovative treatments are essential to contain this dual threat.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a leading cause of infectious mortality worldwide, with a particularly high incidence in sub-Saharan Africa. In this region, the disease burden is exacerbated by the high prevalence of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, which weakens immune defenses and promotes the rapid progression of TB infection to active disease [1], [2]. HIV-TB coinfection is thus a major public health problem, responsible for significantly increased morbidity and mortality rates [3]. At the same time, the emergence and spread of multidrug-resistant forms of tuberculosis (MDR-TB), defined by resistance to at least isoniazid and rifampicin the two first-line anti-tuberculosis drugs, further complicate management [3], [4]. This form of TB represents a growing threat, particularly among people living with HIV (PLHIV), due to their immune vulnerability, complex drug interactions, and limited access to effective treatments in resource-limited settings [5]. The synergistic impact of HIV on the diagnosis, clinical course, and prognosis of MDR-TB is of particular concern. In PLHIV, atypical clinical forms, diagnostic difficulties related to the often low bacillary load, and interactions between antiretrovirals and antituberculosis drugs make therapeutic management more complex, with an increased risk of treatment failure and death [6] .

Sub-Saharan Africa bears a disproportionate burden of both HIV infection and tuberculosis, accounting for more than two-thirds of global HIV-associated TB cases. Despite this, most evidence guiding MDR-TB management is derived from studies conducted in high-income settings, which differ substantially in health system capacity, diagnostic access, and patient profiles. This creates a critical evidence gap for the region. Methodology

This narrative review was conducted to synthesize current knowledge on multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) in people living with HIV (PLHIV) in sub-Saharan Africa. It aims to explore clinical issues, therapeutic challenges, and prospects for improving care in this region.

Type of study: This is a narrative review of the literature, oriented towards a critical and integrative analysis of the available data, without applying a systematic PRISMA-type protocol.

Information sources and search strategy: The literature search was conducted between January 2010 and March 2024 using PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar, and institutional sources. The search strategy was based on the combined use of keywords in French and English, including: “multidrug-resistant tuberculosis”, “MDR-TB”, “HIV”, “sub-Saharan Africa”, “co-infection”, “treatment”, “diagnosis”, “Xpert MTB/XDR”, “therapeutic challenges”, “adherence” and “mortality”.

The following were included in this review: Original articles, reviews, institutional reports or epidemiological data on MDR-TB, peer-reviewed publications or publications from reference organizations.

A total of 312 records were identified, of which 74 duplicates were removed. After title and abstract screening, 168 articles were excluded. Full-text review was performed for 70 articles, and 43 met the inclusion criteria.

Source quality was assessed based on study design, sample size, clarity of outcome definitions, and relevance to the African context. Priority was given to WHO reports, cohort studies, and systematic reviews.

Epidemiology

Coinfection with HIV and tuberculosis (TB) is a public health emergency, particularly in the African region where the prevalence of both diseases is high. PLHIV are 20 to 30 times more likely to develop active TB than HIV-negative individuals [7], [8]. This increased vulnerability is explained by the weakened immune system, which promotes the progression of latent TB infection to active disease. Thus, TB is the leading cause of death among PLHIV, accounting for 40% of HIV-related deaths in 2016 and an estimated 161,000 deaths worldwide in 2023 [8], [9], [10]. Multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB), defined as TB resistant to at least rifampicin and often isoniazid, further exacerbates this situation. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that 451,551 new cases of rifampicin resistance were detected in the African region in 2016, making the region one of the epicenters of MDR-TB. This form of the disease represents a major threat to global health security, particularly among PLHIV, for whom treatment options are more limited and outcomes often worse [7], [8], [10].

In Africa, tuberculosis is responsible for approximately 35% of deaths among PLHIV. In 2015, there were 1.2 million new cases of tuberculosis among HIV-positive people, 71% of whom lived on the continent [2], [9]. The strong interaction between HIV and tuberculosis makes their association a lethal combination, with each disease accelerating the progression of the other. In 2023, only 56% of HIV-positive tuberculosis patients were receiving antiretroviral therapy, despite improved availability of HIV testing for tuberculosis patients (80% of notified cases with known HIV status in 2023, compared to 76% in 2021) [9], [11]. Prevention of MDR-TB among PLHIV remains inadequate. In 2016, only 42% of people newly enrolled in antiretroviral care in the African region were receiving TB preventive therapy [12], [13]. This rate remains well below what is needed to interrupt the chain of MDR-TB transmission. Furthermore, the annual decline in global TB incidence is estimated at 2%, a rate considered insufficient to achieve the targets of the Global Tuberculosis Strategy, which aims for an annual reduction of 4–5% [7], [8].

Clinical manifestations of MDR-TB in people living with HIV

In people living with HIV, the clinical presentation of tuberculosis, and even more so of multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB), is often atypical [14]. Profound immunosuppression disrupts the usual inflammatory response, which can mask the classic signs of the disease [14], [15]. Unlike immunocompetent patients, PLHIV frequently present with extrapulmonary or disseminated forms, associated with nonspecific symptoms such as prolonged fever, weight loss, lymphadenopathy, or respiratory distress without characteristic radiological signs [16].

HIV profoundly alters host immunity by targeting CD4+ T lymphocytes, which play a central role in containing Mycobacterium tuberculosis through macrophage activation and granuloma maintenance. HIV-mediated depletion of Th1 CD4+ cells reduces interferon-gamma production, impairing macrophage bactericidal activity and destabilizing granuloma architecture. In parallel, HIV disrupts antigen-presenting cell function and skews cytokine profiles toward an ineffective immune response. Conversely, active tuberculosis induces systemic immune activation, increasing levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and activated CD4+ T cells, which enhances HIV replication and accelerates disease progression. This bidirectional immunopathological interaction explains the increased susceptibility to MDR-TB, rapid disease progression, and poorer outcomes observed in coinfected patients.

Compared to high-income settings, MDR-TB in sub-Saharan Africa is more frequently diagnosed at advanced stages, with higher rates of disseminated and extrapulmonary disease, particularly among PLHIV. Late presentation, limited access to imaging, and reliance on symptom-based screening contribute to more severe clinical phenotypes. In contrast, patients in high-income regions are more often diagnosed through systematic screening and present with localized disease and lower mortality.

Diagnostic challenges and advances in HIV-associated MDR-TB

Diagnosis of multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) in people living with HIV is particularly complex. The low bacillary load in the sputum of these patients considerably reduces the sensitivity of direct examination microscopy, long used in resource-limited settings [17]. In addition, the coexistence of other opportunistic infections, such as pneumocystosis, cryptococcosis, or histoplasmosis, complicates the interpretation of clinical and laboratory findings, potentially leading to erroneous or delayed diagnoses [18].

Discrepancies between different diagnostic tests are common. For example, a patient may have a negative culture despite active pulmonary disease, or a positive GeneXpert test for tuberculosis without clear detection of resistance in the absence of a targeted mutation [17], [18], [19]. This poses a major challenge for therapeutic decision-making, particularly in settings with limited access to second-line diagnostic tools.

In response to these challenges, major diagnostic advances have emerged over the past decade, including the GeneXpert MTB/RIF assay, which can simultaneously detect Mycobacterium tuberculosis and rifampicin resistance within two hours [19], [20]. This tool has represented a paradigm shift in high HIV prevalence settings. Additional molecular tests, such as Line Probe Assays, allow rapid detection of resistance to fluoroquinolones and second-line injectable agents [21], [22]. The introduction of GeneXpert Ultra, with improved sensitivity, is particularly beneficial for PLHIV with very low bacillary loads. However, access to these technologies remains uneven across sub-Saharan Africa, limiting their overall impact on morbidity and mortality related to MDR-TB/HIV co-infection [17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22], [23].

Therapeutic challenges

Treatment of multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) in people living with HIV poses significant clinical and logistical challenges, compounded by the complexity of treatment regimens, drug toxicity, and pharmacological interactions with antiretrovirals (ARVs) [24], [25]. Treatment regimens for MDR-TB are long, expensive, and often poorly tolerated. While treatment for susceptible TB generally lasts six months, treatment for MDR-TB can last from 9 to 20 months, depending on the resistance profile and national protocols [5]. These treatments are based on a combination of second-line anti-tuberculosis drugs, which have higher toxicity than first-line molecules, with an increased risk of severe adverse effects: hepatotoxicity, neuropathies, myelosuppression, psychiatric or cardiac disorders (prolongation of the QT interval, particularly with bedaquiline) [8], [9], [25].

In HIV-coinfected patients, drug interactions represent a major obstacle to effective treatment. Several anti-tuberculosis drugs can interact with ARVs by altering their hepatic metabolism via cytochromes P450, which can reduce treatment efficacy or increase the risk of toxicity [10], [26], [27]. For example, rifampicin is a potent enzyme inducer, limiting the use of certain protease inhibitors, while linezolid and bedaquiline can exacerbate the toxicity of ARV treatments or increase their side effects [28], [29], [30].

These constraints make therapeutic adherence difficult, particularly in contexts of precariousness, malnutrition or psychiatric comorbidities. Compliance is all the more critical since treatment abandonment or poor adherence promotes the emergence of even more resistant forms (extensively drug-resistant TB or XDR-TB).

The World Health Organization (WHO) now recommends shorter, less toxic, all-oral regimens incorporating molecules such as bedaquiline, linezolid, clofazimine, or pretomanid [7], [8], [31]. These regimens, such as BPaL (bedaquiline, pretomanid, linezolid), have shown promising efficacy, even in coinfected patients, with success rates exceeding 80% in some clinical trials [32]. However, their accessibility remains limited in sub-Saharan Africa due to cost, lack of national availability, regulatory delays, and distribution still concentrated in a few specialized centers [33]. In the DRC, for example, although the National Tuberculosis Control Programme has begun introducing bedaquiline in some treatment centres, coverage remains very partial, and access to linezolid or pretomanid remains restricted to pilot projects supported by international partners [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15].

Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) could play a valuable role in optimizing MDR-TB treatment in PLHIV by adjusting drug dosages in the context of complex pharmacokinetic interactions. However, routine TDM remains largely unavailable in most sub-Saharan African settings due to cost, laboratory infrastructure limitations, and lack of trained personnel, restricting its use to research or highly specialized centers.

Prognosis and clinical outcome

Coinfection with multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) and HIV is a major factor in poor prognosis in sub-Saharan Africa. People living with HIV and MDR-TB have significantly higher morbidity and mortality rates than monoinfected patients [11], [34].

Treatment outcomes of MDR-TB in PLHIV in sub-Saharan Africa remain suboptimal. Treatment success rates range from 45% to 65%, while loss to follow-up may exceed 20% in some settings. Treatment failure and relapse are more frequent than in HIV-negative populations, reflecting delayed diagnosis, drug toxicity, and adherence challenges. Southern African countries report slightly higher success rates compared to Central and West Africa, likely reflecting stronger programmatic support and access to newer regimens.[33], [34].

In several studies conducted in the region, mortality among coinfected patients exceeds 40%, even 50% in settings of late diagnosis and inadequate treatment, compared to approximately 20–30% in MDR-TB patients without HIV [30], [35]. These high rates are explained in particular by the frequent delays in diagnosis and initiation of treatment. The atypical clinical presentation in immunocompromised patients, combined with low sensitivity of tests in settings of reduced bacillary load, delays diagnostic suspicion. In addition, the delays in obtaining a diagnosis of resistance, especially when GeneXpert is not available or only detects rifampicin may prolong the wait before starting appropriate therapy. These delays allow the infection to progress, sometimes to disseminated forms, which are difficult to control [6], [9].

The patient's immune status plays a key role in prognosis. A low CD4 count, particularly below 100 cells/mm3, is strongly associated with an increased risk of death, due to an inability to contain mycobacterial proliferation [36], [37]. Similarly, a high HIV viral load increases vulnerability to opportunistic infections, severe forms of MDR-TB and reduces the overall effectiveness of the therapeutic response [37], [38]. Immune reconstitution under antiretroviral therapy (ART) can also pose challenges, particularly with the risk of immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS), which can complicate the clinical course [39]. However, when well coordinated, ART improves survival, particularly when initiated early after the start of anti-TB treatment. The combination of late diagnosis, degraded immune status, heavy treatments and limited access to specialized care makes MDR-TB in PLWH a major medical challenge. Improving the prognosis requires early, integrated management, supported by appropriate diagnostic and therapeutic resources [40].

Prevention and integrated care strategies

Given the seriousness of multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB)/HIV co-infection, an effective response requires an integrated, patient-centered approach, combining prevention, early diagnosis, coordinated treatment and comprehensive support. The so-called “one-stop shop” or “one-door” approach is a key strategic pillar [25], [41]. It aims to integrate systematic HIV screening in all people diagnosed with MDR-TB, and conversely, to systematically test for TB, including its resistant forms, in people living with HIV (PLHIV), especially in cases of suspected relapse or treatment failure. This strategy makes it possible to identify co-infections early, shorten treatment times and avoid breaks in the care pathway [42]. Early initiation of antiretroviral (ARV) therapy is crucial. The World Health Organization recommends initiating ART within two weeks of starting TB treatment in co-infected individuals with a CD4 count < 50 cells/mm3, and within eight weeks for others [9], [10]. This early initiation improves survival while limiting the risk of serious infectious complications. However, it is essential to adapt ARV regimens to limit pharmacological interactions, in coordination with the MDR-TB treatment protocol [8].

Currently, preventive treatment specifically targeting MDR-TB remains limited in sub-Saharan Africa. Unlike drug-susceptible TB, standardized preventive regimens for contacts of MDR-TB cases are not widely implemented, largely due to limited evidence, drug toxicity concerns, and operational challenges. Recent WHO guidance supports the use of fluoroquinolone-based preventive therapy in selected high-risk contacts, including PLHIV, but implementation in African settings remains sporadic and confined to pilot programs.

Therapeutic success also relies on non-drug support interventions. Nutritional monitoring is particularly important in precarious contexts, as malnutrition aggravates immunosuppression and reduces treatment tolerance. Structured psychosocial support, combined with community support (including trained health workers and peer groups), helps improve adherence, reduce stigma, and strengthen patients' resilience to long and challenging treatments [42,43].

Finally, rigorous pharmacological monitoring is essential. This includes monitoring drug interactions between ARVs and anti-tuberculosis drugs (including the risks of enzyme induction or cumulative toxicity), monitoring adverse effects (hepatotoxicity, neuropathies, QT prolongation), as well as regular biological monitoring (liver function, blood counts, electrocardiogram if necessary) [7], [8], [9], [10], [11].

Thus, an integrated and multidisciplinary strategy is the only viable way to reduce mortality, improve cure rates, and limit community transmission of MDR-TB in populations living with HIV in sub-Saharan Africa.

Conclusion

Multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) in people living with HIV (PLHIV) is a silent health emergency in sub-Saharan Africa. Although often overlooked due to late diagnosis and lack of appropriate resources, its consequences are serious and devastating. The challenges are multiple: complex diagnosis, lengthy and costly treatments, and delicate drug interactions. However, integrated, early, and well-coordinated approaches are key to improving the prognosis of co-infected patients. Implementing a systematic screening strategy for TB in PLWH and for HIV in TB patients, coupled with timely therapeutic management, could transform the care pathway and reduce morbidity and mortality rates. Taken together, these findings indicate that MDR-TB/HIV coinfection outcomes are driven not by a single factor but by the interaction of delayed diagnosis, immune dysfunction, drug toxicity, and health system constraints. Addressing any single component in isolation is unlikely to substantially reduce mortality. The experience of some middle-income countries, which have developed effective intervention models, could serve as a reference and inspire local strategies adapted to African contexts. Strengthening health systems, ensuring the availability of new-generation treatments, and integrating HIV and TB programs are essential levers for addressing this dual epidemic. Although the obstacles are numerous, it is still possible to change the trajectory of MDR-TB/HIV coinfection in sub-Saharan Africa through coordinated actions, resource mobilization, and a patient-centered approach.
