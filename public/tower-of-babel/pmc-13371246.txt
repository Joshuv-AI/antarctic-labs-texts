Association of TREM1 expression with mTOR and DRP1 in gallbladder cancer: a study of 105 cases
Authors: Ziyu Liu, Shumei Wei, Chongyu Wang, Xi Chen, Mengqiu Yin, Tianyu Chen, Jinhui Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGallbladder cancer is highly aggressive and characterized by poor prognosis and limited treatment options. TREM1 (Triggering Receptor Expressed on Myeloid cells 1) may be involved in cancer inflammation and progression, but its relevance to gallbladder cancer is poorly understood. Expression of TREM1 in gallbladder cancer and correlation with mTOR activation, DRP1 upregulation, and clinical outcomes were investigated in the present study.MethodsImmunohistochemical staining of 105 gallbladder cancer specimens from patients receiving cholecystectomy, radical resection or palliative resection between 2015 and 2020 was conducted to show expression of TREM1, mTOR and DRP1. Data was analyzed by Chi-square test, Kaplan-Meier survival curve, Cox regression model and correlation analyses.ResultsHigh TREM1 expression was observed in 63% gallbladder cancer cases and was linked to a more advanced T stage (p = 0.015), the presence of lymph node metastasis (p = 0.041) and an elevated TNM stage (p = 0.006). Positive correlations were shown between TREM1 and mTOR (p < 0.001) and between TREM1 and DRP1 (p < 0.001). Patients with high TREM1 expression had shorter overall survival than those with low expression (median 18 months vs. 50 months, p < 0.001). TREM1 was identified by multivariate analysis as an independent prognostic factor (HR = 1.838, 95% CI: 1.038–3.252, p = 0.037).ConclusionsImmunohistochemical analysis demonstrated that TREM1 is overexpressed in gallbladder cancer and positively correlated with mTOR and DRP1 expression. TREM1 may be a suitable prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target in gallbladder cancer.

Introduction

Gallbladder cancer (GBC) is the most prevalent malignancy of the biliary tract and is the most aggressive form of digestive cancer, characterized by an extremely poor prognosis [1]. The 5-year survival rate remains below 10%, primarily due to delayed diagnosis and the scarcity of effective treatment options. Inflammation characterizes GBC pathogenesis, with chronic cholelithiasis and infections being significant risk factors. TREM1 (Triggering Receptor Expressed on Myeloid cells 1) is a transmembrane receptor belonging to the immunoglobulin superfamily which acts to amplify inflammatory responses during infection and sterile inflammation. TREM1 is known to be overexpressed in hepatocellular carcinoma, bladder urothelial carcinoma and papillary thyroid cancer [2–4], in which it promotes tumor growth, angiogenesis and metastasis, although any involvement in GBC remains poorly understood. Mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) regulates cell growth, proliferation and metabolism and often shows dysregulation in cancer. Activation of mTOR promotes mitochondrial fission via dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1), enhancing metabolic adaptability [5, 6]. However, relationships among TREM1, mTOR and DRP1 in GBC remain to be elucidated. The current work examined TREM1 expression and its correlation with mTOR activation, DRP1 expression, clinicopathological parameters and survival outcomes in a cohort of 105 GBC patients. The aim was to investigate any association of TREM1 with GBC progression which we hypothesized to involve mTOR signaling and DRP1-mediated mitochondrial fission.

Methods

Study population

Archived paraffin-embedded samples from patients diagnosed with gallbladder adenocarcinoma at The Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine between January 2015 and June 2020 have been compiled. The study included subjects who had a minimum of 5-years follow-up history. The cohort represented well-characterized patients with long-term follow-up at a single institution. Diagnoses were confirmed by histological re-examination of paraffin-embedded slides by 2 senior pathologists. Ethical approval was granted by The Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine. Clinicopathological data, including age, gender, tumor size, differentiation status, TNM stage (as defined by the AJCC Cancer Staging Manual), lymph node metastasis, perineural invasion and vascular invasion, were collated from medical records.

Immunohistochemistry

4-µm thick sections were cut from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded blocks, deparaffinized and rehydrated. Antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) in a microwave oven, and endogenous peroxidase activity was inhibited with 3% hydrogen peroxide. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies raised against TREM1 (1:500, Abcam, ab225861), phospho-mTOR (1:200, Abcam, ab32028) and DRP1 (1:500, ab184247), washed and incubated with HRP-labeled secondary antibody for 30 min at room temperature. Target proteins were visualized with DAB substrate and sections counterstained with hematoxylin.

Evaluation of immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

TREM1, mTOR and DRP1 immunoreactivity were graded using an immunoreactivity score (IRS) [7]. Two pathologists, blind to the clinical data, separately evaluated the IHC staining. Staining intensity was scored as 0 (negative), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate) or 3 (strong) and percentage of positive tumor cells as 0 (0%), 1 (1–25%), 2 (26–50%), 3 (51–75%) or 4 (76–100%). The immunoreactivity score (range: 0–12) was determined by multiplying the intensity and percentage scores. Scores of 0–3 were classified as low expression, and those of 4–12 as high expression for statistical analysis. No standard cut-off value for TREM1, mTOR and DRP1 expression has been established. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted and an optimal cut-off value determined from the point maximizing Youden’s index for Gallbladder cancer.

Follow-up

Overall Survival (OS) was defined as the time from initial diagnosis to death from any cause. Patients who were lost to follow-up or still alive at the data cutoff were recorded at the date they were last known to be living. All patients underwent standardized telephone follow-up every 3 months for the first 2 years, every 6 months for years 3 to 5 and annually thereafter.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted utilizing SPSS version 27.0 (IBM). Categorical variables were compared by Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was analyzed by log-rank test for comparative assessment. Multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were constructed to control for confounding variables, including key treatment factors such as radical resection and chemotherapy, and to identify independent prognostic factors. A p-value < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

Association between TREM1 expression and clinicopathological parameters

The 105 GBC patients, 61 females and 44 males (gender ratio: 1.39:1), had a mean age of 66.4 years (range: 38 to 85 years). Staging according to AJCC 8th edition criteria gave the following distribution: stage I (n = 8), stage II (n = 19), stage III (n = 53) and stage IV (n = 25). Lymph node metastasis was detected in 36% patients. Tumors were found to be poorly differentiated in 28% cases, moderately differentiated in 52% and well-differentiated in 20%.

IHC analysis showed TREM1 expression to be predominantly localized to GBC cell cytoplasm and membranes (Fig. 1A). 66/105 (63%) samples had high TREM1 expression and 39/105 (37%) had low expression. Stromal inflammatory cells also stained positively for TREM1 and were considered an internal positive control. Elevated TREM1 expression correlated with a more advanced T stage (p = 0.015), the presence of lymph node metastasis (p = 0.041) and an elevated TNM stage (p = 0.006). No association of TREM1 expression was found with age, gender, tumor size, differentiation, lymphovascular invasion(LVI), perineural invasion (PNI), radical resection status or chemotherapy status (Table 1).

Correlation between TREM1, mTOR and DRP1 expression

Elevated mTOR expression was found in 57% patients and elevated DRP1 in 64% (Fig. 1B and C). TREM1 expression was positively correlated with expression of mTOR (p < 0.001) and of DRP1 (p < 0.001) and levels of mTOR were positively correlated with those of DRP1 (p < 0.001, Table 2).

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed high TREM1 expression to be associated with shorter overall survival (median survival: 18 months) compared with low expression (median survival: 50 months, p < 0.01; Fig. 2A). Elevated mTOR (median survival: 19 months vs. 45 months, p < 0.01) and DRP1 (20 months vs. 40 months, p < 0.01) were also associated with adverse survival outcomes (Fig. 2B C). Patients with high co-expression of all three markers, TREM1, mTOR and DRP1, showed the worst prognosis with a median survival of 16 months and a 5-year survival rate of 3%. These values contrast with those of patients showing low expression of all three markers who had the most favorable outcomes of median survival of 52 months and 5-year survival rate of 43% (p < 0.01, Fig. 2D).

Univariate and multivariate cox regression analysis

Univariate Cox analysis identified tumor size (≥ 2 cm), advanced T stage, lymph node metastasis, higher TNM stage, treatment type and high expression of TREM1, mTOR and DRP1 to be major predictors of poor survival (all p < 0.05). Multivariate Cox regression analysis of all significant variables from the univariate analysis showed high TREM1 expression (HR = 1.838, 95% CI: 1.038–3.252, p = 0.037), high mTOR expression(HR = 1.994, 95% CI: 1.195–3.328, p = 0.008), TNM stage (Stage I: HR = 0.167, 95% CI: 0.042–0.667, p = 0.011; Stage II: HR = 0.190, 95% CI: 0.064–0.564, p = 0.003; Stage III: HR = 0.328, 95% CI: 0.173–0.622, p < 0.001), radical resection status (HR = 0.359, 95% CI: 0.204–0.634, p < 0.001), and chemotherapy status(HR = 0.416, 95% CI: 0.259–0.666, p < 0.001) to be independent prognostic factors for overall survival in GBC patients (Table 3).

Discussion

The positive correlation between TREM1 expression and adverse clinicopathological features found during the present work is consistent with previous reports concerning various cancer types. The high rate of TREM1 overexpression in the current cohort (63%) may reflect the highly inflammatory nature of gallbladder cancer which frequently develops following chronic inflammation due to gallstones. TREM1 expression correlated with markers of tumor aggressiveness, such as a more advanced T stage, the presence of lymph node metastasis and an elevated higher TNM stage, and may reflect a link between TREM1 and GBC aggressiveness.

The mTOR pathway is known to regulate cancer cell growth and metabolism [8, 9], and the finding of high mTOR expression in 57% GBC cases is in agreement with previous reports of mTOR pathway activation in biliary tract cancers [10, 11].TREM1 expression was positively correlated with mTOR expression in GBC. These observations indicate a potential association between TREM1 and mTOR signaling which may involve the PI3K/AKT pathway, as previously described [12]. DRP1 is involved in mitochondrial fission, and overexpression may cause mitochondrial fragmentation, triggering increased cell proliferation, migration and chemotherapy resistance in various cancer types. Indeed, mitochondrial dynamics involving both fission and fusion have been demonstrated to impact cisplatin resistance in cholangiocarcinoma [13]. DRP1 was overexpressed in 64% of the current cohort of GBC patients and was correlated with poor survival outcome, suggesting a role for DRP1-mediated mitochondrial fission in GBC progression and treatment resistance. Positive correlations among TREM1, mTOR and DRP1 expression (p < 0.001) also suggest a potential mechanistic link. A link between TREM1, mTOR signaling and DRP1 expression in GBC is hypothesized based on the present findings and existing literature. This cascade results in mitochondrial fission and a pro-tumorigenic environment that facilitates cancer cell survival, proliferation and metastasis.

TREM1 was overexpressed in 63% gallbladder cancer samples, and overexpression was associated with aggressive clinicopathological features and poor survival outcome. The most significant clinical finding was the identification of elevated TREM1 expression as an independent prognostic indicator of poor survival in GBC patients (HR = 1.838, 95% CI: 1.038–3.252, p = 0.037), after controlling for confounding variables of TNM stage, radical resection status and chemotherapy status. The therapeutic implications of the current findings may be viewed in the context of current drug development. Several mTOR inhibitors are available for the treatment of other cancers and DRP1 inhibitors, such as mdivi-1, have demonstrated promising anticancer effects in preclinical research. The targeting of TREM1 may offer an alternative therapeutic approach if it does, indeed, act upstream of mTOR and DRP1 in GBC. No TREM1 inhibitor is currently available for clinical use, but monoclonal antibodies and peptide antagonists are under investigation in preclinical models.

Several limitations of the current study should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective observational study based on immunohistochemical staining and gives no information regarding mechanisms. Second, the study was conducted at a single center in China which may limit generalizability. Third, multi-omics validation was not performed to confirm our findings at the molecular level. Finally, the retrospective design may carry a potential risk of sampling bias. Despite these limitations, preliminary evidence for the prognostic value of TREM1, mTOR and Drp1 expression in the current patient cohort is presented. Future research may validate the present findings within larger cohorts and investigate molecular mechanisms in vitro and in vivo.

Conclusions

These findings demonstrate a positive correlation among TREM1 expression, mTOR activation and DRP1 upregulation in human GBC tissues, suggesting that TREM1 may be implicated in GBC progression and indicating potential mechanisms for further investigation. TREM1 may be a valuable prognostic biomarker for risk stratification in GBC patients to identify those who may benefit from more aggressive treatment strategies or targeted therapies.
