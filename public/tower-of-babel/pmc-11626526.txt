Endoscopic management of a bleeding Zenker's diverticulum: A case report
Authors: Renishka Sellayah, Nishanthi Gurusinghe
Published: 2024

Introduction

The first description of a pharyngeal pouch was made by Ludlow in 1769 [1]. However, it was not until 1877 that the German pathologists von Zenker and von Ziemssen described this phenomenon in a case series, giving it an eponymous name [2]. Bleeding is a rare complication of a Zenker's diverticulum, and patients typically present with haematemesis or haemoptysis and can often be a diagnostic dilemma. It can be fatal, due to massive bleeding however no guidelines currently exist for the management of a bleeding Zenker's diverticulum. Endoscopic haemostasis is an ideal intervention in elderly patients unfit for surgery and can also be used as a bridge to a definitive procedure. There are fewer than fifteen reported cases of bleeding from a Zenker's diverticulum in the current literature [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. Most have undergone surgical management, however a three have been successfully controlled endoscopically [[8], [9], [10]]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the fourth reported case of bleeding from a Zenker's diverticulum managed endoscopically in a regional Australian hospital, and the first case to achieve control of bleeding with a novel topical haemostatic agent. This case has been reported in line with the SCARE 2023 guideline [11].

Presentation of case

A 63-year-old man presented via ambulance to the emergency department of a regional hospital in Tasmania, Australia with epistaxis, haemoptysis and haematemesis while on dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin 100 mg and clopidogrel 75 mg daily). This occurred eighteen days after emergency coronary artery bypass grafts.

His past medical history included hyperthyroidism, and a pacemaker inserted in 2012 for cardiogenic syncope. He had been diagnosed with a pharyngeal pouch several years prior on imaging for an unrelated presentation but had no previous bleeding episodes or endoscopies. He was a current smoker (40 pack year history) and a retired electrician.

He described a sensation of blood pooling in the oral cavity for 12 h with epistaxis and then 2 episodes of haematemesis and haemoptysis of approximately 250mls. He had no upper respiratory tract infection symptoms, or recent oral trauma. He described daily reflux and regurgitation with solid foods and had self-restricted his diet to liquids and pureed foods to avoid these symptoms. He did not have any significant weight loss. He had not sought medical treatment for these symptoms previously. Given his presentation with epistaxis, haemoptysis, and haematemesis, several specialty units were initially consulted by the emergency department, including respiratory, otolaryngology and gastroenterology.

He was haemodynamically stable on arrival with no evidence of airway compromise. Oral examination did not reveal any ulcers, tonsils were intact and there was no blood in the nasal cavity. Endoscopic examination of the nasopharynx revealed blood but no clear source of bleeding above the vocal cords and the laryngopharynx was unremarkable. Abdominal examination was unremarkable. Initial Hb level was 121 g/L (from 166 g/L on discharge eighteen days prior), platelet level and coagulation function was normal, as was his renal and liver function. After resuscitation (blood and platelet transfusions and tranexamic acid) he underwent a CT angiogram which demonstrated an active contrast blush and pooling of contrast in the pharyngeal diverticulum (Fig. 1). At this stage the patient was referred to the general surgical unit who provided definitive management.

Axial contrast CT of the neck demonstrating an active contrast blush in the pharyngeal diverticulum.

After multidisciplinary discussion the patient proceeded to theatre for endoscopic assessment and management with the general surgery unit later the same day. The patient was optimised for theatre with further blood product resuscitation, and clopidogrel was withheld after discussion with cardiothoracic unit. Endoscopy was performed with general anaesthesia. A large Zenker's diverticulum was encountered in the hypopharynx which contained an ulcerated area with general oozing of blood from multiple points and a single brisk bleeding point (Fig. 2). Haemostasis was achieved with two syringes of a topical haemostatic agent, PuraStat® (3D Matrix Europe SAS, Caluire-et-Cuire, France). There was no other source of upper gastrointestinal bleeding.

Endoscopic image demonstrating diffuse areas of ooze within the Zenker's diverticulum and a single brisk bleeding point.

He was successfully extubated the first day post procedure and discharged on the fourth day of admission on a soft diet, with instructions to crush aspirin prior to ingestion. He had outpatient follow-up to undertake planning for definitive surgery, including repeat endoscopy and further imaging.

Discussion

Zenker's diverticulum is a rare disease, with a reported incidence of 2 per 100,000 individuals and a prevalence between 0.01 and 0.11% [12]. However, this is likely an underestimate as many patients may remain asymptomatic. It is defined as an outpouching of mucosa and submucosa (a false pulsion-type diverticulum) through Killians triangle, an area of weakness between the transverse fibres of the cricopharyngeus muscle and the oblique fibres of the lower inferior constrictor, the thyropharyngeus muscle [12]. Zenker's diverticulum is more frequently seen in an elderly population [13], who are more likely to have co-morbidities and be on therapeutic anticoagulation. Furthermore, there may be delays to prompt diagnosis as the clinical picture is unclear if patients present with haemoptysis. Elderly patients who present with this symptom should be treated with a high index of suspicion.

In the present case, the diagnostic dilemma resulting from presentation with epistaxis, haemoptysis and haematemesis necessitated review by multiple specialty units prior to general surgery. The differential diagnosis for this set of presenting symptoms is broad, and extensive investigation is required, which may not be feasible in an emergency setting. Although the diagnosis of a Zenker's diverticulum was known in this case, a high enough index of suspicion was not immediately apparent. While oral examination and endoscopic examination of the nasopharynx is an appropriate step in investigation, the diagnosis was only made on CT scan several hours later.

While the exact pathophysiology remains elusive, a Zenker's diverticulum is believed to occur due to dysfunction of the upper oesophageal sphincter (causing increased intraluminal pressure during swallowing) and incomplete relaxation of the cricopharyngeus muscle, resulting in herniation of the mucosa and submucosa posterior through an area of weakness [14]. It has been theorised that age-related changes such as impaired pharyngeal swallowing and atrophy of cells in Auerbach's plexus may explain the higher incidence in the elderly population [15]. Bleeding may be caused by antiplatelet or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory agents, commonly seen in the elderly population. It has been suggested that antiplatelet agents may exert a topical effect after lodging in the diverticulum due to stasis of food, causing ulceration and leading to diverticulitis and bleeding [3]. One case report of a bleeding Zenker's diverticulum described advising the patient to crush and swallow aspirin with water, to reduce surface contact time [8].

In most reported cases of bleeding Zenker's diverticulum, emergency operative management was successful. Several surgical options exist for the management of Zenker's diverticulum – diverticulectomy, diverticulopexy, diverticular inversion with or without myotomy and myotomy alone [16]. Several endoscopic techniques have also been described, including diverticulectomy and the Z-POEM technique [14].

In the case of a bleeding Zenker's diverticulum, endoscopic therapy is a viable option if a source can be identified, with emergency surgery reserved for those who fail endoscopic therapy. The first case of successful endoscopic haemostasis of a bleeding ulcer within a Zenker's diverticulum was reported in 2010 [8]. The patient was elderly, comorbid and on antiplatelet agents. In this case, haemostasis was achieved with a clip, and no further interventions were undertaken. In 2011 a similar case was reported where haemostasis was achieved endoscopically with a clip in an elderly patient unfit for surgery [9]. A third case in 2023 described endoscopic band ligation to achieve haemostasis, with a scar demonstrated on endoscopy a month later [10].

In our patient, a topical haemostatic agent was chosen, as the source of bleeding was a large area of ulceration with diffuse ooze. This agent (PuraStat®) uses a self-assembling peptide technology which results in a molecular mesh similar to extracellular matrix when in contact with blood, thereby forming a physical barrier to cause haemostasis [17]. Unlike haemostatic powders which may clog application catheters and decrease visibility, this agent is a viscous transparent gel, which allows for better direct visualisation.

Conclusion

This is the fourth reported case of successful endoscopic management of bleeding from a Zenker's diverticulum, a rare but potentially life-threatening entity. This case highlights the difficulty in the diagnosis of a bleeding Zenker's diverticulum, as evidenced by the presenting symptoms and the multiple specialty units involved. The key learning point from our institution's experience is the importance of early diagnosis and a high index of suspicion for Zenker's diverticulum as a source of bleeding, to prevent delays to management. Our case report also illustrates the use of a novel endoscopic haemostatic agent and highlights the importance of endoscopic intervention in the management algorithm of a bleeding Zenker's diverticulum, either as a definitive procedure in patients unfit for surgery, or as an initial emergency procedure enabling definitive surgical management.
