Quaternionic Green’s Function and the Brown Measure of Atomic Operators
Max Sun Zhou

Abstract
. We analyze the Brown measure the non-normal operators X = p + i ​ q X=p+iq , where p p and q q are Hermitian, freely independent, and have spectra consisting of finitely many atoms. We use the Quaternionic Green’s function, an analogue of the operator-valued R R -transform in the physics literature, to understand the support and the boundary of the Brown measure of X X . We present heuristics for the boundary and support of the Brown measure in terms of the Quaternionic Green’s function and verify they are true in the cases when the Brown measure of X X has been explicitly computed. In the general case, we show that the heuristic implies that the boundary of the Brown measure of X X is an algebraic curve, and provide an algorithm producing a polynomial defining this curve.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Brown measure

In this subsection, we recall the definition of the Brown measure of an operator
X\in(M,\tau)
and the relevant properties. These come from Brown’s original paper
[
4
]
and also from
[
16
]
,
[
18
]
,
[
10
]
.

First, we recall the definition of the Fuglede-Kadison determinant, first introduced in
[
9
]
:

Definition 2.1
.

Let
x\in(M,\tau)
. Let
\mu_{\left\lvert x\right\rvert}
be the spectral measure of
\left\lvert x\right\rvert=(x^{*}x)^{1/2}
. Then, the
Fuglede-Kadison determinant
of
x
,
\Delta(x)
, is given by:

(2)

\Delta(x)=\exp\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\log t\,d\mu_{\left\lvert x\right\rvert}(t)\right]\,.

When
x=X_{n}\in(M_{n}(\mathbb{C}),\frac{1}{n}\text{{tr}})
, then
\Delta(X_{n})=\left\lvert\det(X_{n})\right\rvert^{1/n}
. Thus, the Fuglede-Kadison determinant is a generalization of the normalized, positive determinant of a complex matrix.

To construct the Brown measure for
x\in(M,\tau)
, let
f_{\epsilon}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}
, where
\epsilon>0
, be given by:

(3)

f_{\epsilon}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\tau\left[\log((z-x)^{*}(z-x)+\epsilon)\right]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log(t^{2}+\epsilon)\,d\mu_{\left\lvert z-x\right\rvert}(t)\,.

Then,
f_{\epsilon}(z)
decreases
\log\Delta(z-x)
as
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
. Computation shows that
f_{\epsilon}
is twice-differentiable, with:

(4)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial z}f_{\epsilon}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\tau\left[(x_{z}^{*}x_{z}+\epsilon)^{-1}x_{z}^{*}\right]

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\overline{z}\partial z}f_{\epsilon}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon}{2}\tau\left[(x_{z}x_{z}^{*}+\epsilon)^{-1}(x_{z}^{*}x_{z}+\epsilon)^{-1}\right].

Hence,

(5)

\nabla^{2}f_{\epsilon}(z)=4\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\overline{z}\partial z}f_{\epsilon}(z)=2\epsilon\,\tau\left[(x_{z}x_{z}^{*}+\epsilon)^{-1}(x_{z}^{*}x_{z}+\epsilon)^{-1}\right]\geq 0\,.

Thus,
f_{\epsilon}
is subharmonic. Since
f_{\epsilon}(z)
decreases to
\log\Delta(z-x)
and
\log\Delta(z-x)>-\infty
for
\left\lvert z\right\rvert>\left\lVert x\right\rVert
, then
\log\Delta(z-x)
is also subharmonic. Thus,
f_{\epsilon}(z)
converges to
\log\Delta(z-x)
in
L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{C})
(with respect to the Lebesgue measure), and hence also as distributions. As
\nabla^{2}f_{\epsilon}(z)
are positive distributions, then so is
\nabla^{2}\log\Delta(z-x)
. Hence, this defines a measure, which is the Brown measure:

Definition 2.2
.

Let
x\in(M,\tau)
. The
Brown measure
of
x
is defined as:

(6)

\mu_{x}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\nabla^{2}\log\Delta(z-x)\,.

From the formulas for the derivatives of
f_{\epsilon}
, we have the following distributional limits:

(7)

\mu_{x}=\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z}}\tau\left[(x_{z}^{*}x_{z}+\epsilon)^{-1}x_{z}^{*}\right]=\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\epsilon}{\pi}\tau\left[(x_{z}x_{z}^{*}+\epsilon)^{-1}(x_{z}^{*}x_{z}+\epsilon)^{-1}\right].

The Brown measure of
x
is a probability measure supported on the spectrum of
x
. When
x
is normal, then the Brown measure is the spectral measure. When
x
is a random matrix, then the Brown measure is the empirical spectral distribution.

Further, the Riesz representation for the subharmonic function
L(z)=\log\left\lvert z-x\right\rvert
is given by:

(8)

\log\Delta(z-x)=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\log\left\lvert z-w\right\rvert\,d\mu_{x}(w)\,,

and the Brown measure is the unique complex Borel measure to satisfy this equation.

2.2.
Brown measure of
X=p+iq

In this subsection, we recall our prior work from
[
20
]
on the Brown measure of
X=p+iq
, where
p,q\in(M,\tau)
are Hermitian, freely independent, and have spectra consisting of
2
atoms. Specifically, let

(9)

\displaystyle\mu_{p}

\displaystyle=a\delta_{\alpha}+(1-a)\delta_{\alpha^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\mu_{q}

\displaystyle=b\delta_{\beta}+(1-b)\delta_{\beta^{\prime}}\,,

where
a,b\in(0,1)
,
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\alpha\neq\alpha^{\prime}
, and
\beta\neq\beta^{\prime}

The exact formulas describing the measure are not necessary, only some key properties which we restate.

First, we use the definition of the hyperbola and rectangle associated with
X=p+iq
:

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\mathscr{A}=\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha
and
\mathscr{B}=\beta^{\prime}-\beta
.

The

X
is

(10)

H=\left\{z=x+iy\in\mathbb{C}:\left(x-\frac{\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}}{2}\right)^{2}-\left(y-\frac{\beta+\beta^{\prime}}{2}\right)^{2}=\frac{\mathscr{A}^{2}-\mathscr{B}^{2}}{4}\right\}\,.

The
rectangle associated with
X
is

(11)

R=\left\{z=x+iy\in\mathbb{C}:x\in[\alpha\wedge\alpha^{\prime},\alpha\vee\alpha^{\prime}],y\in[\beta\wedge\beta^{\prime},\beta\vee\beta^{\prime}]\right\}\,.

We summarize the relevant parts of
[
20
, Theorem 6.1]
and
[
20
, Corollary 6.5]
describing the Brown measure of
X=p+iq
:

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
p,q\in(M,\tau)
be Hermitian, freely independent, and

(12)

\displaystyle\mu_{p}

\displaystyle=a\delta_{\alpha}+(1-a)\delta_{\alpha^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\mu_{q}

\displaystyle=b\delta_{\beta}+(1-b)\delta_{\beta^{\prime}}\,,

where
a,b\in(0,1)
,
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\alpha\neq\alpha^{\prime}
, and
\beta\neq\beta^{\prime}
.

The Brown measure of
X
is the convex combination of 4 atoms with another measure
\mu^{\prime}
:

(13)

\mu=\epsilon_{00}\delta_{\alpha+i\beta}+\epsilon_{01}\delta_{\alpha+i\beta^{\prime}}+\epsilon_{10}\delta_{\alpha^{\prime}+i\beta}+\epsilon_{11}\delta_{\alpha^{\prime}+i\beta^{\prime}}+\epsilon\mu^{\prime}\,,

where

(14)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{00}

\displaystyle=\max(0,a+b-1)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{01}

\displaystyle=\max(0,a+(1-b)-1)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{10}

\displaystyle=\max(0,(1-a)+b-1)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{11}

\displaystyle=\max(0,(1-a)+(1-b)-1)

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle=1-(\epsilon_{00}+\epsilon_{01}+\epsilon_{10}+\epsilon_{11})\,.

The Brown measure
\mu
is supported on
H\cap R
. Further,
\mu^{\prime}
has density extending to all
4
corners of the intersection of the hyperbola with the boundary of the rectangle if and only if
a=b=1/2
. Hence, the support of
\mu
is equal to
H\cap R
if and only if
a=b=1/2
.

A useful Lemma from
[
20
, Lemma 4.4]
about the hyperbola and rectangle associated with
X
is:

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\alpha\neq\alpha^{\prime}
and
\beta\neq\beta^{\prime}
. Let
\mathscr{A}=\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha
and
\mathscr{B}=\beta^{\prime}-\beta
.

Let

(15)

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\left\{z=x+iy\in\mathbb{C}:\left(x-\frac{\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}}{2}\right)^{2}-\left(y-\frac{\beta+\beta^{\prime}}{2}\right)^{2}=\frac{\mathscr{A}^{2}-\mathscr{B}^{2}}{4}\right\}

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\left\{z=x+iy\in\mathbb{C}:x\in[\alpha\wedge\alpha^{\prime},\alpha\vee\alpha^{\prime}],y\in[\beta\wedge\beta^{\prime},\beta\vee\beta^{\prime}]\right\}\,.

The equation of
H
is equivalent to:

(16)

(x-\alpha)(x-\alpha^{\prime})=(y-\beta)(y-\beta^{\prime})\,.

The equation of
H
in coordinates

(17)

\displaystyle x^{\prime}

\displaystyle=x-\frac{\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}}{2}

\displaystyle y^{\prime}

\displaystyle=y-\frac{\beta+\beta^{\prime}}{2}

is

(18)

(x^{\prime})^{2}-\frac{\mathscr{A}^{2}}{4}=(y^{\prime})^{2}-\frac{\mathscr{B}^{2}}{4}.

It follows that for
(x,y)\in H
,

(19)

(x,y)\in R\iff(\ref{eqn:lem:hyperbola_rectangle})\leq 0\iff x\in[\alpha\wedge\alpha^{\prime},\alpha\vee\alpha^{\prime}]\text{ or }y\in[\beta\wedge\beta^{\prime},\beta\vee\beta^{\prime}]\,.

Similarly,

(20)

(x,y)\in\accentset{\circ}{R}\iff(\ref{eqn:lem:hyperbola_rectangle})<0\iff x\in(\alpha\wedge\alpha^{\prime},\alpha\vee\alpha^{\prime})\text{ or }y\in(\beta\wedge\beta^{\prime},\beta\vee\beta^{\prime})\,.

Alternatively, the equation of the hyperbola is:

(21)

\text{{Re}}\left(\left(z-\frac{\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}}{2}-i\frac{\beta+\beta^{\prime}}{2}\right)^{2}\right)=\frac{\mathscr{A}^{2}-\mathscr{B}^{2}}{4}\,.

If
z\in H
, then
z\in R
if and only if

(22)

\left\lvert\text{{Im}}\left(\left(z-\frac{\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}}{2}-i\frac{\beta+\beta^{\prime}}{2}\right)^{2}\right)\right\rvert\leq\frac{\left\lvert\mathscr{A}\mathscr{B}\right\rvert}{2}\,.

Proof.

The equivalent equations for the hyperbola are straightforward to check.

The equivalences for the closed conditions follow from the following equivalences and the equation of the hyperbola in
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}
coordinates

(23)

\displaystyle(x,y)\in R

\displaystyle\iff(x^{\prime})^{2}-\frac{\mathscr{A}^{2}}{4}\leq 0\text{ and }(y^{\prime})^{2}-\frac{\mathscr{B}^{2}}{4}\leq 0

(24)

\displaystyle(x^{\prime})^{2}-\frac{\mathscr{A}^{2}}{4}\leq 0

\displaystyle\iff\left\lvert x^{\prime}\right\rvert\leq\frac{\left\lvert\mathscr{A}\right\rvert}{2}

\displaystyle\iff x\in[\alpha\wedge\alpha^{\prime},\alpha\vee\alpha^{\prime}]

\displaystyle(y^{\prime})^{2}-\frac{\mathscr{B}^{2}}{4}\leq 0

\displaystyle\iff\left\lvert y^{\prime}\right\rvert\leq\frac{\left\lvert\mathscr{B}\right\rvert}{2}

\displaystyle\iff y\in[\beta\wedge\beta^{\prime},\beta\vee\beta^{\prime}]\,.

The equivalences for the open conditions follow from similar equivalences with the closed conditions replaced by open conditions.

The last equation of the hyperbola follows from direct computation. For the inequality of the rectangle, observe that

(25)

\text{{Im}}\left(\left(z-\frac{\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}}{2}-i\frac{\beta+\beta^{\prime}}{2}\right)^{2}\right)=2x^{\prime}y^{\prime}\,.

In light of what was previously shown,

(26)

x^{\prime}y^{\prime}\leq\frac{\left\lvert\mathscr{A}\mathscr{B}\right\rvert}{4}\Longrightarrow x^{\prime}\leq\frac{\left\lvert\mathscr{A}\right\rvert}{2}\text{ or }y^{\prime}\leq\frac{\left\lvert\mathscr{B}\right\rvert}{2}\Longrightarrow z\in R\,.

Conversely,

(27)

z\in R\Longrightarrow x^{\prime}\leq\frac{\left\lvert\mathscr{A}\right\rvert}{2}\text{ and }y^{\prime}\leq\frac{\left\lvert\mathscr{B}\right\rvert}{2}\Longrightarrow x^{\prime}y^{\prime}\leq\frac{\left\lvert\mathscr{A}\mathscr{B}\right\rvert}{4}\,.

∎

2.3.
Quaternions

In this subsection, we introduce the notation for quaternions and state some basic properties.

The
quaternions
form a real 4-dimensional algebra generated by the elements
1
,
\bm{i}
,
\bm{j}
,
\bm{k}
, with the relations

(28)

\bm{i}^{2}=\bm{j}^{2}=\bm{k}^{2}=\bm{i}\bm{j}\bm{k}=-1\,.

A general quaternion
Q
can be written as:

(29)

Q=x_{0}+x_{1}\bm{i}+x_{2}\bm{j}+x_{3}\bm{k},\quad x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in\mathbb{R}\,.

From physics, the Pauli matrices in
M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
are defined by:

(30)

\sigma_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\sigma_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\sigma_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

It is easy to see that
i\sigma_{1}
,
i\sigma_{2}
, and
i\sigma_{3}
have the desired relations of the quaternionic generators
\bm{i}
,
\bm{j}
, and
\bm{k}
. The subalgebra of
M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
these elements and the identity matrix generate is
4
-dimensional over
\mathbb{R}
and hence is a representation of the quaternions. Explicitly, we use the following definition for quaternions for the quaternions:

Definition 2.6
.

The
quaternions
are the real subalgebra of
M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
consisting of the following matrices:

(31)

\mathbb{H}=\left\{Q=\begin{pmatrix}A&i\overline{B}\\
iB&\overline{A}\end{pmatrix}:A,B\in\mathbb{C}\right\}.

In terms of the coefficients
x_{i}
in (
29
),

(32)

A=x_{0}+ix_{3}\qquad B=x_{1}+ix_{2},\quad x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in\mathbb{R}\,.

The coefficients
x_{i}
are called the
real coefficients
of
Q
and
A
,
B
are the
complex coefficients
of
Q
.

We will refer to the
real numbers
in the quaternions as the subset where
x_{1}=x_{2}=x_{3}=0
(i.e.
A\in\mathbb{R}
and
B=0
). Similarly, we will refer to the
complex numbers
in the quaternions as the subset where
x_{1}=x_{2}=0
(i.e.
B=0
).

We highlight the fact that the quaternions are not a complex algebra, since the center of
\mathbb{H}
is
\mathbb{R}
and not
\mathbb{C}
.

There are some relevant operations on quaternions:

•

The
inverse
of a quaternion is just the matrix inverse.

•

The
conjugate
of a quaternion
Q=x_{0}+x_{1}i+x_{2}j+x_{3}k
is
\overline{Q}=x_{0}-x_{1}i-x_{2}j-x_{3}k
. In the matrix representation of
Q
, this corresponds to the matrix
Q^{*}
.

•

The
norm
of a quaternion,
\left\lvert Q\right\rvert
, is defined by:

(33)

\left\lvert Q\right\rvert=(Q\overline{Q})^{1/2}=\sqrt{x_{0}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}}

and is equal to the square root of the determinant of
Q
. From the multiplicative property of the determinant, it easily follows that
\left\lvert Q_{1}Q_{2}\right\rvert=\left\lvert Q_{1}\right\rvert\left\lvert Q_{2}\right\rvert
for quaternions
Q_{1}
,
Q_{2}
.

•

The norm on quaternions induces a metric, and when we speak of the
convergence
of a sequence of quaternions
Q_{k}\to Q
, it is with respect to this metric.

Every quaternion
Q
is diagonalizable with eigenvalues

(34)

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle=x_{0}+i\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}}

\displaystyle\overline{g}

\displaystyle=x_{0}-i\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}}\,.

From the (
34
), we observe the following facts:

•

The eigenvalues of
Q
come in conjugate pairs.

•

The eigenvalues of
Q
are distinct if and only if
Q\not\in\mathbb{R}
.

•

The eigenvalues of
Q
are real if and only if
Q\in\mathbb{R}
.

•

\left\lvert g\right\rvert^{2}=\det Q=\left\lvert Q\right\rvert^{2}
(so
\left\lvert g\right\rvert=\left\lvert Q\right\rvert
) and
\det(Q)=\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2}
.

In what follows, it is convenient to define
g^{I}
to be the eigenvalue of
Qi
with non-negative imaginary part. Using the notation in (
31
) and (
32
),

(35)

Qi=\begin{pmatrix}iA&\overline{B}\\
-B&-i\overline{A}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}iA&i\overline{(iB)}\\
i(iB)&\overline{iA}\end{pmatrix}.

Hence,

(36)

g^{I}=-x_{3}+i\sqrt{x_{2}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}+x_{0}^{2}}\,.

Remark 2.7
.

Given a quaternion
Q
, our convention (unless stated otherwise) will be to use
g
(resp
g^{I}
) to denote the eigenvalue of
Q
(resp.
Qi
) with non-negative imaginary part, i.e. as in (
34
) and (
36
).

It is easy to see directly from formulas that
\left\lvert g\right\rvert=\left\lvert g^{I}\right\rvert
. This also follows from the fact that
g^{I}
is an eigenvalue of
Qi
and
\left\lvert i\right\rvert=1
.

We also record the following observations about when
g,g^{I}\in\mathbb{R}
implies that
Q\in\mathbb{R}
or
Q\in i\mathbb{R}
:

Proposition 2.8
.

For
Q\in\mathbb{H}
,
g\in\mathbb{R}
implies that
Q\in\mathbb{R}
and
g^{I}\in\mathbb{R}
implies that
Q\in i\mathbb{R}
. But,
g^{I}\in i\mathbb{R}
does not imply that
Q\in\mathbb{R}
. But,
Q\in\mathbb{C}
and
g^{I}\in i\mathbb{R}
does imply that
Q\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

The first statements about
g\in\mathbb{R}
and
g^{I}\in\mathbb{R}
follow from the fact that
Q
has real eigenvalues if and only if
Q\in\mathbb{R}
(and similarly for
Qi
). If
Q=\bm{i}
, then
g^{I}\in i\mathbb{R}
but
Q\not\in\mathbb{R}
. If
Q\in\mathbb{C}
, then
g^{I}
is either

or
\overline{ig}
depending on the sign of
\text{{Im}}(ig)
, and so
g^{I}\in i\mathbb{R}
implies
g\in\mathbb{R}
, and hence
Q\in\mathbb{R}
.
∎

The conventions
\text{{Im}}(g)\geq 0
and
\text{{Im}}(g^{I})\geq 0
are useful for some general computations, but it has a drawback: if
Q\in\mathbb{C}
, then the eigenvalue
g
may not be equal to
Q
:
g=Q
when
\text{{Im}}(Q)\geq 0
and
g=\overline{Q}
otherwise. Similarly,
Qi\in\mathbb{C}
, but we can only conclude that either
g^{I}=Qi
or
g^{I}=\overline{Qi}
.

But, many of the complex-valued functions of
g
we will consider arise from context as functions of a quaternion
Q
. This means that these functions only depend on the
set
of eigenvalues of
Q
, i.e. these functions are invariant under changing
g
and
\overline{g}
. We record the following Lemma for these situations:

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
Q\in\mathbb{C}
,
g
(resp.
\overline{g}
) as in (
34
) (resp. (
36
), and
f_{1},f_{2}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
where

(37)

f_{1}(g)=f_{1}(\overline{g})\qquad f_{2}(g)=f_{2}(\overline{g})\,.

Then,

(38)

f_{1}(g)=f_{1}(Q)\qquad f_{2}(g^{I})=f_{2}(Qi)\,.

In particular, when we evaluate
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
at
g
and
g^{I}
respectively, we may assume that
g=Q
and
g^{I}=ig
.

Proof.

Recall that
g=Q
when
\text{{Im}}(Q)\geq 0
and
g=\overline{Q}
otherwise. Since
f_{1}(g)=f_{1}(\overline{g})
, in either case
f_{1}(g)=f_{1}(Q)
. Similar logic shows that
f_{2}(g^{I})=f_{2}(Qi)
. Hence, for purposes of evaluating
f_{1}(g)
and
f_{2}(g^{I})
, we may assume that
g=Q
and
g^{I}=Qi=ig
.
∎

We also record the following fact about the convergence of quaternions to a real number:

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
Q_{k}
be a sequence of quaternions where the eigenvalues of
Q_{k}
converge to some real number
g\in\mathbb{C}
. Then,
Q_{k}
converges to
g\in\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

Let
g_{k},\overline{g_{k}}
be the eigenvalues of
Q_{k}
, where

(39)

g_{k}=(x_{0})_{k}+i\sqrt{(x_{1})_{k}^{2}+(x_{2})_{k}^{2}+(x_{3})_{k}^{2}}\,.

g_{k}
converges to a real
t\in\mathbb{R}
if and only if
(x_{1})_{k}\to 0
,
(x_{2})_{k}\to 0
,
(x_{3})_{k}\to 0
, and
(x_{0})_{k}\to t
. This implies that
Q_{k}
converges to
t
.
∎

2.4.
Quaternionic Green’s function

In this subsection, we will define the Quaternionic Green’s function of an arbitrary
X\in(M,\tau)
in terms of the operator-valued Cauchy transform. It generalizes the Stieltjes transform by considering a non-Hermitian operator and being a function defined on quaternions. We will briefly define the relevant concepts in operator-valued free probability and apply them in our situation (summarized from (
[
16
]
, Chapters 9, 10) and (
[
1
]
, Chapter 9)). Then, we will show how to pass from the conventions in the mathematics literature to the conventions in the physics literature in our setup.

First, we review the definitions of the conditional expectation and operator-valued probability spaces:

Definition 2.11
.

Let
M
be a von Neumann algebra and
B
a von Neumann subalgebra. A
conditional expectation
from
E
to
B
is a linear map
E:M\to B
that satisfies the following properties:

(1)

If
x\geq 0
, then
E(x)\geq 0
.

(2)

E(b)=b
for
b\in B
.

(3)

E(b_{1}xb_{2})=b_{1}E(x)b_{2}
for
b_{1},b_{2}\in B
,
x\in M
.

We will refer to
(M,E,B)
as an
operator-valued probability space
.

When
(M,\tau)
is a tracial von Neumann algebra and
B=\mathbb{C}
, we can take
E=\tau
. As in the case when
B=\mathbb{C}
, there is a notion of freeness with respect to
E
:

Definition 2.12
.

Let
(M,E,B)
be an operator-valued probability space. Let
\{A_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a family of von Neumann subalgebras of
M
that each contain
B
. Then,
\{A_{i}\}_{i\in I}
are
freely independent with amalgamation over
\bm{B}
if for any
a_{j}\in A_{k(j)}
with
k(j)\neq k(j+1)
,
j=1,\ldots,n=1
and
E(a_{i})=0
, then

(40)

E(a_{1}\ldots,a_{n})=0\,.

Let
r,(m_{k})_{1\leq k\leq r}
be positive integers. The sets
\{X_{1,p},\ldots,X_{m_{p},p}\}_{1\leq p\leq r}
of non-commutative random variables are
free with amalgamation over
\bm{B}
if the algebras they generate with
B
are free.

Now, we define the operator-valued Cauchy transform and consider its domain of definition.

Recall that the usual complex-valued Cauchy transform for a Hermitian
x\in(M,\tau)
is a complex analytic function
G_{x}:\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
given by:

(41)

G_{x}(z)=\tau\left[(z-x)^{-1}\right].

Letting
\mathbb{H}^{+}(\mathbb{C})
be the upper half-plane and
\mathbb{H}^{-}(\mathbb{C})
be the lower half-plane, then in particular,
G_{x}:\mathbb{H}^{+}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{H}^{-}(\mathbb{C})
.

We can generalize the complex-valued Cauchy transform by considering a Cauchy transform that takes values in a subalgebra
B\subset M
, where
\tau
is replaced by the conditional expectation
E
. We state the definition of the operator-valued Cauchy transform and some facts about it:

Definition 2.13
.

Let
(M,E,B)
be an operator-valued probability space. For
x\in(M,\tau)
, let
\text{{Im}}(x)=\frac{x+x^{*}}{2i}
. Let the
operator upper/lower half-planes
be:

(42)

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{+}(B)

\displaystyle=\{x\in B:\text{{Im}}(x)>0\}

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{-}(B)

\displaystyle=\{x\in B:\text{{Im}}(x)<0\}\,.

Let
x\in(M,\tau)
where
x
is Hermitian. Then, the
operator-valued Cauchy transform

\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}:\mathbb{H}^{+}(B)\to\mathbb{H}^{-}(B)
is given by:

(43)

\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(b)=E\left[(b-X)^{-1}\right].

Further, the function
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}
is Fréchet differentiable on
\mathbb{H}^{+}(B)
.

Analogous to the complex-valued case, there is also an operator-valued
R
-transform:

Definition 2.14
.

Let
(M,E,B)
be an operator-valued probability space. Let
x\in(M,\tau)
where
x
is Hermitian.

Then for
b\in\mathbb{H}^{+}(B)
in a neighborhood of infinity,
\mathcal{G}_{x}(b)
is invertible. Thus, for
c\in\mathbb{H}^{-}(B)
in a neighborhood of
0
, we may define the
operator-valued
\bm{R}
-transform

(44)

\mathcal{R}_{x}(c)=\mathcal{G}_{x}^{\langle-1\rangle}(c)-c^{-1}\,.

Finally, the connection between the freeness and the
R
-transform in the operator-valued case is the operator-valued addition law:

Theorem 2.15
.

Let
(M,E,B)
be an operator-valued probability space, and let
x,y\in(M,E,B)
be Hermitian and freely independent with amalgamation over
B
. Where the functions are defined,

(45)

\mathcal{R}_{x+y}(c)=\mathcal{R}_{x}(c)+\mathcal{R}_{y}(c)\,.

Now, we introduce the setup in the mathematical literature to our problem of determining the Brown measure of
X=p+iq
: Let
(M,\tau)
be a tracial von-Neumann algebra. Then,
\left(M_{2}(M),\tau\left[\frac{1}{2}\text{{tr}}\right]\right)
is another tracial von-Neumann algebra. The unique trace-preserving conditional expectation from
M_{2}(M)
onto
M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
is the
block trace
:
\text{bTr}:M_{2}(M)\to M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
, given by:

(46)

\text{bTr}\left[\begin{pmatrix}m_{11}&m_{12}\\
m_{21}&m_{22}\end{pmatrix}\right]=\begin{pmatrix}\tau(m_{11})&\tau(m_{12})\\
\tau(m_{21})&\tau(m_{22})\end{pmatrix}\,.

Thus, we will work in the operator-valued probability space
(M_{2}(M),\text{bTr},M_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
. We will use
\sim
over the notation coming from the mathematics literature, then drop it for the corresponding objects from the physics literature.

Let
X\in M
. Then, consider

(47)

\mathbf{\tilde{X}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&X\\
X^{*}&0\end{pmatrix}\in M_{2}(M)\,.

Note that
\mathbf{\tilde{X}}
is Hermitian, so we may consider the operator-valued Cauchy transform
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{\tilde{X}}}:\mathbb{H}^{+}(M_{2}(\mathbb{C}))\to\mathbb{H}^{-}(M_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
given by:

(48)

\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{\tilde{X}}}(\tilde{Q})=\text{bTr}[(\tilde{Q}-\mathbf{\tilde{X}})^{-1}]\,,

where
\tilde{Q}\in\mathbb{H}^{+}(M_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
.

We consider
\tilde{Q}\in M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
of the form:

(49)

\tilde{Q}=\begin{pmatrix}i\overline{B}&A\\
\overline{A}&iB\end{pmatrix},\qquad A,B\in\mathbb{C}.

Then,

(50)

\text{{Im}}(\tilde{Q})=\begin{pmatrix}\text{{Re}}(B)&0\\
0&\text{{Re}}(B)\end{pmatrix}

and hence
\tilde{Q}\in\mathbb{H}^{+}(M_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
when
\text{{Re}}(B)>0
.

To pass from the mathematics notation to the physics notation, let
J\in M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
be the following matrix:

(51)

J=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

Note that
J^{2}=I
, so that
J=J^{-1}
. Then, let the corresponding physics quantities be:

(52)

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\tilde{X}}J=\begin{pmatrix}X&0\\
0&X^{*}\end{pmatrix}\in M_{2}(M)

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=\tilde{Q}J=\begin{pmatrix}A&i\overline{B}\\
iB&\overline{A}\end{pmatrix}\in M_{2}(\mathbb{C})\,.

Note that
Q
is exactly the general form of a quaternion from (
31
). Then, we can define the
Quaternionic Green’s function
by:

(53)

\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q)=\text{bTr}[(Q-\mathbf{X})^{-1}]\,.

The Quaternionic Green’s function
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q)
and operator-valued Cauchy transform
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{\tilde{X}}}(\tilde{Q})
are related by the following formula:

(54)

\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q)=J\,\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{\tilde{X}}}(\tilde{Q})\,.

From general facts about the operator-valued Cauchy transform, we know that
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q)
is well-defined for
B>0
and maps into
J\,\mathbb{H}^{-}(M_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
. But, we can say more:
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q)
is defined for quaternions
Q
where
B\neq 0
, and in this case
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q)
is a quaternion. For details of the straightforward computation, see
[
5
]
. Thus, we have the following definition of the Quaternionic Green’s function:

Definition 2.16
.

Let
X\in(M,\tau)
. For
A\in\mathbb{C}
, let
X_{A}=A-X
. Then, the
Quaternionic Green’s function

\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}:\mathbb{H}\setminus\sigma(X)\to\mathbb{H}
is given by:

(55)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{bTr}[(Q-\mathbf{X})^{-1}]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{bTr}\left[\left(\begin{pmatrix}A&i\overline{B}\\
iB&\overline{A}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}X&0\\
0&X^{*}\end{pmatrix}\right)^{-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\tau[((X_{A})^{*}X_{A}+\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2})^{-1}(X_{A})^{*}]&-i\overline{B}\,\tau[((X_{A})^{*}X_{A}+\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2})^{-1}]\\
-iB\,\tau[((X_{A})^{*}X_{A}+\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2})^{-1}]&\tau[X_{A}((X_{A})^{*}X_{A}+\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2})^{-1}]\end{pmatrix}.

When
B=0
in the quaternion
Q
, then
Q=A\in\mathbb{C}
and

(56)

\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q)=\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(A)=\tau[(A-X)^{-1}]\,,

and this formula is well-defined even for non-Hermitian
X
when
A\not\in\sigma(X)
. We will discuss the restriction of
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}
to
\mathbb{C}
in a later section.

Let
\mathbb{H}^{+}
be the
upper Quaternionic half-plane
(and
\mathbb{H}^{-}
be the
lower Quaternionic half-plane
):

(57)

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{+}

\displaystyle=\{Q\in\mathbb{H}:B>0\}

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{-}

\displaystyle=\{Q\in\mathbb{H}:B<0\}\,.

Note that the Quaternionic upper/lower half-planes are not the operator upper/lower half-planes of
M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
restricted to
\mathbb{H}
.

As a generalization of the fact that the complex Cauchy transform swaps the upper/lower half-planes, it is easy to see from the formula for
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}
that:

(58)

\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}:\mathbb{H}^{\pm}\to\mathbb{H}^{\mp}\,.

Similarly, as a consequence of the formula for
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}
, there is a generalization of the conjugate symmetry of the complex Cauchy transform:

(59)

\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X^{*}}}(Q^{*})=\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q)^{*}\,.

Now, we provide the relevant formulas relating the Inverse Quaternionic Green’s function with the inverse operator-valued Cauchy transform and the Quaternionic
R
-transform with the operator-valued
R
-transform. These formulas follow directly from (
52
) and (
54
):

Definition 2.17
.

Let
X\in(M,\tau)
. The
Inverse Quaternionic Green’s function
,
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}=\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\langle-1\rangle}
, is defined for quaternions
W\in J\,\mathbb{H}^{-}(M_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
in a neighborhood of
0
. Its relationship with the inverse operator-valued Cauchy transform
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{\tilde{X}}}^{\langle-1\rangle}
is:

(60)

\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(W)=\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\langle-1\rangle}(W)=\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{\tilde{X}}}^{\langle-1\rangle}(J\,W)\,J.

The
Inverse Quaternionic
\bm{R}
-transform
,
\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{X}}
, is defined for quaternions
W\in J\,\mathbb{H}^{-}(M_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
in a neighborhood of
0
. Its relationship with the inverse operator-valued Cauchy transform
\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{\tilde{X}}}
is:

(61)

\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{X}}(W)=\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\langle-1\rangle}(W)-W^{-1}=\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{\tilde{X}}}(J\,W)\,W.

We conclude that there is an addition law for the Quaternionic
R
-transform:

Proposition 2.18
.

Let
x,y\in(M,\tau)
be freely independent. For quaternions
W\in J\,\mathbb{H}^{-}(M_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
in a neighborhood of
0
,

(62)

\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{x+y}}(W)=\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{x}}(W)+\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{y}}(W)\,.

Proof.

Consider the operator-valued probability space
(M_{2}(M),\text{bTr},M_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
. If
x
and
y
are free in
(M,\tau)
, then it is easy to see that
\mathbf{\tilde{x}}
and
\mathbf{\tilde{y}}
are free with amalgamation over
M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. Hence, from the operator-valued addition law,

(63)

\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{\tilde{x}+\tilde{y}}}(c)=\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{\tilde{x}}}(c)+\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{\tilde{y}}}(c)\,,

and this identity holds for
c\in\mathbb{H}^{-}(M_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
in a neighborhood of
0
. Then, for quaternions
W\in J\,\mathbb{H}^{-}(M_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
in a neighborhood of
0
,

(64)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}}(W)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{\tilde{x}+\tilde{y}}}(J\,W)\,W

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{\tilde{x}}}(J\,W)\,W+\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{\tilde{y}}}(J\,W)\,W

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{x}}(W)+\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{y}}(W)\,.

∎

2.5.
Quaternionic Green’s function and Brown measure

In this subsection, we discuss the relationship between the Quaternionic Green’s function and the Brown measure. There is a strong analogy between the Quaternionic Green’s function for an arbitrary
X\in(M,\tau)
and the usual complex Cauchy transform for a Hermitian
x\in(M,\tau)
. Recall that the key utility of
G_{x}
is that the spectral measure of

is a distributional limit of the Cauchy transform approaching the real axis:

(65)

\mu_{x}=\lim\limits_{b\to 0^{+}}-\frac{1}{\pi}\text{{Im}}\,G_{\mu}(\cdot+ib)\,.

To complete the analogy, we describe how to recover the Brown measure of

as a limit of the Quaternionic Green’s function approaching the complex plane.

First, let
F:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
capture the first part of the quaternion, i.e. for a quaternion

as in (
31
),
F(Q)=A
.

For

and

, consider the quaternion

(66)

z_{\epsilon}=\begin{pmatrix}z&i\epsilon\\
i\epsilon&\overline{z}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{H}.

Then,

(67)

F(\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(z_{\epsilon}))=\tau[((X_{z})^{*}X_{z}+\epsilon^{2})^{-1}(X_{z})^{*}]=2\,\frac{\partial}{\partial z}f_{\epsilon^{2}}(z)\,,

where
f_{\epsilon^{2}}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}
is from (
3
), given by:
Then, from (
7
), the Brown measure of
X
,
\mu_{X}
, is given by the following distributional limit:

(68)

\mu_{X}=\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z}}F(\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(z_{\epsilon}))\,.

In the mathematics notation,
\tilde{z_{\epsilon}}=z_{\epsilon}\,J\in\mathbb{H}^{+}(M_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
for

and we can recover the Brown measure from a similar formula in terms of the operator-valued Cauchy transform as

3.
Outline for computing Inverse Quaternionic Green’s function for

In this section, we consider
X=p+iq
, where
p,q\in(M,\tau)
are Hermitian and freely independent. Consider the operator-valued probability space
(M_{2}(M),\text{bTr},M_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
and the following elements of
M_{2}(M)
:

(69)

\mathbf{p}=\begin{pmatrix}p&0\\
0&p\end{pmatrix}\qquad i\mathbf{q}=\begin{pmatrix}iq&0\\
0&-iq\end{pmatrix}.

From the addition law for the Quaternionic
R
-transform,

(70)

\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q)=\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{p}}(Q)+\mathcal{R}_{i\mathbf{q}}(Q)\,.

In terms of the Inverse Quaternionic Green’s function,

(71)

\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q)=\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{p}}(Q)+\mathcal{B}_{i\mathbf{q}}(Q)-Q^{-1}\,.

In this section, we will outline how to obtain expressions for
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{p}}(Q)
and
\mathcal{B}_{i\mathbf{q}}(Q)
, and hence how to obtain an expression for
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q)
.

The computations in this section are taken from (
[
15
]
, Section 4), so we will justify the domains where the computations make sense and state their results.

3.1.
(Inverse) Quaternionic Green’s function at a complex number

In this subsection, we make some observations about the restriction of the (Inverse) Quaternionic Green’s function of an arbitrary
X\in(M,\tau)
to the complex numbers.

Recall that the Cauchy transform of a Hermitian
x\in(M,\tau)
has
G_{x}(z)\in\mathbb{R}
if and only if
z\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\sigma(x)
. We generalize this fact for
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}
:

Let
X\in(M,\tau)
and consider a complex number quaternion,
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(X)
. From the definition of the Quaternionic Green’s function,

(72)

\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(z)=\tau[(z-X)^{-1}]\,.

Note that this is the same formula for the Cauchy transform
G_{X}(z)
, except
X
is potentially non-Hermitian. Even though
X
is not necessarily Hermitian, this function is defined and analytic for
z\not\in\sigma(X)
. In this context, we will refer to this function as the
complex Green’s function
(or
Holomorphic Green’s function
, as used in
[
15
]
) of
X
and still use the notation
G_{X}
. The significance of the term “Holomorphic Green’s function” will be discussed in a later section.

Conversely, consider
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q)
for
Q\in\mathbb{H}\setminus\mathbb{C}
, i.e.
B\neq 0
. The off-diagonal term of
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q)
is:

(73)

-i\overline{B}\,\tau[((X_{A})^{*}X_{A}+\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2})^{-1}]\,,

where
X_{A}=A-X
. If
B\neq 0
, then this term is non-zero. Hence,

(74)

\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q)\in\mathbb{C}\iff Q\in\mathbb{C}\,,

and in this case
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(z)=G_{X}(z)
.

When
X
is non-Hermitian,
G_{X}(z)
still satisfies
zG_{X}(z)\to 1
as
\left\lvert z\right\rvert\to\infty
. In particular,
G_{X}
is still invertible in a neighborhood of infinity, and its inverse is defined in a neighborhood of
0
. Let
B_{X}=G_{X}^{\langle-1\rangle}
. Let
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}:V\to\mathbb{H}
. For
Q\in V
,

(75)

\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q)\in\mathbb{C}\iff Q=\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q))\in\mathbb{C}\,.

Let
U,V\subset\mathbb{H}
be domains where
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}:U\to V
,
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}:V\to U
,
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}\circ\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}=\text{id}_{U}
, and
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}\circ\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}=\text{id}_{V}
. Then, for
z\in U\cap\mathbb{C}
and
w\in V\cap\mathbb{C}
,

(76)

\displaystyle G_{X}(\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(w))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(w))=w

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(z))=\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(G_{X}(z))\,.

Hence,
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}
is an inverse for
G_{X}
on
U\cap\mathbb{C}
, so
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}=B_{X}
on
U\cap\mathbb{C}
.

We summarize all of this in the following Proposition:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
X\in(M,\tau)
. Then,
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q)\in\mathbb{C}
if and only if
Q\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(X)
. For
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(X)
,

(77)

\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(z)=G_{X}(z)\,.

Let
B_{X}=G_{X}^{\langle-1\rangle}
and let
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}=\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\langle-1\rangle}
. Let
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}:V\to\mathbb{H}
. Then,
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q)\in\mathbb{C}
if and only if
Q\in V\cap\mathbb{C}
. For
w\in V\cap\mathbb{C}
,

(78)

\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(w)=B_{X}(w)\,.

3.2.
(Inverse) Quaternionic Green’s function for a Hermitian operator

In this subsection, we describe how to compute the Quaternionic Green’s Function for a Hermitian
H\in(M,\tau)
,
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{H}}
, in terms of the usual Cauchy transform
G_{H}
. Using analogous computations, we will determine where the Inverse Green’s Function for
H
,
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}
, is well-defined and compute
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}
in terms of the inverse of the usual Cauchy transform,
B_{H}=G_{H}^{\langle-1\rangle}
.

To compute
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q)
, recall when
Q=g\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(H)
,

(79)

\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{H}}(g)=G_{H}(g)\,.

For an arbitrary quaternion
Q\not\in\mathbb{R}
, we may diagonalize
Q
by:

(80)

Q=S^{-1}gS\,,

where

(81)

S=\begin{pmatrix}iB&g-A\\
\overline{g}-\overline{A}&i\overline{B}\end{pmatrix}.

Since
H
is Hermitian, then
\mathbf{H}
commutes
M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. This combined with the bimodularity of
bTr
shows that

(82)

\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q)=\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{H}}(S^{-1}gS)=S^{-1}\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{H}}(g)S\,.

Note that since
Q\not\in\mathbb{R}
, then
g\not\in\mathbb{R}
, so
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{H}}(g)
is well-defined.

Thus, we can express
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q)
in terms of
Q
and
G_{H}(g)
. The result of the computation is:

(83)

\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q)=\begin{cases}\gamma_{H}(g)1-\gamma_{H}^{\prime}(g)Q^{*}&Q\not\in\mathbb{R}\\
G_{H}(g)&Q=g\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(H)\,,\end{cases}

where

(84)

\displaystyle\gamma_{H}(g)

\displaystyle=\frac{gG_{H}(g)-\overline{g}G_{H}(\overline{g})}{g-\overline{g}}

\displaystyle\gamma_{H}^{\prime}(g)

\displaystyle=\frac{G_{H}(g)-G_{H}(\overline{g})}{g-\overline{g}}\,.

It is straightforward to check that both formulas for
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q)
agree for
Q\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
.

While the computation is carried out with the convention that
\text{{Im}}(g)\geq 0
,
\gamma_{H}
and
\gamma_{H}^{\prime}
only depends on the
set
of eigenvalues of
Q
since
\gamma_{H}(g)=\gamma_{H}(\overline{g})
and
\gamma_{H}^{\prime}(g)=\gamma_{H}^{\prime}(\overline{g})
. Finally, note that
\gamma_{H}
,
\gamma_{H}^{\prime}
are real-valued.

For what follows, we define the following notation for the eigenspaces of a quaternion
Q
:

Definition 3.2
.

Let
Q\in\mathbb{H}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. Then, define
\bm{E_{\lambda}(Q)}
to be the
\mathbf{\lambda}
-eigenspace
for
Q
, i.e.
E_{\lambda}(Q)=\text{{ker}}(Q-\lambda)
.

We observe the properties
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{H}}(g)=G_{H}(g)
and
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q)=S^{-1}\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{H}}(g)S
lead to the following coordinate-free characterization of
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{H}}
that will be useful in defining
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
H\in(M,\tau)
be Hermitian. Let
Q\in\mathbb{H}
be a quaternion such that
Q\not\in\sigma(X)
. Then,
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{H}}:\mathbb{H}\setminus\sigma(X)\to\mathbb{H}
is defined by the property that

(85)

E_{G_{H}(g)}(\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q))=E_{g}(Q)\,.

Proof.

First, note that the definition is well-defined, i.e. that
G_{H}
is 1-1 on the set of eigenvalues of
Q
. When
Q\in\mathbb{R}
, then there is only one eigenvalue. When
Q\not\in\mathbb{R}
, then
Q
has two eigenvalues
g,\overline{g}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
. Then, since
G_{H}(g)\in\mathbb{R}
if and only if
g\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\sigma(H)
, then
G_{H}(\overline{g})=\overline{G_{H}(g)}\neq G_{H}(g)
. As
Q
is a quaternion, it is diagonalizable, and hence this property defines
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q)
.
∎

This leads to a coordinate-free definition of
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}=\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{H}}^{\langle-1\rangle}
and a domain where it is defined. Then, the analogous computations as for
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q)
gives an explicit formula for
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}
in terms of
Q
and
B_{H}(g)
. The results of this are summarized in the following Proposition:

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
H\in(M,\tau)
be Hermitian. Let
B_{H}:U\to\mathbb{C}
be the inverse of
G_{H}
, where
U
is an open neighborhood of
0
that is fixed under complex conjugation. Then,
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}=\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{H}}^{\langle-1\rangle}
is defined on
\mathbb{H}_{U}=\{Q\in\mathbb{H}:g\in U\}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}
is defined by the property that:

(86)

E_{B_{H}(g)}(\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q))=E_{g}(Q)\,.

In particular,
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}(g)=B_{H}(g)
for
g\in U
and
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q)=S^{-1}\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q)S
for
Q\in\mathbb{H}_{U}
,
S\in GL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
.

\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}
is continuous on
\mathbb{H}_{U}
and an explicit formula for
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}
is given by:

(87)

\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q)=\begin{cases}\beta_{H}(g)1-\beta_{H}^{\prime}(g)Q^{*}&Q\not\in\mathbb{R}\\
B_{H}(x_{0})1&Q=x_{0}1\in\mathbb{R}\,,\end{cases}

where

(88)

\displaystyle\beta_{H}(g)

\displaystyle=\frac{gB_{H}(g)-\overline{g}B_{H}(\overline{g})}{g-\overline{g}}

\displaystyle\beta_{H}^{\prime}(g)

\displaystyle=\frac{B_{H}(g)-B_{H}(\overline{g})}{g-\overline{g}}\,.

Finally,
\beta_{H}(g)=\beta_{H}(\overline{g})
,
\beta_{H}^{\prime}(g)=\beta_{H}^{\prime}(\overline{g})
, and
\beta_{H}
,
\beta_{H}^{\prime}
are real-valued.

Proof.

First, note that we may extend the domain of
B_{H}
to one that is fixed by complex conjugation, because of the conjugate symmetry of
G_{H}
.

The definition is well-defined because
B_{H}
is 1-1 on the set of eigenvalues of
Q
. When
Q\in\mathbb{R}
, there is only one eigenvalue. When
Q\not\in\mathbb{R}
, then
Q
has two eigenvalues
g,\overline{g}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
. Then, since
B_{H}(g)\in\mathbb{R}
implies that
g\in\mathbb{R}
. As
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q)
is diagonalizable, then this defines
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q)
.

This definition gives an inverse for
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{H}}
, since

(89)

\displaystyle E_{g}(\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{H}}(\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q)))

\displaystyle=E_{G_{H}(B_{H}(g))}(\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{H}}(\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q)))

\displaystyle=E_{B_{H}(g)}(\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q))

\displaystyle=E_{g}(Q)\,.

implies that
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{H}}(\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q))=Q
and similar equalities show that
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}(\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q))=Q
.

For
g\in\mathbb{H}^{+}(\mathbb{C})\cap U
,

(90)

\displaystyle E_{B_{H}(g)}(\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}(g))

\displaystyle=E_{g}(g)=e_{1}

\displaystyle E_{B_{H}(\overline{g})}(\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}(g))

\displaystyle=E_{\overline{g}}(g)=e_{2}\,.

so then
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}(g)=B_{H}(g)
. When
g\in\mathbb{H}^{-}(\mathbb{C})\cap U
,
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
are swapped in the above equalities. For
S\in GL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
and
Q\in\mathbb{H}_{U}
,

(91)

\displaystyle E_{B_{H}(g)}(\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}(S^{-1}QS))

\displaystyle=E_{g}(S^{-1}QS)

\displaystyle=S^{-1}E_{g}(Q)

\displaystyle=S^{-1}E_{B_{H}(g)}(\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q))

\displaystyle=E_{B_{H}(g)}(S^{-1}\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q)S)\,.

As
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q)
is diagonalizable, then
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}(S^{-1}QS)=S^{-1}\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q)S
.

For continuity of
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}
, let
Q_{n}\to Q
, where
Q_{n},Q\in\mathbb{H}_{U}
.

If
Q\in\mathbb{R}
, then
g_{n},\overline{g_{n}}\to g=Q\in\mathbb{R}
and
B_{H}(g_{n}),B_{H}(\overline{g_{n}})\to B_{H}(g)=B_{H}(Q)\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence, the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q_{n})
converge to
B_{H}(Q)
, and from Lemma
2.10
, this implies that
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q_{n})
converges to
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q)=B_{H}(Q)
.

If
Q\not\in\mathbb{R}
, then
g_{n}\to g
,
\overline{g_{n}}\to\overline{g}
, and the diagonalizing transforms for
Q_{n}
,

(92)

S_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}iB_{n}&g_{n}-A_{n}\\
\overline{g_{n}}-\overline{A_{n}}&i\overline{B_{n}}\end{pmatrix}

converge to
S
, the diagonalizing transform for
Q
. Then, using the conjugation property of
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}
shows that
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q_{n})
converges to
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}(Q)
.

The explicit formula for
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{H}}
follows from the analogous computation as for
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{H}}
. The properties for
\beta_{H},\beta^{\prime}_{H}
are self-evident.
∎

3.3.
(Inverse) Quaternionic Green’s function and multiplication by a complex number

In this subsection, we consider an arbitrary
X\in(M,\tau)
. For
c\in\mathbb{C}
, we compare
\mathcal{G}_{c\mathbf{X}}
with
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}
, and similarly
\mathcal{B}_{c\mathbf{X}}
with
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}
.

For
c=0
and
Q\in\mathbb{H}\setminus\{0\}
,

(93)

\mathcal{G}_{c\mathbf{X}}(Q)=\mathcal{G}_{0}(Q)=Q^{-1}\,.

For
c\neq 0
and
Q\in\mathbb{H}\setminus\sigma(cX)=\mathbb{H}\setminus(c\sigma(X))
,

(94)

\mathcal{G}_{c\mathbf{X}}(Q)=\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}\left(\frac{1}{c}Q\right)\frac{1}{c}\,.

The order of multiplication is important, as the quaternion
Q
may not commute with
c\in\mathbb{C}
.

This generalizes the formula for the complex Green’s function,

(95)

G_{cX}(z)=\frac{1}{c}G_{X}\left(\frac{1}{c}z\right).

The formula for the inverse Green’s function
\mathcal{B}_{cX}
can be obtained by inverting this formula. This is summarized in the following Proposition:

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
X\in(M,\tau)
and let
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}:V\to\mathbb{H}
, where
V\subset\mathbb{H}
.

For
c=0
,
\mathcal{B}_{c\mathbf{X}}:\mathbb{H}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{H}
and

(96)

\mathcal{B}_{c\mathbf{X}}(Q)=Q^{-1}\,.

For
c\neq 0
,
\mathcal{B}_{c\mathbf{X}}:V\left(\frac{1}{c}\right)\to\mathbb{H}
, and

(97)

\mathcal{B}_{c\mathbf{X}}(Q)=c\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(Qc)\,.

Proof.

The result for
c=0
follows from
\mathcal{G}_{0\mathbf{X}}=Q^{-1}
, so we consider
c\neq 0
.

Let
U,V\subset\mathbb{H}
be domains where
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}:U\to V
,
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}:V\to U
,
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}\circ\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}=\text{id}_{U}
, and
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}\circ\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}=\text{id}_{V}
. From (
94
),
\mathcal{G}_{c\mathbf{X}}:c\,U\to V\left(\frac{1}{c}\right)
. Similarly, defining
\mathcal{B}_{c\mathbf{X}}(Q)=c\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(Qc)
shows that
\mathcal{B}_{c\mathbf{X}}:V\left(\frac{1}{c}\right)\to c\,U
and it is straightforward to check that
\mathcal{B}_{c\mathbf{X}}\circ\mathcal{G}_{c\mathbf{X}}=\text{id}_{c\,U}
, and
\mathcal{G}_{c\mathbf{X}}\circ\mathcal{B}_{c\mathbf{X}}=\text{id}_{V\left(\frac{1}{c}\right)}
.
∎

For the complex
B_{cX}
, there is a similar formula:

(98)

B_{cX}(z)=cB_{X}(cz)\,.

This can be proved analogously from the formula for the complex Green’s function.

3.4.
Inverse Quaternionic Green’s function for
X=p+iq

In this subsection, we summarize how to compute
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}
when
X=p+iq
, where
p,q\in(M,\tau)
are Hermitian and freely independent. Recall the definition of
g^{I}
from (
36
).

From the addition law for the Quaternionic
R
-transform, and Proposition
3.5
,

(99)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{p}}(Q)+\mathcal{B}_{i\mathbf{q}}(Q)-Q^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{p}}(Q)+i\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{q}}(Qi)-Q^{-1}\,.

Let
B_{p}:U\to\mathbb{C}
and
B_{q}:V\to\mathbb{C}
. Then, the right-hand side of (
99
) is well-defined for
g\in U
,
g^{I}\in V
.

Using (
87
) to rewrite
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{p}}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{q}}
when
g,g^{I}\not\in\mathbb{R}
,

(100)

\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q)=\beta_{p}(g)+\beta_{q}(g^{I})i-\left(\beta_{p}^{\prime}(g)+\beta_{q}^{\prime}(g^{I})+\frac{1}{\det Q}\right)Q^{*}\,.

Expanding this completely,

(101)

\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q)=\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}\left(\begin{pmatrix}A&i\overline{B}\\
iB&\overline{A}\end{pmatrix}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}k+ik^{\prime}-l\overline{A}&li\overline{B}\\
liB&k-ik^{\prime}-lA\end{pmatrix}\,,

where

(102)

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle=\beta_{p}(g)

\displaystyle k^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\beta_{q}(g^{I})

\displaystyle l

\displaystyle=\beta_{p}^{\prime}(g)+\beta_{q}^{\prime}(g^{I})+\frac{1}{\det Q}\,.

We conclude by making some observations:

•

k
,
k^{\prime}
, and
l
are real-valued.

•

The addition law (
71
) defines
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}
and works for all
Q
in the domain of
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}
, but the expanded formula (
101
) only works when
g,g^{I}\not\in\mathbb{R}
.

•

We can apply Lemma
2.9
to
\beta_{p},\beta_{q},\beta_{p}^{\prime},\beta_{q}^{\prime}
, which are all real-valued and respect conjugation. Then, when evaluating these functions at some
Q\in\mathbb{C}
, we can assume that
g=Q
and
g^{I}=ig
in (
100
) and (
101
).

•

Due to the presence of
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{q}}(Qi)
in (
97
), it does not follow that
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(S^{-1}QS)=S^{-1}\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q)S
as in the case when
X
is Hermitian. In particular,
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}
is not determined by how it acts on diagonal (i.e. complex) quaternions. Further, it is not necessarily true like in the Hermitian case that the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}
are just
B_{X}(g)
,
B_{X}(\overline{g})
.

In summary, we will complete the following steps to compute
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}
:

(1)

Compute
B_{p}
(resp.
B_{q}
).

(2)

Use (
88
) to compute
\beta_{p},\beta_{p}^{\prime}
(resp.
\beta_{q},\beta_{q}^{\prime}
).

(3)

Use (
87
) to compute
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{p}}
(resp.
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{q}}
).

(4)

Use (
97
) to compute
\mathcal{B}_{i\mathbf{q}}
.

(5)

Use (
101
) to compute
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}
.

4.
Heuristics

In this section, we provide the heuristics for the boundary and support of the Brown measure of
X=p+iq
when
p,q\in(M,\tau)
are Hermitian and freely independent.

Recall that the Brown measure of
X\in(M,\tau)
can be defined by:

(103)

\mu_{X}=\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z}}F(\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(z_{\epsilon}))\,.

Let
X=p+iq
where
p,q
are Hermitian and freely independent. Consider
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(z_{\epsilon})
is in the domain of
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}
for all sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
.

Let

(104)

\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(z_{\epsilon})=\begin{pmatrix}A_{\epsilon}&i\overline{B_{\epsilon}}\\
iB_{\epsilon}&\overline{A_{\epsilon}}\end{pmatrix}\,,

where
A_{\epsilon}
and
B_{\epsilon}
are implicitly understood to depend on
z
. We wish to analyze
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(z_{\epsilon})
as
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
.

From the free independence of
p
and
q
and the Quaternionic addition law, it is more natural to consider
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}
. If
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(z_{\epsilon})
is in the domain of
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}
, then

(105)

\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}\left(Q_{\epsilon}\right)=z_{\epsilon}

has the unique solution of

(106)

Q_{\epsilon}=\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(z_{\epsilon})\,.

Since we can explicitly compute
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}
, then we can use (
105
) to understand
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(z_{\epsilon})
. Note that we can always use (
101
) to expand
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q_{\epsilon})
: if
Q_{\epsilon}
has
g_{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{R}
or
g_{\epsilon}^{I}\in\mathbb{R}
, then
Q_{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{C}
and from Proposition
3.1
,
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q_{\epsilon})\in\mathbb{C}
, a contradiction to
\epsilon>0
.

By expanding the left-hand side of (
105
) with (
101
), the equation for the off-diagonal terms is:

(107)

l(Q_{\epsilon})iB_{\epsilon}=i\epsilon\,.

As
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
, then either
l_{\epsilon}=l(Q_{\epsilon})
is small or
B_{\epsilon}
is small. If we consider a sequence of
\epsilon_{k}
to
0
, we may extract a subsequence where either
l_{\epsilon_{k}}=l(Q_{\epsilon_{k}})
converges to
0
or
B_{\epsilon_{k}}
converges to
0
.

To understand the situation heuristically, assume that

(108)

\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}Q_{\epsilon}=Q=\begin{pmatrix}A&i\overline{B}\\
iB&\overline{A}\end{pmatrix}\,.

Then, either

(109)

\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}B_{\epsilon}=B=0\qquad\text{ or }\qquad\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}l_{\epsilon}=0\,.

Assume it is possible to interchange the limit and derivative in (
103
). Then,

(110)

\mu_{X}=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z}}F(Q)\,.

If
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}
is defined and continuous at
Q
, then

(111)

z=\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}z_{\epsilon}=\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q_{\epsilon})=\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q)\,.

Thus,

(112)

\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(z)=Q\,,

so that
B=0
. As
G_{X}
is holomorphic at
z
, then

(113)

\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z}}F(Q)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z}}G_{X}(z)=0\,.

Our first heuristic is that whenever
B=0
(i.e. not just in the continuous case), the Brown measure has zero density at
z
. Then, the Brown measure is supported on the set where
B\neq 0
. We also must consider the case where
Q_{\epsilon}
has no limit as
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
. We state the heuristic formally:

Heuristic 4.1
.

Let
p,q\in(M,\tau)
where
p
and
q
are Hermitian and freely independent. Then, the support of the Brown measure of
X=p+iq
is the closure of the set of
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that:

(114)

\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(z_{\epsilon})=\begin{pmatrix}A&i\overline{B}\\
iB&\overline{A}\end{pmatrix}

for some
B\neq 0
or where the limit does not exist.

We can verify this heuristic when
p
and
q
have
2
atoms that have equal weights (Theorem
7.1
).

For the second heuristic, first note that:

(115)

\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}B_{\epsilon}=B\neq 0\Longrightarrow\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}l_{\epsilon}=0\,.

Hence, given the first heuristic, the support of the Brown measure should also be contained in the closure of the set of
z\in\mathbb{C}
where
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}l_{\epsilon}=0
. But, it could be possible that this set has non-empty intersection with the set of
z\in\mathbb{C}
where
B=0
, where the measure has zero density. In particular, when
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}
is continuous at
Q
, then the intersection of these sets corresponds to a second order zero in the off-diagonal terms of (
101
). Our second heuristic is that the intersection of these two sets is the boundary of the Brown measure:

Heuristic 4.2
.

Let
p,q\in(M,\tau)
where
p
and
q
are Hermitian and freely independent. Then, the boundary of the Brown measure of
X=p+iq
is the closure of the intersection of the set of
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that:

(116)

\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(z_{\epsilon})=Q\in\mathbb{C}

with the set of
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}l_{\epsilon}=0
.

In (
[
15
]
, Section 6), the authors verify the boundary heuristic for some random matrix models. With some restrictions on
z
, we will see that the intersection of the sets corresponds to the solutions of a system of equations. When
p
and
q
have
2
atoms, Proposition
6.2
states that its closure is the boundary (i.e. support) of
\mu^{\prime}
.

When
p
and
q
have an arbitrary number of atoms, Mathematica simulations suggest that the solution set to the system of equations contains the boundary of the Brown measure. When
p
and
q
have generic positions of atoms and weights, this solution set is an algebraic curve. We will provide an algorithm to produce a non-zero polynomial whose zero set contains this solution set. This is the content of Theorem
6.3
.

We can now explain the terminology of the “Holomorphic Green’s function” for
G_{X}(z)
. Consider
z\in\mathbb{C}
where the following limit exists:

(117)

\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(z_{\epsilon})=\begin{pmatrix}A(z)&i\overline{B(z)}\\
iB(z)&\overline{A(z)}\end{pmatrix}\,.

When
B(z)=0
and
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}
is defined and continuous at the limit point, recall that
A(z)=G_{X}(z)
. Hence, the
Holomorphic Green’s function
is the limit of the Quaternionic Green’s function towards the complex plane where the resulting complex function is holomorphic.

When
B(z)\neq 0
, then from Heuristic
4.1
, the Brown measure of
X
is not zero in a neighborhood of
z
, so then
A(z)
is not holomorphic in a neighborhood of
z
. This limit is the
Non-Holomorphic Green’s function
.

Remark 4.3
.

5.2.
Auxiliary functions

In this subsection, we will prove some lemmas about some related functions:

Lemma 5.3
.

For
w\in\mathbb{C}\setminus I_{p}
, let

(121)

f(w)=\sqrt{D_{p}(w)}\,.

Then,
f
is continuous on
\mathbb{C}\setminus I_{p}
and analytic on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{I_{p}}

Let

Let

If
w

w_{0}

(122)

\lim_{w\to w_{0}^{+}}f(w)=\text{{sgn}}(\text{{Im}}(w_{0}))i\sqrt{-D_{p}(w_{0})}\,.

If
w
approaches
w_{0}
from the left, then

(123)

\lim_{w\to w_{0}^{-}}f(w)=-\text{{sgn}}(\text{{Im}}(w_{0}))i\sqrt{-D_{p}(w_{0})}\,.

Proof.

Note that
f
is a composition of the principal square root with
D_{p}(w)
.
D_{p}(w)
is well-defined and continuous everywhere, the principal square root is well-defined and continuous except at
\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0)
and analytic on
\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]
. The limit to some

from above is
+\sqrt{-w}i
and the limit from below is
-\sqrt{-w}i
.

Hence,
f
is defined and continuous except when:

(124)

\displaystyle D_{p}(w)=((\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)w+(1-2a))^{2}+4a(1-a)\in(-\infty,0)

\displaystyle\iff((\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)w+(1-2a))^{2}\in(-\infty,-4a(1-a))

\displaystyle\iff(\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)w+(1-2a)\in\left\{iy:\left\lvert y\right\rvert>2\sqrt{a(1-a)}\right\}

\displaystyle\iff(\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)w\in\left\{-(1-2a)+iy:\left\lvert y\right\rvert>2\sqrt{a(1-a)}\right\}

\displaystyle\iff w\in\left\{-\frac{1-2a}{\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha}+iy:\left\lvert y\right\rvert>\frac{2\sqrt{a(1-a)}}{\left\lvert\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha\right\rvert}\right\}

\displaystyle\iff w\in I_{p}\,.

For analyticity,

(125)

D_{p}^{-1}(\{0\})=\left\{-\frac{1-2a}{\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha}\pm i\frac{2\sqrt{a(1-a)}}{\left\lvert\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha\right\rvert}\right\}\,,

so we just need to remove those two points from the domain to make
f
analytic.

For the last points on limits approaching
w_{0}
, it follows from casework considering cases of
\text{{sgn}}(\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)
,
\text{{sgn}}(\text{{Im}}(w_{0}))
, and
w

approaches
w_{0}
from the right.

Then,
(\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)w+(1-2a)
approaches
(\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)w_{0}+(1-2a)\in i\mathbb{R}
from the right. Since
\text{{Im}}((\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)w_{0}+(1-2a))>0
, then
D_{p}(w)
approaches
D_{p}(w_{0})\in(-\infty,0)
from above. Finally, taking square roots,
f(w)
approaches
i\sqrt{-D_{p}(w_{0})}
(from the right).
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

For
w\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(I_{p}\cup\mathbb{R})
, let

(126)

f(w)=\frac{\sqrt{D_{p}(w)}-\sqrt{D_{p}(\overline{w})}}{w-\overline{w}}=\frac{\text{{Im}}\left(\sqrt{D_{p}(w)}\right)}{\text{{Im}}(w)}\,.

The formula for
f
is continuous on
\mathbb{C}\setminus(I_{p}\cup\mathbb{R})

\mathbb{R}
by:

(127)

f(t)=\frac{(\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)((\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)t+(1-2a))}{\sqrt{D_{p}(t)}}\,.

Let
w_{0}\in I_{p}
.

If
w
approaches
w_{0}
from the right, then

(128)

\lim\limits_{w\to w_{0}^{+}}f(w)=\frac{\sqrt{-D_{p}(w_{0})}}{\left\lvert\text{{Im}}(w_{0})\right\rvert}\,.

If
w
approaches
w_{0}
from the left, then

(129)

\lim\limits_{w\to w_{0}^{-}}f(w)=-\frac{\sqrt{-D_{p}(w_{0})}}{\left\lvert\text{{Im}}(w_{0})\right\rvert}\,.

Proof.

From Lemma
5.3
and the fact that
I_{p}
is fixed by complex conjugation,
\sqrt{D_{p}(w)}
,
\sqrt{D_{p}(\overline{w})}
are defined and continuous except on
I_{p}
. Since the denominator vanishes on
\mathbb{R}
, the expression for
f
is valid and continuous on
\mathbb{C}\setminus(I_{p}\cup\mathbb{R})
.

Consider
w\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(I_{p}\cup\mathbb{R})
approaching some
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

As
D_{p}
is a polynomial with real coefficients, from the property of the principal square root,

(130)

\sqrt{D_{p}(\overline{w})}=\sqrt{\overline{D_{p}(w)}}=\overline{\sqrt{D_{p}(w)}}\,.

Hence,

(131)

f(w)=\frac{\text{{Im}}\left(\sqrt{D_{p}(w)}\right)}{\text{{Im}}(w)}\,.

By taking real parts of
\left(\sqrt{D_{p}(w)}\right)^{2}=D_{p}(w)
,

(132)

\displaystyle 2\,\text{{Re}}\left(\sqrt{D_{p}(w)}\right)\text{{Im}}\left(\sqrt{D_{p}(w)}\right)

\displaystyle=\text{{Im}}(D_{p}(w))

\displaystyle=\text{{Im}}(((\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)w+(1-2a))^{2}+4a(1-a))

\displaystyle=\text{{Im}}(((\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)w+(1-2a))^{2})

\displaystyle=2\,\text{{Re}}((\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)w+(1-2a))\,\text{{Im}}((\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)w+(1-2a))

\displaystyle=2((\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)\text{{Re}}(w)+(1-2a))(\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)\text{{Im}}(w)\,.

Dividing both sides by
2\,\text{{Im}}(w)
,

(133)

\text{{Re}}\left(\sqrt{D_{p}(w)}\right)\frac{\text{{Im}}\left(\sqrt{D_{p}(w)}\right)}{\text{{Im}}(w)}=((\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)\text{{Re}}(w)+(1-2a))(\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)\,.

As

,

converges to

since

. Thus, it is possible to divide both sides by

for

. Then,

(134)

f(w)=\frac{(\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)((\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)\text{{Re}}(w)+(1-2a))}{\text{{Re}}\left(\sqrt{D_{p}(w)}\right)}\,.

Finally, taking
w\to t\in\mathbb{R}
,

(135)

\lim\limits_{w\to t}f(w)=\frac{(\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)((\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)t+(1-2a))}{\sqrt{D_{p}(t)}}\,.

This final expression is continuous on
\mathbb{R}
, so
f
may be extended continuously to
\mathbb{R}
by this expression.

For the final limits towards
I_{p}
, using (
131

5.3

(136)

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{w\to w_{0}^{\pm}}f(w)

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{w\to w_{0}^{\pm}}\frac{\text{{Im}}\left(\sqrt{D_{p}(w)}\right)}{\text{{Im}}(w)}

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{\text{{sgn}}(\text{{Im}}(w_{0}))\sqrt{-D_{p}(w_{0})}}{\text{{Im}}(w_{0})}

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{\sqrt{-D_{p}(w_{0})}}{\left\lvert\text{{Im}}(w_{0})\right\rvert}\,.

∎

.

For

if and only if

, in which case letting

Proof.

From Lemma
5.3
and the fact that
I_{p}
is fixed by complex conjugation,
\sqrt{D_{p}(w)}
,
\sqrt{D_{p}(\overline{w})}
are defined and continuous except on
I_{p}
. Since the denominator vanishes on
\mathbb{R}
, the expression for
f
is valid and continuous on
\mathbb{C}\setminus(I_{p}\cup\mathbb{R})
.

Consider
w\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(I_{p}\cup\mathbb{R})
approaching some

As
D_{p}
is a polynomial with real coefficients, from the property of the principal square root,

(141)

\sqrt{D_{p}(\overline{w})}=\sqrt{\overline{D_{p}(w)}}=\overline{\sqrt{D_{p}(w)}}\,.

Hence,

(142)

Expanding the numerator,

\displaystyle\text{{Im}}\left(\overline{w}\sqrt{D_{p}(w)}\right)

\displaystyle=\text{{Im}}(\overline{w})\text{{Re}}\left(\sqrt{D_{p}(w)}\right)+\text{{Re}}(\overline{w})\text{{Im}}\left(\sqrt{D_{p}(w)}\right)

\displaystyle=-\text{{Im}}(w)\text{{Re}}\left(\sqrt{D_{p}(w)}\right)+\text{{Re}}(w)\text{{Im}}\left(\sqrt{D_{p}(w)}\right)\,.

Dividing both sides by

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{w\to t}f(w)

For the limits as

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{w\to w_{0}^{\pm}}f(w)

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{w\to w_{0}^{\pm}}-\text{{Re}}\left(\sqrt{D_{p}(w)}\right)+\lim\limits_{w\to w_{0}^{\pm}}\text{{Re}}(w)\lim\limits_{w\to w_{0}^{\pm}}\frac{\text{{Im}}\left(\sqrt{D_{p}(w)}\right)}{\text{{Im}}(w)}

\displaystyle=0-\frac{1-2a}{\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha}\lim\limits_{w\to w_{0}^{\pm}}\frac{\text{{Im}}\left(\sqrt{D_{p}(w)}\right)}{\text{{Im}}(w)}

Proposition 5.6

For

. Further,

is continuous on

\lim\limits_{w\to 0}\left\lvert B_{p}(w)\right\rvert=\infty\,.

As

Recall that in general,

\infty

w(z-\alpha)(z-\alpha^{\prime})=a(z-\alpha^{\prime})+(1-a)(z-\alpha)\,.

This is true for

and the polynomial equation is not satisfied at

at any

for all

The polynomial equation can be rewritten:

Fixing a

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}}{2}+\frac{1\pm\sqrt{D_{p}(w)}}{2w}\,.

5.3

(159)

6.3.1.
Algorithm

Now, we will state the algorithm that produces a two-variable polynomial whose zero set contains
\Omega_{p,q}
.

First, the system of equations defining
\Omega_{p,q}
, (
260
), can be written as a system of polynomial equations:

(262)

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}(g(x-\alpha_{i})+m)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(a_{i}\prod_{s\neq i}(g(x-\alpha_{s})+m)\right)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{l}(ig(y-\beta_{j})+1-m)-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(b_{j}\prod_{s\neq j}(ig(y-\beta_{s})+1-m)\right)

\displaystyle=0\,.

Next, we recall the resultant of two polynomials and some basic properties:

Definition 6.4
.

Let
A(x)=a_{n}x^{n}+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{0}
and
B(x)=b_{k}x^{k}+b_{k-1}x^{k-1}+\cdots+b_{0}
be one-variable polynomials with coefficients in a commutative ring
R
. The
resultant
of
A
and
B
,
\text{{Res}}(A,B)
, is the determinant of the
(n+k)\times(n+k)

Sylvester matrix
:

(263)

\text{{Res}}(A,B)=\begin{vmatrix}a_{n}&&&&b_{k}&&&\\
a_{n-1}&a_{n}&&&b_{k-1}&b_{k}&&\\
a_{n-2}&a_{n-1}&\ddots&&b_{k-2}&b_{k-1}&\ddots&\\
\vdots&\vdots&&a_{n}&\vdots&\vdots&&b_{k}\\
a_{0}&a_{1}&&\vdots&b_{0}&b_{1}&&\vdots\\
&a_{0}&\ddots&\vdots&&b_{0}&\ddots&\vdots\\
&&\ddots&a_{1}&&&\ddots&b_{1}\\
&&&a_{0}&&&&b_{0}\end{vmatrix}

Suppose that
R
is an integral domain. Then, it makes sense to talk about the roots of
A(x)
and
B(x)
in some algebraically closed field containing
R
.

We state the following well-known result:

Proposition 6.5
.

Let
a_{i},b_{j}\in R
, where
R
is an integral domain. Let
\lambda_{i},\mu_{j}
be the roots of
A(x)=a_{n}x^{n}+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{0}
and
B(x)=b_{k}x^{k}+b_{k-1}x^{k-1}+\cdots+b_{0}
in some algebraically closed field containing
R
, respectively.

(264)

\displaystyle A(x)

\displaystyle=a_{n}(x-\lambda_{1})\cdots(x-\lambda_{n})

\displaystyle B(x)

\displaystyle=b_{k}(x-\mu_{1})\cdots(x-\mu_{k})\,.

Then,

(265)

\text{{Res}}(A,B)=a_{n}^{k}b_{k}^{n}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq n\\
1\leq j\leq k\end{subarray}}(\lambda_{i}-\mu_{j})\,.

A corollary of this result is:

Corollary 6.6
.

Let
a_{i},b_{j}\in R
, where
R
is an integral domain. Let
A(x)=a_{n}x^{n}+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{0}
and
B(x)=b_{k}x^{k}+b_{k-1}x^{k-1}+\cdots+b_{0}
. Then,
A
and
B
have a common root in some algebraically closed field containing
R
if and only if
\text{{Res}}(A,B)=0
.

We can now state the algorithm to produce the two-variable polynomial:

Algorithm 6.7
.

The following algorithm takes
p,q
as in (
257
) and produces a two-variable real polynomial
f(x,y)
:

(1)

Take the resultants of the polynomials in (
262
) with respect to
g
. Let
f_{1}(m,x,y)
be this resultant.

(2)

Divide
f_{1}(m,x,y)
by
m^{n-1}(m-1)^{k-1}
. Let
f_{2}(m,x,y)
be the result of this, so that
f_{2}(m,x,y)m^{n-1}(m-1)^{k-1}=f_{1}(m,x,y)
.

(3)

Take the real and imaginary parts of
f_{2}(m,x,y)
assuming that
m,x,y\in\mathbb{R}
. This produces real polynomials
\text{{Re}}f_{2}(m,x,y)
and
\text{{Im}}f_{2}(m,x,y)
.

(4)

Take the resultant of
\text{{Re}}f_{2}(m,x,y)
and
\text{{Im}}f_{2}(m,x,y)
with respect to
m
. Return this polynomial as
f(x,y)
, a real two-variable polynomial.

This idea of reducing the number of variables in a system of polynomial equations by taking resultants is not new (see
[
17
]
for a description of the technique), but the main issue is that by computing resultants one may introduce too many new solutions in the system. In particular, we wish to avoid the situation that the resultant is the zero polynomial, which gives no information about the solutions of the original system.

The second step of dividing by
m^{n-1}(m-1)^{k-1}
avoids the resultant being the zero polynomial (in general). There is also the detail that
x,y,m
are real variables, but
g
is complex and we start with two complex equations in (
262
). This is handled by taking
\text{{Re}}f_{2}
and
\text{{Im}}f_{2}
in Step 3.

6.3.2.
Proof of correctness for algorithm

Now, we will prove that Algorithm
6.7
produces a polynomial whose zero set contains
\Omega_{p,q}
.

Consider
z_{0}=x_{0}+iy_{0}\in\Omega_{p,q}
where
(x_{0},y_{0},g_{0},m_{0})
solves (
262
). Recall that
x_{0},y_{0},m_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and
g_{0}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(\mathbb{R}\cup i\mathbb{R})
.

In Step 1, substitute
x=x_{0}
,
y=y_{0}
and
m=m_{0}
into the polynomials in (
262
) and treat them as polynomials in
g
. Then, these polynomials both have a root at
g=g_{0}
, so their resultant with respect to
g
must be zero. Hence,
f_{1}(m_{0},x_{0},y_{0})=0
.

In Step 2, we must show that
m^{n-1}(m-1)^{k-1}
divides
f_{1}(m,x,y)
and also that
f_{2}(m_{0},x_{0},y_{0})=0
. Supposing we have proven the first statement, the second statement follows immediately from the fact that
m_{0}\neq 0,1
:

If
m_{0}=0
, then the first equation in (
260
) is
G_{p}(x_{0})=g_{0}
for
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, so then
g_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, a contradiction. Similarly, if
m_{0}=1
, the second equation is
G_{q}(y_{0})=ig_{0}
for
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, so
g_{0}\in i\mathbb{R}
, a contradiction.

The fact that
m^{n-1}(m-1)^{k-1}
divides
f_{1}(m,x,y)
depends on the following Lemma:

Lemma 6.8
.

As elements of
\mathbb{Z}[a_{0},\ldots,a_{n},b_{0},\ldots,b_{k},m]
,
m^{n-1}(m-1)^{k-1}
divides the following determinant:

(266)

\begin{vmatrix}a_{n}&&&b_{k}&&\\
a_{n-1}&\ddots&&b_{k-1}&\ddots&\\
a_{n-2}m&&a_{n}&b_{k-2}(m-1)&&b_{k}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
a_{0}m^{n-1}(m-1)&&\vdots&b_{0}(m-1)^{k-1}m&&\vdots\\
&\ddots&a_{1}m^{n-2}&&\ddots&b_{1}(m-1)^{k-2}\\
&&a_{0}m^{n-1}(m-1)&&&b_{0}(m-1)^{k-1}m\end{vmatrix}

More precisely, this is the determinant of the matrix obtained from the Sylvester matrix for
A(x)=a_{n}x^{n}+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{0}
and
B(x)=b_{k}x^{k}+b_{k-1}x^{k-1}+\cdots+b_{0}
with the following changes:

(1)

Replace all
a_{i}
with
a_{i}m^{n-1-i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n-1
.

(2)

Replace all
b_{i}
with
b_{i}(m-1)^{k-1-i}
for
i=1,\ldots,k-1
.

(3)

Replace all
a_{0}
with
a_{0}m^{n-1}(m-1)
.

(4)

Replace all
b_{0}
with
b_{0}(m-1)^{k-1}m
.

Proof.

As the polynomial ring is a unique factorization domain and
m
,
m-1
are primes, it suffices to check that
m^{n-1}
and
(m-1)^{k-1}
each individually divide the determinant.

We will just prove that
m^{n-1}
divides the determinant, as the other case can be obtained from switching the roles of
A
and
B
and using
m^{\prime}=1-m
instead of
m

Consider the terms that come from evaluating the determinant using the Leibniz formula. To show that
m^{n-1}
divides the determinant, it suffices to check that
m^{n-1}
divides any term that is a product of some non-zero entries of the matrix.

To simplify matters, consider only the power of
m
in each coordinate, i.e. it suffices to check that
m^{n-1}
divides the non-zero terms of the Leibniz formula in the following determinant:

(267)

\begin{vmatrix}1&&&1&&&\\
1&\ddots&&1&\ddots&&\\
m&&1&1&&1&\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&1\\
\vdots&&\vdots&1&&\vdots&\vdots\\
m^{n-2}&&\vdots&m&&1&\vdots\\
m^{n-1}&&m^{n-3}&&\ddots&1&1\\
&\ddots&m^{n-2}&&&m&1\\
&&m^{n-1}&&&&m\end{vmatrix}

The proof follows by induction on
n
(with
k
fixed). The base case
n=1
is trivial as
m^{n-1}=1
. For the inductive step, suppose that the claim has been verified for
n-1
and consider the statement for
n
. Any term in the Leibniz formula where one of the
m^{n-1}
terms is part of the product is clearly divisible by
m^{n-1}
.

Hence, it suffices to consider only those terms in the Leibniz formula that are a product of matrix entries that are not
m^{n-1}
. This corresponds to changing all instances of
m^{n-1}
to
0
and looking for terms in the Leibniz formula that are products of non-zero entries of this new matrix:

(268)

\begin{vmatrix}1&&&1&&&\\
1&\ddots&&1&\ddots&&\\
m&&1&1&&1&\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&1\\
\vdots&&\vdots&1&&\vdots&\vdots\\
m^{-2}&&\vdots&m&&1&\vdots\\
0&&m^{n-3}&&\ddots&1&1\\
&\ddots&m^{n-2}&&&m&1\\
&&0&&&&m\end{vmatrix}

In this new matrix, there is only one non-zero entry in the last row, the lower right
m
. Hence, all non-zero terms in the Leibniz formula for the new matrix are equal to
m
multiplied with a non-zero term in the Leibniz formula for the
(n+k-1)\times(n+k-1)
minor of the first
n+k-1
rows and columns:

(269)

\begin{vmatrix}1&&&1&&\\
1&\ddots&&1&\ddots&\\
m&&1&1&&1\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\vdots&1&&\vdots\\
m^{n-2}&&\vdots&m&&1\\
&\ddots&m^{n-3}&&\ddots&1\\
&&m^{n-2}&&&m\\
\end{vmatrix}

This
(n+k-1)\times(n+k-1)
minor is just the original matrix in (
267
) but with
n-1
instead of
n
. Hence, from induction, all non-zero terms in the Leibniz formula for this matrix are divisible by
m^{n-2}
. Once this is multiplied by the lower right
m
, then all of these terms are divisible by
m^{n-1}
, as desired.
∎

Now, we can prove that
m^{n-1}(m-1)^{k-1}
divides
f_{1}(m,x,y)
, which completes the verification of Step 2 of the algorithm:

Proposition 6.9
.

Let
f_{1}(m,x,y)
be the resultant of the polynomials in (
262
) with respect to
g
. Then,
m^{n-1}(m-1)^{k-1}
divides
f_{1}(m,x,y)
in
\mathbb{C}[m,x,y]
.

Proof.

Consider the two polynomials in (
262
):
For the first polynomial, the first term is homogeneous in
g
and
m
with degree
n
and the second term is homogeneous in
g
and
m
with degree
n-1
. Similarly, for the second polynomial, the first term is homogeneous in
g
and
1-m
with degree
k
and the second term is homogeneous in
g
and
1-m
with degree
k-1
. Thus, by expanding the products in
g
and factoring out
m
and
m-1
respectively, these polynomials can be written respectively as:

(270)

\displaystyle p_{n}(x,m)g^{n}+p_{n-1}(x,m)g^{n-1}+p_{n-2}(x,m)mg^{n-2}+

\displaystyle+p_{n-3}(x,m)m^{2}g^{n-3}\cdots+p_{0}(x,m)m^{n-1}

\displaystyle q_{k}(y,m)g^{k}+q_{k-1}(y,m)g^{k-1}\qquad+q_{k-2}(y,m)(1-m)g^{k-2}+

\displaystyle+q_{k-3}(x,m)(1-m)^{2}g^{k-3}\cdots+q_{0}(y,m)(1-m)^{k-1}.

for some complex polynomials
p_{i}(x,m),q_{i}(y,m)
.

Using that
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}=b_{1}+\cdots+b_{k}=1
, then
p_{0}(x,m)=m-1
and
q_{0}(y,m)=-m
.

The resultant of these polynomials with respect to
g
is exactly the determinant in Lemma
6.8
with
a_{0}=1
,
b_{0}=(-1)^{k}
,
a_{i}=p_{i}(x,m)
for
i=1,\ldots,n
and
b_{i}=q_{i}(y,m)
for
i=1,\ldots,k
. Hence,
m^{n-1}(m-1)^{k-1}
divides
f_{1}(m,x,y)
in
\mathbb{C}[m,x,y]
.
∎

Returning to the verification of the algorithm, consider Step 3.
f_{2}(m_{0},x_{0},y_{0})=0
is equivalent to
\text{{Re}}f_{2}(m_{0},x_{0},y_{0})=\text{{Im}}f_{2}(m_{0},x_{0},y_{0})=0
.

Treating
\text{{Re}}f_{2}(m,x_{0},y_{0})
and
\text{{Im}}f_{2}(m,x_{0},y_{0})
as polynomials in
m
, then these polynomials have a common root at

. Hence, the resultant of these two polynomials vanishes in
m
vanishes at
(x_{0},y_{0})
. This completes the proof of the correctness of the algorithm.

6.3.3.
A specific case

In order to prove Theorem
6.3
for the generic case, we first apply the algorithm in the specific case where
\bm{\alpha}=(0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\bm{\beta}=(0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. We will see that in this case, the algorithm produces a non-zero polynomial:

Proposition 6.10
.

The algorithm for any
\bm{a},\bm{b}
and
\bm{\alpha}=\bm{0}
,
\bm{\beta}=\bm{0}
produces a non-zero polynomial.

Proof.

For any
\bm{a}
and
\bm{b}
, (
262
) is:

(271)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=(gx+m)^{n}-(gx+m)^{n-1}=(gx+m)^{n-1}(gx+m-1)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=(igy+1-m)^{k}-(igy+1-m)^{k-1}=(igy+1-m)^{k-1}(igy-m)\,.

Step 1 of the algorithm is taking the resultant of the polynomials in (
271
) with respect to
g
. From continuity, it suffices to take the resultant when
x\neq 0
and
y\neq 0
. In this situation, we can factor the polynomials:

(272)

\displaystyle(gx+m)^{n-1}(gx+m-1)

\displaystyle=x^{n}\left(g+\frac{m}{x}\right)^{n-1}\left(g+\frac{m-1}{x}\right)

\displaystyle(igy+1-m)^{k-1}(igy-m)

\displaystyle=(iy)^{k}\left(g-\frac{i(1-m)}{y}\right)^{k-1}\left(g+\frac{im}{y}\right)\,.

From Proposition
6.5
,

(273)

\displaystyle f_{1}(m,x,y)

\displaystyle=x^{nk}(iy)^{nk}\left(\frac{m}{x}+\frac{i(1-m)}{y}\right)^{(n-1)(k-1)}\left(\frac{m}{x}-\frac{im}{y}\right)^{n-1}\times

\displaystyle\left(\frac{m-1}{x}+\frac{i(1-m)}{y}\right)^{k-1}\left(\frac{m-1}{x}-\frac{im}{y}\right)

\displaystyle=(imy+(m-1)x)^{(n-1)(k-1)}(imy+mx)^{n-1}\times

\displaystyle(i(m-1)y+(m-1)x)^{k-1}(i(m-1)y+mx)

\displaystyle=m^{n-1}(m-1)^{k-1}(imy+(m-1)x)^{(n-1)(k-1)}(x+iy)^{n-1}\times

\displaystyle(x+iy)^{k-1}(i(m-1)y+mx)

\displaystyle=m^{n-1}(m-1)^{k-1}(x+iy)^{n+k-2}\times

\displaystyle((m-1)x+imy)^{(n-1)(k-1)}(mx+i(m-1)y)\,.

In Step 2 of the algorithm, we divide
f_{1}(m,x,y)
by
m^{n-1}(m-1)^{k-1}
. This is easy to do from the final expression for

:

(274)

f_{2}(m,x,y)=(x+iy)^{n+k-2}((m-1)x+imy)^{(n-1)(k-1)}(mx+i(m-1)y)\,.

In Step 3 of the algorithm, we compute
\text{{Re}}f_{2}(m,x,y)
and
\text{{Im}}f_{2}(m,x,y)
assuming

. In Step 4, we compute the resultant of these two real polynomials in
m
, resulting in a polynomial in
x
and
y
. We will not compute these explicitly, but just argue that the result of the algorithm is a non-zero polynomial.

First, consider a general

:

(275)

f_{2}(m,x,y)=\sum_{j,k,l}c_{j,k,l}m^{j}x^{k}y^{l}\,,\qquad c_{j,kl}\in\mathbb{C}\,.

The result of applying Step 3 of the algorithm to
f_{2}(m,x,y)
is:

(276)

\displaystyle\text{{Re}}f_{2}(m,x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k,l}\text{{Re}}(c_{j,k,l})m^{j}x^{k}y^{l}

\displaystyle\text{{Im}}f_{2}(m,x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k,l}\text{{Im}}(c_{j,k,l})m^{j}x^{k}y^{l}\,.

Thus, the following equalities hold as polynomials in
\mathbb{C}[m,x,y]
:

(277)

\displaystyle f_{2}(m,x,y)

\displaystyle=\text{{Re}}f_{2}(m,x,y)+i\,\text{{Im}}f_{2}(m,x,y)

\displaystyle\overline{f_{2}}(m,x,y)

\displaystyle=\text{{Re}}f_{2}(m,x,y)-i\,\text{{Im}}f_{2}(m,x,y)\,,

where
\overline{f_{2}}(m,x,y)\in\mathbb{C}[m,x,y]
is computed assuming that
m,x,y\in\mathbb{R}
.

In Step 4, we compute the resultant of
\text{{Re}}f_{2}(m,x,y)
and
\text{{Im}}f_{2}(m,x,y)
with respect to
m
and this is the result of the algorithm. This resultant vanishes at
(x_{0},y_{0})
if and only if there is some
m_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\text{{Re}}f_{2}(m_{0},x_{0},y_{0})=\text{{Im}}f_{2}(m_{0},x_{0},y_{0})=0
. This happens if and only if there is some

such that
f_{2}(m_{0},x_{0},y_{0})=\overline{f_{2}}(m,x_{0},y_{0})=0
. Hence, it suffices to show there exists
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathbb{R}
where there does not exist

such that:

(278)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=f_{2}(m,x_{0},y_{0})

\displaystyle=(x_{0}+iy_{0})^{n+k-2}((m-1)x_{0}+imy_{0})^{(n-1)(k-1)}(x_{0}m+i(m-1)y_{0})

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\overline{f_{2}}(m,x_{0},y_{0})

\displaystyle=(x_{0}-iy_{0})^{n+k-2}((m-1)x_{0}-imy_{0})^{(n-1)(k-1)}(x_{0}m-i(m-1)y_{0})\,.

As
f_{2}
and

are factored, it is easy to see that

(279)

\displaystyle f_{2}(m,x_{0},y_{0})

\displaystyle=0\;\iff\;x_{0}=y_{0}=0\quad\text{or}\quad m=\frac{x_{0}}{x_{0}+iy_{0}}\quad\text{or}\quad m=\frac{iy_{0}}{x_{0}+iy_{0}}

\displaystyle\overline{f_{2}}(m,x_{0},y_{0})

\displaystyle=0\;\iff\;x_{0}=y_{0}=0\quad\text{or}\quad m=\frac{x_{0}}{x_{0}-iy_{0}}\quad\text{or}\quad m=\frac{-iy_{0}}{x_{0}-iy_{0}}\,.

Consider

. Then,

(280)

\displaystyle\frac{x_{0}}{x_{0}+iy_{0}}

\displaystyle=\cos\theta e^{-i\theta}\,,\qquad\frac{iy_{0}}{x_{0}+iy_{0}}=i\sin\theta e^{-i\theta}=\sin\theta e^{i(\pi/2-\theta)}

\displaystyle\frac{x_{0}}{x_{0}-iy_{0}}

\displaystyle=\cos\theta e^{i\theta}\,,\qquad\frac{-iy_{0}}{x_{0}-iy_{0}}=-i\sin\theta e^{i\theta}=\sin\theta e^{i(\theta-\pi/2)}\,.

The roots of

∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem

It suffices to show that for any

For the first case, we have the following result:

In particular, this implies that

Proof.

Since

. Since

also. Let

Applying the addition law (

(317)

Hence,

(318)

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=B_{X}(g)

\displaystyle=B_{p}(g)+iB_{q}(ig)-\frac{1}{g}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}}{2}+\frac{1+\sqrt{(\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)^{2}g^{2}+1}}{2g}\right)+

\displaystyle i\left(\frac{\beta+\beta^{\prime}}{2}+\frac{1+\sqrt{(\beta^{\prime}-\beta)^{2}(ig)^{2}+1}}{2ig}\right)-\frac{1}{g}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}}{2}+i\frac{\beta+\beta^{\prime}}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{(\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)^{2}g^{2}+1}}{2g}+\frac{\sqrt{1-(\beta^{\prime}-\beta)^{2}g^{2}}}{2g}\,.

We proceed to show that if

Let

(319)

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}

for some

For

\left\lvert\frac{1+\sqrt{\mathscr{A}^{2}g^{2}+1}\sqrt{1-\mathscr{B}^{2}g^{2}}}{2g^{2}}\right\rvert\leq\frac{\mathscr{A}\mathscr{B}}{2}\,.

Squaring both sides and rearranging,

0=g^{2}((4\eta^{2}-\mathscr{A}^{2}\mathscr{B}^{2})g^{2}-(4\eta+(\mathscr{A}^{2}-\mathscr{B}^{2})))\,.

As

All steps were reversible except for the step where both sides were squared. We proceed to determine which values of
\eta

\eta
, (

Computation shows that the relevant quantities in (

\displaystyle 1-\mathscr{B}^{2}g^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\mathscr{B}^{2}-2\eta)^{2}}{4\eta^{2}+\mathscr{A}^{2}\mathscr{B}^{2}}\,.

To take the square root of these quantities, consider the following cases for

(1)

\left\lvert\eta\right\rvert<\mathscr{A}\mathscr{B}/2

(2)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathscr{A}^{2}g^{2}+1}

Hence,

(331)

\frac{1+\sqrt{\mathscr{A}^{2}g^{2}+1}\sqrt{1-\mathscr{B}^{2}g^{2}}}{2g^{2}}=\frac{\mathscr{A}^{2}\mathscr{B}^{2}+(\mathscr{B}^{2}-\mathscr{A}^{2})\eta}{4\eta+(\mathscr{A}^{2}-\mathscr{B}^{2})}\,.

\frac{\mathscr{A}^{2}\mathscr{B}^{2}+(\mathscr{B}^{2}-\mathscr{A}^{2})\eta}{4\eta+(\mathscr{A}^{2}-\mathscr{B}^{2})}=\frac{\mathscr{A}^{2}\mathscr{B}^{2}}{4\eta}\,.

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathscr{A}^{2}g^{2}+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{-i(\mathscr{A}^{2}+2\eta)}{\sqrt{-4\eta^{2}-\mathscr{A}^{2}\mathscr{B}^{2}}}

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-\mathscr{B}^{2}g^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{i(\mathscr{B}^{2}-2\eta)}{\sqrt{-4\eta^{2}-\mathscr{A}^{2}\mathscr{B}^{2}}}\,.

When
\text{{Im}}(\eta)<-\mathscr{A}\mathscr{B}/2

is in the fourth quadrant, and

is in the first quadrant, so

(335)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathscr{A}^{2}g^{2}+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{i(\mathscr{A}^{2}+2\eta)}{\sqrt{-4\eta^{2}-\mathscr{A}^{2}\mathscr{B}^{2}}}

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-\mathscr{B}^{2}g^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{-i(\mathscr{B}^{2}-2\eta)}{\sqrt{-4\eta^{2}-\mathscr{A}^{2}\mathscr{B}^{2}}}\,.

From Corollary

(356)

\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}iB_{q}(\zeta_{k}i)=iB_{q}(gi)=i\frac{\beta+\beta^{\prime}}{2}+\frac{1+\sqrt{D_{q}(ig)}}{2g}\,.

Hence,

(357)

z=\frac{\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}}{2}+i\frac{\beta+\beta^{\prime}}{2}\pm i\frac{\sqrt{-D_{p}(g)}}{2g}+\frac{\sqrt{D_{q}(ig)}}{2g}\,.

From Lemma
2.5
,
z
is on the hyperbola if and only if:

(358)

\text{{Re}}\left(\left(z-\frac{\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}}{2}-i\frac{\beta+\beta^{\prime}}{2}\right)^{2}-\frac{(\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)^{2}-(\beta^{\prime}-\beta)^{2}}{4}\right)=0\,.

Substituting the expression for
z
into this and simplifying,
z
is on the hyperbola if and only if:

(359)

\text{{Re}}\left(\frac{1\pm i\sqrt{-D_{p}(g)}\sqrt{D_{q}(ig)}}{2g^{2}}\right)=0\,.

Since
g\in I_{p}
, then
\sqrt{-D_{p}(g)},\sqrt{D_{q}(ig)},g^{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, so the real part of the previous equation is
1/(2g^{2})
, which is non-zero. Hence,
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q_{k})
does not converge to a point on the hyperbola.
∎

Corollary 7.11
.

Let
z\in\{\alpha+i\beta,\alpha^{\prime}+i\beta,\alpha+i\beta^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}+i\beta^{\prime}\}
and suppose that
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(z_{\epsilon})
is in the domain of
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}
for sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
. Then,

(360)

\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\left\lvert\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(z_{\epsilon})\right\rvert=\infty\,.

Proof.

The proof is based on the results in the previous subsections and noticing that only in the situation where
z\in\{\alpha+i\beta,\alpha^{\prime}+i\beta,\alpha+i\beta^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}+i\beta^{\prime}\}
is possible is when
\left\lvert Q_{k}\right\rvert\to\infty
and
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q_{k})
converges.

Recall that
Q_{\epsilon}=\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(z_{\epsilon})
. It suffices to show that for every sequence
\epsilon_{k}\to 0^{+}
,
\left\lvert Q_{\epsilon_{k}}\right\rvert\to\infty
.

Suppose for the sake of contradiction that

is bounded for some
\epsilon_{k}\to 0^{+}

If

, then

(361)

z=\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}z_{\epsilon_{k}}=\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q_{\epsilon_{k}})\,,

but from Theorem

, the final limit diverges.

, then we may pass to a subsequence and assume that

. Then, from the results in the previous subsections,

, a contradiction.

Thus, it must be the case that

(362)

\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\left\lvert\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(z_{\epsilon})\right\rvert=\infty\,.

∎

We can verify the domain condition in Corollary
7.11
to get the following concrete result:

Proposition 7.12
.

Let
z\in\{\alpha+i\beta,\alpha^{\prime}+i\beta,\alpha+i\beta^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}+i\beta^{\prime}\}
. Then,

(363)

\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\left\lvert\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(z_{\epsilon})\right\rvert=\infty\,.

Proof.

Let
X_{z}=z-X
. Computation using Definition
2.16
shows that:

(364)

\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(z_{\epsilon})=\begin{pmatrix}\tau[((X_{z})^{*}X_{z}+\epsilon^{2})^{-1}(X_{z})^{*}]&-i\epsilon\tau[((X_{z})^{*}X_{z}+\epsilon^{2})^{-1}]\\
i\epsilon\tau[((X_{z})^{*}X_{z}+\epsilon^{2})^{-1}]&\tau[X_{z}((X_{z})^{*}X_{z}+\epsilon^{2})^{-1}]\end{pmatrix}.

In light of Corollary
7.11
and the domain of
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}
described in Theorem
5.13
, it suffices to show that for
z\in\{\alpha+i\beta,\alpha^{\prime}+i\beta,\alpha+i\beta^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}+i\beta^{\prime}\}
and
\epsilon>0
,
\tau[((X_{z})^{*}X_{z}+\epsilon^{2})^{-1}(X_{z})^{*}]\not\in\mathbb{R}\cup i\mathbb{R}
.

We will just show that
\tau[((X_{z})^{*}X_{z}+\epsilon^{2})^{-1}(X_{z})^{*}]\not\in i\mathbb{R}
, the other case is similar. For this, let
X_{z}=p+iq
, where
p,q
are Hermitian, freely independent, and have
2
atoms. For
z\in\{\alpha+i\beta,\alpha^{\prime}+i\beta,\alpha+i\beta^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}+i\beta^{\prime}\}
,
p
and
q
both have
0
as an atom. Hence,
p
and
q
are either positive or negative operators. At this point, we also drop the subscript
z
so that
X=p+iq
.

Assume for the sake of contradiction that
\tau[(X^{*}X+\epsilon^{2})^{-1}X^{*}]\in i\mathbb{R}
. This is equivalent to

(365)

0=\text{{Re}}\,\tau[(X^{*}X+\epsilon^{2})^{-1}X^{*}]=\tau[(X^{*}X+\epsilon^{2})^{-1}p]\,.

Since
p\geq 0
or
p\leq 0
, without loss of generality assume that
p\geq 0
. Then,

(366)

\displaystyle\tau[((X^{*}X+\epsilon^{2})^{-1/2}p^{1/2})^{*}(X^{*}X+\epsilon^{2})^{-1/2}p^{1/2}]=\tau[(X^{*}X+\epsilon^{2})^{-1}p]=0\,.

Since
\tau
is faithful, then
(X^{*}X+\epsilon^{2})^{-1/2}p^{1/2}=0
. Hence,

(367)

p=(X^{*}X+\epsilon^{2})^{1/2}[(X^{*}X+\epsilon^{2})^{-1/2}p^{1/2}]p^{1/2}=0\,.

But, this is impossible, as
p
has
2
atoms. Hence,
\tau[(X^{*}X+\epsilon^{2})^{-1}X^{*}]\not\in i\mathbb{R}
.
∎

We prove one final Proposition before the proof of Theorem
7.1
:

Proposition 7.13
.

Let
z\in\mathbb{C}
and suppose there exists a sequence
\epsilon_{k}\to 0^{+}
, such that
Q_{\epsilon_{k}}\to Q\neq 0
, where
Q\in\mathbb{C}
. Then,

(368)

\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}}(z_{\epsilon})=\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}Q_{\epsilon}=Q\,.

In particular, when
G_{X}
is continuous at
z
,
Q=G_{X}(z)
.

Proof.

If there is a sequence
Q_{\epsilon_{k}}\to Q\neq 0
where
Q\in\mathbb{C}
, then
B_{\epsilon_{k}}\to 0
. We proceed to upgrade the convergence
B_{\epsilon_{k}}\to 0
along a specific sequence to
B_{\epsilon}\to 0
as
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
: If
B_{\epsilon}\not\to 0
, then from (
107
), on some sequence

. From
B_{\epsilon_{k}}\to 0
and Propositions
7.7
and
7.10
,
z
does not lie on the intersection of the hyperbola and rectangle. But, from
l_{\epsilon_{k}^{\prime}}\to 0
and Propositions
7.4
and
7.6
,
z
does lie on the intersection of the hyperbola and rectangle. This is a contradiction, so we conclude that
B_{\epsilon}\to 0
.

Next, consider an arbitrary sequence
\epsilon_{k}^{\prime}\to 0
such that
Q_{\epsilon_{k}^{\prime}}\to Q^{\prime}\neq 0
, where
Q^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}
. To prove the limit in the statement of the Proposition, it suffices to show that
Q^{\prime}=Q
.

Corollary
7.9
gives two sequences
\zeta_{\epsilon_{k}},\zeta_{\epsilon_{k}^{\prime}}\subset\mathbb{C}
such that
\zeta_{\epsilon_{k}}\to Q
,
\zeta_{\epsilon_{k}^{\prime}}\to Q^{\prime}
, and:

(369)

\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}B_{X}(\zeta_{\epsilon_{k}})=\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}B_{X}(Q_{\epsilon_{k}})=z=\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}B_{X}(Q_{\epsilon_{k}^{\prime}})=\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}B_{X}(\zeta_{\epsilon_{k}^{\prime}})\,.

We consider the cases when
B_{X}
is continuous or discontinuous at
Q
and
Q^{\prime}
:

If
B_{X}
is continuous at both
Q
and
Q^{\prime}
, then since

is invertible on its domain, it is injective. Hence,

(370)

implies that
Q=Q^{\prime}
.

If
B_{X}
is continuous at exactly one of
Q
and
Q^{\prime}
, assume
B_{X}
is continuous at
Q
but not
Q^{\prime}
. From Proposition
5.11
,

is analytic in a neighborhood of

open where

,

. Since

, but
\zeta_{\epsilon_{k}^{\prime}}\not\in U
. This contradicts the injectivity of

.

Finally, consider if
B_{X}
is discontinuous at both
Q
and
Q^{\prime}
, i.e.

or

, and

or

.

From the proof of Proposition
7.10
, the possible limits for

are:

(371)

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(Q_{\epsilon_{k}})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}}{2}+i\frac{\beta+\beta^{\prime}}{2}\pm i\frac{\sqrt{-D_{p}(g)}}{2g}+\frac{\sqrt{D_{q}(ig)}}{2g}&g\in I_{p}\\

(372)

\displaystyle\tilde{z}

\displaystyle=\left(z-\frac{\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}}{2}-i\frac{\beta+\beta^{\prime}}{2}\right)^{2}-\frac{(\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)^{2}-(\beta^{\prime}-\beta)^{2}}{4}

The last point follows from Corollary

∎
