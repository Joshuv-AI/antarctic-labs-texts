Extended mean–field theories for networks of real neurons
Luca Di Carlo, a Francesca Mignacco, a,b Christopher W. Lynn, a,b,c and William Bialek a,b

Abstract
If the behavior of a system with many degrees of freedom can be captured by a small number of collective variables, then plausibly there is an underlying mean–field theory. We show that simple versions of this idea fail to describe the patterns of activity in networks of real neurons. An extended mean–field theory that matches the distribution of collective variables is at least consistent, though shows signs that these networks are poised near a critical point, in agreement with other observations. These results suggest a path to analysis of emerging data on ever larger numbers of neurons.



Throughout much of the brain, the electrical activity of single neurons is effectively binary, and thus equivalent to Ising spins. When we look at many neurons
we would like to know the distribution out of which spin configurations are drawn. There is a long history of using ideas from statistical physics to approach this problem
[
1
]
.

Important landmarks in the statistical physics approach to neural networks are the Hopfield model
[
2
]
and the Boltzmann machine
[
3
]
. In the Hopfield model the dynamics have a Lyapunov or energy function, and local energy minima are stable configurations that we can identify as stored memories or as the results of computations
[
4
,
5
]
. In the Boltzmann machine the states of “visible” neurons represent signals in the outside world, “hidden” neurons represent inferred causes or features of these variables, and the distribution over network states is meant to simulate the distribution over signals. Both models can be described by pairwise interactions among the Ising spins, with learning rules that adjust these interactions to reach the desired behaviors.

There is a path that leads directly from experimental data to pairwise Ising models that are like the Hopfield model or Boltzmann machine
[
1
,
6
]
. Concretely, we write the state of each neuron as
s_{n}
, with
s_{n}=+1
when the cell is active and
s_{n}=-1
when the cell is silent; the state of the network is
\bm{s}\equiv\{s_{1},\,s_{2},\,\cdots,\,s_{N}\}
. We are looking for the probability distribution
P(\bm{s})
and insist that it match the observed means and correlations,

\displaystyle\langle s_{n}\rangle_{P}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle s_{n}\rangle_{\rm exp}

(1)

\displaystyle\langle s_{n}s_{m}\rangle_{P}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle s_{n}s_{m}\rangle_{\rm exp},

(2)

where
\langle\cdots\rangle_{P}
denotes an average over the distribution
P
and
\langle\cdots\rangle_{\rm exp}
denotes an average over the data. There are infinitely many distributions that satisfy these constraints, but among these one is special because it generates states that are as random as possible and hence introduces no knowledge or structure beyond that required to match the expectation values in Eqs (
1
,
2
); this is the maximum entropy distribution
[
7
,
8
]

\displaystyle P_{\rm pairs}(\bm{s})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{\rm pairs}}\exp\left[-E_{\rm pairs}(\bm{s})\right],

(3)

\displaystyle Z_{\rm pairs}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{s}}\exp\left[-E_{\rm pairs}(\bm{s})\right],

(4)

\displaystyle E_{\rm pairs}(\bm{s})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{n}h_{n}s_{n}-{1\over 2}\sum_{n,m=1}^{N}s_{n}J_{nm}s_{m}.

(5)

We recognize this as the Boltzmann distribution for a particular Ising spin glass in which the fields
\{h_{n}\}
and couplings
\{J_{nm}\}
are determined by the data through Eqs (
1
,
2
); once we solve these equations all further predictions are parameter free. These quantitative predictions have been very successful
in several different systems
[
1
]
.

The pairwise maximum entropy model begins by taking
\sim N^{2}
expectation values from the data. But current experimental methods allow for dramatic increases in
N
without proportional increases in the duration
T
of experiments; eventually the measured matrix of pairwise correlations fails to be of full rank, limited by
T
and not
N
. It is acceptable to ask for experimental estimates of
M\propto N
expectation values, and then construct maximum entropy models that are consistent with these measurements. As an example, in an Ising model with local interactions we can recover the correct Boltzmann distribution by asking for the maximum entropy model consistent with near-neighbor correlations, and there are
{\cal O}(N)
of these even without translation invariance. This reconstruction from local correlations also works in flocks of birds
[
9
,
10
]
. How do we choose
{\cal O}(N)
constraints in the absence of guidance from symmetries or locality?
The literature on patterns of activity in populations of neurons offers several intuitions.

Population activity.
It has been suggested that collective effects in networks of neurons can be captured by keeping track of the summed activity
[
11
,
12
]
,

\phi=\sum_{n=1}^{N}s_{n}.

(6)

The maximum entropy model that is consistent with the mean and variance of
\phi
again has the form of a Boltzmann distribution, now with the energy function

E_{\rm pop}(\bm{s})=-h\phi-\frac{\lambda}{2N}\phi^{2},

(7)

which we recognize as a mean-field ferromagnet
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
. We recall that in solving this model we rewrite it (exactly) as spins interacting with an auxiliary field. This connects to the intuition that the high dimensional dynamics of a network is controlled by a small number of “latent fields” to which the cells are responding independently
[
16
,
17
]
; here this latent field is an emergent property rather than externally imposed. At large
N
fluctuations in the latent field are suppressed, and we refer to this as the naive mean-field approximation.

Means and projections.
Focusing on the summed activity we lose the identities of the neurons. An alternative is to ask for a model that matches the mean activity of each individual cell, but tries to capture collective effects by looking only along one or a few projections

\varphi_{\alpha}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}W_{\alpha n}s_{n}.

(8)

The maximum entropy model that matches all the mean activities
\langle s_{n}\rangle
and the covariance matrix
\langle\varphi_{\alpha}\varphi_{\beta}\rangle
is a Boltzmann distribution with energy

E_{\rm proj}(\bm{s})=-\sum_{n=1}^{N}h_{n}s_{n}-{1\over{2N}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{K}\varphi_{\alpha}\Lambda_{\alpha\beta}\varphi_{\beta},

(9)

where we keep track of
K
projections. This connects to the intuition that the high dimensional dynamics of a network are dominated by a low dimensional manifold
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
. Solving the model again introduces auxiliary fields, one conjugate to each projection
\varphi_{\alpha}
, so this is a (slightly) generalized mean-field theory
[
21
]
. For
K\ll N
fluctuations in these fields are suppressed at large
N
, which again we refer to as naive mean-field. If
K\propto N
there should be a more sophisticated mean-field theory analogous to the Hopfield network near saturation
[
22
]
.

Distributions of projections.
The emphasis on the variance of activity along particular projections may be too limiting. An alternative is to match the mean activity of each neuron and the distribution of activity along a limited set of directions in the high dimensional space of neural activity. For simplicity we consider here the case of just one projection
\varphi
. The maximum entropy model now is a Boltzmann distribution with energy

E_{\rm dist}(\bm{s})=-\sum_{n=1}^{N}h_{n}s_{n}+NU(\varphi).

(10)

As usual the fields
\{h_{n}\}
are adjusted to match the measured mean activity of each neuron and the effective potential
U(\varphi)
is adjusted to match the observed distribution
P_{\rm exp}(\varphi)
; the factor
N
keeps the potential of order one. This is equivalent to a model with a single latent field acting on all the neurons, with an arbitrary distribution. It also connects to models for dense associative memory, or “modern Hopfield” networks
[
23
]
. Our major result is the construction of a generalized mean-field theory for this class of models and the demonstration that real networks are in the regime where this approximation is valid. In contrast, we will see that simpler mean-field theories fail in their naive form, and if generalized they provide a bad description of higher order structure in the data. A fuller account will be given elsewhere
[
24
]
.

Calculating the partition function of the population activity model [Eq (
7
)] is a standard exercise
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
. The partition function can be rewritten exactly as

\displaystyle Z_{\rm pop}(h,\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{N\over{2\pi\lambda}}2^{N}\int d\psi\,e^{-Nf(\psi)},

(11)

\displaystyle f(\psi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\lambda}\psi^{2}-\ln\cosh(h+\psi).

(12)

At large
N
the integral in Eq (
11
) should be dominated by the the auxiliary field
\psi=\psi_{*}
that minimizes the free energy
f(\psi)
, and this leads to the naive mean-field approximation

\ln Z(h,\lambda)=-Nf(\psi_{*})+N\ln 2-\frac{1}{2}\ln[2\pi\lambda f^{\prime\prime}(\psi_{*})]+\cdots,

(13)

where
\cdots
are terms
\sim 1/N
. In this approximation

\displaystyle\langle\phi\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\mu=\tanh(h+\lambda\mu)+\mathcal{O}\left(N^{-1}\right),

(14)

\displaystyle{1\over N}\langle(\delta\phi)^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\chi=\frac{1-\mu^{2}}{1-\lambda(1-\mu^{2})}+\mathcal{O}\left(N^{-1}\right).

(15)

These self–consistent equations can be combined to show that there is a maximum
\chi
at fixed
\mu
,

\chi_{\mathrm{max}}(\mu)=\frac{\mu(1-\mu^{2})}{\mu-{\mathrm{atanh}(\mu)}(1-\mu^{2})}\ .

(16)

In Figure
1
we show
\chi
vs
\mu
computed from data on several systems:

N=160
neurons at the output of the vertebrate retina, the cells that send information from eye to brain
[
25
]
; groups of
N\sim 60-190
neurons in each of several regions of the mouse brain, or
N\sim 900-1500
neurons across multiple regions
[
26
]
; and
N\sim 1500
neurons in the mouse hippocampus
[
27
,
28
]
. Measured values of
\chi
consistently exceed the bound from
Eq (
16
).

The conclusion from Fig
1
is that real neurons are not consistent with naive mean-field theory. But what happens to the maximum entropy problem? That is, can we still find values of
h
and
\lambda
in Eq (
7
) such that the predicted
\mu
and
\chi
agree with measured values? The answer is yes, but it must be done very carefully
[
24
]
. We can use the exact integral representation of
Z_{\rm pop}
in Eq (
11
) to construct, numerically, a trajectory
h_{*}(\lambda)
along which the predicted magnetization
\mu=\mu_{\rm exp}
(Fig
2
, inset). This trajectory begins at
h_{*}(\lambda=0)=\mathrm{atanh}(\mu_{\rm exp})
and
h_{*}
moves toward
h_{*}=0
as
\lambda
increases. The trajectory stalls, with
dh_{*}/d\lambda
becoming almost zero at a critical
\lambda
, at which point the susceptibility
\chi
rises rapidly; in the examples from Fig
1
,
\chi_{\rm exp}
intersects this steep rise, so that
\lambda
is determined very precisely by the data.

Having found
h
and
\lambda
to match the mean and variance of the population activity
\phi
, we can look directly at the free energy
f(\psi)
from Eq (
12
); results are shown in Fig
2
for a population of neurons in the mouse hippocampus
[
27
,
28
]
.
There are two local minima, very nearly degenerate.The population of hippocampal neurons analyzed here lives in a plane, and so we can change
N
by expanding the radius of a circle
[
29
,
30
]
. As
N
increases the two minima of the free energy become more nearly degenerate, and indeed the free energy difference scales as
1/N
. This is a defining feature of a first order phase transition. We emphasize that this is driven entirely by the experimental observations
\mu_{\rm exp}
and
\chi_{\rm exp}
.

f(\psi)

h

. As we consider larger populations (increasing
N

(blue) with constant magnetization and the corresponding susceptibility

\chi
with
\chi_{\rm exp}
(dashed line) determines the exact solution of the maximum entropy problem.

The fact that the data drive these minimally structured models toward a first order transition is provocative. This result explains the failure of the naive mean-field approximation and is suggestive of a network balanced between macroscopically ordered patterns of activity. Unfortunately, while this model (by construction) matches the mean and variance of the summed population activity, the presence of two local minima in
f(\psi)
predicts that the distribution of this summed activity will be bimodal, and this is qualitatively wrong.

The problems that we find in matching the mean and variance of population activity persist in models that match the mean activity of individual neurons and variances of projections, as in Eq (
9
), though the details depend on our choice of the projections. If we choose a single projection with coefficients
W_{\alpha n}
given by independent Gaussian random numbers, then the naive mean-field approximation works but the resulting model is not very interesting, since it has an entropy only slightly below that for completely independent neurons.

To order possible choices of the projection coefficients it is useful to think about the correlation matrix

\tilde{C}_{nm}\equiv{{\langle s_{n}s_{m}\rangle-\langle s_{n}\rangle\langle s_{m}\rangle}\over{\left[(1-\langle s_{n}\rangle^{2})(1-\langle s_{m}\rangle^{2})\right]^{1/2}}}.

(17)

This matrix has eigenvectors
\tilde{\mathbf{W}}
and eigenvalues
\rho
,

\sum_{m=1}^{N}\tilde{C}_{nm}\tilde{W}_{\alpha m}=\rho_{\alpha}\tilde{W}_{\alpha n}.

(18)

If we choose projections with coefficients

W_{\alpha n}={1\over\sqrt{1-\langle s_{n}\rangle^{2}}}\tilde{W}_{\alpha n},

(19)

then following the same naive mean-field approximation that leads to Eq (
13
)
gives the entropy
[
21
,
24
]

S=S_{0}-{1\over 2}\sum_{\alpha}[\rho_{\alpha}-1-\ln(\rho_{\alpha})],

(20)

where
S_{0}
is the entropy of independent neurons. Thus the most informative projections are those connected to the largest or smallest eigenvalues of the correlation matrix. But if we choose these optimized projections we are led back to the problem of degenerate local minima.

To tame the multiple minima we insist on matching both the mean activities of individual neurons and the
distribution
of activities along a projection, leading to Eq (
10
).
The partition function

\displaystyle Z_{\rm dist}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{s}}\exp\left[\sum_{n=1}^{N}h_{n}s_{n}-NU\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}W_{n}s_{n}\right)\right]

(21)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{N}\int{{dz}\over{2\pi}}\int d\varphi\,\exp\left[-F_{\rm dist}(\varphi;z)\right],

(22)

where the free energy

F_{\rm dist}(\varphi;z)=iz\varphi+NU(\varphi)-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\ln\cosh(h_{n}+izW_{n}).

(23)

As usual we search for a saddle point
(\varphi_{\rm sp},z_{\rm sp})
that extremizes
F_{\rm dist}(\varphi;z)
, and we find

\displaystyle\varphi_{\rm sp}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}W_{n}\tanh\left(h_{\rm n}+iz_{\rm sp}W_{n}\right)

(24)

\displaystyle z_{\rm sp}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle iNU^{\prime}(\varphi_{\rm sp});

(25)

we will then approximate

\ln Z_{\rm dist}\approx-F_{\rm dist}(\varphi_{\rm sp};z_{\rm sp})+\cdots,

(26)

where
\cdots
are of less importance at large
N
.
Notice that
E_{\rm dist}(\bm{s})
is invariant under the gauge transformation
U(\varphi)\rightarrow U(\varphi)-\varphi U^{\prime}(\varphi_{\rm sp})
and
h_{n}\rightarrow h_{n}+NU^{\prime}(\varphi_{\rm sp})W_{n}
, so we can set
U^{\prime}(\varphi_{\rm sp})=0
.
This fixes
z_{\rm zp}=0
and yields

\langle s_{n}\rangle={{\partial Z_{\rm dist}}\over{\partial h_{n}}}=\tanh h_{n},

(27)

as if the neurons responded independently to the fields

h_{n}=\mathrm{atanh}(\langle s_{n}\rangle_{\rm exp}).

(28)

The distribution of activity along the projection is

P(\varphi)={{2^{N}}\over{Z_{\rm dist}}}\int{{dz}\over{2\pi}}\exp\left[-F_{\rm dist}(\varphi;z)\right].

(29)

The saddle point approximation is

P(\varphi)\propto\exp\left[-F_{\rm dist}(\varphi;z_{*}(\varphi))\right],

(30)

where
z_{*}
depends on
\varphi
and is the solution of

\sum_{\rm n=1}^{N}W_{n}\tanh\left[h_{n}+iW_{n}z_{*}(\varphi)\right]=\varphi.

(31)

Given weights
\{W_{n}\}
and the fields from Eq (
28
), we solve Eq (
31
) numerically to give
z_{*}(\varphi)
. If we substitute into Eq (
30
) we see that all terms except a factor
e^{-NU(\varphi)}
are determined, so we can “read off”
U(\varphi)
from the measured distribution of activity
P_{\rm exp}(\varphi)
along the projection.

We have applied this analysis to experiments on
the mouse hippocampus, with results in Fig
3
. Following the guide above, we choose the projection corresponding to the largest eigenvalue of the correlation matrix, scaled as in Eq (
19
). We see in Fig
3
A that the potential
U(\varphi)
departs significantly from quadratic at large
|\varphi|
, hinting as to why this model might “work” even when fixing the variance of activity along the projection fails, as above.

For some parameter values the curvature of the free energy will vanish at the saddle point, signaling the proximity of a second order phase transition.
The condition for this to happen is
NU^{\prime\prime}(\varphi_{\rm sp})+\chi_{0}^{-1}=0
, where
\chi_{0}
is the variance of activity along the projection that we would find if the neurons were independent,

\chi_{0}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}W_{n}^{2}\left[1-\langle s_{n}\rangle^{2}\right].

(32)

With random projections
NU^{\prime\prime}(\varphi_{\rm sp})
is near zero, far from criticality (Fig
4
A). If we choose random positive coefficients then
NU^{\prime\prime}(\varphi_{\rm sp})
becomes consistently negative but still not close to
-\chi_{0}^{-1}
. The more interesting case is when we choose coefficients related to the eigenvectors of the correlation matrix, as in Eq (
19
). Again most choices are far from criticality, but we see a collection of modes that trail toward the boundary
NU^{\prime\prime}(\varphi_{\rm sp})+\chi_{0}^{-1}=0
.

The approach to criticality depends on the strength of correlations in the network. We can imagine systems in which the mean activity of each neuron is the same, but the correlations between pairs of neurons are weaker, and we can generate such data by shuffling a fraction of the time bins independently for each neuron. For each shuffled data set we repeat the construction above, and we find that
NU^{\prime\prime}(\varphi_{\rm sp})
and
-\chi_{0}^{-1}
gradually move apart as we consider less correlated networks; this shows that plausible populations of neurons are farther from criticality than the real network. This is consistent with other signatures of near–critical behavior
[
1
]
, including the scaling of these same data under coarse–graining
[
28
]
.

We can compute the entropy of the model in Eq. (
10
) following the mean-field approximation,

S=S_{0}+N\left(\langle U(\varphi)\rangle-U(\varphi_{\rm sp})\right)-\frac{1}{2}\ln\left[NU^{\prime\prime}(\varphi_{\rm sp})\chi_{0}+1\right],

(33)

with results in Fig
5
for the same data as in Fig
4
.
Matching the distribution along just one projection leads to an entropy reduction of
\sim 5\%
of the independent entropy. There are roughly
200
projections that each provide a contribution above the independent entropy per neuron.

To summarize, it proves surprisingly difficult to construct a consistent mean-field theory for the patterns of activity in a network of real neurons. We finally succeed with a model that matches the mean activity of individual neurons and the
distribution
of activity along one projection. But there are many projections along which constraining the distribution generates a substantial reduction in entropy. This suggests that an important future direction is to extend the mean-field approximation to a model matching the distribution of multiple projections. This would give a framework for describing networks of
N
neurons with
\mathcal{O}(N)
constraints, making it possible to analyze experiments at much larger
N
.
