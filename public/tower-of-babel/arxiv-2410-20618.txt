Expanding polynomials for sets with additive structure Thanks: This project was conducted as part of the 2024 NYC Discrete Math REU, funded by NSF awards DMS-2051026 and DMS-2349366 and by Jane Street. CP’s work is supported by NSF award DMS-2246659.
Sanjana Das, Cosmin Pohoata, Adam Sheffer

Abstract
. The expansion of bivariate polynomials is well-understood for sets with a linear-sized product set. In contrast, not much is known for sets with small sumset. In this work, we provide expansion bounds for polynomials of the form f ⁡ ( x , y ) = g ⁡ ( x + p ⁡ ( y ) ) + h ⁡ ( y ) f(x,y)=g(x+p(y))+h(y) for sets with small sumset. In particular, we prove that when | A | \left\lvert A\right\rvert , | B | \left\lvert B\right\rvert , | A + A | \left\lvert A+A\right\rvert , and | B + B | \left\lvert B+B\right\rvert are not too far apart, for every ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 we have | f ⁡ ( A , B ) | = Ω ⁡ ( | A | 256 / 121 − ε ​ | B | 74 / 121 − ε | A + A | 108 / 121 ​ | B + B | 24 / 121 ) . \left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert=\Omega\left(\frac{\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{256/121-\varepsilon}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{74/121-\varepsilon}}{\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{108/121}\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert^{24/121}}\right). We show that the above bound and its variants have a variety of applications in additive combinatorics and distinct distances problems. Our proof technique relies on the recent proximity approach of Solymosi and Zahl. In particular, we show how to incorporate the size of a sumset into this approach.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

Most multivariate polynomials
f
have the following curious property: Given any set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, if we evaluate
f
on all inputs drawn from
A
, the resulting set of outputs is much larger than
A
. For example, for the polynomial
f(x,y,z)=x+yz
, any finite set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
satisfies

(1.1)

\left\lvert\{f(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})\mid a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\in A\}\right\rvert=\Omega(\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{3/2}),

and it is conjectured that the exponent
\frac{3}{2}
can be replaced with
2-\varepsilon
for any
\varepsilon>0
(see
[
RNRSS19
]
and
[
SW22
]
for slightly better bounds and further discussion). Many problems can be reduced to studying such expansion properties of polynomials, and the study of these properties is currently an active and exciting research front; for example, see
[
RSdZ15
,
Tao15
,
RSS16
,
BB21
]
.

More formally, for a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}]
and sets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, we write

f(A_{1},\ldots,A_{k})=\{f(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\mid a_{1}\in A_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in A_{k}\}.

We say
f
is
expanding
if for any sets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
of size
n
, the set
f(A_{1},\ldots,A_{k})
has size asymptotically larger than
n
(which we write as
\left\lvert f(A_{1},\ldots,A_{k})\right\rvert=\omega(n)
).

In this paper, we focus on the case of bivariate polynomials
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
. In
[
ER00
]
, Elekes and Rónyai fully characterized
which
bivariate polynomials are expanding.

Theorem 1.1

(Elekes–Rónyai)
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
be a polynomial, and suppose that there do not exist polynomials
p,q\in\mathbb{R}[x]
for which

(1.2)

f(x,y)=g(p(x)+q(y))\quad\text{or}\quad f(x,y)=g(p(x)\cdot q(y)).

Then for all sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
with
\left\lvert A\right\rvert=\left\lvert B\right\rvert=n
, we have
\left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert=\omega(n)
.

The constant in the asymptotic notation of Theorem
1.1
, as well as in similar bounds throughout the paper, depends on
\deg f
.

The condition in Theorem
1.1
that
f
is not of the special form
f(x,y)=g(p(x)+q(y))
or
f(x,y)=g(p(x)\cdot q(y))
is necessary — if
f
were of the first special form, then we could construct sets
A
and
B
of size
n
for which
\left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert=\Theta(n)
by taking
p(A)
and
q(B)
to be arithmetic progressions with the same difference. Similarly, if
f
were of the second special form, then we could take
p(A)
and
q(B)
to be
geometric
progressions. So polynomials of these special forms are not expanding, which means Theorem
1.1
fully answers the qualitative question of which polynomials are expanding.

It is then natural to ask for
quantitative
lower bounds on
\left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert
when we know
f
is expanding. The first such bound was proven by Raz, Sharir, and Solymosi
[
RSS16
]
.

Theorem 1.2

(Raz–Sharir–Solymosi)
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
be a polynomial which is not of either special form in (
1.2
). Then for all sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, we have

\left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert=\Omega(\min\{\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2/3}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2/3},\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2},\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2}\}).

This was then improved by Solymosi and Zahl
[
SZ24
]
to the following bound.

Theorem 1.3

(Solymosi–Zahl)
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
be a polynomial which is not of either special form in (
1.2
). Then for all sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, we have

\left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert=\Omega(\min\{\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{3/4}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{3/4},\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2},\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2}\}).

In particular, when
\left\lvert A\right\rvert=\left\lvert B\right\rvert=n
, Theorem
1.3
gives a bound of
\left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert=\Omega(n^{3/2})
. For more information on expanding bivariate polynomials and their applications, see
[
dZ18
]
.

1.2.
Our expansion results

In this paper, we consider the following question: Can we prove stronger expansion properties if we know that
A
and
B
are ‘additively structured’? To make this precise, we define the
sumset
of a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
as

A+A=\{a+a^{\prime}\mid a,a^{\prime}\in A\}.

Intuitively, a set with a small sumset is similar to a generalized arithmetic progression (for more details, see
[
Zha23
, Chapter 7]
). For this reason, we say that such sets are additively structured. So we want to prove stronger lower bounds on
\left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert
when
A+A
and
B+B
are small.

Throughout this paper, we work over
\mathbb{R}
. We work only with polynomials of the form

f(x,y)=g(x+p(y))+h(y)

for single-variable polynomials
g
,
h
, and
p
. Although this is a rather restrictive form, it encompasses the polynomials that arise from at least two natural problems — the problem of showing that
A
and
g(A)
cannot both have small sumsets (where we consider
f(x,y)=g(x)+g(y)
), and the problem of bounding the number of distinct distances between two lines (where if our lines are the
x
-axis and
x=sy
, we consider
f(x,y)=(x-sy)^{2}+y^{2}
). We derive new results for both problems, which we discuss in Subsections
1.3
and
1.4
.

Remark 1.4
.

Many of the applications that we will discuss involve the case where
p=0
, meaning that
f
is of the form

f(x,y)=g(x)+h(y)

for some
g,h\in\mathbb{R}[x]
. These polynomials are
not
expanding in general, as they are of the first special form in (
1.2
). But the sets
A
and
B
in the construction we used to show they are not expanding do not have additive structure when
\deg g,\deg h\geq 2
. So unlike in results such as Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
, and
1.3
, we can still hope to get lower bounds on
\left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert
for such polynomials.

Our first result considers the setting where only the set
A
has additive structure.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
be of the form
f(x,y)=g(x+p(y))+h(y)
where
g
,
p
, and
h
are single-variable polynomials,
\deg g\geq 2
, and
\deg h\geq 1
. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
, there is some
c>0
such that for any
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
with
\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert\leq c\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{3/2}
, we have

\left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert=\Omega\left(\min\left\{\frac{\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{28/13-\varepsilon}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{4/13-\varepsilon}}{\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{12/13}},\frac{\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{8/3}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{1/3-\varepsilon}}{\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{4/3}},\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2},\left\lvert A\right\rvert\left\lvert B\right\rvert\right\}\right)

(where both
c
and the implicit constant depend on
\deg f
and
\varepsilon
).

In the regime where
\left\lvert A\right\rvert=\left\lvert B\right\rvert=n
and
\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert=\Theta(n)
(intuitively, this means that
A
has the maximum possible additive structure), Theorem
1.5
gives a bound of
\left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert=\Omega(n^{20/13-2\varepsilon})
. Meanwhile, when
A+A
is reasonably small and
B
is much smaller than
A
(e.g.,
\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{9/8}
and
\left\lvert B\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{1/8}
), Theorem
1.5
implies that
\left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert=\Omega(\left\lvert A\right\rvert\left\lvert B\right\rvert)
. This is tight, as we always have
\left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert A\right\rvert\left\lvert B\right\rvert
.

We prove a stronger bound when
both

A
and
B
have additive structure.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
be of the form
f(x,y)=g(x+p(y))+h(y)
where
g
,
p
, and
h
are single-variable polynomials and
\deg g,\deg h\geq 2
. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
, there is some
c>0
such that for any
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
with
\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert\leq c\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{3/2}
and
\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{4/3}
, we have

\left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert=\Omega\left(\min\left\{\frac{\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{256/121-\varepsilon}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{74/121-\varepsilon}}{\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{108/121}\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert^{24/121}},\frac{\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{74/29}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{28/29-\varepsilon}}{\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{36/29}\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert^{12/29}},\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2},\left\lvert A\right\rvert\left\lvert B\right\rvert\right\}\right)

(where both
c
and the implicit constant depend on
\deg f
and
\varepsilon
).

In the regime where
\left\lvert A\right\rvert=\left\lvert B\right\rvert=n
and
\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert
and
\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert
are both
\Theta(n)
, Theorem
1.6
gives a bound of
\left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert=\Omega(n^{18/11-2\varepsilon})
.

Remark 1.7
.

Similarly to the sumset of
A
, the
difference set
of
A
is defined as

A-A=\{a-a^{\prime}\mid a,a^{\prime}\in A\}.

Theorems
1.5
and
1.6
also hold if we replace all the sumsets with difference sets; the same proofs work, with straightforward modifications.

1.3.
Applications to sum-product type bounds

We now discuss some applications of Theorems
1.5
and
1.6
.

The sum-product problem concerns the statement that a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
cannot be both additively and multiplicatively structured. To make this precise, we define the
product set
of
A
as

AA=\{aa^{\prime}\mid a,a^{\prime}\in A\}.

Similarly to the additive case, we think of
A
as multiplicatively structured if its product set is small. Erdős and Szemerédi
[
ES83
]
conjectured that for any set
A
, either its sumset or product set must be of near-maximal size.

Conjecture 1.8

(Erdős–Szemerédi)
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Then for any set
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
, we have

\max\{\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert,\left\lvert AA\right\rvert\}=\Omega(\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2-\varepsilon}).

Erdős and Szemerédi
[
ES83
]
proved that
\max\{\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert,\left\lvert AA\right\rvert\}=\Omega(\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{1+\delta})
for some
\delta>0
. Several authors have improved the value of
\delta
and generalized the result from
\mathbb{Z}
to
\mathbb{R}
; the current best bound is due to Rudnev and Stevens
[
RS22
]
, who showed that we can take
\delta=\frac{1}{3}+\frac{2}{1167}-\varepsilon
for any
\varepsilon>0
.

One commonly studied variant of this problem concerns the statement that for a strictly convex function
g
, the sets
A
and
g(A)
cannot both be additively structured — more precisely,
A+A
and
g(A)+g(A)
cannot both be small. (The case
g(x)=-\log x
corresponds to the sum-product problem.) The current best bound for this problem is due to Stevens and Warren
[
SW22
]
. (For definitions of the asymptotic notation
\Omega^{*}(\,\cdot\,)
and
O^{*}(\,\cdot\,)
, see Section
2
.)

Theorem 1.9

(Stevens–Warren)
.

Let
g\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be convex. Then for all sets
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, we have

\left\lvert g(A)+g(A)\right\rvert=\Omega^{*}\left(\frac{\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{49/19}}{\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert}\right).

In particular, for all
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, we have
\max\{\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert,\left\lvert g(A)+g(A)\right\rvert\}=\Omega^{*}(\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{49/38})
.

Theorem
1.6
leads to the following improved bound when
g
is a polynomial of degree at least
2
.

Corollary 1.10
.

Let
g\in\mathbb{R}[x]
be a polynomial with
\deg g\geq 2
, and let
\varepsilon>0
. Then for all sets
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
such that
\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{4/3}
, we have

\left\lvert g(A)+g(A)\right\rvert=\Omega\left(\frac{\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{30/11-\varepsilon}}{\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{12/11}}\right).

In particular, for all
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, we have
\max\{\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert,\left\lvert g(A)+g(A)\right\rvert\}=\Omega(\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{30/23-\varepsilon})
.

Lower bounds on
\max\{\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert,\left\lvert g(A)+g(A)\right\rvert\}
are one way of quantifying the notion that
g
‘destroys additive structure.’ Another way of quantifying this is by considering the case where
A+A
is minimal and analyzing how big
g(A)+g(A)
must be. This has been studied from the perspective of convex sets. A set
C=\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is
convex
if

c_{2}-c_{1}<c_{3}-c_{2}<\cdots<c_{n}-c_{n-1}.

Equivalently,
C
is a convex set if and only if
C=g([n])
for a convex function
g
. The problem of proving that convex sets must have large sumsets have been studied by many authors; Schoen and Shkredov
[
SS11
]
proved that
\left\lvert C+C\right\rvert=\Omega^{*}(\left\lvert C\right\rvert^{14/9})
.

In the extreme case where
\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert=\Theta(\left\lvert A\right\rvert)
, Theorem
1.9
of
[
SW22
]
gives

\left\lvert g(A)+g(A)\right\rvert=\Omega^{*}(\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{30/19}),

which in particular means
\left\lvert C+C\right\rvert=\Omega^{*}(\left\lvert C\right\rvert^{30/19})
for any convex set
C
(this slightly improves the bound of
[
SS11
]
). Meanwhile, when
g
is a polynomial, Corollary
1.10
gives

\left\lvert g(A)+g(A)\right\rvert=\Omega(\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{18/11-\varepsilon}).

This is a stronger bound, but for a less general class of functions
g
.

Finally, several authors have proved more general lower bounds on
\max\{\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert,\left\lvert g(A)+B\right\rvert\}
for arbitrary sets
B
. In particular, Shkredov
[
Shk15
]
proved the following bound. (Before
[
SW22
]
, this gave the best lower bound on
\max\{\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert,\left\lvert g(A)+g(A)\right\rvert\}
.)

Theorem 1.11

(Shkredov)
.

Let
g\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be continuous and convex. Then for all sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
with
\left\lvert A\right\rvert=\left\lvert B\right\rvert
, we have

\left\lvert g(A)+C\right\rvert=\Omega^{*}\left(\frac{\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{50/21}}{\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{37/42}}\right).

When
g
is a polynomial, if we apply Theorem
1.5
with
f(x,y)=g(x)+y
, then we get the following result, which is an improvement when
\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{26/19-\varepsilon}
for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
.

Corollary 1.12
.

Let
g\in\mathbb{R}[x]
be a polynomial with
\deg g\geq 2
, and let
\varepsilon>0
. Then for all sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
with
\left\lvert A\right\rvert=\left\lvert B\right\rvert
, we have

\left\lvert g(A)+B\right\rvert=\Omega\left(\min\left\{\frac{\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{32/13-\varepsilon}}{\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{12/13}},\frac{\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{3-\varepsilon}}{\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{4/3}}\right\}\right).

As opposed to Theorem
1.5
, Corollary
1.12
does not include the condition
\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert\leq c\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{3/2}
. This is because when
\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert=\Omega(\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{3/2})
, the second term in Corollary
1.12
is
O(\left\lvert A\right\rvert)
, which means the bound of Corollary
1.12
is still true in this case.

1.4.
Applications to distinct distances problems

Next, we discuss some applications of Theorems
1.5
and
1.6
to distinct distances problems. For two points
p,q\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we write
d(p,q)
to denote the distance between them. For a set of points
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we write

\Delta(\mathcal{P})=\{d(p,q)\mid p,q\in\mathcal{P}\}.

Similarly, for two sets of points
\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, we write

\Delta(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2})=\{d(p,q)\mid p\in\mathcal{P}_{1},q\in\mathcal{P}_{2}\}.

The study of distinct distances started with the following question by Erdős
[
Erd46
]
: What is the minimum possible number of distinct distances that
n
points in the plane can form? In other words, what is the minimum value of
\left\lvert\Delta(\mathcal{P})\right\rvert
over all sets
\mathcal{P}
of
n
points? Erdős showed that when
\mathcal{P}
is a
\sqrt{n}\times\sqrt{n}
lattice, we have
\left\lvert\Delta(\mathcal{P})\right\rvert=O(\frac{n}{\sqrt{\log n}})
. The problem of proving a comparable
lower
bound on
\left\lvert\Delta(\mathcal{P})\right\rvert
was studied by many authors; finally, Guth and Katz
[
GK15
]
proved that any set
\mathcal{P}
of size
n
satisfies
\left\lvert\Delta(\mathcal{P})\right\rvert=\Omega(\frac{n}{\log n})
, which almost matches Erdős’s upper bound.

While the above question is almost fully resolved, most distinct distances problems are still wide open. We now discuss some applications of our results to two of these problems.

1.4.1.
Distinct distances between two lines

The first problem we consider is about the minimum number of distinct distances between points on two lines: Let
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
be two lines, and let
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
be finite sets of points on
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
, respectively. How small can
\Delta(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2})
be? This question is one of the most elementary distinct distances problems, in the sense that each point is defined by a single coordinate. However, it is still far from solved; this makes it a central distinct distances problem.

We have
\lvert\Delta(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2})\rvert=\Omega(\left\lvert\mathcal{P}_{1}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\mathcal{P}_{2}\right\rvert)
. When
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
are parallel or orthogonal, this is tight. Indeed, when
\ell_{1}\parallel\ell_{2}
, we can take equally spaced points on the two lines; when
\ell_{1}\perp\ell_{2}
, we can take
\mathcal{P}_{1}=\{(\sqrt{a},0)\mid a\in[m]\}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}=\{(0,\sqrt{b})\mid b\in[n]\}
. However, when
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
are neither parallel nor orthogonal, the number of distances is significantly larger; the current best lower bound is due to Solymosi and Zahl
[
SZ24
]
.

Theorem 1.13

(Solymosi–Zahl)
.

Suppose that
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
are neither parallel nor orthogonal, and let
\mathcal{P}_{1}\subseteq\ell_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}\subseteq\ell_{2}
be finite sets of points. Then

\left\lvert\Delta(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2})\right\rvert=\Omega(\min\{\left\lvert\mathcal{P}_{1}\right\rvert^{3/4}\left\lvert\mathcal{P}_{2}\right\rvert^{3/4},\left\lvert\mathcal{P}_{1}\right\rvert,\left\lvert\mathcal{P}_{2}\right\rvert\}).

The problem of lower-bounding
\Delta(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2})
is an expanding polynomials problem. Indeed, let
\ell_{1}
be the line
y=0
and
\ell_{2}
the line
x=sy
(this setup allows the lines to be orthogonal, but not parallel). Let
A
be the set of
x
-coordinates of
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
B
the set of
y
-coordinates of
\mathcal{P}_{2}
. Then the polynomial

f(x,y)=(x-sy)^{2}+y^{2}

is the square of the distance between the points
(x,0)
and
(sy,y)
, which means
\lvert\Delta(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2})\rvert=\left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert
. Theorem
1.13
immediately follows from applying Theorem
1.3
to this polynomial
f
(if
s\neq 0
, then
f
does not have either special form in (
1.2
)).

Using Theorems
1.5
and
1.6
, we can get improved bounds in the case where
A
and
B
have additive structure, which also hold when
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
are orthogonal. For these bounds, we use the variants of Theorems
1.5
and
1.6
with difference sets rather than sumsets (see Remark
1.7
) because difference sets have a simpler geometric interpretation here — we have
\left\lvert A-A\right\rvert=2\left\lvert\Delta(\mathcal{P}_{1})\right\rvert-1
and
\left\lvert B-B\right\rvert=2\left\lvert\Delta(\mathcal{P}_{2})\right\rvert-1
. So we can view these bounds as stating that if there are few distinct distances
within

\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
(relative to their sizes), then there are many distinct distances
between
them.

To simplify the bounds, we restrict our attention to the case where
\lvert\Delta(\mathcal{P}_{1})\rvert\leq\left\lvert\mathcal{P}_{1}\right\rvert^{5/4}
; this condition ensures that in both Theorems
1.5
and
1.6
, the first term is smaller than the second. Then Theorems
1.5
and
1.6
imply the following bounds.

Corollary 1.14
.

Let
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
be two lines which are not parallel (but may be orthogonal), and let
\mathcal{P}_{1}\subseteq\ell_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}\subseteq\ell_{2}
. If
\lvert\Delta(\mathcal{P}_{1})\rvert\leq\left\lvert\mathcal{P}_{1}\right\rvert^{5/4}
, then for all
\varepsilon>0
we have

\left\lvert\Delta(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2})\right\rvert=\Omega\left(\min\left\{\frac{\left\lvert\mathcal{P}_{1}\right\rvert^{28/13-\varepsilon}\left\lvert\mathcal{P}_{2}\right\rvert^{4/13-\varepsilon}}{\left\lvert\Delta(\mathcal{P}_{1})\right\rvert^{12/13}},\left\lvert\mathcal{P}_{1}\right\rvert^{2},\left\lvert\mathcal{P}_{1}\right\rvert\left\lvert\mathcal{P}_{2}\right\rvert\right\}\right).

Corollary 1.15
.

Let
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
be two lines which are not parallel, and let
\mathcal{P}_{1}\subseteq\ell_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}\subseteq\ell_{2}
. If
\lvert\Delta(\mathcal{P}_{1})\rvert\leq\left\lvert\mathcal{P}_{1}\right\rvert^{5/4}
and
\left\lvert\Delta(\mathcal{P}_{2})\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert\mathcal{P}_{2}\right\rvert^{4/3}
, then for all
\varepsilon>0
we have

\left\lvert\Delta(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2})\right\rvert=\Omega\left(\min\left\{\frac{\left\lvert\mathcal{P}_{1}\right\rvert^{256/121-\varepsilon}\left\lvert\mathcal{P}_{2}\right\rvert^{74/121-\varepsilon}}{\left\lvert\Delta(\mathcal{P}_{1})\right\rvert^{108/121}\left\lvert\Delta(\mathcal{P}_{2})\right\rvert^{24/121}},\left\lvert\mathcal{P}_{1}\right\rvert^{2},\left\lvert\mathcal{P}_{1}\right\rvert\left\lvert\mathcal{P}_{2}\right\rvert\right\}\right).

1.4.2.
Heavy lines in configurations with few distances

After Guth and Katz
[
GK15
]
almost fully resolved the question of the minimum number of distinct distances
n
points can form, it became natural to try to characterize the point sets that achieve this minimum. In fact, Erdős
[
Erd86
]
asked this question decades earlier — specifically, he asked whether any such point set must have ‘lattice structure.’ As a first step towards such a statement, he asked whether any such point set must have a line containing
\Omega(n^{1/2})
points, or even just
\Omega(n^{\varepsilon})
points. A variant of a proof of Szemerédi (communicated in
[
Erd75
]
) shows that there must be a line containing
\Omega(\sqrt{\log n})
points, and
[
She14
]
shows that we can improve this bound to
\Omega(\log n)
.

Meanwhile, we have substantially better bounds in the opposite direction, showing that there cannot be
too many
points on a single line. Sheffer, Zahl, and de Zeeuw
[
SZdZ16
]
proved that in a set of
n
points forming
o(n)
distinct distances, no line can contain
\Omega(n^{7/8})
of these points. Raz, Roche-Newton, and Sharir
[
RRNS15
]
then improved this to the following bound.

Theorem 1.16

(Raz–Roche-Newton–Sharir)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a set of
n
points such that
\left\lvert\Delta(\mathcal{P})\right\rvert\leq\frac{1}{5}n
. Then every line
\ell
satisfies
\left\lvert\ell\cap\mathcal{P}\right\rvert=O^{*}(n^{43/52})
.

Combining the ideas of
[
SZdZ16
]
and
[
RRNS15
]
with Corollary
1.14
, we get the following very slight improvement; we present the proof in Subsection
9.2
.

Corollary 1.17
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a set of
n
points such that
\left\lvert\Delta(\mathcal{P})\right\rvert\leq\frac{1}{5}n
. Then for all
\varepsilon>0
, every line
\ell
satisfies
\left\lvert\ell\cap\mathcal{P}\right\rvert=O(n^{13/16+\varepsilon})
.

1.5.
Almost tight bounds

Interestingly, several authors have studied the expansion of polynomials on sets with
multiplicative
structure, and this problem is much better understood. Chang
[
Cha06
]
first studied this problem for the specific polynomial
f(x,y)=x+y
and proved that when
AA
is very small, this polynomial essentially has the maximum possible expansion.

Theorem 1.18

(Chang)
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Then for all sets
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
with
\left\lvert AA\right\rvert=o(\left\lvert A\right\rvert\log\left\lvert A\right\rvert)
(with the constant in the asymptotic notation depending on
\varepsilon
) and all sets
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, we have

\left\lvert A+B\right\rvert=\Omega(\left\lvert A\right\rvert\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{1-\varepsilon}).

The second author
[
Poh20
]
then studied this problem for general polynomials and showed that when
AA
is very small,
all
polynomials
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
except those of a certain special form have the maximum possible expansion.

Theorem 1.19

(Pohoata)
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
be a polynomial which is not of the form
g(p(x,y))
for some single-variable polynomial
g
and some monomial
p
, and let
K
be a constant. Then for all sets
A
with
\left\lvert AA\right\rvert\leq K\left\lvert A\right\rvert
, we have

\left\lvert f(A,A)\right\rvert=\Omega(\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}).

In contrast, Theorems
1.5
and
1.6
only lead to maximum expansion when
A
is much larger than
B
. In the special case where
f(x,y)=x^{2}+y^{2}
and
A=B
, we obtain a bound that does give nearly the maximum possible expansion when
A+A
is minimal, using different methods.

Proposition 1.20
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a finite set with
\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert\leq K\left\lvert A\right\rvert
. Then we have

\lvert A^{2}+A^{2}\rvert=\Omega\left(\frac{\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}}{K^{12}\log\left\lvert A\right\rvert}\right).

1.6.
Overview of techniques

We now give a high-level overview of the techniques we use to prove Theorems
1.5
and
1.6
.

Expanding polynomials are often studied using incidence geometry. In particular, Raz, Sharir, and Solymosi
[
RSS16
]
proved Theorem
1.2
by studying the quantity

(1.3)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\{(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\in A^{2}\times B^{2}\mid f(a_{1},b_{1})=f(a_{2},b_{2})\}\right\rvert,

which we think of as the
energy
associated to
f
. If
f(A,B)
is small, then this energy must be large (by Cauchy–Schwarz); so to lower-bound
f(A,B)
, it suffices to upper-bound this energy. To do so, they set up a collection of points and curves whose incidences correspond to
4
-tuples of this form, and then used incidence bounds to upper-bound the number of such
4
-tuples.

The way we use the information that
A+A
is small is by using the fact that every
a\in A
can be written as
\alpha-a^{\prime}
for
\alpha\in A+A
and
a^{\prime}\in A
in
\left\lvert A\right\rvert
ways (if we choose
a^{\prime}
, then
\alpha=a+a^{\prime}
is automatically in
A+A
). So instead of working with the energy (
1.3
), we work with

\left\lvert\{(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime},b_{1},b_{2})\in(A+A)^{2}\times A^{2}\times B^{2}\mid f(\alpha_{1}-a_{1}^{\prime},b_{1})=f(\alpha_{2}-a_{2}^{\prime},b_{2})\}\right\rvert.

As before, if
f(A,B)
is small, then this quantity must be large; and we can again get upper bounds on it using incidence bounds. When
A+A
is small, our incidence problem will have a smaller set of points, leading to better bounds.

The proofs of Theorems
1.5
and
1.6
both follow this basic setup. In both cases, the same curve may repeat multiple times in our incidence problem. The main difference between the proofs is that in the proof of Theorem
1.6
, we use the additive structure of
B
to get better control on curve multiplicities.

The above approach does not lead to good bounds; we amplify these bounds by using
proximity
. Solymosi and Zahl
[
SZ24
]
introduced the idea of proximity in order to prove Theorem
1.3
. Roughly speaking, instead of considering
all

4
-tuples satisfying
f(a_{1},b_{1})=f(a_{2},b_{2})
, they restricted their attention to ones where
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
, as well as
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
, are ‘close’ to each other. These proximity conditions shrink our lower bound on the number of such
4
-tuples in terms of
\left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert
, but they also shrink the number of points and curves in the incidence problem. It turns out that they shrink the upper bound on the number of incidences by a smaller factor than they shrink the lower bound, giving us a better bound on
\left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert
. Intuitively, this is because among solutions to
f(a_{1},b_{1})=f(a_{2},b_{2})
, there is a correlation between
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
being close and
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
being close. Thus, imposing
both
proximity conditions does not shrink the lower bound by much more than imposing
one
would.

Our proofs work by incorporating proximity into the ideas mentioned above — instead of considering all
6
-tuples satisfying
f(\alpha_{1}-a_{1}^{\prime},b_{1})=f(\alpha_{2}-a_{2}^{\prime},b_{2})
, we only consider pairs
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
,
(a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime})
, and
(b_{1},b_{2})
whose two elements are close. This shrinks our upper bound on the number of incidences by a smaller factor than the lower bound, resulting in better bounds for
\left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert
.

The organization of this paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we state a few preliminaries needed for our proofs. In Section
3
, we present a simpler version of our proofs without proximity (obtaining weaker bounds), to illustrate some of the other main ideas — in particular, how we set up the incidence problem and make use of the additive structure of
A
and
B
. In Section
4
, we give an outline of the actual proofs with proximity (for both Theorems
1.5
and
1.6
), and in Sections
5
–
8
, we fill in the details of this outline. Finally, in Subsection
9.1
, we prove Proposition
1.20
, and in Subsection
9.2
, we prove Corollary
1.17
.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we state a few preliminaries that we will need for our proofs. We use standard asymptotic notation:

•

x=O(y)
means there is a constant
c>0
such that
x\leq cy
;

•

x=\Omega(y)
means there is a constant
c>0
such that
x\geq cy
;

•

x=\Theta(y)
means we have both
x=O(y)
and
x=\Omega(y)
.

We use
O^{*}(\,\cdot\,)
and
\Omega^{*}(\,\cdot\,)
to suppress logarithmic factors — more precisely,
x=O^{*}(y)
means there are constants
c,d>0
such that
x\leq cy(\log y)^{d}
, and
x=\Omega^{*}(y)
means there are constants
c,d>0
such that
x\geq cy(\log y)^{-d}
.

All logarithms are base
2
.

2.1.
Incidence bounds

Our proofs rely on an incidence bound for algebraic curves from
[
SZ17
]
; in this subsection, we give a few relevant definitions and state this bound.

For a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
, we write

\mathcal{Z}(f)=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid f(x,y)=0\}.

We say a set
\gamma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
is an
algebraic curve
if
\gamma
is nonempty and we can write
\gamma=\mathcal{Z}(f)
for some nonconstant polynomial
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
.

We say a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
is
reducible
if we can write
f=f_{1}f_{2}
for nonconstant polynomials
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
; we say
f
is
irreducible
if it is not reducible. We say an algebraic curve
\gamma
is
reducible
if we can write
\gamma=\mathcal{Z}(f)
for some irreducible
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
.

We say a collection of curves
\mathcal{C}
is a
k
-dimensional family
if the curves in
\mathcal{C}
are of the form
\mathcal{Z}(f)
for polynomials
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
whose coefficients are themselves polynomials in
k
parameters
p_{1}
, …,
p_{k}
. More formally, this means there is a polynomial
F\in\mathbb{R}[x,y,p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}]
such that if we write

f_{p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}}(x,y)=F(x,y,p_{1},\ldots,p_{k})

(where we think of
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
as fixed, and
f_{p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}}
as a polynomial in
x
and
y
), then we have

\mathcal{C}=\{\mathcal{Z}(f_{p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}})\mid p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\in\mathbb{R}\}.

We define the
degree
of
\mathcal{C}
as
\deg F
(more precisely, the minimum value of
\deg F
over all
F
which could be used to define
\mathcal{C}
). For example, the collection of circles in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a
3
-dimensional family of degree
2
, corresponding to

F(x,y,z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})=(x-z_{1})^{2}+(y-z_{2})^{2}-z_{3}^{2}.

For a collection of points
\Pi
and a collection of curves
\Gamma
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we say an
incidence
between
\Pi
and
\Gamma
is a pair
(p,\gamma)\in\Pi\times\Gamma
where
p
lies on
\gamma
. We write

I(\Pi,\Gamma)=\left\lvert\{(p,\gamma)\in\Pi\times\Gamma\mid p\in\gamma\}\right\rvert

to denote the number of incidences between
\Pi
and
\Gamma
.

We will then use the following incidence bound, due to Sharir and Zahl
[
SZ17
]
. (Sharir and Zahl actually work in a somewhat more general setting, but the setting we have given here is easier to describe and is enough for our purposes.)

Theorem 2.1

(Sharir–Zahl)
.

Let
\Pi
be a set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and
\Gamma
a set of (distinct) irreducible algebraic curves in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
from a
k
-dimensional family of degree
d
. Then for all
\varepsilon>0
, we have

I(\Pi,\Gamma)=O(\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert^{\frac{2k}{5k-4}}\left\lvert\Gamma\right\rvert^{\frac{5k-6}{5k-4}+\varepsilon}+\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert^{2/3}\left\lvert\Gamma\right\rvert^{2/3}+\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert+\left\lvert\Gamma\right\rvert)

(where the implicit constant depends on
k
,
d
, and
\varepsilon
).

In our applications of Theorem
2.1
, we will always have
k=3
, in which case this bound gives

I(\Pi,\Gamma)=O(\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert^{6/11}\left\lvert\Gamma\right\rvert^{9/11+\varepsilon}+\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert^{2/3}\left\lvert\Gamma\right\rvert^{2/3}+\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert+\left\lvert\Gamma\right\rvert).

2.2.
Irreducibility of polynomials

When applying Theorem
2.1
, we will need to ensure that certain polynomials are irreducible; we now state a result that we will use for this purpose.

We say a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
is
decomposable
if we can write
f(x,y)=q(r(x,y))
for some
r\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
and
q\in\mathbb{R}[z]
with
\deg q\geq 2
. We say
f
is
indecomposable
if it is not decomposable.

The following result is a combination of theorems of Stein
[
Ste89
]
, who proved this result with
\mathbb{R}
replaced by
\mathbb{C}
, and Ayad
[
Aya02
]
, who proved that for polynomials in
\mathbb{R}[x,y]
, decomposability over
\mathbb{R}
is equivalent to decomposability over
\mathbb{C}
. (See
[
RSS16
]
for more details.)

Theorem 2.2

(Stein, Ayad)
.

If
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
is indecomposable, then there are at most
\deg f
values of
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
for which
f(x,y)-\lambda
is reducible.

2.3.
Cutting curves into monotone pieces

Finally, for the lower bounds involving proximity, we will need a statement that any algebraic curve can be split into a constant number of monotone pieces. To formalize this, we say a set
M\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
is
monotone
if either for all
(x_{1},y_{1})
and
(x_{2},y_{2})
in
M
with
x_{1}<x_{2}
, we have
y_{1}\leq y_{2}
, or for all such
(x_{1},y_{1})
and
(x_{2},y_{2})
, we have
y_{1}\geq y_{2}
.

Fact 2.3
.

For any constant-degree nonconstant polynomial
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
, we can partition
\mathcal{Z}(f)
into
O(1)
monotone sets (where the constant depends on
\deg f
).

Fact
2.3
can be proven by cutting
\mathcal{Z}(f)
at its intersections with
\mathcal{Z}(\partial_{x}f)
and
\mathcal{Z}(\partial_{y}f)
; each piece (i.e., connected component) of the result is monotone, and we can use Bezout’s theorem together with the argument in
[
SZ17
, Section 2.2]
to bound the number of pieces this creates. (This in fact gives a partition of
\mathcal{Z}(f)
into
connected
monotone sets, but we will not need connectivity in our applications of Fact
2.3
.)

3.
Warmup: a simpler argument without proximity

In this section, we sketch a simpler version of the argument that does not use proximity, and ends up with the weaker bounds of

(3.1)

\left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert=\Omega\left(\min\left\{\frac{\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{26/11-\varepsilon}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2/11-\varepsilon}}{\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{12/11}},\frac{\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{8/3}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{1/3-\varepsilon}}{\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{4/3}},\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2},\left\lvert A\right\rvert\left\lvert B\right\rvert\right\}\right)

in place of Theorem
1.5
and

(3.2)

\left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert=\Omega\left(\min\left\{\frac{\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{26/11-\varepsilon}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{52/99-\varepsilon}}{\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{12/11}\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert^{8/33}},\frac{\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{8/3}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{26/27-\varepsilon}}{\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{4/3}\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert^{4/9}},\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2},\left\lvert A\right\rvert\left\lvert B\right\rvert\right\}\right)

in place of Theorem
1.6
. For simplicity, in this section we assume that
\left\lvert A\right\rvert\approx\left\lvert B\right\rvert
and that
A+A
is not much bigger than
A
(then the first terms in both bounds are the smallest ones, so these are the bounds we will end up with). Later in the current proof, when we apply Theorem
2.1
, this assumption will make the first term dominate the bound of the theorem.

For brevity, we set
d=\deg f
. We also use
\eta
to denote a small error parameter which we will eventually take to be sufficiently small with respect to
\varepsilon
; its purpose is essentially to keep track of all ‘subpolynomial’ factors. The implicit constants in our bounds may depend on
d
and
\eta
.

First, instead of directly working with
f(A,B)
, we work with the set

\mathcal{Q}=\{(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\in A^{2}\times B^{2}\mid f(a_{1},b_{1})=f(a_{2},b_{2})\}

(we think of
\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert
as the
energy
associated to
f
). By Cauchy–Schwarz, we have

(3.3)

\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert=\sum_{\delta}\left\lvert\{(a,b)\mid f(a,b)=\delta\}\right\rvert^{2}\geq\frac{(\sum_{\delta}\left\lvert\{(a,b)\mid f(a,b)=\delta\}\right\rvert)^{2}}{\left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert}=\frac{\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2}}{\left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert}.

So to get a lower bound on
\left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert
, it suffices to get an
upper
bound on
\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert
.

With the irreducibility condition of Theorem
2.1
in mind, we say a pair
(b_{1},b_{2})\in B^{2}
is
bad
if either
g(x)-g(y)+h(b_{1})-h(b_{2})
or
h(x)-h(y)+h(b_{1})-h(b_{2})
is reducible, and we define

\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}=\{(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\in\mathcal{Q}\mid\text{$(b_{1},b_{2})$ not bad}\}.

We can show that bad pairs do not contribute too much to
\mathcal{Q}
— specifically, we have

\lvert\mathcal{Q}\setminus\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\rvert=O(\left\lvert A\right\rvert\left\lvert B\right\rvert).

(We will later state this as Lemma
5.2
, which we prove in Subsection
5.2
.) If
\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert\leq 2\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\setminus\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\right\rvert
, then this means
\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert=O(\left\lvert A\right\rvert\left\lvert B\right\rvert)
, and (
3.3
) implies that
\left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert=\Omega(\left\lvert A\right\rvert\left\lvert B\right\rvert)
. So we can assume
\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert\geq 2\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\setminus\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\right\rvert
, which means
\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\right\rvert=\Omega(\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert)
.

To use the fact that
A+A
is small, we consider the set

\mathcal{R}=\{(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime},b_{1},b_{2})\in(A+A)^{2}\times A^{2}\times B^{2}\mid\\
f(\alpha_{1}-a_{1}^{\prime},b_{1})=f(\alpha_{2}-a_{2}^{\prime},b_{2}),\text{$(b_{1},b_{2})$ not bad}\}.

On one hand, we have
\lvert\mathcal{R}\rvert\geq\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}\lvert\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\rvert=\Omega(\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}\lvert\mathcal{Q}\rvert)
— given any
(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\in\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
, we can choose
a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime}\in A
arbitrarily and set
\alpha_{1}=a_{1}+a_{1}^{\prime}
and
\alpha_{2}=a_{2}+a_{2}^{\prime}
to get an element of
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
.

On the other hand, we can get upper bounds for
\left\lvert\mathcal{R}\right\rvert
by setting up an incidence problem with points representing
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
and curves representing
(a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime},b_{1},b_{2})
, such that incidences between these points and curves correspond to elements of
\mathcal{R}
, and applying Theorem
2.1
. More precisely, we define a set of points

\Pi=(A+A)^{2}.

We define a
multiset
of curves
\Gamma
where for each
(a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime},b_{1},b_{2})\in A^{2}\times B^{2}
such that
(b_{1},b_{2})
is not bad, we include the curve defined by
f(x_{1}-a_{1}^{\prime},b_{1})=f(x_{2}-a_{2}^{\prime},b_{2})
, or more explicitly

(3.4)

g(x_{1}-a_{1}^{\prime}+p(b_{1}))+h(b_{1})=g(x_{2}-a_{2}^{\prime}+p(b_{2}))+h(b_{2}).

Then we have
\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert=\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{2}
and
\left\lvert\Gamma\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2}
, and
\lvert\mathcal{R}\rvert=I(\Pi,\Gamma)
by definition.

The curves in
\Gamma
are defined by the three parameters
a_{1}^{\prime}-p(b_{1})
,
a_{2}^{\prime}-p(b_{2})
, and
h(b_{1})-h(b_{2})
; this means they belong to a
3
-dimensional family, as defined in Subsection
2.1
. Also, all curves in
\Gamma
are irreducible, since if the curve defined by (
3.4
) were reducible, then
(b_{1},b_{2})
would be bad. So we are in a setting where we can apply Theorem
2.1
to bound
I(\Pi,\Gamma)
.

However, Theorem
2.1
can only be applied to a set of
distinct
curves, while our collection
\Gamma
may contain repeated curves. To handle this, we first dyadically partition
\Gamma
based on curve multiplicity — more precisely, for each
m\in\{2^{0},2^{1},\ldots\}
, we let
\Gamma_{m}
be the portion of
\Gamma
consisting of curves with multiplicities in
[m,2m)
(where we include curves in
\Gamma_{m}
with the same multiplicities as in
\Gamma
). Then the multisets
\Gamma_{m}
partition
\Gamma
, so

I(\Pi,\Gamma)=\sum_{m}I(\Pi,\Gamma_{m}).

Then for each
m
, we let
\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}
be the variant of
\Gamma_{m}
where each curve appears only once, so that
I(\Pi,\Gamma_{m})=\Theta(m\cdot I(\Pi,\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}))
. We can now use Theorem
2.1
to bound
I(\Pi,\Gamma_{m}^{\prime})
, as
\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}
is a set of
distinct
irreducible algebraic curves from a
3
-dimensional family; this gives

(3.5)

I(\Pi,\Gamma_{m})=O(m\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert^{6/11}\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert^{9/11+\eta}+m\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert^{2/3}\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert^{2/3}+m\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert+m\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert).

For simplicity, right now we only consider the first term of this bound. (When we sum over
m
, the contribution of the first term ends up dominating when
\left\lvert A\right\rvert\approx\left\lvert B\right\rvert
and
A+A
is small, which is the regime we are focusing on.)

We will get an upper bound on
m\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert^{9/11}
by bounding each of
m\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert
and
m^{2}\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert
. For the former, we have
m\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert\leq\lvert\Gamma\rvert
, as each curve in
\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}
appears at least
m
times in
\Gamma
. For the latter,
m^{2}\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert
is at most the number of
pairs
of
4
-tuples
(a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime},b_{1},b_{2})
and
(a_{3}^{\prime},a_{4}^{\prime},b_{3},b_{4})
producing the same curve. Given the equation of a curve of the form (
3.4
), the coefficient of the second-highest power of
x_{1}
uniquely determines
a_{1}^{\prime}-p(b_{1})
; similarly, the coefficient of the second-highest power of
x_{2}
determines
a_{2}^{\prime}-p(b_{2})
. Finally, once we know
a_{1}^{\prime}-p(b_{1})
and
a_{2}^{\prime}-p(b_{2})
, the constant term uniquely determines
h(b_{1})-h(b_{2})
. This means two
4
-tuples producing the same curve must satisfy

\displaystyle a_{1}^{\prime}-p(b_{1})

\displaystyle=a_{3}^{\prime}-p(b_{3}),

\displaystyle a_{2}^{\prime}-p(b_{2})

\displaystyle=a_{4}^{\prime}-p(b_{4}),

\displaystyle h(b_{1})-h(b_{2})

\displaystyle=h(b_{3})-h(b_{4}).

So if we define the set

\mathcal{S}=\{(b_{1},b_{2},c_{1},c_{2})\in B^{2}\times B^{2}\mid h(b_{1})+h(c_{1})=h(b_{2})+h(c_{2}),\,\text{$(b_{1},b_{2})$, $(c_{1},c_{2})$ not bad}\},

then we have
m^{2}\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert\leq\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert
.

Now we have upper bounds on both
m\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert
and
m^{2}\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert
, and we can combine them to get a bound on
m\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert^{9/11}
by writing

m\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert^{9/11}=(m\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert)^{7/11}(m^{2}\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert)^{2/11}\leq\left\lvert\Gamma\right\rvert^{7/11}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{4/11}\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert^{2/11}.

Then for each
m
, the first term of (
3.5
) satisfies

m\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert^{6/11}\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert^{9/11+\eta}\leq\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{18/11+2\eta}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{14/11+2\eta}\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{12/11}\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert^{2/11}

(after plugging in
\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert=\Theta(\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{2})
and
\left\lvert\Gamma\right\rvert=O(\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2})
).

Finally, our dyadic partition has at most
O(\log\left\lvert B\right\rvert)=O(\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{\eta})
values of
m
, since the argument we used to bound pairs of identical curves also shows that all curve multiplicities are
O(\left\lvert B\right\rvert)
. So summing this bound over all chunks of the partition, we get

\lvert\mathcal{R}\rvert=\sum_{m}I(\Pi,\Gamma_{m})=O(\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{18/11+2\eta}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{14/11+3\eta}\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{12/11}\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert^{2/11}).

Now combining this with the lower bound
\lvert\mathcal{R}\rvert=\Omega(\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}\lvert\mathcal{Q}\rvert)
gives

(3.6)

\lvert\mathcal{Q}\rvert=O(\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{-4/11+2\eta}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{14/11+3\eta}\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{12/11}\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert^{2/11}).

Our goal was originally to get an upper bound on
\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert
, and (
3.6
) bounds
\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert
in terms of
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}\right\rvert
, so now we want an upper bound on
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}\right\rvert
. In the setting of Theorem
1.5
, where we do not have any information about the additive structure of
B
, we simply use the crude bound

\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert=O(\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{3})

(this bound comes from the fact that once we choose
b_{1}
,
b_{2}
, and
c_{1}
, there are
O(1)
choices for
c_{2}
such that
h(b_{1})+h(c_{1})=h(b_{2})+h(c_{2})
). This ends up giving (
3.1
) (or rather, its first term), our weaker version of Theorem
1.5
.

Meanwhile, in the setting of Theorem
1.6
, where we
do
have information about the additive structure of
B
, we can do better than this crude bound. The point is that
\mathcal{S}
is almost exactly the analog of
\mathcal{Q}
if we were working with the polynomial
h(x)+h(y)
on
B\times B
, rather than with
f(x,y)=g(x+p(y))+h(y)
on
A\times B
(except that we exclude bad pairs from
\mathcal{S}
). And so far our argument has given an upper bound on
\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert
in terms of
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}\right\rvert
, so if we run it
again
for this new setting, then we will end up with a similar upper bound on
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}\right\rvert
in terms of
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}\right\rvert
itself.

So we define the set

\mathcal{T}=\{(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},b_{1}^{\prime},b_{2}^{\prime},c_{1},c_{2})\in(B+B)^{2}\times B^{2}\times B^{2}\mid\\
h(\beta_{1}-b_{1}^{\prime})+h(c_{1})=h(\beta_{2}-b_{2}^{\prime})+h(c_{2}),\text{$(c_{1},c_{2})$ not bad}\},

which is the analog of
\mathcal{R}
for this new setting. Then we have
\lvert\mathcal{T}\rvert\geq\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2}\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert
, while we can get an upper bound on
\left\lvert\mathcal{T}\right\rvert
by setting up an incidence problem in the same way as before. Combining these bounds gives

\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert=O(\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{10/11+5\eta}\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert^{12/11}\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert^{2/11})

(this is the same bound as in (
3.6
), but with
A
replaced with
B
and
\mathcal{Q}
with
\mathcal{S}
). And now we can cancel out
\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert^{2/11}
from the right-hand side to get

(3.7)

\left\lvert\mathcal{S}\right\rvert=O(\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{10/9+7\eta}\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert^{4/3}),

and plug this upper bound into (
3.6
) to get an upper bound on
\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert
. This ends up giving (
3.2
) (or rather, its first term), our weaker version of Theorem
1.6
.

4.
Outline of the full proof

In this section, we give an outline of the proofs of Theorem
1.5
and
1.6
, in which we state the key steps as lemmas; we will prove these lemmas in the following sections.

As in Section
3
, we set
d=\deg f
, and we let
\eta
denote a small error parameter meant to keep track of subpolynomial factors, which we will eventually take to be sufficiently small relative to
\varepsilon
. The implicit constants in our asymptotic notation may depend on
d
and
\eta
, but not anything else.

As mentioned in Subsection
1.6
, the way we improve the weaker bounds in Section
3
is by using proximity. Roughly speaking, instead of working with
\mathcal{R}
itself, we work with a ‘proximity-restricted’ variant where we require the two elements of each of the pairs
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
,
(a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime})
, and
(b_{1},b_{2})
to be ‘close’ to each other.

To state such a proximity condition on pairs from a set
X
, we write
X=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\}
with
x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots<x_{n}
. For
x\in X
, we let
i_{X}(x)
denote the index of
x
in
X
(i.e., the index
i
for which
x=x_{i}
). For example, if
X=\{1,2,5,6,7\}
, then
i_{X}(6)=4
. For a parameter
t\in[0,1]
, we say a pair
(x_{1},x_{2})\in X^{2}
is
t
-close
if

\left\lvert i_{X}(x_{1})-i_{X}(x_{2})\right\rvert\leq t\left\lvert X\right\rvert.

If
t\left\lvert X\right\rvert<1
, then this definition is silly; but if
t\left\lvert X\right\rvert\geq 1
, then a
\Theta(t)
-fraction of pairs
(x_{1},x_{2})\in X^{2}
are
t
-close. Throughout the argument, we leave
t
as a parameter and assume that
t\left\lvert A\right\rvert,t\left\lvert B\right\rvert\geq 1
. When we set a value of
t
in Subsection
8.1
, we will verify that it satisfies these inequalities.

Then the proximity-restricted variant of
\mathcal{R}
we work with is

\mathcal{R}_{t}=\{(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime},b_{1},b_{2})\in(A+A)^{2}\times A^{2}\times B^{2}\mid f(\alpha_{1}-a_{1}^{\prime},b_{1})=f(\alpha_{2}-a_{2}^{\prime},b_{2}),\\
\text{$(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})$, $(a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime})$, $(b_{1},b_{2})$ $t$-close, $(b_{1},b_{2})$ not bad}\}.

Explicitly, these proximity conditions mean that

\displaystyle\left\lvert i_{A+A}(\alpha_{1})-i_{A+A}(\alpha_{2})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq t\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert,

\displaystyle\left\lvert i_{A}(a_{1}^{\prime})-i_{A}(a_{2}^{\prime})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq t\left\lvert A\right\rvert,

\displaystyle\left\lvert i_{B}(b_{1})-i_{B}(b_{2})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq t\left\lvert B\right\rvert.

For example, when we say
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
is
t
-close, the ambient set is
A+A
.

In Section
3
, we had the lower bound
\lvert\mathcal{R}\rvert=\Omega(\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert)
. The following lemma shows that the proximity conditions do not shrink this lower bound by too much.

Lemma 4.1
.

We have
\lvert\mathcal{R}_{t}\rvert=\Omega(t^{2}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}\lvert\mathcal{Q}\rvert)-O(t\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{3}\left\lvert B\right\rvert)
.

More explicitly, this notation means that there are constants
c,c^{\prime}>0
such that

(4.1)

\lvert\mathcal{R}_{t}\rvert\geq c\cdot t^{2}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}\lvert\mathcal{Q}\rvert-c^{\prime}\cdot t\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{3}\left\lvert B\right\rvert.

The second term in Lemma
4.1
comes from the removal of bad pairs, and we will eventually set
t
large enough to make this term negligible. When focusing on the first term, this bound captures the intuition that our proximity conditions are ‘correlated’ — even though we are imposing
three
proximity conditions and each shrinks a set of pairs by a factor of
t
, we only lose
two
factors of
t
in the lower bound. (This is, very loosely, the intuition behind why proximity leads to a better bound.)

We next set up an incidence problem corresponding to
\mathcal{R}_{t}
and upper-bound
\lvert\mathcal{R}_{t}\rvert
using incidence bounds, in the same way as in Section
3
. There, curve multiplicities in our incidence problem for
\mathcal{R}
were controlled by
\mathcal{S}
. Here they will be controlled by the set

(4.2)

\mathcal{S}_{t}=\{(b_{1},b_{2},c_{1},c_{2})\in B^{2}\times B^{2}\mid h(b_{1})+h(c_{1})=h(b_{2})+h(c_{2}),\\
\text{$(b_{1},b_{2})$, $(c_{1},c_{2})$ $t$-close and not bad}\},

which is a proximity-restricted variant of
\mathcal{S}
. Then this incidence argument leads to the following upper bound on
\lvert\mathcal{R}_{t}\rvert
.

Lemma 4.2
.

We have
\lvert\mathcal{R}_{t}\rvert=O((\mathrm{I})+(\mathrm{II})+(\mathrm{III})+(\mathrm{IV}))
, where

\displaystyle(\mathrm{I})

\displaystyle=t^{2}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{18/11+2\eta}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{14/11+3\eta}\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{12/11}\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\rvert^{2/11},

\displaystyle(\mathrm{III})

\displaystyle=t\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert B\right\rvert,

\displaystyle(\mathrm{II})

\displaystyle=t^{5/3}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{4/3}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2/3+\eta}\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{4/3}\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\rvert^{1/3},

\displaystyle(\mathrm{IV})

\displaystyle=t^{2}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2}.

To finish the argument, we need an upper bound on
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\right\rvert
. For the proof of Theorem
1.5
, we use a crude bound similar to the one in Section
3
— we have

\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\rvert=O(t\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{3}).

This is because if we choose
b_{1}
and
c_{1}
, then there are
\Theta(t\left\lvert B\right\rvert)
choices for
b_{2}
such that
(b_{1},b_{2})
is
t
-close, and
O(1)
choices for
c_{2}
such that
h(b_{1})+h(c_{1})=h(b_{2})+h(c_{2})
. If we plug this bound on
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\right\rvert
into Lemma
4.2
and combine it with Lemma
4.1
, and set

t=\Theta\left(\frac{\left\lvert A\right\rvert\left\lvert B\right\rvert}{\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert}\right),

we end up with Theorem
1.5
. (The reason for this choice is that it is the smallest we can make
t
while ensuring that the subtraction in Lemma
4.1
is negligible.)

For the proof of Theorem
1.6
, we will use the additive structure of
B
to get a better bound on
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\right\rvert
. Unlike in Section
3
, we cannot do so by directly reusing the above argument (where we used the additive structure of
A
to get an upper bound on
\lvert\mathcal{Q}\rvert
in terms of
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\right\rvert
), because it would not maintain the proximity restriction. The issue is that if we defined a proximity-restricted variant of
\mathcal{T}
in the same way as
\mathcal{R}_{t}
(our proximity-restricted variant of
\mathcal{R}
), the analog of Lemma
4.1
would only give a lower bound on its size in terms of
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}\right\rvert
, not
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\right\rvert
.

We overcome the above issue by defining a more complicated proximity-restricted variant of
\mathcal{T}
whose size we
can
lower-bound in terms of
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\right\rvert
(and can again upper-bound using incidences). Combining these bounds leads to the following upper bound on
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\right\rvert
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Assuming that
\deg h\geq 2
and
\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{4/3}
, we have

\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\rvert=O(t^{11/9}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{10/9+8\eta}\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert^{4/3}).

Up to subpolynomial factors, the bound on
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\right\rvert
in Lemma
4.3
matches (
3.7
) (our upper bound on
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}\right\rvert
from Section
3
), with an additional factor of
t^{11/9}
. Finally, plugging this bound on
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\right\rvert
into Lemma
4.2
and combining it with Lemma
4.1
, and setting the value of
t
, gives Theorem
1.6
.

We prove Lemma
4.1
in Section
5
, Lemma
4.2
in Section
6
, and Lemma
4.3
in Section
7
; in Section
8
, we put these lemmas together to deduce Theorems
1.5
and
1.6
.

5.
A lower bound on
\mathcal{R}_{t}

In this section, we prove Lemma
4.1
, which states that adding the proximity conditions to
\mathcal{R}
does not decrease the lower bound on its size by too much. The proof consists of two fairly disjoint parts. We will use the following lemma to handle the proximity conditions. (For the definition of a monotone set in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, see Subsection
2.3
.)

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
M\subseteq A\times B
be monotone. Then there are
\Omega(t^{2}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert M\right\rvert^{2})

6
-tuples

(a_{1},a_{2},a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime},b_{1},b_{2})\in A^{2}\times A^{2}\times B^{2}

with
(a_{1},b_{1}),(a_{2},b_{2})\in M
such that all the pairs
(a_{1},a_{2})\in A^{2}
,
(a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime})\in A^{2}
,
(a_{1}+a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}+a_{2}^{\prime})\in(A+A)^{2}
, and
(b_{1},b_{2})\in B^{2}
are
t
-close.

When ignoring the proximity conditions, there are exactly
\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert M\right\rvert^{2}
such
6
-tuples; so Lemma
5.1
shows that the proximity conditions only cost a factor of
t^{2}
.

We will use the following lemma to handle the exclusion of bad pairs.

Lemma 5.2
.

We have
\left\lvert\{(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\in\mathcal{Q}\mid\text{$(b_{1},b_{2})$ bad}\}\right\rvert=O(\left\lvert A\right\rvert\left\lvert B\right\rvert)
.

We prove Lemma
5.1
in Subsection
5.1
and Lemma
5.2
in Subsection
5.2
. In Subsection
5.3
we put them together to deduce Lemma
4.1
.

5.1.
Handling the proximity conditions

In this subsection, we prove Lemma
5.1
. To do so, we first define a function
\varphi\colon A\to\mathbb{R}
as

\varphi(a)=\frac{\sum_{a^{\prime}\in A}i_{A+A}(a+a^{\prime})}{\left\lvert A\right\rvert}.

Since
\varphi(a)
is the average of various indices of elements of
A+A
, it takes values in
[1,\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert]
. Also,
\varphi
is increasing, since increasing
a
only increases each term
a+a^{\prime}
in this sum (and therefore its index in
A+A
).

Let the elements of
A
be
x_{1}<\cdots<x_{\left\lvert A\right\rvert}
, and let the pairs in
M
be
(w_{1},z_{1})
, …,
(w_{\left\lvert M\right\rvert},z_{\left\lvert M\right\rvert})
, sorted so that
w_{1}\leq\cdots\leq w_{\left\lvert M\right\rvert}
and either
z_{1}\leq\cdots\leq z_{\left\lvert M\right\rvert}
or
z_{1}\geq\cdots\geq z_{\left\lvert M\right\rvert}
(we can sort
M
in this way because
M
is monotone). Finally, let
k=\lfloor\frac{1}{32}t\left\lvert M\right\rvert\rfloor
and
\ell=\lfloor\frac{1}{8}t\left\lvert A\right\rvert\rfloor
.

Claim 5.3
.

There are at least
\frac{1}{2}\left\lvert M\right\rvert
indices
1\leq i\leq\left\lvert M\right\rvert-k
for which we have

(5.1)

\displaystyle i_{A}(y_{i+k})-i_{A}(y_{i})

\displaystyle\leq t\left\lvert A\right\rvert,

\displaystyle\left\lvert i_{B}(z_{i})-i_{B}(z_{i+k})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq t\left\lvert B\right\rvert,

\displaystyle\varphi(y_{i+k})-\varphi(y_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{t\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert}{8}.

Proof.

For each of these three conditions, we will use an averaging argument to show that only a small fraction of indices
i

fail
to satisfy the condition. For the first condition, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lvert M\right\rvert-k}(i_{A}(y_{i+k})-i_{A}(y_{i}))\leq k\left\lvert A\right\rvert\leq\frac{t\left\lvert M\right\rvert\left\lvert A\right\rvert}{32},

since this sum telescopes and leaves
k
positive and
k
negative terms, each of which is at most
\left\lvert A\right\rvert
. Since each term in the sum is nonnegative, at most
\frac{1}{32}\left\lvert M\right\rvert
of its terms can exceed
t\left\lvert A\right\rvert
.

The same reasoning shows that at most
\frac{1}{32}\left\lvert M\right\rvert
indices fail to satisfy the second condition, and that at most
\frac{1}{4}\left\lvert M\right\rvert
indices fail to satisfy the third. So we are left with at least

\left\lvert M\right\rvert-k-\frac{1}{32}\left\lvert M\right\rvert-\frac{1}{32}\left\lvert M\right\rvert-\frac{1}{4}\left\lvert M\right\rvert\geq\frac{1}{2}\left\lvert M\right\rvert

indices which satisfy all three conditions.
∎

Claim 5.4
.

Suppose that
a_{1},a_{2}\in A
are such that
a_{1}\leq a_{2}
and
\varphi(a_{2})-\varphi(a_{1})\leq\frac{1}{8}t\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert
. Then there are at least
\frac{1}{2}\left\lvert A\right\rvert
indices
1\leq i\leq\left\lvert A\right\rvert-\ell
such that

(5.2)

i_{A+A}(a_{2}+x_{i+\ell})-i_{A+A}(a_{1}+x_{i})\leq t\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert.

Proof.

Similarly to the proof of Claim
5.3
, we use an averaging argument to show that only a small fraction of indices
i
fail to satisfy this condition. For this, we consider

\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lvert A\right\rvert-\ell}(i_{A+A}(a_{2}+x_{i+\ell})-i_{A+A}(a_{1}+x_{i})),

which we can split as

(5.3)

\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lvert A\right\rvert-\ell}(i_{A+A}(a_{2}+x_{i+\ell})-i_{A+A}(a_{2}+x_{i}))+\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lvert A\right\rvert-\ell}(i_{A+A}(a_{2}+x_{i})-i_{A+A}(a_{1}+x_{i})).

The first sum telescopes and leaves
\ell
positive and negative terms, each between
1
and
\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert
, so

\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lvert A\right\rvert-\ell}(i_{A+A}(a_{2}+x_{i+\ell})-i_{A+A}(a_{2}+x_{i}))\leq\ell\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert\leq\frac{t\left\lvert A\right\rvert\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert}{8}.

For the second term, by assumption we have

\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lvert A\right\rvert}(i_{A+A}(a_{2}+x_{i})-i_{A+A}(a_{1}+x_{i}))=\left\lvert A\right\rvert(\varphi(a_{2})-\varphi(a_{1}))\leq\frac{t\left\lvert A\right\rvert\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert}{8}.

The second term of (
5.3
) is missing the last few terms of this sum, but the absence of these terms can only make it smaller, so it satisfies the same upper bound. This means

\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lvert A\right\rvert-\ell}(i_{A+A}(a_{2}+x_{i+\ell})-i_{A+A}(a_{1}+x_{i}))\leq\frac{t\left\lvert A\right\rvert\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert}{4},

and since all terms in this sum are nonnegative, at most
\frac{1}{4}\left\lvert A\right\rvert
of them can exceed
t\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert
. This means at most
\frac{1}{4}\left\lvert A\right\rvert
indices
i
fail to satisfy our condition, so at least

\left\lvert A\right\rvert-\ell-\frac{1}{4}\left\lvert A\right\rvert\geq\frac{1}{2}\left\lvert A\right\rvert

indices do satisfy it.
∎

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

To produce a
6
-tuple
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime},b_{1},b_{2})
as in Lemma
5.1
, we first choose an index

satisfying (

), which can be done in at least

(a_{1},b_{1})=(y_{i},z_{i})\quad\text{and}\quad(a_{2},b_{2})=(y_{j},z_{j}).

Then
a_{1}\leq a_{2}
, and (
5.1
) means that
(a_{1},a_{2})
and
(b_{1},b_{2})
are
t
-close and

\varphi(a_{2})-\varphi(a_{1})\leq\frac{t\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert}{8}.

(Here we are using the fact that if (
5.1
) holds, then it still holds if we replace the index
i+k
with any

Now we choose an index

satisfying (
5.2

\frac{1}{2}\left\lvert A\right\rvert

, and an index

Since

The above process shows that the number of

5.2.
Handling bad pairs

In this subsection, we prove Lemma
5.2
, our upper bound on the contribution of bad pairs to
\mathcal{Q}
. Recall that
(b_{1},b_{2})\in B^{2}
is bad if and only if either
g(x)-g(y)+h(b_{1})-h(b_{2})
or
h(x)-h(y)+h(b_{1})-h(b_{2})
is reducible. We will first use Theorem
2.2
to bound the
number
of bad pairs; to apply this theorem, we need the following observation.

Claim 5.5
.

For every nonconstant
p\in\mathbb{R}[x]
, the polynomial
p(x)-p(y)
is indecomposable.

Proof.

Assume for contradiction that
p(x)-p(y)
is decomposable, so we can write

p(x)-p(y)=q(r(x,y))

for some polynomials
r\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
and
q\in\mathbb{R}[z]
with
\deg q\geq 2
. Let the leading coefficients of
p
and
q
be
c_{p}
and
c_{q}
, and let
r_{*}
be the sum of the monomials in
r
of degree exactly
\deg r
. Then the sum of the maximal-degree monomials in
q(r(x,y))
is
c_{q}r_{*}(x,y)^{\deg q}
, while the sum of the maximal-degree monomials in
p(x)-p(y)
is
c_{p}(x^{\deg p}-y^{\deg p})
, so we have

c_{p}(x^{\deg p}-y^{\deg p})=c_{q}r_{*}(x,y)^{\deg q}.

Since the two sides must have the same degree, we have
\deg p=(\deg q)(\deg r)
; then letting
z=\frac{x}{y}
and dividing by
y^{\deg p}
gives

c_{p}(z^{\deg p}-1)=c_{q}r_{*}(z,1)^{\deg q}.

In particular, this means some multiple of
z^{\deg p}-1
is the
(\deg q)
th power of some polynomial (namely
r_{*}(z,1)
). But this is impossible, as its complex roots all have multiplicities
1
, rather than multiplicities divisible by
\deg q
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
5.2
.

Claim
5.5
allows us to apply Theorem
2.2
to
g(x)-g(y)
and
h(x)-h(y)
. Then by Theorem
2.2
, at most
\deg g+\deg h\leq 2d
values
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
satisfy that
g(x)-g(y)+\lambda
or
h(x)-h(y)+\lambda
is reducible, so there are at most
2d
possible values of
h(b_{1})-h(b_{2})
among bad pairs
(b_{1},b_{2})
. Furthermore, given the values of
h(b_{1})-h(b_{2})
and
b_{1}
, there are at most
d
ways to choose
b_{2}
. So there are at most
2d\cdot\left\lvert B\right\rvert\cdot d=O(\left\lvert B\right\rvert)
bad pairs.

Finally, once we have chosen a bad pair
(b_{1},b_{2})
, in order to have
(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\in\mathcal{Q}
we must have
f(a_{1},b_{1})=f(a_{2},b_{2})
, i.e.,

g(a_{1}+p(b_{1}))+h(b_{1})=g(a_{2}+p(b_{2}))+h(b_{2}).

This means after choosing
a_{1}
, there are at most
d=O(1)
choices for
a_{2}
.

So in total, the number of
4
-tuples in
\mathcal{Q}
for which
(b_{1},b_{2})
is bad is
O(\left\lvert B\right\rvert\cdot\left\lvert A\right\rvert\cdot 1)=O(\left\lvert A\right\rvert\left\lvert B\right\rvert)
.
∎

5.3.
Proof of Lemma
4.1

We first define

\text{$(a_{1},a_{2})$, $(a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime})$, $(a_{1}+a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}+a_{2}^{\prime})$, $(b_{1},b_{2})$ $t$-close}\}.

First, we can use Lemma
5.1
to prove a lower bound on

Claim 5.6
.

We have

.

Proof.

We will consider each
\delta\in f(A,B)
separately and show that the number of elements of
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{t}}
where
f(a_{1},b_{1})=f(a_{2},b_{2})=\delta

\Omega(t^{2}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2})

\mathcal{Q}
with

(which we refer to as its contribution to
\mathcal{Q}
). To do so, first define

K_{\delta}=\{(a,b)\in A\times B\mid f(a,b)=\delta\};

then the contribution of
\delta
to
\mathcal{Q}

.

Meanwhile, by Fact
2.3

∎

Now given any

where

is not bad, we can produce an element of

.

6.
An incidence problem and upper bound on
\mathcal{R}_{t}

In this section, we prove Lemma
4.2
, our upper bound on
\lvert\mathcal{R}_{t}\rvert
. We will do so by setting up a collection of points
\Pi
and curves
\Gamma
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
whose incidences correspond to elements of
\mathcal{R}_{t}
, and then using Theorem
2.1
to bound the number of incidences.

As in Section
3
, we create a point for each pair
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
and a curve for each
4
-tuple
(a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime},b_{1},b_{2})
. We define the set of points

\Pi=\{(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\in(A+A)^{2}\mid\text{$(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})$ $t$-close}\}.

We define a multiset
\Gamma
of curves as follows. For each
(a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime},b_{1},b_{2})\in A^{2}\times B^{2}
such that
(a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime})
and

g(x_{1}-a_{1}^{\prime}+p(b_{1}))+h(b_{1})=g(x_{2}-a_{2}^{\prime}+p(b_{2}))+h(b_{2}).

Then
\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert=\Theta(t\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{2})
and
\left\lvert\Gamma\right\rvert=O(t^{2}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2})

\lvert\mathcal{R}_{t}\rvert=I(\Pi,\Gamma)
.

As in Section
3
, the curves of

belong to a

a_{1}^{\prime}-p(b_{1})
,
a_{2}^{\prime}-p(b_{2})

h(b_{1})-h(b_{2})
. Since we exclude bad pairs
(b_{1},b_{2})

are irreducible. So to be able to apply Theorem

As seen in Section

, the equation of a curve in

uniquely determines the values of
a_{1}^{\prime}-p(b_{1})
,
a_{2}^{\prime}-p(b_{2})
, and

a_{1}^{\prime}
,
a_{2}^{\prime}
,
b_{1}
, and
b_{2}
with the three above parameters fixed. First, there are at most

choices for
b_{2}

a_{1}^{\prime}
and
a_{2}^{\prime}
are uniquely determined. So the multiplicity of each curve is

We now dyadically partition
\Gamma
based on curve multiplicities, as in Section
3
: For each
m\in\{2^{0},2^{1},\ldots\}

\Gamma_{m}
be the portion of
\Gamma
consisting of curves with multiplicities in

\Gamma
, so that
I(\Pi,\Gamma)=\sum_{m}I(\Pi,\Gamma_{m})
. Then for each
m
, we let

be the variant of

where we include curves only once, so that

. Then Theorem
2.1
applied to
\Pi
and
\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}
implies that

(6.1)

I(\Pi,\Gamma_{m})=O(m\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert^{6/11}\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert^{9/11+\eta}+m\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert^{2/3}\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert^{2/3}+m\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert+m\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert).

To obtain an upper bound on

, we sum (

) over
m
; we handle the contribution of each term of (
6.1
) to this sum separately.

For the fourth term, we have
m\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert\leq\left\lvert\Gamma_{m}\right\rvert
for each

(\mathrm{IV})
in Lemma
4.2
.

When summing the third term
m\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert
over
m
, we obtain a geometric progression that sums to

O(\left\lvert B\right\rvert\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert)=O(t\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert B\right\rvert),

corresponding to the term
(\mathrm{III})
.

For the first and second terms of (

), we will combine the fact that

m\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert\leq\lvert\Gamma\rvert=O(t^{2}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2})

with the following bound on

Claim 6.1
.

For each

, we have

.

Proof.

We count pairs of
4
-tuples
(a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime},b_{1},b_{2})
and

\displaystyle a_{1}^{\prime}-p(b_{1})

\displaystyle=a_{3}^{\prime}-p(b_{3}),

\displaystyle a_{2}^{\prime}-p(b_{2})

\displaystyle=a_{4}^{\prime}-p(b_{4}),

\displaystyle h(b_{1})-h(b_{2})

(b_{1},b_{2},b_{4},b_{3})\in\mathcal{S}_{t}

and
(b_{1},b_{2})

\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\rvert

,
b_{2}
,
b_{3}
, and
b_{4}

Then to deal with the first term of (

m\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert^{9/11}=(m\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert)^{7/11}(m^{2}\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert)^{2/11}=O(t^{16/11}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{18/11}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{14/11}\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\rvert^{2/11}).

7.
An upper bound on
\mathcal{S}_{t}

We now prove Lemma
4.3
, our upper bound on
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\right\rvert
when
B
has additive structure. We assume in this section that
\deg h\geq 2
and
\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{4/3}
, as in the hypothesis of Lemma

.

7.1.
Overview of the proof

We first give an overview of the proof, where we explain the main ideas and state the key steps as lemmas; we prove these lemmas in the following subsections.

The motivation behind the proof is that
\mathcal{S}_{t}
is essentially an analog of
\mathcal{Q}
for the polynomial
h(x)+h(y)
on
B\times B
(in place of
f(x,y)
on
A\times B
), but with additional proximity conditions — recall that we defined

\mathcal{Q}=\{(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\in A^{2}\times B^{2}\mid f(a_{1},b_{1})=f(a_{2},b_{2})\},

while we defined

\mathcal{S}_{t}=\{(b_{1},b_{2},c_{1},c_{2})\in B^{2}\times B^{2}\mid h(b_{1})+h(c_{1})=h(b_{2})+h(c_{2}),\\
\text{$(b_{1},b_{2})$, $(c_{1},c_{2})$ $t$-close and not bad}\}.

So far (i.e., in Sections
5
and
6
), we have proven an upper bound on
\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert
in terms of
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\right\rvert
by defining

\mathcal{R}_{t}=\{(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime},b_{1},b_{2})\in(A+A)^{2}\times A^{2}\times B^{2}\mid f(\alpha_{1}-a_{1}^{\prime},b_{1})=f(\alpha_{2}-a_{2}^{\prime},b_{2}),\\
\text{$(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})$, $(a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime})$, $(b_{1},b_{2})$ $t$-close, $(b_{1},b_{2})$ not bad}\},

proving a lower bound on
\left\lvert\mathcal{R}_{t}\right\rvert
in terms of
\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert
(Lemma
4.1
), and proving an upper bound on
\left\lvert\mathcal{R}_{t}\right\rvert
in terms of
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\right\rvert
using incidence bounds (Lemma
4.2
). We will use a similar approach to prove an upper bound on
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\right\rvert
in terms of
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\right\rvert
itself — we will define a set somewhat analogously to
\mathcal{R}_{t}
for the polynomial
h(x)+h(y)
, prove a lower bound on its size in terms of
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\right\rvert
, and prove an upper bound on its size using incidence bounds.

However, we cannot define this set
completely
analogously to
\mathcal{R}_{t}
, because unlike in Lemma
4.1
(which gave a lower bound on
\left\lvert\mathcal{R}_{t}\right\rvert
in terms of
\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert
), here we need to incorporate the proximity conditions in
\mathcal{S}_{t}
into our lower bound. So we instead define an analog of
\mathcal{R}_{t}
with ‘looser’ proximity restrictions, so that we
are
able to get a good lower bound on its size in terms of
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\right\rvert
. To do so, we first define

P=\{(b_{1},b_{2})\in B^{2}\mid\text{$(b_{1},b_{2})$ $t$-close and not bad}\},

so that
\mathcal{S}_{t}
is a subset of
P\times P
. We first need to find a subset
R\times S\subseteq P\times P
such that each pair
(b_{1},b_{2})\in R
corresponds to roughly the same number of pairs
(c_{1},c_{2})\in S
— we will define our analog of
\mathcal{R}_{t}
by only considering the portion of
\mathcal{S}_{t}
that lies in
R\times S
, and we will use this property to prove the desired lower bound. Intuitively,
R
being small will weaken our final bound on
\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\rvert
by forcing us to use looser proximity conditions, while
S
being small will strengthen our bound by allowing us to use fewer curves in the incidence problem. The following lemma (whose proof crucially relies on the symmetry of our setting) allows us to ensure that these sets are the
same
size, so that these considerations balance each other out.

Lemma 7.1
.

There exist sets
R,S\subseteq P
such that the following statements hold:

•

We have
\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\cap(R\times S)\rvert=\Omega(\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{-\eta}\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\rvert)
.

•

There is some
m
such that for each pair
(b_{1},b_{2})\in R
, there are between
m
and
2m
pairs
(c_{1},c_{2})\in S
for which
(b_{1},b_{2},c_{1},c_{2})\in\mathcal{S}_{t}
.

•

We have
\left\lvert R\right\rvert=\left\lvert S\right\rvert
.

Then we define
\mathcal{S}_{t}^{*}=\mathcal{S}_{t}\cap(R\times S)
, and we let

r=\max\left\{\frac{\left\lvert R\right\rvert}{64t\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2}},t\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad s=\frac{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}{64t\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2}}.

Note that
r,s\in(0,1)
and
r\geq s
. The analog of
\mathcal{R}_{t}
we work with is the set

\mathcal{T}_{t,r}=\{(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},b_{1}^{\prime},b_{2}^{\prime},c_{1},c_{2})\in(B+B)^{2}\times B^{2}\times S\mid\\
h(\beta_{1}+b_{1}^{\prime})+h(c_{1})=h(\beta_{2}+b_{2}^{\prime})+h(c_{2}),\,\text{$(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})$, $(b_{1}^{\prime},b_{2}^{\prime})$ $\tfrac{t}{r}$-close}\}.

Compared to the direct analog of
\mathcal{R}_{t}
, we have weakened the proximity conditions on
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})
and
(b_{1}^{\prime},b_{2}^{\prime})
based on how small
R
is. We also draw
(c_{1},c_{2})
only from
S
, rather than just requiring it to be
t
-close and not bad (which would correspond to drawing it from
P
).

We will first prove the following lower bound on
\lvert\mathcal{T}_{t,r}\rvert
in terms of
\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}^{*}\rvert
.

Lemma 7.2
.

We have
\lvert\mathcal{T}_{t,r}\rvert=\Omega(\frac{t}{r}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2}\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}^{*}\rvert)
.

We will then use incidence bounds to prove the following upper bound on
\lvert\mathcal{T}_{t,r}\rvert
.

Lemma 7.3
.

We have
\lvert\mathcal{T}_{t,r}\rvert=O((\mathrm{I})+(\mathrm{II})+(\mathrm{III})+(\mathrm{IV}))
, where

\displaystyle(\mathrm{I})

\displaystyle=\frac{s^{7/11}t^{2}}{r^{15/11}}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{32/11+5\eta}\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert^{12/11}\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\rvert^{2/11},

\displaystyle(\mathrm{III})

\displaystyle=\frac{t}{r}\left\lvert B\right\rvert\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert^{2},

\displaystyle(\mathrm{II})

\displaystyle=\frac{s^{1/3}t^{5/3}}{r^{4/3}}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2+\eta}\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert^{4/3}\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\rvert^{1/3},

\displaystyle(\mathrm{IV})

\displaystyle=\frac{st^{2}}{r}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{4}.

We prove Lemma
7.1
in Subsection
7.2
, Lemma
7.2
in Subsection
7.3
, and Lemma
7.3
in Subsection
7.4
; in Subsection
7.5
, we combine these bounds to deduce Lemma
4.3
.

7.2.
Restricting the host sets

In this subsection, we prove Lemma
7.1
. To do so, we rewrite the definition of
\mathcal{S}_{t}
as

\mathcal{S}_{t}=\{(b_{1},b_{2},c_{1},c_{2})\in P\times P\mid h(b_{1})-h(b_{2})=h(c_{2})-h(c_{1})\}.

We then split
\mathcal{S}_{t}
based on the common value of
h(b_{1})-h(b_{2})
and
h(c_{2})-h(c_{1})
, which we denote by
\delta
. More precisely, for each
\delta\in h(B)-h(B)
, we define

P_{\delta}=\{(b_{1},b_{2})\in P\mid h(b_{1})-h(b_{2})=\delta\},

so that
\mathcal{S}_{t}=\bigcup_{\delta}(P_{\delta}\times P_{-\delta})
. Note that
P
is symmetric, meaning that
(b_{2},b_{1})
is in
P
if and only if
(b_{1},b_{2})
is. This means
\left\lvert P_{\delta}\right\rvert=\left\lvert P_{-\delta}\right\rvert
for every
\delta
, so

(7.1)

\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\rvert=\sum_{\delta}\left\lvert P_{\delta}\right\rvert^{2}.

We now dyadically partition the values of
\delta
based on
\left\lvert P_{\delta}\right\rvert
— for each
m\in\{2^{0},2^{1},\ldots\}
, we define

\Delta_{m}=\{\delta\in h(B)-h(B)\mid\left\lvert P_{\delta}\right\rvert\in[m,2m)\}.

Since we have
\left\lvert P_{\delta}\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2}
for all
\delta
, there are
O(\log\left\lvert B\right\rvert)=O(\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{\eta})
parts in this dyadic partition, so there must be some
m
which accounts for an
\Omega(\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{-\eta})
-fraction of (
7.1
). In other words,

\sum_{\delta\in\Delta_{m}}\left\lvert P_{\delta}\right\rvert^{2}=\Omega(\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{-\eta}\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\rvert).

Finally, we fix such a value of
m
and set
R=\bigcup_{\delta\in\Delta_{m}}P_{\delta}
and
S=\bigcup_{\delta\in\Delta_{m}}P_{-\delta}
. These sets have the properties described in Lemma
7.1
— we have
\mathcal{S}_{t}\cap(R\times S)=\bigcup_{\delta\in\Delta_{m}}(P_{\delta}\times P_{-\delta})
, and the fact that
\lvert P_{\delta}\rvert=\lvert P_{-\delta}\rvert
for all
\delta
implies that
\left\lvert R\right\rvert=\left\lvert S\right\rvert
.

7.3.
A lower bound on
\mathcal{T}_{t,r}

In this subsection, we prove Lemma
7.2
, our lower bound on
\lvert\mathcal{T}_{t,r}\rvert
. First, to provide some intuition for Lemma
7.2
, we can try to produce a
6
-tuple in
\mathcal{T}_{t,r}
by starting with some
4
-tuple
(b_{1},b_{2},c_{1},c_{2})\in\mathcal{S}_{t}^{*}
, choosing a
\frac{t}{r}
-close pair
(b_{1}^{\prime},b_{2}^{\prime})\in B^{2}
, and setting
\beta_{1}=b_{1}+b_{1}^{\prime}
and
\beta_{2}=b_{2}+b_{2}^{\prime}
. There are

\Theta\left(\frac{t}{r}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2}\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}^{*}\rvert\right)

ways to make these choices, and the resulting
6
-tuple
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},b_{1}^{\prime},b_{2}^{\prime},c_{1},c_{2})
satisfies all the conditions in the definition of
\mathcal{T}_{t,r}
, except possibly the proximity condition on
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})
. Our proof of Lemma
7.2
will show that a positive proportion of
6
-tuples produced in this way do satisfy the proximity conditions on
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})
.

The definition of
r
implies that
r\geq t
. If
r=t
, then the desired statement is immediate, as the proximity condition on
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})
is vacuous. So it remains to consider the case where
r>t
, in which
\left\lvert R\right\rvert=64rt\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2}
.

For simplicity, we assume without loss of generality that at least half of the pairs
(b_{1},b_{2})\in R
satisfy
b_{1}\leq b_{2}
(otherwise we can swap the roles of the indices
1
and
2
). Let
R^{\prime}
be the subset of
R
consisting of these pairs, so that
\left\lvert R^{\prime}\right\rvert\geq 32rt\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2}
.

We will handle the proximity condition on
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})
in a similar way to how we handled the proximity condition on
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
in Section
5
. We define
\psi\colon B\to\mathbb{R}
as

\psi(b)=\frac{\sum_{b^{\prime}\in B}i_{B+B}(b+b^{\prime})}{\left\lvert B\right\rvert},

so that
\psi
is increasing and takes values in
[1,\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert]
.

Let the elements of
B
be
y_{1}<\cdots<y_{\left\lvert B\right\rvert}
, and let
k=\lfloor t\left\lvert B\right\rvert\rfloor
and
\ell=\lfloor\frac{t}{8r}\left\lvert B\right\rvert\rfloor
.

Claim 7.4
.

There are at most
4r\left\lvert B\right\rvert
indices
1\leq i\leq\left\lvert B\right\rvert-k
for which

(7.2)

\psi(y_{i+k})-\psi(y_{i})>\frac{t\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert}{4r}.

Proof.

We use an averaging argument similar to the one from Claim
5.3
. We have

\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lvert B\right\rvert-k}(\psi(y_{i+k})-\psi(y_{i}))\leq k\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert\leq t\left\lvert B\right\rvert\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert,

since this sum telescopes and leaves
k
positive and negative terms, each at most
\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert
. All terms in this sum are nonnegative, so at most
4r\left\lvert B\right\rvert
of them can be greater than
\frac{t}{4r}\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert
.
∎

Claim 7.5
.

Suppose that
b_{1}\leq b_{2}
and
\psi(b_{2})-\psi(b_{1})\leq\frac{t}{4r}\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert
. Then for at least
\frac{1}{2}\left\lvert B\right\rvert
indices
1\leq i\leq\left\lvert B\right\rvert-\ell
, we have

(7.3)

i_{B+B}(b_{2}+y_{i+\ell})-i_{B+B}(b_{1}+y_{i})\leq\frac{t\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert}{r}.

Proof.

Similarly to the proof of Claim
5.4
, we sum
i_{B+B}(b_{2}+y_{i+\ell})-i_{B+B}(b_{1}+y_{i})
over all
1\leq i\leq\left\lvert B\right\rvert-\ell
, and split this sum into

\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lvert B\right\rvert-\ell}(i_{B+B}(b_{2}+y_{i+\ell})-i_{B+B}(b_{2}+y_{i}))+\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lvert B\right\rvert-\ell}(i_{B+B}(b_{2}+y_{i})-i_{B+B}(b_{1}+y_{i})).

The first sum telescopes, so it is at most
\ell\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert\leq\frac{t}{8r}\left\lvert B\right\rvert\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert
, and the second sum is at most
\left\lvert B\right\rvert(\psi(b_{2})-\psi(b_{1}))\leq\frac{t}{4r}\left\lvert B\right\rvert\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert
. Combining these gives that

\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lvert B\right\rvert-\ell}(i_{B+B}(b_{2}+y_{i+\ell})-i_{B+B}(b_{1}+y_{i}))\leq\frac{3t\left\lvert B\right\rvert\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert}{8r}.

Finally, since all terms in this sum are nonnegative, at most
\frac{3}{8}\left\lvert B\right\rvert
of them can be greater than
\frac{t}{r}\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert
. So the number of indices
i
satisfying the given inequality is at least

\left\lvert B\right\rvert-\ell-\frac{3}{8}\left\lvert B\right\rvert\geq\frac{1}{2}\left\lvert B\right\rvert.\qed

Proof of Lemma
7.2
.

First, we say that an element
y_{i}\in B
is
friendly
if
1\leq i\leq\left\lvert B\right\rvert-k
and
i
satisfies (
7.2
). Otherwise, we say
y_{i}
is
unfriendly
. By Claim
7.4
and the fact that
t\leq r
, the number of unfriendly elements of
B
is at most

4r\left\lvert B\right\rvert+k\leq 4r\left\lvert B\right\rvert+t\left\lvert B\right\rvert\leq 8r\left\lvert B\right\rvert.

This means the number of pairs
(b_{1},b_{2})\in R^{\prime}
in which
b_{1}
is unfriendly is at most

8r\left\lvert B\right\rvert\cdot 2t\left\lvert B\right\rvert=16rt\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2},

since given
b_{1}
, there are at most
t\left\lvert B\right\rvert+1\leq 2t\left\lvert B\right\rvert
choices for
b_{2}
such that
b_{1}\leq b_{2}
and
(b_{1},b_{2})
is
t
-close. Then since
\left\lvert R^{\prime}\right\rvert\geq 32rt\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2}
, there are at least
16rt\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\left\lvert R\right\rvert
pairs
(b_{1},b_{2})\in R^{\prime}
for which
b_{1}
is friendly. By definition, any such pair satisfies
\psi(b_{2})-\psi(b_{1})\leq\frac{t}{4r}\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert
.

Then we can construct
6
-tuples
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},b_{1}^{\prime},b_{2}^{\prime},c_{1},c_{2})
in
\mathcal{T}_{t,r}
as follows. We first choose
(b_{1},b_{2})\in R^{\prime}
such that
b_{1}
is friendly, which can be done in at least
\frac{1}{4}\left\lvert R\right\rvert
ways. Then we choose an index
i
satisfying (
7.3
) (which can be done in at least
\frac{1}{2}\left\lvert B\right\rvert
ways by Claim
7.5
) and an index
i\leq j\leq i+\ell
(which can be done in
\ell+1\geq\frac{t}{8r}\left\lvert B\right\rvert
ways), and set
b_{1}^{\prime}=y_{i}
and
b_{2}^{\prime}=y_{j}
(this immediately means
(b_{1}^{\prime},b_{2}^{\prime})
is
\frac{t}{r}
-close). We then set
\beta_{1}=b_{1}+b_{1}^{\prime}
and
\beta_{2}=b_{2}+b_{2}^{\prime}
; then (
7.3
) means that
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})
is
\frac{t}{r}
-close. Finally, we choose some
(c_{1},c_{2})\in S
such that
(b_{1},b_{2},c_{1},c_{2})
is in
\mathcal{S}_{t}^{*}
; this can be done in at least
m
ways (where
m
is as in Lemma
7.1
). So this gives at least

\frac{1}{4}\left\lvert R\right\rvert\cdot\frac{1}{2}\left\lvert B\right\rvert\cdot\frac{t}{8r}\left\lvert B\right\rvert\cdot m=\frac{t}{64r}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2}\cdot m\left\lvert R\right\rvert

choices. Finally, we have
2m\left\lvert R\right\rvert\geq\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}^{*}\rvert
(since each
(b_{1},b_{2})\in R
corresponds to at most
2m
choices of
(c_{1},c_{2})\in S
for which
(b_{1},b_{2},c_{1},c_{2})\in\mathcal{S}_{t}^{*}
), giving that

\lvert\mathcal{T}_{t,r}\rvert\geq\frac{t}{128r}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2}\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}^{*}\rvert.\qed

7.4.
An incidence problem and upper bound on
\mathcal{T}_{t,r}

In this subsection, we prove Lemma
7.3
. As in Section
6
, we set up a collection of points
\Pi
and curves
\Gamma
whose incidences correspond to elements of
\mathcal{T}_{t,r}
, and use Theorem
2.1
to bound their number of incidences.

First, we define

\Pi=\{(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})\in(B+B)^{2}\mid\text{$(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})$ $\tfrac{t}{r}$-close}\}.

We then define
\Gamma
to be the multiset of curves where for each
(b_{1}^{\prime},b_{2}^{\prime},c_{1},c_{2})\in B^{2}\times S
such that
(b_{1}^{\prime},b_{2}^{\prime})
is
\frac{t}{r}
-close, we include the curve

h(x_{1}-b_{1}^{\prime})+h(c_{1})=h(x_{2}-b_{2}^{\prime})+h(c_{2}).

Then
\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert=\Theta(\frac{t}{r}\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert^{2})
and
\left\lvert\Gamma\right\rvert=O(\frac{t}{r}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert S\right\rvert)=O(\frac{st^{2}}{r}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{4})
, and by definition,
\lvert\mathcal{T}_{t,r}\rvert=I(\Pi,\Gamma)
.

As in Section
6
, the curves in
\Gamma
are irreducible and belong to a
3
-dimensional family, this time with parameters
b_{1}^{\prime}
,
b_{2}^{\prime}
, and
h(c_{1})-h(c_{2})
. So if we can control curve multiplicities, then we can apply Theorem
2.1
to bound
I(\Pi,\Gamma)
. As before, the equation of a curve in
\Gamma
uniquely determines
b_{1}^{\prime}
,
b_{2}^{\prime}
, and
h(c_{1})-h(c_{2})
(this is where we use the assumption that
\deg h\geq 2
); this means all curve multiplicities are
O(\left\lvert B\right\rvert)
.

We again dyadically partition
\Gamma
by curve multiplicity — for each
m\in\{2^{0},2^{1},\ldots\}
, we let
\Gamma_{m}
be the portion of
\Gamma
consisting of curves with multiplicities in
[m,2m)
; then
I(\Pi,\Gamma)=\sum_{m}I(\Pi,\Gamma_{m})
. For each
m
, we let
\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}
be the variant of
\Gamma_{m}
where we include each curve only once. Then
I(\Pi,\Gamma_{m})=\Theta(m\cdot I(\Pi,\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}))
, and we can use Theorem
2.1
to bound
I(\Pi,\Gamma_{m}^{\prime})
; this gives

(7.4)

I(\Pi,\Gamma_{m})=O(m\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert^{6/11}\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert^{9/11+\eta}+m\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert^{2/3}\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert^{2/3}+m\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert+m\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert).

We will deal with the sum of each term over
m
in the same way as in Section
6
. For the fourth term of (
7.4
), we have

\sum_{m}m\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert\leq\sum_{m}\lvert\Gamma_{m}\rvert=\left\lvert\Gamma\right\rvert=O\left(\frac{st^{2}}{r}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{4}\right),

giving the term
(\mathrm{IV})
of Lemma
7.3
. When summing the third term of (
7.4
) over
m
, we obtain a geometric sequence with largest element
O(\left\lvert B\right\rvert\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert)
. So in total, this contributes

O(\left\lvert B\right\rvert\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert)=O\left(\frac{t}{r}\left\lvert B\right\rvert\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert^{2}\right),

giving the term
(\mathrm{III})
of Lemma
7.3
. For the first and second terms of (
7.4
), we note that
m\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert\leq\left\lvert\Gamma\right\rvert=O(\frac{st^{2}}{r}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{4})
and

m^{2}\lvert\Gamma_{m}^{\prime}\rvert=O\left(\frac{t}{r}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2}\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\rvert\right).

The latter bound is obtained by repeating the proof of Claim
6.1
, considering pairs of
4
-tuples
(b_{1}^{\prime},b_{2}^{\prime},c_{1},c_{2})
and
(b_{3}^{\prime},b_{4}^{\prime},c_{3},c_{4})
corresponding to the same curve. Then by repeating the computations in the respective part of Section
6
, the sums of the first and second terms of (
7.4
) over
m
give the terms
(\mathrm{I})
and
(\mathrm{II})
of Lemma
7.3
, respectively.

7.5.
Finishing the proof

Finally, in this subsection we put together Lemmas
7.1
,
7.2
, and
7.3
to prove Lemma
4.3
, our upper bound on
\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\rvert
. First, combining Lemmas
7.1
and
7.2
gives

\lvert\mathcal{T}_{t,r}\rvert=\Omega\left(\frac{t}{r}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2-\eta}\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\rvert\right).

We now combine this with the upper bound on
\lvert\mathcal{T}_{t,r}\rvert
from Lemma
7.3
, splitting into cases according to the largest term of this upper bound.

Case 1 (term
(\mathrm{I})
is largest).
In this case, we get

\frac{t}{r}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2-\eta}\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\rvert=O\left(\frac{s^{7/11}t^{2}}{r^{15/11}}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{32/11+5\eta}\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert^{12/11}\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\rvert^{2/11}\right),

which rearranges to

\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\rvert=O\left(\frac{s^{7/9}t^{11/9}}{r^{4/9}}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{10/9+8\eta}\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert^{4/3}\right).

And since
r\geq s
and
r,s\leq 1
, we have
s^{7/9}\leq r^{4/9}
; so this means

\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\rvert=O(t^{11/9}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{10/9+8\eta}\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert^{4/3}),

giving the bound in Lemma
4.3
.

Case 2 (term
(\mathrm{II})
is largest).
In this case, a similar computation gives

\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\rvert=O\left(\frac{s^{1/2}t}{r^{1/2}}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{3\eta}\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert^{2}\right)=O(t\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{3\eta}\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert^{2}).

Using the facts that
t\left\lvert B\right\rvert\geq 1
and
\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{4/3}
, we can check that this is smaller than the bound from Case 1, as

t\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{3\eta}\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert^{2}\leq t^{11/9}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2/9+8\eta}\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert^{2}\leq t^{11/9}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{10/9+8\eta}\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert^{4/3}.

So Lemma
4.3
is true in this case as well.

Case 3 (term
(\mathrm{III})
is largest).
In this case, a similar computation gives

\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\rvert=O(\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{-1+\eta}\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert^{2}),

and since
t\left\lvert B\right\rvert\geq 1
, this is smaller than the bound from Case 2.

Case 4 (term
(\mathrm{IV})
is largest).
In this case, a similar computation gives

\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\rvert=O(st\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2+\eta}).

Since
s\leq 1
and
\left\lvert B\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert
, the above bound is also smaller than the bound from Case 2.

We conclude that Lemma
4.3
is true in all four cases.

8.
Completing the proofs

We now perform the final computations needed to complete the proofs of Theorems
1.5
and
1.6
.

8.1.
Setting the value of
t

First, we need to set the value of
t
that we will plug into our lemmas. Before doing so, we note that there is at most one
a\in\mathbb{R}
for which
g(a+p(y))+h(y)
is constant (as a polynomial in
y
). By removing this value from
A
if it is originally present (i.e., applying Theorems
1.5
and
1.6
to
A\setminus\{a\}
), we can assume
g(a+p(y))+h(y)
is nonconstant for all
a\in A
.

Our goal is to get an upper bound on
\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert
by combining the lower bound on
\lvert\mathcal{R}_{t}\rvert
from Lemma
4.1
with the upper bound on
\lvert\mathcal{R}_{t}\rvert
from Lemma
4.2
. For this, we need the first term on the right-hand side of Lemma
4.1
to dominate the second. Other than this, we would like
t
to be as small as possible. So we set

(8.1)

t=C\cdot\frac{\left\lvert A\right\rvert\left\lvert B\right\rvert}{\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert}

for a sufficiently large constant
C
(depending on the implicit constants in Lemma
4.1
).

As mentioned in Section
4
, we need to check that
t\leq 1
and
t\left\lvert A\right\rvert,t\left\lvert B\right\rvert\geq 1
.

•

To check that
t\left\lvert A\right\rvert\geq 1
, we claim that
\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert=O(\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert B\right\rvert)
. To see this, imagine constructing a
4
-tuple
(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\in\mathcal{Q}
by first choosing
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
, and
b_{1}
. Then we know the value of
f(a_{2},b_{2})=g(a_{2}+p(b_{2}))+h(b_{2})
; and
g(a_{2}+p(y))+h(y)
is a nonconstant polynomial in
y
of degree at most
d
, so there are at most
d=O(1)
choices for
b_{2}
.

Then as long as the constant
C
in (
8.1
) is sufficiently large, we indeed have
t\left\lvert A\right\rvert\geq 1
.

•

Similarly, to check that
t\left\lvert B\right\rvert\geq 1
, we claim that
\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert=O(\left\lvert A\right\rvert\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2})
. To see this, if we first choose
a_{1}
,
b_{1}
, and
b_{2}
, then we know the value of
f(a_{2},b_{2})=g(a_{2}+p(b_{2}))+h(b_{2})
, which means there are at most
d=O(1)
choices for
a_{2}
.

•

Finally, it is not necessarily true that this choice of
t
satifies
t\leq 1
. But if
t>1
, then we have
\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert=O(\left\lvert A\right\rvert\left\lvert B\right\rvert)
, and using Cauchy–Schwarz as in (
3.3
) gives

\left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert\geq\frac{\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2}}{\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert}=\Omega(\left\lvert A\right\rvert\left\lvert B\right\rvert).

This is where the final terms of Theorems
1.5
and
1.6
come from.

8.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.5

We now perform the final computations for Theorem
1.5
. For our value of
t
, Lemma
4.1
gives that
\lvert\mathcal{R}_{t}\rvert=\Omega(t^{2}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}\lvert\mathcal{Q}\rvert)
, while Lemma
4.2
gives an
upper
bound on
\lvert\mathcal{R}_{t}\rvert
in terms of
\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\rvert
. Since in the setting of Theorem
1.5
we do not know anything about the additive structure of
B
, we simply use the crude bound

\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\rvert=O(t\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{3}).

We then plug this into Lemma
4.2
and combine the result with the lower bound from Lemma
4.1
, performing casework on which term from Lemma
4.2
is largest.

Case 1 (term
(\mathrm{I})
is largest).
In this case, we get that

t^{2}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert=O(t^{24/11}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{18/11+2\eta}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{20/11+3\eta}\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{12/11}).

Rearranging and plugging in our value of
t
gives

\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert=O(t^{2/11}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{-4/11+2\eta}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{20/11+3\eta}\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{12/11})=O\left(\frac{\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{-2/11+2\eta}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2+3\eta}\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{12/11}}{\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert^{2/11}}\right),

and this rearranges to

\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert=O(\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{-2/13+2\eta}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{22/13+3\eta}\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{12/13}).

Finally, applying Cauchy–Schwarz as in (
3.3
) gives that

\left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert\geq\frac{\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2}}{\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert}=\Omega\left(\frac{\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{28/13-2\eta}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{4/13-3\eta}}{\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{12/13}}\right),

giving the first term of Theorem
1.5
.

Case 2 (term
(\mathrm{II})
is largest).
In this case, we get

t^{2}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert=O(t^{2}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{4/3}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{5/3+\eta}\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{4/3}),

which rearranges to

\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert=O(\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{-2/3}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{5/3+\eta}\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{4/3}).

After applying Cauchy–Schwarz, this gives the second term in Theorem
1.5
.

Case 3 (term
(\mathrm{III})
is largest).
In this case, we get

t^{2}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}\lvert\mathcal{Q}\rvert=O(t\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert B\right\rvert).

By (
8.1
), we have
t\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert=\Theta(\left\lvert A\right\rvert\left\lvert B\right\rvert)
, so
\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{3}=O(\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{2})
. This contradicts the assumption of Theorem
1.5
that
\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert\leq c\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{3/2}
(if
c
is sufficiently small), so this case cannot occur.

Case 4 (term
(\mathrm{IV})
is largest).
In this case, we get
\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert=O(\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{2})
, which by Cauchy–Schwarz gives
\left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert=\Omega(\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2})
, corresponding to the third term in Theorem
1.5
.

We conclude that Theorem
1.5
holds in all cases.

8.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.6

Finally, we perform the computations for Theorem
1.6
. We again have
\lvert\mathcal{R}_{t}\rvert=\Omega(t^{2}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert)
, while this time when using the upper bound on
\lvert\mathcal{R}_{t}\rvert
from Lemma
4.2
, we plug in the upper bound on
\lvert\mathcal{S}_{t}\rvert
from Lemma
4.3
. We can again perform casework on which term in the resulting bound is largest. The cases where term
(\mathrm{III})
or
(\mathrm{IV})
is the largest is the same as in Subsection
8.2
— the case where
(\mathrm{III})
is largest gives a contradiction, and the case where
(\mathrm{IV})
is largest gives
\left\lvert f(A,B)\right\rvert=\Omega(\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2})
.

In the case where term
(\mathrm{I})
is largest, we get

t^{2}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert=O(t^{20/9}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{18/11+2\eta}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{146/99+5\eta}\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{12/11}\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert^{8/33}).

After plugging in the value of
t
, rearranging to isolate
\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert
, and repeating the above Cauchy–Schwarz argument, we obtain the first term of the bound of Theorem
1.6
.

Similarly, when term
(\mathrm{II})
is largest, we get

t^{2}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert=O(t^{56/27}\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{4/3}\left\lvert B\right\rvert^{28/27+4\eta}\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert^{4/3}\left\lvert B+B\right\rvert^{4/9}),

which ends up giving the second term of Theorem
1.6
. So Theorem
1.6
is true in all cases.

9.
Additional proofs

In this section, we present our last two remaining proofs: the proofs of Proposition
1.20
and Corollary
1.17
.

9.1.
Proof of Proposition
1.20

In this subsection, we prove our almost tight lower bound for
A^{2}+A^{2}
when
A
has small sumset. Our proof is based on ideas of Elekes and Ruzsa
[
ER03
]
. The idea is to show that

(9.1)

\lvert A^{2}+A^{2}\rvert\cdot\max\{\left\lvert A-A+A\right\rvert^{4},\left\lvert A+A+A\right\rvert^{4}\}\cdot\log\left\lvert A\right\rvert=\Omega(\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{6}).

When
\left\lvert A+A\right\rvert\leq K\left\lvert A\right\rvert
, Plünnecke’s inequality (see
[
Zha23
, Theorem 7.3.3]
, for example) gives that
\left\lvert A-A+A\right\rvert\leq K^{3}\left\lvert A\right\rvert
and
\left\lvert A+A+A\right\rvert\leq K^{3}\left\lvert A\right\rvert
. Plugging these bounds into (
9.1
) gives

\lvert A^{2}+A^{2}\rvert=\Omega\left(\frac{\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}}{K^{12}\log\left\lvert A\right\rvert}\right).

So in order to prove Proposition
1.20
, it suffices to prove (
9.1
).

First, we may assume that
0\in A
, by replacing
A
with
A\cup\{0\}
otherwise (this increases the left-hand side of (
9.1
) by at most a constant factor). Then
A\subseteq A-A+A
and
A\subseteq A+A+A
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}=\{(a,b,c,d)\in A^{4}\mid a^{2}+b^{2}=c^{2}+d^{2}\}
. By Cauchy–Schwarz, we have

(9.2)

\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert\geq\frac{\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{4}}{\lvert A^{2}+A^{2}\rvert}.

To deduce (
9.1
), we will combine this with an upper bound on
\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert
. The idea is that for every
(a,b,c,d)\in\mathcal{Q}
, we have
(a-c)(a+c)=(d-b)(d+b)
. This implies that for all
x,y\in A
, the three points

(x,y),\,(x+a-c,y+d+b),\,(x+d-b,y+a+c)

form a collinear triple in
(A+A-A)\times(A+A+A)
, so the number of collinear triples in this set is at least
\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert
.

On the other hand, by Szemerédi–Trotter, the number of collinear triples in a set of
n
points with at most
\ell
collinear is
O(n^{2}\log\ell+\ell^{2}n)
(see for example
[
ER03
, Lemma 2.2]
).

In our case, we have
n=\left\lvert A+A-A\right\rvert\left\lvert A+A+A\right\rvert\leq\max\{\left\lvert A+A-A\right\rvert^{2},\left\lvert A+A+A\right\rvert^{2}\}
and
\ell=\max\{\left\lvert A+A-A\right\rvert,\left\lvert A+A+A\right\rvert\}
, so this bound gives

\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert\leq\max\{\left\lvert A-A+A\right\rvert^{4},\left\lvert A+A+A\right\rvert^{4}\}\log\left\lvert A\right\rvert.

Combining this upper bound on
\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert
with the lower bound from (
9.2
) implies (
9.1
).

9.2.
Proof of Corollary
1.17

In this subsection, we prove our slight improvement to the results of
[
SZdZ16
]
and
[
RRNS15
]
that a set of points with few distinct distances cannot have too many points on one line. Our proof combines the ideas from
[
SZdZ16
]
and
[
RRNS15
]
with Corollary
1.14
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a set of points as in the statement of Corollary
1.17
, and let
\ell
be a line that contains
m
points of
\mathcal{P}
. We may assume that
m\geq n^{4/5}
, since otherwise there is nothing to prove (as
\frac{13}{16}>\frac{4}{5}
). Let
\mathcal{P}_{1}=\ell\cap\mathcal{P}
. By translating and rotating
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we may assume that
\ell
is the
x
-axis. We denote the coordinates of a point
p\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
by
(p_{x},p_{y})
. Finally, as in the previous proofs, we let
\eta
denote an error parameter which we will eventually take to be small with respect to
\varepsilon
.

The proof works by considering a certain energy and upper-bounding it using incidence bounds. Specifically, we define the sets

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}

\displaystyle=\{(a,b,p,q)\in\mathcal{P}_{1}^{2}\times\mathcal{P}^{2}\mid d(a,p)=d(b,q)\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\{(a,b,p,q)\in\mathcal{Q}\mid p_{y}\neq\pm q_{y}\}.

By Cauchy–Schwarz, we have

\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert\geq\frac{\left\lvert\mathcal{P}_{1}\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert\mathcal{P}\right\rvert^{2}}{\left\lvert\Delta(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P})\right\rvert}\geq\frac{m^{2}n^{2}}{\frac{1}{5}n}=5m^{2}n.

To convert this into a lower bound on
\lvert\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\rvert
, note that when constructing a
4
-tuple
(a,b,p,q)\in\mathcal{Q}\setminus\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
, there are
m^{2}n
choices for
a
,
b
, and
p
. Then the condition
p_{y}=\pm q_{y}
means there are at most
2
choices for
q_{y}
, and the condition
d(a,p)=d(b,q)
means that each leads to at most two choices for
q_{x}
. This implies that
\lvert\mathcal{Q}\setminus\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\rvert\leq 4m^{2}n
, so

\lvert\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\rvert=\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert-\lvert\mathcal{Q}\setminus\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\rvert\geq 5m^{2}n-4m^{2}n=m^{2}n.

We will now get an
upper
bound for
\lvert\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\rvert
by setting up a collection of points
\Pi
and curves
\Gamma
whose incidences correspond to elements of
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
and applying the incidence bound of Theorem
2.1
. We define

\Pi=\{(a_{x},b_{x})\mid(a,b)\in\mathcal{P}_{1}^{2}\}.

We construct a multiset of curves
\Gamma
as follows. For each
(p,q)\in\mathcal{P}^{2}
with
p_{y}\neq\pm q_{y}
, we add to
\Gamma
the curve defined by

(x-p_{x})^{2}+p_{y}^{2}=(y-q_{x})^{2}+q_{y}^{2}.

Then we have
\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert=m^{2}
and
\left\lvert\Gamma\right\rvert\leq n^{2}
, and
\lvert\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\rvert=I(\Pi,\Gamma)
by definition.

The curves in
\Gamma
belong to a
3
-dimensional family defined by parameters
p_{x}
,
q_{x}
, and
p_{y}^{2}-q_{y}^{2}
. The condition
p_{y}\neq\pm q_{y}
implies that these curves are irreducible. So to apply Theorem
2.1
, it remains to obtain an upper bound on the curve multiplicities.

If two pairs
(p,q)
and
(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})
correspond to the same curve, then
p_{x}=p_{x}^{\prime}
and
q_{x}=q_{x}^{\prime}
. So to control curve multiplicities, we let
k
be the maximum number of points of
\mathcal{P}
on a line
\ell^{\prime}
orthogonal to
\ell
; then all curves in
\Gamma
have multiplicity at most
k
.

We first need to show that
k
is not too large. To do so, let
\ell^{\prime}
be a line orthogonal to
\ell
containing
k
points, and let
\mathcal{P}_{2}=\mathcal{P}\cap\ell^{\prime}
. Both
[
SZdZ16
]
and
[
RRNS15
]
use additive combinatorics estimates to show that if
k
is large, then either there are many distances
within

\mathcal{P}_{1}
, or there are many distances
between

\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
(contradicting the fact that there are not too many distances in
\mathcal{P}
). Corollary
1.14
provides a quantitative improvement to these additive combinatorics estimates in the relevant regime. By using it, we obtain the following bound.

Claim 9.1
.

We have
k=O(\frac{n^{25/4+8\eta}}{m^{7}}+\frac{n}{m})
.

Proof.

Since
\left\lvert\mathcal{P}_{1}\right\rvert=m\geq n^{4/5}
and
\left\lvert\Delta(\mathcal{P}_{1})\right\rvert\leq n
, we have
\left\lvert\Delta(\mathcal{P}_{1})\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert\mathcal{P}_{1}\right\rvert^{5/4}
. This means we can apply Corollary
1.14
to the sets
\mathcal{P}_{1}\subseteq\ell
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}\subseteq\ell^{\prime}
, which gives

\left\lvert\Delta(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2})\right\rvert=\Omega\left(\min\left\{\frac{m^{28/13-\eta}k^{4/13-\eta}}{\left\lvert\Delta(\mathcal{P}_{1})\right\rvert^{12/13}},m^{2},km\right\}\right).

Both
\left\lvert\Delta(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2})\right\rvert
and
\left\lvert\Delta(\mathcal{P}_{1})\right\rvert
are at most
n
, so

n=\Omega\left(\min\left\{\frac{m^{28/13-\eta}k^{4/13-\eta}}{n^{12/13}},m^{2},km\right\}\right)=\Omega\left(\min\left\{\frac{m^{28/13}k^{4/13}}{n^{12/13+2\eta}},m^{2},km\right\}\right).

We partition the analysis into cases according to the smallest term inside the minimum. If the first term is smallest, then we get

k=O\left(\frac{n^{25/4+8\eta}}{m^{7}}\right).

If the second term is smallest, then we get
m=O(n^{1/2})
, which contradicts the assumption that
m\geq n^{4/5}
. Finally, if the third term is smallest, then we get
k=O(\frac{n}{m})
.
∎

Now we bound
\lvert\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\rvert=I(\Pi,\Gamma)
by dyadically partitioning
\Gamma
based on curve multiplicity and applying Theorem
2.1
to each chunk of this partition. For each
j\in\{2^{0},2^{1},\ldots\}
, let
\Gamma_{j}
be the multiset consisting of curves whose multiplicities in
\Gamma
are in
[j,2j)
, each with the same multiplicity as in
\Gamma
, so that
I(\Pi,\Gamma)=\sum_{j}I(\Pi,\Gamma_{j})
. Let
\Gamma_{j}^{\prime}
be the set of curves of
\Gamma_{j}
, each appearing just once. Then using Theorem
2.1
to bound
I(\Pi,\Gamma_{j}^{\prime})
gives

\displaystyle I(\Pi,\Gamma_{j})

\displaystyle=\Theta(j\cdot I(\Pi,\Gamma_{j}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=O(j\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert^{6/11}\lvert\Gamma_{j}^{\prime}\rvert^{9/11+\eta}+j\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert^{2/3}\lvert\Gamma_{j}^{\prime}\rvert^{2/3}+j\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert+j\lvert\Gamma_{j}^{\prime}\rvert)

\displaystyle=O(j^{2/11}\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert^{6/11}\left\lvert\Gamma\right\rvert^{9/11+\eta}+j^{1/3}\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert^{2/3}\left\lvert\Gamma\right\rvert^{2/3}+j\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert+\lvert\Gamma_{j}\rvert),

where in the last transition we used the fact that
j\lvert\Gamma_{j}^{\prime}\rvert\leq\lvert\Gamma_{j}\rvert\leq\left\lvert\Gamma\right\rvert
. When we sum over
j
, the total contribution of the fourth term is at most
\sum_{j}\left\lvert\Gamma_{j}\right\rvert=\left\lvert\Gamma\right\rvert
. Each of the first three terms becomes a geometric series whose sum is dominated by its last term, where
j=O(k)
. So we get

\lvert\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\rvert\leq I(\Pi,\Gamma)=\sum_{j}I(\Pi,\Gamma_{j})=O(k^{2/11}\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert^{6/11}\left\lvert\Gamma\right\rvert^{9/11+\eta}+k^{1/3}\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert^{2/3}\left\lvert\Gamma\right\rvert^{2/3}+k\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert+\left\lvert\Gamma\right\rvert).

Now plugging in
\left\lvert\Pi\right\rvert=m^{2}
and
\left\lvert\Gamma\right\rvert\leq n^{2}
and combining this with our lower bound
\lvert\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\rvert\geq m^{2}n
, we get that

m^{2}n=O(k^{2/11}m^{12/11}n^{18/11+\eta}+k^{1/3}m^{4/3}n^{4/3}+km^{2}+n^{2}).

By Claim
9.1
and the assumption
m\geq n^{4/5}
, the third and fourth terms on the right-hand side are
o(m^{2}n)
, so we may ignore them. Then plugging our bound on
k
from Claim
9.1
into the first two terms gives

m^{2}n=O\left(\frac{n^{61/22+3\eta}}{m^{2/11}}+m^{10/11}n^{20/11+\eta}+\frac{n^{41/12+3\eta}}{m}+mn^{5/3}\right).

Finally, performing casework on which term on the right-hand side is largest and rearranging gives

m=O(n^{13/16+2\eta}+n^{3/4+\eta}+n^{29/36+\eta}+n^{2/3})=O(n^{13/16+2\eta}),

completing the proof of Corollary
1.17
.
