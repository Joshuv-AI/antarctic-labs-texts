From vision to value: a mixed methods evaluation of patient-centered care in Saudi Arabian primary healthcare centers
Authors: Mishael Alhuseini, Duaa Aljabri, Arwa Althumairi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe transformation of Saudi Arabia’s healthcare system under Vision 2030 emphasizes the importance of primary healthcare (PHC) centers and patient-centered care (PCC). Despite national reforms, limited research has assessed the extent to which PHC practices have adopted PCC principles. This study evaluated the impact and current status of the implementation of PCC across PHC centers in the Eastern Health Cluster of Saudi Arabia using the Patient-Centered Medical Home Assessment (PCMH-A) tool.MethodsA convergent parallel mixed-methods design was employed. Quantitative data were collected from 59 PHC centers across three health networks using the PCMH-A tool, which evaluates the implementation of PCC across seven domains. Simultaneously, qualitative data were gathered through open-ended responses embedded in the tool and analyzed thematically using Braun and Clarke’s approach.ResultsPCMH-A scores indicated moderate PCC implementation across all networks. The highest performing domain was quality improvement strategy (mean = 7.48), while patient-centered interactions scored the lowest (mean = 5.08). Comparison across networks showed that Network C consistently outperformed the others, supported by its structured leadership approach, stronger team-based care practices, and active community engagement initiatives. The qualitative findings helped explain these results. The participants reported several barriers to PCC, including centralized governance, limited access to services, poor care coordination, and low patient engagement. They also noted strengths such as improvements driven by accreditation and the use of health technology. Many of the barriers—such as limited resources, cultural factors, and uneven policy implementation—contributed to differences in PCC performance across networks.ConclusionDespite promising initiatives, significant gaps remain in PCC delivery in Saudi PHC centers, particularly in access, continuity, and patient involvement. Policy efforts should focus on decentralizing authority, investing in digital integration, and promoting culturally responsive practices. Enhancing these areas is essential for achieving Vision 2030’s goals and building an equitable, person-centered healthcare system.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14574-3.

Background

Primary healthcare (PHC) plays a foundational role in health systems worldwide, as formally recognized in 1978 by the World Health Organization [1]. PHC offers individuals, families, and communities a broad range of preventive and curative services [2]. Over time, PHC has proven to be effective in reducing healthcare costs, decreasing disease prevalence, and promoting overall well-being [2]. Countries such as the United Kingdom, Denmark, and the Netherlands have demonstrated that investments in PHC lead to more community-based care and improved integration with public health and hospital-based services [3].

Globally, chronic conditions such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease, cancer, chronic respiratory diseases, and mental illness are projected to cost $21 trillion annually by 2030 due to treatment needs and lost productivity [4]. This, combined with the aging population, positions PHC as the most effective platform for healthcare systems [5]. Thus, investment in PHC should be a strategic priority for all countries.

Saudi Arabia, like many nations, faces significant challenges in adopting a comprehensive and effective healthcare system [6]. Established in 1925, the Saudi healthcare system comprises three levels—primary, secondary, and tertiary services—all provided free of charge through the Ministry of Health (MOH) network [7]. As of mid-2025, the MOH operates 290 hospitals and 2,126 PHC centers [8]. PHC centers serve as the initial point of contact for patients within the healthcare system, with their importance significantly increasing following the MOH mandate requiring referrals through PHC centers for non-emergency hospital admissions [9].

While the Saudi government has undertaken substantial initiatives to strengthen healthcare delivery, studies continue to identify barriers affecting access and service provision through PHC centers. These challenges include rapid population growth, rising healthcare costs, limited service availability, unequal access, weak intersectoral coordination, and delayed adoption of digital health tools [10]. PHC center capacity also remains uneven, with persistent gaps in service quality, variations in accessibility, and shortages in human resources [11]. Similarly, a 2016 Riyadh-based study found that 75% of respondents did not consider PHC their first choice for care [12] with some attributing this to the narrow scope of PHC services and mistrust in its ability to meet their health needs [13]. Similar patterns have been observed internationally; for example, patients in Kenya reported a preference for specialist care over general practitioners [14]. Together, these findings underscore the need to strengthen public confidence in PHC and clarify its evolving role within the healthcare system.

To address gaps in accessibility and service coordination, the MOH launched a comprehensive reform initiative under the Health Sector Transformation Program (HSTP), a key pillar of Saudi Arabia’s Vision 2030 [15]. This reform aims to restructure the health sector into an integrated, effective, and comprehensive system (see Fig. 1). Thus, the Saudi model of care has shifted from a reactive, hospital-centric system to a proactive, value-based approach grounded in PHC. At the core of this transformation is the reinforcement of the family practice model, which promotes patient- and family-centered care, multidisciplinary teamwork, expanded access, digital health integration, and continuous quality improvement [15].

Patient-centered care (PCC) represents a shift from traditional disease-focused models to care that respects and responds to individual patients’ preferences, needs, and values [16]. Research has shown that PCC leads to better health outcomes, greater satisfaction, and lower healthcare costs [17]. The Picker Institute and Commonwealth Fund have identified eight dimensions of PCC—respect for patient preferences, clear communication, emotional support, physical comfort, family involvement, care coordination, smooth transitions, and accessible services [18]—with surveys showing that respect for patient preferences and involvement in decision-making are perceived as the most important [19].

Within PHC, PCC is widely recognized as essential for improving service quality and patient outcomes [20]. Several models embody PCC principles in PHC settings, including the patient-centered medical home (PCMH) model, the chronic care model, and shared decision-making frameworks [21]. The PCMH model, one of the most widely implemented PCC models in primary care, embodies these principles by fostering continuous, team-based care aimed at managing both chronic and preventive health needs [22]. Through a patient-centered, culturally appropriate, and team-based framework, the PCMH model coordinates care across health systems and has gained considerable attention as a strategy for improving quality while reducing costs [23].

Studies have found that the PCMH model improves quality metrics such as diabetes control, cardiovascular health, and preventive screenings while reducing healthcare utilization and associated costs [24, 25]. In addition to improving patient care, the model has also been found to benefit healthcare providers by increasing their satisfaction rates and lessening their emotional exhaustion [26].

The Patient-Centered Medical Home Assessment (PCMH-A) tool, based on the PCMH model developed by the Safety Net Medical Home Initiative, can be used to guide primary care practices through the transition to PCC [27] The PCMH-A evaluates PHC models of care and identifies opportunities for improvement by assessing 36 items across eight domains.

Existing evidence has shown that PCC dimensions are significant predictors of patient satisfaction in the Saudi context [28]. However, evidence on the current level of PCC implementation within Saudi PHC is limited. Thus, the aim of this study was to assess the level of transformation to PCC in PHC centers in Saudi Arabia using the PCMH-A tool and to identify related challenges and areas for improvement. Findings from this research can be used to ensure a better match between health systems’ capacities and patients’ expectations, ultimately enhancing PHC quality and outcomes in Saudi Arabia.

Methods

Study design

A convergent, parallel, mixed-methods design was employed to assess and evaluate the transformation of PHC centers to PCC practices. In this approach, quantitative and qualitative data are collected simultaneously but analyzed independently, with findings integrated during a subsequent phase [29]. This design was selected to provide a comprehensive and multidimensional assessment of PHC performance in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia.

Study setting and context

The data for this study were collected from urban PHC centers within the Eastern Health Cluster. In 2022, and as part of broader health reforms aimed at granting regional authorities greater autonomy in tailoring services to local needs, Saudi Arabia restructured its healthcare system into 20 health clusters distributed across five major regions. In the Eastern Region, three health clusters were established: the Eastern Health Cluster, the Al-Ahsa Health Cluster, and the Hafer Al-Batin Health Cluster [30]. The Eastern Health Cluster serves over two million individuals through a network of 120 PHC centers and 22 general and specialized hospitals [31]. Within this cluster, healthcare delivery is organized into six geographic networks (A, B, C, D, E, and F). This study focused on 59 urban PHC centers located in networks (A, B, and C). Network D, E, and F were excluded because the PHC centers located there do not have Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval, serve only rural communities, and comprise hospital-based services, respectively.

Participants and sampling

Quantitative sampling

In Saudi MOH PHC centers, each facility is managed by two key figures: an administrative director responsible for operational oversight and a medical director who oversees both clinical operations and patient care. Although the PCMH-A tool should be completed by a multidisciplinary team—including physicians, nurses, medical assistants, residents, and administrative staff—to capture diverse perspectives, this approach was not feasible in the MOH PHC context, as many healthcare professionals were unfamiliar with the administrative components of the PHC model, while administrative staff lacked insight into clinical processes. Consequently, medical directors were identified as the most appropriate respondents due to their dual administrative and clinical responsibilities and their comprehensive understanding of PHC operations. The quantitative phase therefore included all 59 medical directors from the targeted PHC centers, representing a total population sampling strategy. Data collection took place from May to August 2024, with individual sessions lasting between 20 and 60 min.

Medical directors were eligible for inclusion if they were working in PHC centers under the Eastern Health Cluster, were serving in urban PHC centers within Networks A, B, or C, and were employed in centers with active IRB approval during the data collection period. Eligibility also included holding the position of medical director at the time of data collection.

Qualitative sampling

The qualitative data were drawn from the same cohort of participants as in the quantitative phase—specifically, the 59 medical directors who had completed the PCMH-A tool. Although the PCMH-A tool relies on structured scoring for each domain, respondents frequently expanded on their ratings by offering explanations, contextual comments, and reflections related to the barriers and facilitators of implementing PCC. These additional narrative insights, provided voluntarily by the medical directors, were subsequently linked to the domains in the PCMH-A tool and used to enrich the interpretation in this study.

Data collection

Quantitative data collection

To evaluate the performance of PHC centers, this study utilized the publicly available PCMH-A tool. PCMH-A scores range from 1 to 12, with higher scores indicating greater implementation of PCMH recommended practices [32]. The tool assesses 36 PCMH practices and behaviors across eight domains, which were developed based on a review of the literature. The tool has been validated by primary care and quality improvement experts [33].

The PCMH-A was piloted with three primary healthcare professionals from different PHC centers to ensure its cultural and contextual relevance to the Saudi setting. Based on their feedback, minor revisions were made, resulting in a modified version that assessed 31 PCMH practices and behaviors across seven domains: Engaged leadership, quality improvement strategy, contionous and team-based healing relationships, organized evidence-based care, patient-centered interactions, enhanced access and care coordination (see Appendix A). Following these modifications, the internal consistency reliability of the revised instrument was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha. The analysis yielded a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.823, indicating good internal consistency and supporting the reliability of the instrument for use in this study.

Qualitative data collection

Qualitative data were collected concurrently with the quantitative phase using the same assessment instrument. The 59 participating medical directors were invited to elaborate on their responses during data collection, provide contextual insights, and identify specific challenges and facilitators related to the implementation of PCC within their centers. This approach enabled the collection of narrative data directly aligned with the quantitative measures, thereby enhancing the depth and comprehensiveness of the overall interpretation. The embedded design facilitated the generation of rich qualitative insights without requiring separate interviews or focus groups [34]. All qualitative responses were provided in Arabic, the participants’ native language, to ensure comfort, clarity, and authenticity in expression. For analysis and reporting purposes, all relevant quotations and narrative excerpts were translated into English by the research team, ensuring accuracy while preserving the original meaning and tone. This dual-language approach enhanced the cultural validity and linguistic integrity of the data. To ensure accuracy, translations were completed by one research team member and independently reviewed by another.

Data analysis

Quantitative analysis

Descriptive statistics were generated to summarize the characteristics of PHC centers across the included networks. The variables included the number of centers, number of physicians per center, patient-to-physician ratio, and center operating hours. Parametric tests were employed for inferential analyses. The mean and standard deviation (SD) of PCMH-A domain scores were calculated for all networks, as were the mean scores per item within each network.

To compare PCMH-A domain scores across the three networks, a one-way ANOVA test was conducted to determine whether statistically significant differences existed among group means. Additionally, Pearson’s correlation analysis was used to examine the relationship between each domain and the overall PCMH-A score, identifying which domains were most strongly associated with overall performance. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

All collected data were transferred to Microsoft Excel, and statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0 [35, 36].

Qualitative analysis

The research team conducted an inductive thematic analysis of the free-text responses using the six-phase approach outlined by Virginia Braun and Victoria Clarke [37]. This approach was appropriate given the limited research on PCC in Saudi Arabia, as it allowed themes to be generated directly from the data rather than being guided by pre-existing frameworks. Coding was kept close to the explicit meaning of the text without deeper interpretive assumptions. The analysis was conducted from a realist/essentialist perspective, focusing on describing practices and experiences within PHC centers to inform quality improvement.

The coding process involved six steps: (1) familiarization with the data through repeated reading and note-taking, (2) manually generating initial codes to capture meaningful features, (3) searching for themes by clustering related codes, (4) reviewing themes to ensure consistency and alignment with the dataset, (5) defining and naming themes to capture their essence, and (6) producing the final report with illustrative quotations. All phases of the analysis were conducted by the research team to ensure consistency, coherence, and reflexivity throughout the analytic process.

Integration of quantitative and qualitative data

In keeping with the convergent mixed-methods design, the quantitative and qualitative findings were integrated during interpretation. In the discussion section of this paper, the results are examined under shared themes to identify convergence, divergence, and complementarity using a narrative approach.

Statistically significant differences were found in several domains across the three networks, as shown in Table 4. Network C scored significantly higher in quality improvement strategy (p = 0.002), continuous and team-based healing relationships (p = 0.028), patient-centered interactions (p = 0.002), enhanced access (p = 0.028), and care coordination (p < 0.001). However, no significant differences were found in the engaged leadership and organized, evidence-based care domains.

Figure 2 illustrates that all PCMH-A domains showed significant correlations with the networks’ overall scores. The highest correlations were observed for quality improvement strategy (r = 0.842, p < 0.001), continuous and team-based healing relationships (r = 0.778, p < 0.001), and patient-centered interactions (r = 0.756, p < 0.001). The weakest correlation was found with enhanced access (r = 0.460, p < 0.001), indicating that while access is an important component, it may not contribute as strongly to the overall success of PCMH implementation as other domains.

Qualitative findings

Open-ended responses provided by the 59 medical directors during completion of the PCMH-A assessment tool were analyzed to capture their perspectives on PHC practice operations and care processes. Using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework, the research team conducted an inductive thematic analysis that resulted in the development of six themes. These themes are outlined below.

Theme 1: System centralization

This theme highlights the impact of centralized governance structures on PHC centers’ autonomy and responsiveness. The majority of medical directors across all networks reported challenges from limited operational autonomy, attributing this to the highly centralized nature of decision-making and administrative procedures. Participants also raised concerns about resource shortages, particularly in Networks A and B, which further intensified these challenges.

Several respondents noted relying on charitable donations from local communities to bridge resource gaps. Others expressed concerns that ongoing shortages could undermine patients’ trust in primary care services:The center lacks essential supplies such as vitamin B12, vitamin D, various diagnostic tests, and some laboratory resources, leading to patient frustration and a decline in trust in the center. Ensuring the availability of these resources would not only improve patient health but also reduce the rate of hospital referrals. (Medical Director #48)

The center lacks essential supplies such as vitamin B12, vitamin D, various diagnostic tests, and some laboratory resources, leading to patient frustration and a decline in trust in the center. Ensuring the availability of these resources would not only improve patient health but also reduce the rate of hospital referrals. (Medical Director #48)

Conversely, several respondents emphasized that while the current health reform program is underway, the transformation remains in its early stages and will require time to mature:Resources are limited due to unfavorable practices by the previous MOH administration, which established policies that overlooked the importance of PHC. However, the administration is now changing, with new leadership in place, and the focus is gradually shifting toward strengthening the PHC system. (Medical Director #11)

Resources are limited due to unfavorable practices by the previous MOH administration, which established policies that overlooked the importance of PHC. However, the administration is now changing, with new leadership in place, and the focus is gradually shifting toward strengthening the PHC system. (Medical Director #11)

Theme 2: Access to care

This theme highlights the variability in patients’ ability to access PHC services, which is shaped by key dimensions such as availability, accessibility, and acceptability. While most participants described access to PHC services as generally flexible, many noted that use rates vary significantly across networks. Respondents, particularly from Networks A and B, frequently reported that PHC centers—especially those located in rapidly expanding neighborhoods—struggle to meet the growing demand due to limited infrastructure and staffing capacity. In addition to these structural challenges, several participants stressed the importance of tailoring services to meet the unique needs of the communities they serve:The clinic is located in a low-income neighborhood where the majority of patients are non-Saudis with limited educational backgrounds. It is recommended that the center employ at least one full-time social worker to address the social needs of this population. (Medical Director #23)

The clinic is located in a low-income neighborhood where the majority of patients are non-Saudis with limited educational backgrounds. It is recommended that the center employ at least one full-time social worker to address the social needs of this population. (Medical Director #23)

The PHC centers’ physical location also played a significant role in influencing patient volumes. Centers situated along major roads or adjacent to hospitals reported noticeably higher traffic than others. One director highlighted how proximity to a hospital influenced operations:Being located next to the general hospital has its challenges. They often redirect patients who aren’t admitted to the ER to our center, especially since we’re open 24 h. It sometimes feels like we’re functioning as an extension of their emergency services. (Medical Director #50)

Being located next to the general hospital has its challenges. They often redirect patients who aren’t admitted to the ER to our center, especially since we’re open 24 h. It sometimes feels like we’re functioning as an extension of their emergency services. (Medical Director #50)

Accessibility emerged as a key theme in the qualitative findings, with many PHC center leaders highlighting persistent challenges in managing patient flows and ensuring equitable access to care. Although the MOH provides several channels for appointment scheduling, most PHC center leaders reported that their clinics remain consistently crowded with walk-in patients, particularly in Networks B and C. Many attributed this pattern to low levels of digital literacy and socioeconomic barriers that make it difficult for patients to navigate the appointment systems effectively.

Some medical directors reported using designated WhatsApp numbers to facilitate patient communication and manage walk-in visits. However, implementation was inconsistent: In several centers, no staff were formally assigned to monitor messages, and no policy guided their use, whereas others had designated personnel and defined response-time targets, typically within 24 h.

Acceptability, particularly cultural sensitivity, was another frequently cited issue. Gender concordance, defined as the matching of patients and providers based on gender, emerged as a recurring concern across all three networks. Many directors from Network C reported that patient preferences were considered during registration to avoid discomfort or misunderstanding. In other cases, patients requesting gender-specific care were referred to another physician when possible.

Directors of PHC centers serving Bedouin-majority communities described a distinct set of challenges. These populations often showed lower levels of health literacy and engagement with structured care processes, leading to a high volume of walk-in visits:There is a high rate of walk-in visits in our center, which serves a Bedouin community that is generally less familiar with or cooperative in using appointment scheduling systems and therefore often seeks care through walk-ins. (Medical Director #42)

There is a high rate of walk-in visits in our center, which serves a Bedouin community that is generally less familiar with or cooperative in using appointment scheduling systems and therefore often seeks care through walk-ins. (Medical Director #42)

Across Networks A, B, and C, participants consistently reported that their centers frequently accept walk-in patients despite MOH policy requiring triage-based eligibility. They explained that this approach is often taken to avoid patient complaints and to maintain smooth day-to-day operations.

Theme 3: Health technology integration

This theme focuses on how digital infrastructure helps to enhance service delivery and communication within PHC centers. Health technology was widely appreciated by all medical directors for its role in improving care processes and supporting better patient outcomes. All PHC centers in the Eastern Health Cluster are connected through a unified EHR system, Raqeem, which many participants credited with improving chronic disease management and ensuring more consistent clinical documentation [38].

In addition to the EHR, Sehhaty—the MOH’s unified platform that provides individuals with access to personal health data and a wide range of digital healthcare services—was frequently mentioned as a valuable tool for both patients and providers.

Many directors also emphasized the growing importance of digital tools in overcoming communication barriers, especially in linguistically diverse communities. In Network C, for example, translation applications were commonly used to support interactions with non-Arabic- or non-English-speaking patients:Technology has really made a difference in how we communicate with our patients. We now use different translation apps to help us explain things more clearly. (Medical Director #47)

Technology has really made a difference in how we communicate with our patients. We now use different translation apps to help us explain things more clearly. (Medical Director #47)

Theme 4: Accreditation and quality

This theme explores how accreditation standards have shaped quality improvement processes and institutional practices across PHC centers. All of the PHC centers are accredited by the Central Board for Accreditation of Healthcare Institutions (CBAHI), and the participants consistently reported that the accreditation process has played a key role in raising overall quality standards. Clinical guidelines are now widely available, and documentation practices have become more standardized across most centers:The CBAHI requires every accredited center to have a proper care plan and strategy for quality improvement activities. It’s helped us become more structured and focused on how we manage and enhance our services. (Medical Director #49)

The CBAHI requires every accredited center to have a proper care plan and strategy for quality improvement activities. It’s helped us become more structured and focused on how we manage and enhance our services. (Medical Director #49)

Tracking key performance indicators (KPIs) was described as a core accreditation requirement, yet directors—especially in Networks A and B—reported inconsistent communication of performance data to frontline staff. In several centers, results were discussed only in response to low scores or specific issues, whereas Network C directors reported more routine and transparent performance reviews:We regularly participate in Gemba walks led by our network leader to review KPIs. It’s a great opportunity to step back, see how we’re performing, and identify areas where we can improve. (Medical Director #58)

We regularly participate in Gemba walks led by our network leader to review KPIs. It’s a great opportunity to step back, see how we’re performing, and identify areas where we can improve. (Medical Director #58)

Theme 5: Coordination and continuity of care

This theme explores the implementation of the team-based care (TBC) model and the broader challenges surrounding continuity of care within and across PHC centers. The TBC model, introduced by the MOH in 2021, is intended to strengthen continuity by assigning each patient to a dedicated physician–nurse team [39]. However, the participants reported that implementation remains uneven across centers.

Many medical directors noted that awareness of the TBC model is still limited, not just among patients but also among some providers, particularly in low-income neighborhoods:A lot of patients, especially those from low-income areas, don’t really understand what “team-based care” means. They usually just walk in or book with any available doctor to get a re-prescription or referral without realizing that they’re supposed to see the same physician and care team each time they visit. (Medical Director #13)

A lot of patients, especially those from low-income areas, don’t really understand what “team-based care” means. They usually just walk in or book with any available doctor to get a re-prescription or referral without realizing that they’re supposed to see the same physician and care team each time they visit. (Medical Director #13)

System-level policies were also described as barriers to continuity, particularly the open-access scheduling model that allows patients to book with any physician in any PHC center and the high volume of walk-in visits. Although these policies are designed to enhance accessibility, several directors reported that they make it difficult to maintain consistent follow-up or provide relationship-based care:More than half of our visits are walk-ins. I’ve been a physician in primary care for 17 years, and I firmly believe that the only effective way to implement team-based care is through a catchment-area policy—where each center serves patients from a defined geographic area. Without such a system, it’s very difficult to ensure continuity and consistently follow up with my own patients. (Medical Director #42)

More than half of our visits are walk-ins. I’ve been a physician in primary care for 17 years, and I firmly believe that the only effective way to implement team-based care is through a catchment-area policy—where each center serves patients from a defined geographic area. Without such a system, it’s very difficult to ensure continuity and consistently follow up with my own patients. (Medical Director #42)

Continuity of care following emergency department visits was also highlighted as a major challenge, especially in Networks A and B, where PHC centers are not connected to hospital information systems:When a patient comes from the emergency room, we usually don’t have any electronic connection or shared record with the hospital. If we’re lucky, the patient brings a printed discharge summary; otherwise, we have to reassess everything from the beginning. (Medical Director #1)

When a patient comes from the emergency room, we usually don’t have any electronic connection or shared record with the hospital. If we’re lucky, the patient brings a printed discharge summary; otherwise, we have to reassess everything from the beginning. (Medical Director #1)

Network C, however, has begun piloting a cloud-based EHR system in partnership with an MOH hospital within its network. This has improved information sharing and coordination for patients treated within that specific facility. Still, the participants from Network C noted that the benefits were limited, as some patients continue to seek care from hospitals that are not yet integrated into the system.

Theme 6: Patient and community engagement

This theme highlights how PHC centers actively engage patients and community members to ensure that services are responsive, patient-centered, and aligned with local needs. First, across all centers, medical directors consistently identified language barriers as a significant challenge, particularly given the diversity of their patient communities. To address this issue, most centers reported relying on informal interpreters—such as bilingual staff members, family members, or relatives—and mobile translation applications. In contrast, leaders in Network C described more structured and organized approaches, including the distribution of official lists of multilingual translators across their centers, some of whom are trained in sign language. One director described a locally driven initiative:Besides the official translation list, the staff here are really supportive—they actually created their own list of community members, like non-Arabic-speaking residents, who can help translate for patients who don’t speak Arabic. (Medical Director #55)

Besides the official translation list, the staff here are really supportive—they actually created their own list of community members, like non-Arabic-speaking residents, who can help translate for patients who don’t speak Arabic. (Medical Director #55)

Second, a recurring subtheme concerned the inconsistent use of patient feedback across centers despite the existence of formal collection mechanisms. Although all participants confirmed that feedback is gathered through the MOH’s Patient Experience Survey, its internal dissemination and use varied considerably. Some centers actively shared results and used them to guide improvement, whereas others reported limited engagement with the data. In contrast, several centers in Network C supplemented formal surveys with more immediate feedback channels, such as center-developed surveys or digital platforms, enabling more timely patient input and responsiveness.

Third, the qualitative findings showed that Network C excelled in community engagement efforts. There, many PHC centers have established official boards that include members from both the centers and the community. Others have created WhatsApp groups with community representatives to strengthen their connection with local residents. These representatives serve as grassroots links between the community and the health centers, facilitating the airing of local concerns and feedback. One director shared a recent example of this in action:Last month, a community representative informed us that many parents were struggling to book vaccination appointments through the app. In response, the PHC center created a short video to guide them through the process and raise awareness about the importance of vaccinations. (Medical Director #57)

Last month, a community representative informed us that many parents were struggling to book vaccination appointments through the app. In response, the PHC center created a short video to guide them through the process and raise awareness about the importance of vaccinations. (Medical Director #57)

Directors in Network C also emphasized the value of broader partnerships with local organizations and neighborhood associations in sustaining community engagement. One center director described how these collaborations work in practice:Our center has a WhatsApp group with members of the community—teachers, athletes, influencers—and we meet every few months. We also have a designated community representative who serves as a bridge between the PHC center and the neighborhood because we believe that staying connected is essential for improving people’s health. (Medical Director #48)

Our center has a WhatsApp group with members of the community—teachers, athletes, influencers—and we meet every few months. We also have a designated community representative who serves as a bridge between the PHC center and the neighborhood because we believe that staying connected is essential for improving people’s health. (Medical Director #48)

Lastly, some centers across the studied networks reported initiatives that involve collaboration with local charitable organizations in their neighborhoods to help patients access social services and other forms of support. They believe that these partnerships enable PHC centers to address the social determinants of health more effectively and promote a more holistic, person-centered approach to care—one that extends beyond the clinic walls and into the broader community.

Discussion

Overview

Taken together, the mixed-methods findings show that while PHC centers have established many of the structural components of PCC, such as quality improvement systems and evidence-based processes, these foundations have not fully translated into consistent frontline practice. The quantitative results highlight relatively strong organizational structures but weaker performance in patient-centered interactions, care coordination, and access, a pattern echoed in qualitative accounts describing communication gaps, fragmented follow-up, and challenges in maintaining continuity.

Network C stands out as an exception, demonstrating that strong structures paired with collaborative, team-based cultures can enable more effective delivery of PCC even in high-volume settings. Staff within this network attributed their success to inclusive quality improvement practices, culturally responsive communication, shared digital systems, and community partnerships—features that appear to bridge the gap between organizational capacity and everyday care.

Across all centers, the associations among PCMH-A domains indicate that systematic quality improvement, team-based relationships, and patient-centered communication are key drivers of overall performance. In contrast, enhanced access—while valuable—plays a more complex role: Expanded walk-in availability may improve entry points but can also disrupt continuity if not well coordinated. Overall, the integrated findings underscore that structural readiness alone is insufficient; sustained relational, coordinated, and culturally attuned care processes are essential for achieving patient-centered outcomes.

Interpretation of key findings: a mixed methods integration

Barriers to implementation

Access to care

The qualitative findings highlighted several challenges PHC centers face in ensuring adequate access to care. To contextualize these challenges, Penchansky and Thomas’s [40] Access Theory was applied. This framework outlines four interrelated dimensions—availability, accessibility, accommodation, and acceptability—that together assess the alignment between health system characteristics and patient needs.

Availability refers to the adequacy of the number or amount of healthcare resources such as providers, facilities, and services. The medical directors in this study reported difficulties in meeting rising demand, particularly in rapidly expanding neighborhoods such as those in Network A. This aligns with previous studies documenting geographic disparities in PHC distribution across Saudi Arabia [41, 42]. Thus, achieving a more equitable distribution of PHC centers is critical not only for meeting growing population needs but also for reducing hospital overcrowding.

Despite ongoing reform efforts, limited financial constraints remain a major barrier to strengthening the availability of PHC. Several medical directors reported insufficient funding to expand facilities, recruit adequate staff, or upgrade essential equipment, similar to findings of a recent review [43]. These resource limitations compromise the availability and quality of services, particularly in high-demand or underserved areas.

Accessibility pertains to the geographic location of PHC centers relative to patients’ residences. The participants emphasized that location strongly influences utilization, with PHC centers near highways or hospitals experiencing higher patient volumes and those in remote or underdeveloped areas reporting lower utilization, aligning with prior evidence [44].

Accommodation addresses how healthcare services are organized to meet patients’ needs. A major concern of the medical directors in this study was the high volume of walk-in patients, which disrupts scheduled appointments, increases wait times, and compromises continuity of care. Although some studies have suggested that walk-in access can improve patient satisfaction and reduce unnecessary emergency department use [45]—with Baughman et al. [46] estimating potential annual savings of approximately $826,000—leaders in this study viewed these benefits as limited. In their view, unstructured access jeopardizes care coordination and undermines the quality and consistency of clinical follow-up.

Despite these concerns, walk-in volumes remain high even though structured appointment platforms, such as Sehhaty and the 937 medical support hotline, are available. Leaders—particularly in Networks B and C—attributed this persistence to the socioeconomic characteristics of their catchment populations, which include many middle- to low-income communities with limited digital literacy. This is consistent with Kirby and Kaneda [47], who found that individuals from socioeconomically disadvantaged neighborhoods often struggle to navigate healthcare systems.

Service organization also constrains accommodation. Most PHC centers in the examined networks operate from 8 a.m. to 9 p.m. on weekdays, with only three offering 24/7 services. These restricted hours may reduce flexibility for working patients and contribute to non-urgent emergency department use, which has been reported to account for up to 50% of emergency department visits in Saudi Arabia [48].

Communication systems present an additional accommodation limitation. In the absence of dedicated landlines, many centers rely on WhatsApp, yet its use is inconsistently managed due to the lack of formal policies or assigned staff. In contrast, Network C implemented clear monitoring responsibilities and standardized response protocols, reflecting evidence that instant-messaging platforms can enhance access when systematically integrated into care processes [49].

Acceptability refers to how well healthcare services align with patients’ cultural, social, and personal preferences. The PHC medical directors in this study highlighted two major acceptability concerns: gender concordance and the cultural needs of Bedouin communities. According to the directors, many female patients prefer same-gender providers for sensitive health issues, and the absence of female clinicians may contribute to delayed or avoided care. Gender-concordant provider interactions have been associated with greater patient comfort and communication, particularly during physical examinations [50].

Cultural acceptability issues were also prominent among Bedouin patients, whose traditional norms, health beliefs, and lower educational attainment limit engagement with formal healthcare services. Evidence from other studies reinforce this pattern [51]. Together, these findings emphasize the need for culturally sensitive service models that foster trust, accommodate patient preferences, and promote equitable service uptake among underserved populations.

Care coordination

The World Health Organization defines care coordination as the process of ensuring that patients’ needs and preferences for health services and information sharing are consistently met over time and across different levels of care and providers [52]. In this study, three major challenges were identified under the domain of care coordination: implementation of the TBC model, system-level barriers, and integration of primary care centers with hospitals.

The TBC model serves as a cornerstone strategy for effective care coordination in primary healthcare settings, enabling multidisciplinary collaboration to support continuous PCC. However, care coordination showed relatively low performance in the quantitative assessment. Implementation of the TBC model remains inconsistent, particularly in low-income communities While medical directors expressed willingness to transition to TBC, they reported limited knowledge and training. These findings are consistent with those of Haji [53], who reported that family physicians in Saudi PHC centers expressed the need for formal training to implement TBC effectively. Supporting this, Carney et al. [54] found that postgraduate family medicine residents trained in TBC were 5.7 times more likely to work in facilities practicing this model. Furthermore, medical directors in the current study emphasized that full implementation of TBC also requires increased patient awareness and engagement, underscoring the need for public educational initiatives.

Care coordination is also hindered by system-level barriers. Historically, Saudi PHC operated under a catchment-area model in which patients were linked to centers based on residence. Under Vision 2030 reforms, patients can now choose any physician or care team through the Sehhaty application. Although this enhances convenience and patient autonomy, it has unintentionally fragmented care. Patients frequently encounter different providers who are unfamiliar with their medical histories, weakening follow-up processes and the ongoing relationships essential to effective primary care. This shift toward accessibility over continuity aligns with the lower scores observed in care coordination and team-based healing relationships.

Finally, limited integration between PHC centers and hospitals, particularly in terms of efficient information exchange and consistent follow-up across care levels, is another challenge to care coordination. In this study, Networks A and B showed weak post-emergency follow-up, reflecting gaps in care transitions. In contrast, Network C demonstrated the strongest post-emergency follow-up, supported by pilot projects that integrate cloud-based EHRs between PHC centers and the main hospital within the network, thereby improving care transitions and ensuring greater consistency. Broader evidence underscores the persistence of this problem nationally: A recent KPMG report found that 65% of medical directorates described referral systems between primary and secondary care in Saudi Arabia as “weak,” citing minimal structured communication or feedback mechanisms [55]. Similarly, a study of Saudi PHC centers reported that only one-third of clinicians receive useful information about their referred patients from specialists or other services “most of the time” [56].

Patient-centered interactions

Although patient-centered interactions are a cornerstone of the PCMH model, this domain received the lowest overall score and showed a strong correlation with total PCMH-A scores. Both quantitative and qualitative findings identified three main challenges contributing to this gap: language barriers, limited patient involvement, and weak community engagement.

Language barriers pose a substantial obstacle given the diverse patient population in Saudi Arabia. According to the 2024 Saudi Census, non-Saudis make up 44.4% of the national population, with particularly high proportions in Network A (68.2%) and Network B (50.3%) [57]. Many of these individuals come from varied cultural and educational backgrounds and are non-Arabic speakers. In the absence of professional interpreter services, informal solutions—such as using staff or family members as interpreters or relying on mobile applications—are commonly used. However, these methods often lack consistency and may compromise the quality of communication. In fact, Van Den Muijsenbergh et al. [58] found that primary care research often underrepresents underserved populations, including non-native speakers and individuals with low health literacy, leaving their needs insufficiently addressed in existing care models.

Limited patient involvement also remains a significant challenge. Cultural differences—combined with hierarchical medical traditions and limited awareness of patient rights—often lead patients to defer decisions to physicians rather than engage actively in discussions. This tendency is reinforced by time pressures, high patient volumes, and the limited use of shared decision-making tools. International evidence shows similar patterns in collectivist or migrant populations in which patients often prefer physician-led decisions and express less autonomy during consultations [59, 60], and concerns regarding limited patient engagement have also been identified in the Saudi primary healthcare context [61].

Finally, strengthening community engagement appears to be a key determinant of patient-centered interactions. Although community engagement is widely recognized as essential for tailoring services to local needs and improving the impact of PHC interventions [62], our findings show that it remains underdeveloped in most centers. In Networks A and B, limited mechanisms for community participation may help explain weaker performance in patient-centered interactions domain. In contrast, Network C’s stronger results suggest that active and structured engagement—through multilingual support systems, community representatives, digital feedback platforms, and partnerships with local charitable organizations—supports more culturally responsive care. These findings align with broader evidence that community participation can serve as a practical mechanism for strengthening PHC quality in diverse settings [62].

Strengths and successful initiatives

Despite the identified challenges, the study revealed several successful initiatives and strengths that positively impact healthcare delivery within the PHC centers, specifically through quality improvement initiatives and the integration of health technology.

Quality improvement initiatives

Quality improvement strategy was the highest-scoring domain across all PHC centers and showed the strongest correlation with overall PCMH-A score (r = 0.842). This finding suggests that quality improvement has become increasingly embedded in PHC operations, a trend reinforced by national regulatory reforms including the requirement for CBAHI accreditation, which the medical directors identified as a key driver of structured quality practices.

Evidence from both local and international contexts further supports the positive influence of accreditation on quality performance. A Saudi study reported that accreditation enhanced hospital processes and contributed to improvements in patient care and service delivery [63]. Similar findings have been observed in cross-sectional studies from Iran, Denmark, and India [64]. The cumulative effect of these efforts is reflected in PHC performance: A 2020 study found that non-accredited PHC centers scored significantly lower across several primary healthcare domains—particularly comprehensive care and community orientation—compared with accredited centers [56].

Adoption of health technology

The deployment of digital health solutions has seen significant acceleration in the wake of the COVID-19 pandemic, with telemedicine becoming an integral part of healthcare delivery nationwide. This study showed the widespread adoption of a unified EHR system, Raqeem, across all PHC centers in the Eastern Health Cluster to be a significant strength. The medical directors widely expressed appreciation for this system’s role in improving chronic disease management and ensuring more consistent clinical documentation, which enhances data-driven care and information continuity. This study’s findings align with another assessment conducted in Saudi Arabia early in 2025 in which more than 90% of medical doctors agreed with the statement “Information from the EHRs enables me to make better decisions about patient care” [65]. The Sehhaty application was also frequently mentioned as a valuable tool, with the MOH reporting more than 31 million individuals benefiting from it [66].

Implications for healthcare policy and practice

The findings of this study have important implications for advancing patient-centered primary healthcare in the Eastern Health Cluster and, more broadly, across Saudi Arabia. These recommendations align with national healthcare reforms and highlight practical strategies to strengthen both policy and service delivery.

Policy Implications

A key MOH policy priority is the gradual decentralization of decision-making and resource allocation to PHC centers. Granting local leadership greater autonomy in budgeting and procurement would enable more responsive service delivery, especially in underserved or rapidly growing areas.

In terms of digital health, policies should mandate investment in robust, fully functional digital health platforms, ensuring seamless integration between PHC center and hospital information systems. Such integration is essential for improving care coordination, especially during patient transitions from emergency or inpatient settings.

To formalize PCC models, policies should support and fund comprehensive awareness campaigns for the TBC model, targeting both patients and providers. Consideration should also be given to implementing a “catchment area policy” to foster consistent follow-up and relationship-based care.

Finally, policies should promote culturally sensitive care initiatives, including the employment of social workers in low-income districts. Evidence from Saudi-based studies involving family physicians has highlighted a disconnect between primary care providers and social workers, which may negatively affect patient outcomes [67]. Bridging this gap requires targeted efforts to strengthen collaboration within primary care teams. In parallel, establishing and funding formal multilingual translation services—including sign language—and maintaining official registries of trained community translators are essential for improving communication, enhancing patient engagement, and promoting inclusivity in service delivery.

Practice-level recommendations

At the operational level, PHC centers should implement targeted outreach to improve digital literacy, particularly in high walk-in areas, to enhance the use of appointment scheduling systems. Dedicated triage systems could help manage walk-in traffic and redirect patients appropriately, maintaining care continuity.

Formalizing the use of communication tools, such as assigning each PHC center its own telephone and WhatsApp number with clearly defined staff roles and response protocols, would help standardize patient interactions and reduce communication gaps.

To strengthen TBC implementation, PHC centers should actively engage patients by increasing awareness of the model, encouraging appointment booking with assigned care teams, and issuing timely reminders. Moreover, both patients and healthcare providers should receive training on the principles and benefits of the TBC model to ensure consistent understanding and application. Community engagement strategies—such as establishing connections with local representatives, partnering with charitable organizations, and fostering patient advocacy initiatives—should be expanded to address the social determinants of health, amplify patients’ voices in care delivery, and extend care beyond the clinic walls.

This study reveals a persistent “know–do” gap, where policies—such as those promoting team-based care or collecting patient feedback—exist but are not consistently applied in practice. Bridging this gap requires clearer procedures, better staff support, and a stronger culture that sees PCC as essential.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the PCMH-A tool is based on self-reported data, which may introduce social desirability bias. Second, because the study was conducted within three health networks of the Eastern Health Cluster, the findings may not fully represent the broader diversity of PHC centers across Saudi Arabia. Third, the cross-sectional design provides only a single point-in-time assessment, limiting the ability to evaluate changes over time or draw causal inferences. Finally, the data were collected exclusively from medical directors, which means the perspectives of frontline staff and patients, whose experiences are essential to understanding PCC, were overlooked.

Directions for future research

Based on the findings and limitations of this study, several avenues for future research are recommended to deepen the understanding and enhance the implementation of PCC in Saudi Arabia’s primary healthcare sector. Longitudinal studies are essential for assessing the long-term impact of ongoing health reforms and specific interventions on PCMH-A scores and patient outcomes. Incorporating direct patient feedback and perspectives from a wider range of healthcare staff, including nurses and allied health professionals, would provide a more comprehensive and multi-faceted understanding of patient-centered practices. Designing and evaluating targeted interventions based on the identified challenges, such as digital literacy programs for patients, specialized training on patient-centered communication for staff, or pilot programs for catchment area policies, would be beneficial. Investigating the cost-effectiveness of implementing PCMH principles within the Saudi context could provide crucial economic justifications for policy decisions. Finally, conducting comparative studies across different health clusters or regions within Saudi Arabia would help identify regional variations, unique challenges, and transferable best practices.

Conclusions

PHC is the backbone of Saudi Arabia’s health system and plays a vital role in achieving the goals of Vision 2030. This study shows that the implementation of PCC across PHC centers in the Eastern Health Cluster is progressing moderately, with notable strengths in quality improvement efforts and the adoption of digital health technologies. However, persistent gaps remain in access to care, care coordination, and patient-centered interactions. Addressing these weaknesses will require targeted system-level improvements and leadership commitment. Strengthening these areas is essential for advancing Saudi Arabia’s Vision 2030 goals and ensuring that PHC services are equitable, efficient, and patient centered.
