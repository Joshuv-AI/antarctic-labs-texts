Quasi-Newton optimised Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks for wind farm power prediction
Authors: Auwalu Saleh Mubarak, Zubaida Said Ameen, Sagiru Mati, Ayodele Lasisi, Quadri Noorulhasan Naveed, Rabiu Aliyu Abdulkadir
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Having accurate and effective wind energy forecasting that can be easily incorporated into smart networks is important. Appropriate planning and energy generation predictions are necessary for these infrastructures. The production of wind energy is linked to instability and unpredictability. Wind energy forecasting has traditionally been performed using statistical models, but with the advent of artificial intelligence (AI), research indicates that AI is more accurate than the statical technique. In this study, the nominal power of six wind farms in China was predicted using Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KAN) and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) models. KAN as an alternative to the conventional MLP, has the ability to handle problems with scalability, vanishing gradients, and interpretability associated with MLP. The KAN uses learnable B-Spline as activation functions prompting it to address the issues of the MLP. We employed the Radial Basis Function (RBF) with Gaussian kernels to approximate the 3-order B-spline basis. In most deep learning models stochastic gradient-based optimization algorithms such as Adaptive Moment Estimation (ADAM) and Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) optimizer are mostly employed, a quasi-Newton optimization technique Limited-memory Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno algorithm LBFGS was employed in this work to approximate the Hessian matrix and estimate the parameter space's curvature. Also, in the preprocessing of the data, the Interquartile Range (IQR) technique is used to handle outliers and a clustering-based K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) imputer to handle missing values. Based on different sites, the KAN-LBFGS shows superior performance based on the performance evaluation metrics with site 5 achieving MSE of 0.0039, RMSE of 0.0622, MAE of 0.0352, and DC of 0.9468. The study highlights the importance of the model’s architecture, preprocessing and optimization techniques.

Introduction

In the last few decades, there has been an increasing emphasis on sustainable natural resource development and reducing climate change, which has sparked transformations in the energy sector. Goals 13 and 15 of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) can assist in battling climate change by forest preservation, achieving net zero emissions and embracing renewable energy sources such as solar, bioenergy, geothermal hydropower, and wind energy [1]. The incorporation of zero-carbon electrical energy production into energy portfolios has become an area of much interest. Wind power has been viewed as a promising alternative to traditional energy resources, namely fossil fuels which have faced an increase in prices and the worldwide population is ever-growing [2]. The World Wind Energy Council report shows that in 2023, the installed capacity of wind power in the globe has reached 1 TW an increase of 13 % compared to the previous year [3]. The wind is harvested by wind turbines for electricity generation through the conversion of the kinetic energy of wind to electricity. Wind turbines are installed on vast lands of open space where the wind speeds are relatively higher facilitating maximum energy generation.

Even though wind energy provides immense promise, it poses planning and operational issues due to the sporadic nature of wind resources [4]. Several studies have been conducted on how to optimize wind turbines' aerodynamics optimization [5,6], the shape of the blades [7], power curves [8], and transforming intermittent wind power into a controllable and affordable power source [9]. That being said, one of the most important parts of effectively integrating wind energy is accurately predicting wind energy production. Wind forecasting allows coordination in energy demand and supply therefore avoiding overproduction [10] and at the same time allowing maintenance to be scheduled based on power predictions [11]. Power forecasting models are mainly classified into physical and statistical. The systems can accurately estimate wind power generation, but they are fundamentally different in approach [12]. Physical models use mathematical equations to mimic the highly complex and nonlinear dynamics of airflow, providing Numerical Weather Predictions (NWP). The resulting NWPs are customized to local flow conditions and then used as inputs in wind energy forecasting systems [13]. Historical data is used in statistical methods to predict future power generation. These methods mostly rely on models such as autoregressive (AR) or Autoregressive Moving Average Model (ARMA). The advancement of technology, which is mainly marked by artificial intelligence, has enhanced the development of different sectors from transport, communication, weather and many more [14]. In the power and energy sector, machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DP) algorithms such as artificial neural networks (ANNs) have led to rapid output power forecasting. The computational capabilities of deep learning models are more robust in handling complex functions than shallow machine learning approaches. Deep learning models might automatically extract relevant characteristics from data at various degrees of complexity, from low-level to high-level representations, by utilising multi-layered network topologies and nonlinear optimization approaches [15]. To make use of the deep learning models for forecasting, there is a need for data which in recent years has been supplied by the built-in Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) systems. This type of data is large and complex which is the perfect candidate to be processed by machine learning techniques.

This overcomes the above limitations of a single prediction model by identifying the best features, hyperparameters, and training methods. Literature review

As mentioned earlier, wind power forecasting can be approached using two methods, statistical and physical. An example of a physical forecasting model application was done by Ref. [16] who used the Weather Research and Forecasting (WRF) model, to predict the operational performance of a wind farm which was in a complex terrain. The researchers established that the WRF accuracy affirms the use of numerical weather prediction tools for operational data in wind farms [17]. also simulated the wind farm flow for three different horizontal resolutions. Their study concluded that 200 m was appropriate for wake modelling. Additional analysis revealed consistent wake interactions, wind speed, and power output deficits for an offshore wind farm. Wakes were discovered to extend up to 10 km downstream with a wind speed deficit of more than 10 %; for a further distance of 18 km, the shortfall was 2 %. In another study [18], used WRF and Open Foam models to analyze wind resources over Patagonia. Forecasts of wind energy and maps were generated to enable wind energy integration into the electric work. The data was acquired from an operational wind farm which was then used to evaluate the accuracy of the analysis. They concluded that the data analyzed gave a good correlation with the annual wind statistics and are useful where no measurements are available.

While some researchers apply physical models, which mainly excel in immediate forecasts that are affected by the current weather dynamics, some choose to apply statistical models. The main advantage is that they are beneficial for longer-term forecasting through historical patterns and trends. Artificial intelligence techniques can be applied to forecast wind power as they can capture recurrences and non-linearity in datasets. For instance Ref. [19], relied on Markov-switching autoregressive (MSAR) models to capture the fluctuations in wind power production data. With an objective criterion based on penalized maximum likelihood, the MSAR models were employed to predict one step in a10 10-min time series of wind power. The study concluded that the MSAR models can be used to generate offshore wind power fluctuations [20]. proposed an ensemble regressor which comprised of ANNs and Genetic Programming to predict short-term wind power. To overcome the variation in the leading predictor as is in most cases, the authors used a genetic programming-based combination of neural networks. Data was collected from five different parts of Europe and applied to the model whereby the average root mean squared error (RMSE) of the model was 0.117575. In another study [21], predicted the wind speed in a period of between two and 4 h at multiple points using spectral clustering (SC) and Echo State Networks (ESNs) based methods. Similar samples from the historical data were selected using the SC to form training and validation sets. The ESN was used for the prediction of multiple outputs. The proposed model acquired a higher accuracy compared to traditional models in predicting wind speeds at multiple time points [22]. proposed an ensemble model to forecast wind power while also acknowledging the uncertainty of wind power data. The proposed model was based on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and wavelet transform. The authors used wind data from a farm in China and the results showed that the ensemble model could be used to learn the uncertainties in wind power data.

Deciding the number of input features plays a very important role in determining the accuracy of the model. As argued by some research, improving wind power forecasts may be attained by adding more inputs [23]. showed that more accurate predictions can be made when the average wind speed and wind direction are added to the wind power as the input features while using a random forest model (RF). This is, however, not the case sometimes as some features may degrade the results. Another research as illustrated by Ref. [24] added wind direction as one of the input features to assess the performance of his ANN model. The result showed that adding wind direction reduces the errors. Hyper-parameter selection is also very important in wind power prediction [25]. Throughout the training process, the weights and biases which are the model parameters are determined. It must be noted that the algorithms do not directly learn the parameters rather are given outside the training process. The parameters improve forecasting accuracy and reduce the model’s complexity. The most commonly used methods for hyperparameter tuning are the grid and random search [26]. Both search algorithms produce all candidate hyperparameter combinations up front and then evaluate them in parallel. The optimum hyperparameters can be chosen after evaluating all possible combinations. Trying all potential combinations is quite costly as a result, it is critical to create improved approaches to intelligently select which hyperparameters to examine and then decide where to sample next after evaluating their quality.

In [27], two distinct ANN networks optimised using the PSO and GA algorithms are given for wind power forecasting. The dataset used in the study was gathered from Binh Thuan, Vietnam's Tuy Phong wind power plant. The PSO optimises the parameters of the ANN network in the first stage. A second PSO or GA algorithm is then used to fine-tune the hyper-parameters, producing two distinct architectures: GA-PSO-ANN and PSO-PSO-ANN. The models' ability to forecast is assessed using the metrics MSE and MAPE. The Boost-LR is a new integrated method that combines random forest, categorical boosting, extreme gradient boosting, non-parametric models, and linear regression to estimate wind power 1 h in advance. Compared to CatBoost, the second-best performing model, the method improves the MAE of Boost-LR by 31.42 %, 32.14 %, and 27.55 % for the datasets of Bruska, Jelinak, and Inland wind farms, respectively [28].

[29]developed a hybrid model called VMD-CNN-GRU, which combines a convolutional neural network and a gated recurrent unit to estimate wind power forecasts in Shanxi, China. The wind speed uncertainty is decreased by the VMD, the spatial parameter is extracted from wind power data by CNN, and temporal features are extracted from historical data using GRU. Results show that compared to the other deep-learning models, the suggested VMD-CNN-GRU performs better in short-term predicting. The RMSE, MAE, MAPE, and R-squared values reported by the model are 1.5651, 0.8161, 11.62 %, and 0.9964, respectively. In Ref. [30], a categorical generative adversarial network (CGAN) is used to accurately cluster data to perform NWP-based feature categorisation. To improve residual block activation, a gating mechanism is included along with an improved TCN prediction model. A graph attention convolutional recurrent (GACR) approach that combined GCN and LSTM models was presented by the authors in Ref. [31] High dimensional data, including multivariate time series and wind farm geoinformation, is gathered to train the model. In the study, a double-channel feature extraction technique is utilised to extract the most pertinent features. Additionally, the suggested model is contrasted with several methods utilising NMAE, symmetric MAPE (sMAPE), accept rate, RMSE, and MAE. A hybrid CNN and LSTM network model is put forth by Ref. [32]. For the short-term forecasting of solar irradiance and wind power. The CNN-LSTM network's hyper-parameters are optimised through the application of the swarm intelligence (SI) method. The CNN-LSTM network that has been optimised is evaluated against the individual models of CNN and LSTM, as well as against their hybrid designs that have been optimised through the use of various error measurement techniques and other meta-heuristic algorithms [33]. present a bi-party engaged data-driven modelling framework (BEDMF) to address the data privacy issue in renewable power production forecasting. Multiple sites are used to gather temporal and spatial data in the first stage. There are two stages to the suggested BEDMF approach, which uses both local and global models. The local model learns the local latent characteristics during the pre-training phase, and these are combined to create global latent features. Learning local and global latent characteristics from local and global data from the previous iteration, respectively, is the fine-tuning stage's task. The study's conclusions show that, on average, the suggested method outperforms other cutting-edge methods by 3 %. Multi-layer Perceptrons (MLPs) are a type of feedforward artificial neural network that includes a minimum of three layers of nodes, an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer [35,36]. MLPs, as mentioned in the universal approximation theorem [37], can acquire knowledge about non-linear connections and patterns in data. This makes them a fundamental component of contemporary deep learning applications [[38], [39], [40]]. Nevertheless, MLPs suffer from a lack of interpretability [41] because of their intricate and highly layered structure. Additionally, they frequently encounter obstacles like overfitting, disappearing or exploding gradients, and scalability difficulties. Researchers have recently introduced Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KANs) as a potential substitute for MLP. KANs are a novel model that strives to improve accuracy and interpretability compared to MLP [42]. KANs are derived from the Kolmogorov-Arnold representation theorem and may be seen as a fusion of Kolmogorov Networks [[43], [44], [45], [46], [47], [48]] and MLPs with adjustable activation functions [49,50]. KANs and their expanding expansions have demonstrated encouraging results in tackling many challenges in multilayer perceptron (MLP) models, including interpretability and catastrophic forgetting in both supervised and unsupervised learning tasks [51,52] Nevertheless, their formulation depends on the use of learnable B-Splines as activation functions, which leads to a substantial increase in their computational expense. To tackle this issue, several research papers have suggested the use of alternative univariate functions such as radial basis functions (RBF) [53], wavelets [54], or Jacobi polynomials [55] KANs have been investigated for their potential in solving differential equations and facilitating operator learning [42]. utilised physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) [56] and KANs to successfully solve a 2D Poison equation [57]. introduced DeepOKAN, a KAN operator network based on RFB, to address a 2D orthotropic elasticity problem. The authors in Refs. [42,57] demonstrate that KANs exhibit superior performance compared to MLP. However, it is important to note that these investigations were restricted to shallow networks and focused on simplified issue scenarios.

Research has been done on the idea of employing the Kolmogorov-Arnold representation theorem to construct neural networks [43,[58], [59], [60]] Nevertheless, the majority of research has remained trapped with the initial depth-2 width-(2n + 1) representation, and many were unable to use more advanced methods (such backpropagation) to train the networks. The scourge of dimensionality was broken both empirically and with an approximation theory given compositional structures of the function in Ref. [58], which examined a depth-2 width- (2n + 1) representation. KANs are simply combinations of splines and MLPs, utilising their respective advantages and avoiding their respective disadvantages, despite their sophisticated mathematical interpretation. Splines can be easily adjusted locally, are correct for low-dimensional functions, and can transition between multiple dimensions. However, due to the inability to take advantage of compositional structures, splines suffer greatly from the curse of dimensionality (COD). However, because of their incapacity to optimize univariate functions, MLPs are less accurate than splines at low dimensions, even though they experience less COD. The relationship between splines and MLPs with ReLU-k activation functions has been demonstrated in Ref. [60]. A model needs to be able to estimate the univariate functions (internal degrees of freedom) and learn the compositional structure (external degrees of freedom) to learn a function accurately. KANs are an example of this kind of model since they have MLPs outside and splines within. KANs can learn new features and optimize existing ones with exceptional accuracy due to their external resemblance to MLPs and internal similarity to splines. Hence, given a high dimensional function, MLPs may be able to learn the generalised additive structure; yet, when utilising, for example, ReLU activations, they are incredibly inefficient at approximating the exponential and sine functions. Splines, on the other hand, would fail for big N owing to COD. On the other hand, KANs significantly outperform MLPs in learning both the compositional structure and the univariate functions.

Optimizations techniques can be categorized into three categories analytical approaches [61], numerical approaches [62,63], and evolutionary algorithms [60,64,65]. Stochastic gradient-based optimization plays a crucial role in several scientific and engineering disciplines. Many issues in these domains may be framed as the optimization of a scalar parameterized objective function that necessitates either maximising or minimising its parameters. Gradient descent is a very efficient optimization approach when the function is differentiable concerning its parameters. This is because computing the first-order partial derivatives concerning all the parameters has the same processing complexity as just evaluating the function. Frequently, objective functions exhibit stochastic behaviour. For instance, several objective functions consist of a summation of subfunctions assessed at various subsets of data. In such cases, optimization can be enhanced by performing gradient steps concerning each subfunction, which is known as Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) or ascent. Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) has demonstrated its efficacy and effectiveness as an optimization technique that played a crucial role in several successful machine learning applications, including recent advancements in deep learning [66,67]. Objectives may also be affected by sources of noise other than data subsampling, such as dropout and regularisation [63]. Efficient stochastic optimization techniques are necessary for any targets that involve noise. Adam, a very efficient stochastic optimization approach that just relies on first-order gradients and has minimal memory demands. The approach calculates distinct adaptive learning rates for various parameters based on estimations of the first and second moments of the gradients. The term "Adam" is derived from adaptive moment estimation. The purpose of the AdaGrad [68] method is to integrate the benefits of two widely used methods which are effective with sparse gradients, and RMSProp [63] which performs well in online and non-stationary scenarios. L-BFGS [62] is a quasi-Newton optimization algorithm that approximates the curvature of the parameter space by estimating the Hessian matrix. It is efficient for parameter spaces that have long, almost level troughs, but it incurs additional expenses by executing a rank-two update to the Hessian approximation at each step. The effectiveness of BFGS algorithms relies on their convergence rate, which is heavily influenced by the specific situation at hand and is challenging to ascertain. Material and methods

Dataset

Wind farms utilize a supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) system to connect hardware and software for monitoring, controlling, and analyzing activities including data visualization, alarm function, problem detection, and emergency offload. Data from six wind farms were gathered by SCADA systems, representing various climatic zones and geographic regions in North, Central, and Northwest China. The selected farms have a nominal power production capacity ranging from 36 MW to 200 MW. The original datasets were acquired from the Chinese State Grid and made available on GitHub at the following link: https://github.com/Bob05757/Renewable-energy-generation-input-feature-variables-analysis. The wind power generating data is located in the wind farms folder, containing six Microsoft Excel files documenting real-time power generation and meteorological conditions. Wind speeds were measured at various heights, with the speed at the wind turbine's wheel hub being the crucial component in estimating power output.

The wind power generation capacity varied from 36 MW to 200 MW, while the average output ranged from 6.7 MW to 72.7 MW. The wind speed at the wheel hub varied from 0 m/s to 36.9 m/s, with an average of around 6.0 m/s per year. The air temperature fluctuated between −24.5 °C and 37.6 °C, with an average of 8.5 °C annually. Weather conditions at different elevations displayed a uniform trend. Seasonally, the wind speed fluctuated, reaching greater levels in summer and lower levels in winter. Models

This section describes the models theories, data preprocessing, training and evaluation of the models.

Interquartile range (IQR)

The Interquartile Range (IQR) is a data preparation technique used to detect outliers and extreme values. This approach evaluates data dispersion by dividing a ranking dataset into four equal portions called quartiles. The values that divide each part are denoted by Q1, Q2, and Q3. Q1 and Q3 are the medians of the first and second half of the dataset, respectively. The interquartile range (IQR) is determined by subtracting the first quartile (Q1) from the third quartile (Q3). Anomalies are data points that fall below Q1 minus 1.5 times the interquartile range (IQR) or above Q3 plus 1.5 times the IQR. The boxplot illustrates the highest and lower values in a defined range using whiskers, with outliers displayed as individual data points. The IQR is calculated by dividing the dataset into four equal halves, with the lowest value represented by the bottom whisker and the highest value represented by the top whisker.

Kolmogorov-Arnold networks

Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KAN) [42], are a type of Neural Network architecture which are based on the novel research conducted by Andrey Kolmogorov and Vladimir Arnold. Elementary functions were modelled using continuous functions. KANs are specifically designed to efficiently acquire knowledge and estimate intricate, multi-dimensional functions by breaking them down into a sequence of less complicated functions. KANs aim to express a multivariate function f(x) as a combination of smaller functions, which can be efficiently learnt by a neural network. More precisely, a KAN depicts a mathematical function f(x) in Equation (1).(1)f(x)=∑qΦq(∑pϕq,p(xp))Where Φ and ϕ are functions that take just one variable. KANs utilize a hierarchical structure to represent intricate functions by decomposing them into less complicated elements. This enables the network to learn these components more effectively. To define the set of functions Φ and ϕ [42], utilised B-splines, which possess the theoretical capability to accurately approximate any smooth univariate functions inside a specified domain. However, the variables can exceed the specified range during the training step. To address this issue, an extra adjustment is made to the spline grids by rescaling them using the deBoor-Cox iteration [69] grid rescaling are theoretically sound method for determining the B-spline basis, but it can cause significant efficiency issues in KANs. FastKAN [70], is a novel implementation of KANs that greatly enhances the speed of model calculation. FastKAN specifically uses Radial Basis Functions (RBFs) with Gaussian kernels to simulate the 3-order B-spline basis. Additionally, layer normalization [71] is employed to ensure that the inputs do not deviate from the domain of the radial basis functions (RBFs). By integrating these modifications, FastKAN achieves a significantly more streamlined implementation while maintaining its correctness.

Radial basis functions (RBFs) [72] are a type of real-valued function that is determined only by the distance from a centre point, sometimes referred to as the radial distance. These fundamental functions have extensively been investigated in the fields of function approximation, machine learning, and pattern recognition problems. RBFs aim to represent a function by aggregating many radially symmetric functions, with each function centred at a distinct point in the input space. The result of an RBF network is the sum of several radial basis functions, multiplied by configurable coefficients. Mathematically, an RBF network with N centres may be expressed in Equation (2) as:(2)f(x)=∑i=1Nwi∅(‖x−ci‖)

The adjustable weights or coefficients are represented by the symbol wi, whereas the radial basis function, denoted as ϕ, is dependent on the distance between the input x and a centre xi. The Gaussian function is a highly utilised radial basis function (RBF), which may be mathematically expressed in Equation (3) as(3)∅(r)=exp(−r22h2)

The variable r represents the distance from the centre, whereas h is a parameter that determines the breadth or extent of the function. By utilising linear transformations, it is possible to effectively align a sequence of 3rd-order B-spline bases with Gaussian radial bases, as seen in Fig. 2. Thus, one may reliably substitute the computations of B-spline bases with Gaussian RBFs. FastKAN is a means of speeding up KAN by substituting B-splines with Gaussian RBFs. Furthermore, this also implies that KANs are, in fact, radial basis function (RBF) networks with stationary centres.Fig. 2Kolmogorov-Arnold networks (KAN).Fig. 2

Multilayer perceptron

The fundamental deep neural network model is the multilayer perceptron [73], which is referred to as a fully connected feed-forward neural network. Essentially, it is a specialized logistic regression classifier that uses a non-linear modification on the sample data. The input samples undergo non-linear modifications to a space where they may be separated linearly by a set of hidden layers. as presented in Equation (4).(4)y(x)=σ(WLσ(W(L−1)…σ(W(1)x+b1)…+bl−1)+bL)

The formula defines the input vector as x = (x1, x2, · · ·), and L is the number of layers. The output of each layer is used as the input for the subsequent layer, resulting in the final output y as presented in Fig. 3. MLPs, when equipped with enough neurons and the appropriate activation function, can approximate almost any continuous function on compact subsets of R n, as affirmed by the Universal Approximation Theorem [37]. This theorem forms the foundation for the capacity of neural networks to represent intricate and non-linear connections.Fig. 3Multilayer perceptron (MLP).Fig. 3

Training

This research focuses on the prediction power of six wind farms in China. The methodology of the study focuses on the data preprocessing, model training and evaluation of the model performance based on the performance evaluation criteria Mean Square error (MSE), Root Mean Square error (RMSE), Mean Average Error (MAE) and R2 or Differential Coefficient (DC), The data was first preprocessed by initially utilising a clustering-based KNN imputer to address missing values, followed by outlier removal using the Interquartile Range (IQR) approach. The data was normalized to scale the values between 0 and 1. 80 % of the data was allocated for training, while the remaining 20 % was utilised for testing. In the training phase, Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks(KAN) and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) with different optimizers limited-memory Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (LBFGS) [74], ADAM [75] and Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) [66] were trained to predict the power output of a wind farm within six locations. The KAN model consists of the input layer, hidden layer and output layer, the hidden layer is made up of FastKANLayer with 128 neurons, RBF, base activation and spline weight scaling factor for initializing weight. The MLP contain 168 and 64 dimension layers, each having ReLU activation, Batch Normalization and dropout. After the training, the model's performance was evaluated to find the model capable of predicting the power outputs based on the earlier mentioned performance evaluation methods. Conclusion

To predict the energy output of wind farms as governments look for green energy alternatives, wind power prediction is an important field of study. The research considers addressing outliers during the data processing phase and organizing the data to manage missing values using the KNN imputer. This offered more thorough data and addressed outliers that would have lowered the model's performance. Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KAN) and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) models were further compared. The KAN model, which uses Radial Basis Functions (RBFs) with Gaussian kernels instead of B-Splines, can address the MLP problems and increase the accuracy and interpretability. The MLP model often has drawbacks such as overfitting and scalability issues. Both quasi-Newton optimization and stochastic gradient-based optimization approaches were applied. Throughout all six wind farm sites, the KAN-LBFGS model worked very well, highlighting the significance of the model's architecture, optimization, and preprocessing in addressing this challenge. Even while KAN models have produced encouraging results, overfitting in certain instances (such as Site 1) suggests that both the architecture and optimization techniques of KAN still need to be improved. A further area of study might be establishing a balance between generalization and model complexity. To improve wind power prediction models even further, the paper recommends investigating alternative neural architectures and more advanced optimization algorithms. As wind energy becomes more and more integrated into the world's energy infrastructure, this is crucial for satisfying the increasing demand for accurate and dependable renewable energy predictions.

Future work

As all research gaps were mention it would be very important in the future to look into more data preprocessing, most especially the data cleaning and feature extraction. Also, bioinspired optimization and novel models can be employed to improve the accuracy and generalizations of wind power prediction.
