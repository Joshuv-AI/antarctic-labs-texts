The Impact of Hip Osteoarthritis on Patient Reported Outcomes Following Lumbar Fusion
Authors: Ali Farooqi, Joshua Mathew, Mitchell Ng, Roy Miloh, Emily Berthiaume, Yulia Lee, Gregorio Baek, William Green, Morgan Hitchner, Yasmine Eichbaum, Steven Banko, Manav Dasondi, Alan Mathew, Matthew Silva, Darshan Patel, Dipti Venkatesh, Andrew Vanichkachorn, Brandon Martinazzi, Alec Giakas, Bradley Hammoor, Jonathan Dalton, Jose A Canseco, Alan S Hilibrand, Alexander R Vaccaro, Christopher K Kepler, Gregory D Schroeder

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy DesignRetrospective Cohort Study.ObjectivesWith an aging population, the burden of concomitant joint disease continues to rise. The effects of hip OA on lumbar fusion outcomes are underexplored. Therefore, this study aimed to: (1) describe demographic differences between Osteoarthritis (OA) patients and those without OA; (2) assess perioperative differences; and (3) compare patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs).MethodsA retrospective review of a single academic tertiary center was performed (2016-2025). Patients undergoing lumbar fusion with isolated hip OA or no diagnosis of OA, and 12-month ODI scores were included. Patients with prior arthroplasty were excluded. Kellgren-Lawrence scores were collected through chart review or graded on x-ray when missing. Patients were stratified based on the presence of Hip OA. Bivariate comparison was performed as appropriate. Multivariable regressions were performed to identify independent predictors of PROMs.ResultsA total of 271 patients were included (No OA: 229; OA: 42). There were significant differences between groups regarding age (71.8 versus 60.2 years, P<0.001), ethnicity (P=0.025), and smoking status (P<0.001). Most patients with OA had moderate OA based on KL grading, with grade 3 being most common (35.7%). On bivariate analysis, there were no differences in PROMs or improvement at any timepoint except Δ6month VASback (P=0.024). Multivariable regression analysis showed that the presence of hip OA was not an independent predictor of PROMs or improvement from baseline (all P>0.05).ConclusionsThese findings suggest that concomitant hip OA may not independently impact postoperative recovery and should not be considered a contraindication to lumbar fusion.Keywords: Hip, Lumbar fusion, Osteoarthritis, Patient Reported Outcomes

Introduction

Lumbar fusion surgery is an increasingly commonly performed procedure for degenerative spine disease with studies reporting an estimated 88-276% increase in lumbar fusion surgeries in the United States in the past two decades.1,2 There has also been an associated rise in lumbar fusion surgeries being performed in older patients with increasing comorbidities. 3 The prevalence of hip Osteoarthritis (OA), a comorbidity more common in older populations, has also increased in parallel in recent years, reflecting the aging population in the United States.4,5 Expectedly, there has been a growth in patients presenting with coexisting hip and spine degeneration and an associated increase in patients with hip OA undergoing posterior spinal fusion.6-9 Patients with both hip and spinal disease, termed hip-spine syndrome, are more likely to have altered biomechanical load distribution and spinopelvic alignment, potentially affecting outcomes after lumbar fusion surgery.10,11

Studies have suggested that patients with concomitant hip OA have worse outcomes following lumbar fusion. Severe hip OA has been shown to be associated with an increased risk of revision after lumbar fusion.12,13 Moreover, severe hip OA has been shown to result in worse sagittal alignment following posterior spinal fusion surgery. 8 Other studies have suggested that concomitant hip or knee OA leads to more complications and worse patient reported outcomes after surgery for lumbar spinal stenosis. 14 However, few studies have specifically evaluated the impact of hip OA on patient reported outcomes after lumbar fusion surgery. This study sought to determine whether concomitant hip OA impacts patient reported outcomes among patients undergoing lumbar fusion surgery.

Methods

Study Design and Patient Selection

Following Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval, a retrospective chart review was performed at a tertiary academic center (2016-2025). Two independent Structured Query Language (SQL) searches were used for each cohort. The first query identified patients undergoing primary elective lumbar fusion with an additional coded diagnosis of hip OA. Patients without an available 12-month Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) score were excluded. The remaining patients were then manually checked to simultaneously exclude patients with prior hip or knee arthroplasty, radiographic or clinical evidence of OA in a joint other than the hip. Patients were also checked to confirm that they truly did have radiographic and clinical presence of hip OA. The second query was run for the no OA group, identifying patients undergoing primary elective lumbar fusion during the same period independent of OA diagnosis. Patients without 12-month ODIs were excluded, as well as patients undergoing surgery for tumor, trauma, infection, or revision. Patients were then manually screened for presence of OA in any joint, or prior arthroplasty status of any kind (Figure 1). Hip OA grading was obtained from radiology reports and clinic visits. Manual radiographic grading was performed using the Kellgren-Lawrence system (KL) when a report was unavailable.12,13 KL grading of the hip was performed on Anterior-Posterior (AP) pelvis films by 2 blinded medical students. Interobserver reliability was assessed with an agreement of 0.803 between reviewers, indicating strong agreement between reviewers.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome of interest for this study was 12-month ODI score based on the presence of hip OA. PROMs were collected preoperatively and throughout the postoperative period, and changes from baseline were calculated at 6-months and 12-months. Secondary PROMs included Visual Analog Scale (VAS) leg and back. Secondary outcomes included number of revision surgeries and time to revision surgery between groups.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to define baseline demographics and clinical variables between patients with hip OA and those without. Bivariate comparison consisted of t-tests and chi-squared tests as appropriate. Following bivariate comparison, multivariable regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of 6-month and 12-month PROMs and improvement from baseline.

Results

Patient Demographics

A total of 271 patients were included for analysis, with 42 (15.5%) of patients having coexisting hip OA. The overall cohort was a mean age of 62 years old and was 58.3% female. Patients with hip OA were significantly older (71.8 vs. 60.2 years, p<0.001) and differed in ethnicity (P=0.025), and smoking status (P<0.001) compared to patients without hip OA. Racial demographics were comparable between groups (P=0.244). Among patients with hip OA, the most common Kellgren-Lawrence classifications were grade 2 (33.3%) and grade 3 (35.7%). (Table 1).

Perioperative Outcomes

When comparing perioperative outcomes, patients with hip OA were more likely to undergo posterolateral fusion (92.9% vs. 29.3%, P<0.001) and were more likely to have a preoperative diagnosis of isolated lumbar stenosis (21.4% vs. 8.7%) and degenerative scoliosis (11.9% vs. 1.3%) (p<0.001). Spondylolisthesis was the most common preoperative diagnosis in both groups (82.1% vs 50.0%). Both cohorts had a similar number of levels fused and a similar number of single-level (73.8% vs. 64.6%) and multi-level fusions (26.2% vs. 35.4%). There was no significant difference in the number of patients that went on to a first revision surgery (16.7% vs. 13.7%) or a second revision surgery (2.4% vs. 1.3%). There was also no difference in time to first revision surgery between cohorts (635 days vs. 574 days). Additionally, 33.3% of patients with hip OA eventually went on to undergo total hip arthroplasty (Table 2).

Patient Reported Outcome Measures

Regarding patient reported outcomes, patients with and without hip OA had similar preoperative ODI (47.3 vs. 47.7, p=0.82), VAS back (7.12 vs. 6.75, p=0.52), and VAS leg (6.60 vs. 6.97, p=0.35) scores. Postoperatively, there were no significant differences in ODI scores at 6 months (25.9 vs. 23.9, p=0.56) or 12 months (26.6 vs. 23.5, P=0.21). There was also no significant difference in improvement in ODI score at 6-months (-21.1 vs. -23.2, p=0.58) or 12 months (-20.7 vs. -24.2, p=0.26). Similarly, postoperative VAS back and VAS leg scores and improvement from baseline were comparable between groups at both timepoints, except that patients with hip OA had slightly greater improvement in 6-month VAS back scores (-4.42 vs. -3.14, p=0.024) (Table 3).

Patient Reported Outcomes

As a result of the significant demographic differences present between groups, a multivariable regression was performed to identify independent predictors of postoperative patient reported outcome scores. While controlling for age, sex, BMI, and smoking status, hip OA was not found to be an independent predictor of postoperative ODI score at either 6 months (β=2.99, 95% CI: -5.23 to 11.21, p=0.477) or 12 months (β=1.95, 95% CI:-5.20 to 9.09, p=0.594). Hip OA was also not associated with postoperative VAS leg or VAS back at 6 months or 12 months (p>0.05). Likewise, hip OA was not associated with postoperative improvement in ODI, VAS leg, or VAS back at any timepoint (all p>0.05) (Table 4).

Subgroup Analysis of OA Severity

A subgroup analysis was completed comparing patients with mild OA (KL graded 1-2, n= 18) to those with severe OA (grade 3-4, n=24). Baseline demographics and surgical characteristics were comparable between the two subgroups with the exception of two outcomes. Patients with severe OA were significantly more likely to eventually undergo total hip arthroplasty (54.2% vs 5.6%, p =0.003). This is an expected finding due to more severe hip OA. Additionally, race distribution significantly differed between the subgroups (p=0.027), which should be interpreted cautiously due to the limited sample size. There were no statistically significant differences between mild and severe OA subgroups in preoperative, 6-months, or 12-month ODI, VAS back, or VAS leg scores. The deltas in each value at each time point were also not statistically significant.

Discussion

This study found that patients with concomitant hip OA were older and more likely to undergo surgery for lumbar stenosis and degenerative scoliosis. Patients with coexisting hip OA did not have an increased risk of revision or time to revision after lumbar fusion surgery. Moreover, patients with concomitant hip OA did not have worse postoperative patient reported outcomes, including ODI, VAS back, and VAS leg scores, at 6-months or 1-year follow-up. Hip OA was not found to be independently associated with patient reported outcomes after lumbar fusion surgery. Severity of hip OA was also not associated with patient reported outcomes following this procedure.

Patients with hip arthritis were older and more likely to have surgery for lumbar stenosis and degenerative scoliosis in this study. This demographic difference is similar to prior studies that have also shown that patients with hip OA that undergo lumbar fusion surgery are older. 8 This is consistent with the increasing prevalence of hip OA in older patients as hip OA is an age-related degenerative process. 15 Although few studies have compared surgical indications between patients with and without hip OA undergoing lumbar fusion, studies have suggested that patients with hip-spine syndrome frequently present with more complex and overlapping degenerative spinal diseases such as lumbar stenosis and spinal deformity.16-18 This finding is consistent with the results of this study which showed that patients with hip OA had more heterogenous preoperative diagnoses, including more isolated lumbar stenosis and degenerative scoliosis. This heterogenous presentation is important to consider to help guide the clinical evaluation and surgical indications for patients who are presenting with degenerative lumbar spinal disease in the setting of concomitant hip OA.

Previous studies evaluating the impact of hip OA on patient reported outcomes after spinal fusion are limited and have demonstrated mixed results. For example, a prior study reported that patients with severe hip OA undergoing spinal deformity surgery had worse baseline and postoperative VR-12 physical component scores. 8 However, Farias et al found that patients with severe hip OA undergoing transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF) had worse postoperative VAS leg scores and less improvement in VAS leg scores, but otherwise had similar postoperative ODI, VAS back, and PROMIS scores. 13 In comparison, this study found that concomitant hip OA was not associated with any worse baseline or postoperative patient reported outcome scores like ODI, VAS back, or VAS leg after lumbar fusion surgery. This study helps clarify that concomitant hip OA was not associated with a significant association in patient reported outcomes after lumbar fusion surgery and that even patients with hip OA can expect similar postoperative improvement after lumbar fusion surgery. Although this is important to consider during patient selection and surgical counseling, careful clinical and radiographic evaluation should continue to be used in patients presenting with concomitant hip OA given the overlap in clinical symptoms in patients presenting with hip-spine syndrome. 19

This study also found that patients with hip OA did not have an increased risk of revision or time to revision after lumbar fusion surgery. In contrast, other studies have reported that severe hip OA is associated with an increased rate of revision surgery after TLIF. 13 Mills et al also showed that patients with severe hip OA undergoing lumbar fusion are at increased risk of revision spine surgery at 3-years and 5-years postoperatively. 12 This difference in results is likely because these other studies only evaluated the impact of severe hip OA on outcomes after lumbar fusion. Our study helps clarify that hip OA, when including both moderate and severe grades of OA, was not associated with changes in time to revision or the rate of revision after lumbar fusion. Our subgroup analysis showed that in patients with OA, the degree of radiographic severity itself was not significantly associated with differences in postoperative PROMs at any time point (Table 5). However, this sample size of 42 patients may limit our application ability. Further studies are required with larger sample sizes to validate these findings of hip OA severity on outcomes following lumbar fusion surgery.

This study is associated with several limitations, including being a retrospective cohort study which is inherently subject to sampling bias. Another limiting factor is that there were significant differences in surgical approach and indication for surgery between cohorts which could serve as potential confounding variables and affect the results of this study. The OA cohort contained 42 patients, and a post hoc power analysis showed that this study has a power of 0.77 and an effect size of 0.46. Therefore, our study is slightly underpowered. To that end, it is important to view these findings as hypothesis generating and future multicentered studies with larger sample sizes should be conducted to further explore this relationship. Another limiting factor is that this study only evaluated patient reported outcomes up to 1-year postoperatively and was unable to determine whether the presence of hip OA affected longer-term outcomes after lumbar fusion surgery.

Conclusion

Concomitant hip OA was not associated with higher risk of revision or time to revision after lumbar fusion surgery. Patients with concomitant hip OA and patients with more severe OA were not significantly associated with worse postoperative patient reported outcomes, including ODI, VAS back, and VAS leg scores. Moreover, hip OA is not independently associated with patient reported outcomes after lumbar fusion surgery.
