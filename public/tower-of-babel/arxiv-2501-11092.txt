On Gegenbauer polynomials and Wronskian determinants of trigonometric functions
Minjian Yuan

Abstract
In [ 5 ] M. E. Larsen evaluated the Wronskian determinant of functions { sin ⁡ ( m ​ x ) } 1 ≤ m ≤ n \{\sin(mx)\}_{1\leq m\leq n} . We generalize this result and compute the Wronskian of { sin ( m x ) } 1 ≤ m ≤ n − 1 ∪ { sin ( ( k + n ) x } \{\sin(mx)\}_{1\leq m\leq n-1}\cup\{\sin((k+n)x\} . We show that this determinant can be expressed in terms of Gegenbauer orthogonal polynomials and we give two proofs of this result: a direct proof using recurrence relations and a less direct (but, possibly, more instructive) proof based on Darboux-Crum transformations.



Keywords
: Wronskian determinant, Gegenbauer polynomials, Darboux transformation, killed Brownian motion, transition probability

: Primary 15A15, Secondary 33C45

1
Introduction and the main result

Determinants play a crucial role in various branches of mathematics. For example, the Vandermonde determinant is widely used in polynomial interpolation, whereas determinants of Toeplitz matrices feature prominently in random matrix theory and integrable systems. Consequently, there have been numerous studies on finding explicit formulas for specific determinants. In
[
11
]
, R. Houston, A. P. Goucher, and N. Johnston present a new explicit formula for the determinant that contains superexponentially fewer terms than the usual Leibniz formula. In
[
10
]
, Z. Sun evaluates a determinant involving quadratic residues modulo primes. Our inspiration for this work comes from the following explicit formula obtained by M. E. Larsen in
[
5
]
:

{\textnormal{Wr}}\{\sin(x),\sin(2x),\sin(3x),\dots,\sin((n-1)x),\sin(nx)\}=(-2)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}\sin^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}(x)\cdot G(n+1),

(1)

where
G(\cdot)
stands for the Barnes
G
-function and its value at positive integers is given by

G(n+1)=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}j!

To present our main result, which is an extension of (
1
), we denote
W^{(k)}_{1}(x):=\sin((k+1)x)
and

W^{(k)}_{n}(x):={\textnormal{Wr}}\{\sin(x),\sin(2x),\sin(3x),\dots,\sin((n-1)x),\sin((n+k)x)\},\;\;\;n\geq 2.

The following theorem constitutes our main result.

Theorem 1
.

For integer
k\geq 0
and
n\geq 1

W^{(k)}_{n}(x)=(-2)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}\sin^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}(x)G(n+1)C^{(n)}_{k}(\cos(x)),

(2)

where
C^{(n)}_{k}(x)
is the Gegenbauer polynomial.

We provide two proofs of Theorem
1
. In Section
2
, we present a direct proof based on recurrence relations. In Section
3
, we give a more intuitive (though longer) proof using Darboux–Crum transformations. More precisely, the second proof only establishes (
2
) up to a
\pm
sign, but it clearly illustrates how and why Gegenbauer polynomials appear in (
2
).

2
Computing Wronskians using recurrence relations

For a smooth function
f:{\mathbb{R}}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
we define

{\mathbf{V}}_{n}(f(x)):=(f(x),f^{\prime}(x),f^{\prime\prime}(x),\dots,f^{(n)}(x))^{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}

(3)

We now present the following proposition from
[
5
]
:

Proposition 1
.

The Wronskian determinant satisfies

{\textnormal{Wr}}\{f,fg_{1},...fg_{n-1}\}=f^{n}{\textnormal{Wr}}\{g_{1},...,g_{n-1}\}

where
f,g_{1},,\dots,g_{n-1}
can be any smooth functions.

Note that the above formula can be stated in terms of our notation as follows:

\det\big(\mathbf{V}_{n}(f),\mathbf{V}_{n}(fg_{1}),\dots\mathbf{V}_{n}(fg_{n-1})\big)=f^{n}\det(\mathbf{V}_{n-1}(g^{\prime}_{1}),\dots,\mathbf{V}_{n-1}(g^{\prime}_{n-1}))

(4)

We use this result to establish the following lemma:

Lemma 1
.

Equation (
2
) holds for
k=1
, that is

W^{(1)}_{n}(x)=(-2)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}\sin^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}(x)G(n+1)2n\cos(x),

(5)

for all
n\geq 1
.

Proof.

We recall that the Chebyshev polynomial of the second kind
U_{n}(x)
, satisfies

\sin((n+1)x)=\sin(x)U_{n}(\cos(x)),

(6)

see formula (8.940.2) from
[
8
]
. To simplify the notation in the rest of the proof, we will denote

a=a(x):=\sin(x),\;\;\;\;b=b(x):=\cos(x).

(7)

Thus,
W^{(1)}_{n}(x)={\textnormal{Wr}}\{\sin(x),\sin(2x),\dots,\sin((n-1)x),\sin((n+1)x)\}
can be written as

W^{(1)}_{n}(x)=\det\big(\mathbf{V}_{n}(a),\mathbf{V}_{n}(aU_{1}(b)),\dots,\mathbf{V}_{n}(aU_{n-2}(b)),\mathbf{V}_{n}(aU_{n}(b))\big).

The Chebyshev polynomial
U_{n}(b)
is an
n^{\text{th}}
-order polynomial containing only terms of the form
b^{n-2m}
, and its leading coefficient is
2^{n}
. Writing the determinant
W^{(1)}_{n}(x)
as a sum of determinants whose last column equals
\mathbf{V}_{n}(ab^{n-2m})
, we see that each term (except for the one with
m=0
) vanishes because the columns are linearly dependent. Thus, we can simplify the above formula as

W^{(1)}_{n}(x)=\det\big(\mathbf{V}_{n}(a),\mathbf{V}_{n}(a(2b)),\dots,\mathbf{V}_{n}(a(2^{n-2}b^{n-2})),\mathbf{V}_{n}(a(2^{n}b^{n}))\big).

(8)

Applying Proposition
4
we obtain

\displaystyle W^{(1)}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\det\big(\mathbf{V}_{n-1}(-2a),\dots,\mathbf{V}_{n-1}(-2(n-2)a(2^{n-3}b^{n-3})),\mathbf{V}_{n-1}(-2na(2^{n-1}b^{n-1}))\big)

\displaystyle=(-2)^{n-1}n(n-2)!a^{n}W^{(1)}_{n-1}(x).

This gives us a recursion formula for
W_{n}^{(1)}
. Since we know the inital value
W_{1}^{(1)}=\sin(2x)
, formula (
5
) can now be easily obtained by induction.

Next, we establish the following recursion relation.

Lemma 2
.

For
n\geq 2
and
k\geq 0
, we have

W^{(k+2)}_{n}(x)=W^{(k)}_{n}(x)+(-2)^{n-1}(n+k+1)(n-2)!\sin^{n}(x)W^{(k+2)}_{n-1}.

(9)

Proof.

Using (
6
) and (
7
),we write

W^{(k+2)}_{n}(x)=\det\big(\mathbf{V}_{n}(a),\mathbf{V}_{n}(aU_{1}(b)),\dots,\mathbf{V}_{n}(aU_{n-2}(b)),\mathbf{V}_{n}(aU_{n+k+1}(b))\big).

Chebyshev polynomials satisfy the following three-term recurrence relation (see formula (8.940.2) in
[
8
]
):

U_{n+k+1}(b)=-U_{n+k-1}(b)+2b\,U_{n+k}(b).

Thus, we can write
W^{(k+2)}_{n}(x)=A+B
as the sum of two determinants:

A=\det\big(\mathbf{V}_{n}(a),\mathbf{V}_{n}(a(U_{1}(b)),\dots,\mathbf{V}_{n}(aU_{n-2}(b)),\mathbf{V}_{n}(-aU_{n+k-1}(b))\big)=-W_{n}^{(k)},

and

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\det\big(\mathbf{V}_{n}(a),\mathbf{V}_{n}(a(U_{1}(b)),\dots,\mathbf{V}_{n}(aU_{n-2}(b)),\mathbf{V}_{n}(2abU_{n+k}(b))\big)

\displaystyle=\det\big(\mathbf{V}_{n}(a),\mathbf{V}_{n}(a(U_{1}(b)),\dots,\mathbf{V}_{n}(aU_{n-2}(b)),\mathbf{V}_{n}(2b\sin((n+k+1)x))\big)

\displaystyle=\det\big(\mathbf{V}_{n}(a),\mathbf{V}_{n}(a(U_{1}(b)),\dots,\mathbf{V}_{n}(aU_{n-2}(b)),\mathbf{V}_{n}(2\sin((n+k)x))\big)

\displaystyle+\det\big(\mathbf{V}_{n}(a),\mathbf{V}_{n}(a(U_{1}(b)),\dots,\mathbf{V}_{n}(aU_{n-2}(b)),\mathbf{V}_{n}(2a\cos((n+k+1)x))\big)

\displaystyle=2W_{n}^{(k)}+2\det\big(\mathbf{V}_{n}(a),\mathbf{V}_{n}(a(U_{1}(b)),\dots,\mathbf{V}_{n}(aU_{n-2}(b)),\mathbf{V}_{n}(2a\cos((n+k+1)x))\big)

In the second step of simplifying the expression for
B
, we used the identity

\sin((n+k)x)=\sin((n+k+1)\cos(x)-\cos((n+k+1)x)\sin(x)

We now use (
4
) for the second term of
B
and obtain

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=2W_{n}^{(k)}(x)+2a^{n}\det\big(\mathbf{V}_{n-1}(\partial_{x}(U_{1}(b)),\dots,\mathbf{V}_{n-1}(\partial_{x}(U_{n-2}(b)),\mathbf{V}_{n-1}(\partial_{x}(\cos((n+k+1)x))\big)

\displaystyle=2W_{n}^{(k)}(x)+2a^{n}\det\big(\mathbf{V}_{n-1}(\partial_{x}(2b)),\dots,\mathbf{V}_{n-1}(\partial_{x}(2^{n-2}b^{n-2}),\mathbf{V}_{n-1}(\partial_{x}(\cos((n+k+1)x))\big)

\displaystyle=2W_{n}^{(k)}(x)+2a^{n}\det\big(\mathbf{V}_{n-1}(-2a),\dots

\displaystyle\qquad\dots,\mathbf{V}_{n-1}(-2(n-2)a(2^{n-3}b^{n-3})),\mathbf{V}_{n-1}(-(n+k+1)\sin((n+k+1)x))\big)

\displaystyle=2W_{n}^{(k)}(x)+(-2)^{n-1}(n+k+1)(n-2)!a^{n}W_{n-1}^{(k+2)}(x)

In the second step, we applied the same idea as in deriving (
8
) to remove all but the highest-order terms in
U_{n}(b)
. In the last step, we used the “reverse” idea of adding extra lower-order terms to recover
aU_{n}(b)=\sin((n+1)x)
from
ab^{n}
.
Combining the above equations gives us the desired result:

W^{(k+2)}_{n}(x)=A+B=W^{(k)}_{n}(x)+(-2)^{n-1}(n+k+1)(n-2)!a^{n}W^{(k+2)}_{n-1}(x)

\sqcap\kern-8.0pt\hbox{$\sqcup$}

Now, we define

\widetilde{W}_{n}^{(k)}(x):=(-2)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}\sin^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}(x)\,G(n+1)\,C^{(n)}_{k}\bigl(\cos(x)\bigr).

(10)

Our goal is to show that
\widetilde{W}_{n}^{(k)}
also satisfies the recursion relation (
9
).

Lemma 3
.

For
n\geq 2
and
k\geq 0
,

\widetilde{W}^{(k+2)}_{n}=\widetilde{W}^{(k)}_{n}+(-2)^{n-1}(n+k+1)\,(n-2)!\,\sin^{n}(x)\,\widetilde{W}^{(k+2)}_{n-1}.

(11)

Proof.

To prove (
11
), it suffices to show that

C^{(n)}_{k+2}(\cos(x))=C^{(n)}_{k}(\cos(x))+\frac{n+k+1}{n-1}\,C^{(n-1)}_{k+2}(\cos(x)).

(12)

According to formulas (8.933.1) and (8.933.2) from
[
8
]
, the Gegenbauer polynomials satisfy

k\,C_{k}^{(n)}(\cos(x))=2n\bigl[\cos(x)\,C_{k-1}^{(n+1)}(\cos(x))-C_{k-2}^{(n+1)}(\cos(x))\bigr]

and

(2n+k)\,C_{k}^{(n)}(\cos(x))=2n\bigl[C_{k}^{(n+1)}(\cos(x))-\cos(x)\,C_{k-1}^{(n+1)}(\cos(x))\bigr].

By adding these two equations together and then replacing
k
with
k+2
and
n
with
n-1
, we get

2\,(n+k+1)\,C_{k}^{(n)}(\cos(x))=2\,(n-1)\bigl[C_{k+2}^{(n)}(\cos(x))-C_{k}^{(n)}(\cos(x))\bigr],

which is precisely (
12
).

We need to show that

for all

. This result is clearly true when

(for all

). It is also true when

3
Computing Wronskians using the Darboux transformation

For
\mu\geq 0
, we consider a second-order linear differential operator

{\mathcal{L}}_{\mu}:=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}-\mu\csc^{2}(\pi x),

(13)

acting on twice continuously differentiable functions
f:(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}
.
If
\mu=0
, we impose the Dirichlet boundary conditions
f(0)=f(1)=0
. If
\mu>0
, boundary conditions are unnecessary, since both boundary points
0
and
1
are
natural
for the operator
{\mathcal{L}}_{\mu}
in the language of Feller boundary classification
(see
[
1
]
[Section II.6]).
Our first goal is to find a complete eigenbasis
\{f_{k}^{(\mu)}\}_{k\geq 0}
of
L_{2}((0,1),\mathrm{d}x)
. When
\mu=0
, it is well known that the functions

f_{k}^{(0)}(x)=\sin\bigl(\pi(k+1)\,x\bigr),\quad k\geq 0,

provide an orthogonal basis for functions in
L_{2}((0,1),\mathrm{d}x)
that satisfy the Dirichlet boundary conditions
f(0)=f(1)=0
.
To determine the eigenbasis for general
\mu>0
, we need to recall some results about the Gegenbauer polynomials
C_{k}^{(\mu)}(u)
. These polynomials
can be defined as the solutions to the Gegenbauer differential equation (see
[
9
]
).

\displaystyle(1-u^{2})y^{\prime\prime}(u)-(2\mu+1)uy^{\prime}(u)+k(k+2\mu)y(u)=0,

(14)

which satisfy

\int_{-1}^{1}C_{k}^{(\mu)}(t)C_{l}^{(\mu)}(t)(1-t^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}{\textnormal{d}}t=\frac{\pi 2^{1-2\mu}\Gamma(k+2\mu)}{k!(k+\mu)[\Gamma(\mu)]^{2}}\delta_{k,l},\;\;\;k,l\geq 0,

(15)

see formula (8.939.8) from
[
8
]
. The Gegenbauer polynomials form an orthogonal basis of

L_{2}((-1,1),(1-t^{2})^{\mu-1/2}{\textnormal{d}}t
.
Armed with these results, we can state the following

Proposition 2
.

Denote

\nu:=\frac{1}{2}\big(1+\sqrt{1+8\mu/\pi^{2}}\big)

(16)

and

f^{(\mu)}_{k}(x):=2^{\nu-1}\Gamma(\nu)\sin^{\nu}(\pi x)C_{k}^{(\nu)}(\cos(\pi x)),\;\;\;k\geq 0.

Then for all
k\geq 0
and
x\in(0,1)

{\mathcal{L}}_{\mu}f^{(\mu)}_{k}(x)=-\frac{1}{2}\pi^{2}(\nu+k)^{2}f^{(\mu)}_{k}(x).

The functions
\{f_{k}^{(\mu)}\}_{k\geq 0}
form a complete orthogonal basis of
L_{2}((0,1),{\textnormal{d}}x)
and they satisfy

\int_{0}^{1}f^{(\mu)}_{k}(x)^{2}{\textnormal{d}}x=\frac{\Gamma(2\nu+k)}{2(\nu+k)k!}.

(17)

This result follows from
[
4
]
by change of variables
x=u-1/2
. One could also prove it directly by using the equations (
14
) and (
15
).

Remark 1.

The above result allows us to write down a spectral expansion of the transition probability density of diffusion process
X
, which is defined as a Brownian motion on the interval
(0,1)
killed at rate
\mu\csc^{2}(\pi x)
. When
\mu=0
and we impose Dirichlet boundary conditions, this process is just the Brownian motion on
(0,1)
killed at the first time that it hits the boundary of the interval (see page 122 in
[
1
]
). When
\mu>0
, it follows from
[
1
]
[Section II.6] (see also
[
3
]
) that both boundaries are natural for
X
and its dynamics is defined solely by its Markov infinitesimal generator
{\mathcal{L}}_{\mu}
. For
\mu>0
the transition probability density function of
X
is given by

p_{t}(x,y)=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}e^{-\frac{\pi^{2}t}{2}(\nu+k)^{2}}f^{(\mu)}_{k}(x)f^{(\mu)}_{k}(y)\frac{2(\nu+k)k!}{\Gamma(2\nu+k)}.

(18)

This result is a generalization of formulas obtained in
[
3
]
[Section 5.4]. It is also a special case of the general results on diffusion semigroups whose generators have discrete spectrum-see equation 3.20 in
[
7
]
. The series in (
18
) converges pointwise due to the asymptotic result

C_{k}^{(\nu)}(\cos(\pi x))=\frac{\sin(\pi x)^{-\nu}2^{\nu}(2\nu)_{n}}{\sqrt{\pi n}(\nu+1/2)_{n}}\cos((k+\nu)\pi x-\pi\nu/2)+O(n^{-3/2}),\;\;\;n\to+\infty.

which can be derived from formulas
18.7.1 and 18.15.4_5 from
[
13
]
.

We next introduce the concept of the Darboux transformation. We start with a second order linear differential operator

{\mathcal{L}}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}-c(x),\;\;\;x\in(l,r),

and a function
\phi:(l,r)\mapsto(0,\infty)
which satisfies the eigenvalue equation
{\mathcal{L}}\phi=\lambda\phi
. We define a first order differential operator

{\mathcal{D}}_{\phi}:=\partial_{x}-\frac{\phi^{\prime}(x)}{\phi(x)}=\phi(x)\partial_{x}\Big(\frac{1}{\phi(x)}\Big)

(19)

and check that

{\mathcal{L}}=\lambda{\mathcal{I}}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{B}},

where

{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{D}}_{\frac{1}{\phi}}=\partial_{x}+\frac{\phi^{\prime}(x)}{\phi(x)},\;\;\;{\mathcal{B}}={\mathcal{D}}_{\phi}:=\partial_{x}-\frac{\phi^{\prime}(x)}{\phi(x)}.

The Darboux transform of
{\mathcal{L}}
is defined as

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}=\lambda{\mathcal{I}}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{B}}{\mathcal{A}}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}-\tilde{c}(x),

where

\tilde{c}(x):=c(x)-\partial_{x}^{2}\ln\phi(x).

The function
\phi
that is used to construct the Darboux transform is called
the seed function
.
One of the important property of the Darboux transformation, is the following intertwining relation

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}{\mathcal{D}}_{\phi}={\mathcal{D}}_{\phi}{\mathcal{L}},

(20)

In particular, if
f
is a solution to
{\mathcal{L}}f=\kappa f
then
g={\mathcal{D}}_{\phi}f
solves
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}g=\kappa g
.

The Darboux transform can be iterated, in which case it is called
called the Darboux-Crum transformation. The main properties of the Darboux -Crum transformation are collected in the next

Theorem 2

(
[
2
,
6
]
)
.

Let
h_{1},\dots,h_{n}
be a set of
n
eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{L}
. We perform an
n
-step Darboux transformation with these seed eigenfunctions and obtain a chain of second order linear operators

\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{L}^{(1)}\to\dots\to\mathcal{L}^{(n)}.

The operator
\mathcal{L}_{n}
is given by

\mathcal{L}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}_{x}-c_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}_{x}-c(x)+\partial_{x}^{2}\ln\textnormal{Wr}\{h_{1},\dots,h_{n}\}

(21)

If
\psi
is a solution to
\mathcal{L}\psi=\kappa\psi
, then the function

\psi_{n}=\frac{\textnormal{Wr}\{h_{1},\dots,h_{n},\psi\}}{\textnormal{Wr}\{h_{1},\dots,h_{n}\}}

(22)

is a solution to
\mathcal{L}^{(n)}\psi_{n}=\kappa\psi_{n}
.

Consider an operator
{\mathcal{L}}=\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}_{x}
on
x\in(0,1)
with Dirichlet boundary conditions. It is clear that
h_{k}(x)=\sin(\pi kx)
are the eigenfunctions of
{\mathcal{L}}
with eigenvalues
-\pi^{2}k^{2}/2
. We perform Darboux-Crum transformation times with
h_{k}(x)=\sin(k\pi x)
,
k=1,2,\dots,n-1
. According to (
1
), we have
\textnormal{Wr}\{h_{1},\dots,h_{n-1}\}=C(n)\sin^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}(\pi x)
and from (
21
) we conclude that the transformed operator
{\mathcal{L}}^{(n-1)}
is

{\mathcal{L}}^{(n-1)}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)\pi^{2}\csc^{2}(\pi x).

Next we take
\psi(x)=h_{n+k}(x)=\sin((n+k)\pi x)
and compute
\psi_{n-1}
in formula (
22
) (which we denote by
g_{k}^{(n-1)}(x)
)

g^{(n-1)}_{k}(x)=\frac{{\textnormal{Wr}}\{\sin(\pi x),\sin(2\pi x),\dots,\sin((n-1)\pi x),\sin((n+k)\pi x)\}}{{\textnormal{Wr}}\{\sin(\pi x),\sin(2\pi x),\dots,\sin((n-1)\pi x)\}}=\frac{W_{n}^{k}(\pi x)}{W_{n-1}^{0}(\pi x)}.

(23)

According to Theorem
2
, the function
g^{(n-1)}_{k}(x)

is a solution to the second-order linear differential equation
{\mathcal{L}}^{(n-1)}g=-\frac{1}{2}\pi^{2}(n+k)^{2}g
.
On the other hand, we note that
{\mathcal{L}}^{(n-1)}={\mathcal{L}}_{\mu_{n}}
as defined in (
13
), with

\mu_{n}:=\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)\pi^{2}.

(24)

From (
16
) we find that
\nu=n
, and Proposition
2
tells us that
f_{k}^{(\mu_{n})}(x)
is also a solution to
{\mathcal{L}}^{(n-1)}g=-\frac{1}{2}\pi^{2}(n+k)^{2}g
. Because a second-order linear differential equation
{\mathcal{L}}^{(n-1)}g=-\frac{1}{2}\pi^{2}(n+k)^{2}g
has two linearly independent solutions, and Proposition
2
indicates that
f_{k}^{(\mu_{n})}(x)
is the unique solution (up to multiplication by a constant) in
L_{2}((0,1),{\textnormal{d}}x)
, it follows from Crum
[
2
]
that the solutions constructed in Theorem

are
L_{2}((0,1),{\textnormal{d}}x)
closed and complete, and converge to zero as
x\to 0^{+}
or
x\to 1-
. We conclude that the two solutions
g^{(n-1)}_{k}(x)
and
f^{(\mu_{n})}_{k}(x)
to
{\mathcal{L}}^{(n-1)}g=-\frac{1}{2}\pi^{2}(n+k)^{2}g

g^{(n-1)}_{k}(x)=Cf^{(\mu_{n})}_{k}(x),\ \ \ k\geq 0.

(25)

We can determine the constant
C
(up to
\pm

]
.

Proposition 3
.

Consider a second order linear differential operator
{\mathcal{L}}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{y}^{2}-c(y)

is a continuous function of

h
,

functions on
(l,r)

(i)

h

{\mathcal{L}}h=\lambda h
for

;

(ii)

g

{\mathcal{L}}g=\kappa g
for
y\in(l,r)
.

Denote
\tilde{f}={\mathcal{D}}_{h}f
and
\tilde{g}=\mathcal{D}_{h}g

19

\int\tilde{f}(y)\tilde{g}(y){\textnormal{d}}y=f(y)\tilde{g}(y)+2(\lambda-\kappa)\int f(y)g(y){\textnormal{d}}y.

(26)

We apply Proposition
3

(27)
