The effect of remimazolam combined with low-dose propofol on diaphragmatic function in patients undergoing hysteroscopic surgery: a randomized clinical trial
Authors: Qian Liu, Qian Li, Dujuan Qiao, Yan Chen, Ling Zheng, Chunhui Zhao, Jing Duan, Jianhua Yao, Lei Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPropofol frequently induces respiratory depression in hysteroscopy, with diaphragmatic dysfunction being a potential contributing mechanism. This study utilized ultrasound technology to investigate whether remimazolam combined with low-dose propofol can attenuate diaphragmatic dysfunction compared to propofol alone in patients undergoing hysteroscopic surgery.MethodsPatients undergoing hysteroscopic surgery were randomized 1:1 to either remimazolam 0.2 mg/kg with propofol 1 mg/kg (RP group) or propofol 2.5 mg/kg alone (P group) for induction. Both groups received sufentanil pretreatment and propofol-remifentanil maintenance. Hemodynamic parameters (mean arterial pressure, MAP; heart rate, HR), respiratory parameters (respiratory rate, RR; pulse oxygen saturation, SpO₂), and diaphragmatic parameters (diaphragmatic excursion, DE; diaphragm thickening fraction, DTF) were compared at specified timepoints: T0 (Baseline): 5 min after entering the operating room; T1: 6 min after administration of the sedative; T2: 5 min after removal of the laryngeal mask airway (LMA); T3: 5 min before discharge from the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU). Adverse reactions, including respiratory/diaphragmatic depression, injection pain, hypotension, and hiccups, were also compared.ResultsEighty patients completed the final analysis. The RP group showed significantly higher DTF values compared to the P group at T1, T2, and T3 (P = 0.02; P < 0.001; P = 0.030). Additionally, the RP group had a significantly lower incidence of diaphragm dysfunction (P = 0.007), injection pain (P = 0.043), and hypotension (P = 0.048) compared to the P group. Notably, the RP group had a significantly higher rate of spontaneous (assisted) breathing compared to the P group (P = 0.011). However, the incidence of hiccups increased markedly in the RP group compared to the P group (P = 0.044). There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of respiratory depression, delayed awakening, or dizziness (P = 0.235; P = 1.000; P = 0.136).ConclusionCompared to propofol, the combination of remimazolam and low-dose propofol can significantly alleviate diaphragmatic dysafunction in patients undergoing hysteroscopic surgery, while preserving spontaneous breathing more effectively. Optimized therapeutic regimens may prevent respiratory complications and accelerate recovery.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry, identifier: ChiCTR2500102592, Date: 16/5/2025.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-025-03524-x.

Introduction

Hysteroscopy, as a minimally invasive technique, has been widely applied in the diagnosis and treatment of gynecological diseases [1]. Currently, in hysteroscopic surgeries, anesthesia is mainly achieved through the combination of intravenous anesthetics and opioids, which effectively alleviates pain during cervical dilation and curettage and significantly relieves patients’ anxiety and tension [2]. Propofol, as a traditional intravenous anesthetic, has a rapid onset of action, metabolism, and recovery, and is widely used in hysteroscopy surgeries [3]. Although propofol has numerous advantages unmatched by other intravenous anesthetics, it can still lead to non-negligible adverse effects, including dose-dependent respiratory depression, circulatory depression, and injection pain [4]. However, the ideal intravenous anesthetic for hysteroscopic surgery should have a rapid onset of action and offer quick recovery, with no adverse reactions. It is necessary to find an ideal intravenous anesthetic, either used alone or in combination with other drugs [5, 6].

Remimazolam is an ultra-short-acting benzodiazepine with rapid onset of action, short duration of action, and predictable recovery [7]. It is rapidly hydrolyzed by carboxylesterase (CES1) in plasma and tissues to an inactive carboxylic acid metabolite (CNS7054) [8], with a short half-life of approximately 70 min [9, 10]. Flumazenil can rapidly reverse the sedative effects of remimazolam [11]. Remimazolam is a short-acting agonist of the γ-aminobutyric acid type A (GABAA) receptor. It acts primarily by enhancing the binding of γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) to GABAA receptors [12], enhancing chloride ion influx, thereby inducing sedation, anterograde amnesia, and preventing convulsions [13]. Studies have shown that its inhibitory effect on breathing and blood circulation is relatively mild [14]. Remimazolam has gained international recognition for its safety and efficacy during painless gastroscopy. Therefore, the combination of remimazolam is increasingly used in clinical practice, a strategy supported by several studies [15, 16].

The diaphragm is a fibromuscular partition with a dome-shaped configuration, separating the thoracic and abdominal cavities. The diaphragm is the most pivotal respiratory muscle in the human body [17]. However, different intravenous anesthetics, such as propofol, midazolam, and dexmedetomidine, can induce varying degrees of diaphragmatic dysfunction [18–20], reducing the respiratory rate or inducing respiratory arrest during anesthesia, thereby posing a threat to patients’ safety. However, real-time and dynamic monitoring tools, such as diaphragm ultrasound, have not yet been widely adopted in the clinical practice of anesthesia, and research on the effects of intravenous anesthetics on diaphragmatic function remains limited.

Therefore, this study aimed to utilize ultrasound visualization technology to assess whether the combination of remimazolam and low-dose propofol can mitigate diaphragmatic dysfunction in patients undergoing hysteroscopic surgery. We evaluated the incidence of respiratory depression, hypotension, and injection pain to determine whether this combination offers superior respiratory stability and safety compared to propofol alone.

Materials and methods

Study population

Between May 2025 and August 2025, this single-center randomized clinical trial was conducted at Xi’an People’s Hospital (Xi’an Fourth Hospital). This study was approved by the hospital’s Ethics Committee (Approval No. KJLL-ZK-2025045) and registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (ID: ChiCTR2500102592). We obtained written informed consent from all participants before their enrollment. Research procedures adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and followed the CONSORT reporting guidelines.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) undergoing hysteroscopy; (2) expected procedure time being less than 1 h; (3) planned A laryngeal mask (LMA) anesthesia; (4) females aged 18–65 years; (5) body mass index (BMI) 18–30 kg/m² at screening; (6) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) I-II with Mallampati I-II classification; and (7) ability to provide informed consent.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) hypersensitivity to benzodiazepines, flumazenil, opioids, or protocol-specified drugs; (2) severe cardiopulmonary diseases or hepatic/renal dysfunction; (3) chronic use of analgesics/psychotropics or alcohol abuse; (4) history of major thoracic/abdominal surgery; and (5) refusal to provide informed consent.

Randomization and blinding

Eighty patients were randomized 1:1 to the remimazolam combined with low-dose propofol group (n = 40) and the propofol group (n = 40) using a computer-generated random number table. A single-blind design was employed in this clinical trial. The anesthesiologist was directly responsible for administering the medications, and complete blinding was impossible. However, the patients, gynecologists, and sonographers were blinded to treatment allocation. To maintain blinding, normal saline was administered in volumes identical to remimazolam in group P. Masking was maintained for outcome assessors, data analysts, and in-room observers, while anesthesiologists administering the drugs were excluded from assessments.

Anesthetic management

Patients participating in this study underwent fasting for 8 h and did not drink for 2 h preoperatively. All relevant preoperative examinations were completed preoperatively. Upon entering the operating room, intravenous access was established for all patients. The heart rate (HR), electrocardiogram (ECG), respiratory rate (RR), pulse oxygen saturation (SpO2), body temperature, end-tidal CO2 (EtCO2), and BIS were continuously monitored. Noninvasive blood pressure (NIBP) measurement was automatically conducted every 5 min. All patients were positioned in the lithotomy position and received oxygen supplementation at a flow rate of 6 L/min using a Venturi mask. Penehyclidine (0.5 mg) and palonosetron (0.25 mg) were administered before administering sedatives. All cases received an intravenous dose of sufentanil (0.3 µg/kg; Yichang Humanwell Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China) for analgesic preconditioning.

For induction, group RP was administered remimazolam (0.2 mg/kg; Yichang Humanwell Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China) and propofol (1 mg/kg; Guangdong Jiabo Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China), whereas group P received propofol (2.5 mg/kg), which was infused at a rate of 2 mg/s. A laryngeal mask was inserted after the loss of consciousness, as confirmed by a Bispectral Index (BIS) below 60. Volume-Controlled Ventilation (VCV) mode with initial settings of: tidal volume 6 mL/kg, respiratory rate 12 breaths/min, Positive End-Expiratory Pressure (PEEP) of 5 cm H₂O, adjusted to maintain EtCO2 of 35–45 mmHg. Maintenance therapy included propofol (3–6 mg/kg/h) and remifentanil (0.1–0.3 µg/kg/min; Yichang Humanwell Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China) in both groups. Cervical dilation commenced once adequate sedation was achieved. The cervix was dilated to a diameter of 1 cm, followed by the insertion of a hysteroscope with an outer sheath of 27-Fr (9 mm). Anesthesia management was conducted by a single anesthesiologist, and all hysteroscopic procedures were conducted by the same gynecologist.

Postoperatively, patients were monitored in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) for a minimum of 30 min. Pain was assessed via the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), and intravenous ketorolac (30 mg) was administered for VAS > 4. The laryngeal mask was removed when patients achieved full wakefulness and met the standard extubation criteria. Patients were transferred to the ward only after achieving a modified Aldrete score of 9 or more in the absence of significant side effects, such as nausea and dizziness.

During the perioperative period, supplemental propofol boluses (0.5–1.5 mg/kg) were administered with a minimum interval of 1 min if adequate sedation was not maintained. (BIS > 60 or body movement). Hypotension, defined as a mean arterial pressure (MAP) < 60 mmHg or a reduction > 20% from baseline, was treated with 6 mg of ephedrine (Northeast Pharmaceutical Group Co., Ltd., China). A heart rate (HR) < 50 bpm was managed with 0.5 mg of atropine (Chengdu List Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China). Spontaneous (assisted) breathing events refer to unplanned respiratory events that occurred during the intraoperative period. If such an event occurs, the patient can use the pressure support mode (PS) for breathing. For episodes of respiratory depression (SpO2 < 90% for > 10 s), initial management involved assisted ventilation via face mask with a jaw-thrust maneuver; if oxygenation failed to improve, support was escalated to positive-pressure bag-mask ventilation or controlled mechanical ventilation.

Respiratory Management

A standardized respiratory management protocol was followed to ensure identical ventilatory conditions at each measurement time point:

T0 (Baseline, 5 min after entering the operating room): Patients were awake and breathing spontaneously.

T1 (6 min after administration of the sedative): All patients were under general anesthesia with BIS 40–60 and receiving controlled mechanical ventilation.

T2 (5 min after removal of the laryngeal mask airway): All patients had been weaned from the ventilator and were breathing spontaneously.

T3 (5 min before discharge from the post-anesthesia care unit): All patients maintained stable spontaneous breathing.

This protocol ensured that between-group comparisons (Group PR vs. Group P) were performed exclusively at the same time point under identical ventilatory conditions, with no comparisons made across different modes.

Ultrasound assessment of diaphragmatic function

At four predefined time points(T0, T1, T2, and T3), diaphragmatic function was assessed dynamically by an experienced physician using bedside ultrasonography (Shenzhen Mindray Biomedical Electronics Co., Ltd., China). The primary measures were diaphragmatic excursion (DE) and the diaphragm thickening fraction (DTF) [19, 21].

DE measurement

A low-frequency curvilinear probe (3–5 MHz) was positioned at the lower rib margin along the right midclavicular line. The probe was angled medially and cephalad to align the ultrasound beam perpendicular to the posterior diaphragm, using the liver as an acoustic window (Fig. 1a). After visualization in B-mode ultrasound, M-mode ultrasound was activated to measure DE, which is defined as the vertical distance between the end-inspiratory and end-expiratory positions and quantifies the amplitude of diaphragmatic movement [22] (Fig. 1b), and is linked to pulmonary respiratory capacity.

DTF measurement

A high-frequency linear probe (4–12 MHz) was placed perpendicular to the chest wall between the right anterior and midaxillary lines at the 8th −10th intercostal spaces [23]. The diaphragm was identified as a hypoechoic structure bordered by hyperechoic pleural and peritoneal layers (Fig. 1c). End-expiratory thickness (TEE) and end-inspiratory thickness (TEI) were measured [24] (Fig. 1d), and the DTF was calculated, defined as the percentage change in thickness between expiration and inspiration. A decrease in DTF reflects poor diaphragmatic contractility [25].

The reliability of diaphragm measurement by sonographers was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), which showed good consistency for both TEI (ICC = 0.780, 95% CI: 0.690–0.850) and TEE (ICC = 0.796, 95% CI: 0.712–0.860), with all P < 0.001. For each parameter at each time point, three consecutive measurements were obtained, and the average value was used for statistical analysis to ensure accuracy.

Technical note

The quantification of left diaphragm mobility can present certain challenges due to the reduced acoustic window of the spleen and the presence of gas in the stomach; therefore, this study selected the right diaphragm as the measurement target [26].

Using ultrasonography, measurements for both DE and DTF were conducted three times at each time point, and the average value was recorded [23, 28, 29].

Secondary outcomes included the following categories: (1) Procedure- and anesthesia-related parameters: operating time, total propofol consumption, and maintenance infusion rate of propofol and remifentanil; (2) Hemodynamic and vital signs: MAP, heart rate, RR, and SpO₂ were monitored and recorded at the predefined time points.

Safety outcomes included: [1] Respiratory safety indicators: respiratory depression, diaphragmatic dysfunction (DE < 1 cm or DTF < 20%) [30], and pontaneous (assisted) breathing; [2] Other adverse events: injection pain, hypotension, dizziness, hiccups, and delayed awakening.

Sample size

PASS 15.0 was used to calculate sample size. The sample size was calculated based on DTF values from a pilot study (n = 10 per group). At T2, group P exhibited DTF values of 0.364 ± 0.08% and group RP showed DTF values of 0.295 ± 0.09%. Considering α = 0.05, power (1 - β) = 0.9, and a 1:1 allocation ratio between groups, 34 participants were required for each group, yielding a total sample size of 68 patients. Accounting for a 10% dropout rate, 80 participants were finally enrolled. However, Data from this pilot study were not included in the final analysis.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), with continuous data presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables expressed as percentages. The primary outcomes were diaphragmatic thickening fraction (DTF) and diaphragmatic excursion (DE). The normality of all continuous variables was confirmed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Comparisons between the two groups were conducted using the two independent samples t-test. Repeated-measures ANOVA with Bonferroni-adjusted post-hoc tests via general linear models for longitudinal data, while categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Between May and August 2025, 80 patients undergoing hysteroscopic surgery were enrolled and randomly allocated to the RP group (n = 40) or the P group (n = 40). Of the 108 patients initially assessed, 28 were excluded (Fig. 2), and no enrolled patient required anesthesia plan modification, experienced serious complications, or withdrew. Furthermore, as presented in Table 1, the two groups were comparable at baseline with respect to age, height, weight, and body mass index.

Differential effects of the two anesthetic regimens on diaphragmatic function

The two-way repeated measures ANOVA revealed no significant interaction between group and time for either diaphragmatic function parameter (DTF and DE) (F = 2.619, P = 0.052; F = 0.128, P = 0.943). However, a significant main effect of time was observed for both DTF (F = 12.306, P < 0.001) and DE (F = 19.932, P < 0.001), indicating dynamic changes throughout the perioperative period. A significant between-group main effect was found for DTF (F = 20.052, P < 0.001), but not for DE (F = 3.795, P = 0.055).

Post-hoc Bonferroni tests further demonstrated that DTF differed significantly between the two groups at T1 (36.5 ± 8.3 vs. 30.9 ± 12.2; P = 0.020), T2 (41.2 ± 10.4, vs. 29.9 ± 11.6; P < 0.001), and T3 (43.5 ± 13.1, vs. 37.1 ± 13.1; P = 0.030) (Table 2), but not at baseline (T0) (43.2 ± 7.4 vs. 41.1 ± 7.9, P = 0.217)). In contrast, no significant inter-group differences were detected for DE at any time point (all P > 0.05). Regarding the trend of changes, both DTF and DE decreased from T0 to T1, reflecting the inhibitory effect of anesthetic induction on diaphragmatic function, and then increased significantly from T1 to T3, suggesting a gradual postoperative recovery. Notably, the RP group exhibited a significantly greater increase in DTF during the T1 to T3 period compared to the P group (Fig. 3a), indicating that remimazolam combined with low-dose propofol regimen facilitated a more rapid recovery of this specific aspect of diaphragmatic function postoperatively. For DE, despite a similar upward trend in both groups, the recovery profiles were not significantly different (Fig. 3b).

Intraoperative management and vital signs

No significant difference was observed in the operating time between the two groups (26.6 ± 10.0; 26.4 ± 11.5; P = 0.909). And no significant intergroup differences were detected in the maintenance infusion rates for propofol (4.3 ± 0.4 vs. 4.3 ± 0.5; P = 0.865) or remifentanil (0.142 ± 0.014 vs. 0.144 ± 0.017; P = 0.542). However, the total propofol consumption was significantly higher in Group P than in Group RP (217.6 ± 49.5 vs. 173.2 ± 40.3; P < 0.001). Analysis of perioperative vital signs revealed a statistically significant main effect of time on MAP (F = 57.186, P < 0.001) and HR (F = 15.899, P < 0.001), demonstrating reversible anesthetic-induced suppression at T1 and subsequent recovery. The significance of respiratory rate (F = 69.176, P < 0.001) was confounded by controlled ventilation at T1, which was set at a fixed rate of 12 breaths/min. Although SpO₂ also changed significantly over time (F = 25.911, P < 0.001), its values remained within the normal range throughout the procedure, rendering this statistical finding clinically irrelevant (Table 3).

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial compared the effects of remimazolam combined with low-dose propofol versus propofol alone on diaphragmatic function during hysteroscopic surgery. Quantitative analysis showed no significant difference in diaphragmatic excursion (DE) between the two groups. However, the remimazolam–propofol (RP) group exhibited a significantly higher diaphragmatic thickening fraction (DTF) at specific time points: 6 min after administration of the sedative, 5 min after removal of the laryngeal mask, and 5 min before discharge from the post-anesthesia care unit. The difference in DTF suggests that diaphragmatic function was less impaired in the RP group. Furthermore, the lower incidence of perioperative diaphragmatic dysfunction and the higher frequency of spontaneous (assisted) breathing under mechanical ventilation in the RP group are consistent with the DTF results, thereby supporting this interpretation. In addition, the remimazolam-based regimen reduced propofol consumption and decreased the incidence of hypotension and injection pain. Overall, remimazolam may offer potential advantages in reducing sedative-induced diaphragmatic dysfunction and improving anesthetic safety for hysteroscopic procedures.

The results indicated a decline in perioperative diaphragmatic function in both groups, showing a general trend of an initial decrease in DTF followed by a gradual recovery, and reaching the lowest values at time point T1(Fig. 3a), This phenomenon is primarily attributed to the suppression of the respiratory center induced by anesthetic agents, a mechanism previously demonstrated in the study by Bruells et al. [31]. Unlike the chronic diaphragmatic injury associated with mechanical ventilation, the effect of sedative drugs on diaphragmatic function is more rapid in onset. Moreover, the RP group in Table 2 demonstrated a more pronounced recovery in DTF compared to the P group at T1, T2, and T3 (P = 0.02; P < 0.001; P = 0.030), suggesting that the combination of remimazolam with low-dose propofol resulted in less profound inhibition of diaphragmatic function and promoted an earlier and faster functional recovery during the anesthesia recovery phase. Previous research, including an animal study [32] and a human clinical trial [18], has suggested that the ubiquitin-proteasome system, activated by oxidative stress, is the primary factor contributing to diaphragm fiber atrophy. Sedative medications, including midazolam, propofol, and dexmedetomidine, may induce contractile dysfunction in the diaphragm by promoting the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) or compromising the proteolytic capacity. However, the exact mechanism by which remimazolam affects diaphragmatic function has been rarely investigated, and further studies are needed in this regard.

This study controlled for several confounding factors, including infusion rates of anesthetic maintenance drugs, surgical duration, and anesthetic depth (BIS values 40–60). The observed benefits in the RP group—including enhanced recovery of diaphragmatic function (DTF) and earlier return of spontaneous respiration during the maintenance and recovery periods—can therefore be attributed to the drug combination itself [33], which was designed to optimize sedative effects while reducing adverse reactions.

The diaphragm serves as the primary source of respiratory power [34]. Diaphragmatic ultrasound allows direct measurement of both diaphragmatic movement and thickness. Diaphragmatic thickening fraction and diaphragmatic excursion are considered more reliable parameters for assessing diaphragmatic function [35]. In this study, the discrepancy between DE and DTF warrants further discussion. At the four predefined time points (T0, T1, T2, T3), both DE and DTF recovered effectively under standard anesthetic management in both groups. However, DE showed no significant intergroup differences at any time point. In contrast, the RP group consistently demonstrated superior DTF, indicating a differential impact of the two anesthetic regimens on diaphragmatic function. We speculate that during the anesthetic maintenance phase (T1), the lithotomy and Trendelenburg positions [36], combined with the pressure from the distension fluid, imposed a uniform mechanical load that restricted diaphragmatic excursion (DE) in all patients. In contrast, diaphragmatic contractile function (DTF) is less influenced by such mechanical factors. Furthermore, after the mechanical and postural constraints were removed postoperatively (T2 and T3), the extent of DE recovery was comparable between the two groups (Fig. 3b), the RP group maintained a persistent advantage in the term of DTF recovery (Fig. 3a). This suggests that remimazolam combined with low-dose propofol may better preserve diaphragmatic function and promote faster recovery compared to propofol alone. This distinction was quantified by diaphragmatic ultrasonography rather than subjective clinical assessment, thereby supporting the previous view that DTF is a more reliable indicator of diaphragmatic function, being less susceptible to external mechanical confounding [37, 38]. One possible explanation is that during short periods of mechanical ventilation, the diaphragm still maintains a relatively weak respiratory drive [39]. The viscoelasticity of the diaphragm causes it to be passively stretched [40], which helps to maintain its contractile ability.

Furthermore, during laryngeal mask anesthesia, six patients (15%) in the RP group exhibited assisted spontaneous breathing, a phenomenon not observed in the P group (15% vs. 0%, P = 0.011). Given the ventilatory mode limitations of our anesthesia machine, which precluded the use of the recommended SIMV + PS mode, we adopted the Pressure Support (PS) mode for patients displaying spontaneous (assisted) respiration during surgery. This approach aimed to prevent patient-ventilator asynchrony and mitigate ventilator-induced respiratory muscle injury, thereby enhancing diaphragmatic protection. Jiang J. et al. [41] reported that propofol may induce respiratory depression by directly inhibiting the pre-Bötzinger complex in the medulla oblongata via β3-GABA_A receptors. A phase III multicenter randomized controlled trial demonstrated that, during colonoscopy, remimazolam caused less suppression of the brainstem respiratory center compared to propofol, documenting superior maintenance of respiratory function, fewer apnea episodes, and a reduced incidence of adverse events such as hypotension and injection pain [42].

Since propofol frequently induces hypotension during anesthesia induction, a lower infusion rate (2 mg/s) was adopted to prevent this effect [43]. Nevertheless, the incidence of hypotension was significantly higher in the P group than in the RP group (15% vs. 2.5%, P = 0.048) (Table 4). Studies indicated that the incidence of propofol-related injection pain was markedly lower in the RP group compared to the P group. Higher free propofol concentrations directly irritate the endothelial cells of the venous wall or trigger the release of bradykinin, leading to pain upon injection [44]. In contrast, remimazolam is water-soluble, resulting in milder pain at the injection site [13]. Regarding other adverse effects, such as dizziness and delayed awakening, no significant differences were observed between the two groups. These findings indirectly support previous reports on the advantages of the combination therapy [45]. Additionally, four patients in the RP group experienced mild, transient hiccups, with no other serious adverse events reported. Sakurai et al. [46] also documented cases of hiccups associated with remimazolam, although the underlying mechanism remains unclear and warrants further.

This study had several limitations. First, only female patients were included in this study, and to ensure safety and homogeneity, we excluded individuals aged < 18 or > 65 years, those with complex comorbidities, and those with ASA grade ≥ III. Second, complete blinding was impossible as propofol and remimazolam are visually distinct; however, patients, sonographers, and observers remained blinded to group allocation. Third, the quality of diaphragm ultrasound may have varied due to differences in operators’ skill, patients’ anatomy, or acoustic window limitations. Fourth, as this was a single-center study with a limited sample size, our findings need validation through multi-center trials.

Conclusion

Remimazolam combined with low-dose propofol is superior to propofol alone in inducing anesthesia. This regimen can significantly alleviate diaphragmatic dysfunction in patients undergoing hysteroscopic surgery, preserve spontaneous breathing, reduce injection pain and the risk of hypotension, and maintain respiratory safety.
