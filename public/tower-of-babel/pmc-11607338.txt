The application of rigid gas permeable contact lenses after surgery for infantile nystagmus syndrome
Authors: Yueying Jin, Huazhong Wang, Deshun Li, Ni Zhen, Dongsheng Yang, Ping Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe purpose of this study is to investigate the improvement in visual acuity when applying Rigid Gas Permeable Contact Lenses (RGPCL) compared to spectacles in patients after surgical treatment for infantile nystagmus syndrome (INS). A total of 33 patients with nystagmus who had nystagmus surgery from October 2020 to July 2021 were enrolled and all were fitted RGPCL. SPSS statistical software was used to analyze the difference between best-corrected visual acuity with spectacle(BCSVA) and best-corrected visual acuity with RGPCL (BCRVA) in these patients. According to the refractive status of each eye, it was grouped into 2 groups. We study the difference between its BCSVA and BCRVA in different groups. In our study, BCRVA was significantly better than BCSVA in all participants (P < 0.001), with a strong positive correlation between them (overall r = 0.9333). In Group A (SE ≥ -3.00D, 15 eyes), the difference was significant (P = 0.0018), while in Group B (SE < -3.00D, 27 eyes), the difference was highly significant (P < 0.001). However, there was no significant difference in corrective visual acuity between the two groups (P = 0.5934). The mean difference between BCRVA and BCSVA was − 0.1160 ± 0.1221 in all participants, with similar differences in both groups. These findings suggest that BCRVA and BCSVA are closely related but with significant differences. The results of our study show that for patients who have undergone surgery for INS, RGPCL provide better best-corrected visual acuity compared to spectacle correction. This holds true for patients with high myopia, low myopia, and hyperopia. Introduction

Nystagmus is an involuntary, rhythmic, pendular or jerking oscillation of one or both eyes, which severely affects the patient’s vision, visual function, and quality of life. Depending on the age of onset, it can be classified as congenital nystagmus or acquired nystagmus. Congenital nystagmus refers to pathological eye oscillations that occur shortly after birth or within the first three months after birth, which is also been called INS. This type of nystagmus, due to its early onset, has a significant impact on the visual development of infants and young children. According to literature, the incidence rate of INS is reported to be 0.061%1. The disease can be associated with visual perceptual deficits(such as optic nerve hypoplasia, ocular albinism), as well as amblyopia, strabismus, torticollis, and other conditions, leading to varying degrees of visual impairment in patients. Without appropriate treatment and guidance, patients may experience social and psychological developmental disorders.

Treatment of INS remains a global challenge. Currently, surgery continues to be the primary treatment approach for nystagmus, aiming to reduce the severity of eye oscillations at the original location, correct compensatory head positions, and improve visual acuity and visual function in patients. The choice of surgical technique primarily depends on the presence or absence of compensatory head positions or null zone. Traditional surgical procedures for patients with compensatory head positions often involve the use of transposition procedures such as Anderson, modified Kestenbaum, or Parks methods. For patients without compensatory head positions, extraocular muscle tenotomy or myectomy procedures are commonly used. According to literature, among children with nystagmus, 92% of individuals have significant refractive errors2. Failure to provide appropriate refractive correction for significant refractive errors can also lead to suboptimal corrected visual acuity. Different corrective methods can yield varying outcomes for various refractive errors. There is a substantial body of literature reporting that RGPCL have a beneficial effect on patients with nystagmus by reducing oscillations and improving visual acuity. However, there are few research on the differences in corrective outcomes using different refractive correction methods after surgical treatment. Therefore, the purpose of this study is to observe and analyze the corrective effects and differences between wearing spectacle frames and RGPCL in infantile nystagmus syndrome patients after surgical treatment.

Methods

Patient recruitment and evaluation

This experiment was conducted as a controlled experiment involving the fitting of RGPCL by all participants. The experiment took place from October 2020 to August 2021. Prior to the start of the experiment, all participants were accurately informed about the purpose and procedures of the experiment.

In this experiment, a comprehensive ophthalmic and optometric examination was performed on individuals with INS during their initial visit. This included slit-lamp microscopy, fundus examination, visual acuity assessment, optical coherence tomography, and eye movement recordings, among others. Non-INS forms of nystagmus and INS with sensory defects such as color blindness, iris anomalies, congenital stationary night blindness, incomplete development of the fovea centralis, optic nerve malformation/anomaly/hypoplasia/atrophy/papilloma, infantile retinal malnutrition/dystrophy, and infantile visual deprivation were excluded. Parameters such as pupil diameter, anterior segment examination, tear film evaluation, Schirmer’s test, etc., were also measured to rule out contraindications to rigid gas permeable contact lens wear.

All participants in the experiment had INS and had undergone surgical interventions for nystagmus, including strabismus, head position, null zone, and convergence zone (combined surgeries or multiple surgeries). All patients refrained from using any medication related to nystagmus treatment for one month prior to participating in the experiment and did not wear any type of corneal contact lenses before and after surgery. The time interval between the last surgery and participation in the experiment was not more than one year and not less than one month.

Study protocol and fitting procedures

The refractive measurements of all nystagmus patients were conducted by the same experienced optometrist using a 5 m logarithmic visual acuity chart for visual acuity measurement. Comprehensive optometry and slit-lamp examination were performed at least two weeks after the surgery, and spectacle frame fitting was done to determine the best-corrected visual acuity at that time. Prior to RGPCL fitting, verification was done to ensure the accuracy of the spectacle frame prescription and the repeatability of the best-corrected visual acuity. Patients with no changes in refractive power and best-corrected visual acuity were selected, and their best-corrected visual acuity at this time was considered as the baseline data for the control group. The subjects were then referred to the contact lens clinic. Appropriate RGPCL were fitted based on the measurements of refractive power and corneal parameters, trial fitting, and fluorescein staining evaluation. Menicon Z lenses were used for all RGPCL fittings in this study. All participants received education on proper lens usage, adaptation period, and corneal contact lens care.

After the adaptation period, patients were re-examined when they had been using the lenses for more than 6 h per day for at least 3 months. Visual acuity was reassessed and the data before and after using RGPCLs were compared.

Date collection and statistics

We conducted refractive examinations on all eligible subjects, including SE, cylinder power, BCSVA and BCRVA. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 19.0 statistical software. The paired t-test and unpaired t-test were used to test the means, with p < 0.05 indicating statistically significant differences.

Results

Initially, a total of 33 patients were included, but 7 cases were excluded due to intolerance to RGPCL or incomplete examinations. In the end, we analyzed data from 42 eyes of 26 patients. The mean age of the patients was 16.04 ± 9.85 years, with 14 cases (53.8%) being male patients. The average SE was − 5.60 ± 5.76D, the mean spherical power was − 4.815 ± 5.33D, and the average cylinder power was − 1.57 ± 3.12D (Table 1).

Discussion

Results of our study show that RGPCL can improve corrected visual acuity in patients with congenital nystamnus after shock absorption surgery, showing significant statistical differences in mid-to-high myopia group. These findings are consistent with earlier reports on the benefits of using corneal contact lenses in the treatment of patients with nystagmus3–10.

There are various theories to explain the mechanism of how corneal contact lenses improve visual acuity in patients with nystagmus, with the most important one being the optical theory. Among children with nystagmus, 92% of individuals have significant refractive errors2. For patients with nystagmus, when wearing spectacles for correction, the movement of the eyes would generate optical aberrations and prism effects, which often have little effect on improving nystagmus and result in little improvement in visual acuity. In addition, the magnification changes caused by spectacles can decrease imaging quality and may lead to fusion difficulties. Therefore, theoretically, corneal contact lenses are more suitable for correcting refractive errors in patients with nystagmus compared to spectacles. Moreover, for individuals with myopic refractive errors, wearing corneal contact lenses, as opposed to spectacles, increases convergence demands during near vision. This can potentially reduce the amplitude and frequency of nystagmus, leading to an improvement in nystagmus. Corneal contact lenses can provide a clearer retinal image, improve fusion and fixation abilities, better control abnormal eye movements, achieve longer central foveal fixation time, and ultimately improve vision11–15. On the other hand, according to optical theory, corneal contact lenses, compared to eyeglasses, move with the eye during involuntary eye movements. This allows the lenses to stay on the eye’s visual axis for a longer period, resulting in an extended central foveal fixation time. Therefore, corneal contact lenses, by disregarding the peripheral prism effect of eyeglasses, reducing chromatic and spherical aberration, minimizing the magnification effect of glasses, and enhancing the visual field, ultimately provide better binocular fusion. This leads to improved corrected visual acuity4,5,9,11,16–18.

Another theory suggests that the reduction in eye tremor intensity after using RGPCL is due to the continuous contact between RGPCL and the ocular surface and eyelid margins, which provides stimulation to the sensory and proprioceptive nerve endings in the cornea and the trigeminal nerve4,5,11,16,19. Some observational findings support this theory; the use of local anesthetics can neutralize the effects of RGPCL on involuntary movements9, Stimulating the eyelid skin or even vibrations on the neck can suppress eye tremors20. This theory is also supported by the following findings that even zero diopter RGPCL can reduce the intensity of eye tremors8. Furthermore, tenotomy of the horizontal rectus muscles has been found to reduce the intensity of eye tremors. This finding also supports this theory20,21.

Based on these two theories, it can be explained why soft contact lenses (SCL), which are also corneal contact lenses, have less potential to improve visual acuity for patients with eye tremors compared to RGPCL. Firstly, from an optical perspective, SCL cannot correct astigmatism as effectively as RGPCL, resulting in poorer retinal image quality. Secondly, due to the soft material of SCL, the sensory receptors in the cornea and conjunctiva adapt quickly, leading to a diminished sensory stimulus3,9. Due to these drawbacks of SCL, we have employed RGPCL in our current research.

There are reports suggesting that the use of corneal contact lenses is more effective for correcting higher degrees of refractive errors. The study conducted by Allen and Davies4 showed that the only patients who did not benefit from corneal contact lenses were those with low refractive errors. This is in contrast to the findings of Matsubayashi et al.8, whose study showed that even with the use of zero diopter corneal contact lenses, patients with congenital nystagmus benefited in terms of visual acuity. Jayaramachandran et al.17 reported that corneal contact lenses did not provide any benefits for patients with nystagmus.

In our study, subjects underwent one or multiple damping surgeries prior to the study, resulting in a significant reduction in the intensity of nystagmus and improved stability of central foveation time. With the subsequent wearing of RGPCL, we found that it effectively improved visual acuity in post-damping surgery individuals with INS, regardless of their refractive error. This validates the previous optical theory that explains the mechanism of how corneal contact lenses improve visual acuity in individuals with nystagmus. There was no statistically significant difference in visual acuity between the INS post-damping surgery individuals with refractive error less than − 3.00D and those with refractive error greater than − 3.00D, indicating that wearing RGPCL can effectively improve best-corrected visual acuity in all post-damping surgery INS individuals.

The strengths of our study include performing damping surgeries on INS patients to minimize the effect of nystagmus on best-corrected visual acuity. Sufficient time was provided to the subjects for RGPCL wear, ensuring that they were fully adapted to RGPCL during the examination to obtain the best record of corrected visual acuity. Switching to RGPCL reduced the inter-pupillary distance and mitigated some adverse optical effects, such as prism effects and magnification, especially in cases of higher refractive errors.

One limitation of this study is that we only observed the best-corrected visual acuity at 5 m for post-damping surgery INS patients and did not investigate the performance at intermediate or near distances. After surgery, wearing corneal contact lenses instead of spectacles eliminates the reduction in inter-pupillary distance, and the demand for convergence is higher during near vision. Theoretically, this may lead to better improvement in best-corrected visual acuity at intermediate or near distances.

In conclusion, the use of RGPCL in post-damping surgery INS patients effectively improves corrected visual acuity. Therefore, for subjects who are capable of tolerating corneal contact lenses and have no common contraindications such as ocular surface issues, RGPCL is a reasonable choice for enhancing visual quality.

Conclusion

Results of our study show that for patients who have undergone surgery for congenital nystagmus (INS), RGPCL provide better best-corrected visual acuity compared to spectacle correction. Regardless of whether the patient’s refractive state is high myopia, low myopia, or hyperopia. We recommend the use of RGPCL for refractive correction in patients after surgery for INS.
