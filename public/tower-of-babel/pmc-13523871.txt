Effect of Hypertension on Left Ventricular Diastolic Dysfunction Associated with Obstructive Sleep Apnea
Authors: Jia-Le Chen, Shu-Han Chen, Wei-Ze Lin, Ruo-Ying Chen, Wan-Qi Li, Ping-Yu Cai, Hui-Li Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeObstructive sleep apnea (OSA) and hypertension are known cardiovascular risk factors, but the effect of hypertension on OSA-related left ventricular diastolic dysfunction remains unclear.Patients and MethodsThis cross-sectional study enrolled 289 patients from the Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University (January 2013–January 2023). Participants were classified as healthy controls (HC), OSA-only (OSA), hypertension only (HTN), and OSA with hypertension (OSA-HTN). Furthermore, based on the AHI (Apnea-Hypopnea Index) values, the OSA group and the OSA-HTN group were further divided into three subgroups: mild (5≤AHI<15 events/hour), moderate (15≤AHI<30 events/hour), and (AHI≥30 events/hour). Participants underwent biochemical testing, echocardiography, and polysomnography. Echocardiographic parameters of diastolic function—interventricular septum thickness (IVST), left ventricular posterior wall thickness (LVPW), left atrial volume index (LAVI), E/A ratio, and E/e’ ratio—were compared among the groups. One-way analysis of variance and statistical interaction analysis evaluated the independent and interactive effects of hypertension and OSA severity on E/e’.ResultsCompared with the HC group, the HTN, OSA, and OSA-HTN groups showed significantly higher IVST, LVPW, LAVI, and E/e’ ratio (p<0.05), with the OSA-HTN group exhibiting the greatest impairment. The E/e’ ratio increased with OSA severity and correlated positively with systolic blood pressure and diastolic blood pressure, most strongly in the OSA-HTN group. Hypertension (mean difference=−1.784, 95% CI: −2.868 to −0.701, p=0.001) and OSA severity independently affected E/e′ with a significant multiplicative interaction (p<0.05).ConclusionBoth hypertension and severe obstructive sleep apnea are independently associated with impaired left ventricular diastolic function, and their coexistence may have a potential synergistic effect.Keywords: hypertension, obstructive sleep apnea, left ventricular diastolic dysfunction, interaction analysis

Introduction

Hypertension is a major risk factor for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases. Long-term hypertension can lead to a persistent increase in left ventricular afterload, myocardial hypertrophy, fibrosis, ventricular remodeling, abnormal diastolic filling, and elevated left ventricular filling pressure, thereby impairing left ventricular diastolic function.1

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is characterized by recurrent upper airway collapse during sleep, accompanied by intermittent hypoxia and sleep structure disruption.2 The prevalence of OSA in adults worldwide is increasing annually.3 OSA can impair diastolic function by increasing myocardial stiffness through mechanisms such as sympathetic activation, oxidative stress induction,4,5 and increased left ventricular transmural pressure.6,7

Hypertension and OSA frequently coexist. They are closely related epidemiologically and pathophysiologically. Approximately 30%–50% of patients with hypertension have comorbid OSA, and the incidence of hypertension is significantly higher among patients with OSA.8,9 These two conditions may exacerbate their impact on cardiac function, but their interaction with left ventricular diastolic impairment remains unclear. Previous reports have not addressed interaction analysis, and it is still unknown whether a synergistic effect exists when both conditions are present and if this effect varies with OSA severity. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the impact of hypertension on left ventricular diastolic function in patients with varying severities of OSA through group comparisons and interaction analyses, thereby providing evidence for clinical risk stratification and intervention strategies.

In previous articles, we primarily explored the impact of metabolic syndrome on the diastolic function of patients with obstructive sleep apnea.10 In that article, we concluded that blood pressure was the most crucial influencing factor. However, that article only briefly mentioned that blood pressure might affect the diastolic function of patients with obstructive sleep apnea without delving deeply into this association. In the current study, we specifically and comprehensively investigated the association between hypertension and the diastolic function of patients with obstructive sleep apnea and further analyzed the potential correlations and interactions using statistical methods.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

Inclusion Criteria were as follows: Patients hospitalized at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University between January 2013 and January 2023 who completed routine biochemical tests, cardiac ultrasound, and sleep respiratory monitoring were selected. They were divided into hypertension-only (HTN), OSA-only (OSA), and OSA with hypertension (OSA-HTN) groups based on their conditions. The healthy control (HC) group was composed of individuals who underwent physical examinations at the same hospital.

Exclusion Criteria were as follows: (1) Patients with damage to other vital organs such as the liver, heart and kidneys; (2) Sleep monitoring indicates central sleep apnea syndrome or mixed sleep apnea syndrome; (3) Previously diagnosed with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, heart valve disease, pericarditis, restrictive cardiomyopathy, etc. during hospitalization; (4) History of cardiac surgery.

Analysis Methods and Observation Indicators

The general data of all patients were collected, including sex, age, height, weight, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), triglyceride (TG), and high-density lipoprotein (HDL) levels. According to the diagnostic criteria for hypertension in China, hypertension is defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg.Blood pressure values were extracted from medical records, with the mean of three measurements taken on non-consecutive days adopted for analysis. No intervention was applied to or restrictions imposed on participants’ medication status on the day of blood pressure measurement. Furthermore, as detailed antihypertensive medication use could not be retrospectively verified, neither stratification nor exclusion based on medication-taking status was performed during statistical analysis.11

PSG

Experienced respiratory physicians who were not involved in patient enrollment or grouping conducted overnight PSG. The interpretation of respiratory, arousal, leg movement, and cardiac events followed China’s “Multidisciplinary Diagnosis and Treatment Guidelines for Adult Obstructive Sleep Apnea”3 and the American Academy of Sleep Medicine 2012 scoring rules.12 Portable PSG monitors (models ApneaLink Air) were used to monitor the Apnea–Hypopnea Index (AHI). Patients with OSA were subgrouped based on AHI to mild (5≤AHI<15 events/hour), moderate (15≤AHI<30 events/hour), and severe (AHI≥30 events/hour).

Echocardiography

Experienced physicians performed echocardiography using a color Doppler ultrasound system (GE VIVID E95). Measurements, including end-diastolic interventricular septum thickness (IVST), end-diastolic left ventricular posterior wall thickness (LVPW), and left atrial maximum volume index (LAVI), were obtained in the left lateral decubitus position. The E-wave and A-wave were measured at the mitral valve to calculate the E/A and E/e’ ratios. This study primarily used the E/e’ value as the standard for assessing left ventricular diastolic function: E/e’ ≤8 indicated normal diastolic function, E/e’ ≤14 > 8 indicated indeterminate diastolic function, and E/e’ >14 indicated abnormal diastolic function. The IVST, LVPW, LAVI, and E/A values served as auxiliary parameters for further evaluation of changes in diastolic function. These indicators cover hemodynamic, structural, volumetric, and spectral flow dimensions, align with the international guideline consensus,2 and are suitable for cross-sectional study designs.

Statistical Methods

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0. Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed for all data. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and compared using the chi-square test. Significant intergroup differences were further examined using the adjusted residual method. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for intergroup comparisons of normally distributed continuous data. When intergroup differences were significant, the homogeneity of variance was verified. If variances were homogeneous, the least significant difference (LSD) test was used for pairwise comparisons. LSD was employed only after significant global ANOVA results (p<0.05) and for exploratory hypothesis-generating purposes. Given the exploratory nature of this observational study, LSD was used to detect potential between-group differences without formal Bonferroni or false discovery rate (FDR) correction. All findings were interpreted with caution regarding multiple testing risks. A univariate general linear model was used for main effect and interaction analyses. If both the main and interaction effects were significant, a simple effect analysis was performed for comparison using the univariate general linear model based on the estimated marginal means from the least squares method, with pairwise comparisons performed via the LSD method. All tests were two-sided, with p<0.05 considered statistically significant.

Comparison of Left Ventricular Diastolic Function Parameters Among Groups

Significant differences were observed among the HC, HTN, OSA, and OSA-HTN groups in the left ventricular diastolic function indicators IVST, LVPW, LAVI, E/A, and E/e’ (Figure 1). The OSA group had a relatively smaller impact on left ventricular diastolic function compared to the HTN and OSA-HTN groups. The OSA-HTN group had the highest IVST, LVPW, LAVI and E/e’ values, indicating that its impact on left ventricular diastolic function was greater than that of any single disease group.

Comparison of Left Ventricular Diastolic Function Parameters Among the Four Groups. (A) IVST Values: The HC group demonstrated the lowest IVST values. In contrast, the OSA-HTN group exhibited the highest IVST values, which were significantly greater than those in the other three groups (p < 0.05). (B) LVPW Values: The LVPW values were lowest in the HC group. The OSA-HTN group recorded the highest LVPW values, significantly exceeding those of all other groups (p < 0.05). (C) LAVI Values: The lowest LAVI values were found in the HC group. A statistically significant difference in LAVI was observed between the OSA-HTN group and the HTN group (p < 0.05). (D) E/e’ Ratio: The HC group had the lowest E/e’ ratio. The OSA-HTN group presented the highest E/e’ values, which were significantly elevated compared to all other groups (p < 0.05). (E) E/A Ratio: The highest E/A ratio was seen in the HC group. Both the HTN group and the OSA-HTN group showed E/A ratios that were significantly lower than those in the HC group. (* p<0.05, ** p<0.01, *** p<0.001, **** p<0.0001).

Comparison of Left Ventricular Diastolic Function in Patients with Different OSA Severities

Significant differences were found among the HC and the mild, moderate, and severe OSA groups in the left ventricular diastolic function indicators IVST, LVPW, LAVI, and E/e’ (Figure 2). Left ventricular diastolic function showed minor changes across different OSA severity levels. The E/e’ value progressively increased with OSA severity, being most pronounced in the severe OSA group.

Correlation Analysis Between Blood Pressure and the Diastolic Function Indicator E/E

To explore the association between blood pressure levels and left ventricular diastolic function, Pearson correlation analysis was conducted for SBP and DBP with the E/’ value across different groups (Table 2 and Supplementary Table 1). The combined group (All groups) showed the strongest positive correlation between SBP or DBP and E/e’ values. After adjusting for age, BMI, and AHI, multivariate linear regression analysis revealed that both systolic blood pressure (B=0.037; 95% CI: 0.022--0.052; P<0.001) and diastolic blood pressure (B=0.040; 95% CI: 0.019--0.062; P<0.001) were independent predictors of E/e “. Subgroup analyses demonstrated statistically significant associations between blood pressure and E/e” across all three groups. The severity of OSA (AHI) was also an independent predictor of E/e “(B=0.109; 95% CI: 0.085--0.134; P<0.001). After adjustment for BMI and age, neither showed independent influence on E/e” (P=0.318/0.251).

Interaction Between Hypertension and OSA on Left Ventricular Diastolic Function

Hypertension and OSA have a significant multiplicative interaction. To further clarify the relationship between the main and interaction effects, a simple effect analysis was performed. The results indicated that among healthy individuals without OSA, patients with hypertension already had significantly higher E/e’ values than individuals without hypertension. However, in patients with mild or moderate OSA, the difference between the hypertensive and non-hypertensive subgroups was not statistically significant. In patients with severe OSA, those with comorbid hypertension showed a significant increase in E/e’ values (Tables 3 and 4, Figures 3 and 4). We reconstructed the hierarchical interaction model and presented both the unadjusted model (Model 1) and the adjusted model after correction for key confounding factors (Model 2). The critical significance of the interaction terms is explicitly indicated in the Results and Limitations section to remind readers to interpret the findings with caution. Statistical analysis revealed that after adjusting for age and BMI, the interaction term between hypertension and OSA remained statistically significant (P=0.037), confirming the robustness of the results.

Discussion

This study investigated the independent and combined effects of hypertension and OSA on left ventricular diastolic function. The results revealed that the HTN group had significant increases in the IVST, LVPW, LAVI, and E/e’ ratio, along with a decreased E/A ratio. These structural changes in the left ventricle are consistent with adaptive responses to chronic pressure overload, suggesting diastolic impairment due to hypertension. The analyses of all study populations confirmed a highly significant positive correlation between SBP and DBP and the E/e’ value. This finding is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that blood pressure is an important related factor for left ventricular diastolic dysfunction. This study further confirmed the independent association between hypertension and left ventricular diastolic function by adjusting for confounding factors such as age, BMI, and AHI. Notably, BMI lost its statistical significance after adjustment, suggesting that the effect of obesity on left ventricular diastolic function is primarily mediated indirectly through elevated blood pressure and aggravated obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). Previous literature reports have indicated that hypertension plays a prominent role in promoting myocardial hypertrophy and structural remodeling. The results of this study on the impact of hypertension on left ventricular diastolic function are consistent with the conclusions of previous literature.

The OSA group also showed an increase in IVS, LVPW, LAVI and the E/e’ ratio, indicating increased left ventricular filling pressure and impaired diastolic function. However, the degree of impaired diastolic function was milder compared to the HTN group. OSA affects diastolic function more through functional mechanisms, while hypertension plays a more prominent role in promoting myocardial hypertrophy and structural remodeling.13 To further explore the impact of OSA, we analyzed the effect of OSA disease severity on diastolic function indicators. The results showed that compared with the HC group, the IVS and LVPW of patients with mild, moderate and severe OSA gradually increased as the severity of OSA increased. This mechanism may involve sustained sympathetic activation triggered by chronic intermittent hypoxia, leading to blood pressure fluctuations and increased cardiac afterload, thereby promoting adaptive responses in the left ventricle. The LAVI did not differ significantly among the severity subgroups, suggesting that OSA might have a lesser impact on impaired diastolic function through left atrial pressure load,14,15 and left ventricular diastolic abnormalities might precede significant left atrial structural changes. Furthermore, similar to the LAVI results, the E/A ratio showed no significant difference, possibly consistent with the “pseudo-normalization” pattern of diastolic dysfunction,16,17 indicating that the E/A value alone is insufficient for accurate assessment and needs to be combined with more sensitive indicators like E/e’. The E/e’ value progressively increased with OSA severity, peaking in the severe OSA group. This trend suggests that as the disease progresses, left ventricular diastolic compliance decreases and left ventricular filling pressure gradually increases. Therefore, we concluded that OSA severity is significantly associated with the aggravation of cardiac structural and diastolic functional impairment. Early identification and active intervention for OSA are clinically significant for preventing or delaying the onset and progression of related cardiac complications.

Further research revealed that in the OSA-HTN group where hypertension co-exists with OSA, the IVS, LVPW, LAVI and E/e’ ratios were significantly higher than those in any single disease group. The E/A ratio further decreased. In the combined group, the correlation between SBP and DBP and the E/e’ value was the strongest, and the correlation index r was the highest in this group, indicating that the coexistence of the two diseases may have a cumulative effect on cardiac structure.9,15 The negative impact of blood pressure on the left ventricular diastolic function of OSA patients shows a synergistic enhancement effect.

Our statistical interaction analysis further confirmed that hypertension and OSA have a synergistic effect on left ventricular diastolic function, and this synergistic effect is more pronounced in severe OSA. Some previous studies suggested that the effect of OSA is independent of blood pressure,18 while others emphasized the “amplifier” role of hypertension.12 One study reported that in patients with AHI>27 events/hour, every 10 mmHg increase in SBP increased the E/e’ value by 0.8 units.7,8,19 This study suggests that discrepancies in research findings may stem from the different distributions of OSA severity in the study population. In mild-to-moderate OSA, the pathophysiological effects of OSA itself may dominate, whereas in severe OSA, the synergistic effect of hypertension and OSA becomes a key factor in exacerbating diastolic dysfunction. However, this synergy should be regarded as an assumption supported by the observed patterns rather than a confirmed mechanistic discovery. Further mechanism research will be needed to further determine whether there is synergy. Multiple pathways may be involved in the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms. We speculate that the repeated occurrence of hypoxia-reoxygenation processes not only activates the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) but may also play a key role in the process of left ventricular diastolic dysfunction by forming a positive feedback loop with hypertension.20,21 It should be noted that this inference is an exploratory hypothesis and needs to be confirmed by subsequent experiments. The study did not detect RAAS-related indicators, catecholamines or inflammatory markers.

This study has some limitations. First, as a cross-sectional study, causality between the variables could not be established, although previous research has provided some support for the findings.14 Hypertension and OSA have an interdependent relationship over a long period of time. Measurements taken at a single point in time cannot determine whether hypertension occurred before cardiac remodeling, or whether it developed under the influence of untreated OSA. Further research is needed to prove this. Second, all subjects were recruited from a single medical center. The case groups (hypertension-only group, OSA-only group, and OSA combined with hypertension group) consisted of inpatients, while the healthy control group was composed of individuals who underwent physical examinations at the same hospital. Although the present study was conducted at the same center and within the same time window (2013–2023) as the metabolic syndrome cohort study previously published by our team, all enrolled participants were rigorously verified to be an independent population with no individual overlap with the prior cohort. Furthermore, as stated in the Ethics section, no subjects from previous studies were included in the present study. Third, the use of medications, such as antihypertensive drugs, was not systematically controlled, which could have influenced the results. Owing to the unavailability of complete medication history, subgroup analysis stratified by treatment status was not performed in the present study. The pooled analysis may introduce confounding bias, and caution should be exercised when interpreting the results. By grouping treated and untreated patients together under a single hypertension category, we may have underestimated the true association between hypertension and left ventricular diastolic dysfunction and attenuated between-group differences in echocardiographic parameters. Although we attempted to clarify the relationship between these variables and diastolic function by assessing blood pressure levels, future studies should rigorously control for confounding factors related to medication. Finally, the assessment of diastolic function in clinical practice often involves a comprehensive multiparameter approach. Incorporating more detailed clinical data in future studies will help draw conclusions that are more aligned with real-world clinical practice. The severity of obstructive sleep apnea is only described by AHI, without reporting parameters related to hypoxemia, which is a limitation in clarifying the relationship between it and cardiac remodeling. In the context of OSA, there is a lack of assessment of right ventricular dysfunction and pulmonary hypertension. Therefore, more rigorous multicenter prospective studies with broader applicability are required.

Conclusion

Both hypertension and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) contribute to the impairment of left ventricular diastolic function. The coexistence of the two conditions may exert a potential synergistic deteriorative effect on cardiac structure and function, with left ventricular diastolic dysfunction potentially further exacerbated in patients with severe OSA. This synergistic effect warrants further validation and mechanistic investigation in future studies.
