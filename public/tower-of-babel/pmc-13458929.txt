Atrial Fibrillation After Mitral TEER
Authors: Panteleimon E Papakonstantinou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: atrial fibrillation, mitral regurgitation, mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair

Mitral regurgitation (MR) and atrial fibrillation (AF) frequently coexist, but their relationship is heterogeneous and not driven by a single mechanism. In some patients, AF emerges as a consequence of chronic volume overload and progressive left atrial (LA) remodeling, whereas in others it constitutes a key driver of valvular dysfunction itself. This distinction is clinically relevant but often overlooked in contemporary practice.

In this issue of JACC: Asia, Tanaka et al1 provide important insights into this interplay by demonstrating that the prognostic impact of AF following mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (M-TEER) is strongly dependent on the underlying MR phenotype. In a large multicenter analysis from OCEAN-Mitral (Optimized Catheter Valvular Intervention–Mitral registry) including 3,764 patients undergoing M-TEER, AF was highly prevalent (61.7%) across all MR etiologies; however, its association with outcomes was heterogeneous. AF was independently associated with increased 2-year all-cause mortality in degenerative mitral regurgitation (DMR), whereas no such association was observed in ventricular functional mitral regurgitation (VFMR) or atrial functional mitral regurgitation (AFMR). Importantly, in VFMR patients, LA volume index significantly modified this relationship, with AF conferring higher risk in patients with less advanced atrial remodeling, while its prognostic impact diminished as atrial enlargement increased.1

These findings challenge the simplistic view of AF as a uniform risk factor in structural heart disease. Instead, they support a more complex framework in which AF represents a context-dependent marker, whose prognostic relevance varies according to the substrate of MR.2

AF in Degenerative MR: A Persistent Prognostic Signal

The adverse association between AF and outcomes in DMR is consistent with prior literature.2 In degenerative disease, AF often marks the transition from compensated volume overload to a more advanced stage characterized by impaired atrial function and loss of atrioventricular synchrony, and pulmonary vascular involvement. Even after successful correction of MR with transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER), AF appears to retain independent prognostic significance, suggesting that the arrhythmia reflects a broader and potentially irreversible disease process rather than a purely hemodynamic consequence of valvular dysfunction.2

From a clinical standpoint, this raises an important implication: valve repair alone may not be sufficient in DMR patients with AF. These patients may benefit from closer rhythm surveillance and potentially more aggressive and early rhythm-control strategies.3

AF in Functional MR: Marker Rather Than Driver?

In contrast, the absence of an independent association between AF and mortality in VFMR and AFMR suggests that AF may be less a causal driver and more a surrogate of advanced structural disease in these populations. VFMR is typically embedded within a syndrome of ventricular remodeling, heart failure, and systemic comorbidities, while AFMR represents a phenotype of advanced atrial myopathy with significant annular dilation and often concomitant right-sided disease.4,5

In such settings, the incremental contribution of AF to prognosis may be overshadowed by the severity of the underlying substrate. This concept aligns with emerging data emphasizing the importance of global cardiac remodeling, including right ventricular dysfunction, tricuspid regurgitation, and atrial failure, as key determinants of outcome after TEER.5,6

The Role of Atrial Remodeling: Beyond Size Alone

One of the most interesting findings of the present study is the interaction between AF and LA volume index in VFMR. The observation that AF carries prognostic significance in patients with smaller atria, but not in those with severe atrial enlargement, suggests a potential threshold beyond which atrial disease becomes irreversible and AF loses its discriminatory value.

This aligns with the evolving concept of atrial failure as a clinical entity, in which structural, functional, and electrical remodeling converge. Importantly, LA size alone is an imperfect surrogate for atrial disease. Functional parameters such as LA strain, as well as AF burden and chronicity, may provide more refined risk stratification and should be incorporated into future studies.7,8

Notably, the incidence of ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack was comparable between patients with and without AF across all MR etiologies, suggesting that AF per se may not be the principal driver of thromboembolic risk in this population.1 This observation aligns with emerging evidence that stroke risk in AF is not solely mediated by the arrhythmia itself, but rather reflects the underlying atrial substrate, often conceptualized as atrial myopathy.9 In this framework, AF may act as a marker of atrial disease, whereas the prothrombotic state is determined by structural, functional, and endothelial alterations within the atrium.

Beyond simple volumetric assessment, the concept of atrial failure is gaining increasing recognition as a clinically relevant syndrome.10 Atrial failure encompasses the interplay between structural remodeling, impaired reservoir and contractile function, and electrical instability. In this framework, AF represents only 1 manifestation of a broader disease process. Advanced imaging parameters,11 such as LA strain, may provide incremental insight into atrial function and have been shown to predict outcomes in patients with secondary MR. Similarly, continuous rhythm monitoring and quantification of AF burden may further refine risk stratification. Integrating these parameters into clinical practice could allow for a more precise characterization of atrial disease and better identification of patients who may benefit from targeted interventions.

Implications for Rhythm-Control Strategies

These observations raise a critical electrophysiological question: when is AF still a modifiable risk factor? The data from Tanaka et al1 suggest a potential window of opportunity in earlier stages of disease, before advanced atrial remodeling becomes established. In this context, rhythm-control strategies, including catheter ablation, may be more effective when applied early, targeting a substrate that is still partially reversible.

From an electrophysiological perspective, these findings reinforce the concept that AF is not a binary entity but a progressive electro-anatomical disease. In its early phases, AF is predominantly trigger-driven and potentially reversible, whereas in later stages it becomes substrate-maintained, characterized by fibrosis, conduction heterogeneity, and impaired atrial mechanical function. Within the setting of M-TEER, this distinction acquires particular clinical relevance. Patients with DMR and relatively preserved atrial architecture may still lie within a modifiable phase of the disease, where rhythm-control interventions can meaningfully influence outcomes. In contrast, in advanced VFMR or AFMR, AF is more likely to represent the epiphenomenon of an established atrial cardiomyopathy, in which elimination of the arrhythmia does not reverse the underlying substrate.

This paradigm aligns with the broader shift in contemporary electrophysiology toward early intervention in AF, as supported by randomized evidence such as EAST-AFNET 4 (Early Treatment of Atrial Fibrillation for Stroke Prevention Trial)3 and beyond the rhythm per se, contemporary AF management has evolved toward a holistic or integrated care management approach, now advocated in guidelines and patient care pathways.12,13 Accordingly, the timing of rhythm-control strategies, rather than the mere presence of AF, should be considered a key determinant of therapeutic benefit, as well as stroke prevention and the proactive management of comorbidities, including lifestyle changes.

Conversely, in patients with advanced atrial dilation and remodeling, particularly in AFMR, rhythm-control interventions are less likely to modify prognosis, as the arrhythmogenic substrate has become fixed. However, even in this setting, rhythm control remains appropriate for symptom relief and reduction of arrhythmia burden, underscoring the need to balance prognostic expectations with patient-centered outcomes.

Study Limitations and Future Directions

This was an observational registry analysis showing associations (and not causality), and despite comprehensive adjustment, residual confounding cannot be excluded.14 AF burden, subtype, and management strategies, including prior ablation, were not systematically captured, limiting mechanistic interpretation. In addition, LA functional parameters were not assessed, and may provide incremental prognostic information beyond LA size.

Conclusions

Tanaka et al1 provide compelling evidence that AF does not carry a uniform prognostic meaning after M-TEER. In DMR, AF remains an independent marker of adverse outcomes, whereas in VFMR and AFMR its impact appears attenuated and modulated by the extent of atrial remodeling. These findings underscore the need to move beyond binary classifications of AF and toward a more integrated, phenotype-driven approach to patient management.

From a clinical standpoint, these observations support a personalized approach to AF management following M-TEER. In patients with DMR, in which AF retains independent prognostic significance, a more proactive rhythm-control approach may be justified, potentially including early referral for catheter ablation and closer heart rhythm monitoring. Conversely, in VFMR and AFMR, where AF appears to reflect advanced myocardial or atrial disease, management should prioritize optimization of the underlying substrate, including heart failure therapy and comprehensive risk factor modification. However, in these patients, rhythm control remains appropriate for symptom relief and reduction of arrhythmia burden.

In the era of transcatheter mitral interventions, rhythm is not merely an accompanying diagnosis, it is part of the disease. But as this study elegantly demonstrates, its significance depends on the context in which it occurs.
