Extraction, characterization, and efficacy enhancement of natural dye from locally grown plant impatiens tinctoria
Authors: Melaku Assefa, Abdellah Muhammed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this Study, the phytochemical constituents and radical scavenging activity of the MeOH extract of Impatiens Tinctoria were investigated along with the dye capability of the crude tubers. Phytochemical screening has shown the presence of alkaloids, saponins, phenols, terpenoids, steroids, tannins, and glycosides in the MeOH extract of I. Tinctoria tubers. The MeOH extract of I. tinctoria tubers exhibited 39.07, 46.94, and 25.15% scavenging activity at the concentration of 1000 ppm using DPPH, ABTS, and H2O2 antioxidant activity assay, respectively. The methanol extract from the tubers showed an inhibition zone of 9.46 ± 0.03 mm in diameter for the gram-negative bacterium (E. coli). The dyeing of cotton fabrics using crude I. Tinctoria tubers were also evaluated. The effects of different mordants, AlK(SO4)212H2O, NaOH, HNO3, CH3COOH, NH3, and lemon juice, were evaluated on the dyeing of cotton fabrics in crude I. Tinctoria. Treatment of cotton fabrics with NaOH resulted in a better appearance, with the most intense red colour. The study demonstrated that cotton fabrics with desired color depth and appearance could be prepared by varying the concentration of NaOH and immersion time in crude I. Tinctoria.Keywords: Impatiens tinctoria, Antioxidants, Antibacterial activity, Natural dyes, Cotton fabrics, MordantSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Microbiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Impatiens Tinctoria A. Rich. (Family Balsaminaceae and locally known as Gurshtt) is a perennial herb with tuberous roots. Found in most Ethiopian regions, but not known elsewhere (endemic to Ethiopia). Women mash the tubers into a paste and dye the palms and nails of their hands, feet, and hair, resulting in skin and nails that are black and shiny. This treatment helps control fungal infections and builds up the skin1. In the north-eastern part of Ethiopia, women have used the locally available natural dye (Gurshtt, I. Tinctoria) to decorate their palms, nails, and feet at the time of celebrations such as weddings. The plant was also reported to have antioxidant activity2 and antibacterial activities3.

Interest in natural dyes has surged recently, as they are environmentally friendly, offer better biodegradability, and are generally more compatible with the environment compared to synthetic dyes4–10. Due to the harmful impacts of synthetic dyes, strict environmental regulations have been enacted11,12. Natural colorants are alternatives to synthetic dyes and are obtained from a wide range of natural origins. These sources include plants, animals, minerals, microbes, algae, and insects9,13,14. A variety of methods are used to isolate these natural dyes. One such technique is microwave-assisted extraction, which is an example of the processes used to obtain colorants8,10. Historically, plants have served as sources of natural dyes, with over 500 plant species known for producing dyes used in textile coloration15. However, only a limited number of natural dyes exhibit desirable coloring properties on natural fibers16. In Ethiopia, natural dyes have long been used for textile coloration, and the country is rich in plant species with dye-producing properties17.

Cotton is the most common material that people want to dye. However, cotton is much harder to dye with natural dyes, as most natural dyes are acid dyes that cannot easily bond to cotton. Cotton is a cellulosic fiber that requires the addition of mordants in the dyeing process, as it lacks the amino and carboxyl groups that serve as attachment sites for dye molecules. Therefore, mordants have been used to improve the use of natural dyes for dyeing cotton fabrics. The fabric is treated with a mordant, which could form a bridge between the fabric molecules and the dye, a process called mordanting5 Mordants, which include bio-mordants, oil mordants, and metallic salts, play a critical role in forming stable bonds between natural dyes and fibers, thus improving the overall durability of dyed fabrics. Mordants can alter the medium’s pH and bond the dye to the fabric, thereby enhancing the fabric’s color fastness and functional properties18.

In the present study, extraction and phytochemical characterizations of I. Tinctoria tubers, and dyeing of cotton fabrics using crude I. Tinctoria tubers were carried out. The Study demonstrated the presence of alkaloids, saponins, phenols, terpenoids, steroids, tannins, and glycosides in the extract of I. Tinctoria tubers. The MeOH extract of the tubers was also tested against two Gram-positive and two Gram-negative bacterial species, and an increased zone of inhibition was observed with concentration increments. We also demonstrated the preparation of cotton fabrics with different color depths and appearances by varying the concentration of NaOH and immersion time in crude I. Tinctoria.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The plant species I. Tinctoria was gathered from Harbu town, located in the South Wollo zone of the Amhara region in Ethiopia, and subsequently identified by the Biology Department at Wollo University in Dessie, Ethiopia. The tubers are the target of this Study. I. Tinctoria tubers were collected from Harbu town and Dessie city markets, and the image of the tubers is presented in Fig. 1. The harvested tubers were cut and mashed to obtain a paste for subsequent extraction and phytochemical characterizations. However, the ground crude I. Tinctoria tuber was used for the dyeing of cotton fabrics to simplify the operation and avoid the use of chemicals for extraction.

I. Tinctoria tubers (photo taken during preliminary field survey).

Solvent extraction

The fresh tubers of I. tinctoria (500 g) were ground and first extracted using n-hexane (500 mL) for 24 h. After this, the n-hexane was evaporated under reduced pressure, resulting in a crude extract weighing 0.95 g. The remaining marc from the n-hexane extraction was then subjected to a secondary extraction with EtOAc (500 mL) for an additional 24 h. The EtOAc was subsequently removed, yielding a crude extract of 1.27 g. Finally, the leftover marc after the EtOAc removal was extracted with MeOH (500 mL) for 24 h, and the MeOH was evaporated under reduced pressure to produce a crude extract of 18.49 g.

Phytochemical screening of the extract

The preliminary phytochemical analysis of the secondary metabolites in the methanol extract of I. tinctoria tubers was conducted using established testing methods19–22. The results of the phytochemical screening were evaluated qualitatively, indicating the absence of secondary metabolites (-), a weak positive reaction for secondary metabolites (+), and a strong positive reaction for secondary metabolites (++).

Antioxidant activity determination

Antioxidant activities of methanol extracts of I. tinctoria tubers were evaluated by using DPPH assays, ABTS assay, and H2O2 assay.

DPPH radical scavenging activity

The determination of antioxidative potential can be easily and quickly conducted using the DPPH method23. DPPH is a stable nitrogen-centered free radical at room temperature. The radical scavenging activity of the methanol extract from the tubers of I. tinctoria was assessed using DPPH following the method outlined by Hoque et al.24 The extracts’ radical scavenging potential was compared to known antioxidant standards, such as ascorbic acid. When 0.0053 g of DPPH is dissolved in methanol, it exhibits a violet color that turns yellow when reduced by antioxidants or free radical scavengers. A mixture of 2.5 mL of the DPPH solution (0.0053 g in methanol) was combined with various concentrations of extracts (200 ppm, 400 ppm, 600 ppm, 800 ppm, and 1000 ppm) and ascorbic acid (200 ppm, 400 ppm, 600 ppm, 800 ppm, and 1000 ppm) to be analyzed. The mixtures were allowed to incubate for 45 min at room temperature in the dark, after which the absorbance was measured using UV-visible spectroscopy at a wavelength of 517 nm. ABTS scavenging activity

The ABTS assay for the methanol extract of I. tinctoria tubers was performed following the procedure described by Endalew22. A mixture of 12.5 mL of 7 mM ABTS solution and 12.5 mL of 2.45 mM potassium persulfate solution in methanol was stored in the dark for 18 h to produce blue-green ABTS radical cation (ABTS)+. 1 mL of ABTS reagent (ABTS radical cation) was reacted with samples of the extract and standard at different concentrations (200 ppm, 400 ppm, 600 ppm, 800 ppm, and 1000 ppm), and the reaction was reduced to a colorless, neutral ABTS form. The absorbance of the solution was recorded using UV-visible spectroscopy at a wavelength of 734 nm.

H2O2 scavenging activity

The capability of methanol extract from I. tinctoria tubers to scavenge hydrogen peroxide was assessed following the method outlined by Manjusha and Merugu, with some adjustments25. A pale-blue solution of 0.6 mL hydrogen peroxide reagent was mixed with the plant extract and ascorbic acid solutions at different concentrations (200 ppm, 400 ppm, 600 ppm, 800 ppm, and 1000 ppm) in test tubes for 10 min, resulting in a colorless solution, after which the absorbance was recorded at 230 nm. Dyeing of cotton fabrics

At the outset of the investigation, various mordant types were used to dye cotton fabrics with crude I. tinctoria, as shown in Fig. 2. In this study, crude I. tinctoria was used directly and evaluated instead of the extracted plant dye. This dyeing approach is simple, reducing time, cost, and the use of chemicals for extraction. The crude I. tinctoria was prepared by removing the peel of I. tinctoria with a knife, cutting it into pieces, and finally grinding it to homogenize it, as shown in Fig. 2.

Cotton fabrics dyeing process; I. Tinctoria tubers (A), chopped I. Tinctoria tubers (B), grinding (C), bathing of cotton fabrics in different mordants (D), and treatment of mordanted cotton fabrics in crude I. Tinctoria (E).

Mordanting is categorized based on when the mordants are applied. These categories include pre-mordanting (prior to dyeing), simultaneous mordanting, and post-mordanting (following dyeing). In the pre-mordanting process, the fabric is initially soaked in the mordant solution before being dyed, using the material that has been treated with the mordant. In a previous study, pre-mordanting showed a brighter colour appearance26. Therefore, pre-mordanting was applied in this study. Thus, in all mordanting procedures, the cotton fabrics were immersed in respective mordants and then sandwiched in ground crude I. tinctoria in a closed beaker for about 24 h at room temperature (Fig. 2). In the present study, different mordants, including AlK(SO4)212H2O, NaOH, HNO3, CH3COOH, NH3, and lemon juice, were used in order to evaluate their effect on dyeing cotton fabrics in crude I. tinctoria. The effects of NaOH concentration and immersion time on the dyeing of cotton fabrics in crude I. tinctoria were also evaluated.

Antioxidant activity assay of Impatiens tinctoria tubers

The radical scavenging activities of the MeOH extract of I. tinctoria tubers were evaluated using DPPH, ABTS, and H2O2 scavenging assays. The extract exhibited 39.07%, 46.94%, and 25.15% DPPH, ABTS, and H2O2 scavenging activities at a concentration of 1000 ppm, respectively. The standard ascorbic acid scavenged DPPH, ABTS, and H2O2 by 92.51%, 91.42%, and 70.79%, respectively, at the same concentration (Table 2; Fig. 3). The statistical analysis (ANOVA) indicated that the assays in both methanol extract and ascorbic acid standard are significantly different at 95% (P < 0.05).

Dyeing of cotton fabrics using crude Impatiens tinctoria tubers

As shown in Fig. 4, the dyeing of cotton fabrics with different mordants resulted in different shades of color. Dyeing of the cotton fabrics without mordant resulted in dark brown shades of colour with poor appearance, despite the high mean color intensity. The high mean color intensity of dyed cotton fabrics without mordant was due to their dark brown shades of colour, which are closer to a gray color; hence, the ImageJ software detects it as if it is high intensity. Dyeing of cotton fabrics using mordants resulted in better appearance than unmordanted dyed cotton fabrics, indicating that the application of mordant is crucial for achieving a superior appearance in cotton fabrics. Among the mordants used, NaOH showed the best appearance of cotton fabrics with the most intense red colour. The depth (mean color intensity) of the different shades of colour obtained was measured using ImageJ software (Fig. 4). Image analysis using ImageJ software involves adjusting the hue in the color threshold window, converting the image to grayscale, inverting the image, and measuring the average gray intensity using the wand tool selection. Prior to image analysis using ImageJ software, images of dyed cotton fabrics were captured using a cell phone camera. The ImageJ software provides the mean color intensity that is directly proportional to color brightness. Thus, NaOH was selected to fix the dye on the cotton fabrics, and further investigation was conducted to determine the optimum conditions using NaOH.

Color shades of cotton fabrics treated with different mordants and dyed in crude I. tinctoria, and mean color intensities of shades of colours.

The effects of NaOH concentration on the dyeing of cotton fabrics in crude I. tinctoria were evaluated to determine the optimum concentration. As shown in Fig. 5, a series of NaOH concentrations (0.04, 0.4, 1, and 2 mol L−1) was used, and the mean color intensity increased gradually with concentration. Thus, the use of concentrated NaOH could be suitable for prior treatment of cotton fabrics for their subsequent dyeing in crude I. tinctoria.

Effects of NaOH concentration on the dyeing of cotton fabrics in crude I. tinctoria.

The effects of cotton fabrics’ immersion time in crude I. tinctoria on the dyeing of cotton fabrics were also investigated. As shown in Fig. 6, pretreated cotton fabrics with NaOH were immersed in crude I. tinctoria for 4, 16, 24, 28, and 40 h at room temperature, and showed a rapid increase in the color intensity with time. Therefore, cotton fabrics with the desired color depth and appearance can be prepared by varying the immersion time in crude I. tinctoria.

Effects of treatment time in crude I. Tinctoria on the dyeing of cotton fabrics mordanted with NaOH.

Assessment of fastness properties of dyed cotton fabrics

Washing and light effect on dyed cotton fabrics were evaluated as demonstrated in Fig. 7. The dyed cotton fabrics were washed with water and detergent. The mean color intensity declined to 155.89 and 139.67 from 166.15 when the dyed cotton fabrics were washed with water alone and with water and detergent, respectively. Similarly, when the dyed cotton fabrics were exposed to sunlight for 6 h, the mean color intensity decreased to 145.69 from 163.90. Despite the mean color decrement in both washing and sunlight exposure, the appearances of the resulting cotton fabrics were found to be pleasing. Generally, by using concentrated NaOH for the prior treatment of cotton fabrics with prolonged or repeated immersion in crude I. tinctoria, the fastness properties of dyed cotton fabrics can be improved.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the methanol extract of I. tinctoria tubers is rich in various phytochemicals, which contribute to its moderate antioxidant and antibacterial properties. While its radical scavenging activity was lower than that of the standard ascorbic acid, the extract showed notable antibacterial effects, particularly against the Gram-negative bacterium E. coli. The study successfully demonstrates the potential of crude I. tinctoria tubers as a viable source for a natural red dye for cotton fabrics. Mordants can significantly enhance the dyeing process, with sodium hydroxide (NaOH) being the most effective for producing a deep, intense red color. This research highlights the dual utility of I. tinctoria, positioning it as a valuable natural resource for both potential therapeutic applications and as a sustainable colorant for the textile industry. Future research should focus on moving from crude extract analysis to the identification of active components, optimizing the bioactivity and dyeing processes, and conducting essential safety assessments to unlock the full pharmaceutical, cosmetic, and textile potential of I. tinctoria tubers.
