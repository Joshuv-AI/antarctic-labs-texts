Moyamoya disease presenting as galactorrhea
Authors: Sasan D Noveir, Nupur Agrawal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMoyamoya disease (MMD) is a progressive cerebrovascular condition marked by carotid narrowing and collateral vessel formation. Patients are often diagnosed after a stroke, but here we describe a case of galactorrhea as the presenting symptom of MMD. A 30-year-old gravida 1, para 0 woman noted milky, nonbloody discharge leaking from both breasts after manual compression during sexual intercourse. Prolactin was mildly elevated at 29.4 ng/mL (SI [International System of Units]: 29.4 µg/L) (reference range, 3-23.1 ng/mL [SI: 3-23.1 µg/L]). Magnetic resonance imaging of the pituitary gland showed no microadenoma but incidentally revealed a tortuous right internal carotid artery compressing the pituitary, raising the possibility of vascular compression contributing to galactorrhea and mild hyperprolactinemia. Magnetic resonance angiography and catheter angiography confirmed MMD. Mild cognitive impairment, persistent headaches, and secondary hypertension eventually necessitated neurosurgical revascularization. After surgery, the patient experienced a significant reduction in symptoms although challenges with memory and headaches have persisted. This case underscores the importance of brain imaging in evaluating hyperprolactinemia and highlights the potential for endocrinologic sequelae when MMD involves vessels in proximity to the pituitary gland.Keywords: moyamoya disease, galactorrhea, hyperprolactinemia, stroke

Introduction

Moyamoya disease (MMD) is a chronic, progressive cerebrovascular disorder characterized by stenosis of the internal carotid arteries and other blood vessels at the base of the brain that lead to chronic ischemia and subsequent formation of an abnormal, tortuous, compensatory vascular network in the brain [1, 2]. These collateral vessels, which resemble a “puff of smoke” on imaging (moyamoya in Japanese), supply less blood to the brain than needed, causing headaches and neurocognitive impairment. They are also fragile and prone to rupturing, placing patients at risk for intracranial hemorrhage [2].

Moyamoya disease is prevalent in East Asia, particularly in Korea and Japan, but recent data suggest notable prevalence in Caucasians as well [3]. The disease is more common in women and manifests in 2 age-related incidence peaks in the first and fourth decades of life [4]. Pediatric patients most commonly present with ischemia and transient ischemic attacks (TIAs) triggered by hyperventilation, while adults may present with either ischemic or hemorrhagic strokes [5]. Here, we describe a case of MMD presenting with galactorrhea in the setting of tortuous vessels compressing the pituitary stalk.

Case presentation

A 30-year-old right-handed, gravida 1, para 0 Chinese American cis-female with anxiety, depression, elevated blood pressures without diagnosis of hypertension, and rare episodic vertigo with left-sided tinnitus presented to her primary care physician for evaluation of new-onset milky, nonbloody discharge from both breasts. She initially noted the discharge after her partner manually squeezed both breasts during sexual intercourse. She was able to consistently reproduce the discharge afterward and became concerned about pregnancy, prompting her to come in for evaluation. She denied any acute headaches and vision changes. She reported regular monthly cycles since menarche at age 15 with a past obstetric history significant for 1 elective medical termination, with no known galactorrhea until now. She was not on any medications, including any hormonal contraception, at the time of presentation, and her family history was noncontributory.

Diagnostic assessment

Vital signs were notable for blood pressure of 138/96 mmHg and body mass index of 23.23 kg/m2. Physical examination revealed a normal and symmetric appearance of bilateral breasts with dense tissue and no discrete masses. Milky discharge was expressed from both breasts upon manual compression.

Laboratory evaluation for galactorrhea was initiated. A point-of-care urine pregnancy test in clinic was negative. Comprehensive metabolic panel was normal. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) level of 2.2 µIU/mL (SI [International System of Units]: 2.2 mIU/L) (reference range, 0.3-4.7 µIU/mL [SI: 0.3-4.7 mIU/L]) was also normal. However, the prolactin level returned mildly elevated at 29.4 ng/mL (SI: 29.4 µg/L) (reference range, 3-23.1 ng/mL [SI: 3-23.1 µg/L]).

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the pituitary gland with and without contrast to evaluate for an underlying prolactinoma showed focal tortuosity of the cavernous segment of the right internal carotid artery (ICA) and mild chronic remodeling of the bony sellar floor, displacing an otherwise normal appearing and enhancing pituitary stalk slightly to the left (Fig. 1). The left ICA was attenuated with numerous small collateral vessels, prompting concern for MMD. Follow-up magnetic resonance angiogram (MRA) of the brain with and without contrast showed a diffusely small left ICA with chronic severe attenuation of the supraclinoid segment and extensive small-caliber collateral vessels consistent with unilateral MMD. There was also severe stenosis of the left M1 segment of the middle cerebral artery (MCA), with small-caliber collateral vessels along its expected course providing relatively attenuated blood flow to the M2 and M3 segments (Fig. 2). The vertebrobasilar system and right-sided internal carotid and cerebral arteries were patent without significant stenosis.

Three-dimensional reconstructed magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) demonstrating severely attenuated blood flow in the left M1 segment of the middle cerebral artery, with small-caliber collateral vessels along its expected course (yellow arrow). In comparison, the right M1 segment shows normal flow (yellow arrow). The right M3 and M4 branches are visualized laterally, while corresponding left-sided vessels show severely attenuated flow (red brackets).

The patient was referred to neurosurgery for further evaluation. Cerebral angiography—the gold standard for diagnosing MMD—was performed by interventional radiology and redemonstrated the above findings and additionally showed prominent pial collaterals going from the left anterior and posterior cerebral arteries to the MCA (Fig. 3) [4]. Magnetic resonance imaging and MRA brain, repeated using moyamoya protocol, did not show any evidence of infarction, chronic ischemic changes, or hemorrhage.

Cerebral angiography demonstrating severe stenosis of the left internal carotid artery (ICA) (arrow) and M1 segment of the middle cerebral artery (MCA) (arrow). Prominent pial collateral vessels (arrows) are visualized arising from the left anterior cerebral artery (ACA) (arrow).

Comprehensive neuropsychiatric testing was diagnostic of mild neurocognitive disorder related to MMD. The patient exhibited mild executive inefficiencies across testing with specific difficulties in multitasking, word retrieval, short-term memory (eg, remembering codes on a lock box), and learning. Aspirin 81 mg daily was initiated for stroke prevention.

In subsequent years, the patient experienced progression of her symptoms, including worsening tinnitus and short-term memory issues, more intense and frequent headaches, and ongoing bilateral breast discharge. Notably, she had at least one distinct episode of severe headache associated with word-finding difficulty, concerning for a TIA. Her blood pressure continued to rise, meeting criteria for secondary hypertension from MMD.

Treatment

Given her progressive symptoms, the patient sought neurosurgical consultation at a moyamoya specialty center. Three years following her initial diagnosis, she underwent a left frontotemporal craniotomy with microscopic extracranial-to-intracranial revascularization anastomosis under mild hypothermia using intraoperative indocyanine green angiogram. Specifically, a parietal branch of the superficial temporal artery was anastomosed to the M4 branch of the MCA.

Midodrine 10 mg 3 times daily was started postoperatively by the neurosurgical team to maintain an initial systolic blood pressure goal of 120 to 180 mmHg and blood perfusion to the brain, but this was eventually discontinued as the patient recovered. The patient was monitored in the intensive care unit postoperatively, with no evidence of endocrinologic insufficiency.

Outcome and follow-up

Following surgery, the patient noted resolution of her tinnitus as well as decreased intensity and frequency of headaches. She continued oral midodrine postoperatively to maintain systolic blood pressures of 130 to 160 mmHg per neurosurgery, but eventually her blood pressure normalized and midodrine was discontinued. Six months postoperatively, she experienced a transient headache after consuming alcohol followed by word-finding difficulty lasting an hour. The patient was advised to maintain adequate hydration and regularly monitor her blood pressure to establish any correlation with her symptoms. A similar episode of transient headache after alcohol ingestion occurred 3 months later, with systolic blood pressures in the 90s mmHg at the time. Following strict avoidance of alcohol, which was likely triggering hypotension and subsequent intracranial hypoperfusion and ischemia, the patient has not experienced anymore TIAs.

Her prolactin levels remained mildly elevated at 25.1 to 25.7 ng/mL (SI: 25.1-25.7 µg/L) for 2 years after surgery, but her most recent level of 23.72 ng/mL (SI: 23.72 µg/L) was at the upper limit of normal. Follow-up laboratory evaluation of endocrine function has demonstrated a normal TSH at 3.5 µIU/L (SI: 3.5 mIU/L), free thyroxine (T4) at 1.10 ng/dL (SI: 14.2 pmol/L) (reference range, 0.80-1.70 ng/dL [SI: 10.3-21.9 pmol/L]), insulin-like growth factor-1 at 199 ng/mL (SI: 26.1 nmol/L) (reference range, 53-331 ng/mL [SI: 6.9-43.4 nmol/L]), and monomeric prolactin at 12.9 ng/mL (SI: 12.9 µg/L) (reference range, 2.8-19.5 ng/mL [SI: 2.8-19.5 µg/L]) and 81.1% (reference range, ≥50.1%), confirming resolution of hyperprolactinemia and suggesting no evidence of other pituitary axis dysfunction. She is still able to express milk from both breasts with mechanical compression. Cortisol evaluation is pending. The patient has ongoing anxiety, depression, and short-term memory issues, all of which are stable and manageable. Her blood pressure has normalized.

She continues to get an MRI and MRA every 6 to 12 months to evaluate for MMD progression on the untreated right side and for strokes. Her neurosurgeon will repeat the cerebral angiogram and Diamox MRI 3 years postoperatively. She continues to take aspirin 81 mg daily and has been started on high-intensity statin therapy with atorvastatin 40 mg nightly for secondary prevention with only intermittent adherence.

Discussion

Moyamoya disease has been documented to present with endocrinologic symptoms related to pituitary involvement, but this report adds to limited literature on patients presenting with galactorrhea. Whether the galactorrhea in this case was causally related to her MMD, coincidental, or multifactorial remains uncertain, but it underscores the potential for endocrinologic sequelae when MMD involves vessels in proximity to the pituitary gland.

The degree of hyperprolactinemia observed in our patient was relatively mild; however, the relationship between prolactin level and galactorrhea is not strictly linear, and galactorrhea can occur even with modest prolactin elevations in certain clinical contexts. The normalization of prolactin following surgical revascularization suggests stalk effect as a possible cause of the initial hyperprolactinemia. The presence or absence of menstrual disturbance can help distinguish pathological from benign causes of galactorrhea. Notably, our patient maintained regular monthly menstrual cycles throughout, which may argue against a clinically significant degree of hyperprolactinemia or pituitary dysfunction as the primary driver of her galactorrhea [6].

We reviewed the patient's neuroimaging with neuroradiologists at our institution, who confirmed no evidence of a pituitary microadenoma and low likelihood of an occult lesion. Whether the tortuous collateral vessels exerted sufficient mass effect on the pituitary stalk to account for the hyperprolactinemia could not be determined radiographically. Additionally, the persistence of galactorrhea despite this normalization suggests that additional factors, such as heightened mammary gland sensitivity to physiologic prolactin levels following an initial period of hyperprolactinemia, may also play a role. The patient does not regularly stimulate her breasts to express milk, making repeated nipple stimulation a less likely stimulus for her galactorrhea.

A trial of dopamine agonist therapy could serve a diagnostic role: resolution of galactorrhea with further prolactin suppression would support a prolactin-mediated mechanism, whereas persistence would suggest a prolactin-independent process. This option was discussed with the patient; however, she elected to defer treatment in alignment with her broader healthcare goals.

Prior studies have documented a range of pituitary abnormalities occurring in association with MMD. An instance of pituitary gland compression by the vascular mass of MMD was documented in a child presenting with growth failure [7]. Rare co-occurrence with pituitary adenomas, including growth hormone-secreting and nonfunctional adenomas, has presented variably with bitemporal hemianopsia, secondary amenorrhea, acromegaly, and strokes [8, 9]. Moyamoya disease can present with hypopituitarism (eg, secondary amenorrhea, central diabetes insipidus, short stature, growth hormone deficiency, and hypothyroidism) without mass effect, suggesting that attenuated vessels can cause significant pituitary hypoperfusion and hormonal complications [10-12]. Hypopituitarism has also been found after diagnosis of MMD presenting more typically with stroke [13]. Overall, the association of MMD with pituitary pathology appears rare.

A limitation of this report is that comprehensive endocrine evaluation was not performed at initial presentation. A daily prolactin profile may have distinguished pathologic from transient hyperprolactinemia, though serial prolactin measurements remained persistently elevated over several years, limiting the value of such testing retrospectively.

Medical management can prevent cerebrovascular complications of MMD, but evidence remains limited: antiplatelet therapy reduced hemorrhagic but not ischemic stroke in a recent systematic review [14], and statins have shown some evidence of stroke reduction [15]. Blood pressure can rise over time to compensate for attenuated intracranial blood flow [16], so hypertension should be treated to prevent hemorrhagic stroke. Overtreatment should be avoided as hypotension can lead to hypoperfusion and ischemia even after surgery [17, 18]. Our patient's TIAs presented as word-finding difficulties likely due to severe attenuation and hypoperfusion in the left MCA territory involved in initiating speech. She experienced increased bruising and menorrhagia after initiation of aspirin 81 mg daily, making adherence difficult. A high-intensity statin was initiated for secondary prevention through shared decision-making. Notably, she had hypotensive episodes and TIAs after alcohol consumption, which resolved with avoidance.

Surgical revascularization using extracranial-to-intracranial bypass remains the only definitive intervention, improving cerebral hemodynamics, reducing stroke risk, and supporting neurocognitive function [19]. However, chronic cerebral hypoperfusion remains a lifelong concern for patients [20]. Neuropsychiatric sequelae—intellectual disability in children, headaches, anxiety, depression, executive dysfunction—can range from mild to debilitating; testing and treatment can improve function and quality of life [21, 22]. Coordinated long-term care with serial imaging and labs is required for monitoring. Given the potential for pituitary involvement, periodic symptom-based endocrine screening may be considered as part of longitudinal management, particularly when aberrant vessels are identified in proximity to the pituitary gland.
