Plasmonic Ag-decorated Bi2WO6 nanoflakes for photocatalytic Congo red degradation and hydrogen evolution
Authors: Krishna Daware, Mayuri Khade, Sonali Mhaske, Sandhya Gadge, Niteen Jawale, Sudhir Arbuj, Muthupandian Ashokkumar, Mohamed Hashem, Ratna Chauhan, Suresh W. Gosavi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Silver (Ag)-decorated Bi2WO6 (BW) nanoflakes were developed as a visible-light-responsive plasmonic semiconductor photocatalyst for enhanced hydrogen evolution and efficient photocatalytic degradation of Congo red under natural sunlight irradiation. The Ag–Bi2WO6 (ABW) nanocomposites were characterized by UV-visible spectroscopy, XRD, XPS, TRPL analysis, BET analysis, Raman spectroscopy, FESEM and HRTEM. XRD showed the formation of the orthorhombic crystal phase of Bi2WO6 and the face-centered cubic structure of Ag. UV-visible spectroscopy showed the extended absorption of ABW composites in the visible range, whereas BW showed an absorption peak at 320 nm. Raman analysis confirmed the successful decoration of Ag onto BW, altering its vibrational mode and structural bonding. FESEM confirmed the nanoflake morphology with a densely packed and self-assembled plate/disc structure of BW and spherical Ag nanoparticles (diameter ∼6 nm) well-deposited on it. The high-resolution TEM results showed lattice fringes with interplanar spacings of ∼0.22 nm and 0.27 nm, corresponding to the (111) and (113) planes of Ag and BW, respectively. The ABW nanocomposite showed excellent photocatalytic activity, with 94% CR dye (25 ppm) degradation within 6 minutes under natural sunlight, along with a pseudo-first-order rate constant of 0.43 min−1. The obtained cumulative hydrogen production rate using BW and the ABW nanocomposites is 8231 µmol g−1 and 13 054 µmol g−1, respectively, within 4 hours under a mercury vapor lamp (400 W).

Introduction

The escalating global energy demand and environmental pollution crisis have intensified the search for sustainable technologies that can simultaneously address energy conversion and environmental remediation. Photocatalysis, driven by semiconductor materials under solar irradiation, has emerged as a promising approach for both hydrogen generation and the degradation of organic pollutants such as dyes.1 Hydrogen is considered a clean and renewable energy carrier, while photocatalytic dye degradation offers an eco-friendly solution to wastewater treatment, particularly for effluents from textile and chemical industries.2 Photocatalysis, an innovative technique that harnesses abundant solar energy, holds great promise for the complete removal of diverse water pollutants without the need for additional chemical reagents, making it a cost-effective and environmentally friendly technology.3,4 Among various photocatalysts, bismuth tungstate (Bi2WO6), a member of the Aurivillius-type layered perovskite family, has attracted considerable attention due to its suitable band gap (∼2.7–2.8 eV), chemical stability, and strong visible-light absorption. However, its photocatalytic efficiency is limited by the rapid recombination of photogenerated electron–hole pairs, which restricts its practical applications.5 To overcome this limitation, modification strategies such as noble metal deposition, heterojunction construction, and band-gap engineering have been extensively explored. The incorporation of noble metal nanoparticles, such as silver (Ag), gold (Au) and platinum (Pt), into semiconductors like silica (SiO2),6,7 zinc oxide (ZnO)8,9 and Bi2WO6 (BW) has proven to be an effective strategy to enhance photocatalytic activity. Ag nanoparticles facilitate charge separation by capturing electrons and introducing the SPR phenomenon, thereby improving visible-light absorption.4 The synergistic interaction between Ag and BW facilitates efficient charge separation and transfer, thereby improving photocatalytic performance in both hydrogen evolution and pollutant degradation. Recent studies have demonstrated that Ag–Bi2WO6 nanocomposites exhibit superior photocatalytic activity for dye degradation and reduction of toxic pollutants under visible-light irradiation.4 Similarly, noble-metal-modified Bi2WO6 systems have shown enhanced hydrogen generation efficiency, highlighting their dual functionality in energy and environmental applications.3,10

In the present study, a simple, rapid, and spontaneous chemical route was developed for the synthesis of uniform and monodispersed Ag-decorated Bi2WO6 nanocomposites. The influence of the Ag loading on the physicochemical and photocatalytic properties of Bi2WO6 was systematically investigated to optimize its multifunctional performance. Comprehensive structural, morphological, and optical characterizations were carried out to elucidate the role of Ag decoration in enhancing charge separation and light-harvesting capability. The optimized ABW4 photocatalyst exhibited excellent photocatalytic activity for both hydrogen generation under visible-light irradiation and Congo red degradation under natural sunlight. Notably, the photocatalytic degradation efficiency achieved under direct sunlight was significantly higher than that reported for many previously studied Ag–Bi2WO6 photocatalysts, which generally require longer irradiation times and predominantly utilize artificial light sources.11 These results highlight the potential of the developed Ag–Bi2WO6 nanocomposite as an efficient and sustainable multifunctional photocatalyst for environmental remediation and solar energy conversion.

Experimental

Chemicals and reagents

Analytical-grade chemicals were used in the present work. Sodium tungstate dihydrate (Na2WO4·2H2O), bismuth nitrate pentahydrate (Bi(NO3)3·5H2O), polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), acetic acid, sodium borohydride (NaBH4), Congo red dye and silver nitrate (AgNO3) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Deionized water and ethanol were used without further purification.

Synthesis of the Bi2WO6 nanocomposite

Bi2WO6 (BW) was synthesized using a hydrothermal process by dissolving 4.89 g of Bi(NO3)3·5H2O (0.202 M) in 50 mL of acetic acid. Then, we added 2.04 g of Na2WO4·2H2O (0.124 M) and 0.5 g of PVP in 50 mL of deionized water, followed by stirring at ambient temperature for 30 min. Next, the solution was transferred into a stainless-steel autoclave to undergo solvothermal treatment at 180 °C for 18 h. After cooling the autoclave, the synthesized material was washed three times with DI water and dried at 80 °C for 12 h. The powder sample was collected and calcined at different temperatures (T = 350 °C, 450 °C, and 550 °C) for 3 h each. After characterizing all three samples, the one calcined at 550 °C exhibited superior structural and physicochemical properties; therefore, it was selected for the synthesis of the Ag–Bi2WO6 nanocomposite.

Synthesis of the Ag–Bi2WO6 nanocomposite

The Ag–Bi2WO6 (ABW) nanocomposite was synthesized using a chemical route method. We used various percentages of Ag nanoparticles and a fixed amount of the BW nanocomposite to prepare the ABW nanocomposites. Typically, in this method, 1.5 g (≈4.54 mmol) of the synthesized BW nanoparticles was dispersed in 90 mL of DI water. AgNO3 (3.4 mg, 0.20 mmol) and 25 mg of PVP were added to this solution, and stirring was continued for 30 min. 10 mL (≈0.106 mmol) of a NaBH4 solution was added to this reaction. The change in the color of the solution from milky white to yellow indicated the formation of the ABW nanocomposites. Next, in the above-given reactions, the amount of the AgNO3 precursor was varied at 3.4 (0.02 mmol), 6.8 (0.04 mmol), 13.6 (0.08 mmol), 27.2 (0.16 mmol) and 54.4 mg (0.32 mmol), and the resulting samples were labelled as ABW1, ABW2, ABW3, ABW4, and ABW5, respectively. Table 1 presents the synthesis parameters for ABW1–ABW5 samples and the wt% of AgNO3 in 100 mL of DI water. The ABW nanocomposite was washed several times with DI water and separated by centrifuging the solution at 9000 rpm for 10 min, and the obtained precipitate was dried overnight at ambient temperature. The synthesized nanocomposite was used to study methanol-assisted photocatalytic H2 generation and the photocatalytic degradation of Congo red dye in natural sunlight. Photocatalytic hydrogen evolution

The methanol-assisted photocatalytic hydrogen generation experiment was designed to achieve hydrogen production, employing BW and the ABW nanocomposites as catalysts under UV-visible irradiation from a mercury vapor lamp (400 W, illuminance ∼20 000 lx). The reaction was carried out in a closed quartz reactor (100 mL capacity), sealed with a septum to enable gas collection using a gas-tight syringe. Each trial utilized 20 mg of the synthesized catalyst, with 0.5 wt% platinum as a cocatalyst. The catalyst was dispersed in a 30 mL reaction mixture consisting of DI water (25 mL) and methanol (5 mL). Prior to irradiation, nitrogen gas was purged through the suspension to eliminate dissolved gases. For gas analysis, 0.5 mL aliquots of the evolved gases were periodically withdrawn through the septum. The hydrogen concentration was determined using a Shimadzu gas chromatograph with a 5 Å molecular sieve column. Quantification was performed against a fitted standard calibration curve.

Photocatalytic degradation of Congo red

The photocatalytic performance of the synthesized samples was studied under ambient conditions and natural sunlight on sunny days between March and May, from 11 a.m. to 2 p.m., in Pune, Maharashtra, India. The solar light intensity was measured with a digital Lux meter, showing an average intensity of 157 000 lx. In a typical experiment, 15 mg of the ABW4 nanocomposite was dispersed in 25 mL of a Congo red dye solution (25 ppm). Prior to light exposure, the suspension was magnetically stirred in the dark for 30 minutes to attain adsorption–desorption equilibrium between the dye molecules and the catalyst surface. After reaching equilibrium, the mixture was exposed to sunlight while continuously stirring. At specific time intervals, aliquots were withdrawn from the reaction system until complete decolorization of the dye was observed. The collected samples were analysed using UV-visible spectroscopy to monitor changes in absorbance. Physicochemical characterization

The optical properties of the samples were examined using a JASCO V-670 UV-visible spectrophotometer. Crystalline structure and phase identification were carried out by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Bruker D8 advanced diffractometer over the 2θ range of 20–80°, operated at 40 kV and 30 mA. Surface morphology was analyzed by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) on a Hitachi S-4800 instrument, while detailed microstructural features were investigated using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) with a JEOL JEM 2200FS system. The specific surface area was determined by the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method using N2 adsorption–desorption measurements on a Quantachrome Instruments system (v3.01). Raman spectroscopy (Thermo Scientific spectrometer, excitation wavelength = 532 nm) was employed to probe molecular vibrations and structural characteristics. The surface chemical states and elemental composition were evaluated using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) with an ESCA-3000 VG Scientific system. Photoluminescence (PL) spectra were recorded on a Fluorolog spectrophotometer (HORIBA Jobin Yvon) to study charge carrier recombination behaviour. After the reactions, the synthesized particles were recovered from the aqueous medium through centrifugation using a MiniSpin Eppendorf centrifuge.

Results and discussion

UV-visible analysis

Fig. 1 shows the UV-visible spectra, which illustrate the optical absorption behaviour of BW and the ABW nanocomposites with varying Ag contents. In the pristine BW spectrum, a strong absorption edge is observed in the UV region at 315–320 nm, which is the characteristic band gap of BW. The absorption tail extending into the visible region may be due to intrinsic defects or slight conduction band tailing. The steep absorption edge suggests an electronic transition, likely due to the O 2p to W 5d transition in BW. The ABW spectrum shows two distinct absorption peaks at 315 nm and 504 nm. The peak at 315 nm is likely due to the fundamental band gap absorption of ABW, similar to that of pure BW but slightly shifted. The 504 nm peak suggests the localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) due to Ag nanoparticles. The extended absorption into the visible range indicates an enhancement in light absorption, which can improve photocatalytic activity.12 With increasing Ag concentration (wt%), an enhancement in visible-light absorption is observed. The absorption peaks appear at higher wavelengths of 500 nm, 508 nm, 520.8 nm, 521 nm and 522 nm for the ABW1–ABW5, respectively. The optical absorption features of Ag-loaded samples cannot be attributed solely to the narrowing of the Bi2WO6 semiconductor band gap. The presence of Ag nanoparticles introduces LSPR, which contributes significantly to visible-light absorption. Therefore, the enhanced absorption observed in the composites (particularly in the 500–580 nm range) arises from both the intrinsic semiconductor transitions of Bi2WO6 and the plasmonic effects of Ag. The Tauc plot analysis is not applicable to Ag-containing composites, as the absorption edge is influenced by plasmon resonance rather than a true band gap shift, which may lead to misinterpretation.

(a) UV-visible spectra and (b) Tauc plot of BW and the ABW1–ABW5 nanocomposites.

The band gap (Eg) value of BW and ABW1–ABW5 was calculated by Tauc plots using the following equation:2α = A(һν − Eg)n/һνHerein, α is the coefficient of absorption, A is a constant, Eg is the energy band gap, and n is the exponent.13,14 The Eg is found to be 2.83 eV, 2.56 eV, 2.21 eV, 1.99 eV, 1.68 eV and 1.48 eV for BW and ABW1–ABW5, respectively.

Increasing the concentration of Ag nanoparticles in Bi2WO6 does not truly reduce the intrinsic band gap of Bi2WO6; instead, it introduces plasmonic absorption and interfacial electronic effects that make the material appear more active under visible light. Pure Bi2WO6 has a band gap of ∼2.7–2.8 eV. Density functional theory studies have shown that Ag doping or loading causes a little change in this intrinsic band gap value.15 The semiconductor's band edges remain largely unchanged. At a higher Ag concentration, visible-light absorption increases due to LSPR. This effect can mimic band gap narrowing in UV-vis spectra, but it is not a true band gap reduction.16 Ag nanoparticles form Schottky junctions with Bi2WO6, shifting charge transfer dynamics. Electrons are trapped at the Ag interface, while holes remain in Bi2WO6, enhancing photocatalytic efficiency.16

X-ray diffraction

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of pristine BW and the Ag-modified BW nanocomposites (ABW1–ABW5) are presented in Fig. 2(a). All samples exhibit diffraction peaks corresponding to the orthorhombic phase of BW, indexed to the (113), (200), (220), (313), (226), and (139) crystallographic planes, which is in good agreement with the standard JCPDS card no. 79-2381. The average crystallite size of pristine BW, estimated using the Debye–Scherrer equation, is found to be 12.89 nm. The sharp diffraction peaks indicate the good crystalline nature of the synthesized material. To investigate the influence of Ag incorporation, an enlarged view of the diffraction region between 34° and 42° is shown in Fig. 2(b). A gradual increase in the diffraction intensity at 2θ ≈ 38.1° is observed with increasing Ag concentration. This enhancement is attributed to the contribution of the Ag (111) reflection of face-centered cubic metallic silver (JCPDS card no. 04-0783). However, the absence of a distinct and sharp Ag diffraction peak suggests that the Ag nanoparticles are highly dispersed and present in relatively low concentrations.17,18 The small crystallite size of Ag nanoparticles can also result in peak broadening and reduced diffraction intensity, making their reflections difficult to resolve from the BW background. Nevertheless, the progressive increase in the intensity at 38.1° provides evidence for the successful deposition of Ag nanoparticles on the BW surface.

(a) X-ray diffraction spectra of BW and ABW1–ABW5, (b) increase in the intensity as the Ag concentration increases, and (c) decrease in the intensity as the Ag concentration increases.

Fig. 2(c) presents a magnified view of the most intense BW diffraction peak centered at approximately 28.35°. A systematic decrease in peak intensity is observed with increasing Ag loading, while the peak position remains nearly unchanged.19 The absence of any noticeable peak shift indicates that Ag incorporation does not alter the crystal lattice of BW and that no substitutional doping occurs within the host structure. Instead, Ag nanoparticles are deposited on the surface of BW, preserving its orthorhombic framework. The reduction in diffraction intensity may be attributed to the surface coverage of BW crystallites by Ag nanoparticles and the partial attenuation of diffraction from the underlying BW phase. These results confirm that the Ag loading influences the surface characteristics of BW without affecting its crystal structure. The existence of Ag nanoparticles in the BW composite, particle size, shape and so forth were further identified through FESEM and TEM measurements.

Raman analysis

The provided Raman spectra (Fig. 3) illustrate the vibrational modes of BW and the ABW nanocomposites with varying Ag concentrations. BW is a layered Aurivillius oxide with a perovskite-like (WO4)2− layer sandwiched between (Bi2O2)2+ layers. Its Raman spectrum consists of characteristic vibrational modes: Bi–O stretching vibrations appear at 300–400 cm−1 and 850–980 cm−1. W–O stretching modes are observed near 800 cm−1. The additional peaks correspond to the bending and stretching of WO6 octahedra.20 After Ag is introduced into BW, the main Bi–O and W–O peaks remain but shift slightly due to the structural distortion caused in ABW1–ABW5 by Ag. The intensity of certain peaks varies with increasing Ag concentration, which may indicate changes in crystallinity, phonon confinement, or charge transfer effects.21,22 The peak broadening suggests increased structural disorder or defects induced by Ag addition. The Raman analysis confirms the successful decoration of Ag onto BW, altering its vibrational properties. The observed changes suggest modifications in lattice vibrations and potential enhancements in photocatalytic activity due to the addition of Ag.

FESEM analysis

The FESEM images of the BW and ABW samples are shown in Fig. 4(a and b). The surface morphologies of the synthesized BW and the ABW1–ABW5 nanocomposites were detected using FESEM images. The hydrothermal temperature employed during the hydrothermal synthesis plays an important role in obtaining the numerous crystalline and plate-/disc-like nanoflakes of BW. The higher-magnification FESEM image (Fig. 4(a)) of BW shows that the nanoflakes self-assemble in the form of plate/disc-like nanoflakes. The average plate/disc size, calculated by ImageJ, is found to be 55 nm. Each nanoflakes is made up of different numbers of plates/discs, and they have random shapes. This nanoflake-like structure is in favour of the photocatalytic activity of the samples.23Fig. 4(b) shows that the Ag nanoparticles are deposited on the BW nanoflakes. The FESEM images of the ABW1–ABW5 nanocomposites and their corresponding EDS spectra are shown in Fig. 5. The monodispersed, spherical Ag nanoparticles with an average particle size of 6 nm are well-decorated on the BW nanoflakes. The wt% concentration of Ag nanoparticles is clearly reflected from FESEM analysis. The ABW4 nanocomposite (0.16 mmol wt% Ag) exhibited the highest homogeneous deposition of Ag nanoparticles on the BW surface, whereas the ABW5 sample (0.32 mmol wt% Ag) showed significant agglomeration due to excessive Ag loading. In the ABW5 sample, maybe the high concentration of Ag reduces its surface energy and surface area-to-volume ratio; hence, particles are naturally attracted to each other, forming agglomerates. FESEM analysis confirms that the ABW1–ABW4 nanocomposites follow the expected Ag loading trend. Similarly, the EDS spectra reveal a progressive increase in the atomic percentage of Ag from ABW1 to ABW4. The ABW4 nanocomposite may exhibit an increased surface area, and Ag metal nanoparticles help to improve its photocatalytic activity. Elemental mapping analysis was employed to investigate the elemental distribution in ABW samples (Fig. 6). The Ag, Bi, W, and O elements are evenly distributed throughout the sample and provide evidence of the formation of the ABW nanocomposites.

HRTEM analysis

HRTEM analysis provides further morphological and structural confirmation of the synthesized ABW2 and ABW4 nanocomposites (Fig. 7). The ABW sample shows an irregular and nanosheet-like nanostructure. The spherical Ag nanoparticles are anchored onto the large surface of BW. The HRTEM images (Fig. 7(c and d)) show crystal plane spacings of 0.22 nm and 0.27 nm, corresponding to the (111) plane of Ag nanoparticles and the (113) plane of BW nanoparticles, respectively. The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern shows the polycrystalline structure of the ABW2 nanocomposite. Compared to ABW2, the ABW4 sample (Fig. 7(e and f)) shows a higher number of Ag nanoparticles decorated on BW sheets, and this result matches the FESEM results. This is further confirmation of the formation of the ABW nanocomposites.

BET measurements

The BET measurements reveal that the surface areas of BW, ABW1, ABW2, ABW3, ABW4, and ABW5 nanocomposites are 13.10 m2 g−1, 13.75 m2 g−1, 13.78 m2 g−1, 16.17 m2 g−1, 20.87 m2 g−1, and 9.18 m2 g−1, respectively (Fig. 8). The BW nanoflakes have a low surface area; however, the surface area increases with increasing Ag nanoparticle concentration in BW. The ABW4 nanocomposite exhibited the highest specific surface area among all the samples. In contrast, the ABW5 sample showed a lower specific surface area than the pristine BW sample, which can be attributed to the agglomeration of Ag nanoparticles at higher Ag loading. This observation is further supported by the FESEM analysis. However, the superior photocatalytic performance of ABW4 cannot be attributed solely to its increased surface area. The optimized Ag loading plays a crucial role in enhancing charge carrier dynamics. Ag nanoparticles deposited on the Bi2WO6 surface act as electron sinks due to the formation of a Schottky barrier at the Ag/Bi2WO6 interface. This interfacial charge transfer pathway effectively suppresses the recombination of photogenerated electron–hole pairs, prolongs the charge carrier lifetime, and increases the availability of reactive species for photocatalytic degradation. Therefore, the remarkable enhancement in Congo red degradation observed for ABW4 arises from the combined effects of improved adsorption characteristics, an increased surface area, an optimized Ag loading, and efficient charge separation.

BET curves of BW and the ABW1–ABW5 nanocomposites.

In contrast, the ABW5 sample exhibits a significantly reduced surface area (9.18 m2 g−1), even lower than that of pristine BW. FESEM observations reveal substantial particle agglomeration at higher Ag loadings, which decreases the accessible surface area, blocks catalytically active sites, and limits light harvesting. Consequently, despite containing a higher amount of Ag, ABW5 shows reduced photocatalytic activity, highlighting the importance of an optimized Ag content rather than excessive loading. The adsorption–desorption profiles of all samples exhibit type IV isotherms, according to the IUPAC classification, confirming their mesoporous nature, which is advantageous for photocatalytic reactions. PL and TRPL analyses

Fig. 10(a and b) shows the PL and TRPL spectra of pure BW and ABW1–ABW5. The PL spectra were recorded in the 300–700 nm range under 300 nm excitation. Pure BW exhibits a broad blue-green emission band at 420–530 nm.29 In the PL spectrum of BW, three prominent emission peaks are observed at 363 nm, 450 nm, and 467 nm. Upon Ag loading, a notable reduction in PL intensity is observed compared to pristine BW. The prominent emission band centered at approximately 468 nm is assigned to the inherent luminescence of BW, originating from the direct recombination of photogenerated electrons and holes. This transition occurs between the valence band, formed by hybridized Bi 6s and O 2p orbitals, and the conduction band, comprising empty W 5d orbitals within the WO62− framework. Notably, the ABW composites exhibit a markedly reduced PL intensity compared to BW, suggesting that the recombination of charge carriers is significantly inhibited, thereby enhancing charge separation efficiency in the ABW photocatalysts. Moreover, the ABW4 sample exhibits the weakest emission intensity among ABW1–ABW5 composites, which correlates well with its highest photocatalytic activity.13,20 The carrier recombination kinetics and average lifetimes of pristine BW and ABW1–ABW5 nanocomposite samples were examined using time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurements. A biexponential function was used to fit the TRPL decay. The carrier lifetimes obtained from TRPL fitting (Fig. 10(b)) are ∼24.89 ns, 30.54 ns, 31.92 ns, 44.39 ns and 23.90 ns for ABW1–ABW5, respectively. The obtained reduced chi-square is nearly zero, indicating better fitting, and the R2 values for BW and ABW1–ABW5 are 0.70, 0.83, 0.79, 0.87, 0.91 and 0.73, respectively. The ABW4 nanocomposite shows a significantly longer average carrier lifetime of 44.39 ns than the pristine BW, which shows 21.35 ns, with the slow-decay component predominating, indicating more charge separation and reduced recombination; this leads to more efficient charge transfer, which is highly beneficial for photocatalytic activity.

Photocatalytic performance

Hydrogen generation

Hydrogen generation via methanol-assisted water splitting using pristine Bi2WO6 (BW) and Ag-decorated Bi2WO6 (ABW1–ABW5) was systematically evaluated under UV-visible light irradiation. The time-dependent hydrogen generation profiles are illustrated in Fig. 11. The pristine BW exhibits a moderate cumulative hydrogen production rate of 8231 µmol g−1 after 4 h, which can be attributed to its inherent photocatalytic activity under UV-visible light. Upon the incorporation of Ag nanoparticles, a significant enhancement in hydrogen generation is observed. Specifically, the cumulative hydrogen production rate of ABW1–ABW4 is found to be 9520, 10 420, 11 232, and 13 054 µmol g−1, respectively, after 4 h, demonstrating a progressive increase with Ag loading of up to 1.81%. This enhancement can be primarily ascribed to the plasmonic effect of Ag nanoparticles and the formation of a Schottky junction at the Ag–Bi2WO6 interface, which facilitates the efficient separation of photogenerated electron–hole pairs. The Ag nanoparticles act as electron sinks, capturing the excited electrons from the conduction band of Bi2WO6, thereby suppressing charge recombination and prolonging the carrier lifetime. Additionally, the LSPR of Ag enhances visible light absorption, further contributing to improved photocatalytic efficiency. However, a decline in the hydrogen production rate is observed for the ABW5 (3.63% Ag) sample, which exhibits a reduced activity of 7786 µmol g−1 after 4 h.

This deterioration in performance can be attributed to the excessive loading of Ag, leading to nanoparticle agglomeration, as supported by FESEM analysis. Such agglomeration results in (i) blockage of catalytically active sites, (ii) increased recombination centres, and (iii) reduced effective light absorption due to shielding effects.

Therefore, an optimal Ag loading (ABW4) is crucial for maximizing photocatalytic hydrogen production and balancing enhanced charge separation and light absorption while avoiding detrimental aggregation effects. Pristine BW, with a band gap of 2.83 eV, exhibits n-type semiconductor behaviour, making it responsive to visible light and suitable for photocatalytic applications. The presence of Ag nanoparticles generates LSPR, leading to enhanced visible-light absorption. Therefore, increased absorption is exhibited by the ABW composites. The ABW4 shows a band gap of 1.68 eV, which reduces the recombination of photogenerated electron–hole pairs, improves charge separation, and enhances visible light absorption and photocatalytic efficiency. It remains an n-type semiconductor, but the addition of Ag introduces localized states that facilitate charge separation and reduce recombination. Hence, the overall photocatalytic efficiency of the ABW1–ABW4 samples increases with increasing Ag concentration. Photocatalytic degradation of Congo red dye

The photocatalytic degradation efficiencies of Congo red (CR) dye was systematically investigated under natural sunlight irradiation. For each experiment, 15 mg of the synthesized ABW nanocomposites with varying Ag loadings (wt%) was dispersed in 50 mL of a CR dye (25 ppm) solution. At predetermined time intervals (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, and 10 min), aliquots were withdrawn and analyzed using UV-visible spectroscopy to monitor the degradation process. The photocatalytic activity of the ABW nanocomposites exhibited a strong dependence on the Ag content. A progressive enhancement in degradation efficiency was observed with increasing Ag loading, attributed to improved plasmonic effects and charge separation efficiency. However, a decline in activity was noted for the ABW5 sample, primarily due to nanoparticle agglomeration, which reduces the active surface area and hinders effective light absorption. Among all compositions, the ABW4 nanocomposite demonstrated superior photocatalytic performance, achieving ∼94.2% degradation of CR dye within just 6 minutes of sunlight exposure. In contrast, pristine BW exhibited significantly lower activity, achieving only 58% degradation even after 60 minutes. The degradation efficiencies of ABW samples with 0.02 mmol, 0.04 mmol, 0.08 mmol, 0.16 mmol, and 0.32 mmol wt% of Ag (ABW1–ABW5) were found to be 30%, 31%, 37%, 94.2%, and 15%, respectively (Fig. 12).

Reusability and stability of the ABW4 photocatalyst

The practical application of a photocatalyst depends on its reusability and structural stability during repeated operation. Therefore, the recyclability of the optimized ABW4 photocatalyst was evaluated for both photocatalytic hydrogen generation and Congo red (CR) degradation.

For hydrogen production, the ABW4 photocatalyst was subjected to three consecutive cycles under identical experimental conditions. After each cycle, the catalyst was recovered by centrifugation, washed with deionized water and ethanol, and reused. As shown in Fig. 13, ABW4 exhibited excellent stability, with the cumulative hydrogen evolution rates decreasing only slightly from 13 054 µmol g−1 in the first cycle to 12 962 and 12 800 µmol g−1 in the second and third cycles, respectively. This marginal decline may be attributed to the accumulation of reaction intermediates on the catalyst surface and the slight photocorrosion of active sites during prolonged irradiation (Fig. 13 and Table 3).

The recyclability of ABW4 for CR degradation was further investigated through five successive cycles using fresh dye solutions in each run. As shown in Fig. 14, the degradation efficiency gradually decreased from 94% in the first cycle to 64%, 58%, 52%, and 48% in the subsequent cycles. Although a gradual decrease in degradation efficiency was observed with increasing cycle number, the photocatalyst remained active throughout the recycling experiments. The reduction in photocatalytic activity can be primarily attributed to unavoidable catalyst loss during the recovery process, particularly considering the small catalyst dosage employed (15 mg for 50 mL of a 25 ppm CR solution). Furthermore, the adsorption of degradation intermediates and surface fouling may partially block active sites, thereby limiting the availability of catalytic centers during subsequent cycles (Fig. 14).

Reusability of the ABW4 nanocomposite for photocatalytic Congo red (CR) degradation over five consecutive cycles under identical experimental conditions.

The structural stability of the recovered ABW4 photocatalyst was examined by XRD analysis after the fifth degradation cycle. The used catalyst retained all characteristic diffraction peaks without noticeable peak shifts or the appearance of impurity phases, indicating that the crystal structure remained intact. Although a slight decrease in peak intensity was observed, the overall crystallinity was preserved. These results confirm the good structural stability and reusability of the Ag-decorated Bi2WO6 photocatalyst for repeated photocatalytic applications. Radical-trapping tests

Radical-trapping tests were carried out to determine the active reactive species responsible for Congo red dye degradation. The tests involved the use of benzoquinone (BQ, O2*− scavenger), triethanolamine (TEOA, h+ scavenger) and isopropanol (IPA, OH* scavenger) to determine the active species involved in the degradation of dye in the presence of the Ag–Bi2WO6 photocatalyst. In the presence of BQ and TEOA, the photocatalytic degradation of CR dye was strongly suppressed, showing that both O2*− and holes played a dominant role in the degradation process. The modest decrease in the degradation of CR dye in the presence of IPA confirmed the lower contribution of OH* radicals (Fig. 15).

Total organic carbon (TOC) analysis

To evaluate the mineralization extent after the photocatalytic oxidation of Congo red (CR) dye, total organic carbon (TOC) analysis was performed, providing quantitative insights into the transformation of the organic carbon of dye molecules into inorganic carbon (CO2), reflecting the degree of complete mineralization rather than mere decolorization.

The CR dye solution was measured before and after photocatalytic treatment using a TOC analyzer (TOC-VCPN, Shimadzu, Japan) to understand the TOC content by following the Standard Method SM 5310B (high-temperature catalytic combustion method). This method involved the oxidation of the organic carbon to CO2 at an elevated temperature, and the produced CO2 was detected by a nondispersive infrared (NDIR) detector.

The analysis shows a substantial reduction in the organic carbon content upon photocatalytic treatment using the ABW4 catalyst under natural sunlight irradiation. The TOC value decreases from 69 mg L−1 to 6.0–6.2 mg L−1, corresponding to 90–91% TOC removal, indicating the efficient mineralization of the CR dye. This significant decrease in TOC confirms that the dye molecules are largely oxidized into inorganic products, such as CO2 and H2O, rather than merely transformed into intermediate organic species. Furthermore, the TOC removal efficiency closely correlates with the photocatalytic degradation efficiency of CR dye (91%), demonstrating that the observed dye degradation is predominantly associated with effective oxidative mineralization. These results highlight the superior photocatalytic performance of ABW-4 in both dye degradation and deep mineralization under solar irradiation.

Conclusion

We have synthesised Bi2WO6 and Ag–Bi2WO6 (ABW1–ABW5) by a facile, simple and cost-effective hydrothermal process and a chemical route method, respectively. The XRD and Raman analyses confirm the structural and phase purity of the synthesized nanocomposites, and the FESEM and HRTEM analyses confirm the surface topology and interatomic structure of the synthesized nanocomposites. XPS confirms their chemical states, and the UV-visible and PL analyses show their optical properties. Overall, characterization confirms the novel synthesis of the Ag–Bi2WO6 nanocomposites. All synthesised samples were successfully applied for photocatalytic degradation and hydrogen generation via methanol-assisted water splitting. The ABW4 nanocomposite shows excellent photocatalytic activity towards hydrogen generation and dye degradation. The obtained cumulative hydrogen production rate using BW and ABW4 is 8231 µmol g−1 and 13 054 µmol g−1 in 4 h, respectively. The ABW4 sample achieves 94% degradation of CR (25 ppm) within 6 minutes. The cumulative H2 revolution rates of ABW1–ABW5 are 9520 µmol g−1, 10 420 µmol g−1, 11 232 µmol g−1 and 13 054 µmol g−1 and 7786 µmol g−1, respectively, in 4 h. The ABW4 sample shows the best hydrogen generation efficiency and excellent stability and reusability. The ABW4 nanocomposite shows excellent photocatalytic activity, with 94% CR dye (25 ppm) degradation within 6 minutes under natural sunlight, along with a pseudo-first-order rate constant of 0.43 min−1. This work shows that the ABW nanocomposite has good structural stability, enhanced charge separation, broad-spectrum light absorption and significant hydrogen generation capacity.
