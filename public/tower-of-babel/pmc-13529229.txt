From Traditional Spice to Functional Ingredient: Phenolic Profile and Bioactive Properties of Sicilian Rhus coriaria L. Fruit
Authors: Katia Arena, Donatella Ambroselli, Roberto Laganà Vinci, Luigi Mondello, Luca Santi, Laura Dugo, Paola Dugo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDietary strategies focused on reducing sodium intake and increasing plant‐based foods rich in bioactive compounds are increasingly recognized for preventing metabolic disorders. In this context, herbs and spices are valuable functional ingredients with sensory and health‐promoting properties. Rhus coriaria L. (sumac), a traditional Mediterranean spice appreciated for its sour taste and rich phenolic profile, remains only partially characterized biologically. This study investigated the phenolic composition and biological activities of the aqueous extract of Sicilian sumac using chemical, enzymatic, and cell‐based assays. HPLC‐PDA/ESI‐MS analysis revealed a profile dominated by gallic acid derivatives, with gallic acid (46.45 mg/g dried extract) and galloyl malic acid (7.16 mg/g) as the major constituents, alongside minor amounts of protocatechuic acid and the flavonol glycosides myricetin and quercetin rhamnoside. Antioxidant activity was evaluated by DPPH and ABTS assays, while antidiabetic potential was assessed through α‐amylase and α‐glucosidase inhibition compared with acarbose (IC50 = 2.2 mg/mL and 0.04 mg/mL, respectively). Antiglycation activity was assessed using the BSA–methylglyoxal model, and cytoprotective effects were evaluated in H2O2‐stimulated HepG2 cells. The extract exhibited antioxidant, enzyme inhibitory, antiglycation, and ROS‐reducing activities, supporting further investigation as a functional ingredient, while emphasizing that in vivo studies are required to confirm its biological efficacy.Keywords: α‐amylase and α‐glucosidase inhibition, advanced glycation end products, antioxidant activity, phenolic compounds, HepG2 cells

Introduction

Dietary habits play a central role in the prevention of chronic noncommunicable diseases, and international nutritional guidelines consistently emphasize the need to reduce sodium intake while increasing the consumption of plant‐based foods rich in bioactive compounds [1, 2]. In the Mediterranean diet, herbs and spices enhance flavor and provide a healthier alternative to salt, the excess of which is linked to hypertension and metabolic disorders [3, 4].

Replacing salt with herbs and spices is an effective and sustainable dietary strategy for lowering sodium intake while preserving food palatability and consumer acceptance [5]. Beyond their flavor‐enhancing properties, spices are increasingly recognized as functional food ingredients due to their richness in secondary metabolites, particularly phenolic compounds, which exert antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, and metabolic regulatory effects [6, 7]. Importantly, their widespread culinary use and feasibility of daily consumption make spices especially attractive nutraceutical candidates for continuous dietary strategies aimed at promoting health.

Phenolic compounds play a pivotal role in modulating oxidative stress and chronic low‐grade inflammation, which are two key mechanisms involved in the development of metabolic and age‐related diseases [8]. Phenolic compound‐rich diets have been associated with improved glycemic control and protection against oxidative damage [9, 10]. Consequently, the regular consumption of phenolic‐rich spices aligns with dietary strategies aimed at improving metabolic health through simple, culturally acceptable, and sustainable interventions. Within this framework, Rhus coriaria L. (sumac), a traditional Mediterranean/Middle Eastern spice with a distinctive sour taste, has emerged as a promising natural flavor enhancer and salt substitute [11, 12]. In traditional medicine, sumac has long been employed for the management of gastrointestinal, inflammatory, and metabolic disorders, suggesting a close link between its sensory properties and biological activities [13, 14].

From a phytochemical perspective, R. coriaria fruits are characterized by a high content of phenolic compounds, including phenolic acids, flavonoids, anthocyanins, and hydrolyzable tannins [15, 16]. Among these, gallic acid and its derivatives are frequently reported as major constituents and are known for their strong antioxidant activity and ability to modulate biological processes related to glycation, inflammation, and glucose metabolism [17, 18].

These properties are particularly relevant for type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2D), where the inhibition of digestive enzymes such as α‐amylase and α‐glucosidase represents a key strategy to control postprandial glucose absorption. Current inhibitors, including acarbose, are effective but often cause gastrointestinal side effects [19], motivating the search for natural alternatives to these drugs. Recent studies suggest that sumac extracts may help regulate glycemia by inhibiting α‐amylase and α‐glucosidase [20]. Although R. coriaria from the Mediterranean and Middle Eastern regions has been extensively studied for its phenolic composition and antioxidant properties; however, data on Sicilian accessions are limited, and most prior biological work has relied on ethanol or ethyl acetate extracts [11, 13, 21, 22]. Aqueous extracts more directly relevant to nutraceutical and food applications remain comparatively underexplored, and the specific contribution of gallic acid derivatives to enzyme inhibition and antiglycation activity has not been clarified.

Recently, Arena et al. provided a detailed phytochemical characterization of Sicilian R. coriaria fruits, confirming their phenolic richness through HS‐SPME‐GC‐MS, HILIC×RP‐LC‐PDA‐ESI‐MS, and ORAC assays, focusing mainly on chemical profiles and non‐enzymatic antioxidant activity [15]. To extend this knowledge, the present study characterizes the phenolic composition of Sicilian sumac using HPLC‐PDA/ESI‐MS, with an emphasis on gallic acid derivatives, and evaluates the biological activity of an aqueous fruit extract. Antioxidant capacity was assessed using DPPH and ABTS assays; enzyme‐inhibitory effects on α‐amylase and α‐glucosidase were compared to acarbose; antiglycation activity was tested using the BSA–methylglyoxal (MGO) model; and cytoprotective effects against oxidative stress were measured in H2O2‐stimulated HepG2 cells. Overall, this study aims to contribute to the valorization of sumac as a functional spice and nutraceutical ingredient within Mediterranean dietary patterns.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of Phenolic Compounds

The discovery of therapeutic properties of sumac has piqued the interest of researchers over the past 10 years. Sumac has been proposed as a “nutraceutical,” and its application in the production of functional foods has been actively encouraged [12]. Its potent activity could be related to the presence of phenolic compounds, among which, the main compounds found in sumac fruits are phenolic acids, flavonoids, anthocyanins, and hydrolyzable tannins. The identified compounds were quantified using an external standard calibration method. Calibration curves were constructed using representative standards for each chemical class based on the chromatographic peak areas obtained at λ = 280 nm for phenolic acids (gallic acid) and λ = 350 nm for flavonol glycosides (quercetin‐3‐O‐rhamnoside). Each concentration level was injected in triplicate.

As a result, gallic acid was the most abundant compound (46.45 mg/g of dried extract), followed by galloyl malic acid (7.16 mg/g of dried extract); these results are in accordance with studies previously reported in the literature [15]. Overall, the total quantified phenolic content reached approximately 79.9 mg/g of dried extract, of which approximately 94.7% consisted of gallic acid derivatives, confirming the high tannin content of Sicilian sumac fruits.

In addition to mono‐ and di‐galloyl derivatives, appreciable amounts of tri‐ and tetra‐galloyl hexosides were detected, indicating a complex distribution of hydrolyzable tannins that may enhance radical‐scavenging and metal‐chelating capacities.

Two flavonol rhamnosides (myricetin and quercetin derivatives) were also identified (1.44 and 0.91 mg/g, respectively). Although present at lower concentrations than galloyl derivatives, these compounds are known to contribute to antioxidant and enzyme‐modulating effects, suggesting a complementary role in the overall bioactivity of the extract.

Antioxidant Activity

The antioxidant activity of the aqueous extract of R. coriaria fruit was 1.02 ± 0.05 mmol TE/g (DPPH) and 1.76 ± 0.10 mmol TE/g (ABTS), as shown in Table 2. These values indicate a moderate antioxidant capacity compared to the reference standard gallic acid, which showed substantially higher activity (DPPH: 26.68 ± 1.05 mmol TE/g; ABTS: 29.19 ± 2.55 mmol TE/g). This difference is expected, as gallic acid is a single low‐molecular‐weight phenol with three free hydroxyl groups directly available for radical scavenging, whereas the extract is a complex matrix in which galloyl derivatives are present in esterified and polymeric forms; the comparison therefore reflects compositional differences rather than a low intrinsic antioxidant potential of the extract [25].

Therefore, the performance of the sumac extract reflects its rich polyphenol composition and remains competitive within the range reported for polyphenol‐rich fruit extracts. These findings are generally in agreement with previous studies reporting the significant antioxidant activity of sumac fruit extracts, which have been attributed to their high content of phenolic compounds, including gallic acid derivatives, although reported activity levels vary depending on the extraction method and assay conditions [26, 27, 28]. In our previous study, ORAC tests on extracts of R. coriaria fruits confirmed their high antioxidant capacity, with values ranging from 34.321 to 226.661 µmol TE/100 g depending on the sample [15], placing sumac among plant‐derived foods with the highest ORAC‐reported antioxidant capacity [29].

In Vitro Inhibition of α‐Amylase and α‐Glucosidase

The in vitro inhibition assays demonstrated a clear dose–response relationship for both acarbose and sumac fruit extract against α‐amylase enzyme, (Figure 2). As expected, acarbose exhibited strong inhibitory activity, reaching over 90% at the highest tested concentration (100 µg/mL), with statistically significant differences among all concentrations. This behavior is consistent with its established role as a competitive inhibitor specifically designed to interact with the active site of α‐amylase [30, 31, 32]. The sumac fruit extract also exhibited a significant inhibitory effect on α‐amylase activity, achieving a maximum inhibition of approximately 78%–80% at 4 mg/mL. Although higher concentrations were required to achieve inhibition levels comparable to those of acarbose, the extract exhibited a clear and statistically significant dose‐dependent trend, suggesting the presence of bioactive compounds capable of modulating enzyme activity. The lower inhibitory potency of the extract may be attributed to its complex phytochemical composition, which includes phenolic acids, flavonoids, and tannins. Unlike acarbose, which is a single and highly specific molecule, these compounds may interact with the enzyme through multiple and less specific mechanisms, such as non‐competitive or mixed‐type inhibition [33, 34].

The inhibitory activity of acarbose and sumac fruit extracts was also evaluated against the α‐glucosidase enzyme (Figure 3). Acarbose showed a marked dose‐dependent inhibitory effect, with an IC50 value of 0.94 mg/mL, confirming its effectiveness in limiting α‐glucosidase activity and its established role in the management of postprandial hyperglycemia [35, 36, 37].

Notably, the sumac fruit extract exhibited a remarkably strong inhibitory effect on α‐glucosidase, characterized by a considerably lower IC50 value (0.04 mg/mL) than acarbose. The extract achieved inhibition percentages comparable to those of the pharmacological inhibitor at substantially lower concentrations; in particular, approximately 70% inhibition was observed at 0.03 mg/mL, whereas a similar effect was achieved by acarbose at 1 mg/mL. The highly potent inhibition of α‐glucosidase observed for the sumac fruit extract is likely related to its diverse array of bioactive compounds, which may act in concert to modulate enzyme activity more effectively than a single compound. Such synergistic interactions could enhance postprandial glucose regulation, in contrast to acarbose, whose inhibitory effect is restricted to direct binding to the enzyme's active site.

Several studies have highlighted the antidiabetic potential of R. coriaria through the inhibition of carbohydrate‐digesting enzymes. Activity‐guided isolation from R. coriaria fruit and leaf ethanol extracts led to the identification of bioactive constituents, including 1,2,3,4,6‐penta‐O‐galloyl‐β‐D‐glucopyranose, which showed strong inhibitory effects on α‐amylase (IC50 ≈ 6.32 µM) and α‐glucosidase (IC50 ≈ 4.1 µM) in vitro, alongside other flavonoids such as agathisflavone and amentoflavone [38]. These effects are consistent with mechanistic studies indicating that gallotannins inhibit α‐glucosidase through multiple hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions at the active site, with inhibitory potency increasing with the number of galloyl moieties [39, 40, 41].

Earlier reports have also described significant α‐amylase inhibition by ethyl acetate extracts of R. coriaria fruits, with an IC50 value of approximately 28.7 µg/mL [42]. Moreover, R. coriaria extracts have demonstrated notable antidiabetic and antioxidant activities in screening assays, including substantial inhibition of α‐amylase, compared with other plant extracts [33].

Together, these values support the enzyme inhibitory activity observed for our Sicilian R. coriaria fruit extract (α‐amylase IC50 = 2.2 mg/mL; α‐glucosidase IC50 = 0.04 mg/mL) and provide a solid literature basis for comparison with the in vitro dose–response data presented.

In Vitro Inhibition of Advanced Glycation End‐Products (AGEs) Formation

The ability of sumac fruit extract to inhibit the in vitro formation of AGEs was evaluated using the BSA–MGO model. This assay is widely employed to mimic the non‐enzymatic glycation process occurring under hyperglycemic conditions and to assess the formation of fluorescent AGEs [43, 44]. Gallic acid and quercetin, two phenolic compounds abundantly present in sumac extract, were included as reference standards to better interpret the contribution of individual bioactive constituents. As shown in Figure 4, the BSA–MGO system resulted in a marked increase in fluorescence intensity (FI) compared to BSA alone, confirming effective AGE formation. Both gallic acid and quercetin significantly inhibited AGE formation in a concentration‐dependent manner. In particular, quercetin exhibited a strong inhibitory effect, leading to a pronounced reduction in FI at higher concentrations, in agreement with previous studies reporting its ability to trap reactive dicarbonyl species, such as MGO and glyoxal, and to suppress oxidative pathways involved in glycation [45, 46]. Gallic acid also exhibited significant inhibitory activity, although with a more gradual reduction in AGE formation across the tested concentration range.

Notably, the sumac fruit extract demonstrated clear and dose‐dependent inhibition of AGE formation, with FI progressively decreasing as the extract concentration increased. At the highest tested concentrations, the extract markedly reduced AGE‐associated fluorescence, approaching the inhibitory effects observed for reference phenolic compounds. This behavior suggests that the anti‐glycation activity of the extract is not attributable to a single compound but rather to the synergistic action of multiple phenolic constituents present in R. coriaria fruits. AGEs play a central role in diabetic complications, including nephropathy (glomerular damage) and retinopathy (pericyte apoptosis and BRB breakdown), primarily via RAGE‐mediated oxidative stress and inflammation [47, 48].

The inhibitory effect on AGE formation may be explained by several complementary mechanisms, including the scavenging of reactive carbonyl species, such as MGO, chelation of metal ions involved in oxidative reactions, and antioxidant activity of phenolic compounds, which limit oxidative steps that accelerate glycation. Considering the central role of AGEs in the development of diabetic complications, these in vitro results suggest that sumac fruit extract may interfere with more than one biochemical pathway relevant to hyperglycemia‐related damage (digestive enzyme modulation and glycation), warranting further investigation in cellular and in vivo models before any therapeutic or management‐related claim can be made.

Inhibition of ROS Formation in HepG2 Cells

The cytoprotective effect of sumac fruit extract against oxidative stress was investigated in human hepatocellular carcinoma HepG2 cells by evaluating cell viability and intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) production levels. As shown in Figure S1, a preliminary cell viability assay was performed to determine the maximum non‐cytotoxic concentration of the extract. Treatment did not significantly affect cell viability up to 150 µg/mL, whereas a progressive reduction was observed at higher concentrations (≥ 200 µg/mL). Based on these results, 150 µg/mL was selected as the highest concentration for subsequent ROS inhibition assays.

The intracellular ROS levels were measured following oxidative stress induction with hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), as reported in Figure 5. Exposure of HepG2 cells to H2O2 caused a marked and significant increase in FI at all time points (1, 3, and 6 h), confirming effective induction of oxidative stress. Pre‐treatment with the fruit extract significantly attenuated ROS production in H2O2‐stimulated cells at all incubation times, demonstrating a clear protective effect against oxidative stress. In particular, the extract was able to reduce ROS levels to values significantly lower than those observed with H2O2 alone, although it did not completely restore them to values comparable to those in untreated control cells, highlighting its strong antioxidant capacity at the cellular level. This validates HepG2 cells as a relevant model for hepatic protection against hyperglycemia‐induced oxidative damage [49, 50, 51]. The protective effect was maintained over time, suggesting sustained intracellular activity of the bioactive compounds present in the extract. This behavior may be attributed to the high content of phenolic compounds, particularly gallic acid derivatives and flavonoids, which are known to exert antioxidant effects through direct scavenging of reactive oxygen species and modulation of cellular redox homeostasis. Overall, these results indicate that R. coriaria fruit extract effectively counteracts H2O2‐induced oxidative stress in HepG2 cells in vitro, reinforcing the biological relevance of its antioxidant properties observed in chemical assays. These findings should be considered preliminary, as cytoprotective and hepatoprotective effects in vivo remain to be demonstrated.

These integrated in vitro findings suggest that Sicilian sumac aqueous extract is a candidate multifunctional ingredient for functional foods, although this potential remains to be confirmed in vivo. It exhibited α‐glucosidase inhibition (IC50 0.04 mg/mL), comparable to or lower than that reported for acarbose under the same in vitro assay conditions, antiglycation activity, and ROS‐reducing effect in HepG2 cells are consistent with the multitarget mechanisms relevant to postprandial glycemic control and oxidative stress mitigation, though these associations are based solely on biochemical and cell‐based assays and do not establish a clinical or physiological effect. Sumac's distinctive sour profile has separately been reported to support its use as a natural salt replacer, with sensory and behavioral studies suggesting potential sodium reductions of 20%–30% in food matrices [52, 53, 54]; however, this aspect was not investigated in the present study.

Conclusions

The present study provides an in‐depth characterization of the phenolic profile of Sicilian R. coriaria fruits, highlighting the high abundance of gallic acid and its derivatives, along with other phenolic acids and flavanol glycosides that contribute to its bioactive potential. The aqueous fruit extract exhibited measurable antioxidant capacity and pronounced in vitro inhibitory activity against carbohydrate‐digesting enzymes, particularly α‐glucosidase. It also reduced the formation of AGE and H2O2‐induced ROS in HepG2 cells, supporting its antiglycation and cytoprotective effects.

Taken together, these results indicate that sumac fruits can interfere with multiple pathways involved in hyperglycemia‐induced damage, suggesting their potential as multifunctional ingredients in functional foods and nutraceuticals. In this context, the valorization of Sicilian sumac within a circular economy framework offers opportunities for sustainable innovation. Beyond its traditional use as a seasoning, sumac can provide phenolic compounds for reformulated foods, potentially enabling partial sodium reduction while contributing additional bioactive benefits.

From a product development perspective, the exploration of sumac‐enriched prototypes, such as reduced sodium snacks, bakery goods, beverages, or nutraceutical formulations, could exploit both its health‐promoting properties and techno‐functional characteristics (e.g., natural colorant and preservative effects). Furthermore, incorporating sumac extracts as functional ingredients in dietary supplements may represent a promising strategy for delivering concentrated bioactive compounds in a controlled and standardized manner. Nevertheless, further in vivo studies and clinical trials are required to substantiate the efficacy, determine optimal intake levels, and ensure safety and consumer acceptance. It should be emphasized that all biological activities reported in this study were obtained exclusively from in vitro chemical and cell‐based assays; in the absence of bioavailability data and in vivo or human studies, the extrapolation of these findings to actual metabolic‐health benefits remains speculative. A coordinated evaluation of the technological, nutritional, and clinical aspects of Sicilian sumac is essential to define its role in functional food design and sustainable dietary strategies.

Experimental Section

Reagents

For chemical analyses, all solvents, standards, and reagents were purchased from Merck KGaA (Darmstadt, Germany). LC‐MS grade water, acetonitrile (ACN), and formic acid (HCOOH) were used for the HPLC‐PDA/ESI‐MS analyses. Gallic acid, protocatechuic acid, and quercetin‐3‐O‐rhamnoside were used as external standards for the quantitative determination of phenolic compounds. Stock solutions (1000 mg/L) were prepared in ethanol (EtOH).

For biological and enzymatic assays, unless otherwise specified, all chemicals were of analytical grade and obtained from the same supplier. These included DPPH (2,2‐diphenyl‐1‐picrylhydrazyl), ABTS [2,2‐azino‐bis(3‐ethylbenzothiazoline‐6‐sulfonic acid)], phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), potassium persulfate (K2S2O8), Trolox, α‐amylase, α‐glucosidase, acarbose, 3,5‐dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS), p‐nitrophenyl‐α‐D‐glucopyranoside (pNPG), sodium carbonate (Na2CO3), bovine serum albumin (BSA), methylglyoxal (MGO), sodium azide (NaN3), aminoguanidine (AG), Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin, streptomycin, L‐glutamine, 3‐(4,5‐dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl)‐2,5‐diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), gallic acid, quercetin, 2’,7’‐dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH‐DA), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2).

Plant Material and Sample Preparation

Sumac fruits were collected in the Licodia Eubea Municipality (37°09′ N, 14°42′ E), Sicily region (Italy), at an altitude of approximately 600 m above sea level. The plants grew wild in soils classified as “Regosols on sandy and conglomeratic rocks.” According to the Köppen–Geiger climate classification, the area is characterized by a Csa (hot‐summer Mediterranean) climate, with an average annual rainfall of 575 mm and an average annual temperature of 16.1 °C. Drupes were harvested in October, and the fruits were dried in a vacuum stove at 40 °C. Phenolic compounds from sumac fruits were extracted using the procedure described by Arena et al. [15].

Extraction was performed by adding 100 mL of water to 20 g of ground sumac fruit. The mixture was extracted under continuous stirring for 1 h at 40 °C. Subsequently, the aqueous phase was collected and frozen at −80°C for 1 h. Drying was carried out using a freeze dryer (LyoQuest‐55, Telstar, Spain) at −50 °C and a pressure of 0.011 mbar for 72 h. Prior to analysis, 10 mg of the dried extract was dissolved in 1 mL of water and filtered through a 0.45 µm Acrodisc filter (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) before injection. The semi‐quantification of phenolic acids (and derivatives) was performed by HPLC‐PDA analysis.

Calibration curves were constructed using gallic acid (1–1000 mg/L), quercetin‐3‐O‐rhamnoside (1–1000 mg/L), and protocatechuic acid (1–500 mg/L). High linearity was obtained for all standards, with correlation coefficients (R 2) ranging from 0.9996 to 0.9999.

Limits of detection (LoD) and limits of quantification (LoQ) values were calculated based on the parameters of calibration curves by multiplying the standard deviation (SD) of the standard compound area at the lowest concentration level by a factor of 3.3 and 10, respectively, and then by dividing the result by the slope of the calibration curve. The LoD and LoQ values were 0.042 and 0.126 mg/L for gallic acid, 0.034 and 0.103 mg/L for quercetin‐3‐O‐rhamnoside, and 0.115 and 0.350 mg/L for protocatechuic acid, respectively.

Instrumentation and Analytical Conditions for Phenolic Profiles

The HPLC‐PDA/ESI‐MS analyses of phenolic compounds were performed on a Nexera Series LC‐40 (Shimadzu, Duisburg, Germany) equipped with a CBM‐40 controller, one LC‐40B X3 dual‐plunger parallel‐flow pumps, a CTO‐40C column oven, a SIL‐40CX3 autosampler, an SPD‐M40 photo diode array detector, and LCMS‐8050 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer equipped with an ESI interface (Shimadzu, Duisburg, Germany) and used in MS mode only, while the collision cell and the second quadrupole acted as thy through zones. Phenolic compounds were separated on an Ascentis Express C18 column (150 × 2.1 mm I.D., 2.7 µm d.p.; Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany), with 0.1% formic acid in water (pH = 3; solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in ACN (solvent B) was employed under the following conditions: 0–7 min, 0%–0% B; 7–20 min, 0%–10% B; 20–40 min, 10%–25% B; 40‐60 min, 25%–60% B; 60–65 min, 60%–65% B; 65%–66 min, 65%–100%B, 66–71 min, 100%–100%B. The flow rate was set to 0.5 mL/min.

The injection volume was 2 µL, and the oven temperature was set at 30 °C. The PDA parameters were performed in the wavelength range of 190–550 nm, with a sampling frequency of 40 Hz and a time constant of 0.025 s. The chromatograms were extracted at 280 and 350 nm for phenolic acids (and derivatives) and flavonoids (and derivatives), respectively. The MS acquisitions were performed using an ESI source operating in both positive (+) and negative ionization modes (−), with the following parameters: interface and desolvation temperatures were set at 300 °C and 525 °C, respectively; heat block temperature, 400 °C; nebulizing gas flow (N2), 3 L/min; drying gas flow (N2), 10 L/min; acquisition range, 100–1500 m/z (±). Data acquisition and processing were performed using Lab Solution ver. 5.97 software X2 (Shimadzu, Duisburg, Germany).

Biological and Enzymatic Assays

Sample Dissolution

The freeze‐dried extract obtained as described in the Sample Preparation section was also used for the biological assays. Prior to analysis, the lyophilized samples were reconstituted in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS, 75 mM, pH 7.0) and filtered to ensure sample clarity and sterility.

Antioxidant Activity: DPPH and ABTS Assays

Antioxidant activity was evaluated using the DPPH (2,2‐diphenyl‐1‐picrylhydrazyl) and ABTS [2,2′‐azino‐bis(3‐ethylbenzothiazoline‐6‐sulfonic acid)] radical scavenging assays. For both assays, samples were diluted 20‐fold prior to analysis, and measurements were performed in 96‐well microplates (Greiner Bio‐One, Germany) using a multifunctional microplate reader (Infinite 200 PRO, Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland).

The DPPH assay was carried out according to Padmanabhan and Jangle with minor modifications [55]. Briefly, 20 µL of diluted sample was mixed with 180 µL of ethanolic DPPH solution (0.1 mM). Ethanol (100%) was used as a blank, while the control consisted of 20 µL ethanol and 180 µL DPPH solution. After incubation for 20 min in the dark at room temperature, absorbance was measured at 518 nm.

The ABTS assay was performed according to a previously described method, with minor modifications. The ABTS radical cation (ABTS•+) was generated by reacting a 7 mM ABTS stock solution with 2.45 mM potassium persulfate (1:1, v/v) and incubating the mixture in the dark at room temperature for 12–16 h. The ABTS•+ solution was then diluted with ethanol to an absorbance of 0.700 ± 0.020 at 734 nm. For the assay, 20 µL of diluted sample was mixed with 180 µL of ABTS•+ solution. Ethanol (100%) served as blank, while the control consisted of 20 µL ethanol and 180 µL ABTS•+ solution. After 5 min incubation at room temperature, absorbance was recorded at 734 nm. For both assays, antioxidant activity was calculated from the decrease in absorbance relative to the control and expressed as Trolox equivalents (TE), reported as mmol TE/g of sample, using a Trolox calibration curve (20–200 µM; y = 0.0015x – 0.0246, R 2 = 0.995).

Inhibition of ROS Formation in HepG2 Cells

Human hepatocellular carcinoma HepG2 cells (RRID:CVCL_0027; ECACC catalog no. 85011430) were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). These cells were used as an established model for hepatic oxidative stress and insulin resistance in T2D, recapitulating ROS‐induced mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired glucose homeostasis [58, 59]. HepG2 cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 2 mM L‐glutamine at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere. For experiments, cells were seeded at 2 × 104 cells/well in 96‐well plates and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Untreated cells (PBS vehicle) were used as the control for the MTT assay. Cell viability was assessed by MTT after treatment with sumac extract (50–300 µg/mL), gallic acid (5–50 µg/mL), or quercetin (5–50 µg/mL) for 24 h. A concentration‐dependent decrease in cell viability at higher extract concentrations confirmed the sensitivity of the assay. Formazan crystals were solubilized with DMSO, and absorbance was measured at 540 nm.

Intracellular ROS were detected using the DCFH‐DA (dichloro dihydro‐fluorescein diacetate) probe. Cells treated with 10 µM H2O2 alone served as the positive control for oxidative stress induction. Cells were pre‐incubated with 10 µM DCFH‐DA for 45 min at 37°C, followed by treatment with sumac extract, gallic acid, or quercetin alone or in combination with 10 µM H2O2 for 1, 3, and 6 h. FI was measured at excitation/emission 480/520 nm using a multimode reader (Infinite 200 PRO, Tecan, Switzerland) [28].

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates (n ≥ 3). Data are presented as mean ± SD. Prior to statistical analysis, the data were inspected for potential outliers. Differences among groups were evaluated using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) post‐hoc test for multiple comparisons. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and statistical significance was established at a significance level of α = 0.05 (p < 0.05). Statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel.
