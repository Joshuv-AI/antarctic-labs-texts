Artificial intelligence in spatial transcriptomics: a review of methods, datasets, and directions
Authors: Neelam Sharma, Rabia Şen, Cemal Erdem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpatial transcriptomics (STs) measures gene expression while retaining the location of transcripts or cells within tissue sections. Unlike bulk RNA sequencing, which averages expression across cell populations, and single-cell RNA sequencing, which generally requires tissue dissociation, ST links molecular profiles to tissue architecture. This spatial information supports the analysis of tissue heterogeneity, cellular organization, and potential intercellular interactions. The spatially resolved nature of ST has unlocked diverse applications in fields such as cancer, neuroscience, reproductive biology, and plant biology. In parallel, the development of publicly accessible databases and repositories has facilitated large-scale, multi-cohort comparisons and benchmarking. The rapid expansion and increasing complexity of these datasets have driven the adoption of machine learning (ML), deep learning (DL), and artificial intelligence (AI) techniques. These computational tools support a range of tasks, including data pre-processing, image segmentation, spatial domain identification, and pattern recognition. Moreover, they are instrumental in integrative multi-omics analyses and predictive modeling. This AI-focused review provides necessary background on ST technologies and databases and evaluates the use of statistical, ML, and DL methods across the analytical workflow, from image and sequencing-data preprocessing to spatial analysis and LLM-assisted interpretation. We compile key resources related to datasets, AI-driven analytical frameworks, and ST data analysis pipelines, and discuss current challenges, limitations, and future directions for AI-based tools in spatial omics.

Introduction

Multicellular organisms are made up of trillions of cells, each functioning as an essential building block of life. These diverse cells are organized in a specific pattern to constitute tissues and organs, and they perform specialized biological functions. Despite sharing the same genome, these cells differ significantly in function, structure, and behavior, and exhibit distinct gene expression patterns. This variation is caused not only by their tissue type but also by the surrounding microenvironment. Therefore, understanding the distinctive patterns of gene expression and spatial arrangement of cells within those tissues is essential for studying tissue function and the underlying molecular mechanisms of diseases [1].

The emergence of next-generation sequencing (NGS) has changed our ability to analyze gene expression at a genome-wide level [2]. It has transformed and opened new avenues for understanding the complex traits of biological research by enabling high-throughput and cost-effective sequencing of whole transcriptomes [3]. Bulk RNA sequencing and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) are powerful applications of NGS [4, 5]. Bulk RNA-seq quantifies RNA transcripts from tissues, averaging gene expression profiles across the entire cell population and thereby losing cellular heterogeneity. Single-cell RNA-seq enables profiling at the individual cell level while retaining the cellular heterogeneity but disrupting the original tissue architecture, resulting in loss of cellular spatial information, which is essential for understanding cell–cell interactions and tissue functionality [6–8]. Therefore, retaining spatial information is necessary for a comprehensive understanding of tissue architecture and cellular interactions within.

To circumvent the limitations of conventional single-cell technologies, spatial transcriptomics (STs) has emerged as an innovative approach that captures both gene expression profiles and spatial context across tissues [9, 10]. It generates high-resolution maps of gene expression within their native spatial context of intact tissue samples, revealing how cellular identity and function are shaped by their spatial environment [11, 12]. ST assigns each cell a unique spatial label that contains 2D spatial coordinates (x, y positions within a tissue section), and in some 3D implementations, volumetric coordinates (x, y, z), enabling accurate positioning of each cell sub-population within or across the original tissue sections [13, 14]. By providing insights into tissue architecture, ST has elucidated our understanding of tissue function, cellular interaction patterns, and microenvironment-specific gene regulation [15–17].

STs complements and competes with several other approaches for characterizing cellular identity and tissue organization. Apart from bulk and single-cell RNA-seq, multiplexed immunofluorescence (e.g. CODEX/PhenoCycler [18], t-CyCIF [19]) preserves spatial information and can profile 40–60 proteins simultaneously but is limited to protein-level readouts and pre-selected antibody panels. Live-cell imaging combined with single-molecule tracking can capture dynamic RNA processes in real time but is restricted to cultured cells or thin tissue sections. Among these, ST uniquely combines unbiased transcriptome-wide gene expression with spatial context in intact tissues, providing a molecular resolution of cellular heterogeneity and organization that none of the competing methods can achieve alone. However, ST currently lags behind multiplexed imaging in protein-level throughput, live-cell capture, and, for sequencing-based methods, spatial resolution. Integrative multi-modal approaches that combine ST with proteomics, epigenomics, or live imaging are therefore an active area of development, discussed throughout this review.

ST can tackle many biological questions related to single-cell technology and other vital issues that single-cell technology cannot resolve alone. It has a wide range of applications across various fields, including cancer, neuroscience, developmental biology, immunology, mouse biology, and plant biology. In cancer research, ST improves the analysis of tumor transcriptomic signatures and their associated cells, allowing for the profiling of spatial heterogeneity within the tumor microenvironment (TME) and providing deeper insights into tumor progression [20–23]. In neuroscience, ST facilitates the study of the spatial progression of neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer’s, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), and multiple sclerosis [24–26]. In plant biology, ST offers insights into plant microbiota interactions, metabolic pathway analysis, stress responses, and crop improvement [27–29]. ST technologies have significant potential for clinical and translational applications across various disease fields.

ST technologies, employing various approaches, have resulted in the generation of vast amounts of data, including spatial and gene expression information. The rapid accumulation of these datasets has led to the development of various publicly accessible databases and well-curated repositories. These easily accessible resources facilitate comparative studies and large-scale computational analyses. The rapid expansion and increasing complexity of these datasets have driven the adoption of machine learning (ML), deep learning (DL), and artificial intelligence (AI) techniques to analyze and interpret such complex spatial data. Importantly, throughout this review, we use the term AI broadly to encompass the hierarchical family of methods including ML, DL, and generative/foundation models. Where methods are discussed at a specific level of this hierarchy, we use the more precise term (ML, DL, or LLM) accordingly. AI- and ML-based methods are widely used for image segmentation, spatial domain identification, and linking histological images with spatial gene expression. They also enable integration of ST data with other omics modalities such as proteomics, epigenomics, and metabolomics, opening new avenues in spatial biology.

Spatial transcriptomics technologies

STs comprises experimental methods that measure RNA expression while retaining spatial coordinates within a tissue section. Depending on the platform, measurements may represent individual transcripts, segmented cells, spatial bins, beads, or multicellular capture spots. ST technologies can primarily be divided into two categories: imaging-based and sequencing-based approaches [16, 35]. Imaging-based ST methods provide direct visualization of RNA molecules through advanced microscopy techniques, whereas sequencing-based ST methods harness spatially barcoded arrays, enabling high-throughput RNA profiling that offers comprehensive insights into gene expression [36]. Recent advancements in integrating ST with other omics data, such as proteomics, epigenomics, and metabolomics, provide deeper insights into tissue biology [30, 37]. By maintaining the native tissue context, these integrated multimodal approaches enhance our understanding of cellular organization, the intricate dynamics of the TME, and the underlying mechanisms of various diseases [38].

Imaging-based ST methods

Imaging-based ST methods utilize advanced approaches, including fluorescence microscopy and molecular probing, to directly visualize RNA molecules within intact tissues without the need for RNA extraction [36]. The RNA is fixed within the tissue sections and then labeled with special fluorescent tags or barcoded oligonucleotides that precisely bind to targeted RNA molecules. They are subsequently used for detailed imaging of the transcripts through repeated cycles of hybridization and sequencing, resulting in the quantification of RNA transcripts [16, 39].

The imaging-based methods are typically divided into two: in situ hybridization (ISH) and in situ sequencing (ISS). ISH methods utilize nucleic acid hybridization to visualize gene expression directly within fixed tissues or cells. It is done by employing fluorescently labeled DNA or RNA oligonucleotides, which are specifically designed to bind to their complementary target RNA molecules [40, 41]. To determine the spatial distribution of these transcripts, fluorescence microscopy is used to detect the signals of the attached probes, ensuring the precise mapping and quantification of gene expression within its native cellular environment. On the other hand, ISS is a highly specific and sensitive technique that offers multiple levels of molecular specificity and multiplexing, allowing for the analysis of several hundred transcripts per sample [42]. It allows the localization and quantification of over 100 transcripts simultaneously at subcellular resolution, all within a single tissue section using padlock probes, rolling-circle amplification, and barcode decoding [43].

In situ hybridization

Various technologies fall under ISH, such as single-molecule Fluorescence in situ Hybridization (smFISH), which utilizes multiple short probes targeting a single transcript, producing fluorescence corresponding to the individual RNA molecules [44]. It provides precise detection of targeted RNA molecules with high sensitivity and subcellular spatial resolution, however it can only target a few genes at a time and has limited multiplexing [45]. Cyclic smFISH (osmFISH) is an advanced version of the smFISH technique that performs iterative rounds of probe hybridization, imaging, and removal to quantify individual RNA molecules in tissue sections [46]. This non-barcoded approach can handle multiplexing of hundreds of genes with higher accuracy and sensitivity [47]. However, the repeated cycles required for multiplexing make it a time-consuming and labor-intensive method. Sequential FISH (seqFISH) is another multiplexing technique that utilizes multiple rounds of sequential hybridization and imaging of barcoded probes to detect multiple genes in a single cell, while preserving spatial information [48]. seqFISH+ is an even more powerful method that can target 10 000 genes at a time, achieving almost transcriptome-wide coverage while improving sensitivity and spatial resolution [49]. Multiplexed Error-Robust FISH (MERFISH) enables highly multiplexed and error-robust detection of RNA transcripts in individual cells. It assigns unique, error-robust binary barcodes to each RNA molecule. The barcodes are then read in sequential rounds of hybridization and imaging, allowing thousands of transcripts to be imaged simultaneously [50, 51].

There has been a rapid advancement in the commercialization of ISH-based ST tools, providing highly multiplexed RNA detection with enhanced spatial resolution. For instance, NanoString’s CosMx Spatial Molecular Imager (SMI) allows high-plex cyclic smFISH-based detection of up to 1000 RNA targets and over 100 proteins at single-cell resolution [52]. In contrast, GeoMx Digital Spatial Profiler (DSP) offers region-of-interest-based profiling of both RNAs and proteins [53]. Other key platforms include Vizgen’s MERSCOPE; Akoya Bioscience’s PhenoCycler-Fusion [54], 10× Genomics’ Xenium with integrated ISS [55]; RNAscope from Advanced Cell Diagnostics (ACD) Biotechne [56]; and Resolve Biosciences’ Molecular Cartography [57]. These platforms facilitate advanced studies in spatial biology at single-cell and subcellular resolutions, enhancing our understanding of tissue architecture and cellular interactions.

In situ sequencing

ISS methods reverse transcribe the RNA molecule into complementary DNA (cDNA) directly within the tissues, followed by in situ amplification and sequencing [58]. This approach enables the identification of transcripts based on their nucleotide sequence rather than relying on predefined probes. Several ISS-based methods have been developed so far. Padlock probe-based ISS is a targeted sequencing method that utilizes padlock probes that hybridize to cDNA [59]. These probes are then enzymatically ligated to form a circularized DNA molecule, which is amplified through rolling circle amplification (RCA) to form DNA nanoballs. Lastly, the amplified products are interpreted through repeated cycles of sequencing-by-ligation, thereby identifying transcripts directly within the tissues. This approach enables the multiplexed detection of hundreds of genes, as well as the identification of single-nucleotide variants, with high specificity and sensitivity [60].

Another method, FISSEQ [61, 62], enables unbiased transcriptome-wide profiling without requiring a predefined set of probes. It amplifies cDNA using the RCA method, followed by sequencing-by-ligation chemistry with repetitive cycles of hybridization, imaging, and signal removal to accurately read RNA transcripts. However, its relatively low sensitivity and technical complexity have led to the development of more improved ISS-based methods. STARmap employs a padlock probe-based approach to amplify RNA molecules without reverse transcription. It combines hydrogel-tissue chemistry with Specific Amplification of Nucleic Acids via Intramolecular Ligation (SNAIL) probes and Sequencing with Error-reduction by Dynamic Annealing and Ligation (SEDAL). The method provides high-resolution 3D ST profiling within thick tissue samples and has a high multiplexing capacity to profile hundreds to thousands of genes at single-cell resolution simultaneously [63]. ExSeq integrates ISS with expansion microscopy to physically enlarge tissues. This approach enhances spatial resolution and reduces optical crowding, allowing for the precise mapping of RNA transcripts within subcellular localizations in intact tissues [64]. BaristaSeq is a highly efficient method developed to improve the accuracy of traditional ISS. This is achieved by optimizing the design of padlock probes in conjunction with Illumina sequencing-by-synthesis chemistry. BaristaSeq demonstrates a five-fold increase in both signal strength and sequencing accuracy, making it suitable for targeted ST applications like cell lineage tracing, neural circuit mapping, and cancer tissue profiling, all with high spatial precision [65].

Overall, the ISH methods provide higher sensitivity and resolution for identifying targeted transcripts, while ISS methods offer detailed sequence-level information and greater scalability. Together, these approaches enhance gene expression analysis within tissues by combining high-resolution spatial mapping with broader transcriptome coverage.

Sequencing-based ST methods

Sequencing-based methods in ST employ NGS along with spatially barcoded arrays on substrates such as slides or chips, to capture RNA molecules directly from intact tissue samples. In contrast to imaging-based methods, sequencing-based approaches offer unbiased profiling and high scalability across large tissues and diverse sample types [10]. Some notable research and commercial-based platforms include 10× Genomics Visium [66], Slide-seq/Slide-seqV2 [50, 51], HDST [67], DBiT-seq [68], Stereo-seq [69], Seq-Scope [70], and PIXEL-seq [71].

10× Genomics Visium is a widely used commercial platform for transcriptome-wide coverage directly from intact tissue samples. It utilizes specialized glass slides with thousands of capture spots each ~55 μm in diameter [72]. Every spot contains a unique spatial barcode and poly (dT) tails for capturing RNA transcripts. It is compatible with both formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) and fresh-frozen tissue samples. The samples are placed on the barcoded glass slides and sequenced using NGS, resulting in a comprehensive spatial map of gene expression across the whole transcriptome, which has been extensively applied in cancer biology, neuroscience, and developmental biology research [73].

Slide-seq/Slide-seqV2 involves the hybridization of tissue samples onto DNA-barcoded beads, which are ~10 μm in diameter. These beads have a fixed spatial location. Each hybridized bead is sequenced to determine the spatial resolution of RNA transcripts, while the HDST method advances spatial resolution to the sub-cellular level (~2 μm). DBiT-seq is a multi-omics platform that facilitates the co-detection of RNA transcripts and proteins using microfluidic barcoding. Stereo-seq, developed by BGI Genomics, leverages a patterned array of DNA nanoballs (DNB) with nanometer-scale barcoding, enabling subcellular resolution of ~500 nm [69]. Seq-scope offers ultra-high resolution (~0.5–0.8 μm) with nanoscale detail, allowing for spatial transcriptome profiling and sub-cellular level visualization of gene expression. Finally, Pixel-seq employs patterned arrays of spatially barcoded primers to achieve high sensitivity compared to Slide-seq and HDST. It allows uniform spatial sampling, which reduces bias and offers high spatial resolution for mapping fine-scale tissue structures.

Sequencing-based ST methods offer higher scalability and unbiased, high-throughput whole-transcriptome profiling, allowing reconstruction of tissue-wide molecular atlases from intact tissue samples. However, a significant limitation is their low spatial resolution compared to imaging-based methods, making it challenging to achieve single-cell resolution.

Multi-modal, hybrid, and emerging ST platforms

Beyond the established imaging- and sequencing-based categories, a new generation of hybrid platforms is emerging by combining in situ sequencing with NGS barcoding, or by co-profiling RNA with proteins, chromatin accessibility, and metabolites at spatial resolution. For example, DBiT-seq [68] and its successor xDBiT [74], Matrix-seq [75], and MAGIC-seq [76] co-detect RNA and proteins using microfluidic barcoding on the same tissue section. Spatial epigenomics platforms -including ATAC-seq and spatial CUT&Tag- are now being combined with ST to co-profile chromatin accessibility and gene expression from the same tissue. Spatial metabolomics approaches using mass spectrometry imaging and desorption electrospray ionization are increasingly being registered with ST datasets to link metabolic states to transcriptional programs [77].

While PIXEL-seq achieves single-molecule RNA detection alongside spatial barcoding [77], recent platforms such as 10× Genomics Visium HD, released in 2024, extend barcoded array resolution to 2 μm, approaching single-cell resolution for sequencing-based methods. Conversely, Xenium integrates ISS with computational spot-to-cell assignment for direct single-cell localization.

Other advancements include microdissection-based technologies, such as laser-capturing microdissection [78, 79], Tomo-seq [80], and Geo-seq [81], which provide specific positional information that helps reconstruct 3D tissue structures. Approaches such as ProximID [82], PIC-seq [83], and ClumpSeq [84] offer high-resolution molecular analysis by partially dissociating tissue samples while maintaining their spatial context. Optical barcoding methods, NICHE-seq [85], ZipSeq, and TIVA [86], use photoactivated oligonucleotide barcodes or TIVA tags to label and profile regions of interest within the native tissue environment.

These hybrid approaches greatly expand the biological information obtainable from a single tissue section but introduce additional computational challenges related to data integration, resolution mismatches, and multi-modal batch correction. Spatial transcriptomic data resources

The advent of ST technologies has led to the generation of a vast amount of heterogeneous data across tissues, species, and disease contexts. However, the produced datasets were dispersed across the literature and are not systematically organized. To overcome this limitation, various specialized databases and repositories have been developed to make these datasets easily accessible. These resources provide an infrastructure that combines raw, original data with advanced visualization tools to facilitate data sharing, standardization, and large-scale analyses.

Major databases and repositories include DeepSpaceDB, a newer platform that hosts thousands of samples from human and mouse tissues, enabling advanced interactive analysis and promoting large-scale cross-study integration [87]. SOAR is a comprehensive database hosting largest number of ST samples, i.e. 4767 samples with spatial coordinates from 13 species across 58 tissue types [88]. SORC is a cancer-specific ST repository that contains 82 ST datasets across 17 cancer types, comprising over 722 899 spots for 269 tissue slices. It is an open-source, user-friendly platform that facilitates the integration of multi-omics data [89]. STOmicsDB is another comprehensive database for sharing, analyzing, and visualizing ST data. It contains more than 300 manually curated datasets representing 17 species [90]. CROST stores 544 high-quality ST datasets and 48 043 tumor-related spatially variable genes (SVGs) spanning various species, organs, and diseases [91]. SPASCER contains datasets from 43 studies, including 1082 sub-datasets from 16 organ types across four species [92]. Aquila is a spatial omics database and analysis platform, comprising 110 datasets derived from 30 diseases, incorporating more than 6500 regions of interest, and 15.7 million cells. The platform integrates studies across ST and proteomic analyses, as well as 2D and 3D experimental frameworks, and distinct technologies [93]. SODB is a repository that allows cross-platform comparisons and benchmarking. It maintains data from around 3000 experiments from more than 25 different spatial omics technologies [94]. SpatialDB is one of the earliest databases for spatially resolved transcriptomes. It encompasses 24 datasets and 305 sub-datasets across five species generated by eight different ST methodologies [95].

All these repositories emphasize standardized metadata, submission frameworks, and data formats to facilitate FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) data sharing and reusability in ST [96, 97]. These databases are publicly available and feature user-friendly web interfaces, enabling non-computational experts from various biological fields to easily access and analyze the information. Database selection guide

As listed in Table 3, for cancer research, SORC provides the most curated cancer focused ST data with multi-omics integration capability while CROST is ideal for SVG-centered analyses. For neuroscience, STOmicsDB and SOAR contain large brain atlas datasets. For cross-species and plant biology, SOAR and STOmicsDB support the widest species range. Challenges of ST data resources

While providing vastly rich datasets, the STs repositories contain datasets generated using heterogeneous platforms, spatial resolutions, transcriptome coverages, file formats, coordinate systems, segmentation procedures, and metadata conventions (Fig. 1). These differences impair interoperability and introduce batch effects, annotation drift, feature mismatch, and possible train-test contamination in AI studies. The consequences include unreliable benchmarking, limited reproducibility, weak cross-platform transfer, and substantial computational burden. Common data models, controlled vocabularies, provenance tracking, dataset versioning, study-level train-test separation, platform-aware harmonization, and FAIR metadata are therefore needed to support robust integration and AI-model validation.

AI, ML, and DL: a definitional framework in the context of ST

AI is the broadest term, referring to any computational system designed to perform tasks that would otherwise require human-like reasoning or pattern recognition. ML is a subset of AI in which models learn from data without being explicitly programmed. It encompasses methods such as linear regression, support vector machines, and random forests. DL is a subset of ML that uses multi-layer neural networks to learn hierarchical representations from large datasets. It includes convolutional neural networks (CNNs), graph neural networks (GNNs), variational autoencoders (VAEs), and transformer architectures. LLMs are another subcategory of DL, specifically transformer-based models trained on large-scale text collections. In ST, these computational tiers map to the analytical pipelines: classical statistical and machine learning methods (including PCA, clustering, and regression) dominate preprocessing; DL (GNNs, CNNs, VAEs) drives domain identification, deconvolution, and trajectory inference; and LLMs are emerging for interactive, interpretive, and integrative tasks.

Data pre-processing: image-based pre-processing and sequence-based pre-processing

In ST data analysis, pre-processing is essential to ensure data quality and reliability before any in-depth analysis. The first step is quality control to remove low-quality data, followed by normalization to ensure accurate biological findings during downstream analysis. Depending on which type of ST method is used, data pre-processing is classified into two categories: imaging-based pre-processing for ISH and ISS-based methods, which capture RNA molecules using fluorescent imaging, and sequencing-based pre-processing for NGS-based spatial barcode methods.

Imaging-based pre-processing methods in ST are a critical set of computational and image analysis workflows designed to convert raw image data into reliable spatial gene expression information. These methods are primarily applied to imaging-based ST technologies, such as ISH, ISS, and fluorescence-based techniques. The workflow includes image acquisition, image registration and alignment, transcript spot identification, signal decoding, cell segmentation, quality control, and normalization [10, 103]. For image acquisition, raw images from different hybridization methods are corrected for illumination artifacts, focus drift, autofluorescence, and background noise to ensure accurate signal detection. Tools like ImageJ/Fiji, Napari, Elastix, and BigStitcher are commonly used for basic image adjustments, such as contrast correction, noise reduction, and stitching [104–107].

Followed with image registration and alignment, the microscopy images are precisely aligned to create a unified spatial coordinate system [108]. After alignment, transcript spot identification and localization are performed to detect individual RNA molecules in tissue images, using tools like DeepBlink, a representative deep-learning method [109]. Cellpose and StarDist are widely used segmentation baselines in the next step [110, 111]. Quality control and normalization are integrated throughout the imaging pipeline, applying spatially aware smoothing and noise reduction techniques [112]. Recent advances incorporate DL and AI methods to enhance pre-processing accuracy by integrating histology features with spatial gene expression patterns. For instance, GHIST is a DL-based method trained on millions of histological images to extract morphological features, improving spatial domain identification [113].

Sequence-based pre-processing applies to NGS-based technologies that generate sequencing data with spatial barcode information, including Visium, Slide-seq, and Stereo-seq. The goal is to transform raw NGS reads into spatially resolved gene expression matrices through various computational pipelines. The major steps for sequencing-based pre-processing include spatial barcode mapping and demultiplexing, read alignment and gene quantification, histological image processing, and relating gene expression to tissue images [103, 114]. The workflow begins with spatial barcode processing and demultiplexing, utilizing standard pipelines such as Space Ranger for comprehensive barcode mapping, read alignment, and expression quantification [115]. STtools aligns the reads to a reference genome and maps them back to their corresponding spatial coordinates [116]. For read alignment and transcript quantification, tools like STAR and HISAT2 provide high-accuracy gapped alignment of reads [117, 118]. For histological image integration, packages are used to spot coordinates onto H&E-stained images, facilitating spot-to-tissue mapping and extracting morphological features for downstream analysis.

Overall, imaging-based and sequence-based pre-processing ensures the generation of high-quality, spatially resolved transcriptomic datasets, providing a strong foundation for downstream spatial analyses. So, selecting tools according to the specific error source, such as segmentation or transcript assignment, is advantageous rather than applying a single general image-analysis package to the complete workflow. Although Cellpose or StarDist can provide useful baselines, assay-aware methods may be preferable when transcript coordinates, membrane markers, or tissue-specific training data are available. In sequence-based pre-processing, using platform-specific pipelines for initial barcode and coordinate processing makes sense, usually with parameter tuning. Importantly, inspecting the spatial distribution of quality-control metrics can prevent removal of biologically meaningful low-RNA regions. The tools of data pre-processing for ST are enlisted in the Supplementary Table 1.

Downstream analysis

After data pre-processing, where raw images and sequencing reads are transformed into a gene expression or Data Matrix, the next step is downstream analysis (Fig. 1). The process employs various computational and statistical methods to extract biological or clinical insights from the processed ST data. The key steps include batch-effect correction, dimensionality reduction, spatial clustering and domain detection, cell-type annotation and deconvolution, detection of SVGs, spatial interaction analysis, spatial trajectory and temporal analysis, integration with omics and histology data, 3D spatial reconstruction, and advanced visualization [119, 120]. A wide range of advanced ML, DL, and AI tools has been developed for these applications, which are used to interpret ST data and understand the spatial organization of genes, cells, and tissues that underlie complex biological systems and disease processes.

The selection of computational methods should be guided by the biological question, data-generating platform, spatial resolution, availability of histological images and single-cell references, modality matching, and computational scale. Figure 3 provides an evidence-guided decision framework connecting these dataset characteristics to the principal analytical tasks and representative method classes. Rather than identifying a universally optimal tool, the framework emphasizes task-appropriate selection, comparison of alternative approaches, sensitivity assessment, uncertainty reporting, and independent biological validation.

Evidence-guided selection of AI and computational methods for spatial transcriptomics. Method selection should begin with the biological question, data-generating platform, available modalities, spatial resolution, and reference data, rather than with the popularity of a particular model or tool. Imaging-based workflows emphasize transcript detection, decoding, registration, and cell segmentation, whereas sequencing-based workflows require barcode processing, alignment, quality control, and generation of spatial expression matrices. Spatial-domain and deconvolution methods should be selected according to histology availability, reference quality, tissue complexity, and desired uncertainty estimates. Cross-modal integration requires explicit consideration of matched versus unmatched samples and resolution mismatch. LLMs are positioned after quantitative analysis as assistive tools for querying, annotation, and hypothesis generation. Regardless of the selected method, the workflow should include sensitivity analysis, reporting of preprocessing and parameter settings, and validation of major findings using independent data or orthogonal evidence where feasible.

Batch-effect correction

Batch effects are unwanted variations caused by differences and inconsistencies in experimental conditions, such as sample preparation (using different slides or tissue sections) or sequencing platforms and protocols [119, 121]. Such effects can hide the actual biological signals and affect the downstream analysis. Therefore, batch-effect correction is an essential first step for eliminating technical variations that can mislead results and impact research outcomes. Conventional approaches such as Harmony provide a non-spatial integration baseline, whereas spatialMNN and graph-based frameworks such as SPIRAL or SpaCross incorporate spatial neighborhood information [122–125]. Some of these methods were initially developed for scRNA-seq but are now widely used where they can effectively process ST data by utilizing spatial coordinates, expression profiles, and neighborhood graphs. The goal is to minimize batch effects while preserving biological architecture, enabling accurate integration of multiple samples. At this stage, users should identify the expected technical and biological sources of variation before applying the correction and compare corrected and uncorrected analyses to verify that tissue architecture, rare populations, condition effects, and spatial gradients remain detectable. Various tools developed for batch effect correction are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Dimensionality reduction

Dimensionality reduction is the process of transforming high-dimensional data into lower-dimensional data that preserves key information while reducing the number of features. In ST, it condenses the gene expression profiles into low-dimensional embeddings that also retain the spatial context. PCA remains a transparent and computationally efficient baseline, but it does not explicitly model spatial relationships. Spatially aware methods incorporate neighborhood graphs or spatial covariance: SpatialPCA uses a probabilistic framework, STAGATE learns graph-attention embeddings, and STAMP uses topic modeling to generate spatially informed and interpretable latent factors [126–128]. Also, tools like SpaGCN [129] combine dimension reduction with spatial neighborhood analysis, visualization, and clustering, thereby offering integrated workflows for comprehensive exploration of ST data. Regardless of the tool used, the appropriate representation depends on the downstream objective, and embeddings should be evaluated by stability, biological marker coherence, spatial continuity, and downstream reproducibility rather than visual separation alone. The details of many dimensionality reduction tools are given in Supplementary Table 3.

Spatial clustering and domain detection

Spatial clustering, also referred to as spatial domain identification, facilitates the identification of distinct tissue regions and microenvironments that share similar gene expression patterns and spatial organization. These spatial domains are the regions within tissues where cells exhibit similar molecular signatures, cellular interactions, and most likely perform related biological functions. The domains are computationally identified by spatial clustering, where coherent regions are grouped based on their transcriptional profile similarity and spatial organization [130, 131].

Existing approaches include graph-based representation learning, Bayesian spatial modelling, and conventional clustering. Conventional community detection algorithms, like Louvain and Leiden [132], utilize k-nearest neighbor graphs on low-dimensional embeddings and partition them into spatially coherent clusters. STAGATE and GraphST learn spatially informed graph embeddings, SpaGCN can incorporate histological information, and BayesSpace uses probabilistic spatial regularization [128, 129, 133, 134]. Multi-sample frameworks extend domain identification to aligned or jointly modeled tissue sections. Recent advancements to SpaGCN and stLearn [135] integrate morphological features derived from histological images (such as H&E staining) and transcriptomic data, employing graph convolution and smoothing algorithms to enhance spatial domain identification [135]. The details of these tools are given in Supplementary Table 4.

These existing methods differ substantially in how they weight spatial versus transcriptomic signals. Graph-based methods such as GraphST and STAGATE have performed competitively in several benchmarks, but their results depend on graph construction, preprocessing, platform, and tissue architecture. BayesSpace provides probabilistic spatial regularization, whereas Louvain or Leiden clustering offers computationally efficient non-spatial baselines. Selection should be based on the analytical objective, dataset size, spatial resolution, histology availability, and required form of uncertainty assessment.

Cell-type annotation and deconvolution

Cell-type annotation and deconvolution are essential steps in ST data analytics, tackling the challenge of identifying distinct cell types and evaluating their proportions within specific spatial domains [136, 137]. Recent advances in cell-type annotation and deconvolution have produced a diverse array of computational tools, ranging from marker-based methods to sophisticated probabilistic and DL-based frameworks [138].

Reference-based methods transfer information from scRNA-seq atlases to spatial data. Cell2location estimates spatial cell-type abundances using a probabilistic model, CARD uses spatially informed regression, RCTD models mixtures of reference cell types, and DestVI represents both cell-type proportions and continuous within-type states [139–142]. For imaging-based data with cellular resolution, segmentation and annotation frameworks such as Bering may be more appropriate than spot-level deconvolution [143, 144]. The details of the cell-type annotation and deconvolution tools are given in Supplementary Table 5

Here, the deconvolution accuracy depends heavily on the quality of the scRNA-seq reference. Probabilistic frameworks (Cell2location, RCTD) outperform regression-based methods on heterogeneous tissues but are computationally heavier. CARD offers a balance of accuracy and speed via spatial smoothing. For imaging-based platforms with subcellular resolution, segmentation-first methods (Bering) are preferred. Reference-free approaches remain an open challenge.

Spatially variable genes detection

Spatially variable genes are genes that exhibit varying expression patterns across different regions within a sample [145]. Detection of SVGs is a crucial step in downstream analysis to understand tissue organization, cell–cell communication, and disease processes. Numerous computational methods, varying from statistical models to GNNs and ensemble frameworks, have been developed to identify SVGs [146, 147].

For instance, SPARK-X provides a scalable non-parametric framework, Moran’s I offers a transparent spatial-autocorrelation baseline, and methods such as STAMarker use learned spatial representations to identify domain-associated genes [148–150]. There are also cell type-specific SVG detection methods and classical spatial autocorrelation approaches to determine SVGs.

Similar to non-STs, differential expression results can be influenced by sequencing depth, in addition to tissue boundaries, cell-type composition, and the spatial domains used in the analysis. Importantly, a gene can therefore appear spatially variable because its expressing cell type is localized, rather than because it is spatially regulated within that cell type. Thus, findings should be confirmed and interpreted with cellular composition and tissue structure in mind. The list of all the tools for SVG detection are given in Supplementary Table 6.

Spatial interaction analysis

Spatial interaction analysis aims to identify relationships among neighboring cells, cell types, or genes. Gene–gene co-expression analysis can determine spatially correlated regulatory modules and molecular pathways [151], whereas cell–cell communication analysis via ligand-receptor signaling and proximity information could unveil how neighboring cell types interact within their microenvironment [152]. There are cell–cell communication tools, statistical-based methods, network-based methods, and ligand-receptor and spatial proximity frameworks (Supplementary Table 7). Gene–gene co-expression tools detect spatially structured gene networks, with recent advancements such as Smoothie, which uses Gaussian smoothing to infer genome-wide gene correlation networks from denoised ST data [153]. CellChat and NicheNet provide widely used non-spatial or reference frameworks for signaling inference, whereas SpatialDM and CytoSignal explicitly incorporate spatial organization [154–157].

These methods are used to identify genes with pairwise relationships that are spatially structured, thereby revealing regulatory networks that influence tissue phenotype. However, the predicted interactions should be regarded as hypotheses that need to be validated across samples and methods. Methods that combine ligand-receptor evidence with explicit spatial proximity are stronger, but conclusions can still depend on segmentation and deconvolution. The tools for spatial interaction analysis are enlisted in Supplementary Table 7.

Spatial trajectory and temporal analysis

Spatial trajectory and temporal analysis in ST offer insights into the dynamic changes and movements of cells within their native environments over time. It aids in reconstructing dynamic cellular processes, such as differentiation, development, and disease progression [158]. These analyses integrate the physical location of each cell (spatial coordinates) with its gene expression data, as well as the speed and direction of changes [159]. It helps determine the order in which cells transform, how they are related, interact with each other, and evolve over time. The combination of spatial and temporal information provides deeper insights into developmental and disease processes that conventional single-cell RNA-seq methods cannot capture [160, 161]. spVelo combines expression modeling with graph-based spatial information, Cell2fate models transcriptional dynamics through RNA-velocity modules, and SpaTrack uses optimal transport to infer spatial cell-state transitions [162–164]. Most ST datasets provide static observations rather than direct lineage measurements and spatial proximity is not equivalent to actual temporal succession. Thus, application of trajectory analysis should be employed only when the biological system supports the ordering process. Integration with multi-omics and histology data

Integrating ST data with other omics data, such as proteomics, epigenomics, and image-omics (detailed tissue image readings), provides a deeper biological insight into tissues and diseases [165, 166]. This multimodal approach enables a more comprehensive understanding of biological systems, encompassing cellular composition, spatial organization, and molecular interactions within tissues. However, integrating multimodal data presents several challenges, including data heterogeneity, batch effects, and missing values [123]. Employing hypergraph and contrastive learning, SpatialEx facilitates the integration of multiple omics layers guided by histology, expanding molecular profiling beyond single-omics assays [167]. ResST, a self-supervised learning model based on GNNs and margin disparity discrepancy, integrates gene expression, spatial locations, and tissue morphology data to identify spatial domains across the datasets while correcting for batch effects [168]. NicheCompass is a graph-based DL approach that models cellular interactions and characterizes cell niches by integrating spatial multi-omics data at cell- and spot-level resolution [169]. BANKSY is a ML-based framework that utilizes a spatial clustering algorithm to integrate spatial and molecular features through neighborhood transcriptomes and their microenvironment, supporting batch correction and scalable clustering [170]. MOFA+ is a statistical framework and a classical baseline that allows the integration of multi-omics modalities, including single-cell data [171]. Overall, the integration of ST data with multi-omics and histology data enables high-resolution analyses that unveil cellular interactions and tissue architecture, which are essential for biological research and translational medicine. The details of the tools are given in Supplementary Table 9.

Integration is complicated by differences in spatial resolution, feature sets, noise, missing modalities, and sample correspondence. Thus, starting such integrative analyses with matched-cell or matched-section samples is a sound approach when possible. Further, the integrated analyses should be compared with unimodal baselines and evaluated for modality-specific signal and biological interpretability, and out-of-sample robustness. Added algorithmic complexity should be justified by improved accuracy, interpretability, or discovery.

3D spatial reconstruction

3D spatial reconstruction enables creation of a high-resolution 3D model of tissues by combining multiple layers of 2D tissue sections. The reconstructed 3D model can be analyzed for SVGs, signaling domains, or interaction networks in 3D space. Recent computational tools combine image registration, DL, and graph-based methods to perform slice alignment, batch correction, and 3D domain identification, facilitating studies of TME, developmental processes, and neural circuits [172–174]. So3D is a comprehensive 3D spatial omics database containing 72 high-quality 3D spatial transcriptome datasets across four species. It also provides interactive visualization and analysis modules for 3D spatial domain identification, cell-type mapping, and the detection of differential gene expression [173]. Open-ST is a framework that provides tools for efficiently capturing and analyzing transcripts in 2D and 3D spaces at subcellular resolution [172]. Pyxa (a platform by Stellaromics) enables 3D ST of thick, intact tissues using STARmap technology [63]. X-Pression is a cost-effective method that employs deep CNNs to reconstruct 3D gene expression signatures from microcomputed tomography data [175]. isoST uses AI to reconstruct 3D ST profiles with isotropic resolution from sparsely sampled serial sections [176]. SpaBatch is a DL-based framework developed for cross-slice integration and 3D reconstruction. It performs embedding learning, batch correction, and clustering optimization simultaneously for accurate spatial domain identification [177]. Deep-STARmap and Deep-RIBOmap can quantify thousands of transcripts and their translation activities within 200-μm intact thick tissue sections. These tools also facilitate 3D spatial profiling, enabling quantitative analysis of tumor-immune interactions in human skin cancer [174]. Although tissue deformation, missing sections, unequal spacing, batch effects, and limited ground truth can complicate evaluation, these methods uncover hidden patterns in 2D images of tissues by transforming them into 3D reconstructions, thereby enhancing our understanding of tissue architecture and disease mechanisms. Details of these tools are presented in Supplementary Table 10.

Advanced visualization

Visualization is essential for analyzing ST data, as it transforms complex, high-dimensional spatial gene expression data into clear and interpretable visual representations. Effective visualization methods help in studying tissue architecture, interpreting cellular interactions and organization, exploring spatial gene expression patterns, and identifying spatial domains. Various advanced visualization tools have been developed to help researchers interpret complex data more effectively. These include SCNT, a user-friendly R package that offers high-resolution spatial visualization along with customizable gene expression and clustering plots [178]. spatialGE is a web application that facilitates interactive data visualization for quality control, domain detection, and spatial gene expression exploration [179]. Vitessce is another web-based platform for interactive visualization of multi-modal and spatial data [180]. Napari is a Python-based interactive image viewer designed for browsing, annotating, and analyzing large datasets of multi-dimensional images [106]. TissUUmaps can be used to visualize gene expression data on top of whole slide images [181]. Some platform-specific viewers can support initial quality control but may offer limited reproducibility or cross-platform interoperability [182]. Since visualization choices can substantially affect data interpretation, interactive exploration should therefore complement rather than replace quantitative testing. Visualization tools should be selected according to dataset scale, image support, multi-modal capability, and interoperability. Using consistent color/number scales when comparing samples is also essential. Additional platforms that provide insightful visualizations are given in Supplementary Table 11.

Across these downstream analysis tasks, AI contributes most clearly where spatial, image, graph, or multi-modal relationships cannot be represented adequately by conventional (non-spatial) methods. CNNs support image analysis, GNNs model tissue neighborhoods, autoencoders learn nonlinear representations, and multi-modal models connect gene expression with morphology or other molecular layers. However, model selection should be supported by appropriate baselines, sensitivity analyses, uncertainty assessment, and possibly benchmark evidence or external validation. Systematic comparisons show that performance is conditional on the platform, tissue, pre-processing workflow, reference data, ground truth, and evaluation metric [131, 137, 145, 183–186]. No single method consistently dominates across all ST tasks or datasets. AI tools should therefore be selected as part of an evidence-guided workflow rather than according to novelty alone, with measured, inferred, and imputed quantities clearly distinguished throughout the analysis.

Applications of ST

ST has transformed molecular biology by mapping gene expression within its spatial context, linking molecular signatures to tissue architecture [187–189]. Its applications span cancer biology, neuroscience, immunology, developmental biology, infectious disease, drug discovery, and plant biology (Fig. 2). By preserving spatial information, ST enables researchers to study not only which genes are active but also the specific locations where they are active within tissues, offering an extensive view of cellular interactions, microenvironmental cues, and disease mechanisms. These insights support advances in biomarker discovery, therapeutic targeting, and diagnostics.

Cancer and tumor microenvironment

The spatially resolved information from ST reveals patterns of heterogeneity, cellular interactions, and organization niches that bulk and conventional single-cell RNA-seq cannot capture. Hence, its applications extend from basic tumor biology to biomarker discovery and therapy response prediction [190, 191]. One of the significant applications of ST is to characterize the TME, including immune, stromal, and vascular compartments and their spatial relationships to tumor cells [192]. By detailed mapping, ST defines the spatial distribution and functional states of various immune cells, including T cells, B cells, myeloid cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells. It helps in identifying immunosuppressive regions, cancer-associated fibroblast phenotypes, and tertiary lymphoid structures, all of which play a role in initiating antitumor responses [193–195]. The spatially resolved maps of the TME can guide the design of immunotherapies and rational combination of treatments aimed at targeting specific niches, rather than treating the tumor as a uniform mass [196].

Another major ST application in cancer is to define spatial gene expression signatures associated with prognosis, metastasis risk, and treatment response [197]. By integrating ligand-receptor analysis with spatial proximity, ST could reveal specific cell–cell communication axes that drive tumor progression, angiogenesis, and immune suppression [192, 196]. ST is also used to examine invasive margins, pre-metastatic niches, and metastatic lesions to understand local invasion and distant spread [198, 199]. Profiling of primary tumors, surrounding tissue, and metastasis sites has revealed specific gene expression changes that facilitate colonization. These changes include altered fibroblast states and immune exclusion at metastatic sites [200, 201]. This helps us understand how tumors alter local environments during progression and suggests therapies targeting specific areas to prevent or treat metastasis.

In drug discovery and translational oncology, ST is utilized to assess how candidate therapies influence the spatial patterns of gene expression and cell states within the TME, enabling the identification of on-target effects, resistance mechanisms, and off-target effects [187, 202, 203]. Spatial profiling before and after treatment can help identify the emergence of resistant clones and highlight where protective microenvironments persist. This information can guide rational drug combinations, optimize doses, and support development of spatially informed biomarkers for improved clinical decision-making.

Neuroscience

ST offers valuable neurological insights by resolving the complex cellular organization of the brain while preserving anatomical context [204]. By generating high-resolution gene expression atlases across distinct brain regions, possibly over time, ST applications enabled a more precise study of brain development, normal physiology, and disease mechanisms [205]. In neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease, and ALS, ST has been used to examine dysregulated gene networks and transcriptional changes occurring in the structural tissue domain surrounding amyloid plaques [24]. Spatial analyses in AD identified distinct subpopulations of microglia and astrocytes that cluster around plaques. These subpopulations exhibit unique inflammatory and disease-associated transcriptional signatures, revealing localized cellular networks that contribute to neuroinflammation and synaptic dysfunction [206]. In ALS, it maps region-specific changes in glia and neurons within the spinal cord and brainstem, enhancing the understanding of how degeneration occurs over time and in different areas [207].

ST has also provided valuable insights into neuropsychiatric disorders, stroke, and epilepsy. It can help detect changes in gene expression and cell states that occur in specific cortical layers, hippocampal subfields, or other vulnerable regions following injury or in disease models [205]. These studies showed spatially localized changes in synaptic, inflammatory, and metabolic pathways, along with the reorganization of both inhibitory and excitatory circuits, which cannot be detected through bulk profiling [204, 206]. By integrating ST with electrophysiology, imaging, and behavioral data, researchers can directly link molecular pathology to functional circuits and phenotypes in these conditions [208].

In addition to its role in studying diseased conditions, ST also contributes to study brain development and circuit assembly. It can map spatiotemporal gene expression patterns that direct neurogenesis, migration, and lamination in both the embryonic and postnatal brain [204, 209]. ST can help outline the development of neuronal and glial lineages, identify regional patterning cues and niche factors that influence the formation of functional circuits, offering mechanistic insight into how early developmental perturbations may predispose to later neurological or psychiatric diseases [189, 204]. These applications position ST as a pivotal integrative tool in neuroscience, linking molecular states, spatial architecture, and circuit function in both healthy and diseased conditions.

Immunology and developmental biology

ST has transformed immunology by enabling researchers to study immune cells within their native tissue context. This approach reveals how the localization and functional states of these cells are influenced by the surrounding cells and extracellular matrix [210, 211]. Unlike traditional transcriptomics, which dissociate tissues and lose spatial information, ST maps immune cells within their native tissue environments, revealing how location shapes function in organs such as lymph nodes, the spleen, the liver, the intestine, and tumors [169]. It identifies specialized immune niches where B cells, T cells, dendritic cells, or macrophages adopt distinct transcriptional states based on their spatial context [210].

In developmental biology, ST captures spatiotemporal gene expression patterns during embryogenesis, organogenesis, and tissue patterning, while preserving the 3D context that is typically lost in dissociated samples [212, 213]. By profiling whole embryos at high resolution, ST can help reconstruct molecular gradients, cellular trajectories, and signaling centers that drive morphogenesis, revealing how transcription factors, enhancers, and chromatin states vary across tissues and developmental stages [214, 215]. ST is also used to study remodeling and regeneration in organs such as the heart, kidney, liver, and musculoskeletal tissues, revealing cell signaling networks supporting wound healing and tissue regeneration [216].

Infectious diseases

ST is transforming infectious disease research by simultaneously mapping the gene expression of both pathogens and hosts within their native tissue context. This reveals the localization of viruses, bacteria, or parasites and highlights how host responses vary in different spatial regions [217, 218]. In contrast to conventional methods, ST illustrates how infection disperses through organs, identifies microenvironments where immune responses succeed or fail, and reveals patterns of local tissue damage that clarify clinical pathology [219, 220].

In studying viral infections, ST has been extensively used to analyze SARS-CoV-2, where it maps the distribution of viral RNA in the lungs, brain, and other organs, while also identifying host immune signatures [219, 221, 222]. Research has shown that infected cells tend to cluster in specific areas, such as alveolar regions, bronchiolar epithelium, and endothelial cells. These findings reveal diverse interferon responses and macrophage activation, which correlate with disease severity and the neuropathological complications associated with long COVID [223]. Similar approaches have been used for influenza, HIV, and herpesviruses to study tropism, immune evasion niches, and persistent reservoirs [224].

In bacterial infections like tuberculosis, ST is used to analyze the architecture of granulomas. It can reveal necrotic cores surrounded by specific immune layers, each characterized by distinct states of macrophages, T cells, and fibroblasts, hence explaining drug resistance and the persistence of the infection [225]. By providing spatially resolved insights, ST identifies potential therapeutic targets in the areas where the infection is most active, as well as locations where the immune response is failing across viral, bacterial, and parasitic infections. By integrating ST with single-cell resolution imaging, researchers can study the intricate interactions between pathogen dissemination, immune response, and collateral tissue damage that influence infection outcomes [226].

Drug discovery and precision medicine

ST is being investigated in drug-discovery studies to localize treatment-associated transcriptional changes, identify spatially restricted disease pathways, and characterize resistant or treatment-responsive tissue regions. Current applications include target prioritization, analysis of treatment mechanisms, toxicity assessment, and evaluation of candidate spatial biomarkers. Most such applications remain preclinical or retrospective [88, 187]. In contrast to conventional transcriptomic approaches, which often overlook anatomical context, ST reveals the precise locations where drugs modulate gene programs. ST can highlight which cell types respond to treatment and which niches remain resistant. It supports precision medicine by enabling spatially informed biomarker assessment and patient stratification, helping predict treatment response and resistance in clinical and translational studies [227].

In target discovery, ST identifies novel drug targets by highlighting SVGs enriched in disease-specific regions, such as tumor invasive fronts or inflamed tissue zones [187]. It links these genes to druggable pathways, facilitating drug repurposing, such as identifying tumor specific antigens to trigger monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation [228], as well as prioritizing drug candidates through platforms like SOAR, which integrate ST with protein interaction networks [88].

Drug mechanism and resistance profiling represent another key application where ST maps pre- and post-treatment tissues to visualize on-target effects, adaptive resistance mechanisms, and off-target toxicities in situ [120, 209]. In oncology, researchers utilize ST to monitor how therapies alter TME, identifying where resistant clones emerge, such as in hypoxic cores, or where protective stromal niches persist, aiding in drug combination strategies and dose optimization [37, 191].

ST can play a crucial role in developing biomarkers by identifying spatial gene signatures that can predict treatment responses and prognosis [229]. For complex diseases such as cancer, it could map drug targets across diverse tissue types, guiding combination therapies and assessing the likelihood of treatment resistance. ST is positioned to be integrated into clinical workflows for companion diagnostics, digital pathology, and personalized treatment planning, bridging molecular insights with histopathological phenotypes [187, 227].

Plant biology

In plant biology, ST is transformative, by overcoming longstanding challenges posed by plant cell walls, large vacuoles, and complex tissue architectures that complicate traditional single-cell approaches [230]. Recent advances in platforms such as Stereo-seq, Slide-seq, and MERFISH have enabled high-resolution mapping of gene expression in plant tissues. This has revealed insights into cellular diversity, developmental pathways, and responses to stress, all while preserving the spatial context of the samples [28, 231]. In stress and environmental biology, ST examines how gene regulatory networks are activated in specific tissues or cell types under conditions such as drought, salt stress, pathogen attacks, and nutrient deficiencies [230]. It also explores wound responses and symbiotic interactions. It can reveal how different cell types (epidermal, cortical, or endodermal) contribute distinct transcriptional responses to both mutualistic and pathogenic relationships [27, 28].

Finally, crop improvement and functional genomics applications leverage ST to identify trait-associated regulatory hubs and breeding targets [232]. In crops such as maize, rice, and tomato, ST has mapped processes including kernel development, grain filling, and fruit ripening, and revealed spatial regulators that influence yield, nutritional content, and stress tolerance [27]. By incorporating ST with genome-wide association studies and CRISPR screenings, researchers can identify non-coding enhancers and cell-type-specific promoters that drive important agronomic traits. Future integration with spatial proteomics and metabolomics could also lead to comprehensive multi-omics atlases, enhancing the precision of breeding and bioengineering to develop climate-resilient crops [230, 233].

Towards translational studies

To illustrate the translational impact of ST, below we highlight three examples where ST data involves clinical samples or studies that could potentially inform clinical decision-making.

He et al. [229] integrated ST gene expression from primary breast tumors with matched histological images using a deep learning framework. In addition to accurately predicting gene expression, the method captured biologically relevant image features, separating tumor from normal tissue and flagging incongruent annotations. Notably, the work demonstrates the potential to facilitate novel image-based screening approaches for molecular biomarkers exhibiting spatial heterogeneity, thereby creating promising opportunities for new clinical applications and use of DL tools to aid identify subtle biological patterns.

Chen et al. [24] applied ST to AD mouse model brain sections, identifying gene clusters specifically regulated by amyloid plaque depositions, possibly not detectable in bulk or dissociated single-cell analyses. Their findings could implicate adjustments to evaluating drugs and antibodies that target amyloid plaques. Such preclinical work shows how ST incorporation can improve mechanistic and therapeutic hypothesis generation.

Cross et al. [219] applied ST to lung tissue from COVID-19 patients at the point of death and identified spatially structured immune circuits, namely a coordinated immune-cell signaling network in severely affected tissue, driven by interactions between cytotoxic lymphocytes and pro-inflammatory macrophages. With their network-based analysis and mechanistic insights, the authors showed that combination therapies, drugs directly targeting the virus and those tempering the dysregulated host immune responses, could be better at handling heterogeneity of the tissue pathology. The work illustrated how ST use can help mechanistic characterization of the severe disease.

Abovementioned studies demonstrate the translational potential of ST but progress toward clinical use would require prospective cohorts, standardized (FFPE) workflows, predefined endpoints, external validation, and demonstration of value over conventional pathology to alter diagnosis, prognosis, or treatment selection.

Role of AI in ST

AI plays a multifaceted role in ST by transforming raw, high-dimensional data into actionable biological and clinical insights. AI automates important preprocessing tasks, such as tissue segmentation and spot-level deconvolution. For tissue segmentation, DL models such as U-Net variants or StarDist are used to delineate cellular boundaries from H&E stains. In terms of spot-level deconvolution, methods such as Tangram, Spatial-ID, and Cell2location help map scRNA-seq references to larger ST spots to infer cell-type proportions [234]. In addition to data preprocessing, tools such as SpaGCN and STAGATE use GNNs to identify spatial domains. They represent different regions of the tissue as nodes in a graph, enabling the modeling of complex, non-Euclidean relationships, allowing the identification of functionally distinct areas, such as tumor cores, invasive fronts, and immune niches [235]. Transformers and VAEs enhance SVG detection and trajectory inference by learning latent representations that emphasize genes with coordinated, location-dependent expression patterns. A key strength of AI-foundation models is their ability to integrate across different modalities. For example, pre-trained models like PLIP [236], BiomedCLIP [237], OpenAICLIP [238], and CONCH [239] effectively align ST with imaging and omics data [240].

AI can drive clinical translation by bridging spatial signatures to patient outcomes. Some studies have associated spatial expression patterns with prognosis, treatment response, or disease classification, indicating that AI-based analysis may support the development of spatial biomarkers and patient-stratification models. However, most models have been evaluated retrospectively and require external, prospective, and clinically relevant validation before they can support decision-making. AI transforms ST from simple descriptive profiling into predictive and interactive systems, making complex analyses easier to understand for both biologists and clinicians.

Collectively, these benchmarks demonstrate that no single computational method consistently outperforms all alternatives across datasets and analytical objectives. Graph-based models perform strongly for spatial-domain identification, whereas probabilistic and spatially regularized approaches are competitive for cell-type deconvolution. However, spatial-domain performance depends substantially on the experimental platform and tissue architecture, while deconvolution accuracy is strongly influenced by the completeness and compatibility of the single-cell reference. Method rankings may also vary with preprocessing, ground-truth definition, simulation design, evaluation metric, and computational scale.

Limitations of current AI methods

Although AI methods can extract complex spatial and multimodal patterns from ST datasets, the performance is still dependent on the tissue, platform, pre-processing workflows, and reference data. For instance, models trained predominantly on Visium or a restricted set of tissues may not transfer reliably to imaging-based, subcellular, 3D, or clinically archived datasets. So, geographical or chronological differences in tissue preparation, staining, gene panels, and imaging can therefore produce substantial performance degradation that is not apparent from within-dataset validation.

There are also task-specific failure modes. GNNs may oversmooth sharp biological boundaries or propagate segmentation and neighborhood-construction errors. Batch-correction and integration models may remove genuine biological variation. Deconvolution methods inherit biases from incomplete or mismatched scRNA-seq references and may fail to resolve rare, transitional, or disease-specific cell states.

Scalability and reproducibility remain additional concerns. Many methods are evaluated on relatively small, curated datasets and may require substantial memory, GPU resources, or platform-specific parameter tuning when applied to millions of cells or terabyte-scale images. Comparisons are further complicated by inconsistent pre-processing, metrics, ground truths, software versions, and train-test splits. Recent benchmarking work similarly indicates that optimal method performance is frequently dataset-dependent rather than universal [184, 185].

LLM-based systems introduce further risks, including hallucinated biological interpretations, prompt sensitivity, uncertain evidence provenance, and limited independent validation. They should therefore be used primarily for exploratory querying, annotation support, and hypothesis generation after conventional statistical analysis, rather than as standalone analytical or clinical decision systems. Reliable deployment will require externally validated benchmarks, uncertainty quantification, data-provenance safeguards, interpretable outputs, and prospective testing across platforms, institutions, and patient cohorts.

Role of large language models in ST

The AI tools described above (GNN-based or VAE-driven) share a common paradigm that they are trained discriminatively on spatial omics data to perform specific analytical tasks. LLMs represent a fundamentally different paradigm: they are generative, trained on vast text corpora, and are applied to ST not to replace existing tools but to provide a natural-language interface, feature encoding, and automated biological interpretation layered on top of statistical outputs. Therefore, LLMs are emerging as an interpretive and integrative layer above the analytical stack, not as a replacement for the ML and DL methods described above. These models simplify the interpretation of high-dimensional data and link gene expression patterns with biological processes [242]. LLMs can effectively translate complex outputs, such as spatially resolved gene modules, pathway enrichments, and cell–cell interaction scores, into coherent textual explanations that are easier for researchers and clinicians to interpret. Recent frameworks, such as QuST-LLM, demonstrate how LLMs can be integrated into established ST workflows. This integration provides bidirectional querying, summarizing the biology of selected tissue regions and retrieving regions that match user descriptions of microenvironments or processes [243]. In addition to text summarization, LLMs are being utilized as knowledge-infused feature encoders. Methods such as spaLLM create embeddings for genes and cells by incorporating textual knowledge (gene names, functions, and literature context) and merging this information with spatial neighborhood data to enhance spatial domain identification, particularly in multi-omics or sparse datasets [244]. LLMiniST presents a new zero-shot approach for identifying spatial niches. This method transforms ST data into context-aware prompts that include information about neighboring gene expression, cell type composition, tissue metadata, and external knowledge, outperforming traditional clustering methods and offering enhanced generalizability through a two-stage fine-tuning process [245].

TISSUENARRATOR represents tissue sections as spatial sentences, using ranked gene lists that are enhanced with coordinates and metadata. This allows pretrained language models to create realistic cellular profiles, predict interactions, conduct in silico perturbations, and facilitate natural-language queries across technologies such as MERFISH and CosMx [246]. PGL is another LLM-based framework that reinterprets single-cell transcriptomes as a language generation problem. It uses LLMs to create comprehensive scRNA-seq profiles from metadata, such as tissue type and disease context. The generated cell profiles serve as effective references for ST, allowing for accurate cell-type mapping without the need for matched single-cell atlases. PGL facilitates the construction of virtual cohorts, aids in hypothesis generation, and supports on-demand reference analysis, positioning generative language models as essential tools for in silico single-cell biology [247]. spEMO is a computational framework that integrates embeddings from pathology foundation models and LLMs to analyze spatial multi-omics data. It surpasses single-modality models in various downstream tasks, including spatial domain identification, spot-type classification, disease prediction, multicellular interaction inference, and automated medical reporting. Additionally, spEMO has introduced a novel multi-modal alignment benchmark to assess how effectively pathology foundation models retrieve complementary information [248].

Recent reviews of AI in transcriptomics highlight that LLM-derived embeddings can reduce the requirement for handcrafted feature engineering and facilitate the integration of various spatial modalities, such as RNA, proteins, and morphology [249]. Multi-modal foundation models further enhance research by integrating gene expression, histology images, and textual annotations into shared representations. This enables LLMs as interactive systems where researchers can pose natural-language questions about spatial patterns and receive responses that include both spatial selections and textual explanations [248].

While LLMs offer promising interpretive capabilities in ST, several critical limitations temper their widespread adoption. First, most LLM-based tools (QuST-LLM, spaLLM, LLMiniST, and TISSUENARRATOR) have been benchmarked on a small number of datasets, often only MERFISH or CosMx data, limiting generalizability across platforms and tissue types. Independent validation on prospective or clinical cohorts is absent. Second, LLMs are prone to hallucination, generating biologically plausible but factually unsupported interpretations of gene modules or pathway enrichments without providing traceable evidence back to the underlying data. Third, the integration of LLMs with existing statistical pipelines (normalization, deconvolution, and clustering) is still largely ad hoc; no standardized API or benchmarking framework exists to evaluate LLM-ST tool performance against ground truth. Fourth, the computational and financial costs of running large-scale LLMs on full spatial datasets are non-trivial, creating accessibility barriers for many research groups. Finally, most current LLMs are general-purpose and have not been trained on ST-specific biological literature or curated spatial atlases, meaning domain-specific performance remains inferior to dedicated statistical or DL models for core tasks. Until prospective validation studies and standardized benchmarks are established, LLM tools in ST are best viewed as hypothesis-generating assistants rather than reliable analytical engines.

Challenges and limitations of ST

Despite rapid technological advancements, ST faces significant technical, computational, analytical, and translational limitations. One of the key challenges in ST is the technical limitations of the current platforms and their trade-offs between resolution and coverage. For instance, conventional 10× Visium provides whole-transcriptome profiling at 55 μm spot resolution, with each spot potentially containing transcripts from multiple cells. Visium HD increases the density of spatial barcodes to 2 μm, although gene-expression values are commonly aggregated into larger bins to improve sensitivity and interpretability. In contrast, imaging-based platforms such as CosMx, Xenium, and MERFISH provide single-cell or subcellular localization but generally measure predefined gene panels rather than whole transcriptome. High-resolution sequencing platforms such as Stereo-seq and Seq-Scope provide broad gene coverage at fine spatial granularity, but their data can be sparse and computationally demanding. Thus far, no single platform has managed to provide unbiased whole-transcriptome data at true single-cell resolution across large tissue areas [115, 216, 250, 251]. Computational approaches have been proposed to address the persistent resolution-coverage trade-off in ST. For example, whole-transcriptome profiles may be inferred from targeted imaging panels using paired scRNA-seq references. However, these imputation strategies introduce additional uncertainty and have not yet been validated for routine clinical applications [115, 250].

Additionally, most of the platforms exhibit low capture efficiency, particularly for low-abundance mRNAs, non-coding RNAs, and nuclear transcripts. This low efficiency can lead to dropout bias and reduced sensitivity in the data. Sample preparation also poses further challenges. Fresh-frozen samples yield the best results; however, FFPE clinical samples are susceptible to RNA degradation and crosslinking artifacts, which can compromise data quality. Thick or fibrous tissues such as those found in brain, muscle, or plants, present challenges for uniform sectioning and permeabilization [252–254].

Another major challenge is data processing and computational limitations. ST datasets produce large volumes of high-dimensional data, often reaching gigabytes-to-terabytes per tissue sample. Such data include raw images, sequencing reads, and spatial expression matrices, demanding storage systems, GPU clusters, and fast processors. Additionally, these datasets require specialized compression algorithms, as standard transcriptomics tools are not effective at managing spatial coordinates. Batch effects from different platforms, tissues, or labs complicate integration, and the lack of standardization hinders benchmarking and reproducibility [255]. A fundamental unresolved challenge is the absence of a universal data standard for ST. Formats differ across platforms (e.g. Visium’s HDF5 output, Xenium’s Parquet files, and MERSCOPE’s CSV-based transcripts), and metadata schemas are inconsistent, hindering FAIR data sharing and multi-cohort meta-analyses [96]. While initiatives such as the SpatialData framework and the SODB repository are working toward interoperability, widespread adoption remains limited. Compounding this, batch effects across ST platforms are more complex than in scRNA-seq because they involve spatial coordinate systems, image artifacts, and tissue-specific permeabilization efficiencies simultaneously. Benchmark studies have shown that the relative performance of batch correction tools varies drastically depending on tissue type and platform [121].

As ST moves to whole-organ and multi-section datasets, computational demands become prohibitive. Terabyte-scale datasets from Stereo-seq or Slide-seq are incompatible with standard single-machine workflows. Most existing tools were benchmarked on Visium datasets of ~3000–5000 spots; scaling to millions of cells per sample (e.g. Xenium HD) requires algorithmic redesign and GPU-accelerated implementations. Memory-efficient graph representations, approximate nearest-neighbor indices, and streaming data loaders are active research areas but not yet the default in most published tools.

Analytical challenges present additional difficulties, such as spot-level deconvolution using scRNA-seq reference results, which can lead to uncertain cell-type proportions in heterogeneous tissues. Emerging machine learning approaches have sought to address these analytical limitations by integrating STs with complementary data modalities. For instance, SpaIM uses scRNA-seq references to infer unmeasured spatial gene expression, whereas HGGEP predicts spatial gene expression directly from histopathology images using hypergraph neural networks and multi-scale morphological features [256, 257]. These methods have demonstrated improvements in gene-expression prediction accuracy and spatial domain characterization, suggesting that computational inference can partially compensate for current technical limitations of ST platforms. Nevertheless, their performance depends on training data quality, model assumptions, and biological context, raising concerns regarding generalizability, interpretability, and clinical applicability.

Furthermore, multi-omics integration encompassing techniques like spatial proteomics and epigenomics faces issues with mismatched resolutions, differing data types, and a lack of unified data formats, resulting in incompatible files. Additional obstacles include background noise correction from autofluorescence, handling variable resolutions, and scalable spatial domain detection without standardized benchmarks. When it comes to clinical translation, there are regulatory, cost, and validation challenges, along with a limited number of standardized workflows for FFPE samples and prospective studies [250, 253, 254].

Future directions for ST

Looking ahead, the future of ST focuses on developing higher-resolution platforms, enhancing 3D reconstruction, and integrating multi-omics data. These advancements would aim to capture subcellular details and dynamic processes across samples from diverse individuals and conditions. AI holds immense potential to overcome critical challenges in ST, ranging from data processing to biological discovery [234]. Future developments will utilize DL models, including GNNs and variational autoencoders, to automate tasks such as segmentation and deconvolution. Future unified frameworks may use end-to-end multi-task learning with shared multimodal encoders to improve consistency across segmentation, representation learning, domain identification, and biological interpretation [33]. Foundation-model pretraining on large and diverse cross-platform cohorts may improve transfer to new tissues and technologies, while uncertainty quantification, spatial attention maps, interpretable latent factors, and counterfactual analyses could make model outputs more transparent. These developments should be evaluated through independent and prospective studies rather than assumed to confer zero-shot generalizability or clinical readiness [258]. These models will improve accuracy in analyzing complex tissues, enable scaling to terabyte-sized datasets, and facilitate the integration of multi-omics data, such as histology images, to create more streamlined analysis pipelines [249, 259].

AI-driven methods will reveal hidden spatial patterns, cell–cell interactions, and trajectory inference directly from raw ST data. This will support real-time mapping of TME and developmental processes, along with predictive models that forecast disease progression based on spatial gene gradients [260, 261]. AI enables more automated, robust, and interpretable pipelines that are needed for clinical deployment. AI-based models can segment cells, classify tissue regions, and associate spatial signatures with clinical outcomes or treatment responses, hence transforming complex ST data into clinically relevant scores and risk groups [234, 259]. AI is anticipated to transform raw, noisy ST data into standardized, interpretable, and clinically actionable formats, significantly reducing many technical and analytical constraints [262]. Explainable AI frameworks will enable personalized therapies through in silico screening, automate biomarker discovery, and address challenges related to reproducibility, regulatory compliance, and interpretability, ultimately bridging ST from being a specialized research tool to a resource for personalized diagnostics and decision-support applications.
