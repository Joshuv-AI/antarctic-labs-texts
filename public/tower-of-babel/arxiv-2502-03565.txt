Generalized Radial Uncertainty Product for d-Dimensional Hydrogen Atom
Avoy Jana

Abstract
Abstract This paper presents a comprehensive analysis of the generalized radial uncertainty product for the d-dimensional non-relativistic Hydrogen atom in position space. Utilizing the framework of quantum mechanics in d-dimensional spherical coordinates, the study extends the standard radial uncertainty relation to higher dimensions. Taking the solution of the radial Schrödinger equation, the normalized radial wave functions, expectation values, and uncertainties in both position and momentum space are rigorously evaluated. The analytical derivations reveal the dependence of the uncertainty product on the principal and angular quantum numbers, as well as the dimensional parameter d. The results provide deeper insight into the role of dimensionality in quantum uncertainty relations and their implications for higher-dimensional quantum systems. Keywords Radial momentum operator, relative dispersion, Laplace–Beltrami operator, d-dimensional H-atom, general effective potential, Hellmann-Feynman theorem, virial theorem



I
Introduction

We have searched (Ref.
jana2025
) the radial uncertainty product
\Delta\hat{r}\Delta\hat{p}_{r}
for some non-relativistic spherically symmetric potentials including Hydrogen atom. In three dimensional system, it’s not a problem when we are evaluating the squared radial momentum expectation value using the total energy formula. Meanwhile the connection (Ref.
Paz2001
) between the Hamiltonian and the radial momentum operator,

\hat{H}=\frac{\hat{p}_{r}^{2}}{2\mu}+\frac{\hat{L}^{2}}{2\mu r^{2}}

is valid only in one or three spatial dimensions. Then for an arbitrary dimensional Hydrogen atom, we have to sure about the true connection between them. In many literature, the uncertainty relation is shown as the product of the expectation values of the squared position operator,
\langle\hat{r}^{2}\rangle
(Ref.
bracher2011
) and the squared total momentum operator,
\langle\hat{p}^{2}\rangle
, or their uncertainties,
\Delta\hat{r}
and
\Delta\hat{p}
(Ref.
khelashvili2022
) respectively, is expressed as an inequality in two and three dimensions, and even in multidimensional spaces (Refs.
aljaber2016
;
Dehesa_2021
;
dehesa2021
), where
p
represents the total momentum of the system and
r
denotes the radial position. We will see for d-dimensional case, there will be an extra constant term in
\hat{p}_{r}
does not affect
[\hat{r},\hat{p}_{r}]=i\hbar
, so we can make the radial uncertainty principle,
\Delta\hat{r}\Delta\hat{p}_{r}\geq\frac{\hbar}{2}
for any arbitrary dimensional position space as in three dimension as well.

II
Formulation for Radial uncertainty product

The time-independent Schrodinger equation of a quantum particle of mass
\mu
in d-dimensional spherical (hyper-spherical) coordinate (
r,\theta_{1},\theta_{2},...\theta_{d-2},\phi
) is given by

-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\mu}\nabla^{2}\psi+V(r)\psi=E\psi

where
\psi=\psi(r,\theta_{1},\theta_{2},...\theta_{d-2},\phi)\equiv\psi(r,\Omega)
.
As we are interested with d-dimensional Hydrogen atom, then obviously
V(r)
is the hyper-spherically symmetric potential and the Laplacian in hyper-spherical coordinate (Ref.
smirnov2019
) is given by

\nabla^{2}=\frac{1}{r^{d-1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial{r}}\left(r^{d-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial{r}}\right)+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\nabla^{2}_{S^{d-1}}

where
\nabla^{2}_{S^{d-1}}
is the Laplace–Beltrami operator (hyper-spherical Laplacian) on the
(d-1)
sphere with unit radius. A simple derivation of it is attached in Appendix A. The symbol for hyper-spherical Laplacian, we have used
\nabla^{2}
here, while
\Delta
is used by a wide range of authors. The Laplace–Beltrami operator on the sphere is directly related to the angular momentum squared operator,
\hat{L}^{2}
(Ref.
trinhammer2012
) by

\hat{L}^{2}=-\hbar^{2}\nabla^{2}_{S^{d-1}}

The compact form for radial derivative in the Laplacian can be rewritten equivalent in terms of two radial derivatives as

\frac{1}{r^{d-1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial{r}}\left(r^{d-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial{r}}\right)=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}+\frac{d-1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}

We can use a shorthand operator for this radial operator for our convenience, is given by

\nabla_{r}^{2}=\frac{1}{r^{d-1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial{r}}\left(r^{d-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial{r}}\right)

(1)

Now the Laplacian takes form of

\nabla^{2}=\nabla_{r}^{2}-\frac{\hat{L}^{2}}{r^{2}\hbar^{2}}

(2)

and then the Hamiltonian can be expressed as

\hat{H}=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\mu}\nabla_{r}^{2}+\frac{\hat{L}^{2}}{2\mu r^{2}}+V(r)

(3)

Now we want to extract the radial Schrodinger equation for our purpose of radial wave function leads to radial uncertainty product. We know for spherically symmetric potential the solution can be written as

\psi(r,\Omega)=R(r)\times Y(\Omega)

where
R(r)
is the radial wave function and
Y(\Omega)
is hyper-spherical harmonics. Using this fact, the Schrodinger equation can be rewritten as

-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\mu}\frac{1}{R(r)}\nabla_{r}^{2}R(r)+\frac{1}{2\mu}\frac{1}{Y(\Omega)}\hat{L}^{2}Y(\Omega)+V(r)=E

Applying the method of separation of variables, the revised form of the above equation will be

\frac{1}{R(r)}\nabla_{r}^{2}R(r)-\frac{2\mu}{\hbar^{2}}r^{2}V(r)+\frac{2\mu E}{\hbar^{2}}r^{2}=\frac{1}{\hbar^{2}}\frac{1}{Y(\Omega)}\hat{L}^{2}Y(\Omega)=\alpha^{2}

where
\alpha^{2}
is the separation constant and two separated equations are given by

\left[-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\mu}\nabla_{r}^{2}+V(r)+\frac{\alpha^{2}\hbar^{2}}{2\mu r^{2}}\right]R(r)=E\,R(r)

(4)

\hat{L}^{2}Y(\Omega)=\alpha^{2}\hbar^{2}Y(\Omega)

(5)

Clearly Eq. (4) is the radial Schrodinger equation but with unknown constant
\alpha^{2}
and it can be determined from Eq. (5). The hyper-spherical harmonics are eigenfunctions
of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
S^{d-1}
with unit radius which satisfy the
eigenvalue equation (Ref.
cohl2012
) is given by

\nabla^{2}_{S^{d-1}}Y^{K}_{\ell}(\Omega)=-\ell(\ell+d-2)Y^{K}_{\ell}(\Omega)

where
K
stands for the remaining
(d-2)
quantum numbers, are often called as generalized azimuthal quantum numbers and they are among the
d
quantum numbers due to the
d
-dimensional space. Those two are
n
and
\ell
where
n
is the principal quantum number (
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
),
\ell
(often used as
\ell_{1}
in many literature) is the azimuthal quantum number (
\ell\in\{0,1,...,n-1\}
), is defined on
(d-1)
-dimensional subspace. Now using the relation
\hat{L}^{2}=-\hbar^{2}\nabla^{2}_{S^{d-1}}
, we have the eigenvalue and the eigenvalue equation of
\hat{L}^{2}
is given by

\hat{L}^{2}Y^{K}_{\ell}(\Omega)=\ell(\ell+d-2)\hbar^{2}Y^{K}_{\ell}(\Omega)

(6)

This equation gives us
\alpha^{2}=\ell(\ell+d-2)
and which can be proved easily using the homogeneity and harmonicity characteristics of the hyper-spherical harmonics. The hyper-spherical harmonics are the restrictions of the homogeneous harmonic polynomials
\mathbb{P}(\textbf{x})
of degree
\ell
to the unit sphere
S^{d-1}
. We know that a harmonic polynomial satisfies the Laplace equation
\nabla^{2}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{P}(\textbf{x})=0
where

\nabla^{2}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\equiv\nabla^{2}=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}+\frac{d-1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}-\frac{\hat{L}^{2}}{r^{2}\hbar^{2}}

A homogeneous polynomial
\mathbb{P}(\textbf{x})
of degree
\ell
by definition, it satisfies
\mathbb{P}(\lambda\textbf{x})=\lambda^{\ell}\mathbb{P}(\textbf{x})
for
\lambda
is a scalar. Such a polynomial in hyper-spherical coordinate system can be written as
\mathbb{P}(r,\Omega)=r^{\ell}Y(\Omega)
. This separation reflects the radial scaling symmetry of the homogeneous polynomial. The Laplace equation upon it

\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}+\frac{d-1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}-\frac{\hat{L}^{2}}{r^{2}\hbar^{2}}\right)(r^{\ell}Y(\Omega))=0

gives the Eq. (6). The Eq. (4) can be written as

\left[\frac{\hat{p}_{r}^{2}}{2\mu}+V_{\text{eff}}(r)\right]R(r)=E\,R(r)

(7)

where
\hat{p}_{r}
is the radial momentum operator and
V_{\text{eff}}(r)
is the effective potential. Clearly, we can derive
V_{\text{eff}}(r)
formula if we can establish an relation between
\hat{p}_{r}^{2}
and
\nabla^{2}_{r}
. That’s okay, then what will be the radial momentum operator?
In classical mechanics, the radial momentum is defined by,

{p}_{rc}=\frac{1}{r}\vec{r}.\vec{p}

where
\vec{r}
and
\vec{p}
are radial vector and momentum vector respectively.
But in quantum mechanics, this definition becomes ambiguous, since the components of
\hat{r}
and
\hat{p}
don’t commute. Therefore,
\hat{p}_{r}=\frac{1}{r}\hat{r}.\hat{p}
is not Hermitian results it can’t be an observable. To make
\hat{p}_{r}
a Hermitian operator, we need to define newly a symmetric operator, is given by

\hat{p}_{r}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{r}(\hat{r}.\hat{p}+\hat{p}.\hat{r})

where
\hat{r}=r\hat{e}_{r}
and
\hat{p}
is momentum operator, is defined by
\hat{p}=-i\hbar\vec{\nabla}
.
Then the radial momentum operator (Refs.
jana2025
;
Paz2001
) is defined as

\hat{p}_{r}R(r)=\frac{1}{2}(-i\hbar)[\hat{e}_{r}.\vec{\nabla}R(r)+\vec{\nabla}.(\hat{e}_{r}R(r))]

(8)

for an arbitrary radial wave function
R(r)
. For any dimensional spherically coordinate system, the divergence of the system must contain
\hat{e_{r}}\frac{\partial}{\partial{r}}
, which gives the first term of Eq. (8) as

\hat{e}_{r}.\vec{\nabla}R(r)=\frac{\partial R(r)}{\partial r}

The evaluation of the second term in Eq. (8) is bread and butter in two- and three-dimensional spherical coordinate systems, as we have explicit formulas for their divergence. However, for a
d-dimensional spherical coordinate system, it becomes quite tough to go through the concept of explicit formula of the divergence. To handle this, we apply a different approach. The Gauss divergence theorem for any vector field,
\vec{A}
is given by

\int_{V}\vec{\nabla}.\vec{A}\,dV=\oint_{S}\vec{A}.d\vec{S}

(9)

where in d-dimensional spherical coordinate system, the volume and surface elements are given by

dV=r^{d-1}drd\Omega\text{ and }d\vec{S}=\hat{e}_{r}R^{d-1}d\Omega

Then

\int_{V}\vec{\nabla}.\vec{A}\,dV=\int_{0}^{R}\int_{\Omega}\vec{\nabla}.\vec{A}\,r^{d-1}drd\Omega

Taking a purely radial vector field,
\vec{A}=A_{r}\hat{r}
,

\displaystyle\oint_{S}\vec{A}.d\vec{S}=\int_{\Omega}A_{r=R}\hat{e}_{r}.\hat{e}_{r}R^{d-1}d\Omega

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{R}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^{d-1}A_{r}\right)dr\int_{\Omega}d\Omega=\int_{0}^{R}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^{d-1}A_{r}\right)drd\Omega

From Eq. (9),

\vec{\nabla}.\vec{A}=\frac{1}{r^{d-1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^{d-1}A_{r}\right)

Now

\vec{\nabla}.(\hat{e}_{r}R(r))=\frac{1}{r^{d-1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^{d-1}R(r)\right)=\frac{d-1}{r}R(r)+\frac{\partial R(r)}{\partial r}

It’s quite easy to evaluate
\vec{\nabla}.(\hat{e}_{r}R(r))
, if we work with d-dimensional Euclidean space rather than spherical (Ref.
Paz2001
). Using the vector identity

\vec{\nabla}.(\hat{e}_{r}R(r))=(\hat{e}_{r}.\vec{\nabla})R(r)+R(r)\vec{\nabla}.\hat{e}_{r}

and
r^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{2}
,
\hat{e}_{r}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}\hat{e}_{i}
, i.e.,
\vec{\nabla}.\hat{e}_{r}=\frac{d-1}{r}
, we have exactly same result.
Finally from Eq. (8), we can write the radial momentum operator formula in d-dimensional spherical coordinate system, is given by

\hat{p}_{r}=-i\hbar\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial r}+\frac{d-1}{2r}\right)

(10)

Quantum mechanically squaring this operator, we have the squared radial momentum operator, is given by

\hat{p}^{2}_{r}=-\hbar^{2}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}+\frac{d-1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}+\frac{(d-1)(d-3)}{4r^{2}}\right)

(11)

This operator is very important in the context of radial uncertainty product. Now we have reached to our main goal, establishing an relation between
\hat{p}_{r}^{2}
and
\nabla^{2}_{r}
, which is given by

\hat{p}_{r}^{2}=-\hbar^{2}\left(\nabla_{r}^{2}+\frac{(d-1)(d-3)}{4r^{2}}\right)

(12)

using this relation, we can rewrite the Eq. (4) as

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\hat{p}_{r}^{2}}{2\mu}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\mu}\frac{(d-1)(d-3)}{4r^{2}}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\mu}\frac{\ell(\ell+d-2)}{r^{2}}+V(r)\right]R(r)

(13)

\displaystyle=E\,R(r)

and comparing it with Eq. (7), we have the effective potential formula (Ref.
aljaber1998
) is given by

V_{\text{eff}}(r)=V(r)+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\mu}\frac{\ell(\ell+d-2)}{r^{2}}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\mu}\frac{(d-1)(d-3)}{4r^{2}}

(14)

The probability density function for d-dimensional position space (Refs.
aljaber1998
) is given by

P(r)=r^{d-1}|R(r)|^{2}

(15)

The normalization condition is given by

\int_{0}^{\infty}P(r)dr=1\implies\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{d-1}|R(r)|^{2}dr=1

The expectation value of an operator
\hat{A}
in position space is given by

\langle\hat{A}\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{d-1}R^{*}(r)\hat{A}R(r)dr

Now the expectation values of
\hat{r}
,
\hat{r}^{2}
are given by

\langle\hat{r}\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{d-1}R^{*}(r)\hat{r}R(r)dr=\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{d}|R(r)|^{2}dr

(16)

\langle\hat{r}^{2}\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{d-1}R^{*}(r^{2})\hat{r}^{2}R(r)dr=\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{d+1}|R(r)|^{2}dr

(17)

and the expectation values of
\hat{p}_{r}
and
\hat{p}^{2}_{r}
are given by

\langle\hat{p}_{r}\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{2}R^{*}(r)(-i\hbar)\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial{r}}+\frac{d-1}{2r}\right)R(r)dr

(18)

\displaystyle\langle\hat{p}_{r}^{2}\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{2}R^{*}(r)(-\hbar^{2})\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{r^{2}}}+\frac{d-1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial{r}}+\frac{(d-1)(d-3)}{4r^{2}}\right)

(19)

\displaystyle R(r)dr

By using Eq. (19), to find
\langle\hat{p}_{r}^{2}\rangle
is a little bit lengthy, while there is a fantastic way to find
\langle\hat{p}^{2}_{r}\rangle
using the concept of the total energy of the system and the effective potential. From Eq. (3) and using Eq. (12), we can write the Hamiltonian as

\hat{H}=\frac{\hat{p}_{r}^{2}}{2\mu}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\mu}\frac{(d-1)(d-3)}{4r^{2}}+\frac{\hat{L}^{2}}{2\mu r^{2}}+V(r)

(20)

The effective potential operator can defined using
\hat{L}^{2}
instead of
\ell(\ell+d-2)\hbar^{2}
in Eq. (14), which is given by

\hat{V}_{\text{eff}}(r)=V(r)+\frac{\hat{L}^{2}}{2\mu r^{2}}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\mu}\frac{(d-1)(d-3)}{4r^{2}}

(21)

Then the Hamiltonian can be rewritten through the effective potential operator.

\hat{H}=\frac{\hat{p}_{r}^{2}}{2\mu}+\hat{V}_{\text{eff}}(r)

(22)

\langle\hat{p}_{r}^{2}\rangle=2\mu(\langle\hat{H}\rangle-\langle\hat{V}_{\text{eff}}(r)\rangle)=2\mu(E-\langle\hat{V}_{\text{eff}}(r)\rangle)

(23)

where
E
is the energy eigenvalue of the system and the expectation value of the effective potential is given by

\displaystyle\langle\hat{V}_{\text{eff}}(r)\rangle=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\mu}\left(\frac{(d-1)(d-3)}{4}+\ell(\ell+d-2)\right)\langle\frac{1}{r^{2}}\rangle

(24)

\displaystyle+\langle V(r)\rangle

By the definition of uncertainty, the uncertainties of radial position and radial momentum are given by

\displaystyle\Delta\hat{r}=\sqrt{\langle\hat{r}^{2}\rangle-\langle\hat{r}\rangle^{2}}

(25)

\displaystyle\Delta\hat{p}_{r}=\sqrt{\langle\hat{p}^{2}_{r}\rangle-\langle\hat{p}_{r}\rangle^{2}}

In a compact form, the radial momentum operator can represented as

\hat{p}_{r}=-i\hbar\left(\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial{r}}[r]-\frac{d-3}{2}\right)

Lets check whether
\hat{r}
and
\hat{p_{r}}
commute or not.
The commutator of
\hat{r}
and
\hat{p_{r}}
is given by
[\hat{r},\hat{p_{r}}]=(\hat{r}\hat{p_{r}}-\hat{p_{r}}\hat{r})
and considering
\hat{p}_{r}=-i\hbar\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial{r}}[r]
upon any arbitrary radial wave function
R(r)
,

[\hat{r},\hat{p_{r}}]R(r)=(-i\hbar)\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial{r}}[rR(r)]-\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial{r}}[r^{2}R(r)]\right]=i\hbar R(r)

For the extra constant term in
\hat{p}_{r}
does not affect
[\hat{r},\hat{p}_{r}]=i\hbar
, so we can make the radial uncertainty principle,
\Delta\hat{r}\Delta\hat{p}_{r}\geq\frac{\hbar}{2}
for any arbitrary dimensional position space. The one dimensional reflection is nothing but
\Delta\hat{x}\Delta\hat{p}_{x}\geq\frac{\hbar}{2}
corresponding to
{[\hat{x},\hat{p}_{x}]=i\hbar}
.

III
d-Dimensional Non-relativistic Hydro-genic Atom

III.1
Radial wave function

The time-independent Schrodinger equation for the hydro-genic system is given by

-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\mu}\nabla^{2}\psi(r,\Omega)-\frac{Zke^{2}}{r}\psi(r,\Omega)=E\psi(r,\Omega)

(26)

where
Z
,
\mu
is the atomic number and reduced mass of one electron system,
k=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}
and
e
is the electronic charge. For
V(r)=-\frac{Zke^{2}}{r}
, the effective potential can be written as

V_{\text{eff}}(r)=-\frac{Zke^{2}}{r}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\mu}\frac{\ell(\ell+d-2)}{r^{2}}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\mu}\frac{(d-1)(d-3)}{4r^{2}}

(27)

The radial Schrodinger equation can be written as

\displaystyle\left[-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\mu}\left(\frac{1}{r^{d-1}}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^{d-1}\frac{d}{dr}\right)\right)-\frac{Zke^{2}}{r}+\frac{\ell(\ell+d-2)\hbar^{2}}{2\mu r^{2}}\right]

(28)

\displaystyle R(r)=ER(r)

The solution (Ref.
aljaber1998
) of it is given by

\displaystyle R_{n_{r}\ell}(r)=N_{n_{r}\ell}e^{-{\frac{Zr}{\left(n_{r}+\ell+\frac{d-1}{2}\right)a_{0}}}}

(29)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2Z}{\left(n_{r}+\ell+\frac{d-1}{2}\right)a_{0}}r\right)^{\ell}L^{2\ell+d-2}_{n_{r}}\left({\frac{2Z}{\left(n_{r}+\ell+\frac{d-1}{2}\right)a_{0}}r}\right)

where
n_{r}
is radial quantum number, which is specifically used for the radial solution of the Schrodinger equation and
a_{0}
is the Bohr radius.
The principal quantum number,
\nu
(
n^{\prime}
in Ref.
aljaber1998
) for d-dimensional hydro-genic system can be defined by

\nu=n_{r}+\ell+\frac{d-1}{2}

We have the principal quantum number,
n
(Refs.
BransdenJoachain1989
;
aljaber1998
;
jana2025
) of usual three dimensional hydro-genic system is defined through

n=n_{r}+\ell+1

The fact is that the principal quantum number for d-dimensional quantum system,
\nu
can take non-integer value for even dimensional cases. To avoid this, we will use the usual three dimensional quantum number
n
, which can take any positive integer value, they are related by

\nu=n+\frac{d-3}{2}

(30)

because of
d=3
, this relation reflects
\nu\equiv n
. Then the revised radial wave function (Refs.
aljaber1998
) from Eq. (29) can be written as,

\displaystyle R_{n\ell}(r)=N_{n\ell}\,e^{-{\frac{Zr}{\left(n+\frac{d-3}{2}\right)a_{0}}}}\left(\frac{2Z}{\left(n+\frac{d-3}{2}\right)a_{0}}r\right)^{\ell}

(31)

\displaystyle L^{2\ell+d-2}_{n-\ell-1}\left({\frac{2Z}{\left(n+\frac{d-3}{2}\right)a_{0}}r}\right)

where
N_{n\ell}
is the normalization constant, it can be calculated by normalizing condition. Here
L
stands for associated Laguerre function (Generalized Laguerre function)(Ref.
AbramowitzStegun1972
).
In the context of this paper, the three properties of the associated Laguerre function, have an important role, are given by

1. Orthonormal property

\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho^{a}e^{-\rho}L^{a}_{b}(\rho)L^{a}_{c}(\rho)d\rho=\frac{\Gamma{(a+b+1)}}{\Gamma{(b+1)}}\delta_{bc}

(32)

Many authors like Griffith’s (Ref.
Griffiths2018
) uses the orthogonality property as

\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho^{a}e^{-\rho}L^{a}_{b}(\rho)L^{a}_{c}(\rho)d\rho=\frac{\Gamma{(a+b+1)}}{[\Gamma{(b+1)}]^{3}}\delta_{bc}

while this will not affect in our calculation (Ref.
jana2025
).

2. Recursive property

\displaystyle\rho L^{a}_{b}(\rho)=(a+2b+1)L^{a}_{b}(\rho)

(33)

\displaystyle-\frac{b+1}{a+b+1}L^{a}_{b+1}(\rho)-(a+b)^{2}L^{a}_{b-1}(\rho)

3. Derivative property

\frac{d}{d\rho}L^{a}_{b}(\rho)=L^{a+1}_{b}(\rho)=\frac{1}{\rho}[bL^{a}_{b}(\rho)-(b+a)L^{a}_{b-1}(\rho)]

(34)

Let’s introduce a dimensionless parameter
\rho
as

\rho=\frac{2Z}{\left(n+\frac{d-3}{2}\right)a_{0}}r

(35)

With this substitution, the radial wave function takes the form

R_{n\ell}(\rho)=N_{n\ell}e^{-\frac{\rho}{2}}{\rho}^{\ell}L^{2\ell+d-2}_{n-\ell-1}(\rho)

(36)

To ensure proper normalization, we redefine the probability density as

P(r)=\left({\frac{\left(n+\frac{d-3}{2}\right)a_{0}}{2Z}}\right)^{d-1}{\rho}^{d-1}|R_{n\ell}(\rho)|^{2}

(37)

Since the radial coordinate is transformed as in Eq. (35), the differential element transforms accordingly

dr=\frac{\left(n+\frac{d-3}{2}\right)a_{0}}{2Z}d\rho

(38)

This transformation allows us to express the normalization condition in terms of the dimensionless variable.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}P(r)dr

(39)

\displaystyle=\left({\frac{\left(n+\frac{d-3}{2}\right)a_{0}}{2Z}}\right)^{d}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rho}^{d-1}|R_{n\ell}(\rho)|^{2}d\rho

\displaystyle=\left({\frac{\left(n+\frac{d-3}{2}\right)a_{0}}{2Z}}\right)^{d}|N_{n\ell}|^{2}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rho}\,e^{-\rho}{\rho}^{2\ell+d-2}\left[L^{2\ell+d-2}_{n-\ell-1}(\rho)\right]^{2}d\rho

We will use shorthand
I
for large integral expression in our calculations and for this integral,

I_{1}=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rho}\,e^{-\rho}{\rho}^{2\ell+d-2}\left[L^{2\ell+d-2}_{n-\ell-1}(\rho)\right]^{2}d\rho

(40)

Following similar trend, naming
2\ell+d-2=a
and
n-\ell-1=b
, the integral
I_{1}
becomes,

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho.e^{-\rho}\rho^{a}[L^{a}_{b}(\rho)]^{2}d\rho

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho^{a}e^{-\rho}\left[(a+2b+1)L^{a}_{b}(\rho)\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\frac{b+1}{a+b+1}L^{a}_{b+1}(\rho)-(a+b)^{2}L^{a}_{b-1}(\rho)\right]L^{a}_{b}(\rho)d\rho

\displaystyle=(a+2b+1)\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho^{a}e^{-\rho}[L^{a}_{b}(\rho)]^{2}d\rho\text{ (using Eq. (32) )}

\displaystyle=(a+2b+1)\frac{\Gamma{(a+b+1)}}{\Gamma{(b+1)}}

Using our nomenclature, we can recover
a+2b+1=2n+d-3
,
a+b+1=n+\ell+d-2
and
b+1=n-\ell
. This gives us finally,

I_{1}=(2n+d-3)\frac{\Gamma{(n+\ell+1)}}{\Gamma(n-\ell)}=(2n+d-3)\frac{(n+\ell)!}{(n-\ell-1)!}

Here we are able to use factorial instead of Gamma function because of

Appendix A
Hyper Spherical Coordinate System

The Laplacian operator in
d
-dimensional spherical coordinates (Ref.
campos2020
) can be derived starting from its general expression in curvilinear coordinates. In an arbitrary coordinate system
(q^{1},q^{2},\dots,q^{d})
, the Laplacian is given by

\nabla^{2}f=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{i}}\left(\frac{\sqrt{g}}{h_{i}^{2}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial q^{i}}\right),

where
g
is the determinant of the metric tensor and
h_{i}
are the scale factors associated with the coordinate system. In
d
-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, spherical coordinates are defined as

(q^{1},q^{2},\dots,q^{d})=(r,\theta_{1},\theta_{2},...,\theta_{d-2},\phi).

where
r
is the radial coordinate (ranging from
0
to
\infty
),
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{d-2}
are the polar angles (ranging from
0
to
\pi
), and
\phi
is the azimuthal angle (ranging from
0
to
2\pi
). The corresponding metric takes the form

ds^{2}=dr^{2}+r^{2}d\theta_{1}^{2}+r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{1}d\theta_{2}^{2}+\dots+r^{2}\prod_{k=1}^{d-2}\sin^{2}\theta_{k}d\phi^{2}

From this, the scale factors are given by
h_{r}=1
,
h_{\theta_{1}}=r
,
h_{\theta_{2}}=r\sin\theta_{1}
,
h_{\theta_{3}}=r\sin\theta_{1}\sin\theta_{2}
, and so on up to
h_{\phi}=r\prod_{k=1}^{d-2}\sin\theta_{k}
. The volume element is

\sqrt{g}=r^{d-1}\prod_{k=1}^{d-2}(\sin\theta_{k})^{d-k-1}.

Substituting
q^{1}=r
,
h_{r}=1
and
\sqrt{g}=r^{d-1}
into the general Laplacian expression, the radial term simplifies to

\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{1}}\left(\frac{\sqrt{g}}{h_{r}^{2}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial q^{1}}\right)=\frac{1}{r^{d-1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^{d-1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\right)

The angular part, involving the unit
(d-1)
-dimensional sphere, leads to

\nabla^{2}_{S^{d-1}}=\frac{1}{\sin^{d-2}\theta_{1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{1}}\left(\sin^{d-2}\theta_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{1}}\right)+\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\theta_{1}}\nabla^{2}_{S^{d-2}}

In similar trend, the angular part, expanding up to the last term for
\phi
, we obtain

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{S^{d-1}}=\frac{1}{\sin^{d-2}\theta_{1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{1}}\left(\sin^{d-2}\theta_{1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\theta_{1}}\frac{1}{\sin^{d-3}\theta_{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{2}}\left(\sin^{d-3}\theta_{2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle+\dots+\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\theta_{1}\sin^{2}\theta_{2}\dots\sin^{2}\theta_{d-3}}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial\phi^{2}}

Thus, the final form of the
d
-dimensional Laplacian in spherical coordinates is

\nabla^{2}f=\frac{1}{r^{d-1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^{d-1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\right)+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\nabla^{2}_{S^{d-1}}f

Appendix B
Hellmann-Feynman Theorem

The Hellmann-Feynman theorem (Ref.
esteve2010
) states that if
H(\lambda)
is a parameter-dependent Hamiltonian and
|\psi(\lambda)\rangle
is a normalized eigenstate of
H(\lambda)
with eigenvalue
E(\lambda)
, i.e.,
H(\lambda)|\psi(\lambda)\rangle=E(\lambda)|\psi(\lambda)\rangle
, then

\frac{dE}{d\lambda}=\left\langle\psi(\lambda)\left|\frac{dH}{d\lambda}\right|\psi(\lambda)\right\rangle.

A simple proof is attached here. Differentiating both sides of
H(\lambda)|\psi(\lambda)\rangle=E(\lambda)|\psi(\lambda)\rangle
w.r.t.
\lambda
, we get

\frac{dH}{d\lambda}|\psi(\lambda)\rangle+H(\lambda)\frac{d}{d\lambda}|\psi(\lambda)\rangle=\frac{dE}{d\lambda}|\psi(\lambda)\rangle+E(\lambda)\frac{d}{d\lambda}|\psi(\lambda)\rangle

Taking the inner product with
\langle\psi(\lambda)|
, we obtain

\displaystyle\langle\psi(\lambda)|\frac{dH}{d\lambda}|\psi(\lambda)\rangle+\langle\psi(\lambda)|H(\lambda)\frac{d}{d\lambda}|\psi(\lambda)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{dE}{d\lambda}\langle\psi(\lambda)|\psi(\lambda)\rangle+E(\lambda)\langle\psi(\lambda)|\frac{d}{d\lambda}|\psi(\lambda)\rangle

Since
|\psi(\lambda)\rangle
is normalized, using
\langle\psi(\lambda)|\psi(\lambda)\rangle=1
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{dE}{d\lambda}=\langle\psi(\lambda)|\frac{dH}{d\lambda}|\psi(\lambda)\rangle

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\langle\psi(\lambda)|H(\lambda)\frac{d}{d\lambda}|\psi(\lambda)\rangle-E(\lambda)\langle\psi(\lambda)|\frac{d}{d\lambda}|\psi(\lambda)\rangle}_{\text{Zero}}

The last two terms cancel because
H|\psi\rangle=E|\psi\rangle
. Thus, the Hellmann-Feynman theorem is proved. The expectation values of inverse and inverse squared radial position can be evaluated using this beautiful theorem, in a very simple way. Define the parameter
\lambda
as
\lambda=Zke^{2}
, the Hamiltonian of H-atom (Eq. (26)) and energy eigenvalues (Eq. (59)) can be written as

H(\lambda)=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\mu}\nabla^{2}-\frac{\lambda}{r}\text{ i.e. }\frac{dH(\lambda)}{d\lambda}=-\frac{1}{r}

E_{n}(\lambda)=-\frac{\mu}{2\hbar^{2}}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\left(n+\frac{d-3}{2}\right)^{2}}\text{ i.e. }\frac{E_{n}(\lambda)}{d\lambda}=-\frac{Z}{\left(n+\frac{d-3}{2}\right)^{2}a_{0}}

where
a_{0}=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{\mu ke^{2}}
is used and now using the Hellmann-Feynman theorem,

\langle\frac{1}{\hat{r}}\rangle=-\frac{dE_{n}(\lambda)}{d\lambda}=\frac{Z}{\left(n+\frac{d-3}{2}\right)^{2}a_{0}}

Define the parameter
\lambda
once again as
\lambda=\ell
, the Hamiltonian (Eq. (20)) is given by

\hat{H}=\frac{\hat{p}_{r}^{2}}{2\mu}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\mu}\frac{(d-1)(d-3)}{4r^{2}}+\frac{\ell(\ell+d-2)}{2\mu r^{2}}+V(r)

\frac{d\hat{H}}{d\ell}=\frac{2\ell+d-2}{2\mu}\frac{1}{r^{2}}

\frac{dE_{n}(\ell)}{d\ell}=\frac{dE_{n}(\ell)}{dn}\frac{dn}{d\ell}=\frac{Z^{2}\hbar^{2}}{\mu\left(n+\frac{d-3}{2}\right)^{3}a_{0}^{2}}\text{ using }\frac{dn}{d\ell}=1

Once again using the Hellmann-Feynman theorem,

\langle\frac{1}{\hat{r}^{2}}\rangle=\frac{2\mu}{2\ell+d-2}\frac{dE_{n}(\ell)}{d\ell}=\frac{2Z^{2}\hbar^{2}}{(2\ell+d-2)\left(n+\frac{d-3}{2}\right)^{3}a_{0}^{2}}

Even the Hellmann-Feynman theorem is so powerful, it can prove the Virial theorem in just two lines. Define the parameter
\lambda
as
\lambda=\mu
, the Hamiltonian and the energy eigenvalues (Eq. (59)) are given by

H(\mu)=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\mu}\nabla^{2}+V(r)\text{ i.e. }\frac{dH(\mu)}{d\mu}=-\frac{1}{\mu}(H-V)

E_{n}(\mu)=-\frac{\mu}{2\hbar^{2}}\frac{(Zke^{2})^{2}}{\left(n+\frac{d-3}{2}\right)^{2}}\text{ i.e. }\frac{dE_{n}(\mu)}{d\mu}=\frac{1}{\mu}E_{n}

The Hellmann-Feynman theorem directly says

-\langle(H-V)\rangle=E\text{ i.e. }-\langle T\rangle=E

and using
\langle H\rangle=E
, we achieve
E=\frac{1}{2}\langle V\rangle
and
-2\langle T\rangle=\langle V\rangle
, they are results of the Virial theorem for Hydrogen atom, independent of dimensionality.
