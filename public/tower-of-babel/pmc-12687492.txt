The association between white blood cell-to-hemoglobin ratio and mortality risk in critical traumatic brain injury: insights from MIMIC-IV retrospective analysis
Authors: Zhiyuan Xu, Shuaishuai Zhou, Xiangyang Tong, Xianliang Yan, Liang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe white blood cell-to-hemoglobin ratio (WHR) is a composite biomarker indicative of both systemic inflammation and anemia. However, its association with all-cause mortality in intensive care unit (ICU) patients with traumatic brain injury (TBI) is unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the relationship between WHR and all-cause mortality among critically ill TBI patients.MethodsClinical information was extracted from the MIMIC-IV database. The main endpoint was 30-day all-cause mortality. Additional outcomes included 90-day and 1-year mortality. WHR values were divided into quartiles. Kaplan–Meier survival curves assessed interquartile survival differences. Cox regression models and RCS techniques were used to assess the link between WHR and death risk. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses tested the consistency of results.ResultsThe study included 2635 patients, with a median age of 70 years and 62% being male. A significant correlation was observed between elevated WHR and increased mortality across 30-, 90-, and 365-day intervals (log-rank P < 0.05). Cox models confirmed a dose-dependent increase in mortality risk across WHR quartiles (all P < 0.05). The RCS model demonstrated a significant U-shaped pattern between WHR and mortality, indicating nonlinear effects (P for overall <0.001; P for nonlinear <0.05). Robustness of the results was reinforced through subgroup comparisons and sensitivity verification.ConclusionElevated WHR strongly correlated with mortality in critically ill TBI patients. In the ICU setting, WHR provides valuable prognostic information to guide individualized treatment strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03540-0.

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a major global public health concern. Among all neurological conditions, TBI has the highest incidence, with millions of new cases annually, leading to substantial long-term disability and considerable socioeconomic burden [1]. Patients with moderate-to-severe TBI face a 2.2-fold increased risk of mortality compared to the general population. According to the U.S. National Institute on Disability and Rehabilitation Research (NIDRR), this increased risk persists despite standardized rehabilitative interventions [2]. In the United States, TBI accounts for nearly 40% of injury-related deaths, with approximately 1.72 million cases annually and over 275, 000 requiring hospitalization [3]. Prognostic outcomes in TBI are influenced by multiple clinical factors, including patient age, injury severity, nutritional status, and complications such as nosocomial pneumonia [4]. Although several models have been developed to predict mortality, reliable and simple prognostic biomarkers for TBI are limited [5, 6].

Following TBI, hematoma formation triggers a neuroinflammatory cascade that exacerbates secondary brain damage and worsens clinical outcomes [7]. Early assessment of inflammatory responses is crucial for prognostic stratification. White blood cells (WBCs) are key components of the immune system. They comprise diverse subtypes, such as neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils, lymphocytes (B cells, T cells, NK cells), and monocytes (which become macrophages and dendritic cells in tissues). Collectively, these cells regulate systemic inflammatory homeostasis [8]. In patients with ICH, leukocyte infiltration increases markedly between 12 and 30 h after injury, compared to the earlier phase within 12 h [9]. Inflammation also contributes to anemia through mechanisms such as iron sequestration in macrophages and impaired intestinal iron absorption, ultimately disrupting systemic iron balance. Anemia frequently accompanies acute infections and cytokine-driven critical illnesses [10], with a prevalence of up to 72% reported in patients with ICH following TBI [11]. Previous studies have linked elevated WBC counts to new silent cerebral infarctions after ICH [12], and higher WBC levels have been indirectly associated with increased mortality and worse clinical outcomes, possibly reflecting larger hematoma volumes or greater disease severity [13].

Secondary brain injury after TBI is influenced by various mediators, including hypotension, hypoxemia, and anemia. Anemia, in particular, impairs cerebral oxygenation and aggravates ischemic vulnerability during the post-injury metabolic crisis, characterized by oxidative stress and impaired neurovascular function [14]. Animal models have confirmed that anemia reduces brain tissue oxygenation and worsens secondary damage following TBI [15]. Hemoglobin (Hb) concentration is the standard biomarker for diagnosing anemia. Approximately 50% of severely injured TBI individuals admitted to the ICU require RBC transfusions when hemoglobin drops below 9 g/dL [16, 17]. Anemia may also contribute to secondary injury development in acute TBI [18].

Integrating WBC and Hb levels may provide valuable prognostic information in TBI. The ratio of WBC to Hb (WHR) has emerged as a prognostic marker across various conditions, including heart failure (HF), gastric adenocarcinoma, and hepatocellular carcinoma [19–21]. Elevated neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) have been associated with psoriasis and its subtypes [22]. Recent cohort analysis demonstrated that poorly controlled diabetes mellitus exhibits positive correlations with systemic inflammatory biomarkers, specifically elevated NLR and monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), suggesting immune dysregulation in hyperglycemic states [23]. WHR has also been identified as an independent indicator of 30-day mortality in patients with pulmonary hypertension admitted to intensive care [24], and Liu et al. demonstrated that models incorporating WHR could accurately estimate the risk of acute kidney injury in ICU cases with ischemic stroke [25].

Despite these findings, the prognostic significance of WHR in TBI remains unknown. Previous studies have examined either WBC counts or anemia individually in TBI, but none have evaluated WHR directly. The purpose of this investigation was to explore how WHR correlates with all-cause mortality in TBI individuals treated in the ICU and to assess the prognostic utility of WHR in this population.

Methods

Data source

Data from the publicly accessible MIMIC-IV database (v3.1) [26] formed the basis of this retrospective cohort investigation. Data use was authorized following completion of the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Human Subjects Protection Training and certification through the CITI (ID: 14001717). Informed consent was waived due to the anonymized nature of the dataset.

Study design and population

Exclusion criteria included missing hematologic data within 24 h of ICU admission, ICU duration under 1 day due to early discharge or death, or incomplete outcome information. (The survival status of TBI patients on the 30-day, 90-day, and 365-day in the ICU was unknown.) Information retrieval and definitions

Information retrieval was performed using Navicat Premium (v17.0.8) and SQL scripts from the official MIMIC-IV GitHub repository (https://github.com/MIT-LCP/mimic-code). Adult patients admitted with primary diagnoses related to TBI—including concussion, cerebral contusion, traumatic intracranial hemorrhage, and cranial fractures—were identified using ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes (Supplementary Table 1). Comorbidities were retrospectively determined from diagnostic records. Variables collected included age, gender, body mass index (BMI), heart rate (HR), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), mean blood pressure (MBP), respiratory rate (RR), temperature, oxygen saturation (SpO2), and medical history of epilepsy, malignancy, atrial fibrillation (AF), pneumonia, cirrhosis, hypertension, sepsis, hemiplegia, diabetes, HF, and renal dysfunction. Treatment-related variables included mechanical ventilation (MV) and neurosurgical intervention. Laboratory indicators comprised PT, PTT, WBC, RBC, creatinine, potassium, sodium, Hb, glucose, platelets, and chloride. Severity scores included the Acute Physiology Score III (APSIII), GCS, and SOFA. Evaluated endpoints encompassed mortality at 90 and 365 days, as well as the WHR. Data reliability was independently verified by two investigators (ZY Xu and SS Zhou). Only lab values captured during the first 24 h in the ICU were used, and variables with substantial missingness (>20%) were excluded; remaining missing values were imputed using random forest methods [27].

Study endpoints

The main outcome was 30-day overall mortality, while secondary endpoints covered 90- and 365-day mortality rates. WHR was calculated as: WBC (×109/L)/Hb (g/dL).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as medians and IQRs, and group differences were analyzed with the Kruskal–Wallis test. Counts and proportions were used to summarize categorical variables, with differences analyzed using the Chi-squared test. Patients were stratified into four WHR quartiles: Q1 (WHR < 0.66, n = 659), Q2 (0.66 ≤ WHR < 0.89, n = 658), Q3 (0.89 ≤ WHR < 1.19, n = 658), and Q4 (WHR ≥ 1.19, n = 660), defined by index quartile distributions. To assess survival differences, Kaplan–Meier methods were employed. Covariates with high collinearity (VIF > 5) were excluded from further analysis (Supplementary Table 2).

To assess the impact of WHR on mortality, Cox proportional hazard regression was employed. Model 1 included no covariates. Model 2 controlled for age and sex. Model 3 further adjusted for gender, age, hypertension, sepsis, diabetes, HF, renal dysfunction, MV, neurosurgery, potassium, malignancy, cirrhosis, sodium, glucose, chloride, APSIII, GCS score, SOFA score. A nonlinear correlation between WHR and mortality was explored using restricted cubic spline analysis. Sensitivity analyses were conducted after excluding patients with malignancy, cirrhosis, or renal dysfunction. Subgroup analyses were stratified by age (<70 years and ≥70 years), gender, diabetes, hypertension, AF, sepsis, HF, MV, neurosurgery.

The analysis yielded HRs along with 95% CIs for interpretation of effect size. R software (version 4.4.1) was employed to carry out all statistical computations. A P value of <0.05 was used as the criterion for statistical significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of 2635 patients included, the median age was 70 years and 1012 (38%) were female. Overall, 30-day mortality was 15% (n = 403). Individuals in Q4 exhibited greater prevalence of malignancy, pneumonia, sepsis, HF, renal dysfunction, and MV compared to Q1–Q3. Additionally, Q4 patients demonstrated elevated RR, HR, PT, WBC, creatinine, potassium, glucose, platelets, chloride, and severity scores (APSIII, GCS, SOFA), with lower RBC and Hb levels (Table 1). Association between WHR and mortality

The association between WHR and mortality was analyzed through Cox proportional hazards modeling. When WHR was treated as a continuous variable, the HRs and 95% CIs in Model 1 were 1.215 (1.138, 1.297), 1.200 (1.129, 1.275), and 1.172 (1.105, 1.244) for all-cause mortality at 30, 90, and 365 days, respectively. Similar results were observed in the adjusted Model 2. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to exclude the influence of different disease states on the association between WHR and all-cause mortality. Discussion

No prior retrospective analysis has evaluated the link between WHR and overall mortality in TBI populations. Current results reveal a U-shaped trend linking WHR to death risk at 30, 90, and 365 days. Among critically ill TBI patients, increased WHR correlated with a higher likelihood of death. These findings may guide clinical strategies to reduce mortality risk in this population.

WHR represents a composite index of inflammatory and anemic status, reflecting elevated WBC counts and decreased Hb levels. These abnormalities may jointly increase mortality risk in TBI patients. Primary TBI initiates a pathological cascade involving ionic dysregulation, excitotoxicity, and calcium-induced mitochondrial dysfunction, resulting in neuronal apoptosis and subsequent neuroinflammation that may exacerbate both acute and chronic brain injury [28]. White matter, essential for advanced neurological function, is particularly vulnerable. Such injury—especially axonal loss and demyelination—directly impairs neural integrity in TBI [29, 30]. Altered microglial and macrophage function further aggravates white matter injury, leading to maladaptive neuroinflammation [31].

TBI disrupts the blood–brain barrier (BBB), promotes hematoma formation and cerebral edema, increases free radical production, and causes neuronal depolarization and calcium dysregulation [14]. These disruptions allow peripheral immune cells to infiltrate the brain, destabilizing the BBB and inducing pro-inflammatory activation of microglia and macrophages, thereby amplifying neuroinflammation [32]. BBB damage triggers reciprocal glial activation, sustaining a cycle of neurotoxicity and cerebral injury [33, 34]. The synergistic interaction among immune cell invasion, BBB breakdown, and persistent pro-inflammatory microglial activation drives a self-reinforcing mechanism that worsens brain parenchymal damage.

WBC levels reflect systemic inflammatory activity. Neuroinflammation driven by immune dysregulation serves as a critical secondary injury mechanism in TBI, contributing to both acute neuronal apoptosis and long-term cognitive deficits [35]. Following BBB disruption, activated peripheral immune cells interact with central nervous system (CNS)-resident glial cells, impairing the neurovascular unit and intensifying neuroinflammation. Neutrophils, as first-line immune responders, infiltrate brain tissue post-TBI and initiate inflammatory responses [36]. In chronic inflammatory diseases, neutrophils contribute to tissue damage through protease secretion and modulation of interleukin (IL)-22 receptor signaling, enhancing pathogen replication and exacerbating conditions like COPD [37]. Neutrophil extracellular traps further intensify tissue injury and promote thrombotic events in the setting of inflammatory bowel disease [38]. Following TBI, inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6 are markedly increased in preclinical models and individuals with depression, with neutrophils identified as primary contributors to this response [39]. More severe TBI typically correlates with worse outcomes and higher leukocyte counts [40].

Post-TBI anemia arises from multiple mechanisms: impaired erythropoiesis due to cytokine-driven erythropoietin resistance, blood loss, oxidative hemolysis, and hemodilution secondary to fluid resuscitation and hemorrhagic iron depletion [41, 42]. Anemia worsens oxygen deprivation in the brain, impairs tissue-level perfusion, and elevates the likelihood of subsequent traumatic injury [43]. As a result, TBI patients with anemia typically face extended hospital stays, more severe neurological impairment, and greater fatality risk than their non-anemic counterparts [41].

WHR, reflecting inflammatory and anemic status, correlates with mortality in TBI patients. Targeted interventions to control cytokine release and maintain adequate Hb levels may help mitigate secondary injuries, enhance cerebral oxygenation, and suppress inflammasome-mediated cell death, thereby improving clinical outcomes.

Anemia and systemic inflammation following TBI exert synergistic detrimental effects on neurological recovery through interconnected pathophysiological mechanisms. Anemia reduces oxygen-carrying capacity, resulting in cerebral hypoxia. This activates hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs), which upregulate pro-inflammatory pathways and disrupt BBB integrity. Concurrently, systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) triggered by TBI involves cytokine surges, particularly IL-6 and TNF-α, that increase BBB permeability and cerebral edema. The synergy arises as anemia-induced hypoxia amplifies neuroinflammation, while inflammatory mediators suppress erythropoietin production and impair iron metabolism, potentially worsening anemia. This vicious cycle accelerates neuronal apoptosis and exacerbates ischemic damage.

The U-shaped relationship between WHR and mortality in critically ill TBI patients is particularly notable. Low WHR values may also indicate poor prognosis. Leukopenia [44], defined as critically low white blood cell count, is associated with significantly elevated mortality in TBI patients [45]. This adverse outcome primarily stems from a state of acquired immunosuppression. This condition severely compromises the host’s defense mechanisms and heightens susceptibility to nosocomial infections, such as pneumonia and sepsis. These infections trigger systemic inflammatory responses with pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) that cross the compromised BBB [46]. This peripherally generated inflammatory surge synergizes with central neuroinflammation, worsening cerebral edema, elevating intracranial pressure, and amplifying secondary brain injury [47]. Moreover, leukopenia may indicate inadequate bone marrow response or excessive peripheral leukocyte consumption, reflecting profound immune dysregulation that impairs tissue repair [44, 47]. Thus, insufficient leukocytic response not only facilitates uncontrolled infection, but also exacerbates neuroinflammation, establishing a vicious cycle of multi-organ dysfunction that substantially increases mortality risk.

Subgroup analyses revealed stronger associations between WHR and adverse prognosis in TBI patients with comorbid AF, HF, or hypertension. TBI is increasingly recognized as a chronic pathological process with long-term complications that overlap with established AF risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, cardiovascular disease, obesity, and sleep apnea [48–51]. TBI-induced inflammation may further contribute to AF development through cytokine release and oxidative stress, which promote mitochondrial dysfunction and create an arrhythmogenic environment [52].

Anemia at baseline is linked to a higher risk of death and adverse prognosis in patients with cardiovascular comorbidities including CAD, AF, and HF [18, 53, 54]. Systemic inflammation plays a central role in HF pathogenesis, with TLR4/NF-κB signaling contributing to disease progression and predicting adverse events including cardiorenal syndrome and sudden cardiac death [55]. Elevated WBC count serves as an independent predictor of both early and late mortality in individuals with HF[56], while reduced Hb levels similarly correlate with poor long-term prognosis [57, 58].

TBI-related damage to brain regions regulating autonomic function may provoke excessive catecholamine release, leading to arrhythmias [59]. Injury-induced pressure elevation can trigger insular-hypothalamic activation of the sympathetic nervous system, causing adrenal catecholamine surges and β-adrenergic overdrive through cAMP-PKA signaling. This cascade may result in neurogenic stunned myocardium, ATP depletion, and subsequent cell death, ultimately progressing to HF or AF [60].

Hypertension mechanisms post-TBI are multifactorial. Nociceptive stimuli or impaired consciousness may activate the sympathoadrenal system, leading to episodic hypertension via α1-adrenergic-mediated vasoconstriction. Inflammatory cytokines and inflammasomes, including NLRP3, IL-1β, and IL-6, are closely associated with blood pressure elevation following TBI [61]. Structural and functional damage to brainstem autonomic centers may further contribute to neurogenic hypertension.

Significance and limitations of this study

The relationship between WHR and mortality in severely injured TBI patients has not been previously explored. In this study, the optimal range for WHR was found to be between 0.35 and 0.89. The WHR index, as a simple and readily available biomarker, offers a practical approach for early risk stratification in ICU settings. It could help healthcare providers recognize high-risk individuals and refine clinical strategies to improve prognosis.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective nature of the design imposes limitations, such as selection effects and unmeasured confounders, thereby hindering causal interpretation. Second, WBC and Hb levels were derived from initial ICU admission measurements. The absence of data on nutritional status and pre-hospital medications may affect the accuracy and interpretation of these values. Third, the predominantly American patient population may limit generalizability to other ethnic or regional populations. In addition, while Model 3 adjusted for many covariates, residual confounding remains a concern (e.g., enteral or parenteral nutrition status, cardiac function parameters, steroid use, C-reactive protein), preventing assessment of their prognostic value in TBI patients. These limitations may influence the comprehensiveness of conclusions and warrant further validation in future studies.

Conclusion

A U-shaped pattern was observed between WHR values and mortality likelihood among TBI patients in this study. WHR may act as a practical clinical indicator for assessing disease burden and informing therapeutic strategies in ICU populations. These findings contribute to refining clinical risk assessment of prognostic utility for critically ill TBI populations.
