How will independent prescribing affect wellbeing and career progression for the pharmacists of tomorrow?
Authors: Eugene Catangui
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPharmacist independent prescriber numbers continue to increase. In 2025/2026, independent prescribing will be integrated into the foundation year of pharmacy training, which will see a surge of pharmacists with the independent prescribing annotation on the General Pharmaceutical Council register. With increased responsibility and expectation from early careers pharmacists, the risk of burnout may be higher, and we could potentially see an increase in the number of individuals leaving the profession. In contrast, for those who remain in the pharmacy profession, there needs to be a fair evaluation of career progression, to ensure that they understand their skills are valued, and give them a reason to stay in the profession that they devoted years of their life to. As pharmacists are being upskilled, the lines between a pharmacist and physician may start to blur, but it should be noted that both professions have distinct roles in the treatment of a patient, and they are key members of the multidisciplinary team.Keywords: Independent prescribing, Pharmacist, Pharmacy

The role of the pharmacist is ever-changing. We are upskilling pharmacy colleagues in a wide variety of sectors, to help relieve workload pressures experienced in the NHS. Pharmacist independent prescribers (PIPs) are equipped to optimise medication therapy for long-term conditions such as improving the use and target dosing of angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors and beta-blockers in patients with left ventricular systolic dysfunction after acute myocardial infarction.1 PIP numbers have increased by approximately 3,000 since March 2021, with 18,315 PIPs on the pharmacy register at the end of March 2024.2 In 2026/2027, there will be a huge surge of PIPs due to the change in the initial education and training of pharmacists. Traditionally, the route to becoming a PIP would normally take 8 years. With the new standards for the initial education and training of pharmacists, the route to obtaining an independent prescribing (IP) qualification will be shortened to 5 years (Fig. 1).

I believe that this is a massive step forward in the right direction for the pharmacy profession and will inevitably improve patient care. However, there is a growing concern among non-PIPs like me that newly qualified pharmacists with an independent prescribing qualification will be favoured for future job roles. I believe that this concern is valid, and we will never truly know how this will affect those pharmacists that are not IPs until the time comes. My stance on this issue at the time of writing is that practice and clinical experience will trump an IP qualification. While the new structure of the MPharm programme and foundation year provides students with more experiential learning, allowing them to become a participative member within the workforce earlier in their career compared with the traditional model, it does not replace the experience of a fully qualified pharmacist.

With IP being integrated into the foundation year, I ask myself if that would have been something that I would have liked offered to me. The short answer? No. When I was doing my foundation year, my workload was just about manageable. Doing an IP qualification on top of the workload that is required to become a competent pharmacist would just be too overwhelming, tip the trainees over the edge, and subsequently cause them to not want to practise pharmacy in the future. Additionally, choosing your scope of practice for your IP is quite a personal decision, which is based on experience and passion for a specific area of practice. With the sheer number of trainee pharmacists needing to conduct their IP in 2025/2026,3 choice would well and truly become a luxury, and therefore, these trainees may be required to do their IP in an area that they have absolutely no interest in. If we add more work with the addition of IP to an already overwhelming year, and couple that with learning in an area where the trainee has no interest in, the passion and drive for the profession will wane, and more likely cause these trainees to burn out. Those who experience burnout are more likely to leave the profession.4 With burnout scores among pharmacy professionals consistently remaining in the upper end of 80% over the last few years,5 I fear – without proper support and guidance from organisations employing trainee pharmacists in 2025/26 – that this number will rise exponentially and see an even higher rate of individuals leaving the pharmacy profession for good much earlier in their career.

For the majority that will stay in the pharmacy profession, as the role of the pharmacist evolves to meet the healthcare needs and demands of the nation, this evolution comes with a lot more risk and liability with prescribing becoming a core element to the role. Working in secondary care as a pharmacist, salary would be based on the NHS agenda for change banding system.6 Generally, after a pharmacist would complete their IP, they would go into more specialist roles and lead a small pharmacy team within that specialty, normally at a band 8A position, but this would be after years of experience. In the pharmacy profession today, going past a band 8A position your leadership skills thrive, but at times, your clinical skills waver, as you are senior management, who is responsible for the operations of the pharmacy department. Going from a foundation trainee, which is typically a band 5, to a band 8A is too extreme of a jump, but maybe going from a band 5 trainee to a band 6, and then after passing a probationary period of post-registration experience, jumping to a band 7 may be a reasonable method of progression. So, with the shift in pharmacists becoming IPs earlier on in their career, it will be interesting to see how this added responsibility will be remunerated.

Pharmacists are the expert in medicines. We are equipped to understand how a medication will affect the body, but we should not be mistaken as doctors, nor are we to replace doctors to fill the gap. Doctors undergo years of training to understand how to diagnose patients, whereas pharmacists delve into how medications work, and how they can be used safely. Pharmacists’ clinical skills are being utilised more and more to their full potential, and we are therefore more integrated within the multidisciplinary team, as well as being the first point of call for patients. I predict that, in the future, we are to see a rise in numbers of consultant pharmacists, clinical experts in areas of practice, which will help to positively impact the health of individuals and the population. These consultant pharmacists could have come from igniting a passion from conducting their IP in a specific area of practice, or because IP would have been completed early on in their career, they could focus more of their time to find a specific specialty that piques their interest.

An increase in PIPs is what is needed to transform the pharmacy profession and fulfil the needs of the NHS. With IP being integrated into the foundation year of training, this could also cause more individuals to burn out and leave the profession, so getting the right balance in terms of workload in that foundation year is crucial to maintain the wellbeing of the future generation of pharmacists. IP comes with more risk, liability and responsibility for junior pharmacists, so an evaluation of salary banding may be required to ensure that pharmacists feel like they are being paid what they are worth. The pharmacists' roles of tomorrow will look completely different from the roles of the pharmacists of today, and that is indeed a truly exciting concept.
