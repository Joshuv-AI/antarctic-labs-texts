Study on the Mechanism of Berberine Reversing MRSA Resistance to β-Lactam Antibiotics by Inhibiting blaZ Expression
Authors: Fangfang Zhou, Xuemei Gu, Ming Lin, Yue Dai, Yanan Li, Mingming Jiang, Bianba Yangzong, Ying Wang, Xiaochun Sun, Lei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study elucidates a novel mechanism by which the natural product berberine (BBR) reverses methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) resistance to β-lactam antibiotics. It was established that BBR interferes with the BlaR1/BlaI regulatory pathway by means of downregulating the signal sensor gene blaR1 and the repressor gene blaI, thereby significantly suppressing the expression of the β-lactamase-encoding gene blaZ at the transcriptional level. The combination susceptibility testing demonstrated that BBR and Oxacillin exhibited synergistic or additive effects in 76.92% of the bacterial strains that were tested. This enhanced efficacy was closely associated with the presence of the blaZ gene. Molecular docking and dynamics simulations further confirmed that BBR stably binds to the active site of the BlaZ protein. These findings systematically clarify the multi-target mechanism of BBR as an antibiotic synergist and provide an important theoretical basis for developing combination therapeutic strategies against MRSA.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has emerged as a major global public health threat in the 21 st century (Falsey et al. 2025, Antimicrobial Resistance Collaborators 2022). Among these threats, Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA)，one of the most common multidrug-resistant pathogens, can cause life-threatening diseases ranging from skin infections to sepsis (Lakhundi and Zhang 2018). Its resistance to β-lactam antibiotics severely limits clinical treatment options, often forcing reliance on last-line drugs such as Vancomycin, thereby increasing the treatment burden and the risk of adverse effects (Cong et al. 2019, McGuinness et al. 2017).

The core mechanism of MRSA resistance lies in the carriage of the mecA gene, which encodes a low-affinity penicillin-binding protein, PBP2a. This protein allows the bacterium to continue synthesizing its cell wall even in the presence of antibiotics (Peacock and Paterson 2015). However, the development of resistance is far more cnnnnomplex than a single gene can explain. A significant number of MRSA clinical isolates concurrently carry the blaZ gene, which encodes β-lactamase. This enzyme hydrolyzes the β-lactam ring, directly inactivating the antibiotics. The expression of blaZ is tightly regulated by the BlaI-BlaR1 inducible regulatory system: β-lactam antibiotics act as inducers that activate BlaR1, leading to the degradation of the repressor protein BlaI. This derepresses the blaZ gene, initiating its high-level expression and enabling the resistance function (Wysocka et al. 2021, Ersoy et al. 2023, Andrzejczuk et al. 2023).

In the face of the increasingly severe challenge of drug resistance, developing novel antibiotic adjuvants to restore the efficacy of conventional antibiotics is considered more practical and urgent than developing entirely new drugs. Berberine (BBR) is an isoquinoline alkaloid derived from plants like Coptis chinensis, possessing broad-spectrum antibacterial and anti-biofilm activities (Wang et al. 2017). Recent research from our group has revealed that one of its important mechanisms of action is functioning as a Quorum Sensing Inhibitor (QSI), effectively inhibiting the activity of the agr system in Staphylococcus aureus(S. aureus) and significantly reducing the expression of virulence factors. Furthermore, previous studies have found that BBR exhibits a synergistic effect with β-lactam antibiotics against MRSA (Gu et al. 2025, Zhang et al. 2022). However, the molecular mechanisms underlying this synergy, particularly whether it involves interference with the blaZ regulatory circuit, lack systematic and in-depth investigation.

This study aims to systematically evaluate the synergistic anti-MRSA effect of BBR combined with β-lactam antibiotics. Furthermore, we will elucidate the mechanism by which BBR targets and inhibits blaZ expression through phenotypic, enzymatic, gene transcriptional, and global regulatory network analyses. Finally, the in vivo antibacterial efficacy will be validated through animal experiments, aiming to provide a new strategy for clinically addressing β-lactam resistance.

BBR targets the regulatory pathway of blaZ expression

The expression of blaZ, which encodes the penicillin-inactivating enzyme β-lactamase, is controlled by a regulatory circuit comprising BlaI and BlaR1 (Spink et al. 1947). BlaI functions as a DNA-binding repressor protein that silences blaZ transcription. In the presence of β-lactam antibiotics, the sensor-transducer BlaR1 becomes activated and proteolytically cleaves BlaI, thereby derepressing transcription (Hou et al. 2011, Belluzo et al. 2019). qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated that in USA300, BBR treatment markedly reduced blaR1 and blaI expression (Fig. 6A). This suggests that BBR restores β-lactam susceptibility in USA300 by targeting the blaR1/blaI axis, thereby preventing the induction of blaZ expression.To investigate the interaction mechanism between BBR and the BlaI and BlaR proteins at the molecular level, we employed molecular docking. The docking results showed that BBR binds stably within the active site pockets of both BlaI and BlaR, with predicted binding free energies of −8.6 kcal/mol and − 8.2 kcal/mol, respectively (Fig. 6B). These values indicate that BBR exhibits strong binding affinity for both proteins (the higher the absolute binding energy, the more stable the interaction; generally, binding energies below − 4.25 kcal/mol, −5.0 kcal/mol, and − 7.0 kcal/mol are considered to indicate certain, good, and strong binding activity, respectively). As shown in Fig. 6C, four hydrophobic interactions are formed between BlaI and BBR, involving residues Glu7, Thr85, Thr82, and Phe86. Discussion

Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) is characterized by its strong transmissibility, high mortality rate, and multidrug resistance (Chalmers and Wylam 2020). Consequently, developing more effective strategies to prevent and control MRSA infections represents a critical and ongoing challenge in medical research. Currently, most clinically used antibiotics against MRSA target one of three key bacterial sites: the cell wall (via penicillin-binding proteins), the ribosome, or DNA gyrase/topoisomerase (Wang et al. 2022, Wilson 2009, Mlynarczyk-Bonikowska et al. 2022).However, the extensive and often indiscriminate use of antibiotics has accelerated the evolution of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in pathogenic microbes, which is estimated to contribute to at least 700,000 deaths annually worldwide (Tenover 2006).S.aureus exemplifies this challenge by employing a diverse array of mechanisms to evade antibiotic action. It possesses intrinsic resistance strategies, such as modulating membrane permeability, activating chromosomally encoded efflux pumps, and producing antibiotic-inactivating enzymes (Brown and Wright 2016). Beyond these inherent defenses, it can also acquire resistance through genetic mutations or the horizontal transfer of resistance genes, further reducing its susceptibility to antimicrobial agents. Since agents targeting a single site often rapidly select for resistant mutants, combination therapy has emerged as a key strategy to delay or prevent the development of resistance.

This study demonstrates that BBR, a natural isoquinoline alkaloid, can restore the susceptibility of MRSA to β-lactam antibiotics both independently and synergistically with Oxacillin.Previous work from our group has elucidated a multi-faceted mechanism through which BBR compromises the cell wall integrity of USA300: it suppresses wall teichoic acid (WTA) biosynthesis by inhibiting the key synthase TarO, and enhances peptidoglycan hydrolysis by upregulating the hydrolases LytM and SsaA (Gu et al. 2025). Moreover, we previously reported that BBR targets and inhibits several critical enzymes—TarO, TarS, and TarM—thereby disrupting WTA assembly and restoring MRSA susceptibility to β-lactam antibiotics (Zhou et al. 2025). These findings established a theoretical foundation for novel antibacterial strategies.In the present study, we further reveal that BBR suppresses the expression of the β-lactamase-encoding gene blaZ by modulating its upstream regulatory system, BlaR1/BlaI. By interrupting this signal transduction pathway, BBR effectively blocks a central defense mechanism of MRSA against β-lactam antibiotics, providing new insight into its resistance-reversing activity.

The results of this study showed that BBR exhibited comparable antibacterial activity against S.aureus, with a MIC of 128 µg/mL in each case, indicating that its intrinsic antibacterial action is independent of the classic mecA-mediated resistance mechanism. Notably, when combined with oxacillin, BBR displayed synergistic or additive effects in the majority of tested strains (76.92%), whereas all strains that showed no response (indifferent effect) to the combination were blaZ-negative. These findings strongly suggest that the resistance-reversing effect of BBR is closely associated with its interference with β-lactamase activity, thereby providing a clear direction for further mechanistic investigation.It is worth noting that the MIC of BBR ranges from 128 to 256 µg/mL, a relatively high concentration that reflects its weak intrinsic antibacterial potency. Given this limitation, the clinical potential of BBR lies not in its use as a standalone antimicrobial agent, but rather as an antibiotic adjuvant to enhance the efficacy of β-lactam antibiotics. To delve deeper, we employed qRT-PCR to assess whether BBR influences blaZ expression at the transcriptional level.While previous studies have largely focused on the direct antibacterial or anti-biofilm properties of BBR, this work utilized a Nitrocefin-based colorimetric assay to demonstrate that BBR treatment significantly reduces β-lactamase activity. More importantly, qRT-PCR analysis revealed that this reduction was not due to direct inhibition of the enzyme by BBR, but rather resulted from marked downregulation of blaZ mRNA expression. Molecular docking and dynamics simulations indicated that BBR binds stably to the active site of BlaZ, forming a low-energy and stable complex. Although enzymatic assays suggest that this binding may not be the primary mechanism of inhibition, the finding implies that BBR could possess a potential capacity to directly inhibit β-lactamase, or alternatively, modulate its function through allosteric effects.

To elucidate the mechanism by which BBR suppresses blaZ transcription, we further investigated its upstream regulatory genes. Our results demonstrated that BBR significantly downregulated the expression of the signal sensor gene blaR1 and the repressor gene blaI. In the classical BlaR1/BlaI model, β-lactam antibiotics act as inducers that activate BlaR1, leading to proteolytic cleavage and inactivation of BlaI, thereby derepressing blaZ expression (Krüger et al. 2021, Jean et al. 2024). Our findings strongly suggest that BBR disrupts the transmission of this induction signal, thereby preserving the stability and activity of the BlaI repressor. This allows BlaI to persistently bind to the blaZ promoter region, ultimately inhibiting blaZ transcription. As a key innovation of this study, we provide the first evidence linking the sensitizing effect of BBR directly to the regulatory pathway controlling blaZ induction.

Based on previous research from our group, we propose a multi-target synergistic model elucidating BBR’s ability to reverse MRSA resistance. BBR acts through a dual mechanism: first, it inhibits key enzymes such as TarO, impairing wall teichoic acid biosynthesis and compromising cell wall integrity, thereby rendering MRSA more vulnerable to antibiotic penetration. Second, as newly identified in this study, BBR interferes with the BlaR1/BlaI signaling system, suppressing the production of β-lactamase—a key hydrolytic enzyme—thus preventing inactivation of the antibiotic. This two-pronged strategy simultaneously disrupts the physical barrier of the bacterium and disarms its biochemical defense system, resulting in a powerful synergistic effect that significantly restores the susceptibility of MRSA to β-lactam antibiotics.

This study has certain limitations. First, although we have confirmed the regulatory effect of BBR on the BlaR1/BlaI pathway, the precise mechanism by which it influences BlaR1 sensor function—whether through direct binding interference or indirect means—remains to be further elucidated. Second, the in vivo synergistic efficacy of BBR requires validation across a broader range of animal infection models.To address these limitations, our future research will focus on the following directions. First, we will employ techniques such as gene knockout and site-directed mutagenesis to precisely characterize the interaction interface and functional sites between BBR and the BlaR1 protein, thereby clarifying the underlying molecular mechanism. Second, we will establish a diverse panel of animal infection models, including murine models of systemic infection, pneumonia, and biofilm-associated implant infection, to systematically evaluate the synergistic efficacy of BBR in combination with β-lactam antibiotics under different infectious contexts, and to assess key parameters such as pharmacokinetics, tissue distribution, and safety. Third, we aim to explore the synergistic potential of BBR with other classes of antibiotics to expand its clinical applicability as a resistance-modulating agent.

In summary, this study not only demonstrates the ability of BBR to restore MRSA susceptibility to β-lactam antibiotics, but more importantly, elucidates its novel mechanism of action at the molecular level. We have shown that BBR modulates the BlaR1/BlaI regulatory network by downregulating blaR1 and blaI expression, leading to transcriptional suppression of blaZ and consequent inhibition of β-lactamase production. These findings provide deeper insights into the multi-target antibacterial mechanism of this natural product and offer both a theoretical foundation and new research directions for developing BBR as a safe and effective antibiotic synergist in clinical combination therapies against MRSA infections.

Conclusion

This study reveals that BBR restores the susceptibility of MRSA to β-lactam antibiotics through a multi-target synergistic mechanism.Specifically, BBR down-regulates the expression of the signal sensor gene blaR1 and the repressor gene blaI, thereby inhibiting the transcription of the β-lactamase-encoding gene blaZ and blocking the bacterial enzymatic defense. Given its relatively weak intrinsic antibacterial activity, BBR is more suitable for use as an antibiotic adjuvant. This study provides a new strategy and theoretical basis for the clinical combination of BBR with β-lactam antibiotics to reverse MRSA resistance.

Materials and methods

Strain sources

The standard strain USA300 was generously provided by the research group of Professor Lefu Lan at the Hangzhou Institute for Advanced Study, University of Chinese Academy of Sciences (UCAS). The standard strains ATCC 25923 and ATCC 29213 were purchased from the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (CGMCC). Clinical strains were isolated and cultured from respiratory tract samples of inpatients at Shanghai Eighth People’s Hospital.Bacterial culture was performed according to the "National Guide to Clinical Laboratory Procedures." Strain identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing were conducted using the fully automated VITEK MS mass spectrometry microbial identification system (bioMérieux, France) and the VITEK 2 Compact automated microbial identification and susceptibility testing system (bioMérieux, France).

Reagents

Berberine was purchased from Beijing Wokai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Lot No. XW20868312) and dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was purchased from Shanghai Sangon Biotech (Lot No. A460700). Oxacillin was purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Lot No. C15128755) and prepared in sterile deionized water.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) determination

A BBR solution was subjected to two-fold serial dilution using Mueller Hinton Broth (MHB) to achieve final concentrations of 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512 and 1024 µg/mL.An oxacillin solution was diluted with MHB to final concentrations of 0.125, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and 32 µg/mL.A 96-well plate was used, and 100 µL of each diluted drug solution (BBR or oxacillin) at the different concentrations was aliquoted into the respective wells.A bacterial suspension was prepared at a concentration of 5 × 10⁵ CFU/mL.100 µL of the bacterial suspension was added to each well of the 96-well plate.The plate was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h.The MIC was defined as the lowest drug concentration that completely inhibited visible bacterial growth (Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute 2023).

Synergistic antibacterial assay

In this study, 50 µL aliquots of different concentrations of BBR and oxacillin sodium solutions were dispensed into the rows and columns, respectively, of a 96-well plate. A bacterial suspension was prepared at a concentration of 5 × 10⁵ CFU/mL, and 100 µL was added to each well. The plate was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h.The interaction between the drugs was determined by calculating the Fractional Inhibitory Concentration Index (FICI). The formula used was: FICI = (MIC of drug A in combination/MIC of drug A alone) + (MIC of drug B in combination/MIC of drug B alone).The FICI values were interpreted as follows: FICI ≤ 0.5: Synergism;0.5 < FICI ≤ 1: Additive effect༛1 < FICI ≤ 2: Indifference༛FICI > 2: Antagonism (Suzuki and Ohashi 2013).

Time-kill curve assay

Frozen bacterial stocks were inoculated onto LB agar plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Several well-isolated colonies were collected and suspended in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB); the turbidity was then adjusted to the 0.5 McFarland standard (approximately 1.5 × 10⁸ CFU/mL). An initial inoculum was prepared by adding 10 µL of the bacterial suspension to 20 mL of TSB broth supplemented with 0.1% BSA.After 1 h of incubation in a shaking incubator (37 °C, 200 rpm), 2.7 mL of the culture was evenly distributed into 6 test tubes, and 0.03 mL of BBR solutions at different concentrations (8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1024 µg/mL) was added to respective tubes. A 0.03 mL aliquot was immediately removed from each tube to determine the initial CFU (0 h). Incubation was continued, and 0.02 mL aliquots were taken at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 14, and 22 h.Immediately after sampling, the cultures were serially diluted in sterile saline and plated onto Tryptic Soy Agar (TSA). After overnight incubation of the plates, the CFU were determined. Time-kill curves were plotted based on the bacterial survival rates at the different sampling time points. The kinetics of BBR combined with oxacillin were also assessed using the method described above (Foerster et al. 2016).

β-Lactamase activity assay (nitrocefin method)

Nitrocefin is a specific chromogenic substrate for β-lactamase, which turns from yellow to red upon hydrolysis (Rincon et al. 2021). Crude β-lactamase extracts were prepared from S.aureus strains treated with BBR. After the addition of Nitrocefin, changes in absorbance at 486 nm were dynamically monitored using a spectrophotometer to directly assess enzyme activity inhibition. The procedure was as follows: overnight bacterial cultures were diluted to an initial OD600 of approximately 0.1, treated with various concentrations of BBR, and incubated at 37°C for 2 hours. Single colonies were selected, and the bacterial suspension was adjusted to an OD600 of 0.8. Then, 100 μL of the suspension was added to a 96-well plate, followed by 100 μL of Nitrocefin solution (Kilani et al. 2024). Color changes were observed over a period of 10 to 30 minutes; a color shift from yellow to pink indicated β-lactamase production..Quality control testing was performed for each assay using two control strains: ATCC 29213 (blaZ-positve) and ATCC 25923 (blaZ-negative) (Fukushima et al. 2026).

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

Overnight bacterial cultures were diluted to an initial OD₆₀₀ ≈ 0.1 using TSB medium, treated with different concentrations of BBR, and then incubated at 37 °C for 2 h.All strains were cultured to mid-log phase (OD₆₀₀ ≈ 0.8) before collection for RNA extraction.The control group received an equivalent volume of DMSO. Total RNA was extracted using the Qiagen RNeasy kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. When purifying total RNA from bacteria, the bacteria are first disrupted and homogenized in a lysis buffer containing guanidinium thiocyanate using bead milling. Ethanol is then added, and the sample is loaded onto an RNeasy Mini spin column. At this stage, total RNA binds to the silica membrane in the column, while impurities are efficiently washed away. Molecular docking

Molecular docking technology was employed to investigate the interaction between the screened key targets and the core components. The 3D co-crystal structure of the protein receptor and the structure of the small molecule ligand were downloaded from the PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org) and the TCMSP database, respectively. Using PyRx software, water molecules and the original ligand were removed from the macromolecular receptor. The docking parameter model was established using self-docking, with an RMSD < 2.0 Å set as the criterion. Subsequently, on a Linux system, AutoDock Vina software was used to perform the docking between the active molecule ligand and the protein receptor. The docking results were visualized and processed using PyMOL software.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations lasting 100 ns were performed using GROMACS 2020.6 software to further validate the rationality and reliability of the docking results for the BlaZ protein and the small molecule BBR. The CHARMM36m force field was utilized to generate the parameters and topology files for both the protein and the small molecule ligand. Periodic boundary conditions (PBC) were applied, and the dimensions of the simulation box were optimized. The protein was placed at the center of a cubic box, ensuring a minimum distance of 1.0 nm from the box edges, and the box was then filled with water molecules. To achieve electrical neutrality for the simulation system, a portion of the solvent water molecules was replaced with Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions at a concentration of 0.15 mol/L.The steepest descent method was employed for energy minimization of the entire system to eliminate unreasonable contacts or atomic overlaps. This energy minimization step ensured the rationality of the initial structure in terms of both its geometry and the orientation of solvent molecules. Subsequently, to initiate the actual dynamics simulation, the solvent and ions surrounding the protein were pre-equilibrated to obtain a stable thermodynamic ensemble under the desired conditions.The leap-frog algorithm was used for integrating the equations of motion during the equilibration dynamics. All MD simulations were conducted under isothermal-isobaric (NPT) ensemble conditions at a temperature of 303.15 K and a pressure of 1 bar for a total duration of 100 ns. The behavior of the docked complex was predicted over the course of the 100-ns production simulation.Following the completion of the dynamics simulation, trajectory analysis was performed, centered on the protein. Key parameters analyzed included the Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD), Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF), Radius of Gyration (Rg), Solvent Accessible Surface Area (SASA), and Hydrogen Bonds (Hbond).

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 8.0 software was used for statistical analysis. Data were analyzed by Student’s t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. ns indicates no statistical significance. * P < 0.05,** P < 0.01.
