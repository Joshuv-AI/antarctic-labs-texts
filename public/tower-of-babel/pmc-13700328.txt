Comparative outcomes of axillary, jugular, and subclavian venous access for totally implantable venous access port implantation: a 335-case single-center retrospective study
Authors: Lei Tang, Qingchuan Wang, Junyong Yu, Yang Liu, Dingbang Peng, Jiaying Pang, Wen Chen, Meng Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTotally implantable venous access ports (TIVAP) are essential for long-term intravenous access in cancer patients receiving chemotherapy. Controversies persist regarding the optimal venous access route. This study aimed to compare procedural outcomes and complication profiles of axillary, jugular, and subclavian approaches as implemented in routine institutional practice.MethodsThis retrospective study included 335 cancer patients who underwent TIVAP implantation at a single center between February 2021 and December 2022. Ultrasound plus electrocardiogram (ECG) guidance was used for the axillary vein (AxV, n = 51) and internal jugular vein (IJV, n = 84) approaches, whereas the subclavian vein approach (SCV, n = 200) was performed under ECG guidance alone without routine ultrasound. Outcomes assessed included operative time, overall complication rates, and individual complication profiles. Categorical outcomes were compared using Fisher’s exact test; continuous variables were compared using one-way ANOVA and the Kruskal-Wallis test. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed to adjust operative time for BMI. Exact binomial 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for all complication rates.ResultsThe three groups were comparable with respect to age (p = 0.231) and sex distribution (p = 0.184), but differed significantly in body mass index (BMI: SCV 22.54 ± 3.31, AxV 22.07 ± 2.52, IJV 23.68 ± 3.78 kg/m²; ANOVA p = 0.013). The mean operative time was significantly shorter for the AxV group (27.39 ± 4.75 min) compared with both the SCV (33.75 ± 2.77 min) and IJV (37.49 ± 9.59 min) groups (one-way ANOVA p < 0.001; all pairwise post-hoc p < 0.001). After adjustment for BMI, the AxV advantage persisted (BMI-adjusted means: SCV 33.8, AxV 27.6, IJV 37.2 min; p < 0.001). The overall complication rate was 8.0% (16/200; 95% CI: 4.6%–12.7%) in the SCV group, 3.9% (2/51; 95% CI: 0.5%–13.5%) in the AxV group, and 2.4% (2/84; 95% CI: 0.3%–8.3%) in the IJV group. These differences did not reach statistical significance (Fisher’s exact p = 0.18).ConclusionsIn this single-center descriptive comparison, the ultrasound- and ECG-guided axillary vein approach was associated with significantly shorter operative times compared to the subclavian and jugular approaches. Complication rates were numerically lower in the AxV group, but the differences were not statistically significant. The comparison was confounded by the use of different imaging guidance modalities and non-overlapping enrollment periods across groups. These hypothesis-generating findings support the need for prospective studies with standardized guidance protocols.Keywords: Venous access ports, Venous catheterization, Cancer, Chemotherapy, Ultrasound guidance

Introduction

Totally implantable venous access port (TIVAP) represents a valuable medical tool for cancer patients undergoing long-term intravenous therapies like chemotherapy [1, 2]. Owing to its reduced risk of infection and enhanced patient comfort, TIVAP has gained widespread clinical application, especially beneficial for patients in need of intermittent long-term intravenous fluid administration. Since the early clinical experiences with central venous catheterization, refinements in technique and device design have progressively reduced complication rates [3]. The preferred methods for TIVAP implantation are typically through the internal jugular vein or the subclavian vein, chosen for their gradual learning curve and high rates of successful implantation.

Nevertheless, the implantation and utilization of TIVAP carry certain risks and may be associated with a range of complications, encompassing both perioperative and chronic issues. Potential perioperative complications can include hemorrhage, pneumothorax, and nerve damage. Chronic complications are even more varied, encompassing catheter failures (like occlusion, fracture, or dislocation), venous thrombosis, catheter-associated infections, extravasation injuries, and fibrous encapsulation [4–6].

In search of a more rational puncture route, the research focus has increasingly shifted to the axillary vein [7]. Ultrasound guidance has been demonstrated to significantly reduce the risk of insertion failure during the placement of catheters into the internal jugular vein [8–10]. Furthermore, the electrocardiographic positioning technique for catheter tip localization is rapidly gaining popularity due to its ease of operation and the absence of radioactive exposure [11].

This study conducted a retrospective analysis to compare the outcomes and complications associated with the placement of fully implantable venous access ports through the axillary, internal jugular, and subclavian veins.We aimed to compare the clinical outcomes of the three venous access approaches as implemented in routine institutional practice, rather than under identical technical conditions.The aim was to provide descriptive evidence to inform clinical selection among these three approaches.

Methods

Study design and patient enrollment

Between February 2021 and December 2022, a total of 335 cancer patients underwent Totally Implantable Venous Access Port (TIVAP) implantation at our institution and were followed until catheter removal or the conclusion of the study period. The study cohort comprised three distinct approaches: the subclavian vein approach (SCV, n = 200), the axillary vein approach (AxV, n = 51), and the internal jugular vein approach (IJV, n = 84).

The three access approaches were adopted sequentially rather than concurrently at our institution, reflecting the pragmatic evolution of clinical practice. The SCV approach was the long-established institutional standard, with implantations concentrated in 2022. The ultrasound-guided AxV approach was introduced in July 2021 as part of an institutional transition toward imaging-guided techniques, and the IJV approach was used selectively throughout the study period as an alternative or salvage route (Fig. 1). Consequently, the enrollment periods were not fully overlapping across groups (AxV: July 2021–March 2022; SCV: January–December 2022; IJV: February 2021–December 2022)—a pattern also noted in other large comparative TIVAP series, in which ultrasound guidance was progressively incorporated into clinical practice over time [12].

The venous access route was determined by the operating surgeon based on the following institutional protocol: the right axillary vein was preferred as the first-choice access route; in the presence of right-sided tumor lesions, scar tissue from prior surgery, or a history of right mastectomy, the left axillary vein was chosen as an alternative. The subclavian vein approach was used when axillary vein puncture was not feasible or had failed. Prior to the surgical intervention, it is imperative that the patient’s platelet count remains above 50,000/µL, and the international normalized ratio (INR) must be maintained below 1.8 [13]. Throughout the surgical process, lidocaine is administered for local anesthetic purposes, while diluted heparinized water saline solution with heparin and norepinephrine are utilized for anticoagulation measures and hemorrhage prophylaxis.

The present study adhered strictly to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki of the World Medical Association, secured approval from the Institutional Review Committee, and obtained written informed consent from patients, encompassing authorization for the anonymous handling of patient-related materials.

Technique of the procedure

All TIVAP implantations were performed or directly supervised by senior breast surgeons, and all operators underwent standardized training, including a minimum of 10 supervised procedures, prior to independent performance. All surgeries involving the implantation of Totally Implantable Venous Access Ports (TIVAP) through the axillary vein are conducted in the operating room, guided by ultrasound and electrocardiogram, under the supervision of senior breast surgeons. At the beginning of the surgical procedure, the patient assumes a supine position, with a thin pillow placed beneath the shoulders to elevate them slightly. The head is tilted backwards and turned towards the contralateral side, while the upper limbs hang naturally with a slight outward extension. Subsequently, a color Doppler ultrasound examination is performed to assess the blood flow signals and spectral patterns in the region extending from the subclavian to the axillary area of the patient. This is done to differentiate between the axillary vein and artery, as well as to establish their anatomical relationship with the pleural line. Throughout this procedure, the point at which the cephalic vein merges into the axillary vein, as well as its continuation towards the subclavian vein, are identified and marked. The chosen segment of the axillary vein serves as the target for puncture, and the location for the implantation of the pouch is concurrently planned. Following this, the skin is prepared in a sterile environment, utilizing sterile ultrasound gel and drapes to ensure the protection of the ultrasound probe and its cables. Unless the patient presents with tumorous lesions, scar tissue, a history of right mastectomy, the preferred approach is via the right AxV. In the presence of the aforementioned conditions, the left AxV approach is chosen as an alternative.

The infiltration of local anesthetic was applied to the designated skin region. Guided by ultrasound, all patients received venous puncture with a 21G needle. Upon verification of venous patency, the tip of a 0.035-inch J-shaped guidewire was threaded through the puncture needle into the superior vena cava and fixed in place. Next, a 2-centimeter horizontal incision was made approximately 2–3 centimeters caudal to the midpoint of the clavicle, and a subcutaneous pocket was formed using blunt dissection techniques under local anesthesia. Subsequently, the vascular sheath was inserted into the vessel while guided by the guidewire, followed by the withdrawal of the guidewire. On the surgical table, the guidewire was inserted into the catheter, and once the markings were aligned, the wire-catheter assembly was inserted along the vascular sheath, followed by the removal of the sheath. The RA lead wire was attached to the catheter’s distal end, and adjustments were made to the catheter’s depth to monitor P-wave variations in the electrocardiogram, thereby determining the optimal position. Subsequently, the guidewire was withdrawn. Redundant catheter length was cut off and securely connected to the infusion port base. The infusion port base was left unanchored to the underlying fascia. The skin incision was subsequently closed using absorbable sutures. The patency of the infusion port was verified by inserting a Huber needle. Lastly, the infusion system was rinsed with 10 milliliters of heparinized saline containing 1000 units of heparin. In the event of failed axillary vein puncture, the option of ipsilateral or contralateral internal jugular vein puncture was considered. Following the surgery, the patient underwent a chest X-ray examination to verify the location of the catheter tip (Fig. 2) and to screen for potential complications such as pneumothorax, hemothorax, and pinch-off syndrome.

Chest fluoroscopy showing port catheter placed via axillary vein approach

The implantation of fully implantable intravenous access ports (TIVAPs) through the subclavian vein (SCV) is supervised and performed by senior surgeons from the breast surgery department. To reduce the likelihood of pinch-off syndrome, the puncture point for the subclavian vein is commonly situated in the middle or outer third of the clavicle. The surgical procedure, conducted within the surgical suite, is guided by electrocardiogram monitoring. In all patients, a 21G Seldinger needle is employed for venous puncture, with the guidewire tip subsequently advanced into the vena cava. It is noteworthy that ultrasonic guidance is not employed during the surgical process involving the subclavian vein. Once the puncture is successfully completed, a surgical incision is made to create a subcutaneous pocket. The subcutaneous pocket is positioned identically for both the axillary vein (AxV) and subclavian vein (SCV) access routes. The remaining surgical procedures are fundamentally comparable.

Postoperatively, patients undergo a 2-4-hour observation period, during which hematoma and other immediate complications are examined every 30 min. Chemotherapy commences generally within 0 to 3 days following the surgical procedure. All catheter maintenance was performed exclusively by hospital-based nursing staff from the pain department, outpatient chemotherapy clinic, or inpatient wards; patients were not trained for home flushing. The flushing protocol was identical across all three venous access groups, consisting of 10 mL of normal saline containing 1000 units of heparin, administered either after each port use or at least once every 28 days when the port was not in active use. Dressing changes were performed by the same nursing teams according to standardized institutional protocols.

Crossover cases

Three patients (0.9% of the total cohort) were identified in whom the initial venous access route failed and was converted to an alternative route: one SCV catheter malposition was redirected to IJV; one failed axillary access with subsequent SCV arterial puncture was converted to IJV; and one patient had multiple failed SCV and AxV attempts before successful IJV implantation. In the primary analysis, these patients were analyzed according to the final successful access route (as-treated). A sensitivity analysis using intention-to-treat classification produced nearly identical results, given the small number of crossover events (3/335, 0.9%).

Statistical analysis

This study retrospectively reviewed medical records, encompassing laboratory data pertaining to infections, in order to ascertain the incidence of complications related to infusion ports and document pertinent details. The entire dataset was sourced from our documentation records and the electronic databases maintained by the hospital’s information system. The study utilized IBM SPSS 27.0.1 software to perform a normality test on the variables. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test revealed a p-value of 0.092, and both the coefficient of variation and the Q-Q plot suggested that the data distribution was within normal limits.

This study utilized descriptive statistics to summarize fundamental features, including mean and median values to reflect central tendencies, and minimum, maximum, and standard deviation values to exhibit variability. At a statistical significance level of 0.05, the study adopted chi-square tests to assess the statistical significance of patient gender and cancer location. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to investigate differences in normally distributed continuous variables (including age, BMI, and operative time) among the SCV, AxV, and IJV groups, followed by pairwise post-hoc comparisons using the Bonferroni correction where appropriate.

Additionally, an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed to compare operative time across groups after adjusting for BMI. Exact binomial 95% confidence intervals were calculated for all complication rates.

Results

The three groups (SCV, AxV, and IJV) exhibited comparability in most baseline patient features and the locations of primary malignancies, as outlined in Table 1. Except for BMI (p = 0.013), no statistically significant differences were observed among the three cohorts regarding age, gender, or cancer distribution. The majority of TIVAP insertions across all cohorts occurred on the right side, accounting for 168 patients (84.0%) in the SCV group, and a combined 105 patients (77.8%) in the AxV and IJV groups.

The mean operative time was significantly shorter for the AxV group (27.39 ± 4.75 min) compared with both the SCV (33.75 ± 2.77 min) and IJV (37.49 ± 9.59 min) groups (one-way ANOVA p < 0.001; all pairwise post-hoc p < 0.001 with Bonferroni correction).Because BMI differed significantly across groups (p = 0.013), an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed. After adjustment for BMI, the mean operative times were 33.8 ± 0.4 min for SCV, 27.6 ± 0.8 min for AxV, and 37.2 ± 0.6 min for IJV (p < 0.001). The AxV advantage over SCV (approximately 6.2 min) and IJV (approximately 9.6 min) remained essentially unchanged after BMI adjustment, indicating that the between-group differences in operative time were not attributable to BMI.The shorter surgical duration implies a potential decrease in the risks and discomfort experienced by patients undergoing the operation. Additionally, it contributes to the efficient utilization of healthcare resources.

The overall complication rate was numerically higher in the SCV group (8.0%, 16/200; 95% CI: 4.6%–12.7%) compared to the AxV group (3.9%, 2/51; 95% CI: 0.5%–13.5%) and the IJV group (2.4%, 2/84; 95% CI: 0.3%–8.3%). However, these differences did not reach statistical significance (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.184).The wide confidence intervals for the AxV and IJV groups reflect the small sample sizes and low event counts. For example, the upper 95% confidence bound for the overall complication rate in the AxV group (13.5%) exceeds that of the SCV group (12.7%), indicating that clinically meaningful differences in either direction cannot be excluded.Among early complications in the SCV cohort, a single case of delayed pneumothorax (0.5%; 95% CI: 0.0%–2.8%) was documented ) (Fig. 3), resolving spontaneously without chest tube intervention. In contrast, no pneumothorax events were observed in the IJV/AxV groups. Meanwhile, the AxV group reported 1 case of inadvertent artery puncture (2.0%; 95% CI: 0.0%–10.4%), whereas the SCV group exhibited a numerically higher incidence of this complication (3 cases, 1.5%; 95% CI: 0.3%–4.3%).In late complications, three patients in the SCV group (1.5%; 95% CI: 0.3%–4.3%) experienced pinch-off syndrome. The SCV group exhibited four cases of catheter malposition (2.0%; 95% CI: 0.5%–5.0%), whereas the AxV group demonstrated one such case (2.0%; 95% CI: 0.0%–10.4%). A single case of port-flip occurred in the SCV group (0.5%; 95% CI: 0.0%–2.8%), which was successfully rectified through manual reduction. The final data revealed that the SCV group had 2 cases each of venous thrombosis and catheter-related infections, while the IJV group had 1 case of venous thrombosis and 1 case of catheter-related infection. In contrast, no cases of venous thrombosis or catheter-related infections were observed in the AxV group.The detailed distribution of early and late complications across the three cohorts with 95% confidence intervals is summarized in Table 2.

Furthermore, our analysis revealed that the average operative time in the AxV group was notably shorter than in the SCV group and the IJV group, with 27.39 min compared to 33.75–37.49 min. The observed difference was statistically meaningful. The shorter surgical duration implies a potential decrease in the risks and discomfort experienced by patients undergoing the operation. Additionally, it contributes to the efficient utilization of healthcare resources.

In the SCV group, 3 patients (1.5%) experienced inadvertent arterial puncture during the initial puncture attempt; all cases were successfully managed with compression and a second puncture attempt. One patient (0.5%) developed a delayed pneumothorax that resolved spontaneously without intervention. In the AxV group, 1 patient (2.0%) had an arterial puncture on first attempt, which was successfully managed with compression and re-puncture. No pneumothorax or other major early complications occurred in the AxV group. In the IJV group, 3 patients (3.6%) were recorded as crossovers from failed SCV or AxV punctures: one SCV catheter misplacement was redirected to IJV, one patient had failed axillary access with subsequent SCV arterial puncture and was converted to IJV, and one patient had multiple failed SCV and axillary attempts before successful IJV implantation. All crossover patients ultimately underwent successful port implantation via the IJV route. Accounting for these crossovers on an intention-to-treat basis, the effective first-attempt success rates were 98.5% for the SCV route, 98.0% for the AxV route, and 96.4% for the IJV route.

Discussion

In this retrospective study, we compared the clinical outcomes of three venous access approaches—axillary, internal jugular, and subclavian—for TIVAP implantation in 335 cancer patients at a single institution. TIVAP occupies a pivotal position in the long-term management of cancer patients, with its extended lifespan outperforming other vascular access catheters and its capacity to markedly mitigate infection risks. As medical technology continues to evolve, ultrasound-guided minimally invasive techniques have gained widespread adoption in infusion port surgery over the past decade. Intraoperative electrocardiographic guidance localization technology, which enables accurate determination of the catheter tip’s position based on P-wave changes, has gained popularity due to its simplicity, real-time positioning capability, and elimination of radioactive exposure.Notably, the narrow standard deviation of operative time in the SCV group (2.77 min) reflects the high degree of procedural reproducibility achieved by a consistent team of experienced breast surgeons. Multiple studies have corroborated that ultrasound-guided techniques significantly enhance the safety of axillary vein catheter insertion, reducing the risks of arterial puncture and hematoma formation [14]. Nevertheless, in our clinical setting, no statistically significant differences were observed between landmark-guided SCV catheter insertion and ultrasound-guided approaches in terms of overall complication rates or first-attempt success. This may be attributable to the operators’ extensive experience in landmark puncture techniques, combined with the subclavian vein’s relatively stable anatomical structure.

The low overall complication rates observed across all three groups (SCV: 8.0%, AxV: 3.9%, IJV: 2.4%) may be attributable to several institutional factors. All implantations were performed or supervised by a consistent team of three senior surgeons with extensive experience in central venous access. Catheter maintenance was performed exclusively by hospital-based nursing staff following standardized flushing and dressing protocols, which likely reduced the risk of catheter-related infections compared with settings where maintenance is delegated to patients or community providers. Intraoperative ECG guidance for catheter tip positioning was used across all three groups, ensuring consistent catheter tip placement and potentially contributing to the low rates of malposition and thrombosis.

According to the literature, TIVAP-related infection rates range from 2.6% to 9.3% [15]. In this study, three cases of catheter-related infections were observed (SCV: 2/200, 1.0%; IJV: 1/84, 1.2%), at the lower end of the reported range. No cases of catheter fracture, breakage, or extravasation occurred in any group during the study period, further supporting the safety profile of TIVAP when implanted and maintained according to standardized protocols. However, the absence of thrombosis or infection in the AxV group should be interpreted with caution given the small sample size (n = 51); the upper 95% confidence bound for the overall AxV complication rate extends to 13.5%, indicating that rates comparable to or exceeding those in the SCV group cannot be excluded.

Pneumothorax poses a significant risk during subclavian vein access, with a reported occurrence rate of approximately 0.1%, varying with practitioner expertise and individual anatomy [16, 17]. Ultrasound-guided axillary vein puncture can effectively avert pneumothorax by allowing direct visualization of the pleural line. In our series, one case of delayed pneumothorax occurred in the SCV group (0.5%), whereas no pneumothorax was observed in the ultrasound-guided AxV or IJV groups.

Comparison with published studies (Table 3) provides useful context. Yildirim et al. compared ultrasound-guided axillary (n = 1,231) versus landmark subclavian (n = 922) TIVAP implantation and found significantly fewer complications with ultrasound guidance, with pinch-off syndrome occurring only after subclavian puncture. Liu et al. [18] reported that pinch-off syndrome was unique to the subclavian group, while Chen et al. [19] found that AxV/SCV offered better comfort but higher early complication rates. Our findings are broadly consistent with this literature. The three cases of pinch-off syndrome in our series all occurred in the SCV group, consistent with prior reports. The integration of ultrasound guidance with tip-positioning techniques may further enhance procedural accuracy; Marano et al. described an ultrasound-guided index for PICC tip positioning that achieved 94% accuracy without fluoroscopy [20]. A recent 5-year analysis of TIVAP safety by Abou-Mrad et al. reported a long-term complication rate of 2.9% and a 100% procedural success rate [21], consistent with our low complication rates.

The substantially shorter mean operative time in the AxV group persisted after BMI adjustment. This can be attributed to the relative simplicity of the axillary vein’s anatomical course, the enhanced precision provided by real-time ultrasound visualization [22], and the absence of subcutaneous tunneling required for IJV access. The clinical significance of a 6–10 min reduction merits consideration. For an individual patient, this difference is unlikely to substantially affect perioperative risk or recovery. However, at the institutional level, cumulative time savings across a high-volume TIVAP program could improve operating room utilization; in our series, the total time saved for 51 AxV procedures relative to SCV was approximately 5.4 h. Shorter operative times also reduce local anesthetic exposure and may improve the procedural experience.

Most TIVAP-related complications occur within the first two postoperative years [23, 24], and the majority arise from improper handling of the infusion port by non-specialists [25]. The incidence of complications has been mitigated as the multidisciplinary team accumulates experience. Continued training of professional staff and patient education remain essential for maintaining low complication rates.

The sequential, non-concurrent adoption of the three access approaches in the present study reflects the real-world pattern of surgical innovation. This pattern has precedent in the TIVAP literature: Yildirim et al. compared approaches performed during different periods reflecting sequential ultrasound adoption, and Liu et al. compared techniques under evolving dual-guidance protocols. Our data therefore capture a real-world comparison of three approaches during a period of institutional technical transition.

We acknowledge that ultrasound guidance was not routinely utilized in the SCV group, which presents a confounding factor when comparing operative times and complication rates with the ultrasound-guided AxV and IJV groups. This discrepancy accurately reflects the pragmatic evolution of clinical practice at our institution, where landmark-guided SCV puncture was a long-established routine executed with high proficiency. In alignment with ASCO and ESMO guidelines advocating ultrasound navigation, our institution progressively adopted ultrasound-guided approaches. Reliable venous access is particularly critical for patients receiving multi-agent chemotherapy regimens, where treatment adherence directly affects oncological outcomes [26]. Current clinical practice guidelines recommend ultrasound guidance to reduce mechanical complications during central venous catheterization [27, 28]. While our data do not permit definitive conclusions regarding the optimal venous route, they are consistent with the guideline emphasis on ultrasound-guided techniques.

The present study has several limitations. First, the comparison between access routes is confounded by the use of different imaging guidance modalities. Second, enrollment periods were not fully overlapping, reflecting the sequential adoption of techniques at our institution; similar patterns have been reported in other retrospective TIVAP comparisons. Third, SCV implantations were concentrated in 2022, resulting in a shorter observation window for late complications in this group. Fourth, small and unequal group sizes combined with low complication event counts precluded multivariable adjustment for complications and limited statistical power. Fifth, three crossover patients were analyzed as-treated rather than by intention-to-treat, although a sensitivity analysis confirmed that this did not affect the primary conclusions. Sixth, as a retrospective single-center study with surgeon-determined access route selection, the findings are subject to selection bias and limited generalizability; late complications from chart review may be under-reported. Finally, formal cost-effectiveness analysis was beyond the scope of this descriptive study. Future prospective, multicenter studies with standardized imaging guidance protocols are needed to definitively compare these three venous access routes.

Conclusions

In this single-center retrospective comparison, the ultrasound- and ECG-guided transaxillary approach was associated with significantly shorter operative times compared with the subclavian vein and internal jugular vein approaches, even after adjustment for BMI. Complication rates were numerically lower in the axillary vein group, but these differences did not reach statistical significance, and confidence intervals were wide. Complications including pneumothorax, arterial puncture, pinch-off syndrome, catheter malposition, thrombosis, and infection were less frequently observed in the axillary vein group compared with the SCV group. However, the comparison was confounded by the use of different imaging guidance modalities and non-overlapping enrollment periods, and route-specific effects could not be separated from guidance-related effects. These hypothesis-generating findings highlight the need for prospective, randomized studies with standardized imaging guidance protocols to definitively establish the relative merits of each venous access route. Until such evidence is available, the choice of venous access for TIVAP implantation should be individualized based on patient anatomy, operator experience, and institutional resources, with preference given to ultrasound-guided techniques in accordance with current clinical practice guidelines.
