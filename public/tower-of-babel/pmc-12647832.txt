Molecular dynamics and COSMO-RS model of menthol–fatty acid deep eutectic solvents: thermodynamic, structural, and dynamics insights
Authors: Mitra Dabbagh Hosseini Pour, Jaber Jahanbin Sardroodi, Naghmeh Hadidi, Gholamreza Pazuki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeep eutectic solvents (DESs) have emerged as sustainable alternatives to conventional solvents; however, our molecular-scale understanding of their intrinsic properties remains limited. In this study, we investigate menthol–fatty acid (valeric acid, enanthic acid, and pelargonic acid) DESs through a synergistic framework that combines molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, the conductor-like screening model (COSMO-RS) predictions, and Kirkwood–Buff integral (KBI) analysis. Our MD simulations reveal how the concentration and length of fatty acid chains influence the microscopic structure and dynamics within mixtures. COSMO-RS calculations provide predictive insights into activity coefficients and solvation tendencies, aligning closely with our atomistic results. Additionally, KBI analyses quantify preferential interactions, and isothermal compressibility, establishing a strong thermodynamic connection between microscopic ordering and macroscopic properties. Our findings demonstrate that subtle variations in fatty acid chain length affect the stability, heterogeneity, and transport behavior of these mixtures. This provides a rational approach for tuning their properties. Beyond these systems, the combined use of MD, COSMO-RS, and KBI methods presents a transferable and predictive protocol for designing DESs with tailored functionalities. This research enhances our understanding of the organization and thermodynamics of DESs, while also providing essential design principles to promote their application in bioprocessing and separation technologies. DES systems were designed using VMD and PACKMOL to arrange HBDs and HBAs. After performing energy minimization and equilibration at a temperature of 298.15 K, these systems were simulated under NPT conditions for 50 nanoseconds to allow for structural relaxation. Thermodynamic and solvation properties were estimated using COSMO-RS model, while electronic structure calculations were conducted using TURBOMOLE at the triple zeta valence potential (TZVP) basis set. Introduction

Industrial solvents are essential in various industries such as pharmaceuticals, petrochemicals, and food production, where they facilitate key processes like extraction, drug delivery, and purification1. These solvents account for approximately 80–90% of the total mass used in industrial operations, and their disposal contributes to about 85% of chemical waste generated across various industries2. A conventional solvent system typically comprises 60% organic solvents and 30% water3. However, due to increasing environmental and regulatory concerns, a noticeable decline in the use of organic solvents has been observed, particularly in the pharmaceutical industry, highlighting a transition toward safer and more sustainable alternatives.

In response to increasing sustainability demands, green chemistry has emerged as a transformative approach, emphasizing the design of safer chemical processes and environmentally benign alternatives to conventional solvents4. Deep eutectic solvents (DESs), introduced in 2003, offer a more accessible and environmentally friendly alternative5. Formed through hydrogen bonding between hydrogen bond donors (HBDs) and acceptors (HBAs)6, DESs exhibit low toxicity, tunable properties, and high thermal stability7. Their melting points are lower than those of their individual components due to molecular interactions within the mixture8. DES applications span biocatalysts9, electrochemistry10, drug delivery11, extraction12, and separation13.

DESs remarkable ability to enhance the solubility of poorly soluble compounds makes them promising alternatives to conventional solvents. Understanding the solubility behavior and mixing characteristics of DES components is crucial for optimizing their performance and expanding their use in chemical and biochemical processes. Predictive models, such as COSMO-RS, have been successfully utilized to correlate solubility with thermodynamic parameters, facilitating the rational design of DESs in improving the solubility of active pharmaceutical ingredients and natural compounds.

Jeliński et al. investigated the solubility of six sulfonamides in DESs composed of choline chloride and polyhydroxy compounds. They found that solubility correlated with favorable Gibbs free energy interactions, with the highest solubility observed in a 1:1 choline chloride–glycerol mixture14. In a separate study, the same group evaluated the solubility of ibuprofen and ketoprofen in DESs formed from choline chloride and various polyols at different molar ratios, using both experimental methods and COSMO-RS descriptors. Their results demonstrated that DES composition significantly influences drug solubility15. Additional studies have reinforced the utility of COSMO-RS in predicting solubility trends in diverse DES systems16,17. Furthermore, DESs have been applied to the solubilization of natural compounds such as ferulic acid, with COSMO-RS modeling showing that water-enhanced DES mixtures, particularly those based on choline chloride and triethylene glycol, offer superior solubility and strong solute–solvent interactions18.

Díaz-Álvarez and Martín-Esteban developed a series of DESs using L-menthol combined with various natural organic acids, including citric, formic, acetic, decanoic, nonanoic, lactic, and valeric acids at different molar ratios. Except for the menthol–citric acid mixture, most formulations yielded stable, transparent liquids at room temperature. Notably, the formic acid–menthol system exhibited phase separation, likely due to excess water content. Physicochemical characterization revealed that the density of these mixtures decreased with increasing alkyl chain length of the organic acid, and with higher menthol content, attributed to menthol’s low intrinsic density19.

Among DESs, terpene-based systems—especially those incorporating monoterpenes such as menthol—stand out due to their hydrophobic nature and potential in separation technologies20. Fatty acids (FAs) possess desirable physical properties21, excellent biodegradability, cost-effectiveness, and biological significance, making them ideal candidates for developing eutectic solvents22. The chain length of these fatty acids considerably affects the physicochemical behavior of these mixtures, influencing factors such as hydrogen bonding, viscosity, and phase behavior23. Studies have shown that binary menthol–FAs mixtures exhibit characteristics that depend on both composition and chain length24. These properties are crucial for tailoring solvent behavior in various applications, including extraction and drug delivery23.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations have emerged as a powerful tool to complement experimental DES studies by providing detailed insights into molecular interactions25, structural organization, and transport phenomena26. Recent simulations have elucidated the effects of alkyl chain length and temperature on menthol–FA mixtures27, revealing critical trends in hydrogen bonding and molecular diffusion28.

Despite significant advancements in the study of DESs, the molecular-level behavior of binary systems composed of menthol and short- to medium-chain FAs remains inadequately understood. Odd-chain FAs have a wide range of applications in therapeutic, nutritional, and biofuel industries. Their unique properties, along with their abundant presence in dairy products like milk and their low viscosity, make these fatty acids suitable components for creating eutectic solvents. This study aims to bridge this knowledge gap through a comprehensive investigation of menthol–FA mixtures using MD simulations and COSMO-RS modeling. Specifically, we examined the influence of fatty acid chain length (C5, C7, and C9) and concentration on the structural, dynamic, and thermodynamic properties of these systems. Furthermore, the solubility of various FAs in menthol was quantitatively predicted using the COSMO-RS approach, offering valuable thermodynamic insights. To complement these predictions, Kirkwood–Buff integrals were calculated from MD simulations to quantify solute–solvent interactions. These integrated computational insights are crucial for the rational design and optimization of hydrophobic DESs, particularly for applications in green separation processes, pharmaceutical formulations, and the development of sustainable solvents. Methods (computational details)

MD simulations were conducted on all systems comprising FAs and L-menthol at various mole fractions using the NAMD 2.14 software package in conjunction with the CHARMM-36 force field29. The molecular structures of all components were initially generated using MarvinSketch 24.1.230, and atomic charges were assigned using the restricted electrostatic potential (RESP) method31. Topology and parameter files were prepared via the SwissParam server32.

The initial configurations of L-menthol and FAs, with varying mole fractions, were constructed using the Packmol program33, as detailed in Table 1. The equations of motion were integrated using the velocity Verlet al.gorithm34 with a time step of 1 fs, ensuring accurate computation of atomic positions and velocities. Electrostatic long-range interactions were handled with the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method35, with a cutoff distance of 12 Å. Van der Waals interactions were modeled using the Lennard-Jones 12 − 6 potential36, and periodic boundary conditions (PBC)37 were applied in all three spatial dimensions. One widely used approach is COSMO-RS (Conductor-like Screening Model for Real Solvents), which predicts the thermodynamic properties and molecular structures of mixtures without relying on experimental data. In this study, four molecules were utilized: (1R, 2 S, 5R)−2-isopropyl-5-methylcyclohexanol (L-menthol, L-MN), valeric acid (VAL), enanthic acid (ENA), and pelargonic acid (PEL). Geometry optimization for each molecule was performed at the density functional theory (DFT) level using the def-TZVP basis set (triple-zeta valence with polarization)39 after generating the molecular structure with MarvinSketch version 24.1.2. The optimization process was carried out using TURBOMOLE software (Tmolex version 4.2)40. This software utilizes electronic structure methods to calculate the energy and forces acting on molecular structures. By doing so, it helps identify local energy minima, ensuring accurate three-dimensional representations of the molecules. Identifying the atomic configuration with the lowest energy is more complex than simply finding a local minimum; in fact, locating the global minimum is an even greater challenge. A major difficulty in global optimization is the presence of high energy barriers that separate different local minima, making it much harder to transition between them. Therefore, Tmolex aims to find a local minimum on the potential energy surface—one that corresponds to a stable molecular geometry with lower energy than nearby configurations. Frequency calculations for the components of binary mixtures were conducted using Tmolex 4.2 software to predict vibrational frequencies and identify transition or stable states for the molecules. The positive frequency values indicate that all molecular geometries used in this study correspond to local minima on the potential energy surface. COSMO files were generated for all studied molecules, processed using the COSMOTherm18.0.1 software41, which predicts the thermodynamic properties. The formulas for predicting thermodynamic properties using the COSMO-RS model are available in the supplementary information.

Results and discussion

Sigma-profiles prediction

The σ-surfaces visualize the spatial distribution of charge on the molecular surface, where green regions denote neutral surface areas, red represents electronegative atoms, and blue indicates electropositive atoms. As illustrated in Fig. 3, these variations in surface charge play a crucial role in determining the physicochemical behavior of FAs and L-menthol. In this analysis, σ-profiles describe the charge density distribution across a molecule’s surface. In the σ-profile plot (Fig. 3), the horizontal axis corresponds to the screening charge density (σ), and the vertical axis indicates the relative frequency of these charge values on the molecular surface. The σ-profiles of FAs reveal a characteristic peak in the donor region at σ < −0.0084 e/Å², attributed to the hydrogen atom of the carboxyl group. This peak signifies a strong ability of FAs to act as hydrogen bond donors. Smaller peak at σ > 0.0084 e/Å² in the acceptor region indicates their ability to participate as hydrogen bond acceptors. Within the non-polar region (−0.0084 e/Å² < σ < 0.0084 e/Å²) the intensity of peaks increases with the alkyl chain length of FAs, reflecting an enhanced proportion of hydrophobic surface areas, primarily due to CH₂ and CH₃ groups. These trends confirm the increasing hydrophobicity of long-chain fatty acids. L-menthol exhibits a dominant peak in the acceptor region (σ > 0.0084 e/Å²), corresponding to the oxygen atom in its hydroxyl group. This suggests that L-menthol primarily act as a hydrogen bond acceptor in these mixtures.

The predicted melting points for binary mixtures are substantially lower than those of the pure components, confirming the formation of DESs. Additionally, the eutectic temperature increases progressively from VAL < ENA < PEL. The LMN: PEL mixture displays higher melting points across all compositions compared to LMN: VAL, likely due to the increased molecular size and stronger van der Waals interactions in PEL. Such significant melting point depressions are indicative of strong intermolecular interactions, primarily via hydrogen bonding between menthol and the VAL, consistent with the characteristics of DESs. Although there is limited experimental data on the eutectic points of DESs, existing literature frequently mentions the use of 1:1 molar ratios of menthol and fatty acids, usually studied at room temperature. This observation aligns with COSMO-RS predictions, indicating that these systems can form liquid eutectic mixtures at room temperature or lower19,42.

The phase diagrams also reveal composition-dependent behavior. In the LMN: VAL system, the eutectic point occurs at x VAL > 0.6, whereas the 1:1 molar ratio yields the lowest eutectic temperatures for the ENA and PEL mixtures. This suggests that eutectic formation is favored when the mole fraction of HBD is lower than or equal to that of the HBA—a trend consistent with previous studies19,42. Overall, the COSMO-RS predictions are in strong agreement with available experimental trends and demonstrate the critical role of molecular size, polarity, surface charge distribution, and intermolecular interactions in governing eutectic behavior.

Activity coefficient at infinite Dilution

Benabid et al. previously demonstrated the use of COSMO-RS to calculate activity coefficients at infinite dilution for DES components at ambient conditions43. By employing a similar methodology, we acquired the activity coefficients at infinite dilution for HBD across various systems featuring a range of carbon atom counts in fatty acids. The activity coefficient at infinite dilution describes the thermodynamic non-ideality of a trace component dissolved in a solvent. COSMO-RS, when using standard symmetric normalization, provides activity coefficients relative to the pure liquid as the reference state. Thus, while i (pure) equals one, the activity coefficient at infinite dilution () of a solute in a solvent is typically not equal to one; it actually reflects the strength of the interactions between the solute and the solvent. The value offers valuable insights into the behavior of the FA molecule in the presence of menthol molecules, revealing a strikingly low concentration of FAs. For detailed findings, please refer to the computed values in Table S2.

As shown in Fig. 5, lower values of the ln indicate favorable interactions and good mutual miscibility between the components. For example, the calculated activity coefficient at infinite dilution for the LMN + VAL mixture is 0.261 at 298.15 K, which suggests high affinities of VAL towards menthol and relatively strong interactions. In contrast, for the LMN + PEL mixture, the activity coefficient rises to = 0.347, indicating comparatively weaker interactions. The order of increasing activity coefficients for acids is as follows: VAL (= 0.261) < ENA (= 0.294) < PEL ( = 0.347). The higher values observe in the LMN + PEL system indicate weaker solvation and limited interaction, driven by increased hydrophobic character and molecular bulk of the long-chain fatty acid. Consequently, this parameter offers a useful predictive tool for tailoring DES formulations with desired solvation and interaction properties.

Excess of enthalpy and Gibbs free energy

To investigate the thermodynamic mixing behavior of menthol with fatty acids (VAL, ENA, and PEL), we computed the excess enthalpy (Hᴱ) and excess Gibbs free energy (Gᴱ) at 298.15 K using the COSMO-RS model. Figure 6 (panel a) illustrates the predicted Hᴱ as a function of the fatty acid molar fraction (xFAs). All binary mixtures exhibit negative excess enthalpy values across the composition range, indicating exothermic mixing behavior. This suggests that the interactions between dissimilar components (HBA-HBD) are energetically more favorable than the interactions within similar species (HBA-HBA and HBD-HBD). These results confirm strong attractive interactions and good miscibility between fatty acids and menthol across all studied systems. A clear trend emerges with respect to fatty acid chain length: VAL < ENA < PEL. As the alkyl chain length increases, the magnitude of the negative Hᴱ decreases, suggesting that longer-chain fatty acids interact less strongly with menthol.

Thermodynamic properties of binary mixtures of L-menthol and selected fatty acids at T = 298.15 K, predicted by COSMO-RS: (a) Excess enthalpy (HE) as a function of FA molar fraction, (b) interaction energy contributions between L-menthol and FAs, and (c) excess Gibbs free energy (GE) of the studied DESs—MVAL, MENA, and MPEL.

To further dissect the molecular origins of these thermodynamic trends, we analyzed the individual interaction contributions to Hᴱ using the COSMO-RS framework: hydrogen bonding (HB), van der Waals (vdW), and electrostatic misfit (MF) interactions (see Fig. 6 (panel b)). All interactions are negative values and contribute to the negative total excess enthalpy, thereby favoring the mixing process. Among them, HB interactions dominate, as evidenced by more negative values relative to and . The MF contributions represent repulsive electrostatic interactions due to a mismatch in surface charge density. These repulsive effects are more pronounced for FAs with longer chains, which have greater charge density distributions and bulkier hydrophobic regions, leading to reduced accessibility of polar sites. Consequently, hydrogen bonding becomes less efficient, and tighter molecular packing occurs due to increased van der Waals forces and steric hindrance from methyl groups on the FAs chains.

The excess Gibbs free energy (Gᴱ) was also calculated using COSMO-RS at 298.15 K (Fig. 6 (panel c)), to further assess the thermodynamic stability of the binary systems. All mixtures exhibit negative or near-zero Gᴱ values, confirming that the formation of these DESs is thermodynamically favorable and spontaneous. The magnitude of Gᴱ is less negative than that of Hᴱ, which reflects the modulating effect of excess entropy (Sᴱ). Although Sᴱ contributes less significantly than enthalpy to the total Gibbs free energy, its negative values support the conclusion that intermolecular interactions between unlike components dominate over pure component interactions (e.g., LMN–LMN or FAs–FAs). The minimum Gᴱ occurs at xFAs=0.5, suggesting that equimolar mixtures represent the most stable configurations, consistent with typical DES compositions reported in the literature. A notable observation is that shorter-chain fatty acids yield more negative Gᴱ values, reinforcing the conclusion that alkyl chain length is inversely correlated with DES stability. This may be attributed to reduced steric hindrance and better hydrogen bonding compatibility with menthol in shorter-chain acids. The trend can be attributed to the entropy contribution from the fatty acid side chain and the relatively weaker interactions between different molecules compared to their pure-state interactions. According to the direct relationship between Gᴱ and the activity coefficient at infinite dilution (), the negative values observed for FAs support the corresponding negative Gᴱ values.

This trend highlights the significant influence of the fusion Gibbs energies of FAs on solubility parameter. Additionally, the SLE plot (Fig. 4) shifts closer to the origin as the alkyl chain length of FAs increases. In other words, the menthol solubility decreases with longer alkyl chain lengths in FAs. Reduced density gradient (RDG)

Johnson et al. introduced the powerful tool of non-covalent interaction (NCI) analysis, which brilliantly examines the distribution of electron density within molecular systems. By concentrating on areas characterized by low electron density and minimal gradient values, this innovative approach provides profound insights into the interactions that govern molecular behaviour45. Recently, a captivating new approach has emerged in the realm of theoretical strategies: the visualization of weak interactions through non-covalent interactions based on the reduced density gradient (RDG) method, which enables the visualization of the types and strengths of molecular interactions. The results of the NCI-RDG technique provide high accuracy and precision because it relies on fundamental computation. The 2D scatter plot displays the RDG as a function of electron density, multiplied by the sign of the second eigenvalue of the electron density Hessian. This representation can reveal various types of weak non-covalent interactions. In the 2D scatter plots, blue regions indicate strong attractive interactions (e.g., hydrogen bonding), green regions represent weaker van der Waals forces, and red regions correspond to steric repulsion. Complementary 3D RDG isosurface plots visualize the spatial distribution of these interactions throughout DES complexes. The reduced density gradient, along with the sign of the second principal local curvature of the electron density (sign (λ2) ρ), helps determine whether interactions are attractive or repulsive and allows for a qualitative and visual assessment of their relative strength. Figure 8 displays both the 2D scatter plots and 3D RDG isosurfaces for eutectic binary mixtures of L-menthol (HBA) and fatty acids (HBDs) at a 1:1 molar ratio. The prominent blue regions in the MVAL system indicate strong attractive interactions, predominantly conventional hydrogen bonds formed between the hydroxyl group of menthol and the carboxylic acid group of VAL. The green areas correspond to transitional zones associated with van der Waals interactions, mainly arising from H···H contacts. Further characterization of these interactions is provided by plotting the electron density (ρ(r)) multiplied by the sign of the second eigenvalue (λ₂) of the Hessian matrix, denoted as sign (λ₂) ρ, against the RDG. Regions with negative sign (λ₂)ρ values (typically < − 0.03 a.u.) signify strong hydrogen bonding, while positive values are indicative of repulsive electrostatic interactions. Van der Waals interactions are generally associated with values of sign (λ₂) ρ near zero. A high sign (λ₂) ρ value (~ + 0.02 a.u.) observed near the center of the menthol ring suggests a localized destabilizing interaction present across all mixtures. Among the studied systems, the MVAL mixture exhibits the most prominent hydrogen bonding interactions, evidenced by prominent spikes at sign(λ₂)ρ < − 0.03 a.u. and extensive blue regions on the RDG isosurface. In contrast, the MPEL system features more green and fewer blue regions, indicating a predominance of van der Waals interactions. This shift is attributed to the longer alkyl chains in PEL, which primarily contribute to dispersive interactions, as shown by the expansion of green areas. Overall, the RDG analysis confirms that hydrogen bonding plays a dominant role in the formation of eutectic mixtures between menthol and shorter-chain fatty acids, consistent with COSMO-RS predictions.

Radial distribution function

where p(r) is the average number of atom pairs at a distance between r and r + dr, N pairs is the number of atomic pairs, V is the total volume of the system, and g(r) is the radial distribution function47. In this study, RDFs were computed for all DESs composed of menthol and FAs at varying molar fractions and at a temperature of 298.15 K. Due to the direct relationship between structural organization and thermodynamic and transport properties, atom–atom RDFs were analyzed in detail, as illustrated in Fig. 9 and Figure S2. Fatty acids possess two key functional groups that actively participate in hydrogen bonding: the carbonyl oxygen of the carboxyl group (–C = O), acting as a hydrogen bond acceptor, and the hydroxyl hydrogen of the –COOH group, acting as a donor. The RDF of the hydroxyl hydrogen (H10) in menthol with respect to O1 in FAs exhibits a sharp peak at 1.65 Å, indicating a strong hydrogen bond. In contrast, the RDF peak corresponding to H10–O2 interactions appears at 4.65 Å, suggesting a much weaker, longer-range interaction. In all systems, the H10–O1 interaction occurs at a shorter distance, further confirming the preferential hydrogen bonding at the O1 site. The presence of a broad peak at longer distances in the RDFs suggests weak interactions, likely between hydrogen atoms covalently bonded to carbon atoms in FAs and the oxygen atoms of menthol. Conversely, sharp and intense peaks at shorter distances reflect strong interactions between donor and acceptor sites. The RDFs also show that the hydroxyl hydrogen of FAs forms strong hydrogen bonds with the oxygen atom (O1) of menthol, with a distinct peak at 1.65 Å. Fatty acids contain both acceptor (C = O) and donor (–OH) functional groups, which enable them to form hydrogen bonds in two distinct ways. In the case of menthol–fatty acid deep eutectic solvents, the main interaction occurs when the acidic hydroxyl hydrogen of the fatty acid donates a proton to the hydroxyl oxygen of menthol. This establishes fatty acids as HBDs and menthol as HBA. While menthol can act as a weak donor through its –OH group, it is significantly less effective compared to the carboxylic acid functionality of fatty acids. This conclusion is supported by the COSMO-RS σ-profile analysis. Further analysis of H(-OH)–O2 interactions within FAs shows a strong RDF peak at 1.65 Å, indicating intermolecular hydrogen bonding. Additional peaks at 2.35 Å and 3.05 Å likely arise from intramolecular interactions between H(-OH) and O2 in the same FA molecule. Figures 9 (panels c and d) display the probability distributions of hydroxyl hydrogens in FAs with respect to O1 of menthol and O2 of FAs, respectively. In panel c, a sharp peak at 1.65 Å is observed, with intensity varying based on FA concentration. Notably, for PEL, this peak reaches maximum intensity in DES8, indicating an optimal concentration for strong PEL–menthol hydrogen bonding. Figure 9 (panel d) illustrates RDFs between FA molecules. A consistent peak at ~ 1.65 Å is observed across molar compositions of PEL, signifying strong intermolecular FA–FA interactions. The variation in peak intensity with concentration suggests dynamic rearrangement in FA spatial distribution, with the highest probability occurring at pure PEL. Increasing FA content reduces the RDF peak intensity between L-menthol molecules, signifying weaker intermolecular interactions and decreased structural cohesion. Number of hydrogen bond

A hydrogen bond is formed when a donor atom, bearing an attached hydrogen atom, interacts with an acceptor atom that is not bonded to the donor. This interaction creates significant molecular connectivity. Hydrogen bonding requires that the distance between donor and acceptor atoms be ≤ 3.5 Å and that the donor–hydrogen–acceptor angle (∠D–H···A) be ≥ 150°51. The force field is widely used to essentially model hydrogen bonding as a purely electrostatic interaction: positive partial charges are placed on the proton and the acceptor base, and negative partial charges on the acceptor and donor atoms. The hydrogen bond modeled in this way is dominated by dipole-dipole interaction, and the energy of two dipoles is at a minimum when all four atoms are collinear. Additionally, the angle between points H and B in A-H…B is not straight, meaning that the interaction is not purely electrostatic, and that a partial degree of covalency exists. This effect can be understood in terms of the partial overlap of molecular orbitals, which are preferentially oriented along specific directions. Two electrons from each of the atoms are shared to forms a covalent bond between the two atoms, according to the valence bond theory, two orbitals overlap, allowing the two electrons to form a pair within the two overlapping orbitals. Therefore, both electrons now spend more time between the two nuclei and thus hold the atoms together. The two electrons must now occupy a shared orbital space. When the two nuclei be too far apart, their respective orbitals cannot overlap, thus no covalent bond can form. As they move closer and closer together, orbital overlap begins to occur, and a bond begins to form. This lowers the potential energy of the system, attractive positive-negative electrostatic interactions become possible between the nucleus of one atom and the electron of the second. As the atoms get closer, the repulsive positive-positive interaction between the two nuclei also begins to increase. The number of hydrogen bonds between respective atoms in the studied DESs was evaluated using RDF data. A Gaussian distribution was fitted to each DES system to statistically describe hydrogen bonding, using Gnuplot version 5.452. The normal distribution function is given by Eq. (3), where a is the amplitude, σ is the standard deviation, and µ is the mean53.

Non-bonded energy analysis

The non-bonded interaction energies are composed of electrostatic and van der Waals (vdW) components, which together determine the strength of intermolecular interactions between different molecular species. The interaction energies were calculated using the NAMD energy plugins within the VMD package57, focusing on the final 10 ns of the MD simulation trajectory. Table 2 presents the total non-bonded, electrostatic, and van der Waals interaction energies for the various binary mixtures studied. The results clearly indicate that electrostatic interactions are the primary contributors to total non-bonded interaction energies, while vdW contributions are comparatively negligible. In the VAL–menthol mixture, the total non-bonded interaction energy is composed of a strong electrostatic contribution of − 64.280 kcal/mol and a minor vdW contribution of 0.288 kcal/mol. The molecular geometry and surface charge distribution are key factors affecting vdW interactions: molecules with larger sizes and broader charge distributions typically exhibit higher vdW energy values. Moreover, the electronegativity difference between bonded atoms plays a significant role in determining the magnitude of electrostatic energy. These findings underscore the dominant role of electrostatic interactions in the overall non-bonded interaction energy between mixture components. The shorter alkyl chain and lower steric hindrance of VAL may further enhance these electrostatic effects.

Dipole moment and orientation angle distribution of dipole vector

One of the primary indicators of polarity is the molecular dipole moment (µ), which reflects the spatial distribution of electrical charges within a molecule. The dipole moment of DES components is essential for predicting solubility, which plays a key role in green chemistry and solvent design. By adjusting the molar ratios, DESs can be customized for specific polarities and uses58. The average dipole moments for each component were extracted from the MD simulation trajectories and statistically fitted using Eq. 3. These average values and their corresponding standard deviations are summarized in Table 3, representing Gaussian-like distributions across all molecular configurations. The results in Table 3 show that all components in the binary mixtures possess non-zero dipole moments. This implies that the molecules adopt favorable orientations, enabling effective intermolecular interactions and polarization. Two main factors influence the dipole moment of a molecule: (1) changes in atomic charge and (2) modifications to the molecule’s geometry. For example, in a water molecule, even a slight alteration in the angle between its atoms can significantly impact its overall dipole moment. Additionally, variations in the bond length between hydrogen and oxygen can also affect the dipole moment, even if the atomic charges remain constant. In a non-polarizable force field, geometric changes—such as adjustments in bond lengths, angles, and dihedral angles—can lead to fluctuations in the dipole moment. Therefore, to effectively understand the orientation of the molecule, it is essential to focus on these geometric modifications within the non-polarizable force field. This highlights the important role that geometry plays in molecular behavior and interactions. Across all binary mixtures, the dipole moments of FAs are notably higher than those of menthol, with FA values nearly twice as large. This significant difference indicates that the mutual solubility of the two components is likely governed primarily by hydrogen bonding. These findings are consistent with predictions from the COSMO-RS model. This observation supports the hypothesis that the hydroxyl (-OH) groups of FAs are accessible for hydrogen bonding with menthol molecules. For example, the dipole moment of the H(-OH)--O2 bond in pure VAL is calculated to be 2.752600244 D. In the presence of menthol, this value increases slightly to 2.752600288 D in mixtures containing nFAs=500, indicating enhanced polarization due to interactions with the HBA component. In the MVAL binary system, the average dipole moment of the H(-OH)FAs–O1LMN bond remains at 2.752600288 D, whereas it decreases to 2.729277203 D in MPEL mixtures with longer alkyl chains. This reduction is likely due to reorientation of the H(-OH)FAs–O1LMN bond vectors in response to the HBD environment. These results further confirm the strong interaction between menthol and VAL in the binary system. The relative errors were approximately 8% and 6%, respectively, which fall within acceptable margins given the inherent challenges in simulating viscosity in complex DES systems. The observed viscosity trends are attributed to two complementary molecular mechanisms: (1) the concentration-dependent decrease in viscosity is primarily governed by hydrogen bonding networks among menthol molecules, and (2) the chain-length-dependent increase in viscosity arises from stronger van der Waals interactions as FA alkyl chains become longer. Overall, the molecular dynamics simulations not only replicate experimental viscosity trends with satisfactory accuracy but also validate the reliability of the employed computational methodology for predicting transport properties in hydrophobic NADESs.

Density

In this study, the densities of mixtures composed of menthol and various FAs were determined at 298.15 K using data extracted from MD simulations and the COSMO-RS model (Table 6). The density of binary mixtures is governed by the concentration, chemical nature, and molecular size of the HBD and HBA components. Additionally, the presence of voids or free volume within DESs can significantly affect their density. A consistent trend observed in this study is the decrease in density with increasing molar percentage of menthol. As shown in Table 6, mixtures containing nVAL=667 exhibit notably higher densities. This can be attributed to the more compact molecular packing of HBD molecules around the HBA, which results in a denser structural arrangement. Such packing effects are crucial in determining the macroscopic properties of the mixture. The alkyl chain length of FAs also plays a critical role in shaping the physical characteristics of DESs. An inverse relationship is observed between alkyl chain length and mixture density. This trend is clearly illustrated in the order of densities among the studied systems: MVAL > MENA > MPEL (Fig. 17). For instance, MPEL displays a lower density compared to MVAL and MENA, suggesting a greater amount of free volume within the binary liquid mixture. The reduction in density with longer FA chains is primarily attributed to steric hindrance, which disrupts the efficiency of hydrogen bond formation. Weakened hydrogen bonding interactions reduce molecular cohesion and increase the free volume in the system, leading to lower overall density. The simulation results presented in this work are in strong agreement with available experimental data, thereby confirming the reliability and robustness of the employed computational methodologies for modelling physical properties of binary mixtures.

Validation of force field

The manuscript presents viscosity data for mixtures of menthol and fatty acids, although some of this information may already be available in the existing literature. As seen from Fig. 16, panel b, the predicted viscosities for the nFAs=667 of enanthic acid and pelargonic acid were 16.05 mPa·s and 17.19 mPa·s, respectively. These values align closely with experimental data reported by Shishov et al.62, who measured viscosities of 14.8 mPa·s and 18.3 mPa·s for comparable systems containing 66.7% mole of FAs. The relative errors were approximately 8% and 6%, respectively, which fall within acceptable margins given the inherent challenges in simulating viscosity in complex DES systems. Importantly, experimental data are scarce in the literature regarding other parameters related to these mixtures. To address this gap, we have carefully provided the standard deviations of these parameters across various systems and mixtures, thereby enhancing the comprehensiveness of our study.

Gurtovenko et al. conducted an intriguing study on the viscosity and diffusion coefficient of n-eicosane samples in the liquid state, employing several cutting-edge force fields, including CHARMM 36, at a temperature of 450 K. Their findings revealed that the viscosity values, derived from the CHARMM 36 force field, were impressively close to experimental measurements, with figures of 0.60 ± 0.04 mPa·s and 0.594 mPa·s, respectively. Additionally, the diffusion coefficients for n-eicosane samples showed values of 2.1 cm²/s within the CHARMM 36 framework, compared to 2.2 cm²/s obtained experimentally. These results highlight that the CHARMM 36 force field not only aligns closely with experimental data but also exemplifies remarkable accuracy, underscoring its efficacy in simulating the behavior of complex molecular systems63.

Kirkwood-Buff analysis was performed on aqueous methanol solutions modeled using the CHARMM force field. The results showed that the experimental KBIs were well reproduced over the entire concentration range by the CHARMM force field, except for a somewhat large overestimation for the mole fraction of xc(methanol) = 0.125 for Gcc. The partial molar volumes calculated using the CHARMM force field demonstrated remarkable concordance with experimental data, depicted as a function of menthol mole fraction in the manuscript. Furthermore, the isothermal compressibility of the methanol was in good agreement with experiment at the medium and high concentrations64.

Conclusion

In this study, we provided a comprehensive understanding of DESs based on menthol and selected fatty acids (VAL, ENA, and PEL) by combining MD simulations, COSMO-RS predictions, and KBI analyses. The synergistic application of these approaches revealed that hydrogen bonding, van der Waals interactions, and the effects of fatty acid chain length govern the structural organization and thermodynamic stability of these systems.

MD simulations have clarified how the length of fatty acid chains influences the microscopic arrangement, diffusion dynamics, and heterogeneity of liquid structures. Additionally, COSMO-RS provided predictions for activity coefficients and solvation tendencies. The KBI framework offered quantitative insights into preferential interactions, partial molar volumes, and compressibility, effectively linking molecular organization to macroscopic thermodynamic properties.

Overall, this comprehensive study establishes a rigorous approach to connecting the structure, dynamics, and thermodynamics of DESs across multiple scales. A comparison of the excess volume obtained from MD simulations with that predicted by COSMO-RS showed good agreement.

Our findings emphasize that subtle variations in fatty acid composition can be utilized to fine-tune the physicochemical properties of menthol-based DESs for specific applications in green chemistry, separations, and biocompatible formulations. Furthermore, the integrated use of MD simulations, COSMO-RS, and the KBI strategy provides a transferable protocol for the rational design and predictive screening of emerging DESs with tailored functionalities, extending beyond the specific systems studied.
