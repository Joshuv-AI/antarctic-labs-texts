Idiopathic radial artery pseudoaneurysm: A rare case in a renal transplant recipient – A case report
Authors: Ali M Bayrouti, P Régine Mydlarski, Paul F Petrasek, Fatemeh Jafarian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDistal radial artery pseudoaneurysm is an uncommon vascular abnormality, usually occurring after trauma or iatrogenic arterial injury. Spontaneous cases are rare and have been reported primarily as isolated case reports in the general population. To our knowledge, no other published reports describe this presentation, especially within a dermatologic context among solid organ transplant recipients. We describe a 55-year-old immunocompromised male presenting with a 2-month history of a progressively enlarging, pulsatile nodule with overlying erythema and skin thinning on the surface of the right radial wrist. Physical examination revealed a subtle pulsatile subcutaneous mass. Biopsy was deferred, and the patient was referred for diagnostic imaging, which revealed a distal radial artery pseudoaneurysm. Vascular surgery was consulted, and the patient underwent urgent surgical resection with an unremarkable recovery. This case highlights that vascular lesions can mimic benign, inflammatory, or other soft tissue nodules in dermatologic practice. Carefulpalpation and imaging are essential when evaluating atypical wrist masses to avoid biopsy-related hemorrhage and ensure safe management.Keywords: radial artery pseudoaneurysm, transplant dermatology, vascular lesions in dermatology, immunosuppression, wrist mass

Introduction

True aneurysms are defined as permanent, localized dilatations of an artery that involve all three layers of the arterial wall, whereas pseudoaneurysms represent a contained rupture of the arterial wall with blood extravasation confined by surrounding tissue. In pseudoaneurysms, blood escapes through a breach in the arterial wall and is contained by perivascular tissue rather than by all three vascular layers. 1

Radial artery pseudoaneurysm (RAP) is a rare but clinically significant vascular pathologic entity, accounting for <3% of upper-extremity aneurysms (Ho, Weiland and McClinton, 1987). Most RAPs arise secondary to trauma, iatrogenic injury (i.e., arterial cannulation), or infection (Rivera and Dattilo, 2024). 2 Distal RAPs occurring without an identifiable precipitant are exceedingly rare, and their recognition can be challenging.3,4

Clinically, RAPs typically present as a pulsatile swelling in the wrist or forearm, occasionally accompanied by pain or distal ischemic symptoms. Presentations can be variable, and some cases may be asymptomatic. Diagnosis is frequently delayed, particularly when no clear precipitating event is identified. 5 Despite this variability, these lesions carry significant risks, including thrombus formation, distal embolization, rupture, and local mass effect, highlighting the importance of timely recognition and management.3,5,6

Given the rarity of distal RAPs occurring without traumatic or iatrogenic causes, evidence to guide their evaluation and management remains limited. Reporting such cases is therefore essential for enhancing clinical awareness and informing the development of evidence-based strategies for the management of these uncommon presentations.

Herein, we report a rare case of a distal RAP in an immunocompromised patient that was managed with surgical resection, highlighting the importance of vigilant physical examination, early imaging, and multidisciplinary care.

Case report

A 55-year-old Caucasian man living with end-stage renal disease secondary to IgA nephropathy presented to our transplant dermatology clinic. He was receiving peritoneal dialysis following a failed renal transplant. His medical history was notable for hypertension and long-term post-transplant immunosuppression with tacrolimus and prednisone. He reported a 2-month history of an enlarging mass on the volar radial aspect of his right wrist, accompanied by overlying erythema and skin thinning (Figure 1). The patient had no history of preceding trauma, instrumentation, arterial lines, endovascular procedures, or infection to the wrist. He also denied constitutional symptoms.

Initial presentation, right wrist.

On examination, a 1.5-cm pulsatile, non-tender subcutaneous nodule was noted. Given its suspected vascular etiology, biopsy was deferred. Doppler ultrasound revealed a saccular pseudoaneurysm arising from the distal radial artery with partial thrombus, measuring 3.0 × 2.5 × 3.5 cm. CT angiography confirmed a 3.5-cm pseudoaneurysm with axial dimensions of 4.3 × 4.8 cm (Figure 2).

(a) Right wrist ultrasound. (b) Right wrist CT.

Our patient was referred to vascular surgery. Intraoperative exploration revealed a pseudoaneurysm arising from a partial disruption of a calcified radial artery, containing organized thrombus. A 6–7 mm defect was repaired via direct end-to-end anastomosis without the need for grafting. Perfusion remained intact postoperatively, with no complications at 6-month follow-up.

Discussion

In our patient, chronic immunosuppression, end-stage renal disease, and systemic inflammation likely contributed to arterial wall fragility and impaired vascular repair.1,7 These factors may predispose to pseudoaneurysm formation even in the absence of overt injury, although the underlying pathogenesis remains incompletely understood. Proposed mechanisms include intrinsic degenerative changes, subclinical vascular inflammation, and systemic disease-mediated compromise of arterial wall integrity.7,8

In dermatology, a rapidly enlarging subcutaneous nodule in an immunocompromised patient warrants a broad differential diagnosis, including infectious, inflammatory, and neoplastic etiologies. 9 Due to the rarity of idiopathic distal RAPs, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for vascular lesions in atypical subcutaneous masses of the wrist. Though biopsies are often performed to confirm a diagnosis, they should be avoided when an arterial vascular lesion is suspected, as they carry a risk of catastrophic hemorrhage. Dermatologists and clinicians should remain mindful that vascular lesions can mimic benign or inflammatory nodules and ensure that pulse palpation or Doppler assessment is performed prior to any invasive diagnostic procedure. Imaging modalities such as duplex ultrasound, often demonstrating bidirectional (“yin-yang”) flow within the sac, and computed tomography angiography are key to establishing the diagnosis and guiding management.1,3

Therapeutically, intervention is often recommended for pseudoaneurysms given the risk of complications, though the optimal approach can vary depending on lesion characteristics. 1 Early recognition is therefore critical to prevent iatrogenic harm and ensure timely diagnosis and safe management. Once a RAP is confirmed, urgent referral to vascular surgery is critical to ensure safe management and prevent morbidity.

This case underscores the importance of including vascular lesions, such as pseudoaneurysms, in the differential diagnosis of atypical or pulsatile subcutaneous nodules. Even though idiopathic RAPs are exceedingly rare, they should be considered in appropriate clinical contexts, particularly in immunocompromised patients. Prompt recognition and appropriate imaging, with avoidance of biopsy, can prevent serious complications and support safe, patient-centered dermatologic care.
