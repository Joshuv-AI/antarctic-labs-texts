Identification of 5mC regulatory factors mediating methylation patterns and prognosis models in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Yanwei Li, Yunfeng Cao, Nuerbiyamu Maimaitiyiming, Yue Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study explores the role of 5mC regulatory genes in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), focusing on their association with tumor progression, metastasis, and prognosis. A prognostic model was also developed to predict patient survival.Materials and methodsUsing expression profile data from the GEO and TCGA databases, the study analyzed the differential expression of 5mC-related genes, their connection with somatic mutations, and their impact on the immune microenvironment. A prognostic risk model based on these genes was constructed through the differential expression of 5mC-related genes.Results(1) Several 5mC regulatory factors were found to be abnormally expressed in HNSCC, with a significant association with somatic mutations. (2) Different 5mC methylation regulation patterns showed variations in immune infiltration, immune checkpoint expression, tumor mutation burden, and cancer stem cell gene set expression, potentially influencing immunotherapy effectiveness and prognosis. (3) Prognosis-related risk genes, such as DNMT1 and TET2, were identified as predictors of survival outcomes in HNSCC patients.ConclusionsThe methylation patterns of 5mC regulatory factors are critical in HNSCC progression and are closely linked to tumor microenvironment infiltration. These findings offer new perspectives for treatment strategies and prognosis prediction in HNSCC.Keywords: Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, DNA methylation, Tumor microenvironment, Immune infiltration

Background

Malignant tumors of the head and neck mainly include cancers of the lips, mouth, larynx, nasopharynx, oropharynx, and hypopharynx. There were about 878,250 new cases of such malignancies reported globally in 2020, and such malignancies are expected to increase by 30% by 2030 [1]. Of these tumors, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) accounts for 90% [2]. For patients with early-stage HNSCC, the main treatments are surgery, and radiotherapy combined with chemotherapy, which have good therapeutic effects [3]. However, more than 60% of HCSCC patients are already at a locally advanced stage or metastatic stage at the time of definitive diagnosis due to the insidious development of the disease. After receiving standards of care, patients with locally advanced non-metastatic HNSCC had tumor recurrence rates between 30% and 60%, while those with distant metastasis had recurrence rates between 4% and 26%, with a 5-year survival rate < 40% [4]. Therefore, the diagnosis and treatment of HNSCC remain a great challenge.

Epigenetics focuses on how genes are regulated to make heritable changes without changes in the sequence of deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA). Currently, the known epigenetic modifications (EMs) mainly include DNA methylation (DNAm), RNA methylation, histone modification, and non-coding RNA regulation [5–8]. EMs are a crucial player in cell proliferation, differentiation, and development, and influence susceptibility to certain diseases. DNAm is the most widespread EM in the human research area. Typically, DNAm is caused by the replacement of H on C or N of cytosine and adenine in a DNA sequence by CH3, thereby leading to various types of methylation, e.g., 5-Methylcytosine (5mC), N6-Methyladenine (6 mA), and N4-Methylcytosine (4mC) [9–11]. 5mC is a DNA modification resulting from the transfer of methyl from S-adenosylmethionine as a methyl donor to the C-5 position on the cytosine ring in unmethylated Cytosine-phosphate-Guanine (CpG) dinucleotides under the catalytic action of DNMT [11]. 5mC is a main player in such processes as transcriptional regulation of genes and cell differentiation. It is important for biological functions. In mammalian genomic DNA, 5mC is the most abundant type of methylation, making up 3%−5% of total cytosine [12].

Regulation of 5mC is dynamically controlled by three main regulators, namely “writers”, “readers”, and “erasers”, which add specific modifications, recognize and bind modified nucleotides, and remove specific modifications, respectively [12, 13]. This regulation process of 5mC is influenced by the expression levels of related regulatory genes. Currently, numerous studies have demonstrated the important role of DNAm change in tumor progression and metastasis [14–16]. However, currently, there are few studies on the impact of 5mC regulators on the progression, metastasis, and prognosis of HNSCC. Therefore, this study intended to comprehensively evaluate the correlation of the mRNA expression levels of 5mC regulators with TME heterogeneity, immunophenotypes, clinicopathological characteristics, and treatment of HNSCC. Then, a new 5mC regulator-mediated scoring system was developed, and a 5mC-based prognosis model was generated to evaluate the prognosis of HNSCC.

Materials and methods

Downloading and preprocessing of data

The GSE41613 dataset on gene expression spectra in HNSCC was downloaded from the GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) using the GEO query package of the R software (version 4.3.1, http://r-project.org/) [17] based on GPL 570 [Hg-U133 _ Plus _ 2] Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 Array. Gene expression data, mutation data, and clinical data of TCGA-HNSCC were downloaded based on UCSC Xena (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/). The TCGA-HNSCC dataset included 494 HNSCC samples and 44 paraneoplastic samples. The GSE41613 dataset included 97 HNSCC samples. Data annotation and RMA normalization of the GSE41613 dataset were performed through the hgu133plus2.db package and the affy package. The mRNA expression matrix in HNSCC was obtained after annotation of the TCGA-HNSCC and GSE41613 datasets using mRNAs listed in the HUGO Gene Nomenclature Committee (HGNC) (http://www.genenames.org/) [18] database. 21 5mC-related genes were downloaded from a previous study [13], including a total of 3 writers (DNMT3A, DNMT3B, DNMT1), 4 erasers (TET1, TET2, TET3, TDG) and 14 readers (MBD1, MBD2, MBD3, MBD4, MECP2, NEIL1, NTHL1, SMUG1, UHRF1, UHRF2, UNG, ZBTB33, ZBTB38, ZBTB4). Two independent cohorts of treatment-naïve patients were analyzed. The TCGA-HNSCC cohort included 494 patients (age range: 41–90 years; male: 70.2%), distributed across tumor stages: Stage I (n = 17), Stage II (n = 81), Stage III (n = 90), Stage IVA (n = 290), Stage IVB (n = 13), Stage IVC (n = 3). The GSE41613 cohort included 97 patients (age range: 36–85 years; male: 73.1%), with stages: Stage I (n = 7), Stage II (n = 11), Stage III (n = 20), Stage IVA (n = 56), Stage IVB (n = 2), Stage IVC (n = 1).

Landscape map of 5mC regulatory genes in HNSCC

The cancer and normal cases in the TCGA-HNSCC dataset were subjected to differential gene expression analysis. Using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test in R, 5mC-related genes with significant differences in expression were obtained, with significant expression differences at p < 0.05. The correlation heatmap was plotted with the corrplot package for correlation analysis to visualize the link between 5mC and related genes. SNP analysis of 5mC-related genes was performed using the maftool package [19].

Consensus clustering analysis based on 5mC regulatory genes

Consensus clustering [20], a common method for cancer subtyping, can be used to distinguish samples into several subtypes based on different histological datasets, thereby detecting new subtypes or comparatively analyzing different subtypes. While traditional cancer subtyping relies on histological features (e.g., tissue architecture, cell morphology), our approach leverages molecular profiles of 5mC regulatory genes to define subtypes. Unlike histological classification, which groups tumors based on visual characteristics, consensus clustering of the 5mC-related gene matrix captures functionally distinct epigenetic states that may transcend histological boundaries. This enables: (i) Deeper stratification of HNSCC beyond conventional histology, (ii) Identification of subtypes with differential clinical outcomes (e.g., survival, metastasis risk), and (iii) Mechanistic insights into epigenetic drivers of tumor behavior (e.g., immune evasion, stemness).A consensus clustering algorithm was used for consensus clustering of the TCGA-HNSCC samples based on the 5mC-related gene matrix to select the best clusters. The optimal cluster number (k = 3) was determined by: (1) Maximizing the area under the cumulative distribution function (CDF) curve; (2) Minimal increase in CDF delta area (k = 2 to k = 10); (3) Consensus matrix heatmap showing clear segregation (Fig. 1D–E).

Analysis of biological heterogeneity among 5mC-related subtypes

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) of different subtypes were screened by the limma package [21]. DEGs met the criteria P < 0.05 and |log2FC|>1. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted on DEGs among the subtypes by the clusterProfiler package to explore the biological functions among the subtypes. Differences in ESTIMATE score, immune score, stromal score, and tumor purity among the subtypes were compared to delve into their differences in the degree of immune infiltration. CIBERSORT [22] uses linear support vector regression (SVR) for the deconvolution of the expression matrix of a transcriptome to estimate the composition and abundance of immune cells in a mixture of cells. GSEA was performed using Hallmark (v7.5.1), KEGG (2021 release), and GO Biological Process (2021) gene sets from MSigDB (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org). The source code and files corresponding to immune cells were downloaded from the CIBERSORT website, and an infiltration matrix of immune cells was derived in R according to their gene expression profiles in TCGG-HNSCC and the immune cell files. Then, 28 immune cells were analyzed for differences among the subtypes based on the ssGSEA algorithm. The R package maftools was used to process the TCGA somatic mutation data, GISTIC 2.0 was used to process the gene copy number data, and an oncoplot was drawn to show the distribution of mutations among the subtypes. A set of 26 cancer stemness genes was downloaded from StemChecker (http://stemchecker.sysbiolab.eu/) [23]. Cancer stemness scores were calculated based on the ssGSEA method and compared among the subtypes. Three algorithms for samples were obtained from the TCGA database: Buffa, Ragnum, and Winter hypoxia scoring systems.

GSEA of genes among 5mC-related subtypes

GSEA [24] was used to assess the trend of the distribution trend of genes from a predefined gene set in a gene list ranked based on their relevance to a phenotype, thus determining how genes contribute to the phenotype. All DEGs among TCGG-HNSCC subtypes were subjected to GSEA using the cluster Profiler package. The parameters used in GSEA were as follows: 1000 calculations, at least 10, and at most 500 genes in each gene set. The P value was adjusted by the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method. P < 0.05 and FDR < 0.05 indicated that a gene set was significantly enriched. A gseaplot was drawn to visualize functional enrichment results.

Set-up and validation of a prognosis model related to 5mC

StepAIC is a stepwise regression analysis that uses the AIC to remove or add variables by selecting the smallest AIC. The 5mC-related genes in TCGG-HNSCC were screened by stepAIC (multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis), and finally, 5mC-related prognostic genes in TCGG-HNSCC were obtained. The obtained 5mC-related prognostic genes were subjected to the multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis. A prognostic risk assessment model was established based on the coefficients of the multivariate regression model. Using the established model, the prognostic risk was scored for each sample from TCGA (training cohort) or GSE41613 (validation cohort). The median prognostic risk score in a patient was expressed as a cutoff, based on which high- and low-risk groups of patients were established. They were subjected to survival analysis with data visualized by survival and survminer packages in R. Patients were further ranked by prognostic risk scores to explore the changes in survival information as risk scores increased, and a linkage map of risk factors was constructed. Clinical characteristics with null data in TCGG-HNSCC were removed with R to obtain relevant clinical characteristics. Then univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were conducted for the prognostic risk scores and clinical characteristics of patients in TCGA to explore their prognostic factors. The results were visualized using forestplot in R.

Statistical analysis

Data were all processed and statistically analyzed by R version 4.3.1. R-survivalROC was employed to plot ROC curves of genes for patient survival time and survival status. The diagnostic impact of gene expression on patient survival was assessed using the area under the curve (AUC). Indicators including gene expression levels were comparatively analyzed by the wilcox.test or kruskal.test in the R package ggpubr (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001). 5mC-related genes were subjected to correlation analysis by the Pearson’s correlation coefficient with the R package corrplot. 0.00–0.19 (very weak), 0.20–0.39 (weak), 0.40–0.59 (moderate), 0.60–0.79 (strong), and 0.80–1.0 (very strong) were used as absolute values to determine the strength of the correlation. Differences among KM survival curves were shown by log-rank p values calculated by the survival R package. A log-rank P < 0.05 for KM denoted a significantly different survival outcome.

Results

Differences between HNSCC and paracancerous tissues with respect to the acquisition and expression of 5mC regulatory genes

Firstly, gene expression data, mutation data, and clinical data of TCGA-HNSCC were downloaded from the UCSC Xena website. The gene expression profiles of GSE41613 were downloaded from the GEO website. After processing, it was identified that 17,098 mRNAs were expressed in 538 samples from the TCGA dataset; 17,167 mRNAs were expressed in 97 samples from the GSE41613 dataset. Subsequently, 21 5mC regulatory genes were downloaded from the reference [13]. In tumor tissues compared with normal samples, DNMT3A, DNMT3B, DNMT1, TET1, TET3, TDG, MBD1, MBD3, MBD4, MECP2, NTHL1, SMUG1, UHRF1, UHRF2, UNG, ZBTB38, and ZBTB33 were significantly highly expressed (p < 0.05); TET2 and NEIL1 were significantly downregulated (p = 1.2e − 4 and p = 0.008, respectively).; the differences in the expression of MBD2 and ZBTB4 were not significant (Fig. 1A). Mutation in 5mC regulatory genes in HNSCC was analyzed by the R package maftools (Fig. 1B). The results showed that missense mutations were the most predominant type of mutation, with DNMT3B, DNMT3A, and TET1 having higher mutation rates. The heatmap showed a significantly positive association of UHRF1 with DNMT1, a significantly positive association of MECP2 with ZBTB33, a significantly positive association of UNG with DNMT1 and UHRF1, and a significantly negative association of NTHL1 with ZBTB38 (Fig. 1C). The ConsensusClusterPlus package in R was used for consensus clustering of the gene expression profiles in the TCGG-HNSCC group based on the twenty-one 5mC regulatory genes. The best clustering effect was achieved at k = 3 (Fig. 1D–E). Subtypes A, B, and C were obtained. Among them, Subtype A contained 252 samples, Subtype B contained 158 samples, and Subtype C contained 55 samples. The relationship between different subtypes and clinical characteristics was explored based on the expression profile data and clinical information from 494 TCGA-HNSCC cases with complete clinical data. In the gene expression heatmap, 5mC regulatory genes in Subtype A mostly had slightly lower mRNA expression levels. In Subtype B, TET1 had an increased mRNA expression level, while the remaining genes mostly had slightly higher expression levels. In Subtype C, the mRNAs of MBD3 and NTHL1 were increased, and the mRNA expression levels of TDG, ZBTB38, and TET3 were decreased (Fig. 1F). After consensus clustering, principal component analysis (PCA) of 5mC-related genes showed significant differences among Subtypes A, B, and C (Fig. 1G). These results suggested significant differences in clinical and pathological characteristics among the three 5mC regulatory modes (Subtypes A, B, and C).

Analysis of the 5mC regulatory modes and immune microenvironment in HNSCC

As shown in the box plot of 22 immune cells different in infiltration (Fig. 2A), the degree of immune infiltration of M1 macrophages and neutrophils was the highest in Subtype A; follicular helper T (Tfh) cells, eosinophils, and activated mast cells (AMCs) had the highest degree of immune infiltration in Subtype B; regulatory T cells, activated NK cells, M2 macrophages, and resting dendritic cells had the highest degree of immune infiltration in Subtype C. As illustrated in the box plot of the degree of enrichment of 28 immune cells (Fig. 2B), basically, all immune cells except activated dendritic cells, immature B lymphocytes, and NK cells were significantly differentially enriched among the three subtypes, with macrophages having a significantly higher immune enrichment score in Subtype C.

Immune cells and stromal cells, two main types of non-tumor components in the tumor microenvironment (TME), have been shown to be of great value for diagnosis and prognostic assessment of tumors. Regarding the three subtypes, the ESTIMATE algorithm was employed to obtain their ESTIMATE score, immune score, and stromal score. The violin plot showed that the Subtype C group had the highest immune score, and the Subtype B group had higher tumor purity (Fig. 2C).

Immune checkpoints are immunosuppressive molecules expressed on immune cells, and can modulate the degree of immune activation. They are a crucial player in preventing autoimmune reactions. Immunotherapy with immune checkpoints can regulate T cell activity in a variety of ways, including co-inhibition or co-stimulation of signals, to destroy tumor cells. Differences in immune checkpoints among the subtypes were analyzed. The box plot (Fig. 2D–E) showed that CD70, CD276, NRP1, PDCD1, PDCD1LG2, TNFSF14, and TNFSF15 were significantly differentially expressed among the subtypes.

Human leukocyte antigen (HLA) is the result of coding for the HLA gene complex. HLA is located on the short arm of Chromosome 6. It is an important genetic marker that is closely related to the body’s immune system. HLA can be categorized into HLA-I, HLA-II, and HLA-III. Of them, HLA-I (classical types: HLA-A, HLA-B, and HLA-C) presents endogenous antigenic fragments primarily on the cell surface, which are presented to CD8 + T lymphocytes (cytotoxic T lymphocytes), thereby activating them to carry out cell-mediated immune responses to destroy infected or abnormal cells. HLA-II (including HLA-DP, HLA-DQ, HLA-DR) displays exogenous antigenic fragments, such as those of microorganisms including bacteria and fungi, primarily located on professional antigen-presenting cells (APCs) (including macrophages (MPs), B lymphocytes, and dendritic cells (DCs)), which are presented to CD4 + T lymphocytes, thereby triggering their immune responses and coordinating the involvement of other immune cells. The Subtype C group had the highest HLA gene expression level (Fig. 2F).

Tumor markers in the 5mC regulatory modes in HNSCC

Hypoxia characteristics are closely linked to tumor proliferation, differentiation, angiogenesis, energy metabolism, drug resistance in cancer, and patient prognosis. All hypoxia scores obtained by Buffa, Ragnum, and Winter differed significantly among the 5mC regulatory modes (Fig. 3A). Cancer stem cells have the ability to proliferate unlimitedly, metastasize, and resist damage by chemical toxicants. This ability is called cancer cell stemness, and can be understood as common characteristics shared between cancer stem cells and stem cells. However, cancer stem cells proliferate out of control, lose the ability to differentiate normally, and form heterogeneous tumors after metastasis to tissues, thereby destroying the functions of normal tissues and organs. The majority of a set of 26 cancer stemness genes was highly enriched in Subtype B (Fig. 3B). Subtype B had higher tumor mutational burden (TMB), microsatellite instability (MSI), fraction genome altered (FGA), and an aneuploidy score (AS) (Fig. 3C). These results demonstrated stronger mutational activity in Subtype B tumors. Tumor mutation patterns were significantly different among the three 5mC regulatory modes (Fig. 3D). The oncogene TP53 had the highest mutation frequency in Subtype A, followed by Subtype C, and the lowest mutation frequency in Subtype B. The oncogene PIK3CA had a higher mutation frequency in Subtype A and Subtype B. The expression profiles in TCGA-HNSCC in the three 5mC regulatory modes were subjected to GSEA by the clusterProfiler package. P value < 0.05 and FDR < 0.05 were used as thresholds to screen for significant enrichment. GSEA showed that Subtype A was mainly linked to DNA replication, cell cycle, Fanconi anemia pathway, and platinum resistance; Subtype B was enriched primarily in the extracellular matrix (ECM) − receptor interaction pathway, proteoglycans, focal adhesions (FAs), as well as actin cytoskeleton regulation; Subtype C was mainly enriched in tight junctions, ferroptosis, and cancer pathways (Fig. 3E).

Construction of a prognostic risk model based on 5mC regulatory genes

A multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression model was established based on 21 5mC-related regulatory genes and optimized using the stepAIC method, with the optimization direction being “both”, to obtain the optimal model. A total of 7 prognostic risk-related genes were identified: DNMT1, TET2, MBD3, UHRF1, UHRF2, ZBTB4, and ZBTB33. The model was expressed as risk score = (−0.4864) * DNMT1 + (−0.3794) * TET2 + (−0.3587) * MBD3 + (0.2558) * UHRF1 + (0.1857) * UHRF2 + (−0.3342) * ZBTB4 + (0.4693) * ZBTB33. Multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed the independent prognostic impact of each gene (Supplementary Table 1): Protective factors (HR < 1): DNMT1 (HR = 0.615, 95% CI: 0.502–0.754), TET2 (HR = 0.684, 95% CI: 0.548–0.854), MBD3 (HR = 0.698, 95% CI: 0.581–0.840), and ZBTB4 (HR = 0.716, 95% CI: 0.602–0.851). Risk factors (HR > 1): UHRF1 (HR = 1.291, 95% CI: 1.102–1.514), UHRF2 (HR = 1.204, 95% CI: 1.038–1.398), and ZBTB33 (HR = 1.599, 95% CI: 1.321–1.937). The model was used to score the prognostic risk of each sample in TCGA (training cohort) or GSE41613 (validation cohort). The median prognostic risk score in patients was used as a cutoff, based on which, patients were divided into high- and low-risk groups. Survival analysis was performed for high- and low-risk groups, and the results were visualized by survival and survminer packages in R (Fig. 4A, B). The results showed that the log rank p value for survival analysis was less than 0.05 in both datasets, and a better prognosis was achieved in the low-risk group. Patients in the TCGA-HNSCC and GSE41613 datasets were ranked by prognostic risk score, and a linkage map of risk factors (Fig. 4C, D) was then drawn. In both datasets, patients experienced more death events and shorter survival as the prognostic risk score increased. For the clinical characteristics in TCGA-HNSCC, R was used to remove characteristics with null data and meaningless characteristics, and 7 characteristics were finally obtained: age, sex, grade, stage, primary lesion, lymph node metastasis status, and whether radiotherapy was performed or not. After data preprocessing, univariate and multivariate Cox proportional risk regression analyses were performed to ascertain the clinical correlation of prognostic risk scores with clinical factors. Prognostic risk score was found to be an independent prognostic factor (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4E, F).

Discussion

Various organs in the head and neck, such as the nose, mouth, and throat, are responsible for breathing, eating, and vocalizing, respectively. They are integral parts of people’s daily lives and work. With the completion of human genome sequencing and the development of related techniques in recent years, increasing evidence has shown that head and neck tumors are closely related to genetic mutations. Malignant tumors of the head and neck mainly include cancers of the lips, mouth, larynx, nasopharynx, oropharynx, and hypopharynx. There is a wide variety of such malignancies, including cancers of the lips, mouth, larynx, nasopharynx, paranasal sinuses, nasopharynx, oropharynx, and hypopharynx. There were about 878,250 new cases of such malignancies reported globally in 2020, and such tumors are expected to increase by 30% by 2030 [1]. Of these tumors, HNSCC accounts for 90% [2]. With the rising incidence of tumors and advances in diagnostic techniques, the number of patients with malignant lymphoma of the head and neck is also increasing year by year in China. The pathogenesis of HNSCC is quite complex. The risk factors for HNSCC include smoking, excessive alcohol consumption, infection with EBV, and infection with HPV16. For an early-stage disease, radiotherapy or surgery is usually chosen, while at the middle and late stages of the disease, a combination of treatments including surgery, postoperative adjuvant radiotherapy, immunotherapy, targeted therapy, and novel adjuvant therapies, is used [25]. However, previous study data show that more than 60% of HCSCC patients are already at a locally advanced stage or metastatic stage at the time of definitive diagnosis. In contrast, patients with locally advanced non-metastatic HNSCC had a tumor recurrence rate of about 30% after receiving standards of care, while patients with distant metastasis had a recurrence rate of about 25%, which resulted in a 5-year survival rate of less than 40% in patients with advanced HNSCC [4]. Due to a lack of reliable early screening methods, the early diagnosis and treatment of HNSCC are significantly challenging. Tumorigenesis and tumor progression have been recently shown to be affected by both gene mutation and altered epigenetic characteristics [25]. It has been shown that DNAm, histone modification and RNA methylation serve as important players in cancer [26]. However, fewer studies have been conducted on the association of 5mC with the occurrence, progression and prognosis of HNSCC. This study was aimed at investigating 5mC regulatory modes and the differences in infiltration in the tumor microenvironment among the different modes, so as to help determine the prognosis of patients with HNSCC.

In tumor tissues compared with paracancerous tissues, there were significantly highly expressed genes, such as DNMT1, DNMT3A, DNMT3B, TET1, TET3, TDG, MBD1, MBD3, MBD4, MECP2, NTHL1, SMUG1, UHRF1, UHRF2, UNG, ZBTB33, and ZBTB38 (p < 0.05). In contrast, TET2 and NEIL1 were significantly lowly expressed (p < 0.05). It has been shown that DNMT1 is expressed at increased levels in numerous tumors, including breast cancer, pancreatic cancer, and hematologic malignancies [27–29]. The expression level of DNMT1 in HNSCC is increased compared with paracancerous tissues, which is similar to a relevant previous finding that high expression levels of DNMT1 might contribute to poor prognosis of HNSCC [30]. It has also been shown that DNMT1 inhibitors, such as azacitidine and decitabine, can enhance the sensitivity of HNSCC cells to radiation therapy, and that radiation therapy combined with DNMT1 inhibitors may benefit HNSCC patients [31]. TET2, on the other hand, is increasingly recognized as an important factor in suppressing tumorigenesis, and there is clear evidence that an extremely low expression level of TET2 may contribute to the malignant progression of leukemia and lymphoma [32, 33]. In addition, decreased TET2 expression and its negative correlation with patient survival have been observed in various solid cancers, e.g., hepatocellular carcinoma, prostate cancer, and gastric cancer; too low TET2 expression predicts a poorer prognosis [34–37]. It was shown that the low expression and activity of TET2 in HNSCC was possibly related to its gene deletion, and forced overexpression of TET2 inhibited the proliferation and migration of HNSCC cells and induction of their apoptosis, thereby enhancing their sensitivity to cisplatin [38]. Both in vivo and vitro experiments have demonstrated that increasing the expression level of TET2 can effectively inhibit tumor growth. Therefore, targeting TET2 by genetic or pharmacological approaches may be a viable alternative strategy for treating TET2-deficient HNSCC.

In this study, three different 5mC-related subtypes were identified in patients with HNSCC. These subtypes exhibited significant differences in clinical characteristics and pathological factors. In addition, the three 5mC-related subtypes were found to be associated with varying degrees of metastasis, mutation, and the immune microenvironment. Methylation as a crucial player in tumor metastasis is widely accepted. Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) has been shown to serve as a key player in metastasis of tumor cells, in which E-cadherin is downregulated, vimentin upregulated, cell adhesions reduced, and migration & invasion enhanced. It has been found that DNAm can regulate the EMT process [39]. The mechanism of its action may be related to changes in the levels of EMT-associated E-cadherin and vimentin, which increase the risk for HNSCC metastasis [40, 41].

In this study, the three 5mC regulatory subtypes exhibited different characteristics of infiltration by immune cells. Immune components of the TME in HNSCC are tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (including T cells, B cells, and natural killer (NK) cells) and cells of the myeloid lineage (including macrophages, neutrophils, and DCs). HNSCC generally contains highly infiltrative immune cells. Improved survival was shown to be correlated with the percentage levels of CD8 + T cells and NK cells in the TME [40]. In contrast, advanced HNSCC or poor prognosis was related to increased levels of regulatory T cells, neutrophils, or M2 macrophages [40]. HPV-positive tumors have an immune cell abundance and composition significantly different from those in HPV-negative ones [42, 43]. There are usually more abundant lymphocytes in HPV-positive tumors compared with HPV-negative tumors. More importantly, the prognosis was good in those with HPV-positive tumors containing lymphocytes at high levels, while the survival outcome was similar in those with HPV-positive tumors containing TIL at low levels and those with HPV-negative HNSCC [44].

HLA is divided into two categories: HLA-I and HLA-II. In particular, classical HLA-I molecules (HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C) present endogenous antigens to CD8 T cells, whereas HLA-II molecules (HLA-DR, HLA-DQ, HLA-DM) present exogenous antigens to CD4 Th cells [45, 46]. Tumor cells may evade immune surveillance if they have decreased gene expression levels of HLA-I or do not express them [47, 48]. Subtype C has a relatively higher gene expression level of HLA-I, suggesting that perhaps immunotherapy is more effective for Subtype C.

In addition, TMB, which is defined as the number of somatic mutations per million bases in the coding region of tumor cells in a tumor sample, is considered a biological marker of the degree of tumor mutation [49] TMB is related to the number of neoantigens on the surface of tumor cells, and new immunogenic neoantigens are more likely to be produced in patients with high TMB [50]. A clinical trial also showed a positive association of TMB with antigen recognition by T lymphocytes and the efficacy of immunotherapy; therefore, TMB can be used to predict the efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors against cancers, e.g., melanoma [51]. We analyzed the TMB values for the samples among the subtypes and found that Subtype B had significantly higher TMB. This result suggests that Subtype B is more likely to produce new antigens and may have a good response to immunotherapy.

Cancer stem cells are characterized by their ability to self-renew, proliferate uncontrollably, differentiate into a variety of cell types, and initiate tumorigenesis, and they are widely recognized to be closely related to the progression, metastasis, and drug resistance of tumors [52]. The ability of cancer stem cells to self-renew and differentiate contributes to the initiation and maintenance of tumor growth and plays an important role in the poor prognosis of tumor patients [53]. In this study, most stem cell genes were significantly highly expressed in Subtype B, suggesting that Subtype B may be more prone to progression and metastasis, which in turn leads to a poor prognosis. This finding was also consistent with the poor overall survival (OS) in Subtype B compared with Subtypes A and C. GSEA of the three 5mC regulatory modes showed that Subtype A was mainly linked to DNA replication, cell cycle, Fanconi anemia pathway, and platinum resistance; Subtype B was enriched primarily in the ECM-receptor interaction pathway, focal adhesions, proteoglycans, as well as actin cytoskeleton regulation; and Subtype C was mainly enriched in tight junctions, ferroptosis, and cancer pathways. The enrichment results for Subtype B were also closely related to tumor progression and metastasis. Our 5mC-based molecular subtyping (Subtypes A/B/C) revealed clinically distinct groups that would not be discernible through histology alone. For example: Subtype B exhibited high stemness and TMB (Fig. 3B, C), correlating with poor prognosis (Fig. 4A, B).Subtype C showed elevated HLA expression and immune infiltration (Fig. 2F), suggesting enhanced immunotherapy responsiveness.This demonstrates that 5mC-driven subtyping refines histological classification by exposing epigenetic heterogeneity with direct therapeutic implications.

HNSCC is also characterized by hypoxia, and high hypoxia levels predict poor prognosis and radiotherapy resistance [54–57]. Hypoxia may induce HIF1α (a subunit of the transcription factor HIF1) to drive the expression of a series of genes encoding proteins that promote angiogenesis (VEGF) and ECM (MMP) degradation. HIF1 also upregulates the expression of glucose transporter proteins (e.g., GLUT1) and enzymes in tumor cells, and these enzymes cause the metabolic reprogramming of tumors from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis (known as the Warburg effect) [58]. The expression of pro-inflammatory & immunomodulatory cytokines and chemokines (e.g., IL-1β and TNF) was induced by hypoxia and HIF1 in immune cells infiltrating tumors [29].

Importantly, this study identified seven genes that affected HNSCC prognosis. These genes predicted the prognosis of HNSCC based on the methylation profile of 5mC. Risk scoring models have been shown to reliably predict OS in patients with HNSCC and can be used as an independent prognostic factor. Beyond prognosis stratification, this 5mC-based risk model holds significant promise for early diagnosis. The dysregulation of key regulators like DNMT1 and TET2 (Fig. 1A) occurs in pre-malignant stages of HNSCC [30, 38]. Detecting these epigenetic alterations in oral premalignant lesions (e.g., leukoplakia) or liquid biopsies could identify high-risk patients before histological malignancy develops. For instance, Subtype B’s signature (high stemness/TMB) might serve as a biomarker for aggressive transformation, enabling earlier clinical intervention. As known to participate in tumor progression and immune cell infiltration in the TME, these seven genes (DNMT1, TET2, MBD3, UHRF1, UHRF2, ZBTB4, ZBTB33) have been more intensively investigated. DNMT1 at a high expression level has been found to be closely correlated with the occurrence, development, prognosis and drug resistance of such tumors as esophageal cancer, hypopharyngeal cancer, pancreatic cancer, breast cancer and bladder cancer [28, 59, 60]. Targeting DNMT1 may also increase the expression levels of immunogenic genes (HLA-DR, MICA, MICB) [61]. Methyl-CpG binding domain protein 3 (MBD3) is a key epigenetic regulator that is aberrantly expressed in many cancers and involved in progression and metastasis [62]. Ubiquitin-like with PHD and RING finger domains 1 (UHRF1) is a main player in cellular energy metabolism and cell cycle control. UHRF1 also inhibits the transcription of tumor suppressor genes through DNA and/or histone methylation to contribute to the occurrence and development of cancer [63]. Emerging evidence suggests the role of UHRF2 in the development and progression of several human cancers, such as esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), lung cancer, and colorectal cancer [64]. Zinc finger and BTB domain containing 4 (ZBTB4) is a known transcriptional repressor that may inhibit the pathogenesis of many malignant tumors, such as prostate cancer, breast cancer, lung cancer, and Ewing sarcoma [65]. Furthermore, ZBTB 4 overexpression was linked to prolonged recurrence-free survival in cancer patients. Kaiso (ZBTB33) is a protein biomarker previously shown to be elevated in breast cancer, prostate cancer, pancreatic cancer, and colon cancer, and involved in cancer metastasis and progression [66].

However, this study has its significant limitations. Firstly, this study speculated the 5mC regulatory modes in HNSCC based only on the expression of 5mC regulators. BS-DNA sequencing is required to examine 5mC. Hopefully, this technique will be employed in future research to elucidate 5mC in HNSCC. Secondly, this study failed to provide sufficient evidence to conclude a causal link between methylation and immune infiltration. It can be hypothesized that different methylation levels in HNSCC may indicate different immune infiltration, but further experiments are still required to elucidate the regulatory relationship between 5mC and immune infiltration, to detect the modification patterns of 5mC based on interference with the expression of 5mC regulators. Additionally, while our current analysis leveraged TCGA’s 44 paraneoplastic samples to identify dysregulation of 5mC regulators in HNSCC versus normal tissues (Fig. 1A), the inclusion of larger normal tissue cohorts (e.g., GTEx) and paired tumor-normal samples in future studies will strengthen the biological and clinical relevance of these findings. Such comparisons could further reveal early diagnostic biomarkers (e.g., TET2 downregulation in precancerous lesions) and therapeutic vulnerabilities (e.g., DNMT1 inhibitors in DNMT1-high tumors).

Conclusion

The 5mC regulatory genes DNMT3A, DNMT3B, DNMT1, TET1, TET3, TDG, MBD1, MBD3, MBD, MECP2, NTHL1, SMUG1, UHRF1, UHRF2, UNG, ZBTB38, and ZBTB33 were imbalancedly expressed in HNSCC, and involved in their somatic mutations. The 5mC regulatory modes differed in the levels of immune infiltration, immune checkpoints, TMB, and expression of cancer stem cell gene sets. These differences may affect the efficacy of immunotherapy and tumor prognosis. DNMT1, TET2, MBD3, UHRF1, UHRF2, ZBTB4, ZBTB33 are prognostic risk-related genes. They can be used to establish a prognosis model to determine prognosis in HNSCC patients. While our study leveraged TCGA’s paraneoplastic samples to identify dysregulated 5mC regulators in HNSCC, future work with larger normal tissue cohorts (e.g., GTEx) and paired tumor-normal samples will strengthen the biological and clinical relevance of these findings.
