Peripheral inner retinal dimples observed on ultrawide field optical coherence tomography angiography in X-linked retinoschisis
Authors: Alexandra Miere, Eric H Souied, Caroline Atef Tawfik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo report a novel observation of peripheral inner retinal dimples (IRDs) in a patient with X-linked retinoschisis (XLRS).MethodsA 30-year-old male with molecularly confirmed XLRS presented for his regular follow up with a new complaint of photopsia for the past month preceding examination. He underwent complete ophthalmological examination and multimodal imaging with special emphasis on peripheral retinal changes. Ultrawide-field color fundus photography, fundus autofluorescence (FAF), fluorescein angiography optical coherence tomography angiography (UWF-OCTA) were done.ResultsHis best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 20/40 in the right eye and 20/60 in the left eye with unremarkable anterior segment examination. Fundus examination demonstrated foveoschisis associated with peripheral retinal changes most notable in the inferiorotemporal quadrant manifesting as with horizontal, band-like vitreous membrane. UWF multimodal imaging confirmed presence of inner retinal dimples which corresponded to hyporeflective dark spots observed on enface OCT and UWF OCTA.ConclusionTo the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of IRDs in the inferotemporal quadrants of the peripheral retinae in a patient with XLRS.Keywords: X-linked retinoschisis, Ultrawide field optical coherence tomography angiography, RS1 gene, Peripheral retinal changes, Inner retinal dimples

Introduction

X-linked retinoschisis (XLRS) (MIM# 312700) is one of the most prevalent inherited retinal diseases in males, with a reported prevalence of 1 in 5000–20,000. It is a leading cause of visual impairment in male children of school age.1 It is caused by pathogenic variants in the RS1 gene (MIM# 300839) on the Xp22.2 chromosome, which encodes retinoschisin—a protein containing a discoidin domain that mediates cell-cell adhesion in the retina. Disruption of retinoschisin leads to defective assembly, secretion, or localization of the protein, with subsequent loss of retinal cell adhesion, splitting of retinal layers (schisis) and impaired synaptic transmission.2

Peripheral retinal changes are observed in approximately half of cases of XLRS in the form of peripheral retinoschisis with splitting of retinal layers at different levels predominantly affecting the inferotemporal quadrants. It can vary in severity from being unremarkable on fundoscopy (occult retinoschisis) up to being bullous.3 Retinal honeycomb appearance has also been reported.4 Other peripheral retinal changes include a metallic tapetal reflex, vitreous veils and vitreous membranes, retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) pigmentary changes, white spiculations, and vascular sheathing. These peripheral retinal changes may be associated with higher risk of complications as vitreous hemorrhage and retinal detachment.5

Inner retinal dimples (IRDs) were first described by Tadayoni et at. in 2001 following internal limiting membrane (ILM) peeling when a peculiar ‘moth-eaten appearance’ of the retina was noted postoperatively. It was observed 1 to 3 months postoperatively in 42 to 62% of cases that underwent ILM peeling and was termed “dissociated optic nerve fiber layer” (DONFL) owing their appearance as dark arcuate striae along the course of optic nerve fibers. Subsequently, spectral domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT) showed that they corresponded to inner retinal defects often referred to as inner retinal dimples (IRDs).6

Here we describe a 30-year-old male with XLRS caused by a hemizygous pathogenic missense variant NM_000330.4:c.214G > A in RS1 gene where IRDs were observed in the peripheral retina predominantly in inferotemporal quadrants.

Case description

We examined a 30-year-old male patient presenting to our clinic for the follow up of his molecularly confirmed XLRS. The patient reported a family history of XLRS in his elder brother.

Ophthalmic examination and investigations

The patient underwent comprehensive ophthalmological evaluation. Best corrected distance visual acuity (BCVA) was assessed with a Snellen chart which was limited to 20/40 for right eye and 20/60 for left eye. His refraction was hyperopic with +2.00/-2.25x120 for right eye and +2.75/-2.50x60 for left eye. The slit lamp examination was unremarkable for anterior segment. Fundus examination demonstrated foveal spoke-wheel appearance suggestive of foveoschisis. Peripheral retinal changes were observed in both eyes in the form of horizontal, band-like vitreous membranes inferiorly, and honeycomb appearance with metallic tapetal reflex in the inferotemporal quadrants, more prominent in the left eye. Spectral domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT) of the maculae was obtained using Heidelberg Spectralis OCT (Heidelberg Engineering GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany) and confirmed the presence of foveoschisis in both eyes.

Ultra-wide field fundus (UWF) photography and green-light fundus autofluorescence (FAF) using Optos Silverstone (Optos plc, Scotland, UK) was obtained. UWF pseudocolor imaging demonstrated horizontal, band-like vitreous membranes (Fig. 1, panel A) and metallic tapetal reflex. UWF fundus autofluorescence imaging revealed hypoautofluorescent areas corresponding to the horizontal, band-like vitreous membranes with hyperautofluorescent rounded areas arranged in a curvilinear manner, most notable in the inferotemporal quadrants (Fig. 1, panel B). On UWF fluorescein angiography these lesions corresponded to well-circumscribed hyperfluorescent lesions surrounding hypofluorescent centers arranged in a curvilinear manner reminiscent of Schlaegel lines (Fig. 1, panel C). Ultrawide field swept-source optical coherence tomography angiography (UWF SS-OCTA) was obtained by TowardPi (BM-400K BMizar, TowardPi Medical Technology Ltd., Beijing, China) where the structural and flow enface SS-OCTA superficial capillary plexus images delineated multiple hypodense dots distributed inferior to the temporal arcade (Fig. 1, panel D and E and Fig. 2). These darker spots corresponded to inner retinal dimples (IRD) on cross-sectional OCT (Fig. 1, panel F and Fig. 2).

Genetic findings

Genetic testing was performed on a blood sample using a next-generation sequencing (NGS)-based inherited retinal dystrophy panel comprising 212 genes. Bioinformatic analysis, confirmation and biological validation were performed by the Molecular Genetics Unit, Necker-Enfants Malades Hospital in Paris, France, a COFRAC (Comité Français d’Accréditation)-accredited laboratory. A pathogenic hemizygous missense variant c.214G > A p.(Glu72Lys) was identified in exon 4 of the RS1 gene (NM_000330.4). This nucleotide substitution results in the amino acid substitution p.Glu72Lys. This variant is a well-established pathogenic founder mutation in the Finnish population and is associated with XLRS.

Missense variants such as c.214G > A typically interfere with proper protein folding, leading to intracellular retention of altered retinoschisin within the endoplasmic reticulum, preventing its secretion and extracellular function.7,8 This loss-of-function (LOF) of retinoschisin impairs cell-cell adhesion in the retina, resulting in schisis and progressive retinal dysfunction.

Discussion

In this report, we used multiple UWF imaging modalities to observe the inner retinal layer changes in a patient with molecularly confirmed XLRS. We were able to document IRDs in the inferotemporal quadrants of peripheral retina in both eyes. These IRDs are typically seen following ILM peeling for various indications, but in our case, they were not sequelae of any prior surgical intervention. The accompanying horizontal, band-like membranes our patient had differed from true vitreous veils, which consist of separated inner retinal layers projecting into the vitreous cavity. Because inner retinal tissue remained visible at their base, we refer to them as band-like vitreous membranes.9

While the fovea is most consistently affected in XLRS, peripheral retinal changes are commonly observed in up to 50% of patients. These changes typically manifest as peripheral schisis cavities and may be associated with inner retinal layer splitting.3 These peripheral changes are clinically significant because they can predispose to complications such as vitreous hemorrhage, retinal breaks, and rhegmatogenous retinal detachment, which in turn are leading causes of vision loss in XLRS beyond macular dysfunction.5 In some cases, peripheral schisis may regress with age, but it can also progress to atrophic thinning or lead to tractional events. Careful monitoring of the peripheral retina is therefore essential for timely detection and management of sight-threatening complications.

Müller cell processes extend across nearly the entire thickness of the retina, forming structural bridges that interact closely with photoreceptor axons. Müller cell bodies are located in the inner nuclear layer (INL). ILM is composed of vitreous-facing footplates of Müller cells and basal lamina.10

In XLRS, the primary defect is disruption of the function of retinoschisin which is a protein crucial for retinal cell adhesion and integrity. Müller cell dysfunction has emerged as an important contributor to disease pathology. Normally, Müller cells maintain retinal milieu, regulate fluid and ion balance, and support synaptic activity, but in XLRS they show signs of stress and reactive gliosis, with subsequent dysfunction which contributes to the formation and persistence of schisis cavities, and underlies the characteristic reduction in the ERG b-wave. Thus, Müller cell pathology is increasingly recognized as both a secondary effect of retinoschisin deficiency and a driver of disease progression in XLRS.2,11

IRDs are thought to result from structural alterations in the inner retina following surgical or mechanical disruption of Müller cell and RNFL architecture. They are most commonly observed after ILM peeling during macular hole or epiretinal membrane surgery, where ILM peeling induces injury and reorganization of Müller cell footplates with subsequent Müller cell dysfunction or degeneration.10 This disruption can lead to focal thinning and pitting within the RNFL associated with concomitant GCL thinning, producing a dimpled appearance on enface imaging and focal depressions observed on structural OCT.6

Hence, Müller cell pathology in XLRS can explain the occurrence of IRDs in the peripheral retina in our patient.

In conclusion, we describe the presence of peripheral IRDs in a case of a molecularly confirmed XLRS via UWF multimodal imaging, thus underscoring the importance of regular wide-field imaging for early detection of peripheral and prompt intervention when complications arise.
