Abstract
Political agents often aim to influence elections through troll farms – organisations that disseminate messages emulating genuine information. We study the behaviour of a troll farm that faces a heterogeneous electorate of partially informed voters, and aims to achieve a desired political outcome by targeting each type of voter with a specific distribution of messages. We show that such tactics are more effective when voters are otherwise well-informed, for example, when the media is of high quality. At the same time, increased polarisation, as well as deviations from Bayesian rationality, can reduce the negative effect of troll farms and restore efficiency of electoral outcomes.



Keywords
: social media, disinformation, persuasion, information design, elections, polarisation, bounded rationality.

Introduction

“
What we’re facing is a new form of propaganda that

Sean Illing, Vox

“
The real opposition

One of the consequences of the rise of social media is that voters receive a significant amount of politically relevant information through social media platforms. This has enabled voters to acquire information from many sources, but has also given malicious agents new tools to manipulate information.
One growing concern are the so-called troll farms, also known as bot factories or keyboard armies – groups of coordinated social media accounts that disseminate biased messages purporting to come from real individuals or reliable news sources.
Such troll farms are increasingly common – one report estimates that in 38 out of 65 surveyed countries, political leaders use them to manipulate elections and other domestic political events
(
Freedom
House 2019
)
.
For example, the use of troll farms has been detected in the 2016 Brexit referendum and the 2016 US presidential election
(
Gorodnichenko et al. 2021
)
, as well as in the online debate in China
(
King
et al. 2017
)
.

Several features of troll farms make them more effective than more traditional propaganda tools such as biased media. First, unlike messages delivered via traditional channels, messages from troll farms shared via social media are able to emulate genuine information
(
Ferrara et al. 2016
;
Haustein et al. 2016
)
. Thus, the target audience is left uncertain whether a particular message comes from a real person communicating a piece of news or an experience, or from a bot.

Second, social media platforms collect vast amounts of personal data, enabling them to infer users’ political preferences. This makes it possible to target specific voters with specific messages designed to maximise persuasive effect.

1

For example, already in 2012 political campaigns could deliver specific ads to, for example, “all registered 50-to-60-year-old male Democrats in Pennsylvania’s 6th district who are frequent voters and care about the environment.”
(
Leber 2012
)
.

The impact of this microtargeting capacity of social media influence operations
has been demonstrated in 2018, when the consulting firm Cambridge Analytica was found to have collected data on millions of Facebook users to determine their political preferences and target them with custom-made political messages
(
New York
Times 2018
)
. The effectiveness of such persuasive microtargeting has been documented by
Simchon
et al. 2024
.

Third, modern technology allows large numbers of trolls to be deployed at almost no cost, as multiple fake accounts can be controlled by a single user or even by automated algorithms
(
The
Guardian 2011
;
Freedom
House 2017
)
.
As a result, troll farms are producing a substantial proportion of content in online political discussions.
2
2

2

During the first impeachment of US president Donald Trump, an estimated 31% of impeachment related tweets were coming from bots
(
Rossetti and
Zaman 2023
)
. In the early weeks of the Russia-Ukraine war, bot accounts produced around 17% of tweets related to the conflict
(
Shen
et al. 2023
)
.

The emergence of generative language models such as ChatGPT can make it particularly easy to flood social media platforms with content generated by fake accounts
(
Goldstein et al. 2023
)
, potentially drowning out other messages.

In this paper, we analyse the impact of troll farms on voting outcomes. We develop a constrained information design framework that reflects the three aforementioned features of troll farms. In our model, a continuum of voters choose between two actions. To fix ideas, we will say that voters choose whether to vote for or against the government. There is a binary state of the world, which can be high or low – this indicates, for example, whether the government is competent. All voters share a common prior about the state, and each voter independently receives an imperfect continuous signal about it. Voters differ in their political preferences, or
types
. The type of each voter corresponds to the minimum probability that the voter needs to put on the high state to be willing to vote for the government.

A sender wants to increase the share of voters voting for the government. She cannot commit to an arbitrary experiment that sends messages to voters conditional on the state.
Instead, she sends uninformative messages that mimic exogenous informative signals. Each voter receives exactly one message, and does not know whether it is an informative signal or comes from the troll farm. These messages can be targeted at specific voters. For each voter type, the sender chooses the distribution of trolls’ messages, and the number of trolls – that is, the probability that a voter of this type receives a message from the troll farm instead of a genuine informative signal.

We then characterise the optimal strategy of the sender.
To voters who want to vote against the government at the prior belief, the sender sends pro-government messages to increase the probability that such voters receive them. On the other hand, voters that want to vote in favour of the government at the prior belief are targeted with anti-government messages in order to eliminate their informational content.

We then derive three results related to the ability of the sender to manipulate electoral outcomes.
First, we show that the presence of a troll farm changes the impact of the voters’ independent signals. In the absence of trolls, making voters’ independent signals more informative increases the share of voters voting for the government in the high state, and reduces it in the low state. Thus, increased signal precision improves the efficiency of the political outcome. However, the presence of trolls changes this picture: higher precision of voters’ independent signals increases the share of the voters who back the government in
both
states. This contrasts with the standard literature on persuasion with independent signals and no uncertainty about signal sources, in which more precise independent signals make it harder for the sender to manipulate beliefs, as we discuss below.

Second, we analyse how increased polarisation of the electorate affects the power of the troll farm. Existing evidence suggests that polarisation of voters is increasing in a number of democracies.
3
3

3

See
Iyengar and
Krupenkin 2018
for the United States, and
Boxell
et al. 2022
for several other OECD countries.
There is considerable discussion of the potential negative effects of increased polarisation.

4

See
McCoy
et al. 2018
;
Martherus et al. 2021
.
We show, however, that in the presence of a troll farm, polarisation can be beneficial: if the sender can ensure that the government wins the election in both states, an increase in polarisation can restore the socially optimal outcome.

Third, we discuss what happens when voters perceive information in a boundedly rational way. There is considerable evidence that individuals can deviate from Bayesian updating – depending on the situation, they may overweight or underweight signals

(
Tversky and
Kahneman 1992
;
Augenblick
et al. 2024
;
Ba
et al. 2023
;
Denter 2024
)
.
We show that a tendency of voters to under-weigh signals relative to Bayesian updating, while preventing voters from forming correct beliefs, can nevertheless restore the efficiency of the political process by limiting the power of the troll farm.

Model

A continuum of voters of mass one needs to choose whether to reelect the government. There is an unknown state of the world
\theta\in\left\{0,1\right\}
, which indicates, for example, whether the government is competent.
The preferences of each voter are characterised by a type
x\in\mathbb{R}
. We assume that types are distributed across voters according to a cdf
H\left(x\right)
with the associated density
h\left(x\right)
and with full support on
\mathbb{R}
.
If a voter with type
x
votes for the government, she receives a payoff
1-x
if the state turns out to be 1 – that is, if the government is competent – and a payoff
-x
is the state turns out to be 0. The payoff of a voter who votes against the government is normalised to zero.
7
7

7

Thus, voters receive payoffs from their actions, and not from the outcome of the election. Since the set of voters is a continuum, each voter is pivotal with probability zero. Hence, allowing voters’ payoffs to also depend on the voting outcome has no effect on their behaviour at an equilibrium.
Thus, a voter with a higher type is more opposed to the government. Note that we allow for types to be outside the
\left[0,1\right]
interval, that is, to prefer to vote the same way irrespective of the state. We assume that a voter who is indifferent votes for the government.

The government is reelected if the share of voters who vote for it is at least
\frac{1}{2}
. We will say that the election aggregates information if it achieves the “correct” outcome – that is, if the government is reelected in state one, and is not reelected in state zero.

Voters share a common prior belief about the state of the world being 1. We normalise that belief to
\frac{1}{2}
. Note that this is without loss of generality: changing the belief is equivalent to shifting the distribution
H
.

At the beginning of the game, each voter
i
receives a private signal
s_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
. In each state
\theta\in\left\{0,1\right\}
, the signal of each voter is drawn from a cdf
F_{\theta}
with density
f_{\theta}
and full support on
\mathbb{R}
. We assume, without loss of generality, that
f_{0}\left(0\right)=f_{1}\left(0\right)
. Let
m\left(s\right):=\frac{f_{1}\left(s\right)}{f_{0}\left(s\right)}
denote the likelihood ratio. We will assume that
m\left(s\right)
is strictly increasing in
s
. This imposes a standard monotone likelihood ratio assumption, implying that a higher realisation of the signal makes a voter believe that the state equals one with a higher probability. It also implies that
F_{0}\left(s\right)>F_{1}\left(s\right)
for all
s
.
We will refer to the pair
\left(F_{0},F_{1}\right)
as the
information structure
.

A player, whom we will call the sender, is trying to help the government. She can do it by setting up a troll farm, that is, by sending messages that imitate and replace the informative signals but are not correlated with the true state. For each type of voter, the sender can select the the mass of trolls targeting her, as well as the distribution of the trolls’ messages. Formally, for each type of voter
x
, the sender chooses the probability
\alpha_{x}
with which the voter observes a signal from trolls instead of an informative signal; and a distribution
\tilde{F}_{x}
of the trolls’ signals (with the associated density
\tilde{f}_{x}
). For example,
\alpha_{x}=0
means that no trolls are targeting voters with type
x
, and thus all signals observed by these voters are coming from informative sources. On the other hand,
\alpha_{x}\rightarrow 1
means that a signal is almost surely coming from a troll.

The timing of the game is as follows. First, for each
x
, the sender selects
\alpha_{x}
and
\tilde{F}_{x}
. Then, nature draws the state
\theta
and the signals. Each voter then receives either a signal from the troll farm or an informative signal, without being able to distinguish between the two.
Voters then form their posterior beliefs and vote. Afterwards, payoffs are realised. The payoff of each voter is as described above, while the payoff of the sender is
u\left(V\right)
, where
V
is the share of voters that vote for the government, and
u
is a strictly increasing function.

As will be shown later, there may be several strategies that result in the same share of votes for the government. As an equilibrium selection rule, we will assume that, all other things being equal, the sender prefers to use as few trolls as possible.
Formally, suppose that choosing

\left(\alpha_{x},\tilde{F}_{x}\right)_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
and
\left(\alpha_{x}^{\prime},\tilde{F}_{x}^{\prime}\right)_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
produce the same vote share for the government in both states, but
\alpha_{x}\leq\alpha_{x}^{\prime}
for
all

x
, with strict inequality for some
x
. Then the sender prefers
\left(\alpha_{x},\tilde{F}_{x}\right)_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
to
\left(\alpha_{x}^{\prime},\tilde{F}_{x}^{\prime}\right)_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
.

3
Benchmark: No Trolls

Take a voter with type
x
. Let

\pi(s)=\frac{f_{1}\left(s\right)}{f_{1}\left(s\right)+f_{0}\left(s\right)}=\frac{m\left(s\right)}{m\left(s\right)+1}

be the probability that a voter assigns to the government being competent when she observes signal
s
. Her expected payoff is
\pi\left(s\right)-x
if she votes for the government, and zero otherwise. Hence, she votes for the government if and only if
\pi\left(s\right)\geq x
. If
x\leq 0
, the voter votes for the government regardless of the signal, while if
x\geq 1
, she voters against the government regardless of the signal. If
x\in\left(0,1\right)
, the voter votes for the government if and only if
\frac{m\left(s\right)}{m\left(s\right)+1}\geq x
, or, equivalently, if and only if
s\geq s^{*}\left(x\right)
, where
s^{*}\left(x\right):=m^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{1-x}\right)
. Therefore, in each state
\theta\in\left\{0,1\right\}
, a voter with type
x
votes for the government with the ex ante probability
1-F_{\theta}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]
. This probability depends on the information structure, which also affects the cutoff
s^{*}\left(x\right)
through the likelihood ratio.

We say that signals are more informative if each voter is more likely to make the correct decision in each state. This also implies greater informativeness in the Blackwell sense.
Formally, we define greater informativeness as follows:

Definition 1
.

Information structure
\left(\hat{F}_{0},\hat{F}_{1}\right)
is more informative than information structure
\left(F_{0},F_{1}\right)
if and only if for all
x\in\left[0,1\right]
we have

\hat{F}_{0}\left[\hat{s}^{*}\left(x\right)\right]\geq F_{0}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]
and

\hat{F}_{1}\left[\hat{s}^{*}\left(x\right)\right]\leq F_{1}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]
,
where
s^{*}\left(x\right)
and
\hat{s}^{*}\left(x\right)
are defined as above.

Given the distribution of signals in each state, the share of voters voting for the government in state
\theta\in\left\{0,1\right\}
equals

V_{\theta}=H\left(0\right)+\int_{0}^{1}\left(1-F_{\theta}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]\right)dH\left(x\right).

(1)

Hence, increased informativeness decreases
V_{0}
and increases
V_{1}
.

4
Equilibrium with Trolls

Now consider the case when trolls are available. Suppose the sender chooses
\alpha_{x}>0
and
\tilde{F}_{x}
. A voter with type
x
who observes signal
s
forms the following posterior belief:

\pi\left(s\right)=\frac{\left(1-\alpha_{x}\right)f_{1}\left(s\right)+\alpha_{x}\tilde{f}_{x}\left(s\right)}{\left(1-\alpha_{x}\right)f_{1}\left(s\right)+\alpha_{x}\tilde{f}_{x}\left(s\right)+\left(1-\alpha_{x}\right)f_{0}\left(s\right)+\alpha_{x}\tilde{f}_{x}\left(s\right)}.

For all
s
, an increased chance of receiving message
s
from trolls – that is, a larger
\alpha_{x}
or a larger
\tilde{f}_{x}\left(s\right)
–
moves the belief towards the prior. The voter votes for the government whenever
\pi\left(s\right)\geq x
.

The sender chooses
\alpha_{x}
and
\tilde{F}_{x}
for each type of voter
x
to maximise the expectation of
u\left(V_{\theta}\right)
.
As before, voters with types
x\notin\left(0,1\right)
cannot be persuaded to change their vote by any signal. From now on, we will focus on voters with types
x\in\left(0,1\right)
.

Recall that without trolls, a voter with type
x\in\left(0,1\right)
will vote for the government if and only if she receives signal
s\geq s^{*}\left(x\right)
. Consider first a voter with type
x\leq\frac{1}{2}
. When that voter’s belief equals the prior, she is willing to vote for the government. By instructing trolls to send her signals below
s^{*}\left(x\right)
, the sender can weaken these signal realisations. With sufficiently many trolls, the belief after each signal
s<s^{*}\left(x\right)
will be above
x
. At the same time, after receiving a signal
s\geq s^{*}\left(x\right)
the voter is already willing to vote for the government even without trolls. Hence, the sender is able to ensure that the voter votes for the government after any signal realisation. Recall that the optimal strategy should achieve this with the smallest mass of trolls. To do so, the sender sets

\tilde{f}\left(s\right)=0
for all
s\geq s^{*}\left(x\right)
, while for all
s<s^{*}\left(x\right)
she sets
\tilde{f}\left(s\right)
in such a way that the belief after any such signal is exactly
x
. This gives a unique optimal pair
\left(\alpha_{x},\tilde{F}_{x}\right)
for each voter type
x\leq\frac{1}{2}
.

Now consider a voter with type
x>\frac{1}{2}
. This voter is ex ante opposed to the government. Since a signal
s<s^{*}\left(x\right)
cannot persuade this voter to vote for the government even without trolls, it cannot make her willing to vote for the government with trolls, either. It is then optimal for the sender to minimise the probability that these voters receive such a signal. Thus, the sender sets
\tilde{f}\left(s\right)=0
for all
s<s^{*}\left(x\right)
.

On the other hand, having trolls send messages
s\geq s^{*}\left(x\right)
increases the probability that the voter receives signals that, in the absence of trolls, induce her to vote for the government. However, doing this reduces the posterior beliefs after such signals. Hence, it is optimal for the sender to set
\alpha_{x}
and
\tilde{f}\left(s\right)
such that the posterior belief after observing signal
s\geq s^{*}\left(x\right)
equals exactly
x
.

The next proposition formalises this intuition:

Proposition 1
.

Let
\kappa_{x}\left(s\right):=\frac{xF_{0}\left(s\right)-\left(1-x\right)F_{1}\left(s\right)}{1-2x}
. The sender has a unique optimal strategy.
For all
x\leq\frac{1}{2}
, the mass of trolls is

\alpha_{x}=\frac{\kappa_{x}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]}{\kappa_{x}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]+1},

and the density of trolls’ messages is

\tilde{f}_{x}\left(s\right)=\begin{cases}\frac{1-\alpha_{x}}{\alpha_{x}}\kappa_{x}^{\prime}\left(s\right)&\text{if }s<s^{*}\left(x\right)\\
0&\text{if }s\geq s^{*}\left(x\right)\end{cases},

such that
\pi\left(s\right)=x
for all
s\leq s^{*}\left(x\right)
.
For all
x>\frac{1}{2}
, the mass of trolls is
\alpha_{x}=\frac{\kappa_{x}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]+1}{\kappa_{x}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]},

and the density of trolls’ messages is

\tilde{f}_{x}\left(s\right)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }s<s^{*}\left(x\right)\\
\frac{1-\alpha_{x}}{\alpha_{x}}\kappa_{x}^{\prime}\left(s\right)&\text{if }s\geq s^{*}\left(x\right)\end{cases},

such that
\pi\left(s\right)=x
for all
s\geq s^{*}\left(x\right)
.

The sender thus chooses very different communication strategies for voters who ex ante support the government and for those who ex ante oppose it. Pro-government voters are targeted with anti-government messages – those below
s^{*}\left(x\right)
– while anti-government voters are targeted with pro-government messages. Thus, with pro-government voters the sender pursues the strategy of “flooding the zone”, trying to make unfavourable information unreliable, while with anti-government voters she aims to increase the probability of them receiving favourable information.

When it comes to the amount of trolls targeting each voter, the sender places the most effort on persuading neutral voters, that is, voters whose types are close to
\frac{1}{2}
:

Corollary 1
.

\alpha_{x}^{*}
increases in
x
if
x<\frac{1}{2}
and decreases in
x
if
x>\frac{1}{2}
.

The sender’s strategy ensures that in each state, all voters with
x\leq\frac{1}{2}
vote for the government. At the same time, a voter with
x>\frac{1}{2}
votes for the government when she receives a signal from a troll, or when she receives an informative signal that happens to be greater than
s^{*}\left(x\right)
. The former event happens with the same probability in both states, while the latter is more likely to happen in state 1 – therefore, the share of voters voting for the government is greater in state 1. This implies the following result:

Corollary 2
.

The share of voters voting for the government in state
\theta=0
equals

V_{0}^{*}=H\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)+\int_{\frac{1}{2}}^{1}\frac{F_{0}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]-F_{1}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]}{xF_{0}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]-\left(1-x\right)F_{1}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]}\left(1-x\right)dH\left(x\right),

while in state
\theta=1
it equals

V_{1}^{*}=H\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)+\int_{\frac{1}{2}}^{1}\frac{F_{0}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]-F_{1}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]}{xF_{0}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]-(1-x)F_{1}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]}\,x\,dH\left(x\right);

furthermore,
V_{0}^{*}<V_{1}^{*}
.

The election aggregates information whenever the government wins in state
1
but not in state
0
. This happens if and only if
V_{0}^{*}<\frac{1}{2}\leq V_{1}^{*}
. On the other hand, if
V_{0}^{*}\geq\frac{1}{2}
, the government wins the election in both states.

5
Informativeness, Polarisation, and Voting Outcomes

In this section we analyse the effects of different features of the voting process on election outcomes.

We start by looking at the effects of informativeness. Recall from Section
3
that without interference from the sender, under a more informative information structure, the government receives more votes in state 1, and fewer votes in state 0. With trolls, however, the dynamic is different, as the next result shows:

Proposition 2
.

If information structure
\left(\hat{F}_{0},\hat{F}_{1}\right)
is more informative than information structure
\left(F_{0},F_{1}\right)
, then in each state more voters vote for the government under
\left(\hat{F}_{0},\hat{F}_{1}\right)
than under
\left(F_{0},F_{1}\right)
.

Hence, a more informative information structure helps the sender manipulate the voters’ decisions, increasing the probability that a voter votes incorrectly in state
0
.

Intuitively, the sender cannot send informative signals of her own but can only replace voters’ informative signals with uninformative ones. Hence, the beliefs she can induce in voters are constrained to be closer to the prior than those induced by informative signals. When the latter become more informative, the sender’s constraint is relaxed. Hence, the set of beliefs she can induce expands, increasing her persuasive power.

Next, consider effect of the distribution
H
of voters’ types. Recall that a voter with type
\frac{1}{2}
is indifferent between supporting and opposing the government at the prior belief. More generally, voters whose types are closer to
\frac{1}{2}
need a weaker signal to be convinced to shift their vote. Thus, for any two voters whose types are on the same side of
\frac{1}{2}
, the voter whose type is closer to
\frac{1}{2}
is more moderate. We can then say that the electorate becomes more polarised when the fraction of moderates decreases and the fraction of extreme voters increases. More formally, we use the following definition of polarisation:

Definition 2
.

A distribution
\hat{H}

admits greater polarisation
than distribution
H
if and only if

\hat{H}\left(x\right)\geq H\left(x\right)
for all
x\leq\frac{1}{2}
, and

\hat{H}\left(x\right)\leq H\left(x\right)
for all
x\geq\frac{1}{2}
.

This defines a partial order on the set of distributions. Intuitively, Definition
2
says that polarisation is higher if, for any type
x
, there are more voters who are on the same side of
\frac{1}{2}
as
x
, but more extreme. This definition of polarisation is a special case of the definition in
Ginzburg 2024
.

If
H\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}
, Corollary
2
implies that the government always wins the election in both states. A change in polarisation does not affect this. In the more interesting case when
H\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)<\frac{1}{2}
, polarisation has an effect. The next result shows that if the government wins the election in both states, increased polarisation can restore information aggregation:

Proposition 3
.

Suppose the government wins the election in both states under some distribution
H\left(x\right)
. Suppose further that
H\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)<\frac{1}{2}
. Then there exists a distribution
\hat{H}\left(x\right)
that admits greater polarisation than
H\left(x\right)
and under which the election aggregates information.

Intuitively, increased polarisation means that for each voter on either side of
\frac{1}{2}
, there are more voters that are further away from
\frac{1}{2}
. This means that there are more voters who very supportive of the government, as well as more voters who are very opposed to it. However, these two changes affect the power of the sender in different ways. As Proposition
1
shows, voters who are ex ante supportive of the government can always be persuaded to vote for the government. Hence, an increase in the mass of extreme pro-government voters compared to the mass of moderate pro-government voters does not change the ability of the troll farm to manipulate elections. On the other hand, a shift of anti-government voters towards more extreme positions makes it harder for the sender to achieve her desired outcome.

Finally, we can analyse how interaction of informativeness and polarisation affects the ability of the election to achieve the socially optimal outcome. Higher informativeness implies that for all
x
,
\frac{F_{0}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]}{F_{1}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]}
is closer to infinity, while lower informativeness implies that it is closer to one.
One can define sequences of information structures such that along the sequence, the ratio
\frac{F_{0}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]}{F_{1}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]}
increases from one to infinity. As we move along this sequence and informativeness increases, what happens to voting outcomes?

As before, if
H\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}
, then by Corollary
2
the government always wins the election in both states. When
H\left(1\right)<\frac{1}{2}
, the government always loses the election because the share of voters with type
x>1
who always vote against the government is greater than
\frac{1}{2}
. Consider now the more interesting case when
H\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)<\frac{1}{2}
and
H\left(1\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}
. Then, as information structure moves from being completely uninformative towards being perfectly informative, we have the following result:

Proposition 4
.

Suppose that
H\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)<\frac{1}{2}\leq H\left(1\right)
. Take a sequence of information structures
\left(F_{0}^{r},F_{1}^{r}\right)
indexed by
r\in\left(-\infty,\infty\right)
, such that informativeness increases with
r
,
\lim_{r\rightarrow-\infty}\frac{F_{0}^{r}\left(s\right)}{F_{1}^{r}\left(s\right)}=1
for all
s
, and
\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{F_{0}^{r}\left(s\right)}{F_{1}^{r}\left(s\right)}=\infty
for all
s
. Then there exist
r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime}
with
r^{\prime}<r^{\prime\prime}
, such that:

•

for
r<r^{\prime}
, the government loses the election in both states;

•

for
r\in\left(r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime}\right)
, the government wins the election in state
1
only, and the election aggregates information;

•

for
r>r^{\prime\prime}
, the government wins the election in both states whenever the distribution of types admits sufficiently low polarisation; and wins the election in state 1 only otherwise.

In words, when signals are not very informative, they cannot induce sufficiently many voters to move their belief sufficiently far from the prior, so the government cannot get enough votes even in state 1. By Proposition
2
, increased informativeness raises the government’s vote share in each state, and by Corollary
2
, because that share is higher in state 1 than in state 0. Hence, for moderately high informativeness, the government wins the election in state 1 only. If signals are very informative and polarisation is not too high, the government wins the election in both states. On the other hand, if signals are very informative but polarisation is high, then by Proposition
3
, the election still aggregates information.

Proposition
4
means that when polarisation is relatively low, an increase in informativeness can move the voting outcome away from information aggregation. Hence, increased informativeness, beyond a certain level, is harmful.

6
Troll Farms with Limited Reach

In our previous analysis, we assumed that the sender can set any mass of trolls to target a voter of a particular type. This meant that
\alpha_{x}
, the share of voters of type
x
receiving a message from a troll instead of an informative signal, could take any value between zero and one. In some situations, however, it is realistic to assume that this probability has an upper limit. For example, some voters may be sufficiently sophisticated to distinguish between a genuine signal and one originating from a troll farm – these voters would ignore a message from trolls. Other voters may simply not be using social media, instead receiving information from other channels. It is also possible that voters are optimising their information search, and will choose not to use social media if too many messages there are coming from trolls. Finally, increasing
\alpha_{x}
beyond a certain limit may be prohibitively costly for the sender. All of these factors would put a cap on
\alpha_{x}
. This cap may depend on
x
, as voters with different political preferences may make different information acquisition decisions.

Suppose that for a voter of type
x
, the mass of trolls is limited such that
\alpha_{x}
cannot exceed a certain maximal value
\bar{\alpha}_{x}
. If
\bar{\alpha}_{x}\geq\alpha_{x}^{*}
as described in Proposition
1
, the optimal strategy is unchanged. Otherwise, the constraint on
\alpha_{x}
is binding for voters of type
x
. In that case, the optimal strategy of the sender is as follows:

Proposition 5
.

Suppose
\bar{\alpha}_{x}<\alpha_{x}^{*}
. For each
x\in\left[0,1\right]
, the sender chooses the mass of trolls
\alpha^{\text{C}}_{x}=\bar{\alpha}_{x}
.
For
x\leq\frac{1}{2}
, the sender chooses the density of trolls’ messages

\tilde{f}_{x}^{\text{C}}\left(s\right)=\begin{cases}\frac{1-\bar{\alpha}_{x}}{\bar{\alpha}_{x}}\kappa_{x}^{\prime}\left(s\right)&\forall s\in\left[\hat{s}\left(x\right),s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]\\
0&\forall s\notin\left[\hat{s}\left(x\right),s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]\end{cases},

for some uniquely determined
\hat{s}\left(x\right)<s^{*}\left(x\right)
. For
x>\frac{1}{2}
, there are multiple optimal densities of trolls’ messages, including the one described in Proposition
1
.

As in the baseline case described in Proposition
1
, the optimal strategy of the sender is different for voters that are ex ante pro-government and those that are ex ante anti-government. For the former, instead of sending all anti-government signals as before, the trolls now only send signals between the decision threshold
s^{*}\left(x\right)
and some smaller signal realisation
\hat{s}\left(x\right)
. Thus, trolls send moderately strong anti-government messages in order to eliminate their informational content (that is, to pull the posterior belief that they induce to
x
), while ignoring pro-government messages as well as more extreme anti-government messages. In comparison, without the constraint on
\alpha_{x}
, trolls aim to distort all anti-government signals. Intuitively, moderate anti-government messages contain less information that needs to be distorted, so they are cheaper (in terms of the required mass of trolls) to target.

On the other hand, for anti-government voters, trolls send all anti-government messages as before. However, the constraint on
\alpha_{x}
means that it is no longer possible to push the belief after these messages down to
\alpha_{x}
. Hence, the sender selects a strategy that pushes the belief after these messages as far down as possible.
8
8

8

Because the belief after messages
s\geq s^{*}\left(x\right)
can now be above
x
, this means that there are multiple signal densities that are optimal for the sender.

In short, the sender responds to the constraint on
\alpha_{x}
by reducing the set of messages that target pro-government voters, and the intensity of messages that target anti-government voters.

Allowing
\alpha_{x}
to be bounded adds nuance to the earlier result on the connection between the informativeness of voters’ signals and the voting outcomes. When there are no trolls, an increase in informativeness as defined by Definition
1
makes the electorate more likely to make the correct decision. On the other hand, Proposition
2
shows that in presence of trolls, an increase in informativeness makes it more likely that the government wins the election in each state. The former case is equivalent to assuming that
\bar{\alpha}_{x}=0
for all
x
, while the latter case is equivalent to assuming that
\bar{\alpha}_{x}=1
for all
x
. By continuity, this means that increased informativeness makes the electoral outcome more efficient
when
\bar{\alpha}_{x}
is sufficiently small, and less efficient – when it is sufficiently large.

7
Non-Bayesian Belief Updating

Substantial theoretical and empirical research has pointed to the fact that individuals may systematically deviate from Bayesian updating. In particular, voters can overweight or underweight signals
(
Tversky and
Kahneman 1992
;
Augenblick
et al. 2024
;
Ba
et al. 2023
;
Denter 2024
)
.
To account for such phenomena, suppose that upon observing signal realisation
s
, a voter forms her posterior belief as if she observed signal realisation
\beta\left(s\right)
, where
\beta\left(\cdot\right)
is a strictly increasing function that distorts the perception of the signal according to a particular type of belief updating. We assume that
\beta\left(0\right)=0
. Thus, not receiving an informative signal does not lead the voter to change her belief. Underweighting signals corresponds to the case when
\beta\left(s\right)>s
for
s<0
, and
\beta\left(s\right)<s
for
s>0
. In this case, the posterior belief for a given
s
is more conservative – that is, closer to the prior – than the Bayesian posterior belief. On the other hand, with overweighting,
\beta\left(s\right)<s
for
s<0
, and
\beta\left(s\right)>s
for
s>0
, implying less conservative beliefs.
9
9

9

Another potential deviation from Bayesian updating is voters being unaware of the existence of trolls. Evidence of such strategic naïveté has been found in communication (
Cai and
Wang 2006
;
Jin
et al. 2021
), and in voting interactions (
Patty and
Weber 2007
;
Ginzburg and
Guerra 2019
). We discuss the implications of this in the Online Appendix.

More generally, we can introduce a partial order on belief distortion functions
\beta
in terms of how conservative the resulting posterior beliefs are. We define it as follows:

Definition 3
.

A belief distortion function
\hat{\beta}
is
more conservative
than belief distortion function
\beta
if and only if

\hat{\beta}\left(s\right)>\beta\left(s\right)
for all
s<0
, and

\hat{\beta}\left(s\right)<\beta\left(s\right)
for all
s>0
.

We can then show that deviations from Bayesian updating can improve information aggregation. Specifically, the next result shows that whenever the sender can prevent information aggregation by ensuring government victory in both states, there exists a more conservative belief distortion function under which information aggregation is restored:

Proposition 6
.

Suppose
H\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)<\frac{1}{2}
. Suppose further that the government wins the election in both states under some belief distortion function
\beta\left(s\right)
. There exists a belief distortion function
\hat{\beta}\left(s\right)
that is more conservative than
\beta\left(s\right)
and under which the election aggregates information.

The intuition is similar to that of Proposition
3
: a more conservative distortion function moves the posterior belief of a given voter towards the prior. At the level of an individual voter, this has the same effect as a decrease in polarisation at the aggregate level.

8
Discussion

The paper has proposed an information design framework in which the sender is constrained in several ways. First, she cannot commit to an arbitrary mapping from states to signal distributions. Instead, she commits to a distribution of messages unconditional on the state. Second, the sender cannot commit to an arbitrary distribution of messages. Rather, she can replace the receivers’ independent signals, and receivers are uncertain as to the source from which signals originate. Because of this, the sender can induce information structures that are less precise than those induced by independent signals, but cannot induce information structures that are more precise.

Suppose that, in addition to source uncertainty, the sender also had the ability to commit to mappings from states to messages, as in standard models of Bayesian persuasion. Because the sender can fully replace the independent signals with messages of her own, she would be able to induce any Bayes-plausible distribution of posteriors. Thus, it would be optimal to replace all independent signals, and the model would become a standard model of Bayesian persuasion.

On the other hand, suppose that the sender had the same restriction on commitment power, but was also unable to mimic and replace voters’ private signals. In this case, the sender’s messages would complement voters’ private signals, as in a standard signalling game. Then, at the equilibrium, voters would ignore her messages, and so the sender would have no persuasive power. If, however, the sender had no commitment power at all, and could observe the state before selecting the message, then the game would be a form of a cheap talk game, in which, as explained below, no separating equilibrium would exist, and hence the sender would also have no ability to persuade voters.

Another feature of our model is that the sender cannot observe the state of the world, and hence chooses the same action in each state. Suppose instead that she is informed about the state.
In this case she can condition
\left(\alpha_{x},\tilde{F}_{x}\right)
on it. Then
\left(\alpha_{x},\tilde{F}_{x}\right)
serves as a signal about the state, and voters can update their beliefs after learning
\left(\alpha_{x},\tilde{F}_{x}\right)
.
Since trolls are costless, the setting becomes that of a cheap talk game. As usual, there exists a babbling equilibrium, in which the sender selects the same
\left(\alpha_{x},\tilde{F}_{x}\right)
in each state. This equilibrium outcome is equivalent to the setup of our model.
On the other hand, a separating equilibrium, in which the sender with positive probability chooses different values of
\left(\alpha_{x},\tilde{F}_{x}\right)
in different states, cannot exist. The reason is that if the sender’s choice reveals some information about the state, the government will receive more votes in state 0 than in state 1. Then in state 0 the sender will prefer to deviate to the same choice of
\left(\alpha_{x},\tilde{F}_{x}\right)
that she makes in state 1. Because only a babbling equilibrium exists, allowing the sender to observe the state does not change the outcome of the game.

9
Conclusions

This paper has analysed the strategy of a troll farm that aims to persuade a heterogeneous electorate to vote for the government by targeting each voter with a signal that emulates informative signals. It has shown that the optimal strategy of the troll farm differs considerably depending on whether a voter is ex ante in favour or against the government. For the former types of voters, the troll farm floods the information environment with anti-government messages in order to undermine their persuasive ability. For the latter voters, the troll farm sends pro-government messages in order to increase their quantity while keeping them persuasive.

From the analysis it follows that features of the political environment that are normally considered to be positive can in fact reduce the efficiency of electoral outcomes when troll farms are present. In particular, more informative independent signals
can amplify the power of troll farms, impeding information aggregation through voting. On the other hand, higher polarisation of the electorate, often considered to be a problem, can restore information aggregation. Likewise, deviations from Bayesian rationality can make electoral outcomes more efficient.

Apart from voting, the model can apply to various other interactions in which heterogeneous individuals make binary decisions.
For example, individual decisions on whether to take a vaccine, or on whether to participate in a climate protest, are based on information that individuals acquire also from social media. Troll farms are known to have spread vaccine-related
(
Ferrara et al. 2016
;
BBC 2018
)
and climate-related
(
Strudwicke and
Grant 2020
;
Euronews 2023
)

misinformation. Although discussing the efficiency of information aggregation is more difficult in these settings – unlike in electoral contexts, there is no clear binary political outcome – our analysis suggests a framework for analysing the optimal strategy of troll farms in these situations.

p_{\theta}\left(x\right)=\alpha_{x}+\left(1-\alpha_{x}\right)\int_{s\in\mathcal{S}\left(x\right)\cup\left[s^{*}\left(x\right),\infty\right)}f_{\theta}\left(s\right)ds.

This increases in
{\alpha}_{x}
for each
\theta
, which implies that at the optimum,
\alpha_{x}=\bar{\alpha}_{x}
.

Take a given support
\mathcal{S}\left(x\right)
. Take a signal
s
in the interior of
\mathcal{S}\left(x\right)
, and a sufficiently small
\delta>0
such that the interval
D:=\left(s-\delta,s+\delta\right)\in\mathcal{S}(x)
.
When
\delta
is sufficiently small, the mass of trolls’ signals in the interval
\left(s-\delta,s+\delta\right)
approximately equals

2\delta\tilde{f}_{x}\left(s\right)=2\delta\frac{1-\bar{\alpha}_{x}}{\bar{\alpha}_{x}}\kappa_{x}^{\prime}\left(s\right)=2\delta\frac{1-\bar{\alpha}_{x}}{\bar{\alpha}_{x}}\frac{xf_{0}\left(s\right)-\left(1-x\right)f_{1}\left(s\right)}{1-2x}.

Now consider the following deviation: remove
D
from
\mathcal{S}(x)
, and instead move the mass of trolls’ signals to an interval around some signal
s^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{S}\left(x\right)
such that
s^{\prime}<s^{*}\left(x\right)
, while making sure that
\pi\left(s^{\prime}\right)=x
. The latter condition means that
\tilde{f}_{x}\left(s^{\prime}\right)=\frac{1-\bar{\alpha}_{x}}{\bar{\alpha}_{x}}\frac{xf_{0}\left(s^{\prime}\right)-\left(1-x\right)f_{1}\left(s^{\prime}\right)}{1-2x}
. The
width of the interval around
s^{\prime}
that is added to
\mathcal{S}(x)
as a consequence of this deviation equals approximately

2\delta\frac{\tilde{f}_{x}\left(s\right)}{\tilde{f}_{x}\left(s^{\prime}\right)}=2\delta\frac{xf_{0}\left(s\right)-\left(1-x\right)f_{1}\left(s\right)}{xf_{0}\left(s^{\prime}\right)-\left(1-x\right)f_{1}\left(s^{\prime}\right)}.

In state
\theta\in\left(0,1\right)
, this deviation leads to a change in the probability that a voter of type
x
votes for the government that approximately equals

\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{x}\right)2\delta\frac{xf_{0}\left(s\right)-\left(1-x\right)f_{1}\left(s\right)}{xf_{0}\left(s^{\prime}\right)-\left(1-x\right)f_{1}\left(s^{\prime}\right)}f_{\theta}\left(s^{\prime}\right)-\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{x}\right)2\delta f_{\theta}\left(s\right).

The deviation is beneficial if and only if this is positive, i.e., if and only if

\dfrac{xf_{0}\left(s\right)-\left(1-x\right)f_{1}\left(s\right)}{xf_{0}\left(s^{\prime}\right)-\left(1-x\right)f_{1}\left(s^{\prime}\right)}>\dfrac{f_{\theta}\left(s\right)}{f_{\theta}\left(s^{\prime}\right)}.

In state
\theta=0
this is satisfied if and only if

\frac{xf_{0}\left(s\right)-\left(1-x\right)f_{1}\left(s\right)}{xf_{0}\left(s^{\prime}\right)-\left(1-x\right)f_{1}\left(s^{\prime}\right)}>\frac{f_{0}\left(s\right)}{f_{0}\left(s^{\prime}\right)}\iff\frac{f_{1}\left(s^{\prime}\right)}{f_{0}\left(s^{\prime}\right)}>\frac{f_{1}\left(s\right)}{f_{0}\left(s\right)}\iff s^{\prime}>s,

while in state
\theta=1
it has to hold that

\frac{xf_{0}\left(s\right)-\left(1-x\right)f_{1}\left(s\right)}{xf_{0}\left(s^{\prime}\right)-\left(1-x\right)f_{1}\left(s^{\prime}\right)}>\frac{f_{1}\left(s\right)}{f_{1}\left(s^{\prime}\right)}\iff\frac{f_{1}\left(s^{\prime}\right)}{f_{0}\left(s^{\prime}\right)}>\frac{f_{1}\left(s\right)}{f_{0}\left(s\right)}\iff s^{\prime}>s.

Hence, in each state
\theta
a deviation is profitable if and only if there is some
s^{\prime}
that is larger than any
s\in\mathcal{S}(x)
. This in turn implies that there exists no beneficial deviation if and only if
\mathcal{S}(x)=\left[\hat{s}\left(x\right),s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]
for some
\hat{s}\left(x\right)<s^{*}\left(x\right)
. Therefore, letting the superscript “
C
” denote the optimal choice for a constrained sender, when
x<\frac{1}{2}
and
\bar{\alpha}_{x}<\alpha_{x}^{*}
, the sender chooses
\alpha_{x}^{\text{C}}=\bar{\alpha}_{x}
and

\tilde{f}_{x}^{\text{C}}\left(s\right)=\begin{cases}\frac{1-\bar{\alpha}_{x}}{\bar{\alpha}_{x}}\kappa_{x}^{\prime}\left(s\right)&\forall s\in\mathcal{S}(x)\\
0&\forall s\notin\mathcal{S}\left(x\right),\end{cases}

where
\mathcal{S}=\left[\hat{s}\left(x\right),s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]
, and where
\hat{s}\left(x\right)
solves

\int_{\hat{s}\left(x\right)}^{s^{*}\left(x\right)}\frac{1-\bar{\alpha}_{x}}{\bar{\alpha}_{x}}\kappa_{x}^{\prime}\left(s\right)ds=1
.
Further, to see that
\hat{s}\left(x\right)
always exists and is unique, note that when
\hat{s}\left(x\right)\rightarrow s^{*}(x)
, the integral approaches zero. At the same time, as
\hat{s}\left(x\right)\rightarrow-\infty
, the integral becomes strictly greater than one because
\bar{\alpha}_{x}<\alpha_{x}^{*}
and the integral equals one when
\bar{\alpha}_{x}=\alpha_{x}^{*}
. Existence of
\hat{s}(x)
then follows because the integral is continuous in
\hat{s}(x)
. Uniqueness follows from the fact that
\kappa_{x}^{\prime}\left(s\right)>0
for all
s<s^{*}\left(x\right)
, and hence the integral is strictly monotone in
\hat{s}\left(s\right)
.
This proves the second part of the proposition.∎

A.8
Proof of Proposition
6

In the absence of trolls, a voter, upon observing signal
s
updates her belief in a manner similar to the one described in Section
3
, except that she perceives the signal to equal
\beta\left(s\right)
. She thus votes for the government if and only if
\beta\left(s\right)\geq s^{*}\left(x\right)
, where, as before,
s^{*}\left(x\right):=m^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{1-x}\right)
. Using the same steps as in Proposition
1
and Corollary
2
, with
s
replaced by
\beta\left(s\right)
, we can show that at the equilibrium, the share of voters voting for the government in state
\theta=0
equals

V_{0}=H\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)+\int_{\frac{1}{2}}^{1}\frac{F_{0}\left(\beta^{-1}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]\right)-F_{1}\left(\beta^{-1}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]\right)}{\frac{x}{1-x}F_{0}\left(\beta^{-1}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]\right)-F_{1}\left(\beta^{-1}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]\right)}dH\left(x\right),

while in state
\theta=1
it equals

V_{1}=H\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)+\int_{\frac{1}{2}}^{1}\frac{F_{0}\left(\beta^{-1}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]\right)-F_{1}\left(\beta^{-1}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]\right)}{F_{0}\left(\beta^{-1}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]\right)-\frac{1-x}{x}F_{1}\left(\beta^{-1}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]\right)}dH\left(x\right).

Furthermore,
V_{0}<V_{1}
.

Suppose that when voters form their beliefs according to some distortion function
\beta\left(s\right)
, the government wins the election in both states, that is,
V_{1}>V_{0}\geq\frac{1}{2}
.
Take a family of belief distortion functions indexed by
r\in\left(0,\infty\right)
, of the form
\beta_{r}\left(s\right)=\frac{\beta\left(s\right)}{r}
. Note that
\beta_{r}\left(s\right)
is more conservative whenever
r
is higher. Furthermore,
\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\beta_{r}\left(s\right)=0
for all
s
. Since
\beta\left(0\right)=0
, we have

\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}V_{0}=H\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)+\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\frac{1}{2}}^{1}\frac{F_{0}\left(0\right)-F_{1}\left(0\right)}{\frac{x}{1-x}F_{0}\left(0\right)-F_{1}\left(0\right)}dH\left(x\right)=H\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)<\frac{1}{2},

and

\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}V_{1}=H\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)+\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\frac{1}{2}}^{1}\frac{F_{0}\left(0\right)-F_{1}\left(0\right)}{F_{0}\left(0\right)-\frac{1-x}{x}F_{1}\left(0\right)}dH\left(x\right)=H\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)<\frac{1}{2}

Hence, in the limit, the government loses the election in both states. Since
V_{0}<V_{1}
for all
r
, by continuity there exists
r>1
at which
V_{1}>\frac{1}{2}>V_{0}
, that is, at which the election aggregates information.∎

Supplement to
Troll Farms

In this supplementary appendix, we consider two extensions to the main model.

Strategic Naïveté

Suppose voters are strategically naive, and do not understand the incentives of the sender. This would mean that they are unaware of existence of trolls, and update their beliefs thinking that all signals are informative.

Formally, suppose that a fraction
\phi\in[0,1]
of voters update their beliefs assuming that
\alpha_{x}=0
. When
\phi=0
, the setting is equivalent to our baseline model in Section
4
. If these voters can be targeted – that is, if for every type
x
of the voter the sender can condition her choice of
\alpha_{x}
and
\tilde{F}_{x}
on whether the voter is naive – then any naive voter can be persuaded to vote for the government by setting
\alpha_{x}\rightarrow 1
and choosing
\tilde{F}_{x}
in such a way that
\tilde{F}\left[s^{*}\left(x\right)\right]=0
. Under this strategy, every naive voter will, with probability one, receive a signal that induces her to vote for the government. All naive voters vote for the government, while for the remaining voters, the sender chooses a strategy equivalent to the one described in Section
4
. Hence, adding naive voters is isomorphic to increasing
H\left(0\right)
; thus, the results of Section
5
remain unchanged.

Anti-Government Trolls

The government may be not the only side that has trolls on its side. Suppose that the opposing side can use the same tactic. In terms of our model, suppose that there are two senders, one of which is trying to ensure that the government wins, and the other is trying to achieve the opposite outcome. Each sender can set up a troll farm that would, as before, send messages uncorrelated with the state. The setting then becomes a zero-sum game between the two senders.

As in the baseline model, the pro-government sender can persuade any voter with
x\leq\frac{1}{2}
– that is, any voter who is in favour of the government at her prior belief – to vote for the government by setting
\alpha_{x}\rightarrow 1
, which eliminates the informative signal for this voter. On the other hand, by similar logic the anti-government sender can persuade any voter with
x>\frac{1}{2}
to vote against the government by also setting
\alpha_{x}\rightarrow 1
. Hence, in this setting, the government wins the election if and only if
H\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}
. Because the outcome of the election does not depend on the state, the election does not aggregate information.
