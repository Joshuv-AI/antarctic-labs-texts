Abstract
. We present scalable distributed-memory algorithms for sparse matrix permutation, extraction, and assignment. Our methods follow an Identify–Exchange–Build (IEB) strategy where each process identifies the local nonzeros to be sent, exchanges the required data, and then builds its local submatrix from the received elements. This approach reduces communication compared to SpGEMM-based methods in distributed memory. By employing synchronization-free multithreaded algorithms, we further accelerate local computations, achieving substantially better performance than existing libraries such as CombBLAS and PETSc. We design efficient software for these operations and evaluate their performance on two university clusters and the Perlmutter supercomputer. Our experiments span a variety of application scenarios, including matrix permutation for load balancing, matrix reordering, subgraph extraction, and streaming graph applications. In all cases, we compare our algorithms against CombBLAS, the most comprehensive distributed library for these operations, and, in some scenarios, against PETSc. Overall, this work provides a comprehensive study of algorithms, software implementations, experimental evaluations, and applications for sparse matrix permutation, extraction, and assignment.



1.
Introduction

Sparse matrices are foundational to many scientific computing and machine learning problems in science and engineering.
Among the most fundamental operations involving sparse matrices are permutation, submatrix extraction, and submatrix assignment. For instance, random permutations can help balance load in distributed environments
(
Azad et al. 2021
)
, while user-defined permutations may impose desirable structures, such as concentrating nonzeros along the diagonal in sparse linear solvers
(
Duff and Koster 2001
)
.
In dynamic and streaming graph applications, batches of edges can be added or removed using matrix extract and assign operations, respectively.
Due to the broad applicability of these operations, they are supported in nearly all major sparse matrix libraries, including PETSc
(
Balay et al. 2014
)
, CombBLAS
(
Azad et al. 2021
)
, Trilinos
(
Heroux et al. 2005
)
, GraphBLAS
(
Davis 2019
)
, and SuperLU
(
Li and Demmel 2003
)
, among others.

Algorithms for sparse matrix permutation, extraction, and assignment generally follow one of two approaches:
(1) those that leverage sparse matrix-matrix multiplication (SpGEMM), and
(2) those that directly access and manipulate the relevant nonzeros in the matrix without constructing intermediate matrices.
In the first approach, matrix permutation can be defined as
\mathbf{A^{\prime}}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q^{T}}
, where
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{Q}
are Boolean row and column permutation matrices and
\mathbf{A}
is the original matrix.
Similarly, matrix extract and assign operations can be expressed as combinations of two SpGEMM operations
(
Buluç and Gilbert 2012
)
.
Buluc and Gilbert proposed efficient SpGEMM-based indexing algorithms, which were implemented in the CombBLAS library
(
Buluç and Gilbert 2012
;
Azad et al. 2021
)
.
The second approach performs these operations by directly communicating the necessary data using collective or point-to-point communication.
We call this approach
Identify-Exchange-Build
(IEB), where each process identifies the local nonzero elements that need to be sent to other processes, performs the required data exchange, and then builds the local submatrix using the received elements.
Several widely used libraries, such as PETSc
(
Balay et al. 2014
)
and SuperLU_Dist
(
Li and Demmel 2003
)
, implement matrix permutation using this communication-based approach.

While SpGEMM-based algorithms are asymptotically efficient
(
Buluç and Gilbert 2012
)
, our experiments show that their practical performance is often inferior to that of IEB approaches. The suboptimal performance of SpGEMM-based permutation, assign, and extract operations can be attributed to several factors.
These methods typically require two separate SpGEMM operations, which can increase both communication overhead and memory usage.
Furthermore, general-purpose SpGEMM implementations are not optimized for permutation and indexing tasks.
For instance, in matrix permutation, the permutation matrix is extremely sparse, containing only one nonzero per row or column. Distributed SpGEMM algorithms that are effective for multiplying matrices with comparable sparsity patterns are not well-suited for such highly imbalanced sparsity.
As an example, CombBLAS implements matrix permutation using Sparse SUMMA
(
Buluç and Gilbert 2012
)
, which keeps the output matrix stationary while broadcasting submatrices of the input matrices.
However, when one of the matrices (e.g., the permutation matrix) is extremely sparse, much of the communicated data may be unused, resulting in wasted communication. Consequently, even Sparse SUMMA, one of the best-performing distributed SpGEMM algorithms, performs poorly for permutation, and by extension, for extract and assign operations.

By contrast, IEB-based models can be carefully tailored to exploit the structural characteristics of permutation, extraction, and assignment.
However, they also present inherent challenges, such as the need for substantial local computation to identify the positions of new elements and to rebuild and sort the local submatrix after data exchange.
Addressing these challenges requires efficient parallelization strategies at both the process and thread levels, along with optimized communication routines.

This paper presents scalable algorithms for sparse matrix permutation, extraction, and assignment on distributed-memory systems.
Our algorithms are designed under the IEB model and exploit shared computational and communication patterns across these operations.
For communication, our algorithms achieve theoretical lower bounds for matrix permutation, extraction, and assignment operations.
For local computation, we develop multithreaded algorithms that avoid synchronization among threads to achieve linear thread-level scalability within nodes.
As a result, our algorithms demonstrate strong scalability, running efficiently on up to 4,096 processes (65,536 cores across 512 nodes) of the Perlmutter supercomputer.

Our algorithms enable customized fusion of permutation, extraction, and assignment operations, which are frequently required in applications involving dynamic matrices and graphs.
We demonstrate this flexibility through an incremental protein clustering application on a large-scale metagenomic dataset. Achieving such fused operations is significantly more difficult with existing approaches.

Overall, this paper makes the following contributions:

(1)

Distributed-memory algorithms
for sparse matrix permutation, extraction, and assignment based on the Identify-Exchange-Build (IEB) model. These operations share similar computation and communication patterns, enabling a unified and efficient design.

(2)

Scalability:
Our algorithms demonstrate linear thread-level scalability within nodes and excellent parallel efficiency across nodes, scaling up to 4,096 processes (65,536 cores across 512 nodes) on the Perlmutter supercomputer.

(3)

Application-driven evaluation:
We evaluated our algorithms across various application scenarios, including load-balanced permutation, bandwidth-reducing reordering, cluster extraction from graphs, and streaming graph updates. We also integrated multiple sparse matrix operations into an incremental protein clustering application, showcasing both the flexibility and performance of our approach.

2.
Problem Formulation and Related Work

2.1.
Permuting a sparse matrix

Given a sparse matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, we consider a row permutation vector
\mathbf{pvec}
representing a permutation of
(1,2,\ldots,m)
, and a column permutation vector
\mathbf{qvec}
representing a permutation of
(1,2,\ldots,n)
.
Then, the permuted matrix
\mathbf{A^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
shuffles nonzero entries in
\mathbf{A}
such that
\mathbf{A^{\prime}}[i,j]=\mathbf{A}[\mathbf{pvec}[i],\mathbf{qvec}[j]]
. Matrix permutation can also be expressed using matrix multiplication. Let
\mathbf{P}
be a
m\times m
sparse Boolean matrix such that
\mathbf{P}[i,j]=1
if
j=\mathbf{pvec}[i]
, and
\mathbf{P}[i,j]=0
otherwise.
Similarly, let
\mathbf{Q}
be a
n\times n
sparse Boolean matrix such that
\mathbf{Q}[i,j]=1
if
j=\mathbf{qvec}[i]
, and
\mathbf{Q}[i,j]=0
otherwise.

Then, matrix permutation can be defined as
\mathbf{A^{\prime}}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q^{T}}
.
For square matrices, the same permutation can be applied to both rows and columns in the form
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}^{\mbox{\scriptsize\sf T}}
.
In the matrix formulation, matrix permutations can be implemented using sparse matrix-matrix multiplication (SpGEMM).
Figure
1
(a) illustrates an example of permuting
\mathbf{A}
by two randomly generated permutation vectors.

Variants of matrix permutations.

Different applications permute matrices for various purposes, resulting in multiple variants of this operation.
In parallel applications, a randomly generated permutation vector typically redistributes nonzeros throughout the matrix, helping to balance computational and memory loads evenly across processors.
On the other hand, a user-defined permutation can impose a desired structure, such as concentrating nonzeros along the diagonal
(
Balaji et al. 2023
;
Trotter et al. 2023
)
.
For example, we can permute a matrix to reduce its bandwidth in iterative solvers or ensure a zero-free diagonal during the pivoting step of direct solvers.
Various algorithms can be used to obtain such orderings, including Reverse Cuthill-McKee (RCM)
(
Cuthill and McKee 1969
;
Azad et al. 2017
)
, minimum-degree ordering
(
George and Liu 1989
)
, and bipartite matching
(
Duff and Koster 2001
;
Azad et al. 2020
)
.
When
\mathbf{A}
represents the adjacency matrix of a graph, we use a symmetric permutation, which means that
\mathbf{pvec}=\mathbf{qvec}
.
In this setting, the permutation reorders the nodes of the graph.

2.2.
Extracting a submatrix from a matrix

The extract operation retrieves a submatrix from a larger matrix
\mathbf{A}\in{\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}}
based on specified subsets of row and column indices.
Let
\mathbf{pvec}
be a set of
m^{\prime}
row indices selected from the set of all row indices
\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
and
\mathbf{qvec}
be a set of
n^{\prime}
row indices selected from the set of all column indices
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.
The extracted submatrix
\mathbf{B}\in{\mathbb{R}^{m^{\prime}\times n^{\prime}}}
includes nonzeros from
\mathbf{A}
selected by Cartesian products of
\mathbf{pvec}
and
\mathbf{qvec}
:
\mathbf{B}[i,j]=\mathbf{A}[\mathbf{pvec}[i],\mathbf{qvec}[j]]
for all
(i,j)\in\mathbf{pvec}\times\mathbf{qvec}
.
A matrix multiplication-based method can also perform submatrix extraction, formulated as
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}^{T}
.
Here
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{m^{\prime}\times m}
is a sparse Boolean matrix where
\mathbf{P}[i,j]=1
if
j=\mathbf{pvec}[i]
and
\mathbf{P}[i,j]=0
otherwise. Similarly,
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime}\times n}
is a sparse Boolean matrix where
\mathbf{Q}[i,j]=1
if
j=\mathbf{qvec}[i]
and
\mathbf{Q}[i,j]=0
otherwise
(
Buluç and Gilbert 2012
)
.
The submatrix extraction is achieved through a two matrix multiplication: first,
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}
selects the target rows, and then
(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A})\mathbf{Q}^{T}
extracts the desired columns from that intermediate result of the first multiplication.
Figure
1
(c) demonstrates an example of extracting a submatrix corresponding to the selecting vectors
\mathbf{pvec}
and
\mathbf{qvec}
.

Variants of matrix extract.

The row and column indices in
\mathbf{pvec}
and
\mathbf{qvec}
are not required to be unique. For instance, it is possible to extract the same row multiple times if it appears more than once in
\mathbf{pvec}
.
However, in some applications, the indices must be unique. For example, if
\mathbf{A}
represents the adjacency matrix of a graph, then the extracted matrix
\mathbf{B}
corresponds to the adjacency matrix of a subgraph defined by the node indices in
\mathbf{pvec}
and
\mathbf{qvec}
.
In this case, both
\mathbf{pvec}
and
\mathbf{qvec}
contain unique values.
Additionally, the indices in
\mathbf{pvec}
and
\mathbf{qvec}
may or may not be ordered. When the indices are sorted in ascending order, the extracted submatrix preserves the original row and column order. When the indices are unordered, the resulting submatrix permutes the rows and columns according to the order specified in
\mathbf{pvec}
and
\mathbf{qvec}
. This behavior allows the extract operation to serve as a general mechanism for matrix permutation. In particular, if
\mathbf{pvec}
and
\mathbf{qvec}
represent full permutations of the row and column index sets, the extract operation effectively performs a matrix permutation. Thus, matrix permutation can be viewed as a special case of submatrix extraction.

2.3.
Assigning a matrix to a larger matrix

The assign operation inserts a sparse matrix
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m^{\prime}\times n^{\prime}}
into a larger sparse matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
where
m^{\prime}\leq m
and
n^{\prime}\leq n
.
The location where
\mathbf{B}
is assigned within
\mathbf{A}
is specified by two vectors,
\mathbf{pvec}
and
\mathbf{qvec}
, which contain distinct row and column indices of
\mathbf{A}
, respectively.
The elements of
\mathbf{B}
are placed at the positions in
\mathbf{A}
indicated by these indices, such that
\mathbf{A}[\mathbf{pvec}[i],\mathbf{qvec}[j]]=\mathbf{B}[i,j]
for all nonzero indices
i
and
j
.
Matrix assignment can be done through matrix multiplication and a user-defined addition operation, indicated as
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{P}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{Q}^{T}
where
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m^{\prime}}
is sparse Boolean matrix where
\mathbf{P}[i,j]=1
if
i=\mathbf{pvec}[j]
and
\mathbf{P}[i,j]=0
otherwise.
Likewise,
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n^{\prime}}
is a sparse Boolean matrix where
\mathbf{Q}[i,j]=1
if
i=\mathbf{qvec}[j]
and
\mathbf{Q}[i,j]=0
otherwise
(
Buluç and Gilbert 2012
)
.
The choice of the addition operator is application-specific. It includes arithmetic addition, the select-second operator, and a logical OR (union) operator.
Figure
1
(b) demonstrates an example of operation on sample matrices.

Variants of matrix assignment.

In the most common variant of the assign operation, any existing value at position
\mathbf{A}[\mathbf{pvec}[i],\mathbf{qvec}[j]]
is overwritten by the corresponding value from
\mathbf{B}
.
However, in another variant, the values are added instead, following the rule
\mathbf{A}[\mathbf{pvec}[i],\mathbf{qvec}[j]]=\mathbf{A}[\mathbf{pvec}[i],\mathbf{qvec}[j]]+\mathbf{B}[i,j]
. This additive form is useful for updating sparse matrices, as seen in multifrontal methods
(
Liu 1992
)
.
In dynamic and streaming graphs, the subgraph assign operation is equivalent to adding a batch of new edges to a graph.
Let
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
be the adjacency matrices of graphs
G_{\mathbf{A}}
and
G_{\mathbf{B}}
, respectively.
Assigning
\mathbf{B}
to
\mathbf{A}
using row and column index vectors
\mathbf{pvec}
and
\mathbf{qvec}
corresponds to adding each edge
(i,j)
in
G_{\mathbf{B}}
as an edge
(\mathbf{pvec}[i],\mathbf{qvec}[j])
in
G_{\mathbf{A}}
.

3.
Related Work

Classic applications of sparse matrix permutation are found in numerical solvers
(
Davis et al. 2016
;
Davis and Palamadai Natarajan 2010
)
.
When solving large systems of equations, one often encounters large sparse matrices that require permutation to enhance numerical stability and computational efficiency.
For instance, Reverse Cuthill–McKee (RCM) algorithm attempts to pull non-zero elements toward the diagonal, thus producing bandwidth-minimized sparse matrix
(
Cuthill and McKee 1969
)
.
Or the Approximate Minimum Degree (AMD) algorithm attempts to permute the sparse matrix for fill-in reduction
(
Amestoy et al. 1996
)
.
Consequently, most numerical solvers and numerical linear algebra libraries include support for sparse matrix permutation.
In addition to numerical applications, sparse matrix permutation plays a significant role in graph algorithms.
Here, the arrangement of rows and columns is altered to ensure an even distribution of work during graph processing.
Beyond permutation, the extraction or assignment of submatrices is also utilized in the context of streaming batch updates to a graph
(
Hassani et al. 2024
)
.
As a result, these operations are also supported by various graph analysis frameworks.
We review related work from both parallelization and application perspectives, covering shared-memory algorithms, distributed-memory algorithms, and graph update frameworks.

Distributed memory frameworks.

CombBLAS
(
Azad et al. 2021
)
is an MPI-based distributed memory graph library that leverages 2D/3D partitioning of sparse matrices to minimize communication overhead and optimize performance
(
Buluç and Gilbert 2012
)
.
It supports different functions like sparse matrix permutation and reordering, extracting a submatrix, and matrix assignment using an SpGEMM-based implementation.
Trilinos
(
Heroux et al. 2005
;
Trilinos Project Team 2020
)
is another well-known software framework designed for scalable scientific computing, featuring a variety of linear algebraic packages.
Packages like Tpetra and Epetra
(
Tpetra Project Team 2020
;
Epetra Project Team 2020
)
with Kokkos can be adapted for tasks such as submatrix extraction, matrix permutation, and assignment.
Both Tpetra and Epetra packages, follow IEB-like approach where Export/Import objects perform communication between the processes in a particular distribution pattern call Maps, and subclasses define how to pack elements into a buffer for sending with MPI, and how to unpack received elements.
Additionally, for assigning functions,
SumIntoGlobalValues
or
ReplaceGlobalValues
is used to update entries in local matrix classes.
While Trilinos is primarily a 1D row block distributed framework, it can also be adapted for 2D distribution problems
(
Mayr et al. 2025
)
.

Another distributed memory library PETSc
(
Zhang et al. 2022
)
is designed for scientific computing focused on numerical solutions.
PETSc’s data structures and algorithms are capable of different sparse matrix computations including permutation, reordering for special structures, submatrix extraction and assignment.
This framework uses 1D row block distribution MPI with support for inter process parallelization with MPI and local parallelization through OpenMP.
However, not all functions in PETSc is OpenMP parallel.
PETSc permute function
MatPermute
reorders a sparse matrix by creating a new matrix that incorporates the new ordering by performing necessary inter process communication.
Extracting a submatrix with
MatCreateSubMatrix
function also involves communication between processes to gather non-local data required for creating a submatrix.
To assign a matrix in PETSc,
MatSetValues
is used to assign new elements to any location of the global matrix.
For elements that belongs to a different process, PETSc buffers the values locally until
MatAssemblyBegin
is called, followed by
MatAssemblyEnd
.
These functions involve collection communication to ensure all buffered data is exchanged.

SuperLU
(
Demmel et al. 1999b
;
Li 2005
)
is a general purpose library for the direct solution of sparse linear systems.
SuperLU mainly provides functions for LU factorization of sparse matrices along with related routines for matrix reordering.
This library provides separate implementations optimized for distributed memory systems, SuperLU_DIST
(
Li and Demmel 2003
)
, shared-memory multicore processors, SuperLU_MT
(
Demmel et al. 1999a
)
, GPU-accelerated platforms through the SuperLU_GPU extension
(
Li et al. 2023
)
.
SuperLU’s design avoids explicit matrix permutation due to its complexity.
However, in
Preprocessing
step of its distributed memory algorithms on matrix
\mathbf{A}
, permutation vectors
{\mathbf{P_{r}}}
and
{\mathbf{P_{c}}}
computed such that
{\mathbf{P_{r}}}A{\mathbf{P_{c}}}
represent a more numerically stable and sparser matrix suitable for LU factorization algorithms.
Then in further steps of algorithms,
{\mathbf{P_{r}}}
and
{\mathbf{P_{c}}}
guide the access pattern for better locality
(
Li et al. 2023
;
Li 1998
)
.

Cyclops Tensor Framework (CTF)
(
Solomonik et al. 2014
)
is a distributed memory numerical library designed for parallel computation on tensors.
It supports a variety of data distribution models, including 1D, 2D, and 3D, and leverages a hybrid MPI+OpenMP parallelism model.
Indexing operations, such as permutation, extraction, and assignment, are implemented in CTF using a method conceptually similar to the IEB model.
Each process first identifies the locations of elements to be manipulated, then redistributes the data, and finally performs the local computation to update values.
However, CTF uses the index mapping vectors for subsequent computations instead of explicitly relocating elements or creating a new submatrix, thus delaying communication until it is strictly necessary
(
Solomonik et al. 2013
)
.

Shared-memory frameworks.

GraphBLAS
(
Davis 2019
;
Davis 2023
)
is a shared memory linear algebraic graph framework that provides sparse matrix operations to support parallel graph algorithms.
This library supports core indexing operations such as permutation, subgraph extraction, and graph assignment.
Permutation and submatrix extraction are achieved using
GrB_extract
primitive which creates a new matrix.
This operation is done in multiple phases to ensure work is evenly divided into parallel tasks, leading to better parallelism.
In GraphBLAS a sparse matrix can be updated with another matrix using the assignment operation
GrB_assign
.

GrB_assign
operation postpones changes to the matrix data structure, buffering new or modified entries in an intermediate format until a subsequent phase or explicit call.
Parallel implementations of these operations are highly optimized and outperform MATLAB when multiple OpenMP threads are used
(
Aznaveh et al. 2020
)
.
GraphBLAST
(
Yang et al. 2022
)
is the GPU implementation of GraphBLAS, which supports all three indexing operations mentioned.

Galois
(
Nguyen et al. 2013
)
is another shared memory graph analytics library designed for parallel implementations of graph operations, supporting irregular graph processing tasks.
Permuting a graph can be achieved using
permute
function in Galois.
Galois has a direct approach to remap matrix locations for permutation, and it is OpenMP parallel.
Galois doesn’t have a specific function to extract a submatrix.
Since Galois supports dynamic graph capabilities with its
MorphGraph
data structure, matrix assignment is possible when the graph is represented this way.
The assign operation is not supported for graphs represented with the CSR data structure in Galois.
D-Galois
(
Dang et al. 2018
)
is the distributed version, and CUDA-integrated Galois for GPUs extends Galois’s core operations to distributed and GPU environments.
However, both D-Galois and the GPU version are optimized for specific graph functions and currently lack support for dynamic graph updates or subgraph extraction.

Batch graph update frameworks.

To support batch graph updates, several frameworks employ different data structures and memory models to optimize for graph assignment.
STINGER
(
Ediger et al. 2012
)
is a shared memory framework that offers a blocked linked list data structure suitable for streaming graphs and supports batch and single updates on graphs.
Because STINGER employs a blocked adjacency structure with preallocated memory for vertices it is highly optimized for batch updates without the need for rebuilding the graph adjacency matrix.
DISTINGER
(
Feng et al. 2015
)
extends STINGER’s capabilities to distributed memory systems, using MPI for communication.
For GPU environments, cuSTINGER
(
Green and Bader 2016
)
and Hornet
(
Busato et al. 2018
)
both leverage GPU parallelism, with cuSTINGER adapting STINGER’s features with different data structures of arrays for the neighbor lists and Hornet introducing a data structure of compressed adjacency list on dynamic graphs.
Aspen
(
Dhulipala et al. 2019
)
is another shared memory graph streaming system with tree based data structure which supports batch update of graphs with high throughput.
SSTGraph
(
Wheatman and Burns 2021
)
, a shared memory parallel framework, leverages a dynamic set data structure for streaming graphs and supports batch graph update through sort merge method.
GraphIn
(
Sengupta et al. 2016
)
is a distributed framework for incremental graph processing that handles updates and querying of the dynamic graph.
Its data structure is hybrid with compressed matrix format for static and edgelist for updates.

Although batch updates can achieve higher throughput, many streaming and dynamic graph frameworks must sort batches before updating their graph data structure.
This sorting impacts the performance of the batch updates
(
Wheatman and Burns 2021
;
Dhulipala et al. 2019
;
Pandey et al. 2021
)
.

4.
HipPerm: High-performance Matrix Permutations in Distributed Memory

4.1.
Data Distribution

Distributed matrix computations typically partition matrices across processes using 1D (row/column), 2D (block/checkerboard), or 3D (layers of 2D partitions) layouts.
Each of these partitioning techniques comes with distinct communication and memory footprints.
One-dimensional partitions often scale poorly at large process counts, particularly for irregular and highly imbalanced sparsity where nonzeros are concentrated in only a few rows or columns.
Two-dimensional layouts mitigate these issues by distributing nonzeros more evenly across processes, reducing memory and communication bottlenecks.
Three-dimensional schemes are used mainly for matrix–matrix multiplication to lower communication at the cost of additional replication and complexity, and are not general-purpose for arbitrary sparse kernels.
Given the general-purpose nature of permutation, extraction, and assignment operations, we adopt a 2D partitioning of matrices and vectors for all algorithms presented in this paper.

Vectors are also partitioned among all
p

We denote the subvector of the vector
\mathbf{vec}
stored on

\mathbf{vec}_{r,c}
.
Considering a
\sqrt{p}\times\sqrt{p}

\mathbf{vec}

with the length of
\lfloor\frac{\lvert\mathbf{vec}\lvert}{\sqrt{p}}\rfloor
.
Within the same row, the subvector
\mathbf{vec}(r,:)
is further divided between
\sqrt{p}
process, such that each process holds a subvector of length
\lfloor\frac{\lvert\mathbf{vec}(r,:)\lvert}{\sqrt{p}}\rfloor
.

We denote the number of nonzeros in a matrix

\mathbf{A}
as
nnz(\mathbf{A})
.
When the average number of nonzeros per column is on the same order as the number of processes, some local submatrices may contain columns with no nonzero entries. Such matrices are referred to as hypersparse matrices.
To efficiently store such matrices, we use the doubly compressed sparse column (DCSC) format, an adaptation of the standard compressed sparse column (CSC) structure that stores only the columns containing at least one nonzero element
(
Buluc and Gilbert 2008
)
.
All algorithms discussed in this paper use the same 2D data distribution and DCSC data structures.

4.2.
The HipPerm Algorithm

As defined in Section
2.1
, the permutation of a matrix is defined as
\mathbf{A^{\prime}}[i,j]=\mathbf{A}[\mathbf{pvec}[i],\mathbf{qvec}[j]]
, where the input matrix, permutation vectors and the permuted matrix are distributed on a 2D process grid.
In a distributed setting, the nonzeros
\mathbf{A}[\mathbf{pvec}[i],\mathbf{qvec}[j]]
and
\mathbf{A^{\prime}}[i,j]
may reside on different processes.
Therefore, each process must determine the destination of its local nonzeros, exchange data with other processes accordingly, and reconstruct the permuted matrix after communication.
We refer to this three-step approach as
Identify-Exchange-Build
: the first step
identifies
the destination process for each local nonzero; the second step performs data
exchange
using an

Alltoallv
communication; and the third step
builds
the permuted matrix locally on each process.

Our distributed permutation algorithm, HipPerm, is based on the three phases of the Identify–Exchange–Build framework.
We describe these steps using Figure

4.2.1.
Step 1: Identify Permuted Indices and Values

The first step of HipPerm is to identify the destination process for each local nonzero and populate a buffer for data exchange.
We subdivide this step into three substeps.

(a) Gather necessary indices.

By definition of permutation, the entry
\mathbf{A}[\mathbf{pvec}[i],\mathbf{qvec}[j]]

\mathbf{A^{\prime}}[i,j]

\mathbf{pvec}[i]

\mathbf{qvec}[j]

To avoid such remote indexing of
\mathbf{A}

\mathbf{pvec}
(and similarly for
\mathbf{qvec}

\mathbf{pvec}[\mathbf{pvec\_inv}[i]]=i

In other words, each element
\mathbf{pvec\_inv}[i]

\mathbf{pvec}

Substep (a) in Figure

Alltoall

\mathbf{pvec}
and
\mathbf{qvec}

Next, each process gathers
\mathbf{pvec\_inv}
along the process grid row and
\mathbf{qvec\_inv}
along the process grid column to obtain the necessary indices to access the local submatrix.
For example, in Substep (b) of Figure

P_{1,2}
gathers
\mathbf{pvec\_inv}
from
P_{1,1}
and
P_{1,2}

\mathbf{pvec\_loc}

This step performs two

(b) Access the local submatrix, identify destination processes, and populate a send buffer.

\mathbf{A}_{r,c}[i,j]

lrow

lcol

4.3.
Multithreaded Algorithms for Local Computations

As shown in Algorithm
1
, the HipPerm algorithm includes two local computation functions:
PrepareSendBuffer
and
BuildLocalMatrix
.
Among these,
PrepareSendBuffer
is by far the most computationally intensive, as it involves accessing all local nonzeros on each process
P_{i,j}
, determining their destination processes, and preparing the buffer for data exchange.
Our approach to parallelizing these local computations within each node is illustrated in Algorithm
2
and Figure
3
.

The primary objectives of our multithreaded local computation are: (a) to achieve load-balanced execution across threads, and (b) to eliminate thread synchronization entirely, enabling linear scalability within each node.
To ensure balanced workload across the
nthreads
threads, we partition the local submatrix by columns into
nthreads
parts, each containing approximately
\frac{\textit{nnz}}{\texttt{nthreads}}
nonzeros.
Each thread then processes one part independently and in parallel.

To eliminate thread synchronization when populating the shared send buffer, we process the local submatrix
\mathbf{A}_{r,c}
in two passes.
In the first pass, each thread counts how many nonzero elements need to be sent to each process.
This step fills a
\texttt{nthreads}\times p
array called
nnzCounter
, where
nnzCounter
[
i,j
] stores the number of nonzeros identified by thread
i
that are destined for process
j
, as shown in line 5-10 in Algorithm
2
and Figure
3
(i).

We use
nnzCounter
to compute unique write indices into
SendBuffer
, allowing all threads to write without conflicts.
First, we apply a row-wise prefix sum on
nnzCounter
, followed by a column-wise sum to generate the
proc_pointer
vector. The data to be sent to the
i
th process is stored starting at
\texttt{SendBuffer}[\texttt{proc\_pointer}[i]]
.
Since multiple threads may simultaneously process data destined for the same process, concurrent writes to
SendBuffer
can lead to conflicts. To avoid this, each thread computes a specific offset within the range
SendBuffer
[
proc_pointer
[
i
]] to
SendBuffer
[
proc_pointer
[
i+1
]] where it can safely write its data for process
i
.
These per-thread offsets are computed via a column-wise prefix sum on the
nnzCounter
array, resulting in the
thread_offset
array. For example,
thread_offset
[
t,i
] stores the offset where thread
t
writes data destined for process
i
.
The pointer and offset calculations are illustrated in Figure
3
(ii) and are encapsulated in the function
SendBufferIndex
in Algorithm
2
.

After the thread-specific indices in
SendBuffer
are computed, the
t
th thread processes the nonzeros in the
t
th partition of the local submatrix
\mathbf{A}_{r,c}
. For each nonzero, it determines the destination process and computes the corresponding local row and column indices in the destination process (lines 14–15 of Algorithm
2
).
Finally, using the previously computed indices and offsets, the thread stores the data tuples into the appropriate locations in the
SendBuffer
array (line 17 of Algorithm
2
).
Although this approach involves accessing the data twice, it eliminates the need for thread synchronization, enabling strong thread-level scalability within each process, as demonstrated in the results section.

4.4.
HipPerm Complexity Analysis

We analyze HipPerm’s complexity separately for
Identify
,
Exchange
, and
Build
stages. For simplicity, we assume a square input matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and permutation vectors of length
n
.
For communication complexity, we use the
\alpha-\beta
model
(
Thakur et al. 2005
)
,
where
\alpha
is the latency constant corresponding to the fixed cost of communicating a message regardless of its size, and
\beta
is the inverse bandwidth corresponding to the cost of transmitting one word of data. Consequently, communicating a message of
m
words takes
\alpha+\beta m
time.

Identify:

The
Identify
step performs two collective communications on the permutation vectors.
Swapping indices and values of vectors requires an
Alltoall
involving all
p
processes, each holding
n/p
vector elements.
Considering the pairwise-exchange algorithm for
Alltoall
, the communication complexity is

\mathcal{O}(\alpha(p-1)+\beta\frac{n}{p})
.
The next communication uses an
Allgather
operation among
\sqrt{p}
processes to gather necessary indices of the inverted permutation vectors.
Considering the Ring algorithm for
Allgather
, the communication complexity is

\mathcal{O}(\alpha(\sqrt{p}-1)+\beta\frac{n}{\sqrt{p}})
.

Let
\textit{nnz}(\mathbf{A}_{i,j})
denote the number of nonzeros in process
P_{i,j}
.
Since Algorithm
2
accesses local nonzeros twice in a load-balanced manner across
nthreads
threads, the per-process complexity is
\mathcal{O}(\textit{nnz}(\mathbf{A}_{i,j})/\texttt{nthreads})
.
If the matrix is evenly distributed, the overall cost is
\mathcal{O}(\textit{nnz}/(p*\texttt{nthreads}))
.
However, if nonzeros are unevenly distributed, the cost is dominated by the process with the largest submatrix.

Exchange:

The exchange step performs an
Alltoallv
, where each process sends
3\textit{nnz}(\mathbf{A}_{i,j})
data.
Assuming a balanced matrix and pairwise-exchange
Alltoall
, each process sends and receives
3\textit{nnz}/p
data.
The communication complexity is
\mathcal{O}(\alpha(p-1)+\beta\textit{nnz}/p)
.
It is difficult to derive the communication complexity for imbalanced input and output matrix distributions.
However, as shown in our experiments, such imbalances typically lead to higher communication costs.

Build:

We assume that each process receives approximately
\textit{nnz}/p
nonzeros.
First, we sort the received data with a time complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\frac{\textit{nnz}}{p*\texttt{nthreads}}\log\frac{\textit{nnz}}{p*\texttt{nthreads}})
.
After sorting, a multiway merge of the sorted partitions is performed. We use a hash-based multiway merge that takes
\mathcal{O}(\frac{\textit{nnz}}{p*\texttt{nthreads}})
.

4.5.
The HipExtract Algorithm

As defined in Section
2.2
, a submatrix
\mathbf{B}
is extracted from a larger matrix
\mathbf{A}
as
\mathbf{B}[i,j]=\mathbf{A}[\mathbf{pvec}[i],\mathbf{qvec}[j]]
, where
\mathbf{pvec}
and
\mathbf{qvec}
specify the rows and columns extracted from
\mathbf{A}
.
In a distributed setting, the input matrix
\mathbf{A}
, the index vectors
\mathbf{pvec}
and
\mathbf{qvec}
, and the output matrix
\mathbf{B}
are all distributed across processes.
Similar to HipPerm, our distributed algorithm HipExtract follows the three steps of the Identify–Exchange–Build approach: (i) each process identifies the relevant local elements using
\mathbf{pvec}
and
\mathbf{qvec}
, (ii) exchanges the selected elements with other processes, and (iii) builds its local portion of
\mathbf{B}
.
The key difference from HipPerm lies in the Identify step: HipExtract extracts only the subset of nonzeros specified by
\mathbf{pvec}
and
\mathbf{qvec}
before determining their new distributed locations. As a result, extracting a small submatrix with HipExtract is less expensive than performing a full permutation with HipPerm.

\mathbf{A}[\mathbf{pvec}[i],\mathbf{qvec}[j]]

\mathbf{pvec}

\mathbf{qvec}

\mathbf{pvec}
and
\mathbf{qvec}

\mathbf{SpPvec}

\mathbf{SpQvec}

4.6.
HipExtract Complexity Analysis

We analyze the complexity of HipExtract for each stage of the algorithm separately. For ease of computation, we assume both input and output matrices to be square, such as
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
.
Consequently,
|\mathbf{pvec}|=|\mathbf{qvec}|=m
.

Identify:

Similar to HipPerm, HipExtract performs an
Alltoall
to exchange indices and values in
\mathbf{SpPvec}
and
\mathbf{SpQvec}
, producing
\mathbf{SpPvec\_inv}
and
\mathbf{SpQvec\_inv}

Using the pairwise-exchange algorithm, this incurs a communication cost of
\mathcal{O}(\alpha(p-1)+\beta m/p)
. An
Allgather

\mathbf{SpPvec\_loc}
and
\mathbf{SpQvec\_loc}
across
\sqrt{p}

\mathcal{O}(\alpha(p-1)+\beta\frac{m}{\sqrt{p}})
.

To extract elements in a local submatrix, each process executes Algorithm
5
.
For simplicity, we assume that
\mathbf{A}
is distributed evenly among processes such that each local submatrix has
\frac{nnz(\mathbf{A})}{p}
nonzero elements.
The maximum number of nonzero columns on each local submatrix is
\frac{n}{\sqrt{p}}
and each column has
\frac{nnz(\mathbf{A})*\sqrt{p}}{n}
nonzero elements on average.

Suppose we are extracting random row and column indices, the length of the extracting vectors on each process is
|\mathbf{SpPvec\_loc}|=|\mathbf{SpQvec\_loc}|=\frac{m}{\sqrt{p}}
.
Based on Algorithm
5

\mathbf{SpQvec\_loc}

\frac{m}{\sqrt{p}}

As indices in
\mathbf{SpQvec\_loc}
are equally dived among

\mathcal{O}(\frac{m}{\texttt{nthreads}*\sqrt{p}}*\frac{m}{\sqrt{p}}*\log\frac{nnz*\sqrt{p}}{n})
.

Exchange:

In step 2 of HipExtract, where an
Alltoall
communication is performed, the communication cost has complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\alpha(p-1)+\beta\frac{nnz(\mathbf{B})}{p})
.

Build:

Finally, in

\frac{nnz(\mathbf{B})}{p}

Identical to the HipPerm complexity analysis in

4.7.
The HipAssign Algorithm

As explained in Section
2.3
, assigning
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m^{\prime}\times n^{\prime}}
to
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
means replacing the entries of
\mathbf{A}
at rows
\mathbf{pvec}
and columns
\mathbf{qvec}
with the corresponding elements of
\mathbf{B}

\mathbf{A}[\mathbf{pvec}[i],\mathbf{qvec}[j]]=\mathbf{B}[i,j]
.
Mathematically, an assignment can be viewed as adding two matrices.
However,
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
cannot be added directly due to their different sizes.
We address this by permuting
\mathbf{B}
with
\mathbf{pvec}
and
\mathbf{qvec}

\mathbf{A}

This produces

\mathbf{B}^{\prime}[\mathbf{pvec}[i],\mathbf{qvec}[j]]=\mathbf{B}[i,j]

\mathbf{A}

\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}^{\prime}

Similar to HipPerm and HipExtract, the distributed assignment algorithm, HipAssign, follows the three steps of the Identify–Exchange–Build approach to compute

HipAssign then performs one additional step to add two matrices.

HipAssign Complexity Analysis

To analyze the complexity of HipAssign, we consider each step of the algorithm separately.
To simplify the calculation, we assume both input matrices to be square, such as
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
where
m<n
and lengths of the vectors are
|\mathbf{pvec}|=|\mathbf{qvec}|=m
.

Identify:

In this step, two
Allgather
communication is applied to gather subvectors, each among
\sqrt{p}
processes. Considering the Ring algorithm for
Allgather
, the communication complexity is
\mathcal{O}(\alpha(\sqrt{p}-1)+\beta\frac{m}{\sqrt{p}})
.
In the next substep, the computation on each local submatrix follows Algorithm
2

4.4

\mathcal{O}(\alpha(p-1)+\beta\frac{nnz(\mathbf{B})}{p})

5.
Results

5.1.
Experimental Setup

Experiment Objectives

We designed experiments to evaluate the performance of our algorithms in terms of runtime speedup, scalability, and overall efficiency.
We aim to cover a range of matrix computation applications, each characterized by distinct input/output sparsity patterns.
For matrix permutation, we consider both random permutation and matrix reordering, demonstrating the robustness of our approach while highlighting that performance is sensitive to the specific application and underlying sparsity structure.
In submatrix extraction, we demonstrate the algorithm’s applicability through applications involving both random submatrix selection and specific cluster extraction.
Furthermore, we integrate permutation and assignment operations to demonstrate their application in streaming batch updates for distributed graphs.

We demonstrate the scalability of our distributed algorithms at both the process and thread levels.
For large-scale matrices, we show scalability up to 4096 MPI processes (512 nodes; 65,536 cores).
Furthermore, we benchmark our methods against CombBLAS, a leading 2D distributed memory sparse library; PETSc, representing the 1D distributed memory approach; and GraphBLAS, a shared-memory baseline known for its superior single-node performance.

Implementation Details

Our algorithms are implemented in C/C++ and compiled using gcc 11.2.0.
We employed Cray’s MPI and OpenMP for hybrid parallelization. We used data structures from the CombBLAS library
(
Azad et al. 2021
)
that distribute vectors and matrices on a 2D square process grid.

Datasets

The datasets used in our experiments are listed in Table
2
.
We selected them to cover a diverse range of real-world and synthetic matrices and graphs, varying in size, structure, and sparsity.
For instance, our selection includes road networks with low average degrees, as well as social and protein networks with high average degrees.
We also used rectangular matrices and matrices arising from optimization problems.
These matrices cover diverse sparsity patterns, ranging from diagonal or column-heavy nonzero patterns to more evenly distributed or entirely random graphs.
Additionally, we include extremely large matrices, such as
Hyperlink
and
Metaclust50
, to assess our approach at a large scale.

5.2.
Experiments with Matrix Permutations

We evaluate matrix permutations in two scenarios: (a) random permutation and (b) matrix reordering.
In the first scenario, we randomly permute a distributed matrix to ensure that nonzero entries are uniformly distributed across processes. This permutation helps balance the load in subsequent graph and sparse matrix algorithms.
The goal of random permutation is to destroy any structure present in the matrix. It is common in most distributed-memory libraries, including CombBLAS and PETSc.

In the second scenario, we apply a given permutation vector to reorder the matrix, concentrating nonzero entries near the diagonal. This approach reduces matrix bandwidth in iterative solvers or ensures a zero-free diagonal during the pivoting step of direct solvers. Various algorithms can be used to obtain such orderings, including Reverse Cuthill-McKee (RCM)
(
Cuthill and McKee 1969
;
Azad et al. 2017
)
, minimum-degree ordering
(
George and Liu 1989
;
Karypis and Kumar 1998
)
, and bipartite matching
(
Duff and Koster 2001
;
Azad et al. 2020
)
.
In this paper, we used the RCM ordering to permute matrices for concentrating nonzero entries near the diagonal.

5.3.
Performance of HipPerm relative to CombBLAS and PETSc

Table
3
shows the runtime of HipPerm, CombBLAS, PETSc on 64 processes of the Big Red 200 supercomputer.
We also present the load imbalance before and after the permutations. In this context, load imbalance is measured as the ratio of the maximum non-zero entries (nnz) in a process to the average nnz per process. For example, a random matrix, GAP_urand, has a load imbalance of 1 before permutation, where an imbalance factor of 1 indicates that all processes have an equal number of nonzeros in their local submatrix. In contrast, scale-free social networks like soc-LiveJournal1 exhibit a load imbalance of 15.5 before permutation.
Applying a random permutation to a matrix typically reduces the load imbalance factor, bringing it closer to 1. On the other hand, RCM reordering may increase the load imbalance factor as it moves nonzeros close to the diagonal.

Random permutation

Table
3
shows that HipPerm runs faster than CombBLAS and PETSc when we perform random permutations.
We observe an average speedup of 55 (stdev = 108) over CombBLAS and 5.54 (stdev = 5.16) over PETSc permutation.
The lower performance of random permutations in CombBLAS stems from its implementation of symmetric permutations as two SpGEMM operations,
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}^{\mbox{\scriptsize\sf T}}
. Its distributed SpGEMM relies on sparse SUMMA
(
Buluç and Gilbert 2012
)
, which keeps the output stationary while moving both input matrices.
In particular, sparse SUMMA broadcasts submatrices of
\mathbf{A}
to remote processes that may not need them, substantially increasing communication overhead.
For several datasets, we observed that CombBLAS’s communication time alone exceeded the total runtime of HipPerm. This result reinforces our argument that a general-purpose SpGEMM is too costly for distributed matrix permutation.

The last two rows in Table
3
show a large-scale permutation experiment using 128 nodes (16,384 cores) on the Perlmutter supercomputer. At this extreme scale, HipPerm is more than
50\times
faster than CombBLAS for Metaclust50.
The random permutation on Hyperlink could not be completed with CombBLAS due to an internal error.
PETSc also fails on these matrices, reporting a file I/O error.

Table
3
shows that matrices with higher load imbalance factors take longer to permute.
For example, GAP-urand (imbalance 1.0) runs faster than com-Friendster (imbalance 9.0) despite the former having more nonzeros.
This indicates that the load imbalance increases both the computation and communication overhead of matrix permutation.

RCM reordering

Table
3
also reports the performance of HipPerm and CombBLAS with RCM orderings.
As before, HipPerm is an order of magnitude faster than CombBLAS across all matrices. However, RCM reordering takes longer than random permutations for both libraries because it clusters nonzeros near the diagonal, increasing the imbalance factor after reordering.
Consequently, diagonal processes receive more data and perform more work, raising both computation and communication costs and thus the overall runtime.

5.8.
Performance Evaluation of Submatrix Assign

We evaluate the performance of HipAssign by updating a graph with subgraphs.
To capture a streaming graph application, we partition an input graph into five parts: one containing 80% of the original edges and four smaller subgraphs with 5% of the edges each.
The assign operation is then performed in four steps, where the adjacency matrices of the smaller subgraphs are incrementally merged into the larger graph.
The final result of these four assignments is the original input graph with its full set of edges. The runtimes of these four assign operations, measured for both our algorithm and CombBLAS, are reported in Figure
14
.

The speedup of our assign algorithm to CombBLAS’s assign is 182.4 on average, with stdev=29.2, but it highly depends on the structure of the graph and the assignment indexing vectors.
In particular, CombBLAS failed to complete the assignment for larger graphs such as
Twitter7
and
com-Friendster
on fewer nodes due to out-of-memory errors.
We observe that HipAssign stops scaling at 64 processes for smaller graphs and at 256 processes for larger graphs.
This behavior arises because HipAssign’s runtime is proportional to the size of the subgraph being assigned.
As a result, scalability stalls when a small subgraph is distributed across many processes.

Batch updates of graphs in real applications generally result in load imbalance, which can affect the performance of subsequent distributed analysis algorithms.
Permuting graphs during the update process can be a solution to this issue.
Using our algorithm, batch update and permutation can be fused to reduce the overhead of performing a separate permutation step
(
Hassani et al. 2024
)
.
Figure
14
illustrates the performance and scalability of the fused update with the permute algorithm, which is scaling across different datasets.
In this experiment, we update each graph using a randomly generated Erdős-Rényi graph in a single step.
Runtimes in some cases were omitted due to memory limitations.
Permuting the matrix during the update preserves the load balance between processes without incurring additional computational cost.
Compared to performing assign and permute separately using CombBLAS, our method is at least 25
\times
faster while preserving the load balance.

5.9.
Fusing Operations in an Incremental Graph Application

A key benefit of the IEB approach is its ability to fuse multiple operations with little overhead. To illustrate this benefit, we consider incremental graph updates where new subgraphs are added to an existing graph.
An example arises in protein sequence databases such as the IMG/M database
(
Chen et al. 2019
)
.
In some applications, such databases are modeled as a graph where vertices denote proteins, and edges connect proteins with similarity above a threshold. As new sequences arrive, they form subgraphs that are integrated into the existing graph.

Incremental graph updates correspond to repeated assignments of submatrices.
In a distributed-memory setting, this may lead to load imbalance, requiring periodic matrix permutations.
While assignment and permutation can be invoked sequentially (e.g., HipAssign followed by random HipPerm), doing so adds runtime overhead.
By modifying the Identify step, we developed a fused HipAssign+HipPerm operation that performs both tasks simultaneously. Its performance is analyzed in this section.

6.
Conclusions

Matrix permutation, extraction, and assignment are fundamental operations in sparse matrix algorithms and libraries.
Their performance is especially critical in distributed-memory settings, where inefficient design can lead to significant communication overheads.
Despite their ubiquity, the literature on parallel algorithms for these operations remains limited.

We showed that direct implementations of permutation, extraction, and assignment using the identify–exchange–build (IEB) approach outperform SpGEMM-based methods in distributed memory.
For example, our experiments demonstrated that HipPerm and the permutation routine in PETSc achieve better performance than the SpGEMM-based implementation in CombBLAS.
The performance gap arises because CombBLAS’s SpGEMM treats both operands symmetrically, whereas our algorithms communicate only the required data, leading to significant reductions in both communication and overall runtime.

Efficient and scalable implementations also require highly optimized multithreaded local kernels. For fine-grained matrix access and updates, minimizing thread synchronization is crucial to achieving high performance.
This paper presented shared-memory parallel algorithms that eliminate synchronization, enabling linear thread scalability within a process.

In many applications, these operations must be combined.
The IEB-style framework naturally supports such fusion, enabling multiple operations to be executed together with reduced communication.
Although we illustrated this in the context of a streaming graph application, the approach also generalizes to other scenarios.

In summary, this work provides scalable parallel algorithms and software for matrix permutation, extraction, and assignment. These implementations offer practical building blocks that can be integrated into existing sparse matrix libraries to enhance performance across a wide range of applications.
