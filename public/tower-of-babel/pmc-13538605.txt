Diabetic plaque calcification: mechanisms, imaging phenotypes, and clinical implications
Authors: Mengmeng Zhu, Wenting Wang, Shihan Xu, Yiwen Li, Yanfei Liu, Yue Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetes is associated with a higher burden and heterogeneous phenotypes of plaque calcification, which may influence plaque stability and cardiovascular risk. This review examines diabetic plaque calcification as a dynamic process driven by hyperglycemia and other metabolic disturbances through downstream mechanisms including inflammation, apoptosis, matrix vesicles, and osteogenic transdifferentiation. It highlights the importance of multidimensional imaging assessment beyond calcification burden and discusses current therapeutic limitations and future research directions.

Introduction

The prevalence of diabetes is increasing worldwide, and the number of affected individuals is projected to reach 853 million by 2050, making diabetes a major global public health problem1. Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) is the leading cause of death in patients with diabetes, accounting for two-thirds of all-cause mortality, with approximately 15% of deaths directly attributed to major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), including myocardial infarction2. Plaque rupture, plaque erosion, and high-risk calcification patterns are key pathological substrates for MACE. Diabetes-specific pathological conditions, including glucose and lipid metabolic dysregulation, can accelerate plaque injury, alter plaque composition, and reduce plaque stability3. Among these, plaque calcification is an important pathological and imaging feature of atherosclerotic plaques and is more common and complex in patients with diabetes.

Traditionally, plaque calcification has been regarded as a marker of chronic atherosclerotic progression or plaque healing, and extensive calcification may contribute to plaque stabilization4. However, pathological and imaging studies over the past decade have shown that the impact of calcification on plaque stability depends not only on the extent of calcification, but also on its density, location, morphology, and biological activity5. Large confluent calcifications, such as sheet calcification, may increase load-bearing capacity and distribute stress across the fibrous cap, whereas microcalcification and spotty calcification may create local mechanical mismatch and stress concentration, thereby increasing the risk of plaque rupture or erosion6. Clinical studies have shown that patients with diabetes often exhibit a distinct calcified plaque phenotype, characterized by the coexistence of high calcification burden and high-risk calcification morphology, which may contribute to their increased risk of cardiovascular events7–10. Therefore, cardiovascular risk assessment in patients with diabetes should not be limited to calcification burden alone but should also include calcification morphology and other imaging features.

Diabetic plaque calcification is an active, cell-mediated biological process regulated by multiple mechanisms. Metabolic factors characteristic of the diabetic milieu, such as hyperglycemia and advanced glycation end products (AGEs), may promote plaque calcification progression and influence calcification phenotypes by modulating inflammatory responses, cell apoptosis, matrix vesicles (MVs) release and fusion, and osteogenic differentiation11–13. However, how these factors interact and jointly shape the unique calcification patterns in diabetes, ultimately influencing clinical outcomes, remains insufficiently defined. Therefore, this review summarizes current evidence on mechanisms, imaging phenotypes, clinical evidence, and potential therapeutic strategies of diabetic plaque calcification, thereby providing new perspectives for future cardiovascular risk stratification and treatment in patients with diabetes.

Anatomical location, morphological evolution, and evaluation dimensions of plaque calcification

Anatomical location of plaque calcification: intima-derived calcification

Vascular calcification can be classified according to its predominant anatomical location as intimal plaque calcification or medial arterial calcification. Plaque calcification occurs within atherosclerotic plaques, most commonly in large arteries such as the coronary and carotid arteries. It coexists with lipid cores, necrotic debris, macrophages, and other plaque components, and may directly influence plaque progression and the risk of cardiovascular events6,14–16. In contrast, medial calcification primarily involves the elastic lamellae and vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) of the medial layer, particularly in peripheral arteries. Its clinical consequences are more commonly reflected by increased arterial stiffness, elevated pulse pressure, and impaired peripheral perfusion17,18. In patients with diabetes, plaque calcification and medial calcification may coexist; however, they differ in pathological mechanisms, imaging manifestations, and prognostic implications (Table 1). Morphological evolution of plaque calcification: from microcalcification to nodular calcification

The morphology of plaque calcification is generally described along a continuum during plaque progression15,19. The earliest form is histological microcalcification, typically measuring approximately 0.5 to 15 μm in diameter. Because this is below the spatial resolution of conventional clinical imaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT) and intravascular ultrasound (IVUS), microcalcification usually cannot be directly detected by routine imaging. Over time, microcalcifications may gradually aggregate into larger punctate calcifications, approximately 15 μm to 1 mm, and fragmented calcifications, approximately 1 to 3 mm. These deposits may further coalesce into larger sheet-like calcifications, typically defined as ≥3 mm. When sheet-like calcification fractures, protrudes into the lumen, or extends toward the medial layer, calcified nodules may form. It should be noted that imaging-defined spotty calcification, usually 1 to 3 mm, represents a small focal calcification phenotype that becomes visible on clinical imaging after further aggregation of microcalcifications. Therefore, this review distinguishes between histological microcalcification and imaging-defined spotty calcification.

Evaluation dimensions of plaque calcification: burden, density, morphology, and biological activity

Given the high heterogeneity of plaque calcification, current studies generally evaluate its clinical significance across four domains: burden, density, morphology, and biological activity6,20–22. Calcification burden refers to the total amount or volume of calcification and is commonly quantified using the coronary artery calcium score (CACS) or calcification volume, reflecting the cumulative burden of atherosclerotic disease. Calcification density reflects the CT attenuation intensity or average density of calcified regions, usually expressed as Hounsfield units (HU) or density scores, and may indicate the compactness or maturation of calcification. Calcification morphology refers to the size and shape of calcified deposits. Microcalcification and spotty calcification are more commonly associated with unstable plaque lesions, whereas dense sheet-like calcification is more frequently observed in fibrocalcific plaques and healed plaque ruptures and is generally considered closer to a stable plaque phenotype. Calcification biological activity refers to dynamic processes such as ongoing microcalcification formation or calcification progression within plaques, which can be indirectly assessed using molecular imaging modalities such as 18F-NaF PET/CT. The subsequent discussion in this review is organized around these dimensions.

Mechanisms of diabetic plaque calcification

Diabetic plaque calcification represents a pathological process in which the biological mechanisms underlying plaque calcification are modified and amplified by the diabetic metabolic milieu. Current evidence indicates that upstream metabolic disturbances, including hyperglycemia, AGEs/receptor for AGEs (RAGE) signaling, and dyslipidemia, can individually or collectively modulate inflammatory responses, apoptosis and defective efferocytosis, matrix vesicle release and fusion, and osteogenic transdifferentiation of vascular wall cells. In this context, inflammation primarily promotes a pro-calcific microenvironment through alterations in cellular interactions and extracellular matrix remodeling; apoptotic remnants and MVs provide structural substrates for mineral nucleation; and osteogenic transdifferentiation facilitates calcium phosphate deposition within plaques. Inflammatory response

Atherosclerotic plaques are lipid-driven chronic inflammatory lesions23. Inflammation contributes not only to plaque initiation and progression but also to plaque calcification. Human longitudinal FDG-PET/CT studies have shown that local inflammatory signals in the arterial wall temporally precede the development of calcification at the same site, suggesting that inflammation may represent a precursor event in plaque calcification24. However, inflammation does not directly provide the nucleation sites required for calcium salt deposition; rather, its pro-calcific effects are mediated mainly through alterations in cellular interactions and the extracellular matrix environment within plaques. Animal studies have shown that, during plaque progression in ApoE−/− mice, increased macrophage infiltration is accompanied by increased expression of pro-calcific molecules25. Mechanistically, infiltrating monocytes/macrophages promote the transition of VSMCs from a contractile phenotype toward an osteogenic phenotype through direct cellular interactions and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IL-1β, with increased expression of calcification-associated molecules such as alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2)26–28.

In the diabetic setting, hyperglycemia, AGEs, and lipid abnormalities further exacerbate plaque inflammation and amplify inflammation-mediated pro-calcific effects. Human pathological studies have shown that, compared with individuals without diabetes, plaques from patients with diabetes exhibit greater macrophage and T-lymphocyte infiltration, larger necrotic cores, and more extensive calcification distribution, suggesting that enhanced inflammatory activity frequently coexists with increased calcification burden in diabetic plaques29. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that hyperglycemia induces S100A9 secretion and RAGE expression in macrophages. Through activation of the S100A9/RAGE axis, hyperglycemia enhances the release of pro-inflammatory mediators, including IL-1β, TNF-α, and MCP-1, accompanied by activation of pro-calcific programs characterized by increased expression of osteogenic markers, including ALP, RUNX2, and BMP2/4, as well as increased release of calcification-prone vesicles11. In addition, hyperglycemia promotes pro-inflammatory M1 macrophage polarization through the poly ADP-ribose polymerase 1 (PARP-1)-signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) signaling pathway, and conditioned media from these macrophages induce osteogenic differentiation of VSMCs, thereby promoting plaque calcification30. Collectively, these findings suggest that diabetes-associated inflammation contributes to plaque calcification through enhanced VSMC osteogenic transition and increased production of calcification-promoting vesicles.

Importantly, inflammatory status is also associated with calcification burden and plaque morphologic features. Clinical studies in patients with coronary artery disease (CAD) have shown that greater plaque calcification burden is associated with lower perivascular inflammation and fewer vulnerable plaque features detected by optical coherence tomography (OCT), suggesting that advanced dense calcification may represent a stabilized or healing stage following inflammatory resolution31. However, whether similar relationships between inflammatory activity, calcification burden, and calcification morphology exist in diabetes, and how the diabetic metabolic environment modifies inflammatory dynamics to influence different calcification phenotypes, remain to be determined.

Cell apoptosis

Apoptosis is a common form of programmed cell death within atherosclerotic plaques, whereas efferocytosis refers to the recognition and clearance of apoptotic cells and their cellular debris by phagocytic cells, particularly macrophages. An imbalance between increased apoptotic burden and impaired efferocytic clearance in atherosclerotic plaques promotes the persistent accumulation of apoptotic cells and apoptotic bodies, thereby contributing to necrotic core expansion and plaque instability32,33. Apoptotic bodies, which are phospholipid-rich membrane structures, provide important nucleation sites for calcium phosphate crystal deposition and participate in the initiation of plaque calcification14,34. Pathological studies of human plaques have shown that early calcification is frequently observed in proximity to apoptotic VSMCs or macrophages35. In vitro studies have further shown that VSMC apoptosis precedes the formation of calcific nodules, whereas inhibition of apoptosis attenuates the extent of calcification34.

In the diabetic environment, this apoptosis-associated calcification process may be further amplified by metabolic abnormalities, including the accumulation of AGEs and Nε-carboxymethyl-lysine (CML). Clinical studies have shown increased coronary calcium deposition in patients with diabetes, accompanied by reduced expression of the AGE clearance receptor AGER1 and increased expression of the RNA-binding protein NF90/110. Further studies in diabetic mouse models and human aortic vascular smooth muscle cells (HAVSMCs) demonstrated that chronic hyperglycemia activates NF90 in VSMCs, promotes AGER1 ubiquitination and degradation, and subsequently induces local accumulation of AGEs within plaques. Excessive AGE accumulation activates RAGE signaling, induces VSMC apoptosis, and promotes osteogenic differentiation, thereby accelerating vascular calcification12. Another study in lower extremity arteries from patients with diabetes showed that enhanced CML-RAGE signaling was associated with increased arterial wall calcium deposition, with further activation of this signaling axis during calcification progression36. In diabetic mouse models, activation of the CML-RAGE axis promoted macrophage apoptosis. Furthermore, in vitro experiments demonstrated that the ability of CML-RAGE signaling to induce osteogenic transdifferentiation of VSMCs depended on a microenvironment containing oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL) and macrophage-derived apoptotic bodies37. These findings suggest that, in diabetes, the CML-RAGE axis may promote macrophage apoptosis and, in the presence of apoptotic bodies and oxLDL, enhance VSMC osteogenic transformation, thereby contributing to plaque calcification.

In addition, hyperglycemia may exacerbate macrophage foam cell formation and impair efferocytic clearance, further promoting the accumulation of apoptotic cellular debris and providing a persistent substrate for calcification38,39. Together, these processes may contribute to the progression of diabetes-associated plaque calcification. Increased glycemic variability has also been suggested to promote VSMC apoptosis and calcification through endoplasmic reticulum stress–related pathways, indicating that glucose fluctuations may represent an additional contributor beyond chronic hyperglycemic exposure40. However, current evidence is primarily derived from small clinical studies and in vitro VSMC models, and the underlying mechanisms and clinical implications require further investigation.

Matrix vesicle release and fusion

Similar to apoptotic bodies, MVs are membrane-bound extracellular vesicle-like structures that participate in calcification nucleation41. However, unlike apoptotic bodies, MVs are primarily generated through regulated cellular release under stress conditions rather than as passive remnants of dying cells. Within atherosclerotic plaques, VSMCs and macrophages represent major sources of calcification-prone MVs. These vesicles contain calcium-binding proteins, including annexins, tissue-nonspecific alkaline phosphatase, and calcium–phosphate components involved in mineral nucleation, thereby providing initial platforms for hydroxyapatite crystal formation42–44. Subsequently, calcification-prone vesicles accumulate, aggregate, and undergo progressive mineralization within the plaque extracellular matrix, contributing to the initiation and expansion of MV-derived microcalcifications.

In the diabetic environment, hyperglycemia and AGE/CML-related signaling may increase the pro-calcific properties of extracellular vesicles derived from different cell types. Studies of carotid atherosclerotic plaques from patients with diabetes have shown increased S100A9 expression in these lesions, which is associated with RAGE expression, ALP activity, and calcification-associated vesicles. Further experiments using hyperglycemia-stimulated human macrophages and diabetic mouse models demonstrated that activation of the S100A9-RAGE axis promotes the release of calcification-prone MVs and enhances plaque microcalcification11. These findings suggest that the diabetic microenvironment contributes to microcalcification formation by altering MV cargo composition and functional properties.

Beyond regulating vesicle release, the diabetic microenvironment may also influence interactions between MVs and the extracellular matrix, thereby affecting vesicle retention, anchoring, and subsequent expansion within plaques. In anterior tibial artery plaques from patients undergoing amputation for diabetic foot complications, DJ-1 expression was inversely associated with the number of microcalcification nodules. Further studies in diabetic mice and VSMCs demonstrated that hyperglycemia upregulates N-acetyltransferase 10 (NAT10), promotes acetylation-dependent ubiquitination and degradation of DJ-1 in VSMCs, thereby activating the STAT1-RAGE signaling axis. This pathway alters the surface molecular profile of VSMC-derived MVs, resulting in enrichment of milk fat globule-EGF factor 8 protein (MFGE8) on the vesicle surface, enhanced MV–collagen interactions, and ultimately increased diabetic plaque microcalcification13.

In diabetes, accumulation of AGE/CML may further influence MV aggregation and fusion after vesicle anchoring, thereby affecting calcification morphology. Clinical studies have shown that serum CML levels are associated with CD9 and sortilin levels in circulating MVs among patients with diabetes, suggesting that AGE/CML burden may regulate vesicle calcification potential through alterations in vesicle cargo composition. Further studies in diabetic mouse models and VSMCs demonstrated that CML not only promotes the release of VSMC-derived MVs with enhanced calcification potential but also facilitates sortilin recruitment into MVs45. Another diabetes-related study showed that RAGE and galectin-3 expression patterns in anterior tibial artery plaques from patients undergoing amputation for diabetic foot complications were associated with microcalcification and macrocalcification, respectively. Subsequent studies in diabetic mice and VSMCs suggested that these molecules regulate MV aggregation and calcification nodule size through opposing effects on sortilin expression46. Therefore, sortilin may represent a potential molecular link between diabetic metabolic abnormalities, MV aggregation, and calcification pattern transition. However, its association with local MV accumulation, calcification morphology, and plaque stability needs further validation in human diabetic coronary plaques.

Cellular osteogenic transdifferentiation

Osteogenic transdifferentiation refers to the process by which VSMCs lose their contractile properties under pathological stimuli and acquire osteogenic or chondrogenic features, thereby actively contributing to mineral deposition. This process is considered an important mechanism of vascular calcification47,48. Within the atherosclerotic plaque microenvironment, local stimuli, including oxLDL, pro-inflammatory cytokines, and disturbances in calcium–phosphate metabolism, induce osteogenic transformation of VSMCs and other vascular wall cells, thereby promoting plaque calcification and progression.

Diabetes-associated metabolic abnormalities may further promote VSMC osteogenic transformation. As described above, inflammatory mediators, apoptotic products, and other diabetes-related alterations in the plaque microenvironment promote VSMC osteogenic differentiation, while osteogenic VSMCs release MVs with increased calcification potential, further accelerating mineral deposition. Recent studies have begun to define the molecular mechanisms by which metabolic abnormalities regulate VSMC osteogenic transformation. Analysis of anterior tibial artery specimens from patients undergoing amputation for diabetic foot complications demonstrated enhanced branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) catabolism in calcified regions, accompanied by increased expression of branched-chain amino acid transaminase 2 (BCAT2) in calcified VSMCs. Further mechanistic studies demonstrated that, under diabetic conditions, BCAT2-mediated BCAA catabolism increases propionyl-CoA availability, enhances H3K23 propionylation at the RUNX2 promoter, and activates RUNX2-dependent osteogenic differentiation of VSMCs, thereby contributing to diabetic plaque calcification49. These findings suggest that diabetes-associated metabolic abnormalities may alter the pro-calcific transcriptional program of VSMCs through metabolite-mediated epigenetic regulation.

In addition, diabetes may weaken endogenous protective mechanisms that normally restrain abnormal osteogenic transformation in the vascular wall. Studies in diabetic mouse models and VSMCs have shown that CML downregulates USP10 expression in calcified vessels and VSMCs, attenuates the USP10–AMPK anti-calcification pathway, and promotes VSMC osteogenic transformation50. In vitro VSMC studies have shown that ghrelin, a gastric peptide hormone, attenuates osteogenic transformation by increasing osteoprotegerin (OPG) expression and suppressing receptor activator of nuclear factor κB ligand (RANKL) signaling51. Clinical studies have shown that reduced circulating and arterial wall levels of ghrelin are associated with increased arterial calcification and stenosis severity in patients undergoing amputation for diabetic foot complications, suggesting a potential role for ghrelin in diabetic vascular calcification. However, how hyperglycemia modulates ghrelin-mediated regulation of VSMC osteogenic differentiation remains unclear.

Nevertheless, osteogenic transdifferentiation alone does not fully account for the biological complexity of plaque calcification. Evidence from human studies indicates that early plaque microcalcification is frequently associated with cell death, MV release, and an imbalance in endogenous calcification inhibitors, whereas typical osteoid or cartilage-like structures are uncommon in human calcified plaques. These findings suggest that human plaque calcification may not depend exclusively on classical osteogenic differentiation pathways15. Currently, mechanistic evidence regarding osteogenic transdifferentiation in diabetic plaque calcification is derived predominantly from mouse models and in vitro cellular studies. The contribution of this process to human diabetic plaque calcification requires cautious interpretation and further validation.

In summary, diabetic plaque calcification should not be considered a pathological process independent of conventional plaque calcification biology. Rather, it represents a process in which established calcification mechanisms are modified and amplified by the diabetic metabolic milieu. However, current evidence regarding diabetic plaque calcification is derived mainly from animal models, cellular experiments, and studies of peripheral arterial tissues or circulating biomarkers. Whether these mechanisms adequately explain the distinct stages and morphological patterns of calcification in human diabetic coronary plaques requires further investigation using direct human tissue analyzes and longitudinal imaging studies.

Imaging evaluation of calcified plaques in diabetes

For assessment of overall calcified plaque burden, the CACS, commonly calculated using the Agatston method on CT, remains one of the most accessible and widely used clinical tools. Data from the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis showed that CACS improved long-term risk stratification for coronary heart disease and ASCVD in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) or metabolic syndrome52. The National Lipid Association scientific statement on the use of CACS to guide primary prevention of ASCVD further states that, among patients aged 40 to 75 years with T2DM for whom statin therapy has already been selected, a CACS > 100 may reasonably support the use of high-intensity statin therapy, corresponding to a class IIa recommendation with level C-LD evidence53. However, high-quality prospective evidence supporting risk reclassification by CACS in patients with diabetes remains limited. The 2023 ESC Guidelines for the management of cardiovascular disease in patients with diabetes state that current evidence is insufficient to support routine use of CAC for cardiovascular risk reclassification in this population54. Thus, the value of CACS as an independent risk-stratification tool in patients with diabetes requires further prospective validation. CT also has important limitations, particularly its inability to reliably characterize high-risk calcification morphologies. In this context, coronary CT angiography (CCTA), with intravenous contrast, can visualize plaque composition, distribution, and calcification size, thereby facilitating the identification of high-risk plaque features in patients with diabetes. However, whether calcification morphology on CCTA can directly guide treatment decisions and improve clinical outcomes in patients with diabetes remains insufficiently established.

For patients with established CAD, particularly those scheduled for percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or those with complex lesion morphology, intravascular imaging enables real-time and detailed lesion assessment55. Specifically, IVUS can evaluate the distribution, length, and arc of calcification, which is important for determining whether severe calcified lesions require rotational atherectomy, cutting balloons, or other lesion preparation strategies. The IVUS-ACS randomized trial showed that, compared with angiography-guided PCI, IVUS-guided PCI significantly improved 1-year clinical outcomes in patients with diabetes and acute coronary syndrome56. Nevertheless, acoustic shadowing caused by calcification on IVUS may limit the assessment of structures behind the calcified region. OCT provides higher resolution and can assess calcification area and volume, as well as distinguish superficial from deep calcification. Studies of OCT-guided PCI have shown that the OCT-derived calcification volume index and lesion length independently predict mortality risk in patients with T2DM57. However, both IVUS and OCT are invasive techniques and are not suitable as screening tools for the general diabetic population. Instead, they are mainly used for lesion characterization and procedural optimization during coronary intervention58. Moreover, evidence supporting diabetes-specific calcification imaging features to guide PCI strategies remains limited, and clear imaging-based decision standards have not yet been established.

The emergence of molecular imaging has extended calcified plaque assessment from morphological characterization to the detection of active mineralization. Positron emission tomography (PET) combined with CT imaging (PET/CT) using 18F-sodium fluoride (18F-NaF) can detect active mineralization signals in regions where calcification is not yet visible on CT, thereby enabling sensitive identification of early microcalcification activity22,59. However, because of high cost, limited tracer availability, and the lack of standardized image acquisition protocols, 18F-NaF PET/CT remains mainly a research tool for scientific research and has not become a routine risk assessment tool for calcified plaques in patients with diabetes. Artificial intelligence (AI)-based analysis and multimodal imaging may improve calcified plaque phenotyping, but their clinical utility remains to be validated60–62.

The burden, heterogeneity, and clinical risk assessment of calcified plaques in diabetes

A substantial body of clinical research evidence indicates that patients with diabetes have a greater burden of calcified plaque in multiple vascular beds, including the coronary arteries, carotid arteries, and vertebrobasilar arteries8–10. Moreover, as the blood glucose levels and age of diabetic patients increase, the trend of visible calcification in coronary plaques becomes more pronounced. This underscores the significance of plaque calcification in diabetic patients, which cannot be overlooked63,64. This increase in macroscopic calcified plaque burden is closely associated with adverse prognosis in diabetes. Studies have shown that diabetes is an independent predictor of coronary plaque burden, particularly calcified plaques, and significantly increases the risk of myocardial infarction8. In imaging assessments, the coronary plaque burden scores, particularly the segment involvement score (SIS), and calcified plaques are strong predictors for predicting MACE in diabetic patients, with prognostic value that may exceed that of traditional assessments based on vascular stenosis and ischemia65. Furthermore, in diabetic patients undergoing PCI, the calcified plaque volume index measured by OCT independently predicts mortality risk57. Similar conclusions have been drawn in studies of carotid artery plaque calcification, which has been associated with future cardiovascular events and mortality in patients with diabetes. As the degree of plaque calcification increases, the risk of MACE also increases66.

Traditional views suggest that macroscopic calcification, as indicated by a high CACS, is typically associated with increased plaque stability and reduced risk of rupture67. However, although patients with diabetes generally have greater calcified plaque burden and higher CACS7,8, with nearly half of diabetic patients demonstrating severe calcification (CACS > 400)68, their plaque stability does not correspondingly improve. In contrast, they may exhibit more rapid progression of vulnerable plaques69,70. Specifically, in diabetic patients with low-to-intermediate CACS, mild calcification is actually associated with a higher risk of vulnerable plaques, indicating that a low or intermediate CACS does not exclude high-risk plaque features71,72. This suggests that relying solely on CACS may significantly underestimate the risk of vulnerable plaques in diabetic patients, especially in those with mid-to-low CACS. Pathological and imaging studies have also shown that diabetes-related plaques exhibit more frequent spotty calcification or microcalcification features across multiple vascular beds, including the coronary, carotid, vertebrobasilar, and anterior tibial arteries10,29,36,73. Moreover, 18F-NaF PET imaging has shown that early and active coronary plaque microcalcification independently predicts coronary calcification progression in patients with diabetes74. Therefore, calcification assessment in patients with diabetes should integrate information on calcification burden, morphology, spatial distribution, and activity to better identify high-risk plaque phenotypes and improve cardiovascular risk stratification.

Treatment advances in diabetic plaque calcification

At present, evidence for direct pharmacological intervention in diabetic plaque calcification remains limited (Table 3). Existing observational studies have shown that metformin use is associated with lower CACS, a lower frequency of spotty calcification and cholesterol crystals, and a more stable plaque microstructure in patients with T2DM and CAD75. In addition to metformin, sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors have also been reported to be associated with lower CACS in patients with T2DM, although this evidence is likewise derived from retrospective studies76. Therefore, the effects of metformin and SGLT2 inhibitors on diabetic plaque calcification still require confirmation by higher-quality clinical evidence, such as randomized controlled trials. Beyond glucose-lowering agents, most existing pharmacological studies on plaque calcification have been conducted in populations not specifically defined by diabetes, with statins receiving the most attention. The prospective multinational observational PARADIGM study found that statins were associated with progression of coronary calcified plaque while slowing progression of noncalcified plaque77. Another study showed that high-intensity rosuvastatin reduced plaque microcalcification activity in patients at high cardiovascular risk but without previous cardiovascular events78. The effects of proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitors, another class of lipid-lowering agents, on plaque calcification also remain controversial. One study showed that evolocumab did not significantly alter plaque composition, including calcification79, whereas another study reported that evolocumab reduced non-calcified plaque volume, increased calcified plaque volume, and decreased plaque microcalcification activity80. However, these studies were not specifically conducted in diabetic populations. Emerging therapeutic targets may also suggest possible approaches for the treatment of diabetic plaque calcification. For example, the BCAT inhibitor BAY-069, the AMPK inhibitor compound C, and L-thyroxine have shown anti-calcific potential in preclinical studies of diabetic plaque calcification49,50,81. However, their safety and efficacy in real-world clinical settings are key issues that must be addressed for future clinical translation. In addition, the effect of tirzepatide, a dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP)/glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist, on the progression of coronary calcified plaques in patients with T2DM is still under evaluation. Future findings may provide new evidence for the broader vascular benefits of glucose-lowering therapies82.

Conclusion

Plaque calcification in diabetes represents a multidimensional phenotype characterized by calcification burden, morphology, and biological activity. Diabetes-related metabolic disturbances, such as hyperglycemia, AGEs, and dyslipidemia, may drive the development and progression of plaque calcification through mechanisms including inflammatory responses, cell apoptosis, and MV-mediated processes. However, current mechanistic evidence remains largely preclinical, and the mechanisms by which diabetic metabolic abnormalities affect human plaque calcification require further clarification through pathological and molecular imaging studies. Advances in imaging technologies, including CCTA, IVUS, OCT, and 18F-NaF PET/CT, have shifted plaque calcification assessment from measurement of calcification burden alone to evaluation of calcification density, morphology, distribution, and activity. Studies using these modalities have shown that patients with diabetes not only have a higher calcification burden, but also often exhibit more complex calcification morphology and greater plaque risk heterogeneity. However, the standardization of different imaging-defined calcification phenotypes and their incremental value for clinical decision-making remain incompletely established. In terms of treatment, direct pharmacological evidence targeting diabetic plaque calcification is still lacking, and clinical management continues to rely mainly on conventional interventions for metabolic risk factors, including glucose lowering and lipid lowering.

In summary, plaque calcification in diabetes exhibits multidimensional characteristics and marked risk heterogeneity. Future studies should integrate multimodal imaging, multi-omics, and AI-based approaches to improve individualized risk assessment and evaluate targeted intervention strategies, with the aim of improving cardiovascular outcomes in patients with diabetes.
