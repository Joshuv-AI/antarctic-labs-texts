Depressive and Anxiety Symptoms in Women with Threatened Abortion: A Case-Control Study
Authors: Seyyedeh Mahboobeh Mirtabar, Shahnaz Barat, Farzan Kheirkhah, Amrolah Mostafazadeh, Hoda Shirafkan, Zeynab Pahlavan, Mahbobeh Faramarzi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Threatened abortion (TA) is associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes. Despite the attention paid to the adverse outcomes of obstetrics, only a few studies have been conducted on the psychological status of women with threatened abortion. This study aimed to compare the symptoms of depression and anxiety in women with TA and those without threatened abortion.Materials and Methods:In a case-control study, 136 women with TA (the case group) and 136 women without TA (the control group), were matched with the case group in terms of gestational age, education level, age, and number of pregnancies, were examined from Obstetric clinics of Babol University of Medical Sciences. Demographic information and pregnancy history were obtained from all participants. Additionally, the women filled out the BSI-18 questionnaire. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 22 software.Results:The results showed that in women with threatened abortion, the likelihood of experiencing depression symptoms was 1.9 times higher [odds ratio (OR)=1.91, 95% confidence interval (CI)=1.13-3.23, P=0.015], anxiety symptoms were 1.8 times higher (OR=1.83, 95% CI=1.08-3.10, P=0.024), and somatization was 2.6 times higher (OR=2.65, 95% CI=1.61-4.37, P<0.001) compared to pregnant women without threatened abortion. Additionally, in women with threatened abortion, the risk of psychological distress was 3.3 times higher (OR=3.30, 95% CI=1.96- 5.56, P<0.001) than in women without threatened abortion.Conclusion:This study suggests that gynecologists, midwives, and healthcare providers, in addition to providing medical care for women threatened with abortion, should identify women at risk of experiencing symptoms of depression and anxiety. Providing appropriate psychological support should be prioritized in the management of women with threatened abortion.

Introduction

Threatened abortion (TA) is spontaneous abortion characterized by spotting or vaginal bleeding with a closed cervix before 20 weeks of pregnancy (1). Approximately 25% of pregnant women experience some degree of vaginal bleeding in the first trimester of pregnancy, and half may lose their fetuses (2). In an Iranian study, 83% of women with TA lost the fetus (3). However, another study reported that 53.84% of pregnancies continued to 24 weeks if the fetal heart was detected by ultrasound (4). The incidence of TA is increasing every year. This issue involves changing lifestyles and eating habits, increasing severe environmental pollution, increasing job and work stress among women, pregnancy at an older age, and decreasing egg quality (5).

TA may have adverse consequences for the pregnant woman. In the past, much attention has been given to the pregnancy consequences of threatened abortion. Many studies have identified obstetric complications of threatened abortion. Evidence supports that the rate of antepartum bleeding, premature membrane rupture, preterm labor, and intrauterine growth restriction was higher in women at risk of abortion than those with an uncomplicated pregnancy (6-8). Despite much attention on adverse pregnancy outcomes, few studies have addressed the psychological consequences of TA. Evidence shows that the continuation of unpredictable pregnancy in women with TA may increase the psychological burden on these women (9), and these consequences may be long-term (10). Psychological consequences include an increased risk of anxiety, depression, posttraumatic stress disorder, and suicide (11). The results of Zhu et al.’s (12) study showed that in women with TA compared to stable pregnancies, symptoms of major depression and anxiety were significantly higher. There is also evidence that worrying about losing a fetus may be associated with sadness, distress, guilt, isolation, stress, and anger (13-15).

Considering the high prevalence of TA in women, the few international studies on the psychological aspects of threatened abortion, and the lack of information on the psychological problems of Iranian women with threatened abortion, it is necessary to describe the psychological state of women with TA. To our knowledge, this is the first case-control study that compares four psychological variables, including depression, anxiety, somatization, and psychological distress, in women with and without TA. The results of this study can answer an important question for gynecologists, midwives, and healthcare providers: Are women with TA more likely to have psychological risks such as adverse pregnancy outcomes than those with normal pregnancies?

Materials and Methods

This case-control study was conducted from April 2023 to Decmber 2023 in the pregnancy clinics of Babol University of Medical Sciences (Rohani, Yahyanejad, Marzikola). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Babol University of Medical Sciences (IR.MUBABOL. REC.1401.158), and informed consent was obtained from all participants before entering the study.

Inclusion criteria for TA group (case) included the diagnosis of intrauterine pregnancy (based on ultrasound), gestational age of 5 to 12 weeks, bleeding and spotting, and singleton pregnancy. Women with vaginal bleeding for reasons other than threatened abortion, missed abortion, or inevitable abortion, and patients diagnosed with ectopic pregnancy, history of mental illness and drug use were excluded from the study. The control group included pregnant women with a gestational age of 5 to 12 weeks without signs of threatened abortion, who were the same as the case group (based on the method of frequency matching) regarding age, education level, and the number of pregnancies.

Convenience sampling was used. The sample size, according to the information from a previous similar article (16), where the mean depression in the case group was equal to 0.56, and the standard deviation was 0.75. The mean value of depression in the control group was equal to 0.23 with a standard deviation of 0.75, assuming a power of 95% at the 0.05 significance level; the number of samples was estimated at 272 (136 in the case group and 136 in the control group).

In the obstetric clinic of each hospital, a trained midwife who was not aware of the research project evaluated the inclusion/exclusion criteria of the pregnant woman by conducting an interview. If the pregnant woman was eligible to enter the study, the research plan was explained to her. If she agreed to participate, she completed the demographic questionnaire that included personal information and obstetric history. Then, she was given the self-reported research questionnaire to answer. During the research, to select the case group, 310 pregnant women were evaluated, of which 38 women did not meet the conditions for entering the study (twin pregnancy, unavoidable abortion, ectopic pregnancy, and unwillingness to participate). Also, in the sample selection for the control group, according to the frequency matching conditions, about 356 pregnant women were evaluated until 136 people were matched with the case group.

All participants completed the 18-item Short Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI-18). According to the study of Hamidia et al. (16), BSI-18 has the best sensitivity and specificity for diagnosing any psychiatric disorder in pregnant women with high-risk pregnancies. Demographic information for both groups was collected, including age, education level, gestational age, the number of births, and the history of pregnancies. The BSI-18 assesses the three factors of depression, anxiety, and somatization along with the Global Severity Index (GSI) symptom severity index. The scoring of questions is based on a five-point Likert scale (0=not at all, 1=slightly, 2=moderately, 3=slightly, 4=severely). A GSI score greater than 0.5 is diagnosed as mental distress. In this research, the valid Persian version of BSI-18 was used. The reliability and retest coefficients were 0.90 and 0.81, respectively (17).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was done using SPSS software version 22 (version 17.0, IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics are presented as mean ± standard deviation and percentage. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. The comparison of frequency of depression and anxiety symptoms in women with TA and without TA was determined using the chisquare test. Also, t tests were used to comparison of means of depression and anxiety symptoms in two groups. Logistic regression was used to calculate the chance of each variable. In order to evaluate the impact of confounding factors, independent variables were entered into a logistic regression model, and then we compared adjusted and unadjusted odds ratios. A P<0.05 was considered the cutoff value for statistical significance.

Results

The demographic characteristics of the participants in the two study groups (with and without threatened abortion) are presented in Table 1. The mean age of all participants was 31.5 ± 6.58 years old. Approximately 41.9% of the women had higher education at the university level. About 31.3% of the women were experiencing their first pregnancy, while 30.9% had their second pregnancy. There was no significant difference between the case and control groups regarding age (P=0.299), education (P=0.607), gravidity (P=0.080), and gestational age (P=0.113). The mean gestational age did not significantly differ between the two groups (P=0.113).

Furthermore, the frequency of total BSI-18 scores exceeding the cut-off≥ 13 was 42.6% in the case group and 23.5% in the control group (P=0.001). Additionally, the frequency of psychological distress, indicated by GSI >0.5, was significantly higher in women with TA than those without TA (75 vs. 47%, P<0.001).

Table 4 displays the results of multiple logistic regressions. The findings of the logistic regression analyses indicated that the likelihood of experiencing depressive symptoms in the TA group is 1.97 times higher in the nonadjusted state [odds ratio (OR)=1.97, 95% confidence interval (CI)=1.17-3.31, P=0.014] and 1.91 times higher in the adjusted state compared to women without TA (OR= 1.919, 95% CI=1.13-3.23, P=0.015). Similarly, the likelihood of encountering anxiety symptoms in women with TA is higher than in women without TA in both the nonadjusted model (OR=1.857, 95% CI=1.1-3.12, P=0.026) and the adjusted model (OR=1.833, 95% CI=1.08-3.10, P=0.024).

Furthermore, the results indicate that the likelihood of experiencing somatization is higher in the TA group compared to the group without TA, both in the non-adjusted model (OR=2.784, 95% CI=1.7- 4.55, P<0.001) and the adjusted model (OR=2.652, 95% CI=1.61-4.37, P<0.001). Finally, the likelihood of psychological distress (GSI) among women with TA is 3.37 times higher in the non-adjusted state (OR=3.375, 95% CI=2.01-5.64, P<0.001) and 3.30 times higher in the adjusted state (OR=3.304, 95% CI=1.96-5.56, P<0.001).

Discussion

The present study aimed to compare the symptoms of depression and anxiety in pregnant women with and without threatened of abortion. The results indicated that the mean scores of depression, somatization, depression/ anxiety, and psychological distress were higher in women with TA compared to the group without TA. This finding is consistent with the results of Adib Rad et al. (18). That study concluded women who experience recurrent abortion exhibit higher levels of psychological distress than those with a normal pregnancy. Additionally, our results align with the findings of Kolte et al. (19), who reported that both psychological stress and major depression are significantly more prevalent among women with recurrent miscarriages than those with a normal pregnancy. Similar results have been reported in populations from Kazakhstan (20) and China (21). However, comparing the findings of our study with previous research is challenging due to the focus of many earlier studies on various types of abortions, including spontaneous abortion, induced abortion, and recurrent abortion. These findings highlight the potential significant stress on women experiencing miscarriage symptoms, which might not resolve independently and necessitate appropriate diagnostic services, care, and psychological interventions.

Our findings indicated that women with TA experienced 1.9 times higher odds of experiencing depression symptoms, 1.8 times higher odds of anxiety symptoms, and 2.3 times higher odds of somatization compared to those without TA. A study by Zhu et al. (12) obtained similar results. They reported significantly higher symptoms of major depression and anxiety among women with TA compared to those with a stable pregnancy.

In Australia, Chojenta et al. (22) conducted a prospective cohort study on women's health, examining the wellbeing of over 40,000 Australian women. They reported a higher frequency of sad mood and excessive anxiety in women who have experienced abortion, and this experience persists into subsequent pregnancies. However, another study indicated that although depression is common during pregnancy, its prevalence does not significantly differ in pregnant women without obstetric complications compared to those with TA. While this result may imply that TA does not directly cause anxiety, it might contribute to heightened anxiety levels during pregnancy. Another study examining depression and anxiety during pregnancy in women with TA found that women without obstetric complications (bleeding and spotting) exhibited the same level of depression as women with threatened abortion. However, anxiety levels were higher among the latter group (9).

Our study demonstrated that women with TA are 3.3 times more likely to experience psychological distress than those without TA. Although no similar study regarding this specific finding was found, a meta-analysis supported the notion that psychological stress before and during pregnancy is associated with abortion (23). Another study investigating the relationship between mental disorders and abortion reported higher miscarriage risks in women diagnosed with psychiatric disorders. The study revealed an increased risk of miscarriage among women with anxiety disorders (1.25 times higher), depressive disorders (1.25 times higher), and somatic disorders (1.18 times higher). Additionally, the study reported heightened miscarriage risks among women with multiple psychiatric diagnoses (24). A 2017 study in Greece focused on women hospitalized in intensive care units due to the risk of premature birth. The study found that about a quarter of the women exhibited depression based on the Edinburgh Depression Questionnaire (EPDS), and the risk of depression was associated with sixfold increased odds of considering abortion (25). Also, the evidence is growing that maternal psychological well-being should be screened promptly to implement prevention programs and early interventions, thereby reducing the risk of negative physical and mental health outcomes for both the mother and the baby (26). Thus, examining maternal psychological distress during pregnancy is not merely a research topic but a clinical concern for public health and healthcare.

This study has several clinical implications. The results inform us mental health of women with TA, a specific group of pregnant women that have received less attention in prior research. Also, and the findings highlight the importance of structured mental health screening in early pregnancy. Women with TA, apart from the additional obstetric risks, encounters faced typical anxiety and psychological distress during pregnancy. Our study recommended that obstetricians, gynecologists, and healthcare providers should recognize that screening and identifying women at risk and sometimes referring them to specialized counseling centers can alleviate the extent and severity of psychological complications among women with TA.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, our results relied on patients' self-report measures, and we did not employ structured clinical interviews. Incorporating clinical interviews in future research could better ascertain the prevalence of mental disorders in pregnant women with threatened abortion. Secondly, this case-control study was conducted within a single university medical center and city. More extensive studies encompassing multiple centers and universities, especially prospective cohort studies, are recommended for further exploration.

Conclusion

Women with TA have approximately twice the odds of experiencing depression symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and somatization compared to normal pregnant women. Additionally, these women face a 3.3-fold increased risk of psychological distress. This study recommends that gynecologists, obstetricians, and healthcare providers prioritize the timely identification and treatment of anxiety and depressive symptoms alongside medical management for women with threatened abortion.
