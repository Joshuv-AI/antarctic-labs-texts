Impact of aetiology on short- and long-term outcomes in open descending and thoracoabdominal aneurysm repair
Authors: Joshua R Chen, Christopher Pritting, Vishal N Shah, Colin King, Jacqueline McGee, Konstadinos A Plestis
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOBJECTIVESWe analysed the impact of chronic type B aortic dissection (cTBAD) and degenerative aneurysm (DA) on the short- and long-term outcomes of patients undergoing open descending thoracic aortic aneurysm and thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm repair.METHODSWe performed a retrospective analysis of a prospectively maintained aortic database. From 1999 to 2023, 245 patients underwent open descending thoracic aortic aneurysm or thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm repair. We compared preoperative comorbidities, postoperative complications and operative and long-term mortality for patients with cTBAD (120 patients, 49%) and DA (125 patients, 51%).RESULTSPatients with cTBAD were significantly younger, more likely to be male and less likely to have comorbidities including diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and coronary artery disease. There were no statistically significant differences in the incidence of operative mortality between cTBAD (7.5%) and DA patients (11.2%) (P = 0.44). Survival at 1, 5, 10 and 15 years for cTBAD versus DA patients was 90% versus 76%, 79% versus 59%, 59% versus 42% and 50% versus 29%, respectively (P < 0.01); however, this lost statistical significance in the Cox regression (P = 0.86). Following multivariable analysis, older age, female gender, coronary artery disease, urgent or emergent procedure and descending thoracic aortic aneurysm were independent predictors of long-term outcomes.CONCLUSIONSThere were no statistically significant differences in the incidence of postoperative complications, operative deaths and long-term survival between cTBAD and DA patients.IRB number(#20D.802), Obtained: 1/31/23.Keywords: Atherosclerotic aneurysm, Thoracoabdominal, Chronic type B aortic dissection

INTRODUCTION

The most common aetiologies of descending thoracic aortic aneurysms (DTAs) and thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysms (TAAs) are chronic type B aortic dissections (cTBADs) and degenerative aneurysms (DAs) [1, 2]. The latter can further be categorized into those with medial degeneration (MD) and atherosclerotic aneurysms (AAs) [3, 4].

Although DTA and TAA open surgical repair (OSR) is the gold standard, pulmonary complications and paraplegia can occur particularly in elderly or higher risk patients with mortality rates as high as 27% [5–12]. While some studies report increased operative mortality after OSR for cTBAD, a recent study comparing chronic dissection and non-dissection aneurysms in 3470 open TAA repairs showed lower operative mortality in dissection patients (P = 0.03) and no difference in long-term mortality (P = 0.1) [13, 14]. The aim of this study is to compare postoperative and survival follow-up outcomes and identify predictors of mortality in DTA and TAA patients undergoing OSR by a single surgeon for cTBADs and DAs.

METHODS

Patient selection

Two hundred and eighty-one consecutive patients undergoing DTA or TAA from July 1999 to September 2023 by a single surgeon (K.A.P.) were identified from a prospectively maintained Aortic Wellness Database containing demographic, clinical, operative and follow-up data. After excluding patients undergoing OSR for other aetiologies (i.e. acute dissection, infection, pseudoaneurysm and trauma), 245 patients were included in the analysis and were stratified by cTBAD (120 patients, 49%) and DA (125 patients, 51%). The DA cohort was divided into AA (60 patients, 48%) and MD (65 patients, 52%) groups. Mortality was assessed using an internal repository, direct provider and patient contact, queries to the Social Security Death Index and screening of web-based obituaries. Follow-up ended on 31 October 2023. Institutional Review Board approval for use of the de-identified database for analysis and the study (No. 20D.802) was obtained with a waiver of informed consent owing to the study’s retrospective nature.

Surgical technique

Our surgical technique has been previously described [15]. For DTAs, a left posterolateral thoracotomy was performed at the fifth or sixth intercostal space and extended to a standard S-shaped thoraco-abdominal incision in extent I, II or III TAAs. A thoraco-abdominal incision is made at the seventh intercostal space in extent IV TAAs. Distal aortic perfusion was established through partial left heart bypass using atrial-femoral cannulation or partial cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) using femoral-femoral cannulation. A clamp-and-sew technique was rarely used in patients with isolated DTAs or extent IV aneurysms. In cases where proximal aortic cross-clamping was not feasible, full CPB with deep hypothermic circulatory arrest (DHCA) was initiated at 20–22°C. A cerebrospinal fluid drain was routinely placed on the day of surgery in extent I, II and III repairs and selectively in extent IV repairs. Starting in 2005 and 2010, respectively, somatosensory-evoked potentials and motor-evoked potentials were routinely employed. Upper intercostal arteries down to T6 were routinely ligated. Intercostal arteries were reimplanted from T7 to L1 using the island technique if vessel diameter was large and backbleeding was poor. Renovisceral vessels were selectively perfused at 250–300 cc/min with cold blood at 4°C. The renovisceral vessels were subsequently reattached using the island technique or interposition grafts. The island technique was avoided in connective tissue disorders.

Statistical analysis

After descriptive analysis of baseline patient variables, comparative analysis was performed between the two groups (cTBAD and DA) followed by a survival analysis. Categorical data were presented as proportions and compared using the chi-squared test. Continuous data were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and compared with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or Kruskal–Wallis test. To identify predictors of short- and long-term mortality, a univariate analysis was performed on all demographic variables using a Cox proportional hazards regression to evaluate the association between various clinical factors and short- or long-term survival. Variables with statistically significant effects on long-term survival were then run through a multivariate analysis. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated and confirmed using Schoenfeld residuals, indicating that the assumption held for all covariates except renal insufficiency and rupture. This resulted in 10 covariates being included in the model which, given a total of 138 deaths, met the commonly held rule of having 10–15 events per covariate.

After adjusting for the effects of each of the covariates, a hazard ratio was calculated for each covariate addressing its associated risk on long-term survival. A value of P ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. R software, version 4.3.3, was used for data analysis and visualization (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

The cTBAD and AA patients were statistically significantly different in the following baseline characteristics. The cTBAD patients were younger [median 57 years (IQR, 50–64 years) vs 69 years (IQR, 63–75 years); P < 0.01] and more frequently male (70% vs 55.2%; P = 0.02). The cTBAD patients were more likely to have previous aortic surgery (49.2% vs 20%; P < 0.01), Marfan syndrome (9.2% vs 2.4%; P = 0.04), DTAs (42% vs 18.5%; P < 0.01), preoperative dialysis (8.8% vs 1.7%; P = 0.03) and extent I TAAs (32.8% vs 16.9%; P < 0.01) than DA patients, respectively. Furthermore, DHCA was utilized more often in cTBAD patients (50% vs 16%; P < 0.01). The DA patients had a greater prevalence of diabetes (26.4% vs 10.8%; P < 0.01), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (44.8% vs 21.7%; P < 0.01), coronary artery disease (CAD) (55.2% vs 33%; P < 0.01), procedures without distal aortic perfusion (32% vs 2.5%; P < 0.01), extent II TAAs (14.5% vs 7.6%; P = 0.01) and extent V TAAs (1.6% vs 0.8%; P < 0.01). The MD patients were more likely to have DTAs (26.6 vs 10%; P = 0.03) and extent I TAAs (21.9% vs 11.7%; P = 0.03) than AA patients. Operative outcomes

The cTBAD patients had statistically significantly longer median CPB times [138 min (IQR, 44–205 min) vs 58.5 min (IQR, 41–143 min); P = 0.02] than DA patients. There were no statistically significant differences in median aortic cross-clamp [53.5 min (IQR, 40–65 min) vs 48 min (IQR, 37–61 min); P = 0.18] and DHCA [30 min (IQR, 26–35 min) vs 28 min (IQR, 24–35 min); P = 0.28] times between cTBAD versus AA groups. Selective intercostal artery reimplantation was performed more frequently in cTBAD patients (34.2% vs 22.4%; P < 0.01). Other operative results are shown in Table 2.

Postoperative outcomes

Postoperative results are shown in Table 3. There was no statistically significant difference in operative mortality between cTBAD (7.5%) versus DA (11.2%) (P = 0.44). Causes of operative mortality are demonstrated in Supplementary Material, Table S7. Median intensive care unit lengths of stay were [6 days (IQR, 4–12 days) for cTBAD vs 5 days (IQR, 3–12) for DA patients; P = 0.05]. Furthermore, there was no difference in occurrence of combined neurological dysfunction (i.e. transient neurological dysfunction, transient ischaemic attack and stroke) between cTBAD (6.4%) and DA (8.7%) patients (P = 0.68). Other postoperative outcomes also demonstrated no differences between groups. The AA patients were more likely to have tracheostomies (23.3% vs 4.6%; P = 0.01), transient neurological dysfunction (9.4% vs 0%; P = 0.04), prolonged ventilatory support (51.7% vs 31.2%; P = 0.03) and longer time on the ventilator [48 h (IQR, 24–162.5) vs 40.5 h (IQR, 24–49)] than MD patients (P = 0.03).

Survival at 1, 5, 10, 15 and 20 years for cTBAD and DA patients was 90% and 76%, 79% and 59%, 59% and 42%, 50% and 29% and 45% and 21%, respectively (P < 0.01). There was no statistically significant difference in survival between AA and MD patients (P = 0.36) (Supplementary Material, Fig. S1).

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrated that although cTBAD patients had statistically significant greater number of prior aortic surgeries, longer CPB times and increased requirement for DHCA, they did not have a statistically significant difference (P = 0.44) in operative mortality (7.5%) when compared with DA patients (11.2%), who were older and sicker. In fact, the univariable analysis suggested that cTBAD patients had better long-term survival (P = 0.03), but lost significance after multivariable adjustment using Cox regression (P = 0.86). Furthermore, in the DA cohort, AA patients were more likely to have tracheostomies (P = 0.01), transient neurological dysfunction (P = 0.04) and prolonged ventilatory support (P = 0.03) with no statistically significant difference in operative mortality (16.7%) compared to MD patients (6.2%) (P = 0.11). Although there was no statistically significant difference in operative mortality possibly due to insufficient power, AA patients may have severe atherosclerotic disease in the thoraco-abdominal aorta that may contribute to nefarious postoperative sequelae leading to a higher likelihood of operative mortality.

Furthermore, this analysis reiterates the acceptable mortality risk for OSR of cTBADs and DAs at high volume centres [6, 16]. While perioperative mortality for open DTA and TAA repair has been reported to approximate or exceed 27%, recent national claims data report an in-hospital mortality of 12.1% at high volume centres, in line with the 9.4% operative mortality in our study [16, 17]. Our morbidity profile is also consistent with high volume centres [5–10].

Moreover, our outcomes are consistent with available literature reporting operative mortality rates ranging from 5.8–11% for cTBAD (ours 7.5%) and 5.5–20% for DA (ours 11.2%) [18–20]. Studies investigating the impact of aetiology on early and long-term outcomes have been discordant. Comparing chronic dissection and non-dissection aneurysms in 3470 open TAA repairs, Cook et al. [14] found aetiology was not associated with operative or long-term mortality in their multivariable analysis, which corroborates our findings. Cook et al. [14] found that emergency repair, chronic kidney disease, extent II repair, increasing age and longer aortic cross-clamp time were predictors of operative mortality or adverse events. Additionally, increasing age, hereditary thoracic aortic disease, CAD, chronic kidney disease and COPD were associated with worse long-term survival. In 453 patients, Latz et al. [13] published aetiology to be an independent predictor of perioperative mortality. In their study, the cTBAD patients despite being younger and having less comorbidities had higher mortality (12.2%) than DA patients (5.5%) [13]. Notwithstanding, aetiology was not a predictor of long-term mortality. Age, COPD, higher preoperative creatinine, extent II repair and female gender were associated with worse long-term survival [13]. We also found older age, female gender, CAD and extent to be independent predictors of worse long-term survival. These results may suggest that the higher preoperative risk profile for DA patients particularly older age and female gender rather than aetiology itself could be contributing to their diminished long-term survival. Females with TAAs present at an older age and more likely with AAs and have increased risk for postoperative respiratory and renal complications [21, 22]. Several studies document that female gender represents an independent predictor of decreased long-term survival after open TAA repair even when adjusting for age, aneurysm extent and other comorbidities [21, 22]. While we did not find Extent II specifically to be an independent predictor of worse long-term survival, likely due to the smaller sample size, we did find that DTA repair, the least extensive of the types included, were associated with better long-term survival. This is also corroborated by the findings of Tanaka et al. [7] that show that DTA repair was associated with fewer long-term complications among patients undergoing DTA and TAA repair.

With the rapid rise of endovascular procedures, it is important to compare their outcomes with OSR. Tong et al. [23] found that in 278 matched pairs of patients undergoing open and endovascular DTA and TAA repair, OSR resulted in better 10-year survival (52% vs 33%, P < 0.0001), and aortic reintervention was less frequent (4% vs 21%, P < 0.0001). The cTBAD patients undergoing endovascular repair have higher rupture rates and reinterventions for endoleak and dilation with no midterm survival benefit [24]. Patients with cTBAD may also have multiple fenestrations or re-entry tears, which can complicate endovascular repair [25]. Consequently, we favour OSR in cTBAD patients, especially younger ones without significant comorbidities.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. This was a retrospective study, which has inherent limitations including selection bias. All cases were performed by a single surgeon with expertise in thoracic aortic surgery, which limits generalizability of findings. Inevitable advances in surgical techniques and perioperative care over the long study period may have influenced the contemporary outcomes. Additionally, indications for OSR have changed during the study period with the introduction of endovascular therapy. That said, to further interrogate the impact of calendar time, we compared results in the subgroups of the former and latter cohort of patients in Supplementary Material, Table S6. Lastly, this study was likely underpowered to independently analyse outcomes between cTBAD, AA and MD. Thus, AA and MD were combined to form DA.

CONCLUSIONS

There were no statistically significant differences in operative mortality and long-term survival between cTBAD and DA patients. Older age, female gender, CAD and urgent or emergent procedure were associated with worse long-term survival. The cTBAD and DA patients had acceptable rates of morbidity and mortality after OSR.
