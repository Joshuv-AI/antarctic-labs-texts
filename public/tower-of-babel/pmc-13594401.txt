58 | Maternal Outcomes After Fetoscopic Open Neural Tube Defect Repair With Body Mass Index (BMI) >35

Brian A. Burnett; Christian M. Parobek; Rebecca M. Johnson; William E. Whitehead; Cara M. Buskmiller; Roopali V. Donepudi; Magdalena Sanz Cortes; Ahmed A. Nassr; Michael A. Belfort; Jessian L. Munoz

Baylor College of Medicine, Houston, TX

Objective: Although maternal obesity is a risk factor for neural tube defects, BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2 was an exclusion criterion in the Management of Myelomeningocele Study. Outcomes data on fetoscopic repair in patients with obesity remain limited. We evaluated whether preoperative BMI ≥ 35 is associated with gestational age at delivery and maternal outcomes following fetoscopic myelomeningocele (MMC) repair.

Study Design: We performed a retrospective cohort study of patients undergoing fetoscopic MMC repair from 2014 to 2026. Patients with preoperative BMI ≥ 35 were compared with BMI <35. The primary outcome was gestational age at delivery. Secondary outcomes assessed maternal complications and procedure‐related risks, including preterm prelabor rupture of membranes (PPROM), placental abruption and intraamniotic infection. Comparisons were performed using bivariate analyses and multivariable logistic regression.

Results: A total of 215 patients underwent fetoscopic MMC repair, 24 (11%) with BMI ≥ 35. Baseline characteristics were similar. Operative characteristics, including skin‐to‐skin operative time, CO2 insufflation time, and total fetoscopic time, were comparable. Gestational age at delivery was similar in patients with BMI ≥ 35 (35.9 weeks [IQR, 34.1–39.0]) and BMI <35 (37.0 weeks [IQR, 34.2–38.8]; p = 0.70). PPROM (36% vs 36%, p > 0.90), placental abruption (7.3% vs 0%, p = 0.40), and intraamniotic infection (3.2% vs 4.3%, p = 0.60) were similar. Rates of maternal perioperative complications, including pulmonary edema, blood transfusion, wound infection, and thromboembolism, did not differ. However, BMI ≥ 35 was associated with higher rates of cesarean delivery (81% vs 51%, p = 0.01) and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (13% vs 2.1%, p = 0.03).

Conclusion: In fetoscopic MMC repair, maternal BMI ≥ 35 was not associated with differences in gestational age at delivery, operative characteristics, or maternal perioperative morbidity. These findings did not identify significant differences in maternal safety and feasibility of fetoscopic repair in patients with obesity.

59 | Developing an Individualized Clinical Risk Prediction Tool for Fetal Cystic Hygroma

Michelle J. Wang; Tina Hsieh; Ai‐ris Y. Collier

Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, Boston, MA

Objective: Current counseling for first trimester cystic hygroma (CH) is based on data from the 2005 FASTER trial, prior to the availability of cell‐free DNA (cfDNA) aneuploidy screening. We sought to use machine learning models to integrate cfDNA results into a CH‐focused clinical risk calculator.

Study Design: A secondary analysis was performed on a cohort of 259 patients diagnosed with a first trimester CH at our institution. Outcomes examined included genetic diagnosis, structural anomalies, perinatal loss, livebirth, and a composite of all adverse outcomes. Predictor variables were nuchal translucency (NT), crown rump length (CRL), maternal age, cfDNA aneuploidy results, and second trimester hygroma resolution status. Nine machine learning classifiers were compared, SHAP analysis identified and ranked importance, and Bootstrapping (n = 1000) was utilized to construct 95% confidence intervals. The best‐performing model was used to develop clinical risk calculators using the Streamlit framework.

Results: Two clinical risk calculators were developed (Figure 1). The first trimester calculator was developed using the best performing LASSO logistic regression model trained on 259 fetuses with CH and allows users to input patient‐specific values (maternal age, NT, CRL, and cfDNA results) to obtain individualized risk estimates for any genetic diagnosis, individual aneuploidies, structural anomalies, perinatal loss, livebirth and the adverse composite. The second trimester calculator, which included only 66 non‐aneuploidy cases with known resolution status, allows users to input NT size and resolution status to obtain risk estimates for non‐aneuploidy diagnoses, structural anomalies, and livebirth. An example case is illustrated in Figure 2.

Conclusion: This is a promising tool for individualized risk assessment for CH outcomes that incorporates cfDNA screening results. Given the small cohort, results should be interpreted cautiously. External validation in larger populations is needed to confirm generalizability and improve accuracy.

60 | Pregnancy Outcomes Following Detection of Early First Trimester Embryonic Edema

Roberto X. Calix1; Shannon McCloskey2; Lisa Bernhard3; James D. Toppin1; Talia Suner4; Joseph R. Biggio, Jr.5; Frank B. Williams5

1Ochsner Health, Ochsner Health/New Orleans, LA; 2Ochsner Health, New Orleans, LA; 3University of Texas at Austin, Dell Medical School, Austin, TX; 4Ochsner Clinic Foundation, New Orleans, LA; 5Ochsner Health, Ochsner Health, LA

Objective: Fetal edema on first trimester ultrasound (1TUS) is associated with congenital anomalies and pregnancy loss. The implications of embryonic edema, identified with crown rump length (CRL) <44 mm (where standards for nuchal translucency are not established), are less well described. We hypothesize that embryonic edema is associated with adverse perinatal outcomes.

Study Design: This was a retrospective cohort study of pregnancies receiving care at a large regional health system from 2018–2024. Pregnancies with normal fetal cardiac activity, CRL 16–44 mm, and embryonic edema characterized on early ultrasound report were matched to normal controls. Images were assessed by two investigators blinded to original reports and outcomes to validate and classify edema by degree of subcutaneous hypoechogenicity & compartments affected. Severe edema was defined as hydrops fetalis or cystic hygroma, while subcutaneous or isolated nuchal edema was classified as non‐severe. Primary outcome was a composite of major fetal anomaly (cytogenetically confirmed aneuploidy or major structural anomaly) & pregnancy loss (miscarriage or stillbirth, excluding therapeutic abortion). A subgroup analysis by severity of edema was conducted. Chi square compared outcomes; confounding was addressed through matching for CRL and maternal age.

Results: We matched 170 pregnancies with edema reported at CRL <44 mm 1:1 to controls with normal 1TUS. Characteristics were similar between those with and without edema (Table 1). Most embryonic edema was non‐severe (65.1%). Composite outcome (anomaly or pregnancy loss) was more common in the embryonic edema group compared to controls (32.2% vs 2.9%, OR 16.8, 95% CI 6.4, 43.6, Table 2). Structural/chromosomal anomalies and pregnancy loss were each more common in the edema group, as was therapeutic abortion & preterm delivery. Subgroup analyses demonstrated consistent findings, with an apparent dose‐dependent relationship between degree of edema and adverse perinatal outcomes.

Conclusion: Embryonic edema identified on early 1TUS is associated with fetal anomalies & pregnancy loss.

61 | Defining Fetal Growth Restriction in Zambia: Comparison of Ultrasound‐Based Definitions for Identifying Small‐for‐Gestational‐Age Infants

Austin Oberlin1; Madison Calvert2; Imanga Ikabongo3; Katelyn J. Rittenhouse4; M. Bridget Spelke5; Margaret P. Kasaro6; Bellington Vwalika7; Yuri Sebastião8; Jeffrey Stringer8; Elizabeth M. Stringer5

1Yale University, New Haven, CT; 2University of North Carolina School of Medicine, Chapel Hill, NC; 3University of Zambia, Lusaka, Zambia; 4University of North Carolina, Durham, NC; 5Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, University of North Carolina School of Medicine, Chapel Hill, NC; 6UNC Global Projects‐Zambia, LLC, Lusaka, Zambia; 7Department of Obstetrics and Gynaecology, University of Zambia School of Medicine, Lusaka, Zambia; 8Division of Global Women's Health, Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, University of North Carolina School of Medicine, Chapel Hill, NC

Objective: Multiple fetal growth curves and definitions of fetal growth restriction (FGR) exist, with no international standard. We compared commonly used fetal weight formulas and FGR definitions for identifying small‐for‐gestational age (SGA) infants in a Zambian cohort, to inform development of national ultrasound guidelines.

Study Design: We combined participants from one observational cohort and two randomized trials conducted in Lusaka, Zambia between 2015–2022. Singleton pregnancies without fetal anomalies were included. All had at least one growth US after 20 weeks, most frequently between 32–34 weeks. Our primary outcome was SGA, by WHO criteria. We compared several FGR definitions across biometric formulas (Hadlock, Intergrowth, and Shepard) and growth standards (EFW < 10th percentile, EFW or abdominal circumference [AC]< 10th percentile, and Delphi). For each model, we estimated area under the ROC curve, sensitivity, specificity, PPV and NPV. Separate logistic regression models for the odds of perinatal mortality were fit using each definition and maternal age, BMI, hypertensive disorders, diabetes, and maternal HIV as covariates.

Results: Among 3239 pregnancies included, 657 (20.3%) were born SGA (Table 1). Among the models evaluated, Hadlock and Shepard with FGR <10th percentile demonstrated the best discrimination for identifying SGA (AUC 0.64 and 0.65 respectively; Table 2). Adding AC < 10th percentile marginally improved sensitivity with no improvement in overall performance (AUC 0.64). The adjusted model for SGA was concordant with these findings. Doppler studies were available in a subset of 611 pregnancies. A modified Delphi definition that incorporated umbilical artery Doppler demonstrated higher specificity but lower sensitivity for SGA (Specificity 94.8%, Sensitivity 30.1%, AUC 0.62).

Conclusion: The Hadlock and Shepard formulae with an EFW < 10th percentile performed best as a screening test for SGA in Zambia, although none performed well. Incorporation of Doppler criteria for FGR did not improve detection, though this finding must be evaluated with caution given the smaller sample size.

62 | Clinical Outcomes Associated With Abnormal Middle Cerebral Artery Doppler Velocities in Fetal Sacrococcygeal Teratomas

Brian A. Burnett; Christian M. Parobek; Westley Pollard; Cara M. Buskmiller; Roopali V. Donepudi; Magdalena Sanz Cortes; Ahmed A. Nassr; Michael A. Belfort; Jessian L. Munoz

Baylor College of Medicine, Houston, TX

Objective: To evaluate the association between abnormal fetal middle cerebral artery (MCA) Doppler velocities and neonatal outcomes in pregnancies complicated by fetal sacrococcygeal teratoma (SCT).

Study Design: We conducted a retrospective cohort study of prenatally diagnosed sacrococcygeal teratomas managed at a tertiary fetal center between 1/2014 and 1/2026. Cases were stratified by fetal middle cerebral artery (MCA) Doppler status (normal vs abnormal), with abnormal defined as a peak systolic velocity ≥1.5 multiples of the median (MoM). Prenatal sonographic findings, fetal interventions, and perinatal outcomes were compared between groups. The primary outcome was neonatal hemoglobin concentration at birth. Secondary outcomes included platelet count, neonatal mortality, gestational age at delivery, age at postnatal surgical resection and histopathologic findings from postnatal tumor specimens.

Results: Among 36 fetuses with prenatally diagnosed SCT, 14 (39%) had abnormal MCA Doppler velocities. Abnormal MCA Dopplers were associated with more severe prenatal disease, including higher tumor‐to‐fetal weight ratio (median 0.67 vs 0.15, p < 0.01), cardiomegaly (93% vs 27%, p < 0.01), hydrops (71% vs 9.1%, p < 0.01), and placentomegaly (50% vs 9.1%, p = 0.01). Neonates with abnormal MCA Dopplers had markedly lower hemoglobin concentrations at birth compared with those with normal MCA Dopplers (median 8.4 vs 16.9 g/dL, p < 0.01), accompanied by lower hematocrit (25% vs 51%, p < 0.01) and platelet counts (148 vs 220 × 103/µ L, p < 0.01), with increasing MCA peak systolic velocity associated with higher odds of neonatal anemia. Abnormal MCA Dopplers were also associated with higher neonatal mortality (40% vs 0%, p = 0.01) and earlier gestational age at delivery (28 vs 36wk, p < 0.01).

Conclusion: In fetuses with sacrococcygeal teratoma, abnormal MCA Doppler velocities are associated with severe neonatal anemia, thrombocytopenia, distinct tumor histopathology, and adverse neonatal outcomes. MCA Doppler assessment may help identify higher‐risk cases and guide multidisciplinary perinatal planning.

63 | Actionable Parental Diagnoses From Expanded Carrier Screening

Michelle J. Wang1; Lauren O'Grady2; Allissia Gilmartin3; Carrie Blout Zawatsky4; Harini Somanchi2; Julie Yeo2; Rachel E. Hickey5; Staci Kallish6; Amanda Pritchard7; Anne Mardy8; Allegra Quadri5; Colby Chase7; Emily Shelkowitz9; Irene J. Chang10; Joshua Baker5; Katherine Swanson10; Katherine Anderson11; Maria Laura Duque Lasio12; Nina B. Gold2

1Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, Boston, MA; 2Massachusetts General Hospital, Boston, MA; 3Boston Children's Hospital, Boston, MA; 4Brigham and Women's Hospital, Boston, MA; 5Lurie Children's Hospital, Chicago, IL; 6Penn Medicine, Philadelphia, MA; 7University of Michigan Health, Ann Arbor, MI; 8University of Texas at Austin, Austin, TX; 9University of Washington, Seattle, WA; 10University of California, San Francisco, San Francisco, CA; 11University of Vermont Health, Burlington, VT; 12Washington University in St. Louis, St. Louis, MO

Objective: In addition to identifying at‐risk carrier couples, expanded carrier screening (ECS) is also increasingly identifying seemingly asymptomatic prospective parents with pathogenic variants associated with disease. True estimates of the population prevalence, phenotypic variability, disease penetrance and real‐world clinical impact of these incidentally identified pathogenic variants are not well described in the literature.

Study Design: We completed a retrospective case series across eight participating institutions to assess the real‐world clinical implications of an incidental parental diagnosis made through carrier screening.

Results: 133 cases of incidental diagnoses were identified, of which 35/133 (26%) were X‐linked, 42/133 (32%) were autosomal recessive, 46/133 (35%) could be autosomal dominant or autosomal recessive, and 10/133 (8%) were autosomal dominant or considered a risk factor (Figure 1). Of the cases included, 114/133 (86%) of the individuals identified as female, 18/133 (14%) identified as male. Of the reported cases, 65/133 (49%) had a consultation with a genetics provider prior to the carrier screen being sent (39 preconception and 26 prenatally) and 121/133 (90%) saw a genetics provider after their carrier screening results returned. 38/133 (29%) cases had symptoms of the disorder that was identified on ECS and 76/133 (57%) had changes to their medical or reproductive health care (Figure 2). Finally, ECS findings led to additional referrals to subspecialty providers in 79/133 (59%) instances.

Conclusion: This study demonstrates that a significant proportion of patients with incidental genetic diagnoses from expanded carrier screening have significant changes to their general and/or reproductive health and reinforces that many female‐identifying patients can experience symptoms of X‐linked conditions. As carrier screening expands beyond its traditional reproductive scope, clinicians must be prepared to address its broader implications for personal genetic health.
