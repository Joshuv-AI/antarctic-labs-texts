Identifying and characterizing the germinal genetic landscape of men with prostate cancer: A real-life, retrospective, multicenter study
Authors: Ran Svirsky, Batya Sonnenfeld, Inbal Kedar, Miora Linial, Noy Azulay, Adi Sharabi-Nov, Hadar Shalev, Yael Goldberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeGenetic factors play a role in prostate cancer (PCa) development. This real-life, retrospective, multicenter study aimed to identify and characterize the germline genetic testing landscape in men with PCa and to assess the added benefit of next-generation sequencing (NGS) testing compared with testing for founder disease-causing variants.MethodsThis study included all men who were referred for genetic counseling because of PCa. All patients were offered germline genetic testing. Demographic, clinical, and pathological information was retrieved from their medical records. Variant classification was determined using the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guidelines.ResultsOf the 293 men with PCa tested, in 39 (13.3%), a positive genetic finding (pathogenic variant, likely pathogenic, or risk variant) was reported: 18 (6.1%) men had a positive test result for high-penetrance genes (BRCA1, BRCA2, ATM, NF1, and MSH6), and 21 (7.2%) had risk allele variants. One-third (33%) of the disease-causing or likely disease-causing variants identified through NGS in our PCa cohort were unique rather than founder variants. The only significant correlation between positive results and demographic or clinical factors was observed between a positive test result and a family history of cancer.ConclusionOur findings underscore the importance of NGS genetic investigations in patients with PCa, regardless of ethnicity or disease stage.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) represents a significant global health burden as the second most common malignancy worldwide, with approximately 1.4 million new cases and 375,000 deaths annually.1 It is now well established that approximately 5% to 10% of PCa cases, and up to 40% of those occurring earlier in life, are attributable to hereditary factors, often mediated through germline disease-causing variants or likely disease-causing variants in genes such as BRCA2, HOXB13, ATM, and DNA damage repair genes. This recognition has led to increasing use of germline genetic testing to identify individuals at increased risk and to inform management of both patients and their families.

These genetic alterations not only increase the risk of developing PCa but also influence its clinical course and treatment response. Specific demographic factors have been correlated with positive genetic testing results, providing valuable criteria for identifying high-risk individuals.2, 3, 4 These factors include Ashkenazi Jewish descent, age at diagnosis, family history of related cancers, and high Gleason scores (≥7).5,6 The identification of these risk factors has enabled more targeted genetic screening strategies, potentially improving early detection and intervention rates.

In response to these findings, organizations such as the National Comprehensive Cancer Network and the Israeli Medical Genetics Association have established guidelines for genetic testing in patients with PCa. Current guidelines recommend testing for patients with high-risk disease (Gleason score ≥7), metastatic disease, Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry, or relevant family history.3,4 These guidelines aim to optimize the use of genetic testing resources while maximizing the identification of individuals at increased risk for hereditary PCa.

Despite the growing implementation of germline genetic testing in PCa, the optimal approach to genetic testing for PCa may vary across populations, particularly in regions with founder disease-causing variants or unique genetic backgrounds, such as Israel. In particular, the prevalence and spectrum of germline variants, including founder disease-causing variants, and their association with clinical and family history characteristics are not fully characterized in this setting. The objective of our study was to characterize the germline genetic landscape of patients with PCa and provide insights into the applicability and potential limitations of current genetic testing guidelines.

Materials and Methods

Study design and patient cohort

This retrospective study was conducted over a 4.5-year period, from August 2019 through February 2024. The study population comprised all male patients referred for genetic counseling due to a PCa diagnosis at the genetics institutions of 2 hospitals during the study time frame.

All participants in the study were offered the opportunity to undergo germline genetic testing. It is important to note that during most of the study period, germline genetic testing for patients with PCa in Israel, including testing for founder disease-causing variants, was not routinely reimbursed and was predominantly paid out of pocket. Public funding for founder disease-causing variant testing in this clinical setting was introduced only in recent years, near the end of the study period.

The decision between founder disease-causing variants testing and next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel testing was made in routine clinical practice by the treating physician together with the patient. All patients were offered germline testing. During most of the study period, all patients who chose to pay out of pocket opted for NGS panel testing (Invitae or Color), whereas founder disease-causing variant testing was usually preferred when publicly funded, particularly in patients with Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry and no strong or complex family history. Patients with strong or complex family histories, early-onset or high-risk disease, non-Ashkenazi ancestry, or prior uninformative founder testing were more often referred directly for NGS panel testing.

All variants categorized as founder disease-causing variants in this study are listed in Supplemental Table 1. The NGS panel is an out-of-pocket expense. Testing modalities included either the targeted panel of Israeli founder disease-causing variants or a more comprehensive, multigene NGS commercial panel. The NGS panel included an extensive set of genes for which disease-causing variants or likely disease-causing variants are recognized as established risk factors for PCa, such as ATM, BRCA1, BRCA2, CHEK2, EPCAM, HOXB13, MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, NBN, PMS2, TP53, RAD51D, and PALB2. Germline genetic testing was primarily performed using commercial multigene panels: the Invitae Multi-Cancer Panel (Invitae) and the Color hereditary cancer panel (Color Health). All patients tested with these panels underwent analysis of key PCa–related genes, including ATM, BRCA1, BRCA2, CHEK2, EPCAM, HOXB13, MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, NBN, PMS2, TP53, RAD51D, and PALB2. According to manufacturer specifications, during the study period, these assays detect single-nucleotide variants and small insertions/deletions and include exon-level deletion/duplication analysis for covered genes. The panels also contained additional cancer-related genes; however, complete historical records of the full gene content of all panel versions were unavailable, and therefore, we report only the genes consistently included across all panels. Detailed demographic and clinical information for each patient was retrieved from their medical records and computerized patient management systems.

Sequencing and variant interpretation

For this patient cohort, the genetic testing sample type was exclusively blood. The classification of identified variants as disease-causing variants, variants of uncertain significance (VUS), or increased-risk alleles was determined based on the guidelines established by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics. A positive test result was defined as the detection of a pathogenic/likely pathogenic (P/LP) variant, which could be either a low penetrance variant (LPV) or a high penetrance variant. For the LPV category, the study included variants previously defined as risk alleles, such as APC:c.3920T>A;p.Ile1307Lys and CHEK2:c.1283C>T;P.Ser428Phe.

For analytic purposes, patients with at least 1 disease-causing variant or likely disease-causing variant were classified as positive, irrespective of additional VUS. Patients were classified as “VUS only” if their germline testing identified 1 or more VUS but not a disease-causing variant or a likely disease-causing variant. Conversely, P/LP variants exhibiting mosaic results or heterozygosity in a recessive context were deemed negative findings.

Data collection

For each patient participating in the study, a comprehensive set of data points was collected, including the following: (1) ancestry and consanguinity, with patients classified based on their Ashkenazi descent as either full (3 or more grandparents of Ashkenazi descent), mixed (2 grandparents of Ashkenazi descent), or none (fewer than 2 grandparents of Ashkenazi descent); (2) age at the time of genetic counseling; (3) Gleason score; (4) age at diagnosis of PCa; (5) family history of cancer; (6) type of genetic test taken (founder disease-causing variant panel or NGS panel); and (6) variants identified through genetic testing, categorized as disease-causing variants, likely disease-causing variants, VUS, and increased-risk alleles.

Statistical analysis (descriptive statistics)

For categorical variables, summary tables provided the sample size (n) and relative frequencies (%). For continuous variables, summary tables included the mean and SD or the median and interquartile range, depending on the normality of the data distribution as assessed by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Pearson χ2 tests were used to examine the correlations between the study groups and categorical parameters. The effects of continuous variables on the groups were assessed using an independent-samples t test or a Mann-Whitney nonparametric test, based on the sample size and data normality distribution. A multivariate logistic regression model was used to investigate the correlations between the genetic results of P+LP/Low, Ashkenazi ethnicity, and family cancer history while adjusting for age, presenting odds ratios and 95% CIs. A P value of 5% or less was considered statistically significant. The data were analyzed using SPSS version 29 (IBM).

Results

Patient clinical and demographic characteristics

From August 2019 to February 2024, 455 Israeli patients with PCa received genetic counseling at 2 Israeli medical centers. The average age at diagnosis was 67.8 years (SD = 8.6). Of 455 patients, 193 (42.4%) were of Ashkenazi Jewish descent, meaning they had at least 2 grandparents who were Ashkenazi. Additionally, 93 patients (20.4%) had metastatic disease.

A family history of ovarian, breast, or pancreatic cancer was reported by 13 patients with PCa (2.9%). Most patients (362, 79.6%) had nonmetastatic disease. Of 455 patients, 285 patients (62.6%) had a Gleason score of ≥8. Germline genetic testing outcomes

Of the 455 patients in the cohort, 293 (64.4%) underwent germline genetic testing. Significant variations were observed in several parameters between the tested and nontested groups. The tested group was younger (67.1 years, SD = 8.3), had a higher proportion of Ashkenazi descent (136, 46.4%), and reported a greater occurrence of malignancy in their family (12, 4.1%) compared with the nontested group.

Of the 293 men tested, 190 (64.8%) underwent testing for the presence of known germline founder disease-causing variants, whereas the remaining 103 (35.2%) underwent germline genetic panel testing using NGS-based commercial gene panels.

Overall, 39 men (13.3% of the tested patients) had a positive genetic finding. Specifically, 18 men (6.1% of the tested cohort) presented with a disease-causing variant or likely disease-causing variant in a high-penetrance gene (Table 1) (in MSH6, BRCA1, or BRCA2), and 21 men (7.2%) had results associated with an increased-risk allele (Supplemental Table 2). Additionally, VUS were reported in 39 (13.3%) patients (Supplemental Table 3). Interestingly, 12 (67%) patients diagnosed with disease-causing variants or likely disease-causing variants in high-penetrance genes using NGS panels had a founder disease-causing variant, whereas 6 (33%) had unique variants (Figure 1).

Ethnicity and genetic testing outcomes

Of the 455 participants, 232 (51%) had partial (at least 2 grandparents of Ashkenazi descent) or complete (all 4 grandparents of Ashkenazi descent) Ashkenazi ancestry, whereas 223 (49%) patients were of non-Ashkenazi descent. Discussion

This study offers real-life insights into the germline genetic landscape of PCa and distinguishes between high-penetrance variant and LPV. It also highlights the prevalence of unique germline disease-causing variants. Our investigation evaluates the significance of demographic and clinical factors associated with disease-causing germline variants in this patient cohort, providing a comprehensive analysis that has not been previously undertaken in this context.

Prevalence of germline variants

Our analysis revealed a 13.3% prevalence of germline disease-causing or likely disease-causing variants in our cohort, with 6.1% in high-penetrance genes and 7.2% in low-penetrance genes (Supplemental Tables 1 and 2). These findings are consistent with prior studies, such as Nicolosi et al (2019),7 who reported a 17.2% prevalence, and Raychaudhuri et al5 and Darst et al,6 who reported prevalence of 10% to 15%.5,6 Our real-world observations are also consistent with recent data from the PROCLAIM trial and related analyses,8,9 which also demonstrated a substantial prevalence of germline P/LP variants in patients with PCa.

Correlation with risk factors

Contrary to common expectations, our analysis showed that only a family history of cancer was significantly associated with positive genetic test results. This finding supports the hereditary nature of PCa. However, other well-known risk factors, including age at diagnosis, ethnicity, and Gleason score, did not show significant associations with positive test results observed in our study. This discrepancy from previous research raises important questions about the universality of these risk factors and the possible influence of population-specific genetic backgrounds or environmental factors. Our observation, along with that of others such as Shore et al10, supports the idea of universal germline testing for all patients with PCa, regardless of age, ethnicity, or disease stage.10

Ethnicity

Our study found no significant difference in the prevalence of high-penetrance disease-causing variants between Ashkenazi and non-Ashkenazi individuals. This observation contrasts with previous studies that reported higher rates of disease-causing variants in Ashkenazi populations.2 This finding highlights the complexity of studying genetic predisposition in ethnically diverse populations and emphasizes the need for more nuanced approaches to defining and analyzing ethnic subgroups in genetic studies.

Unique vs founder disease-causing variants

The finding that 33% of disease-causing variants or likely disease-causing variants in high-penetrance genes identified by NGS in our PCa cohort were unique rather than founder disease-causing variants is significant and has far-reaching implications for genetic testing in PCa. Because only a subset of patients underwent multigene NGS panel testing, our estimate of the prevalence and diversity of germline P/LP variants is likely to be conservative. It is plausible that if all patients in the cohort had undergone NGS, additional rare and unique P/LP variants would have been detected.

This observation is consistent with findings from Desai et al11, who reported that a BRCA1/2 founder panel limited to the 3 classic Ashkenazi Jewish founder variants missed 10% of BRCA1/2 P/LP variants in patients with full Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry and 30% in those with partial Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry.11 Together, these data challenge the adequacy of founder-disease-causing variant panels for capturing the full spectrum of germline variants in patients with PCa. Although founder disease-causing variant panels are valuable for detecting common, well-characterized disease-causing variants, they may miss rare or population-specific variants that could be equally important for disease risk and progression, assuming a similar distribution across the broader cohort. A founder-only strategy would have missed approximately 33%. One hundred ninety men in our cohort were tested using a founder-variant-only approach. Consequently, relying solely on these panels may underestimate the true prevalence of P/LP variants in patients with PCa.12

Study limitations

Our study has several limitations. Its retrospective design introduces potential biases in patient selection and data collection, particularly regarding self-reported family histories. The uptake of germline testing was likely influenced by the historical requirement for out-of-pocket payment for much of the study period, despite all patients being offered testing. This may limit the generalizability of our observed testing rates to current practice under broader public reimbursement. The limited use of NGS panels (35.2% of patients) constrains our understanding of the prevalence of unique variants and VUS. However, several factors support the interpretation that the identified variants are primarily of germline origin. These include that the variants were identified in blood samples, which primarily reflect germline DNA, and that no mosaicism was reported, as well as the high prevalence of these variants in our cohort compared with the general population. Another limitation is that although most patients underwent testing with either the Invitae Multi-Cancer Panel or the Color hereditary cancer panel, we did not have complete historical records of the full gene content of all panel versions. We can confirm the consistent inclusion of key PCa-related genes (ATM, BRCA1, BRCA2, CHEK2, EPCAM, HOXB13, MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, NBN, PMS2, TP53, RAD51D, and PALB2), but it is possible that variants in genes included only in some panel versions were underdetected.

Clinical implications

The high prevalence of germline disease-causing variants observed in our study (13.3%) suggests that a substantial proportion of patients with PCa may benefit from genetic testing. This could lead to a more personalized treatment approach for the proband. For example, individuals who are heterozygous for a disease-causing variant in a germline DNA damage repair gene, such as BRCA1, BRCA2, ATM, or PALB2, are candidates for poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitor therapy in appropriate disease settings.11,13 Similarly, germline disease-causing variants in mismatch repair genes (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, and EPCAM) may identify patients whose tumors are mismatch repair–deficient, supporting consideration of immune checkpoint inhibitors and enhanced colorectal cancer surveillance.14,15 In addition, identification of germline high-risk disease-causing variants can guide eligibility for precision oncology clinical trials. Beyond the proband, these findings also inform surveillance and risk-reduction strategies for relatives who carry the same germline variant and improve risk assessment for family members through cascade testing. Furthermore, the finding that 33% of P/LP variants were unique rather than founder disease-causing variants challenges the adequacy of founder disease-causing variants panels for capturing the full spectrum of germline variants in patients with PCa. This supports the use of more comprehensive genetic testing approaches in clinical practice.

Conclusion

Our study highlights the critical role of genetic factors in diagnosing and managing PCa, particularly in populations with identifiable risk factors. Establishing comprehensive protocols for genetic screening based on demographic and familial history could improve outcomes for patients and their families, providing a clearer pathway to targeted prevention and intervention strategies. Future research using NGS is essential to elucidate the prevalence of disease-causing variants, both founder and unique, in patients with PCa and to refine genetic testing guidelines accordingly.

By addressing these key findings, health care providers and public health stakeholders can work toward a more nuanced and practical approach to PCa risk assessment, prevention, and treatment.
