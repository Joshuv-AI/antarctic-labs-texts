“Quadrupling” the protein family space with global metagenomics
Authors: Eleni Aplakidou, Fotis A Baltoumas, Maria N Chasapi, Efthimia Lamari, Iro N Chasapi, IIias Georgakopoulos-Soares, Evangelos Karatzas, Ioannis Iliopoulos, Aydin Buluç, Robert D Finn, Antonio Pedro Camargo, Nikos C Kyrpides, Georgios A Pavlopoulos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe known universe of protein families represents only a small fraction of nature’s molecular diversity. From 40.3 billion predicted open reading frames across 40 446 metagenomes, 9540 metatranscriptomes, and 539 million proteins from 167 415 reference genomes, we identified 608 258 previously uncharacterized putative protein families with ≥100 members and 6.5 million families with ≥25 members, none matching known Pfam domains or reference proteins. This effort doubles the known repertoire of large families and quadruples that of smaller families. Integration of AlphaFold2-based structural predictions with gene-neighborhood and taxonomic analyses enables the characterization of these previously unannotated proteins, revealing candidates for both novel and known biological functions in understudied microbial lineages and biomes. This expanded repertoire provides insights into microbial adaptation and broadens the molecular toolkit available for biotechnology, highlighting the power of global metagenomics to uncover hidden protein diversity.

Introduction

Proteins serve as the fundamental molecular machines of life, playing essential roles in metabolism, gene expression, cellular defense, and structural integrity. Gaining a comprehensive understanding of proteins is crucial for advancing knowledge in biology, evolution, and disease mechanisms. Although their central role in life, the immense diversity of microbial proteins remains largely uncharacterized, representing a vast reservoir of functional and ecological information yet to be explored. With the explosion of sequencing technologies, we now have access to vast amounts of metagenomic protein sequence data, housed in databases that are growing at an unprecedented rate [1–3]. Existing databases containing protein information, such as sequences, structures, and functional annotations, are confined to well-characterized protein families; others serve mainly as collections of unstructured datasets, and many focus on specific environments, resulting in large portions of protein diversity being overlooked (Supplementary Table S1). Consequently, despite this rapid expansion of data, a vast and largely uncharted metagenomic “protein dark matter” remains underexplored and underrepresented in current repositories. These novel proteins, often hidden within metagenomic datasets, are not merely rare variants; recent studies have shown that they include abundant families that are globally distributed and ecologically significant [4].

By clustering billions of metagenomic sequences and integrating structural prediction, gene-neighborhood context, and ecological metadata, it becomes possible to define both abundant and rare protein families, identify novel folds, and assign putative functions to previously uncharacterized sequences. This approach not only increases the coverage of microbial protein space but also bridges the gap between sequence discovery and potential functional insight, enabling targeted exploration of enzymes, metabolic pathways, and molecular scaffolds for biotechnology [5, 6].

Previously published studies, including FESNov [3], AGNOSTOS [7], and NMPFams v1 [8, 9], have specifically aimed to explore this uncharted protein space and systematically characterize novel proteins from metagenomic data. FESNov leverages a large collection of assembled prokaryotic genomes (MAGs and SAGs) to identify novel gene families with strong evolutionary and functional signals, thereby focusing on genome-resolved uncultivated taxa while not addressing unassembled metagenomic sequences or non-prokaryotic components. AGNOSTOS is a computational workflow and conceptual framework developed to unify the known and unknown microbial coding sequence space by integrating large-scale genomic and metagenomic data from human-associated and marine ecosystems. The framework operates by inferring, validating, and refining homologous gene clusters and higher-order gene cluster communities. NMPFams v1 is a large-scale catalog of 106 198 novel metagenomic protein families, each comprising at least 100 related sequences and showing no detectable similarity to reference microbial genes or Pfam entries while also providing taxonomic, environmental, and predicted structural annotations.

In this study, we present a curated expansion of microbial protein families that builds upon and extends previous large-scale efforts in the field. Compared to these prior studies, our approach differs substantially in methodology, dataset composition, and scale. Specifically, we analyze raw metagenomic data across all domains of life, including viruses, and incorporate a significantly larger and more diverse protein dataset spanning multiple habitat types, together with metatranscriptomic data to enhance functional interpretation. Notably, the present study builds upon NMPFams v1 by substantially expanding the resource through the integration of a much larger and more diverse collection of metagenomic sequences. In addition to increasing its scale, this new version moves beyond sequence cataloging by incorporating biological context and putative functional annotations, thereby enhancing both the comprehensiveness and biological interpretability of the database.

Here, using a scalable, biologically validated computational pipeline applied to one of the most extensive microbial datasets assembled to date [5] (Fig. 1), we generate a large-scale catalog of novel protein families and predicted folds with potential functions. This framework enables systematic exploration of the functional “dark matter” of metagenomes, uncovering new structural and biological diversity. It further supports applications ranging from enzyme discovery to synthetic biology, helping to transform metagenomic sequence space into biological insight.

Materials and methods

Data collection and filtering

All publicly accessible metagenomes, metatranscriptomes, and reference genomes were obtained from the Integrated Microbial Genomes & Microbiomes (IMG/M) database provided by the Joint Genome Institute (Fig. 1). Metagenomes and metatranscriptomes were retrieved in October 2023 [5], while reference genomes were collected in December 2024. A total of 522 135 784 protein sequences were extracted from 143 797 bacterial genomes, 761 975 sequences from 19 747 viral genomes, 6 651 575 sequences from 3128 archaeal genomes, and 9 491 122 sequences from 803 eukaryotic genomes, culminating in 539 040 456 sequences. All sequences were solely derived from isolated reference genomes, excluding metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs), single-amplicon genomes (SAGs), cell enrichment samples, and single particle sorts. All data used in this analysis are publicly available and all domains of life have been included in the analysis. The study analyzed 49 986 microbiome datasets (40 446 metagenomes and 9540 metatranscriptomes, totaling 10 851 187 196 scaffolds) that initially contained 40 315 976 846 open reading frames (ORFs) of varying lengths from assembled metagenomes. Protein predictions in IMG/M [5] are generated using Prodigal v2.6.3 [6] and GeneMarkS-2 [10]. To maintain a dataset composed primarily of reliable protein predictions, specific filtering criteria were employed. Given that genes situated toward the center of a contig are more likely to be complete. At the same time, those closer to the ends may be truncated; sequences located within 10 nucleotide residues of scaffold ends were also removed to mitigate the inclusion of potentially truncated genes. Furthermore, only sequences with 35 or more residues from scaffolds exceeding 500 nucleotides in length were retained to ensure that mainly complete genes were represented. This is because shorter scaffolds increase the risk that genes are located near the ends, which could lead to truncation. Low-complexity regions were masked using the tantan application (version 26) [11]. After these filtering steps, any remaining sequences shorter than 35 amino acids were also discarded. The resulting final catalog comprised 6 231 725 853 sequences derived from 44 856 microbiome datasets and 2 893 181 669 scaffolds. We consider this to be the high-quality non-redundant microbiome protein sequence set (high quality/non redundant), which accounted for 15.46% of the starting dataset.

Identification of novel proteins: hits to Pfam and Antifam

To determine which of the filtered proteins possess known domains, the filtered metagenome dataset was compared against the Pfam-A (version 37) database of profile Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) (Fig. 1), which contains 21 979 families and 709 clans. Pfam hits were identified using the hmmsearch tool from the HMMER 3.3.2 package [12], applying the default trusted cutoff (–cut_tc). Using the trusted cutoff (TC) threshold provides the most stringent filtering available, ensuring that sequences excluded from our novelty search match Pfam families with the same high confidence as their founding members. While this conservative approach prioritizes high-confidence novelty, it naturally enriches for smaller, single-domain proteins and intentionally excludes multidomain proteins that might otherwise contain novel domains. Following the removal of Pfam hits, the final dataset comprised 2 664 804 251 proteins, extracted from 44 796 datasets and 1 579 469 832 scaffolds.

The same methodology was applied to Antifam [7], a database specifically designed to identify and eliminate spurious or erroneous protein sequences arising from low-complexity regions or other repetitive patterns, improperly extended ORFs, or incorrectly translated non-protein-coding RNA genes (Fig. 1). While Pfam catalogs functional protein domains and families, Antifam focuses on detecting sequences or families that are likely artifacts (such as those arising from low-complexity regions, coiled-coil structures, or other repetitive patterns) that may falsely resemble functional domains. The Antifam filtering process yielded 398 073 hits, resulting in a final dataset of 2 664 406 178 proteins (43% of the HQ-NR-MB set).

Identification of novel proteins: hits to reference genomes

To identify novel proteins in the filtered microbiome protein dataset, additional comparisons were made against the most recent reference genomes collected in late 2024, which encompass 539 040 456 protein sequences. Two sequential methods were employed for the comparison (Fig. 1). Initially, the LAST algorithm [13] was utilized with parameters set at 30% similarity and 70% coverage bidirectional [14]. LAST is designed explicitly for large-scale genomic or protein sequence comparisons, utilizing a variation of spaced seeds to enhance sensitivity while minimizing memory usage. Due to its speed and ability to handle large datasets, LAST is well-suited for metagenomic data analysis but can miss hits with lower sequence identity. For this reason, the dataset that yielded no hits in the LAST comparison (1 957 621 091 proteins) was subsequently analyzed against the reference genomes using DIAMOND [15], which is more sensitive, albeit slower. The DIAMOND search was performed using the same search parameters. Requiring bidirectional coverage ensures that both the query and target are substantially aligned, maximizing biological relevance and eliminating false positives when matching metagenomic sequences against reference databases. This stringent requirement is critical given that metagenomic proteins are often truncated relative to full-length references; however, because meeting high coverage against larger reference proteins is difficult for longer candidates, this step naturally enriched our dataset for smaller proteins. Following these steps, the final dataset comprised 1 736 930 453 proteins, sourced from 44 721 microbiome datasets and 1 115 001 758 scaffolds.

Clustering and generation of protein families

In this analysis, MMseqs2 Linclust [16] was run in bidirectional mode with 30% sequence identity and 80% coverage, balancing sensitivity and computational load (Fig. 1). Clustering was performed on all sequences irrespective of taxonomy. A direct comparison of the previous clustering methodology (NMPFam v.1) is presented in Supplementary File S1. Using these parameters, 370 185 087 proteins were clustered into 6 588 876 families, each containing at least 25 members. These families were derived from 44 198 datasets and spanned 253 359 325 scaffolds, representing 21.31% of the total 1 736 930 453 proteins analyzed. Proteins that did not meet the minimum clustering threshold were excluded from further analysis.

Mapping to metagenome-assembled genomes

In this study, all prokaryotic MAGs were sourced from the IMG/M database, while eukaryotic MAGs were excluded. According to the MIMAG standards [17], the bins were classified as high, medium, or low quality, with high-quality MAGs characterized by >90% completeness and <5% contamination, as determined with CheckM [18]. As of June 2024, the dataset encompassed 21 817 high-quality prokaryotic MAGs, providing a robust foundation for the analysis of microbial diversity [19]. For this comparison, DIAMOND was utilized with parameters set at 30% similarity and 70% coverage bidirectional. Rather than serving as a filter for novelty identification, given that many MAGs contain functionally unknown proteins, the comparison with MAGs was conducted primarily as a contextual analysis to evaluate sequence overlap and distribution across reconstructed genomes.

Profile generation

Novel protein families containing 100 or more members were gathered and subjected to multiple sequence alignment (MSA) generation using MAFFT [20]. After the initial alignment, trimming was applied to minimize gaps as follows: The “central” or “pivot” sequence of each MSA was identified by converting aligned sequences to numerical values, constructing an all-versus-all matrix, and computing the Hamming distance. Subsequently, the central sequence was used as a guide to identify gaps, isolate conserved regions, and ensure that poorly aligned or gapped areas were excluded from subsequent analysis. Finally, hhfilter [21] was employed to reduce sequence redundancy within each family MSA, with parameters set to an identity threshold of 95% and a coverage of 70%, thereby reducing bias by eliminating highly similar sequences. Only families with at least 16 effective sequences after filtering were retained for structural prediction, following the protocol established in the original version of NMPFamsDB. This cutoff was chosen as it was previously found to be the minimum number of sequences per MSA capable of producing the highest percentage of high-quality results [8].

Candidate families for structural model prediction

Structure prediction was performed using the ColabFold [22] implementation of AlphaFold2 [23] in de novo mode (no template input), following the protocol established in the original version of NMPFamsDB [8]. Five models were generated per run, with three recycle rounds computed per model. For each cluster, the model with the highest average predicted template-modeling (pTM) score and best average predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) score was selected for downstream evaluation.

Structural homology search

Only high-quality (pTM ≥ 0.7) and medium-quality (0.5 ≤ pTM < 0.7) structures were retained for further analysis. To assess structural similarity with experimentally determined structures, these models were searched using Foldseek [24] against 5653 CATH [25] (v4.4, released October 2024) to detect matches to structural domains, and PDB assemblies [26] (March 2024 release), to account for multi-domain models with matches to multimeric complexes, as well as any PDB structures not already incorporated in CATH. Hits were collected for all family structural models with an alignment TM-score >0.5. For the remaining family models, query and target lengths were considered. If the query length was shorter than the target, the query alignment score was assessed with a 0.5 threshold. Conversely, for cases where the target length was shorter, a family was considered a hit if the target alignment length was at least 0.5. For the remaining families, an additional search against AlphaFoldDB [23, 27] was performed to identify models that lacked experimental structure but had been previously predicted by AlphaFold. For each hit identified in AlphaFoldDB, further annotation was carried out using metadata retrieved from the corresponding UniProt entries to determine whether the hits were functionally annotated or merely predicted.

Superfamily generation

To assess how many families can be grouped into superfamilies based on structural information, Foldseek clustering was performed in bidirectional mode with a coverage threshold of 80% and a TM-score threshold of 0.5.

Taxonomic annotation

The taxonomic analysis in this study focused on families containing over 100 members, adhering to criteria established in previous studies [8, 9] to ensure statistical robustness. Protein families were defined as clusters meeting this threshold, and their associated scaffolded sequences were retrieved from the IMG/M database. Taxonomic classification of these sequences began with Kraken2 [28] (version 2.1.3), a highly accurate tool for taxonomic identification. To ensure comprehensive coverage across all domains of life, we utilized the full RefSeq database (∼1 TB) rather than the standard Kraken2 database. To enhance the annotation of proteins and minimize false positives (given the limitations of taxonomic algorithms like Kraken2 in handling eukaryotic sequences [29]), a supplementary analysis using MMseqs2 taxonomy against UniRef100 [30] (version 13) for eukaryotic and unclassified scaffolds was performed. For scaffolds that remained unclassified after this step, the Whokaryote tool [31] (version 1.1.2) was used to differentiate eukaryotic from prokaryotic contigs based on domain-specific gene structural features. Scaffolds still unclassified following Whokaryote analysis were further analyzed with EukRep [32] (version 0.6.7), a tool for detecting eukaryotic sequences in metagenomic and metatranscriptomic datasets. Lastly, geNomad [33] (version 1.8.1) was used to detect additional plasmid and viral sequences among the remaining unclassified scaffolds.

Biome distribution

A protein family distribution across various ecosystems was performed to enhance our understanding of habitat-specific diversity within these protein families. To explore this distribution across different biomes, metadata from each environmental sample was extracted from the IMG/M database and organized using the GOLD classification system [34].

In this study, the three primary GOLD ecosystems (environmental, host-associated, and engineered) were further divided into 10 specific ecosystem types for direct comparison and enhanced detail, consistent with the NMPFamsDB study. These 10 ecosystem types include air, freshwater, marine, other aquatic environments, engineered, human, mammal host-associated, other host-associated environments, plants, and terrestrial. Network visualizations were created using Gephi [35], with the layout generated by the Yifan-Hu algorithm [36], and the geographic distribution was computed from each dataset’s geolocation metadata in IMG.

Gene neighborhood

The analysis focuses on gene families with >100 members. The majority of novel proteins are located close to known genes, suggesting that Pfam annotations of the known genes can provide valuable insights into the functions of the novel proteins. Specifically, 81 908 529 out of 87 393 063 scaffolds contain both novel and known genes, highlighting potential interactions or shared functions. To conduct the gene neighborhood analysis, scaffolds associated with families comprising >100 members were collected. To maximize data recovery, gene neighborhood analysis was performed across entire scaffolds. This approach aligns with previous studies [8] and is justified by the relatively short length of the majority of scaffolds in our dataset. However, to prevent the inclusion of distant, functionally unrelated genes, a supplementary analysis has been performed by limiting the search window to 10 ORFs flanking each side of the protein of interest. These scaffolds were then examined against Pfam annotations. For each scaffold, the top five most frequent Pfam domains were identified. The Pfam domains were associated with their respective Pfam descriptions. In addition, these domains were subsequently matched to Gene Ontology (GO) terms using the InterPro2GO mapping from InterPro. To simplify and interpret the results, GO subsets (commonly referred to as GO slims), condensed versions of GO that contain curated subsets of terms, were used. The GO terms were further grouped into the three primary GO categories: molecular function, cellular component, and biological process, enabling distinct, targeted analysis within each category.

Biome distribution

Metagenomic and metatranscriptomic datasets from IMG/M [5] included 49 986 samples with geographic coordinates (latitude and longitude) and GOLD [34] ecosystem classifications (environmental, engineered, host-associated). Most samples were environmental (67.7%), followed by host-associated (23%) and engineered (9.2%). Focusing on microbial families with at least 100 members and at least 5% of samples originating from a single ecosystem type, we identified 608 258 families across 42 975 datasets: 67.5% environmental, 22.6% host-associated, and 9.7% engineered. Geographic coordinates for environmental samples were used to plot their origin locations on a world map (Fig. 2A), underscoring the global reach and diversity of sampled environments.

Gene neighborhood

The analysis of gene neighborhoods within novel protein families, particularly those specific to different environments, provides valuable functional insights into microbial activities across various ecosystems. To gain a more accurate understanding of functions, this study focuses on biome-specific families, aiming to identify the most frequent functions and those that are relatively unique compared to the 50 most common functions in each environment. Gene neighborhood analysis was performed using two complementary approaches: considering the entire scaffold and analyzing a window of 10 ORFs from each side. The key findings presented below were consistently observed and are supported by both approaches. (See Supplementary Figs S3–S8 and Supplementary Tables S2–S7).

Focusing on human-associated specific protein families, the majority of the analyzed samples originate from the digestive system. Gene neighborhood analysis reveals that the MatE [40] (multi antimicrobial extrusion) family, a member of the multidrug/oligosaccharidyl-lipid/polysaccharide (MOP) flippase superfamily, is significantly more prevalent in this environment. Metagenomic studies indicate that genes related to MatE contribute to antimicrobial resistance in gut microbiota. In addition, the unique enrichment of AraC-family transcriptional regulators as gene neighbors in human-associated metagenomes implies that these novel protein families are functionally integrated into host-adaptive regulatory networks, potentially representing uncharacterized effectors of gut colonization and host–microbe interaction [41].

In plant-specific environments, microbes must adapt to unique ecological and physiological conditions, including interactions with plant hosts. The Plant mobile domain and PPR repeat are particularly relevant, as they play essential roles in RNA processing and gene regulation, often found in plant-associated microbes and organelles [42]. Additionally, the NB-ARC domain [43, 44], commonly associated with resistance genes, contributes to microbial adaptation and defense mechanisms in plant environments, highlighting the intricate microbial–plant interactions. Plant-associated novel protein families are also uniquely neighbored by retrotransposon-derived genes [45] (reverse transcriptase, RNase H, and gag-like structural proteins) reflecting the extraordinary proliferation of Ty1/Copia and Ty3/Gypsy LTR retrotransposons that can constitute up to 85% of plant genomes. These functions were identified as exclusively enriched in gene-neighbor protein families specific to biome-restricted plant families, providing interesting, valuable functional insights into previously uncharacterized protein families.

In freshwater ecosystems, hotspots for viral and phage activity, gene neighborhood analysis highlights the exclusive presence of viral-related proteins such as Caudovirus prohead serine proteases [46], phage portal protein [47], and YqaJ-like viral recombinases [48]. These proteins are crucial for viral replication, assembly, and horizontal gene transfer, emphasizing the central role of viruses in shaping microbial communities. Additionally, the microbial reliance on carbohydrate metabolism in freshwater environments is evident in the exclusive presence of proteins such as chitinases, glycosyltransferases, and transglycosylases [49]. These enzymes facilitate the degradation of plant matter, detritus, and chitinous material, supporting nutrient cycling and microbial adaptation in these nutrient-rich but variable habitats.

In terrestrial environments, gene neighborhood analysis identifies proteins with transaminase activity and carboxypeptidase regulatory-like domains as highly prevalent in soil ecosystems. These proteins are essential for amino acid metabolism, contributing to nitrogen cycling [50] by facilitating the synthesis, degradation, and transformation of amino acids and organic nitrogen compounds. Additionally, the significant presence of the tripartite tricarboxylate transporter (TTT) family receptor in these environments highlights its role in the uptake of organic acids from plant root exudates, decaying organic matter, and microbial metabolites. These organic acids serve as crucial energy sources and biosynthetic precursors, supporting microbial survival and activity in soil ecosystems.

Gene neighborhood analysis of novel protein families revealed a dual-layered functional architecture across biomes. While each biome harbored a distinct repertoire of unique protein families reflecting environment-specific adaptations and ecological pressures, a core set of functional categories, including membrane transport, DNA replication and repair, and transcriptional regulation, emerged consistently across all nine biomes examined. Crucially, however, these shared functions were not carried by the same protein families; instead, they were fulfilled by biome-specific gene families of independent evolutionary origin, as evidenced by the divergence in family composition despite functional convergence at the domain level. This pattern suggests that while the selective pressures governing fundamental biological processes are universal, the molecular solutions have arisen repeatedly and independently across distinct ecological contexts, a hallmark of convergent functional evolution.

These findings underscore that the functional landscape of the biosphere is far more conserved than its sequence landscape: biomes that are radically different in taxonomic composition and environmental conditions nonetheless converge on the same core functional repertoire, implying that ecological constraints shape function far more stringently than they shape sequence. Together, the combination of biome-unique families and convergently evolved shared functions positions gene neighborhood analysis as a powerful lens not only for characterizing environment-specific genomic innovations, but also for illuminating the universal functional grammar that underlies microbial life across the Earth’s diverse ecosystems.

Taxonomy

Of the 87 393 064 scaffolds analyzed, 65 471 449 (74.92%) were successfully classified taxonomically, while 21 921 615 (25.08%) remained unclassified. Integrating multiple taxonomic algorithms in the analysis pipeline improved classification rates, reducing the proportion of unclassified scaffolds compared to the previous study [51]. Among the classified scaffolds, the majority were categorized as Bacteria (76.01%), followed by Viruses (14%), Eukaryota (7%), and Archaea (3%), with a small fraction classified as organelles and plasmids. The high classification rate achieved through multi-stage classification, utilizing a succession of taxonomic tools to resolve unassigned sequences, facilitates a deep exploration of diversity and the mapping of functions to specific taxonomic groups.

Using a threshold of at least 95% of samples derived from a single taxonomic domain, 115 377 families (18.9%) were taxonomically assigned to unique domains, while the majority encompassed scaffolds from multiple taxonomic groups. Specifically, 72 590 families (12%) were exclusively bacterial, 23 782 (4%) were solely eukaryotic, 10 547 (2%) were viral, and 3124 (1%) were archaeal. Most families displayed mixed taxonomic compositions, including combinations such as Bacteria and Unclassified (360 610 families, 59.2%), Bacteria and Viruses (105 338 families, 17.3%), and Viruses and Unclassified (30 355 families, 4.99%) (Fig. 2D).

Proteins from families that matched the 21 817 high-quality MAGs were used for taxonomic analysis. Since most MAGs are taxonomically classified, their taxonomy provides valuable insights into family taxonomy. Most families (74%) show no hits against MAGs, while the remaining families exhibit hits in descending order relative to the number of MAGs, indicating that more families have hits in fewer MAGs, while the number of families decreases as the number of MAGs with hits increases (Fig. 2E). Of the MAGs, 90% are bacterial, 6% remain unclassified, and 3% are archaeal. A taxonomic analysis of families based on MAGs identifies the top 20 bacterial phyla most commonly represented across these families (Fig. 2F).

Structural distribution

Focusing on protein families with >100 members, 3D structures were predicted using AlphaFold2/ColabFold [22, 27], following the workflow presented in Fig. 3A. Of the 608 258 families, representative structural models were successfully generated for 156 609. For each family, the model with the highest average pLDDT score was retained. Among these, 26 767 (17.09%) models had a pTM score >0.7, indicating high-quality models, while 47 823 (30.54%) had a pTM score between 0.5 and 0.7, indicating medium-quality models. This part of the analysis focuses on the 74 590 family models that fall into these two quality categories.

To provide insights into functional annotation and explore the novel structural space, these models were searched against well-known structural databases containing both experimentally validated and computationally predicted 3D models. Searches were conducted against CATH [25], PDB [26], and AlphaFoldDB [35] (Fig. 3B). The total number of hits for the 74 590 family models included 68 649 hits from CATH, 70 843 from PDB, and 69 190 from AlphaFoldDB. Specifically, searching against CATH yielded 68 649 family model hits, with 25 766 originating from high-quality models and 42 883 from medium-quality models. The remaining unmatched structures were then searched against the PDB, yielding 2812 additional hits (978 high-quality models and 1834 medium-quality models). Finally, a search against AlphaFoldDB identified 180 more hits (15 high-quality and 165 medium quality). An example of structural annotated folds is presented in Fig. 3D. Clustering with Foldseek produced 2781 singletons and 69 multi-member clusters, indicating most folds are unique. Further structural clustering of the whole dataset suggests these models represent genuinely novel partial or complete folds, rather than variations of known architectures.

These findings enhance functional annotation by leveraging structurally characterized proteins and uncover 2948 potential novel folds (Fig. 3C). Notably, 947 family models exhibit structural similarities to entries in AlphaFoldDB, yet their corresponding structures remain uncharacterized. This not only expands the pool of potential novel folds, as many remain unidentified, but also presents an opportunity to infer functional annotations for these previously uncharacterized structures.

Functional annotation through structural modeling

Structural annotations were performed based on structural homolog hits in CATH, PDB, and AlphaFoldDB. Out of the 26 767 high-quality structural models, 10 493 were derived from habitat-specific families, including 2668 from aquatic marine environments, 5290 from aquatic freshwater environments, 217 from other aquatic habitats, 22 from engineered environments, 8 from host-plants, 323 from mammals, 15 from humans, 4 from other host-associated environments, and 1946 from terrestrial environments. Out of 47 823 medium-quality structural models, 21 460 were derived from habitat-specific families. This includes 6088 from marine environments, 10 029 from freshwater environments, 369 from other aquatic environments, 82 from engineered environments, 131 from host plants, 318 from mammals, 48 from humans, 23 from other host-associated environments, and 4372 specifics to terrestrial habitats.

For each family, the predicted fold was compared with the structural hits from these databases and combined with the gene neighborhood analysis. The F139409 family for example, which is specific to the human habitat, exhibits structural similarity to the winged helix DNA-binding domain (CATH: 1.10.10.10). Gene neighborhood analysis indicates that its neighboring genes predominantly encode helix–turn–helix motifs (PF01381), ERF superfamily domains (PF04404), and single-nucleotide strand DNA binding protein domains (PF00436), all of which are known for their roles in DNA binding. The helix–turn–helix domain is a well-characterized structural motif involved in DNA recognition, while the ERF superfamily includes single-strand annealing proteins. The presence of exclusively DNA-binding proteins in this gene neighborhood suggests a strong functional connection to DNA interactions (Supplementary Fig. S9A). Similarly, the F004558 family, which consists of proteins found exclusively in terrestrial environments, exhibits high structural similarity (as indicated by TM scores) to the CRAL-TRIO lipid-binding domain (CATH: 3.40.525.10). The CRAL-TRIO domain is primarily associated with lipid transport and the binding of small lipophilic molecules. Gene neighborhood analysis of the F004558 family reveals a strong correlation with membrane transport functions, with neighboring genes encoding ion channels (PF07885), sodium/calcium exchangers (PF01699), and transmembrane transport proteins (GO:0 055 085). Furthermore, this family is closely related to the SLOG superfamily (PF18306), which includes proteins that bind low-molecular-weight biomolecules. Notably, SLOG proteins and CRAL-TRIO domains share functional similarities as small-molecule-binding domains. This relationship extends to lipid transfer and membrane interactions, particularly in the context of membrane trafficking [36], further reinforcing their functional link in cellular transport mechanisms (Supplementary Fig. S9B).

Additionally, to focus on high-quality models, the 50 most frequent structural hits in each environment were selected, and the unique domains identified in each environment were reported (Supplementary Table S8). This analysis, along with the gene neighborhood analysis, does not pinpoint the uniquely structured domains for each habitat, but rather highlights the unique domains among the most frequently encountered ones. Interestingly, the most frequent and characteristic gene neighborhood functions presented in Supplementary Tables S2–S4 are closely related to the habitat-specific unique structural domains. For instance, the carboxypeptidase-like regulatory domain (CATH: 2.60.40.1120) is found in families specific to the terrestrial habitat, and this is further supported by neighborhood analysis, which identifies it as one of the most frequently associated functions (Pfam domain: PF13620). Another example is the STAS domain, which is strongly associated with regulation and signal transduction in bacterial systems [52]. This domain appears at the top of the list in terrestrial-specific families (CATH: 3.30.750.24). At the same time, the gene neighborhood analysis reveals that the top Pfam domains are related to bacterial regulatory proteins (Pfam: PF00196). Considering all of the above, structural homology not only gives insights into annotating novel protein families but also underscores the significance of gene neighborhood analysis, which plays a crucial role in characterizing novel or unannotated folds.

Discussion

The microbial world contains vast, largely unexplored protein diversity that underpins microbiome function and ecological interactions. By analyzing tens of billions of sequences from metagenomes, metatranscriptomes, and reference genomes, we substantially expand protein family space, identifying over 600 000 families with ≥100 members and 6.5 million families with ≥25 members, effectively quadrupling current coverage. Importantly, smaller and niche families capture rare proteins with potential functional and evolutionary significance. Our scalable framework integrates structural prediction with taxonomic, ecological, and genomic context, revealing biome-specific patterns of adaptation and uncovering both novel folds and known folds within previously uncharacterized sequence space. Beyond novel folds, we identify protein families that adopt established structural architectures but lack detectable sequence similarity to known domains.

An insightful example (protein family F431767) exemplifies this structure-guided functional inference (Fig. 4). Although no similarity to Pfam domains or isolated genomes is detected, members of this family exhibit clear structural similarity to penicillin V acylase (PVA) chain A (CATH: 3.60.60.10; PDB: 3PVA) (Fig. 4A). PVA catalyzes the hydrolysis of penicillin V to 6-aminopenicillanic acid (6-APA), a key intermediate in the semi-synthesis of penicillin products in industry [53, 54]. F431767 proteins are distributed primarily across marine and terrestrial environments and occur mainly within Pseudomonadota, Actinomycetota, and Chloroflexota (Fig. 4C and D). The presence of this family in Chloroflexota [55], an ecologically and metabolically versatile phylum, highlights its potential functional breadth and biotechnological relevance. Sequence comparisons with structurally characterized PVAs, guided by the penicillin V-bound structure of 2Z71, reveal conservation of catalytic features (Fig. 4E), including the N-terminal nucleophile Cys12 generated by autocatalytic self-cleavage, consistent with the N-terminal nucleophile (Ntn) hydrolase superfamily [56]. Additional conserved residues (Asp30 and Asn155) contribute to substrate binding, while substitution of Tyr80 with His80 in F431767 members suggests adaptive tuning, potentially enhancing electrostatic flexibility and stability in environmentally diverse or extreme habitats. Molecular docking confirms the penicillin V model potential binding (Fig. 4B).

(B) Molecular docking by manual fitting of penicillin V in the F431767 structural model. Key residues conserved between penicillin V acylase and F431767 are highlighted. (C) KRONA visualization showing the taxonomic distribution of proteins belonging to the F431767 family. Chloroflexota includes aerobic thermophiles that utilize oxygen and thrive at high temperatures. Key residues such as Cys12, the catalytic N-terminal nucleophile, are highly conserved, while Asp30 is also conserved and contributes to the penicillin V binding site.

Recent studies have leveraged metagenomic datasets to expand and characterize the global landscape of microbial protein diversity, focusing on taxonomic distribution, functional potential, and the discovery of novel sequence space (Supplementary Tables S9–S13). Comparisons of our dataset with MAGs from IMG/M and gcMeta [57] revealed only limited overlap (Supplementary Tables S14 and S15). Although SPIRE [58] substantially expanded the known protein space by organizing metagenomic proteins across taxa and biomes, it showed no overlap with the proteins identified in our study. These findings demonstrate that our identified proteins represent novel sequence space not only relative to reference genomes but also relative to previous metagenomic efforts aimed at characterizing uncultivated microbial communities.

The Global Microbial Gene Catalogue (GMGC) [59] organized metagenomic datasets into protein families to investigate the distribution of genes across diverse habitats. Comparison with GMGC revealed limited overlap with our dataset, and none of the overlapping proteins received functional annotations. These findings highlight the significance of our study, which extends beyond the identification of novel protein families by providing a multilayered annotation framework to characterize their potential functions, taxonomic associations, and biological relevance.

Previous studies investigating novel protein family space, including FesNOV, NMPFam v1, and AGNOSTOS, employed distinct methodological approaches (Supplementary Table S16) and showed limited overlap with our dataset (Supplementary File S3 and Supplementary Table S17), highlighting the complementary nature of these efforts and the expansion of unexplored protein diversity.

Overall, this study shows that integrating global metagenomic clustering with structural and contextual annotation converts billions of uncharacterized sequences into a biome-resolved protein family landscape, providing new insights into microbial adaptation and revealing a broad reservoir of functional and biotechnological potential.

Limitations

All data used in this analysis were retrieved from IMG/M, where gene prediction is performed using established tools, including Prodigal v2.6.3 and GeneMarkS-2. Most eukaryotic gene prediction methods either rely on similarity to known proteins (e.g. MetaEuk) or require prior taxonomic classification to optimize ab initio models (e.g. AUGUSTUS). Consequently, prokaryotic gene callers are often the only practical option. However, this introduces a potential bias, as genes of eukaryotic origin may be missed or inaccurately predicted. Therefore, the quality and completeness of the predicted protein set are inherently dependent on the underlying gene-calling strategy. Protein sequence fragmentation is a major challenge in metagenomic studies. Despite the implementation of filtering strategies to reduce its impact, workflows for novel protein identification can still be biased toward short protein families. While the present computational framework provides a valuable resource for the discovery, annotation, and characterization of novel proteins, all functional and biological inferences remain computational predictions and should be considered hypotheses requiring experimental validation. While our 35-amino-acid threshold aligns with Pfam standards for functional domains, our strict novelty criteria naturally enriched the dataset for smaller sequences. Omitting coverage parameters during Pfam filtering intentionally excluded multidomain proteins to eliminate false positives, while high bidirectional coverage thresholds against larger reference proteins further constrained sequence length. Although these conservative filters may omit longer sequences containing isolated novel domains, they ensure that the retained candidates represent genuinely novel proteins, leaving multidomain protein discovery as a promising direction for future research.
