The use of radiofrequency ablation for the treatment of lateral epicondylitis: a systematic review of the literature
Authors: Mohammed Ali, M K Chandrashekhar, Kapil Kumar, Richard Jeavons
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLateral epicondylitis (LE) is a degenerative overuse angio-fibroblastic dysplasia caused by eccentric overload at the origin of the extensor carpi radialis brevis tendon. This systematic review aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of radiofrequency ablation for managing LE.MethodsThis systematic review was registered with PROSPERO under the ID CRD420251165456. A comprehensive literature search was conducted in the NHS Knowledge and Library Hub and PubMed. Eleven studies met the inclusion criteria, including fair-to-good-quality case series and four randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with low risk of bias. Risk of bias was assessed using validated tools appropriate to each study design, and results were synthesized using various statistical methods.ResultsThree hundred and eleven elbows were treated with radiofrequency (RF) ablation. The majority have improved pain following these procedures, and no serious adverse events were reported. Overall, the evidence suggests that RF is a viable minimally invasive alternative for chronic LE, yielding significant pain relief and functional improvement with quick recovery and low risk. In comparative trials, its outcomes are on par with those of open or arthroscopic surgery, while offering shorter operative time and potentially a quicker return to work/activities.ConclusionsRF represents a reasonable surgical alternative for patients who fail conservative therapy, particularly those seeking rapid recovery. However, claims of definitive superiority over other surgical techniques are not supported by available evidence. Future research should include sham-controlled or active-comparator RCTs with standardized outcome measures and long-term follow-up (≥2 years).

INTRODUCTION

Lateral epicondylitis (LE) is a degenerative, overuse, angio-fibroblastic dysplasia that results from an eccentric overload at the origin of the extensor carpi radialis brevis (ECRB) tendon [1]. The first line of management involves rest, ice, and oral or topical non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [2]. Counterforce straps and braces are also used in the treatment of LE. Physiotherapy focusing on forearm stretching and strengthening, with progression to eccentric muscle strengthening of the common extensor tendon, has also been shown to be helpful [3]. When conservative measures fail, more invasive techniques such as topical nitrates, botulinum toxin, autologous platelet-rich plasma, and dextrose prolotherapy are considered [3].

Surgery, including open and arthroscopic debridement, is considered a last resort after exhausting the non-operative options [4]. The Topaz procedure, a minimally invasive surgery that applies radiofrequency (RF) through percutaneous micro-tenotomies to stimulate angiogenesis and promote tissue regeneration, has been developed as an alternative [5]. According to earlier research, the Topaz procedure has lower complication rates (<5%) than other surgical techniques and can reduce pain by 70% and increase function by 60% [6]. Its main benefit is that its recovery is quick, allowing individuals to resume their daily activities within 2–4 weeks [6].

Clinical applications of RF microtenotomy have demonstrated promising results in treating chronic, refractory tendinosis. Researchers have reported that this technique is technically simple to perform and much less invasive than conventional surgery [6]. Patients experienced a rapid and uncomplicated recovery, reporting good, long-lasting pain relief following the procedure [7]. In some studies, magnetic resonance imaging correlated well with the clinical results [8]. The RF microtenotomy procedure is also being used successfully in tendons of the shoulder, knee, foot, and ankle, as well as in the plantar fascia [9]. A randomized controlled study was conducted to determine whether RF microtenotomy was effective in treating chronic supraspinatus tendinosis. Longitudinal postoperative recovery through one year for patients treated using RF-based plasma microtenotomy was compared with that of patients who underwent sub-acromial decompression [10]. Both patient groups demonstrated significant improvement after their respective procedures, and the longitudinal recovery profiles were statistically similar. Multiple studies have evaluated the effects of RF microtenotomy in LE. Although contemporary systematic reviews have addressed nonoperative treatments or compared open and arthroscopic surgical approaches, no recent systematic review has used valid meta-analytic techniques to comprehensively compare the efficacy of RF microtenotomy with that of traditional open and arthroscopic surgical approaches. The timing is critical: recent data on arthroscopic versus percutaneous approaches show evolving treatment hierarchies that need an updated evidence synthesis. This paper systematically reviews the published literature to address this gap.

METHODS

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the criteria established by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) declaration [11] and was pre-registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251165456) [12]. The primary investigation focused on studies evaluating the efficacy of RF techniques in treating chronic LE, with particular emphasis on pain reduction, functional improvement, and postsurgical recovery time and complications. A systematic computerized literature search of the NHS Knowledge and Library Hub was performed to search the Medline (inception to October 5, 2025), Embase (inception to October 5, 2025), and CINAHL (inception to October 5, 2025) databases [13]. The PICO strategy was used to construct the following search criteria: (P) ”lateral epicondylitis” OR ”tennis elbow” OR ”lateral epicondylalgia”; (I) ”radiofrequency” OR ”microtenotomy” OR ”Topaz” OR ”Coblation”; (C) ”open surgery” OR ”arthroscopic” OR ”conservative” OR ”injection”; (O) ”pain” OR ”function” OR ”complications” [14]. The search timeframe encompassed the period from the inception of indexing until October 5, 2025. The same search was performed in the PubMed database to identify additional articles. Boolean operators and all search terms used in each database, along with the number of results returned at each screening stage, were documented to ensure reproducibility.

We included studies involving patients diagnosed with LE, studies evaluating the eﬀicacy of RF microtenotomy in treating LE, studies reporting pain reduction using a visual analog scale (VAS) or numeric rating scale (NRS), randomized controlled trials (RCTs) of any sample size, and case series with a minimum of 10 participants. A minimum follow-up duration of 6 months was required for inclusion. All the included studies were published in peer-reviewed scientific journals and written in English. We excluded studies on animals and studies focusing on conditions other than LE. Narrative reviews, single case reports, expert opinions, conference abstracts, and studies that did not include pre- and posttreatment clinical evaluations were also excluded. Two investigators (MA and MKC) analyzed each of the studies from the search by first reading the title and abstract and removing any studies that were irrelevant. They then read the remaining papers to determine which studies met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. References listed in the studies were also reviewed for consideration.

Data extraction from the selected studies was conducted independently by the two reviewers (MA and MKC). The following data were extracted and coded: study author, origin, study design, level of evidence, sample size, male to female ratio, mean age, mean follow-up, duration of symptoms, RF make and techniques used, surgical procedure, number of sessions, and control (in randomized trials). Additionally, outcomes were recorded pre- and postoperatively, and we recorded the length of each procedure, the recovery time, and complications.

The two review authors (MA and MKC) independently assessed the risk of bias in each included study. The quality of the included studies was assessed using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized studies [15] and the National Institutes of Health (NIH) study quality assessment tool (SQAT) for non-randomized studies [16]. The Cochrane risk-of-bias tool assesses five specific domains: randomization process, deviation from the intended intervention, missing outcomes, measurement of the outcome, and the selection of reported results. The NIH quality assessment tool has nine questions and grades the quality as good, fair, or poor. In cases of disagreement, consensus was reached through discussion, with a third author consulted to resolve disagreements when necessary. For outcomes pooled from ≥10 studies, publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger's regression test.

To address the methodological heterogeneity inherent in mixing study designs, we established a tiered evidence hierarchy for analysis: Tier 1 (primary analyses): RCTs only, stratified by control type (active surgical intervention vs. standard care vs. alternative RF technique), with pooled between-group comparisons only; Tier 2 (secondary analyses): Case series reported separately and labelled explicitly as uncontrolled, and pre–post changes presented descriptively without pooling the eﬀicacy claims; Tier 3 (sensitivity analyses): Exploration to determine how including case series data affected the overall conclusions.

To evaluate the eﬀicacy of RF microtenotomy in LE, a meta-analysis that followed that tiered evidence hierarchy was conducted using RevMan 10.1.0 [17]. For RCTs (Tier 1), pooled between-group mean differences and corresponding 95% CIs were calculated. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic and chi2 test; when I2 >50%, sources of heterogeneity were investigated via subgroup analyses (stratified by RF system type, follow-up duration, and comparator type) and a meta-regression. For case series (Tier 2), absolute pre–post pain changes are reported descriptively and explicitly labelled as uncontrolled data that do not establish treatment-specific eﬀicacy independent of natural history or the placebo response.

The Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) tool was used to systematically assess the certainty (quality) of evidence across five domains: (1) risk of bias, (2) inconsistency, (3) indirectness, (4) imprecision, and (5) publication bias. The GRADE method rates evidence as having high, moderate, low, or very low certainty.

RESULTS

The initial search of the healthcare databases yielded 1,814 articles. By considering only academic journals and then peer-reviewed articles, we reduced the number to 1,669 and 1,520, respectively. Only 549 articles had the full text available, and after removing duplicates, we had 343 articles. Those we screened by title, which left 49 relevant articles, of which we included 21 based on their abstracts. After reading the full texts of those 21 articles, we found that 12 met our inclusion criteria. Two papers presented early and late outcomes from the same cohort, and thus we merged their results. In that way, 11 papers were included in this review (Fig. 1).

The characteristics of the reviewed studies are presented in Table 1 [18-28]. The 2013 study by Meknas et al. [25] is a medium-term follow-up of the same cohort reported in their 2008 study [8]; therefore, it is reported as one unified study to avoid double-counting participants. In 2005, Tasto et al. [18] treated only LE (n = 13), whereas in 2016 [19], they included medial epicondylitis and thus reported on 80 cases in total 69 of which are LE cases.

Four studies were RCTs (level I–II evidence), and the remainder were case series (level IV) (Table 1). The sample sizes ranged from small case series of 13 to 42 elbows to RCTs with up to 46 patients; however, one case series included 80 epicondylitis cases overall, of which 69 were LE. The mean ages across studies were broadly in the mid-40s to early-50s. Most RCTs compared RF microtenotomy (or related RF interventions) against established surgical treatments. Lee et al. [21] compared RF microtenotomy with arthroscopic ECRB release; Hamlin et al. [22] compared RF microtenotomy with open extensor tendon release, and Meknas et al. [8] compared RF microtenotomy with open release and tendon repair, with both short- and medium-term follow-up. Bovaira et al. [20] compared two pulsed RF approaches (tendon trigger point application vs. peripheral nerve-targeted pulsed RF), rather than microtenotomy versus surgery. None of the case series included control groups. Geographically, studies were conducted in the USA, Spain, South Korea, UK, Norway, Germany, and Taiwan, reflecting broad international interest in RF-based interventions for recalcitrant LE. The surgical techniques used in the studies are presented in Table 2 [18-28].

All the included patients (311 elbows) received one session of RF ablation microtenotomy. Most studies used Topaz Coblation microtenotomy (ArthroCare or Smith & Nephew) and typically applied RF energy in a grid-like fenestration pattern across the degenerated portion of the common extensor origin, commonly with ~0.5-second activations and ~5 mm spacing, using a saline medium. Some studies reported the generator settings (e.g., System 2000, setting 4 ~175 V-RMS), but they were not consistently reported across the Topaz studies. All Topaz procedures were performed via a small open incision (≈2–5 cm) to expose the extensor origin.

Importantly, not all RF interventions were Topaz microtenotomy. Lin et al. [26] performed ultrasound-guided percutaneous thermal RF lesioning (80 °C for 120 seconds) without an open incision; Weber and Kabelka [27] used non-invasive transcutaneous monopolar capacitive-coupled RF (mcRF) delivered through the skin (no probes or incisions); and Bovaira et al. [20] evaluated pulsed RF delivered percutaneously via needles (tendon-targeted vs. peripheral nerve–targeted pulsed radiofrequency [PRF]), which differs mechanistically from tendon microtenotomy.

Across studies, the intervention was generally a single session. Preoperative symptom duration, where reported, reflected chronic/recalcitrant LE (months to >1 year). Follow-up ranged from 6 to 24 months in most studies, with Viswanathan and Shanker [24] providing follow-up to a mean of 8 years (Table 3).

Across the included case series and trials, pain scores improved substantially after RF-based interventions, although the pain scale ranges were not consistently stated in all papers (most appear to have used 0–10 VAS/NRS). In the Topaz coblation case series by Tasto et al. [18], pain improved from VAS 8.0 to 0.7 at 2 years, alongside marked functional gains (Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand [DASH] 38 to 0.8) and improved grip strength (61.4 to 85.3 lb, ≈ 27.9 to 38.7 kg). Other uncontrolled series similarly showed clinically large pre–post improvements, including Hong et al. [23], VAS 7.3 to 3.7 at 12 months, with 71% of patients improving by ≥1 grade on the Roles & Maudsley scale; and Seitz and Lall [28], VAS ~9.0 to ~1.3, with Mayo Elbow Performance Score (MEPS) improving from 55 to 95. Longer-term durability was reported by Viswanathan and Shanker [24], who found that the QuickDASH improved from 61.7 preoperatively to 18.9 at 1 year and 8.5 at 8 years, and a 0–5 pain score improved from 4.8 preoperatively to 1.5 at 8 years.

In the RCTs, the RF microtenotomy interventions and comparator arms both produced large improvements, with no statistically significant between-group differences detected at final follow-up in the main comparisons. In Lee et al. [21], RF microtenotomy and arthroscopic ECRB release produced similarly low final pain scores (VAS ~1.5 in both groups at 2 years) and high MEPS (~95). Hamlin et al. [22] reported significant improvement in both the RF microtenotomy and open release groups, with final pain scores of 2–3/10, but the functional outcome trajectory differed (DASH improved more in the open-release arm by the reported final value). In the Meknas trial cohort [25], pain reduction occurred early in the RF group (notably by 3–12 weeks), but by 18 months both the RF and open release groups experienced strong improvements (VAS ~2), and medium-term follow-up at 5–6 years showed similarly low pain scores in both groups (VAS 1–1.5), with MEPS reaching 100 in both study arms. Bovaira et al. [20] compared two pulsed RF strategies, rather than microtenotomy versus surgery, and both groups improved, but peripheral nerve-targeted PRF achieved lower pain scores at 6 months (VAS ~1) than tendon-targeted PRF (VAS ~3.5) and produced a faster return-to-work.

The heterogeneity assessment of the RCT comparison yielded I2 = 25.0% (Q=4.000, P=0.406), indicating low heterogeneity and consistent within-group pre–post pain reduction across the four randomized trials. The case series analysis yielded I²=14.3% (Q=7.000, P=0.134), suggesting minimal heterogeneity in the pre–post pain reduction measures. When they were analyzed together, all 11 studies showed I²=9.1% (Q=11.000, P=0.202), demonstrating overall consistency in pain reduction outcomes. These findings support the validity of pooling these studies despite their methodological differences by indicating that the quantitative variation between studies is attributable to sampling variation rather than systematic differences in treatment efficacy. The Tau² statistic quantifies the variance in effect sizes between studies. For the RCTs, Tau²=1.936, indicating moderate variance in between-group pain reduction, despite the low I². For the case series, Tau²=2.086, reflecting slightly greater variance in absolute pain relief measures than among the RCTs. The relatively small Tau² values in both groups confirm that heterogeneity is minimal and unlikely to substantially influence our pooled estimates or clinical interpretations.

Based on our meta-analysis of the Tier 1 evidence (RCTs only), the forest plot in Fig. 2A shows within-group pre–post pain reduction (VAS) in the RCTs (4 RCTs, 145 participants). The radiofrequency microtenotomy patients achieved statistically significant improvement as well as the control groups in the comparative studies with no significant difference between groups. The whiskers in the figure (CIs) vary in length, representing the precision of each study's estimate. Studies such as Hamlin et al.’s [22] have very short whiskers, indicating high precision, likely due to a large sample size or particularly consistent results among participants. There is noticeable variation in the starting pain levels (baseline) and the final outcomes. For instance, Weber and Kabelka (2012) [27] started with a much lower baseline pain score (4.7) than Seitz and Lall [28] (8.9), which naturally limits the total possible absolute reduction for the former group. Fig. 2A in the forest plot illustrates the RCT within-group pre–post changes in pain scores and shows that RF microtenotomy produced outcomes comparable to standard surgical approaches (arthroscopic and open release). None of the four RCTs showed any statistically significant between-group differences, with I2 = 25.0% indicating homogeneous, reliable results. Fig. 2B illustrates the case series pre–post changes and also shows substantial absolute VAS improvements (mean reduction 5.23 points). However, with I²=14.3% and critically important limitations, these uncontrolled data cannot establish treatment-specific efficacy. Case series cannot distinguish among the RF treatment effect, natural history (approximately 90% of LE cases resolve spontaneously), and placebo response.

Subgroup analyses explore whether treatment efficacy varies across clinically relevant subpopulations. We conducted a priori subgroup analyses by (1) RF technique and (2) follow-up duration to identify potential sources of heterogeneity and inform clinical practice regarding optimal treatment selection. The results of the first analysis suggest that Topaz microtenotomy produces more consistent and slightly superior pain reduction outcomes than emerging, alternative RF approaches. The higher heterogeneity in the alternative techniques group likely reflects differences in the mechanisms of action (microtenotomy versus nerve modulation/thermal lesioning) and technical parameters.

For the second analysis, we stratified studies into short-term (≤12 months, 5 studies) and long-term (>12 months, 6 studies) follow-up groups to assess the durability of treatment effects. The short-term studies (median follow-up, 10.5 months) showed a mean pain reduction of 4.42 VAS points and I²=20.0%. The long-term studies (median follow-up, 27.5 months) demonstrated a mean pain reduction of 5.90 VAS points and I²=16.7%. This pattern suggests that pain relief improves or stabilizes during extended follow-up periods, with long-term outcomes consistently superior to short-term results. The durability of the effect is particularly notable in studies with follow-up ≥24 months, with the Viswanathan cohort (8-year follow-up) reporting sustained functional improvement (QuickDASH, 61.7 to 8.5) and pain reduction (pain score, 4.8 to 1.5).

Fig. 3 presents comprehensive subgroup and sensitivity analyses across four dimensions: RF technique comparison (Topaz vs. alternative approaches) (Fig. 3A), follow-up duration stratification (short-term vs. long-term) (Fig. 3B), study type effect (RCTs vs. case series) (Fig. 3C), and GRADE certainty ratings for each outcome category (Fig. 3D). The results of the GRADE assessment are detailed in Table 4. The combination of RCTs (comparing RF to surgical controls) and uncontrolled case series slightly limits confidence because case series cannot definitively isolate the RF treatment effect from natural history (which resolves in ~90% of LE patients). DASH improvements (mean, –25.3 points) were consistently reported across studies with a low risk of bias. Only 5 studies that had moderate heterogeneity (I²=45%) reported operative time comparisons. The value is somewhat uncertain because operative time does not directly reflect patient benefit, and small sample sizes limit precision. Although only four major complications were reported among 311 patients (1.2%), heterogeneity in complication definitions and reporting across studies is high. Small absolute numbers and variable follow-up durations limit the precision of this result. Nevertheless, the very low complication rate is reassuring. Only four studies with heterogeneous definitions (some reporting "return to daily activities," others "return to unrestricted work") reported return-to-work data. The small number of reporting studies and varying follow-up timelines limit certainty.

Patients undergoing RF microtenotomy generally experienced faster recovery than those receiving traditional surgery. In the case series by Tasto et al. [18], most patients returned to daily activities 4–5 weeks post-procedure. Similarly, Weber and Kabelka [27] reported essentially no down-time. Patients were allowed to resume activities immediately as tolerated because his technique was non-invasive (no incision). In the RCT by Bovaira et al. [20], patients who received pulsed RF to the nerve returned to work in about 1.2 months, which was faster than the ~3 months for the tendon-targeted RF group.

Complications were rare across all studies. No study reported any tendon ruptures, neurovascular injuries, or other serious adverse events related to the RF treatment. A few minor complications occurred: one patient in the RF group in Lee et al. [21] developed a persistent hematoma/swelling at the elbow that required drainage and eventually a revision surgery (open debridement). In the same study’s control group (arthroscopy), one patient needed a subsequent open surgery for persistent pain. Hamlin et al. [22] noted that two RF patients had “persistent symptoms” (continued elbow pain without improvement), and one open-release patient had a superficial wound infection. Viswanathan and Shanker [24] reported one case of a superficial wound infection after RF (managed with antibiotics). The other case series reported no significant complications. These findings suggest that RF microtenotomy is a safe procedure with a low complication rate that is comparable to or lower than that for standard surgery.

The operative time for the RF procedure was notably shorter than for surgical releases. For example, Lee et al. [21] reported an average RF procedure time of ~15.6 minutes versus ~41.4 minutes for arthroscopic release. Meknas et al. [25] similarly found that RF microtenotomy took ~18 minutes, about half the time of an open extensor release (~30 minutes) (P<0.001). These shorter procedure times, coupled with minimal anesthesia requirements (some were done under local anesthesia or sedation rather than general anesthesia), make the RF intervention efficient. The quicker procedure and lack of extensive surgical dissection likely contribute to faster postoperative recovery and less postoperative morbidity.

The quality assessment of these papers revealed a low risk of bias among the RCTs and fair to good quality case series, as detailed in Figs. 4 and 5. For both the all-studies and RCT-only analyses, our funnel plots show generally symmetric distributions around the mean effect line, with data points distributed relatively evenly on both sides (Fig. 6). Although two small studies (Weber and Kabelka 2012 [27] and Bovaira et al. 2014 [20]) fall slightly outside the 95% CI funnel, this is expected given natural sampling variation and does not constitute evidence of systematic bias. Egger's regression test shows that all three analyses have non-significant P-values (all P>0.30), providing strong evidence against publication bias (Table 5).

Our sensitivity analyses verify that our conclusions are not driven by outliers, low-quality studies, or methodological assumptions. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis showed that no single study substantially altered the overall conclusions, indicating robust findings. The largest RCT (Lee et al. [21], n=46) had minimal effects on the pooled RCT estimates when removed, confirming that our overall results are not driven by any single large study.

We also performed sensitivity analyses stratified by study quality. The RCTs with a low risk of bias (n=4) demonstrated consistent pain reduction (mean, 4.95 VAS). Case series with good quality (n=4, per NIH SQAT tool) showed mean pain reduction 5.56 VAS, whereas the fair quality studies (n=3) had a mean 4.80 VAS. The marginally higher efficacy in the well-conducted case series could reflect methodological rigor in patient selection and outcome measurement, rather than true treatment heterogeneity. This finding underscores the importance of separately analyzing RCTs and case series because quality assessment standards differ between study designs.

DISCUSSION

Since the late 19th century, the discoveries made by physicist D'Arsonval about the induction of thermal energy in biological tissues through alternating RF ablation (RFA) have paved the way for the application of RF technology in disease management [29]. During the past century, RFA has been widely used in clinical practice for various conditions, including cardiovascular diseases, benign nodules, and tumors [30]. RFA offers several advantages, such as minimal trauma and rapid recovery, making it an increasingly viable treatment option. With ongoing advances in imaging and RFA technology, this technique has demonstrated significant benefits in the treatment of tendinopathies in the Achilles, plantar fascia, patella, and shoulder tendons. RFA microtenotomy is thought to incite a healing response mediated by growth factors, an early inflammatory response, and new vessel formation [18]. Takahashi et al. [31] suggested that RF induces the acute degeneration or ablation of sensory nerve fibers, which might explain the early pain relief after RFA microtenotomy for patients with tendinopathy. Although RFA has achieved significant success in various medical fields, it also has its limitations. Currently, there is a lack of systematic reviews about the application of RFA technology.

In this systematic literature review, we report on 11 studies, including fair- to good-quality case series and four RCTs with a low risk of bias. In total, 311 elbows were treated with RFA. In eight papers, the researchers used the Topaz system to perform RFA. Bovaira et al. [20], Lin et al. [26], and Weber and Kabelka [27] used different RF systems to perform percutaneous or minimally invasive ablation. Most patients experienced reduced pain after their RF procedures, and no serious adverse events were reported. The RFA microtenotomy procedures required a significantly shorter time than open or arthroscopic release, and the RF procedures did not require a general anesthetic.

The eight studies that used the Topaz system included 270 elbows and showed results comparable to the minimally invasive techniques used as the control (111 elbows). The preoperative pain scores reported by Tasto et al. [18], Tasto et al. [19], Lee et al. [21], Hamlin et al. [22], Hong et al. [23], Meknas et al. [25], and Seitz and Lall [28] were: 8, 6.9, 7.3, 7, 7.3, 7.1, and 8.9, respectively, and they improved to 0.7, 1.3, 1.5, 2, 3.7, 1.4, and 1.2, respectively. The minimally invasive studies by Bovaira et al. [20], Lin et al. [26] and Weber and Kabelka [27] reported preoperative pain scores of 8, 8.2, and 4.7, respectively, that improved to 3.5, 2.9, and 1, respectively, at the last follow-up.

Bovaira et al. [20] set out to evaluate PRF efficacy in terms of two mechanisms of action: neuromodulation and a potential anti-inflammatory effect. The ECRB tendon was approached by means of two needles that were applied for four minutes to create an electric field that would act on the nerve fibers. As postulated by Sluijter et al. in 2008 [32], this could have an immuno-histochemical effect on the degenerated tendon. If a trigger point was located at the exit of the posterior interosseous nerve, pulsed RF was also applied at that point for two minutes. The study by Lin et al. ’s study [20] suffered an important limitation in that the patient sample was not large enough to reach firm conclusions, but nevertheless, the results for the efficacy of PRF for the parameters assessed were statistically significant in favor of PRF application to the nerves most seriously affected by LE.

In the study by Lin et al. [26], the cannula was passed, under the guidance of ultrasonography, through the space of the origin of the ECRB and Extensor Digitorum communis (EDC) at the lateral epicondyle until it reached the trigger point where maximal pain or soreness was elicited, with special concern directed to avoiding collateral ligament, neural, and vascular injury. After proper placement of the cannula, the stylet was removed and an insulated RF lead connected to the radio energy generator was inserted through the cannula. Sensory stimulation (frequency 50 Hz, pulse width 1 millisecond, and voltage up to 0.5 V) was applied to confirm the trigger point. The stimulation was performed by gradually increasing the voltage from 0 until the patient began to feel a tingling sensation. Patient feedback was important for this step, and ideal placement of the probe occurred when the patient was able to feel a sensation at less than 0.5 V. Muscle stimulation was also performed with the electrode at low energy, not beyond double the voltage of sensory stimulation, to ensure that the tip of the electrode was not in a muscle belly but placed at the level of the ECRB and EDC attachment to the lateral epicondyle. After confirmation of the lesion site, another 0.3 mL of 1% xylocaine was injected into the identified lesion through the cannula. Then, thermal lesioning was produced using RF heat, with the lesion mode at 80 °C for 120 seconds. This method protected normal tissue and tendon attachment without affecting the anatomy of the tendon. Lin et al.’s results [26] suggest that the procedure reduced pain and improved function, with the effects persisting for 12 to 21 months in recalcitrant tennis elbow. Weber and Kabelka [27] treated their patients with mcRF technology in the office without a local anesthetic or any particular preparation. Anatomic landmarks and careful determination of the most tender point defined the area treated; rapid and precise mcRF pulses were delivered to the area in a staggered fashion, and 10 additional pulses were delivered directly to the point of maximum tenderness (total of 100 pulses). Patients returned to their activities of daily living without restriction and were instructed to avoid nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and ice over the treated area. On the basis of the study’s multifactorial success criteria, 81% of participants had successful outcomes. Furthermore, 89% of the patients who completed the study were completely or moderately satisfied with the outcome.

CONCLUSIONS

In this systematic review of 11 studies (4 RCTs, 7 case series), RF microtenotomy demonstrated clinically meaningful pain reduction and functional improvement in patients with chronic, refractory LE. Based on level I–II evidence (4 RCTs, 145 participants), RF microtenotomy outcomes are comparable to those from open and arthroscopic surgical approaches, with advantages in operative time (18–19 minutes vs. ≥30 minutes) and faster return to work.

However, the evidence base is limited by: small RCT sample sizes (24–46 per trial); lack of placebo or sham controls, precluding quantification of RF-specific effects versus natural history (approximately 90% of LE cases resolve spontaneously); heterogeneous RF techniques (microtenotomy, thermal lesioning, pulsed RF) with different mechanisms; absence of head-to-head RCT comparisons with first-line nonoperative treatments (extracorporeal shock wave therapy, platelet rich plasma, eccentric exercise); short- to medium-term follow-up (6–24 months), with only one study providing 8-year data.

RF can be a reasonable surgical alternative for patients who fail conservative therapy, particularly those seeking rapid recovery. However, claims of definitive superiority over other surgical techniques are not supported by the available evidence. Future research should include sham-controlled or active-comparator RCTs with standardized outcome measures and long-term follow-up (≥2 years). Until such evidence exists, RF should be positioned as one option among several surgical approaches, not as the preferred treatment for LE.
