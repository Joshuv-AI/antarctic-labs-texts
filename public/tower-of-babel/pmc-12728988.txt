The impact of professional values on the quality of professional life among ICU nurses in China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Weiwei Ni, Shichao Zhu, Xiaomei Yu, Yulei Ma, Xinwei Jiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and purposeICU nurses in China experience substantial psychological stress due to understaffing, employment instability, and emotionally intensive care. While organizational factors have been studied, the role of intrinsic Nursing Occupational Values (NOV) in shaping Professional Quality of Life (ProQOL) is underexplored. This study examined associations between NOV and ProQOL among ICU nurses and tested potential mediating mechanisms.MethodsWe conducted a multi-site cross-sectional study in 12 hospitals in Henan Province (January–March 2022). A convenience sample of ICU nurses (n = 356) completed the Chinese Nurses’ Professional Values Scale (7 domains) and the Chinese ProQOL Scale (Compassion Satisfaction, Job Burnout, Secondary Trauma). Analyses included descriptive statistics, Pearson correlations, multiple linear regression adjusting for significant covariates (education, employment type, hospital level), and mediation models using bootstrapping (5,000 resamples; α = 0.05).ResultsNOV differed by education, professional title, employment type, income, hospital level, and marital status (all p < 0.05). Compassion Satisfaction was positively predicted by Leadership Attitude and Reward (β = 0.137, p = 0.037), Job Security (β = 0.086, p = 0.028), and Colleague Relationship (β = 0.284, p = 0.019). Job Burnout decreased with Intention of Career Choice (β = −0.164, p = 0.020) and Colleague Relationship (β = −0.308, p = 0.011) but increased with Nurse-Patient Relationship (β = 0.183, p = 0.040). Secondary Trauma increased with Nurse-Patient Relationship (β = 0.204, p = 0.044). Mediation analysis indicated that Job Security significantly mediated the effect of Leadership Attitude and Reward on Compassion Satisfaction (95% CI [0.01, 0.27]).ConclusionsIntrinsic professional values, especially Job Security, are associated with higher Compassion Satisfaction and lower Job Burnout among ICU nurses. Interventions that strengthen leadership support and employment stability may enhance nurse well-being and care quality in resource-constrained critical care settings.Keywords: Nursing occupational values, Professional quality of life, ICU nurses

Introduction

Intensive care nursing requires rapid decision-making, sustained emotional engagement, and continuous exposure to critically ill patients [1]. These conditions place nurses at considerable risk of diminished Professional Quality of Life, reflected in reduced Compassion Satisfaction and elevated levels of Burnout and Secondary Trauma [2]. In China, structural challenges such as chronic understaffing, employment instability, and emotionally demanding nurse–patient interactions further intensify these pressures [3]. Understanding how intrinsic professional values function in this context is therefore of critical importance [4, 5].

Nursing Occupational Values represent the beliefs and commitments that sustain motivation and resilience in clinical practice [6]. In Chinese intensive care units, values such as Job Security, Colleague Relationship, and Intention of Career Choice are particularly relevant [7, 8]. Stable employment fosters predictability and commitment [9]. Supportive colleagues provide essential socioemotional resources. A strong sense of calling reinforces professional purpose [10]. Previous studies have linked these values to job satisfaction among general nursing populations, yet their specific associations with the three dimensions of Professional Quality of Life in intensive care settings remain underexplored [11].

The Job Demands–Resources model offers a valuable theoretical framework for understanding these relationships [12]. It suggests that personal resources strengthen positive outcomes through a gain process, while job demands increase strain through a demand–strain process [13]. Building on this perspective, we propose that Nursing Occupational Values function as key personal resources that may enhance Compassion Satisfaction and mitigate adverse outcomes, while intensive care job demands such as emotionally intensive nurse–patient interactions may contribute to Burnout and Secondary Trauma [14, 15].

In this study, the Job Demands–Resources model provides the guiding framework. Nursing Occupational Values, particularly Job Security, Colleague Relationship, and Intention of Career Choice, are conceptualized as personal resources that reinforce motivation and resilience. By contrast, job demands in Chinese intensive care units include high patient acuity, persistent understaffing, unstable employment contracts, and emotionally challenging nurse, patient interactions. Guided by this framework, we examined two central processes: the gain process, in which Nursing Occupational Values enhance Compassion Satisfaction, and the demand, strain process, in which intensive care job demands contribute to Burnout and Secondary Trauma. The buffering effect, which assumes an interaction between resources and demands, was not tested in this cross-sectional study and is acknowledged as a limitation.

Aim

The aim of this study was to examine the impact of Nursing Occupational Values on the Professional Quality of Life of intensive care nurses in China.

Design and methods

Study design

This study adopted a cross-sectional survey design conducted between January and March 2022.

Participants and setting

Convenience sampling recruited ICU nurses from 12 hospitals in Henan Province, China, between January and March 2022. Inclusion criteria included: (1) registered nurses currently in ICU roles; (2) ≥ 6 months of ICU experience for environmental familiarity; (3) informed consent provided. Nurses on extended leave or in non-clinical roles were excluded. Sample size was determined using G*Power 3.1 [16], with power = 0.80, α = 0.05, and medium effect size (f ² = 0.15) for multiple regression, yielding a minimum of 316 participants; 384 were targeted.

Data collection

Questionnaires were administered via Wenjuanxing. Participation was entirely voluntary, and informed consent was obtained prior to data collection. Within the online system, all items were set as required fields to reduce missing data, and single-submission IP restrictions were applied to ensure data integrity. A pilot with 15 ICU nurses confirmed practicality. Of 384 distributed surveys, 356 were returned (response rate: 92.7%).

Demographic characteristics

A self-designed form collected gender, age, education, professional title, marital status, employment type, monthly income, and hospital level.

Nursing occupational values (NOV)

Nursing Occupational Values (NOV) were measured with the Chinese Nurses’ Professional Values Scale [17], a 38-item tool across seven domains: Intention of Career Choice (6 items), Salary Expectation (5 items), Leadership Attitude and Reward (8 items), Colleague Relationship (5 items), Nurse-Patient Relationship (4 items), Job Security (6 items), and Professional Technical Competence (4 items). Responses used a 5-point Likert scale (1 = never, 5 = always), with higher scores reflecting stronger values. Cronbach’s α was 0.961 overall, with subscales from 0.860 to 0.929; here, subscales ranged from 0.896 to 0.975.

Professional quality of life (ProQOL)

Professional Quality of Life (ProQOL) was assessed using the Chinese ProQOL Scale [4], a 30-item measure with three subscales: Compassion Satisfaction (10 items), Job Burnout (10 items), and Secondary Trauma (10 items). Items were scored on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = never, 5 = always), with five items reverse-scored. Scores were standardized to T-scores (T = 10Z + 50), grouped as low (< 43), moderate (43–57), or high (> 57). Original α values were 0.82 (Compassion Satisfaction), 0.73 (Job Burnout), and 0.76 (Secondary Trauma); in this study, they were 0.88, 0.86, and 0.85.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 and the PROCESS macro version 4.1. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic characteristics and scale scores. Independent-sample t-tests and one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc tests were conducted to examine differences in Nursing Occupational Values (NOV) across demographic groups. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated to assess the associations between NOV and dimensions of Professional Quality of Life (ProQOL). Multiple linear regression analyses were applied to evaluate the predictive effects of NOV on Compassion Satisfaction, Job Burnout, and Secondary Trauma, while adjusting for significant demographic covariates (p < 0.05). Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF). Variables with VIF values exceeding 5 were carefully reviewed, and sensitivity analysis was conducted to ensure robustness. Additionally, stepwise regression was applied to mitigate the effects of multicollinearity, allowing for the selection of the most relevant predictors while minimizing redundancy in the model, with additional verification by sensitivity analyses. Mediation effects were tested using PROCESS Models 4 and 6 [18], with 5,000 bootstrap resamples and bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals; p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Normality of continuous variables (NOV and ProQOL) was examined using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and Q–Q plots. All tests were non-significant (p > 0.05), indicating approximate normality and supporting the use of parametric statistical methods.

Results

Comparative analysis of NOV among ICU nurses across different demographic characteristics

Preliminary normality tests indicated that both NOV and ProQOL variables approximated a normal distribution (Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, all p > 0.05), supporting the use of parametric analyses. The study included 356 ICU nurses, of whom 84.6% were female, with a mean age of 32.4 years; approximately half (50%) had 5–10 years of ICU experience.

Analysis of variance and t-tests revealed significant differences in NOV across several demographic characteristics. Education level (F = 15.310, p < 0.05), professional title (F = 11.090, p < 0.05), employment type (F = 13.838, p < 0.05), and hospital level (F = 8.568, p < 0.05) were all associated with NOV. In addition, nurses with higher monthly income (F = 6.530, p < 0.05) and married nurses (t = − 2.341, p < 0.05) reported significantly different NOV scores compared to their counterparts. Secondary Trauma was significantly negatively correlated with most dimensions (r = -0.112 to -0.155, p < 0.05), but showed no significant correlation with Colleague Relationship (r = -0.076, p > 0.05), possibly reflecting the limited role of peer support in mitigating trauma within the ICU environment.

These findings suggest that NOV, particularly Intention of Career Choice and Job Security, may improve Compassion Satisfaction and reduce Job Burnout and Secondary Trauma by enhancing intrinsic motivation and a sense of security, providing potential directions for optimizing ICU team management.

Discussion

Demographic influences on nursing occupational values (NOV) and professional quality of life (ProQOL)

Our findings demonstrated that demographic characteristics, including education, employment type, hospital level, and marital status, significantly shaped NOV and, in turn, influenced ICU nurses’ ProQOL. Higher education levels strengthened professional values, consistent with Caldeira et al.’s review [19], which emphasized that advanced academic training reinforces professional commitment and resilience [20]. Similarly, stable employment and permanent contracts were associated with higher NOV, echoing Lee et al.’s findings [21] on Job Security’s protective role in nurse well-being. In China, where many ICU nurses face unstable contract arrangements, these findings highlight the urgent need to stabilize employment structures [22, 23]. Hospitals should provide career development opportunities through continuing education and prioritize permanent or long-term contracts to promote stronger professional values and enhance nurses’ ProQOL [20].

Impact of NOV on compassion Satisfaction, Job Burnout, and Secondary Trauma

The present study identified Intention of Career Choice, Job Security, and Colleague Relationship as key predictors of Compassion Satisfaction, while Nurse-Patient Relationship was positively associated with Job Burnout and Secondary Trauma. These results align with Poorchangizi et al. [24] and Wang et al. [13], who reported that intrinsic motivation and stable values mitigate burnout. However, our findings diverge from Yu et al. [25], who observed that team support plays a stronger protective role against Secondary Trauma in emergency nursing [10, 18, 21]. This difference may stem from ICU nurses’ highly autonomous work patterns, which may limit the buffering effects of peer support [13]. Nursing managers should implement structured peer support programs, while also promoting resilience workshops and stress management training, to help ICU nurses sustain Compassion Satisfaction and reduce burnout.

Mediating mechanisms and theoretical frameworks

Our mediation analysis revealed that Job Security mediated the effect of Leadership Attitude and Reward on Compassion Satisfaction, consistent with Zhang et al. [7] and the Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) framework [4, 7]. Leadership support enhanced nurses’ sense of stability, which in turn strengthened well-being. Conversely, Colleague Relationship showed a complex mediating pattern, at times suppressing the positive influence of leadership [16, 26], suggesting cultural nuances in collectivist contexts where interpersonal dependence may increase emotional strain [13, 27].

Although the confidence intervals of the indirect effects in Models 1 and 2 included zero, indicating non-significant mediation, the direct paths remained statistically significant. This suggests that Intention of Career Choice and Nurse–Patient Relationship influence Compassion Satisfaction and Job Burnout primarily through direct mechanisms rather than through the mediators of Job Security or Secondary Trauma. Within the framework of the Job Demands–Resources model, this pattern reflects that under the high cognitive and emotional load of ICU environments, the direct motivational and strain processes tend to dominate, while indirect pathways may exert weaker effects. Therefore, even when mediation effects are statistically insignificant, the persistence of significant direct relationships highlights the robustness of these associations in intensive care contexts.

Nurse–Patient Relationship did not show a strong mediating effect on burnout, contrasting with Lee et al. [18], possibly due to ICU patients’ critical conditions and high emotional demands. Despite the lack of significance in the mediation path, the direct effect of ‘Nurse–Patient Relationship’ on ‘Job Burnout’ remained significant (p = 0.040), highlighting the importance of direct relationships even when indirect effects are not significant. Leadership training programs should emphasize not only reward systems but also strategies that reinforce job stability and provide transparent communication about employment prospects [7].

Comparison with previous literature

This study contributes to the broader literature by positioning professional values as key personal resources in ICU nursing. Unlike studies in resource-rich contexts that emphasize staffing adequacy [11, 28], our findings highlight how intrinsic motivators sustain resilience even under chronic understaffing. Furthermore, our results support the JD-R model [12] by confirming that personal values can offset high job demands, though their influence on Secondary Trauma remains limited compared to Western studies [29]. This suggests that cultural and organizational contexts significantly shape how NOV impact ProQOL outcomes [25]. Global nursing policies should integrate culturally sensitive approaches, recognizing that resilience-building strategies effective in one region may require adaptation in another [29].

Clinical implications for ICU nursing practice

The findings of this study provide several actionable strategies for clinical practice. Leadership development through training ICU leaders to provide emotional support and recognition can enhance Compassion Satisfaction [25, 30]. Employment stabilization, such as transitioning contract-based nurses to permanent staff status, could reduce burnout and reinforce professional commitment [29]. Team-building interventions, including the establishment of peer discussion groups and interprofessional collaboration workshops, may strengthen Colleague Relationships while mitigating stress [27]. Communication training, such as patient communication workshops suggested by Kavoussi et al. [31], can equip ICU nurses with strategies to handle emotionally intense interactions, thereby reducing the risk of Secondary Trauma. Furthermore, continuing education should be encouraged, as hospitals that support academic advancement help reinforce professional identity and values, ultimately contributing to long-term resilience [1, 7, 32, 33].

In summary, this study reinforces the importance of Nursing Occupational Values as a vital personal resource for ICU nurses’ ProQOL. The findings highlight education, employment stability, leadership support, and colleague relationships as central to sustaining Compassion Satisfaction and reducing burnout. However, the limited impact of values on Secondary Trauma underscores the need for further research into ICU-specific stressors. Integrating these insights into clinical practice offers a roadmap to enhance nurse well-being and improve patient care quality in critical care settings.

Limitations

Despite its robust sample size and multidimensional approach, this study has several limitations that should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences about the relationships between NOV and ProQOL, limiting the ability to confirm, for example, whether Job Security drives Compassion Satisfaction over time. Second, the use of convenience sampling from 12 hospitals in Henan Province, China, may introduce selection bias and restrict generalizability, as variations in healthcare resources, staffing ratios, or regional cultural norms may influence findings elsewhere. Third, reliance on self-reported data raises the potential for social desirability bias, particularly given China’s collectivist emphasis on duty, which may inflate NOV or ProQOL scores. Additionally, our demographic data were limited to a few variables, and future research should expand sociodemographic factors to provide deeper insights.

Recommendations or implications for practice

This study on NOV and ProQOL among Chinese ICU nurses suggests key practice and research implications. Clinically, hospitals should offer permanent contracts to enhance NOV and Compassion Satisfaction, provide leadership training to foster supportive management, and implement team-building and communication training to reduce Job Burnout and Secondary Trauma. Supporting nurses’ education can further strengthen resilience [10]. For research, longitudinal and cross-cultural studies are needed to confirm causality and generalizability [19]. Qualitative research on Secondary Trauma, studies exploring additional mediators, and experimental intervention trials can refine strategies [17, 20, 34]. Investigating technology’s role in NOV and ProQOL will also advance global ICU nurse well-being and care quality. Future studies should consider collecting a broader range of sociodemographic factors, such as years of ICU experience, shift patterns, and family responsibilities, to gain a more comprehensive understanding of how demographic variables influence Nursing Occupational Values and Professional Quality of Life.

Conclusion

This study advances understanding of how professional values shape Chinese ICU nurses’ ProQOL, positioning education and Job Security as resilience pillars, with intrinsic motivators easing Job Burnout and Secondary Trauma. It offers a multidimensional framework for resource-scarce settings, contrasting staffing-focused approaches Tailored interventions—stable employment and team support—promise to enhance well-being and care quality, urging cross-cultural validation for global ICU practice.
