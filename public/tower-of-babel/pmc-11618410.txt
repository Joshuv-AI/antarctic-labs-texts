Hydrophobicity of periodic structure with taper angle under pressure impact
Authors: Ren Goto, Yuki Oshima, Masaki Yamaguchi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiomimetic periodic structures have garnered attention due to their excellent water repellency. The normal-taper angle, which is aspects of the cross-sectional structure, is important factor in achieving water repellency and pressure resistance; however, the underlying physical phenomenon has not been fully explained. Moreover, once a surface becomes hydrophobic, it is difficult to measure the apparent contact angle. The purpose of this paper is to clarify the taper angle that provides high water repellency under pressure impact conditions by formulating the relationship between the taper angle and the height of a droplet bouncing, instead of traditional contact angles, using experimental results. We fabricated multiple samples with different taper angles and groove width/tooth width ratios, through micro-processing using a femtosecond-pulsed laser and a control algorithm, and investigated their effects on water repellency. By using height of a droplet bouncing as an evaluation parameter, we were able to effectively differentiate between taper angles in terms of water repellency. Additionally, we suggested that in the bouncing phenomenon, where droplets are given velocity by falling, the sidewall of the periodic structure and the taper angle affect liquid repellency. To explain this phenomenon, we proposed a pressured-taper angle model where a droplet is pressed against the taper angle. Based on both experimental findings and the pressured-taper angle model, a relationship between the equilibrium contact angle, the taper angle, and the lifting force angle was revealed. Moreover, using this pressured-taper angle model, the taper angle of the periodic structure to achieve maximum liquid repellency was estimated from the equilibrium contact angle of the base material.Keywords: Droplet bouncing, Femtosecond-pulsed laser, Hydrophobicity, Periodic structure, Taper angle, Pressured-taper angle modelSubject terms: Biomimetics, Ultrafast lasers, Mechanical engineering, Biophysics

Introduction

Biomimetics, which aims to artificially imitate the superior surface functions organisms have acquired through the process of evolution to adapt to their environments, has been studied worldwide1. In particular, the expression of hydrophobicity and hydrophilicity through periodic structures in the micro/nano region is gaining attention as a carbon–neutral method that does not require chemical treatment2,3. Traditionally, the contact angle of smooth surfaces has been determined chemically, with 120° representing the highest attainable contact angle. Surfaces that exhibit superhydrophobic properties are defined by a static contact angle of > 150° with water and a contact angle hysteresis (CAH) of < 5°4. This is because “physical water repellency” through functional textures and “chemical water repellency” through treatments such as Teflon coatings can be simultaneously exhibited on the same surface. Functional textures can surpass conventional limits, exhibiting water repellency with contact angles exceeding 120°5.

The fundamental static mechanism that provides hydrophobicity and hydrophilicity is widely established through the Cassie-Baxter (C–B)6 and Wenzel7 regimes, which are based on two-dimensional uneven periodic structures8. Johnson and Dettre showed that homogeneous and composite interfaces correspond to the two equilibrium states of a droplet9. Nosonovsky and Bhushan proposed a two-dimensional model for the wetting of rough surfaces associated with the equilibrium state, showing that the contact angle is calculated by the configuration of a droplet occurs as superposition of numerous metastable states10,11. Bhushan and Jung introduced a transition criterion from a metastable composite state to a wetted state based on the pitch distance between the pillars and the curvature of droplet, which is governed by the Laplace equation that relates to the pressure inside the droplet12,13. Various criteria have been formulated to establish the relationships between the surface roughness and the contact angle, as well as the CAH14. Models from the early 2000s can explain the repellency of structure of hydrophobic materials, including two-dimensional structures such as the lotus leaf.

Focusing on water repellency, it becomes difficult for droplets to land on the surface when the apparent contact angle exceeds 120°, such as with fluororesin. Previous models can predict static and dynamic contact angles under pressure conditions, however, the differences in indices are not sufficient for superhydrophobic surfaces. In other words, it is not easy to compare surfaces that exhibit high water repellency based solely on contact angles. Therefore, the bouncing phenomenon of droplets is used. When a drop bounces on a surface, the height of the bounce serves as an effective measure of the energy lost during the process. On a flat surface, this energy loss is primarily due to a combination of liquid viscosity, which becomes evident as the droplet thins, and the interfacial tension resulting from the droplet’s adhesion to the surface. Esmaeili et al. compared chemical treatments using the bouncing phenomenon15. Under conditions where the deformation of droplets is well controlled, the bounce height of a droplet bouncing serves as a suitable indicator of static water repellency16–18. When a droplet bounces on a surface, the droplet wedges into the three-dimensional periodic structure, resulting in the energy loss being influenced by the three-dimensional periodic structure, including the normal- and reverse-tapers.

On the other hand, three-dimensional periodic structures based on biological surface motifs that exhibit extremely high and robust water repellency have been revealed recently19. Examples of such hydrophobic surfaces include the hierarchical structure of Nelumbo nucifera (lotus effect)20,21, the re-entrant structure, reverse-taper angle structure, found in duck feathers22–25, and the omniphobicity structure of Nepenthes26,27. In particular, the re-entrant structure exhibits promising geometry not only for hydrophobic but also for pressure resistant surfaces. Although several studies have attempted to create re-entrant structures using various techniques including nanoparticles28, microbeads immobilizations29, short-pulsed laser processing30,31, photolithography32,33, nanoimprinting34,35, and electrospinning36, the relationship between hydrophobicity and this 3D periodic structure with a taper on the sidewall of the convex part has not been fully understood. Panter et al. mentioned that despite recent substantial advancements in fabrication methods for re-entrant structures, design optimization remains a major challenge37. Meanwhile, Tuteja et al. reported the relationship between the robustness height and the sagging height of a droplet on the re-entrant structure under the condition that the pressure balance inequality was satisfied23. The optimal value of the taper angle has not yet been determined. Apparent contact angles on the taper angle structure under pressure impact conditions cannot be accurately explained using existing static wetting models, indicating the need for further research to precisely describe the behavior of droplets.

This study focuses on a normal-taper angle model for periodic structures under pressure impact conditions, where the droplet wedges into the three-dimensional periodic structure. It also examines a pressured-taper angle model, based on experimentally obtained height of a droplet bouncing without using contact angles and aims to determine the taper angle that demonstrates excellent hydrophobicity and pressure resistance. The taper angle and the groove width/tooth width ratio are used as the geometric parameters of the periodic structure. Since the higher the base material quality, the higher the water repellency, a fluororesin is used as the base material. Test-pieces with these geometric parameters are fabricated using femtosecond-pulsed laser processing. Based on the measured heights of a droplet bouncing, a pressured-taper angle is proposed and simulated about the relationship between the geometric structures. Through these studies, we discuss the validity of a model including the re-entrant structure.

Materials and experimental methods

Fabrication of test-pieces by laser processing. A laser system for processing test-pieces of periodic structure with a taper on the sidewall comprised (i) a femtosecond-pulsed laser oscillator (Pharos-6W, Light Conversion UAB, Vilnius, Republic of Lithuania) (ii) an x–y galvano mirror (1.2 μm of repeated positioning accuracy, CUA32-MST-AC, Newson NV, Dendermonde, Belgium), (iii) a telecentric fθ lens (15 mm × 15 mm of maximum scanning range, TSL-532-15-67Q, Wavelength Opto-Electronic (S) Pte Ltd, Singapore), (iv) an x–y electric-stage (HST-50XY, 2 μm of repeated positioning accuracy, Sigmakoki Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), and (v) a z-stage (OSMS60-10ZF, 5 μm of repeated positioning accuracy, Sigmakoki Co., Ltd.) (Fig. 1). The processing conditions of the femtosecond-pulsed laser for the fabrication of the test-pieces were as follows: an oscillatory wavelength of 515 nm, a pulse width of 277 fs, and a repetition rate of 50 kHz. A transistor-transistor logic signal (2 kHz of frequency, 5 V of voltage) output from the controller of the galvano mirror was used as the control signal for feedback control of all devices.

Figure 2 shows the two geometric parameters of the periodic structure such as the taper angle, ϕ, and the groove width/tooth width (f2/f1) ratio (where pitch, τ = f1 + f2). The waffle-shaped periodic structure was processed by performing biaxial laser drilling by scanning the laser beam in the x–y directions. The taper angle was formed on the surfaces of the inner sidewall of the waffle-shaped periodic structure. The f2/f1 ratios were varied in the range of 1.5 to 9, while the tooth width and the pitch were kept constant (f1 = 12 μm and τ = 120 μm). The taper angles were varied in the range of 65° to 90° while the groove depth was maintained at a constant value of d = 100 μm.

Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE, TOMBO No. 9000, 50 mm × 50 mm of area and 8 mm of thickness, Nichias Co., Tokyo, Japan) was used for fabricating the test-pieces. Prior to processing, the surface roughness of the unprocessed PTFE was measured using a non-contact laser confocal microscope (10 nm resolution for depth, OLS4100, Olympus Co., Tokyo, Japan) and a field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, 0.5 nm resolution, JSM-IT800, JOEL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Unless otherwise specified, the measurements were repeated five times, and the mean ± standard deviation (SD) values were calculated. The surface roughness measured using the laser microscope was Ra = 2.7 ± 0.6 μm. The diameter of the surface particles, dp, measured using the FE-SEM was 3.5 ± 1.0 μm (Fig. S1). As no significant difference was observed in Ra and dp, the main cause of surface roughness was considered to be the presence of micro-particles that originally existed on the PTFE surface.

In the laser drilling, the waffle-shaped periodic structure with a taper angle was machined by scanning from the center of the groove in a single stroke using the galvanometer scanner and the telecentric fθ lens. The pulse-to-pulse overlap process was used to obtain the desired f2/f1 ratios (Fig. 2A) and taper angles (Fig. 2B) through trial and error. Evaluation of height of droplet bouncing. Firstly, the apparent contact angles, θ′ (pendant drop method), the advancing contact angle, θA, the receding contact angle, θR, and the CAH of the test-pieces were measured using a contact angle analyzer (DM-701, Kyowa Interface Science Co., Ltd., Saitama, Japan)40. Distilled water (72.0 mN/m) and a 70% aqueous solution of ethanol (C2H5OH; 70%EtOH, 26.3 mN/m) were used as the sample solutions (2 μL of volume). Prior to this, the static equilibrium contact angle, θ, for each sample solution was measured using the commercial contact angle analyzer. The equilibrium contact angles of distilled water and 70%EtOH on the flat PTFE surface were found to be 116.8 ± 5.8°and 69.9 ± 8.4°, respectively.

Figure 3A shows a schematic diagram illustrating the height of droplet bouncing on the test-pieces with the periodic structure. A digital high-speed camera (1.54 μs/frame of time resolution in maximum, MEMERCAM HX-6, Nac Image Technology Inc., Tokyo, Japan) and the contact angle analyzer were used to characterize the bounce height of the test pieces. The C-B regime necessitates droplets larger than the roughness scale. Therefore, 2 μL droplets with a diameter exceeding 600 μm (635.4 ± 59.3 μm) were used. A 2–10 μL of droplet was released using a syringe to achieve impact velocities, u, of 0.3, 0.6, and 0.9 m/s through free fall onto the periodic structure. The droplet impact velocity maintained at less than one-sixth of its terminal velocity to prevent splashing. The impact velocities were controlled by appropriately adjusting the release height. The resolution of the high-speed camera was 1,280 × 960 pixels (2,000 fps). Prior to measuring the height of droplet bouncing, the test-pieces were neutralized with a static eliminator (SJ-M400, Keyence Co., Osaka, Japan). Immediately after this, a noncontact-type capacitive voltmeter (SK-035/200, Keyence Co.) was used to confirm that the surface voltage had decreased to ± 5 V. A stainless steel scale with a groove at 0.5 mm was fabricated and placed next to the droplet. The width of the groove was considered a standard length to measure the bounce height of the droplet.

System used for measuring the height of droplet bouncing.

Figure 3B shows an example of the observation of time-course changes in droplet jumping behavior using this system (t = 0–150 ms). The height of droplet bouncing was determined by image analysis using a software library (OpenCV, Open Source Vision Foundation, opencv.org). The image analysis was performed through the following steps: i. obtaining the outline of a droplet from the raw image, and ii. identifying the center coordinates by fitting an ellipse to the outline. The height of droplet bouncing, hwater, was defined as the distance between the centers of the droplets when it landed and bounced to its maximum height.

Evaluation of height of droplet bouncing. Figure 4 shows the measured results of relationship between the height of droplet bouncing and the taper angle with droplet volume, V, and droplet impact velocity, u, used as dynamic parameters. The height of droplet bouncing increased in proportion to the taper angle, regardless of the droplet volume, when the impact velocity was 0.9 m/s (H = 40 mm, Fig. 4A). However, as the droplet velocity decreased, the height of droplet bouncing did not increase significantly with the increasing taper angle (Fig. 4B). These results indicate that as the droplet velocity increases, the droplet wedges deeper into the surface structure, resulting in an increased height of droplet bouncing due to the influence of the taper angle16. Since the droplet was not observed to reach the bottom of the periodic structure, even at the maximum velocity, subsequent experiments were conducted at impact velocity of u = 0.9 m/s.

Results of height of droplet bouncing using the dynamic parameter (Sample no. s-64.3-9-s-87.7-9). (A) The droplet volume, V, was varied as the dynamic parameter, with the droplet impact velocity fixed at 0.9 m/s. (B) The droplet impact velocity, u, was varied as the dynamic parameter, with the droplet volume fixed at 2 μL.

Figure 5 shows the measured results of wettability using the taper angle as the geometric parameter. In both liquid samples of distilled water and 70%EtOH, liquid samples showed an increase in apparent contact angle, θ′, advancing contact angle, θA, and receding contact angle, θR, in proportion to the taper angle, ϕ, of the test-pieces (Fig. 5A,B). However, it was difficult to accurately compare the differences in θ′, θA, θR, and CAH due to changes in the taper angle. Particularly with distilled water, it was difficult to attach droplets to the sample surface because the apparent contact angles reached 159° at a taper angle reached 60°. These results are consistent with those reported by Xi et al. that fluororesin show such an extremely high contact angle41. Liang et al. reported that PTFE with a periodic structure exhibits superhydrophobicity, making it difficult for droplets to adhere to it42. Esmaeili et al. compared chemical treatments using the bouncing phenomenon15, and Leo et al. showed that re-entrant structures maintain high apparent contact angles even with significant changes in liquid surface tension17. Therefore, it is challenging to accurately identify differences in apparent contact angles due to geometric parameters. A similar tendency was observed for 70%EtOH whose interfacial tension was one-third that of distilled water. The bounce height of the distilled water, hwater, increased in proportion to the taper angle (Fig. 5C). While it was not possible to entirely eliminate the effects of vibration after a droplet made contact with a solid surface, we minimized this occurrence by reducing the droplet height. Esmaeili et al. noted that differences in water repellency of surface coating agents could be evaluated based on bouncing heights15. Additionally, the height of droplet bouncing decreased with the number of bounces, however, the relationship with the taper angle remained consistent without reversing. This phenomenon suggests that kinetic energy was consumed due to energy loss caused by droplet viscoelasticity and air resistance. Therefore, by using the data from the first bounce of a 2 μL droplet, a sine approximation curve was calculated between the height of droplet bouncing and the taper angle by the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm (Fig. 5C R2 = 0.95)43. These results suggest that the sidewall of periodic structure and the taper angle affect liquid repellency in the bouncing phenomenon under pressure impact conditions, such as when droplets are given velocity by falling. In other words, these results cannot be explained using conventional static model in which droplets are lifted up at the corner of the convex part as a three-phase contact line (TCL)44,45. The shape of the droplet on the C–B regime under pressure impact conditions depends on the 3D geometries of the periodic structure23,46.

The taper angle required to obtain maximum static water repellency is determined by the material’s equilibrium contact angle. For materials with an equilibrium contact angle of 90° or more, there exists a minimum required reverse taper angle that provides maximum pressure resistance. For example, when the maximum contact angle of materials is 120°, the maximum pressure resistance can be obtained by setting the reverse taper angle to 150°, and there is no need to make it any larger. However, for many materials with an equilibrium contact angle less than 90°, a larger reverse taper angle is better.

Conclusions

We fabricated multiple samples with different taper angles and groove width/tooth width ratios through micro-processing using both a femtosecond-pulsed laser and a control algorithm to clarify their effects on water repellency and pressure resistance. By evaluating the height of droplet bouncing, we were able to detect slight differences in water repellency. We proposed that the sidewall of the periodic structure and the taper angle have an impact on liquid repellency in the bouncing phenomenon, where droplets gain velocity upon falling. Thus, we introduced a pressured-taper angle model, where a droplet under pressure impact conditions is pressed against a taper angle while bouncing. Based on both the experimental results and the pressured-taper angle model, it was revealed that there was a relationship between equilibrium contact angle, taper angle, and force angle at the TCL. When a drop bounces on a flat surface, the energy loss is determined solely by the liquid viscosity and the interfacial tension of the surface material. We have demonstrated that when a droplet bounces on a surface with periodic structure, the droplet wedges into the periodic structure, resulting in the energy loss being influenced by the periodic structure such as taper. In this pressured-taper angle model, we determined the taper angle of the periodic structure, which achieves maximum water repellency based on the equilibrium contact angle of the base material.

While the authors have achieved a micro-machining technology at a reverse taper angle of 110° using femtosecond-pulsed laser processing31, it is still difficult to perform large-scale reverse tapered processing on any material in micro/nano machining. Therefore, future work should focus on conducting an experimental verification of the height of droplet bouncing, including the reverse taper angles of various other materials. Additionally, it is important to this study further investigate how to address the behavior of droplets during bouncing, given this study’s inherent limitations.
