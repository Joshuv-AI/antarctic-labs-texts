Wave-aware mortality prediction in COVID-19: a multi-stage feature selection and explainable machine-learning framework
Authors: Elaheh Fereidouni, Shadi Shafaghi, Hamidreza Jamaati, Morteza Mohammadzadeh, Mohammad Abiad, Fatemeh Sadat Hosseini-Baharanchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMortality among hospitalized COVID-19 patients changed markedly across successive epidemic waves as circulating variants shifted, population immunity increased, and health-system pressures fluctuated. These increasingly heterogeneous conditions raise concerns about whether prediction models developed during early phases remain reliable in later epidemic contexts.ObjectiveTo develop a wave-aware machine-learning framework that differentiates between stable physiological predictors of mortality and context-dependent predictors whose importance changed across waves.MethodsWe analyzed 732,654 adult hospitalizations from Iran’s national COVID-19 registry across Waves 2–5 and the post–Wave-5 period. All preprocessing, feature selection, and model training were conducted independently within each wave to preserve temporal structure and prevent leakage. A three-stage feature-selection approach Elastic Net shrinkage, Random Forest importance ranking, and Variance Inflation Factor filtering identified both stable and time-varying predictors. Model performance for Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and Deep Neural Networks was evaluated on wave-specific held-out test sets, and temporal robustness was assessed through cross-wave validation.ResultsA consistent physiological vulnerability core age, hypoxemia, and major chronic comorbidities was present across all waves. The divergence between preserved discrimination and degraded F1 demonstrates that static prediction models are highly susceptible to temporal drift. Drift-aware approaches such as recalibration, periodic refitting, or adaptive thresholding may be essential to maintain clinical utility in evolving epidemic environments. Introduction

COVID-19 emerged in late 2019 and rapidly escalated into a global public-health emergency [1]. As the pandemic progressed, many countries—including Iran—experienced several epidemiologically distinct waves driven by shifts in circulating variants, population immunity, and healthcare capacity [2–5]. National surveillance in Iran revealed pronounced variation in hospital admissions, demographic patterns, clinical presentations, and in-hospital mortality across successive waves [3–5], underscoring the speed with which the clinical and operational context evolved during the pandemic. For analytic clarity, epidemic waves were defined a priori based on national surveillance data and correspond to the following time intervals: Wave 2 (June 1–August 31, 2020), Wave 3 (December 1, 2020–February 28, 2021), Wave 4 (May 1–July 15, 2021), Wave 5 (September 1–November 15, 2021), and Post-Wave 5 (November 16, 2021–May 31, 2022).

These dynamics pose a fundamental challenge for prognostic modeling. Traditional prediction models implicitly assume that observations arise from a stable, stationary data-generating process. In a prolonged pandemic, however, admission thresholds, diagnostic practices, treatment availability, and variant characteristics continually change. As a result, the relationship between predictors and outcomes can drift over time—an established phenomenon referred to as covariate shift or concept drift [6–10]. If temporal drift is not adequately addressed, models developed using early-wave data may perform poorly in later epidemic contexts.

Identifying hospitalized COVID-19 patients at high risk of deterioration remains crucial for triage and resource allocation. Established benchmark tools—such as the ISARIC 4 C Mortality Score—have demonstrated strong clinical utility when appropriately validated [6, 7]. Yet many early models suffered from calibration failures, limited external validity, and insufficient attention to temporal heterogeneity [8–10]. Prior studies from Iran have similarly shown that hospital strain, vaccination rollout, and variant-specific severity contributed to substantial wave-to-wave differences in patient risk profiles [11].

Machine-learning (ML) methods such as Random Forests and Deep Neural Networks offer the flexibility to model complex nonlinear relationships in high-dimensional clinical data [12]. However, the majority of previous ML-based COVID-19 prediction studies pooled multi-wave data without explicitly examining temporal drift or the evolution of feature importance over time [13]. This approach risks masking clinically meaningful changes associated with variant prevalence [14], immunity patterns [15], care-seeking behavior [16], and healthcare-system pressures [17]. A growing body of evidence suggests that ignoring such heterogeneity can yield misleadingly optimistic estimates of model performance and hinder practical deployment.

To address these limitations, we adopted a wave-stratified modeling design in which preprocessing, feature engineering, feature selection, and model training were each performed independently within every epidemic wave. This design preserved temporal structure, prevented cross-wave information leakage, and enabled us to examine three key questions:

How did the distribution of predictors and clinical characteristics vary across waves?

To enhance interpretability while maintaining flexibility, we implemented a structured three-stage feature-selection pipeline—Elastic Net shrinkage, Random Forest importance ranking, and variance inflation factor filtering—applied separately within each wave. This hybrid approach was specifically designed to identify wave-specific signals, capture nonlinear interactions, and isolate a stable physiological core underlying mortality risk.

By integrating wave-stratified modeling with explainable ML, our goal was to delineate stable physiological predictors of mortality from dynamic, context-dependent predictors shaped by variant evolution, population immunity, and healthcare-system strain. This framework provides a principled basis for characterizing temporal drift and evaluating when recalibration, adaptive thresholding, or model updating may be required for reliable real-world deployment of COVID-19 prediction tools.

Materials and methods

Study design

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using data from the National COVID-19 Hospitalization Database, a nationwide registry coordinated by the Ministry of Health and Medical Education in Iran [18, 19]. The database systematically captured all hospital admissions with confirmed COVID-19 from July 2020 to April 2022, covering Waves 2 through the post–Wave-5 period (Fig. 1).

COVID-19 hospital admissions over time with pandemic waves highlighted

For each hospitalization, the registry included detailed demographic variables, clinical characteristics, diagnostic findings, and outcomes. Eligible cases were adults aged ≥ 18 years with laboratory-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection by RT-PCR or clinical–radiological criteria based on national guidelines.

Several exclusion steps were applied to maintain consistency and analytic quality. Duplicate entries were removed, records with ≥ 70% missingness were excluded, and patients discharged against medical advice directly from emergency departments were omitted because outcome ascertainment was unreliable. Following exclusions, 732,654 hospitalizations remained. Wave-specific complete-case filtering for the selected features resulted in minimal additional loss, as detailed in Appendix Table M1: Wave 2 (n = 167,120), Wave 3 (n = 121,992), Wave 4 (n = 66,061), Wave 5 (n = 196,869), and post–Wave-5 (n = 180,612).

Clear differences in baseline characteristics and outcomes were observed across waves, including a decline in mortality from 12.9% in Wave 2 to 6.7% after Wave 5 (Table 2). Because these temporal shifts suggested non-stationarity in the underlying data-generating process, all analytic steps including preprocessing, feature engineering, feature selection, and model training were conducted independently within each wave. This wave-specific design prevented temporal leakage and preserved the evolving epidemiological structure. A schematic overview of the analytic pipeline is shown in Fig. 2.

Data preprocessing

All preprocessing steps were performed separately for each wave to avoid cross-wave information leakage. Non-informative identifiers and free-text fields were removed. Predictors with > 70% missingness most commonly vaccination date variables and select laboratory tests were excluded. Implausible physiologic values were recoded as missing.

Continuous variables were standardized using z-score normalization:

The normalization parameters were computed on the training split of every wave and applied to the respective test set to avoid leakage [20]. Categorical variables were one-hot encoded, while “missing” categories were preserved only when clinically meaningful, such as unknown vaccination status.

Because completeness of variables varied considerably across waves - moderate in Waves 2–3 and more limited in later waves - we adopted a wave-specific complete-case strategy. Within each wave, observations with missing values on any of the features selected for modeling were excluded, thus ensuring internally consistent feature matrices without mixing missingness patterns across waves. Missingness patterns and analytic sample sizes are shown in Table 2 and Appendix Table M1.

Feature selection

In each wave, feature selection was done only within the training split of the cross-validation loop: Elastic Net → RF ranking → VIF. The final selected set was then applied to the held-out test data in order to avoid any leakage of information, respecting the temporal structure and distinguishing between the stable predictors from those that were sensitive to changes in variant dominance, immunity, or admission practices.

Leakage screening and univariate profiling

Before applying the main feature-selection pipeline, all candidate variables were screened for potential leakage. Univariate AUROC was computed for every variable, and predictors with near-perfect discrimination-AUC > 0.995-usually administrative codes or variables strongly coupled to mortality-were excluded.

This step provided an initial understanding of high-signal and wave-specific predictors.

Results

Baseline characteristics and clinical outcomes

A total of 732,654 COVID-19–related hospitalizations were included across five epidemic periods: Wave 2 (n = 167,120), Wave 3 (n = 121,992), Wave 4 (n = 66,061), Wave 5 (n = 196,869), and the post–Wave-5 period (n = 180,612). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics varied substantially across waves (Table 2), reflecting changes in circulating variants, population immunity, and hospital admission patterns.

Mean age varied from 53.2 years in Wave 4 to 60.5 years in the post–Wave-5 period, suggesting a shift toward an older case mix later in the pandemic. Duration of hospitalization steadily decreased from a mean of 8.1 days in Wave 2 to 4.6 days after Wave 5, consistent with expanded home oxygen programs and more streamlined discharge practices. The prevalence of comorbidities demonstrated considerable heterogeneity, with diabetes ranging from 12.8% to 18.3%, cardiovascular disease from 9.2% to 16.6%, and chronic kidney disease from 1.6% to 3.2%.

While classical respiratory symptoms of fever, cough, and respiratory dis-tress decreased in frequency across waves, gastrointestinal and neurologic symptoms increased modestly. These trends reflect evolving clinical presentations associated with variant replacement and growing population immunity.

Clinical outcomes also changed significantly between periods: in-hospital mortality decreased from 12.9% in Wave 2 to 6.7% after Wave 5 (Table 3). Admissions to the ICU varied nonlinearly, between 13.4% and 20.7%, because of dependence on both disease severity and hospital strain. Similarly, mechanical ventilation varied between 5.8% and 9.5%. These large differences between waves further supported the approach of wave-stratified analyses over pooling the combined dataset as a single homogeneous sample.

Wave 3

Performance modestly improved from Wave 2. RF and DNN resulted in AUCs of about 0.90–0.91, with an F1 of about 0.64 from RF. SHAP results (Fig. 5c–d) showed persistence of core physiologic predictors with rising importance of altered consciousness and hospitalization duration.

Wave 4

Models continued to show excellent discrimination, AUC ≈ 0.90–0.92; Brier ≈ 0.04–0.05. SHAP patterns were dominated by respiratory failure markers hypoxia, respiratory distress, intubation followed by gastrointestinal symptoms and chronic comorbidities (Fig. 5e–f).

Wave 5

RF achieved the best overall performance (AUC ≈ 0.94, F1 ≈ 0.68), and DNN maintained favorable calibration (Brier ≈ 0.04). SHAP analyses (Fig. 5g–h) showed preserved importance of acute respiratory predictors, alongside increasing influence of vaccination-related variables and oxygen-therapy indicators, consistent with widespread immunity and shifts in hospital admission thresholds.

Post–wave 5

Whereas discrimination remained high (AUC ≈ 0.90–0.93), the F1-scores of all models decreased, reflecting the lower mortality prevalence of 6.7% and shifts in the risk distribution.

SHAP analyses (Fig. 5i–j) continued to show age, hypoxia, ward level, and intubation as major predictors, with cancer and vaccine type becoming more prominent.

Across all waves, RF and DNN generally outperformed LR in both AUROC and F1. LR occasionally achieved higher recall at comparable thresholds, but overall performance favored nonlinear models. SHAP results consistently supported a two-layer structure: a stable physiological “core” overlaid by a dynamic epidemiologic layer influenced by immunity, care-seeking behaviors, and healthcare-system pressures.

Cross-wave drift analysis

To assess temporal robustness, a model trained on early waves (Waves 2–3) was tested on Wave 5 and post–Wave-5 data (Appendix Figure H1). A characteristic divergence between discrimination and classification performance emerged: AUROC declined only modestly (0.925 → 0.895 → 0.878), yet F1 declined sharply in the post–Wave-5 period (e.g., RF F1: 0.68 → 0.13 → 0.07). These F1 values were computed using a fixed probability threshold of 0.5. The marked decline therefore reflects the combined effects of prevalence shifts and miscalibration under temporal drift, whereby a fixed decision threshold no longer corresponds to an appropriate operating point in later waves. Figure 6 summarizes the top 20 drifting admission-time features. The strongest shifts concerned vaccination status and vaccine type, but changes in age distribution, hypoxia prevalence, and intubation and ward category frequencies were also prominent. These changes are consistent with evolving levels of population immunity, relative variant severity, and fluctuating hospital strain over time.

Taken together, these findings show that even when ranking ability is relatively preserved, substantial distributional drift can destabilize threshold-dependent classification performance. This underlines the need for recalibration or wave-specific updating of prediction models in rapidly changing epidemic environments.

Threshold sensitivity and calibration

In threshold-sweep analyses, the F1-optimal decision threshold varied across waves and model types. F1–threshold curves (Appendix Figure A5) indicate that DNNs usually performed best for lower probability cut-offs (≈ 0.20–0.35), reflecting smoother output distributions. In contrast, RF generally required thresholds around 0.45–0.55, whereas LR required substantially higher thresholds (≈ 0.70–0.85) to achieve balanced precision–recall performance. Discussion

In this large nationwide cohort of 732,654 hospitalized adults, mortality risk during Iran’s COVID-19 epidemic reflected the joint influence of a relatively stable set of physiological predictors and a series of contextual, wave-specific modifiers. By combining wave-stratified analyses with a structured feature-selection strategy, we were able to separate predictors that remained consistent across epidemic phases from those whose contributions shifted as variants changed, immunity accumulated, and hospital pressures fluctuated. Within each wave, Random Forest (RF) and Deep Neural Network (DNN) models generally outperformed Logistic Regression (LR) in terms of discrimination and F1-score on held-out test data (Table A1, Table A2). However, under cross-wave drift, LR preserved discrimination and F1 more consistently than RF, highlighting a trade-off between flexibility within a fixed context and stability under temporal shift (Table A5).

Our findings are broadly consistent with international evidence identifying age, hypoxemia, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease as reproducible predictors of severe COVID-19 outcomes [32–35]. Studies from China, Italy, and previous Iranian cohorts have similarly emphasized the central role of respiratory compromise and major comorbidities across diverse epidemic contexts [36–38]. The recurrent appearance of these variables in our SHAP analyses across all waves supports the notion of a “physiologic core” of patient vulnerability that persisted despite substantial shifts in case mix and viral epidemiology. Within-wave test performance in our data was compatible with this picture: RF achieved AUCs between 0.87 and 0.94 and F1-scores between 0.57 and 0.68 across waves, while DNN models achieved AUCs of 0.88–0.93 with F1-scores around 0.50–0.61 (Table A1). Ten-fold cross-validation showed similar patterns, with RF and DNN outperforming LR in AUC and F1 in every wave (Table A2). These results align with prior work showing that flexible nonlinear models can better capture complex interactions in clinical data than conventional logistic regression [8, 39, 40].

At the same time, our results reinforce concerns about pooled or time-agnostic models for COVID-19 [10]. Baseline characteristics and outcomes shifted markedly across waves: mortality declined from 12.9% in Wave 2 to 6.7% in the post–Wave-5 period, comorbidity profiles changed, and classical respiratory symptoms decreased while gastrointestinal and neurologic presentations became more frequent (Tables 2 and 3). These changes are consistent with evolving variant severity, population immunity, and admission thresholds [3–5, 11, 16, 41]. In such a setting, aggregating all waves into a single “stationary”cohort risks masking meaningful temporal structure and can create a misleading sense of model stability.

The impact of temporal drift became most apparent in cross-wave experiments. When admission-only RF models trained on early waves (Waves 2–3) were applied to Wave 5 and post–Wave-5 data, cross-wave AUCs remained in a seemingly acceptable range (0.80–0.88), but F1-scores dropped to 0.13 and 0.07, respectively (Table A5). In contrast, early-wave LR models achieved slightly higher cross-wave AUCs (0.83 and 0.90) and substantially higher F1-scores (≈ 0.31–0.32) on the same target periods. These patterns illustrate a characteristic divergence: ranking ability (AUC) can remain relatively preserved while threshold-dependent performance at a fixed decision threshold deteriorates under drift. Within waves, weighted F1-scores for all three models exceeded 0.82 (Table A4), yet the same models performed poorly when transported across markedly different epidemic phases. This underscores that point estimates of discrimination in a single time window can substantially overstate the practical robustness of a model in real-world, time-evolving environments.

Temporal variation in predictor importance further illuminates these dynamics. Early-wave models were dominated by markers of acute respiratory failure hypoxia, respiratory distress, intubation, and longer hospitalization consistent with initial clinical reports from Wuhan, Lombardy, and Tehran [32–37]. In later waves, vaccination status, vaccine dose, and vaccine type, along with changes in age distribution and comorbidities, became increasingly influential in SHAP analyses, especially in Wave 5 and the post–Wave-5 period. Feature drift analysis of admission-time predictors (Table A6) quantified these changes: for example, mean vaccine dose received increased from 0 in the early waves to 0.47 in Wave 5 and 1.38 in the post–Wave-5 period, and days since symptom onset and respiratory rate also shifted markedly. These trends are consistent with global observations that growing population immunity and changing care-seeking behavior altered the clinical profile of hospitalized patients once vaccination coverage increased [11, 16, 41]. Overall, our findings support a two-layer risk structure: a stable physiological kernel overlaid by a dynamic epidemiologic layer shaped by variant characteristics, immunity, and healthcare-system pressures.

Methodologically, wave-stratified modeling provides a transparent framework for characterizing concept drift without explicitly implementing online-learning algorithms. While our approach does not attempt to correct drift in real time, it makes clear when predictor distributions and calibration properties change enough to warrant recalibration or refitting. The combination of relatively preserved AUC and deteriorating F1 in cross-wave experiments exemplifies a well-described pattern in drifting environments: models may continue to rank patients correctly while failing to classify them accurately when calibration deteriorates [42]. Threshold-sweep analyses demonstrated that F1 is highly sensitive to the decision threshold and that the F1-optimal cut-off varies across waves, further emphasizing that fixed decision thresholds (e.g., 0.5) can become suboptimal under simultaneous class imbalance and temporal drift (Appendix Figure A1, Table A5). In our data, DNNs generally performed best at lower probability thresholds (≈ 0.20–0.35), whereas RF and LR required thresholds closer to 0.50–0.80 to balance precision and recall, with the optimal operating point differing across waves [31, 43].

From a clinical standpoint, our findings suggest that early-wave predictors primarily captured acute physiologic deterioration, while later waves increasingly reflected the impact of immunity, demographic shifts, and changes in comorbidity profiles. Intervention-related features such as ICU admission or intubation were consistently associated with mortality but, because they occur after admission, they should not be interpreted as deployable triage predictors. In admission-only models, RF tended to achieve higher recall for high-risk patients within each wave, which may be desirable for triage where missing high-risk cases is costly. However, cross-wave analyses show that this advantage can be fragile under drift: when early-wave RF models were transported to later waves, their F1-scores deteriorated far more than those of LR (Table A5). Conversely, the DNN’s smoother calibration and better precision within waves may help reduce overtreatment and resource burden in settings with constrained ICU capacity [44], but these benefits must also be reassessed whenever underlying risk distributions change.

This study has several limitations. High missingness in key laboratory biomarkers particularly CRP and D-dimer restricted the feature space and may have introduced selection bias if missingness correlated with disease severity [7, 32]. The retrospective design limits our ability to evaluate treatment effects or changes in clinical management over time [45, 46]. All data were drawn from a single national health system, which may constrain generalizability to settings with different healthcare structures, resource constraints, or epidemic trajectories [6]. Although we applied standard imbalance-correction strategies class weighting for LR and RF, and loss weighting for DNNs residual class imbalance and prevalence shifts likely remain and may contribute to instability in threshold-dependent performance [26–28, 47]. Finally, epidemic waves were defined descriptively rather than optimized for statistical homogeneity, so temporal recalibration remains necessary before any real-time implementation.

Despite these limitations, the integration of wave-stratified modeling, a principled multi-stage feature-selection pipeline, and explainable ML provides a framework that may extend well beyond COVID-19. Many infectious syndromes including influenza, RSV, and seasonal sepsis patterns exhibit changing risk distributions across epidemic phases. Tools such as SHAP-based drift characterization and feature drift tables (e.g., Table A6), coupled with systematic cross-wave performance assessment (Table A5), can yield insights that inform the design of adaptive decision-support systems capable of responding to evolving epidemiologic conditions. Future work should prioritize external validation across different health systems, evaluation of adaptive methods such as prequential or online learning, and prospective implementation studies to examine usability and impact in real-world settings. Restricting models to admission-time variables, quantifying temporal variability in SHAP values, and leveraging more advanced imbalance-handling strategies (e.g., focal loss or boosting-based reweighting) may further improve robustness. Combining temporal cross-validation with principled dynamic updating represents a promising direction for constructing prediction tools that remain reliable under shifting epidemiologic environments.

Conclusions

In this nationwide cohort of 732,654 hospitalized adults across five distinct COVID-19 waves, we identified a stable physiological vulnerability profile dominated by age, hypoxemia, and major chronic comorbidities superimposed on a set of dynamic, wave-specific predictors shaped by shifting variants, evolving population immunity, and fluctuating healthcare strain. Modeling each wave independently showed that mortality risk evolved meaningfully over time and that pooling observations across waves obscures this temporal heterogeneity in both predictor distributions and model performance.

Wave-stratified analyses revealed a clear divergence between discrimination and threshold-dependent performance at a fixed operating point under temporal drift. For example, while Random Forest AUROC declined only moderately when early-wave models were applied to Wave 5 (≈ 0.87 within-wave vs. 0.80 early→Wave-5), F1 scores decreased sharply (≈ 0.68 within-wave vs. 0.13 early→Wave-5; 0.07 early→ Post-Wave-5). Logistic Regression maintained discrimination more consistently across waves (≈ 0.90 within-wave vs. 0.89 early→Wave-5), yet also showed reductions in positive-class F1. These patterns provide quantitative evidence that even when ranking ability is preserved, miscalibration and distributional shift can substantially degrade classification performance in later waves.

From a practical standpoint, prediction architectures that anchor their structure on stable physiologic predictors while allowing contextual or epidemic-specific components to be updated may better maintain calibration, recall, and overall utility as epidemic conditions change. Such designs are particularly relevant for admission-time models intended for triage or early clinical decision support.

Future research should include external validation across diverse healthcare systems, exploration of adaptive or prequential learning methods that explicitly account for drift, and prospective evaluation at deployment time. More broadly, the wave-aware, explainable machine-learning framework presented here offers a structured approach for characterizing temporal drift and may support the development of more temporally robust prediction tools for COVID-19 and other evolving infectious diseases.
