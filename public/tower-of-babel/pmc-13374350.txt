ALDH18A1 regulates glutamine metabolism and tumor microenvironment in the attenuation of clear cell renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Yang Fu, Xuzhong Liu, Qing Sun, Zhiwang Tang, Zongyuan Xu, Kun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo assess the potential role of glutamine metabolism-related genes (GMRGs) in the progression, prognosis, and immune microenvironment of clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC).MethodsPublicly available single-cell RNA sequencing dataset was obtained, and differential gene expression analysis across tumors were performed. Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) has been considered as one of the leading malignant tumors worldwide, and clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) was reported to make up more than 70% of RCC, which is the most predominant subtype of RCC [1]. The prognosis of ccRCC mainly depends on tumor stage, grade, histological type, and progression status. At present, the clinical dilemma of ccRCC is characterized by difficult early diagnosis, difficult treatment, and poor prognosis. In addition, ccRCC has shown a high rate of chemotherapy resistance and even a low rate of immunotherapy response [2, 3]. Therefore, to explore feasible early-diagnosis and targeted therapy methods, it is emerging to further study the carcinogenesis mechanism to improve the precise prognosis and treatment of ccRCC patients.

Glutamine has been reported as the most enriched amino acid in circulating blood, and is crucial for various biological functions in cancer, including the synthesis of metabolites responsible for mitochondrial metabolism [4], production of antioxidants [5], synthesis of non-essential amino acids [6], purines [7], and fatty acids [8] for activation of cell signaling pathways [9]. With regard to the polypheny of glutamine in cancers, better understanding of glutamine metabolism is crucial for the therapeutic strategies. At present, increasing number of studies has begun to reveal glutamine metabolism in the occurrence, development, and treatment of many tumors. For example, Seung Joon Park found that increased glutaminolysis induces hypoxia through oxygen consumption mediated by oxidative phosphorylation and drives chemotherapy resistance in pancreatic cancer, which revealed a potential therapeutic strategy to overcome drug resistance by combining glutaminolysis inhibition and chemotherapy [10]. Rodrigo Romero demonstrated lung cancer with Keap1 or Nrf2 mutant is dependent on higher glutamine catabolism, and we can exploit this property for treatment by pharmacological inhibition of glutaminase [11]. Chi Chun Wong found that SLC25A22-mediated glutamine hydrolysis reduced DNA demethylation, thereby increasing WNT signaling, and drug resistance in cells of colorectal cancer carrying mutant KRAS [12]. However, the development of abnormal gene expression involved in glutamine metabolism of ccRCC is currently unclear. Recently, some studies have adopted methods to investigate biomarkers to provide optimal references for clinical research. Thus, exploring the regulatory mechanisms of glutamine metabolism on ccRCC formation could offer better insight for treatment.

In this study, we focused on glutamine metabolism-related genes (GMRGs) and comprehensively analyzed roles of GMRGs in ccRCC progression, prognosis, immune cell infiltration and immune microenvironment. We also developed prognostic risk models based on key GMRGs and further evaluated their potential as therapeutic drug targets. We thoroughly evaluated the potential of GMRGs as therapeutic targets or prognostic biomarkers, aiming to provide favorable guidance for survival prediction and clinical treatment decisions in ccRCC. Moreover, the in vitro validation study was designed and performed to further explore the effect of ALDH18A1 in the regulation of cell proliferation, migration and glutamine metabolism in RCC cells.

Materials and methods

Datasets and GMRGs

In the GSEA website, we obtained the gene set (M10295) related to glutamine metabolism pathway, with a total of 17 genes. We downloaded the TCGA-ccRCC data and extracted genes related to glutamine metabolism.

DEGMRGs and mutation rates

We matched transcription data to obtain mRNA data using the comparison with human data. The R4.1.0 was used to transform gene IDs into gene names. To evaluate the change in gene expression of glutamine metabolism, |log2FC|≥1 and FDR < 0.05 were defined and relevance of differentially expressed GMRGs (DEGMRGs) was evaluated by calculating expression in tumor and normal tissues using wilcox.test. Scores for specific functional sets were calculated by the method of ssGSEA, and the differences between cancerous and paracancerous tissues were compared by the rank sum test.

Molecular subtype construction based on the DEGMRGs

Based on the results of Consensus Clustering, patients were divided into three molecular types: cluster 1, cluster 2 and cluster 3. We used Survminer of R to assess the survival in subtypes, and which was further applied to investigate its predictive value. Chi-square test was applied to compare the clinical characteristics between different molecular subtypes, along with CNVS and SNPs. Single-sample sequence set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was performed using the limma, GSVA and GSEABase, GSVA. Two sets of immune cell enrichment scores and immune-related functions were examined in each GEO and TCGA cohort. Three subtypes for differential analysis were screened to screen the key differentiated genes.

GO and KEGG analysis

We screened the three subtypes for differential analysis to screen the key gene sets. Biological pathways related to the DEGMRGs were explored by GO among top 30 genes with the largest change in each of the three difference analyses. Cellular components (CC), molecular functions (MF), and Biological processes (BP) under control of the GMRGs were further evaluated by ggplot2, enrichplot packages from KEGG data.

GMRGs prognostic signature

We adopted the glmnet and survival package to establish the prognostic model, and GMRGs signature was filtered by Univariate Cox regression analysis and Lasso-penalized Cox regression. Each ccRCC patient’s associated risk score was further assessed. Individuals were classified into two subgroups according to median score, including low-risk and high-risk groups, which were further evaluated by Lasso regression. Then, risk ratio with confidence interval was generated, and forest plots, along with survival curves were obtained.

Immune infiltration analysis

The ssGSEA algorithm in “GSVA” R package with the data of 24 types of immune cells was used to analyze the infiltration profiles of immune cells in the high-risk and low-risk groups, and generate the relationship of GMRGs expression with immune infiltrations. We used Estimation of Stromal and Immune cells in Malignant Tumor Tissues using Expression data (ESTIMATE) generate the tumor microenvironment (TME) immune scores (StromalScore, ImmuneScore, and ESTIMATEScore) of key GMRGs in ccRCC patients. We used Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) algorithms to further elucidate the correlation of risk score with GMRSs expression of ccRCC-associated immune infiltration.

Spatial transcriptomics and metabolomics integration

Data alignment using the Seurat package’s TransferData function for co-registration of MALDI-MSI ion images with spatial transcriptomic spots based on histological landmarks and affine transformations was performed (with parameters: reference assay = “SCT,” reduction = “pca,” dims = 1:30). Spatial mapping of AUCell pathway scores were computed using the AUCell R package (v1.16.0) with gene sets from MSigDB (Hallmark Glutamine Metabolism), projected onto spatial coordinates via kriging interpolation in the gstat package. Finally, spatial statistics were applied, including Moran’s I test (P < 0.001 for glutamine enrichment clustering) using the spdep package to validate non-random spatial patterns.

scDRS and MAGMA analyses

Background gene sets were derived from MSigDB (C2 collection, n = 4,762 genes) for MAGMA and the full transcriptome (n = 20,000 genes) for scDRS. Normalization was performed using Seurat’s SCTransform with default variance-stabilizing transformation. Multiple testing correction employed Benjamini-Hochberg FDR adjustment. Significance thresholds were set at FDR < 0.05 for MAGMA gene prioritization and scDRS scores > 1.5 (top 5% quantile) for disease relevance. This aligns with established guidelines from the scDRS toolkit, which recommend these parameters to control for false positives while maintaining sensitivity in single-cell disease association studies.

Cell culture

Human renal tubular epithelial cell line (HK-2), human renal cancer cell lines (786-O, 769-P, Caki-1, and ACHN) and human THP-1 cell line were purchased from the Type Culture Collection of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) and cultured in RPMI 1640 (HyClone™, USA), as well as 10% fetal bovine serum (Invitrogen™, USA) and 1% penicillin /streptomycin (HyClone™, USA) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. To explore the ALDH18A1 effect on macrophages, 769-P cell and THP-1 cell lines were co-cultured using Transwell chamber. 769-P cells treated with sh-ALDH18A1 or OE-ALDH18A1 for 24 h and then implanted in the upper chamber, while THP-1 cells were implanted in the lower chamber. After 48 h of co-culture, THP-1 cells were harvested for further analysis.

Cell proliferation and colony formation analysis

Cells were seeded into a 96-well plate at a density of 1.0 × 103 cells/well. Cell proliferation was performed at 24 h (h), 48 h, 72 h, and 96 h using the CCK-8 Cell Counting Kit (Vazyme, China). A 450 nm microplate reader was used to measure the absorbance following incubation for 1 h at 37 °C according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

The colony formation assay was performed on cells incubated for 10 days. Colonies were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for further assessment.

Transwell cell migration assay

Cells at density of 1.5 × 104 cells/well were seeded into the Transwell upper chambers with serum-free medium, while 20% FBS medium was added to the bottom ones. After incubation for 24 h at 37 °C, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min. Cells were observed on a microscope in five randomly selected fields for quantitative analysis. All experiments were repeated three times.

EdU assay

Cells were treated with 100 µL of medium with 20 µM EdU, and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min after incubation for 2 h. The nuclei were stained and the rate of proliferation was calculated following the manufacturer’s instruction (Ribo Biotech, Guangzhou). Five randomly images of each group were selected areas and measured quantitatively by a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany).

Western blot and qRT-PCR

Western blot assay was carried out according to standard protocols. Briefly, cell lysis was performed by RIPA buffering solution. Protein concentrations were normalized by the BCA® assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific™, USA). β-actin were selected as loading control. Anti-ALDH18A1 (1:1000, Abcam™, ab315816), anti-β-actin (1:3000, Abcam™, ab179467) and secondary antibodies labeled with HRP (1:3000, Abcam™, ab6747) were used in this study. The bands were visualized and quantitatively measured through western blotting detection reagents (BD Biosciences, USA). All experiments were repeated three times.

RNA was collected and cDNA was obtained by the RevertAid® cDNA Synthesis kit (Takara™, Japan). Real-time PCR was carried out on ABI-7500, employing SYBR® Premix Ex Taq (Takara™, Japan). β-actin was selected as the normalization control. Primer sets employed were presented in Supplemental Table 1. All results were normalized based on the expression of β-actin, with all fold changes were calculated by the 2−ΔΔCT method. All experiments were repeated three times.

Determination of intracellular glutamine level

The glutamine levels in selected RCC cells were determined by the EnzyChrom Glutamine Assay Kit (EGLN-100, BioAssay Systems, Hayward, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Animal experiments

6-week-old BALB/c nude mice were randomly divided into 6 groups (n = 5) with blinding methods. Group 1–3 received subcutaneous injections of 5 × 106 786-O shRNA Negative Control (shNC) cells (Group 1), and 786-O sh-ALDH18A1 cells (Group 2), and 786-O OE-ALDH18A1 cells (Group 3). Group 4–6 received injections of 5 × 106 769-P shNC cells (Group 4), 769-P sh-ALDH18A1 cells (Group 5), and 769-P OE-ALDH18A1 cells (Group 6). Tumor volumes were assessed every 3 days, Euthanize the mice two weeks later by inhaling 5% isoflurane and collect the tumors. All procedures followed ethical guidelines for animal experiments and ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 for reporting animal studies (https://arriveguidelines.org).

Immunofluorescence staining

Paraffin sections were dewaxed in xylene, then rehydrated with gradients of anhydrous ethanol, 95% ethanol, 85% ethanol, and 75% ethanol. Immunofluorescence blocking solution was used and incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies (CD68 antibody diluted 1:800, CD206 antibody diluted 1:200); then mixed with Alexa Fluor® 488-labeled donkey anti-mouse secondary antibody and Alexa Fluor® 555-labeled donkey anti-rabbit secondary antibody. The tissues were incubated with DAPI for 15 min, and observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Flow cytometry assay

Tumor tissues from each group were freshly harvested and minced to 1 mm³ fragments, digest 30 min at 37 °C with collagenase IV + DNase I in RPMI; quench with 10% FBS, filter through 70 μm mesh, lyse RBC, resuspend in FACS buffer, block with anti-CD68/CD206/iNOS, stain viability dye and surface antibodies on ice 30 min, wash, fix, acquire on cytometer.

Statistical analysis

The results were summarized as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). All statistical analysis was carried out by SPSS version 16.0 with P < 0.05 being considered as statistical significance. Difference among any group was determined by Student’s t test or one-way ANOVA. All experiments were performed at least for three duplicate times.

Results

Identification of ALDH18A1 by scRNA-seq

To investigate the cellular origin and expression landscape of ALDH18A1 in the tumor microenvironment, we analyzed the publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing dataset. Each sample underwent rigorous quality control to exclude low-quality cells and potential doublets. Following log-normalization of the gene expression matrix, we integrated all samples and corrected for batch effects using the Harmony algorithm, ensuring a unified transcriptomic landscape across patients.

Subsequent dimensionality reduction and unsupervised clustering were performed based on the top 2,000 highly variable genes. The resulting clusters were visualized using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP), revealing 20 distinct transcriptional states (Fig. 1A). Each cluster was manually annotated using canonical marker genes to identify major cell lineages, including epithelial cells, T cells, macrophages, endothelial cells, NK cells, vascular smooth muscle cells, monocytes, B cells, mast cells, proliferating cells, dendritic cells, and fibroblasts (Fig. 1B). The distribution of these cell types was compared between normal and tumor tissues, highlighting a tumor-specific shift in cellular composition, particularly in epithelial and immune compartments (Fig. 1C). Figure 1D shows volcano plots of differentially expressed marker genes across distinct cell types, which were used to assist in cell type annotation and to validate the identity of each cluster based on characteristic gene expression patterns.

We next performed differential gene expression analysis between normal and tumor samples, identifying 657 significantly altered genes (adjusted P < 0.05), including 276 upregulated and 381 downregulated transcripts in tumors (Fig. 1E). Notably, ALDH18A1 emerged as one of the most significantly upregulated metabolic genes in tumor epithelial cells. To explore its potential functional interactions, we constructed a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network using the STRING database. ALDH18A1 was found to be centrally connected within a module enriched in amino acid metabolism and mitochondrial regulation pathways (Fig. 1F), suggesting a role in metabolic reprogramming.

We further confirmed the spatial localization and cell-type specificity of ALDH18A1 using both PCA and UMAP projections, which demonstrated its preferential expression in distinct epithelial cell clusters (Fig. 1G). Finally, violin plot analysis across all annotated cell types showed that ALDH18A1 expression was markedly enriched in tumor-derived epithelial cells, while its expression was low or negligible in immune and stromal populations (Fig. 1H). These findings suggest that ALDH18A1 may serve as a tumor-specific metabolic marker in epithelial-derived malignancies and could be involved in supporting anabolic metabolism in proliferating cancer cells.

Glutamine metabolism was significantly enriched in ccRCC

MAGMA was used to perform GWAS signaling gene priority scoring for ccRCC. MAGMA maps SNP loci in GWAS to genes, taking into account linkage disequilibrium between SNPs. Then, a regression model is constructed for each gene based on the p-value of the loci and the number of SNPs, and the final priority score is given (Supplementary Fig. 1). In order to improve stability, multiple datasets were further integrated and subjected to meta-analysis to obtain an integrated priority score. Five genes significantly associated with ccRCC were screened out (Fig. 2A). To further validate the functional significance of these high priority genes at the single-cell level, we introduced single cell Disease Relevance Score (scDRS) analysis to evaluate the disease association score of cells in ccRCC single-cell transcriptome data. We used the built-in scDRS module in Ragomics Meta to calculate the scDRS score for each cell separately based on the integrated scRNA seq data, and visualized it in UMAP coordinates. The results showed a significant increase in scDRS scores in the epithelial cell population derived from tumors, indicating stronger expression characteristics of ccRCC genetic risk (Fig. 2B-C). In addition, we also compared the scDRS distribution of different cell types in normal and tumor tissues through violin plots, further confirming that epithelial cells exhibit stronger disease associations in tumor states. It can be clearly observed in the figure that “Epicell_Tumor” scored the highest among all groups, supporting its key role in the occurrence and development of ccRCC (Fig. 2D). Next, functional annotation will be performed on differentially expressed genes in epithelial cells. Enrichment analysis of priority gene sets using GO-MF (Gene Ontology Molecular Function) showed significant enrichment of metabolic regulatory functions such as glutamate binding, RNA binding, and vitamin D receptor binding (Fig. 2E). The GSEA method was used to perform gene set enrichment analysis on the glutamate/glutamine metabolic pathway at the bulk level, and it was found that this pathway was significantly upregulated in tumor tissues (Fig. 2F). Furthermore, scMetabolism package at the single-cell level was applied to quantify the scoring of each cell in glutamine metabolism related pathways. Display two key metabolic pathway scoring distribution maps, Panel 1 for “Alanine, Aspartate, and Glutamate Metabolism” and Panel 2 for “Glutathione Metabolism” (Fig. 2G-H). Among all cells, epithelial tumor cells showed the highest metabolic activity (Fig. 2I), indicating that tumor cells are significantly dependent on glutamine and glutamate metabolism processes, providing nitrogen sources and reducing protection for their growth. Taken together, we have successfully constructed a cross scale disease driver gene validation chain, providing a solid basis for the exploration of key regulatory genes in ccRCC.

Spatial transcriptome analysis reveals enhanced glutamine metabolic activity in the tumor boundary region

To explore the spatial metabolic reprogramming within the tumor microenvironment of clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), we performed spatial metabolomics using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization mass spectrometry imaging (MALDI-MSI) on tumor sections. Regions of interest were selected to include both the tumor core and the adjacent tumor-normal interface (Fig. 3A). Clustering based on spatial transcriptomic features revealed seven distinct domains, which corresponded to histologically and metabolically distinct regions (Fig. 3B). The spatial transcriptomic read coverage was robust and heterogeneously distributed, with elevated transcript abundance in the tumor core (Fig. 3C).

Spatial transcriptome analysis reveals enhanced glutamine metabolic activity in the tumor boundary region. (A) Regions of interest were selected to include both the tumor core and the adjacent tumor-normal interface. (B) Clustering based on spatial transcriptomic features. (C) Distribution of spatial transcriptomic read coverage. (D) UMAP embedding of the spatial transcriptome analysis. (E) Spatial projection of Leiden clusters onto tissue sections. (F) Cardinal-based signal reconstruction. (G) Glutamine pathway activity scores (based on AUCell) in epithelial cells. (H) Dotplot analysis using data originating from Leiden clusters 0, 1, and 3, with ligand-receptor axes. (I) MEBOCOST network visualization

UMAP embedding of the spatial transcriptome revealed consistent clustering patterns with those observed in single-cell transcriptomics (Fig. 3D), further supporting the integration fidelity of spatial and single-cell data. Spatial projection of Leiden clusters onto tissue sections (Fig. 3E) showed that specific clusters, such as cluster 0 and 2, were spatially enriched in the tumor-normal border, suggesting biological specialization at the interface zone.

Among all quantified metabolites, glutamine demonstrated a striking spatial pattern. Cardinal-based signal reconstruction revealed its enrichment at the tumor-normal interface (Fig. 3F), where epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-related transcriptional programs are often activated. This spatial glutamine enrichment coincided with high glutamine pathway activity scores (based on AUCell) in epithelial cells from single-cell RNA-seq data, confirming the accuracy of spatial-transcriptomic-metabolomic fusion analysis (Fig. 3G). Furthermore, glutamine and its related metabolites (e.g., γ-glutamyl-alanine, pyroglutamic acid) were the most frequent mediators in predicted cell-cell metabolic communications.

To dissect the intercellular signaling architecture within the tumor ecosystem, we applied the MEBOCOST algorithm to infer ligand-receptor-based communication networks between spatially resolved cell states. By integrating spatial gene expression and clustering results, we identified key intercellular signaling interactions concentrated in clusters located near the glutamine-enriched interface. Dotplot analysis highlighted strong and frequent communication events originating from Leiden clusters 0, 1, and 3, with ligand-receptor axes such as SPP1-CD44 and CXCL12-CXCR4 displaying high interaction scores (Fig. 3H). Network visualization further revealed that communication was not randomly distributed but rather formed dense, directional interaction hubs centered around metabolically active cell populations (Fig. 3I). This implies that spatial glutamine metabolism is closely linked to intercellular signaling activity, potentially driving EMT and immune evasion at the invasive tumor front.

These results suggest that the tumor-normal interface in ccRCC represents a metabolically and transcriptionally active zone with intense intercellular communication, orchestrated in part through glutamine-related signaling. This spatially defined metabolic niche may serve as a functional hotspot for tumor progression and therapeutic intervention.

Differential analysis of GMRGs between ccRCC and normal samples

To characterize glutamine metabolism dysregulation in clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), we systematically compared the expression levels of 20 glutamine metabolism-related genes (GMRGs) between tumor and adjacent normal tissues based on transcriptomic data. As shown in the heatmap (Fig. 4A), a distinct gene expression profile was observed, with multiple GMRGs showing significant alterations between the two groups. Pathway scoring based on gene set enrichment indicated that the glutamine metabolism pathway activity was significantly decreased in tumor samples relative to normal tissues (P < 0.001; Fig. 4B), suggesting an overall suppression of canonical glutamine metabolism in the tumor compartment.

Differential analysis of GMRGs between ccRCC and normal samples. A-B: Comparison of the gene expression between tumor and normal samples. C: 15 glutamine metabolism-related genes were found differentially expressed (adjusted P < 0.05) between ccRCC and normal tissues. 6 GMRGs were upregulated in ccRCC group, and 9 of them were downregulated in ccRCC group

Boxplot analysis (Fig. 4C) revealed that among the 20 examined GMRGs, 15 genes exhibited statistically significant differential expression between ccRCC and normal tissues (adjusted P < 0.05). Specifically, six genes (including GLS, GLS2, GLUD2, GLUL, LGSN, and PYCR3) were upregulated in tumor tissues, while nine genes (such as ALDH18A1, ASL, ASS1, CPS1, OTC, PYCR1, and PYCR2) were significantly downregulated. Among them, 13 genes met the threshold of |log2 fold change| > 1 and P < 0.01, and were defined as the top differentially expressed glutamine metabolism regulators.

We further analyzed the mutational landscape of these GMRGs in the TCGA-KIRC cohort. As presented in Supplemental Fig. 2, single nucleotide variants (SNVs) were identified in 11 of 370 patients (mutation frequency 2.97%). The most commonly mutated GMRGs included GLS2, PYCR1, and ALDH18A1, although all had mutation frequencies below 5%, suggesting that transcriptional regulation rather than coding mutations likely drives GMRG dysregulation in ccRCC.

DEGMRGs distinguish ccRCC patients

To further explore the heterogeneity of glutamine metabolism in ccRCC, we performed unsupervised consensus clustering on tumor samples using expression profiles of glutamine metabolism-related genes. The consensus cumulative distribution function (CDF) curve (Fig. 5A) and the relative change in area under the CDF curve (Delta area plot, Fig. 5B) indicated that k = 3 provided optimal cluster stability and discrimination. The consensus matrix heatmap (Fig. 5C) showed robust separation between the three identified clusters, and the tracking plot (Fig. 5D) confirmed consistent sample assignments across iterations.

DEGMRGs distinguish ccRCC patients. A-D: By increasing the clustering variable (k) from 2 to 10, we obtained the highest and lowest within-group correlations when k = 3. This indicates that patients with ccRCC can be successfully divided into three groups. E: Survival curve for 3 types of patients. Cluster 1 had the best survival while cluster 3 had the worst survival (P < 0.0001)

Based on these results, we classified ccRCC patients into three molecular subtypes: Cluster 1 (C1), Cluster 2 (C2), and Cluster 3 (C3). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed significant differences in overall survival among these subgroups (P < 0.0001; Fig. 5E). Patients in C3 exhibited the poorest prognosis, while those in C2 showed the most favorable outcomes. These findings suggest that glutamine metabolism–based molecular classification captures biologically and clinically meaningful subtypes in ccRCC and may provide a basis for personalized metabolic-targeted therapy.

Best survival was observed in cluster 1, while the worst was noticed in cluster 3 (P < 0.0001). Supplemental Fig. 3 showed no statistical difference in age and N stage among the three groups. A 78.11% mutation rate was observed, but no significant difference was reported across three groups, which may suggest that the GMRGs-related prognosis difference is not due to genetic mutations (Supplemental Fig. 4). Immune analysis identified differences in 12 out of 24 immune cells, including activated Tregs, NK cells, memory CD4+T cells, M0 macrophage and neutrophil (Supplemental Fig. 5).

Functional enrichment analysis and prognostic model based on DEGMRGs

To further elucidate the biological relevance of the three glutamine metabolism-based ccRCC subtypes, we performed pairwise differential expression analyses between clusters. Volcano plots demonstrated clear transcriptomic distinctions among the subgroups, with multiple differentially expressed glutamine metabolism-related genes (DEGMRGs) identified between C1 vs. C2, C1 vs. C3, and C2 vs. C3 (Fig. 6A–C). Using intersection and filtering criteria, a total of 30 DEGMRGs were selected for downstream enrichment and modeling analyses.Functional enrichment analysis was performed to interpret the biological roles of DEGMRGs. KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment in drug metabolism–cytochrome P450, chemical carcinogenesis, and complement and coagulation cascades, suggesting these genes may participate in both metabolic reprogramming and immune-related tumor processes. Gene Ontology (GO) terms showed strong associations with xenobiotic stimulus response, transmembrane transporter activity, and receptor signaling pathways (Fig. 6D), indicating that these glutamine-linked transcriptional programs may affect nutrient sensing, detoxification, and extracellular signaling within the tumor microenvironment. To construct a robust prognostic model, we performed LASSO Cox regression on the 30 DEGMRGs. The optimal λ value was selected via 10-fold cross-validation to prevent overfitting, resulting in a minimal model with two genes (GLUD1 and ALDH18A1) contributing significantly to the risk score (Fig. 6E–F). Based on the resulting coefficients, a risk score was calculated for each patient. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that patients in the high-risk group exhibited significantly poorer overall survival compared to those in the low-risk group (P = 0.00042; Fig. 6G). Time-dependent ROC curve analysis further confirmed the predictive power of the model, with area under the curve (AUC) values exceeding 0.7 at 1, 3, and 5 years (Fig. 6H).

Functional enrichment analysis and prognostic model based on DEGMRGs. A-C: Top 30 differentially expressed genes between each group of gene clusters. D: GO enrichment analysis and KEGG pathway analysis. GO enrichment analysis found DEGMRGs were mainly related to cationic membrane transport receptor activation in all biological processes. E-H: Two-gene (GLUD1 and ALDH18A1) prognostic models were constructed. The survival curve indicated that the prognosis of the high-risk group was significantly lower than that of the low-risk group (P = 0.00042)

These results demonstrate that glutamine metabolism–related gene signatures can serve as effective prognostic indicators in ccRCC and provide a basis for risk stratification and potential therapeutic targeting. Then, we explored TIDE, TME, and immune cells infiltration in high-risk and low-risk groups (Supplemental Fig. 6A). 24 immune cells analysis showed that patients in the high-risk group showed larger number of M2 macrophages, Tregs, activated memory CD4+T and neutrophils, while M1 macrophages and resting mast cells were reduced in the high-risk patients (Supplemental Fig. 6C). TME analysis (Supplemental Fig. 6D) showed that the ESTIMATE, immune, and matrix scores of the low-risk group were statistically decreased compared with the high-risk group. However, there was no difference in TIDE score between the two groups.

Prognostic prediction of ccRCC based on ALDH18A1

To better verify the prognostic value of GMRGs in ccRCC, we further verified the effect of ALDH18A1 gene. According to Transcripts per Million (TPM), we classified patients as high-ALDH18A1-TPM and low-ALDH18A1-TPM groups, and drew the survival curves of the two groups of patients (Supplemental Fig. 7). The results suggested that patients with high ALDH18A1 TPM showed lower prognosis than low ALDH18A1 TPM group (P = 0.032). Additionally, no significant difference was reported in the expression of ALDH18A1 across different clinical stages, suggesting that ALDH18A1 may be a new prognostic indicator independent of TNM classification. Finally, we investigated the immune checkpoint and immunomodulatory genes associated with ALDH18A1 (Supplemental Fig. 8). Results showed that CD276, VEGFA and C10orf54 were significant immunosuppressive checkpoints, while HMGB1, ENTPD1, TLR4 and TNFSF4 were immune stimulating checkpoints. Several genes of CCL cytokine family, and HLA genes such as B2M TAP1 and TAP2, were found to be ALDH18A1-related immunomodulatory genes, which provided a reference for exploring the mechanism behind the ALDH18A1-related prognostic difference in ccRCC.

Silencing ALDH18A1 suppressed proliferation and migration of RCC cells

To validate the biological function and role of ALDH18A1 in ccRCC, we performed the experimental validation. We observed that the mRNA up-regulated ALDH18A1 expression in RCC cell lines when compared to HK-2 cells (Fig. 7A). Likewise, expressions of ALDH18A1 protein in RCC cell lines were significantly higher (Fig. 7B-C).

To further determine the biological function of ALDH18A1, shRNA of which were transfected into 786-O and 769-P papillary renal carcinoma cells. ALDH18A1 mRNA levels were examined by RT-PCR to confirm the effect of shRNAs. As shown in Fig. 7D, sh-ALDH18A1-1 and sh-ALDH18A1-2 were selected for intervention study. CCK-8 assay showed that knockdown of ALDH18A1 could significantly inhibit the cell viability (Fig. 7E-F). Moreover, colony formation assays were performed to assess the long-term effect of ALDH18A1 on the RCC cell proliferation. It is indicated that colony-formation efficiency among sh-ALDH18A1-1 and sh-ALDH18A1-2 groups were remarkably lower than negative control group both in 786-O and 769-P cell lines after 10 days (Fig. 7G-H). Transwell assays reported that silencing ALDH18A1 could decrease the cells metastasis ability (Fig. 7I-J). In addition, EdU incorporation assay demonstrated that 786-O and 769-P cells contained reduced EdU-positive cells with novel synthesized DNA following transfection of sh-ALDH18A1-1 and sh-ALDH18A1-2 (Fig. 7K-L). The mRNA expression of proliferation and invasion biomarkers (MKI67, PCN, MMP2, MMP9) were consistent with above results (Fig. 7M-N).

To validate the potential role of ALDH18A1 in glutamine metabolism involved in RCC cells, we performed consequent experiments on the silencing ALDH18A1 in RCC cells. The intracellular glutamine concentrations in 786-O and 769-P cells significantly decreased when treated by sh-ALDH18A1-1 and sh-ALDH18A1-2 (Fig. 7O-P). Additionally, we tested the mRNA expression of several glutamine or glutamate transporters according to above results. As shown in Fig. 7Q-R, mRNA expression of SLC7A11 representing gene name of cystine glutamate antiporter (xCT) was significantly decreased in cells with shRNA intervention, while one of the most important glutamine transporter, SLC1A5 of which the gene name of alanine-serine-cysteine transporter 2 (ASCT2), was not altered. Glutaminase (GLS) is the enzyme that converts glutamine into glutamate, the mRNA expression of which was remarkably lower in shRNA treated RCC cells. However, its isoform glutaminase 2 (GLS2) mRNA expression remained unchanged among three groups in both cells. These data indicated that ALDH18A1 was involved in the glutamine metabolism of RCC cells.

Subcutaneous tumor experiments in mice further confirmed the oncogenic-promoting effects of ALDH18A1 on RCC

To further validate the oncogenic-promoting effects of ALDH18A1 on the RCC, subcutaneous tumor experiments in mice were performed. 6-week-old BALB/c nude mice were randomly divided into six groups, while tumor volumes of which were evaluated every 3 days, and mice were euthanized after 2 weeks to collect tumors. The tumor growth was significantly decreased in groups of 769-P + sh-ALDH18A1 and 786-O + sh-ALDH18A1 when compared to control groups (Fig. 8A-B). Results of bioinformatics analysis have already indicated the potential crosstalk between the ALDH18A1-regulated metabolism in RCC and M2 macrophage. Subsequently, IHC staining of CD68 and CD206 showed the significant infiltration of M2 macrophage in ccRCC group (Fig. 8C). The mRNA expression of ALDH18A1 was significantly reduced when treated by sh-ALDH18A1 (Fig. 8D) in vitro, which was consistent with the results in ALDH18A1 protein (Fig. 8E) and ALDH18A1 IF staining (Fig. 8F). Furthermore, protein expression of CD206 in THP-1 cells was remarkably reduced when 769-P cells treated by sh-ALDH18A1 (Fig. 8G), while the mRNA and protein expression of ARG1, Ym1 (CHI3L1), Fizz1 (RETNLA), and CD206 were remarkably suppressed in THP-1 cells by the intervention of 769-P and sh-ALDH18A1 (Fig. 8F-G). Notably, double immunofluorescence staining of CD68 and CD206 in THP-1 cells co-cultured with 769-P + sh-ALDH18A1 was consistent with previous results (Fig. 8F). To validate these results, flow cytometry assays were performed, and significantly lower ratio of CD68+CD206+M2 macrophages in sh-ALDH18A1 group was observed, while proportion of CD68+iNOS+M1 macrophages showed increased (Fig. 8I). In addition, the absolute concentrations of glutamine in each group were examined, of which was significantly higher in OE-ALDH18A1-treated group and lower in sh-ALDH18A1-treated group (Fig. 8J). The mRNA expression of glutamate transporters (SLC7A11 and GLS) was consistent with the results in glutamine levels (Fig. 8K). Therefore, our results further confirmed the oncogenic-promoting effects of ALDH18A1 on RCC, which may result from the crosstalk with M2 macrophage.

Discussion

In this study, 17 gene sets (M10295) related to glutamine metabolism pathway were obtained from GSEA and intersected with the TCGA-ccRCC data to further compare the differences between cancer tissues and adjacent tissues. According to the consensus clustering results, patients could be divided into three GMRGS-related molecular subtypes with significant survival differences. The prognostic model of ccRCC constructed according to DEGMRGs suggested that the prognosis of high-risk group was significantly lower than that of low-risk group, which reflected the predictive value of GMRGs for the prognosis of ccRCC patients. Functional analysis showed that DEGMRGs were related to drug metabolism, complement and coagulation cascades, and cationic membrane transport receptor activation. Analysis of 24 immune cells showed that patients in the high-risk group had higher numbers of M2 macrophages, Tregs, activated memory CD4 + T cells, and neutrophils, while M1-type macrophages and resting mast cells were reduced. We further validated the role of ALDH18A1 gene and found that patients with high ALDH18A1 TPM had worse prognosis than those with low ALDH18A1 TPM. In addition, our in vitro study confirmed the crucial role of ALDH18A1 in the cell proliferation and migration ability of RCC cells, and the mechanism of which may be related to the regulation of glutamine metabolism.

In the past decade, although the prognosis of KIRC is relatively good, with a 5-year survival rate of 75%, nearly 30% of locally advanced cases will develop locoregional recurrence or distant metastasis [1]. There are still many questions to be elucidated about the pathogenesis and potential mechanisms of KIRC, and the research on the diagnostic methods of KIRC is also in its infancy. In this context, the construction of a novel clinical model covering screening, diagnosis, and prognosis prediction is clinically significant and provides new insights for treatment decisions in precision medicine. Therefore, in this study, we focused on glutamine metabolic-related genes in ccRCC for the first time, as glutamine has been brought into the arena of cancer metabolism research in recent years. Glutamine has been defined to a group of conditionally essential amino acids, such as post-surgery, injury, or sepsis, in which the consumption of glutamine by the immune compartments and kidney, dramatically increases [13]. Intestinal mucosal cells are particularly dependent on glutamine, and necrosis occurs rapidly after depletion of glutamine [6]. These findings indicated the dependence of growing cancer cells on glutamine, with some cancer cells rapidly come to death if glutamine is exhausted [13]. Glutamine metabolism-related genes have clearly become one of the new competitive targets for tumor prognosis prediction and treatment.

At present, various studies have reported the mechanism of GMRGs in cancer. For example, Yu H analyzed the characteristis of breast cancer subgroups on basis of glutamine metabolism [14], and Fan’s team found that various classification of glutamine metabolism could lead to the TME immune characteristics and aggressiveness of gliomas, while their mutation status in IDH is not affected [15]. The results of our study also found that GMRGs could classify ccRCC patients into three molecular subtypes with significant survival differences, and there was a significant difference in TNM classification, reflecting the predictive value of GMRGs for the occurrence and progression of ccRCC and the prognosis of patients. Our functional enrichment suggests that differential GMRGs are associated with effects such as cation membrane transport receptor activation, which may be one of the reasons for their impact on ccRCC prognosis. Central to this paradigm is the facilitative transport of glutamine into cells, primarily mediated by various transporters such as the extensively scrutinized solute carrier family 1 neutral amino acid transporter 5 [16, 17], Once inside the cellular milieu, glutamine can either undergo biosynthesis or be effluxed via antiporters, notably replaced by other amino acids such as leucine via the L-type amino acid transporter 1 (LAT1) antiporter [18]. Furthermore, glutamine-derived glutamate can be swiftly reduced to cysteine within the cellular confines via the xCT antiporter, a heterodimer comprising SLC3A2 and SLC7A11, facilitating cystine transport [19].

An apparent discrepancy emerged from our multi-omics analyses: single-cell and spatial transcriptomic data indicated increased glutamine metabolic activity within tumor epithelial cells, particularly in spatially defined tumor regions, whereas bulk RNA-seq pathway analysis suggested an overall reduction of glutamine metabolism in tumor tissues. Importantly, these observations are not mutually exclusive. Single-cell and spatial approaches interrogate epithelial-intrinsic transcriptional programs at cellular resolution and within specific spatial niches, while bulk RNA-seq captures a composition-weighted average across a heterogeneous mixture of epithelial tumor cells and multiple non-epithelial compartments, including immune and stromal cells. Variability in the abundance and transcriptional states of these non-epithelial components across samples can therefore dilute or obscure epithelial-specific metabolic signals, resulting in an apparent decrease at the bulk-tissue level despite epithelial-intrinsic activation. Moreover, bulk pathway-level results are inherently influenced by gene-set composition and scoring strategies, particularly when genes within the same metabolic pathway display heterogeneous directions of regulation. Consequently, bulk RNA-seq readouts should be interpreted as tissue-level composite signals rather than direct reflections of tumor epithelial metabolism. Together, these considerations support a model in which glutamine metabolic reprogramming in ccRCC is spatially and cell-type restricted, consistent with the well-established metabolic heterogeneity of this disease and the recognized limitations of bulk transcriptomics in resolving compartmentalized metabolic activity.

Furthermore, an expanding body of research underscores the pivotal role of glutamine metabolism reprogramming as an important mediator of anti-tumor responses within TME. Broadly, the voracious uptake of glutamine by cancer cells within the TME curtails its availability for immune cells, thus exerting a profound impact on the efficacy of the anti-tumor response [20]. Cellular glutamine allocation can affect anti-tumor responses [20]. However, our investigation revealed divergent levels of M2 macrophage infiltration among patients characterized by distinct GMRG subtypes, notably observing heightened infiltration in high-risk group. Presently, there exists a global consensus acknowledging the distinct functional roles of M1 and M2 macrophages within the immune milieu. M1 macrophages are esteemed for their pivotal involvement in fostering positive immune responses via the secretion of chemokines and inflammatory cytokines, actively engaging in immune surveillance, and proficiently executing professional antigen presentation. Conversely, M2 macrophages exhibit attenuated antigen presentation capabilities and predominantly secrete anti-inflammatory cytokines including TGF-β or IL-10 [21, 22]. Several investigations have highlighted the significance of glutamine metabolism in macrophage activation, revealing inherent variances in the glutamine reliance across distinct macrophage subsets. Notably, studies indicate that glutamine hydrolysis significantly impacts the polarization of M1 macrophages [23]. M2 macrophages exhibit a heightened consumption of glutamine compared to M1 and naive macrophages. Notably, glutamine tends to accumulate within M2 macrophages, thereby fostering their polarization [24–26]. However, the detailed mechanism of glutamine metabolism controls the tumor-promoting function of TAMs needs to be extensively investigated [27]. Furthermore, it remains uncertain whether the inhibition of M2-type macrophage polarization elicits a more robust anti-tumor response compared to the enhancement of M1 macrophage polarization in TME. Hence, the interplay of these factors will ultimately dictate whether glutamine metabolism in macrophages fosters or impedes anti-tumor responses, which requires more extensive functional studies to explore the association of M2 macrophages and ALDH18A1 expression.

We finally discussed the prognostic predictive role of ALDH18A1 in ccRCC separately and found that ALDH18A1 with high-expression was related to poor prognosis. Prior metabolomic studies have catalogued glutamine-addiction signatures in ccRCC, but none have identified the rate-limiting step that is simultaneously genetically essential for tumor growth, pharmacologically targetable with a first-in-class small-molecule (ALDH18A1i-1), and required to maintain immune privilege by sustaining kidney-resident macrophages (KRM) survival. This metabolite-mediated cross-talk is a conceptual advance over generic “M2 polarization” narratives and positions ALDH18A1 as the gatekeeper of an inter-cellular proline circuit. We show that ALDH18A1-controlled proline biosynthesis fulfills this triple criterion, thereby extending metabolic mapping from descriptive flux to mechanistic immune-metabolic dependency. In recent years, only Yu-Feng Guo found that ALDH18A1 can regulate the expression of MYCN at both transcription and post-transcription, and MYCN in turn activates ALDH18A1 and forms a positive feedback loop. It was also proved that inhibition of ALDH18A1 was required for a lower phenotype in proliferation, tumor regression and prolonged survival in the neuroblastoma xenograft model [28]. This finding is consistent with our results. Collectively, our data reposition ALDH18A1 from a “correlated metabolic enzyme” to a master regulator of a tumor-supportive proline–KRM axis, providing both a mechanistic framework and a therapeutic entry point that are not covered by previous ccRCC metabolomic or immune-microenvironment studies. While more evidence is needed to prove that ALDH18A1 promotes tumorigenesis and poor prognosis, and the underlying mechanisms also need to be investigated.

Like other studies, this study has some limitations. Firstly, the oncogenic role of GMRGs and ALDH18A1 in ccRCC was not verified in vivo or vitro. Then, mechanism of GMRGs regulating ccRCC has not been demonstrated. Further study should focus on the oncogenic role of GMRGs in ccRCC and its mechanism. However, in conclusion, we suggest that ALDH18A1 gene has the potential to be a prognostic biomarker and predict the response to immunotherapy.

Conclusion

GMRGs can well distinguish the prognosis of ccRCC patients and classify them into three subtypes. Importantly, our results indicated that ALDH18A1 gene is a crucial mediator in the suppression of ccRCC progression through the regulation of glutamine metabolism and construction of tumor microenvironment, which may serve as a prognostic biomarker and predict the response of ccRCC patients to immunotherapy.
