Lyndon Word Transduction to Improve the Efficiency of Computing Chen–Fliess Series
W. Steven Gray, Fatima Raza, Lance Berlin, Luis A. Duffaut Espinosa

Abstract
. The class of input-output systems representable as Chen–Fliess series arises often in control theory. One well known drawback of this representation, however, is that the iterated integrals which appear in these series are algebraically related by the shuffle product. This becomes relevant when one wants to numerically evaluate these series in applications, as this redundancy leads to unnecessary computational expense. The general goal of this paper is to present a computationally efficient way to evaluate these series by introducing what amounts to a change of basis for the computation. The key idea is to use the fact that the shuffle algebra on (proper) polynomials over a finite alphabet is isomorphic to the polynomial algebra generated by the Lyndon words over this alphabet. The iterated integrals indexed by Lyndon words contain all the input information needed to compute the output. The change of basis is accomplished by applying a transduction, that is, a linear map between formal power series in different alphabets, to re-index the Chen–Fliess series in terms of Lyndon monomials. The method is illustrated using a simulation of a continuously stirred-tank reactor system under a cyber-physical attack.

1.
Introduction

The class of input-output systems representable as Chen–Fliess series arises often in control theory
[
13
,
14
,
26
]
.
Their convergence properties are well understood
[
2
,
13
,
23
]
, and their structure as series of iterated integrals
makes them amenable to many forms of algebraic analysis
[
10
,
11
,
13
,
14
,
18
,
16
]
.
One well known drawback of this representation, however, is that iterated integrals are algebraically related, in the simplest instance by the
integration by parts formula, and more generally in terms of a shuffle algebra over the rational field
[
13
,
43
]
.
Therefore, such series representations contain redundant
information.
This fact becomes relevant when one wants to numerically evaluate these series in applications,
as this redundancy leads to unnecessary computational expense. Therefore, the general goal of this paper is
to present a computationally efficient way to evaluate these series by introducing what amounts to a change of
basis for the computation.

The closest related literature to the proposed problem is that for computing the signature of a path in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}

[
35
]
.
Signatures have found wide application in finance
[
1
,
33
]
and machine learning
[
7
,
36
]
.
Similar to Chen–Fliess series, they involve (formal) sums of iterated integrals, but there are some key differences:

i)

Signatures operate on paths, that is, parameterized curves that are normalized to have unit length and unit speed along the path. Chen–Fliess series
are defined on control signals, which are usually not normalized in any way.

ii)

Signatures (and their close relative
Chen series

[
45
]
) are exponential Lie series. Chen–Fliess series are generally not of this type.

iii)

Chen–Fliess series involve a generating series, namely a formal power series that can be computed from an underlying
state space model (if one exists
[
13
,
14
,
26
]
) or identified from input-output data
[
22
,
17
]
. It can also be completely arbitrary.
The coefficients of the generating series provide weights for the iterated integrals.
Signatures have no such counterpart.

There are a number of software packages available for efficiently computing
signatures
[
15
,
31
,
37
,
41
,
42
,
46
]
.
The key idea behind optimizing this computation is to use the fact that the logarithm of a signature is a Lie series in the free Lie algebra
over an alphabet
X
containing
m
letters.
There are known basis sets for this Lie algebra, for example, the Lyndon word basis
[
34
]
.
In which case, the only iterated integrals that need to be computed are those corresponding to the Lyndon words
L
over
X
, which are
considerably fewer than in the general case.
In many machine learning
applications, computing the log-signature is in fact the primary goal.
In the context of Chen–Fliess series, however, the log signature is not so
relevant an object. Nevertheless, Lyndon words are still useful in solving the proposed problem.

The main idea behind a more efficient computation of the Chen–Fliess series is to use the fact that
the (commutative) shuffle algebra on the linear space of (proper) polynomials in
X
is isomorphic to the commutative polynomial algebra
generated by the Lyndon words
L
over
X

[
24
,
25
,
34
,
40
,
43
]
.
The shuffle algebra is the graded dual of the universal enveloping algebra of the free Lie algebra on
X

[
34
]
.
It turns out that the iterated integrals indexed by Lyndon words contain all the input information needed to compute the output.
The objective therefore is to rewrite the Chen–Fliess series in terms of
an expansion indexed solely by Lyndon word monomials.
This is accomplished by applying a
transduction
from the finite alphabet
X
to the countably infinite alphabet
L
.
A transduction is a linear map between formal power series in different alphabets
[
12
,
28
,
27
,
32
,
44
]
.
In the current context, it is useful to first present a transduction as an action on the universal control systems of Kawski and Sussmann
[
30
]
.
These behave in much the same way that linear transformations act on finite dimensional state space realizations, albeit universal control systems are
not

finite dimensional.
This in turn induces a transduction on the Chen–Fliess representation of the input-output map. These
Chen–Fliess transductions

will initially be viewed as formal maps without regard to convergence. But for the particular transduction of interest from
X
to
L
,
a matrix representation will be derived in order to perform convergence analysis. In particular, it will be shown that this
transduction preserves the local convergence of the original series. It should also be noted that transductions were first used in the context of Chen–Fliess
series by Ferfera in
[
10
]
to provide a sufficient condition under which the series interconnection of two rational (i.e., bilinear) systems
remains rational. But the notion of a Chen–Fliess transduction as presented here is only implicit in this earlier work.

Finally, the proposed solution is evaluated using
the concept of
computational efficiency
. This is a relative measure of the reduction in the number of iterated integrals
needed to represent a generic input-output map up to a given word length (level in the language of signatures) in the new series expansion in terms of
L
as compared to the original series in terms of
X
. An asymptotic approximation of this measure as a function of word length is given
in order to easily assess a priori the potential benefits of applying a Chen–Fliess transduction in a given application. A number of examples are considered, including
one involving a type of cyber-physical attack on a continuously stirred-tank reactor (CSTR) system
[
20
,
3
,
29
,
39
]
.

The paper is organized as follows. In the next section, a summary of the mathematical preliminaries is given to establish the notation and the
key concepts needed for the rest of the paper.
Then, in Section
3
, the notion of a Chen–Fliess transduction is presented.
This is applied in Section
4
to rewrite Chen–Fliess series in terms of Lyndon words. Applications to improving the computational
efficiency of such series representations are described in Section
5
. The conclusions of the paper are given in the final section.

2.2.
Transductions

Let
X
and
Y
be two arbitrary alphabets.
Let
{\mathbb{R}}\langle\langle X\rangle\rangle
and
{\mathbb{R}}\langle\langle Y\rangle\rangle
be
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear spaces of formal power series over
X
and
Y
, respectively.
Any
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear mapping

\tau_{XY}:\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}\langle\langle X\rangle\rangle$}\rightarrow\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}\langle\langle Y\rangle\rangle$}
is called a

[
12
,
28
,
27
,
32
,
44
]
.
It is completely specified by

\tau_{XY}(\eta)=\sum\limits_{\xi\in Y^{*}}(\tau_{XY}(\eta),\xi)\xi,\;\;\forall\eta\in X^{*},

if for each

, the summation

(\tau_{XY}(c),\xi)=\sum_{\eta\in X^{\ast}}(c,\eta)(\tau_{XY}(\eta),\xi)

converges for all
\xi\in Y^{\ast}
.
Otherwise, it is only partially defined on
{\mathbb{R}}\langle\langle X\rangle\rangle
.
One can canonically associate with any transduction
\tau_{XY}
a series

\tau\in\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}\langle\langle X\otimes Y\rangle\rangle$}
, namely

\tau=\sum\limits_{\eta\in X^{*}}\eta\otimes\tau_{XY}(\eta)=\sum\limits_{\eta\in X^{\ast},\;\xi\in Y^{\ast}}(\tau_{XY}(\eta),\xi)\,\eta\otimes\xi.

From
\tau
define the
adjoint
transduction
\tau^{\ast}_{XY}:\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}\langle\langle Y\rangle\rangle$}\rightarrow\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}\langle\langle X\rangle\rangle$}
via

\tau^{\ast}_{XY}(\xi)=\sum\limits_{\eta\in X^{\ast}}(\tau_{XY}(\eta),\xi)\eta,\;\;\forall\xi\in Y^{*},

so that

(\tau_{XY}(\eta),\xi)=(\eta,\tau^{\ast}_{XY}(\xi)),\;\;\forall\eta\in X^{\ast},\;\xi\in Y^{\ast}.

It is easily verified that
\tau_{XY}^{\ast\ast}=\tau_{XY}
.
Finally, if
\tau_{XY}
corresponds to a canonical isomorphism between the two

{\mathbb{R}}
-linear spaces
{\mathbb{R}}\langle\langle X\rangle\rangle
and
{\mathbb{R}}\langle\langle Y\rangle\rangle
, then the inverse transduction is written as
\tau^{-1}_{XY}
.
Its dual is abbreviated by

\tau^{{\dagger}}_{XY}(\eta):=(\tau^{-1}_{XY})^{\ast}(\eta)=\sum\limits_{\xi\in Y^{\ast}}(\tau^{-1}_{XY}(\xi),\eta)\xi,\;\;\forall\eta\in X^{\ast}.

2.3.
Lyndon words and the shuffle algebra

Given an alphabet
X
with finite cardinality,
{\rm card}(X)
, a

necklace
of length
n
is an
n
-gon in an oriented plane with
n

[
43

.
Two necklaces are said to be equivalent when they can be made identical via rotation. The set of words generated by all rotations defines an equivalence class. A necklace is called

if only the trivial rotation leaves it invariant. The number of primitive necklaces

where

denotes the

, and

is the set of all divisors

of

.

Suppose the letters in

are ordered as
x_{0}<x_{1}<\cdots<x_{m}
so as to induce a lexicographical ordering on
X^{+}
. A word
\eta\in X^{+}
is called a

if all factorizations
\eta=\xi\nu
with
\xi,\nu\in X^{+}
have the property that
\eta<\nu\xi
. Each primitive necklace contains a single Lyndon word in its equivalence class
[
43
, Corollary 7.7]
.
Therefore, counting primitive necklaces of length
n
is the same as counting Lyndon words of length
n
.

.

Let

Lemma 2.1

For

(4)

\displaystyle L(n)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{n}({\rm card}(X))^{n}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{n}({\rm card}(X))^{n+1}.

Proof:
As described in Subsection

gives (

Transductions for Universal Control Systems and Chen–Fliess Series

In this section, the action of a transduction applied to a

The following theorem describes a
universal control system transduction
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume
{\mathbb{R}}\langle\langle X\rangle\rangle
and
{\mathbb{R}}\langle\langle Y\rangle\rangle
are
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear spaces over alphabets
X
and

2

\tau_{XY}
on
(V_{p},\hbox{$1$},c)

(V_{p},\hbox{$1$},c)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tau_{XY}}}{{\xrightarrow{\hskip 28.45274pt}}}(V_{\bar{p}},\bar{z}_{0},\bar{c}),

where

\displaystyle\bar{p}(\bar{z})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\tau_{XY}(x_{i}\tau^{-1}_{XY}(\bar{z}))u_{i}

\displaystyle=\tau_{XY}(z)

\displaystyle\bar{z}_{0}

\displaystyle\bar{c}

The expressions for

The next theorem describes a
Chen–Fliess transduction
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Assume
{\mathbb{R}}\langle\langle X\rangle\rangle
and
{\mathbb{R}}\langle\langle Y\rangle\rangle
are
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear spaces over alphabets
X
and
Y
, respectively,
which are related by a vector space isomorphism
\tau_{XY}
. If
c\in\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}\langle\langle X\rangle\rangle$}
, then

\displaystyle F_{c}[u]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\eta\in X^{\ast}}(c,\eta)E_{\eta}[u]

\displaystyle=F_{\bar{c}}[u]:=\sum_{\xi\in Y^{\ast}}(\tau^{\dagger}_{XY}(c),\xi)F_{\tau^{\ast}_{XY}(\xi)}[u].

Proof:
Let
(V_{p},\hbox{$1$},c)
be the universal control system (
2
) corresponding to
F_{c}
.
The claim is equivalent to saying in the context of Theorem
3.1
that
the input-output maps for
(V_{p},\hbox{$1$},c)
and
(V_{\bar{p}},\bar{z}_{0},\bar{c})
coincide on some neighborhood
W

of
1
and
\tau_{XY}(W)
of
\bar{z}_{0}
, respectively, where
\bar{z}=\tau_{XY}(z)
.
That is,
y=(c,z)=(\bar{c},\bar{z})
on their respective neighborhoods.
First observe that

\displaystyle\bar{z}

\displaystyle:=\tau_{XY}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\eta\in X^{\ast}}\tau_{XY}(\eta)E_{\eta}[u]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\eta\in X^{\ast}}\left[\sum_{\xi\in Y^{\ast}}(\tau_{XY}(\eta),\xi)\xi\right]E_{\eta}[u]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi\in Y^{\ast}}\xi F_{\sum_{\eta\in X^{\ast}}(\tau_{XY}(\eta),\xi)\eta}[u]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi\in Y^{\ast}}\xi F_{\tau_{XY}^{\ast}(\xi)}[u].

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=(c,z)

\displaystyle=(c,\tau^{-1}_{XY}\tau_{XY}(z))

\displaystyle\tau^{-1}_{XY}(\bar{z})

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu\in Y^{\ast}}(\tau^{\dagger}_{XY}(c),\nu)(\bar{z},\nu)

4.
Lyndon Word Transductions for Chen–Fliess Series

In this section, Chen–Fliess transductions are applied to rewrite
a given Chen–Fliess series in terms of Lyndon words.
That is,
{\mathbb{R}}\langle\langle Y\rangle\rangle
in Theorem
3.2
is taken to be the linear space
{\mathbb{R}}[L]
.
There is clearly more than one way to do this.
Each such map will be called a
Lyndon word transduction
.

First, the mappings
{\mathscr{L}}
and
{\mathscr{L}}^{-1}
are
described in terms of transductions without
regard to convergence issues.
Then the focus turns to two possible choices for Lyndon word transductions.
The Lyndon word transduction of particular interest has the special property that it can be used
to improve the efficiency of computing Chen–Fliess series as described in the next section.
In preparation for the convergence analysis, matrix representations of

{\mathscr{L}}
and
{\mathscr{L}}^{-1}
are then presented. Finally, the convergence properties of
Chen–Fliess series under the Lyndon word transduction of interest are developed.

4.1.
Mappings
{\mathscr{L}}
and
{\mathscr{L}}^{-1}
as transductions

The goal is to prove that
{\mathscr{L}}:\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}_{p}\langle\langle X\rangle\rangle$}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}[[L]]
,
where
\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}_{p}\langle\langle X\rangle\rangle$}\subset\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}\langle\langle X\rangle\rangle$}
is the subspace of all proper series,
is a graded
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector space isomorphism.
Hence,
{\mathscr{L}}
and
{\mathscr{L}}^{-1}
can be viewed
as transductions.
In light of the discussion above, this result is a modest refinement of what is known in the literature.
But the constructive approach used here will be useful later for providing explicit matrix
representations of
{\mathscr{L}}
and its inverse.
Henceforth, the distinction will be made between a Lyndon word
(l_{i})
as an
element in
X^{+}
and the element
l_{i}\in L
. That is, in general
(l_{i})(l_{j})\neq(l_{j})(l_{i})
,
while
l_{i}l_{j}=l_{j}l_{i}
.
The polynomial case is addressed first in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

The
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear map
{\mathscr{L}}:\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}_{p}\langle X\rangle$}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}[L]

is a graded
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector space isomorphism.

Proof:
Let
\Psi:{\mathbb{R}}[L]\rightarrow\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}_{p}\langle X\rangle$}
be the
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear map uniquely determined by

\Psi:L^{+}\rightarrow\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}\langle X\rangle$},\,l=l_{i_{1}}\cdots l_{i_{n}}\mapsto(l_{i_{1}}){\,{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 3.5pt\vrule height=5.0pt,width=0.0pt}\hrule}\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 3.5pt\vrule height=5.0pt,width=0.0pt\vrule}\hrule}}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 3.5pt\vrule height=5.0pt,width=0.0pt}\hrule}\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 3.5pt\vrule height=5.0pt,width=0.0pt\vrule}\hrule}}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 2.6pt\vrule height=3.0pt,width=0.0pt}\hrule}\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 2.6pt\vrule height=3.0pt,width=0.0pt\vrule}\hrule}}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 2.6pt\vrule height=3.0pt,width=0.0pt}\hrule}\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 2.6pt\vrule height=3.0pt,width=0.0pt\vrule}\hrule}}}}}\,}\cdots{\,{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 3.5pt\vrule height=5.0pt,width=0.0pt}\hrule}\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 3.5pt\vrule height=5.0pt,width=0.0pt\vrule}\hrule}}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 3.5pt\vrule height=5.0pt,width=0.0pt}\hrule}\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 3.5pt\vrule height=5.0pt,width=0.0pt\vrule}\hrule}}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 2.6pt\vrule height=3.0pt,width=0.0pt}\hrule}\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 2.6pt\vrule height=3.0pt,width=0.0pt\vrule}\hrule}}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 2.6pt\vrule height=3.0pt,width=0.0pt}\hrule}\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 2.6pt\vrule height=3.0pt,width=0.0pt\vrule}\hrule}}}}}\,}(l_{i_{n}}).

In light of (

\displaystyle({\mathscr{L}}\circ\Psi)(l)

\displaystyle=({\mathscr{L}}\circ\Psi)(l_{i_{1}}\cdots l_{i_{n}})

\displaystyle={\mathscr{L}}((l_{i_{1}}){\,{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 3.5pt\vrule height=5.0pt,width=0.0pt}\hrule}\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 3.5pt\vrule height=5.0pt,width=0.0pt\vrule}\hrule}}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 3.5pt\vrule height=5.0pt,width=0.0pt}\hrule}\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 3.5pt\vrule height=5.0pt,width=0.0pt\vrule}\hrule}}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 2.6pt\vrule height=3.0pt,width=0.0pt}\hrule}\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 2.6pt\vrule height=3.0pt,width=0.0pt\vrule}\hrule}}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 2.6pt\vrule height=3.0pt,width=0.0pt}\hrule}\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 2.6pt\vrule height=3.0pt,width=0.0pt\vrule}\hrule}}}}}\,}\cdots{\,{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 3.5pt\vrule height=5.0pt,width=0.0pt}\hrule}\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 3.5pt\vrule height=5.0pt,width=0.0pt\vrule}\hrule}}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 3.5pt\vrule height=5.0pt,width=0.0pt}\hrule}\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 3.5pt\vrule height=5.0pt,width=0.0pt\vrule}\hrule}}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 2.6pt\vrule height=3.0pt,width=0.0pt}\hrule}\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 2.6pt\vrule height=3.0pt,width=0.0pt\vrule}\hrule}}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 2.6pt\vrule height=3.0pt,width=0.0pt}\hrule}\vbox{\hbox{\vrule\hskip 2.6pt\vrule height=3.0pt,width=0.0pt\vrule}\hrule}}}}}\,}(l_{i_{n}}))

\displaystyle=l_{i_{1}}\cdots l_{i_{n}}

\displaystyle=l.

It is well known
that any word
\eta\in X^{+}
can be written in terms of a unique linear combination
of shuffle products of Lyndon words
[
34
,
40
]
.
That is, there exists a unique

such that

\eta=\sum_{l\in L^{+}}(q_{\eta},l)\Psi(l).

Therefore,

\displaystyle(\Psi\circ{\mathscr{L}})(\eta)

\displaystyle=(\Psi\circ{\mathscr{L}})\left(\sum_{l\in L^{+}}(q_{\eta},l)\Psi(l)\right)

\displaystyle=\Psi\left(\sum_{l\in L^{+}}(q_{\eta},l)l\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l\in L^{+}}(q_{\eta},l)\Psi(l)

\displaystyle=\eta.

That is,
{\mathscr{L}}
is injective with
{\mathscr{L}}^{-1}=\Psi
.
One cannot immediately deduce from this that
{\mathscr{L}}
is surjective since neither vector space
{\mathbb{R}}[L]
nor
{\mathbb{R}}_{p}\langle X\rangle
is finite dimensional.
However, these vector spaces are graded in terms of word length with respect to the alphabet
X
. That is, the degree of

\eta\in X^{+}
is defined to be
\left|\eta\right|
, and the degree of
l\in L^{+}
is defined by

\left|l\right|=\left|l_{i_{1}}\cdots l_{i_{k}}\right|=\left|(l_{i_{1}})\right|+\cdots+\left|(l_{i_{k}})\right|
.
Let
\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}_{p}\langle X\rangle$}=\bigoplus_{k>0}{\mathbb{R}}\langle X^{k}\rangle
be the corresponding grading of
{\mathbb{R}}_{p}\langle X\rangle
,
where
{\mathbb{R}}\langle X^{k}\rangle
denotes the
k

{\mathbb{R}}
-vector subspace spanned by the
words of degree
k
. Likewise,
{\mathbb{R}}[L]=\bigoplus_{k>0}{\mathbb{R}}[L^{k}]
is the corresponding grading of
{\mathbb{R}}[L]
,
where
{\mathbb{R}}[L^{k}]
is a
k
-dimensional subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}[L]
.
A property of the shuffle product yields the key fact that

{\mathscr{L}}
is degree preserving on the homogeneous components of these graded spaces.
Therefore,
{\mathscr{L}}

restricted to any homogeneous component of
{\mathbb{R}}_{p}\langle X\rangle
, say

{\mathscr{L}}_{k}:{\mathbb{R}}\langle X^{k}\rangle\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}[L^{k}],\;\;k>0

is injective. Since
{\mathbb{R}}\langle X^{k}\rangle
is finite dimensional, it is also surjective.
Thus,
{\mathscr{L}}
is surjective on the entire vector space
{\mathbb{R}}_{p}\langle X\rangle
.

The series case is addressed next.

Theorem 4.2
.

The
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear map
{\mathscr{L}}:\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}_{p}\langle\langle X\rangle\rangle$}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}[[L]]

is a graded
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector space isomorphism.

Proof:
The result follows from two key facts. First, the gradings on
{\mathbb{R}}_{p}\langle X\rangle
and

extend naturally to gradings on

{\mathbb{R}}_{p}\langle\langle X\rangle\rangle
and
{\mathbb{R}}[[L]]
, respectively. Second, the images of words under
{\mathscr{L}}
and
{\mathscr{L}}^{-1}
as defined above are
always polynomials. This implies that the maps
{\mathscr{L}}
and
{\mathscr{L}}^{-1}
when extended to formal power series yield series

and
{\mathscr{L}}^{-1}(c_{L})
that are locally finite, that is, every coefficient of these series is computed from a finite sum.
For example, given any

and

and using the fact that

Thus, the mappings

is bijective with

Example 4.3

Set

for

Growth bounds for
T_{k}
and
T^{-1}_{k}
are considered next.
Given a matrix
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
, define the
seminorm

{|||A|||}_{\infty}=\max_{i}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}\right|.

If the entries
A_{ij}
are all nonnegative, then the matrix norm

\left\|A\right\|_{\infty}={|||A|||}_{\infty}
.
Given any sequence of matrices
T_{k}
,
k\geq 0
with
T_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{k}\times n_{k}}
, its

growth bounds
are the triples
(K_{+},M_{+},s_{+})
and
(K_{-},M_{-},s_{-})
with
K_{\pm},M_{\pm}>0
and
s_{\pm}\in{\mathbb{R}}

such that

K_{-}M_{-}^{k}(k!)^{s_{-}}\leq{|||T_{k}|||}_{\infty}\leq K_{+}M_{+}^{k}(k!)^{s_{+}},\;\;\forall k\geq 0.

Deriving the growth bounds for the sequence of Lyndon transformation matrices and the sequence of inverses
requires two technical lemmas. Proofs for these lemmas are given
in the appendices.
The first lemma is needed for the growth bounds for
T_{k}
,
k\geq 0
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
X
be a finite alphabet. For any integer
k\geq 0
,

{\mathscr{L}}({\rm char}(X^{k}))=({\rm char}({\mathscr{L}}(X)))^{k}\frac{1}{k!}.

Theorem 4.4
.

The matrix representation
T_{k}
of the
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear map
{\mathscr{L}}_{k}
has growth bounds

\frac{1}{k!}\leq{|||T_{k}|||}_{\infty}<\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi/2}}2^{k}\frac{1}{k!},\;\;k\geq 1.

Proof:
From Lemma
4.1
,
the
\ell_{i}
-th row sum of
T_{k}
is

\displaystyle\sum_{\eta_{j}\in X^{k}}[T]_{\ell_{i},\eta_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\eta_{j}\in X^{k}}({\mathscr{L}}(\eta_{j}),\ell_{i}),\;\;\ell_{i}\in L^{k}

\displaystyle=({\mathscr{L}}({\rm char}(X^{k})),\ell_{i})

\displaystyle=(({\rm char}({\mathscr{L}}(X)))^{k},\ell_{i})\frac{1}{k!}.

Therefore, each row sum is a positive integer of the form
{k\choose n}\frac{1}{k!}
,
n\in\{0,1,\ldots,k\}
.
Thus, the maximum row sum cannot fall below
1/k!
or exceed
{k\choose\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\frac{1}{k!}
.
The integer sequence
a(k)={k\choose\lfloor k/2\rfloor}
,
k\geq 0
is OEIS sequence A001405 and is known to grow asymptotically
as
a(k)\sim 2^{k}/\sqrt{k\pi/2}
. Thus, the upper bound is proved.

As a side note, the sequence of matrix norms for the case where
{\rm card}(X)=2
is

\displaystyle\{\|T_{k}\|_{\infty},0\leq k\leq 12\}

\displaystyle=\{1,1,2,4,8,36,104,1140,9608,80956,

\displaystyle\hskip 21.68121pt1334692,37207684,7919797428\}

despite the fact that the entries in these matrices are not all integers.
At present, there is no integer sequence in the OEIS database that matches this data.
In addition, this data appears to have a growth constant
s_{+}>1
.
Thus, for the convergence analysis presented in the next section, this norm sequence is too conservative to be useful.
On the other hand, the growth bounds above for the semi-norm sequence will provide the desired convergence results.

The next lemma is needed for the growth bounds for the sequence
T^{-1}_{k}
,
k\geq 0
.
In this instance, since all entries in each

are non-negative,
{|||T_{k}^{-1}|||}_{\infty}=\left\|T_{k}^{-1}\right\|_{\infty}
for all

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{k}\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}>0\atop k=i_{1}+\cdots+i_{n}}\binom{k}{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}}.

Note that the summation over the
i_{v}
’s above assumes
i_{v}>0
since Lyndon words are never empty. In addition,
\xi_{v}\in X^{i_{v}}
may not
always be a Lyndon word. This explains the inequality in the second line. The more delicate task is to evaluate the final summation above,
which cannot be done via the usual multinomial theorem.
Instead, observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}>0\atop k=i_{1}+\cdots+i_{n}}\binom{k}{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}}

\displaystyle=(({\rm e}^{z}-1)^{n},z^{k})

\displaystyle=n!\frac{(({\rm e}^{z}-1)^{n},z^{k})}{n!}

\displaystyle=n!\left\{k\atop n\right\},

where
\left\{k\atop n\right\}
denotes a Stirling number of the second kind. Therefore,

(9)

\sum_{\ell_{j}\in L^{k}}[T^{-1}]_{\eta_{i},\ell_{j}}\leq\sum_{n=1}^{k}n!\left\{k\atop n\right\}.

The integer sequence

a(k)=\sum_{n=0}^{k}n!\left\{k\atop n\right\},\;\;k\geq 1

is called the
ordered Bell numbers
. Since
\left\{k\atop n\right\}=0
for
n=0
and
k\geq 1
, the integer sequence
in (
9
) bounding the matrix norms is simply this sequence without the first term
a(0)=1
.
The ordered Bell numbers is sequence A000670 in the OEIS database. The first few terms are

\displaystyle\{a(k):k\geq 0\}

\displaystyle=\{1,1,3,13,75,541,4683,47293,545835,7087261,102247563,\ldots\}.

The asymptotic behavior of this sequence is known to be

a(k)\sim\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\ln(2)}\right)^{k+1}k!,\;\;k\gg 1.

From this result, it can be verified that

\left\|T_{k}^{-1}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\left(\frac{1}{\ln 2}\right)^{k}k!
,
k\geq 0
.

The data suggests that
\left\|T_{k}^{-1}\right\|_{\infty}=k!
,
k\geq 0
. But the limit given above has
the geometric growth constant that
1/\ln 2=1.4426950>1
. So it remains an open problem how to verify this
tighter upper bound.

4.4.
Local convergence under Lyndon word transduction

The main result of this section is that
the Lyndon word transduction presented in Theorem
4.3

preserves local convergence. But first it is necessary to establish sufficient conditions under which
a Chen–Fliess series indexed by Lyndon monomials is locally convergent. Consider the following
theorem.

Theorem 4.6
.

Suppose
c_{L}\in\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}[[L]]$}\cup{\mathbb{R}}\emptyset
is a series with coefficients that satisfy

|(c_{L},l)|\leq K_{L}M_{L}^{\left|l\right|},\;\;\forall l\in L^{\ast}

for some real numbers
K_{L},M_{L}>0
.
Then there exists real numbers
R_{L},\Delta_{L}>0
such that
for each
u\in B_{1}^{m}(R_{L})[t_{0},t_{0}+\Delta_{L}]
, the series

y(t)=F_{c_{L}}[u](t)=\sum_{l\in L^{\ast}}(c_{L},l)\,E_{{\mathscr{L}}^{-1}(l)}[u](t,t_{0})

converges absolutely and uniformly on
[t_{0},t_{0}+\Delta_{L}]
.

Proof:
Without loss of generality, assume
t_{0}=0
and abbreviate
E_{\eta}(t,0)
by
E_{\eta}(t)
.
For any
u\in L_{1}^{m}[0,\Delta_{L}]
define
\bar{u}_{j}(t)=\left|u_{j}(t)\right|
,
j=1,2,\ldots,m
so that for all

\eta\in X^{\ast}

\left|E_{\eta}[u](t)\right|\leq E_{\eta}[\bar{u}](t),\;\;0\leq t\leq\Delta_{L}.

It is known that for any
k\geq 0
and
u\in L_{1}^{m}[0,\Delta_{L}]
satisfying
\max\{\left\|u\right\|_{1},\Delta_{L}\}\leq R_{L}
that

(10)

k!\sum_{\eta\in X^{k}}\left|E_{\eta}[u](t)\right|\leq(R_{L}(m+1))^{k},\;\;t\in[0,\Delta_{L}]

(see, for example,
[
9
]
).
Observe

\displaystyle\left|F_{c_{L}}[u](t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l\in L^{\ast}}|(c_{L},l)\,E_{{\mathscr{L}}^{-1}(l)}[u](t)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{l\in L^{k}}|(c_{L},l)|\left|E_{{\mathscr{L}}^{-1}(l)}[u](t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{l\in L^{k}}K_{L}M_{L}^{|l|}\left|E_{{\mathscr{L}}^{-1}(l)}[u](t)\right|.

Applying (
10
) and Theorem
4.5
, it follows that

\displaystyle\left|F_{c_{L}}[u](t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq K_{L}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}M_{L}^{k}\sum_{l\in L^{k}}\,\sum_{\eta\in X^{k}}({\mathscr{L}}^{-1}(l),\eta)E_{\eta}[\bar{u}](t)

\displaystyle\leq K_{L}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}M_{L}^{k}\sum_{\eta\in X^{k}}\left(\sum_{l\in L^{k}}[T^{-1}_{k}]_{\eta,l}\right)E_{\eta}[\bar{u}](t)

\displaystyle\leq K_{L}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}M_{L}^{k}\left\|T^{-1}_{k}\right\|_{\infty}\sum_{\eta\in X^{k}}E_{\eta}[\bar{u}](t)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{K_{L}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}({\rm e}M_{L}R_{L}(m+1))^{k}.

Thus, the series converges provided
{\rm e}M_{L}R_{L}(m+1)<1
, and the claims in the theorem then follow directly.

5.
Improving the Efficiency of Computing Chen–Fliess Series

In this section, the concept of computational efficiency is introduced in order
to determine a priori how beneficial a change of basis for a Fliess operator evaluation could be from
the point of view of reducing the number of integrals that need to be computed.
It is not meant to apply to a particular Fliess operator, nor is it
a precise indicator of the computational complexity of a specific integration algorithm.
Given this generality, it is nevertheless not trivial to compute
exactly. But it will be shown that reasonable estimates of this performance
parameter are possible, and they provide some guidance
in this regard. The concept will be demonstrated in the
context of a cyber-physical security application
involving a continuous stirred-tank reactor system.

5.1.
Computational efficiency

For
p\in\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}\langle X\rangle$}
, let
J_{X}(p)
be the minimum number of integrals required to compute
F_{p}
in terms of iterated integrals indexed by words over
X
.
No existing algebraic relationships between iterated integrals are used except for the fact that integrals for longer words rely on integrals for shorter words.
For example, if
p_{1}=x_{i}^{2}
and
p_{2}=x_{i}+x_{i}^{2}
, then
J_{X}(p_{1})=J_{X}(p_{2})=2
.
The worst case scenario for polynomials
of degree
n\geq 1
in
{\mathbb{R}}\langle X\rangle
is denoted by
I_{X}(n)=\max_{\deg(p)=n}J_{X}(p)
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
X
be a finite alphabet. If
p\in\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}\langle X\rangle$}
with
\deg(p)\geq 1
,
then

\deg(p)\leq J_{X}(p)\leq I_{X}(\deg(p)),

where

I_{X}(n)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}({\rm card}(X))^{k}.

In particular, if
{\rm card}(X)=1
, then
J_{X}(p)=\deg(p)
so that
I_{X}(n)=n
,
n\geq 1
. Otherwise,

(11)

I_{X}(n)=\frac{{\rm card}(X)(({\rm card}(X))^{n}-1)}{{\rm card}(X)-1},\;\;n\geq 1.

(Note this upper bound appears in
[
41
, p.7]
.)

Proof:
If
{\rm card}(X)=1
and
\deg(p)=n\geq 1
, then necessarily
p=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(p,x_{i}^{k})x_{i}^{k}
, where
X=\{x_{i}\}
and
(p,x_{i}^{n})\neq 0
.
Thus,
J_{X}(p)=\deg(p)
.
If
{\rm card}(X)>1
, then the lower bound on
J_{X}(p)
is established in an analogous fashion.
If
p={\rm char}(X^{n})
, then
J_{X}(p)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}({\rm card}(X))^{k}
. All other cases
fall between these two extremes.
The expression for
I_{X}(n)
in this case follows from a standard formula for geometric series.

Let
q\in{\mathbb{R}}[L]\cup{\mathbb{R}}\emptyset
and
J_{L}(q)
be the minimum number of integrals required to
compute

in terms of iterated integrals indexed by Lyndon words. Again, the only assumption made in the counting process is that
integrals for longer words will rely on integrals for shorter words.

.

Example 5.3
.

In applications, where usually
\deg(p)\gg 1
, it is common to have

J_{L}({\mathscr{L}}(p))\ll J_{X}(p)
.
There are, however, exceptional cases where
J_{X}(p)<J_{L}({\mathscr{L}}(p))
.
For example, if
p=x_{1}x_{0}
, then
q={\mathscr{L}}(p)=-l_{2}+l_{1}l_{0}
so that

J_{X}(p)=2<J_{L}(q)=I_{L}(2)=3
.

For any
n\geq 1
, the quantity

CE(n):=\frac{I_{X}(n)-I_{L}(n)}{I_{X}(n)}

will be called the

computational efficiency
for degree
n
.
It is clearly the relative difference between the upper bounds on
J_{X}(p)
and
J_{L}({\mathscr{L}}(p))
when
\deg(p)=n
. It will be viewed as the
maximum possible relative reduction in the number of integrals needed to compute
F_{p}[u]

as a result of applying the Chen–Fliess transduction
{\mathscr{L}}^{\dagger}
.
For example, when
{\rm card}(X)=1
, it follows that
CE(n)=1-(1/n)
for all
n\geq 1
.
For the general case, the following theorem provides bounds for
CE(n)
.

Theorem 5.2
.

If
{\rm card}(X)\geq 2
and finite, then

CE_{-}(n)\leq CE(n)\leq CE_{+}(n),\;\;n\geq 1,

where

\displaystyle CE_{-}(n)

\displaystyle:=\frac{I_{X}(n)-(nL(n)+1)}{I_{X}(n)}

\displaystyle CE_{+}(n)

\displaystyle:=\frac{I_{X}(n)-L_{+}(n)}{I_{X}(n)}.

Proof:
The claim follows from the assertion that

L_{+}(n)\leq I_{L}(n)\leq nL(n)+1,\;\;n\geq 1.

The lower bound follows from the conservative assumption that as the word length increases from
n
to
n+1
, only a single new integral needs to be computed for
each additional Lyndon word.
As shown in Example
5.1
, this happens often but not always. So in general,
L_{+}(n)
will under count
the number of required integrals as a function of
n
.
To derive the upper bound, a suboptimal scheme based on Lyndon word factorizations is used.
A well known alternative characterization of Lyndon words is that
a word
\eta\in X^{+}
of length greater than one is a Lyndon word if and only if
\eta=l_{i}l_{j}
with
l_{i},l_{j}\in L
and
l_{i}<l_{j}

[
34
, Proposition 5.1.3]
. Thus, given a Lyndon word of length
n>1
, every Lyndon word
l_{j}
will appear as a right factor of
a Lyndon word in
L(n)
with one exception, the smallest Lyndon word
l_{0}
will never appear as a right factor.
Thus, it is necessary to only consider the integrals for words in
L(n)
, each of which requires
n
integrals, and
then to add one to compensate for the missing integral
E_{x_{0}}
when
n>1
. This results in the given upper bound.

The following is an asymptotic version of Theorem
5.2
.

Theorem 5.3
.

If
{\rm card}(X)\geq 2
, then for all
n\gg 1
,

0<\widehat{CE}_{-}(n)\leq CE(n)\leq\widehat{CE}_{+}(n)<1,

where

\displaystyle\widehat{CE}_{-}(n)

Conclusions

A computationally efficient way to evaluate Chen–Fliess series was presented by introducing a change of
basis for the computation using Lyndon words.
Specifically, it was shown that one can improve the efficiency of computing the output function
using the fact that
the shuffle algebra on (proper) polynomials over a finite alphabet is isomorphic to the
polynomial algebra generated by the Lyndon words over this alphabet.
Thus, the iterated integrals indexed by Lyndon monomials contain all the input information needed to compute the output.
The change of basis was accomplished by applying a transduction
to re-index the Chen–Fliess series in terms of Lyndon monomials.
It preserves the local
convergence property of the original series.
The method was illustrated by simulating a zero dynamics attack on a continuously stirred reactor tank system.
