Predictors of survival and transplantation outcomes in Japanese patients with interstitial lung disease referred for lung transplantation: a retrospective single-center study
Authors: Takaaki Kitayama, Hideya Kitamura, Eri Hagiwara, Takashi Ogura, Jun Araya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung transplantation is a vital therapeutic option for patients with advanced interstitial lung diseases (ILDs) unresponsive to conventional treatments. In Japan, challenges for this treatment include a long waiting period (average, 2.5 year) and the absence of a comprehensive scoring system. Identifying predictors of survival and transplantation outcomes is critical for improving patient management and optimizing donor lung allocation. This study aimed to examine the clinical characteristics and outcomes of Japanese ILD patients referred for lung transplantation, focusing on factors associated with death and transplantation as competing risks.MethodsThis retrospective, single-center study included patients with ILD referred to a lung transplant clinic between April 2009 and March 2020. Patients were followed until death or study-end censoring. Diagnoses were established through multidisciplinary discussion, incorporating surgical lung biopsy, bronchoscopic examinations, and high-resolution computed tomography. Statistical analyses (R 4.4.2) treated death and transplantation as competing events. Univariate and multivariate Fine–Gray models were used to evaluate factors influencing the subdistribution hazard ratio (sHR) for death. Cumulative incidence functions for death and transplantation were compared using the Gray test.ResultsAmong 65 patients (mean age, 52.0 ± 7.9 years; 66% male), 15% underwent transplantation and 42% died before transplantation. The median time to transplantation was 585 days. Univariate Fine–Gray analysis showed that log-transformed C-reactive protein (CRP_ln) was significantly associated with increased mortality risk, whereas partial pressure of oxygen in arterial blood (PaO₂) and percent predicted diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (%DLCO) were significantly associated with low mortality risk. Multivariate analysis confirmed that CRP_ln and PaO2 independently influenced mortality. Significant differences in the cumulative incidence of death were observed for PaO₂ (cutoff, 80 Torr), %DLCO (cutoff, 40%), and home oxygen therapy. For transplantation probability, Krebs von den Lungen-6 (KL-6; cutoff, 1,400 U/mL) showed a significant difference (p = 0.03).ConclusionsIn Japanese ILD patients referred for lung transplantation, PaO₂, %DLCO, and CRP_ln were independent predictors of mortality. KL-6 was associated with transplantation probability. Background

Lung transplantation continues to expand as a critical therapeutic field, with 7,811 procedures performed worldwide in 2023 [1]. Among lung transplant recipients, the proportion of patients with interstitial lung diseases (ILDs) has steadily increased [2]. Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), the prototypical fibrotic ILD, carries a median survival of 3–5 year after diagnosis if untreated [3], making it the most severe ILD subtype. Other ILD forms may also progress to a fibrosing phenotype, mirroring the course of IPF and leading to similarly poor outcomes despite standard treatment with corticosteroids and immunosuppressive agents [4–6].

Although lung transplantation remains the only effective option for patients with advanced ILD unresponsive to medical therapy, determining the optimal timing for referral remains challenging. Several factors contribute to this difficulty: the phenotypic heterogeneity and unpredictable clinical course of ILD, prolonged waiting times for suitable organs and the need for precise size matching, and the generally poorer transplantation outcomes observed in ILD compared with other indications [7]. In Japan, the average waiting time for lung transplantation is approximately 2.5 year [8], posing a significant survival challenge for patients with advanced ILD.

In the United States, the Lung Allocation Score (LAS; 0–100) was historically used to prioritize candidates based on waitlist urgency and predicted 1-yr posttransplant survival [9]. LAS aimed to objectively stratify patient severity and optimize allocation by directing organs to those with the best expected outcomes. In March 2023, the LAS was replaced by the Lung Composite Allocation Score (CAS) under a continuous distribution framework [10]. By contrast, in Japan, the Japan Organ Transplant Network (JOTNW) recipient selection criteria remain the basis for organ allocation rather than a nationwide scoring system such as LAS or CAS. The lack of a comprehensive model incorporating diverse clinical and laboratory parameters to quantify patient severity and prognosis represents an ongoing challenge [11].

Given the scarcity of donor lungs and complexities of ILD, identifying reliable prognostic factors and predicting the risk of death during the waiting period are crucial for improving transplant outcomes and ensuring efficient donor allocation. Established physiological parameters, such as percent predicted forced vital capacity (%FVC), percent predicted diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (%DLCO), and 6-minute walk distance (6MWD), are recognized as important indicators [12]. However, investigation of additional clinical and laboratory biomarkers that may provide more precise prognostic information and support timely interventions are warranted.

This study aimed to evaluate the clinical characteristics and outcomes of ILD patients referred to a lung transplant center in Japan. Specifically, we assessed physiological and laboratory factors associated with death and transplantation events, analyzed as competing risks, to inform management strategies and optimize candidate selection in this unique clinical setting. Notably, this study focuses specifically on ILD patients referred for lung transplant evaluation, and the findings may therefore not be directly generalizable to the broader ILD population encountered in routine clinical practice.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective single-center study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kanagawa Cardiovascular and Respiratory Center, Yokohama, Japan (KCRC-20–0065; approved March 26, 2021). The requirement for patient consent was waived due to the retrospective study design. All procedures were performed in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study population

The study cohort included 65 patients with ILD who were referred from our hospital to the lung transplantation outpatient clinic at another institution between April 1, 2009, and March 31, 2020, and whose clinical course could be traced at our hospital. The observation period was censored on March 31, 2021, to ensure a minimum follow-up duration of 1 year. A total of 12 patients were excluded because they moved to another facility during the observation period and could not be followed at our institution. Patients were followed from the date of referral until death or study-end censoring. Referral to the outpatient lung transplantation clinic was made at the discretion of the attending physician. The sample size in the study was determined with reference to that of previous studies [13].

Each ILD diagnosis was established by an in-house multidisciplinary discussion, referencing findings from surgical lung biopsy, bronchoscopic examinations (including transbronchial lung biopsy, transbronchial cryobiopsy, and bronchoalveolar lavage), and high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT). All clinical and laboratory data were collected as single-point measurements at the time of initial referral to our center for lung transplant evaluation.The 6-min walk test (6MWT) was performed in accordance with American Thoracic Society guidelines [14]. Patients were categorized as receiving home oxygen therapy (HOT) if it was required at rest or during exertion. Infection status was defined as a documented history of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, mycobacterial species, or Aspergillus detection in sputum culture.

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate clinical factors associated with the occurrence of death and lung transplantation events in patients with ILD.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2 (R Core Team, 2024; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Distributions of numerical variables were graphically assessed. C-reactive protein (CRP) and Krebs von den Lungen-6 (KL-6) showed significant skewness; therefore, log-transformed values (CRP_ln and KL-6_ln) were used in subsequent analyses, as their distributions approximated normality after transformation.

Death and lung transplantation were treated as competing events, under the assumption that the occurrence of one event precludes further observation for the other. Univariate and multivariate Fine–Gray models were applied to assess the influence of clinical variables (e.g., sex, age, and laboratory values) on the subdistribution hazard ratio (sHR) for death, accounting for competing risks. For continuous variables, spline curves were used to identify potential cutoff points where the risk of death changed meaningfully. The sHR for death was then examined at various dichotomized cutoff values, and trends in the spline curve were evaluated to identify clinically relevant thresholds.

Cumulative incidence functions (CIFs) for death and transplantation were estimated, and group differences were compared using the Gray test, a nonparametric method for assessing CIF differences in the presence of competing risks. Groups were defined according to categorical variables or dichotomized continuous variables based on identified cutoff values. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Survival analysis

Univariate Fine–Gray analysis (Table 3) showed that log-transformed CRP (CRP_ln) was significantly associated with increased risk of death (sHR = 1.460; 95% confidence interval [95% CI], 1.093–1.951; p = 0.010), indicating that a one-unit increase in CRP_ln (e.g., CRP increasing from 1 to 2.718) raised the risk of death by 1.46-fold. In contrast, higher partial pressure of oxygen in arterial blood (PaO₂) was associated with a lower risk of death (sHR = 0.960; 95% CI, 0.936–0.984; p = 0.002). Similarly, %DLCO was associated with decreased mortality risk (sHR = 0.964; 95% CI, 0.935–0.992; p = 0.014). While not statistically significant, HOT use (sHR = 4.300, p = 0.062) and %FVC (sHR = 0.979, p = 0.058) demonstrated p values near the significance threshold, suggesting potential clinical importance.

Multivariate Fine–Gray models were developed to evaluate combined effects of significant univariate and clinically relevant variables (CRP_ln, PaO₂, and %DLCO) on mortality risk (Table 3). In the CRP_ln + PaO₂ model, both CRP_ln (sHR = 1.391, p = 0.028) and PaO₂ (sHR = 0.964, p = 0.009) remained independent predictors of death, indicating that elevated CRP and reduced PaO₂ independently increased mortality risk. In contrast, the CRP_ln + %DLCO model and the PaO₂ + %DLCO model did not yield statistically significant results for either variable.

Pearson correlation analysis showed a significant positive correlation between PaO₂ and %DLCO (R = 0.56, p < 0.001), supporting their clinical association but not to a degree suggestive of problematic multicollinearity. Correlations between CRP_ln and PaO₂ (R = − 0.13) and between CRP_ln and %DLCO (R = − 0.41) indicated a stronger negative relationship between CRP_ln and %DLCO. Given that models with lower inter-variable correlations are preferable for independent effect evaluation, the CRP_ln + PaO₂ model was considered the most statistically appropriate for this study.

Cumulative incidence analysis

The Gray test was applied to evaluate differences in CIFs for death and transplantation under competing risks. Significant differences in CIF for death were observed for PaO₂ (cutoff: 80 Torr, p = 0.03; Fig. 1), %DLCO (cutoff: 40%, p = 0.01; Fig. 2), and HOT use (p = 0.03; Fig. 3). For transplantation, significant differences in CIF were noted for KL-6 (cutoff: 1,400 U/mL, p = 0.03; Fig. 4).

Discussion

This study was conducted on patients with ILD who were referred for lung transplantation in Japan. Among the 65 patients included, 15% successfully underwent transplantation, whereas 42% died before transplantation. Lung cancer accounted for 11% of pre-transplant deaths, highlighting the substantial risk associated with the prolonged transplant waiting period in Japan. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, it remains unclear whether these cancers developed de novo or arose from occult lesions masked by underlying fibrosis, which is a well-recognized diagnostic challenge in advanced ILD. Regardless of when the malignancy developed, this notable incidence underscores the need for rigorous and continuous cancer screening throughout the waiting period to maintain both candidate safety and transplant eligibility.

Two key findings emerged regarding the prognosis of lung transplant candidates with ILD. First, PaO₂, %DLCO, and CRP_ln levels were associated with mortality. Notably, PaO₂ and CRP_ln were independently and significantly correlated with death. Although not statistically significant, HOT use and %FVC also suggested a possible association with mortality. Second, KL-6 with a cutoff of 1,400 U/mL appeared to influence transplantation priority. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study in Japan to specifically examine the impact of CRP and KL-6 on the competing risks of death and transplantation in ILD patients referred for lung transplantation. Unlike regions that use allocation systems such as the LAS or CAS, Japan does not have a standardized, urgency-based organ allocation mechanism. In this clinical context, our findings may offer practical guidance for physicians managing ILD when determining the optimal timing for transplant referral. Readily available markers, including KL-6, PaO2, and even mildly elevated CRP levels, may serve as important “red flags.” These parameters may help identify high-risk patients who could require expedited evaluation or closer monitoring, thereby potentially providing a data-driven link between routine clinical surveillance and timely referral for transplantation within the current Japanese system.

The International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT) recommends a 6MWD < 250 m as one of the criteria for listing ILD patients for transplantation [12]. The association between 6MWD and survival has been reported in patients with IPF and ILD [15–17]. A study by Ikezoe et al. [13] also suggested that 6MWD is a prognostic factor. However, in our study, although longer 6MWD tended to be associated with a lower risk of death, this result was not statistically significant. In addition, the mean 6MWD of our patients was considerably higher than that reported by Ikezoe et al. This discrepancy may be explained by differences in study populations: the patients in Ikezoe et al. were already listed for transplantation, whereas our cohort comprised patients referred for evaluation. The lack of statistical significance for 6MWD in this study, despite a direction of effect consistent with previous literature, may be attributable to the limited sample size or to relatively preserved functional status in a subset of patients at the time of referral. Nevertheless, the observed trend may still support the clinical importance of 6MWD as a complementary tool in the longitudinal assessment of transplant candidates. Although guidelines recommend 6MWD as an assessment tool [14], results vary across facilities due to differences in testing environments and operators [18]. The test also has limitations, including susceptibility to daily activity levels unrelated to respiratory disease. Furthermore, demographic and clinical variables—such as age, sex, height, weight, peripheral arterial disease, musculoskeletal conditions, nutritional status, and cognitive function—can influence 6MWD performance [19]. In our study, some 6MWD data were missing, whereas PaO₂ data were complete. PaO₂ was also easier to measure than %DLCO or 6MWD, suggesting its potential role as a practical predictor in evaluating candidates for transplantation.

Our findings also highlighted PaO₂ as an independent risk factor for mortality. With a cutoff of 80 Torr, PaO₂ was associated with cumulative incidence of death. In Japan’s severity classification for IIP, a PaO₂ of 80 Torr distinguishes grade I from grade II disease. It is also known that patients with PaO₂ >80 Torr who show SpO₂ desaturation during 6MWD or a decline in %DLCO have poor prognosis [20, 21]. Snyder et al. [22] similarly reported that oxygen use at rest, lower %FVC, and lower %DLCO were associated with mortality in patients with IPF. Considering the combined results of PaO₂, 6MWD, and %DLCO, early referral of eligible patients to a lung transplant center is warranted. The ISHLT consensus document recommends %FVC < 80% or %DLCO < 40% as criteria for referral [12]. In our study, a %DLCO cutoff of 40% was also significantly associated with mortality.

There is limited evidence regarding the role of CRP in lung transplantation, and clear cutoff values have not been established. Ouyang et al. [23] reported that IPF patients who died or underwent transplantation had lower high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and HDL-C/CRP ratios as well as higher CRP levels compared with surviving patients, suggesting that the HDL-C/CRP ratio may be a prognostic factor. Tsubouchi et al. [24] demonstrated that elevated serum CRP levels predicted mortality and acute exacerbations in patients with non-IPF IIP. Similarly, Stock et al. [25] found that higher CRP levels were independently associated with reduced 5-yr survival in ILD. In our study, logarithmically transformed CRP significantly increased the hazard ratio for mortality. The association between log-transformed CRP and death risk suggests that CRP is a robust prognostic marker across a wide range of values. This suggests that nontransformed CRP may also be associated with mortality risk. Using spline curve analysis, we applied a cutoff of 0.22 for CRP. Although not statistically significant at this threshold, CRP tended to be associated with the cumulative incidence of death. Although this value lies near the normal range, it may reflect a subclinical inflammatory state rather than severity of ILD itself. A single-point measurement cannot distinguish whether this elevation is due to occult disease activity, chronic infection, or a predisposition to acute exacerbation. However, our findings suggest that even mild systemic inflammation may indicate underlying physiological vulnerability in this population. Accordingly, CRP values near the normal range at the time of referral should not be overlooked and may warrant heightened clinical vigilance and optimized management during the prolonged waiting period. Our cohort may have been more clinically complex, with a higher proportion of unclassifiable IIPs than IPF, which is supported by the finding that 62% of patients required anti-inflammatory therapy. In addition, although not statistically significant, infection also tended to be associated with mortality (Fig. 6). Taken together, these findings suggest that both anti-inflammatory therapy and infection control are essential for managing ILD activity and optimizing transplant referral.

Although numerous studies have examined prognostic factors in ILD, few have evaluated predictors of transplantation achievement. In this study, spline curve analysis using a KL-6 cutoff of 1,400 U/mL revealed a significant association with transplantation events. This is consistent with prior research linking KL-6 with disease progression and acute exacerbations in ILD [26–30]. Acute exacerbation was the leading cause of death prior to transplantation in our cohort (44%). Notably, while 42% (27 patients) of the overall cohort had KL-6 > 1,400 U/mL, this proportion rose to 75% among patients who died of acute exacerbation. We speculate that ILD patients with KL-6 > 1,400 U/mL experience high disease activity, which prevents them from surviving to transplantation. Therefore, it is crucial to administer appropriate medical therapies to suppress disease activity and manage complications, thereby facilitating transplantation.

Our study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective single-center study, our findings may not be generalizable to the broader ILD population in Japan or elsewhere. This design also introduces potential recall, selection, and information biases, limits our ability to control for confounders, and precludes causal inferences. The relatively small sample size (65 patients) further reduces statistical power, making it difficult to detect subtle associations and requiring cautious interpretation of nonsignificant trends. Second, our outcome classification included an “alive without transplantation” group, whose long-term outcomes (eventual transplantation or death) could not be assessed, potentially influencing our results. Third, a considerable proportion (34%) of patients referred to our clinic were not listed for transplantation. Because we adopted the perspective of treating physicians, our analysis focused on patients referred by primary care physicians. It is worth noting that some unlisted patients were excluded due to stable disease, and depending on age and disease progression, they may require reconsideration for transplantation in the future.

Fourth, due to the retrospective nature of this study, several core prognostic domains could not be fully evaluated. Longitudinal changes in lung function and detailed histories of acute exacerbations were not consistently available in a standardized format at the time of referral. Additionally, although pulmonary hypertension was often assessed using echocardiography, right heart catheterization was not routinely performed during the initial referral phase. Therefore, these factors could not be incorporated into our multivariate analysis and may have influenced the comprehensiveness of risk assessment.

Fifth, %DLCO data were missing for approximately 9% of the cohort. Notably, five of the six patients without measurements died during follow-up, suggesting that they were likely clinically unstable to undergo testing at the time of referral. This non-random missingness may have led to an underestimation of the true prognostic impact of %DLCO in our survival analysis.

Sixth, detailed quantitative or semi-quantitative assessment of HRCT findings was not performed. While HRCT patterns were central to multidisciplinary discussions for establishing clinical diagnoses, we prioritized these discussion-based diagnoses over individual radiological patterns to avoid potential interpretative variability. The lack of detailed morphological scoring is therefore a limitation that may restrict a more granular interpretation of disease severity.

Future research should aim to clarify these uncertainties. Multicenter studies with larger cohorts are essential to validate our findings and improve generalizability. Further investigation into factors that showed suggestive but nonsignificant associations—such as HOT, %FVC, and infection—is warranted to better understand their prognostic value. In addition, long-term follow-up studies are needed to evaluate how pretransplant factors influence posttransplant outcomes, including graft survival and overall patient survival. Such work will be critical to refining patient selection criteria and improving the success of lung transplantation for ILD patients in Japan and globally.

Conclusions

The findings of this study have important clinical implications for managing and selecting ILD patients awaiting lung transplantation. In regions with prolonged waiting times, such as Japan, where donor organ availability is limited, inflammatory and physiological parameters at the time of referral—including PaO₂, %DLCO, and CRP—may serve as valuable prognostic indicators. PaO₂, %DLCO, and CRP were associated with mortality among Japanese ILD patients referred for lung transplantation, with PaO₂ and CRP independently and significantly predicting mortality. In addition, KL-6 with a cutoff of 1,400 U/mL was suggested to influence transplantation outcomes. These markers may contribute to the development of scoring models for predicting disease progression. Moreover, continuous monitoring of these biomarkers, particularly CRP and KL-6, could support personalized management strategies for ILD patients. Regular assessment may enable earlier detection of disease progression or increased disease activity, facilitating timely interventions to suppress disease activity and optimize patients’ condition for transplantation.
