Abstract
To enable Integrated Communications and Sensing (ICAS) in a peer-to-peer vehicular network, precise synchronization in frequency and phase among the communicating entities is required. In addition, self-driving cars need accurate position estimates of the surrounding vehicles. In this work, we propose a joint, distributed synchronization and localization scheme for a network of communicating entities. Our proposed scheme is mostly signal-agnostic and therefore can be applied to a wide range of possible ICAS signals. We also mitigate the effect of finite sampling frequencies, which otherwise would degrade the synchronization and localization performance severely.



I
Introduction

Vehicular networks are envisioned to be able to support Integrated Communications and Sensing (ICAS).
That is, data transmission and radar applications shall be possible using the same signal.
Such signals may be communication signals like OFDM (communication-centric ICAS)
or dedicated radar signals like FMCW or pulse radar (radar-centric ICAS).
Hence, there is a rich zoo of signals that might be considered for future ICAS applications.

In situations where there is no central coordinating unit, the
communicating entities, from now on called
hosts
, need to cooperate on a peer-to-peer (P2P) basis.
In order to allow for the most accurate radar applications, coherent signal processing is required.
[
1
]

For P2P scenarios, this means that hosts need to be synchronized exactly in both, frequency and phase.
Moreover, precise position information may be required to support self-driving applications.
However, especially in urban scenarios low-cost global navigation satellite systems (GNSS) receivers
often fail to provide exact position estimates.
[
2
,
3
]

Phase and frequency synchronization is a long-standing problem.
Consensus based algorithms as proposed in
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
, and
[
6
]

are able to solve this problem in a fully distributed manner.
However, they either neglect the propagation delay completely or do not contribute to position estimation.

This work builds on the synchronization schemes proposed in
[
6
]
and
[
4
]
.
The basis is the Kuramoto model
[
7
]
,
that is used to describe the convergence behavior of a large number of coupled oscillators.
In
[
4
]
, it has been shown that it is possible to obtain frequency consensus in a network,
that is designed to behave as a network of coupled oscillators.
While the phase relation become stable, the phases themselves will not converge to a consensus.
This problem is solved by the two-stage Kuramoto model described in
[
6
]
.
However, both works ignore the effect of finite sampling frequencies and propagation delays in real-world applications.
We close this gap making the two-stage Kuramoto method applicable to real-world scenarios.
Since our proposed method also estimates the propagation delays between the hosts,
we are able to perform mutual localization of all hosts.

This work is organized as follows:
In

In
Section
III
, we review the two-stage Kuramoto method and show how to acquire the required phase differences.

Section

In
Section

Finally,
Section
VI
concludes this work.

II-A

Signal model

We consider a number of
P
simultaneously transmitting and receiving hosts.
Each host transmits a signal

\mathrm{x}_{p}\!\left(t\right)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
,
where
p
is the index of the host.
This signal is a concatenation of
base signals

\mathrm{s}_{p,n}\!\left(u\right)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
, with
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
The actual form of the base signals
\mathrm{s}_{p,n}\!\left(u\right)
is not important
as long as the following constraints are guaranteed:

1.

A receiving host must be able to deduce the host which transmitted a base signal.

2.

A receiving host must be able to determine the reception time of each transmitted base signal
\mathrm{s}_{p,n}\!\left(u\right)
with sufficient precision.
This must hold true even if multiple hosts are transmitting simultaneously and even if the base signals overlap in time due to multipath propagation.

3.

The base signals are causal (
\mathrm{s}_{p,n}\!\left(u\right)=0
for
u<0
)
and have unit duration (
\mathrm{s}_{p,n}\!\left(u\right)=0
for
u\geq 1
).

These constraints are often already fulfilled by practically used signals.
Hence, the synchronization and localization algorithm presented later on is applicable to existing systems.

Let
T_{p,n}
be the duration of the
n
-th base signal of host
p

and let
\tau_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{start}},p}
be the time of the first base signal transmitted by host
p
.
The overall signal transmitted by host
p
is a concatenation of stretched or compressed base signals.

\displaystyle\mathrm{x}_{p}\!\left(t\right)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{s}_{p,n}\!\left(\frac{t-\tau_{p}\!\left[n\right]}{T_{p}\!\left[n\right]}\right)

(1)

\displaystyle\tau_{p}\!\left[n\right]

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\tau_{p}\!\left[n-1\right]+T_{p}\!\left[n-1\right]=\tau_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{start}},p}+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}T_{p}\!\left[k\right]

(2)

\displaystyle\tau_{p}\!\left[0\right]

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\tau_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{start}},p},\qquad T_{p}\!\left[0\right]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=T_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{start}},p}

Even though a host may send a series of different base signals,
we regard this model as a kind of quasi-periodic function where the signal duration
T_{p}\!\left[n\right]
may change with every base signal.

On the receiving side, we consider a signal model, where some host
q
receives
K_{p,q}

\mathrm{x}_{p}\!\left(t\right)
transmitted by host
p

These copies are attenuated by
\alpha_{p,q,k_{p}}

\delta_{p,q,k_{p}}
, where
k_{p}=0\ldots K_{p,q}-1
.

\displaystyle\mathrm{r}_{q}(t)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=0\\
p\neq q\end{subarray}}^{P-1}\sum_{k_{p}=0}^{K_{p,q}}\alpha_{p,q,k_{p}}\,\mathrm{x}_{p}(t-\delta_{p,q,k_{p}})

(3)

In this model,
k_{p}=0
corresponds to the line of sight (LOS) component.
We presume that
\alpha_{p,q,0}
is always sufficiently large such that
for any pair of receiving host

and transmitting host
p

p\neq q
)
the LOS component of the
n_{p,q}
-th base signal from host
p

q

can be detected and its reception time

t_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}},p,q}\!\left[n_{p,q}\right]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\tau_{p}\!\left[n_{p,q}\right]+\delta_{p,q,0}

can be estimated.

As stated above, all hosts transmit their sequences of base signals.
Every host starts to transmit at a different point in time.
Moreover, each host has its own clock which is usually not synchronized in time with the other hosts.
Our goal is to synchronize all hosts (
p=0\ldots P-1
) so that their signal durations
T_{p}\!\left[n_{p}\right]

and their base signal start times
\tau_{p}\!\left[n_{p}\right]
each converge to a common value across all hosts
as
n_{p}\to\infty
.
Additionally, we will use the synchronization process to estimate the relative positions of hosts.

II-B

Phases and phase differences

We aim to synchronize the waveforms in signal duration and in pulse start times by
utilizing the two-stage Kuramoto method.
This method relies on a notion of a
phase
, for which we will now provide a definition tailored to our signal model.

A classical approach to define a phase involves the construction of an analytical signal

\mathrm{x}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{analytic}},p}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathrm{x}_{p}(t)+\jmath\mathcal{H}\left\{\mathrm{x}_{p}(t)\right\}

using the Hilbert transform
\mathcal{H}
.
In this case, the phase would be the angle in the complex plane
\varphi_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{analytic}},p}(t)=\arg\left(\mathrm{x}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{analytic}},p}(t)\right)

However, this only works well for narrowband signals. Wideband signals could exhibit sudden spikes in
\varphi_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{analytic}},p}(t)
.
Therefore, we resort to an alternative phase definition.

Let
n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}},q}\!\left(t\right)
be the index of the base signal being transmitted by host
q
at time instant
t
.
We define the
transmit phase
of
\mathrm{x}_{q}(t)
as follows.

\displaystyle\mathrm{\varphi_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}},q}\!\left(t\right)}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=2\pi\!\left(n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}},q}\!\left(t\right)+\frac{t-\tau_{q}\!\left[n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}},q}\!\left(t\right)\right]}{T_{q}\!\left[n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}},q}\!\left(t\right)\right]}\right)

(4)

The same idea is applied to the LOS signal
\mathrm{x}_{p}(t-\delta_{p,q,0})
from host
p
to host
q
.
At some time instant
t
, let
n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)
be the index of the last base signal that host
q
received from host
p
,
Then, we define the
receive phase
of
\mathrm{x}_{p}(t-\delta_{p,q,0}(t))
as:

\displaystyle\mathrm{\varphi_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=2\pi\!\left(n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)+\frac{t-\delta_{p,q,0}-\tau_{p}\!\left[n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)\right]}{T_{p}\!\left[n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)\right]}\right)

(5)

The phase definitions presented in (
4
) and (
5
) are designed to
have two main properties:
First, the phase increases linearly from
0
to
2\pi
within each base signal.
Hence, the lengths of previous base signals do not influence the phase - only their number does.
Second, besides the factor of
2\pi
the slope of the phase within each base signals is an instantaneous frequency
(i.e., inverse of signal duration).

The synchronization and localization approach presented in this work
relies on the knowledge of a phase difference between the signal
\mathrm{x}_{q}(t)
sent by host
q

and the LOS signal
\mathrm{x}_{p}(t-\delta_{p,q,0})
sent by host
p
and received at host
q
.
Note that pure synchronization would be possible with non-LOS components as well.
Even a combination of multiple components may be possible.
However, only the LOS allows us to estimate the distances between each host
and therefore render localization possible.

Formally, the phase difference
\tilde{\mathrm{\psi}}_{p,q}\!\left(t\right)
between hosts
q
and
p
at time instant
t
could be defined as

\tilde{\mathrm{\psi}}_{p,q}\!\left(t\right)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathrm{\varphi_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}},q}\!\left(t\right)}-\mathrm{\varphi_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)}.

Unfortunately,
\mathrm{\varphi_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)}
includes the signal duration
T_{p}\!\left[n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)\right]
of host
p
,
which is not known at host
q
.
One solution would be to use the previous signal duration of host
p
estimated by host
q
.
Another approach presented in
[
4
]
simplifies the phase difference by assuming that host
q
’s signal duration
is roughly equal to host
p
’s signal duration leading to the approximation

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathrm{\psi}}_{p,q}\!\left(t\right)\approx\,2\pi\left(n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\Delta}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)+\frac{\tau_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\Delta}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)+\delta_{p,q,0}}{T_{q}\!\left[n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}},q}\!\left(t\right)\right]}\right),

(6)

where

\displaystyle\tau_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\Delta}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\,

\displaystyle\tau_{p}\!\left[n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)\right]-\tau_{q}\!\left[n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}},q}\!\left(t\right)\right]

\displaystyle n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\Delta}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\,

\displaystyle n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}},q}\!\left(t\right)-n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}},p,q}\!\left(t\right).

In order to improve the accuracy of the phase difference estimate, a sampling factor
N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}}
is introduced in
[
4
]
,
that includes multiple base signals into a single effective phase difference
\mathrm{\psi}_{p,q}\!\left(t\right)
.

\displaystyle\mathrm{\psi}_{p,q}\!\left(t\right)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\,\frac{2\pi}{N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}}}\left(n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\Delta}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)+\frac{\tau_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\Delta}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)+\delta_{p,q,0}}{T_{q}\!\left[n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}},q}\!\left(t\right)\right]}\right)

(7)

III
Synchronization and localization

In this section, we review the two-stage Kuramoto method.
After that, we show how we can estimate propagation delays
based on the phase differences defined in (
7
).

III-A

The two-stage Kuramoto method

The two-stage Kuramoto method proposed in
[
6
]

can be used to synchronize hosts in frequency and phase.
This method assumes that each host transmits at an instantaneous frequency
f_{q}(t)
, with
q=0\ldots P-1
,
that can be decomposed into a base frequency
f_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{b}},q}(t)
and
into an update term that depends on a phase differences
\varDelta_{q,n_{q}}(t)
.

The first stage consists of a simple consensus filter which updates the base frequency.

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}f_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{b}},q}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\epsilon\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=0\\
p\neq q\end{subarray}}^{N-1}\left(f_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{b}},q}(t)-f_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{b}},p}(t)\right)

(8)

The second stage describes the evolution of the instantaneous frequency
f_{q}(t)
.
It resembles a first-order Kuramoto model with the intrinsic natural frequency being replaced by the base frequency
f_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{b}},q}(t)
.

\displaystyle f_{q}(t)=f_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{b}},q}(t)-\varepsilon\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=0\\
p\neq q\end{subarray}}^{P-1}\sin\left(\varDelta_{q,n_{q}}(t)\right)

(9)

The authors of
[
6
]
could show that their two-stage Kuramoto method is capable of synchronizing the frequencies and the phases of a number of entities in a network.
In the following sections, we will extend this method to add localization capabilities.
Moreover, we will extend the first stage such that the method works also in the presence of sampling effects.

III-B

Estimating transmit times and propagation delays

The phase difference
\mathrm{\psi}_{p,q}\!\left(t\right)
defined in (
7
) includes the LOS propagation delay
\delta_{p,q,0}
.
Hence, we cannot use
\mathrm{\psi}_{p,q}\!\left(t\right)
directly for frequency and phase synchronization.

To combat this, we decompose
\mathrm{\psi}_{p,q}\!\left(t\right)
into a phase difference
\mathrm{\psi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\tau}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)
that only
depends on the base signal start times and into a phase difference
\mathrm{\psi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\delta}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)

that includes the propagation delay:

\displaystyle\mathrm{\psi}_{p,q}\!\left(t\right)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathrm{\psi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\tau}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)+\mathrm{\psi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\delta}},p,q}\!\left(t\right),

(10)

where

\displaystyle\mathrm{\psi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\tau}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\,

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi}{N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}}}\left(n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\Delta}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)+\frac{\tau_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\Delta}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)}{T_{q}\!\left[n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}},q}\!\left(t\right)\right]}\right)

(11)

\displaystyle\mathrm{\psi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\delta}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\,

\displaystyle 2\pi\frac{\delta_{p,q,0}}{N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}}T_{q}\!\left[n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}},q}\!\left(t\right)\right]}

(12)

The joint synchronization and localization algorithm described in the following section
requires knowledge of

\mathrm{\psi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\tau}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)
and
\mathrm{\psi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\delta}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)
.
Hence, we will now analyze, how the decomposition given in (
10
) can be computed.
To this end, we rewrite (
10
) in terms of matrices as

\displaystyle\bm{\varPsi}=\ \bm{\varPsi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\tau}}}+\bm{\varPsi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\delta}}},

(13)

where

\displaystyle\left[\bm{\varPsi}(t)\right]_{p,q}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\,

\displaystyle\mathrm{\psi}_{p,q}\!\left(t\right),

\displaystyle\left[\bm{\varPsi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\tau}}}(t)\right]_{p,q}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\,

\displaystyle\mathrm{\psi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\tau}},p,q}\!\left(t\right),

\displaystyle\left[\bm{\varPsi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\delta}}}(t)\right]_{p,q}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\,

\displaystyle\mathrm{\psi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\delta}},p,q}\!\left(t\right).

For notational convenience, we will omit writing the dependency of these matrices on the time
t
.

Suppose that no base signal is lost during transmission.
That is, all base signals transmitted by one host are eventually received by all other hosts.
In this case, the number of transmitted base signals
n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}},p}\!\left(t\right)
equals the number of received base signals
n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)
.
It follows that
n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\Delta}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)=-n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\Delta}},q,p}\!\left(t\right)
.
In the limit of perfectly synchronized pulse start times (
\tau_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\Delta}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)=0
),
it holds that
\mathrm{\psi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\tau}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)=-\mathrm{\psi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\tau}},q,p}\!\left(t\right)
.
Hence,
\bm{\varPsi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\tau}}}
becomes a skew-symmetric matrix
(
\bm{\varPsi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\tau}}}=-\bm{\varPsi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\tau}}}^{\mathrm{T}}
).

Next, we exploit the fact that propagation delays are the same in both directions
(
\delta_{p,q,0}\!=\!\delta_{q,p,0}
).
In the limit of perfectly synchronized pulse durations
(
T_{p}\!\left[n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}},p}\!\left(t\right)\right]\!=\!T_{q}\!\left[n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}},q}\!\left(t\right)\right]
)
the phase differences
\mathrm{\psi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\delta}},p,q}\!\left(t\right)
and
\mathrm{\psi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\delta}},q,p}\!\left(t\right)
become equal.
Hence,
\bm{\varPsi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\delta}}}
becomes a symmetric matrix
(
\bm{\varPsi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\delta}}}=\bm{\varPsi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\delta}}}^{\mathrm{T}}
).

Finally, consider the case where both, the base signal start times and signal durations, are sufficiently synchronized,
such that

\bm{\varPsi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\tau}}}\approx-\bm{\varPsi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\tau}}}^{\mathrm{T}}

and

\bm{\varPsi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\delta}}}\approx\bm{\varPsi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\delta}}}^{\mathrm{T}}
.
In this case, the decomposition given in (
13
) can be estimated as follows.

\displaystyle\bm{\varPsi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\tau}}}\approx

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\bm{\varPsi}-\bm{\varPsi}^{\mathrm{T}}\right),

\displaystyle\bm{\varPsi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\delta}}}\approx

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\bm{\varPsi}+\bm{\varPsi}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)

(14)

IV-B

Distribution of estimated phase differences

During the synchronization process, each host
p
estimates a vector
\bm{\hat{\psi}}_{p}
of phase differences
using (
7
).
This vector is transmitted to all other hosts either in-band (modulated on the base signals) or via a separate communication channel (out-of-band).
Note that, in order to save network resources, this vector may be transmitted less frequently than the base signals.

When a host
q
receives such a vector from host
p
, host
q
updates the
p
-th column
of its estimated phase difference matrix
\bm{\hat{\varPsi}}_{q}

(host
q
’s estimate of
\bm{\varPsi}
).

\displaystyle\left[\bm{\hat{\varPsi}}_{q}\right]_{\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}},p}\leftarrow\,\bm{\hat{\psi}}_{p}

(15)

The
q
-th column of
\bm{\hat{\varPsi}}_{q}
is updated upon reception of base signals
a described in the next subsection.

IV-C

Base signal reception

Upon reception of a base signal from some host
p
at time instant
\hat{t}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}},p,q}
,
host
q
estimates the phase difference between itself and host
p
.
The estimated phase difference is stored in the matrix
\bm{\hat{\varPsi}}_{q}
.
Based on (
7
), the phase difference is estimated as

\displaystyle\left[\bm{\hat{\varPsi}}_{q}\right]_{p,q}\leftarrow\,\frac{2\pi}{N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}}}\left(n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\Delta}}}+\frac{\tau_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\Delta}}}}{T_{q}\!\left[n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}}}\right]}\right),

(16)

where

\tau_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\Delta}}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\hat{t}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}},p,q}-t_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}},q}

and

n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\Delta}}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}},q}-n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}},p,q}
.

In order to conduct the synchronization, host
q
needs to estimate the signal durations of all other hosts.
To this end, every time host
q
receives a base signal from host
p
,
it updates its estimate of host
p
’s signal duration
\hat{T}_{p,q}

using the current reception time
\hat{t}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}},p,q}
and the previous reception time
\hat{t}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{LR}},p,q}
.

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{p,q}\leftarrow\,\hat{t}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}},p,q}-\hat{t}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{LR}},p,q}

IV-D

Localization

Once all columns of host
q
’s phase difference matrix
\bm{\hat{\varPsi}}_{q}
are known,
host
q
can estimate the phase matrix
\bm{\hat{\varPsi}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\delta}},q}

according to (
14
). After this, host
q
can utilize the
propagation delays in
\bm{\hat{\varPsi}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\delta}},q}
to estimate relative
positions of all other hosts using techniques like multidimensional scaling
[
8
,
9
]
.

IV-E

Synchronization

The synchronization step estimates the duration
T_{q}\!\left[n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}},q}+1\right]
of
the next base signal to be transmitted by host
q
.
This step requires that host
q
has estimated the signal durations
\hat{T}_{p,q}

of all other hosts
p
as well as the
q
-th column of
\bm{\hat{\varPsi}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\tau}},q}
.
The matrix
\bm{\hat{\varPsi}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\tau}},q}
may either be computed
via (
14
) or by subtracting
\bm{\hat{\varPsi}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\delta}},q}

from
\bm{\hat{\varPsi}}_{q}
.

First stage of Kuramoto method

This sub-step corresponds to the first stage of the two-stage Kuramoto method presented in
[
6
]

with an additional penalty term to prevent drift of the signal durations.

In this step, host
q
computes a base frequency
f_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{b}},q}

using its estimated signal durations
\hat{T}_{p,q}
,
where
\hat{T}_{q,q}
shall be set to host
q
’s own current signal duration.

\displaystyle f_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{b}},q}\leftarrow\,\frac{1}{P}\sum_{p=0}^{P-1}{\hat{T}_{p,q}}^{-1}-\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{a}}{}\varDelta_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{a}},q}{t}

(18)

The quantity
\varDelta_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{a}},q}
is a penalty that comes into play if the signal durations of all
hosts jointly drift away. For this, host
q
has to choose an anchor frequency
f_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{a}},q}
.

\displaystyle\varDelta_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{a}},q}\leftarrow\,\frac{1}{P}\sum_{p=0}^{P-1}{\hat{T}_{p,q}}^{-1}-f_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{a}},q}

(19)

The anchor frequency
f_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{a}},q}
is assumed to be constant over the synchronization period.
In this work, we chose
f_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{a}},q}
to be the average over the initial signal durations

\hat{T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{start}},p,q}
,
where
\hat{T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{start}},p,q}
is host
q
’s estimate of the true initial pulse duration
T_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{start}},p}
.

\displaystyle f_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{a}},q}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\,\frac{1}{P}\sum_{p=0}^{P-1}{\hat{T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{start}},p,q}}^{-1}

(20)

The factor
\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{a}}
should be a small positive value close to zero.

Second stage of Kuramoto method

The first stage of the Kuramoto method achieves synchronization of the signal durations
T_{q}\!\left[n\right]
.
In order to synchronize the base signal start times
\tau_{q}\!\left[n\right]
we use the second stage of the two-stage Kuramoto method,
which defines the signal duration
T_{q}\!\left[n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}},q}+1\right]
used for the next base signal.

\displaystyle T_{q}\!\left[n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}},q}+1\right]\leftarrow\,\left(f_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{b}},q}-\frac{K}{P}\sum_{p=0}^{P-1}\sin\!\left(\left[\bm{\hat{\varPsi}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\tau}},q}\right]_{p,q}\right)\right)^{\text{-}1}

(21)

Here, the main diagonal of
\bm{\hat{\varPsi}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{\tau}},q}
is defined to be zero.

The improved synchronization affects the localization accuracy (

Here, the relative error does not approach zero as in scenario A,
but the position estimates have become stable and much more reliable compared to scenario B.

VI
Conclusion

In this work, we extended the two-stage Kuramoto method for the synchronization of hosts in a wireless network to also support localization capabilities.
We could show that the two-stage Kuramoto method is indeed capable of providing enough information to jointly localize the hosts.
However, we demonstrated that real-world effects like sampling can significantly deteriorate the performance of both, the synchronization and the localization.
To this end, we proposed a simple mechanism to mitigate the negative effects of finite sampling frequencies.
The mitigation improved the frequency and phase synchronization significantly and rendered the localization much more reliable.
