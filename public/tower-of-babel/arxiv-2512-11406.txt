Network Estimation for Stationary Time Series
Madeline A. Shelley, Chiara Boetti, Marina I. Knight, Matthew A. Nunes

Abstract
High-dimensional multivariate time series are common in many scientific and industrial applications, where the interest lies in identifying key dependence structure within the data for subsequent analysis tasks, such as forecasting. An important avenue to achieve this is through the estimation of the conditional independence graph via graphical models, although for time series data settings the underpinning temporal dependence can make this task challenging. In this article, we propose a novel wavelet domain technique that allows the data-driven inference of the (sparse) conditional independence graph of a high-dimensional stationary multivariate time series. By adopting the locally stationary wavelet modelling framework, we repose the estimation problem as a well-principled wavelet domain graphical lasso formulation. Theoretical results establish that our associated estimation scheme enjoys good consistency properties when determining sparse dependence structure in input time series data. The performance of the proposed method is illustrated using extensive simulations and we demonstrate its applicability on a real-world dataset representing hospitalisations of COVID-19 patients.



Keywords:
Multivariate time series; graphical models; wavelets; network estimation.

Introduction

In the statistical literature, graphical models are powerful tools to capture key dependence structures within multivariate data through the conditional independence graph (
\mathcal{CIG}
): given a collection of random variables, one wishes to assess the relationship between two variables, conditioned on the remaining variables. In the classical Gaussian graphical model literature, undirected graphs represent such dependencies among variables: each node is a variable, and the absence of an edge between two nodes encodes conditional independence between their corresponding variables given all other nodes (variables). For high-dimensional data, it is often desirable to impose sparsity in the graphical structure of the covariance
(
25
)
, as driven by applications in various settings, spanning genetics
(
60
)
, biology
(
33
;
50
)
, economics
(
10
)
, neurosciences
(
16
;
29
)
, and environmental science
(
18
)
. For comprehensive statistical reviews of graphical models, see e.g.,
41
and
43
.

Improvements in modern data collection methods have resulted in large multivariate time series being routinely collected across many fields. Consequently, the estimation of conditional independence structures in such data has received growing attention in recent years
(
8
;
26
;
34
;
12
;
13
)
. The work in this article is in particular motivated by the analysis of spatially-observed hospitalisations, namely time series representing COVID-19 ventilation bed occupancy rates at 81 hospitals across the UK from April 2020 to July 2021. In this context, it is of interest to model and e.g., forecast the data in order to inform staffing resource needs. The data show clear spatial and temporal dependence (see Figure
1
), but due to the dimension of the multivariate time series, analysis is challenging without the use of sparse modelling frameworks
(
48
)
. Thus, being able to identify the key dependencies within the data in a principled manner is a vital initial aspect of analysis.

By direct analogy to the classical setting, the dependencies in multivariate time series processes are mapped onto the Fourier spectral domain
(
8
;
12
)
. Specifically, missing edges in the
\mathcal{CIG}
correspond to zeros in the inverse spectral density (precision) matrix across all frequencies.
Estimating the inverse spectral density matrix, however, poses significant challenges
(
20
)
. A naive approach involves computing the smoothed periodogram—as it is a consistent estimator of the spectral density matrix—and subsequently inverting it. This strategy is suboptimal since the smoothed estimator often becomes ill-conditioned in high-dimensional settings or when the number of available observations is limited
(
6
)
. As a result, its inversion can severely amplify estimation errors, leading to inaccurate graphical structure inference.

These difficulties have motivated the development of two broad classes of regularized estimators. The first class of methods involves shrinking the spectral density matrix, either before or after inversion
(
21
;
22
;
23
;
56
)
. While computationally straightforward, such techniques do not directly promote sparsity in the precision matrix, potentially obscuring the most informative conditional dependencies. The second class leverages the property that, for second-order stationary processes, the discrete Fourier coefficients at distinct frequencies are asymptotically independent and follow a complex-valued Gaussian distribution. This enables the extension of Gaussian graphical modelling frameworks to the frequency domain, yielding sparse and well-defined estimates of the precision matrix. We refer to this methodology as the
time series graphical lasso
.
In this context,
34
developed an estimation algorithm to learn the graphical dependence structure of time-dependent data. Later work extended this approach
(
13
;
58
)
and studied related settings, including cases where the data exhibit long memory
(
4
)
or complex-valued time series models
(
14
)
.

While these approaches naturally extend graphical models to time series, they all rely on the Fourier representation of the process. In this article we propose a wavelet domain approach to conditional independence graph estimation, motivated by the observation that wavelet-based time series decompositions can provide improvements over Fourier-based methods in a range of analysis tasks, even in the stationary time series setting. More precisely, we adopt the
locally stationary wavelet
(LSW) modelling framework introduced by
47
and extended to the multivariate setting by
51
, in which the wavelet spectral matrix captures process dependence through a time-scale representation of a multivariate time series.

Similar to Fourier-based methodology, the inverse of the wavelet spectrum matrices can be used for the inference of direct and indirect associations among the components of the process across both time and scale. Building on this,
27
introduced an algorithmic sparse estimation method for the inverse local wavelet spectrum based on an estimator of the spectrum;
however, their work does not consider the true distributional properties of the wavelet coefficients associated to the process when finding an optimal inverse spectrum, leading to an unprincipled estimation scheme.

In this article we develop an alternative approach, the
wavelet-based time series graphical lasso
framework for multivariate stationary time series, by considering the wavelet precision matrix and properly accounting for the distributional properties associated to the spectral matrix estimator. Our methodology allows sparse estimation of the inverse wavelet spectra and aids the recovery of a multi-resolution decomposition of the conditional independence graph structure. Our proposed approach shows improved recovery of the true
\mathcal{CIG}
compared to both Fourier-based techniques in the literature and to the proposal of
27
, particularly for challenging setups.

In addition to estimation of the
\mathcal{CIG}
for stationary time series, we also develop a tool for identifying the underlying graph, or network, in so-called network time series models.
Originally proposed by
39
, Generalized Network Autoregressive (GNAR) processes model autoregressive dynamics while explicitly exploiting inter-dependencies encoded by a given graph,
\mathcal{N=(K,E^{N})}
, via nodal neighbourhoods (subsets of
\mathcal{K}
) as determined through the presence / absence of edges in
\mathcal{E^{N}}
. Focusing on relevant connections was shown to improve parameter estimation and forecasting accuracy, as well as to enhance computational efficiency
(
40
)
.
However, when the underlying graph is unknown (i.e., unknown edge set
\mathcal{E^{N}}
), determining it from the data is a crucial first modelling task
(
31
)
. One way to approach this issue is to fit network time series models on several candidate graphs and to select the one with the smallest prediction error
(
40
)
. This strategy, however, identifies a graph that aids prediction, which may differ from the graph that best represents the data-generating structure.
Hence in this work we also propose a data-driven procedure that infers the graph
\mathcal{N}
directly from the dependence structure
\mathcal{CIG}
estimated by our wavelet-based time series graphical lasso, by means of an edge-clustering approach.

This article is organised as follows. Section
2
introduces the time series graphical lasso approach based on Fourier decomposition and provides an overview of the multivariate locally stationary process model of
51
upon which we build our proposed wavelet-based
\mathcal{CIG}
recovery methodology. Our proposed wavelet-based time series graphical lasso (to which we shall refer under the acronym
WavTSglasso
) is described in Section
3
, which also addresses its related theoretical properties. We demonstrate the efficacy of our estimation procedure by extensive simulation studies in Section
4
, while Section
5
proposes the discovery of the underlying graph for GNAR-type models via edge-wise clustering. Finally, Section
6
presents an application to a real dataset regarding COVID-19 patients hospitalisations, and illustrates the benefit of using the data-driven discovered network in a forecasting task.

2
Background

In this section, we review the time series graphical lasso (TSglasso) for multivariate stationary time series as developed by
34
, as well as giving a brief overview of multivariate locally stationary wavelet processes
(
51
)
. These two aspects provide the necessary background to formulate our proposed modelling framework in Section
3
.

In what follows, we consider a
P
-dimensional, zero-mean stationary Gaussian process

\mathbf{X}=\{\mathbf{X}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
, where
\mathbf{X}_{t}=\left(X^{(1)}_{t},\ldots,X^{(P)}_{t}\right)^{\top}
is observed at time
t
across
P
channels.

Our aim is to characterise the interactions among the
P
components of the process
\mathbf{X}
by means of its underlying conditional independence graph
\mathcal{CIG}=\left(\mathcal{K},\mathcal{E}\right)
, with node set
\mathcal{K}=\left\{1,\ldots,P\right\}
and undirected edge set

\mathcal{E}=\left\{(p,q)\in\mathcal{K}\times\mathcal{K},\,p\neq q:p\leftrightsquigarrow q\right\}
, where the edge
p\leftrightsquigarrow q
indicates conditional dependence between channels
p
and
q
. In particular, we denote by
\mathbf{G}
the
P\times P
-dimensional adjacency matrix of the
\mathcal{CIG}
, with entries
\mathbf{G}_{p,q}=1
if
(p,q)\in\mathcal{E}
, and
\mathbf{G}_{p,q}=0
otherwise. By definition,
\mathbf{G}
is symmetric with zeros on the diagonal.

The
\mathcal{CIG}
can be determined from the process spectral properties, as follows.
Let
\mathbf{f}(\omega)\in\mathbb{C}^{P\times P}
be the spectral density matrix of
\mathbf{X}
at frequency
\omega\in[0,1]
, and consider the precision matrix function
\mathbf{Q}(\omega):=\mathbf{f}^{-1}(\omega)
.
12
shows that the channel
p,\,q
processes,
\{X_{t}^{(p)}\}
and
\{X_{t}^{(q)}\}
, are independent conditional on all remaining components if and only if
\mathbf{Q}_{p,q}(\omega)=0,\forall\omega
, thus rendering a bijective characterisation of the edge set
\mathcal{E}
.

2.1
Fourier-based Time Series Graphical Lasso (TSglasso)

Let us now consider the task of estimation of the conditional dependencies in the data. Denote the
P
-dimensional Fourier coefficients of
\mathbf{X}
by
\left\{\mathbf{d}(\omega_{n})\right\}_{n=0}^{T-1}
, obtained by applying the normalised discrete Fourier transform to
\mathbf{X}
, where
\omega_{n}=\tfrac{n}{T}
.
Under the assumption that
\mathbf{X}
has an absolutely summable autocovariance function,
9
showed that, as
T\to\infty
,
\mathbf{d}(\omega_{n})\dot{\sim}N^{\mathbb{C}}_{P}\left(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{f}(\omega_{n})\right)
independently for
n=1,\ldots,\lfloor\tfrac{T}{2}\rfloor-1
. Moreover, for
n=0,\lfloor\tfrac{T}{2}\rfloor
, we have
\mathbf{d}(\omega_{n})\dot{\sim}N_{P}\left(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{f}(\omega_{n})\right)
though these coefficients are typically ignored. Here, the notations
\dot{\sim}N^{\mathbb{C}}_{P}
and
\dot{\sim}N_{P}
indicate that the variables are approximately distributed as complex- and real-valued Gaussian, respectively.

Using standard Fourier spectral estimation that employs local periodogram smoothing over
M
consecutive aggregated frequencies
\tilde{\omega}_{l}=\frac{(l-1)L+m_{t}+1}{T}
for
l=1,\ldots,M
, with
M=\lfloor\tfrac{T/2-m_{t}-1}{L}\rfloor
the number of frequencies after smoothing, half-window length
m_{t}
, and
L=2m_{t}+1
, then the smoothed sample spectral density is defined as

\tilde{\mathbf{f}}(\tilde{\omega}_{l})=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{k=-m_{t}}^{m_{t}}\mathbf{d}(\tilde{\omega}_{l,k})\mathbf{d}^{H}(\tilde{\omega}_{l,k}),

with
\tilde{\omega}_{l,k}=\frac{(l-1)L+m_{t}+1+k}{T}
,
k=-m_{t},\ldots,m_{t}
, denoting the
L
disjoint frequencies in the
l
-th window, and
{\cdotp}^{H}
denoting the Hermitian transpose.

As usual in the time series Fourier spectral domain literature, following from the (complex-valued) Gaussian distributions of the Fourier coefficients and the sample spectral density matrix above, the log-likelihood function for the precision matrix
\mathbf{Q}(\cdot)
can be written as

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{Q}(\cdot))=\sum_{l=1}^{M}L\left[\log\det\left(\mathbf{Q}(\tilde{\omega}_{l})\right)-\text{tr}\left(\mathbf{\tilde{f}}(\tilde{\omega}_{l})\mathbf{Q}(\tilde{\omega}_{l})\right)\right],

(1)

and in the spirit of the graphical lasso, a sparse estimator
\hat{\mathbf{Q}}(\cdot)
may be obtained by solving the regularised optimisation problem

\min_{\mathbf{Q}(\cdot)}\left\{-\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{Q}(\cdot))+\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{Q}(\cdot),\lambda)\right\},

(2)

where in the above equations
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{Q}(\cdot),\lambda)
is a sparsity inducing penalty function and
\text{tr}(\mathbf{A})
denotes the trace operator applied to the matrix
\mathbf{A}
. For example,
34
use an
\ell_{1}
-penalty,
58
defines a sparse group penalty, whilst
13
explore an
\ell_{2}
-penalty in a two-stage sparse estimation procedure for vector autoregressive processes.
The optimisation problem (
2
) is typically solved using the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM)
(
7
)
, exploiting variable splitting and the separability of the objective function across frequencies
\{\tilde{\omega}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{M}
. Because
\mathbf{Q}(\cdot)
is complex-valued, Wirtinger calculus is used to derive suitable ADMM update steps while incorporating the assumption of smooth variation across nearby frequencies
(
13
)
. This translates into choosing an appropriate smoothing span
L
in (
1
).

A further step in the TSglasso framework is how to infer the adjacency matrix
\mathbf{G}
from the estimated precision matrix functions. Indeed, note that solving (
2
) yields
M
estimates, i.e.,
\hat{\mathbf{Q}}(\tilde{\omega}_{l})\in\mathbb{C}^{P\times P}
for all
\tilde{\omega}_{l}
for
l=1,\ldots,M
. Techniques in the literature aggregate these estimates in different ways to form an estimated adjacency,
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
. For example,
13
set
\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{p,q}=0
, i.e., no edge between nodes
p
and
q
, iff
\hat{\mathbf{Q}}_{p,q}(\tilde{\omega}_{l})=0
for all
l
. However, this approach can reduce sparsity: small nonzero entries at some frequencies will still result in edges in the final graph.
As an alternative,
58
determines the presence of an edge between the
p
-th and
q
-th in the
\mathcal{CIG}
if the
\ell_{2}
-norm of the vector
\left(\hat{\mathbf{Q}}_{p,q}(\tilde{\omega}_{1}),\dots,\hat{\mathbf{Q}}_{p,q}(\tilde{\omega}_{M})\right)
is different from zero.

To preserve sparsity, in our TSglasso implementation we summarise the
M
estimates using the entrywise
\ell_{1}
-norm for each
p,q=1,\ldots,P
,

\overline{\mathbf{Q}}_{p,q}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{l=1}^{M}|\hat{\mathbf{Q}}_{p,q}(\tilde{\omega}_{l})|,

where
\overline{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P\times P}
is the resulting matrix. We then form
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
by setting the corresponding entries to 1 where
\overline{\mathbf{Q}}
is non-zero. In practice, to account for numerical noise, we set
\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{p,q}=0
if
|\overline{\mathbf{Q}}_{p,q}|<c
, where we use
c=10^{-5}
as in
13
.

Tuning Parameter Selection.

The parameter
\lambda
of the penalty term
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{Q}(\cdot),\lambda)
in the TSglasso problem (
2
) controls the sparsity of the estimated precision matrix
\mathbf{Q}(\cdot)
. Traditionally,
\lambda
is selected by minimizing the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC)
(
3
)
, the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC)
(
57
)
, or the extended Bayesian Information Criterion (eBIC)
(
11
)
.
An alternative is cross-validation (CV), as suggested by
45
, however, we do not use it in this work since it is more computationally expensive and it underperforms when compared to information criteria in practical tasks.

By denoting the frequency-specific terms
\mathcal{L}\big(\mathbf{Q}(\tilde{\omega}_{l})\big):=\log\det\left(\mathbf{Q}(\tilde{\omega}_{l})\right)-\text{tr}(\mathbf{\tilde{f}}(\tilde{\omega}_{l})\mathbf{Q}(\tilde{\omega}_{l}))
in (
2
) for each
l
,
58
and
13
define the BIC as

\operatorname{BIC(\lambda)}=-2L\sum_{l=1}^{M}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{Q}\big(\tilde{\omega}_{l})\big)+\log(2LM)\sum_{l=1}^{M}E_{l},

where
E_{l}
is the number of non-zero elements in
\mathbf{Q}(\tilde{\omega}_{l})
.
Similarly, the AIC and eBIC
(
13
)
are

\operatorname{AIC}(\lambda)=-2L\sum_{l=1}^{M}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{Q}\big(\tilde{\omega}_{l})\big)+2LM\sum_{l=1}^{M}E_{l},

and

\operatorname{eBIC}(\lambda)=-2L\sum_{l=1}^{M}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{Q}\big(\tilde{\omega}_{l})\big)+\log(2LM)\sum_{l=1}^{M}E_{l}+4\gamma\log(P)\sum_{l=1}^{M}E_{l},

where
\gamma\in[0,1]
is an additional hyper-parameter.

2.2
Multivariate Locally Stationary Wavelet Processes

Although the process under consideration in this work is assumed to be stationary, we shall employ the locally stationary wavelet (LSW) modelling framework of
47
to allow for projections into the wavelet spectral domain. This framework has found a variety of applications across fields such as biology
(
28
)
, neuroscience
(
17
;
38
)
, healthcare
(
15
)
and economics
(
37
;
36
)
.
51
propose an extension, the
multivariate locally stationary wavelet
(mvLSW) model for the process
\mathbf{X}=\left\{\boldsymbol{X}_{t}\right\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
, with
T=2^{J}
and
J\in\mathbb{N}
, which allows for time-varying dependence within and across channels of the multivariate time series. The model expresses the process as a superposition of discrete non-decimated wavelets across scales
j
and times
k

\mathbf{X}_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbf{V}_{j}(\nicefrac{{k}}{{T}})\psi_{j,t-k}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j,k},

where
\left\{\psi_{j,t-k}\right\}_{j,k}
is a set of non-decimated wavelets at scale
j
and location
k
,
\mathbf{V}_{j}\left(u\right)
is the scale
j
transfer function matrix at rescaled time
u=\nicefrac{{k}}{{T}}\in[0,1]
, and
\left\{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j,k}\right\}_{j,k}
are uncorrelated
P
-dimensional random vectors with zero mean and identity covariance matrix. Each
\mathbf{V}_{j}\left(\cdot\right)
is assumed to have a lower-triangular matrix form, whose entries are Lipschitz continuous functions with Lipschitz constant
L_{j}^{(p,q)}
satisfying
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}2^{j}L_{j}^{(p,q)}<\infty
. For clarity, here we use the indexing convention that small
j
corresponds to fine levels, and large
j
to coarse scales.

The
local wavelet spectral
(LWS) matrix function
\mathbf{S}_{j}(u)\in\mathbb{R}^{P\times P}
yields a time-scale decomposition of the process power at each scale
j
and rescaled time
u
, and it is defined as

\mathbf{S}_{j}(u)=\mathbf{V}_{j}(u)\mathbf{V}_{j}(u)^{\top}.

The LWS function is connected to the (localised) cross-channel covariance between channels
p
and
q
of
\mathbf{X}
, as

\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(X^{(p)}_{\lfloor uT\rfloor,T},X^{(q)}_{\lfloor uT\rfloor+\tau,T})\approx c^{(p,q)}(u,\tau)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}S^{(p,q)}_{j}(u)\Psi_{j}(\tau),

(3)

where
\Psi_{j}(\tau)=\sum_{k}\psi_{j,k}\psi_{j,k-t}
is the discrete autocorrelation wavelet
(
47
)
, and
\approx
denotes equality up to an
\mathcal{O}\left(T^{-1}\right)
factor
(
51
)
.

Note the LWS is a real-valued continuous function defined on the rescaled time domain
[0,1]
; since in practice, we only observe the process at discrete time points
k=\lfloor uT\rfloor
with
k=0,\dots,T-1
, the notation
\mathbf{S}_{j,k}=\mathbf{S}_{j}(\nicefrac{{k}}{{T}})
is often adopted
(
51
)
. In this vein, denote the precision matrix function as
\mathbf{\Theta}_{j}(u):=\mathbf{S}^{-1}_{j}(u)
.

Let
\mathbf{d}_{j,k}=\big(d_{j,k}^{(1)},...,d_{j,k}^{(P)}\big)^{\top}
be the empirical wavelet coefficient vector, defined as

\mathbf{d}_{j,k}=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{X}_{t}\psi_{jk}(t),

and let
\mathbf{I}_{j,k}=\mathbf{d}_{j,k}\mathbf{d}_{j,k}^{\top}
be the wavelet matrix periodogram. Then,
51
define an asymptotically unbiased estimator of
\mathbf{S}_{j,k}
for each
j
and each
k
as

\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{j,k}:=\sum_{l=1}^{J}(\mathbf{C}^{-1})_{jl}\tilde{\mathbf{I}}_{l,k},

(4)

where
J=\log_{2}(T)
,
\tilde{\mathbf{I}}_{l,k}
is the wavelet periodogram, smoothed across time to achieve consistency, and
\mathbf{C}
is the discrete autocorrelation wavelet inner product matrix, with
C_{jl}=<\Psi_{j},\Psi_{l}>

(
51
)
.

27
propose introducing sparsity in the inverse local wavelet spectrum,
\mathbf{\Theta}_{j,k}:=\mathbf{S}_{j,k}^{-1}
, at each scale
j
and location
k
by means of the following
\ell_{1}
-regularised optimisation

\hat{\mathbf{\Theta}}_{j,k}:=\underset{\mathbf{\Theta}\succeq 0}{\arg\min}\left\{-\log\det(\mathbf{\Theta})+\text{tr}\bigr({\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{j,k}}\,\mathbf{\Theta}\bigl)+\lambda_{j}\lVert\mathbf{\Theta}\rVert_{1}\right\}.

(5)

Although it is reasonable to assume that the wavelet coefficients are zero-mean Gaussian and uncorrelated across locations
k
within each scale
j
, as shown in
51
, the expectation of the raw wavelet periodogram
\mathbf{I}_{j,k}
satisfies

\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{I}_{j,k})\approx\sum_{l=1}^{J}C_{jl}\mathbf{S}_{l}(\nicefrac{{k}}{{T}})=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}(\nicefrac{{k}}{{T}})=:\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j,k}.

(6)

Since
\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{d}_{j,k})=\mathbf{0}
due to the zero-mean definition of the random increments
\{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j,k}\}_{j,k}
, the above implies
\mathbf{d}_{j,k}\dot{\sim}N_{P}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j,k})
, so that the actual inverse covariance matrix of the wavelet coefficients is
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j,k}^{-1}
. This is in contrast to the one employed in the optimisation problem (
5
), namely
\mathbf{S}_{j,k}^{-1}
, thus illustrating how this formulation does not correctly account for the true distribution of the wavelet coefficients. Under the process stationarity assumption, we next propose a principled way to bypass this problem.

3
Proposed Wavelet-based Time Series Graphical Lasso

In what follows we show that under the assumption of process stationarity, we can characterise the adjacency matrix of the
\mathcal{CIG}
in the
12
sense by the corresponding adjacency matrix formed from the scale-dependent wavelet precision matrices,
\{\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}:=\mathbf{S}^{-1}_{j}\}_{j}
, where under stationarity, we drop the time-localisation typical of LSW processes. This characterisation then becomes central to our proposal for wavelet-based
\mathcal{CIG}
estimation.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=\{\mathbf{X}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
be a
P
-dimensional, zero-mean stationary Gaussian process, where
\mathbf{X}_{t}=\left(X^{(1)}_{t},\ldots,X^{(P)}_{t}\right)^{\top}
is observed at time
t
across
P
channels (nodes). Assume that the process
\mathbf{X}
admits a multivariate locally stationary wavelet representation as in
51
whose spectral structure we denote as
\{\mathbf{S}_{j}\}_{j}
across the scales
j=1,\ldots,J=\log_{2}(T)
.

Then the conditional independence of two nodes
p
and
q
may be characterised at each scale
j
through the presence of a zero-valued entry in the corresponding wavelet precision matrix, i.e.
\left(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}\right)_{p,q}=\big(\mathbf{S}_{j}^{-1}\big)_{p,q}=0
.

The proof appears in Appendix
A.1
and makes use of the Fourier spectral domain characterisation typically used in graphical modelling
(
12
)
applied to scale-dependent subprocesses defined akin to
62
.

3.1
Scale-based
\mathcal{CIG}
Estimation

Following the arguments in Section
2.2
and recalling process Gaussianity and stationarity, at each scale
j
the wavelet coefficients of the stationary process
\mathbf{X}
are zero-mean
P
-dimensional Gaussian random vectors with covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}(\nicefrac{{k}}{{T}})=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}
for every time
k
, or equivalently,
\mathbf{d}_{j,k}\dot{\sim}N_{P}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j})
for
k=0,\ldots,T-1
.
As discussed in Section
2
, the distributional properties of the discrete Fourier coefficients were exploited to formulate a sparse estimation problem for the inverse spectral density matrix function. In contrast, the discrete wavelet coefficients yield the quantities
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}
—linear combinations of the
\mathbf{S}
-spectra, as shown in equation (
6
)—and cannot be directly used instead of
\mathbf{S}_{j}
.

Explicitly, the joint probability distribution of the wavelet coefficients
\{\mathbf{d}_{j,k}\}_{k}
at scale
j
is

p(\mathbf{d}_{j,0},...,\mathbf{d}_{j,T-1})=\prod_{k=0}^{T-1}\frac{1}{\pi^{P}}(\det(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}))^{-1}\exp\{-\mathbf{d}_{j,k}^{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}^{-1}\mathbf{d}_{j,k}\},

and hence the log-likelihood expressed in terms of
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}^{-1}
can be derived as

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}^{-1})&=\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\big[\log\det(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}^{-1})+\mathrm{tr}\left(-\mathbf{d}_{j,k}\mathbf{d}_{j,k}^{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}^{-1}\right)\big]\\
&=T\log\det(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}^{-1})-\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\mathrm{tr}\left(\mathbf{I}_{j,k}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}^{-1}\right)\\
&=T\big[\log\det(\mathbf{B}_{j})-\mathrm{tr}\left(\mathbf{\bar{I}}_{j}\mathbf{B}_{j}\right)\big],\end{split}

where
\bar{\mathbf{I}}_{j}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{I}_{j,k}
and
\mathbf{B}_{j}:=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}^{-1}
. Note here that smoothing over the entire time domain is used for the periodogram since
\mathbf{X}
is a stationary process. Hence, in the wavelet context an optimisation problem akin to (
2

\mathbf{\hat{B}}_{j}=\underset{\mathbf{B}_{j}}{\arg\min}\left\{-T\left[\log\det(\mathbf{B}_{j})-\mathrm{tr}\left(\bar{\mathbf{I}}_{j}\mathbf{B}_{j}\right)\right]+\mathcal{P}\left(\mathbf{B}_{j},\lambda\right)\right\},

(7)

where
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{B}_{j},\lambda)=\lambda\sum_{p\neq q}|(\mathbf{B}_{j})_{p,q}|

Observe the evident multiresolution aspect, resulting in a separate optimisation problem for each scale
j

\boldsymbol{\beta}
-spectrum. Consequently, any optimisation problem we consider directly deriving from the

-spectra, with the penalty enforcing sparsity in

We begin by considering an approach that allows us to enforce sparsity directly in the precision matrix,
\mathbf{S}_{j}^{-1}
, while still using the distributional properties of the wavelet coefficients.
We propose
to define a new process

\mathbf{Y}=\{\mathbf{Y}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}

\mathcal{CIG}
of the process
\mathbf{X}
on a

. Note we now use the superscript
(⋅)
to explicitly indicate the process.

To this end, our proposal is to simulate the new process

\mathbf{S}_{j}^{(Y)}=\sum\limits_{l=1}^{J}(\mathbf{C}^{-1})_{jl}\mathbf{S}_{l}^{(X)}

\forall j

\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{d}_{j,k}^{(Y)}\left(\mathbf{d}_{j,k}^{(Y)}\right)^{\top}\right)\approx\mathbf{S}_{j}^{(X)},

Assumption 1 ensures that we have a stationary Gaussian process whose true spectrum,

We now focus on the tuning parameter

of the penalty term in (

We select the parameter

\displaystyle\operatorname{AIC}_{j}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j})+2E_{j}^{*},

(12)

\displaystyle\operatorname{BIC}_{j}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j})+\log(T)E_{j}^{*},

(13)

\displaystyle\operatorname{eBIC}_{j}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j})+\log(T)E_{j}^{*}+4\gamma\log(P)E_{j}^{*},

where

3.3
Proposed Method for
\mathcal{CIG}
Estimation:
WavTSglasso

Using our proposed method in Section
3.1
, we obtain a consistent estimated precision matrix
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{j}

at each scale

j=1,\dots,J
. While the Fourier-based approach calls for an aggregation mechanism over the frequencies (Section
2.1
), in our approach we need to aggregate over scale in order to estimate the
\mathcal{CIG}
of the process
\mathbf{X}
, or equivalently, an adjacency matrix
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
.
From Proposition
1
, the scale-dependent conditional independence graphs coincide across all scales and this condition will form the basis to our proposal to aggregate the estimated precision matrices across scales, following the strategy introduced next.

We propose to use the following
similarity measure
for each pair of scales
j
and
j^{\prime}
,

\Delta(j,j^{\prime})=\sum_{p=1}^{P}\sum_{q\neq p}\mathds{1}\big\{\big(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}\big)_{p,q}\neq 0,\big(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j^{\prime}}\big)_{p,q}\neq 0\big\},

(15)

from which we can compute the
scaled similarity measure

\Delta_{S}(j,j^{\prime})=\frac{\Delta(j,j^{\prime})}{\sqrt{E_{j}}\sqrt{E_{j^{\prime}}}}.

(16)

The similarity measure in equation (
15
) calculates the number of common predicted edges in scales
j
and
j^{\prime}
, or put simply, it identifies the common dependencies across scales. Equation (
16
) then scales
\Delta(j,j^{\prime})
by the number of edges corresponding to each of the scales
j
and
j^{\prime}
individually, so that
\Delta_{S}(j,j^{\prime})=1
if two the scales
j
and
j^{\prime}
yield exactly the same edge(s). We compute
\Delta_{S}(j,j^{\prime})
for every pair
(j,j^{\prime})
and choose the scale pair
({j}_{1},{j}_{2})
which maximises
\Delta_{S}(j,j^{\prime})
. If there are no edges in either of the scales, then
\Delta(j,j^{\prime})=0
, and thus
\Delta_{S}(j,j^{\prime})=0
.

Our proposal for the estimation of the conditional graph dependencies is to
combine the estimated precision matrices corresponding to the pair of scales with highest scaled similarity
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{j_{1}}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{j_{2}}
, in order to construct the estimated
\mathcal{CIG}
adjacency,
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
. Specifically, we define
\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{p,q}=1
if
(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{j_{1}})_{p,q}\neq 0
or
(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{j_{2}})_{p,q}\neq 0
, and 0 otherwise.
This approach aims to balance the trade-off between sparsity and accuracy. Indeed, when computing the similarity, we focus on edges that are commonly predicted across scales, while the final adjacency incorporates all edges identified at either of the selected scales.

4.3
VAR and VARMA Models

In the previous sections, we evaluated our proposed
WavTSglasso
technique on data simulated from models with a common cross-nodal dependence parameter
\beta
across all connections. Here, we examine how variability in the connections affects recovery of
\mathbf{G}
.
To this end, we consider a 10-dimensional VAR(1) processes,
\mathbf{X}_{t}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}_{t-1}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}
, where
\mathbf{A}=(A_{i,j})\in\mathbb{R}^{10\times 10}
is the coefficient matrix and
\{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}\}_{t}
is a zero-mean Gaussian noise process with
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}=\mathbf{I}_{10}
for all
t
.
Define

\mathbf{A}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\beta_{1}&0&\cdots&\cdots&\beta_{1}\\
\beta_{2}&0&\beta_{2}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\beta_{3}&0&\beta_{3}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&\beta_{9}&0&\beta_{9}\\
\beta_{10}&0&\cdots&0&\beta_{10}&0\\
\end{pmatrix},

(17)

so that variability in the coefficient matrix is introduced via the coefficients
\{\beta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{10}
. Specifically, we consider two intervals of differing widths, namely
I_{1}=(0.6,0.7)
,
I_{2}=(0.4,0.9)
, and we randomly draw
\beta_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,10
, from each interval in turn.
The dependence graph of these two VAR(1) processes matches the 10-dimensional GNAR(1,[1]) models with Ring graph of Section
4.2
. In particular, the special case
\beta_{i}=\beta_{1,1}
for all
i=1,\ldots,10
coincides with the GNAR(1,[1]) Ring model with network parameter
\beta_{1,1}
.

All simulation studies used
K=50
realisations of length
T=1024
, and we fix
R=50
bootstrap samples for our wavelet-
\ell_{1}
method.
As shown by the edge detection rate results in Table
B.7
(Appendix
B.2
), performance gets worse for all four approaches as variability in
\mathbf{A}
increases, but the wavelet method still performs better than the Fourier-based approaches. In particular, the FPRs of the wavelet estimates remain relatively stable across the four simulations, while the TPR decreases as variability grows, indicating sparser estimated adjacency matrices. Even so, the wavelet-based estimates remain reasonable compared with the Fourier counterparts, which tend to be either sparser (Fourier-
\ell_{1}
and Fourier-
\ell_{2}
with ADMM algorithm) or denser but with higher FPR (Fourier-
\ell_{1}
with glasso algorithm).

We also consider the case when the dependence graph is not determined by the coefficient matrix
\mathbf{A}
.
Similar to
20
, we construct a 10-dimensional VAR(1) process in which the true sparse structure of
\mathbf{G}
matches that of the precision matrix of the noise process. This is achieved by using a diagonal coefficient matrix, here we set
\mathbf{A}=0.5\,\mathbf{I}_{10}
. Then, the off-diagonal entries of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{-1}
are randomly set to
0
or
0.5
with probability
0.5
, and the diagonal entries are adjusted to ensure positive definiteness.

Further, we also consider the 20-dimensional VARMA(2,2) model in
61

\mathbf{X}_{t}=\mathbf{A}_{1}\mathbf{X}_{t-1}+\mathbf{A}_{2}\mathbf{X}_{t-2}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}+\mathbf{B}_{1}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-1}+\mathbf{B}_{2}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-2},

(18)

where
\{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}\}_{t}
is a zero-mean Gaussian noise process with identity covariance matrix.
The autoregressive coefficient matrices are diagonal:
\mathbf{A}_{1}=0.4\,\mathbf{I}_{20}
and
\mathbf{A}_{2}=0.2\,\mathbf{I}_{20}
.
The moving-average matrices are block-diagonal, with
5\times 5
-dimensional blocks
1.5\,(\mathbf{I}_{5}+\mathbf{J}_{5})
for
\mathbf{B}_{1}
, and
0.75\,(\mathbf{I}_{5}+\mathbf{J}_{5})
for
\mathbf{B}_{2}

\mathbf{J}_{5}
denotes the
5\times 5
-dimensional matrix whose entries are all
1

) inherits this block-diagonal structure.

For the VARMA model (

5
Underlying Graph Discovery

So far, we have focussed on inferring the conditional dependence structure,
\mathcal{CIG}=(\mathcal{K},\mathcal{E})
underlying the observed data. In this section, we focus on finding the underlying network (graph)
\mathcal{N}=(\mathcal{K},\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{N}})
, when such a network is indeed present in the data, such as for the GNAR models in Section
4
. Note that these two tasks are crucially not identical.

If the data follows a GNAR(
p,[\mathbf{s}]
) process or, more generally, if we assume the data has an underlying graph structure
\mathcal{N}
, then estimating the edge set
\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{N}}
of such a graph is crucial for inference. We denote the adjacency matrix of the underlying graph by
\mathbf{G}^{\mathcal{N}}
, and recall we denote the adjacency of the conditional independence graph,
\mathcal{CIG}
, by
\mathbf{G}
. Since
\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{N}}
is contained within
\mathcal{E}

(
48
, Theorem 2)
, the task is to determine which edges in
\mathbf{G}
correspond to those of
\mathbf{G}^{\mathcal{N}}
.

From the proposed
WavTSglasso
approach described in Section
3
, we obtain the sparse estimated precision matrices
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{j}
for each scale
j=1,...,J
, and define the corresponding scale-dependent edge and precision value sets as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{(\mathbf{G})}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(p,q)\in\mathcal{K}\times\mathcal{K},\,p\neq q:\big(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{j}\big)_{p,q}\neq 0\},

\displaystyle{\vartheta}^{(\mathbf{G})}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\big(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{j}\big)_{p,q}:(p,q)\in\mathcal{E}^{(\mathbf{G})}_{j}\},\mbox{ respectively}.

We propose applying
k
-means clustering to the precision values
{\vartheta}_{j}^{(\mathbf{G})}
to separate them into
k=2
clusters: those that correspond to edges in
\mathbf{G}^{\mathcal{N}}
and the additional edges in
\mathbf{G}
(a ‘noise’ cluster). The
k
-means clustering procedure yields a cluster,
{C}_{1j}
, containing edges with low values of
\big|\big(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{j}\big)_{p,q}\big|
, or in other words, those edges between node pairs
(p,q)
that are the least significant; this is the cluster whose centre is closest to
0
, hence the ‘noise’ cluster. The second cluster,
{C}_{2j}
, contains the remaining edges in
\mathbf{G}
, thus we let
(\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{j}^{\mathcal{N}})_{p,q}=1
when
(p,q)\in{C}_{2j}
, and zero otherwise, in order to obtain a
scale
j
-dependent
sparse estimator for the underlying graph adjacency
\mathbf{G}^{\mathcal{N}}
.

Our strategy for scale selection in order to yield
\hat{\mathbf{G}}^{\mathcal{N}}
is data-dependent. When the underlying network is expected to be sparse and the GNAR neighbourhood structure is simple (such as a GNAR(1,[1]) or a GNAR(2,[1,1])), we recommend using the finest scale (scale 1). For denser networks with similarly simple neighbourhood structure or if the neighbourhood structure is more complex (such as a GNAR(1,[2])), then a middle scale (
j=\lfloor J/2\rfloor
) is recommended.

The effects of dimension, sparsity and length of the time series on the network estimation are assessed in what follows via simulation, akin to our
WavTSglasso
experiments. We compute the TPR, FPR and TDR for each simulation, comparing the estimated adjacency matrix
\hat{\mathbf{G}}^{\mathcal{N}}
to the true adjacency matrix
\mathbf{G}^{\mathcal{N}}
.
Since, to our knowledge, there are no other algorithms which attempt to identify the underlying network,
\mathcal{N}
, we only report results using our clustering methodology.

Use of other clustering techniques.

We choose
k
-means for edge-selection due to its simplicity and speed but other clustering algorithms can be used. We have found that experimental results change only minimally when other clustering techniques such as tree-based procedures,
(
32
)
or
k
-medoids
(
35
)
are used.

7
Conclusions and Further Work

In this article, we proposed a novel wavelet domain method for estimating the conditional independence graph (
\mathcal{CIG}
) of second-order stationary time series. By considering the distributional properties of the wavelet coefficients associated to the process, we formulate a principled penalised optimisation problem to achieve sparse estimation of the inverse wavelet spectra of the time series, upon which our
\mathcal{CIG}
estimation procedure is built. We establish theoretical results showing that our
\mathcal{CIG}
estimator achieves desirable consistency properties, and demonstrate that the underlying conditional independence structure of the process can be successfully recovered.

Through a range of numerical experiments, we evaluated performance under different scenarios, including varying data-generating processes, graph structures, and data dimensions. We also proposed an agnostic procedure to recover the underlying graph of GNAR models from the estimated conditional independence graph. Finally, we applied the network estimation method to a dataset of COVID-19 patients in England and found spatial variation in graph density across the study area; the estimated underlying network improved forecasting performance in comparison to the user-defined adjacency built by
48
. A natural extension would be to adapt the wavelet-based TSglasso to nonstationary settings. This extension is non-trivial, as it requires careful definitions of network locally stationary processes and their conditional independence graphs, as well as their efficient aggregation.

A.1
Proof of Proposition
1

Proof.

Akin to
62
, let us define a scale-dependent subprocess of the original process
\mathbf{X}
to be
\mathbf{X}_{j,t}=\left(X_{j,t}^{(1)},\ldots,X_{j,t}^{(P)}\right)^{T}
, with the following representation,

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}_{j,t}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbf{V}_{j}(\nicefrac{{k}}{{T}})\psi_{j,k}(t)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j,k}

(A.1)

where the terms
\mathbf{V}_{j}(\nicefrac{{k}}{{T}}),\psi_{j,k}
and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j,k}
are as in the multivariate LSW definition. We can conceptualise the time series
\{X_{j,t}^{(p)}\}_{t}
as giving the scale-
j
representation of the original node
p
process,
\{X_{t}^{(p)}\}_{t}
.
From the zero-mean assumption for the innovations
\{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j,k}\}_{j,k}
, we immediately obtain that the subprocess is also zero-mean, i.e.
\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{X}_{j,t})=\mathbf{0}
, at all times
t
.
Coupling the zero-mean property with representation (
A.1
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(X_{j,[uT]}^{(p)},X_{j,[uT]+\tau}^{(q)})=\mathbb{E}\left[X_{j,[uT]}^{(p)}X_{j,[uT]+\tau}^{(q)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum\limits_{k}\mathbf{V}_{j}^{(p)}(\nicefrac{{k}}{{T}})\psi_{j,k}([uT])\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j,k}\right)\times\left(\sum\limits_{k^{\prime}}\mathbf{V}_{j}^{(q)}(\nicefrac{{k^{\prime}}}{{T}})\psi_{j,k^{\prime}}([uT]+\tau)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j,k^{\prime}}\right)^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k}\sum\limits_{k^{\prime}}\mathbf{V}_{j}^{(p)}(\nicefrac{{k}}{{T}})\psi_{j,k}([uT])\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j,k}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\top}_{j,k^{\prime}})\psi_{j,k^{\prime}}([uT]+\tau)\left(\mathbf{V}_{j}^{(q)}(\nicefrac{{k^{\prime}}}{{T}})\right)^{\top},

where
\mathbf{V}_{j}^{(p)}(u)
denotes the
p
th row of
\mathbf{V}_{j}(u)
.
From the assumptions on the innovation process, namely
\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j,k}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\top}_{j,k^{\prime}})=\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j,k},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j,k^{\prime}})=\delta_{j,j^{\prime}}\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}\mathbf{I}_{P}
, with
\mathbf{I}_{P}
denoting the identity matrix, and using the definition of the LWS matrix,
S_{j}^{(p,q)}(u)=\mathbf{V}_{j}^{(p)}(u)\left(\mathbf{V}_{j}^{(q)}(u)\right)^{\top}
, by letting
m=k-[uT]
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(X_{j,[uT]}^{(p)},X_{j,[uT]+\tau}^{(q)})=\sum\limits_{m}S^{(p,q)}_{j}\left(\nicefrac{{([uT]+m)}}{{T}}\right)\psi_{j,m}(0)\psi_{j,m}(\tau).

Define
c_{j}^{(p,q)}(u,\tau)=S^{(p,q)}_{j}(u)\Psi_{j}(\tau)
and,
using the Lipschitz continuity of
S_{j}^{(p,q)}(u)
,

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(X_{j,[uT]}^{(p)},X_{j,[uT]+\tau}^{(q)})-c_{j}^{(p,q)}(u,\tau)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum\limits_{m}S^{(p,q)}_{j}\left(\nicefrac{{([uT]+m)}}{{T}}\right)\psi_{j,m}(0)\psi_{j,m}(\tau)-c_{j}^{(p,q)}(u,\tau)\right|

\displaystyle\leq T^{-1}\sum\limits_{m}|m|L^{(p,q)}_{j}|\psi_{j,m}(0)\psi_{j,m}(\tau)|=\mathcal{O}(T^{-1}),

where we used the scale-
j
autocorrelation wavelet property of having a compact support of length
2^{j}
, coupled with
\Psi_{j}(\tau)=\mathcal{O}(1)
and the property of the Lipschitz constants.

Thus the process covariance is
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(X_{j,[uT]}^{(p)},X_{j,[uT]+\tau}^{(q)})\approx c_{j}^{(p,q)}(u,\tau)
, which shows that under the assumption of stationarity of the original process
\mathbf{X}
, the covariance function of the scale-
j
subprocess is

c_{j}^{(p,q)}(\tau)=S^{(p,q)}_{j}\Psi_{j}(\tau),\,\forall\tau
. Also note that the process covariance is given by
c^{(p,q)}(\tau)=\sum_{j}S^{(p,q)}_{j}\Psi_{j}(\tau)

(
51
)
, which can be reformulated using the subprocess notation as

c^{(p,q)}(\tau)=\sum_{j}c_{j}^{(p,q)}(\tau).

(A.2)

Continuing under the process
\mathbf{X}
stationarity assumption, since the mean and covariance structures for the scale-
j
subprocess are not time-dependent, as we have seen above, the scale-dependent subprocesses
\{\mathbf{X}_{j,t}\}_{t}
are themselves (weakly) stationary for each scale
j
.

In this setting, we can establish a relationship between the (Fourier) spectral density matrix and the stationary wavelet spectrum for each
subprocess
. Specifically, let
\breve{g}\left(\omega\right)
denote the Fourier transform of
g(\cdot)
. Then for a node pair
(p,q)
and scale-
j
subprocess
\{\mathbf{X}_{j,t}\}_{t}
, using linearity of the Fourier transform, we have the following relationship between the associated Fourier and wavelet spectra

f_{j}^{(p,q)}(\omega)=\breve{c}_{j}^{(p,q)}\left(\omega\right)=S^{(p,q)}_{j}\breve{\Psi}_{j}(\omega),\mbox{ for any frequency }\omega,

(A.3)

where we denote by
\mathbf{f}_{j}(\cdotp)
and
\mathbf{S}_{j}
the
P\times P
-dimensional Fourier and wavelet spectral matrices of the scale-
j
subprocess, respectively. Then using (
A.3
), we obtain the precision matrix equivalent,

\mathbf{f}^{-1}_{j}(\omega)=\mathbf{S}^{-1}_{j}\breve{\Psi}_{j}^{-1}(\omega),\,\forall\omega.

(A.4)

If the nodal pair
(p,q)
is not associated to an edge in the conditional independence graph of the scale-
j
subprocess, then by the results in
12
applied to the stationary subprocess
\{\mathbf{X}_{j,t}\}_{t}
, we have
(\mathbf{f}^{-1}_{j}(\omega))_{p,q}=0
for all
\omega
, and (
A.4
) implies that
(\mathbf{S}^{-1}_{j})_{p,q}=0
. This argument applies for every scale-
j
subprocess, showing that the edge absence maps into a zero entry in the wavelet precision matrix at each scale. Conversely, if we set
(\mathbf{S}^{-1}_{j})_{p,q}=0
, then the right-hand side of (
A.4
) vanishes and
(\mathbf{f}^{-1}_{j}(\omega))_{p,q}=0
for all
\omega
, which again by
12
means that there is no
(p,q)
edge in the conditional independence structure of the scale-
j
subprocess.

We next show that the conditional independence of two nodes given the remaining ones is equivalent to the conditional independence of their subprocesses across all scales
j
. Let us denote by
\{e_{t}^{(p)}\}_{t}
the channel
p
residual process obtained by removing the linear effects of the nodes other than
p,\,q
from the original process
\mathbf{X}
and
\{e_{j,t}^{(p)}\}
denote the corresponding residual subprocess at scale-
j
; similarly, define the residual process at node
q
and its associated subprocesses. Following the arguments in
12
, the process
\{(e_{t}^{(p)},e_{t}^{(q)})^{\top}\}_{t}
is bivariate stationary, and the Fourier and wavelet cross-spectra of their scale
j
-subprocess are connected akin to equation (
A.3
), namely

f_{j}^{e;(p,q)}(\omega)=S^{e;(p,q)}_{j}\breve{\Psi}_{j}(\omega).

(A.5)

Additionally, following (
A.2
) for the residual process, we may express its covariance using the covariances of the residual subprocesses as

c^{e;(p,q)}(\tau)=\sum_{j}c_{j}^{e;(p,q)}(\tau)=\sum_{j}S^{e;(p,q)}_{j}\Psi_{j}(\tau),\,\forall\tau.

(A.6)

Now recall the fact that the nodal pair
(p,q)
is not associated to an edge in the conditional independence graph of the process
\mathbf{X}
means that
c^{e;(p,q)}(\tau)=0,\,\forall\tau

(
12
)
. Then the linear independence of the autocorrelation wavelets and (
A.6
) translate into
S^{e;(p,q)}_{j}=0,\,\forall j
(see e.g. the proof of Theorem 1 of
47
). Coupled with (
A.5
) for the residual subprocess, we obtain that

f_{j}^{e;(p,q)}(\omega)=0,\,\forall\omega,\,\forall j

which is equivalent in the Dahlhaus sense to the conditional independence of the nodes
p
and
q
for the subprocesses across all scales
j
. The same argument follows in the opposite direction, hence the conditional independence graphs coincide across all scales.

∎

A.2
Proof of Theorem
1

Our proof of Theorem
1
follows the general strategy of
53
and
58
but is adapted to the wavelet-domain likelihood and scale-wise formulation. We first obtain a high-probability bound for the deviation of the spectral estimator (Lemmas
1
–
2
), then develop a Taylor expansion for the log-determinant with explicit integral remainder (Lemmas
3
-
4
), and finally combine these to obtain a bound on the Frobenius norm of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}
. Note that in the below, for notational simplicity we drop the dependency on the scale
j
in the bounding constants.

Lemma
1
provides a tail bound for entries of a sample covariance matrix of a Gaussian vector and is taken from
53
where a proof is given.

Lemma 1
.

Consider a zero-mean Gaussian random vector
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
with covariance matrix
\mathbf{c}_{z}\succ\mathbf{0}
. Given
T
independent, identically distributed (iid) samples
\mathbf{z}_{t},t=0,...,T-1
of
\mathbf{z}
, let
\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{z}=(1/T)\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{z}_{t}\mathbf{z}_{t}^{T}
denote the sample covariance matrix. Then
\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{z}
satisfies the tail bound

\mathbb{P}\bigg(|(\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{z}-\mathbf{c}_{z})_{p,q}|>\delta\bigg)\leq 4\exp\bigg(-\frac{T\delta^{2}}{3200\max_{p}(c_{z;p,p}^{2})}\bigg)

for all
\delta\in(0,40\max_{p}(\mathbf{c}_{z;p,p}))
, for any
p,q=1,\ldots,P
.

Lemma
2
applies Lemma
1
to the wavelet spectral estimator
\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{j}
at a fixed scale
j
.

Lemma 2
.

Under Assumption 1 in Section
3.1.3
, the smoothed periodogram
\bar{\mathbf{I}}^{(Y)}_{j}
used as an estimator of
{\mathbf{S}}_{j}^{(X)}
in the optimisation problem (
9
), here denoted as
\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{j}^{(X)}
, satisfies the tail bound

\mathbb{P}\biggl(\max_{p,q}\big|\big(\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{j}^{(X)}-\mathbf{S}_{0j}^{(X)}\big)_{p,q}\big|>C_{0}\sqrt{\frac{\log(P_{T})}{T}}\biggr)\leq\frac{1}{P_{T}^{\nu-2}},

for
\nu>2
if
T
is such that
T>N_{1}=2\log(4P_{T}^{\nu})
and
C_{0}=\biggl(40\max_{p}\big(\mathbf{S}_{0j}^{(X){}}\big)_{p,p}\biggr)\sqrt{\frac{N_{1}}{\log(P_{T})}}
, where we recall that
P_{T}
denotes the dimension of the process, allowed to increase with
T
.

Proof.

Recall that for each scale
j
, we have
\mathbb{E}\big(\mathbf{d}_{j,k}^{(Y)}(\mathbf{d}_{j,k}^{(Y)})^{\top}\big)\approx\mathbf{S}_{0j}^{(X)},\,\forall k
from equation (
8
) and
\mathbf{d}_{j,k}^{(Y)}\dot{\sim}N_{P_{T}}\big(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{S}_{0j}^{(X)}\big)
, where
\mathbf{S}_{0j}^{(X)}
denotes the true spectrum of
\mathbf{X}
.

Under stationarity, we have that
\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{j}^{(X)}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{I}_{j,k}^{(Y)}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{d}_{j,k}^{(Y)}\big(\mathbf{d}_{j,k}^{(Y)}\big)^{\top}
and using the approximate uncorrelatedness of the wavelet coefficients coupled with the process Gaussianity, we apply Lemma
1
with
\mathbf{z}:=\mathbf{d}_{j,k}^{(Y)}
and
\mathbf{c}_{z}:=\mathbf{S}_{0j}^{(X)}
to obtain for all
\delta\in\big(0,40\max_{p}\big(\mathbf{S}_{0j}^{(X)}\big)_{p,p}\big)

\mathbb{P}\big(\big|\big(\hat{\mathbf{S}}^{(X)}_{j}-\mathbf{S}_{0j}^{(X)}\big)_{p,q}\big|>\delta\big)\leq 4\exp\bigg(-\frac{T\delta^{2}}{3200\max_{p}\big(\big(\mathbf{S}_{0j}^{(X){}}\big)_{p,p}^{2}\big)}\bigg).

Taking a union bound over the
P_{T}^{2}
entries of the matrix
\left[\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{j}^{(X)}-\mathbf{S}_{0j}^{(X)}\right]
for a fixed
j
yields

\mathbb{P}\big(\max_{p,q}|\big(\hat{\mathbf{S}}^{(X)}_{j}-\mathbf{S}_{0j}^{(X)}\big)_{p,q}\big|>\delta\big)\leq 4P_{T}^{2}\exp\bigg(-\frac{T\delta^{2}}{3200\max_{p}\big(\big(\mathbf{S}_{0j}^{(X){}}\big)_{p,p}^{2}\big)}\bigg):=\kappa,

for
\delta\in\big(0,40\max_{p}\big(\mathbf{S}_{0j}^{(X)}\big)_{p,p}\big)
.

Letting
\delta:=C_{0}\sqrt{\frac{\log(P_{T})}{T}}
, with
C_{0}
as in the statement of the lemma, fulfils the condition
\delta\in\big(0,40\max_{p}\big(\mathbf{S}_{0j}^{(X)}\big)_{p,p}\big)
due to the construction
N_{1}<T
. Additionally, since the auto-spectrum entries
\big(\mathbf{S}_{0j}^{(X)}\big)_{p,p}
are all positive (hence the maximum of their squares is just the squared maximum), we use
N_{1}=2\log(4P_{T}^{\nu})
, and rearrange to obtain
\kappa={P_{T}^{2-\nu}}
, as desired. (To aid manipulations, we may denote
c_{*}=\big(40\max_{p}\big(\mathbf{S}_{0j}^{(X)}\big)_{p,p}\big)^{-1}
so that

\kappa=4P_{T}^{2}\exp\bigg(-\frac{\delta^{2}Tc_{*}^{2}}{2}\bigg)
and note that
c_{*}\delta=\sqrt{\frac{2\log(4P_{T}^{\nu})}{T}}=\sqrt{\frac{N_{1}}{T}}
, thus
\kappa=\frac{1}{P_{T}^{\nu-2}}
.)

Next, Lemma

gives a second-order Taylor expansion for terms of the form

Lemma 3
.

At each scale
j
, denote
g(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j})=\log\det(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j})
and let
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}=\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}
, with
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}
. Then the Taylor series expansion of
g(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j})
at
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}
is given by

g(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j})=g(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j})+\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})-\frac{1}{2}\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1})\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})+\text{h.o.t},

(A.7)

where the acronym
h.o.t
stands for higher order terms in
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}
.

Proof.

In the notation above,
g(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j})=\log\det(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})
. Note that using the invertibility of the spectra/precision matrices, we can write

\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}=\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{1/2}(\mathbf{I}_{P_{T}}+\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1/2})\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{1/2},

(A.8)

where
\mathbf{I}_{P_{T}}
denotes the identity matrix of size
P_{T}\times P_{T}
.
As all matrices are square, using basic properties of determinants we obtain

\displaystyle\det(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\det(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j})\det(\mathbf{I}_{P_{T}}+\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1/2}),\mbox{ from which we derive}

\displaystyle\log\det(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\det(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j})+\log\det(\mathbf{I}_{P_{T}}+\mathbf{U}),

(A.9)

where to simplify notation we use
\mathbf{U}=\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1/2}
.

Focusing on the second term in the right-hand side of equation (
A.9
), we have
\log\det(\mathbf{I}_{P_{T}}+\mathbf{U})=\text{tr}(\log(\mathbf{I}_{P_{T}}+\mathbf{U}))

(
59
)
since
(\mathbf{I}_{P_{T}}+\mathbf{U})
is symmetric and positive definite. The symmetry follows immediately from the definition of
\mathbf{U}
and the symmetry of the precision matrices. The positive definiteness follows by observing that (
A.8
) can be re-written as

\mathbf{I}_{P_{T}}+\mathbf{U}=\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1/2}
, where the precision matrix
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}
is positive definite as the inverse of a positive definite matrix, the scale-
j
spectrum
\mathbf{S}_{j}
.

Hence we can use a Taylor expansion to write
\log\det(\mathbf{I}_{P_{T}}+\mathbf{U})=\text{tr}(\mathbf{U})-\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}(\mathbf{U}^{2})+\text{h.o.t}
, which coupled with equation (
A.9
) yields

\log\det(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})=\log\det(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j})+\text{tr}(\mathbf{U})-\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}(\mathbf{U}^{2})+\text{h.o.t}.

(A.10)

Considering each term separately, we have

\displaystyle\text{tr}(\mathbf{U})

\displaystyle=\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1/2})=\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}),\mbox{ since }\text{tr}(\mathbf{ABC})=\text{tr}(\mathbf{BCA}),

(A.11)

\displaystyle\text{tr}(\mathbf{U}^{2})

\displaystyle=\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1/2})

\displaystyle=\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})

\displaystyle=\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1})\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}),

(A.12)

where for the last equality we used the relationship
\text{tr}(\mathbf{ABCD})=\text{vec}(\mathbf{D}^{\top})^{\top}(\mathbf{C}^{\top}\otimes\mathbf{A})\text{vec}(\mathbf{B})

(
1
, Chapter 10.2)
and the symmetry of
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}
.

Replacing equations (
A.11
) and (
A.12
) into equation (
A.10
), we have

g(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j})=g(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j})+\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})-\frac{1}{2}\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1})\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})+\text{h.o.t},

(A.13)

where we have also used
g(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j})=\log\det(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j})
.

Lemma
4
refines the expansion in Lemma
3
to an integral remainder form to aid bounding.

Lemma 4
.

With
g(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j})=\log\det(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j})
and
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}=\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}
defined as in Lemma
3
at each scale
j
, the Taylor series expansion of
g(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j})
in integral remainder form is given by

g(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j})=g(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j})+\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})-\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})^{\top}\biggl[\int_{0}^{1}(1-v)H(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j},v)dv\biggr]\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}),

(A.14)

where
H(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j},v)=\big((\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}+v\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})^{-1}\big)^{\top}\otimes(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}+v\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})^{-1}
and we use the usual notation,
\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})
for the operator that stacks the columns of the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}
;
\otimes
for the Kronecker product, see e.g.
1
.

Proof.

Using the integral form of the remainder for the Taylor expansion of a function
h(\cdotp)
, we have

h(1)=h(0)+h^{\prime}(0)+\int_{0}^{1}(1-v)h^{\prime\prime}(v)dv.

(A.15)

Let
h(v):=g(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}+v\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})
, and we denote
\boldsymbol{\Theta}(v)=\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}+v\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}
for
v\in[0,1]
.

Then
h(0)=\log\det(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j})
,
h(1)=\log\det(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})
, and

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(v)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dv}g(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}+v\Delta_{j})

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\Theta}(v)^{-1}\frac{d}{dv}\boldsymbol{\Theta}(v)\right)

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\Theta}(v)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\right),

where the second equality follows from Jacobi’s identity
(
44
)
and
\frac{d}{dv}\boldsymbol{\Theta}(v)=\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}
. Hence we obtain
h^{\prime}(0)=\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})
.

For the second derivative,
h^{\prime\prime}(v)=\frac{d}{dv}\text{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\Theta}(v)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\right)
, thus
h^{\prime\prime}(v)=\text{tr}\bigg(\frac{d}{dv}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}(v)^{-1})\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\bigg)
and we first find
\frac{d}{dv}\left(\boldsymbol{\Theta}(v)^{-1}\right)
.
This follows from
\frac{d}{dv}\left(\boldsymbol{\Theta}(v)\boldsymbol{\Theta}(v)^{-1}\right)=\frac{d}{dv}\mathbf{I}_{P_{T}}=\mathbf{0}_{P_{T}}
which gives

\frac{d}{dv}\left(\boldsymbol{\Theta}(v)^{-1}\right)=-\boldsymbol{\Theta}(v)^{-1}\left(\frac{d}{dv}\boldsymbol{\Theta}(v)\right)\boldsymbol{\Theta}(v)^{-1}.

Hence,

\displaystyle h^{\prime\prime}(v)

\displaystyle=-\text{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\Theta}(v)^{-1}\bigg(\frac{d}{dv}\boldsymbol{\Theta}(v)\bigg)\boldsymbol{\Theta}(v)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=-\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}^{\top})^{\top}((\boldsymbol{\Theta}(v)^{-1})^{\top}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Theta}(v)^{-1})\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})

\displaystyle=-\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})^{\top}H(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j},v)\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}),

where we have used
\frac{d}{dv}\boldsymbol{\Theta}(v)=\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}
in the second equality and
\text{tr}(\mathbf{ABCD})=\text{vec}(\mathbf{D}^{\top})^{\top}(\mathbf{C}^{\top}\otimes\mathbf{A})\text{vec}(\mathbf{B})
. We are now in position to replace each component into equation (
A.15
) and obtain

\displaystyle g(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j})

\displaystyle=g(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j})+\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ -\int_{0}^{1}(1-v)\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})^{\top}H(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j},v)\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})dv

\displaystyle=\log\det(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j})+\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ -\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})^{\top}\bigg(\int_{0}^{1}(1-v)H(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}^{-1},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j},v)dv\bigg)\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}).

∎

Proof of Theorem
1

Proof.

Recall the objective function in the optimisation problem (
9
) is

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j})=-T[\log\det(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j})-\text{tr}(\bar{\mathbf{I}}_{j}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j})]+\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j},\lambda_{j}),\,\forall j

where we write
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}
to denote
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}^{(X)}
and
\bar{\mathbf{I}}_{j}
to denote
\bar{\mathbf{I}}_{j}^{(Y)}
for notational simplicity. Dropping the dependence on
j
, we equivalently define

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})=\frac{\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})}{T}=-\log\det(\boldsymbol{\Theta})+\text{tr}(\bar{\mathbf{I}}\boldsymbol{\Theta})+\lambda^{\prime}\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{-}\|_{1},

where
\lambda^{\prime}=\lambda/T
and
\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{-}
denotes the off-diagonal entries of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
. Set
\boldsymbol{\Delta}=\boldsymbol{\Theta}-\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}
and define

\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}):=\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}+\boldsymbol{\Delta})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}).

The estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}
minimizes
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})
, or equivalently
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})
, and therefore
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}-\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}
minimizes
\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})
.

Following the argument in
58
, if we can show that with high probability

\inf_{\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F}=Rr_{T}}\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})>0,

then the minimizer
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}-\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}
is such that
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\|_{F}\leq Rr_{T}
. It would therefore follow that

\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}-\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}\|_{F}\leq Rr_{T}.

This argument follows from the proof of Theorem 1 from
54
. We now express
\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})
in terms of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
and
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})

\displaystyle=\text{tr}(\bar{\mathbf{I}}\boldsymbol{\Delta})-[\log\det(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}+\boldsymbol{\Delta})-\log\det(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0})]

\displaystyle+\lambda^{\prime}[\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}^{-}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{-}\|_{1}-\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}^{-}\|_{1}].

Recall that our construction in Section
3.1
ensures that the periodogram of the process
\mathbf{Y}
is an asymptotically unbiased estimator of the target spectrum of the process
\mathbf{X}
, see equation (
8
), namely under stationarity we have
\mathbb{E}(\bar{\mathbf{I}}^{(Y)})\approx\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(Y)}=\mathbf{S}^{(X)}
, and hence
\bar{\mathbf{I}}^{(Y)}
is an estimate for
\mathbf{S}^{(X)}
and so in what follows we write
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
to replace
\bar{\mathbf{I}}
. Let
\mathbf{S}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{-1}_{0}
denote the true wavelet spectrum.

Let us split the spectral estimator as
\hat{\mathbf{S}}=\mathbf{S}_{0}+(\hat{\mathbf{S}}-\mathbf{S}_{0})
which gives
\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}\bar{\mathbf{I}})=\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}\mathbf{S}_{0})+\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}(\hat{\mathbf{S}}-\mathbf{S}_{0}))
and leads us to splitting the objective function into three parts

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3},\mbox{ where}

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\text{tr}(\mathbf{S}_{0}\Delta)-\log\det(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}+\boldsymbol{\Delta})+\log\det(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}),

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\text{tr}((\hat{\mathbf{S}}-\mathbf{S}_{0})\boldsymbol{\Delta}),

\displaystyle A_{3}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}(\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}^{-}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{-}\|_{1}-\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}^{-}\|_{1}).

Our next aim is to lower bound each of the three terms
A_{1},A_{2}
and
A_{3}
in order to show that
\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})>0
when
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
is such that
\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F}=Rr_{T}
. Let us first bound
A_{3}
.

Using the edge set notation
\mathcal{E}_{0}
from Assumption 2 and denoting by
\mathcal{E}_{0}^{c}
its complement, we split the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
into matrices
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}
and
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{E}_{0}^{c}}
, where
\left(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}\right)_{p,q}=\left(\boldsymbol{\Delta}\right)_{p,q}
if
(p,q)\in\mathcal{E}_{0}
and
0
otherwise, and similarly for the edge complement set. Hence
\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{-}=\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{-}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{E}_{0}^{c}}^{-}
, and

\displaystyle A_{3}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\prime}(\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}^{-}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{-}\|_{1}-\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}^{-}\|_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{p,q\in\mathcal{E}_{0}}\lambda^{\prime}(\|(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0})_{p,q}^{-}+(\boldsymbol{\Delta})_{p,q}^{-}\|_{1}-\|(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0})_{p,q}^{-}\|_{1})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{p,q\in\mathcal{E}_{0}^{c}}\lambda^{\prime}(\|(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0})_{p,q}^{-}+(\boldsymbol{\Delta})_{p,q}^{-}\|_{1}-\|(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0})_{p,q}^{-}\|_{1}).

Since
(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0})_{p,q}=0
if
(p,q)\notin\mathcal{E}_{0}
, we have,

\displaystyle A_{3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p,q\in\mathcal{E}_{0}}\lambda^{\prime}(\|(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}^{-})_{p,q}+(\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{-})_{p,q}\|_{1}-\|(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}^{-})_{p,q}\|_{1})+\lambda^{\prime}\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{E}_{0}^{c}}^{-}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\prime}\left(\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}^{-}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{-}\|_{1}-\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}^{-}\|_{1}\right)+\lambda^{\prime}(\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{E}_{0}^{c}}^{-}\|_{1})

\displaystyle\geq\lambda^{\prime}(-\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{-}\|_{1})+\lambda^{\prime}(\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{E}_{0}^{c}}^{-}\|_{1}),\mbox{by the triangle inequality.}

Hence we have,

A_{3}\geq\lambda^{\prime}(\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{E}_{0}^{c}}^{-}\|_{1}-\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{-}\|_{1}).

Turning to
A_{1}
, using Lemma
4
this term can be equivalently written as

A_{1}=\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})^{\top}K(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0},\boldsymbol{\Delta})\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}),

where
K(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0},\boldsymbol{\Delta})=\int_{0}^{1}(1-v)H(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0},\boldsymbol{\Delta},v)dv
, with
H(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0},\boldsymbol{\Delta},v)
defined as in Lemma
4
.

Using the notation therein, recall that
\boldsymbol{\Theta}(v)=\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}+v\boldsymbol{\Delta}
with
v\in[0,1]
fixed. By Assumption 3 on the conditions on the boundedness of the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}
, coupled with Weyl’s identity
(
30
)
, we obtain that all eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}(v)
must lie within the interval
[\tilde{\phi}_{\min},\tilde{\phi}_{\max}]
where
\tilde{\phi}_{\min}\geq\phi_{\min}-\eta
and
\tilde{\phi}_{\max}\leq\phi_{\max}+\eta
and
\eta=\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{2}
.

Hence, the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}(v)^{-1}
are contained within
[\tilde{\phi}_{\max}^{-1},\tilde{\phi}_{\min}^{-1}]
. Given that
H(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0},\boldsymbol{\Delta},v)=(\boldsymbol{\Theta}(v)^{-1})^{\top}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Theta}(v)^{-1}
, the eigenvalues of
H(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0},\boldsymbol{\Delta},v)
are such that
\phi_{\min}(H(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0},\boldsymbol{\Delta},v))\geq\tilde{\phi}_{\max}^{-2}
. Therefore
H(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0},\boldsymbol{\Delta},v)-\tilde{\phi}_{\max}^{-2}\mathbf{I}_{P_{T}}
is positive semi-definite, where
\mathbf{I}_{P_{T}}
denotes the identity matrix with dimension
P_{T}
. This in turn yields

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle\geq\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})^{\top}\bigg(\int_{0}^{1}(1-v)\tilde{\phi}_{\max}^{-2}\mathbf{I}_{P_{T}}dv\bigg)\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{\phi}_{\max}^{-2}}{2}\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})^{\top}\mathbf{I}_{P_{T}}\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{\phi}_{\max}^{-2}}{2}\|\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})\|^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{\phi}_{\max}^{-2}}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=c_{1}\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2},\mbox{ with }c_{1}=\frac{\tilde{\phi}_{\max}^{-2}}{2}.

(A.16)

Moving on to the term
A_{2}
which can be equivalently written as
A_{2}=\sum_{p,q}(\hat{\mathbf{S}}-\mathbf{S}_{0})_{p,q}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{q,p}
and applying Lemma
2
, we can see that

\max_{p,q}|(\hat{\mathbf{S}}-\mathbf{S}_{0})_{p,q}|\leq C_{0}\sqrt{\frac{\log(P_{T})}{T}}

with high probability
(1-\frac{1}{P^{\nu-2}_{T}})
. So,

|A_{2}|\leq C_{0}\sqrt{\frac{\log(P_{T})}{T}}\sum_{p,q}|\boldsymbol{\Delta}|_{q,p}=C_{0}\sqrt{\frac{\log(P_{T})}{T}}\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{1}.

(A.17)

As there are
P_{T}^{2}
entries in the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
, it follows that
\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{1}\leq P_{T}\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F}
by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Coupled with (
A.17
), we obtain

|A_{2}|\leq C_{0}\sqrt{\frac{\log(P_{T})}{T}}P_{T}\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F}=C_{0}r_{T}\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F},

where
r_{T}=P_{T}\sqrt{\frac{\log(P_{T})}{T}}
and we have the following lower bound for
A_{2}
:

A_{2}\geq-C_{0}r_{T}\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F}.

(A.18)

We now consider the term
A_{3}
. We have
A_{3}\geq\lambda^{\prime}(\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{E}_{0}^{c}}^{-}\|_{1}-\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{-}\|_{1})\geq-\lambda^{\prime}\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{-}\|_{1}
, since the first term is non-negative. Hence similarly to
A_{2}
we can write

A_{3}\geq-\lambda^{\prime}\sqrt{E_{0T}}\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F},

(A.19)

since there are
E_{0T}
non-zero values in
\mathcal{E}_{0}
by Assumption 2.

Putting together all three lower bounds in equations (
A.16
), (
A.18
) and (
A.19
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})

\displaystyle\geq c_{1}\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2}-C_{0}r_{T}\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F}-\lambda^{\prime}\sqrt{E_{0T}}\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F}

\displaystyle=r_{T}(c_{1}R^{2}r_{T}-C_{0}Rr_{T}-\lambda^{\prime}\sqrt{E_{0T}}R)

\displaystyle\geq r_{T}(c_{1}R^{2}r_{T}-C_{0}Rr_{T}-C_{1}\frac{r_{T}}{P_{T}}\sqrt{E_{0T}}R)

\displaystyle=r_{T}^{2}\bigg(c_{1}R^{2}-C_{0}R-C_{1}\frac{\sqrt{E_{0T}}}{P_{T}}R\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq r_{T}^{2}(c_{1}R^{2}-(C_{0}+C_{1})R),

where we used matrices
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
such that
\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F}=Rr_{T}
. The last inequality follows from Assumption 2 sparsity constraint, namely
E_{0T}\ll P_{T}(P_{T}-1)
, thus
\frac{\sqrt{E_{0T}}}{P_{T}}\leq 1
. Hence from Lemma
2
, we obtain
\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})>0
with high probability if
R
is such that
R>\frac{C_{0}+C_{1}}{c_{1}}=:\tilde{C}
, so we let
R=\tilde{C}+\delta
, for
\delta>0
.

Therefore with at least probability
(1-1/P_{T}^{\nu-2})
, no minimiser of
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j})
(and therefore of
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j})
) can lie outside the Frobenius ball of radius
Rr_{T}
around
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0j}
, which implies that

\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}-\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}\|_{F}=\mathcal{O}_{p}(Rr_{T}).

This yields the claimed
\mathcal{O}_{p}(Rr_{T})
bound.

A.3
Proof of Proposition
2

Proof.

Consider the
\mathcal{CIG}
s for two scales
j_{1}
and
j_{2}
. From Proposition
1
, the
\mathcal{CIG}
s across scales
j
coincide, i.e., for a pair of nodes
(p,q)
corresponding to no edge in the conditional independence graph of the process, we have
(\mathbf{f}^{-1}_{j_{1}}(\omega))_{p,q}=0
and
(\mathbf{f}^{-1}_{j_{2}}(\omega))_{p,q}=0
for all frequencies
\omega
and fixed scales
j_{1},\,j_{2}
. This is equivalent to the sum of their modulus
|(\mathbf{f}^{-1}_{j_{1}}(\omega))_{p,q}|+|(\mathbf{f}^{-1}_{j_{2}}(\omega))_{p,q}|=0
for all
\omega
, which in turn is equivalent to (see proof of Theorem
1
)
|(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j_{1}})_{p,q}|+|(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j_{2}})_{p,q}|=0
.

Since Theorem
1
establishes consistency of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{j_{1}}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{j_{2}}
, by the continuous mapping theorem applied to the bivariate random variable
(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{j_{1}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{j_{2}})
with the function
f(x,y)\to|x|+|y|
, we obtain that
|(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{j_{1}})_{p,q}|+|(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{j_{2}})_{p,q}|
is consistent for
|(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j_{1}})_{p,q}|+|(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j_{2}})_{p,q}|
.

The condition for the combination of scales,
\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{p,q}=1
iff
(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{j_{1}})_{p,q}\neq 0
or
(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{j_{2}})_{p,q}\neq 0
is equivalent to
\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{p,q}=0
iff
|(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{j_{1}})_{p,q}|+|(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{j_{2}})_{p,q}|=0
, and thus the scale combination procedure in Section
3.3
results in a consistent estimator of the
\mathcal{CIG}
.
