Prospective management of biopsy-proven ocular mucous membrane pemphigoid using an inflammation-oriented stepladder treatment strategy
Authors: Konstantina Bachtalia, Veatriki Athanasiou, Aikaterini Athanasiadou, Maria Mavrommati, Sotiria Palioura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective/Purpose:To prospectively describe the implementation and clinical outcomes of an inflammation-oriented management strategy for biopsy-proven ocular mucous membrane pemphigoid (MMP), in which the maximum conjunctival inflammation grade of the more severely affected eye was used to assign the initial treatment-intensity tier.Design:Prospective cohort study.Participants:Forty-eight (48) patients (96 eyes) with biopsy-proven ocular MMP.Methods:Patients with biopsy-confirmed ocular MMP were prospectively managed between 2018 and 2025 using an inflammation-oriented stepladder treatment protocol. Moderate to severe cases received intravenous cyclophosphamide or rituximab, while milder cases were treated with oral mycophenolate mofetil. Clinical outcomes, treatment response, disease relapse, and treatment-related adverse events were prospectively recorded.Results:Disease activity at presentation was minimal in 1 patient (2.1%), mild in 28 (58.3%), moderate in 7 (14.6%), and severe in 12 (25.0%). . Initial treatment corresponded to the intended inflammation-based tier in 46 patients (95.8%). Complete inflammatory control (grade 0 in both eyes) was achieved in 23 patients (47.9%) at a median of 9 months (range, 3–12 months), and 3 patients (6.3%) subsequently achieved drug-free remission. During follow-up, disease relapse occurred in 6 patients (12.5%), all of whom were successfully managed with escalation of systemic immunosuppression. At final follow-up, no eyes demonstrated moderate or severe inflammation. Treatment-related adverse events were manageable, with mycophenolate mofetil dose reduction required in 5 patients (10.4%). No treatment-related deaths occurred.Conclusion:This prospective study supports the implementation and clinical outcomes of an inflammation-oriented management protocol for biopsy-proven ocular MMP. Future comparative studies are warranted to validate this approach and determine its broader clinical utility.Keywords: Tauber staging, Foster staging, Immunosuppression, Direct immunofluorescence, Ocular cicatricial pemphigoid

Introduction

Mucous membrane pemphigoid (MMP) is a rare, chronic, cicatrizing, immune-mediated disease that may involve the conjunctiva and lead to progressive fibrosis, symblepharon, ocular surface failure, and vision loss.1-3 Diagnosis is based on compatible clinical findings supported by conjunctival biopsy with direct immunofluorescence (DIF), which remains the diagnostic gold standard.1,4 Management of ocular MMP is challenging because structural disease severity and current inflammatory activity represent related but distinct components of the disease. The Tauber/Foster staging system characterizes cumulative conjunctival cicatrization,5,6 whereas assessment of active inflammation provides complementary information relevant to ongoing disease activity and treatment decisions.7-9 Therefore, both structural disease stage and current inflammatory activity should be incorporated into therapeutic decision-making and longitudinal follow-up. The European Guidelines for the diagnosis and management of MMP emphasize that the primary aim of treatment is to suppress inflammation and thereby prevent further fibrotic damage to mucosal tissues, particularly in the conjunctiva.10

Systemic immunosuppressive therapy remains the standard of care for treating ocular MMP,2,5,11 with treatment intensity individualized according to disease activity, severity, risk of progression, and patient-specific factors. The European Guidelines recommend a tiered, stepladder approach to systemic therapy with oral corticosteroids used for initial inflammatory control, in conjunction with a corticosteroid-sparing immunosuppressant and escalation to more potent agents for severe, progressive, or refractory disease.10 Because these therapies differ substantially in toxicity and practical considerations, treatment selection requires balancing adequate inflammatory suppression against the risks of immunosuppression.10-12

In this prospective study, we evaluated the clinical outcomes of an inflammation-oriented stepladder treatment strategy for biopsy-proven ocular MMP. Conjunctival inflammatory activity was graded from 0 to 4 in each conjunctival quadrant using clinical descriptors based on those described by Ong et al.11 For each eye, the highest grade observed among the four quadrants was recorded as the maximum conjunctival inflammation grade. The maximum grade of the more severely affected eye was used to assign the initial treatment-intensity tier, while additional clinical and logistical considerations informed individualized treatment decisions. Rather than validating a novel grading instrument, our objective was to determine the feasibility and clinical outcomes of this management strategy in a prospective cohort of patients treated at a tertiary referral center.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

This prospective cohort study, conducted between January 2018 and March 2025 at a tertiary referral center in Athens, Greece, enrolled 48 patients (96 eyes) with biopsy-proven ocular MMP. Eligible patients were staged at presentation using the Tauber/Foster staging criteria for conjunctival scarring. ,56 Eligibility required biopsy confirmation from conjunctiva, skin, or oral mucosa and a minimum clinical follow-up of 9 months. Patients were excluded if they had received systemic immunosuppressive therapy within the preceding 3 months, had another cicatrizing ocular surface disorder (e.g. atopic keratoconjunctivitis, Stevens-Johnson syndrome or chemical injury), had inadequate follow-up, or were nonadherent to therapy. During the study period, 4 patients were excluded because of systemic immunosuppressive therapy within the preceding 3 months. In cases of conjunctival biopsy, bilateral sampling was performed when both eyes were clinically involved, in order to enhance diagnostic yield given the known variability in direct immunofluorescence sensitivity between eyes.13 When one eye was severely cicatrized and monocular vision remained in the fellow eye, only the more affected eye was biopsied to minimize patient risk.

The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and received Institutional Review Board approval (AEE 269/05–01–18). Informed consent was obtained from all participants and included the publication of clinical details and accompanying images.

All patients were evaluated by an ocular surface specialist (SP), screened for extraocular manifestations of MMP, and co-managed with a rheumatologist specializing in MMP management (MM).

Outcome measures

The primary outcome measure was achievement of complete inflammatory control, defined as absence of clinically detectable conjunctival inflammation (grade 0) in both eyes while receiving systemic immunosuppressive therapy. Secondary outcomes included drug-free remission, disease relapse, and treatment-related adverse events.7, 8, 14-16 Drug-free remission was defined as complete inflammatory control in both eyes, without evidence of progressive conjunctival cicatrization, maintained for at least 3 months after discontinuation of systemic immunosuppressive therapy. Disease relapse was defined as recurrent conjunctival inflammation or progressive conjunctival cicatrization after documented drug-free remission or new-onset conjunctival inflammation after prior achievement of complete inflammatory control. Therapy-related side effects were defined as any adverse event requiring dose reduction or discontinuation of systemic treatment.

Treatment protocol

Conjunctival inflammatory activity was assessed by direct visualization of bulbar conjunctival hyperemia and graded as previously described.11,25 For each eye, the most inflamed conjunctival quadrant was assigned an inflammation grade ranging from 0 to 4 (0, none; 0.5–1.0, minimal; 1.5–2.5, mild; 3.0–3.5, moderate; and 4.0, severe), and this value was recorded as the maximum conjunctival inflammation grade. Representative clinical examples are shown in Fig. 1. Corneal melt, conjunctival ulceration, and the extent of conjunctival cicatrization were not components of the inflammation grade and were assessed separately.

For treatment assignment, patients were stratified according to the maximum conjunctival inflammation grade of the more severely affected eye. This grade established the intended initial treatment-intensity tier: observation for minimal inflammation (0.5–1.0; Figs. 1A, 1B), oral mycophenolate mofetil 2 g daily for mild inflammation (1.5–2.5; Figs. 1C, 1D), and intravenous therapy with cyclophosphamide or rituximab for moderate (3.0–3.5; Figs. 1E, 1F) or severe (4.0; Figs. 1G, 1H) inflammation (Fig. 2)7-9,14-16,24 Patients with moderate disease were subsequently transitioned to mycophenolate mofetil 2 g daily and those with severe disease to 3 g daily.

Prior to finalizing inflammation grading and treatment assignment, potential local contributors to conjunctival injection – such as trichiasis, topical medication toxicity, ocular surface dryness – were identified and managed conservatively to minimize their effect on conjunctival hyperemia. Specifically, trichiatic lashes were epilated, offending topical medications were discontinued when appropriate, and ocular surface dryness was treated with lubrication and autologous serum tears, followed by reassessment of conjunctival inflammation after 7–10 days. Definitive surgical correction of eyelid malposition was deferred until adequate systemic inflammatory control had been achieved.

For patients with moderate or severe inflammation, intravenous cyclophosphamide was preferentially selected in the presence of active corneal melt or conjunctival ulceration, as well as for monocular or younger patients (<55 years), in whom rapid inflammatory suppression was considered particularly important. Rituximab was used in the remaining patients when clinically appropriate and logistically feasible. Cyclophosphamide was administered monthly for 4–6 months until complete inflammatory control, according to body surface area (400–1800 mg/m2).16 Rituximab was administered as 1 g on days 0 and 15 and repeated every 6 months as clinically indicated.9,15,24

All patients requiring systemic immunosuppressive therapy received oral prednisone at an initial dose of 1 mg/kg/day. In patients treated with mycophenolate mofetil, Prednisone was gradually tapered over approximately 3 months. In patients treated with intravenous cyclophosphamide or rituximab, prednisone was tapered individually according to clinical response under the joint supervision of the treating rheumatologist and ophthalmologist. Patients treated with rituximab received intravenous methylprednisolone 125 mg before each infusion as premedication to reduce the risk of infusion-related reactions, consistent with the manufacturer’s prescribing recommendations.17 Patients treated with intravenous cyclophosphamide received intravenous methylprednisolone 500 mg with each infusion as part of the institutional rheumatology pulse-induction regimen. Repeated intravenous methylprednisolone pulses administered in conjunction with cyclophosphamide have been described in contemporary rheumatologic induction protocols for severe autoimmune diseases (e.g. lupus nephritis), although corticosteroid dose and scheduling vary across institutions.18,19

When both cyclophosphamide and rituximab were considered clinically appropriate, additional real-world logistical considerations influenced treatment selection. Intravenous cyclophosphamide was favored in cases requiring immediate intervention due to its faster availability, whereas rituximab, which required regulatory approval with a typical delay of up to three weeks, was selected in non-urgent cases. During the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2022), cyclophosphamide was also preferred in some cases to avoid the elevated infection risk associated with B-cell depletion therapy.20,21

All patients receiving systemic immunosuppressive therapy were comanaged by an expert rheumatologist (MM), who oversaw laboratory workups, treatment clearance, and monitoring for systemic complications. Patients underwent regular blood tests and clinical reviews, and any new symptoms – such as viral upper respiratory infections – prompted a temporary discontinuation of immunosuppressive therapy until clinical recovery. This collaborative approach ensured optimal patient safety and treatment continuity throughout the study period.

Statistical analysis

All patient data were entered and managed in Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and median (range), whereas categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Given the descriptive observational design of the study and the absence of predefined comparative hypotheses or treatment groups, inferential statistical analyses were not performed.

Results

Demographics and baseline clinical data

Demographics, biopsy results and medical history of each patient in the study with ocular MMP are presented in Table 1. Key clinical features, the stepladder therapeutic approach and treatment outcomes are summarized in Table 2. The study enrolled 48 patients, 19 males (39.6%) and 29 females (60.4%), with a mean age of 74.8 ± 12 years (median 77 years, range 39 – 91). The mean duration of follow up was 40.6 ± 30.3 months (median 32.5 months, range 12 – 87). The mean best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) across all study eyes at presentation was 1.00 logMAR (median 0.52 logMAR, range 0 – 3.2). Pathology and immunostaining findings

All participants had biopsy-confirmed MMP from the conjunctiva (n = 42/48, 87.5%), the skin (n = 3/48, 6.3%) or the oral mucosa (n = 3/48, 6.3%). Of the 42 patients that underwent conjunctival biopsy, 34 had bilateral biopsy and 8 unilateral due to severe keratinization in the contralateral eye (i.e. 76 biopsy samples in total). All biopsies were performed at our center using a standardized technique.22 None of the patients were receiving systemic immunosuppression at the time of biopsy or during the preceding 3 months, per the study’s inclusion criteria. However, some patients had received topical corticosteroids or cyclosporine prior to referral. Immunostaining findings are summarized in Table 1.

Disease activity & severity at presentation

At the time of initial assessment, 8 eyes (8/96; 8.3%) presented with minimal conjunctival inflammation (grade 0.5 – 1), 55 eyes (55/96; 57.3%) with mild (grade 1.5 – 2.5), 16 eyes (16/96; 16.7%) with moderate (grade 3.0 – 3.5), and 17 eyes (17/96; 17.7%) with severe (grade 4.0), based on the maximum conjunctival inflammation grade used in this study. Corneal melt was noted in 6 eyes (6/96; 6.3%), and conjunctival ulceration was present in 4 eyes (4/96; 4.2%). In terms of conjunctival scarring at presentation, significant scarring, defined as Tauber/Foster stage greater than IIb (lower fornix shortening >25%) or Tauber stage greater than IIIb (presence of lower lid symblepharon >25%),11 was present in 86 eyes (86/96; 89.6%). Only 2 patients (4/96; 4.2%) had milder scarring in both eyes, and 5 patients (10/96; 10.4%) presented with milder scarring in one eye and significant in the other eye.

Trichiasis emerged as a prevalent issue, affecting 79 eyes (79/96; 82.3%); 36 of these eyes (36/96; 37.5%) also had entropion at presentation. A total of 14 patients (14/48; 29.2%) had undergone bilateral eyelid or conjunctival surgery (e.g. blepharoplasty, entropion repair, symblepharon release) prior to presentation and 2 (2/48; 4.2%) had undergone penetrating keratoplasty in one eye, both failed. Additionally, glaucoma was diagnosed in nearly half of the patients, with 23 cases (23/48; 47.9%) in total.

Prior to referral to our center, 4 patients (4/48; 8.33%) had received oral prednisone and 8 patients (8/48; 16.6%) had been treated with steroid-sparing immunosuppressive agents (e.g., azathioprine, methotrexate, or mycophenolate mofetil) at some point. In all cases, systemic treatment had been discontinued for at least 3 months before enrollment, per study inclusion criteria. A total of 16 patients (16/48; 33.3%) were using topical corticosteroids, 6 (6/48; 12.5%) topical cyclosporine 0.05%, and 2 (2/48; 4.2%) were using both topical corticosteroids and topical cyclosporine at initial assessment in both eyes.

Ten patients (10/48; 20.8%) also had extraocular symptoms and manifestations of MMP; 3 (3/48; 6.3%) had skin lesions, 4 (4/48; 8.3%) dysphagia, 2 (2/48; 4.2%) mouth sores, and 1 (1/48; 2.1%) throat irritation. In terms of co-existent systemic autoimmune diseases, 2 patients (2/48; 4.2%) had history of autoimmune hepatitis and 3 (3/48; 6.3%) had Hashimoto’s thyroiditis. Three patients (3/48; 6.3%) had history of treated cancer prior to the initiation of immunosuppression for MMP.

Response to treatment and management

Based on the maximum conjunctival inflammation grade of the more severely affected eye, inflammatory activity at presentation was minimal in 1 patient (2.1%), mild in 28 (58.3%), moderate in 7 (14.6%), and severe in 12 (25.0%) (Table 3). The maximum conjunctival inflammation grade identified the intended initial treatment-intensity tier in 47 of 48 patients (97.9%), and actual initial treatment corresponded to that tier in 46 of 48 patients (95.8%). One patient with moderate inflammation was assigned to intravenous induction therapy but declined it because of geographic/logistical constraints and initially received MMF. A second patient with minimal inflammation in both eyes received MMF despite an inflammation-based assignment to observation because treatment was individualized in the setting of advanced bilateral cicatricial disease (Tauber/Foster stage IIIa in one eye and IIIb in the fellow eye). At the onset of the study, 17 patients (17/48; 35.4%) received intravenous cyclophosphamide as their initial treatment, while only 1 patient (1/48; 2.1%) was started on rituximab, and the majority, 30 patients (30/48; 62.5%), began treatment with mycophenolate mofetil (MMF). Over the course of the study, MMF proved to be the predominant medication, used by 39 patients (39/48; 81.3%) at the last follow-up. Additionally, 6 patients (6/48; 12.5%) continued with rituximab at the end of the study period. Complete inflammatory control (grade 0 in both eyes) was achieved in 23 of 48 patients (47.9%), at a median of 9 months (range 3–12 months). Three patients (3/48; 6.3%) subsequently achieved drug-free remission and remained in drug-free remission for more than one year.

Disease relapse was observed in 6 patients (6/48; 12.5%) on MMF after prior achievement of complete inflammatory control (grade 0 in both eyes). The median time to relapse was 20 months (range: 11–26 months). Salvage therapies included rituximab in 5 cases (5/48; 10.4%) and cyclophosphamide in 1 case (1/48; 2.1%). By the last follow-up, a significant number of patients were on supportive treatments: 15 patients (15/48; 31.3%) used topical steroids, 7 (7/48; 14.6%) were on topical cyclosporine, 11 (11/48; 22.9%) utilized serum tears and an equal number required a bandage contact lens to manage ocular surface irritation. In all cases, systemic inflammation had been previously controlled, and topical treatment was maintained for ongoing ocular surface discomfort, punctate staining, or mechanical irritation rather than active cicatrizing inflammation.

Cicatricial progression occurred in 5 of 48 patients (10.4%) and 5 of 96 eyes (5.2%), exclusively in eyes experiencing disease relapse. Progression consisted of worsening of Tauber/Foster stage in the affected eye. One additional patient experienced bilateral inflammatory relapse without a change in Tauber/Foster stage. No Tauber/Foster stage progression was observed in patients without disease relapse.

At the final evaluation, complete inflammatory control (grade 0) was achieved in 32 eyes (32/96; 33.3%). An additional 23 eyes (23/96; 24.0%) demonstrated minimal residual inflammation (grade 0.5–1.0), whereas 41 eyes (41/96; 42.7%) showed mild disease activity (grade 1.5–2.5). No eyes demonstrated moderate or severe inflammation at the final follow-up. The mean BCVA at the last follow-up had improved to 0.82 logMAR (median 0.40 logMAR, range 0.0 – 3.2).

Duration of treatment and complications

The duration of immunosuppressive treatment averaged 25.8 months (median 23.5 months, range 9 – 74). Complications possibly related to long-term immunosuppression included gastrointestinal irritation in 3 patients (3/48; 6.3%) receiving MMF, urinary tract infections in 2 patients (2/48; 4.2%) also treated with MMF, and one case of pneumonia (1/48; 2.1%) in a patient on IV cyclophosphamide. Two patients (2/48; 4.2%) developed colon and esophageal cancer, respectively, both of whom had been treated with MMF for 25 months and 10 months, respectively. Notably, 5 patients (5/48; 10.4%) required MMF dose reduction due to gastrointestinal symptoms or recurrent urinary tract infections. No treatment-related deaths were recorded.

Discussion

In this prospective cohort of 48 patients with biopsy-proven ocular MMP, we describe the implementation and clinical outcomes of an inflammation-oriented stepladder treatment strategy in which the maximum conjunctival inflammation grade of the more severely affected eye was used to assign the initial treatment-intensity tier. The intended treatment tier was identified by the maximum inflammation grade in 47 of 48 patients (97.9%), and treatment actually administered corresponded to that tier in 46 patients (95.8%). In this cohort, the inflammation grade therefore functioned primarily as a treatment-stratification variable rather than as an independent grading instrument. Additional clinical and logistical considerations were used to modify the intended treatment tier when necessary and, in patients requiring intravenous induction therapy, to inform the choice between cyclophosphamide and rituximab. Thus, the inflammation grade should be interpreted as one component of a broader clinical management strategy rather than as an independently validated tool for therapeutic decision-making.

The cohort was restricted to biopsy-proven MMP to reduce diagnostic heterogeneity. We acknowledge that negative immunopathology does not exclude ocular MMP in patients with characteristic clinical findings and that such patients may require similarly aggressive treatment.11 Accordingly, the findings of this study should not be interpreted as suggesting a different management approach for biopsy-negative ocular MMP.

While all biopsies were performed in a standardized manner at our center and patients were off systemic immunosuppression for at least 3 months, some had been using topical anti-inflammatory agents prior to referral. It is possible that prior topical treatment or disease chronicity may have influenced the intensity or composition of immunoreactant deposition, as has been previously reported.13,22

All clinical assessments and treatment decisions were performed by a single ocular surface specialist (SP), while systemic immunosuppressive therapy was co-managed by a rheumatologist. This included pre-treatment assessment of comorbidities, preventive health and vaccination status, laboratory monitoring, and treatment adjustments, with additional specialist consultation obtained when clinically indicated. This close collaboration enabled timely identification of complications and allowed for proactive management decisions, such as pausing immunosuppression in patients with active infections or systemic symptoms. Although this provided consistency in clinical assessment and systemic monitoring within the cohort, the single-center, single-ophthalmologist design may limit generalizability. The predominance of cyclophosphamide over rituximab in this cohort reflected clinical urgency, treatment availability, and COVID-19-related considerations rather than a presumption of superior efficacy, despite the greater potential toxicity of cyclophosphamide.

We recognize that conjunctival injection may be influenced by factors unrelated to autoimmune inflammation, such as ocular surface disease, exposure (e.g. dry eye or exposure), mechanical irritation (e.g., trichiasis), or topical medication toxicity. To address this, we ensured that conjunctival inflammation grading was performed after assessing and, when necessary, treating these local confounders. Nevertheless, distinguishing autoimmune conjunctival inflammation from nonspecific ocular surface hyperemia remains inherently subjective and represents a limitation of the inflammation assessment used in this study.

Extraocular symptoms and manifestations of MMP comprising skin lesions, dysphagia, mouth sores and throat irritation were recorded in 20.8% of our cases (Table 1). This aligns with previous findings, such as those by Ong et al. ,11 reporting that 27.4% of MMP cases presented with ocular-only involvement. Similarly, Shiffera et al.7 found that 24.3% of ocular MMP cases exhibited no active extraocular disease. Interestingly, MMP cases with initial extraocular involvement have an estimated annual risk of developing ocular disease of 0.014 per person-year.7 Patients with mild disease confined to the oral mucosa or skin often respond well to topical corticosteroids or immunomodulatory medications.9 However, severe cases with isolated laryngotracheal or esophageal involvement typically require more aggressive immunosuppressive treatments, similarly to ocular MMP, including cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate mofetil or rituximab.9

We also acknowledge that approximately one-third of patients remained on topical corticosteroids at final follow-up. However, these agents were prescribed as adjunctive therapy for ocular surface discomfort, punctate epithelial staining, or mechanical irritation rather than for control of active cicatrizing inflammation. In all such cases, systemic inflammation had previously been controlled with immunosuppression, and there was no evidence of progressive scarring or new symblepharon formation. Patients receiving adjunctive topical corticosteroids were therefore not classified as having drug-free remission unless systemic immunosuppressive therapy had been discontinued and the predefined remission criteria were fulfilled.

Disease relapse occurred in six patients (12.5%) after prior achievement of complete inflammatory control (grade 0 in both eyes). All six patients were receiving mycophenolate mofetil at the time of relapse and required escalation of systemic immunosuppressive therapy, consisting of rituximab in five patients and intravenous cyclophosphamide in one patient. A retrospective study by Saw et al.14 on ocular MMP assessing the effectiveness of immunosuppression strategies, including the use of mycophenolate mofetil and cyclophosphamide, reported a 20% relapse rate. Similarly, Thorne et al.16 detected an ocular relapse rate of 18.2% following disease remission and recorded a markedly low risk (0.03/person-years) of relapse in patients treated with cyclophosphamide. Moreover, Shifera et al.7 reported an incidence rate of ocular relapse of 0.02/person-years following systemic treatment for MMP. Specifically, the authors noted a relapse rate of 17.6% following drug-free remission, highlighting that cyclophosphamide and rituximab were associated with a significantly lower risk of relapse compared to other immunosuppressive agents.7

Complications potentially related to long-term immunosuppression in our study were diverse, including gastrointestinal irritation, urinary tract infections, pneumonia, as well as colon and esophageal cancer. As a result, the dosage of mycophenolate mofetil had to be reduced in 10.4% of patients due to these adverse effects. The reported complications of MMP treatment range from minor effects such as diarrhea, vomiting, and fatigue to major organ toxicity (e.g. nephrotoxicity, hepatotoxicity, neurotoxicity) and malignancies after prolonged use of immunosuppressants.2,9,12,14 Thorne et al.16 reported a cyclophosphamide discontinuation rate of 0.20/person-year due to side effects. Bohelay et al.9 described primarily infectious complications in 31.2% of patients with MMP following 2 cycles of rituximab. Saw et al.14 recorded adverse events requiring therapy modifications in 29% of ocular MMP patients, with mycophenolate mofetil showing the lowest frequency of side effects (15%) compared to other immunomodulatory agents.14 This was corroborated by Sobelweska et al.2 who reported mycophenolate mofetil to have the lowest rate (10–15%) of discontinuation due to side effects compared to other therapies.

Currently, there is no standardized treatment protocol for managing ocular MMP, largely due to the retrospective nature of previous studies.8,9,14,15,23,24 This limitation affects the ability to accurately assess the time required for disease control and the incidence of treatment-related adverse events. Indeed, in a recent study by Ruiz-Lozano et al.15 on ocular MMP the authors reported their inability to provide uniform treatment guidelines due to the 32-year time span of their retrospective study. In contrast, the prospective nature of the present study allowed us to monitor the need for systemic immunosuppressive therapy and its impact on treatment outcomes in real time.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single tertiary referral center with treatment decisions made by one cornea specialist, potentially limiting generalizability. Second, the maximum conjunctival inflammation grade used for treatment stratification was based on quadrant-level clinical descriptors described by Ong et al.11 Our prospective protocol was initiated in 2018, before publication of the subsequently validated Cicatrising Conjunctivitis Assessment Tool,25 and the individual quadrant grades required to retrospectively calculate its cumulative inflammation score were not retained in the study dataset. Consequently, concordance between the maximum conjunctival inflammation grade and the cumulative score could not be assessed, and the maximum inflammation grade was not independently evaluated for interobserver or intraobserver reproducibility. Third, although the maximum conjunctival inflammation grade was used to assign the intended initial treatment-intensity tier, additional clinical and logistical considerations could modify treatment selection. Furthermore, among patients requiring intravenous induction therapy, these considerations influenced the choice between cyclophosphamide and rituximab. Consequently, this prospective cohort was not designed to directly compare the efficacy or relapse rates of cyclophosphamide and rituximab. Finally, the rarity of ocular MMP precluded randomized comparisons between individual immunosuppressive agents.

In conclusion, this prospective study describes the implementation and clinical outcomes of an inflammation-oriented stepladder treatment strategy in 48 patients with biopsy-proven ocular MMP. The maximum conjunctival inflammation grade of the more severely affected eye was used to assign the initial treatment-intensity tier, with additional clinical and logistical considerations incorporated when necessary. These findings provide prospective descriptive data on the application of this management strategy but do not establish its effectiveness, superiority, or the independent clinical utility of the inflammation assessment. Future multicenter comparative studies are needed to evaluate its reproducibility and clinical utility.
