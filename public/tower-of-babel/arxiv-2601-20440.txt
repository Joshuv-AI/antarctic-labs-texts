On Portenko’s approximation of skew Brownian motion
Adam Bobrowski, Andrey Pilipenko

Abstract
From the perspective of the theory of operator semigroups, we reflect back on the classical theorem of Portenko devoted to approximation of skew Brownian motion. The theorem says that by concentrating the power of drift of a diffusion process around a point one obtains an equivalent of a semi-permeable membrane at this point, described by skew Brownian motion’s boundary condition. We prove convergence of the corresponding Feller semigroups and in doing so, generalize Portenko’s theorem to the case of the Walsh processes on star graphs. Our analysis leads through singular perturbations of Sturm–Liouville equations, and reveals that as a result of Portenko-type approximation parameters of Walsh processes are transformed in a simple and elegant manner.

Introduction

Skew Brownian motion has been introduced in Itô and McKean’s monograph
[
28
]
(see Problem 1, p. 115 there) as a modification of the standard Brownian motion in which the sign of each excursion from
0
is chosen (independently from other excursions) to be positive with probability
p\in(0,1)
, and negative with probability
1-p
; this characteristic property of skew Brownian motion is expressed in the boundary condition (see further on for details)

pf^{\prime}(0+)=(1-p)f^{\prime}(0-).

Later the process, together with its generalizations, including the Walsh’s spider process, gained its due recognition with the seminal paper of J.B. Walsh
[
46
]
. It is still a plausible object of study, perhaps because of surprising symmetries and regularities in its structure
[
5
,
9
,
11
,
10
,

,
2
,
26
,

,
31
,

,
34
,
38
,
47
]
.

In the somewhat forgotten work
[
40
]
of the second part of 1970s, described also in
[
41
]
*Thm. 3.4., p. 146 and preceding the paper of J.B. Walsh, N.I. Portenko also, independently, defined what turns out to be the skew Brownion motion, as a particular case of his generalized diffusions, and found the following approximation.

Let
a_{\varepsilon},\varepsilon>0
be nonnegative, continuous and integrable functions on
\mathbb{R}
with the following property: we have

\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}a_{\varepsilon}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z<\infty
, and
there is a constant
\alpha\geq 0
such that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{x}^{y}a_{\varepsilon}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z=\alpha[xy<0],\qquad\,x,y\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\},x<y,

(1.1)

where
[\cdot]
is the Iverson bracket. Such a family of functions can be constructed, for example, as follows: given a nonnegative, continuous and integrable function
a
on
\mathbb{R}
we define

a_{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon^{-1}a(\varepsilon^{-1}x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R};

(1.2)

then (
1.1
) holds with
\alpha\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}}a(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
. Portenko has proved that the Feller semigroups generated by

\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}f=\tfrac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}+a_{\varepsilon}f^{\prime}

where
a_{\varepsilon}
s have property (
1.1
), converge, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, to the skew Brownian motion with parameter

\widetilde{p}=\tfrac{\mathrm{e}^{\alpha}}{\mathrm{e}^{\alpha}+\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha}}.

(1.3)

Since
\widetilde{p}\geq\frac{1}{2}
, this skew Brownian motion can be interpreted as having an infinitesimal drift to the right at
x=0
, that is, a tendency to assign positive rather than negative signs of excursions from
0
.

It is the goal of this note to give a semigroup-theoretic proof of the Portenko theorem and in doing so to generalize it.
To this end, in Section
2
we consider diffusion processes on a finite interval with fixed diffusion coefficient, reflecting barriers at either end, a semi-permeable membrane in the interval center, and a drift that concentrates more and more around this center; we show that the limit process is a Brownian motion with transformed permeability coefficient of the membrane (see Theorem
2.1
). The proof, contained in Section
3
, leads through the analysis of the resolvents of the generators of the approximating processes and has the advantage of showing that the Portenko theorem involves an elegant singular perturbation of the related Sturm–Liouville equations.

In Section
4
the result is generalized further (see Theorem
4.1
) to the case where the underlying processes have values in a finite star graph. Interestingly, the proof of Theorem
4.1
is not a straightforward adaptation of the proof of Theorem
2.1
to the more complex situation. Rather, it involves deviceing a different, more handy, formula for the resolvents of approximating processes and that of the limit process (see formulae (
4.9
)–(
4.10
), (
4.20
) and Remark
4.4
).

In Section
5
we show that the results obtained on finite graphs can be extended to the case of infinite graphs as well. Here, we provide two proofs. In the first (see Section
5.4
), we treat
Theorem
4.1
as a key lemma, and examine the distributions of the processes involved. In the second (see Section
5.5
), in keeping with the spirit of the paper, we come back to the analysis of resolvents.

Before completing this introduction, we mention the paper by Le Gall
[
33
]
, which in a sense provides a converse to Portenko’s approximation. To elaborate on this succinct statement: Portenko’s theorem reveals that by concentrating the power of drift around a point one obtains an equivalent of a semi-permeable membrane at this point, described by skew Brownian motion’s boundary condition. Le Gall goes in the other direction: he considers processes that need to pass through a sequence of semi-permeable membranes, and shows that (under natural conditions) in the limit, as the membranes get closer to each other and their number grows appropriately, the processes converge to a one with ‘regular’ drift. See also
[

,

]
.

2.
Portenko-type approximation of skew Brownian motion

Let
C[-r,r]
be the space of continuous functions on the closed interval
[-r,r]
, where
r>0
is fixed throughout Sections
2
–
4
. Let also
p\in(0,1)
be given, and let
\mathcal{D}_{p,\varepsilon}\subset C[-r,r],\varepsilon>0
be the set of
f
such that

(a)

f
is twice continuously differentiable at either of the subintervals
[-r,0]
and
[0,r]
separately (with one-sided derivatives at each intervals’ ends),

(b)

f^{\prime}(-r)=f^{\prime}(r)=0
and the one-sided derivatives at
0
are related by
pf^{\prime}(0+)=(1-p)f^{\prime}(0-)
,

(c)

we have
\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0+)+a_{\varepsilon}(0)f^{\prime}(0+)=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0-)+a_{\varepsilon}(0)f^{\prime}(0-)
,

where
a_{\varepsilon},\varepsilon>0
is a family of functions described in the introduction. For
f\in\mathcal{D}_{p,\varepsilon}
it makes sense to define

\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}f=\tfrac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}+a_{\varepsilon}f^{\prime}

and it turns out that the so-defined
\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}
s are Feller generators
C[-r,r]
(see Section
3.2
for details).

Our first main theorem speaks of the convergence of the semigroups generated by
\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}
s.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\left(\mathrm{e}^{t\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}}\right)_{t\geq 0}
be the semigroup generated by
\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}
. Then

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathrm{e}^{t\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}}=\mathrm{e}^{t\mathfrak{A}_{\textnormal{skew}}},

strongly and uniformly with respect to
t
in compact subsets of
[0,\infty)
, where
\left(\mathrm{e}^{t\mathfrak{A}_{\textnormal{skew}}}\right)_{t\geq 0}
is the semigroup related to the skew Brownian motion with parameter

\widetilde{p}\coloneqq\tfrac{p}{p+(1-p)\mathrm{e}^{-2\alpha}}

(2.1)

(which is no smaller than
p
) and reflecting barriers at
-r
and
r
.

The semigroup
\left(\mathrm{e}^{t\mathfrak{A}_{\textnormal{skew}}}\right)_{t\geq 0}
featured in the theorem is generated by the operator
\mathfrak{A}_{\textnormal{skew}}
defined as follows. Its domain is composed of
f\in C[-r,r]
such that

(a)

f
is twice continuously differentiable at either of the subintervals
[-r,0]
and
[0,r]
separately (with one-sided derivatives at each intervals’ ends),

(b)

f^{\prime}(-r)=f^{\prime}(r)=0
and the one-sided derivatives at
0
are related by
\widetilde{p}f^{\prime}(0+)=(1-\widetilde{p})f^{\prime}(0-)
,

(c)

we have
f^{\prime\prime}(0+)=f^{\prime\prime}(0-)
,

and for such
f
we agree that
\mathfrak{A}_{\textnormal{skew}}f=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}
. The fact that
\mathfrak{A}_{\textnormal{skew}}
is a Feller generator can be seen as a by-product of the generation theorem for
\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon},\varepsilon>0
— it suffices to consider
a_{\varepsilon}=0
and take
\widetilde{p}
instead of
p
.

In Remark
4.4
we argue that the Feller process generated by
\mathfrak{A}_{\textnormal{skew}}
is a limit, as
\delta\to 0+
, of Brownian motions in
[-r,r]
that are reflected at the interval’s ends and upon any visit at
0
jump to
\delta
or
-\delta
with probabilities
\widetilde{p}
and
1-\widetilde{p}
, respectively. This supports our view of the process as a skew Brownian motion on
[-r,r]
, reflected at the interval’s ends. As a further, and stronger, support of this fact, in Section
4.3
we construct the process by means of excursions of the reflected Brownian motion on
[0,r]
.

The Trotter–Kato–Neveu theorem
[
12
,
13
,
21
,
25
,
39
,
30
]
is our main tool in proving Theorem
2.1
. To recall, this classical result of the theory of semigroups of operators asserts, in its simplest form, that the convergence of contraction semigroups, such as Feller semigroups in particular, is equivalent to the convergence of their Laplace transforms, that is, to the convergence of the resolvents of their generators. As applied to the case of our interest, it says that to establish Theorem
2.1
it suffices to show, once we know that
\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}
s are Feller generators, that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left(\lambda-\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}\right)^{-1}=\left(\lambda-\mathfrak{A}_{\textnormal{skew}}\right)^{-1}

strongly for all
\lambda>0
. We recall furthermore (see e.g.
[
30
]
p. 385, see also
[
21
]
*Chapter 4, Theorem 2.5) that such convergence implies (and is implied by) weak convergence of distributions of the corresponding processes in the Skorokhod space of paths, provided that the initial distributions of these processes converge weakly also.

Remark 2.2
.

(a)
A skew Brownian motion can be equivalently characterized by
\gamma\coloneqq\frac{1-p}{p}\in(0,\infty)
. This parameter transforms in a simpler way: we have
\widetilde{\gamma}=\mathrm{e}^{-2\alpha}\gamma
. Yet different parameter, used preferably by Portenko, is

c\coloneqq 2p-1=\frac{1-\gamma}{1+\gamma}\in(-1,1),

and here we have

\widetilde{c}=\tfrac{1-\gamma\mathrm{e}^{-2\alpha}}{1+\gamma\mathrm{e}^{-2\alpha}}=\tfrac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\alpha}+c(1+\mathrm{e}^{-2\alpha})}{1+\mathrm{e}^{-2\alpha}+c(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\alpha})}.

For
c=0
, that is,
\gamma=1
or
p=\frac{1}{2}
, the last formula reduces to that of Portenko, who proved in that case that the limit skew Brownian motion’s parameter is
\widetilde{c}=\tanh\alpha
. Similarly, (
2.1
) with
p=\frac{1}{2}
reduces to (
1.3
). Finally, we note that in terms of
\beta\coloneqq-\frac{1}{2}\ln\gamma
the above transformations take the following elegant form:

\displaystyle\gamma=\mathrm{e}^{-2\beta}\qquad

\displaystyle\longmapsto\qquad\widetilde{\gamma}=\mathrm{e}^{-2(\alpha+\beta)},

\displaystyle c=\tanh\beta\qquad

\displaystyle\longmapsto\qquad\widetilde{c}=\tanh(\alpha+\beta).

(b)
As already remarked in
[
37
]
, the assumption of non-negativity of
a_{\varepsilon}
s in Theorem
2.1
is not essential: it suffices to assume that

\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|a_{\varepsilon}(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x\eqqcolon M<\infty

(2.2)

and that (
1.1
) holds with certain constant
\alpha
which now need not be non-negative. (Of course, in this case
\widetilde{p}
o (
2.1
) can be smaller than
p
.) Hence, in Section
3
containing the proof of Theorem
2.1
, we drop the assumption of positivity and assume (
2.2
) instead.

Interestingly (and this will turn out important later), the assumption that
a_{\varepsilon}
s are continuous at
0
is also superfluous: we can work with
a_{\varepsilon}
s that have one-sided limits at this point, provided that condition (c) in the definition of
\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon})
is changed to read
\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0+)+a_{\varepsilon}(0+)f^{\prime}(0+)=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0-)+a_{\varepsilon}(0-)f^{\prime}(0-)
. In the proof presented in Section
3
we use only this weaker assumption on
a_{\varepsilon}
.

3.
Proof of Theorem
2.1

To prove our theorem we express the resolvents of
\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}
as integral operators

\left(\lambda-\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}\right)^{-1}g(x)=\int_{-r}^{r}K_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y)g(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad\lambda,\varepsilon>0,g\in C[-r,r]

(3.1)

where for certain two solutions, say,
k_{\varepsilon}
and
\ell_{\varepsilon}
, the first non-decreasing, the second non-increasing, of the related Sturm–Liouville eigenvalue problem, we have

K_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y)=\frac{2k_{\varepsilon}(x)\ell_{\varepsilon}(y)}{w_{\varepsilon}(y)}[x\leq y]+\frac{2k_{\varepsilon}(y)\ell_{\varepsilon}(x)}{w_{\varepsilon}(y)}[x>y],\quad x,y\in[-r,r],

(3.2)

with
w_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq k_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\ell_{\varepsilon}-k_{\varepsilon}\ell_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}
. The existence and convergence of these solutions is established in Section
3.1
, and the convergence of resolvents — in Section
3.2
. The latter convergence, in conjunction with the Trotter–Kato–Neveu theorem, will be used to prove our theorem.

To recall, it is a classical result (see e.g.
[
16
]
*Section VIII.2.7.1,
[
17
]
*Section 5.10 or
[
42
]
*Chapter 17)) that the resolvents of Sturm–Liouville operators can be expressed by means of two independent eigenvectors as in (
3.1
) and (

). In the probabilistic context, however, it is natural to use two special eigenvectors: one non-decreasing and one non-increasing (as above) so that in particular the Green’s function

is nonnegative. W. Feller attributes this idea to E. Hille (
[

]
*p. 483,
[
23
]
*p. 13) end develops it in his fundamental paper
[

]
*Section 12 devoted to the classification of boundary conditions for diffusion processes; see also

]
*Section VI.4.b.

3.1.
The existence and convergence of eigenvectors of related Sturm–Liouville problems

Throughout the rest of this section, instead of working with parameter
p
that transforms in a more complex way, we work with
\gamma=\frac{1-p}{p}
. Our first goal, given
\lambda>0
, is to find, for each
\varepsilon>0
, a solution to the Sturm–Liouville eigenvalue problem

\tfrac{1}{2}k_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}+a_{\varepsilon}k_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}=\lambda k_{\varepsilon}

(3.3)

with boundary and transmission conditions

k_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(-r)=0,k_{\varepsilon}(-r)=1\qquad\text{ and }\qquad k_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(0+)=\gamma k_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(0-).

(3.4)

By a solution we understand a function
k_{\varepsilon}\in C[-r,r]
such that

(a)

k_{\varepsilon}
is twice continuously differentiable in either of the subintervals
[-r,0]
and
[0,r]
separately (with one-sided derivatives at each intervals’ ends), and satisfies equation (
3.3
) there,

(b)

conditions (
3.4
) are met.

We note that such a

need not belong to the domain
\mathcal{D}_{p,\varepsilon}
of
\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}
(with
p=\frac{1}{1+\gamma}
) because
k_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(r)
in general differs from

(in fact, one can argue that

never vanishes). Nevertheless, since

is required to be a continuous function and (

) is to be satisfied, condition (c) of the definition of

holds automatically for a solution

Proposition 3.1
.

For each
\varepsilon>0
there is unique solution

to the problem (
3.3
)–(
3.4
), and it is strictly increasing. As

, the

\tfrac{1}{2}k^{\prime\prime}=\lambda k,\qquad k^{\prime}(-r)=0,k(-r)=1,k^{\prime}(0+)=\widetilde{\gamma}k^{\prime}(0-),

where

. Together with

For the proof of Proposition

we need a couple of lemmas.

Lemma 3.2
.

A function

is a solution to the problem (
3.3
)–(
3.4

k_{\varepsilon}(x)=1+2\lambda\int_{-r}^{x}\int_{-r}^{y}V_{\varepsilon}(y,z)k_{\varepsilon}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad x\in[-r,r]

(3.6)

where

V_{\varepsilon}(y,z)\coloneqq\mathrm{e}^{-2\int_{z}^{y}a_{\varepsilon}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u}\left(\gamma[yz<0]+[yz\geq 0]\right).

Proof.

Multiplying both sides of (
3.3
) by the integrating factor
\mathrm{e}^{2\int_{-r}^{x}a_{\varepsilon}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z}
and integrating we check that a solution has to satisfy

k_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(x)=2\lambda\int_{-r}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{-2\int_{y}^{x}a_{\varepsilon}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z}k_{\varepsilon}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad x\in[-r,0-].

(3.7)

Thus, we must have

k_{\varepsilon}(x)=1+2\lambda\int_{-r}^{x}\int_{-r}^{y}\mathrm{e}^{-2\int_{z}^{y}a_{\varepsilon}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u}k_{\varepsilon}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}y

and this is precisely condition (

Next, since

is to satisfy

\displaystyle k_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\gamma k_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(0-)\mathrm{e}^{-2\int_{0}^{x}a_{\varepsilon}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z}+2\lambda\int_{0}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{-2\int_{y}^{x}a_{\varepsilon}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z}k_{\varepsilon}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=2\lambda\int_{-r}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{-2\int_{y}^{x}a_{\varepsilon}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z}\left(\gamma[y<0]+[y\geq 0]\right)k_{\varepsilon}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=2\lambda\int_{-r}^{x}V_{\varepsilon}(x,y)k_{\varepsilon}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad x\in[0+,r].

(3.9)

Finally, using the fact that

is to be continuous at

, we see that

\displaystyle k_{\varepsilon}(x)=k_{\varepsilon}(0)+2\lambda\int_{0}^{x}\int_{-r}^{y}V_{\varepsilon}(y,z)k_{\varepsilon}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad x\in[0,r];

this establishes (

Vice versa, a direct differentiation shows that

In the proof of our next lemma a crucial role is played by the Bielecki-type norm (see

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
T_{\varepsilon}
be the transformation of

into itself given by

(T_{\varepsilon}f)(x)=1+2\lambda\int_{-r}^{x}\int_{-r}^{y}V_{\varepsilon}(y,z)f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad x\in[-r,r]

\|T_{\varepsilon}f-T_{\varepsilon}g\|_{\omega}\leq\frac{2\lambda\mathrm{e}^{2M}\max(1,\gamma)}{\omega^{2}}\|f-g\|_{\omega},\qquad f,g\in C[-r,r].

(3.11)

Proof.

Since
|V_{\varepsilon}(y,z)|\leq\mathrm{e}^{2M}\max(1,\gamma)\eqqcolon\widetilde{M}
, we see that

\displaystyle\|T_{\varepsilon}f-T_{\varepsilon}g\|_{\omega}

\displaystyle\leq 2\lambda\widetilde{M}\max_{-r\leq x\leq r}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\omega(x+r)}\int_{-r}^{x}\int_{-r}^{y}|f(z)-g(z)|\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}y\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\lambda\widetilde{M}\|f-g\|_{\omega}\max_{-r\leq x\leq r}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\omega(x+r)}\int_{-r}^{x}\int_{-r}^{y}\mathrm{e}^{\omega(z+r)}\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}y\right]

\displaystyle<\frac{2\lambda\mathrm{e}^{2M}\max(1,\gamma)}{\omega^{2}}\|f-g\|_{\omega}.\qed

Our final lemma speaks of convergence of

Lemma 3.4
.

We have
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}T_{\varepsilon}f=Tf
, where

(Tf)(x)=1+2\lambda\int_{-r}^{x}\int_{-r}^{y}V(y,z)f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad x\in[-r,r]

V(y,z)\coloneqq\mathrm{e}^{-2\alpha}\gamma[yz<0]+[yz\geq 0].

Proof.

Since

\displaystyle\|T_{\varepsilon}f-Tf\|

\displaystyle\leq 2\lambda\max_{-r\leq x\leq r}\int_{-r}^{x}\int_{-r}^{y}|V_{\varepsilon}(y,z)-V(y,z)|\,|f(z)|\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=2\lambda\int_{-r}^{r}\int_{-r}^{y}|V_{\varepsilon}(y,z)-V(y,z)|\,|f(z)|\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}y,

and the integrand above is bounded by

, it suffices to show that

4.

Walsh’s spider process is a generalization of skew Brownian motion, a process with values on a star graph — see the works cited above. \displaystyle f_{i}^{\prime}(x)

2.

4.3.
The process generated by
\mathfrak{A}_{\textnormal{Walsh}}
can be constructed by means of excursions of the reflected Brownian motion on
[0,r]

Formula (
4.11
) provides also a hint on how the process related to
\mathfrak{A}_{\textnormal{skew}}
can be constructed. Notably, as already remarked in Introduction, Itô and McKean have originally defined the skew Brownian motion by means of excursions of the reflected Wiener process on the line: each excursion of the former is defined as the excursion of the latter but with a random sign, being positive with probability
p
and negative with probability
1-p

[
2
]
): he takes an excursion of the reflected Brownian motion and puts it on the
i
th edge of the star graph with probability
p_{i}
.

We claim that the process related to
\mathfrak{A}_{\textnormal{Walsh}}
can be constructed analogously: the only difference is that
instead of excursions of the reflected Brownian motion on
[0,\infty)
we use those of the reflected Brownian motion on
[0,r]
, the Brownian motion with two reflecting barriers.

To begin with, let
X
be a Feller process on
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}
that behaves like a Brownian motion on each edge and is reflected at the outer nodes of the graph. The strong Markov property implies that the resolvent of
X
satisfies the relation

R^{X}_{\lambda}g(x)=R^{0}_{\lambda}g(x)+\mathrm{E}_{x}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda\sigma}R^{X}_{\lambda}g(0),\qquad g\in\mathcal{C},x\in K_{1,{\mathpzc k}},

(4.21)

in which
\sigma
is the random moment when
X
reaches the center of the graph for the first time, and

R^{0}_{\lambda},\lambda>0
is the resolvent of the process
X^{0}
obtained from
X
by killing it at
\sigma
. Furthermore, it can be checked that
R_{\lambda}^{0},\lambda>0
coincides with the resolvent
R_{\lambda}^{0},\lambda>0
of (
4.12
). Also, the calculation
\lambda R^{0}_{\lambda}\mathsf{1}(x)=\mathrm{E}_{x}\int_{0}^{\sigma}\lambda\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\,\mathrm{d}t=1-\mathrm{E}_{x}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda\sigma},
shows that
\mathrm{E}_{x}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda\sigma}
equals
1-\lambda R^{0}_{\lambda}\mathsf{1}(x)
and in particular coincides with

\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(x)
, introduced in (
4.19
). More intrinsically, (
4.21
) now reveals that to completely characterize
R_{\lambda}^{X},\lambda>0
all one needs to know is
R_{\lambda}^{X}g(0)
for
g\in\mathcal{C}
and
\lambda>0
and the resolvent
R_{\lambda}^{0},\lambda>0
of (
4.12
).

To continue, let
B^{\textrm{refl}}
be the reflected Brownian motion on
[0,r]
and let
R_{\lambda}^{\textrm{refl}},\lambda>0
be its resolvent. The relation
R_{\lambda}^{\textrm{refl}},\lambda>0
satisfies is a particular case of (
4.21
) with
{\mathpzc k}=1
and
R_{\lambda}^{0},\lambda>0
describing the minimal process in the interval
[0,r]
. Moreover,

R^{\textrm{refl}}_{\lambda}g(0)=\tfrac{C(g)}{\lambda C(\mathsf{1}_{[0,r]})},\qquad g\in C[0,r],\lambda>0,

(4.22)

where, as a counterpart of (
4.15
),

C(g)\coloneqq\frac{2}{\cosh\sqrt{2\lambda}r}\int_{0}^{r}\cosh\sqrt{2\lambda}(r-y)g(y)\,\mathrm{d}y
.

With these preparations out of the way, using
B^{\textrm{refl}}
, we can define the process, say,
W
, with values in the star graph, as follows. To begin with, we demand that, when started away from the graph center,
W
behaves like
B^{\textrm{refl}}
on the edge where it started, up to the moment of first reaching the graph center. Also, to describe its further behavior,
we first enumerate excursions of

in a measurable way (to recall, an excursion is a part of the trajectory of
B^{\textrm{refl}}
that is positive, and starts and ends at
0
, with no zeros in the middle), and then place an excursion (independently of other excursions and of
B^{\textrm{refl}}
) on the
i
th edge of the graph with probability
\widetilde{p}_{i}
of (
4.3
). It is thus clear that
W

behaves like a Brownian motion on each edge and is reflected at the outer nodes of the graph, and so, in particular, its resolvent

satisfies (
4.21
). Moreover,

R_{\lambda}^{W}g(0)=\sum_{i\in{\mathpzc K}}\widetilde{p}_{i}R^{\textrm{refl}}_{\lambda}g_{i}(0),\qquad g\in\mathcal{C},\lambda>0.

Finally, since (a) the constant
C_{i}(\mathsf{1})
, defined in (
4.15

and coincides with

featured in (

, the factor of

featured in (

\frac{\sum_{i\in{\mathpzc K}}\widetilde{p}_{i}C_{i}(g)}{\lambda C(\mathsf{1}_{[0,r]})}
and thus equals

. It follows that
R_{\lambda}^{W},\lambda>0
coincides with
\left(\lambda-\mathfrak{A}_{\textnormal{skew}}\right)^{-1},\lambda>0

5.
A version on the infinite graph

Our final goal is to establish a counterpart of Theorem
4.1
on the infinite star graph

K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}

with
{\mathpzc k}\geq 2
edges, that is, on the set obtained from
{\mathpzc k}
copies of the compactified half-line
[0,\infty]
by lumping all zeros into one point. Unfortunately, the methods developed in Sections
3
and
4
cannot be translated
verbatim
to this setting, mainly because there is no handy equivalent of the Bielecki-type norm in the space

C[0,\infty]

of continuous functions on
[0,\infty)
that have finite limits at infinity.

We will show, however, that the convergence result we are interested in can be proved with the help of Theorem
4.1
, treated as a key lemma, and some simple manipulations on measures. To this end, rather than beginning, as in Section
4
, with the description of generators and semigroups, we start with the construction of processes that uses those underlying Theorem
4.1
as building blocks. A direct proof of the convergence of the related resolvents is given later, in Section
5.5
.

5.1.
Construction of approximating processes

Our point of departure is a family
a_{\varepsilon,i},\varepsilon>0,i\in{\mathpzc K}
of continuous functions on
[0,\infty)
such that

M\coloneqq\max_{i\in{\mathpzc K}}\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\int_{0}^{\infty}|a_{\varepsilon,i}(u)|\,\mathrm{d}u<\infty

(5.1)

and, for certain reals
\alpha_{i}
,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{y}^{z}a_{\varepsilon,i}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u=\alpha_{i}[y=0],\qquad 0\leq y<z,i\in{\mathpzc K};

(5.2)

recall from (
4.2
) that
{\mathpzc K}\coloneqq\{1,\dots,{\mathpzc k}\}
. As already stated, such a family can be constructed with the help of
{\mathpzc k}
continuous, integrable functions
a_{i},i\in{\mathpzc K}
on
[0,\infty)
by
a_{\varepsilon,i}(x)=\varepsilon^{-1}a_{i}(\varepsilon^{-1}x),x\geq 0
, and then
\alpha_{i}=\int_{0}^{\infty}a_{i}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u,i\in{\mathpzc K}
. We are also given positive parameters
p_{i},i\in{\mathpzc K}
with the property that
\sum_{i\in{\mathpzc K}}p_{i}=1
.

These objects allow constructing the following two types of processes, of which our main processes of interest are build.

1.

It is clear that (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) imply that, for any
r>0
, the restrictions of
a_{\varepsilon,i}
s to
[0,r]
satisfy conditions (
4.1
) and (
4.2
). Hence, Theorem
4.1
applies, and in particular there are Feller processes generated by the operators
\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}
of this theorem. These processes, denoted
Y_{\varepsilon,r}
in what follows, have values in the finite star graph
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{r}
with
{\mathpzc k}
edges of length
r
(see Figure
2
) — the superscript in
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{r}
is to stress dependence on
r
.

2.

Let
C[-\infty,\infty]
be the space of continuous functions
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
that have finite limits at
\pm\infty
. Given

i\in{\mathpzc K}
and
\varepsilon>0
, we
let
a_{\varepsilon,i}^{e}
be the even extension of
a_{\varepsilon,i}
to the entire real line. With the help of this extended function, we define the domain of the operator
\mathfrak{G}_{\varepsilon,i}
in
C[-\infty,\infty]
to be composed of twice continuously differentiable
f
such that
\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}+a_{\varepsilon,i}^{e}f^{\prime}
belongs to this space, and for such
f
agree that

\mathfrak{G}_{\varepsilon,i}f=\tfrac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}+a_{\varepsilon,i}^{e}f^{\prime}.

To take a closer look at this operator, we recall that by assumption the limits
\lim_{x\to\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\pm\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(x)}
, where
\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(x)\coloneqq 2\int_{0}^{x}a_{\varepsilon,i}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,x\geq 0
, exist, are finite and differ from
0
. Hence,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\pm\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(x)}\int_{0}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{\mp\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(y)}\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x=\infty.

In the language of the Feller boundary classification (see the original
[
23
]
*p. 487 or e.g.
[
21
]
*p. 366,
[
36
]
*pp. 24–25) this means that
\infty
is a natural boundary for the related diffusion process, and by symmetry the same is true for
-\infty
. Theorem 1 on p. 38 in
[
36
]
asserts thus that
\mathfrak{G}_{\varepsilon,i}
is a Feller generator, and Remark 2 on the same page there supplies the information that the related Feller process is honest. We denote this process
Z_{\varepsilon,i}
; it has values in
\mathbb{R}
.

With processes
Y_{\varepsilon,r},\varepsilon>0
(for a fixed
r>0
) and
Z_{\varepsilon,i},\varepsilon>0,i\in{\mathpzc K}
under our belt, we construct processes
X_{\varepsilon},\varepsilon>0
that have values in the infinite graph
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}
via the procedure explained below; as we shall see later, the final result does not depend on the choice of
r
.

(
\star
)

If started at one of the points at infinity, that is, one of the outer nodes of
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}
,
X_{\varepsilon}
stays there for ever.

(
\star\star
)

If started at an
i
th edge at a distance
\geq r
from the graph center,
X_{\varepsilon}
evolves according to the rules of
Z_{\varepsilon,i}
up to the moment when
Z_{\varepsilon,i}
reaches
0
for the first time; from that moment on
X_{\varepsilon}
forgets its past and starts behaving as in rule (
\star\star\star
) below. Of course, in this description we identify points of the half-line
[0,\infty)
(contained in the state-space of
Z_{\varepsilon,i}
) with the corresponding points of the
i
th edge.

(
\star\star\star
)

If started at a distance
<r
from the graph center,
X_{\varepsilon}
behaves like
Y_{\varepsilon,r}
up to the the moment when its distance from the graph center reaches the level
r
for the first time. From that moment on
X_{\varepsilon}
forgets its past and starts to behave as in point (
\star\star
) above. Here, we see the finite graph
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{r}
as a subset of
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}
:

K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{r}\subset K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}.

A few remarks are here in order. Firstly, constructions of this type are well-known in stochastic analysis. For example, they are found useful in building
(a) global solutions to stochastic differential equations with locally Lipschitz coefficients
[
24
]
*Section 6, and (b) diffusions on manifolds as concatenations of diffusions defined on local charts
[
45
]
*Section 6.6.

Secondly, the construction works for two reasons: both kinds of processes involved possess the strong Markov property (which allows restarting them at Markov times) and they are locally consistent. The elaborate on the second reason stated succinctly above: a process which happens to be at a distance larger than
0
but smaller than
r
from the graph center does not need to know whether it has reached this position and is to proceed by following the rules of
Y_{\varepsilon,r}
or the rules of
Z_{\varepsilon,i}
(for some
i
); in this region both rules are the same.

Thirdly, to make sure the so-constructed process is honest we need to rule out the possibility of its explosion. But explosion could happen in two and only two scenarios. In the first of these, consecutive times of alternating between the graph center and points of distance
r
from it, and back, could be so small that they form a converging series. In the second, the process might, using the rules of
Z_{\varepsilon,i}
for some
i
, reach infinity in finite time.

Fortunately, we need not worry of the first scenario, because the time needed to go from the graph center to one of the points that lie at the distance
r
from the center is a positive random variable, and by the strong Markov property the terms in the series are independent; it follows that the sum of the series is infinite almost surely. Neither do we need to worry of the second scenario; since
\infty
is a natural boundary for
Z_{\varepsilon,i}
, with probability one it cannot be reached from the regular state-space in finite time.

Fourthly, and last, we readily check that the construction in fact does not depend on
r
; the final process does not depend on whether we have used
r
large or small.

5.2.
X_{\varepsilon}

The following lemma is a key to understanding processes

and

Lemma 5.1
.

For
\varepsilon,r>0
and

, let

be the time needed for

Then, for any

\lim_{r\to\infty}\sup_{\varepsilon>0,i\in{\mathpzc K}}\sup_{|x|\leq\rho}\mathsf{P}_{x}(\sigma_{\varepsilon,i,r}\leq t)=0.

Let, as before,
a_{\varepsilon,i}^{e}
be the even extension of

\displaystyle s_{\varepsilon,i}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{0}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(y)}\,\mathrm{d}y,\quad x\in\mathbb{R},\text{ and}

\displaystyle A_{\varepsilon,i}(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq 2\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}\circ s_{\varepsilon,i}\circ Z_{\varepsilon,i}(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s,\quad t\geq 0,

where

, respectively. It is well-known that the formula

B_{\varepsilon,i}(t)\coloneqq s_{\varepsilon,i}\circ Z_{\varepsilon,i}\circ A_{\varepsilon,i}^{-1}(t),\qquad t\geq 0

defines then a Brownian motion starting at

if

starts at

. (The general statement can be found e.g. in
[

*Thm. 23.9, one can also combine Theorems 46.12 and 47.1 in
[

\mathfrak{G}_{\varepsilon,i}f(x)=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(x)}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(x)}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}f(x))
, that by transforming time (this is the role of

) and space (the role of

can be seen as a standard Brownian motion.) Hence,
Z_{\varepsilon,i}\overset{d}{=}\widetilde{Z}_{\varepsilon,i}
(in distribution) where

\widetilde{Z}_{\varepsilon,i}(t)\coloneqq s_{\varepsilon,i}^{-1}(B(A_{\varepsilon,i}(t))+s_{\varepsilon,i}(x)),\qquad t\geq 0

(5.3)

and

is a standard Brownian motion starting at

.

Moreover, since
-2M\leq\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(x)\leq 2M

2\mathrm{e}^{-2M}t\leq A_{\varepsilon,i}(t)\leq 2\mathrm{e}^{2M}t,t\geq 0

\mathrm{e}^{2M}x\leq s_{\varepsilon,i}(x)\leq\mathrm{e}^{-2M}x

\mathrm{e}^{-2M}x\leq s_{\varepsilon,i}^{-1}(x)\leq\mathrm{e}^{2M}x

\mathrm{e}^{2M}x\leq s_{\varepsilon,i}^{-1}(x)\leq\mathrm{e}^{-2M}x
for

Therefore, by the formula just mentioned, our thesis reduces to the statement that

\lim_{r\to\infty}\sup_{|x|\leq\rho}\mathsf{P}(\tau_{x,r}\leq t)=0
,
where

. ∎

K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}

(for

\left(x_{n}\right)_{n\geq 1}
of elements of
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}
such that
x_{\infty}\coloneqq\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n}
exists.

Case 1. Finite

.

If

is not one of the outer nodes of
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}
, there is a

. Also, given
\epsilon>0
, one can find an
r>0
such that, by Lemma
5.1
,
\max_{i\in{\mathpzc K}}\mathsf{P}_{y}(\sigma_{\varepsilon,i,r}\leq t)<\frac{\epsilon}{6\|f\|}
for any real
y
with
|y|\leq\rho
.

Now, how could
X_{\varepsilon}
started in
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\rho}
differ from
Y_{\varepsilon,r}
? By definition they are the same up to (but not including) the moment when
Y_{\varepsilon,r}
reaches one of the outer nodes of
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{r}
for the first time. However,
Y_{\varepsilon,r}
cannot reach one of these nodes unless
Z_{\varepsilon,i}
, for some
i\in{\mathpzc K}
, goes from the level of
\rho
to the level of
r
, and the probability of this event has been chosen above to be small. It follows that for
x\in K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\rho}
,
g(x)
and
\mathsf{E}_{x}f(Y_{\varepsilon,r}(t))1_{\{\sigma_{\varepsilon,i,r}>t\}}
differ by at most
\frac{\epsilon}{6}
and by the same token so do

\mathsf{E}_{x}f(Y_{\varepsilon,r}(t))1_{\{\sigma_{\varepsilon,i,r}>t\}}
and
\mathsf{E}_{x}f(Y_{\varepsilon,r}(t))
. Hence,

|g(x)-\mathsf{E}_{x}f(Y_{\varepsilon,r}(t))|<\tfrac{\epsilon}{3},\qquad x\in K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\rho},

(5.4)

and, as a consequence,

|g(x_{\infty})-g(x_{n})|<\tfrac{2\epsilon}{3}+|\mathsf{E}_{x_{\infty}}f(Y_{\varepsilon,r}(t))-\mathsf{E}_{x_{n}}f(Y_{\varepsilon,r}(t))|,\qquad n\geq 1.

Since, by Theorem
4.1
,
Y_{\varepsilon,r}
is a Feller process in
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{r}
, letting
n\to\infty
above yields
\limsup_{n\to\infty}|g(x_{\infty})-g(x_{n})|\leq\frac{2\epsilon}{3}<\epsilon
. This shows
\lim_{n\to\infty}g(x_{n})=g(x_{\infty})
,
\epsilon>0
being arbitrary.

Case 2. Infinite
x_{\infty}
.

If
x_{\infty}
is one of the outer edges of
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}
, say, the
i
th, then without loss of generality we may assume that all
x_{n}
s lie at the
i
th edge, away from the graph center. Hence, by definition,
X_{\varepsilon}
started at these points behaves like
Z_{\varepsilon,i}
up to, but not including, the moment
when
Z_{\varepsilon,i}
reaches
0
for the first time.
Also, arguing as in the proof of Lemma
5.1
, that is, using representation (
5.3
), we can show that, for any
\rho>0
,

\lim_{y\to\infty}\mathsf{P}_{y}[\mathcal{B}_{\rho}]=1,

where
\mathcal{B}_{\rho}
is the set where
\inf_{s\in[0,t]}Z_{\varepsilon,i}(s)\geq\rho
. The above relation implies that
X_{\varepsilon}
started sufficiently far away from the center is indistinguishable from
Z_{\varepsilon,i}
with probability close to
1
.

So prepared, given
\epsilon>0
, let us choose
\rho
so large that

|f(x)-f(x_{\infty})|<\tfrac{\epsilon}{3},\qquad x\in V_{\rho},

(5.5)

where
V_{\rho}
is the set of points of the
i
th edge of
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}
that lie at a distance at least
\rho
from the graph center. Since
\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n}=x_{\infty},
the distance, say,
y_{n}
, of
x_{n}
from the graph center tends to infinity. Thus, for sufficiently large
n
we have, as established above,

\mathsf{P}_{y_{n}}(\mathcal{B}_{\rho})>1-\tfrac{\epsilon}{3\|f\|}.

As a result, the difference between
g(x_{n})
and
\mathsf{E}_{y_{n}}f^{\sharp}(Z_{\varepsilon,i}(t))1_{\mathcal{B}_{\rho}}
is less than
\frac{\epsilon}{3}
;
f^{\sharp}
featured here is the restriction of
f
to the
i
th edge of
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}
, as identified with a function on
[0,\infty]
. Since
\mathsf{E}_{y_{n}}f^{\sharp}(Z_{\varepsilon,i}(t))1_{\mathcal{B}_{\rho}}
, in turn, differs from
f(x_{\infty})\mathsf{P}_{y_{n}}(\mathcal{B}_{\rho})
, because of (
5.5
), by less than
\frac{\epsilon}{3}\mathsf{P}_{y_{n}}(\mathcal{B}_{\rho})\leq\frac{\epsilon}{3}
, we see that

|g(x_{n})-f(x_{\infty})|\leq\tfrac{2\epsilon}{3}+|f(x_{\infty})|(1-\mathsf{P}_{y_{n}}(\mathcal{B}_{\rho}))<\epsilon.

whenever
x_{n}
belongs to
V_{\rho}
, that is, for all sufficiently large
n
. Since
\epsilon
is arbitrary, this shows that

\lim_{n\to\infty}g(x_{n})=f(x_{\infty})=g(x_{\infty})
, as desired. This completes the proof of the fact that
g
belongs to
C(K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty})
.

We are thus left with showing that, for any
f\in C(K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty})
and
x\in K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}
,
\lim_{t\to 0+}\mathsf{E}_{x}f(X_{\varepsilon}(t))=f(x)
; we recall that according to the definition of Feller semigroup, we should in fact prove that this convergence is uniform with respect to
x\in K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}
, but the well-know result says that in this case pointwise convergence implies uniform convergence — see e.g.
[
30
]
*p. 369. However, condition
\lim_{t\to 0+}\mathsf{E}_{x}f(X_{\varepsilon}(t))=f(x)
is clear by the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem, because
X_{\varepsilon}
has continuous paths; we are done. ∎

5.3.
Generators of
X_{\varepsilon}

It is our next goal to characterize the generators of the processes
X_{\varepsilon}
. To this end, it will be convenient to think of the space
C(K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty})
as a subspace of the Cartesian product
(C[0,\infty])^{\mathpzc k}
of
{\mathpzc k}
copies of the space
C[0,\infty]
introduced at the beginning of this section. In other words, each
f\in C(K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty})
will be seen as a vector
\left(f_{i}\right)_{i\in{\mathpzc K}}
of elements of
C[0,\infty]
such that
f_{i}(0)
does not depend on
i\in{\mathpzc K}
.

This perspective on
C(K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty})
allows us to introduce operators
\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}
, later to be shown to be generators of
X_{\varepsilon}
, as follows. The domain
\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon})
is composed of elements
f
of
(C[0,\infty])^{\mathpzc k}
such that

(a)

each coordinate
f_{i}\colon[0,\infty)
of
f
is twice continuously differentiable in
[0,\infty)
(with one-sided derivatives at
x=0
), the limit
\lim_{x\to\infty}[\frac{1}{2}f_{i}^{\prime\prime}(x)+a_{\varepsilon,i}(x)f^{\prime}_{i}(x)]
exists and is finite,

(b)

\sum_{i=1}^{\mathpzc k}p_{i}f_{i}^{\prime}(0)=0
,

(c)

\frac{1}{2}f_{i}^{\prime\prime}(0)+a_{\varepsilon,i}(0)f_{i}^{\prime}(0)
does not depend on
i\in{\mathpzc K}
.

Also, for
f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon})
we agree that

\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}f=\left(\tfrac{1}{2}f_{i}^{\prime\prime}+a_{\varepsilon,i}f_{i}^{\prime}\right)_{i\in{\mathpzc K}}.

As a preparation for the proof of Proposition
5.3
(presented further down), we recall the following facts established in
[
36
]
*pp. 25–33. In the work cited they were proved in greater generality; we present them in a way that pertains directly to our scenario.

In the following list
\varepsilon>0
and
i\in{\mathpzc K}
are fixed and so is
\lambda>0
; the latter, however, is suppressed notationally.

1.

There is a unique continuous function
j_{\varepsilon,i}\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
such that

j_{\varepsilon,i}(x)=1+2\lambda\int_{0}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(y)}\int_{0}^{y}\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(z)}j_{\varepsilon,i}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad x\geq 0,

where, as before,
\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(x)\coloneqq 2\int_{0}^{x}a_{\varepsilon,i}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,x\geq 0
. Moreover,
j_{\varepsilon,i}(x)\geq\cosh\omega_{1}x
for
x\geq 0
, where
\omega_{1}\coloneqq\sqrt{2\lambda\mathrm{e}^{-2M}}
.

2.

Since
j_{\varepsilon,i}
grows exponentially, the integral
\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(y)}\frac{1}{[j_{\varepsilon,i}(y)]^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}y
is finite, and thus it makes sense to define

\displaystyle k_{\varepsilon,i}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq j_{\varepsilon,i}(x)\int_{0}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(y)}\tfrac{1}{[j_{\varepsilon,i}(y)]^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}y,

\displaystyle\ell_{\varepsilon,i}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq j_{\varepsilon,i}(x)\int_{x}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(y)}\tfrac{1}{[j_{\varepsilon,i}(y)]^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad x\geq 0.

Then all the three functions
j_{\varepsilon,i},k_{\varepsilon,i}
and
\ell_{\varepsilon,i}
are solutions to the differential equation
\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}+a_{\varepsilon,i}f^{\prime}=\lambda f
. Moreover,
k_{\varepsilon,i}
increases whereas
\ell_{\varepsilon,i}
decreases. The expression
w_{\varepsilon,i}(x)\coloneqq\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(x)}(k_{\varepsilon,i}^{\prime}(x)\ell_{\varepsilon,i}(x)-\ell_{\varepsilon,i}^{\prime}(x)k_{\varepsilon,i}(x))
in fact does not depend on
x
and equals
w_{\varepsilon,i}(0)=\ell_{\varepsilon,i}(0)\not=0
.

3.

For
\omega\in[0,\omega_{1})
,
\lim_{x\to\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\omega x}\ell_{\varepsilon,i}(x)=0
. It follows that for any
h\in C[0,\infty]
one can let

\displaystyle f_{\varepsilon,i}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\tfrac{2\ell_{\varepsilon,i}(x)}{w_{\varepsilon,i}}\int_{0}^{x}k_{\varepsilon,i}(y)\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(y)}h(y)\,\mathrm{d}y

(5.6)

\displaystyle\phantom{=======}+\tfrac{2k_{\varepsilon,i}(x)}{w_{\varepsilon,i}}\int_{x}^{\infty}\ell_{\varepsilon,i}(y)\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(y)}h(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad x\geq 0.

It turns out that
f_{\varepsilon,i}
belongs to
C[0,\infty]
and
\lambda f_{\varepsilon,i}-\frac{1}{2}f_{\varepsilon,i}^{\prime\prime}-a_{\varepsilon,i}f_{\varepsilon,i}^{\prime}=h
.

Proposition 5.3
.

Each
\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon},\varepsilon>0
is a Feller generator.

Proof.

Checking that
\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon})
is densely defined is relatively easy. Also, to establish the positive maximum principle, it suffices to consider the following three cases: (a) a positive maximum of an
f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon})
is attained at the graph center, (b) it is attained at a finite nonzero distance from the center, and (c) it is attained at one of the infinite nodes. Case (b) is immediate by a classical result of elementary analysis, and (a) can be taken care of as in e.g.
[
13
]
*p. 17, leaving us with (c).

To take care of (c), we claim that the value of
\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}f
can be positive at no infinite node. For, assume that it is positive at an
i
th node. Then, there is a
\delta_{0}>0
such that
\frac{1}{2}f_{\varepsilon,i}^{\prime\prime}(x)+a_{\varepsilon,i}(x)f^{\prime}_{\varepsilon,i}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(x)}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(x)}f_{\varepsilon,i}^{\prime}(x))\geq\delta_{0}
for
x
large enough. It follows that there is a
\delta_{1}
such that, for such
x
s,
\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(x)}f_{\varepsilon,i}^{\prime}(x)\geq\delta_{1}+2\delta_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-2M}x
, and so
f_{\varepsilon,i}^{\prime}(x)\geq\delta_{1}\mathrm{e}^{-2M}+2\delta_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-4M}x
. As a result,
\lim_{x\to\infty}f_{\varepsilon,i}^{\prime}(x)=\infty
. Since this contradicts the fact that
\lim_{x\to\infty}f_{\varepsilon,i}(x)
exists and is finite, the positive-maximum principle is established.

We are thus left with showing that, for any
g\in C(K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty})
and
\lambda>0
, there is an
f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon})
such that
\lambda f-\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}f=g
. To this end, we first define
R_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{0}g
by requiring that

R_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{0}g\coloneqq\left(f_{\varepsilon,i}\right)_{i\in{\mathpzc K}}

(5.7)

where
f_{\varepsilon,i}
is given by
(
5.6
) with
h
replaced by
g_{i}
. Next, we note that

f_{\varepsilon,i}^{\prime}(0)=\frac{2}{w_{\varepsilon,i}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\ell_{\varepsilon,i}(y)\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(y)}g_{i}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\eqqcolon C_{\varepsilon,i}(g),

and define

f\coloneqq R_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{0}g+\frac{\sum_{j\in{\mathpzc K}}p_{j}C_{\varepsilon,j}(g)}{\lambda\sum_{j\in{\mathpzc K}}p_{j}C_{\varepsilon,j}(\mathsf{1})}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda,\varepsilon},\qquad g\in C(K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}),

(5.8)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}\coloneqq\mathsf{1}-\lambda R_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{0}\mathsf{1}
whereas
\mathsf{1}\coloneqq(1_{[0,\infty)})_{i\in{\mathpzc K}}
and
1_{[0,\infty)}(x)\coloneqq 1,x\geq 0
.
To show that this
f
belongs to
\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon})
and
\lambda f-\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}f=g
holds, we argue as in Step 3 of the proof of Proposition
4.3
: in particular, we check that
p_{i}f_{\varepsilon,i}^{\prime}(0)=p_{i}C_{\varepsilon,i}(g)-p_{i}\frac{\sum_{j\in{\mathpzc K}}p_{j}C_{\varepsilon,j}(g)}{\sum_{j\in{\mathpzc K}}p_{j}C_{\varepsilon,j}(\mathsf{1})}C_{\varepsilon,i}(\mathsf{1}),
and so summing over all
i\in{\mathpzc K}
yields
0
. We omit the remaining details.
∎

Proposition 5.4
.

For
\varepsilon>0
, the process
X_{\varepsilon}
is generated by
\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}
.

Proof.

The proof becomes rather straightforward if the notion of
characteristic operator of Dynkin
is invoked.
To recall see
[
43
]
*p. 256, given
f\in C(K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty})
and
x\in K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}
we define

\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}f(x)\coloneqq\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{\mathsf{E}_{x}f(X_{\varepsilon}(\tau_{\delta}))-f(x)}{\mathsf{E}_{x}\tau_{\delta}},

(5.9)

if the limit exists and is finite; here,
\tau_{\delta}
is the first time
X_{\varepsilon}
exits the open ball centered at
x
with radius
\delta
. This definition needs to be modified if
x
is one of the external nodes of
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}
: in this case we simply let
\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}f(x)=0
. We say that an
f\in C(K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty})
belongs to the domain of the Dynkin operator iff
\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}f(x)
exists at all
x\in K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}
and
\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}f
is a member of
C(K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty})
.

Interestingly, even though more often than not, that is, under quite general mild conditions, the Dynkin operator extends the generator of a Markov process
(see
[
18
]
* §3 of Chapter 5), for each Feller processes it turns out to coincide with the generator (see
[
43
]
*p. 256, comp.
[
30
]
*Thm. 19.23).
Thus, our strategy of proving the proposition is to show that
\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}
is an extension of
\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}
. Since the former operator is no different than the generator of
X_{\varepsilon}
and no generator can be a proper extension of another generator, this will establish our claim.

Let, therefore,
f
belong to
\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon})
. We will show that
f
is in the domain of
\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}
and that
\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}f=\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}f
.

To this end, let first
x\in K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}
lie at an
i
th edge at a positive distance
y
from the graph center, and let
\delta
be smaller than
y
. By definition, the process
X_{\varepsilon}
started at
x
, up to the moment when it exists the interval
(y-\delta,y+\delta)
on the
i
th edge, is no different than
Z_{\varepsilon,i}
of Section
5.1
. Also,
f_{i}
can be modified in the interval
[0,y-\delta)
and extended to the entire left axis in such a way that the new function, say,
f_{i}^{\flat}
, belongs to the domain of the generator of
Z_{\varepsilon,i}
. Then the limit spoken of in the definition of the Dynkin operator for
Z_{\varepsilon,i}
at
f_{i}^{\flat}
and
y
exists and equals
\frac{1}{2}(f_{i}^{\flat})^{\prime\prime}(y)+a_{\varepsilon,i}(y)(f_{i}^{\flat})^{\prime}(y)=\frac{1}{2}f_{i}^{\prime\prime}(y)+a_{\varepsilon,i}(y)f_{i}^{\prime}(y)
. Since
f_{i}
and
f_{i}^{\flat}
coincide in
(y-\delta,y+\delta)
and, as already mentioned, up to exiting this interval
X_{\varepsilon}
differs not from
Z_{\varepsilon,i}
, we conclude that
\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}f(x)
is well-defined and equals
\frac{1}{2}f_{i}^{\prime\prime}(y)+a_{\varepsilon,i}(y)f_{i}^{\prime}(y)=\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}f(x)
.

The case in which
x
is the graph center is taken care of similarly. First of all, we fix
r
, and consider only
\delta<r
. Then,
X_{\varepsilon}
starting at the graph center is, up to the time when it reaches one of the outer nodes of
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\delta}
, the same as
Y_{\varepsilon,r}
. Secondly,
f
can be modified outside of
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\delta}
in such a way that this modification, say,
f^{\sharp}
, when restricted to
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{r}
belongs to the domain of the generator of
Y_{\varepsilon,r}
. It follows that the limit involved in the definition of the Dynkin operator for
Y_{\varepsilon,r}
exists and is equal to the value of the generator of
Y_{\varepsilon,r}
at
f^{\sharp}
and
x
, that is, to the common value of
\frac{1}{2}f_{i}^{\prime\prime}(0)+a_{\varepsilon,i}(0)f_{i}^{\prime}(0),i\in{\mathpzc K}
. Since the limit just spoken of is manifestly the same as the limit involved in the definition of
\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}f(x)
, we conclude that also the latter quantity is the common value of
\frac{1}{2}f_{i}^{\prime\prime}(0)+a_{\varepsilon,i}(0)f_{i}^{\prime}(0),i\in{\mathpzc K}
, and this is noting else but
\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}(x)
.

The final case is the simplest: if
x
is one of the outer nodes of
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}
,
\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}f(x)
is by definition
0
.

It remains to check that
\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}f
is a continuous function on
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}
, and the only points in doubt in
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}
are the outer nodes.
We need to check, thus, that for each
i\in{\mathpzc K}
, the limit
\lim_{x\to\infty}[\frac{1}{2}f_{i}^{\prime\prime}(x)+a_{\varepsilon,i}(x)f^{\prime}_{i}(x)]
is zero.
This, however, is rather simple: the argument presented in the proof of Proposition
5.3
persuades us that the limit cannot be larger than zero, and its straightforward modification shows that neither can it be strictly negative.

To summarize: we have established that at each point
x\in K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}
, the quantity
\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}f(x)
is well-defined and coincides with
\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}f(x)
; moreover,
\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}f=\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}f
is a continuous function on
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}
. This means that
f
is in the domain of the Dynkin operator for
X_{\varepsilon}
, and that the value of the Dynkin operator is the same as
\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}f
. Since
f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon})
is arbitrary, this completes the proof. ∎

5.4.
Convergence of
X_{\varepsilon}
, as
\varepsilon\to 0

Let us take another look at the definition of processes
X_{\varepsilon},\varepsilon>0
. Theorem
4.1
says that, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, their building blocks, that is, processes
Y_{\varepsilon,r}
, converge, for each
r>0
, to the Walsh processes on
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{r}
. It is thus natural to expect that
X_{\varepsilon}
s converge, to a process
X_{0}
that can be described as follows.

(
\star
)

If started at one of the outer nodes of
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}
,
X_{0}
stays there for ever.

(
\star\star
)

If started at an
i
th edge at a distance
\geq r
from the graph center,
X_{0}
behaves like a one-dimensional Brownian motion up to the moment when it reaches the graph center for the first time; from that moment on
X_{0}
forgets its past and starts behaving as in rule (
\star\star\star
) below.

(
\star\star\star
)

If started at a distance
<r
from the graph center,
X_{0}
behaves like the Walsh process on
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{r}
(with parameters
\widetilde{p}_{i},i\in{\mathpzc K}
specified in (
4.3
)) up to the the moment when its distance from the graph center reaches the level
r
for the first time. From that moment on
X_{0}
forgets its past and starts to behave as in point (
\star\star
) above.

Arguing as in Section
5.3
we check that
X_{0}
is a Feller process and its generator, say,
\mathfrak{A}_{0}
can be described thusly:
Its domain
\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{A}_{0})
is composed of elements
f
of
C(K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty})\subset(C[0,\infty])^{\mathpzc k}
such that

(a)

each coordinate
f_{i}\colon[0,\infty)
of
f
is twice continuously differentiable in
[0,\infty)
(with one-sided derivatives at
x=0
); the limit
\lim_{x\to\infty}f_{i}^{\prime\prime}(x)
exists and is finite,

(b)

\sum_{i=1}^{\mathpzc k}\widetilde{p}_{i}f_{i}^{\prime}(0)=0
,

(c)

f_{i}^{\prime\prime}(0)
does not depend on
i\in{\mathpzc K}
,

and for such
f
we agree that
\mathfrak{A}_{0}f=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}
. This allows us to conclude, if it was not clear enough from the very description of
X_{0}
given above, that
X_{0}
is the Walsh process on
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}
(see e.g.
[
7
,
11
,
8
,
31
]
).

The characterization of
X_{0}
given above in points (
\star
)—(
\star\star\star
) is apparently a bit more involved than the classical description of the Walsh process, but it has an obvious advantage over the latter: it exhibits a natural connection with processes
Y_{\varepsilon,r}
. We exploit this advantage in the proof of our third main theorem.

Theorem 5.5
.

Processes
X_{\varepsilon}
converge in distribution to
X_{0}
, provided that they start at the same point in
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}
.

Before embarking on a proof, we note an important change in perspective involved in the theorem: instead of speaking of convergence of semigroups
\left(\mathrm{e}^{t\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}}\right)_{t\geq 0}
we concentrate on the convergence of distributions of processes
X_{\varepsilon}
. Of course, as we know from the Trotter–Kato–Neveu–Mackevičius Theorem
[
30
]
*Thm. 19.25, convergence of semigroups is equivalent to convergence of distributions, but the view on the latter will turn out to be a bit clearer in our case.

We will need the following abstract result, concerning convergence of a family of measures
\mu_{\varepsilon,r}
, defined on a complete separable metric space
E
and indexed by two parameters:
\varepsilon\geq 0
and
r\in(r_{0},\infty]
, for a certain
r_{0}\geq 0
. To connect these measures to the objects under study, fix an
x\in K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}
, a starting point for all stochastic processes involved, let
r_{0}
be the distance of
x
from the graph center, and think of

•

E
as the space of paths; that is, given
t>0
, let
E
be the space of continuous functions
\chi\colon[0,t]\to K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}
, such that
\chi(0)=x
,

•

\mu_{\varepsilon,r},\varepsilon>0,r\in(r_{0},\infty)
as the distribution of
Y_{\varepsilon,r}
,
\mu_{\varepsilon,\infty}
as the distribution of
X_{\varepsilon}
,
\mu_{0,r}
as the distribution of the Walsh process on
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{r}
, and the limit measure
\mu_{0,\infty}
as the distribution of
X_{0}
(all of these processes are seen as random elements having values in
E
).

Lemma 5.6
.

Suppose that

(i)

for every
r>0
, the measures
\mu_{\varepsilon,r}
converge weakly, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, to
\mu_{0,r}
,

(ii)

for each
r>0
the is an open set
U_{r}
with the following two properties:

(a)

as restricted to
U_{r}
the measures
\mu_{\varepsilon,r}
and
\mu_{\varepsilon,\infty}
coincide:
\mu_{\varepsilon,r}|_{U_{r}}=\mu_{\varepsilon,\infty}|_{U_{r}},\varepsilon>0
,
and

(b)

\lim_{r\to\infty}\sup_{\varepsilon\geq 0}\mu_{\varepsilon,\infty}(E\setminus U_{r})=0
.

Then the weak limit
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mu_{\varepsilon,\infty}
exists and equals
\mu_{0,\infty}
.

Proof.

Since the Prokhorov distance
\,\mathrm{d}_{P}
is dominated by the total variance distance
\,\mathrm{d}_{TV}
, the triangle inequality yields

\,\mathrm{d}_{P}(\nu_{\varepsilon,\infty},\nu_{0,\infty})\leq\,\mathrm{d}_{TV}(\nu_{\varepsilon,\infty},\nu_{\varepsilon,r})+\,\mathrm{d}_{P}(\nu_{\varepsilon,r},\nu_{0,r})+\,\mathrm{d}_{TV}(\nu_{0,r},\nu_{0,\infty}).

Next, sub-point (a) of condition (ii) implies that the total variation distance between
\mu_{\varepsilon,r}
and
\mu_{\varepsilon,\infty}

does not exceed
\mu_{\varepsilon,\infty}(E\setminus U_{r})
and thus, by sub-point (b), converges to
0
uniformly in
\varepsilon\geq 0
. Hence, given
\delta>0
, one can choose an
r
so large that the sum of the first and the third terms in the display above is smaller than
\frac{1}{3}\delta
regardless of what
\varepsilon\geq 0
is. Condition (i) says, moreover, that the Prokhorov distance between
\mu_{\varepsilon,r}
and
\mu_{0,r}
converges to
0
. It follows that for
\varepsilon
small enough the second term in the display does not exceed
\frac{1}{3}\delta
. This, however, means that
\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\,\mathrm{d}_{P}(\nu_{\varepsilon,\infty},\nu_{0,\infty})\leq\frac{2}{3}\delta<\delta
, and implies
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\,\mathrm{d}_{P}(\nu_{\varepsilon,\infty},\nu_{0,\infty})=0
,
\delta>0
being arbitrary. Since the convergence in the Prokhorov distance is equivalent to the weak convergence, we are done. ∎

Proof of Theorem
5.5
.

In the notations introduced before Lemma
5.6
, our task is to show that
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mu_{\varepsilon,\infty}=\mu_{0,\infty}
weakly, and of course to this end we want to use the lemma.

Condition (i) is satisfied by Theorem
4.1
. To prove (ii) we introduce
U_{r}\coloneqq\{\chi\in E\colon\max_{s\in[0,t]}|\chi(s)|<r\}
; this is the space of paths that up to time
t
never touch the outer nodes of
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{r}
. It is clear that, as restricted to
U_{r}
, the distributions of
Y_{\varepsilon,r}
and
X_{\varepsilon}
are the same, so that assumption (a) is satisfied. This is just to say that up the first time
\tau_{\varepsilon,r}
when
X_{\varepsilon}
touches one of the outer nodes, this process is indistinguishable from
Y_{\varepsilon,r}
.
Moreover,
\mu_{\varepsilon,\infty}(E\setminus U_{r})=\mathsf{P}_{x}(\tau_{\varepsilon,r}\leq t)
and, as a consequence of Lemma
5.1
,
\lim_{r\to\infty}\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\mathsf{P}_{x}(\tau_{\varepsilon,r}\leq t)=0
. Since, clearly,
\lim_{r\to\infty}\mathsf{P}_{x}(\tau_{0,r}\leq t)=0
, also condition (b) holds, and we are done. ∎

5.5.
Convergence of resolvents

Emboldened by the simplicity of the proof of Theorem
5.5
, in this section, in keeping with the semigroup-theoretic spirit of the paper, we give a direct proof of the convergence of the resolvents of
\left(\mathrm{e}^{t\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}}\right)_{t\geq 0}
to the resolvent of
\left(\mathrm{e}^{t\mathfrak{A}_{0}}\right)_{t\geq 0}
. Since the statement on this convergence is, by the Trotter–Kato–Neveu–Mackevičius Theorem, equivalent to our Theorem
5.5
, rather than providing all the details, we restrict ourselves to an extensive sketch of the full reasoning.

The following lemma provides information on the convergence of the functions defined in points 1.-3. in Section
5.3
.

Lemma 5.7
.

For each
i\in{\mathpzc K}
,

(a)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}j_{\varepsilon,i}(x)=\cosh(\sqrt{2\lambda}x)
uniformly with respect to
x
in compact subintervals of
[0,\infty)
,

(b)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}k_{\varepsilon,i}(x)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-2\alpha_{i}}}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}\sinh(\sqrt{2\lambda}x)
uniformly with respect to
x
in compact subintervals of
[0,\infty)
,

(c)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\ell_{\varepsilon,i}(x)}{w_{\varepsilon,i}}=\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}x}
uniformly with respect to
x\geq 0
,

(d)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}f_{\varepsilon,i}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}\int_{0}^{\infty}(\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}|x-y|}-\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}(x+y)})h(y)\,\mathrm{d}y
,
uniformly with respect to
x\geq 0
.

Proof.

(a)
Given an
\omega>0
and a natural
n
, let
C_{\omega}[0,n]
be the space of continuous functions
f\colon[0,n]\to\mathbb{R}
equipped with the Bielecki norm
\|f\|_{\omega}\coloneqq\max_{x\in[0,n]}\mathrm{e}^{-\omega x}|f(x)|
. The operator
T_{\varepsilon,i}
given, for
f\in C_{\omega}[0,n]
, by

(T_{\varepsilon,i}f)(x)\coloneqq 1+2\lambda\int_{0}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(y)}\int_{0}^{y}\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(z)}f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad x\in[0,n],

maps
C_{\omega}[0,n]
into itself, and we check, as in the proof of Lemma
3.3
that

\|T_{\varepsilon,i}f-T_{\varepsilon,i}g\|_{\omega}\leq\tfrac{2\lambda\mathrm{e}^{2M}}{\omega^{2}}\|f-g\|_{\omega},\qquad f,g\in C_{\omega}[0,n],\omega>0.

It follows that for
\omega>\omega_{0}\coloneqq\sqrt{2\lambda\mathrm{e}^{2M}}
,
T_{\varepsilon,i}
is a contraction in
C_{\omega}[0,n]
;
j_{\varepsilon,i}
, as restricted to
[0,n]
is the unique fixed point of
T_{\varepsilon,i}
.

Also, as in Lemma
3.4
we check that
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}T_{\varepsilon,i}f=Tf
(in the standard supremum norm, and hence in the norm of
C_{\omega}[0,n]
) where

(Tf)(x)=1+2\lambda\int_{0}^{x}\int_{0}^{y}f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad f\in C_{\omega}[0,n].

Therefore,
j_{\varepsilon,i}
s, as the fixed points of
T_{\varepsilon,i}
s, converge to the fixed point of
T
, that is, to
\cosh(2\lambda\cdot)
, uniformly with respect to
x\in[0,n]
. Since
n
is arbitrary, this proves (a).

(b)
Let

(S_{\varepsilon,i}f)(x)\coloneqq(T_{\varepsilon,i}f)(x)+\int_{0}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(y)}\,\mathrm{d}y-1,\qquad f\in C_{\omega}[0,n],x\in[0,n],

where
T_{\varepsilon,i}
was defined in the proof of point (a). It is clear that
S_{\varepsilon,i}
inherits crucial properties of
T_{\varepsilon,i}
, and in particular is a contraction in
C_{\omega}[0,n]
provided that
\omega>\omega_{0}
.
Since
k_{\varepsilon,i}(0)=0
and
k_{\varepsilon,i}^{\prime}(0)=1
, a straightforward calculation establishes that
k_{\varepsilon,i}
, as restricted to
[0,n]
, is a (necessarily unique) fixed point of
S_{\varepsilon,i}
. Finally,
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}S_{\varepsilon,i}f=S_{i}f
where

(S_{i}f)(x)=\mathrm{e}^{-2\alpha_{i}}x+2\lambda\int_{0}^{x}\int_{0}^{y}f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad f\in C_{\omega}[0,n],x\in[0,n].

Therefore,
k_{\varepsilon,i}
s, as the fixed points of
S_{\varepsilon,i}
s, converge to the fixed point of
S_{i}
, that is, to
\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-2\alpha_{i}}}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}\sinh(2\lambda\cdot)
, and we complete the proof as in (a).

(c)
Let
c_{\varepsilon,i}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(y)}\frac{1}{(j_{\varepsilon,i}(y))^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}y
. Since
j_{\varepsilon,i}(y)\geq\cosh\omega_{1}y
, the integral is finite. By the same token, because of (a),
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}c_{\varepsilon,i}=c_{i}\coloneqq\mathrm{e}^{-2\alpha_{i}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(\cosh 2\lambda y)^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}y=\mathrm{e}^{-2\alpha_{i}}\left[\frac{\tanh\sqrt{2\lambda}y}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}\right]_{y=0}^{y=\infty}=\mathrm{e}^{-2\alpha_{i}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}
.

Now,
\frac{\ell_{\varepsilon,i}(0)}{w_{\varepsilon,i}}=1
and
\frac{\ell_{\varepsilon,i}^{\prime}(0)}{w_{\varepsilon,i}}=-\frac{1}{c_{\varepsilon,i}}
. As above we can argue therefore that
\frac{\ell_{\varepsilon,i}}{w_{\varepsilon,i}}
is a unique fixed point of the map
U_{\varepsilon,i}
defined by

(U_{\varepsilon,i}f)(x)\coloneqq(T_{\varepsilon,i}f)(x)-\tfrac{1}{c_{\varepsilon,i}}\int_{0}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(y)}\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad f\in C_{\omega}[0,n],x\in[0,n].

Since
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}U_{\varepsilon,i}f=U_{i}f
, where

(Uf)(x)=1-\sqrt{2\lambda}x+2\lambda\int_{0}^{x}\int_{0}^{y}f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad f\in C_{\omega}[0,n],x\in[0,n],

\frac{\ell_{\varepsilon,i}}{w_{\varepsilon,i}}
s converge, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, to the fixed point of
U
, that is, to
\exp\{-\sqrt{2\lambda}\cdot\}
.

This establishes uniform convergence in compact subintervals of
[0,\infty)
,
n
being arbitrary. However, all
\ell_{\varepsilon,i}
s are non-increasing, and this allows deducing that the limit is in fact uniform on the entire half-line.

(d)
For
\lambda>0
, let
R_{\lambda,\varepsilon,i}^{\sharp}
denote the map
h\mapsto f_{\varepsilon,i}
(recall, that in
f_{\varepsilon,i}
of (
5.6
) dependence on
\lambda
is notationally suppressed), and let, as before,
\mathsf{1}_{[0,\infty)}
be the member of
C[0,\infty]
defined by
\mathsf{1}_{[0,\infty)}(x)=1,x\geq 0
. It is easy to see that
|R_{\lambda,\varepsilon,i}^{\sharp}h(x)|\leq\|h\|R_{\lambda,\varepsilon,i}^{\sharp}\mathsf{1}_{[0,\infty)}(x),x\geq 0
.

Next, the relation
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(x)}\ell_{\varepsilon,i}^{\prime}(x))=2\lambda\ell_{\varepsilon,i}(x)\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(x)}
tells us that the function
x\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(x)}\ell_{\varepsilon,i}^{\prime}(x)
, which we know has negative values, is increasing, and thus has a limit as
x\to\infty
. Also, the limit cannot be smaller than zero, as this would contradict
\lim_{x\to\infty}\ell_{\varepsilon,i}(x)=0
(because
\lim_{x\to\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(x)}=\mathrm{e}^{2\alpha_{i}}
). Hence, using the relation once again, we check that the second term in
R_{\lambda,\varepsilon,i}^{\sharp}\mathsf{1}(x)
equals
-\frac{k_{\varepsilon,i}(x)\ell_{\varepsilon,i}^{\prime}(x)}{\lambda w_{\varepsilon,i}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(x)}
. Similarly, the first term amounts to
\ell_{\varepsilon,i}(x)\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(x)}k^{\prime}_{\varepsilon,i}(x)-1}{\lambda w_{\varepsilon,i}}
, and so recalling the definition of
w_{\varepsilon,i}
yields

R_{\lambda,\varepsilon,i}^{\sharp}\mathsf{1}_{[0,\infty)}=\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\mathsf{1}_{[0,\infty)}-\tfrac{\ell_{\varepsilon,i}}{\lambda w_{\varepsilon,i}}\leq\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\mathsf{1}_{[0,\infty)}.

It follows that the operators
R_{\lambda,\varepsilon,i}^{\sharp},\varepsilon>0,i\in{\mathpzc K}
(
\lambda
is fixed) are equibounded (with bound
\frac{1}{\lambda}
), and so to complete the proof it suffices to show the convergence of
R_{\lambda,\varepsilon,i}^{\sharp}h
to the appropriate limit for
h
in a dense subset of
C[0,\infty]
. Moreover, the formula in the display above in conjunction with (c)
shows that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}R_{\lambda,\varepsilon,i}^{\sharp}\mathsf{1}(x)=\tfrac{1}{\lambda}-\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}x}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}\int_{0}^{\infty}(\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}|x-y|}-\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}(x+y)})\,\mathrm{d}y

uniformly in
x\geq 0
. We have thus proved the required convergence in the case of
h=\mathsf{1}_{[0,\infty)}
.

Hence, we will be done once we take care of the limit
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}R_{\lambda,\varepsilon,i}^{\sharp}h
for
h
with the following property: there is an integer
n=n(g)
such that
h(x)=0
whenever
x\geq n
.

For such
h
and
x\geq n
, the second term in
R_{\lambda,\varepsilon,i}^{\sharp}h(x)
vanishes whereas the first reduces to
\frac{2\ell_{\varepsilon,i}(x)}{w_{\varepsilon,i}}\int_{0}^{n}k_{\varepsilon,i}(y)\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(y)}h(y)\,\mathrm{d}y
. Also, by (b), the integral above converges, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, to
\int_{0}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}\sinh\sqrt{2\lambda}y\,h(y)\,\mathrm{d}y
. Thus, by (c), uniformly in
x\geq n
,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}R_{\lambda,\varepsilon,i}^{\sharp}h(x)=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\lambda}}\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}x}\int_{0}^{n}\sinh\sqrt{2\lambda}y\,h(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

Here, the right-hand side is nothing else than

\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\lambda}}\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}x}\int_{0}^{x}\sinh\sqrt{2\lambda}y\,h(y)\mathrm{d}y=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}\int_{0}^{x}(\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}|x-y|}-\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}(x+y)})h(y)\mathrm{d}y

and the upper limit in the last integral can be replaced by
\infty
, because
h(y)=0
for
y\geq x\geq n
.

Similarly for
x\in[0,n]
: in this case, the first term of
R_{\lambda,\varepsilon,i}^{\sharp}h(x)
is a product of two functions, of which the second,
x\mapsto\int_{0}^{x}k_{\varepsilon,i}(y)\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon,i}(y)}h(y)\,\mathrm{d}y
, by (b), converges, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, to
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}\int_{0}^{x}\sinh\sqrt{2\lambda}y\,h(y)\mathrm{d}y
uniformly in
x\in[0,n]
. Thus, by (c), the first term converges to
\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\lambda}}\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}x}\int_{0}^{x}\sinh\sqrt{2\lambda}y\,h(y)\mathrm{d}y
uniformly in
x\in[0,n]
. Since an analogous analysis establishes the convergence of the second term, we conclude that

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}R_{\lambda,\varepsilon,i}^{\sharp}h(x)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\lambda}}\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}x}\int_{0}^{x}\sinh\sqrt{2\lambda}y\,h(y)\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\phantom{==========}+\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\lambda}}\sinh\sqrt{2\lambda}x\int_{0}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}y}h(y)\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}\int_{0}^{\infty}(\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}|x-y|}-\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}(x+y)})h(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,

uniformly in
x\in[0,n]
. This completes our argument.
∎

We are now ready to give an independent, semigroup-theoretic, proof of the following restatement of Theorem
5.5
.

Theorem 5.8
.

For
g\in C(K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty})
and
\lambda>0
, let
R_{\lambda,\varepsilon}g
denote the right-hand side of
(
5.8
). Then,
R_{\lambda,\varepsilon}
s converge strongly, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, to the operator
R_{\lambda}
defined by

R_{\lambda}g=R_{\lambda}^{0}g+\frac{\sum_{i\in{\mathpzc K}}p_{i}\mathrm{e}^{2\alpha_{i}}C_{i}(g)}{\lambda\sum_{i\in{\mathpzc K}}p_{i}\mathrm{e}^{2\alpha_{i}}C_{i}(\mathsf{1})}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda},\qquad g\in C(K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty}),

(5.10)

where

(a)

(R_{\lambda}^{0}g)_{i}(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}\int_{0}^{\infty}(\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}|x-y|}-\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}(x+y)})g_{i}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y
,
x\geq 0
,

(b)

C_{i}(g)\coloneqq 2\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}y}g_{i}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y
,

(c)

\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\coloneqq\mathsf{1}-R_{\lambda}^{0}\mathsf{1}
, and, to recall,
\mathsf{1}\coloneqq\left(\mathsf{1}_{[0,\infty)}\right)_{i\in{\mathpzc K}}
.

Proof.

Lemma
5.7
(d) states that the operators
R_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{0}
of (

R_{\lambda}^{0}
defined above, and so, in particular,
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}=\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}C_{\varepsilon,i}(g)=\mathrm{e}^{2\alpha_{i}}C_{i}(g),i\in{\mathpzc K},g\in C(K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}^{\infty})
. This, however, is clear from the definition of
C_{\varepsilon,i}(g)
s and Lemma

(c). ∎
