Abstract
. A topological group G G is said to have the Lévy property if it admits a dense subgroup which is decomposed as the union of an increasing sequence of compact subgroups 𝒢 = { G i : i ∈ ℕ } \mathcal{G}=\{G_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\} of G G which exhibits concentration of measure in the sense of Gromov and Milman. We say that G G has the strong Lévy property whenever the sequence 𝒢 \mathcal{G} is comprised of finite subgroups. In this paper we give several new classes of isometry groups and countable topological groups with the strong Lévy property. We prove that if Δ \Delta is a countable distance value set with arbitrarily small values, then Iso ⁡ ( 𝕌 Δ ) \Iso(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}) , the isometry group of the Urysohn Δ \Delta -metric space equipped with the pointwise convergence topology, where 𝕌 Δ \mathbb{U}_{\Delta} is equipped with the metric topology, has the strong Lévy property. We also prove that if ℒ \mathcal{L} is a Lipschitz continuous signature, then Iso ⁡ ( 𝕌 ℒ ) \Iso(\mathbb{U}_{\mathcal{L}}) , the isometry group of the unique separable Urysohn ℒ \mathcal{L} -structure, has the strong Lévy property. In addition, our approach shows that any countable omnigenous locally finite group can be given a topology with the Lévy property. As a consequence to our results, we obtain at least continuum many pairwise nonisomorphic countable topological groups or isometry groups with the strong Lévy property.

1.
Introduction

All topological spaces appearing in this paper are assumed to be Hausdorff. A topological group
G
is
extremely amenable
if every continuous action of
G
on a compact space admits a fixed point. This is a remarkable property for a topological group to have, particularly in view of the theorem of Veech
[
32
]
which implies that no locally compact groups can be extremely amenable. In fact, the question of whether extremely amenable groups even exist was only first posed explicitly by Mitchell
[
19
]
in 1970, almost twenty years after the settlement of Hilbert’s fifth problem. The first examples of extremely amenable groups were constructed by Herer and Christensen
[
14
]
in the form of the so-called exotic groups. A topological group
G
is
exotic
if it admits no nontrivial unitary representations. The groups constructed in
[
14
]
have the form
L_{0}(\mu,\mathbb{R})
, the additive group of all real-valued
\mu
-measurable functions with the topology of convergence in measure, where
\mu
is a pathological submeasure. Since then, many groups of the form
L_{0}(\mu,G)
, where
\mu
is a submeasure and
G
is a topological group, have been found to be extremely amenable. This thread of research includes the following work:

(a)

Glasner
[
11
]
and, independently, Furstenberg and Weiss (in unpublished work), for
\mu
the Lebesgue measure on the unit interval
[0,1]
and
G
the circle group
\mathbb{T}
;

(b)

Pestov
[
22
]
for
\mu
the Lebesgue measure on the unit interval
[0,1]
and
G
any amenable locally compact second countable group;

(c)

Farah and Solecki
[
5
]
for
\mu
any diffused submeasure and
G
any solvable compact second countable group, and for
\mu
any strongly diffused submeasure and
G
any compact group or any amenable locally compact second countable group;

(d)

Sabok
[
26
]
for
\mu
any diffused submeasure and
G
any solvable group.

Historically, though, after Herer and Christensen’s work
[
14
]
, the next examples of extremely amenable groups were discovered by Gromov and Milman
[
12
]
through a concentration-of-measure phenomenon exhibited by certain groups, which is the main focus of this paper. Following Glasner
[
11
]
, we say that a topological group
G
satisfy
the Lévy property
(or is a
Lévy group
) if
it admits an increasing sequence
\mathcal{G}=\{K_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}
of compact subgroups (equipped with the normalized Haar measures
\mu_{i}
, regarded as measures on
G
) with the following properties:

(i)

The union
\bigcup\mathcal{G}
is dense in
G
, and

(ii)

For each open neighborhood
V
of the identity of
G
and each sequence
\{A_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}
of Borel subsets
A_{i}\subseteq K_{i}
satisfying

\liminf\mu_{i}(A_{i})>0,

we have

(1)

\lim\mu_{i}(VA_{i})=1.

We call the sequence
\mathcal{G}
in the above definition a
witnessing sequence
for the Lévy property of
G
. Gromov and Milman
[
12
]
showed that the Lévy property implies extreme amenability, and they gave several important examples of groups with the Lévy property. Among them, they showed that the unitary group
U(H)
, where
H
is a separable infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, has the Lévy property. By definition,
U(H)
is not exotic; thus it is extremely amenable for a different reason other than exoticity. They also gave two examples of countable metric groups with the Lévy property, which we will review in Section
2
below. In fact, in the above list of
L_{0}
-type groups known to be extremely amenable, many turn out to have the Lévy property, which we also review in Section
2
. Farah and Solecki
[
5
]
also gave examples of extremely amenable
L_{0}
-type groups which do not have the Lévy property (see our review in Section
3
below).

Other than
L_{0}
-type groups, more operator groups were shown to be extremely amenable, some even to have the Lévy property, after Gromov and Milman’s work on
U(H)
. Most noteworthy among them is the work of Pestov showing that
\mbox{Iso}(\mathbb{U})
, the group of all isometries of the universal Urysohn metric space
\mathbb{U}
with the pointwise convergence topology, is extremely amenable
[
22
]
, and in fact has the Lévy property
[
24
]
. In this paper, we give more examples of isometry groups with the Lévy property. Pestov
[
21
]
also showed the extremely amenability of
\mbox{Homeo}_{+}([0,1])
, the group of all orientation-preserving autohomeomorphisms of the unit interval with the pointwise convergence topology, by using a Ramsey type argument. Giordano and Pestov
[
9
,
10
]
showed, among other things, that
\mbox{Aut}(X,\mu)
, the group of all measure-preserving automorphisms of a standard nonatomic finite or sigma-finite measure space
(X,\mu)
, equipped with the weak topology, has the Lévy property, and therefore is extremely amenable.

A third class of topological groups known to be extremely amenable in the literature comes from automorphism groups of countable structures. Pestov
[
21
]
gave the first example of such a group, which is
\mbox{Aut}(\mathbb{Q},<)
, the group of all automorphisms of a countable dense linear order without endpoints, equipped with the pointwise convergence topology, where the underlying countable structure is equipped with the discrete topology. This was proved using a Ramsey type argument, which was then broadened in the work of Kechris, Pestov, and Todorčević
[
17
]
to establish a complete characterization of all automorphism groups of countable structures which are extremely amenable. This work has been used to establish the extreme amenability of many automorphism groups of countable structures (see Nguyen Van Thé
[
20
]
for a survey). However, no groups of this type were found to have the Lévy property. In this paper, we isolate an argument of Schneider
[
28
]
to clarify that no non-Archimedean Polish group can have the Lévy property, thus explaining the lack of examples of Lévy groups in this widely studied class.

Among the wide variety of groups which have been shown to have the Lévy property, a substantial number of them have a witnessing sequence consisting of finite groups. This motivates the following definition. We say that a topological group
G
satisfy
the strong Lévy property
if there is an increasing sequence of finite groups, equipped with the normalized counting measures, witnessing the Lévy property of
G
. Note the trivial fact that a group with the strong Lévy property cannot be torsion-free. Also, for a countable topological group
G
, the Lévy property and the strong Lévy property are equivalent. One of the focuses of this paper is to study countable locally finite groups which can be given a group topology with the (strong) Lévy property. Recall that a group
G
is
locally finite
if any finitely generated subgroup of
G
is finite.

The main goal of this paper is to give more examples of groups with the Lévy property. A secondary goal is to analyze the isomorphism types of these examples as topological groups, and to justify the claim that there are many of them which are pairwise nonisomorphic to each other. In doing this, we concentrate on isometry groups and countable groups. As mentioned above, Gromov and Milman
[
12
]
gave the first two examples of countable groups with the Lévy property. Algebraically, their examples are, respectively, the direct sum of countably many copies of
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
and the group of all permutations of
\mathbb{N}
which have finite support. Here, we give several new classes of examples of countable groups with the Lévy property. In particular, we show that any countable omnigenous locally finite group carries a group topology with the Lévy property. In view of the result of Gao and Li
[
7
]
which states that the isomorphism relation of countable omnigenous locally finite groups is Borel complete, this shows that there are many countable groups with the Lévy property that are provably pairwise nonisomorphic to each other.

We also give more examples of isometry groups with the Lévy property. The first class of these have the form
\mbox{Iso}(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})
, where
\Delta
is a countable distance value set with
\inf\Delta=0
and
\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}
is the countable Urysohn
\Delta
-metric space, equipped with the topology of pointwise convergence in metric. These groups were known to be extremely amenable by the work of Etedadialiabadi, Gao and Li
[
4
]
, and it is known that they are separable metrizable but not Polish. The second class consists of Polish groups arising from continuous model theory. They are denoted
\mbox{Iso}(\mathbb{U}_{(\mathbb{Q},V)})
, where
(\mathbb{Q},V)
is a countable good value pair for a Lipschitz continuous signature
\mathcal{L}
with only finitely many relation symbols, but the group is in fact the automorphism group of the continuous Urysohn
\mathbb{Q}
-metric
\mathcal{L}
-structure
\mathbb{U}_{(\mathbb{Q},V)}
. The structures
\mathbb{U}_{(\mathbb{Q},V)}
were shown to exist by Gao and Ren
[
8
]
, and their automorphism groups are closed subgroups of
\mbox{Iso}(\mathbb{U})
, and therefore Polish.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.

In Section
2
we give a review of the previously known examples of topological groups with the Lévy property. In Section
3
we discuss some currently known methods for disproving the Lévy property for a topological group. In particular, we discuss in detail the method of Schneider
[
28
]
which proves that a non-Archimedean Polish group cannot have the Lévy property. In Section
4
we define two new classes of countable metric groups with the Lévy property.

In Section
5
we accomplish two things. First,
we develop a technical tool about adjusting distance values in a finite
\Delta
-metric space without changing its isometry group.
Afterwards, we apply the martingale technique to deal with a technical problem that arises in the construction of witnessing sequences of the strong Lévy property in the context of
\Delta
-metric spaces.
With the technical tools in hand, we prove our main results in Sections
6
through
8
. In Section
6
we use an elementary density argument to prove that a canonical isometric copy of the Hall’s group in
\mbox{Iso}(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})
has the Lévy property. In Section
7
we give a recursive construction for proving that all countable omnigenous locally finite groups can witness the Lévy property of
\Iso(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})
, and at the same time they themselves become Lévy groups with the subspace topology. Eventually, Section
8
contains our results for the setting in continuous logic.

In the final Section
9
we study the isomorphism types of the Lévy groups considered in this paper from the point of view of descriptive set theory of equivalence relations.

2.
A Review of Known Constructions

In this section we review previously known constructions of topological groups with the Lévy property.

2.1.
The Gromov–Milman constructions

The following examples were constructed in the original 1983 paper
[
12
]
by Gromov and Milman.

Example 2.1

(
[
12
, Gromov–Milman]
)
.

Let
E_{2}^{n}
be the direct sum of
n
copies of the group
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
. Let
\theta_{n}\colon E_{2}^{n}\to E_{2}^{n+1}
be the group embedding

\theta_{n}(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})=(g_{1},\dots,g_{n},0).

Let
E_{2}^{\infty}
be the direct limit of the direct system
(E_{2}^{n},\theta_{n})
, i.e., each element of
E_{2}^{n}
can be viewed as an infinite sequence
(g_{n})
, where for all but finitely many
n
,
g_{n}=0
. For
g=(g_{n}),h=(h_{n})\in E_{2}^{\infty}
, define

\rho(g,h)=|\{n\colon g_{n}\neq h_{n}\}|

and let

\varphi(g,h)=\displaystyle\frac{\rho(g,h)}{\max\{\rho(g,\vec{0}),\rho(h,\vec{0})\}},

where
\vec{0}=(0)
, if
g\neq h
, and let
\varphi(g,h)=0
if
g=h
.
Finally, define a metric

\delta(g,h)=\inf\left\{\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\varphi(k_{i},k_{i+1})\colon g=k_{0},k_{1},\dots,k_{m}=h\in E_{2}^{\infty}\right\}.

Then
(E_{2}^{\infty},\delta)
is a group with the Lévy property.

Example 2.2

(
[
12
, Gromov–Milman]
)
.

Let
S_{n}
be the symmetric group of the
n
-element set
\{0,\dots,n-1\}
. Let
\theta_{n}\colon S_{n}\to S_{n+1}
be the group embedding defined by

\theta_{n}(g)(i)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}g(i),&\mbox{if $i<n$,}\\
n,&\mbox{if $i=n$.}\end{array}\right.

Let
S_{\omega}
be the direct limit of the direct system
(S_{n},\theta_{n})
. Define functions
\rho
,
\varphi
and metric
\delta
in the same fashion as in the previous example, except that we redefine
\vec{0}
to be the trivial permutation. Then
(S_{\omega},\delta)
is a group with the Lévy property.

Example 2.3

(
[
12
, Gromov–Milman]
; see also
[
23
, Corollary 4.1.5]
)
.

The unitary group
U(H)
, where
H
is a separable infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, equipped with the strong operator topology, has the Lévy property.

In fact, Gromov and Milman essentially showed that any group that can be approximated by the sequence of unitary groups
U(n)
of finite rank indeed has the Lévy property. The following is an example from Pestov
[
23
]
.

Example 2.4

(
[
23
, Corollary 4.1.17]
)
.

The group of unitary operators of the form
\mathbb{I}+K
, where
\mathbb{I}
is the identity and
K
is a compact operator, equipped with the uniform operator topology, has the Lévy property.

2.2.
L_{0}
-type groups

Consider the notion of submeasure as in Herer and Christensen
[
14
]
. Given a set
X
and an algebra
\mathcal{B}
of subsets of
X
, a real-valued map
\phi:X\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be a
submeasure
if
\phi(\varnothing)=0
and for each pair
U,V\in\mathcal{B}
one has

(a)

\phi(U)\leq\phi(V)
whenever
U\subseteq V
, and

(b)

\phi(U\cup V)\leq\phi(U)+\phi(V)
.

A submeasure
\phi
is called a
measure
whenever item (b) above is an equality for disjoint sets
U,V\in\mathcal{B}
. Following Farah and Solecki
[
5
]
, a submeasure
\phi
is said to be
diffused
if for each
\epsilon>0
there exists a covering of
X
by sets from
\mathcal{B}
of
\phi
-submeasure less than
\epsilon
, and
\phi
is
strongly diffused
if for each
\epsilon>0
there exists some
\delta>0
so that for any positive integer
N
there exists a finite partition
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
of
X
with
|\mathcal{P}|>N
, such that for each
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
satisfying
|\mathcal{A}|<\delta\cdot|\mathcal{P}|
one has that
\phi(\bigcup\mathcal{A})<\epsilon
. Note that each diffused measure is strongly diffused.

Let
G
be a topological group and
\phi
be a submeasure (on a set
X
with an algebra
\mathcal{B}
). We shall say that a function
f
from
X
to
G
is a
G
-step function
whenever
f
takes finitely many values in
G
, and the pre-images of points under
f
are elements in
\mathcal{B}
. Given
G
-step functions
f,g
, we define
f\sim g

if

\phi(\{x\in X\colon f(x)\neq g(x)\})=0.

Then
\sim
is an equivalence relation on the family of all
G
-step functions. We let
S(\phi,G)
be the space of all equivalence classes of
G
-step functions under
\sim
, which we regard as a topological group with the topology of

convergence in measure
,
for which a basis of the identity is given by the sets

O[V,\epsilon]:=\left\{f\in S(\phi,G)\colon\phi(\left\{x\in X\colon f(x)\not\in V\right\})<\epsilon\right\},

where
V
is an arbitrary open neighborhood of the identity of
G
and
\epsilon>0
. Whenever
G

admits a right-invariant metric
\rho
, the group
S(\phi,G)
admits a right-invariant metric in the form

d_{\phi}(f,g)=\inf\left\{\epsilon>0\colon\phi(\left\{x\in X\colon\rho(f(x),g(x))>\epsilon\right\})<\epsilon\right\}.

We let
L_{0}(\phi,G)
be the completion of
S(\phi,G)
with respect to the metric
d_{\phi}
.

(
[
11
, Glasner, Furstenberg–Weiss]

Let
\lambda
be the Lebesgue measure on the unit interval
[0,1]
and let

Example 2.6

(
[

Let
\phi
be a strongly diffused submeasure. If

is a compact group, then

satisfies the Lévy property.

3.
Known Methods for Proving Non-Lévy-ness

Pestov has noted that
\mbox{Aut}(\mathbb{Q},<)
does not have the Lévy property (see
[
23
, Page 7]
) since it does not contain any nontrivial compact subgroup. This result will be superseded by Corollary
3.7
below, but for a different reason.

The first explicit method developed for disproving the Lévy property was given by Farah and Solecki
[
5
]
. The way the method works is by proving that any group
G
satisfying the Lévy property must also satisfy a “minimum” amount of measure concentration when one considers an arbitrary increasing sequence comprised of its compact subgroups.

Lemma 3.1
.

[
5
, Lemma 4.1]

Let
G
be a topological group satisfying the Lévy property. Let
\{K_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}
be an increasing sequence (by inclusion) of compact subgroups of
G
with
\bigcup K_{n}
dense in
G
. Let
V
be an open neighborhood of the identity of
G
and let
F\subseteq G
be an arbitrary compact subset of
G
. Then, for any sequence
\{A_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}
where each
A_{n}
is a Borel subset of
K_{n}
, we have the inequality

\liminf_{n}\mu_{n}(VA_{n})-\mu_{n}(FA_{n})\geq 0,

where
\mu_{n}
is the probability Haar measure on
K_{n}
, considered as a measure on
G
with support
K_{n}
.

They used the above lemma to show that certain
L_{0}
-type groups do not have the Lévy property.

Theorem 3.2

(Farah–Solecki
[
5
, Theorem 4.2]
)
.

Let
G
be any compact topological group. If
G
is not connected, then there exists a sequence of natural numbers
\{M_{i}\colon i\in\mathbb{N}^{+}\}
and a diffused submeasure
\psi_{0}
on the algebra of all clopen subsets of the space
\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}^{+}}M_{i}
such that the group
L_{0}(\psi_{0},G)

fails
to have the Lévy property.

In contrast, Sabok
[
26
, Theorem 1]
has shown that the group
L_{0}(\phi,G)
, where
\phi
is any diffused submeasure and
G
is any abelian topological group, is always extremely amenable. Thus there is an abundance of examples of extremely amenable groups which do not have the Lévy property. For instance, for any profinite abelian group
G
, there exists a diffused submeasure
\psi_{0}
such that
L_{0}(\psi_{0},G)
is such a group.

Let us now discuss a method of Schneider
[
28
]
for proving that a group fails to have the Lévy property. This method involves a concept of
measure dissipation
. This concept was originally introduced for a sequence of mm-spaces (see
[
28
, Lemma 3.4]
) and the more general case of uniform spaces (see
[
28
, Definition 3.12]
), and here we employ a particular case of it for topological groups.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
G
be a topological group. Let
\mathcal{G}=\{K_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}\}
be a sequence of compact subgroups of
G
, with
\mu_{n}
being the normalized Haar measure on each
K_{n}
. For a open neighborhood
V
of the identity of
G
, the sequence
\mathcal{G}
is said to be
V
-dissipating
if there exists a collection of finite families
\{\mathcal{B}_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}\}
of Borel subsets of each corresponding
K_{n}
, with the following properties:

(i)

for each
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and any two distinct
B,C\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
we have
VB\cap C=\varnothing
(we say that
B
is
V
-separated
from
C
),

(ii)

\lim_{n}\mu_{n}(\bigcup\mathcal{B}_{n})=1
; and

(iii)

\lim_{n}\sup\{\mu_{n}(B)\colon B\in\mathcal{B}_{n}\}=0
.

\mathcal{G}
is said to be
dissipating
if it is
V
-dissipating for some open neighborhood
V
of the identity of
G
.

The following is essentially proved in
[
28
, Lemma 3.4]
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
G
be a topological group, let
V
be an open neighborhood of the identity of
G
, and let
\mathcal{G}=\{K_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}\}
be a
V
-dissipating sequence of compact subgroups of
G
, with
\mu_{n}
being the normalized Haar measure on each
K_{n}
. Then, there exist
M\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and a sequence
\{A_{n}\colon n\geq M\}
of Borel subsets of the corresponding
K_{n}
, such that

(a)

\mu_{n}(A_{n})\geq 1/2
for all
n\geq M
, and

(b)

\limsup_{n}\mu_{n}(VA_{n})\leq 3/4
.

Proof.

Fix three positive integers
p_{1},p_{2},q\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
so that
p_{1}+p_{2}<q-2
,
2p_{1}>q
and
4p_{2}>q
. Let
\{\mathcal{B}_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}\}
witness that
\mathcal{G}
is
V

The following is an immediate corollary of Lemma

.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
G
be a topological group. Let
\mathcal{G}=\{K_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}\}
be a sequence of compact subgroups of
G
. Suppose
\bigcup K_{n}
is dense in
G
and
\mathcal{G}
is dissipating. Then
G
does not have the Lévy property.

The following is one of the main results of Schneider
[
28
]
, which states that the above hypotheses hold for metrizable groups which admit open subgroups of infinite index.

Theorem 3.6

(Schneider
[
28
, Corollary 5.3]
)
.

If
G
is a metrizable group with an open subgroup of infinite index, then any increasing sequence
\{K_{n}:i\in\mathbb{N}^{+}\}
of compact subgroups of
G
with
\bigcup K_{n}
dense in
G
is dissipating.

Recall that a topological group
G
is
Polish
if it is separable and completely metrizable,
G
is
non-Archimedean
if it admits a neighborhood basis of the identity which consists of open subgroups of
G
, and
G
is
precompact
(or
totally bounded
) if for each open neighborhood
U
of the identity of
G
, there exists a finite set
F\subseteq G
such that
UF=FU=G
(we say that
U
is of
finite index
in
G
).

A prototypical example of a non-Archimedean Polish group is
S_{\infty}
, the group of all permutations of
\mathbb{N}
with the pointwise convergence topology (where
\mathbb{N}
is equipped with the discrete topology). By a classical result of Becker and Kechris (see
[
2
]
; also see
[
6
, Section 2.4]
), a topological group
G
is a non-Archimedean Polish group if and only if
G
is isomorphic to a closed subgroup of
S_{\infty}
, and if and only if
G
is isomorphic to an automorphism group of a countable structure.

Also note that a topological group
G
is precompact if and only if it is isomorphic to a subgroup of a compact group (see
[
1
, Corollary 3.7.17]
). If
G
is a non-Archimedean group, then
G
is not precompact if and only if it admits an open subgroup of infinite index.

Combining all of the above, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 3.7
.

No non-Archimedean Polish groups can have the Lévy property.

Proof.

Toward a contradiction, assume that
G
is a non-Archimedean Polish group with the Lévy property. Then
G
is extremely amenable. In view of Theorem
3.6
and Proposition
3.5
, it suffices to show that
G
is not precompact. However, if
G
were precompact, then as an isomorph of a closed subgroup of
S_{\infty}
, it is itself a complete group and therefore compact (since its completion would be compact). Consider the action of
G
on itself by left translation. This action does not have a fixed point. Hence
G
cannot be extremely amenable, a contradiction.
∎

Equivalently, no closed subgroups of
S_{\infty}
or automorphism groups of countable structures (with the pointwise convergence topology, where the underlying countable domain is equipped with the discrete topology) can have the Lévy property.

4.
Two Classes of Countable Lévy Groups

The following example is motivated by the Gromov–Milman Example
2.1
.

Example 4.1
.

Consider the directed set
D=(\mathbb{N}^{+},\sqsubseteq)
, where
n\sqsubseteq m
iff

. For each

, let

be the group defined in Example
2.1
. If

, let

be the group embedding

\varphi_{n,m}(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})=(g_{1},\dots,g_{n},g_{1},\dots,g_{n},\cdots,g_{1},\dots,g_{n}).

Thus

is a diagonal embedding of

into

be the direct limit of the direct system

,

is a countable abelian group. For each

and

as in Example

. Then

is a metric on

, and
\varphi_{n,m}
is an isometric embedding from

. Thus we can define the following metric

on

Now

To justify the Lévy property of

, we use the following concept of
concentration function
and result (see Pestov
[
23
, Theorem 4.3.19]
) proved using a martingale technique. Similar technique and results can be traced back to the work of Milman–Schechtman
[
18
]
and Schechtman
[
27
]
. It is well known (see
[
23
, Exercise 1.3.1]
) that in considering the measure concentration, it is enough to focus on sets whose measures are bounded away from zero by an apriori chosen constant between 0 and 1. In the rest of this paper, we follow Pestov
[
23
]
and use

.

Definition 4.2

(
[
23
, Definition 1.3.2]
)
.

Given a metric-measure space

, the

is a real-valued function
\alpha_{X}\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
such that
\alpha_{X}(0)=1/2
, and for each

(5)

\alpha_{X}(\varepsilon)=1-\inf{\left\{\mu(B_{\varepsilon}):B\subseteq X,\mu(B)\geq 1/2\right\}},

where
B_{\varepsilon}=\{y\in X\colon d(x,y)<\varepsilon\mbox{ for some $x\in B$}\}
.

Theorem 4.3

(
[
23
, Theorem 4.3.19]
)
.

Let
(X_{i},d_{i},\mu_{i})
,

, be metric-measure spaces, with

. Equip the product space
X=\prod_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
with the product measure

and the Hamming metric

Then the concentration function of

\alpha_{X}(\varepsilon)\leq 2e^{-\varepsilon^{2}/(8\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{2})}.

By Theorem

, the concentration function of

(where

is the counting measure) satisfies
\alpha_{X}(\varepsilon)\leq 2e^{-n\varepsilon^{2}/8}
. Thus it goes to

has the Lévy property.

yields the same group as given by the following linear direct system. Let

We call a countable group
G

universal for finite groups
if it contains an isomorphic copy of every finite group as a subgroup. The group

in Example

and the groups

in Example

We show below that
S_{\omega}
is not isomorphic to any of the groups

. This is done by considering
an algebraic property known as MIF.

Definition 4.6

(Hull–Osin
[
15
, Definition 5.2]
)
.

Let
G
be a group. Given a free group
F_{n}
of rank

generated by letters
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
, a
non-trivial mixed identity
in the group

is a word
w(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\in(G*F_{n})\setminus G
such that
w(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})=e
for all
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\in G
, where

is the identity of
G
. If there are no non-trivial mixed identities in
G
, then we say that
G
is

(

Hull and Osin
[
15
, Theorem 5.9]
showed that for a highly transitive countable subgroup

of
S_{\infty}
,

is not MIF if and only if

contains a normal subgroup isomorphic to
A_{\omega}
, where
A_{\omega}
is the subgroup of

consisting of all even permutations. In particular, this implies that
S_{\omega}
is not MIF. In contrast, we show below that

is MIF for any infinite supernatural number
\mathfrak{n}
.

Proposition 4.7
.

For any scale
(n_{k})
with
\mathfrak{n}=\lcm(n_{k})
,
G_{\mathfrak{n}}
is MIF.

Proof.

Toward a contradiction, assume

is not MIF, i.e. there exist

x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\in G_{\mathfrak{n}}

and a word

w=w(v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell},x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\in\mathbb{F}(v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell})*\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell}\rangle,

where
\mathbb{F}(v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell})
is the free group generated by letters
v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell}
, such that

w(y_{1},\dots,y_{\ell},x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=e

for all
y_{1},\dots,y_{\ell}\in G_{\mathfrak{n}}
. In particular, for the word

w^{\prime}=w^{\prime}(v,x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=w(v,\dots,v,x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\in\mathbb{F}(v)*\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell}\rangle,

where

is a new letter, we have

for all

. Let

where for each

,

, and for each

,

.

5.
\Delta
-Metric Approximations on Finite Powers

Starting from this section, we develop the main results of this paper. They are all about the notion of
\Delta
-metric space for a countable distance value set
\Delta
. We first recall the definitions.

Definition 5.1

(
[
3
, Definition 2.5]
)
.

A
distance value set
is a nonempty set
\Delta
of positive real numbers satisfying that for all
x,y\in\Delta
,
\min\{x+y,\sup\Delta\}\in\Delta
. Given a distance value set
\Delta
, a
\Delta
-metric space
is a metric space whose nonzero distances belong to
\Delta
.

We also recall the following basic facts about
\Delta
-metric spaces from
[
3
]
. If
\Delta
is a countable distance value set, then the class of all finite
\Delta
-metric spaces is a Fraïssé class. We denote its Fraïssé limit by
\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}
, and call it the
Urysohn
\Delta
-metric space
. Then
\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}
is a countable
\Delta
-metric space with the following properties:

•

\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}
is
universal for finite
\Delta
-metric spaces
, i.e., it contains an isometric copy of every finite
\Delta
-metric space;

•

\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}
is
ultrahomogeneous
, i.e., for any isometric finite subspace
F_{1},F_{2}
of
\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}
, there is an isometry
\varphi
of
\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}
such that
\varphi(F_{1})=F_{2}
.

In fact,
\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}
is, up to isometry, the unique countable
\Delta
-metric space with both these properties.

The following facts are from Etedadialiabadi, Gao and Li
[
4
]
. Let
\mbox{Iso}(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})
denote the group of all isometries of
\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}
equipped with the pointwise convergence topology, i.e. a basic open neighborhood of the identity of
G
is of the form

(6)

V[x_{1},\dots,x_{n};\epsilon]=\left\{g\in\mbox{Iso}(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})\colon d_{\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}}(x_{i},g(x_{i}))<\epsilon\mbox{ for $i=1,\dots,n$}\right\}

where
f\in\mbox{Iso}(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})
,
\epsilon>0
,
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}
, and
d_{\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}}
is the metric on
\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}
. Then
\mbox{Iso}(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})
is a separable metrizable group. It is Polish if and only if
\inf\Delta>0
, and it is extremely amenable if and only if
\inf\Delta=0
.

One of our main results in this paper is that
\mbox{Iso}(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})
has the strong Lévy property when
\inf\Delta=0
. We give two proofs for this fact. However, in both proofs, we have to deal with the following issue if we try to mimic Pestov’s proof of the strong Lévy property for
\mbox{Iso}(\mathbb{U})
. In a general step of the construction of the witnessing sequence, Pestov considers the diagonal extension of a finite metric space
(X,d)
to a finite power
X^{m}
with the
normalized Hamming metric

d^{\prime}(x,y)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}d(x_{i},y_{i}).

In the
\Delta
-metric context this is no longer legitimate (since the values do not necessarily belong to
\Delta
anymore) and we have to find
\Delta
-metric spaces that approximate the extension. The rest of this section is devoted to developing some tools to deal with this issue.

5.1.
\Delta
-remetrization of finite metric spaces

In this section we develop a tool to accomplish the following. Given a finite metric space
(X,d)
and a countable distance value set
\Delta
with
\inf\Delta=0
, we would like to reassign the distance values of
(X,d)
by values from
\Delta
so that the resulting
\Delta
-metric space
(X,d^{\prime})
has the same isometries as
\mbox{Iso}(X,d)
. Moreover, we will maintain the following properties:

•

if
d(x,y)\in\Delta
, then
d^{\prime}(x,y)=d(x,y)
; and

•

for all
x,y\in X
,
|d^{\prime}(x,y)-d(x,y)|<\epsilon
for some predetermined
\epsilon>0
.

In the following, we are able to do this in a stronger way so that

•

if
d(x,y)=d(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
, then
d^{\prime}(x,y)=d^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
.

That is, we are going to make the
\Delta
-remetrization by only working with the distance values of
(X,d)
rather than with the
(X,d)
itself.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
\epsilon>0
and let
\Delta
be a distance value set. For a metric space
(X,d)
, a
(\Delta,\epsilon)
-remetrization
of
(X,d)
is a
\Delta
-metric
d^{\prime}
on
X
such that

(a)

for all
x,y\in X
, if
d(x,y)\in\Delta
, then
d^{\prime}(x,y)=d(x,y)
;

(b)

for all
x,y\in X
,
|d^{\prime}(x,y)-d(x,y)|<\epsilon
;

(c)

for all
x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime}\in X
, if
d(x,y)=d(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
, then
d^{\prime}(x,y)=d^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
;

(d)

\Iso(X,d)=\Iso(X,d^{\prime})
.

Our main theorem of this subsection is the following.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\epsilon>0
and let
\Delta
be a distance value set with
\inf\Delta=0
. Then for any finite metric space
(X,d)
with
\diam(X,d)\leq\sup\Delta
, there is a
(\Delta,\epsilon)
-remetrization
d^{\prime}
on
X
.

The rest of this subsection is devoted to a proof of Theorem
5.3
. We use the following notion of
S
-triangle in the proof.

Definition 5.4

(
[
3
, Definition 7.5]
)
.

Let
S
be a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{+}
. We say that a triple
(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})\in S^{3}
is an
S
-triangle
whenever the inequalities
|s_{1}-s_{2}|\leq s_{3}\leq s_{1}+s_{2}
hold.

Remark 5.5
.

It is easily seen that a triple
(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})\in S^{3}
is an
S
-triangle if and only if
(s_{\sigma(1)},s_{\sigma(2)},s_{\sigma(3)})
is an
S
-triangle for any permutation
\sigma
on
\{1,2,3\}
.

The following is our main technical lemma.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
S
and
S^{\prime}
be two finite subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{+}
with the property:

if
s,t\in S\cap S^{\prime}
and
s+t\in S
hold, then
s+t\in S\cap S^{\prime}
.

Define the set

(7)

\Gamma_{S}=\left\{r>0\colon\mbox{ for some $S$-triangle $(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})$, $r=s_{1}+s_{2}-s_{3}$}\right\},

and let
0<\gamma=\min\Gamma_{S}
. Let
t_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq m
, be an increasing enumeration of the elements of
S\setminus S^{\prime}
. Assume there exist
0<\epsilon<\gamma/2
and a bijection
\Phi:S\to S^{\prime}
with the following properties:

(i)

\Phi
fixes the intersection
S\cap S^{\prime}
pointwise;

(ii)

for any
1\leq i\leq m
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon}{2^{i+1}}<\Phi(t_{i})-t_{i}<\frac{\epsilon}{2^{i}}.

Then,
(\Phi(s_{1}),\Phi(s_{2}),\Phi(s_{3}))
is an
S^{\prime}
-triangle whenever
(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})
is an
S
-triangle.

Proof.

First note that our assumptions imply that for any
s\in S
,
\Phi(s)\geq s>0
.
Now assume that
(\Phi(s_{1}),\Phi(s_{2}),\Phi(s_{3}))
fails to be an
S^{\prime}
-triangle for some triple
(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})\in S^{3}
. This implies that
\Phi(s_{3})>\Phi(s_{1})+\Phi(s_{2})
or
\Phi(s_{3})<|\Phi(s_{1})-\Phi(s_{2})|
.

Claim 2
.

If
\Phi(s_{3})>\Phi(s_{1})+\Phi(s_{2})
holds, then
(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})
is not an
S
-triangle.

Proof of Claim
2
.

We note that the assertion is true whenever the inequality
s_{3}>\Phi(s_{1})+\Phi(s_{2})
holds. Indeed, in this case we have
s_{3}>\Phi(s_{1})+\Phi(s_{2})\geq s_{1}+s_{2}
, and thus
(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})
is not an
S
-triangle. We may therefore assume that

(8)

\Phi(s_{3})>\Phi(s_{1})+\Phi(s_{2})\geq s_{3}.

By (i), this implies that
s_{3}\in S\setminus S^{\prime}
. It also follows from (
8
) that
s_{3}\neq s_{1}
and
s_{3}\neq s_{2}
.
Toward a contradiction, assume
(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})
is an
S
-triangle, and in particular
s_{3}\leq s_{1}+s_{2}
. We consider two cases.

Case 1:
s_{3}=s_{1}+s_{2}
. We first argue that for at least one of
i\in\{1,2\}
,
s_{i}\in S\setminus S^{\prime}
. Otherwise,
s_{1},s_{2}\in S\cap S^{\prime}
and
s_{1}+s_{2}=s_{3}\in S
both hold, and by our assumption of
S
and
S^{\prime}
, we must have
s_{3}\in S\cap S^{\prime}
and so
\Phi(s_{3})=s_{3}
by (i), contradicting (
8
). Without loss of generality, we may assume
d_{1}\in S\setminus S^{\prime}
.

We now have
s_{3},s_{1}\in S\setminus S^{\prime}
with
s_{3}>s_{1}
. Thus there are
1\leq i<j\leq m
such that
s_{1}=t_{i}
and
s_{3}=t_{j}
. By (ii), we have

\Phi(s_{3})-s_{3}<\frac{\epsilon}{2^{j}}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2^{i+1}}<\Phi(s_{1})-s_{1}.

Now we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}s_{1}+s_{2}-s_{3}&\leq&s_{1}+\Phi(s_{2})-s_{3}\\
&=&s_{1}-\Phi(s_{1})+\Phi(s_{1})+\Phi(s_{2})-s_{3}\\
&<&s_{1}-\Phi(s_{1})+\Phi(s_{3})-s_{3}<0,\end{array}

contradicting our case assumption.

Case 2:
s_{3}<s_{1}+s_{2}
. Let
1\leq i\leq m
be such that
s_{3}=t_{i}
. Then we have

\gamma\leq s_{1}+s_{2}-s_{3}\leq\Phi(s_{1})+\Phi(s_{2})-s_{3}<\Phi(s_{3})-s_{3}<\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon}{2^{i}}<\frac{\gamma}{2^{i+1}}<\gamma,

again a contradiction.

These contradictions imply that
(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})
is not an
S
-triangle.
∎

Claim 3
.

If
\Phi(s_{3})<|\Phi(s_{1})-\Phi(s_{2})|
then
(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})
is not an
S
-triangle.

Proof of Claim
3
.

Let
\sigma
be a permutation of
\{1,2,3\}
for which
d_{3}=d_{\sigma(1)}
, and

\Phi(d_{\sigma(1)})=\Phi(d_{3})<|\Phi(d_{1})-\Phi(d_{2})|=\Phi(d_{\sigma(3)})-\Phi(d_{\sigma(2)}).

This implies that

(9)

\Phi(d_{\sigma(3)})>\Phi(d_{\sigma(1)})+\Phi(d_{\sigma(2)}).

Note that (
9
) is precisely the case of Claim
2
, implying that
(d_{\sigma(1)},d_{\sigma(2)},d_{\sigma(3)})
is not an
S
-triangle. By Remark
5.5
, the triple
(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3})
is not an
S
-triangle either, proving our claim. ∎

The lemma now follows from Claims
2
and
3
.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
5.3
.

Proof of Theorem
5.3
.

Let
\epsilon>0
and let
\Delta
be a distance value set with
\inf\Delta=0
. Let
(X,d)
be a finite metric space satisfying
\diam(X,d)\leq\sup\Delta
.

Let
S
be the set of all nonzero distance vales of
(X,d)
. The theorem is trivial if
S\subseteq\Delta
. We assume
S\setminus\Delta\neq\varnothing
. Let
t_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq m
, be an increasing enumeration of the elements of
S\setminus\Delta
. Define
\Gamma_{S}
as in (
7
) and let
\gamma=\min\Gamma_{S}
. Let

\delta=\min\{|s-t|\colon s,t\in S\mbox{ and }s\neq t\}.

Then
\gamma,\delta>0
. If
\sup\Delta
is finite, then by Definition
5.1
,
\sup\Delta\in\Delta
; in this case
t_{m}<\sup\Delta
. In this case, we also set
\beta=\sup\Delta-t_{m}
. Let
\beta=1/2
if
\sup\Delta=+\infty
. Finally let

\epsilon^{\prime}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\min\left\{\gamma,\delta,\beta,\epsilon\right\}.

Since
\inf\Delta=0
,
\Delta
is dense in the interval
(0,\sup\Delta)
. Hence for each
1\leq i\leq m
, we can find
t^{\prime}_{i}\in\Delta
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{\prime}}{2^{i+1}}<t^{\prime}_{i}-t_{i}<\frac{\epsilon^{\prime}}{2^{i}}.

Since
\epsilon^{\prime}<\delta
, we have that
t^{\prime}_{i}\not\in S
for all
1\leq i\leq m
.

Let
S^{\prime}=(S\cap\Delta)\cup\{t_{i}^{\prime}\colon 1\leq i\leq m\}
and define
\Phi\colon S\to S^{\prime}
by letting
\Phi(s)=s
if
s\in S\cap\Delta
and
\Phi(t_{i})=t_{i}^{\prime}
for
1\leq i\leq m
. Define a distance function
d^{\prime}
on
X
by letting, for any
x,y\in X
,

d^{\prime}(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}d(x,y),&\mbox{ if $d(x,y)\in\Delta$,}\\
\Phi(d(x,y)),&\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Since
\Delta
is a distance value set,
S
and
S^{\prime}
satisfy the hypothesis of Lemma
5.6
. It is also clear that
\Phi
satisfies the hypothesis of Lemma
5.6
. It now follows from Lemma
5.6
that
d^{\prime}
is a metric on
X
. Now conditions (a)–(c) of Definition
5.2
are obvious with this definition of
d^{\prime}
.

We only verify (d). For this suppose
\varphi\in\Iso(X,d)
first. Then for any
x,y\in X
,
d(\varphi(x),\varphi(y))=d(x,y)
. Then by (c),
d^{\prime}(\varphi(x),\varphi(y))=d^{\prime}(x,y)
. Thus
\varphi\in\Iso(X,d^{\prime})
. Conversely, suppose
\varphi\in\Iso(X,d^{\prime})
and
x,y\in X
. Then
d^{\prime}(\varphi(x),\varphi(y))=d^{\prime}(x,y)
. If
d^{\prime}(x,y)=d(x,y)
, then
d(x,y)\in\Delta
and

d(\varphi(x),\varphi(y))=d^{\prime}(\varphi(x),\varphi(y))=d^{\prime}(x,y)=d(x,y).

If
d^{\prime}(x,y)\neq d(x,y)
, then
d(x,y)\not\in\Delta
and we must have
d^{\prime}(x,y)=\Phi(d(x,y))
and
d^{\prime}(\varphi(x),\varphi(y))=\Phi(d(\varphi(x),\varphi(y))
. Since
\Phi
is a bijection, we conclude again
d(\varphi(x),\varphi(y))=d(x,y)
. Thus
\varphi\in\Iso(X,d)
.
∎

5.2.
Concentration functions on finite powers

In this subsection we deal with finite powers of finite
\Delta
-metric spaces. In previous research, the finite powers are given the normalized Hamming metric, which is not necessarily a
\Delta
-metric. Here we remetrize the finite powers using Theorem
5.3
, but need to verify that the concentration of measure still takes place with the
\Delta
-metric approximations. The goal of this subsection is to give an estimate of the concentration function of the approximate
\Delta
-metric space which will then be used in the proofs in the rest of the paper. We do this by applying the martingale technique of
[
23
, Section 4.3]
, in particular
[
23
, Theorem 4.3.18]
, which boils down to a computation of the so-called
length
of a metric-measure space.

We use the following notation. Given a set
X
and its finite power
X^{m}
, for a given element
x\in X^{m}
we shall write
x_{i}
, for
1\leq i\leq m
, to refer to its
i
-th coordinate. If
G
is a subset of the group
\Sym(X)
of all permutations of
X
, then for
g\in G^{m}
and
x\in X^{m}
, by
g(x)
we mean the element of
X^{m}
where
g(x)_{i}=g_{i}(x_{i})
for all
1\leq i\leq m
, that is

\begin{array}[]{rcl}g(x)&=&(g(x)_{1},g(x)_{2},\dots,g(x)_{m-1},g(x)_{m})\\
&=&(g_{1}(x_{1}),g_{2}(x_{2}),\dots,g_{m-1}(x_{m-1}),g_{m}(x_{m})).\end{array}

For each
i=1,\dots,m
and
x\in X^{m}
, define

(10)

\Omega_{i}(x)=\left\{y\in X^{m}\colon x_{j}=y_{j}\text{ for all }j\leq i\right\}.

Also let
\Omega_{0}(x)=X
. Then

X=\Omega_{0}(x)\supseteq\Omega_{1}(x)\supseteq\Omega_{2}(x)\supseteq\cdots\supseteq\Omega_{m-1}(x)\supseteq\Omega_{m}(x)=\{x\}.

For any
1\leq i\leq m
, let

\Omega_{i}=\{\Omega_{i}(x)\colon x\in X^{m}\}.

Then each
\Omega_{i}
is a partition of
X^{m}
,
\Omega_{0}=\{X\}
,
\Omega_{m}=\{\{x\}\colon x\in X^{m}\}
consists of all singletons in
X^{m}
, and each
\Omega_{i+1}
is a refinement of
\Omega_{i}
(we denote
\Omega_{i}\prec\Omega_{i+1}
). In summary,

(11)

\{X\}=\Omega_{0}\prec\Omega_{1}\prec\Omega_{2}\prec\dots\prec\Omega_{m-1}\prec\Omega_{m}=\{\{x\}:x\in X^{m}\}.

Given a finite metric space
(X,d)
and its finite power
X^{m}
we denote by
d_{m}
the normalized Hamming metric on
X^{m}
, that is, for
x,y\in X^{m}
, we have

(12)

d_{m}(x,y)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}d(x_{i},y_{i}).

Let
\Delta
be a countable distance value set with
\inf\Delta=0
. Let
(X,d)
be a finite
\Delta
-metric space. For each integer
m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, the product space
X^{m}
equipped with
d_{m}
satisfies that
\diam(X^{m},d_{m})\leq\sup\Delta
. Therefore, for each
\epsilon>0
we may apply Theorem
5.3
to find a
(\Delta,\epsilon)
-remetrization
D=d^{\prime}_{m}
for
X^{m}
.

Definition 5.7
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, and assume
(X^{m},D)
is a power of a finite metric space
X
equipped with a metric
D
. Assume
G
is a set of permutations of
X
, and
G^{m}
is its power. We define the metric
\delta_{D}
on
G^{m}
as follows:

(13)

\delta_{D}(g,h)=\sup_{x\in X^{m}}D(g^{-1}(x),h^{-1}(x))

for any
g,h\in G^{m}
.

The slightly awkward inverses are used to make the metric
\delta_{D}
right invariant when
G
is closed under composition, i.e., for any
f,g,h\in G^{m}
, we have
\delta_{D}(gf,hf)=\delta_{D}(g,h)
. The right-invariance is not needed until Section
6
.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
\Delta
be a countable distance value set with
\inf\Delta=0
, and let
\epsilon>0
and
m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
be arbitrary. Let
(X,d)
be a finite
\Delta
-metric space, let
a=\diam(X,d)
, and let
G
be a set of isometries of
(X,d)
. Let
D=d_{m}^{\prime}
be a
(\Delta,\epsilon/m)
-remetrization of
(X^{m},d_{m})
. Let
\{\Omega_{i}\colon 1\leq i\leq m\}
be the collection of partitions of
G^{m}
as in (
11
). Fix
1\leq i\leq m
. Let
f\in G^{m}
and
g,h\in\Omega_{i-1}(f)
be arbitrary. Define a map
\phi:\Omega_{i}(g)\to\Omega_{i}(h)
as follows:

(14)

\phi(w)_{j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}w_{j},&\mbox{ if $j<i$,}\\
h_{i},&\mbox{ if $j=i$, }\\
w_{j},&\mbox{ if $j>i$,}\end{array}\right.

for each
w\in\Omega_{i}(g)
. If
G^{m}
is equipped with the metric
\delta_{D}
as in (
13
) with respect to
D
, then the map
\phi
is an isometry between the subspaces
\Omega_{i}(g)
and
\Omega_{i}(h)
of
(G^{m},\delta_{D})
. Moreover, for each
w\in\Omega_{i}(g)
, we have

(15)

{\delta_{D}}(w,\phi(w))\leq\frac{a+\epsilon}{m}.

Proof.

Note that the map
\phi
is well defined: indeed, for each
j<i
we have
w_{j}=g_{j}=f_{j}=h_{j}
, and
w_{i}=h_{i}
by construction. The map is also injective: given
v,w\in\Omega_{i}(g)
we have that
v\neq w
if and only if there exists
j>i
satisfying
v_{j}\neq w_{j}
; in this case,
\phi(v)_{j}=v_{j}\neq w_{j}=\phi(w)_{j}
holds, proving injectivity. Surjectivity is clear from the definition of
\phi
.

By definition, for any
v,w\in\Omega_{i}(g)
and
x\in X^{m}
,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}d_{m}(v^{-1}(x),w^{-1}(x))&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j>i}d(v_{i}^{-1}(x_{i}),w_{i}^{-1}(x_{i}))\\
\\
&=&d_{m}(\phi(v)^{-1}(x),\phi(w)^{-1}(x)).\end{array}

By Theorem
5.3
and Definition
5.2
(c), we have

D(v^{-1}(x),w^{-1}(x))=D(\phi(v)^{-1}(x),\phi(w)^{-1}(x)).

It follows that
\phi
is an isometry between
\Omega_{i}(g)
and
\Omega_{i}(h)
as subspaces of
(G^{m},\delta_{D})
.

Let us now verify (
15
).
Note that, for any
w\in\Omega_{i}(g)
and
x\in X^{m}
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}d_{m}\left(w^{-1}(x),\phi(w)^{-1}(x)\right)&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}d\left(w^{-1}(x)_{i},\phi(w)^{-1}(x)_{i}\right)\\
\\
&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}d(g_{i}^{-1}(x_{i}),h_{i}^{-1}(x_{i}))\leq\displaystyle\frac{a}{m}.\end{array}

By Theorem
5.3
and Definition
5.2
(b), we have

\left|D\left(w^{-1}(x),\phi(w)^{-1}(x)\right)-d_{m}\left(w^{-1}(x),\phi(w)^{-1}(x)\right)\right|<\frac{\epsilon}{m}.

Therefore,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\delta_{D}(w,\phi(w))&=&\displaystyle\sup_{x\in X^{m}}D\left(w^{-1}(x),\phi(w)^{-1}(x)\right)\\
&\leq&\displaystyle\sup_{x\in X^{m}}d_{m}\left(w^{-1}(x),\phi(w)^{-1}(x)\right)+\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon}{m}\leq\displaystyle\frac{a+\epsilon}{m}\end{array}

as desired.
∎

Any finite metric space becomes a metric-measure space with the normalized counting measure. We can now apply the martingale technique (
[
23
, Theorem 4.3.18]
) to obtain an estimate of the concentration function (see Definition
4.2
) of
(G^{m},\delta_{D})
.

Theorem 5.9
.

Let
\Delta
be a countable distance value set with
\inf\Delta=0
, and let
\epsilon>0
and
m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
be arbitrary. Let
X
be a finite
\Delta
-metric space, let
a=\diam(X,d)
, and let
G
be a set of isometries of
(X,d)
. Let
D=d_{m}^{\prime}
be a
(\Delta,\epsilon/m)
-remetrization of
(X^{m},d_{m})
. Then the concentration function
\alpha_{G^{m}}
of
(G^{m},\delta_{D})
satisfies that

(16)

\alpha_{G^{m}}(\rho)\leq 2e^{\frac{-m\rho^{2}}{16(a+\epsilon)^{2}}}.

Proof.

According to
[
23
, Definition 4.3.16]
, Lemma
5.8
yields an estimation for the so-called length
\ell
of
G^{m}
as follows:

\ell^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\frac{a+\epsilon}{m}\right)^{2}=\frac{(a+\epsilon)^{2}}{m}.

We then apply
[
23
, Theorem 4.3.18]
to deduce (
16
).
∎

6.
The Lévy Property of
\Iso(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})

In this section we prove that the isometry group
\Iso(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})
has the strong Lévy property for any countable distance value set
\Delta
with
\inf\Delta=0
. Recall again that the
\Iso(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})
is equipped with the pointwise convergence topology, where the underlying space
\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}
is equipped with the metric topology.

A special case of our theorem is when
\Delta
is the set of all positive rationals, in which case
\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}
is customarily denoted
\mathbb{QU}
and known as the
rational Urysohn metric space
. Since
\mathbb{QU}
is a dense subspace of
\mathbb{U}
and
\Iso(\mathbb{QU})
is a dense topological subgroup of
\Iso(\mathbb{U})
, our result implies Pestov’s theorem
[
24
]
that
\Iso(\mathbb{U})
has the strong Lévy property.

Our proof is different from Pestov’s proof in
[
24
]
. Instead of constructing the witnessing sequence in
\Iso(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})
, we consider the Fraïssé class of actions of finite isometry groups on finite
\Delta
-metric spaces (as in
[
3
]
). In this setting a witnessing sequence naturally emerges from the canonical construction of the Fraïssé limit.

Our proof also shows that the finite groups in a witnessing sequence for the strong Lévy property of
\Iso(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})
union up to a countable locally finite group which is isomorphic to Hall’s group
\mathbb{H}
, thus in particular this gives a topology on
\mathbb{H}
to make it a Lévy group. By our results in Sections
2
and
4
,
\mathbb{H}
is thus a new countable group which admits a topology with the Lévy property.

We remark that the notation
\Iso(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})
has sometimes been used in the literature (including
[
3
]
) to denote the automorphism group of
\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}
as a countable metric structure, in which case the topology on it is the pointwise convergence topology, where the underlying space
\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}
is equipped with the discrete topology. To avoid confusion, in this paper we denote this group by
\Aut(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})
. Thus
\Aut(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})
and
\Iso(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})
are the same as sets, but they carry different topologies.
\Aut(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})
is essentially an automorphism group of a countable structure, and cannot have the Lévy property by Corollary
3.7
.

We recall some basic results proved in
[
3
]
.

Definition 6.1

(
[
3
, Definition 3.8]
)
.

Given a countable distance value set
\Delta
, let
\mathcal{K}_{\Delta}
denote the set of all structures
(X,G)
where
X
is a finite
\Delta
-metric space, and
G
is a finite group acting isometrically on
X
.

Given two pairs
(X_{1},G_{1}),(X_{2},G_{2})\in\mathcal{K}_{\Delta}
, an
embedding
from
(X_{1},G_{1})
to
(X_{2},G_{2})
is a pair
(\phi,\pi)
, where
\phi:G_{1}\to G_{2}
is a group embedding and
\pi:X_{1}\to X_{2}
is an isometric embedding, such that
\phi(g)(\pi(x))=\pi(g(x))
holds for each
g\in G_{1}
and
x\in X_{1}
. With this concept in place, one can consider the defining properties of a Fraïssé class, and it was shown in
[
3
, Theorem 3.9]
that
\mathcal{K}_{\Delta}
is a Fraïssé class.
If we denote by
(X_{\Delta},H_{\Delta})
the Fraïssé limit of
\mathcal{K}_{\Delta}
, then
X_{\Delta}
is isometric to
\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}
and
H_{\Delta}
is isomorphic to Hall’s group
\mathbb{H}
(see
[
3
, Lemmas 3.10 and 3.12]
). It was also shown in
[
3
, Lemma 3.13]
that
H_{\Delta}
is a dense topological subgroup of
\Aut(X_{\Delta})
; however, since
\Aut(X_{\Delta})
and
\Iso(X_{\Delta})
are the same sets but the topology of
\Aut(X_{\Delta})
is finer than that of
\Iso(X_{\Delta})
,
H_{\Delta}
continues to be dense in
\Iso(X_{\Delta})
.

Definition 6.2
.

For each
(X,G)\in\mathcal{K}_{\Delta}
, let
d_{X}
be the
\Delta
-metric on
X
, and define

\delta_{G}(g,h)=\sup_{x\in X}d_{X}(g^{-1}(x),h^{-1}(x))

for
g,h\in G
. Then
\delta_{G}
is a right-invariant
\Delta
-metric on
G
.

Definition 6.3
.

We say that a subset
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{\Delta}
is
cofinal
in
\mathcal{K}_{\Delta}
if for any structure
(X,G)\in\mathcal{K}_{\Delta}
there exist a structure
(Y,H)\in\mathcal{D}
and an embedding from
(X,G)
to
(Y,H)
.

Definition 6.4
.

Given a positive integer
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, we shall use
\beta_{n}
to denote the function
\beta_{n}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
defined by

(17)

\beta_{n}(\varepsilon)=2e^{-n\varepsilon^{2}}.

Given two functions
\beta,\gamma:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
we shall write
\gamma\leq\beta
if and only if
\gamma(t)\leq\beta(t)
holds for all
t\in[0,\infty)
.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
\Delta
be a countable distance value set with
\inf\Delta=0
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, the set

(18)

\mathcal{D}_{n}=\left\{(X,G)\in\mathcal{K}_{\Delta}\colon\alpha_{G}\leq\beta_{n}\right\}

is cofinal in
\mathcal{K}_{\Delta}
, where
\alpha_{G}
is the concentration function of
(G,\delta_{G},\mu)
and
\mu
is the normalized counting measure on
G
.

Proof.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and
(X,G)\in\mathcal{K}_{\Delta}
be arbitrary, and set
a=\diam(X,d)
. Let
m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
be a positive integer satisfying

(19)

m\geq 16(a+2^{-n})^{2}\cdot n.

Let
d_{m}
be the normalized Hamming metric on
X^{m}
and let
D=d^{\prime}_{m}
be a
(\Delta,2^{-n}/m)
-remetrization for
(X^{m},d_{m})
. Consider
(X^{m},G^{m})\in\mathcal{K}_{\Delta}
, where
X^{m}
is equipped with the
\Delta
-metric
D
, and the action of
G^{m}
on
X^{m}
is given by

\gamma\cdot y=\gamma(y)=(\gamma_{1}(y_{1}),\dots,\gamma_{m}(y_{m}))

for
\gamma\in G^{m}
and
y\in X^{m}
. Let
\pi\colon(X,d)\to(X^{m},D)
be defined as

\pi(x)=(x,\dots,x)\in X^{m}.

Then
\pi
is an isometric embedding by Definition
5.2
(c). Let
\phi\colon G\to G^{m}
be defined as

\phi(g)=(g,\dots,g)\in G^{m}.

It is easily seen that
\phi(g)(\pi(x))=\pi(g(x))
for all
g\in G
and
x\in X
. Thus
(\phi,\pi)
is an embedding from
(X,G)
to
(X^{m},G^{m})
,
Moreover, for any
\gamma,\eta\in G^{m}
, by Definitions
5.7
and
6.2
, we have

\delta_{D}(\gamma,\eta)=\sup_{y\in X^{m}}D(\gamma^{-1}(y),\eta^{-1}(y))=\delta_{G^{m}}(\gamma,\eta).

By Theorem
5.9
, we have

\alpha_{G^{m}}(\varepsilon)\leq 2e^{\frac{-m\varepsilon^{2}}{16(a+2^{-n})^{2}}}\leq 2e^{-n\varepsilon^{2}}=\beta_{n}(\varepsilon).

This shows that
(X^{m},G^{m})\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
.
∎

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
\Delta
be a countable distance value set with
\inf\Delta=0
. Then
H_{\Delta}
equipped with the subspace topology of
\Iso(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})
has the strong Lévy property.

Proof.

By the canonical construction of the Fraïssé limit of a Fraïssé class, we obtain a direct system
\{(X_{n},G_{n}),(\phi_{n},\pi_{n})\colon n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}\}
so that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
,
(X_{n},G_{n})\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
, the direct limit of
X_{n}
is
X_{\Delta}
, and the direct limit of
G_{n}
is
H_{\Delta}
. Since
X_{\Delta}
is isometric to
\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}
, we may assume
X_{\Delta}=\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}
and the metric on it is
d_{\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}}
. For notational simplicity, we also assume
\phi_{n}
and
\pi_{n}
are all identity maps. Thus each
X_{n}
is a finite subspace of
\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}
, and each
G_{n}
is a finite subgroup of
H_{\Delta}
, the latter being a dense subgroup of
\Iso(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})
.

To verify the (strong) Lévy property of
H_{\Delta}
, take an open neighborhood
V
of the identity of
H_{\Delta}
. Since
H_{\Delta}
has the subspace topology of
\Iso(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})
, whose basic open neighborhoods are given by (
6
), we may assume that
V=V[x_{1},\dots,x_{k};\varepsilon]\cap H_{\Delta}
for some
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}
and
\varepsilon>0
. Let
A_{n}\subseteq G_{n}
be subsets with
\mu_{n}(A_{n})\geq 1/2
, where
\mu_{n}
is the normalized counting measure on
G_{n}
.
Let

V_{n}(\varepsilon)=\left\{g\in G_{n}\colon d_{X_{n}}(g(x),x)<\varepsilon\mbox{ for all $x\in X_{n}$}\right\}.

Let
N
be large enough such that
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in X_{N}
. Then by definition, for all
n\geq N
, we have

V_{n}(\varepsilon)A_{n}\subseteq G_{n}\cap VA_{n}=G_{n}\cap V[x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{k};\varepsilon]A_{n}.

Note that

V_{n}(\varepsilon)A_{n}=\left\{g\in G_{n}\colon\delta_{G_{n}}(g,f)<\varepsilon\mbox{ for some $f\in A_{n}$}\right\}.

In fact, if
g\in V_{n}(\varepsilon)A_{n}
, then for some
f\in A_{n}
,
gf^{-1}\in V_{n}(\varepsilon)
; by the definition of
\delta_{G_{n}}
, we have
\delta_{G_{n}}(fg^{-1},e)<\varepsilon
, and by the right-invariance of
\delta_{G_{n}}
, we have
\delta_{G_{n}}(f,g)<\varepsilon
. The argument for the converse is similar.

Now for all
n\geq N
, since
(X_{n},G_{n})\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle 1-\mu_{n}(V_{n}(\varepsilon)A_{n})\leq\alpha_{G_{n}}(\varepsilon)\leq\beta_{n}(\varepsilon)\leq 2e^{-n\varepsilon^{2}}.

i.e.

\displaystyle\mu_{n}(V_{n}(\varepsilon)A_{n})\geq 1-2e^{-n\varepsilon^{2}}.

Thus

\displaystyle 1\geq\mu_{n}(VA_{n})\geq\mu_{n}(V_{n}(\varepsilon)A_{n})\geq 1-2e^{-n\varepsilon^{2}}\to 1

as
n\to\infty
. Thus
H_{\Delta}
is a strong Lévy group.
∎

Since
H_{\Delta}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{H}
and is dense in
\Iso(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})
, we have the following immediate corollary.

Corollary 6.7
.

Let
\Delta
be a countable distance value set with
\inf\Delta=0
. Then
\Iso(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})
has the strong Lévy property. Moreover, there is a topology
\tau
on
\mathbb{H}
such that
(\mathbb{H},\tau)
has the Lévy property.

7.
The Lévy Property of Omnigenous Groups

In this section, we will give another proof of Corollary
6.7
. This proof is more similar to Pestov’s proof of the strong Lévy property of
\Iso(\mathbb{U})
. Namely, we will construct the witnessing sequence by a recursive construction within
\Iso(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})
. This approach allows us to also generalize the result from
\mathbb{H}
to arbitrary countable omnigenous locally finite groups. By results of
[
3
]
and
[
7
]
, there are many distinct isomorphism types of such groups (see Section
9
for more details), therefore we obtain many more examples of countable groups with the Lévy property.

We first recall some basic concepts and results from
[
3
]
.

Definition 7.1

(
[
3
, Definition 5.2]
)
.

Let
G
be a group. We say that
G
is
omnigenous
if for every finite group
G_{1}\leq G
, finite groups
\Gamma_{1}\leq\Gamma_{2}
and group isomorphism
\Psi_{1}\colon G_{1}\cong\Gamma_{1}
, there is a finite subgroup
G_{2}\leq G
with
G_{1}\leq G_{2}
and an onto homomorphism
\Psi_{2}\colon G_{2}\rightarrow\Gamma_{2}
such that
\Psi_{2}\restriction G_{1}=\Psi_{1}
.

Note that in Definition
7.1
, if we replace the onto homomorphism
\Psi_{2}
by an isomorphism, then we obtain a defining property (property (iii)) of
\mathbb{H}
, that is, up to isomorphism,
\mathbb{H}
is the unique countable locally finite group with this property. In particular,
\mathbb{H}
is omnigenous.

A notion of
\infty
-MIF was also introduced in
[
3
, Definition 6.17]
, and it was proved that for a locally finite group,
\infty
-MIF implies MIF, and
\infty
-MIF is equivalent to omnigenousness (
[
3
, Proposition 6.18]
). In particular, all omnigenous locally finite groups are MIF. It follows, for example, the examples in Examples
2.1
,
2.2
and
4.4
are all not omnigenous. We do not know, however, if the examples in Example
4.5
are omnigenous.

Our recursive construction of the witnessing sequence of the strong Lévy property of
\Iso(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})
follows the general outline of the proof of
[
3
, Theorem 5.3]
, where it was shown that any omnigenous group embeds into
\Aut(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})
as a dense subgroup. In particular, we will use the following concepts and results.

Definition 7.2

(
[
3
, Definition 2.9]
)
.

Let
X
be a metric space, and let
\mathcal{P}_{X}
be the space of all partial isometries of
X
, i.e. isometries between finite subspaces of
X
. Let
P\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{X}
be such that
P=P^{-1}
. An
S-extension
of
X
with respect to
P
is a pair
(Y,\phi)
, where
Y\supseteq X
is a metric space extending
X
, and
\phi\colon P\rightarrow\Iso(Y)
satisfies that
\phi(p)
extends
p
for all
p\in P
. We also require
\phi(p^{-1})=\phi(p)^{-1}
for all
p\in P
. When
P=\mathcal{P}_{X}
we call
(Y,\phi)
an
S-extension
of
X
.

Definition 7.3

(
[
3
, Definition 2.10]
)
.

Let
X
be a metric space. An S-extension
(Y,\phi)
of
X
is
strongly coherent
if for every triple
(p,q,r)
of partial isometries of
X
such that
p\circ q=r
, we have
\phi(p)\circ\phi(q)=\phi(r)
.

Theorem 7.4

(Solecki
[
25
,
29
,
30
]
)
.

Let
\Delta
be any distance value set and let
X
be a finite
\Delta
-metric space. Then
X
has a finite, strongly coherent S-extension
(Y,\phi)
where
Y
is a
\Delta\mbox{-}
metric space.

Lemma 7.5

(
[
3
, Lemma 2.12]
)
.

Let
X
be a metric space and
(Y,\phi)
be a strongly coherent S-extension of
X
. For every
D\subseteq X
, the map
p\mapsto\phi(p)
gives a group embedding from
\Iso(D)
to
\Iso(Y)
.

Lemma 7.6

(
[
3
, Lemma 5.1]
)
.

Let
\Delta
be any countable distance value set. Let
X
be a finite
\Delta\mbox{-}
metric space. Let
\Lambda\leq\Gamma
be finite groups and
\pi:\Lambda\rightarrow\Iso(X)
be an isomorphic embedding. Then there is a finite
\Delta\mbox{-}
metric space
Y
extending
X
and an isomorphic embedding
\pi^{\prime}:\Gamma\rightarrow\Iso(Y)
such that for any
\gamma\in\Lambda
and
x\in X
,
\pi^{\prime}(\gamma)(x)=\pi(\gamma)(x)
.

Note that, in the proof of the original
[
3
, Theorem 5.3]
, metrics on finite groups are irrelevant. Here, however, metrics on these finite groups play an important role as we unravel the concentration-of-measure phenomenon. For a finite metric space
X
and a subgroup
G
of
\Iso(X)
, we always use the sup-inverse metric
\delta_{G}
of Definition
6.3
. Recall that
\delta_{G}
is a right-invariant
\Delta
-metric if
X
is a
\Delta
-metric space. Moreover, if
X
is a finite metric space and
\pi\colon G\to\Iso(X)
is an embedding of
G
into
\Iso(X)
, we regard
G
as a subgroup of
\Iso(X)
and equipped it with the metric
\delta_{G}=\delta_{\pi(G)}
accordingly.

The following is our key technical lemma.

Lemma 7.7
.

Let
\Delta
be a countable distance value set with
\inf\Delta=0
, and
X\subseteq Y
be finite
\Delta
-metric spaces. Let
G_{1}\leq G_{2}
be finite subgroups, and let
\pi_{1}\colon G_{1}\rightarrow\Iso(X)
and
\pi_{2}\colon G_{2}\rightarrow\Iso(Y)
be group embeddings such that for all
g\in G_{1}
,
\pi_{2}(g)
extends
\pi_{1}(g)
. Let
H
be a countable omnigenous locally finite group. Suppose
G_{1}\leq H
. Then there exist a finite subgroup
G_{3}\leq H
, a finite
\Delta
-metric space
Z
and an embedding
\pi_{3}\colon G_{3}\rightarrow\Iso(Z)
such that:

•

G_{1}\leq G_{3}
;

•

Y\subseteq Z
as metric spaces;

•

for any
g\in G_{1}
,
\pi_{3}(g)
extends
\pi_{1}(g)
;

•

for any
g\in G_{2}
there is
h\in G_{3}
such that
\pi_{3}(h)
extends
\pi_{2}(g)
; and

•

the concentration functions satisfy
\alpha_{G_{3}}\leq\alpha_{G_{2}}
.

Proof.

Since
H
is omnigenous, there is a finite subgroup
G_{3}\leq G
such that
G_{1}\leq G_{3}
and there is an onto homomorphism
\Psi:G_{3}\rightarrow G_{2}
that extends the isomorphism between copies of
G_{1}
in
G_{3}
and in
G_{2}
. Let
a=\diam(Y,d_{Y})
and choose
b\in\Delta
small enough such that

b<\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}\min\left\{r>0\colon r=d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})\mbox{ for some $y_{1},y_{2}\in Y$}\right\}.

Now consider the disjoint union
Z=Y\sqcup G_{3}
. Define a metric
d_{Z}
on
Z
by

\displaystyle d_{Z}(z_{1},z_{2})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}d_{Y}(z_{1},z_{2}),&\mbox{if $z_{1},z_{2}\in Y$},\\
b,&\mbox{if $z_{1},z_{2}\in G_{3}$,}\\
a,&\mbox{if $z_{1}\in Y$, $z_{2}\in G_{3}$ or $z_{2}\in Y$, $z_{1}\in G_{3}$.}\end{array}\right.

for
z_{1},z_{2}\in Z
. It is easily seen that
(Z,d_{Z})
is a
\Delta
-metric space. Next, we construct an embedding
\pi_{3}\colon G_{3}\to\Iso(Z)
. For any
g\in G_{3}
, define

\displaystyle\pi_{3}(g)(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\pi_{2}(\Psi(g))(x),&\mbox{ if $x\in Y$},\\
gx,&\mbox{ if $x\in G_{3}$}.\end{array}\right.

Then it is easily seen that
\pi_{3}
satisfies all the conditions except perhaps the last one about the concentration functions. Before considering the concentration functions, observe the following easy facts:

(i)

for any
\gamma,\eta\in G_{2}
, we have that
\delta_{G_{2}}(\gamma,\eta)<3b
if and only if
\delta_{G_{2}}(\gamma,\eta)=0
;

(ii)

for any
g,h\in G_{3}
, if
\Psi(g)=\Psi(h)
, then
\delta_{G_{3}}(g,h)\leq b
;

(iii)

for any
A\subseteq G_{3}
,
\mu_{G_{3}}(A)\leq\mu_{G_{2}}(\Psi(A))
;

(iv)

for any
B\subseteq G_{2}
,
\mu_{G_{2}}(B)=\mu_{G_{3}}(\Psi^{-1}(B))
.

It follows from (i) that
\alpha_{G_{2}}(\varepsilon)=1/2
for
\varepsilon\leq 3b
, and therefore
\alpha_{G_{3}}(\varepsilon)\leq 1/2=\alpha_{G_{2}}(\varepsilon)
for
\varepsilon\leq 3b
. Now fix
\varepsilon>3b
. Let
A\subseteq G_{3}
be such that
\mu_{G_{3}}(A)\geq 1/2
. Let

A_{\varepsilon}=\left\{g\in G_{3}\colon\delta_{G_{3}}(g,h)<\varepsilon\mbox{ for some $h\in A$}\right\}.

Then by (iii),
\mu_{G_{2}}(\Psi(A))\geq\mu_{G_{3}}(A)\geq 1/2
. By (ii), we have that
\Psi^{-1}(\Psi(A))\subseteq A_{\varepsilon}
. It then follows from (iv) that

1-\mu_{G_{3}}(A_{\varepsilon})\leq 1-\mu_{G_{3}}(\Psi^{-1}(\Psi(A)))=1-\mu_{G_{2}}(\Psi(A))\leq\alpha_{G_{2}}(\varepsilon).

Since
A
is arbitrary, we get that
\alpha_{G_{3}}(\varepsilon)\leq\alpha_{G_{2}}(\varepsilon)
.
∎

We are now ready for the main theorem of this section, which is a strengthening of
[
3
, Theorem 5.3]
.

Theorem 7.8
.

Let
\Delta
be a countable distance value set with
\inf\Delta=0
, and let
H
be a countable omnigenous locally finite group. Then there is a group embedding
\pi\colon H\to\Iso(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})
with dense image such that
H
equipped with the weakest topology to make
\pi
continuous has the strong Lévy property.

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}
is countable, we may fix an enumeration
\{q_{n}\}_{n}
of all partial isometries of
\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}
. Also fix an enumeration
\{h_{n}\}_{n}
of
H
. We will inductively construct sequences of following objects:

•

an increasing sequence
\{X_{n}\}_{n}
of finite subsets of
\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}
;

•

an increasing sequence
\{G_{n}\}_{n}
of finite subgroups of
H
;

•

group embeddings
\pi_{n}\colon G_{n}\rightarrow\Iso(X_{n})
, for
n\geq 1
.

They will satisfy the following properties for all
n\geq 1
:

(1)

X_{n}\subseteq X_{n+1}
and
\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}=\bigcup_{n}X_{n}
;

(2)

h_{n}\in G_{n}
and there exists
r_{n}\in G_{n}
with
q_{n}\subseteq\pi_{n}(r_{n})
;

(3)

if
g\in G_{n}
and
x\in X_{n}
, then
\pi_{n}(g)(x)=\pi_{n+1}(g)(x)
.

Granting (1)–(3), we have
H=\bigcup_{n}G_{n}
and we may define a group embedding
\pi\colon H\to\Iso(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})
so that
\pi=\bigcup_{n}\pi_{n}
. The following will be satisfied in addition:

(4)

the sequence
\{G_{n}\}_{n}
of subgroups witnesses the Lévy property of
\pi(H)
equipped with the subspace topology of
\Iso(\mathbb{U}_{\Delta})
.

The conclusions of the theorem will follow from (2) and (4).

Without loss of generality, assume
q_{1}
is the empty function and
h_{1}=e
is the identity of
H
. Let
X_{1}
be an arbitrary singleton in
\mathbb{U}_{\Delta}
, let
G_{1}=\{e\}
, and let
\pi_{1}\colon G_{1}\to\Iso(X_{1})
be the obvious embedding. This takes care of the construction for
n=1
. Next, assume that
X_{n}
,
G_{n}
and
\pi_{n}
have been defined. We define
X_{n+1}
,
G_{n+1}
and
\pi_{n+1}
. This will take several steps.

Step 1: Let

Lévy Groups from Continuous Logic

In this section we use continuous logic to produce more examples of isometry groups with the strong Lévy property. These groups are Polish.

Much of our discussion in this section is based on concepts and results proved by Gao and Ren in
[
8
]
regarding the existence of Urysohn continuous structures and the properties of their isometry groups. We first recall these concepts and results.

Definition 8.1

(
[
8
, Definition 2.1]
)
.

(i)

A
modulus of continuity
is a nondecreasing function

\displaystyle u\colon(0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty]

such that
\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}u(x)=0
.

(ii)

Let
u
be a modulus of continuity. Let
(X_{1},d_{1})
and
(X_{2},d_{2})
be metric spaces. We say a mapping
f\colon X_{1}\rightarrow X_{2}
is
uniformly continuous with respect to
u
if for all
x,y\in X_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle d_{2}(f(x),f(y))\leq u(d_{1}(x,y))

Definition 8.2

(
[
8
, Definition 2.4]
)
.

In continuous logic, a
continuous signature

\mathcal{L}
is a set consisting of a
distance symbol

d
,
relation symbols

(R_{i})_{i\in I}
and
function symbols

(f_{j})_{j\in J}
. Each symbol of
\mathcal{L}
is associated with two pieces of data: its arity, which is a natural number, and its modulus functions, where each coordinate is associated with a modulus function. We allow no
0
-ary relations, but
0
-ary functions are allowed (and they are understood as constants).

Definition 8.3

(
[
8
, Definition 2.5]
)
.

Given a continuous signature
\mathcal{L}
, an
\mathcal{L}
-pre-structure

\mathcal{A}
consists of a metric space
(A,d_{A})
, along with

•

R^{A}\colon A^{n}\rightarrow[0,1]
for each
n
-ary relation symbol
R\in\mathcal{L}
, and

•

f^{A}\colon A^{n}\rightarrow A
for each
n
-ary function symbol
f\in\mathcal{L}
,

so that all of them are uniformly continuous with respect to the corresponding modulus functions.

If
(A,d_{A})
is a complete metric space, then we say that
\mathcal{A}
is an
\mathcal{L}
-structure
.

Substructures, isomorphisms, and isomorphic embeddings between continuous (pre-)structures can be naturally defined.

Definition 8.4

(
[
8
, Definition 4.1]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a continuous signature, and let
\mathcal{M}
,
\mathcal{N}
,

be
\mathcal{L}
-pre-structures.

(i)

We say that
\mathcal{N}
is a

of

is a substructure of
\mathcal{N}
and

is a singleton.

(ii)

We say that

has the

-structure

\mathcal{N}
of

\mathcal{N}
to
\mathcal{U}

. We say
\mathcal{U}
is

if it satisfies the Urysohn property.

Definition 8.5

(
[
8
, Definitions 3.2 and 4.11]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a continuous signature with only finitely many relation symbols.

(i)

We say that
\mathcal{L}
is

if the following hold:

•

For any
n\mbox{-}

there is

(ii)

\mathcal{L}
is

is
\mathcal{L}
is semiproper and each

.

(iii)

We will also use the following theorem.

Theorem 8.12

(

)

Let

Lemma 8.16

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a Lipschitz continuous signature. Let

be a normal good value pair for
\mathcal{L}
. For each

\mathcal{D}_{n}=\{(\mathcal{M},G)\in\mathcal{C}_{(\Delta,V)}\colon\alpha_{G}\leq\beta_{n}\}.

\mathcal{C}_{(\Delta,V)}
.

Proof.

Suppose
(G\curvearrowright\mathcal{M})\in\mathcal{C}_{(\Delta,V)}
and
\mathcal{M}=(M,d_{M},\{R_{i}^{M}\}_{i\leq k})

be fixed. Let

be a positive integer such that

.
We define an
\mathcal{L}
-structure
\mathcal{N}=(N,d_{N},\{R_{i}^{N}\}_{i\leq k})
, where

and

is the normalized Hamming metric on

. For every

is

\displaystyle R_{i}^{N}(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},...,\bar{x}_{r_{i}})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{l=1}^{m}R_{i}^{M}(x^{l}_{1},x^{l}_{2},...,x^{l}_{r_{i}})

for

is the modulus function for the

\begin{array}[]{rl}&|R_{i}^{N}(\dots,\bar{x}_{j-1},\bar{x}_{j},\bar{x}_{j+1},\dots)-R_{i}^{N}(\dots,\bar{x}_{j-1},\bar{y}_{j},\bar{x}_{j+1},\dots)|\\

\mathcal{N}
is an
\mathcal{L}
-structure. Since

The Isomorphism Types of Strong Lévy Groups

In this final section we draw some conclusions about the isomorphism types of isometry groups and countable groups with the strong Lévy property.

We first focus on countable groups. The countable groups we considered in this paper are all infinite and metrizable. Thus we can use the following coding space for all of them. Let
\mathfrak{G}
be the subset of

X=2^{\mathbb{N}^{2}}\times\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}^{2}}

consisting of all pairs
(R,d)
where
(\mathbb{N},R)
is a group and
d
is a metric on
\mathbb{N}
. Equipped with the product topology,
X
is a Polish space, and
\mathfrak{G}
is a Borel subset. Hence
\mathfrak{G}
is a standard Borel space (see, e.g., Kechris
[
16
, Corollary 13.4]
). We view elements of
\mathfrak{G}
to be codes for countable metrizable groups. For each
\gamma\in\mathfrak{G}
, we let
G_{\gamma}
denote the countable metrizable group coded by
\gamma
. For
\gamma,\eta\in\mathfrak{G}
, write
\gamma\cong_{\mathfrak{G}}\eta
if
G_{\gamma}
and
G_{\eta}
are isomorphic as topological groups. Then
\cong_{\mathfrak{G}}
is an equivalence relation on
\mathfrak{G}
.

In descriptive set theory, we use the notion of Borel reducibility to compare the relative complexity of equivalence relations on standard Borel spaces (see, e.g.,
[
6
, Chapter 5]
). We recall this notion below. If
X,Y
are standard Borel spaces and
E,F
are equivalence relations on
X,Y
, respectively, then we say that
E
is
Borel reducible
to
F
, denoted
E\leq_{B}F
, if there is a Borel function
f\colon X\to Y
such that for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
,

x_{1}Ex_{2}\iff f(x_{1})Ff(x_{2}).

In this case we intuitively regard
E
to be no more complex than
F
. We say that
E
is
strictly Borel reducible
to
F
, denoted
E<_{B}F
, if
E\leq_{B}F
but
F\not\leq_{B}E
. In this case we intuitively regard
E
to be strictly simpler than
F
.

The following benchmark equivalence relations are very useful in distinguishing between the complexities of equivalence relations.

Example 9.1
.

(1)

The identity or equality equivalence relation on
\mathbb{R}
, i.e., for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
,
x
and
y
are equivalent if and only if
x=y
. We use
=_{\mathbb{R}}
or
\mbox{id}_{\mathbb{R}}
to denote this equivalence relation.

(2)

The equivalence relation
=^{+}
on
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
, defined by

(x_{n})_{n}=^{+}(y_{n})_{n}\iff\{x_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}=\{y_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}.

The
graph isomorphism
relation. Let
\mathfrak{R}
be the subset of
2^{\mathbb{N}^{2}}
consisting of all binary relations
R
where
(\mathbb{N},R)
is a graph. Then
\mathfrak{R}
is a Borel subset of the Polish space
2^{\mathbb{N}^{2}}
, and hence is a standard Borel space. For each
\alpha\in\mathfrak{R}
, let
\Gamma_{\alpha}
be the countable graph coded by
\alpha

\alpha,\beta\in\mathfrak{R}
, write
\alpha\cong_{\mathfrak{R}}\beta
if
\Gamma_{\alpha}
and
\Gamma_{\beta}
are isomorphic as graphs. Then
\cong_{\mathfrak{R}}
is known as the graph isomorphism relation.

All of these equivalence relations have been well studied, and in particular it is well known that

=_{\mathbb{R}}\ <_{B}\ =^{+}\ <_{B}\ \cong_{\mathfrak{R}}.

Statements about Borel reducibility are strengthenings of statements about cardinalities. For example, if
=_{\mathbb{R}}\ \leq_{B}E
, then it follows that there are continuum many distinct
E
-classes.

It was shown in
[
3
, Theorem 5.5]
that there are continuum many pairwise nonisomorphic countable omnigenous locally finite groups. Restated in the language of Borel reducibility, it is essentially saying that
=_{\mathbb{R}}
is Borel reducible to the isomorphism relation for countable omnigenous locally finite groups. In the recent paper
[
7
]
, Gao and Li showed that the graph isomorphism relation is Borel reducible to the isomorphism relation for countable omnigenous locally finite groups (
[
7
, Theorem 1.1]
). Combined with our results in Section
7
, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 9.2
.

The graph isomorphism relation is Borel reducible to the isomorphism relation for countable metrizable groups with the Lévy property. In other words, there is a Borel map
f\colon\mathfrak{R}\to\mathfrak{G}
such that

(i)

for any
\alpha\in\mathfrak{R}
,
G_{f(\alpha)}
has the Lévy property;

(ii)

for any
\alpha,\beta\in\mathfrak{R}
, we have
\alpha\cong_{\mathfrak{R}}\beta
if and only if
f(\alpha)\cong_{\mathfrak{G}}f(\beta)
.

In some sense, this is saying that there are significantly more isomorphism types of countable Lévy groups than just continuum many.

Next we turn to isometry groups. The isomorphism types of groups of the form

(1)

(2)

\Delta

(f(x),f(y),f(z))

\sim_{\mathsf{CDVS}}

=^{+}\ <_{B}\ \sim_{0}\ <_{B}\ \cong_{\mathfrak{R}}.
