Anti-integrable limits for generalized Frenkel-Kontorova models on almost-periodic media
Jianxing Du, Xifeng Su

Abstract
. We study the equilibrium configurations for generalized Frenkel-Kontorova models subjected to almost-periodic media. By contrast with the spirit of the KAM theory, our approach consists in establishing the other perturbation theory for fully chaotic systems far away from the integrable, which is called “anti-integrable” limits. More precisely, we show that for large enough potentials, there exists a locally unique equilibrium with any prescribed rotation number/vector/plane, which is hyperbolic. The assumptions are general enough to satisfy both short-range and long-range Frenkel-Kontorova models and their multidimensional analogues.



1.
Introduction

The classical Frenkel-Kontorova models describe one dimensional chains of interacting particles subjected to one dimensional periodic media as in
[
24
,
21
]
.
The state of a system is given by
u=\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
with
u_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
, and we call it the configuration of that system.
The interaction of the particles with the substratum is modeled by a periodic function
V:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
The physical states are then selected to be the critical points of the following formal energy functional:

(1)

\mathcal{S}(\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}})=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left[\frac{1}{2}(u_{n}-u_{n+1})^{2}+\lambda V(u_{n})\right]\qquad\text{ for some }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

The critical points of
\mathcal{S}
are obtained by taking its formal
derivatives and setting them to zero, i.e.,

(2)

u_{n+1}+u_{n-1}-2u_{n}-\lambda V^{\prime}(u_{n})=0.

In this paper, we are concerned with models subjected to almost-periodic media, that is, we choose
V
to be an almost-periodic function
V(\theta)=\widehat{V}(\theta\alpha)
,
\widehat{V}:\mathbb{T}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{R}
, with infinite-dimensional rationally independent frequency
\alpha\in[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}}
. One example to keep in mind would be
V(\theta)=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{n}}\cos{\frac{1}{\pi^{n}}\theta}
.

As is standard in the literature, there are two perturbation theories for constructing solutions to the equilibrium equation (
2
): one is the celebrated Kolmogorov-Arnold-Moser(KAM) theory which essentially focuses on perturbations of integrable dynamical systems, and the other is the theory of the anti-integrable limits, which deals with perturbations of fully chaotic dynamical systems.

On one hand, the KAM theory for equilibria in one dimensional almost-periodic media was recently established in
[
17
]
, while the KAM theory for periodic and quasi-periodic potential could be found in works such as
[
22
,
35
,
34
]
and the references therein. We point out that these KAM equilibria for periodic potentials can be interpreted as orbits of some twist map and are indeed elliptic.

On the other hand, the theory of anti-integrable limits for periodic potentials was first obtained in
[
19
]
(see e.g.
[
20
]
for a survey), and the extension of this theory to pattern-equivariant potentials was provided in
[
36
]
. Note that these equilibria for periodic potentials derived from anti-integrable limits are hyperbolic, see
[
29
,
20
]
. Moreover, they could be hyperbolic Cantori, which could be thought of as remnants of KAM tori (see
[
31
]
).

As for non-perturbative approaches, it is worthwhile to mention the celebrated Aubry-Mather theory developed independently by S. Aubry
[
18
]
and J. Mather
[
32
]
, which utilizes variational methods to look for ground states. However, extending the Aubry-Mather theory to quasi-periodic or almost-periodic media presents significant challenges, and we will go in this direction in the present paper.

The aim of this paper is to develop the theory of anti-integrable limits for generalized mechanical systems driven by almost-periodic potentials. Specifically, we make the following contributions:

•

we establish anti-integrable limits in a more general setting, see Theorem
3.1
in Section
3
and

•

we prove the hyperbolicity of the anti-integrable equilibria, see Theorems
5.3
and
5.7
in Section
5
.

As an application of the above results, we present the following theorem for the short-range model (
1
) with an almost-periodic potential. To the best of our knowledge, this result is new.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
V
be an almost-periodic function of class
C^{2}
with at least one non-degenerate critical point. Then, there exists a constant
\lambda_{0}>0
, determined by
V
, such that for any
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
and
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists a sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
that satisfies the equilibrium equation (
2
) and fulfills the condition

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{u_{n}-u_{0}}{n}=\rho.

Moreover, the sequence
\{(u_{n},u_{n}-u_{n-1})\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
constitutes a hyperbolic orbit of the map
(x,p)\mapsto(x+p+\lambda V^{\prime}(x),p+\lambda V^{\prime}(x))
.

The proof strategy is as follows:

•

we first provide a verifiable criterion in Section
2
, called the Aubry criterion (see Definition
2.6
), which a modification of the extensions in
[
19
, Section 5]
(see also
[
20
, Section 4]
);

•

we then establish a unified framework on anti-integrable limits for functions satisfying the Aubry criterion in Section
3
, that is, we show that if the derivative of potential
V
in (
2
) satisfies the Aubry criterion, the anti-integrable limits exist for large enough parameter
\lambda
;

•

consequently, we verify in Section
4
that periodic functions, pattern-equivariant functions and almost-periodic functions satisfy the Aubry criterion;

•

we finally we prove the existence of a uniformly hyperbolic structure on the orbits constructed from the anti-integrable limits for functions satisfying the Aubry criterion in Section
5
.

Preliminary

2.1.
Invariant operators

Let
d>0
and
s>0
be two integers. Consider the space
\mathcal{C}
, which consists of all functions from
\mathbb{Z}^{s}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We refer to the elements in
\mathcal{C}
as configurations, and typically write
u_{i}:=u(i)
for
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}
and
u\in\mathcal{C}
. Let
\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}^{s},\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the subspace of
\mathcal{C}
containing all homomorphisms in
\mathcal{C}
.

Let
\|\cdot\|
represent the standard norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The interior and boundary of a subset
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
are denoted by
\operatorname{Int}(A)
and
\partial A
, respectively. The open ball centered at
x
with radius
r
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is denoted by by
B_{r}(x)
, and the closed ball is denoted by
\overline{B}_{r}(x)
. For simplicity, the symbol
0
is used interchangeably to represent the zero natural number, the zero vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the zero configuration in
\mathcal{C}
.

We define an extended metric
1
1

1

An extended metric is a metric so as to allow the distance function
d
to attain the value
+\infty
.
on
\mathcal{C}
as follows:

\displaystyle d(u,u^{\prime})=\sup_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}}\|u_{i}-u^{\prime}_{i}\|.

Remark 2.1
.

(i)

If we focus only on the topological properties of an extended metric space, the extended metric
d
can be replaced by its bounded counterpart

\displaystyle\bar{d}=\min\{d,1\}.

The topology defined by
\bar{d}
is equivalent to that defined by
d
. However, properties related to boundedness may no longer hold under this analogy.

(ii)

Another way to transform the extended metric
d
into a proper metric is to restrict the distance function to a subspace of
\mathcal{C}
. This approach will be utilized in the proof of Theorem
3.1
to ensure the applicability of Banach’s fixed point theorem.

Let
S^{k}:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
denote the shift operator along
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}
, defined as
S^{k}(u)_{i}=u_{i+k}
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}
. Similarly, let
T^{c}:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
denote the translation operator along the vector
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, defined as
T^{c}(u)_{i}=u_{i}+c
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}
.

Definition 2.2

(Invariant operator)
.

(i)

An operator
\Delta:\,\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
is called
horizontally invariant
if it commutes with the shift operator, i.e.,

\displaystyle\Delta\circ S^{k}(u)=S^{k}\circ\Delta(u),\quad\forall u\in\mathcal{C},k\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}.

(ii)

An operator
\Delta:\,\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
is called
vertically invariant
if it is invariant under translations, i.e.,

\displaystyle\Delta\circ T^{c}(u)=\Delta(u),\quad\forall u\in\mathcal{C},c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(iii)

An operator
\Delta:\,\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
is called invariant if it is both horizontally and vertically invariant.

Lemma 2.3
.

If
\Delta
is an invariant operator, then for any
\rho\in\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}^{s},\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\Delta(\rho)
is a constant sequence. That is,
\Delta(\rho)_{k}=\Delta(\rho)_{0}
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}
.

Proof.

For any
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}
, since
\Delta
is invariant, we have:

\displaystyle\Delta(\rho)=\Delta\circ T^{\rho(k)}(\rho)=\Delta\circ S^{k}(\rho)=S^{k}\circ\Delta(\rho).

Taking the value at
0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta(\rho)_{0}=\left[S^{k}\circ\Delta(\rho)\right]_{0}=\Delta(\rho)_{k}.

∎

Using the proceeding lemma, we can define a function

\displaystyle\left.\Delta\right|_{\operatorname{Hom}}:\,\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}^{s},\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\rho\mapsto\Delta(\rho)_{0}

Definition 2.4

(Lipschitz continuous operator)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{C}
be a subset of
\mathcal{C}
. An operator
\Delta:\,\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
is said to be
Lipschitz continuous
on
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
if there exists
K>0
such that
d(\Delta u,\Delta u^{\prime})\leq Kd(u,u^{\prime})
for all
u,u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
.

An important case is when
\Delta
is Lipschitz continuous on the closed ball
\{u\mid d(u,\rho)\leq R\}
, where
\rho\in\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}^{s},\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
R>0
. The Lipschitz constant on this closed ball is denoted by
K(\rho,R)
.

Example

(Long-range Frenkel-Kontorova models)
.

Let
s=d=1
. Consider the long range cubic interaction given by:

\displaystyle\Delta(u)_{i}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{-|k|}(u_{i}-u_{i+k})^{3},\quad\forall i\in\mathbb{Z}.

It is easy to verify that
\Delta
is invariant. To show that
\Delta
is Lipschitz continuous on
\{u\mid d(u,\rho)\leq R\}
, observe that:

\displaystyle\quad\,\|\Delta(u)_{i}-\Delta(u^{\prime})_{i}\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{-|k|}\|(u_{i}-u_{i+k})^{3}-(u_{i}^{\prime}-u_{i+k}^{\prime})^{3}\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{-|k|}2d(u,u^{\prime})(\|u_{i}-u_{i+k}\|^{2}+\|u_{i}-u_{i+k}\|\cdot\|u_{i}^{\prime}-u_{i+k}^{\prime}\|+\|u_{i}^{\prime}-u_{i+k}^{\prime}\|^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{-|k|}2d(u,u^{\prime})3(k|\rho|+2R)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left[\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{6(k|\rho|+2R)^{2}}{2^{|k|}}\right]d(u,u^{\prime})

for all
u,u^{\prime}\in\{u\mid d(u,\rho)\leq R\}
and
i\in\mathbb{Z}
. Thus,
\Delta
is Lipschitz continuous on
\{u\mid d(u,\rho)\leq R\}
with
K(\rho,R)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{6(k|\rho|+2R)^{2}}{2^{|k|}}
.

2.2.
Aubry criterion

Definition 2.5

(Pointwise operator)
.

An operator
\Psi:\,\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
is called a
pointwise operator
if there exists a function
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\displaystyle\Psi(u)_{i}=\psi(u_{i})

for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}
and
u\in\mathcal{C}
. The function
\psi
is referred to as the
pointwise function
of the operator
\Psi
.

Definition 2.6

(Aubry criterion)
.

A continuous function
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is said to satisfy
Aubry criterion
if there exists a subset
O\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and constants
R>0
,
r>0
and
m>0
such that:

(i)

every ball with radius
R
(whatever be its center) contains at least one point in
O
;

(ii)

\psi(z)=0,\,\forall z\in O
;

(iii)

for any
z\in O
and
x,y\in\overline{B}_{r}(z)
,
\|\psi(x)-\psi(y)\|\geq m\|x-y\|
.

Remark 2.7
.

(i)

The concept of the Aubry criterion was first introduced in
[
19
]
. Several other works, such as
[
31
]
,
[
36
]
, and
[
20
]
, have explored similar or generalized notions. Our criterion encompasses the class of functions described in these papers. Figure 1 illustrates a typical example of a one-dimensional function that satisfies the Aubry criterion. For additional examples, refer to Section
4
.

(ii)

If
\psi
satisfies the Aubry criterion with parameters
O,R,r
and
m
, then
\psi
admits a
1/m
-Lipschitz local inverse near any point of
O
(see Proposition
2.8
).

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
\psi
be a continuous function satisfying the Aubry criterion with parameters
O,R,r,m
. For any
z\in O
, there exists a function
\varphi_{z}:\overline{B}_{rm}(0)\rightarrow\overline{B}_{r}(z)
such that

(i)

\psi\circ\varphi_{z}(x)=x,
for all
x\in\overline{B}_{rm}(0).

(ii)

\|\varphi_{z}(x)-\varphi_{z}(y)\|\leq\frac{1}{m}\|x-y\|
, for all
x,y\in\overline{B}_{rm}(0)
.

Before proving Proposition
2.8
, we first establish the following topological lemma.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
K
be a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If
f:K\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is continuous and injective, then

(i)

f(\operatorname{Int}(K))\subset\operatorname{Int}[f(K)]
;

(ii)

\partial[f(K)]\subset f(\partial K)
.

Proof.

(i)

Let
y\in f(\operatorname{Int}(K))
. Then there exists a unique
x\in\operatorname{Int}(K)
such that
f(x)=y
. Since
x\in\operatorname{Int}(K)
, there exists a ball
B_{r}(x)\subset\operatorname{Int}(K)
. By Brouwer’s invariance of domain,
f(B_{r}(x))\subset f(K)
is open. Since
y=f(x)\in f(B_{r}(x))
, we conclude that
y\in\operatorname{Int}[f(K)]
.

(ii)

Let
y\in\partial[f(K)]
. Then there exists a sequence
\{y_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset f(K)
such that
y_{k}\rightarrow y
. Since
f
is injective, we can define the sequence
\{x_{k}:=f^{-1}(y_{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset K
. As
K
is compact, there exists a subsequence
\{x_{k_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to some
x\in K
. By the continuity of
f
, we have
f(x)=y
. Next, we show that
x\in\partial K
. Since
K
is compact, it is closed, so
K=\operatorname{Int}(K)\cup\partial K
. If
x\in\operatorname{Int}(K)
, then
y\in f(\operatorname{Int}(K))\subset\operatorname{Int}[f(K)]
(from (i)), which contracts the fact that
y\in\partial[f(K)]
. Therefore,
x\not\in\operatorname{Int}(K)
, implying
x\in\partial K
.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.8
.

Let
z\in O
and
x,y\in\overline{B}_{r}(z)
. Since
\psi
satisfies the Aubry criterion, we have

(3)

\displaystyle\|\psi(y)-\psi(x)\|\geq m\|y-x\|.

Thus,
\psi(x)=\psi(y)
if and only if
x=y
. That is,
\psi
is injective on
\overline{B}_{r}(z)
.
Since
\psi
is continuous and injective, by the Jordan-Brouwer separation theorem, the image
S:=\psi(\partial B_{r}(z))
of the boundary sphere divides
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus S
into two connected components
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
, with
S=\partial V_{1}=\partial V_{2}
. Let
V_{1}
denote the bounded component. We claim that
V_{1}=\psi(B_{r}(z))
:

(a)

on one hand,
\psi(B_{r}(z))
is open in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus S
by Brouwer’s invariance of domain;

(b)

on the other hand,
\psi(B_{r}(z))
is closed in
\mathbb{R}^{d}-S
since its boundary
\partial\left[\psi(B_{r}(z))\right]\subset S
by Lemma
2.9
.

Thus, by the boundedness and connectedness of
V_{1}
, we conclude that
V_{1}=\psi(B_{r}(z))
. Next, by the inequality (
3
), we have:

\displaystyle S\cap B_{rm}(0)=\emptyset.

Indeed, suppose there exists
p\in S\cap B_{rm}(0)
. Then:

\displaystyle mr=m\|\psi^{-1}(p)-z\|\leq\|p-\psi(z)\|=\|p\|<mr,

which is a contradiction.
Thus
B_{rm}(0)
is contained entirely in either
V_{1}
or
V_{2}
.
Since
0\in B_{rm}(0)
and
0=\psi(z)\in V_{1}
, we conclude
B_{rm}(0)\subset V_{1}
. Hence,
\overline{B}_{rm}(0)\subset\overline{V_{1}}=V_{1}\cup S=\psi(\overline{B}_{r}(z))
.
Since
\psi
is injective on
\overline{B}_{r}(z)
and
\overline{B}_{rm}(0)\subset\psi(\overline{B}_{r}(z))
, we can define the local inverse of
\psi
:

\displaystyle\varphi_{z}:\overline{B}_{rm}(0)\rightarrow\overline{B}_{r}(z)

For all
X,Y\in\overline{B}_{rm}(0)
, their preimages
\varphi_{z}(X),\varphi_{z}(Y)\in\overline{B}_{r}(z)
. By the inequality (
3
), we have:

\displaystyle\|\varphi_{z}(X)-\varphi_{z}(Y)\|\leq\frac{1}{m}\|X-Y\|.

This completes the proof of Proposition
2.8
.
∎

3.
Main theorem and its proof

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\Delta
be an invariant operator, and let
\Psi
be a pointwise operator with a pointwise function
\psi
. Suppose the following conditions hold:

(i)

\Delta
is Lipschitz continuous on any closed ball
\{u\mid d(u,\rho)\leq R\}
, with a Lipschitz constant
K(\rho,R)
, where
\rho\in\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}^{s},\mathbb{R}^{d})
;

(ii)

\psi
satisfies the Aubry criterion with parameters
O,R,r,m
.

Then, for all
\rho\in\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}^{s},\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\lambda\geq\frac{1}{rm}\left(K(\rho,r+R)\cdot(r+R)+\|\left.\Delta\right|_{\operatorname{Hom}}(\rho)\|\right)
, there exists a configuration
u
such that:

\displaystyle(\Delta+\lambda\Psi)u=0,\quad\sup_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}}\inf_{z\in O}\|u_{i}-z\|\leq r\quad\text{ and }\quad d(u,\rho)\leq r+R.

Moreover, the configuration
u
is unique in the sense that, if
(\Delta+\lambda\Psi)u^{\prime}=0
and for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}
there exists
x\in O
such that
u_{i},u_{i}^{\prime}\in\overline{B}_{r}(x)
, then
u=u^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
\rho\in\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}^{s},\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Define a configuration
a:=a(\rho)\in\mathcal{C}
by

(4)

\displaystyle a_{i}:=\operatorname{argmin}_{x\in O}\left\|x-\rho(i)\right\|

for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}
.
From the definition of the Aubry criterion, we have:

\displaystyle d(a,\rho)\leq R.

Next, consider the space

\displaystyle\Pi:=\left\{u:d(u,a)\leq r\right\}.

We equip the space
\Pi
with the metric
d
. Note that while
d
is an extended metric on
\mathcal{C}
, its restriction on
\Pi
is a proper metric. Referring to Theorem 43.5 in
[
33
]
, the metric space
(\Pi,d)
is complete. The following properties hold:

\forall u\in\Pi,\quad\sup_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}}\inf_{z\in O}\|u_{i}-z\|\leq r\quad\text{ and }\quad d(u,\rho)\leq r+R,

which follow directly from the inequalities
d(u,a)\leq r
and
d(a,\rho)\leq R
. Consequently,
\Delta
is Lipschitz continuous on
\Pi
with Lipschitz constant
K(\rho,r+R)
.
We now claim that the operator

\displaystyle\Phi:\Pi\rightarrow\Pi,\quad u\mapsto\left(\varphi_{a_{i}}\left(\frac{\Delta(u)_{i}}{\lambda}\right)\right)_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}}

where
\varphi_{a_{i}}
is the local inverse of
\psi
, is both well-defined and contractive.

For any
u\in\Pi
and
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\Delta(u)_{i}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|(\Delta(u)_{i}-\Delta(a)_{i})+(\Delta(a)_{i}-\Delta(\rho)_{i})+\Delta(\rho)_{i}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle K(\rho,r+R)d(u,a)+K(\rho,r+R)d(a,\rho)+\|\left.\Delta\right|_{\operatorname{Hom}}(\rho)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle K(\rho,r+R)\cdot(r+R)+\|\left.\Delta\right|_{\operatorname{Hom}}(\rho)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle rm\lambda.

Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta(u)_{i}}{\lambda}\in\overline{B}_{rm}(0).

By Proposition
2.8
, for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}
and
u\in\Pi
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\Phi(u)_{i}-a_{i}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\varphi_{a_{i}}\left(\frac{\Delta(u)_{i}}{\lambda}\right)-\varphi_{a_{i}}(0)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m}\left\|\frac{\Delta(u)_{i}}{\lambda}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m}rm=r.

Hence
\Phi
is well-defined.

Again by Proposition
2.8
, for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}
and
u,u^{\prime}\in\Pi
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\Phi(u)_{i}-\Phi(u^{\prime})_{i}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\varphi_{a_{i}}\left(\frac{\Delta(u)_{i}}{\lambda}\right)-\varphi_{a_{i}}\left(\frac{\Delta(u^{\prime})_{i}}{\lambda}\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m}\left\|\frac{\Delta(u)_{i}-\Delta(u^{\prime})_{i}}{\lambda}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{K(\rho,r+R)}{m\lambda}d(u,u^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r}{r+R}d(u,u^{\prime}).

Therefore,
\Phi
is a contraction. Then by the Banach’s Fixed Point Theorem,
\Phi
admits a unique fixed point
u\in\Pi
. For all
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}
, we then have

\displaystyle\Psi(u)_{i}=\psi(u_{i})=\psi\circ\varphi_{a_{i}}\left(\frac{\Delta(u)_{i}}{\lambda}\right)=\frac{\Delta(u)_{i}}{\lambda}.

That implies,

\displaystyle(\Delta+\lambda\Psi)u=0.

If, for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}
, there exists
x\in O
such that
u_{i},u_{i}^{\prime}\in\overline{B}_{r}(x)
, it follows that
u,u^{\prime}\in\Pi
. Moreover, if both
u
and
u^{\prime}
satisfy the equation
(\Delta+\lambda\Psi)u=0
, then
u
and
u^{\prime}
are the fixed points of
\Phi
. By the uniqueness of fixed points in contraction mappings, we conclude that
u=u^{\prime}
.
∎

4.
Examples of functions satisfying the Aubry criterion

4.1.
Periodic functions

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a function. The set

\displaystyle\operatorname{per}(f):=\{t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid f(x+t)=f(x),\,\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}

is called the
set of periods
of
f
. It always contains the trivial period
0
and forms a subgroup of
(\mathbb{R}^{d},+)
. The structure of the group
\operatorname{per}(f)
determines the type of periodicity of the function
f
.

A point set
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is called a
lattice
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if there exist
d
linearly independent vectors
b_{1},\dots,b_{d}
such that

\displaystyle\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}b_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}b_{d}:=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}m_{i}b_{i}\mid\text{ all }m_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}

and the
\mathbb{R}
-span of
\Gamma
is
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The set
\{b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\}
is called a basis of the lattice
\Gamma
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
f
be a
C^{2}
function with at least one non-degenerate critical point. If
\operatorname{per}(f)
contains a lattice
\Gamma
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then
\nabla f
satisfies the Aubry criterion.

Proof.

Let
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a non-degenerate critical point of
f
. That is,
\nabla f(z)=0
and
\det(\nabla^{2}f(z))\neq 0
. Since
\nabla^{2}f(z)
is symmetric and invertible, its square
(\nabla^{2}f(z))^{2}
is positive definite.
As
f
is
C^{2}
, there exist constants
r>0
and
m>0
such that, for any
x,y\in\overline{B}_{r}(z)
, we have

\xi^{T}\nabla^{2}f(x)\nabla^{2}f(y)\xi\geq m^{2}\|\xi\|^{2},\quad\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

So for any
x,y\in\overline{B}_{r}(z)
, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}f(y+t(x-y))(x-y)\ dt\cdot\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}f(y+s(x-y))(x-y)\ ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}(x-y)^{T}\nabla^{2}f(y+t(x-y))\nabla^{2}f(y+s(x-y))(x-y)\ dtds

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}m^{2}\|x-y\|^{2}\ dtds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m^{2}\|x-y\|^{2},

which simplifies to
\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|\geq m\|x-y\|
.
Suppose
b_{1},\dots,b_{d}
form a basis of
\Gamma
. For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there exists a unique
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
x=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}b_{i}
. Since
\sum_{i}\lfloor\alpha_{i}\rfloor b_{i}\in\Gamma
and

\displaystyle\left\|x-\sum_{i}\lfloor\alpha_{i}\rfloor b_{i}\right\|<\sum_{i}\|b_{i}\|,

for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the ball with radius
\sum_{i}\|b_{i}\|
contains a point in
\Gamma
.

Let
O:=\Gamma+z
and
R:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\|b_{i}\|
. Let
r>0
and
m>0
be as defined above. Since
\Gamma\subset\operatorname{per}(f)
, the properties of
f
at any point in
O
are identical to those at
z
.
It follows that
\nabla f
satisfies the Aubry criterion with parameters
O,R,r,m
.
∎

4.2.
Pattern-equivariant functions

A cluster of the point set
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the intersection
K\cap\Lambda
for some compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Two clusters
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
are said to be equivalent if there exists a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
P_{1}+v=P_{2}
.

Definition 4.2

(finite local complexity)
.

A point set
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is of finite local complexity if for any
R>0
, the point set
\Lambda
possesses only finitely many equivalence classes of clusters with diameters smaller than
R
, where the diameter of a subset
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
\sup_{x,y\in A}|x-y|.

Definition 4.3

(repetitive)
.

A point set
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is repetitive if for any cluster
P\subset\Lambda
, there exists
R>0
such that any ball with radius
R
contains a cluster equivalent to
P
.

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a repetitive point set with finite local complexity (see
[
25
]
[
26
]
[
36
]
, and
[
23
]
for details). A continuous function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is said to be
\Lambda
-equivariant if there exists
R>0
such that whenever

(\Lambda-x)\cap B_{R}(0)=(\Lambda-y)\cap B_{R}(0),

it follows that
f(x)=f(y)
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
f
be a
C^{2}
function with at least one
non
-degenerate critical point. If
f
is
\Lambda
-equivariant, then
\nabla f
satisfies the Aubry criterion.

Proof.

Since
f
is
\Lambda
-equivariant, there exists
R>0
such that

(\Lambda-x)\cap B_{R}(0)=(\Lambda-y)\cap B_{R}(0)\quad\Rightarrow\quad f(x)=f(y).

As
\Lambda
has finite local complexity, the point set
\Lambda
contains only finitely many equivalence classes of clusters with diameters smaller than
4R
. By repetitivity of
\Lambda
, we can find
R^{\prime}>0
such that any ball with radius
R^{\prime}
contains all clusters with diameters smaller than
4R
. Let
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a non-degenerate critical point of
f
. Then any ball with radius
R^{\prime}
contains the cluster
\Lambda\cap B_{2R}(z)
. In other words, for any ball of radius
R^{\prime}
, there exists
x
in the ball such that

(\Lambda-x)\cap B_{2R}(0)=(\Lambda-z)\cap B_{2R}(0).

Since
f
is
\Lambda
-equivariant, in each ball with radius
R^{\prime}
, there exists at least one point
s
, such that

f(s+v)=f(z+v),\forall v\in B_{R}(0).

Let
O
be the collection of all such points
s
. Now, we can replicate the proof of Theorem
4.1
. Specifically, there exist constants
r>0
and
m>0
such that for any
s\in O
and
x,y\in\overline{B}_{r}(s)
, we have
\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|\geq m\|x-y\|
. Hence
\nabla f
satisfies the Aubry criterion with parameters
O,R^{\prime},r,m
.
∎

4.3.
Almost-periodic functions

Definition 4.5
.

A point set
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is called relatively dense if there exists
R>0
such that each ball with
radius
R
(whatever be its center) contains at least a point in
\Lambda
.

Definition 4.6
.

A
C^{k}
, function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called almost-periodic of class
C^{k}
,
k=0,1,\dots,\omega
, if for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a relatively dense subset
\Lambda_{\varepsilon}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\|f(\cdot+x)-f(\cdot)\|_{C^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}<\varepsilon,\quad\forall x\in\Lambda_{\varepsilon}.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
f
be a
C^{2}
function with at least one non-degenerate critical point. If
f
is almost-periodic of class
C^{2}
, then
\nabla f
satisfies the Aubry criterion.

Proof.

Let
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a non-degenerate critical point of
f
, i.e.,
\nabla f(z)=0
and
\det(\nabla^{2}f(z))\neq 0
. A classic result
[
28
, Proposition 2.2]
states that there exists
r>0
and
\varepsilon>0
such that

\|g-f\|_{C^{2}(\overline{B}_{r}(z))}<\varepsilon\quad\Rightarrow\quad g=\varphi_{g}\circ f\circ\psi_{g}\text{ in }\overline{B}_{r}(z),

where
\varphi_{g}
and
\psi_{g}
are
C^{2}
-diffeomorphisms. Let
\sigma_{\min}(A)
be the smallest singular value of a matrix
A
. Assume that
\varepsilon<\sigma_{\min}(\nabla^{2}f(z))/4
and
r
is small enough so that

\sigma_{\min}(\nabla^{2}f(x))>\sigma_{\min}(\nabla^{2}f(z))/2,\quad\forall x\in\overline{B}_{2r}(z).

Then, the following hold:

\displaystyle\|g-f\|_{C^{2}(\overline{B}_{2r}(z))}<\varepsilon

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\|g-f\|_{C^{2}(\overline{B}_{r}(z))}<\varepsilon

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle g=\varphi_{g}\circ f\circ\psi_{g}\text{ in }\overline{B}_{r}(z)

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\exists z_{g}\in\overline{B}_{r}(z)\text{ s.t. }\nabla g(z_{g})=0.

In fact,
z_{g}=(\nabla\psi_{g})^{-1}(z)
. Furthermore,

\|g-f\|_{C^{2}(\overline{B}_{2r}(z))}<\varepsilon\quad\Rightarrow\quad\sigma_{\min}(\nabla^{2}g(x))\geq\sigma_{\min}(\nabla^{2}f(z))/4>\varepsilon,\quad\forall x\in\overline{B}_{2r}(z).

By replicating the proof of Theorem
4.1
, there exist constants
m>0
and
r^{\prime}>0
(independent of
g
) such that,

\displaystyle\|g-f\|_{C^{2}(\overline{B}_{2r}(z))}<\varepsilon

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\exists z_{g}\in\overline{B}_{r}(z),\text{ s.t. }\nabla g(z_{g})=0,\forall x,y\in\overline{B}_{r^{\prime}}(z_{g}),\|\nabla g(x)-\nabla g(y)\|\geq m\|x-y\|.

Since
f
is almost-periodic of class
C^{2}
, there exists a relatively dense subset
\Lambda_{\varepsilon}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\|f(\cdot+x)-f(\cdot)\|_{C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}<\varepsilon,\quad\forall x\in\Lambda_{\varepsilon}.

Define
O:=\{x+z_{g}\mid x\in\Lambda_{\varepsilon},g(\cdot)=f(\cdot+x)\}
. Let
R
be the sum of the relatively dense distance of
\Lambda_{\varepsilon}
and
2r
. Then, any ball of radius
R
contains at least one point from
O
. For any
x\in\Lambda_{\varepsilon},
with
g(\cdot)=f(\cdot+x)
, we have

\nabla f(x+z_{g})=\nabla g(z_{g})=0,

and

\forall x_{0},y_{0}\in\overline{B}_{r^{\prime}}(x+z_{g}),\|\nabla f(x_{0})-\nabla f(y_{0})\|=\|\nabla g(x_{0}-x)-\nabla g(y_{0}-x)\|\geq m\|x_{0}-y_{0}\|.

Hence
\nabla f
satisfies the Aubry criterion with parameters
O,R,r^{\prime},m
.
∎

5.
Hyperbolicity

Let
d>0
be an integer. Consider a family of generating functions defined by

\displaystyle S_{\lambda}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle(x,y)

\displaystyle\mapsto I(x-y)+\lambda V(x)

where
\lambda>0
,
I,V\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R})
. Let
\mathcal{C}
denote the set of all functions from
\mathbb{Z}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A configuration
u\in\mathcal{C}
is said to admit a rotation vector
\rho\in\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{R}^{d})
if

\lim_{i\to\pm\infty}\frac{u_{i}-\rho_{i}}{i}=0.

A sufficient condition for
u
to admit
\rho
as a rotation vector is
d(u,\rho)<+\infty.

We define
u\in\mathcal{C}
to be an equilibrium configuration of
S_{\lambda}
if it satisfies

(5)

\nabla I(u_{i}-u_{i+1})-\nabla I(u_{i-1}-u_{i})+\lambda\nabla V(u_{i})=0,\quad\forall i\in\mathbb{Z}.

Linearizing of (
5
) yields the equation:

(6)

\nabla^{2}I(u_{i}-u_{i+1})(\xi_{i}-\xi_{i+1})-\nabla^{2}I(u_{i-1}-u_{i})(\xi_{i-1}-\xi_{i})+\lambda\nabla^{2}V(u_{i})\xi_{i}=0,

where
\xi_{i}\in T_{u_{i}}\mathbb{R}^{d}
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Definition 5.1
.

We say that an equilibrium configuration
u\in\mathcal{C}
is hyperbolic if there exists constants
\mu>1
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and
\beta\in(0,1)
such that, for any
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, and for any
\xi_{i-1}\in T_{u_{i-1}}\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\xi_{i}\in T_{u_{i}}\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\xi_{i+1}\in T_{u_{i+1}}\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying (
6
), the following conditions hold:

(i)

if
\|\xi_{i-1}\|\leq\alpha\|\xi_{i}\|
, then
\|\xi_{i}\|\leq\alpha\|\xi_{i+1}\|
and
\|(\xi_{i},\xi_{i+1})\|\geq\mu\|(\xi_{i-1},\xi_{i})\|
;

(ii)

if
\|\xi_{i+1}\|\leq\beta\|\xi_{i}\|
, then
\|\xi_{i}\|\leq\beta\|\xi_{i-1}\|
and
\|(\xi_{i-1},\xi_{i})\|\geq\mu\|(\xi_{i},\xi_{i+1})\|
.

Remark 5.2
.

This definition differs from the usual definition of hyperbolicity, as it only assumes
I\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R})
. However, under the assumption that
I
is uniformly strongly convex, we will demonstrate (see Theorem
5.7
) that this definition implies the standard hyperbolicity of the orbits of twist maps associated with
S_{\lambda}
.

For now, we will prove that the equilibrium configurations of
S_{\lambda}
, as found via Theorem
3.1
, are all hyperbolic.
Define operators
\Delta:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
and
\Psi:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
as follows:

\Delta(u)_{i}=\nabla I(u_{i}-u_{i+1})-\nabla I(u_{i-1}-u_{i}),\quad\Psi(u)_{i}=\nabla V(u_{i}).

Then
u\in\mathcal{C}
is an equilibrium configuration of
S_{\lambda}
if and only if

(\Delta+\lambda\Psi)u=0.

We will demonstrate later that
\Delta
satisfies the following properties: it is invariant with
\Delta|_{\operatorname{Hom}}=0
, and it is Lipschitz continuous with a constant
K(\rho,R)
on the closed ball
\{u\mid d(u,\rho)\leq R\}
, for all
\rho\in\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
R>0
(see Lemma
5.4
). If
\nabla V
satisfies the Aubry criterion with parameters
O,R,r,m
, then Theorem
3.1
establishes that, for every
\rho\in\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\lambda\geq\frac{r+R}{rm}K(\rho,r+R)
, the generating function
S_{\lambda}
admits an equilibrium configuration
u
satisfying
\sup_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\inf_{z\in O}\|u_{i}-z\|\leq r
and
d(u,\rho)\leq r+R
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
I,V\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R})
and assume that
\nabla V
satisfies the Aubry criterion with parameters
O,R,r,m
.
If
\rho\in\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\lambda\geq\frac{r+R}{rm}K(\rho,r+R)
, and
u
is an equilibrium configuration satisfying
\sup_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\inf_{z\in O}\|u_{i}-z\|\leq r
and
d(u,\rho)\leq r+R
, then
u
is hyperbolic.

Before proving Theorem
5.3
, we will first establish the following three lemmas. Throughout the discussion, let
\sigma_{\max}(A)
(respectively,
\sigma_{\min}(A)
) denote the largest (respectively, smallest) singular value of a matrix
A
.

Lemma 5.4
.

The operator
\Delta
satisfies the following properties:

(i)

\Delta
is invariant with
\Delta|_{\operatorname{Hom}}=0
;

(ii)

\Delta
is
Lipschitz continuous on the closed ball
\{u\mid d(u,\rho)\leq R\}
for all
\rho\in\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
R>0
.

The Lipschitz constant on
\{u\mid d(u,\rho)\leq R\}
is given by

K(\rho,R):=4\sup_{x\in B_{\|\rho\|+2R}(0)}\sigma_{\max}(\nabla^{2}I(x)).

Proof.

First, recall that the shift operator
S^{k}
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
is defined by
S^{k}(u)_{i}=u_{i+k}
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then we have:

\displaystyle[\Delta\circ S^{k}(u)]_{i}

\displaystyle=\nabla I(S^{k}(u)_{i}-S^{k}(u)_{i+1})-\nabla I(S^{k}(u)_{i-1}-S^{k}(u)_{i})

\displaystyle=\nabla I(u_{i+k}-u_{i+k+1})-\nabla I(u_{i+k-1}-u_{i+k})

\displaystyle=\Delta(u)_{i+k}

\displaystyle=[S^{k}\circ\Delta(u)]_{i}.

Next, recall that the translation operator
T^{c}
along a vector
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined by
T^{c}(u)_{i}=u_{i}+c
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then:

\displaystyle[\Delta\circ T^{c}(u)]_{i}

\displaystyle=\nabla I(T^{c}(u)_{i}-T^{c}(u)_{i+1})-\nabla I(T^{c}(u)_{i-1}-T^{c}(u)_{i})

\displaystyle=\nabla I(u_{i}-u_{i+1})-\nabla I(u_{i-1}-u_{i})

\displaystyle=\Delta(u)_{i}.

To verify
\Delta|_{\operatorname{Hom}}=0
, let
\rho\in\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then:

\displaystyle\Delta(\rho)_{i}

\displaystyle=\nabla I(\rho_{i}-\rho_{i+1})-\nabla I(\rho_{i-1}-\rho_{i})

\displaystyle=\nabla I(\rho_{-1})-\nabla I(\rho_{-1})

\displaystyle=0.

For the Lipschitz continuity, consider
u,u^{\prime}\in\{u\mid d(u,\rho)\leq R\}
and calculate:

\displaystyle\quad\,\|\Delta(u)_{i}-\Delta(u^{\prime})_{i}\|

\displaystyle=\|\nabla I(u_{i}-u_{i+1})-\nabla I(u_{i-1}-u_{i})-\nabla I(u^{\prime}_{i}-u^{\prime}_{i+1})+\nabla I(u^{\prime}_{i-1}-u^{\prime}_{i})\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla I(u_{i}-u_{i+1})-\nabla I(u^{\prime}_{i}-u^{\prime}_{i+1})\|+\|\nabla I(u_{i-1}-u_{i})-\nabla I(u^{\prime}_{i-1}-u^{\prime}_{i})\|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\in B_{\|\rho\|+2R}(0)}\sigma_{\max}(\nabla^{2}I(x))(\|u_{i}-u_{i+1}-u_{i}^{\prime}+u_{i+1}^{\prime}\|+\|u_{i-1}-u_{i}-u_{i-1}^{\prime}+u_{i}^{\prime}\|)

\displaystyle\leq 4\sup_{x\in B_{\|\rho\|+2R}(0)}\sigma_{\max}(\nabla^{2}I(x))d(u,u^{\prime}).

Thus
\Delta
is Lipschitz continuous on
\{u\mid d(u,\rho)\leq R\}
, with Lipschitz constant

K(\rho,R)=4\sup_{x\in B_{\|\rho\|+2R}(0)}\sigma_{\max}(\nabla^{2}I(x)).\qed

Lemma 5.5
.

If
\rho\in\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
d(u,\rho)\leq r+R
, then

\sigma_{\max}(\nabla^{2}I(u_{i}-u_{i+1}))\leq\frac{K(\rho,r+R)}{4},\quad\forall i\in\mathbb{Z}.

Proof.

Since
d(u,\rho)\leq r+R
, we have

\displaystyle\|u_{i}-u_{i+1}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|u_{i}-\rho_{i}\|+\|\rho_{i+1}-u_{i+1}\|+\|\rho\|

\displaystyle\leq 2(r+R)+\|\rho\|.

Thus:

\sigma_{\max}(\nabla^{2}I(u_{i}-u_{i+1}))\leq\sup_{x\in B_{\|\rho\|+2(r+R)}(0)}\sigma_{\max}(\nabla^{2}I(x))=\frac{K(\rho,r+R)}{4}.\qquad\qed

Lemma 5.6
.

If
\nabla V
satisfies the Aubry criterion with parameters
O,R,r,m
, and
u
is a configuration satisfying
\sup_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\inf_{z\in O}\|u_{i}-z\|\leq r
, then

\sigma_{\min}(\nabla^{2}V(u_{i}))\geq m>0,\quad\forall i\in\mathbb{Z}.

Proof.

For any
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, since
\sup_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\inf_{z\in O}\|u_{i}-z\|\leq r
, there exists
z\in O
such that
u_{i}\in\overline{B}_{r}(z)
.
For any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\|v\|=1
, we compute

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}V(u_{i})\cdot v\|=\lim_{t\to 0}\left\|\frac{\nabla V(u_{i}+tv)-\nabla V(u_{i})}{t}\right\|\geq\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{m\|tv\|}{|t|}=m.\qquad\qed

Proof of Theorem
5.3
.

Let
\mu
and
\alpha
denote the roots of the quadratic equation
x^{2}-(2+\frac{4R}{r})x+1=0
, where
\mu>1
and
\alpha<1
. We first verify item (i) of Definition
5.1
.
Assume
\|\xi_{i-1}\|\leq\alpha\|\xi_{i}\|
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\frac{K(\rho,r+R)}{4}\|\xi_{i+1}\|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}I(u_{i}-u_{i+1})\xi_{i+1}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}I(u_{i}-u_{i+1})\xi_{i}-\nabla^{2}I(u_{i-1}-u_{i})(\xi_{i-1}-\xi_{i})+\lambda\nabla^{2}V(u_{i})\xi_{i}\|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(\lambda\sigma_{\min}(\nabla^{2}V(u_{i}))-\frac{K(\rho,r+R)}{4}-\frac{K(\rho,r+R)}{4}-\frac{K(\rho,r+R)}{4}\alpha\right)\|\xi_{i}\|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{K(\rho,r+R)}{4}\left(2+\frac{4R}{r}-\alpha\right)\|\xi_{i}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{K(\rho,r+R)}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{\alpha}\|\xi_{i}\|,

where the three inequalities follow from Lemma
5.5
, the triangle inequality, and Lemma
5.6
, respectively.

Since
\|\xi_{i-1}\|\leq\alpha\|\xi_{i}\|
and
\|\xi_{i}\|\leq\alpha\|\xi_{i+1}\|
, it follows that

\displaystyle\|(\xi_{i},\xi_{i+1})\|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\|\xi_{i}\|^{2}+\|\xi_{i+1}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\|\xi_{i-1}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\|\xi_{i}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\|(\xi_{i-1},\xi_{i})\|

\displaystyle=\mu\|(\xi_{i-1},\xi_{i})\|.

The proof of item (ii) in Definition
5.1
follows a similar argument.
∎

Let
I:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a
C^{2}
function such that

0<\varepsilon\leq\sigma_{\min}(\nabla^{2}I(x))\leq\sigma_{\max}(\nabla^{2}I(x))\leq\mathcal{E}<+\infty,\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

for some constants
\varepsilon
and
\mathcal{E}
. The classical results from
[
27
,
30
]
establish the following:

(i)

S_{\lambda}
can be associated with the twist map

F_{\lambda}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

defined by:

\displaystyle F_{\lambda}(x,p)=(y,p^{\prime})\Longleftrightarrow\begin{cases}p=-\nabla I(x-y)-\lambda\nabla V(x)\\
p^{\prime}=-\nabla I(x-y)\end{cases}.

(ii)

F_{\lambda}
is a
C^{1}
-diffeomorphism on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
;

(iii)

u\in\mathcal{C}
is an equilibrium configuration of
S_{\lambda}
if and only if
F_{\lambda}(u_{i},p_{i})=(u_{i+1},p_{i+1})
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, where
p_{i}=-\nabla I(u_{i}-u_{i+1})-\lambda\nabla V(u_{i})
.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
\lambda>0
and
I,V\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R})
. Suppose there exist
\varepsilon>0
and
\mathcal{E}>0
such that

0<\varepsilon\leq\sigma_{\min}(\nabla^{2}I(x))\leq\sigma_{\max}(\nabla^{2}I(x))\leq\mathcal{E}<+\infty,\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

If
u
is a hyperbolic equilibrium configuration of
S_{\lambda}
, then the sequence
\{(u_{i},p_{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a hyperbolic orbit of
F_{\lambda}
, where
p_{i}=-\nabla I(u_{i}-u_{i+1})-\lambda\nabla V(u_{i})
and
F_{\lambda}
is the twist map associated with
S_{\lambda}
.

Proof.

Consider the discrete Legendre transform defined as:

\displaystyle L:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle(x,y)

\displaystyle\mapsto(y,-\nabla I(x-y)).

Since
I
is a
C^{2}
function satisfying

0<\varepsilon\leq\sigma_{\min}(\nabla^{2}I(x))\leq\sigma_{\max}(\nabla^{2}I(x))\leq\mathcal{E}<+\infty,\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

We can estimate the upper bound of largest singular value of

DL(x,y)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{O}&\mathbb{I}\\
-\nabla^{2}I(x-y)&\nabla^{2}I(x-y)\end{bmatrix}

as follows:

\displaystyle\sigma_{\max}(DL(x,y))=

\displaystyle\sup_{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\xi_{1}\|^{2}+\|\xi_{2}\|^{2}\leq 1}\left\|DL(x,y)(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\right\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\xi_{1}\|^{2}+\|\xi_{2}\|^{2}\leq 1}\left\|(\xi_{2},\nabla^{2}I(x-y)(\xi_{2}-\xi_{1}))\right\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\xi_{1}\|^{2}+\|\xi_{2}\|^{2}\leq 1}\sqrt{\|\xi_{2}\|^{2}+\|\nabla^{2}I(x-y)(\xi_{2}-\xi_{1})\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\xi_{1}\|^{2}+\|\xi_{2}\|^{2}\leq 1}\sqrt{\|\xi_{2}\|^{2}+\mathcal{E}^{2}\|\xi_{2}-\xi_{1}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{1+4\mathcal{E}^{2}}.

Similarly, the upper bound of the largest singular value of
DL^{-1}(x,y)
can be estimated as:

\sigma_{\max}(DL^{-1}(x,y))\leq\sqrt{1+4\varepsilon^{-2}}.

Define
\Sigma_{\lambda}:=L^{-1}\circ F_{\lambda}\circ L
. Then
u
is an equilibrium configuration if and only if
\Sigma_{\lambda}(u_{i-1},u_{i})=(u_{i},u_{i+1})
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
. Consequently,
\{\xi_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
satisfies (
6
) if and only if

D\Sigma_{\lambda}(u_{i-1},u_{i})(\xi_{i-1},\xi_{i})=(\xi_{i},\xi_{i+1}),\quad\forall i\in\mathbb{Z}.

If
u
is a hyperbolic equilibrium configuration, there exists
\mu>1
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and
\beta\in(0,1)
such that Definition
5.1
holds.
Define two cones in
T_{u_{i-1}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\oplus T_{u_{i}}\mathbb{R}^{d}
as:

\displaystyle C^{u}_{i}:=\{(\xi_{i-1},\xi_{i})\mid\|\xi_{i-1}\|\leq\alpha\|\xi_{i}\|\}\quad\text{and }\quad C^{s}_{i}:=\{(\xi_{i-1},\xi_{i})\mid\|\xi_{i}\|\leq\beta\|\xi_{i-1}\|\}.

Then:

(i)

D\Sigma_{\lambda}(u_{i-1},u_{i})(C^{u}_{i})\subset C^{u}_{i+1}
;

(ii)

D\Sigma_{\lambda}^{-1}(u_{i},u_{i+1})(C^{s}_{i+1})\subset C^{s}_{i}
;

(iii)

\|D\Sigma_{\lambda}(u_{i-1},u_{i})(\xi_{i-1},\xi_{i})\|\geq\mu\|(\xi_{i-1},\xi_{i})\|,\forall(\xi_{i-1},\xi_{i})\in C^{u}_{i}
;

(iv)

\|D\Sigma_{\lambda}^{-1}(u_{i},u_{i+1})(\xi_{i},\xi_{i+1})\|\geq\mu\|(\xi_{i},\xi_{i+1})\|,\forall(\xi_{i},\xi_{i+1})\in C^{s}_{i+1}
.

Using the Alekseev Cone Field Criterion
[
30
, Chapter 6]
for
\Sigma_{\lambda}
, we obtain a hyperbolic decomposition:
\{T_{u_{i-1}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\oplus T_{u_{i}}\mathbb{R}^{d}=E^{u}_{i}\oplus E^{s}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
along the orbit
\{(u_{i-1},u_{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
of
\Sigma_{\lambda}
. Consequently, for
F_{\lambda}
, we get:

T_{u_{i}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\oplus T_{p_{i}}\mathbb{R}^{d}=(DL(u_{i-1},u_{i})E_{i}^{u})\oplus(DL(u_{i-1},u_{i})E_{i}^{s}),\quad\forall i\in\mathbb{Z}.

Since
F_{\lambda}=L\circ\Sigma_{\lambda}\circ L^{-1}
, this decomposition is
DF_{\lambda}
-invariant, and for any
n\geq 0
, we have:

\displaystyle\|DF_{\lambda}^{n}(u_{i},p_{i})(\xi_{i},\zeta_{i})\|=

\displaystyle\|DL\circ D\Sigma_{\lambda}^{n}\circ DL^{-1}(u_{i},p_{i})(\xi_{i},\zeta_{i})\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{1+4\varepsilon^{-2}}\sqrt{1+4\mathcal{E}^{2}}\mu^{-n}\|(\xi_{i},\zeta_{i})\|,\quad\forall(\xi_{i},\zeta_{i})\in DL(u_{i-1},u_{i})E_{i}^{s}

and

\displaystyle\|DF_{\lambda}^{-n}(u_{i},p_{i})(\xi_{i},\zeta_{i})\|=

\displaystyle\|DL^{-1}\circ D\Sigma_{\lambda}^{-n}\circ DL(u_{i},p_{i})(\xi_{i},\zeta_{i})\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{1+4\varepsilon^{-2}}\sqrt{1+4\mathcal{E}^{2}}\mu^{-n}\|(\xi_{i},\zeta_{i})\|,\quad\forall(\xi_{i},\zeta_{i})\in DL(u_{i-1},u_{i})E_{i}^{u}.

For any
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
v_{i}\in DL(u_{i-1},u_{i})E_{i}^{u}
and
w_{i}\in DL(u_{i-1},u_{i})E_{i}^{s}
be two unit vectors. Then, we have the inequality:

\displaystyle\|v_{i}-w_{i}\|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+4\varepsilon^{-2}}}\|DL^{-1}(u_{i-1},u_{i})v_{i}-DL^{-1}(u_{i-1},u_{i})w_{i}\|.

Define
v_{i}^{\prime}:=DL^{-1}(u_{i-1},u_{i})v_{i}
and
w_{i}^{\prime}:=DL^{-1}(u_{i-1},u_{i})w_{i}
. Clearly,
v_{i}^{\prime}\in E_{i}^{u}
and
w_{i}^{\prime}\in E_{i}^{s}
.
Given that
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\beta\in(0,1)
, there exists a uniform angle
\gamma>0
between the two cone fields
\{C_{i}^{u}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
and
\{C_{i}^{s}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
. Consequently,
\gamma
is also the uniform angle between
v_{i}^{\prime}
and
w_{i}^{\prime}
. Additionally, since
\|v_{i}^{\prime}\|\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+4\mathcal{E}^{2}}}
and
\|w_{i}^{\prime}\|\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+4\mathcal{E}^{2}}}
, the angle
\gamma
ensures the existence of a constant
\delta>0
, independent of
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, such that:

\|v_{i}^{\prime}-w_{i}^{\prime}\|\geq\delta.

This implies:

\|v_{i}-w_{i}\|\geq\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{1+4\varepsilon^{-2}}}>0.

Thus, there exists a uniform angle between
\{DL(u_{i-1},u_{i})E_{i}^{u}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
and
\{DL(u_{i-1},u_{i})E_{i}^{s}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
.

As a result,
\{(u_{i},p_{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a hyperbolic orbit of
F_{\lambda}
.
∎
