Uromodulin alleviates fibrosis in acute kidney injury to chronic kidney disease transition by reducing EGFR
Authors: Zheyu Xing, Kunjing Gong, Na Wang, Lufeng Bai, Nan Hu, Di Song, Yuqing Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUromodulin has been speculated as a protective factor for acute kidney injury (AKI) and chronic kidney disease (CKD), possibly based on the uromodulin-mediated cross-talk between thick ascending limbs and proximal tubules. Here, we explored the roles of uromodulin in the AKI-CKD transition.MethodsWild-type SD rats and UMOD−/− rats were subjected to the AKI-CKD transition models. Cisplatin-stimulated HK-2 cells were treated with or without uromodulin. The renal injury and cell damage were evaluated.ResultsUMOD−/− rats observed no spontaneous kidney injury and fibrosis. In two rat models of AKI-CKD transition, induced by repeated cisplatin nephrotoxicity and ischemia-reperfusion injury, UMOD deficiency exacerbated the kidney insufficiency and fibrosis along with the over-activation of EGFR signaling. In the kidneys treated with cisplatin repeatedly, immunofluorescence showed the translocation of uromodulin and its proximity to EGFR and coimmunoprecipitation experiments also verified their interaction. Molecular docking and microscale thermophoresis demonstrated a significant binding capacity of EGFR with uromodulin and its EGF-like domains. Supplying uromodulin to cisplatin-stimulated HK-2 cells decreased the fibrotic process and the EGFR abundance, associated with the elevated EGFR ubiquitylation. Recombinant EGF-like domains of uromodulin showed a similar binding capacity and EGFR suppression effect with uromodulin. Uromodulin-induced EGFR endocytosis was impaired by the EGFR kinase inhibitor or monoclonal antibody.ConclusionsCollectively, we discovered uromodulin may alleviate the progression of AKI-CKD transition via the EGFR suppression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02537-w.

Introduction

Uromodulin, also known as Tamm-Horsfall protein, is the most abundant protein in human urine and is mainly produced by the thick ascending limb (TAL) of the Henle’s loop and proximal segment of the distal tubule [1]. After synthesis, uromodulin is modified in the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus and then transported to the cell membrane. Apically targeted uromodulin is proteolytically cleaved into tubular lumen [1]. Uromodulin consists of four epidermal growth factor (EGF)-like domains (important for protein-protein interactions) [2], a D8C domain and a bipartite Zona Pellucida (ZP) domain [1]. The classical roles of uromodulin in kidney homeostasis have been extensively proved [3], including regulating salt transportation and preventing from urinary tract infection and kidney stones [4]. Emerging research focused on its immunomodulation function and protection against acute kidney injury (AKI) [5–8]. A widely recognized model to elucidate possible mechanism is the uromodulin-mediated cross-talk between TAL and proximal tubules (PT) [9], based on the translocation of uromodulin from apical to the basal lateral, interstitium and even basolateral surface of proximal tubules after AKI [10]. Several targets on injured PT cells have been confirmed to interact with the translocated uromodulin for AKI alleviation, including TLR4 [5], MIP2 [11], IL-23/IL-17 axis [12] and TRPM2 [9]. These data suggest that uromodulin may act as an endogenous regulator of incomplete recovery or maladaptive repair after AKI and contribute to alleviating AKI-CKD transition [13–15].

Overactivation of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) has shown an essential impact on the AKI-CKD transition [16, 17]. Briefly, in response to injury factors, such as ischemia or toxicity, the EGFR pathway is upregulated to promote tubular recovery and regeneration. However, sustained activation of the EGFR pathway results in renal fibrogenesis, which is induced by the overactivation and proliferation of myofibroblast. Previously, an in vitro study with polymorphonuclear neutrophil (PMN) inferred that uromodulin bound to EGFR through its repeated EGF-like domains and further affected phagocytosis via MAPK signaling [18]. Whether changes in uromodulin abundance and trafficking are associated with EGFR levels during the AKI-CKD transition remains unclear and, if present, would further inform mechanisms of renal fibrosis.

In this study, we knocked out the UMOD gene in Sprague Dawley (SD) rats and projected to observe the effect of uromodulin deficiency under the circumstance of AKI-CKD transition. To this end, we used two rat models of AKI-CKD transition, induced by repeated cisplatin administration, as we reported previously [19], and ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI), to trigger sustained EGFR activation in fibrotic progression. By comparing the renal dysfunction and fibrosis between the wild type and UMOD−/− rats in the two models, the protective roles and possible mechanisms of uromodulin were characterized in more detail. Furthermore, experiments in vitro, including cell culture and protein binding assays, were used to explore the interaction between uromodulin and EGFR.

Methods

Animals

Wild-type (UMOD+/+) male Sprague Dawley (SD) rats (180–200 g) were purchased from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology (Beijing, China). UMOD knockout (UMOD−/−) rats were reproduced on the SD background using CRISPR/Cas9 system by Beijing Biocytogen Co.Ltd. The Cas9/sgRNAs were designed against rat UMOD gene (NCBI ID: 25128) from exon 2 to exon 6 (guide sequences were 5’ATTGGAAAGGCTGTTAGCCC3’ and 5’AGAGAGTTCCGTACGTTCAC3’ respectively) and microinjected with Cas9 RNA into zygotes. We used the littermate rats typed UMOD+/+ and UMOD−/− for animal experiments. Newborn pups were born via heterozygous F1 generation hybridization and genotyped by PCR using tail-derived DNAs with specific primers, named UE6 and UE1-6 (Supplementary Table S1). The UE6 primers were designed against the exon 6 of the rat UMOD gene. The forward primer of UE1-6 was upstream of exon 1 while the reverse the downstream of exon 6 (Supplementary FigureS1). For further identification, total protein from rat kidney was subjected to Western blot and total RNAs to qPCR (Supplementary FigureS1). All rats were housed in a temperature-controlled room (with 12 h of light per day; 40–70% relative humidity) with free access to chow and water. All animal experiments were in accordance with the protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Peking University First Hospital (Approval Number: J202054, J2023120).

Protocols for cisplatin-treated rat model

After adaptive feeding for a week, 6-week-old UMOD+/+ and UMOD−/− male rats matched with weight and were randomly divided into the control or cisplatin (CP) groups respectively. Cisplatin (P4394, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) was dissolved in sterile 0.9% NaCl (NS) at a concentration of 1 mg/ml and the same volume of NS was used as sham treatment.

Single high-dose cisplatin administration for the model of severe AKI

In the rat model of severe AKI, both UMOD+/+ (n = 9) and UMOD−/− (n = 9) rats were injected intraperitoneally with a single dose of 5 mg/kg cisplatin after a 12 h-starvation and sacrificed after 6 days. The NS control groups of UMOD+/+ (n = 5) and UMOD−/− (n = 5) rats were injected with the same volume of NS. On the baseline, 2nd and 4th days, the blood samples were collected via orbital sinus after anesthesia by intraperitoneal injection of 3% pentobarbital sodium solution (0.2 ml/100 g) body weight. On the 4th day, 6 h-urine was collected and applied for microscopic examination.

Repeated low-dose cisplatin administration for the model of AKI-CKD transition

As for the model of AKI-CKD transition, we applied the stable rat model reported before [19]. Briefly, UMOD+/+ (n = 36) and UMOD−/− (n = 36) rats were treated with 3.2 mg/kg cisplatin every two weeks for a total of four times. At the same time, UMOD+/+ (n = 12) and UMOD−/− (n = 12) rats in the NS control group were injected with NS. The 12 h-urine and blood samples were collected at baseline and on the 4th, 7th and 14th days after cisplatin or NS injection. We randomly sacrificed 6 rats of the CP group on the 4th, 14th, 46th and 56th days after the first cisplatin injection and 3 on the 18th, 28th, 32nd and 42nd days. Before the first injection, randomly selected UMOD+/+ (n = 6) and UMOD−/− (n = 6) rats were sacrificed as healthy control (CT). The other UMOD+/+ (n = 6) and UMOD−/− (n = 6) rats of the CT group were kept until the 56th day. The kidneys were dissected immediately following sacrifice, then stored at −80℃ or preserved in formalin.

Protocols for ischemia-reperfusion injured (IRI) rat model

After adaptive feeding for a week, 6-week-old UMOD+/+ and UMOD−/− male rats matched with weight and were randomly divided into the sham or ischemia-reperfusion injured (IRI) groups respectively. First, rats were anesthetized by i.p. injection of sodium pentobarbital. The back skin was cut open for the exposure of renal pedicle. The rats were kept at a constant body temperature of 37 ℃ during the operation. The sham group only performed anesthesia, dorsal incision and the exposure of renal pedicle.

Severe IRI for the model of AKI

In the severe model of AKI, UMOD+/+ (n = 17) and UMOD−/− (n = 17) rats underwent a simultaneous right nephrectomy and 45 min left kidney pedicle clamping followed by reperfusion (Unx + uIRI-45 min). These rats were kept with the sham littermates (n = 5*2) until 14 days later. The blood samples were collected on the baseline, 24 h, 48 h and 72 h for renal function examination.

Moderate IRI for the model of AKI-CKD transition

The moderate IRI model was induced by bilateral renal ischemia for 35 min (bIRI-35 min) in UMOD+/+ (n = 13) and UMOD−/− (n = 13) rats. After 24 h (n = 6*2) and 34 days (n = 7*2), rats were sacrificed for kidney collection. The sham group (n = 5*2) didn’t apply the renal pedicle clamps and were maintained for 34 days.

Blood and urine examination

Serum creatinine (SCR) and urine creatinine (UCRE) were measured using a Creatinine (Cr) Assay kit (C011-2-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bio, China) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) by a Urea Assay kit (DIUR-100, BioAssay Systems, USA). The level of urine neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin-2 (NGAL) was detected by an ELISA kit (SEB388Ra, Cloud-Clone Corp) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Urinary kidney injury molecule 1 (KIM-1) was tested by Rat KIM-1 Quantikine ELISA kit (RKM100, R&D SYSTEMS, USA). Then, we normalized the levels of urinary NGAL and KIM-1 to UCRE.

Urine sediment analyses

Six-hour fresh urine was obtained using metabolism cages and then was centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 10 min. Urine sediment was examined at high power (×400) on the bright-field microscope by an experienced nephrologist. Renal tubular epithelial cells (RTECs) per high-power field were counted for statistical analysis. RTECs were defined as size variation (11 to 15 μm in diameter), shape variation (round to columnar) and a well-evident nucleus with nucleoli.

Renal histology

Paraffin-embedded kidneys were sectioned at 2 μm and stained using periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) (BA4114, Baso, China) and Sirius red (G1472, Solarbio, China). Tubular damage was defined as loss of tubular dilation, brush border, cast formation and cell lysis. According to PAS staining, the semiquantitative assessment of tubular damage scored 0 to 4 according to the lesion area percentage (0 = no lesion, 1 = < 25%, 2 = 25–50%, 3 = 51–75%, 4 = 76–100%). To evaluate the renal fibrosis, the percentage of red stained area to the entire field (glomeruli, tubule lumina and blood vessels, if any, excluded) was assessed by Image-Pro Plus 6.0. Ten fields (×100 magnification) of each rat kidney were randomly selected for morphometric examination in a blinded manner. The mean score for each group was calculated and plotted.

Intestine histology

After rats were executed, the jejunum was separated from small intestine. Paraffin-embedded jejunum were sectioned at 6 μm and stained using periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) (BA4114, Baso, China) to visualize villi and goblet cells. Villus height was measured from the tip to the crypt-villus junction and goblet cell numbers per villus were counted manually using ImageJ (NIH, USA). For each animal, at least 10 well-oriented villi were analyzed. Measurements were performed blinded to experimental group.

Antibodies

The primary antibodies used for Western blots, immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) included rabbit monoclonal anti-uromodulin antibody (ab207170, Abcam, UK, RRID: AB_2889163), mouse monoclonal anti-uromodulin antibody (sc-271022, Santa Cruz, USA, RRID: AB_10610634), rabbit monoclonal anti-EGFR(p1068) antibody (ab40815, Abcam, UK, RRID: AB_732110), rabbit monoclonal anti-EGFR antibody (ab32077, Abcam, USA, RRID: AB_732101), rabbit monoclonal anti-pERK1/2 antibody (ab76299, Abcam, UK, RRID: AB_1523577), rabbit monoclonal anti-ERK1/2 antibody (ab184699, Abcam, UK, RRID: AB_2802136), rabbit monoclonal anti-vimentin antibody (#5741, CST, USA, RRID: AB_10695459), rabbit monoclonal anti-AQP1 antibody (ab300463, Abcam, UK, RRID: AB_3675721), mouse monoclonal anti-alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) antibody (ab7817, Abcam, UK, RRID: AB_262054), rabbit monoclonal anti-collagen I antibody (ab270993, Abcam, UK, AB_2927551), mouse monoclonal anti-GAPDH antibody (ab8245, Abcam, UK, RRID: AB_2107448) and rabbit monoclonal anti-ubiquitin antibody (ab134953, Abcam, UK, RRID: AB_2801561). Secondary antibodies were horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG(H + L) (ZB-2305, ZSGB-BIO, China, RRID: AB_2747415), mouse anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (sc-2357, Santa Cruz, USA, RRID: AB_628497), FITC labeled donkey anti-sheep IgG(H + L) (ab6896, Abcam, China, RRID: AB_955329

), AF647-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG(H + L) (A0473, Beyotime, China, RRID:AB_2891322) FITC labeled goat anti-mouse IgG(H + L) , FITC labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG(H + L) (A0562, Beyotime, China, RRID: AB_2923335) and Cy3-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG(H + L) (A0516, Beyotime, China, RRID: AB_2893015).

Western blots

Total protein from rat kidneys or HK-2 cells was extracted on ice with Cell Lysis Buffer for Western and IP (P0013, Beyotime, China) with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (P1051, Beyotime, China) following standard protocols. Extracted proteins were denatured, separated on SDS-polyacrylamide gel and transferred to PVDF membranes. Then, the membranes were blocked in 5% fat-free milk dissolved in TBST buffer for 1 h at room temperature, incubated with the appropriate primary antibody at 4℃ overnight and HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at 26℃. The target bands were visualized using an ImageQuant LAS 4000 mini system (GE, healthcare, USA) and their intensity was quantified using ImageJ software (Media, Cybernetics, USA).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

IHC staining of the kidney was performed on paraffin sections. After antigen retrieval, sections were incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4℃. Having washed with PBS three times, the slides were exposed to HRP-labeled secondary antibodies (PV-6000, ZSGB-BIO, China) and DAB substrate (ZLI-9018, ZSGB-BIO, China) to visualize antigens. Finally, the slides were counterstained with hematoxylin and mounted after dehydration. For quantification, 10 fields (×400) were randomly selected from each tissue section and the staining was analyzed using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 in a blinded manner.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Renal IF staining was performed on paraffin sections. After antigen heating retrieval, kidney sections were incubated with the following primary antibodies: rabbit monoclonal anti-EGFR antibody (ab32077, Abcam, USA) at 1:200, sheep anti-uromodulin antibody (ABD85950054, Bio-Rad, USA) at 1:50 and mouse monoclonal anti-AQP1 antibody (sc-55466, Santa Cruz, USA) at 1:2000. The slides were then exposed to FITC labeled donkey anti-sheep IgG(H + L) (ab6896, Abcam, China), Cy3-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG(H + L) (A0516, Beyotime, China) and AF647-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG(H + L) (A0473, Beyotime, China) at 1:200. Then, the sections were coverslipped with Mounting Medium with DAPI (ZLI-9557, ZSGB-BIO, China,). The IF images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany) at ×400 magnification.

Cells in chambers with indicated treatments were fixed in 4% PFA. The rabbit anti-EGFR antibody (ab32077, Abcam, USA) at 1:500 was applied as the first antibody, followed by FITC labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG(H + L) (A0562, Beyotime, China) as the second antibody. After being stained with DAPI, the cells were photographed by the STELLARIS platform for confocal microscopy (Leica, Germany) at ×400 magnification.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analyses

Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (15596026, Thermo Fisher, USA). The cDNA synthesis kit was HiScript III All-in-one RT Supermix Perfect for qPCR (R333, Vazyme, China). The sequences of the primers (Supplementary Table 2) were tested with excellent performance. qPCR was performed using PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (A25742, Thermo Fisher, USA). GAPDH was used as an internal control for normalization. Relative quantification was performed according to the 2−△△Ct method.

Native uromodulin isolation from healthy human urine

Urine from a healthy human was collected to extract native uromodulin using a rapid isolation method [20]. AW Standard Super-Cell® (221791, Sigma, Germany) was added to the urine by thoroughly mixing and filtering through Whatman filter paper (1001, GE, USA) on a Buchner funnel. The filtered earth was suspended in deionized water and discarded by centrifugation. The supernatant added with PBS buffer containing 0.14 mol/L NaCl was mixed with earth and filtered again. After dialysis and ultrafiltration, the protein solution was identified by western blots with Tamm-Horsfall Glycoprotein Native Protein (MBS537268, MyBioSource, USA) as the positive control. The purity was tested by Coomassie brilliant blue staining.

Plasmid and recombinant protein segment production

Plasmid pCAG-GFP (#11150) was acquired from Addgene. We reconstructed the plasmid as pCAG-SP-6xhis-EGF by replacing the GFP with the EGF-like domains from UMOD. We confirmed the plasmid construction by DNA sequencing. After amplification and extraction by EndoFree Maxi Plasmid Kit (DP117, Tiangen, China), the plasmid was used to produce recombinant fragment U-EGF. HEK293FT cells (Cobioer biosciences, China) were maintained as adherent cultures in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (10013CV, Corning, USA) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) (10099141, Gibco, USA) and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (15140122, Invitrogen, USA) at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2. Having transfected using X-tremeGENE™ HP DNA Transfection Reagent (6366236001, Roche, Switzerland) following the manufacturer’s instructions, the cells were cultured in the FreeStyle™ 293 expression medium (12338018, Gibco, USA) for 60 h. Then, the culture supernatants were collected, centrifuged and ultrafiltrated. Commercial His-tag protein purification beads (70501-100, Beaver, China) were incubated for 1 h at room temperature to purify the His-tagged recombinant segment proteins. The elution buffer with imidazole was applied to acquire the purified recombinant U-EGF segment. Western blots and Coomassie brilliant blue staining were used to identify the products.

Molecular docking

The modeled structures of uromodulin and EGFR extracellular domain were obtained from the AlphaFold database. Protein molecular docking analysis was tested on the GRAMM-X platform [21]. Pymol [22] (V.2.4) and PDBePISA server [23] were applied to visualize the docking model.

Microscale Thermophoresis (MST)

The binding capacity was measured by MST using NanoTamper (Monolith NT.115, Germany). Recombinant human EGFR protein (ab155639, Abcam, UK) was labeled with red fluorescent in MST buffer including 0.05% Tween and was kept at 23.5nM. Ligands were diluted with a serial 1:1 ratio of 16 gradients and mixed with labeled EGFR at a 1:1 ratio respectively. Recombinant human EGF protein (ab259398, Abcam, UK) was used as the positive control and diluted from 41.5 µM. Bovine serum albumin (BSA) (A9418, Sigma, Germany) was used as the negative control. Purified human native uromodulin was diluted from 4.55 µM and recombinant U-EGF segment 6.45 µM. Monolith NT.115 Capillaries were then filled and put into the device tray for measurements. Data were acquired using medium MST power and analyzed by MO Affinity Analysis Software (Nano Temper) with the Kd model for calculation. The MST curves were rendered using MO Affinity Analysis Software (Nano Temper).

In vitro microtiter plate binding assay

The binding assay detected the binding capacity of solubilized, purified human uromodulin and U-EGF with immobilized EGFR. Briefly, rhEGFR protein (ab155639, Abcam, UK) was coated to the microtiter wells at a concentration of 3 µg/ml in 0.1 M carbonate buffer (pH 9.6) overnight at 4 ℃. After washing with 0.01 M PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 (PBST), the wells were blocked with 1% BSA for 1 h at 37 ℃. The purified uromodulin and U-EGF (in PBS containing 0.05% Tween 20 and 1% BSA) at various concentrations were added to bind the immobilized EGFR by incubation at 37 ℃ for 1 h. After three-time washings to remove the unbound protein, mouse anti-uromodulin monoclonal antibody (CL1032AP, Cedarlane, Canada) was added, followed by goat anti-mouse IgG HRP-conjugate (172–1011, Bio-Rad, USA). The substrate solution was added as a chromogenic reagent and stopped by 1 M H2SO4. The color of the reaction was read at an absorbance of 450 nm. The inhibition assays showed the inhibition of soluble EGFR to the binding of coating EGFR (3 µg/ml) with uromodulin (25 µg/ml) or U-EGF (50 µg/ml). Soluble EGFR was incubated with uromodulin or U-EGF at 37 ℃ for 1 h before loading onto EGFR-coated wells. Curves were fitted from three independent experiments data using a dose-response model with variable slope (four parameters).

HK-2 cell culture and treatment

The HK-2 cells (ATCC, USA) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium-F12 (11330032, Invitrogen, USA) with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) (10099141, Gibco, USA) and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (15140122, Invitrogen, USA) under a humidified atmosphere consisting of 5% CO2 and 95% air at 37 ℃. The cells were seeded at 5 × 105 cells per well into 6-well plates and incubated for 24 h with three wells for each assayed group. Then, the cells were treated with 5 µg/ml cisplatin or vehicle (NS) for 3 times, each consisting of 8 h-treatment and an interval of 16 h. Meanwhile, uromodulin extracted from human urine (20 µg/ml) or recombinant U-EGF segment (20 µg/ml) were added along respectively. After washing twice with cold PBS, cells were harvested into the lysis buffer for Western blots assay. Furtherly, HK-2 cells stimulated with MG132 (10 µM) or NH4Cl (250 µM) were also collected for Western blots to determine the EGFR levels.

Cell viability assay (CCK8)

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Dojindo, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, HK-2 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well and cultured overnight to allow adherence. The cells were then treated with cisplatin (5 µg/ml) for 24–48 h in the presence or absence of uromodulin (20 µg/ml) or U-EGF (20 µg/ml). Following treatment, 10 µl of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. Relative cell viability was normalized to the control group and expressed as a percentage. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

Immunoprecipitation

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments were performed using Pierce Classic Magnetic IP/Co-IP kit (88804, Thermo Fisher, USA) following the manufacturers’ instructions. Basically, normal or fibrotic kidneys from UMOD+/+ rats, which were sacrificed on baseline or 28 days after the first cisplatin injection, were lysed in IP-lysis buffer and then quantified the protein concentration by a Pierce BCA Protein Assay kit (23227, Thermo Fisher, USA). The supernatants were incubated with rabbit anti-uromodulin (ab207170, Abcam, UK), anti-EGFR (ab32077, Abcam, USA) antibody and rabbit IgG control antibody (30000-0-AP, Proteintech, China) overnight at 4℃, followed by protein A/G-conjugated agarose for 1 h at 26℃. The co-IP products were washed and detected through Western blots. For EGFR ubiquitylation assays, the stimulated HK-2 cell lysates were subjected to anti-EGFR immunoprecipitation followed by anti-ubiquitin Western blotting. To validate the reliability of the EGFR degradation assay, recombinant human EGF (20 ng/mL) was used as a positive control.

Statistics

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0 statistical software (SPSS, USA). Variables distributed normally were shown as mean ± SD and compared using a t-test, while non-normally or nonparametric as median and interquartile range and compared between groups using the Mann-Whitney U-test. All p-values were two-tailed, and P < 0.05 was considered significant. GraphPad Prism 8.0 was used to visualize the vector diagrams (GraphPad, USA).

Results

Genetic depletion of UMOD of SD rats showed no spontaneous kidney injury and fibrosis

UMOD gene was knocked out on the genetic background of SD rats. To examine the effect of UMOD ablation on the rat kidneys, we followed the vital characteristics of UMOD+/+ and UMOD−/− rats from 6 to 14 weeks of age (Fig. 1A). There was no difference in their body weight gain rate (Fig. 1B), SCR (Fig. 1C) and BUN (Fig. 1D). Under light microscopy, there was no detectable renal morphological lesion according to PAS (Fig. 1E) and Sirius red (Fig. 1F) stains in the two strains at 6-week-old and 14-week-old. Western blots of kidney tissues showed no upregulation in the protein levels of EGFR, pERK1/2, α-SMA, vimentin and AQP1 after UMOD deletion up to 14 weeks (Fig. 1G, H). We used qPCR to compare the expression of biomarkers related to renal injury (KIM-1, NGAL and CCL2), fibrosis (α-SMA and COL1) and macrophage infiltration (F4/80) (Fig. 1I) and no difference was identified. Moreover, the UMOD deficiency did not influence the expression of renal EGFR mRNA. These results suggested that UMOD−/− rats did not develop spontaneous kidney injury and fibrosis by 14 weeks of age. The UMOD ablation seemed to have no damage to renal histology and function.

Uromodulin deficiency exacerbated tubular damage in two rat models of severe AKI

To determine the role of uromodulin in severe AKI, we established two models in UMOD+/+ and UMOD−/− littermates, including cisplatin nephropathy and ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) models. The severe cisplatin nephropathy was induced by a single i.p. injection of high-dose cisplatin (5 mg/kg) (Fig. 2A). We compared the two strains’ mortality, renal function and tubular damage. Uromodulin deficiency caused a higher 6-day mortality rate (P = 0.013 < 0.05) in the acute phase of cisplatin nephrotoxicity. As shown in survival analysis (Fig. 2B), 6 of 9 UMOD−/− rats died after high-dose of cisplatin administration while 8 of 9 UMOD+/+ rats survived. Meanwhile, UMOD−/− rats displayed a significant weight loss (P < 0.05) on the 2nd (−0.046 ± 0.041 vs. 0.013 ± 0.049, P < 0.05) and the 4th (−0.123 ± 0.041 vs. −0.047 ± 0.075, P < 0.05) day after cisplatin injection (Fig. 2C). Their renal function was evaluated by the levels of SCR (Fig. 2D) and BUN (Fig. 2E). On the 2nd and 4th days, UMOD ablation in cisplatin-treated rats caused an upward trend of the SCR level but without significance. Notably, a significantly higher level of BUN was detected in UMOD−/− rats on day 2 (45.68 ± 17.01 mg/dL vs. 28.94 ± 8.07 mg/dL, P < 0.05) and day 4 (180.45 ± 18.90 mg/dL vs. 125.18 ± 55.28 mg/dL, P < 0.05) after cisplatin administration. Consistent with impaired kidney function, the number of renal tubular epithelial cells (RTECs) per high-power field surged in UMOD−/− rats (14.9 ± 7.1 vs. 2.3 ± 1.3, P < 0.05) according to urine sediment microscopic examination (Fig. 2F, G). The severe IRI lesion was induced by a simultaneous right nephrectomy and 45 min left kidney pedicle clamping (Unx + uIRI-45 min) (Fig. 2H). UMOD−/− rats exhibited a higher mortality rate (P = 0.0002 < 0.05) than UMOD+/+ rats following Unx + uIRI-45 min (Fig. 2I). In addition, kidney dysfunction was markedly aggravated in UMOD−/− rats after IRI, as revealed by SCR level (Fig. 2J). These findings indicated the lack of uromodulin made the kidneys more sensitive to AKI induced by IRI and cisplatin toxicity.

Uromodulin deficiency accelerated renal fibrosis in two rat models of AKI-CKD transition

We treated the UMOD+/+ and UMOD−/− rats with low-dose of cisplatin for 4 times at 2-week intervals (Fig. 3A). There was a significant difference between the UMOD+/+ and UMOD−/− rats in the change of body weight gain rate (Fig. 3B), SCR (Fig. 3C) and BUN (Fig. 3D) levels. All four times of cisplatin administration decreased the weight gain rate (Fig. 3E), but UMOD−/− rats showed significantly greater (P < 0.05) weight loss than UMOD+/+ rats after the third cisplatin treatment. At the 4th peak values of SCR (Fig. 3C) and BUN (Fig. 3D) curves induced by cisplatin, UMOD−/− rats exhibited worse renal dysfunction (P < 0.05). After each AKI phase, the lack of uromodulin tended to delay renal recovery (P < 0.05). Specifically, two weeks after the first cisplatin injection, the renal function of UMOD+/+ rats recovered to normal while UMOD−/− rats remained insufficient. A marked increase in renal injury biomarkers, KIM-1 and NGAL, was observed in cisplatin-treated UMOD−/− rats compared with the UMOD+/+ rats at the indicated time points. The Fig. 3E presented the discrepancy of UKIM-1/UCRE between UMOD+/+ and UMOD−/− rats on the 4th, 18th and 32nd days (P < 0.05). Meanwhile, in UMOD−/− rats, UNGAL/UCRE (Fig. 3F) was significantly higher on the 4th, 28th, 32nd, 42nd and 46th days (P < 0.05). These results demonstrated that the deficiency of uromodulin exacerbated the damage induced by cisplatin during AKI. Consistent with the discrepancy of biochemical traits, PAS-stained kidney tissue (Fig. 3G, I) on day 4, 18 and 32, corresponding to the three peaks of AKI respectively, revealed more aggressive damages in UMOD−/− rats. In addition, cisplatin-induced renal fibrosis developed to a greater extent on day 14, 28, 42 and 56 in the UMOD−/− rats (Fig. 3H, J) as revealed by Sirius red staining. The more excessive accumulation of renal fibrosis indicated that uromodulin had a protective effect on renal fibrosis during the cisplatin-induced AKI-CKD transition.

Uromodulin deficiency associated with the over-activation of EGFR signaling in AKI-CKD transition

To further validate the role of uromodulin in the development of renal fibrosis, Western blot and immunohistochemistry (IHC) were performed at a series of time points. Compared with the cisplatin-treated UMOD+/+ rats, the absence of UMOD significantly promoted the renal α-SMA accumulation and collagen I deposition detected by Western blot (Fig. 5A) and IHC scoring (FigS2). Vimentin, another biomarker of fibroblasts, also exhibited an upregulated trend in cisplatin-treated UMOD−/− rats. Meanwhile, we also found a significant increase in EGFR (Fig. 5A, B, C) and its phosphorylated form (p1068) (Fig. 5A) in UMOD−/− rats after cisplatin re-exposure. Consistent with the upregulation of EGFR in UMOD−/− rats, we also observed an increased level of phosphorylated ERK1/2, AKT and STAT3 (Fig. 5A). However, there was no significant difference in renal EGFR mRNA between the two strains on the 4th, 14th and 56th days after the first cisplatin administration (Fig. 5D). In the IRI model of AKI-CKD transition, Western blot (Fig. 5E, F) also showed a higher level of fibrosis markers and EGFR activation in UMOD−/− rats. Collectively, these results suggested that the UMOD deficiency was related to excessive activation of the EGFR signaling pathway at the protein level.

Uromodulin bound to EGFR via EGF-like domains of Uromodulin

We further performed the co-staining for uromodulin and EGFR on the kidney sections from the shamed UMOD+/+ rat and the cisplatin-treated UMOD+/+ rat respectively (Fig. 6A, FigS3). Immunofluorescence (IF) images from a shamed UMOD+/+ rat kidney showed that uromodulin (green) was mainly distributed on the apical membrane of the TAL cells and little EGFR (red) in proximal tubule cells. By day 56 after the first cisplatin administration, uromodulin was also translocated into cellular and basal stained. More importantly, the uromodulin signal was also observed into the renal interstitium, as reported before [10], and was contiguous with the basolateral membrane of the EGFR-positive proximal tubular epithelial cells, marked by AQP1 in purple (Fig. 6A). Given the interaction between uromodulin and EGFR, we modeled the binding of uromodulin and the extracellular domains of EGFR by rigid protein docking. As shown in Fig. 6B and Supplementary Table 3, a total of 19 predicted hydrogen bonds through amino acid residue sites between the extracellular domains of EGFR and uromodulin, particularly within the EGF-like domains, were predicted to enhance the binding capacity of the two proteins, which may impede the binding of EGFR and its ligands. Figure 6C, the immunoblotting image of immunoprecipitation, demonstrated the interaction of uromodulin and EGFR in the rat fibrotic kidney, instead of healthy kidney. We further performed the binding assay for EGFR and uromodulin in vitro. First, we extracted the human uromodulin from healthy human urine according to the methods reported before [20]. To figure out the target domain of uromodulin interacting with EGFR, we constructed a plasmid to produce the EGF-like domains of uromodulin, named U-EGF recombinant fragment, using HEK293FT cells. Western blot and Coomassie brilliant blue staining were used to identify the products (Fig. 6D). To detect the binding capacity of EGFR with uromodulin, we performed MST assays (Fig. 6E). For a better quality of measurement, we used rhEGF as the reference control with a KD value of 397 ± 94nM binding to EGFR in our testing system. The binding strength of EGFR and human native uromodulin was 176 ± 133nM. U-EGF fragment had a similar binding capacity (628 ± 173nM) with rhEGF and uromodulin. We also performed the microtiter plate binding assays to detect the in vitro binding capacity of uromodulin or U-EGF to immobilized EGFR (Fig. 6F). The binding assays showed that the binding of liquid-phase uromodulin or U-EGF to pre-fixed EGFR was in a dose-dependent manner. The inhibition reaction revealed a specific interaction between EGFR and uromodulin or U-EGF.

Uromodulin attenuated fibrosis via the EGFR pathway in cisplatin-treated HK-2 cells

Because we found the interaction between EGFR and uromodulin or its recombinant fragment U-EGF, we used the cisplatin-treated HK-2 cells to test the protective mechanism of uromodulin and U-EGF (Fig. 7A, B). Western blot showed that the 48 h-stimulation of cisplatin elevated the activation of EGFR signaling and the protein levels of α-SMA and vimentin (P < 0.05). When the 20 µg/ml uromodulin or U-EGF was added, the HK-2 cells downregulated the EGFR pathway and the expression of α-SMA and vimentin (P < 0.05) after the 48 h-stimulation of cisplatin. Specifically, the protein levels of total EGFR and phosphorylated EGFR (p1068) reduced simultaneously. The findings confirmed that uromodulin or its recombinant fragment U-EGF could decrease the expression of fibrotic factors after adding cisplatin in HK-2 cells by reducing the EGFR level. To determine whether uromodulin exerts a cytoprotective effect on tubular epithelial cells, HK-2 cells were treated with cisplatin in the presence or absence of native uromodulin or recombinant U-EGF. Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. As shown in Fig. 7C, both uromodulin and U-EGF markedly improved HK-2 cell viability compared with the cisplatin + BSA group.

Discussion

Over the past decades, increasing investigations have demonstrated the protective and diagnostic values of uromodulin in AKI and CKD. In the study, we identified that the genetic ablation of UMOD exacerbated the progression of AKI to CKD, accompanied by the over-activation of the EGFR signaling pathway. In vitro, native uromodulin and its EGF-like domains showed appreciable affinity with EGFR. Furthermore, supplementation of uromodulin to cisplatin-treated HK-2 cells alleviated the fibrotic progress and the over-activation of EGFR, associated with the upregulated EGFR ubiquitylation. These findings collectively suggested a novel protective mechanism of uromodulin in renal fibrosis, as depicted in Fig. 7G.

Since the protective role of uromodulin in AKI was first reported [5], uromodulin-mediated cross-talk between TAL and proximal tubules has been considered as one of the classical models for the mechanism. Based on this model, several targets of uromodulin mediating AKI alleviation have been identified, including TLR4 [5], MIP2 [11], IL-23/IL-17 axis [12] and TRPM2 [9]. Apart from the model, uromodulin exhibits more extensive protection against AKI, such as the capacity to trap lymphokines [24, 25] and regulation of mononuclear phagocytes [6]. In this study, we first identified that uromodulin deficiency exacerbated the progression of AKI-CKD transition, induced by re-exposure to cisplatin as well as IRI attack. We currently found the interaction between uromodulin and EGFR, mainly dependent on the EGF-like domains, potentially caused the reno-protection in the transition of AKI to CKD. Given the above, it appears that uromodulin is a multifaceted glycoprotein to maintain the homeostasis of the renal micro-environment [5], so multiple effects of uromodulin still need to be discovered and the answers may not be single.

In our study, we observed a disproportionate mortality in knockout rats compared with the relatively modest renal histologic changes. Given previous reports indicating intestinal expression of uromodulin [26], we also examined small intestinal morphology and found more pronounced intestinal injury in knockout animals, including shortened villi and reduced goblet cell numbers (Fig. 4S). These findings suggested that the high mortality may not be solely attributable to renal injury but could also involve systemic effects. While the detailed mechanisms require further investigation, our results highlight a potential new direction for exploring distant organ contributions to outcomes in acute kidney injury.

EGFR is a receptor tyrosine kinase of the ErbB family widely expressed in epithelial cells [27]. The activation of EGFR leads to a transcription effect on cell proliferation and dedifferentiation via multiple downstream pathways, including MAPK/ERK pathway, PI3K/AKT pathway and JAK/STAT pathway [28, 29]. In the current study, we observed the robust activation of EGFR signaling in the AKI-CKD transition. In fact, the role of EGFR in AKI is model dependent and therefore controversial. Generally, EGFR has been confirmed to facilitate renal recovery in response to AKI induced by IRI and folic acid [16, 30, 31]. In contrast, cisplatin [32]-, LPS [33]- and vancomycin (VAN) [34]-induced AKI could be ameliorated by the inhibition of the EGFR pathway. Although EGFR has converse effects during AKI depending on injury factors, the sustained and recurrent activation of EGFR is a hallmark of the fibrotic response in the progression of CKD [35–37]. The EGFR signaling pathway contributes to the activation of renal interstitial fibroblasts, induction of tubular atrophy, overproduction of inflammatory factors and promotion of glomerular and vascular injury [35]. On the contrary, EGFR inhibition can limit the progression of renal fibrosis [17, 38–41], as proved by the application of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) or genetic approaches (EGFR mutant Wa-2 mice) [33, 34].

Given these findings, our data suggested that uromodulin may act as an endogenous, kidney-specific EGFR inhibitor restraining maladaptive fibrotic signaling. Consistent with this possibility, multiple clinical datasets indicate that uromodulin is dysregulated in human CKD, with lower urinary or serum levels associated with faster kidney function decline [42–45]. Common UMOD promoter variants further highlight context-dependent effects on CKD and hypertension [1, 3, 46]. Although reduced uromodulin and EGFR activation have each been independently linked to kidney fibrosis, no human study has simultaneously evaluated uromodulin expression and EGFR activation or demonstrated a direct correlation between them, underscoring the need for future translational validation.

For decades, the function study of uromodulin has largely relied on the in vitro experiments to regulate PMN phagocytosis [18, 47–50]. The lactoferrin and cathepsin G on the PMN membrane were identified as the binding sites [49]. Later, EGFR was speculated to be involved in the uromodulin function according to the fact that uromodulin had a potency comparable to EGF on ERK1/2 activation [18]. In the present study, for the first time, we reported direct evidence for the interaction between uromodulin and EGFR in vitro and in vivo. In vivo, we observed that uromodulin deficiency accelerated the progression of renal fibrosis and the overactivation of the EGFR pathway in cisplatin-treated and ischemia-reperfusion-injured rats. Moreover, the co-staining images supported the possible interaction between uromodulin and EGFR in spatial distribution. As for in vitro experiments, because of the special viscosity, uromodulin is reported to use MST as the most suitable method for binding capacity study [51]. The KD values detected by MST tests indicated that uromodulin or its EGF-like domains had an appreciable binding capacity with EGFR, approaching that of rhEGF. We also observed that uromodulin exhibited the ability to inhibit the EGFR pathway in the cisplatin-treated tubular epithelial cells.

The possible mechanism of uromodulin-EGFR interaction could be inferred according to the following evidence. First, there were no significant functional or morphological disparities between UMOD+/+ rats and UMOD−/− rats when they were kept without any renal damage factors. Meanwhile, the mRNA and protein levels in renal EGFR remained steady regardless of uromodulin deficiency in sham rats. When exposed to renal toxicity or IRI, the UMOD−/− rats presented more sustained activation of EGFR signaling compared with the UMOD+/+ rats. These findings suggested that uromodulin exerted inhibition on EGFR signaling in tubular epithelium. Secondly, there was no significant difference in the mRNA level of renal EGFR between UMOD+/+ rats and UMOD−/− rats after repeated cisplatin administration. However, as evidenced by Western blot and IHC, the protein level of renal EGFR was significantly elevated after UMOD knockout in the cisplatin model. The results suggested that uromodulin regulated the protein level rather than the mRNA level of EGFR to exert its inhibitory effect. The conventional model of ligand-induced EGFR trafficking proposed that physiological concentrations of EGF mainly induced clathrin-mediated endocytosis (CME) while the saturated range drove clathrin-independent endocytosis (CIE) [52–55]. CME contributed to recycling and sustained EGFR pathway activation. Conversely, CIE was a major route for ubiquitylation and lysosomal degradation to attenuate the EGFR pathway. Uromodulin may mimic the saturated EGF to upregulate CIE in damaged renal epithelial cells. Further investigations are needed to validate the hypothesis above.

Specific enzyme degradation experiments proved that the protein-core structure of uromodulin was more responsible for the protein interactions than the carbohydrate-side chains [48, 49]. The protein core of uromodulin is predicted to be composed of EGF-like domains, a D8C domain and a ZP domain [1]. The EGF-like domains are generally found in the extracellular membrane-bound proteins and are implicated in protein-protein interactions [2]. The conserved ZP domain helps with extracellular polymerization, while the D8C domain is rich in cysteine but of unknown function [56]. Given the above, we produced the recombinant EGF-like domains of uromodulin, named U-EGF, for further investigation. Interestingly, the recombinant fragment U-EGF was observed to exert similar effects on EGFR in vitro, indicating that the EGF-like domains possibly contributed to the interaction between EGFR and uromodulin. Whether the EGF-like domains play a crucial role in EGFR binding and inhibition requires more evidence. For instance, it might be meaningful to investigate the alteration in the animals with EGF-like regions being deleted in UMOD.

The study infers a dominant role for the EGF-like domain using a U-EGF surrogate; however, we did not experimentally rule out contributions from other uromodulin domains (e.g., the ZP domain). This limitation will be addressed by domain-specific perturbations and polymerization-state controls in future studies. We focused on the uromodulin-induced EGFR reduction and did not experimentally rule out alternative mechanisms, including direct inhibition of pro-fibrotic cytokines or modulation of innate immune pathways.

This work primarily employs rodent models; thus, species-specific differences may limit direct clinical translation. We did not include validation in human kidney tissue. Future studies will evaluate the mechanism in human kidney biopsy specimens. In addition, although a recombinant EGF-like fragment of uromodulin (U-EGF) recapitulated the reduction of EGFR abundance and signaling, potential off-target effects cannot be excluded.

Conclusions

In summary, uromodulin deficiency exacerbated the progression of AKI-CKD transition induced by re-exposure to cisplatin and IRI via the activation of EGFR signaling. Uromodulin has a considerable binding capacity to EGFR through its EGF-like domains. By reducing the renal EGFR in protein level, uromodulin seems to be a potential therapeutic target during kidney fibrosis.
