Reduced Motor Preparation and Altered EEG Signatures of Prediction in Autistic Adults
Authors: Soroush Bagheri, Annie Cardinaux, Lindsay Bungert, Cindy E Li, Amanda M O'Brien, Pawan Sinha, Jonathan Cannon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA standing hypothesis proposes that the social and behavioral diagnostic features of autism may develop as a result of more fundamental difficulties in forming and utilizing predictions. More specifically, in the present study, we hypothesized that autistic individuals are less likely to mobilize motor preparatory processes when given reason to anticipate an imperative cue. In a preregistered experiment, we asked participants to respond as quickly as possible to target sounds embedded in a regular periodic sequence of tones, and informed them that the three distinct auditory tones in the sequence signal different probabilities of a target on the next beat. We found that a group of autistic participants showed significantly less benefit of cues signaling high target probability on target response time than an IQ‐ and language‐matched control group of non‐autistic participants. In concurrent electroencephalography (EEG) recordings, the autistic group also showed a reduced effect of high probability cues on the late CNV, a motor preparatory ramping potential. Group differences in anticipatory desynchronization of motor‐related mu and beta oscillations were less straightforward—rather than showing broadly weaker predictive desynchronization, the autistic group showed reduced desynchronization specifically following medium probability cues. We also found evidence of a group difference in neural entrainment to the 1 Hz periodic sequence: the autistic group showed shallower ramping potentials anticipating the next event across all cue types, and reduced phase entrainment at posterior electrodes. Our results focus attention on reduced spontaneous predictive motor preparation as a possible sensorimotor contribution to autism's more visible manifestations.Keywords: autism, electroencephalography, entrainment, motor, oscillations, prediction

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is diagnostically identified in part through difficulties in social communication (American Psychiatric Association 2021). However, researchers have argued that non‐social neural or cognitive mechanisms may underlie the observable social symptoms. Various unifying accounts of autism have attempted to trace the social manifestations of autism back to domain‐general neural causes (Vasa et al. 2016; Raul et al. 2024; Rubenstein and Merzenich 2003; Noel and Angelaki 2023) or domain‐general perceptual, cognitive, and motor differences (Gomot and Wicker 2012; Happé and Frith 2006; Noel and Angelaki 2023). Attempts to unify the characteristics of autism under such “umbrella” accounts are valuable in their potential to help us understand and support autistic individuals 1 both within and beyond social environments and to shed light on the neurocognitive roots of human social behavior.

One set of domain‐general accounts of autism posits that differences in predictive processes underlie diverse aspects of autism. As outlined by a recent review (Cannon et al. 2021), a subset of these accounts hypothesize that the influence of top‐down expectations (“priors”) on perception and judgment is attenuated in autism (Gomot and Wicker 2012; Pellicano and Burr 2012); others hypothesize that the influence of these top‐down factors is less flexible and context‐dependent in autism (Lawson et al. 2014; Van de Cruys et al. 2014); and one, formulated by this lab, hypothesizes autistic individuals have trouble detecting predictive relationships that are weak and/or connect events over longer intervals of time (Sinha et al. 2014).

Prediction‐related accounts of autism show some promise, as highlighted by a recently developed self‐report measure of prediction‐related experiences that found difficulties in both the learning and behavioral expression of predictions (both social and nonsocial) in an autistic group (N = 59) relative to controls (N = 66) (O'Brien et al. 2024). However, attempts to empirically isolate the aspects of prediction impaired in autism have yielded a mixed and complex picture (Angeletos Chrysaitis and Seriès 2023; Cannon et al. 2021).

A systematic review by this lab (Cannon et al. 2021) suggested several possible categories of predictive differences in autism, including differences in both the learning/detection of predictive relationships and in the utilization of known predictive relationships. One surprising category of results described reduced spontaneous preparation for predicted events through anticipatory movement (including gaze in adults (Schuwerk et al. 2016; Barzy et al. 2020; Ganglmayer et al. 2020; Krogh‐Jespersen et al. 2018) and gross movements in at‐risk infants (Landa et al. 2016; Northrup et al. 2017)) and anticipatory activation of movement‐facilitatory brain processes (as observed in electroencephalography (EEG) measures) (Grisoni et al. 2019; Thillay et al. 2016). The designs of many of these studies made it difficult to separate a possible difficulty in learning predictive antecedent/consequent associations over time from a possible reduction in the spontaneous utilization of learned predictions to prepare and deploy motor responses. However, two reviewed studies found differences in motor correlates of prediction in experiments when participants were explicitly informed of the predictive rules, minimizing the requirement of implicit learning of predictive associations and thus rendering implicit learning a less plausible source of group differences (Greene et al. 2019; Thillay et al. 2016). The results of these two studies did not fit neatly into any of the recent theories of predictive differences in autism, which have focused on the effects of predictive priors on perception and judgment rather than movement and motor preparation, though they are consistent with earlier proposals of impaired motor anticipatory function (Schmitz et al. 2003).

Of these two studies, one found a lesser influence of prediction on gaze in the autistic group (Greene et al. 2019), and the other found a reduced effect of predictability on response time in autistic individuals (Thillay et al. 2016). The latter also explored a range of EEG correlates of motor preparation, and found differences in the effect of predictability of an upcoming target stimulus on two markers of preparation for a likely motor response: the anticipatory ramp of voltage at central and parietal electrodes called the contingent negative variation (CNV), and the anticipatory desynchronization of mu (~10 Hz sensorimotor) oscillations. However, this study included only 12 participants in each group, leaving ample room for further exploration of the impact of predictive cue probability on response time and EEG correlates of motor preparation in autism. A recent preprint also supports reduced effect of probabilistic target predictability on response time in autism, accompanied by a reduced effect of target predictability on CNV (Vanneau et al. 2026), though in this study they did not inform participants about shifts in target probability.

Since the publication of this group's systematic review (Cannon et al. 2021), a new line of research on predictive modulation of motor‐related behavior and neural activity has focused on the predictability induced by steady (“isochronous”) rhythm. One set of EEG studies of age‐ and IQ‐matched autistic and non‐autistic children (Beker et al. 2021; Beker, Kadosh, et al. 2025) had participants respond to auditory targets whose timing was cued with isochronous visual sequences. In the initial study, the autistic group's EEG showed less oscillation phase alignment (“entrainment”) to the isochronous cues than the non‐autistic group's; further, in the autistic group, the ramping potentials measured at frontal sites did not seem to grow steeper from one visual cue to the next as they did in the NA group, suggesting a lack of mounting “anticipation” as the likely target drew nearer (Beker et al. 2021). A subsequent preprint with a similar task found oculomotor differences in the autistic group, including reduced preparatory oculomotor suppression and reduced pupillary entrainment that paralleled the neural differences (Beker, Kadosh, et al. 2025). Another study in adults found that the autistic group showed reduced inter‐trial EEG phase coherence and reduced influence of cue rhythmicity on phase coherence during a rhythmic visually‐cued visual detection task (Beker, Vanneau, et al. 2025).

Several additional recent studies from our lab have also found behavioral group differences related to processing ongoing rhythm. In a threshold auditory target detection task with timing cues based either on learned durations or ongoing rhythm, the latter induced a substantial increase in false‐positive target responses in the autistic group but not the NA group (Cannon et al. 2023). And in a timing judgment task, the autistic group showed reduced precision in reporting whether a tone in an otherwise isochronous sequence was early or late (Cannon et al. 2024). Since perceptual entrainment to rhythm is supported by the motor system and closely related to spontaneous rhythmic movement (Patel and Iversen 2014; Cannon and Patel 2021; Merchant et al. 2015), these group differences may be closely related to differences in spontaneous anticipatory motor activity.

To explore possible autism‐related differences in the neural and behavioral manifestations of predictive motor preparation and entrainment to rhythmic sequences, we designed a task in which we could measure both response time (RT) and EEG evidence of motor preparation in the context of ongoing rhythm. We based the task on an auditory speeded response task previously run with Parkinsonian patients (Te Woerd et al. 2017) that combined rhythmic auditory stimulus presentation with probabilistic cueing of auditory targets (Figure 1). Presenting stimuli in a steady rhythm allows for precise prediction of possible target times; probabilistic cueing of targets creates differential degrees of target predictability based on instructed associations between cue pitch height and probability of a target on the next beat. Although previous work used visual cues (Thillay et al. 2016; Beker et al. 2021; Vanneau et al. 2026; Beker, Kadosh, et al. 2025; Beker, Vanneau, et al. 2025), we chose auditory cues (pitched beeps) and targets (woodblock taps) because rhythmic auditory sequences draw on beat perception, a motor‐associated perceptual phenomenon (Cannon and Patel 2021) that gives rise to a stronger desire to move (Repp and Penel 2004) and more precise predictions in time (Di Luca and Rhodes 2016). Where previous work used cue sequences to create target predictability, presenting targets with either a fixed probability at the end of each sequence (Thillay et al. 2016; Beker et al. 2021; Beker, Vanneau, et al. 2025; Beker, Kadosh, et al. 2025), we used multiple probabilistic cue/target dependencies: low, medium, and high pitches were followed by targets with probabilities 10%, 30%, and 50%, respectively. This allowed us to test multiple levels of target predictability. Importantly, like Thillay et al. (2016) and Greene et al. (2019), and unlike a recent preprint that also explored effects of varying target probability on CNV (Vanneau et al. 2026), we informed participants of the relationships between cues and target probability prior to the task and reminded them of the relationship before every task block, thus better isolating prediction‐related motor preparation without the complicating factor of learning predictive associations through exposure.

Illustration of predictive tone paradigm. Participants responded as quickly as they could to “target sounds” (yellow circles) which were embedded in a 1 Hz sequence of three different tones (sound symbols). Three distinct cue tones signaled the probability of being followed by a target sound: low tones (red) signaled low (10%) probability, medium tones (blue) signaled intermediate (30%) probability, and high tones (green) signaled high (50%) probability.

In our pre‐registration (available online at https://osf.io/uwt5z), we hypothesized that the autistic group would show less of a response time benefit from high target predictability than the non‐autistic group. We further hypothesized that the CNV and anticipatory mu desynchronization, both EEG measures of sensorimotor anticipation (Alegre et al. 2003; van Ede et al. 2012), would be less affected by target prediction in the ASD group than in the NA group. In addition to our preregistered comparisons, we looked at frontocentral beta (13–30 Hz) oscillations, also thought to index motor preparation (Alegre et al. 2003), and compared the consistency of neural activity entrained to the periodic sequence to investigate possible differences in rhythm‐based entrainment and prediction.

NA participants were recruited from online networks such as social media ads and local recruitment outlets. Autistic participants were recruited from the same sources, as well as from a research database of autistic individuals maintained by the Department of Brain and Cognitive Sciences at Massachusetts Institute of Technology. All participants were native English speakers, had normal or corrected vision and hearing, were not color blind, were born full term (> 37 weeks gestation), and had no history of head trauma with loss of consciousness. NA participants were screened for autism, mood disorders, attention‐deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and antipsychotic and ADHD medications. All participants in the ASD group had received a clinical diagnosis of autism, and also met the cutoff criteria for ‘autism spectrum’ or ‘autism’ on the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule‐Revised (ADOS‐2) Module 4, using either the original or the revised algorithm (Lord et al. 2000, 2006; Hus and Lord 2014). The ADOS‐2 was administered by a research‐reliable member of the research team. The overall study design involved: (1) completing an in‐person behavioral characterization session, (2) completing an online screening session, and (3) an hour‐long in‐person EEG session that included two listening tasks and a speeded response task lasting about 20 min (not including breaks). Only the speeded response task, which we will refer to as the “predictive tone paradigm,” is reported here. The online screening session included questionnaires about the participant's health history, the autism quotient (AQ) test to quantify autistic traits, and a rhythmic musical sophistication test with questions adapted from the Goldsmiths MSI (Müllensiefen et al. 2014) and Ollen MSI (Ollen 2006) to assess participants' rhythm perception and understanding capabilities (see Table 1). This screening session was completed on the online research platform Qualtrics.

Ethics Statement

Our study was approved by the MIT Committee on the Use of Humans as Experimental Subjects (Internal Review Board), in accordance with the ethical standards in the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants gave informed consent. They were able to cease participation at any time and were compensated for the portion of the study that they completed. Compensation was not linked to performance.

In‐Person Behavioral Characterization

Once participants' eligibility was confirmed, they were invited for an in‐person visit at MIT. Participants completed an in‐person behavioral characterization session immediately following their EEG session. The behavioral session included the List Sorting Working Memory test, the Flanker Inhibitory Control and Attention test, and a vocabulary size test, all from the NIH Toolbox (http://www.nihtoolbox.org). Participants also completed the Kaufman Brief Intelligence Test, Second Edition (KBIT‐2) Matrices (a nonverbal intelligence scale) (Kaufman 2004), the Social Responsiveness Scale, Second Edition (SRS‐2, a self‐report measure of autism traits) (Constantino 2012), a music experience survey adapted from the Goldsmith Musical Sophistication Index (Müllensiefen et al. 2014) and the Ollen Musical Sophistication Index (Ollen 2006) (details available in preregistration at https://osf.io/uwt5z), and the Prediction‐Related Experiences Questionnaire (PRE‐Q) (O'Brien et al. 2024). Average scores are reported in Table 1.

Predictive Tone Paradigm

The predictive tone paradigm (Figure 1) was adapted from Te Woerd et al. (2017). The task consisted of 5 blocks, each comprising a steady stream of interspersed low (900 Hz), middle (1100 Hz), and high (1300 Hz) tones and target sounds (woodblock taps). In what follows, each tone or target will also occasionally be referred to as a “trial” when it improves clarity. Each of the 5 blocks was 150 s long (excluding instructions) and consisted of a stream of 151 events delivered with an inter‐onset interval of 1 s. Each block started with two low tones and then unfolded according to probabilistic rules. Target events occurred with a probability determined by the pitch of the previous event: a target came after a low tone with a probability of 10%, after a middle tone with a probability of 30% and after a high tone with a probability of 50%. Because of their probabilistic association with subsequent targets, we will refer to these tones as “cues” below. Non‐target events were randomly selected to be low tones with a probability of 60% and middle or high tones with a probability 20% for each. These probabilities were selected to ensure an adequate number of targets following low tones while matching the frequency of occurrence of middle and high tones. Over all five blocks, the participants heard 390 low tones, 120 medium tones, 122 high tones, and 123 targets, for a total of 755 events. Due to random chance, the empirical probabilities of targets did not perfectly match the generative probabilities: 40 low tones (10.3%) were followed by targets, 27 medium tones (22.5%) were followed by targets, and 56 high tones (46.9%) were followed by targets.

Participants were informed of the probabilities of hearing a target after each type of tone, and were reminded before the start of each block that “target sounds are more likely to follow higher beeps [50% beep example] than lower beeps [10% beep example]”. They were asked to react to targets as quickly as possible by pressing a button. All participants completed a practice block prior to the task during which they received verbal reminders of the instructions if they were not responding to targets, and all participants were offered breaks between blocks.

The predictive tone task was preceded by three blocks, each 300‐s long and consisting of 280 auditory events; participants were asked to respond as quickly as possible to targets embedded in a stream of identical tones. The first block consisted of low tones, the second of medium tones, and the third of high tones. ERPs to these tones were used to look for evidence of group and tone effects on ERPs outside of a predictive context (Figure S1).

Behavioral Analysis

Response times to targets were grouped by cued target probability (10%, 30%, or 50%, as determined by preceding cue tone) and averaged for each participant. False positive responses were all ignored in the response time calculations. Response times beyond three standard deviations from the group average or above 2000 ms were excluded from analysis. Of note, the response time data of autistic participants after 10% target probability cues were not normally distributed, as determined by the Shapiro–Wilk normality test (Shapiro and Wilk 1965); as a result, the response time data were log‐transformed for all statistical tests.

All included participants completed the task, and response data showed that participants in both groups were alert and on‐task throughout the experiment. Two ASD participants failed to fully depress the button in response to targets for approximately 1 min at the end of a block for unknown reasons; however, a forearm electrode showed movement following the target, suggesting that participants were engaged with the task but failed to fully depress the button. Excluding these misses, there were 29 missed targets and nine response times longer than 1 s in the ASD group; there were 26 missed targets and three response times longer than 1 s in the NA group; and no participant in either group missed more than two targets.

EEG Data Preprocessing

A standard 32‐channel electrode array was used to collect EEG signals at 512 Hz referenced to an average of two mastoid electrodes, with additional vertical and horizontal electrooculogram channels retained during preprocessing. EEG data analysis was performed on MATLAB R2026a with EEGLAB and ERPLAB. Raw event‐marked EEG files were first filtered using a 2–200 Hz bandpass filter. Bad scalp channels were identified using EEGLAB's clean_artifacts procedure, using flatline, channel‐correlation, and line‐noise criteria. These channels were removed before Adaptive Mixture Independent Component Analysis (AMICA) but saved for later interpolation.

AMICA was then run on the cleaned data using the EEGLAB iclabel function to automatically label “brain” and “other” signals and remove the remaining artifacts (e.g., muscle contraction, eye movement, etc.). Previously removed bad channels were then interpolated and the EEG channels were reordered to the original montage. Post‐AMICA data were bandpass filtered from 0.01 to 70 Hz, and cropped to include from 2 s before the start of each block to 2 s after the block ended. The continuous data was then divided to 2‐s epochs from −1000 to +1000 ms around the relevant event for ERP and power analyses, and to 4‐s epochs from −1500 to +2500 ms for the inter‐trial phase coherence (ITPC) analysis. Epochs were baseline corrected using the −1000 to 0 ms epoch baseline and then mean‐corrected using −200 to 0 ms. The 2‐s epochs were rejected only when an artifact exceeded the rejection threshold in one of the a priori analysis channels: Cz, Pz, C3, C4, FC1, or FC2. Artifact rejection used a subject‐specific adaptive threshold, calculated as 6 × 1.4826 × MAD, where MAD is the median absolute deviation across all channels. The threshold was bounded between 100 and 500 μV and symmetric around zero (Beker et al. 2021). This led to an average epoch rejection rate of 3.6%, with a maximum of 36% for a single participant. For the 4‐s epochs, the same −1000 to 0 ms epoch baseline and −200 to 0 ms mean‐correction window was used; in contrast to the 2 s epochs, all 32 channels were evaluated, bad channels within individual epochs were interpolated using spherical interpolation, and epochs were rejected if they had more than 6 bad channels. This led to an average epoch rejection rate of 6.1%, with a maximum of 38% for a single participant.

Event‐Related Potential Analysis

We were interested in the “late CNV”, a ramping potential at central and parietal sites peaking at the time of an anticipated stimulus and thought to indicate anticipatory motor preparation (Gaillard 1977). We therefore investigated event‐related potentials (ERPs) from the Cz and Pz electrodes. ERPs in response to cues signaling 10%, 30%, and 50% target probability were calculated using the EEGLAB pop_averager function on the 2 s epochs for both groups. Late CNVs were quantified by finding the slope of a regression line fit to the ERP in the 500–1000 ms range for each participant in each cue condition (“CNV slope”). A slope analysis was preferred as it is independent of baseline voltage, which may vary due to the identity of preceding cues and due to baseline drift not fully removed by the 0.01 Hz high pass filter (Vanhoutte et al. 2015). Late CNV slopes were calculated for the trial‐averaged ERPs for each participant, and also for each trial (see Trial‐By‐Trial Analysis below).

Time‐Frequency Analysis

EEGLAB function newtimef was utilized to calculate event‐related spectral power using 2‐s epochs time‐locked to each type of predictive cue from −1000 to +1000 ms around cue onset. The time‐frequency decomposition was performed using a one‐cycle Morlet wavelet transform (EEGlab's ‘newtimef’), with logarithmic frequency spacing, no internal baseline subtraction and no trial baseline correction. Multitaper decomposition was not used. Mu power traces were calculated by summing absolute power over the 8–14 Hz range and averaging over a preregistered central ROI consisting of electrodes C3, C4 and Cz (McFarland et al. 2004). To avoid drawing on post‐target power, mu power was only calculated in the range [−930 ms, +930 ms]. Beta power traces were calculated by summing absolute power over the 12–30 Hz range and averaging over a preregistered ROI consisting of FC1 and FC2 (though no hypothesis was preregistered for beta). To avoid drawing on post‐target power, beta power was only calculated in the range [−960 ms, +960 ms]. For each participant, electrode, frequency band, the power for each retained trial was first calculated. Spectral power was trial‐averaged by cue probability, and also retained at a single‐trial level for trial‐by‐trial analysis (see Trial‐By‐Trial Analysis below). For the trial‐averaged data, power was summed across frequencies within the relevant band at each time point. The resulting frequency‐summed power traces were then averaged across the electrodes in the corresponding ROI, and normalized by dividing by the participant's mean power in the same band and electrodes over the whole task. For visualization, these normalized power traces were averaged over participants and displayed from +500 ms to the end of the calculated epoch (+930 ms or +960 ms). For statistical comparison across cue probabilities and groups, the same normalized power traces were summed over the same time range and log transformed.

Statistical Testing

Response times and EEG measures of prediction and motor preparation were first analyzed with linear mixed‐effects models (MATLAB, 'fitlme'), with preceding cue type, diagnostic group, and their interaction as fixed categorical effects. To account for repeated measures, we included random intercepts and random slopes of cue for each participant (i.e., (probability|SubjectID)), allowing both baseline values and effects of cue to vary across participants. Statistical significance of the fixed effects was initially evaluated using F‐tests (Type III Analysis of Variance/ANOVA tests) with Satterthwaite's approximation for degrees of freedom.

Following F‐tests, we used t‐tests to test the significance of specific comparisons of interest. For main effects of target probability on a measure, we compared 10% to 50% cued target probability, which represented the largest available difference in predictability, and 30% to 50% cued target probability, which represented a pair of conditions occurring equally often in the task. When we made these pairs of comparisons, we evaluated significance for these pairs of contrasts using the Holm‐Bonferroni method: one contrast was considered significant if p < 0.025, and the second was additionally considered significant if p < 0.05.

Our main contrasts of interest were “differences of differences”: group differences in the within‐subject effects of higher relative to lower target predictability on measures of interest, which we called the “predictive boost” in those measures. We looked at the predictive boost from 10% to 50% cued target probability (a comparison representing the maximum difference in cued probability) and at the predictive boost from 30% to 50% cue target probability (a comparison between two cue types occurring with equal incidence rate over the course of the auditory sequence), again applying the Holm‐Bonferroni method. Cohen's d effect sizes were calculated for these comparisons based on t‐score and Satterthwaite degrees of freedom (df ≈ 42) from the mixed model. The same predictive boost difference scores were used in correlational analyses in the supplemental analyses.

A post hoc sensitivity analysis using ɑ = 0.05 and Satterthwaite degrees of freedom from the mixed model (df ≈ 42) showed that our study was powered to detect predictive boost effects of magnitude d>0.39 in one‐tailed tests or d>0.43 in two‐tailed tests (both “small to medium”) with 80% reliability. We also performed sensitivity analyses for the linear mixed‐effects models, approximating the sensitivity by treating the tests as repeated‐measures ANOVAs. A between‐subjects sensitivity estimate indicated that our study was powered to detect main effects of group of approximately f = 0.44 (80% power, ɑ = 0.05). Power to detect interactions was calculated with a repeated‐measures ANOVA approximation; this power varied with the correlation structure of repeated observations (see Table 2).

Correlations

As a supplemental analysis, we calculated correlations between cognitive assessment scores and the RT and CNV measures (on Pz, the electrode showing the most pronounced group differences and interactions), both in the form of raw measures and “predictive boost” difference scores, and between RT and CNV measures. Correlations were calculated within each group and were FDR‐corrected within each group and each family of tests (RT/CNV, cognitive/RT, cognitive/CNV). Note that correlation over the whole population would be problematic: several measures differ significantly between groups, so whole‐population correlations could reflect group differences.

Trial‐By‐Trial Analysis

As a supplemental analysis, we fit trial‐by‐trial CNV slope following cues to a mixed linear model that introduced predictors that seemed likely to affect the degree of preparatory motor activity: predictors were group, cue, group/probability interaction, age, event preceding the cue (one of the three cues or a target), and trial number (representing progress through the task), with random intercepts and slopes by subject for cue and preceding event. We fit another mixed model with the same predictors to predict trial‐by‐trial log RT for all target trials.

Inter‐Trial Coherence

As an exploratory measure of neural entrainment, we quantified inter‐trial phase coherence (ITPC) at 1 Hz at each electrode. For this we used the subset of 4‐s epochs consisting of three consecutive non‐target cue tones. This analysis was motivated by a similar analysis by Beker et al. (2021).

Group differences in ITPC across electrodes were evaluated with a nonparametric cluster‐based permutation test implemented in FieldTrip (Maris and Oostenveld 2007). Spatial adjacency of electrodes was defined based on the distance between Cartesian electrode coordinates in the standard 32‐channel grid (< 65 mm). Independent‐samples t‐statistics were computed at each electrode, and clusters were formed from spatially adjacent electrodes exceeding a two‐tailed threshold of p < 0.05. Cluster significance was evaluated with 10,000 permutations, with the maximal cluster statistic used to control for multiple comparisons. Clusters were considered significant at a two‐tailed threshold of p < 0.05.

Artificial Intelligence Usage

Microsoft Copilot was consulted in the development of appropriate statistical approaches to the data. AI‐assisted tools were used for code generation only in the process of reorganizing analysis scripts into functions, after which the scripts were manually reviewed and all analyses were rerun to make sure that the results were unchanged.

Results

Behavior

We found behavioral evidence supporting hypothesized group differences in the effect of target predictability on response time. A linear mixed‐effects model with group and cue as fixed effects (see Methods) showed a significant effect of cue on response time (F(2,42) = 23.2, p < 0.001, 𝜂p 2 = 0.52, f = 1.05) and an interaction between cue and group (F(2,42) = 3.55, p = 0.04, 𝜂p 2 = 0.14, f = 0.41), with no main effect of group (F(1,42) = 0.14, p > 0.05, 𝜂p 2 = 0.003, f = 0.06). Sensitivity analysis indicated that the study was powered to detect group effects at f = 0.44 and interactions at f = 0.11 (Table 2). At Pz, our primary statistical analysis found compelling evidence for our hypothesis of reduced predictive boost of the late CNV in the ASD group; at Cz, weaker but significant evidence for the same effect was present in one‐tailed tests. A supplemental trial‐by‐trial analysis found compelling evidence for our hypothesis at both electrodes and additionally found evidence that CNV slopes were shallower overall in the ASD group.

We calculated correlations between CNV measures on Pz (slopes and predictive boosts of slope) and both cognitive measures and response time measures (response times and predictive boosts of response time) within each group (Tables S6 and S7). We also found strong, significant Pearson correlations between multiple CNV measures of anticipation and multiple RT measures of anticipation within the NA group (corresponding correlations were large but not significant in the ASD group), indicating that CNV slope induced by the anticipation of a target is indeed a neural correlate of response time to predictable targets, at least across individuals.

Time‐Frequency

A linear mixed effects model showed a significant effect of cue on anticipatory beta power (13–30 Hz, electrodes FC1 and FC2, 500–1000 ms post‐cue), F(2,42) = 3.78, p = 0.03, 𝜂p 2 = 0.15, f = 0.42, but not mu power (8–14 Hz, electrodes C3, C4, and Cz, 500–1000 ms post‐cue), F(2,42) = 1.59, p > 0.05, 𝜂p 2 = 0.07, f = 0.28. The main effect of group was not significant for either beta, F(1,42) = 0.050, p > 0.05, 𝜂p 2 = 0.001, f = 0.03, or mu, F(1,42) = 0.00, p > 0.05, 𝜂p 2 = 0.00, f = 0.00. However, we found significant interactions of group and target probability for beta power, F(2,42) = 3.45, p = 0.04, 𝜂p 2 = 0.014, f = 0.41, and a marginally significant interaction for mu power, F(2,42) = 3.01, p = 0.06, 𝜂p 2 = 0.13, f = 0.38. Sensitivity analysis indicated that the study was powered to detect group effects at f = 0.44 and interactions at f = 0.28 (Table 2). Visual inspection of power traces (Figure 4, panels A to D) and time‐averaged power (Figure 4, panels E and F) indicated that the critical group difference in power was when targets were predicted with medium probability. In the NA group, oscillatory power in both frequency bands was low following 30% cues, comparable to the power following 50% cues, indicating that they were spontaneously preparing for targets even after medium probability cues; by contrast, in the ASD group, power was high following 30%, comparable to the power following 10% cues, indicating that they were spontaneously preparing like the NA group after high probability cues but less than the NA group after medium probability cues. Indeed, pairwise tests showed minimal group differences in anticipatory power between 50% and 10% target probability, but substantial group differences in anticipatory power between 50% and 30% target probability. These differences were opposite the direction we had preregistered for mu power: we expected smaller differences between greater and lesser predictable conditions in the ASD group, but instead we saw more pronounced 50%–30% differences in the ASD group. Since the beta band showed a significant interaction between group and cued probability and we had not preregistered a direction for this comparison, we performed the pairwise tests and observed a nonsignificant group difference in 50%–10% power difference, t(42) = −0.71, p > 0.05, d = −0.11, 95% CI = [−0.42, 0.20], but a significant group difference in 50% – 30% power difference, t(42) = −2.51, p = 0.02, d = −0.39, 95% CI = [−0.70, −0.08], with a larger difference in mu power between 50% and 30% cues in the ASD group. Sensitivity analysis indicated power to detect pairwise interaction effects at d = 0.43.

Inter‐Trial Coherence

As a measure of neural entrainment to the periodic 1 Hz rhythm of the cues and targets, we quantified inter‐trial phase coherence (ITPC) at 1 Hz at each electrode for epochs of three consecutive non‐target cue tones, allowing us to look only at periods of rhythmic stimulus listening uninterrupted by motor responses. In a similar analysis by Beker et al. (2021), the autistic group showed reduced ITPC at the rate of their rhythmic visual stimuli at a cluster of posterior and a cluster of central sites. Across all electrodes, we observed a general shift of peak entrainment from right‐central sites in the NA group toward frontal sites in the ASD group (Figure 5A,B). We found a cluster of where the ASD group showed significantly less inter‐trial coherence than the NA group (8 electrodes, cluster mass = 23.4, p = 0.015) at posterior sites O1, Oz, O2, PO3, PO4, P3, Pz, P4, and CP2.

Neural entrainment, quantified as inter‐trial phase coherence (ITPC) at stimulus frequency (1 Hz), plotted across scalp topography. The ASD group (A) showed an overall shift in the locus of maximal entrainment toward frontal sites relative to the NA group (B), with significantly reduced entrainment at a cluster of posterior sites (highlighted with black dots on a difference plot in panel C).

Discussion

We designed this probabilistic auditory cueing experiment in order to explore proposed challenges of autistic people in spontaneously mobilizing motor preparatory processes based on known predictive relationships, as hypothesized in Cannon et al. (2021) and supported by additional observations from the literature (Brisson et al. 2012; Cattaneo et al. 2007; Thillay et al. 2016). Importantly, all participants were explicitly informed of the correspondence between high‐pitched cues and high target probability before every block, with the intent of focusing on predictive preparation rather than learning of prediction relationships.

We hypothesized that autistic participants would receive less of a response time advantage from cues that they knew signaled a target with high probability, relative to cues rarely followed by targets. Our response time measurements decisively supported this hypothesis. Although the ASD group showed slower response times than the NA group across target probabilities, this difference was within the margin of error for low target probabilities and grew significantly larger for higher target probabilities, indicating that the ASD group was not effectively utilizing the predictive value of the cue.

The clearest neural signatures reflecting this difference in response time following the more predictive cues were in the CNV, a negative‐going voltage ramp in anticipation of upcoming stimuli, especially those that demand a response (Walter et al. 1964). Greater CNV amplitude has been hypothesized to indicate greater preparatory disinhibition of sensorimotor circuits (Kononowicz and Penney 2016). The amplitude of the “late CNV”, a voltage ramp at central and parietal midline electrodes that peaks at the time of an anticipated stimulus, was of particular interest to us because it is specifically thought to indicate the degree of anticipatory “motor preparation” (Gaillard 1977).

Our measure of the late CNV was the slope of the ERP in the 500 ms before the anticipated event on electrodes Cz and Pz. We saw a clear interaction of comparison group and cued target probability at Pz, a weaker interaction on Cz, and an effect of group at both electrodes, all clearly visible in the grand averages (Figure 3). The interactions supported our hypothesis of reduced difference between higher‐probability and lower‐probability CNVs in the ASD group, consistent with previous results (Thillay et al. 2016; Vanneau et al. 2026). This effect paralleled the group difference in the benefit of predictability for response time, and indicated less flexibility in modulating the degree of motor preparation as a function of target probability.

CNV slopes in the ASD group were significantly shallower overall, indicating an overall reduction in timely motor preparation during the task. Our result is consistent with previous observations of reduced predictive CNVs in autistic groups, both for motor‐imperative (Thillay et al. 2016) and non‐imperative (Beker et al. 2021) rhythmically paced stimuli. As a complementary analysis, we looked at inter‐trial phase coherence (ITPC) at the stimulus frequency, a measure of neural entrainment to the rhythm of the cues. We found weaker entrainment in the ASD group on a cluster of posterior electrodes, a region consistent with our large group differences in CNV at posterior sites and with a previous observation of ASD/NA group differences in ITPC at posterior sites (Beker et al. 2021) (though in this previous work the posterior results were thought to be related to the visual cueing paradigm). This subset of our results suggested that posterior brain regions in the ASD group were not as attuned to the metronomic predictability of the sequence to the same degree as those in the NA group. This lack of metronomic prediction may relate to the slower RTs in the ASD group (though the main effect of group on RT did not reach the level of statistical significance). The ITPC measure used here does not purely reflect entrainment of endogenous rhythms: it can also be reduced by greater variability in ERP latency, which has indeed been observed in autistic children relative to NA children (Kovarski et al. 2019; Milne 2011). But greater ERP latency jitter would likely correspond with a reduction in the perceived regularity of the stimulus (consistent with the observation of weaker anisochrony detection in autistic adults (Cannon et al. 2024)), so even a latency jitter account of group differences in ITPC could indicate difficulty utilizing isochronous rhythm to make precisely timed predictions.

Desynchronization of beta and mu power in preparation for likely motor‐imperative targets is thought to serve as another indicator of motor preparation (Tzagarakis et al. 2015; Bidet‐Caulet et al. 2012), with mu desynchronization specifically indexing motor cortex excitability (Krol et al. 2020; Matsumoto et al. 2010). The pattern of mu and beta power we saw following predictive cues is not easy to interpret. Our preregistered hypothesis of predictive differences in mu desynchronization was not supported, and indeed the 50%–30% difference in the ASD group seemed to be more pronounced than in the NA group in both bands. Overall, the main contrast between the groups seemed to be weaker desynchronization in the ASD group after 30% cues, especially in the beta band (Figure 4), though our statistical testing plan was not designed for this specific comparison. Reduced mu desynchronization in the ASD group for 50% relative to 10% targets is at odds with previous results showing less anticipatory desynchronization on central electrodes in the same or a similar power band (Thillay et al. 2016; Martineau et al. 2004), though overall weaker desynchronization in the ASD group following 30% cues bears some similarity to those results. This pattern is broadly consistent with the hypothesis in (Sinha et al. 2014) that autistic individuals may utilize strong predictive relationships but are less attuned to weak predictive relationships than non‐autistic individuals—perhaps 30% cued target probability was a strong enough prediction to induce desynchronization in the NA group but not in the ASD group. However, since we did not see the same pattern in the CNV results, this interpretation remains speculative. Our oscillation results provide a new, ambiguous datapoint in the ongoing exploration of possible mu desynchronization differences in autism (Lockhart et al. 2024).

There exist several possible alternative explanations for the many differences in the effect of predictability on response time and anticipatory neural activity. First, the ASD group may have differently interpreted or retained the explicit auditory instructions about target probability. Since we did not test for comprehension of cue pitch predictability after the experiment, we cannot rule out the possibility that autistic participants did not fully understand the relationships; they were, however, given the opportunity to ask clarifying questions. Participants were verbally reminded that higher pitches signaled higher target probability before each block of the experiment, but they might have had difficulty processing the verbal reminders or retaining that information through each block; there might also have been group differences in how participants interpreted “higher probability,” or whether they were inclined to consciously utilize these relationships. However, such differences would not account for differences in entrainment or overall CNV slope.

Second, the results might have been driven by the presence of ADHD in the ASD cohort. Given the common co‐occurrence of ADHD and autism, it is possible that the ASD group may not have attended as much to the predictive value of the tones, to their metronomic predictability, or to the task overall. Indeed, the autistic group did have lower average scores on the NIH Flanker task (a measure of inhibitory control and attention) than the non‐autistic participants. However, within each diagnostic group, these scores did not correlate with participants' response times, CNV slopes, or predictive boosts in either measure (see Supporting Information), suggesting that more generalized reduced attention was not driving the group differences. Alternatively, individuals with ASD and ADHD may be more irregular in their motor preparation than individuals with ASD but without ADHD, as suggested by a recent study (Migó et al. 2022). A preliminary look at the data excluding ADHD participants suggests that this was not the case, but future work with a similar task including groups with and without ADHD would be necessary to determine this with confidence.

Third, the ASD group may have been prioritizing accuracy over speed, as suggested by a recent study (Hann et al. 2025). Since participants were near the ceiling for accurately responding to targets, this would be difficult to detect, and also difficult to conceptually and experimentally separate from our proposed “predictive impairment.” A fourth possible explanation is that the ASD group was less able to recognize or attend to differences in cue pitch; however, the pitches were well separated, the ASD group had a higher mean level of self‐reported musicianship, and differences in pitch perception would not account for differences in entrainment or overall CNV slope. Finally, the CNV group differences are consistent with the possibility of an overall weaker EEG signal in the ASD group; however, the similarity of the two groups' average N100 amplitudes to tones during this task and during an uncued control task (Figure S1) suggests that weaker EEG signal is not causing the results.

Overall, our findings indicate a trend toward reduced motor preparatory benefit from predictability in autism as measured by response time and CNV slope. They further indicate noteworthy but ambiguous NA/ASD group differences in anticipatory motor‐related oscillatory desynchronization and less neural entrainment to rhythm, a key element of predictive engagement with the world that is closely tied to the motor system in autism.

Our findings should be interpreted with caution given the present study's small sample size and limited power relative to its effect sizes, the novel auditory prediction paradigm, and the lack of statistically significant within‐group correlations between behavioral and EEG measures and autism‐related cognitive assessment scores (AQ, ADOS‐2, SRS). However, they build on a growing collection of evidence supporting motor preparation differences in autism (Cannon et al. 2021). As this hypothesis accumulates more support, we suggest that it be considered as a possible contributor to the motor impairments commonly observed in autism (Fournier et al. 2010; Gowen and Hamilton 2013) and that it be incorporated into the growing collection of non‐social autistic traits that may underpin social differences, e.g., in conversational turn‐taking (Nguyen et al. 2022; Ochi et al. 2019; Wehrle et al. 2023; see Bolis and Schilbach 2018 for a discussion of similar ideas). Efforts should be made to connect differences in predictive motor processes to differences in the lived experiences of people with autism and to leverage them toward the development of services and supports for this community.
