Efficient Quantum Measurements: Computational Max- and Measured Rényi Divergences and Applications
Álvaro Yángüez, Thomas A. Hahn, Jan Kochanowski

Abstract
Quantum information processing is limited, in practice, to efficiently implementable operations. This motivates the study of quantum divergences that preserve their operational meaning while faithfully capturing these computational constraints. Using geometric, computational, and information theoretic tools, we define two new types of computational divergences, which we term computational max-divergence and computational measured Rényi divergences . Both are constrained by a family of efficient binary measurements, and thus useful for state discrimination tasks in the computational setting. We prove that, in the infinite-order limit, the computational measured Rényi divergence coincides with the computational max-divergence, mirroring the corresponding relation in the unconstrained information-theoretic setting. For the many-copy regime, we introduce regularized versions and establish a one-sided computational Stein bound on achievable hypothesis-testing exponents under efficient measurements, giving the regularized computational measured relative entropy an operational meaning. We further define resource measures induced by our computational divergences and prove an asymptotic continuity bound for the computational measured relative entropy of resource. Focusing on entanglement, we relate our results to previously proposed computational entanglement measures and provide explicit separations from the information-theoretic setting. Together, these results provide a principled, cohesive approach towards state discrimination tasks and resource quantification under computational constraints.

Relation to prior and concurrent work.

Our work sits at the intersection between measured Rényi divergences which consider restricted sets of measurements and complexity–aware information theory. With regard to the first point,
[
RSB24
,
MH23
]
derive many properties for measured Rényi divergences. In particular,
[
RSB24
]

introduces
locally–measured Rényi divergences
, and optimizes classical Rényi divergences over locality–constrained POVMs. They additionally derive variational formulae that are directly related to the conic formulations which appear when defining max-divergences in a conic framework, and calculate
Stein/strong–converse exponents for symmetric data–hiding families. We essentially replace the locality constraints on the measurements with
efficiency
constraints. So while the measurements that are considered differ, many operational implications remain the same. Similarly,
[
BaHLP14
]
prove a quantum Stein’s lemma for several classes of
restricted
measurements via an adversarial reduction; our “computational Stein’s lemma” is a converse bound in the poly–copy, efficient–measurement regime and incorporates complexity constraints rather than locality/separability constraints.

For the computational max-divergence, our cone-geometric approach is inspired by
[
RKW11
,
GC24
,
RSB24
]
. Here, they incorporate locality (or other entanglement-related constraints) restrictions on the measurements or states directly into the underlying conic structure. Similar to our approach for the computational measured Rényi divergences, we apply this framework to the computational setting.

As mentioned above, our computational divergences are close in spirit to the complexity entropy and
complexity relative entropy introduced in
[
YHKH+22
,
MKH+25
]
. Both our work and theirs consider efficient binary measurements and both can be related to efficient hypothesis testing, which is one of the applications we consider in this work. The main difference of course is that while they wanted to define a computational hypothesis-testing relative entropy, our goal is to define computational versions of the max-divergence and measured Rényi divergences, whose information-theoretic (unrestricted) counterparts have proven to be instrumental and found a plethora of applications in information theory
[
Tom16
,
RSB24
]
. We expect this to extend to the computational setting as well.

Since we study a slightly different setting, it is not immediately clear if and how our computational max-divergence can be related to the quantum computational entropies defined in
[
CCL+17
,
AA25
,
AHRA25
]
. In particular, a direct comparison to the quantum unpredictability entropy
[
AA25
]
and computational min-entropy
[
AHRA25
]
is hard to make. They require multi-output measurements that are constrained to act solely on quantum registers to which an adversary has access to, whereas we consider binary measurements that can act on the entire state. Nevertheless, we believe it should be possible to slightly extend or adapt our definitions to incorporate these measurement constraints.

2.1
Notation

In this work we assume all Hilbert spaces to be finite dimensional. Hilbert spaces will be denoted by
\mathcal{H}
. Given a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, we denote the set of positive semidefinite operators acting on
\mathcal{H}
by
\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{H})
. An operator
\rho\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{H})
is called a
quantum state
if we have
\Tr\left[\rho\right]=1
. The set of quantum states on
\mathcal{H}
is denoted by
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
. The set of bounded operators on
\mathcal{H}
is given by
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and the corresponding (sub)set of Hermitian operators is denoted by
\mathcal{B}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{H})
. The Hilbert-Schmidt inner product on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is given by
\langle a,b\rangle\coloneqq\Tr[a^{*}b]
, where
∗
denotes the Hilbert-Schmidt adjoint. We will also denote the adjoint of a quantum channel
\Phi:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2})
by
\Phi^{*}:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
.
For two operators
\rho,\sigma\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{H})
we write
\rho\ll\sigma
if
\ker{\sigma}\subseteq\ker{\rho}
, where
\ker{\tau}\coloneqq\{\ket{v}\,:\,\tau\ket{v}=0\}
. Further, we say the state
\rho
is orthogonal to the state
\sigma
, denoted by
\rho\perp\sigma
, if
\Tr\left[\rho\sigma\right]=0
.

In Landau notation, given two functions
f(n)
and
g(n)
, we write
f(n)=o(g(n))
if

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}f(n)/g(n)=0
. In the same way,
f(n)=\omega(g(n))
if
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}f(n)/g(n)=\infty
.
f(n)=O(g(n))
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}f(n)/g(n)\leq C
. Similarly,
f(n)=\Omega(g(n))
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}f(n)/g(n)\geq C
. Lastly,
f(n)=\Theta(g(n))
if both
f(n)=O(g(n))
and
f(n)=\Omega(g(n))
. A function
f(n)
is
\mathsf{negl}(n)
, i.e., negligible, if, for every fixed
c
,
f(n)=o(1/n^{c})
.

With
\log
we denote the logarithm to base 2 and with
\ln
the logarithm to base
e
.

Information-Theoretic Concepts

Definition 2.1

(Sandwiched Rényi Divergence
[
MLDS+13
,
WWY14
]
)
.

Given any two positive semidefinite operators
\rho,\sigma\in\text{Pos}(\mathcal{H})

\operatorname{Tr}\left[\rho\right]>0

{\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty)}

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\frac{\norm{\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\rho\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}}_{\alpha}^{\alpha}}{\Tr\left[\rho\right]}&\text{ if }\left(\alpha<1\land\rho\not\perp\sigma\right)\vee\rho\ll\sigma\\
+\infty&\text{else}\end{cases}\;.

(2.1)

In this work, the divergence of interest is the sandwiched Rényi divergence. For
\alpha\geq\frac{1}{2}
, it satisfies the data-processing inequality (DPI) and, for
0\leq\alpha\leq\beta
, it is monotonic in
\alpha
, i.e.
{D_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)\leq D_{\beta}(\rho\|\sigma)}
. Moreover, we use
Q_{\alpha}
to denote

\displaystyle Q_{\alpha}\coloneqq\norm{\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\rho\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}}_{\alpha}^{\alpha}\;.

(2.2)

Definition 2.2

(Relative Entropy & Max-divergence
[
Dat09b
]
)
.

Given any two positive semidefinite operators
\rho,\sigma\in\text{Pos}(\mathcal{H})
with
\operatorname{Tr}\left[\rho\right]>0
and
\rho\ll\sigma
, the (Umegaki)
relative entropy
and

between
\rho
,
\sigma
are, respectively, given by:

\displaystyle D(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\Tr[\rho(\log\rho-\log\sigma)]}{\Tr\left[\rho\right]}\,,

(2.3)

\displaystyle D_{\text{max}}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\log\inf\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}|\rho\leq\lambda\sigma\}\;.

(2.4)

Definition 2.3

(Fidelity)
.

Given two quantum states
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
, the
fidelity
between
\rho
,
\sigma
is given by:

\displaystyle F(\rho,\sigma)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\norm{\sqrt{\rho}\sqrt{\sigma}}_{1}^{2}\;.

(2.5)

We are particularly interested in the sandwiched Rényi divergences for
\alpha\in\{\frac{1}{2},1,\infty\}

These three cases are of particular use and one retrieves that

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\to 1}D_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle=D(\rho\|\sigma)\,,

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\nearrow\infty}D_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle=D_{\text{max}}(\rho\|\sigma)\,,

\displaystyle D_{\frac{1}{2}}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle=-\log F(\rho,\sigma)\;.

A closely related divergence is the measured Rényi divergence. To properly define them, we require POVM operators which are defined as follows.

Definition 2.4

(POVM)
.

A
positive operator-valued measure
(POVM) is given by a tuple of positive semidefinite operators
M=(E_{1},\dots,E_{m})
(for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
) such that
{\sum_{k=1}^{m}E_{k}=\mathds{1}}
. Moreover, if
{E_{i}E_{j}=\delta_{ij}E_{i}}
for all

(PVM).
The operators

Equally one can associate to any

M=(E_{1},...,E_{m})

\{0,\dots,m-1\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{M}\,:\;\rho\mapsto\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\Tr[\rho E_{i+1}]\,\outerproduct{i}{i}\,.

(2.6)

We will sometimes drop the subscript

Definition 2.5

(Measured Divergence)
.

Let
\mathbf{M}
be a set of POVMs. Given any two positive semidefinite operators
\rho,\sigma\in\text{Pos}(\mathcal{H})
with
\operatorname{Tr}\left[\rho\right]>0
, for any divergence
\mathbb{D}
, the corresponding
measured divergence
between
\rho
,
\sigma
is given by:

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}^{\mathbf{M}}(\rho\|\sigma)=\sup_{M\in\mathbf{M}}\mathbb{D}(\mathcal{M}_{M}(\rho)\|\mathcal{M}_{M}(\sigma))\,.

(2.7)

We note that the RHS of Eq. (
2.7
) is evaluated on classical states. If
\mathbb{D}=D_{\alpha}
, then we call
D_{\alpha}^{\mathbf{M}}(\rho\|\sigma)
a

, and the RHS reduces to the classical Rényi divergence. As such, it is not strictly necessary to consider the sandwiched Rényi divergence; the Petz-Rényi divergence, see
[
Tom16
]
for a formal definition, would, e.g., also yield the same definition. Nevertheless, it seems somewhat natural to consider the sandwiched Rényi divergence for the following reason. For any
\alpha\geq\frac{1}{2}
and
\rho,\sigma\in\text{Pos}(\mathcal{H})
, if
\mathbf{M}_{n}=\mathbf{M}_{n,\textup{ALL}}
, where

denotes the set of all POVMs that can act on

(or

), then (see, e.g.,
[
MO14
]
)

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}D^{\mathbf{M}_{n}}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\otimes n}\|\sigma^{\otimes n})\;.

This naturally leads to the question of how one generally defines the regularization of measured divergences.

Definition 2.6

(Regularized Measured Divergence
[
RSB24
,
MH23
]
)
.

Let
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}=\left(\mathbf{M}_{1},\mathbf{M}_{2},\dots\right)

\operatorname{Tr}\left[\rho\right]>0
, for any divergence
\mathbb{D}
, the corresponding

between
\rho
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}^{\widetilde{\mathbf{M}},\infty}(\rho\|\sigma)\coloneqq\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{D}^{\mathbf{M}_{n}}(\rho^{\otimes n}\|\sigma^{\otimes n})\;.

(2.8)

If the corresponding measured divergence satisfies super-additivity, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}^{\mathbf{M}_{k+l}}(\rho^{\otimes k+l}\|\sigma^{\otimes k+l})\geq\mathbb{D}^{\mathbf{M}_{k}}(\rho^{\otimes k}\|\sigma^{\otimes k})+\mathbb{D}^{\mathbf{M}_{l}}(\rho^{\otimes l}\|\sigma^{\otimes l})

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}^{\widetilde{\mathbf{M}},\infty}(\rho\|\sigma)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{D}^{\mathbf{M}_{n}}(\rho^{\otimes n}\|\sigma^{\otimes n})=\sup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{D}^{\mathbf{M}_{n}}(\rho^{\otimes n}\|\sigma^{\otimes n})\;.

Definition 2.7

(Convex Cone)
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a finite-dimensional real vector space with inner product

.
A

\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{V}
is a subset s.t.

\displaystyle a,b\in\mathcal{C}\implies\lambda a+\mu b\in\mathcal{C}\quad\forall\lambda,\mu\geq 0\,.

Moreover, for any
K\subset\mathcal{V}
, we denote by
\mathsf{cone}(K)\coloneqq\mathsf{conv}(\bigcup_{\lambda\geq 0}\lambda K)
the smallest (convex) cone that contains
K
.

A cone is called

if it is closed with respect to the norm topology induced by the inner product of
\mathcal{V}
, and, as a consequence, includes its boundary. Following the notation of
[
RKW11
]
we further call a cone

if
\mathcal{C}\cap(-\mathcal{C})=\{0\}
, where the elements of
-\mathcal{C}
are given by

\displaystyle a\in-\mathcal{C}:\hskip-2.84526pt\iff-a\in\mathcal{C}\;.

Similarly, a cone is
solid
if
\mathsf{span}(\mathcal{C})=\mathcal{V}
. In finite-dimensional
\mathcal{V}
this is equivalent to
\mathcal{C}
having a non-empty interior
\text{int}(\mathcal{C})\neq\emptyset
.

Definition 2.8

(Proper Cone)
.

A convex, closed, pointed, and solid cone is called a
proper cone
.

Definition 2.9

(Cone Base)
.

Given some convex cone
\mathcal{C}

and if every
c\in\mathcal{C}\setminus\{0\}
has a unique representation
c=\lambda b
for some
b\in B,\lambda>0
. If
\mathcal{C}
is in addition closed and with non-empty interior, then
B
is the
base of a proper cone
.

One can also assign to each cone a dual cone.

Definition 2.10

(Dual Cone)
.

Given a cone
\mathcal{C}
, its
dual cone

\mathcal{C}^{*}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{*}\coloneqq\{x\in\mathcal{V}^{*}|\langle x,c\rangle\geq 0,\ \forall c\in\mathcal{C}\}\;.

One natural consequence of this definition is that if
\mathcal{C}_{1}\subset\mathcal{C}_{2}
, then
\mathcal{C}^{*}_{2}\subset\mathcal{C}_{1}^{*}
. We additionally note that the dual cone is also closed and convex. Moreover, if
\mathcal{C}
is a proper cone, then so is
\mathcal{C}^{*}
. Lastly, in finite-dimensional vector spaces, closed convex cones are their own bi-dual cones, meaning that the corresponding dual cone is also its pre-dual cone, i.e.
(\mathcal{C}^{*})^{*}=\mathcal{C}
.
Given a real vector space
\mathcal{V}
and a proper cone
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{V}
, one can define a natural partial order on
\mathcal{V}
.

Definition 2.11

(Partial Order)
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a finite-dimensional real vector space and
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{V}
be a proper cone. We say that
a\geq_{\mathcal{C}}b
if and only if
a-b\in\mathcal{C}
.

In the following, we will take
\mathcal{V}
to be a vector space of Hermitian matrices
\mathcal{B}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{H})
, over some Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. This vector space is isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{d^{2}}
, where
d=\dim(\mathcal{H})
.
Over the space of Hermitian matrices, one of the most common examples of (proper) cones in quantum information theory is the cone of
positive semidefinite
operators in
\mathcal{B}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{H})
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{H})\coloneqq\{a\in\mathcal{B}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{H})\,|\,a\geq 0\}\,.

(2.9)

Notably, this cone is self-dual in
\mathcal{V}
and we denote its induced (positive operator) partial order simply by
\geq
. This particular partial order is relevant for the definition of the max-divergence
[
Dat09b
,
RKW11
]
, which satisfies

\displaystyle D_{\text{max}}(a\|b)

\displaystyle=\log\inf\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}|a\leq\lambda b\}

\displaystyle=\log\sup_{v\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{H})}\frac{\langle v,a\rangle}{\langle v,b\rangle}\;.

Remark 2.12
.

Note that in the above optimization one should interpret the
v
as being an effect operator living in the (pre-)dual of cone of
\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{H})
, in which
a,b
live, rather than that cone itself. However, since
\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{H})
is self-dual with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product these cones are the same. This differentiation will become important for arbitrary cones in
Definition

2.13
.

If one considers other proper cones inside of
\mathcal{B}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{H})
, then one can use
this relation between the max-divergence and the conic structure of
\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{H})
to derive a more general notion of max-divergences.

Definition 2.13

(
\mathcal{C}
-Max-divergence
[
RKW11
,
GC24
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a proper cone. For any
a,b\in\mathcal{C}\setminus\{0\}
, the
\mathcal{C}
-max-divergence
between
a,b
is given by

\displaystyle D^{\mathcal{C}}_{\text{max}}(a\|b)\coloneqq\log\inf\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}|a\leq_{\mathcal{C}}\lambda b\}\;.

(2.10)

If no
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
exists such that
\lambda b-a\in\mathcal{C}
, i.e. the infimum is over an empty set, then we set
D^{\mathcal{C}}_{\text{max}}(a\|b)=\infty
.

We note that sometimes the notation
\sup(a/b)
is used instead of
\exp(D_\text{max}(a\|b))
, see e.g.
[
RKW11
]
. Moreover, there exists a dual formulation of the
\mathcal{C}
-max-divergence.

Lemma 2.14

(Dual Expression
[
RKW11
,
GC24
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a proper cone. Then

\displaystyle D^{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{max}}(a\|b)\coloneqq\log\inf\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}|a\leq_{\mathcal{C}}\lambda b\}=\log\sup_{v\in\mathcal{C}^{*}}\frac{\langle v,a\rangle}{\langle v,b\rangle}\;.

(2.11)

With these quantities, one can also additionally define a

Hilbert’s projective metric

[
Bus73a
,
Eve95
]
on a proper cone
\mathcal{C}
as
d^{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{H}}(a,b)\coloneqq D^{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{max}}(a\|b)+D^{\mathcal{C}}_{\text{max}}(b\|a)
.
For the applications we are considering in this work, it will be natural to consider the following setup.
Let
\mathbb{M}\subset\mathcal{V}^{*}
be a closed, convex, and pointed set with a non-empty interior in the dual space, which contains an element
e\in\mathbb{M}
such that

\displaystyle E\in\mathbb{M}\implies e-E\in\mathbb{M}\quad\forall E\in\mathbb{M}\;.

(2.12)

In particular, this ensures that
\mathbb{M}
generates a proper cone
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{M}}^{*}
.

Definition 2.15

(Distinguishability Norm)
.

Let
\mathbb{M}\subset\mathcal{V}^{*}
be a closed, convex, and pointed set with a non-empty interior. Moreover let
\mathbb{M}
contain an element
e\in\mathbb{M}
for which Eq. (
2.12
) holds. For any element
v\in\mathcal{V}
, we denote the
distinguishability norm
of
v
by

\displaystyle\|v\|_{(\mathbb{M})}\coloneqq\sup_{E\in\mathbb{M}}\langle 2E-e,v\rangle\;.

(2.13)

In particular, it is known that the distinguishability norm
defines a norm on
\mathcal{V}

[
RKW11
]
.

Remark 2.16
.

In quantum information theory, this norm is simply the trace norm. Here, one chooses
\mathbb{M}
to be the set of all effect operators, i.e.,
E\in\mathbb{M}
if and only if
0\leq E\leq\mathds{1}
.
The operational interpretation of the trace norm is given by the task of distinguishing two equiprobable quantum states
\rho
and
\sigma
. In this case, one chooses
v=\rho-\sigma
and
e=\mathds{1}
, retrieving

\displaystyle\|\rho-\sigma\|_{(\mathbb{M})}

\displaystyle=2\sup_{E\in\mathbb{M}}\Tr\left[E\left(\rho-\sigma\right)\right]-\Tr\left[\rho-\sigma\right]

\displaystyle=2\sup_{E\in\mathbb{M}}\Tr\left[E\left(\rho-\sigma\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}\;.

If one additionally chooses
\mathbb{M}
to be a subset of all effect operators, then
\mathbb{M}
can be related to a set of binary (POVM) measurements. The corresponding
minimal error probability
for discriminating between two equiprobable states
\rho
and
\sigma
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{\mathbb{M}}_{err}\left(\rho,\sigma\right)=\frac{1}{2}-\sup_{E\in\mathbb{M}}\frac{1}{2}\left|\Tr[(\rho-\sigma)E]\right|=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4}\|\rho-\sigma\|_{\mathbb{M}}\,.

(2.14)

3
Cone of Efficient Binary Measurements

We first study in
Section

3.1
the consequences of complexity constraints for quantum measurements, defining sets of efficient two-output measurements. We then show in
Section

3.2
that one can naturally associate to these sets a proper cone inside of
\mathcal{B}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{H})
; this is our main contribution for this section. As we then discuss in Section
4
, this allows us to define a computational max-divergence via Definition
2.13
. Moreover, we also discuss relations between
our sets of efficient measurements and corresponding notions of a
computational trace norm
and
computational trace distance
in
Section

3.3
, and relate this connection to cryptographic constructions.

3.1
Complexity-Constrained POVMs

When we consider (generic) two-output measurements, we refer to POVMs
M=(E_{1},E_{2})
. It is known that any two-output POVM on
\mathcal{H}
is implemented by appending ancillas to the quantum system, applying a quantum circuit on the entire system, applying a projective measurement on a
single
qubit in the computational basis, and tracing out the remaining subsystems. The formal way to express one specific implementation of
M
is typically via Naimark’s dilation theorem, see e.g.
[
Hol11
, Theorem 2.5.1]
, which we include below for completeness and holds for arbitrary
m
-outcome POVMs.

Theorem 3.1

(Naimark’s Dilation Theorem in Finite Dimensions)
.

Any POVM
M=(E_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
on
\mathcal{H}
can be implemented via an isometry and a projective measurement on ancilla systems. In particular, there exists an isometry
V:\mathcal{B}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{B}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{K})
, where
\mathcal{H}_{K}
denotes a Hilbert space of dimension
m
, such that

\displaystyle E_{k}=V^{*}\Bigl(\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{H}}\otimes|k\rangle\langle k|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}\Bigr)V\quad\forall k\in[m].

(3.1)

Remark 3.2
.

One can alternatively view
V
as a unitary,
U
, on the space
\mathcal{B}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{K})
which acts on states of the form
\rho\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0}_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}
.
A simple consequence of Theorem
3.1
is that for any state
\rho
,

\displaystyle\Tr\left[E_{k}\rho\right]=\Tr\left[\Bigl(\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{H}}\otimes|k\rangle\langle k|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}\Bigr)\Bigl(U\left(\rho\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0}_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}\right)U^{*}\Bigr)\right]\;.

(3.2)

Since only the ancillary system is being measured, one can trace out
\mathcal{H}
, thus retrieving the aforementioned interpretation of Naimark’s dilation.

Remark 3.3

(Gate complexity of the POVM)
.

When we refer to the complexity of a POVM, we are referring to the complexity of the (unitary) quantum circuit
U
that was necessary
to implement it. We adopt the approach from, e.g.,
[
AA25
,
AHRA25
]
, where, in other words, one considers the process of adding ancillas, tracing out subsystems, and measuring in the computational basis, to be ‘free’ operations. To characterize the complexity of a quantum circuit,
U
, one first chooses an approximately universal gate set
\mathcal{G}
consisting of
|\mathcal{G}|
unique gates.
The complexity of
U
is equal to the minimal number of gates that are necessary to generate
U
.

For binary measurements, there exists a bijection between POVMs
M=(E,\mathds{1}-E)
and effect operators
E
. We take advantage of this bijection for the remainder of this section and will in abuse of notation sometimes refer to both effect operators and POVMs as measurements, depending on context. In particular, whenever we consider the complexity of an effect operator
E
, we are referring to the complexity of the associated POVM
M
.

Definition 3.4

(Polynomially Generated Sets of Effect Operators)
.

Given an approximate universal quantum gate set
\mathcal{G}
of fixed size
|\mathcal{G}|<\infty
, a
polynomially generated set of effect operators
is a family of sets of effect operators

\displaystyle\{\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\quad\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\subseteq\{E\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}):E\leq\mathds{1}\}

(3.3)

such that there exists a polynomial
p
with the property that any element of
E\in\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
can be implemented using at most
p(n)
elementary gates in the sense of
3.3
. We will further impose that if
E\in\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
, then so is
\mathds{1}-E\in\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
. Lastly, given
E_{1}\in\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n_{1}}
and
E_{2}\in\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n_{2}}
, then
E_{1}\otimes E_{2}\in\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n_{1}+n_{2}}
, if the gate complexities of
E_{i}
, say
c_{i}
, are such that
c_{1}+c_{2}<p(n_{1}+n_{2})
.

If each
\mathbf{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{eff}}
is informationally complete, i.e. it holds that
\mathsf{span}(\mathbf{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{eff}})=\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})
, then we further call this family an
informationally complete polynomially generated set of effect operators
.

One should think of polynomially generated effect operators informally as ‘efficiently’ implementable measurement operators.
To better understand this definition, a couple of observations and remarks are in order.
Observe that we (almost) trivially have that
0,\mathds{1}\in\mathbf{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{eff}}
, since these measurements corresponds to trivially outputting
0,1
, respectively, regardless of input. A way to implement this is to generate an ancilla qubit in the state
\ket{0}
(for
1
one additionally applies an
X
gate) and subsequently measuring it in the computational basis, which we assume to be free operations.
We will usually assume that the polynomial
p
in the definition is strictly super-additive, satisfying
p(n+m)\geq p(n)+p(m)+1
, unless otherwise mentioned.

Remark 3.5

(Efficient effect operators, classical post-processing, and information completeness)
.

Implicit in this definition is that we assume that polynomial classical post-processing with NOT and OR operations is also efficient. To motivate that
E\in\mathbf{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{eff}}\implies\mathds{1}-E\in\mathbf{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{eff}}
, we highlight that the POVMs, which are the physical implementation of the effect operators,
M_{1}=(E,\mathds{1}-E)
and
M_{2}=(\mathds{1}-E,E)
differ only by a single classical NOT post-processing step. As such, if
M_{1}
is ‘efficient’, then
M_{2}
should also be considered efficient, which we enforce with this condition.

Likewise, the stability under tensor product condition (assuming the polynomial
p
grows fast enough, which is the case if it is super-additive) is motivated by our implicit assumption that polynomially many classical OR post-processing operations are also assumed to be efficient. For example, for two efficient effect operators
E_{1},E_{2}
the corresponding binary measurements are
M_{1}=(E_{1},\mathds{1}-E_{1})
and
M_{2}=(E_{2},\mathds{1}-E_{2})
, and
M_{3}=(E_{1}\otimes E_{2},\mathds{1}-E_{1}\otimes E_{2})
corresponds to the tensor product measurement
E_{1}\otimes E_{2}
. It is implementable by implementing
M_{1},M_{2}
in parallel using at most
p(n_{1})+p(n_{2})
gates and mapping the output ‘
\,00
’ to
0
and the other three outcomes to
1
. This corresponds to a logical OR gate so is only polynomial classical post processing.

We point out that one way to ensure that the set of effect operators is informationally complete is if for every
n
,

\displaystyle U\outerproduct{k}{k}U^{*}\in\mathbf{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{eff}}\quad\forall k\in[\textup{dim}(\mathcal{H})^{n}]\ \forall U\in\mathcal{U}\;,

where
\mathcal{U}
is the set of
Weyl operators
on
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}

[
Wat18
, Section 4.1.2]
and
|k\rangle\langle k|
is the computational basis. For the case that
\textup{dim}(\mathcal{H})=2
, this can be reduced to the Pauli group. Therefore, it corresponds to first applying (for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
) a unitary
U_{i}\in\{X,Y,Z\}
on the
i
-th qubit. Then one has to apply a measurement from some set, where the corresponding effect operators are of the form

\displaystyle\outerproduct{1^{i_1}\cdots 1^{i_n}}{1^{i_1}\cdots 1^{i_n}}\;.

One way to generate the corresponding measurement would be to first apply the gate
X^{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes X^{i_{n}}
. Subsequently, one applies a computational measurement on all
n
qubits. Since we are only considering binary measurements, one has to then map the outcome
0\cdots 0
to
0
and all other outcomes to
1
. This ‘
2^{n}
-output measurement
+
post-processing step’ is generally efficient, and can be implemented by an efficient quantum circuit and a computational measurement on a single qubit. So in effect, the informationally complete condition means that the polynomial
p(n)
cannot be too small.

Unless specified otherwise, we will for the following always assume that the polynomially generated set of effect operators is informationally complete. Using the bijection between effect operators
E
and binary POVMs
(E,\mathds{1}-E)
, we consequently get the following definition.

Definition 3.6

(Polynomially Generated Sets of Binary POVMs)
.

Let
\{\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be an (informationally complete) polynomially generated set of effect operators. The family

\displaystyle\{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\quad\text{where}\quad\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\coloneqq\left\{(E,\mathds{1}-E)|E\in\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\right\}\;,

(3.4)

is the associated
(informationally complete) polynomially generated set of binary POVMs
.

We recall that directly from the properties of
Definition

3.4
it follows that if
{M=(E,\mathds{1}-E)\in\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}
, then so is
{M^{\prime}}=(\mathds{1}-E,E)
and if
M_{i}=(E_{i},\mathds{1}-E_{i})\in\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n_{i}}
for
i=1,2
, then
(E_{1}\otimes E_{2},\mathds{1}-E_{1}\otimes E_{2})\in\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n_{1}+n_{2}}
, if the polynomial implicit in the definition of the family
\{\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
grows sufficiently fast.

Remark 3.7
.

Our definition of efficiently generated measurements encapsulates non-uniform quantum poly time generated POVMs, which have found applications in cryptography due to their ability to model an adversary with quantum advice
[
AD13
]
. Moreover, it closely mirrors, e.g., the circuit complexity definition used in
[
AA25
,
AHRA25
]
to define computational entropies that are useful for cryptography. As such, any divergences we define in
Section
4
via
\{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
may similarly be relevant for cryptographic applications.

3.2
The Proper Cone of Efficient Measurements

Given any family
\{\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
as defined above, for each value
n\in\mathbb{N}
one can naturally define the smallest convex set that contains all corresponding effect operators. The same also holds true for the corresponding family
\{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of binary POVMs.

Definition 3.8

(Polynomially Generated Convex Sets of Effect Operators and Binary POVMs)
.

Let
\{\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
denote a (informationally complete) polynomially generated set of effect operators. The corresponding
(informationally complete) polynomially generated convex set of effect operators
is given by the family

\displaystyle\left\{\overline{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\quad\text{where}\quad\overline{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\coloneqq\mathrm{conv}(\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n})=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}E^{i}_{n}\,\Bigg|\,k\in\mathbb{N},\,\lambda_{i}\geq 0,\,\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}=1,\,E^{i}_{n}\in\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\,\right\}\;.

(3.5)

Similarly, for the corresponding polynomially generated set of binary POVMs
\{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, we define the
(informationally complete) polynomially generated convex set of binary POVMs
as

\displaystyle\{\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\quad\text{where}\quad\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\coloneqq\left\{(E,\mathds{1}-E)\Big|E\in\overline{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\right\}\;.

(3.6)

Remark 3.9
.

Note that the convex sets
\overline{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
and
\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
are closed
\forall n\in\mathbb{N}
. This is due to the fact that both
\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
and
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
are finite sets in a finite-dimensional vector space. As such, their convex hulls are simplexes and therefore closed.

One key property of these sets is that every measurement in
\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}

can be efficiently approximated. This ensures that one can also view
\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
as a set of efficiently approximable measurements.
To prove this property, we require the following proposition. The proof is partly based on the techniques used in
[
Bar15
]
.

Proposition 3.10
.

For every effect operator
E\in\overline{\mathbf{E}}^{\text{eff}}_{n}
, there exists a subset of
k=\frac{5\left(n\ln{\textup{dim}(\HH)}+1\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}}
effect operators
\{E_{j_{1}},\dots,E_{j_{k}}\}\subset\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
such that

\displaystyle\Bigl\|E-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\,E_{j_{i}}\Bigr\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon\;.

(3.7)

Proof.

Let
m=|\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}|
denote the cardinality of
|\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}|
and let
E_{1},\dots,E_{m}
denote the elements of
\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
. By construction, there exist weights
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}E_{i}=E\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}=1\;.

Let
X
be a self-adjoint random matrix valued variable which with probability
\alpha_{i}
satisfies

\displaystyle X=E-E_{i}\;

Notably, this random variable satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X]=0\quad\text{and}\quad\big\|\mathbb{E}[X^{\,2}]\big\|_{\infty}\leq 1\;.

Consequently, for
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{k}
,
k
IID copies of
X
, the variance satisfies

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\coloneqq\Big\|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{\,2}]\Big\|_{\infty}\leq k\;.

Applying the matrix Bernstein inequality
[
MJC+14
, Cor. 5.2]
for any
0\leq\varepsilon\leq 1
, it holds that

\displaystyle\Pr\!\left[\lambda_{\max}\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}X_{j}\right)\geq\varepsilon\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr\!\left[\lambda_{\max}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}X_{j}\right)\geq k\varepsilon\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\text{dim}(\mathcal{H})^{n}\right)\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{k^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}{3k+2k\varepsilon}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\text{dim}(\mathcal{H})^{n}\right)\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{k^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}{5k}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\left(\text{dim}(\mathcal{H})^{n}\right)\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{k\varepsilon^{2}}{5}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\exp\!\Bigl(n\ln{\text{dim}(\HH)}-\frac{k\varepsilon^{2}}{5}\Bigr)\;,

where
\lambda_{\max}\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}X_{j}\right)
denotes the largest eigenvalue of
\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}X_{j}
.
Setting
k=\frac{5\left(n\ln{\text{dim}(\HH)}+1\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}}
hence ensures that

\displaystyle\Pr\!\left[\lambda_{\max}\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}X_{j}\right)\geq\varepsilon\right]\leq e^{-1}<\frac{1}{2}\;.

By the same argument for
-X_{i}
it holds that, for the same value
k
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr\!\left[\lambda_{\min}\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}X_{j}\right)\leq-\varepsilon\right]\leq e^{-1}<\frac{1}{2}\;,

where
\lambda_{\min}\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}X_{j}\right)
denotes the smallest eigenvalue of
\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}X_{j}
.
Using a union bound argument, it follows from these two inequalities that

\displaystyle\Pr\!\left[\lambda_{\min}\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}X_{j}\right)\geq-\varepsilon\,\land\,\lambda_{\max}\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}X_{j}\right)\leq\varepsilon\right]\geq 1-\frac{2}{e}>0\;.

As such, there must exist values for
X_{1},\dots,X_{k}
such that the maximal eigenvalue of
|\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}X_{j}|
is less than or equal to
\varepsilon
. For these specific values of
X_{i}
, we know that there exists
\{E_{j_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{k}\subset\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
such that

\displaystyle X_{i}=E-E_{j_{i}}\;.

By construction, it must hold for these effect operators that

\displaystyle\Bigl\|E-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\,E_{j_{i}}\Bigr\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon\;,

thus concluding the proof.
∎

We now discuss two consequences that follow from Proposition
3.10
. First, even if one chooses
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{\mathsf{poly}(n)}
, i.e. the allowed error to scale inverse polynomially in
n
, one can approximate any
E\in\overline{\mathbf{E}}^{\text{eff}}_{n}
using an (in general non-unique and non-degenerate) polynomial subset of effect operators
\{E_{j_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{k}\subset\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
.
Secondly, our bound on the size of this set holds true for every effect operator
E\in\overline{\mathbf{E}}^{\text{eff}}_{n}
. This allows one to efficiently approximate every binary POVM
M\in\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}

to inverse‑polynomial accuracy.
Note that this in particular applies to
\alpha E
for some
E\in\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}
and
0\leq\alpha\leq 1
, as
0\in\mathbf{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{eff}}
.
This consequence is formally stated in Corollary
3.11
.

Lemma 3.15
.

The family of cones of informationally complete polynomially generated effect operators

As is discussed in, e.g.,
[

]

As such, one binary measurement that would generally be inefficient is, given a (uniformly) random function

However, as we discussed in

are efficiently implementable. As such, the effect operator doing just that is

\displaystyle E_{f}=\sum_{x:f(x)=0}\outerproduct{x}{x}\in\mathcal{C}^{\textbf{E}_{\text{eff}}}_{n}

and lies in the cone
\mathcal{C}^{\textbf{E}_{\text{eff}}}_{n}
, but would correspond to a binary measurement which is not efficient.
Let us consider such a computationally inefficient function

.
We highlight that the corresponding effect operators

as they are not convex mixtures of efficient effect operators. However, there exists an

such that

and
{\alpha=|\{x:f(x)=0\}|}
, by construction of
\overline{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
. In this case, since the average size of

Remark 3.18

(Inefficient measurements vs.
\left\{\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{eff}}}_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
)
.

Our efficient cone of effect operators is non-uniform, in the sense that its elements are prepared by families of polynomial-size circuits without a uniform generation requirement. Many natural effects and measurements nevertheless lie outside this cone due to computational hardness. Next we give explicit non-membership examples.

Since
\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
is finite for each

\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{eff}}}_{n}
is generated by finitely many rays. A rank-one projector

can belong to

only if one of these generators is proportional to

. Hence, for all but finitely many

. In addition, by a standard counting argument, with overwhelming probability a Haar–random rank-one projector

requires exponential circuit size to implement

NC00
,
Gol08
]
. Moreover,
[
JW23
]
construct explicit families of states with exponential circuit lower bounds; thus the corresponding rank-one projectors are, eventually, not in

(even up to inverse-polynomial approximation).

For QMA-complete local Hamiltonians, if one could (given the instance description) efficiently compile a polynomial-size circuit that implements a projector onto the ground space to constant precision, this would yield a BQP algorithm for a QMA-complete promise problem, implying
\mathrm{QMA}\subseteq\mathrm{BQP}
, which is considered unlikely
[
KKR05
]
. Finally, under standard pseudorandomness assumptions (against non-uniform quantum distinguishers), any measurement that separates pseudorandom states or unitaries from Haar-random distributions with non-negligible bias cannot be efficient—otherwise one could break the initial assumptions

,
MPSY24

ABF+23
]

3.3
Computational Trace Norm and Trace Distance

Having constructed our bases and cones of efficiently implementable measurements one straightforwardly via
Definition

2.15
gets corresponding distinguishability norms, which also appear in the computational quantum cryptographic setting, see e.g.
[
JLS18
,
ABF+23
,
BCQ23
]
.

Without computational constraints, where every measurement is implementable, the best possible distinguishability advantage between two quantum states
\rho
and
\sigma
is related to the trace distance
\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}
. However, if the measurements are instead constrained to be implementable with poly-size quantum operations, the information-theoretic trace distance loses its operational meaning. In such cases, one instead considers the
computational distance
. This distance can essentially be recovered by a suitable
distinguishability norm
, see
Definition

2.15
, which in this context we call a
computational trace norm
.

Let us recall that the convex hull of efficient effect operators
\{\overline{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a closed, convex, and informationally complete set, which additionally contains the trivial measurement
\mathds{1}\in\overline{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
and satisfies the condition

\displaystyle E\in\overline{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\implies\mathds{1}-E\in\overline{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\quad\forall E\in\overline{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}

(3.12)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
For such sets, it follows from
[
RKW11
, Section V]
that

\displaystyle\|v\|_{\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\coloneqq\sup_{E\in\overline{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\langle 2E-\mathds{1},v\rangle

(3.13)

defines a norm on the vector space of Hermitian operators
v\in\mathcal{B}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
This naturally allows one to define a
computational trace norm
, where
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})
,
v=\rho-\sigma
, and
\Tr\left[v\right]=0
.

Definition 3.19

(Computational Trace Norm)
.

Given any two quantum states
{\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})}
and a family of informationally complete polynomially generated convex set of binary POVMs
\{\overline{\mathbf{M}}_{n}^{\text{eff}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, the
computational trace norm
between these states is given by

\displaystyle\|\rho-\sigma\|_{\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\coloneqq 2\sup_{E\in\overline{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\Tr\left[E\left(\rho-\sigma\right)\right]\;.

(3.14)

For general
\rho,\sigma\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})
, one gets via Eq. (
3.13
), the more general

\displaystyle\|\rho-\sigma\|_{\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}=2\sup_{E\in\overline{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\Tr\left[E\left(\rho-\sigma\right)\right]-\Tr\left[\rho-\sigma\right]\;.

(3.15)

Remark 3.20
.

It was shown in
[
MWW09
]
(and further discussed in
[
RKW11
]
), that norms defined via the theory of proper cones (such as the one in Eq. (
3.13
)) naturally satisfy the triangle inequality, positive definiteness and are absolute homogeneous, i.e.

•

Triangle Inequality: for all
\rho,\sigma,\tau\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})

\displaystyle\|\rho-\sigma\|_{\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\leq\|\rho-\tau\|_{\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}+\|\tau-\sigma\|_{\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\;.

(3.16)

•

Absolute Homogeneity: for all
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\|\lambda\left(\rho-\sigma\right)\|_{\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}=\left|\lambda\right|\|\rho-\sigma\|_{\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\;.

(3.17)

•

Positive Definiteness: for all
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})

\displaystyle\|\rho-\sigma\|_{\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}=0\implies\rho=\sigma\;.

(3.18)

One straightforward property of the computational trace norm is that the optimization appearing on the RHS of Eq. (

This follows from well-known arguments; for completeness we have included the proof in

4.1

In the rest of this paper, let
\{\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

\{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
its corresponding informationally complete polynomially generated set of binary POVMs, and its associated family of proper cones
\{\mathcal{C}_{n}^{\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

For each proper cone of efficient effect operators
\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{eff}}}_{n}

Definition 4.1

(Computational Max-Divergence)
.

Given a family of proper cones
\{\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{eff}}}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and any two positive semidefinite operators

with
\operatorname{Tr}\left[\rho\right]>0
and
\rho\ll\sigma

by:

\displaystyle\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 2.0417pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}(\rho\|\sigma)\coloneqq\log\inf\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}|\rho\leq_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathcal{S_{\mathrm{eff}}}}_{n}}\lambda\sigma\}=\log\sup_{v\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{eff}}}_{n}}\frac{\Tr[v\rho]}{\Tr[v\sigma]}\;,

(4.2)

where we recall that
\mathcal{C}^{\mathcal{S_{\mathrm{eff}}}}_{n}
is the dual cone to
\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{eff}}}_{n}
and thus also proper.

We will occasionally also denote the computational max-divergence by
\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 2.0417pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}^{p(n)}
to highlight the underlying polynomial
p(n)
that is used in the definition of the family
\{\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{eff}}}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. The equality in Eq. (
4.2
) holds whenever
\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{eff}}}_{n}
is a proper cone, see
[
RKW11
, Eqs. 4-6]
and
[
GC24
, Proposition 10]
.

Remark 4.2
.

Since we construct the cone of efficient effects
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{\textbf{E}_{\text{eff}}}
rather implicitly, depending on some fixed polynomial and approximately universal gate set, existence of a tractable membership or linear-minimization oracle is unclear. Projection or Frank–Wolfe–type methods would require such an oracle to solve the membership problem. Hence we cannot provide a general algorithm for approximating the computational divergences that are based on these cones and introduced in the following section. Designing relaxations or hierarchies that admit verifiable oracles is an interesting open direction.

4.1.1
Super- and Sub-additivity Properties

Recall that the elements of
\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
are given by
all
POVM elements which can be generated with circuit size of (at most)
p(n)
and possible polynomial classical post-processing with NOT and OR operations. So if we assume this polynomial to be strictly super-additive, i.e.
p(n+m)\geq p(n)+p(m)+1
, then the family
\{\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and the family of its induced cones
\{\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{eff}}}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are stable under tensor products, in the sense that if
E_{i}\in\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n_{i}}
, then
E_{1}\otimes E_{2}\in\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n_{1}+n_{2}}
and the same holds for
\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{eff}}}_{n}
.
With this in mind we are now ready to study some of the super-additivity and sub-additivity properties of the computational max-divergence, where we follow the proof techniques from
[
RLW24
]
.

Lemma 4.3

(Super-additivity of
\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 2.0417pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}
)
.

The computational max-divergence
\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 1.53334pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}(\rho\|\sigma)
is super-additive, i.e. for any
\rho=\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2}
,
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\otimes\sigma_{2}
such that
\rho_{i},\sigma_{i}\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n_{i}})
,

\displaystyle\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 1.53334pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}^{p(n_{1}+n_{2})}(\rho\|\sigma)\geq\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 1.53334pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}^{p(n_{1})}(\rho_{1}\|\sigma_{1})+\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 1.53334pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}^{p(n_{2})}(\rho_{2}\|\sigma_{2})\;.

(4.3)

Proof.

By construction and super-additivity of
p
we have for
E_{i}\in\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n_{i}}
(for
i\in\{1,2\}
), that
{E_{1}\otimes E_{2}\in\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n_{1}+n_{2}}}
. Due to convexity, it then naturally follows that if
E_{i}\in\overline{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n_{i}}
(for
i\in\{1,2\}
), then
E_{1}\otimes E_{2}\in\overline{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n_{1}+n_{2}}
. We refer to
3.5
for a more detailed discussion on this.

For any
v_{i}\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}}_{n_{i}}
, there exists a
\lambda_{i}
and
E_{i}\in\overline{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n_{i}}
such that
v_{i}=\lambda_{i}E_{i}
. Since
E_{1}\otimes E_{2}\in\overline{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n_{1}+n_{2}}
, it must hold that
v_{1}\otimes v_{2}\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}}_{n_{1}+n_{2}}
.
As such

\displaystyle\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 2.0417pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}^{p(n_{1}+n_{2})}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle=\log\sup_{v\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}}_{n_{1}+n_{2}}}\frac{\Tr[v\left(\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2}\right)]}{\Tr[v\left(\sigma_{1}\otimes\sigma_{2}\right)]}

\displaystyle\geq\log\sup_{v_{i}\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}}_{n_{i}}}\frac{\Tr[\left(v_{1}\otimes v_{2}\right)\left(\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2}\right)]}{\Tr[\left(v_{1}\otimes v_{2}\right)\left(\sigma_{1}\otimes\sigma_{2}\right)]}

\displaystyle=\log\sup_{v_{1}\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}}_{n_{1}}}\frac{\Tr[v_{1}\rho_{1}]}{\Tr[v_{1}\sigma_{1}]}+\log\sup_{v_{2}\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}}_{n_{2}}}\frac{\Tr[v_{2}\rho_{2}]}{\Tr[v_{2}\sigma_{2}]}

\displaystyle=\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 2.0417pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}^{p(n_{1})}(\rho_{1}\|\sigma_{1})+\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 2.0417pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}^{p(n_{2})}(\rho_{2}\|\sigma_{2})\,.

∎

The same proof can be extended to operators of the form
\rho^{\otimes m},\sigma^{\otimes m}\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n\cdot m})
. Here, one simply finds that

\displaystyle\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 2.0417pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}^{p(n\cdot m)}(\rho^{\otimes m}\|\sigma^{\otimes m})\geq m\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 2.0417pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}^{p(n)}(\rho\|\sigma)\;.

(4.4)

In other words, the super-additivity property follows from the observation that, informally, tensor products of efficient measurements are also an efficient measurement. This measurement can efficiently be reduced to a binary measurement, using logical OR gates if
m\in\mathsf{poly}(n)
.

Sub-additivity cannot be proven via the same techniques. Nevertheless, acting on quantum states, it is possible to prove that the computational max-divergence must also be approximately sub-additive.

Lemma 4.4

(Sub-additivity of
\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 2.0417pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}
)
.

The computational max-divergence
\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 1.53334pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}(\rho\|\sigma)
is approximately sub-additive for quantum states. For any
\rho=\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2}
,
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\otimes\sigma_{2}
such that
{\rho_{i},\sigma_{i}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n_{i}})}
with cost
\kappa(\rho_{i})
,
\kappa(\sigma_{i})
,
8
8

8

Here, the cost is the minimal circuit size required to prepare them using gates from the prior fixed gate set
\mathcal{G}
.
, it holds that

\displaystyle\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 1.53334pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}^{p(n_{1}+n_{2})}(\rho\|\sigma)\leq\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 1.53334pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}^{p(n_{1}+n_{2})+\kappa}(\rho_{1}\|\sigma_{1})+\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 1.53334pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}^{p(n_{1}+n_{2})+\kappa}(\rho_{2}\|\sigma_{2})\;,

(4.5)

where
\kappa\coloneqq\max\{\kappa(\rho_{1}),\kappa(\rho_{2}),\kappa(\sigma_{1}),\kappa(\sigma_{2})\}
.

Proof.

The proof technique is again inspired by
[
RLW24
]
. For any
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, it holds that

\displaystyle(\lambda_{1}\cdot\lambda_{2})\sigma_{1}\otimes\sigma_{2}-\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2}=\left(\lambda_{1}\sigma_{1}-\rho_{1}\right)\otimes\lambda_{2}\sigma_{2}+\rho_{1}\otimes\left(\lambda_{2}\sigma_{2}-\rho_{2}\right)\;.

Let us consider any
\lambda=\lambda_{1}\cdot\lambda_{2}\geq 0
for which it holds that

\displaystyle\Tr[(\lambda\sigma-\rho)E]\geq 0\;,\qquad\forall E\in\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n_{1}+n_{2}}\;.

(4.6)

By construction it must similarly hold that

\displaystyle\Tr[(\lambda\sigma-\rho)v]\geq 0\;,\qquad\forall v\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}}_{n_{1}+n_{2}}\;,

and, by
2.14
, one then finds that

\displaystyle\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 2.0417pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}^{p(n_{1}+n_{2})}(\rho\|\sigma)\leq\log\lambda\;.

Our goal is to show that
\lambda=\lambda_{1}\cdot\lambda_{2}
satisfies Eq. (
4.6
), if one sets

\displaystyle\log\lambda_{i}=\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 2.0417pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}^{p(n_{1}+n_{2})+\kappa+1}(\rho_{i}\|\sigma_{i})\;.

(4.7)

Note that, in particular, Eq. (
4.7
) implies
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
for
i\in\{1,2\}
. Since it takes at most
\kappa
extra gates to generate
\sigma_{2}
, for any
E\in\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n_{1}+n_{2}}
acting on
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n_{2}}
there exists another effect operator
E^{\prime}
on
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n_{1}}
that first additionally prepares
\sigma_{2}
and then implements
E
. By construction, it must hold that

\displaystyle\Tr[(\lambda_{1}\sigma_{1}-\rho_{1})\otimes\sigma_{2}E]=\Tr[(\lambda_{1}\sigma_{1}-\rho_{1})E^{\prime}]\;.

Moreover, since
E^{\prime}
uses at most
p(n_{1}+n_{2})+\kappa
gates, it follows from the definition of
\lambda_{1}
that

\displaystyle\Tr[(\lambda_{1}\sigma_{1}-\rho_{1})\otimes\sigma_{2}E]=\Tr[(\lambda_{1}\sigma_{1}-\rho_{1})E^{\prime}]\geq 0\;.

Similarly, it must hold for any
E\in\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n_{1}+n_{2}}
that

\displaystyle\Tr[\rho_{1}\otimes(\lambda_{2}\sigma_{2}-\rho_{2})E]\geq 0\;.

(4.8)

Combining this, we find for
\lambda=\lambda_{1}\cdot\lambda_{2}
and any
E\in\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n_{1}+n_{2}}
that

\displaystyle\Tr[(\lambda\sigma-\rho)E]

\displaystyle=\lambda_{2}\Tr[(\lambda_{1}\sigma_{1}-\rho_{1})\otimes\sigma_{2}E]+\Tr[\rho_{1}\otimes(\lambda_{2}\sigma_{2}-\rho_{2})E]\geq 0\;,

thus satisfying Eq. (
4.6
). As a result, it must hold that

\displaystyle\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 2.0417pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}^{p(n_{1}+n_{2})}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle\leq\log\lambda

\displaystyle=\log\lambda_{1}+\log\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle=\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 2.0417pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}^{p(n_{1}+n_{2})+\kappa}(\rho_{1}\|\sigma_{1})+\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 2.0417pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}^{p(n_{1}+n_{2})+\kappa}(\rho_{2}\|\sigma_{2})\;.

∎

Remark 4.5
.

It generally makes sense to consider quantum states
\rho
and
\sigma
that are efficiently preparable, i.e. where
\kappa
scales polynomially in
n_{1},n_{2}
. This way, the max-divergences that appear in
4.4
are all related to cones generated by efficient effect operators. Moreover, the proof can straightforwardly be modified to hold for positive semidefinite operators
\rho,\sigma\in\mathrm{Pos}\left(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n_{2}}\right)
. However, for any
\rho_{i}\in\mathrm{Pos}\left(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n_{i}}\right)
,
\kappa(\rho_{i})
would then be the gate complexity required to generate the corresponding normalized quantum state
\frac{\rho_{i}}{\Tr\left[\rho_{i}\right]}
and analogously for
\sigma_{i}
.

For states of the form
\rho^{\otimes m},\sigma^{\otimes m}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n\cdot m})
, the same proof can be slightly modified, using
[
RLW24
]

\displaystyle\lambda^{m}\sigma^{\otimes m}-\rho^{\otimes m}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda^{j-1}\rho^{\otimes m-j}\otimes(\lambda\sigma-\rho)\otimes\sigma^{\otimes j-1}\;.

(4.9)

One then retrieves the bound

\displaystyle\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 2.0417pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}^{p(n\cdot m)}(\rho^{\otimes m}\|\sigma^{\otimes m})\leq m\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 2.0417pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}^{p(n\cdot m)+(m-1)\kappa}(\rho\|\sigma)\;,

(4.10)

where
\kappa\coloneqq\max\{\kappa(\rho),\kappa(\sigma)\}
. For fixed
\rho,\sigma
, one should expect the RHS of Eq. (
4.10
) to converge to the information-theoretic max-divergence in the limit
m\to\infty
. This is due to the fact that while the states are fixed, the gate complexity of the allowed measurement is increasing as a function of
m
. This inequality is not unexpected, as it always holds that

\displaystyle\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 2.0417pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}(\rho^{\otimes m}\|\sigma^{\otimes m})\leq D_{\text{max}}(\rho^{\otimes m}\|\sigma^{\otimes m})=mD_{\text{max}}(\rho\|\sigma)\;.

(4.11)

Lastly, let us relate the computational max-divergence with the computational trace distance.

Lemma 4.6

(Computational Max-divergence and Computational Trace Distance)
.

Given two quantum states
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})
, we have that

\displaystyle\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 1.53334pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}(\rho\|\sigma)\geq\log\left(1+\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{\Delta}\left(\rho,\sigma\right)\right)\;.

(4.12)

Proof.

The proof is straight forward and can be found in
Section

B.1
.
∎

4.2
Computational Measured Rényi Divergences

This section is devoted to computational measured Rényi divergences. Here, we consider an informationally complete polynomially generated set of binary POVMs
\{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, who’s underlying polynomial is strictly super-additive. For each set
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
, we will define a measured Rényi divergence.

Definition 4.7

(Computational Measured Rényi Divergence)
.

Given any two positive semidefinite operators
\rho,\sigma\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})
and an element
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
of an informationally complete polynomially generated set of binary POVMs
\{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, we denote the corresponding
computational measured Rényi divergence
for any
\alpha\in\left(0,1\right)\cup\left(1,\infty\right)
by:

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)\coloneqq\sup_{M\in\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}D_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}_{M}(\rho)\|\mathcal{M}_{M}(\sigma))\;,

(4.13)

where
D_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)
is the sandwiched Rényi divergence (
Definition

2.1
) and
\mathcal{M}(\cdot)
is the measurement channel corresponding to the POVM
M
.

Moreover, we use
\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{Q}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)
to denote

\displaystyle\ \accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{Q}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}\inf_{M\in\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}Q_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}(\rho)\|\mathcal{M}(\sigma))&\text{ if }\alpha\in(0,1)\\
\sup_{M\in\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}Q_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}(\rho)\|\mathcal{M}(\sigma))&\text{ if }\alpha\in(1,\infty)\end{cases}\;.

(4.14)

Definition 4.8

(Computational Measured Relative Entropy & Max-divergence)
.

Given any two positive semidefinite operators
\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})
with
\operatorname{Tr}\left[\rho\right]>0
and
\rho\ll\sigma
and an element
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
of an informationally complete polynomially generated set of binary POVMs
\{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, the
computational measured relative entropy
and
max-divergence
between
\rho
,
\sigma
are, respectively, given by:

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sup_{M\in\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\frac{\Tr[\mathcal{M}\left(\rho\right)(\log\mathcal{M}\left(\rho\right)-\log\mathcal{M}\left(\sigma\right))]}{\Tr\left[\mathcal{M}\left(\rho\right)\right]}=\lim_{\alpha\nearrow 1}\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)\;,

(4.15)

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}_{\text{max}}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sup_{M\in\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\log\inf\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}|\mathcal{M}\left(\rho\right)\leq\lambda\mathcal{M}\left(\sigma\right)\}=\lim_{\alpha\nearrow\infty}\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)\;.

(4.16)

By taking the appropriate limits, one can retrieve the computational measured relative entropy and max-divergence from the computational measured Rényi divergences, see e.g.
[
RSB24
, Lemma 2]
.
We note that a priori one could have also defined the computational measured Rényi divergences using

\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
instead of
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
. We show in
4.9
below, however, that these definitions are equivalent.

Lemma 4.9
.

For any
\mathbb{D}\in\{D_{\alpha},D,D_{\text{max}}\}
, where
\alpha\in\left(0,1\right)\cup\left(1,\infty\right)
, it holds that

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip-0.25557pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{\mathbb{D}}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\rho\|\sigma)=\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip-0.25557pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{\mathbb{D}}^{\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\rho\|\sigma)\;.

(4.17)

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\subset\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
, it always holds that

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip-0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{\mathbb{D}}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\rho\|\sigma)\leq\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip-0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{\mathbb{D}}^{\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\rho\|\sigma)\;.

(4.18)

To prove the reverse inequality, we first note that
the relative entropy
D
and
D_{\alpha}
are jointly convex on classical states in the range
{\alpha\in\left(0,1\right)}
; the max-divergence
D_{\text{max}}
and
D_{\alpha}
are jointly quasi-convex for
{\alpha\in\left[1,\infty\right)}

[
Tom16
]
. Denoting the extremal points of
\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
by
\mathsf{extr}\left(\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\right)
, a consequence of the (quasi-) convexity is that

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip-0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{\mathbb{D}}^{\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sup_{M\in\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\mathbb{D}(\mathcal{M}(\rho)\|\mathcal{M}(\sigma))=\sup_{M\in\mathsf{extr}\left(\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\right)}\mathbb{D}(\mathcal{M}(\rho)\|\mathcal{M}(\sigma))\;.

Since the extremal points of
\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
satisfy

\mathsf{extr}\left(\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\right)\subset\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
by construction of
\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
,
it holds for all
\mathbb{D}\in\{D,D_{\alpha},D_{\text{max}}\}
that

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip-0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{\mathbb{D}}^{\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle=\sup_{M\in\mathsf{extr}\left(\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\right)}\mathbb{D}(\mathcal{M}(\rho)\|\mathcal{M}(\sigma))\leq\sup_{M\in{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}}\mathbb{D}(\mathcal{M}(\rho)\|\mathcal{M}(\sigma)=\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip-0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{\mathbb{D}}^{\mathbf{M}_{n}^{\mathrm{eff}}}(\rho\|\sigma)\;.

∎

4.2.1
Properties of Measured Rényi Divergences

In
[
RSB24
]
, properties of generic measured Rényi divergences are derived. For completeness, in this section, we rephrase the results of
[
RSB24
, Lemmas 2 & 5]
in our setting of efficient measurements.

Lemma 4.10
.

For any element
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
of an informationally complete polynomially generated set of binary POVMs
\{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\alpha,\beta\in\left(0,1\right)\cup\left(1,\infty\right)
, the corresponding computational measured Rényi entropies satisfy

1.

(Non-negativity): For any two normalized quantum states
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})
,

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.51114pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\alpha}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\rho\|\sigma)\geq 0\;.

(4.19)

2.

(Monotonicity in
\alpha
): For any
\alpha\leq\beta
and
\rho,\sigma\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})
,

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.51114pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\alpha}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\rho\|\sigma)\leq\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.51114pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\beta}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\rho\|\sigma)\;.

(4.20)

3.

(Joint (Quasi-) Convexity): For any two normalized quantum states
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})
, we have that
\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.25557pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{Q}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)
is jointly concave in
\rho,\sigma
if
\alpha\in\left(0,1\right)
and jointly convex if
\alpha\in\left(1,\infty\right)
. Moreover,
\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.51114pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)
is jointly convex if
\alpha\in\left(0,1\right)
and jointly quasi-convex if
\alpha\in\left(1,\infty\right)
.

4.

(Data Processing Inequality): For any
\rho,\sigma\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})
, let
\mathcal{E}\in CPTP\left(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n},\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}\right)
be a completely positive trace-preserving map such that for any
E\in\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
,
\mathcal{E}^{*}\left(E\right)\in\overline{\mathbf{E}}^{\text{eff}}_{n}
. Then

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.51114pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\alpha}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\rho\|\sigma)\geq\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.51114pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\alpha}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\mathcal{E}(\rho)\|\mathcal{E}(\sigma))\;.

(4.21)

The first three properties are directly inherited from the information-theoretic sandwiched Rényi divergence. The last property can be understood as stating that we only consider
\mathcal{E}
such that for every
M\in\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
there exists another measurement

\bar{M}\in\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
which satisfies
\bar{\mathcal{M}}\left(\rho\right)=\mathcal{M}\circ\mathcal{E}\left(\rho\right)
for every
\rho
. In this case, optimizing over
M\circ\mathcal{E}
is more restrictive than simply optimizing over
M
.

Proof.

The proof for
4.10
directly follows from
[
RSB24
]
. However, for completeness, we include it in
Section

B.2
.
∎

Next let us prove a Pinsker inequality for the computational measured Rényi divergences.

Lemma 4.11

(Computational Pinsker inequality)
.

Given two quantum states
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})
, we have that for
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
,

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.51114pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)\geq\frac{\min\{1,\alpha\}}{2\ln 2}\|\rho-\sigma\|_{\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}^{2}\,.

(4.22)

Proof.

As discussed in
[
RSB24
]
, the bound for
\alpha\in(0,1]
follows directly from the classical result
[
Gil10
]
. Additionally, we convert the logarithm to base 2 and take the supremum over all efficient measurements. The bound for

follows from the monotonicity in
\alpha
property of the computational measured Rényi divergence (see
4.10
).
∎

4.2.2
Equivalence Between Conic and Measured Max-Divergence

We are now ready to prove one of the main results of this section, namely that the initially geometrically defined computational max-divergence (
\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\text{max}}
from
Definition

4.1
) and via the measured Rényi divergences constructed computational measured max-divergence (
\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}_{\text{max}}
from
Definition

4.8
) are equivalent in our setting. The fact that these two divergences are actually equivalent is a priori not clear and this result highlights the strength of applying conic techniques to the computational setting.

This also naturally complements the known information-theoretic result, which states that there exists an optimal measurement which leaves the max-divergence invariant
[
MH23
,
MO14
]
.

Theorem 4.12

(Max-Divergence Equivalence)
.

Let the families
\{\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}},
and
\{\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{eff}}}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be as in
Section

3
and informationally complete and define
\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.51114pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\text{max}}
and
\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.51114pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}_{\text{max}}
w.r.t. these as in
Section

4.1
and
Section

4.2
, respectively. Then for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and two positive semidefinite operators
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})
, it holds that

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.51114pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\textup{max}}(\rho\|\sigma)=\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.51114pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}_{\textup{max}}(\rho\|\sigma)\eqqcolon\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.51114pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)\;.

(4.23)

Proof.

Using the approach of
[
RSB24
]
, we first note that the computational max-divergence can alternatively be expressed as

\displaystyle\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 2.0417pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}(\rho\|\sigma)=\log\sup_{v\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{eff}}}_{n}}\frac{\Tr[v\rho]}{\Tr[v\sigma]}\;.

(4.24)

By construction, for every
v
, there exists a
\lambda\geq 0
and an element
E\in\overline{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
such that
v=\lambda E
. Since the RHS of Eq. (
4.24
) is invariant under the scaling of
\lambda
, it must also hold that

\displaystyle\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 2.0417pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle=\log\sup_{E\in\overline{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\frac{\Tr[E\rho]}{\Tr[E\sigma]}=\sup_{M=(E^{1},E^{2})\in\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\log\max_{i\in\{1,2\}}\frac{\Tr[E^{i}\rho]}{\Tr[E^{i}\sigma]}\;.

The last equality holds due to the condition that
E\in\overline{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
implies
\mathds{1}-E\in\overline{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
.
We will now derive the same expression for the computational measured max-divergence.
Let
M=(E^{1},E^{2})\in\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
be a binary measurement with associated measurement map
\mathcal{M}
, then it is easy to check that the condition
\mathcal{M}\left(\rho\right)\leq\lambda\mathcal{M}\left(\sigma\right)
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\lambda\geq\max_{i\in\{1,2\}}\frac{\Tr[E^{i}\rho]}{\Tr[E^{i}\sigma]}\;.

Hence it follows that

\displaystyle\inf\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}|\mathcal{M}\left(\rho\right)\leq\lambda\mathcal{M}\left(\sigma\right)\}=\max_{i\in\{1,2\}}\frac{\Tr[E^{i}\rho]}{\Tr[E^{i}\sigma]}\;.

Combining these observations with
4.9
, we directly find that

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}_{\text{max}}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle=\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}_{\text{max}}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle=\sup_{M=(E^{1},E^{2})\in\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\log\inf\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}|\mathcal{M}\left(\rho\right)\leq\lambda\mathcal{M}\left(\sigma\right)\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{M=(E^{1},E^{2})\in\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\log\max_{i\in\{1,2\}}\frac{\Tr[E^{i}\rho]}{\Tr[E^{i}\sigma]}

\displaystyle=\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 2.0417pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}(\rho\|\sigma)\;,

which concludes the proof.
∎

One interesting consequence of
Theorem

4.12
can be stated as follows. If two sets of measurements generate the same cone, then their corresponding measured max-divergences are also equal to each other. As we discussed in
Section

3
the cone
\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{eff}}}_{n}
may contain POVM elements which are on their own not efficiently preparable, however, due to the above argument, they do not aid in distinguishability when using the max-divergence as a distance measure.

Remark 4.13
.

That these two divergences —one defined via the geometric construction of cones of efficient effect operators, and the other via the optimization over measured
\alpha=\infty
-Rényi divergences— are equivalent is a priori not clear in general. Let us consider a slightly more general setting where one starts with a family of admissable (not necessarily binary) POVMs
\mathbf{M}
and generates from this the cone
\mathcal{C}^{E}
as the smallest cone that contains all effect operators (POVM elements) appearing in all
M\in\mathbf{M}
. This is, among others, the setting of
[
RSB24
]
. Then, if one defines these two max-divergences w.r.t. these objects, one can show that the measured max-divergence is always upper bounded by the conical max-divergence; see
[
RSB24
, Proposition 13]
. In other words, the “
\leq
” inequality of (
4.23
) is always true. Intuitively, this is because every POVM element is also element of the cone
\mathcal{C}^{E}
. The other inequality and thus equality, however, may not always be true. This direction requires an extra condition. Up to rescaling, every element of the cone
\mathcal{C}^{E}
needs to either be an effect operator that appears in some binary POVM in
\mathbf{M}
or needs to be expressible as a convex mixture of such binary measurements. In our case this is not an issue, since we consider only binary POVMs.
However, if one, e.g., considers
\mathbf{M}
to be the set of LO measurements, which are necessarily generally non-binary, then the above proof of equality generally breaks down
[
RSB24
]
. This is because generating the required two-output measurements would require classical communication, which is not allowed.

4.3
Regularized Computational Measured Rényi Divergences

Let us define the regularized versions of the computational max-divergence and the computational measured Rényi divergences. We now pass from finite-block, per-instance quantities to
asymptotic rates
. In computational settings, an efficient tester is constrained not only in circuit size but also in the number of samples; accordingly, we adopt a
poly-sample
regime in which the number of copies satisfies
m(k)\in\mathsf{poly}(k)
. Since we are studying asymptotic behavior, we henceforth consider families of states
\{\rho_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. Again, we fix families of efficient measurements
\{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. While the single-instance measures are evaluated at each fixed
k
, the regularized quantities are meaningful primarily at the family level.

Definition 4.14

(Regularized Computational Max-divergence)
.

Given two family of states
\{\rho_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}},

\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in\{\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

regularized computational max-divergence
when
m(k)\in\mathsf{poly}(k)
is given by,

\displaystyle\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 2.0417pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}^{\infty}(\{\rho_{k}\}_{k}\|\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k})\coloneqq\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{m(k)}\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 2.0417pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}(\rho_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}\|\sigma_{k}^{\otimes m(k)})\;.

(4.25)

For the simple case in which
\rho_{k}=\rho^{\otimes k}
(
\sigma_{k}=\sigma^{\otimes k}
) for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, this regularization (denoted by
\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 2.0417pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}^{\infty}(\rho\|\sigma)
) admits a convenient equivalent form.

Lemma 4.15
.

Given two quantum states
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
such that, their regularized computational max-divergence can be expressed as,

\displaystyle\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 1.53334pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}^{\infty}(\rho\|\sigma)=\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{m}\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 1.53334pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}(\rho_{k}^{\otimes m}\|\sigma_{k}^{\otimes m})=\sup_{m>0}\frac{1}{m}\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 1.53334pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}(\rho_{k}^{\otimes m}\|\sigma_{k}^{\otimes m})\;.

(4.26)

Proof.

The proof follows from the super-additivity property of the computational max-divergence (see
4.3
) together with Fekete’s lemma.
∎

Definition 4.16

(Regularized Computational Measured Rényi Divergences)
.

Given two families of states
\{\rho_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}},\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in\{\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, for any
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty)
their
regularized computational measured Rényi divergence
when
m(k)\in\mathsf{poly}(k)
is given by,

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\alpha}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}},\infty}(\{\rho_{k}\}_{k}\|\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k})\coloneqq\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{m(k)}\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\alpha}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{k\cdot m(k)}}(\rho_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}\|\sigma_{k}^{\otimes m(k)})\;.

(4.27)

Definition 4.17

(Regularized Computational Measured Relative Entropy)
.

Given two families of quantum states
\{\rho_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}},\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in\{\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, their
regularized computational measured relative entropy
is given by,

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}},\infty}(\{\rho_{k}\}_{k}\|\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k})\coloneqq\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{m(k)}\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{k\cdot m(k)}}(\rho_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}\|\sigma_{k}^{\otimes m(k)})\;.

(4.28)

Remark 4.18
.

In fact, for binary measurements super-additivity fails for general

MH23
]
. It remains an interesting open question whether the regularized computational measured relative entropy can be equivalently expressed as a supremum, similar to the computational max-divergence.

We conclude by relating the various regularized divergences to each other.

Lemma 4.19
.

For any two families of quantum states
\{\rho_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}},\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in\{\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
,
\alpha\in(0,1)

{\beta\in(1,\infty)}

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.51114pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}},\infty}_{\alpha}(\{\rho_{k}\}_{k}\|\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k})\leq\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.51114pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}},\infty}(\{\rho_{k}\}_{k}\|\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k})\leq\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.51114pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}},\infty}_{\beta}(\{\rho_{k}\}_{k}\|\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k})\leq\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.51114pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\max}^{\infty}(\{\rho_{k}\}_{k}\|\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k})\;.

(4.29)

Proof.

This follows directly from the monotonicity property provided in
4.10
.

4.4
Computational Fidelity

In this section we define the
computational (binary) fidelity
using the approach from
[
RKW11
]
. This is the computational analog to the
fidelity

[
Tom16
,
Uhl76
,
Fuc96
]
between two post-measurement single-bit outputs and measures the similarity between two quantum states when restricted to binary efficient measurements. Additionally, we show that the resulting fidelity is effectively the computational measured Rényi divergence for
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
. Lastly, we relate it to the
computational trace distance
and the
computational max-divergence

[
RKW11

Definition 4.20

(Computational (Binary) Fidelity)
.

Given any two quantum states
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{F}(\rho,\sigma)\coloneqq\inf_{M=(E_{1},E_{2})\in\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2}\sqrt{\Tr[E_{i}\rho]\Tr[E_{i}\sigma]}\right)^{2}\;.

(4.30)

Lemma 4.21

(Computational Fidelity and Computational Measured Rényi Divergence)
.

Given two quantum states
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})
, it follows that

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.51114pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{1/2}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\rho\|\sigma)=-\log\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.25557pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{F}(\rho,\sigma)\,.

(4.31)

Proof.

The proof can be found in
Section

B.3

∎

Since the computational fidelity is related to the computational measured Rényi divergence for
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
, due to
4.9
it automatically follows that

\displaystyle\inf_{M=(E_{1},E_{2})\in\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2}\sqrt{\Tr[E_{i}\rho]\Tr[E_{i}\sigma]}\right)^{2}=\inf_{M=(E_{1},E_{2})\in\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2}\sqrt{\Tr[E_{i}\rho]\Tr[E_{i}\sigma]}\right)^{2}\;,

(4.32)

meaning that one could have equivalently defined the computational fidelity by solely optimizing over measurements

The computational fidelity and computational trace distance additionally straightforwardly satisfy a Fuchs-van de Graaf type inequality.

Lemma 4.22

(Computational Fuchs-van de Graaf)
.

Given two quantum states
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})

\displaystyle 1-\sqrt{\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.25557pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{F}(\rho,\sigma)}\leq\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{\Delta}(\rho,\sigma)\leq\sqrt{1-\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.25557pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{F}(\rho,\sigma)}

(4.33)

Proof.

These inequalities follow directly from
[
RKW11
, Proposition 19]
applied to our family of informationally complete polynomially generated set of binary POVMs
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
. For completeness, we include a proof in
Section

B.4
.
∎

We conclude this section by relating the fidelity to the computational max-divergence. Applying the computational Fuchs-van de Graaf inequality to this bound then yields a result similar to
4.6
.

Lemma 4.23
.

Given two quantum states
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.25557pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{F}(\rho,\sigma)\geq 2^{-\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.51114pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\textup{max}}(\rho\|\sigma)}\;.

By monotonicity of the measured Rényi

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{1/2}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\rho\|\sigma)\leq\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\max}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\rho\|\sigma)=\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\text{max}}(\rho\|\sigma)\;.

An explicit cryptographic separation

We now exhibit an explicit pair of states that witnesses a sharp separation between the
computational
and
informational
divergences. The same calculations can be extended to other cryptographic constructions which exhibit a gap between the computational trace distance and the informational counterpart. Nevertheless, due to its simplicity, we focus on the pseudoentanglement construction proposed by

\displaystyle\psi^{AB}_{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{4}\left(\outerproduct{\Phi^+}{\Phi^+}^{AB}+\outerproduct{\Phi^-}{\Phi^-}^{AB}\right)\otimes\left(\rho^{A}_{0,n}+\rho^{A}_{1,n}\right)\,,

(4.36)

\displaystyle\phi^{AB}_{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left(\outerproduct{\Phi^+}{\Phi^+}^{AB}\otimes\rho^{A}_{0,n}+\outerproduct{\Phi^-}{\Phi^-}^{AB}\otimes\rho^{A}_{1,n}\right)\,,

(4.37)

where
\ket{\Phi^+}^{AB}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{00}^{AB}+\ket{11}^{AB})

\ket{\Phi^-}^{AB}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{00}^{AB}-\ket{11}^{AB})
and
\{\rho^{A}_{0,n},\rho^{A}_{1,n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are EFI pairs.

9

9

An EFI pair

]
consists of two efficiently generated families of quantum states which are far in trace distance (
\Delta(\rho_{0,n},\rho_{1,n})\approx 1
), which are indistinguishable by computationally bounded adversaries given
m\in\mathsf{poly}(n)
copies , i.e.,
\left|\operatorname{Pr}\left[\mathcal{D}(\sigma_{n},\rho_{n,0}^{\otimes m})=1]-\operatorname{Pr}[\mathcal{D}(\sigma_{n},\rho_{n,1}^{\otimes m})=1\right]\right|\leq\nu(n)
.

Assuming EFI pairs exist,
[
GE24
]
proves

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{\Delta}(\psi^{AB}_{n},\phi^{AB}_{n})\leq\mathsf{negl}(n)\,.

Therefore, by the computational Fuchs–van de Graaf inequality (

\displaystyle 1-\mathsf{negl}(n)\leq\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{F}(\psi^{AB}_{n},\phi^{AB}_{n})\leq 1\,.

(4.38)

This allows us to calculate the following upper bound for the computational measured Rényi divergence for
\alpha\in(0,1/2)

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}_{\alpha}(\psi^{AB}_{n},\phi^{AB}_{n})\leq-\log( 1-\negl(n))\;,

(4.39)

which follows from
4.21
together with the monotonicity property of the measured Rényi divergences (
4.10

Nevertheless, in the information-theoretic setting one has, by the Pinsker inequality, e.g.,
[

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(\psi^{AB}_{n},\phi^{AB}_{n})\gtrsim\frac{\min\{1,\alpha\}}{8\ln 2}\,,

since the information-theoretic trace distance satisfies
\Delta(\psi^{AB}_{n},\phi^{AB}_{n})\approx 1/2
and the fidelity is bounded away from

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\leq F(\psi^{AB}_{n},\phi^{AB}_{n})\leq\frac{3}{4}\,.

(4.40)

Therefore, under standard hardness assumptions, the computational measures collapse

(\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{\Delta}(\psi^{AB}_{n},\phi^{AB}_{n})\leq\mathsf{negl}(n)
,
1-\mathsf{negl}(n)\leq\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{F}(\psi^{AB}_{n},\phi^{AB}_{n})\leq 1
, and
\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\alpha}(\psi^{AB}_{n},\phi^{AB}_{n})^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\leq\mathsf{negl}(n)
for
\alpha\leq\tfrac{1}{2}

\Theta(1)
.

5.1

Let us consider the problem of binary hypothesis testing, where one is tasked with discriminating between two input states. The main result of this section is a rigorous study of the optimal type II error decay rate in asymmetric hypothesis testing, commonly known as the Stein’s exponent, when restricted to efficient binary measurements
[
HP91

ON00

In binary quantum hypothesis testing, the goal is to discriminate between two quantum states; one of the states,
\rho

H_{0}

\sigma

alternate hypothesis

\{\rho_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of which we are given
m(k)\in\mathsf{poly}(k)
polynomially many copies.

Thus, we study the distinguishing rate in the asymptotic limit, i.e., when
k\rightarrow\infty
given
m(k)
copies of
\rho_{k},\sigma_{k}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k})
under the restriction that the observer is constrained to implement efficient binary measurements. We say that the observer determines the system to be in state
\rho_{k}
, instead of
\sigma_{k}
if the performed measurement
M
outputs
0
. Conversely, if the output is
1
, the observer assumes that they initially held
m(k)
copies of
\sigma_{k}
.

For any
k,m(k)\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{k\cdot m(k)}
denote the set of efficient binary measurements acting on
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k\cdot m(k)}
. For any feasible binary measurement
{M=(E_{k\cdot m(k)},\mathds{1}-E_{k\cdot m(k)})\in\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{k\cdot m(k)}}
, there are two types of errors that arise:

•

Type-I error
: the probability of incorrectly rejecting the null hypothesis (i.e., mistakenly inferring
\sigma_{k}
when the state is actually
\rho_{k}
). It is hence defined as

\displaystyle\alpha_{m(k)}(M)\coloneqq\Tr[\rho_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}(\mathds{1}-E_{k\cdot m(k)})]\;.

(5.1)

•

Type-II error
: the probability of incorrectly accepting the null hypothesis (i.e., mistakenly inferring
\rho_{k}
when the state is actually
\sigma_{k}
). It is given by

\displaystyle\beta_{m(k)}(M)\coloneqq\Tr[\sigma_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}E_{k\cdot m(k)}]\;.

(5.2)

We are primarily interested in the
asymmetric
setting, where the aim is to minimize the Type-II error while the Type-I error is constrained to not exceed a prescribed threshold
\varepsilon\in(0,1)

[
HP91
,
ON00
]
. Formally, one defines

\displaystyle\beta^{\varepsilon}_{m(k)}(\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{k\cdot m(k)})\coloneqq\min_{M\in\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{k\cdot m(k)}}\{\beta_{m(k)}(M)\,|\,\alpha_{m(k)}(M)\leq\varepsilon\}\;,

(5.3)

to be the optimal type-II error given a type-I error bound by
\varepsilon
. As mentioned above, our goal is to characterize the scaling of
\beta^{\varepsilon}_{m(k)}(\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{k\cdot m(k)})
in the asymptotic regime. In this setting, the optimal Type-II error probability is expected to decay exponentially with the number of copies
m(k)=\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{poly}(n))
, i.e.,
\beta_{m(k)}^{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{k\cdot m(k)})\sim 2^{-\xi^{\mathrm{eff}}m(k)+o(m(k))}
,
where the optimal (asymptotic) error exponent can be expressed as,

\displaystyle\xi^{\mathrm{eff}}=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}-\frac{1}{m(k)}\log\beta^{\varepsilon}_{m(k)}(\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{k\cdot m(k)})\;.

(5.4)

If one adopts the approach from
[
YHKH+22
,
MKH+25
]
, then this exponent can be expressed via the complexity relative entropy, which we define below for completeness.

Definition 5.1

(Complexity Relative Entropy
[
MKH+25
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
be the set of efficient effect operators on
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}
that can be generated using at most
p(n)
elementary gates. Then for any two positive semidefinite operators
\rho,\sigma\in\text{Pos}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})
and any
\eta\geq 0
, the
complexity relative entropy
is given by:

\displaystyle D_{\text{H}}^{p(n),\eta}\left(\rho\|\sigma\right)\coloneqq-\log\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}E\in\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\\
\Tr\left[E\rho\right]\geq\eta\end{subarray}}\frac{\Tr\left[E\sigma\right]}{\Tr\left[E\rho\right]}\;.

(5.5)

It then directly follows from
[
MKH+25
, Proposition 1]
that the optimal type II error is expressed as

\displaystyle\beta^{\varepsilon}_{m(k)}(\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{k\cdot m(k)})=\left(1-\varepsilon\right)2^{-D_{\text{H}}^{p(k\cdot m(k)),1-\varepsilon}(\rho_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}\|\sigma_{k}^{\otimes m(k)})}\;.

This yields an optimal error exponent of

\displaystyle\xi^{\mathrm{eff}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}-\frac{\log(1-\varepsilon)}{m(k)}+\frac{D_{\text{H}}^{p(k\cdot m(k)),1-\varepsilon}(\rho_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}\|\sigma_{k}^{\otimes m(k)})}{m(k)}

(5.6)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{D_{\text{H}}^{p(k\cdot m(k)),1-\varepsilon}(\rho_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}\|\sigma_{k}^{\otimes m(k)})}{m(k)}\;.

(5.7)

Here, our main contribution is
Theorem

5.2
, which shows that the optimal error exponent is
bounded by the regularized computational measured relative entropy. In doing so, we also establish a connection between the computational measured Rényi divergences of this work and the complexity relative entropy from
[
MKH+25
]
.

Theorem 5.2

(Computational Stein’s Lemma)
.

Let
\{\rho_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}},\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in\{\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be two families of quantum states and let
\{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a family of informationally complete polynomially generated set of binary POVMs. For any
0<\varepsilon<1
, the optimal type II error exponent
\xi^{\mathrm{eff}}(\{\rho_{k}\}_{k}\|\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k})
for testing
H_{0}:\sigma_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}
against
H_{1}:\rho_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}

\displaystyle\xi^{\mathrm{eff}}(\{\rho_{k}\}_{k}\|\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k})\coloneqq\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\limsup_{k\to\infty}-\frac{1}{m(k)}\log\beta_{m(k)}^{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{k\cdot m(k)})\leq\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.51114pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}},\infty}(\{\rho_{k}\}_{k}\|\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k})\;,

(5.8)

where
\beta_{m(k)}^{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{k\cdot m(k)})
is the minimal type II error given the type I error
\leq\varepsilon
, and
\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.51114pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}},\infty}(\{\rho_{k}\}_{k}\|\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k})
is the regularized computational measured relative entropy.

Proof.

The proof follows from the general result of
[
BaHLP14
]
. We adapt it to the polynomially constrained scenario and include it in
Section

C.1
for completeness.
∎

In the simple case, where
\rho,\sigma
are fixed and one distinguishes
\rho^{\otimes m}
from
\sigma^{\otimes m}
, this reduces to

\displaystyle\xi^{\mathrm{eff}}(\rho\|\sigma)\coloneqq\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\limsup_{m\to\infty}-\frac{1}{m}\log\beta_{m}^{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{m})\leq\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}},\infty}(\rho\|\sigma)\;.

(5.9)

This bound is tight for the original quantum Stein’s lemma
[

]
, which one
recovers by removing the computational constraints on

. Moreover, it immediately follows from

.

5.2
Computational Resource Theory

Properties of quantum systems such as entanglement or magic can be studied
systematically by
quantum resource theories

[
CG19
]
.
A resource theory is a pair

\mathcal{R}=(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{O})
where, for any Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}
,

•

\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H})\subseteq\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
is the set of

free states
on
\mathcal{H}
, i.e., those that lack the resource and

•

\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})\subseteq\textup{CPTP}(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})
is the set of

free operations
.

A free operation,
\Lambda\!\in\!\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})
, must minimally satisfy
\Lambda(\sigma)\!\in\!\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H}_{2})

for every

\sigma\!\in\!\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
. When studying, e.g. entanglement as a resource, one chooses
\mathcal{F}=\mathrm{SEP}
(separable states) and
\mathcal{O}=\mathrm{LOCC}
(local operations + classical communication).

When restricted to polynomially implementable operations, families of states with high resource content can become computationally indistinguishable from
low-resource families. This phenomenon, known as pseudoresourced quantum states
[
HBK24
,
BMB+24
,
GY25
]
has a direct consequence: when constrained to polynomially implementable operations, information-theoretic resource measures will generally not be operationally meaningful. For entanglement there exist, e.g., exponential separations between information-theoretic and computational measures
[
ABF+23
,
ABV23
,
LREJ25
]
. Nevertheless, to the best of our knowledge, no general measure for polynomially-constrained resources has been proposed in the literature.

There exist several measures for quantifying resources in quantum information theory, ranging from geometric to witness-based approaches. Typically, these measures assess the resource content of a state by evaluating its “distance” from the set of free states. In this section, we focus on entropic measures, defining the computational counterpart of the
measured relative entropy of resource
and the
max-relative entropy of resource
. Throughout this section,
\mathcal{O}_{n}(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})
will denote the free operations realized by poly-size circuits, i.e., the “efficient” free operations from the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{1}
to
\mathcal{H}_{2}
. In the same way,
\mathcal{F}_{n}\equiv\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})
represents the set of free states, i.e. the ones without the resource.

Definition 5.3

(Set of Efficient Free Quantum Operations)
.

Given
\{\mathbf{E}_{n}^{\text{eff},(i)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}},
where

{\mathbf{E}_{n}^{\text{eff},(i)}\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{i}^{\otimes n})}
are families of informationally complete polynomially generated sets of effect operators on, respectively systems
i=1,2
, with possibly different polynomials. On the sets of free states
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\otimes n})
and
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H}_{2}^{\otimes n})
, a CPTP map
\Lambda_{n}\in\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}_{1},\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}_{2})
is said to be an
efficient free quantum operation
if the following two conditions hold.

1.

State condition:

\Lambda_{n}(\sigma)\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H}_{2}^{\otimes n})
for all
\sigma\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\otimes n})
.

2.

Test stability:

\Lambda_{n}^{*}({\mathbf{E}}^{\text{eff}}_{n}(\mathcal{H}_{2}^{\otimes{n}}))\subseteq\overline{\mathbf{E}}^{\text{eff}}_{n}(\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\otimes{n}})
.

Therefore, the set of free operations can be seen as quantum channels that map free states into free states, not generating the studied resource. The main difference with respect to the information-theoretic case lies in the added complexity constraint: these transformations cannot generate more complexity either. Let us now define computational resource distance measures through our proposed computational divergences.

Definition 5.4

(Computational Measured Relative Entropy of Resource)
.

Given a quantum state
\rho\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})
, its
computational measured relative entropy of resource
is given by,

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\mathcal{F}_{n}}(\rho):=\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\rho\|\sigma)\,.

(5.10)

Definition 5.5

(Computational Max-Relative Entropy of Resource)
.

Given a quantum state
\rho\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})
, its
computational max-relative entropy of resource
is given by,

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max},\mathcal{F}_{n}}(\rho):=\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 2.0417pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}(\rho\|\sigma)\,.

(5.11)

In the information-theoretic setting, the (unmeasured) relative entropy of resource and the smoothed max-relative entropy have standard operational roles: the former captures asymptotic hypothesis-testing/interconversion rates, while the latter characterizes one-shot formation/distillation (e.g., entanglement cost and distillable entanglement) under non-entangling or catalytic transformations
[
Dat09b
,
BD11
,
LBT19
,
RFWG19
,
RW20
]
. In the computational setting, a full operational characterization remains open. Nevertheless, by parallelism with the unconstrained case, we adopt their measured and restricted counterparts. Moreover, they satisfy the crucial properties of resource monotones under our allowed maps, as we now prove.

Lemma 5.6
.

Assuming that the set of free states
\mathcal{F}_{n}=\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})
is closed, the computational resource measures
\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip-0.25557pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{\mathbb{D}}\in\{\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.51114pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D},\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 1.53334pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}\}
that we have defined satisfy the following properties,

•

Faithfulness
: given a quantum state
\rho\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})
, its resource measure
\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.51114pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\mathcal{F}_{n}}(\rho)
is zero iff
\rho\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})
.

•

Monotonicity:
For any free operation
\Lambda_{n}\in\mathcal{O}_{n}(\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\otimes n},\mathcal{H}_{2}^{\otimes n})
,

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip-0.25557pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{\mathbb{D}}_{\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H}_{2}^{\otimes n})}(\Lambda_{n}(\rho))\leq\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip-0.25557pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{\mathbb{D}}_{\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\otimes n})}(\rho)\,.

(5.12)

Proof.

On the one hand, if
\rho\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})
, then it naturally holds that

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip-0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{\mathbb{D}}_{\mathcal{F}_{n}}(\rho)=0\;.

(5.13)

The other direction which is necessary to prove faithfulness then directly follows for
\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\max}
from
4.6
and for the measured relative entropy measures from
4.11
together with the fact that the computational trace distance
\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{\Delta}
is a norm and the set
\mathcal{F}_{n}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})
is closed.
For the monotonicity property, it follows from the state condition of
Definition

5.3
that,

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip-0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{\mathbb{D}}_{\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H}_{2}^{\otimes n})}(\Lambda_{n}(\rho))=\hskip-5.69046pt\min_{\tau\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H}_{2}^{\otimes n})}\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip-0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{\mathbb{D}}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\Lambda_{n}(\rho)\|\tau)\leq\hskip-5.69046pt\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\otimes n})}\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip-0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{\mathbb{D}}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\Lambda_{n}(\rho)\|\Lambda_{n}(\sigma))\leq\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip-0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{\mathbb{D}}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\Lambda_{n}(\rho)\|\Lambda_{n}(\sigma))\,,

for
\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip-0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{\mathbb{D}}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\in\{\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}},\overset{\smash{\text{\tiny\hskip 2.0417pt\raisebox{-0.64583pt}{c}}}}{D}_{\mathrm{max}}\}
and any
\sigma\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\otimes n})
, since
\Lambda_{n}(\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\otimes n}))\subseteq\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H}_{2}^{\otimes n})
. Then, by the test stability property of
Definition

5.3
together with Property 4 of
4.10
(see also
[
RSB24
, Lemma 5]
) it follows that, for all
\sigma\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\otimes n})
,

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip-0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{\mathbb{D}}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\Lambda_{n}(\rho)\|\Lambda_{n}(\sigma))\leq\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip-0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{\mathbb{D}}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\rho\|\sigma)\,.

The proof then concludes by taking the minimum.
∎

For the case of the computational measured relative entropy, it follows from
[
SW23
, Theorem 11]
that it a satisfies asymptotic continuity with respect to the trace norm. Concretely, for any convex set
\mathcal{F}_{n}
and assuming
\frac{1}{2}\|\rho-\rho^{\prime}\|_{1}\leq\varepsilon
it then holds that

\displaystyle\left|\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\mathcal{F}_{n}}(\rho)-\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\mathcal{F}_{n}}(\rho^{\prime})\right|\leq g(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon\sup_{M\in\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\kappa_{M}\,

(5.14)

where
\kappa_{M}=\sup_{\mu,\nu}\left|D_{\mathcal{F}_{n}}^{M}(\mu)-D_{\mathcal{F}_{n}}^{M}(\nu)\right|
with
D_{\mathcal{F}_{n}}^{M}(\rho)\coloneqq\inf_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}D(\mathcal{M}_{M}(\rho)\|\mathcal{M}_{M}(\sigma))
is the maximal absolute difference of the measured relative entropy of resource between any two states and
g(\varepsilon)\coloneqq(1+\varepsilon)h\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\right)
and
h(\cdot)
is the binary entropy.

Since we demonstrate an exponential separation between the information-theoretic trace distance and its computational counterpart, continuity bounds phrased in the computational metric can be exponentially sharper. This makes the above bound in practice less useful and motivates a tighter relation between our computational divergences and the corresponding restricted trace distance—so that the right-hand side of the inequality depends only on a computational notion of distinguishability.
Theorem

5.7
provides exactly this: an asymptotic continuity bound for the computational measured relative entropy of resource in terms of the computational trace distance, furnishing a direct analytic bridge with clear operational meaning. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first continuity bound that relates a measurement-restricted divergence to its matching restricted trace distance, and it is likely of independent interest.

Theorem 5.7

(Asymptotic Continuity Bound for the Computational Measured Relative Entropy of Resource)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{n}\subset\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})
be closed, convex, and bounded containing a full rank state
\sigma^{\star}
.
For
\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})
and
\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{\Delta}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\|\rho-\rho^{\prime}\|_{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\leq\varepsilon
it holds that,

\displaystyle\big|\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.51114pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\mathcal{F}_{n}}(\rho)-\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.51114pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\mathcal{F}_{n}}(\rho^{\prime})\big|\leq(1+\varepsilon)h\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\right)+\varepsilon\left(\kappa+\log\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)\,,

(5.15)

where
h(\cdot)
is the binary entropy and
\kappa\coloneqq-\log(\lambda_{\textup{min}}(\sigma^*))>0
is the negative logarithm of the smallest eigenvalue of the full rank state
\sigma^{\star}
.

Proof.

In order to end up with the desired bound for
\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\mathcal{F}_{n}}
, we will first swap the infimum and supremum implicitly appearing in
\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\mathcal{F}_{n}}
. We do this by using
[
BaHLP14
, Lemma 13]
, see also
[
SW23
, Lemma 10]
, which, however, a priori does not directly apply to our situation, since the set of measurement maps
\mathcal{M}
corresponding to binary POVMs
\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
is not closed under flagged finite convex combinations. We say a set
\mathscr{M}
of CPTP maps is closed under flagged finite convex combinations
[
SW23
,
BaHLP14
]
if for any finite probability distribution
\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
and any
\mathcal{M}_{i}\in\mathscr{M}
it follows that
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\mathcal{M}_{i}\in\mathscr{M}
. So as a first step we define

\displaystyle\mathscr{M}^{\text{eff}}_{n}\coloneqq\left\{\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\mathcal{M}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\mathcal{M}_{i}\otimes|i\rangle\langle i|\,\Bigg|\,m\in\mathbb{N},\,p_{i}\geq 0,\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}=1,\,\mathcal{M}_{i}\equiv\mathcal{M}_{M_{i}},\,M_{i}\in\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\right\}\;,

where here
\mathcal{M}_{i}\equiv\mathcal{M}_{M_{i}}
is the measurement channel implementing the binary POVM
M_{i}\in\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
.
This set of flagged measurement channels is now closed under flagged finite convex combinations by construction, and so it follows by
[
SW23
, Lemma 10]
that

\displaystyle\inf_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sup_{\mathcal{M}\in\mathscr{M}^{\text{eff}}_{n}}D(\mathcal{M}(\rho)\|\mathcal{M}(\sigma))=\sup_{\mathcal{M}\in\mathscr{M}^{\text{eff}}_{n}}\inf_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}D(\mathcal{M}(\rho)\|\mathcal{M}(\sigma))\;.

Importantly by expanding our set of measurement channels we do not change our objective values. It holds that

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathcal{M}\in\mathscr{M}^{\text{eff}}_{n}}D(\mathcal{M}(\rho)\|\mathcal{M}(\sigma))=\sup_{M\in\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}D(\mathcal{M}_{M}(\rho)\|\mathcal{M}_{M}(\sigma))\,.

(5.16)

To prove this one easily checks that for a channel
\mathcal{M}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\mathcal{M}_{i}
it holds that

D(\mathcal{M}(\rho)\|\mathcal{M}(\sigma))=\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}D(\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho)\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma))
which is an average and hence the claimed Eq. (
5.16
) follows. Completely analogously one shows that

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathcal{M}\in\mathscr{M}_{n}^{\text{eff}}}\|\mathcal{M}(\rho)-\mathcal{M}(\rho^{\prime})\|_{1}=\sup_{M\in\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\|\mathcal{M}_{M}(\rho)-\mathcal{M}_{M}(\rho^{\prime})\|_{1}=2\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{\Delta}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})\;.

(5.17)

Now putting these observations together we get

\displaystyle\big|\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\mathcal{F}_{n}}(\rho)-\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\mathcal{F}_{n}}(\rho^{\prime})\big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sup_{M\in\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}D(\mathcal{M}_{M}(\rho)\|\mathcal{M}_{M}(\sigma))-\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sup_{M\in\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}D(\mathcal{M}_{M}(\rho^{\prime})\|\mathcal{M}_{M}(\sigma))\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sup_{\mathcal{M}\in\mathscr{M}^{\text{eff}}_{n}}D(\mathcal{M}(\rho)\|\mathcal{M}(\sigma))-\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sup_{\mathcal{M}\in\mathscr{M}^{\text{eff}}_{n}}D(\mathcal{M}(\rho^{\prime})\|\mathcal{M}(\sigma))\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\sup_{\mathcal{M}\in\mathscr{M}^{\text{eff}}_{n}}\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}D(\mathcal{M}(\rho)\|\mathcal{M}(\sigma))-\sup_{\mathcal{M}\in\mathscr{M}^{\text{eff}}_{n}}\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}D(\mathcal{M}(\rho^{\prime})\|\mathcal{M}(\sigma))\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\mathcal{M}\in\mathscr{M}^{\text{eff}}_{n}}\Big|\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}D(\mathcal{M}(\rho)\|\mathcal{M}(\sigma))-\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}D(\mathcal{M}(\rho^{\prime})\|\mathcal{M}(\sigma))\Big|

\displaystyle=\sup_{\mathcal{M}\in\mathscr{M}^{\text{eff}}_{n}}\Big|\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum^{m}_{i=1}p_{i}D(\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho)\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma))-\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}D(\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho^{\prime})\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma))\Big|\;,\qquad

(5.18)

where in the last line the supremum is over all
\mathcal{M}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\mathcal{M}_{i}
, where
\mathcal{M}_{i}
is a measurement channel corresponding to the binary POVM
M_{i}\in\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
.
Before we now continue to bound the last term of Eq. (
5.2
) —inspired by the proof of
[
Win16
, Lemma 7]
— we introduce some notation. Fix some
\mathcal{M}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\mathcal{M}_{i}\in\mathscr{M}_{n}^{\text{eff}}
and define

\delta\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{M}(\rho)-\mathcal{M}(\rho^{\prime})\|_{1}
,
\delta_{i}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho)-\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho^{\prime})\|_{1}
,
then it follows that

\displaystyle\delta=\sum_{i}p_{i}\delta_{i}\leq\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{\Delta}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})

and that
\eta_{i}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\delta_{i}}\left(\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho)-\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho^{\prime})\right)_{+}
and
\eta_{i}^{\prime}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\delta_{i}}\left(\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho^{\prime})-\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho)\right)_{+}

are both valid quantum states, where
(A)_{+}
is the projection onto the positive part of
A
. We can now bound

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho^{\prime})+(\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho)-\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho^{\prime})+\delta_{i}\eta_{i}

\displaystyle=(1+\delta_{i})\left(\frac{1}{1+\delta_{i}}\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho^{\prime})+\frac{\delta_{i}}{1+\delta_{i}}\eta_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=:(1+\delta_{i})\omega_{i}\;,

to get that

\displaystyle\omega_{i}=\frac{1}{1+\delta_{i}}\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho^{\prime})+\frac{\delta_{i}}{1+\delta_{i}}\eta_{i}=\frac{1}{1+\delta_{i}}\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho)+\frac{\delta_{i}}{1+\delta_{i}}\eta^{\prime}_{i}\;.

Since the relative entropy is jointly convex, we have that
\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum_{i}p_{i}(1+\delta_{i})D(\cdot\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma))
is a convex functional and hence

\displaystyle\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum_{i}p_{i}(1+\delta_{i})D(\omega_{i}\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma))\leq\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum_{i}p_{i}D(\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho^{\prime})\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma))+\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum_{i}p_{i}\delta_{i}D(\eta_{i}\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma))\;,

which rearranged gives

\displaystyle\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum_{i}p_{i}D(\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho^{\prime})\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma))\geq\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum_{i}p_{i}(1+\delta_{i})D(\omega_{i}\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma))-\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum_{i}p_{i}\delta_{i}D(\eta_{i}\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma))\;.

(5.19)

On the other hand, for any
\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
we have that,

\displaystyle D(\omega_{i}\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma))

\displaystyle=-S(\omega_{i})-\Tr[\omega_{i}\log(\MM_i(\sigma))]

\displaystyle\geq-h\left(\frac{\delta_{i}}{1+\delta_{i}}\right)-\frac{1}{1+\delta_{i}}S(\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho))-\frac{\delta_{i}}{1+\delta_{i}}S(\eta^{\prime}_{i})

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{1+\delta_{i}}\Tr[\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho)\log(\MM_i(\sigma)))]-\frac{\delta_{i}}{1+\delta_{i}}\Tr[\eta^{\prime}_{i}\log(\MM_i(\sigma))]

\displaystyle=-h\left(\frac{\delta_{i}}{1+\delta_{i}}\right)+\frac{1}{1+\delta_{i}}D(\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho)\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma))+\frac{\delta_{i}}{1+\delta_{i}}D(\eta^{\prime}_{i}\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma))\,,

where the first inequality is from
[
Win16
, Eq. (1)]
and follows from the strong sub-additivity of the von Neumann entropy.
Multiplying with
p_{i}(1+\delta_{i})
, then rearranging the terms, and subsequently taking the minimum yields

\displaystyle\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum_{i}p_{i}D(\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho)\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma))

\displaystyle\leq\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\left(\sum_{i}p_{i}(1+\delta_{i})D(\omega_{i}\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma))+\sum_{i}p_{i}(1+\delta_{i})h\left(\frac{\delta_{i}}{1+\delta_{i}}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.-\sum_{i}p_{i}\delta_{i}D(\eta^{\prime}_{i}\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma))\right)

\displaystyle\leq\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum_{i}p_{i}(1+\delta_{i})D(\omega_{i}\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma))

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{i}p_{i}(1+\delta_{i})h\left(\frac{\delta_{i}}{1+\delta_{i}}\right)-\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum_{i}p_{i}\delta_{i}D(\eta^{\prime}_{i}\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma))\;.

(5.20)

Putting both inequalities together we get that,

\displaystyle\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum_{i}p_{i}D(\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho)\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma))

\displaystyle-\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum_{i}p_{i}D(\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho^{\prime})\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma))\leq\sum_{i}p_{i}(1+\delta_{i})h\left(\frac{\delta_{i}}{1+\delta_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle+\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum_{i}p_{i}\delta_{i}D(\eta_{i}\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma))-\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum_{i}p_{i}\delta_{i}D(\eta_{i}^{\prime}\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma))\;.

(5.21)

Observing that swapping
\rho\leftrightarrow\rho^{\prime}
implies
\eta\leftrightarrow\eta^{\prime}
and thus effectively a minus term in the above inequality where
\rho,\rho^{\prime},\eta,\eta^{\prime}
yields the same inequality with absolute values. Then, we get

\displaystyle\Big|\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum_{i}p_{i}D(\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho)\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma))-\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum_{i}p_{i}D(\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho^{\prime})\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma))\Big|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sum_{i}p_{i}\left((1+\delta_{i})h\left(\frac{\delta_{i}}{1+\delta_{i}}\right)\right)+\left|\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum_{i}p_{i}\delta_{i}D(\eta_{i}\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma))-\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum_{i}p_{i}\delta_{i}D(\eta_{i}^{\prime}\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma))\right|

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{i}p_{i}\left((1+\delta_{i})h\left(\frac{\delta_{i}}{1+\delta_{i}}\right)\right)+\Bigg|\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum_{i}p_{i}\delta_{i}D\left(\frac{(\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho)-\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho^{\prime}))_{+}}{\delta_{i}}\Big\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 170.71652pt-\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum_{i}p_{i}\delta_{i}D\left(\frac{(\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho^{\prime})-\mathcal{M}_{i}(\rho))_{+}}{\delta_{i}}\Big\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma)\right)\Bigg|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sum_{i}p_{i}\left((1+\delta_{i})h\left(\frac{\delta_{i}}{1+\delta_{i}}\right)\right)+\kappa_{\mathscr{M}}\,,

(5.22)

where in the second equality we substituted by the definitions of
\eta_{i},\eta^{\prime}_{i}
and, lastly, defined

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mathscr{M}}\coloneqq\sup_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})}\Bigg[

\displaystyle\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum_{i}p_{i}\delta_{i}D\left(\frac{(\mathcal{M}_{i}(\tau)-\mathcal{M}_{i}(\tau^{\prime}))_{+}}{\delta_{i}}\big\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum_{i}p_{i}\delta_{i}D\left(\frac{(\mathcal{M}_{i}(\tau^{\prime})-\mathcal{M}_{i}(\tau))_{+}}{\delta_{i}}\big\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma)\right)\Bigg]\;,

which doesn’t require absolute values since exchanging
\tau\leftrightarrow\tau^{\prime}
gives a global minus sign.

We bound this last term in the following way,

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mathscr{M}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})}\Bigg[\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum_{i}p_{i}\delta_{i}D\left(\frac{(\mathcal{M}_{i}(\tau)-\mathcal{M}_{i}(\tau^{\prime}))_{+}}{\delta_{i}}\big\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum_{i}p_{i}\delta_{i}D\left(\frac{(\mathcal{M}_{i}(\tau^{\prime})-\mathcal{M}_{i}(\tau))_{+}}{\delta_{i}}\Bigg\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma)\right)\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}}\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum_{i}p_{i}\delta_{i}D\left(\frac{(\mathcal{M}_{i}(\tau)-\mathcal{M}_{i}(\tau^{\prime}))_{+}}{\delta_{i}}\Bigg\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}}\sum_{i}p_{i}\delta_{i}D\left(\frac{(\mathcal{M}_{i}(\tau)-\mathcal{M}_{i}(\tau^{\prime}))_{+}}{\delta_{i}}\Bigg\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma^{\star})\right)

\displaystyle\leq-\sum_{i}p_{i}\delta_{i}\log\lambda_{\text{min}}(\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma^{\star}))

\displaystyle\leq\kappa\cdot\delta+\sum_{i}p_{i}\delta_{i}\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta_{i}}\right),

(5.23)

where in the second inequality we upper bounded by plugging in the full rank state
\sigma^{\star}
. The third inequality follows from the fact that
\tilde{\tau}_{i}=\frac{(\mathcal{M}_{i}(\tau)-\mathcal{M}_{i}(\tau^{\prime}))_{+}}{\delta_{i}}
is a valid quantum state and hence the inequality
D(\tilde{\tau}_{i}\|\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma^{\star}))\leq-\log\lambda_{\text{min}}(\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma^{\star}))
holds.
The fourth inequality above stems from the following lower bound

\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{min}}(\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma^{\star}))

\displaystyle=\min\{\Tr[E_{i}\sigma^{\star}],\Tr[(\mathds{1}-E_{i})\sigma^{\star}]\}

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{\text{min}}(\sigma^{\star})\cdot\min\{\Tr[E_{i}],\Tr[\mathds{1}-E_{i}]\}

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{\text{min}}(\sigma^{\star})\cdot\frac{\delta_{i}}{2},

(5.24)

where
M_{i}=(E_{i},\mathds{1}-E_{i})
and
where the last inequality itself follows from the following observation. We have

\displaystyle\delta_{i}

\displaystyle=|\Tr[E_{i}(\rho-\rho^{\prime})]|\leq\|E_{i}\|_{\infty}\|\rho-\rho^{\prime}\|_{1}\leq 2\Tr[E_{i}],

\displaystyle\delta_{i}

\displaystyle=|\Tr[(\mathds{1}-E_{i})(\rho-\rho^{\prime})]|\leq\|\mathds{1}-E_{i}\|_{\infty}\|\rho-\rho^{\prime}\|_{1}\leq 2\Tr[\mathds{1}-E_{i}],

which imply that
\min\{\Tr[E_{i}],\Tr[\mathds{1}-E_{i}]\}\geq\frac{\delta_{i}}{2}
.
Now setting
\kappa\coloneqq-\log\lambda_{\text{min}}(\sigma^{\star})
, recalling
\delta=\sum_{i}p_{i}\delta_{i}
and applying the logarithm yields the desired inequality.

Now putting everything together we have that,

\displaystyle\big|\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\mathcal{F}_{n}}(\rho)-\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\mathcal{F}_{n}}(\rho^{\prime})\big|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\mathcal{M}\in\mathscr{M}^{\text{eff}}_{n}}\sum_{i}p_{i}\underbrace{\left[(1+\delta_{i})h\left(\frac{\delta_{i}}{1+\delta_{i}}\right)+\delta_{i}\left(\kappa+\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta_{i}}\right)\right)\right]}_{=:f(\delta_{i})}

\displaystyle\equiv\sup_{\mathcal{M}\in\mathscr{M}^{\text{eff}}_{n}}\sum_{i}p_{i}f(\delta_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\mathcal{M}\in\mathscr{M}^{\text{eff}}_{n}}f\left(\sum_{i}p_{i}\delta_{i}\right)=f(\delta)\leq f(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=(1+\varepsilon)h\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\right)+\varepsilon\left(\kappa+\log\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\right)\right),

(5.25)

where we have used concavity and monotonicity, since
f(\delta_{i})\coloneqq(1+\delta_{i})h\left(\frac{\delta_{i}}{1+\delta_{i}}\right)+\delta_{i}\left(\kappa+\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta_{i}}\right)\right):[0,1]\to[0,3+\kappa]
is a positive, continuous, concave, and monotonically increasing function.
∎

Win16
]

contains at least one full-rank state. For example, in the case of the entanglement resource,

and so

.
Therefore, for the
computational measured relative entropy of resource
another property is fulfilled:
computational faithfulness
.

.

Let

\displaystyle\big|\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.51114pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\mathcal{F}_{n}}(\rho_{n})-\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.51114pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\mathcal{F}_{n}}(\rho^{\prime}_{n})\big|\leq\mathsf{negl}(n)\,

Let us now focus on the resource of entanglement. Analogously to the information-theoretic scenario, we define the computational measured relative entropy of entanglement as

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 1.0208pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{E}_{R}(\rho)\coloneqq\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\mathrm{SEP}_{n}}(\rho)=\min_{\sigma\in\mathrm{SEP}_{n}}\,\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\rho\|\sigma)\;,

Definition 5.9

(LOCC Map
[
ABV23
]
)
.

A quantum channel is said to be an LOCC map

We now consider their complexity-constrained counterparts.

\Phi\in\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{2}
whenever the polynomial in the construction of the family
\{\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is (super)-additive.

Proof.

We first show that
\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 1.0208pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{E}_{R}(\Phi^{\otimes n})\geq n
. Since
\Phi
can be efficiently generated, the measurement
M_{{\Phi_{n}}}=({\Phi_{n}},\mathbb{I}-{\Phi_{n}})\in\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{2n}
.
Then, since
\langle\Phi_{n}|\sigma|\Phi_{n}\rangle\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}
for any
\sigma\in\mathrm{SEP}_{n}

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 1.0208pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{E}_{R}(\Phi_{n})

\displaystyle=\min_{\sigma\in\mathrm{SEP}_{n}}(-\log\langle\Phi_{n}|\sigma|\Phi_{n}\rangle)\geq n\,.

We now show

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 1.0208pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{E}_{R}(\Phi_{n})

\displaystyle=\min_{\sigma\in\mathrm{SEP}_{n}}\sup_{M\in\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}(\mathcal{M}_{M}(\Phi_{n})\|\mathcal{M}_{M}(\sigma))

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{M\in\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}(\mathcal{M}_{M}(\Phi_{n})\|\mathcal{M}_{M}(\sigma^{*}))\,,

where
\sigma_{n}^{*}=2^{-n}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\outerproduct{x}{x}_{A}\otimes\outerproduct{x}{x}_{B}
which is separable. Lastly, by data processing,

\displaystyle\sup_{M\in\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}(\mathcal{M}_{M}(\Phi_{n})\|\mathcal{M}_{M}(\sigma^{*}))\leq D(\Phi_{n}\|\sigma^{*})=-\langle\Phi_{n}|\log\sigma^{*}|\Phi_{n}\rangle=n\,.

∎

Therefore, in this simple case of effectively not assuming computational restrictions, our measure attains the expected maximum. Let us now also relate

6
Discussion and Open Questions

We examine central information-theoretic quantities and naturally incorporate computational constraints, building a cohesive framework. Our guiding principle is to retain the clear operational meaning of the original quantities while enforcing that the measurements are efficient.

6.1
Discussion

On the one hand, we take a geometric approach to incorporate computational constraints into the max-divergence. We construct families of proper cones of polynomially generated effect operators, which we define to be generated by efficiently approximate effect operators using non-uniform polynomial circuits. The computational max-divergence then naturally arises
[
RKW11
,
GC24
]
, induced by the partial order given by those cones. On the other hand, we define the set of binary POVMs composed by the elements of our polynomially generated effect operators. This set allows us to define a computational notion of measured Rényi divergences, based on constructions from
[
RSB24
,
MH23
]
.
We show that these two, a priori, quite different approaches to computational divergences are consistent in the sense that in the limit
\alpha\to\infty
, the computational measured Rényi divergence coincides with the computational max-divergence (see
Theorem

4.12
). This equivalence is not only an analogue of the information-theoretic case, but also shows the consistency of our defined divergences and highlights the usefulness of conic theory to computational quantum information. Inspired by
[
RKW11
]
, we further consider computational notions of the fidelity and trace distance. These quantities are related in meaningful — yet straightforward — ways to our computational divergences, such as via a computational Pinsker inequality (
4.11
). Moreover, they are related to each other via a computational Fuchs–van de Graaf inequality (
4.22
). These relations reflect the robustness and flexibility of our framework, and allow us to provide an explicit example for which these quantities differ from their information-theoretic counterparts in
Section

4.5
.

Lastly, we consider two relevant applications. Firstly, in the context of asymmetric binary hypothesis testing, we show that the regularized version of our computational measured relative entropy has a sharp operational meaning as an upper bound on the Stein-exponent,
Theorem

5.2
. In other words, it upper-bounds the error exponent of asymmetric binary hypothesis testing under our computationally restricted measurements. This result is a direct analog to the information–theoretic setting.

Secondly, in the context of resource theories, these computational divergences allow us to define different resource measures. The main result of that section is an asymptotic continuity bound for the computational measured relative entropy,
Theorem

5.7
.
This bound is the computational analogue of
[
Win16
,
SW23
]
and we believe it to be of independent interest as it is, as far as we are aware, the first asymptotic continuity bound relating a measurement-restricted divergence with its corresponding restricted trace distance. This bound allows us to state the following analytical characterization: computationally indistinguishable families of states will also have computationally indistinguishable resource contents. This phenomenon had been previously stated in a slightly more heuristic manner, under cryptographic reductions. Moreover, focusing on entanglement,
Theorem

5.7
lets us compare the computational measured relative entropy of entanglement
\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 1.0208pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{E}_{R}
induced by our divergences with the computational distillable entanglement and entanglement cost of
[
ABV23
]
: under efficient LOCC (up to a continuity term), one has
\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 1.0208pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{E}_{D}(\rho^{n}_{AB})\leq\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 1.0208pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{E}_{R}(\rho^{n}_{AB})\leq\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 1.0208pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{E}_{C}(\rho^{n}_{AB})
, thereby reproducing the information-theoretic hierarchy.

Apart from these applications we consider, we expect this formalism to be relevant across various areas of computational quantum information theory. In part, this is because our quantities directly incorporate a central practical constraint — scalability — on which manipulations and measurements remain implementable as the systems grow. Complexity and practical polynomial constraints are already relevant in many-body physics, where tools dealing with the exponentially growing Hilbert-space dimension as a function of the particle number have proven very valuable
[
Has07
,
PVWC07
,
CPSV21
]
. Only transformations with at most polynomial cost are asymptotically viable, as made precise by Hamiltonian-complexity results
[
AE11
,
GHLS15
]
. On the measurement side, compressed-acquisition schemes such as classical shadows estimate observables with polynomial resources, reinforcing the value of complexity-aware information measures
[
HKP20
,
KGKB25
]
. Related ideas surface in quantum thermodynamics, where resource costs are analyzed under explicit complexity restrictions
[
MKH+25
]
, and in high-energy theory, where “complexity = action/volume” links boundary complexity to bulk geometry in AdS/CFT
[
BRS+16
,
BS18
]
. Lastly, our approach interfaces naturally with cryptography, where indistinguishability is explicitly computational; see e.g. pseudorandom states
[
JLS18
]
and pseudorandom unitaries
[
MPSY24
]
.

6.2
Open questions

A variety of interesting and promising avenues for future research follow from this work. It is a mathematically natural question to extend our formalism beyond the binary setting to multi-outcome POVMs, keeping implementability and classical post-processing explicit. This would broaden the scope of our work and connect it to operational tasks such as multi–hypothesis testing and multi-party decoupling. This direction is particularly appealing since it opens the possibility of defining a computational min–entropy within our framework and allows one to study its connection to previously studied computational entropies, such as the HILL entropies from
[
CCL+17
]
or the computational min–/max–entropies defined in
[
AA25
,
AHRA25
]
.

On the technical side, a key open problem is to establish nontrivial lower bounds on the computational Stein exponent. From an operational viewpoint, it would be valuable to clarify the role of our computational divergences in one-shot resource tasks under efficient free operations. Pursuing this direction—including smoothed variants of the computational max divergence and (efficient) non-entangling maps—may illuminate further connections between the measures introduced here and prior work in computational entanglement theory
[
ABV23
,
LREJ25
]
.

Proof of
4.10
.

The properties of the computational measured Rényi divergences follows directly from
[
RSB24
, Lemmas 2 & 5]
and
[
Tom16
, Chapter 4]
. We will use
\mathbb{D}_{\alpha}(\mu\|\nu)
for denoting a classical Rényi divergence over probabilities distributions
\mu,\nu
. Recall that, when
\mu_{\rho}^{M}(i)=\Tr[\rho E_{i}]
and
\nu_{\sigma}^{M}(i)=\Tr[\sigma E_{i}]
,
\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\alpha}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\rho\|\sigma)=\sup_{M\in\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\mathbb{D}_{\alpha}(\mu_{\rho}^{M}\|\nu_{\sigma}^{M})
.

(1) Follows from the positivity of the classical Rényi divergence.

(2) For every pair of probabilities distributions
\mu,\nu
, we have that for all
\alpha,\beta
such that
0<\alpha\leq\beta
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}_{\alpha}(\mu\|\nu)\leq\mathbb{D}_{\beta}(\mu\|\nu)\;.

Therefore, by taking the supremum, it directly follows that
\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\alpha}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\rho\|\sigma)\leq\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\beta}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\rho\|\sigma)
.

(3) By linearity of the trace,
\mu^{M}_{\lambda\rho_{1}+(1-\lambda)\rho_{2}}=\lambda\mu^{M}_{\rho_{1}}+(1-\lambda)\mu^{M}_{\rho_{2}}
. Let us first consider the case
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{Q}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}_{\alpha}(\lambda\rho_{1}+(1-\lambda)\rho_{2}\|\lambda\sigma_{1}+(1-\lambda)\sigma_{2})

\displaystyle=\inf_{M\in\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\mathbb{Q}_{\alpha}(\lambda\mu_{\rho_{1}}^{M}+(1-\lambda)\mu_{\rho_{2}}^{M}\|\lambda\nu_{\sigma_{1}}^{M}+(1-\lambda)\nu_{\sigma_{2}}^{M})

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{M\in\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\lambda\mathbb{Q}_{\alpha}(\mu_{\rho_{1}}^{M}\|\mu_{\rho_{2}}^{M})+(1-\lambda)\mathbb{Q}_{\alpha}(\nu_{\sigma_{1}}^{M}\|\nu_{\sigma_{2}}^{M})

\displaystyle\geq\lambda\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{Q}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}_{\alpha}(\rho_{1}\|\rho_{2})+(1-\lambda)\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{Q}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}_{\alpha}(\sigma_{1}\|\sigma_{2})\;,

where
\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{Q}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}_{\alpha}(\cdot\|\cdot)
and
\mathbb{Q}_{\alpha}(\cdot\|\cdot)
are the computational and classical counterparts of Eq. (
2.2
). The first inequality follows from joint concavity of the classical measure and the last one from the superadditivity of the infimum, proving the joint concavity of the function. Let us also set
f(t)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log(t)
(see
Definition

2.1
). In the case of
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
f(t)
is a non-increasing convex function. Therefore, it follows that
\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}_{\alpha}
is jointly convex in
(\rho,\sigma)
for
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}_{1}
is jointly convex as the supremum of a family of jointly convex functionals (see
[
RSB24
, Prop. 3, Lemma 2]
. For
\alpha\in(1,\infty)
,
f(t)
is non-decreasing and quasi-convex. Moreover, by using the fact of
\mathbb{Q}_{\alpha}
being joint convex and the supremum being superadditive,
\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{Q}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}_{\alpha}
is joint convex. Therefore,
\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}_{\alpha}
is jointly quasi-convex. Lastly, the joint quasi convexity of
\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}_{\infty}
follows by taking the supremum of a family of jointly quasi-convex functions (
[
RSB24
, Prop. 3, Lemma 2]
).

(4) By the duality of the quantum channels,
\mu^{M}_{\mathcal{E}(\rho)}=\mu^{\mathcal{E}^{*}\left(M\right)}_{\rho}
. Since for every admissible
\mathcal{E}
, it follows that
\mathcal{E}^{*}\left(M\right)\in\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
, then,

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\alpha}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\mathcal{E}(\rho)\|\mathcal{E}(\sigma))=\sup_{M\in\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\mathbb{D}_{\alpha}(\mu_{\mathcal{E}(\rho)}^{M}\|\nu_{\mathcal{E}(\sigma)}^{M})=\sup_{M\in\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\mathbb{D}_{\alpha}(\mu_{\rho}^{\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\left(M\right)}\|\nu_{\sigma}^{\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\left(M\right)})\leq\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{\alpha}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\rho\|\sigma)\;.

∎

B.3
Proof of
4.21

Recall that we want to show that

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{1/2}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\rho\|\sigma)=-\log\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{F}(\rho,\sigma)\;.

Proof of
4.21
.

By definition of the computational measured Rényi divergence we have that for
\alpha=1/2
,

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}_{1/2}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\rho\|\sigma)=-2\log\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{Q}_{1/2}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}(\rho\|\sigma)\;.

(B.1)

Then, by Eq. (
4.14
),

\displaystyle\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{Q}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}_{1/2}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle=\inf_{M\in\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}Q_{1/2}(\mathcal{M}_{M}(\rho)\|\mathcal{M}_{M}(\sigma))

\displaystyle=\inf_{M\in\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}Q_{1/2}(\mathcal{M}_{M}(\rho)\|\mathcal{M}_{M}(\sigma))

\displaystyle=\inf_{M\in\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\|\mathcal{M}_{M}(\sigma)^{1/2}\mathcal{M}_{M}(\rho)\mathcal{M}_{M}(\sigma)^{1/2}\|_{1/2}^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\inf_{(E_{1},E_{2})\in\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\sqrt{\Tr[E_{i}\rho]\Tr[E_{i}\sigma]}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{F}(\rho,\sigma)}\;,

where the second equality follows from
4.9
. Together with Eq. (
B.1
), this yields the claim.
∎

B.4
Proof of
4.22

Recall that we want to prove here a computational Fuch-van-Graaf inequality

\displaystyle 1-\sqrt{\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{F}(\rho,\sigma)}\leq\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{\Delta}(\rho,\sigma)\leq\sqrt{1-\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.3403pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{F}(\rho,\sigma)}\;.

Proof of
4.22
.

We note that it suffices to prove that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} 1-\sqrt{\min_{M\in\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}F(\mathcal{M}_{M}(\rho),\mathcal{M}_{M}(\sigma))}\leq\max_{M\in\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}\Delta(\mathcal{M}_{M}(\rho),\mathcal{M}_{M}(\sigma))\leq\sqrt{1-\min_{M\in\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}}F(\mathcal{M}_{M}(\rho),\mathcal{M}_{M}(\sigma))}\;,\end{aligned}

(B.2)

since it directly implies our bounds.
Starting with the usual information-theoretic Fuch-van Graaf inequality
[
FvdG99
]
applied to the states
\mathcal{M}_{M}(\rho),\mathcal{M}_{M}(\sigma)
for any
M\in\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle 1-\sqrt{F(\mathcal{M}_{M}(\rho),\mathcal{M}_{M}(\sigma))}\leq\Delta(\mathcal{M}_{M}(\rho),\mathcal{M}_{M}(\sigma))\leq\sqrt{1-F(\mathcal{M}_{M}(\rho),\mathcal{M}_{M}(\sigma))}\;.

Now optimizing over all
M\in\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n}
yields Eq. (
B.2
).
∎

C.1
Proof of
Theorem

5.2

Proof.

The upper bound on the Stein’s exponent can be proven in the following way. Let us consider for an
\varepsilon>0
and
m(k)\in\mathsf{poly}(k)>0
, a binary measurement
M:=(E_{k\cdot m(k)},\mathds{1}-E_{k\cdot m(k)})\in\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{k\cdot m(k)}
that satisfies
\Tr[E_{k\cdot m(k)}\rho_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}]\geq 1-\varepsilon
.
Recall that the infimum over all such measurements is the one that defines
\beta^{\varepsilon}_{m(k)}(\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{k\cdot m(k)})
in Eq. (
5.3
).
Then we have

\displaystyle\sup_{M^{\prime}\in\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{k\cdot m(k)}}

\displaystyle D(\mathcal{M}_{M^{\prime}}(\rho_{k}^{\otimes m(k)})\|\mathcal{M}_{M^{\prime}}(\sigma_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}))\geq D(\mathcal{M}_{M}(\rho_{k}^{\otimes m(k)})\|\mathcal{M}_{M}(\sigma_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}))

\displaystyle=\Tr[E_{k\cdot m(k)}\rho_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}]\log\Tr[E_{k\cdot m(k)}\rho_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}]-\Tr[E_{k\cdot m(k)}\rho_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}]\log\Tr[E_{k\cdot m(k)}\sigma_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}]

\displaystyle+\Tr[(\mathds{1}-E_{k\cdot m(k)})\rho_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}]\log\Tr[(\mathds{1}-E_{k\cdot m(k)})\rho_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}]

\displaystyle-\Tr[(\mathds{1}-E_{k\cdot m(k)})\rho_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}]\log\Tr[(\mathds{1}-E_{k\cdot m(k)})\sigma_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}]

\displaystyle\geq-h_{2}\left(\Tr[E_{k\cdot m(k)}\rho_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}]\right)-\Tr[E_{k\cdot m(k)}\rho_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}]\log\Tr[E_{k\cdot m(k)}\sigma_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}]

\displaystyle\geq-1-(1-\varepsilon)\log\Tr[E_{k\cdot m(k)}\sigma_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}]\;,

where
h_{2}(p)=-p\log(p)-(1-p)\log(1-p)
is the binary entropy. For the second-to-last line, we use the fact that

\displaystyle\Tr[(\mathds{1}-E_{k\cdot m(k)})\rho_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}]\log\Tr[(\mathds{1}-E_{k\cdot m(k)})\sigma_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}]\leq 0\;.

(C.1)

The last line then follows from the fact that the binary entropy is upper bounded by
1
and the constraint that
\Tr[E_{k\cdot m(k)}\rho_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}]\geq 1-\epsilon
. Rearranging this inequality and subsequently taking the supremum over all such efficient measurements
M
yields

\displaystyle-\log\beta^{\varepsilon}_{m(k)}(\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{k\cdot m(k)})\leq\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\Big(1+\sup_{M^{\prime}\in\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{k\cdot m(k)}}D\big(\mathcal{M}_{M^{\prime}}(\rho_{k}^{\otimes m(k)})\|\mathcal{M}_{M^{\prime}}(\sigma_{k}^{\otimes m(k)})\big)\Big)\;.

Now dividing by
m(k)
, and taking the
\limsup
in
k
, gives

\displaystyle\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}-\frac{1}{m(k)}\log\beta^{\varepsilon}_{m(k)}(\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{k\cdot m(k)})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{m(k)}\left(1+\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{k\cdot m(k)}}(\rho_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}\|\sigma_{k}^{\otimes m(k)})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{m(k)}\left(\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{k\cdot m(k)}}(\rho_{k}^{\otimes m(k)}\|\sigma_{k}^{\otimes m(k)})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\accentset{\text{\tiny\hskip 0.6806pt\raisebox{0.10764pt}{c}}}{D}^{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{eff}},\infty}(\{\rho_{k}\}_{k}\|\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k})\;.

Finally taking the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, yields the claimed result.
∎
