Design and development of a high-throughput multipattern aerosol deposition device for respiratory toxicity and health-risk assessment (MARTHA)
Authors: Amin Shirkhani, Sudheer Salana, Stavros Amanatidis, Mohammad Washeem, Arantzazu Eiguren-Fernandez, Steven R Spielman, Sripriya Nannu Shankar, Gregory S Lewis, Tara Sabo-Attwood, Chang-Yu Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn vitro toxicity assessments of aerosols are often limited by their physiological relevance. Conventional aerosol exposure systems provide uniform particle deposition, whereas in vivo deposition is inherently heterogeneous across lung regions. To address this, we have developed a high-throughput Multipattern Aerosol deposition device for Respiratory Toxicity and Health-risk Assessment (MARTHA). MARTHA employs laminar-flow, water-based condensation technology to efficiently grow nanoparticles from as small as 5 nm to > 3 μm while maintaining physiological conditions in the lungs (37 ◦C, ≥95% humidity). A key innovation is its ability to generate both uniform and non-uniform deposition patterns using three distinct nozzle configurations (3-jets, slit, and 7-jets). Deposition validation using aerosolized sodium fluorescein demonstrated consistent particle dose distribution across nozzle types, with a low coefficient of variation (CoV <15%) among wells. Experimental results demonstrated that MARTHA achieved high collection efficiency (>95%) for particle number concentrations ranging from 2000 to 200,000 #/cm3 and enabled simultaneous exposure of cells at the air-liquid interface (ALI) in 12 Transwell inserts. As proof-of-concept functional validation of the platform, exposure of aerosolized e-liquid to A549 cells at the ALI resulted in significant differences in trans-epithelial electrical resistance (TEER), underscoring the impact of deposition patterns. Overall, MARTHA is an advanced in vitro platform that more realistically mimics pulmonary aerosol deposition than existing systems and enables high-throughput, pattern-controlled ALI exposure studies.

Introduction

Rapid expansion of environmental nanoparticles across consumer and industrial sectors has intensified concerns about their adverse health effects from inhalation exposure (Madl & Pinkerton, 2009; Murphy et al., 2015; Wu et al., 2019). Nanoparticles possess high surface area to volume ratios, which may promote interactions with the airway surface and provoke oxidative stress, inflammation, barrier dysfunction, and other adverse effects (Biswas & Wu, 2005; Kroll et al., 2012; Thu et al., 2023; Xuan et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2023). However, despite growing concerns about their health impacts, significant gaps remain in understanding the extent of worker and consumer exposure via inhalation and the associated health effects (Kumah et al., 2023; Kumar et al., 2014; Ray et al., 2009; Wigger et al., 2020).

So far, in vitro toxicological studies of airborne nanoparticles have yielded inconsistent results, primarily due to variations in aerosol generation, sampling methods, exposure conditions, dosimetry, and assays used to evaluate their toxicity (Johnston et al., 2018; Oberdörster et al., 2005). A major challenge in assessing nanoparticle toxicity lies in the delivery of airborne nanoparticles to biological systems under physiologically relevant conditions. In this regard, air-liquid-interface (ALI) cell exposure models have been shown to be superior to conventional submerged cell cultures, due to improved physiological relevance by mimicking the respiratory tracťs exposure (Loret et al., 2016; Tilly et al., 2019; Ward et al., 2020).

Consequently, several commercial ALI exposure systems, including Vitrocell®, Cultex®, and CellTox®, have been developed to facilitate in vitro nanotoxicology studies (Ding et al., 2020; Guénette et al., 2022; Steinritz et al., 2013). However, these existing ALI systems often face constraints such as inefficient deposition for nanoparticles. Another important limitation is the lack of control over spatial deposition patterns on the cell surface. Several studies have been utilizing imaging or modelling techniques to suggest that in vivo deposition across the bronchial and alveolar region is intrinsically heterogeneous rather than uniform (Balásházy et al., 2003; Darquenne et al., 2024; Gu & Darquenne, 2021; Sadafi et al., 2024). Hence, platforms that deliver high and well-controlled doses with spatial patterning are needed for a more physiologically relevant assessment of the health impacts of airborne nanoparticle exposure (Fröhlich, 2018; Latvala et al., 2016; Nannu Shankar et al., 2025; Tsoutsoulopoulos et al., 2019).

Water-based condensational particle growth (CPG) has emerged as a practical approach to address limitations in particle deposition by enlarging ultrafine particles to micrometer droplets followed by gentle inertial collection (Hering et al., 2014; Hering & Stolzenburg, 2005). Building on this principle, Lednicky et al. (Lednicky et al., 2016), developed a device called Viable Virus Aerosol Sampler (VIVAS) which employs laminar flow CPG to collect airborne virus, bacteria, and yeast in both laboratory and indoor field settings (Lednicky et al., 2020; Nannu Shankar et al., 2022; Pan et al., 2016, 2018; Shirkhani et al., 2025; Vass et al., 2022, 2023, 2024). To assess nanoparticle toxicity, Tilly et al. (2019) developed a lung mimetic exposure system called Dosimetric Aerosol in-Vitro Inhalation device (DAVID), which uses the same water condensation mechanism to achieve high nanoparticle collection efficiency, short exposure durations, and conservation of ALI cell cultures under near physiologic conditions (≈37 ◦C, ≥95% RH) (Tilly et al., 2019, 2023; Ward et al., 2020). Subsequent work also highlighted the importance of online dosimetry, such as SMPS-based monitoring, to connect aerosol size/number dynamics with the delivered dose (Nannu Shankar et al., 2023). Although such CPG-based devices have substantially improved the physiological relevance of in vitro toxicity assessment of nanoparticles, a crucial gap remains unaddressed.

In the past two decades, experimental and modeling studies have indicated that localized hotspots of deposition are common in the lung (e.g., along alveolar duct centerlines or near bifurcations) (Darquenne et al., 2009; Mutuku et al., 2020; Ou et al., 2020). Notably, a recent ALI-based in vitro toxicity study showed that spatial dose heterogeneity (e.g., spot, line, ring, circle) at a matched global dose can modulate viability and gene expression outcomes, underscoring the need for exposure platforms that allow controlled, multipattern deposition under physiologically relevant conditions (Nannu Shankar et al., 2025). This implies that it is essential for any in vitro aerosol exposure system to include the effects of deposition patterns to provide a more realistic assessment of aerosol health effects. However, currently, no devices are available that can provide such assessments. Although DAVID provides the ability to evaluate the toxicity of different deposition patterns, it allows evaluation of only one deposition pattern at a time, and therefore, it is constrained by the number of samples that can be processed in each period. Devices with high-throughput assessment capabilities are warranted for large-scale aerosol toxicity studies whose results can be integrated into epidemiological models for devising better health-risk prediction tools.

To enable systematic evaluation of how spatial dose heterogeneity influences toxicological responses, an aerosol exposure system is needed that couples precise multipattern deposition with physiologically relevant ALI conditions and practical throughput. Therefore, we have developed a high-throughput Multipattern Aerosol deposition device for Respiratory Toxicity and Health risk Assessment (MARTHA). This device integrates (i) a water-based condensation growth tube section to activate and enlarge airborne nanoparticles; (ii) a water handling module to stabilize vapor supply and prevent nozzle fouling; and (iii) a modular cell exposure stage with interchangeable nozzle geometries and optional low-speed rotation to generate uniform and nonuniform deposition patterns. The platform supports high throughput operation (12 inserts per run) for studies of pattern, dose, and aerosol type.

In this study, we described the design and operating principles of MARTHA, which uses three nozzle configurations (3-jets, 7-jets, and slit designs) to provide a controlled and physiologically relevant bridge between in vitro ALI exposure and the heterogeneous deposition encountered in vivo. We first described the fluid/heat/mass transfer modeling to set growth tube conditions and aerosol transport. This was followed by validation of the deposition performance and well-to-well uniformity with aerosolized sodium fluorescein. Finally, we demonstrated the utility of this device and the importance of considering different aerosol deposition patterns by relating deposition patterns of e-liquid aerosols to the transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) of A549 cells grown as ALI cultures. The TEER measurement was used as a proof-of-concept functional endpoint to verify that the controlled deposition patterns generated by MARTHA can produce distinguishable epithelial responses.

Materials and methods

Design of MARTHA

The schematic diagram of MARTHA is shown in Fig. 1. MARTHA consists of three major units: the growth tube unit, the water handling unit, and the cell exposure unit. Each unit is described in detail below.

(a) Experimental setup and various units of MARTHA; (b) Three nozzle designs used in MARTHA and the patterns created due to rotation of the nozzle shaft.

Growth tube unit

The growth tube (GT) unit consists of an array of four identical GTs, which enlarge the incoming aerosol particles by condensing water vapor on their surface, coupled with inertial deposition stages for particle collection downstream. As shown in Fig. 2, a common inlet receives the incoming aerosol sample and splits the flow into the four GTs. Each GT contains a water-saturated wick with an internal diameter of 4.8 mm (0.188″) and a total length of 8.3″ spanning vertically in three stages: a 4″ Conditioner, followed by a 1″ Initiator, and a 3” Moderator. These dimensions were selected based on prior modeling studies and optimization experiments that demonstrated effective activation and growth of submicron particles while maintaining laminar flow and sufficient residence time (Hering et al., 2007, 2014; Hering & Stolzenburg, 2005; Lewis & Hering, 2013).

Mechanical design and core components of the growth tube system. Partial section view through one of the growth tubes (Right), and Impactor stage (Left).

Water handling unit

The primary function of this unit is to maintain water saturation in the growth tube wicks while avoiding accumulation of excess water into the instrument. For each system, water is drawn from a sufficiently large reservoir by means of a solenoid pump (LPLA 1250650L, Lee Co., Essex, CT). The pump output is split into 4 identical tubing sections that supply water into the Initiator stages of each wick (Fig. 1a). Water injection flowrate is controlled by adjustment of the pump activation frequency (selectable between 1 and 30 s), with each pulse providing approximately 50 μL total water volume (12.5 μL per tube). Excess water is removed by pulling approximately 10% of the total air flow from the base of each growth tube by using peristaltic pumps.

Cell exposure unit

The cell exposure unit is the third major unit of MARTHA, where droplets exiting the GT are collected via inertial deposition on the impactor stage, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The outlets of all impactor stages are combined into a common outlet port that is connected to vacuum through a flow-control orifice. Each impactor stage consists of a flow acceleration nozzle and a cell culture well insert (Corning® 3470 Transwell), which contains the cells for exposure and serves as the particle collection surface. The collection stages can be rotated along their center axes with a geared DC motor that engages with all collection stages via inter-connected gears, providing a maximum rotational frequency of 8 RPM. Rotation was enabled for all testing in this study.

Three nozzle designs are developed to attain different deposition patterns with the rotating collection stages (Fig. 1). The “3-jets” design consists of three 0.029″ diameter orifices distributed uniformly along a single radius with respect to the nozzle center. The 3-jets design was considered as the “baseline” design (same as the first-generation DAVID system). With the 3-nozzle design, the deposition pattern is effectively 3 spots when the collection surface is not rotated, and a ring with rotation. The “7-jets” design consists of seven 0.016″ diameter orifices which are distributed into 3 groups: the first includes only one orifice positioned slightly offset from the center; the second group includes two orifices positioned at twice the first radius; and the third includes the remaining 4 orifices at four times the first distribution radius. The seven orifices are distributed in such a way that during rotation, there is nominally equal deposition concentration per surface area among the 3 orifice groups. The “slit” design features a continuous narrow slit that aims to produce a more uniform particle deposition while the stage rotated. The slit begins as a 0.010″ hole near the nozzle center and gradually transforms into a triangular slit towards the nozzle edge. This gradual increase in the slit width with radius targets a uniform flow velocity across the nozzle radius. Along the vertical dimension of the nozzle, the slit transforms gradually into a cylindrical shape that matches the diameter of the “standpipe” tube outlet.

Performance evaluation of MARTHA

Numerical modeling

The GT design was based on particle activation and growth simulations in COMSOL Multiphysics for a single GT using a 2D axisymmetric geometry, since the GT was cylindrically symmetric. The modeled geometry included the GT, downstream pipe, and nozzle inlet. The water vapor saturation ratio profile was calculated by solving concurrently the flow, water vapor transport, and heat transfer equations as previously described in detail by Hering et al. (2014). Laminar flow (Re = 150 – 300) was simulated using a fully developed inlet flow boundary, an outlet flow boundary at the base of the GT with an “excess” flow, and an open pressure boundary at the geometry outlet. The excess flow was set at 10% of the inlet flowrate. The wick walls were modeled as evaporative wet surfaces (fully saturated with water). Particle growth was simulated at three inlet flowrates: 0.50, 0.75, and 1.0 Lpm per GT to evaluate the influence of residence time on the final droplet size within the physiologically relevant flow range for in vitro exposure.

Conditioner, Initiator, and Moderator wall temperatures were set at 4 ◦C, 40 ◦C, and 25 ◦C, respectively, based on prior condensation growth tube studies showing optimal conditions for activating ultrafine particles without excessive coalescence (Eiguren Fernandez et al., 2014; Hering et al., 2014). The nozzle was set at 30 ◦C to ensure no water condensation on the nozzle walls. Inlet flow conditions were set to 25 ◦C and 30% RH, representing typical indoor aerosol exposure conditions. Particle trajectories and growth were modeled in a transient study with input from the steady-state transport solution. Particle activation and growth were simulated following Köhler theory (Seinfeld & Pandis, 2019, pp. 1–1152), with wettable seed particles 30 nm in diameter. Particle number concentration effects (condensational heating and vapor depletion) were neglected for simplicity.

To evaluate the cutoff size and collection efficiency, a 3D geometry COMSOL Multiphysics model was set up for each nozzle design. A steady-state model was used to solve the flow field, assuming the effect of relative rotation between the nozzle and the impaction surface was marginal due to the low rotational frequency. A fully developed inlet flow boundary and an open pressure boundary at the outlet were used, with symmetric boundary conditions where appropriate. The flowrate through the impactor geometry was assumed to be 90% of the growth tube inlet sample flow to account for the 10% water excess removal flow. Particle motion was calculated in a transient study with input from the steady-state flow field. Motion outside of the Stokes region in the accelerating flow was considered in the calculations, as was the slip effect. The collection efficiency was evaluated by injecting unit density, monodisperse particles into the flow, over a range of 10 sizes, for each geometry and flowrate.

Experimental setup

We first evaluated the ability of MARTHA to deliver a consistent dose in all 12 wells (4 wells for each of the 3 nozzle types) by measuring the mass of uranine deposited in each well using a fluorescence method. Before each experiment, fresh stocks of aqueous fluorescein solutions with concentrations ranging from 0.01 to 5.0 mg/mL were prepared by dissolving uranine powder (sodium fluorescein, Cat. No. A-833, water-soluble, Fisher Scientific, USA) in deionized (DI) water. A fluorescence calibration curve in the range of 0 – 20 μg was prepared by diluting the stock solution with DI water to 200 μL volume to known mass concentrations in 96-well plates to measure the fluorescent intensity (487 nm excitation/528 nm emission) using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1 multimode reader, Agilent Technologies Inc., CA, USA). The mass concentration of the uranine dilutions was plotted against the measured raw fluorescence, and the regression equation of the linear trend line was determined with an R2 >0.99 (Fig. S1). The resulting equation for the linear line was used to quantify the mass of uranine particles deposited in each well. Aerosol particles were collected using ELISA wells (8-well detachable flat bottom, DiagnoCine LLC, NJ, USA) and a custom 3D-printed sleeve which mimics a Transwell insert (Fig. S2).

A schematic of the experimental setup to evaluate the deposition of particles in MARTHA is shown in Fig. 1a. Aerosols were generated from a suspension of uranine by a 6-jet Collison nebulizer (CH Technologies, Westwood, NJ, USA) using particle-free air at a flowrate of 3 Lpm controlled by a rotameter. Although uranine is water-soluble, the nebulized droplets were mixed with 15 Lpm of dry air before entering MARTHA, producing dried, solid sodium fluorescein nanoparticles (NPs) upstream of the growth tubes. Its water solubility permits quantitative recovery of the deposited material by fluorescence after collection. The uranine NPs then entered MARTHA where they were enlarged inside four parallel growth tubes for each nozzle type by water vapor condensation to droplet sizes for impaction through 3 sets of nozzles as explained above, onto the ELISA wells. After 10 min of sampling, the collected samples were diluted by 200 μL of DI water and pipetted to a black-walled 96-well plate to measure the raw fluorescence, and the deposited mass was then determined by the calibration curve. The size distribution of the uranine NPs upstream of MARTHA was monitored by a particle mobility spectrometer (Spider-MAGIC 810, Aerosol Dynamics Inc., CA, USA).

At the start of each trial, we inspected the flowrate of all three collectors and adjusted it to 2.0 Lpm. Before the start of each experimental run, we also inspected the nozzles to ensure that they were clean and unclogged to have an accurate comparison among the collectors and the respective wells (4x).

Physical collection efficiency measurement

The collection efficiency of each collector was assessed for different aerosol concentrations (nebulizer stock concentration) and for different nozzle types, based on the measurements recorded by the Spider-MAGIC for 10 min. The physical collection efficiency (η) was determined based on the concentration at the outlet (Cout) over that at the inlet (Cin); η = 1 − (Cout/Cin). While determining the collection efficiency at high aerosol concentrations, we cleaned the nozzles between each experimental run to ensure negligible effect on collection efficiency due to nozzle blockage by uranine deposit from previous runs.

Transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) measurement

To demonstrate the utility of the instrument, we evaluated the effect of different deposition patterns on the integrity or permeability of the epithelial monolayer by measuring TEER using an Epithelial Voltohmmeter (World Precision Instruments). We used e-liquid (Brand: Ritual Craft Vapor Liquid; 6 mg/mL nicotine content) as the toxicant for this assessment, and A549 cells (adenocarcinoma human alveolar basal epithelial cells; ATCC CCL-185) grown in Ham's F-12 K medium (supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin). A549 cells were cultured in ALI Transwells at 125,000 cells per well for 5 days before exposing them to e-liquid, according to the ALI cell culture protocol adapted from previous studies (Barosova et al., 2021; Braakhuis et al., 2023). On the day of the exposure, the Transwells were removed from the cell culture plates and placed into the rotating shafts of the Transwell holder (Fig. 2). The cells were then exposed for 15 min to the aerosols generated by nebulizing the e-liquid in the Collison nebulizer. The flow rate of air into the Collison nebulizer was adjusted such that the final particle concentration was ~100,000 cm− 3. After exposure, the Transwells were immediately removed and placed in 24-well culture plates with 600 μL of fresh culture medium. The cells were then incubated at 37 ◦C and 5% CO2 for 72 h. Thereafter, the cells were gently washed using 1X phosphate buffer solution (PBS), and the TEER was measured according to the protocol in previous publications (Barosova et al., 2021; Braakhuis et al., 2023). TEER was selected as a functional indicator of epithelial barrier integrity for proof-of-concept validation of the exposure platform and should not be interpreted as a direct measure of cell survival or comprehensive cytotoxicity.

Statistical analysis

The influence of the uranine aerosol concentration on mass deposition and the impact of e-liquid aerosols on TEER with different nozzle designs were evaluated by linear regression and determination of the coefficient of variation (CoV) in Microsoft Excel. The mean, standard deviation, and CoV were determined for each exposure trial. Linear regression of aerosol concentrations vs. delivered dose in each collector was conducted using the regression tool in Excel's Data Analysis. In each trial, four inserts were used in each collector (N = 4, 12 in total) and each trial was repeated three times.

Results

Numerical modeling

Fig. 3 shows the simulated saturation ratio field in the growth tube geometry at different operating flowrates. At the design flowrate of 0.5 L/min, the incoming 30% relative humidity flow reaches near 100% saturation at the end of the Conditioner (C) stage. As the cold aerosol flow enters the warm Initiator (I) stage, supersaturation occurs, reaching a peak at the core of the flow, where particles as small as 5 nm can be activated, as indicated by the Kelvin diameter contours. Supersaturation is maintained in the Moderator (M) stage and almost throughout the geometry, except within the Nozzle Inlet (N) region where it reaches about 90%. At higher flowrates, the peak supersaturation ratio reduces in magnitude, thereby shifting the smallest activation size to larger particles. Moreover, the Kelvin diameter contours are expanded and shifted further downstream into the Moderator stage.

The resultant droplet size distribution at the geometry outlet, in comparison to the predicted collection efficiency curves of the various nozzle designs, is illustrated in Fig. 4. At the design flow of 0.5 L/min, most sampled particles are predicted to grow to 4–5 μm droplets. At this size range, all impactor nozzle designs are estimated to have nearly 100% efficiency.

Collection efficiency of the “3-jets”, “7-jets”, and “Slit” nozzle designs (left y-axes), and size distribution of droplets exiting the growth tube (right y-axes), for sample flowrates of 0.5, 0.75, and 1.0 L/min.

As the sample flow increases, particle growth is limited by the shorter residence time in the growth tube, thereby reducing the output droplet size. The median droplet diameter drops from 4.6 μm at 0.5 L/min, to about 3.6 μm at 0.75 L/min, and 2.7 μm at 1.0 L/min. However, the reduced droplet size at the higher flowrates is counteracted by the shift of the cutoff curves toward smaller sizes. To first order approximation, the impactor cut-off size is expected to scale with the inverse of the square root of the flowrate (Hinds & Zhu, 2022, p. 448). As demonstrated in Fig. 4 and Table S1, the resulting shift appears to be sufficient in maintaining the predicted overall collection efficiency of the system close to 100% for all flows and nozzle designs, albeit the margin between the median droplet size and cut-off size becomes narrower at 1.0 L/min.

Experimental validation of collection efficiency

The collection efficiency of each nozzle design was evaluated over a wide range of input aerosol concentrations by comparison of upstream and downstream samples measured by Spider-MAGIC. Aerosol concentration was varied between approximately 1.8×103–1.8 × 105 cm−3 by adjustment of nebulizer stock concentration. All three nozzles exhibited high and stable collection efficiencies, with mean values ≥ 0.90, when the number concentration was lower than about 8 × 104 cm−3 (Fig. S3). The collection efficiency decreased at higher concentrations presumably due to limited growth caused by concentration effects in the growth tube (Lewis & Hering, 2013). The 7-jet nozzles had the highest efficiency at the highest aerosol concentrations compared to the other two nozzle designs.

Mass deposition

To assess the reproducibility of deposition, we quantified uranine deposition across the four wells for each nozzle design. Across the nebulizer concentration range tested (10 – 5000 μg/mL, corresponding to aerosol concentrations of 1.8 × 103 – 1.8 × 105 cm−3), all nozzle types delivered mass in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. S4). The average deposited mass for each nozzle type across independent trials is summarized in Fig. 5. For all three nozzle designs, the mass delivery increased proportionally with aerosol concentration, demonstrating predictable and scalable performance. All nozzles showed nearly similar mass–response curves at the lower end of the concentration range, while 7-jets yielded higher deposited mass at the upper end of the tested range, consistent with its higher collection efficiency at the higher aerosol number concentrations.

Average mass deposition in MARTHA (± standard deviation, μg) vs NEB. Concentration (μg/mL). Y-axis is in Log scale.

As illustrated in Fig. 6, CoV values were generally below 15% for all nozzle types, except at the lowest aerosol concentration where variability increased, as the particle concentration was very close to background (1900 cm−3 vs. 1800 cm−3 for aerosolized DI water). Among the designs, the 3-jet nozzle produced the most consistent deposition across all concentrations (average CoV 8–10%), followed by the 7-jet (10–12%) and slit nozzle (12–15%). Despite these differences, the magnitude of variability remained within acceptable limits, confirming that MARTHA can reproducibly deliver uniform aerosol doses across multiple wells.

Coefficient of Variation for different nozzle geometries of MARTHA over a range of nebulizer concentrations.

TEER measurements

All exposed cells exhibited a reduction in TEER due to exposure to the aerosolized e-liquid. This reduction as illustrated in Fig. 7, varied by the nozzle geometries (i.e., deposition pattern). Normalized to deposited mass, the magnitude of reduction in TEER (ΔTEER per deposited mass, Ω/μg) depended strongly on nozzle design. Here, ΔTEER was defined as the difference between the negative control and exposed groups (ΔTEER = TEERNegativeControl − TEERExposed). The 7-jets nozzle produced the largest TEER reduction per unit mass (≈23 Ω/μg), followed by the slit nozzle (≈18 Ω/μg), while the 3-jets nozzle produced the smallest response (≈11 Ω/μg). Pairwise comparisons between different nozzles showed that the 7-jets reduction was significantly higher than both the 3-jets (p = 0.00512) and slit (p = 0.0465), and the reduction caused by slit nozzle was also significantly higher than the 3-jets (p = 0.0425). These differences demonstrate the platform's ability to generate pattern-dependent functional responses under the tested conditions; they should not be interpreted as a complete toxicological characterization.

Reduction in TEER measurements normalized by deposited mass for different nozzle geometries. The Y–axis represents the difference (ΔTEER) between the negative control (TEERNegativeControl) and the exposed groups (TEERExposed).

Discussion

This study describes the design and evaluates the performance of a high-throughput condensational particle growth ALI platform that is (i) designed to achieve high supersaturation and particle aerodynamic cutoff size, (ii) highly efficient and reproducible in delivered mass across multiple inserts (12 in our study), (iii) capable of simultaneously generating multiple deposition patterns from the same aerosol source and demonstrating a pattern–response relationship, and (iv) allowing simultaneous exposure of multiple cell types which can mimic different parts of the human respiratory system. Prior studies demonstrating devices using the same technology (e.g., DAVID) established that water-based condensational growth can capture ultrafine particles with high efficiency and deliver biologically meaningful doses on ALI cultures in short time duration, without confounding cytotoxicity from the device itself (Hering et al., 2014; Hering & Stolzenburg, 2005; Tilly et al., 2019; Ward et al., 2020). Here, by design, we use interchangeable nozzles that can generate spatial heterogeneity, a determinant of injury that has long been emphasized by lung dosimetry and imaging studies (Balásházy et al., 2003; Darquenne et al., 2016; Man et al., 2023; Porra et al., 2018).

The design of MARTHA was guided by various CPG simulations that helped us choose the most optimal design and operational parameters. The Conditioner-Initiator-Moderator temperature sequence produced peak supersaturation in the Initiator stage, activating particles as small as 5 nm and growing them to micrometer-sized droplets. Across all tested flow rates, modeled growth and impaction behavior indicate robust collection across nozzle geometries (Fig. 4 and Table S1), which is important for fixed-duration toxicology exposure (Eiguren Fernandez et al., 2014; Tilly et al., 2019). Increasing sample flow from 0.50 to 1.0 Lpm shortens residence time and reduces the median droplet diameter from 4.6 to 2.7 μm; meanwhile, the corresponding aerodynamic cut-off (D50) is also shifted to smaller sizes - from 2.6/2.5/2.8 μm (3-jet/7-jet/slit) at 0.50 Lpm, to 1.8/1.8/2.0 μm at 1.00 Lpm, maintaining a favorable margin between droplet size and cut-off. As a result, the overall collection efficiencies are predicted to be high for every configuration and flow condition. Because increasing the flow to 0.75 – 1.00 Lpm did not meaningfully improve the already near-unity collection (0.97 – 0.98 across all conditions), we chose 0.5 Lpm for conducting experiments. Lower jet velocities also reduce the impaction force and potentially minimize flow-induced mechanical stress on ALI cell cultures.

Physical collection efficiency testing of MARTHA validated the numerical model predictions. All nozzle designs used in our study exhibited >90% efficiency when the input aerosol number concentration was below 8 × 104 cm−3. Efficiency reduced with increasing number concentration for all nozzle designs, which can be attributed to limited condensational particle growth due to concentration effects in the growth tube. The efficiency reduction was smaller with the 7-jets nozzle, which sustained >90% efficiency up to 1.8 × 105 cm−3, likely due to a comparatively smaller impaction cut-off size than the other designs. Uranine mass deposition scaled proportionally (Fig. 5 and S4) with nebulizer stock concentrations of 0.01 – 5.0 mg/mL, and well-to-well reproducibility was strong (CoV < 15% except for lowest concentration which is near the assay limit, Fig. 6). These results align with prior water-based CPG exposure studies that efficiently collected ultrafine particles and bioaerosols while maintaining near-physiological temperature/humidity (Lednicky et al., 2016; Nannu Shankar et al., 2025; Pan et al., 2016; Tilly et al., 2019, 2023; Ward et al., 2020), and with dose-controlled dosimetry practices advocated for ALI systems (Nannu Shankar et al., 2023, 2025; Paur et al., 2011; Schmid & Cassee, 2017). Importantly, nozzle geometry modulated reproducibility without compromising average dose which means that spatial distribution can be tuned independently of global delivery (Fig. 5).

The uranine experiments evaluated deposition using dried, solid sodium fluorescein particles rather than liquid droplets entering the instrument. Uranine was chosen because its water solubility enables sensitive post-collection mass quantification. Nevertheless, soluble sodium fluorescein does not capture all physicochemical behaviors of poorly soluble or insoluble engineered nanoparticles, including possible differences in hygroscopic activation, agglomeration, surface chemistry, and recovery. Validation with representative insoluble nanoparticle aerosols is therefore an important next step. Prior work with related water-based condensational growth exposure systems has demonstrated effective delivery of metal-oxide nanoparticles, including CeO2 and CuO, supporting the applicability of the underlying approach to solid, poorly soluble materials (Tilly et al., 2023).

To demonstrate the utility of MARTHA, we showed that after e-liquid aerosol exposure, the reduction in TEER of the cells depended on the pattern of the dose delivery, and not just on the magnitude of the dose. The stronger TEER reduction observed for the 7-jets configuration compared to the slit nozzle is consistent with micro-dosimetry theory and existing literature showing that spatially concentrated (“hotspot”) deposition can amplify epithelial stress even at comparable nominal dose (Asgharian et al., 2001; Balásházy et al., 2003; Schlesinger & Lippmann, 1978). At the same time, the slit nozzle produced a larger response than 3-jets despite a more uniform pattern, suggesting a hidden contributor that we do not fully understand yet. A detailed investigation into the toxicity mechanisms behind the observed differences in the TEER caused by different deposition patterns is beyond the scope of this study. Nevertheless, overall, these results reinforce prior ALI studies that showed inhaled toxicants, including e-cigarette aerosols, can potentially influence epithelial barrier integrity (Raduka et al., 2023; Staal et al., 2024), while the magnitude and nature of effects depend on particle deposition pattern and delivery mechanics (Buckley et al., 2024; Ghosh et al., 2020; Lacroix et al., 2018; Omaiye et al., 2022).

The biological experiment was intentionally limited to a single functional endpoint because the principal objective of this study was instrument design and performance validation. TEER is a noninvasive measure of ionic conductance across an epithelial monolayer and is widely used to assess tight-junction and epithelial barrier integrity; however, it is not a direct measurement of cell viability and should therefore be complemented by viability or cytotoxicity assays when broader biological conclusions are intended (Shaughnessey et al., 2022; Srinivasan et al., 2015). Accordingly, the present TEER data is interpreted only as proof-of-concept evidence that MARTHA can resolve responses associated with different deposition patterns. Future application studies should combine TEER with complementary endpoints such as metabolic activity, membrane integrity, apoptosis/necrosis, inflammatory signaling, oxidative stress, and gene-expression analyses, preferably across multiple delivered doses.

Commercial and research ALI systems have been designed for spatially uniform delivery and reproducible dosing, for instance, VITROCELL® 24/48 and AMES 48 modules have been quantitatively characterized for deposition efficiency and uniformity with monodisperse aerosols (Oldham, Castro, Zhang, Lucci, et al., 2020a, Oldham, Castro, Zhang, Lucci, et al., 2020b). The CULTEX® RFS was explicitly optimized/validated as a homogeneous radial-flow exposure for acute inhalation hazard prediction (Tsoutsoulopoulos et al., 2019). ALICE provides dose-controlled spatially uniformed delivery via nebulized cloud settling (Lenz et al., 2009), and NAVETTA uses homogenous electro-deposition under laminar flow for targeted delivery to A549 cell cultures (Frijns et al., 2017). For ENMs and complex aerosols, delivered dose (not just exposure) governs response (Schmid & Cassee, 2017), and spatial context modulates toxicity (Asgharian et al., 2001; Balásházy et al., 2003). Because MARTHA can impose distinct spatial patterns at comparable delivered mass, it provides a practical way to interrogate how local micro-dosimetry can affect responses expected from heterogeneous or homogenous in-vivo deposition along bifurcations and alveolar ducts (Gu & Darquenne, 2021; Porra et al., 2018). It also strengthens the 3R approach (replacement, reduction, and refinement of animal use in scientific testing) for inhalation hazard assessment and aligns with efforts to reduce vertebrate animal use (Paur et al., 2011; USEPA, 2023).

Beyond the proof-of-concept e-liquid exposure demonstrated here, MARTHA can support a broad range of environmental and occupational health investigations, including exposures to poorly soluble engineered nanoparticles, welding fumes and other occupational aerosols, e-cigarette emissions, and bioaerosols (Nannu Shankar et al., 2022; Pan et al., 2018; Staal et al., 2024; Tilly et al., 2023; Vass et al., 2023, 2024; Ward et al., 2020). Because the platform can deliver multiple spatial patterns from the same aerosol source at comparable global doses, it can be used to model regional deposition scenarios relevant to airway bifurcations and alveolar regions (Darquenne et al., 2009; Gu & Darquenne, 2021), compare responses across respiratory cell types and co-culture models (Barosova et al., 2021; Braakhuis et al., 2023; Lacroix et al., 2018), evaluate dose–response relationships, and incorporate complementary biological endpoints (Nannu Shankar et al., 2023, 2025; Schmid & Cassee, 2017). These capabilities may help distinguish the effects of total delivered dose from those of local deposition heterogeneity, thereby improving the physiological relevance of quantitative ALI studies.

Conclusion

In this study, we discussed the design and development of MARTHA, a CPG-based ALI platform that delivers short-term, physiologically relevant exposures with precise control of spatial deposition. Across the tested range of aerosol concentration, the system achieved high and reproducible collection with stable, controlled dosing and low well-to-well variability. Interchangeable nozzles enabled uniform and non-uniform delivery at comparable doses, allowing direct interrogation of deposition-pattern effects. In proof-of-principle exposures, TEER measurements differed among deposition patterns; this functional endpoint validates the platform's ability to distinguish pattern-dependent responses, although it does not constitute a comprehensive assessment of cell viability or toxicity. These results establish MARTHA as a precise, pattern-tunable platform for quantitative ALI studies. It is therefore positioned for future studies of diverse environmental and occupational aerosols, including engineered nanomaterials, combustion-derived particles, workplace aerosols, e-cigarette emissions, and bioaerosols. Its multi-pattern, multi-well design also enables systematic comparisons across regional deposition patterns, respiratory cell models, delivered doses, and complementary biological endpoints.
