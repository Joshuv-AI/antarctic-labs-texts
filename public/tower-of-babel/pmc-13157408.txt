Cerebello–Brainstem Dominant Form of X-linked Adrenoleukodystrophy Without Apparent Brain MRI Abnormalities at Disease Onset
Authors: Yuki Nakagawa, Atsuhiko Sugiyama, Kazumoto Shibuya, Hajime Yokota, Takashi Matsukawa, Kazuki Ishiwata, Masahiro Mori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCerebello–brainstem dominant form of X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy (X-ALD) is a rare adult-onset phenotype that typically presents with slowly progressive spasticity and cerebellar ataxia. This phenotype can exhibit no apparent parenchymal signal abnormalities on brain MRI, thereby mimicking spinocerebellar ataxia. We encountered a 48-year-old Japanese man who developed slowly progressive spasticity and cerebellar ataxia beginning at age 35. Brain MRI performed 4 years later revealed only subtle cerebellar atrophy. Repeat-expansion testing identified an intermediate-length ATXN3 allele with 49 CAG repeats, and he received a provisional diagnosis of spinocerebellar ataxia type 3. Thirteen years after onset, follow-up MRI revealed new bilateral T2 hyperintensities in frontopontine fibers and cerebellar white matter. Markedly elevated very-long-chain fatty acid levels in plasma and a pathogenic ABCD1 variant confirmed the diagnosis of cerebello–brainstem dominant form of X-ALD. Detailed assessment identified compensated adrenal insufficiency, and his mother displayed mild neurologic symptoms, suggesting symptomatic carriage. This case highlights the importance of careful evaluation for adrenal insufficiency and a detailed family history assessment to detect subtle X-linked features in recognizing cerebello–brainstem dominant form of X-ALD in patients with progressive ataxia. It also suggests that longitudinal brain MRI can provide important diagnostic clues in patients with undiagnosed progressive ataxia, as characteristic demyelinating lesions along the frontopontine tract might emerge over time. Furthermore, because intermediate alleles in polyglutamine diseases are low-penetrance variants present in the general population, clinicians should avoid premature diagnostic closure and maintain careful diagnostic follow-up when encountering this finding to avoid missing treatable alternatives.Keywords: Adrenoleukodystrophy, Magnetic Resonance Imaging, Spinocerebellar Ataxias, Case Reports

Introduction

X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy (X-ALD) is a peroxisomal disorder caused by pathogenic variants in ABCD1, leading to impaired peroxisomal transport and defective β-oxidation of very-long-chain fatty acids (VLCFAs) [1]. VLCFAs accumulate in multiple tissues, but additional genetic and/or environmental factors are believed to modulate downstream mechanisms that predominantly cause central nervous system demyelination and adrenal cortical dysfunction [1]. X-ALD comprises several clinical phenotypes, including childhood cerebral ALD, adult cerebral ALD, adrenomyeloneuropathy, and primary adrenal insufficiency, yet a clear genotype–phenotype correlation has not been established [2]. Cerebello–brainstem dominant form of X-ALD is a rare phenotype, accounting for approximately 1%–2% of all cases [2], but its frequency is relatively higher in Japan (8.4%) [3]. This phenotype typically presents in adulthood with slowly progressive spasticity and cerebellar ataxia resembling spinocerebellar ataxia (SCA), and it might subsequently progress to involve cerebral white matter [3, 4]. Once cerebral involvement develops, the course can become rapidly progressive with poor outcomes [3, 5]. Because allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation might attenuate demyelination when performed at an early stage [6], prompt diagnosis is crucial to avoid missing a potential treatment window. Here, we present a case of cerebello–brainstem dominant form of X-ALD with initially unremarkable brain MRI findings, in which characteristic abnormalities emerged on follow-up imaging.

Case Presentation

A 48-year-old Japanese man presented with slowly progressive gait disturbance. At 35 years old, he began to stumble occasionally, followed by gradually worsening unsteadiness. He first visited our hospital at age 39. Neurologic examination revealed truncal and lower-limb cerebellar ataxia with pyramidal signs, including hyperreflexia and bilateral extensor plantar responses. Cervical spine MRI was unremarkable. Brain MRI revealed subtle cerebellar atrophy without parenchymal signal abnormalities (Fig. 1). N-isopropyl-p-[123I]-iodoamphetamine single-photon emission computed tomography did not demonstrate cerebellar hypoperfusion. Hereditary SCA with pyramidal features was suspected. Repeat-expansion panel testing (SCA1, SCA2, SCA3, SCA6, SCA7, SCA8, SCA12, SCA17, SCA31, and dentatorubral–pallidoluysian atrophy) revealed a heterozygous intermediate-length repeat expansion with 27/49 CAG repeats in ATXN3 (intermediate, 45–59; pathogenic, 60–87) [7]. The patient received a provisional diagnosis of SCA3. Brain MRI at 43 again showed only subtle cerebellar atrophy. At 45, he developed overactive bladder and began mirabegron therapy. By age 48, his gait impairment had progressed, necessitating T-cane usage.

Brain MRI at first presentation (4 years after symptom onset). (a, b) Axial T2-weighted imaging revealed no abnormal signal intensity in the cerebellum or brainstem. (c) Coronal T1-weighted imaging suggested subtle cerebellar hemispheric atrophy (yellow arrows)

His childhood developmental milestones were normal, and he had no significant past medical history. He reported drinking approximately 300 mL of wine daily (approximately 30 g ethanol/day). His mother had a similar but milder gait disturbance, but she had not sought a medical evaluation. There was no other family history of neuromuscular disease or adrenal insufficiency, including in his brother and sister.

At age 48, his general examination was unremarkable, with no findings suggestive of adrenal insufficiency such as hypotension, hyperpigmentation, or alopecia. On neurologic examination, cognitive function and cranial nerves were intact. He exhibited spasticity and cerebellar ataxia in all limbs with preserved muscle strength. Pain and vibration sensation were reduced below both knees. Tendon reflexes were brisk in all extremities. His gait demonstrated both spastic and wide-based ataxic features. Head-up tilt testing identified no orthostatic hypotension. Routine laboratory testing disclosed no notable abnormalities, including eosinophilia, hypoglycemia, or electrolyte disturbances. Follow-up brain MRI at 48 years old demonstrated progression of cerebellar atrophy and newly developed bilateral T2 hyperintense lesions along the frontopontine tract and in the cerebellar white matter surrounding the dentate nuclei (Fig. 2). The frontopontine tract lesions were conspicuously hypointense on T1-weighted imaging and exhibited gadolinium enhancement at their superior aspect, corresponding to the level of the internal capsule.

Given these lesions suggestive of demyelination along white matter tracts, X-ALD was suspected. VLCFA levels in plasma were markedly elevated (C24:0/C22:0, 1.737 [reference, 0.628–0.977]; C25:0/C22:0, 0.058 [0.012–0.023]; C26:0/C22:0, 0.030 [0.003–0.006]). Early-morning endocrine testing revealed adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) elevation (147.0 pg/mL; reference, < 63.3) with a normal cortisol level (9.8 µg/dL; reference, 7.1–19.6). Despite a normal cortisol response to rapid ACTH stimulation, ACTH levels remained elevated on repeated testing, and dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate levels were low (51 µg/dL; reference, 70–465), suggesting compensated primary adrenal insufficiency. Adrenal autoantibodies were not assessed because testing was not readily available. ABCD1 sequencing identified a hemizygous nonsense variant (c.397 C > T, p. Gln133Ter), previously reported as pathogenic [8]. Taken together with the clinical and radiologic findings, he was ultimately diagnosed with cerebello–brainstem dominant form of X-ALD. In consultation with a specialized center, we interpreted the gadolinium enhancement at the level of the internal capsule as indicative of progression toward the cerebral form of X-ALD, and therefore, proposed allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. He chose not to pursue this option at present, and we continued close outpatient follow-up.

Discussion

In this case, the patient developed adult-onset, chronically progressive cerebellar ataxia and spasticity and was provisionally diagnosed with SCA3 on the basis of subtle cerebellar atrophy on brain MRI and an intermediate-range ATXN3 repeat expansion. However, follow-up MRI later demonstrated new T2-hyperintense lesions in the frontopontine tract and peridentate cerebellar white matter, prompting further targeted evaluation. This revealed compensated subclinical primary adrenal insufficiency and his mother’s similar but milder gait disturbance, suggesting that she was a symptomatic carrier. ABCD1 testing then confirmed the diagnosis of cerebello–brainstem dominant form of X-ALD. This case highlights several practical points for differentiating X-ALD from SCA in patients with adult-onset progressive ataxia.

In cerebello–brainstem dominant form of X-ALD, diagnostically informative parenchymal MRI signal abnormalities can emerge later than clinical symptoms. Common brain MRI findings in this phenotype include T2 hyperintensities in the cerebellar white matter around the dentate nuclei and/or along the corticospinal tract in the brainstem [4]. Conversely, patients presenting with cerebellar atrophy without apparent parenchymal signal abnormalities and patients diagnosed at autopsy despite unremarkable MRI findings have also been reported [4, 9]. In our patient, MRI performed 4 and 8 years after symptom onset revealed only subtle cerebellar atrophy, whereas new parenchymal lesions involving the cerebellum and brainstem became apparent 13 years after onset. To our knowledge, explicit documentation of such lesion emergence on serial MRI after initially unremarkable imaging is limited; however, similar cases might have been overlooked in the absence of longitudinal neuroimaging. Clinicians should recognize that longitudinal brain MRI can provide critical diagnostic clues in patients with undiagnosed progressive ataxia.

Bilateral T2 hyperintense lesions in the frontopontine tract, as observed in our patient, and those in the peridentate cerebellar white matter might provide useful radiologic clues to X-ALD. Loes et al. classified brain MRI abnormalities in X-ALD into five patterns, including a pattern characterized by predominant involvement of the frontopontine or corticospinal projection fibers [5]. This pattern was observed in 12% of X-ALD cases, predominantly in adult-onset cases, and it tended to progress more slowly [5]. However, subsequent reports focusing on frontopontine tract involvement have been scarce, and the clinicopathologic correlates remain poorly defined. In our patient, the frontopontine tract lesions were conspicuously hypointense on T1-weighted imaging, consistent with demyelinating changes. Contrarily, peridentate cerebellar white matter is preferentially involved in pathologic studies; accordingly, early lesions might be confined to this area, as in our patient, whereas extension into the cerebellar peduncles can occur over time [10, 11]. Although peridentate lesions can occur in several other conditions, frontopontine tract abnormalities have rarely been reported in other disorders. An important differential diagnosis is alanyl-tRNA synthetase 2 (AARS2)-related leukodystrophy, which can involve the frontopontine tract and present in adulthood with slowly progressive frontal dysfunction, spasticity, and ataxia [12]. Accordingly, when frontopontine tract lesions are identified, VLCFA measurement should be prioritized, and if negative, AARS2-related disease should be considered.

Finally, this case underscores the risk of anchoring bias when an intermediate-length repeat expansion is detected in a polyglutamine disease. Intermediate alleles represent low-penetrance, genetically unstable variants with repeat lengths between clearly normal and full-penetrance ranges [7]. Such alleles are not uncommon in the general population. In one European cohort, 10.7% of healthy individuals carried an intermediate allele associated with at least one of nine polyglutamine diseases, including SCA3 [13]. Because repeat length generally displays an inverse correlation with age at onset and a positive association with disease severity, even when an intermediate allele causes disease, it typically manifests with later onset and milder or atypical phenotypes [7]. In our patient, chronic progressive spasticity and cerebellar ataxia made SCA3 a leading consideration, complicating interpretation of the intermediate ATXN3 expansion. However, the relatively early onset for an intermediate allele carrier and the discordance between clinical severity and minimal cerebellar atrophy/hypoperfusion were atypical for spinocerebellar degeneration, prompting careful longitudinal follow-up that ultimately led to the correct diagnosis. Although the clinical and radiologic findings in this case could be attributable to the cerebello–brainstem dominant form of X-ALD alone, we cannot exclude the potential contribution of SCA3 to the phenotype, as no neuropathologic assessment was performed. It is also possible that the intermediate ATXN3 expansion functioned as a modifier of disease expression, potentially contributing to the emergence of this rare phenotype.

In summary, this case emphasizes that, among men with progressive ataxia, accurate recognition of potentially treatable cerebello–brainstem dominant form of X-ALD requires a careful evaluation for adrenal insufficiency, detailed family history assessment for subtle X-linked symptoms, and longitudinal brain MRI follow-up. Although this phenotype can initially exhibit nonspecific, or even absent, MRI abnormalities and closely mimic spinocerebellar degeneration, characteristic demyelinating lesions along projection fibers including the frontopontine tract can emerge over time and provide a critical diagnostic clue. Moreover, because intermediate alleles in polyglutamine diseases are low-penetrance variants found in the general population, clinicians should avoid premature diagnostic closure and maintain diagnostic vigilance with longitudinal follow-up to avoid missing treatable alternatives.
