Engineering Small Extracellular Vesicles for Colon-Targeted Delivery: Microenvironment-Responsive Design, Therapeutic Mechanisms, and Clinical Translation
Authors: Guoqing Liu, Meirong Li, Jiayu Liu, Yi-Ping Li, Xin Huang, Xu Guo, Qingfeng Zhai, Fengxiang Zhang, Wenjing Yan, Jinyue Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmall extracellular vesicles (sEVs) are endogenous lipid-bilayer nanovesicles that combine cargo protection, biological membrane compatibility, and modifiable surface recognition. For colon-targeted delivery, their value depends on engineering them to withstand gastric acidity and proteolysis, negotiate the mucus barrier, and respond to colon-specific cues, including the distal gastrointestinal pH profile, microbiota-derived enzymes, inflammatory reactive oxygen species, and lesion-associated receptors such as integrins, CD44, and folate receptors. This review critically compares sEVs with conventional colon-targeted drug delivery systems and summarizes sEV biogenesis, cargo sorting, isolation, characterization, exogenous and endogenous loading, surface engineering, and passive, active, and stimuli-responsive targeting strategies. Therapeutic applications are evaluated in inflammatory bowel disease and colorectal cancer, with attention to epithelial barrier restoration, immune regulation, macrophage polarization, apoptosis, tumor-microenvironment remodeling, and emerging intestinal indications such as irritable bowel syndrome. Recent clinical trials, Good Manufacturing Practice requirements, potency and release specifications, safety assessment, and regulatory uncertainties are also discussed. A disease- and route-oriented decision framework is proposed to connect cargo properties, administration route, target-cell biology, manufacturing feasibility, and clinically meaningful endpoints. Although engineered sEVs remain investigational and no therapeutic product has achieved full regulatory approval, integration of microfluidic manufacturing, organ-on-chip validation, artificial intelligence-assisted design, and image-guided precision delivery may improve reproducibility and translational readiness.Keywords: small extracellular vesicles, sEVs, colon-targeted delivery, inflammatory bowel disease, IBD, colorectal cancer, CRC, irritable bowel syndrome, IBS, engineering strategies

Introduction

Colonic disorders encompass inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), colorectal cancer (CRC), functional conditions such as irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), and infectious colitis. IBD is characterized by recurrent mucosal inflammation, epithelial barrier disruption, dysregulated innate and adaptive immunity, and microbial dysbiosis; persistent inflammation can promote fibrosis and colitis-associated neoplasia. CRC develops through progressive genetic and epigenetic alterations within a complex tumor microenvironment containing epithelial, stromal, vascular, immune, and microbial components. Although IBS lacks the destructive mucosal pathology typical of IBD, altered barrier function, visceral hypersensitivity, immune activation, and microbiota-derived metabolites may contribute to selected subgroups. Infectious colitis adds pathogen- and toxin-mediated injury to the mucus layer and tight junctions, intense innate-cell recruitment, and, for some organisms, intracellular or biofilm-associated reservoirs that restrict effective drug exposure.1,2 These distinct disease mechanisms require delivery systems that can protect therapeutics during gastrointestinal transit while differentiating the healthy colon from inflamed or malignant tissue. Conventional immunosuppressants, biologics, chemotherapeutics, and antibiotics can be effective, but durable responses may be limited by disease heterogeneity, resistance, systemic exposure, and off-target toxicity.3–6

Oral administration is attractive for colon-targeted therapy because it is non-invasive and generally compatible with long-term patient use.7 However, cargoes must survive gastric acidity, digestive enzymes, variable intestinal transit, and the mucus barrier before achieving adequate colonic exposure.8–11 Conventional colon-targeted drug delivery systems (CTDDS), including pH-dependent coatings, time-controlled multiparticulates, microbiota-degradable polysaccharides, liposomes, and polymeric nanoparticles, can improve regional release and are supported by comparatively mature manufacturing processes.9–16 However, their clinical utility is constrained by fundamental drawbacks.17 pH- and time-dependent systems are susceptible to premature release due to inter-patient variability in gastrointestinal transit and luminal pH.18 Microbiota-degradable matrices rely on disease-sensitive enzyme activity, which becomes unreliable in dysbiosis. Many synthetic nanomaterials, particularly non-biodegradable or slowly degradable polymers, raise persistent concerns regarding long-term accumulation and material-associated toxicity.17,19,20

By contrast, small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) offer a biologically derived alternative. Their native lipid bilayer protects labile proteins and nucleic acids during gastrointestinal transit, supports cellular uptake, and can be functionally modified without replacing the biological membrane.21–23 Food- and plant-derived vesicles are particularly attractive for oral use.24 Edible plant materials provide broad, renewable, and widely accessible sources with practical scale-up potential. Several plant-derived preparations resist gastric acid, digestive enzymes, and intestinal degradation sufficiently to preserve cargo during oral transit. Their native lipids, RNAs, proteins, and phytochemicals may also provide intrinsic anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and microbiota-modulating activity.25,26 These advantages are preparation- and source-dependent rather than universal, but they support sEVs as a versatile biological platform for oral colon delivery.27

These same abnormalities also serve as mechanistic entry points for engineered sEVs, enabling cell-level delivery beyond the regional release offered by conventional CTDDS.28 In IBD, tight-junction disruption, increased mucosal permeability, altered mucus, activated macrophages and neutrophils, oxidative stress, matrix metalloproteinase activity, and dysbiosis create both access points and lesion-responsive triggers for regional release coupled to barrier repair and immune modulation.29 In CRC, overexpression of receptors such as CD44, A33, EpCAM, and the folate receptor, together with abnormal vasculature, extracellular-matrix remodeling, acidity, oxidative stress, and immunosuppressive stromal and myeloid compartments, can support ligand-mediated uptake, responsive release, intracellular gene or cytotoxic delivery, and tumor-immune microenvironment remodeling.30 The native lipid membrane and cargo-protective properties of sEVs therefore make them particularly suited to linking disease-associated signals with cell-level delivery rather than serving only as passive regional carriers. Infection presents a complementary rationale: pathogen- and toxin-induced barrier damage, intense innate-cell recruitment, and intracellular or biofilm-associated reservoirs can favor local delivery of antimicrobial or host-directed cargo together with barrier repair and immunomodulation.24

The colon provides several engineering opportunities. The distal gastrointestinal pH profile can trigger dissolution of enteric coatings, whereas pectinase, azoreductase, nitroreductase, and other microbiota-derived enzymes can activate degradable matrices.14–16,42,43 Mucus thickness and composition, epithelial permeability, local reactive oxygen species, and matrix metalloproteinases differ between healthy and diseased tissue and can be exploited for mucoadhesive, mucus-penetrating, inflammation-responsive, or enzyme-cleavable designs. At the cellular level, receptors such as integrin α4β7 in intestinal inflammation, and CD44, folate receptor, A33 antigen, EGFR, and selected integrins in CRC, offer opportunities for ligand-directed uptake44–48 Because each signal is variable and rarely disease-exclusive, robust colon targeting is more likely to require layered design—regional protection, lesion recognition, and intracellular release—rather than reliance on a single trigger.

Recent reviews have separately examined extracellular-vesicle engineering across multiple physiological barriers,23 plant-derived vesicle-like nanoparticles for oral ulcerative-colitis therapy,25 and mesenchymal stem/stromal cell-derived extracellular vesicles in inflammatory bowel disease.29,49 Taken together, these reviews do not provide a unified colon-specific engineering framework that links gastrointestinal regional barriers and triggers—including pH, transit time, mucus, microbiota-derived enzymes, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs)—to surface and cargo engineering, disease-specific target-cell biology in IBD versus CRC, administration route, manufacturability, and clinical-regulatory readiness. The distinctive aim of the present review is therefore not simply to catalogue sEV engineering methods, but to organize them into a disease- and route-oriented decision framework for colon-targeted delivery. Important unresolved questions include how sEVs terminology and source heterogeneity should be standardized; which targeting strategy is most appropriate for a given disease, cargo, and route; how microbiome variability alters release and response; which potency assays predict clinical benefit; and whether added engineering complexity produces sufficient benefit to justify scale-up and cost. Accordingly, this review critically synthesizes sEVs biology, preparation and characterization, cargo loading, surface engineering, colon-specific targeting and responsive release, microbiome interactions, disease mechanisms, clinical evidence, and regulatory considerations. Overview of sEVs

Biogenesis, Molecular Composition, and Functional Mechanisms of sEVs

Extracellular vesicles comprise heterogeneous lipid-bilayer particles released by cells. Operationally, the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles recommends describing vesicles by physical characteristics, biochemical composition, or experimental source when endosomal origin has not been directly demonstrated. Exosomes are generated as intraluminal vesicles within multivesicular bodies and are released after fusion with the plasma membrane, whereas microvesicles arise predominantly by outward budding of the plasma membrane; apoptotic bodies are generated during programmed cell death.50–52 Because common isolation methods do not completely separate these populations, the operational term “sEVs” is used throughout this review for particles within the small-size range unless biogenesis has been established.

sEVs biogenesis and cargo sorting involve endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT)-dependent machinery, ESCRT-independent lipid pathways, tetraspanins, Rab GTPases, and SNARE proteins.53 Cargo selection is not random: RNA-binding proteins, sequence motifs, post-translational modifications, membrane microdomains, and donor-cell state influence enrichment of proteins, lipids, messenger RNAs, microRNAs, long non-coding RNAs, and DNA fragments.54,55 The membrane is enriched in cholesterol, sphingomyelin, ceramide, and proteins including CD9, CD63, CD81, Alix, and TSG101, although marker abundance varies by source and preparation. Recipient cells internalize sEVs through clathrin- or caveolin-dependent endocytosis, macropinocytosis, phagocytosis, receptor-ligand binding, or direct membrane fusion. Subsequent endosomal escape or lysosomal processing determines whether cargo reaches the cytosol and remains functional.56–59

Comparison of sEV Isolation and Purification Technologies

Preparation of therapeutic sEVs is a multistep process rather than a single isolation step. A concise workflow comprises: selection and qualification of the donor source; controlled cell or biological-fluid collection; removal of cells, debris, and large vesicles; enrichment by ultracentrifugation, filtration, size-exclusion chromatography, precipitation, immunoaffinity, or microfluidics; orthogonal characterization of particle number, size, morphology, markers, purity, and biological activity; cargo loading or surface engineering; removal of unincorporated reagents; sterile formulation; and stability testing. Since high recovery, high purity, preserved functionality, throughput, and scalability are seldom achieved simultaneously, the choice of methods should be carefully aligned with the intended application (Table 2 and Figure 2). Beyond the isolation protocol, storage buffer, temperature, freeze-thaw exposure, and sample concentration are additional critical variables that substantially influence sEV recovery and potency.41,60

Ultracentrifugation (100,000–150,000 g) remains one of the most widely used methods for bulk sEV enrichment because it can process relatively large sample volumes using well-established workflows.84 However, it is time-consuming, requires specialized equipment, and high shear forces can damage vesicles, while co-pelleted protein aggregates limit purity.85,86 By comparison, size-based methods (ultrafiltration and size-exclusion chromatography) offer milder conditions—especially SEC, which preserves vesicle integrity—but neither fully removes similarly sized lipoproteins, and SEC suffers from low throughput and diluted eluates.87–89 Polymer precipitation (eg, PEG) is even simpler and faster, ideal for small volumes, yet it yields the lowest purity because reagents co-precipitate abundant proteins and lipoproteins, and residual polymers interfere with downstream assays.90 Stimuli-responsive polymers have emerged to improve purity and recovery but remain laboratory-stage.91

Immunoaffinity capture can provide high-purity, subset-selective isolation by targeting exosomal surface markers (CD63, CD81, EpCAM), making it uniquely suited for diagnostic applications requiring origin discrimination.92 In contrast, its high cost, limited capture capacity, potential elution-induced activity loss, and restriction to marker-positive subsets hinder large-scale use. Microfluidic technologies integrate multiple principles (immunoaffinity, size, acoustics, dielectrophoresis) on miniaturized chips, enabling automated, low-consumption isolation directly from whole blood with preserved bioactivity.79,80,93 While microfluidics holds great promise for clinical diagnostics and point-of-care testing, its processing throughput remains lower than ultracentrifugation or precipitation, and scale-up poses significant engineering challenges—mirroring the scalability limitations of immunoaffinity but offering greater flexibility in sample type and labeling options.

Advances in kits and novel platforms have aimed to bridge the gap between purity, speed, and scalability. Early kits like ExoQuick (polymer precipitation) prioritized speed, whereas Qiagen’s membrane affinity improved reproducibility for GMP compliance, and MagCapture enabled clinical throughput via magnetic beads. Newer designs such as Exodisc integrate filtration for efficient urine processing; ultrathin membranes support continuous low-cost production; ExoSearch offers closed-loop analysis; and iKnife uses AI to optimize parameters and reduce variability.94,95 None of these emerging techniques, however, completely resolve all trade-offs between purity, yield, vesicle integrity, and throughput—highlighting the need for method selection based on specific research or production goals.

In summary, sEVs isolation requires trade-offs among purity, yield, throughput, and vesicle integrity. No single method is optimal for all applications. Future development should prioritize scalable, standardized isolation systems that preserve structural and functional integrity and are compatible with clinical translation.

Key Technologies for sEV Characterization

Although substantial progress has been achieved in sEV isolation and purification, preparations may still contain proteins, lipoproteins, nucleic-acid complexes, or other vesicles that confound biological interpretation and reduce therapeutic consistency. Comprehensive characterization and quality control are therefore essential.52 sEV characterization should address at least three domains: particle concentration and size, morphology, and biochemical identity. Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA), Tunable Resistive Pulse Sensing (TRPS), and Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) are commonly employed to quantify the particle concentration and size distribution of sEVs in the sample, representing the most fundamental and initial step in sEV characterization.96–98 Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and cryo-electron microscopy can visualize vesicular morphology. Conventional dehydration-based electron microscopy often produces cup-shaped profiles, which should be interpreted as preparation-related artifacts rather than the native morphology of sEVs in solution. Morphology should therefore be evaluated together with complementary methods such as cryo-electron microscopy or liquid-phase AFM.99,100 It should be noted that different characterization methods and equipment may lead to differences in detection results. Therefore, accurate characterization of individual sEVs typically necessitates the integration of at least two complementary methodologies for validation.52

Commonly utilized markers for exosomal surface identification encompass tetraspanin proteins (eg, CD9, CD63, and CD81) and late endosome-associated proteins (eg, Tsg101 and Alix).101 ISEV guidance recommends assessing multiple complementary positive markers together with source-appropriate negative or contaminant markers rather than relying on a fixed universal marker panel.102 Accordingly, transmembrane proteins (eg, CD9, CD63, and CD81) and cytosolic EV-associated proteins (eg, ALIX and TSG101) should be assessed alongside source-appropriate negative or contaminant markers to support EV identity and preparation purity.103,104

Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) has emerged as a powerful complementary tool for sEV characterization, offering unique advantages beyond conventional techniques. Unlike ensemble methods such as NTA or DLS, AFM enables label-free, high-resolution imaging of individual sEVs under near-physiological conditions (eg, liquid environment), preserving their native morphology and mechanical properties.105 AFM provides three-dimensional topographic images with sub-nanometer vertical resolution, directly revealing the size, height, and surface ultrastructure of sEVs. More importantly, it simultaneously quantifies nanomechanical parameters such as Young’s modulus, adhesion force, and deformation, allowing discrimination of sEVs subpopulations based on their biophysical signatures.106 In contrast to the “cup-shaped” or “biconcave” artifacts commonly observed under dried TEM/SEM conditions, AFM in liquid typically visualizes spherical or disk-like sEVs with intact membranes.

Nano-flow cytometry (NanoFCM) has emerged as a powerful complementary tool for sEV characterization, offering distinct advantages over ensemble methods such as DLS. Unlike DLS, NanoFCM enables direct, label-free detection of individual sEVs at the single-particle level, resolving particles as small as 40 nm with high sizing resolution covering the entire sEV size range of 40–200.107 It simultaneously quantifies particle size, concentration, and biochemical properties such as surface markers at rates up to 10,000 particles per minute, providing statistically robust data from as little as 1 μL of sample.108 In contrast to the population-averaged outputs of conventional techniques, NanoFCM resolves sEVs subpopulations based on both physical size and molecular signatures, revealing heterogeneity that is otherwise masked. While AFM delivers three-dimensional topography and nanomechanics of individual sEVs, NanoFCM offers high-throughput, multiparameter analysis of sEV populations.109 Due to the inherent variability among different characterization platforms, the integration of NanoFCM with at least one orthogonal method (eg, AFM for structural details, NTA for cross-validation) is strongly recommended to ensure accurate and comprehensive sEV profiling.110

In summary, existing isolation and characterization technologies provide a strong research foundation, but cross-platform variability, incomplete impurity removal, and limited process standardization remain major barriers to industrial-scale manufacturing. Donor-cell physiology must also be controlled because hypoxia, three-dimensional culture, senescence, inflammation, and other stressors can alter sEVs yield and cargo composition; such conditioning may improve a desired function but can also introduce unintended biological activity.111 Future development should prioritize scalable, closed, and standardized processes that preserve structural integrity and demonstrate consistent potency.

Drug Loading Strategies for Engineered sEVs

A key advantage of sEVs as drug-delivery vehicles is their ability to protect and transport small molecules, proteins, and nucleic acids. However, efficient incorporation into the lumen or membrane remains technically challenging. Current approaches can be classified as post-isolation loading through physicochemical membrane perturbation and pre-isolation loading by engineering donor cells to package cargo during biogenesis (Figure 2 and Table 4). Each strategy must balance loading efficiency, cargo activity, vesicle integrity, reproducibility, and downstream purification.112,113

Physicochemical Methods: Exogenous Loading via Membrane Perturbation

Physicochemical methods mainly use physical fields or chemical reagents to transiently alter the permeability of the exosomal lipid bilayer, allowing therapeutic molecules in the sEVs suspension to cross the membrane into the lumen. The core design principle of this paradigm is the precise control of reversible membrane rearrangement, with the goal of improving loading efficiency while minimizing irreversible damage to the intrinsic structure (eg, surface targeting proteins) and function of sEVs.126

Electroporation is one of the most widely used physical loading techniques. It works by applying high-voltage electrical pulses to transiently generate hydrophilic nanopores in the lipid bilayer of cells or vesicles, providing a diffusion pathway for charged molecules (such as siRNA, miRNA, or certain small-molecule chemotherapeutic drugs) in the surrounding medium. After the pulse ceases, the fluidity of the lipid bilayer enables spontaneous rearrangement and pore repair, thereby entrapping the drug inside.127 This technique is particularly effective for nucleic acid loading, with reported siRNA loading efficiencies exceeding 30% in some studies.128 Nevertheless, the critical design parameters include optimization of buffer ionic strength and electrical pulse conditions to avoid nucleic acid aggregation or irreversible fusion and rupture of exosomal membranes caused by excessive electric fields. For instance, by optimizing electroporation parameters, doxorubicin has been successfully loaded into sEVs on the surface of intraocular lenses, effectively inhibiting the proliferation of lens epithelial cells.129 In colon-targeted applications, researchers employed electroporation to load CX5461 into extracellular vesicles derived from Sophora japonica flowers, achieving a loading efficiency of 23%. In a DSS-induced colitis mouse model, these engineered sEVs significantly promoted an anti-inflammatory (M2) phenotypic polarization of macrophages in colonic tissues and effectively repaired the intestinal barrier function.130

Sonication and extrusion represent another category of loading strategies that rely on mechanical shear forces. Sonication increases membrane permeability via transient cavitation-induced disturbances, while extrusion forces sEVs through nanoporous membranes with smaller pore sizes smaller than the sEVs diameter; the intense mechanical shear and deformation cause temporary membrane disruption, followed by re-sealing, thus encapsulating drugs from the surrounding medium.131 In the context of colorectal cancer treatment research, 5-fluorouracil was loaded into milk-derived sEVs via sonication, yielding a drug loading efficiency of 37.65%.132 This formulation exhibited no obvious toxicity in Caco-2 cell uptake assays when compared with the saponin method. Studies have shown that extrusion significantly improves loading efficiency for large proteins (eg, catalase) compared with simple co-incubation.133 The extrusion method generally achieves a loading efficiency of 20–40% for macromolecular proteins, which is 2.45 times higher than that obtained with the simple co-incubation method.134 However, an inherent drawback is that mechanical forces may alter sEVs size or cause surface protein shedding, requiring a balance between loading efficiency and vesicle integrity.135

In addition, chemical-assisted approaches such as saponin treatment and freeze–thaw cycles are commonly employed. As a surfactant, saponin increases lipid bilayer fluidity by extracting membrane cholesterol, facilitating the entry of macromolecules.136 Studies have demonstrated that the saponin-assisted approach elevates the loading efficiency of catalase from 4.9% ± 0.5% (achieved by conventional incubation) to 18.5% ± 1.3%, but rigorous elimination of residual saponin is necessary to prevent cytotoxicity.123 Freeze–thaw cycles mechanically disrupt and reorganize membrane structures through repeated ice crystal formation and melting. For instance, the freeze-thaw method enables successful construction of sEVs–nanoliposome hybrids that retain the key characteristics of exosomal proteins, lipid membranes, and encapsulated drugs.137,138 Although these methods are operationally simple, they often require rigorous removal of chemical reagents or carry the risk of vesicle aggregation and size enlargement, limiting their use in clinical translation settings that demand high purity and homogeneity.139

Genetic Engineering Methods: Endogenous Loading via Donor Cell Modification

In contrast to physicochemical methods, genetic engineering shifts the loading process from in vitro to in situ by genetically modifying donor cells to naturally sort therapeutic cargo into sEVs during their biogenesis.140 A major advantage of this paradigm is that it maximally preserves the native structure and biological activity of sEVs, making it especially suitable for the delivery of proteins and complex nucleic acid drugs.

The fusion protein strategy is a classic approach for endogenous loading of protein drugs. Its design principle involves genetically fusing the protein of interest to highly abundant exosomal membrane proteins (eg, lysosome-associated membrane protein 2b (Lamp2b) or tetraspanins CD9, CD63, and CD81)141 When expressed in donor cells, the fusion protein is naturally enriched in the exosomal membrane during biogenesis. Using this principle, researchers fused the rabies virus glycoprotein-derived targeting peptide RVG with Lamp2b, achieving active targeting of sEVs to the central nervous system.114,142 Furthermore, fusing soluble proteins to the cytoplasmic domains of these membrane proteins, or using viral proteins with efficient sorting signals (eg, the pX protein of HAV), can also efficiently package target proteins into the exosomal lumen.143

Nucleic acid-binding protein-mediated loading is tailored for nucleic acid drugs such as miRNAs, siRNAs, or mRNAs. Researchers fuse specific RNA-binding proteins (eg, HuR, MS2 coat protein) to exosomal membrane proteins (eg, CD9), while introducing corresponding binding motifs (eg, AU-rich elements or MS2 stem-loops) into the target RNA.144 When co-expressed in donor cells, the target RNA is efficiently recruited into sEVs via protein-RNA interactions. For example, the CD9-HuR system for loading miRNA inhibitors has demonstrated effective target gene silencing in both in vitro and in vivo models.145

Light- or chemically induced protein-protein interactions represent more sophisticated pre-loading strategies. For example, the EXPLORs technology uses the heterodimerization of the photosensitive proteins CRY2 and CIBN under blue light illumination to achieve controllable recruitment of target proteins into sEVs at specific time points.146 Such “on-demand loading” provides a powerful tool for studying the biological functions of proteins at specific stages and developing spatiotemporally controllable protein delivery systems.

In summary, physicochemical methods are simple and universal, suitable for rapid loading of small molecules and selected nucleic acids but require caution regarding vesicle integrity. Genetic engineering approaches, despite higher technical barriers and longer processing times, may preserve vesicle structure while enabling more controlled cargo incorporation, but they also add manufacturing and regulatory complexity.147 Continued optimization of both strategy classes will be important for translational development.

Colon-Targeting Strategies of Engineered sEVs

Regional Localization Based on Physiological Barrier Differences (Passive Targeting)

The unique physiological environment of the colon provides a natural basis for the regional localization and site-specific release of engineered sEVs.25 The core design principle of passive targeting is to construct sEVs delivery systems that can remain stable during upper gastrointestinal transit and achieve “on-demand unlocking” specifically in the colonic microenvironment, thereby realizing route protection, colonic localization, and responsive release in an integrated manner (Figure 3).

Mechanistically, colon-specific targeting can be viewed as a four-step sequence: (1) protect sEVs from gastric acid, digestive enzymes, and premature small-intestinal release; (2) trigger regional exposure using distal-GI pH or microbiota-derived enzymes; (3) increase contact with the diseased mucosa through controlled mucoadhesion or mucus penetration; and (4) add lesion-selective recognition or activation using inflammatory signals or cell-surface receptors. Regional release alone therefore does not guarantee disease specificity; retention, epithelial access, and lesion-triggered activation must be considered together.

pH-Responsive Passive Targeting and Release

pH-responsive delivery is among the most established passive approaches, but gastrointestinal pH is dynamic and disease-dependent rather than a fixed sequence of values. The stomach is strongly acidic, the small intestine is generally near neutral, and the distal ileum/colon may become neutral to mildly alkaline; active IBD, diet, transit, and concomitant medication can shift these profiles.42 Consequently, pH-sensitive coatings should be validated under biorelevant ranges and, where possible, combined with a second trigger.148,149

One strategy encapsulates sEVs within pH-responsive polymer matrices. Mesenchymal stromal cell-derived sEVs have been incorporated into alginate microspheres, coated with gelatin, and protected by Eudragit® FS 30 D (a methacrylic acid-methyl methacrylate copolymer) to limit release in the upper gastrointestinal tract. Dissolution of the enteric layer at higher intestinal pH enabled preferential colonic release and improved anti-inflammatory activity in experimental colitis.150,151

An alternative approach employs layer-by-layer coating with pH-sensitive polymers. Human placental mesenchymal stem cell-derived sEVs coated with N-(2-hydroxypropyl)-3-trimethylammonium chitosan chloride (HTCC) and oxidized konjac glucomannan (OKGM) exhibited resistance to gastrointestinal degradation and enabled targeted release in inflamed colons, where the slightly alkaline colonic pH triggers coating dissociation and subsequent sEVs release.152

Enzyme-Responsive Passive Targeting and Release

The colonic microbiota provides diverse enzymatic activities, including glycosidases and polysaccharidases, as well as azoreductases and nitroreductases, that are scarce in the upper gastrointestinal tract and can serve as biological triggers for colonic release.43

Pectin resists human gastric and small-intestinal digestion but can be cleaved by microbiota-derived pectinases, making it a useful component of enzyme-responsive matrices.14,153 Pectin-calcium hydrogels have been explored for sEVs encapsulation, with approximately 70% greater release in enzyme-containing simulated colonic fluid than in enzyme-free medium.154 Beyond sEVs-specific formulations, studies on conventional CTDDS have also shown that pectin source, degree of esterification, coating architecture, and microbial activity materially influence release.14,15 These findings are relevant to sEVs formulations because dysbiosis or reduced bacterial diversity may weaken single enzyme-dependent triggers; time/pH or pH/enzyme combinations can provide redundancy.16

Time-Dependent and Transit-Aware Passive Targeting

Time-dependent systems use a programmed lag phase generated by coating hydration, swelling, erosion, or rupture to delay sEV release until the dosage form is expected to reach the distal ileum or colon.155 Their targeting mechanism is temporal rather than tissue specific because release is governed primarily by coating kinetics and an assumed small intestinal transit window. However, gastric emptying and small intestinal transit vary substantially among individuals and may be further altered by intestinal inflammation, motility disturbances, and IBD-associated lesions.156–158 Consequently, timing alone may lead to premature release or release after the intended site has already been passed. Transit-aware timing should therefore be used as a secondary safeguard in combination with elements that respond to pH, microbial enzymes, mucus properties, or inflammatory signals.152 These systems should also be evaluated under fed, fasted, and disease-relevant transit conditions, with direct confirmation of the release location whenever feasible.159

pH/Enzyme Dual-Responsive Passive Systems

Single-response mechanisms may fail because pH, transit time, microbial composition, and enzymatic activity differ across individuals and disease states. Dual-responsive platforms therefore combine independent triggers to improve release reliability. For example, a functionalized chitosan/HP-β-cyclodextrin hydrogel combined pH- and enzyme-sensitive elements for two-stage oral delivery of plant-derived nanovesicles and kaempferol in experimental ulcerative colitis, achieving greater release under simulated colonic conditions than under upper-gastrointestinal conditions.160,161 The additional complexity, however, must be justified by reproducible manufacturing and a demonstrable improvement over simpler formulations.

Mucus-Interactive and Inflamed-Mucosa Retention

The colonic mucus layer is both a barrier and a potential retention site.162 Mucoadhesive cationic or polysaccharide coatings can increase residence time through electrostatic and hydrogen-bond interactions, whereas hydrophilic or near-neutral surfaces can reduce nonspecific mucin binding and favor penetration toward the epithelium.25 In active IBD, mucus composition, thickness, and barrier integrity are altered; consequently, a design optimized for healthy mucus may either become trapped superficially or cross too rapidly. Colon targeting therefore requires an explicit balance between retention and penetration, validated under disease-relevant mucus conditions rather than buffer alone.13,163

Inflammation-Responsive Regional Targeting

Inflamed colonic lesions exhibit higher oxidative stress, increased matrix-metalloproteinase activity, and disrupted epithelial barriers.164,165 These features can be used as regional activation mechanisms by incorporating ROS-cleavable materials, MMP-cleavable linkers, or inflammation-responsive surface elements that expose targeting ligands or release cargo preferentially within diseased mucosa.166 This strategy differs from pH- or microbiota-dependent release because the trigger is linked to inflammatory pathology rather than colon location alone.

Active Navigation Based on Receptor–Ligand Recognition

Passive localization solves the problem of “release in the colonic region” but cannot distinguish between “healthy colon” and “diseased colon”. The core of active targeting strategies is to equip sEVs with the ability to recognize specific markers on diseased cells, thereby achieving precise localization at the “specific address” level. According to the manner of ligand anchoring on the membrane, active targeting strategies can be further divided into genetic engineering and chemical modification methods.

Genetic Engineering: Display of Targeting Ligands Using Scaffold Proteins

Genetic engineering modifies donor cells so that the sEVs they secrete naturally display targeting ligands on their surface. The key advantage of this paradigm is that it maximally preserves the integrity and native topological structure of the exosomal membrane.167

The Lamp2b scaffold system is the most widely used genetic engineering platform. Lamp2b is a type I transmembrane protein with an N-terminal domain extending outside the exosomal membrane and a C-terminus located inside the lumen, allowing the N-terminus to serve as a fusion display site for exogenous ligands.168 This design was first applied by Alvarez-Erviti et al to display the rabies virus glycoprotein-derived peptide RVG, achieving targeted delivery of siRNA to the mouse brain.114

In the field of colon targeting, researchers have used this strategy to display arginine-glycine-aspartate (RGD) peptides on the sEV surface. RGD peptides specifically recognize integrins αvβ3/αvβ5, which are highly expressed in colon cancer cells and tumor neovascular endothelial cells.46 RGD-engineered sEVs co-deliver miR-21 inhibitors and 5-fluorouracil, significantly enhancing the therapeutic efficacy against colon cancer by targeting and suppressing drug resistance-related genes.47 Similarly, anti-A33 antibody-engineered sEVs enable the specific delivery of doxorubicin to colorectal cancer cells overexpressing the A33 antigen.48

CAP-sEVs represent a recent extension of the Lamp2b display strategy. Inspired by the evolution of adeno-associated virus capsid proteins, researchers fused functional peptides responsible for blood-brain barrier crossing and brain parenchyma targeting (including nuclear localization signals, endosomal escape signals, and two surface-exposed loop regions) from the AAV9 capsid in tandem to the N-terminus of Lamp2b, constructing a CAP-Lamp2b fusion protein.169 After displaying these CAP peptides on sEVs derived from bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells, intravenous injection achieved transgene expression throughout the brain, with delivery efficiency increased by more than 20-fold compared with the control group and significantly reduced liver distribution.170 Although developed for brain rather than colon delivery, this study illustrates how multivalent peptide modules can be displayed on sEVs; colon-specific candidates would require independent screening and validation.

Chemical Modification: Ligand Display Based on Lipid Anchoring and Covalent Conjugation

Chemical modification directly conjugates targeting ligands onto the surface of isolated and purified sEVs. Its advantage lies in being unrestricted by the modifiability of donor cells, making it suitable for sEVs from various sources.

The DSPE-PEG lipid anchoring system is a classical strategy that uses the FDA-approved pharmaceutical excipient DSPE (1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine) to anchor ligands onto the exosomal membrane. The hydrophobic double chains of DSPE can spontaneously insert into the exosomal lipid bilayer, while the hydrophilic PEG chain extends outside the membrane, with its terminus able to link various targeting molecules.171 Kim et al anchored aminoethyl anisamide, a high-affinity ligand for sigma receptors, onto macrophage sEV surfaces via DSPE-PEG, achieving targeted delivery of paclitaxel to lung metastases and markedly improving therapeutic efficacy.172

A cholesterol-anchored RNA nanostructure provides a well-defined strategy for post-isolation ligand display on EVs. Positioning membrane-anchoring cholesterol at the tail of a three-way-junction RNA construct promotes its insertion into the vesicle membrane while exposing folate or RNA aptamers on the outer surface. In a primary study, folate-displaying EVs loaded with survivin siRNA inhibited tumor growth in a patient-derived colorectal cancer xenograft model, supporting the feasibility of combining cholesterol-mediated membrane anchoring with folate-receptor-directed delivery.173

The DBCO (dibenzocyclooctyne) click chemistry platform is a widely used bioorthogonal approach for chemical modification. DBCO can undergo strain-promoted azide–alkyne cycloaddition with azide groups under physiological conditions without copper catalysts. The mildness and efficiency of this bioorthogonal reaction make it particularly suitable for the modification of sensitive biological nanoparticles.174 In brain targeting studies, researchers conjugated mitochondria-targeting ligands to brain-derived sEV surfaces using the DBCO-SPAAC reaction, successfully preserving the morphological integrity and endocytic dynamics of sEVs.175 In colon targeting applications, this strategy can be used to controllably conjugate colon cancer-specific aptamers (such as AS1411 and EpCAM aptamers) onto sEV surfaces.

The combination of metabolic glycoengineering and DBCO click chemistry provides another modular route for sEVs modification.176 Researchers added tetraacetylated N-azidoacetyl-D-mannosamine to the donor cell culture system, allowing cells to integrate azide groups into the glycan chains of surface glycoproteins during metabolic processes.177 Subsequently, fluorescent dyes or targeting ligands were conjugated to the secreted sEV surfaces via DBCO click chemistry.178 This tandem strategy of “endogenous labeling + exogenous conjugation” avoids in vitro membrane perturbation and enables efficient and specific sEVs labeling and tracking.

Chemical Biology-Mediated Programmed Anchoring

Although genetic engineering and chemical modification have been widely used for sEVs targeting engineering, both have inherent limitations: genetic engineering relies on the modifiability of donor cells and cannot be applied to sEVs from isolated clinical samples; chemical modification often lacks site specificity and may mask key functional domains on the sEV surface.179 Chemical biology hybrid strategies have emerged accordingly, with the core design principle of integrating the advantages of different technologies to achieve efficient and controllable ligand display while maintaining site specificity.180

For instance, the sialyltransferase CSTII was utilized to install 9-N3-Neu5Ac onto the glycan chains on the surface of sEVs, followed by DBCO-based click chemistry to conjugate biotin or fluorescent probes, thereby enabling the engineering of sEVs derived from various cell types, including A549, PC3, and COLO-1 cells.181

Additionally, Sortase-mediated site-specific ligation represents a representative example of this paradigm. Sortase A is a transpeptidase derived from Staphylococcus aureus that recognizes the LPXTG motif and catalyzes the formation of an amide bond with oligoglycine (GGG).182 Zhang et al applied this enzymatic tool to the functional modification of milk-derived sEVs. They first synthesized an NH2-GG-PEG2000-DSPE molecule bearing diglycine, which was inserted into the exosomal membrane via hydrophobic DSPE anchoring. Next, Sortase A and the EGFR nanobody 7D12 carrying the LPETG motif were added to the system.183 Sortase A specifically recognized the LPETG motif of the nanobody and covalently linked it to the GG group on the membrane, thereby achieving site-specific display of the nanobody on the sEV surface. Doxorubicin loading experiments showed that 7D12-functionalized sEVs specifically killed EGFR-overexpressing tumor cells, while exhibiting significantly reduced toxicity toward EGFR-negative cells.184

This chemo-enzymatic hybrid strategy offers three potential advantages: (1) Sortase-catalyzed ligation is site-selective and may preserve ligand activity; (2) the reaction conditions are comparatively mild and can limit membrane disruption; and (3) the modular PEG-DSPE anchoring and LPXTG-ligand components facilitate systematic ligand substitution and screening. These advantages should nevertheless be confirmed for each vesicle source and ligand through structural, functional, and stability assays.

This platform may be adaptable to colon-targeted sEVs by coupling validated LPXTG-tagged ligands against accessible lesion-associated receptors, such as integrin α4β7, CD98, or CD206. However, receptor specificity, ligand orientation, conjugation efficiency, product stability, and in vivo off-target distribution must be demonstrated before the approach can be considered broadly transferable.

Comparative Selection of Surface-Engineering Strategies

Surface engineering spans genetically encoded peptide or protein display, lipid insertion, antibody or aptamer conjugation, glycoengineering, copper-free click chemistry, enzymatic ligation, membrane fusion, and receptor-directed hybrid systems.167,171 Genetic display offers controlled topology and stable inheritance from donor cells but requires a modifiable production cell and may reduce yield. Lipid anchoring is rapid and source-agnostic but can exchange or desorb in biological fluids. Covalent antibody and aptamer conjugation can increase affinity, yet random coupling may mask binding domains or alter membrane proteins. Glycoengineering and bioorthogonal click chemistry provide modularity and site selectivity, whereas membrane fusion can combine the loading capacity of synthetic particles with biological membranes but introduces compositional and regulatory complexity.174–184 A rational choice should therefore consider target abundance and accessibility, ligand internalization, receptor heterogeneity, administration route, product stability, analytical measurability, and manufacturing cost rather than targeting affinity alone.

Bidirectional Interactions Between Engineered sEVs and the Gut Microbiome

The gut microbiome is both a targeting variable and a potential therapeutic mediator. Microbiota-derived enzymes can activate pectin-, dextran-, azo-, or polysaccharide-based carriers, but antibiotic exposure, diet, inflammation, and inter-individual dysbiosis can alter the timing and completeness of release.43 Conversely, plant-, food-, mammalian-, or microbial-derived vesicles may change bacterial abundance, metabolite production, epithelial signaling, and mucosal immunity.25,163,185 For example, nanovesicle-associated interventions have been reported to alter microbial composition and metabolites while improving experimental colitis or CRC endpoints;186 however, causality is often difficult to separate from reduced inflammation itself.187

Microbial membrane vesicles also participate in host-microbiota communication by carrying proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, and microbe-associated molecular patterns across mucus and epithelial barriers.188 Depending on microbial origin and dose, these vesicles may support barrier homeostasis, facilitate immune education, or amplify inflammation and tumor-promoting signaling.189,190 Engineered sEVs development should therefore measure microbiome-dependent variability rather than treating the microbiota solely as a release switch. Recommended studies include antibiotic or gnotobiotic controls, longitudinal metagenomics and metabolomics, fecal transfer or defined-consortium experiments when causal claims are made, and assessment of how the formulation itself changes microbial ecology.

Responsive Release Strategies of Engineered sEVs

The precise spatiotemporal control of drug release at the target site represents the ultimate goal of any drug delivery system. Although surface engineering endows sEVs with the ability to recognize and bind to diseased cells, therapeutic cargoes must ultimately be released in a biologically active form to exert their pharmacological effects. To better evaluate the targeting efficiency of engineered sEVs, a systematic evaluation system is required, including in vitro binding, in vivo distribution, targeting specificity, and functional efficacy (Table 5). Premature drug leakage during systemic circulation or gastrointestinal transit not only reduces therapeutic efficacy but also increases the risk of off-target toxicity.25 Conversely, inefficient release even after reaching the target site renders even the most sophisticated targeting strategies ineffective. Therefore, engineering of sEVs with stimuli-responsive release mechanisms has emerged as a key frontier in the development of next-generation colon-targeted therapies.

The colonic environment provides multiple endogenous signals for responsive release, including pH, microbiota-derived enzymes, reactive oxygen species, matrix metalloproteinases, and disease-associated metabolic changes.148,163,191 None is perfectly specific or constant. Single Physiological Signal-Responsive Systems

Reactive Oxygen Species-Responsive Release

Both inflammatory bowel disease and colorectal cancer are characterized by significantly elevated levels of reactive oxygen species in the lesional microenvironment.191 In inflamed colonic mucosa, infiltrating immune cells-particularly neutrophils and macrophages-generate large amounts of superoxide anions and hydrogen peroxide via respiratory burst.192 This pathological ROS accumulation not only drives tissue damage but can also serve as a disease-specific trigger for drug release.

One strategy involves directly loading ROS scavengers into sEVs. Regulatory T cell-derived sEVs loaded with cerium oxide nanoparticles were developed as bifunctional antioxidants and anti-inflammatory agents for IBD treatment.193 Cerium oxide nanoparticles possess potent ROS-scavenging properties due to their unique autocatalytic redox activity cycling between Ce3+ and Ce4+ oxidation states. In both TNF-α-stimulated cellular models and DSS-induced mouse colitis models, exo@nCeO effectively reduced ROS levels in intestinal epithelial cells, maintained intestinal barrier integrity, and hindered IBD progression.194 In this formulation, sEVs serve as carriers for ROS-scavenging nanozymes, coupling vesicle delivery with antioxidant activity at inflamed sites.

Dual and Multi-Responsive Systems

Although single stimulus-responsive systems offer improved selectivity over non-responsive formulations, they remain vulnerable to inter-individual physiological variations and fluctuations in disease states. For example, colonic pH varies significantly among individuals and may be altered in disease states such as active IBD. Similarly, microbial enzymatic activity is influenced by diet, medication use, and disease-associated dysbiosis.195 To overcome these limitations, researchers have increasingly focused on developing dual or multi-responsive systems that integrate two or more trigger mechanisms, thereby enhancing targeting precision and release reliability.

A representative example is the ROS/enzyme dual-responsive oral drug delivery system developed for colorectal cancer therapy. This system is designed to respond to both elevated ROS levels characteristic of CRC and overexpressed β-mannanase in colorectal tissues. The design comprises two hierarchical components: (1) ROS-responsive prodrug micelles composed of methoxy polyethylene glycol and mesalazine linked via a thioether bond cleavable under oxidative conditions; (2) konjac glucomannan (KGM) microspheres encapsulating curcumin-loaded prodrug micelles, which can be specifically degraded by colonic β-mannanase. Following oral administration, the KGM shell is first degraded by β-mannanase in the colon, releasing the curcumin-loaded prodrug micelles. Subsequently, exposure to the high-ROS tumor microenvironment triggers thioether bond cleavage and site-specific drug release.185 In azoxymethane/dextran sulfate sodium (AOM/DSS)-induced CRC mice, this dual-responsive system significantly improved survival rates, suppressed tumor growth, reduced inflammatory cytokine expression, and restored near-normal gut microbiota composition.

Similarly, a functionalized chitosan/HP-β-CD hydrogel platform combining pH-sensitive and enzyme-sensitive elements has been developed for the synergistic “two-stage” oral delivery of sEVs and kaempferol in ulcerative colitis therapy.196 This dual-responsive system achieved over 85% drug release within 48 hours in simulated colonic environments, significantly outperforming single-responsive systems.

Enzyme-Cleavable Activated Smart Delivery Systems

A particularly sophisticated class of responsive systems involves the use of enzyme-cleavable linkers to tether therapeutic or targeting moieties to the sEV surface, enabling programmed activation in the diseased microenvironment. We term this design concept “programmable anchoring”, which combines the targeting specificity of surface engineering with the release control of stimuli-responsive chemistry.

In the context of intestinal inflammation, a representative example of this paradigm comes from recent work on reprogrammed Treg cell-derived sEVs. Researchers engineered sEVs to deliver the antioxidant trace element selenium for IBD therapy, with one key innovation: synthetic mitochondria-targeting peptide SS-31 was modified on sEVs via a peptide linker designed to be specifically cleaved by matrix metalloproteinases significantly upregulated in inflammatory lesions.164 This design achieves spatiotemporally controlled activation via a two-step process: (1) engineered sEVs home to inflamed intestinal tissues, then (2) elevated MMP activity cleaves the peptide linker, releasing the SS-31 peptide and enabling its mitochondria-targeting function. The resulting system precisely scavenges excess mitochondrial ROS in inflamed intestinal regions, prevents PANoptosis-a coordinated form of programmed cell death encompassing pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis-and restores intestinal barrier integrity. This represents a paradigm in which sEVs act not merely as passive carriers but as actively unlocked vehicles whose full therapeutic potential is unleashed only upon encountering disease-specific enzymatic activity.

Inflammatory Bowel Disease: Barrier Repair and Immune Rebalancing

In IBD models, therapeutic sEVs exert their effects through several interconnected mechanisms. Within the intestinal epithelium, uptake of sEVs enhances tight-junction and adherens-junction protein expression, stimulates mucin production, reduces epithelial apoptosis and PANoptosis, and facilitates wound healing and barrier restoration.49,130,151,152 In the immune compartment, sEV cargo suppresses NF-κB and related pro-inflammatory pathways, lowers levels of tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6), promotes macrophage polarization from a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, and rebalances the Th17/Treg axis.164,193,194 Additionally, antioxidant payloads can mitigate both mitochondrial and cytosolic oxidative stress.164,194 However, these mechanisms should not be generalized across different sEV preparations: their activity is critically dependent on donor cell source, cargo composition, dosage, administration route, and disease stage. Moreover, anti-inflammatory effects observed in acute murine colitis models do not, by themselves, predict durable mucosal healing in human patients.197

Colorectal Cancer: Cytotoxic Delivery and Tumor-Microenvironment Remodeling

In CRC, engineered sEVs are being used to increase intratumoral delivery of chemotherapy, small interfering RNA, microRNA modulators, and proteins.198–201 Proposed effects include induction of apoptosis, reversal of drug resistance, suppression of proliferation and epithelial-mesenchymal transition, inhibition of angiogenesis, macrophage or dendritic-cell reprogramming, and enhancement of antitumor T-cell responses.47,184,185,198,201–203 Ligand-directed systems targeting A33, integrins, folate receptors, EGFR, or aptamer-defined antigens can improve cellular uptake, whereas combination with immune-checkpoint blockade may help remodel an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.48,183,184,200,202,204 Such combinations should be benchmarked against both free-cargo and checkpoint-inhibitor controls, because increased immune-cell infiltration alone does not establish improved checkpoint responsiveness. A critical counterpoint is that tumor- or stromal-cell-derived sEVs may carry pro-angiogenic, pro-metastatic, or immunosuppressive cargo.205 Source qualification and comprehensive molecular profiling are therefore essential, and therapeutic benefit must be separated from possible parent-cell-derived tumor-promoting signals. Clinical Translation of sEVs as Drug Carriers: Current Status and Future Perspectives

Clinical investigation of extracellular-vesicle products has expanded rapidly, but engineered therapeutic sEVs remain at an early translational stage.202,212,213 No engineered sEV therapeutic dosage form has received full regulatory approval.120,214 Products that have reached clinical use are primarily diagnostic assays, whereas therapeutic candidates remain investigational.202,215 This distinction is important because analytical validation of an sEVs biomarker test does not establish manufacturing feasibility, dose reproducibility, or clinical efficacy for a therapeutic product.120,212,214

Clinical Trial Landscape

Most registered extracellular-vesicle studies evaluate naturally derived preparations, biomarkers, or unmodified products, whereas comparatively few investigate actively engineered therapeutic delivery systems.120,213 A systematic review and meta-analysis of 21 clinical reports found generally acceptable short-term safety across heterogeneous indications and preparations, but small samples, variable manufacturing, and inconsistent outcome reporting preclude broad conclusions regarding efficacy or class-wide safety.214 These limitations reinforce the need for product-specific dose, biodistribution, potency, and long-term follow-up data.

Diagnostic assays based on extracellular-vesicle-associated biomarkers have progressed faster than therapeutic products. The ExoDx Prostate IntelliScore urine assay illustrates the clinical translation of an extracellular-vesicle-associated molecular test.215 This diagnostic precedent supports the feasibility of standardized sample handling and analytical validation, but it should not be interpreted as validation of therapeutic sEVs manufacturing, dosing, or efficacy.

These outcomes illustrate that trial feasibility, route burden, eligibility criteria, and access to competing therapies are important translational constraints in addition to product biology.

A Phase I registry record describes intraperitoneal administration of human placental mesenchymal stromal cell-derived exosomes for preventing postoperative anastomotic leakage after low anterior resection for rectal cancer (NCT06536712). The study remains listed as not yet recruiting, although its estimated study dates have passed. Plant- and food-derived vesicles are attractive for oral delivery because of potential availability and lower production cost. Ginger-derived vesicles with or without curcumin (NCT04879810) have been registered for IBD, plant vesicle-conjugated curcumin has been investigated in colon cancer (NCT01294072), and milk-derived vesicles have been explored for dose tolerability relevant to IBS/IBD. Public registry information is incomplete for several studies, and particle dose, product identity, and results should be interpreted cautiously.

Translational Evidence and Current Limitations

Despite growing clinical interest, several interconnected challenges impede the regulatory approval and widespread adoption of sEV-based therapeutics.

First, biodistribution and targeting efficiency remain suboptimal. Intravenous sEVs are predominantly sequestered by the mononuclear phagocyte system (liver, spleen, lungs), resulting in low accumulation in colon lesions.32 Orally delivered sEVs must overcome gastric acid, enzymatic degradation, and mucus barriers, which severely reduce bioavailability.12,13

Second, scalable and standardized production under GMP conditions is limited by low yield, high cost, and lack of unified isolation protocols.36,216 The inherent heterogeneity of sEVs further complicates quality control and batch-to-batch consistency.37

Third, regulatory uncertainty persists. The FDA, EMA, and other agencies have not established specific guidelines for engineered sEVs therapeutics, leading to ambiguity in manufacturing standards, nonclinical evaluation, and clinical trial design.38,202

Key Scientific and Translational Challenges

To accelerate translation, future research should focus on four priorities: (1) Develop closed automated bioreactors and high-throughput isolation technologies for GMP-grade production.22,36 (2) Establish unified quality control standards covering particle size, concentration, surface markers, loading efficiency, purity, and safety.39,52 (3) Optimize colon-targeting and microenvironment-responsive design to enhance lesion-specific accumulation and therapeutic efficacy.25,120 (4) Promote regulatory harmonization and launch well-designed Phase I/II trials dedicated to engineered sEVs for colon diseases.38,202

Challenges and Prospects

Engineered sEVs provide a versatile platform for colon-targeted therapy, but added biological functionality is accompanied by source heterogeneity, analytical uncertainty, manufacturing complexity, and regulatory burden. Future progress will depend less on adding isolated engineering features and more on demonstrating that each feature improves a clinically relevant outcome relative to a simpler comparator.

Production, Quality Control, and Drug Loading: Current Bottlenecks and Emerging Solutions

Large-scale sEV production remains constrained by donor-cell productivity, media requirements, purification losses, and cost. No universal isolation method simultaneously maximizes yield, purity, integrity, and throughput.38,40,60 Closed bioreactors, tangential-flow filtration, scalable chromatography, microfluidic or acoustofluidic process analytics, and standardized storage can improve reproducibility, but each process change requires comparability testing.38,40,41,60 Process analytical technology should monitor cell health, particle output, impurities, and functional potency rather than particle count alone.213

For a clinical candidate, release specifications should be product- and mechanism-specific and should define acceptance criteria for identity, particle concentration and size distribution, vesicle-associated markers, purity and process-related impurities, cargo content or loading, sterility and microbial safety, residual reagents, and a potency assay linked to the proposed mechanism of action.

Low loading efficiency and limited cargo capacity remain central bottlenecks.113 Electroporation, sonication, extrusion, and membrane fusion can increase loading but may alter size, aggregation, membrane proteins, or cargo activity.123,128 Donor-cell engineering can improve endogenous loading and product uniformity but lengthens development and raises genetic and regulatory complexity.144–147 Hybrid sEV–liposome or sEV–lipid nanoparticle systemsmay combine biological recognition with higher loading capacity, although their composition and mechanism must be defined as a new product rather than assumed to retain all advantages of either parent platform.23,126,137,138,217

Targeting Specificity, in vivo Fate, and Safety

Achieving colon or lesion specificity while avoiding off-organ uptake is critical. Orally administered sEVs must survive gastrointestinal transit and negotiate mucus, whereas intravenously administered sEVs are frequently sequestered by the liver, spleen, and lungs.32,217 Receptor expression is heterogeneous and may also occur in healthy tissues; increased binding in vitro therefore does not guarantee favorable biodistribution in vivo.23,34 Targeting claims should be supported by quantitative tissue distribution, receptor-blocking controls, non-targeted vesicle controls, and dose-normalized functional outcomes.23,213,217

For oral delivery, gastrointestinal and intracellular stability are both important. Akkermansia muciniphila membrane vesicles have been reported to promote active mucus penetration through mucin degradation. Following uptake by tumor cells, these vesicles bypassed the conventional endosomal–lysosomal route through Golgi-to-endoplasmic-reticulum trafficking, thereby increasing endoplasmic-reticulum accumulation of siRNA and improving gene silencing in a mouse colorectal cancer model.218 Stimuli-responsive sEVs that sense pH, ROS, or enzymes in inflamed colon represent a key direction. More directly relevant sEV-based systems have used protective multilayer or hydrogel coatings for oral colon delivery. For example, layer-by-layer-coated exosomes showed improved gastrointestinal protection and colonic delivery in experimental colitis, while galactose-modified, IL-10-carrying sEVs enclosed within a chitosan–alginate coating combined gastrointestinal protection, colonic release, and macrophage-directed delivery.152,219

Although many sEVs preparations are less immunogenic than synthetic or cellular therapies, safety cannot be inferred from endogenous origin alone.52,101,220 Source cells may contribute oncogenic, pro-angiogenic, immunosuppressive, infectious, or alloimmune signals; engineering can introduce residual reagents, neoepitopes, altered biodistribution, or unexpected uptake.101,111,220,221 Safety assessment should cover source-cell qualification, genomic and proteomic cargo, biodistribution, persistence, immunogenicity, thrombogenicity, tumorigenicity, reproductive risk when relevant, and dose-dependent toxicity.101 Multi-omics and functional assays should be interpreted together because molecular profiling alone does not establish biological safety.

Regulatory Pathways and Integrated Therapeutic Applications

Regulatory frameworks for engineered sEVs therapeutics are still evolving.221,222 Product definition, manufacturing control, potency, comparability, and long-term safety must be resolved through collaboration among regulators, academia, clinicians, and industry.223 Clinical development should begin with mechanistically justified, analytically well-defined products rather than highly complex constructs whose active components cannot be measured.224 Early trials should prioritize safety, dose, pharmacodynamics, feasibility, and informative biomarkers before efficacy claims are expanded.225

Engineered sEVs may support combination therapy and theranostics by co-delivering drugs, nucleic acids, immune modulators, or imaging probes. In CRC, this could complement immune-checkpoint blockade or chemotherapy; in IBD, it could combine mucosal repair with pathway-specific immune regulation. Image-guided tracking may help connect gastrointestinal transit and tissue exposure with pharmacodynamic response. However, each additional component increases analytical, toxicological, and manufacturing requirements, and combination products should be benchmarked against the individual components and current standard of care.226

AI-Enabled Engineering and Precision Translation

Artificial intelligence and machine learning could assist ligand screening, cargo-sequence selection, formulation optimization, image-based particle analysis, process control, and prediction of biodistribution or immunogenicity.227–229 These models require curated, interoperable datasets with negative results, batch metadata, and external validation; otherwise, algorithmic optimization may reproduce laboratory-specific bias. Digital twins that integrate gastrointestinal physiology, disease state, microbial composition, and formulation properties could support virtual dose and release simulations,230 but they should complement rather than replace experimental pharmacology.

Organ-on-chip and gut-on-chip systems can incorporate flow, mucus, epithelium, immune cells, microbiota, and patient-derived tissues to test penetration, release, barrier effects, and toxicity under more realistic conditions than static monolayers.231–233 Coupling these models with microfluidic manufacturing and multimodal imaging may enable rapid iteration between product design and biological validation.234,235 In the longer term, patient stratification by receptor expression, inflammatory state, microbiome function, and pharmacodynamic biomarkers may support personalized sEV therapy; clinical adoption will nevertheless require simple, scalable products and prospective evidence that personalization improves outcomes.236,237

Conclusions

Engineered sEVs combine a natural lipid bilayer, cargo protection, cellular uptake, and programmable modification of their surfaces and cargo. These features make them suitable for overcoming the sequential barriers encountered during oral delivery to the colon. Within a single system, they can provide protection during gastrointestinal transit, regional release, recognition of diseased tissue, delivery into target cells, and biological signaling. Nanovesicles derived from edible plants further expand this design space because their sources are abundant, renewable, and potentially scalable. Several preparations tolerate gastrointestinal conditions and contain components that suppress inflammation, reduce oxidative stress, or regulate the intestinal microbiota. However, these properties depend on the source and preparation method. The most immediate opportunities lie in applications with clearly defined mechanisms, including restoration of epithelial barrier integrity and immune homeostasis in IBD and targeted delivery of cytotoxic or immunomodulatory agents in CRC.

Clinical translation now depends on resolving three priorities. First, heterogeneity among sources and uncertainty in potency require nomenclature that identifies the source, benchmarks for composition, and potency assays tied to mechanisms of action. Second, variable yield and product instability require closed production systems that comply with good manufacturing practice. Tangential-flow filtration followed by size-exclusion chromatography may be used where appropriate, together with controls during production and protective formulations or lyophilization. Third, limited knowledge of biodistribution, safety over extended periods, and clinical efficacy requires quantitative fate studies, organoids and microphysiological intestinal models that reproduce relevant diseases, and early clinical trials that include pharmacodynamic measures and outcomes meaningful to patients. A development framework that integrates the route of administration, disease context, cargo, mechanism of action, and manufacturing requirements will therefore be essential. Progress in these related areas could convert promising vesicle biology into reproducible and clinically useful sEV products for delivery to the colon.
