Beyond Visibility: Social Media, Trust, and Responsibility in Oncology
Authors: Joud Hajjar, Mark A. Lewis
Published: 2026

Social media is now part of healthcare, a fact that must be accepted by both its proponents and its detractors. It shapes how patients look for information, how clinicians communicate, how institutions present themselves, and how medical information is shared and interpreted. In a recent publication in the Journal of Immunotherapy and Precision Oncology (JIPO), Drs. Jibran Ahmed, Atlal Abusanad, and Gil Morgan address this reality in their review of the benefits and risks of social media in modern healthcare.[1]

There is clear value in social media. It can improve access to information, connect patients and caregivers with support, increase awareness of clinical trials, and allow physicians and scientists to communicate beyond traditional academic spaces. In oncology, this has particular relevance. Patients with cancer and their families often turn to digital platforms while facing uncertainty, urgency, and fear.[2,3] In those moments, social media can offer education, connection, and a sense of community that formal medical systems do not always provide quickly or well. It also increasingly shapes how patients understand clinical trials, novel therapies, treatment toxicities, and even what they believe good cancer care should look like.[4,5] At the same time, these platforms can also oversimplify complex issues, amplify misinformation, and create expectations that are not always aligned with evidence or clinical reality.[2,6]

For that reason, the discussion should go beyond whether social media is good or bad, a false binary. It is both. The more important issue is how it is used and by whom and whether the environment we are creating in these spaces is one that truly serves patients and the profession.

Responsibility must be shared. Physicians are part of that responsibility but so are institutions, healthcare systems, businesses, patients, and the public. Much of the discussion around social media in medicine focuses on individual behavior, yet the role of institutions deserves equal attention.

Healthcare institutions and business entities increasingly use social media to build visibility, promote programs, and reach patients directly. Some of this is appropriate and useful. Institutions should educate, communicate clearly, and make their work visible. However, visibility should remain grounded in substance. Social media should not be used to overstate outcomes, market hope without context, or blur the line between education and promotion. This is especially important in oncology, where patients may be particularly vulnerable to persuasive messaging during difficult moments. Like oncologists themselves, those crafting such content must carefully calibrate hope against hype.

Institutions should also be careful not to place excessive weight on digital visibility when making decisions about hiring, promotion, or leadership. A social media presence may reflect communication skills and public engagement, and those qualities may have value. However, they are not substitutes for scholarship, mentorship, leadership, sound clinical judgment, or meaningful long-term contribution. Academic medicine should be careful not to reward visibility over merit.

At the same time, institutions should not respond to social media only through restriction and control. Professional standards are necessary, especially around privacy, conflicts of interest, and boundaries.[7,8] But there is a difference between setting reasonable guardrails and trying to control every aspect of a physician’s public voice. The goal should be to support thoughtful and responsible engagement, not to suppress individual expression or to expect physicians to function only as institutional representatives. Attestations as to the true origins of content (e.g., “Tweets my own”) protect physicians from being seen solely as the mouthpiece of their employers.

Patients and caregivers also have an important role in this space. They have every right to share their experiences, including criticism, frustration, and disappointment. Public feedback can improve accountability and may bring attention to important gaps in care, communication, and access. Healthcare should not be insulated from scrutiny, and patient voices should not be dismissed because they are expressed publicly.

At the same time, there is a meaningful distinction between legitimate criticism and the use of social media as a means of pressure or coercion. Public platforms should not become tools to intimidate individuals, force concessions, or reduce complex clinical and operational decisions to public demands. Social media is most valuable when it promotes transparency, reflection, and improvement, not when it undermines fair process.

These concerns become even more important when misinformation and artificial intelligence are added to the discussion. In oncology, misinformation is not harmless. It can influence treatment decisions, create false hope, deepen mistrust, and worsen disparities.[2,6] Artificial intelligence may improve access and communication, but it may also magnify bias, confabulate to fill knowledge gaps, and spread inaccurate information more efficiently if used without sufficient caution.[9,10] These are not separate concerns. They are part of the same digital environment.

Social media will remain part of medicine. The question is not whether it belongs in healthcare but whether we are willing to approach it with honesty, restraint, and responsibility. If social media is to serve oncology well, it cannot be driven only by reach, visibility, or influence. It must also be guided by substance, professionalism, fairness, and trust. The question is: Are we willing to shape its role with the same seriousness, humility, and sense of responsibility that we ask of one another everywhere else in medicine?
