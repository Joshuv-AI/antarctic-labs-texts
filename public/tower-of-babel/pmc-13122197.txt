Intraoperative motor evoked potentials (MEP) improvement predicts functional outcomes in intracranial surgery
Authors: Prasuna Velur, Marvin Gong, Gabriel Zada, Robert Briggs, Vibha Velur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Improvement in intraoperative Motor Evoked Potentials during intracranial surgery.•The patient experienced enhanced postoperative neurological strength outcome.•Literature paucity of such correlation related to brain tumors and cranial surgery.Keywords: Improvement in motor evoked potentials, TcMEPs, MEPs, Intracranial surgery

Introduction

Motor evoked potentials (MEPs) are crucial for assessing the integrity of motor pathways during high-risk surgeries, such as craniotomies, spinal surgeries, and thoracic or thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm (TAA or TAAA) procedures. The primary goal of MEP monitoring is to prevent neurological complications such as post-op weakness. Key warning signs indicating potential issues include a decrease in amplitudes greater than 50%. These indicators enable the surgical team to take timely measures with the goal of improving patient outcomes.

Limited research indicates that improvements in intraoperative motor evoked potentials (MEPs) can be associated with improved clinical outcomes. In a study involving patients with cervical compressive myelopathy undergoing spinal surgery, a latency reduction of over 10% or an amplitude increase of more than 50% was seen as a “positive change,” which correlated with better functional recovery one-month post-surgery and faster hospital discharge. Although these changes did not significantly affect recovery at the six-month mark, Park et al. (2018) that they provide valuable potential insights for future patient care. In another study by Wang et al. (2016) that focused on patients who underwent cervical cord decompression for compressive myelopathy, patients demonstrating significant motor improvement—defined as more than a 50% increase in MEP amplitude—had a more favourable prognosis for neurological recovery compared to two other groups: one that showed no noticeable change in MEP and another that experienced a decline in MEP. In addition, Pratheesh et al. (2014) showed that improved MEPs during intraoperative monitoring of spine surgeries, such as tethered cord release procedures, indicate a positive prognosis regarding neurologic outcome. Moreover, MEP monitoring has proven beneficial in predicting outcomes for patients undergoing surgery for brain tumours. In a case series involving 121 brain tumour patients by Silverstein et al. (2023), a sub-analysis of those patients without tumour recurrence or progression at follow-up found that all patients with stable monitoring either maintained or improved their neurological status compared to their preoperative condition.

There is limited literature suggesting that improvements in intraoperative motor evoked potentials (MEPs) during intracranial surgeries may lead to improvements in neurological status. We present a case of a patient who underwent fenestration of a left thalamic cyst along with a biopsy of the associated pilocytic astrocytoma. During the procedure, the patient exhibited improved MEPs in the right lower extremity immediately following cyst fenestration and drainage, which could have correlated with an improved clinical outcome postoperatively.

Case presentation

A 39-year-old man presented to the local emergency department with complaints of right sided weakness. Imaging revealed a cystic lesion measuring 30 x 15 x 20 mm, located at the junction of the left posterior basal ganglia and thalamus. Adjacent to this lesion was a smaller enhancing solid nodule along with significant vasogenic edema surrounding both lesions.

On physical examination, the patient demonstrated right upper extremity weakness, rated 3/5 on the Medical Research Council (MRC) grading scale proximally and 1/5 distally. Additionally, he exhibited right lower extremity weakness rated at 4/5, which resulted in notable difficulties with ambulation. The patient also showed a right-sided facial droop and sensory changes on the right side. Previous pathological assessments via a stereotactic biopsy had been non-diagnostic, even with positive frozen specimen results suggestive of glioma. Consequently, the patient was referred to our institution for higher level of care and underwent a left pterional craniotomy with a trans-Sylvian approach for cyst fenestration and tumour biopsy.

During surgery, transcranial motor-evoked potential (TC-MEP) monitoring was performed under general anaesthesia using a Cascade TCS 4 device (Cadwell Labs, Kennewick, Washington) with TIVA anaesthesia. Using corkscrew electrodes, and High Cut and Low Cut filters at 3000 Hz-30 Hz, three motor stimulation channels were set at C1/C2 and C3-Cz/C4-Cz, with the montage providing the most reliable TC-MEP recordings without ipsilateral coactivation. Needle electrodes were placed in target muscles of the upper and lower extremities, using an ipsilateral upper extremity muscle as a control to prevent activation of deep structures. Non-depolarizing neuromuscular blocking agents were administered during induction for endotracheal tube placement. These medications were cleared before baseline measurements for motor-evoked potentials (MEP) were performed as Train of four showed 4 out of 4 twitches present. Subsequently, total intravenous anaesthesia (TIVA) was used for the procedure.

No further muscle relaxants were administered during surgery. Baseline MEP results indicated that no compound muscle action potentials (CMAPs) were detected in the right lower extremity, specifically in the tibialis anterior (TA) muscle and the right foot. Somatosensory-evoked potentials(SSEPs) were followed in bilateral upper and lower extremities during the procedure. Right lower extremity SSEPs were absent at baseline (Fig. 2). Absent right lower extremity SSEPs with absent MEPs in the right TA and right foot could correlate with the preoperative weakness noted on the exam. Thalamic tumour-associated cyst fenestration and drainage was performed prior to 10:14, with the intraoperative technician’s system entry logged at 10:15 following the MEP stimulation trial. CMAPs in the right foot were observed immediately during the MEP trial at 10:14, representing the first recorded electrophysiological response after cyst fenestration (Fig. 1). The apparent temporal discrepancy — whereby the MEP improvement captured at 10:14 precedes the system-logged fenestration entry at 10:15 — reflects a documentation interval rather than a physiological paradox: the surgical maneuver was completed before the 10:14 MEP trial, and the technician’s event entry followed the stimulation run. Stimulation parameters for MEPs remained unchanged around the time of the cyst fenestration. By the end of the procedure, polymorphic CMAPs were variably recorded in the right foot. MEPs of the left hand and the left foot remained stable. SSEPs remained stable during the entire procedure with no noted changes around the time of cyst fenestration. The patient remained on TIVA for the major operative part with no significant changes in the anesthetic regimen. The patient's cardiovascular parameters remained stable during the procedure with consistent mean arterial pressure greater than 65 and stable temperatures were documented. Postoperatively, the examination showed improvement in right upper extremity weakness, with an MRC grading of 4/5 proximally and 3/5 distally. The right lower extremity demonstrated an MRC grading of 5/5. During a follow-up visit at the neurosurgery clinic after discharge, the patient exhibited full recovery of right-sided function and was able to ambulate independently. Postoperative CT imaging obtained immediately after surgery demonstrated complete collapse of the cyst.

Discussion

In this rare case report, we show near real time improvement of associated MEPs in a patient undergoing tumour-associated cyst fenestration and drainage with decompression of the corticospinal tract. The primary objective of using intraoperative neurophysiologic monitoring during neurosurgery is to prevent new neurological deficits. The surgical approach may be adjusted based on changes observed in the neurophysiological signals. Free-running signals arise from spontaneous electrical activity in muscles. To measure evoked potentials, we apply electrical stimulation to one end of a neural pathway, such as the primary motor cortex, and measure the response at the other end, typically in the muscles responsible for the movement. We describe the waveforms by their amplitude, latency, morphology, and stimulation threshold shift. A significant drop in amplitude, longer latency, poorer morphology, increased stimulation threshold or any change in pattern can indicate potential nerve damage. Although guidelines for motor evoked potentials (MEPs) are not defined as precisely as those for sensory evoked potentials, a common rule is that a decrease in amplitude of 50% or more is considered significant and can portend negative clinical outcomes.

While the exact mechanism behind improvements in intraoperative MEPs remains uncertain, it is possible that reducing compressive forces on neural structures leads to clearer and more favourable outcomes. A case report by Kombos et al. (2004), indicated an intraoperative increase in electrical motor system excitability, with improved MEPs following tumour removal from the primary motor area. This improvement may suggest decompression of the pyramidal tract as a potential explanation. In a report of 20 cases by Shah et al. (2019) of patients undergoing atlantoaxial fixation, intraoperative improvements in MEPs have varied between 20% and 35%, with an average increase of 30% observed immediately after tightening the screws. This finding provides strong evidence supporting the hypothesis that atlantoaxial instability is directly related to the development of Chiari malformation and relief of that instability resulted in improved MEPs.

Intracranial MEP outcome study by Fan et al. (2024) showed that irreversible MEP changes showed good performance in predicting postoperative motor deficit and their absence can offer an optimistic expectation for the long-term motor outcome. With subcortical dynamic mapping, Seidel et al. (2025) showed that irreversible MEP alterations, and MEP loss increase the risk of postoperative motor deficits. These findings underscore the importance of monitoring improvements in intraoperative MEPs to predict enhanced recovery outcomes. However, there is limited literature on intracranial neurosurgical procedures that demonstrate improved neurological outcomes linked to enhanced intraoperative MEPs. Our case report aims to contribute to this area of study. Regarding the apparent temporal discrepancy in our case — wherein right foot CMAPs were first observed at 10:14 while the cyst fenestration was logged at 10:15 — this reflects a documentation interval rather than a physiological paradox. The fenestration was completed before the 10:14 MEP stimulation trial; the technician’s system entry at 10:15 followed the run. The near-immediate emergence of CMAPs after decompression is consistent with rapid restoration of corticospinal tract conduction following relief of mechanical compression, as demonstrated in cervical decompression literature where intraoperative MEP improvement correlates with early and long-term neurological recovery, as shown by Wang et al. (2016). The absence of concurrent SSEP improvement despite striking motor recovery is explained by the anatomical independence of the motor and somatosensory pathways. MEPs reflect the integrity of the corticospinal tract, while SSEPs monitor the dorsal column-medial lemniscal system; these are functionally and spatially distinct, and focal compression may affect one without the other, as established by Legatt and Ellen (2004) and Machida et al. (1988). This pathway-specific dissociation has been demonstrated intraoperatively: Weinzierl et al. (2007) reported that critical MEP changes with stable SSEPs occurred in 12 of 69 monitored operations, confirming that MEP monitoring is superior to SSEP monitoring in detecting impending impairment of the motor pathways. In our case, cyst-mediated compression selectively impacted the corticospinal tract in the thalamic region, while the somatosensory pathway was anatomically remote from the site of decompression, accounting for the isolated MEP improvement without SSEP change. Given the close temporal relationship noted with thalamic cyst fenestration and improvement in MEPs with subsequent improved postoperative motor outcomes, we hypothesize that an increase in the frequency of MEP testing as surgical conditions permit at specific stages in the procedure can assist us to improve our prognostication in the future. Our report is limited because despite the stability of all extraneous factors, the causality of MEP improvement cannot be uniquely ascertained to the cyst fenestration. Our hope is that a larger sample of cases with similar findings can support such claims in future.

Conclusion

In this case report, we observed an immediate improvement in intraoperative Motor Evoked Potentials (MEPs) following a fenestration of a thalamic cyst, and the patient experienced enhanced neurological outcomes, including increased strength in the right lower extremity and complete relief from ambulation difficulties. Such findings if present consistently in a larger sample, could help direct intraoperative MEP testing at particular operative junctures and aid to prognosticate postoperative outcomes.
