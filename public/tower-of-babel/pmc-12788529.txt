Vaping Troubles: Exploring the Link Between E-Cigarettes and Oral Lesions in India’s Urban Youth
Authors: Gowrishankar Sekar, Pratheeth Gundlapalle, Senthilkumar Elango, Sangamithra Surendran, K. Anitha, K A Acchuthakrishnan, Nagappan Nagappan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:The rising popularity of e-cigarettes among Indian youth raises concerns about potential oral health consequences, particularly mucosal lesions. Limited data exist on this association in the Indian context.Objectives:To assess the prevalence of oral lesions among young e-cigarette users and examine usage patterns and risk awareness.Materials and Methods:This is a cross-sectional study of 300 urban Indians (150 users, 150 nonusers) aged 18–35 years. Participants underwent standardized oral examinations and completed structured questionnaires. Statistical analyses included prevalence ratios (Poisson regression) and Chi-square tests.Results:E-cigarette users showed significantly higher prevalence of leukoplakia (9.3% vs. 3.3%, relative risk [RR] = 2.80, P = 0.038) and oral ulcers (8.0% vs. 2.7%, RR = 3.00, P = 0.045), daily users had 2.1 × higher lesion risk (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.25–3.52, P = 0.005), and only 15.3% of users recognized oral health risks versus 28.7% of nonusers (P = 0.008).Conclusion:E-cigarette use was independently associated with oral lesions, demonstrating clear dose–response relationships. Critical knowledge gaps exist regarding vaping risks, underscoring the need for targeted public health interventions

INTRODUCTION

The rapid proliferation of electronic cigarettes (e-cigarettes) among young adults in urban India has become a growing public health concern. Marketed as a safer alternative to conventional tobacco, e-cigarettes have gained popularity due to their sleek designs, diverse flavors, and perceived lower health risks.[1] However, emerging research indicates that e-cigarette aerosols—containing nicotine, propylene glycol, glycerin, and various flavoring agents—may contribute to adverse oral health effects, including mucosal irritation, leukoplakia, and an increased susceptibility to periodontal diseases.[2]

India, already burdened with high rates of tobacco-related oral pathologies, faces a new challenge with the unregulated rise of e-cigarette use, particularly among college students and young professionals.[3] While traditional tobacco products like cigarettes and smokeless tobacco have well-documented associations with oral cancer and precancerous lesions, the long-term effects of e-cigarettes on oral mucosa remain understudied in the Indian context.[4] Preliminary studies suggest that the heating elements and chemical compositions in e-cigarettes may induce oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in oral tissues, potentially leading to lesions.[5]

Despite government bans and regulatory measures, e-cigarettes remain widely accessible through online platforms and informal markets, exacerbating the risk of nicotine addiction and oral health complications among young adults.[6] Given this scenario, there is an urgent need to investigate the prevalence and nature of oral lesions associated with e-cigarette use in urban India to guide preventive healthcare strategies and policy interventions.

Aim

This study aims to assess the association between e-cigarette use and oral lesions among young adults (18–35 years) in urban India.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This cross-sectional analytical study will be conducted among young adults aged 18–35 years in urban India to assess the association between e-cigarette use and oral lesions. A stratified random sampling technique will be employed to ensure representation across different socioeconomic and demographic groups. The sample size has been calculated as approximately 300 participants (138 per group with 10% adjustment for nonresponse) based on an estimated 25% prevalence of oral lesions among e-cigarette users compared to 10% in nonusers, with 95% confidence and 80% power. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee prior to study initiation, and written informed consent will be secured from all participants while maintaining strict confidentiality through data anonymization.

Participants were recruited based on predefined inclusion criteria: age 18–35 years, either current e-cigarette users (minimum once weekly for ≥6 months) or nonusers (never used e-cigarettes or tobacco), and willingness to undergo oral examination. Exclusion criteria include systemic diseases affecting oral health (e.g., diabetes, immunosuppression), current use of other tobacco products or alcohol dependence, and pregnancy/lactation.

Data collection will involve a structured questionnaire capturing demographics, e-cigarette usage patterns (frequency, duration, nicotine concentration, flavors), oral hygiene practices, and awareness of e-cigarette risks. Clinical oral examinations will be performed under standardized conditions using mouth mirrors and probes to identify mucosal lesions (leukoplakia, erythroplakia, ulcers, nicotine stomatitis) according to WHO criteria, with photographic documentation for validation.

To ensure data quality, examiners will undergo prestudy calibration to achieve interexaminer reliability (kappa >0.85), and the survey instrument will be pilot-tested for internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha >0.7). Statistical analysis will include descriptive statistics, Chi-square/Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables, independent t-tests/Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables, and multivariate logistic regression to identify risk factors, with significance set at P < 0.05 using SPSS v26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL).

DISCUSSION

The findings of this study reveal significant associations between e-cigarette use and oral lesions among urban Indian youth, even after implementing conservative prevalence estimates. Our results demonstrate that e-cigarette users had nearly three times higher risk of developing leukoplakia (RR = 2.80, P = 0.038) and oral ulcers (RR = 3.00, P = 0.045) compared to nonusers. These findings align with emerging global evidence suggesting that vaping may induce oral mucosal damage through mechanisms involving thermal injury, oxidative stress, and inflammation.[67] Daily vapers showed 2.1 times higher lesion prevalence (P = 0.005), while users of high-nicotine (≥5%) liquids had 1.85 times greater risk (P = 0.025). These findings corroborate laboratory studies demonstrating that nicotine concentrations ≥5 mg/mL significantly increase cytotoxicity in oral keratinocytes.[8] Interestingly, while flavored e-liquids showed a nonsignificant trend toward higher risk (aPR = 1.60, P = 0.078), this may reflect the limited power to detect effects of specific flavoring compounds, which have shown variable toxicity profiles in in vitro studies.[9]

CONCLUSION

This study provides compelling evidence linking e-cigarette use with oral lesions among urban Indian youth, even after controlling for potential confounders. The observed 2.8–3.0 × higher risk of leukoplakia and ulcers, coupled with dose-dependent effects of vaping frequency and nicotine concentration, substantiate biological plausibility. The alarming discrepancy between actual risks (evidenced by lesion prevalence) and perceived safety (48% believing vaping is harmless) highlights an urgent need for:
