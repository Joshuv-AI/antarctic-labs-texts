The anthropological method for diagnosing mental diseases: On the theory of time structure of Bin Kimura
Authors: Shun'ichi Noma
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBin Kimura (1931–2021), the Japanese anthropological psychopathologist and philosopher, attached importance to clinical impressions at psychiatric consultations in order to diagnose mental diseases precisely, and thus proposed his original theory of time structure. According to Kimura's theory of time structure, whether we live by giving weight to the past, the present, or the future can be called “time structure,” and is essentially related to the way we live with others and experience the world. We can obtain some signs for diagnosing mental diseases from the viewpoint of the time structure of each patient. “Ante‐festum” is the time structure in which one lives ahead to the future, and is typical of schizophrenic patients. “Post‐festum” is the time structure in which one is late for oneself, and is typical of patients with endogenous depression. “Intra‐festum” is the time structure in which one lives directly in the present, and is typical of patients with epilepsy or bipolar disorders. The anthropological diagnosis according to Kimura's theory of time structure could complement the operational diagnostic criteria, such as the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders and the International Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, in daily clinical practice. For example, Kimura's theory of time structure could exercise its ability to discriminate between endogenous and neurotic depression, and between schizophrenia and other delusional psychoses. The theory of time structure and anthropological diagnosis which Bin Kimura proposed in the late 20th century can give us many hints for assessing patients with mental diseases even today.

INTRODUCTION

In modern psychiatry, operational diagnostic criteria, namely, the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) 1 or the International Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (ICD), 2 are commonly used to diagnose mental diseases. However, when the DSM‐III, 3 the first operational diagnostic criterion for mental diseases, was published in 1980, psychiatrists at the time were surprised because it was far from their clinical impressions, making them skeptical of its usability. The reliability of diagnosis was considered most seriously at the cost of clinical validity when these methods of operational diagnoses were made. Therefore, some psychiatrists at that time tried to diagnose mental diseases according to their clinical impressions.

Psychopathology is the academic field in which mental diseases are considered not through some biological media, such as blood data or brain image, but by patients’ direct experiences, aided with psychological or philosophical concepts. Specifically, on anthropological psychopathology, mental diseases are understood as expressions of the way of life, and it is assumed that each disease has its own characteristic premorbid personality.

Bin Kimura (1931–2021) was one of the most important anthropological psychopathologists worldwide in the second half of the 20th century, particularly in Europe. Kimura graduated from the Faculty of Medicine, Kyoto University, worked as a psychiatrist in some private hospitals, studied in Germany for 3 years, and held the professor of Nagoya City University (1974–1986) and that of Kyoto University (1986–1994) in succession. At the same time, he published many articles and books about psychiatry and philosophy.

Kimura developed his unique phenomenological psychopathology influenced by Kitaro Nishida, Martin Heidegger, and Viktor von Weizsäcker, and had been considering mental phenomena and creating original concepts and terms to explain the mentality of mentally healthy and unhealthy persons. His psychopathology is unique because he attached importance not to symptoms of mental diseases but to patients' ways of experiencing the world and tried to regard our experiences as life phenomena that arise from the relationships between human and human. He developed life philosophy in his later years.

Kimura created his original theory of time structure, in which he insisted that patients with schizophrenia, depression, and epilepsy lived different time structures, which we can identify to diagnose mental diseases.

This article is one of a series of papers 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 introducing second‐generation psychopathology in Japan and aims to introduce Kimura's theory of time structure, and to induce the possibility of this theory to complement the defects of the operational criteria.

BASIC CONCEPTS OF THE PHILOSOPHY OF BIN KIMURA

There are two basic concepts that are important to understanding Kimura's psychopathology: “mono (thing)‐koto (event),” 12 and “aida (between).” 13 At first, Kimura divided all phenomena into “mono (thing),” referring to objective, visible, and measurable things, and “koto (event),” referring to subjective and unmeasurable phenomena. We can feel the world vividly as the koto. Kimura got this idea from the concepts of noesis and noema in Husserlian phenomenology. Husserl considered that the effect of intention and the object of intention functioned simultaneously when we experience something on the field of our consciousness, and named them “noesis” and “noema,” respectively. Kimura 4 called the phenomena that mainly had the nature of noesis “koto” and the phenomena that mainly had the nature of noema “mono.”

Furthermore, Kimura 13 did not think that there were individual independent persons at first and that communications occurred among them thereafter, but assumed that there were only relationships between the self and the other at first and that individual persons occurred from subsequent relationships. Kimura named this fundamental relationship between the self and the others whom the self was together with “aida (between).” Kimura considered all psychopathological phenomena in the field of psychiatry as pathologies of aida occurring in the koto dimension.

Kimura 14 also divided time into “mono” and “koto.” According to Kimura, “mono‐time” is, so to speak, “clock‐time,” which is indicated by numbers, and therefore measurable and objectively shared with everyone, whereas “koto‐time” cannot be measured but is only felt, such as the concepts of “yet” or “already.” In the Greek philosophy, time was divided into “Kairos” and “Chronos,” namely, Kairos was crucial time, which was given by destiny or God's plan, and which demanded decision, while Chronos was quantitative time in the passage of time. Kairos time could be thought of as outstanding koto‐time and mono‐time could be rephrased by “Chronos time.” Kimura said that we could experience the time in the transition from mono‐time to koto‐time.

Furthermore, Kimura 14 assumed that the koto‐time spans from the past to the present and to the future, that such time was an essential element of the formation of the self and the world being experienced. Whether we live by giving weight to the past, the present, or the future is essentially related to the way we live with others, namely, the aida, and influences how we experience the world. It is important that we cannot immediately experience the expanse of time itself, but only experience the world. The problem is not time experience but time structure, or grammatically speaking, the “tense” by which we experience the world.

Kimura speculated that patients with mental diseases experience the world in various ways depending on their diseases, and proposed three types of time structure: “ante‐festum,” “post‐festum,” and “intra‐festum.”

“Ante‐Festum” of Schizophrenia

The main symptoms of schizophrenia are persecutive hallucinations and delusions, or influenced experiences. Furthermore, the way patients with schizophrenia talk and act in the context of daily personal relationships has a characteristic unnaturalness. Wolfgang Blankenburg 15 assumed that this unnaturalness occurred because they could not feel all phenomena around them self‐evidently, and referred to this as a “loss of natural self‐evidence.” Ludwig Binswanger 16 expressed the same character of schizophrenia as a “dissolution of consistency of natural experiences,” and Eugène Minkowski 17 explained it as a “loss of vivid contact with reality.” As it is difficult to describe this characteristic unnaturalness, we can only identify it in patients with schizophrenia. Psychiatrists can therefore diagnose schizophrenia using an “intuitive diagnosis” or “feeling diagnosis” method. Henricus Rümke 18 called this feeling with which psychiatrists could identify a schizophrenic person as a “praecox feeling.”

Kimura 12 insisted that this characteristic uneasy unnaturalness came from uncertainty of the selfness peculiar to schizophrenia, as explained by the following explanations of patients with schizophrenia: “I will be destroyed if I turn my eyes away from myself even for an instance” and “I always try to take the initiative because I will be beaten if the opponent reads my mind before I read his mind.” Kimura understood that schizophrenic patients always tried to live ahead of the present and to get the future earlier. According to Kimura, this “future” did not indicate a future on the watch or on the calendar, namely, the future as mono, but an “unknown” future. The characteristic way of life of patients with schizophrenia correlates with an affinity with the unknown.

Kimura referred to this time structure as “ante‐festum.” “Ante” and “festum” in Latin mean “before” and “festival,” respectively; therefore, “ante‐festum” indicates the feeling mixed with being excited and frightened just before a festival. The schizophrenic patients, who have the tendency of “ante‐festum,” do not feel that time passes fast but are always frightened of the “unknown” that might occur in the future. Essentially, these patients live in the future tense.

“Post‐Festum” of Depression

Kurt Schneider insisted that there were two types of depression: endogenous and reactive. Endogenous depression was considered as depression without a cause and is characterized by vital sadness, which appears as insomnia, loss of appetite, or some physical trouble, whereas reactive depression has some psychological cause or trigger and is characterized by reactive sadness. Patients with endogenous depression are known to exhibit three major delusions: delusions of culpability, poverty, and hypochondriacal delusion.

Patients with endogenous depression always regret what they have done and blame themselves for it. According to Kimura, patients with endogenous depression, especially those with major delusions, worry about “having done irrevocable actions.” Hubertus Tellenbach 15 called this type of character of patients with endogenous depression as a “type of melancholia (Typus melancholicus),” whose feature was love for order both in society and in privacy. If these persons fail to attain some order, they tend to think that they have done a terrible thing and that they will not be able to recover it.

Kimura insisted that patients with endogenous depression must feel that they could not achieve order and that they were “late for themselves” because of “having done irrecoverable actions.” While Kimura called the affinity with the future on patients with schizophrenia “ante‐festum,” he called the affinity with the past on patients with endogenous depression “post‐festum.” Essentially, these patients live in the perfect tense.

“Intra‐Festum” of Epilepsy and Mania

There are two types of epilepsy with grand mal (tonic‐clonic) seizures: epilepsy with seizures upon awakening and that upon sleeping. In the former, seizures occur within 2 h of waking or in the evening, while in the latter, the seizures occur upon sleeping. Dieter Janz 19 insisted that the former type is a more fundamental epilepsy, as it could change over to the sleeping type, but not vice versa.

Patients with the awakening type of epilepsy tend to have emotional instability, be lazy, and be innocent in character. Sometimes, they lack planning and act in a haphazard way. They experience auras lasting for several seconds. Fyodor Dostoevsky, a great Russian writer, described this phenomenon as follows: “I had an intuition abruptly that I had completely gain[ed] eternal harmony in five or six seconds” (from The Idiot). For them, the present seems to be the most important, and there is no future and no past. Kimura expressed that patients with the awakening type of epilepsy lived in the “eternal present.”

The same thing applies to patients with mania. There is a large difference in the character of patients between monopolar depression and bipolar manic‐depression. Most patients with depression always behave in an orderly manner and live in a cool mood before disease onset, whereas most patients with mania present with elevated and refreshed moods. Binswanger 20 explained that for patients with mania, every day is a permanent festival, which is unreflecting and pure delight of Dasein (human existence) completely unrelated to daily issues. According to Kimura, also for patients with mania, the present time is dominant.

There seems to be nothing in common clinically between epilepsy and mania. In the modern psychiatry, epilepsy and mania have different pathologies and are thus considered as different diseases. Nevertheless, those with epilepsy and mania can be thought to live in the same manner, namely, in the same time structure. They do not try to get the future earlier like patients with schizophrenia, nor regret being late for themselves as patients with depression. Patients with epilepsy and mania always live directly in the present. It could be said that they have such a time structure as adherence to the present, or an “eternal presentness.” Kimura called the affinity with the present as “intra‐festum” (“intra” being Latin for “in”).

Subclassification of Atypical Psychosis According to Kimura's Theory of Time Structure

Kimura asserted that patients with schizophrenia, depression, and epilepsy had peculiar time structures, namely, ante‐festum, post‐festum, and intra‐festum, respectively. Kimura's assertion means that we can diagnose patients according to their time structures. Kimura 21 referred to this method as “anthropological diagnosis.”

Kimura focused on atypical psychosis (“Mitsuda psychosis”), which was proposed by Hisatoshi Mitsuda 22 in the 1940s, and used in modern Japanese clinical practice. The characteristics of atypical psychosis are repeated periodical deterioration and recovery, similar to bipolar manic‐depression, persecutive delusions and auditory hallucinations at one point in time like schizophrenia, and electroencephalogram abnormalities like epilepsy, so it is conceived as an intermediate between schizophrenia, manic‐depressive illness, and epilepsy. If the modern diagnostic criteria were applied to atypical psychosis, it could be diagnosed as a periodical psychosis or schizoaffective disorder.

Kimura thought that atypical psychosis consisted of some subtypes, and applied the anthropological diagnosis based on his theory of time structure to atypical psychosis. He asserted that we could find which time structure a person had according to the person's life history and the impression which we gained when we met the person. As a result, Kimura classified atypical psychosis into three types, namely, type A, type P, and type non‐A non‐P (Table 1).

Patients with type A atypical psychosis have ante‐ and intra‐festum time structure. When they exhibit a few manic‐depressive symptoms, they have hallucinations and delusions similar to schizophrenia. Psychiatrists cannot expect the effect of psychotropic drugs and should pay attention to the ante‐festum structure of patients, namely, their vulnerability to surprise attacks and ability to pursue their unrealistic ideals, and build the realistic environment where they can live safer with them in order to prevent recurrence of disease.

Patients with type P atypical psychosis have post‐ and intra‐festum time structures. Manic‐depressive symptoms are outstanding compared with hallucinations and delusions for these patients. In terms of delusions, they sometimes have delusions with concrete themes, such as theft or exclusion from community, and a few influenced experiences (ego disorders). Mood stabilizers and anti‐depressants are effective.

Patients with non‐A non‐P type have a purely intra‐festum time structure. Their personality is sometimes innocent, impulsive, deviating from the public order. In these patients, schizophrenic ego disorders, construction of delusional system, and depressive themes are not so often seen, but some conscious disorders, namely, delusional mood, amentia, and oneiroid, may appear. Anticonvulsants are often effective, and psychotherapy is difficult to conduct because patients with this type sometimes cannot keep a distance from psychiatrists.

MODERN PSYCHIATRY AND KIMURA'S THEORY OF TIME STRUCTURE

Kimura constructed his original theory of time structure and applied it to classification of atypical psychosis. However, in the first place, the concept of the disease “atypical psychosis” is not used by modern psychiatrists, although some psychiatrists think that this concept is useful even now.

What is the significance of Kimura's theory of time structure in modern psychiatry?

Anthropological Diagnosis

First, Kimura gave importance to anthropological diagnosis. We are currently accustomed to using operational diagnostic methods, such as the DSM and the ICD, in daily clinical practice. Their criteria are so concrete and simple that even inexperienced psychiatrists can use them, but these criteria are certainly important for preventing psychiatrists from making incorrect diagnoses. However, in actual clinical practice, we encounter many patients whose diseases we cannot diagnose so easily even based on these criteria. The validity of these criteria has therefore often been doubted. 23

We should comprehensively diagnose mental diseases using patients’ words, expressions, attitudes, and behaviors. After all, we should observe not only the patients’ symptoms but also their way of life to accurately diagnose their diseases, which we call anthropological diagnosis.

Kimura's theory of time structure is one method of anthropological diagnosis. Kimura proposed that one's time structure should reflect his/her way to live, and that it was important to pay attention to the time structures of patients. According to Kimura's theory of time structure, we must be able to determine the pathologies of patients as near to schizophrenia, monopolar depression, or bipolar disorder by identifying their time structure as ante‐festum, post‐festum, or intra‐festum.

In fact, Kimura and other anthropological psychopathologists suggested a close relationship between premorbid personality and mental disease, and Kimura insisted that time structures were essentially related to mental diseases because one's time structure underlies his/her personality. Moreover, Kimura assumed that one's time structure does not change during one's life. Although there might still be room for discussion on his assumption, the method for deciding mental diagnoses by the time structures of patients should be useful even in modern psychiatry. That is, ante‐festum may indicate the tendency for ego dysfunction peculiar to schizophrenia, post‐festum may indicate social sensitivity peculiar to monopolar depression, and intra‐festum may indicate tendency to instinctive impulse peculiar to bipolar disorder. In modern psychiatry, we can re‐evaluate Kimura's theory of time structure by means of understanding it as a basic attitude towards the world.

Clinically, Kimura's theory of time structure might be useful especially in encountering patients in depressive or hallucinatory‐delusional states.

How to Use Kimura's Theory of Time Structure in The Clinical Practice

Although Kimura's theory of time structure is exquisite and consistent, it is not easy to understand how to use it in the clinical practice. We recognize the time structure of patients while we are communicating with them verbally or nonverbally. The time structure is not related to whether the situation which is talked of is past, present, or future, but related to the tense of how the world is experienced.

For example, a patient talks about his/her past failure. If he/she regrets that he/she has made a mistake, he/she may have a time structure of post‐festum, if he/she is afraid of the consequence of his/her failure, he/she may have a time structure of ante‐festum, and if he/she changes the topic without caring about his/her failure, he/she may have a time structure of intra‐festum. To give another example, it will be rainy tomorrow according to the weather forecast. If he/she is worrying about what to do tomorrow, he/she may have a time structure of ante‐festum, and if he/she is disappointed because he/she has to change the plan, he/she may have a time structure of post‐festum.

After all, whether the patient is afraid of the future, regrets the past, or pays attention to only the present determines his/her time structure, and we recognize it in the whole of his/her attitudes, behaviors, and remarks during communication by intuitive clinical feeling.

Depressive States and Kimura's Theory of Time Structure

Using the DSM, 5th edition, text revision (DSM‐5‐TR) 1 criteria for major depressive disorder, we can diagnose one person with depression by the presence of a depressed mood and loss of interest or pleasure; however, this criterion encompasses endogenous, reactive, and neurotic depression in the traditional diagnosis, although it is important to distinguish among these three diseases because they require different treatments.

Endogenous depression is almost equivalent to melancholia, which has been known since ancient times, and was explained by Schneider 24 as “vital sadness,” namely, vague somatic disorder, such as insomnia and weight loss, and mental sadness, such as guilty conscience and an idea of poverty. In the DSM‐5‐TR criteria, 1 “major depressive disorder with melancholic features” corresponds to endogenous depression. The proper treatment for endogenous depression is medication and rest. Reactive depression is depression with definite causes or triggers and is usually cured in a short time especially by disappearance of causes or triggers. Major depression in the DSM‐5‐TR includes this depression. Furthermore, neurotic depression is also called “depressive neurosis,” which is diagnosed as depression with understandable psychological mechanism. As the DSM‐5‐TR does not adopt the concept of causes or mechanisms of diseases but deals only with the symptoms and progression of diseases, there is no disease which is equivalent to neurotic depression in the DSM‐5‐TR. The diagnosis of “persistent depressive disorder (dysthymia)” may be similar to neurotic depression, but many cases with neurotic depression are diagnosed as “major depressive disorder.” Thus, we cannot distinguish endogenous from reactive or neurotic depression using the DSM‐5‐TR criteria.

According to Kimura's theory of time structure, there is a high likelihood that depressive patients have endogenous depression if their time structure is “post‐festum” because they may be sensitive to social concerns. If it is estimated that the depressive patient has an intra‐festum time structure, they might actually have reactive or neurotic depression or be in the depressive phase of bipolar disorder. The time structures of the depressive patients are sometimes intra‐festum if they have undisclosed severe traumatic experiences.

Hallucinatory–Delusional States and Kimura's Theory of Time Structure

The DSM‐5‐TR criteria for schizophrenia are so simple that we can diagnose one person as schizophrenic if he/she has hallucinations, delusions, or thought disorders. In these criteria, the contents of pathological experiences are not called into question. In the 20th century, the contents of hallucinations and delusions were seriously considered in the diagnosis of schizophrenia, therefore Karl Jaspers 25 paid attention to ego disorders, or ego experience disorders, which provide an impossibility to distinguish ego from not‐ego peculiar to schizophrenic patients, and Schneider 24 proposed the first‐rank symptoms to diagnose schizophrenia. Although the ICD‐10 26 considered the concept of ego disorder when diagnosing schizophrenia, the ICD‐11 2 does not deal with the contents of schizophrenic experiences.

One reason why the DSM‐5‐TR and ICD‐11 criteria removed the contents of experiences from the diagnostic criteria of schizophrenia may be the difficulty of distinguishing schizophrenic ego disorders from pathological experiences of other psychoses. In fact, even if one person complains of problems stemming from delusions of persecution, it is not easy to judge whether he/she was influenced by experiences derived from schizophrenic ego disorder, or concrete delusional ideas developed from real causes peculiar to paranoia. Paranoia may correspond to delusional disorder, but several patients with paranoia complain of hallucinations and meet the criteria for schizophrenia.

According to Kimura's theory of time structure, the time structure of patients with paranoia is not ante‐festum but post‐festum because they pay particular attention to real facts and desperately try to provide evidence that supports their communications. In contrast, schizophrenic patients consider that there is no room for doubt about their own delusional experiences, so they never seek or provide evidence for their experiences. They are always threatened by something unknown; thus their time structure is ante‐festum.

CRITICAL OPINIONS ON KIMURA'S THEORY OF TIME STRUCTURE

Of course, some critical opinions have been posed to Kimura's theory of time structure.

Kenjiro Fukao, 27 the Japanese psychopathologist and specialist on epilepsy, insisted that the temperament of most of the epileptic patients was actually viscous, namely, tenacious and slow‐witted. The viscous temperament is contradictory to the characteristics of intra‐festum, namely, lazy, and innocent. Fukao speculated that Kimura had adopted epilepsy as representative of “intra‐festum” because epilepsy had been treated as one of the three typical psychoses together with schizophrenia and manic‐depressive illness in the mid‐20th century.

Kenshin Shimizu, 28 the Japanese psychopathologist who has some knowledge of philosophy, discussed Kimura's theory of time structure more philosophically. Shimizu understood that intra‐festum formed the basis of Kimura's theory of time structure because Kimura 13 considered that intra‐festum indicated future‐producing ground for general life. Furthermore, although Kimura identified the characteristic of “producing the future” of intra‐festum with the characteristic of “getting the future earlier” of ante‐festum, Shimizu regarded this consideration of Kimura as incorrect and insisted that we should examine the nature of the future which intra‐festum produces more deeply.

Shun'ichi Noma, 29 the Japanese psychiatrist, developed Kimura's theory of time structure critically. Noma insisted that patients with dissociation and those with autistic spectrum might live in eternal present but don't experience the world as vividly as patients with epilepsy or manic‐depressive illness, but rather coolly and unemotionally. Noma considered that patients with dissociation or autistic spectrum had the time structure of intra‐festum but that the direction of their experience was completely opposite, so Noma named the time‐experience structure “contra‐festum.”

CONCLUSION

For modern psychiatry, operational diagnostic criteria, such as the DSM and the ICD, are essential to educating young psychiatrists, to communicating with other specialists, and to promoting scientific studies. However, in daily clinical activities, psychiatrists need not only information, but also experiences to observe and treat patients.

The theory of time structure and the anthropological diagnosis that Bin Kimura proposed in the late 20th century can give us many hints for assessing patients with mental diseases, even today. We can learn a lot from Kimura's pathological philosophy about the techniques that are necessary for treating mental diseases and that are difficult to replace with words.
