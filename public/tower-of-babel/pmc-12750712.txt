Oncology nurses at the frontline of palliative care: knowledge, attitudes, and factors associated with palliative care practice
Authors: Nabiha Al Hasni, Asma Al Yahyaei, Omar Al Omari, Joshua Muliira, Eilean Rathinasamy Lazarus, Nasr Al Riyami, Atika Al Musalami, Amna Al-Harrasi, Nawal Al Mahyijari, Omar Ayaad, Namitha Sivankutty
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCancer is a major cause of mortality worldwide, and the demand for palliative care (PC) is increasing. In Oman, PC services remain limited, with oncology nurses providing most frontline care. However, little is known about their knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) toward PC.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 189 oncology nurses at the Royal Hospital and Sultan Qaboos Comprehensive Cancer Care and Research Center. Data were collected using the Palliative Care Knowledge Questionnaire (PEACE-Q), Frommelt’s Attitude Toward Care of the Dying – Form B (FATCOD-B), and the Palliative Care Self-Reported Practices Scale (PCPS). Descriptive statistics, Pearson correlations, t-tests, ANOVA, and multiple regression analyses were performed.ResultsNurses reported a mean knowledge score of 28.95 (SD = 4.25), attitude score of 3.44 (SD = 0.38), and practice score of 3.60 (SD = 0.58). Knowledge correlated positively with attitude (r = .47, p < .001), while attitude correlated positively with practice (r = .18, p = .013). Knowledge was not significantly associated with practice (r = –.08, p = .248). Subscale analysis showed stronger practice in pain management and family support, but weaker performance in delirium and dyspnea management. Regression analysis indicated that attitude, knowledge, in-service training, and prior education were significantly associated with higher practice scores.ConclusionOncology nurses in Oman demonstrate good knowledge and positive attitudes toward PC, yet practice gaps remain. Structured, culturally relevant education and ongoing in-service training are needed to strengthen PC delivery.Keywords: Palliative care, Oncology nursing, Knowledge, Attitudes, Practices, Oman

Introduction

Cancer is the second leading cause of death worldwide, with its incidence and mortality projected to rise steadily in the coming decades [1–3]. By 2050, the International Agency for Research on Cancer estimates that new cases will rise to 33 million annually, with cancer-related deaths reaching 18.2 million. Patients with advanced cancer frequently experience escalating symptoms, including pain, dyspnea, delirium, and psychological distress, as they approach the end of life [4–6]. Addressing these needs requires not only clinical expertise but also the integration of palliative care (PC) into cancer services through a multidisciplinary approach.

Despite its recognized importance of PC, access remains highly unequal in most settings, especially those in countries with less established healthcare systems. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that 56.8 million people worldwide require PC annually, yet 78% of these individuals live in low- and middle-income countries with limited or no access to such services [7, 8]. Globally, only about 14% of those in need receive PC, with barriers including misconceptions that PC is solely for the terminal phase, insufficient national policies, lack of trained staff, limited insurance coverage, and restrictive opioid prescribing regulations [9].

In Oman, the cancer burden has been steadily increasing, with 2,358 new cases reported in 2020, of which 45% were diagnosed at advanced stages [2]. While healthcare services have advanced considerably, palliative care remains in its early stages of development, largely confined to two tertiary centers in Muscat, the Royal Hospital and the Sultan Qaboos Comprehensive Cancer Care and Research Centre (SQCCCRC), leaving patients in remote regions with poor access [10]. Integration of PC into primary healthcare is lacking, and neither medical nor nursing curricula comprehensively cover PC. At present, training during study typically referred to exposure to a single elective course on palliative care in the Bachelor of Science in Nursing (BSN) curriculum or occasional lectures embedded in oncology modules. In-service training referred to short workshops and seminars provided by hospital education departments or the Ministry of Health, usually covering basic symptom management and communication skills. These were often optional and varied in content and depth across institutions [11].

Nurses are central to the provision of PC, playing a critical role in pain and symptom management, medication administration, psychosocial support, and communication with patients and families. However, research suggests that deficiencies in nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices remain significant barriers to effective PC delivery [12, 13]. Previous studies in Oman have primarily focused on undergraduate nursing students [14] leaving a gap in understanding how practicing oncology nurses deliver PC. Addressing this gap is essential, given their frontline role in managing complex symptoms and supporting patients and families during advanced illness.

This study was guided by the Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices (KAP) framework [15, 16], which is often used to examine how knowledge influences attitudes and practice behaviors. Nonetheless, the limitations of this framework are well recognized, as knowledge alone may not directly predict practice without system-level support, mentorship, and organizational policies [17, 18]. In this study, the KAP framework provided a structure for examining individual-level factors, while also acknowledging the broader institutional and cultural influences that shape palliative care delivery.

Conceptual framework

This study was guided by the Knowledge, Attitude, and Practice (KAP) model, a widely used framework in nursing and public health research to examine how knowledge influences attitudes and subsequently shapes practice [19]. The model assumes that adequate knowledge is foundational for developing positive attitudes, which then facilitates the translation of understanding into effective practice. In the context of palliative care (PC), the KAP framework offers a useful lens for assessing whether oncology nurses in Oman possess the knowledge needed to inform care, hold favorable attitudes to motivate action, and ultimately implement PC practices at the bedside.

However, recent evidence suggests that knowledge alone does not always predict practice behavior [12, 20, 21]. Instead, attitudes, cultural context, and system-level factors, such as institutional policies, availability of resources, and workload, often mediate or override this relationship [1, 17, 18]. These dynamics explain why knowledge–practice gaps are frequently observed in palliative care research.

For this study, the KAP framework was used as a starting point to structure the measurement of individual-level variables, while acknowledging that broader systemic and organizational influences were not captured. By applying KAP, we aimed to identify where breakdowns occur, whether in knowledge transfer, attitude formation, or practice implementation and to provide baseline evidence for designing targeted interventions. Future research in Oman should extend this framework by integrating organizational and system-level constructs, enabling a more comprehensive understanding of the factors that shape palliative care practice.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional design was employed to investigate predictors of palliative care knowledge, attitudes, and practices among oncology nurses. This design was appropriate as it allowed the assessment of relationships between demographic, educational, and professional factors and nurses’ palliative care practices at a single point in time. This manuscript was prepared following the STROBE (cross sectional studies) guidelines for cross-sectional studies to ensure comprehensive reporting of all methodological and analytical aspects [22].

Population and setting

The study was conducted at the Royal Hospital (RH) and the Sultan Qaboos Comprehensive Cancer Care and Research Centre (SQCCCRC) in Muscat, Oman. These two tertiary hospitals are the main national referral centers for oncology and palliative care (PC) services. Eligible participants were registered nurses providing direct care to adult cancer patients. Inclusion criteria were: (a) registered nurses working in oncology or PC units, and (b) at least six months of clinical experience in cancer or PC care. Nurses on long-term leave or not directly involved in patient care were excluded.

Sampling and sample size

Sample size was calculated a priori using G*Power for fixed-model multiple regression (R2 deviation from zero), with 12 predictors, a medium effect size (f2 = 0.15), α = 0.05, and power of 0.80. The selection of a medium effect size (f2 = 0.15) was based on conventions established by Cohen [23] and supported by prior cross-sectional nursing studies examining relationships between knowledge, attitudes, and practice [20, 21]. This magnitude was considered a reasonable and conservative estimate, balancing the likelihood of detecting meaningful associations without overestimating the strength of relationships among variables. Calculating the sample size with these measures indicated a minimum of 139 participants. A final sample of 189 nurses was obtained, exceeding the required number and ensuring adequate power. Recruitment followed convenience sampling, facilitated by nursing unit coordinators. A total of 250 eligible nurses were invited, yielding a response rate of 75.6%.

Instrument use for data collection

The study utilized the following instruments:

Palliative Care Knowledge Questionnaire (PEACE-Q): A 34-item true/false scale covering nine domains (philosophy, cancer pain, opioid side effects, dyspnea, nausea/vomiting, psychological distress, delirium, communication, and community PC) [24]. Total scores range from 0–34, with higher scores indicating greater knowledge. Reported reliability includes KR-20 = 0.87 and test–retest intraclass correlation = 0.84. In this study, KR-20 was 0.82.

Palliative Care Knowledge Questionnaire (PEACE-Q): A 34-item true/false scale covering nine domains (philosophy, cancer pain, opioid side effects, dyspnea, nausea/vomiting, psychological distress, delirium, communication, and community PC) [24]. Total scores range from 0–34, with higher scores indicating greater knowledge. Reported reliability includes KR-20 = 0.87 and test–retest intraclass correlation = 0.84. In this study, KR-20 was 0.82.

Frommelt’s Attitude Toward Care of the Dying – Form B (FATCOD-B): A 30-item Likert-scale instrument (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree) assessing attitudes toward care of dying patients [25]. It includes 15 positively and 15 negatively worded items. Prior studies reported α = 0.90 [26]. Internal consistency reliability was re-evaluated in the current sample (α = 0.71), and no additional validity testing was undertaken beyond this reliability assessment, which are acknowledged as a limitation.

It includes 15 positively and 15 negatively worded items. Prior studies reported α = 0.90 [26]. Internal consistency reliability was re-evaluated in the current sample (α = 0.71), and no additional validity testing was undertaken beyond this reliability assessment, which are acknowledged as a limitation.

Palliative Care Self-Reported Practices Scale (PCPS): An 18-item scale assessing frequency of PC practices across six domains: pain, dyspnea, delirium, dying-phase care, communication, and patient/family-centred care [27]. Responses range from 1 (not at all) to 5 (always). Scores range 18–90, with higher scores reflecting more frequent practice. Reported α = 0.80–0.91. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.88.

Responses range from 1 (not at all) to 5 (always). Scores range 18–90, with higher scores reflecting more frequent practice. Reported α = 0.80–0.91. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.88.

Permission for use of all instruments was obtained from the original authors. The questionnaire was pilot tested with 10 oncology nurses to assess clarity, flow, and item relevance. Feedback confirmed that all items were understandable and appropriate for the study context, and no modifications were required. Internal consistency reliability was re-evaluated in the main study sample, yielding acceptable coefficients for all scales (FATCOD-B α = 0.71; PCPS α = 0.88).

Data collection procedures

Data were collected between January and March 2025 using an online self-administered questionnaire developed and distributed through QuestionPro. Eligible nurses were contacted via institutional mailing lists and nursing unit coordinators, who facilitated distribution of the survey link. The survey remained open for six weeks. To enhance response rates, two reminder messages were sent at two-week intervals.

The online platform ensured convenience and anonymity, as responses were stored securely on QuestionPro’s encrypted servers and were accessible only to the research team. Before accessing the questionnaire, participants were provided with detailed study information, and electronic informed consent was obtained.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from three institutions in Oman including the College of Nursing Research and Ethics Committee at Sultan Qaboos University (Ref. No. CON/MSN/2025/8), the Institutional Review Board of the SQCCCRC (ID number: SQCCCR-IRB&EC-2025–28-1), and the Scientific Research Committee of the Royal Hospital (Approval No. MoH/CSR/25/29653). Participation was voluntary, with assurances of confidentiality and anonymity. Electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants before completing the questionnaire. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data analysis

All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 26). Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations) were calculated to summarize participant demographics and study variables. Independent sample t tests were used to examine group differences in knowledge, attitudes, and practice across dichotomous variables (e.g., gender, nationality, education, in-service training). One-way analyses of variance (ANOVA) were conducted for categorical variables with more than two groups (e.g., age, marital status, years of clinical experience, education level). Because several demographic groups were unequal in size, the Games–Howell post-hoc test was employed for pairwise comparisons following significant ANOVA results, as it provides a more robust adjustment when homogeneity of variances cannot be assumed

Bivariate relationships between knowledge, attitudes, and practices were assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficients. Finally, multiple linear regression analysis was performed to explore factors associated with palliative care practice. Assumptions were checked prior to analysis: normality was confirmed by skewness and kurtosis values within −2 to +2, internal consistency reliability was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.71 for attitudes and 0.88 for practices), and multicollinearity was not detected (tolerance > 0.20, variance inflation factor < 10). Assumptions for both t-tests and ANOVA were verified. Homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene’s Test, and all analyses satisfied this assumption (p > 0.05).

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 189 nurses participated in the study. Most were female (84.1%), under 30 years of age (58.2%), married (69.8%), and Omani nationals (57.1%). The majority held a bachelor’s degree in nursing (85.7%), and 72% reported more than three years of clinical experience. Only 18% had received formal palliative care education, and 35.4% had completed in-service training. (See Table 1 for full demographics).

Subscale analysis of the practice domain revealed variability. The highest scores were reported in pain management (M = 4.29, SD = 0.62) and patient and family-centered care (M = 4.28, SD = 0.62), while lower scores were observed for delirium (M = 3.68, SD = 0.81) and dyspnea management (M = 4.05, SD = 0.75). More than half of participants scored below the mean in delirium (52.4%) and dyspnea (57.1%), suggesting weaker practice in these areas.

Correlation analysis showed a significant positive association between knowledge and attitudes (r = 0.47, p < 0.001) and between attitudes and practice (r = 0.18, p = 0.013). The correlation between knowledge and practice was not significant (r = −0.08, p = 0.248).

Group comparisons

Table 3 presents the results of independent tests and ANOVA. There were no significant differences in knowledge, attitudes, or practices across age groups, marital status, education level, or nationality (all p > 0.05). However, practice scores differed significantly by years of clinical experience, F(3,185) = 6.51, p < 0.001. Games–Howell post-hoc analysis revealed that nurses with more than five years of experience reported higher practice scores than those with fewer years of experience (all p < 0.05). Gender differences were observed in practice, with male nurses reporting lower scores than females (t(187) = −1.97, p = 0.050). Nurses who had received formal education in palliative care reported significantly more positive attitudes (t(187) = 3.08, p = 0.002), and in-service training was also associated with higher attitudes (t(187) = 2.42, p = 0.017).

Factors associated with palliative care practice

Multiple regression analysis identified several factors associated with practice (Table 4). The model was significant, F(12, 176) = 16.01, p < 0.001, and explained 52.2% of the variance (R2 = 0.52, Adjusted R2 = 0.49). Significant factors included Omani nationality (β = 0.15, p = 0.010), receipt of in-service training (β = 0.26, p = 0.006), receipt of palliative care education during study (β = 0.23, p = 0.016), knowledge scores (β = 0.27, p < 0.001), and attitudes (β = 0.22, p < 0.001). Gender, marital status, education level, and years of clinical experience were not significantly associated with practice.

Discussion

This study explored the knowledge, attitudes, and self-reported practices of oncology nurses in Oman regarding palliative care (PC), while also exploring the demographic and educational factors associated with these outcomes. The results provide a nuanced understanding of how individual characteristics and training exposure shape practice, and they highlight areas for both educational and policy interventions.

Overall, the findings showed that nurses reported comparatively high levels of knowledge and generally positive attitudes toward palliative care. However, knowledge did not translate into better self-reported practice, consistent with prior work in Oman and the region [12, 14]. Instead, attitudes and training emerged as more influential, with both in-service training and training received during nursing education significantly associated with stronger practice. This suggests that the translation of knowledge into clinical behavior is mediated by motivation, reinforcement, and contextual supports rather than knowledge alone [17, 18].

Subscale analysis revealed important strengths and gaps. Nurses reported higher practice scores in pain management, patient- and family-centered care, and communication, but weaker scores in delirium and dyspnoea management. These findings mirror global reports where symptom control for less visible or more complex conditions, such as delirium, is often neglected in practice [1]. The weak knowledge–practice correlation further emphasizes systemic barriers, including limited training resources, restricted opioid policies, and heavy workloads, which prevent nurses from translating what they know into what they do at the bedside [20].

Sociodemographic analysis also yielded notable insights. Although there were no statistically significant differences between Omani and non-Omani nurses in the bivariate analyses, the multivariate regression indicated that Omani nationality was a positive factor associated with stronger reported practice when controlling for knowledge, attitude, and training. This pattern may reflect the advantage of contextual familiarity with Omani family structures, religious values, and end-of-life traditions, which can facilitate culturally sensitive palliative care. At the same time, the finding highlights the importance of providing culturally tailored training and mentorship for expatriate nurses to ensure equitable and consistent care delivery.

Nurses with more than five years of clinical experience reported significantly higher palliative care practice scores, suggesting that professional maturity and accumulated bedside exposure enhance skill application and confidence in end-of-life care. This aligns with evidence from Ethiopia and Jordan, where years of experience were found to significantly influence nurses’ palliative care practice [28, 29]. Experienced nurses are more likely to have encountered complex clinical scenarios, refined their symptom management abilities, and developed effective communication with patients and families, which translated into more consistent and holistic care. These findings underscore the importance of structured mentorship and experiential learning strategies to help early-career nurses build competence and deliver high-quality palliative care.

Gender differences were less consistent, although male nurses reported slightly lower practice scores compared with female nurses, echoing some international findings on gendered perceptions of caring roles. Educational level (BSc vs. Diploma vs. Postgraduate) was not significantly associated with practice, suggesting that targeted training and ongoing professional development may be more influential than formal degree level in shaping effective practice. Interestingly, although training was inconsistent and often limited to brief workshops or elective courses, even this fragmented exposure was significantly associated with better practice scores. This underscores the potential benefit of embedding structured programs such as the ELNEC framework or locally adapted palliative care modules into both undergraduate education and continuing professional development.

The study also highlights the central role of attitudes. Nurses with more positive attitudes were more likely to report higher levels of practice, aligning with evidence from Spain [13] and Oman [12]. Positive attitudes reflect empathy, holistic care values, and willingness to engage with dying patients and families, making them a critical driver of actual practice behaviors. Educational interventions that enhance both attitudes and skills, such as the End-of-Life Nursing Education Consortium (ELNEC) program, are therefore essential for improving palliative care delivery [30].

While the knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) framework guided this study, the findings also underscore its limitations. Knowledge did not directly predict practice, thereby challenging the assumption of a linear relationship. Instead, practice appears to be mediated by attitudes and system-level supports. This echo calls from implementation science to adopt multi-level frameworks that consider not only individual learning but also organizational structures, mentorship, and policy reinforcement [1].

Implications for practice and policy

The study has several implications for practice and policy in Oman. Nursing education programs should embed structured palliative care modules within undergraduate curricula, covering essential domains such as symptom control, psychosocial care, communication, and ethical decision-making. In-service training must also be mandatory and ongoing, reinforcing competencies and sustaining positive attitudes.

Culturally sensitive training programs should be developed to support both Omani and expatriate nurses, ensuring that care delivery aligns with patient and family values in the Omani context. Mentorship programs can further strengthen practice by pairing less experienced staff with senior nurses. At the system level, organizations should adopt standardized tools such as the Edmonton Symptom Assessment System (ESAS) and the Palliative care Outcome Scale (POS), introduce multidisciplinary team rounds, and explore tele-palliative platforms to expand access for patients in underserved areas.

Future research directions

The study highlights several avenues for future research. Qualitative studies are needed to explore nurses lived experiences, training needs, and the ethical challenges they face in providing palliative care. Such work would provide a deeper understanding of the contextual factors influencing practice. Intervention trials should also be conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of structured educational programs such as ELNEC on improving clinical practice in Oman. Moreover, patient-focused outcomes should be measured to determine whether enhanced nursing competencies translate into improved comfort, satisfaction, and quality of life for patients and their families.

Strengths and limitations

This study offers valuable insights, but its strengths and limitations should be acknowledged. Among its strengths, the multi-site design enabled the inclusion of a broad sample of oncology nurses working in Oman, providing a more representative picture of workforce preparedness. The use of validated measurement tools and rigorous regression analysis also ensured that the identified predictors of practice were robust and reliable. However, limitations must be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design restricts causal inference, meaning findings should be interpreted as associations rather than predictors. The reliance on self-reported data may have introduced social desirability bias, inflating reports of positive practices. The use of convenience sampling, although practical for recruiting busy oncology nurses, introduces potential selection bias that may limit the representativeness of the sample. Nurses who were more motivated or knowledgeable about palliative care may have been more likely to participate, which could affect the generalizability of the findings. Consequently, the external validity of the regression results should be interpreted with caution. While the model explained a consderable proportion of variance (Adjusted R2 = 0.49), the associations identified may not extend to all oncology nurses in Oman. Future studies using probability-based sampling across multiple regions are recommended to enhance representativeness and strengthen the robustness of predictive analyses. While validated instruments were used, the reliability of the FATCOD-B in this study (α = 0.71) was at the lower boundary of acceptable internal consistency, compared with α ≈ 0.90 reported in prior studies. Although cultural and contextual differences may partly explain this variation, the finding indicates that the scale’s measurement stability within the Omani nursing population requires further examination. Future studies should consider conducting local reliability testing and confirmatory factor analysis to ensure consistent construct measurement.

Conclusion

This study identifies the factors associated with palliative care practice among oncology nurses in Oman. Positive attitudes and prior training, both academic and in-service, were consistently linked with stronger reported practices, whereas knowledge alone was insufficient to drive behavior. Subscale analysis revealed strengths in pain, communication, and family support but weaknesses in delirium and dyspnoea management, indicating areas for targeted improvement. These findings highlight the need to integrate comprehensive, structured palliative care education into nursing curricula, provide ongoing in-service training, and culturally adapt programs to support both Omani and expatriate nurses. At the policy level, reinforcing organizational supports and addressing systemic barriers will be essential to strengthen palliative care delivery across Oman.

By addressing gaps in training, fostering positive attitudes, and creating supportive practice environments, oncology nurses in Oman can be better equipped to meet the growing palliative care needs of patients with advanced cancer and their families.
