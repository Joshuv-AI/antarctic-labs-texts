Assessing Natural Health Product-Drug Interactions and Coding Feasibility: Analysis of a New Zealand General Population Dataset from an Online Market Research Panel
Authors: E Lyn Lee, Fraser Ogle, Jyadeep Nagra, Sofia Su Ye, Jeff Harrison, Joanne Barnes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNatural health products (NHPs) include complementary/alternative medicines, dietary supplements, nutraceuticals, and traditional medicines. Natural health products are often self-prescribed and used alongside conventional medicines, raising safety concerns.AimsThis study aims to explore NHP-conventional medicine (NHP-drug) combinations used by respondents in New Zealand (NZ), identify those with the potential to cause NHP-drug interactions, and assesses the feasibility of coding NHPs using the WHODrug dictionary.MethodsA dataset from a pilot study on NHP use in NZ was analysed. Data on NHP and conventional medicine use were descriptively analysed. Where possible, specific product ingredient lists were verified. Two coders independently assigned WHODrug Global codes to each NHP and conventional medicine. Fleiss’ Kappa measured inter-coder agreement.ResultsOf 992 participants, 381 (38.4%) were current NHP users, reporting 1–18 NHPs. Among them, 271 (71.1%) used conventional medicines concurrently. Natural health product-drug combinations with potential interactions were found with fish oil, coenzyme Q10, turmeric, and glucosamine. An interaction involving fish oil and warfarin was rated as ‘may result in significant hazard.’One-third (33.1%) of NHPs could not be coded using WHODrug. Codability agreement was higher for conventional medicines (93.7%) than for NHPs (63.5%). Fleiss’ Kappa indicated moderate agreement for NHPs (κ = 0.576) and very good agreement for conventional medicines (κ = 0.911).ConclusionSubstantial concurrent NHP-drug use was identified, including combinations with potential adverse reactions. A larger, nationally representative dataset on NHP exposures in NZ is needed. Current challenges in coding NHPs highlight the need for standardised, comprehensive coding tools in future research for comprehensive analysis.

Key Points

Natural health products (NHPs) are easily accessible and often self-prescribed, posing potential safety concerns when used alongside conventional medicines.This study found that 71.1% of NHP users in New Zealand were concurrently using conventional medicines. Natural health product-drug use combinations were identified that may cause adverse reactions.Challenges were encountered in coding NHPs using WHODrug, with approximately one-third unable to be coded. A standardised coding dictionary for NHPs is crucial for comprehensive analysis and monitoring their use alongside conventional medicines to safeguard public health.

Introduction

Worldwide, a variety of health products, preparations, practitioners, and practices is collectively referred to as ‘traditional, complementary and alternative medicine’ (TCAM) [1, 2]. TCAM is broadly categorised into ‘natural health products/preparations (NHPs),’ including vitamins, minerals, probiotics, herbal medicines, and traditional medicines (TMs), and ‘practices/therapies’, such as chiropractic and acupuncture. Some NHPs contain isolated ingredient(s), such as glucosamine and omega-3 fatty acids, while others, such as herbal and TMs, consist of complex mixtures containing many potentially active constituents. Often, the active constituent(s) responsible for pharmacological activity are unknown. Dose forms for NHPs range from crude forms, such as fresh or dried plant material, to semi-crude preparations (e.g., tinctures, extracts), and fully manufactured or finished products (e.g., tablets, capsules) [3].

Data from a systematic review of studies from 15 countries suggest substantial TCAM use internationally (9.8–76%) [4]; however, prevalence of use varies across countries owing to differences in access, regulations, cultural aspects, historical influence, and conventional healthcare system advancement [3]. In NZ, the Adult Nutrition Survey 2008/9 revealed that 47.6% of respondents used dietary supplements within the previous 12 months [5].

TCAM popularity is driven by its affordability, accessibility, and perceived safety. Consumers typically self-treat with NHPs without professional guidance and often do not disclose their use of NHPs to healthcare practitioners [6], raising concerns about potential adverse drug reactions (ADRs) from interactions where patients use NHPs and conventional medicines concurrently [7, 8]. Despite perceived safety, serious ADRs associated with NHPs and interactions with conventional medicines have been reported, such as hepatotoxicity associated with black cohosh (Actaea racemosa L.) [9, 10] and increased bleeding risk with concurrent use of ginkgo (Ginkgo biloba L.) and antiplatelet or anticoagulant drugs [11]. The ease of access and self-prescription of TCAM products, combined with consumers’ perceptions of these products/preparations as being free from potential harm, necessitate vigilant monitoring. While studies on concurrent NHP and conventional medicine use exist in many countries, including the USA, UK, Canada, and Australia [12, 13], similar data for NZ are lacking.

Effective monitoring of NHP use, including concurrent use with conventional medicines, requires consistent data collection and coding. WHODrug Global, the international reference for medicinal product information, covers conventional medicines and herbal remedies, offering a standardised drug dictionary for identification and data aggregation [14]. However, NHPs are generally excluded from its standardised drug groupings (SDG) [15], limiting data aggregation and analysis [SDGs are part of a hierarchical structure that groups medications/products based on one or more common properties, such as indication, chemical properties, and pharmacodynamic or pharmacokinetic properties]. Despite its limitations, WHODrug Global remains the most robust tool for coding medicinal products, including NHPs.

This study aimed to identify NHP-conventional medicine (NHP-drug) combinations used by respondents and, in particular, those with the potential to cause NHP-drug interactions. Additionally, this study assessed the feasibility of coding NHPs using a drug dictionary (WHODrug Global).

Methods

This study analysed data from a pilot study investigating the prevalence and patterns of NHP use among adults in New Zealand (NZ). Data collection, using a structured questionnaire created in Qualtrics, occurred in November 2021, targeting adults aged ≥ 18 years. A market research firm was contracted to obtain a nationwide sample of 1000 responses, covering a spectrum of age, sex, geographic location, and ethnicity that aligned with the demographics of the NZ census 2018.

The questionnaire was designed for web-based self-administration; pretesting demonstrated good validity and reliability [16]. Participants reported their use of NHPs (lifetime, past 12 months, and current) and conventional medicines (current), listing all product names/types. Participants categorised NHPs into formulated/specially compounded and manufactured/commercial products. Comprehensive details, including proprietary names, dose forms, and costs, were provided for each listed product. For manufactured/commercial NHPs, participants also uploaded photographs of product labels and ingredient lists.

For the purposes of the study, NHPs were defined as: including, but [is] not limited to, products or preparations described as natural health products, complementary/alternative medicines/remedies, dietary supplements, nutraceuticals, and/or traditional medicines (products or preparations used in traditional medicine systems, such as rongoā Māori, or traditional Chinese medicine); NHPs may be used in different dose forms, including tablets, capsules, powders, liquids, creams, ointments, and fresh or dried herbs. Natural health products may contain a single ingredient (such as one dietary supplement ingredient, e.g., glucosamine, or one herbal medicine ingredient, e.g., kava root) or several ingredients (e.g., many ‘traditional medicines’ are preparations of several herbal ingredients or herbal ingredients and other substances).

Current use was defined as: Current use refers to products/medicines that you are taking daily, or at regular intervals over time (e.g., you take the product once a week), as well as products/medicines that you only take when needed (e.g., products for seasonal allergies).

Data from the questionnaire were exported from Qualtrics® to Microsoft Excel v16.75.2 (Seattle, USA) for descriptive analysis. The feasibility of coding individual NHPs using WHODrug Global was tested for manufactured/commercial products, for which participants provided product photographs. These photographs were used to verify the specific product ingredients. Subsequently, two of five undergraduate pharmacy research students independently, and blinded to each other’s assessments, coded each NHP, assigning a WHODrug Global code, if available. For comparison, conventional medicines were also coded. Fleiss kappa (κ) was used to determine the level of agreement between coders. There are no definitive guidelines for interpreting Fleiss kappa values, but Cohen’s kappa coefficient is suggested to assess the strength of agreement between coders (< 0.2: poor, 0.21–0.40: fair, 0.41–0.60: moderate, 0.61–0.80: good, 0.81–1.00: very good) [17].

A third researcher (ELL) reviewed and resolved coding disagreements between the student coders. The WHODrug code is an 11-character sequence consisting of a Drug Record Number (6 characters), Seq 1 (2 characters), and Seq 2 (3 characters). The drug record number identifies active moieties, referring to scientific plant names for herbal products: Seq 1 identifies variations (e.g., salts, esters, plant parts); Seq 2 represents the medication name in WHO-Drug (trade or generic names) [18, 19]. For NHPs, ingredient variations and plant part information are often not listed on labels; thus, coding focused on the drug record number. Researcher ELL also checked and confirmed codes where there was agreement between the two student coders.

To examine potential interactions between NHPs and conventional medicines, five popular NHPs were selected: fish oil (omega-3), coenzyme Q10, turmeric, glucosamine, and probiotics. These were chosen based on market research highlighting high revenue shares in the “digestive and heart” and “bone and joint” segments in Australia and New Zealand, with demand expected to grow until 2027 [20, 21]. As the grouping of WHODrug codes by NHP ingredient (e.g., fish oil-containing products) is unavailable, keywords, such as ‘fish oil’, ‘omega’, ‘turmeric’, and ‘curcumin’, were used to identify users of the five selected products in the dataset. Conventional medicines used concurrently with these NHPs were identified, and NHP-drug combinations were checked for potential interactions using Stockley’s Herbal Medicines Interactions [22] and Lexicomp® Drug Interactions [23] resources.

Results

Data from 992 participants representative of the demographic of the NZ census 2018 were analysed (Table 1); of these, 381 (38.4%) were current NHP users and reported using 1–18 different NHPs (Table 2). Among current NHP users, 271 (71.1%) were using conventional medicines concurrently. In total, 880 NHPs and 2321 conventional medicine entries were recorded in the dataset (Table 3).

Coding NHP Entries Using WHODrug Global

Of the 683 manufactured/commercial NHPs in the dataset, participants had uploaded photographs of the front label and ingredient list for 231. Upon verification, 9 products were excluded from further analysis: 2 were conventional medicines, and photographs were incorrect or unclear for 7 products. About one-third (33.1%) of the NHPs were not assignable to a WHODrug code as the ingredient, or combination of ingredients, was not found in WHODrug. There was 63.5% (n = 141/222) agreement between coders regarding NHP product codability (Yes/No) using WHODrug Global. In contrast, conventional medicines had a higher assignability rate to a WHODrug code (92.1%, n = 2138/2321) and showed higher coder agreement (93.7%) regarding their codability. Using the drug record numbers assigned to each product/medicine, Fleiss kappa showed moderate agreement between coders for NHPs (κ = 0.576, 95% CI 0.538–0.614, p < 0.001) and very good agreement for conventional medicines (κ = 0.911, 95% CI 0.905–0.918, p < 0.001).

Of the 141 NHPs where there was an agreement between the two student coders, the third coder (ELL) made changes (e.g., removed or selected a different code) to Drug Record Numbers that were assigned to 17 products.

Discussion

This study identified NHP-drug combinations used by NZ adult respondents and explored those with the potential to cause NHP-drug interactions. The study also assessed the feasibility of coding NHPs using an international reference for medicinal product information, WHODrug Global [14].

In this study, 71.1% of current users of NHPs were also using conventional medicines, with 59.8% using prescribed medications and NHPs concurrently. This concurrent NHP-drug use raises safety concerns due to potential adverse interactions between certain NHPs and conventional medicines [25, 26]. From the NHP-drug use analysis for a subset of this dataset, NHP-drug combinations that may cause NHP-drug interactions were identified based on two authoritative specialist NHP interactions references. Although most interactions were based on limited scientific evidence with unclear clinical significance, an interaction (fish oil + warfarin) that may result in a significant hazard was documented. Other local [27] and international studies [12, 28] have reported similar findings: concurrent use of NHPs and prescription medicines is prevalent, putting consumers at risk of adverse reactions resulting from NHP-drug interactions. Organ toxicity (nephrotoxicity, hepatotoxicity) from the use of specific NHPs has also been observed [29–31]; these effects may be more likely to occur where patients are taking conventional medicines that can also cause renal and/or hepatic toxicity. As NHPs can have inadequate and/or incorrect labelling, and as some are adulterated with undeclared conventional medicines, patients (and their healthcare providers) may be unaware of the substances to which they (patients) are being exposed. Thus, it is critical that there is continuous monitoring of suspected adverse reactions associated with NHPs through spontaneous reporting and, possibly, other vigilance methods. Ideally, patients’ use of NHPs would be systematically and comprehensively recorded on their electronic health records to facilitate optimisation of use of medicines and other healthcare approaches.

In a broader context, capturing data on patients’ use of NHPs in electronic health records, akin to the routine collection of data on dispensed medicines in NZ and elsewhere, holds promise for optimising patient care through generating evidence based on ‘real-world’ data. While NZ [32] and other countries [33, 34] have successfully utilised routinely collected data on conventional medicines’ exposures in observational studies based on ‘real-world’ data, including exploring associations with health outcomes, the scarcity of NHP exposure data hampers similar investigations in the TCAM area. In Taiwan, where conventional medicine and TCAM coexist as mainstream healthcare, a few studies have investigated the concurrent use of herbal products and conventional medicines, alongside health outcomes, using national health insurance claims data [35]. Population-based studies leveraging real-world data sources, such as national databases and registries, focusing on conventional medicines, have demonstrated their potential to influence health policy and enhance clinical care and patient outcomes [34]. Therefore, establishing robust NHP exposure data collection mechanisms could facilitate further exploration of health outcomes, including the actual - rather than potential - impacts of concurrent NHP-drug use.

Collecting accurate and reliable data on NHP exposures from users is challenging even in the context of research studies designed for this. In this study, coding NHP product exposures at a granula, (i.e. specific manufacturer’s product) level with WHODrug was achieved for those products with uploaded photographs of labels and ingredient lists for verification. Previous research has highlighted the inaccuracies in self-reported NHP exposure data, with consumers struggling to list ingredients and categorise products, especially for multi-ingredient formulations [16]. Although manufactured/commercial NHPs typically have labels, there are no global standards for NHP labelling. This, alongside the complexities of nomenclature for plant-derived substances [36, 37] and other substances of natural origin, such as animal parts, and bacterial strains used in probiotics, means that labels can be inconsistent, incorrect and/or incomplete. In countries such as the USA [38], Canada [39], and Australia [40], dietary supplements/NHPs must comply with registration and labelling requirements, including providing ingredient names and registration numbers, which aids data collection. However, formulated or specially compounded NHPs from TCAM practitioners often lack labels or complete ingredient information, complicating data collection. Overall, for any meaningful analysis of NHP exposure, including NHP-drug interactions, obtaining an accurate list of product ingredients is crucial. Therefore, it is essential for future prevalence of use (and de novo observational) studies to consider using alternative data collection methods, such as interviewer-administered surveys, to collect precise information on NHP exposures.

Notwithstanding data quality issues, NHPs must be coded in a standardised manner for data aggregation and analysis. Despite its extensive coverage for both conventional medicines and NHPs, WHODrug is challenging to apply for NHPs because ingredient labels for NHPs are frequently incomplete (e.g., truncated and/or incorrect plant [scientific] names) and not labelled consistently (e.g., a mixture of common and scientific names for different ingredients in a product, different terms/names used throughout the label). Depending on the keywords entered and/or ingredient names selected in the WHODrug search engine, the resulting code identifying a particular ingredient or combination of ingredient(s) differs (see Box 1). Additionally, about one-third of the NHPs in this study with photograph-verified ingredients were not codable as the ingredients, or combination of ingredients, were not found in WHODrug. While it is acknowledged that new codes can be requested when not found, where codes can be assigned, further analysis is restricted as NHPs are generally excluded from the WHO-Drug Standardised Drug Groupings (SDGs) hierarchical structure, although some exceptions exist (e.g., essential fatty acids). The Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification system, integrated with WHODrug, categorises active substances according to the organ or system on which they act and their therapeutic, pharmacological and chemical properties [19]. However, as with the WHODrug, ATC codes returned are dependent on the keywords used to search in WHODrug (see Box 1). Thus, coding NHPs with WHODrug and the integrated ATC for analysis remains limited.

The development of a comprehensive international classification and coding framework for NHPs is essential. This framework should provide a hierarchical structure for all NHPs from category level to species (where relevant), bacterial strain (where relevant), animal/plant part(s) used (where relevant), active ingredient(s), type of extract, and linked to names of proprietary products (where relevant). Such universal coding would enable trend analysis and comparison across studies. Although primarily for medical diagnoses rather than treatments/products, the inclusion of Traditional Chinese Medicine diagnoses in the latest International Classification of Diseases 11th revision (ICD-11) signifies progress towards a global standard coding system for some TM systems, offering a tool for robust pharmacoepidemiologic research [41]. WHODrug Global is the most extensive international coding dictionary available for NHPs [14], and new products can be added upon request. In addition, the Herbal-ATC system is conveniently integrated as part of WHODrug Global [42]. However, WHODrug’s applicability for NHPs remains limited. While the coverage for NHPs continues to expand in WHODrug global, future studies should also explore how the WHODrug codes for NHPs can be grouped or classified in meaningful ways (e.g., by active constituent(s) or pharmacokinetic properties such as cytochrome P450 [CYP] inducers or inhibitors) for data analysis. Other than WHODrug Global and the Herbal-ATC system, coding systems such as the Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine—Clinical Terms (SNOMED CT) [43] and the British National Formulary (BNF) code [44, 45] also include NHP coding and could be explored in future studies for their potential use in classification.

This work has several strengths and limitations. The present study is the first national study in NZ that explored prevalence of concurrent NHP-drug use in the adult general population. Due to the pilot nature of this study, and the self-selected nature of the sample, caution is advised when interpreting results, and their applicability to a broader population is limited. Additionally, as previously mentioned, data quality concerns arose due to inaccuracies in self-reported NHP ingredient data. In analysing NHP-drug combinations that have the potential to cause NHP-drug interactions, users of the 5 selected NHPs were identified in a non-systematic manner using a keyword search. For this reason, some participants may have been missed (e.g., incomplete ingredients listed and/or typographical errors entered by participants), resulting in an incomplete list of potential drug interactions observed in the sample. Also, the data were explored for NHP-drug interactions but not NHP-NHP interactions, as clinical evidence for these is currently limited. As with all self-reported surveys, data may be affected by social desirability and recall biases.

Conclusion

Substantial concurrent use of NHPs and conventional medicines was observed, with some NHP-drug combinations used having the potential to lead to adverse reactions. A larger, nationally representative dataset on NHP exposure in NZ is warranted to confirm findings from this pilot study. Overall, the use of NHPs, alongside conventional medicines in NZ warrants systematic and continuous monitoring to safeguard public health. Currently, coding NHPs exposures is challenging. Future research should prioritise further development of a standardised coding dictionary and hierarchy to enable comprehensive data coding and, ultimately, data analysis.
