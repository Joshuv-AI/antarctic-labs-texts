Ongoing Outbreak of Neisseria meningitidis Serogroup B in Pará State, Brazilian Amazon Region, Following the COVID-19 Pandemic
Authors: Diana da Costa Lobato, Ângela Cristina dos Reis Maia de Souza, Caroline Gava, Camilla de Miranda Ribeiro, Raissa dos Santos Calado Sampaio de Alencar, Greice Madeleine Ikeda do Carmo, Rosa Márcia Saraiva Gentil, Susan Beatriz Batista de Oliveira, Carlos Henrique Camargo, Claudio Tavares Sacchi, Karoline Rodrigues Campos, Maria Eduarda de Araújo Morais, Gabriela Andrade de Carvalho, Adrianne da Silva Zifirino, Ana Paula Silva de Lemos, Danielle Melo Sardinha, Karla Valéria Batista Lima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInvasive meningococcal disease (IMD) remains a major cause of mortality worldwide, yet its dynamics in the Brazilian Amazon are poorly understood. Following the COVID-19 pandemic, serogroup B IMD resurged in Brazil, raising concerns about changing transmission patterns in regions with limited healthcare access.MethodsWe conducted an epidemiological and genomic investigation of an ongoing IMD outbreak in Pará State, Brazilian Amazon, between 2023 and 2025. Clinical, demographic, and outcome data were integrated with whole-genome sequencing of 69 isolates. Multivariate logistic regression identified independent predictors of mortality, and phylogenomic analyses characterized transmission dynamics and clonal expansion.ResultsA total of 343 IMD cases were identified, of which 321 (93.6%) were caused by serogroup B, resulting 34 deaths (case-fatality rate, 10.6%). Incidence increased up to 5-fold compared with national rates and disproportionately affected young children and adults. Mortality was independently associated with meningococcemia (adjusted odds ratio, 84.52; 95% confidence interval, 5.70–1253.65; P = .001); however, this finding should be interpreted with caution due to the small number of meningococcemia cases. Genomic analysis identified a predominant ST-7983/cc60 lineage, accounting for 64 of 69 sequenced isolates (92.7%). This lineage displayed limited genetic diversity (median, 7 single nucleotide polymorphisms), consistent with sustained regional transmission across multiple municipalities.ConclusionsA highly clonal serogroup B meningococcal outbreak emerged in the Brazilian Amazon following the COVID-19 pandemic, with substantial mortality and rapid geographic spread. These findings highlight the importance of strengthened genomic surveillance and support targeted vaccination strategies to improve outbreak preparedness in underserved regions.Keywords: invasive meningococcal disease, Neisseria meningitidis, outbreak, serogroup B

Invasive meningococcal disease (IMD), caused by Neisseria meningitidis, is a severe and rapidly progressive infection that may present as meningitis, septicemia, or both, and is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality. Twelve serogroups have been identified based on the antigenic composition of the capsular polysaccharide. However, most IMD worldwide is caused by a limited number of serogroups, A, B, C, W, Y, and X, which together account for the majority of IMD cases and are frequently associated with endemic transmission, outbreaks, and epidemics [1].

The epidemiology of IMD in Brazil has been characterized by epidemic waves associated with shifts in predominant serogroups, with 4 major epidemic periods documented over the past century. Early epidemics in the 1920s and late 1940s were predominantly caused by serogroup A and reached incidence rates exceeding 20 cases per 100 000 inhabitants [2]. A third epidemic wave occurred in the early 1970s, driven by the co-circulation of serogroups A and C, culminating in a large epidemic in São Paulo State that led to a nationwide mass vaccination campaign with a bivalent A/C polysaccharide vaccine in 1975, followed by a marked decline in incidence [3]. A fourth and prolonged epidemic period began in the late 1980s and extended through the early 2000s, initially dominated by serogroup B and later by increasing circulation of serogroup C. In response to the rise of serogroup C disease, the meningococcal C conjugate vaccine was introduced into the Brazilian National Immunization Program in 2010 for infants [4]. Following vaccine introduction, a substantial decline in serogroup C IMD was observed, and serogroup B has since become the predominant cause of IMD in Brazil, particularly among infants (Supplementary Figures 1 and 2).

Since 2013, 2 protein-based vaccines against serogroup B (MenB-FHbp and 4CMenB) have been licensed in Europe and the Americas, including Brazil. MenB-FHbp contains recombinant factor H binding proteins from 2 subfamilies [5], while 4CMenB is a multicomponent vaccine comprising several recombinant antigens, including factor H-binding protein (fHbp), Neisseria adhesin A (NadA), Neisserial Heparin Binding Antigen (NHBA), and porin protein A (PorA) [6]. Since 2015, both vaccines have been implemented in national immunization programs in several countries, as well as used for outbreak control, as demonstrated by the recent serogroup B outbreak in South West, United Kingdom [7] and individual protection strategies; however, in Brazil, their availability remains restricted to private clinics, with no inclusion in the routine public immunization schedule [8–11].

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the incidence of IMD decreased substantially worldwide, likely reflecting the impact of nonpharmaceutical interventions that reduced transmission of respiratory pathogens [12]. However, with the relaxation of COVID-19 restrictions, a rebound in IMD has been observed in multiple regions, including Latin America [13, 14]. In Brazil, a rebound of serogroup B IMD has been observed in the post-COVID-19 pandemic period, accompanied by a rising incidence rate from 0.45 in 2021 to 1.73 among infants in 2025 (Supplementary Figure 2).

Based on surveillance data, incidence curves, and observed trends, an outbreak of serogroup B IMD was declared in Belém capital of the Pará State in 2023. Among infants, the incidence increased from 0.8 per 100 000 population in 2023 to 6.59 per 100 000 in 2025 in Pará State. In the same age group, incidence in the Metropolitan Region of Belém, the State capital, increased from 3.72 to 32.36 per 100 000 population during the same period, currently representing the highest incidence reported in the state (Supplementary Figures 3 and 4). This report presents an integrated analysis of the ongoing serogroup B IMD outbreak (January 2023–December 2025), combining clinical, epidemiological, and genomic data to characterize transmission and public health impact.

METHODS

A descriptive and analytical study was conducted of the ongoing serogroup B IMD outbreak (2023–2025) in Pará State, in the Amazon region of northern Brazil. Cases were characterized by age, sex, municipality of residence, clinical presentation, and outcome (Supplementary 1 Table), and analyzed over time by epidemiological week, month, and year of notification. The clinical presentation of IMD cases was classified according to the record in the Notifiable Diseases Information System (Sinan) and the evaluation of signs and symptoms recorded on the notification form. Cases and deaths were summarized using absolute and relative frequencies, incidence, mortality, and case-fatality rates (CFRs). Incidence and mortality rates were calculated using annual population estimates provided by the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics, the official source of demographic data in Brazil. Age-specific incidence rates were used to describe temporal and demographic patterns of disease occurrence during the study period. Population denominators remained relatively stable throughout the study period.

Case Definitions, Investigation, and Control Measures

Case and outbreak definitions followed Brazilian national guidelines (Supplementary Methods). Suspected IMD was defined as acute febrile illness with meningeal signs, neurological symptoms, or petechial rash; confirmation required laboratory evidence or epidemiologic linkage. An outbreak was defined as ≥3 primary cases of the same serogroup within ≤3 months in a defined area, exceeding baseline incidence. Cases were reported by healthcare facilities and investigated by hospital and public health teams. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples were analyzed at the state reference laboratory (LACEN-PA) by culture and molecular methods. Management included prompt treatment, contact tracing, and chemoprophylaxis, with expanded prophylaxis during outbreaks, primarily rifampicin, with ceftriaxone or ciprofloxacin as alternatives, according to Brazilian national guidelines.

Epidemiological Data

State control charts were constructed using IMD incidence data from the Sinan (2014–2017 and 2019) to estimate the mean and standard deviation, including confirmed cases without serogroup specification. Data from 2018 were excluded due to substantially higher incidence compared with the surrounding baseline period and potential distortion of the upper control limits. Data from 2020 to 2022 were excluded due to disruptions in surveillance and healthcare utilization associated with the COVID-19 pandemic. The upper threshold was defined as the mean plus 1.96 standard deviations. Calculations were performed by month and year of notification. Incidence rates for 2023–2025 were obtained from the Pará State Health Department database and used for graphical comparison only, including confirmed meningococcal serogroup B cases.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics v29. Univariate associations with mortality were assessed using Pearson's χ2 or Fisher's exact tests, as appropriate, with crude odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Multivariable logistic regression was performed using the forced-entry method, also known in SPSS as the Enter method, in which all covariates defined a priori were entered simultaneously into the model. The adjusted model was restricted to variables with biological, clinical, or epidemiological plausibility as potential predictors or confounders, including age group and clinical presentation. Variables with P < .20 in univariate analysis were considered only as an exploratory screening criterion to avoid prematurely excluding potentially relevant covariates; however, inclusion in the final adjusted model was based primarily on pre-specified clinical and epidemiological relevance, not on statistical significance alone. The 5- to 14-year-age group was used as the reference category. Model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, and explanatory power was assessed using Nagelkerke's R2. Multicollinearity was examined using the variance inflation factor and Condition Index. Discrimination was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. Sensitivity to unmeasured confounding was assessed using E values. Statistical significance in the final model was set at α = .05 [15, 16].

Laboratory Procedures

A total of 343 IMD cases were included in the study. Diagnosis was established by reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR; 266 cases), culture (73 cases), or other criteria (4 cases). Among culture-confirmed cases, 73 isolates were recovered and further characterized by conventional identification methods and matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS), serogrouped by slide agglutination, and tested for antimicrobial susceptibility according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was performed on 69 serogroup B isolates (Illumina). Genomes were assembled and analyzed to assess genetic relatedness using core-genome single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), with maximum-likelihood phylogenies and pairwise SNP distances to define clusters, integrated with epidemiological data. A lineage-specific analysis, including public genomes from PubMLST (https://pubmlst.org/), was conducted to investigate the origin and spread of the dominant lineage, Supplementary Methods.

RESULTS

During 2023–2025, 343 IMD cases were confirmed among residents of Pará State: 266 by RT-PCR, 73 by culture, and 4 by other criteria. Among 339 laboratory-confirmed cases, serogroup was determined for 329/339 (97.0%) predominantly serogroup B (321), with few cases of serogroup C (7) and serogroup Y (1).

Among sero/genogroup B cases, 34 deaths occurred (CFR 10.6%). The highest number of cases and deaths was observed in 2025, with elevated CFR among individuals aged 40–59 years. Adults aged 20–39 (30.3%) and 40–59 years (23.4%) accounted for most cases, while the highest incidence rates were in children <1 and 1–4 years in 2025 (Table 1).

Univariate Analysis

Univariate analysis (Table 2) showed that age, race, and clinical presentation were associated with mortality (P < .05). Overall CFR was 10.6%, with a bimodal age pattern: highest in children 1–4 years (22.2%; OR 18.29; 95% CI 2.18–153.3) and ≥60 years (21.4%; OR 17.45; 95% CI 1.67–182.7), and lowest in 5–14 years (1.5%). White individuals had higher CFR than the Parda majority (28.6% vs 9.6%; OR 3.78; P = .028). Clinical presentation strongly influenced outcomes: meningococcemia had very high CFR (87.5%; OR 74.15; 95% CI 8.79–625.2; P < .001), whereas meningitis was associated with lower risk (5.6%; OR 0.18; 95% CI 0.09–0.39; P < .001). No association was observed with sex (P = .697).

Multivariate Analysis

In the multivariate logistic regression model, clinical presentation and age group were independently associated with mortality. Meningococcemia was the strongest predictor of death (OR 84.52; 95% CI 5.70–1253.65; P = .001), whereas meningitis was associated with lower odds of death (OR 0.28; 95% CI 0.12–0.65; P = .003). Using the 5- to 14-year-age group as reference, higher odds of death were observed among individuals aged ≥60 years (OR 36.27; P = .015), 1–4 years (OR 27.39; P = .017), and 25–59 years (OR 20.66; P = .023). Infants <1 year and individuals aged 15–24 years were not significantly associated with mortality.

Laboratory Findings

All 69 serogroup B isolates were susceptible to the antimicrobials tested. Four sequence types (STs) were identified, clustering into 3 clonal complexes: cc60 (64/69; 92.8%), cc162 (3/69; 4.3%), and cc865 (2/69; 2.9%). Three PorA/FetA profiles were detected, with P1.5,2:F1-7 predominating in cc60 (n = 64). The outbreak strain was characterized by the presence of fHbp peptide 13 and NHBA peptide 24 within cc60, and by the absence of an intact nadA due to frameshift mutations or insertion elements (Supplementary 3 Table 1). According to the MendeVAR index (https://pubmlst.org/organisms/neisseria-spp/mendevar), fHbp peptide 13 has been reported as potentially cross-reactive with the MenB-FHbp vaccine, and on the other hand, as having insufficient data about cross-reactivity to predict coverage by 4CMenB vaccine. Previous studies have also highlighted variability in antigen expression, suggesting that isolates carrying fHbp peptide 13 may be either covered or not covered by the 4CMenB [17]. In contrast, NHBA peptide 24, it is not predicted to be covered based on genetic Meningococcal Antigen Typing System (gMATS) predictions [18].

Whole-genome sequencing revealed 3 phylogenetic clades (Figure 2). Clade II, dominated by ST-7983/cc60, accounted for 64/69 (92.7%) of isolates and included 2 ST-18160 strains within cc60. Clade I comprised 2 ST-3327/cc865 isolates, and Clade III comprised 3 ST-162/cc162 isolates.

Most isolates originated from the metropolitan region of Belém, but the dominant lineage was also detected across multiple municipalities, indicating widespread circulation. The ST-7983/cc60 lineage persisted from May 2023 to December 2025, with continuous detection over time. The SNP analysis (Supplementary 2 Table 3) showed limited diversity within this clade: 1261 pairs differed by ≤10 SNPs, 132 by 11–20 SNPs, and 498 by ≥21 SNPs (median 7), with some identical genomes. In contrast, clades I and III showed much greater divergence. In the global context (Supplementary Figure 7), Brazilian ST-7983 isolates formed a tight, low-diversity monophyletic cluster, consistent with clonal expansion. Globally, ST-7983 is mainly associated with other serogroups (notably E and C), with only one serogroup B isolate (France, 2022) clustering near the Brazilian lineage.

DISCUSSION

The Brazilian Amazon is a recognized hotspot for emerging and reemerging infectious diseases, with complex ecological and social factors facilitating pathogen circulation [19–21]. Here, we describe the epidemiological and genomic features of an ongoing serogroup B IMD outbreak. This potentially fulminant bacterial event is rare in the region and contrasts with the usual predominance of viral and parasitic diseases, which may contribute to late suspicion and delays in appropriate management.

The high proportion of meningitis cases observed during this outbreak may partly reflect the intensified surveillance response following outbreak recognition. Increased clinical awareness and expanded use of RT-PCR testing likely improved the detection and laboratory confirmation of meningitis cases, particularly among patients undergoing CSF collection. Conversely, isolated meningococcemia cases may be underrepresented in laboratory-based surveillance because of their rapid clinical progression and limited specimen availability. Meningococcemia remained an independent predictor of case fatality, consistent with its recognized association with severe invasive disease and poor clinical outcomes. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution given the small number of meningococcemia cases included in the analysis, which resulted in a wide CI around the estimated OR. While the observed association is biologically plausible and consistent with previous reports, the magnitude of the effect should be interpreted cautiously [22]. Older age (≥65 years) was independently associated with increased risk of death. Although IMD classically affects younger populations, older adults have consistently been shown to experience worse outcomes, likely reflecting delayed diagnosis, atypical presentation, and comorbidities, supporting ongoing discussion of targeted vaccination strategies in this group [23].

Despite the well-recognized healthcare access challenges in the Brazilian Amazon, the case-fatality rate observed in this study was comparable to that reported in higher-resource settings. This finding may reflect effective management in referral centers, the predominance of meningitis presentations over fulminant septic disease, or differences in case ascertainment. Additional studies are needed to better understand the determinants of IMD outcomes in this setting.

After 3 decades of predominance of serogroup B cc32 in Brazil, the meningococcal population structure has become increasingly diverse, with no single clonal complex currently dominating nationwide [24–26]. In contrast, cc60 has historically been rare, but during the study period a single lineage, ST-7983/cc60, emerged and accounted for nearly 90% of isolates in the Pará state.

Genomic analyses revealed low SNP diversity and a wide geographic distribution, supporting sustained regional transmission of a successful clone. The persistence and expansion of this lineage may reflect increased biological fitness, potentially associated with virulence determinants, immune evasion mechanisms, or changes in capsule expression [27, 28]. Although its evolutionary origins remain uncertain, the emergence of this lineage highlights the importance of continued genomic surveillance.

Several studies have demonstrated the diversity and distribution of key vaccine antigen variants over time and across geographic regions, which may lead to affect predicted vaccine coverage, although current evidence suggests that these reductions are not yet substantial [29, 30].

Although both protein-based serogroup B vaccines (4CMenB and MenB-FHbp) are licensed in Brazil, their use remains restricted to the private sector, with no inclusion in the National Immunization Program. This study highlights the importance of monitoring circulating strain antigenic profiles to infer potential vaccine coverage in outbreak settings.

The antigenic profile of outbreak strain observed suggests a differential potential for coverage by currently available vaccines. The fHbp peptide 13 may be cross-reactive with the MenB-FHbp vaccine, whereas evidence supporting coverage by 4CMenB remains limited and inconclusive [17]. The NHBA peptide 24 is not predicted to be covered based on current gMATS estimates [18]. Overall, these findings underscore the need for continued genomic and antigenic surveillance to inform vaccine policy and outbreak preparedness.

This study has important limitations. The large geographic extent of the Pará state and the challenges in reaching remote areas constrain laboratory diagnosis and public health surveillance, likely resulting in underestimation of disease burden. The high proportion of meningitis cases observed during this outbreak may partly reflect the intensified surveillance response following outbreak recognition. Increased clinical awareness and expanded use of RT-PCR testing likely improved the detection and laboratory confirmation of meningitis cases, particularly among patients undergoing CSF collection. Conversely, isolated meningococcemia cases may be underrepresented in laboratory-based surveillance because of their rapid clinical progression and limited specimen availability. Furthermore, most sequenced isolates were recovered from CSF. Therefore, patients presenting without meningitis may be underrepresented in the genomic analysis, potentially limiting the representativeness of the circulating meningococcal population structure.

Despite control measures, including health promotion and chemoprophylaxis, the outbreak remained ongoing at the end of the study period. The findings underscore ongoing challenges in clinical recognition, laboratory capacity, and timely treatment in the Brazilian Amazon. In line with the World Health Organization’s Defeat Meningitis by 2030 roadmap, strengthened surveillance, high and homogeneous vaccination coverage, and continuous monitoring of circulating lineages and serogroups are essential for effective disease prevention and control.
