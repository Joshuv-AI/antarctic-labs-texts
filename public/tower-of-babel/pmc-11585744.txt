Introduction

The ultrasonication method is a widely employed technique for the preparation of nanoparticles due to its effectiveness in achieving precise control over particle size, morphology, and drug encapsulation efficiency. This method involves the application of high-frequency ultrasound waves to a liquid medium containing the desired drug and carrier material. The ultrasonic waves induce various physical phenomena, including cavitation, mechanical agitation, and shear forces, leading to the formation of nanoparticles [1]. Ultrasonication plays a crucial role in nanoparticle production due to its ability to generate nanoparticles with a uniform size distribution and enhanced colloidal stability. This capability is vital for creating nanoparticulate systems with reduced particle aggregation, which is essential for maintaining drug stability and enhancing therapeutic efficacy. In pharmaceutical applications, ultrasonication-based techniques are commonly employed to produce drug-loaded nanoparticles for targeted drug delivery, controlled release, and improved bioavailability [2]. Additionally, ultrasonication finds applications in the synthesis of nanocarriers for gene delivery, imaging agents for diagnostic purposes, and nanomaterials for tissue engineering.

Diblock polymer (DIP) is synthesized by forming polymers consisting of two distinct, chemically linked blocks. This process is typically achieved using controlled radical polymerization techniques, such as atom transfer radical polymerization. In this method, a 24-hour reaction mechanism is employed under Nitrogen condition, with stannous octoate serving as the initiator (Fig. 1). These techniques allow for precise control over the length and composition of the polymer chains, enabling the creation of well-defined block structures. By adjusting the reaction conditions and monomer feed ratios, the properties of the DIP polymers can be optimized to meet specific functional requirements, making them suitable for advanced materials and drug delivery applications [3].

Representation of Di-block Polymer synthesis.

The synthesis of DIP polymers offers several benefits in various applications. DIP polymers exhibit unique properties arising from the combination of two distinct polymer blocks. This allows for the design of materials with tailored properties such as improved mechanical strength, thermal stability, or compatibility with specific solvents or surfaces. Additionally, DIP polymers can self-assemble into well-defined nanostructures such as micelles or vesicles, making them useful in drug delivery, coatings, and nanotechnology. The rapid advancement of block copolymers in drug delivery formulations is driven by their versatile and adaptable structural design. By functionalizing both the hydrophilic and hydrophobic segments, the molecular weight of block copolymers can be adjusted across a broad range while preserving a consistent hydrophilic-lipophilic balance. Additionally, block copolymers are widely utilized due to their low immunogenicity, biodegradability, and biocompatibility, making them ideal for safe human administration [4].

Dolutegravir (DTG) is a key antiretroviral medication used in the management of HIV infection [5]. It belongs to the class of integrase strand transfer inhibitors and works by blocking the integrase enzyme, which HIV uses to insert its viral DNA into the host’s cells [6]. By inhibiting this critical step in the virus’s replication process, DTG effectively prevents the virus from multiplying and spreading throughout the body [7]. Known for its high potency, favourable safety profile, and low risk of developing resistance, DTG has become an essential component of many HIV treatment regimens, significantly improving patient outcomes and quality of life [8].

Nanotechnology in drug delivery enables targeted therapies with remarkable precision. Engineered nanoparticles can cross physiological barriers, delivering therapeutic agents directly to specific cellular targets, thereby reducing side effects. Lipid and polymer-based nanoparticles encapsulate drugs, ensuring controlled release and enhancing pharmacokinetics for improved therapeutic outcomes [9].

SLNs are lipid-based nanocarriers designed for drug delivery. Composed of biocompatible lipids, they offer advantages including improved drug stability, controlled release, and enhanced bioavailability. Despite challenges such as limited drug loading and potential storage issues, ongoing research aims to optimize SLNs formulations for clinical translation [10].

This research article focuses on the synthesis of DIP polymer-based nanoparticles using the ultrasonication method and explores their potential applications in drug delivery systems. The synthesis of DIP copolymers follows a 24-hour mechanism, with stannous octoate serving as the initiator, and DTG is employed for analysis. Additionally, the study aims to unravel the underlying mechanisms governing nanoparticle formation under ultrasonication conditions, while also assessing the biocompatibility and therapeutic efficacy of the synthesized nanoparticles.

Materials and methods

Materials

The experiment employed several materials including DTG, polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) with a molecular weight ranging from 30,000 to 50,000 Da and 87 % hydrolyzed, methyl poly(ethylene glycol) (mPEG) with a molecular weight of 14,500 obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, and stannous-II octoate (Sn(Oct)2) with a purity range of 92.5–100.0 % also acquired from Sigma Aldrich. The technical-grade solvents obtained from Sigma Aldrich include dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) with a purity of 99.5 %, as well as polystyrene (PS), chloroform, and dichloromethane (DCM).

Synthesis of mPEG-PS block copolymer

The synthesis of the mPEG-PS block copolymer was carried out using a Parallel Synthesizer (Radleys, Germany). This apparatus features a precise thermostat for temperature regulation and includes a 250 ml round-bottom flask with an internal diameter of 8 cm, equipped with a 5 ml Teflon-coated stirrer. The synthesizer offers various reaction containers, including a 250 ml Azeotropic (Dean and Stark) flask. With the capacity to heat and stir up to six reactions simultaneously, this device utilizes standard carousel stirrers. To enhance stirring, a unique elliptically shaped PTFE stirring bar containing rare earth elements was employed, promoting a swirling motion and intense agitation. Additionally, the parallel synthesizer features a water-supporting device and a dedicated port for water addition. Sn(Oct)2 functioned as a catalyst to accelerate the ring-opening polymerization process involving PS and mPEG, leading to the formation of the mPEG-PS copolymer. Initially, premeasured quantities of mPEG and PS (1:2 ratio) were combined in a dry, two-necked flask containing Sn(Oct)2.

The mixture was dissolved in anhydrous toluene under a nitrogen gas atmosphere. Subsequently, the reaction system was heated to 130 °C and maintained at this temperature for an entire day (Fig. 2) [11]. Upon completion of the reaction period, the system was allowed to cool to room temperature. Once the catalyst-monomer mixture reached room temperature, it was precipitated in extremely cold diethyl ether and then immersed in chloroform to remove the solvent [12]. This procedure facilitated the separation of the mPEG-PS copolymers from the reaction mixture. The precipitated mPEG-PS copolymers were collected and subjected to drying in a pressure oven at 45 °C for 48 h to eliminate any residual solvents and moisture, resulting in the desired copolymer product [13].

Formulation and optimization of drug loaded di-block copolymer silver nanoparticles DTG- DIP-SLNs

The mPEG-PS copolymer was synthesized through the ring-opening polymerization of PS, with mPEG- acting as the initiator and Sn(Oct)2 as the catalyst. In brief, a pre-weighed amount of mPEG and PS (w/w ¼ 1:2) was added to a flask containing Sn(Oct)2, dissolved in anhydrous toluene. The system was maintained under a nitrogen atmosphere at 130 °C for 24 h, followed by cooling to room temperature. Afterward, the resulting mixture was dissolved in chloroform and precipitated using excess cold diethyl ether to eliminate unreacted monomers and catalyst. The mPEG-PS copolymer was then isolated by filtration and dried in a vacuum oven at 45 °C for 48 h.

This study focused on optimizing the formulation of SLNs tailored for antiretroviral drug delivery using a Box-Behnken design. Three key variables − surfactant concentration (X1), ultrasonication amplitude (X2), and ultrasonication time (X3) − were systematically adjusted across three levels (−1, 0, +1). Employing Design Expert software 12.0, a series of experiments was conducted based on a predetermined matrix (Table 1.). Each test meticulously assessed the influence of these variables on the crucial responses: entrapment efficiency (EE) and particle size (PS). Statistical modeling and analysis yielded regression equations, elucidating the intricate relationships between inputs and responses, facilitating predictive capabilities across the experimental domain. This approach enabled the identification of optimal formulation parameters for enhanced medication delivery efficacy. Employing the Box-Behnken design with −1, 0, +1 levels, we comprehensively explored the response surface, capturing nonlinear effects and factor interactions. Our systematic methodology aims to advance the development of efficient polymeric nanocomposites for antiretroviral drug delivery through insightful formulation optimization. ANOVA was employed for statistical inference to guide optimization, identifying relevant components and interactions across 17 experimental trials. Quadratic models were proposed for PS and EE. SLNs were synthesized using the ultrasonic-nanoemulsification technique, which began with the dispersion of surfactants in distilled water, followed by heating the lipid phase beyond its melting point. Once the lipid was completely liquefied, it was mixed with the surfactant solution to form a coarse emulsion and drug is loaded. This emulsion then underwent ultrasonic treatment for varying lengths of time to reduce droplet size and create a fine nanoemulsion. After the ultrasonic process, the SLNs formulations were filtered to eliminate larger particles and impurities, ensuring a homogeneous product. The final SLNs were stored under controlled conditions for further evaluation and characterization [16].

Characterization of DTG- DIP-SLNs

Determination of PS and Polydispersity Index

Dynamic light scattering was employed to assess the polydispersity index and PS of DTG-DIP-SLNs using the Zetasizer instrument (Litesizer 500) manufactured by Anton paar, Austria. To prepare the sample, the DTG-DIP-SLNs combination was diluted with 10 ml of deionized water. Measurements were conducted at 25 °C utilizing a 4.0 mW laser operating at a wavelength of 633 nm and requiring a medium with a refractive index of 1.33 (typically water). To ensure accuracy, three sets of readings were obtained. This analysis provides crucial information regarding the size distribution and uniformity of the particles [17].

Entrapment Efficiency

The drug loading capacity and entrapment efficiency of DTG-DIP-SLNs were evaluated using UV–Vis spectroscopy at a wavelength of 260 nm. To prepare the samples, 20 mg of DTG-DIP-SLNs was dissolved in 10 mL of phosphate buffer saline (pH 7.4) and subsequently centrifuged. The supernatants were collected, treated with ninhydrin reagent, and then analysed [18].

Vsvg-pseudotyped HIV-1 Luc

The HIV-1 proviral vector pNL4-3.Luc.R − E − and a vector expressing VSVg were co-transfected into 293 T cells, media containing HIV-1 virions were harvested 48–72 h post-transfection, sterilized with a filter, and subsequently frozen. The acquired HIV-1 Luc was then exposed to either PBMCs or CEM T cells. After a 48-hour incubation period, cell lysis was carried out using PerkinElmer's Steady Lite Luciferase buffer, followed by PBS cleaning. Light emission measurements were obtained using a PerkinElmer Luminoskan device. Trypan blue cell counting was performed to adjust for cell number, as detailed in the subsequent section [19].

Cell viability assays

Cells were cultured in 96-well plates for a complete day at 37 °C, exposed to varying nanoparticle doses. Cell vitality was assessed using a trypan blue-based assay. The cells, supplemented with 0.2 % trypan blue, were counted using a Cellometer Automatic Cell Counter (Nexcelom Bioscience) and transferred to a disposable plastic counting chamber.

Quantitative PCR for viral DNA analysis

Following exposure to HIV-1 Luc, Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cells were treated with nanoparticles for 6 h. Total DNA from 4 × 106 cells was extracted using a lysis solution containing 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8), 10 mM EDTA, 5 mM NaCl, and 200 µg/mL proteinase K. Cell lysis occurred for twenty to thirty minutes at room temperature, followed by proteinase K inactivation for five minutes at 95 °C. Real-time PCR analysis was performed using a Roche Light Cycler 480 II device (Roche Diagnostics) and SYBR Green Master mix (Roche Diagnostics), amplifying 100 ng of DNA. The PCR procedure comprised 45 cycles, including denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing and extension at 60 °C for 45 s, and final extension at 72 °C for 10 s. Pre-incubation lasted five minutes at 45 °C, followed by a three-minute incubation at 95 °C. β-globin DNA was used as the normalization reference for quantifying early LTR. Primer sequences were available for both early LTR and β-globin. Mean Cp values for β-globin and early LTR were determined using the AACT method to calculate relative expression levels. Statistical significance was assessed using an unpaired t-test [20].

Caspase-3 enzyme assay

For the caspase-3 enzyme assay, three distinct samples were utilized: DTG, DTG-NP, and DTG-DIP-NP. Human cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 media supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum at 37 °C until reaching confluency. Following cell lysis using cell extraction buffer, the resulting lysate was diluted with standard buffer. Subsequently, each well of a 96-well plate was filled with 100 µL of DMEM, and cells were seeded at a density ranging from 1.3 to 1.9 × 109 cells per well. After incubating for 24 h, the samples underwent the caspase test on the pre-treated 96-well plate. Spectrophotometric measurement at 450 nm was then performed according to the manufacturer's protocol to quantify the level of activated human caspase-3 [21].

Quantitative uptake Caco-2 cell using HPLC

For quantitative evaluation, cells were initially seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells/cm2 in a 12-well plate. Upon reaching approximately 80 % confluence, they underwent rinsing with Hank's Balanced Salt Solution at a physiological pH of 7.4 and were allowed to equilibrate for one hour. Subsequently, the cells were exposed to DTG solution at varying concentrations (10, 100, and 200 mg/ml) and optimized DTG-DIP-NP suspensions for three hours. Following this incubation period, the DTG solution and DTG-DIP-NP suspension were carefully aspirated, and the sample plates were thoroughly cleaned with cold phosphate-buffered saline at pH 7.4 to eliminate any residual particles. To permeabilize the cell membranes, the cells were then incubated with 0.5 % Triton X-100 for 30 min. Following the addition of 250 µl of the mobile phase, samples underwent centrifugation for 10 min at 14,000 rpm. The resulting supernatant was subsequently injected into an HPLC for analysis. This systematic procedure facilitated the assessment of various DTG concentrations impacted cellular uptake of nanoparticles, providing valuable insights into the mechanism of cellular uptake and the efficacy of the DTG-DIP-NP formulation [22].

Cell culture

A 25 cm × 25 cm culture plate was utilized to cultivate the Caco-2 cell line using RPMI complete medium. Growth conditions were maintained at 37 °C, 5 % CO2, and 95 % relative humidity. When the cells reached 70–80 % confluency, trypsin was applied to the monolayer to detach them from the growth plate. Following a PBS wash, the detached cells underwent another round of trypsin treatment to generate a cell suspension. This suspension was promptly reconstituted in RPMI medium and centrifuged at 2000 rpm for five minutes. The resulting pellet was centrifuged, then reconstituted for cell counting purposes. These counted cells were subsequently utilized in other research endeavors [22], [23].

Transport study

Six transwell plates, each with a surface area of 4.7 cm2, were employed to seed cells at a density of 10 × 5 cells per cm2. Monolayers were allowed to mature for 21 to 30 days post-seeding, with daily media changes to maintain cell viability. Prior to experimentation, the growth medium was replaced with transfer buffer (HBSS, pH 7.4). Optical examination confirmed the formation of tight monolayers on the transwell membrane. Lucifer yellow (LY), a paracellular leakage marker, was utilized to assess paracellular permeability, with the Apical side of the transwell inserts receiving a LY solution (100 μg/ml) and the Basolateral side receiving HBSS to evaluate monolayer integrity. Monolayers were deemed sufficiently tight for transport tests when the permeability coefficient for LY was less than 0.5 × 10-6 cm/s. For transport experiments, the Apical side of the inserts was coated with transport buffer containing DTG solution at concentrations of 10, 100, and 200 μg/ml in HBSS, or optimized DTG-DIP-NP. Additionally, 2 ml of transfer buffer was added to the Basolateral side. To maintain experimental equilibrium, 200 μl aliquots were withdrawn from the Basolateral side and replaced with an equivalent volume of transport buffer at 30, 60, 120, and 180 min post-incubation. All experiments were conducted at 37 °C. High-Performance Liquid Chromatography was employed to determine the DTG content of the samples following extraction and centrifugation [24].

Results and Discussion

FT-IR

The synthesized copolymer underwent FT-IR spectral analysis to further interpretations of its structural composition. The FT-IR spectrum of the mPEG-PS copolymers, as depicted in Fig. 3, revealed a distinct absorption peak at 1602 cm−1, indicating the presence of the carbonyl group characteristic of esters (C = O). The peak results from the stretching vibrations of the carbonyl group, a feature unique to esters, providing direct evidence of the successful formation of the mPEG-PS copolymer. This distinct peak signifies the chemical bonding and integration of the polystyrene (PS) segment into the mPEG structure, thereby confirming the effectiveness of the polymerization process. This peak serves as compelling evidence for the formation of the mPEG-PS copolymer..

NMR

The mPEG-PS copolymers were synthesized via a ring-opening polymerization reaction, and their structural characterization was conducted using 1H NMR spectroscopy, as illustrated in Fig. 4. Within the spectrum, a signal at 2.520 ppm corresponds to the DMSO solvent peak. Notably, a sharp peak in the ppm range of 3.524–3.366, with an integral value of 3, indicates the formation of methyl groups derived from polystyrene post the ring-opening reaction. Furthermore, the absence of peaks in the aromatic region (typically between 6.5 and 8.0 ppm), where aryl protons reside, provides compelling evidence for the occurrence of the ring-opening reaction of polystyrene. This absence underscores the successful transformation of the aromatic rings, affirming the structural alterations brought about by the polymerization process.

Cell viability assays

In this experimental paradigm, cells were cultured in 96-well plates under tightly controlled conditions at 37 °C. Following cultivation, the cells were subjected to nanoparticle exposure for a duration of 24 h at predetermined doses. This critical incubation period facilitated the exploration of potential effects on cellular viability resulting from the interaction between cells and nanoparticles. The assessment of cellular viability post-exposure was conducted using a trypan blue-based assay, a widely accepted method for evaluating cell membrane integrity and overall viability. To discern between live and dead cells accurately, a vital step involved the addition of 0.2 % trypan blue to the cell culture. Subsequently, the cells were transferred to a disposable plastic counting chamber, enabling precise enumeration of viable and non-viable cells. The enumeration process was executed meticulously utilizing a Cellometer Automatic Cell Counter (Nexcelom Bioscience), an advanced instrument designed to optimize cell counting procedures while ensuring reproducibility and accuracy. Cell viability remains relatively high at lower concentrations, suggesting minimal cytotoxicity. However, as the concentration increases, viability declines, with a significant reduction observed at 1e-5 M and 1e-4 M, dropping to around 73 % and 62 %, respectively (Fig. 8). These results emphasize the concentration-dependent cytotoxicity of the nanoparticles, with higher doses leading to a greater decrease in cell viability. Therefore, optimizing the nanoparticle dosage is essential to ensure biocompatibility while maximizing therapeutic efficacy.

Quantitative PCR for viral DNA analysis

In this experimental setup, peripheral blood mononuclear cells were treated with nanoparticles after HIV-1 Luc infection for six hours. Subsequently, a substantial cell population of 4 × 106 cells underwent DNA extraction. The extraction process employed a lysis solution containing 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8), 10 mM EDTA, 5 mM NaCl, and 200 µg/mL proteinase K. Following cell lysis for 20 to 30 min at room temperature, the mixture was heated to 95 °C for five minutes to deactivate the proteinase K. Amplification of 100 ng of the extracted DNA was carried out using SYBR Green Master mix in a Roche Light Cycler 480 II device for subsequent real-time PCR analysis. The PCR process initiated with a five-minute preincubation step at 45 °C, followed by a three-minute step at 95 °C. Subsequently, 45 cycles were executed, involving 15 s of denaturation at 95 °C, 45 s of annealing and extension at 60 °C, and a final extension step at 72 °C for 10 s. For measuring an early LTR (long terminal repeat), β-globin DNA served as a normalization standard. Specific primer sequences were employed for amplification, such as GGCTAACTAGGGAACCCACTG for early LTR and CAACCTCAAACAGACACCATGG for β-globin. Relative expression levels were calculated using the ΔΔCT technique once mean Cp values for early LTR and β-globin were determined. In subsequent analysis, statistical significance was assessed using an unpaired t-test (Fig. 9). Experimental data encompassed various treatment conditions, including Untreated Control, Plain DTG, Blank NP, and DTG-DIP-NP, each offering quantitative estimates of gene expression under distinct experimental conditions. These values, ranging from 0.08 to 0.07, elucidate the impact of nanoparticle treatments on HIV-1 infection dynamics, underscoring the significance of precise analytical techniques and rigorous experimental design in identifying promising treatment modalities and elucidating underlying mechanisms of action.

Caspase-3 enzyme assay

In the assay assessing caspase-3 enzyme activity, three distinct samples were employed: Plain DTG, DTG-NP, DTG-DIP-NP integrated into an in situ gel (ISG). Human cells underwent meticulous cultivation until achieving confluency, indicating complete coverage of the culture surface, at 37 °C in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum. Employing a specialized cell extraction buffer, cell lysis was executed, yielding a lysate subsequently diluted with standard buffer to ensure optimal conditions for further experimentation. Post-dilution, cells were seeded at a density ranging from 1.3 to 1.9 × 109 cells per well in a 96-well plate, with 100 µL of DMEM (Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium) added to each well. Following a 24-hour incubation period to allow for cellular interactions and responses to the varied treatments, the samples underwent the caspase assay on a pre-treated 96-well plate. Quantification of human active caspase-3 levels was performed by USCN Life Science Inc. in Wuhan, China, utilizing spectrophotometric analysis at a wavelength of 450 nm in strict adherence to the manufacturer's protocol (Fig. 10). The dataset comprises values corresponding to multiple treatments, including Control, Plain DTG, DTG-NP, and DTG-DIP-NP, represented by numerical values such as 96 ± 8 pg/ml, 241 ± 27 pg/ml, 198 ± 8 pg/ml and 112 ± 12 pg/ml. These numerical representations serve to quantify caspase-3 activity across varied experimental conditions. This research elucidates the impacts of simvastatin and nanoparticle treatments on caspase-3 activity in human cells, facilitated by meticulous experimental design and precise analytical methodologies. These findings provide valuable insights into potential therapeutic strategies targeting apoptosis regulation.

Quantitative uptake Caco-2 cell using HPLC

Initially, cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10 × 5 cells per cm2 in a 12-well plate for quantitative analysis. Once reaching approximately 80 % confluency, they were washed with pH 7.4 Hank's Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) and allowed to equilibrate for an hour. Subsequently, exposure to optimized DTG-DIP-NP and suspensions of DTG solution at dosages of 10, 100, and 200 mg/ml occurred. Following a three-hour incubation period, the sample plates were rinsed with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at pH 7.4 to ensure the elimination of any residual particles. The DTG solution and optimized DTG-DIP-NP suspension were then aspirated. To effectively permeabilize the cell membrane, 0.5 % Triton X-100 was added for thirty minutes. The samples were subsequently centrifuged for 10 min at 14,000 rpm after adding 250 µl of the mobile phase to extract the supernatant. Analysis of this supernatant enabled investigation into cellular responses and interactions with the delivered Dolutegravir formulations (Fig. 11). Simultaneously, numerical datasets describing the reactions under various experimental conditions were collected. The values for the Plain DTG and DTG-DIP-NP treatments were meticulously recorded, providing quantitative results of the doses administered at different intervals. These values, ranging from 2 to 84 for Plain DTG and 0.2 to 16 for DTG-DIP-NP, serve as crucial quantitative measures of cellular responses to Dolutegravir formulations. This study offers vital insights into cellular uptake and responses to DTG and DTG-DIP-NP, crucial for advancing our understanding of drug delivery systems and therapeutic efficacy. It accomplishes this by integrating rigorous experimental methodologies with precise analytical techniques.

Transport study

In this experimental setup, cells were seeded at a density of 10 × 5 cells per cm2 into 6-well transwell plates, each with a surface area of 4.7 cm2. These seeded cells were allowed to grow into monolayers over a period of 21 to 30 days, crucial for ensuring the formation of robust cellular barriers that closely mimic physiological conditions. Daily media changes were performed during the culture phase to uphold optimal cell viability and health, crucial for precise experimental outcomes. To prepare the cellular monolayers for testing, Hank's Balanced Salt Solution replaced the growth media before commencing the transport research. Visual verification of monolayer integrity using an optical lens confirmed the formation of a tight and impermeable barrier. The assessment of monolayer tightness was further validated using Lucifer yellow (LY), a paracellular leakage indicator. For transport experiments, monolayers were deemed sufficiently tight when the permeability coefficient for LY was below 0.5 × 10-6 cm/s, indicating minimal paracellular leakage. In transport tests, the AP side of the transwell inserts was exposed to DTG solutions at different concentrations (10, 100, and 200 μg/ml) in HBSS or optimized DTG-DIP-NP formulations (Fig. 12). To mimic physiological conditions, transfer buffer was supplied to the BL side. To maintain experimental equilibrium, aliquots were withdrawn from the BL side at specified intervals (30, 60, 120, and 180 min) post-incubation and replaced with an equivalent volume of transport buffer. At all tested concentrations, DTG-DIP-NP exhibits significantly higher Papp values than plain DTG, demonstrating enhanced permeability when DTG is encapsulated in DIP nanoparticles. This effect is particularly pronounced at higher concentrations, where DTG-DIP-NP shows a marked increase in permeability compared to plain DTG. These results indicate that the nanoparticle formulation improves drug absorption, which may lead to increased bioavailability. The enhanced permeability is likely due to the nanoscale size and surface properties of the DIP-NP, facilitating more efficient transport across biological membranes. This underscores the potential of DTG-DIP-NP as a delivery system for boosting the therapeutic efficacy of DTG.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study successfully synthesized and optimized mPEG-PS diblock copolymer-based SLNs using ultrasonication for targeted drug delivery against HIV-1. The methodology involved a systematic approach encompassing synthesis, characterization, and optimization of the nanoparticles, allowing for precise control over their formulation. The optimization process utilized RSM to explore the effects of key formulation factors, including the concentration of surfactants (X1) set at 50 g, 62.5 g, and 75 g; ultrasonication amplitude (X2) at 50 %, 75 %, and 100 %; and ultrasonication time (X3) at 2, 3.5, and 5 min. By applying RSM, established a comprehensive model to identify the optimal conditions for achieving desirable physicochemical properties, such as PS and EE, with the goal of maximizing EE while maintaining a minimum particle size. Experimental results from cell viability assays, quantitative PCR for viral DNA analysis, caspase-3 enzyme assays, and quantitative uptake studies using HPLC provided valuable insights into the therapeutic efficacy and biocompatibility of the synthesized nanoparticles. These findings underscore the potential of mPP-DIP-SLNs as effective drug delivery systems for Antiretroviral treatment. Overall, by integrating advanced experimental techniques and analytical methodologies, this research significantly contributes to the refinement and advancement of drug delivery strategies, offering promise for improved therapeutic outcomes in HIV treatment and beyond.
