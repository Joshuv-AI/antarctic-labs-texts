Comparing Fixation Techniques in Metacarpal Fractures: Intramedullary Screw Versus Open Reduction Internal Fixation With Plate and Screw Construct
Authors: Kathleen F. Lundquist, Zachary N. Jodoin, Casey D. McDonald, Jonathan Bethiel, Ryan Rose
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose:The purpose of this study is to compare intramedullary headless screw (IMN) versus plate and screw fixation (open reduction internal fixation [ORIF]) in metacarpal (MC) fractures, specifically looking at implant cost, operating room time, outcomes, and complications. We hypothesize that IMN will provide at least equivalent outcomes and complication rates compared with ORIF while simultaneously decreasing operating room time and lowering overall costs.Methods:Patients with MC fractures treated surgically at a single, level-one, trauma center between January 2018 and December 2022 were identified. Eighty-five patients and 108 fractures were included. Records were retrospectively reviewed for demographic information, injury data, surgical details, and follow-up information.Results:The average age of included patients was 32.4 years. The leading causes of injury included altercations, falls, and motor vehicle collisions. Tourniquet time was an average of 79.9 minutes in the ORIF group and 37.6 minutes in the IMN group. Average cost of the implant and time to union were similar in both the groups. IMN fixation had faster time to motion and time to full range of motion. Complications, including revision surgery rates, wound infections, implant complications, and non/mal unions, were collected. Nine patients underwent revision surgery, eight in the ORIF group and one in the IMN group. Two superficial wound infections were noted in the ORIF group. One implant complication was observed in the ORIF group, as defined by implant breakage after the patient punched a wall. Three implant removals were noted, all in the ORIF group.Conclusion:In our experience, the use of an intramedullary screw for fixation of MC fractures is a reliable and safe method of fixation. The use of this procedure provides patients with quicker return to motion, faster time to full range of motion, and similar time to union with fewer complications. Furthermore, it resulted in decreased tourniquet time resulting in decreased overall cost.Level of Evidence:Retrospective review, Level III Therapeutic.

Metacarpal fractures are among the most common fractures encountered within the United States healthcare system, accounting for almost 13% of all upper extremity fractures and 33% of all hand fractures.1,2 Despite the high incidence, when metacarpal (MC) fractures are deemed operable, little standardization exists regarding the best fixation method. Many surgical options exist, including Kirschner wire fixation, plate and screw construct, and intramedullary implant.

There is current debate in the literature regarding outcomes and complications of various fixation options, but little data exist on the use of intramedullary headless screws (IMNs) for MC fracture fixation. From a biomechanical standpoint, studies have shown that intramedullary fixation has suitable load-to-failure strength when compared with Kirschner wires and plating.3-6 A handful of studies, including a large 837 patient meta-analysis, have also provided evidence that headless screw fixation can provide excellent clinical outcomes with decreased surgical times, but to our knowledge, no study has directly compared plate and screw construct versus intramedullary fixation in the clinical setting.7-11

Intramedullary screw fixation has multiple theoretical advantages, including decreased surgical times, minimal soft-tissue disruption, lower overall cost when compared with plate fixation, and avoidance of prolonged postoperative immobilization. The purpose of this study is to compare IMN (Figure 1) versus plate and screw fixation (open reduction internal fixation [ORIF]; Figure 2) in MC fractures, specifically looking at implant cost, operating room (OR) time, outcomes, and complications. We believe that intramedullary screws will provide at least equivalent clinical outcomes and complication rates while simultaneously decreasing OR time and lowering overall costs.

Methods

In this institutional review board–approved study, all patients with MC fractures treated surgically by one of four hand surgeons, presenting to a single, level-one, trauma center between January 2018 and December 2022 were identified.

Initially, 466 fractures were identified. Patients were excluded if they were treated nonsurgically, underwent Kirschner wire fixation, or had a combination of Kirschner wire fixation with ORIF. Patients were also excluded if incomplete data or follow-up information was available, or if an open fracture required vascular or nerve repair. After excluding these patients, a total of 85 patients and 108 fractures were included in the study.

Patient records were retrospectively reviewed and data collected included standard demographic information (age, sex, hand dominance, medical comorbidities), injury data (date of injury, mechanism of injury, involved MC, fracture location, polytrauma status, fracture pattern), treatment information (surgery performed: headless intramedullary screw versus plate and screw construct, time to surgery), surgical details (estimated blood loss [EBL]), tourniquet time, procedure time, cost of implant), and follow-up information (time to union, time to motion, revision surgery rate, complications). Tourniquet usage protocol was standardized among hand surgeon staff. The tourniquet was elevated after marking skin and before the initial incision was made. The tourniquet was then let down at the conclusion of final imaging and before closing to allow for appropriate hemostasis. Postoperative dressing and splint immobilization was monitored in both the surgical report instructions and postoperative clinic notes to assess true patient time to motion.

Statistical analysis was done. Categorical variables were analyzed using a chi-squared test and calculating expected values in a standard fashion. Continuous variables were analyzed using a Student T-test or Fischer exact test. A P value of 0.05 was used to denote statistical significance.

Results

A total of 85 patients and 108 fractures were included in this study. Only patients who underwent IMN or ORIF fixation were included. The average age of all included patients was 31 years old, 31.9 years in the ORIF group and 29.8 years in the IMN group (P = 0.1); 79% of patients were male, 84% in the ORIF group and 73% in the IMN group (P = 0.23). Three total patients had diabetes, two in the ORIF group (4%) and one in the IMN group (3%; P = 0.73); five patients had hypertension, four in the ORIF group (8%) and one in the IMN group (3%; P = 0.28). Overall, 20 were current smokers, 15 in the ORIF group (31%) and five in the IMN group (14%; P = 0.06). Twelve polytrauma patients were there in the IMN group and 15 polytrauma patients in the ORIF group (P = 0.86; Table 1). No notable differences were noted between the two groups in terms of fracture pattern (P = 0.16). The mechanism of injury was similar in both groups with the leading causes of injury being altercations/boxers, motor vehicle collisions, and falls.

An average of 130 days of follow-up was found in the ORIF group and 70 days in the IMN group. Average time to motion was 18.6 days in the ORIF group and 12 days in the IMN group (P = 0.0016). Time to union was similar, being 42 days in the ORIF group and 48 days in the IMN group (P = 0.26). Discussion

Many surgical options to treat MC fractures exist, including Kirschner wire fixation, plate and screw construct, and intramedullary implant, but there is not a consensus in the literature on the best fixation method. In a meta-analysis by Beck et al in 2019, nine articles and a total of 169 MC fractures were reviewed. It was demonstrated that intramedullary fixation of MC fractures using headless compression screws appears to be a safe and successful treatment option.12 To our knowledge, however, there is no study directly comparing intramedullary screw fixation versus plate and screw construct, specifically, a comparison of implant cost, OR time, and outcomes. By understanding both the clinical outcomes and the financial implications of MC fracture fixation, one can provide both the standard of care to patients, while simultaneously serving as a responsible steward of limited healthcare resources.

The primary postoperative outcomes for this study were centered around ROM and time to radiographic fracture union (Table 4). Intramedullary nail fixation did have multiple advantages over plate fixation, including faster time to motion and faster time to full ROM. Time to motion, as determined by when the patient was first allowed to range digits postoperatively, was on average 1 week sooner in the IMN group, which was statistically significant. Multiple reasons for this can be theorized, but it is more than likely that surgeon and patient comfort with earlier ROM was favored in the IMN group due to the substantially less soft-tissue dissection. Extensive soft-tissue stripping is required for ORIF, which may lead to increased pain and greater potential for wound complication or dehiscence, both of which may contribute to a more conservative approach to beginning ROM. Unfortunately, despite the notable findings, this postoperative motion is based on surgeon preference and patient comfort, which makes it difficult to draw dependable conclusions from these data. In addition, the IMN group had a markedly faster return to full ROM when compared with the plate fixation group at 46 and 124 days, respectively. These time to motion outcomes are reflective of recent orthopedic trauma literature focused on early weight-bearing and motion based on robustness of fixation constructs, as well as surgeon and patient comfort.13,14 Time to union was similar between IMN and ORIF fixation, at 48 and 42 days, respectively. Ultimately, those treated with IMN were allowed to begin working on ROM sooner, resulting in a quicker return to full ROM with no change in time to union.

The most common complication in this study was revision surgery with a total of nine patients or 10.6% undergoing revision surgery (8 ORIF and 1 IMN). Three of the eight patients undergoing revision surgery in the ORIF group were implant removals, one for broken implant after additional trauma. This was a statistically significant difference in revision surgery rate for the ORIF group versus IMN group (P = 0.041). The remaining complications, including wound infection, implant complication, and nonunion or malunion, were all higher in the ORIF group, although not statistically significant. Overall, decreased complications were noted in the IMN group. As it has been noted previously in the literature, percutaneous intramedullary techniques are thought to provide minimally invasive advantages of not stripping periosteum and minimal devascularization while avoiding the main complications of open procedures.15 Furthermore, the prior theoretical disadvantages of intramedullary screw fixation, including MC head articular damage and extensor tendon injury, have been disproved.12,15,16 Figure 3 depicts a cadaver dissection demonstrating the small amount of cartilage on the dorsal third of the MC nail that is disrupted by IMN placement. This area of the MC head is only engaged during hyperextension and is well tolerated by patients postoperatively.

In our study, no notable difference was found in the average cost of the implant for the IMN versus the plate and screw construct. Where we found a notable difference, however, is in OR time. To negate the inclusion of polytrauma patients, which would artificially inflate overall OR time, we used tourniquet time as a surrogate for procedure length. A statistically significant difference was found in tourniquet time with an average of 79.9 minutes in the ORIF group and 37.6 minutes in the IMN group (P = 0.0005), with a difference of 42.3 minutes. According to Moody et al,17 total cost per minute to run an orthopedic trauma OR was $16.21, which did not include professional fees of the anesthesiologist or surgeon. This would equate to an average of $685.68 of OR time cost savings with the use the intramedullary device. Even with no notable difference in implant cost, the markedly less OR time spent in the IMN group accounts for a large cost savings.

The primary limitations of this study include its retrospective nature, selection bias, inclusion of polytrauma patients, and short-term patient follow-up data. In terms of fixation choice, this was left to surgeon preference with no standardization. Postoperative return to motion was also surgeon specified, which can introduce bias. Attempts were made to accurately record true patient time to motion, however, including reviewing postoperative splint instructions, as well as postoperative clinic appointment ROM data. It is well documented that polytrauma victims have reduced quality of life and poorer outcomes when compared with nonpolytrauma patients.18,19 Each of our groups had almost identical polytrauma percentages, but, theoretically, varying types and severity of injuries can bias the overall outcomes. In addition, although not statistically significant, a higher rate of smokers was found in the ORIF group, which could increase complication rate. In future studies, longer-term follow-up including patient-reported outcomes could be prioritized. Finally, a post hoc statistical power analysis was not done for this study due to its retrospective nature. Every patient who met the inclusion criteria was included in the study to maximize the n-value, however, and this article is the first one directly comparing ORIF and IMN fixation. Overall, despite the limitations of this study, we believe that it depicts a unique and fair comparison of the two fixation techniques for the treatment of MC fractures.

In our experience, the use of an intramedullary screw for fixation of MC fractures is a reliable and safe method of fixation. The use of this procedure provides patients with quicker return to motion, faster time to full ROM, and similar time to union with fewer complications. Furthermore, it resulted in decreased tourniquet time, resulting in decreased associated cost. Additional long-term follow-up studies will be beneficial in the future, but based on our short-term data, we are optimistic that intramedullary screw fixation is a reliable and safe method of fixation for MC fractures.
