ICD-Coded Versus Undiagnosed Acute Kidney Injury in the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) Study
Authors: Grace G von Oiste, Aditya Surapaneni, Carina M Flaherty, Insa M Schmidt, Kunihiro Matsushita, Josef Coresh, Wan-Jin Yeo, Morgan E Grams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRationale & ObjectiveAcute kidney injury (AKI) is common among older adults and associated with adverse outcomes, yet it often goes undiagnosed. Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a common and costly condition, with studies estimating that 1 in 5 hospitalizations in individuals aged ≥65 years are complicated by AKI.1 AKI is defined clinically by the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria as an abrupt decline in kidney function, characterized by an increase in serum creatinine (Scr) by ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours, a ≥1.5-fold increase from baseline within 7 days, or a urinary output of <0.5 mL/kg/h for >6 hours.2 Older adults are uniquely susceptible to AKI due to many factors, including an increased presence of comorbid conditions and greater use of medications that affect kidney perfusion.3, 4, 5 AKI is associated with adverse outcomes such as increased morbidity, progression to chronic kidney disease, and prolonged hospitalizations, as well as increased short-term and long-term mortality.6,7 The association between AKI and adverse outcomes underscores the need for targeted efforts to mitigate AKI, including the early identification of individuals at risk. However, AKI often goes unrecognized in health care settings,8 limiting opportunities for identification, prevention, and treatment.

International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes are commonly used to identify AKI in epidemiologic studies. However, previous studies have found that ICD code-identified AKI poorly captures AKI diagnoses compared with laboratory-based AKI diagnosed by KDIGO criteria, suggesting that a large number of AKI cases may go undiagnosed.9 Although ICD-diagnosed AKI is known to be associated with adverse outcomes, less is known about the risk profile and molecular characteristics of individuals who meet the laboratory-based KDIGO criteria for AKI but are not formally diagnosed. It remains unclear whether undiagnosed AKI reflects a distinct clinical phenotype or simply limitations in clinical recognition. Characterizing these cases and their implications may help inform strategies to improve AKI detection and clinical awareness.

To address these gaps in evidence, we leveraged data from the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study, a prospective, community-based cohort study of individuals across 4 centers in the United States.10 This study aimed to quantify the extent of undiagnosed AKI, evaluate whether phenotypes based on clinical and molecular risk factors differed between diagnosed and undiagnosed AKI, and compare the prognosis of diagnosed and undiagnosed AKI with respect to adverse outcomes.

Methods

Study Population

The ARIC study is a prospective cohort study designed to investigate atherosclerosis and its clinical consequences.10 It includes data from 15,792 individuals who were aged 45-64 years at baseline and were enrolled from Washington County, Maryland; Forsyth County, North Carolina; Jackson, Mississippi; and Minneapolis, Minnesota. Follow-up exams were performed every 3 years until visit 4 and subsequent visits at varying intervals, with each consisting of clinical examination, specimen collection, and questionnaires. Because of the detailed abstraction of creatinine in hospitalizations after visit 5, we considered this visit as the baseline. The study population was thus derived from 6,538 participants who attended ARIC visit 5, excluding those who withdrew from follow-up (n < 11), those with missing baseline creatinine (n = 94), those with missing urinary albumin-creatine ratio (UACR) (n = 717), and those with kidney failure at visit 5 (n = 25). After these exclusions, 5,692 patients were included in the analyses. Approval of this study was covered under the New York University Langone Health Institutional Review Board site approval for ARIC (i23-01344). Informed consent was obtained from all participants in the ARIC study.

Assessing AKI

Among the 13,183 hospitalizations after visit 5, 11,316 had data for the first, last, and highest Scr values. A laboratory-based AKI was defined as an increase of ≥1.5 times the baseline Scr during a single hospitalization using these 3 measures to establish a gold standard based on the KDIGO definition. We further classified AKI into 3 stages based on the magnitude of Scr increase: stage 1 was defined as a ≥1.5 to <2.0-fold increase from baseline, stage 2 as a ≥2.0 to <3.0-fold increase, and stage 3 as a ≥3-fold increase. We used the lowest Scr value during hospitalizations as the baseline. In sensitivity analyses, we also evaluated a definition of baseline creatinine that used the lowest Scr value during a previous, non-AKI hospitalization or antecedent study visit, whichever was more proximal. ‘ICD-coded AKI’ was defined as the presence of one of the following ICD codes during a hospitalization, irrespective of laboratory measures: ICD-9 584.5-584.9 and ICD-10 N17.0-N17.9. ‘Undiagnosed AKI’ was defined as laboratory-based AKI without a corresponding ICD code.

Protein Measurements

The relative concentration of plasma proteins in blood collected at ARIC visit 5 was measured using a multiplexed slow off-rate modified aptamer (SOMAmer)-based capture array.11 This technique uses single-stranded DNA aptamers and protein-binding reagents with defined 3-dimensional structures and unique nucleotide sequences that can be identified using DNA detection technology.12, 13, 14, 15 Plasma samples were collected at visit 5 using a standardized protocol and frozen at −80 °C. Samples were then shipped on dry ice to the ARIC central laboratory and continuously frozen until being sent to SomaLogic for quantification and quality control. Of 5,327 ARIC visit 5 samples, 15 were excluded for failing quality control.12 The median interassay coefficient of variation was 4.7% on the ∼4% blind duplicate samples (visit 5), with a corresponding split-sample reliability coefficient of 0.94. SOMAmers with poor reproducibility (coefficient of variation > 50%) or low variance were excluded, along with those binding to nonprotein targets or contaminants. After quality control, 4,877 SOMAmers targeting 4,697 unique proteins or complexes were retained for analysis. We applied a log2 transformation to SOMAmer measurements to address skewness. Outliers (>5 standard deviations from the log2 mean) were winsorized.

Metabolite Measurements

Serum samples from ARIC visit 5, stored at −80 °C, underwent untargeted metabolic profiling using 4 methods of ultra-high performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry at Metabolon, Inc. In total, 1,464 serum metabolites were quantified through comparison to a library reference of purified standards, with peaks quantified using the area under the curve, and pathway assignment to the following major pathways: amino acids, carbohydrates, lipids, nucleotides, energy, peptides, cofactors and vitamins, xenobiotics, partially characterized molecules, and unknowns.16 Unknown metabolites were excluded from the analysis. Metabolite intensities were batch-normalized according to Metabolon guidelines to account for run-day variation. For endogenous metabolites, missing values were replaced using the lowest observed value. Metabolites with variance ≤0.01 were removed, as were endogenous metabolites missing in >80% of the samples. Outliers in the remaining metabolites were winsorized at 5 standard deviations. The final dataset included 945 metabolites. All metabolites were then median-scaled to 1 and log2-transformed to reduce skewness.

Covariate Selection

Demographic covariates included self-reported age, sex, and race–center combinations (eg, Forsyth and White). Clinical covariates included systolic blood pressure, determined as the average of the second and third blood pressure measurements taken while seated after 5 minutes rest. Use of antihypertensive medications was determined through self-report of medications taken in the 4 weeks before the study visit and confirmed through direct inspection at the study visit. Smoking status was self-reported and categorized as either current, former, or never smoker. Diabetes was defined by a fasting glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, nonfasting glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL, use of diabetes medications, or reported physician diagnosis. Prevalent cardiovascular conditions included coronary heart disease (CHD), stroke, and heart failure (HF). Kidney function was assessed using estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) based on creatinine, calculated with the race-free 2021 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation.17 UACR was derived from random spot urine samples.

Outcomes

Outcomes of interest after visit 5 included CHD, HF, stroke, and all-cause mortality and were adjudicated by a physician panel. Follow-up was considered until the first occurrence of the event of interest, death, or administrative censoring on December 31, 2020. CHD was defined as definite fatal cases or confirmed myocardial infarctions.18 HF was defined as hospitalizations and deaths with the ICD Ninth Revision code 428 or Tenth Revision code 150 with additional 2-physician adjudication of HF events.19,20 Definite and probable stroke diagnoses were made through physician evaluation according to National Survey of Stroke guidelines.21 All-cause mortality was tracked through follow-up with next of kin, hospital documentation, public obituaries, death certificates, and linkage with state death registries and the National Death Index.

Statistical Analysis

To evaluate the severity of ICD-coded and undiagnosed AKI, we used all 11,316 hospitalizations after ARIC visit 5 that had Scr measurements. We calculated the sensitivity and specificity of ICD-coded AKI for each stage using our laboratory-based AKI criteria as the gold standard. We evaluated the distribution of fold change in creatinine during each hospitalization by ICD-coded versus undiagnosed AKI status.

Among the 5,692 participants who were included in the present study, we assessed whether baseline demographic and clinical characteristics differed between participants who experienced undiagnosed or ICD-coded AKI using t- and χ2 tests, classifying participants based on their first occurrence of either ICD-coded or undiagnosed AKI. We summarized characteristics using means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges for normally distributed and skewed variables, respectively, or percentages for binary variables.

We also evaluated whether baseline omics biomarkers (4,877 proteins and 945 metabolites measured at ARIC visit 5) differed between participants who experienced undiagnosed or ICD-coded AKI. For each protein or metabolite, we used a Cox proportional hazards model for each AKI outcome, and significance in results were assessed with a Bonferroni-corrected P value threshold to account for multiple comparisons. The models were adjusted for demographics, use of antihypertensive medications, systolic blood pressure, diabetes, smoking status, creatinine-based eGFR, and log-transformed UACR. We compared the hazards ratios (HRs) between undiagnosed and ICD-coded AKI for each protein or metabolite in a scatter plot. Each protein or metabolite was scaled to its respective standard deviation to achieve comparability of effect sizes.

To assess whether the prognosis associated with AKI differed based on undiagnosed or ICD-coded status, we used multivariate Cox proportional hazards models for the adverse outcomes of CHD, HF, stroke, and all-cause mortality. For each adverse outcome, we used a model with 2 time-varying exposures: ICD-coded AKI and undiagnosed AKI. Follow-up started with visit 5, and AKI status was updated as it occurred. For all Cox proportional hazards analyses, follow-up stopped with the occurrence of an adverse outcome, death, or administrative censoring (December 31, 2020).

In sensitivity analyses, we evaluated the concordance of our primary definition of AKI and AKI defined using a baseline creatinine abstracted from a previous study visit or non-AKI hospitalization. All analyses were performed using Stata version 18.0.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Among the 5,692 participants, the mean age was 76.3 (±5.2 SD) years and 56.6% were women (Table 1). Of the participants, 5.9% were current smokers and 52.7% were former smokers. The prevalence of cardiovascular disease was 32.7%. The mean creatinine-based eGFR was 71.0 mL/min/1.73 m2 (±17.4 SD) and the median UACR was 10.9 mg/g (interquartile range, 6.4-23.8).

Frequency and Severity of Undiagnosed AKI

Laboratory-based AKI occurred in 17.1% of the 11,316 hospitalizations with available Scr measurements (n = 1,937) (Table 2). The distribution of days between highest and lowest abstracted creatinine are shown in Fig S1. For detection of stage 1 or higher AKI based on fold change in Scr, ICD-coded diagnoses demonstrated a sensitivity of 51.4% and specificity of 87.0%. For detection of stage 2 or higher AKI, ICD-coded diagnoses demonstrated a sensitivity of 70.2%; and for stage 3 or higher AKI, sensitivity was 86.3%. Of the ICD-coded AKI, 45.0% did not meet laboratory-based criteria, although the latter was based only on creatinine measures during a hospitalization. The distribution of AKI severity was skewed toward slightly higher fold change in creatinine in the ICD-coded AKI group compared with the undiagnosed AKI group (Fig 1). In sensitivity analyses using a laboratory-based definition with prehospitalization creatinine as a baseline, there were 2,676 AKI hospitalizations. The sensitivity and specificity of ICD-coded AKI were 52.5% and 90.7%, respectively, and 36.4% of ICD-coded AKI did not meet laboratory-based criteria (Fig S2). The overlap between the AKI definitions is shown in Fig S3.

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics of ICD-coded AKI Versus Undiagnosed AKI

Demographic and clinical characteristics were generally similar between participants with ICD-code AKI and undiagnosed AKI. Participants with AKI were older, had higher prevalences of hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease, and had lower mean creatinine-based eGFR and higher UACR compared with participants without AKI. Smoking status and other clinical factors such as systolic blood pressure did not significantly differ between the ICD-coded and undiagnosed AKI groups (P > 0.05). Notably, the proportion of women was significantly higher in the undiagnosed AKI group than in the ICD-coded AKI group (70.6% vs 46.2%; P < 0.001), and the baseline eGFR was significantly higher in the undiagnosed AKI group (72 vs 61 mL/min/1.73 m2; P < 0.001).

Proteomic and Metabolomic Associations with ICD-coded Versus Undiagnosed AKI

The HR associations with metabolomic and proteomic data were similar between ICD-coded and undiagnosed AKI. As shown in Fig 2A, the HRs for metabolites demonstrated concordance across the 2 AKI types (r = 0.72, P < 0.001). Notably, higher levels of N2,N2-dimethylguanoise were positively associated with both ICD-coded AKI (HR per SD, 1.49; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.34-1.66) and undiagnosed AKI (HR, 1.36; 95% CI, 1.16-1.59), as were erythronate (a potential marker of oxidative stress) and C-glycosyltryptophan (a filtration marker) (Table S1). In contrast, ergothioneine, a diet-derived antioxidant, and specific androgenic steroids were protective against both diagnosed and undiagnosed AKI. Proteomic associations also showed concordance between ICD-coded and undiagnosed AKI outcomes (r = 0.60, P < 0.001; Fig 2B). Proteins such as growth differentiation factor 15 (a stress-responsive cytokine belonging to the transforming growth factor-β superfamily) were positively associated with both ICD-coded and undiagnosed AKI, as were β2-microglobulin (a filtration marker) and N-terminal pro–brain natriuretic peptide (a marker of cardiac stretch). Epidermal growth factor receptor (also known as erythroblastic oncogene B1, a transmembrane receptor tyrosine kinase) and anthrax toxin receptor 2 (a renally expressed cellular receptor) were both negatively associated with ICD-coded AKI and undiagnosed AKI (Table S2).

Prognosis of ICD-Coded AKI Versus Undiagnosed AKI

Over a mean follow-up of 7.4 years, there were 272 cases of CHD, 211 cases of stroke, 625 cases of HF, and 1,748 deaths. ICD-coded AKI and undiagnosed AKI had similar associations with CHD, stroke, all-cause mortality, and HF (Fig 3). Both ICD-coded and undiagnosed AKI were significantly associated with increased risk of CHD with HRs of 2.60 (95% CI, 1.75-3.86) and 2.05 (95% CI, 1.16-3.63), respectively. ICD-coded and undiagnosed AKI were also significantly associated with risk of all-cause mortality with HRs of 6.37 (95% CI, 5.64-7.21) and 4.94 (95% CI, 4.16-5.87), respectively. Finally, ICD-coded and undiagnosed AKI were significantly associated with risk of HF with HRs of 2.50 (95% CI, 1.95-3.21) and 2.50 (95% CI, 1.76-3.56), respectively. Neither was significantly associated with stroke.

Discussion

In this large community-based study of 5,692 older adults, we identified that a substantial proportion of AKI events were undiagnosed. ICD codes detected only a portion of laboratory-defined AKI, although sensitivity improved for more severe AKI (from 51% to 86% for AKI stage 1+ to 3+). Participants with undiagnosed AKI shared similar demographic and clinical characteristics with those with ICD-coded AKI, with the notable exception of female predominance and higher eGFR. Metabolomic and proteomic profiles were also highly concordant between ICD-coded and undiagnosed AKI, suggesting shared underlying biological profiles between the 2 groups. Additionally, we found that during long-term follow-up, both ICD-coded and undiagnosed AKI were associated with a similar risk of adverse outcomes, including CHR, HF, and all-cause mortality. These findings suggest that undiagnosed AKI is clinically significant and is similar to diagnosed AKI in terms of its characteristics and severity. Thus, improving identification of AKI in hospitalized patients is critical.

Our findings build upon prior validation studies that report low sensitivity of ICD codes for identifying AKI.12,22, 23, 24, 25 Findings from earlier studies in ARIC reported a sensitivity < 20% of billing code-identified AKI compared with that meeting KDIGO criteria using Scr only.9 In contrast, we observed a sensitivity of 51%, with more severe stages of AKI exhibiting higher sensitivities. This difference may reflect our study’s older population, in whom AKI may be more clinically apparent and thus more frequently recognized,26 or evolving hospital billing practices. Additionally, our study builds upon the existing literature by incorporating long-term outcomes and detailed proteomic and metabolomic profiling for both undiagnosed and ICD-coded AKI. By doing so, we provide a comprehensive view that undiagnosed AKI is biologically and clinically comparable to ICD-coded AKI, a comparison that has not previously been evaluated with respect to adverse outcomes and characterized at a molecular level.

Undiagnosed AKI was more common among women than men, suggesting a sex-based difference in the recognition and diagnosis of AKI. Similar patterns have been observed in other conditions, such as hypertension. A large, community-based study found that women were significantly more likely to have uncontrolled hypertension than men, despite comparable comorbidity profiles.27 Furthermore, many cardiac and pulmonary conditions, such as CHD and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, have been reported to be undiagnosed in women due to atypical presentations and biases in clinical evaluation.28 Our observation for AKI is unlikely to be due to differences in health-seeking behavior or atypical presentations, since all patients were hospitalized. Instead, it may be explained in part by the fact that women tend to have lower baseline Scr levels on average,29 so the absolute change required to meet an AKI diagnosis may be lower, resulting in missed diagnoses. Indeed, in our cohort, women with AKI had a baseline creatinine of 1.0 mg/dL compared with men with AKI, whose baseline creatinine was 1.3 mg/dL. A 50% increase in the latter would result in a peak Scr of nearly 2.0 mg/dL, which may be more likely to draw clinical attention than a corresponding value of 1.5 mg/dL seen in women. A similar explanation may drive the higher baseline eGFR in the undiagnosed AKI compared with ICD-coded AKI groups. However, certain sex-specific metabolites, such as androgenic steroids, were also protective for both diagnosed and undiagnosed AKI, suggesting biological factors may also play a role.

Metabolomic and proteomic analyses revealed consistent associations between specific metabolites and proteins in both ICD-coded and undiagnosed AKI, reinforcing the similarity between the 2 groups and highlighting their utility as potential biomarkers for AKI. N2,N2-dimethylguanosine, erythronate, and C-glycosyl tryptophan were significantly associated with higher AKI risk in both groups. These findings are consistent with those of prior studies, demonstrating their role in kidney dysfunction and as markers of filtration.16,30 Several proteomic markers that are known to be implicated in inflammation, impaired filtration and HF, such as β2-microglobulin, and N-terminal pro–brain natriuretic peptide, showed positive associations with AKI in our study, in line with prior literature.31, 32, 33 In contrast, epidermal growth factor receptor (erythroblastic oncogene B1) demonstrated a strong inverse association with AKI, suggesting a potential protective effect. Supporting this, prior animal studies have shown that epidermal growth factor receptor activation may facilitate renal recovery after AKI by promoting tubular cell proliferation and survival through the extracellular signal-regulated kinase and Akt (protein kinase B) signaling pathways.34

There are several approaches that might be used to better identify AKI in the hospital setting. Perhaps the most accessible is through electronic health record-based surveillance, in which a real-time system automatically detects AKI using KDIGO criteria. Leveraging continuous queries that compare creatinine with historical baseline values, a surveillance system can identify AKI and then route alerts to the primary team. This has been implemented in some health systems but requires a careful approach that couples identification with suggested action and balances the utility35, 36, 37, 38 of the alert with the risk of ‘alert fatigue.’ Other approaches to improving AKI diagnosis include improved urinary output monitoring, either through automated integration of documentation into electronic health record algorithms or via digitized urinary meters. More lofty ideas include embedding AKI risk prediction algorithms into the medical record and testing emerging kidney injury biomarkers in those deemed high-risk, but the latter markers are generally not available in clinical laboratories.

Our study has some limitations. AKI was defined using an approximation of the KDIGO criteria based solely on fold change in Scr using 3 values abstracted during hospitalization, without incorporating urinary output and without regard to the timing of creatinine rise. This may have led to an under- or overcount of cases of AKI that met KDIGO criteria, respectively. Additionally, using the lowest in-hospital Scr as a proxy for baseline may have resulted in misclassification, specifically for patients who presented to the hospital already above their true baseline at admission, leading to missed AKI identification.39 Alternatively, our sensitivity analysis using prehospitalization creatinine as baseline may have overestimated AKI, because the baseline creatinine may have been collected many years before hospitalization. We used ICD codes as a proxy for diagnosed AKI; however, it is possible that some cases of AKI were recognized by the treating clinician but not billed and thus were uncaptured in our study. Thus, no method of AKI identification was perfect. However, this study has several strengths, including the use of a large community-based sample and an extended follow-up allowing for longitudinal assessment of major risk factors and the prognosis after AKI occurred. High-quality metabolomic and proteomic profiling technology allowed for detailed biomarker analysis for both ICD-coded and undiagnosed AKI. Sensitivity analyses showed numerical differences, but the overall conclusions remained consistent, suggesting that irrespective of definition, AKI represents a high-risk state.

In summary, we found that a substantial proportion of AKI events were undiagnosed, particularly in women. ICD-coded and undiagnosed AKI shared similar demographic characteristics, clinical profiles, and biological risk factors and had comparable prognoses. These results underscore the need for enhanced recognition of AKI.
