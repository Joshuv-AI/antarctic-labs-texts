Lnc-H19-derived protein shapes the immunosuppressive microenvironment of glioblastoma
Authors: Junju Chen, Yixin Gao, Jian Zhong, Xujia Wu, Zhaojie Leng, Ming Liu, Yesheng Wang, Yuan Wang, Xuesong Yang, Nunu Huang, Feizhe Xiao, Maolei Zhang, Xuesong Liu, Nu Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) is a prominent feature of glioblastoma (GBM), the most lethal primary brain cancer resistant to current immunotherapies. The mechanisms underlying GBM-TME remain to be explored. We report that long non-coding RNA (LncRNA) H19 encodes an immune-related protein called H19-IRP. Functionally separated from H19 RNA, H19-IRP promotes GBM immunosuppression by binding to the CCL2 and Galectin-9 promoters and activating their transcription, thereby recruiting myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), leading to T cell exhaustion and an immunosuppressive GBM-TME. H19-IRP, overexpressed in clinical GBM samples, acts as a tumor-associated antigen (TAA) presented by major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I). A circular RNA vaccine targeting H19-IRP (circH19-vac) triggers a potent cytotoxic T cell response against GBM and inhibits GBM growth. Our results highlight the unrevealed function of H19-IRP in creating immunosuppressive GBM-TME by recruiting MDSCs and TAMs, supporting the idea of targeting H19-IRP with cancer vaccine for GBM treatment.Keywords: GBM, TME, LncRNA, H19, H19-IRP, circular RNA vaccine, immunotherapy

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most common and lethal brain tumor in adults. Because of the limited treatment options, patients outcomes are tragic, with a median survival time of less than 2 years.1 Although currently available immunotherapies have been shown to benefit patients with various cancer types, including those with colorectal cancer, melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, and renal cell carcinoma,2,3,4 satisfactory efficacy and outcomes have not been demonstrated in GBM clinical trials of immunotherapies.5

GBM demonstrates significant resistance to current immunotherapeutic approaches.6 Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy, as the mainstream of current immunotherapy, is designed to invigorate the anti-tumor capabilities of T cells.7 However, the dense presence of immunosuppressive cells within GBM, coupled with a sparse infiltration of T cells, severely restricts the therapeutic potency of ICB.8 Moreover, immune evasion is a hallmark of malignancy including GBM.9 GBM can enhance tumor microenvironment (TME) immune suppression and promote immune escape through epigenetic modifications such as DNA methylation.10 The failure of immunotherapy can be attributed to the highly immune-hostile GBM-TME. The mechanism underlying the immunosuppressive GBM-TME is multifactorial; the contributing factors include the low mutation burden of tumor cells, high infiltration of immunosuppressive myeloid cells, paucity of effector T cells, and lack of effective antigens.1,11,12 The most critical challenge in GBM immunotherapy is how to overcome the highly immunosuppressive TME.1

Multiple cell populations in the TME, including tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), neutrophils, dendritic cells (DCs), and natural killer cells, have been implicated in the modulation of immunosuppression and the promotion of brain cancer progression.13,14,15 For example, studies have shown that bone marrow-derived macrophages tend to exhibit an immunosuppressive phenotype in GBM, which facilitates tumor progression.16 Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) profiles of GBM also revealed that tumor progression coincided with the infiltration of immunosuppressive cells such as TAMs and MDSCs.17 The immunosuppressive TME also impedes treatment efficacy. A lack of infiltration of cytotoxic T cells, memory T cells, TH1 cells, and other lymphocytes in the TME tends to result in poor immunotherapy outcomes.18,19,20 Based on the aforementioned findings, recent studies have attempted to convert the “cold” TME to a “hot” TME as a therapeutic strategy. Examples include targeting Siglec-9 to alter the immunosuppressive phenotype of macrophages to enhance anti-tumor immunity or inhibiting Legumain to reduce the proportion of immunosuppressive TAMs to increase the efficacy of ICB therapy.21,22 However, these strategies are still in the preclinical stage and have uncertain clinical benefits. Therefore, there is an urgent need to find more effective targets that can improve or reverse the immunosuppressive GBM-TME.

Long non-coding RNAs (LncRNAs), covering more loci of the genome than protein-coding genes,23 have been shown to participate in numerous physiological and pathological processes, including immune-related functions such as myeloid cell polarization, T cell activation, and antigen presentation.24,25 Lnc-H19, also known as H19, stands as one of the pioneering LncRNAs to be characterized and has been recognized for its role as an oncogene in various malignancies, including GBM.26 H19 exerts its influence by interacting with mRNAs in a competitive endogenous RNA mechanism, thereby modulating the expression of genes crucial for tumor progression.27 Furthermore, H19 can activate key signaling pathways to promote cell survival.28 This multifaceted function underlines the complexity of H19’s contribution to cancer biology. Notably, recent progress in translatomics supports that certain LncRNAs have coding ability and functions mediated by their encoded products, enriching the functional spectrum of LncRNAs. For example, LINC00673-derived protein RASON (a novel protein that regulates Ras signaling, termed RASON) can activate the kirsten ratsarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) pathway to promote tumor progression, and LOC90024-derived splicing regulatory small protein (SRSP) can induce tumor-specific alternative splicing to promote tumor progression.29,30 Furthermore, differentially expressed LncRNA-derived proteins, such as LncRNA-derived MELOE-1, in tumors can act as tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) or tumor-specific antigens (TSAs) to activate specific T cell responses.31 Due to their critical functions and expression patterns in tumors, these unrevealed LncRNA-derived proteins may be important factors in mediating the immunosuppressive GBM-TME and are expected to serve as promising therapeutic targets for GBM.

Cancer vaccines have attracted increasing attention due to their unique advantages, such as the ability to target intracellular and extracellular antigens and stimulate antigen-specific immune responses.32,33,34 In the past, there was a risk of integrating DNA vaccines and viral vector vaccines into the genome. However, the introduction of RNA vaccines has circumvented this risk and improved safety.35 Compared with mRNA vaccines, circular RNA vaccines are highly stable due to their closed ring structure and longer half-life, which facilitate stable expression.36,37 Circular RNA vaccines have been proven to effectively stimulate the immune response in different diseases. For example, a circular RNA vaccine targeting the cryptic peptide derived from circFAM53B effectively activated anti-tumor immunity.38 The circRNA-RBD vaccine targeting the SARS-CoV-2 receptor-binding domain (RBD) antigen elicits effective neutralizing antibodies and T cell responses, resulting in effective protection.39 Although circular RNA vaccines have promising applications, effective antigenic targets against GBM remain to be explored.

In this study, we reported that Lnc-H19 is capable of encoding the protein product H19-IRP. H19-IRP, but not Lnc-H19, plays a critical role in shaping the immunosuppressive GBM-TME by potentiating the infiltration of MDSCs/TAMs and the exhaustion of T cells by directly activating the transcription of CCL2 and Galectin-9. H19-IRP also serves as a TAA due to its highly specific expression in GBM cells, and a circular RNA vaccine targeting H19-IRP (circH19-vac) stimulates an effective T cell anti-tumor response and constrains GBM progression in vivo. Our findings highlight the prospect of LncRNA-derived proteins as targets for cancer immunotherapy.

Results

H19 is associated with the TME and is highly expressed in GBM

To probe the causes of the immunosuppressive GBM-TME mediated by LncRNAs, we first sought to identify immune-related LncRNAs in clinical GBM samples. We performed single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) to infer 28 types of infiltrating immune cells in each GBM sample from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) datasets to evaluate the TME immunophenotype40,41 (Figure S1A). To identify LncRNAs potentially associated with the immunosuppressive GBM-TME, we analyzed the correlations between LncRNA expression levels and the proportions of various immune cell types based on the ssGSEA results (Figure S1B). To identify the key gene/factor(s) that could drive immunosuppressive TME formation, we selected LncRNAs according to specific criteria as follows. (1) We started with LncRNA that correlated with the infiltration of immune cells with immunosuppressive features (immune-related LncRNA) (|R|>0.2, p < 0.01 as cutoff value). A total of 6,477 and 4,084 immune-related LncRNAs were identified in the TCGA and CGGA datasets, respectively. (2) To narrow the LncRNA candidates, we analyzed LncRNAs whose expression was inversely correlated with TCGA or CGGA-GBM patient survival. Through this step, 282 and 91 LncRNAs were further identified, respectively. Overlapping these two lists revealed 5 LncRNAs, namely, H19, TP73-AS1, RP11-568N6-1, RP11-219G17-4, and CTC-467M3-3. (3) Finally, we determined the levels of these LncRNAs that were differentially expressed in tumor tissues (from the TCGA dataset) and normal tissues (from the Genotype-Tissue Expression [GTEx] dataset). H19 was the only LncRNA significantly upregulated in GBM tumor tissues (Figures 1A, S1C, and S1D). Specifically, H19 expression was negatively correlated with the proportion of activated CD8+T cells and positively correlated with the proportions of MDSCs, macrophages, and regulatory T cells (Figures 1B–1E), and GBM patients with high H19 expression tended to have a worse prognosis (Figure 1F).

To further assess the expression of H19 in GBM, we next analyzed 17 GBM scRNA-seq samples in the dataset (GSE182109) and found that H19 was expressed exclusively in tumor cells (Figure 1G). In addition, H19 was overexpressed in patient-derived GBM stem cell (GSC) lines, especially in the MES28 and GSC23, compared with normal human astrocytes (NHAs) and established glioma cell lines (Figure 1H). In a cohort of clinical samples (40 randomly selected paired GBM samples), the levels of H19 were significantly higher in the tumor samples than those in the paired adjacent brain tissues (Figures 1I and 1J). Analysis of cohorts from the TCGA and GTEx datasets also revealed that H19 was highly expressed in GBM (Figure 1K). These results indicate that H19 is highly upregulated in GBM tissues and is a potential immunosuppressive LncRNA in GBM.

H19 encodes a 256-amino-acid protein

H19 was one of the first LncRNAs reported in the 1980s, and it is highly conserved across different mammalian species.26,42 Past studies of H19 concentrated on its pro-tumorigenic functions, like boosting tumor growth and preventing apoptosis, with less focus on TME impact.43,44 Previous studies indicate that LncRNA-derived proteins may differ functionally from LncRNAs.45 We hypothesized that the effect of H19 on GBM-TME may stem from its unreported functions, for example, its coding ability. We performed ribosome nascent chain sequencing (RNC-seq) of three patient-derived GSC lines and two neural stem cell (NSC) lines to reveal differentially expressed genes with coding ability (Figure 2A). Among the 1,776 differentially expressed genes (|log2FC|>2, FDR<0.01), 339 LncRNAs annotated (Figures 2B; Table S1), including ZNF667-AS1, LINC01224 and PCAT7 (potentially translatable in induced pluripotent stem cells46), and HOXB-AS3 (encodes a 53-aa peptide47). The coding potential of H19 was also detected, and it was found to be significantly overexpressed in GSCs (Figures 2C; Table S2). Only 32 LncRNAs were upregulated in all three GSCs. H19 was the only gene inversely correlated with TCGA-GBM patients’ survival (Figure S2A; Table S2). To further validate the coding ability of H19, we performed polysome profile analysis (Figure 2D). H19 was detected in the 40–80s ribosome and polysome fractions with a similar distribution pattern to that of LINC00673,29 indicating the binding of H19 to ribosomes and the coding potential of H19. In contrast, the majority of the LncRNA HOTAIR48 transcripts were distributed in the non-ribosome fraction (Figure 2E).

Inspection of the H19 transcript revealed four potential open reading frames (ORFs) (Figure 2F). To further confirm the translatable ORFs of H19, we generated several tagged H19 constructs (Figure 2G). The 3×FLAG sequence was inserted at the N terminus of ORF1 to verify the position of the translation start codon. Since ORF1 and ORF2 share the same stop codon, we inserted a 3×FLAG tag at the C terminus of ORF2 to determine whether ORF2 was also translated. Similarly, we chose to insert a 3×FLAG tag at the C terminus of ORF3 and ORF4. By immunoblotting, we found that despite the insertion of 3×Flag at different positions in H19-ORF1 and ORF2, the protein bands appeared at the same molecular weight, and no bands were detected in the groups transfected with ORF3 and ORF4 (Figure 2H). The results suggested that only ORF1, which was predicted to encode a 256-amino-acid protein (hereafter termed as H19-immune-related protein, or H19-IRP, owing to its potential involvement in immunosuppression), could be translated. A consistent conserved ORF was also identified in murine H19, which encodes a 132-amino-acid protein H19-Irp (Figure S2B).

Subsequently, we generated a polyclone antibody against human H19-IRP and murine H19-Irp (Figures 2I and S2C). This antibody recognized endogenous H19-IRP encoded by H19 as well as H19-IRP-3×FLAG in transfected 293T cells. The decreased levels of H19-IRP induced by small interfering RNA (siRNA)-mediated silencing of H19 confirmed the specificity of the anti-H19-IRP antibody (Figure 2J). When the start codon “ATG” in H19 was deleted, H19-IRP expression was not elevated in H19-transfected 293T cells (Figures 2K and S2D). Additionally, endogenous H19-IRP peptides in MES28 and H19-Irp in the murine glioma cell line GL261 were identified by mass spectrometry (MS) (Figures 2L and S2E). The identified sequences found had no matches in the reference proteins database (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). H19-IRP expression was enriched in GSCs compared with that in NHA and glioma cell lines (Figure 2M). In 7 randomly selected GBM samples and paired normal tissues, H19-IRP was highly expressed in the tumor tissues (Figure 2N). Since the anti-H19-IRP antibody targets a common sequence in the human H19-IRP and murine H19-Irp sequences, murine H19-Irp can also be detected by this antibody, and H19-Irp is relatively more highly expressed in GL261 cells than in CT2A cells (Figure S2F). Semiquantitative scoring indicated H19-IRP was preferentially expressed in tumor tissues in the cohort of 40 GBM samples (Figure 2O). Based on semiquantitative immunoblot analysis, we found that higher H19-IRP expression was negatively correlated with patient overall survival (Figure 2P). Taken together, these results demonstrated that H19, one of the earliest identified LncRNAs, encodes a previously undetected protein, H19-IRP, linking to poor survival in patients with GBM.

H19-IRP deficiency disrupts the formation of immunosuppressive TME in GBM progression

Given that H19-IRP is mainly expressed in tumor cells (Figure 1G), we subsequently determined whether H19-encoded protein influences tumor cell activities. H19-Irp knockout mediated by small guide RNAs (sgRNAs) did not affect H19 expression but significantly repressed H19-Irp expression in GL261 cells (Figures S3A and S3B). Similarly, H19-Irp overexpression by inducing the H19-Irp ORF alone in CT2A cells (Figure S3C) exclusively increased the protein level. Neither knockout nor overexpression of H19-Irp affected cell proliferation, migration, or apoptosis in vitro, suggesting H19-Irp is unrelated to these tumor characteristics (Figures S3D–S3J). As bioinformatics analysis revealed that H19 is associated with the immunosuppressive TME (Figures 1B–1E), we hypothesized that H19 functions through its coding product to affect the GBM-TME. We implanted GL261 cells with or without H19-Irp knockout into the C57BL/6 mice brains orthotopically. H19-Irp deficiency reduced the tumor volume and prolonged the overall survival of the mice (Figures 3A, 3B, and S3K). Flow cytometric profiling of the TME revealed that the percentages of MDSCs, monocytic(M)-MDSCs, and TAMs were declined in the sgH19-Irp group compared to the scramble group, while the percentages of polymorphonuclear(P)-MDSCs and regulatory T cells were not significantly different between the two groups. Moreover, the percentages of CD8+T cells and CD4+T cells increased in the sgH19-Irp group (Figures 3C–3G). The expression of tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) and interferon (IFN)-γ in T cells was upregulated, and the percentage of exhausted T cells was decreased in the sgH19-Irp group compared with the scramble group (Figures 3H and 3I), indicating that H19-Irp knockout sensitizes GL261 cells to T cell-mediated cytotoxicity. Immunofluorescence assays revealed changes in TME components consistent with those detected by flow cytometry (Figure 3J). The disruption of the immunosuppressive TME induced by H19-Irp deficiency was in line with the results of an in vitro migration assay, which revealed decreased MDSC and TAM recruitment and T cell exhaustion when cells were cultured in the supernatant from GL261 cells with H19-Irp knockout (Figures 3K–3M). Together, these results indicate that the loss of H19-IRP alters the TME and thus suppresses tumorigenesis.

H19-IRP, but not H19, exerts the immunosuppressive function

It was well established that H19 promotes GBM progression by promoting tumor proliferation and invasion.49,50,51 To further exclude the possibility that H19, but not H19-IRP, plays a role in shaping the immunosuppressive TME, we established stable H19 knockdown GL261 cells via short hairpin RNA (shRNA) transfection. The H19 delATG plasmid, unable to initiate H19-Irp translation but minimally affecting RNA sequence and structure (Figure S4A), restored H19 RNA expression in H19 knockdown GL261 cells without affecting H19-Irp level. The H19-Irp plasmid was used to restore H19-Irp expression in H19 knockdown GL261 cells without influencing H19 RNA level. H19 shRNA successfully inhibited both H19 and H19-Irp expression, while H19 delATG and H19-Irp restored H19 RNA and H19-Irp, respectively (Figure S4B). Stable H19 knockdown inhibited cell proliferation and migration in vitro, while H19-IRP cannot recover these in vitro phenotypes (Figures 4A, S4C, and S4D). However, re-expression of either H19 RNA (delATG) or H19-Irp promoted tumor progression. Stable knockdown of H19 prolonged the overall survival of mice, and restoring either H19 delATG or H19-Irp expression partially reversed the survival benefit (Figures 4B, 4C, and S4E). The differences between the in vitro and in vivo phenotypes suggested that H19-Irp promotes tumor progression in a different manner from H19 RNA.

To further confirm whether the immunosuppressive function of H19-IRP is independent of H19 RNA, we also detected changes in the TME. The proportions of different immune cell types associated with immunosuppression, such as M-MDSCs and TAMs, were decreased in the shH19 group compared with the control group. The proportions of immune cell types associated with anti-tumor effects, such as CD8+T cells and CD4+T cells, were increased in the shH19 group (Figures 4D–4G). Notably, the extent to which the proportion of immunosuppressive and anti-tumor cell populations were restored was more pronounced in the recovering H19-Irp (shH19+H19-Irp) group than in the recovering H19 RNA (shH19+delATG) group. Compared with those in the recovering H19 RNA (shH19+delATG) group, the proportions of infiltrating MDSCs and TAMs in the recovering H19-Irp (shH19+H19-Irp) group were greater; T cells exhibited lower infiltration levels in the H19-Irp re-expression group, had weaker anti-tumor activity, and were more exhausted (Figures 4D–4G). Compared with those in the scramble group, the levels of TNF-α and IFN-γ in T cells were increased, and the proportion of exhausted T cells was decreased in the shH19 group (Figures 4H and 4I). Although recovery of both H19 RNA (shH19+delATG) and H19-Irp (shH19+H19-Irp) restored the changes in TNF-α and IFN-γ levels and the proportion of exhausted T cells, these changes were more significant in the recovering H19-Irp group (Figures 4H and 4I). Consistently, immunofluorescence staining revealed that stable H19 knockdown by shRNA resulted in a decrease in M-MDSC and TAM infiltration and an increase in CD8+T cell and CD4+T cell infiltration. Both recovering H19 RNA (shH19+delATG) and H19-Irp (shH19+H19-Irp) reversed the decreases in M-MDSC and TAM infiltration and the increases in CD8+T and CD4+T cell infiltration, while the re-expression of H19-Irp (shH19+H19-Irp) had a more pronounced effect than the recovery of H19 RNA (shH19+delATG) (Figures 4J–4L).

Taken together, the results indicate that H19-IRP affects tumor progression by directly shaping the immunosuppressive TME, while H19 RNA indirectly alters the TME by promoting tumor proliferation and invasion.

H19-IRP shapes the inhibitive TME by targeting CCL2 and Galectin-9

To investigate the mechanism by which H19-IRP mediates immunosuppression, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on GL261 cells with or without H19-Irp knockout via sgRNAs. Upon H19-Irp knockdown, a total of 335 upregulated genes and 377 downregulated genes were identified by RNA-seq (p < 0.05, |log2FC|>0.3). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that downregulation of monocyte chemotaxis and the cellular response to IFN-γ were among the top 20 pathways (Figure 5A; Table S3). Monocyte chemotaxis52,53 and the cellular response to IFN-γ 54,55 have been associated with anti-tumor immunity. Consistent with H19-IRP recruiting MDSCs, TAMs, and promoting T cell exhaustion, we performed qPCR to quantify expression for the top genes associated with anti-tumor immunity. We found that CCL2 and LGALS9 (gene encoding Galectin-9, a ligand for TIM-3) were significantly downregulated in GL261 sgH19 cells (Figures 5B, 5C, and S5A). Moreover, we used a Quantibody cytokine antibody array to perform a more comprehensive proteomic analysis of culture medium from MES28 with or without sgH19 transfection. We observed that loss of H19-IRP dramatically compromised CCL2 production (Figures 5D and 5E). The enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) results confirmed that CCL2 and Galectin-9 levels were lower in the sgH19 group than in the scramble group (Figures 5F and 5G). Consistently, immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining revealed that the expression of CCL2 and Galectin-9 decreased upon H19-IRP loss in xenografts (Figure 5H). Depletion of CCL2 in GL261 cells significantly suppressed the migration of MDSCs and TAMs toward conditioned medium in vitro, and Galectin-9 knockdown decreased the proportion of exhausted T cells in vitro (Figures S5B–S5D). Recombinant mouse CCL2 and Galectin-9 reversed MDSCs, TAMs migration inhibition, and T cell exhaustion upon H19-IRP loss (Figures 5I–5K), further supporting that H19-IRP shapes the immunosuppressive TME through CCL2 and Galectin-9. Thus, we suggest that H19-IRP promotes immunosuppression in the TME by increasing CCL2 and Galectin-9, and blocking this can significantly hinder tumor growth.

Previous work has shown that CCL2 and Galectin-9 are associated with resistance to ICB therapy.56,57 Since H19-IRP modulates CCL2 and Galectin-9 to shape the immunosuppressive TME, GBM’s resistance to ICB therapy could be partially due to H19-IRP elevating these proteins. We next investigated whether H19-IRP deficiency combined with anti-PD1 therapy can remodel the immunosuppressive TME and strengthen anti-tumor immunity (Figure 5L). Anti-PD1 treatment led to reduced tumor volume and prolonged survival, and these effects were further enhanced when anti-PD1 treatment was combined with H19-Irp knockout (Figures 5M, 5N, and S5E). Although anti-PD1 therapy alone promoted T cell killing and alleviated T cell exhaustion, it also increased M-MDSC recruitment, which may partly limit the efficacy of anti-PD1 therapy. Notably, combination therapy significantly amplified the aforementioned anti-tumor effects (Figures 5O and 5P). These data reinforce our hypothesis that H19-IRP contributes to the inherent resistance of GBM to ICB therapy and H19-IRP inhibition sensitizes GBM to ICB therapy.

H19-IRP directly activates the transcription of CCL2 and Galectin-9

To elucidate the mechanism by which H19-IRP mediates the upregulation of CCL2 and Galectin-9, we next determined the subcellular localization of H19-IRP. Immunofluorescence imaging and subcellular fractionation followed by immunoblotting showed that H19-IRP was localized primarily to the nucleus (Figures 6A and 6B). Considering the nuclear localization of H19-IRP, we further explored the possibility that H19-IRP is directly involved in the transcription of CCL2 and LGALS9. We found CCL2 and LGALS9 were activated by H19-IRP in a dose-dependent manner after CCL2 and LGALS9 promoter regions were cloned and inserted into a luciferase reporter vector and co-transfected with H19-IRP (Figures 6C and S6A). Using truncated CCL2 and LGALS9 promoters, we observed that the −2,000 to −1,500 bp segment of the CCL2 promoter and the −1,000 to −500 bp segment of the LGALS9 promoter are critical for H19-IRP-mediated upregulation (Figures 6D and S6B).

Subsequently, we conducted a chromatin immunoprecipitation assay utilizing MES28 transfected with H19-IRP-3×FLAG. Primers were strategically designed to hybridize with the promoter regions of CCL2 and LGALS9. H19-IRP-3×FLAG bound to the CCL2 and LGALS9 promoters, further indicating that H19-IRP plays a direct role in driving CCL2 and LGALS9 transcription (Figure 6E). To further delineate the interaction between H19-IRP and the CCL2 and LGALS9 promoters and to pinpoint the specific binding sites, we designed a set of probes targeting the aforementioned precipitated regions (Figure 6F) and performed an electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA). H19-IRP-3×FLAG specifically interacted with CCL2 probe 3 and its truncated derivative probe 5 (spanning from −1,755 bp to −1,711 bp). Similarly, LGALS9 probe 3 and its truncated form, probe 6 (−721 bp to −667 bp), successfully interacted with H19-IRP-3×FLAG (Figure 6F). By analyzing the aforementioned shifted probes, the consensus sequence “TGAAATG” was identified as the potential binding motif. Further addition of anti-FLAG antibody resulted in the formation of a supershifted band, validating the specificity of the interaction (Figure 6G). The aforementioned evidence collectively demonstrated that H19-IRP can promote the transcription of CCL2 and LGALS9 by directly binding to their promoters. As mentioned earlier, the homo-H19-IRP and mus-H19-Irp sequences are conserved (Figure S2B). To further elaborate the consistency of their functions, we designed a series of truncations. In homo-H19-IRP, 50–150 aa is the key sequence that promotes CCL2 and LGALS9 transcription (Figures 6H and 6I). In mus-H19-Irp, 80–132 aa is the key sequence for promoting CCL2 and LGALS9 transcription (Figures 6J and 6K). These two sequences are conserved between human and mice (Figure S2B), which indicates that homo-H19-IRP and mus-H19-Irp drive CCL2 and LGALS9 transcription via a conserved mechanism.

The key function of H19-IRP in GBM-TME makes it vital to explore how it is highly expressed in GBM, aiding in the understanding of GBM progression and targeted therapy creation. H19 is regulated by several factors, including hypoxia, a hub factor in tumors.58 The association between hypoxia and H19-IRP deserves further exploration. Using ssGSEA on the CGGA-GBM dataset, we found a positive correlation between H19 and hypoxia score, consistent with the findings of a previous study58 (Figure 6L). We next investigated whether hypoxia influences the expression of H19-IRP. Both CoCl2 and atmospheric hypoxia induced H19-IRP expression in GSCs (Figures 6M, 6N, S6C, and S6D), while knockdown of HIF-1α, a well-established hypoxia-induced transcription factor, completely abolished this effect (Figure 6O), which supports that H19-IRP is driven by the hypoxia/HIF-1α axis. Therefore, our data suggested that hypoxia is a potential modulator of H19-IRP expression in GBM.

H19-IRP, a TAA, has potential value as a vaccine target

The highly specific expression of H19-IRP in tumor cells (Figure 1G) strongly suggests that H19-IRP could be developed as a cancer vaccine target. A qualified cancer vaccine should be characterized by high expression specificity and antigen immunogenicity.59,60 We next explored the feasibility of H19-IRP as a vaccine target. We investigated human and mouse H19 expression in GEO datasets and found that H19 is only highly expressed in both human and mouse placentas and expressed at relatively low levels in other organs, suggesting that H19 is expressed at low levels in somatic cells in the general population (Figure 7A). Immunoblotting of GBM tumor tissues and normal tissues also consistently showed that H19-Irp was particularly highly expressed in tumor rather than the other healthy organ tissues (Figure 7B). We next investigated whether H19-IRP can be presented by major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I). We performed human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-I immunoprecipitation followed by MS in MES28. After matching the MS results with the H19-IRP sequence, we identified two HLA-I-bound peptides (pep1: ASVGSSTV and pep2: TRRGGRGGVNEL) derived from H19-IRP (Figures 7C; Table S4). Since binding of CD8+T cells to the HLA-I complexes is required for anti-tumor immunity, we next investigated whether H19-IRP-derived peptides could effectively activate CD8+T cells. Peripheral T cells were labeled with carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester and separately stimulated with artificial H19-IRP-derived peptides to identify the presence of T cells that recognize the corresponding peptides. There was a greater number of proliferated T cells in the peptide group than in the DMSO group (Figure S7A). Consistently, peripheral T cells released an abundant amount of IFN-γ when cocultured with PBMCs treated with artificial H19-IRP-derived peptides (Figure 7D). Taken together, these results indicate that H19-IRP is specifically expressed in tumor and exhibits considerable antigenic immunogenicity, suggesting that it may be a promising target for cancer vaccines.

Circular RNA vaccines, with their higher stability and longer expression than mRNA, have been developed for vaccine preparation in recent years.39 To prepare a circular RNA vaccine targeting H19-Irp, the linear H19-ORF was made into circH19 using an Anabaena intronic self-cleaving ribozyme system (Figures S7B and S7C). Next, circH19 was coated with lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) to prepare a circular RNA vaccine (circH19-vac). The H19-ORF was in tandem with a luciferase reporter gene to enable evaluation of its expression efficiency (Figure S7D). Significant changes in luciferase activity and H19-Irp levels were detected, indicating that circH19 was successfully expressed in vitro (Figures S7E and S7F). Next, we administered circH19-vac to mice via intramuscular injection. Compared to vector group, those receiving circH19-vac showed greater luminescent activity (Figure S7G). These data indicate that circH19-vac can efficiently express the H19-Irp antigen both in vitro and in vivo.

To assess the immunogenicity of circH19-vac, C57BL/6 mice were immunized with circH19-vac twice. We then prepared splenocytes from vaccinated mice, cocultured them with bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) that had been transfected with circH19 for 24 h, and evaluated the activation of CD8+T cells using an IFN-γ enzyme-linked immunospot (ELISpot) assay (Figure S7H). Splenic T cells from circH19-vac-inoculated mice released an abundant amount of IFN-γ after challenge with BMDCs transfected with circH19 in vitro (Figure S7I). To further evaluate the therapeutic potential of circH19-vac, we immunized C57BL/6 mice bearing GL261 orthotopic tumors via a vaccination strategy (Figure S7J). CircH19-vac significantly decreased tumor volume and prolonged survival (Figures S7K and S7L). We found that the TME of mice in the circH19-vac group was remodeled, with less M-MDSCs and TAMs, more CD8+ and CD4+T cells, increased T cell killing capacity, and decreased degree of exhaustion (Figure S7M).

The aforementioned circH19-vac was a vaccine specifically tailored for the mouse model. To investigate the clinical potential of vaccine targeting human H19-IRP, we further designed a vaccine targeting H19-IRP. The immunogenic peptide sequences identified through HLA class I co-immunoprecipitation were found at positions 19–26 (ASVGSSTW) and 174–185 (TRRGGRGGVNEL) within the amino acid sequence (Figure 7C). To safeguard these immunogenic sequences and avoid potential pro-tumorigenic effects, we selected the 1–188 amino acid sequence of H19-IRP to create a human circH19 (homo-circH19) (Figure 7E)61,62 and coated it with LNPs to prepare a circular RNA vaccine (homo-circH19-vac). To assess the therapeutic efficacy of homo-circH19-vac, we immunized huHSC-NCG mice, a humanized mouse model, bearing MES28 orthotopic tumors via a vaccination strategy (Figure 7F). Consistent with the effects observed with circH19-vac, homo-circH19-vac significantly reduced tumor volume and prolonged mice survival (Figures 7G and 7H). Additionally, we observed a remodeling of the TME in the homo-circH19-vac group, with a reduction of M-MDSCs and TAMs, an increase of CD8+T cells and CD4+T cells, enhanced T cell cytotoxicity, and a decrease in the level of T cell exhaustion (Figure 7I). These data reveal that tumor vaccines utilizing LncRNA-derived antigenic peptides might serve as an effective strategy for tumor immunotherapy.

Discussion

By MS, ribosome analysis, and CRISPR-based screens, recent studies have managed to reveal that LncRNA can function as templates for translating proteins30,31,47 and have identified a large number of uncharacterized functional micropeptides in the human genome, revealing the prevalence of unannotated ORFs with protein translational capacity.46

H19, a well-known LncRNA, has been extensively studied for its non-coding function in recent decades.63,64,65 Studies have consistently reported that H19 is involved in the progression of GBM, orchestrating a spectrum of cellular activities including cell proliferation, autophagy, and angiogenesis. For instance, H19 has been shown to function as a molecular sponge for miR-491-5p, thereby activating autophagy and fueling the development of GBM.66 Additionally, the interaction of H19 with miR-138 is implicated in promoting the angiogenic processes within GBM, facilitating the tumor vascularization.67 The regulatory scope of H19 is further expanded because it acts as an upstream repressor of miR-29b, modulating the expression of nerve/glial antigen 2 through the intricate c-Myc/H19/miR-29b axis.68 The results of our GBM model also indicate that H19 has canonical LncRNA-mediated functions, including promoting tumor proliferation and invasion. The ORF of H19 has been noted by scholars previously.69 However, due to the limitations of translatomic technology at that time, the coding potential and translation product of H19 were not found. While previous research has confirmed H19’s significant role in GBM progression, our current investigation unveils a new dimension of the spectrum of H19 function. Our study revealed a cryptic regulator involved in shaping the GBM-TME. H19-IRP’s discovery enhances the understanding of TME formation, offering a promising therapeutic target for GBM patients.

The infiltration of MDSCs and TAMs and the exhaustion of T cells are important features of the immunosuppressive TME, which severely limit the efficacy of anti-tumor immunotherapy.70,71,72 CCL2 is an important driver of MDSC and TAM infiltration, which inhibits CD8+T cell activities and promotes tumor progression.73 Blocking the CCL2/CCR2 axis reduces MDSC and TAM infiltration and enhances anti-tumor response.52,74 Galectin-9, a TIM-3 ligand, has been shown to induce T cell exhaustion. However, treatment strategies involving neutralizing free secreted proteins such as CCL2 and Galectin-9 with antibodies have yielded limited clinical benefit,75,76 and inhibition of their production at the source may be a more promising approach. Our study revealed that H19-IRP promotes the expression of CCL2 and Galectin-9 by binding directly to promoter regions. Targeting H19-IRP may be an option for addressing the failure of currently available treatments targeting CCL2 and Galectin-9.

Another important finding of our study is that H19-IRP has tumor expression specificity and is an ideal cancer vaccine target candidate. Our study demonstrated that H19-IRP can be specifically expressed in GBM tumor cells and is recognized as a TAA; thus, immunotherapy targeting H19-IRP is expected to selectively target tumor cells. The existing effective glioma tumor vaccine targets are limited. The DC vaccine targeting Survivin and the DC vaccine loaded with autologous tumor lysate are currently effective drugs reported in phase 2/3 clinical trials.77,78 Most of these existing targets are derived from known coding genes. Thus, the vast expanse of non-coding regions presents an uncharted territory rich with potential vaccine targets that merit further exploration. Non-coding region-derived neoantigens have been shown to be presented by MHC-I and activate T cell responses.79 The discovery of neoantigens derived from LncRNAs such as H19-IRP has enriched the pool of candidate targets for tumor immunotherapy. Furthermore, based on the innate advantages of circular RNA and the effectiveness of circular RNA vaccines,38,39 we pioneeringly combined H19-IRP, a LncRNA-derived protein, with the circular RNA vaccine platform to prepare a circular RNA vaccine, circH19-vac, and validated its effectiveness. CircH19-vac provides a new option for GBM immunotherapy.

The circH19-vac developed in this study targets the H19-Irp sequence of murine origin and was tested in a preclinical model. Further studies of circH19-vac against H19-IRP of human origin as well as clinical trials are needed. Previous GBM vaccines, such as the EGFRvIII-targeting peptide vaccine Rindopepimut and the DC vaccine ICT-107, rely on effective T cell activation for inducing the anti-tumor immune response. However, these vaccines have little impact on the GBM-TME due to their therapeutic targets, and the highly immunosuppressive GBM-TME has not been effectively ameliorated, greatly limiting T cell anti-tumor efficacy. Since H19-IRP is involved in shaping the GBM-TME, compared with previous GBM vaccines, circH19-vac not only activates anti-tumor T cell responses but also modifies the GBM-TME to improve T cell anti-tumor function.

The upstream mechanism of H19-IRP-specific expression in GBM remains to be further explored. In our study, we focused on dissecting the impact of hypoxia on H19-IRP expression. Tumorigenesis is characterized by the complex interplay of multiple factors, such as hypoxia, genome mutations, and vasculature.9 It is conceivable that hypoxia may play a pivotal role in the upstream regulation of H19-IRP expression, but it is likely not the only mechanism involved. There may be additional regulatory mechanisms that are worth examining to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of the network regulating H19-IRP expression.

In conclusion, we identified a previously undetected protein, H19-IRP, that plays an important role in shaping the immunosuppressive TME of GBM. The therapeutic value of our study was emphasized by the preparation of the circular RNA vaccine circH19-vac and its efficacy in preclinical models. Thus, H19-IRP is a safe and promising immunotherapeutic target for enhancing the efficacy of GBM immunotherapy.

Limitations of the study

Our research has unveiled the promising potential of H19-IRP-targeted tumor vaccines for advancing to clinical trials among cancer patients. However, there remains a pressing need for comprehensive mechanistic investigations and extensive pharmacological studies to fully understand the vaccine’s impact. Although our findings indicate that H19-IRP is scarcely expressed in human somatic cells and is uniquely present in GBM tumor cells, more accurate experimental evaluations of the anti-tumor efficacy and pharmacological characteristics of targeted H19-IRP vaccines are needed. This is crucial for optimizing their clinical translation and preparing for future clinical trials. We have documented that H19-IRP plays a pivotal role in tumor progression by modulating the GBM-TME. Our study also revealed a synergistic effect when targeted H19-IRP therapy is combined with ICB therapy. Moving forward, future research will delve into the synergistic efficacy of circH19-vac in conjunction with ICB therapy for GBM patients.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mice and animal housing

Six-week-old female C57BL/6 mice were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Center of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. The animals were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility under a 12-h light-dark cycle. The temperature ranged from 24°C to 26°C, and the humidity ranged from 50 to 70%. All the animal experiments conducted in this study were approved by the Ethics Institutional Review Boards of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Approval No. [2023]127).

Humanized mouse model

The huHSC-NCG mice were purchased from GemPharmatech (Cat# T037620). Briefly, human CD34+HSCs were isolated from cord blood. NCG mice (ShiLtJGpt-Prkdcem26Cd52Il2rgem26Cd22/Gpt) were irradiated before CD34+HSC transplantation. the CD34+HSCs were injected via tail vein. after 12–16 weeks, the immune system of these mice was rebuilt. Mice engrafted at >25% human CD45+ in peripheral blood were used for subsequent intracranial tumorigenesis and vaccine immunization therapy.

Human high-grade gliomas, paired adjacent tissue samples and blood samples

Human high-grade glioma tumor and adjacent tissues, as well as peripheral blood, were collected at the Department of Neurosurgery of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University after obtaining patient consent and after confirmation by neuropathologists. Patient consent to publish potential identifying clinical information for individuals was also obtained. The study was approved by the Ethics Institutional Review Boards of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University and complied with all relevant ethical regulations regarding human participants.

Tumor models and treatments

An orthotopic tumor model was established in this study as previously described.81 This study was approved by the Ethics Institutional Review Boards of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups for all the experiments. For the animal survival analysis, mice were intracranially injected with 5×104 sgH19 or scrambled GL261 cells in 5 μL of PBS using a 10-μL Hamilton syringe through a guide screw into the right frontal lobe at a depth of 3 mm until pathological symptoms from tumor burden developed. For survival studies, mice were observed and weighed daily and humanely euthanized once they presented neurological symptoms, displayed 20% weight loss or became moribund to ensure that the intracranial tumor burden did not exceed the ethical limits.

For the intracranial tumor model treated with anti-PD1 antibody, the mice received an intraperitoneal injection of anti-PD1 antibody (BE0146, BioXcell, 10 mg/kg, 1:5 diluted in PBS) or PBS every 5 days. The mice were observed and weighed daily and humanely euthanized once they presented neurological symptoms, displayed 20% weight loss or became moribund to ensure that the intracranial tumor burden did not exceed the ethical limits.

For circH19-vac/homo-circH19-vac treatment model, tumor models were constructed by intracranial injection of cells, as previously described. Following the establishment of the in-situ tumor model, immunization with the vaccine was conducted on days 6 and 16 post-construction. The vaccine was administered intramuscularly at a dosage of 10 μg per animal, with a total of two injections given. The mice were observed and weighed daily and humanely euthanized according to the euthanasia criteria mentioned above.

For the detection of H19-Irp expression in tumour-bearing mice, mice were intracranially injected with 5×104 GL261 cells in 5 μL of PBS. The mice were observed and weighed daily and humanely euthanized once they presented neurological symptoms, displayed 20% weight loss. After the mice were euthanized, intracranial tumors and other organ tissues were obtained for further detection.

The intracranial tumor volume (mm3) was calculated by measuring the length (L) and width (W) of the tumor from the HE-stained sections (V = π/6×L×W2).

Cell lines and cell culture

GSC lines, including MES28, GSC23, 387 and 3691, were kindly provided by Dr. Jeremy N. Rich (UPMC). These cell lines were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco) supplemented with B27 (Life Technologies), bFGF and EGF (both 20 ng/mL, R&D Systems). NHAs were purchased from Lonza and cultured with an AGM Astrocyte Growth Medium Bullet Kit (Lonza) according to the manufacturer’s recommendation. The U118, HS683, SW1783 and Ln18 cell lines were kindly provided by Dr. Suyun Huang (VCU). These cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) according to standard protocols.

Method details

Data processing to identify immune-related LncRNAs

Genomic and clinical data for GBM samples were downloaded from the TCGA and CGGA datasets. We performed ssGSEA to determine the proportions of 28 infiltrating immune cells in GBM samples based on reported immune-related gene sets.40 We further calculated the correlation between the proportions of various immune cells and the expression of all the LncRNAs. |R|>0.2 and p < 0.01 were used as the cut-off value. Based on previous studies,41 we considered 12 immune cell types to be anti-tumour cell types, namely, activated CD4 T cell, activated CD8 T cell, central memory CD4 T cell, central memory CD8 T cell, effector memory CD4 T cell, effector memory CD8 T cell, type1 T helper cell, type17 T helper cell, activated dendritic cell, CD56bright natural killer cell, natural killer cell and natural killer T cell. Additionally, 8 kinds of immune cells, including regulatory T cell, type 2 T helper cell, CD56dim natural killer cell, immature dendritic cell, macrophage, MDSC, neutrophil and plasmacytoid dendritic cell were considered pro-tumour cell types. We considered those LncRNAs that were negatively correlated with the infiltration of anti-tumour cell types or positively correlated with the infiltration of pro-tumour cell types to be immune-related LncRNAs.

Ribosome-nascent chain complex (RNC) sequencing and data processing

RNC-seq was performed as described previously.81 Briefly, cells were pretreated with 100 μg/mL cycloheximide for 15 min before lysis. Cell lysates were transferred onto the surface of 30% sucrose RB buffer and then subjected to ultracentrifugation at 185,000 × g for 5 h at 4°C for RNC precipitation. RNA in total cell lysates and RNC-mRNAs were extracted and subjected to RNA sequencing. RNC-RNA was extracted using a TRIzol reagent kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The RNA quality was assessed with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA) and checked using RNase-free agarose gel electrophoresis. After the RNC-RNA was extracted, the rRNAs were removed to retain the mRNAs and ncRNAs. The enriched mRNAs and ncRNAs were broken into short fragments using fragmentation buffer and reverse transcribed into cDNA with random primers. Second-strand cDNA was synthesized with DNA polymerase I, RNase H, dNTPs (dUTP instead of dTTP) and buffer. Next, the cDNA fragments were purified with a QiaQuick PCR extraction kit (Qiagen, Venlo, The Netherlands), end repaired, poly(A)-conjugated, and ligated to Illumina sequencing adapters. Then, uracil-N-glycosylase (UNG) was used to digest the second-strand cDNA. The digested products were size selected by agarose gel electrophoresis, PCR amplified, and sequenced using an Illumina NovaSeqTM 6000 by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co. (Guangzhou, China).

Polysome profiling analysis

293T cells were treated with 100 μg/mL cycloheximide (CHX) in DMSO for 5 min at 37°C, washed with CHX containing PBS and harvested for subsequent polysome profiling. Cells were lysed in 500 μL of polysome lysis buffer (5 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 2.5 mM MgCl2, 1.5 mM KCl, 1× protease inhibitor cocktail (EDTA-free), 0.5% Triton X-100, 2 mM DTT, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 100 units of RNase inhibitor, and 100 μg/mL CHX) on ice for 15 min, followed by centrifugation at 4°C for 7 min at 16000×g to pellet nuclei and mitochondria. The supernatant was then loaded onto a 5–50% (w/v) sucrose density gradient, ultracentrifuged at 20,000×g for 2 h at 4°C in a Beckman SW41 rotor and subsequently fractionated using a BioComp PGFip Piston Gradient Fractionator Model 152. The absorbance at 254 nm was measured using an absorbance detector connected to the fraction collector. RNA was extracted from the fractions using TRIzol LS solution, and RT‒PCR was conducted to evaluate the H19, LncRNA HOTAIR (negative control) and LINC00673 (positive control) levels in the indicated fractions.

Plasmids and transfection

The H19-ORF1-3×Flag, H19-ORF2-3×Flag, H19-ORF3-3×Flag, H19-ORF4-3×Flag, H19-delATG-3×Flag, H19-IRP-3×Flag, and H19-Irp-3×Flag plasmids were constructed by chemical gene synthesis (Generay Biotech, Shanghai, China) employing pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1-T2A-Puro or pcDNA3.1(+) as the backbone. Plasmids were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen).

RNA interference and transfection

SiRNAs were obtained from Hanyi Biotech (Guangdong, China). The target sequences are listed in Table S5. Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent (Invitrogen) was used for transfection according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

LC‒MS analysis

Proteins were separated via SDS‒PAGE and subjected to digestion with sequencing-grade trypsin (Promega). The digested peptides were analyzed with a QExactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The fragment spectra were analyzed using the National Center for Biotechnology Information nonredundant protein database with Mascot (Matrix Science).

Lentivirus production and stable cell line establishment

The target sequences of the guide RNAs (gRNAs) were obtained from WZ Biosciences, Inc. (Shandong, China). To produce CRISPR lentivirus, gRNAs were transfected with the packaging plasmid psPAX2 (Addgene) and the envelope plasmid pMD2.G (Addgene) into 293T cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). The scramble (sgRNA-scramble) sequence was served as the negative control. Lentiviral shRNA vectors were obtained from WZ Biosciences, Inc. (Shandong, China). For rescue experiments, lentiviral vectors expressing H19 delATG and H19-132aa-3×Flag were cotransfected with the packaging plasmid psPAX2 (Addgene) and the envelope plasmid pMD2.G (Addgene) into 293T cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) for lentivirus production. To establish stable cell lines, GL261 cells were transduced with the above lentiviruses with the aid of polybrene (8 μg/mL, Sigma). After 72 h of incubation, the cells were screened with 2 μg/mL puromycin for 3 days. The target sequences of gRNAs and shRNAs are listed in Table S5.

CCK-8 assay

Two thousand of the indicated cells were seeded into each well of a 96-well plate. The viability of different cell lines was determined by using CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo) and measuring the absorbance at 450 nm (BioTek) every 24 h following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Colony formation assay

Cell proliferation was analyzed by colony formation assays. Two thousand cells were seeded in each well of 6-well plates and cultured for 2 weeks. The cells were then fixed and stained with 0.3% crystal violet in 25% methanol for 1 h and counted.

Migration assay

Migration assays were conducted in Transwell chambers with 8-μm-pore membranes. A total of 5×105 tumor cells in serum-free medium were seeded into the top chamber of the Transwell insert. The cells were allowed to migrate toward the lower chamber containing complete medium for 24 h. The migrated cells were fixed, stained, imaged and quantified using ImageJ.

Cell apoptosis assay

Annexin V/PI staining (C1062M, Beyotime) was used to detect cell apoptosis by flow cytometry. In brief, cells were seeded in 6-well plates. After 24 h, the cells were collected, washed with PBS and resuspended in binding buffer. The cells were then incubated with Annexin V-FITC and PI at room temperature in the dark for 15 min. Flow cytometry analysis was conducted on a BD LSRFortessa X-20, and the data were analyzed using FlowJo (version 10.6.2).

Cell suspension preparation from mouse tissue

For the isolation of cells, fresh mouse brain samples were cut into pieces and digested in DMEM supplemented with collagenase IV (1 mg/mL, Gibco), DNase I (20 U/ml, Sigma‒Aldrich) and hyaluronidase (0.01%, Solarbio) for 30 min at 37°C. After digestion, the cells were filtered through a 70-μm strainer, and Debris Removal Solution (130-109-398, Miltenyi Biotec) was applied to remove myelin according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cell pellets were then subjected to RBC lysis (C3702, Beyotime) and resuspended in FACS staining buffer (PBS containing 2% FBS).

Flow cytometry

The cell suspensions were surface-labelled with fluorescent antibodies for 30 min at 4°C. The cells were then stained for other intracellular markers with a Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (00-5523-00, eBioscience). Dead cells were excluded using a Fixable Viability Dye eFluor 455. In C57BL/6 mouse model, MDSCs were identified with a CD45+CD11b+Gr-1+ gate, M-MDSCs were identified with a CD45+CD11b+Ly6G−Ly6C+ gate, P-MDSCs were identified with a CD45+CD11b+Ly6G+Ly6C− gate, TAMs were identified with a CD45+CD11b+F4/80+ gate, T cells were identified with a CD45+CD3+ gate, Tregs were identified with a CD45+CD3+CD4+Foxp3+ gate. In huHSC-NCG humanized mouse model, M-MDSCs were identified with a CD45+CD11b+CD14+HLA-DR- gate, TAMs were identified with a CD45+CD11b+CD14+CD16+CD49d+ gate, CD8+ T cells were identified with a CD45+CD3+CD8+ gate, CD4+ T cells were identified with a CD45+CD3+CD4+ gate. Flow cytometry was performed with a Cytek Aurora instrument, and the data were analyzed with FlowJo software (version 10.6.2).

Immunofluorescence

Cultured cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min at room temperature, blocked with 1% BSA in PBS, and incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. The next day, fluorescent secondary antibodies (Invitrogen) were added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. The nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6′-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). For multiplex immunofluorescence, this assay was conducted with the Quadruple-Fluorescence Immunohistochemical Mouse/Rabbit Kit (pH 9.0) (RS0037, ImmunoWay) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Paraffin-embedded samples were sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. The nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6′-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Images were acquired using an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope.

MDSC/TAM isolation and in vitro migration assay

MDSCs were isolated from fresh mouse brains with tumors using the Mouse MDSC Isolation Kit (#19867, Stemcell) and plated in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and antibiotics. TAMs were sorted by a BD LSRFortessa X-20 controlled by FACSDiva software (BD). MDSCs/TAMs (1 × 105 cells/well) were seeded in the top chamber of the Transwell plate (353097, Falcon). Cell culture supernatants from cultured cell lines (GL261 scramble or GL261 sgH19 for 48 h) were collected and added to the bottom chamber of the Transwell plate. After 4 h of incubation, the cells that had completely migrated to the bottom chamber were counted. To validate the role of CCL2 in the recruitment of MDSCs/TAMs in vitro, MDSCs/TAMs isolated from fresh mouse brain tumors were cultured in the following medium types: (i) supernatant from the GL261 scramble group, (ii) supernatant from the GL261 sgH19 group, or (iii) supernatant from the GL261 sgH19 + recombinant CCL2 protein group (Z03284, GeneScript).

CD3+ T cell isolation and coculture system

CD3+ T cells were isolated from the spleens of C57BL/6 mice using the MojoSort Mouse CD3 Selection Kit (480099, Biolegend). CD3+ T cells were added to the cell culture supernatant from cultured cell lines (GL261 scramble or GL261 sgH19 cultured for 48 h). After 72 h of incubation, the cells were collected for further analysis. To validate the role of Galectin-9 in the induction of T cell exhaustion in vitro, CD3+ T cells isolated from the spleen were treated with (i) the supernatant from the GL261 scramble group, (ii) the supernatant from the GL261 sgH19 group, or (iii) the supernatant from the GL261 sgH19 + recombinant Galectin-9 protein group (HY-P72637, MCE).

RNA-seq

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol LS solution. An Agilent 4200 bioanalyzer was used to evaluate the concentration and size distribution of the cDNA library before sequencing with an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 instrument. The protocol for high-throughput sequencing was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Illumina). The raw reads were filtered by Seqtk before they were mapped to the genome using HISAT2 (version 2.0.4). The gene fragments were counted using Stringtie (v1.3.3b) and then subjected to trimmed mean of M values (TMM) normalization. Significant differentially expressed genes were identified using DESeq2 software.

Human chemokine array

The cell culture supernatants were collected from MES28 scramble or MES28 sgH19 cultured for 48 h. Human Chemokine Array C1 (AAH-CHE-1, RayBiotech) was used to detect the different chemokines in the cell culture supernatant. The detailed experimental protocols are available at www.RayBiotech.com.

ELISA

Cells (2×106/100 mm dish) were cultured for 24 h. The supernatants were collected, and any floating cells were removed via 0.45-μm filtration. The amount of CCL2 protein and Galectin-9 protein in the supernatant was determined using a mouse CCL2-specific ELISA kit (RayBiotech) and a mouse Galectin-9-specific ELISA kit (RayBiotech). All experiments were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

All paraffin-embedded GBM tumor sections (4 μm thick) were deparaffinized and blocked. Primary antibodies were diluted in bovine serum albumin and applied overnight at 4°C in a wet chamber. After incubation with secondary antibodies, diaminobenzidine reagent was added to the tumor sections, which were then counterstained with hematoxylin to visualize the nuclei. We quantitatively scored the tumor tissue sections according to the percentage of positive cells and staining intensity. The scores were multiplied to yield a scoring range of 0–12.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

For promoter binding site confirmation, mutated or nonmutated fragments spanning a range from −2000 to 0 bp with respect to the transcription start site of the CCL2 or LGALS9 genomic sequence were ligated into the pGL3-Basic vector. GSCs were transfected with the corresponding plasmids and Renilla luciferase vector. The full-length sequence of the CCL2/LGALS9 promoter and vector plasmid were co-transfected as a negative control. The full-length sequence of the CCL2/LGALS9 promoter and H19-IRP plasmid were co-transfected as a positive control. For conformation of functional consistently between human and mouse H19-IRP, GSCs or GL261 cells were transfected with a series of different truncation vectors. A dual-luciferase reporter assay system (RG027, Beyotime) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For each sample, firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

This assay was conducted with a ChIP Kit–One Step (ab117138, Abcam) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. In brief, chromatin was first extracted and sonicated in accordance with the instructions of a chromatin extraction kit (ab117152, Abcam). Reagents, including chromatin, antibodies and ChIP buffer, were added to the strip wells provided in the kit and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. The wells were washed, and the precipitated DNA was released by proteinase K digestion in DNA release buffer. The collected DNA was subsequently analyzed by RT‒PCR. The primers used are listed in Table S5.

EMSA

A nuclear protein extraction kit (P0027, Beyotime) and chemiluminescent EMSA kit (GS009, Beyotime) were used in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols. A set of biotin-labelled probes was designed to target putative binding sites in the CCL2 or Galectin-9 promoter. Nuclear extracts from MES28 cells overexpressing H19-IRP-3×Flag were incubated with the indicated probes. A competition assay was conducted with either an unlabelled probe containing the WT CCL2/Galectin-9 binding site or an unlabelled probe containing the mutated binding site. To identify specific DNA-binding proteins, an anti-Flag antibody (F1804, Sigma‒Aldrich) was used to visualize the supershifted band. The EMSA probes used are listed in Table S5.

RT‒PCR

RNA was reverse transcribed using Evo M-MLV RT Master Mix (AG11706, Accurate Biology). qPCR was conducted with SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Premix (AG11718, Accurate Biology) in a QuantStudio 5 system (Applied Biosystems). Information about the primers used is summarized in Table S5.

Immunoblotting

Proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer (P0013B, Beyotime) containing a protease inhibitor and quantified with a BCA kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cell or tissue lysates containing equal amounts of protein were loaded in each well of a 10–17% SDS–PAGE gel. After electrophoresis, membrane transfer and blocking, the membranes were incubated with the indicated primary antibodies and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (31430, 31460, Invitrogen). The bands were visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence using Clarity Western ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad). All immunoblot results were quantified based on gray scale analysis. See Table S6 for detailed quantitative results.

Immunoprecipitation

Cells were lysed with weak RIPA buffer (P0013D, Beyotime) supplemented with protease inhibitors. The supernatant was immunoprecipitated using the indicated primary antibodies on a rotator at 4°C overnight. Then, the lysates were incubated with 40 μL of protein A/G agarose beads (Gibco) for 2 h at room temperature. The immunoprecipitates were washed five times with ice-cold PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 (PBST), subjected to SDS–PAGE and analyzed by liquid chromatography‒tandem MS (LC‒MS/MS) or immunoblotting.

T cell proliferation assay

To verify the presence of H19-peptide-reactive T cells among peripheral T cells, CSFE (Cat# 565082, BD Biosciences) was used to label the cells. In brief, cells were isolated from peripheral blood using an EasySep Direct Human CD8+ T cell Isolation Kit (Cat#19663, Stemcell) and incubated with CFSE (final concentration of 0.5 μM) for 10 min at 37°C. Next, the labeling was immediately stopped using serum-containing medium, and the cells were washed before they were seeded (5×104 cells/well) in the presence of irradiated autologous PBMCs (45 Gy) (2×105 cells/well) in 200 μL of RPMI 1640 containing 10% FBS and the peptide of interest (final concentration of 10 μM). After 10 days, the cells were pooled, washed once with RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% FBS, stained with Fixable Viability Dye eFluor 455 and surface markers and detected with a BD LSRFortessa X-20 instrument.

IFN-γ ELISpot

CD8+ T cells were collected from peripheral blood or mouse spleens and then stimulated with peptide-pulsed or RNA-transfected DCs for 24–72 h. IFN-γ ELISpot assays were performed to detect IFN-γ-secreting T cells according to the manufacturer’s instructions (human: 2110005, mouse: 2210005, DaKewe). The group with only T cells added served as a negative control. Phorbol Myristate Acetate (PMA) and Ionomycin (Iono) stimulation of T cells served as positive controls. Spot images were acquired using an ImmunoSpot S6 Ultra analyser (Cellular Technology Limited).

Production and purification of circH19 and homo-circH19

circH19 was obtained from Geneseed Biotech (Guangdong, China). circRNAs were produced in vitro using a permuted intron‒exon (PIE) system with Anabaena group I introns. The homologous arm sequence, intron sequence, IRES sequence, H19 ORF sequence, P2A peptide sequence, luciferase sequence, 3′ UTR sequence, and spacer sequence were assembled and cloned and inserted into the puc19 plasmid backbone. For circH19 RNA synthesis, 1 μg of linearized plasmid template was mixed with other reaction components (3 μL of 10× Transcription Buffer, 4.5 μL of 25 mM NTP, and 6 U of T7 RNA Polymerase Mix) and incubated for 1 h at 37°C, after which the reactions were incubated at 42°C for 2 h to catalyze the cyclization of circRNAs. Then, the reactions were treated with DNase I (NEB, #M0303S) for 20 min to digest the DNA templates. After DNase treatment, the remaining RNA was purified with LiCl. Finally, the circular products were treated with RNase R (Geneseed, #R0301) at 37°C for 15 min to further enrich the circRNAs, which were subsequently purified with LiCl.

LNP encapsulation of circRNA

LNP encapsulation was performed by Geneseed Biotech (Guangdong, China). circH19 was encapsulated in LNPs according to a previously described process.82 Briefly, circRNA was diluted in sodium acetate buffer (pH = 5.2) and mixed with lipid solution through an R-SDM formulation screening cartridge (Micro & Nano Biologics Co., Ltd.) using INano L (Micro & Nano Biologics Co., Ltd.). The flow rate ratio of the circRNA solution to the lipid solution was 3:1, and the total flow rate was 12 mL/min. Then, the LNP-circRNA formulations were diluted with 1× PBS (pH = 7.2) and purified in 100-kDa ultrafiltration tubes (Amicon Ultra Centrifugal Filter Unit, Millipore) at 4°C. The encapsulation efficiency of LNP-circRNA and the concentration of encapsulated circRNA were measured with the Quant-it RiboGreen RNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen).

Quantification and statistical analysis

The experimental data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of at least three biological replicates. Unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test was used to determine the statistical significance of differences in parametric data. Paired two-tailed Student’s t tests were used for comparisons of parametric data between high-grade glioma tissues and paired adjacent tissue samples. The log rank test was applied to determine the significance of differences in survival data. A p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. The degree of significance is indicated as follows: ∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; and ∗∗∗p < 0.001. Statistical tests were carried out with GraphPad Prism (version 8).
