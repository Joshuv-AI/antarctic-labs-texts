Plasmon-enhanced nonlinear optical response in Au–SnS2 nanohybrids for optical limiting applications
Authors: Adarsh Raj K. P., Hasana Jahan Elamkulavan, Anoop C. Sathyadevan Nair, Abhinand Ramachandran, Abhijith Thazhathenair, Chandrasekharan Keloth, Vari Sivaji Reddy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Integrating plasmonic metal nanoparticles (NPs) with semiconductor nanostructures is widely used as an invaluable tool for tailoring the light–matter interaction and thereby improving the nonlinear optical activity of the latter. In the present work, inherent plasmonic effects of spherical Au NPs of size 20 nm were utilized to enhance the nonlinear optical absorption of SnS2 nanoplates of lateral size 890 nm. The pristine SnS2 nanoplates exhibited significant reverse saturable absorption with an effective nonlinear absorption coefficient (βeff) of 50 cm GW−1, which increased to 85 cm GW−1 after incorporation of Au NPs, when their nonlinear optical characteristics were investigated using an open-aperture Z-scan technique with a nanosecond pulsed laser operating at a wavelength of 532 nm. The enhancement in nonlinear optical absorption is mainly attributed to the strong influence of plasmonic hotspots of Au NPs, whose characteristic resonance range (450–600 nm) aligns with the laser wavelength. The interaction of such intense plasmonic fields with SnS2 nanoplates was further confirmed by simulating E-field intensity enhancement profiles using the finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) method. Furthermore, Au–SnS2 nanohybrids exhibited excellent optical limiting behavior with an onset value of 0.07 J cm−2 and a low limiting threshold of 1.16 J cm−2, which are found to be very significant compared to the values previously reported for eminent 2D materials and their nanohybrids. These findings highlight the significance of the present strategy in developing nanohybrids to enhance the nonlinear optical activity of conventional semiconductor nanosystems.

Introduction

Nanotechnology offers a viable route to overcome the limitations of individual semiconductor materials in controlling the inherent photophysical processes, including excitonic dynamics, free-charge-carrier generation, and their transport.1,2 Engineering the dimensionality of bulk semiconductors to the nanoscale induces new electronic, optical, and mechanical properties and thereby establishes a versatile platform to achieve enhanced tunability of these conventional characteristics.1 Owing to such superior characteristics attained via nanoscaling, the two-dimensional materials such as graphene, metal dichalcogenides (MDCs), and MXenes have been extensively explored for modern electronics, optoelectronics, energy storage and conversion, sensing technologies, and photonics.3,4 Due to the rapid growth in the use of high-power lasers in areas such as optical communication systems, industrial machining, military gadgets, and biomedical devices, there is an increasing demand for the development of high-performance laser safety materials. In this context, the 2D materials exhibiting third-order nonlinear absorption with high optical limiting activity are particularly suitable for the development of laser safety devices.5

The MDCs, an emerging class of 2D materials, exhibit variety of nonlinear optical (NLO) phenomena such as saturable absorption, reverse saturable absorption, second and third harmonic generation making them suitable for various interesting applications including frequency conversion, mode-locking, Q-switching, and optical limiting.6–8 The superior optical nonlinearity of MDCs arises from the layer dependent band gap, and strong excitonic effects.5,8 The chemical structure of MDCs is denoted as MX2, where M represents a metal atom (Mo, W, Nb, Sn, V, etc.) and X represents a chalcogenide atom (S, Se and Te). Here, the metal atom is sandwiched between two layers of chalcogenide atoms. Within these materials, the surface chalcogenide atoms exhibit no dangling bonds and are covalently bonded to the central metal atoms, resulting in excellent chemical stability.9

Among MDCs, SnS2 has been widely explored for diverse applications including solar cells,10 transistors,11 photodetectors,12 photocatalysis13 and sensors14 due to its superior optoelectronic properties, direct wide bandgap and environment friendly nature.10–15 The nonlinear optical properties such as reverse saturable absorption (RSA) and saturable absorption (SA) have been reported in the nanostructures of SnS2.16,17 Various methods like doping, defect engineering and hybrid formation were effectively used to enhance the NLO response of SnS2 nanostructures.17–20 Wu et al. reported nonlinear optical absorption in hydrothermally synthesized SnS2 nanosheets using the Z scan technique with nanosecond and picosecond laser pulses.16 An optical limiting threshold of 0.23 J cm−2 was obtained using 532 nm picosecond laser. Diao et al. achieved a nine-fold enhancement in nonlinear absorption coefficient in hydrothermally synthesized SnS2 nanosheets using Ar plasma treatment.18 Formation of in-gap states due to creation of sulfur vacancies during plasma treatment, which act as intermediate states that induce excited state absorption (ESA) during femtosecond laser excitation, was found to be responsible for the large improvement in nonlinear optical response. Wei et al. demonstrated covalent functionalization of SnS2 with porphyrin as an effective method for achieving ultrafast optical limiting.17 Ke et al. achieved enhancement in saturable absorption, two-photon absorption and three-photon absorption under femtosecond laser excitation across a broad wavelength range (515–1550 nm) using electrostatic doping of SnS2via proton intercalation for optical neural network applications.19 Li et al. constructed a SnS2/cetyltrimethylammonium superlattice and obtained significant nonlinear absorption enhancement across a broad wavelength range due to dielectric confinement and bandgap reduction effects.20

Plasmonic effects of metal nanostructures are widely utilized in solar cells,21 photodetectors,22 photocatalysis23 and SERS applications24 to boost light–matter interaction. The localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) arising from the collective oscillation of surface electrons in metal nanostructures enhances electromagnetic fields around the metal nanostructures.25 Especially, when metal nanostructures of size below 20 nm are incorporated in a medium, large electromagnetic fields generated around the nanostructures can significantly improve the optical properties of the surrounding medium.26 In some recent studies, the LSPR effects of metal nanostructures have been effectively used to improve the NLO properties of MDCs like MoS2 and WS2.27,28 Significant improvement in photochemical sensing, photodetection and photocatalytic activities of SnS2 nanostructures has been achieved by utilizing LSPR effects of Au nanostructures such as near-field enhancement and hot-electron injection.29–31 Though both these effects are useful for improving light–matter interaction, to the best of our knowledge, these effects are not utilized for enhancing NLO response of SnS2 nanostructures.

In the present study, plasmonic effects of Au NPs were successfully employed to improve the nonlinear optical absorption of SnS2 nanoplates. The size of chemically synthesized Au NPs was strategically tuned such that the laser wavelength lies perfectly within the plasmonic band of Au NPs to induce resonant absorption. The Raman spectroscopy and finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) simulations were employed to understand the role of Au NPs in improving the nonlinear optical response of SnS2 nanoplates. The simulation results revealed the generation of intense electric field around Au nanoparticles. The interaction of this plasmonic field with the SnS2 nanoplates resulted in improved nonlinear absorption leading to enhanced optical limiting properties. Through the correlation between experimental observations and simulation results, a mechanism was proposed to elucidate plasmon resonance enhanced nonlinear optical response in Au–SnS2 nanohybrids.

Experimental procedure

For the synthesis of Au–SnS2 nanohybrids, chemicals such as tin chloride pentahydrate (SnCl4·5H2O) (98%, Sigma-Aldrich), thiourea (CH4N2S) (99%, Sigma-Aldrich), gold(iii) chloride hydrate (HAuCl4·xH2O) (99.995%, Sigma-Aldrich), and tri-sodium citrate (HOC(COONa)(CH2COONa)2·2H2O) (99%, Merck) were purchased and used without any further purification. The SnS2 nanostructures were synthesized through a one-step hydrothermal process as schematically represented in Fig. 1(a).32 In this procedure, 2 mmol of tin chloride pentahydrate and 8 mmol of thiourea were mixed in 20 ml of DI water and stirred for 30 min until the solution becomes clear. The solution was then moved to a 100 ml autoclave reactor and maintained at 180 °C for 12 h in a hot air oven. Subsequently, the system was allowed to cool naturally and the yellow colour precipitate was collected by centrifugation. Here, an excess amount of thiourea was used to provide sulfur-rich environment which facilitates the controlled nucleation and growth of SnS2 nanoplates. The precipitate was washed several times with deionized water and ethanol to remove the unreacted precursors and impurities, followed by drying in vacuum at 60 °C for 6 h.

Schematic diagrams showing (a) synthesis of SnS2 nanoplates and (b) open aperture Z-scan experimental setup.

For the preparation of Au–SnS2 nanohybrid, Au nanoparticles were separately synthesized by citrate reduction method.33 In this method, gold(iii) chloride hydrate (HAuCl4·xH2O) was used as the Au precursor and trisodium citrate acted as both reducing and stabilising agent. Initially, 10 ml of 0.5 mM aqueous gold chloride was boiled using a magnetic stirrer-hot plate arrangement. Then, 0.5 ml of 38.8 mM trisodium citrate solution was injected into the boiling aqueous gold chloride solution. The colourless solution slowly changed to faint blue and then to red wine colour after 15 min of boiling indicating the formation of mono-dispersed Au nanoparticles. For the preparation of Au–SnS2 nanohybrids, the synthesized Au nanoparticles were directly used, without any further purification. The nanohybrids were prepared by adding 0.75 ml of Au NP solution (0.17 mg ml−1) to 1 ml dispersion of SnS2 nanoplates (0.5 mg ml−1), followed by continuous overnight stirring to ensure uniform mixing of the nanostructures. No additional purification was performed to remove Au nanoparticles that remained unattached to the SnS2 surface. The morphology of the nanostructures was analysed using a high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) (JEOL JEM 2100) and field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) (ZIESS, Gemini 1, Sigma 300). The formation and phase purity of SnS2 nanostructures were confirmed using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD) with Cu Kα radiation (Rigaku smart lab SE). The colloidal stability of the nanostructures was studied using the Zeta potential measurement (Microtrac, PMX 500). The linear absorption of the nanostructures was recorded using an UV-visible spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer Lambda 365+). For this study, the Au nanoparticle and SnS2 nanoplate solutions were prepared with concentrations 0.017 mg ml−1 and 0.029 mg ml−1, respectively, by suitably diluting initial solutions with DI water. In the case of Au–SnS2 nanohybrid, 1 ml solution contained 0.029 mg of SnS2 nanoplates and 0.0073 mg of Au nanoparticles. The electric field intensity distribution around the Au NPs was simulated using a commercially available Ansys Lumerical FDTD simulation software.

To investigate the NLO properties of SnS2 nanoplates, open aperture Z-scan measurements were carried out with a Q-switched Nd:YAG pulsed laser (Quanta Ray, Spectra Physics) operating at 532 nm wavelength with a 7 ns pulse width and 10 Hz repetition rate. Fig. 1(b) shows the schematic diagram of Z-scan arrangement, where a convex lens of 15 cm focal length was used to focus the beam onto the sample. Before the measurement, sample was taken in a cuvette of thickness 1 mm and diluted using DI water to obtain 80% linear transmittance. The diluted Au nanoparticle and SnS2 nanoplate solutions have concentrations of 0.17 mg ml−1 and 0.05 mg ml−1, respectively. On other hand, 1 ml of nanohybrid contained 0.0435 mg of SnS2 nanoplates and 0.011 mg of Au nanoparticles. Then the sample was fixed at the focal plane where the beam waist and Rayleigh range were 17.5 µm and 1.8 mm, respectively. The nonlinear scattering was found negligible when measured using a detector positioned at 45° from the sample. The Z-scan measurements were performed at two pulse energies, 10 µJ and 20 µJ, corresponding to peak on-axis intensities of 0.27 GW cm−2 and 0.55 GW cm−2, respectively. The incident pulse fluence was varied by adjusting the output pulse energy of the laser while maintaining identical experimental conditions. The pulse energy corresponding to each measurement was monitored using a calibrated laser energy meter to ensure accurate determination of the incident fluence. The Raman spectra of the SnS2 nanoplates and Au–SnS2 nanohybrid were recorded using a HORIBRA, LabRam HR Evo confocal Raman spectrometer operating at a 532 nm excitation wavelength and 5 mW excitation power. The concentrations of solutions used for the measurement of Raman spectra are same as those used for Z-scan measurements.

Results and discussion

The morphology of hydrothermally synthesized SnS2 nanostructures was studied using FE-SEM and HR-TEM. The FE-SEM image shown in Fig. 2(a) and the size distribution histogram presented in Fig. S1 reveal the formation of hexagonal SnS2 nanoplates with an average size of 890 nm. Further, the approximate thickness of the nanoplates was found to be around 70 nm as shown in Fig. S2. The EDS spectrum shown in Fig. S3 reveals the presence of Sn and S in the nanostructures with a ratio of 1 : 1.54. The EDS colour mapping images presented in Fig. S4 confirm the uniform distribution of Sn and S in the sample. The hexagonal SnS2 nanoplates with sharp edges were also observed in the HR-TEM image shown in Fig. 2(b). The lattice fringes with a spacing of 0.32 nm shown in the inset of Fig. 2(b) correspond to (100) planes of hexagonal SnS2.34 The SAED pattern recorded on a single SnS2 nanoplate contains an ordered array of sharp spots confirming the single crystalline nature of the SnS2 nanoplates (Fig. 2(c)). From the SAED pattern, d-spacing values of 0.32 nm and 0.18 nm were obtained which match well with the (100) and (110) planes of hexagonal phase of SnS2.34 The crystal structure of SnS2 nanoplates was further analysed using XRD measurements. The XRD pattern shown in Fig. S5 contains peaks at 15.02°, 28.23°, 32.12°, 41.89, 46.12°, 59.96°, 52.41°, 55.17°, 60.66° and 67.37° corresponding to the hexagonal crystal structure of SnS2 (JCPDS No. 023-0677).35 In addition, the absence of any impurity peaks in the XRD pattern confirms the formation of high purity SnS2 nanostructures.

The Au nanoparticles were synthesized using citrate reduction method as it offers excellent control over the size and shape of nanostructures compared to other methods like pulsed laser ablation.36 The HR-TEM image of synthesized Au NPs is shown in Fig. 2(d). Highly uniform spherical nanoparticles with an average diameter of 20 nm were observed as shown in the size distribution histogram presented in Fig. S6. Presence of lattice fringes with a d-spacing value of 0.23 nm was observed in the magnified HR-TEM image shown in Fig. 2(e) which correspond to (111) planes of fcc gold.37 Concentric rings observed in the SAED pattern presented in Fig. 2(f) indicate that the synthesized Au nanoparticles are polycrystalline. The HR-TEM image of Au–SnS2 nanohybrid shown in Fig. 2(g) and S7 reveals the uniform distribution of Au nanoparticles on the surface of SnS2 nanoplates. The lattice fringes corresponding to both Au and SnS2 were observed in the magnified HR-TEM image of Au–SnS2 nanohybrid shown in Fig. 2(h). On the other hand, the SAED pattern of nanohybrid mainly contained a systematic array of spots corresponding to SnS2 nanoplates due to the low concentration of Au NPs (Fig. 2(i)).

The linear optical properties of Au NPs, SnS2 nanoplates and Au–SnS2 nanohybrid were studied by measuring UV-vis absorption spectra and are presented in Fig. 3(a). The SnS2 nanoplates exhibited a broad absorption in the visible range which increased sharply below 500 nm. A band gap energy of 2.23 eV was obtained for SnS2 nanoplate sample using Tauc plot method as shown in Fig. S8 which is in agreement with earlier reports.38 On the other hand, Au NPs showed a distinct plasmonic band in the wavelength range 450–600 nm with a peak at 521 nm. This strong absorption peak arises from the collective oscillation of conduction electrons on the surface of Au NPs during the excitation of light. The absorption spectrum of Au–SnS2 nanohybrid contained the combined characteristics of both the Au NPs and SnS2 nanoplates. In spite of the lower amount of Au NPs in the Au–SnS2 nanohybrid, the absorption spectrum contained a clear plasmonic band which is beneficial for improving the nonlinear optical properties of SnS2 nanoplates.

(a) UV-visible absorption spectra of SnS2 nanoplates, Au NPs and Au–SnS2 nanohybrid. (b) Raman spectra of SnS2 nanoplates and Au–SnS2 nanohybrid.

To further understand the surface plasmon resonance effect of Au NPs, Raman spectra of the samples were recorded and presented in Fig. 3(b). The Raman spectra of both pristine SnS2 and Au–SnS2 nanohybrid contained a sharp peak at 314 cm−1 that corresponds to A1g mode representing out of plane vibration of S atoms in opposite directions with respect to Sn atom.35 The Au–SnS2 nanohybrid exhibited significantly higher intensity compared to pure SnS2 nanoplates, which is attributed to the LSPR effect of Au NPs distributed on the surface of SnS2 nanoplates. However, no additional peaks or shifting of peaks were observed in both the absorption and Raman spectra of Au–SnS2 nanohybrid indicating that there is no strong chemical interaction between the Au nanoparticles and the SnS2 nanoplates. A weak van-der-Waals type physical interaction is expected between Au nanoparticles and the SnS2 nanoplates.

Prior to nonlinear optical measurements, colloidal stability of the samples prepared in DI water was tested by measuring the zeta-potential values. The Au nanoparticles, SnS2 nanoplates and the Au–SnS2 nanohybrids exhibited average zeta-potential values of −70.5 mV, −50.2 mV and −66.4 mV, respectively. These values indicate high colloidal stability of all the samples which is essential for conducting optical measurements over a long period. Moreover, the Au–SnS2 nanohybrid has shown high stability under laser light illumination and also with time as shown in Fig. S9. The nonlinear optical properties of pure nanostructures and the nanohybrid were investigated using open aperture Z-scan measurements with a Q-switched Nd:YAG laser operated at 532 nm excitation wavelength as schematically shown in Fig. 1(b). The NLO response of SnS2 nanoplates, Au NPs and Au–SnS2 nanohybrids at two different pulse energies 10 µJ and 20 µJ is presented in Fig. 4(a) and (b). At 10 µJ pulse energy, Au NPs did not exhibit any noticeable nonlinear response. Whereas, in the case of SnS2 nanoplates and Au–SnS2 nanohybrid a continuous decrease in the transmittance was noticed when the sample was moved closer to the focus with a minimum transmittance at the focus, indicating the presence of RSA-like behaviour. The minimum transmittance at 10 µJ pulse energy was found to be 0.60 and 0.49 for SnS2 nanoplates and Au–SnS2 nanohybrid, respectively.

Nonlinear optical response SnS2, Au–SnS2 and Au nanostructures at a pulse energy of (a) 10 µJ and (b) 20 µJ.

The uncertainty in the extracted nonlinear optical parameters was estimated to be within approximately 7%, based on the established experimental uncertainty of our open aperture Z-scan setup, as reported in our earlier work.39 This uncertainty mainly arises from pulse-to-pulse laser energy fluctuations, beam alignment, detector response, and fitting uncertainties. The nonlinear absorption coefficient of pure SnS2 nanoplate sample at 10 µJ pulse energy was found to be 50 cm GW−1. Significant improvement in the nonlinear optical response of SnS2 nanoplates was noticed after adding Au NPs leading to a high βeff value of 85 cm GW−1. Although Au NPs alone have not shown any considerable NLO response at 10 µJ energy, their incorporation into SnS2 nanoplates resulted in 1.7 times enhancement in the value of βeff. In addition, the Au–SnS2 nanohybrid exhibited significantly lower Isat values than the pristine SnS2 nanoplates at both investigated pulse energies, indicating a lower characteristic saturation intensity. Together with the enhanced βeff, these results indicate an improved nonlinear optical response upon Au incorporation.

The nonlinear optical response of the samples at 20 µJ pulse energy is shown in Fig. 4(b). Unlike at the 10 µJ energy, Au NPs exhibited a significant nonlinear absorption at 20 µJ energy, with two humps appearing on either side of the valley in Z-scan profile. This feature indicates an interplay of SA and RSA with SA dominating away from the focal point and RSA leading at the focal point. The SA response arises from the depletion of ground state population due to resonant absorption of Au nanoparticles which prevents further absorption leading to slight increase in transmittance.40 Further increase in intensity resulted in sharp reduction in the transmittance value leading to RSA-like behavior. The βeff value of pure Au NPs was found to be 9 cm GW−1 at 20 µJ pulse energy. While Au NPs exhibited improved nonlinear absorption at 20 µJ pulse energy, it is significantly weaker than that of bare SnS2 nanoplates which showed a βeff value of 37 cm GW−1. On the other hand, the Au–SnS2 nanohybrid showed an enhanced nonlinear activity with a βeff of 56 cm GW−1, which is 1.5 times higher than the bare SnS2 nanoplates at 20 µJ pulse energy.

Enhancement in the intensity of SnS2 Raman peak after incorporating Au NPs indicates that there is improvement in light–matter interaction due to the plasmonic effects of nanoparticles. To further understand the role of plasmonic effects in improving the nonlinear absorption, FDTD simulations were performed on Au–SnS2 nanohybrid. The morphological characterization of Au–SnS2 nanohybrid revealed the presence of isolated Au NPs of size 20 nm on the surface of hexagonal SnS2 nanoplates. In addition, nanoparticles separated by smaller gaps were also observed in few locations. Therefore, to match with the experimental observations, a pair of 20 nm sized Au NPs separated by various possible distances in the range 1–20 nm were modelled. The Au NPs were placed on an SnS2 nanoplate of thickness 70 nm kept on the x − y plane as shown in Fig. 5(a). The simulation structure was illuminated using a total-field scattered-field (TFSF) light source with a wavelength of λ = 532 nm, E-field polarization along the x-axis (Ex), and propagation along the -z direction (kz). The simulations were performed by taking the refractive index and extinction coefficient of SnS2 at 532 nm wavelength as 2.97 and 0.029, respectively, as reported by Ermolaev et al.41 The E-field intensity enhancement during illumination was monitored on x − y, y − z and x − z cross-sections using five frequency domain power monitors as indicated in Fig. 5(a). To attain high accuracy in simulation results, a mesh size of 0.5 nm was used in the simulation region. The simulations were performed by applying the perfectly matched layer (PML) boundary conditions along z-direction and symmetric boundary conditions in both x- and y-directions. The simulation results corresponding to the particle separations, d = 2, 5 and 10 nm are presented in Fig. 5(b–d). In the case of larger separations above 10 nm, very strong plasmonic field was observed on the x − y and x − z cross-sections as shown in Fig. 5(b) and (c). These profiles clearly indicate that the induced E-field is mainly concentrated on the lateral sides of the Au nanoparticles in the x-direction. On the other hand, the E-field was found to be relatively weaker on the y − z cross-section since the incident light polarization is along x direction. When the particle separation was decreased below 5 nm, formation of hotspots (high E-field intensity regions) was noticed due to the coupling of plasmon oscillations in neighbouring nanoparticles. The presence of strong E-field was observed on all the three cross-sections x − y, x − z and y − z as shown in Fig. 5(b–d). Interaction of this intense plasmonic field with the SnS2 nanoplates can significantly boost the light–matter interaction in Au–SnS2 nanohybrid.

The nonlinear absorption phenomena in pristine SnS2 nanoplates is schematically illustrated in Fig. 6(a). Here, S0, S1 and S2 represent the ground state, first excited state and higher excited state, respectively. As the band gap energy of SnS2 nanoplates is slightly lower than the incident photon energy, the laser pulse can enable efficient excitation of electrons from S0 to S1 (ground state absorption). Following this, if the electron in state S1 absorbs an additional photon, it will be excited to the higher energy state S2 leading to a phenomenon called excited state absorption as shown in Fig. 6(a). A reduction in the βeff value from 50 cm GW−1 to 37 cm GW−1 was observed as the pulse energy increased from 10 µJ to 20 µJ. Similar intensity-dependent behaviour has been reported for SnS2 nanosheets and other nonlinear optical nanomaterials under nanosecond excitation, where ESA was identified as the dominant nonlinear absorption mechanism.16,42 However, the contribution of two-photon absorption cannot be completely ruled out. As shown in Fig. 3(a), the wavelength of the laser photons lies well within the plasmonic absorption band of Au NPs used in the present study, which indicates that there is a high probability for resonant absorption. When the laser pulses are incident on Au–SnS2 nanohybrid, strong electromagnetic fields will be generated around the Au NPs due to localized surface plasmon resonance effect as shown in Fig. 5. Interaction of this intense plasmonic field with the surrounding SnS2 nanoplates enhances excitation of electrons from (i) S0 to S1 and (ii) S1 to S2 as schematically represented in Fig. 6(b). In addition, the Au NPs can also act as suppliers of hot-electrons. Hot-electron injection from metal nanoparticles to various MDC semiconductors such as MoS2, WS2 and WSe2 has been reported in earlier studies.46,47 According to the literature, SnS2 exhibits lower work function (4.4–4.9 eV)48,49 compared to Au (5.1 eV). Therefore, when Au NPs make intimate contact with the SnS2 nanoplates in the nanohybrid, electrons from the conduction band of SnS2 will flow to Au side till their Fermi levels are equal at equilibrium, leading to the band bending and Schottky barrier formation at the Au/SnS2 interface. During laser light illumination, when plasmons in Au nanoparticle decay nonradiatively, the resulting high energetic electrons may overcome the energy barrier at the Au/SnS2 interface and move to the excited state S1 of SnS2 as shown in Fig. 6(b). Rise in the electron population in the state S1 due to the above-mentioned factors further enhance excited state absorption. Hence, the Au–SnS2 nanohybrid exhibits superior RSA-like behaviour compared to the pristine SnS2 nanoplates.

The enhanced nonlinear absorption exhibited by Au–SnS2 nanohybrid is beneficial for optical limiting applications. The materials with strong optical limiting behavior, which transmit low intensity radiation and decrease the transmittance at higher intensities, are highly desirable for the development of laser safety devices that protect optical sensors and human from high power laser radiation. The performance of an optical limiting material is characterized by the onset value and limiting threshold. The onset value refers to the minimum input fluence at which the transmittance starts decreasing from its initial constant value. On other hand, the limiting threshold is defined as the input fluence at which the transmittance reduces to half of its initial value. To understand the optical limiting properties of the samples, variation of normalized transmittance with input fluence is carefully analyzed at 10 µJ pulse energy and presented in Fig. 7. In the case of bare SnS2 nanoplates, though there is a significant optical limiting behavior, the transmittance has not reached the limiting threshold. On the other hand, due to the plasmon-enhanced light–matter interaction, Au–SnS2 nanohybrid has shown strong optical limiting activity exhibiting onset value and limiting threshold of 0.07 J cm−2 and 1.16 J cm−2, respectively. A comparative study presented in Table 2 indicates that the limiting threshold and onset values obtained in this work are significant compared to the values previously reported for similar 2D materials and their nanohybrids. Present study reveals that utilizing plasmonic effects of metal nanostructures is a powerful technique to improve the NLO properties of MDC nanostructures.

Conclusion

In summary, spherical Au NPs of diameter 20 nm and hexagonal SnS2 nanoplates of average size 890 nm were synthesized using simple and cost-effective chemical methods. Their morphological, structural and optical properties were systematically investigated. The Au–SnS2 nanohybrids were prepared by mixing pre-synthesized Au NPs and SnS2 nanoplates. The NLO response of pristine nanostructures and the nanohybrid was recorded using Z-scan technique with a pulsed laser operating at 532 nm wavelength. Substantial enhancement in nonlinear absorption of SnS2 nanoplates leading to improved optical limiting activity was observed after incorporating Au NPs. The superior optical limiting behavior exhibited by Au–SnS2 nanohybrids is attributed to enhancement in excited state absorption due to the plasmonic effects of Au NPs. In addition, theoretical simulations were used to unveil the mechanism for enhancement of NLO activity in the nanohybrid. Present study demonstrates that the application potential of MDCs in the area of photonic devices can be improved by combining their inherent properties with the plasmonic effects of metal nanostructures.
