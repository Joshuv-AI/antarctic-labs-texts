The impact of contextual effects in exercise therapy for low back pain: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Aaltje de Roode, Martijn W Heymans, Wim van Lankveld, J Bart Staal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLow back pain is the leading cause of global disability for which exercise therapy is a widely recommended treatment. Research indicates that contextual factors may also influence treatment outcomes in low back pain. Examples include the patient-therapist relationship and other treatment-related circumstances that affect patient expectations. By focusing on the specific treatment effect, clinical trials often ignore the effect of contextual factors, thereby contributing to the so-called efficacy paradox. This means that treatment effects observed in clinical practice are often greater than those reported in clinical trials.This systematic review aims to investigate the proportion of improvement in pain and disability that can be attributed to contextual effects in the outcome of exercise therapy for patients with low back pain.MethodsA meta-analysis was conducted. PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane database were searched for eligible articles reporting randomized controlled trials that compared exercise therapy to placebo interventions. Risk of bias was assessed with the Revised Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool. Outcomes of interest were pain and disability. Meta-analysis was carried out to calculate the proportion attributable to contextual effects for both pain and disability. The body of evidence was assessed using the GRADE methodology.ResultsEight studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the meta-analysis. Five studies were rated as having a moderate risk of bias and two studies had a low risk of bias. Proportion attributable to contextual effects was 0.60 (95% CI 0.40–0.89) for pain and 0.69 (95% CI 0.48–1.00) for disability. Certainty of the evidence as assessed with the GRADE methodology was low.ConclusionsA large extent of pain and disability improvement after exercise therapy in low back pain is attributable to contextual effects although this conclusion is based on low certainty evidence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-024-03679-3.Keywords: Low back pain, Exercise, Placebo, Contextual factors

Background

Low back pain is the most common musculoskeletal complaint worldwide. It is also the leading global cause of years lived with disability, and this disability results in enormous economic costs [1]. In at least 95% of all cases of low back pain, the low back pain is considered nonspecific, meaning not attributable to a known cause or illness [2]. Many people with low back pain, particularly in primary care settings, are treated with physiotherapy and then in particular exercise therapy. A systematic review of clinical practice guidelines by Oliveira et al. found that primary care guidelines for low back pain recommend exercise therapy, although the type of exercise recommended varies widely [3, 4].

There is consensus that exercise therapy is effective in improving pain and disability in low back pain, but effect sizes in research tend to be small or moderate [4–6]. In their Cochrane systematic review, Hayden et al. found a mean difference of − 15.2 points (95% confidence interval (CI) − 18.3 to − 12.2) for pain and a mean difference of − 6.8 (95% CI − 8.3 to − 5.3) for physical functioning (the inverse of disability), both on a scale of 0–100 favoring exercise therapy compared to no treatment, placebo treatment, or usual care [4].

Research into exercise therapy for low back pain usually aims to determine the specific effect of exercise therapy. In clinical practice, however, the observed change after treatment is also influenced by the natural course of the disease, regression to the mean, and contextual factors related to the context in which a treatment is delivered, such as the patient-therapist relationship and other treatment-related circumstances that affect patient expectations [7]. The contextual factors can be viewed as physical, psychological, and social elements that characterize the therapeutic encounter with the patient and may induce placebo or nocebo effects [8]. All these effects, together with the specific treatment effect, add up to the overall treatment effect that is relevant to the patient.

Effects induced by contextual factors can also be called placebo or nocebo effects depending on the direction of the effects. In explanatory randomized controlled trials (RCTs), the specific effect of an intervention is expressed as the observed change over and above that observed in the placebo control group. To isolate specific treatment effects, randomized placebo-controlled trials subtract the observed change in the placebo group from the observed change in the active treatment group and report the difference as the effect of treatment. As a result, reported intervention effects may tend to be small, while the total observed change in the treatment group (i.e., the improvement over time in patients receiving the active intervention) may be much larger.

In clinical practice, the physiotherapist administering exercise therapy might therefore observe good results, despite the relatively small effects found in RCTs and meta-analyses. These good results constitute the accumulated effects of the natural course of the disease, regression to the mean, contextual factors, and the specific effects of the intervention. This difference between effect sizes found in research and those observed in practice is called the efficacy paradox [9].

Contextual factors are known to influence the outcome of physiotherapy treatments [8, 10]. There is evidence that these contextual factors also affect the outcome of low back pain treatment. Several studies have shown that for example therapeutic alliance and perceived treatment credibility are associated with an improved outcome [11–17]. Moreover, over the past few years, some systematic reviews have been published on the role of the placebo effect in pain populations including low back pain. In these reviews placebo treatments have been compared to no intervention groups. The reviews conclude that placebo effects are present but probably short-lived and not great [18–21]. Some other reviews using a different approach have found that contextual effects can explain a sizable part of the treatment effect. Zou et al. showed that the magnitude of contextual effects in the treatment of osteoarthritis is up to 75% of the overall treatment effect by calculating the proportion attributable to contextual effects (PCE) [22]. In other conditions, such as fibromyalgia, the PCE is also significant, with 60% of pain reduction explained by contextual effects [23].

The PCE is a measure calculated by dividing the observed change in the placebo group (i.e., placebo effect, natural course, and regression to the mean) by the total observed change in the intervention group (i.e., specific treatment effect, placebo effect, natural course, and regression to the mean). The PCE indicates what proportion of the observed change in the active treatment group can be achieved by the placebo intervention [24]. A score of 0 means that none of the observed changes in the treatment group can be attributed to contextual factors, while a PCE score of 1 means that the total observed change in the treatment group can be attributed to contextual factors. Thus, a higher PCE means a greater effect of contextual factors [22–24]. In rare cases, a PCE greater than 1 indicates a larger observed change in the placebo group than in the active intervention group [24].

The extent of these contextual effects has not been investigated in physiotherapy treatment of low back pain so far. It is important to gain an understanding of contextual effects and their magnitude in the treatment of low back pain, in order to influence them [12]. A recent meta-epidemiological study by Tsutsumi et al. found a PCE of 0.65 in their analysis of 328 Cochrane reviews for a wide range of conditions and treatments. They also found that PCEs can vary considerably and advised that for every condition, intervention, and outcome the PCE should be assessed in its context [24]. To date, clinical trials on low back pain and exercise have primarily focused on the specific effect of exercise interventions. However, if contextual effects determine a large proportion of the outcome it would be very important to focus much more on this part of the treatment effect, both in research and in clinical practice.

Therefore, the aim of this systematic review is to investigate the PCE for improvement in pain and disability as an outcome of exercise therapy for patients with low back pain. The primary outcome of interest is pain because it is the most prevalent complaint of low back pain [1, 25]. The secondary outcome is disability because it is a commonly used outcome in low back pain studies and measures the impact of the disease [25].

Methods

Design

To answer the research question, a systematic review of placebo-controlled RCTs was conducted. A meta-analysis was performed to calculate the PCE of pain and disability improvement. The systematic review was conducted according to the PRISMA statement for systematic reviews and meta-analysis [26]. The protocol of the systematic review was registered in the Prospero database in July 2021 under the following registration number: PROSPERO 2021 CRD42021266899.

Information sources

The electronic databases PubMed/Medline, Embase, and Cochrane were searched for eligible articles from the dates the databases started up to February 2, 2024.

Search strategy

The search itself had no language restrictions and was carried out in the databases PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane controlled trial register. The following MeSH terms and keywords were used: “low back pain,” “exercise therapy,” “pain,” and “placebos.” The complete search strategy can be found in Additional file 1.

Articles were eligible for inclusion if they (1) were RCTs; (2) included patients aged 16 years or older with nonspecific low back pain; (3) had at least one group that received exercise therapy; and (4) had at least one group that received a placebo treatment. Nonspecific low back pain was defined as pain, typically located between the lower rib margins and the buttock creases, from which the specific nociceptive source cannot be identified [2, 27]. Exercise therapy was defined as any form of supervised therapy in which participants performed prescribed physical exercises. Articles where patients received co-interventions besides exercise therapy were excluded. We considered a treatment to be a placebo treatment if it was reported as such by the authors. Articles were excluded if they were not available in full text and if they were not published in English or Dutch.

Selection of studies

Two independent reviewers (AR and JK) conducted the inclusion of eligible articles. Initially, articles were screened for eligibility based on title and abstract. If titles and abstracts indicated that an article was potentially eligible for inclusion, a full paper copy of the report was obtained. Disagreements between the reviewers regarding an article’s eligibility were resolved by discussion until consensus was reached. Additionally, reference tracking was performed on all included articles to identify potentially additional relevant studies.

Data extraction and management

The reviewers extracted data using a standard extraction form. If studies had more than two arms, the arms that were irrelevant to the research question, such as arms with no treatment, were excluded. Data extracted from the included articles were (1) authors, publication year, and study design; (2) participants, including age and type and/or duration of low back pain; (3) supervised exercise therapy; (4) placebo intervention; (5) duration and frequency of the intervention; (6) pain and disability measurement instrument; and (7) pain and disability score at baseline and follow-up for both interventions. To calculate within-group effect sizes, the mean change from baseline and its within-group standard deviation were collected and, if not reported, calculated from the outcomes at baseline and follow-up [28]. In case of randomized cross-over trials, all measurements from the intervention periods and all measurements from the control periods were collected and analyzed as if the trial were a parallel-group trial [28]. If data were missing or further information was required, the first two authors were contacted to request the required information.

Assessment of risk of bias

Two reviewers (AR and IF) independently assessed the risk of bias of the included articles. The risk of bias was scored using the Revised Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool [28, 29]. Disagreements were resolved in a consensus meeting between the two raters. The strength of interrater agreement was measured by Cohen’s κ coefficient (95% confidence interval (CI)), with κ = 0.41–0.60 indicating moderate agreement, κ = 0.61–0.80 representing good agreement, and κ ≥ 0.81 representing very good agreement [30, 31].

Meta-analysis

The PCE was calculated according to Tsutsumi et al. [24]. The effect size was calculated as the within-group mean change scores for pain and disability and standardized by dividing this value by the within-group standard deviation [24, 28]. Pain and disability outcome scores were used to calculate mean changes from baseline [32]. The correlation between baseline and endpoint is needed to calculate the within-group standard deviation. If it was not reported in a study, r was calculated with data from another similar study in a similar population [33]. The overall exercise therapy improvement was defined as the effect size of the exercise therapy group. The contextual effect was defined as the effect size of the placebo treatment group. The follow-up measurement closest to 4 weeks from baseline was chosen to calculate the effect sizes.

The PCE was calculated using the effect size ratio between the contextual effect and the overall exercise therapy improvement [24]. Statistical heterogeneity was determined to quantify the extent to which the variation observed among the included studies was due to heterogeneity as opposed to chance. It was assessed using the chi-squared test, with p ≤ 0.1 indicating heterogeneity. The I2 statistic was used to determine the degree of heterogeneity [28, 34]. Random effect meta-analysis with inverse variance was used because of heterogeneity in exercise therapy parameters [28]. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test if at least ten studies were included in the meta-analysis [28, 35]. All statistical tests were performed using R version 4.2.2.

Assessment of the body of evidence

The certainty of the body of evidence was assessed using the GRADE methodology for the two comparisons: effects of exercise versus placebo for pain and effects of exercise versus placebo for disability. Potential reasons for downgrading were study limitations, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias [28]. We applied and interpreted the rules as described in the Cochrane Handbook for Interventions [28] and in a paper by Strijkers et al. on the effects of placebo interventions in low back pain [19].

The following rules were followed:

Limitations: one level if 50% to 75% of included trials scored “low or some concerns” on risk of bias and two levels if less than 50% of included trials scored “low or some concerns” on risk of bias.

Limitations: one level if 50% to 75% of included trials scored “low or some concerns” on risk of bias and two levels if less than 50% of included trials scored “low or some concerns” on risk of bias.

Inconsistency: one level if the I2 statistic was greater than 40%.

Inconsistency: one level if the I2 statistic was greater than 40%.

Indirectness: one level if the target population (adult patients with nonspecific low back pain) was unclear, or if it was unclear whether the interventions could be considered exercise or placebo interventions, or if they differed substantially across trials.

Indirectness: one level if the target population (adult patients with nonspecific low back pain) was unclear, or if it was unclear whether the interventions could be considered exercise or placebo interventions, or if they differed substantially across trials.

Imprecision: one level if the total sample for a specific outcome was less than 400 patients.

Imprecision: one level if the total sample for a specific outcome was less than 400 patients.

Publication bias: one level if the funnel plot indicates that publication bias could be present. In case funnel plots could not be inspected (less than 10 studies) this domain was considered low risk.

Results

Study selection

A total of 508 studies were identified in PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane on February 3rd, 2024. After removal of duplicates, 240 articles remained and were screened on title and abstract. Twelve studies were assessed on full text and five of these were removed for reasons described in the flowchart (see Fig. 1). Eight studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in this systematic review [36–43]. Reference tracking was performed but generated no new studies. The selection procedure is summarized in a PRISMA flowchart (see Fig. 1) [26].

Flowscheme of included studies. PRISMA 2020 flow diagram

Study characteristics

The included studies were published between 1993 and 2017, two of which were crossover trials [38, 42]. A total of 1149 participants with nonspecific low back pain were included. One study with 318 participants with acute low back pain (< 4 weeks) [36], one study with 259 participants with subacute low back pain (> 4 weeks and < 3 months) [37], and three studies with a total of 326 participants with chronic low back pain (> 3 months) [38–40]. In three studies including 246 participants, the duration of low back pain was not clearly reported [41–43].

Exercise therapy varied across studies and ranged from one session of segmental stabilization exercises to twelve sessions of aerobic exercises. Two studies reported that participants received advice in addition to exercise therapy [40, 42], and one study added posture education to exercise therapy [41]. Five studies used similar placebo treatments: detuned or lowest dose ultrasonography [36], low-level laser therapy [41], short-wave diathermy [37], or a combination of these placebo treatments [39, 40]. Other placebo treatments were passive automated cycling, prone lying, semi-hot packs, and intermittent traction [38, 42, 43]. Two studies checked the credibility of their placebo treatments and this showed that patients found the placebo interventions credible (i.e., the same credibility as the active interventions) [37, 39].

Pain was measured with the visual analog scale (VAS), Numeric (Pain) Rating Scale (N(P)RS), and Present Pain Index (PPI). Disability was measured with the Roland Morris Disability Questionnaire in four studies [37, 39–41]. The follow-up period ranged from measurements directly after treatment to 12 months follow-up. As mentioned above, the measurement point closest to 4 weeks follow-up was used for the meta-analyses. An overview of the characteristics of the included studies is given in Table 1.

Risk of bias

The strength of interrater agreement was moderate with Kappa k = 0.481 (95% CI − 0.062–1.024). There were some concerns about the risk of bias on elements in all but two of the included studies [39, 40]. Most risk of bias was related to the selection of the reported results not being verifiable due to a lack of pre-published study protocols [36, 38, 41–43]. Two studies did not describe whether allocation to a treatment was concealed until participants were enrolled [38, 42] and two studies did not describe the handling of missing data [38, 43]. An overview of the risk of bias assessment is provided in Fig. 2.

Results of individual studies

All studies reported improvement in pain and disability in both the exercise and placebo groups. Three studies reported no significant differences in outcome between exercise and placebo treatment [36, 41, 43]. Five studies reported small significant differences in outcome between exercise and placebo treatment [37–40, 42]. The results of the individual studies are presented in Table 1.

Meta-analysis

One study could not be included in the meta-analysis due to missing outcome information [43]. The PCE was calculated both for the individual study results and for the pooled results. The PCE per study ranged from 0.17 to 1.05. The pooled standardized mean difference of exercise therapy was 1.15 for pain (95% CI 0.57 to 1.74) and 0.55 (95% CI 0.28 to 0.82) for disability. The pooled PCE of exercise therapy was 0.60 (95% CI 0.40–0.89) for pain and 0.69 (95% CI 0.48–1.00) for disability. Forest plots of the PCE for both pain and disability are presented in Fig. 3A and B.

For pain, the statistical heterogeneity of the PCE was statistically significant with p = 0.04 and I2 = 59.21%. For disability, the statistical heterogeneity of the PCE was not statistically significant with p = 0.70 and I2 = 0%.

Discussion

The aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to investigate the proportion of pain and disability improvement attributable to contextual effects in exercise therapy for patients with low back pain. The PCE of the pooled effect size was 0.60 for pain and 0.69 for disability. This means that 60% of the pain reduction and 69% of disability reduction after exercise therapy for low back pain can be explained by contextual effects. For the interpretation of the magnitude of these PCEs, one should keep in mind that the effects reported for exercise therapy in low back pain are small and that their clinical importance can be disputed (4). Sixty or 69% of a small effect is a small effect. Furthermore, the certainty of evidence we found was low which implies that the true effect might be markedly different from the effect estimated in our review.

A major strength of the present review is, that it assesses the magnitude of the contextual effect as part of exercise therapy in a head-to-head comparison by calculating a ratio. Two other systematic reviews took a different approach and compared placebo treatment to no treatment in low back pain. A review by Strijkers et al. included RCTs and another review by Van Lennep et al. included both RCTs and experimental studies in low back pain [19, 44]. Both meta-analyses reported positive significant short-term effects of placebo treatments versus no treatment in chronic low back pain [19, 44]. A recently published systematic review included only three-arm RCTs comparing the effects of conservative, mainly passive, interventions versus placebo interventions versus no interventions in chronic low back pain. On average, participants allocated to all three groups improved in pain and function but the largest improvements were attributable to changes observed with no treatment [18]. Another systematic review and narrative synthesis by Sherriff et al. focused on intervention studies in low back pain, not limited to RCTs, that specifically manipulated certain contextual factors such as patient beliefs and patient-practitioner relationships. Overall, positive effects were found for pain and physical functioning, although the lack of a meta-analysis limited the interpretation of the results [45].

Our findings are comparable to those of the meta-analysis by Zou et al. and the meta-analysis by Whiteside et al., both of which investigated the PCE in pain populations. Zou et al. found a PCE of 0.75 for osteoarthritis pain treatments and Whiteside et al. found a PCE of 0.60 for fibromyalgia pain treatment [22, 23]. Both systematic reviews included RCTs on pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatments in their calculation of the PCE. The PCE for exercise treatment in low back pain has never been investigated and our study shows that similar proportions apply to this population.

The type of exercises and placebo treatments differed between studies, although the duration and number of sessions for the intervention and placebo arms were held constant within the studies. The two crossover studies included only one intervention session, whereas the other study included between six and twelve sessions [38, 42]. Our analysis approach for the two randomized cross-over trials, to collect all measurements from the intervention and control periods as if the trial were a parallel group trial, is simple and most feasible given the data usually presented in publications. However, it is generally not recommended and can therefore be seen as a limitation [28]. On the other hand, this approach is considered conservative and is likely to result in wider confidence intervals and under- rather than over-weighting of trials [28]. We therefore considered it defensible in the context of our review. We performed additional analyses excluding the two crossover studies, but the heterogeneity and PCE remained approximately the same (data not shown). The large confidence interval for the PCE in the study by Preyde et al. [41] was caused by the small mean difference between the baseline and follow-up measurement in that study. This small difference is used to calculate the standard error of the PCE with the delta method, which results in an inflated but correct value and consequently a large value for the confidence interval.

Furthermore, there was some heterogeneity between study populations because we included all patients with low back pain regardless of the duration of symptoms. However, we performed a further analysis of possible differences in outcomes between patients with acute low back pain (< 3 months duration) and chronic low back pain (> 3 months duration) [46]. This PCE for both pain and disability remained similar with 0.64 (95% CI 0.48–0.85) for pain and 0.68 (95% CI 0.45–1.02) for disability, indicating that longer duration of low back pain did not lead to lower PCEs.

The type of placebo interventions used in the included studies could be a limitation because they do not meet many of the current recommendations for control interventions as described by the Control Interventions in Efficacy Trials of Physical, Psychological, and Self-Management Therapies (CoPPS) statement [47]. The majority of studies used sham devices in their placebo treatments, while these treatments should ideally be structurally equivalent to active treatments as much as possible. This is, of course, difficult to achieve in exercise interventions for the obvious reasons. Initiatives such as the Tidier-placebo checklist and the CoPPS statement will help researchers to improve the design, conduct, and reporting of control interventions in this field [47, 48]. In order to make a valid comparison between an active intervention and a placebo treatment in an RCT, the expectations regarding the effects of the treatment (i.e., the credibility of the treatment) should be similar in both groups. Only two of the included RCTs measured credibility prior to the start of the intervention. They found no differences in treatment credibility between the groups prior to the start of the intervention [37, 39].

Publication bias may occur when studies with less favorable outcomes are less likely to be published. We did not assess publication bias with the funnel plot and Egger’s test because fewer than ten studies were included in the meta-analysis. However, less favorable outcomes in research usually mean a small difference between the placebo treatment and the real treatment, so it is not unlikely that these unpublished studies would have increased the PCE.

There was a risk of bias in the studies that were included, but this was not our main concern. Figure 2 shows that the most common shortcomings were related to the selection of reported results. They were not verifiable due to a lack of pre-published study protocols. The majority of studies were however published at a time when this was not common practice. In addition to the risk of bias, there were other concerns, as reported above, that led us to downgrade the certainty of the evidence.

In the introduction, it was hypothesized that contextual effects may account for a large proportion of the reduction in pain and disability following exercise therapy for low back pain. The results of our meta-analyses support this hypothesis although the certainty of evidence is still low. It is important for researchers and clinicians to realize that contextual effects are an important part of the treatment effect. These contextual effects can be optimized to enhance treatment outcomes [49]. A study by Rossettini et al. showed that 52% of physical therapists surveyed already use contextual effects to enhance treatment outcomes by improving the empathetic therapeutic alliance, adopting a patient-centered approach and positive verbal communication [50].

It is important that future studies focus on identifying important contextual factors, to assess whether they are being adequately used by clinicians, and on finding ways for clinicians to influence and optimize them. Improving contextual care could enhance treatment outcomes, which would reduce the global burden of low back pain. It would be helpful if future RCTs comparing active and placebo interventions report PCE as well as overall treatment effects to gain insight into the magnitude of contextual effects in low back pain care. It is also necessary that placebo-controlled intervention RCTs adhere to the CoPPS guidelines as much as possible [47].

Conclusions

A substantial part of pain and disability improvement after exercise therapy in low back pain is attributable to contextual effects. It should be noted however that this conclusion is based on low certainty evidence indicating that the true effect may differ noticeably from the effect estimated in our review.
