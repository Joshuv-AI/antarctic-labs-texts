A novel colchicine-binding site inhibitor suppresses triple-negative breast cancer growth and metastasis and synergizes with anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy in an immunogenic cancer model
Authors: Kelli L. Adeleye, Damilola Oluwalana, Raisa Krutilina, Yang Xie, Satyanarayana Pochampally, Lily Waltz, Gustavo A. Miranda-Carboni, Duane D. Miller, Tiffany N. Seagroves, Wei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) accounts for approximately 15% of all breast cancer cases and is defined by the absence of estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) expression. Approximately 40% of patients with TNBC develop metastatic disease, reducing median survival to 13.3 months. Paclitaxel remains a standard chemotherapeutic option; however, its efficacy is often limited by ATP-driven efflux pumps and βIII-tubulin overexpression. These limitations underscore the need for agents that can overcome resistance and suppress metastasis. Colchicine-binding site inhibitors (CBSIs) target the colchicine-binding site on tubulin to induce G2/M arrest and apoptosis and can bypass taxane resistance. SP-1–39, a novel CBSI, exhibits potent anti-cancer activity in a paclitaxel-resistant prostate cancer in vivo model, confirming its capacity to overcome chemoresistance. We also discovered that SP-1–39 upregulates PD-L1 expression in vitro, suggesting synergy with immune checkpoint blockade. This study evaluates the anti-metastatic activity of SP-1–39 in metastatic TNBC and the therapeutic potential of combining SP-1–39 with anti-PD-L1 immune checkpoint inhibition in a PD-L1-responsive colon cancer model. Our findings provide preclinical evidence supporting SP-1–39 as a promising therapeutic agent for TNBC and other solid tumors, either as a single agent or in combination with immunotherapy.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer in women worldwide, accounting for approximately 2.3 million new cases annually and representing about 11.7% of all cancer diagnoses [1]. It remains the leading cause of cancer-related death among women, with an estimated 685,000 deaths per year [2]. The disease is classified into several subtypes based on molecular and histopathological characteristics. The major subtypes include hormone receptor-positive (HR+) breast cancer, which expresses estrogen receptor (ER) and/or progesterone receptor (PR); HER2-positive breast cancer, characterized by overexpression of the human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2); and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), which lacks ER, PR, and HER2 expression [3]. While HR+ and HER2 + breast cancers have targeted therapies available, TNBC remains the most challenging to treat due to its aggressive nature, high metastatic potential, and limited treatment options [4].

TNBC accounts for approximately 10–15% of all breast cancer cases and is characterized by a higher risk of recurrence and metastasis compared to other subtypes [5]. Notably, around 40% of patients with TNBC develop metastatic disease, which drastically worsens prognosis: the five-year survival rate drops to 12% in metastatic cases, compared to over 90% in patients with localized disease [6,7]. Current therapeutic approaches for TNBC include a combination of systemic chemotherapy, immunotherapy, radiation, and surgery, with chemotherapy remaining the mainstay of treatment [8]. However, frontline chemotherapeutics such as paclitaxel often elicit resistance through mechanisms like P-glycoprotein (P-gp)-mediated drug efflux and overexpression of βIII-tubulin [9,10]. At present, the only approved targeted therapy for TNBC is sacituzumab govitecan, an antibody-drug conjugate targeting trophoblast cell-surface antigen 2 (TROP2), which is used as a second-line option following failure of standard chemotherapy [8,11]. These limitations underscore the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies to improve clinical outcomes in TNBC.

Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) plays a pivotal role in tumor immune evasion by binding to its receptor, programmed death cell protein 1 (PD-1), on T cells, leading to T cell exhaustion and suppression of the antitumor immune response [12]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly antibodies targeting PD-L1, have revolutionized oncology by reactivating T cell-mediated cytotoxicity [13,14]. Approximately 20% of TNBC patients express sufficient PD-L1 to qualify for anti-PD-L1 therapy [15,16], with eligibility defined by PD-L1 expression levels ranging from 1% to 20% [17]. Higher PD-L1 expression has been correlated with increased responsiveness to PD-L1 blockade across multiple tumor types [18–20].

Colchicine-binding site inhibitors (CBSIs) are a class of microtubule-targeting agents that disrupt microtubule dynamics, induce mitotic arrest, and promote apoptosis in rapidly dividing cells [21–23]. SP-1–39 is a novel small-molecule inhibitor that binds to the colchicine site of tubulin, leading to microtubule destabilization [24]. SP-1–39 has been studied in several solid tumors including prostate cancer and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [24,25]. However, it has yet to be evaluated in breast cancer and metastatic models. Importantly, CBSIs can circumvent mechanisms of resistance associated with taxanes, including P-gp-mediated efflux and βIII-tubulin overexpression [22–24].

Emerging evidence supports a combinatorial approach involving microtubule inhibitors and PD-L1 blockade as a promising therapeutic strategy for TNBC. In addition to their direct cytotoxic activity, microtubule inhibitors can modulate the tumor immune microenvironment, including upregulation of PD-L1 expression, thereby potentially sensitizing tumors to immune checkpoint inhibition [26]. In an important advancement for TNBC treatment, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) recently approved the combination of paclitaxel and atezolizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting PD-L1, for the treatment of PD-L1-positive, unresectable locally advanced or metastatic TNBC. This approval was based on data demonstrating improved progression-free survival in patients receiving the combination therapy compared to chemotherapy alone [27]. However, this approval was withdrawn in 2021 after confirmatory studies failed to demonstrate a survival benefit, underscoring the complex nature of immunotherapy responsiveness in TNBC [28]. Recent studies have demonstrated that small-molecule agents capable of inducing PD-L1 expression can synergize with anti-PD-L1 therapy to enhance antitumor activity and prolong survival [29,30]. This synergistic interaction suggests that co-treatment with SP-1–39 and an anti-PD-L1 antibody may enhance immune-mediated tumor cell killing while simultaneously disrupting microtubule-dependent tumor progression.

For preclinical evaluation, the MDA-MB-231 LM2 (human TNBC) and 4T1 (murine TNBC) cell lines serve as robust models due to their highly metastatic phenotypes, which closely recapitulate the aggressive clinical behavior of TNBC [31,32]. In this study, we investigate the biological activity of SP-1–39 in LM2 and 4T1 cells, with a focus on its effects on cell viability, apoptosis, and PD-L1 expression. We tested whether SP-1–39 can act as a dual-function agent that not only exerts cytotoxic effects via microtubule disruption but also primes tumors for enhanced responsiveness to PD-L1-targeted immunotherapy.

To further explore the therapeutic synergy between SP-1–39 and PD-L1 blockade, we extended our investigation to the MC38 murine colorectal carcinoma model. MC38 tumors are immunogenic, grow in immune-competent C57BL/6 mice, and endogenously express PD-L1, making them a well-established and widely accepted system for preclinical immunotherapy studies [33]. Unlike poorly immunogenic TNBC models such as 4T1, which exhibit limited T cell infiltration and resistance to PD-L1 blockade [34,35], the MC38 model provides a robust platform for assessing immune checkpoint modulation and T cell-mediated anti-tumor responses. We evaluated the potential of SP-1–39 to enhance the efficacy of anti-PD-L1 therapy in an immunocompetent setting, thereby providing broader insights into the translational potential of this combination strategy in cancer immunotherapy.

Results

SP-1–39 exhibits potent anti-proliferative activity in TNBC models

The anti-proliferative activity of SP-1–39 (Fig. 1A) was evaluated in two triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cell lines: human LM2 cells, a luciferase-labeled, lung-metastasis enriched subline of MDA-MB-231 cells [36], and a luciferase-labeled subline of murine 4T1 cells. MTS viability assays demonstrated that SP-1–39 exhibits potent cytotoxicity in both cell lines, with IC50 values of 2.1 nM in LM2 and 3.7 nM in 4T1 cells (Fig. 1B, C, Table 1). These values are comparable to the potency of paclitaxel in LM2 cells. However, paclitaxel exhibited limited efficacy in 4T1 cells, with significant cytotoxicity only observed at concentrations approaching 10 μM, consistent with previously reported IC50 values for this drug in the 4T1 model [37–39]. A recent study demonstrated that inhibition of kinesin family member 2 C (KIF2C) restores drug sensitivity in 4T1 cells [40]. However, additional mechanistic studies are required to determine whether SP-1–39 overcomes resistance through modulation of this pathway.

To further assess the kinetics of growth inhibition, real-time proliferation assays were performed using increasing concentrations of SP-1–39 and paclitaxel. SP-1–39 effectively suppressed LM2 cell proliferation at concentrations as low as 1 nM, while paclitaxel demonstrated comparable inhibition at 3 nM (Fig. 1 D, E). In contrast, 4T1 cell proliferation was significantly reduced by 1 nM SP-1–39, whereas paclitaxel failed to elicit a response even at the highest tested concentration of 12 nM (Fig. 1 F, G).

To evaluate long-term proliferative capacity, a colony formation assay was conducted. SP-1–39 markedly suppressed colony formation at 1 nM in both LM2 and 4T1 cells (Fig. 1 H, I). In LM2 cells, paclitaxel and colchicine inhibited colony formation at 1 nM and 5 nM, respectively (Fig. 1 H, I). However, neither paclitaxel nor colchicine significantly impaired clonogenic growth in 4T1 cells until concentrations reached 10 nM (Fig. 1 H, I). Collectively, these findings highlight the superior anti-proliferative potency of SP-1–39 across both human and murine TNBC models, including those less responsive to standard microtubule-targeting agents.

SP-1–39 impairs cell motility and invasive potential in TNBC models

To evaluate the anti-migratory potential of SP-1–39, we first conducted wound healing assays in LM2 and 4T1 breast cancer cell lines treated with increasing concentrations of the compound. SP-1–39 significantly inhibited wound closure in a dose-dependent manner, with notable inhibition observed at concentrations as low as 4 nM in LM2 cells and 2 nM in 4T1 cells (Fig. 2A, B). For comparison, paclitaxel impaired wound closure in LM2 cells only at a higher concentration of 8 nM (Fig. 2A). Due to its limited cytotoxic potency in 4T1 cells, paclitaxel was not included in the wound healing assessment for this cell line.

To further characterize the anti-migratory and anti-invasive effects of SP-1–39, we performed Transwell migration and Matrigel-coated invasion assays. In both LM2 and 4T1 cells, SP-1–39 potently suppressed migration and invasion at concentrations as low as 2 nM (Fig. 2 C, D). In contrast, paclitaxel demonstrated only minimal inhibition of migration in LM2 cells and had no appreciable effect in 4T1 cells (Fig. 2 C). With respect to invasion, paclitaxel had no effect on LM2 cells but did significantly suppress invasion in 4T1 cells (Fig. 2 D). These data collectively suggest that SP-1–39 exerts superior inhibitory effects on both migration and invasion compared to paclitaxel, particularly in cell lines displaying intrinsic resistance to taxane-based therapies.

SP-1–39 promotes apoptotic cell death and G2/M phase arrest in TNBC

CBSIs suppress tumor growth primarily by inducing apoptosis and causing cell cycle arrest at the G2/M phase. To elucidate the mechanism of action of SP-1–39, we evaluated its impact on apoptotic markers and cell cycle progression. LM2 and 4T1 breast cancer cells were treated with increasing concentrations of SP-1–39 (1, 2, and 5 nM) for 24 h, resulting in a dose-dependent decrease in acetylated α-tubulin expression (Fig. 3 A, B). This observation aligns with previous findings that decreased acetylation of α-tubulin at Lys40 correlates with microtubule stabilization [41]. In contrast, treatment with 10 nM paclitaxel, a known microtubule-stabilizing agent, led to an increase in acetylated α-tubulin levels, confirming microtubule stabilization.

Furthermore, SP-1–39 induced apoptosis in both cell lines, as evidenced by a dose-dependent increase in cleaved caspase-3 expression and elevated cleaved PARP levels in LM2 cells (Fig. 3 A, B). These apoptotic effects were more pronounced than those observed with 10 nM paclitaxel or colchicine, suggesting superior pro-apoptotic efficacy of SP-1–39. Additionally, increased phosphorylation of an anti-apoptotic protein, p-Bcl2 [42], was observed in LM2 cells following SP-1–39 treatment, further supporting its role in modulating apoptotic signaling pathways (Fig. 3 A).

Cell cycle analysis corroborated these findings, demonstrating that SP-1–39 induces a concentration-dependent accumulation of cells in the G2/M phase. Notably, even at the low concentration of 2 nM, significant G2/M arrest was detected, with further enrichment observed at 5 nM and 10 nM (Fig. 3 C, D). In contrast, treatment with 10 nM paclitaxel or colchicine resulted in minimal G2/M arrest under the same experimental conditions, indicating a more robust cell cycle-arresting capability of SP-1–39.

SP-1–39 suppresses tumor growth and metastasis in an orthotopic TNBC xenograft model

To evaluate the in vivo efficacy of SP-1–39, female NSG mice were orthotopically inoculated with LM2 cells into the inguinal mammary fat pads. Treatment began once the average tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3. Mice received intravenous (IV) administration of SP-1–39 at a dose of 2.5 mg/kg twice weekly for 18 days. This regimen resulted in a significant reduction in tumor volume compared to the vehicle-treated control group, with no appreciable loss in body weight, indicating minimal systemic toxicity (Fig. 4A, B). Following euthanasia, tumors were excised and analyzed ex vivo. Both final tumor volume and wet tumor weight were significantly reduced in SP-1–39–treated mice relative to controls (Fig. 4C–E).

In addition, metastatic burden was assessed by measuring total light photon flux by bio-imaging the lungs and liver ex vivo at the study endpoint. SP-1–39 treatment markedly decreased the extent of metastatic dissemination to both organs compared to vehicle controls (Fig. 4 F, G). These findings collectively demonstrate that SP-1–39 effectively inhibits primary tumor growth and metastatic progression in a TNBC xenograft model with limited toxicity.

SP-1–39 suppresses tumor growth and metastatic dissemination in a dose-responsive manner in TNBC-bearing mice

An additional in vivo study was conducted using the LM2 orthotopic model to assess the dose-dependent anti-tumor activity of SP-1–39. As previously described, female NSG mice were inoculated with LM2 cells into the mammary fat pad and assigned to treatment groups once the average tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3. Mice were then administered either vehicle control, 10 mg/kg paclitaxel, or SP-1–39 at doses of 2, 3, or 5 mg/kg via intraperitoneal (IP) injection three times weekly for 25 days. By the end of the treatment period, SP-1–39 induced a clear dose-dependent reduction in primary tumor volume compared to the vehicle group (Fig. 5 A). Despite its in vitro potency, paclitaxel did not significantly affect tumor volume in vivo, consistent with previously published findings [43].

While a gradual decrease in body weight was observed in SP-1–39–treated mice, body weight remained above 80% of baseline throughout the study, indicating the absence of overt toxicity (Fig. 5 B). This modest weight loss may be attributable to the IP route of administration, as no comparable weight reduction was observed with intravenous (IV) dosing in the previous study.

Ex vivo analysis of excised tumors further confirmed the dose-dependent efficacy of SP-1–39, with significant reductions in tumor volume, tumor mass, and bioluminescence signal compared to controls (Fig. 5 C–E). Additionally, quantification of metastatic burden in the lungs and liver at study endpoint revealed decreased metastatic dissemination in all SP-1–39–treated groups relative to vehicle (Fig. 5 F–H). Interestingly, paclitaxel-treated mice also exhibited reduced metastasis despite showing no significant suppression of the primary tumor (Fig. 5 F–H), suggesting potential anti-metastatic activity independent of primary tumor growth suppression.

Dose-dependent effects of SP-1–39 on tumor apoptosis and proliferation in LM2 xenograft model

Excised tumors from the SP-1–39 dose-dependent LM2 xenograft model were sectioned onto unstained slides and stained for markers of proliferation and apoptosis. Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis revealed a dose-dependent increase in the apoptotic markers cleaved-PARP and cleaved caspase-3, which corroborated the in vitro findings (Fig. 6 A, C, D). Additionally, the proliferative marker Ki67 exhibited a dose-dependent decrease in SP-1–39-treated tumors (Fig. 6 A, B). In contrast, paclitaxel treatment had no significant effect on Ki67 or apoptotic marker expression, which likely contributed to the lack of primary tumor reduction observed in this study (Fig. 6 A, B).

SP-1–39 increases PD-L1 expression in vitro

It has been previously reported that certain enhancer small molecules can enhance the efficacy of anti-PD-L1 immunotherapies by upregulating PD-L1 expression in tumor cells [29,30]. To investigate whether SP-1–39 possesses this capability, we evaluated its effect on PD-L1 expression in both 4T1 and MC38 colon cancer cell lines. Western blot analysis revealed that SP-1–39 treatment led to a dose-dependent increase in total PD-L1 protein expression (Fig. 7 A). To further validate these findings and assess spatial distribution, we conducted immunofluorescence staining, which confirmed the upregulation of PD-L1 in both 4T1 and MC38 cells following SP-1–39 treatment (Fig. 7B, C). Finally, flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that SP-1–39 induced a dose-dependent increase in cell surface PD-L1 expression at concentrations ranging from 1 to 10 nM (Fig. 7 D).

SP-1–39 synergizes with anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy to treat colorectal cancer

Given our in vitro findings demonstrating that SP-1–39 induces PD-L1 expression, we next sought to evaluate this effect in vivo. We employed the MC38 murine colorectal cancer model, which has previously been shown to be highly responsive to anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy and revealed potent anti-cancer responses when treated with SP-1–39 in vitro (Supplementary Fig. 1 A–C, Supplementary Table 1). Female C57BL/6 mice were subcutaneously inoculated with MC38 cells in the right flank. Once the average tumor volume reached approximately 80 mm3, mice were randomized and treated according to the designated treatment groups (Fig. 8 A). Mice were euthanized when tumors reached a volume of 800 mm3. Survival analysis revealed that the combination of SP-1–39 and anti-PD-L1 significantly extended median survival to 35 days, compared to vehicle control (14 days), anti-PD-L1 monotherapy (19 days), and SP-1–39 monotherapy (19 days) (Fig. 8 B, Table 2). Combination treatment had a significant hazard ratio compared to both the vehicle and anti-PD-L1 treatment groups of 4.375 and 4.298, respectively (Table 2). Individual tumor growth curves indicated rapid tumor progression in the vehicle and monotherapy groups, whereas the combination therapy markedly delayed tumor growth (Fig. 8 C–F). To further validate our findings, an additional in vivo study was conducted using larger cohorts (n = 9 per group), in which treatment was initiated when tumors were smaller (approximately 20 mm3). Some tumor-bearing mice were excluded during the study due to ulcerated tumors, which reduced the cohort size of each treatment group to ~ 6–7 mice. Notably, earlier intervention further enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of SP-1–39 monotherapy, extending median survival to 28 days (Supplementary Table 2). In contrast, anti-PD-L1 monotherapy produced similar outcomes to those observed in the initial study, with a median survival of 20 days (Supplementary Fig. 2 A–F).

SP-1–39 and anti-PD-L1 combination therapy increases necrosis and cytotoxic T-cell infiltration into tumor tissue

We next performed H&E staining to evaluate tumor viability and the extent of necrosis across treatment groups. Vehicle-treated tumors exhibited dense, darkly stained regions indicative of high cellularity and widespread viable, proliferative tumor cells (Fig. 9 A). Similarly, tumors from the SP-1–39 monotherapy group contained mostly viable tissue with only a small central area of necrosis. In contrast, both the anti-PD-L1 monotherapy and combination therapy groups showed markedly increased necrotic regions, accompanied by a lower density of viable tumor cells. Notably, tumors from the combination group displayed the lightest overall hematoxylin staining, consistent with extensive necrosis. These histologic observations align with the experiment’s survival data, as mice receiving the combination therapy lived the longest (Fig. 9 A).

Further histological analysis was conducted on the lungs, liver, and kidneys of mice across all treatment groups, which revealed no evidence of overt tissue toxicity (Fig. 9A). In addition, these findings are consistent with our previously published study in which SP-1–39 was administered at 5 mg/kg three times per week for 8 weeks to evaluate its efficacy in a breast cancer brain metastasis model [44]. Notably, no signs of systemic toxicity were observed in that study, even with prolonged dosing. Collectively, these results support a favorable preliminary safety profile for SP-1–39 under the dosing regimens tested.

To investigate the immunological correlates of treatment efficacy, we performed immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis to assess CD8+ T-cell infiltration within the tumor microenvironment. Notably, combination therapy induced a level of CD8+ T-cell infiltration comparable to that observed in the anti-PD-L1 monotherapy group, suggesting that the dual treatment effectively supports the intratumoral recruitment of cytotoxic T cells. In contrast, tumors from the vehicle and SP-1–39 monotherapy groups exhibited minimal CD8+ T-cells present, indicating that SP-1–39 alone does not significantly promote T-cell infiltration in vivo (Fig. 9 A, B). We next evaluated the presence of CTLA4+ cells within tumor tissues and observed that treatment with SP-1–39 significantly reduced their abundance (Fig. 9 A, C). In contrast, anti-PD-L1 monotherapy had a comparatively modest effect on CTLA4+ cell levels. These findings suggest that SP-1–39 facilitates CD8+ T cell infiltration into the tumor microenvironment, at least in part, by diminishing the population of immunosuppressive CTLA4+ cells.

Discussion

Despite advances in chemotherapeutic and immunotherapeutic approaches, TNBC remains associated with high rates of recurrence and metastasis, underscoring the need for novel therapeutic strategies that can overcome intrinsic and acquired therapeutic resistance. In this study, we report that SP-1–39, a novel colchicine-binding site inhibitor, exerts potent anti-tumor activity in both in vitro and in vivo well-characterized models of TNBC and colorectal cancer. Moreover, we demonstrate that SP-1–39 upregulates PD-L1 expression in tumor cells and synergizes with anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy to improve survival outcomes in immunocompetent murine models.

Our findings reveal that SP-1–39 exhibits potent cytotoxicity across both human (MDA-MB-231 LM2) and murine (4T1) TNBC cell lines, with low nanomolar IC50 values and superior efficacy compared to paclitaxel, particularly in the 4T1 model, which is characteristically resistant to taxanes. In addition to its anti-proliferative effects, SP-1–39 significantly impaired cellular motility and invasion, indicating its potential to limit metastatic dissemination. These findings were supported by wound healing and transwell assays, wherein SP-1–39 demonstrated greater anti-migratory activity than paclitaxel, even in models with low sensitivity to conventional agents.

Mechanistically, SP-1–39 functions by destabilizing microtubules, as evidenced by the marked reduction in acetylated α-tubulin levels following treatment. This disruption of microtubule integrity led to G2/M phase cell cycle arrest and the induction of apoptosis, characterized by increased levels of cleaved caspase-3 and cleaved PARP. The observed increase in phosphorylated BCL-2 further supports the pro-apoptotic role of SP-1–39, distinguishing it from taxanes, which often require higher concentrations to achieve comparable apoptotic induction, particularly in drug-resistant TNBC models.

In two independent orthotopic xenograft models using LM2 cells, SP-1–39 significantly reduced primary tumor burden and metastatic dissemination to the lungs and liver in a dose-dependent manner, with minimal toxicity as evidenced by stable body weight profiles and lack of overt declines in body condition scores [45]. This in vivo efficacy was further supported by immunohistochemical analysis, which revealed a clear dose-dependent increase in apoptosis (cleaved PARP and caspase-3) and a concomitant decrease in the proliferation marker Ki67. These findings not only validate the potent anti-tumor activities of SP-1–39 but also suggest it has a favorable therapeutic index for further development into a lead clinical candidate compound.

A particularly novel aspect of our study is the demonstration that SP-1–39 upregulates PD-L1 expression in tumor cells. This observation is clinically significant, given that PD-L1 expression is a predictive biomarker for response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). We show that SP-1–39 induces a dose-dependent increase in both total and cell surface PD-L1 expression in 4T1 and MC38 cells, as determined by western blotting, immunofluorescence, and flow cytometry. These findings are consistent with recent reports suggesting that microtubule disruption can lead to compensatory upregulation of immune checkpoints, potentially enhancing the immunogenicity of tumors and sensitizing them to PD-L1 blockade [26,46].

To assess whether SP-1–39 could indeed potentiate the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade, we utilized the immunocompetent MC38 murine colorectal cancer model. This model is well-characterized for its endogenous PD-L1 expression and responsiveness to anti-PD-L1 therapy [47–49], making it a suitable platform for evaluating potential synergy with immune checkpoint blockade. In two independent survival studies, we demonstrated that combination treatment with SP-1–39 and anti-PD-L1 significantly prolonged overall survival compared to either monotherapy or the vehicle control. While both SP-1–39 and anti-PD-L1 monotherapies each extended survival modestly to 19–20 days, the combination therapy nearly doubled survival duration to 35 days. Importantly, when treatment was initiated earlier (when tumors were ~20 mm3), SP-1–39 was more effective as a monotherapy, in contrast to no change in anti-PD-L1 responses, suggesting that earlier intervention with colchicine-binding site inhibitors (CBSIs) may improve outcomes. The MC38 model was selected for these studies because of its high immunogenicity and established responsiveness to checkpoint blockade, which facilitates evaluation of immunotherapy synergy. In contrast, the 4T1 model commonly used to study triple-negative breast cancer is generally poorly immunogenic and exhibits limited responsiveness to checkpoint inhibitors [29,35]. A limitation of this study is therefore that the in vivo immunotherapy experiments were not conducted in a TNBC model, and future studies will evaluate the combination of SP-1–39 and anti-PD-L1 therapy in immunocompetent 4T1 tumor models to determine whether similar therapeutic effects can be observed in TNBC.

Immunohistochemical analysis of MC38 tumors demonstrated that both the combination therapy and anti-PD-L1 monotherapy groups exhibited similarly high levels of CD8+ T-cell infiltration, while tumors treated with SP-1–39 alone or vehicle showed minimal T-cell presence. Despite the comparable levels of infiltration, only the combination group exhibited significantly improved survival outcomes, suggesting that enhanced efficacy may not be attributable solely to T-cell recruitment. Rather, these findings imply that SP-1–39 may enhance the functional activity of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells or increase tumor cell susceptibility to T-cell-mediated cytotoxicity. This interpretation aligns with previous studies showing that chemotherapy- or targeted agent-induced PD-L1 upregulation in tumor cells can potentiate the effects of immune checkpoint blockade, not necessarily by attracting more T cells, but by reactivating their antitumor functions or overcoming tumor-intrinsic resistance mechanisms [26,29,30]. The observed reduction in CTLA4+ cells following SP-1–39 treatment suggests an additional immunomodulatory mechanism beyond its known effects on microtubule dynamics. CTLA4 is a key immune checkpoint receptor involved in dampening T cell activation, and its expression within the tumor microenvironment is associated with immune evasion [50,51]. By reducing the prevalence of CTLA4+ cells, SP-1–39 may help to alleviate local immunosuppression and thereby support enhanced CD8+ T cell infiltration and function. This effect was not as pronounced with anti-PD-L1 monotherapy, highlighting the potential for SP-1–39 to uniquely reshape the tumor immune landscape. These findings further support the utility of SP-1–39 as a dual-function agent that not only disrupts tumor cell division but also promotes a more favorable immunological setting for checkpoint blockade therapy. Although the precise mechanisms by which chemotherapies modulate immune checkpoint pathways remain unclear, previous studies have suggested that disruption of microtubule dynamics can influence immune signaling pathways and cytoskeleton-dependent trafficking of checkpoint proteins [52,53]. Further investigation will therefore be required to elucidate the molecular mechanisms through which SP-1–39 regulates PD-L1 and CTLA4 expression within the tumor microenvironment.

These data provide strong preclinical evidence that SP-1–39 functions as a dual-action agent-exerting direct cytotoxicity via microtubule destabilization and priming tumors for immunotherapy by upregulating PD-L1 expression. This dual mechanism positions SP-1–39 as a compelling candidate for combinatorial strategies in TNBC and other immunogenic cancers. Furthermore, the superior efficacy of SP-1–39 over paclitaxel in drug-resistant TNBC models such as 4T1 underscores its translational potential, especially for patients with limited responses to taxane-based therapies.

Nonetheless, several questions remain. First, the molecular pathways linking microtubule destabilization to PD-L1 upregulation require further elucidation. It is possible that SP-1–39 induces PD-L1 expression through activation of stress-response pathways such as the MAPK pathway or NF-κB signaling, as has been reported with other microtubule-disrupting agents [26,54]. Second, while our study highlights enhanced survival and reduced tumor growth with combination therapy, future experiments should evaluate T-cell functionality and immune contexture using flow cytometry to determine whether SP-1–39 alters the immune microenvironment in a manner that enhances T-cell cytotoxicity or antigen presentation.

In conclusion, this study positions SP-1–39 as a promising microtubule-targeting agent with potent anti-tumor activity in preclinical models of TNBC and colorectal cancer. By simultaneously disrupting microtubule dynamics and enhancing tumor immunogenicity through PD-L1 upregulation/CTLA4 downregulation, SP-1–39 offers a unique opportunity for therapeutic synergy with immune checkpoint inhibitors. These findings support further preclinical development of SP-1–39 and rational design of future clinical trials exploring its use in combination with PD-L1 inhibitors in aggressive and treatment-resistant cancers.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and cell culture

Paclitaxel and colchicine were purchased from LC Laboratories (P-9600) and Sigma-Aldrich, respectively (C9754). SP-1–39 was synthesized as previously reported in [24]. Human MDA-MB-231 LM2 lung metastasis, luciferase-labeled cells were obtained from Dr. Yibin Kang (RRID: CVCL_DG21) and a luciferase-labeled subline of murine 4T1 cells originally purchased from Caliper Life Sciences (cat#124087, RRID: CCL_L899). Murine MC38 colorectal cancer cells were purchased from Kerafast (cat#ENH204-FP, RRID: CVCL_B288). MDA-MB-231 LM2 (DMEM-Hi) and MC38 (RPMI1640) cells were cultured in the indicated base medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotic-antimitotic solution in 5% CO2 at 37°C. All cells were regularly tested for mycoplasma using the MycoAlert kit (Lonza).

Cytotoxicity and growth inhibition assays

LM2, 4T1, or MC38 cells were seeded in 96-well plates (3500 cells/well) overnight and treated the following day with increasing concentrations of paclitaxel, colchicine, or SP-1–39 (0.1 nM – 3 μM) for 72 h before adding MTS as described previously [55]. Similarly, LM2 (3500 cells/well), 4T1 (2000 cells/well), or MC38 (2000 cells/well) cells were plated into 96-well plates overnight and treated with increasing concentrations of paclitaxel (1, 2, 4, 8, 10, or 12 nM) or SP-1–39 (1, 2, or 4 nM) and imaged with the Incucyte S3 live-cell imaging system. Cell confluence was determined using phase-masking algorithms.

Colony formation assay

LM2 or 4T1 cells were seeded in 12-well plates (250 cells/well) overnight and treated the following day with increasing concentrations of paclitaxel (1, 2, 5, or 10 nM), colchicine (1, 2, 5, or 10 nM) or SP-1–39 (1, 2, or 5 nM). Fresh media with drug was replaced every 5 days. At endpoint, cells were fixed with methanol, stained with crystal violet, and quantified using ImageJ.

Wound healing assay

LM2 or 4T1 cells were seeded in 96-well ImageLock plates (Essen Biosciences) (30,000 cells/well). The following day, a wound was created using the WoundMaker and cells were washed twice before adding fresh media containing either paclitaxel (1, 2, 4, 8, or 10 nM) or SP-1–39 (1, 2, 4, 8, or 10 nM). Images were taken every 2 h on the Incucyte S3 live-imaging system and the wound width was normalized to the vehicle control.

Transwell migration and invasion assay

LM2 and 4T1 cells were serum-starved for 24 h then 20,000 or 30,000 cells were plated respectively, into the upper chamber of the non-coated transwell insert for migration (Falcon, #353097) or a Matrigel-coated transwell insert for invasion (Corning, #354480). Cells were incubated in 0% FBS media with increasing concentrations of paclitaxel (2 or 4 nM) or SP-1–39 (2 or 4 nM), and 10% FBS growth media was added to the plate as the chemoattractant. After 48 h, inserts were fixed with methanol, stained using crystal violet, and quantified with ImageJ.

Western blotting

Cells were incubated with paclitaxel (10 nM), colchicine (10 nM), or SP-1–39 (1, 2, or 5 nM) for 24 h and whole cell lysates were resolved on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membrane. The membranes were blocked with blotting grade blocker (BioRad #1706404) and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C against cleaved-PARP, phosphorylated BCL2, acetylated α-tubulin, cleaved caspase-3 or GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology #5625, #2827, #3971, #9661, #3683). For PD-L1 detection, cells were treated with increasing concentrations of SP-1–39 (1, 2, or 5 nM) for 24 h and proteins were separated on SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to membranes as described previously. The membrane was incubated with anti-PD-L1 antibody overnight at 4°C (CST, #60475, 1:1000). The membranes were washed and incubated with anti-rabbit IgG HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (CST, #7074) at RT for 1 h and detected with SuperSignal West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate (ThermoFisher #34094) using the BioRad Chemidoc imager.

Cell cycle analysis

LM2 and 4T1 cells were seeded overnight, serum-starved for 24 h, and treated with paclitaxel (10 nM), colchicine (10 nM), or increasing concentrations of SP-1–39 (1, 2, 5, or 10 nM) for 24 h. Cells were harvested, washed, and fixed with 70% ethanol overnight at −20°C. Cells were washed twice with PBS and incubated with propidium iodide and RNase A. Cells were analyzed using the BioRad ZE5 instrument and data were analyzed by the University of Tennessee Health Science Center (UTHSC) Flow Cytometry and Cell Sorting Core as previously described [22].

MDA-MB-231 LM2 in vivo orthotopic IV model

All animal studies adhered to the NIH Principles of Laboratory Animal care and were conducted under approved Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee protocols at UTHSC, #23–0467 and #23–0454. LM2 (2 ×105 cells in 10 μL HBSS) were surgically inoculated into the left and right inguinal mammary fat pad of 6–8 week old female Nod-Scid-Gamma (NSG) mice using a Hamilton syringe mounted with a ½” 26 G needle. When the average tumor volume reached ~100 mm3, mice were randomized into treatment groups: 1. (Vehicle, IV, 2x/week, 1:4 PEG300:saline, n = 7) or 2. (SP-1–39, 2.5 mg/kg, IV, 2x/week, 1:4 PEG300:saline, n = 7). Primary tumor size and body weight were measured twice per week. Tumor volume was measured using digital calipers and calculated using the formula: (width2 x length)/2. All mice were euthanized when the vehicle tumor volume reached an average of 500 mm3, due to the tails of the mice becoming necrotic caused by the multiple IV injections. Before euthanasia, mice were administered luciferin (Revvitty, cat# 122799) and organs were imaged by the IVIS XMRS imaging system to analyze metastasis using the LivingImage software.

MDA-MB-231 LM2 in vivo orthotopic IP dose-dependent model

Female NSG mice aged 6–8 weeks old were obtained from the UTHSC Center for Cancer Research Xenograft Core inoculated as described above with LM2 cells. When the average tumor volume reached ~100 mm3, mice were randomized into treatment groups: 1) Vehicle (IP, 3x/week, 1:4 PEG300:saline, n = 8), 2) Paclitaxel (10 mg/kg, IP, 3x/week 1:1:18, ethanol:cremaphor EL:saline, n = 8), 3) SP-1–39 (2 mg/kg, IP, 3x/week, 1:4 PEG300:saline, n = 8), 4) SP-1–39 (3 mg/kg, IP, 3x/week, 1:4 PEG300:saline, n = 7), or 5) SP-1–39 (5 mg/kg, IP, 3x/week, 1:4 PEG300:saline, n = 7). Primary tumor size and body weight were measured as described above.

Flow cytometry for PD-L1 detection

4T1 and MC38 cells were seeded overnight and treated with increasing concentrations of SP-1–39 (1, 2, 5, or 10 nM) for 24 h. The cells were gently removed from the plate with an PBS-based and enzyme-free dissociation buffer, centrifuged, and resuspended in PBS containing 1% BSA, then adjusted to 1 × 106 cells/100 μL. The cells were incubated with Zombie Aqua dye (1:500) for 20 min at RT and then washed and resuspended with PBS + 1% BSA. The cells were then blocked with BioLegend TruStain FcX PLUS (#156604) at 1 μg per 106 cells for 10 min on ice before adding PE/Cyanine5 anti-mouse CD274 (PD-L1) antibody (Biolegend #124344) at 0.25 μg per sample and incubated for 20 min on ice. The cells were washed twice, resuspended in PBS + 1% BSA then strained through a 35 μm cell strainer before analysis at the UTHSC Flow Cytometry and Cell Sorting core.

MC38 high tumor burden survival study

MC38 (3 ×105 cells in 100 μL HBSS) were inoculated into the right flank of female C57BL/6 J mice (Jackson Labs, stock #000664). When the average tumor volume reached ~80 mm3, mice were randomized into treatment groups: 1. (Vehicle, n = 5), 2. (PD-L1, IP, 200 μg, 2x/week, n = 5), 3. (SP-1–39, 2 mg/kg, IV, 2x/week, DMSO:PEG300:saline 1:3:16 n = 5), or 4. [PD-L1 and SP-1–39 combination treatment, n = 5). Primary tumor size and body weight were measured twice per week. Tumor volume was measured using digital calipers as described. Mice were euthanized as they reached euthanasia criteria, including the formation of ulcers, or tumor volume exceeding 800 mm3. All major organs and tumor tissues were harvested and subjected to histopathology at the UTHSC Research Histology Core.

MC38 low tumor burden survival study

MC38 were inoculated as described above. When the average tumor volume reach ~20 mm3, mice were randomized into treatment groups: 1) Vehicle (n = 7), 2) PD-L1 (IP, 200 μg, 2x/week n = 7), 3) SP-1–39 (2 mg/kg, IV, 2x/week, DMSO:PEG300:saline 1:3:16 n = 6), or 4) PD-L1 and SP-1–39 combination treatment (n = 7). Primary tumor size and body weight were measured as described above. Mice were euthanized as they reached euthanasia criteria, including the formation of an ulcer or tumor volume exceeding 1000 mm3. All major organs and tumor tissues were harvested for histopathology.

Histology and immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis

Tumor tissues were fixed, embedded in paraffin, and cut onto slides for IHC analysis. Slides were heated to 60°C overnight and deparaffinized. A 3% hydrogen peroxide solution was used to block endogenous peroxidase activity for 30 min at RT. The LM2 slides were prepared as previously described in [24], using anti-Ki67, anti-cleaved caspase-3, and anti-cleaved PARP antibodies (CST, #9027, 1:400; CST, #9661, 1:200; CST, #5625, 1:200). Slides from the MC38 low tumor burden study were heated to boiling in a tris-acetate antigen recovery buffer then cooled to RT. Slides were then blocked for 1 h in a solution consisting of 5% normal goat serum, 2.5% BSA, and 0.1% sodium azide in PBS. Anti-CD8 or anti-CTLA4 primary antibody was added and incubated in humidity chambers overnight at 4°C (CST, #98941, 1:100; CST, #53560, 1:100). Slides were thoroughly washed the following day and blocked for 15 min at RT. The HRP-conjugated secondary antibody was incubated at RT for 1 h (DAKO EnVision+, #K0043). After washing, slides were developed using the DAKO liquid DAB+ substrate chromogen system (#K3468) and neutralized with tap water to stop the reaction. The slides were counterstained using Modified Mayer’s hematoxylin (Epredia, #72804). Slides were then dehydrated and cover slipped with Cytoseal XYL.

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise described, all raw data were entered into GraphPad Prism 9.0 and analyzed using one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by multiple pairwise comparison tests (Student’s t-tests), and the mean ± SEM is shown. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was created by GraphPad Prism and the hazard ratio logrank and 95% confidence intervals were calculated. Significance was determined using a 95% confidence threshold. P-values are indicated by asterisks defined as, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001, n.s. not significant.
