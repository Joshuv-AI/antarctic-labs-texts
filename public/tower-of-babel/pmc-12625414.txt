Prevalence and associated risk factors for depression, anxiety, insomnia, and loneliness among the geriatric population in Bangladesh: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Maisha Rahman, Mayesha Khan, Tasin Fattah, Zarin Tasnim, Proma Rani Das, Kazi Milenur Rahman Prattay, Md Siddiqul Islam, Md Rabiul Islam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBangladesh is currently going through a demographic shift, with a swift rise in the proportion of older individuals among the population. The increasing prevalence of mental health issues among older adults is placing heightened demands on community health services. This study aims to assess mental health symptoms of depression, anxiety, insomnia, and loneliness, along with their associated factors, among the geriatric population in Bangladesh.MethodsThe current study collected data from several geographical regions in the Bangladesh through face-to-face cross-sectional surveys employing stratified random sampling techniques. A total of 1,607 older respondents initially participated in the survey, and 1,544 responses were entered into the final analysis after careful evaluation. A pre-structured questionnaire was used to collect socio-demographic and lifestyle factors of participants. We employed the PHQ-9, GAD-7, AIS-8, and UCLA-3 to assess symptoms of depression, anxiety, insomnia, and loneliness, respectively.ResultsThe prevalence rates for depression, anxiety, insomnia, and loneliness were estimated at 58% (mild: 42%, moderate: 13%, severe: 3%), 82% (mild: 39%, moderate: 31%, severe: 12%), 77% (mild: 28%, moderate: 38%, severe: 11%), and 9% (mild: 7%, moderate: 2%), respectively. In Bangladeshi geriatric population, the primary characteristics linked to depression, anxiety, insomnia, and loneliness include female sex, spousal separation or bereavement, unemployment or retirement, low to moderate economic status, and living without family, smoking habit, lack of regular physical exercise, assistance required for regular activities, involvement in social activities, and chronic disease or condition. The current investigation also revealed statistically significant correlations among the assessed mental health concerns, indicating a tendency to co-occur among older adults.ConclusionMental health symptoms were highly prevalent and influenced by socio-demographic and lifestyle-related factors among the older population in Bangladesh. Context-specific associations underscore the need for culturally appropriate interventions addressing social and structural determinants in geriatric mental health care. The current findings possess practical ramifications for clinical psychology, psychotherapy, and associated policy deliberations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07500-0.Keywords: Geriatrics, Mental health, Depression, Anxiety, Loneliness, Insomnia, Public health

Introduction

Aging is a natural and complex biological process influenced by a range of genetic, environmental, and social factors, often accompanied by evolving health and support needs. Regrettably, older people in Bangladesh are experiencing significant hardships in this regard due to a deficiency in national, cultural, and individual foresight, prudence, adequate preparations, and balanced initiatives and arrangements [1]. The global population is aging swiftly, with the demographic of individuals aged 60 and above projected to increase from 962 million in 2017 to 2.1 billion by 2050 and 3.1 billion by 2100. The majority of older individuals inhabit low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), predominantly in Asia, where it is projected that 80% of older adults will reside by 2050 [2, 3]. Bangladesh possesses one of the most rapidly expanding older adult demographics in Southeast Asia, and currently, about 8% of the overall population of Bangladesh is aged 60 years or older, with projections indicating a growth to 11.5% (21.526 million) by 2030 and to 21.5% (43.491 million) by 2050 [4, 5]. Old age is a transitional phase during which individuals must navigate challenges affecting their mental and social well-being, alongside the physical aspects of aging. The overall prevalence of mental and behavioral disorders often increases with age, attributed to the natural aging of the brain, deteriorating physical health, and cerebral pathology [6]. Mental health issues in older individuals (aged 60 and above) are becoming increasingly significant in public health due to rising prevalence, disease burden, disability, morbidity, and mortality rates [7]. Approximately 15% of adults aged 60 and above experience a mental condition, accounting for 6.6% of all disabilities worldwide [8].

Depression, a universally acknowledged public health issue, is more prevalent in older adults. Data from the Global Burden of Diseases and the World Health Organization (WHO) reveal that around 264 million older people, aged 60 years and above, exhibit prevalent depressive symptoms [9]. Depression diminishes an individual’s quality of life and increases their reliance on others. Untreated depression in older individuals can lead to significant clinical and social repercussions [6]. Nonetheless, despite its increasing prevalence, depression has garnered minimal attention in most low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including Bangladesh. Significantly, LMICs exhibit a markedly greater prevalence of depression among older persons compared to analogous estimates from high-income nations [9]. Depression presents a wide array of symptoms, many of which are individualized. Depression in older adults might be difficult to recognize due to the potential variance in symptoms compared to younger populations. Individuals may be reluctant to articulate their emotions or may display other, subtler manifestations of depression [10]. Individuals diagnosed with depression exhibited markedly elevated rates of diagnosed anxiety and sleep disturbances compared to those without depression [11].

Anxiety has become prevalent among older people, affecting approximately 10–20% of this demographic. Nevertheless, it frequently remains unrecognized and unaddressed in the majority of instances [12]. Anxiety is an adaptive emotional response that equips individuals, regardless of age, to manage an expected or ongoing situation deemed harmful or dangerous, through resource mobilization or avoidance strategies. Such anxiety can become pathological when it is overwhelming, upsetting, and substantially disrupts an individual’s capacity to perform and fulfill various life duties, including occupational, familial, and social responsibilities [13]. Geriatric adults exhibiting anxiety symptoms primarily attributable to depression frequently report experiencing pain. The anxiety accompanying depression in older individuals results in diminished self-esteem and can occasionally manifest as a life-threatening illness, as it may exacerbate suicide ideation and impulses. The suicide rate escalates with age, peaking among individuals aged 60 to 80 years [14].

Insomnia is a prevalent sleep disorder in the older population, often defined by the subjective experience of difficulty initiating or sustaining sleep, or non-restorative sleep, resulting in notable daytime symptoms such as impaired concentration and mood disturbances. Up to 50% of older people report challenges in establishing or sustaining sleep. The prevalence of insomnia is greater in older adults compared to the younger population. The prevalence of insomnia symptoms in older people varies from 30% to 48%, whereas the occurrence of the insomnia problem ranges from 12% to 20% [15]. Untreated insomnia is linked to considerable morbidity. Older adults with insomnia exhibit a 23% heightened chance of developing depressive symptoms [16]. A study indicated that 44% of older individuals with recurrent insomnia continued to experience depression six months later, in contrast to only 16% of those without insomnia [17]. Insomnia and mental diseases, including anxiety and depression, exhibit a bidirectional link. Moreover, insomnia elevates the likelihood of suicidal tendencies [15].

Loneliness denotes a subjective state associated with the feeling of insufficient attention from, or absence of interactions with, others. Consequently, it is associated with feelings of isolation or neglect by family and friends, who may appear indifferent or inattentive to the emotional and social requirements of their older relatives or companions. Therefore, loneliness is the perception of a disparity between actual and desired social connections [18]. Experimental evidence suggests that, in older adults, feelings of being a burden and a weakened sense of purpose correlate with decreased resilience to psychological stresses, consequently heightening the likelihood of suicidal thoughts in the context of chronic loneliness [19]. Surveys indicate that loneliness has emerged as a significant issue jeopardizing the quality of life and personal satisfaction of older people. Loneliness correlates with adverse health outcomes, including depression, diminished sleep quality and insomnia, cardiovascular disease, and all-cause mortality [20].

Furthermore, mental diseases in older adults are frequently underestimated due to insufficient awareness and the assumed necessity for medical care focused on physical health issues, whereas concomitant psychiatric conditions typically go undiagnosed in this population [21]. Therefore, this study aims to investigate specific psychosocial factors such as age, sex, marital status, education, occupation, economic status, residence, living status, family structure, social activity, social media engagement, smoking habit, physical exercise, religious practices, body mass index (BMI) and presence of chronic conditions and examine their association with the prevalence of depression, anxiety, insomnia, and loneliness symptoms within the geriatric population in Bangladesh, thereby addressing the evidence gap for effective program implementation concerning these matters. We expect that the results of this study will improve the comprehension of symptoms related to depression, anxiety, insomnia, and loneliness, along with their contributing factors, potentially aiding in the development of a comprehensive intervention strategy for addressing these mental health concerns.

Methods

Study design and participants

The current study conducted an extensive face-to-face survey with a nationwide sample. From October 1, 2024, to March 30, 2025, we conducted this cross-sectional study with older people in Bangladesh. We assumed the response rate, confidence interval, and margin of error at 75%, 95%, and 5%, respectively. According to the assumptions, the current study necessitated 933 responses. We employed a stratified random sampling technique to identify possible respondents. We utilized age, sex, and geographic region as factors for stratification. A pilot study was carried out with 50 older participants to assess the intelligibility of the questions. Initially, we approached 2,000 individuals through in-person surveys. We received 1,607 responses; however, 63 were excluded following screening due to partial and incomplete information. The final analysis comprised 1,544 responses, with 846 males and 698 females (Fig. 1). Participants with comorbid psychiatric disorders were excluded, specifically those with a current or past diagnosis of bipolar disorder, schizophrenia spectrum disorders, substance use disorders, or any other condition that could significantly affect cognitive function or interfere with study participation, as assessed through clinical interview and medical history. All participants are of Bangladeshi descent and reside in Bangladesh. Before participation, they were provided with a summary of the consent forms, eligibility criteria, objectives, protocols, and questionnaire. They willingly submitted their information once they were informed of the prerequisites. The participants received no remuneration for their participation in the study.

Estimations

We employed a detailed methodology for data collection with two distinct sets of questionnaires. The initial one was a structured questionnaire developed by the researchers, encompassing informed consent and sociodemographic data (Supplementary File 1). The second set comprised a self-reported structured questionnaire derived from various psychometric assessment scales. We generated both sets of questionnaires in English and subsequently translated them into Bangla. Initially, all questionnaires were translated into Bangla with the aid of two native Bengali speakers, both fluent in English; one possessed a medical background, while the other had a non-medical background. An independent researcher amalgamated the translated versions to develop a Bengali-forward edition, rectifying discrepancies. The Bengali version was subsequently translated into two English questionnaires by certified medical translators. We reconciled and amalgamated these two English versions to develop a unified English rendition under the guidance of the author of this research. Subsequently, we conducted a pilot study with a restricted sample of randomly selected older participants to evaluate the validity of the questionnaires for readability and comprehension. We excluded this pilot data from the final dataset. To facilitate comprehension, we provided the survey questionnaire in both Bengali and English. The educated close family members, relatives of the older respondents, assisted in gathering their responses, where the assistance was limited to facilitating comprehension and communication, not guiding the responses.

Socio-demographic measures

We gathered the most pertinent sociodemographic data of the respondents. The collected data pertained to age, sex, BMI, marital status, level of education, occupational status, economic status, area of residence, living status (with or without family), family structure (nuclear or joint), smoking habits, regular physical exercise, religious or spiritual practices, daily hobbies or interests, social media usage, participation in social activities, consumption of dietary/nutritional, or vitamin supplements, and chronic diseases or conditions.

Patient Health Questionnaire

The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) comprises nine distinct items that evaluate respondents’ depression symptoms. The cumulative score varies from 0 to 27 points, with each question evaluated on a scale from 0 to 3 based on the response: 0 (not at all), 1 (several days), 2 (half of the days), and 3 (nearly every day). The presence of depressive symptoms was defined using a cut-off score of ≥ 10, whereas a score under 10 signifies the absence of depressive symptoms. To assess the severity of depression, the total score was categorized into four levels: cumulative scores of < 10 indicate no depression, 10–15 signify mild depression, 16–21 represent moderate depression, and 22–27 denote severe depression [22].

Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale

The Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-Item Scale (GAD-7) comprises seven fundamental questions that assess respondents’ anxiety disorders. The cumulative score varies from 0 to 21. Each question is assigned one of four distinct ratings based on the response: 0 (not at all), 1 (several days), 2 (more than half the days), and 3 (nearly every day). A cut-off score of ≥ 5 was used to define the presence of anxiety symptoms. A higher score signifies a greater level of anxiety, categorized into four segments: <5 shows no anxiety, 5–9 suggests mild anxiety, 10–14 denotes moderate anxiety, and 15–21 indicates severe anxiety [23].

Athens Insomnia Scale

The Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS-8) is an eight-item self-report questionnaire that assesses the severity of sleep disturbances in accordance with the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD-10) diagnostic criteria for insomnia. Each question is assigned a score between 0 and 3, indicating the severity of insomnia. The total score for an individual was calculated by summing the scores of each item on the AIS-8, ranging from 0 to 24. The presence of insomnia symptoms was defined by a score of ≥ 6, and the entire score was subsequently classified into four tiers: cumulative scores of 0–5, 6–9, 10–15, and 16–24, corresponding to no, mild, moderate, and severe insomnia, respectively [24].

UCLA loneliness scale

The three-item variant of the University of California, Los Angeles, Loneliness Scale (UCLA-3) comprises three items that assess three interconnected dimensions of isolation, relational connectedness, and trait loneliness: “how often do you feel that you lack companionship, how often do you feel left out, and how often do you feel isolated from others.” The respondents were presented with three response options: ‘Hardly ever or never’, ‘Some of the time’, and ‘Often’, with scores of 1, 2, and 3, respectively. The results for each item were aggregated to assess the respondents’ loneliness status. The total scores of UCLA-3 vary from a minimum of 3 to a maximum of 9. A cut-off score of ≥ 6 was used to define the presence of loneliness symptoms, and the entire score was subsequently classified into four categories: cumulative scores of 3–5 for no loneliness, 6 for mild loneliness, 7–8 for moderate loneliness, and 9 for severe loneliness [25, 26].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using Microsoft Excel 2021 and IBM SPSS Version 30.0, the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences. Data processing was conducted using Microsoft Excel. The Excel file was subsequently imported into IBM SPSS for further analysis. Descriptive statistics were employed to identify and compare the attributes of the study participants. We performed chi-square (χ2) tests to evaluate the correlation between mental health outcomes and associated variables, and the 95% CI confidence intervals (CIs) of the prevalence of health outcomes were calculated. Multivariable binary logistic regression was performed to calculate the odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs while controlling for the confounding factors to assess the association between potential risk factors and the presence of psychiatric symptoms (depression, anxiety, sleep disturbance, and loneliness). p-values below 0.05 are deemed the criterion for statistical significance.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of the respondents

In Table 1, the descriptive statistics for all socio-demographic variables of the respondents are demonstrated. Among the 1,544 Bangladeshi older respondents, 55% were men and 45% were women. Additionally, 57% were aged 60–69 years, 31% were aged 70–79 years, and 11% were aged 80 years or older. A significant proportion of respondents were married (73%), unemployed or retired (57%), belonged to the middle economic class (55%), resided in rural areas (56%), lived with family (90%) and predominantly in nuclear family structures (56%), identified as religious or spiritual (91%), maintained a normal BMI (53%), and had chronic diseases or conditions (59%). Two-thirds of the individuals were non-smokers (68%), and consumed dietary, nutritional, or vitamin supplements (69%). One-third of the participants had completed secondary education (30%) and never participated in social activities (35%). A significant proportion of individuals indicated that they do not participate in a hobby or interest daily (53%), and do not engage in regular physical exercise (62%). Many respondents were non-users of social media (58%) and did not require assistance with regular activities (75%).

Psychometric parameters

Discussion

Psychosocial disorders are prevalent among older adults and are primary contributors to impairment, morbidity, and death in this demographic [27]. The current study revealed that 58% of geriatric respondents exhibited depressive symptoms, categorized as mild (42%), moderate (13%), and severe (3%). This finding contrasts with prior studies, which reported the prevalence of depressive symptoms as mild (38.1%), moderate (16.8%), and severe (12.1%) in the Nepalese population (27); mild (26.3%), moderate (15.6%), and severe (12.4%) in Indians [28]; and mild (16%), moderate (11%), and severe (6%) in Saudi Arabians [29]. This present study found that the likelihood of experiencing depression was markedly elevated among female participants, individuals with low to middle socioeconomic status, rural inhabitants, those living without family, those who infrequently engage in social activities, individuals with chronic physical ailments, and those with pre-existing conditions of anxiety, insomnia, and loneliness. Consistent with our findings, a prior study indicated that loneliness may serve as a risk factor for the onset of depression and can exacerbate its severity [30]. Social networks offer essential support, mitigate social isolation, and enhance the utilization of mental health services and adherence to therapy. As social isolation escalates, feelings of loneliness correspondingly intensify, which is closely associated with depression [9, 31]. In our study, the prevalence of both moderate and severe depressive symptoms was greater among females than males, aligning with prior research conducted in Bangladesh [32, 33] and globally [29, 34, 35]. A study in Bangladesh reported that females are more vulnerable to risk factors for depressive symptoms, such as lower educational attainment, diminished workforce participation, adverse financial circumstances, and exposure to severe abuse [36]. In addition to these causes, biological factors such as hormonal fluctuations, diminished estrogen levels, sensitivity to interpersonal relationships, postpartum and postmenopausal depression, variations in brain circuitry development, disparities in physical strength, and personality traits contribute to a greater prevalence of depression in women [37]. Two distinct prior studies indicated that persistent insomnia is a risk factor for both new-onset and recurrent depressive disorder in older individuals, aligning with our findings [17, 38]. Depression was significantly heightened among older individuals of low socioeconomic status, those residing alone, and those with infrequent social interactions in a study conducted in Bangladesh, which supports our findings. A credible explanation is that high income may alleviate daily living stress by facilitating improved access to essential resources and healthcare, encompassing affordability and mental health services [9]. Previous studies involving 400 older adults in Bangladesh, 5,830 older adults from Spain, and 752 older adults from China, revealed a greater likelihood of depression among participants with chronic conditions compared to those without, consistent with our findings [32, 34, 39]. Due to the intricate relationship between physical and mental health, older people with chronic illnesses should undergo early screening for depression and receive optimal care [32].

The present study indicated that 82% of older adults in Bangladesh exhibited anxiety symptoms, with 39% categorized as mild, 31% as moderate, and 12% as severe. This conclusion diverges with previous studies, which indicated that in Bangladesh, the prevalence of anxiety symptoms was mild (44.3%), moderate (21.0%), and severe (7.3%) [13]; in Nepal, the prevalence was mild (17.5%), moderate (13.4%), and severe (29.4%) (27); whereas in India, the prevalence was mild (13%), moderate (46.5%), and severe (29%) [40]. This study revealed that the probability of experiencing anxiety was significantly heightened among females, unemployed or retired individuals, those with low to middle economic status, urban residents, individuals living without family, smokers, those not participating in regular physical exercise, individuals with chronic illnesses, and those with pre-existing depression and insomnia. Our study also indicated a greater prevalence of anxiety among individuals requiring assistance with daily chores. A recent study identified that infrequent or absent physical exercise, moderate capacity for daily living activities, significant dependence, poor self-rated health, the presence of infirmity, low socioeconomic status, and sleep disorders were risk factors for anxiety in chronically ill Chinese older adults, aligning with our findings [41]. A prior study indicated that rural older people exhibit a greater prevalence of anxiety compared to their urban counterparts, which contradicts our findings [42]. Insomnia and poor health status were found as influential variables contributing to the prevalence of anxiety and depression among the older ones in a previous study, corroborating our findings [43]. A prior investigation indicated a favorable correlation between depression levels and specific anxiety symptoms, including psychiatric, somatic, and behavioral manifestations, with insomnia identified as one of the most prevalent anxiety indicators, aligning with our findings [14]. We advocate for a multifaceted method to accurately diagnose depression and anxiety, facilitating precise and streamlined selection of optimal treatment.

The present study indicated that 77% of the older population in Bangladesh experienced insomnia, with 28%, 38%, and 11% reporting mild, moderate, and severe insomnia, respectively. Our findings are contradicted by a prior study, which demonstrated that 37.6% of the Indian older adults experienced insomnia, comprising mild insomnia (23.6%), moderate critical insomnia (12.9%), and severe critical insomnia (1.1%) [28]. The current study revealed that the possibility of suffering from insomnia was markedly increased among divorced individuals, unemployed or retired respondents, rural residents, those living without family, smokers, religious individuals, those requiring assistance with regular activities, and individuals with pre-existing depression, anxiety, and loneliness. Consistent with our findings, functional reliance and daily living activities were substantially correlated with insomnia, as indicated by a prior study [27]. Divorced individuals exhibit a significant prevalence of insomnia, with smoking habits and decreased physical activity serving as relevant contributors associated with elevated insomnia rates among older people, aligning with our findings [15, 44]. Prior research demonstrated a robust correlation between sleep quality and loneliness among the older Chinese demographic, suggesting that loneliness influences sleep and vice versa, aligning with our findings [20]. Furthermore, our findings align with a previous study that also identified a statistically significant correlation between smoking and insomnia, as well as between geriatric depression and insomnia. Smoking may contribute to insomnia through various mechanisms, including nicotine-induced restlessness, changes in sleep architecture, upper airway inflammation resulting in obstructive sleep apnea, and disruptions to circadian rhythm. Sleep deprivation can result in mood swings and may hinder individuals’ ability to interact with others. Conversely, depression may reduce the duration of nightly sleep and modify sleep architecture [28]. Other prior studies have also identified sleep deprivation as a substantial risk factor for depression in older people [45, 46].

Among the present study participants, 9% were suffering from loneliness, with 7% and 2% identified as having mild to moderate loneliness, respectively. Previous research indicated that 11.6% of Norwegian participants often experienced feelings of loneliness, a figure that is marginally higher than our prevalence findings [47]. The current study revealed that the likelihood of experiencing loneliness was significantly elevated among unemployed or retired individuals, those who do not engage in regular physical exercise, those lacking a daily hobby or interest, those who seldom partake in social activities, and those with pre-existing depression and insomnia. Depression syndrome was identified as a primary associated risk factor for feelings of loneliness in previous studies, corroborating our findings [48, 49]. Our study revealed a higher prevalence of loneliness among those with chronic health issues and those requiring assistance with daily tasks. Consistent with our findings, a prior study identified mobility issues and reduced mobility as important predictors of loneliness. Limited mobility hindered social interactions and leisure activities, thus exacerbating feelings of loneliness [48]. Deteriorating physical and cognitive health renders older adults increasingly susceptible to loneliness [50]. Moreover, social interaction and participation are two significant protective factors against loneliness. Infrequent interactions of older adults with relatives, neighbors, and friends, insufficient social engagement in voluntary and religious activities, and restricted routine activities may lead to heightened feelings of loneliness, aligning with our findings [51]. The findings of our study underscore the imperative to implement interventions that engage older adults in activities, enhance social interactions, and promote community participation to mitigate the risk of loneliness. Our study revealed a greater prevalence of loneliness among divorced individuals and older adults living without family. Prior studies identified that separated or widowed older individuals, as well as those living alone, were significant risk factors for the development of loneliness among the aged population, corroborating our findings. Widowed or separated women lack substantial social security and support, resulting in profound feelings of loneliness [47, 49]. Consequently, our study indicates that the government and NGOs should proactively assist these women through social safety net programs. Retired older people encounter a decline in social connections, while physical ailments restrict their participation in social activities. Additionally, the absence of spouses diminishes their social support, thereby weakening social bonds and resulting in loneliness, which is consistent with our findings [20]. In our study, the prevalence of loneliness was higher among illiterate individuals. Illiteracy impedes access to information regarding community events and resources, constraining social engagement and participation, thereby heightening the probability of loneliness [51].

Enhancing the mental health system in a resource-limited nation like Bangladesh presents various challenges, requiring the application of pertinent research findings and policy-level initiatives for the sustainable advancement of mental healthcare. The current study underscores the significance of recognizing psychosocial issues in the older population to develop a context-specific, inclusive intervention strategy, thereby contributing to the existing body of evidence in the literature. Tackling psychosocial issues such as depression, anxiety, insomnia, and loneliness in older adults may significantly contribute to alleviating and managing these mental health concerns among older people in Bangladesh. Psychotherapists and health care providers can extract significant insights from these studies to improve their therapeutic methodologies. Considering the long-term consequences of population aging in Bangladesh, it is essential to implement various multisectoral strategies to prioritize the mental health of older adults, ensuring their well-being, and creating a comprehensive system to address the increasing geriatric population in the future.

Strengths and limitations of this study

The large participant pool of our study improves mental health evaluations, fortifies the research, and offers valuable insights into potential correlations between psychometric assessment scales and the diverse sociodemographic characteristics of the older population in Bangladesh. First of all, the study seeks to elucidate the association between psychometric assessment parameters and various sociodemographic characteristics, as well as lifestyle-related factors, to ascertain the prevalence and associated risk factors of mental health issues among the geriatric population in Bangladesh. Secondly, the study investigates the interrelationship among different mental health conditions or psychological distress. Thirdly, the data collection from various locations around Bangladesh facilitated the incorporation of diverse socioeconomic backgrounds and contributed to the acquisition of comprehensive information on various psychometric assessment scales and sociodemographic characteristics, hence strengthening the robustness of the study. The translated Bangla questionnaire facilitated an in-depth understanding of the inquiries. Ultimately, the findings of this study possess significant implications for future context-specific research on diverse psychosocial problems, underscoring the necessity of thorough mental health evaluation and further investigation among older individuals and other age demographics in Bangladesh.

The present study also has some limitations. The cross-sectional design of the study restricts the ability to determine causal linkages and monitor temporal changes. Future research utilizing longitudinal studies to identify psychosocial problems through psychometric assessment scales is essential and would further validate our existing findings. Moreover, while validated tools such as the PHQ-9, GAD-7, AIS-8, and UCLA-3 were used to assess symptoms of depression, anxiety, sleep disturbances, and loneliness, these scales were not formally validated within the Bangladeshi population. Although the instruments were translated into Bangla following standard procedures, the absence of rigorous validation limits the interpretability and generalizability of the findings. The piloting of the study occasionally failed to yield precise insight into respondents’ comprehension of the questions. A few of the participants required assistance from close family members due to hearing, language, or emotional dependency, which may have introduced response bias despite efforts to minimize external influence on their answers. In addition, the study did not assess participants’ cognitive function, which may affect the accuracy of self-reported data. Some participants may have had cognitive impairments or dementia that influenced their ability to comprehend and respond accurately to the survey questions. Lastly, while the current study identified significant correlations between mental health disorders and various sociodemographic factors, among others, the potential mechanisms through which these variables influence mental health conditions are not explored herein and fall outside the purview of our present investigation.

Conclusion

A significant portion of the geriatric population in Bangladesh experiences mental health issues. Our findings underscore the imperative for comprehensive strategies to tackle the challenges associated with these psychosocial issues at both national and global levels. We recommend intensive mental healthcare services for the older population in Bangladesh. Therefore, local healthcare providers must implement and conduct regular screenings, counseling, and awareness initiatives for the older population in the community. In addition, further investigations employing enhanced study designs are necessary to validate the preliminary findings of our study.
