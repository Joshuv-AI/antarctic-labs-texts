Comparing microlearning and flipped classroom methods to enhance arterial blood gas interpretation learning in nurse anaesthesia students using Kirkpatrick’s evaluation model: a study protocol
Authors: Shaghayegh Miri, Afzal Shamsi, Sajad Yarahmadi, Elham Shahidi Delshad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionToday, arterial blood gas (ABG) testing is widely used in various hospital departments to support diagnosing diseases and predicting patient conditions. However, interpreting the numerical results of this test, its complex concepts continue to pose challenges for nurses and physicians. Therefore, the use of innovative educational approaches can simplify the learning of ABG interpretation. Two educational approaches that have recently received attention are the Flipped classroom and Microlearning. To evaluate and compare these approaches, we will develop a comprehensive evaluation protocol based on Kirkpatrick’s model.Methods and analysisThis study is a quasi-experimental design that will involve 188 undergraduate nurse anaesthesia students at Tehran University of Medical Sciences, randomly allocated to a flipped classroom or microlearning groups (94 per group). The intervention will consist of one session for all registered students and three sessions for each group with their own educational approach. Two sets of educational content, according to each approach method, will be developed based on a needs assessment and will be shared via secure social media. The flipped classroom group will receive pre-session materials, followed by in-person classes. The microlearning group will engage in in-person sessions supported by micro videos, podcasts and micro games delivered via Telegram. Effectiveness will be evaluated by using Kirkpatrick’s four-level model. 1 week after the intervention concludes, participants will be invited to a meeting to complete the Training Satisfaction Rating scale (Level 1, Reaction). We will use a pre-test and a post-test for evaluating Level 2 (Learning). Level 3 (Behaviour) will be evaluated through observations of ABG interpretation at internship sites. Level 4 (Results) will be evaluated via semi-structured interviews with specialists and colleagues. Data will be analysed blind; normality will be assessed (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test), and appropriate statistical tests (t-tests, ANCOVA, χ2 or Fisher’s exact) will be applied.Ethics and disseminationThis study received permission and ethical approval from Tehran University of Medical Sciences (IR.TUMS.SPH.REC.1404.08). Since the study is not clinical, obtaining a trial registration number is not required under Iranian research policy, and an ethical approval alone is sufficient. Informed consent will be obtained before participants are enrolled. The study’s results will ultimately be submitted as a research paper to a peer-reviewed journal.Keywords: EDUCATION & TRAINING (see Medical Education & Training), Nursing research, Nurses, Knowledge

Introduction

These days, arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis has become more important.1 The success of ABG analysis begins with an understanding of the blood pH level and knowing the impact of acidosis and alkalosis on the body, as that knowledge plays a critical role in therapeutic decision making.2 3 This test is recognised as a standard assessment tool in various hospital departments, especially in the operating room and intensive care unit, where it is considered a valuable tool for assessing various diseases.2 4 5

Nurses play a vital role in interpreting ABGs, conducting this interpretation both under physician supervision and independently to monitor and evaluate the patient’s clinical condition.3 5 To perform this role more effectively and with greater clinical insight, nurses must be capable of accurately analysing ABG results and identifying critical challenges in the patient’s condition.6 Although ABG interpretation provides rapid and valuable diagnostic information, its complexity of interpretation continues to remain a challenge for healthcare providers.5 The numerical result of the ABG test can complicate interpretation and lead to disagreement in diagnosis. Additionally, this complexity may also lead to errors during interpretation.7 Zaboli et al. In 2025, it was demonstrated that the overall performance of healthcare professionals in interpreting ABGs remains below the desired standard, with particularly significant differences in agreement with physicians about critical clinical conditions.5 On the other side, the integration of digital tools and electronic learning modules can enhance the learning experience of ABG analysis.8 9 The best way to make informed decisions about selecting the most effective educational methods for students lies in comprehensive research.10 In this context, various teaching strategies can facilitate student learning.11

Today, continuous changes in various fields lead to the emergence of new concepts and strategies; learning processes and approaches are no exception to this rule.12 As a result of these changes, the flipped classroom approach has become widely trending in recent years.13

The flipped classroom is an innovative educational approach that challenges traditional teaching methods. In this approach, students engage with instructional content prior to attending class, and then they have more opportunities for participation in interactive and collaborative activities during in-person sessions.14 15 To expand on this, pre-session activities play a critical role in the success of the flipped classroom approach.16 Pre-session educational content can include video lectures, quizzes, slides, podcasts, handouts and presentations delivered through various technologies.17 18 In-class activities are designed to focus students on applying the course content and gaining a deeper understanding of the educational content. These activities can be conducted individually or in groups.18 19 Previous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of the flipped classroom in improving nursing students’ clinical skills.20 Furthermore, it has been suggested that future research should focus on the long-term effects of this method and compare various innovative teaching models to provide more effective recommendations for medical education.21

Another learning approach is microlearning, which is recognised as an effective educational strategy characterised by learner-centredness, easy accessibility, interactivity and well-designed content.22 23 Microlearning facilitates the student learning process through concise and brief learning units that can be integrated into daily routines and utilised.24 In this approach, extensive educational content is divided into small, comprehensible segments that can typically be studied in a short amount of time and accessed at any time, from anywhere. These segments are usually designed to last between 3 and 10 min, enabling rapid and effective learning.25 26 In microlearning, educational content can be presented in various formats, such as videos, images, animations, podcasts, infographics and gamification.27 In addition, researchers believe that microlearning should be considered a promising research approach.28

On the other hand, education is a complex activity, and this complexity presents a significant challenge in evaluating approaches aimed at improving educational processes.29 One of the models used for evaluating educational programmes is Kirkpatrick’s evaluation model.30 Several studies have pointed to the positive impact of using Kirkpatrick’s evaluation model.31 This evaluation can reveal the strengths and weaknesses of a training course.32 Kirkpatrick’s model comprises four levels: reaction, learning, behaviour and results.33 The first level, reaction, focuses on evaluating participants’ satisfaction with the educational programme. At this stage, participants’ feelings about the training programme are assessed, and their perceptions of the educational experience are measured. The second level of Kirkpatrick’s model, learning, evaluates the increase in knowledge or skills resulting from the educational programme. This assessment typically involves pre-tests and post-tests to measure learning outcomes. The third level, behaviour, assesses changes in participants’ behaviour and performance in the workplace. The final level of the Kirkpatrick’s model, results, evaluates the impact of the training on organisational outcomes, such as improvements in key performance indicators or reductions in workplace errors.33 34 This evaluation model is compatible with many educational environments and can be applied in various fields, especially medical training.35 It is noteworthy that previous studies on the two new educational approaches, microlearning and flipped classroom, have so far been examined separately, and direct comparisons between them have rarely been made. Additionally, most existing research has focused on the lower levels of Kirkpatrick’s evaluation model. This highlights the need for research examining these approaches at higher levels of Kirkpatrick’s model. In this context, the present study is designed as a comparative effectiveness evaluation between two innovative instructional approaches, rather than an efficacy trial against traditional teaching. Accordingly, this study protocol aims to design and conduct a comparative study of the effectiveness of microlearning and flipped classroom approaches on learning ABG analysis, using Kirkpatrick’s four-level evaluation model.

Methods and analysis

Study design and setting

This protocol presents a study designed for a quasi-experimental educational intervention with a pre-test and post-test design that includes all undergraduate nurse anaesthesia students at Tehran University of Medical Sciences. The protocol will evaluate the effectiveness of two innovative teaching approaches, microlearning and flipped classroom, for training ABG interpretation in Tehran University of Medical Sciences. The project is scheduled to commence in January 2026 and is expected to be completed by January 2027.

Data collection

A demographic questionnaire will be used to collect information on participants’ age, gender, academic semester, year of university entry and overall grade point average. The effectiveness of the educational intervention will be evaluated using Kirkpatrick’s evaluation model.

Primary outcome

Training satisfaction (Level I: Reaction): For the first level of the Kirkpatrick’s model, reaction, we will use a training satisfaction rating (TSR) scale,36 a 12-item instrument designed to measure participants’ overall satisfaction with the training. The TSR scale covers topics including ‘objectives and content’, ‘Method and Training Context’ and ‘usefulness and overall rating’ of the training. The content validity and construct validity of this scale have been confirmed, and its reliability has also been reported, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.888. Participants will respond to each item on a five point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Totally Disagree) to 5 (Totally Agree).

learning improvement (Level II: Learning): We will develop two standard multiple choice tests that cover the course content, designed as a pre-test and a post-test. To ensure content validity, these tests will be reviewed and approved by a panel of 10 faculty members. To assess the reliability of the tests, the questions will be given to a separate group of 30 students from a similar population who will not participate in the study. The internal consistency of the tests will be evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha.

Secondary outcomes

Practical application skills (Level III: Behaviour): The third level of the model will be measured by evaluating students’ ability to interpret a real ABG sample during their internship.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria will be undergraduate nurse anaesthesia students who participate in the course, must have access to social media and have not previously attended classes related to the interpretation of ABGs. Students will be excluded if they do not want to continue participating, fail to complete either the pre-test or post-test, do not attend all course sessions in full, or have prior clinical work experience related to ABG interpretation.

Content and educational materials

A needs assessment conducted among undergraduate nurse anaesthesia students at Tehran University of Medical Sciences identified deficiencies in the skills of interpreting ABGs. Based on this, we designed a course that has one session for all students who registered, and after that, we will separate the students and we will have three sessions for each group. In each session, educational content will be presented following the specific objectives of that session (table 1). A panel of 10 faculty members evaluated the content validity of the educational materials. Each member of the panel was reviewing the content for clarity and relevance to ensure that the materials align with the educational objectives of the study. Subsequently, based on our study objectives, two sets of educational materials were developed: one set designed according to the microlearning approach, and the other designed as flipped classroom. These materials were also evaluated by a panel of 10 faculty members.

Sample size consideration

The sample size for this study was determined using the standard formula for comparing the means of two independent groups, referencing a previous similar study.37 Based on the calculations, 84 participants will be required in each group (flipped classroom and microlearning). To account for an expected 10% dropout rate, the sample size was increased to 94 participants per group, resulting in a total of 188 participants. This sample size provides 90% power to detect statistically significant differences between the groups, assuming an effect size of 0.5 and a 95% confidence level. Participants will be recruited through convenience sampling and will be randomly allocated to either the flipped classroom or microlearning group.

Registration

The first place involves initiating registration for the course. There will be advertising for the course registration. During the registration process, students will be provided with an overview of the study, including its objectives, nature and inclusion criteria. Eligible students who express interest will be enrolled in the course.

Course implementation

In this phase, an educational course will be conducted, with everyone participating in a general session and then with each group attending three in-person sessions that each last about 2 hours for 4 weeks. All eligible students who express interest in participating will be invited to the first session. The first session will be conducted following the course objectives. During this session, participants will receive additional information about the course, study procedures, questionnaire completion process, study objectives and the benefits of participation. They will also be randomised into two groups. To address potential curiosity among students, they will all receive clear instructions about the confidentiality of the educational content. To further minimise cross-contamination, in-person sessions for the two groups will be held at separate times and in different classrooms to reduce the chance of inadvertent observation of instructional activities. Access to digital educational materials will be restricted to private Telegram groups with disabled forwarding and downloading functions.

Randomisation procedure

In the first session, each participant will be assigned an identification number. Participants will then be randomly allocated to either the flipped classroom or microlearning group using blind randomisation. Simple randomisation will be performed using a random number table. The allocation data will be recorded in a list that includes each participant’s identification number and the group to which they will be assigned. To ensure the accuracy of the randomisation process, the allocation will be independently verified by two researchers.

Contamination Monitoring

Because both groups study within the same educational environment, monitoring potential cross-contamination is essential. At the end of the intervention, a brief structured post-intervention check will be conducted to document whether students were inadvertently exposed to the other group’s instructional content, engaged in informal discussions about learning activities or encountered shared digital materials. This information will allow us to qualitatively assess the extent of potential contamination and will be taken into account when interpreting the comparative outcomes.

Group Setup and Content Access

Based on the randomised allocation, separate groups will be created on the Telegram platform for each group to facilitate effective communication with all students and share educational materials. Each group will be given exclusive access to its designated educational materials. Additionally, to prevent bias and unapproved access, user accounts used on social media will be private, and downloading or saving the content will not be permitted.

Data analysis

Data analysis will be conducted in a blinded manner to maintain objectivity, with the analyst being uninformed of group assignments. Descriptive statistics will be used to summarise demographic characteristics (age, gender, academic semester and overall grade point average) and questionnaire responses, reporting measures of central tendency (mean, median) and dispersion (SD, range) for quantitative variables, and frequencies and percentages for qualitative variables. The normality of quantitative data will be assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed data will be analysed using independent t-tests and paired t-tests. If normality is not established, the corresponding non-parametric tests will be used. If adjustment for covariates is required, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) will be applied. Qualitative variables will be compared between groups using χ2 tests or Fisher’s exact test, where appropriate (table 2).

Patient and public involvement

No patients or public participation will be involved in the design, recruitment, or implementation of the study. Students will not be involved in the design of this study beyond participating as study subjects. The research question was formulated based on a formal needs assessment conducted among undergraduate nurse anaesthesia students at Tehran University of Medical Sciences, which identified deficiencies in ABG interpretation skills.

Ethics and dissemination

This study received permission and ethical approval from Tehran University of Medical Sciences on June 29, 2025, with reference code IR.TUMS.SPH.REC.1404.081. Although international guidelines such as those recommended by the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors encourage prospective registration for all pre-specified interventional studies, current national research policy in Iran does not permit registration of non-clinical educational interventions in the national clinical trial registry. As a result, the present study was not eligible for registration under local regulatory frameworks. To ensure full transparency, the complete protocol, including all predefined outcomes and analytic plans, is provided within this manuscript, and no outcome modifications will be made after the initiation of data collection. Participation in this study is entirely voluntary. The educational intervention is not part of the students’ mandatory curriculum but was developed as an additional learning opportunity based on identified educational needs. Informed consent will be obtained by a research assistant who is not involved in teaching or grading to avoid any perception of coercion. Students who choose not to participate will continue to receive all standard ABG-related educational content through the routine curriculum without any disadvantage. All personal information and assessment results will remain confidential, and on completion of the study, all instructional materials will be made available to all students. The study’s results will be submitted to esteemed journals and will be presented at both national and international conferences.

Discussion

This is a protocol for a randomised experimental study that evaluates the effectiveness of two innovative educational approaches (flipped classroom and microlearning) for teaching ABG interpretation. While different teaching methods have been used to teach ABG interpretation,10 38 our research has not identified a study that has identified one approach as superior and has also evaluated its effectiveness using Kirkpatrick’s four-level evaluation model. By comparing these two approaches, this research aims to determine an effective, practical and digitally compatible method to enhance ABG interpretation skills among undergraduate nurse anaesthesia students, in this way contributing to the advancement of modern medical education. Like any research, this study faces some limitations and challenges. For instance, interactions among students within shared learning environments may result in knowledge transfer and the creation of initial prior knowledge that will affect the results of this study. To control for these conditions, an appropriate statistical method will be used to adjust for the initial difference between the participants’ prior knowledge. In addition, recognising that cross-group contamination cannot be fully eliminated in real-world educational settings, a brief structured post-intervention check will be conducted to document any inadvertent exposure of students to the other group’s instructional content or learning processes. These data will enable qualitative monitoring of potential contamination and will be considered when interpreting the comparative findings of the study. By evaluating educational effectiveness at the four levels of Kirkpatrick’s evaluation model, this research provides evidence to support the innovative teaching methods in medical education. Given the natural interactions within a shared academic environment, this study reflects real-world conditions of educational delivery. Therefore, the comparative findings should be interpreted within an effectiveness-oriented rather than tightly controlled efficacy framework. Furthermore, the absence of a lecture-based control group limits our ability to determine whether the observed effects surpass those achieved through traditional ABG instruction. To partially address this, historical academic performance data from previous cohorts will be reviewed to provide contextual reference for interpreting the findings. Future research should incorporate a third instructional arm representing standard teaching to establish absolute benchmarks of effectiveness and should further evaluate these approaches across diverse educational settings and populations to enhance generalisability and assess long-term outcomes.
