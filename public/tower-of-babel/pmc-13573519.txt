Associations of light and moderate-to-vigorous physical activity with health-related outcomes: findings from the FINFIT 2017 and 2021 studies
Authors: Pauliina Husu, Henri Vähä-Ypyä, Kari Tokola, Olli-Pekka Nuuttila, Harri Sievänen, Ilkka Heinonen, Tommi Vasankari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealth associations of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) are well-established, but those of light physical activity (LPA) are much less studied. This study analysed associations of LPA and MVPA assessed both in absolute and relative terms with predicted maximum oxygen consumption (VO2max), perceived health, physical and mental quality of life (QoL), and depressive symptoms.Materials and methodsThe study is based on pooled data of two cross-sectional population-based FINFIT studies. PA was measured by a triaxial hip-worn accelerometer among 20–69-year-old adults (N = 3587, 62% women). VO2max was predicted by the 6 min walk test and analysed in sex- and age-group-specific tertiles. Other outcomes were self-reported. Absolute LPA was defined as 1.5–2.9 metabolic equivalents (METs) and MVPA as ≥3 METs. Cut points for relative LPA were ≥1.5 METs and <40% of oxygen uptake reserve (VO2R), and for MVPA ≥40% of VO2R. Data were analysed by general linear model (GLM) multivariate analysis adjusted for sex and age-groups.ResultsIn absolute terms, participants spent daily 3.6 h in LPA and 48 min in MVPA. In relative terms, the corresponding times were 4.1 h and 19 min. Participants with the poorest fitness accumulated less absolute and relative LPA, less absolute MVPA, and more relative MVPA than those with better fitness (p < 0.001). Longer daily times of absolute and relative LPA and absolute MVPA were associated with better perceived health, mental and physical QoL and fewer depressive symptoms (p < 0.05).ConclusionsDaily time of both absolute and relative LPA and absolute MVPA was associated with better predicted VO2max, perceived health, QoL and fewer depressive symptoms, whereas relative MVPA indicated the opposite or no association.Keywords: Accelerometry, health, intensity, population study

Introduction

Light physical activity (LPA) has typically been defined as any physical activity that increases the energy expenditure from the resting state to 1.5–2.9 metabolic equivalents (METs). Examples of LPA include casual walking, light household chores, body-mind activities like light pilates and yoga exercises, and incidental activities that do not substantially increase heart rate or breathing rate [1].

Although some inconsistencies in the terminology used to categorize physical activity (PA) intensity have been noticed [2], health benefits of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) are well-established [3,4], while the effects of LPA have remained much less studied [5]. According to review studies, frequent short bouts of LPA can decrease adiposity and waist circumference and improve blood pressure, lipidaemia, and glycaemic control [6,7]. LPA has also been reported to be associated with better metabolic flexibility [8] and better insulin sensitivity and favourable plasma lipid profile [9]. Several review studies [6,7,10,11] and a recent causal inference analysis [12] have shown that PA, even in light intensity, has potential to reduce the risk for all-cause mortality, especially among individuals with low levels of MVPA [12]. According to a recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Nuuttila et al. [13], low intensity exercise training improves aerobic fitness and cardiometabolic health (total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglycerides, and blood pressure), although intensity seems to influence the magnitude of fitness adaptations [13].

Besides cardiometabolic benefits, LPA seems to be beneficial for cognitive functions like executive functions and memory [14], and a higher amount of LPA may buffer against age-related cognitive decline [15]. However, there is limited and partly contradictory evidence on mental health benefits of LPA. According to a systematic review by Felez-Nobrega et al. [16], the evidence on the associations between LPA and depression, anxiety, psychological stress and overall mental health is inconclusive and a higher intensity activity, corresponding to MVPA, may be needed for achieving these benefits. Thus, LPA seems to be beneficial for several cardiometabolic risk factors, executive functions, memory and mortality risk. Furthermore, more LPA and MVPA seem to be associated with younger phenotypic age, and replacement of sedentary time with LPA or MVPA may be associated with a lower biological age burden. Thus, maintaining a certain level of PA could delay the process of aging and intensity matters [17]. However, to the best of our knowledge, the associations of LPA with several perceived indicators of health and well-being have not yet been studied.

LPA covers over one-fifth of waking hours among working-aged adults [18], and it accounts for most daily movement [5]. Thus, it may have great potential for substituting stationary behaviour [19] and achieving above-described health benefits. LPA may be a feasible option to be physically active among individuals who are unable or unwilling to engage in more intense PA. LPA comprises mainly of unstructured movements that can easily be incorporated into everyday life. Increasing LPA may also entail fewer potential barriers compared to more intense PA or more structured forms of exercise, which generally require more time, energy, skills, equipment, costs and facilities [16]. Frequent bouts of LPA seem beneficial especially for inactive individuals and for those having metabolic challenges [7].

In many previous studies, for example [6,7], LPA has typically been defined according to absolute intensity. Besides absolute MET-values, PA intensity can be defined in relation to an individual’s fitness level, considering individual variation in fitness and functional ability [2,20,21]. Previous studies have assessed the relative intensity based on the oxygen uptake reserve (VO2R), reserve between the resting and maximum oxygen uptake level [22,23], maximal oxygen consumption [21,24], maximum heart rate [25] or perceived exertion [25]. However, to our knowledge, previous studies have focused mostly on MVPA, while studies on the relative LPA in relation to perceived health outcomes have remained scarce. The purpose of the present study was to analyse the associations of LPA and MVPA, assessed both in absolute and relative terms, with predicted maximum oxygen consumption (VO2max), perceived health, physical and mental quality of life (QoL), and depressive symptoms. The hypothesis was that more PA, regardless of the intensity or terms to define it, would be associated with better cardiorespiratory fitness, perceived health, better physical and mental QoL and fewer depressive symptoms.

Materials and methods

This study is based on pooled data of two cross-sectional population-based studies, FINFIT2017 and FINFIT2021, which are multifactorial studies on PA, fitness and health conducted with stratified random samples of 20–69-year-old Finnish adults drawn from the population registry in seven city-centred regions of Finland [18,26]. In 2017, 300 men and 300 women from both Helsinki and Tampere regions and 150 men and 150 women from each of the Turku, Kuopio, Jyväskylä, Oulu and Rovaniemi regions were drawn and spread across five age groups (20–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59 and 60–69 years). In 2021, 150 men and 150 women from Tampere, Turku, Kuopio, Jyväskylä, Oulu and Rovaniemi regions were drawn from the registry in the five age groups. From Helsinki region, the sampling was 300 men and women in each age group. Further, an additional sample was drawn from nine surrounding municipalities in Tampere (7 × 500) and Turku (2 × 500) regions. Other inclusion or exclusion criteria were not used. The recruitment and data collection periods of the study were from August 2017 to March 2019 and from August 2021 to April 2022. The data collection comprised three parts: (1) health and fitness examinations at the local research centres; (2) accelerometer-measured PA, standing, sedentary behaviour and time in bed 24/7 over one week (7 consecutive days); and (3) a health-related questionnaire either online or in paper form [26].

The present data are pooled from cross-sectional FINFIT 2017 and FINFIT 2021 studies. Both studies included similar population sampling, the same health and fitness examinations, same methods to assess PA, standing, sedentary behaviour, and time in bed and similar health-related questionnaire. The pooled data included all 3587 participants who wore accelerometers for at least four days 24 h per day and performed the 6 min walk test. The studies were carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The Regional Ethics Committee of the Expert Responsibility Area of Tampere University Hospital approved the studies (R17030 and R2105). All participants gave written informed consent before participating.

Measurement of physical activity

Physical activity in terms of LPA and MVPA was measured using a tri-axial accelerometer (UKK RM42, UKK Terveyspalvelut Oy, Tampere, Finland) 24/7. During waking hours, the accelerometer was attached to an elastic belt and worn on the right side of the hip, excluding water-based activities. The accelerometer collected and stored raw triaxial data in actual g-units in ±16 G range at a 100 Hz sampling rate [26].

During waking hours, the mean amplitude deviation (MAD) was calculated from the resultant acceleration signal in 6 s epochs, and the epoch-wise acceleration values were converted to METs (1 MET = 3.5 ml(O2)/kg/min) [23,27]. The MAD classifies PA intensity into LPA or MVPA with at least 97% sensitivity and specificity irrespective of the accelerometer brand [27]. The epoch-wise MET values were further smoothed by calculating a one-minute exponential moving average. Using the smoothed MET values, the total PA was classified as absolute intensities based on daily means as follows: LPA (1.5–2.9 METs), moderate PA (3.0–5.9 METs) and vigorous PA (6 METs or more). In the present study, moderate and vigorous PA were combined into MVPA. Besides absolute intensities, relative intensities denoting the reserve between the resting and maximum oxygen uptake level, were also defined: for LPA, the cut points were ≥1.5 METs and <40% of VO2R, and for MVPA, ≥3.0 METs, and ≥40% of VO2R [28].

Assessment of cardiorespiratory fitness

Prediction of VO2max was based on the 6 min walk test [29] measured at the health examinations of the studies. The participants were asked to walk back and forth along the 15 m walking track as fast as possible for 6 min and their heart rate was recorded with a heart rate monitor (Polar M61, Polar Electro, Kempele, Finland). Weight in kilograms and height in meters were measured, and body mass index (BMI, kg/m2) was used as an indicator of body composition. These measurements were conducted with light clothing without shoes before the 6 min walk test. For men, the prediction of VO2max was based on the distance walked in 6 min, age, BMI, heart rate and height; and for women, on the distance walked in 6 min, weight and age [29]. In the validation study [29], these predictors explained 82% and 79% of men’s and women’s measured VO2max with the standard error of estimate of 3.6 ml kg−1 min−1 and 3.5 ml kg−1 min−1, respectively.

Assessment of the outcome variables

The outcome variables included predicted VO2max described above, perceived health status, physical and mental QoL, and self-reported depressive symptoms. VO2max was predicted by the 6 min walk test as described above and result was categorized into age group and sex specific tertiles. Perceived health status and physical and mental QoL and depressive symptoms were assessed by the study questionnaire. Perceived health was assessed by asking the participants how they perceived their health status, and the answer options were: 1 = excellent, 2 = fairly good, 3 = good, 4 = fair and 5 = poor. In the analysis, the 4th and 5th categories were combined. QoL was also assessed as a part of the self-reported questionnaire by the Finnish version of the Rand-36 [30]. Both physical and mental components of QoL were assessed. The physical component included questions on physical functioning, role physical functioning, bodily pain, general health and vitality. The mental component was assessed in terms of social functioning, role emotional functioning and mental health. Higher QoL score for both physical and mental component indicated better QoL and for the final analysis, the score was categorized into quartiles (the best quartile, better quartile, poorer quartile and the poorest quartile) based on the distribution of the variable. Depressive symptoms were assessed by the modified nine-item Finnish version of the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9-mFIN) [31]. The items were adapted from the original nine-item version of the PHQ [32]. In the modified version, participants reported how often they had been bothered by any of the following problems over the past week: lacking enthusiasm for doing anything, feeling depressed, having trouble getting to sleep or staying asleep, feeling low in energy, or slowed down, having a poor appetite, crying easily or feeling like crying, feeling bored or having little interest in doing things, feeling yourself lonely and feeling hopeless about the future. The response options were 0 = hardly ever; 1 = seldom; 2 = often; 3 = very often, which were summed up as a total score used both as a continuous variable (0–27) and categorical variable: 0–4 = no symptoms, 5–9 = mild symptoms and ≥10 = at least moderate symptoms [31]. The internal consistency of the PHQ-9-mFIN has been reported to be high and the test–retest repeatability moderate. Furthermore, the PHQ-9-mFIN has shown excellent construct validity against the mental component items and their summary scale of the RAND-36 health-related QoL among female nursing personnel [31].

Statistical analysis

Multivariable analysis of general linear model (GLM) was used to analyse the associations of daily mean times of absolute and relative LPA and MVPA with cardiorespiratory fitness, perceived health, QoL and depressive symptoms. In the main analysis, the outcome variables were categorized to make the presentation of the results easier to interpret. However, for sensitivity analysis and to improve statistical power, we conducted the analysis also with continuous variables for VO2max, QoL and depressive symptoms (PHQ-9-mFIN). Adjustments were performed by age group and sex and further by education, marital status, BMI, smoking and employment status.

Further, receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess the discriminatory potential of both absolute and relative LPA and MVPA to identify poor cardiorespiratory fitness (the poorest tertile), fair or poor perceived health, poorest fourth of both physical and mental QoL and moderate to high depressive symptoms. Areas under the curve (AUC) values with their statistical significance were analysed. All analyses were conducted using the SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics, V.29, Armonk, NY).

Results

Majority (62%) of the 3587 participants were females. The participants’ mean age was 50.4 years (SD 13.2). More than half (55%) of the participants were married, and one-fourth (25%) had a master’s degree (Table 1). Slightly over one-fourth (23%) of the participants were out of work and nearly one-third (31%) were senior salaried employees. Slightly over one-tenth (11%) reported smoking and over two-fifths (43%) had BMI 25 kg/m2 or less. Over half (58%) of the participants reported at least mild depressive symptoms, women more than men. Women also perceived their mental QoL to be poorer compared to that of men.

Absolute and relative LPA showed generally consistent favourable associations with the outcomes when age group and sex were adjusted for (Figure 1, Supplementary Table 1). The associations of absolute MVPA showed similar patterns to LPA for several outcomes, while relative MVPA indicated different patterns. In more detail, participants in the poorest fitness tertile accumulated on average less absolute and relative LPA, less absolute MVPA and more relative MVPA than those in the middle and best fitness tertiles (p < 0.001) (Figure 1(A)). Correspondingly, participants in the middle fitness tertile accumulated less absolute and relative LPA, less absolute MVPA and more relative MVPA than those in the best fitness tertile. Both absolute and relative LPA and absolute MVPA were positively associated with physical and mental QoL (Figure 1(C,D)), but more relative LPA and absolute MVPA had stronger associations with both components of QoL than absolute LPA. Relative MVPA indicated no association either with physical or mental QoL. Correspondingly, more absolute and relative LPA and absolute MVPA were associated with better perceived health (Figure 1(B)) and fewer depressive symptoms (Figure 1(E)) while participants with poor perceived health and moderate to high level of depressive symptoms accumulated the greatest minutes of relative MVPA.

Discussion

This study analysed the associations of LPA and MVPA, assessed both in absolute and relative terms, with predicted VO2max, perceived health, physical and mental QoL, and self-reported depressive symptoms. The main finding of the study was that more LPA, both in absolute and relative terms, was associated with positive health outcomes, i.e. better cardiorespiratory fitness and perceived health, better physical and mental QoL, and fewer depressive symptoms. The associations of absolute MVPA were similar to those of LPA, whereas relative MVPA indicated weaker and opposite associations. More relative MVPA was associated with poorer fitness and health and more depressive symptoms, which may reflect that people with poorer fitness may more easily reach relatively high intensity even during ordinary daily activities and they may experience higher relative physiological burden during daily activities [23]. In absolute terms, individuals with better fitness can reach MVPA threshold with lower relative effort. They have more reserve above the MVPA threshold allowing lower physical strain during daily activities. Therefore, the absolute threshold is sensitive for both the movement performed and level of fitness (VO2max).

Regarding LPA, a higher amount of activity was beneficially associated with health outcomes regardless of individual fitness level and thresholds used. Better fitness permits one to operate at a higher absolute intensity level, if needed, but strenuous and exhausting PA may not be needed during daily routines. Too strenuous and exhausting PA may have negative health outcomes [33], and therefore, it is reasonable to use relative thresholds when available. However, due to circularity between relative intensity variables and predicted VO2max as the outcome the findings need to be interpreted with caution. Among working-aged adults, VO2max of 30 ml/kg/min seems to be a reasonable cut-off value for poor physical functioning [34] and higher fitness can protect from strenuous PA during daily routines [35].

In the present study, we used the proportions of VO2R to define relative intensities which consider both maximum and resting VO2 of a person. We used <40% of VO2R as the cut-point for LPA and ≥40% of VO2R for MVPA. According to the results, VO2R of less than 40% had similar associations with measured cardiorespiratory fitness and perceived outcomes of health and well-being, while 40% or higher weakened the associations and turned them in the negative direction. These findings support the notion that insufficient cardiorespiratory fitness relative to the physical demands of daily chores or work tasks may contribute to adverse health outcomes [36]. It has been found that individuals with greater cardiorespiratory fitness (VO2max) need to utilize a lower fraction of their heart rate reserve during working hours [37]. Utilizing a greater fraction of heart rate reserve during work has also associated with impaired recovery [38] and tiredness after work associated with less leisure-time PA [39]. Thus, when the daily routines do not strain the person too much, people may have more resources for leisure-time activities at varying intensities and thus potential for improving their cardiorespiratory fitness.

According to Swain and Franklin [40], individuals with higher fitness (initial VO2max > 40 ml/kg/min) can expect improvements in VO2max by training intensities of at least 45% of VO2R. In contrast, individuals with lower fitness (initial VO2max < 40 ml/kg/min) obtain improvements in VO2max with training intensities as low as 30% of VO2R [40]. Compared with low-fit individuals, high-fit ones reach the absolute target PA intensity more frequently, but regarding the relative intensities, the frequency of reaching the target is more similar between the low- and high-fit individuals [22]. Thus, adding some daily chores with light relative intensity might be enough to improve cardiorespiratory fitness of the low-fit individuals, although higher relative intensities are generally more effective at improving cardiorespiratory fitness. A recent meta-analysis [13] is in line with this interpretation indicating that there is no definitive minimum intensity of activity for cardiorespiratory fitness and other cardiometabolic adaptations, at least in sedentary individuals. However, in the intervention setting among sedentary adults with metabolic syndrome, changes in LPA did not correlate with improved VO2max, but total daily steps did [41]. Nevertheless, the changes in LPA, and also in MVPA, were inversely correlated with the changes in blood pressure during light-to-moderate intensity exercise in the same intervention setting [42], suggesting reduced cardiac load during light intensity activities, resulting also in reduced left ventricular mass [43].

In the present study, both absolute and relative LPA were associated with better cardiorespiratory fitness. Further, both absolute and relative LPA were associated with better perceived health, physical and mental QoL, and fewer depressive symptoms. Previously LPA has been linked with better cognitive functions [14], but associations with evidence on other mental health outcomes have remained limited and partly contradictory. Although causal inference cannot be drawn from the present cross-sectional associations, higher cardiorespiratory fitness, better perceived health status and physical and mental QoL as well as fewer depressive symptoms may give the people the energy and vigour to be more active and allow them to accumulate more LPA and absolute MVPA, while poor fitness, perceived health, poor physical QoL and more depressive symptoms may limit the ability to be active. Reduced sedentary behaviour has been shown to increase perceived vitality among physically inactive adults with metabolic syndrome in the intervention setting [44]. These perceived health-related outcomes may be indirectly and at least partly mediated by inflammatory-related associations of PA. A recent study [45] indicated that accelerometer-measured PA and sedentary behaviour are associated with C-reactive protein, which is a well-established marker of inflammation: higher sedentary time and lower levels of LPA or MVPA were associated with higher C-reactive protein levels. Further, replacing sedentary time with PA may be linked to a more favourable inflammatory profile [45].

In the present study, absolute cut points provided more MVPA minutes than relative ones. This is in line with Siddique et al. [25] who used MET units as an absolute scale, and percentage of maximum heart rate and ratings of perceived exertion as a relative scale. They reported three times more MVPA based on absolute cut points compared to relative ones. In the present study, the difference in the PA minutes between absolute and relative cut points was the greatest among the younger participants and declined with increasing age.

According to Fridolfsson et al. [21], both absolute and relative intensities were associated with health indicators. The strongest associations found for the absolute intensity depended on fitness levels, whereas the associations for the relative intensity were more synchronized across the fitness groups. In general, the dose–response relationships between MVPA and the health indicators observed by Fridolfsson et al. [21] were stronger for the relative than for the absolute intensity, contradicting with the present findings that found stronger associations for absolute MVPA. This discrepancy between the studies may be due to differences in health outcomes. Fridolfsson et al. [21] used measured waist circumference, systolic blood pressure, high-density lipoprotein, triglycerides and glycated haemoglobin as indicators of health whereas the present study was based on perceived health indicators. Further, the age range of the study population in the study by Fridolfsson et al. [21] was much narrower (55–64 years) than that of the present study (20–69 years).

Fridolfsson et al. [21] concluded that individuals with low fitness should start PA with intensity that is light on the absolute scale but corresponds to moderate on the relative scale and then progress to more intense PA. Despite expending the same amount of energy during certain activity, the two individuals may work at very different levels of relative intensity, resulting in different physiological stimuli and training adaptations [46]. Thus, in promoting MVPA among inactive, low-fit or obese individuals, relative intensity guidelines may be more feasible than absolute intensity classifications [22].

The main strength of the study is that PA was assessed with valid methods to identify absolute intensities of PA [20,23,35], and cardiorespiratory fitness was predicted by the 6 min walk test allowing the assessment of relative intensity of PA. Further, a relatively large population-based sample with a wide age range of adults aged 20–69 years is a strength of the study.

As limitations, accelerometer measurements are unable to identify activity context, water-based activities, cycling and sliding activities like skiing and skating, as well as some upper-body movements. Further, accelerometers may misclassify some activity types. The calculation of relative intensity was based on predicted rather than directly measured VO2max, and standard error of estimation for cardiorespiratory fitness was 3.5 ml/kg/min, which forms an important limitation of the study. The co-dependency between relative intensity exposure variables and predicted VO2max as one of the outcomes forms also a limitation. Further, the cross-sectional nature of the study does not allow any causal interpretation of the findings. Additionally, only 42% of the intended sample was reached, and just over half (51%) of these people agreed to participate in the study, which indicates selective participation and consequent bias [18]. The study participants were slightly more educated than the general population in Finland [47], which may have affected the generalizability of present findings. There is evidence that more educated individuals are physically more active [48] and have generally better health than people with low education [49].

Conclusions

Higher amount of LPA was beneficially associated with estimated maximal cardiorespiratory fitness and perceived health and QoL regardless of individual fitness level. The associations of higher intensity activities, like MVPA, were quite opposite according to whether the individual fitness level was considered or not. In general, absolute and relative intensities of PA offer different but complementary utilities. Absolute intensity has been considered the best for PA guideline adherence and relative intensity for personalized exercise prescription [50]. However, most of the previous studies have included only absolute intensity of PA. The present study supports the potential value of LPA in health promotion, but due to cross-sectional nature of the study it does not prove that increasing LPA will necessarily improve these outcomes. In the practice of PA promotion, it may be useful to promote LPA regardless of individual fitness level, but in promoting MVPA, it would be reasonable to use relative intensities whenever possible.
