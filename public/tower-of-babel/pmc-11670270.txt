Artificial Internalizing Receptors: Intracellular Delivery of Cargo Through Bio‐Orthogonal Recognition
Authors: Ane Bretschneider Søgaard, Rikke Fabech Hansson, Anne Selch Tvilum, Alexander N. Zelikin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrug targeting is a methodology that helps to overcome the side effects of therapeutic molecules. However, insufficient targeting specificity and the on‐target/off‐site delivery leave much room for improvement in the targeting endeavors. One approach to enhance the specificity of drug targeting is to engineer artificial receptors with recognition ligands not observed in nature. To this end, artificial internalizing receptors that feature cholesterylamine as the artificial pull‐in mechanism, and an anti‐fluorescein antibody as the exofacial recognition and capture tool are developed. Fluorescein labeling is among the most routine techniques in biochemistry and can readily provide a way to make cognate derivatives for receptor‐mediated endocytosis using these artificial receptors. Herein, the synthesis and the structure‐activity relationship for these artificial receptors are detailed, their potency and efficacy in mediating drug delivery for the antibody‐drug conjugates are illustrated, and the scope and limitations of targeting the chemically engineered cells via artificial receptors are investigated. Taken together, the presented data explore an innovative approach to drug targeting and contribute to the development of techniques in cell engineering using the tools of chemistry.

Introduction

Medicinal chemistry is a highly successful discipline of science. Indeed, from the identification of the molecular origin of a disease, through drug discovery and optimization, in many cases, it is now possible to correct the biomolecular malfunction and in so doing, to cure the disease or alleviate the disease symptoms.[ 1 , 2 , 3 ] The key to a successful drug is that this molecule exerts therapeutic activity without significant side effects. However, this is not always possible and specifically anticancer medication is often characterized by significant toxicity to the normal, non‐cancerous cells.[ 4 , 5 ] A powerful approach to deal with non‐specific effects relies on navigating the drug specifically to its nominated site of action. This opportunity is termed “drug targeting” and can be accomplished based on the physiology of the human tissues (passive drug targeting) or via the cognate biomolecular recognition events (active drug targeting).[ 5 , 6 , 7 ] The benefits of drug targeting are best illustrated by the success of the antibody‐drug conjugates (ADCs) that have been approved for marketing in the past few decades.[ 6 ] Outside the realm of ADCs, drug targeting has been accomplished using peptides, aptamers, and small molecules.[ 5 ] What unites all these endeavors is that targeting has most successfully been mediated by the natural cell surface receptors. Powerful in their own right, this can lead to on‐target / off‐tumor effects, which have been documented for ADCs and even for the chimeric antigen receptor T cells (CAR T cells).[ 8 , 9 ] In other words, there is room for improvement as regards the specificity of drug targeting.

A cunning methodology to enhance the specificity or even to enable drug targeting is to employ molecules that do not occur naturally in the body.[ 10 , 11 ] Arguably the most successful example of this methodology is targeting of cells that has been metabolically engineered using the so‐called “click chemistry”, which is bio‐orthogonal, biocompatible, and can be successfully realized in vivo.[ 11 , 12 , 13 ] At the core of this technology lies the metabolic labeling of the cell surface glycans, using the systemically administered sugars that are very similar to their natural counterpart but are equipped with specific chemical groups for subsequent modification via the click chemistry (azides or alkynes). Cancerous cells are characterized by enhanced rates of metabolism and for this reason, these cells intake, process, and present at their cell surface a higher content of modified sugars and the groups for click chemistry than the healthy cells. The subsequently administered (pro)drugs or drug carriers react to the metabolically engineered, cancerous cells preferentially as compared to the healthy cells. This methodology is highly successful, at least in preclinical settings.[ 11 , 12 , 13 ] However, metabolic labeling cannot be limited exclusively to the cancerous cells, and off‐target effects upon targeting cannot be excluded.

A highly innovative approach to enable drug targeting, developed specifically for the case of ex vivo engineered cells (e.g., CAR T cells or other cells for adoptive transplantation), relies on the engineering of artificial internalizing receptors.[ 10 , 14 , 15 ] In this case, the receptor‐ligand recognition pair can be engineered using antibodies against synthetic, non‐biological antigens, namely haptens. In one example, Roffler et al. engineered mammalian cells to express internalizing receptors with affinity to poly(ethylene glycol), PEG.[ 16 ] This polymer is recognized for its non‐fouling properties and is widely used in biomedicine, e.g., to enhance the circulation time of biological drugs.[ 17 ] The cells that were engineered to recognize PEG could therefore capture and internalize liposomes and nanoparticles that were decorated with PEG and that were essentially “invisible” to the non‐engineered cells.[ 16 ] In this example, the receptor design was based on an engineered, chimeric protein.[ 16 ]

In another example, Peterson et al. developed artificial internalizing receptors that are based on the tools of organic chemistry rather than proteins: these receptors featured fluorescein as an exofacial recognition ligand and contained cholesterylamine as a lipid bilayer anchor.[ 18 , 19 ] Cholesterylamine is a rather unique molecule that undergoes endocytosis and merges into the natural clathrin‐mediated uptake pathway, and thereafter is recycled back to the cell surface.[ 15 ] Fluorescein‐containing cholesterylamine receptors could interact with the anti‐fluorescein antibody at the cell surface and internalize the associated immunoglobulin.[ 15 ] In our own recent work, we developed this technique into a tool for drug delivery and illustrated that artificial receptors afford nanomolar potency of drug delivery using the cognate ADC.[ 20 ] The artificial receptors performed well even in hard‐to‐transfect cells such as primary human T cells.[ 20 ] In our hands, the artificial receptors afforded the delivery of ADC that was more potent and at the same time significantly faster compared to the corresponding free drug (monomethyl auristatin F).[ 21 ] In a mixed cell culture, the artificial receptor and the administered ADC could specifically eliminate only the receptor‐containing chemically engineered cells with minimal harm to the receptor‐negative cells.[ 21 ]

Encouraged by these findings, we aimed to investigate if artificial internalizing receptors for mammalian cells can be engineered using appropriately lipidated antibodies, specifically the anti‐fluorescein antibody. The motivation for this undertaking is that in such a case, any molecule modified with fluorescein can in principle be recognized by the artificial receptor. Amine‐, thiol‐, and elsewise reactive derivatives of fluorescein are readily available commercially, and as such, labeling (bio)molecules with fluorescein is a routine step in imaging applications. We hypothesized that fluorescein labeling can be used to facilitate cell entry for diverse cargo, using the fluorescein‐specific artificial internalizing receptors. To investigate this, in this work i) we established the protocols for modifying an antibody with cholesterylamine and other aliphatic lipids, using click chemistry; ii) we visualized receptor incorporation in mammalian cells; iii) quantified the potency and the efficacy of drug delivery via these artificial receptors using fluorescein‐labeled ADCs and other fluorescein‐containing cargos. Our data illustrate that the structure of the artificial receptor is the decisive factor in its performance in drug delivery and that diverse (but not all) fluorescein‐containing cargo is internalized by the new artificial receptors. Our results enrich the toolbox of biomolecular engineering and contribute to the development of artificial receptors for applications within the fields of biomedicine and biotechnology.

Results and Discussion

Artificial internalizing receptors realized in this work contained the monoclonal murine anti‐fluorescein IgG antibody as the exofacial recognition arm and cholesterylamine for receptor anchoring into the lipid bilayer (Figure 1 ). For comparison and control purposes, we also prepared derivatives of the anti‐fluorescein IgG that contained an aliphatic lipid anchor, 1,2‐distearoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphoethanolamine (DSPE). The main bioconjugation chemistries employed in this work were the amine modification using N‐hydroxysuccinimide‐activated esters (NHS esters) and click chemistry, specifically the copper‐free strain‐promoted azide‐alkyne cycloaddition using an azide and a dibenzocyclooctyne (DBCO).

As the first optimization step, the NHS ester derivative of DBCO was reacted with the anti‐fluorescein IgG using increasing mole equivalents of the small molecule reagent, in a 0.15 m borate buffer at pH 8.3. The results of bioconjugation were analyzed using matrix‐assisted laser‐induced desorption ionization time‐of‐flight mass spectometry (MALDI‐TOF MS). This analysis revealed that the experimentally attained degree of antibody modification corresponded well with the feed ratio of the reagents (Figure 2 ).

The azide‐containing cholesterylamines were synthesized starting from the commercially available 5‐cholesten‐3‐one via the reactions with azide/amine heterobifunctional derivatives of PEG, with 11 or 67 repeat units in the polymer (see experimental methods for synthesis protocol and compound characterization). DBCO‐IgG was then reacted with the azide‐containing cholesterylamines via the click chemistry (Figure 3 ). In these experiments, we chose to use the IgG samples prepared using 5 or 20 mole equivalents of DBCO‐NHS, and to use 10 mole equivalents of the azido‐containing cholesterylamine. The resulting receptor molecules that contained PEG with 11 monomer units are denoted throughout the manuscript as IR1, and for PEG with 67 monomer units as IR2; receptors obtained using 5 or 20 mole equivalents DBCO are further denoted with L or H, for Low and High stoichiometry, respectively (e.g., IR1‐L is a receptor with 11 ethylene glycol units and based on 5 mole equivalents of NHS‐DBCO). For control purposes, we also considered artificial receptors that contain an aliphatic lipid, DSPE, denoted in this work as receptors IR3 and IR4. Of these, IR3 is the receptor that was obtained in one step from IgG via a direct reaction with a commercially available NHS derivative of DSPE, taken in 5 or 20 mole equivalents to IgG (IR3‐L and IR3‐H, respectively). Receptor IR4 was synthesized from the DBCO‐modified IgG using click chemistry via reaction with the commercially available azide derivative of DSPE.

A,B) Schematic illustration of the synthesis of the lipid‐containing IgG via A) click reaction between the DBCO‐derivative of the IgG and an azide‐containing derivative of a lipid (cholesterylamine or DSPE) or B) via direct NHS coupling; C) characterization of the synthesized artificial receptors via MALDI‐TOF MS.

Analyses of the synthesized lipid derivatives of IgG were conducted via MALDI‐TOF MS (Figure 3C). Results of bioconjugation via click reactions were rather similar as regards the DBCO content on IgG but significantly different as regards the azide component of the reaction. Specifically, the azide derivative of cholesterylamine with the shorter PEG chain (11 monomer units PEG, IR1‐L, and IR1‐H) exhibited minor if any reactivity and even with the significant excess of this reagent and at the highest DBCO content on IgG, MALDI revealed that the protein largely remained lipid‐free. In contrast, for the azide derivative of cholesterylamine with a longer PEG spacer (67 monomer units, IR2‐L and IR2‐H), even modest access of this reagent afforded IgG with a significant, if not exhaustive, modification of the protein with the lipid. Notably, at the higher DBCO content, the lipid‐containing IgG exhibited poor desorption in MALDI. For the DSPE counterpart (IR4‐L), we observed a considerable degree of modification of IgG, and with the higher DBCO content, the protein exhibited virtually no desorption and rendered MALDI‐TOF MS analysis impossible (spectrum not shown). Conjugation via the direct reaction between NHS‐DSPE and IgG also afforded the protein with evident lipid modifications, with better results obtained at a higher excess of the amine‐reactive lipid reagent (IR3‐H).

Reactions with IgG were also analyzed via protein gel electrophoresis (Figure 4 ; Figure S1, Supporting Information), which largely confirmed the MALDI‐TOF MS analyses. At 5 mole equivalents of NHS‐DBCO or NHS‐DSPE, IR1‐L and IR3‐L exhibited migration hardly different from that of the pristine protein, illustrating a minor if any degree of protein conjugation with the lipid. In turn, IR2‐L and IR4‐L exhibited a reduced migration compared to the pristine IgG and this confirms modification with the lipid. Analysis of the protein samples that were reduced with dithiothreitol (DTT) revealed that lipid modification in IR2‐L and IR4‐L occurred largely at the heavy chain of the IgG antibody, with up to three copies of the lipid per protein heavy chain. Taken together, MALDI‐TOF MS and gel electrophoresis confirm reliable protein modification with cholesterylamine using the azide derivative with a longer PEG spacer. For the DSPE derivatives, the two‐step click chemistry approach appears to be superior to the direct, single‐step conjugation using NHS ester chemistry.

Receptor incorporation into mammalian cells was visualized via confocal microscopy, using a fluorescently labeled secondary antibody (Figure 5A). Independently, cell fluorescence was quantified using flow cytometry (Figure 5B). In these experiments and throughout the manuscript, MOLT‐4 cells were used, a cell line model of acute lymphoblastic leukemia. Receptor stock solutions were prepared in the range of 10–30 µm. Receptor aliquots were administered onto cells at a final concentration of 1 µm in the absence of serum and incubated for 15 min before a complete growth medium was added. Thereafter, the unbound receptor was removed by washing, and the cells were exposed to a solution of the fluorescently labeled secondary antibody. Confocal microscopy images illustrate that receptor IR2‐L afforded the strongest cell fluorescence of all the receptors synthesized in this work. This conclusion was supported by the flow cytometry analyses, which further illustrated that the receptor IR1‐L also showed increased fluorescence, confirming receptor incorporation into the cells. Receptors based on DSPE (IR3 and IR4) afforded a minor change in fluorescence of the cells, corresponding to the secondary antibody control alone, which indicates that these receptors have likely not been incorporated into the cells. Thus, cholesterylamine‐containing receptor designs are superior to their DSPE‐containing counterpart. Further analysis of data presented in Figure 5 reveals that for both IR1 and IR2, an increase in the DBCO content on the antibody is detrimental as regards to the receptor incorporation into the lipid bilayer of cells. Indeed, flow cytometry reveals that the fluorescence of the cells is approximately ten‐fold lower for the cells that were exposed to IR2‐H compared to the cells incubated with IR2‐L (Figure 5B). A possible explanation of this observation is that with increased lipid content, preparations of IgG may aggregate, which impedes the incorporation of cholesterylamine into the lipid bilayer. Worthy of note, we have previously compared incorporation into cells for cholesterylamine‐ and DSPE‐ derivatives of fluorescein and observed minor differences in cell fluorescence.[ 20 ] Thus, the pronounced difference between the receptor designs observed herein is specific to the lipid derivatives of IgG and may prove useful for diverse techniques and applications in the interphase sciences.

We further characterized the receptor incorporation in cells using the receptor IR2‐L. Receptor incorporation into cells could be further enhanced, at least three‐fold, by increasing concentrations of the receptor in the solution administered onto cells, as detected by the flow cytometry and using the fluorescently labeled secondary antibody (Figure 5C). Receptor molecules were effectively incorporated into the cell membrane but were non‐harmful to the cells, and we observed no cytotoxicity for the receptor IR2‐L alone, at least up to 6 µm concentration in the feed solution, as quantified via the plate reader‐based assay and via flow cytometry (Figure 5D).

As the first step toward the use of antibody‐based artificial receptors in drug targeting, we analyzed the recognition between the lipid derivatives of IgG and fluorescein, to evaluate if anchor conjugation has an effect on the binding ability between the antibody and its cognate hapten antigen. The association between fluorescein and the antibody should lead to a pronounced quenching of fluorescence, which can be readily registered using a multi‐well plate reader.[ 20 ] Herein, 100 nm solutions of fluorescein were charged with receptor molecules to concentrations up to 400 nm. In these experiments, we observed that the behavior of the receptor molecules was indistinguishable from that of the pristine anti‐fluorescein antibody (Figure 6A). Specifically, with increasing receptor content, the solution fluorescence was decreased and at IgG concentration exceeding 200 nm, fluorescence was quenched essentially in full. Thus, antigen binding for the antibody in solution was not affected by the conjugation of anchors in any of the tested receptor molecules.

To investigate the use of artificial receptors in targeted drug delivery, we prepared an ADC based on the generic IgG from human serum. As the conjugated drug, we chose monomethyl auristatin F (MMAF), a membrane‐impermeable toxin with minimal bystander cytotoxicity. The drug was coupled into the structure of the ADC through the valine‐citrulline‐aminobenzyl alcohol linker, which is cleavable by the lysosomal proteases.[ 21 ] Thus, this ADC is a convenient tool for quantifying intracellular drug delivery. For recognition by the receptors, the ADC was further labeled with fluorescein (via a reaction with the isothiocyanate derivative of the dye). MOLT‐4 cells were functionalized with the receptors and then exposed to solutions of ADC with concentrations up to 100 nm for 72 h in a complete growth medium. After this time, cytotoxicity was quantified using a plate‐reader‐based viability assay (Presto Blue). In these experiments, free MMAF exhibited a potency of approximately 250 nm, in good accordance with prior reports (Figure 6B). Receptors IR1‐L, IR3‐L, and IR4‐L afforded minimal if any enhancement of cytotoxicity for the administered ADC. In stark contrast, the cells functionalized with receptor IR2‐L became highly susceptible to the treatment with ADC, with the toxicity‐related IC50 significantly lower than that of the non‐conjugated drug. In other words, receptor IR2‐L, the cholesterylamine‐anchored IgG, is the most successful of the designs investigated in this work. Cell killing in the cells functionalized with IR2‐L was near‐complete at the ADC concentration of just 10 nm, whereas toxicity to the receptor‐negative cells was minimal up to the tested 100 nm content of the ADC alone. We believe that the superiority of IR2‐L over its counterpart with a shorter PEG linker, IR1‐L, is likely not inherent in the receptor structure but rather reflects that IR1‐L preparation contains fewer lipid anchor groups (c.f. Figures 3 and 4). The same cannot be said about receptor IR4‐L, which is lipid‐modified to a similar extent as the receptor IR2‐L. Thus, the results in Figure 6 highlight the importance of cholesterylamine over DSPE as a lipid anchor for the function of the antibody‐based artificial internalizing receptors.

In these experiments, we also used a small‐molecule conjugate between MMAF and fluorescein. The linker between the drug and the dye was PEG101‐valine‐citrulline‐aminobenzyl alcohol, similar to that in the ADC (albeit with a larger PEG spacer). Despite expectations, the small molecule conjugate revealed no measurable toxicity in cell culture, regardless of receptor functionalization of the cells (Figure 6C). We find that this result is highly surprising. It illustrates that with the same targeting agent and the same cleavable linker in the conjugate structure, the success of drug delivery was possible for the ADC but not observed for the small molecule analog. We acknowledge that the origin of this phenomenon remains unclear (even though a similar observation has been made previously by Cazzamalli et al.[ 22 ]). It may indicate that the affinity of the proteases is higher to the protein than to the small molecule substrate.

One of the envisioned uses of artificial receptors in biotechnology relates to the intracellular delivery of proteins. Numerous tools have been developed for this purpose, including physical and chemical methods, but much room is left for improvement, and as such, protein delivery into cells remains challenging.[ 23 ] Results in Figure 6 illustrate that the artificial receptors designed in this work facilitate the entry of ADC into cells. Next, we aimed to investigate if this mode of activity extends to other proteins. To this end, we labeled the samples of bovine serum albumin (BSA), Cytochrome C (Cyt C), β‐Glucuronidase (GUS), and β‐Galactosidase (GAL) with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) over the self‐quenching level (Figure S4, Supporting Information). Proteolytic processing of these proteins (for example, by the lysosomal proteases upon the protein cell entry and lysosomal trafficking) should be accompanied by a strong increase in fluorescence, which should be readily quantified and/or visualized.[ 24 ] The labeled proteins were administered onto MOLT‐4 cells, optionally containing receptor IR2‐L, and the fluorescence of cells was quantified using flow cytometry (Figure 7A,B). For all the FITC‐labeled proteins, we observed enhanced fluorescence in the cells that contained IR2‐L. This was not observed for the receptor‐negative cells, in which case the cells showed only minor signs of unspecific protein uptake. Thus, the artificial receptor designed in this work facilitates protein delivery into mammalian cells. To achieve this, the protein was modified using the readily available reagent (FITC) via a routine protocol. To gain further insight into the protein uptake, the cells were incubated with the FITC‐labeled albumin and thereafter imaged using fluorescence microscopy. Confirming the flow cytometry data, we found that the addition of the fluorescein‐labeled albumin to the cells that were equipped with receptor IR2‐L afforded cells with bright fluorescence (Figure 7C). This indicates the uptake of albumin and its intracellular, likely lysosomal processing. Fluorescence was not observed in cells kept at 4 °C indicating that protein cell entry was via the energy‐dependent endocytic mechanisms. Cells were devoid of fluorescence if no receptor was added to the cells, indicating the cell entry for albumin was indeed receptor‐mediated. Quite surprisingly, despite evidence of lysosomal processing for albumin, we observed no receptor‐mediated cytotoxicity when using an albumin derivative that contains fluorescein and a copy of MMAF (Figure 7D). Equally so, receptor IR2‐L afforded no enhancement of cytotoxicity for a fluorescein‐containing conjugate of Cyt C (Figure 7D), a mitochondrial protein that naturally triggers apoptosis upon cytosolic release.[ 25 ] Taken together, the results presented in Figure 7 illustrate that the developed artificial receptors mediate protein cell entry. However, these results also indicate the current limitations of the targeting platform developed in this work. It is not unlikely that these limitations are related to the lysosomal escape, a phenomenon that precludes the activity of a broad range of therapeutic cargo upon cell entry.[ 23 ]

Lastly, we aimed to specifically compare the efficacy and the potency of drug delivery for artificial receptors that are engineered around the same cognate pair but differ in the exofacial ligand: fluorescein or anti‐fluorescein antibody (Figure 8 ). In both cases, artificial internalizing receptors were targeted by an ADC that contained MMAF, conjugated to the protein via the valine‐citruline‐aminobenzyl alcohol linker. For the ADC targeted to fluorescein, this was achieved using the anti‐fluorescein antibody itself. For the ADC targeted to IR2‐L, drug conjugation was performed to a unspecific generic IgG that was labeled with FITC. In these experiments, we also considered that targeting the antibody‐containing artificial receptor IR2‐L, which is based on a murine antibody, can be performed using an anti‐mouse secondary antibody. This would make it possible to compare the drug delivery mediated by the artificial receptor IR2‐L that interacts with the ligand via the protein‐protein interactions or via an interaction with a hapten. To this end, we engineered an ADC based on a goat‐anti‐mouse polyclonal secondary antibody. In all three cases, MOLT‐4 cells were engineered to contain the artificial receptor and then incubated in the presence of the corresponding ADC for 72 h. Thereafter, cytotoxicity was quantified using a plate reader‐based viability assay (Presto Blue).

The presented results are based on three independent experiments and shown as mean ± SD (except panel B, ADC 1:based on two independent experiments due to depleted antibody stocks).

In these experiments, we observed that the preparations of ADC, in pristine MOLT‐4 cells and in the absence of artificial receptors, were devoid of toxicity (marked in Figure 8 as “ADC”), which is highly beneficial and ensures minimized side effects. Targeting of the fluorescein‐containing receptor with cognate ADC afforded a strong benefit and for the receptor‐mediated toxicity, we registered an IC50 of 6 ± 3 nm, which constitutes an over 50‐fold toxicity enhancement over MMAF and is in agreement with previously published data (Figure 8A).[ 21 ] Targeting the antibody‐containing receptor IR2‐L using a secondary polyclonal goat anti‐mouse antibody was also successful. Indeed, we observed strong toxicity enhancement over the use of non‐targeted MMAF, due to the presence of the artificial receptor (Figure 8B). This result highlights that cholesterylamine is a powerful internalizing anchor and can mediate the cell entry for protein complexes >300 kDa. However, we observed significant batch‐to‐batch variation for the polyclonal secondary antibodies: the toxicity‐related IC50 values for the different ADC preparations varied quite significantly, from 3 nm to over 20 nm. In stark contrast, targeting the antibody‐containing receptor IR2‐L using a fluorescein‐labeled ADC consistently afforded an IC50 of 3.5 ± 0.3 nm, over 70‐fold lower than that for MMAF (Figure 8C). This receptor‐mediated enhancement of toxicity for ADC was reproducible over numerous rounds of experiments (c.f. Figures 6 and 8) and we observed minimal if any batch‐to‐batch variation for the anti‐fluorescein antibody preparations, be it used in the synthesis of ADC (Figure 8A) or the design of the receptor IR2‐L (Figure 8C).

The results presented in Figure 8 illustrate that the artificial internalizing receptors are a flexible, adaptable platform that achieves potent and efficacious drug targeting to the chemically engineered cells. Targeting using haptens and artificial receptors derived thereof afforded consistent data, with minimal batch‐to‐batch variation. The same cognate pair could be used to engineer drug targeting in at least two different ways, both being equally successful, whereby both the antibody and the cognate antigen could be used as the targeting agent or be used in the structure of the receptor at the cell surface.

Conclusion

In this work, we explored the possibility of drug targeting using a hapten‐based cognate pair, for drug targeting achieved via bio‐orthogonal interactions. Toward the overall goal, we developed the synthesis of lipid‐anchored antibodies. Through the structure‐function analysis, we identified the lead composition, based on the cholesterylamine anchor, an anti‐fluorescein antibody, and a PEG spacer with an optimized length. Cholesterylamine is a peculiar lipid that inserts into the outer leaflet of the bilayer, is spontaneously internalized into the cell, and thereafter recycled to the cell surface. Indeed, our own data and that from others illustrate that cholesterylamine is confined largely to the cell surface and is not distributed throughout the lipidated intracellular compartments,[ 18 , 21 ] as is highly beneficial for the design of artificial internalizing receptors. This distribution comes in contrast to the proteins that are naturally lipidated for trafficking and localization in the cell.[ 26 ]

A range of fluorescein‐labeled cargo was screened for cellular uptake, with read‐outs based on functional activity. For proteins, we documented enhanced cell entry but not ensuing intracellular (cytosolic) activity, indicating that limitations of the proposed platform of drug targeting using artificial internalizing receptors could be related to the endosomal escape phenomena. Of all the cargo used in this work, antibody‐drug conjugates proved to be particularly well‐suited for internalization using artificial internalizing receptors, in which case we documented nanomolar potency of drug delivery. Future work will aim at engineering tools of endosomal escape into the structure of artificial receptors, and thereby use the artificial internalizing receptors for intracellular delivery of diverse cargo.

Experimental Section

Chemical Methods—General Information on Chemical Experiments

All chemicals and solvents were acquired from Sigma Aldrich and used without further purification unless otherwise stated. N,N‐Dimethylformamide (DMF), triethylamine (TEA), and methanol were obtained in the anhydrous state. The solvents acetonitrile, dichloromethane, and tetrahydrofurane (THF) were dried over aluminum oxide using an MBraun SP800 purification system. The deuterated solvents used for 1H‐NMR and 13C‐NMR analysis were purchased from EurisoTop. Thin layer chromatography (TLC) analyses were performed using silica‐coated aluminum foil plates (Merck Kieselgel 60 F254) and analyzed by either by UV irradiation and/or staining with KMnO4 for visualization. High‐purity grade silica gel (w/Ca, ≈0.1%, 230–400 mesh particle size, 60 Å pore size) was the stationary phase, which was also used for silica column chromatography. NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker AVANCE III HD spectrometer operating at 1H‐NMR 400 MHz or 13C‐NMR 101 MHz. The spectra were referenced to the solvent peak. CDCl3 (δ H 7.26 ppm, δ C 77.16 ppm), CD3OD (δ H 3.31 ppm, δ C 49.00 ppm), DMSO‐d6 (δ H 2.50 ppm, δ C 39.52 ppm). High‐resolution mass spectrometry (HR‐MS) was performed using a Bruker Maxis Impact TOF‐MS spectrometer with electrospray ionization (ESI) and analyzed with Bruker DataAnalysis. High‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) experiments were conducted with an Agilent 1260 Infinity II connected to an EC‐C18 column with a particle size of 2.7 µm, length of 100 mm, and diameter of 4.6 mm (flow rate: 0.4 mL min−1). The mobile phase was a combination of ultrapure water with trifluoroacetic acid (TFA, 0.1% v/v%, eluent A) and HPLC grade acetonitrile with TFA (0.1 % v/v%, eluent B). Preparatory HPLC method for purification; 30 % MeCN and 70 % MQ water to 100 % MeCN over 20 min followed by isocratic at 100 % MeCN for an additional 2 min.

Synthesis of Cholesterylamine‐Peg11‐azide (Compound 1)

5‐cholesten‐3‐one (51 mg, 0.13 mmol, 1.0 eq.) and azido‐PEG11‐NH2 from IRIS Biotech (97 mg, 0.17 mmol, 1.3 eq.) were stirred in dry MeOH (1.0 mL) followed by addition of TEA (0.04 mL, 0.3 mmol, 2 eq.). Ti( i OPr)4 (0.06 mL, 0.2 mmol, 1.5 eq.) was dissolved in a mixture of dry MeOH (0.5 mL) and CH2Cl2 (0.5 mL) and subsequently added dropwise to the mixture containing azido‐Peg3‐NH2. Biological Methods—General Cell Culture Procedures

MOLT‐4 cells, a human T lymphoblast cell line for acute lymphoblastic leukemia were used in all the experiments. The cells were grown as a suspension culture in RPMI‐1640 Medium. Complete growth medium contained 10% FBS, 2 mm L‐Gln, and 1% Penicillin‐Streptomycin. Complete media were always heated in a hot chamber to 37 °C before addition, to prevent further stress inflicting the cells. The suspended cells were grown in 25 cm2 untreated culture flasks and stored upright in the incubator at 37 °C with 5 % CO2. Sub‐culturing was performed when cells were concentrated to ≈1 × 106 cells mL−1 and performed by spinning down cells at 300 g in a centrifuge for 5 min, removing the old media, and adding new complete media in a 1:3 or 1:4 split every second or third day, respectively. The cells were sub‐cultured at least four times after being unfrozen and used no longer than passage 30. Counting of cells was performed by the addition of a Trypan Blue staining solution (Gibco, Ref.: 15‐250‐061) in a 1:1 ratio followed by counting using an automated LUNA cell counter (Logos Biosystems). Cell viability was in the same procedure controlled and only used above 90 % viability. Presto Blue cell viability assays were performed by the addition of 10X viability reagent (Ref.: A13261) reagent to the cell media followed by a 1 h incubation at 37 °C, 5 % CO2 before conversion of Resazurin was monitored via fluorescence measurements (excitation 554 nm, emission 593 nm). All cell viability data were prepared by making an average of the technical replicates, subtracting the background (media only) from the raw data and normalizing to the independent viability control.

DBCO Functionalization of Anti‐Fluorescein Antibody

The monoclonal anti‐fluorescein antibody (antiFITC‐mAb) was buffer exchanged to a 0.15 m borate buffer pH 8.3 by spin filtration (30 kDa MWCO) and the antiFITC‐mAb concentration was adjusted to > 3 g L−1 using UV–vis. The DBCO‐NHS ester (Ref.: 761524) was added to the antiFITC‐mAb solution in varying molar excess (2, 5, 10, 20, 50 equiv.) from a 10 mm DMSO stock before the reaction was incubated at r.t. for 1 h under rotary shaking (800 rpm). The samples were then purified by three times spin filtration (30 kDa MWC and the buffer was exchanged for a 50 mm PBS buffer pH 7.4. MALDI was used for characterization with the pristine antiFITC‐mAb as control.

Preparation of the Artificial Receptors

The antiFITC‐mAb was buffer exchanged to a 0.15 m borate buffer pH 8.3, by spin filtration (30 kDa MWCO), and the antiFITC‐mAb concentration was adjusted to be > 3 g L−1 using UV–vis measurements. The NHS ester reagents: DBCO‐NHS ester or DSPE‐PEG2000 Da‐NHS ester (Avanti, Ref. 881038P, for IR3) were stored as aliquots in DMSO and left to unfreeze to r.t. before they were added to the antiFITC‐mAb solution in a molar excess of 5 (IR#‐L) or 20 equiv (IR#‐H). The reactions were left at r.t. for 1 h under rotary shaking (800 rpm). The DBCO functionalized antiFITC‐mAb samples were then purified by spin filtration (30kDa MWCO) and the buffer was exchanged for a 50 mm PBS at pH 7.4 and kept at > 2 g L−1 for the following SPAAC reaction. The azide click reagents: N3‐PEG11‐cholesterylamine, compound 1 (for IR1), N3‐PEG67‐cholesterylamine, compound 2 (for IR2), and commercially available N3‐PEG45‐DSPE (for IR4) were added independently to the antiFITC‐mAb‐DBCO samples (5 or 20 equiv. DBCO) in a molar excess of 10 equiv. The reactions were incubated at r.t. for 3 h under rotary shaking (800 rpm). General Protocol for Incorporation of the Artificial Receptors in MOLT‐4 Cells

MOLT‐4 cells were diluted to 3 × 105 cells mL−1 and spun down. The media was then replaced with 1 µm IR from a PBS stock with 50 µL PBS with receptor/1 mL cells, while control cells received PBS only. The cells were incubated for 15 min. at r.t. and dissolved in comlplete media. The final cell concentration is stated in each independent experimental protocol.

Incorporation of the Artificial Receptors

All the artificial receptors (IR1‐L‐IR4‐L and IR1‐H‐IR4‐H or pristine antiFITC‐mAb) were individually added to the cells as stated in the general incorporation protocol above before they were washed twice with cold PBS with 2% FBS and kept on ice for the rest of the preparation. The PBS solution was exchanged with a solution of 0.2% BSA in PBS including a 1:100 dilution (1.2–1.5 µm) of Goat Anti‐Mouse IgG with TRITC label (2nd Ab‐TRITC). The samples were incubated at r.t. for 30 min, followed by three times washing in cold PBS before the cells were dissolved in PBS with 2% FBS, and fluorescence of the 2nd Ab‐TRITC was monitored using flow cytometry or confocal microscopy. For flow cytometry, TRITC fluorescence was monitored and the gating strategy can be found in Figure S2 (Supporting Information). For confocal microscopy: TRITC was excited using the 555 diode laser line at 2% intensity and Hoechst was excited using the 405 diode laser line at 2% intensity. The imaging settings were optimized in the +IR2‐L and +Hoechst sample and kept constant for all other samples.

General Protein Labeling Protocol

The protein was buffer exchanged to a 0.1 m sodium bicarbonate buffer pH 9, using an Amicon spin filter (10 or 30kDa MWCO), and the concentration was determined using UV–vis and kept at >3 g L−1. Alexa Flour 680 NHS Ester (Ref.: A37574) or Fluorescein isothiocyanate isomer 1 (FITC) (Ref.: F2750) was diluted in DMSO to 10 g L−1 and added to the antibody solution in an excess of 10 molar equiv. The reaction was left at r.t. for 1 h with gentle shaking (800 rpm) and purified using repeated rounds of spin filtration and final purification using a NAP‐5 column (Sephadex G‐25 DNA Grade) by eluting with 50 mm PBS pH 7.4. The final protein concentration was determined with UV–vis and the degree of labeling (DOL) was determined by measuring the maximum Alexa‐680 absorbance at 680 nm or fluorescein at 494 nm and using a correction factor for correction for overspill into the 280 nm protein absorbance. For the Alexa‐680 labeled 2nd Ab was estimated to be 5.0 Alexa‐680/2° Ab; for FITC labeled human MMAF‐IgG (DAR = 1.3) DOL was estimated to be 2.44 FITC/IgG‐MMAF; for FITC labeled MMAF‐BSA DOL was 2.0 FITC/MMAF‐BSA; for FITC labeled Cyt C DOI was estimated to be 1.5 FITC/Cyt C.

Incorporation and Toxicity Test of IR2‐L

IR2‐L was added as stated in the general incorporation protocol in different concentrations: 0.5‐1‐2‐4‐6 µm from a 23 µm PBS stock. Complete growth media were then added to a final conc. of 3 × 105 cells mL−1 and the cells were seeded in triplicates in a 96‐well plate and incubated for 72 h at 37 °C 5% CO2. The plate was spun down and media was exchanged with PBS including 0.2% BSA and 1:500 dilution of the 2nd Ab‐Alexa680 (the concentration was determined based on a titration experiment, data not included) and incubated at r.t. for 15 min. The cells were then washed one time with cold PBS and dissolved in PBS with 2% FBS. The dead cell control was removed and the sample was incubated at 55 °C for 20 min to kill the cells. The cells were transferred back to the plate in a new well and all samples were finally spun down, aspirated, and exchanged with cold PBS with 2% FBS including 1 µg mL−1 PI. Single‐stained controls were included for each fluorophore (Alexa680/PI). All samples were kept on ice until measured at the flow cytometer. For flow cytometry, Alexa680 and PI fluorescence were monitored and the gating strategy for flow cytometry can be found in Figure S3(Supporting Information). At least 3 × 104 events were collected in each sample. The experiments were reproduced three independent times.

Statistical Analysis

Unless stated otherwise, all reported data are based on at least three independent experiments and presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was carried out using GraphPad Prism 10.2.2. All cell viability data were prepared by making an average of the three technical replicates, subtracting the background (media only) from the raw data, and normalizing to the independent viability control. The viability was then plotted as a function of the logarithm of the concentration. The independent IC50 values were estimated by fitting them to a sigmoidal curve (four parameters, variable slope).
