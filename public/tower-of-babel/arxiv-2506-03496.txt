Abstract
We consider the multiple hypothesis testing (MHT) problem over the joint domain formed by a graph and a measure space. On each sample point of this joint domain, we assign a hypothesis test and a corresponding p p -value. The goal is to make decisions for all hypotheses simultaneously, using all available p p -values. In practice, this problem resembles the detection problem over a sensor network during a period of time. To solve this problem, we extend the traditional two-groups model such that the prior probability of the null hypothesis and the alternative distribution of p p -values can be inhomogeneous over the joint domain. We model the inhomogeneity via a generalized graph signal. This more flexible statistical model yields a more powerful detection strategy by leveraging the information from the joint domain.

Introduction

Detection is an important task in statistical signal processing.
Multiple hypothesis testing (MHT)
, which aims to make decisions simultaneously for many hypotheses, has been considered since the nominal works
[
1
,
2
]
. The detection strategy is expected to discover as many alternatives as possible, while maintaining the
false discovery rate (FDR)
below a nominal level. When the hypotheses are placed on a known structure, it is expected that the detection power could be boosted by leveraging the underlying structure. There are works that address this problem when the underlying structure is a graph
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
or spatial domain
[
6
]
. In this paper, we consider the
MHT
problem on a joint graph-measure space structure by utilizing the basic concepts in
generalized graph signal processing (GGSP)
.

Graph signal processing (GSP)
is a set of techniques that address signal processing problems on graphs, which mainly includes sampling
[
7
,
8
]
, reconstruction
[
9
,
10
]
and filtering
[
11
,
12
]
. In
GSP
, signals are functions on the vertex set of a graph. This signal model is extended by
[
13
,
14
]
to a more general setting where the domain of signal is the Cartesian product between the vertex set and a general measure space. We refer to this product domain as
joint domain
, which resembles the joint time-vertex domain. The signal reconstruction and filtering problem for
GGSP
has been addressed in
[
15
,
16
]
. In this work, we consider the
MHT
problem on the joint domain. We propose to parameterize the distributions of
p
-values on graphs by a generalized graph signal. This strategy is different from the existing works in that we model and estimate continuously varying
probability density functions (pdfs)
of
p
-values over the domain, instead of homogeneous or having a fixed number of choices. Therefore, this strategy is better at leveraging the domain knowledge than the existing approaches.

The contributions of this paper are as follows.

1.

We formulate the empirical-Bayesian model for
MHT
over the joint domain, and derive the optimal oracle detection strategy.

2.

We provide a method for estimating probability densities of
p
-values varying over the joint domain. By substituting this estimate into the oracle solution, we propose a detection strategy for
MHT
problem with
FDR
control over the joint domain.

3.

We illustrate our method on the seismic dataset.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In
section

II
, we introduce the problem formulation of
MHT
on the joint domain. In
section

III

p
-values. In
section

IV
, we prove the consistency of
maximum likelihood estimator (MLE)
estimator for the underlying generalized graph signal. By substituting this estimator into the oracle solution, we obtain a strategy with asymptotic
FDR
control.

II
Problem Formulation

In this section, we introduce the mathematical formulation of the
MHT
problem. We first introduce the construction of the domain where the problem is based on. We consider an undirected graph
G=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
where
\mathcal{V}
denotes the vertex set and
\mathcal{E}
denotes the edge set. We assume that
G
does not have self-loops. Apart from the graph, we consider an additional domain
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{A},\tau)
which is a measure space. The domain of the problem is then
\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{T}
. For simplicity, we write
\mathcal{J}
to denote
\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{T}
. Suppose
\lvert\mathcal{V}\rvert=N
. We denote the
graph shift operator (GSO)
as
\mathbf{S}_{G}
and its corresponding graph Fourier basis as
\{\phi_{k}(\cdot)\nonscript\,:\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}k=1,\dots,N\}
. Note that each
\phi_{k}(\cdot)
is a function on
\mathcal{V}
.

The
MHT
problem aims to make decisions based on
p
-values observed by multiple hypotheses simultaneously. In this paper, we consider a finite sample set of
\mathcal{J}
, denoted as
\mathsf{S}:=\{(\mathsf{v}_{m},\mathsf{t}_{m})\nonscript\,:\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}m=1,\dots,M\}
. Here
\mathsf{S}
can be a random set. Each sample
(\mathsf{v}_{m},\mathsf{t}_{m})\in\mathsf{S}
is associated with a hypothesis and a
p
-value
\mathsf{p}_{m}
. The set of sample points with null hypotheses is
\mathsf{J}_{0}:=\{(\mathsf{v}_{m},\mathsf{t}_{m})\nonscript\,:\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}\text{null hypothesis is true}\}
, and
\mathsf{J}_{1}:=\mathsf{S}\setminus\mathsf{J}_{0}
denotes those with alternative hypotheses. Let
\mathbf{p}:=(\mathsf{p}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{p}_{M})
be the vector containing all
p
-values. We consider a
detection strategy
as a map
\mathbf{h}:[0,1]^{M}\to\{0,1\}^{M}
, so that the value of
\mathbf{h}
is a
M
-dimensional vector with
0
-
1
entries. If the
m
-th entry of
\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{p})
is
0
(or
1
), then it means that the hypothesis on
(\mathsf{v}_{m},\mathsf{t}_{m})
is identified as null (or alternative) by
\mathbf{h}
. We write
\widehat{\mathsf{J}}_{0}
(or
\widehat{\mathsf{J}}_{1}
) as the sample points where the associated hypotheses are identified as null (or alternative) by
\mathbf{h}
. There are two measurements for the quality of a detection strategy:
FDR
and power. They are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{FDR}(\mathbf{h})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\lvert\widehat{\mathsf{J}}_{1}\bigcap\mathsf{J}_{0}\rvert}{\max(\lvert\widehat{\mathsf{J}}_{1}\rvert,1)}\right],

(1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{pow}(\mathbf{h})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\lvert\widehat{\mathsf{J}}_{1}\bigcap\mathsf{J}_{1}\rvert}{\max(\lvert\mathsf{J}_{1}\rvert,1)}\right].

(2)

Similar to a single hypothesis test, the target of
MHT
is to find a way to design
\mathbf{h}
whose power is as large as possible while the
FDR
is controlled below a nominal level.

III
Statistical Model and Oracle Solution

III-A

Statistical Model

In this section, we introduce the empirical-Bayesian model for the
MHT
problem described in
section

II
. This model extends what is known as the two-groups model in traditional
MHT
, which has two main assumptions: first, each hypothesis being null or alternative is Bernoulli with the same distribution. Second, under null and alternative hypothesis, the
p
-value has different distributions. In this paper, since the hypotheses are placed on different sample points on
\mathcal{J}
, we allow the prior probability of being null and the distributions of
p
-values to vary among different points of
\mathcal{J}
. By doing so, the model has better fitness on the data than the existing models in
[
5
,
6
,
3
,
8
]
.

We illustrate the hierarchical Bayes model in
fig.

1
.

Fig. 1
:
The scheme of the Bayesian model for
MHT
in
GGSP
.

In this model,
\ttgamma
is a stochastic process on
\mathcal{J}
. We use it to parameterize the varying alternative distribution of
p
-values and the probability of null hypothesis for different
(v,t)\in\mathcal{J}
. We assume that the range of
\ttgamma
as a function of
(v,t)
is a subset of
\mathcal{Z}
.
A sample point
(\mathsf{v},\mathsf{t})
is drawn from a positive probability measure
\rho
on
\mathcal{J}
, independently with
the process
\ttgamma
.
Let
\pi_{0}
be a continuous function from
\mathcal{Z}
to
[0,1]
. Given
\ttgamma
and
(\mathsf{v},\mathsf{t})
, we obtain a probability
\pi_{0}\circ\ttgamma(\mathsf{v},\mathsf{t})\in[0,1]
.
We assume the following hierarchical model for generating the
p
-value: first, generate
\tttheta
as

\displaystyle\tttheta=\begin{cases}0&\mathrm{w.p.~}\pi_{0}\circ\ttgamma(\mathsf{v},\mathsf{t}),\\
1&\mathrm{w.p.~}1-\pi_{0}\circ\ttgamma(\mathsf{v},\mathsf{t}),\end{cases}

where
\tttheta=1
indicates that the alternative hypothesis on
(\mathsf{v},\mathsf{t})
is true. Then, under null and alternative hypotheses, the
p
-value
\mathsf{p}
is generated independently from the following different distributions:

\displaystyle\mathsf{p}\mid\{\tttheta=0\}

\displaystyle\sim f_{0}(\cdot;(\mathsf{v},\mathsf{t})),

\displaystyle\mathsf{p}\mid\{\tttheta=1\}

\displaystyle\sim f_{1}(\cdot;\ttgamma(\mathsf{v},\mathsf{t})),

where
f_{1}
is from a parametric family of
pdfs

\mathcal{P}^{1}:=\{f_{1}(\cdot;\zeta)\nonscript\,:\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}\}
on
[0,1]
with parameter
\zeta
. Under this model, given
\ttgamma
, the conditional joint
pdf
of the
p
-value
\mathsf{p}
, the indicator
\tttheta
, and the sample point
(\mathsf{v},\mathsf{t})
is

\displaystyle f_{\mathsf{p},\tttheta,(\mathsf{v},\mathsf{t})}(p,\theta,(v,t)\mid\ttgamma)=\Big(\pi_{0}\circ\ttgamma(v,t)\mathbb{I}\{\theta=0\}f_{0}(p;(v,t))

\displaystyle+(1-\pi_{0}\circ\ttgamma(v,t))\mathbb{I}\{\theta=1\}f_{1}(p;\ttgamma(v,t))\Big)\rho(v,t),

where
p\in(0,1]
,
\theta\in\{0,1\}
, and
(v,t)\in\mathcal{J}
. From
f_{\mathsf{p},\tttheta,(\mathsf{v},\mathsf{t})}
, we derive the conditional
pdf
of
\mathsf{p}
and
(\mathsf{v},\mathsf{t})
as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} f_{\mathsf{p},(\mathsf{v},\mathsf{t})}(p,(v,t)\mid\ttgamma)=\Big(\pi_{0}\circ\ttgamma(v,t)f_{0}(p;(v,t))\\
+(1-\pi_{0}\circ\ttgamma(v,t))f_{1}(p;\ttgamma(v,t))\Big)\rho(v,t),\end{aligned}

(3)

and the conditional
pdf
of
\mathsf{p}
given
\ttgamma
and
(\mathsf{v},\mathsf{t})
as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} f_{\mathrm{mix}}(p\mid\ttgamma(v,t))&=\pi_{0}\circ\ttgamma(v,t)f_{0}(p;(v,t))\\
&+(1-\pi_{0}\circ\ttgamma(v,t))f_{1}(p;\ttgamma(v,t)).\end{aligned}

(4)

In other words, we parameterize the distribution of
p
-values on
\mathcal{J}
by a
random generalized graph signal

\ttgamma
. This model combines the information from the joint domain
\mathcal{J}
via
\ttgamma
and the two-groups model widely adopted in the
MHT
literature
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
.

In this paper, we assume that
f_{0}(\cdot;(v,t))
is known and deterministic for all
(v,t)\in\mathcal{J}
. By definition of
p
-value, it can be verified that
f_{0}(\cdot;(v,t))
under simple null hypothesis is
\mathrm{Unif}\left(0,1\right)
. In other cases when
f_{0}(\cdot;(v,t))
is unknown,
f_{0}(\cdot;(v,t))
may be estimated from data beforehand by controlling the null hypotheses to be true.
For the
M

independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.)
samples
(\mathsf{v}_{m},\mathsf{t}_{m})
in
\mathsf{S}
, we obtain
M
independent samples
(\tttheta_{m})_{m=1}^{M}
, and
p
-values
(\mathsf{p}_{m})_{m=1}^{M}
.

We note that this model generalizes the two-groups model in the
MHT
literature. In traditional
MHT
, each
\tttheta_{m}
is modeled as a Bernoulli random variable
\mathrm{Bern}\left(1-\pi_{0}\right)
where
\pi_{0}
is a fixed value. The distributions
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
are also assumed to be the same for all
p
-values. In this model, by parameterizing
f_{1}
and
\pi_{0}
with
\ttgamma(v,t)
, we indicate that both the probability of hypotheses being null and the distribution of alternative
p
-values are inhomogeneous over
\mathcal{J}
.

In this paper, we assume that
\pi_{0}\circ\ttgamma(v,t)
and
f_{1}(\cdot;\ttgamma(v,t))
are identifiable from
f_{\mathrm{mix}}(\cdot\mid\ttgamma(v,t))
using
eq.

4
. This can be guaranteed by the following assumptions:

Assumption 1
.

1.

f_{0}(p;(v,t))
is non-decreasing
with respect to (w.r.t.)

p\in[0,1]
.

2.

f_{1}(\cdot;\zeta)
is non-increasing on
(0,1]
for all
\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}
.

3.

\min\limits_{p\in[0,1]}f_{1}(p;\zeta)=0
for all
\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}
, i.e., with
item

2
,
f_{1}(1;\zeta)=0
for all
\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}
.

4.

f_{0}(p;(v,t))
is continuous on
[0,1]\times\mathcal{J}
, and
f_{1}(p;\zeta)
is continuous on
(0,1]\times\mathcal{Z}
.

In
1
,
items

1
and
2
indicate that
p
-value is more likely to be small under the alternative hypothesis and more likely to be large under the null hypothesis.
The assumptions in
1
are commonly assumed in the
MHT
literature (cf .
[
18
, Theorem 2]
and
[
19
, Section 2.1]
).
They ensure that
\pi_{0}\circ\ttgamma(v,t)
and
f_{1}(\cdot;\ttgamma(v,t))
are identifiable from
f_{\mathrm{mix}}(\cdot\mid\ttgamma(v,t))
, since according to
items

1
,
2
and
3
, for each
(v,t)\in\mathcal{J}
, we have

\displaystyle\pi_{0}\circ\ttgamma(v,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{\mathrm{mix}}(1\mid\ttgamma(v,t))}{f_{0}(1;(v,t))},

(5)

\displaystyle f_{1}(p;\ttgamma(v,t))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\pi_{0}\circ\ttgamma(v,t)}\Big(f_{\mathrm{mix}}(p\mid\ttgamma(v,t))

\displaystyle-\pi_{0}\circ\ttgamma(v,t)f_{0}(p;(v,t))\Big).

(6)

The
local false discovery rate (lfdr)
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{lfdr}(p;\ttgamma(v,t))=\frac{\pi_{0}\circ\ttgamma(v,t)f_{0}(p;(v,t))}{f_{\mathrm{mix}}(p\mid\ttgamma(v,t))}.

(7)

The
lfdr
for
\mathsf{p}_{m},(\mathsf{v}_{m},\mathsf{t}_{m})
is the conditional probability
\mathbb{P}\left(\tttheta_{m}=0\nonscript\,\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}\ttgamma(\mathsf{v}_{m},\mathsf{t}_{m}),\mathsf{p}_{m}\right)
.
In
section

IV
, we estimate
f_{\mathrm{mix}}(\cdot\mid\ttgamma(\mathsf{v}_{m},\mathsf{t}_{m}))
, and thus
\pi_{0}\circ\ttgamma(\mathsf{v}_{m},\mathsf{t}_{m})
and
f_{1}(\cdot;\ttgamma(\mathsf{v}_{m},\mathsf{t}_{m}))
.

III-B

Oracle Solution

In this subsection, we show that, if
\pi_{0}\circ\ttgamma(\mathsf{v}_{m},\mathsf{t}_{m})
and
f_{1}(\cdot;\ttgamma(\mathsf{v}_{m},\mathsf{t}_{m}))
are known for
m=1,\dots,M
, then the optimal detection strategy is thresholding the
lfdr
. To see this, we introduce two complementary definitions, marginal
FDR
and marginal power, defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{mFDR}(\mathbf{h};\ttgamma,\mathsf{S})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert\widehat{\mathsf{J}}_{1}\bigcap\mathsf{J}_{0}\rvert\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}\ttgamma(\mathsf{S})\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert\widehat{\mathsf{J}}_{1}\rvert\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}\ttgamma(\mathsf{S})\right]},

(8)

\displaystyle\mathrm{mpow}(\mathbf{h};\ttgamma,\mathsf{S})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert\widehat{\mathsf{J}}_{1}\bigcap\mathsf{J}_{1}\rvert\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}\ttgamma(\mathsf{S})\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert\mathsf{J}_{1}\rvert\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}\ttgamma(\mathsf{S})\right]}.

(9)

In classical single hypothesis test, the null hypothesis is rejected if the
p
-value is lower than the nominal significance level. In this paper, for each individual test on
(\mathsf{v}_{m},\mathsf{t}_{m})
, we use the following thresholding rule:

\displaystyle(\mathbf{h}(\bm{\mathsf{p}}))_{m}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\ \mathsf{p}_{m}\leq s_{m},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(10)

The difference is that, in
eq.

10
, the threshold of
p
-value is no longer the significance level but chosen by certain criteria that we will soon introduce. Under this rule, designing the detection strategy
\mathbf{h}
amounts to designing
\mathbf{s}:=(s_{1},\dots,s_{M})
. Since the rejection rule
\mathbf{h}
is fully determined by
\mathbf{s}
, we denote
\mathrm{mFDR}(\mathbf{h};\ttgamma,\mathsf{S})
and
\mathrm{mpow}(\mathbf{h};\ttgamma,\mathsf{S})
by
\mathrm{mFDR}(\mathbf{s};\ttgamma,\mathsf{S})
and
\mathrm{mpow}(\mathbf{s};\ttgamma,\mathsf{S})
under
eq.

10
. We thus consider the following problem:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \max_{\mathbf{s}\in[0,1]^{M}}\mathrm{mpow}(\mathbf{s};\ttgamma,\mathsf{S})\\
\ST\mathrm{mFDR}(\mathbf{s};\ttgamma,\mathsf{S})\leq\alpha.\end{aligned}

(11)

In
1
, we show that the optimal solution to problem
eq.

11
is a level surface of
lfdr
. This theorem is a slight generalization of
[
18
, Theorem 2]
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose
1
holds and there exists
p\in(0,1)
and
(v,t)\in\mathsf{S}
such that
\mathrm{lfdr}(p;\ttgamma(v,t))<\alpha
.
Then the optimal solution
\mathbf{s}^{*}=(s_{1}^{*},\dots,s_{M}^{*})
to problem
eq.

11
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{lfdr}(s_{m}^{*};\ttgamma(\mathsf{v}_{m},\mathsf{t}_{m}))=\eta,\ m=1,\dots,M,

where
\eta
is a constant independent of
m
.

Proof.

The proof of this theorem is a slight modification of the proof of
[
18
, Theorem 3]
. We refer the reader to
[
20
, Appendix B]
for the proof details.
∎

Different from
[
18
, Theorem 2]
and
[
19
, Proposition 2.1]
, in
1
, we do not require that
f_{1}(\cdot;\ttgamma(v,t))
is continuous on the
closed interval

[0,1]
. This means that we allow for unbounded
f_{1}
such as the Beta distribution.
1
implies that the optimal threshold for
\{\mathsf{p}_{m}\}
corresponds to a level set of
lfdr
. Therefore, the rejection rule becomes

\displaystyle(\mathbf{h}(\bm{\mathsf{p}}))_{m}=\begin{cases}1&\mathrm{lfdr}(\mathsf{p}_{m};\ttgamma(\mathsf{v}_{m},\mathsf{t}_{m}))\leq\eta,\\
0&\mathrm{lfdr}(\mathsf{p}_{m};\ttgamma(\mathsf{v}_{m},\mathsf{t}_{m}))>\eta.\end{cases}

(12)

To choose
\eta
, we define the following quantities:

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}_{1,M}(\eta)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathrm{lfdr}(\mathsf{p}_{m};\ttgamma(\mathsf{v}_{m},\mathsf{t}_{m}))\cdot

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}\{\mathrm{lfdr}(\mathsf{p}_{m};\ttgamma(\mathsf{v}_{m},\mathsf{t}_{m}))\leq\eta\},

(13)

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}^{\prime}_{1,M}(\eta)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}(1-\tttheta_{m})\mathbb{I}\{\mathrm{lfdr}(\mathsf{p}_{m};\ttgamma(\mathsf{v}_{m},\mathsf{t}_{m}))\leq\eta\}

(14)

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}_{0,M}(\eta)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbb{I}\{\mathrm{lfdr}(\mathsf{p}_{m};\ttgamma(\mathsf{v}_{m},\mathsf{t}_{m}))\leq\eta\}.

(15)

By conditioning on
\mathsf{p}_{m}
and noting that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(1-\tttheta_{m})\mathbb{I}\{\mathrm{lfdr}(\mathsf{p}_{m};\ttgamma(\mathsf{v}_{m},\mathsf{t}_{m}))\leq\eta\}\nonscript\,:\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}\ttgamma,\mathsf{S}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{lfdr}(\mathsf{p}_{m};\ttgamma(\mathsf{v}_{m},\mathsf{t}_{m}))\mathbb{I}\{\mathrm{lfdr}(\mathsf{p}_{m};\ttgamma(\mathsf{v}_{m},\mathsf{t}_{m}))\leq\eta\}\nonscript\,:\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}\ttgamma,\mathsf{S}\right].

. Therefore, the quantity
\mathsf{r}_{M}(\eta):=\dfrac{\mathsf{d}_{1,M}(\eta)}{\mathsf{d}_{0,M}(\eta)}

approximates
\mathrm{mFDR}(\mathbf{h};\ttgamma,\mathsf{S})
in
eq.

8
. On the other hand, note that
\mathrm{mpow}(\mathbf{h};\ttgamma,\mathsf{S})
in
eq.

9
increases with
\eta
. Therefore, we choose the optimal
\eta
(denoted by
\tteta_{M}
) in the following way:

\displaystyle\tteta_{M}:=\sup\{\eta\nonscript\,:\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}\mathsf{r}_{M}(\eta)\leq\alpha\}.

(16)

Note that in practice we usually do not have access to the ground truths
\pi_{0}\circ\ttgamma(\mathsf{v}_{m},\mathsf{t}_{m})
and
f_{1}(\cdot;\ttgamma(\mathsf{v}_{m},\mathsf{t}_{m}))
, hence the
lfdr
is unknown. Therefore, we call
eqs.

12
and
16
the
oracle
solution.

IV
Estimation of Local False Discovery Rate

In this section, we state our method of substituting the
MLE
estimator to the oracle solution. Specifically, we first estimate
\ttgamma
, and thus obtain an estimate of
\mathsf{r}_{M}
. To estimate
\ttgamma
, we consider it as a band-limited generalized graph signal.
We make the following assumptions to ensure the consistency of the
MLE
estimation of
\ttgamma
.

Assumption 2
.

1.

The signal
\ttgamma
is bandlimited, i.e., for all
(v,t)\in\mathcal{J}
,

\displaystyle\ttgamma(v,t)=\sum_{k_{1}=1}^{K_{1}}\sum_{k_{2}=1}^{K_{2}}\ttxi_{k_{1},k_{2}}\cdot\phi_{k_{1}}(v)\psi_{k_{2}}(t),

(17)

where
K_{1},K_{2}
are positive integers,
\{\phi_{k}(\cdot)\nonscript\,:\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}k=1,\dots,N\}
is the graph Fourier basis and
