Ruled zero mean curvature surfaces in the three-dimensional light cone
Joseph Cho, Dami Lee, Wonjoo Lee, Seong-Deog Yang

Abstract
. We obtain a complete classification of ruled zero mean curvature surfaces in the three-dimensional lightcone. En route, we examine geodesics and screw motions in the space form, allowing us to discover helicoids. We also consider their relationship to catenoids using Weierstrass representations of zero mean curvature surfaces in the three-dimensional lightcone.

Preliminaries

In this section, we briefly review the basic differential geometry of three dimensional lightcone, including curve and surface theory, mainly to set the notations to be used throughout. (We refer the readers to

2.1.
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
as a quadric

We identify Lorentzian
4
-space
\mathbb{L}^{4}
with
\mathrm{Herm}(2)
as follows:

\mathbb{L}^{4}\ni(\mathrm{x}_{0},\mathrm{x}_{1},\mathrm{x}_{2},\mathrm{x}_{3})\sim\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{x}_{0}+\mathrm{x}_{3}&\mathrm{x}_{1}+i\mathrm{x}_{2}\\
\mathrm{x}_{1}-i\mathrm{x}_{2}&\mathrm{x}_{0}-\mathrm{x}_{3}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{Herm}(2).

Then

\langle X,X\rangle=-\det{X}.

An arbitrary
F\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
acts on
\mathbb{L}^{4}
as an orientation preserving isometry via the action

(2.1)

\mathrm{Herm}(2)\ni X\mapsto FXF^{\star}\in\mathrm{Herm}(2)

where
F^{\star}
is the conjugate transpose of
F
.
In fact,
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
is a two-to-one covering of the group of orientation-preserving and origin-fixing isometries of
\mathbb{L}^{4}
.

The submanifolds of
\mathbb{L}^{4}
we are interested in are

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}^{3}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{X\in\mathrm{Herm}(2)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathrm{x}_{0}-\mathrm{x}_{3}=0\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{X\in\mathrm{Herm}(2)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\langle X,X\rangle=0,\operatorname{tr}X>0\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{-}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{X\in\mathrm{Herm}(2)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\langle X,X\rangle=0,\operatorname{tr}X<0\}.

In particular, the action of
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
given by (
2.1
) acts as orientation preserving isometries of
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
.
Conversely, any orientation preserving isometry of
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
can be described by this action.

Example 2.1
.

Rotations in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
can be described as isometries that fix a
2
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{L}^{4}
(see, for example,
[

]

Normalizing the

D_{1}(\mu)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mu&0\\

2.2.
Curve theory

Given a unit-speed regular curve
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}I\to\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}\subset\mathbb{L}^{4}
given on an interval
I
, define

\kappa\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=-\frac{1}{2}\langle\gamma^{\prime\prime},\gamma^{\prime\prime}\rangle,\quad\mathbf{T}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\gamma^{\prime},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{N}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\kappa\gamma-\gamma^{\prime\prime}.

Then there exist uniquely a function
\tau
and a vector field
\mathbf{B}
along
\gamma
which form a null basis of
\mathbb{L}^{4}
with
\det(\gamma,\mathbf{T},\mathbf{N},\mathbf{B})<0
:

\displaystyle\langle X,Y\rangle

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr\text{\diagbox[]{{\shortstack[l]{$X$}}}{{\shortstack[r]{$Y$}}}}&\bm{\gamma}&\mathbf{T}&\mathbf{N}&\mathbf{B}\\
\hline\cr\bm{\gamma}&0&0&1&0\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{T}&0&1&0&0\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{N}&1&0&0&0\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{B}&0&0&0&1\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

Using such
\{\gamma,\mathbf{T},\mathbf{N},\mathbf{B}\}
as a moving frame along the curve
\gamma
, the Frenet equations are given by

(2.2)

\begin{pmatrix}\gamma^{\prime}\\
\mathbf{T}^{\prime}\\
\mathbf{N}^{\prime}\\
\mathbf{B}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&0\\
\kappa&0&-1&0\\
0&-\kappa&0&-\tau\\
\tau&0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\gamma\\
\mathbf{T}\\
\mathbf{N}\\
\mathbf{B}\end{pmatrix},

The values
\kappa
and
\tau
are referred to as the
cone curvature
and the
cone torsion
, respectively.
See
[
11
,
14
]
for details.

2.3.
Surface theory

Given an immersion
X\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
with coordinates
(u,v)=(u^{1},u^{2})\in\mathcal{U}
, there is a unique map
\mathrm{G}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{-}
which satisfies

\langle\mathrm{G},\mathrm{G}\rangle=\langle\mathrm{G},X_{u}\rangle=\langle\mathrm{G},X_{v}\rangle=\langle\mathrm{G},X\rangle-1=0,

which is called the
lightlike Gauss map
of
X

[
9
]
(also referred to as the
associated surface

[
12
]
).
The first and the second fundamental forms of
X
are then given by

\mathbf{g}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\langle X_{i},X_{j}\rangle\dif{u}^{i}\dif{u}^{j},\quad\mathbf{A}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\langle\mathrm{G},X_{ij}\rangle\dif{u}^{i}\dif{u}^{j},

respectively, from which the definition of mean curvature and the (extrinsic) Gauss curvature for
X
follows:

\mathrm{H}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{g}^{-1}\mathbf{A}),\quad\mathrm{K}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\det{(\mathbf{g}^{-1}\mathbf{A})}.

When a surface has
\mathrm{H}\equiv c\neq 0
for some constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
, then the surface is called a
constant mean curvature (CMC) surface
, while if it has
\mathrm{H}\equiv 0
, then the surface is referred to as a
zero mean curvature (ZMC) surface
.

When a surface is conformally parametrized, we will introduce the complex structure via
z=u+iv
.

An immersion
X\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
admits three different representations, each of which we will make use in this manuscript:
An immersion
X
can be viewed as a graph of any function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
over the surface

(u,v)\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}u^{2}+v^{2}&u+iv\\
u-iv&1\end{pmatrix}

so that

X(u,v)=e^{f(u,v)}\begin{pmatrix}u^{2}+v^{2}&u+iv\\
u-iv&1\end{pmatrix}.

When we represent an immersion
X
as a graph using the function
f
, we will denote it by
X_{f}
.

On the other hand, using the fact that the map

\mathbb{C}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}\ni\begin{pmatrix}z\\
w\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}z\\
w\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}z\\
w\end{pmatrix}^{\star}=\begin{pmatrix}z\bar{z}&z\bar{w}\\
\bar{z}w&w\bar{w}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}

is onto, we can represent an immersion via

X=\begin{pmatrix}A\\
C\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A\\
C\end{pmatrix}^{\star}=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\varphi\varphi^{\star}

for some complex-valued functions
A
and
C
defined on
\mathcal{U}
.
We will refer to such
\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
as the
lift of
X
into
\mathbb{C}^{2}
.

Finally, for any
F\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{U}\to\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
, we can also represent an immersion
X
via

X=F\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}F^{\star}.

We will refer to such
F
as the
lift of
X
into
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
.
Using this last characterization, we introduce the following notion:

Definition 2.2
.

We say two immersions
X
and
\tilde{X}
are
equal up to isometries and homothety of
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
if they satisfy
X(s,t)=rFX(s,t)F^{\star}
for some
r\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
F\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
, and denote this as

X\simeq\tilde{X}.

Remark 2.3
.

For
c\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, we have

\begin{pmatrix}c&0\\
0&c^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c&0\\
0&c^{-1}\end{pmatrix}^{\star}=c\bar{c}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

If
F_{c}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=F\begin{pmatrix}c&0\\
0&c^{-1}\end{pmatrix}
, then

\displaystyle X_{c}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=F_{c}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}F_{c}^{\star}=c\bar{c}F\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}F^{\star}=c\bar{c}X,\quad\dif{F}_{c}F_{c}^{-1}=\dif{F}F^{-1}.

In other words, homothetic images of
X
can be obtained via
F_{c}
.

2.4.
Representations of ZMC immersions

Suppose
X=X_{f}
is an immersion regarded as a graph using some function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
.
Then the mean curvature of
X_{f}
can be calculated as

(2.3)

\mathrm{H}=\frac{1}{2}e^{-2f(u,v)}(f_{uu}(u,v)+f_{vv}(u,v)).

Example 2.4

(A family of CMC surfaces in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
)
.

Let us consider the surface
X_{f}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
given via the function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}

f(u,v)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=d+\ln{\operatorname{sech}{(au+bv+c)}}

for some constants
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{R}
with
a^{2}+b^{2}\not=0
.
Then one can directly check that the mean curvature of
X_{f}
is

\mathrm{H}=-e^{-2d}(a^{2}+b^{2})/2.

If
\mathrm{H}\equiv 0
so that
X_{f}
is a ZMC surface, then
f
must be harmonic, so
f=\varphi+\bar{\varphi}
for some holomorphic function
\varphi
in a simply connected domain.
Then, after a conformal change of parameters if necessary, we see the following:

Lemma 2.5

(
[
13
]
)
.

X\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
has ZMC if and only if

X(z)=\varphi(z)\varphi(z)^{\star},\quad\varphi(z)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{pmatrix}A(z)\\
C(z)\end{pmatrix}

for some holomorphic functions
A
and
C
.

Now we would like to see how the lift of a ZMC immersion
X
to
\mathbb{C}^{2}
induces the lift into
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
:
A holomorphic
F\in C^{\omega}(\mathcal{U},\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C}))
is called
null
if
\det{F_{z}}=0
.
Given an arbitrary holomorphic
\varphi(z)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{pmatrix}A(z)\\
C(z)\end{pmatrix}
that is the lift of a ZMC immersion to
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, one can find a null-holomorphic

F=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}\in C^{\omega}(\mathcal{U},\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C}))

such that

F\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}F^{\star}=\begin{pmatrix}A\\
C\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A\\
C\end{pmatrix}^{\star}

via

(2.4)

F=\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
C&A^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&-E\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad E\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int\frac{((1/A)^{\prime})^{2}}{(C/A)^{\prime}}\dif{z}.

For a given null-holomorphic
F
we define meromorphic functions
G,g
and holomorphic
1
-forms
\Omega,\omega
by

(2.5)

\dif{F}F^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}G&-G^{2}\\
1&-G\end{pmatrix}\Omega,\quad F^{-1}\dif{F}=\begin{pmatrix}g&-g^{2}\\
1&-g\end{pmatrix}\omega.

This is the
Weierstrass representation
of ZMC surfaces in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}

[
13
,
12
,
17
,
16
]
, and
(g,\omega)
is called the
Weierstrass data
.
We call
G
and
g
the hyperbolic Gauss map and the secondary Gauss map, respectively.
The Hopf differential is

Q\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Omega\dif{G}=\omega\dif{g}.

For
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, the change of the Weierstrass data

(2.6)

(g,\omega)\mapsto(g,\lambda\omega)

induces a transformation where the metric, Hopf differential, and the second fundamental form transform as follows:

\mathbf{g}\mapsto|\lambda|^{2}\mathbf{g},\quad Q\mapsto\lambda Q,\quad\mathbf{A}=2\operatorname{Re}Q\mapsto\mathbf{A}_{\lambda}=2\operatorname{Re}\lambda Q.

When
|\lambda|^{2}=1
, this gives an isometric deformation of a ZMC surface, commonly referred to as the
associated family of ZMC surfaces
.

2.5.
Catenoids in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}

As examples of ZMC surfaces, we review catenoids in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
, ZMC surfaces that are invariant under rotations
[
5
]
.
For real constants
a,b,c
, consider

(2.7)

\varphi^{E}_{a}(z)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=e^{iaz}\begin{pmatrix}e^{iz}\\
e^{-iz}\end{pmatrix},\quad\varphi^{H}_{b}(z)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=e^{ibz}\begin{pmatrix}e^{z}\\
e^{-z}\end{pmatrix},\quad\varphi^{P}_{c}(z)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=e^{icz}\begin{pmatrix}z\\

They are lifts to
\mathbb{C}^{2}
of elliptic, hyperbolic, parabolic catenoids in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
, respectively.
Their lifts to
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
are

\displaystyle F^{E}_{a}(z)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{i(a+1)z}&-\frac{(a+1)^{2}}{4a}e^{-i(a-1)z}\\
e^{i(a-1)z}&-\frac{(a-1)^{2}}{4a}e^{-i(a+1)z}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle F^{H}_{b}(z)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{(ib+1)z}&-\frac{i(b-i)^{2}}{4b}e^{-(ib-1)z}\\
e^{(ib-1)z}&-\frac{i(b+i)^{2}}{4b}e^{-(ib+1)z}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle F^{P}_{c}(z)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{pmatrix}ze^{icz}&-\frac{1}{2}(2+icz)e^{-icz}\\
e^{icz}&-\frac{1}{2}ice^{-icz}\end{pmatrix}.

Their Weierstrass data can be written as

g^{E}(w)=g^{H}(w)=g^{P}(w)=w

and

(2.8)

\omega^{E}=\left(-\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{4a^{2}}\right)\frac{\operatorname{d}\!w}{w^{2}},\quad\omega^{H}=\left(-\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{4b^{2}}\right)\frac{\operatorname{d}\!w}{w^{2}},\quad\omega^{P}=-\frac{1}{4}\frac{\operatorname{d}\!w}{w^{2}}.

Remark 2.6
.

Note that there is only one parabolic catenoid up to isometries and homothety of
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
.
For CMC-1 surfaces in de Sitter three-space, a similar fact has been observed in
[
8
]
.

For elliptic catenoids, the time component
\mathrm{x}_{0}(u,v)
of
X^{E}_{a}(u,v)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=F^{E}_{a}(z)(F^{E}_{a}(z))^{\star}
satisfies
\mathrm{x}_{0}(u,v)=e^{-2av}\cosh{2v}.

So, for any

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr&a<-1&-1<a<1&1<a\\
\hline\cr\lim_{v\to\infty}\mathrm{x}_{0}(u,v)&+\infty&+\infty&0\\
\hline\cr\lim_{v\to-\infty}\mathrm{x}_{0}(u,v)&0&+\infty&+\infty\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

In other words, the two ends go to the future boundary if and only if

One end goes to the future boundary and the other end goes to the origin if and only if
-\tfrac{1}{4}<-\tfrac{1}{4}+\tfrac{1}{4a^{2}}<0
.

3.
Geodesics of
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}

As the induced metric on
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
is degenerate, we will consider geodesics in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
by using characterizations of geodesics in better known quadrics of
\mathbb{L}^{4}
: hyperbolic
3
-space
\mathbb{H}^{3}
and de Sitter
3
-space
\mathbb{S}^{3}_{1}
.
In both cases, geodesics are obtained as intersections of each corresponding space and a two dimensional plane in
\mathbb{L}^{4}
which passes through the origin; thus, they are curves in a totally geodesic surface, i.e. a totally umbilic surface with vanishing mean and (extrinsic) Gaussian curvature, whose geodesic curvature vanishes.

3.1.
Planes in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}

We first define planes as follows:

Definition 3.1
.

A
plane
is an immersion in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
that is totally geodesic, that is, the second fundamental form
\mathbf{A}
vanishes everywhere.

We will characterize planes within the class of totally umbilic surfaces in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
: they are given by intersections with affine
3
-planes, namely,

S[M,q]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{X\in\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\langle X,M\rangle=q\}

for some constant
M\in\mathbb{L}^{4}
and
q\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.
Then it can be directly checked that the lightlike Gauss map of
S[m,q]
is given by

\mathrm{G}=-\frac{1}{2q^{2}}\langle M,M\rangle X+\frac{1}{q}M

so that its mean and Gaussian curvature satisfies

\mathrm{H}=\frac{1}{2q^{2}}\langle M,M\rangle,\quad\mathrm{K}=\frac{1}{4q^{4}}\langle M,M\rangle^{2}.

Thus:

Lemma 3.2
.

An immersion is part of a plane if and only if it is part of
P[M,q]
defined via

P[M,q]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{X\in\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\langle X,M\rangle=q,\langle M,M\rangle=0\}.

3.2.
Geodesics

Now that we have the notion of a plane, we define geodesics as follows:

Definition 3.3
.

A regular curve in a plane of
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
is called a
geodesic
if its geodesic curvature vanishes in the plane.

To obtain an explicit formulation of geodesics, let us suppose that a plane is given via
P[M,q]
with
\langle M,M\rangle=0
; we will now calculate the metric induced on
P[M,q]
from the ambient space by constructing a coordinate chart.
Choosing any vector
\tilde{M}\in\mathbb{L}^{4}
such that
\langle M,\tilde{M}\rangle=q\neq 0
, note that
\mathrm{span}\{M,\tilde{M}\}
must have signature
(-+)
.
Thus,

\mathfrak{R}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathrm{span}\{M,\tilde{M}\}^{\perp}\cong\mathbb{E}^{2}

where
\mathbb{E}^{2}
denotes the usual Euclidean
2
-plane.

Let us define a bijection
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathfrak{R}\to P[M,q]
by

\psi(Y)=Y+\tilde{M}-\frac{1}{2q}\langle Y,Y\rangle M

with inverse

\psi^{-1}(X)=X-\tilde{M}-\frac{1}{q}\langle X,\tilde{M}\rangle M.

Viewing
\psi
as a coordinate chart, let
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}I\to P[m,q]
be a unit speed curve with
Y\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}I\to\mathfrak{R}
such that

\gamma=\psi\circ Y.

Then since

\gamma^{\prime}=(Y+\tilde{M}-\tfrac{1}{2q}\langle Y,Y\rangle M)^{\prime}=Y^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2q}\langle Y,Y^{\prime}\rangle M,

it must follow

\langle\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}\rangle=\langle Y^{\prime},Y^{\prime}\rangle.

Therefore, any plane
P[m,q]
is isometric to
\mathfrak{R}\cong\mathbb{E}^{2}
, and thus
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}I\to P[m,q]
is a curve in
P[m,q]
with vanishing geodesic curvature if and only if
Y\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\psi^{-1}\circ\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}I\to\mathfrak{R}\cong\mathbb{E}^{2}
is a line.

To obtain explicit parametrizations of geodesics, let
Y\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}I\to\mathfrak{R}
be a line parametrized by arc-length so that

Y(s)=Vs+W

for any constant
V,W\in\mathfrak{R}=\mathrm{span}\{M,\tilde{M}\}^{\perp}
with
\langle V,V\rangle=1
.
We then have

\displaystyle\gamma(s)=\psi\circ Y(s)

\displaystyle=Vs+W+\tilde{M}-\tfrac{1}{2q}(s^{2}+2\langle V,W\rangle s+\langle W,W\rangle)M

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2q}Ms^{2}+(V-\tfrac{1}{q}\langle V,W\rangle M)s+W+\tilde{M}-\tfrac{1}{2q}\langle W,W\rangle M

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{2}\vec{a}s^{2}+\vec{b}s+\vec{c}.

Then we can check directly that

(3.1)

\langle\vec{a},\vec{a}\rangle=\langle\vec{a},\vec{b}\rangle=\langle\vec{b},\vec{c}\rangle=\langle\vec{c},\vec{c}\rangle=0,\quad\langle\vec{a},\vec{c}\rangle=-\langle\vec{b},\vec{b}\rangle=1.

On the other hand, let
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}I\to\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
given via

\gamma(s)=\frac{1}{2}\vec{a}s^{2}+\vec{b}s+\vec{c}

where
\vec{a},\vec{b},\vec{c}\in\mathbb{L}^{4}
satisfies (
3.1
).
Then we have

\langle\gamma(s),\vec{a}\rangle=-1,

so that
\gamma
is a curve in the plane
P[\vec{a},-1]
.
Therefore, using
\psi^{-1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}P[\vec{a},-1]\to\mathfrak{R}
, we write
Y\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\psi^{-1}\circ\gamma
with
\vec{c}=\tilde{M}
as

Y=\tfrac{1}{2}\vec{a}s^{2}+\vec{b}s+\vec{c}-\vec{c}+\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\vec{a}s^{2}+\vec{b}s+\vec{c},\vec{c}\rangle\vec{a}=\vec{b}s

so that
Y
is a line.

Summarizing:

Theorem 3.4
.

A curve
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}I\to\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
is a geodesic of
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
if and only if there exists some
\vec{a},\vec{b},\vec{c}\in\mathbb{L}^{4}
satisfying

(3.2)

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|}\lx@intercol\hfil\langle X,Y\rangle\hfil\lx@intercol\\
\hline\cr\text{\diagbox[]{{\shortstack[l]{$X$}}}{{\shortstack[r]{$Y$}}}}&\vec{a}&\vec{b}&\vec{c}\\
\hline\cr\vec{a}&0&0&-1\\
\hline\cr\vec{b}&0&1&0\\
\hline\cr\vec{c}&-1&0&0\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

such that

(3.3)

\gamma(s)=\frac{1}{2}\vec{a}s^{2}+\vec{b}s+\vec{c}.

Using the parametrization, we can also deduce the following:

Lemma 3.5
.

All geodesics are congruent to each other.

Proof.

Let
\vec{a},\vec{b},\vec{c}
be arbitrary vectors of
\mathbb{L}^{4}
which satisfy (
3.2
).
By applying an isometry of
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
, we may assume that
\vec{c}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
.
Then (
3.2
) implies that

\vec{a}=\begin{pmatrix}2&w\\
\bar{w}&\frac{1}{2}w\bar{w}\end{pmatrix},\quad\vec{b}=\begin{pmatrix}0&e^{i\theta}\\
e^{-i\theta}&\frac{1}{2}(we^{-i\theta}+\bar{w}e^{i\theta})\end{pmatrix}

for some
w\in\mathbb{C}
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
.
Let
F\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{-i\theta/2}&0\\
-\frac{1}{2}\bar{w}e^{i\theta/2}&e^{i\theta/2}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
, then

F\vec{a}F^{\star}=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad F\vec{b}F^{\star}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad F\vec{c}F^{\star}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},

and thus the claim follows.
∎

3.3.
Geodesics as space curves

To make connection with the curve theory reviewed in Section
2.2
, let
\gamma
be a geodesic given by the unit speed parametrization as in Theorem
3.4
, so that

\gamma^{\prime}(s)=\vec{a}s+\vec{b},\quad\gamma^{\prime\prime}(s)=\vec{a}.

Thus the cone curvature of
\gamma
vanishes:

\kappa=-\frac{1}{2}\langle\gamma^{\prime\prime},\gamma^{\prime\prime}\rangle=0.

On the other hand, since

\mathrm{span}\{\gamma,\mathbf{T},\mathbf{N}\}=\mathrm{span}\{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime\prime}\}=\mathrm{span}\{\vec{a},\vec{b},\vec{c}\}

is constant in
s
, we have that
\mathbf{B}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}I\to\mathrm{span}\{\gamma,\mathbf{T},\mathbf{N}\}^{\perp}
is a constant vector, and hence the cone torsion
\tau
also vanishes.

To consider the converse, let us assume that
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}I\to\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
is a unit speed curve with
\kappa=\tau=0
.
Then the Frenet equation (
2.2
) implies
\gamma^{\prime\prime\prime}=T^{\prime\prime}=-N^{\prime}=\vec{0}
.
Hence,
\gamma^{\prime\prime}
is a constant vector, say
\vec{a}\in\mathbb{L}^{4}
.
Since
\kappa=0
if and only if
\gamma^{\prime\prime}
is lightlike, we have
\langle\vec{a},\vec{a}\rangle=0
.

Integrating once with respect to
s
, we obtain
\gamma^{\prime}(s)=\vec{a}s+\vec{b}
for some
\vec{b}\in\mathbb{L}^{4}
, and since
\gamma
is unit speed,

1=\langle\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}\rangle=\langle\vec{a}s+\vec{b},\vec{a}s+\vec{b}\rangle=2\langle\vec{a},\vec{b}\rangle s+\langle\vec{b},\vec{b}\rangle,

allowing us to conclude that

\langle\vec{a},\vec{b}\rangle=0,\langle\vec{b},\vec{b}\rangle=1
.

Integrating once more with respect to
s
, we may write

\gamma(s)=\frac{1}{2}\vec{a}s^{2}+\vec{b}s+\vec{c}

for some
\vec{c}\in\mathbb{L}^{4}
.
The fact that
\gamma
takes values in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
tells us

0=\langle\gamma(s),\gamma(s)\rangle=\langle\vec{a},\vec{c}\rangle s^{2}+s^{2}+2\langle\vec{b},\vec{c}\rangle s+\langle\vec{c},\vec{c}\rangle,

so that
\langle\vec{a},\vec{c}\rangle=-1
,
\langle\vec{b},\vec{c}\rangle=0
, and
\langle\vec{c},\vec{c}\rangle=0
.

Thus we conclude:

Theorem 3.6
.

A unit speed curve is a geodesic if and only if its cone curvature and cone torsion vanishes.

Remark 3.7
.

In Riemannian manifolds, a geodesic is determined by its initial position and initial velocity.
However, that is not the case in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
.
For example, let
\gamma
be given as in (
3.3
) and take
\vec{a}
,
\vec{b}
, and
\vec{c}
as in the proof of Lemma
3.5
with
\theta=0
.
Then any arbitrary choice of
w=i\mu\in i\mathbb{R}
gives geodesics all with the same initial position and initial velocity.
This is due to the freedom of choice of
\tilde{M}
.

4.
Two examples of ruled ZMC surfaces
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}

In this section, we will examine two important ruled ZMC surfaces in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
, with an eye on obtaining a complete classification of all ruled ZMC surfaces in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
.

4.1.
Screw motions in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}

One of the most important example of a ruled minimal surface in Euclidean space is a helicoid, obtained by applying a certain screw motion to a geodesic.
Our first step in the classification for ruled ZMC surface in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
is to mimic the Euclidean case, and apply a certain screw motion to a geodesic, and see if the resulting surface has zero mean curvature.
Thus, we first devote our attention to screw motions in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
, by first examining isometries that form a one-parameter subgroup under composition:

Fact 4.1
.

Let
\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\to\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
be a one-parameter subgroup of isometries of
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
, so that

\varphi(s+t)=\varphi(s)\varphi(t).

Hence
\varphi(s)^{-1}(\varphi(s+t)-\varphi(s))=\varphi(t)-\varphi(0)
, which implies

\varphi(s)^{-1}\varphi(s)^{\prime}=\varphi(0)^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C}).

Then
\varphi(s)=e^{s\varphi(0)^{\prime}}
.
For
a,b,c\in\mathbb{C}
with
\Delta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sqrt{-(a^{2}+bc)}\not=0
,

\exp\left(s\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&-a\end{pmatrix}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos{\Delta s}+\frac{a}{\Delta}\sin{\Delta s}&\frac{b}{\Delta}\sin{\Delta s}\\
\frac{c}{\Delta}\sin{\Delta s}&\cos{\Delta s}-\frac{a}{\Delta}\sin{\Delta s}\end{pmatrix}.

So let us consider all cases of
A\in\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})
, so that
A^{2}=-(\det{A})I_{2}
, generating such one-parameter subgroups, where the rotation matrices of Example
2.1
will be used.

Case 1.
Suppose that
\det{A}\not=0
.
Then
\lambda\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sqrt{-\det{A}}
is an eigenvalue of
A
and

A=M\,\operatorname{diag}(\lambda,-\lambda)\,M^{-1}

for some
M\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
.
Then the one-parameter subgroup
s\mapsto e^{sA}
is similar to
s\mapsto D_{1}(e^{\lambda s})
.
If
\lambda
is purely imaginary,
\varphi
are elliptic rotations.
If
\lambda
is real,
\varphi
are hyperbolic rotations.
If
\lambda
is neither real nor purely imaginary, then
\varphi
are the
screw motions
.

Case 2.
Suppose that
\det{A}=0
.
Then
A=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\beta&-\alpha^{2}\\
\beta^{2}&-\alpha\beta\end{pmatrix}
for some
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
and
e^{sA}=I+sA
.
Let

\lambda\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sqrt{-i\alpha/\beta},\qquad h\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=2i\alpha\beta\qquad\text{for }\ \alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}\ \backslash\ \{0\}.

Then

D_{2}(\lambda)e^{sA}D_{2}(\lambda)^{-1}=\varphi(hs)

where

\varphi(k)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{pmatrix}1+i\frac{k}{2}&\frac{k}{2}\\
\frac{k}{2}&1-i\frac{k}{2}\end{pmatrix}=BP(k)B^{-1},\quad B\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&-i\\
-i&1\end{pmatrix}.

So the one-parameter subgroup
s\mapsto e^{sA}
is similar to
s\mapsto P(sh)
in
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
for some
h\in\mathbb{C}
.
If
\alpha
or
\beta
is 0, it’s trivial. Note that, if
h=re^{i\theta}
, then

\begin{pmatrix}e^{-i\theta/2}&0\\
0&e^{i\theta/2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&sre^{i\theta}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}e^{-i\theta/2}&0\\
0&e^{i\theta/2}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&rs\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},

so it is conjugate to a single parabolic rotation.
In conclusion, we obtain the following:

Proposition 4.2
.

Any one-parameter subgroup of isometries of
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
is similar to

D_{1}(e^{\lambda s})=\begin{pmatrix}e^{\lambda s}&0\\
0&e^{-\lambda s}\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{or}\quad P(rs)=\begin{pmatrix}1&rs\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},

for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
r\in\mathbb{R}
.

Now that we have the notions of geodesics and screw motions, we define the following special classes of surfaces:

Definition 4.3
.

Let
X\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
be an immersion.

•

If
X
is invariant under screw motions, then
X
is called a
helicoidal surface
.

•

If
X
is foliated by geodesics, then
X
is called a
ruled surface
.

4.2.
Helicoids

Now let us consider the surface obtained by applying a screw motion to a geodesic:

\displaystyle X(u,v)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{(a+ib)v}&0\\
0&e^{-(a+ib)v}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u^{2}&u\\
u&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}e^{(a+ib)v}&0\\
0&e^{-(a+ib)v}\end{pmatrix}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}e^{2av}u^{2}&e^{2ibv}u\\
e^{-2ibv}u&e^{-2av}\end{pmatrix}.

Then one can calculate the lightlike Gauss map
\mathrm{G}
and the corresponding fundamental forms to find that
\mathrm{H}\equiv 0
.
Thus, as in the case of minimal surfaces in Euclidean space, we define helicoids in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
as follows:

.

For arbitrary real numbers

with

, let
X
be a ZMC surface defined by

We call

the

of
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
.
Any surface that is congruent to

up to isometries of

are referred to as

(see left of Figure

Now we note some important geometric facts about helicoids in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
:

Lemma 4.5
.

The following hold:

•

For any real numbers
a,b
with
b\not=0
, the curve

is a unit-speed helix and has constant cone curvature

and constant cone torsion
\tau=4ab
.

•

The metric is given by
\mathbf{g}=\operatorname{d}\!u^{2}+4au\operatorname{d}\!u\operatorname{d}\!v+4(a^{2}+b^{2})u^{2}\operatorname{d}\!v^{2}
, so
u
-parameter curves and
v
-parameter curves of

make angles of constant magnitude not equal to
\tfrac{\pi}{2}
.

•

The metric is singular if
u=0
.

Proof.

The proof follows from direct calculations.
∎

In particular, application of screw motion to geodesics does not result in orthogonal parametrization of the resulting helicoid, which makes the case of

stand out from the cases of other space forms.
However, we can find a conformal reparametrization of the standard helicoid as follows:
If we let
\tilde{v}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=2bv

\tilde{H}^{a,b}(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=H^{a,b}\left(e^{\tilde{u}-\tfrac{a}{b}\tilde{v}},\tfrac{1}{2b}\tilde{v}\right)

is conformally parametrized, and

(4.1)

\displaystyle\tilde{H}^{a,b}(u,v)

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{a}{b}v}\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\frac{v}{2}}&0\\
0&e^{-i\frac{v}{2}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}e^{2u}&e^{u}\\
e^{u}&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\frac{v}{2}}&0\\
0&e^{-i\frac{v}{2}}\end{pmatrix}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\varphi^{\mathrm{Hel}}_{i\frac{a}{2b}}(u+iv)\varphi^{\mathrm{Hel}}_{i\frac{a}{2b}}(u+iv)^{\star},\qquad\varphi^{\mathrm{Hel}}_{c}(z)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=e^{cz}\begin{pmatrix}e^{z}\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

Direct calculations show that the

4.3.
Parabolic catenoids

We now give another important example that is a ruled ZMC surface in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
: the parabolic catenoid reviewed in Section

.
To check that parabolic catenoid is a ruled surface, we first observe that if
z=u+iv
and
\varphi^{P}_{c}
is from (
2.7
), then

(4.2)

C^{P}_{c}(u,v)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\varphi^{P}_{c}(u+iv)\varphi^{P}_{c}(u+iv)^{\star}=e^{-2cv}\begin{pmatrix}1&iv\\

(4.3)

C^{P}_{c}(e^{2cv}u,v)=\begin{pmatrix}1&iv\\

Remark 4.6
.

The map

\mathbb{R}\ni c\mapsto p_{c}(v)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&iv\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}e^{cv}&0\\
0&e^{-cv}\end{pmatrix}

in (
4.3
) is not a one-parameter subgroup of isometries, that is,
p_{c}(v_{1}+v_{2})\not=p_{c}(v_{1})p_{c}(v_{2})
.
Hence it is not a screw motion.
On the other hand, the map

\mathbb{R}\ni c\mapsto q_{c}(v)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=e^{-cv}\begin{pmatrix}1&iv\\

in (
4.2
) induces a one-parameter group, that is,
q_{c}(v_{1}+v_{2})=q_{c}(v_{1})q_{c}(v_{2})
.
However,
q_{c}(v)
does not represent an isometry.

5.
Helicoids and the associate family of catenoids

In the Euclidean case, helicoids and catenoids are related by an isometric deformation, called the associated family.
In this section, we examine whether helicoids in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
can also be found in the associated family of catenoids in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
.

5.1.
Associated family of catenoids in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}

Recall from (
2.6
) that a ZMC surface with Weierstrass data
(g,\omega)
admits an isometric deformation known as the
associated family
by a change in the Weierstrass data via

(g,\omega)\mapsto(g,\lambda\omega)

for any unit complex constant
\lambda\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\subset\mathbb{C}
.
Taking the Weierstrass data as

(g,\omega_{\delta})=\left(w,\delta\frac{\dif{w}}{w^{2}}\right),

for some
\delta\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, we note from (
2.8
) that the resulting immersion
X_{\delta}
is

•

an elliptic catenoid if
\delta>-\frac{1}{4}
,

•

a parabolic catenoid if
\delta=-\frac{1}{4}
, or

•

a hyperbolic catenoid if
\delta<-\frac{1}{4}
.

Therefore, any surface with Weierstrass data

(g,\omega_{\delta})=\left(w,\delta\frac{\dif{w}}{w^{2}}\right),

for any
\delta\in\mathbb{C}
must be in the associated family of a catenoid, given by the Weierstrass data

(g,\omega_{|\delta|})=\left(w,|\delta|\frac{\dif{w}}{w^{2}}\right).

5.2.
Helicoids and the associated family of catenoids

Turning our attention to the conformally parametrized (standard) helicoid
\tilde{H}^{a,b}
in (
4.1
), we first note that we may assume without loss of generality that
b=\frac{1}{2}
.
Then the lift of
\tilde{H}^{a}
to
\mathbb{C}^{2}
is given in (
4.1
) by

\varphi^{\mathrm{Hel}}_{a}(z)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=e^{iaz}\begin{pmatrix}e^{z}\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

Thus we can find the lift
F^{a}
to
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
using (
2.4
):

F^{a}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}e^{\tfrac{z}{2}}&0\\
0&e^{-\tfrac{z}{2}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&-\frac{(1+ia)^{2}}{1+2ia}\\
1&-\frac{a^{2}}{1+2ia}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}e^{iaz}&0\\
0&e^{-iaz}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}e^{\tfrac{z}{2}}&0\\
0&e^{-\tfrac{z}{2}}\end{pmatrix}.

This in turn allows us to find the Weierstrass data using (
2.5
), so that

\displaystyle(G,\Omega)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{a-i}{a}e^{z},a^{2}e^{-z}\dif{z}\right)

\displaystyle(g,\omega)

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{a(1+ia)}{2a-i}e^{(-1-2ia)z},-e^{(1+2ia)z}\dif{z}\right).

Making a change of coordinate via
w\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=g
, we may normalize the Weierstrass data as

(\tilde{g},\tilde{\omega})=\left(w,-\frac{a(a-i)}{(2a-i)^{2}}\frac{\dif{w}}{w^{2}}\right).

Therefore, we learn:

Theorem 5.1
.

Every helicoid in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
is in the associated family of some catenoid in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
.

However, the converse is not true, namely, there are catenoids that do not have helicoids in the associated family.
To see this, we note that for

\delta(a)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=-\frac{a(a-i)}{(2a-i)^{2}},

one can directly check that for real functions of
\mathrm{x}
and
\mathrm{y}
of
a
given by

\mathrm{x}(a)+i\,\mathrm{y}(a)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=-\frac{1}{4}-\delta(a),

we have

\left(\mathrm{x}(a)^{2}+\mathrm{y}(a)^{2}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{x}(a)\left(\mathrm{x}(a)^{2}+\mathrm{y}(a)^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{64}\mathrm{y}(a)^{2}=0,

which is the formula for a cardioid.
As
a
varies from
-\infty
to
\infty
, the image starts from
(-1/4,0)
and wraps around in the counterclockwise direction.

In particular, we learn that

|\delta(a)|\in[0,\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt{3}}],

so that when
|c|>\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt{3}}
, the catenoids with Weierstrass data
(g,\omega_{c})=\left(z,c\frac{\dif{w}}{w^{2}}\right)
, have no helicoids in its associated family.
We summarize:

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
X
be a ZMC catenoid in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
given by the Weierstrass data

\mathrm{g}=\zeta,\qquad\omega=\frac{\delta}{\zeta^{2}}\operatorname{d}\!\zeta\qquad\text{for}\quad\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{+}.

Then the catenoids can be classified according to the different catenoids and helicoids they admit under their associated families (see also Figure
2
):

•

0<\delta<\frac{1}{4}
:
two elliptic catenoids and two helicoids (Fig.
3
),

•

5.3.
Associated family of catenoids under Lawson-type correspondence between
\mathbb{I}^{3}
and
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}

Lawson-type correspondence between ZMC surfaces in the isotropic
3
-space
\mathbb{I}^{3}
and the
3
-dimensional lightcone
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
has been established in
[
16
]
as a generalization of the Umehara-Yamada perturbation
[
21
]
.

In this section, we derive a similar type of correspondence between ZMC surfaces in
\mathbb{I}^{3}
and in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
, and consider the correspondence between the associated family of catenoids in
\mathbb{I}^{3}
and
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
.
The derivation is obtained efficiently by viewing both classes of surfaces as graphs:
Suppose that
X\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
is an immersion represented as a graph of a function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
, that is,

X(u,v)=e^{f(u,v)}\begin{pmatrix}u^{2}+v^{2}&u+iv\\
u-iv&1\end{pmatrix}.

We have seen in (
2.3
) that
X
is a ZMC surface if and only if
f
is a harmonic map.
However, it is known
[
20
]
that any ZMC surface in
\mathbb{I}^{3}
must be a graph of a harmonic map, giving us the following correspondence between ZMC surfaces in
\mathbb{I}^{3}
and
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
:

Lemma 5.3
.

The following map

(5.1)

T\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{I}^{3}\to\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+},\qquad T(\mathrm{x},\mathrm{y},\ell)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=e^{\ell}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{x}^{2}+\mathrm{y}^{2}&\mathrm{x}+i\mathrm{y}\\
\mathrm{x}-i\mathrm{y}&1\end{pmatrix}

sends ZMC surfaces in
\mathbb{I}^{3}
to ZMC surfaces in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
.

Note that

T(\mathrm{x},\mathrm{y},\ell)=P(\mathrm{x}+i\mathrm{y})D_{1}(e^{-\ell/2})\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}\left(P(\mathrm{x}+i\mathrm{y})D_{1}(e^{-\ell/2})\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}\right)^{\star}.

Example 5.4
.

The image of the plane

in
\mathbb{I}^{3}
under
T
in (
5.1
) is

u-iv&1\end{pmatrix},

which is the parabolic catenoid.

We shall now examine the relationship between the associated families of catenoids in
\mathbb{I}^{3}
and
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
.
For arbitrary real constants
\alpha
and
\beta

With

By Lemma

Then using

(5.2)

\displaystyle X_{c}(u,v)

\displaystyle=X_{\alpha,\beta}(u,v)

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha}e^{r(u\cos\beta-v\sin\beta)}D_{1}(e^{\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+i\beta)})\begin{pmatrix}e^{2u}&e^{u+iv}\\

6.
Classification of ruled ZMC surfaces in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}

In this section, we show that any ruled ZMC surface in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
must be either a helicoid (
4.1
) or a parabolic catenoid (
4.3
) up to isometry and homothety.
As noted in Lemma
4.5
, the surface obtained by applying screw motions to a geodesic result in a ZMC surface, which we call helicoid; however, helices and geodesics do not meet orthogonally (even though they meet at a constant angle).
Therefore, the standard techniques of classifying ruled ZMC surface do not work in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
.

To overcome this obstacle, we will view ruled surfaces as application of a curve of isometries to a geodesic, and find the condition on the isometries for the resulting surface to have ZMC.

Thus, without loss of generality, we assume that any spacelike ruled surface in
\mathbb{Q}^{3}_{+}
is parameterized as

X(s,t)=F(s)\delta(t)F(s)^{\star}

for a geodesic

\delta(t)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{pmatrix}t^{2}&t\\
t&1\end{pmatrix}

and some curve

in
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
with

.
Let

\displaystyle X^{F}_{*}(s,t)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=F(s)^{-1}X_{*}(s,t)(F(s)^{-1})^{\star},

\displaystyle\Omega(s)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=F(s)^{-1}F(s)^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha(s)&\beta(s)\\
\gamma(s)&-\alpha(s)\end{pmatrix}.

\displaystyle X^{F}(s,t)=\delta(t),\quad X^{F}_{s}(s,t)=\Omega(s)\delta(t)+\delta(t)\Omega(s)^{\star},\quad X^{F}_{t}(s,t)=\delta(t)^{\prime},

\displaystyle X^{F}_{ss}(s,t)=F(s)^{-1}F(s)^{\prime\prime}\delta(t)+2\Omega(s)\delta(t)\Omega(s)^{\star}+\delta(t)(F(s)^{-1}F(s)^{\prime\prime})^{\star},

1&0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}2t&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\Omega(s)^{\star},\qquad X^{F}_{tt}(s,t)=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\

Case 2.
