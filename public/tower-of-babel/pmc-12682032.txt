A conserved teleost motor asymmetry requires evolutionary retention of vision
Authors: Jacob Starkey, John Hageter, Rob Kozol, Erik R Duboué, Eric J Horstick
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBehavioral laterality is a prominent Bilateria feature, where motor functions are performed with a directional preference. Underlying such behaviors is thought to be hemispheric specializations that improve behavioral performance. Despite the prevalence, how behavioral lateralization is modulated throughout evolution is poorly understood. Comparative approaches are a powerful strategy for addressing fundamental biological questions from individual to evolutionary scales. Previously, we described a robust visuomotor asymmetry in larval zebrafish search pattern behavior. Here, we compare visuomotor asymmetry across several larval teleost species, demonstrating that general motor patterns are variable while behavioral asymmetry is conserved across sighted teleosts, yet not naturally blind cavefish. Our data indicate that visuomotor asymmetry in larval teleosts is a conserved behavior that can be modulated by natural selection against vision. This establishes larval teleosts as a powerful system for dissecting the molecular and evolutionary forces that govern behavioral lateralization, bridging the gap between mechanism and adaptation.Subject areas: biological sciences, zoology, neuroscience, evolutionary biology

Introduction

Behavioral asymmetries were long thought to be unique to humans.1,2 We now know that paw, hand, foot, eye, and many other motor preferences are observed in nearly all bilateral species, and neural asymmetries likely underlie these behaviors.3,4,5,6,7,8,9 This conservation underscores important behavioral to functional associations. Indeed, a comparative study of handedness across diverse primates illustrated the conservation of handedness in primate evolution and also the overall diversity of hand-use strength, directional preference (left or right-handed), and level of task association.10 Similarly, visual asymmetries have been observed across avian and fish species, where each eye is preferred for specific tasks.7,11,12 Pigeons and chickens are canonical models for visual asymmetry, choosing to use the right eye for visual discrimination tasks.13,14 Diverse fish species also show an asymmetric eye preference for visualizing conspecifics.15,16 These examples illustrate that various forms of behavioral asymmetries exist, show a range of phenotypic expressions, and can be broadly conserved within phylogenetic groups. Cross-species comparisons are a powerful approach to assess not only the conservation of behaviors but also to define how behavioral responses change according to the environment, behavioral adaptation, or genetic heterogeneity.17,18,19,20 A comparative approach may be particularly valuable for understanding laterality because many well-established asymmetric behaviors, like handedness or eye preferences, are only partially instructed by genetics,21,22,23,24,25 which suggests that environmental factors are also likely important. Nonetheless, the lack of clear mechanisms highlights the complexity underlying functional and behavioral lateralization. We hypothesize that comparative approaches might reveal insights into the interactions that drive behavioral asymmetry and brain lateralization at individual and evolutionary scales. However, many of these paradigms are challenging to study, or use models not amendable for resolving instructive neural mechanisms.

Light-driven behaviors are conserved and essential for survival across species.26,27 Recently, we described a visuomotor asymmetry in larval zebrafish that occurs during light-search behavior that is dependent on a subset of genetically defined thalamic neurons and requires direct retinal input.25,28,29 Following the loss of illumination, zebrafish larvae initiate a search pattern behavior that shows a strong rightward or leftward directional bias. At the population level, the initiation of search leads to increased directional turning, increased localized movement, and decreased displacement.28 These patterns are consistent with search patterns observed across species.30,31,32,33 However, at the individual level, the left or right turn direction is persistent across trials and represents a highly trackable lateralized behavior.25,29 This robust behavioral assay provides a foundational tool to reveal neural substrates underlying lateralized behaviors. Zebrafish provides a genetically tractable model to explore the basis of behavioral asymmetry in the form of a straight-forward left or right motor asymmetry, a fish handedness. It is unclear however, whether light-search pattern behavior and associated motor asymmetry is conserved across other fish species. Comparative approaches have proven to be instrumental in understanding conservation and natural selection forces modulating phenotypic traits. We reasoned that larval teleosts would provide a laboratory tractable system; thus, we assessed visually mediated motor asymmetry in diverse teleost taxa. We compare behavior across six different sighted teleost species representing four different phylogenetic orders. By capitalizing on teleosts as a model we could also leverage the availability of Astyanax, which includes cavefish with evolutionarily derived blindness. The use of Astyanax mexicanus allows for the unique intra-species comparison of sighted “surface” fish that reside in terrestrial rivers compared to blind populations exclusively found in underground cave systems.34,35 Through the analysis of light search behavior across these diverse species, we posit that while motor patterns will vary across species, visually mediated motor asymmetry will be conserved within sighted teleost.

Results

Motor behavior during light search is conserved in sighted teleost evolution

To determine if visuomotor asymmetry during light-search pattern behavior is a conserved property of larval teleost fish, we assessed the response to changes in illumination in six fish species. We assessed 4–5 days post fertilization (dpf) old Danionella cerebrum (N = 39),36,37,38 6–7 dpf old fathead minnow (Pimephales promelas, N = 77),39,40,41 5–6 dpf Mexican tetras (Astyanax mexicanus – including same-species surface, N = 45, and cave morphs, Pachón, N = 50 & Tinaja, N = 47),42,43 12–14 dpf medaka (Oryzias latipes, N = 55),44,45 and 6–7 dpf sheepshead pupfish (Cyprinodon variegatus, N = 80)46,47 to compare responses with Zebrafish (Danio rerio, TL strain, N = 80). Across all species the timepoints used for testing corresponded with free-swimming larval stages. Danionella, fathead minnows, and zebrafish are all in the order Cypriniformes, medaka belongs to Beloniformes, sheepshead in Cyprinodontiformes, and Mexican tetras the order Characiformes (Figure 1A). The species selected represent four phylogenetic orders and diverse geographically distinct regions (Figure 1B). Altogether, our analysis covers approximately 220 million years of teleost evolution. In our comparative strategy we tested the Mexican tetra, Astyanax mexicanus. In addition to sighted fish, there are same-species blind cavefish, which have evolved in diverse cave systems in perpetual darkness for approximately 20,000 to 1 million years.48,49 Astyanax cavefish morphs from the Pachón and Tinaja cave system were subjected to the same assay as surface conspecifics and other sighted teleost larvae. The Pachón and Tinaja cavefish come from geographically distal caves and exhibit convergent trait evolution, thus suggesting some unique underlying change during the evolution of these two groups.34,50 Path trajectories were recorded over four repeated 30-s light transitions using custom designed behavioral apparatus, which, as we have previously demonstrated, provides a rigorous readout of individual visuomotor asymmetry.24,28,29,51 Key features of larval zebrafish search pattern behavior include an increase in total turning angle (TTA) (Figure 2B), reduced displacement (Figure 2C), and increased local movement (fractal dimension metric; [Figure 2D]).24,28,29 Following the light-to-dark transition sighted species demonstrated photo-mediated responses that were mostly consistent with zebrafish search behavior, in particular, an increase in localized movement (fractal dimension) (Figures 2A–2D). However, we also observed that search motor performance significantly varied across species (main effect of species 1-way ANOVA F[7,439]=60.00, R2 = 0.5275, p < 0.0001 [TTA]; F[7,439]=22.89, R2 = 0.2446, p < 0.0001 [displacement]; F[7,439]=22.40, R2 = 0.3033, p < 0.0001 [fractal dimension]) and displayed significant variation from zebrafish (Figures 2B–2D). Conversely, no indication of photo-mediated search was observed for either blind cavefish population (two-tailed t test for Pachón [P] and Tinaja [T] cavefish; TTA, P:p = 0.1369, T:p = 0.0760; displacement, P:p = 0.1682, T:p = 0.6476; fractal dimension, P:p = 0.7206, T:p = 0.5627) (Figures 2A–2D). However, prior work has shown that blind cavefish have retained some photo-mediated behaviors, yet display a reversed valence compared to sighted conspecifics.52,53 Therefore, for Astyanax surface and cave morphs we included a slight modification: we added another 30-s recording interval following the restoration of environmental illumination (Figure S1A). Persistent same-direction turning in surface fish was abated by light restoration, consistent with previously observed patterns in zebrafish.28 Conversely, in Pachón & Tinaja cavefish, no visually induced motor asymmetry or search patterns for a “dark search” were observed following the dark-to-light transition (dark search MI with 1-sample Wilcoxon signed rank test to 0.5: Pachón, 0.530 ± 0.040, p = 0.471; Tinaja, 0.493 ± 0.046, p > 0.999) (Figures S1B and S1C). Therefore, cavefish have a complete loss-of-light search behavior.

To further compare search pattern responses, we calculated the fold change in baseline-to-dark behavior to give a magnitude of photo-responsiveness, which we used to perform a principle component analysis (PCA) (Figures 2E–2H, S2H, and S2I). From the PCA analysis, qualitatively, several distinct clusters emerge (Figure 2H). We reason these data are consistent with the presence of multiple search motor profiles across species, likely selected based on different environmental conditions from their native habitats. Notably, C. variegatus, while shown to maintain asymmetric turn behavior, closely clustered with the cavefish populations. This is consistent with the overall lower levels of photo-responsiveness relative to other sighted species and likely a selected feature for this species inhabiting turbid water with lower light levels, which has been demonstrated to impact visually mediated behaviors.54,55,56 Such variation in search motor outputs could suggest genetic regulation. To provide some evidence for this hypothesis we tested five additional zebrafish strains. In zebrafish, different strains exhibit substantial genetic heterogeneity.57,58,59 Indeed, significant variation in search motor performance was also observed across the different zebrafish strains (main effect of species 1-way ANOVA F[5,378]=43.28, R2 = 0.3641, p < 0.0001 [TTA]; F[5,378]=47.31, R2 = 0.3849, p < 0.0001 [displacement]; F[5,378]=32.62, R2 = 0.3014, p < 0.0001 [fractal dimension]) (Figures S2A–S2C). Although, a PCA analysis using light-to-dark fold change showed more uniform, but not exclusive, grouping (Figures S2D–S2G). The more consistent grouping compared to that observed for different species is likely expected for a consistent level of overall photo-responsiveness despite differences in single motor metric performance. Therefore, while we cannot make direct statistical comparisons between the behavior of the diverse teleost species and zebrafish strains, together these datasets show that general visuomotor search features vary across species and even among same-species genetic strains, suggesting genetic regulation of some motor features.

Visuomotor bias in teleost is dependent upon the conservation of vision

Next, we wanted to show whether light-induced motor asymmetry was conserved, and if so, is this a generalized motor feature or variable through teleost evolution. Larval zebrafish visuomotor asymmetry is observed as an individual animal preference to perform persistent leftward or rightward turns following the loss of light, which at the individual level the directional preference is stable over time24,29 (Figure 3A). Motor asymmetry was assessed using a “match index,” which is a statistical measure of the proportion of trials where turn direction matches first trial direction. A sustained motor asymmetry results in a match index significantly elevated from random, indicated by a match index of 0.5. Comparing all species, we found a significant difference in motor asymmetry (Kruskal-Wallis multiple comparisons across species: dark [gray bars] H[8] = 15.27, p = 0.033) (Figure 3B). However, naturally blind Pachón and Tinaja cavefish showed no evidence of visually induced motor asymmetry (one-sample Wilcoxon to 0.5, Pachón, p = 0.970; Tinaja, p = 0.112) (Figure 3B). Conversely, comparing only the sighted species, a strong motor asymmetry was observed to the loss of environmental illumination (dark search match index, one-sample Wilcoxon signed rank test to 0.5: D. rerio: 0.700 ± 0.035, p < 0.0001; P. promelas: 0.656 ± 0.037, p < 0.0001; D. cerebrum: 0.697 ± 0.044, p = 0.0001; C. variegatus: 0.646 ± 0.031, p < 0.0001; O. latipes: 0.648 ± 0.038, p = 0.0007; A. mexicanus surface fish: 0.704 ± 0.044, p < 0.0001) (Figure 3B). Moreover, across sighted species no significant difference was observed during baseline illumination (Kruskal-Wallis, H[6] = 5.617, p = 0.3453) or following the loss of light (Kruskal-Wallis, H[6] = 3.146, p = 0.6775). These patterns are highly similar to the well-established patterns in zebrafish where baseline movement is random, and motor asymmetry is observed during light search. A similar trend was also observed across all zebrafish genetic strains tested (Figure S2J). Furthermore, at the population level, all sighted species showed an equal distribution of left and right turn bias individuals (D. rerio: χ2[1] = 0.20, p = 0.65; P. promelas: χ2[1] = 1.05, p = 0.31; D. cerebrum: χ2[1] = 0.23, p = 0.63; C. variegatus: χ2[1] = 0.80, p = 0.37; O. latipes: χ2[1] = 1.47, p = 0.23; A. mexicanus surface fish: χ2[1] = 0.56, p = 0.46) (Figure 3C), suggesting an equal percentage of fish exhibiting left or right motor asymmetry, consistent with larval zebrafish motor asymmetry during loss of illumination.

Since metrics like match index represent left or right motor asymmetry as a binary output, we next wanted to evaluate if leftward and rightward motor asymmetry was equivalent, like in larval zebrafish,24,29 or if some species had a stronger trend for a single direction. Across species, individuals were sorted as left or right based on performance of the first dark trial, and the three remaining dark trials were averaged as well as the equivalent three baseline trials. In all sighted species, but not cavefish, left and right groups exhibited robust visuomotor asymmetry (two-tailed t test: Pachón, p > 0.9999; Tinaja, p > 0.9999) (Figure 3D). While there was no statistical difference between left and right sorted cavefish, a leftward trend in turning is present (Figure 3D). This trend in was not stimulus locked, being observed during both baseline and dark trials, suggesting no correlation to light-induced motor bias. By reversing the sign of left-group individuals, we could directly compare directional strength. In all species, individuals expressing leftward or rightward visually induced motor asymmetry exhibited similar directional performance (Figure S3C). Altogether, our data suggest genetic variation drives differences in behavioral performance, yet visuomotor asymmetry is a generalizable feature to sighted larval teleosts and dependent on visual evolution.

Discussion

Lateralization in the brain is proposed to improve function and behavioral performance in humans as well as other animal species,4,5,6,60,61,62 indicating a property that would ideally be conserved and selected for during evolution. We show that a specific form of functional lateralization, light-induced motor asymmetry, is broadly conserved in teleosts. Visuomotor asymmetry underlies a zebrafish search pattern behavior, and search strategies are necessary to identify food, shelter, and mates across different species.30,31,63 Such strategies are likely to emerge in evolution as organisms develop sensory systems to assess their environments. Despite the importance of such behaviors for survival and the overall prevalence of behavioral asymmetries, there is still much not known about the neural mechanisms, behavioral implications, and evolutionary forces underlying lateralization and hemispheric specializations. While we demonstrate here that multiple characteristics consistent with light-search behavior in zebrafish are present in the motor asymmetry of other species, no comprehensive analysis for light search was conducted. Considering this, we acknowledge other species following the loss of illumination may not be performing a directed light search. However, we also posit the responses are inconsistent with other previously described light-evoked behaviors like visual startle or loom-induced startle that occur within a second or less64,65 Conversely, the motor patterns described in this study occur over tens of seconds consistent with an evoked behavioral state response such as a search state.28,66 Therefore, our work establishes that motor asymmetry associated with zebrafish light-search is a tractable behavioral asymmetry that is widely conserved in teleost evolution. Moreover, our work shows that larval teleosts provide a laboratory-tractable model and tools for understanding the evolution of brain lateralization.

Diverse species show visual asymmetry. One well characterized example is the eye preference asymmetry in chicken and pigeon, where each eye is preferentially used for distinct tests.7,67 For this avian visual asymmetry, a prominent determinant is the differential visual experience between the eyes during early development.13 Interestingly, our prior work demonstrated that the leftward or rightward turning direction of zebrafish visually mediated motor asymmetry as described here, which is also instructed by early visual experience during a defined developmental critical period, similar to critical period plasticity observed in other species including humans.25,68,69 In avian species, these light experience-dependent changes in eye specialization are dependent on the thalamofugal pathway, a path that traverses from the retina, thalamus to the visual forebrain.70,71,72 In zebrafish, developmental visual experience is encoded in the thalamus and causes functional asymmetries correlated with motor asymmetry direction.25 Collectively, these findings suggest the thalamus may be a more broadly evolutionarily conserved substrate for visually mediated asymmetries. In fish, diverse behavioral asymmetries have been described that have been previously discussed in detail.73 However, one visual asymmetry to highlight here is detour behavior, where one eye is preferentially used for certain visual tasks.74,75 Detour behavior is observed in diverse fish species, yet under most conditions is observed as a consistent population- or sex-specific right- or left-eye preference.76,77,78 In zebrafish, we have shown asymmetric visual experience can dictate motor asymmetry direction, yet without methods that actively control visual experience the population shows equal proportions of individuals with leftward or rightward motor asymmetry. Since detour visual biases appear more locked at the population level, they suggest a distinct neural mechanism. This further insinuates that during evolution diverse mechanisms arose to instruct a variety of visuomotor-associated asymmetries. In sighted fish, search-pattern behavior allows larvae to rapidly identify and navigate to illuminated areas.28 The ability to rapidly locate light is likely beneficial as prey capture is heavily dependent on vision.79,80 We posit the incorporation of motor asymmetry as a motor feature of search likely provides some benefit through improved rates of responsiveness, functional performance, or energy efficiency. In other animals, behavioral lateralization has been associated with improved problem-solving skills, visual discrimination, and social behavior performance.4,5,7 Regardless of the specific benefit, our work showing the broad conservation of visually induced motor asymmetry argues for a fitness benefit that ensured motor asymmetry was retained across phylogenetic orders and diverse environments. This observation predicts that a fish that cannot utilize vision would lose or change their responsiveness to light. Indeed, all associated retinal structures have been lost in Astyanax cavefish.81,82,83,84 Consistent with this idea, we observed a loss-of-light search behavior and associated motor asymmetry from two independent populations of cavefish. Such a loss of vision in cavefish eliminated energy-costly structures and processing, which improved fitness in a challenging environment with limited nutrient sources.85 Moreover, corresponding behaviors were likely lost as a result. Interestingly, cavefish have retained some photo-mediated behaviors including diurnal changes, shadow-responses, and activity within the primary teleost visual processing center—the optic tectum.52,53,86 This suggests that the visually mediated motor asymmetry may have been actively selected against in Astyanax adapted to cave environments.

One hypothesis to explain the lack of motor asymmetry in cavefish, despite conservation of some photo-mediated behavior,53,87 is a general loss of lateralization. In vertebrates, conserved molecular signaling pathways are responsible for asymmetric left-right body patterning.88 For example, well-described mechanisms in teleosts impose leftward heart looping and habenula asymmetries.89,90,91,92,93,94 Conversely, cavefish show weaker left-right body patterning and a higher prevalence of reversed or symmetric heart positioning, consistent with relaxed lateralization.95 Our prior work demonstrated no correlation between zebrafish motor asymmetry and markers of left-right body patterning.29 Despite weaker morphological asymmetries in cavefish, they maintain functionally relevant asymmetries related to vibration attraction behavior (VAB).96 VAB underlies the prey capture mechanism for cavefish and uses vibration versus vision to identify prey, which is well-suited to navigate and forage in a cave ecosystem.97,98 Therefore, forms of lateralization can be observed in cavefish. For crucial behaviors, in this case capturing foods, may actively retain asymmetries during evolution: a behavioral asymmetry to stay in light to improve predatory success or VAB system structure to capture food in the absence of light. These ideas suggest the active selection of asymmetry during evolution that supports the performance of critical behaviors. Our work demonstrates that teleosts are well-positioned to address fundamental questions about how asymmetry is regulated during evolution to modulate behavior across diverse environments.

Limitations of the study

In this study, we utilize larval zebrafish light-search behavior to analyze differences in motor performance and behavioral lateralization across teleost species. However, in this work, we do not test the various species to discern if this behavior is utilized as a light search. Verifying light search and conducting other behavioral tests would give a more holistic view of how motor behavior differs between species.

Experimental model and subject details

Animal husbandry

All experiments were approved by the West Virginia University Animal Care and Use Committee. All comparative zebrafish (Danio rerio) experiments were conducted utilizing the Tübingen long-fin (TL) strain. All zebrafish strains: Tüpfel Longfin (TL), Tübingen (Tü), AB, EkkWill (EKW), Singapore (Sing), and Wild Indian Kolkata (WIK), were raised at 28°C on a 14/10-h light-dark cycle with a 75 μW/cm2 intensity light. Individuals were kept in standard E3 media (5 mM NaCl, 0.17 mM KCl, 0.33 mM CaCl2, 0.33 mM MgSO4-7H2O, and 1 mM HEPES buffer pH 7.5) at a density of 40 embryos per 30 mL of E3. All experiments were conducted within the first 7 days post fertilization (dpf). Species: Pimephales promelas, Cyprinodon variegatus, Oryzias latipes, Danionella cerebrum (Douglass lab, University of Utah), and Astyanax mexicanus (Duboue lab, Florida Atlantic University) were received at 1–2 dpf. P. promelas, C. variegatus, and O. latipes were all purchased from Aquatic Research Organisms (Hampton, NH). Larvae were assessed upon arrival, and any dead, deformed, or underdeveloped individuals were removed. D. cerebrum, fathead minnow (P. promelas), and Mexican tetras (A. mexicanus) were raised using the standard procedure stated above until 5, 7, and 6 dpf respectively. Sheepshead pupfish (C. variegatus) were raised in 5 parts per thousand (ppt) salt solution at 27°C with standard light-dark cycle until 7dpf. Medaka (O. latipes) were raised at 25°C under standard conditions until 14–16 dpf.

Method details

Behavior tracking and analysis

Tracking was conducted at the following ages: 4–5 dpf (D. cerebrum), 5–6 dpf (A. mexicanus), 6–7 dpf (Danio rerio, P. promelas, C. variegatus), and 12–14 dpf (Oryzias latipes) unless otherwise stated. Individuals were not separated by sex prior to testing, as sex is not characterizable at the developmental stages tested. Larvae were tracked in a multiplexed arena made up of four 9 cm dishes. Larvae were tracked under infrared illumination (940 nm, CMVision Supplies) using a μEye IDS1545LM-M CMOS camera (1st Vision) and 19 mm lens (Thorlabs, MVL12WA) equipped with a 780 nm long-pass filter (Thorlabs, FGL780). Environmental illumination was supplied by a cool white LED (Thorlabs, MCWHL5) tuned to ∼55 μW/cm2 recorded at the stage level using an optometer (International Light Technologies, ILT2400). Behavior was recorded between 26°C and 28°C during the day light cycle at least 2 h following lights coming on and 2 h before lights turning off. Larvae were tracked using a custom DAQtimer script operating through IDL to control the camera, lights, and tracking parameters. Tracking parameters consisted of four repeated 30 s tracking periods at 10 fps in baseline illumination and light off stimulus separated by 150 s of reacclimating to baseline illumination. Tracking parameters for comparison between cave and surface variants of A. mexicanus were conducted as stated above with an additional 30 s tracking period of baseline illumination following light off stimulus. We analyzed these trajectories using 6 measures: Net turn angle (NTA), match index (MI), absolute NTA, fractal dimension (FD), total turning angle (TTA), and displacement. Trials with less than 10 s of points collected during a tracking period were excluded. Individuals missing three datapoints during baseline or dark tracking were excluded. NTA is the summation of all trajectory changes during the tracking period with leftward and rightward movement having (−) or (+) sign, respectively. For L/R NTA comparison, individuals were sorted L/R based on first dark trial direction with averages representing the average NTA during the last three light or dark trials. MI is the proportion of trials that match the direction of the first trial. TTA is the summation of the magnitude for all trajectory changes. Fractal dimension is a measure used to calculate the complexity of a trajectory, where 1 is a straight line and 2 is a line completely filling 2D space. Displacement shows the distance between the first and final points of a trial. Fold change shows the difference between light and dark trials for each individual, transformed with a log10 conversion to reduce variance.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in Prism (Graphpad) and RStudio (R Core Team). Data presented in figures represent means ± SEM. Non-normally distributed data was analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test, adjusted with the Dunn’s correction, for factorial comparisons, or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for one- and two-way comparisons. Normally distributed data was compared using either 1- or 2-way t-tests. For multiple comparisons, ANOVAs were performed and adjusted with a Bonferroni correction. Chi-square tests compared to an expected proportion of 50:50 was used for comparisons of left-right distribution. Tree design and analysis was composed utilizing the public database for evolutionary timescale, TimeTree 5.99 Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted in R using the base R stat package “prcomp” function, Rstudio version 2025.05.1 + 513, and the libraries: tidyverse, readxl, corrplot, FactoMineR, factoextra, corr, and ggcorrplot. To reduce variance, the log10-fold changes for each metric of fractal dimension, distance, and total turn angle for PCA. “FactoMineR” was utilized for calculating eigenvalues and variance.
