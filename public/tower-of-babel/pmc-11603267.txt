Exploring molecular mechanisms of postoperative delirium through multi-omics strategies in plasma exosomes
Authors: Fuhui Yan, Bowang Chen, Zhen Ma, Qirong Chen, Zhi Jin, Yujie Wang, Feng Qu, Qiang Meng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrently, the diagnosis of delirium is solely based on clinical observation, lacking objective diagnostic tools, and the regulatory networks and pathological mechanisms behind it are not yet fully understood. Exosomes have garnered considerable interest as potential biomarkers for a variety of illnesses. This research aimed to delineate both the proteomic and metabolomic landscapes inherent to exosomes, assessing their diagnostic utility in postoperative delirium (POD) and understanding the underlying pathophysiological frameworks. Integrated analyses of proteomics and metabolomics were conducted on exosomes derived from plasma of individuals from both the non-postoperative delirium (NPOD) control group and the POD group. Subsequently, the study utilized the Connectivity Map (CMap) methodology for the identification of promising small-molecule drugs and carried out molecular docking assessments to explore the binding affinities with the enzyme MMP9 of these identified molecules. We identified significant differences in exosomal metabolites and proteins between the POD and control groups, highlighting pathways related to neuroinflammation and blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity. Our CMap analysis identified potential small-molecule therapeutics, and molecular docking studies revealed two compounds with high affinity to MMP9, suggesting a new therapeutic avenue for POD. This study highlights MMP9, TLR2, ICAM1, S100B, and glutamate as key biomarkers in the pathophysiology of POD, emphasizing the roles of neuroinflammation and BBB integrity. Notably, molecular docking suggests mirin and orantinib as potential inhibitors targeting MMP9, providing new therapeutic avenues. The findings broaden our understanding of POD mechanisms and suggest targeted strategies for its management, reinforcing the importance of multidimensional biomarker analysis and molecular targeting in POD intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available 10.1038/s41598-024-80865-6.Keywords: Postoperative delirium (POD), Exosome, Proteomics, Metabolomics, Biomarker, Neuroinflammation, Blood-brain barrier (BBB)Subject terms: Blood-brain barrier, Cognitive neuroscience, Biomarkers, Cardiology

Introduction

Delirium is a neurobehavioral syndrome that arises from the dysregulation of neuronal activity due to systemic disturbances1. This condition is characterized by altered attention and awareness that deviate significantly from the patient’s baseline cognitive state. These changes occur rapidly and underscore the acute nature of delirium2. Clinically, it presents as a mental syndrome, either acute or subacute in nature, presenting with diminished attention (impaired ability to direct, focus, sustain, and shift attention), reduced awareness (including diminished orientation to the environment), and cognitive impairments (notably memory deficits and disorientation). Additionally, disturbances in language, visuospatial abilities, and perception have been observed. These symptoms are distinct from those attributable to preexisting, established, or evolving neurocognitive disorders2,3.

In the clinical context, 10–46% of patients who undergo general surgical procedures experience POD. This rate increases to 50–67% in patients undergoing cardiac surgery, with specific types of heart surgeries having an incidence as high as 72%4. Increasingly, research indicates that advanced age, pre-existing cognitive deficits, specific medications administered during the perioperative period, and pre-operative conditions such as anemia, electrolyte imbalances, dehydration, and undernutrition are all risk factors for POD5,6. Although POD is a predominant complication in older adults7, delirium’s precise causal mechanisms are still unknown. Numerous hypotheses have been suggested to elucidate the pathophysiological aspects, including a deficit in acute central cholinergic activity, a downturn in GABAergic functioning, irregularities in melatonin and serotonin pathways, an increase in noradrenergic activity, neuroinflammation causing harm to neurons, and reduced cerebral blood flow. Recent studies suggest that neuroinflammation may be involved in this mechanism8,9. Peripheral insults such as infection, surgical procedures, and fractures can trigger neuroinflammation as a response10.

The pathogenesis of POD may involve systemic inflammation and disruption of BBB11. There is a growing consensus that these two processes are interconnected12. Zheng et al. argued that BBB disruption and neuroinflammation triggered via surgery and anesthesia are pivotal in the development of POD13,14. Understanding these mechanisms, particularly the BBB damage and neuroinflammation, is crucial for the prevention of delirium14. BBB deterioration or dysfunction is associated with multiple neurologic conditions including Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias15. Numerous experimental studies, predominantly in animal models, have demonstrated the association between BBB dysfunction and neuroinflammation in the context of POD, though clinical data remain limited16,17. Strategies that alleviate neuroinflammation and restore BBB integrity, particularly through the suppression of certain molecular pathways, have been associated with improved neurocognitive outcomes18,19.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) have been identified as promising biomarkers for various neurodegenerative disorders including Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and multiple sclerosis20. The scope of studies exploring the physiological and pathological roles of EVs in neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, Huntington’s disease, and Parkinson’s disease has been expanding21. A growing body of evidence suggests that EVs play a critical role in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases. For instance, previous research has demonstrated that releasing α-synuclein from plasma-derived exosomes may lead to postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD) in patients with geriatric hip fracture22. Numerous studies have revealed that a specific circular RNA (circRNA) in EVs is a candidate biomarker of POCD23–25, highlighting the importance of EVs in this context. In contrast, alterations in the microenvironment of the nervous system influence neural cell exosome secretion, which contains significant components such as miRNAs and proteins crucial for epigenetics25. Therefore, alterations in protein or RNA expression within EVs can serve as prognostic indicators of neurological diseases. Furthermore, it has also been confirmed that EVs can pass unimpeded into the circulatory system via the BBB, enabling an indirect assessment of neural activity by analyzing EV miRNA or proteins in the blood23. The proteomic analysis of plasma EV proteins may result in the unveiling of novel predictive biomarkers. These biomarkers, which are often challenging to identify in the bloodstream owing to their scarce presence, hold promise for the early detection of neurological diseases. However, further research is being conducted in the expanding field of extracellular vesicles’ potential significance in the pathogenesis of neurological disorders.

The complexity of POD remains a significant challenge in clinical research. Despite increased research efforts, the regulatory networks and pathological mechanisms underlying POD are not yet fully comprehended. Recently, the swift advancement in post-genomic methodologies has led to the widespread application of multi-omics data integration for unraveling the intricacies of complex diseases26. Given that biological systems are interdependent and interconnected, it is necessary to collectively consider multiple omics datasets for a comprehensive analysis. In this study, we conducted comprehensive proteomic and metabolomic analyses of EVs to aim to unearth potential biomarkers and key biological pathways associated with POD. This holistic approach not only broadens our understanding of the pathophysiology behind POD but also opens new avenues for clinical diagnosis and treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Ethics

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Jining First People’s Hospital (2022 Ethics Review No. (087)) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study has been registered with the Clinical Trial Registration (registration number NCT06194474). Proteomic and metabolomic examinations were conducted on plasma specimens, adhering strictly to applicable guidelines and regulations. Prior to inclusion, written informed consent was secured from each participant. The recruitment period for this study began on October 15, 2022, and ended in May 2023. Each participant provided written informed consent, including signature documentation.

Patients

This research included twenty-four individuals undergoing cardiac surgery involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) and coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) at Jining First People’s Hospital from January 2023 through May 2023. The inclusion criteria were as follows: aged 55–80 years, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status II-III, New York Heart Association grade II–III, underwent a planned cardiac surgery, hospitalization of 5 days or longer, and had an anesthesia time of at least 2 h. All participants demonstrated normal auditory perception, literacy, and engagement in the cognitive tests. The exclusion criteria were as follows: Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score below 25 before surgery, presence of dementia, any history of neurological or psychiatric disorders, a score above eight on the hospital’s anxiety depression scale (HADS)27, current use of sedatives or antidepressants, dependency on drugs or alcohol, or liver or kidney impairments. Additionally, candidates with a history of cardiac surgery, those admitted to the intensive care unit for more than 2 days in the month before the latest surgery, and those unable to participate in research activities were excluded. The primary objective of this study was to detect POD after cardiac surgery and perform early-stage metabolomic and proteomic analyses of plasma exosomes. The secondary objectives included assessing the risk factors for POD and comparing the postsurgical complications.

Plasma sample collection

Post a fasting duration of 12 h, morning blood collections were performed. For this procedure, 10 ml of whole blood was drawn from each participant’s peripheral vein into ethylenediaminetetraacetic-acid-containing glass tubes. This was followed by centrifugation within 2 h of collection to obtain platelet-free plasma. The plasma was centrifuged twice at 2500×g for 15 min at ambient temperature, and the supernatant was carefully extracted up to 1 cm above the buffy coat layer after each spin. Right after collection, the samples underwent immediate flash-freezing using liquid nitrogen and were then conserved at − 80 °C for analyses that would follow.

Isolation and determination of plasma exosome characteristics

EVs were isolated using the EV-trap method, an affinity technology developed by Tymora Analytical (Lafayette, IN, USA) that does not rely on antibodies. We employed the E-Vtrap method for exosome isolation due to its affinity-based capture, which offers enhanced purity and yield compared to traditional ultracentrifugation and because it avoids potential biases introduced by antibody-based selection. Frozen serum was thawed, and larger debris was removed by centrifugation at 2500 × g for 10 min. The clarified plasma was then diluted in phosphate-buffered saline (20-fold) and mixed with the EV-Trap beads for 60 min. The beads were then separated using a magnetic rack, washed in phosphate-buffered saline, and eluted with 200 mM triethylamine (Millipore Sigma) for 10 min. The eluted EV samples were dried in a vacuum centrifuge.

Western blot analysis

Western blot analysis of exosome marker protein concentrations in the samples was performed using a Bicinchoninic Acid assay. In this protocol, we separated 10 µg of protein per sample utilizing 12% SDS-PAGE. This was followed by protein transfer onto 0.45 μm PVDF membranes. Blocking was achieved with 5% bovine serum albumin for one hour at room temperature to block unspecific sites. Specific antibodies were employed at the mentioned concentrations for the study : anti-TSG101 antibody (1:1000) (No. ab125011; Proteintech Group, Abcam), anti-CD9 mouse polyclonal antibody (1:3000) (Cat No. EV50-02-01, Proteintech Group, EVLiXiR), anti-CD81 mouse polyclonal antibody (1:1000) (Cat EV50-02-01, Proteintech Group, EVLiXiR), and anti-HSP70 (1:1000) (Cat No. 66183-1-Ig, Proteintech Group, Proteintech). Subsequent to washing, the membranes were treated with a peroxidase-bound secondary antibody (dilution 1:5000), and allowed to sit for one hour at ambient temperature. After three washes with tris-buffered saline with polysorbate 20, an Odyssey Fc imager (LI COR) was used. Densitometric quantification of the bands was carried out using computer-aided image processing software.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis and transmission electron microscopy

A NanoFCM instrument (N30E; DKSH, USA) was used to measure plasma EV concentrations and dimensions using nanoparticle tracking analysis. An HT-7700 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) operating at an acceleration voltage of 100 kV was used for transmission electron microscopy analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Proteomic analysis

Nanoparticle tracking analysis and transmission electron microscopy

The most abundant proteins in the plasma pool were isolated using the Human 14/Mouse 3 Multiple Affinity Removal Column and lysed with SDS-dithiothreitol-tris buffer (4% SDS, 100 mM dithiothreitol, 150 mM Tris-HCl; pH 8.0). Proteins were extracted and quantified using a bicinchoninic acid assay kit (Bio-Rad, USA). Using the filter-aided sample preparation strategy, proteins were enzymatically digested using trypsin provided by Promega. Subsequent steps included peptide desalting utilizing a C18 column (EmporeTM SPE Cartridges, C18 [standard density], 7 mm inner diameter, 3 ml volume, produced by Sigma). The peptides were then dried via vacuum centrifugation and rehydrated in 40 µL of 0.1% (v/v) formic acid.

Fractionation and liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry

Peptide isolation focused on low-abundance components of plasma specimens. The digested peptides were fractionated into 10 segments using a high pH reversed-phase peptide fractionation kit (Thermo Scientific Pierce). Each segment then underwent vacuum concentration and reconstitution in 0.1% (v/v) formic acid (15 µl). These peptides, at a concentration of 1.5 µg each, were introduced onto a Thermo Scientific EASY-Spray™ C18 trap column (part number 164946, 3 μm, 75 μm × 2 cm) and subsequently separated on an EASY-Spray™ C18 LC analytical column (ES802, 2 μm, 75 μm × 25 cm) over a 120-min period using a linear gradient of buffer B (84% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid) at a flow rate of 250 nl/min. The Q Exactive™ mass spectrometer from Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. (Waltham, MA, USA) was used in conjunction with the Easy-nLC™ system to perform the analysis.

Data processing and analysis

The analysis of the data was conducted through the (Spectronaut™ 14.4.200727.47784), and DIA data were imported into Spectronaut Pulsar X for protein identification and quantification. A t-test was used to calculate fold changes > 1.5 or < 0.67 with p < 0.05 to screen for proteins differentially expressed in the two groups. The CELLO platform was used to predict the subcellular locations of various proteins. InterPro Scan was used to ascertain the characteristics of protein structural domains. Enrichment of these protein domains was evaluated using Fisher’s exact test with a false discovery rate of less than 0.05. The annotation of Gene Ontology (GO) was performed using the Blast2Go suite (https://www.blast2go.com/), a tool designed for high-throughput functional annotation and data mining. Furthermore, the KEGG pathway database played a crucial role in annotating the proteins, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05 for noteworthy enrichment28,29. KEGG pathway enrichment was performed using the ClueGO plugin within Cytoscape software (version 3.6.1)30. This plugin integrates KEGG data for functional annotation and visualization, ensuring that no direct download or redistribution of KEGG data was involved. The STRING database was used to explore protein-protein interactions (PPIs), specifically targeting interactions with a confidence level of 0.9 or higher. These interactions were integrated and visualized in a network using Cytoscape version 3.6.1. The MCODE plugin for Cytoscape facilitated the analysis of modules within this PPI network, focusing on significant modules comprising more than five nodes with scores exceeding 4. Lastly, clusterProfiler version 3.8.1 in R was used to investigate KEGG pathways that were prominently enriched in proteins derived from these key modules.

Untargeted metabolomic analysis

Metabolite extraction

Following a gradual thaw at 4 °C, a measured quantity of the sample was extracted and introduced into a pre-cooled methanol/acetonitrile/water solution (2:2:1, v/v). The mixture was vortexed, ultrasonicated at low temperature for 30 min, left to stand at − 20 °C for 10 min, and then centrifuged at 14,000g at 4 °C for 20 min. For the purpose of mass spectrometry, the supernatant underwent vacuum drying and was then reconstituted in a 100 µL mixture of acetonitrile and water (in a 1:1 ratio by volume). Subsequently, this solution was subjected to vortexing and centrifugation at 14,000g, then left at a temperature of 4 °C for 15 min. The resulting supernatant was then subjected to injection analysis using ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UHPLC/Q-TOF-MS).

UHPLC-Q-TOF MS

Sample separation was achieved using the Vanquish LC UHPLC system equipped with an HILIC column with the autosampler temperature held at 4 °C and the column at 25 °C. In the chromatographic procedure, the mobile phase was formed of solution A (containing water, 25 mM ammonium acetate, and ammonia) and solution B (composed of acetonitrile). The elution began with a phase of 95% solution B for the initial half-minute, transitioning linearly from 95 to 65% B from the half-minute mark to 7 min, then dropping to 40% B over the next minute, staying constant at 40% B for one minute, before swiftly shifting back to 95% B in a span of 0.1 min, and finally stabilizing at 95% B for the remainder of the run until 12 min. An AB SCIEX Triple TOF 6600 mass spectrometer was employed for mass spectrometry, applying electrospray ionization techniques in both the positive and negative ion modes. Electrospray ionization source parameters included ion source gases 1 and 2 at 60 psi, curtain gas at 30 psi, and an ion source temperature of 600 °C.

Data processing and analysis

Raw data underwent conversion to mass spectrometry data format (.mzXML) using ProteoWizard, followed by peak alignment, retention time correction, and peak area extraction using XCMS software (version 3.6.1). The XCMS data were first subjected to metabolite structural identification (using MetaboAnalyst 4.0) and preprocessing. Subsequently, the experimental data and its analysis underwent a quality evaluation. Multivariate statistical analysis was performed using the ROPLS package (version 1.6.2) in R. In the PCA, the autoscaling method was used for data scaling. In this study, two components were chosen for the partial least squares discriminant analysis, while the orthogonal variant, orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis, incorporated one predictive and one orthogonal component. These settings were selected to maximize the explanatory power and predictive performance of the models.

Connectivity map analysis

Connectivity Map (CMap, https://portals.broadinstitute.org/cmap/) is an online analytical platform that uses whole-genome transcriptional expression data to explore the mechanisms and reuse of small molecules. The CMap database includes over 7000 expression profiles for 1309 different compounds. Utilizing its pattern matching algorithm, CMap is employed here to filter potential small-molecule medications for POD, adopting the following screening criteria: Tau-Score from − 90 to -100 and a P-value < 0.05 as the threshold. Structural information on the analyzed pharmaceuticals was sourced from the PubChem online resource (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/).

Construction of PPI networks and screening of key targets

In order to identify crucial targets for POD, genes were associated with the STRING database, accessible at https://string-db.org/, which catalogs both existing and hypothetical PPIs. A PPI network was then established, necessitating a combined interaction score exceeding 0.4. Network topology was analyzed utilizing the CytoNCA tool within the Cytoscape 3.7.2 software, from which key topological metrics of the PPI network were derived. This study utilized algorithms including MNC, Degree, and EPC found in the Cytohubba plugin to pinpoint key targets.

Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation

Molecular optimization was performed using SYBYL-X 2.0 software with the following parameters: the Tripos force field was applied, Gasteiger-Hückel charges were assigned, the maximum number of iterations was set to 10,000, and the energy gradient was limited to 0.005 kcal/(mol·A). All unspecified parameters were kept at their default settings31,32. The 3D structures of key targets were obtained from the RCSB database (http://www.rcsb.org/) (Supplementary Table 1.5). Receptor preparation, including the removal of water molecules and metal ions, was carried out using mgltools_win32_1.5.6, and the receptor was saved in PDBQT format33. AutoDock Vina 1.1.2 (http://vina.scripps.edu/) was employed to evaluate the binding affinities between the ligands and the target proteins34. The final visualization of molecular docking data was completed using Discovery Studio35. Molecular simulations were performed using Amber 24 (San Francisco, CA, USA)36.The ff19SB force field37 was used to calculate the system’s force field parameters.Solvation was carried out using the TIP3P water model, and counterions were added to neutralize the system.Once the system’s energy was minimized, it was heated from 0 K to 310.15 K within 500 ps.The system was constrained within a canonical ensemble and pre-equilibrated at 310.15 K.Finally, a 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation was performed in the isothermal-isobaric ensemble, with periodic boundary conditions applied.All covalent bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the SHAKE method.The analysis of the dynamical results was performed using AmberTools2338,39.

Results

Differential expression of plasma exosomal metabolites

In the present study, we performed metabolite profiling on plasma samples. Following the preprocessing of the data and normalization of the batches, we identified 9134 precursor molecules in positive ion mode and 9,831 in negative ion mode. PCA highlighted the high reproducibility of quality control samples, indicating minimal analytical system variation. Notably, approximately 70% of the characteristic peaks in the quality control samples showed a relative standard deviation of less than 30%, confirming the reliability of the data for further analysis.

In our comprehensive analysis, the PCA score plots for both ionization modes (positive and negative) displayed a clear pattern; samples from the POD and control groups exhibited internal clustering (Supplementary Fig. 2). This observation implies a similar metabolic composition and concentration within each group, shedding light on the underlying biochemical processes. Furthermore, the partial least squares discriminant analysis results demonstrated a complete separation of the samples between the two groups, highlighting significant differences in their metabolite profiles. Additionally, a permutation test conducted on the partial least squares discriminant analysis model confirmed the absence of overfitting. In terms of specific metabolite alterations, 16 metabolites were upregulated and 26 were downregulated in positive ion mode. Similarly, in negative ion mode, eight metabolites were upregulated and 16 were downregulated in the POD group (Supplementary Fig. 3). These findings underscore the substantial changes in the metabolic profiles of patients who experience delirium after major cardiac surgery, indicating potential metabolic pathways implicated in the development of POD.

We successfully identified 229 metabolites by cross-referencing established databases of known metabolites. This set comprised 170 metabolites identified in positive-ion mode and 59 in negative-ion mode (Supplementary Table 3.1-3.2). Analysis of the 1,362 metabolites revealed significant differences in their profiles, which were instrumental in distinguishing the two groups. Further investigation of the metabolic pathways enriched by these metabolites led to the identification of the top 20 most significantly enriched KEGG pathways (Supplementary Fig. 3). Notably, these pathways include arginine biosynthesis, amino acid biosynthesis, and arginine and proline metabolism. Among the enriched metabolic pathways, key metabolites such as citrate, glutamic acid, pyruvate, vitamin C, leukotriene F4, and arginine were identified, providing valuable insights into the biochemical underpinnings of the studied sample groups and laying a foundation for further exploration of their metabolic distinctions and potential implications.

Exosomal proteins are dysregulated in POD

DEPs were distinguished among various groups in the discovery cohort using liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry. A significant alteration was marked by either a log2 (fold change) > 1.5 or < -0.67, coupled with a p-value of < 0.05. Hierarchical clustering heatmap demonstrates the differential protein expression between postoperative delirium patients (Group A) and controls (Group B) (Fig. 1a). proteins identified in the plasma exosomes. Among these, 2,980 overlapped with the ExoCarta database (Fig. 1B). Heatmaps revealed 445 upregulated and 12 downregulated DEPs in the purified exosomes derived from patients with POD (Fig. 1C), and approximately 30% of these proteins were extracellular (Fig. 1D). GO enrichment analysis provided deeper insight into the biological significance of these differentially expressed proteins, as detailed in Fig. 1E and Supplementary_table4.3. The differential expression of proteins is further highlighted in the volcano plot shown in Fig. 1G, illustrating the upregulated and downregulated proteins. Analysis of the differential protein pathway/metabolic pathway annotation and attribution revealed a primary association with the immune response (Fig. 1F). Key pathways identified through the KEGG pathway annotation analysis included ECM-receptor interactions, cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions, leukocyte transendothelial migration, neutrophil extracellular trap formation, and adherens junctions (Fig. 1F and Supplementary_table4.3). The combined results of GO and KEGG analyses suggest the mechanisms underlying neurocognitive dysfunction in patients after cardiac surgery are primarily influenced by inflammatory processes and multicellular communication. There may also be a correlation between cell junctions and endothelial disorders.

Biomarker candidates for POD

MOGONET uses a sophisticated deep multi-view learning framework to integrate diverse omics data, such as genomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, for enhanced biomedical classification. This method excels at deciphering complex biological interactions and offers significant potential for personalized medicine and biomarker discovery. Compared with existing methods such as k-nearest neighbors, support vector machine, Lasso, GRridge, and radio frequency, MOGONET exhibits superior performance and interpretability40. Using MOGONET for feature selection, we identified specific protein regulation patterns that facilitated the diagnostic classification of cognitive impairment. Following the method described in the “Methods” section of MOGONET, we incorporated all differential entities from the proteomics and metabolomics analyses. This led to the identification of key biomarkers ranked using MOGONET. The top 75 most important biomarkers in this dataset are reported in Supplementary_table 1.2, and the corresponding rankings are shown in the Supplementary Table. By intersecting these data with the top 15 hub proteins and metabolites derived from the PPI (Fig. 2A), MMP9, TLR2, ICAM1, and glutamate were found to be present in both subsets (Supplementary_table 1.3).

The four most interesting discriminatory proteins included MMP9, MPO, TLR2, and ICAM1 (Fig. 2B), with overall performance as follows: area under the curve 0.86 and 95% confidence interval 0.69-1.00. We focused on determining the effectiveness of both metabolite and protein markers in diagnosing POD. Receiver operating curves were generated, and the area under the curve values was determined (Fig. 2C); these were 0.81, 0.85, 0.83, 0.77, 0.81, and 0.74 for MMP9, MPO, S100B, TLR2, ICAM1, and glutamic acid, respectively. We assessed the diagnostic utility of MMP9, MPO, TLR2, ICAM, and glutamic acid at the cutoff values. The Youden index was used to confirm the optimality of the thresholds. In addition, we attempted to differentiate POD from non-POD using a model combining multiple metabolites and proteins, and their respective sensitivities and specificities were compared. In the POD and non-POD cohorts, a combination of four proteins and one metabolite was chosen for logistic regression analysis. This study’s findings showed that the diagnostic capability for POD, as indicated by the area under the curve, was 0.68, referencing Fig. 2D. This level is below that provided by the diagnostic use of an individual protein. In contrast, a model that integrated five different proteins demonstrated improved diagnostic accuracy for POD with an AUC of 0.86, indicating combination of the aforementioned proteins enhanced the diagnostic value of the biomarkers.

Metabolite-protein pathway network

The IMPaLA tool was used to analyze the pathways enriched with both differential metabolites and proteins, revealing 49 significantly enriched KEGG pathways (Fig. 3A). KEGG pathway visualization was also conducted using the ClueGO plugin in Cytoscape software (version 3.6.1), ensuring compliance with KEGG database licensing terms. These pathways encompass ECM-receptor interactions, neutrophil extracellular trap formation, leukocyte transendothelial migration, and various metabolic pathways, including arginine biosynthesis and alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism. The integrated metabolite-protein pathway network, depicted in Fig. 3B, comprised 45 metabolites, 79 proteins, and 44 pathways. Further analysis of this network identified the key hub metabolites and proteins involved in these pathways. The top five nodes in each part of the analysis network included hub metabolites (glutamic acid, pyruvate, citrate, arginine, and vitamin C), hub proteins (MMP9, CD44, VWF, MPO, and TLR2), and five pathways (neutrophil extracellular trap formation, ECM-receptor interaction, neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, Staphylococcus aureus infection, and leukocyte transendothelial migration).

Furthermore, we reconstructed the PPI network containing 270 interacting proteins based on the analysis genes used in the CMap screening. In our PPI network analysis, MMP9 not only exhibited high centrality in the overall network but also maintained a critical position in the sub-network analysis for upregulated and downregulated differential proteins, regardless of whether analyzed by Degree, MNC, or EPC algorithms. This consistency highlights MMP9’s central role in delirium-related biological processes, indicating that it might be a key node connecting multiple biological pathways and molecular events. Therefore, MMP9 is considered to be a key target for the next step of molecular docking.

Protein-metabolism dual mediator analysis

In this analysis, the positive pathway was significant at pMDEI = 0.036, whereas the negative pathway was not significant at pINV. MDEI = 0.288. Additionally, by conducting a Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient analysis on S100B and glutamate, a significant negative correlation was found, with a Spearman r value of -0.52 and a p-value of 0.02. This suggests glutamate has a significant mediating effect on the relationship between S100B and POD, indicating that S100B influences the occurrence of POD via glutamate (Fig. 3C).

Validation of hub proteins using western blotting

To further verify that the four common proteins were altered after surgery and delirium, we measured the expression of the four proteins using western blotting. The results showed that MMP9, TLR2, and S100B levels were increased, while the claudin-5 level was significantly decreased in the disease group compared with the control group (p < 0.05 or p < 0.001; Fig. 4).

Molecular docking

Molecular docking serves as a fast and effective approach for screening compounds, leveraging the calculation of free binding energy to estimate the stability of binding interactions. The top 18 compounds demonstrating favorable binding affinity with the primary target are presented in Suppl. Table 6. Both mirin and orantinib exhibit favorable binding energies of -11.4 kcal/mol and − 11 kcal/mol, respectively, indicating a strong binding affinity for MMP9. In comparison, known MMP9 inhibitors such as Minocycline (− 8.1 kcal/mol)41,42 display higher binding energies, suggesting weaker interactions with MMP9. These results highlight that mirin and orantinib may have a stronger inhibitory potential for MMP9 than these established inhibitors. Additionally, the three-dimensional interaction diagrams of the two most significant compounds with MMP9 are depicted in Fig. 5, illustrating the outcomes of the docking studies. Mirin forms van der Waals forces with amino acid residues around MMP9, hydrogen bonds with GLN227, TYR248, ALA189, and Pi-Pi stacking interactions with MET247, TYR248, etc. Likewise, orantinib establishes van der Waals forces with nearby amino acid residues, creates hydrogen bonds with HIS230, HIS236, HIS226, TYR245, and engages in Pi-alkyl interactions with LEU188, VAL223, LEU22, among others.

Molecular dynamics simulation and free energy calculations

To validate the molecular docking results, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were employed to analyze the MMP9-mirin, MMP9-orantinib and MMP9-Minocycline complexes. The dynamic stability of the complexes was evaluated using root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) analysis, as shown in Fig. 6A. During the molecular dynamics simulations, the MMP9-Mirin and MMP9-Orantinib complexes gradually reached equilibrium after 20 ns, with RMSD values stabilizing at approximately 2.0 Å and 2.2 Å, respectively. In contrast, the RMSD value of the MMP9-Minocycline complex stabilized at around 3.0 Å. These findings suggest that Mirin and Orantinib form more dynamically stable complexes with MMP9 compared to the known MMP9 inhibitor, Minocycline41,42. Additionally, hydrogen bond analysis revealed that all three small molecules established a significant number of interactions with the MMP9 protein. The radius of gyration (Rg) values for the MMP9-Mirin and MMP9-Orantinib complexes remained stable throughout the simulations (Fig. 6B and C), indicating that these complexes maintain a compact and stable structure. Furthermore, the root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) values for residues in the MMP9-Mirin and MMP9-Orantinib complexes were markedly lower than those in the MMP9-Minocycline complex (Fig. 6D), providing further evidence for the enhanced stability of these interactions.

Molecular dynamics simulation of mirin, orantinib, and minocycline with target protein MMP9. (A) RMSD values of the three complexes; (B) number of hydrogen bonds in the three complexes; (C) radius of gyration (Rg) values of the three complexes; (D) root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) values of the three complexes.

Subsequently, the binding free energies of the complexes were calculated using the molecular mechanics/Poisson–Boltzmann surface area (MM/PBSA) method. The binding free energies for the MMP9-Mirin, MMP9-Orantinib, and MMP9-Minocycline complexes were determined to be − 9.901 kJ/mol, − 21.741 kJ/mol, and − 2.358 kJ/mol, respectively. The negative binding free energy values indicate favorable binding interactions between the small molecules and MMP9, with lower values representing stronger binding affinity. These results clearly demonstrate that Mirin and Orantinib possess significantly higher binding affinity for MMP9 compared to the known MMP9 inhibitor Minocycline, highlighting their potential as more effective MMP9 inhibitors.

Discussion

The precise mechanisms underlying delirium remain unclear. Delirium is influenced by various factors, leading to a complex interplay between vulnerability and exposure to harmful substances or triggers. This complexity means that each episode of delirium may have a unique set of contributing factors representing distinct yet comprehensive causal mechanisms that vary with each occurrence. As a result, the pursuit of a single cause or mechanism, the “ultimate common pathway” for delirium, has not provided definitive answers11. Accumulating research indicates that various interacting biological elements play a role in disturbing extensive neuronal networks in the brain, leading to acute cognitive impairment. The multifactorial etiological model of delirium has received substantial validation and enjoys broad acceptance11. Recent research has highlighted a close association between POD and neuroinflammation as well as disruption of the BBB14,43. Dysfunction of the BBB and neuroinflammation are now considered potential mechanisms driving POD14,44,45. The relationship between these two processes is intriguing. For example, sterile surgical trauma triggers homeostatic inflammation within the nervous system. However, detrimental effects may occur when this response is dysregulated. It has been demonstrated that surgical procedures can elevate pro-inflammatory cytokine concentrations in the bloodstream, involving cytokines such as interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha, potentially contributing to the onset of delirium9. These factors are closely associated with neuroinflammation and may be accompanied by a cascade of BBB dysfunction43. In this scenario, the entry of proinflammatory cytokines and macrophages originating from monocytes results in the stimulation of neuroglial cells, encompassing microglia and astrocytes. This interaction between peripheral immune responses and the brain can amplify inflammatory responses within the CNS46. Such cascading effects of neuroinflammation could result in synaptic anomalies and neuronal cell death, consequently deteriorating cognitive abilities43. Therefore, changes in the BBB’s structure and functionality are pivotal for this process.

MMP-9, a key enzyme of the matrix metalloproteinase family, plays a central role in degrading the BBB components such as occludin, claudin-5, and collagen IV47, leading to increased BBB permeability48,49. Abnormal expression is directly associated with BBB breakdown50. This disruption is pivotal, as it allows inflammatory cells and cytokines to enter the brain, thereby intensifying neuroinflammation and contributing to cognitive decline. MMP-9’s involvement extends to several neurodegenerative conditions, emphasizing its potential as a therapeutic target19,51. Specifically, elevated MMP-9 and reduced claudin-5 levels have been identified in various POCD animal models, correlating with BBB disruption and subsequent cognitive decline19,52. The efficacy of MMP-9 inhibition in reversing BBB disruption and improving cognitive functions further highlights its significance19,53. MRI studies have shed light on the association between MMP-9 activity and BBB disruption, reinforcing the critical relationship between MMP-9 levels and BBB integrity54.

Furthermore, Zheng et al. identified a surge in MMP-9 following anesthesia/surgery, correlating with cognitive deficits and decreased claudin-5 and occludin levels55. notably, these effects were reversible by SB-3CT19, highlighting the intricate relationship between MMP-9 activity, BBB integrity, and cognitive function.

The relationship between MMP-9 activity and cognitive outcomes post-surgery suggests a critical link to neuroinflammation and BBB function, positioning MMP-9 as a focal point in POD research. This connection, further evidenced by serum MMP-9 correlations with cognitive impairment levels, offers a significant insight into POD’s pathophysiology and hints at novel interventional strategies56.

The BBB, a specialized construct composed of endothelial cells connected through tight and adherens junctions, integrates with pericytes, the basement membrane, and astrocyte endfeet, forming the neurovascular unit. Claudin-5, a crucial tight junction protein, significantly influences BBB permeability43,57, evidenced by the heightened BBB and endothelial permeability following its downregulation52. Surgical interventions lead to reductions in claudin levels, subsequently increasing BBB permeability to substances such as 10-kDa dextran19,52. Furthermore, MMP-9’s ability to downregulate claudin-5 contributes to BBB disruption55,58, a phenomenon well-documented in our observations and supported by the western blot analysis. Despite known effects, the precise pathways through which MMP9 modulates claudin-5 remain underexplored. However, given MMPs’ role in brain injury recovery and certain disorders, they are posited as potential therapeutic targets19. Specifically, MMP9, likely via the NF-κB pathway, affects claudin-5 and other critical adhesion molecules, compromising the BBB’s integrity and exacerbating neuroinflammation13. Compounding this effect is the infiltration of neutrophils, which release MMP9 along with other inflammatory mediators, contributing to the BBB’s further disruption. This ongoing cycle of barrier compromise and inflammation, signified by increased MMP-9 expression19, underscores delirium’s pathophysiology, suggesting a potential intervention point that might alleviate such conditions.

In this study, despite the absence of standard inflammatory indicators such as C-reactive protein and interleukin-6, we identified key proteins such as MPO, CYBB, ICAM1, and TLR2 through PPI analysis, indicating a strong link to inflammation. Myeloperoxidase (MPO), mainly found in neutrophils59, is known for its role in exacerbating inflammation and tissue damage60. Its high expression and release can significantly impact adjacent tissues and is correlated with the severity of inflammatory conditions61. Neutrophils have recently been recognized for their substantial role in neuroinflammation, affecting CNS immune functions and contributing to the pathogenesis seen in neurological models such as Alzheimer’s disease62,63. ICAM1, critical for white blood cell migration and endothelial cell signaling during inflammation, contributes to neuroinflammatory exacerbation and is associated with BBB disruption64. Furthermore, the TLR2/TLR4 signaling pathway has been identified as a key mechanism in CNS inflammation, with research suggesting TLR2’s involvement in postoperative cognitive dysfunction and neuroinflammation, making it a potential target for reducing POCD65,66.

Plasma S100B is not a stranger to the nervous system; it serves not only as a marker for neuronal injury but also as a biomarker for assessing BBB permeability and damage14,67. In patients with POD, the perioperative cerebrospinal fluid/plasma albumin ratio and plasma S100B levels significantly increased, suggesting perioperative BBB disruption. The severity of POD is correlated with BBB damage14.

The mediation analysis results suggest that S100B may influence the occurrence of POD by affecting glutamate through a certain pathway. Interestingly, the S100B and glutamate levels were significantly negatively correlated. S100B is a calcium-binding protein secreted by astrocytes, known to be involved in glial-neuronal communication, and possesses neurotrophic activities in the extracellular medium68,69. S100B in the extracellular space safeguards hippocampal neurons against excitotoxic injury69. A substantial body of literature describes the effects of S100B on glutamate levels. For instance, Ahlemeyer et al. suggested that extracellular S100B at the molar level protects hippocampal neurons from glutamate-induced damage70. The addition of S100B protects hippocampal neurons from N-methyl-D-aspartate–mediated glutamate toxicity69. Glutamate uptake by astrocytes is positively regulated by extracellular S100B69. The discovery of this pathway indicates its existence. Regarding the onset of delirium, there have been no previous reports of S100B acting through glutamate. Hopefully, further experiments will explore the occurrence and significance of this pathway. Although S100B was not specifically mentioned in the literature, it remained a significantly elevated protein, showing a marked increase relative to the control group. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis indicated it also had good diagnostic efficacy for POD.

Glutamate is the most common free amino acid in the brain. It serves as a key excitatory neurotransmitter and is instrumental in cognitive processes and neurodegenerative diseases. A healthy BBB effectively prevents the excessive exchange of glutamate between the brain tissue and blood71. In a healthy brain, the BBB maintains low levels of glutamate through promotion and active transport systems, effectively transporting glutamate from the brain to the blood72. However, if the BBB is damaged, these systems cannot effectively clear glutamate from the cerebrospinal and extracellular fluid into the blood, nor can they balance its concentration with that in the blood. Therefore, BBB integrity is closely linked to glutamate imbalance73. Given the association between glutamic acid, neurotoxicity, and neurodegeneration74,75 excessive glutamic acid levels have been shown to cause neuronal degeneration and functional impairment, leading to neurotoxicity74. A clinical study of preoperative serum metabolites in older adult patients with hip fractures conducted by Guo et al. showed that glutamic acid levels in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients with POD were significantly higher (fold change POD/non-POD = 4.21, p = 0.01) and were closely related to the occurrence of POD76. The relationships among neuroinflammation, the BBB, glutamate dysfunction, and postoperative cognitive disorders are complex73,77. Surgical injury may initiate a cascade of immune and inflammatory reactions in the periphery, leading to severe peripheral inflammation, which in turn causes neuroinflammation77. Glutamate is essential in the mechanism of long-term potentiation, regarded as crucial for learning and memory processes. Peripheral neuroinflammation may increase the synaptic currents mediated by NMDAR subunit 2 B (NR2B) and NR2B receptors in the anterior cingulate cortex. These inflammatory responses affect the function of glutamate receptors and transport proteins, thereby impairing cognitive function. AMPAR participate in excitotoxicity by activating NMDAR, and the overstimulation of NMDAR or AMPAR may induce neuronal apoptosis77.

Given the critical role of neuroinflammation and BBB integrity in the pathogenesis of postoperative delirium, as demonstrated by our proteomic and metabolomic analyses, therapeutic strategies aiming to mitigate these pathological features hold promise. In this context, the role of specific small molecules in addressing these underlying mechanisms becomes particularly relevant. Specifically, Esmolol, a highly selective β1 receptor blocker, has been found to alleviate lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced hippocampal damage and neuroinflammation, thereby improving cognitive dysfunction78. Similarly, PKCβ-inhibitors have been proven to reduce BBB disruptions and local inflammation in the hypothalamus and hippocampus, decrease neuroinflammation, and alleviate cognitive impairments79. The small molecules identified could potentially be applied in future clinical treatments of POD or might be combined with current medications to improve treatment outcomes for POD.

Furthermore, based on the pathological features of POD, we constructed a PPI network and identified MMP9 as a key target. The molecular docking analysis demonstrated that both Mirin and Orantinib exhibited markedly higher binding affinity toward MMP9 compared to the known MMP9 inhibitor, Minocycline38,39. This was further substantiated through molecular dynamics simulations, which revealed lower RMSD, Rg, and RMSF values, indicating the formation of more stable and compact complexes between Mirin/Orantinib and MMP9. The MM/PBSA binding free energy calculations provided additional quantitative support for these findings, underscoring the strength of these interactions. These comprehensive results suggest that Mirin and Orantinib possess significantly enhanced binding affinity and stability when targeting MMP9, indicating their potential as promising therapeutic agents. Considering MMP9’s key role in the development of postoperative delirium, especially in promoting BBB disruption and neuroinflammation, the discovery of these two compounds offers promising prospects for exploring new treatment pathways. Specifically, orantinib, as a known FGFR inhibitor, several studies have revealed the connection between FGF/FGFR and the regulation of neuroinflammation80,81, its anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects may be related to its inhibition of MMP9 activity. By targeting MMP9, it may be possible to reduce or prevent the severity of postoperative delirium, thus improving patient outcomes. Even so, these potential compounds and their specific molecular mechanisms require further exploration and experimental validation.

Limitations

Our study had some limitations. First, the cohort was small, and the sample size was insufficient to provide robust support for clinical practice. We recruited only 12 patients with POD and 12 patients without POD for proteomic and metabolomic analyses and did not verify whether different cardiac surgery methods or the severity of delirium could affect the screening of biomarkers or their differential levels. Therefore, further validation analyses in larger cohorts are required. Second, we did not conduct long-term cognitive assessments postoperatively, making it difficult to determine any differences in the long-term impact on permanent cognitive impairment, including the potential long-term effects of MMPs, between the control and POD groups. Finally, the findings from this initial exploratory study are tentative, necessitating more comprehensive and extensive research to thoroughly evaluate the functions of these proteins, metabolites, and pathways in POCD and related perioperative neurocognitive disorders.

Conclusions

This study unveils the intricate biological backdrop of POD, especially underlining the vital roles of MMP9, MPO, TLR2, ICAM1, and glutamate in its pathophysiology and accentuating the significance of neuroinflammation and BBB integrity on POD. This study identifies Mirin and Orantinib as promising MMP9 inhibitors with potential therapeutic benefits for postoperative delirium. Through integrative proteomics, metabolomics, and computational analyses, we demonstrated their superior binding stability and affinity compared to Minocycline, offering new avenues for targeted treatment strategies. Additionally, a notable negative correlation was observed between S100B and glutamate, offering fresh perspectives on the molecular mechanisms behind POD. The study enhances our comprehension of the molecular mechanisms underlying postoperative delirium and introduces new biomarkers and potential targets for its diagnosis and treatment. Future work will need to further explore the clinical application of these findings and validate the effectiveness of these new biomarkers and therapeutic targets.
