The highest weight theory for Representations of General Linear groups in the Verlinde categories in positive characteristic
Alexandra Utiralova

Abstract
. Following the work of Venkatesh ( [ Ven24 ] ), we study further the categories of representations of the general linear groups G ​ L ​ ( X ) GL(X) in the Verlinde category V ​ e ​ r p Ver_{p} in characteristic p p . The main question we answer is how to translate between highest weight labelings for different choices of the Borel subgroup B ⁡ ( X ) ⊂ G ​ L ​ ( X ) B(X)\subset GL(X) . We do this by reducing the general case to the study of representations of the group G ​ L ​ ( X ) GL(X) for X = L m ⊕ L n X=L_{m}\oplus L_{n} using the method of odd reflections. On the category of representations of G ​ L ​ ( L m ⊕ L n ) GL(L_{m}\oplus L_{n}) we introduce the structure of a highest weight category, as well as a categorical action of 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 ^ p \widehat{\mathfrak{sl}}_{p} through translation functors. It allows us to understand projective and injective objects, BGG reciprocity, duality and lowest weights for simple modules, and standard filtration multiplicities for projective objects.



1.
Introduction

The famous reconstruction theorem by Deligne
[
Del90
]
says that any symmetric tensor category
\mathcal{C}
admitting a fiber functor (i.e. a symmetric tensor functor) to some category
\mathcal{D}
can be reconstructed as a category of representations of some affine group scheme in
\mathcal{D}
. Thus, it is interesting to study group schemes in categories that appear as targets of fiber functors.

Let
\mathbbm{k}
be an algebraically closed field. In characteristic zero, Deligne (
[
Del02
]
) proved that any
\mathbbm{k}
-linear symmetric tensor category (STC for short)
\mathcal{C}
of moderate growth (such that the length of
X^{\otimes n}
grows at most exponentially with
n
for any
X\in\mathcal{C}
) admits a fiber functor to the category
sVec
of finite dimensional super vector spaces. In characteristic
p
, however, the situation is more complicated and this result no longer holds verbatim. One of the simplest counterexamples is the Verlinde category
Ver_{p}
defined as the semisimplification of the category of representations of
{\mathbb{Z}}/p{\mathbb{Z}}
over the field
\mathbbm{k}
of characteristic
p>0
. It is known (see for example
[
BEO23
]
) that
Ver_{p}
is incompressible, so it cannot be mapped to a smaller category like
sVec
. The theorem of Deligne was partially generalized for the characteristic
p
case by Coulembier, Etingof and Ostrik in
[
CEOK23
]
, who proved that when char
\mathbbm{k}=p
, any
\mathbbm{k}
-linear
Frobenius exact
STC
\mathcal{C}
of moderate growth admits a fiber functor to
Ver_{p}
. The additional condition of being Frobenius exact is necessary and is specific to positive characteristic. As a consequence of this result and the reconstruction theorem, we get that any such
\mathcal{C}
is tensor-equivalent to the category of representations of some affine group scheme in
Ver_{p}
, which motivates our study of representation theory in the Verlinde categories.

In his thesis
[
Ven19
]
, and later in
[
Ven23
]
, Venkatesh laid some ground work in representation theory in the Verlinde categories. In
[
Ven24
]
, he also proved the classification result about representations of the general linear group
G=GL(X)
for
X\in Ver_{p}
. His classification runs roughly as follows. Let
X=X_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus X_{k}
, where
X_{i}
’s are simple objects in
Ver_{p}
. Define the maximal torus
T=GL(X_{1})\times\ldots\times GL(X_{k})\subset G
and the Borel subgroup
B
with
Lie(B)=\bigoplus_{i\leq j}X_{i}\otimes X_{j}^{*}\subset{\mathfrak{gl}}(X)
. The category
\operatorname{Rep}_{Ver_{p}}(T)
of representations of
T
is equivalent to
Ver_{p}(GL_{n_{1}})\boxtimes\ldots\boxtimes Ver_{p}(GL_{n_{k}})
, where
n_{i}=\operatorname{dim}X_{i}
, and
Ver_{p}(GL_{n})
is the semisimplification of the category of tilting modules for
GL_{n}
. Let us call simple objects in
\operatorname{Rep}_{Ver_{p}}(T)

weights
. For any weight
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
one can define a generalized Verma module
M({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
as
Dist(G)\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}\displaylimits_{Dist(B)}}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
, where
Dist(G)
is the algebra of distributions at the identity (hyperalgebra) of
G
. Then
M({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
, as a
Dist(G)
-module, has a unique simple quotient
L({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
, with
L({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\not\simeq L({\boldsymbol{\mu}})
if
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\not\simeq{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
. Under some conditions on
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
, the action of
Dist(G)
on
L({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
integrates to the action of
G
, and we get that the collection of all
L({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
’s with
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
integrable is in bijection with the set of isomorphism classes of simple objects in
\operatorname{Rep}_{Ver_{p}}(G)
.

In this paper we study in more detail the highest weight theory for representations of
GL(X)
. The main question we answer is how to translate between highest weight labels for simple modules for different choices of the Borel subgroup
B
. In the above notations, different orderings (i.e. permutations) of simple summands
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}
provide different choices of the Borel subgroup. Some of the permutations of these summands, namely those that only permute isomorphic
X_{i}
’s, can be realized through the action of some subgroup of
G(\mathbbm{k})
(the
\mathbbm{k}
-points of
G
) which we call the classical Weyl group of
G
. The resulting Borel subgroups are then conjugate to each other, just like for classical reductive groups. We describe what happens in this case in Section
8.2
. However, for permutations that swap nonisomorphic
X_{i}
’s we get Borel subgroups which are not conjugate to each other. This situation is similar to that for quasi-reductive supergroups. We adapt Serganova’s method of odd reflections to our case and reduce the question to studying what happens when we permute just two consequent nonisomorphic simple summands
X_{i}\simeq L_{m}
and
X_{i+1}\simeq L_{r}
with
m<r
, where
L_{n}
denotes the unique
n
-dimensional simple object in
Ver_{p}
(see Section
8.3
). The whole question is then reduced to the study of lowest weights for representations of the group
GL(L_{m}\oplus L_{r})
.

The remainder of the paper is dedicated to the study of the category

\mathcal{C}=\operatorname{Rep}_{Ver_{p}}(GL(L_{m}\oplus L_{r})),

which turns out to be of interest on its own. The main idea for working with it is to make use of the subcategory of super vector spaces inside
Ver_{p}
(it is generated by
L_{1}
and
L_{p-1}
). One can then consider
L_{r}
as an odd version of
L_{n}
for
n=p-r
, since
L_{r}=L_{n}\otimes L_{p-1}
, and thus the group
GL(L_{m}\oplus L_{r})
can be thought of as a super group scheme
GL(L_{m}|L_{n})
(see Section
9.1
). It turns out that there are multiple similarities between the representation theory of
GL(L_{m}|L_{n})
and the representation theory of the supergroup
GL(m|n)
. Getting inspiration from works about
GL(m|n)
, especially
[
Bru03
]
and
[
BS12
]
(but also
[
Zou96
]
,
[
Ser11
]
,
[
GS11
]
), we use the highest weight structure and the action of translation functors to study
\mathcal{C}
.

We first reprove in Theorem
5.5

the result of Venkatesh (
[
Ven24
]
) stating that the category of representations of
GL(L_{n})
is equivalent to the category
Ver_{p}(GL_{n})
(see Definition
4.1
). We then study the categories
\mathcal{C}_{n}=Ver_{p}(GL_{n})
through the categorical action of
\widehat{\mathfrak{sl}}_{p}
on them via translation functors. We prove in Theorem
6.7
that
\mathcal{C}_{n}
categorifies the loop module
(\Lambda^{n}{\mathbb{C}}^{p})^{loop}
for
\widehat{\mathfrak{sl}}_{p}
(where
{\mathbb{C}}^{p}
is the tautological representation of
{\mathfrak{sl}}_{p}
) and that simple objects in
\mathcal{C}_{n}
correspond to the standard basis vectors. We then use this result to prove that
\mathcal{C}=\operatorname{Rep}_{Ver_{p}}(GL(L_{m}|L_{n}))
categorifies the tensor product

(\Lambda^{m}{\mathbb{C}}^{p})^{loop}\otimes((\Lambda^{n}{\mathbb{C}}^{p})^{*})^{loop},

see Section
9.5
.

In Section
9.2
, we prove that
\mathcal{C}
is a highest weight category in the sense of Cline, Parshall and Scott
[
CPS88
]
, where the role of standard objects is played by the generalized Verma modules
M({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
. We prove that, just like for
GL(m|n)
, projective objects are also injective, and irreducible standard objects are projective. We then prove an irreducibility criterion for standard objects, that is identical to that for Kac modules for
GL(m|n)
(see Section
9.3
). In Section
9.6
, we introduce the language of weight diagrams to describe the action of translation functors on
\mathcal{C}
. The weight diagrams we obtain are a
circular
version of the weight diagrams for
GL(m|n)
. Those were introduced by Brundan and Stroppel in
[
BS08
]
and
[
BS12
]
in a slightly more general setting. It is more common now to use a slightly modified version of these diagrams in the literature on
GL(m|n)
(see for example
[
GS11
]
), which we also do. In Section
9.7
we describe the action of translation functors on simple and indecomposable projective modules (IPMs) using the language of weight diagrams. We deduce from this the computations of multiplicities in the standard filtration of IPMs (see Theorem
9.46
). Finally, in Section
9.8
we use this to compute the lowest weight in
L({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
.

2.2.
Affine group schemes in symmetric tensor categories

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a symmetric tensor category.

Definition 2.1
.

An
affine group scheme

G
in
\mathcal{C}
is an object in

GpSch(\mathcal{C}):=CommHopfAlg(\mathcal{C})^{op},

where
CommHopfAlg(\mathcal{C})
is the category of commutative Hopf algebras in the ind-completion
\operatorname{Ind}(\mathcal{C})
of
\mathcal{C}
. The corresponding Hopf algebra is called the
algebra of functions
on
G
and is denoted by
\mathcal{O}(G)
.

For any
G\in GpSch(\mathcal{C})
we can define the
functor of points

G:CommAlg(\mathcal{C})\to Gps,

which sends a commutative algebra
A
in
\operatorname{Ind}(\mathcal{C})
to

G(A)=\operatorname{Hom}_{CommAlg(\mathcal{C})}(\mathcal{O}(G),A).

Example 2.2
.

(1)

As
Vec
is naturally a tensor subcategory (spanned by
\mathbbm{1}
) in any symmetric tensor category
\mathcal{C}
, one can consider any affine group scheme
G
as an affine group scheme in
\mathcal{C}
.

(2)

An affine super group scheme is an affine group scheme in
sVec
.

Sometimes we will need to consider affine group schemes of
finite type
in
\mathcal{C}
. Similarly to the classical case,
G\in GpSch(\mathcal{C})
is said to be of
finite type
if
\mathcal{O}(G)
is
finitely generated
, i.e. if there exists
X\in\mathcal{C}
(as opposed to
\operatorname{Ind}(\mathcal{C})
), for which
\mathcal{O}(G)
is a quotient of the symmetric algebra
S(X)
. We denote the corresponding category by
GpSch(\mathcal{C})^{ft}
.

For any
G\in GpSch(\mathcal{C})
one can define the corresponding
Lie algebra
object in the pro-completion of
\mathcal{C}
as follows:

{\mathfrak{g}}=Lie(G)=(I/I^{2})^{*},

where
I
is the augmentation ideal in
\mathcal{O}(G)
.

Remark 2.3
.

The definition of a Lie algebra object in a STC in characteristic
p
is subtle and can be found for example in
[
Ven23
]
, Section 5.1. It is easy to see that
Lie(G)
is an operadic Lie algebra object, i.e. an object with a skew-symmetric bracket, satisfying the Jacobi identity. However, operadic Lie algebras are not well-behaved, and one should impose additional conditions to ensure nice properties like Poincare-Birkhoff-Witt (PBW) theorem. By a
Lie algebra object
we will mean an operadic Lie algebra object for which PBW theorem holds.

Etingof proved in
[
Eti18
]
, Lemma 4.2, that any subobject of an associative algebra object closed under the commutator is a Lie algebra object. This implies that
Lie(G)
is an actual Lie algebra object, as it is a Lie subalgebra of the algebra of distributions on
G
constructed below.

The Lie algebra object
{\mathfrak{g}}=Lie(G)
can also be described through the functor of points. Let us consider
{\mathfrak{g}}
as an affine scheme whose algebra of functions is
S({\mathfrak{g}}^{*})
. For any
A\in CommAlg(\mathcal{C})
put

{\mathfrak{g}}(A)=\operatorname{Hom}_{CommAlg(\mathcal{C})}(S({\mathfrak{g}}^{*}),A)=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}({\mathfrak{g}}^{*},A)=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(I/I^{2},A),

where
I
is the augmentation ideal in
\mathcal{O}(G)
.

Thus similarly to the classical case, we deduce that

{\mathfrak{g}}(A)=\operatorname{Ker}(G(A[\epsilon])\to G(A)),

where
\epsilon^{2}=0
.

Remark 2.4
.

Let
G
be of finite type so that
\mathcal{O}(G)
is a quotient of
S(X)
for some
X\in\mathcal{C}
. Then
{\mathfrak{g}}=Lie(G)
is an object of
\mathcal{C}
(rather than of the pro-completion of
\mathcal{C}
), since
{\mathfrak{g}}
is a quotient of
X
.

Similarly to the classical case, one defines the
algebra of distributions
on
G
as:

Dist(G)=\varinjlim_{k}(\mathcal{O}(G)/I^{k})^{*}.

This algebra is filtered with

Dist(G)^{k-1}=(\mathcal{O}(G)/I^{k})^{*}

and naturally acts on every
\mathcal{O}(G)
-comodule object.

The Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
is a subobject of
Dist(G)^{1}
closed under the commutator.

Definition 2.5
.

A
Dist(G)
-module
M\in\operatorname{Ind}(\mathcal{C})
is called
integrable
if it admits a compatible
\mathcal{O}(G)
-comodule structure.

2.3.
The fundamental group of a symmetric tensor category

Let us consider the (dual of) internal hom bifunctor on
\mathcal{C}
:

h:\mathcal{C}^{op}\times\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C},

~~(X,Y)\mapsto Y\otimes X^{*}.

In
[
Del90
]
Deligne defined the Hopf algebra object in
\operatorname{Ind}(\mathcal{C})

H_{\mathcal{C}}=\int^{X\in\mathcal{C}}h(X,X)=\int^{X\in\mathcal{C}}X\otimes X^{*},

where for any bifunctor
F:\mathcal{C}^{op}\times\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
the
coend
of
F
, denoted

\int^{X\in C}F(X,X),

is the coequalizer of

\prod_{X,Y\in\mathcal{C}}\prod_{f:X\to Y}F(Y,X)\rightrightarrows\prod_{X\in\mathcal{C}}F(X,X)

in
\mathcal{D}
, with one arrow sending
F(Y,X)
to
F(X,X)
via
F(f\times id_{X})
, and the second one sending
F(Y,X)
to
F(Y,Y)
via
F(id_{Y}\times f)
.

Remark 2.6
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is semisimple then

H_{\mathcal{C}}=\bigoplus_{X\in sOb(\mathcal{C})}X\otimes X^{*},

where
sOb(\mathcal{C})
is the set of isomorphism classes of simple objects in
\mathcal{C}
.

\bullet
Multiplication:

The multiplication on
H_{\mathcal{C}}
is inherited from the multiplication map on
\prod_{X\in\mathcal{C}}X\otimes X^{*}
which sends
(X\otimes X^{*})\otimes(Y\otimes Y^{*})
to
(X\otimes Y)\otimes(X\otimes Y)^{*}
.

\bullet
Unit:

The unit map on
H_{\mathcal{C}}
is inherited from the unit map

\eta=\prod_{X\in\mathcal{C}}coev_{X}:\mathbbm{1}\to\prod_{X\in\mathcal{C}}X\otimes X^{*}.

\bullet
Comultiplication:

The comultiplication on
H_{\mathcal{C}}
is inherited from
\prod_{X\in\mathcal{C}}X\otimes X^{*}
, on which it is defined via

\Delta|_{X\otimes X^{*}}=id_{X}\otimes coev_{X}\otimes X:X\otimes X^{*}\to(X\otimes X^{*})\otimes(X\otimes X^{*}).

\bullet
Counit:

The counit map is inherited from the evaluation map
\prod_{X\in\mathcal{C}}ev_{X}
on

\prod_{X\in\mathcal{C}}X\otimes X^{*}
.

\bullet
Antipode:

The antipode is inherited from the antipode on
\prod_{X\in\mathcal{C}}X\otimes X^{*}

S|_{X\otimes X^{*}}=\tau_{X,X^{*}}:X\otimes X^{*}\to X^{*}\otimes X.

Definition 2.7
.

The
fundamental group
of a symmetric tensor category
\mathcal{C}
is the affine group scheme
\pi_{\mathcal{C}}
in
\mathcal{C}
with

\mathcal{O}(\pi_{\mathcal{C}})=H_{\mathcal{C}}.

Remark 2.8
.

For any
A\in CommAlg(\mathcal{C})
the
A
-points of
\pi_{\mathcal{C}}
are given by

\pi_{\mathcal{C}}(A)=\mathrm{Aut}^{\otimes}(I_{A}),

where
I_{A}:\mathcal{C}\to A
-
mod_{\mathcal{C}}
is the functor sending
X\in\mathcal{C}
to a free
A
-module generated by
X
, i.e.
A\otimes X
.

In particular the
\mathbbm{k}
-points of
\pi_{\mathcal{C}}
are the tensor automorphisms of the identity functor
id_{\mathcal{C}}
.

Any object
X\in\mathcal{C}
is naturally an
H_{\mathcal{C}}
-comodule (and thus a
\pi_{\mathcal{C}}
-module). The coaction map

X\to X\otimes H_{\mathcal{C}}

is dual to the natural map

X^{*}\otimes X\to H_{\mathcal{C}}.

Definition 2.9
.

For any
G\in GpSch(\mathcal{C})
and any homomorphism

\varepsilon:\pi_{\mathcal{C}}\to G,

define the category
\operatorname{Rep}_{\mathcal{C}}(G,\varepsilon)
to be the category of
G
-modules
M
in
\mathcal{C}
(i.e.
\mathcal{O}(G)
-comodules), whose restriction to
\pi_{\mathcal{C}}
along
\varepsilon
coincides with the natural action of
\pi_{\mathcal{C}}
on the object
M
.

Let
F:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
be a tensor functor. There is a natural group scheme homomorphism

\varepsilon:\pi_{\mathcal{D}}\to F(\pi_{\mathcal{C}})

corresponding to the Hopf algebra object map

\varepsilon^{*}:F(H_{\mathcal{C}})\to H_{\mathcal{D}},

which in turn is induced from the map

F(X^{*}\otimes X)=F(X)^{*}\otimes F(X)\to H_{\mathcal{D}}.

Theorem 2.10

(
[
Del90
]
, Theorem 8.17)
.

The functor
F
induces an equivalence of categories

\mathcal{C}\to\operatorname{Rep}_{\mathcal{D}}(F(\pi_{\mathcal{C}}),\varepsilon).

Remark 2.11
.

For any
A\in CommAlg(\mathcal{D})
the
A
-points of
F(\pi_{\mathcal{C}})
are given by

F(\pi_{\mathcal{C}})(A)=\mathrm{Aut}^{\otimes}(I_{A}\circ F),

where
I_{A}:\mathcal{D}\to A
-
mod_{\mathcal{D}}
is the functor from Remark
2.8
.

Thus, the
\mathbbm{k}
-points are

F(\pi_{\mathcal{C}})(\mathbbm{k})=\mathrm{Aut}^{\otimes}(F).

Example 2.12
.

(1)

For
\mathcal{C}=Vec
the fundamental group
\pi_{\mathcal{C}}
is trivial, so we recover the standard Tannakian reconstruction theorem: If
\mathcal{C}
is a Tannakian category, i.e. a symmetric tensor category admitting a fiber functor

F:\mathcal{C}\to Vec,

then
\mathcal{C}
is equivalent to the category
\operatorname{Rep}(G)
, where
G
is an affine group scheme with

G(A)=\mathrm{Aut}^{\otimes}(I_{A}\circ F)

for any commutative
\mathbbm{k}
-algebra
A
.

(2)

For
\mathcal{C}=sVec
the fundamental group is
{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
, which acts trivially on even vector spaces and via multiplication by
-1
on odd vector spaces.

If
\mathcal{C}
is a super Tannakian category, i.e. a symmetric tensor category admitting a super fiber functor:

F:\mathcal{C}\to sVec,

then
G=F(\pi_{\mathcal{C}})
is an affine super group scheme receiving a group scheme homomorphism

\varepsilon:{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}\to G.

Let
z=\varepsilon(\overline{1})
, then
\mathcal{C}
is equivalent to
\operatorname{Rep}(G,z)
(see the last notation in Section
2.1
).

(3)

If
\mathcal{C}=\operatorname{Rep}G
for some affine (super) group scheme
G
then
\pi_{\mathcal{C}}=G
and
H_{\mathcal{C}}=\mathcal{O}(G)
is an ind Hopf algebra object in
\operatorname{Rep}(G)
via the conjugation action of
G
on it.

For any other affine (super) group scheme
K
we get a bijection

\{\text{Tensor functors }F:\operatorname{Rep}G\to\operatorname{Rep}K\}\leftrightarrow\{\text{Group homomorphisms }\varepsilon:K\to G\}.

Recall that the Lie algebra object
{\mathfrak{g}}=Lie(\pi_{\mathcal{C}})
can also be described through the functor of points:

{\mathfrak{g}}(A)=\operatorname{Ker}(\pi_{\mathcal{C}}(A[\epsilon])\to\pi_{\mathcal{C}}(A)),

where
\epsilon^{2}=0
.

Theorem 2.13
.

As an affine scheme in
\mathcal{C}
, the Lie algebra object
{\mathfrak{g}}=Lie(\pi_{\mathcal{C}})
represents the functor of points

A\mapsto Der^{\otimes}(I_{A}),

where
Der^{\otimes}(I_{A})
are “tensor derivations” of
I_{A}
, that is, natural transformations
\beta:I_{A}\to I_{A}
satisfying

\beta_{X\otimes Y}=\beta_{X}\otimes I_{A}(id_{Y})+I_{A}(id_{X})\otimes\beta_{Y}.

Proof.

Any automorphism
\phi
in
\pi_{\mathcal{C}}(A[\epsilon])=\mathrm{Aut}^{\otimes}(I_{A[\epsilon]})
is of the form

\phi_{X}=\alpha_{X}+\epsilon\beta_{X},\text{ for }X\in\mathcal{C},

where
\alpha,\beta\in\operatorname{End}(I_{A})
with
\alpha_{X},\beta_{X}:A\otimes X\to A\otimes X
. The natural map
\pi_{\mathcal{C}}(A[\epsilon])\to\pi_{\mathcal{C}}(A)
sends
\alpha+\epsilon\beta
to
\alpha
. Therefore, if

It is not difficult to prove that indecomposable representations of

By looking closely at the formula above as well as at the explicitly defined tensor product on

•

•

•

•

L_{3}
tensor generates

(

We conclude this section by describing
the
level-rank duality
for the Verlinde categories in positive characteristic.

There is a well-known equivalence of categories (see for example
[
CEOK23
]
):

(4.6.1)

\mathbf{D}:Ver_{p}(PGL_{n})\xrightarrow{\sim}Ver_{p}(PGL_{p-n}).

This equivalence will also become apparent in Section
5
.

Let us give some explicit description of this equivalence on the level of simple objects. For any
GL_{n}
-weight
\lambda\in C_{p}
of degree
0
(i.e. a
PGL_{n}
-weight) define
D(\lambda)
to be the
PGL_{p-n}
-weight, for which
V_{D(\lambda)}=\mathbf{D}(V_{\lambda})
.

First note that if
V_{\lambda}
corresponds to a polynomial representation of
GL_{n}
(i.e. all
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
) with
\lambda_{n}=0
then we can assign a Young diagram
Y(\lambda)
to
\lambda
, and the condition
\lambda\in C_{p}
translates to saying that
\lambda_{1}\leq p-n
, or equivalently, that
Y(\lambda)
fits into the
(n-1)
-by-
(p-n)
rectangle. Simultaneously any polynomial weight
\lambda
, such that
Y(\lambda)
fits into the
n
-by-
(p-n)
rectangle, is in
C_{p}
.

For a polynomial
GL_{n}
-weight
\lambda
(or a partition
\lambda
of some positive integer
N
) let
\lambda^{t}
denote the
transpose
of
\lambda
(that is
Y(\lambda^{t})
is obtained by reflecting
Y(\lambda)
around the
x=y
line on he plane). Note that if
\lambda_{1}\leq p-n
, partition
\lambda^{t}
has at most
p-n
parts, and thus can be thought of as a weight for
GL_{p-n}
. Moreover, in this case
\lambda^{t}\in C_{p}
, since
Y(\lambda^{t})
fits into the
(p-n)
-by-
n
rectangle.

Roughly speaking, the correspondence
\lambda\mapsto D(\lambda)
is transposition, but one needs to be careful defining it.

Let
\lambda
be a
GL_{n}
-weight. Let
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}
be the
positive
parts, and
\lambda_{n-l+1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
be the
negative
parts of
\lambda
. Put

\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k})=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}),

\beta=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{l})=(-\lambda_{n},\ldots,-\lambda_{n-l+1}).

This defines a bijection between
GL_{n}
-weights of degree zero and pairs of partitions
(\alpha,\beta)
with length(
\alpha)+
length
(\beta)\leq n
, and
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}=\sum_{j}\beta_{j}
. We have
\lambda\in C_{p}
if and only if
\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1}\leq p-n
.

Now for any
GL_{n}
-weight
\lambda\in C_{p}
of degree zero define
D(\lambda)
to be the
GL_{p-n}
-weight corresponding to the pair
(\alpha^{t},\beta^{t})
. Note that since length
(\alpha^{t})+
length
(\beta^{t})

=\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1}\leq p-n
, this weight is well-defined. Similarly, since
\alpha^{t}_{1}+\beta^{t}_{1}=
length
(\alpha)+
length
(\beta)

\leq n=p-(p-n)
, we have
D(\lambda)\in C_{p}
.

Note that the correspondence
\lambda\to D(\lambda)
is a bijection on degree zero weights in
C_{p}
for
GL_{n}
and for
GL_{p-n}
.

Remark 4.7
.

This construction can also be obtained as follows:

(1)

Take a weight
\lambda\in C_{p}
for
GL_{n}
of degree zero.

(2)

Turn
V_{\lambda}
into a polynomial representation of degree
-n\cdot\lambda_{n}
by tensoring it with
det^{-\lambda_{n}}
. Call the corresponding polynomial weight
\mu
.

(3)

The condition
\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{n}\leq p-n
translates to the condition
\mu_{1}\leq p-n
for
\mu
.

(4)

The transpose
\mu^{t}\in C_{p}
is a weight for
GL_{p-n}
. Tensor

with

to turn it into an object of degree zero (here we work in the category
Ver_{p}(PGL_{p-n})
).

(5)

This object is
\mathbf{D}(V_{\lambda})
.

This approach makes the combinatorial description of the bijection on simple objects less evident. However, it allows us to work only with polynomial representations of
GL_{n}
, which can be understood through Schur functors.

Remark 4.8
.

The equivalence
\mathbf{D}
can be extended to an equivalence of
sVec
-module categories

\mathbf{D}:Ver_{p}(GL_{n})\boxtimes sVec\xrightarrow{\sim}Ver_{p}(GL_{p-n})\boxtimes sVec.

In particular, we can extend the bijection
\lambda\leftrightarrow D(\lambda)
to weights of arbitrary degree.

For now, let us avoid ambiguity by denoting
V\in Ver_{p}(GL_{k})
by
V^{(k)}
for each
k<p
, similarly, object
V_{\lambda}
will be denoted by
V^{(k)}_{\lambda}
.

We start with the object
V^{(n)}_{\lambda}=V^{(n)}_{\mu}\otimes det^{\lambda_{n}}=\mathbb{S}_{\mu}V^{(n)}\otimes det^{\lambda_{n}}
, where
\mathbb{S}_{\mu}
is the Schur functor corresponding to
\mu
, and
\det=\Lambda^{n}V^{(n)}=\mathbb{S}_{\nu}V^{(n)}
for
\nu=(1,\ldots,1)
. Note here that
\nu^{t}=(n,0,\ldots,0)
, so
\mathbb{S}_{\nu^{t}}V^{(p-n)}=\chi
in
Ver_{p}(GL_{p-n})
.

Let
I
be the purely odd super vector space of super dimension
(0|1)
. We define
\mathbf{D}(V^{(n)})
to be

. Note for instance that this map behaves well on dimensions:

\operatorname{dim}V^{(p-n)}\otimes I=-(p-n)=n\in\mathbbm{k}.

Then we have

\mathbf{D}(V^{(n)}_{\lambda})=\mathbf{D}(V^{(n)}_{\mu}\otimes det^{\lambda_{n}})=

=\mathbb{S}_{\mu}(V^{(p-n)}\otimes I)\otimes(\mathbb{S}_{\nu}(V^{(p-n)}\otimes I))^{\otimes\lambda_{n}}=

=\mathbb{S}_{\mu^{t}}V^{(p-n)}\otimes I^{\otimes\sum\mu_{i}}\otimes(\mathbb{S}_{\nu^{t}}V^{(p-n)})^{\otimes\lambda_{n}}\otimes I^{\otimes\lambda_{n}}=

=V^{(p-n)}_{\mu^{t}}\otimes\chi^{\lambda_{n}}\otimes I^{\sum\lambda_{i}}.

When
\sum\lambda_{i}=0
,

\mathbf{D}(V_{\lambda}^{(n)})=V_{\mu^{t}}^{(p-n)}\otimes\chi^{\lambda_{n}}

has degree zero and lies in the subcategory
Ver_{p}(PGL_{p-n})
.

.

Suppose

and
\lambda=(6,5,2)\in C_{p}
for

. Then
\mu=(4,3,0)
and

Therefore,

\mathbf{D}(V_{\lambda}^{(3)})=V^{(4)}_{\mu^{t}}\otimes\chi^{2}\otimes I^{|\lambda|}=V^{(4)}_{(2,2,2,1)}\otimes V^{(4)}_{(3,0,0,0)}\otimes V^{(4)}_{(3,0,0,0)}\otimes I=

=V^{(4)}_{(2,2,2,2)}\otimes V^{(4)}_{(2,0,0,0)}\otimes V^{(4)}_{(3,0,0,0)}\otimes I=V^{(4)}_{(2,2,2,2)}\otimes V^{(4)}_{(3,2,0,0)}=V^{(4)}_{(5,4,2,2)}\otimes I.

So,

.

5.
Representations of
GL(L_{n})
for simple objects
L_{n}

In this section let us assume char
\mathbbm{k}>2
.

5.1.
Classification of
GL(L_{n})
-modules via fiber functors

Let us start our discussion of representations of
GL(X)
in
Ver_{p}
by describing the category of representations of
GL(L_{n})
for some
n=1,\ldots,p-1
. Moreover, as
L_{1}
and
L_{p-1}
span the subcategory equivalent to super vector spaces, we already know the representation theory of
GL(L_{1})
and
GL(L_{p-1})
. Let us assume therefore that
2\leq n\leq p-2
.

In
[
Ven24
]
, Venkatesh proved the following

Lemma 5.1

(
[
Ven24
]
, Corollary 4.3)
.

There is an isomorphism of affine group schemes of finite type in
Ver_{p}
:

GL(L_{n})\simeq GL(L_{n})_{0}\times SL(L_{n})=\mathbb{G}_{m}\times SL(L_{n}),

where
SL(L_{n})
is a group scheme defined in Example
3.15
.

Proof.

It follows immediately from the observation that, since
\operatorname{dim}L_{n}\neq 0
, we have a Lie algebra decomposition

{\mathfrak{gl}}(L_{n})=\mathbbm{1}\oplus{\mathfrak{sl}}(L_{n})

(the splitting map
\mathbbm{1}\to{\mathfrak{gl}}(L_{n})
is given by
\tau_{L_{n}^{*},L_{n}}\circ coev_{L_{n}}
).

Now
\mathbf{HC}(SL(L_{n}))=(1,{\mathfrak{sl}}(L_{n})),~\mathbf{HC}(\mathbb{G}_{m})=(\mathbb{G}_{m},\mathbbm{1})
, and

\mathbf{HC}(GL(L_{n}))=(\mathbb{G}_{m},{\mathfrak{gl}}(L_{n}))=(\mathbb{G}_{m}\times 1,\mathbbm{1}\oplus{\mathfrak{sl}}(L_{n})).

Remark 5.2
.

As
SL(L_{n})
is simultaneously a subgroup and a quotient of
GL(L_{n})
, we might as well denote it by
PGL(L_{n})
. This notation is more suitable in light of the results of this section.

Remark 5.3
.

Note that we ought to keep track of what happens to the homomorphism

\varepsilon:\pi_{Ver_{p}}\to GL(L_{n}).

Let
p:GL(L_{n})\to\mathbb{G}_{m}
be the projection. Then

p\circ\varepsilon:\pi_{Ver_{p}}\to\mathbb{G}_{m}

is not always trivial.

Recall that
\pi_{Ver_{p}}=\mu_{2}\times\pi_{Ver_{p}^{+}}
. Then
p\circ\varepsilon
factors through
\mu_{2}
and sends the generator
z\in\mu_{2}
to
(-1)^{n-1}
.

Corollary 5.4
.

We have an equivalence of categories

\operatorname{Rep}_{Ver_{p}}(GL(L_{n}),\varepsilon)=\operatorname{Rep}(\mathbb{G}_{m},z)\boxtimes\operatorname{Rep}_{Ver_{p}}(SL(L_{n}),\varepsilon^{\prime}),

where
\varepsilon^{\prime}
is the composition of the natural map
\varepsilon:\pi_{Ver_{p}}\to GL(L_{n})
with the projection
GL(L_{n})\to SL(L_{n})
.

Thus, we will focus on studying the representations of
SL(L_{n})
. Let us reprove here the following result of Venkatesh:

Theorem 5.5

(
[
Ven24
]
, Corollary 4.10)
.

The fiber functor

F^{+}:Ver_{p}^{+}(SL_{n})=Ver_{p}(PGL_{n})\to Ver_{p}

induces an equivalence of categories

Ver_{p}(PGL_{n})=Ver_{p}^{+}(SL_{n})\xrightarrow{\sim}\operatorname{Rep}_{Ver_{p}}(SL(L_{n}),\varepsilon^{\prime}).

Let us denote the fundamental group of the category
Ver_{p}(GL_{n})
by
\pi
and let us denote the fundamental group of the subcategory
Ver_{p}^{+}(GL_{n})=Ver_{p}(PGL_{n})
by
\pi^{+}
. Note that
\pi^{+}
is naturally a quotient of
\pi
.

Consider the fiber functor

F:Ver_{p}(GL_{n})\to Ver_{p},

and denote by
F^{+}
its restriction to the subcategory
Ver^{+}_{p}(GL_{n})
.

5.2.
Proof of Theorem
5.5

•

First, let us note that the category
Ver_{p}(GL_{n})
is tensor generated by the object
V
. The object
{\mathfrak{sl}}(V)=V_{(1,0,\ldots,0,-1)}
is simple and tensor generates the subcategory
Ver_{p}(PGL_{n})
.

•

By Theorem
2.19
, we get that
\pi
is a subgroup of
GL(V)
. The Lie algebra object
Lie(\pi)
is then a subalgebra of
{\mathfrak{gl}}(V)=\mathbbm{1}\oplus{\mathfrak{sl}}(V)
.

•

Note that
Lie(\pi)
cannot be trivial or isomorphic to
\mathbbm{1}
, because this would imply that
F(\pi)
is an classical group scheme in
Vec\subset Ver_{p}
(due to Harish-Chandra correspondence, see Theorem
3.11
). The latter, however, is impossible since
F({\mathfrak{gl}}(V))=L_{n}\otimes L_{n}^{*}
is a subobject in
F(\mathcal{O}(\pi))
(here we need the assumption that
2\leq n\leq p-2
).

•

Therefore,
Lie(\pi)
is either isomorphic to
{\mathfrak{gl}}(V)
or to
{\mathfrak{sl}}(V)
.

Now,
Lie(\pi^{+})
is a quotient of
Lie(\pi)
, and for similar reasons it is neither trivial, nor isomorphic to
\mathbbm{1}
. Therefore, again, there are only two possible options for it:
{\mathfrak{gl}}(V)
or
{\mathfrak{sl}}(V)
.

•

Consider now
F(\pi^{+})
as a group scheme in
Ver_{p}
. We have

F(\pi^{+})_{0}(\mathbbm{k})=F(\pi^{+})(\mathbbm{k})=\mathrm{Aut}^{\otimes}(F^{+}).

•

Let us prove that there are no nontrivial tensor automorhisms of the functor
F^{+}
. This would force the classical part
F(\pi^{+})_{0}
of
F(\pi^{+})
to be trivial.

Let
\alpha\in\mathrm{Aut}^{\otimes}(F^{+})
. Consider the object
\widetilde{V}=V\otimes\psi^{-1}
in
Ver_{p}(GL_{n})
, where
\psi
is the degree one invertible object of dimension
(-1)^{n-1}
in
Ver_{p}(GL_{n})
constructed in Section
4.2
. Since
V
has degree one, we get
\widetilde{V}\in Ver_{p}(PGL_{n})
(as
Ver_{p}(PGL_{n})
is precisely the degree zero part of
Ver_{p}(GL_{n})
). Then we get

{\mathfrak{sl}}(V)={\mathfrak{sl}}(\widetilde{V})\subset\widetilde{V}\otimes\widetilde{V}^{*}.

Moreover,
F^{+}(\widetilde{V})=F(V)\otimes F(\psi)=L_{n}\otimes(L_{p-1})^{\otimes(n-1)}
is a simple object of
Ver_{p}
. Therefore,
\alpha_{\widetilde{V}}
is a constant. We have

\alpha_{\widetilde{V}\otimes\widetilde{V}^{*}}=\alpha_{\widetilde{V}}\cdot\alpha_{\widetilde{V}}^{-1}=1,

since
\alpha
is a tensor automorphism. We get that

\alpha_{{\mathfrak{sl}}(V)}=1.

As
{\mathfrak{sl}}(V)
tensor generates the category
Ver_{p}(PGL_{n})
, we get that
\alpha_{X}=1
for each
X\in Ver_{p}(PGL_{n})
.

•

We deduce that
\mathbf{HC}(F(\pi^{+}))=(1,F(Lie(\pi^{+})))
with
F(Lie(\pi^{+}))
isomorphic to either
{\mathfrak{gl}}(F(V))={\mathfrak{gl}}(L_{n})
or
{\mathfrak{sl}}(F(V))={\mathfrak{sl}}(L_{n})
. Since we must have
F(Lie(\pi^{+}))_{0}=Lie(1)=0
, we obtain the desired result:

\mathbf{HC}(F(\pi^{+}))=(1,{\mathfrak{sl}}(L_{n})),

and thus

F(\pi^{+})=SL(L_{n})=PGL(L_{n}).

•

The statement of the theorem follows from the reconstruction theorem.

Corollary 5.6
.

Since
L_{n}=L_{p-1}\otimes L_{p-n}
, and the Lie algebra objects
{\mathfrak{sl}}(L_{n})
and
{\mathfrak{sl}}(L_{p-n})
are isomorphic in
Ver_{p}
(as
L_{p-1}
is invertible), we also get the isomorphism of group schemes
SL(L_{n})\simeq SL(L_{p-n})
(together with the homomorphism out of the fundamental group). Therefore, we obtain the level-rank duality
4.6.1
:

Ver_{p}(PGL_{n})\simeq\operatorname{Rep}_{Ver_{p}}(SL(L_{n}))\simeq\operatorname{Rep}_{Ver_{p}}(SL(L_{p-n}))\simeq Ver_{p}(PGL_{p-n}).

Corollary 5.7
.

The fiber functor

F:Ver_{p}(GL_{n})\to Ver_{p}

induces an equivalence of categories

Ver_{p}(GL_{n})\xrightarrow{\sim}\operatorname{Rep}_{Ver_{p}}(GL(L_{n}),\varepsilon).

Corollary 5.8
.

The fundamental group scheme
\pi
of the category
Ver_{p}(GL_{n})
is isomorphic to
GL(V)
. Thus, every object in
Ver_{p}(GL_{n})
enjoys the natural action of the group scheme
GL(V)
and the Lie algebra object
{\mathfrak{gl}}(V)
.

Corollary 5.9
.

The category
\operatorname{Rep}_{Ver_{p}}(GL(L_{n}),\varepsilon)
of representations of
GL(L_{n})
in
Ver_{p}
is semisimple, and simple objects are in bijection with weights
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
with

\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{n},~\text{ and }\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{n}\leq p-n.

The bijection sends
\lambda
to
F(V_{\lambda})
, where
V_{\lambda}\in Ver_{p}(GL_{n})
.

In this section we describe in more detail the combinatorics of the “highest weights" for representations of

for simple object

Remark 6.1

Here

Lemma 6.2

For each

and each admissible weight

contains at most one element.

Proof.

Suppose

Without loss of generality assume

and
-i>-j

Thus

We get the decomposition

Further, let us define the operator

the sum
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}
.
We then use the standard dominance order on characters of
T_{0}=\mathbb{G}_{m}^{k}
.

More specifically, we say that
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=(\lambda^{(1)},\ldots,\lambda^{(k)})
is

if

|\lambda^{(1)}|\geq\ldots\geq|\lambda^{(k)}|.

And we write

{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=(\lambda^{(1)},\ldots,\lambda^{(k)})\preceq{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=(\mu^{(1)},\ldots,\mu^{(k)})

if

|\mu^{(1)}|-|\lambda^{(1)}|\geq\ldots\geq|\mu^{(k)}|-|\lambda^{(k)}|.

It makes sense now to talk about “weight decomposition" and “highest weights" for
G
-modules and
Dist(G)
-modules admitting a compatible action of
T
. The role of weights is played by irreducible representations of
T
.

Example 7.2
.

Consider the adjoint action of
T
on

\mathfrak{n}^{-}=Lie(N^{-})=\bigoplus_{1\leq j<i\leq k}L_{n}^{(i)}\otimes(L_{n}^{(j)})^{*}.

The component
L_{n}^{(i)}\otimes(L_{n}^{(j)})^{*}
is a simple
T
-submodule with character
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i,j}
, such that

{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i,j}\simeq(V^{*})^{(j)}\boxtimes V^{(i)}:=

=\mathbbm{1}^{(1)}\boxtimes\ldots\boxtimes\mathbbm{1}^{(j-1)}\boxtimes(V^{*})^{(j)}\boxtimes\mathbbm{1}^{(j+1)}\boxtimes\ldots\boxtimes\mathbbm{1}^{(i-1)}\boxtimes V^{(i)}\boxtimes\mathbbm{1}^{(i+1)}\boxtimes\ldots\boxtimes\mathbbm{1}^{(k)}

as objects in
\operatorname{Rep}_{Ver_{p}}(T,\varepsilon)=Ver_{p}(GL_{n})^{(1)}\boxtimes\ldots\boxtimes Ver_{p}(GL_{n})^{(k)}
. The corresponding
T_{0}
-character is

\alpha_{i,j}=e_{i}-e_{j}:=(0,\ldots,0,\underbrace{-1}_{j},0,\ldots,0,\underbrace{1}_{i},0,\ldots,0).

The highest weight in a module
M
is a weight
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
(i.e.
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in sOb(\operatorname{Rep}_{Ver_{p}}(T,\varepsilon))
), such that

\operatorname{Hom}_{T}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}},M)=\mathbbm{k},

and for any other weight
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
with
\operatorname{Hom}_{T}({\boldsymbol{\mu}},M)\neq 0
we have

{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\preceq{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}.

A
T
-module possessing the highest weight is called a

.

.

is a highest weight module with highest weight
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
.

Proof.

It follows directly from the decomposition

.

7.2.
Representations of
GL(X)
for general
X

Consider now any object
X\in Ver_{p}
together with a decomposition into the direct sum of irreducibles

X=\bigoplus_{n=1}^{p-1}L_{n}^{\oplus k_{n}}.

The group scheme
GL(X)
is the generalization of the general linear super group
GL(m|n)
. In a sense, we have

GL(X)=GL(k_{1}|k_{2}|\ldots|k_{p-1}).

Consider a “Levi” subgroup

M=\prod_{n=1}^{p-1}GL(L_{n}^{\oplus k_{n}})\subset GL(X).

In the super group case
GL(m|n)
the role of this subgroup would be played by the even subgroup
GL_{m}\times GL_{n}
. Note that in our case
M_{0}=GL(X)_{0}
as well.

By Theorem
7.7
, simple
M
-modules are of the form

L({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1})\otimes\ldots\otimes L({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{p-1}),

where
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{n}
is a dominant weight in
\operatorname{Rep}_{Ver_{p}}(T(L_{n}^{\oplus k_{n}}),\varepsilon_{n})
.

Define
\mathfrak{p}:=\mathfrak{b}(X)\oplus Lie(M)
. It is clear that
\mathfrak{p}
is a Lie subalgebra in
{\mathfrak{gl}}(X)
with

\mathfrak{p}_{0}=Lie(M)_{0}.

Thus we can define the “parabolic" subgroup
P\subset G
with

\mathbf{HC}(P)=(M_{0},\mathfrak{p})=(\prod_{n=1}^{p-1}GL_{k_{n}},\mathfrak{p})=(GL(X)_{0},\mathfrak{p}).

We have

{\mathfrak{gl}}(X)=\mathfrak{p}\oplus\mathfrak{u},

with
\mathfrak{u}\subset\mathfrak{n}^{-}
satisfying

\mathfrak{u}_{0}=0.

Thus, by the PBW theorem and Lemma
3.14
,

Dist(GL(X))=Dist(P)\otimes U(\mathfrak{u}),

with
U(\mathfrak{u})
of finite length in
Ver_{p}
.

The action of
M
on the simple module
L({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1})\otimes\ldots\otimes L({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{p-1})
can be extended trivially to
P
.

Definition 7.8
.

Define the “Kac module” for
GL(X)
as the parabolic induction:

K({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{p-1}):=Dist(GL(X))\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}\displaylimits_{Dist(P)}}[L({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1})\otimes\ldots\otimes L({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{p-1})].

Since
P_{0}=GL(X)_{0}
, the
Dist(GL(X))
module

K({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{p-1})\simeq U(\mathfrak{u})\otimes[L({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1})\otimes\ldots\otimes L({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{p-1})]

has finite length in
Ver_{p}
and is integrable by Theorem
7.5
.

Recall that the choice of Borel subgroup
B(X)
in
GL(X)
required the ordering of simple components in
X
:

X=X_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus X_{k},

where
k=k_{1}+\ldots+k_{p-1}
and

\underbrace{X_{1}\simeq\ldots\simeq X_{k_{1}}}_{k_{1}}\simeq L_{1},

\underbrace{X_{k_{1}+1}\simeq\ldots\simeq X_{k_{1}+k_{2}}}_{k_{2}}\simeq L_{2},

\cdots

\underbrace{X_{k_{1}+\ldots+k_{p-2}+1}\simeq\ldots\simeq X_{k}}_{k_{p-1}}\simeq L_{p-1}.

This ordering induces the standard dominance order on characters of

T(X)_{0}=(GL(X_{1})\times\ldots\times GL(X_{k}))_{0}=\mathbb{G}_{m}^{k},

and consequently on simple representations of
T(X)
.

Note that
K({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{p-1})
is a highest weight module with respect to this dominance order with highest weight

{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1}\boxtimes\ldots\boxtimes{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{p-1}.

The classical torus
T(X)_{0}=\mathbb{G}_{m}^{k}
acts on this highest weight with character

(|\lambda_{1}^{(1)}|,\ldots,|\lambda_{1}^{(k_{1})}|,|\lambda_{2}^{(1)}|,\ldots,|\lambda_{2}^{(k_{2})}|,\ldots,|\lambda_{p-1}^{(1)}|,\ldots,|\lambda_{p-1}^{(k_{p-1})}|).

Consequently, the
GL(X)
-module
K({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{p-1})
has a unique simple quotient
L({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{p-1})
.

(

Ven24
]
, Proposition 6.2 and Proposition 6.3)

(1)

(2)

8.
Different Borels

The whole construction of simple representations of
GL(X)
described in Section
7
depends heavily on the choice of Borel subgroup
B(X)\subset GL(X)
, that is, on the choice of ordering of simple components of
X
.

8.1.
Permuting simple summands

Let
X=\bigoplus_{n=1}^{p-1}L_{n}^{\oplus k_{n}}=X_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus X_{k}
, and suppose as before that each
X_{i}
is simple and

\text{if }X_{i}\simeq L_{a},X_{i+1}\simeq L_{b}\text{ then }a<b,

that is,
k=k_{1}+\ldots+k_{p-1}
and

\underbrace{X_{1}\simeq\ldots\simeq X_{k_{1}}}_{k_{1}}\simeq L_{1},

\underbrace{X_{k_{1}+1}\simeq\ldots\simeq X_{k_{1}+k_{2}}}_{k_{2}}\simeq L_{2},

\cdots

\underbrace{X_{k_{1}+\ldots+k_{p-2}+1}\simeq\ldots\simeq X_{k}}_{k_{p-1}}\simeq L_{p-1}.

For any permutation
w
in the symmetric group
S_{k}
we can define the Borel subalgebra
\mathfrak{b}_{w}(X)\subset{\mathfrak{gl}}(X)
as

\mathfrak{b}_{w}(X)=\bigoplus_{1\leq w(i)\leq w(j)\leq k}X_{i}\otimes X_{j}^{*}.

The standard Borel subalgebra
\mathfrak{b}(X)
is the same as
\mathfrak{b}_{1}(X)
for the identity element
1\in S_{k}
.

For each
n=1,\ldots,p-1
let
w^{(n)}\in S_{k_{n}}
be the unique permutation satisfying the following property: for any
1\leq i,j\leq k_{n}

w^{(n)}(i)<w^{(n)}(j)\text{ if and only if }w(k_{1}+\ldots+k_{n-1}+i)<w(k_{1}+\ldots+k_{n-1}+j).

That is
w^{(n)}
is the permutation controlling the total ordering of elements

w(k_{1}+\ldots+k_{n-1}+1),w(k_{1}+\ldots+k_{n-1}+2),\ldots,w(k_{1}+\ldots+k_{n}).

Then
\mathfrak{b}_{w}(X)_{0}=\bigoplus_{n=1}^{p-1}\mathfrak{b}_{w^{(n)}}(\mathbbm{k}^{\oplus k_{n}})\subset{\mathfrak{gl}}(X)_{0}=\bigoplus_{n=1}^{p-1}{\mathfrak{gl}}_{k_{n}},

where
\mathfrak{b}_{w^{(n)}}(\mathbbm{k}^{\oplus k_{n}})
is the subalgebra of
{\mathfrak{gl}}_{k_{n}}
spanned by matrices
E_{i,j}
with
1\leq i,j\leq k_{n}
and
w^{(n)}(i)\leq w^{(n)}(j)
.

Note that in the classical case we have a description of the subalgebra
\mathfrak{b}_{w^{(n)}}=\mathfrak{b}_{w^{(n)}}(\mathbbm{k}^{\oplus k_{n}})
in terms of the standard Borel subalgebra
\mathfrak{b}
of upper-triangular matrices in
{\mathfrak{gl}}_{k_{n}}
and the Weyl group
W\simeq S_{k_{n}}
of
GL_{k_{n}}
:

\mathfrak{b}_{w^{(n)}}=ad_{\widetilde{w}^{(n)}}^{-1}(\mathfrak{b})

for some representative
\widetilde{w}^{(n)}
of
w^{(n)}
in
GL_{k_{n}}
.

Let
B_{w^{(n)}}(\mathbbm{k}^{\oplus k_{n}})=Ad_{\widetilde{w}^{(n)}}^{-1}(B(\mathbbm{k}^{\oplus k_{n}}))
be the corresponding Borel subgroup in
GL_{k_{n}}
.

Definition 8.1
.

For each
w\in S_{k}
define the Borel subgroup
B_{w}(X)\subset GL(X)
with

\mathbf{HC}(B_{w}(X))=(\prod_{n=1}^{p-1}B_{w^{(n)}}(\mathbbm{k}^{\oplus k_{n}}),~\mathfrak{b}_{w}(X)).

Remark 8.2
.

Note that by construction,
T(X)
is a subgroup of
B_{w}(X)
for each
w\in S_{k}
.

Our goal is to understand what happens to the highest weight theory for
GL(X)
described in Section
7
when we use
B_{w}(X)
instead of
B(X)
.

As in Section
7
, we can construct
the generalized Verma module

M_{w}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}):=Dist(GL(X))\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}\displaylimits_{Dist(B_{w}(X))}}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}.

First, let us define a new dominance order induced by
w\in S_{k}
on simple representations of
T(X)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}GL(X_{i})
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=(\lambda^{(1)},\ldots,\lambda^{(k)})=V_{\lambda^{(1)}}\boxtimes\ldots\boxtimes V_{\lambda^{(k)}}
be a simple representation of
T(X)
. Here for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, if
X_{i}\simeq L_{a}
, we identify
\lambda^{(i)}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{a}
, an admissible weight for
GL_{a}
, with the corresponding simple object
V_{\lambda^{(i)}}
in
Ver_{p}(GL_{a})
.

Permutation
w\in S_{k}
defines the dominance order on characters of
T(X)_{0}=\mathbb{G}_{m}^{k}
. We call
\alpha\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{k}

w
-
dominant
if

\alpha_{w^{-1}(1)}\geq\ldots\geq\alpha_{w^{-1}(k)}.

We say that
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=(\lambda^{(1)},\ldots,\lambda^{(k)})
is
w
-dominant
if
(|\lambda^{(1)}|,\ldots,|\lambda^{(k)}|)
is
w
-dominant.

For two
k
-tuples of admissible weights
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=(\lambda^{(1)},\ldots,\lambda^{(k)})
and
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=(\mu^{(1)},\ldots,\mu^{(k)})
we write

{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\preceq_{w}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},\text{ if }

(|\lambda^{(1)}|,\ldots,|\lambda^{(k)}|)-(|\mu^{(1)}|,\ldots,|\mu^{(k)}|)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{k}\text{ is }w\text{-dominant}.

The standard dominance order
\preceq
on representations of
T(X)
introduced in Section
7
coincides with
\preceq_{1}
for
1\in S_{k}
.

Definition 8.3
.

A weight
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
is called
w
-integrable
if the action of
Dist(GL(X))
on

integrates to the action of

.

Example 8.4
.

A weight
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=(\lambda^{(1)},\ldots,\lambda^{(k)})
is
1
-integrable if and only if for every

|\lambda^{(i)}|\geq|\lambda^{(j)}|

for each
k_{1}+\ldots+k_{n-1}+1\leq i\leq j\leq k_{1}+\ldots+k_{n}
.

With respect to partial order
\preceq_{w}
the generalized Verma module

is a highest weight module with highest weight
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
. It has the unique simple quotient
L_{w}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
.

•

Every simple
Dist(GL(X))
-module admitting a highest weight with respect to the partial order
\preceq_{w}
, in particular, every simple

-module must be isomorphic to
L_{w}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
for some simple
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\operatorname{Rep}_{Ver_{p}}(T(X),\varepsilon)
.

•

Clearly,
L_{w}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\simeq L_{w}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})
if and only if
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\simeq{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
.

•

If
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
is
w
-integrable then

must be isomorphic to

for some
1
-integrable weight
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
. Let us denote this weight by

.

.

For any

-integrable weight
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
the weight

-integrable weight for which

8.2.
Conjugate Borels

Definition 8.7
.

For
X=\bigoplus_{n=1}^{p-1}L_{n}^{\oplus k_{n}}
with
\sum_{n=1}^{p-1}k_{n}=k
, put
W=S_{k}
, and define the
classical Weyl group

W_{0}
to be the parabolic subgroup
S_{k_{1}}\times\ldots\times S_{k_{n}}\subset W=S_{k}
, i.e. the Weyl group of the classical subgroup
GL(X)_{0}=\prod_{n=1}^{p-1}GL_{k_{n}}
.

One can embed
W_{0}
into
GL(X)(\mathbbm{k})=GL(X)_{0}(\mathbbm{k})
as permutation matrices.

The group of
\mathbbm{k}
-points of any affine group scheme
G
in
Ver_{p}
acts on
\mathcal{O}(G)
by conjugation as follows. Let
g\in G(\mathbbm{k})
be a map

g:\mathcal{O}(G)\to\mathbbm{k},

and let
\Delta:\mathcal{O}(G)\to\mathcal{O}(G)\otimes\mathcal{O}(G)
be the comultiplication map.
We define

Ad_{g}=(g^{-1}\otimes id_{\mathcal{O}(G)}\otimes g)\circ\Delta^{2}:\mathcal{O}(G)\to\mathcal{O}(G)\otimes\mathcal{O}(G)\otimes\mathcal{O}(G)\to\mathcal{O}(G).

Definition 8.8
.

Let
g
be an element of
GL(X)(\mathbbm{k})=G(\mathbbm{k})
and let
H\subset G
be a closed subgroup scheme with

\mathcal{O}(H)=\mathcal{O}(G)/J_{H}

for an ideal
J_{H}\subset\mathcal{O}(G)
. Define

H^{g}=Ad_{g}(H)\subset GL(X)

be the subgroup cut out by the ideal

J_{H}^{g}=Ad_{g}(J_{H}).

The adjoint action of
G(\mathbbm{k})
on
\mathcal{O}(G)
induces the adjoint action on
{\mathfrak{g}}=Lie(G)
.

When
w\in W_{0}\subset GL(X)(\mathbbm{k})
, we get

Ad_{w}(X_{i}\otimes X_{j}^{*})=X_{w(i)}\otimes X_{w(j)}^{*}\subset{\mathfrak{gl}}(X),

so

Ad_{w}^{-1}(\mathfrak{b}(X))=\mathfrak{b}_{w}(X).

Moreover, for
w\in W_{0}
we have

B_{w}(X)_{0}=\prod_{n=1}^{p-1}B_{w^{(n)}}(\mathbbm{k}^{\oplus k_{n}})=Ad_{w}^{-1}(\prod_{n=1}^{p-1}B(\mathbbm{k}^{\oplus k_{n}}))\subset\prod_{n=1}^{p-1}GL_{k_{n}},

where
w
corresponds to
(w^{(1)},\ldots,w^{(p-1)})
under the isomorphism
S_{k_{1}}\times\ldots\times S_{k_{n}}\simeq W_{0}
.

We get
B_{w}(X)=Ad_{w}^{-1}(B(X))
if
w\in W_{0}\subset GL(X)(\mathbbm{k})
.

Theorem 8.9
.

Let
w\in W_{0}
and let
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=(\lambda^{(1)},\ldots,\lambda^{(k)})
be a simple representation of
T(X)
. Then

w\cdot{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}:=(\lambda^{(w^{-1}(1))},\ldots,\lambda^{(w^{-1}(k))})

is also a simple representation of
T(X)
(it lies in the same category since

, so
X_{i}\simeq X_{w(i)}
). This defines an action of
W_{0}
on the set of simple representations of
T(X)
.

(1)

If
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
is
w
-integrable then the set of weights of
L_{w}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
is
W_{0}
-stable.

(2)

We have

{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{w}=w\cdot{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},

that is,
L_{w}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\simeq L(w\cdot{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
.

(3)

Consequently,
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
is
w
-integrable if and only if for each
n=1,\ldots,p-1

|\lambda^{(w^{-1}(i))}|\geq|\lambda^{(w^{-1}(j))}|

for each
k_{1}+\ldots+k_{n-1}+1\leq i\leq j\leq k_{1}+\ldots+k_{n}
.

Proof.

(1)

The action of
W_{0}\subset GL(X)(\mathbbm{k})
on any

-module

sends a weight

to a weight
w\cdot{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
.

(2)

Let
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
be any weight of

then

{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\preceq_{w}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},

i.e.

|\lambda^{(w^{-1}(1))}|-|\mu^{(w^{-1}(1))}|\geq|\lambda^{(w^{-1}(2))}|-|\mu^{(w^{-1}(2))}|\geq\ldots\geq|\lambda^{(w^{-1}(k))}|-|\mu^{(w^{-1}(k))}|.

This condition is equivalent to saying that

Thus
w\cdot{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
is the highest weight in

with respect to the standard dominance order. Thus,
L_{w}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\simeq L(w\cdot{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
.

(3)

This condition follows immediately from part (

) and the integrability condition for the standard Borel subgroup (see Example

)

8.3.
Odd reflections

Similarly to the case of the super group
GL(m|n)
, not all Borel subgroups
B_{w}(X)
in

are conjugate to each other.

The group

is generated by
W_{0}=S_{k_{1}}\times\ldots\times S_{k_{p-1}}
and

for

, where

is the elementary transposition

.

That is,

is responsible for permuting isomorphic simple summands of

, while

It is enough, therefore, to answer Question
8.6
for simple odd reflections, i.e. for

.

Consider the subgroup

Suppose
X_{a_{j}}\simeq L_{m}
and
X_{a_{j}+1}\simeq L_{n}
. Since
s_{j}
is an “odd" reflection, we know that

.

Simple representations of
H_{j}
are of the form

\lambda^{(1)}\boxtimes\ldots\boxtimes\lambda^{(a_{j}-1)}\boxtimes L({\boldsymbol{\mu}})\boxtimes\lambda^{(a_{j}+2)}\boxtimes\ldots\boxtimes\lambda^{(k)},

where
\lambda^{(i)}
denotes the corresponding objects
V_{\lambda^{(i)}}
in
Ver_{p}(GL_{a})
when
X_{i}\simeq L_{a}
, and
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
, where
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
are admissible weights for
GL_{m}
and
GL_{n}
respectively. Representation
L({\boldsymbol{\mu}})
is the simple representation of
GL(X_{a_{j}}\oplus X_{a_{j}+1})=GL(L_{m}\oplus L_{n})
constructed in Section
7
.

Theorem 8.10
.

Let
a=k_{1}+\ldots+k_{j}
for some
j=1,\ldots,p-1
, let
s\in S_{k}
be the transposition
(a,~a+1)
.
Suppose as before that
X_{a}\simeq L_{m},X_{a+1}\simeq L_{n}
with
m<n
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=(\lambda^{(1)},\ldots,\lambda^{(k)})
be a simple,
s
-integrable representation of

, and let
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=(\lambda^{(a)},\lambda^{(a+1)})
.

Let
\sigma=(1~2)\in S_{2}
, suppose
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}=(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})
is such that

{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\sigma}={\boldsymbol{\nu}},

That is, we have an isomorphism of representations of
GL(L_{m}\oplus L_{n})
:

L_{\sigma}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})\simeq L({\boldsymbol{\nu}}).

Then

{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{s}=(\lambda^{(1)},\ldots,\lambda^{(a-1)},\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\lambda^{(a+2)},\ldots\lambda^{(k)}).

Therefore, the description of the bijection
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\leftrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{s}
for a simple odd reflection
s
boils down to the description of the bijection
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\leftrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\sigma}
on weights of
GL(L_{m}\oplus L_{n})
for
\sigma=(1~2)
.

Proof.

First, let us note that the generalized Verma module with highest weight
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
for
GL(L_{m}\oplus L_{n})
coincides with the corresponding Kac module, which we will denote by
K({\boldsymbol{\mu}})
. To avoid confusion, we will reserve the notation
M({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
for the generalized Verma module for
GL(X)
.

For each
i=1,\ldots,k
, denote the subobject
X_{i}\otimes X_{j}^{*}\subset{\mathfrak{gl}}(X)
by
\mathbf{E}_{i,j}
. Given any
GL(X)
-module
M
and any subobject
\mathbf{v}\subset M
denote by
\mathbf{E}_{i,j}\cdot\mathbf{v}
the image of
\mathbf{E}_{i,j}\otimes\mathbf{v}
under the action map

{\mathfrak{gl}}(X)\otimes M\to M.

Note that
\mathbf{E}_{a,a},\mathbf{E}_{a,a+1},\mathbf{E}_{a+1,a}
, and
\mathbf{E}_{a+1,a+1}
are naturally subobjects of
{\mathfrak{gl}}(X_{a}\oplus X_{a+1})={\mathfrak{gl}}(L_{m}\oplus L_{n})
.
As a representation of
T(L_{m}\oplus L_{n})=GL(L_{m})\times GL(L_{n})

K_{\sigma}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})\simeq Dist(\mathfrak{n}_{\sigma}^{-}(X_{a}\oplus X_{a+1}))\otimes{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=S(\mathbf{E_{a,a+1}})\otimes{\boldsymbol{\mu}}.

By construction,

\lambda^{(1)}\boxtimes\ldots\boxtimes\lambda^{(a_{j}-1)}\boxtimes K_{\sigma}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})\boxtimes\lambda^{(a_{j}+2)}\boxtimes\ldots\boxtimes\lambda^{(k)}

is a submodule of
\operatorname{Res}_{H_{j}}^{GL(X)}M_{s}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
, containing the
s
-highest weight
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
. For any weight
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
of
K_{\sigma}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})
, let us abuse the notation and denote the corresponding weight

\lambda^{(1)}\boxtimes\ldots\boxtimes\lambda^{(a_{j}-1)}\boxtimes{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\boxtimes\lambda^{(a_{j}+2)}\boxtimes\ldots\boxtimes\lambda^{(k)}

of
M_{s}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
simply by
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
.

Note that, being the maximal weight with respect to partial order
\preceq_{s}
, the subobject
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
of
M_{s}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
satisfies:

\mathbf{E}_{i,i+1}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=0\text{ for all }i\neq a-1,a,a+1,

\mathbf{E}_{a-1,a+1}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=0,

\mathbf{E}_{a,a+2}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=0,

\mathbf{E}_{a+1,a}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=0.

Let
N_{\sigma}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})
be the maximal submodule of
K_{\sigma}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})
. Let
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
be some maximal weight of
N_{\sigma}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})
with respect to partial order
\preceq_{\sigma}
. It means that

\mathbf{E}_{a+1,a}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=0.

Since
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\subset\mathbf{E}^{k}_{a,a+1}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
for some
k
, we get

\mathbf{E}_{i,i+1}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\subset\mathbf{E}_{i,i+1}\cdot\mathbf{E}^{k}_{a,a+1}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\mathbf{E}^{k}_{a,a+1}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{i,i+1}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=0,\text{ for all }i\neq a-1,a,a+1.

Moreover,

\mathbf{E}_{a-1,a+1}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\subset\mathbf{E}_{a-1,a+1}\cdot\mathbf{E}^{k}_{a,a+1}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\mathbf{E}^{k}_{a,a+1}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{a-1,a+1}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=0,

\mathbf{E}_{a,a+2}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\subset\mathbf{E}_{a,a+2}\cdot\mathbf{E}^{k}_{a,a+1}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\mathbf{E}_{a,a+1}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{a,a+2}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=0,

Therefore,
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\subset M_{s}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
must lie in the maximal submodule
J_{s}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
. We deduce that
N_{\sigma}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})\subset J_{s}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
.

Therefore, the
H_{j}
-module generated by the highest weight (with respect to
\preceq_{s}
) subobject
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\subset L_{s}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
is isomorphic to

\lambda^{(1)}\boxtimes\ldots\boxtimes\lambda^{(a_{j}-1)}\boxtimes L_{\sigma}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})\boxtimes\lambda^{(a_{j}+2)}\boxtimes\ldots\boxtimes\lambda^{(k)}\simeq\lambda^{(1)}\boxtimes\ldots\boxtimes\lambda^{(a_{j}-1)}\boxtimes L({\boldsymbol{\nu}})\boxtimes\lambda^{(a_{j}+2)}\boxtimes\ldots\boxtimes\lambda^{(k)}.

Consequently,
\lambda^{(1)}\boxtimes\ldots\boxtimes\lambda^{(a_{j}-1)}\boxtimes{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\boxtimes\lambda^{(a_{j}+2)}\boxtimes\ldots\boxtimes\lambda^{(k)}
is a weight in
L_{s}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
. Moreover, it is the highest weight with respect to the standard dominance order
\preceq=\preceq_{1}
.

We deduce that
L_{s}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\simeq L(\lambda^{(1)}\boxtimes\ldots\boxtimes\lambda^{(a_{j}-1)}\boxtimes{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\boxtimes\lambda^{(a_{j}+2)}\boxtimes\ldots\boxtimes\lambda^{(k)}).

9.
Representations of

In light of Theorem

, we will now focus on studying representations of

with
1\leq m<r\leq p-1

G=GL(L_{m}\oplus L_{r}),B=B(L_{m}\oplus L_{r}),T=T(L_{m}\oplus L_{r}),N^{\pm}=N^{\pm}(L_{m}\oplus L_{r})
.

9.1.
Super group scheme convention

Let us adopt a useful convention. Put
n=p-r
so that

L_{n}\simeq L_{p-1}\otimes L_{r}.

We will treat
G=GL(L_{m}\oplus L_{r})
as a super group scheme
GL(L_{m}|L_{n})
, where
L_{n}
is now “odd". The torus
T=T(L_{m}\oplus L_{r})=GL(L_{m})\times GL(L_{r})
is isomorphic to
GL(L_{m})\times GL(L_{n})
.

Remark 9.1
.

Note that even though the group schemes
GL(L_{r})
and
GL(L_{n})
are isomorphic, the natural homomorphisms from
\pi_{Ver_{p}}
to them are slightly different.

With respect to the natural map
\varepsilon:\pi_{Ver_{p}}\to GL(L_{n})
we have

\operatorname{Rep}_{Ver_{p}}(GL_{n},\varepsilon)\simeq Ver_{p}(GL_{n}).

Whereas if we take the map
\varepsilon^{\prime}:\pi_{Ver_{p}}\to GL(L_{r})\xrightarrow{\sim}GL(L_{n})
, we get a twisted category:
\operatorname{Rep}_{Ver_{p}}(GL_{n},\varepsilon^{\prime})
, which is isomorphic to the tensor subcategory of
Ver_{p}(GL_{n})\boxtimes sVec
generated by
V\boxtimes I
, where
V
is the
n
-dimensional object in
Ver_{p}(GL_{n})
coming from the tautological representation of
GL_{n}
and
I
is the
(0|1)
-dimensional super vector space.

Using level-rank duality (
4.6.1
)

Ver_{p}(GL_{r})\boxtimes sVec\xrightarrow{\sim}Ver_{p}(GL_{n})\boxtimes sVec,

we will regard the highest
GL(L_{m}|L_{n})
-weights as simple objects in the tensor subcategory of
Ver_{p}(GL_{m})\boxtimes Ver_{p}(GL_{n})\boxtimes sVec
generated by
V^{(m)}\boxtimes\mathbbm{1}\boxtimes\mathbbm{1}
and
\mathbbm{1}\boxtimes V^{(n)}\boxtimes I
. That is,
to each pair of admissible weights
\mu\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m},\nu\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
we assign a Kac module

K(\mu|\nu):=Dist(G)\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}\displaylimits_{Dist(B)}}V^{(m)}_{\mu}\boxtimes V^{(n)}_{\nu}\boxtimes I^{\otimes|\nu|};

and its simple quotient
L(\mu|\nu)
.

Notations:

•

Let us abuse the notation and denote the
T
-module
V^{(m)}_{\mu}\boxtimes V^{(n)}_{\nu}\boxtimes I^{\otimes|\nu|}
simply by
(\mu|\nu)
.

•

For an admissible weight
\mu=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{m})
let us denote by
\mu^{*}
both the weight
(-\mu_{n},\ldots,-\mu_{1})
and the object
(V^{(m)}_{\mu})^{*}=V^{(m)}_{\mu^{*}}
.

•

For any partition
\lambda
denote by

the transpose of
\lambda
.

•

When it is suitable, we will treat
(\mu|\nu)
as a vector
(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{m}|\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{n})
in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m+n}
, so that the sum of two weights is defined. To distinguish from the classical situation we will denote
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m+n}
by
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m|n}
.

•

We will henceforth refer to all elements of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m|n}
as weights, and weights
(\mu|\nu)
with both
\mu
and
\nu
admissible will be called admissible weights.

•

Fix standard bases
\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{m}
and
\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{n}
of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
and
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
correspondingly, so that
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m|n}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}{\mathbb{Z}}\epsilon_{i}\oplus\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbb{Z}}\delta_{j}
.

•

Weights
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i,j}=\epsilon_{i}-\delta_{j}
will be referred to as odd positive roots. We put
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\sum_{i\leq m}\sum_{j\leq n}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i,j}=(n,\ldots,n|-m,\ldots,-m)
.

•

Define

2{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{(m|n)}:=2(\rho^{(m)}|\rho^{(n)})-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=

=(m-1-n,m-3-n,\ldots,1-m-n|n-1+m,n-3+m\ldots,1-n+m).

The weight
2{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{(m|n)}
is the sum of all “even” positive roots minus the sum of all odd positive roots.

•

Define a symmetric form
\langle-,-\rangle
on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m|n}
via

\langle\epsilon_{i},\epsilon_{j}\rangle=\mathrm{\delta}_{i,j},~\langle\epsilon_{i},\delta_{j}\rangle=0,~\langle\delta_{i},\delta_{j}\rangle=-\mathrm{\delta}_{i,j}.

Recall that as a
Dist(N^{-})
-
Dist(T)
-bimodule

K(\mu|\nu)\simeq Dist(N^{-})\otimes(\mu|\nu)=S(\mathfrak{n}^{-})\otimes(\mu|\nu)=

=S((V^{(m)})^{*}\boxtimes V^{(n)}\boxtimes I)\otimes(\mu|\nu)\simeq\bigoplus_{\lambda}(\lambda^{*}|\lambda^{t})\otimes(\mu|\nu),

where the sum is taken over all partitions
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})
with
\lambda_{1}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{k}\geq 0
, such that
\lambda
is an admissible weight for
GL_{m}
and
\lambda^{t}
is an admissible weight for
GL_{n}
. That is, we ask that

(9.1.1)

k\leq m\text{ and }\lambda_{1}\leq n.

Note that our condition that
m<r=p-n
ensures that
\lambda
is an admissible weight for
GL_{m}
as
\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{m}\leq\lambda_{1}\leq n<p-m
; and that
\lambda^{t}
is an admissible weight for
GL_{n}
as
\lambda_{1}^{t}-\lambda_{n}^{t}\leq\lambda_{1}^{t}=k\leq m<p-n
.

This isomorphism induces a natural
{\mathbb{Z}}
-grading on
K(\mu|\nu)
with
K(\mu|\nu)^{q}=S^{q}(\mathfrak{n}^{-})\otimes(\mu|\nu)
. Moreover, it is easy to deduce from condition (
9.1.1
) that the top nonzero graded component is

K(\mu|\nu)^{m\cdot n}=S^{m\cdot n}(\mathfrak{n}^{-})\otimes(\mu|\nu)=(det^{-n}\boxtimes det^{m})\otimes(\mu|\nu).

So
(\widetilde{\mu}|\widetilde{\nu}):=(\mu|\nu)-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=(\mu_{1}-n,\ldots,\mu_{m}-n|\nu_{1}+m,\ldots,\nu_{n}+m)
is the lowest weight of
K(\mu|\nu)
.

The question of describing the bijection
(\mu|\nu)^{\sigma}\leftrightarrow(\mu|\nu)
for
\sigma=(1~2)\in S_{2}
is equivalent to the question of determining the lowest weight in
L(\mu|\nu)
. More precisely, if
(\mu^{\prime}|\nu^{\prime})
is the lowest weight in
L(\mu|\nu)
with respect to the standard dominance order (i.e.
|\mu^{\prime}|
is minimal among
\{|\alpha|,~\text{with }(\alpha|\beta)
a weight of

) then

L_{\sigma}(\mu^{\prime}|\nu^{\prime})\simeq L(\mu|\nu).

By necessity every submodule in
K(\mu|\nu)
contains the lowest weight subobject
(\widetilde{\mu}|\widetilde{\nu})
. In particular, it must have a simple submodule
L(\alpha,\beta)
(its socle), whose lowest weight is
(\widetilde{\mu},\widetilde{\nu})
. Thus,

L_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\mu}|\widetilde{\nu})\simeq L(\alpha,\beta),

i.e.

(\widetilde{\mu}|\widetilde{\nu})^{\sigma}=(\alpha|\beta).

The rest of this section will be dedicated to determining the socle of
K(\mu|\nu)
.

9.2.
Highest weight category

The statements and proofs in this section are almost identical to those made about
{\mathfrak{gl}}(m|n)
-modules in
[
Zou96
]
.

Let
m+n<p
.

Consider the locally finite poset
\Lambda
of all weights
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=(\mu|\nu)
with
\mu=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{m})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m},\nu=(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{n})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
satisfying

\mu_{1}\geq\ldots\geq\mu_{m},~\nu_{1}\geq\ldots\geq\nu_{m},\text{ and}

\mu_{1}\leq p-m,~\nu_{1}\leq p-n;

with the partial order

(\alpha|\beta)\preceq(\mu|\nu)\text{ if }|\alpha|+|\beta|=|\mu|+|\nu|\text{ and }|\alpha|\leq|\mu|.

Remark 9.2
.

We identify elements of
\Lambda
with simple representations of the torus
T\subset G=GL(L_{m}|L_{n})
.

The category
\mathcal{C}=\operatorname{Rep}_{Ver_{p}}(G,\varepsilon)
has simple objects
L({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})=L(\mu|\nu)
labeled by elements
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=(\mu|\nu)
of poset
\Lambda
. The Kac modules
K({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})=K(\mu|\nu)
play the role of standard objects in
\mathcal{C}
. We have for each
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\Lambda
a surjection

p_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}:K({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\to L({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})

satisfying
[\operatorname{Ker}p_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}:L({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})]\neq 0
only if
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\prec{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
. Note that
\operatorname{Hom}_{G}(K({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}),K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}))=\operatorname{Hom}_{B}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}},K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}))
and thus it is nonzero only if
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\preceq{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
, whereas
\mathrm{End}_{G}(K({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}))=\mathbbm{k}
.

Theorem 9.3
.

The category
\mathcal{C}
has enough projectives.

Proof.

For any
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\Lambda
define

\widetilde{P}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}):=Dist(G)\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}\displaylimits_{Dist(T)}}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}.

The triangular decomposition for
G
yields the following isomorphism of objects in
Ver_{p}
:

\widetilde{P}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\simeq Dist(N^{-})\otimes Dist(N^{+})\otimes{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\simeq S(\mathfrak{n}^{-})\otimes S(\mathfrak{n}^{+})\otimes{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}.

Both
S(\mathfrak{n}^{-})
and
S(\mathfrak{n}^{+})
have finite length in
Ver_{p}
(since in our case
\mathfrak{n}^{\pm}\simeq L_{m}\otimes L_{p-n}
does not contain a copy of
\mathbbm{1}
), which makes
\widetilde{P}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
an integrable
Dist(G)
-module.

By Frobenius reciprocity we get

\operatorname{Hom}_{G}(\widetilde{P}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}),-)=\operatorname{Hom}_{T}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}},\operatorname{Res}^{G}_{T}(-)).

As all representations of
T
are semisimple, we get that
\operatorname{Hom}_{G}(\widetilde{P}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}),-)
is exact, and thus
\widetilde{P}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
is projective.

Moreover, there is a natural surjective homomorphism
\widetilde{\pi}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}:\widetilde{P}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\to K({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
, and thus we get a surjection
\widetilde{P}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\to L({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
.

Let us denote by
P({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
the indecomposable summand of
\widetilde{P}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
, such that the homomorphism
\widetilde{\pi}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
factors through
P({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
:

\textstyle{\widetilde{P}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{~\widetilde{\pi}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}
\textstyle{K({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})}
\textstyle{P({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{~\pi_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}

Clearly,
P({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
is the projective cover of
L({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
.

Note that
\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{Hom}_{G}(\widetilde{P}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}),K({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}))=\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{Hom}_{T}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}},\operatorname{Res}^{G}_{T}(K({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})))=1
, so we deduce that the multiplicity of
P({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
in
\widetilde{P}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
is one. We get

(9.3.1)

\widetilde{P}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})=P({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\oplus\bigoplus_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\neq{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}m_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}P({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}).

As
Dist(G)
is a free right
Dist(B)
-module, the induction functor
Dist(G)\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}\displaylimits_{Dist(B)}}(-)
is exact. Since, as a
Dist(B)
-module,
Dist(B)\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}\displaylimits_{Dist(T)}}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
has a filtration by representations
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
of
Dist(B)
on which
Dist(N^{+})
acts trivially (i.e. those pulled back from a
T
-module
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
), we get that

\widetilde{P}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})=Dist(G)\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}\displaylimits_{Dist(B)}}(Dist(B)\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}\displaylimits_{Dist(T)}}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})

has a filtration by Kac modules
K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
with
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
a weight of
Dist(B)\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}\displaylimits_{Dist(T)}}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=S(\mathfrak{n}^{+})\otimes{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
.

By the above, the multiplicity
d_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}=[\widetilde{P}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}):K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})]
is equal to the multiplicity of
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
in the (semisimple finite length)
T
-module
S(\mathfrak{n}^{+})\otimes{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
, i.e.

(9.3.2)

[\widetilde{P}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}):K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})]=\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{Hom}_{T}(S(\mathfrak{n}^{+})\otimes{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})=\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{Hom}_{T}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}},S(\mathfrak{n}^{+})^{*}\otimes{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})=

=\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{Hom}_{T}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}},S(\mathfrak{n}^{-})\otimes{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})=\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{Hom}_{T}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}},\text{Res}^{G}_{T}K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}))=\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{Hom}_{G}(\widetilde{P}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}),K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})).

Consequently,
P({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
has a standard filtration by
K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
satisfying

(1)

[P({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}):K({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})]=1
,

(2)

[P({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}):K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})]\neq 0
only if
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\preceq{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
,

(3)

[P({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}):K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})]=\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{Hom}_{G}(P({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}),K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})).

Let us prove the last part by induction. We can assume that
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
is a weight of
S(\mathfrak{n}^{+})\otimes{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
(which is equivalent to saying that
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
is a weight of
S(\mathfrak{n}^{-})\otimes{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=\text{Res}^{G}_{T}K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
, since otherwise the statement holds trivially, as

[P({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}):K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})]=0=\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{Hom}_{G}(P({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}),K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}))=[K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}):L({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})].

By (
9.3.1
) and (
9.3.2
), we get

(9.3.3)

[\widetilde{P}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}):K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})]=[P({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}):K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})]+\sum m_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}[P({\boldsymbol{\mu}}):K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})]=

=\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{Hom}(P({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}),K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}))+\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\neq{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}m_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}\cdot\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{Hom}(P({\boldsymbol{\mu}}),K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}))

Clearly,
m_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}=[\widetilde{P}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}):P({\boldsymbol{\mu}})]\leq d_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}=[\widetilde{P}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}):K({\boldsymbol{\mu}})]
and thus it is nonzero only if
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
is a weight in
S(\mathfrak{n}^{+})\otimes{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
. So, only the weights
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
in the range
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\preceq{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\preceq{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
are contributing non-trivially to the sum above.

Let us fix
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
and assume that

[P({\boldsymbol{\mu}}):K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})]=\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{Hom}_{G}(P({\boldsymbol{\mu}}),K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}))

for all weights
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
satisfying

{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\prec{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\preceq{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},

with the base case
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}={\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
, for which we have

[P({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}):K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})]=1=\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{Hom}_{G}(P({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})).

We can now prove it for
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}={\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
using equation (
9.3.3
).

Corollary 9.4
.

\mathcal{C}
is a
highest weight category
in the sense of Cline, Parshall, Scott
[
CPS88
]
.

Corollary 9.5

(BGG reciprocity)
.

For any
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\Lambda
we get

[P({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}):K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})]=[K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}):L({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})].

Proof.

We get

[K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}):L({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})]=\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{Hom}_{G}(P({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}),K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}))=[P({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}):K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})].

Corollary 9.6
.

We have

Ext^{i}(K({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}),K({\boldsymbol{\mu}}))\neq 0\text{ for some }i>0

only if
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\prec{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
.

Proof.

Recall that

[P({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}):K({\boldsymbol{\mu}})]\neq 0~\Rightarrow~{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\preceq{\boldsymbol{\mu}},

with
[P({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}):K({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})]=1
.

The kernel
N_{1}
of the projection
P({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\to K({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
has standard filtration by
K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
’s with
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in A_{1},
where
A_{1}
is some finite multiset of weights satisfying
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\succ{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
for all
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in A_{1}
.
N_{1}
can then be covered by
P_{1}=\bigoplus_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in A_{1}}P({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
, and the kernel
N_{2}
of the map
P_{1}\to N_{1}
has standard filtration by
K({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
’s with
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in A_{2}
for some finite multiset of weights
A_{2}
. For each weight
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}
in
A_{2}
there must exist a weight
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}\in A_{1}
so that
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}\succeq{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}
, and consequently
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}\succ{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
. We then put

9.3.1.
The Shapovalov form

Consider the top nonzero graded component
K(\mu|\nu)^{mn}=S^{mn}(\mathfrak{n}^{-})\otimes(\mu|\nu)=(\widetilde{\mu}|\widetilde{\nu})
of
K(\mu|\nu)
. The action map restricted to
S^{mn}(\mathfrak{n}^{+})
sends
K(\mu|\nu)^{mn}
to the highest weight component
K(\mu|\nu)^{0}=(\mu|\nu)
. Under the identification
\mathfrak{n}^{+}=(\mathfrak{n}^{-})^{*}
we get the dualized map:

(\widetilde{\mu},\widetilde{\nu})=S^{mn}(\mathfrak{n}^{-})\otimes(\mu|\nu)\to S^{mn}(\mathfrak{n}^{-})\otimes(\mu|\nu)=(\widetilde{\mu},\widetilde{\nu}),

which is
T
-equivariant, and thus must be multiplication by some constant
Sh(\mu|\nu)
. We claim that this constant depends polynomially on the components of
\mu
and
\nu
.

Remark 9.12
.

The constant
Sh(\mu|\nu)
is a shadow of the actual Shapovalov form on
K(\mu|\nu)
. Namely, using the same logic, for any graded component

K(\mu|\nu)^{k}=S^{k}(\mathfrak{n}^{-})\otimes(\mu|\nu)

we get a
T
-equivariant map

Sh^{k}:K(\mu|\nu)^{k}\to K(\mu|\nu)^{k},

obtained from dualizing the action map

S^{k}(\mathfrak{n}^{+})\otimes S^{k}(\mathfrak{n}^{-})\otimes(\mu|\nu)\to(\mu|\nu).

Now for each weight
(\alpha|\beta)
with
|\alpha|+|\beta|=|\mu|+|\nu|
and
|\alpha|=|\mu|-k
we define a form
Sh_{\mu,\nu}^{\alpha,\beta}
on the finite-dimensional vector space

H^{\alpha,\beta}_{\mu,\nu}:=\operatorname{Hom}_{T}((\alpha|\beta),K(\mu|\nu)^{k})

as follows:

Sh_{\mu,\nu}^{\alpha,\beta}(f,g)=(Sh^{k}\circ f,g),

where
(-,-)
is a scalar product on
H_{\mu,\nu}^{\alpha,\beta}
defined with respect to any choice of basis.

The nullity of the determinant of the form
Sh_{\mu,\nu}^{\alpha,\beta}
determines the multiplicity of
(\alpha|\beta)
in the maximal proper submodule
J(\mu|\nu)
of
K(\mu|\nu)
.

If the multiplicity of weight
(\alpha|\beta)
in
K(\mu|\nu)
is
1
then it lies in the maximal proper submodule
J(\mu|\nu)
if and only if the map

(\alpha|\beta)\hookrightarrow S^{k}(\mathfrak{n}^{-})\otimes(\mu|\nu)\xrightarrow{Sh^{k}}(\alpha|\beta)\subset S^{k}(\mathfrak{n}^{-})\otimes(\mu|\nu)

is zero upon restriction to the weight subspace
(\alpha|\beta)
.

9.3.2.
The classical case

In fact, the construction above is defined categorically, so it may be applied to representations of the super group

of the even part
