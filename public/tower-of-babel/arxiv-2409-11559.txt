Abstract
. We study a class of combinatorial objects that we call “decorated trees”. These consist of vertices, arrows and edges, where each edge is decorated by two integers (one near each of its endpoints), each arrow is decorated by an integer, and the decorations are required to satisfy certain conditions. The class of decorated trees includes different types of trees used in algebraic geometry, such as the Eisenbud and Neumann diagrams for links of singularities and the Neumann diagrams for links at infinity of algebraic plane curves. By purely combinatorial means, we recover some formulae that were previously understood to be “topological”. In this way, we extend the generality of those formulae and show that they are in fact “combinatorial”.

Introduction

We study a class of combinatorial objects that we call “decorated trees”.
These consist of vertices, arrows and edges,
where each edge is decorated by two integers (one near each of its endpoints), each arrow is decorated by an integer,
and the decorations are required to satisfy certain conditions.
The class of decorated trees includes different types of trees used in algebraic geometry,
such as the Eisenbud and Neumann diagrams for links of singularities
[
EN85
]

and the Neumann diagrams for links at infinity of algebraic plane curves
[
Neu89
]
.
It also includes the larger set of trees that arise in the study of numerical semigroups, in particular in the calculation of the Frobenius number of
complete intersection numerical semigroups.
By purely combinatorial means, we recover some formulae that were previously understood to be “topological”.
In this way, we extend the generality of those formulae and show that they are in fact “combinatorial”.
This article is used in
[
ACN
]
, as well as in work in preparation
[
CND
]
and
[
CN
]
.

The first two sections are mostly dedicated to definitions.
In the first section we define what is a decorated tree
\mathcal{T}
,
its multiplicity
M(\mathcal{T})\in\mathbb{Z}
and a number
F(\mathcal{T})\in\mathbb{Z}

(in the special case where all arrows of
\mathcal{T}
are decorated by
(0)
or
(1)
,
F(\mathcal{T})
is simply the number of arrows in
\mathcal{T}
decorated with
(1)
).
Then we describe some operations of simplification that do not change the values of
M(\mathcal{T})
and
F(\mathcal{T})

(the simpler trees produced by these operations are called “minimal trees” in the context of singularities).
The second section explains Splitting and ENsplitting, which are operations for breaking a tree into two pieces.
The splitting operations allow the use of induction and are very useful tools.

The remaining sections consider formulae that are known for singularities and algebraic curves,
and prove them in a purely combinatorial way and for more general trees.

Section
3
proves that
M(\mathcal{T})+F(\mathcal{T})
is even.
This fundamental result allows us to define
(in section
4
) the genus and the delta-invariant of a decorated tree
\mathcal{T}
by

\textstyle g(\mathcal{T})=\frac{1}{2}(2-M(\mathcal{T})-F(\mathcal{T}))\quad\text{and}\quad\textstyle\delta(\mathcal{T})=\frac{1}{2}(F(\mathcal{T})-M(\mathcal{T})).

These two invariants are important not only in the case of complex plane curves,
but also for plane curves in any characteristic.
Thm
3.1
and Prop.
4.13
(a)
are used in particular in
[
ACN
]
to prove that if
f(x,y)\in K[[x,y]]
with

of arbitrary characteristic, then the
\delta
-invariant of its Newton tree is equal to the
\delta
-invariant of the singularity.
Moreover, the invariants
g(\mathcal{T})
and
\delta(\mathcal{T})
are relevant in the study of numerical semigroups.
Section 4 proves formulae that compute these invariants in terms of subtrees with fewer arrows.

By analogy to the Alexander polynomial of a fibered multilink
[

Section

defines and studies the Alexander polynomial

of
\mathcal{T}

\mathcal{T}

We show, among other things, that

Section

proves a genus formula for a large class of decorated trees,

In addition to giving the results mentioned in the above paragraphs,

1.1

In this work, a

are called

If

If

If

A

Given

Suppose that

A graph

If

Definition 1.2
.

A

is a
5

\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{E},f,q)

(i)

(ii)

(iii)

(iv)

(v)

if
e\in\mathcal{E}
,
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
and

;

(vi)

for every

and every choice of distinct edges

(so each edge has two decorations, one near each of its endpoints).
If

then the integer

Define

An edge incident to an element of

The notations of

denotes the valency of

Definition 1.4
.

Let
\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{E},f,q)\in\operatorname{{\rm\bf DT}}
.
Given
x\in\mathcal{V}\cup\mathcal{A}
, let
E_{x}
temporarily denote the set of all edges incident to
x
.
For each
e\in E_{x}
, define

Q(e,x)=\prod_{e^{\prime}\in E_{x}\setminus\{e\}}q(e^{\prime},x)
.

Here and throughout this paper, empty products are equal to
1
.

This defines a map

Q:\big\{\,(e,x)\in\mathcal{E}\times(\mathcal{V}\cup\mathcal{A})\,\mid\,x\in e\,\big\}\to\mathbb{Z}.

Note that the maps
q
and

have the same domain and same codomain, and that
Q
is determined by
q
.
Also note that if

satisfies
\delta_{x}=1
, and if
e
is the edge incident to
x
, then
Q(e,x)=1
because
Q(e,x)
is an empty product.

For instance, consider the edges
e=\{u,v\}
,
e^{\prime}=\{v,\alpha_{1}\}
and

in the tree
\mathcal{T}

.
Also,

.

Let

(i)

EN-splitting

Eisenbud and Neumann have an operation that they call “splicing”, which is a way to glue trees together in order to form more complicated trees.
We shall now define an operation that is the inverse of splicing (it cuts a tree into two pieces).
We call it the Eisenbud-Neumann splitting, or EN-splitting, denoted “
\operatorname{{\rm ENSpl}}
”.

3.

The EN-splitting is used in the proof of the following fundamental result.

If

Proof.

We use the following temporary notations:

Suppose that

Then we can choose an edge

\Phi(\mathcal{T})\equiv\Phi(\mathcal{T}_{1})+\Phi(\mathcal{T}_{2})
,
and it is clear that
\|\mathcal{T}_{i}\|<\|\mathcal{T}\|
for

So, in order to prove the Theorem, it suffices to verify that

\Phi(\mathcal{T})\equiv 0
for every
\mathcal{T}\in\operatorname{{\rm\bf DT}}

.
Assume that

Then
\mathcal{T}
is

4.
Genus and
\delta

This section proves formulae that compute
g(\mathcal{T})
and
\delta(\mathcal{T})
in terms of subtrees with fewer arrows.
Proposition
4.13
treats the special case where all the arrows are decorated with
(0)
or
(1)
,
and Theorem
4.19
gives the general case.
The section concludes with Examples
4.20
and
4.21
, which illustrate these formulae.

Notation 4.1
.

Recall from the introduction that, given
\mathcal{T}\in\operatorname{{\rm\bf DT}}
, we define

\textstyle g(\mathcal{T})=\frac{1}{2}(2-M(\mathcal{T})-F(\mathcal{T}))\quad\text{and}\quad\textstyle\delta(\mathcal{T})=\frac{1}{2}(F(\mathcal{T})-M(\mathcal{T})).

Note that
g(\mathcal{T}),\delta(\mathcal{T})\in\mathbb{Z}
by Theorem
3.1
.
We refer to these integers as the
genus
and
\delta
-invariant
of
\mathcal{T}
, respectively.

We shall now define (see
4.2
) the class
\operatorname{{\rm\bf DT}_{\text{\rm r}}}
of rooted decorated trees and the class
\operatorname{{\rm\bf DT}_{\text{\rm pr}}}
of pseudo-rooted decorated trees,
where
\operatorname{{\rm\bf DT}_{\text{\rm r}}}\subset\operatorname{{\rm\bf DT}_{\text{\rm pr}}}
.
In fact we are mostly interested in
\operatorname{{\rm\bf DT}_{\text{\rm r}}}
, and we introduce the larger class
\operatorname{{\rm\bf DT}_{\text{\rm pr}}}

because it is sometimes necessary to use the theory of pseudo-rooted trees for proving results about rooted trees.
Given
\mathcal{T}\in\operatorname{{\rm\bf DT}_{\text{\rm pr}}}
and a nonempty subset
X
of
\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{0}
, an element
\mathcal{T}_{X}
of
\operatorname{{\rm\bf DT}_{\text{\rm pr}}}
is defined in
4.3
.
Then the remainder of the section is devoted to finding formulae that,
given
\mathcal{T}\in\operatorname{{\rm\bf DT}_{\text{\rm pr}}}
and a partition
\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
of
\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{0}
,
express
M(\mathcal{T})
(resp.
g(\mathcal{T})
,
\delta(\mathcal{T})
)
in terms of the numbers
M(\mathcal{T}_{X_{i}})
(resp.
g(\mathcal{T}_{X_{i}})
,
\delta(\mathcal{T}_{X_{i}})
).

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{E},f,q)\in\operatorname{{\rm\bf DT}}
.
A

for every

\gamma_{v_{0},v}

.

\mathcal{N}(v,e)=\big\{\,w\in\mathcal{N}\,\mid\,\text{$e$ is in $\gamma_{v,w}$}\,\big\}

and
c(v,e)=\gcd\big\{\,\rho_{w}\,\mid\,w\in\mathcal{N}(v,e)\,\big\}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{E},f,q)\in\operatorname{{\rm\bf DT}}
,
\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{T})
and
E=E(\mathcal{T})
.

(a)

If
e\in\mathcal{E}
is incident to
v\in\mathcal{V}
then
\rho_{v}\mid\rho_{e}\mid N_{v}
.

(b)

If
e\in\mathcal{E}
has at least one endpoint in
\mathcal{N}
then
\rho_{e}>0
.

(c)

For each
v\in\mathcal{N}
,
\rho_{v}>0
.

(d)

If
e=\{v,w\}\in E
then
c(v,e),c(w,e)>0
,
c(v,e)\mid\rho_{w}\mid\rho_{e}
and
c(w,e)\mid\rho_{v}\mid\rho_{e}
.

Proof.

(a) Since
N_{v}=Q(e,v)p(v,e)+q(e,v)p^{*}(v,e)
and
\rho_{e}
divides both
p(v,e)
and
p^{*}(v,e)
, we have
\rho_{e}\mid N_{v}
.
The definition of
\rho_{v}
immediately implies that
\rho_{v}\mid\rho_{e}
, so (a) follows.

(b) By assumption,
e
is incident to some
v\in\mathcal{N}
.
Since
N_{v}>0
and (by (a))
\rho_{e}\mid N_{v}
, we have
\rho_{e}\neq 0
, i.e.,
\rho_{e}>0
.

(c) Let
v\in\mathcal{N}
.
Clearly,
\mathcal{E}_{v}\neq\varnothing
; choose
e\in\mathcal{E}_{v}
, then
\rho_{e}>0
by (b) and
\rho_{v}\mid\rho_{e}
by (a), so
\rho_{v}\neq 0
and hence
\rho_{v}>0
.

(d) Let
e=\{v,w\}\in E
. We have
w\in\mathcal{N}(v,e)
, so
c(v,e)\mid\rho_{w}
.
Part (a) gives
\rho_{w}\mid\rho_{e}
, so
c(v,e)\mid\rho_{w}\mid\rho_{e}
. Part (b) gives
\rho_{e}>0
, so
c(v,e)>0
.
By symmetry,
c(w,e)>0
and
c(w,e)\mid\rho_{v}\mid\rho_{e}
.
∎

Definition 5.3
.

We say that a tree
\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{E},f,q)\in\operatorname{{\rm\bf DT}}
is
positive
if

\mathcal{V}\neq\varnothing
and
N_{x}>0
for all
x\in\mathcal{V}\cup\mathcal{A}_{0}
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{E},f,q)
be a positive tree.

(a)

\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{0}\neq\varnothing
and
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{T})=\mathcal{V}

(b)

E(\mathcal{T})=\big\{\,e\in\mathcal{E}\,\mid\,\text{both endpoints of $e$ are in $\mathcal{V}$}\,\big\}

(c)

\omega(\mathcal{T})=\gcd\big\{\,\rho_{v}\,\mid\,v\in\mathcal{V}\,\big\}\geq 1

Proof.

(a) and (b) are clear; we prove (c). Let
\rho=\gcd\big\{\,\rho_{v}\,\mid\,v\in\mathcal{V}\,\big\}
.
Note that
\omega(\mathcal{T})\geq 1
, because
\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{0}\neq\varnothing
.
We have
\omega(\mathcal{T})\mid p(x,e)
for every choice of
x\in\mathcal{V}\cup\mathcal{A}
and
e\in\mathcal{E}
such that
e
is incident to
x
.
So
\omega(\mathcal{T})\mid\rho_{e}
for all
e\in\mathcal{E}
, and consequently

\omega(\mathcal{T})\mid\rho_{v}
for all
v\in\mathcal{V}
. Thus,
\omega(\mathcal{T})\mid\rho
.
Conversely, consider
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{0}
. Let
e=\{v,\alpha\}
be the unique edge incident to
\alpha
.
Then
\rho_{e}\mid f(\alpha)
is clear; since
v\in\mathcal{V}
, we have
\rho\mid\rho_{v}\mid\rho_{e}\mid f(\alpha)
.
This being true for each
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{0}
, we get
\rho\mid\omega(\mathcal{T})
, so
\rho=\omega(\mathcal{T})
.
∎

Definition 5.5
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a positive tree. Let

and recall that

E=\big\{\,e\in\mathcal{E}\,\mid\,\text{both endpoints of $e$ are in $\mathcal{V}$}\,\big\}.

Lemma

implies that
(\rho_{v})_{v\in\mathcal{V}}

is incident to

Define

.
Proposition

is in fact a product of cyclotomic polynomials.

Remark 5.6
.

If

If

We will show in Prop.

More precisely, we will show that

Notation 5.7
.

Let

.
If

.

5.8
.

Let

be the set of monic polynomials

such that every

Let

The following are easily verified.

(a)

{\mathbb{M}_{\text{\rm pol}}}

.

(b)

If

and

is the gcd of

and

\mathbb{Q}[t]
, where we choose

then

and

belong to
{\mathbb{M}_{\text{\rm pol}}}
.

(c)

{\mathbb{M}}
is a subgroup of the multiplicative group

.

(d)

If

and

in

(by which we mean that there exists

.

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
(V,E)
be a tree,
V^{\prime}
a subset of
V
and
E^{\prime}
the set of edges
e\in E
that are incident to at least one element of
V^{\prime}
.
If
V^{\prime}\neq V
then
|V^{\prime}|\leq|E^{\prime}|
.

Proof.

Define

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle=\big\{\,e\in E\,\mid\,\text{exactly one endpoint of $e$ is in $V^{\prime}$}\,\big\},

\displaystyle E_{2}

\displaystyle=\big\{\,e\in E\,\mid\,\text{both endpoints of $e$ are in $V^{\prime}$}\,\big\}

and note that
E^{\prime}=E_{1}\cup E_{2}
and
E_{1}\cap E_{2}=\varnothing
.
Consider the subgraph
F=(V^{\prime},E_{2})
of the tree
(V,E)
.
As
F
is a forest, we have
|V^{\prime}|-|E_{2}|=|\mathscr{C}|
, where
\mathscr{C}
denotes the set of connected components of

.
It follows that
|V^{\prime}|-|E^{\prime}|=|\mathscr{C}|-|E_{1}|
; so, to prove the Lemma, it suffices to show that
|E_{1}|\geq|\mathscr{C}|
.

For each
e\in E_{1}
, there is exactly one
C_{e}\in\mathscr{C}
such that
e
has an endpoint in
C_{e}
.
We claim that the map
f:E_{1}\to\mathscr{C}
,

, is surjective.
Indeed, let
C\in\mathscr{C}
. Choose
v\in V\setminus V^{\prime}
and let
\gamma=(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})
be the shortest path in
(V,E)
such that
x_{0}=v
and
x_{n}
is in
C
.
Observe that

and that if
x_{n-1}\in V^{\prime}
then
x_{n-1}
is in
C
, contradicting the minimality of
\gamma
; so
x_{n-1}\notin V^{\prime}

and consequently the edge

belongs to

and satisfies

. So

is surjective.
This implies that
|E_{1}|\geq|\mathscr{C}|
, which proves the Lemma.
∎

Lemma 5.10
.

Given a tree
(V,E)
such that

.

.

Proof.

Given
s\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
and
f\in{\mathbb{M}_{\text{\rm pol}}}
, let
V_{s}(f)
be the largest

such that
\Phi_{s}^{n}
divides

.
Let

The assumption

We have to show that

Let

.

We have

If

Proposition 5.11
.

If

\displaystyle x_{v}+x_{v}^{*}

\displaystyle=Q_{v}\left(\frac{Q_{\bar{v}}}{y_{v}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{x_{\bar{v}}+x^{*}_{\bar{v}}}{Q_{\bar{v}}}\right)\ \ \text{for all $v\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{\eta\}$ not adjacent to $\eta$.}

By (
43
) and (
45
), if
x^{*}_{\bar{v}}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
Q_{\bar{v}}\mid(x_{\bar{v}}+x^{*}_{\bar{v}})

then
x^{*}_{v}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
Q_{v}\mid(x_{v}+x^{*}_{v})
.
It follows by induction that there is a unique good family
\big(x_{v}^{*}\big)_{v\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{\eta\}}
,
and that this family is such that
Q_{v}\mid(x_{v}+x^{*}_{v})
for all
v\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{\eta\}
.
Since
\gcd(x_{v},Q_{v})=1
and
Q_{v}\mid(x_{v}+x^{*}_{v})
, we have
\gcd(x^{*}_{v},Q_{v})=1
.
This guarantees that if we set
q^{*}(e_{v},v)=x_{v}^{*}
for each
v\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{\eta\}
then,
in the tree
\mathcal{T}^{(\eta)}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{E},f,q^{*})
,
the edge decorations near any given vertex are pairwise relatively prime; so
\mathcal{T}^{(\eta)}\in\operatorname{{\rm\bf DT}}
.
This proves the lemma.
∎

Theorem 6.12
.

Let
\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{E},f,q,v_{0})\in\operatorname{{\rm\bf DT}_{\text{\rm r}}}
be such that
f(\alpha)\in\{0,1\}
for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
.
Let
n=\delta_{v_{0}}
, assume that
n\geq 1
,
and consider
\mathcal{T}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{T}_{n}\in\operatorname{{\rm\bf DT}}
as in Def.
6.10
.
Let
d=\deg(\mathcal{T})
. Then

g(\mathcal{T})=\frac{(d-1)(d-2)}{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta(\mathcal{T}_{i})\,.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
2g(\mathcal{T})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}2\delta(\mathcal{T}_{i})=(d-1)(d-2)
.
We have
g(\mathcal{T})=\frac{1}{2}(2-M(\mathcal{T})-F(\mathcal{T}))
and
\delta(\mathcal{T}_{i})=\frac{1}{2}(F(\mathcal{T}_{i})-M(\mathcal{T}_{i}))
by definition,
and the hypothesis
\operatorname{{\rm im}}(f)\subseteq\{0,1\}
implies that
F(\mathcal{T})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}F(\mathcal{T}_{i})
;
so
2g(\mathcal{T})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}2\delta(\mathcal{T}_{i})=2-M(\mathcal{T})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}M(\mathcal{T}_{i})

(47)

2-M(\mathcal{T})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}M(\mathcal{T}_{i})=(d-1)(d-2).

Let

(\mathcal{V}\cup\mathcal{A}_{0})\setminus\{v_{0}\}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
is a disjoint union.
Since
N_{v_{0}}(\delta_{v_{0}}-2)=d(n-2)
, we have

(48)

-M(\mathcal{T})=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}\cup\mathcal{A}_{0}}N_{x}(\delta_{x}-2)=d(n-2)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{x\in X_{i}}N_{x}(\delta_{x}-2).

Let
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.
For each
x\in X_{i}
we have
N_{x}=\varphi(v_{0},x)d-N^{(i)}_{x}
by Lemma
6.11
, so

(49)

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X_{i}}N_{x}(\delta_{x}-2)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in X_{i}}(\varphi(v_{0},x)d-N^{(i)}_{x})(\delta_{x}-2)

\displaystyle=d\sum_{x\in X_{i}}\varphi(v_{0},x)(\delta_{x}-2)-\sum_{x\in X_{i}}N^{(i)}_{x}(\delta_{x}-2).

d_{i}=p(v_{0},e_{i})

X_{i}=Y(v_{0},e_{i})

6.10
for

6.6
