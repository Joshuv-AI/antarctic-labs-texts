The inhibitory effects of Melicope pahangenesis T. G. Hartley extract on LPS-induced acute lung injury
Authors: Hee Jung Choi, Min Jeong Kim, Jin Hyub Paik, Dong Joon Kim, Sung Keun Jung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Since COVID-19 pandemic, the protection of lung health against infection with various pathogens has been emphasized. Acute lung injury (ALI) is a severe respiratory disease that causes sudden loss of lung function via pathogenic invasion. Although diverse anti-inflammatory materials have been investigated, the preventive efficacy remains limited. The purpose of this study is to investigate the preventive effects of Melicope pahangenesis T. G. Hartley extract (MPE) on lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced ALI. In vivo, oral MPE administration inhibits LPS-induced intestinal thickening, hyperplasia, mucus production, alveolar macrophage recruitment, and IL-1β expression in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid. The production of IL-6 and expression of iNOS and COX-2 were strongly suppressed. In mechanism studies, MPE inhibited phosphorylation of p65, IκB kinase, p38, and JNK1/2 and nuclear translocation of p65. Furthermore, MPE reduced reactive oxygen species and induced radical scavenging activities. Taken together, MPE consistently exerted anti-inflammatory effects in vitro and in vivo.

Introduction

Acute lung injury (ALI), a syndrome involving acute inflammation caused by microbial infections, disrupts the lungs’ endothelial and epithelial barriers, leading to acute respiratory failure (Mowery et al., 2020). ALI often progresses to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), which is a life-threatening lung injury caused by sepsis and pneumonia (Lee et al., 2018; Rubenfeld et al., 2005). ARDS caused 67–85% mortality in intensive care units in the early stages of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) outbreak (Yang et al., 2020). Patients with ALI are histologically characterized by an acute inflammatory response and exhibit interstitial thickening, neutrophil accumulation in alveolar spaces, and the collapse of lung structure (Matute-Bello et al., 2011). Therefore, several researchers have investigated the molecular mechanisms of preventive materials against ALI using various in vivo models (Matute-Bello et al., 2011). Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) is an endotoxin that is present in the outer-most layer of Gram-negative bacteria. It is reportedly an ALI inducer that causes lung injury by activating M1 alveolar macrophages (AMs) in the alveolar lumen (Alam et al., 2022), thereby stimulating them to secrete inflammatory cytokines and recruit immune cells in the lungs (Wang et al., 2021).

Nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) and activator protein-1 (AP-1)—integral to the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway—are transcription factors that regulate the release of proinflammatory cytokines, leading to inflammation-induced lung injury (Granet et al., 2004; Liu et al., 2017). In particular, NF-κB is a key regulator of M1 macrophages and an essential transcriptional factor of inflammatory genes, including interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) (Wang et al., 2014), and these inflammatory genes are heavily involved in ALI symptoms (Mokra and Kosutova, 2015). The abnormal secretion of IL-1β from macrophages stimulates the release of platelet-derived growth factor and transforming growth factor beta 1, causing ARDS-associated lung fibrosis through the activation of fibrosis-related proteins (Deng et al., 2015), and elicits progressive fibrotic changes, thereby inducing ALI in vivo (Kolb et al., 2001). Inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), a key signaling regulator of the inflammatory response, can be produced by M1 macrophages and is also a critical factor for macrophage activation (Golden et al., 2021). iNOS inhibition attenuates the inflammatory response in an LPS-induced ALI model (Guo et al., 2016). The LPS-induced expression of IL-1β, iNOS, and COX-2 as well as the subsequent release of NO and IL-1β into culture medium can be inhibited by reducing NF-κB and AP-1 levels (Choi et al., 2009). In addition, patients with ALI/ARDS (Parsons et al., 2005) exhibit elevated IL-6 expression levels in their plasma, while those with ARDS (Pugin et al., 1996) display increased bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) levels. NF-κB and AP-1 are positive regulators of IL-6 expression (Herold et al., 2011). Therefore, they are potential preventive/therapeutic targets in ALI/ARDS. Numerous researchers have investigated the development of protective materials against ALI by targeting NF-kB and AP-1 (Peng et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2017, 2020).

LPS-induced excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production is a critical factor of inflammatory responses and key symptom of ALI (Zhao et al., 2017). Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2), as a transcriptional factor, binds to antioxidant response elements (AREs) and protects against oxidative stress in cells and tissues (Ngo and Duennwald, 2022). Although prolonging NRF2 activation promotes cancer development, short-term NRF2 activation prevents inflammation and tissue injury (Wang et al., 2023). Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1) acts as a negative regulator of NRF2 activation by binding to it, leading to NRF2 protein degradation (Cuadrado et al., 2019). Therefore, optimized NRF2 activation is an important preventive and therapeutic strategy against inflammation and ALI.

Melicope pahangenesis T. G. Hartley (MP) is a plant belonging to the Rutaceae family, and its pharmaceutical and nutraceutical capabilities are yet to be elucidated. Rutaceae are well known for their fragrance, and their leaves are used as an insect repellent, an antipyretic, and a treatment for skin diseases (Alam et al., 2022).

In this study, we hypothesized that MP extract (MPE) significantly alleviates LPS-induced ALI symptoms by inhibiting NF-κB and AP-1-mediated inflammatory responses in vitro and in vivo. Herein, we confirm that MPE is a potent material that augments the efficacy of ALI prevention.

Materials and methods

Materials

Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) was purchased from HyClone (Logan, UT, USA). Lipopolysaccharide (Escherichia coli O127:B8), sulfanilamide (≥ 99%), phosphoric acid, potassium persulfate, and 2,2-diphenyl-1-picryl-hydrazyl-hydrate (DPPH) were procured from Sigma Aldrich Co. (St. Louis, MO, USA). N1-(naphthalene-1-yl) ethane-1,2-diamine dihydrochloride was acquired from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co. (Tokyo, Japan). In addition, 2,2-azinobis-(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) was secured from Roche Holding AG (Basel, Switzerland). Primary antibodies used for western blotting included iNOS (#13,120), COX-2 (#12,282), phospho-NF-κB p65 (#3033), NF-κB p65 (#8242), phospho-IKKα/β (#2697), IKKα (#61,294), phospho-SAPK/JNK (#4668), SAPK/JNK (#9252), phospho-p38 MAPK (#4511), p38 MAPK (#8690), α/β-tubulin (#2148), HO-1 (#43,966), KEAP1 (#8047), NRF2 (#12,721), and PCNA (#2586), all obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). And Lamin B1 (ab16048) was purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, UK).

MPE preparation

MP samples obtained from the Hòa Ninh community of Hòa Vang district, Đà Nẵng City, Vietnam, were identified and collected by Dr. Tran The Bach from the Institute of Ecology and Biological Resources (Hanoi, Vietnam). The voucher specimen recorded as VK 2493 was deposited at the Korea Research Institute of Bioscience and Biotechnology herbarium (Daejeon, Korea). For in vitro analysis, 104 g of dried MP leaves and branches were extracted with methanol, and an extraction yield of 8% was confirmed. For in vivo analysis, 15 g of dried MP leaves and branches were extracted with 150 mL of ethanol, filtered, and concentrated under reduced pressure at 55 °C using a rotary evaporator (R-215, BUCHI). The freeze-dried ethanol extract was diluted in distilled water (DW) and subjected to in vivo experiments.

Cell culture and viability

Murine macrophage RAW 264.7 cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (HyClone, Logan, UT, USA) and incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The cells were treated with MPE (6.25, 12.5, 25, and 50 μg/mL) for 24 h, and 10 μL of 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) solution was subsequently added to each well and the resulting mixture incubated for 2 h. Afterwards, 80 μL of supernatant was removed from each well and diluted with 100 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Finally, absorbance was measured at 595 nm using a SpectraMax iD3 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, California, USA).

Nitric oxide (NO) production assay

RAW 264.7 cells were cultured in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/mL for the nitrite assay. Cells previously pretreated with MPE (25 and 50 μg/mL) for 1 h were exposed to LPS (1 μg/mL) and incubated for 24 h. The level of NO in the cell medium was measured at 100-µL volume, comprising equal volumes of reagents A (1% 4-aminobenzenesulfonamide and 0.2% N1-[naphthalene-1-yl] ethane-1,2-diamine dihydrochloride) and B (5% phosphoric acid), and it was obtained from 100 µL of medium for 15 min. The absorbance value was calibrated using a SpectraMax iD3 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, California, USA) at 550 nm.

Western blotting

When RAW 264.7 cells (3 × 105 cells per 6-cm dish) had covered 70% of the dish’s surface, they were pretreated with MPE at 25 and 50 μg/mL for 1 h and subsequently treated with LPS at a concentration of 1 μg/mL. At the end of the reaction time, lysed cells were incubated on ice for 30 min and vortexed every 10 min. The supernatant obtained via centrifugation (4 °C, 13,000× g, 15 min) was quantified using the Detergent-Compatible Protein Assay Kit (Bio-Rad Inc., Hercules, CA, USA) and bovine serum albumin standards. Proteins separated using 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel were transferred to Immobilon®-P Polyvinylidene difluoride (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) membranes (pore size: 0.45 μm) at 230 mA for 1 h. After blocking the membrane for 1 h using 5% skim milk, all iNOS, COX-2, p65, p-p65, inhibitor of kappa B alpha (IκBα), p-IκBα, IκB kinase (IKK)α, and p-IKKα/β primary antibodies were diluted (1:1,000) in TBST containing either 5% bovine serum albumin or 5% non-fat dry milk, as recommended by manufacturers, and reacted with the membrane overnight at 4 °C. After reaction completion, the phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)-washed membrane was incubated with goat anti-rabbit IgG heavy-and-light-chain (H + L) and goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Eugene, OR, USA) for 1 h at room temperature (RT). Finally, the membranes were exposed for 1 min, and band images were acquired within a maximum exposure time of 5 min using the GeneGnome XRQ-NPC imaging system (Syngene, Cambridge, UK) and EzWestLumi Plus (ATTO, Tokyo, Japan).

Cytoplasm and nuclear fraction

Treatment with MPE and LPS was conducted in the same manner as that in Western blotting. Thereafter, nuclear and cytoplasm proteins were isolated using NE-PER™ Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction kits (Thermo Scientific, Rockford, Illinois, USA) containing Halt™ Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail kits (Thermo Scientific, Rockford, Illinois, USA). Each isolated protein was used in the experiment as described in Western blotting.

Immunofluorescence assay

For in vitro analysis, RAW 264.7 cells were seeded on eight-well chamber slides (ibidi, Gräfelfing, Germany) and cultured for 24 h with MPE (50 μg/mL) and LPS (1 μg/mL) for 30 min. For in vivo analysis, mouse lung tissues were frozen into sections using optimal cutting temperature solution (Leica Biosystems Richmond Inc., Richmond, IL, USA), and 8 μm-thick sections were attached to slides using a Cryocut Microtome (Cryostat CM1850; Leica Biosystems Richmond Inc., Richmond, IL, USA). Formaldehyde solution (4%; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was used as a fixation solution and reacted at RT for 15 min. Cells permeabilized with 100% ice-cold methanol for 10 min were blocked using 5% FBS and 0.3% Triton™ X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) in PBS at RT. After 1 h, the cells were incubated with p65 and NRF2 primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The next day, they were incubated with a goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 for 1 h. Tissue slides were fixed in the same manner as that in the in vitro experiments. For mouse lung tissue, iNOS and F4/80 were used as primary antibodies, while goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) conjugated to Alexa Fluor 594 and 488 as well as anti-mouse IgG were used as secondary antibodies. For nuclear staining, 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (VECTASHIELD®; Vector Laboratories Inc., Burlingame, CA, USA) was employed. Processed slides were observed and subsequently imaged using Leica Application Suite X software (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

ROS assay

ROS production was measured using 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCF-DA) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). MPE and LPS were administered to cells in 96 wells in the same manner as that in the NO assay. After 24 h, the cells from which the medium had been removed were treated with DCFH-DA (20 μM) dissolved in DMEM and incubated for 30 min. Thereafter, the cells were washed with warm PBS and measured using a microplate fluorometer and SoftMax Pro v5.4.1 software (Molecular Device, Danville, PA, USA). Fluorescence intensity was measured using a fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

ABTS and DPPH assays

The experiment was performed using ABTS and DPPH. The prepared 7 mM ABTS solution was cultured in the dark at RT for 16 h and subsequently diluted with PBS to obtain an absorbance of 0.7 ± 0.02 at 750 nm. Serial dilution of MPE (12.5, 25, and 50 µg/mL) and ascorbic acid (0.125, 0.25, and 1 µg/mL) was performed using PBS according to concentration. They were subsequently incubated with 100 μL of diluted ABTS solution for 30 min in the dark at RT. For the DPPH radical assay, MPE (25 and 50 µg/mL) and a standard (0.25, 0.5, and 1 µg/mL) were diluted with methanol and dispensed in a 96-well plate. Thereafter, they were reacted with 40 μg/mL DPPH solution for 30 min. A microplate reader (Bio-Rad Inc., Hercules, CA, USA) was used to measure the absorbance (750 nm) of ABTS and DPPH.

LPS-induced ALI mouse model in vivo

C57BL/6 mice (7 weeks old, male) supplied by Samtako Bio Korea Co., Ltd. (Osan, Korea) were provided with water and food ad libitum and raised in cages in an air-conditioned room (23 ± 2 °C). All mouse experiments in this study (2023-0433) were approved and performed in accordance with the Animal Use and Care Guidelines of Kyungpook National University. We randomly divided 36 mice into control, LPS-administration, low-concentration, and high-concentration groups (n = 9 per group) and orally nourished them for 3 days. Equal volumes of distilled water were administered to the control and LPS groups. One hour after the last oral administration, the mice were intranasally administered LPS (1.25 mg/mL per mouse) and euthanized 24 h later. Before intranasal inhalation, the mice were anesthetized using isoflurane (Hana Pharm. Co., Seoul, Korea) using a precision vaporizer (Harvard Apparatus, Holliston, MA, USA).

BALF and serum collection

PBS (1 mL) was injected into the trachea of each anesthetized mouse using a catheter, and the lungs were washed thrice. The BALF supernatant obtained via centrifugation (500× g, 5 min, 4 °C) was used for cytokine measurement. Blood was obtained from the portal vein and allowed to clot at RT for 30 min. Subsequently, it was centrifuged at 2,000× g for 15 min at 4 °C. The serum was stored on ice and subsequently used for experiments.

Cytokine assay

IL-6, IL-1β, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) levels in the collected blood, lung lysate, and BALF were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, Illinois, USA). Experimental procedures followed the manufacturer’s instructions.

Histological analysis

Slides for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining obtained in 4-μm sections from paraffin blocks and slides were stained using Dako CoverStainer (Agilent, CA, USA). For PAS staining, the paraffin blocks were treated twice with xylene for 5 min for hydration and subsequently with 100%, 95%, and 70% ethanol for 1 min each. Tissues fixed with 4% formaldehyde solution were treated with 3% acetic acid (5 min) and subsequently stained with 1% Alcian blue at RT (30 min). After washing with running tap water, each slide was left at RT for 10 min with 0.5% periodic acid and reacted with Schiff reagent solution in a dark room for 30 min. Cell nucleus staining was performed using 1% Mayer’s hematoxylin for 20 s. Dehydration was performed by reversing the hydration process. Finally, the slides were mounted using Eukitt® Quick-hardening mounting medium (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for observation.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation values from three independent experiments. The significance of differences among groups was determined using Student’s t-test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

MPE inhibits LPS-induced immune cell infiltration and cytokine production in BALF in vivo

We initially investigated whether MPE prevents LPS-induced ALI symptoms. Mice were orally administered MPE for 3 days, followed by the intranasal administration of LPS (1.25 mg/mL per mouse) for 1 day and subsequent euthanization (Fig. 1A). Lung tissues from the control and MPE-treatment groups were collected and analyzed using H&E and PAS staining. The results indicated that MPE suppressed immune cell infiltration, interstitial thickening (Fig. 1B, upper panel), and polysaccharide production (Fig. 1B, lower panel) in the lung tissues. Thereafter, to determine LPS-induced alveolar macrophage residence, the lung tissues were stained with F4/80, an antigen of alveolar macrophages, using immunofluorescence analysis. F4/80 expression exhibited intense staining in the LPS-treatment group and was strongly inhibited in the MPE-treatment group (Fig. 1C). In addition, we examined whether MPE affects the number of alveolar macrophages and product levels of IL-1β, a macrophage-derived inflammatory cytokine, in the BALF. Since the COVID-19 pandemic, society’s perceptions and life patterns in relation to respiratory health have evolved. Consequently, research on respiratory diseases has accelerated. The lung is a key respiratory organ that exchanges oxygen for carbon dioxide in the alveolar–capillary unit and also provides an entryway for diverse pathogens. When the alveolar sac is exposed to pathogens through breathing, activated AMs release various inflammatory substances, leading to ALI (Zhang et al., 2024). Although multiple candidates have demonstrated significant efficacy in preclinical animal models and the early phases of clinical trials, they remain subpar and/or lack approval (Zhang et al., 2024). Therefore, numerous anti-inflammatory materials have been investigated. In this study, to develop nutraceuticals or pharmaceuticals that manage acute lung inflammation, we screened 147 plant extracts from various countries. MPE is a prominent active extract that exerts inhibitory effects on LPS-induced NO generation in RAW 264.7 cells (data not shown). We primarily investigated the preventive effects of MPE on LPS-induced ALI symptoms in vivo. Histologically, oral MPE administration alleviated LPS-induced acute inflammation, including hyperplasia, mucin accumulation, and macrophage infiltration, in mouse lungs (Fig. 1A, B). BALF is a vesicular biomarker that represents the pathological status of lung diseases in vitro and in vivo. IL-1β and TNF-α cause pulmonary fibrosis and immune cell infiltration, resulting in ALI and chronic repair (She et al., 2021). In addition, elevated serum IL-6 levels impair lung function in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and ARDS (Bhargava et al., 2013; Wei et al., 2015). Therefore, we subsequently determined whether MPE affects these inflammatory cytokines. MPE exerted inhibitory effects on the LPS-induced production levels of IL-1β and TNF-α in BALF (Fig. 1E, F) and IL-6 in serum (Fig. 2A). Therefore, MPE presumably prevents LPS-induced ALI by mitigating the production of inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β and TNF-α. However, MPE’s anti-inflammatory effects were not dose-dependent for several macrophage and inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 1D–F). Therefore, we need to optimize MPE’s efficacy and potential toxicity in vivo.

NF-kB signaling acts as a master regulator of inflammation by inducing the expression of iNOS, COX-2, and various inflammatory cytokines (Millar et al., 2022). In addition, COX-2 or iNOS deficiency has been found to suppress LPS-induced inflammation and ALI in a mouse model (Nelin et al., 2022; Speyer et al., 2003). Therefore, NF-kB is a potential therapeutic target in ALI. Interestingly, we found MPE to significantly inhibit the expression of iNOS and COX-2 in vivo (Fig. 2B, C) and in vitro (Fig. 3C, D) as well as inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 3E–H) by reducing NF-kB activation in RAW 264.7 cells (Fig. 4A–E).

Although MPE downregulated the LPS-induced expression and production levels of IL-6 and IL-1β in RAW 264.7 cells (Fig. 3E–H), it had a negligible effect on lung tissue lysates (data not shown). Based on these differences, MPE evidently contains diverse active substances that potentially exhibit varying efficacies in terms of drug metabolism and absorption in the body and experimental cells. Furthermore, MPE strongly inhibited ROS production through the NRF2/HO-1 signaling pathway in RAW 264.7 cells (Fig. 5E–G). However, we did not address the antioxidant activity of MPE in vivo. Future studies on the antioxidant activity of MPE in vivo should seek to (i) identify the individual substances in MPE that exert its anti-inflammatory effects and (ii) elucidate its drug metabolism and absorption mechanisms.

In conclusion, MPE significantly inhibits LPS-induced ALI symptoms by reducing the expression and production levels of inflammatory cytokines and related proteins; it also mitigates ROS production by activating the NRF2/HO-1 signaling pathway. Overall, we suggest that MPE is a promising respiratory health functional food material possessing anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties.
