Diagnostic accuracy of interferon-gamma release assay and mucosal-associated invariant T cells in spinal tuberculosis
Authors: Korawish Mekariya, Pisanukiet Saneewong Na Ayutthaya, Akkaraporn Naowanirut, Artit Wongsa, Boonrat Tassaneetrithep, Wiwit Tantibhedhyangkul, Popchai Ngamskulrungroj, Sorranart Muangsomboon, Pipat Cheiwvit, Sirichai Wilartratsami, Panya Luksanapruksa, Nasuda Danchaivijitr, Kitidete Boonchai, Apisit Rattanatanasarn, Monchai Ruangchainikom
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Conventional diagnostic methods for tuberculous spondylodiscitis (TS) require tissue biopsy, which can delay diagnosis. Immunodiagnostic approaches, including interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs), have shown potential for improving TS diagnosis. Recent advancements have also identified the potential role of mucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells as biomarkers for TB infection. This study aims to evaluate the diagnostic performance of IGRA using the QuantiFERON-TB Gold In-Tube test (QFT-GIT) and MAIT cell analysis for diagnosing TS. Sixty-five patients suspected TS were prospectively enrolled, of whom 24 (37%) were categorized as confirmed TS. QFT-GIT demonstrated a sensitivity of 82.61% and specificity of 77.78%. No significant differences were found in the proportion of MAIT cells, CD4 + MAIT, CD8 + MAIT, and Double negative (CD4- CD8-) MAIT when compared between TS and non-TS patients. However, The MAIT cell population in CD8 + T lymphocytes subset was significantly higher in patients with TS (p = 0.0081). MAIT cell percentage > 2.63% in CD8 + T cell subset demonstrated fair discriminative ability, with an AUC of 0.746, sensitivity of 90.5%, and specificity of 51.4%. Combining QFT-GIT and the proportion of MAIT cells in CD8 + T cells subset improved specificity to 96.9%. These findings highlight the potential of these tests as promising, non-invasive diagnostic tools for differentiating TS in spondylodiscitis patients, especially when tissue diagnosis is not feasible.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23408-x.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) continues to be a major global health concern, with an estimated 10.6 million cases reported in 2020, equivalent to 134 per 100,000 individuals1. The most common causative species is Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Musculoskeletal involvement develops in approximately 10% of patients with extrapulmonary TB; half of these affected patients have tuberculous spondylodiscitis (TS)2,3. Neurologic deficits usually occur in 10% to 41% of patients TS4. Thus, early diagnosis and appropriate treatment are crucial in reducing the rates of disability and permanent functional impairment3.

Traditionally, TB detection has relied on acid-fast bacilli (AFB) staining and smear microscopy. However, this method requires a high bacillary load to achieve detection, resulting in limited sensitivity, ranging from 30% to 70%5. While Mycobacterium tuberculosis cultures offer higher sensitivity (80–95%), their utility is restricted by lengthy processing times, typically requiring 3 to 8 weeks6. The GeneXpert polymerase chain reaction (PCR) test has demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity for diagnosing tuberculous spondylodiscitis (TS) at 95.6% and 96.2%, respectively7. However, all of these diagnostic techniques necessitate closed or open surgical biopsy to obtain specimens, often resulting in delays in confirming the diagnosis.

Currently, immunodiagnostic tests, such as interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs), are available to assist in diagnosing TB, the interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs). IGRAs assess T-cell-induced interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) responses to TB-specific antigen ESAT6 and CFP10. It has been approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration as an in vitro diagnostic tool for TB detection, particularly when used in conjunction with risk assessments, radiographic imaging, and other diagnostic techniques8. However, only a limited number of studies have investigated the diagnostic accuracy of IGRAs in the case of TS.

In recent years, advances in immunology have uncovered the potential role of mucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT) as a biomarker for infectious disease. MAIT cells are a unique subset of unrestricted T lymphocytes, constituting up to 10% of circulating T-cells, and are prevalent in mucosal tissues9. Research suggests that MAIT cells are activated in response to cells infected with bacterial and fungal pathogens, including Mycobacterium tuberculosis10,11. These cells are thought to contribute to the immune system’s defense against infectious organisms9. Notably, previous study has observed that patients with active TB exhibit significantly lower peripheral blood MAIT cell counts than healthy individuals, possibly due to MAIT cell recruitment to sites of infection12. Given their immunological significance, MAIT cells have emerged as promising diagnostic markers for infectious diseases, including TB. Immunodiagnostic approaches targeting MAIT cell responses are currently being explored as novel methods for diagnosing TB13.

This study explores the MAIT cell population in patients with TS and assesses the diagnostic yield of immunodiagnostic techniques—specifically, IGRAs and MAIT cell assay for diagnosing TS. By evaluating the sensitivity and specificity of these advanced methods, we aim to determine whether they offer significant advantages over conventional diagnostic techniques, potentially improving the accuracy and early detection of TS.

Method

This cross-sectional study was conducted at a single-center tertiary care hospital between July 2017 and January 2023. The Siriraj Institutional Review Board of the Faculty of Medicine of Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University, Bangkok, Thailand (COA no. si 716/2015) approved the study protocol. All methods were performed following relevant guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was obtained from all participants involved in the study. All participants obtained written informed consent before being included in the study. Patients eligible for inclusion were over 18 years of age with clinically and radiographically (conventional spinal radiography and spinal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with gadolinium enhancement) suspected tuberculous spondylodiscitis (TS), as assessed by four senior spine surgeons. Patients were included if MRI findings were suggestive of spondylodiscitis, indicated by increased T2 and decreased T1 signal intensity in affected discs, adjacent endplates, and vertebrae, or paravertebral soft tissue or psoas muscle abscesses formation, or vertebral endplate disruption and collapse of the vertebral body. The Exclusion criteria included active disseminated TB infection, prior anti-TB medication use, HIV infection, autoimmune disease, or currently taking immunosuppressive drugs.

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were collected for all patients, followed by a diagnostic tissue biopsy at the suspected TS site. Biopsy was performed using either a closed imaging-guided, percutaneous transpedicular, or open surgical biopsy. The collected tissue or pus was sent for gram staining, AFB examination, bacterial culture, M. tuberculosis culture on solid media (Lowenstein-Jensen media) and liquid media (BD BACTEC MGIT 960 system), molecular examination using GeneXpert MTB/RIF assay, and histopathological examination. All testing procedures were conducted under standard laboratory protocols.

Clinical samples were first subjected to Gram staining based on Gram reaction and morphology for preliminary characterization. Following staining, samples were cultured on appropriate agar media, including blood agar or MacConkey agar, and incubated under optimal conditions based on the suspected organism (e.g., 37 °C for 18–24 h in aerobic or anaerobic environments as required). When bacterial growth occurred, single colonies were selected and subjected to matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) analysis for species identification. Colonies were directly smeared onto a MALDI-TOF MS target plate, overlaid with a matrix solution (saturated α -cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid in 50% acetonitrile and 2.5% trifluoroacetic acid), and allowed to air dry. Spectral data were acquired using a MALDI-TOF MS instrument and matched against a reference database for species identification. Quality control was ensured using known bacterial strains as positive controls in each run.

For culturing Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB), acid-fast bacilli (AFB) were initially detected using auramine staining and examined under fluorescence microscopy14. Then the MTB culture was done according to standard of practice. Positive cultures were first screened with auramine staining and further confirmed using in-house real-time PCR and line probe assays, following previously established protocols15,16. GeneXpert MTB/RIF (Cepheid, CA, USA) testing was performed per the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, each tissue or pus sample was mixed with 2.0 mL of reagent and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. A 2.0 mL aliquot of each inactivated sample mixture was then transferred to a separate GeneXpert MTB/RIF cartridge, which was subsequently inserted into the GeneXpert instrument. The GeneXpert MTB/RIF results were available within 90 min. Additionally, all patients underwent IGRA and MAIT cell examinations on the day of enrollment during admission for biopsy.

QuantiFERON-TB gold In-Tube test (QFT-GIT)

Peripheral blood samples were collected for the IGRAs test using the QFT-GIT assay (Cellestis, Carnegie, Victoria, Australia) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The blood was added to the blank control tube (Nil), the TB antigen tube, and the mitogen-positive control tube. Samples were incubated at 37 °C for 16–18 h, then tested to measure IFN-γ levels (IU/mL) by ELISA. Results were categorized as positive, negative, or indeterminate (due to high nil or low mitogen values). The cutoff value for a positive test was 0.35 IU/ml.

Mucosal-associated invariant T-cell examination

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by gradient centrifugation using Histopaque (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, Missouri) as the manufacturer’s recommendation and cryopreserved before batch testing. All samples were thawed and stained with LIVE/DEAD Fixable Aqua (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California). Consequently, the surface antigen was stained with conjugated antibodies cocktail including anti-CD3 Brilliant Violet 605, anti-CD4 Brilliant Violet 421, anti-CD8 Allophycocyanin (APC)-Cyanine 7, anti-CD161 Phycoerythrin (PE)-Cyanine7, and anti-TCR Vα7.2 APC (BioLegend, San Diego, California). All samples were acquired with BD FACSymphony A1 Cell Analyzer (BD Bioscience, San Jose, California). Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo software (Version 10.10.0, Tree Star, Ashland, Oregon). MAIT cells were defined as CD3 + CD161 + Vα7.2 + T cells. Each cell subpopulation was estimated as a percentage of MAIT cells without dead cells, debris, and doublets.

Reference test for TS

The diagnostic criteria for confirmed TS in this study included at least one of the following: (1) a positive culture for M. tuberculosis, (2) a positive GeneXpert PCR result, or (3) histopathological features showing caseous necrosis, granulomatous inflammation, or AFB positivity, along with clinical improvement following anti-TB treatment. Patients diagnosed with confirmed TS were used as the reference test in this study. Patients with progressive neurologic deficits, severe back pain, or mechanical instability underwent surgical debridement, decompression, and posterior instrumentation. All patients received consultations with infectious disease specialists, and the consulting infectious specialist determined prescriptions for anti-TB or antibacterial medications.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows (version 23.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Normally distributed variables are presented as mean and standard deviation, while non-normally distributed variables are presented as median and interquartile range. The diagnostic accuracy of IGRAs and MAIT cells for diagnosing TS was evaluated in terms of sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to determine the optimal cutoff value and its corresponding sensitivity and specificity for diagnosing TS.

Result

A total of 77 patients suspected of TS were assessed for eligibility; 5 had disseminated TB, three were HIV-positive, and 4 had autoimmune disease or were currently taking immunosuppressive drugs. Consequently, 65 patients were included in the study. Utilizing the previously described criteria, 24 patients were diagnosed with confirmed TS, while the remaining 41 patients had non-tuberculous spondylodiscitis (non-TS). Patient demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The mean age of patients was 65 ± 9 years, with 50.8% being male. Fever and neurological deficits were significantly more frequent in the TS group than in the non-TS (p = 0.031 and p = 0.009, respectively).

Diagnostic performance of the MAIT cell assay

Seven of 65 patients (three TS and four non-TS) were excluded due to the poor quality of cryopreserved PBMC for analysis. Figure 1 demonstrates MAIT cell populations in patients with TS and non-TS. No significant differences were found in the proportion of total MAIT cells, CD4 + MAIT, CD8 + MAIT, and Double negative (CD4- CD8-) MAIT cell subpopulations when compared between TS and non-TS patients. When exploring cells expressing MAIT markers (CD161 + Vα7.2+) among CD4+, CD8+, and double-negative T lymphocytes subset, the proportion of MAIT cells within CD8 + T lymphocytes subset was significantly higher in patients with TS (p = 0.0081). Diagnostic performance of combining QFT-GIT assay and % of MAIT cells in CD8 + T cells subset assay

Further analysis of the diagnostic performance was conducted using a parallel testing model combining QFT-GIT and the %of MAIT cells within the CD8 + T cell subset. A cutoff value of > 6.00% for % of MAIT cells in CD8 + T cells subset was selected based on ROC curve analysis, which demonstrated a specificity of 89.2% and a sensitivity of 47.6%. This cutoff was chosen to establish a high-specificity, rule-in diagnostic criterion and to minimize false-positive results. In the parallel testing model, a combined test result was considered positive only when both QFT-GIT was positive and % of MAIT cells in CD8 + T cells subset > 6.00%. This approach yielded a sensitivity of 45.00% (95% CI: 23.06%–68.47%) and a specificity of 96.88% (95% CI: 83.78%–99.92%), with a PPV of 90.00% (95% CI: 55.19%–98.50%), a NPV of 73.81% (95% CI: 65.36%–80.80%), and an overall accuracy of 72.69% (95% CI: 63.16%–87.47%).

Discussion

TB is a prevalent global health issue, particularly in developing countries. Prompt diagnosis of TS is essential to initiate early treatment with anti-tuberculosis medication, reducing the risk of long-term neurological complications. However, diagnosing TS presents unique challenges, as standard diagnostic procedures often require a spinal tissue biopsy, which can be time-consuming and challenging to obtain. This study evaluates the accuracy of immunological diagnostic methods using QFT-GIT assay and MAIT cell assay for the diagnosis of TS in patients present with clinically and radiologically suspected spondylodiscitis.

Our results indicate that the QFT-GIT assay demonstrated acceptable sensitivity (82.61%) and specificity (77.78%) for differentiating TS. Previous studies have reported varying accuracy for TS diagnosis. For instance, Hu et al18. found that the QFT-GIT test showed a sensitivity of 92.16% and a specificity of 67.14% in hospitalized patients with suspected TS. Similarly, S. Choi et al.19 investigated 115 patients with suspected TS, reporting a sensitivity of 91% and specificity of 65%. These findings align with our study, which showed high sensitivity and moderate specificity for the QFT-GIT test in suspected TS cases. The high sensitivity indicates that the QFT-GIT test accurately identified a significant portion of TS cases, with minimal false negatives. It supports its use as a screening tool to rule out TS in patients when the test results are negative. However, the specificity of 77.78% reflects a moderate rate of false positives, as seen in the PPV of 70.37%, suggesting that nearly one-third of positive QFT-GIT results may be false positives. The low specificity may be attributed to the QFT-GIT assay’s limited ability to distinguish between active TB disease and latent TB infection (LTBI), particularly in regions with a high prevalence of LTBI, as was the case in our study19,20. Additionally, certain nontuberculous mycobacterial infections, such as Mycobacterium kansasii, Mycobacterium szulgai, and Mycobacterium marinum, can also yield positive results. Therefore, it is essential to confirm positive results if using the IGRA test alone with further biopsy before initiating anti-TB therapy.

Another immunodiagnostic tool evaluated in this study was MAIT cell analysis; MAIT cells are a subset of non-classically restricted T lymphocytes that recognize and rapidly respond to MTB infection. Previous studies have demonstrated that MAIT cells are found in the lungs and pleural effusions of patients with active TB21 and their frequency in peripheral blood is significantly reduced in patients with active pulmonary TB and tuberculous pleural effusions compared with healthy control subjects12. Therefore, the MAIT cell number has been proposed as a reliable marker to discriminate latent from active pulmonary TB in children22. In contrast, we observed no significant difference in the frequency of MAIT cells in the total CD3 + T lymphocyte population between the TS and non-TS groups. This lack of distinction may be explained by the composition of the non-TS group, which included a high proportion (58.5%) of bacterial spondylodiscitis cases. Prior studies have reported that MAIT cell frequencies decrease in other bacterial infections, making them less effective in distinguishing bacteria from mycobacterial spondylodiscitis11.

Interestingly, we found a significantly higher frequency of MAIT cells in the CD8 + T cells subset among patients with TS. This finding contrasts with earlier studies by Yang et al. and Sharma et al. (25,26), which reported lower frequencies of CD8 + CD161 + T cells in patients with active TB compared to healthy controls or those with LTBI. However, these studies did not specifically assess spondylodiscitis patients. Additionally, MAIT cells are predominantly localized in mucosal sites such as the lungs and intestinal tract23, and their presence in spinal tissue, as well as their response to spinal infections, has not been extensively studied. This difference in localization and function may partly explain the discrepancy in results. Further studies are warranted to explore the role and distribution of MAIT cells in spinal infections, similar to their established roles in pulmonary and pleural TB, and to confirm the findings related to peripheral blood MAIT cell levels in a larger cohort of TS patients.

We performed the ROC analysis to further assess the diagnostic value of % of MAIT cells in CD8 + T cell subset. Our results showed that the frequency of MAIT cells within CD8 + T cell subset had an AUC of 0.746, indicating fair discriminative performance in distinguishing TS from non-TS cases. The optimal cutoff value for % of MAIT cells in CD8 + T cell subset was > 2.63%, yielding a sensitivity of 90.48% and specificity of 51.35%. In contrast, the frequency of MAIT cells in the CD4 + T cell population demonstrated non-significant, lower diagnostic utility (AUC = 0.538). This suggests that analysis of MAIT cell frequency within CD8 + T cell subset could serve as a supplementary diagnostic tool for TS. Further research is needed to validate these findings and explore their integration into clinical diagnostic algorithms for TS.

To improve the specificity of immunological tests for diagnosing TS, we combined the QFT-GIT assay and % of MAIT cells in CD8 + T cell subset, using a positive cutoff value greater than 6.00% and defining a positive result only when both tests are positive. This combined criterion yielded a high specificity of 96.9% and a PPV of 90% for TS diagnosis. We propose that this combined approach may be particularly useful in routine clinical practice, especially in situations where tissue biopsy is contraindicated, such as in patients with coagulopathy, unstable medical conditions, or in settings lacking image-guided biopsy or access to spine surgeons It may also be beneficial when biopsy results are inconclusive, such as in patients with a negative biopsy despite high clinical suspicion, or in probable TB cases with compatible histopathological findings but without positive AFB, culture, or PCR results. Additionally, obtaining spinal tissue for culture is more invasive and technically challenging, with lower sensitivity than other TB sites. There is considerable clinical value in establishing a diagnosis of spinal TB without the need for open biopsy, as most patients with TS can be managed conservatively with favorable outcomes24, particularly those without neurological deficits or spinal instability25. Therefore, we propose a simplified diagnostic algorithm that incorporates both tests as an alternative to help establish TS diagnosis (Fig. 3). further study is needed to investigate the incorporation of the QFT-GIT assay and MAIT cell analysis into standard diagnostic algorithms for TS. Implementing these tests may provide valuable support in establishing a TS diagnosis and initiating anti-TB treatment in these uncertain scenarios.

The strengths of this study are notable. This is the first study to analyze the diagnostic accuracy of MAIT cells for diagnosing TS; most studies were performed in active pulmonary TB and with prospective data collection to minimize bias and ensure validity. However, several limitations must be acknowledged, particularly in using QFT-GIT in an endemic region like Thailand. A significant concern is that IGRA is incapable of distinguishing between LTBI and active disease26. While QFT-GIT effectively detects active TB infection, its specificity decreases in populations with high rates of LTBI. Studies indicate that IGRA positivity can be as high as 80–90% in active TB cases, but similar rates are observed in LTBI26,27. This overlap between active and latent TB in IGRA results is a critical challenge in such settings, making it difficult to distinguish between the two conditions and necessitating careful interpretation of results.

In addition, the practical application of IGRA and MAIT cell testing in real-world settings is limited by the availability and accessibility of these tests. Both QFT-GIT and MAIT cell analysis require specialized laboratory facilities that may not be readily available in all healthcare settings, particularly in rural or under-resourced areas. This limitation is compounded by the relatively high cost of these tests. It may be prohibitive for widespread use, especially in resource-limited settings where the burden of TB is often most tremendous. Therefore, while these tests show promise in a controlled research environment, their implementation in routine clinical practice may be constrained by these logistical and financial barriers. Specifically, larger, multi-center studies should be conducted to validate the use of MAIT cells in diagnosing TS. Additionally, further studies should explore the diagnostic performance of IGRA and MAIT cells in diverse populations and settings, particularly in regions with varying TB prevalence, such as in non-endemic areas.

Conclusion

The QFT-GIT assay demonstrated high sensitivity and moderate specificity in diagnosing TS, while % of MAIT cells in CD8 + T cell subset showed fair discriminative capability in patients with TS. Combining the QFT-GIT assay with % of MAIT cells in CD8 + T cell subset analysis can achieve high specificity for TS diagnosis. These findings highlight the potential of these tests as promising, non-invasive diagnostic tools for differentiating TS in patients suspected of spondylodiscitis, especially when tissue diagnosis is unavailable to avoid delayed diagnosis and initiation of treatment.
