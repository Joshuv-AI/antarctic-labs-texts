Organizational patient safety culture and workforce well-being among healthcare workers: associations with resilience and work–life balance in a hospital-wide cross-sectional study
Authors: Mei-Fei Hsieh, Po-Ming Ku, Chin-Ming Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient safety culture is widely recognized as a core determinant of healthcare quality, safety, and organizational performance (1). Increasing attention has also been directed toward healthcare workforce well-being, particularly positive psychological resources such as resilience (2) and work–life balance. These factors, alongside the mitigation of occupational stress (3), are essential in influencing safety-related behaviors and the sustainability of health service delivery. However, empirical evidence integrating multidimensional patient safety culture with positive workforce well-being outcomes at the organizational level remains limited (4).MethodsA hospital-wide cross-sectional survey was conducted among healthcare workers at a regional teaching hospital in Taiwan during 2024. Patient safety culture was assessed using a validated multidimensional safety culture instrument, while workforce well-being was measured in terms of resilience and work–life balance. Descriptive statistics, Pearson correlation analyses, and multiple linear regression models were applied to examine associations between safety culture dimensions and well-being outcomes, with subgroup analyses across demographic and professional characteristics.ResultsA total of 1,779 healthcare workers participated. Multiple linear regression revealed that while most safety culture dimensions positively influenced work-life balance, job satisfaction exhibited a significant negative association with resilience (β = -0.400, p < 0.001). Stress perception emerged as a strong predictor of higher resilience but poorer work-life balance.ConclusionsThe study underscores the dialectical relationship between organizational culture and workforce well-being. The findings suggest that resilience may function as a compensatory resource for healthcare workers in demanding environments. Health services managers should integrate workforce well-being into safety frameworks, moving beyond satisfaction scores to address the structural conditions that shape both resilience and work-life balance.Keywords: Patient safety culture, Workforce well-being, Resilience, Work–life balance, Safety Attitudes Questionnaire

Introduction

Patient safety remains a central global priority as healthcare systems confront increasing service complexity, persistent workforce shortages, and sustained pressure on care delivery. Despite decades of improvement efforts, preventable harm continues to occur, and contemporary policy frameworks emphasize that patient safety is shaped by system-level determinants—such as organizational culture, staffing environments, and workforce conditions—rather than by individual clinical performance alone [1]. Consistent with this perspective, patient safety culture provides a structured lens for understanding how shared values and expectations translate into daily work processes that support or undermine safe care [2, 3]. Within this context, resilience is an important and potentially modifiable workforce resource that may help healthcare workers cope with occupational demands while supporting patient safety [4].

Within this system framework, patient safety culture reflects the organizational norms that influence teamwork, communication, learning, and management practices related to safety. A growing synthesis of the evidence indicates that safety culture is foundational for organizational learning and for enabling error reporting and quality improvement processes [3]. At the level of measurement, the Safety Attitudes Questionnaire (SAQ) captures multiple dimensions that operationalize this culture (e.g., teamwork climate, safety climate, job satisfaction, stress recognition, perceptions of management, and working conditions), supporting the view that safety culture is not a single construct but a multidimensional organizational phenomenon [5, 6]. However, prior work has been uneven in identifying which SAQ dimensions most strongly relate to different organizational and staff outcomes, and which aspects of safety culture remain less understood when examined simultaneously across roles and settings.

Concurrently, workforce well-being has become recognized as a critical determinant of safe and sustainable care. While much of the literature has focused on negative states—particularly burnout and psychological distress—evidence also suggests that positive psychological resources may support adaptive functioning in demanding clinical environments. Systematic review evidence indicates that poor well-being and burnout are associated with poorer patient safety outcomes, though causal inference remains limited by study designs [7]. Meta-analytic findings on clinician burnout further highlight the breadth of downstream quality and safety impacts [8, 9]. Importantly, growing research attention has turned toward resilience and work–life balance/integration as protective resources that may help buffer occupational stressors and sustain performance; yet these positive resources are comparatively understudied relative to burnout, particularly in designs that link them directly to multidimensional safety culture constructs.

Nevertheless, several key research gaps remain. First, existing studies have predominantly emphasized burnout and other negative well-being indicators, limiting understanding of how safety culture relates to positive psychological resources [8, 9]. Second, although integrative conceptual work suggests that teamwork and occupational well-being are intertwined with patient safety, comparatively few empirical studies incorporate multidimensional safety culture measures alongside positive well-being outcomes within a unified analytical framework [10]. Third, much of the evidence base has relied on single professional groups or relatively restricted samples, and is often concentrated in Western contexts, thereby constraining organization-wide generalization [7, 11]. Fourth, and most specifically, prior research has not adequately examined, in a hospital-wide population, the joint associations between multiple SAQ dimensions and both resilience and work–life balance/work–life integration across diverse professional roles. Studies developing and applying safety- and work–life climate measures show meaningful relationships between work–life norms and safety culture, but have not combined these with resilience while simultaneously modeling multiple SAQ dimensions in broad cross-disciplinary hospital samples [2, 11]. Moreover, empirical work in Taiwan demonstrates that patient safety culture is related to staff well-being, but the integration of multidimensional SAQ perspectives with positive resilience indicators and work–life balance at the organization-wide level remains insufficiently established, leaving uncertainty about how distinct safety culture dimensions map onto the dual positive outcomes of resilience and work–life balance [8, 12].

To address these gaps, the present study investigates associations between organizational patient safety culture and healthcare workers’ resilience and work–life balance using data from a large hospital-wide cross-sectional survey [8, 13]. By linking multiple SAQ dimensions with two positive workforce well-being outcomes across diverse professional roles, this study aims to generate organization-level evidence that can inform strategies for simultaneously strengthening patient safety culture and supporting work–life integration. In doing so, it contributes to the discourse on work–life integration by examining how organizational safety culture shapes the workforce resources that enable health services to remain safe and sustainable under ongoing demands.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a hospital-wide cross-sectional survey design conducted at a regional teaching hospital in Taiwan during 2024, Data were collected from healthcare workers across multiple professional roles using a structured, self-administered questionnaire. The survey assessed perceptions of patient safety culture together with key indicators of workforce well-being, including resilience and work–life balance. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and no identifiable personal information was collected to reduce potential social desirability bias and protect respondent confidentiality.

Participants and data collection

All healthcare workers employed at the institution during the study period were eligible to participate, including physicians, nurses, allied health professionals (including medical technologists, radiological technologists, physical therapists, and dietitians), administrative staff, and other clinical and non-clinical personnel. The survey was distributed through the institutional electronic survey platform, and participants completed the questionnaire within the designated data collection period. A total of 2,000 eligible healthcare workers were invited to participate in the survey.

Measures

Patient safety culture

Patient safety culture was assessed using the Safety Attitudes Questionnaire (SAQ), originally developed by Sexton et al. The survey instrument followed the standardized Taiwanese version implemented nationally under the guidance of the Joint Commission of Taiwan (JCT). The JCT-endorsed version has been formally authorized, translated, and culturally adapted for use in Taiwanese healthcare institutions.

The validated Taiwanese version includes six core dimensions: teamwork climate, safety climate, job satisfaction, stress recognition, perceptions of management, and working conditions, comprising 30 core items.

In 2014, two additional institutional modules assessing emotional exhaustion (9 items) and work–life balance (7 items) were incorporated into the survey system to support organizational monitoring of workforce psychological well-being.

All items were rated on a Likert-type scale, with higher scores indicating more positive perceptions.

Workforce well-being

Workforce well-being was assessed using two supplementary modules embedded in the 2014 revised Taiwanese New Safety Attitudes Questionnaire (New-SAQ), developed under the Joint Commission of Taiwan (JCT) national patient safety culture survey platform. The resilience dimension consists of items adapted from the emotional exhaustion subscale of the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI), reverse-coded to reflect resilience rather than burnout [14]. The work-life balance dimension was developed as part of the same JCT New-SAQ revision to capture workforce perceptions of the interface between professional demands and personal life. Both modules have been administered nationally as part of the JCT patient safety culture monitoring system since 2014. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater resilience and better perceived work-life balance.

Covariates

Demographic and occupational characteristics were collected, including sex, age, professional role, supervisory status, employment type, years of service at the institution, years of service within the current unit, educational level, frequency of patient contact, and prior experience with incident reporting.

These variables were used to describe the study population and to examine group differences in patient safety culture perceptions and workforce well-being. In addition, selected covariates were considered in multivariable regression analyses to account for potential confounding effects based on prior health services research and theoretical relevance.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics and to calculate mean scores for each safety culture dimension and workforce well-being outcome. Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to examine associations between dimensions of patient safety culture and workforce well-being outcomes. Multiple linear regression analyses were performed to identify safety culture factors independently associated with resilience and work-life balance while accounting for relevant covariates.

In the primary regression models, all six SAQ dimensions were entered simultaneously. Adjusted models further included demographic and occupational covariates selected based on prior literature and theoretical relevance.

Model assumptions, including linearity and multicollinearity, were evaluated prior to regression analyses. Group differences across demographic and occupational characteristics were examined using independent-samples t tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), as appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p value < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using SPSS statistical software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chi Mei Hospital, Liouying, TaiwanTainan, Taiwan (IRB No. 11408-L04). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was implied through completion of the anonymous questionnaire.

Results

Participant characteristics

Of the 2,000 eligible healthcare workers invited, 1,779 completed the survey, yielding a response rate of 89.0%. The majority of participants were female (81.7%), and the largest age group was 41–50 years (40.8%), followed by 31–40 years (26.8%) and 21–30 years (23.4%). Nurses constituted the largest professional group (51.4%), followed by administrative staff (19.7%) and allied health professionals (11.2%). Most participants were employed full-time (94.7%) and reported frequent direct contact with patients (73.4%).

The sample reflected the workforce composition of the institution, with nurses and allied health professionals comprising the majority of respondents. The relatively small proportion of physicians and pharmacists should be considered when interpreting profession-specific findings. In contrast, stress perception showed negative correlations with job satisfaction (r = -0.18, p < 0.001), resilience (r = -0.48, p < 0.001), and work-life balance (r = -0.25, p < 0.001), indicating that higher perceived stress was associated with poorer psychological well-being. Factors associated with resilience

Multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to identify patient safety culture dimensions independently associated with resilience (Table 4). The overall model explained a substantial proportion of variance in resilience, with an adjusted R² of 0.376. Job satisfaction and stress perception emerged as the strongest predictors of resilience. After adjustment for other patient safety culture dimensions, higher stress perception was positively associated with resilience (β = 0.366, p < 0.001), suggesting a potential suppression or statistical adjustment effect within the multivariable model.

The overall model explained a modest proportion of variance in work-life balance (adjusted R² = 0.139, p < 0.001). Higher job satisfaction was significantly associated with better work-life balance (β = 0.128, p = 0.001), whereas greater stress perception was associated with poorer work-life balance (β = -0.161, p < 0.001). Perceptions of management (β = 0.107, p = 0.012) and working conditions (β = 0.073, p = 0.048) were also positively associated with work-life balance. Teamwork demonstrated a small but statistically significant positive association (β = 0.079, p = 0.045), while safety climate was not significantly related to work-life balance in the adjusted model.

Overall, these findings indicate that both organizational safety culture dimensions and perceived stress play meaningful roles in shaping healthcare workers’ work-life balance.

Group differences in workforce well-being

Significant overall group differences in resilience and work–life balance were observed across several demographic and occupational characteristics (Table 6). Healthcare workers in supervisory roles had higher mean resilience and work–life balance scores than non-supervisory staff (p < 0.001). Significant overall differences were also observed across professional roles for resilience (p < 0.001). Descriptively, physicians reported higher resilience scores, whereas administrative staff reported lower resilience scores. Participants with prior incident-reporting experience also demonstrated higher resilience scores than those without such experience (p = 0.034).

Discussion

This hospital-wide cross-sectional study examined the associations between organizational patient safety culture and healthcare workers’ well-being, operationalized as resilience and work-life balance. Overall, several dimensions of patient safety culture were associated with better work-life balance, while job satisfaction showed a negative association with resilience (β = -0.400, p < 0.001). Stress perception also emerged as a key correlate: it was positively associated with resilience yet negatively associated with work-life balance after adjustment for other safety culture dimensions. These findings align with global patient safety frameworks that emphasize system-level determinants and workforce well-being as essential conditions for sustained safe care delivery [7].

Our results further reinforce that patient safety culture is not only a determinant of error-related processes but also an organizational resource shaping workforce psychological well-being. Evidence from meta-analytic and empirical studies indicates that patient safety culture and related organizational climates are associated with burnout and well-being outcomes, underscoring that organizational contexts can either protect or undermine staff health [8, 9]. In our analyses, job satisfaction operated as a central mechanism linking safety culture to positive well-being: job satisfaction was positively associated with work-life balance (β = 0.128, p = 0.001), supporting the idea that satisfaction may reflect organizational conditions that enable workers to manage demands in ways compatible with personal life. Importantly, we use the term ‘association’ rather than ‘correlation,’ because these findings derive from multivariable regression estimates rather than simple bivariate relationships.

A particularly notable finding was the counterintuitive negative association between job satisfaction and resilience (β = -0.400, p < 0.001). This finding should not be interpreted as indicating that job satisfaction reduces resilience. Rather, it may reflect an adversity-growth dynamic, in which healthcare workers facing more demanding work environments experience lower job satisfaction while simultaneously developing adaptive coping capacities. In this interpretation, resilience reflects sustained engagement with occupational strain and the ability to recover under pressure, which may be fostered in challenging rather than purely comfortable work settings. This explanation is compatible with evidence suggesting that resilience varies across occupational groups and is shaped by workplace experiences within hospitals [15]. Moreover, resilience and burnout are intertwined constructs that may shift differently depending on exposure to ongoing stress and the degree of organizational support available [9, 15]. Consistent with this view, our findings suggest that job satisfaction and resilience may represent distinct aspects of psychological adaptation under real-world clinical conditions [10].

Stress perception also showed a complex relationship with resilience. Although stress perception would ordinarily be expected to relate negatively to well-being, our multivariable model indicated that higher stress perception was positively associated with resilience after adjustment, despite being negatively associated with work-life balance (β = -0.161, p < 0.001). This unexpected positive association may reflect suppression effects or shared variance among overlapping safety culture dimensions, whereby stress perception becomes an indicator of coping-oriented adaptation under certain organizational conditions. In East Asian healthcare settings, resilience has been discussed in relation to how safety culture elements and workforce conditions interact with stressful circumstances, particularly during periods of intense strain [12]. Similar patterns have been observed when staff perceptions of safety culture overlap with how workers interpret and respond to workload pressures [8]. Therefore, our findings suggest that stress-related perceptions may coexist with resilient adaptation, while still undermining work-life balance through sustained strain on recovery and personal life.

These results have direct implications for the work-life integration theme of this collection. The data suggest that organizational safety culture dimensions shape not only worker distress but also the behavioral and environmental conditions that allow integration between work demands and non-work life. Specifically, job satisfaction, perceptions of management, and working conditions were positively associated with work-life balance (management: β = 0.107, p = 0.012; working conditions: β = 0.073, p = 0.048), indicating that supportive organizational climates facilitate better integration. Prior studies have described work-life integration as a climate-like construct that co-varies with teamwork and safety norms and predicts burnout-related outcomes [8]. In addition, organizational strategies aimed at promoting clinician well-being emphasize that work-life integration cannot be achieved solely through individual coping; it requires leadership and structural interventions aligned with safety culture [10]. In our study, supervisory roles were associated with higher resilience and work-life balance. Physicians showed higher mean resilience scores, whereas nurses reported lower work-life balance scores. These findings suggest that work-life integration experiences differ across professional roles and organizational hierarchies, highlighting the need for tailored interventions within hospital systems.

From a health services management perspective, these findings support integrated organizational strategies that simultaneously address safety culture and workforce well-being. Prior work has argued that improving clinician well-being, patient safety, and organizational change should be treated as a ‘triple challenge,’ requiring coordinated rather than isolated interventions [2]. Our results suggest that hospitals should embed workforce well-being considerations-particularly factors tied to job satisfaction, management support, and working conditions-into patient safety culture enhancement programs. The evidence that patient safety culture can function as an organizational resource for both care quality and staff well-being further supports investment in culture-level change [8]. Practically, this implies that leadership walkthroughs and improvement cycles targeting both safety climate and staff working environments may yield synergistic benefits, reducing the risk that strengthening safety culture inadvertently intensifies stress without supporting work-life integration.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, because the design was cross-sectional, causal inference regarding the directionality between patient safety culture dimensions and workforce well-being outcomes is not possible; longitudinal studies are needed to clarify temporal relationships. Second, data were derived from a single regional teaching hospital, which may limit generalizability to other settings with different organizational structures, staffing models, and cultural contexts. Third, resilience and work–life balance were measured using self-reported survey instruments, which may be influenced by response bias or social desirability, despite anonymous data collection. Furthermore, the study sample reflected the workforce composition of the participating institution, with a predominance of female participants (81.7%) and nurses (51.4%). This uneven distribution of gender and professional roles may limit the generalizability of the findings to healthcare settings with different workforce structures.

Additionally, although multivariable regression models adjusted for selected covariates, unmeasured contextual factors—such as leadership style nuances, actual workload intensity, and recent institutional policy or operational changes—may still influence staff well-being and may partially explain subgroup differences observed by professional role. Future research using multi-center designs and longitudinal approaches, ideally incorporating objective organizational indicators and repeated measures of safety culture and work–life integration, would strengthen causal interpretation and enhance applicability.

Conclusions

This study underscores the dialectical relationship between organizational patient safety culture and workforce well-being. The findings suggest that resilience may function as a compensatory resource for healthcare workers in demanding environments, while work-life balance is more directly supported by favorable management and working conditions. Health services managers should integrate workforce well-being into patient safety frameworks, moving beyond satisfaction scores to address the structural conditions that shape both resilience and work-life balance.
