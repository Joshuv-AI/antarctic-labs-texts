On the relation between time-reversed acoustics and Green’s function retrieval in space-variant and in time-variant materials
Kees Wapenaar a , Johannes Aichele b , Dirk-Jan van Manen b

Abstract




Abstract

The methods of time-reversed acoustics and Green’s function retrieval are traditionally deployed for classical inhomogeneous, time-invariant materials. The mutual relation between these methods is well-established. Recently, similar methods have been proposed for homogeneous, time-variant materials. Here we investigate their mutual relation and their relation with the corresponding methods in classical materials. For this analysis we make use of the fact that the wave equations for both types of material are similar, with the roles of time and space interchanged. However, the principle of causality holds for both types of material, hence, here the roles of time and space are not interchanged. We find that

•

whereas classical time-reversed acoustics involves emission of a time-reversed single-component wave field from a (ideally closed) boundary into the inhomogeneous material, its idealized counterpart involves emission of a sign-reversed two-component wave field, recorded in a time-reversed material, from a single time instant into the actual time-variant material;

•

whereas classical Green’s function retrieval involves temporal crosscorrelation of wave fields at two space locations in response to single-component sources on a (ideally closed) boundary, its counterpart involves spatial crosscorrelation of wave fields at two time instants in response to two-component sources at a single time instant.

Another aspect that complicates the analogy is the fact that the number of time dimensions (one) is different from the number of space dimensions (two or three),
except of course for 1D situations.

Time-reversed acoustics and Green’s function retrieval in inhomogeneous, time-invariant materials

In this section we review classical time-reversed acoustics and Green’s function retrieval in inhomogeneous, time-invariant materials, and their mutual relation.
In section

III.1
Wave equation for an inhomogeneous, time-invariant material

We consider an arbitrarily inhomogeneous, time-invariant material, with parameters
\alpha({\bf x})
and
\beta({\bf x})
.
Substituting constitutive relations (
3

4

1
) and (
2

\alpha({\bf x})
and

are time-invariant,
we obtain

\displaystyle\alpha\partial_{t}P+{\bf\nabla}\cdot{\bf Q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a,

(5)

\displaystyle\beta\partial_{t}{\bf Q}+{\bf\nabla}P

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf b}.

(6)

\kappa\partial_{t}p+{\bf\nabla}\cdot{\bf v}=q

By eliminating

\displaystyle\biggl(\frac{1}{\beta c^{2}}\partial_{t}^{2}-{\bf\nabla}\cdot\frac{1}{\beta}{\bf\nabla}\biggr)P({\bf x},t)=s({\bf x},t),

\displaystyle c=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha\beta}}

III.2
Green’s function of an inhomogeneous, time-invariant material

We introduce the Green’s function
{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,t_{A})
of an inhomogeneous, time-invariant
material as the response to an impulsive point source at
{\bf x}={\bf x}_{A}
at time instant
t=t_{A}
,
observed at position
{\bf x}
and time
t
. Hence, it obeys wave
equation (
7
), with the source term
s({\bf x},t)
on the right-hand side replaced by an impulsive point source, according to

\displaystyle\biggl(\frac{1}{\beta c^{2}}\partial_{t}^{2}-{\bf\nabla}\cdot\frac{1}{\beta}{\bf\nabla}\biggr){\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,t_{A})=\delta({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{A})\delta(t-t_{A}),

(10)

where
\delta(\cdot)
is the Dirac delta function. The subscript
x
in
{\cal G}_{x}
denotes that this is the Green’s function of a space-variant material.
The Green’s function obeys the causality condition

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,t_{A})=0\quad\mbox{for}\quad t<t_{A}.

(11)

Assuming that the material is homogeneous outside a sphere with arbitrarily large but finite radius, this causality condition implies that
{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,t_{A})

is outward propagating for
|{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{A}|\to\infty
.

Since the material is time-invariant, the Green’s function is time-shift invariant, hence

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,t_{A})={\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t-t_{A},0).

(12)

In the remainder of section
III
we take
t_{A}=0
.

The Green’s function obeys the following reciprocity relation

;
Hoop95Book

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x}_{A},{\bf x}_{B},t,0)={\cal G}_{x}({\bf x}_{B},{\bf x}_{A},t,0).

(13)

Hence, in an inhomogeneous, time-invariant material,
the response to a source at
{\bf x}_{B}
, observed at
{\bf x}_{A}
, is equal to the response to a source at
{\bf x}_{A}
, observed at
{\bf x}_{B}
.

Since wave equation (
10
) contains only even-order time derivatives, the time-reversed Green’s function
{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},-t,0)
is also a solution
of this equation (with
t_{A}=0
). This acausal Green’s function is inward propagating for
|{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{A}|\to\infty
. It propagates backward through the inhomogeneous material,
converges to a sink at
{\bf x}={\bf x}_{A}
and
t=0
, after which it disappears.

We define the homogeneous Green’s function of an inhomogeneous, time-invariant material as the difference of the Green’s function and its time-reversed version, according to

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{x}^{h}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,0)={\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,0)-{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},-t,0).

(14)

Both terms on the right-hand side obey the same wave equation, with the same singularity
\delta({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{A})\delta(t)
.
This singularity cancels when we subtract the equations for the Green’s function and its time-reversed version. Hence, the homogeneous Green’s function
obeys the following equation

\displaystyle\biggl(\frac{1}{\beta c^{2}}\partial_{t}^{2}-{\bf\nabla}\cdot\frac{1}{\beta}{\bf\nabla}\biggr){\cal G}_{x}^{h}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,0)=0.

(15)

Note that
{\cal G}_{x}^{h}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,0)
is called the homogeneous Green’s function because it is a solution of this homogeneous differential equation
(hence, here “homogeneous” does not refer to the material parameters, which may still be arbitrarily inhomogeneous, but to the absence of the singularity in the wave equation).
Unlike the Green’s function
{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,0)
and its time-reversed version
{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},-t,0)
,
the homogeneous Green’s function
{\cal G}_{x}^{h}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,0)
is not singular for
{\bf x}\to{\bf x}_{A}
and
t\to 0
.

From equations (
13
) and (
14
) it follows that the homogeneous Green’s function obeys the following reciprocity relation

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{x}^{h}({\bf x}_{A},{\bf x}_{B},t,0)={\cal G}_{x}^{h}({\bf x}_{B},{\bf x}_{A},t,0).

(16)

III.3
Homogeneous Green’s function representation for an inhomogeneous, time-invariant material

In the research fields holographic imaging and inverse scattering,

The original representation is in the space-frequency domain. In the space-time domain, the homogeneous Green’s function representation reads

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{x}^{h}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,0)

\displaystyle\oint_{\cal S}\frac{1}{\beta({\bf x}^{\prime})}\Bigl[\{{\bf\nabla}^{\prime}{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}^{\prime},t,0)\}*_{t}{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x}^{\prime},{\bf x}_{A},-t,0)

\displaystyle-{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}^{\prime},t,0)*_{t}{\bf\nabla}^{\prime}{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x}^{\prime},{\bf x}_{A},-t,0)\Bigr]\cdot{\bf n}{\rm d}{\bf x}^{\prime}.

Here

is an arbitrarily-shaped closed boundary with outward pointing normal vector

(for the 3D situation,

The points indicated by

Hence,

III.4
Time-reversed acoustics in an inhomogeneous, time-invariant material

We start by reviewing the principle of time-reversed acoustics in an inhomogeneous, time-invariant material, in an intuitive way.
Consider an impulsive point source at
{\bf x}_{A}
and
t=0

in an arbitrarily inhomogeneous, time-invariant material. The response to this source,

, is recorded by receivers at
{\bf x}^{\prime}

on a closed boundary, see Figure

Next, the responses are reversed in time, yielding

The time-reversed wave field obeys the same wave equation as the original wave field. Hence, the time-reversed field emitted by the sources at the boundary

{\cal S}
to any point

inside
{\cal S}

The second term on the left-hand side,

, is the field converging to

III.5
Green’s function retrieval in an inhomogeneous, time-invariant material

Under specific circumstances, the temporal crosscorrelation of passive noise measurements at two receivers in an inhomogeneous, time-invariant material
converges to the response at one of these receivers as if there were a source at the position of the other (i.e., the Green’s function between the two receivers)

Weaver2001PRL
;
Campillo2003Science
;
Wapenaar2003GEO
;
Snieder2004PRE
;
Malcolm2004PRE
;
Roux2004JASA2
;
Haney2009GRL
;
Sabra2007APL
.
We review the mathematics behind this principle of Green’s function retrieval, using equation (
18
) as the starting point
Wapenaar2003GEO
;
Wapenaar2005JASA
.
Using reciprocity relation (
13
) and substituting equation (
14
) into the left-hand side yields

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,0)-{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},-t,0)=

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\frac{2}{\beta_{0}}\oint_{\cal S}\{{\bf\nabla}^{\prime}{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}^{\prime},t,0)\cdot{\bf n}\}*_{t}{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x}_{A},{\bf x}^{\prime},-t,0){\rm d}{\bf x}^{\prime}.

(20)

The Green’s function
{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x}_{A},{\bf x}^{\prime},t,0)
on the right-hand side represents the response to an impulsive monopole source at
{\bf x}^{\prime}
on
{\cal S}
and
t=0
,
recorded by a receiver at
{\bf x}_{A}
.
Similarly,
{\bf\nabla}^{\prime}{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}^{\prime},t,0)\cdot{\bf n}
represents the response to an impulsive dipole source at
{\bf x}^{\prime}
on
{\cal S}
and
t=0
,
recorded by a receiver at
{\bf x}
. Both responses are illustrated in Figure
2
(a). The entire right-hand side quantifies the temporal crosscorrelation of these
responses at
{\bf x}_{A}
and
{\bf x}
, integrated over all sources at
{\bf x}^{\prime}
on
{\cal S}
.
The left-hand side consists of the Green’s function
{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,0)
(minus its time-reversed version).
This is the response to an impulsive virtual source at
{\bf x}_{A}
and
t=0
, recorded by a physical receiver at
{\bf x}
, see Figure
2
(b).

Figure 2:

{\bf x}^{\prime}
and
t=0
,
recorded by receivers at
{\bf x}_{A}
and
{\bf x}
.
(b) Response to a virtual source at
{\bf x}_{A}
(indicated by the pink dot), recorded by a receiver at
{\bf x}
, obtained from the temporal crosscorrelation of the responses in (a), integrated over all
sources at
{\bf x}^{\prime}
on
{\cal S}
.

When only monopole sources are available on
{\cal S}
, we need to approximate the dipole responses on the right-hand side of equation (
20
) by monopole responses.
We already assumed that the material at and outside
{\cal S}
is homogeneous, with parameters
\alpha_{0}
,
\beta_{0}
and
c_{0}
.
If we assume in addition that
{\cal S}
is a sphere with very large radius, we may use the following approximation on
{\cal S}

\displaystyle{\bf\nabla}^{\prime}{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}^{\prime},t,0)\cdot{\bf n}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{c_{0}}\partial_{t}{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}^{\prime},t,0).

(21)

Substituting this into equation (
20
), using
\{\partial_{t}f(t)\}*_{t}g(t)=\partial_{t}\{f(t)*_{t}g(t)\}
, yields

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,0)-{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},-t,0)\approx

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt-\frac{2}{\beta_{0}c_{0}}\partial_{t}\oint_{\cal S}{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}^{\prime},t,0)*_{t}{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x}_{A},{\bf x}^{\prime},-t,0){\rm d}{\bf x}^{\prime}.

(22)

Now the right-hand side quantifies the time-derivative of the crosscorrelation of monopole responses, integrated over the sources.
An alternative form, without the time-derivative, reads
Wapenaar2006GEO

\displaystyle G_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,0)+G_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},-t,0)\approx

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\frac{2}{\beta_{0}c_{0}}\oint_{\cal S}G_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}^{\prime},t,0)*_{t}G_{x}({\bf x}_{A},{\bf x}^{\prime},-t,0){\rm d}{\bf x}^{\prime},

(23)

where the Green’s function
G_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,0)
is the response to a source
a({\bf x},t)=\delta({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{A})\delta(t)
,
{\bf b}({\bf x},t)={\bf 0}
.
Since
s({\bf x},t)=\partial_{t}a({\bf x},t)
(equation
9
) and the material is time-invariant,
we have
G_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,0)=\partial_{t}{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,0)
.
Expressions (
22
) and (
23
) are useful when the responses to all sources on
{\cal S}
are individually available,
such as in controlled-source seismic interferometry
(
Bakulin2006GEO
;
Schuster2009Book
;
Neut2011GEO
)
.
Of course approximations are introduced when the sources are not available on a closed boundary, similar as in
time-reversed acoustics, as noted in section
III.4
.

For the derivation of Green’s function retrieval from ambient noise,
consider the situation in which the sources at all
{\bf x}^{\prime}
on
{\cal S}
are simultaneously acting noise sources
N({\bf x}^{\prime},t)
.
In that case, the fields at
{\bf x}_{A}
and
{\bf x}
are given by

p({\bf x}_{A},t)=\oint_{{\cal S}}{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x}_{A},{\bf x}^{\prime},t,0)*_{t}N({\bf x}^{\prime},t){\rm d}{\bf x}^{\prime}
and

p({\bf x},t)=\oint_{{\cal S}}{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}^{\prime\prime},t,0)*_{t}N({\bf x}^{\prime\prime},t){\rm d}{\bf x}^{\prime\prime}
,
respectively. We assume that the noise sources are mutually uncorrelated,
according to
\langle N({\bf x}^{\prime\prime},t)*_{t}N({\bf x}^{\prime},-t)\rangle=\delta({\bf x}^{\prime}-{\bf x}^{\prime\prime})C(t)
.
Here
C(t)
is the temporal autocorrelation of the noise (which is assumed to be the same for all sources)
and
\langle\cdot\rangle
stands for ensemble averaging, which in practice is replaced by averaging over very long time.
Further,
\delta({\bf x}^{\prime}-{\bf x}^{\prime\prime})
is a delta function defined in
{\cal S}
.
The crosscorrelation of the fields at
{\bf x}_{A}
and
{\bf x}
thus gives
Wapenaar2006GEO

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\langle p({\bf x},t)*_{t}p({\bf x}_{A},-t)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\oint_{{\cal S}}{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}^{\prime},t,0)*_{t}{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x}_{A},{\bf x}^{\prime},-t,0)*_{t}C(t){\rm d}{\bf x}^{\prime}.

(24)

Convolving both sides of equation (
22
) with the autocorrelation
C(t)
,
using
\{\partial_{t}f(t)\}*_{t}g(t)=\partial_{t}\{f(t)*_{t}g(t)\}
and equation (
24
) to simplify the right-hand side, we obtain

\{{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,0)-{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},-t,0)\}*_{t}C(t)\approx-\frac{2}{\beta_{0}c_{0}}\partial_{t}\langle p({\bf x},t)*_{t}p({\bf x}_{A},-t)\rangle.

(25)

This is the basic expression for Green’s function retrieval from noise, also known as ambient-noise interferometry

(
Weaver2001PRL
;
Derode2003JASA
;
Wapenaar2003GEO
;
Campillo2003Science
;
Snieder2004PRE
;
Wapenaar2006GEO
)
.
It states that the time-derivative of the crosscorrelation of two noise recordings at receiver positions
{\bf x}_{A}
and
{\bf x}
,
averaged over long enough time, converges to the
Green’s function between
{\bf x}_{A}
and
{\bf x}
(minus its time-reversed version), convolved with the temporal autocorrelation of the noise.
Hence, the receiver at
{\bf x}_{A}
is turned into a virtual source.

An alternative form of equation (
25
), in terms of the Green’s function
G_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,0)=\partial_{t}{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,0)
, reads

\{G_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,0)+G_{x}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},-t,0)\}*_{t}C(t)\approx\frac{2}{\beta_{0}c_{0}}\langle p({\bf x},t)*_{t}p({\bf x}_{A},-t)\rangle.

(26)

In most practical situations the sources are not distributed along a closed boundary, the noise sources may be mutually correlated, the noise spectra may not be the same for all sources,
the wave fields may not obey a simple scalar wave equation, etc.
Hence, the expressions derived in this section are at best approximations for real situations. Much research has been done to overcome these limitations, of which we mention
iterative correlation
Stehly2008JGR
, multidimensional deconvolution
Wapenaar2010JASA
;
Neut2011GEO
, directional balancing
Curtis2010GEO

and generalized interferometry
Fichtner2017GJI
. A further discussion is beyond the scope of this paper.

IV
Time-reversed acoustics and Green’s function retrieval in homogeneous, time-variant materials

We discuss time-reversed acoustics and Green’s function retrieval in homogeneous, time-variant materials, and their mutual relation.
The setup is analogous to that in section
III
for inhomogeneous, time-invariant materials, which facilitates the comparison of the methodologies for both types of material.
In section
IV.1
we discuss the wave equation for a homogeneous, time-variant material, as a special case of the equations in section
II
.
In section
IV.2
we discuss the Green’s function of a homogeneous, time-variant material.
In section
IV.3
we discuss the propagator matrix for a homogeneous, time-variant material.
In section
IV.4
we use this propagator matrix as the basis for deriving a representation of the homogeneous Green’s function of a homogeneous, time-variant material.
In sections
IV.5
and
IV.6
we show how this homogeneous Green’s function representation leads to
time-reversed acoustics and Green’s function retrieval, respectively, in homogeneous, time-variant materials.

IV.1
Wave equation for a homogeneous, time-variant material

We consider a homogeneous, arbitrarily time-variant material, with parameters
\alpha(t)
and
\beta(t)
.
Substituting constitutive relations (
3
) and (
4
) into equations (
1
) and (
2
),
using the fact that
\alpha(t)
and
\beta(t)
are space-invariant, we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}U+\frac{1}{\beta}{\bf\nabla}\cdot{\bf V}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a,

(27)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\bf V}+\frac{1}{\alpha}{\bf\nabla}U

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf b}.

(28)

For acoustic waves these expressions become
-\partial_{t}\Theta+\frac{1}{\rho}{\bf\nabla}\cdot{\bf m}=q
and
\partial_{t}{\bf m}-\frac{1}{\kappa}{\bf\nabla}\Theta={\bf f}
.
We continue with the general notation of equations (
27
) and (
28
), so that all that follows also holds for 2D SH, TE and TM waves.

The parameters
\alpha(t)
and
\beta(t)
in equations (
27
) and (
28
) may vary continuously with time.
For a material with piecewise continuous parameters, these equations are supplemented with boundary conditions.
At a time boundary, where the parameters undergo a finite jump, the boundary conditions
state that
U
and
{\bf V}
are continuous over that boundary.

By eliminating
{\bf V}
from equations (
27
) and (
28
) we obtain the second order wave equation

\displaystyle\bigl(\partial_{t}\beta\partial_{t}-\beta c^{2}{\bf\nabla}^{2}\bigr)U({\bf x},t)=s({\bf x},t),

(29)

with
c(t)
being the time-variant propagation velocity, defined again as
c=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha\beta}}
, and
s({\bf x},t)
the source distribution, defined as

\displaystyle s=\partial_{t}(\beta a)-{\bf\nabla}\cdot{\bf b}.

(30)

IV.2
Green’s function of a homogeneous, time-variant material

We introduce the Green’s function
{\cal G}_{t}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,t_{A})
of a homogeneous, time-variant
material as the response to an impulsive point source at
{\bf x}={\bf x}_{A}
at time instant
t=t_{A}
,
observed at position
{\bf x}
and time
t
. Hence, it obeys wave
equation (
29
), with the source term
s({\bf x},t)
on the right-hand side replaced by an impulsive point source, according to

\displaystyle\bigl(\partial_{t}\beta\partial_{t}-\beta c^{2}{\bf\nabla}^{2}\bigr){\cal G}_{t}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,t_{A})=\delta({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{A})\delta(t-t_{A}).

(31)

The subscript
t
in
{\cal G}_{t}
denotes that this is the Green’s function of a time-variant material.
The Green’s function obeys the causality condition

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{t}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,t_{A})=0\quad\mbox{for}\quad t<t_{A}.

(32)

Assuming that the material is time-invariant after an arbitrarily large but finite time, this causality condition implies that
{\cal G}_{t}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,t_{A})
is
outward propagating for
|{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{A}|\to\infty
.
Analytical expressions of
{\cal G}_{t}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,t_{A})
for the special case of a homogeneous, time-invariant material, are given in Appendix
A.1
.
Here we continue with expressions for a time-variant material.

Since the material is homogeneous, the Green’s function is space-shift invariant, hence

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{t}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,t_{A})={\cal G}_{t}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{A},{\bf 0},t,t_{A}).

(33)

In the remainder of section
IV
we take
{\bf x}_{A}={\bf 0}
.

We introduce an acausal Green’s function
{\cal G}_{t}^{a}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t,t_{A})
,
obeying the same wave equation as the causal Green’s function (equation (
31
), with
{\bf x}_{A}={\bf 0}
),
but with the acausality condition

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{t}^{a}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t,t_{A})=0\quad\mbox{for}\quad t>t_{A}.

(34)

Assuming that the material is time-invariant before an arbitrarily large but finite negative time, this acausality condition implies that

{\cal G}_{t}^{a}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t,t_{A})
is inward propagating for
|{\bf x}|\to\infty
. It propagates backward through the time-variant material,
converges to a sink at
{\bf x}={\bf 0}
and
t=t_{A}
, after which it disappears.
Note, however, that since the material is time-variant, this acausal Green’s function is not the time-reversal of the causal Green’s function,
i.e.,
{\cal G}_{t}^{a}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t,0)\neq{\cal G}_{t}({\bf x},{\bf 0},-t,0)
.

The causal and acausal Green’s functions are mutually related via the reciprocity relation
Wapenaar2025RS

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{t}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t_{A},t_{B})={\cal G}_{t}^{a}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t_{B},t_{A}).

(35)

Hence, in a homogeneous, time-variant material, the causal response to a source at
t_{B}
, observed at
t_{A}
, is equal to the acausal response observed at
t_{B}
,
absorbed by a sink at
t_{A}
. This is a useful relation for
t_{A}>t_{B}
. For
t_{A}<t_{B}
, equation (
35
) reduces to the trivial relation
0=0
,
on account of equations (
32
) and (
34
).

We define the homogeneous Green’s function of a homogeneous, time-variant material, as the difference of the causal and acausal Green’s functions, according to

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{t}^{h}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t,t_{A})={\cal G}_{t}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t,t_{A})-{\cal G}_{t}^{a}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t,t_{A}).

(36)

Both terms on the right-hand side obey the same wave equation, with the same singularity
\delta({\bf x})\delta(t-t_{A})
.
This singularity cancels when we subtract the equations for the causal and acausal Green’s functions. Hence, the homogeneous Green’s function
obeys the following equation

\displaystyle\bigl(\partial_{t}\beta\partial_{t}-\beta c^{2}{\bf\nabla}^{2}\bigr){\cal G}_{t}^{h}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t,t_{A})=0.

(37)

Unlike the Green’s function
{\cal G}_{t}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t,t_{A})
and its acausal counterpart
{\cal G}_{t}^{a}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t,t_{A})
,
the homogeneous Green’s function
{\cal G}_{t}^{h}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t,t_{A})
is not singular for
{\bf x}\to{\bf 0}
and
t\to t_{A}
.

From equations (
35
) and (
36
) it follows that the homogeneous Green’s function obeys the following reciprocity relation

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{t}^{h}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t_{A},t_{B})=-{\cal G}_{t}^{h}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t_{B},t_{A}).

(38)

In the next section we discuss the propagator matrix for a homogeneous, time-variant material. The elements of this matrix can be expressed in terms of the
homogeneous Green’s function
{\cal G}_{t}^{h}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t,t_{A})
. The propagator matrix formalism will be used in section
IV.4
as a basis for deriving
a representation of the homogeneous Green’s function for a homogeneous, time-variant material
(as the counterpart of the representation of equation (
17
) for an inhomogeneous, time-invariant material).

IV.3
Propagator matrix of a homogeneous, time-variant material

We reorganize equations (
27
) and (
28
) into a matrix-vector wave equation for a homogeneous, time-variant material,
as the counterpart of a matrix-vector wave equation for an inhomogeneous, time-invariant material

Corones75JMAA
;
Kosloff83GEO
;
Fishman84JMP
;
Ursin83GEO
;
Wapenaar86GP2
;
Loseth2007GJI
.
This equation will form the basis for discussing the propagator matrix of a homogeneous, time-variant material.

We reduce the number of field quantities in equations (
27
) and (
28
) by defining a scalar wave field
V_{\rm d}({\bf x},t)
as

\displaystyle V_{\rm d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf\nabla}\cdot{\bf V},

(39)

where subscript
{\rm d}
refers to “divergence”. Using this in equations (
27
) and (
28
), we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}U+\frac{1}{\beta}V_{\rm d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a,

(40)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}V_{\rm d}+\frac{1}{\alpha}{\bf\nabla}^{2}U

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b_{\rm d},

(41)

with

\displaystyle b_{\rm d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf\nabla}\cdot{\bf b}.

(42)

Equations (
40
) and (
41
) can be reorganized into a matrix-vector wave equation, according to

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\bf q}={\bf A}{\bf q}+{\bf d},

(43)

with

\displaystyle{\bf q}=\begin{pmatrix}U\\
V_{\rm d}\end{pmatrix},\quad{\bf A}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\frac{1}{\beta}\\
-\frac{1}{\alpha}{\bf\nabla}^{2}&0\end{pmatrix},\quad{\bf d}=\begin{pmatrix}a\\
b_{\rm d}\end{pmatrix},

(44)

where
{\bf q}({\bf x},t)
is a wave field vector,
{\bf A}(t)
an operator matrix and
{\bf d}({\bf x},t)
a source vector.
The parameters
\alpha(t)
and
\beta(t)
in operator matrix
{\bf A}(t)
may vary continuously with time.
For a material with piecewise continuous parameters, equation (
43
) is supplemented with boundary conditions:
at each time boundary, the wave field vector
{\bf q}({\bf x},t)
is continuous over that boundary.

We introduce the
2\times 2
propagator matrix
{\bf W}({\bf x},t,t_{A})
as the solution of equation (
43
) for the source-free situation

Salem2015arXiv
;
Torrent2018PRB
;
Wapenaar2025RS
, hence

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\bf W}={\bf A}{\bf W}.

(45)

For
t=t_{A}
it obeys the initial condition

\displaystyle{\bf W}({\bf x},t_{A},t_{A})={\bf I}\delta({\bf x}),

(46)

where
{\bf I}
is the identity matrix.
The propagator matrix
{\bf W}({\bf x},t,t_{A})
forms the counterpart of the propagator matrix for an inhomogeneous, time-invariant material

Thomson50JAP
;
Haskell53BSSA
;
Gilbert66GEO
;
Woodhouse74GJR
;
Kennett79GJRAS
.
From equations (
43
), (
45
) and (
46
), assuming
\alpha(t)
and
\beta(t)
vary continuously and
there are no sources between
t_{A}
and
t
, it follows that
{\bf q}({\bf x},t)
can be expressed as

\displaystyle{\bf q}({\bf x},t)={\bf W}({\bf x},t,t_{A})*_{x}{\bf q}({\bf x},t_{A}).

(47)

Here
t
may be larger or smaller than, or equal to
t_{A}
.
The symbol
*_{x}
stands for a 3D or 2D spatial convolution, according to
f({\bf x})*_{x}g({\bf x})=\int f({\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime})g({\bf x}^{\prime}){\rm d}{\bf x}^{\prime}
.

Let
t_{1}\dots t_{n}\dots t_{N-1}
be time instants where the parameters
\alpha(t)
and
\beta(t)
may be discontinuous. Then, by applying equation (
47
) recursively
(starting with
t_{A}=t_{0}
), we obtain

\displaystyle{\bf W}({\bf x},t_{N},t_{0})={\bf W}({\bf x},t_{N},t_{N-1})*_{x}\cdots*_{x}{\bf W}({\bf x},t_{n},t_{n-1})*_{x}\cdots*_{x}{\bf W}({\bf x},t_{1},t_{0}).

(48)

This expression is useful for numerical modeling, in which case the
t_{n}
are ordered from small to large (an example is given at the end of this section).
However, this expression remains valid for arbitrarily ordered
t_{n}
.

We partition the
2\times 2
matrix
{\bf W}({\bf x},t,t_{A})
as follows

\displaystyle{\bf W}({\bf x},t,t_{A})=\begin{pmatrix}W^{U,U}&W^{U,V}\\
&\\
W^{V,U}&W^{V,V}\end{pmatrix}({\bf x},t,t_{A}).

(49)

According to equation (11.27) in
Wapenaar2025RS
,
W^{U,V}({\bf x},t,t_{A})
is related to the causal and acausal Green’s functions via

\displaystyle W^{U,V}({\bf x},t,t_{A})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{t}^{a}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t,t_{A})-{\cal G}_{t}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t,t_{A})

(50)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\cal G}_{t}^{h}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t,t_{A}).

According to equations (11.18), (11.20) and (11.21) in
Wapenaar2025RS
, the other components of the propagator matrix can be expressed in terms of

W^{U,V}({\bf x},t,t_{A})
. Combining those relations with equation (
50
) we obtain

\displaystyle W^{V,V}({\bf x},t,t_{A})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta(t)\partial_{t}{\cal G}_{t}^{h}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t,t_{A}),

(51)

\displaystyle W^{U,U}({\bf x},t,t_{A})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\beta(t_{A})\partial_{t_{A}}{\cal G}_{t}^{h}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t,t_{A}),

(52)

\displaystyle W^{V,U}({\bf x},t,t_{A})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta(t)\beta(t_{A})\partial_{t}\partial_{t_{A}}{\cal G}_{t}^{h}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t,t_{A}).

(53)

Equations (
49
) – (
53
) show that the propagator matrix is expressed in terms of the
Green’s function and its derivatives. This property is restricted to homogeneous, time-variant materials
(i.e., it does not hold for the analogous case of inhomogeneous, time-invariant materials).
This is explained as follows.
For inhomogeneous, time-invariant materials, the initial condition of the propagator matrix (equation (
46
))
is replaced by a boundary condition, but the causality condition of the Green’s function (equation (
32
)) remains
valid (i.e., it is not replaced by a boundary condition).
Therefore, since time and space are interchangeable for the propagator matrix but not for the Green’s function,
the relations between the propagator matrix and the Green’s function, as expressed by
equations (
50
) – (
53
), have no counterpart for inhomogeneous, time-invariant materials.
In section

IV.4
Homogeneous Green’s function representation for a homogeneous, time-variant material

As a counterpart of equation (
17
), which is a representation of the homogeneous Green’s function
{\cal G}_{x}^{h}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{A},t,0)
in an inhomogeneous, time-invariant material,
here we seek for a representation of the homogeneous Green’s function
{\cal G}_{t}^{h}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t,t_{A})
in a homogeneous, time-variant material.
To this end, we first formulate a representation of the propagator matrix
{\bf W}({\bf x},t,t_{A})
.
As a special case of equation (
48
) we write

\displaystyle{\bf W}({\bf x},t,t_{A})={\bf W}({\bf x},t,t^{\prime})*_{x}{\bf W}({\bf x},t^{\prime},t_{A}).

(54)

In general, the order of
t
,
t^{\prime}
and
t_{A}
is arbitrary, but in the following we assume that
t^{\prime}
is smaller than both
t_{A}
and
t
.
Hence, on the right-hand side, the elements of matrix
{\bf W}({\bf x},t^{\prime},t_{A})
(with
t^{\prime}<t_{A}
)
consist only of (derivatives of) the acausal part of the homogeneous Green’s functions.
On the other hand, the elements of matrix
{\bf W}({\bf x},t,t^{\prime})
(with
t^{\prime}<t
)
consist only of (derivatives of) the causal part of the homogeneous Green’s functions.
Using the partitioning defined in equation (
49
), we thus obtain for the upper-right element of
{\bf W}({\bf x},t,t_{A})

\displaystyle W^{U,V}({\bf x},t,t_{A})=W^{U,U}({\bf x},t,t^{\prime})*_{x}W^{U,V}({\bf x},t^{\prime},t_{A})+W^{U,V}({\bf x},t,t^{\prime})*_{x}W^{V,V}({\bf x},t^{\prime},t_{A}),

(55)

with
t^{\prime}<t_{A}
and
t^{\prime}<t
, or, substituting equations (
50
) – (
52
),

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{t}^{h}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t,t_{A})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta(t^{\prime})\Bigl[\partial_{t^{\prime}}{\cal G}_{t}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t,t^{\prime})*_{x}{\cal G}_{t}^{a}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t^{\prime},t_{A})

(56)

\displaystyle-{\cal G}_{t}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t,t^{\prime})*_{x}\partial_{t^{\prime}}{\cal G}_{t}^{a}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t^{\prime},t_{A})\Bigr].

This is the homogeneous Green’s function representation for a homogeneous, time-variant material.
It is the counterpart of equation (
17
), with coordinates
{\bf x}
,
{\bf x}^{\prime}
,
{\bf x}_{A}
and
t
replaced by
t
,
t^{\prime}
,
t_{A}
and
{\bf x}
, respectively.
Furthermore,
{\bf\nabla}^{\prime}
is replaced by
\partial_{t^{\prime}}
and
1/\beta({\bf x}^{\prime})
by
\beta(t^{\prime})
.
Whereas the temporal convolution in equation (
17
) is a 1D integral,
and the integral over
{\bf x}^{\prime}
is a 2D surface integral (for the 3D situation) or a 1D line integral (for the 2D situation),
the spatial convolution in equation (
56
) is a 3D or 2D integral, respectively, and there is no integral over
t^{\prime}
(
t^{\prime}
has only a single value in equation (
56
)).
Hence, the total number of dimensions of the integrals is the same in both representations.
Last, but not least, the time-reversed Green’s function
{\cal G}_{x}({\bf x}^{\prime},{\bf x}_{A},-t,0)
and its derivative in equation (
17
) are replaced
by the acausal Green’s function
{\cal G}_{t}^{a}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t^{\prime},t_{A})
and its derivative in equation (
56
).

Whereas time-reversal can be applied to recordings of physical measurements, acausal responses cannot be obtained from physical measurements.
Therefore we need to modify equation (
56
), or more generally, equation (
54
), before we can use it in practical situations.
In particular, we need to use symmetry properties of the Green’s functions
to transform equation (
54
) into a form that complies with physics. In section
IV.5
we transform the acausal matrix
{\bf W}({\bf x},t^{\prime},t_{A})
,
propagating from
t_{A}
to
t^{\prime}
(with
t^{\prime}<t_{A}
), into a causal
matrix, propagating from
-t_{A}
to
-t^{\prime}
(with
-t^{\prime}>-t_{A}
) in a time-reversed material. In this case, equation (
54
) will form the theoretical basis for
time-reversed acoustics. In section
IV.6
we transform the acausal matrix
{\bf W}({\bf x},t^{\prime},t_{A})
into a causal matrix, propagating from
t^{\prime}
to
t_{A}
(with
t_{A}>t^{\prime}
).
In that case, equation (
54

Next, we formalize this principle of idealized time-reversed acoustics in a homogeneous, time-variant material, using the representation of equation (

\displaystyle\bigl(\partial_{t}\beta(-t)\partial_{t}-\beta(-t)c^{2}(-t){\bf\nabla}^{2}\bigr){\cal G}_{t,{\rm TR}}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t,-t_{A})=\delta({\bf x})\delta(t+t_{A}),

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{t,{\rm TR}}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t,-t_{A})=0\quad\mbox{for}\quad t<-t_{A}.

\displaystyle\bigl(\partial_{t}\beta(t)\partial_{t}-\beta(t)c^{2}(t){\bf\nabla}^{2}\bigr){\cal G}_{t,{\rm TR}}({\bf x},{\bf 0},-t,-t_{A})=\delta({\bf x})\delta(-t+t_{A}),

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{t,{\rm TR}}({\bf x},{\bf 0},-t,-t_{A})=0\quad\mbox{for}\quad-t<-t_{A}.

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{t}^{a}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t,t_{A})={\cal G}_{t,{\rm TR}}({\bf x},{\bf 0},-t,-t_{A}).

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{t}({\bf x},{\bf 0},t,t_{A})

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{t,{\rm TR}}^{a}({\bf x},{\bf 0},-t,-t_{A}),

V
Conclusions

We started this paper with a review of the principles of classical time-reversed acoustics and Green’s function retrieval for inhomogeneous, time-invariant materials.
Along the same lines, we discussed the counterparts of these principles for homogeneous, time-variant materials, and compared those with the classical principles.
One observation from this comparison
is that the space and time coordinates are interchanged between the methods for the two classes of material.
As a consequence, temporal convolutions and correlations in the classical approaches for inhomogeneous, time-invariant materials,
are replaced by spatial convolutions and correlations in their counterparts for homogeneous, time-variant materials. However, the interchangement of space and time
does not apply to causality conditions, which are always related to the time coordinate.
This complicates the translation of methods from one class of material to the other and vice versa.
Another complicating factor is that the number of time dimensions (one) is not the same as the number of space dimensions (two or three).

For time-reversed acoustics and for Green’s function retrieval, we separately discuss the relations between applications in the two classes of material.

•

The principle of classical time-reversed acoustics in an inhomogeneous, time-invariant material involves in essence:

1.

Emission of a wave field by a source at
{\bf x}_{A}

2.

{\bf x}_{A}

The emitted wave field focuses at
t_{A}
