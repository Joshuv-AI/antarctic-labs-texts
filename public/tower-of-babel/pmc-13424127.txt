Nrf2/Bach1-ARE pathway are involved in the ameliorative effects of astaxanthin on D-galactose-induced liver and brain aging and injury
Authors: Panpan Zhang, Xun Zhang, Siyu Ma, Mengqiang Jiang, Wenhong Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Astaxanthin (ATX), a natural antioxidant whose benefits in age-related liver and kidney damage remain unclear. We established a D-galactose-induced ageing model in rats and observed the daily behaviour of the rats. Using staining methods to detect ROS, apoptosis and histopathological changes in liver and brain tissue. Determination of antioxidant levels of IL-2, IL-6 and AGES in rats. Assessment of cognitive function using the Morris water maze and ChAT. The mRNA and protein expression levels of Nrf2, Bach1, SOD1, SOD2, HO-1 were determined by real-time PCR and Western blotting. To investigate the role of the Nrf2/Bach1-ARE pathway, we used ML385, a specific inhibitor of the Nrf2 pathway, to treat rats in the inhibitor group. Aging rats showed impaired learning and memory, along with decreased levels of neurotransmitters and antioxidant enzymes. ATX and vitamin E (VE) interventions significantly alleviated these symptoms and activated the Nrf2/Bach1-ARE pathway in liver and brain tissues of aged SD rats. Furthermore, the protective effects of ATX were attenuated by the addition of the Nrf2 inhibitor ML385. ATX reduces oxidative stress and ameliorates liver and brain damage in aged rats via activation of the Nrf2/Bach1-ARE pathway.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52939-0.

Introduction

Aging is a normal physiological process; however, it is considered a risk factor for age-related diseases, including cardiovascular diseases, cancer, immune dysfunction, and neurodegenerative disorders1–4. With the global increase in the aging population, the socioeconomic burden associated with age-related diseases is also growing. An imbalance between oxidative and antioxidant activity in the body, tending toward excessive or overly reactive free radical accumulation, leads to overproduction of ROS. The excess ROS damage intracellular DNA, proteins, and lipids, thus resulting in cellular dysfunction, which in turn may trigger cellular senescence and aging processes5. Therefore, decreasing oxidative stress in the body to mitigate aging is a viable strategy. Studies have demonstrated that many natural plant extracts exert antioxidant effects that help slow aging6,7. Developing these naturally antioxidant-rich compounds is therefore a promising anti-aging strategy.

ATX, a natural carotenoid, has demonstrated excellent antioxidant properties and offers advantages such as safety, efficiency, and affordability8. Astaxanthin has been demonstrated to slow the progression of various age-related disorders, including neurodegenerative diseases, bone-related diseases, cancer, and cardiovascular conditions9. Some studies have indicated that astaxanthin exerts its antioxidant effects by activating Nrf2, which in turn binds antioxidant response elements (AREs), whereas Bach1 competes with Nrf2 for ARE binding in the nucleus, and its expression increases with aging10,11. Whether the anti-aging effects of astaxanthin involve the Nrf2/Bach1-ARE pathway, and what the role of Bach1 might be in this process, remains unclear. The liver, a key organ for metabolic and synthetic functions, is a major site of redox reactions, and produces high levels of ROS while also being particularly vulnerable to ROS-related damage12. The brain, with its high specific metabolic activity, is particularly sensitive to oxidative damage13. Therefore, liver and brain tissues are prominently affected during aging and were the main focus of this study. Many Studies have indicated that the signaling pathway of Nrf2/Keap1-ARE plays an important role in protecting the organism from oxidative damage caused by excessive ROS. Nrf2, as the core regulatory factor of this pathway, can encode antioxidant proteins through interaction with ARE by dominating regulation of antioxidant stress response genes and also acts as a sensor for exogenous toxic substances and oxidative stress14. Usually, the activity of Nrf2 is inhibited because it is binded to Keap1 protein which is one of cytoplasmic chaperone molecule in the cytoplasm. While the activity of Nrf2 is released with antioxidative response occuring and Nrf2 being decoupled from Keap1, in the same time, which upregulating the expression of several antioxidant or detoxifying enzymes such as Ho-1 and SOD15.AS one of repressor of Nrf2, Bach1 can compete with Nrf2 for ARE. When it formes the “Bach1-ARE pathway”,it will downregulate the levels of antioxidant enzyme genes.Therefore, how to regulate the nuclear balance of the Nrf2/Bach1-ARE pathway should be a innovative strategy against antioxidation16. This strategy has been confirmed by studies, for example, dimethyl fumarate can raise the activity of SOD and Ho-1 in cells and attenuate the levels of MDA ROS by enhancing Nrf2 expression in the nucleus14. Many exeperiments have shown that Nrf2 possesses the effects of antioxidation and anti-aging, but the relationship and changes between Nrf2 and Bach1 in the course have not yet been reported.This research was aimed at elucidating the protective effects of astaxanthin in the liver and brain during aging, and investigating the roles of, and changes in, the Nrf2/Bach1-ARE pathway during this process.

Materials and methods

Materials

Experimental animals

A total of 64 male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats, 6 weeks of age and weighing 180–220 g, were provided by Hangzhou Ziyuan Laboratory Animal Technology (license No: SCXK (浙) 2019-0004). The animals were housed in compliance with the Regulations for the Administration of Affairs Concerning Experimental Animals.The study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Bengbu Medical College (approval No.: 2023 − 647). Before the experiment, the rats were acclimated for 1 week under controlled conditions: ambient temperature of 22–26 °C, humidity of 50–60%, and natural light-dark cycles, and were given ad libitum access to food and water.

Reagents and instruments

Astaxanthin (Solarbio, 427-61-7); Vitamin E (Yuan Ye, S66679); D-Galactose (Aladdin, G100366); DPPH (Aladdin, D141366); kits for T-SOD (A001-1-2), T-AOC (A015-3-1), GSH-PX (A005-1-2), CAT (A007-1-1), MDA (A003-1-2) were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute. ELISA kits for IL-6 (ml0642920), IL-2 (ml102822-1), and AGES (ml003305) were obtained from Meilian; reactive oxygen species (ROS) assay kit (Bestbio, BB-470515); superoxide anion fluorescent probe (dihydroethidium, DHE) kit (S0063, Beyotime). HE staining kit (Servicebio, GP1031). Western blot antibodies used included Nrf2 (16396-1-AP); SOD1 (24127-1-AP); SOD2 (66474-1-lg); HO-1 (66473-1-lg); GAPDH (10494-1-AP); Lamin B1(12987-1-AP); β-Actin༈11313-2-AP༉were obtained from Proteintech; Bach1 (ab300130, Abcam).Reverse transcription kit (Thermo Fisher, K1622) and real-time PCR detection kit (Kangrun Biotech, A304-05). Nuclear protein extraction kit (KGB5302, KeyGEN).

Instruments used included a microplate reader (BioTek, USA), paraffin microtome (LEICA, Germany), micropipette (Eppendorf, Germany), fully automated inverted fluorescence microscope (ZEISS, Germany), real-time PCR instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and a WB imager (Bio-Rad, USA).

Methods

Animal grouping and treatment

After 1 week of adaptive feeding, the 64 SD rats were randomly divided into groups 1–8 (n = 8 per group) in the following order: control, model (200 mg/kg D-gal), VE (200 mg/kgD-gal + 60 mg/kg VE), L-ATX (200 mg/kgD-gal + 5 mg/kg ATX), M-ATX (200 mg/kgD-gal + 10 mg/kg ATX), H-ATX (200 mg/kgD-gal + 15 mg/kg ATX), H-ATX+ML385 (200 mg/kgD-gal + 15 mg/kgATX + 30 mg/kgML385),and ATX (15 mg/kg ATX), with treatments administered for 9 weeks17,18.The rats of the Model group were subcutaneously injected with 200 mg/kg D-gal into the nape of the neck. The rats in the Control group were injected with the same volume of normal saline into the nape of the neck. The rats in each ATX-intervention group, in addition to being injecting the same D-gal treatment as the Model group, were given intragastric administration of 5 mg/kg(L-ATX), 10 mg/kg(M-ATX) and 15 mg/kg ATX(H-ATX), as well as 60 mg/kg VE (VE group) respectively. The rats in the ATX group were subcutaneously injected the same volume of normal saline and were simultaneously given intragastric administration of 15 mg/kg ATX. The rats in ML385-inhibition group, besides receiving the same H-ATX intervention, were treated with 30 mg/kg ML385. The intervention experiment sustained for 9 weeks, and the schema was continuously in the state of test for 5 days/ per week with 2 days of rest.

ML385 is one of recognized Nrf2 inhibitor and was used in the mechanistic research of our study. Its dosage in this study was decided by reference to relevant studies reported by Wang R et al.19and Taufani, IP et al.At the end of the experiment, rats were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 3% pentobarbital sodium (90 mg/kg). Blood was collected from the abdominal aorta; serum was extracted; and liver, brain, thymus, and spleen samples were harvested and stored at -80 °C. Portions of brain and liver tissue were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and stored at 4 °C. All experiments in this manuscript were planned and conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.The entire experimental procedure is shown in Fig. 1.

Observation of general condition and organ coefficients

The general condition of each rat was observed, including activity level, food intake, coat appearance, mental state, and stress response. The organ coefficients for the liver, brain, thymus, and spleen were calculated as follows: organ coefficient = organ weight (mg)/body weight (g).

Hematoxylin and eosin staining

Liver and brain tissues fixed in paraformaldehyde were dehydrated, embedded, sectioned, and stained. After mounting, the sections were observed under a microscope to evaluate tissue morphology and structural damage across groups. In hematoxylin and eosin staining, the cytoplasm appears purplish-red, and the nuclei are stained purple-blue.

Detection of antioxidant enzyme levels in liver and brain tissue with assay kits

Liver and brain tissue homogenates were prepared, and the levels of antioxidant enzymes in the liver and brain were measured according to the assay kit instructions.

ROS staining in liver and brain tissues

Fresh liver and brain tissues were prepared as frozen sections, rewarmed at room temperature, and incubated in the dark at 37 °C with 100 µmol/L dihydroethidium dye for 1 h. After washing and nuclear staining, excess dye was removed, and the sections were mounted for microscopy. DHE staining intensity (red fluorescence) indicated ROS levels, whereas DAPI staining (blue fluorescence) labeled cell nuclei.

TUNEL assay for detecting cell apoptosis

Paraffin-embedded liver and brain sections were deparaffinized, treated with Proteinase K working solution for 15 min at room temperature, and washed with PBS. TUNEL staining solution was added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. After PBS washing and DAPI nuclear staining, sections were mounted for microscopic examination. Apoptotic cells were labeled with red fluorescence after TUNEL staining, whereas nuclei were stained with DAPI and showed blue fluorescence.

ELISA for detecting IL-2, IL-6, and AGEs

Serum levels of IL-2, IL-6, and AGEs were measured with ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Immunohistochemistry for AGEs in liver and brain tissue and ChAT in brain tissue

Paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin for 30 min. Primary antibodies (AGEs, 1:1000; ChAT, 1:1000) were added, and sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C. After incubation with HRP-labeled secondary antibodies for 50 min, DAB (3,3’-diaminobenzidine) was applied and was followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Sections were then observed under a microscope.

Morris water maze task

Based on the method described by Vorhees et al.20, we used the Morris water maze task to assess spatial learning and memory through a place navigation test and spatial probe test. On the first day, a visible platform (10 cm in diameter) was placed in the pool. Subsequently, the rats underwent 5 days of training with the platform submerged 1–2 cm below the surface of the water, which was dyed black. On day 1, the rats were trained to locate the visible escape platform; over the following 5 days, the rats were trained to locate the hidden escape platform. Rats were placed in the water facing the wall in each quadrant in sequence. If the rat did not reach the platform within 60 s, it was guided to the platform and was allowed to remain there for 15 s. On day 7, a spatial probe test was conducted. Rats were placed in the water facing the wall in the third quadrant, and the time taken to reach the platform, known as the escape latency, was recorded. After a 2-hour rest, the platform was removed, and the number of times in which the rats crossed the target platform area and the time spent in that area within 60 s were recorded.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from rat liver and brain tissues with TRIzol reagent. cDNA was synthesized through reverse transcription according to the kit protocol. A 20 µL reaction system was prepared, and a two-step magnification method was performed. Primers were synthesized by Shanghai Shenggong Biotechnology. Primer sequences are shown in Table 1.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from rat liver and brain tissues with lysis buffer. After protein concentrations were determined, equal amounts of protein were subjected to electrophoresis and transferred to membranes. After blocking, the membranes were incubated at 4 °C with primary antibodies to GPX1 (1:1000), SOD2 (1:2000), and GAPDH (1:2000) for 12 h. After being washed with TBST, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies (1:10000) for 1.5 h at room temperature. After further washing, the membranes were exposed to imaging film after a 2-minute soak in developing solution.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0 software, and GraphPad Prism 8.0 was used to generate graphs. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (± S). For data meeting homogeneity of variance, ANOVA was used for comparisons, followed by LSD-t tests for pairwise group comparisons. For data not meeting homogeneity of variance, Dunnett’s T3 test was applied. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Changes in rats’ general condition and organ coefficients

Rats in the control group displayed glossy and smooth fur, and firm and elastic skin, and were highly agile. In contrast, rats in the model group exhibited dull, dry fur with a yellowish tint and noticeable hair loss; rough, dry skin lacking elasticity; and sluggish movement. All intervention groups showed significant improvements in behavior, skin, and fur condition. The liver index and spleen index increased, and the thymus index decreased, in the model group of rats, and no significant differences in body weight were observed among groups (Table 2).

Effects of ATX on D-gal-induced histopathological changes in rat tissues

Liver Changes: The model group exhibited central vein dilation, disorganized and loose hepatic cell arrangement, extensive vacuolization indicative of steatosis, and significant dilation of hepatic sinusoids. Both VE and ATX treatments ameliorated these conditions, and higher doses of astaxanthin demonstrated more pronounced effects. In the H-ATX+ML385 group, in contrast to the H-ATX group, the liver cell cords were highly disorganized and loose, and abundant vacuoles indicative of steatosis were observed (Fig. 2a).

Brain Changes: In the hippocampal region, rats in the model and H-ATX+ML385 groups showed disorganized neuronal arrangement, diminished neuron count, and a significant presence of pyramidal cells in the CA1 and CA3 regions. These pyramidal cells appeared shrunken, with dark staining, enhanced basophilia, poorly defined cytoplasm-nuclear boundaries, and enlarged cell bodies with unclear nuclei in the CA3 region (Fig. 2b).

Protective effects of ATX against D-gal-induced oxidative stress in aged rats

Liver Changes: Compared with the control group, the model group showed less T-SOD, T-AOC, GSH-PX, and CAT enzymatic activity, along with greater levels of MDA and ROS (P < 0.05). The astaxanthin treatment group displayed elevated T-SOD and CAT enzymatic activity and diminished MDA content (P < 0.05). Compared with that in the model group, the T-SOD (VE and H-ATX), T-AOC (M-ATX), GSH-PX (H-ATX), and CAT (M-ATX and H-ATX) activity was greater, whereas MDA (H-ATX) and ROS (VE, L-ATX, M-ATX, and H-ATX) levels were lower (P < 0.05). Compared with those in the VE group, low-dose astaxanthin exhibited non-significantly greater ROS levels (P > 0.05). Compared with the H-ATX group, the H-ATX+ML385 group showed lower T-SOD, T-AOC, GSH-PX, and CAT activity, and higher MDA and ROS levels (P < 0.05) (Figs. 3a and 4a).

Brain Changes: Compared with those in the control group, the model group displayed lower T-SOD, T-AOC, GSH-PX, and CAT enzymatic activity, along with higher MDA and ROS levels (P < 0.05). The astaxanthin treatment group showed elevated CAT activity (P < 0.05). Compared with those in the model group, T-SOD (L-ATX, M-ATX, and H-ATX), T-AOC (L-ATX and M-ATX), GSH-PX (VE, L-ATX, M-ATX, and H-ATX), and CAT (H-ATX) enzymatic activity was significantly higher, whereas MDA (H-ATX) and ROS (VE, M-ATX, and H-ATX) levels were significantly lower (P < 0.05). Compared with those in the VE group, T-SOD (M-ATX) and GSH-PX (H-ATX) activity was higher, whereas MDA content (H-ATX) was lower (P < 0.05). Compared with the H-ATX group, the H-ATX+ML385 group exhibited lower T-SOD, T-AOC, GSH-PX, and CAT activity, but higher MDA and ROS levels (P < 0.05) (Figs. 3b and 4b).

Effects of astaxanthin on cell apoptosis in rats with D-galactose-induced aging

Liver Changes: The model group showed a greater apoptosis rate than the control group (P < 0.05). Apoptosis rates were lower in the VE, M-ATX, and H-ATX groups than the model group (P < 0.05). Compared with those in the VE group, apoptosis rates were further decreased in the M-ATX and H-ATX groups (P < 0.05). Finally, the H-ATX+ML385 group exhibited a greater apoptosis rate than the H-ATX group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5a and c).

Brain Changes: The model group showed a higher apoptosis rate than the control group (P < 0.05). The VE, L-ATX, M-ATX, and H-ATX groups had lower apoptosis rates than the model group (P < 0.05). No significant changes were observed between the astaxanthin intervention groups and the VE group (P > 0.05). Compared with the H-ATX group, the H-ATX+ML385 group showed a higher apoptosis rate (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5b and d).

Effects of ATX on AGEs in rats with D-gal-induced aging

Serum Results: AGE levels were lower in the model group than the control group (P < 0.05), and were lower in the VE, L-ATX, M-ATX, and H-ATX groups than the model group. Compared with those in the VE group, AGE levels further decreased in the M-ATX group. Finally, the AGE levels were higher in the H-ATX+ML385 group than the H-ATX group (Fig. 6c).

Liver Results: The model group showed a greater area positive for AGEs than the control group (P < 0.05), with no significant differences observed in the astaxanthin groups (P > 0.05). Both the astaxanthin and VE groups showed smaller AGE positive areas than the model group (P < 0.05), whereas no significant differences were observed between the astaxanthin and VE groups. Compared with the H-ATX group, the sH-ATX+ML385 group showed a greater area positive for AGEs (P < 0.05) (Fig. 7a).

Brain Results: In the brain, the model group showed a greater AGE positive area than the control group (P < 0.05). No significant differences were observed between the astaxanthin groups and the control group (P > 0.05). Compared with the model group, the low-dose astaxanthin group showed a smaller area positive for AGEs (P < 0.05) (Fig. 7b).

Discussion

Aging is a fundamental biological process that is characterized by a decline in physiological function, and leads to the onset and progression of various diseases. The treatment costs for these age-related diseases substantially affect society and families. Anti-aging strategies are aimed at mitigating, delaying, or reversing aging-associated conditions, to enhance quality of life and alleviate the burden of age-related diseases. Natural dietary antioxidant supplements have shown substantial pharmacological and therapeutic effects in various disease states21,22. Studies have demonstrated that astaxanthin promotes health and extends the lifespan in nematodes by enhancing their activity, decreasing age pigment accumulation, and increasing thermal resistance23. Because of its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antithrombotic, anticancer, and anti-aging properties, astaxanthin has attracted considerable attention in pharmacological research24,25. Our findings confirmed that astaxanthin treatment significantly ameliorated liver and brain damage, as well as cognitive impairment, in aging rats.

D-gal induces oxidative stress damage and apoptosis through the generation of free radicals in its metabolic processes, thereby promoting aging with characteristics similar to those observed in naturally aged animals26. In this study, D-gal-treated rats in the model group exhibited yellow, dry fur, significant hair loss, dry and rough skin with poor elasticity, sluggish movement, and inactivity. The Morris Water Maze results indicated longer escape latency and fewer platform crossings, thus reflecting a marked decline in learning and memory abilities, and supporting the successful establishment of the aging model, in agreement with a review by Zhao27.

Aging is characterized by the gradual loss of tissue and organ function, and oxidative stress plays a major role in the pathogenesis of multiple clinical diseases and aging. Antioxidant therapy positively influences the natural course of some diseases28. In D-gal-induced aging, decreased levels of endogenous antioxidants contribute to organ dysfunction, and AGEs generated through D-gal oxidation are important markers of aging that accumulate across various tissues in humans and animals, and increase ROS production29. In D-gal-induced aging, decreased activity of key endogenous antioxidant enzymes, such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and CAT, leads to organ dysfunction. SOD acts as the first line of defense against ROS, and, together with GSH-Px, SOD, and CAT, forms the body’s antioxidant enzyme system. T-AOC reflects the body’s overall antioxidant level, whereas MDA is a marker of lipid peroxidation caused by ROS in cell membranes30. In this study, model group rats showed diminished antioxidant enzymatic activity and elevated levels of ROS, AGEs, and MDA. These findings indicated that enhanced oxidative stress and diminished antioxidant capacity lead to oxidative damage in both the liver and brain. Astaxanthin effectively scavenges free radicals and reduces lipid peroxidation, and shows superior inhibition of lipid peroxidation to VE31. Astaxanthin treatment significantly increased antioxidant enzyme levels, while decreasing ROS, AGE, and MDA levels. Additionally, astaxanthin at 10 mg/kg had a protective effect comparable to that of 60 mg/kg VE, but achieved a more pronounced improvement in T-SOD enzymatic activity in the liver. Thus, astaxanthin exerts antioxidant effects that might delay D-gal-induced aging progression.

IL-2, known as a T-cell growth factor, and IL-6 actively participate in inflammatory and immune regulation mechanisms32,33. Our study indicated diminished IL-2 and elevated IL-6 levels in model rats, along with spleen enlargement and thymus atrophy, thereby indicating impaired immune function. The pathological observations of inflammatory cell infiltration further supported the presence of inflammation in D-gal-induced aging. The finding that astaxanthin treatment reversed changes in these markers suggested that astaxanthin modulates oxidative stress and inflammatory responses. Inflammation and oxidative stress play key roles in the pathogenesis of various diseases, although the complex interactions and causal relationships between them require further study.

Learning and memory functions are particularly susceptible to impairment during aging. The central cholinergic nervous system is essential for learning and memory, and ChAT expression reflects acetylcholine synthesis and maintains acetylcholine system function, thereby enhancing learning and memory capabilities34. In the Morris Water Maze test to measure spatial learning and memory in rats, we observed significant learning and memory impairment and diminished ChAT levels in the aging group. However, the astaxanthin groups showed marked improvements in learning and memory, in agreement with previous studies indicating that astaxanthin ameliorates cognitive impairment in Alzheimer’s disease models. Our findings suggest that astaxanthin alleviates cognitive deficits in D-gal-induced aging rats.

Nrf2 was first identified as a key regulator of oxidant resistance over a decade ago. Since then, it has been associated with various toxicities and chronic diseases linked to oxidative damage. In the course of antioxidation, Nrf2 modulates multiple programmed processes, including autophagy, inflammasome signaling, apoptosis, mitochondrial biogenesis, and stem cell regulation. Numerous studies have emphasized that exposure to toxic metals or metalloids increases Nrf2 activation through pathways dependent on Keap1, protecting against their toxicity35. An increasing number of researches indicated that dietary nutraceuticals individually or in combination have a variety of pleiotropic benefits, indcluding reducing oxidative stres, protection against metal-induced toxicity, cancer prevention and reduction of inflammatory damage. Now, our study suggests that ATX may be one of these food components to exert the fundation of the Nrf2 activators36.ML385 is a specific Nrf2 inhibitor which can suppress the activation of Nrf2 by binding to Nrf2 and preventing Nrf2 from translocating into the nucleus, and restrain the function of the Nrf2/Bach1 pathway. So ML385 is widely used in mechanistic researchs related Nrf2 pathways37. The results of our study showed that after using ML385, even rats were administered with a high dose of ATX, liver and brain damage still did not get improved. The possible mechanism is that ML385 might significantly reduce the nuclear ratio of Nrf2 and Bach1 through inhibiting the nuclear expression of Nrf2 in rat tissues. When Bach1 becomes relatively abundant, Nrf2 will break away from the ARE site in the promoter region of the Ho-1 gene, which should suppresse the expression of downstream antioxidant enzymes such as Ho-1, Sod1, Sod2. Therefore, oxidative stress and ROS increases and organismal aging happens. Nrf2 is a key nuclear transcription factor that regulates antioxidant stress responses, and serves as a sensor for exogenous toxic substances and oxidative stress38. After nuclear translocation, Nrf2 binds AREs and upregulates antioxidant enzymes and detoxifying enzymes, such as HO-1 and SOD, which scavenge free radicals39. Bach1 acts as an Nrf2 repressor that competes with Nrf2 for ARE binding and downregulates antioxidant enzyme gene expression through the Bach1-ARE pathway40. In our study, rats with D-gal-induced aging exhibited diminished Nrf2 nuclear translocation and elevated Bach1 nuclear translocation, thus enhancing cellular susceptibility to oxidative stress. These findings were confirmed by diminished HO-1 and SOD1 expression. Antioxidants such as VE and astaxanthin both promoted Nrf2 nuclear translocation, thereby activating Nrf2; however, when Nrf2 was inhibited with ML385, astaxanthin was unable to activate Nrf2 nuclear translocation and exert its antioxidant effect. These findings indicated that the antioxidant effects of astaxanthin are mediated through the Nrf2/Bach1 axis. Previous research has suggested that in the presence of Bach1, Nrf2 cannot bind the ARE site in the HO-1 gene promoter region, thus indicating Bach1’s crucial role in repressing HO-1 expression41. In our study, although HO-1 protein levels in the aging rats’ brains were diminished, significant changes in Bach1 nuclear translocation and HO-1 expression were observed. Therefore, these findings suggested that HO-1 is more prominently regulated by Bach1. BACH1 and FGF21 have been found to inhibit obesity and extend the lifespan in progeroid mice under high-fat diet conditions, although their roles under normal dietary conditions and in wild-type mice without progeroid disorders remain unclear. Further research is needed to elucidate Bach1’s role in aging. The combined modulation of the transcription factors NRF2 and Bach1 might be considered in the development of anti-aging supplements and interventions for age-related diseases.This study shows that ATX can exert an antiaging effect. But the result were derived from animal experiments. There are certain limitations in the conversion from animal experiment results to human practice, and subsequent human studies must be required to explicit the anti-aging effect of ATX. In addition, the detailed change or role of Bach1 in this process needs further investigation and discussion.

Conclusion

This study revealed the protective effects and mechanisms of astaxanthin in liver and brain aging. Astaxanthin effectively mitigates oxidative stress and decreases liver and brain tissue damage in the aging process. Astaxanthin exerts its protective effects against aging-related liver and brain damage through Nrf2/Bach1-mediated signaling pathways (Fig. 13).
