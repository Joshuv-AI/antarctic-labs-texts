Remote, unsupervised assessment of cognitive impairment and decline in Alzheimer's disease using the Cogstate Brief Battery in ADNI‐3
Authors: Paul Maruff, Bruce Albala, Danielle Harvey, Naomi Saito, Shaveta Kanoria, Paul Aisen, Ronald Petersen, Michael W. Weiner, Laurel Beckett, for the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONRemote neuropsychological testing can monitor brain health in individuals with or at risk of Alzheimer's disease (AD). We evaluated acceptability and validity of the Cogstate Brief Battery (CBB) for remote administration.METHODSNon‐demented adults from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) completed the CBB in‐clinic under supervision, then remotely every six months, with annual in‐clinic reassessments. Groups included cognitively unimpaired (CU) adults with normal amyloid (CU Aβ−) or elevated amyloid (CU Aβ+), and individuals with mild cognitive impairment with or without amyloid (MCI Aβ+, MCI Aβ−). Performance was compared across contexts and longitudinal changes were examined.RESULTSAt baseline, MCI Aβ+ participants performed worse than CU Aβ−. CBB performance was similar across contexts, with consistent group differences. Over time, MCI Aβ+ participants showed greater decline on remote CBB measures than CU Aβ− adults.DISCUSSIONRemote unsupervised CBB assessments are valid, reliable, and sensitive to AD‐related cognitive impairment.

BACKGROUND

Assessing cognition in individuals with or at risk of Alzheimer's disease (AD), is crucial for clinical and differential diagnosis, monitoring disease progression and evaluating the effects of pharmaceutical or lifestyle interventions. 1 , 2 Advances in understanding the biological changes associated with AD, along with improvements in biomarkers of these changes, have improved clinical pathological models of the disease. 3 , 4 This has increased the accuracy of neuropsychological assessments, reduced the dependency on cognitive testing for AD diagnosis and laid the groundwork for identifying the disease prior to the onset of dementia. 5 Biomarkers combined with cognitive tests provide a strong foundation for understanding early cognitive impairment in preclinical and prodromal AD. 6

Clinical trials involving AD‐related drugs and research studies on the natural history of AD continue to depend on in‐clinic neuropsychological assessments conducted by trained experts. 7 , 8 , 9 Meanwhile, more households now have reliable internet and are familiar with web applications, including many older adults. This shift now enables the design of studies that use web‐based portals to collect relevant health information, mainly through responses to questionnaires from older adults. 10 , 11 This information has been used alone or in combination with data collected in clinics to increase understanding of AD. Web‐based data collection in individuals with, or at risk of, AD dementia has also been used as a basis for identifying individuals with specific characteristics, such as a subjective opinion of memory decline or a willingness to undergo lumbar puncture, for clinical studies that involve more invasive or time‐consuming study procedures. 10 , 12

Given older adults’ growing acceptance of unsupervised participation in AD research, the ability to collect cognitive data in these contexts could provide valuable information about AD risk. However, unlike questionnaires, data on cognition require performance‐based tests, which involve active engagement and adherence to specific rules and requirements. In supervised settings, raters ensure participants hear and understand the instructions, control test presentation and record responses. In unsupervised contexts, the cognitive test must fulfill these requirements and promote engagement. The Cogstate Brief Battery (CBB) includes four neuropsychological tests assessing psychomotor function, attention, working memory and learning. 13 In supervised clinical contexts, the CBB test is sensitive to cognitive impairment and decline associated with AD dementia, prodromal AD and preclinical AD. Recently, it has been optimized for unsupervised use, and early studies have shown high acceptability, usability and sensitivity to AD‐related cognitive impairment in remote unsupervised contexts. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 A key challenge is how the sensitivity of the unsupervised CBB to AD‐related cognitive impairment and biological changes compares with that observed in supervised settings. Understanding of the characteristics of cognitive data collected from the CBB in an unsupervised context will also provide a basis for understanding how to deploy any remote cognitive assessments in future prospective studies, such as the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI). 18

The current study was designed as an ad hoc study to be conducted within the ADNI protocol to address three aims. First, to assess the acceptability, validity and sensitivity of the CBB under supervised in‐clinic settings in cognitively unimpaired (CU) and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) groups. Second, to evaluate the acceptability and validity of the CBB in unsupervised remote contexts by comparing performance with supervised in‐clinic administration. Third, to examine the sensitivity of the remote CBB to early AD‐related cognitive decline in individuals with preclinical and prodromal AD.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: Previous research has mainly focused on supervised, in‐clinic administration of cognitive testing, with few studies examining unsupervised, remote use in detecting cognitive decline in Alzheimer's disease (AD). Interpretation: Our findings show that the Cogstate Brief Battery (CBB), when administered remotely and unsupervised, can reliably measure cognition. Performance was comparable to in‐clinic assessments, sensitive to mild cognitive impairment due to AD, and capable of detecting longitudinal decline. Future directions: Future research should evaluate the use of remote CBB testing for clinical trial screening, monitoring disease progression, and integration into digital health platforms.

METHODS

ADNI

Data used in the preparation of this article were obtained from ADNI, 18 a public‐private partnership, launched in 2004 and led by Principal Investigator Michael W. Weiner, MD, at the Veterans Administration Medical Center and the University of California, San Francisco. ADNI is the outcome of collaborative efforts of many coinvestigators from various academic institutions and private corporations, with participants recruited from over 50 sites across the United States and Canada. Current information is available at https://adni.loni.usc.edu/.

The primary goal of ADNI is to identify and validate clinical, imaging, and biological markers for detecting AD in its earliest stages, monitoring disease progression,' and supporting the development of new treatments.

Participants

Samples for this study were drawn from participants enrolled in ADNI‐3, which was conducted from 2016 to 2022 (https://adni.loni.usc.edu/). Recruitment into ADNI, as well as the assessments/tests and procedures used for classifying AD and clinical disease severity, have been described earlier. 19 All participants signed informed consent. Only individuals with suitable devices for the task, and who were either CU or MCI, were enrolled. Those diagnosed with dementia were excluded as the ADNI study committee determined there was insufficient pilot data to indicate adults with dementia would be able to complete the CBB tasks at home. Therefore, investigating remote assessment in adults with dementia would increase participant burden and potentially increase the risk of these adults withdrawing from the ADNI study. Second, because this study sought to assess the use of the CBB specifically and not of computer technology generally, the study was limited to adults with access to suitable devices.

Clinical and neuropsychological assessments conducted in‐clinic using standard methods

ADNI clinical and neuropsychological assessments used for comparison with the CBB included the Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale–Cognitive 13‐item scale (ADAS‐Cog 13), the Clinical Dementia Rating Scale Sum of Boxes score (CDR‐SOB), Logical Memory Delayed Recall Total Score (LDELTOTAL), the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE), and the Trail Making Test Part B (TMT B), which were assessed annually. The clinical disease stage of each participant, classified as CU, MCI, or dementia, was determined following the ADNI protocol. Amyloid status was classified as positive (Aβ+) or negative (Aβ−) based on positron emission tomography (PET) neuroimaging using the florbetapir (standardized uptake value ratio [SUVR] > 1.11) or florbetaben (SUVR > 1.08) radiotracer 20 or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis based on the Roche Elecsys platform (Aβ42 ≤ 980 pg/ml), using the available amyloid‐PET or CSF from the initial ADNI‐3 visit; at least one result had to be positive to be Aβ+.

CBB for in‐clinic and remote cognitive assessment

In this study, the CBB was administered in both remote unsupervised settings and in‐clinic, supervised contexts. It was presented to all participants by trained and certified raters using a laptop or tablet computer, following standardized procedures. 14 The CBB features a game‐like interface with playing card stimuli, where participants respond with “Yes” or “No”. It includes four distinct tests: Detection (DET), Identification (IDN), One Card Learning (OCL), and One‐Back (ONB). 21

The DET test measures psychomotor function using a simple reaction time paradigm. Participants must focus on the center of the screen and follow the rule, “Has the card turned face up? Press ‘yes’ as soon as it does”. The task continued until either 25 correct responses were recorded or the maximum allowed time of 2 min had elapsed.

The IDN test assesses visual attention using a choice reaction time paradigm. Participants must focus on the card in the center of the screen and follow the rule “Is the face‐up card red? As the card turns, press the ‘yes’ or ‘no’ buttons”. The task continued until 35 correct responses were recorded or the maximum allowed time of 2 minutes had elapsed.

The OCL test assesses visual learning using a pattern separation paradigm. Participants must focus on the card in the center of the screen and follow the rule, “Have you seen this card before in this task? As the card turns, press ‘yes’ or ‘no’ ”. Participants were required to memorize four target cards presented in a pseudo‐random order, along with six distracter (i.e., non‐repeating) cards in groups of 10 trials, totaling 80 trials or until the 3‐minute time limit was reached.

The ONB test assesses working memory and is based on the n‐back (1‐back condition) paradigm. Participants must focus on the card in the center of the screen and follow the rule “Is this card the same as that on the immediately previous trial? As the card turns over, press ‘yes’ or ‘no’ ”. Forty‐two cards were presented, with correct responses occurring on 50% of trials. This task continued until the 42 trials were completed or the maximum allowed time (3 minutes) had elapsed.

During in‐clinic annual assessments, participants were seated quietly under the supervision of a trained and credentialed rater. Each test began with written instructions and testing rules displayed on a screen and read aloud to ensure understanding. An interactive demonstration of the CBB was provided, followed by practice trials to confirm participants’ grasp of the testing procedures and understanding of the rules.

The CBB tests were designed as card games, where participants responded with a “Yes” or “No” button, using their right hand for “Yes” responses. Participants were instructed to respond as quickly and as accurately as possible. Responses faster than 100 milliseconds were defined as anticipatory, while responses slower than 3000 milliseconds were considered abnormally slow and excluded from further analysis.

Data from each test included the proportion of correct responses and the average response speed, which were computed automatically. To improve the usability of performance data for parametric statistical analyses, distributions of proportion correct scores were transformed using an arcsine square root transformation (arcsine), and distributions of mean reaction times were transformed with a base 10 logarithmic (log10msec) transformation. In total, both speed (spd) and accuracy (acc) measures from each test were used to define performance, resulting in eight CBB outcome measures.

The CBB tests are described below in their order of presentation. For the unsupervised assessment, the same method was used, except that participants started the test themselves and then read and followed the test instructions displayed on the screen at the beginning of each test and completed the practice trials. Once this was complete, the CBB test commenced. Remote assessment could be done as frequently as quarterly. At the end of each test in the CBB, whether administered in the clinic or remotely, performance data for that test were uploaded automatically to the Cogstate database. All CBB data were captured by Cogstate servers, which were subsequently provided to ADNI. All data for this publication are available at https://adni.loni.usc.edu/.

Statistical methods

Data analysis was limited to participants who had their first CBB (in‐clinic) within 90 days of their ADNI‐3 baseline evaluation and for whom amyloid β (Aβ) status was known. Individual CBB scores were included only if they met the criteria for test completion. If a participant attempted a test during any study visit, the final test in that session was used for analysis. For the baseline assessment, participant demographics, apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 allele carrier status, and CBB results were summarized using percentages for categorical variables and means with standard deviations for variables with continuous measurement scales.

Data analysis

First, to characterize the ADNI subsample used in this study and evaluate the sensitivity of the CBB tests to participants’ clinical status, demographic, clinical, neuropsychological, and CBB data were compared between clinically defined CU and MCI groups at baseline using chi‐square tests for categorical variables and Student's t‐test for quantitative variables. These analyses were then repeated with the groups further classified by their amyloid status, using Student's t‐tests to compare Aβ− and Aβ+ subgroups within each clinical disease stage classification. Finally, data were combined across clinical disease and amyloid groups, and correlations between the CBB test, composite measures and the ADNI clinical and neuropsychological tests were computed using Spearman's r.

Second, to understand the nature and magnitude of any differences in performance between in‐clinic and remote administration of the CBB, performance measures from each test and the composite scores were compared within the same participants. This comparison included participants' in‐clinic baseline and first remote reassessment (within 90 days of the baseline) and was analyzed using mixed‐effects repeated measures models. These data were also used to estimate the test‐retest reliability of each CBB test and composite measure, as well as ADNI clinical and neuropsychological test scores, using Spearman's r.

Third, the extent of changes in performance on the CBB and ADNI clinical neuropsychological tests during in‐clinic visits was investigated for all participants who completed a baseline CBB, using linear mixed models (LMM). Only baseline and follow‐up visits were included in these analyses. From these models, estimates of annualized change were calculated for each combination of Aβ status and clinical disease severity group. The LMM models also adjusted for baseline age, sex, and APOE and included random effects for individuals.

Fourth, to understand the extent to which repeated remote assessment was sensitive to cognitive decline associated with AD, performance on the remote CBB over time was examined. We carried out analyses using CBB data from the in‐clinic baseline and then from remote follow‐up CBB assessments. Thus, in these analyses, participants who did not undergo remote testing at follow‐up were excluded. Linear mixed models analysis estimated an annualized change of CBB test scores across four groups (CU, MCI, Aβ+, and Aβ−). The models also adjusted for baseline age, sex, and APOE and included random effects for person.

All statistical analyses were carried out using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) software. For all comparisons, the level of probability required to reject the null hypothesis was p < 0.01. For the same comparisons, measures of effect size were computed using Cohen's method. 22 To facilitate interpretation, the effect size signs for Cogstate and neuropsychological tests based on performance speed were reversed, ensuring that across all CBB outcomes, negative effect sizes indicated performance deficits or declines over time, while positive values indicated superior performance over time. The effect sizes were interpreted within the framework provided by Cohen, 22 with effect sizes of ≥ 0.2 and < 0.5 classified as small, ≥ 0.5 to < 0.8 as medium and ≥ 0.8 as large. To reduce family‐wise Type I error, both statistical significance and effect sizes were considered: (a) a threshold of p < 0.01 was applied to account for multiple comparisons, given that remote assessment in AD is a new and emerging area; (b) moderate correlations were expected among clinical, neuropsychological, and cognitive outcomes; and (c) only comparisons with non‐trivial effect sizes (i.e., d ≥ 0.2) were interpreted irrespective of statistical significance.

RESULTS

Not all ADNI‐3 participants invited to join this sub‐study chose to participate. Among those enrolled, none of the participating sites reported reluctance to complete the CBB during either scheduled in‐clinic or remote visits and no adverse events were reported.

Description of participants

Details of the adults included in the in‐clinic and remote follow‐up, are described in Figure 1. A total of 594 adults classified as CU and 366 classified as MCI were included. Of these, 180 were excluded due to missing CBB data. An additional 42 participants did not provide remote CBB data within 90 days of their baseline clinical assessment, and 55 (of the remaining 738 participants) were excluded because their baseline CBB, which was scheduled to be an in‐clinic assessment, was instead completed remotely or because the clinical location could not be validated. Data from another 39 adults were excluded due to missing Aβ PET results. The final baseline included 644 participants, of whom 360 also completed a valid remote assessment within 90 days. For longitudinal analyses, the total sample consisted of 490 individuals who completed at least one follow‐up assessment and had known Aβ PET status.

Baseline comparisons

Baseline comparisons between CU and MCI participants showed significantly worse performance in the MCI group across all individual CBB tests and composite scores (i.e., all negative effect sizes, Table 1). Analysis of effect sizes for these differences indicated that MCI‐related cognitive impairment was greatest for OCL accuracy and in composite scores that included the OCL (OCL/ONB acc/spd and OCL/ONB acc). In the MCI group, the performance on CBB tests, which measured performance speed as the outcome were consistently lower (i.e., d < 0.51) than that observed for ONB and OCL tests.

Group performance on the CBB individual tests, composite scores, and ADNI clinical and neuropsychological test scores, classified by amyloid status within each clinical disease severity group, is shown in Table 2. For the CU sample, only performance on the TMT B score was significantly impaired in the Aβ+ group, though with a small magnitude difference. For all other tests, differences between the Aβ‐ and Aβ+ groups were trivial. In the MCI group, the Aβ+ subgroup showed significant impairment on LDELTOTAL and the ADAS‐Cog 13, with the magnitude of impairment for both tests being small (i.e., |d|∼0.4). However, in the MCI group, there were no significant differences between Aβ subgroups for any of the CBB tests or composite scores, with the largest effect sizes observed for the ONB test and the ONB/OCL composite (i.e., |d|∼0.18). The sensitivity of the ADNI clinical and neuropsychological tests and the CBB to prodromal AD (i.e., MCI Aβ+) was also examined by comparing the performance of the CU Aβ– group to that in the MCI Aβ+ group. In the MCI Aβ+ group, performance was significantly impaired on the ADNI clinical and neuropsychological tests (|d| > 1.0), CBB ONB and OCL accuracy tests (|d| > 0.6), and CBB DET, IDN, and ONB speed tests (|d| ∼ 0.5).

Correlations between CBB and other tests

Intercorrelations between the CBB test and composite outcomes and the ADNI clinical and neuropsychological tests are summarized in Table 3. The table shows moderate associations between the ADAS‐Cog 13 and the OCL accuracy score, as well as composite scores that included the OCL (i.e., |r| ∼ 0.4). A qualitatively similar pattern of association with the OCL accuracy and OCL inclusive composite scores was observed for the CDR‐SOB and the LDELTOTAL, although the magnitudes of association were slightly less than those observed with the ADAS‐Cog13 (|r| = 0.31–0.37 for CDR‐SOB and |r| ∼ 0.3 for LDELTOTAL). The MMSE total score showed weaker associations overall, with CBB outcomes showing somewhat stronger correlations (i.e., |r| ∼ 0.20) with OCL/ONB acc/spd and OCL acc (|r| ∼0.30). Finally, the TMT B score showed a moderate correlation with all CBB outcomes (i.e., |r| = 0.33–0.46), the strongest association observed for the OCL/ONB accuracy/speed composite.

Comparison of in‐clinic and remote CBB

Table 4 summarizes the comparison of performance on the CBB within the same individuals, first assessed under supervision in‐clinic and then while completing a second assessment in a remote, unsupervised context, which occurred within 90 days of the baseline. Data are shown separately for CU and MCI groups. The analysis indicated that for the ONB speed outcomes, performance improved when they were re‐tested in the remote context, and this improvement occurred in both the CU and MCI groups, although the CU group did not quite meet our threshold for significance (p = 0.01). In the MCI group, performance also improved on the IDN speed outcome but still did not quite meet our threshold for significance. However, for both tests and across both groups, the observed improvements were trivial in magnitude. No statistically significant differences were found for the remaining CBB tests or composite scores between in‐clinic and remote assessments. Table 5 summarizes the test‐retest reliability using Spearman's correlations for the CBB tests. In the CU group, reliability estimates ranged from 0.71 (DET) to 0.27 (ONB accuracy). In the MCI group, the highest reliability was observed for ONB speed. Overall, performance speed measures showed greater test‐retest reliability than accuracy‐based scores; however, accuracy measures demonstrated greater sensitivity to cognitive impairment associated with AD.

DISCUSSION

This ad‐hoc study evaluated remote, unsupervised cognitive assessments in CU older adults, preclinical AD, MCI due to AD, and MCI unrelated to AD, recruited from ADNI. First, the performance on each CBB test, administered remotely and unsupervised, was equivalent to that obtained in‐clinic under direct supervision. Second, the construct and criterion validity of the CBB tests were consistent between in‐clinic and remote unsupervised contexts. Third, performance on the CBB working memory and learning tests was sensitive to cognitive decline in both preclinical and prodromal AD. These findings demonstrate that cognitive impairments seen in non‐demented adults, under supervised clinical and research settings, 23 , 24 , 25 are also detectable remotely and unsupervised with CBB.

In‐clinic sensitivity of CBB in MCI

At baseline in‐clinic visit, performance on the OCL and related composite scores distinguished clinically defined MCI (Table 1), consistent with previous supervised CBB studies, although impairment (e.g., d ∼ −0.75) was slightly smaller than observed previously (e.g., d ∼ −1). 24 , 25 This reduced impairment likely arises from the inclusion of adults with MCI unrelated to AD. 26 , 27 , 28 Comparison of CU Aβ− adults with MCI Aβ+ adults showed greater impairment in MCI on OCL and related composite scores (Table 2), consistent with findings from the AIBL and MAYO cohorts. 13 , 29 However, impairment was smaller than on conventional ADNI neuropsychological tests (e.g., LDELTOTAL, TMT B, and MMSE), where MCI Aβ+ performance was > 1 standard deviation (SD) below controls.

The MCI Aβ+ group performed worse than the MCI Aβ− group on OCL, ONB, and the ADNI neuropsychological tests, with small effect sizes (0.2–0.4), being significant for differences over 0.4 (Table 2). Although this systematic, small performance impairment has been observed in studies seeking neuropsychological methods for detecting AD in MCI adults, these differences are too small for clinical utility. Comparison of CU Aβ− and CU Aβ+ groups revealed the in‐clinic sensitivity of the CBB and conventional neuropsychological tests to preclinical AD (Table 2). Consistent with meta‐analysis results, adults with preclinical AD (e.g., CU Aβ+) showed subtle cognitive impairments, significant only for the TMT B. 30

A recent study compared CBB performance between Aβ− and Aβ+ CU adults, finding small but consistent differences on the OCL, ONB, and related composite scores. The large sample (∼5000) rendered these small impairments statistically significant. 31 Neuropsychological tests from the Alzheimer's Disease Cooperative Study–Preclinical Alzheimer Cognitive Composite (ADCS‐PACC) were impaired similarly in this sample (d’s 0.2–0.5).

While larger sample sizes make small cognitive impairments in preclinical AD statistically significant, their clinical importance remains low. 32 Their importance is that they indicate that neuronal death has commenced despite these adults being asymptomatic. Baseline CBB and ADNI neuropsychological test performances correlated, especially for OCL and ONB. Overall, these results indicate the initial CBB in‐clinic assessment was acceptable, sensitive to disease status, and aligned with conventional neuropsychological tests for MCI and AD.

Remote unsupervised performance of CBB

The second aim of the study was to determine the acceptability and sensitivity of CBB measures to AD‐related cognitive dysfunction remotely and unsupervised. Most neuropsychological assessments require trained raters for proper administration and scoring, ensuring rules are understood, stimuli are presented correctly, and responses recorded accurately. For unsupervised computer‐based assessments, these functions must be integrated into the software and hardware that controls test administration and scoring. Previous studies of unsupervised use of the CBB in young and middle‐aged adults show the simple rules and requirements of each test facilitate high acceptability and sensitivity to cognitive impairment. 14 However, the acceptability of remote administration of cognitive assessments is challenged further when assessment must occur in individuals with substantial cognitive impairment, such as those diagnosed with MCI.

This study provided a framework for determining the extent to which an unsupervised CBB could be used in older adults with, or at risk of, AD‐related cognitive impairment. To do this, performance on the CBB was measured six months after the baseline in‐clinic administration. Comparison of in‐clinic and remote assessments showed that most CBB performance outcomes remained consistent in the CU and MCI groups (Table 4). Thus, once older adults were familiar with the CBB, they could understand and comply with its rules and requirements when it was administered unsupervised, even when cognitive impairment was sufficient to warrant classification of MCI. Hence, the nature and magnitude of cognitive impairment in the MCI group detected from a baseline supervised assessment was preserved when the CBB was re‐administered unsupervised six months later. The only change observed across assessment contexts was a statistically significant but trivial magnitude improvement in working memory speed. Performance on all other CBB tests and composite scores was not different over the 6‐month interval (Table 4) and the reliability of each CBB performance measure in the CU and MCI groups was acceptable (Table 5).

Sensitivity of the remote CBB to AD‐related cognitive decline

A third study aim was to determine the sensitivity of the remotely and unsupervised administred CBB to cognitive decline in early AD. Changes in performance over time on each CBB test and composite score were compared between the CU adults with normal levels of Aβ, and adults with prodromal AD (e.g., MCI Aβ+). When measured in‐clinic, the prodromal AD group showed greater decline over a year on the learning working memory composite scores (ONB/OCL acc & ONB/OCL) and their components (Table 6). The in‐clinic assessments identified no decline in composite or individual measures of attention. Performance on the ADAS‐Cog13, MMSE, CDR‐SOB, and TMT B tests was also sensitive to cognitive decline in prodromal AD. These results confirm the sensitivity to cognitive decline characteristic of early symptomatic AD, in which memory and working memory are most affected, while attentional functions remain stable. Although speed‐based measures demonstrated higher test–retest reliability, accuracy‐based measures were more sensitive to cognitive impairment associated with AD, consistent with earlier involvement of learning and working memory processes relative to attentional processes. No CBB or conventional neuropsychological test detected decline over the same interval in the preclinical AD group, at the selected statistical criterion (Table 6). Analysis of CBB data from unsupervised assessments, which were conducted more often (i.e., each quarter) at the same time‐period, extended the findings from the in‐clinic assessments. The results from the remote assessments replicated those from in‐clinic assessments (Table 6), although the remote assessment also identified a decline in attention in the MCI Aβ+ group, evident from group differences on the CBB attentional composite scores (DET/IDN spd) and its component tests. Comparison of unsupervised CBB data between the CU Aβ− and CU Aβ+ groups identified no differences, though ONB performance speed and OCL performance accuracy showed a non‐significant decline. These findings indicate that, for prodromal AD, the greater number of unsupervised assessments allowed detection of the expected decline in working memory and learning, but also extended this to the detection of a decline in attention. In the preclinical AD group, the trend toward significant decline on the unsupervised learning and working memory tests suggests that longer follow‐up may be necessary to detect this reliably. Together, these results confirm that the more frequent assessments afforded by unsupervised remote cognitive assessment can provide insight into the nature and magnitude of cognitive decline in early AD.

Growing evidence from longitudinal studies indicates that a characteristic of early cognitive decline in AD is the failure to benefit from repeated exposure to the same cognitive assessments. 33 This characteristic was observed in the current study. For example, the data in Figure S1 show that while scores on OCL/ONB acc composite improved over the study in the CU Aβ− group, this improvement was reduced in preclinical AD and diminished completely in prodromal AD. The reason for this reduced benefit from experience in early AD is still being sought, but one parsimonious hypothesis is that in early AD, deficits in learning occur prior to deficits in memory. 33

Considered together, these results indicate that performance on the CBB, given in unsupervised and remote contexts, is sensitive to amyloid‐related cognitive decline in both preclinical and prodromal AD. The acceptability and reliability of remote assessment allow cognition to be assessed longitudinally, even at short retest intervals, without requiring participants attend a research unit. Although data supporting the validity of remote clinical assessments are still developing, the current results show that the remote administration of the CBB can be used to characterize cognitive decline in very early AD. Importantly, the generalizability of the current findings may be limited by the restriction of the study sample to adults with access to appropriate devices and who did not have dementia. It is possible that unsupervised CBB performance would have lower acceptability and greater variability in CU or MCI groups who were unfamiliar with use of internet connected computers, or individuals with dementia. Extension of the current results to these groups can now occur in studies with appropriate designs.

CONCLUSION

The CBB can be applied effectively to measure cognition in remote and unsupervised contexts in older adults who are CU or who have MCI. The results provide solid evidence demonstrating the acceptability, validity and sensitivity of the remote CBB to AD‐related cognitive impairment and decline.
