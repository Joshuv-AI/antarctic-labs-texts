Co-treating opioid and alcohol use disorders in the United States, 1960–2026: A historical policy review in the glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) era
Authors: Fares Qeadan, William A Barbeau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis narrative review reconstructs the U.S. history of treating co-occurring opioid use disorder (OUD) and alcohol use disorder (AUD) from the rise of methadone maintenance in the 1960s through contemporary interest in immunometabolic mechanisms and repurposed therapies. Drawing on peer-reviewed literature and selected grey sources, we trace four eras including early single-substance paradigms and maintenance; federally regulated opioid treatment programs and the broader medicalization of addiction care; recognition of co-occurring disorders, parity and Affordable Care Act reforms, and uneven diffusion of medications for OUD and AUD; and a renewed metabolic framing, including preclinical, clinical, and real-world evaluation of glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP)/glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. Across eras, regulation, reimbursement, and professional authority shaped access as much as evidence, producing persistent geographic and socioeconomic inequities. Emerging GLP-1-based therapies may offer a distinctive cross-disorder adjunct, but their value for OUD/AUD remains unproven. Population benefit will require rigorous trials and delivery reforms that expand equitable access to integrated care.Keywords: Opioid use disorder, Alcohol use disorder, Methadone, Buprenorphine, Naltrexone, Integrated treatment, Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists

Introduction

Treatment of opioid use disorder (OUD) and alcohol use disorder (AUD) changed substantially from 1960 through 2026. Care was initially siloed by substance and organized largely around abstinence. Many people, however, have co-occurring substance use disorders (SUDs) and mental health conditions. In our weighted analysis of National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH) public-use files, approximately 0.4% of U.S. residents aged 12 years or older had past-year co-occurring AUD and OUD in 2021–2023. In 2022, about 8.0 million people aged 12 years or older (2.8%) had both AUD and any drug use disorder (DUD), illustrating the broader prevalence of co-occurring alcohol and other drug use disorders (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2023, pp. 33–34). Co-occurring OUD/AUD is also associated with greater clinical severity and psychiatric comorbidity (Arias and Kranzler, 2008).

The institutional separation of OUD and AUD treatment helps explain persistent implementation challenges. Medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD) and medications for alcohol use disorder (MAUD) diffused through different regulations, care settings, and prescribing cultures. Patients still encounter parallel programs, duplicative requirements, and unequal access. This history also cautions that new pharmacology can reproduce earlier diffusion failures unless financing and delivery systems are aligned. That concern is timely for glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP)/glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. In a multi-health-system electronic health record (EHR) cohort of 107,217 patients with OUD and AUD, prescriptions for these agents were associated with lower overdose and SUD-related hospitalization risk, although the observational findings require prospective confirmation (Qeadan et al., 2026).

This review traces treatment practice, the emergence of co-occurring-disorder frameworks, and major policy changes from 1960 through 2026. It also examines disparities, medicalization, and cultural and institutional change using scholarly literature and selected policy, regulatory, program-history, pharmaceutical, and oral-history sources. Methods / source selection

We conducted a narrative historical review of U.S. treatment for co-occurring OUD and AUD from 1960 through 2026. Sources were selected to reconstruct changes in federal policy and regulation, clinical practice, advocacy and recovery culture, and clinical frameworks. We searched peer-reviewed literature on OUD, AUD, MOUD, MAUD, integrated treatment, co-occurring SUDs, and GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists, supplemented by federal documents, published program histories, pharmaceutical documentation, pivotal trials, and published oral histories and interviews. We prioritized sources addressing access, reimbursement, regulation, implementation, and disparities. Because the purpose was historical synthesis and policy interpretation rather than pooled effect estimation, we did not conduct a systematic review or meta-analysis. Contextual prevalence estimates used weighted 2021–2023 NSDUH public-use files for U.S. residents aged 12 years or older (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2021–2023).

Early treatment paradigms (1960s-1970s)

In the early 1960s, treatment options for substance use were limited and mostly segregated by substance. AUD was typically addressed via 12-step groups (Alcoholics Anonymous, founded in 1935) and short-term hospitalization or “Minnesota Model” rehabilitation (Alcoholics Anonymous, n.d.; Anderson et al., 1999); the only medication was disulfiram (Antabuse), an aversive therapy introduced in the 1950s (Lanz et al., 2023). Heroin addiction, by contrast, was largely criminalized or dealt with in a few specialized settings. Two U.S. Public Health Service “narcotics farms” (Lexington, KY and Fort Worth, TX) provided detoxification and research, but these had limited capacity (Besteman, 1992). Outside of prisons, a new grassroots approach emerged namely the therapeutic community (TC) model, starting with Synanon in 1958, which placed people with substance use disorders in long-term peer-run residences emphasizing abstinence and confrontational therapy (Besteman, 1992). By the mid-1960s, Synanon and offshoot TCs like Daytop Village and Phoenix House were spreading in urban areas (Clark, 2017, pp. 54–57, 86–121; Weill Cornell Medicine, n.d.).

A major breakthrough came in 1964 when Drs. Vincent Dole and Marie Nyswander in New York City pioneered methadone maintenance as the first pharmacological treatment for opioid addiction (Heidbreder et al., 2023). They discovered that daily oral methadone, a long-acting opioid agonist, could stabilize individuals with heroin dependence, eliminating withdrawal and cravings while blocking heroin’s euphoria (Heidbreder et al., 2023). In their landmark 1965 report, Dole and Nyswander proposed a “metabolic” treatment for what they viewed as a chronic, relapsing disease (Dole and Nyswander, 1965). Methadone maintenance clinics began to spread by the late 1960s following dramatic early results. According to an oral history by Dole, early programs under Nyswander’s direction emphasized holistic rehabilitation, tailoring treatment to each patient’s medical and social needs stating that “the strength of the early programs… was in their sensitivity to individual human problems” (Courtwright et al., 1989, p. 338). Simply dispensing methadone without counseling and social work was seen as insufficient. Dole noted that thinking methadone alone could “solve a complicated social problem [like addiction] seems to me beyond comprehension,” underscoring the importance of the “assisted” part of medication-assisted treatment (Courtwright et al., 1989, p. 338).

Federal policy simultaneously opened and constrained treatment. The Narcotic Addict Rehabilitation Act of 1966 permitted civil treatment for some people who otherwise faced imprisonment (Besteman, 1992). The Controlled Substances Act of 1970 then organized drugs into schedules and placed methadone in Schedule II, allowing medical use under tight control (Stolberg, 2011).

Importantly, during the 1970s the previously separate spheres of “alcoholism treatment” and “drug abuse treatment” began to converge. Debates raged over whether alcohol and drug programs should be administratively and clinically merged as they had been distinct fields throughout most of the 20th century. By the late 1970s and into the 1980s, many state and local systems moved toward integrating services for alcohol and drug disorders, although at the national level they remained separate bureaucracies for some time (White, 2014, pp. 380–381). This convergence laid groundwork for recognizing multiple substance dependencies within one individual. Still, the term “co-occurring disorders” was not yet in use for substance combinations. Patients with co-occurring OUD and AUD might cycle between different programs (a patient on methadone, for instance, might be referred to Alcoholics Anonymous for their drinking). The need for integrated approaches to polydrug addiction was just beginning to be acknowledged in clinical circles. Care availability was largely determined by geography and program gatekeeping, so patients with co-occurring OUD/AUD often had to navigate separate entry points rather than a single coordinated treatment pathway.

The war on drugs and its impact (1970s-1980s)

On June 17, 1971, President Richard Nixon formally characterized illicit drug use as “public enemy number one,” launching what came to be known as the War on Drugs (Nixon Foundation, 2016). Notably, Nixon’s strategy was two-pronged given that alongside law enforcement measures, his administration significantly expanded federal support for treatment. In 1971 Nixon created the Special Action Office for Drug Abuse Prevention (SAODAP) under Dr. Jerome Jaffe, which oversaw a rapid scale-up of methadone programs and community-based treatment slots (Besteman, 1992, Courtwright, 1992, pp. 30–31). Federal funding for addiction treatment in the early 1970s grew dramatically, reflecting a recognition that treatment could reduce crime (Courtwright, 2001, pp. 170–174). By 1972, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) issued the first regulations to standardize methadone clinics, and in 1974 Congress passed the Narcotic Addict Treatment Act (NATA). NATA confined methadone dispensing to licensed Opioid Treatment Programs (OTPs), effectively creating a “closed” clinic system for OUD treatment (Heidbreder et al., 2023). This had lasting consequences such that methadone clinics were often located in urban centers and served largely low-income and minority populations. As one review notes, the highly regulated OTP system was geographically biased toward urban, economically disadvantaged areas, with a greater representation of Black and Latino patients, contributing to racial disparities and persistent stigma in OUD care (Heidbreder et al., 2023). In essence, the methadone clinic model (a product of 1970s policy) separated OUD treatment from mainstream healthcare, and the patients were often viewed through a criminal-justice lens rather than a medical lens (American Association for the Treatment of Opioid Dependence Inc., 2022, pp. 3–4; Courtwright, 2001, pp. 170–174).

Alcohol treatment in the 1970s remained divided between public programs serving lower-income patients and private or hospital-affiliated programs serving insured patients (Saxe et al., 1983, pp. 31, 70–72; White, 2014, p. 385). The Hughes Act of 1970 established the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism (NIAAA) and funded treatment services (National Institutes of Health, 2025, White, 2014, pp. 375–379). Early “dual diagnosis” programs addressed mental illness plus alcohol or drug use, not usually two substance disorders, but helped establish the later co-occurring-disorders framework (Drake and Wallach, 2000).

The 1980s intensified punitive drug policy and the “Just Say No” message while reducing federal treatment support and increasing incarceration (Britannica Editors, n.d.; Courtwright, 2001; Walker, 2011; White, 2014). Moralizing campaigns conflicted with emerging disease models (Marian House, n.d.). Insurance restrictions and reduced inpatient coverage shortened stays and closed programs, while outpatient units scaled back social supports (Coulam and Gaumer, 1992; D’Aunno and Vaughn, 1995; Etheridge et al., 1995; Friedmann et al., 1999). Care therefore shifted toward acute intervention rather than chronic management (White, 2014).

Naltrexone, an opioid antagonist approved for opioid dependence in 1984, had limited OUD uptake because initiation requires opioid abstinence and retention was poor (Singh and Saadabadi, 2023). Trials later supported its 1994 approval for alcohol dependence, the first new AUD medication in decades (Center for Substance Abuse Treatment, 2009b). Methadone remained central to OUD treatment but politically stigmatized. A 1990 federal review documented variable clinic quality, including inadequate dosing and services (American Association for the Treatment of Opioid Dependence Inc., 2022; U.S. Government Accountability Office, 1990). The Institute of Medicine (IOM) recommended patient-centered, outcome-based oversight in 1995 (Institute of Medicine Committee on Federal Regulation of Methadone Treatment, 1995). Federal rules subsequently transferred primary oversight from the FDA to the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA) in 2001 and established accreditation standards (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2024).

Throughout the 1980s, the concept of “co-occurring disorders” primarily referred to mental illness accompanied by an SUD. Addiction treatment and mental health care were still largely separate silos, causing many “parallel treatment” failures. Innovators like Dr. Robert Drake at Dartmouth began developing integrated approaches in the late 1980s . As Drake reflected, in that era, “the addiction treatment system… was completely antagonistic towards medical care and psychiatrists,” and at the same time “the mental health side, I think we were just terribly, terribly naïve, and the addictions people realized that” (Drake, 2008). By 1989, the term “dual diagnosis” was appearing in psychiatric literature, focusing attention on the high prevalence of substance use among psychiatric patients. However, within SUD treatment, care for patients with multiple SUDs (e.g., co-occurring OUD and AUD) was not yet systematically integrated. Typically, one disorder was considered “primary.” For instance, an opioid treatment program might require a patient to also attend alcohol counseling separately. The need for comprehensive, one-stop treatment for co-occurring SUDs was increasingly evident, but robust models would not be fully realized until the 1990s and 2000s (Drake et al., 1998, Drake and Wallach, 2000). Confining methadone to licensed OTPs expanded capacity but entrenched a clinic-only channel that concentrated access in certain locales and reinforced stigma and unequal availability.

New medications and mainstreaming of treatment (1990s-2000s)

By the early 1990s, attitudes began to shift from the punitive approaches of the 1980s toward a more medical and public health-oriented framework. This was partly driven by accumulating evidence that addiction is a chronic, relapsing disorder with biological underpinnings. In 1987, the American Medical Association formally declared AUD and other SUDs “diseases” (echoing its earlier 1956 statement on AUD; American Medical Association, 2017; Indiana University Health, 2020). Researchers in neuroscience were demonstrating lasting brain changes in addiction, bolstering the “brain disease model” by the 1990s (Leshner, 1997). One consequence of these shifts was greater openness to medication-assisted treatment (MAT) for both OUD and AUD (Kunz et al., 2024).

The 1990s saw important pharmacological innovations. For OUD, a partial opioid agonist called buprenorphine was under development. Buprenorphine had actually been synthesized in 1966 in the United Kingdom as a painkiller, but its potential as an addiction treatment was realized later (Heidbreder et al., 2023). Clinical trials in the 1990s showed that buprenorphine could suppress withdrawal and cravings like methadone and with a similar safety profile (Johnson et al., 1992, Ling et al., 1998). In 2000, the U.S. Congress passed the Drug Addiction Treatment Act (DATA 2000) which was a landmark policy that allowed qualified physicians to prescribe approved Schedule III-V medications for OUD in general medical practice (Heidbreder et al., 2023). This law explicitly aimed to “mainstream” opioid treatment by moving it beyond the restrictive clinic system. The FDA approved buprenorphine (Subutex and the buprenorphine/naloxone combo Suboxone) for OUD in October 2002, the culmination of 36 years of development (Food and Drug Administration, 2002, Heidbreder et al., 2023). This was a sea change considering that for the first time since the early 1910s, American doctors in regular offices could treat opioid addiction with an opioid-based medication (something made illegal by interpretation of the 1914 Harrison Act; Murphy, 2012). Buprenorphine’s introduction created a two-track system where methadone continued in specialized OTP clinics, while buprenorphine was available by prescription in primary care or psychiatry clinics. In practice, this led to socio-demographic differences in access and studies later found that buprenorphine patients were more often white and privately insured, whereas methadone clinic patients were more often low-income and minorities (Andraka-Christou, 2021, Hansen et al., 2016). This disparity reflected lingering structural inequities such that the newer, more flexible treatment was reaching a more advantaged population, whereas marginalized groups remained in the more regulated system.

For AUD, the mid-1990s brought naltrexone’s approval (1994) as noted, and gradually this medication gained acceptance as an adjunct to counseling for alcohol dependence (Center for Substance Abuse Treatment, 2009b). In 2004, the FDA approved acamprosate (Campral) for AUD, following its success in Europe (Kranzler and Gage, 2008). Acamprosate modulates glutamate and gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) transmission to reduce post-withdrawal craving (Kalk and Lingford-Hughes, 2014); it was the first medication aimed specifically at maintaining abstinence in alcohol dependence (distinct from naltrexone’s role in reducing heavy drinking). By the late 1990s, another AUD medication, disulfiram, had fallen somewhat out of favor due to adherence issues, but it remained available as a deterrent therapy (Suh et al., 2006). Thus, entering the 2000s, clinicians had a small arsenal for AUD including disulfiram, naltrexone (now available in oral form and eventually an extended-release injection by 2006), and acamprosate. Still, these medications were underutilized; many addiction programs, influenced by the abstinence-based tradition, were slow to adopt pharmacotherapy for alcohol (Ducharme et al., 2006, Qeadan et al., 2021).

SAMHSA’s creation in 1992 aligned federal substance-use and mental-health programs administratively (Duff, 2020). Integrated Dual Disorder Treatment (IDDT) and related models were tested during the 1990s, and SAMHSA later promoted “no wrong door” care (Center for Substance Abuse Treatment, 2005; Drake et al., 2001). Programs became more willing to address all presenting substance use rather than exclude people for a second disorder, but implementation remained uneven.

Policy milestones in the late 1990s and 2000s also improved the landscape of treatment access. In 1996, Congress passed the Mental Health Parity Act (MHPA), the first federal law against insurance discrimination for behavioral health. MHPA was limited as it required parity in annual and lifetime dollar limits for mental health benefits (in large group plans) but did not mandate coverage of SUD treatment and had many loopholes (Hegner, 1997). Still, it set an important precedent. More sweeping change came with the 2008 passage of the Mental Health Parity and Addiction Equity Act (MHPAEA), championed by Senators Paul Wellstone and Pete Domenici. MHPAEA required insurers that offer mental health and SUD benefits to provide them at parity with medical/surgical benefits in all quantitative and nonquantitative treatment limitations (Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, 2024). In effect, if an insurance plan covered addiction treatment, it could no longer impose higher copays or stricter limitations than for other chronic illnesses. This was a major step in medicalizing addiction in policy by recognizing SUDs as conditions deserving equal coverage. The Affordable Care Act (ACA) of 2010 then cemented and expanded these gains. It made mental health and substance use treatment an “Essential Health Benefit” that must be included in individual and small-group insurance plans, and it extended parity requirements to those plans as well (Beronio et al., 2013). The ACA also expanded Medicaid in many states, which brought insurance coverage to thousands of low-income individuals with SUDs (Saloner et al., 2017, Wen et al., 2015).

Coverage remains policy-dependent. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act of 2025 introduced Medicaid eligibility and marketplace changes projected to reduce insurance coverage over the following decade (Jaffe, 2025). Because Medicaid expansion improved access to behavioral-health treatment, future implementation may erode some of the gains achieved after 2014.

Buprenorphine access also broadened through higher patient limits, expanded clinician eligibility under the Comprehensive Addiction and Recovery Act (CARA) of 2016, and elimination of the special Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) X-waiver by the Mainstreaming Addiction Treatment Act, enacted in 2022 and implemented in 2023 (Dhillon et al., 2024, Fornili and Fogger, 2017). These changes reduced a major barrier in rural and underserved areas (Andrilla et al., 2019, Barnett et al., 2019). Nevertheless, MOUD and MAUD still diffused through different incentives and settings, leaving many patients with partial pharmacotherapy and fragmented care.

Disparities and evolving attitudes: race, gender, and socioeconomic status

Throughout the history of OUD/AUD treatment, disparities in access and outcomes have been evident across racial, gender, and socioeconomic lines. Early in the 20th century, racist enforcement (e.g. opium laws targeting Chinese immigrants, cocaine scares targeting Black Americans) shaped who was criminalized versus who got treatment (Drug Policy Alliance, n.d.). By the 1960s-70s, when modern treatment systems developed, racial disparities persisted in subtle ways. For example, methadone clinics, as noted, were predominantly sited in inner-city neighborhoods and disproportionately served Black and Hispanic clients (Heidbreder et al., 2023; National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (NASEM), 2022). This was partly by design, as heroin-related OUD was framed largely as a condition affecting urban racial and ethnic minority communities in the 1970s (Courtwright, 1992; Raz, 2017). While methadone undoubtedly helped many, the decision to confine it to specialized clinics with daily supervised dosing and strict rules contributed to stigma (NASEM, 2022). By contrast, alcohol treatment in mid-century America often took place in private facilities or via family doctors, avenues more accessible to white, middle-class individuals (White, 2014). Thus, one could argue that the medicalization of AUD treatment exerted less social control over patients with AUD, who were more often perceived as belonging to the dominant social group, than over patients with OUD, who were more often associated with marginalized groups (Bourgois, 2000).

These contrasts sharpened during the crack-cocaine era, when Black communities encountered intensive policing and racially disparate sentencing (Finklea and Sacco, 2021; Ghandnoosh et al., 2023; James and Jordan, 2018). When prescription opioids and heroin later affected more suburban, rural, and White communities, policy discourse emphasized compassion and treatment (Rudd et al., 2016). Analysts linked this demographic shift to faster medicalization and destigmatization (Hansen and Netherland, 2016). Bipartisan reforms such as CARA and the SUPPORT Act reflected a public-health narrative less consistently applied to earlier crises (Ghandnoosh and Anderson, 2017; Murthy, 2016).

Women historically faced greater stigma and treatment barriers, while early programs were designed largely around men (Campbell and Ettore, 2011, Green, 2006, Greenfield et al., 2007). Federal attention to women’s addiction grew in the 1970s, Women for Sobriety was founded in 1975, and specialized services for pregnant and postpartum women expanded during the 1980s and 1990s (McMurtrie et al., 1999, White, 2014). Programs increasingly addressed childcare, trauma, and gender-specific patterns of use (Greenfield and Grella, 2009). Betty Ford’s public disclosure and the 1982 Betty Ford Center helped reduce stigma for women with alcohol and other substance use disorders (White, 2014). Yet patients with co-occurring OUD/AUD may still struggle to find trauma-informed, family-compatible care.

Socioeconomic status heavily influences access to care. Throughout our timeframe, wealthier individuals could afford private detox and rehab (or now, medications via private physicians), whereas poorer individuals relied on public clinics, which might have waiting lists or stricter rules (White, 2014). Prior to parity laws, many insurance plans simply did not cover much addiction treatment, leading to high out-of-pocket costs that were prohibitive for lower-income families (White, 2014). The ACA’s expansion of Medicaid after 2014 dramatically improved access for the poor; indeed, states that expanded Medicaid saw larger increases in addiction treatment utilization compared to non-expansion states (Maclean and Saloner, 2019, Wen et al., 2015). Yet even today, disparities persist such that rural areas have fewer buprenorphine prescribers, and Black and Hispanic patients with OUD are statistically less likely to get medication treatment than white patients, reflecting systemic issues (Barnett et al., 2019, Barnett et al., 2023, Drake et al., 2024). Recent data show that despite progress, about 86% of Americans with OUD still do not receive MOUD with underuse most severe in marginalized populations (Jones et al., 2023, Krawczyk and Rivera, 2022).

From moral failure to chronic-disease framing

Culturally, the framing of addiction underwent a profound shift by the 2000s. Early on, addiction was viewed through lenses of morality, criminality, or willpower. Over time, thanks to advocacy, science, and shifts in public awareness, a medical model took hold. By 2016, the U.S. Surgeon General’s Report on Alcohol, Drugs, and Health explicitly called addiction a “chronic brain disease” and urged a “cultural shift” in how Americans talk about it (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS), 2016, pp. v-vi, Chapter 2). The “chronic disease” concept emphasizes that, like diabetes or hypertension, addiction requires long-term management, not a one-time cure. This model also normalizes the expectation of relapse as a potential part of the course, arguing that we should treat relapse as a signal to adjust treatment, not as a personal failure (DiClemente and Crisafulli, 2022, McLellan et al., 2000). Treatment programs gradually adopted this philosophy. For example, where 1970s programs might discharge a patient for a single relapse (“failing treatment”), modern approaches are more likely to continue engagement after a lapse just as an oncologist would not drop a cancer patient who briefly worsens. As one treatment administrator put it, “We do not state that patients with hypertension or asthma ‘successfully completed treatment,’” implying that we should not do so for addiction, which requires ongoing care management (Marian House, n.d.). Despite these advances, public stigma dies hard. A 2018 survey found that a majority of Americans simultaneously viewed addiction as a medical condition requiring treatment and attributed it to “bad character” or “lack of discipline” (AP-NORC, 2018).

Integrated models and co-occurring disorder treatment

By the 2000s and 2010s, it became standard to talk about “co-occurring disorders” in both clinical and policy contexts encompassing both co-occurring mental illness with SUD and co-occurring multiple SUDs. SAMHSA and professional organizations developed guidelines for treating “the whole person” (Center for Substance Abuse Treatment, 2005). One influential approach was the IDDT model, which provides concurrent, coordinated care for mental health and substance issues. Research by Drake and colleagues showed that integrated care (versus parallel, siloed care) led to better outcomes for dual-diagnosis patients (Drake and Mueser, 2000, Kikkert et al., 2018, Mangrum et al., 2006). The paradigm shifted to assert that every program should be capable of at least basic co-occurring disorder care which led to the concept of “dual diagnosis capability” as a criterion for program licensing and funding. In practice, this meant mental health centers needed to be able to address substance use, and addiction programs needed mental health competency. Clinically, more providers became cross-trained (McGovern et al., 2007, Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2009).

For co-occurring OUD and AUD, pharmacotherapy remains constrained by drug interactions and delivery systems. Naltrexone is approved for both disorders, but it precipitates withdrawal in opioid-dependent patients and cannot be used with methadone or buprenorphine (Center for Substance Abuse Treatment, 2009a, p. 11; Soyka, 2015). Acamprosate is therefore a principal AUD option during opioid-agonist treatment. OUD treatment itself may reduce alcohol-related acute events by improving stability, supported by observational and mechanistic evidence (Ciccocioppo et al., 2007, Xu et al., 2021), but dedicated AUD care is often still required. Workforce, reimbursement, and coordination remain bottlenecks for combined pharmacotherapy.

The metabolic turn: GLP-1 receptor agonists and co-occurring AUD/OUD

GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs), developed for type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and obesity, also modulate central reward pathways. Semaglutide reduced binge-like drinking in mice and alcohol consumption in dependent rats (Chuong et al., 2023). Opioid-specific experiments strengthen the cross-disorder rationale where exendin-4 reduced oxycodone taking, seeking, and cue-induced reinstatement in rats without compromising oxycodone antinociception (Zhang et al., 2020), and exendin-4 and liraglutide reduced heroin seeking, self-administration, or drug-induced reinstatement (Douton et al., 2021, Douton et al., 2022). Related preclinical effects have been reported for nicotine and cocaine (Herman and Schmidt, 2024, Jerlhag, 2025, Sørensen et al., 2015). These models establish biological plausibility, not clinical efficacy.

Human evidence remains preliminary but now extends across AUD and OUD. In patients with T2DM and OUD, Wang et al., 2024b reported lower one-year opioid-overdose risk for semaglutide than for other antidiabetic medications (hazard ratios 0.32–0.58). In a separate obesity cohort, semaglutide was associated with approximately 50%-56% lower incidence or recurrence of AUD (Wang et al., 2024a). Dai et al. (2025) emulated a target trial among Medicare beneficiaries aged 65 years or older with T2DM. Compared with dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, GLP-1RA initiation was associated with lower hospitalization for any SUD (hazard ratio 0.76, 95% confidence interval 0.67–0.86) and OUD (0.64, 0.43–0.96), but not significantly lower AUD hospitalization (0.76, 0.53–1.08); differences versus sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors were not significant. Xie et al. (2025), mapping 175 outcomes among 215,970 U.S. veterans initiating GLP-1RAs, found lower risks for several SUD outcomes but higher risks for gastrointestinal and selected other adverse outcomes. The older and predominantly male populations limit generalizability. Among the studies reviewed here, Qeadan et al. (2025) was the largest published real-world analysis, including separate cohorts of 503,747 patients with OUD and 817,309 patients with AUD across 136 U.S. health systems; GIP/GLP-1RA prescriptions were associated with lower rates of opioid overdose (adjusted incidence rate ratio 0.60, 95% confidence interval 0.43–0.83) and alcohol intoxication (0.50, 0.40–0.63), respectively. Extending this work, Qeadan et al. (2026) evaluated 107,217 patients with co-occurring OUD and AUD across 149 U.S. health systems and, to our knowledge, remains the only published study specifically examining associations between GIP/GLP-1RA prescriptions and outcomes in this concurrent population; prescriptions were associated with lower overdose and substance use disorder-related hospitalization risks in several treatment and metabolic strata.

The real-world studies by Wang and Volkow, 2024a, Wang and Volkow, 2024b, Dai et al. (2025), Xie et al. (2025), and Qeadan et al., 2025, Qeadan et al., 2026 provide convergent but hypothesis-generating evidence. As observational analyses, they remain susceptible to residual confounding, confounding by indication, healthy-user bias, selection, exposure misclassification, and differential health-care contact; some also include small GLP-1RA-exposed or treatment-defined subgroups. Nevertheless, the consistency of associations across multiple data sources, populations, comparators, and analytic approaches strengthens the rationale for prospective evaluation, although it does not establish causal efficacy. Randomized evidence, although limited, is currently available only for alcohol outcomes where once-weekly semaglutide reduced alcohol craving and drinks per drinking day in a small phase 2 AUD trial (Hendershot et al., 2025). Larger addiction-specific trials must establish dosing, durability, safety, and interactions with established MOUD and MAUD.

Voices from the field: histories and reflections

The historical narrative of co-occurring OUD/AUD treatment is enriched by the firsthand accounts of clinicians, policymakers, and people in recovery. For instance, the book Addicts Who Survived: An Oral History of Narcotic Use in America, 1923–1965 includes recollections of people who used heroin in the mid-20th-century who often also drank alcohol. They described a time when the only formal treatment options for opioid addiction were two “narcotics farms” in Lexington, KY and Fort Worth, TX (Courtwright et al., 1989). Dr. Vincent Dole recalled how controversial his team’s work was, receiving threats of legal action from the Bureau of Narcotics. Dr. Dole describes how when they published their report in JAMA, they were met with skepticism from the medical community and accusations of fraud from the Bureau of Narcotics (Courtwright et al., 1989). Drs. Dole and Marie Nyswander’s advocacy for the metabolic disease concept of addiction in medical journals and conferences as well as the general press helped spur the medicalization of addiction treatment (Anderson, 2024, Courtwright, 1997). These pioneers’ research and advocacy helped shift skeptical attitudes among colleagues and laid intellectual groundwork for the chronic disease model.

Dr. Jerome Jaffe, Nixon’s drug czar, reflected on the early 1970s federal treatment expansion that due to budget constraints, he favored broad access to methadone maintenance therapy over comprehensive rehabilitation that could only serve a minority of people who used heroin. Jaffe’s pragmatic approach of getting as many people with heroin addiction as possible into treatment, even if services were basic, was pivotal in scaling up methadone nationally (Besteman, 1992). Conversely, others like psychiatrist Dr. Robert DuPont (the first director of the National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA) in 1973) have recounted how the pendulum swung under Reagan, when funding dried up and the focus moved to “user accountability” and urine testing (DuPont later became a proponent of a drug-free approach and urine testing, illustrating the internal debates in the field; DuPont, 2000; Raz, 2017).

Frontline providers from the 1980s have described how the human immunodeficiency virus/acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (HIV/AIDS) epidemic forced more integration. For example, methadone clinics started providing HIV education and mental health counseling, becoming comprehensive health centers out of necessity (Rothman et al., 2007, Selwyn et al., 1993, Selwyn et al., 1989). This move toward integrated addiction treatment and HIV prevention was spurred by grassroots activists like the National AIDS Brigade, which defied drug paraphernalia laws in the late 1980s to provide homegrown needle exchange programs in the Northeast. This kind of grassroots harm reduction that emerged outside traditional treatment settings was soon adopted by public health clinics, departments, and agencies, reflecting a shift in ethos toward public health pragmatism even during a punitive era (Heller and Paone, 2011, Lowry, 2021; McLean, 2011).

In the alcohol treatment arena, renowned figures like William White (historian, addiction treatment professional, and recovery advocate) have commented on institutional shifts over time. White notes that the 1980s-90s saw a “new recovery advocacy movement” emerge, partly in response to the overly clinical/medical approach where recovery community organizations like Faces and Voices of Recovery pushed to keep peer support and “recovery” at the center along with advancing science (White, 2021).

Conclusion

From 1960 through 2026, U.S. treatment of OUD and AUD moved from siloed systems toward partially integrated frameworks (Table 1). Methadone maintenance, therapeutic communities, and early administrative convergence marked the 1960s and 1970s. Punitive policy dominated the 1980s while researchers advanced disease models. The 1990s and 2000s expanded OUD and AUD medications, parity, and integrated-care models. The 2010s through 2026 emphasized implementation, harm reduction, mainstream prescribing, and biologically informed targets. Across all eras, institutional arrangements determined who could benefit. Clinical innovation has never guaranteed access. MOUD and MAUD continue to travel through different regulatory, financing, and care-setting pathways, leaving patients with co-occurring OUD/AUD to navigate fragmented services. Stigma, workforce shortages, geography, and structural inequity still determine who receives evidence-based care.

If efficacy and safety are confirmed, GLP-1RAs could provide a distinctive, metabolically targeted strategy for shared reward-related mechanisms across AUD and OUD. Naltrexone is already approved for both disorders, but its opioid-antagonist action requires opioid abstinence and prevents concurrent use with methadone or buprenorphine. GLP-1RAs may therefore be relevant as a cross-disorder adjunct that does not rely on opioid-receptor blockade, although that role remains unproven. Even a successful medication will require coverage, prescriber access, safety monitoring, and integration with MOUD and MAUD. Without delivery reform, innovation will reproduce the fragmentation and inequity of earlier eras.
