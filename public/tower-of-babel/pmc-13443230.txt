Introduction

Ileosigmoid knotting (ISK), also known as compound volvulus, represents a rare yet catastrophic form of double closed-loop intestinal obstruction where the ileum wraps around the sigmoid colon base (or vice versa), predominantly in sigmoid volvulus-endemic “volvulus belt” regions of Africa and Asia.1-4

ISK accounts for approximately 7% of all sigmoid volvulus cases, with an estimated global incidence of about 0.3 patients per 100,000 population per year. 5 Recent reviews of cases report that ISK typically affects adults in their third to fifth decade of life and shows a marked male predominance. 4

Clinically, patients with ISK usually present with acute abdominal pain, abdominal distension, vomiting, and obstipation or an inability to pass flatus, findings that overlap with other forms of intestinal obstruction.4-6 Large-series reviews show abdominal pain or tenderness in about 99%, abdominal distension in 88%, vomiting in 56%, and obstipation in 59% of cases, underscoring the nonspecificity of the symptom complex. 4 Plain abdominal radiography typically reveals features of mixed-type bowel obstruction, whereas computed tomography (CT) demonstrates twisted bowel loops, a mesenteric “whirl” sign, and deviation of the distal colon, with diagnostic accuracy exceeding 90% in selected centers.4,7

This case illustrates ISK in a 62-year-old male from a volvulus-belt setting, managed with a damage-control strategy in the context of massive necrosis and intraoperative shock, highlighting the evolving role of staged operative approaches in complicated compound volvulus. This case report was prepared in accordance with the CARE (CAse REport) guidelines, and the completed checklist is provided as supplementary material (Supplementary file 1).

Case Presentation

A 62-year-old man presented to the emergency department with a 36-hour history of severe crampy abdominal pain associated with bilious vomiting, progressive abdominal distension, and failure to pass stool or flatus. His only known comorbidity was gastroesophageal reflux disease managed with as-needed proton pump inhibitors. On physical examination, he appeared acutely ill with vital signs showing hypotension (blood pressure 90/50 mmHg) and tachycardia (pulse rate 114 bpm). Abdominal exam revealed visible distension with moderate diffuse tenderness. Digital rectal examination confirmed a collapsed rectum with a small amount of stool ballooned out. Laboratory investigations demonstrated leukocytosis with left shift on complete blood count. Plain abdominal X-ray (Figure 1) showed massively distended small bowel loops and sigmoid colon suggestive of obstruction.

Preoperative plain abdominal radiograph showing multiple dilated bowel loops with several air–fluid levels, consistent with acute intestinal obstruction

Following initial resuscitation, the patient was taken for emergency laparotomy with borderline hemodynamic stability. Intraoperatively, a tightly knotted ileosigmoid complex was identified, with the small bowel twisted around the sigmoid colon (Figure 2A and B).

(A-B): Tightly knotted ileosigmoid complex with markedly distended, congested bowel loops indicating vascular compromise

The involved bowel loops were markedly distended, edematous, and darkly discolored, indicating compromised perfusion. Further exploration revealed extensive gangrene involving nearly the entire small intestine, sparing only approximately 70 cm of proximal jejunum and 10 cm of distal ileum. The sigmoid colon was also gangrenous and formed part of the knot. A clear transition between viable and non-viable bowel segments was identified intraoperatively, guiding the extent of resection.

Given the patient’s intraoperative instability and persistent shock despite ongoing resuscitation (mean arterial pressure < 60 mmHg), a damage control approach was adopted. Rapid untwisting of the knot was performed to relieve the obstruction (Figure 3), followed by en bloc resection of all non-viable bowel segments. To achieve rapid source control and avoid prolonged anesthesia, no anastomosis or stoma was created during the initial operation. The bowel ends were stapled closed, and temporary abdominal closure was achieved using a sterile 3-liter irrigation bag sutured to the skin edges (Bogota bag technique). The initial procedure was completed within approximately 30 minutes.

Intraoperative maneuver demonstrating careful untwisting of the ileosigmoid knot

After aggressive resuscitation in the intensive care unit (ICU), including vasopressor support and administration of 4 units of packed red blood cells, a second-look laparotomy was performed 12 hours later. At the second operation, the patient was hemodynamically stable. The Bogota bag was removed, and primary fascial closure was achieved. Definitive surgical management included jejuno-ileal end-to-end anastomosis and formation of an end colostomy. The final resected specimen confirmed extensive gangrene involving both the small bowel and sigmoid colon (Figure 4).

Final resected specimen following damage control surgery, showing en bloc resection of gangrenous ileum and sigmoid colon

Postoperatively, he was transferred to intensive care on continued intravenous fluids, antibiotics, and monitoring. He spent 4 days in the ICU, during which total parenteral nutrition (TPN) was initiated to mitigate the high risk of Short Bowel Syndrome given his residual anatomy. TPN was successfully transitioned over 7 days to specialized enteral nutrition as stoma function established with a high-output effluent (initially 1.2–1.5 L/day), which was managed with oral antimotility agents and rehydration solutions. No major immediate complications, such as anastomotic leak or wound infection, occurred. The patient remained hemodynamically stable and was discharged on hospital day 18 to a specialized care facility for continuing nutritional rehabilitation and stoma care. At his 3-month outpatient follow-up, his weight had stabilized, his nutritional status was acceptable via a customized oral diet supplemented with key micronutrients, and he exhibited no signs of severe malabsorption.

Discussion

ISK is an uncommon but highly lethal form of intestinal obstruction. 4 The clinical presentation of ISK mimics other acute intestinal obstructions, causing diagnostic delay. 8 Over 90% of cases present with abdominal pain, and a majority also have distension, vomiting, and obstipation, a constellation of symptoms that precisely matched our patient’s presentation on admission. 4 Digital rectal examination may reveal an empty or collapsed rectum with or without blood, as observed in the current case, but such findings remain nonspecific. 9 Plain abdominal radiography typically shows mixed-type bowel obstruction with dilated small bowel and sigmoid loops; however, this appearance overlaps with sigmoid volvulus and other strangulating obstructions, thereby limiting its specificity.4,10 CT scanning is far more informative, with twisted bowel segments and a mesenteric “whirl” sign described in over 90% of accurately imaged cases, but access to CT remains limited in many volvulus-belt settings, including our own.4,7,8 This regional pattern is heavily reflected in East African literature, where large case series from Ethiopia, 9 Kenya, 10 and Uganda 11 underscore the endemic nature of the disease.

ISK arises from a combination of a long, mobile small-bowel mesentery and a redundant sigmoid colon with a narrow mesenteric base, allowing the ileum to rotate around the sigmoid and its mesentery in a closed-loop configuration.4,12,13 This configuration creates a compound volvulus that produces two simultaneously strangulated closed loops, leading to a more rapid and severe compromise of vascular supply compared with isolated sigmoid volvulus.11,12 The resulting venous and arterial occlusion causes progressive ischemia, edema, and ultimately transmural gangrene of both the ileum and the sigmoid colon. 4 In the present case, the patient’s age of 62 years likely contributed to diminished mesenteric vascular reserve and reduced collateral circulation, accelerating the progression from ischemia to irreversible necrosis. 14 Moreover, the 36-hour history of symptoms before presentation placed this patient in a delayed-presentation category, during which the unrelieved closed-loop obstruction would have caused escalating intraluminal pressure, progressive impairment of microvascular perfusion, and eventual transmural infarction.

The management of ISK in the context of hemodynamic instability and extensive necrosis has evolved toward staged “damage-control” approaches. 15 Initial management includes aggressive fluid resuscitation and correction of electrolyte and acid-base imbalances.2,13,16 In the current case, initial resuscitation followed this same approach, including aggressive fluid and blood administration. The traditional surgical management of ISK with gangrenous loops relies on standard single-stage resections.4,12 When patients are hemodynamically stable, primary ileo-ileal or jejuno-ileal anastomosis combined with a Hartmann’s procedure or end colostomy is widely preferred.4,13 However, attempting time-consuming reconstructions or primary stoma maturations in a patient experiencing intraoperative shock can decisively increase mortality. 15 A staged damage-control strategy, resecting necrotic sources rapidly, leaving the abdomen open, and delaying reconstruction, bypasses this risk, shifting the physiological burden to a controlled ICU environment before definitive reconstruction.15,16 While regional reports, such as a recent case from Ethiopia by Ewnte and Girma, describe stable patients or localized volvulus successfully managed with single-stage resection and primary anastomosis, 17 our patient’s severe intraoperative shock required a distinct, staged damage-control approach. This comparison highlights that while ISK is encountered across the volvulus belt, the extent of necrosis and the patient’s hemodynamic status dictate the operative strategy, with damage control reserved for the most critically ill patients.

The choice of operative procedure should be guided by the patient’s hemodynamic status, operative risk assessment, and intraoperative findings.2,12,13 For patients who remain unstable either before surgery or after anesthesia induction despite adequate fluid resuscitation, damage control laparotomy should be the default approach. This strategy consists of en bloc resection of the involved segment without spillage of bowel contents, closure of the bowel ends with sutures or staples when available, peritoneal lavage, and temporary abdominal closure. 15 In this case, although rapid unknotting was performed to facilitate the assessment, the decision to resect was vital given that only 70 cm of proximal jejunum and 10 cm of distal ileum remained viable. This aggressive approach aims to control the source of sepsis while preserving sufficient intestinal length to reduce the long-term risk of short bowel syndrome. 12 Rapid untwisting of the knot and resection of all non-viable bowels were completed within 30 minutes, followed by temporary abdominal closure using a Bogota bag. After 12 hours of intensive resuscitation, our patient underwent a definitive second-look procedure consisting of a jejuno-ileal end-to-end anastomosis and an end colostomy, aligning with published principles that advocate deferring definitive reconstruction until hemodynamic stability is restored.2,12,18

The prognosis for ISK is notably poorer than that of simple sigmoid volvulus, with global mortality rates reported between 16% and 44%.1,9,12,13,19 Advanced age (>60 years), late presentation (beyond 24 hours), and the presence of double-segment gangrene are established predictors of a fatal outcome.12,19 Despite presenting with all three risk factors, an age of 62 years, a 36-hour delay, and diffuse necrosis, this patient achieved a successful recovery. However, the limited remaining small bowel length (70 cm of jejunum and 10 cm of ileum) places this patient at lifelong risk for short bowel syndrome, necessitating long-term nutritional monitoring, dietary modification, and possibly periodic parenteral nutrition support. This particular case exemplifies how a damage-control strategy can salvage a patient with massive necrosis and intraoperative shock, provided that intensive-care support and resuscitative capacity are available.

The experience also underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for ISK in areas with a high background incidence of sigmoid volvulus.8,20 When a patient presents with gangrenous-type obstruction and rapidly evolving septic physiology, the possibility of a double closed-loop obstruction should be considered.10,12 In such settings, early laparotomy may be preferable to prolonged attempts at non-surgical decompression, which are appropriate for simple sigmoid volvulus but can be hazardous when ISK is present. 2

Limitations of the Study

This case report has several inherent limitations. As a single case study, the findings and outcomes observed in this patient may not be generalizable to the broader population of patients with ileosigmoid knotting, given the wide variability in clinical presentations and treatment responses. The diagnosis was established intraoperatively, as preoperative imaging was limited to plain abdominal radiography due to resource constraints; computed tomography, which offers superior diagnostic accuracy for ISK, was not available at our center. Furthermore, the available follow-up data extend only to three months post-discharge, precluding a definitive assessment of the patient’s long-term nutritional status and the eventual development of short bowel syndrome complications. Additionally, as a retrospective case report, this study is susceptible to recall and documentation biases inherent to such designs. Notwithstanding these limitations, this case provides valuable insights into the successful application of damage control surgery in a resource-limited setting for a rare and life-threatening surgical emergency.

Conclusion

This case illustrates the successful application of a two-stage damage-control approach to ileosigmoid knotting with massive bowel necrosis and intraoperative shock. Early clinical awareness, timely surgical intervention, and staged operative strategies that prioritize hemodynamic stabilization before definitive reconstruction appear to improve survival in selected patients. In resource-constrained settings, strengthening emergency surgical pathways, expanding access to critical-care support, and implementing standardized protocols for gangrenous bowel obstruction are essential to reduce diagnostic delays and optimize outcomes in ISK.
