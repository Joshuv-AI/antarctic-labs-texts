Fermionic Averaged Circuit Eigenvalue Sampling
Adrian Chapman, Steven T. Flammia

Abstract
Fermionic averaged circuit eigenvalue sampling (FACES) is a protocol to simultaneously learn the averaged error rates of many fermionic linear optical (FLO) gates simultaneously and self-consistently from a suitable collection of FLO circuits. It is highly flexible, allowing for the in situ characterization of FLO-averaged gate-dependent noise under natural assumptions on a family of continuously parameterized one- and two-qubit gates. We rigorously show that our protocol has an efficient sampling complexity, owing in-part to useful properties of the Kravchuk transformations that feature in our analysis. We support our conclusions with numerical results. As FLO circuits become universal with access to certain resource states, we expect our results to inform noise characterization and error mitigation techniques on universal quantum computing architectures which naturally admit a fermionic description.

Introduction

As fundamental building blocks of our universe, fermions are naturally the mediators of quantum information in many quantum computing architectures, such as superconducting
[
1
]
and semiconductor devices
[
2
]
.
Many of the systems we hope to simulate with a quantum computer are systems of interacting fermions.
These include quantum materials and quantum chemical systems, where the relevant degrees of freedom are interacting electrons
[
3
]
.
It is thus natural to consider models of quantum computation that regard fermions as central objects.
In principle, this removes any overhead from switching between fermion- and spin-based encodings, but it also presents unique challenges for other quantum information processing tasks due to the nonlocal nature of fermionic antisymmetric exchange statistics.

Predominant among these tasks is the characterization of quantum noise.
Whereas noise is usually assumed to act independently on local degrees of freedom, subsequent gates will cause the effect of any such noise process to spread.
In particular, fermionic models of quantum computing generally involve families of continuously parameterized gates.
In this setting, even local noise will become complex—and thus difficult to learn—on the circuit timescales necessarily to realize quantum computational advantage.
To address this issue, we can take inspiration from solutions in the qubit setting.
In particular, we consider the strategy of
noise tailoring
, whereby noise is first physically driven to a desired form via averaging, or
twirling
, and the averaged noise parameters can be more easily learned
[
4
,
5
]
.

Noise tailoring is well-suited to the setting of gate-dependent noise, where we assume each distinct physical operation on the experiment introduces a unique, reproducible noise channel.
The general problem of estimating all noise in a gate set having gate-dependent noise
without
any averaging is quite challenging
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
, so it is natural to ask what role noise tailoring can play to simplify the problem.
Ref.
[
9
]
introduced averaged circuit eigenvalue sampling (ACES) for simultaneously characterizing the gate-dependent noise of many noisy Clifford gates with Pauli twirling.
Clifford circuits with Pauli noise preserve the set of Pauli observables up to an overall factor—called the circuit eigenvalue—which quantifies the noise in the circuit.
The main idea behind ACES is that the eigenvalues of many different noisy circuits are all dependent on a common set of gate-level noise eigenvalues, which can be extracted with access to enough experimental data.

It is well-understood that Clifford circuits with stabilizer measurements are not universal for quantum computation.
Any attempt to extend the ACES protocol to a universal circuit family will thus require a generalization to circuits and noise models that do not preserve unnormalized Pauli operators.
In this work, we provide a protocol for the characterization of noisy
fermionic linear optical
(FLO) circuits, or equivalently, their qubit-embedded versions, noisy nearest-neighbor
matchgate
circuits.
We call this FACES, for
fermionic averaged circuit eigenvalue sampling
.
FLO circuits describe general time-dependent free-fermion dynamics, and matchgate circuits describe these same dynamics on qubits under a Jordan-Wigner transformation
[
10
]
.
We work almost exclusively in the fermion picture here, but our results are interchangable across the two paradigms.

Though FLO and matchgate circuits are comprised of non-Clifford gates, they are nonetheless efficiently simulable classically.
However, they become universal for quantum computation when we additionally include the application of arbitrary Clifford gates; for example, even adding nearest-neighbor SWAP operations between qubits is sufficient.
Similarly, Clifford circuits become universal for quantum computation with the inclusion of arbitrary matchgates.
Since characterizing gate-dependent noise in Clifford circuits is a well-studied problem, we expect that FACES can be used in tandem with other methods to estimate averaged noise in universal gate sets.
Furthermore, we expect that FACES will find natural applications in the setting of quantum materials and chemistry simulations, for which fermionic degrees of freedom are fundamental.

Our main results include an algorithm for extracting the FLO-averaged eigenvalues of the elements of an elementary set of FLO unitary gates.
Additionally, we rigorously prove that our protocol has an efficient sample complexity when reasonable assumptions about our setting are met.
Finally, we demonstrate the efficacy of our protocol by numerically showing that it is feasible for realistic error rates.

Background and Notation

2.1
Fermionic Linear Optics

Fermionic linear optical (FLO) circuits constitute the most general group of unitary circuits that describe free-fermion dynamics.
This group is isomorphic to the group of matchgate circuits on many-body qubit systems by the Jordan-Wigner transformation.
For our purposes, we mainly keep the discussion phrased in terms of fermionic modes, with the understanding that all of our results admit corresponding descriptions in terms of qubits.

We consider FLO circuits acting on even-many fermion modes.
For such a system of
2n
modes, define the set of Majorana operators satisfying the canonical anticommutation relations

\displaystyle\{\gamma_{\mu},\gamma_{\nu}\}=2\delta_{\mu\nu}I\mathrm{,}\mbox{\hskip 14.22636pt}\mathrm{and}\mbox{\hskip 14.22636pt}\gamma_{\mu}^{\dagger}=\gamma_{\mu}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}

(3)

for all
\mu
,
\nu\in[2n]
, where the notation
[n]
means the set of integers
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Denote the associated group of FLO circuits as
\mathds{F}(2n)
.
The elements of this group are Gaussian unitaries up to reflection
U\coloneqq\gamma_{1}^{x}\exp(\sum_{\mu,\nu}h_{\mu\nu}\gamma_{\mu}\gamma_{\nu})
for
x\in\{0,1\}
and
\mathbf{h}
a real antisymmetric matrix.
These preserve linear combinations of Majorana modes under conjugation.
Writing
\mathcal{U}(A)\equiv UAU^{\dagger}
as the unitary conjugation channel by an element
U\in\mathds{F}(2n)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(\gamma_{\mu})=\sum_{\nu=1}^{2n}R_{\mu\nu}\gamma_{\nu}

(4)

for
\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}^{x}_{1}e^{-4\mathbf{h}}\in O(2n)
the single-particle transition matrix and
\mathbf{R}_{1}
the canonical reflection with elements
(\mathbf{R}_{1})_{\mu\nu}=-(-1)^{\delta_{\mu,1}}\delta_{\mu\nu}
.
By the canonical anticommutation relations,
eq.

3
, FLO circuits also preserve fixed-degree polynomials of Majorana operators.
For a subset
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\subseteq[2n]
, let
\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\equiv\prod_{\mu\in\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\gamma_{\mu}
denote the
Majorana monomial
labeled by
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, where the order of the product is taken such that the index is ascending from left to right.
Eq. (
4
) generalizes as

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})=\sum_{\{\boldsymbol{\beta}\subseteq[2n]||\boldsymbol{\beta}|=|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|\}}\mathrm{det}(\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\beta}})\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}

(5)

where
\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\beta}}
denotes the submatrix of
\mathbf{R}
with rows taken from
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and columns taken from
\boldsymbol{\beta}
(that is, Majorana monomials transform by a
compound
of
\mathbf{R}
).
We adopt the notational shorthand

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}|k,\ell\rightarrow\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\subseteq[\ell]||\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=k\}\,.

(6)

Linear transformations of the form in
eq.

5
constitute a representation of
O(2n)
by the Cauchy-Binet formula, and this representation is irreducible.
As explained in ref.
[
18
]
, FLO transformations are uniquely determined up to an overall phase by their corresponding element of
O(2n)
.

It is convenient to generalize our index-subset notation in
eq.

6
to binary strings with a fixed Hamming weight.
We refer to a binary string by a bold alphabetic character, and, using the same notation as in
eq.

6
, we denote
\mathbf{x}|k,\ell\rightarrow\{\mathbf{x}\subseteq\mathds{Z}_{2}^{\ell}||\mathbf{x}|=k\}
.
In both use cases, we drop explicit reference to
\ell
where clear from context and simply write “
\boldsymbol{\alpha}|k
” or “
\mathbf{x}|k
”.

We also need to consider these objects in terms of Pauli operators.
Denote by
\mathds{P}(n)
the
n
-qubit Pauli group, and let
{\sf{P}}(n)
denote this group modulo an overall phase.
We can label an
n
-qubit Pauli operator (with a convenient choice of phase) by a
2n
-bit string
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
as
\sigma_{\mathbf{x}}=i^{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}\otimes_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}^{a_{j}}Z_{j}^{b_{j}}
, denoting single-qubit Pauli operators on a fixed site by
\{X_{j},Y_{j},Z_{j}\}
.
Since we are exclusively concerned with Paulis modulo phase, multiplication in
\mathds{P}(n)
maps conveniently to mod-2 addition of the associated binary strings in
{\sf{P}}(n)
.

As stated earlier, Majorana monomials and Pauli operators are in one-to-one correspondence (up to a phase) by the Jordan-Wigner transformation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\gamma_{2j-1}=Z^{\otimes(j-1)}\otimes X_{j}&\\
\gamma_{2j}=Z^{\otimes(j-1)}\otimes Y_{j}&\end{cases}\mathrm{.}

(7)

These operators satisfy the canonical anticommutation relations
eq.

3
.
In this context, the group of FLO circuits is isomorphic to the group of matchgate circuits, which we denote as
\mathds{M}(n)
.
Matchgate circuits can be decomposed into 2-qubit matchgates of the form

\displaystyle G_{j}(A,B)\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}a_{00}&0&0&a_{01}\\
0&b_{00}&b_{01}&0\\
0&b_{10}&b_{11}&0\\
a_{10}&0&0&a_{11}\end{pmatrix}

(8)

on nearest-neighbor qubits
\{j,j+1\}
on a 1-d line.
Namely,
A
acts on the even-parity 2-qubit subspace of these qubits, and
B
on the odd-parity subspace.
We require
\mathrm{det}(A)=\mathrm{det}(B)
for
G_{j}(A,B)
to be a matchgate.
Note that, in particular, single-qubit rotations of the form
e^{i\theta Z_{j}}
are matchgates for all
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
and all
j\in[n]
.
Where clear from context, we refer to the groups
\mathds{F}(2n)
and
\mathds{M}(n)
interchangeably.
For example, by
\mathcal{U}(\sigma_{\mathbf{x}})
for
\mathcal{U}\in\mathds{F}(2n)
, we mean conjugation by the matchgate circuit associated to
\mathcal{U}
under the Jordan-Wigner transformation.

2.2
Kravchuk Matrices

A central object for our analysis is the following matrix

Definition 3
.

(Kravchuk matrix)
The
Kravchuk matrix
of order
\ell
, denoted by
\mathbf{M}^{(\ell)}\in\mathds{Z}^{(\ell+1)\times(\ell+1)}
, is an integer-valued matrix whose elements are symmetric polynomials evaluated on
\pm 1
.

\displaystyle M^{(\ell)}_{jk}\coloneqq e_{k}(-\mathbf{1}_{j}\oplus\mathbf{1}_{\ell-j})\mathrm{,}

(9)

where

,
k\in[\ell]\cup\{0\},

is the degree-
k

\boldsymbol{v}
, and
\mathbf{1}_{j}
is the length-

The Walsh-Hadamard matrix of size
2^{n}
has matrix elements
(-1)^{\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}}
in the standard basis, where
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
are bit-strings of length
n
that label matrix elements.
The elements of the Kravchuk matrix are obtained by summing
\sum_{\mathbf{y}|k}(-1)^{\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}}
to get a matrix element that depends only on the Hamming weight
k
and the bit-string
\mathbf{x}
.
In fact, this expression depends only on the Hamming weight of
\mathbf{x}
.
By keeping one copy of the resulting sum for each value that
|\mathbf{x}|
can take, we get the Kravchuk matrix elements in
eq.

9
.

The Kravchuk matrix plays an analogous role in FACES as the Walsh-Hadamard matrix does in the original (Clifford) ACES protocol.
Namely, it is the essential link between the eigenvalues and error probabilities of a FLO-twirled channel, as explained below.

We now give some alternative expressions for

which clearly show that this matrix can be computed efficiently.
Let us introduce the notation

will be understood from context.
Then we have the following lemma.

(11)

\displaystyle M_{jk}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=\max{(0,k+j-\ell)}}^{\min{(j,k)}}\binom{\ell-j}{k-m}\binom{j}{m}(-1)^{m}\,.

(12)

Additionally,

Where clear from context, we drop the label

2.3
Noise Channels and Twirling

A quantum noise channel is described by a completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) map, admitting a Kraus representation

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(X)=\sum_{j}K^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{j}XK_{j}^{\dagger},

(17)

where
\{K_{j}\}
are the Kraus operators satisfying

\displaystyle\sum_{j}K_{j}^{\dagger}K^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{j}=I\mathrm{.}

(18)

For a unitary ensemble
\mathcal{S}
(either discrete or continuous), we define the
\mathcal{S}
-twirling of
\mathcal{E}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{S}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}\in\mathcal{S}}\bigl[\mathcal{U}\circ\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}\bigr]

(19)

where we employ the unitary channel notation from
section

2.1
, and
\mathbb{E}_{x}[f(x)]
denotes the expectation value over
x
of
f(x)
.
The main examples we consider are Pauli and FLO twirling, where the expectation is given respectively by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\mathds{P}(n)}(X)

\displaystyle=4^{-n}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in{\sf{P}}(n)}\sum_{j}\left(\sigma_{\mathbf{x}}K_{j}\sigma_{\mathbf{x}}\right)X\left(\sigma_{\mathbf{x}}K_{j}\sigma_{\mathbf{x}}\right)^{\dagger}\,,

(20)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\mathds{F}(2n)}(X)

\displaystyle=\int_{U\in\mathds{F}(2n)}dU\ \sum_{j}\mathcal{U}\left(K_{j}\right)X\ \mathcal{U}\left(K_{j}\right)^{\dagger}

(21)

where
eq.

21
is a Haar average over the group of FLO unitaries.
Note also that twirling is idempotent.
That is, twirling twice is the same as twirling once.
Additionally,
\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{S}}
commutes with every element of
\mathcal{S}
when
\mathcal{S}
is a group, by construction.

A channel that is invariant under Pauli twirling is called a Pauli channel.
It is easy to show that Pauli twirling a general channel results in a Pauli channel
[
20
, Lemma 5.2.4]
.
Pauli channels can be parameterized as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\mathds{P}(n)}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in{\sf{P}}(n)}p_{\mathbf{x}}\sigma_{\mathbf{x}}X\sigma_{\mathbf{x}}

(22)

where the
\{p_{\mathbf{x}}\}_{\mathbf{x}\in{\sf{P}}(n)}
form a probability distribution over bit-strings of length
2n
.
These are called the
Pauli error probabilities
.
Starting from a general channel with Kraus operators
K_{j}\equiv\sum_{j}c_{j\mathbf{x}}\sigma_{\mathbf{x}}
as in
eq.

17
, the relationship to the Pauli error probabilities of the twirled channel in
eq.

22
is
[
20
, Lemma 5.2.4]

\displaystyle p_{\mathbf{x}}=\sum_{j}|c_{j\mathbf{x}}|^{2}\,.

(23)

Similarly, a
FLO-twirled channel
is one that is invariant under FLO twirling.
Since all Pauli operators are matchgates, any FLO-twirled channel is also invariant under Pauli twirling, and is therefore a Pauli channel.
Note the following contrast between Pauli twirling and FLO twirling: Pauli twirling a unitary Pauli (i.e. rank-1) channel leaves that channel invariant, but it is
not
the case that FLO unitary channels are invariant under FLO twirling since they generally become non-unitary after twirling.
FLO-twirled channels on
2n
modes can be parameterized as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\mathds{F}(2n)}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{2n}\binom{2n}{k}^{-1}q_{k}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}|k,2n}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}X\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\dagger}\,,

(24)

where
q_{k}
form a probability distribution over
\{0,\ldots,2n\}
.
We call the
q_{k}
the
fermionic error probabilities
.

of

are given by

\displaystyle q_{k}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}|k}p_{\mathbf{x}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}\,.

See

.
∎

We remark that the FLO twirl of a
general
channel has fermionic error probabilities that are related to the Kraus operators by concatenating the expressions in

and

We model a noisy implementation
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}

\mathcal{V}

.

For a noisy implementation

\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}\coloneqq\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}\widetilde{\mathcal{V}},

Prepending a random FLO unitary
\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}

The following statement of this is a standard result.

For
\mathcal{S}\in\{\mathds{P}(n),\mathds{F}(2n)\}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}^{\mathcal{S}}\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{S}}d\mathcal{U}\left(\mathcal{V}\mathcal{U}\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}\right)\left(\mathcal{V}\mathcal{E}\right)\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}=\mathcal{V}\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{S}}\,.

(27)

Proof.

Immediate from the definition of twirling.
∎

In practice, twirling is done by taking a sample average over the ensemble of unitary circuits appearing in the twirl

In the Pauli-twirl case, the overhead that this induces is negligible in the sense that the depth-one Pauli unitary circuits only slightly increase the depth overall, or can often be compiled into the underlying circuit

However, the best-known implementations of a generic FLO circuit require

It would be interesting to investigate reducing this depth penalty by sampling generators of the FLO group and analyzing the mixing time of the resulting random walk on

2.4
Channel Eigenvalues

If
\mathcal{E}^{\mathds{P}(n)}
is a Pauli channel, then
\mathcal{E}^{\mathds{P}(n)}
satisfies an eigenvector relation with respect to the Pauli operators

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\mathds{P}(n)}(\sigma_{\mathbf{x}})=\lambda_{\mathbf{x}}\sigma_{\mathbf{x}}\,.

(28)

The
\lambda_{\mathbf{x}}
are called the

.
They are related to the Pauli error probabilities by

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathbf{x}}=\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in{\sf{P}}(n)}(-1)^{\left\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\right\rangle}p_{\mathbf{y}}

(29)

where
\left\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\right\rangle

\mathbf{x}
,
\mathbf{y}\in\{0,1\}^{\times 2n}

Up to a column permutation, this is the Walsh-Hadamard transformation.

Now let
\mathcal{E}^{\mathds{F}(2n)}
be a FLO-twirled channel.
Then there is a similar eigenvalue relationship where the eigenvectors are Majorana monomials.
From the parameterization in

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\mathds{F}(2n)}(\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{2n}\binom{2n}{k}^{-1}q_{k}\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}|k}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\dagger}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\right)

(30)

\displaystyle=\left\{\sum_{k=0}^{2n}\left[\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}|k}(-1)^{|\boldsymbol{\beta}|k+|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cap\boldsymbol{\beta}|}\right]\binom{2n}{k}^{-1}q_{k}\right\}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\,.

The coefficient in braces can be simplified using
lemma

4

FLO-twirled channel eigenvalue

\displaystyle\xi_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{2n}(-1)^{jk}e_{k}(-\mathbf{1}_{j}\oplus\mathbf{1}_{2n-j})\binom{2n}{k}^{-1}q_{k}

\displaystyle=\left[(\mathbf{s}\cdot\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{d}^{-1})\cdot\mathbf{q}\right]_{j}\mathrm{.}

(32)

This constitutes a proof of the following lemma, for which we adopt a vector notation.

Lemma 10
.

(FLO-twirled Channel Eigenvalues)

We have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}=\left(\mathbf{s}\cdot\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{d}^{-1}\right)\cdot\mathbf{q}\,.

(33)

Proof.

Immediate.
∎

As
2^{-\ell/2}\left[\mathbf{s}^{(\ell)}\cdot\mathbf{M}^{(\ell)}\right]
is an involution, we can invert this relation and express the probabilities
\mathbf{q}
from
eq.

33
in terms of the eigenvalues
\boldsymbol{\xi}
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}=4^{-n}\left(\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{s}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}\,.

(34)

Owing to this linear relation, we can learn either
\mathbf{q}
or
\boldsymbol{\xi}
to completely characterize a FLO-twirled channel, but one must be careful about the precision in these different cases due to the relative normalization.

A FLO-twirled channel commutes with every element of the group
\mathds{F}(2n)
, so we can extend the notion of a channel eigenvalue to circuits as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{\mathds{F}(2n)}\coloneqq\prod_{g}\left(\mathcal{U}_{g}\mathcal{E}_{g}^{\mathds{F}(2n)}\right)=\left(\prod_{g}\mathcal{U}_{g}\right)\left(\prod_{g}\mathcal{E}_{g}^{\mathds{F}(2n)}\right).

(35)

This gives

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{\mathds{F}(2n)}(\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\left(\prod_{g}\xi_{g,k}\right)\mathcal{U}(\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\mbox{\hskip 14.22636pt}(k\coloneqq|\boldsymbol{\beta}|)

(36)

where
\xi_{g,k}
is the degree-
k

gate eigenvalue
for the circuit factor
g
.
We define the product in parentheses as the degree-
k
circuit eigenvalue for
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{\mathds{F}(2n)}
as

\displaystyle\Lambda_{k}\coloneqq\prod_{g}\xi_{g,k}

(37)

3
Measurement Protocol

Efficiently estimating noise in a quantum system requires that we fit to models with certain simplifying assumptions.
Following from
definition

8
, we assume the parameters describing the noise channel
\mathcal{E}
are independent of time and, furthermore, depend only on the gate we apply.
This is similar to the time-stationary and Markovian assumptions of
[
23
, def. 2]
, but unlike ref.
[
23
]
, and much related literature, we allow wide latitude for gate dependence.
In that regard our protocol can be viewed as a kind of “fermionic gate-set tomography” in the FLO-twirled subspace
[
6
,
24
,
7
,
8
]
.

We broadly refer to the map that takes ideal physical operations—state preparations, unitary gate operations, and measurements—to their parameterized noisy versions as a
model
(and we view the FACES model given in
definition

1
as a special case of this).
Denote the set of idealized physical operations by
\mathcal{S}
and the set of noisy operations by
\mathcal{N}
, then the model is a bijective map
\phi:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{N}
.
We only consider circuits composed of gates for which the model is defined.
This is the
realizable setting
.
If the “true” noise belongs to the realizable setting, then we provide convergence/correctness guarantees that we can learn that model.
Otherwise, one of our assumptions is violated.
We next present some refining assumptions about our models in the following subsection.

3.1
Model assumptions

Denote a noisy quantum circuit by
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}=\prod_{g=1}^{L}\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{g}
, where each noisy gate factor is given by
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{g}\coloneqq\mathcal{U}_{g}\mathcal{E}_{g}
as in
definition

8
, extended to a circuit of length
L
as in
eq.

35
.
The ideal part
\mathcal{U}_{g}
of each noisy gate is known from the physical operation we applied, but the accompanying noise channel
\mathcal{E}_{g}
contains free parameters to be learned.
In principle, this definition allows for the case where we have multiple ways of physically implementing the same idealized circuit, resulting in multiple circuits with the same ideal part but possibly distinct accompanying noise channels.
As stated above, we will restrict to the setting where the applied noise channel is unique to the corresponding idealized gate (that is, our model
\phi
is bijective), but it is straightforward to relax this assumption.
In addition, the noisy part of each gate may also depend on the idealized gate itself.
In principle, the twirling gates
\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}
and
\mathcal{V}\mathcal{U}\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}
in
eq.

27
are noisy in the FLO-twirling case as well, but we assume that this noise is only weakly correlated with
\mathcal{V}
, since we can implement them without necessarily implementing
\mathcal{V}
due to the FLO group structure.
Our expectation is that, in practice, this noise will introduce some additional uncertainty on our gate-eigenvalue estimates.
Under these assumptions, the FACES model given in
definition

1
is a special case of the general model described in this section where each individual FLO unitary gate is associated to its FLO-twirled noisy version through its collection of gate eigenvalues.
In summary, we assume that any noise model we wish to learn is Markovian in at least two senses: first, that it can be described by a completely positive trace-preserving map, but also that the noise specific to a given gate depends only on the idealized gate itself.

We require that our state preparations and measurements (SPAM) are not too noisy so that we can obtain useful information from experiments.
Specifically, we define
\widetilde{\rho}_{\mathbf{x}}
to be the density matrix describing a noisy preparation of the computational basis state
\left|\mathbf{x}\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle\mathbf{x}\right|
.
Similarly, let
\widetilde{E}_{\mathbf{x}}
correspond to the noisy POVM element associated to measuring the computational basis state
\left|\mathbf{x}\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle\mathbf{x}\right|
.
Our first assumption is that SPAM errors are sufficiently weak, as quantified by the following inequality,

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbf{0}\right|\widetilde{\rho}_{\mathbf{0}}\left|\mathbf{0}\right\rangle\left\langle\mathbf{x}\right|\widetilde{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left|\mathbf{x}\right\rangle\geq 1-c

(38)

for some sufficiently small constant
c
, say
c\leq\frac{1}{3}
.
The noise channels
\mathcal{E}
we consider should be also such that their FLO-twirled versions are not too noisy, otherwise they will be hard to learn.
We quantify this mathematically as

\displaystyle||\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{E}_{j}^{\mathds{F}(2n)}||_{\infty}\leq c^{\prime}

(39)

for all noise channels
\mathcal{E}_{j}
that we are trying to learn.
Here again,
c^{\prime}
is a sufficiently small constant.
Since our channels are always diagonal in the Pauli basis, this is equivalent to requiring that any noisy-channel eigenvalue lies in the interval
[1-c^{\prime},1]
(in the proof of
theorem

13
, we require that
c^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}
).
In practice, we routinely expect that these numbers
c,c^{\prime}
are less than
0.05
or even
0.01
in some quantum architectures, so these are not strong assumptions.

3.2
FLO-Specific Considerations

A fundamental challenge attendant to the setting of noisy FLO circuits arises due to the fact that the group
\mathds{F}(2n)
is continuous.
Since we can only ever estimate finitely many noise parameters, we thus assume that the noise can nonetheless be described by a finite set of channels.
For completeness, we choose a particular gate set given by
\mathcal{G}\coloneqq\{e^{i\theta Z_{j}}\}_{j,\theta}\cup\{G_{j}(H,H)\}_{j}
in the qubit picture, where
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
is allowed to be an arbitrary continuous parameter for each
j
.
The matrix
H
is the single-qubit Hadamard operator, and
G_{j}(A,B)
is the 2-qubit matchgate as defined in
section

2.1
.
While the 2-qubit gates
\{G_{j}(H,H)\}_{j}
form a discrete set, the single qubit rotations
\{e^{i\theta Z_{j}}\}_{j,\theta}
do not.
We partition the interval
[0,2\pi)
into
N
bins of the form
B_{k}\coloneqq[\frac{2\pi(k-1)}{N},\frac{2\pi k}{N})
for
k\in[N]
, and we assume that the single-qubit rotation
e^{i\theta Z_{j}}
is subject to a fixed noise channel
\mathcal{E}_{1,jk}
if
\theta\in B_{k}
.
Similarly, we assume that each of the
\{G_{j}(H,H)\}_{j}
is subject to a fixed noise channel
\mathcal{E}_{2,j}

An interesting related question is whether we can use these methods to learn a finite set of
noise generators
for a continuous family of noise operations.
We leave this question for future work, and we return to it in the Discussion.

Finally, we review some practical details related to twirling over the FLO group.
Importantly, FLO twirling cannot generally be made to be constant-depth.
One way to see this is to note that, in the qubit picture, matchgate twirling averages together the error probabilities of local Paulis with those of arbitrarily high weight (e.g.
X_{1}
and
Z^{\otimes(n-1)}Y_{n}
).
Thus, any FLO unitary ensemble we use to apply the twirling must involve at least one deep circuit if we require our gates to have constant support.
We again return to these points in the Discussion.

3.3
Protocol Details

Our measurement protocol for obtaining FLO-averaged circuit eigenvalues closely follows the ACES protocol of ref.
[
9
]
, and it is detailed graphically in
fig.

1
.
To learn all the eigenvalues of a FLO-twirled noise channel, we present two complementary protocols, which differ slightly in the required input state, measurement basis, and net FLO circuit applied.
An important unitary conjugation channel in our analysis is
\mathcal{U}_{+}(X)=U_{+}XU_{+}^{\dagger}
, related to the unitary
U(R_{+})
defined in
[
18
]
, and satisfying

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{+}(\left|\mathbf{+}\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle\mathbf{+}\right|^{\otimes n})=\left(\frac{I+i\gamma_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\left|\mathbf{0}\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle\mathbf{0}\right|\left(\frac{I-i\gamma_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\mathrm{.}

(40)

We have the following decomposition in terms of the gate set from the previous section

\displaystyle U_{+}=e^{-i\frac{\pi}{4}Z_{1}}\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\left[e^{-i\frac{\pi}{4}Z_{j+1}}G_{j}(H,H)e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}Z_{j}}G_{j}(H,H)e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}Z_{j+1}}\right]

(41)

up to an overall phase, where the product is taken in ascending order in

from left to right.

In general, we label an ensemble of noisy FLO circuits as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{r}=\left\{\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{(j)}\coloneqq\prod_{m=1}^{L(j)}\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{m}^{(j)}\right\}_{j}

(42)

where
r\in\{``x",``z"\}
, and the net ideal unitary varies with

as follows:
for all
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{(j)}\in\mathcal{C}_{z}
, we have
\mathcal{U}^{(j)}=\mathcal{I}
(the identity channel), and for all
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{(j)}\in\mathcal{C}_{x}
we have
\mathcal{U}^{(j)}=\mathcal{U}_{+}
.
Recall that a tilde is used to indicate the noisy version of a gate or circuit.
To avoid overloading the superscript notation, we denote
\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\mathds{F}(2n)}
as the ensemble whose elements correspond to the FLO-twirled elements of
\mathcal{C}_{r}
, and we drop the explicit superscript on the elements of
\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\mathds{F}(2n)}
themselves, with the understanding that they are twirled.

We can organize our circuits into two cases depending on whether the first qubit is prepared in the
x
or
z
basis; we call these “type-
x
” and “type-
z
”, respectively.
The readout data, and thus the expected signal, takes two different forms in these two cases.
We thus organize our empirical estimates of the outcome probabilities according to the circuit (or equivalently, readout) type.
Readout data of type-
z
is the Hamming weight of an
n
-bit string, whereas readout data of type-
x
consists of a sign (
\pm
) together with the Hamming weight of an
(n-1)
-bit string.
When we collect these data into empirical probability estimates over the relevant Hamming weights, we denote these empirical estimates by
\mathbf{P}
regardless of the
x
/
z
type.
Nevertheless, we can distinguish the type by adding an index
s
that takes values in
\{+,-,0\}
where
0
indicates
z
-type data, and
\pm
corresponds to the sign label of
x
-type data.
For example, the estimated probability of weight-
j
in a
+
-outcome
x
-type experiment would be labeled
\mathrm{P}_{+,j}
, and so on.

We define the
z
-type Born-rule probability distribution sampled in experiment
j
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}^{(j)}_{0,\ell}\coloneqq\sum_{\mathbf{w}|\ell,n}\left\langle\mathbf{w}\right|\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{(j)}(\left|\mathbf{0}\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle\mathbf{0}\right|)\left|\mathbf{w}\right\rangle

(43)

for
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{(j)}\in\mathcal{C}_{z}^{\mathds{F}(2n)}
.
Similarly, define the
x
-type Born-rule probability distribution in experiment
j
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}_{\pm,\ell}^{(j)}\coloneqq\sum_{\mathbf{w}|\ell,n-1}\left\langle\pm_{y}\mathbf{w}\right|\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{(j)}(\left|\mathbf{+}\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle\mathbf{+}\right|^{\otimes n})\left|\pm_{y},\mathbf{w}\right\rangle\mathrm{.}

(44)

Here, the first qubit is measured in the
y
-basis, and the outcome of this measurement corresponds to the
\pm
index on this probability.
Let
\mathbf{P}^{(j)}_{s}
be the vector with components
\mathrm{P}^{(j)}_{s,\ell}
for
s\in\{+,-,0\}

The Born-rule probability distributions are related to the circuit eigenvalues of a FLO-twirled quantum circuit.
Let
\Lambda^{(j)}_{k}
denote the degree-

in either ensemble
\mathcal{C}_{x}^{\mathds{F}(2n)}
or
\mathcal{C}_{z}^{\mathds{F}(2n)}
, for

.
We collect these eigenvalues into vectors as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{even}}^{(j)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(\Lambda_{2k}^{(j)}\right)_{k=0}^{n}

(45)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{even}\setminus\{2n\}}^{(j)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(\Lambda_{2k}^{(j)}\right)_{k=0}^{n-1}

(46)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{odd}}^{(j)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(\Lambda_{2k+1}^{(j)}\right)_{k=0}^{n-1},

(47)

and we have

Lemma 11
.

For an ensemble of noisy and FLO-twirled FLO circuits

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}^{(j)}_{0}=2^{-n}\mathbf{d}^{(n)}\cdot\mathbf{M}^{(n)}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{even}}^{(j)}

(48)

for
r=``z"
.
Similarly,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{P}^{(j)}_{+}+\mathbf{P}^{(j)}_{-})

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\mathbf{d}^{(n-1)}\cdot\mathbf{M}^{(n-1)}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{even}\setminus\{2n\}}^{(j)}

(49)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{P}^{(j)}_{+}-\mathbf{P}^{(j)}_{-})

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\mathbf{d}^{(n-1)}\cdot\mathbf{M}^{(n-1)}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{odd}}^{(j)}

(50)

for
r=``x"
.

We collect these transformations into a global one over all experiments as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\mathbf{V}\mathbf{P}

(51)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{V}_{z}\oplus\mathbf{V}_{x}

(52)

and

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathbf{V}_{z}=\bigoplus_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{z}}\left[\mathbf{M}^{(n)}\cdot(\mathbf{d}^{(n)})^{-1}\right]&\\
\mathbf{V}_{x}=\bigoplus_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{x}}\left\{\left[\mathbf{M}^{(n-1)}\cdot(\mathbf{d}^{(n-1)})^{-1}\right]\oplus\left[\mathbf{M}^{(n-1)}\cdot(\mathbf{d}^{(n-1)})^{-1}\right]\right\}\cdot(\sqrt{2}\mathbf{H}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{n})&\end{cases}

(53)

with
\mathbf{H}
is the
2\times 2

Using these transformations, we reconstruct the global set of circuit eigenvalues and subsequently reconstruct the the gate eigenvalues.
Our protocol proceeds as follows: given a set of elementary gates
\mathcal{G}
we would like to characterize, we implement an ensemble of these circuits composed of the corresponding FLO-twirled gates.
Each circuit in our ensemble has eigenvalue given by the design matrix

\displaystyle\Lambda_{k}^{(j)}=\prod_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\xi_{g,k}^{A_{j,g}}

(54)

where the design matrix element

corresponds to the number of occurences of gate

Physically, we sample the Born rule probabilities defined in
eq.

43

44

\hat{\mathbf{P}}^{(j)}
of these distributions for each circuit

, and we apply the transformations in

\hat{\mathbf{\Lambda}}^{(j)}
for the associated circuit eigenvalues.
Finally, we fit our gate eigenvalue estimates to our circuit eigenvalue data using a

.
That is, our estimator for each Majorana degree
k
is the solution to a system of equations of the form
\mathbf{Ax}=\mathbf{b}
, where
\mathbf{A}
is the design matrix,
x_{g,k}=-\mathrm{log}(\xi_{g,k})
, and

. More formally, we give the following definition

Definition 12

(Estimator)
.

For a FACES model
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{G},\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathcal{C})
, from which we obtain the design matrix
\mathbf{A}
, the FACES estimator for
\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
is taken as

\hat{\xi}_{g,k}=\exp(-\hat{x}_{g,k})

k
, with

\hat{x}_{g,k}=\begin{cases}\left(\mathbf{A}^{+}\hat{\mathbf{b}}\right)_{g,k}&\left(\mathbf{A}^{+}\hat{\mathbf{b}}\right)_{g,k}\geq 0\\
0&\mathrm{otherwise}\end{cases}

(55)

That is, we take the estimator given by ordinary least-squares, projected onto the positive orthant for each Majorana degree

.
One could of course consider other estimators, such as
\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}
taken to be the element of the positive orthant that minimizes
\lVert\mathbf{A}\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}-\hat{\mathbf{b}}\rVert_{2}
, or even an estimator that maximizes a likelihood function subject to a positivity constraint.
These are convex programs and would presumably be efficient to consider as practical estimators.
However, our choice of estimator is convenient from the standpoint of proving sample-complexity guarantees.
We give a pseudocode description of the relevant subroutines in
appendix

3.4
Sample Complexity

In this section, we analyze the sample complexity required to bound the error on the estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
in
eq.

55
.
We provide the following theorem:

Theorem 13
.

Suppose we have both
\Lambda_{k}^{(j)}\geq\frac{1}{2}
and
\hat{\Lambda}_{k}^{(j)}\geq\frac{1}{4}
for all degree sectors
k
and experiments
j
, then the FACES estimator satisfies

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}-\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert_{\infty}\leq 4\lVert\mathbf{A}^{+}\rVert_{\infty}\varepsilon

(56)

We thus require
S=O\left(\frac{n\lVert\mathbf{A}^{+}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\log(J/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)
samples per experiment to guarantee error
\epsilon
with probability
1-\delta
.

We prove this in two parts.
We first prove a lemma that bounds the sensitivity of the gate-noise eigenvalue estimates in terms of the sample estimates of the Born rule probabilities of each experiment.
The main theorem on sample complexity then follows from well-known results about estimating probability distributions from samples in the 1-norm.

Lemma 14
.

Suppose we have both
\Lambda_{k}^{(j)}\geq\frac{1}{2}
and
\hat{\Lambda}_{k}^{(j)}\geq\frac{1}{4}
for all degree sectors
k
and experiments
j
, then for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1/4]
we have

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}-\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert_{\infty}\leq 4\lVert\mathbf{A}^{+}\rVert_{\infty}\varepsilon

(57)

where

\displaystyle\mathrm{max}_{j}\lVert\delta\mathbf{P}^{(j)}\rVert_{1}\leq\varepsilon

(58)

is a uniform upper bound on the 1-norm error in the estimate of the Born rule probabilities.

Here the matrix norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
is defined in terms of the vector
\infty
-norm as

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{A}\|_{\infty}=\max_{x\neq 0}\frac{\|\mathbf{Ax}\|_{\infty}}{\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\infty}}

(59)

and can be computed as the maximum value of the vector
1
-norm of each row.

Our proof relies on several applications of the mean value theorem, which we recall is stated as:

Lemma 15

(Mean-Value Theorem)
.

Let
f:[a,b]\rightarrow\mathds{R}
be a continuous function on the closed interval
[a,b]
and differentiable on the open interval
(a,b)
for
a<b
.
There exists some
c
in
(a,b)
such that

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}\,.

(60)

Proof.

(
lemma

14
)
The logarithms of the circuit and gate eigenvalues satisfy a linear relationship

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}

(61)

where
x_{i}=-\mathrm{log}(\xi_{i})
, and
b_{i}=-\mathrm{log}(\Lambda_{i})
, and we have bundled the compound indices into single indices for simplicity.
Letting
\delta x_{i}\equiv\hat{x}_{i}-x_{i}
, define the function

\displaystyle u_{i}(t)=\mathrm{exp}\left(x_{i}+t\delta x_{i}\right)

(62)

for each
i
, such that
u_{i}(0)=\xi_{i}
and
u_{i}(1)=\hat{\xi}_{i}
.
Applying
lemma

15

\displaystyle\hat{\xi}_{i}-\xi_{i}

\displaystyle=u_{i}(1)-u_{i}(0)

(63)

\displaystyle=u^{\prime}_{i}(c_{i})

(64)

\displaystyle\hat{\xi}_{i}-\xi_{i}

\displaystyle=\delta x_{i}\mathrm{exp}\left(x_{i}+c_{i}\delta x_{i}\right)

(65)

for some

The exponential function is monotonic, so we are guaranteed that the absolute value of the error is less than or equal to the value of the right-hand side for

(if
\delta x_{i}<0
) or
c_{i}=1
(if
\delta x_{i}>0

Using the definition of the gate eigenvalue and the estimate thereof gives

\displaystyle|\hat{\xi}_{i}-\xi_{i}|

\displaystyle\leq|\delta x_{i}|\mathrm{max}(\hat{\xi}_{i},\xi_{i})\leq|\delta x_{i}|

(66)

where the second inequality follows since we are assuming the true gate eigenvalue and its estimate are upper-bounded by 1.
This gives the inequality in terms of vector norms

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}-\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\lVert\delta\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\infty}.

(67)

Next, let
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\coloneqq\mathbf{A}^{+}\hat{\mathbf{b}}
be the ordinary least-squares estimator.
We have

\displaystyle|\delta x_{i}|=\begin{cases}|\tilde{x}_{i}-x_{i}|&\tilde{x}_{i}>0\\
|x_{i}|&\tilde{x}_{i}\leq 0\end{cases}

(68)

for all
i

If

5
Conclusion

We have shown how to learn the matchgate-averaged noise parameters on many matchgates simultaneously via FACES, our fermionic adaptation of the ACES protocol.
We envision that this will enable experiments to build a complete characterization of averaged noise parameters for universal gate sets, though several open problems remain.
Foremost is the technical challenge of performing matchgate twirling.
Unlike Pauli twirling, it seems unlikely that matchgate twirling can be achieved in constant depth.
However, there have recently been promising results to show that low-depth circuits can nevertheless approximate Haar random unitaries
[
25
,
26
]
.
If a corresponding result holds for Haar random matchgates, then this issue can be mitigated, if not entirely avoided.
Alternatively, we might consider only reproducing the Haar ensemble of FLO circuits up to the second moment, which is sufficient for exactly twirling the channel.
It is known that this can be achieved using only gates in the intersection of the matchgate and Clifford groups
[
27
,
28
]
.
Finally, we might employ restricted circuit structure to address this challenge in a similar strategy to interleaved randomized benchmarking (IRB)
[
29
]
, which has historically enjoyed a great deal of success in practice.
In a setting where the circuit structure is limited to the FLO analog of IRB, for example, we expect that FACES will exhibit a “repeated convolution structure” similar to that of matchgate benchmarking
[
18
]
, since we are considering Haar averages over the group of FLO unitaries.

Another open question involves the notion of a
gauge
freedom in the learned parameters.
Formally, such a freedom exists when the design matrix has a nontrivial kernel, whose elements correspond to unlearnable functions of gate eigenvalues.
Recent works
[
11
,
12
]
have shown that these functions correspond to the cut space of a particular graph in the Clifford setting.
It is thus germane to ask whether a corresponding characterization is possible in the FLO setting.

It was also shown in
[
12
]
that, in the Clifford setting, one can construct design matrices that enable learning all learnable Pauli eigenvalues to
relative
precision.
We expect similar results to hold in the setting of FACES, but we leave this open.

Ultimately, we would like to utilize these protocols to not only learn circuit-level noise, but to also mitigate it.
With this in mind, an important feature of our protocol is that the design matrix is necessarily block diagonal.
In the aforementioned question of gauge parameters, it is already clear that each unlearnable function thus depends only on gate eigenvalues from the same Majorana-degree sector.
Additionally, noisy expectation values of fixed Majorana-degree observables are necessarily linear functions of the ideal expectation values, even in the setting where these observables are not Majorana monomials (e.g. measuring the expected energy for a Hamiltonian of fixed Majorana degree).
This contrasts with the setting of Pauli observables under Clifford-circuit evolution, and is reminiscent of training methods for error mitigation
[
30
,
31
]
.
We leave a careful consideration of this problem and other applications of our results to quantum simulation for future work.

are nonvanishing.
This gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\mathds{F}(2n)}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\subseteq[2n]\\
\boldsymbol{\beta}||\boldsymbol{\alpha}|,2n\end{subarray}}p_{\mathbf{x}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}\left[\int_{O(2n)}d\mathbf{R}\ |\det\left(\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right)|^{2}\right]\left(\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\odot\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\dagger}\right)

(110)

To evaluate this integral, note that it is invariant upon making the change-of-variables
\mathbf{R}\rightarrow\mathbf{S}^{T}\mathbf{R}
, where
\mathbf{S}
is a permutation matrix which maps
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
to some subset
\boldsymbol{\eta}
with
|\boldsymbol{\eta}|=|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|
, as

\displaystyle\int_{O(2n)}d\mathbf{R}\ |\det\left(\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{O(2n)}d\mathbf{R}\ |\det\left[\left(\mathbf{S}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{R}\right)_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right]|^{2}

(111)

\displaystyle\int_{O(2n)}d\mathbf{R}\ |\det\left(\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{O(2n)}d\mathbf{R}\ |\det\left(\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right)|^{2}

(112)

In this step, we used the Cauchy-Binet formula and the fact that
\det\left[\left(\mathbf{S}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right]=\pm\delta_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\boldsymbol{\eta}}
.
Summing over all such
\boldsymbol{\eta}
gives

\displaystyle\int_{O(2n)}d\mathbf{R}\ |\det\left(\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\binom{2n}{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|}^{-1}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\eta}||\boldsymbol{\alpha}|}\int_{O(2n)}d\mathbf{R}\ |\det\left(\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right)|^{2}

(113)

\displaystyle=\binom{2n}{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|}^{-1}\int_{O(2n)}d\mathbf{R}\ \det\left[\left(\mathbf{R}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{R}\right)_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right]

(114)

\displaystyle\int_{O(2n)}d\mathbf{R}\ |\det\left(\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\binom{2n}{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|}^{-1}

(115)

From the first to the second equality, we used the fact that
\det\left(\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right)=\det\left(\mathbf{R}^{\mathrm{T}}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\boldsymbol{\eta}}\right)
in one of the factors and used the Cauchy-Binet formula again.
Finally, we evaluated the determinant to 1 and used the normalization of the Haar integral.
This gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\mathds{F}(2n)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\subseteq[2n]\\
\boldsymbol{\beta}||\boldsymbol{\alpha}|\end{subarray}}\binom{2n}{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|}^{-1}p_{\mathbf{x}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})}\left(\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\odot\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\dagger}\right)

(116)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\mathds{F}(2n)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{2n}\binom{2n}{k}^{-1}\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}|k}p_{\mathbf{x}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}\right)\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}|k}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\odot\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\dagger}\right)

(117)

Identifying
q_{k}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}|k}p_{\mathbf{x}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}
gives the second statement of the lemma.
∎

B.4
Proof of
lemma

Lemma
11

(Restatement)
.

For an ensemble of noisy and FLO-twirled FLO circuits
\mathcal{C}^{\mathds{F}(2n)}_{r}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}^{(j)}_{0}=2^{-n}\mathbf{d}^{(n)}\cdot\mathbf{M}^{(n)}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{even}}^{(j)}

(118)

for
r=``z"
.
Similarly,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{P}^{(j)}_{+}+\mathbf{P}^{(j)}_{-})

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\mathbf{d}^{(n-1)}\cdot\mathbf{M}^{(n-1)}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{even}\setminus\{2n\}}^{(j)}

(119)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{P}^{(j)}_{+}-\mathbf{P}^{(j)}_{-})

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\mathbf{d}^{(n-1)}\cdot\mathbf{M}^{(n-1)}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{odd}}^{(j)}

(120)

for
r=``x"
.

Proof.

We begin with the statement for
\mathcal{C}_{z}^{\mathds{F}(2n)}
.
We have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{(j)}(\left|\mathbf{0}\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle\mathbf{0}\right|)

\displaystyle=\prod_{m=1}^{L(j)}\left(\mathcal{E}_{m}^{(j)}\right)^{\mathds{F}(2n)}\mathcal{U}^{(j)}_{m}(\left|\mathbf{0}\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle\mathbf{0}\right|)

(121)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{(j)}(\left|\mathbf{0}\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle\mathbf{0}\right|)

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathds{Z}_{2}^{\times n}}\prod_{m=1}^{L(j)}\left(\mathcal{E}_{m}^{(j)}\right)^{\mathds{M}(n)}\mathcal{U}^{(j)}_{m}(Z^{\mathbf{x}})

(122)

where
Z^{\mathbf{x}}\coloneqq\prod_{j=1}^{n}Z_{j}^{x_{j}}
.
Under the Jordan-Wigner transform, we have

\displaystyle Z^{\mathbf{x}}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(-i\gamma_{2j-1}\gamma_{2j}\right)^{x_{j}}

(123)

Applying the argument leading to
eq.

37
gives

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{(j)}(\left|\mathbf{0}\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle\mathbf{0}\right|)

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathds{Z}_{2}^{\times n}}\prod_{m=1}^{L(j)}\xi_{m,2|\mathbf{x}|}^{(j)}\mathcal{U}^{(j)}_{m}(Z^{\mathbf{x}})

(124)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{(j)}(\rho_{0})

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathds{Z}_{2}^{\times n}}\Lambda_{2|\mathbf{x}|}^{(j)}\mathcal{U}^{(j)}(Z^{\mathbf{x}})\mathrm{.}

(125)

Circuits in
\mathcal{C}_{z}^{\mathds{F}(2n)}
are chosen to interact as the identity on net.
This gives

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{(j)}(\left|\mathbf{0}\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle\mathbf{0}\right|)

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathds{Z}_{2}^{\times n}}\Lambda_{2|\mathbf{x}|}^{(j)}Z^{\mathbf{x}}

(126)

and

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbf{w}\right|\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{(j)}(\left|\mathbf{0}\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle\mathbf{0}\right|)\left|\mathbf{w}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathds{Z}_{2}^{\times n}}\Lambda_{2|\mathbf{x}|}^{(j)}\left\langle\mathbf{w}\right|Z^{\mathbf{x}}\left|\mathbf{w}\right\rangle

(127)

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathds{Z}_{2}^{\times n}}\Lambda_{2|\mathbf{x}|}^{(j)}(-1)^{\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{w}}

(128)

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\Lambda_{2k}^{(j)}\sum_{\mathbf{x}|k}(-1)^{\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{w}}

(129)

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbf{w}\right|\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{(j)}(\left|\mathbf{0}\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle\mathbf{0}\right|)\left|\mathbf{w}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\Lambda_{2k}^{(j)}M_{|\mathbf{w}|k}

(130)

Summing over all
\mathbf{w}
with a fixed Hamming weight
\ell
gives

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{w}|\ell}\left\langle\mathbf{w}\right|\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{(j)}(\left|\mathbf{0}\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle\mathbf{0}\right|)\left|\mathbf{w}\right\rangle=2^{-n}\binom{n}{\ell}\sum_{k=0}^{n}M_{\ell k}\Lambda_{2k}^{(j)}

(132)

which is the desired result.

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}^{(j)}_{0}=2^{-n}\mathbf{d}^{(n)}\cdot\mathbf{M}^{(n)}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{even}}^{(j)}.

(133)

In the
\mathcal{C}_{x}^{\mathds{F}(2n)}
-case, instead of
eq.

122
, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{(j)}(\left|+\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle+\right|^{\otimes n})

\displaystyle=\prod_{m=1}^{L(j)}\left(\mathcal{E}_{m}^{(j)}\right)^{\mathds{F}(2n)}\mathcal{U}^{(j)}_{m}(\left|+\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle+\right|^{\otimes n})

(134)

As shown in
[
18
]
, there exists a FLO unitary
\mathcal{U}_{+}
effecting

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{+}(\left|+\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle+\right|^{\otimes n})=\left(\frac{I+i\gamma_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\left|\mathbf{0}\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle\mathbf{0}\right|\left(\frac{I-i\gamma_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)

(135)

This gives

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{(j)}(\left|+\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle+\right|^{\otimes n})

\displaystyle=\prod_{m=1}^{L(j)}\left(\mathcal{E}_{m}^{(j)}\right)^{\mathds{F}(2n)}\mathcal{U}^{(j)}_{m}\left\{\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{+}\left[\left(\frac{I+i\gamma_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\left|\mathbf{0}\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle\mathbf{0}\right|\left(\frac{I-i\gamma_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right]\right\}

(136)

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathds{Z}_{2}^{\times n}}\prod_{m=1}^{L(j)}\left(\mathcal{E}_{m}^{(j)}\right)^{\mathds{F}(2n)}\mathcal{U}^{(j)}_{m}\left\{\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{+}\left[\left(\frac{I+i\gamma_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)Z^{\mathbf{x}}\left(\frac{I-i\gamma_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right]\right\}

(137)

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathds{Z}_{2}^{\times n}}\prod_{m=1}^{L(j)}\left(\mathcal{E}_{m}^{(j)}\right)^{\mathds{F}(2n)}\mathcal{U}^{(j)}_{m}\left\{\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{+}\left[(i\gamma_{1})^{x_{1}}Z^{\mathbf{x}}\right]\right\}

(138)

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathds{Z}_{2}^{\times n}}\prod_{m=1}^{L(j)}\xi_{m,2|\mathbf{x}|-x_{1}}^{(j)}\mathcal{U}^{(j)}_{m}\left\{\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{+}\left[(i\gamma_{1})^{x_{1}}Z^{\mathbf{x}}\right]\right\}

(139)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{(j)}(\left|+\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle+\right|^{\otimes n})

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathds{Z}_{2}^{\times n}}\Lambda_{2|\mathbf{x}|-x_{1}}^{(j)}\prod_{m=1}^{L(j)}\mathcal{U}^{(j)}_{m}\left\{\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{+}\left[(i\gamma_{1})^{x_{1}}Z^{\mathbf{x}}\right]\right\}\mathrm{.}

(140)

Since these circuits act as
\mathcal{U}_{+}
on-net, this gives

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{(j)}(\left|+\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle+\right|^{\otimes n})

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathds{Z}_{2}^{\times n}}\Lambda_{2|\mathbf{x}|-x_{1}}^{(j)}(i\gamma_{1})^{x_{1}}Z^{\mathbf{x}}

(141)

Measuring in the
Y
-basis on the first qubit and the
Z
-basis on all other qubits, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle+_{y}\mathbf{y}\right|\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{(j)}(\left|+\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle+\right|^{\otimes n})\left|+_{y}\mathbf{y}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathds{Z}_{2}^{\times(n-1)}}\left(\Lambda^{(j)}_{2|\mathbf{x}|}+\Lambda^{(j)}_{2|\mathbf{x}|+1}\right)(-1)^{\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}}

(142)

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(\Lambda^{(j)}_{2k}+\Lambda^{(j)}_{2k+1}\right)\sum_{\mathbf{x}|k}(-1)^{\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}}

(143)

\displaystyle\left\langle+_{y}\mathbf{y}\right|\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{(j)}(\left|+\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle+\right|^{\otimes n})\left|+_{y}\mathbf{y}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(\Lambda^{(j)}_{2k}+\Lambda^{(j)}_{2k+1}\right)M^{(n-1)}_{|\mathbf{y}|k}\mathrm{.}

(144)

Summing again over all probabilities of a fixed Hamming weight gives

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{y}|\ell,n-1}\left\langle+_{y}\mathbf{y}\right|\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{(j)}(\left|+\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle+\right|^{\otimes n})\left|+_{y}\mathbf{y}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(\Lambda^{(j)}_{2k}+\Lambda^{(j)}_{2k+1}\right)\sum_{\mathbf{y}|\ell,n-1}M^{(n-1)}_{|\mathbf{y}|k}

(145)

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{y}|\ell,n-1}\left\langle+_{y}\mathbf{y}\right|\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{(j)}(\left|+\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle+\right|^{\otimes n})\left|+_{y}\mathbf{y}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\binom{n-1}{\ell}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(\Lambda^{(j)}_{2k}+\Lambda^{(j)}_{2k+1}\right)M^{(n-1)}_{\ell k}

(146)

The corresponding probability for the “
-
” outcome on the first bit is given by

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{y}|\ell,n-1}\left\langle-_{y}\mathbf{y}\right|\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{(j)}(\left|+\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle+\right|^{\otimes n})\left|-_{y}\mathbf{y}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\binom{n-1}{\ell}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(\Lambda^{(j)}_{2k}-\Lambda^{(j)}_{2k+1}\right)M^{(n-1)}_{\ell k}

(147)

and this gives the desired relations

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{P}^{(j)}_{+}+\mathrm{P}^{(j)}_{-})

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\mathbf{d}^{(n-1)}\cdot\mathbf{M}^{(n-1)}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{even}\setminus\{2n\}}^{(j)}

(148)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{P}^{(j)}_{+}-\mathrm{P}^{(j)}_{-})

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\mathbf{d}^{(n-1)}\cdot\mathbf{M}^{(n-1)}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{odd}}^{(j)}

(149)

By estimating these probabilities, we can collectively estimate all of the noise eigenvalues.
∎
