Clinical and radiological outcomes of balloon kyphoplasty: lateral decubitus versus prone positioning: a retrospective comparative study
Authors: Ali Murat Başak, Ali Aydilek, Ömer Levent Karadamar, Bariş Çakmak, Erkan Sabri Ertaş, Mustafa Aydin, Tolga Ege
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures are common in the elderly, leading to pain, disability, and reduced quality of life. Balloon kyphoplasty (BK) is a minimally invasive treatment for osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures, typically performed in the prone position. However, the prone position is not suitable for patients with severe comorbidities, such as morbid obesity. The lateral decubitus position has emerged as an alternative, although limited data exist on its short-term outcomes. This study aims to compare the short-term clinical, radiological, and intraoperative outcomes of unipedicular balloon kyphoplasty (BK) performed in the prone position versus the lateral decubitus position, particularly in patients with contraindications to prone positioning, including morbid obesity and associated medical comorbidities. In a retrospective study, 50 patients aged over 65 who underwent unipedicular BK under local anesthesia between 2021 and 2024 were divided into 2 groups: 25 in the prone position and 25 in the lateral decubitus position due to contraindications for prone positioning. Pain levels were assessed using the visual analog scale at 5 time points: preoperatively, postoperative day 1, month 1, month 3, and month 6. Fluoroscopy time, surgeon comfort, and technical difficulty were measured using the Surgical Comfort and Difficulty Index. Radiological outcomes, including vertebral height loss at 6 months, were also recorded. The results indicated that patients in the lateral decubitus position experienced significantly lower postoperative pain on day 1 (visual analog scale: 2.6 vs 3.4, P = .02) and had shorter fluoroscopy times (81.5 vs 95.2 seconds, P = .01). The lateral decubitus group also reported lower Surgical Comfort and Difficulty Index scores, indicating improved surgeon comfort (3.9 vs 5.6, P = .001). However, radiological vertebral height loss at 6 months was similar between the groups. There were no major complications, with 1 patient in each group experiencing asymptomatic cement extravasation. Unipedicular BK performed in the lateral decubitus position provides clinical and radiological outcomes comparable with the prone position. In selected patients with contraindications to prone positioning, the lateral decubitus approach may represent a safe and effective alternative.

1. Introduction

Osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCFs) represent a significant clinical concern, particularly in the elderly population, due to their association with substantial morbidity and mortality.[1] If not appropriately managed, these fractures may lead to progressive sagittal imbalance, resulting in height loss, decreased mobility, pulmonary and gastrointestinal complications, secondary fractures, depression, and a marked decline in quality of life.

Because of demographic shifts toward an aging population, the incidence of OVCFs is rising notably.[2] In cases where conservative treatment fails, minimally invasive surgical interventions have gained prominence. The primary objectives of these interventions are to reduce pain, prevent progression of deformity, and, when feasible, restore vertebral height. Vertebroplasty and balloon kyphoplasty (BK) are the most commonly employed minimally invasive techniques for this purpose.[3–8]

Vertebroplasty[9] involves percutaneous injection of low-viscosity bone cement into the collapsed vertebral body, while BK[10,11] allows partial restoration of vertebral height via inflation of a high-pressure balloon, followed by injection of high-viscosity cement into the created cavity, thereby reducing the risk of cement extravasation. Unipedicular access is increasingly favored in selected cases due to its reduced invasiveness, shorter operative times, and decreased radiation exposure, although bipedicular access is widely accepted.[12–18] The standard position for both procedures is the prone position, which enables optimal visualization of the vertebral column and facilitates safe percutaneous access.[19] The prone position, however, can be contraindicated in specific patient populations, including those with advanced heart failure, severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) or restrictive lung disease, morbid obesity (body mass index > 40 kg/m2), severe spinal instability, high-risk anesthesia profiles (American Society of Anesthesiologists [ASA] class IV–V), large intra-abdominal masses, pregnancy, or prior shoulder/hip arthroplasty or pseudarthrosis. In such cases, the prone position was contraindicated, and we used the lateral decubitus position as an option.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the safety, efficacy, and short-term clinical outcomes of BK performed in the lateral decubitus position in patients for whom prone positioning was considered relatively or absolutely considered clinically unsuitable, particularly in the presence of morbid obesity and associated medical comorbidities.

2. Materials and methods

This study was approved by the Gülhane Scientific Research Ethics Committee of the University of Health Sciences (Approval No: 2025-468, date of approval: October 21, 2025). This retrospective study included 50 patients who underwent unipedicular BK under local anesthesia at Gülhane Training and Research Hospital between 2021 and 2024. Patients were treated either in the prone or lateral decubitus position based on clinical appropriateness. The objective of the study was to assess and compare clinical and radiographic outcomes, as well as intraoperative surgeon-reported comfort and technical difficulty levels. Patients were categorized into 2 groups. Patients in the lateral decubitus group were selected because prone positioning was considered clinically unsuitable due to severe cardiopulmonary comorbidities and/or morbid obesity. These comorbidities included advanced heart failure, COPD, restrictive pulmonary disease, or high anesthetic risk. Because of the retrospective nature of the study, detailed comorbidity severity classifications such as ASA score, New York Heart Association functional class, and Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease stage were not consistently available for all patients. Group 1 included patients with OVCFs who underwent BK in the prone position. Group 2 consisted of patients in whom the prone position was considered contraindicated or clinically unsuitable because of morbid obesity and associated medical comorbidities, and who therefore underwent unipedicular BK in the lateral decubitus position.

2.1. Inclusion criteria

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they had a radiologically confirmed diagnosis of OVCF (by computed tomography and/or magnetic resonance imaging), persistent thoracic or lumbar spinal pain for more than 6 weeks despite conservative management, a T score below −3.5 on bone densitometry, and contraindications to prone positioning due to severe cardiopulmonary comorbidities and contraindications to prone positioning due to morbid obesity (body mass index ≥ 40 kg/m2) and associated medical comorbidities.

2.2. Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded if they had spinal fractures of neoplastic or infectious origin, a history of previous spinal surgery and/or BK, or presented with significant neurological deficits.

2.3. Surgical protocol

All procedures were performed under local anesthesia using standardized BK techniques under continuous fluoroscopic guidance. High-viscosity polymethylmethacrylate bone cement was used in all procedures. Balloon inflation and cement injection were performed according to standard surgical practice and manufacturer recommendations under real-time fluoroscopic monitoring. Patient positioning (prone or lateral decubitus; Figs. 1 and 2), access route (unipedicular or bipedicular), amount of cement used, and injection pressure were standardized based on the surgeon’s experience and anatomical considerations. In patients treated in the lateral decubitus position, the upper pedicle was used as the access point. All procedures were carried out by 2 spine surgeons with a minimum of 10 years of surgical experience.

2.4. Outcome measures

Pain levels were assessed using the visual analog scale (VAS) at 5 time points: preoperatively, postoperative day 1, month 1, month 3, and month 6. Additional parameters recorded included fluoroscopy time (in seconds), perioperative and early postoperative complications, and radiological vertebral height loss during follow-up. Radiological outcomes were assessed by comparing preoperative and postoperative vertebral body height.

2.5. Assessment of surgeon comfort and technical difficulty

To objectively evaluate the surgeon’s technical experience during the procedure, the Surgical Comfort and Difficulty Index (SCDI) was utilized (Table 1). The SCDI includes 8 parameters: difficulty in patient positioning, skin and pedicle access, cement injection control, procedure duration, patient cooperation, fluoroscopic image quality, and surgeon satisfaction post-procedure. Each parameter was scored from 0 (no difficulty) to 2 (significant difficulty), resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 16. Because the operating surgeons were directly involved in patient positioning and intraoperative assessment, blinding to group allocation was not feasible. Therefore, assessment bias related to surgeon-reported outcomes cannot be completely excluded.

2.6. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables between the 2 groups were analyzed using the independent-samples t test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were assessed using the chi-square test. A P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis was performed using SPSS software (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Armonk).

3. Results

A total of 50 patients with OVCFs were retrospectively evaluated. Twenty-five patients underwent BK in the prone position, while the remaining 25 were operated in the lateral decubitus position. All patients were over the age of 65, and there were no statistically significant differences between the groups in terms of demographic characteristics (age, gender, or bone mineral density values; P > .05).

Patients who underwent BK in the lateral decubitus position demonstrated significantly lower early postoperative pain scores compared with those in the prone group (mean postoperative day 1 VAS: 2.6 vs 3.4; P = .02). Although postoperative day 1 pain was significantly lower in the lateral decubitus group, VAS scores at 1, 3, and 6 months were comparable between groups (P > .05 for both). In addition, the lateral group had significantly shorter fluoroscopy times (mean 81.5 seconds vs 95.2 seconds; P = .01). The mean SCDI scores were also significantly lower in the lateral decubitus group (mean score: 3.9 vs 5.6; P = .001), indicating greater intraoperative technical comfort for the surgeon (Table 2). During the follow-upperiod, there were no statistically significant differences between the prone and lateral decubitus groups regarding vertebral height loss (P > .05). No major complications were observed in either group. Asymptomatic cement extravasation was noted in 1 patient from each group (complication rate: 4%, P = 1.00).

4. Discussion

With the increasing aging population worldwide, the incidence of OVCFs continues to rise.[1] The primary goals of both conservative and surgical treatments are to relieve pain, maintain sagittal balance, and minimize the risk of subsequent vertebral fractures. When conservative measures fail, vertebroplasty[9] and percutaneous BK[10,11] are among the most commonly employed minimally invasive surgical options.[4–9]

Although the standard surgical approach is bipedicular access in the prone position, several studies in the literature have demonstrated that unipedicular access can be as effective as the traditional bipedicular method.[12–15] Compared with bipedicular access, the unilateral approach offers advantages such as shorter operative time, reduced cement volume, lower radiation exposure, and comparable clinical outcomes.[12–18] The conventional surgical position for both procedures is accepted as the prone position, which enables optimal visualization of the vertebral column and facilitates safe percutaneous access.[19] However, not every patient is suitable for the prone position because of their comorbidities. We used the lateral decubitus position in our patients who were not suitable for the prone position. We wanted to determine the advantages and disadvantages of the lateral decubitus position in patients who cannot use the prone position and who have to undergo surgery in the lateral decubitus position. Therefore, we compared the patients who underwent kyphoplasty in the lateral decubitus position and in the prone position regarding technical difficulties such as patient positioning, skin and pedicle intervention, finding anatomical landmarks under scopy, controlling cement injection, operation time, patient cooperation, fluoroscopy imaging quality, overall procedure satisfaction of the surgeon, and complication rates. We revealed that the lateral decubitus position yields superior results in comparison with the prone position. When we evaluated why BK performed in the lateral decubitus position yielded superior results, we observed that there were some potential reasons. First, this position places less restriction on respiratory function, especially in morbidly obese patients, thereby helping maintain hemodynamic stability and improving patient cooperation during the procedure. Second, accessing the upper pedicle in the lateral position allows for a more favorable entry angle and more stable fluoroscopic imaging. These factors enhance the surgeon’s maneuverability, reduce technical difficulty, and contribute to shorter operative times. In our study, procedures performed in the lateral decubitus position were associated with significantly higher surgeon comfort scores, shorter fluoroscopy durations, and lower postoperative pain levels. These findings suggest that the lateral decubitus position may not only serve as an alternative in selected patients but may also offer clinical advantages, particularly in populations for whom the prone position is contraindicated. Nevertheless, the lateral decubitus position may not be suitable for all patients or fracture patterns. Technical difficulties may arise in cases involving multilevel vertebral fractures, severe spinal deformities, or patients with unilateral hip or shoulder pathologies that limit safe lateral positioning. Therefore, careful patient selection remains essential when considering the lateral decubitus approach for BK.

In the literature search, we found only 1 article in which the authors used the lateral decubitus position for kyphoplasty operation. Aguirre et al presented 3 patients who, due to significant comorbidities, safely and effectively underwent kyphoplasties in the lateral decubitus position.[20] Similarly, Cardoso et al also used a unilateral extrapedicular approach under local anesthesia/mild general sedation, while the patient was lying in the lateral decubitus position for treatment of vertebral metastases. The procedure was performed in the same fashion as a kyphoplasty, and the side was chosen according to the location of the metastasis.[21] In literature, the advantages of the lateral decubitus position in patients undergoing spine surgery were observed as reductions in the cardiac index/stroke volume and reduced left ventricular distention/venous return.[22,23] Sun et al documented the increased safety/efficacy of the lateral decubitus position for performing vertebroplasties in obese patients with COPD.[23] Several previous studies evaluating patient positioning during spine surgery have demonstrated that the lateral decubitus position may reduce cardiopulmonary stress compared with the prone position, particularly in obese or medically fragile patients. Reduced abdominal compression, improved venous return, and less restrictive pulmonary mechanics in the lateral decubitus position may contribute to better hemodynamic and respiratory tolerance during surgical procedures. These physiological advantages may partially explain the favorable intraoperative findings observed in the present study.

In our cases, in all 25 patients with major comorbidities, 25 kyphoplasties were performed in the lateral position without experiencing any intraoperative/postoperative major complications.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective non-randomized design may have introduced significant selection bias, as patients in the lateral decubitus group were specifically assigned to this position because prone positioning was considered contraindicated or clinically unsuitable. In addition, detailed classifications regarding comorbidity severity, including ASA score, New York Heart Association functional class, and Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease stage, were not consistently available because of the retrospective design, which may have limited adjustment for baseline clinical differences between groups. The relatively small sample size (25 patients per group) may have limited the statistical power of the study, particularly for detecting rare complications and evaluating long-term clinical and radiological outcomes. Because of the limited sample size, multivariable regression analysis was not performed to avoid statistical overfitting and unreliable model estimates. Consequently, the ability to control for potential confounding variables and establish causal relationships between patient positioning and clinical outcomes remains limited. Although baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable between groups, unrecognized confounding factors may still have influenced the results. Second, the relatively short follow-up period of 6 months limits the evaluation of important long-term clinical and radiological outcomes, including sustained vertebral height maintenance, adjacent-level vertebral fractures, late cement-related complications, long-term pain control, and functional recovery. Surgeon-reported outcomes such as the SCDI, while useful for assessing technical aspects of the procedure, may include subjective elements and currently lack external validation. In addition, because the operating surgeons were directly involved in patient positioning and intraoperative assessment, blinding to group allocation was not feasible. Therefore, assessment bias cannot be completely excluded. Future prospective, randomized studies with longer follow-up periods are warranted to validate these findings and to better define the long-term clinical and radiological benefits of the lateral decubitus position in BK.

5. Conclusions

Unipedicular BK performed in the lateral decubitus position provides clinical and radiological outcomes comparable with the traditional prone position. In carefully selected patients with contraindications to prone positioning, particularly those with morbid obesity and associated medical comorbidities, the lateral decubitus approach may represent a safe and effective alternative. However, based on the retrospective design and limited sample size of the present study, this approach should be considered an alternative option rather than a first-line standard approach for all patients undergoing BK. Larger prospective studies with longer follow-up periods are required to further validate these findings.
