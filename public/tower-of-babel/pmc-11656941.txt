Prevalence of depression among women with polycystic ovary syndrome in mainland China: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Zhenzhen Hong, Peiya Wu, Huihong Zhuang, Liying Chen, Shanshan Hong, Jiawei Qin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePolycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), a prevalent endocrine disorder among women, is frequently comorbid with depression. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to estimate the prevalence of depression among women with PCOS in mainland China.MethodA comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, EMBASE, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and Chinese databases (CNKI, WanFang, and VIP) up to August 2024. Random-effects models were applied to estimate the pooled prevalence of depression with 95% confidence intervals. The methodological quality assessment was assessed using the risk of bias tool. The primary outcome was the pooled prevalence of depression in women with PCOS.ResultsThirty-nine studies encompassing 9796 participants were analyzed. The pooled prevalence of depression was 37% (95% CI, 29%-44%), with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 96%, p < 0.001). In subgroup analysis, the prevalence of depression among women with PCOS in West, Central, East, and Northeast China was 37%, 37%, 38%, and 25%, respectively. The prevalence of depression among women with PCOS surveyed before and after 2015 was 40% and 35%, respectively. The prevalence of depression among women with PCOS aged < 26 and > / = 26 years were 42% and 33%, respectively. The prevalence of depression among women with PCOS that participants’ BMI < 24 and > / = 24 kg/m2 were 38% and 34%, respectively. The prevalence of depression among women with PCOS that used Self-rating Depression Scale (SDS), Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), and Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) were 40%, 34%, 31%, and 24%, respectively. Sensitivity analysis and meta-regression confirmed the stability of the findings. Evidence of publication bias was detected.ConclusionsThe high prevalence of depression among women with PCOS in China underscores the need for integrated screening and management. The results of this meta-analysis show high heterogeneity and indicate publication bias, which reduces the generalizability of the findings. Future research should address heterogeneity and enhance the applicability of results.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-024-06378-8.Keywords: Depression, Prevalence, Polycystic ovary syndrome, China, Systematic review

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a complex and heterogeneous endocrine disorder characterized by hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and polycystic ovarian morphology [1].It is the most common endocrine disorder affecting women of reproductive age, with a global prevalence estimated to range between 8 and 13% [2]. This trend shows an annual increase and a decline in the average age of onset, making it one of the most prevalent chronic diseases [3]. In China, the prevalence of PCOS has been reported to be 5.6%, similar to global estimates, but varying depending on the population studied [4]. PCOS is mainly characterized by persistent anovulation, hyperandrogenism, insulin resistance, and polycystic ovarian changes, which lead to long-term complications such as obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and endometrial cancer, posing serious long-term health risk to women [5].

Depression is a prevalent mental health disorder worldwide, with significant variability across regions [6]. The lifetime prevalence of depression in women is approximately 10% to 25%, higher than in men, likely due to hormonal, biological, and psychosocial factors [7]. In China, the prevalence of depression has been reported to range between 6.9% and 9.6% among the general population, with women being more affected than men [8]. Depression significantly impacts quality of life and is associated with increased morbidity and mortality.

A growing body of evidence suggests that, compared with women without PCOS, those with PCOS are more likely to have emotional disorders, manifesting as anxiety, depression, and other psychological issues [9–11]. The prevalence of depression among women with PCOS is estimated to be 36%, significantly higher than in women without PCOS [12]. Due to the combined effects of reproductive endocrine disorders, changes in self-image, and fear of infertility, the psychological burden on PCOS patients is significantly increased, and the chronic course of the disease may exacerbate endocrine abnormalities and lead to emotional disorders. In the U.S., the additional direct healthcare costs associated with depression in PCOS were estimated to be $1.678 billion per year in 2021 [13].

Although there have been three meta-analysis [14–16] describing the prevalence of depression in women with PCOS, these studies did not include relevant studies conducted in China. Given the high prevalence of both PCOS and depression and their significant impact on women's health, it is crucial to understand the burden of depression among women with PCOS, particularly in the context of China, where cultural, social, and healthcare factors may influence the presentation and management of these conditions. So far, only one meta-analysis included Chinese databases to explore the global prevalence of depression in women with PCOS [17]. However, this meta-analysis did not conduct more in-depth analysis, such as exploring variations in prevalence across different countries or regions, age groups, or the impact of BMI on prevalence [17]. In addition, many recent studies on depression in Chinese women with PCOS have been conducted, with findings published in both Chinese and international journals [18–26]. These studies will provide new evidence to make the conclusions more comprehensive.

Therefore, we conducted a meta-analysis to examine the prevalence of depression among women diagnosed with PCOS in mainland China. Additionally, we aimed to assess other factors potentially related to depressive symptoms, including study regions, survey periods, participant age, BMI, depression screening tools, and PCOS diagnostic criteria. This evidence synthesis will support the implementation of appropriate guidelines for routine screening and early intervention, potentially reducing the health and financial burden associated with depression among Chinese women with PCOS.

Method

This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines. Ethical approval and patient informed consent were not necessary, as the data extraction and analysis relied exclusively on published literature.

Search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted across both international and Chinese databases, including PubMed, EMBASE, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), WanFang, and Chinese VIP Information (VIP), from their inception dates until August 10, 2024. The search strategy employed a combination of medical subject headings (MeSH) and text words related to depression, depressive disorder, polycystic ovary syndrome, China, and Chinese populations. No language restrictions were applied, but the search was limited to human studies. For conference abstracts considered for inclusion, we searched the databases for corresponding full-text publications and contacted the principal investigator when full texts were unavailable. Additionally, the reference lists of eligible articles were reviewed at the full-text screening stage to identify any additional studies that met the inclusion criteria. The search strategy for all databases is detailed in Supplementary Table 1, and reference management was performed using EndNote X9 (Thomson Reuters).

Study inclusion and exclusion criteria

The criteria for study inclusion were as follows: (1) Condition: Studies involving women with a confirmed diagnosis of polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) according to validated criteria (e.g., Rotterdam criteria for diagnosing PCOS, and Patient Health Questionnaire-9 for depression); (2) Context: Studies that reported the prevalence of depression among women with PCOS or provided sufficient data to calculate the prevalence. The study designs considered were cross-sectional, retrospective, or prospective cohort studies, each including a minimum of 30 participants. Validated standard screening tools were used for diagnosing both PCOS and depression; (3) Population: Participants were women recruited from mainland China (defined as excluding Hong Kong, Macau, and Taiwan); (4) If articles used the same participant sample, only the most comprehensive article was included.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Condition: Studies that reported the prevalence of PCOS in patients primarily diagnosed with depression; (2) Context: Excluded study types included case reports, reviews, meta-analysis, letters, or study protocols. Studies where PCOS diagnosis was entirely self-reported by participants were also excluded; (3) Population: Studies in which depression was assessed solely in relation to specific PCOS symptoms (e.g., obesity, infertility, or hirsutism) were excluded, as were studies that included pregnant patients with PCOS.

Any discrepancies during the inclusion/exclusion process were resolved by consultation with a third reviewer (JQ).

Study selection, data abstraction, and quality appraisal

Duplicates were removed, and two blinded reviewers (HZZ and LC) conducted the initial screening of titles and abstracts, followed by a full-text review for potentially relevant studies. Data extraction was performed using a standardized form to collect details such as author name, publication year, publication language, study site, survey period, age, BMI, depression screening tool, PCOS diagnostic criteria, sample size of depression cases, sample size of PCOS cases, and the prevalence of depression among PCOS participants, where available. When necessary, consultation with a third reviewer (JQ) was sought. Missing data were obtained by contacting the principal investigators.

The methodological quality assessment tool [27], developed specifically for prevalence studies, was utilized by two reviewers. This checklist includes 10 items designed to evaluate both external and internal validity, including study selection bias, non-response bias, measurement bias, and analysis-related bias. Studies were rated as low risk of bias (score > 8), moderate risk (score 6–8), or high risk (score ≤ 5).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis were performed using STATA/SE version 15.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). The pooled estimate of depression prevalence among women with PCOS was calculated with a 95% confidence interval (CI). Heterogeneity was qualified using the I2 statistic and the χ2 test, where I2 > 75% and P < 0.10 considered significant. Sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the robustness of the pooled results and to identify potential sources of heterogeneity. Subgroup analysis was conducted based on study region, survey period, participant age, BMI, depression screening tool, PCOS diagnostic criteria, and study quality. Both subgroup analysis and meta-regression were performed to explore sources of heterogeneity. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test, with a P value < 0.05 suggesting the presence of publication bias.

Result

Study selection and study characteristics

A total of 917 unique citations were identified from six electronic databases. After removing duplicates, 625 records were screened based on their titles and abstracts. We reviewed the full texts of 138 articles, ultimately including 39 studies (comprising 9,796 participants) in the meta-analysis. The study selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Flow diagram for studies selection

Among the 39 included studies, 34 were published in Chinese [21–26, 28–55] and 5 in English [18–20, 56, 57]. The publication date ranged from 2008 to 2024, and the studies were conducted across 16 provinces, autonomous regions, or municipalities: Beijing, Chongqing, Guangdong, Hebei, Heilongjiang, Henan, Hunan, Jiangsu, Ningxia, Shandong, Shanghai, Shanxi, Sichuan, Tianjin, Xinjiang. Based on geographical divisions according to the National Bureau of Statistics of China (West, East, Central, and Northeast), 15 studies were conducted in the West China, 6 studies in Central China, 15 studies in East China, and 2 studies in Northeast China. The participants were recruited between 2007 and 2024, with a mean age ranging from 18 to 40 years. Studies reporting participants' BMI indicated a range of 21 to 27 kg/m2. Eight depression assessment tools were used: Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D), Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), Quick Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology Self-Report 16-item (QIDS-SR16), Symptom Checklist-90 (SCL-90), Hamilton Depression Scale 17-item (HDS-17), and Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS), with more than half of the included studies using the SDS. The PCOS diagnostic criteria were Rotterdam, People’s Republic of China Health Industry Standards (PRCHIS), or a combination of both. The pooled sample size of women with PCOS was 9796, with individual study sample sizes ranging from 81 to 1130. The characteristics of the included studies are presented in Table 1.

Pooled prevalence of depression among women with PCOS in mainland China

The pooled prevalence of depression among women with PCOS was 37% (95% CI, 29–44), accompanied by considerable heterogeneity (I2 = 96%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). Subgroup analysis were conducted to investigate potential sources of this heterogeneity.

Pooled prevalence of depression in women with PCOS

Regarding the geographical regions, the pooled prevalence was 37% (95% CI, 21–53) in West China, 37% (95% CI, 22–52) in Central China, 38% (95% CI, 32–45) in East China, 25% (95% CI, 5–45) in Northeast China, with significant heterogeneity observed within each subgroups (I2 = 97.8%, p < 0.001; I2 = 93.2%, p < 0.001; I2 = 75.5%, p < 0.001; I2 = 95.4%, p < 0.001, respectively) (Supplement Fig. 1).

Regarding the survey periods, the pooled prevalence was 35% (95% CI, 25–45) for studies conducted after 2015 and 40% (95% CI, 30–50) for those before 2015, with significant heterogeneity was observed within both subgroups (I2 = 97.1%, p < 0.001; I2 = 86.1%, p < 0.001, respectively) (Supplement Fig. 2).

Regarding the participants’ age, the pooled prevalence was 42% (95% CI, 27–57) for participants’ age < 26 years and 33% (95% CI, 22–44) for participants’ age > / = 26 years, with significant heterogeneity was present within both subgroups (I2 = 97.4%, p < 0.001; I2 = 93.7%, p < 0.001, respectively) (Supplement Fig. 3).

Regarding the participants’ BMI, the pooled prevalence was 38% (95% CI, 26–50) for participants’ BMI < 24 kg/m2 and 34% (95% CI, 15–53) for those with a BMI > / = 24 kg/m2, with significant heterogeneity was present in both subgroups (I2 = 94.5%, p < 0.001; I2 = 98.2%, p < 0.001, respectively) (Supplement Fig. 4).

Regarding the depression assessment tools, the pooled prevalence was 40% (95% CI, 28–52) for studies using SDS, 34% (95% CI, 11–57) for those using the PHQ-9, 31% (95% CI, 25–37) for the BDI, 24% (95% CI, 20–29) for the HADS. Significant heterogeneity was observed in the SDS and PHQ-9 subgroups (I2 = 96.9%, p < 0.001; I2 = 97.1%, p < 0.001, respectively), while estimates for the BDI and HADS showed no significant heterogeneity (I2 = 11.1%, p = 0.343; I2 = 0.0%, p = 0.798, respectively). Only one study each used QIDS-SR16, SCL-90, HDS-17, and CES-D, with the prevalence of 31%, 26%, 49%, and 49% respectively (Supplement Fig. 5).

Regarding the PCOS diagnostic criteria, the pooled prevalence was 34% (95% CI, 17–51) in the PRCHIS subgroup, 38% (95% CI, 29–48) in the Rotterdam subgroup, 33% (95% CI, 18–49) in the combined PRCHIS and Rotterdam subgroup. Significant heterogeneity existed in all subgroups (I2 = 95.5%, p < 0.001; I2 = 95.7%, p < 0.001; I2 = 94.2%, p < 0.001; respectively) (Supplement Fig. 6).

Regarding the methodological quality, the pooled prevalence was 35% (95% CI, 26–44) in the low risk of bias subgroup and 37% (95% CI, 28–46) in the moderate risk subgroup with significant heterogeneity existed in both subgroups (I2 = 65.8%, p = 0.02; I2 = 96.5%, p < 0.001,respectively). Only one study was rated as high risk of bias, with a prevalence of 36% (Supplement Fig. 7).

Sensitivity analysis and meta-regression

A sensitivity analysis was conducted by sequentially excluding each included study to assess its impact on the pooled results. The analysis indicated that no single study significantly influenced the pooled estimate, suggesting that the results were relatively stable (Supplement Fig. 8).

Meta-regression analysis was conducted to identify factors contributing to heterogeneity in the depression prevalence among women with PCOS, including geographical regions, survey periods, age, BMI, depression assessment tools, PCOS diagnostic criteria, and methodological quality. Univariate meta-regression indicated that these factors were unlikely to be sources of heterogeneity in the pooled results. (Supplement Table 2).

Publication bias and study quality assessments

Significant asymmetry was detected in the funnel plot (Supplement Fig. 9), and Egger’s test confirmed the presence of publication bias in our meta-analysis (p = 0.003). The risk of bias assessment revealed that most studies were classified as a low or moderate risk of bias, with only one study rated as a high risk. Detailed individual study assessments are provided in Supplement Table 3.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis was the first one to examine the prevalence of depression among women with PCOS in mainland China. The overall pooled prevalence was found to be 37% (95% CI, 29–44), with significant heterogeneity across studies (I2 = 96%, p < 0.001). Subgroup analysis based on geographical regions, survey periods, age, BMI, depression assessment tools, PCOS diagnostic criteria, and methodological quality did not identify clear sources of heterogeneity. Sensitivity analysis indicated that the pooled results were robust, while meta-regression confirmed that these variables were unlikely to account for the observed heterogeneity. However, the presence of publication bias was detected, which may have influenced the overall estimates.

The findings of this meta-analysis align with global data on the prevalence of depression among women with PCOS [12, 17]. A meta-analysis including 18 studies demonstrated that the prevalence of depression in women with PCOS was 36.6% [12], which is consistent with our findings in the Chinese population. At the same time, the overall pooled prevalence of 37% reported in our study is slightly higher than the estimates found in several previous meta-analysis [15, 16]. A meta-analysis conducted by Barry et al. reported a global depression prevalence of approximately 30% among women with PCOS [15]. Another recent meta-analysis by Pawel et al. reported the mean prevalence of depression among women with PCOS was 31% [16]. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. One possible reason may be the depression assessment tool in Pawel et al.’s meta-analysis only used HADS [16], while various depression assessment tools were used in our meta-analysis and SDS was the most commonly used tool. Additionally, the variance in depression prevalence between our study and previous global reports may also be attributed to the unique cultural, social, and healthcare contexts in China. In China, mental health issues have traditionally been subject to considerable stigma, which can often result in underreporting of depressive symptoms within the general population. However, within specific patient groups like those with PCOS, there has been growing awareness and recognition of mental health concerns. This heightened awareness may lead to more accurate reporting and diagnosis of depression among PCOS patients in clinical settings, contributing to the higher prevalence observed in our study. Moreover, differences in healthcare systems and accessibility to mental health services may play a significant role in the reporting and treatment of depression. In many Western countries, mental health screening and intervention are more deeply integrated into healthcare systems, which could lead to more consistent and widespread identification of depressive symptoms. Conversely, in China, mental health care is still evolving, and disparities in access to mental health services may contribute to both the underreporting of depression in some areas and the increased recognition of mental health challenges in others, particularly in specialized clinical settings.

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) and depression are closely linked, with each condition potentially exacerbating the other. The prevalence of depression in women with PCOS is significantly higher than in the general population [58], and this association appears to be multifactorial, involving both physiological and psychological mechanisms. Women with PCOS often exhibit elevated levels of androgens, such as testosterone, which have been linked to mood disorders [59]. Hyperandrogenism can lead to symptoms such as hirsutism, acne, and alopecia, which can negatively impact body image and self-esteem, contributing to the development of depressive symptoms [59]. Chronic low-grade inflammation observed in PCOS has also been suggested to play a role in depression, with inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein being elevated in women with PCOS and correlating with depressive symptoms [60]. Women with PCOS often face societal pressures related to physical appearance, particularly in cultures where beauty standards are narrowly defined, exacerbating feelings of inadequacy and depression [61]. Additionally, the stress associated with managing a chronic condition like PCOS, which often requires ongoing medical interventions and lifestyle changes, can lead to or worsen depressive symptoms [62]. The relationship between PCOS and depression is bidirectional. Depression is associated with lower adherence to treatment regimens, such as lifestyle interventions and medication, which can lead to worse physical health outcomes, thereby exacerbating PCOS symptoms [12]. Moreover, depression can lead to changes in the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, resulting in hormonal dysregulation that may further aggravate the symptoms of PCOS [63].

Contrasting our findings with a recent meta-analysis by Cooney et al. [64], which focused on Western populations, reveals a similar overall prevalence but with less heterogeneity. The higher heterogeneity in our study could be influenced by the diverse settings within China, where healthcare delivery, diagnostic practices, and societal factors vary considerably across regions. The significant heterogeneity observed in this meta-analysis is likely multifactorial.

The findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis regarding the geographical variation in the prevalence of depression among women with PCOS in mainland China exhibit notable differences compared to similar global meta-analysis. The prevalence in certain regions, such as Northeast China (25%), was notably lower compared to other regions like West, Central, and East China, where prevalence ranges from 37 to 38%. This discrepancy may be due to the relatively few studies included from the Northeast region of China, with only two studies. The pooled prevalence of depression in women with PCOS was different before and after 2015, with the prevalence in the past decade being slightly lower than that in the previous decade. There may have been increased awareness and more rigorous diagnostic criteria for both PCOS and depression with the continuous development of medicine and social progress [65, 66]. As awareness grows, more cases of mild or subclinical depression may be identified, leading to a slightly lower prevalence rate in more recent studies (35%) compared to those conducted before 2015 (40%). Younger women with PCOS may experience higher rates of depression in this meta-analysis which was inconsistent with previous study [12]. Another study also noted that younger women with PCOS are more susceptible to depression [15], but the reported prevalence rates in their study were generally lower than those observed in the Chinese population. Issues such as body image concerns, and menstrual irregularities are particularly distressing during early adulthood, potentially leading to higher rates of depression [67]. As women age, they may develop better-coping mechanisms and have a more established support system, which might help mitigate the impact of PCOS on mental health [68].

Although PCOS was closely associated with obesity, it could lead to symptoms of depression and lower quality of life [69]. The pooled prevalence of depression among women with PCOS for participants’ BMI < 24 kg/m2 subgroup was slightly higher than participants’ BMI > / = 24 kg/m2 subgroup in our meta-analysis. A previous study found that higher BMI was associated with an increased prevalence of depression in women with PCOS [70]. Despite the association between high BMI and PCOS, a cross-sectional study in Saudi Arabia showed that depression prevalence was not associated with BMI in PCOS women [71]. Recent research found that one-year weight loss program resulted in weight loss in obese women with or without PCOS, however, reduced depression symptoms only occurred in women without PCOS [72]. The pooled prevalence of depression varied across different depression assessment tools in our meta-analysis, with the highest prevalence reported by the SDS at 40%, and the lowest by the HADS at 24%. A meta-analysis found that while SDS often yielded higher prevalence rates due to its self-report nature [73], tools like HADS and BDI are more structured and may have stricter criteria for diagnosing depression, resulting in lower prevalence rates and reduced heterogeneity. The pooled prevalence of depression in women with PCOS varied slightly depending on the diagnostic criteria used, with the Rotterdam subgroup showing the highest prevalence at 38%, followed by other subgroups. The Rotterdam criteria include a broader spectrum of PCOS phenotypes, including women with milder symptoms who might still experience significant psychological distress, contributing to the slightly higher prevalence of depression in this subgroup [74]. The presence of publication bias, as detected by Egger’s test, further complicates the interpretation of results, suggesting that studies with significant findings may have been preferentially published, thus inflating the overall prevalence estimate.

The findings of this meta-analysis underscore the importance of routine depression screening for women with PCOS, particularly in China, where the prevalence of depression is significant. Clinicians should consider integrating depression assessments into standard PCOS management protocols, recognizing the potential regional variations in depression prevalence. Moreover, the identification of additional factors contributing to depression in this population remains crucial for developing targeted interventions. Further research is needed to explore the underlying mechanisms linking PCOS with depression and to establish effective prevention and treatment strategies tailored to the needs of women in different regions of China. For researchers, the high heterogeneity observed suggests the need for further investigation into other potential contributing factors, such as lifestyle, genetic predispositions, and environmental influences, that were not fully explored in this study. Future research should also prioritize longitudinal designs to better understand the causal relationships between PCOS and depression.

This study's primary strength lies in its comprehensive search strategy, encompassing both Chinese and international databases, ensuring a thorough inclusion of relevant studies. The rigorous methodological approach, including subgroup and meta-regression analysis, allowed for an in-depth exploration of potential sources of heterogeneity. However, the study still had several limitations. The high level of heterogeneity, despite extensive analysis, remains partially unexplained, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, the presence of publication bias raises concerns about the potential overestimation of depression prevalence. The lack of protocol preregistration and potential biases due to missing covariates for this systematic reviews and meta-analysis were also limitations of this study. Additionally, the predominance of studies published in Chinese could introduce cultural bias, as mental health reporting and interpretation may differ from international standards.

Conclusion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we found that the prevalence of depression among women with PCOS in mainland China is substantial, with a pooled prevalence of 37% (95% CI, 29–44). Although this study provides a comprehensive overview of the depression burden in Chinese women with PCOS, the high level of heterogeneity and the presence of publication bias indicate that future research should focus on more uniform study designs and larger, more diverse samples. Routine depression screening is recommended for women with PCOS, especially in China, to facilitate early identification and intervention, thereby improving overall health outcomes.
