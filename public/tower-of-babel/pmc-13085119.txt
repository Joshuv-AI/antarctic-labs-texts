Peer review of the pesticide risk assessment of the active substance metyltetraprole
Authors: European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), Fernando Álvarez, Maria Arena, Domenica Auteri, Sofia Batista Leite, Marco Binaglia, Anna Federica Castoldi, Arianna Chiusolo, Angelo Colagiorgi, Mathilde Colas, Federica Crivellente, Chloe De Lentdecker, Isabella De Magistris, Mark Egsmose, Gabriella Fait, Franco Ferilli, German Giner Santonja, Varvara Gouliarmou, Alessio Ippolito, Frederique Istace, Dimitra Kardassi, Aude Kienzler, Anna Lanzoni, Roberto Lava, Renata Leuschner, Alberto Linguadoca, Mariano Lopez Romano, Jochem Louisse, Christopher Lythgo, Oriol Magrans, Iris Mangas, Andrea Mioč, Ileana Miron, Tunde Molnar, Laura Padovani, Vincenzo Padricello, Martina Panzarea, Juan Manuel Parra Morte, Simone Rizzuto, Miguel Santos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe conclusions of the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) following the peer review of the initial risk assessments carried out by the competent authority of the rapporteur Member State France for the pesticide active substance metyltetraprole and the assessment of applications for maximum residue levels (MRLs) are reported. The context of the peer review was that required by Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009 of the European Parliament and of the Council. The conclusions were reached on the basis of the evaluation of the representative uses of metyltetraprole as a fungicide on wheat (spring and winter), barley (spring and winter) and cucumber in greenhouse (permanent). MRLs were assessed in gherkins, courgettes, oat and rye. The reliable endpoints, appropriate for use in regulatory risk assessment and the proposed MRLs, are presented. Missing information identified as being required by the regulatory framework is listed. Concerns are identified.

SUMMARY

Metyltetraprole is a new active substance for which, in accordance with Article 7 of Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009 of the European Parliament and of the Council, the rapporteur Member State (RMS), France, received an application from Sumitomo Chemical Agro Europe S.A.S. on 28 March 2018 for approval. In addition, in accordance with Article 8(1)(g) of the Regulation, Sumitomo Chemical Agro Europe S.A.S. submitted applications for maximum residue levels (MRLs) as referred to in Article 7 of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005. Complying with Article 9 of the Regulation, the completeness of the dossier was checked by the RMS and the date of admissibility of the application was recognised as being 23 May 2018.

An initial evaluation of the dossier on metyltetraprole was provided by the RMS in the draft assessment report (DAR) and subsequently, a peer review of the pesticide risk assessment on the RMS evaluation was conducted by EFSA in accordance with Article 12 of Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009. The following conclusions are derived.

The uses of metyltetraprole according to the representative uses as a fungicide, applied as a foliar spray, on wheat (spring and winter), barley (spring and winter) and cucumber in greenhouse (permanent), as proposed at EU level result in a sufficient fungicidal efficacy against the target pests. MRLs were assessed in gherkins, courgettes, oat and rye.

The assessment of the data package revealed no issues that could not be finalised or that needed to be included as critical areas of concern with respect to identity, physical–chemical and technical properties of the active substance and the formulation for representative uses, and analytical methods.

In the area of mammalian toxicology, no critical areas of concern have been identified. A data gap has been identified for the characterisation of the general toxicity profiles of some residue metabolites.

In the section of residues, the finalisation of the consumer dietary risk assessment considering representative and intended (MRL) uses is pending due to data gaps in rotational crops, processed plant commodities, animals and on general toxicological information of some metabolites. Therefore, the requested MRLs on cereals cannot be proposed as the related risk assessment cannot be finalised due to the aforementioned data gaps. The consumer risk assessment for drinking water is also not finalised due to insufficient information on the effect of water treatment processes on residues in surface water used for the production of drinking water.

In the area of environmental fate and behaviour additional data/information is needed to fully characterise the route of degradation of metyltetraprole in soil under aerobic conditions, resulting in an assessment not finalised. For surface water a data gap has been identified for further information to exclude the potential formation of chlorophenol metabolites (including 4‐chlorophenol and hydroquinone) at levels triggering risk assessment under aqueous photolytic conditions. As consequence, an aquatic exposure assessment for chlorophenol metabolites could not be finalised. A data gap was also identified regarding the information on the effect of water treatment processes on the nature of residues of both the active substance and its identified (and unidentified) metabolites potentially present in surface water, when surface water is abstracted for drinking water.

In the area of ecotoxicology an issue not finalised was identified for the aquatic risk assessment for metabolites 4‐chlorophenol and hydroquinone. No other issues that could not be finalised or that need to be included as critical areas of concern were identified.

Regarding the endocrine disruption properties, based on the available data and assessment, it can be concluded that metyltetraprole does not meet the ED criteria for humans according to point 3.6.5 of Annex II to Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009, as amended by Commission Regulation (EU) 2018/605. Based on the available information on non‐target organisms (NTOs) other than mammals, the assessment of the endocrine disruption potential of metyltetraprole according to point 3.8.2 of Annex II to Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009, as amended by Commission Regulation (EU) 2018/605, cannot be concluded.

BACKGROUND

Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009 of the European Parliament and of the Council 1 (hereinafter referred to as ‘the Regulation’) lays down, inter alia, the detailed rules as regards the procedure and conditions for approval of active substances. This regulates for the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) the procedure for organising the consultation of Member States and the applicant(s) for comments on the initial evaluation in the draft assessment report (DAR), provided by the rapporteur Member State (RMS), and the organisation of an expert consultation, where appropriate.

In accordance with Article 12 of the Regulation, EFSA is required to adopt a conclusion on whether an active substance can be expected to meet the approval criteria provided for in Article 4 of the Regulation (also taking into consideration recital (10) of the Regulation) within 120 days from the end of the period provided for the submission of written comments, subject to an extension of 30 days where an expert consultation is necessary, and a further extension of up to 150 days where additional information is required to be submitted by the applicant(s) in accordance with Article 12(3).

Metyltetraprole is a new active substance for which, in accordance with Article 7 of the Regulation, the RMS, France (hereinafter referred to as the ‘RMS’), received an application from Sumitomo Chemical Agro Europe S.A.S. on 28 March 2018 for approval of the active substance metyltetraprole. In accordance with Article 8(1)(g) of the Regulation, Sumitomo Chemical Agro Europe S.A.S. submitted applications for maximum residue levels (MRLs) as referred to in Article 7 of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005. 2 Complying with Article 9 of the Regulation, the completeness of the dossier was checked by the RMS and the date of admissibility of the application was recognised as being 23 May 2018.

The RMS provided its initial evaluation of the dossier on metyltetraprole in the DAR, which was received by EFSA on 4 December 2022 (France, 2022). The DAR included a proposal to set MRLs, in accordance with Article 11(2) of the Regulation. The peer review was initiated on 9 January 2023 by dispatching the DAR to the Member States and the applicant, Sumitomo Chemical Agro Europe S.A.S., for consultation and comments. EFSA also provided comments. In addition, EFSA conducted a public consultation on the DAR. The comments received were collated by EFSA and forwarded to the RMS for compilation and evaluation in the format of a reporting table. The applicant was invited to respond to the comments in column 3 of the reporting table. The comments and the applicant response were evaluated by the RMS in column 3.

The need for expert consultation and the necessity for additional information to be submitted by the applicant in accordance with Article 12(3) of the Regulation were considered in a telephone conference between EFSA, the RMS and the European Chemicals Agency (ECHA) on 15 September 2023. On the basis of the comments received, the applicant's response to the comments and the RMS's evaluation thereof, it was concluded that additional information should be requested from the applicant, and that EFSA should conduct an expert consultation in the areas of mammalian toxicology, residues, environmental fate and behaviour, and ecotoxicology. In November 2024, outside the regulatory clock‐stop window, 3 the applicant submitted additional information on mammalian toxicology, residues and ED assessment, which was acknowledged, but not peer‐reviewed.

The outcome of the telephone conference, together with EFSA's further consideration of the comments is reflected in the conclusions set out in column 4 of the reporting table. All points that were identified as unresolved at the end of the comment evaluation phase and which required further consideration, including those issues to be considered in an expert consultation, were compiled by EFSA in the format of an evaluation table.

The conclusions arising from the consideration by EFSA, and as appropriate by the RMS, of the points identified in the evaluation table, together with the outcome of the expert consultation and the written consultation on the assessment of additional information, were reported in the final column of the evaluation table.

In accordance with Article 12 of the Regulation, EFSA should adopt a conclusion on whether metyltetraprole can be expected to meet the approval criteria provided for in Article 4 of the Regulation, taking into consideration recital (10) of the Regulation, and give a reasoned opinion concerning MRL applications as referred to in Article 10(1) of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005.

A final consultation on the conclusions arising from the peer review of the risk assessment and on the proposed MRLs took place with Member States via a written procedure in December 2025–January 2026.

This conclusion report summarises the outcome of the peer review of the risk assessment on the active substance and the formulation(s) for representative uses evaluated on the basis of the representative uses of metyltetraprole as a fungicide on wheat (spring and winter), barley (spring and winter) and cucumber in greenhouse (permanent) as proposed by the applicant. In accordance with Article 12(2) of Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009, risk mitigation options identified in the DAR and considered during the peer review, if any, are presented in the conclusion. MRLs were assessed in in gherkins, courgettes, oat and rye.

Furthermore, this conclusion also addresses the requirement for an assessment by EFSA under Article 12 of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005, provided that the active substance will be approved under Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009 without restrictions affecting the residue assessment. In the event of a non‐approval of the active substance or an approval with restrictions that have an impact on the residue assessment, the MRL proposals, if any, from this conclusion might no longer be relevant and a new assessment under Article 12 of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 will be required.

THE ACTIVE SUBSTANCE AND THE FORMULATION FOR REPRESENTATIVE USES

Metyltetraprole is the ISO common name for 1‐[2‐({[1‐(4‐chlorophenyl)‐1H‐pyrazol‐3‐yl]oxy}methyl)‐3‐methylphenyl]‐1,4‐dihydro‐4‐methyl‐5H‐tetrazol‐5‐one (IUPAC). The formulation for representative uses for the evaluation was ‘S‐2367 6EC’, an emulsifiable concentrate (EC) containing 60 g/L of pure metyltetraprole.

The information on the active substance and the formulation for representative uses, including the co‐formulants in this formulation, was considered in the overall assessment during the peer review. One of the component of a co‐formulant is a non‐approved active substance under Regulation (EC) 1107/2009.1, 4 One of the components of a co‐formulant is an unacceptable co‐formulant listed in Annex III of Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009 (below the level set in Regulation (EU) 2021/383 paragraph (14) for acceptable unintentional impurity). A data gap was set for sufficient identification information for one co‐formulant 5 (see Section 10). Details on the composition of the formulation cannot be reported in conclusions because of the provisions in Article 63(2)(d) of Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009. A proposal for classification of the formulation according to Regulation (EC) 1272/2008 was provided by the applicant and assessed by the RMS (please see Volumes 3 CP of the DAR).

The representative uses evaluated were foliar spray application against fungi on wheat (spring and winter), barley (spring and winter) and cucumber in greenhouse (permanent). Full details of the GAPs can be found in the list of end points in Appendix B. Data were submitted to conclude that the use of metyltetraprole according to the representative uses proposed at EU level results in a sufficient fungicidal efficacy against the target pests, following the guidance document SANCO/10054/2013‐rev. 3 (European Commission, 2013).

CONCLUSIONS OF THE EVALUATION

General aspects

The toxicological profile of the formulation for representative uses was discussed at the Pesticides Peer Review Teleconference 152 in November 2024 (see Section 2).

With regard to the mammalian toxicity information available for the formulation for representative uses ‘S‐2367 6EC’, studies were performed for six endpoints (acute oral, dermal and inhalation toxicity, skin and eye irritation and skin sensitisation). With regard to the co‐formulants contained in ‘S‐2367 6EC’, the component mentioned above as non‐approved active substance is also classified as Carc. 2 (present between 0.1% and 1% in the PPP), while sufficient toxicological data were not available for any of the other components, so these could not be assessed (co‐formulants present at > 90% of the PPP). Therefore, the genotoxicity and repeated dose toxicity information over short‐ and long‐term might be considered to reach a final conclusion on the safety of ‘S‐2367 6EC’ (data gap, see Section 10).

The availability of ecotoxicity data with the formulation for representative uses was discussed at the experts' meeting 6 (see Section 5). It was noted that, based on the available data, the formulation for representative uses is more toxic to honey bees and algae than expected from the active substance. The available acute ecotoxicity data did not show any increase in toxicity for mammals, fish and aquatic invertebrates. A data retrieval search for available ecotoxicity data for the individual components was not available and therefore it was not possible to reach a conclusion on the safety of the formulation for representative uses (data gap, see Section 10).

IDENTITY, PHYSICAL/CHEMICAL/TECHNICAL PROPERTIES AND METHODS OF ANALYSIS

The following guidance documents were followed in the production of this conclusion: European Commission (2000a, 2000b, 2010, 2013).

A common reference specification covering all manufacturing sources is proposed based on batch data from pilot scale production systems. It is noted that new batch data shall again be provided for all manufacturing sources once industrial scale production methods and procedures are stabilised. The proposed minimum purity is 935 g/kg. ISS7, OHTM, OMTM, heptane and xylenes (composed of m‐xylene, p‐xylene, o‐xylene and ethylbenzene) are considered toxicologically relevant impurities but of no concern at the specified reference levels of 32, 2, 5, 2 and 6 g/kg respectively (see Section 2). For one manufacturing source sufficient data were not available to demonstrate that additional relevant impurities are not present in the pilot batches (data gap, see Section 10). The batches used in the (eco)toxicological assessment support the proposed reference specification. There is no FAO specification available for metyltetraprole.

The main data regarding the identity of metyltetraprole and its physical and chemical properties are given in Appendix B. A data gap was set for a safety data sheet pursuant to Article 31 of Regulation (EC) No 1907/2006 for the active substance (data gap, see Section 10). A data gap was also set for data for the relevant impurities OHTM, OMTM, heptane and xylenes (composed of m‐xylene, p‐xylene, o‐xylene and ethylbenzene), consisting of spectral data and content of each impurity before and after storage of the formulation (data gap, see Section 10). In addition, the content of the relevant impurity ISS7 before and after 2 years of storage of the formulation at ambient temperature was not provided (data gap, see Section 10).

In general, adequate methods are available for the generation of data required for the risk assessment, except for specific plant residue studies for which the efficiency of the extraction procedure used was not addressed (data gap, see Section 10).

Validated methods of analysis are available for the determination of the active substance and impurities in the technical materials and for the determination of the active substance in the formulation for representative uses. A data gap was set for methods of analysis of the relevant impurities ISS7, OHTM, OMTM, heptane and xylenes (composed of m‐xylene, p‐xylene, o‐xylene and ethylbenzene) in the formulation for representative uses (data gap, see Section 10). An appropriate QuEChERS–HPLC–MS/MS method is available for monitoring metyltetraprole residues in food and feed of plant origin, with limits of quantification (LOQs) of 0.01 mg/kg in dry, high‐water, high‐oil and high‐acid content commodities. Extraction efficiencies were demonstrated for dry, high‐water and high‐oil content commodities, but not for high‐acid commodities (a data gap was not set as high‐acid commodities are not included in the evaluated representative uses). Residues of metyltetraprole can be monitored in meat, fat and milk using a QuEChERS–HPLC–MS/MS with an LOQ of 0.01 mg/kg for each matrix, the extraction efficiency was demonstrated only for fat, but not for meat and milk matrices (data gap, see Section 10). Residues of 3‐CH2OH‐S‐2367 in eggs can be monitored using a HPLC–MS/MS method with an LOQ of 0.01 mg/kg as the extraction efficiency of the method was demonstrated. Residues of 3‐COOH‐S‐2367 in liver can be monitored using a HPLC–MS/MS method with an LOQ of 0.01 mg/kg as the method extraction efficiency was demonstrated. Residue of metyltetraprole in soil can be monitored by HPLC–MS/MS, with a LOQ of 0.01 mg/kg. An appropriate HPLC–MS/MS method is available for monitoring residues of metyltetraprole in groundwater, drinking water and surface water with LOQs of 0.03 μg/L. Residues of metyltetraprole in air can be monitored by HPLC–MS/MS, with a LOQ of 0.48 μg/m3. According to the residue definition for body fluids and tissues, analytical methods are required for monitoring the metabolites N‐Des‐Me‐3‐CH2OH‐S‐2367, 3‐COOH‐S‐2367 and 3‐CH2OH‐S‐2367. A suitable HPLC–MS/MS method was provided for monitoring metabolites 3‐CH2OHS‐2367 and 3‐COOH‐S‐2367 in body tissues (liver, kidney muscle and fat) with a LOQ of 0.01 mg/kg for each analyte. However, validated analytical methods were not provided to monitor metabolite N‐Des‐Me‐3‐CH2OH‐S‐2367 in body tissues and N‐Des‐Me‐3‐CH2OH‐S‐2367, 3‐COOH‐S‐2367 and 3‐CH2OH‐S‐2367 in body fluids (data gap, see Section 10).

MAMMALIAN TOXICITY

The toxicological profile of the active substance metyltetraprole and its metabolites was discussed at the Pesticides Peer Review Experts' Teleconference 152 in November 2024. The assessment is based on the following guidance documents: European Commission (2003, 2012), EFSA (2022), EFSA PPR Panel (2017) and ECHA (2017).

The applicant submitted a set of valid toxicity studies according to Regulation (EC) 283/2013 7 supporting the toxicological assessment of metyltetraprole.

Regarding the proposed reference specification (RS), the impurities OHTM, OMTM, ISS7, heptane and xylenes are identified as relevant, with maximum toxicologically acceptable levels set at 2, 5, 32, 2 and 6 g/kg, respectively.

The test material used in toxicity studies is considered representative of the proposed RS for the active substance and associated relevant impurities.

The analytical methods used in feed, body fluids and tissues, air and any additional matrices used in support of the toxicity studies are overall considered fit‐for‐purpose/validated.

The oral absorption of metyltetraprole is estimated to account for 11.5% of the administered dose. 8 Excretion occurs predominantly through the faecal route, with appreciable amounts excreted in urine.

In the rat, metyltetraprole is well distributed, with the highest levels being reached in the plasma, kidney and liver. There is no significant accumulation after repeated administration.

The main metabolic pathways identified are N‐demethylation, oxidation of the 3‐methyl group of the phenyl ring followed by further oxidation to carboxylic acid and glucuronide conjugation.

Based on comparative in vitro metabolism in mouse, rat, rabbit, dog and human liver microsomes, the majority of experts in the Peer Review meeting concluded that there were no major metabolic inter‐species differences and no unique or disproportionate human metabolites. On the other hand, the RMS expressed the view that the limitations in the available in vitro comparative metabolism prevented any robust conclusion to be drawn on the formation of unique or disproportionate human metabolites.

The residue definition for body fluids and tissues includes metyltetrapole, N‐Des‐Me‐3‐CH2OH‐S‐2367, 3‐COOH‐S‐2367 and 3‐CH2OH‐S‐2367.

Metyltetraprole has low acute toxicity by oral, dermal and inhalation exposure, and has no irritating or sensitising properties. Although an in vitro 3T3 NRU phototoxicity assay was available and negative, it is not considered suitable to test compounds, like metyltetraprole, not showing absorption in the UV‐A range (i.e. at wavelengths > 310 nm), but only in the UV‐C and UV‐B ranges. Thus, it is neither possible to conclude on phototoxicity, nor on photomutagenicity (data gap, see Section 10). 9

Short‐term oral toxicity studies were provided for rats, mice and dogs. Liver is the target organ in all species. Other adverse short‐term effects were reported on kidney (rat), blood (rat, dog), thymus (rat, dog), heart and lung (rat), lacrimal glands (mouse). The relevant short‐term no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) is < 100 mg/kg bw per day (being the latter a lowest observable adverse effect level (LOAEL)), based on a 90‐day study in dogs where liver effects (periportal inflammatory cell infiltrates rich in neutrophils, hepatocellular necrosis with inflammatory cell infiltrate and haemorrhage, and decreased glycogen mainly in males) and decreased thymus weight were observed at all doses in both sexes.

The relevant dermal NOAEL in rat is 1000 mg/kg bw per day (systemic) from a 28‐day study, while it is 300 mg/kg bw per day for local effects (eschar formation).

Based on the available genotoxicity data package, the substance is unlikely to be genotoxic. 10 This is in line with the proposed harmonised classification by the ECHA Risk Assessment Committee (RAC) (ECHA, 2024).

After long‐term exposure, target organs for toxicity included liver both in rats and mice, and blood (white and red blood cells), kidney and heart in rat. The relevant long‐term NOAEL is 82.2 mg/kg bw per day based on increased absolute and relative liver weight in male mice.

Treatment‐related increased incidence of tumours was seen both in rats and mice (malignant lymphomas in both rat and mouse; malignant uterine schwannomas and mammary gland adenomas/adenocarcinomas in rats). The relevant carcinogenic NOAEL is 82.2 mg/kg bw per day based on increased incidence of malignant lymphomas in mice. 11

In this respect, the proposed harmonised classification as Carc Cat 2 by the ECHA RAC is noted (ECHA, 2024).

With regard to reproductive toxicity studies, fertility and overall reproductive performance were not affected in rats. In the multigeneration rat study, the parental NOAEL is 409 mg/kg bw per day based on effects in F0 and/or F1 females consisting of increased liver and thyroid weights, and decreased uterus weight. The offspring NOAEL is 409 mg/kg bw per day, on the basis of decreased pup body weight and body weight gain during late lactation and reduced absolute and relative weights of thymus and spleen in the absence of associated histopathological findings. Finally, the reproductive NOAEL is 1385 mg/kg bw per day, the highest dose tested. 12

The substance was concluded unlikely to be a reproductive toxicant in humans.

With regard to foetal development, increased incidence of skeletal alterations was observed both in rats and rabbits. In the rat teratogenicity study, the maternal NOAEL is the top dose of 1000 mg/kg bw per day, while the developmental NOAEL is 500 mg/kg bw per day, based on increased incidence of misaligned sternebral hemicentres and costal cartilages. In the rabbit teratogenicity study, the maternal NOAEL is 100 mg/kg bw per day, based on increased number of females with markedly decreased food consumption, clinical signs (scant or no faeces), abortion and the developmental NOAEL is 100 mg/kg bw per day based on increased incidence of misaligned sacral arch. 13

The ECHA RAC concluded that no classification for reproductive and/or developmental toxicity is warranted for metyltetraprole (ECHA, 2024).

With respect to neurotoxicity, increased incidence of tremors and landing footsplay was observed in males in the acute neurotoxicity study in rats, triggering a NOAEL for neurotoxicity of 500 mg/kg bw per day; in the absence of effects, the systemic toxicity NOAEL was set at the top dose of 2000 mg/kg bw per day. Due to the equivocal evidence of neurotoxicity in the acute neurotoxicity study and after repeated oral exposure to metyltetraprole in the available dataset, a data gap for a repeated dose neurotoxicity study was agreed (data gap, see Section 10). 14

The fungicidal mode of action (MoA) of metyltetraprole involves inhibition of fungal respiration via inhibition of mitochondrial cytochrome bc1 complex III. Metyltetraprole and its metabolites N‐des‐Me‐S‐2367, 3‐CH2OH‐S‐2367 and 3‐COOH‐S‐2367 were demonstrated to inhibit the mitochondrial complex III activity in human and rat liver mitochondrial fractions 15 in a concentration‐dependent manner. The effective inhibitory concentrations (IC25 and IC50) of metyltetraprole and its metabolites in rat and human test systems were in the same order of magnitude, suggesting a similar sensitivity of rats and humans to Complex III inhibition in vitro.

ADME data supports that the active substance and its metabolites can reach the brain. Complex III inhibition producing mitochondrial dysfunction and degeneration of dopaminergic neurons of nigrostriatal pathway can contribute as a risk factor to the development of parkinsonian motor deficits (AOP 3) 16 (data gap, see Section 10).

No immunotoxicity pattern was identified across studies provided in the available dataset. 17

Health‐based guidance values (HBGVs) have been derived for metyltetraprole. The acceptable daily intake (ADI) of metyltetraprole is 0.082 mg/kg bw per day, based on the long‐term toxicity and carcinogenicity NOAEL of 82.2 mg/kg bw per day from the 18‐month mouse study (as also supported by the carcinogenic NOAEL of the 2‐year rat study of 83.9 mg/kg bw per day) to which an overall uncertainty factor (UF) of 1000 is applied. Besides the standard 100x UF for inter‐ and intra‐species differences, the 10x extra UF accounts for the: (i) incomplete dataset, (ii) uncertainties concerning the potential human health implications (e.g. parkinsonian motor deficits) associated with the SDHI fungicidal mode of action, (iii) uncertainty about the limited rat‐to‐human difference in sensitivity to in vitro inhibition of mitochondrial complex III (see Section 10), as well as for the (iv) severity of the findings (cancer) and adequate MoS.

The acceptable operator exposure level (AOEL) is 0.0115 mg/kg bw per day based on the LOAEL of 100 mg/kg bw per day from the 13‐week dog study to which an overall UF of 1000 is applied. The 10x extra UF accounts for the (i) incomplete dataset, (ii) uncertainties concerning the potential human health implications (e.g. parkinsonian motor deficits) associated with the SDHI fungicidal mode of action, (iii) uncertainty about the limited rat‐to‐human difference in sensitivity to in vitro inhibition of mitochondrial complex III (see Section 10), as well as for the (iv) LOAEL‐to‐NOAEL extrapolation. A correcting factor of 11.5% for limited oral absorption is also included in the derivation of this health‐based guidance value.

The acute reference dose (ARfD) and the acute AOEL (AAOEL) are not considered necessary since acute neurotoxic effects are slight and of transient nature; effects in the rabbit prenatal developmental toxicity study are considered secondary to malnutrition (abortion) or not relevant for acute effects (skeletal variations).

Dermal absorption of metyltetraprole in the representative product (S‐2367 6EC) has been assessed in an in vitro study with human skin. Based on the EFSA guidance (EFSA PPR Panel, 2017), the dermal absorption values to be used for risk assessment are 10% for the concentrate (60 g a.s./L), 3% for the dilution 0.04 g a.s./L (for use on cucumber) and 10% for the dilution > 0.04 g a.s./L and < 60 g a.s./L (for outdoor use on cereals). 18

Regarding the non‐dietary exposure (based on the EFSA model/calculator 2014 and 2022), the estimates for operators during the field use on cereals are below the AOEL when gloves are worn during mixing/loading (in addition to workwear). For indoor use on cucumbers, workwear and gloves are necessary for mixing and loading, as well as workwear for application in case of manual knapsack application for a normal scenario. For dense scenario, workwear, gloves and faceshield are necessary for mixing and loading and gloves and rainsuit for application for both manual hand‐held and knapsack application. The resident exposure is estimated to be below the AOEL for both outdoor and indoor uses; in the absence of the AAOEL for the active substance, the acute exposure assessment for bystanders is considered to be covered by the resident exposure. The worker exposure for the indoor use is below the AOEL for the inspection/irrigation task, while the use of gloves is required for reaching and picking activity. For the outdoor uses, the estimated exposure for workers doing inspection/irrigation activities is below the AOEL. 19

For most residue metabolites 20 the genotoxicity potential was excluded on the basis of experimental data available for each metabolite, whereas for the metabolites 3‐COOH‐S‐2367 and 3‐CH2OH‐S‐2367 genotoxicity was concluded as unlikely based on in silico analysis and ADME data. For residue metabolites 21 with a toxicological profile qualitatively and quantitatively similar to that of metyltetraprole, the HBGVs of the parent apply. Metyltetraprole's HBGVs are considered sufficiently protective regarding Complex III inhibition by metabolites 3‐COOH‐S‐2367 and 3‐CH2OH‐S‐2367, since the latter were less potent than metyltetraprole in an in vitro inhibition assay addressing this endpoint. For other residue metabolites, individual ADIs (and ARfDs) were set based on individual toxicological data/information (see Table 1). For ISS7 – evaluated as a residue metabolite – a specific ADI was set, based on a short‐term study (see Table 1). Nevertheless, ISS7 is also present in groundwater: in the absence of proper long‐term testing, ISS7 carcinogenic potential cannot be dismissed in view of the proposed harmonised classification as Carc 2 for the parent metyltetraprole (see Sections 4 and 7). For CPOH, a common metabolite to pyraclostrobin, the same ADI of 0.1 mg/kg bw per day established during pyraclostrobin's peer review (EFSA, 2025) was considered applicable. This ADI is in fact sufficiently close to that of metyltetraprole for lifting any remaining concern about the MoA of complex III inhibition. Differently from the assessment available in the renewal assessment report (RAR) of pyraclostrobin, an ARfD (same value than the ADI) has been set for the metabolite CPOH as a worst‐case assumption in the absence of appropriate data.

RESIDUES

The assessment in the residue section is based on the following guidance documents: OECD (2009, 2011), European Commission (2011) and JMPR (2004, 2007).

Metyltetraprole was discussed at the Pesticides Peer Review Experts' Teleconference 156 in December 2024.

Representative uses residues

Metabolism was studied in fruits (apple) and pulses/oilseeds (soya bean) after foliar application and in cereal/grass crops (wheat) after foliar application and seed treatment. Three radiolabel positions were employed in wheat and soya bean studies (pyrazolyl, benzyl, tetrazolyl) and two in apple (pyrazolyl and benzyl). It was agreed by experts that no further studies in fruits with tetrazolyl ring are needed. For the lack of studies with chlorophenyl label, experts agreed that sufficient evidence was presented on the likelihood of molecule cleavage and on possible formation of degradation products containing chlorophenyl ring only. 22 Accordingly, no further studies were required with chlorophenyl ring too. Metyltetraprole was the main residue in apple fruit (62% total radioactive residue (TRR)), soya bean pods (75% TRR) and wheat straw and grain (48% and 42% TRR). Several metabolites were identified below 10% of TRR, except OHTM‐COOH (20% TRR in soya bean seed). Others were present at significant absolute levels: 3‐CH2OH‐S‐2367 (0.03 mg/kg in apple, 1 mg/kg in wheat straw), ISS7 (0.1 mg/kg in apple, 0.03 mg/kg in grain, 0.9 mg/kg in straw), OHTM (0.05 mg/kg in apple, 0.6 mg/kg in straw) and 3HM‐OHTM‐COOH (0.04 mg/kg in wheat grain). Field trials confirmed the presence of 3‐CH2OH‐S‐2367, ISS7 in cereal grain, straw and cucumber above the LOQ, while OHTM was predominantly present in cereal straw. Methyl‐tetrazol derivatives (1‐MTD) metabolites were high in soya bean seeds (1‐MT alanine: 67% TRR, 0.1 mg/kg) and present at significant levels in wheat grain (0.02 mg/kg). In field trials these metabolites were found above the LOQ in cereal commodities. Since the metabolism is similar in all investigated crops, a general residue definition for risk assessment (RA‐RD) for all crops (foliar and seed treatment) is proposed: Sum of metyltetraprole and metabolite 3‐CH2OH‐S‐2367 and its conjugate, expressed as metyltetraprole (RA‐RD1);ISS7 (RA‐RD2);1‐MT alanine, 1‐MT acetic acid, 1‐MT lactic acid (expression is open, pending the data gap on the toxicological profile of 1‐MT lactic acid and 1‐MT acetic acid, see Sections 2 and 9.1) (RA‐RD3).

Metyltetraprole is considered as good marker in plant commodities and therefore confirmed as plant residue definition for enforcement purposes.

A sufficient number of residue field trials for all representative uses, covered by storage stability data for all analytes except for 1‐MT alanine in wheat and barley straw treated with one application, was presented. No further data is needed as the available field trials treated with two applications cover the risk assessment. The measurements for all compounds are reliable, while for 1‐MTD and CPOH the results need further verification due to the analytical method validation (extraction efficiency not addressed) (data gap, see Section 10).

The relevance of CPOH and OHTM metabolites for inclusion in plant RA‐RD was considered as they were quantified up to 0.2 mg/kg in feed items tested in field trials, and dietary burden was exceeded in one animal species (0.007 mg/kg bw/day, sheep). While the calculated exposure does not indicate significant residue transfer to other animal species, an increased dietary burden for all species resulting from potential future uses may require reassessment of inclusion of CPOH, OHTM and their conjugates in the RA‐RD.

In a valid confined rotational crop study, overdosed with respect to the maximal seasonal rate (2.5 N), similar qualitative metabolism as in primary crops was found. No labelling on chlorophenyl ring was employed and, as in primary crop, it is not required. Metyltetraprole was predominant in lettuce (18% TRR) and carrot roots (70% TRR). Major metabolites included 3‐CH2OH‐S‐2367 (13% lettuce), OHTM and its glucose conjugate (43% carrot tops), 3‐HM‐OHTM‐COOH (30% grain) and 1‐MTD metabolites (1‐MT lactic acid, 1‐MT acetic acid, 1‐MT alanine: 48% straw, 37% grain, 28% grain, respectively). Based on the overall data presented, the same residue definitions as in primary crops apply to rotational crops. In four rotational crop field trials, dosed sufficiently to cover soil accumulation of metyltetraprole, only 1‐MT lactic acid was found above the LOQ (up to 0.02 mg/kg in cereals straw; 0.05 mg/kg in carrot leaves) from all the analysed compounds (ISS7, 3‐CH2OH‐S‐2367, OHTM, 3HM‐OHTM‐COOH, 1‐MTDs). For two field trials further verification of the analytical method (extraction efficiency) is needed for 3‐HM‐OHTM‐COOH (data gap, see Section 10). The available field trials sufficiently cover ISS7 residues resulting from the maximal seasonal rate, with all measured residues in soil and crops remaining < 0.01 mg/kg. Nevertheless, the studies do not address the potential accumulation of the soil‐persistent metabolite ISS7 under repeated multi‐annual use of metyltetraprole. Consequently, it cannot be excluded that higher residues of ISS7 in rotational crops might arise under such conditions. Given that ISS7 is also a residue in primary crops, there is currently uncertainty as to whether primary or rotational crops would eventually be the principal source of dietary exposure. To resolve this uncertainty, appropriately dosed rotational crop studies would be necessary (data gap, see Section 10), especially in the context of any prospective expansion of the uses of metyltetraprole. The current consumer and livestock exposure assessments for ISS7 are based solely on the available dataset and, due to the absence of fully dosed rotational crop studies, may not reliably reflect the full range of potential exposure. At present, it is not possible to determine whether the assessment is conservative. Alternatively, a risk mitigation measure involving the removal of the soil/growing substrate after each planting for uses on cucurbits has been proposed. However, the feasibility of this measure has not been assessed, and it is not applicable to the uses on cereal crops in the field.

Hydrolysis studies conducted with metyltetraprole, ISS7 and 3‐CH2OH‐S‐2367 demonstrated stability of these compounds. A hydrolysis study with 1‐MTD is also required considering their occurrence in wheat and barley field trials (data gap, see Section 10). Therefore, the same RA‐RD as in raw commodities can be only provisionally proposed for processed commodities. For cucurbits, where 1‐MTD were < 0.01 mg/kg, this data might be triggered by future uses potentially resulting in higher 1‐MTD residues. Studies investigating magnitude of residues in processed wheat and barley allowed derivation of processing factors in line with the RA‐RD1 and RA‐RD2 (see Appendix B).

Metabolism studies with metyltetraprole in hens and goats (pyrazolyl, benzyl labelling) resulted in two metabolites, 3‐CH2OH‐S‐2367 and 3‐COOH‐S‐2367. Metyltetraprole was found in most matrices (up to 67% TRR), 3‐CH2OH‐S‐2367 reaching 52% TRR in eggs and 3‐COOH‐S‐2367 54% TRR in liver. Due to limited reference standards and labelling employed in the available metabolism studies, to allow a derivation of a final RA‐RD, further evidence is needed on feed metabolites formation and presence (i.e. CPOH, OHTM metabolites) in animal commodities (data gap, see Section 9.1).

Two studies conducted with ISS7 in goat and hen, were of limited use due to tentative residue identifications. In other two valid studies, ISS7 was found only in liver at low levels (4% TRR). Based on dietary burden calculations and dosing levels in the metabolism studies for ISS7, applying the criterium of metabolic capacity according to test guideline OECD 505 several metabolites may be expected at or above 0.01 mg/kg in animal commodities: M830F007, M830F011 and M830F012 in ruminant liver and kidney, M830F008 in all ruminant tissues and M830F013 in poultry liver and eggs. Under the trigger laid down in Commission Regulation (EU) No 283/2013 based on DM feed intake, all metabolites are estimated < 0.01 mg/kg and no feeding study is required. This difference arises from the distinct triggering criteria and should be noted accordingly. Uncertainty relating to possible higher ISS7 exposure from rotational crops also warrants consideration (data gap, see Section 10), and any future expansion of the use patterns may increase dietary burden and could confirm the need for feeding studies (data gap, see Section 10) and addressing the toxicity of the aforementioned metabolites.

The metabolism study with 1‐MT lactic acid in ruminants showed low TRRs in all matrices except the kidney, where 1‐MT lactic acid was dominant (58% TRR), followed by 1‐MT alanine (22% TRR) and 1‐MT (4% TRR). No studies were conducted with 1‐MT acetic acid and 1‐MT alanine, as the dietary burden was not exceeded. Given the high dosing level in the present study (55N), and assuming similar behaviour for 1‐MT acetic acid and 1‐MT alanine, 1‐MTD are unlikely to exceed 0.01 mg/kg in animal commodities. If future uses increase the dietary burden, their inclusion in RA‐RD should be reconsidered. Goat and hen valid feeding studies with metyltetraprole showed 3‐COOH‐S‐2367 above 0.01 mg/kg in hen liver at the lowest dose level, and in goat kidney (second dose level). At the highest dose level, 3‐CH2OH‐S‐2367 and 3‐COOH‐S‐2367 appeared in eggs, liver and fat. Metyltetraprole reached 0.01 mg/kg in hen eggs (highest dose) and in goat milk and muscle (highest dose).

ENVIRONMENTAL FATE AND BEHAVIOUR

Metyltetraprole was discussed at the Pesticides Peer Review Experts' Teleconference 154 in November 2024.

The rates of dissipation and degradation in the environmental matrices investigated were estimated using FOCUS (2006) kinetics guidance. In soil laboratory incubations under aerobic conditions in the dark, metyltetraprole exhibited very high persistence. No major metabolite (> 10% applied radioactivity (AR)) was formed. However, due to the very slow degradation of the parent compound and the limited duration of the available guideline studies (up to 120 days), the formation and decline of certain metabolites, such as 3‐COOH‐S‐2367, could not be fully characterised. As a result, a data gap was identified for additional information to clarify the degradation pathway of metyltetraprole under aerobic soil conditions, including the formation, persistence and relevance of such metabolites for residue definition and environmental exposure assessments (data gap, see Section 9.1). Mineralisation of pyrazolyl, benzyl and tetrazolyl ring to carbon dioxide accounted for 0.9%–2% AR after 90–120 days. The formation of unextractable residues (not extracted by sodium hydroxide or acetonitrile/water and/or methanol and/or methanol / water) for these radiolabels accounted for 6.1%–9.9% AR after 90–120 days. In anaerobic soil incubations, metyltetraprole was essentially stable. The available laboratory soil photolysis study indicates that the process of photolysis can significantly enhance the overall dissipation of metyltetraprole at the soil surface, with the formation of the major metabolite ISS7 (max. 16.4% AR). Under aerobic conditions in the dark, metabolite ISS7 exhibited very high persistence.

Metyltetraprole can be considered low mobile to immobile in soil. ISS7 exhibited medium to low mobility. It was concluded that the adsorption of metyltetraprole and its soil photolysis metabolite ISS7 was not pH dependent.

In satisfactory field dissipation studies carried out at four sites from the Netherlands, the United Kingdom, France and Italy (spray application to the soil surface on bare soil plots), soil degradation rate (DegT50) values were derived following normalisation to FOCUS reference conditions (20°C and pF2 soil moisture) following the legacy EFSA (2014b) DegT50 guidance approach. Metyltetraprole exhibited moderate to high persistence. Sample analyses were carried out for the parent metyltetraprole and metabolite ISS7. The field data endpoints were not combined with laboratory values to derive modelling endpoints for metyltetraprole. Considering the results of the field dissipation trials, a soil accumulation study of the active substance and soil photolysis metabolite ISS7 under field conditions was conducted at two European locations (the Netherlands and Italy), where the formulation for representative uses was applied once per year for five consecutive years to bare soil without incorporation. Although the metabolite ISS7 was not quantified at level triggering assessment during the field trials, the residue levels of this metabolite slowly increased through the years with the repeated applications of the active substance. Considering the metabolite's persistence and its tendency to accumulate in soil, the experts of the Pesticides Peer Review Teleconference 154 concluded that a groundwater risk assessment is needed for metabolite ISS7.

In laboratory incubations in dark aerobic natural sediment water systems, metyltetraprole exhibited very high persistence. No major metabolite (> 10% AR) was formed. The unextractable sediment fraction (not extracted by sodium hydroxide or acetonitrile / water and/or methanol and/or methanol / water) was accounting for 7.1%–9.7% AR at study end (100 days). Mineralisation accounted for 0.7%–1.6% AR at the end of the study. The rate of decline of metyltetraprole in a laboratory sterile aqueous photolysis experiment was faster relative to that occurred in the aerobic sediment water incubations. Chromatographically resolved components for radiolabels pyrazolyl, tetrazolyl and benzyl ring accounting for > 10% AR were ISS7 (max. 30.6% AR), de‐ClPh‐S‐2367 (max. 17.3% AR), OHTM (max. 15.5% AR), S‐2367‐R1 (max. 19.4% AR), S‐2367‐R2 (max. 16.2% AR) and S‐2367‐R6 (max. 12.3% AR). In the absence of an aquatic photolytic degradation study with metyltetraprole radiolabelled in the chlorophenyl ring, a data gap was identified for further information to exclude the formation of chlorophenol metabolites (including 4‐chlorophenol and hydroquinone) and any other related chlorophenyl transformation products of metyltetraprole under photolytic conditions (data gap, see Section 9.1). During the Pesticides Peer Review Experts' Teleconference 154 experts decided to include 4‐chlorophenol and hydroquinone in the residue definition.

The necessary surface water and sediment exposure assessments (predicted environmental concentrations (PEC) calculations) were carried out for the metabolites ISS7, de‐ClPh‐S‐2367, OHTM, S‐2367‐R1, S.2367‐R2 and S‐2367‐R6 using the FOCUS (2001) step 1 and step 2 approach (version 3.2 of the Steps 1–2 in FOCUS calculator) for all representative uses. A data gap was identified for the exposure assessment in aqueous systems for 4‐chlorophenol and hydroquinone (data gap, see Sections 5 and 9.1).

For metyltetraprole, appropriate step 3 (FOCUS, 2001) and step 4 calculations were available. 23 The step 4 calculations appropriately followed the FOCUS (FOCUS, 2007) guidance, with no‐spray drift buffer zones of up to 5m being implemented for the drainage scenarios (representing a 13.5%–73% spray drift reduction), and combined no‐spray buffer zones with vegetative buffer strips of up to 10m (reducing solute flux in run‐off by 60% and erosion run‐off of mass adsorbed to soil by 85%) being implemented for the run‐off scenarios. The SWAN (Surface Water Assessment eNabler) tool (version 4.0.1) was appropriately used to implement these mitigation measures in the simulations. However, risk managers and others may wish to note that whilst run‐off mitigation is included in the step 4 calculations available, the FOCUS (2007) report acknowledges that for substances with K Foc < 2000 mL/g (i.e. metyltetraprole), the general applicability and effectiveness of run‐off mitigation measures had been less clearly demonstrated in the available scientific literature, than for more strongly adsorbed compounds. For the representative uses on cucumbers in permanent greenhouses, calculations were available for metyltetraprole with the FOCUS STEP 3 scenario considering drainage (D6) and GEM (Greenhouse Emission Model – version 3.3.2) (Step 3, FOCUS, 2001; EFSA, 2014a). It should be noted that the GEM model and scenario definition used were an EU guidance agreed example scenario reflecting Dutch conditions for greenhouses (permanent). However, it also needs to be noted that it may not be representative for the range of these structure types present in all EU territories.

The necessary groundwater exposure assessments were appropriately carried out using FOCUS (European Commission, 2014) scenarios and the models PEARL 4.4.4, PELMO 5.5.3 and MACRO 5.5.4. 24 For all representative uses, metyltetraprole 80th percentile annual average concentrations moving below 1m depth were predicted to be below the parametric drinking water limit of 0.1 μg/L in all FOCUS groundwater scenarios. ECOTOXICOLOGY

The risk assessment is based on the following documents: European Commission (2002), SETAC (2001), EFSA (2009, 2013) and EFSA PPR Panel (2013).

The ecotoxicology assessment was discussed during the Pesticides Peer Review Experts' Teleconference 155 in December 2024. The batches used in the ecotoxicity studies were considered sufficiently representative of the proposed reference specification.

The applicant confirmed that the representative use of metyltetrapole on cucumbers in greenhouses is for permanent structures only. Consequently, a low risk to birds, mammals, bees, non‐target arthropods and non‐target terrestrial plants was concluded on the basis of minimal exposure. Since the soil DT90 for the outcome of the available risk assessment for aquatic and soil organisms refer to the paragraphs below.

Suitable acute and long‐term studies with the active substance were available for birds and mammals. The appropriate long‐term endpoint to be selected for the risk assessment of wild mammals was discussed and agreed during the experts' meeting. 25

On the basis of the available screening‐level risk assessments, a low acute and long‐term risk to both birds and mammals from the active substance, via dietary exposure, was concluded. Furthermore, a low long‐term risk to fish‐eating and earthworm‐eating birds, and mammals was concluded for the active substance. A low acute and long‐term risk to birds and mammals was also concluded for exposure via contaminated water.

Two plant metabolites were identified to require further consideration of the risk to birds and mammals (ISS7 and 3‐CH2OH‐S‐2367 glucose conjugate). An acute toxicity study with metabolite ISS7 was available for both birds and mammals. A sub‐chronic toxicity study for mammals was also available for metabolite ISS7. Furthermore, an acute toxicity study with the formulation for representative uses was available for mammals. Based on the available data, a low risk to metabolite ISS7 was concluded. For 3‐CH2OH‐S‐2367 glucose conjugate a low risk to birds and mammals was concluded since it was demonstrated that the toxicity data and risk assessment performed for the parent substance also covers this metabolite.

For aquatic organisms, toxicity data with the active substance were available for fish (11 acute studies, two chronic studies), Daphnia magna (acute and chronic), algae and sediment dwelling organisms. Nevertheless, during the peer review, a concern was raised regarding the mode‐of‐action of metyltetraprole (i.e. acting on the respiratory chain in the mitochondria) and the possibility that other aquatic invertebrates, such as the mysid shrimp (Americamysis bahia), could be more sensitive than the tested species (Daphnia magna). This issue was discussed during the experts meeting 26 where the experts agreed that, although the risk assessment is driven by the toxicity to fish, a data gap should be identified for an acute toxicity study with the mysid shrimp (data gap, see Section 10).

For the formulation for representative uses, ‘S‐2367 6EC’, suitable toxicity data were available for fish (acute), aquatic invertebrates (acute) and algae. The data indicated that the formulation for representative uses showed higher toxicity to algae than for metyltetraprole and therefore this endpoint was selected for the risk assessment.

For the risk assessment for the active substance, a low risk to aquatic invertebrates (acute and chronic), algae and sediment dwelling organisms was indicated for all representative uses at either FOCUS step 1, 2 or 3. For the chronic risk to fish a low risk was indicated for all representative uses at FOCUS step 3 except for the D2 scenario for the representative use to winter cereals (early application, 2 x 120 g a.s./ha). A risk assessment using FOCUS step 4 PEC values was not available. Regarding the acute risk to fish, a high risk was indicated using the lowest of the available toxicity endpoints and FOCUS step 3 exposure estimates. However, since toxicity data were available for multiple species, these were used to plot a species sensitivity distribution and refine the acute Regulatory Acceptable Concentration (RACacute). 27 Using the refined RACacute, a low risk to fish was concluded for all representative uses using FOCUS step 2 exposure estimates.

The exposure assessment identified eight surface water metabolites (ISS7, de‐ClPh‐S‐2367, OHTM, S‐2367‐R1, S‐2367‐R2, S‐2367‐R6, 4‐chlorophenol, 28 hydroquinone25) and two sediment metabolites (4‐chlorophenol, hydroquinone) that require an aquatic risk assessment. For metabolite ISS7, suitable toxicity data were available for fish (acute), aquatic invertebrates (acute) and algae. For metabolites de‐ClPh‐S‐2367, OHTM, S‐2367‐R1, S‐2367‐R2, S‐2367‐R6 only acute fish toxicity data were available. Owing to the low acute toxicity of the metabolites relative to the active substance, chronic toxicity studies were not considered necessary. No toxicity data were available for metabolites 4‐chlorophenol and hydroquinone in the dossier.

For metabolite ISS7, a low acute risk to fish, aquatic invertebrates and algae was concluded on the basis of the available data and risk assessment. For metabolites de‐ClPh‐S‐2367, OHTM, S‐2367‐R1, S‐2367‐R2, S‐2367‐R6, a low acute risk to fish was concluded using a quantitative risk assessment. A low risk to aquatic invertebrates and algae was also concluded for these metabolites since fish were notably more sensitive to the parent substance. No reliable risk assessments were available for 4‐chlorophenol 29 and hydroquinone, in surface water or sediment, and therefore a data gap leading to an issue not finalised was concluded (data gap, see Sections 4 and 9.1).

For honey bees, toxicity studies were available for both the active substance and the formulation for representative uses (S‐2367 6EC), covering acute contact, acute oral and chronic oral (adults). Only an endpoint for S‐2367 6EC was available for honey bee larvae (chronic). The data indicated that the formulation for representative uses showed higher toxicity to bees than for metyltetraprole and therefore endpoints for the formulation were selected for the risk assessment. No data for accumulation toxicity or covering sublethal effects were available (data gap, see Section 10). Acute oral and contact toxicity data with metyltetrapole were also available for bumble bees.

A risk assessment according to EFSA (2013) was available which indicated a low risk to honey bees (acute oral, acute contact, chronic oral and honey bee larvae). No risk assessment according to European Commission (2002) were presented, but a similar conclusion could be expected as for the acute risk assessments performed in accordance with EFSA (2013). Available risk assessments also indicated a low acute oral and contact risk to bumble bees from the active substance.

Two plant metabolites were identified that require further consideration regarding the risk to bees (ISS7 and 3‐CH2OH‐S‐2367 glucose conjugate). The applicant provided argumentation to address the risk to bees from these two metabolites. However, the argumentation only covered the potential exposure to metabolites formed in the treated crop and did not cover other plants which may be contaminated (i.e. weeds, plants in the field margin or adjacent crops), therefore, a data gap was identified by EFSA (data gap, relevant for field uses, see Section 10). The RMS does not agree with the need for a data gap considering the argumentation presented in the DAR.

The toxicity to non‐target arthropods other than bees was investigated using formulation for representative uses (S‐2367 6EC). Specifically, tier‐I data on the two indicator species, Typhlodromus pyri and Aphidius rhopalosiphi, were available. Extended laboratory tests were available for A. rhopalosiphi and Chrysoperla carnea.

The tier‐I risk assessment indicated a low off‐field risk for all representative uses, but a high in‐field risk was indicated for A. rhopalosiphi. Nevertheless, the available tier‐II risk assessment was sufficient to conclude a low in‐field risk to non‐target arthropods.

Toxicity data for earthworms, other soil macroorganisms and soil microorganisms were available for the active substance and metabolite ISS7. Pending the data gap identified in Section 4 for the soil residue definition for risk assessment, further consideration of the risk to soil organisms may be required (see Section 4). No toxicity data for soil organisms were available for the formulation for representative uses. The experts at the meeting discussed and agreed that a data gap should be identified for such data 30 (data gap, see Section 10). The available risk assessments demonstrated a low risk to soil organisms from the active substance and metabolite ISS7 for all representative uses.

For the assessment of non‐target terrestrial plants, suitable toxicity data investigating the effects of exposure to the formulation for representative uses on seedling emergence and vegetation and vigour were available. On the basis of the available data and risk assessment, a low risk to non‐target terrestrial plants was concluded for all representative uses.

A low risk to organisms involved in sewage treatment processes was concluded for the representative uses.

ENDOCRINE DISRUPTION PROPERTIES

The endocrine disruption properties of metyltetraprole were discussed at the Pesticides Peer Review Experts' Teleconference 152 for Mammalian Toxicology and Ecotoxicology in November 2024.

With regard to the assessment of the endocrine disruption potential of metyltetraprole for humans according to the ECHA/EFSA guidance (2018), in determining whether metyltetraprole interacts with the oestrogen, androgen and steroidogenesis (EAS) and thyroid (T) mediated pathways, the number and type of effects induced; and the magnitude and pattern of responses observed across studies were considered. Additionally, the conditions under which effects occur were considered, in particular, whether or not endocrine‐related responses occurred at dose(s) that also resulted in overt toxicity. The assessment is therefore providing a weight of evidence analysis of the potential interaction of metyltetraprole with the EAS and T signalling pathways using the available evidence in the dataset.

With regard to T‐modality, the data set was considered complete, and a pattern of T‐mediated adversity was not identified.

For the EAS‐modalities, the dataset was considered complete, and a pattern of EAS‐mediated adversity was not observed.

Therefore, based on the available and sufficient dataset, it was concluded that the ED criteria are not met for the EATS‐modalities (Scenario 1a of the ECHA/EFSA, 2018 ED Guidance).

The outcome of the assessment reported above for humans also applies to wild mammals as non‐target organisms.

For the ED assessment of non‐target organisms (NTOs) other than mammals, an amphibian metamorphosis assay (AMA, OECD TG 231) and a fish short‐term reproductive assay (FSTRA, OECD TG 229) were available. In the AMA, statistically significant effects were observed only on the normalised hind limb length (nHLL) at day 7 and day 21 (decrease and increase at the middle and highest treatment, respectively). No significant effects were observed on the developmental stage. 31 Thyroid histopathology data were available only for the highest treatment, while data for the low and middle treatment were made available after the regulatory clock‐stop window, and therefore were not eligible for consideration in the ED assessment of metyltetraprole. Overall, considering the findings of the AMA, although some drawbacks/uncertainties were identified (see above), no pattern of effects that could be related to an ED Mode of Action through the T‐modality was observed.

In the FSTRA, statistically significant effects were observed on fish of both sexes on a number of EAS‐mediated and sensitive to, but not diagnostic of, EAS parameters. 32 Gonad histopathology data were made available only after the regulatory clock‐stop window, and therefore were not eligible for consideration in the ED assessment of metyltetraprole. After a preliminary evaluation of gonad histopathology, some changes were, nevertheless, noted. This additional data would require full consideration in the weight of evidence to draw a conclusion on whether the observed effects follow a pattern that may be related to an ED MoA through EAS‐modalities, although a concern cannot be excluded. It has also to be noted that no further information was provided (i.e. MoA analysis and/or further data) to contextualise the observed effects.

Overall, when considering the adverse effects observed in the FSTRA, a concern regarding the ED properties of metyltetraprole through the EAS‐modalities cannot be fully excluded for NTOs other than mammals. In order to reach a steady conclusion, the newly available data i.e. gonad histopathology would need to be properly evaluated and considered in the weight of evidence, including a MoA analysis (issue not finalised, see Section 9.1).

According to point 3.6.5 of Annex II to Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009, as amended by Commission Regulation (EU) 2018/605, it can be concluded that metyltetraprole is not an endocrine disruptor.

Based on the available information on NTOs other than mammals, the assessment of the endocrine disruption potential of metyltetraprole according to point 3.8.2 of Annex II to Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009, as amended by Commission Regulation (EU) 2018/605, cannot be concluded.
