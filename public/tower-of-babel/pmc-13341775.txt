Shared decision making using augmented reality a multicentre pilot randomised controlled trial in orthognathic surgery
Authors: S. J. C. Sander Tabernée Heijtmeijer, N. Noa Nicolai, N. G. Nard Janssen, J. Joël Kortes, B. Behrus Hinrichs – Puladi, A. Ashkan Rashad, O. Oliver Vladu, P. A. J. Peter Pijpker, M. J. H. Max Witjes, S. E. C. Sarina Pichardo, J. Joep Kraeima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Effective patient education is critical for informed consent. Augmented Reality (AR) offers a novel approach to improving patient understanding and satisfaction, although current evidence is limited and of low quality. This study evaluated the added value of AR compared to traditional monitor-based 3D models for patient education and decision making for orthognathic surgery. A multicentre randomised controlled trial was conducted between August 2023 and June 2024 at three university medical centres in north-west Europe. Sixty referred patients were randomised to either the intervention group or the control group. Patient satisfaction and knowledge were assessed using two questionnaires. The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov(NCT06140043). Patient satisfaction was significantly higher in the control group (p = 0.04). No significant differences were found in knowledge acquisition (p = 0.74). Women showed a significant preference for the monitor-based consultation (p = 0.01), while men did not show a significant difference in satisfaction. Despite some centre heterogeneity, no clear added benefit of AR in satisfaction or knowledge was visible. Observed effect sizes (d = 0.76) and post-hoc power estimates (84%) are provided for context but should be interpreted cautiously. In the absence of clear evidence a monitor remain the most practical option for patient education.

Introduction

Ethical medical practice requires informed consent; it is one of the most important fundamentals and is especially important for surgery. Patients need to make important decisions about how they want to be treated. Effective communication is essential between the patient and surgeon so that the patient’s preferences and values are respected1. Street Jr. et al.2 showed that communication is also associated with better overall health. It is essential for patients to understand the nature, benefits and risks of a treatment to make informed choices3. Informed consent is particularly important for elective surgery. Shared decision-making is thus a core component and promotes patient-centred treatment4,5.

Although physicians were once the primary source of information for patients who relied mainly on their expertise, today’s patients often seek additional details about symptoms, diagnoses, and treatments and use online platforms for a more comprehensive picture6. While artificial intelligence technologies such as large language models have made medical knowledge more accessible, they increase the likelihood of misinformation, which can cause confusion and alter patients’ understanding and expectations of their treatment7. Meanwhile, the time available for doctor-patient consultations has remained limited, increasing the need for physicians to effectively communicate essential information. Explaining diagnoses and treatments to patients can be made easier with 3D representations of human anatomy, including pathology and treatment options8. Extended reality (XR) devices, such as virtual and augmented reality (AR) technologies, offer promising tools for improving patient education through immersive, interactive representations of surgical procedures and outcomes9. Despite the increasing availability and accessibility of XR devices to healthcare providers10, their potential remains underexplored in a prospective and multicentre setting, and the current literature on its effectiveness in improving patient understanding and satisfaction is limited11,12.

Orthognathic surgery, also known as corrective jaw surgery, is an elective procedure that is performed worldwide and includes both functional and aesthetic considerations13. It is common in this field to use 3D virtual surgical planning, which provides access to detailed 3D models of the patient’s planned outcome. 3D models can improve the surgical planning process and the ability to effectively educate patients about their treatment options and expected outcomes14,15. It was hypothesised that this patient group would have a better understanding of their surgery after being informed with 3D models via XR. The difference between the two techniques was expected to be greater for practically trained patients, and differences in satisfaction and knowledge was anticipated to be greater for double jaw surgeries compared to single jaw surgeries. Finally, it was expected that satisfaction would correlate with age as older patients may find it more difficult to adapt to this modern technology and may be more critical of its use.

While XR has been studied for surgical planning and training in orthognathic surgery16, its use specifically for patient education has not been adequately evaluated. As an elective procedure already adapted to the use of 3D virtual planning, this patient group was selected as an example with a view to potential wider applicability. This study aims to address the limited evidence on the added value of XR for patient education by evaluating the use of AR. Specifically, this study investigates whether patient satisfaction and knowledge are improved when AR is used to inform patients undergoing orthognathic surgery. This is being examined in a multicentre randomised controlled trial, which compares the use of AR with patient education involving 3D models on a standard computer monitor.

Results

Patient recruitment

Between the 7th of August 2023 and the 5th of July 2024, a total of sixty participants were enrolled, with 30 randomised to the control group and 30 randomised to the intervention group. Participants were all evenly distributed across centres and groups. No participants were excluded or lost to follow-up after enrolment. Qualitative Analysis

The open-ended questionnaire responses were analysed qualitatively. Of the participants, 59 indicated that they would recommend additional education involving 3D models to other patients, while one participant did not respond to this question. Thematic analysis of their explanations and general comments revealed several recurring themes. Participants frequently emphasised that the 3D models improved their understanding of jaw deviations and the planned surgical procedures, providing a clearer picture than verbal explanations alone. They also perceived the treatment process as more transparent, and the models prompted new questions about their care.

Several suggestions for improvement were identified. Participants recommended including more illustrations and providing a step-by-step explanation starting with the external anatomy. They also suggested highlighting specific areas for patients to focus on, which is not currently feasible with the AR software used. Patient-specific models were preferred to demonstration models by one patient. Participants in the AR group also suggested improvements to model sharpness and transparency, which are influenced by the device, software and environmental conditions.

Overall, the general comments reflected a positive experience of the consultation and appreciation of the opportunity to engage actively in their education and decision-making processes.

Effect on patient satisfaction

For the primary outcome, there was a statistically significant difference in patient satisfaction, with higher scores given by the control group as measured by the 3DOS-Q, F(1,50) = 7.37, p = 0.01, partial η² = 0.13), and the group×centre interaction was not significant (F(2,50) = 0.29, p = 0.75), indicating the monitor advantage was consistent across centres. Centre‑adjusted estimated marginal means were 8.44 (Standard Error (SE) = 0.19; 95% CI [8.06, 8.82]) for the monitor and 7.69 (SE = 0.19; 95% CI [7.32, 8.07]) for AR. The adjusted mean difference was Δ = 0.75 (Bonferroni‑adjusted 95% CI [0.20, 1.30]), corresponding to a standardized effect of d = 0.76 (95% CI [0.20, 1.32]). With the centre-adjusted standardised effect size of d = 0.76 derived from the ANCOVA model and a sample size of n = 30 per group, the estimated post hoc power was approximately 0.84. The results of Spearman’s correlation with 1000-sample bootstrap CI revealed that there was no statistically significant correlation between age and satisfaction scores in the control group (n = 30, p = 0.66, 95% CI [ − 0.44, 0.28]). Similarly, no significant correlation was found between age and satisfaction scores in the intervention group (n = 30, p = 0.68, 95% CI [ − 0.44, 0.29]).

For the secondary outcome, the 1–10 general satisfaction score, there was a visible trend but no significant difference between the control group and the intervention groups, (F(1,49) = 3.30, p = 0.08, partial η² = 0.06). Centre‑adjusted estimated marginal means were 8.82 (SE = 0.22; 95% CI [8.38, 9.27]) for the monitor and 8.23 (SE = 0.23; 95% CI [7.78, 8.68]) for AR. The adjusted mean difference was Δ = 0.60 (Bonferroni‑adjusted 95% CI [-0.06, 1.26]), corresponding to a standardized effect of d = 0.51 (95% CI [-0.05, 1.08]).

Centre

Significant differences in satisfaction between centres were found. For the primary outcome, the 3DOS-Q score (F(2,50) = 6.04, p = 0.004), Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons showed participants in center C were more satisfied compared to participants from centre A by 1.27 points (95% CI [0.36, 2.17], p = 0.003; d = 1.28). Differences involving centre B were not significant (centre A vs centre B Δ = −0.61, 95% CI [ − 1.58, 0.35], p = 0.37; centre C vs centre B Δ = 0.65, 95% CI [ − 0.27, 1.58], p = 0.26). No significant differences were observed for the secondary outcome: the 1–10 general satisfaction score, centre A vs centre B by Δ = 0.72 points (95% CI [-0.42, 1.85], p = 0.38), centre A vs centre C by Δ = 1.05 points (95% CI [-0.02, 2.11], p = 0.06) and centre B vs centre C by Δ = 0.33 points (95% CI [-0.76, 1.42], p = 1.00).

Although the centre A – centre B contrast approached significance for the total objective knowledge score, centre differences did not reach conventional statistical significance after adjustment for multiple comparisons. The estimated marginal means (covariate-adjusted) were as follows: centre A = −1.26 (SE = 0.19, 95% CI − 1.65 to −0.87), centre B = −0.53 (SE = 0.20, CI − 0.92 to −0.13) and centre C = −0.77 (SE = 0.19, CI − 1.14 to −0.39). The omnibus test for centre showed a trend-level effect (F(2, 50) = 3.17, p = 0.05, partial η² = 0.11). Pairwise (Bonferroni-adjusted) contrasts were: centre A vs. centre B: Δ = −0.73 (SE = 0.30), p = 0.05, 95% CI: −1.47 to 0.01; centre A vs. centre C: Δ = −0.49 (SE = 0.28), p = 0.25, 95% CI: −1.19 to 0.20; centre B vs. centre C: Δ = 0.24 (SE = 0.29), p = 1.00, 95% CI: −0.47 to 0.94.

Gender

To examine differences in satisfaction scores between the control and intervention group regarding women and men, the Mann-Whitney U test was performed. The women showed a significant difference in 3DOS-Q scores between the two techniques (n = 36, p = 0.01, r = 0.43, 95% CI [0.11, 0.66]), indicating a preference for a consultation with the monitor. The difference in 1–10 general satisfaction score approached significance (n = 36, p = 0.08, r = 0.29, 95% CI [ − 0.04, 0.57]). In contrast, no significant differences were found for men in either 3DOS-Q scores (n = 24, p = 0.88, r = −0.03, 95% CI [ − 0.43, 0.38]) or 1–10 general satisfaction (n = 24, p = 0.30, r = −0.22, 95% CI [ − 0.58, 0.22]). The Mann-Whitney U Test showed no significant differences in satisfaction scores between women and men for both 3D techniques.

On testing the control group, neither 3DOS-Q scores (n = 30, p = 0.15, r = −0.26, 95% CI [ − 0.57, 0.11]) nor 1–10 general satisfaction (n = 30, p = 0.75, r = −0.06, 95% CI [ − 0.43, 0.31]) differed significantly for gender. Similarly no significant differences were observed for the interventions group’s 3DOS-Q scores (n = 60, p = 0.32, r = −0.19, 95% CI [ − 0.52, 0.19]) or 1–10 general satisfaction (n = 60, p = 0.75, r = −0.06, 95% CI [ − 0.43, 0.31]).

Education

Education level appeared to have a significant effect on only one subgroup’s satisfaction scores. The control group participants with an education level of 3 reported significantly higher 3DOS-Q scores (Median = 9.5) compared to the intervention group (Median = 8.0, n = 18, p = 0.02, r = 0.57). However, no significant differences were observed for other education levels regarding 1–10 general satisfaction scores with the two techniques. The 3DOS-Q satisfaction score, the primary outcome, was not significantly associated with education level, as determined by a Kruskal–Wallis test (n = 30, H = 9.80, p = 0.08). However, the 1–10 general satisfaction question showed a significant association with education level (n = 30, H = 12.89, p = 0.02), with those with higher education levels reporting higher satisfaction. In the control group, no correlation was found for both satisfaction scores (n = 30, H = 5.64, p = 0.34, for the 3DOS-Q scores) (n = 30, H = 5.97, p = 0.31, for the 1–10 general satisfaction scores).

Single or double jaw surgery

Regarding the participants who were educated for a single jaw osteotomy (LF1 or BSSO), the control group reported significantly higher satisfaction scores than the intervention group as tested by the Mann-Whitney U test: 3DOS-Q (Control (8.18, IQR 1.27) versus Intervention (7.45, IQR 1.82), n = 27, p = 0.01, r = -0.50, 95% CI [–0.74, –0.15] and secondary outcome (Control (9.00, IQR 2.00) against intervention (8.00, IQR 3.00), n = 60, p = 0.04, r = -0.40, 95% CI [–0.68, –0.01])). The total score for objective knowledge was not significantly different (n = 27, p = 0.40, r = -0.16, 95% CI [–0.47, 0.20]) for the single jaw osteotomy subgroup. Among those participants who were educated for a double jaw osteotomy (BIMAX), the control group scored higher on primary (n = 33, p = 0.41, r = –0.14, 95% CI [–0.45, 0.20]) and secondary outcomes (n = 33, p = 0.26, r = –0.20, 95% CI [–0.49, 0.13]), whereas the intervention group scored higher on total objective knowledge (p = 0.78, r = –0.05, 95% CI [–0.36, 0.29]). However, none of these differences were statistical significant.

Effect on patient knowledge

There were no significant differences between the groups in terms of knowledge when answering each individual objective question (n = 60, Q1: p = 0.69, r = -0.05 (95% CI -0.30 – 0.20), Q2: p = 0.23, r = -0.15 (95% CI -0.39 – 0.10), Q3: p = 0.70, r = -0.05 (95% CI -0.30 – 0.21), Q4: p = 0.13, r = -0.20 (95% CI -0.43 – 0.06)), as well as for the total score (n = 60, p = 0.74, r = -0.04 (-0.29–0.21), determined by the Mann-Whitney U test.

Discussion

In this multicentre, randomised, controlled trial, the use of AR to educate patients with 3D models undergoing orthognathic surgery was compared to patient education with a standard computer monitor. The trial included 60 Dutch and German participants, equally divided across the two techniques and three centres. Contrary to the hypothesis, patient satisfaction was higher in the control group educated with 3D models on a standard monitor. This difference was significant for women, whereas men showed insignificantly higher satisfaction in the intervention group with AR. In terms of knowledge, no significant difference was observed between the two groups. Overall, this study found no clear added value of educating patients using XR technology, as it did not lead to higher satisfaction or better understanding of the diagnosis and treatment.

In contrast to our hypothesis and the findings of a systematic review by Urlings and colleagues9 on the added value of AR in patient education, this study obtained higher satisfaction scores from the control group on viewing a regular monitor. However, all participants in the both the intervention group and the control group recommended the technique. Van der Kruk and colleagues17 found in a scoping review that virtual reality, another XR technology similar to this context’s AR, was effective as an educational tool. In contrast, our study yielded an opposite result, probably because the control group was also shown 3D models. It is important to note that our study specifically evaluated the impact of AR, rather than the effect of displaying 3D models. Sezer and colleagues18 compared AR with 3D printed models on patient knowledge, and their results led to a recommendation to use physical 3D models over AR. Consistent with our study, the intervention group, which was AR educated on their prospective procedure, noted lower satisfaction scores compared to the control group.

The results of this study demonstrate that satisfaction with 3D imaging techniques differs between genders. Women may perceive the monitor and AR techniques differently, as indicated by the significant difference in 3DOS-Q scores; 1-10 general satisfaction score was not significantly different but suggests a trend worth exploring. This trend may also point to varying levels of preference for the techniques based on gender. Future studies could use qualitative follow-up methods, such as interviews or focus groups, to gain a deeper understanding of gender-based perceptions and experiences of using technology for patient education. In contrast, there were no significant differences in satisfaction among the men between the two techniques, with a trend in the opposite direction whereupon the intervention group scored higher than the control group, suggesting a more consistent experience or preference. It should also be noted that the lower level of satisfaction observed in the AR group may have been influenced by the participants’ limited familiarity with AR interfaces, rather than by the technology’s intrinsic educational value. Factors such as patient discomfort, device ergonomics or interface complexity may also have contributed to these results. Additionally, factors such as cognitive overload resulting from the immersive AR environment and the novelty effect may have influenced satisfaction and knowledge acquisition. This highlights that unfamiliar technology can initially distract from learning objectives. Future research could clarify these aspects through a structured qualitative usability study. In the present study, qualitative insights were derived from open-ended questionnaire responses. However, only limited feedback was provided regarding the practical use of the AR headset and software from the patient perspective. One participant reported that the visualisations appeared insufficiently sharp and somewhat vague under the regular lighting conditions of a standard outpatient consultation room. Despite setting the device to maximum brightness, ambient lighting appeared to affect image clarity. This could potentially be improved by slightly dimming the room lights or by using an AR headset with a brighter display in future implementations. Satisfaction may also be influenced by other patient or demographic factors, such as age and education level. Nevertheless, it is important to note that, despite there being a significant difference in satisfaction scores between the two techniques, only the participants with education level 3 demonstrated a significant difference in scores. Due to the relatively small number of participants in each educational subgroup, hard conclusions cannot not be drawn. Further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to understand better how educational background and other demographic factors might influence patient satisfaction with 3D imaging techniques. This RCT may have lacked the statistical power to detect subtle yet meaningful differences in satisfaction scores. With 30 participants per group, the study was primarily designed to detect relatively large standardised differences. Based on the centre-adjusted effect size from the ANCOVA model (d = 0.76), the post hoc power was approximately 0.84, which indicates that there was sufficient power to detect the observed difference. A considerably larger sample size would have been required to reliably detect smaller differences in patient satisfaction. Therefore, while the trial was adequately powered to detect large effects, it may have lacked the power to detect smaller, yet potentially clinically meaningful, improvements. Although the observed effect size was relatively large, post hoc power analyses should be interpreted with caution. As this pilot trial was not powered prospectively, the results should be considered primarily informative for the design of future, adequately powered studies, and exploratory. Future studies should use the pooled standard deviation reported here to perform an a priori sample size calculation and recruit larger sample sizes. This is particularly important for subgroup analyses, which were exploratory in this study due to the small sample size.

Since AR education requires more input from medical professionals, it is recommended that patients be educated using 3D models displayed on a computer monitor. The participants were included during their first visit after referral, a method designed to minimise differences between participants and thereby reduce the potential for bias. Consequently, demonstrative models were used to display the diagnosis and the proposed treatment to the participants, with the models being specific to the occlusion class and the proposed treatment. Patient-specific models were not available yet, as any required scans are made at a later stage in the treatment. We hypothesise that patient-specific models will result in elevated satisfaction scores and could lead to higher satisfaction in the AR group, because seeing one’s own simulated face may be more meaningful to the patient. However, as these models become available, it is possible that, patients may no longer consider consultation necessary given that they will have received sufficient explanation. From a clinical practice perspective, therefore, integrating AR into routine workflows requires careful consideration. The hypothesized added value of AR must be balanced against the additional time, technical requirements, and training needed for effective implementation. In the present study, AR was not fully integrated into the consultation process. This design choice enabled the educational component to be evaluated more clearly, ensuring that patient satisfaction primarily reflected the quality of the explanation provided rather than other aspects of the consultation. Future research should therefore focus on evaluating the feasibility of integrating AR systems into standard consultation workflows, as well as assessing their impact on patient comprehension, shared decision-making, consultation efficiency, and overall patient satisfaction.

The participant group, i.e. orthognathic patients, provided a controlled and highly relevant context in which to evaluate patient education tools. However, the specific characteristics of this population, such as elective treatment planning, familiarity with 3D imaging and the presence of multiple treatment options, may limit the generalisability of the findings. To broaden the applicability of the findings, future research should include other patient populations, particularly those undergoing acute or less elective procedures, or those with lower baseline exposure to digital health technologies. Investigating a wider range of clinical settings will help determine whether the observed effects of AR and monitor-based education hold across different medical contexts and patient needs.

The AR consultation was implemented as an additional, separate consultation rather than being fully integrated into the standard clinical workflow. This design choice was made to minimise confounding factors and allow clearer comparisons between the AR-based intervention and the monitor-based control condition. This ensures that patient satisfaction primarily reflects the educational modality rather than differences in consultation structure or duration. However, we acknowledge that future studies should evaluate the integration of AR directly into routine consultations to better reflect real-world implementation. Such studies should also assess whether AR could improve the efficiency of consultations, for example by reducing the time spent on patient education.

The study utilised a commercial software from a single provider for both the AR and monitor group, thereby ensuring a controlled comparison with minimal differences between them. The approach minimised differences between groups and provided a more standardised evaluation of AR intervention. It is expected that these findings can be generalised to all AR applications if they have the same display capabilities. However, small differences may occur because different software may produce different results. Moreover, the use of commercially available medical software may limit the reproducibility of the study, as access to this particular software may be limited or subject to updates that modify functionality over time. In terms of costs, the AR-based consultation required dedicated hardware (a head-mounted display costing approximately €3500) as well as software licensing fees, since medically certified software was used. The licensing costs were comparable between AR and monitor-based consultation. Open-source software alternatives could reduce direct software costs, but this would need to be balanced against the additional time required for development, validation and clinical personnel.

While the learning curve for patients and clinicians was not formally assessed in this study, the AR application was specifically designed for medical use and aimed to be intuitive for users and educators alike. Future work should systematically investigate learning curves, usability and training requirements, as these factors are likely to influence the scalability and adoption of AR in routine care.

In addition, this study was conducted using the HoloLens 2, which will receive critical security and functionality updates until December 2027, and therefore will not be available for clinical use after that date. Nevertheless, the underlying principles of AR-based patient education are not limited to this device and can be applied to other AR headsets. Therefore, the findings of this study can probably be generalised to similar AR devices, provided they offer similar visualisation capabilities.

Although this study found that AR-based education does not currently outperform traditional 3D visualisation on a monitor, the potential of AR should not be overlooked as the technology continues to evolve. Our observations during the study suggest that the possibility of using two AR devices simultaneously or an alternative method in which the image is controlled externally, could be useful. Combined with patient-specific content, future developments in interface design, display quality and interactivity may significantly enhance AR’s role in patient communication and shared decision-making. The level of participant interaction with the AR software varied considerably, from passive viewing to using almost all available features. Unfortunately, interaction was not systematically tracked, so the potential impact on patient satisfaction and knowledge could not be analysed. Future studies should track the extent of participant interaction to better understand the added value of AR for education and satisfaction. It is expected that participants who are more able to interact with the technology will be more satisfied.

In this study, knowledge acquisition was assessed using four objective questions tailored to the specific surgical context. These addressed key aspects of understanding, such as the purpose of treatment, the reason for surgery, the associated risks and how the jaw moves. Although this instrument was carefully designed to align with the information provided during the consultation, it was not formally validated. Future studies should consider adopting or developing validated tools aimed specifically at assessing surgical understanding, and providing a more detailed description of their scope and structure would enhance reproducibility and comparability across studies.

Another limitation of this study is that we did not assess the impact of the education technique on parents or caregivers. As many of the participants included in the trial were young adults, the role of their caregivers could have been crucial to their understanding and overall experience of AR-based education. Parents and guardians often play an important role in reinforcing medical information, guiding decision-making, and providing emotional support. Their perceptions of AR interventions could influence how well young patients retain information and how satisfied they feel with the educational experience. Future research should consider including the perspective of caregivers to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the effectiveness of AR in patient education.

An additional limitation is that the study could not resolve the potential influence of the surgeon and researcher on patient satisfaction. The surgeon and researcher should remain constant and switch between centres, but this was not deemed possible for our study because of utilising centres with two different languages. An attempt was made to minimise bias by keeping the teams as small and constant as possible. The scores’ analyses found significant differences in results between centres, suggesting that factors beyond the AR intervention itself - such as the way the information was presented by the surgeon or researcher - may have influenced patient satisfaction. Differences in communication style, clarity of the explanation, or overall interaction with the participant may have played a role. Future research should investigate how these factors contribute to patient education outcomes to understand the true added value of AR better, and should also focus on the generalisability of this technique to other surgical procedures and patient groups.

In conclusion, the present study found no clear benefit of AR in the context of patient education. Despite some heterogeneity between centres, the advantage of using a monitor was consistent, and a post-hoc analysis demonstrated a substantial effect size of 0.76 with 84% power, which supports the robustness of this finding. The use of AR in a consultation setting was more complicated than the conventional monitor that is currently in use. Therefore, standard 3D models displayed on a monitor appear to be the most feasible and effective approach for supporting patient understanding and facilitating shared decision-making. While future research with larger and more homogeneous samples may clarify the potential role of AR further, the current results provide no evidence of added value over 3D models on a monitor.

Methods

Study design

A multicentre, parallel-group, superiority randomised controlled trial was performed between August 2023 and June 2024 at three university medical centres (RTWH Aachen, Germany, UMC Utrecht, the Netherlands, and UMC Groningen, the Netherlands) in northwestern Europe. The Medical Ethical Committees of the aforementioned hospitals reviewed the study and deemed it exempt from a full ethical review (METc Groningen 2023/524, METc Utrecht 23U-0394, Ethical commission of the medical faculty of the RWTH Aachen University EK 23-382). Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from each participant. The study was conducted and reported according to the CONSORT guidelines. The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT06140043. Each centre had a dedicated trained researcher who provided education using the monitor or AR glasses. In the intervention group, participants were educated with AR glasses, and in the control group, they were educated with a standard monitor. Patients were involved in the design and conduct of this study during the pre-validation of the validated patient satisfaction questionnaire19.

Participants

Included in the study were all patients in the specified period part of the routine orthognathic surgery work-up, patients were over 16 years and with dentofacial deformity (dysgnathia) including class II malocclusion, class III malocclusion, or open bite referred for a (Bilateral Sagittal Split Osteotomy (BSSO, a mandibular osteotomy), Le Fort 1 (LF1, a maxillary osteotomy), or Bimaxillary Osteotomy (BIMAX, both maxillary and mandibular osteotomy)). The planned treatment was either with or without a genioplasty procedure. Patients with craniofacial syndromes, such as cleft lip and palate or with an osteotomy involving two or more segments, were excluded.

Randomisation and masking

The study followed a parallel group design. After informed consent, participants were randomly assigned to one of two groups according to a 1:1 random allocation rule: (1) Control group (monitor group) and (2) intervention group (AR group). The study workflow is shown in Fig. 2, and an example of patient education for each group is shown in Fig. 3. Randomisation was stratified according to the study site. Due to the nature of the intervention, it was not possible to blind participants, the treating surgeons, or researchers. Procedures

First, participants were seen according to the standard protocol. At this stage of treatment (intake consultation), the protocol was similar in all centres. After the standard consultation, the participants were asked by the specialist to participate in the study. If informed consent was given, participants were taken to another consultation room for an additional explanation to clarify the surgical procedure. The diagnosis of the participant and the surgical procedure were clarified by showing and explaining 3D sample models matching the participant’s indication and planned surgery. The method of presenting the images differed between the groups: in the control group, the 3D models were presented on a monitor using the Mimics web Viewer application (Materialise, Leuven); in the intervention group, the 3D models were presented in AR through the HoloLens 2 using the Mimics Viewer XR application (Materialise, Leuven). The AR education was provided in the standard lighting conditions of an outpatient consultation room.

Patient education tasks using 3D models were categorised as either skeletal or soft tissue (see Table 3). Skeletal tasks included demonstrating relevant anatomical structures and the planned surgical procedure (including osteotomy lines), as well as associated risks such as nerve injury. Soft tissue simulations showed the expected postoperative changes to the face. Models of the current anatomy and the planned postoperative situation were shown for both groups. The 3D models could be rotated and zoomed to facilitate explanation and understanding by the patient. The educational content was identical for both groups and followed a standardised script. This began with an explanation of skeletal anatomy, followed by an explanation of the surgical plan and associated risks, and concluded with a simulation of the expected soft tissue outcome. To minimise bias related to consultation duration, explanations were limited to 20 minutes for both groups. Outcomes

After the consultation, the participant was asked to complete two short questionnaires: (1) The 3DOS-Q, a validated patient education questionnaire for Dutch and German speakers with 3D models to measure the patient satisfaction19; and (2) A custom- designed questionnaire with objective questions (see Supplementary Note 1) to assess the acquired knowledge about the jaw deviation and surgery. The aim of this instrument was to evaluate whether participants had comprehended the key aspects of their treatment, including (1) the underlying reason for surgical intervention, (2) the jaw involved and to be treated, (3) the associated risks, and (4) the direction of the surgical movement. This approach allowed an objective assessment of whether patients had correctly understood what would occur during the procedure and why it was necessary. In addition, patients were asked whether they would like to explain their answers regarding whether they would recommend the use of the educational consultation (yes or no), and space was provided for general comments. The primary outcome was the overall satisfaction score based on the eleven Likert-scale questions of the 3DOS-Q. Secondary study parameters included the 1–10 general satisfaction score and the knowledge score based on the knowledge questionnaire. Study parameters comprised the centre, occlusion class, age, gender and education level of the patient, and the proposed treatment.

Statistical analysis

The study aimed to include sixty participants, with ten participants per group in each of the three participating centres. As there was no previous literature available on the use of AR education or a specific questionnaire for orthognathic surgery patients, no formal sample size calculation was performed and no formal a priori power analysis was conducted. The expected effect size of AR in patient education was considered small, so the sample size chosen aimed to balance feasibility and statistical relevance to enable useful comparisons while keeping the study practical and manageable. A post-hoc power analysis was performed to assess the sensitivity of the study design using the centre-adjusted standardised effect size, which was derived from the ANCOVA model accounting for between-centre variability. Power was estimated from the corresponding non-central F distribution for the ANCOVA (α = 0.05, n per group).

The statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Differences in the results of the questionnaire were evaluated between the control and intervention. Results are presented as mean and standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data and as median and interquartile range for non-normally distributed data. Findings are presented with effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Appropriate statistical tests were used to assess group differences based on the data distribution. Potential confounding variables (age, gender, centre, or treatment plan) were considered and accounted for.

To determine the relationship between participant age and satisfaction with the use of 3D medical imaging techniques during the consult, Spearman’s rank-order correlation was conducted separately for both control and intervention group. The analysis aimed to examine whether age was associated with satisfaction levels for each technique. As the sample size was not formally calculated, subgroup analyses (e.g. by gender or level of education) were considered exploratory. They were primarily interpreted as indicating potential trends rather than definitive effects.

Role of the funding source

For a portion of the study, the Mimics Viewer XR license was unrestrictedly provided by Materialise (Leuven, Belgium). The funder played no role in the study’s design, data collection, data analyses, data interpretation, or writing of the report.
