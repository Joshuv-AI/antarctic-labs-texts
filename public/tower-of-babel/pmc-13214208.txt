Description of the Hospitals’ transition towards a culture of magnet attraction in Finland
Authors: Anu Nurmeksela, Kristiina Junttila, Asta Heikkilä, Marja Kaunonen, Tiina Kortteisto, Riitta Suhonen, Tarja Kvist
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Magnet Model framework has been a model for developing attractiveness in hospitals. This study aimed to describe the development of Finnish hospitals from 2008 to 2021 to meet the culture of the Magnet Model. This study provides a novel way to describe similarities and differences from a longitudinal perspective in addition to combining qualitative and quantitative aspects.MethodsA descriptive multi-method study design was used. Data on patient satisfaction (n = 2566) and nurse job satisfaction (n = 2707) in 2008–2009 were collected from four acute care hospitals in Finland. Furthermore, in 2021, data on patient satisfaction (n = 14401) and nurse job satisfaction (n = 5823) were collected from six acute care hospitals and were supplemented with interviews of nurse leaders (n = 9) in 2023.ResultsSurveys showed that patient satisfaction has reached an excellent level, whereas nurse job satisfaction has declined in recent years. According to nurse leaders, the attractiveness of hospitals has been systematically developed in accordance with the Magnet Model principles and structures.ConclusionsMagnet Model principles provide a foundation for improving nurse job satisfaction and patient outcomes, but full implementation requires a clear nursing leadership framework, visible authority, and coherent strategic actions. Sustaining Magnet Model work further depends on an organizational culture that values evidence-based leadership, shared decision-making, and adequate resources to support longterm -changes.Implications for nursing managementThe study indicates that despite efforts to strengthen transformational leadership and leader visibility, improvements in nurse job satisfaction require deeper cultural change, particularly in shared decision-making and professional autonomy. Developing hospital attractiveness, therefore, calls for systematic, organization-level support—such as participatory structures, adequate resources, and supportive leadership.

Introduction

The attractiveness of hospitals to nurses has been the object of development and research in US since the 1980s. In this study, attractiveness refers to nurses’ perceptions of the hospital as a desirable workplace with organizational characteristics, such as supportive leadership, professional autonomy, adequate resources, and opportunities for development, that align with principles of the Magnet Model. In the 1990s, the Magnet Recognition Program® [1] was created by the American Nurses Credentialing Center® (ANCC) based on a model of transformational leadership, excellent professional performance, new knowledge, innovation and quality improvement, functioning structures and processes, and nursing-sensitive outcomes [1, 2]. The first hospital received Magnetic Hospital recognition in 1994. As of February 2026, 605 hospitals around the world have received recognition, two of which are in Finland [1].

Hospitals in Finland have also been developed and researched following the Magnet Model principles since the 2010s, and the first wave of interest in developing attractive hospitals already emerged over 10 years ago [3, 4]. Thus, a new wave of development is now occurring [5]. According to a recent study, nurse managers are ready for it and their expectations for the future of their work are in line with the Magnet Model [6]. In 2024, two hospitals were the first in Finland to achieve Magnet Hospital® status [7]. These hospitals are included in the data collection for this study. This study describes the development of Finnish hospitals from 2008 to 2021 to meet the culture of the Magnet model.

Background

The main principle of a Magnet Model is transformational leadership. Transformational leadership, as defined initially in 1999 by Bass [8], is a process whereby leaders inspire and motivate followers to exceed expectations by fostering vision, intellectual stimulation, and individualized consideration, ultimately transforming values and goals for higher performance. Consistently demonstrating positive impacts across multiple domains, it is strongly associated with greater nursing job satisfaction, stronger organizational commitment, improved team collaboration, and enhanced work climate [9–11]. It also contributes to better staff health and well-being by reducing emotional exhaustion, stress, and burnout, while fostering empowerment and intent to stay [12]. Furthermore, transformational leadership drives productivity and effectiveness, encouraging extra effort and improved organizational outcomes, making it a critical leadership style for creating healthy work environments and achieving sustainable performance in healthcare [9–12]. The American Nurses Credentialing Center® characterizes transformational leaders as those leaders who are translating the vision into action by helping others reach their highest potential [1]. Transformational leadership practice should be evident throughout an organization―from the chief nurse officer to the front-line personnel. The Chief Nursing Executive is critical when it comes to gaining staff trust, coaching future leaders, and creating rewarding networks of multi-professional teams [13, 14].

Nursing work environments in Magnet hospitals are safer and are associated with higher quality than in non-Magnet hospitals [15]. A good work environment [16] has been associated with reduced nursing shortages, emotional exhaustion, and burnout compared with non-Magnet hospitals [15–17]. In a study by Stone et al. (2019), nearly 90% of nurses were satisfied with their current position in Magnet hospitals and were highly committed to their employers [17].

Moreover, the nursing-sensitive patient outcomes in Magnet and non-Magnet hospitals differ [18]. For example, the rates of patient mortality, inpatient falls, hospital-acquired infections, and pressure ulcers are reported to be lower in Magnet hospitals than in non-Magnet hospitals [15, 18]. Compared to patients in non-Magnet hospitals, patients in Magnet hospitals have been more satisfied [16] and have been willing to recommend the hospital [19]. Patients have also been satisfied with communication with nurses at Magnet hospitals [19].

The Magnet Model principle of excellence has resulted in Magnet hospital nurses being more educated [16] and possessing more evidence-based practice knowledge than their non-Magnet hospital counterparts [2]. The Magnet Model principles of new knowledge, innovation and quality improvement, and functioning structures and processes underline the significance of developing and maintaining a shared governance model as well as empowering nursing staff to question their current care processes to improve patient outcomes [18]. Finally, Magnet hospitals have also been found to provide care more cost-effectively [15].

A recent Finnish study indicates that nurses’ engagement has declined in recent years [20]. Junttila et al. (2023) showed that leadership and professional autonomy play a key role in supporting engagement. In other Finnish studies, higher satisfaction with leadership, particularly regarding support, communication, and involvement, significantly increased engagement, and positive perceptions of leadership also strengthened nurses’ autonomy [21, 22]. Pursio et al. (2024) further found that work environments have a significant impact on professional autonomy and job satisfaction [22]. Professional autonomy has been identified as a central predictor of engagement [21], underscoring the importance of empowering nurses through participative leadership and increased decision-making authority to promote a committed and engaged workforce.

With credit to the active actions of nurse leaders, the Consortium for the National Benchmarking of Nursing-Sensitive Outcomes (HoiVerKe) started in 2016 in Finland and has since been joined by 18 wellbeing services counties, covering 78% of the Finnish public healthcare sector. The goal of national benchmarking is to create a system for measuring, producing, evaluating, and developing the quality of nursing in Finland as part of the quality work of social and healthcare organizations. Unified nursing-sensitive quality indicators have been in use since 2018 [23]. Therefore, in this study, patient satisfaction and nurses’ job satisfaction have been measured with different metrics in 2008–2009 and 2021; consequently, comparisons across time are limited by the use of different measures and contextual changes. Methods

This study used a descriptive multi-method study design.

Data collection

In November 2008 and February 2009, patient satisfaction data were obtained through paper surveys distributed both by post and during the discharge process, where nurses or unit secretaries invited patients to provide feedback on the nursing care they had received [3]. In 2021, patient satisfaction data were obtained quarterly via paper and electronic surveys administered to hospitalized patients at discharge and to ambulatory care patients during their clinic appointments. Nurse job satisfaction data were collected through electronic surveys in four acute care hospitals in Finland in November 2008 and February 2009 [3] and through electronic surveys conducted in six acute care hospitals between March and April 2021.

In both study periods (2008–2009 and 2021), data were collected from university hospitals, representing tertiary healthcare, and central hospitals, which provide secondary healthcare. Secondary healthcare includes specialist consultations, diagnostic tests, and treatments that cannot be performed in primary care. Tertiary healthcare, on the other hand, is delivered in university hospitals and focuses on highly specialized services such as complex surgeries, cancer treatments, and rare disease management. It also involves advanced diagnostics, research, and teaching functions [24]. All participating hospitals are located in urban areas, with bed capacities ranging from 400 to 4,600 [25].

In both study periods (2008–2009 and 2021), patient satisfaction surveys were available for adult patients, pediatric patients, and for the parents of pediatric patients. In this study, only the responses from adult patients were included. The nurses’ job satisfaction surveys were administered to registered nurses at all levels, including midwives and nurse leaders. In 2021, nurses received by email a personal link to the survey or an open link was distributed to them by the nurse manager of each unit. Due to differences in recruiting eligible respondents, the exact response rate cannot be calculated. This data is register data and is available with permission from the organization maintaining it. In addition, the qualitative interview data were collected between June and August in 2023 by interviewing current and retired nurse leaders (n = 9) from hospitals that participated in data collection in 2008–2009 and or in 2021. The data used in this article included 142 coded expressions (Fig. 2). The rest of the data have been reported in a separate article [26].

In this study, different instruments were used at different time points. Consequently, comparisons over time are constrained by variations in measurement tools and contextual changes. Therefore, any observed differences should be interpreted as indicative trends rather than direct equivalence across cohorts. Notably, the major national social and healthcare reform in Finland was under preparation during the nurse and patient data collection in 2021. However, the reform was not implemented until early 2023, after our follow‑up data collection had been completed. However, the nurse leader interviews were conducted in summer 2023.

Instruments

The data of nurse job satisfaction (n = 2707) were collected using the Kuopio University Hospital Job Satisfaction Scale (KUHJSS) [3, 27] and patient satisfaction (n = 2566) data with the Revised Humane Caring Scale (RHCS) [3, 28] in 2008–2009 (Fig. 2). The data was collected within the project The Attractive and Safe Hospital project (At Safe) [29]. The Kuopio University Hospital Job Satisfaction Scale (KUHJSS) was developed through a multistage research process that began with comprehensive literature reviews and continued with expert panel evaluations and pilot studies. The initial version of the scale was based on the literature review and subsequently refined using findings from pilot studies [3, 27] and feedback from expert panels [30]. The KUHJSS includes 37 items divided into 7 subscales (Table 1).

The original Humane Caring Scale (HCS) was developed at Kuopio University Hospital in the early 1990s, when a dedicated working group used quality‑assurance methods to create an instrument for assessing humane caring [31, 32]. The concept of humane caring was first explored through a literature review in 1991, which identified a range of human needs and approaches to caring. Since its development, the HCS has been used widely to evaluate the quality of care from the patient’s perspective. It has also undergone further revisions [28, 33], resulting in the Revised Humane Caring Scale (RHCS), which was shown to be practical in contemporary settings in a 2007 pilot study. In the present study, the RHCS was used to measure patient satisfaction. The RHCS includes 42 items divided into 6 subscales (Table 1).

The items of both scales (KUHJSS and RHCS) were rated on a 5-point scale (5 = strongly agree, 1 = strongly disagree). In previous studies, Cronbach’s alpha values for KUHJSS fell between 0.641 and 0.950 [27, 34], and between 0.775 and 0.970 for RHCS [3, 30, 34].

In 2021, the comparison data on nurse job satisfaction were collected from nursing staff (n = 5823) using the modified Nurse Engagement Survey (mNES) (Fig. 2) [20]. The Nurse Engagement Survey (NES) was originally developed by the Nursing Executive Centre [35] in collaboration with the Global Centre for Nursing Executives, part of the Advisory Board Company [36]. The original NES survey was translated into Finnish and Swedish and validated in one university hospital in Finland [21]. Before final use, the survey was modified (mNES) in collaboration with the American Nurses Credentialing Center (ANCC) to include all relevant aspects for Magnet Hospital accreditation purposes [21]. The mNES measurement contains 53 items that are rated on a 6-point Likert scale (strongly agree = 6, agree, slightly agree, slightly disagree, disagree, strongly disagree = 1). The 53 items comprise 8 work-related drivers of engagement (subscales 1–8) and a subscale of personal engagement (subscale 9), which is not included in this study’s data (Table 1). The reported Cronbach’s alpha values for the total mNES scale were 0.91 and 0.92 in previous studies [20, 21].

In 2021, patient satisfaction data were collected from adult patients (n = 14401) in six acute care hospitals using the Nursing Sensitive Patient Reported Experience Measure (NsPREM) instrument (Fig. 2). The NsPREM was initially developed by Helsinki University Hospital [37] with versions for adult patients, children, and children’s parents. The instrument was further developed by the Consortium for the National Benchmarking of Nursing-Sensitive Outcomes (HoiVerKe), with items derived from the national patient satisfaction questionnaire, official patient complaints, and relevant literature, including Kvist et al. [3]. The NsPREM-instrument for adults includes 21 items with nine themes (Table 1) and uses 5-point response options (5 = completely agree, 1 = completely disagree with the option “Does not apply”). No demographic questions were included. The Cronbach alpha value for the total NsPREM was 0.960. More information about this instrument will be published in different articles.

In this study, no demographic information about participants (patients, nurses, and nurse leaders) is reported. Outcomes for job satisfaction and patient satisfaction in 2008–2009 were defined as excellent when the mean score was 4.0 or higher [3] as well as in 2009 data on patient satisfaction. However, for nurses in 2021 to be considered excellent, the mean score needed to be 4.50 or higher [21] because of the different scale.

Interview

A purposive and targeted sampling approach was employed for the interviews. The inclusion criteria were as follows: having a nurse leadership position, having experience in Magnet Model-related development work within the past 5–10 years, and being employed at the participating hospital during the relevant development period. Participants were excluded if they did not hold a nurse leadership position (e.g., staff nurses, assistant nurses, non‑nurse leaders). Twelve nurse leaders were individually invited to participate in this study by email that included a statement about the study. A total of nine (n = 9) nurse leaders agreed to be interviewed. Interviews were conducted and recorded by one researcher (AN) as an individual online or face-to-face interview in summer 2023. The themes of the interview were based on the principles of the Magnet Model and the development of attractive organizations based on these principles. An example of an interview question is “What kind of changes have been observed in your organization in nursing and patient outcomes, such as nurse job satisfaction and patient satisfaction?” The first interview served as a pilot; however, as no questions required modification, it was included in the final dataset. The interviews took an average of 55 min. Data saturation was reached when no new concepts or ideas emerged from the research perspective [38]. The main body of the interview data is published in another article [26].

Data analysis

Descriptive methods were used in the analysis of the dataset, as only previously published information from the 2008–2009 materials was available. The aim was to obtain indicative trend data on nurse and patient satisfaction. Survey data are described by indicators (percentages, mean). Total mean values were calculated for all data (patient satisfaction (RHSC, NsPREM) and job satisfaction (KUHJSS, mNES). This study is primarily descriptive and trendoriented, as differences in the measurement instruments across time points prevent direct inferential comparisons. As scales in instruments vary, the percentage (%) from the ratio of the mean value and the maximum range value of the range was calculated, which made the descriptive comparison possible (Table 1). The results of the 2008–2009 data and 2021 data were compared at the total mean for each scale (RHSC, NsPREM, KUHJSS, and mNES). Only fully completed survey responses were included in the quantitative analysis because missing data on key variables would have limited the comparability and reliability of the results. Cases with several unanswered items could not be meaningfully incorporated without introducing uncertainty or weakening the validity of the statistical analyses. Although this approach strengthened the internal consistency of the dataset, excluding incomplete responses may introduce selection bias if individuals who skipped items systematically differ from fully completed respondents, for example, those with heavier workloads or lower satisfaction. Such bias may affect the representativeness of the analytic sample and the generalizability of the findings. Sensitivity or robustness checks were considered but could not be meaningfully conducted due to the small number and inconsistent pattern of missing responses.

A deductive and inductive analysis was used in the qualitative data from interviews [38]. In this study report, the qualitative analysis focused on three themes: the path to an attractive hospital; patient satisfaction; and nurses’ job satisfaction. The analysis was conducted by one researcher (A.N.), who holds a PhD and has experience and expertise in nursing leadership and qualitative research, which supported the use of a single‑coder approach. This researcher carried out the coding, categorization, and interpretation of the data. In total, 142 original expressions were identified. To mitigate potential subjectivity, we strengthened the description of the following strategies: (1) Systematic documentation of the analytical process, including coding decisions, category development, and interpretative steps, was documented in detail to ensure transparency and traceability, was conducted. (2) The primary researcher maintained reflexive notes throughout the analysis to critically examine potential preconceptions and their influence on interpretation. (3) Research team involvement was established in preliminary findings. Coding logic and category structures were regularly reviewed and discussed with the research team, all of whom hold PhDs and have nursing expertise. These peer debriefings enhanced credibility and provided an additional layer of analytical observation.

Findings

Patient satisfaction

A total of 2,566 patients participated in the study in 2008–2009, and 14,401 patients participated in 2021. The results show that patient satisfaction has improved in all hospitals (Table 2; Fig. 3). This is evident when comparing patient satisfaction of Hospitals 1, 2, 3, and 4 by the mean values of the scale and mean share (%) of maximum range in 2009 to data of Hospitals 5, 6, 7, and 8 in 2021. In 2009, mean values of patient satisfaction were between 4.11 and 4.31 (82%-86%) and in 2021 between 4.67 and 4.77 (93%-95%). Hospitals 1 and 2 measured patient satisfaction both in 2009 and 2021. In Hospital 1, patient satisfaction increased from 86% to 95% and in Hospital 2 from 82% to 94% when measured as the mean share of the maximum range. In 2009, patient satisfaction was already at an excellent level, and it was even higher in all hospitals in 2021. No markable differences were observed comparing hospitals together.

Nurse job satisfaction

A total of 2,707 nurses participated in the study in 2008–2009, and 5,823 nurses participated in the survey in 2021. The mean values of nurse job satisfaction ranged between 3.39 and 3.72 in 2008–2009 in Hospitals 1–4. When comparing nurse job satisfaction mean share (%) of the maximum range in 2008–2009 and 2021 in all hospitals [1–8], a decrease can be observed from 68% to 74% in 2009 to 65–70% in 2021. A clear decrease was revealed in Hospital 1, which fell from 72% to 65%. A comparison of nurse job satisfaction in 2021 revealed that Hospital 6 (70%) had a 5% higher job satisfaction rate than Hospital 1 (65%). None of the hospitals received an excellent level of job satisfaction. In the KUHJSS the level of excellence was over 4, and in the mNES for nurses to be considered content, the value needed to be greater than 4.50 (Table 2; Fig. 3).

In interviews, nurse leaders (n = 9) stated that nurse job satisfaction had weakened over the last few years, despite the development of nurses’ structural empowerment and the promotion of nurses’ career paths. According to nurse leaders, many changes in society (e.g. COVID-19 pandemic, the nursing shortage, the nurses’ strike in 2022, the public healthcare economy, and the major reform of social and healthcare in Finland) have influenced nurses’ job satisfaction. The path to an attractive hospital – Nurse leaders’ experiences

According to interviewed nurse leaders (n = 9), The Attractive and Safe Hospital (At Safe) project, initiated under the guidance of the Ministry of Social Affairs and Health in Finland in 2009, was a motivational step toward an attractive hospital. The project was described by nurse leaders as including components aimed at reforming management practices and developing well-being at work. Furthermore, nurse leaders described that these components included action plans to increase the effectiveness and attractiveness of nursing care through improved management and to enhance quality and well-being at work in the health services sector using management strategies.“The direction of the attractive hospital probably started around 2010, that is, when there was this national attractive healthcare, where our hospital had one sub-project.” NL6

Discussion

This study described the development of Finnish hospitals from 2008 to 2021 to meet the culture of the Magnet Model. The results show that hospital attractiveness has been developed systematically according to Magnet Model principles in Finland for over ten years. Patient satisfaction has risen to an excellent level; however, nurse job satisfaction has decreased over the past few years. In interpreting our findings, we consider patient satisfaction and nurse job satisfaction in relation to a Magnet Model culture. However, it is important to note that the observed associations between Magnet Model-related structures, patient satisfaction, and nurse job satisfaction are based on observational data and may be confounded by external factors such as the COVID-19 pandemic, staffing shortages, and ongoing system-level reforms.

Organizational and leadership structures supporting Magnet culture.

In this study, nurse leaders considered organizational and leadership structures as essential for supporting Magnet Model culture. Interviewed nurse leaders stated that the hierarchical structure of the nursing leadership line, encompassing the presence of authority within the organization, along with participatory development structures, are prerequisites for hospital attractiveness today. Powerful nurse leaders are essential because they provide guidance, stability, and strategic direction that help healthcare professionals adapt effectively to the continual changes taking place in healthcare environments [1]. It is important that nurse leaders work at all levels of organizational decision-making, from strategic planning to implementation [14, 26]. This also means that nurse leaders need an equal position with other professionals at the top management level in healthcare organizations to initiate policies and strategies and make decisions [26]. In this study, the visibility of nurse leaders in top decision-making and with clinical care was observed. However, this is not directly reflected in the nurse job satisfaction survey results.

Equally important is interprofessional collaboration and the enablement of nurses to engage in professional shared governance [26]. Structural empowerment has been associated with better nurse well-being, including higher job satisfaction and lower levels of emotional exhaustion [39]. Empowerment is often discussed as a leadership approach associated with reduced burnout and improved nurses’ well-being, which may also relate to certain indicators of patient care quality [39, 40].

In this study, nurse leaders described the effort they have put in to increase transformational leadership at all nursing leadership levels. Previously, transformational leadership was weaker than other nursing outcomes (nurse job satisfaction and patient satisfaction) in Finnish hospitals [3, 4]. Embracing transformational leadership requires a profound cultural shift toward shared leadership, open communication, and continuous learning. This is because inclusive structures succeed only when nurses also develop the internal sense of responsibility and autonomy needed to support meaningful change [41]. Increasing nurses’ autonomy at work may increase their engagement to work, as they can influence their work and its development themselves [41].

Patient-centered outcomes and patient satisfaction.

Our survey findings suggest that Finnish patients report a high level of satisfaction with the care they receive, consistent with observations from earlier studies [4, 30, 42]. In Finland, everyone is entitled to adequate health and social services, and healthcare is regulated by legislation [43]. However, some non-urgent patient groups may have to wait long periods to receive services in the public sector, but when they get the care, they are very satisfied [44, 45]. The interviews revealed that hospitals have promoted patient-centered care in many ways. Patient safety culture has also advanced [46], which may contribute to patients’ satisfaction with the quality of care. Interventions to implement evidence-based practices are also being conducted [47]. The novelty in recent years is the increasingly active participation of patients, for example, in customer panels and activities as experts by experience [48] which also came out in this study’s results. In this study, patient satisfaction remained high despite the COVID-19 pandemic in 2021.

The increase in patient satisfaction according to surveys during the study period may reflect broader system‑level improvements in care processes and service quality, as nurse leaders described in interviews. At the same time, such developments may also have increased nurses’ workload and stress, particularly if expectations for efficiency and patient flow rose more rapidly than the available resources or staffing. It is also possible that the Magnet Model implementation process produces positive outcomes for patients earlier than it does for staff. Nurse leaders’ reported improvements in patient‑centered care processes and practices may be reflected in patient experiences relatively quickly, whereas positive effects on nurses’ work environments, job satisfaction, or professional development may require more time. This potential divergence underscores the importance of examining patient and staff outcomes of simultaneously examining in future research.

Nurse job satisfaction challenges and contextual factors.

In the interviews with nurse leaders, the influence of changing society on nurse job satisfaction was highlighted. A recent study found that US Magnet hospitals were characterized as having too few nurses and unfavorable work environments and had higher rates of nurse and physician burnout and turnover [49]. As a solution, they required that leaders act to remedy the insufficient nurse staffing, insufficient workload control, and poor work environments [49]. However, Lal (2021) has stated that in today’s challenging and complex environment, the Magnet Model addresses many of the principles that could diminish stress, thus leading to a stronger, better-balanced clinical and leadership workforce [50]. Perhaps today’s healthcare environment also needs tools and action models by which to respond to sudden changes, for example, to increase resilience in workplaces [49]. In this study, nurse leaders identified one solution to improve nurses’ engagement: the need for work–life-related changes through flexible scheduling (e.g., reduced shift lengths, increased availability of part-time roles, and remote or hybrid work options for certain tasks) to meet current employee needs. This requires offering greater flexibility based on the individual needs of the nursing staff.

During the job satisfaction survey data collection in 2021, the COVID-19 pandemic was still ongoing and was strongly affecting nursing and nurses’ job satisfaction worldwide [51]. For example, in one Finnish study [52] nurses were less engaged in nursing work during the pandemic than before. In addition, high job satisfaction was associated with lower intentions to leave their job, both before and during the pandemic. Similar results on nurses’ work engagement have been revealed in studies in different countries [53, 54]. It is also important to consider other factors that may have influenced the results during the COVID-19 pandemic—such as personal factors like resilience and organizational factors like resources—which have been found to be associated with job satisfaction and engagement [52–54].

Implications for the Magnet Model and future development.

The study survey and interview findings support the need for systematic approaches to the development of hospital attractiveness based on Magnet Model principles. Magnet Model structures are an excellent foundation for such development. This interpretation aligns with preliminary positive experiences from the first Finnish Magnet hospital, although this study did not examine such outcomes directly.

In the future, nurse leaders should concentrate on increasing their visibility among nurses to foster job satisfaction. Moreover, the influence of participatory structures on nurses’ experiences of autonomy and job satisfaction should be further promoted. Finally, effective interventions are needed to enhance nurses’ resilience in the ever-changing healthcare environment. Organizational-level interventions are primary and essential structural and cultural principles within nurses’ work environment. Supportive leadership, adequate staffing, shared governance, and access to professional development create stable foundations required for resilience. Nurse-level interventions, such as training in coping strategies, leadership skills, and self‑leadership, can strengthen individual capacity only when broader structures are established.

Limitations

Different instruments were used to measure nurse job satisfaction (KUHJSS in 2008–2009 and mNES in 2021) and patient satisfaction (RHCS in 2008–2009 and NsPREM in 2021) in comparison years because in Finland unified nursing-sensitive quality indicators have been in use only since 2018. The response rate of KUHJSS was 47% and of RHCS 36%. However, for the 2021 data, the response rate was not determined because the exact number of distributed surveys could not be confirmed, as the mNES and NsPREM was originally collected for developmental, not research, purposes of the study hospitals. These instruments are built upon partly different theoretical frameworks, item structures, and response formats (scales), which may influence how respondents interpret and answer the items. However, similar themes appear with the instruments in comparison (Table 1), and only the trend in total mean score values was compared. Because the measurement instruments varied between time points, the findings should be interpreted as descriptive trends rather than inferentially comparable results, which limits the extent to which changes over time can be statistically evaluated. Only two hospitals were the same in both measures because the Consortium for the National Benchmarking of Nursing-Sensitive Outcomes (HoiVerKe) collaboration started in 2016 and has been joined by 18 out of 23 well-being services counties as of early 2026 [55], and at the time of data collection in 2021, not all hospitals were yet involved in common data collection. However, interviews with nurse leaders confirm, refine, and enrich the results of survey data.

Although the interviewed informants (n = 9) all had experience with nursing development toward a Magnet culture and data saturation was achieved, the possibility of selection bias remains [38]. Nurse leaders who are already engaged in or supportive of Magnet Model‑related development may have been more willing to participate, potentially overrepresenting positive perceptions of organizational culture or readiness for Magnet Model principles. This may limit the diversity of viewpoints captured and constrain the extent to which the qualitative findings reflect the experiences of leaders who are more critical, less involved, or differently positioned within the organizational hierarchy. Future qualitative research should aim for a broader sampling strategy, including leaders with varying levels of Magnet Model exposure and differing organizational roles, to enhance the representativeness and robustness of the evidence.

One limitation of the study is that the qualitative data were coded by a single researcher, which may introduce subjective interpretation and reduce reliability. Although the analytical process was carefully documented and discussed with the research team, the use of one coder may have influenced the final interpretations.

In addition, all outcomes in this study relied on self‑reported satisfaction measures, which are susceptible to response bias and may not accurately reflect actual care quality or workforce conditions. The absence of objective patient and workforce indicators, such as survival rates, 30 days readmission, hospital-acquired infections, incidence of pressure ulcers, and personnel turnover, limits the ability to determine whether higher satisfaction aligns with measurable improvements. Future research should incorporate such objective metrics to strengthen the validity of findings.

The study was conducted solely in urban Finnish hospitals, which operate within a specific publicly funded health system. This limits the generalizability of the results to rural areas or other countries with different healthcare organizational structures, resources, or workforce models. Comparative studies across more diverse healthcare settings are needed to assess the broader applicability of the detected associations in this study.

Conclusions

Nurse leaders are in a key position to promote hospital attractiveness. Magnet Model principles provide a strong foundation for enhancing both nurse job satisfaction and patient outcomes. Although the organizational structures required for the Magnet Model have been established, its full implementation is still in progress. Systematic development further requires a clearly defined nursing leadership framework that includes visible authority within the organization and coherent strategic action plans. A successful Magnet Model implementation ultimately depends on an organizational culture that consistently values evidence-based leadership, professional autonomy, and shared decision-making. This cultural foundation cannot be sustained without adequate resource allocation, including sufficient staffing, supportive leadership structures, and time dedicated to professional development. At the same time, Magnet Model work represents a long-term change process that demands clear communication, active engagement of key stakeholders, and continuous follow-through to address implementation challenges. Together, these elements are essential for achieving and maintaining Magnet Model principles.
