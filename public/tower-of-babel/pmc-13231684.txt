Omphalocele with a patent vitelline duct: intraoperative identification of an uncommon presentation
Authors: Zoe Paige, Jay Kerecman, Sean Barnett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOmphalocele with a concomitant patent vitelline duct is a rare presentation, with very few cases reported in the literature. Most reported cases present with visible bowel prolapse, meconium drainage or mucosal exposure at birth. Presentations without obvious intestinal herniation or mucosal exposure are extremely uncommon and pose diagnostic challenges.Case presentationA male neonate was born to a G3P2 mother at 38 + 6/7 weeks via spontaneous vaginal delivery after a pregnancy notable for a 20-week prenatal ultrasound concerning for omphalocele. Upon delivery, the omphalocele membrane was intact. Shortly after birth, the neonate developed persistent bilious emesis. Abdominal ultrasound demonstrated normal rotation, and abdominal X-ray was reassuring without evidence of obstruction or volvulus. No other congenital anomalies were demonstrated after VACTERL workup, including echocardiogram, abdominal and spinal ultrasounds. Genetic testing for Beckwith-Wiedmann syndrome was found to be normal. Surgical exploration revealed a patent vitelline duct fistula connecting the small bowel to the omphalocele sac. The fistula and adjacent small bowel were resected, followed by primary end-to-end anastomosis and primary fascial closure. The postoperative course was uncomplicated, and the neonate was thriving without any evidence of infection or obstruction at 2-month and 6-month follow-up visits.ConclusionThere have been very few cases of omphalocele with a patent vitelline duct reported in the literature, and those that have been described present with either visible mucosal prolapse or intestinal extrusion at birth. This case demonstrates that a vitelline duct anomaly may remain clinically occult and present only with obstructive symptoms such as bilious emesis. In neonates with omphalocele who develop gastrointestinal obstruction despite unrevealing imaging studies, clinicians may entertain the possibility of a concurrent vitelline duct remnant as a part of their differential diagnosis. Recognition of this association may guide operative planning and improve diagnostic awareness of this uncommon embryologic anomaly.Keywords: Omphalocele, Patent vitelline duct, Patent omphalomesenteric duct, Case report

Introduction

An omphalocele is a congenital midline abdominal wall defect in which the viscera herniate into a membranous sac composed of peritoneum on the innermost layer and amnion on the outermost layer, with Wharton’s jelly in between. In normal embryologic development, the midgut temporarily herniates into the umbilical cord around the sixth week of gestation due to rapid intestinal growth and returns to the abdominal cavity by approximately the tenth week, allowing closure of the ventral body wall [1]. An omphalocele arises from failure of the herniated midgut to return to the abdominal cavity [1]. Prenatal ultrasound can typically identify omphalocele by approximately 18 weeks gestational age, with a reported sensitivity of roughly 75% [2]. In the United States, the incidence of isolated omphalocele is estimated to be 1.5–3 per 10,000 live births [3].

During early embryologic development, the midgut is in communication with the yolk sac via the vitelline duct (also commonly referred to as the omphalomesenteric duct). In normal development, the vitelline duct involutes during early gestation and ultimately becomes a fibrous ligament [4]. Failure of the vitelline duct to involute results in an abnormal patency, which can lead to herniation, hemorrhage, intussusception and obstruction [4]. Prenatal identification of vitelline duct anomalies is uncommon, although rare cases of Meckel’s diverticulum with significant dilation have been described on prenatal ultrasound [5, 6].

Concomitant presentation of omphalocele with a patent vitelline duct is rare, with only a small number of cases reported in the literature (Table 1) [7–20]. Most cases present with visible mucosal prolapse or intestinal extrusion through the duct at birth. Cases without obvious bowel prolapse are particularly rare and may be difficult to recognize prior to surgical exploration. We report the clinical presentation and intraoperative identification and management of a neonate with omphalocele and a concomitant patent vitelline duct.

Case report

On a 20-week fetal anatomy survey, a male fetus was found to have an umbilical cord mass with an echogenic rim superior to the fetal cord insertion, thought to be consistent with an umbilical cord hernia or a fetal omphalocele (Fig. 1a, b). A fetal echocardiogram was performed and was unremarkable and amniotic fluid level was normal. Cell-free DNA testing was negative for associated t13, t18 and t21 abnormalities. The pregnancy course was otherwise complicated by maternal obesity and uterine fibroids, and fetal growth restriction was distinctly absent.

a) Twenty-week anatomy ultrasound with sagittal view of the abdominal wall defect, likely omphalocele. b) Abdominal wall defect, containing intestine

The pediatric surgery team was consulted prenatally for recommendations and discussed the possibility of reducing the abdominal defect surgically or treating the membrane with Silvadene cream and allowing granulation to occur, if the defect was found to be too large for initial reduction.

The male neonate was born at 38 + 6/7 weeks via primary vaginal delivery to a G3P2 mother. The neonate’s birth weight was 3.295 kg (47th percentile for gestational age). Upon delivery, cord clamping was delayed for one minute, and the infant was placed in a bowel bag in preparation for transport to the NICU. APGAR scores were 8 at 1 min and 9 at 5 min. There was no evidence of any open defect in the omphalocele membrane, and the delivery was otherwise uncomplicated (Fig. 3a and b).

a, b) Omphalocele with intact membrane

The neonate was promptly transferred to the NICU and was hemodynamically stable and breathing comfortably on room air. The omphalocele was wrapped in normal saline-soaked gauze until the defect could be further evaluated by the pediatric surgery team. Ampicillin and gentamicin were initiated due to the open abdominal defect and the increased risk of neonatal sepsis.

On his first day of life, the neonate experienced multiple episodes of moderate bilious emesis, and his abdomen was noted to be full but soft. Abdominal ultrasound and radiography ruled out malrotation as a cause of the obstructive symptoms (Fig. 2). He was voiding and stooling normally. He was made NPO and required intermittent nasogastric suctioning with a Replogle tube for persistent bilious emesis, a finding atypical for the classic omphalocele presentation. A percutaneously inserted central catheter (PICC) was placed for parenteral nutrition.

Abdominal radiograph unremarkable for signs of malrotation and obstruction

On initial examination, the pediatric surgery team identified an omphalocele with an intact membrane (4.5 × 4 cm) (Fig. 3a, b). Management included daily dressing changes with Vaseline gauze and Kerlix until the day of surgery. While in the NICU, he underwent a full workup for associated VACTERL abnormalities, including a chest X-ray, echocardiogram, spinal and renal ultrasounds, which demonstrated no associated anomalies. Given the known association between omphalocele and Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome, additional testing of the 11p15.5 region was performed and was normal. The neonate demonstrated no clinical features of Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome.

In the operating room, the omphalocele membrane was opened at the thinnest point, located on the left lateral side, and carefully dissected away from the fascia. The small bowel was noted to be strongly adherent to the fascia at a central point, but no Ladd’s bands or evidence of malrotation were noted. Along the antimesenteric border of the small bowel, there appeared to be an intact vitelline duct fistula to the omphalocele membrane. As this was dissected, a hole within the antimesenteric border of the bowel was identified. The entire membrane was removed, and a hole in the small bowel corresponding to the location of the vitelline duct was observed (Fig. 4a). The adjacent small bowel was thickened but intact. The patent vitelline duct and thickened small bowel were resected (4.5 cm in length) until normal- appearing small bowel was encountered both proximally and distally (Fig. 4b, d). The free ends of normal bowel were brought together with an end-to-end hand-sewn anastomosis, which was noted to be widely patent and came together without complication, as the ends were very proportional in size (Fig. 4c). The small bowel single-layer mesenteric defect was then approximated. Finally, the bowel was reduced into the abdomen, and the fascial defect was closed primarily without any changes in airway pressure, and a neo-umbilicus was created. The location of the primary anastomosis was in the expected position for a patent vitelline duct.

a) Exteriorized fistula with thickened small bowel proximally and distally. b) Small bowel after fistula resection. c) End-to-end anastomosis of small bowel d) Resected small bowel and fistula

Post-operatively, the neonate did exceptionally well. He was initially treated with an orogastric tube to suction and bowel rest. Small volume feeds were initiated on post-op day 4 and were advanced as tolerated. By post-operative day 6, the PICC line was removed, and the neonate was tolerating full enteral intake by mouth and stooling well. He was discharged on day of life 11, post-operative day 7, with a corrected gestational age of 40 + 3/7.

The pathology report of the surgical specimen was consistent with intraoperative findings. The membranous portion was described as inflamed and fibrotic membranous tissue consistent with omphalocele membrane and the suspected diverticulum specimen revealed a portion of the small bowel with acute serositis, features of a fistula tract and bowel wall outpouching, consistent with a patent vitelline duct. Chromosomal microarray analysis later identified a duplication of 17q12, a variant which has not been associated with omphalocele but has been reported in association with learning disabilities, delayed language development, delayed motor milestones, and a range of psychiatric and neurologic features. Placental pathology was positive for focal acute chorionitis, subchorionitis, and umbilical vasculitis, that was deemed irrelevant to the clinical presentation or abdominal anomaly at the time of discovery.

At 2-month and 6-month follow-up visits with Pediatric surgery and NICU graduate clinic, the infant was thriving. He was gaining weight appropriately, feeding well by mouth, and showing no signs of infection or complications. The surgical site was healed with no signs of dehiscence or infection, and there was no abnormal abdominal distension or concerns related to bowel function.

Discussion

Omphalocele with a patent vitelline duct is a rare presentation, with only a few cases reported in the literature in the past 70 years (Table 1) [7–20]. Most reported cases of omphalocele associated with a patent vitelline duct describe a conspicuous postnatal finding, typically characterized by visible mucosal fistula or persistent drainage from the sac, as reported in earlier cases by Allen and Fitchett, Gratiot, Ng, and Chattopadhyay [7, 8, 13, 14], or by prolapsed bowel through the duct, as described by Davis and Kehm, Storms et al., Blair et al., Panait et al., and Lone et al. [9, 11, 12, 15, 16]. In contrast, this case demonstrates a more clinically occult presentation, in which the vitelline duct remnant was not externally apparent and instead manifested with signs of intestinal obstruction despite initially reassuring imaging. We noted that the initial presentation of bilious emesis requiring nasogastric suction was atypical for the classic presentation of omphalocele. Isolated omphaloceles are usually functionally normal, while this neonate was presenting with signs of obstruction [7–20]. Midgut volvulus was initially considered, as a recent multicenter study has found it to occur at a rate of 4.4% in those with omphalocele, which is approximately 67 times the rate of occurrence in general [21]. However, abdominal ultrasound and radiography were unremarkable, and intraoperatively, no Ladd bands were observed. The definitive diagnosis of concomitant patent vitelline duct was made intraoperatively and confirmed upon pathologic examination of the surgical specimen.

Surgical approach for this patient included fistulectomy and primary anastomosis upon intraoperative discovery of a patent vitelline duct. In a review of existing case reports discussing management of omphalocele with a patent vitelline duct, the surgical approach commonly consisted of excision of the fistula with or without ileal resection and primary anastomosis, with single stage closure of the abdominal defect [7–20]. Previous reports have identified post- operative complications in approximately 25% of patients, including presentations such as paralytic ileus, anastomosis breakdown, septicemia and small bowel obstruction [19]. There were no post-operative complications following surgical correction for this neonate.

The presentation of omphalocele has been associated with a variety of different anatomic abnormalities including cardiac, urogenital, neural tube and musculoskeletal defects [22–24].

Omphalocele has also been associated with Beckwith Weidman syndrome in 6.3% of cases. Trisomy 13, 18 and 21 frequently coexist with omphalocele, with trisomy 18 being the most common necessitating thorough imaging and genetic testing [24]. For this neonate, the workup for co-occurring anatomic anomalies was unrevealing. Additionally, there were no remarkable findings on genetic workup for associated syndromes and commonly associated aneuploidies, apart from an incidental finding of a 17q12 duplication, which has a known association with the development of learning disabilities, delayed motor milestones, and onset of psychiatric illnesses later in life.

A literature review of previous case reports found the incidence of co-occurring congenital anomalies to be less frequent (43%) in patients with omphalocele and a patent vitelline duct [19], versus classic omphalocele 71% [25], suggesting that a complete genetic workup may be less likely to identify other anatomic and genetic abnormalities when patients present with both anatomic defects. The genetic and imaging workup for common concomitant anomalies was unrevealing in this case, consistent with the finding that other anatomic and genetic anomalies are less common when both omphalocele and patent vitelline duct are present, versus omphalocele alone. However, due to very limited literature, we continue to recommend full workup for the genetic aneuploidies and anatomic anomalies associated with the classic presentation of omphalocele.

It has been proposed that the presentation of omphalocele and patent vitelline duct may be a result of mechanical obstruction of the ability of physiologically herniated midgut to return to the embryonic cavity, in the presence of the vitelline duct abnormality [7, 20]. This theory is supported by the finding that associated genetic abnormalities and syndromes are less frequently identified in the cases of concurrent omphalocele and patent vitelline duct, as opposed to isolated omphalocele. Another theory suggests that the complex interplay of signaling cascades found to guide differentiation and specification of mesenchyme during ventral wall closure, may be interrupted in the presence of the patent vitelline duct [26, 27]. Interruption of signaling cascades may subsequently impact the ability of the physiologically herniated midgut to reduce and signal for ventral wall closure. It is unclear whether there is a mechanical or molecular basis of these concurrent anomalies. Further research should be done to elucidate the basis of this process.

Limitations and strengths

Description of the management and outcome for this neonate has limited direct application to management of future patients with similar presentations due to the narrative nature of case reports and should not be used to directly guide management. This case report will contribute to the small pool literature wherein management of omphalocele with a patent vitelline duct is described and illustrated. We hope that this report will further the development of a broad understanding and recognition of this rare presentation.

Conclusion

While prior limited literature reports visibly exposed mucosa or intestinal prolapse as the hallmark of omphalocele with patent vitelline duct, this case highlights that a patent duct may remain concealed and present with obstructive symptoms such as bilious emesis, despite unrevealing imaging. This expands the known clinical spectrum of omphalocele-associated vitelline duct anomalies and underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion in neonates with omphalocele who develop gastrointestinal obstruction without a clear radiographic etiology. We recommend that the presence of bowel obstructive symptoms in patients with known omphalocele pre-operatively should be interrogated and could lead one to entertain concomitant vitelline duct remnant as part of the differential diagnosis. Further research is necessary to elucidate the mechanical and molecular mechanisms involved in this rare presentation, as well as the strength of association with other genetic and anatomic anomalies. We also recommend a full workup for associated aneuploidies and anatomy anomalies prior to surgical intervention, given the limited body of literature on occurrence of concomitant anomalies.
