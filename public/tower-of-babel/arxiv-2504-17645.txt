A common first integral from three-body secular theory and Kepler billiards
Gabriella Pinzari 1 , Lei Zhao 2

Abstract
. We observe that a particular first integral of the partially-averaged system in the secular theory of the three-body problem appears also as an important conserved quantity of integrable Kepler billiards. In this note we illustrate their common roots with the projective dynamics of the two-center problem. We then combine these two aspects to define a class of integrable billiard systems on surfaces of constant curvature.



1.
Introduction

In a Euclidean space of dimension at least
2
, the Kepler problem is super-integrable: It possesses more independent first integrals for the system to be integrable in the sense of Liouville. Concretely, in addition to the total energy and the angular momentum, additional conserved quantities are provided by the components of the Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector. Dynamically, this implies that the system is properly-degenerate: All bounded, non-singular orbits are closed. Out of
d
-frequencies (in a proper set of Action-Angle coordinates), only
1
-frequency is non-zero. This proper-degeneracy of the Kepler problem is the main reason for many degeneracies in celestial mechanics. This makes it hard to study perturbations, but also leaves the possibility of considering systems with varying Kepler orbits, in a discrete or a continuous way, without destroying the integrability. Moreover, this can even be made with fixed Keplerian energy.

The function

(1)

D=C^{2}-2hA_{1}

serves as a conserved quantity for two types of these systems recently studied, in which
C
is the total angular momentum,
A_{1}
is a component of the Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector, and
h
is a parameter:

—

Keplerian orbits evolving under a partially-averaged Newtonian potential, in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. This has been noticed by the first author in
[
16
]
. In this situation, up to certain normalization,
h
denotes half of the distance between the origin and the position of the second, motionless particle.

—

In a planar Kepler billiard system, with a line as a wall of reflection. This has been first proved by Gallavotti-Jauslin in
[
9
]
. In this case,
h
denotes the distance from the Kepler center to the wall, and
A_{1}
is the vertical component of the Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector with respect to the wall of reflection.

The study in
[
16
]
is continued in
[
17
,
18
,
20
]
toward further applications to the study of three-body problem. An apparently different first integral for close encounters in the regularized circular restricted three-body problem has been discussed in
[
12
,
7
]
, respectively for the planar and spatial case.

The existence of
D
in the study of Gallavotti-Jauslin
[
9
]
is used to disprove an ergodic assertion in a paper of Boltzmann
[
2
]
. This has intrigued many studies ever since
[
10
,
25
]
. The function
D
is shown to hold as well for Kepler billiard systems with a branch of conic section as reflection wall, provided that the Kepler center lies at a focus of the conic section,
h
is the focus-center distance of the conic section, and
A_{1}
the component of the Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector along the axis of foci
[
22
,
23
]
.

In particular, in the system that Gallavotti-Jauslin considered, it is helpful to put an additional massless Kepler center symmetrically with respect to the line of reflection. This line can be considered as a degeneracy along a family of confocal hyperbola with foci at the Kepler centers. Then
h
is again the half distance between the Kepler centers.

A main observation of this article is that the appearance of
D
in both of systems can be understood in terms of projective dynamics
[
1
]
. Consequently we make several further generalizations:

—

In
[
16
]
, the partially-averaged Newtonian potential is defined with respect to a Kepler map. We propose a coordinate-free formulation.

—

We generalize the theorem of
[
16
]
on the sphere, and in the hyperbolic space.

—

We generalize
[
16
]
to the partially-averaged sum of a Newtonian potential and a properly placed Hooke potential, for the system defined in a Euclidean space, on a hemisphere, and in the hyperbolic space.

—

We define new integrable mechanical billiard systems based on the partially-averaged system.

This note is organized accordingly.

2.
The two-center problems

2.1.
Two center problems on constant curvature spaces

We consider the two-center problem in an Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In this system, a moving particle of unit mass moves in the space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
under the attractions of two fixed Kepler centers. This system is an integrable system, already known to Euler
[
4
]
and Lagrange
[
11
]
for
d=2,3
. Indeed rotations around the axis of the centers provides
d-2
first integrals commuting with the energy in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by Noether’s theorem, and thus the integrability of the system follows from one additional non-trivial first integral, which has been identified by these authors.

This type of problems can be imposed on the sphere as well. The spherical 2-center problem describes the motion of a particle moving on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
attracted by two pairs of antipodal centers of Kepler-Coulomb type, generated from potentials proportional to the cotangent of the central angle. The identification of such 1-center potentials of Kepler-Coulomb type is made to preserve the Kepler law on a spherical universe as much as possible, in particular to preserve the elliptic law. This was done by Graves
[
8
]
and Serret
[
21
]
. The two-center problem in the hyperbolic 3-space has been studied by Killing
[
13
]
.

2.2.
Projective Dynamics of the Two-Center/Lagrange Problems

In
[
1
]
, Albouy gave an explanation that the additional non-trivial first integral of the two center problem in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is ultimately related to the energy of a
corresponding system
defined on the sphere, which is the spherical two center problem on a hemisphere of
\mathbb{S}^{d}
.

We recall the construction more precisely. We consider
\mathbb{S}^{d}
embedded as unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, with
\mathbb{R}^{d}
embedded as the hyperplane
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{-1\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. The lower hemisphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}_{SH}:=\{(x_{1},\cdots,x_{d+1})\in\mathbb{S}^{d},x_{d+1}<0\}
of
\mathbb{S}^{d}
is thus projected to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
via central projection from the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. If the centers of the two center problem are put symmetrically with respect to the contact point of
\mathbb{S}^{d}
with
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{-1\}
, then this projection also project unparametrized orbits of this spherical problem to unparametrized orbits of the two-center problem in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, defined with respect to a modified metric
\|\cdot\|_{a}
given below
[
1
]
. The spherical energy thus induces an additional first integral for the two-center problem in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and vice versa, the energy of the Euclidean two-center problem is an additional first integral for the spherical two-center problem. Moreover, we may as well put a harmonic potential in the middle of the two Kepler centers and all these hold for the modified system, called the Lagrange problem
[
11
]
as well. This can as well be defined on
\mathbb{S}^{d}_{SH}
, with a potential proportional to the squared tangent of the central angle as the spherical harmonic potential.

We denote the masses of the two Kepler centers and the string constant of the Hooke center in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
m_{1},m_{2},f
respectively. We put the centers in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
at
(0,\cdots,0,\pm a)
. In
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the systems are defined with respect to the affine norm
\|(v_{1},\cdots,v_{d})\|_{a}:=\sqrt{v_{1}^{2}+\cdots+v_{d-1}^{2}+\frac{v_{d}^{2}}{1+a^{2}}}
.

We recall the following proposition from
[
1
,
22
]
:

Proposition 2.1
.

The Lagrange problem in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the metric from the norm
\|\cdot\|_{d}
and with parameters
(m_{1},m_{2},f)
and on
\mathbb{S}^{d}_{SH}
with the round metric inherited from
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
and with parameters
(m_{1}\sqrt{1+a^{2}},m_{2}\sqrt{1+a^{2}},f)
are in projective correspondence.

These systems, as well as those defined on
\mathbb{S}^{d}_{SH}
are thus integrable. Similar result holds between systems defined in
\mathbb{S}^{d}
and in
\mathbb{H}^{d}
as well. We refer to
[
22
]
for precise statements.

For the problem in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote the energy of the problem as
E_{Eucl}
. We consider
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as a chart for the lower hemi-sphere
\mathbb{S}_{SH}
and we denote by
E_{sph}
the spherical energy in terms of positions and velocities, so as a function on the tangent bundle
T\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Note that Legendre transformation allows to identify the tangent and cotangent bundles. In the sequel we shall also regard them as functions on cotangent bundle when necessary.

2.3.
Relationship among first integrals for the two center problem

We consider the two center problem and argue in the case
d=2
. Due to the rotation invariance the formulas hold for any
d\geq 2
as well.

We put the Kepler centers at
(0,a)
and
(0,-a)
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, with masses
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
respectively. We equip
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{a}
, defined as
\|(u,v)\|_{a}=\sqrt{u^{2}+\frac{v^{2}}{1+a^{2}}}
.

This is the same setting as in
[
25
]
. Same argument therein, by just adding the potential of the second center, leads to

(2)

E_{Eucl}=\dfrac{1}{2}\Bigl(\dot{x}^{2}+\dfrac{\dot{y}^{2}}{1+a^{2}}\Bigr)-\dfrac{m_{1}}{\sqrt{x^{2}+\frac{(y-a)^{2}}{1+a^{2}}}}-\dfrac{m_{2}}{\sqrt{x^{2}+\frac{(y+a)^{2}}{1+a^{2}}}},

(3)

E_{sph}=\dfrac{1}{2}\Bigl(\dot{x}^{2}+\dot{y}^{2})+\dfrac{1}{2}(x\dot{y}-\dot{x}y)^{2}-\dfrac{m_{1}\sqrt{1+a^{2}}(ay+1)}{\sqrt{(y-a)^{2}+(1+a^{2})x^{2}}}-\dfrac{m_{2}\sqrt{1+a^{2}}(-ay+1)}{\sqrt{(y+a)^{2}+(1+a^{2})x^{2}}}.

We use the affine coordinate change
(\xi,\eta)\mapsto(x=\xi,y=\sqrt{1+a^{2}}\eta+a)
to bring the norm on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
into the standard Euclidean one. With this change we have put the second center at
(0,-2a/\sqrt{1+a^{2}})
in this new coordinates. And we have

E_{Eucl}=\dfrac{1}{2}\Bigl(\dot{\xi}^{2}+\dot{\eta}^{2}\Bigr)-\dfrac{m_{1}}{\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\eta^{2}}}-\dfrac{m_{2}}{\sqrt{\xi^{2}+(\eta+2a/\sqrt{1+a^{2}})^{2}}},

E_{sph}=(1+a^{2})\Bigl(\dfrac{1}{2}(\dot{\xi}^{2}+\dot{\eta}^{2})-\dfrac{m_{1}}{\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\eta^{2}}}\Bigr)+\dfrac{(1+a^{2})}{2}\Bigl((\xi\dot{\eta}-\eta\dot{\xi})^{2}-\dfrac{2a}{\sqrt{1+a^{2}}}((\xi\dot{\eta}-\eta\dot{\xi})\dot{\xi}+\dfrac{m_{1}\eta}{\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\eta^{2}}})\Bigr)-\dfrac{m_{2}(-a\sqrt{1+a^{2}}\eta+1-a^{2})}{\sqrt{(\eta+2a/\sqrt{1+a^{2}})^{2}+\xi^{2}}}.

Further set
E_{Kep}:=E_{Eucl}|_{m_{2}=0}
the Keplerian energy with respect to the first center. Then

E_{Eucl}=E_{Kep}+V_{Eucl,2}:=E_{Kep}-\dfrac{m_{2}}{\sqrt{\xi^{2}+(\eta+2a/\sqrt{1+a^{2}})^{2}}}.

From this it is clear that

(4)

E_{sph}=(1+a^{2})(E_{Kep}+V_{Eucl,2}+(D+K)/2).

in which
D
as appeared in (
1
) is a first integral of
E_{Kep}
. The term
V_{Eucl,2}
is linear in
m_{2}
, as well as
K
:

K:=-\dfrac{2}{1+a^{2}}\dfrac{m_{2}(a\sqrt{1+a^{2}}\eta+2a^{2})}{\sqrt{(\eta+2a/\sqrt{1+a^{2}})^{2}+\xi^{2}}}.

We have

(5)

\{E_{Eucl},E_{sph}\}=0,

since
E_{sph}
is invariant under the flow of
E_{Eucl}
.

Thus we get

(6)

\{E_{Kep}+V_{Eucl,2},D+K\}=0.

2.4.
Averaging in the two center problem

We switch to the cotangent bundle while keeping the notations for the functions unchanged. We argue on the region on which
E_{Kep}
defines a circle action (
i.e.
region on which all the orbits are closed) for which the orbits in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
avoid the second Kepler center. Denote the set of orbits of
E_{Kep}
in
\mathbb{T}^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}
whose projection passes through the second Kepler center by
\mathcal{I}
. We shall exclude this set as
V_{Eucl,2}
encounters singularities along these orbits. Then the region
\mathbb{R}
defined by having negative Keplerian energy
E_{Kep}<0
and non-zero Kepler angular momentum
C_{Kep}\neq 0
to exclude collisional orbits in
T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathcal{I}
:

\mathcal{R}:=\{E_{Kep}<0,C_{Kep}\neq 0\}\subset T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathcal{I}.

Moreover, the latter condition excluding collision orbits can likely be dropped since averaging is well-defined up to collisional orbits by mean of regularization. In dimension 2 and 3 this can be justified using methods and coordinates explained in
[
6
]
and
[
27
]
respectively. This is likely the case for all dimensions but we shall not deal with this issue here.

Definition 2.1
.

The partially-averaged system is the system defined on
\mathcal{R}
by the Hamiltonian

E_{Eucl,a}=E_{Kep}+\int_{S^{1}}V_{Eucl,2}\,ds,

in which the integration is understood as averaging over closed orbits of
E_{Kep}
.

The definition does not require the choice of a set of canonical coordinates. When
d=2,3
, several sets of coordinates are available, including the classical Delaunay variables and Poincaré coordinates. Moser constructed action-angle coordinates of Delaunay type for
\mathbb{R}^{d}

[
15
]
. In general it is not always easy to construct these coordinates, which is even more complicated in the case when more bodies were involved
[
19
]
. Moreover, all these coordinates become singular at certain types of orbits.

Nevertheless, in a trivialization of this circle bundle, we can have an (abstract) set of canonical coordinates
(L,\tilde{\ell},u_{1},\cdots,u_{d-1},v_{1},\cdots v_{d-1})
after a further localization on the space of closed Keplerian orbits, in which
L
is the circular angular momentum as in the Delaunay coordinates, and
\tilde{\ell}
is an angle parametrizing the Keplerian orbits in this trivialization (shifted from the mean anomaly by a phase, when both are well-defined), and
(u_{i},v_{i})
are secular variables, in the sense that they are functions of the orbits only, independent of the actual position and velocity of the particle on the orbit. We refer to
[
26
]
for a detailed discussion on these abstract local coordinates. It is not hard to see that Definition
2.1
is independent on the choice of local coordinates. Thus averaging is well-defined in a non-trivial principal
S^{1}
-bundle, in our case defined by motions along closed Keplerian orbits.

The following proposition is a coordinate-free formulation of a main observation in
[
16
]
. Without specifying the coordinates used in the announcement we bring a small generalization.

Proposition 2.2
.

D
is a first integral of the partially-averaged system
E_{Eucl,a}
:

\{E_{Eucl,a},D\}=0.

Proof.

We cover
M
by open submanifolds on which the above-mentioned canonical coordinates are defined. Let
\tilde{M}
be any of these submanifolds, with local canonical coordinates
(L,\tilde{\ell},u_{1},\cdots,u_{d-1},v_{1},\cdots v_{d-1})
.

In any set of these coordinates, we may write

E_{Eucl,a}=E_{Kep}+\langle V_{pl,2}\rangle_{\tilde{\ell}}:=E_{Kep}+\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}V_{pl,2}d\tilde{\ell}.

Then

E_{Eucl}=E_{Eucl,a}+V_{pl,2}-\langle V_{pl,2}\rangle_{\tilde{\ell}}.

We apply averaging theory. There is only one frequency that we need to average. Moreover we may use
m_{2}
to play the role of a small parameter. In this setting, we know that there is a canonical transformation
\phi:\tilde{M}\mapsto\tilde{M}
of order
O(m_{2})
which conjugate
E_{Eucl}
to
E_{Eucl,a}
, that is to say,

E_{Eucl,a}=E_{Eucl}\circ\phi.

And

\{E_{Eucl,a},D\circ\phi+K\circ\phi\}=0.

Write

D\circ\phi+K\circ\phi=D+D\circ\phi-D+K\circ\phi
and notice that

D\circ\phi-D+K\circ\phi=O(m_{2}),

since
\phi
is
O(m_{2})
close to identity. Also,
K
is of order
m_{2}
. This means that

D\circ\phi+K\circ\phi=D+O(m_{2}).

Thus

\{E_{Eucl,a},D\circ\phi+K\circ\phi\}=0=\{E_{Eucl,a},D\}+O(m_{2}).

Since the above equation holds for all
m_{2}
, we have

\{E_{Eucl,a},D\}=0.

Moreover, this equality is independent of the choice of
\tilde{M}
. Therefore it holds on all
M
. This ends the proof.
∎

Note that the proof does not rely on that the system is defined in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and can therefore be generalized to many other cases.

2.5.
The Spherical Case

We now consider the system on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
. The Kepler problem on the sphere has the nice and highly specific property that
all
non-singular orbits are closed. This allows us to average along all non-singular orbits of the spherical Kepler problem.

Again we restrict ourselves to the case
d=2
. To avoid complexification with coordinate changes we put the reference Kepler center at
(0,\dfrac{a}{\sqrt{1+a^{2}}},-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+a^{2}}})
, corresponding to the point
(0,a)
in the chart.

We set

E_{sKep}:=\dfrac{1}{2}\Bigl(\dot{x}^{2}+\dot{y}^{2})+\dfrac{1}{2}(x\dot{y}-\dot{x}y)^{2}-\dfrac{m_{1}\sqrt{1+a^{2}}(ay+1)}{\sqrt{(y-a)^{2}+(1+a^{2})x^{2}}}

and write

E_{sph}=E_{sKep}+V_{sph,2}.

By (
3
), the term
V_{sph,2}
is linear in
m_{2}
. Note that by analyticity, all the terms and this relationship are well-defined on the whole sphere outside the four singular points of the individual spherical Kepler potentials, even though we have only deduced this formula in a chart for a hemisphere (see relative discussions in
[
22
]
). This argument applies also to what follows.

It follows from (
4
) and (
5
), that the analogue of (
6
) holds in the spherical case:

(7)

\{E_{sKep}+V_{sph,2},D+K\}=0.

The subset in
T^{*}\mathbb{S}^{d}
such that the spherical Kepler orbits are all non-singular is a dense open set in
T^{*}\mathbb{S}^{d}
. We denote by
\mathcal{I}_{Sph}
by the set of orbits of
E_{sKep}
in
T^{*}\mathbb{S}^{d}
whose projections to
\mathbb{S}^{d}
pass through other Kepler centers. We set
\mathcal{R}_{sph}=T^{*}\mathbb{S}^{d}\setminus\mathcal{I}_{Sph}
.

Definition 2.2
.

The partially-averaged system is given on the averaging region
\mathcal{R}_{sph}
by

E_{sph,a}=E_{sKep}+\int_{S^{1}}V_{sph,2}\,ds,

in which the integration is made over closed orbits of
E_{sKep}
.

Now with exactly the same proof to Prop.
2.2
, with the Euclidean quantities replaced by their spherical counterparts, we get

Proposition 2.3
.

D
is a first integral of the partially-averaged system
E_{sph,a}
on
\mathcal{R}_{sph}
:

\{E_{sph,a},D\}=0.

3.
Averaging in the Lagrange Problem

We now consider the Lagrange problem in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The result and argument extends easily to other cases as well. In
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, two Kepler centers are put at
(0,a)
and
(0,-a)
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, with masses
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
respectively. In addition, a Hooke center is put at
(0,0)
, with a Hooke factor
f
. The metric on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is
\|\cdot\|_{a}
, defined as
\|(u,v)\|_{a}=\sqrt{u^{2}+\frac{v^{2}}{1+a^{2}}}

The energy of this system is

(8)

\tilde{E}_{Eucl}=\dfrac{1}{2}\Bigl(\dot{x}^{2}+\dfrac{\dot{y}^{2}}{1+a^{2}}\Bigr)-\dfrac{m_{1}}{\sqrt{x^{2}+\frac{(y-a)^{2}}{1+a^{2}}}}-\dfrac{m_{2}}{\sqrt{x^{2}+\frac{(y+a)^{2}}{1+a^{2}}}}+f(x^{2}+\frac{y^{2}}{1+a^{2}}).

As before we write

\tilde{E}_{Eucl}=E_{Kep}+\tilde{V}_{Eucl,2},

with now

\tilde{V}_{Eucl,2}:=-\dfrac{m_{2}}{\sqrt{x^{2}+\frac{(y+a)^{2}}{1+a^{2}}}}+f(x^{2}+\frac{y^{2}}{1+a^{2}}).

According to Prop

this system has a spherical corresponding system, which gives rise to an additional first integral deduced from the spherical energy, to which we need to add the term

from the spherical harmonic potential. Comparing to (

\tilde{E}_{sph}=(1+a^{2})(E_{Kep}+\tilde{V}_{Eucl,2}+(D+\tilde{K})/2).

in which now

is a linear combination of terms linear in

and

we now define

.

The partially-averaged system is given on

\tilde{E}_{Eucl,a}=E_{Kep}+\int_{S^{1}}\tilde{V}_{Eucl,2}\,ds,

in which the integration is made with respect to the closed orbits of

.

is a first integral of the partially-averaged system

of the Lagrange problem:

\{\tilde{E}_{Eucl,a},D\}=0.

We follow the same proof as that of Prop.

, but now proceed with single frequency averaging along closed Keplerian orbits twice. The first eliminate oscillating part of

4.
The Systems in Hyperbolic Spaces

As in Killing
[
13
]
it is possible to define Kepler, two-center and Lagrange problems in the hyperbolic spaces in a similar fashion. Projective dynamics extends to this case as well. One way to realize the hyperbolic space is to write it as a sheet of the unit pseudosphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d,1}
. The Euclidean-hyperbolic correspondence is now again given via central projection from the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{d,1}
. Analogous of Kepler and Hooke potentials can be defined analogously as in the spherical case, by replacing cotangent and tangent by hyperbolic cotangent and hyperbolic tangent respectively. This way we obtain the same results also for partially averaged systems from two-center problem and Lagrange problem in a hyperbolic space:
D
is always a first integral of the partially-averaged systems analogously defined. Here the partially-averaged system is again defined in the region such that the Keplerian orbits are closed.

We refer to
[
22
]
for precise settings of these problems.

5.
Integrable billiards with the partially-averaged system

5.1.
Integrable Kepler billiards

Let
(M,g,V)
be a natural mechanical system, in which
(M,g)
is a Riemannian manifold and
V:M\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is the potential. Adding a codimension-1 submanifold
\mathcal{B}
of
M
to the data defines a system of mechanical billiards. In such a system, the particle moves under the influence of the potential
V
and in addition reflected elastically while reaching
\mathcal{B}
. This defines a continuous, non-smooth dynamical systems. The total energy of the system is a preserved quantity of this system. As in the smooth case, the system is called integrable if it possesses
d:=\dim{M}
preserved quantities which are independent and are in involution with respect to the standard Poisson structure on the cotangent bundle. In particular, if
\dim{M}=2
, then the system is integrable provided a conserved quantity in addition to the energy exists.

In the plane, now several families of mechanical billiards in a Kepler potential field have been identified. Similar results hold on the 2-dimensional sphere and in the hyperbolic plane as well. In its most general setting, we have

Theorem 5.1
.

(
[
22
]
)
In the plane, on the sphere, and in the hyperbolic plane, any finite combination of arcs from a fixed confocal family of conic sections and their degeneracies, with a Kepler center put at one of the focus, gives rise to an integrable Kepler billiard, with
D
being the additional first integral for which
h
is the focus-center distance.

We consider a family of confocal conic sections in the plane and we put the Kepler potential at one of their common foci. This always gives rise to integrable Kepler billiards in the plane, with
D
the additional first integral, in which
h
is interpreted as the focus-center distance.
[
22
]
. Likely these are all the integrable cases
[
3
]
.

Theorem 5.2
.

(
[
24
]
)
In the Euclidean space, consider a family of rotational invariant quadrics around a fixed axis, such that the intersections of these quadrics with any plane passing through this fixed axis belongs to the same confocal family of conic sections. The foci of the intersection is the same for all the planes through the fixed axis and are referred to as the foci of the family. On the sphere and in the hyperbolic space, consider natural analytic extensions of images of these family of quadrics from the Euclidean space under central projection. The images of the foci of the family in the Euclidean space are called the foci of the family on the sphere and in the hyperbolic space.

By further putting a Kepler center at one of the common foci of this family, and consider any finite combination of open subsets from these family, we obtain integrable Kepler billiards in the Euclidean space, on the sphere and in the hyperbolic space, in which
D
is an additional first integral.

Such a system does not fit into the above definition of mechanical billiards though, for it is not necessarily defined with a natural mechanical system, whose total energy is the sum of kinetic and potential energies. Theorem 5.3
.

The Keplerian energy
E_{Kep}
and
D
are conserved quantities for the billiard systems defined as above. They are therefore integrable.

Proof.

We show that
E_{Kep}
is a conserved quantity. First due to averaging the Hamiltonian of any partially-averaged system does not depend on the Keplerian fast angle in any local action-angle coordinates and thus the semi major axis of the Keplerian orbit is invariant. As
E_{Kep}
is only a function of this semi major axis it is also invariant. On the other hand,
E_{Kep}
is the sum of the kinetic and potential energies of the particle in the pure Kepler system. As both the kinetic energy and the potential energy does not change at an elastic reflection,
E_{Kep}
is also invariant under reflections at a reflection wall.
∎

For example, this asserts that the system in the plane, in which a particle moves according to the flow of
E_{Eucl,a}

E_{Kep}
and
D
. The Hamiltonian
E_{Eucl,a}
of the system though, is not necessarily preserved under the reflection.

We have thus identified a large family of integrable billiard systems with an underlying dynamics of mechanical nature on spaces of constant curvatures.
