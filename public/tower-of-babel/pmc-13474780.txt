Epidemiological characteristics of varicella in Xuzhou from 2015 to 2024: surveillance study
Authors: Ge Jin, Xiaozhe Song, Heqing Lou, Yonghong Sun, Yue Gu, Shu Han, Mengya Min, Ying Zhao, Dongsheng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVaricella remains a major public health challenge in China, with limited evidence on long-term urban-rural epidemiological trends. This study analyzes varicella characteristics in Xuzhou, Jiangsu, 2015–2024, focusing on incidence, seasonality, and public health emergency events (PHEEs).MethodsThis surveillance study analyzed population-based varicella case data and public health emergency events (PHEEs) in Xuzhou City, China, from January 2015 to December 2024. Data were extracted from the China Disease Prevention and Control Information System and the National Notifiable Disease Reporting System. Varicella PHEEs were defined as ≥ 10 cases occurring in a collective unit within one week. We employed descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and chi-square trend tests (for temporal trends of incidence rates) to analyze temporal trends and demographic distribution; the Mann-Kendall trend test was used to assess trends in PHEE-related indicators (duration, reporting latency, and case counts per event).ResultsAnnual incidence increased sharply to a peak of 65.01 per 100,000 in 2019, decreased in 2020, transiently rebounded in 2021, and then showed an overall declining pattern through 2024; overall, annual incidence varied significantly over the 10-year period (trend χ2 = 6789.65, p < 0.001). Two peaks emerged: a winter peak (Oct–Jan, 52.74% of cases) and a minor early summer peak (May–Jul, 26.36%). Urban areas had higher case counts but lower attack rates than rural areas (1.52% vs. 1.81%, p < 0.001). Males had an incidence rate 1.24 times that of females (p < 0.001). Children aged 0–14 years accounted for 83.22% of cases, with the highest rate in 5–9-year-olds (219.26/100,000), while adults aged ≥ 30 years showed a significant upward trend in case proportion (trend χ2 = 260.09, p < 0.01). All reported varicella PHEEs emerged in educational sites, 90.29% in primary schools and kindergartens, with 67.96% involving 10–30 cases. Kindergartens had the highest attack rate (5.60%). PHEE duration, case count, and reporting latency correlated strongly (Spearman’s ρ=0.97–0.99, p < 0.01).ConclusionsXuzhou’s varicella epidemiology features seasonal clustering, age-specific vulnerability, and school-associated outbreaks. Background

Varicella, caused by the varicella-zoster virus (VZV), is a highly contagious acute respiratory disease transmitted primarily through respiratory droplets or direct contact [1, 2]. The clinical presentation characteristically includes systemic papules and blisters, which can lead to severe complications such as bacterial infections, encephalitis, pneumonia, and, in rare cases, death [1–3]. While varicella is most common among children under 14 years of age, immunocompromised adults and elderly individuals are also susceptible, with the latter group at risk of developing herpes zoster following VZV infection [1, 2].

The 2019 Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study reported 83.96 million new cases of varicella-zoster virus (VZV) infection worldwide, representing a 17.85% increase compared to 1990. Of these cases, varicella accounts for more than 90% of VZV infections, with cases primarily concentrated in children and adolescents. Globally, approximately 14,533 deaths are attributed to varicella-related complications each year, among which children under 5 years old make up over 60%. Notably, the mortality rate in adult patients is substantially higher than in children—roughly seven times the pediatric rate. Varicella also causes significant socioeconomic impacts, with global annual productivity losses exceeding billions of US dollars. This loss stems mainly from patient absences (work/school), caregiver costs, and follow-up treatment expenses for herpes zoster [4]. In China, varicella has emerged as the most frequently reported vaccine-preventable infectious disease among children in recent years [5]. Data from the 2019 National Infectious Disease Reporting System indicate that varicella ranks as the third most common vaccine-preventable disease—following tuberculosis and influenza—highlighting its status as a critical public health issue in the country [6].

Vaccination remains the most effective strategy for varicella prevention and control [7]. As of 2021, 51 WHO member states have integrated varicella vaccination into their national immunization programs, resulting in a substantial reduction in disease burden [8]. In China, however, despite the availability of the varicella vaccine since 2000, it has not yet been included in the national immunization program. Consequently, varicella incidence has risen in recent years, attaining historical peaks in several provinces [9, 10]. Notably, epidemiological data specific to Xuzhou City remain limited, underscoring the need for localized studies to inform evidence-based prevention and control strategies.

The WHO has emphasized the importance of robust disease surveillance to quantify varicella burden prior to integrating the vaccine into routine immunization programs [11]. In China, varicella has been monitored through the Public Health Emergency Events (PHEEs) reporting management information system since 2006 [12]. In Xuzhou City, surveillance was further enhanced on July 1, 2017, when the city standardized case reporting through the China Disease Control and Prevention Information System, in accordance with monitoring requirements for Class C infectious diseases. This system provides a foundational dataset for analyzing the epidemiological characteristics of varicella in the region.

Transmission dynamics of respiratory infectious diseases, including varicella, are influenced by climatic factors (e.g., temperature, humidity, precipitation), yielding distinct seasonal patterns [13, 14]. Varicella demonstrates two annual incidence peaks: a primary peak from October to December and a secondary peak from April to June [12, 15]. Additionally, regional heterogeneity in incidence has been documented [16], reinforcing the necessity of localized epidemiological analyses to guide targeted interventions. Given the paucity of comprehensive data on varicella in Xuzhou City, this study aims to characterize the epidemiological features of reported cases from 2015 to 2024. By examining temporal trends, seasonal periodicity, and demographic distributions, this research seeks to provide evidence-driven recommendations for optimizing varicella prevention and control measures in the region.

Methods

Setting

Xuzhou City, with a total area of 11,765 square kilometers, is located at longitude 116°22‘−119°18’ E and latitude 33°43‘−35°58’ N. It is situated in the southeastern portion of the North China Plain and the northwestern region of Jiangsu Province. The city exhibits a warm temperate semi-humid monsoon climate, with an average annual temperature ranging from 13 to 16 °C. Strategically positioned, Xuzhou is traversed by the Beijing-Hangzhou Grand Canal and serves as a critical intersection of the Longhai Railway and Beijing-Shanghai Railway, thereby acquiring the designation “Thoroughfare of Five Provinces.” Administratively, Xuzhou City encompasses 10 county-level divisions. As of the end of 2024, the city’s permanent population was estimated at approximately 9.02 million. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Xuzhou Center for Disease Control and Prevention (approval no. 20231012).

Varicella surveillance

Prior to July 2017 (when varicella was formally classified as a Category C notifiable disease in China), Xuzhou had already established a multi-source varicella monitoring system led by the Xuzhou Center for Disease Control and Prevention (XZCDC), which differed from the post-2017 mandatory statutory reporting but ensured systematic case capture. The specific mechanisms included Institutional Focused Reporting and Medical Institution Passive Reporting, supplemented by laboratory-assisted case confirmation.

For institutional focused reporting, key settings with high varicella transmission risk (including primary schools, kindergartens, and childcare facilities) were required to report suspected or confirmed varicella cases to local CDCs within 24 hours via the “Xuzhou School Infectious Disease Surveillance Platform”. This practice was based on Jiangsu provincial guidelines for key infectious disease monitoring in collective settings, which designated varicella as a priority surveillance disease in schools before its national Category C classification. School health workers performed daily morning health checks and student absenteeism monitoring, and all suspected cases were further verified by CDC epidemiologists through field investigations.

For medical institution passive reporting, major hospitals (including Xuzhou Infectious Disease Hospital, the only tertiary specialized infectious disease hospital in the city) and community health service centers submitted varicella case information to XZCDC through the China Disease Prevention and Control Information System, which has been operational for statutory infectious disease reporting since 2004. Although national mandatory reporting regulations for varicella were not yet implemented before 2017, local public health administrative rules required consistent case reporting by secondary and above hospitals and community health facilities in Xuzhou.

For laboratory-assisted case confirmation, the XZCDC Virology Laboratory performed varicella-zoster virus (VZV) DNA polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing on vesicular fluid samples for clinically ambiguous cases with atypical rashes or unclear epidemiological links. Among 110 suspected cases tested during 2015–2016, 68.2% were laboratory-confirmed VZV-positive.

Strict quality control procedures were implemented for the 2015–2016 dataset to minimize information bias and ensure reliability, in collaboration with XZCDC. First, dual verification was performed for all case information, cross-checking medical records for clinical and demographic details and school or community investigation records for exposure history and onset time; any inconsistencies were resolved through communication with clinicians or school health staff. Second, standardized duplicate checking and data cleaning were conducted to eliminate repeated reports submitted by both hospitals and schools, with a duplicate rate of 3.7% during 2015–2016. Supplementary field investigations were completed within 7 days for records with missing core variables (e.g., age, onset date), achieving a final data completeness of 94.3%. Third, surveillance data were cross-validated with the China Immunization Planning Information System (fully covered in Xuzhou since 2012) to verify vaccination status and exclude misclassified vaccine-related adverse events.

Since July 1, 2017, following the national classification of varicella as a Category C notifiable infectious disease, standardized surveillance and management have been fully implemented in Jiangsu Province. All clinically diagnosed and laboratory-confirmed varicella cases must be reported to local CDCs within 24 hours of identification. Upon receiving suspected case or cluster reports from healthcare institutions or the public, local CDCs conduct timely on-site verification, epidemiological investigation, and emergency outbreak control.

All verified cases are systematically registered in the National Notifiable Disease Reporting System (NNDRS), a unified national online reporting platform. Core standardized variables for each case include age, sex, residential address, household registration, symptom onset date, diagnosis date, hospitalization information, reporting institution, occurrence and report timestamps, outbreak location, number of affected individuals, onset time of index and final cases, and the completion time of control measures.

Educational settings, including preschools, primary and secondary schools, and higher education institutions, are legally required to report varicella outbreaks to public health authorities. CDC staff at the county and municipal levels conduct daily active monitoring of school-based varicella clusters through the NNDRS platform.

Varicella case data and public health emergency event (PHEE) records in Xuzhou from 2015 to 2024 were extracted from the China Disease Prevention and Control Information System, screened according to residential address and symptom onset date. Corresponding population baseline data were obtained from the official Xuzhou Statistical Yearbook.

This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Xuzhou Center for l no. 20231012). All original data were fully anonymized before analysis, with all personal identifying information permanently removed. Therefore, the ethics committee waived the requirement for written informed consent, as the anonymized retrospective data posed no identifiable privacy risks to participants.

Based on official demographic criteria from the Xuzhou Municipal Statistics Bureau, the study population was stratified into eight age groups: 0–4 years, 5–9 years, 10–14 years, 15–19 years, 20–24 years, 25–29 years, 30–34 years, and ≥35 years.

Definition

Varicella

Varicella cases were diagnosed based on clinical manifestations, including successive maculopapular rashes, papules and vesicular eruptions on skin and mucous membranes. The superficial oval vesicles are 3–5 mm in diameter with fragile and easily ruptured blister walls. Patients commonly present with systemic symptoms such as chills, fever, headache, sore throat and poor appetite. All cases were divided into clinically diagnosed and laboratory-confirmed cases. Clinically diagnosed cases refer to individuals who had contact history with varicella or herpes zoster patients within 2 to 3 weeks before onset, or developed symptoms during local varicella epidemic period.

Laboratory confirmation criteria were listed as follows: (1) Positive serum varicella-zoster virus (VZV)-IgM detected by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, excluding people vaccinated against varicella in the latest four weeks; (2) Successful isolation of VZV, or positive viral antigen identified by direct immunofluorescence assay or polymerase chain reaction; (3) A fourfold or above increase of VZV IgG antibody titer in paired serum samples collected at an interval of 2–4 weeks.

Varicella public health emergency events (PHEEs)

Varicella-related public health emergency events are defined as no less than 10 confirmed or clinically diagnosed cases occurring in the same collective institution within seven days. Events were classified into small scale (10–30 cases), medium scale (31–100 cases) and large scale (≥100 cases). Reporting latency indicates the interval from symptom onset of the index case to official report submission, and its median value was adopted to evaluate reporting timeliness. Outbreak duration represents the time span between symptom onset of the first and last case, whose median value reflects the control efficiency. Attack rate was calculated as the ratio of confirmed cases to total exposed population during the outbreak.

Statistical analysis

Temporal and population distribution characteristics of varicella from 2015 to 2024 in Xuzhou were analyzed and visualized by statistical charts. Categorical variables were described with frequency and proportion, and numerical variables were presented as median and interquartile range. The chi-square trend test was used to analyze annual incidence trends, including the changing tendency among adults aged ≥ 30 years. Ordinary chi-square test was adopted to test correlations between categorical variables. Spearman correlation analysis was used to explore the association between outbreak duration and reporting latency. The Mann-Kendall trend test was applied to analyze PHEE indicators including case volume, outbreak duration and reporting latency. A two-tailed p value less than 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Given the surveillance system adjustment in July 2017, potential bias was fully considered during interannual trend comparison. Data sorting was completed via WPS Office 2019, and statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 17.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Incidence and temporal trends

From 2015 to 2024, a total of 30,886 varicella cases were reported in Xuzhou City, Jiangsu Province, with an average annual incidence rate of 34.87 per 100,000. The annual incidence rates from 2015 to 2024 were 15.57, 15.35, 32.65, 52.25, 65.01, 42.31, 50.03, 34.24, 22.39, and 18.74 per 100,000, respectively. After the peak in 2019, the incidence decreased in 2020, increased slightly in 2021, and then showed a sustained decline from 2022 to 2024; overall, annual incidence varied significantly over the 10-year period (trend χ2 = 6789.65, p < 0.001). The incidence rates for 2015–2016 may have been underreported due to the non-mandatory reporting framework in place at that time; therefore, caution is warranted when directly comparing their absolute values with those of subsequent years.

Temporal distribution

Varicella cases were reported year-round in Xuzhou City, Jiangsu Province, between 2015 and 2024, with two distinct seasonal peaks in incidence. The primary peak occurred from October to January of the following year, accounting for 52.74% (16,287 of 30,886) of total cases, while the secondary peak was observed from May to July, representing 26.36% (8,141 of 30,886) of cases. The lowest incidence rates were recorded in March and September, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Notably, the typical peak in January was absent in 2023, with incidence rates resembling those of February. Regional distribution

From 2015 to 2024, the average annual incidence rate of varicella across counties and districts in Xuzhou City ranged from 10.66 to 86.70 per 100,000 population, with significant regional variation (χ2 = 25450.00, p < 0.001). The three regions with the highest incidence rates were Suining County (86.70 per 100,000), Pizhou City (55.88 per 100,000), and Gulou District (50.91 per 100,000).

Population distribution

Among varicella cases reported in Xuzhou City between 2015 and 2024, there were 17,213 males and 13,673 females, with incidence rates of 38.62 and 31.08 per 100,000 population, respectively. The incidence rate ratio between males and females was 1.24, and the crude case ratio was 1.26; the difference was statistically significant (χ2 = 361.27, p < 0.001). The male-to-female incidence ratios from 2015 to 2024 were 1.24, 1.37, 1.22, 1.28, 1.22, 1.21, 1.25, 1.22, 1.33, and 1.50, respectively.

A total of 25,703 cases (83.22%) were reported in the 0–14 years age group. The highest incidence rate was observed in the 5–9 years age group (219.26 per 100,000 population), followed by the 0–4 years group (117.30 per 100,000) and the 10–14 years group (91.31 per 100,000). The differences in incidence rates across age groups were statistically significant (χ2 = 82,517.46, p < 0.001). The lowest incidence rate of 1.38 per 100,000 population was recorded in the age group over 35 years, as illustrated in Table 2. From 2015 to 2024, the proportion of varicella cases in the 0–14 age group showed a decreasing trend, with percentages of 88.86%, 88.13%, 83.46%, 84.47%, 87.73%, 87.81%, 84.64%, 84.56%, 69.79%, and 46.78%, respectively. Conversely, the proportion of cases in individuals aged 30 and above demonstrated a significant upward trend, increasing from 1.73% to 6.38% over the study period (Fig. 2). Statistical analysis revealed a significant increasing trend in the proportion of varicella cases among individuals aged 30 and above (trend χ2 = 260.09, p < 0.01).

Occupational distribution

The top five occupations with the highest proportion of varicella in Xuzhou City between 2015 and 2024 were students (51.72%), preschool children (27.81%), scattered children (11.49%), farmers (3.14%), and individuals engaged in household chores or unemployed (2.61%). Together, these groups accounted for 96.77% of all reported cases.

Outbreak of the PHEEs

Scale of varicella PHEEs

Between 2015 and 2024, a total of 103 varicella PHEEs were reported in Xuzhou City, involving 2,776 cases and 171,090 exposed individuals. The annual attack rate ranged from 0.94% to 2.27%, with an overall attack rate of 1.62% (2,776 of 171,090). Of these PHEEs, 70 (67.96%) involved 10–30 cases, 32 (31.07%) involved 31–100 cases, and one (0.97%) involved ≥ 100 cases. The annual epidemic scale of varicella PHEEs ranged primarily from 15.50 to 38 cases. The median number of cases per PHEE was 22 (IQR 12–42.5), the median duration was 9 days (IQR 3.5–34.5), and the median reporting latency was 18.25 days (IQR 4–49.5), as detailed in Table 3. To assess temporal changes, the Mann-Kendall trend test was applied. The results indicated that there were no significant increasing or decreasing trends from 2015 to 2024 in the cases per event median (S = −1, p = 0.929), median duration (S = −13, p = 0.285), or median reporting latency (S = −5, p = 0.720) of varicella PHEEs.

Correlation between duration of varicella PHEEs, reporting latency, and case numbers

The Spearman rank correlation coefficients for varicella PHEEs reported between 2015 and 2024 were as follows: 0.99 (p < 0.01) for the association between PHEEs duration and the number of varicella cases, and 0.97 (p < 0.01) for the association between PHEEs duration and reporting latency. Distribution of varicella PHEEs

Between 2015 and 2024, all 103 reported varicella PHEEs in Xuzhou City occurred in schools, accounting for 33.33% (103 of 309) of all school-based PHEEs during the same period. Although the number of reported cases and illnesses was higher in urban areas than in rural areas, the attack rate was lower in urban areas compared to rural areas. This difference in attack rates between urban and rural areas was statistically significant (χ2 = 21.89, p < 0.001). Primary schools were the most common setting for PHEEs (72 cases, 69.90%), followed by kindergartens (21 cases, 20.39%), together accounting for 90.29% of all reported PHEEs. Discussion

Our analysis of varicella epidemiology in Xuzhou from 2015 to 2024 reveals dynamic trends characterized by distinct fluctuations, spatiotemporal clustering, and clear demographic patterns. The incidence rate rose to a peak of 65.01 per 100,000 in 2019 before declining substantially to 18.74 per 100,000 by 2024. The initial increase likely reflects the enhanced surveillance sensitivity following varicella’s reclassification as a Category C notifiable disease in Jiangsu Province in 2017. The subsequent overall decline aligns with broader national efforts in vaccination and public health intervention [5, 7, 17].

With an average annual incidence of 34.87 per 100,000, Xuzhou’s rate was comparable to some cities like Qingyang (36.99) [15], but lower than Anhui Province (44.8) [9]. It was, however, significantly higher than the national average (19.8) and rates in Dalian (26.3) [5, 12]. The 2019 peak in Xuzhou (65.01) also remained below that of Anhui (81.2) and Hangzhou (120) [9, 18]. These variations underscore regional differences in the implementation and impact of control strategies.

Xuzhou’s varicella incidence rose to a peak in 2019 and declined thereafter through 2024. From the 2019 peak of 65.01 per 100,000 to 18.74 per 100,000 in 2024, the incidence decreased by approximately 71.17% contrasting with the national trend of increasing cases observed in most Chinese provinces [9, 15]. We suggest several non-overlapping factors may explain this trend. First, the decline may partly come from stronger local ability to detect and respond to outbreaks quickly. Specifically, Xuzhou’s surveillance rules required schools and childcare centers to report when they found varicella clusters—this probably helped act faster to control the spread than in other regions. We do not have direct data comparing reporting latency between Xuzhou and other areas, but quick isolation of cases and targeted emergency vaccinations are proven strategies for controlling varicella outbreaks [11, 19]. Using these strategies consistently in Xuzhou may have stopped small outbreaks from getting worse. Second, better surveillance sensitivity over time is another possible factor. After varicella became a notifiable disease and surveillance systems were improved (details in the Methods section), we likely identified more cases accurately. Though this improvement may have first made reported cases seem higher, the later big drop is probably not due to surveillance issues—it may instead show a real decrease in varicella spread. Third, the indirect impact of COVID-19 non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) is a reasonable third factor for this trend. During the COVID-19 pandemic, measures like keeping social distance, wearing masks and hand hygiene were shown to reduce the spread of diseases like varicella that spread through breathing or contact [20, 21]. Xuzhou’s active use of these NPIs during local outbreaks may have made the incidence drop even more. Notably, while we do not have published city-level varicella vaccination data, Xuzhou has high overall coverage for vaccines in the National Expanded Program on Immunization (EPI)—over 95% for other EPI vaccines, according to reports. This suggests high and steady population immunity may have been a key factor keeping incidence low.

Varicella shows a seasonal pattern—main peak in winter (Oct–Jan), minor peak in early summer (May–Jul)—matching observations in other temperate regions [5, 9, 10, 18]. The winter peak likely stems from increased indoor gatherings and school attendance, boosting viral spread [20]. Xuzhou’s varicella incidence dropped 42.72% from 2020 to 2023 (mean: 37.24/100,000) versus the 2019 peak (65.01/100,000), consistent with global reduced VZV transmission during pandemic control [21]. In 2020, November and December accounted for 52.30% of the annual incidence burden, with the December incidence reaching 9.93 per 100,000, similar to altered seasonality of measles (65.46% drop) and influenza (91.69% drop) under China’s non-pharmaceutical interventions [22]. Multinational studies have demonstrated that non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs), such as school closures and universal masking, are effective in reducing the transmission of respiratory infectious diseases. The implementation of similar NPIs during the COVID-19 pandemic was also associated with a significant decline in varicella incidence, suggesting their potential cross-protective effect [21, 23, 24]. COVID-19 measures likely disrupted VZV and other respiratory pathogen transmission. Increased fear of nosocomial SARS-CoV-2 exposure and reduced healthcare use may have caused underreporting of mild cases. Given VZV’s biennial cycles, we recommend interepidemic interventions: (1) strengthen vaccine awareness; (2) prioritize catch-up vaccination for school-aged groups; (3) enhance syndromic surveillance via integrated fever-rash reporting. This strategy can reduce seasonal outbreaks by boosting population immunity pre-peak.

Significant regional disparities in varicella incidence were observed, with Suining County recording the highest annual incidence (86.70 per 100,000)—a finding consistent with epidemiological patterns reported in previous studies [9, 10]. These observed urban–rural differences may reflect variations in vaccination coverage, healthcare access, surveillance sensitivity, or outbreak response capacity [8, 25, 26]; however, the present surveillance dataset does not allow these potential mechanisms to be directly tested. Further studies with individual-level vaccination and exposure data are needed to establish causality.

Varicella shows male predominance (1.24:1), matching existing patterns [5, 9–11, 18], possibly due to biological or behavioral differences [1], requiring tailored prevention education for boys. 83.22% of cases were in 0–14-year-olds, peaking at 219.26/100,000 in 5–9-year-olds, linked to dense kindergartens/primary schools; unvaccinated/uninfected children face higher transmission risks [27]. Three school interventions: integrate prevention into curricula (focusing on hand hygiene); enforce health screenings with isolation for suspected cases until cleared; prioritize school-based vaccination [19]. The incidence of varicella in adults aged ≥ 30 years in Xuzhou exhibited an increasing trend, which is consistent with relevant published reports [9, 28]. Childhood vaccination reduced youth infections but may cause age shifts—supported by some [17, 29], but not all [17, 30, 31] studies, possibly due to regional herd immunity differences. Mandatory kindergarten vaccine checks improved coverage; homeschooled children have lower compliance. Needs: (1) enhanced surveillance for adults ≥ 30; (2) consider booster shots for waning immunity [20].

Analysis of 103 PHEEs showed spatial clustering, with preschools having the highest attack rate (5.60%), emphasizing stronger infection control in early education. In Xuzhou (2015–2024), urban areas had more cases but lower attack rates than rural regions, likely due to higher vaccine coverage, better healthcare access, and faster outbreak control [21, 23]. Across the study period, there were no statistically significant monotonic trends in the median number of cases per event, median duration, or reporting latency associated with varicella PHEEs. This result demonstrates that no shifts occurred in the scale, duration, or timeliness of the events being reported. Spearman correlation analysis showed that PHEEs duration strongly correlated with case burden (Spearman’s ρ=0.97, p < 0.001); The decline in varicella outbreaks over time, as observed in settings like Shanghai during the voluntary vaccination period [32], underscores the importance of ongoing surveillance and control measures. Priorities: real-time school surveillance, 24-hour reporting, rapid isolation. Urban-rural attack rate differences persist despite similar density, linked to vaccination gaps and healthcare access. This study has several limitations. First, reliance on passive surveillance data probably resulted in underreporting, particularly in rural areas. Second, the lack of detailed vaccination records prevented a direct analysis of vaccine impact on disease trends. Third, potential underestimation in the 2015–2016 data may exaggerate the apparent magnitude of the post-2017 increase. Nevertheless, the primary value of this study lies in its comprehensive description of the long-term epidemiology and trends of varicella in Xuzhou, especially within the improved surveillance framework post-2017.

Conclusion

In conclusion, Xuzhou confronts a substantial public health challenge from varicella transmission patterns demonstrating three epidemiologically significant determinants: First, infection rates exhibit bimodal seasonal peaks synchronized with academic calendars. Second, marked disparities exist between urban and rural infection rates, highlighting critical gaps in healthcare accessibility. Third, population-based surveillance identifies preschool and early school-aged children (5–9 years) as the most affected age group, accounting for nearly half (46.91%, 14,489/30886) of all reported cases. This underscores their pivotal role in transmission dynamics and highlights the critical importance of enhancing vaccination coverage and surveillance in educational settings. These determinants suggest an association between educational institution operations and viral transmission dynamics, necessitating prioritized interventions targeting scholastic environments where aerosol transmission is likely a primary mode of spread.

Implications for public health

The findings from this study emphasize the need for enhanced varicella surveillance, particularly in high-incidence regions and among vulnerable populations. The introduction of a two-dose varicella vaccination schedule, as recommended by the WHO, could significantly reduce disease burden and prevent outbreaks [11]. Additionally, public health campaigns targeting schools and kindergartens, coupled with improved vaccination coverage or targeted catch-up strategies among susceptible adults aged ≥ 30 years, are essential to mitigate the rising incidence in these groups.
