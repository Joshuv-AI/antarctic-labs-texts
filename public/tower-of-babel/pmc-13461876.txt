To the Editor,

Articular cartilage and the inner (white) zone of the knee meniscus share a distinctive physiological triad: minimal vascularity, a dense extracellular matrix (ECM), and cellular metabolism compatible with diffusion-limited oxygen and nutrient delivery.1-3 These features are often discussed separately, yet together they suggest a coherent and clinically relevant trade-off: preserving a continuous, hydrated load-bearing architecture may require biological mechanisms that also limit vascular-dependent intrinsic repair.

Avascularity in these tissues is not necessarily a passive absence of vessels. Anti-angiogenic mediators have been described in cartilage-related contexts, and the meniscus contains factors consistent with active maintenance of an avascular inner zone. 4 Fujii et al showed that chondromodulin-I (ChM-I) is predominantly detected in the inner meniscus, is present at higher concentration in inner-meniscus–derived conditioned medium, and that ChM-I removal or functional blockade significantly increases endothelial cell proliferation. 4 This supports a plausible mechanism by which anti-angiogenic signaling helps preserve inner-zone avascularity.

The metabolic component of this triad also merits explicit consideration. As a consequence of the avascular microenvironment, chondrocytes and inner-zone meniscal cells must maintain energy generation and matrix synthesis under low oxygen tension and limited substrate delivery.5,6 HIF-1alpha is widely regarded as a key regulator of cellular adaptation to hypoxia in cartilage, supporting chondrocyte survival and matrix homeostasis under hypoxic, inflammatory, and mechanical stress. 5 In parallel, classic experiments in articular cartilage show that severe hypoxia/anoxia can, in some experimental conditions, suppress glucose uptake and lactate production, consistent with a negative Pasteur effect and highlighting potential vulnerability to energy shortage when oxygen tension and substrate delivery fall below critical thresholds. 6 Together, these observations support the concept that metabolic compatibility may not be incidental: it provides biological plausibility for how avascular, diffusion-limited tissues can persist while sustaining ECM turnover over decades.

The key mechanobiological question is how vascular “space” formation would interact with the functional requirements of a hydrated, load-bearing ECM. For meniscus and cartilage, clinically meaningful mechanical descriptors are not primarily “strength” as failure load, but stiffness-related and time-dependent properties that influence stress distribution, shear transfer, permeability, and fluid pressurization under cyclic physiological loading.7-9 A dense and relatively continuous ECM can support load through coupled solid-matrix deformation and interstitial fluid pressurization, whereas discontinuities can reroute stress paths and amplify local strain.7-9

By “vascular space as mechanics,” we refer to the physical consequence of creating lumen-like, fluid-filled discontinuities within an otherwise continuous, hydrated load-bearing matrix. Such discontinuities may alter local stress transfer and interstitial fluid pressurization even if the invading vasculature also brings biological benefits (oxygen, nutrients, inflammatory and reparative cells). The central question is therefore not whether vascularity is “good” or “bad,” but whether improved transport can be achieved while preserving the mechanical architecture required for durable load support.

Within this framing, finite-element analysis (FEA) is best positioned not as definitive evidence, but as an illustrative sensitivity concept: representing vascular channels as compliant inclusions or void-like discontinuities within an ECM-like material is expected to reduce apparent compressive stiffness and increase local shear/strain concentration, with magnitude dependent on channel geometry, boundary conditions, and constitutive/poro-viscoelastic assumptions.7-9 Importantly, this hypothesis does not claim that the vascularized red zone is mechanically inferior to the avascular white zone in bulk. Rather, it predicts that introducing vascular-space discontinuities into a pressurization-dependent inner-zone architecture may carry a measurable mechanical penalty—a proposition that can be tested.

This perspective yields practical, testable predictions for meniscus/cartilage repair and tissue engineering. First, controlled microchanneling (or engineered channel-like inclusions) at matched solid content should reduce apparent compressive stiffness and increase spatial heterogeneity of strain compared with non-channeled controls.7-9 Second, perturbing anti-angiogenic factors implicated in inner-zone avascularity (including ChM-I–related pathways) should couple vascular invasion propensity with measurable changes in matrix continuity and mechanical outcomes, enabling separation of “vascularity as biology” from “vascular space as mechanics.” 4 Third, red-versus-white zone comparisons should emphasize functional properties (e.g., stiffness, permeability, time-dependent response) and composition rather than a simplified vascular-versus-avascular dichotomy. 1

In summary, we propose a unifying, testable mechanobiological trade-off: anti-angiogenic maintenance of avascularity may help preserve a dense ECM architecture that supports load-bearing function in cartilage and the inner meniscus, while imposing a biological cost in reparative capacity.4,7-9 This framing is directly relevant to regenerative strategies aimed at improving transport because it motivates approaches that decouple nutrient delivery enhancement from disruptive vascular-space formation. 10
