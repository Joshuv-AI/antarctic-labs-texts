Clinicopathologic Features and Outcomes of Basal Cell Carcinoma Coexisting with Other Tumors or Precancerous Lesions: A Single-Center Analysis
Authors: Qiannan Jia, Shuaixin Li, Xinran Li, Aiping Fan, Jun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo characterize the clinicopathologic spectrum, treatment patterns, and outcomes of basal cell carcinoma (BCC) coexisting with other tumors or precancerous lesions.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed pathologically confirmed cases diagnosed at Peking Union Medical College Hospital between 2020 and 2025. Demographic, clinical, histopathologic, treatment, and outcome data were extracted and analyzed.ResultsAmong 860 pathologically confirmed BCC cases, 17 met the inclusion criteria. The cohort comprised 9 women (52.9%) and 8 men (47.1%), with a mean age of 56.71 ± 21.14 years. Coexisting lesions were classified as hamartoma/nevus-associated (7 cases), keratinocytic lesion-associated (5 cases), or adnexal/cyst-associated (5 cases). Nevus sebaceus was the most frequent coexisting lesion. All lesions involved the head and neck, and 16 (94.1%) showed recent enlargement or another new change on a preexisting lesion. Nodular and superficial BCC predominated. Exploratory comparison with a contemporary head and neck BCC cohort suggested that the present cohort was characterized by younger age, more frequent scalp/frontotemporal involvement, enrichment of superficial BCC, and fewer aggressive subtypes. Most patients had favorable outcomes without recurrence or metastasis observed.ConclusionBCC coexisting with other tumors or precancerous lesions has a distinct clinicopathologic profile, and often presents as recent changes in a long-standing head/neck lesion. Classification by coexisting lesion type may help integrate clinical morphology, pathology, and risk assessment.

Introduction

Collision tumors are generally defined as two or more adjacent tumor components arising at the same anatomic site while retaining relatively distinct cellular boundaries. The broader concept of multiple skin neoplasms at one site (MUSK IN A NEST) also encompasses colonization, combination, and biphenotypic patterns.1,2 Basal cell carcinoma (BCC) is among the most frequent components of cutaneous composite lesions.1–4 However, the spatial relationship between BCC and the coexisting lesion is not uniform. Some cases represent true incidental juxtaposition, whereas others arise in a congenital hamartomatous background such as nevus sebaceus. Still others coexist with Bowen disease, squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) in situ, seborrheic keratosis, or actinic keratosis, suggesting shared photodamage or a local field-cancerization effect.1,2,5

In real-world datasets, the label of “BCC with a coexisting condition” may include inflammatory dermatoses, infections, or non-neoplastic changes, which provide limited insight into collision tumors.6 We therefore excluded inflammatory conditions such as atopic dermatitis, psoriasis, and eczematous dermatitis, and focused specifically on BCC coexisting with other tumors or precancerous lesions to establish a more clinically and pathologically meaningful analytical framework.

These lesions can be oversimplified as a single process clinically. A second component may be overlooked, particularly when a previously stable lesion develops recent focal hyperpigmentation, nodular elevation, ulceration, or eczematous change. In this study, we summarized the clinical morphology, pathologic patterns, treatment, and outcomes of these lesions and hypothesized that BCC coexisting with other tumors showed clinicopathological and prognostic characteristics distinct from conventional BCC.

Materials and Methods

Case Identification and Study Design

We initially retrieved 860 pathologically confirmed BCC cases from the electronic medical record system and pathology database of the Department of Dermatology, Peking Union Medical College Hospital, between 2020 and 2025, using keyword search for “basal cell carcinoma” in pathological reports. From these, we screened 36 cases with coexisting conditions. Exclusion criteria were incomplete clinicopathologic data; a coexisting diagnosis representing another BCC subtype; infection; infectious granuloma; foreign-body granuloma; scar; ulcer; or inflammatory dermatoses such as dermatitis and eczema. All histopathologic slides were reviewed by two senior dermatopathologists. A total of 17 cases met the criteria for final analysis. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (K23C0753) and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all the patients and patients in Figure 1 provided written informed consent for images to be published. Data Extraction and Standardization

The extracted variables included age, sex, presenting complaint, disease duration, lesion site, congenital or childhood-onset background, progressive enlargement, pruritus, pain, erythema, ulceration or surface breakdown, elevation, exophytic growth, pathologic diagnosis, treatment modality, follow-up status and outcome.

Working Classification of Coexisting Lesions

Because the cohort included both collision tumors in the narrow sense and secondary tumors arising in a hamartomatous background, we used the following pragmatic working classification: Hamartoma/nevus-associated: BCC arising in association with lesions such as nevus sebaceus or intradermal nevus; Keratinocytic lesion-associated: BCC coexisting with intraepidermal carcinomas (Bowen disease/SCC in situ), benign epidermal tumors (seborrheic keratosis), and precursor lesions (actinic keratosis); Adnexal/cyst-associated: BCC coexisting with tubular apocrine adenoma, hidrocystoma, epidermoid cyst, or trichoblastoma.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 28.0. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for proportions were calculated using the Wilson method. We calculated Cohen’s d for continuous variables and Cramér’s V or odds ratios with 95% CIs for categorical variables. Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

We performed an exploratory comparison with the primary head and neck BCC published by Di Maio et al in 2025.7 Age was compared at the patient level using Welch’s t test. Sex and categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test. For variables reported at the lesion level in the external cohort, including anatomic site, histologic subtype, and recurrence, we performed corresponding lesion-level comparisons. Lesions classified as not otherwise specified (NOS) or indeterminate were excluded from the histologic subtype comparison.

Results

General Clinical Characteristics

Among 860 pathologically confirmed BCC cases, 17 cases were included in this study (Figure 2). The cohort comprised 9 women and 8 men, with a mean age of 56.71 ± 21.14 years. All lesions occurred on the head and neck: 7 involved the scalp or frontotemporal region, 5 the nose or perinasal region, and 5 the cheek, periorbital area, or other facial sites. Nine cases (52.9%) had a congenital or childhood-onset background lesion, and 16 (94.1%) had a history of progressive enlargement or another new change in a pre-existing lesion. Clinical morphology included solitary gray-brown to black papules or nodules, sometimes arising on pre-existing yellow or erythematous plaques (Figure 1). Patch or plaque components were present in 10 cases (58.8%). Erythema was observed in 4 cases (23.5%), ulceration or surface breakdown in 6 (35.3%), and a definite exophytic growth in 4 (23.5%). All 17 lesions showed some degree of elevation, and 12 (70.6%) had a pigmented elevated component. Pruritus was reported in 2 cases (11.8%), whereas none of the patients reported pain.

Coexisting Components and BCC Histologic Subtypes

The 17 lesions were distributed across the three working categories shown in Table 1. Nevus sebaceus was the most frequent coexisting lesion, followed by Bowen disease/SCC in situ. BCC component usually consisted of nests or cords of basaloid cells connected to the epidermis or located in the superficial-to-mid dermis. In hamartoma/nevus-associated cases, the non-BCC background was most often nevus sebaceous (Figure 3A) or intradermal nevus. Keratinocytic lesion-associated cases included Bowen disease/SCC in situ (Figure 3B), seborrheic keratosis, and actinic keratosis, each with its characteristic histopathologic features. Adnexal/cyst-associated cases included sweat gland adenoma, hidrocystoma, epidermoid cyst, and trichoblastoma with distinctive structural features (Figure 3C–F). Treatment and Outcomes

Treatment was predominantly surgical. Surgery was included in the treatment plan for 15 of 17 cases (88.2%): 14 patients underwent direct surgical excision, and 1 underwent surgical biopsy or local removal. One elderly patient with dementia received radiotherapy because surgery could not be tolerated. One patient with locally advanced BCC coexisting with an epidermoid cyst received multimodal treatment comprising targeted therapy, surgical excision, and radiotherapy.

Outcome information was evaluable for 13 of 17 cases (76.5%). The median interval from disease onset to the last follow-up or clinical endpoint was 10 months (range, 2–63 months). Twelve patients had a favorable outcome without recurrence or metastasis. One patient died in 2024 of age-related comorbidities, with no evidence that the death was related to tumor progression.

Exploratory statistical comparisons showed that patients in the present cohort were significantly younger (P = 0.001), had more frequent scalp/frontotemporal involvement (P = 0.004), and had less frequent auricular/preauricular involvement (P = 0.009). Histologically, superficial BCC was significantly enriched (P < 0.001), whereas aggressive subtypes were significantly less common (P = 0.001). Although neither difference reached statistical significance, the present cohort had a higher proportion of women and fewer recurrences.

Discussion

This study indicates that BCC remains a frequent component of cutaneous composite tumors, but its clinical profile in composite lesions is not identical to that of conventional head and neck BCC. Previous studies have consistently shown that BCC is common in collision lesions and within the MUSK IN A NEST framework, with a predilection for the head and neck and chronically sun-exposed areas.1–5 All lesions in our cohort likewise occurred on the head and neck. However, compared with conventional head and neck BCC cohorts and current consensus, our patients were younger, the proportion of women was slightly higher, scalp/frontotemporal involvement was more frequent, and auricular/preauricular involvement was less common.7,9 Clinically, the more distinctive presentation was a recently developed pigmented papule or nodule, focal ulceration, or eczematous change arising within a congenital or long-standing lesion. Almost half of patients had a congenital lesion or a lesion of more than 10 years’ duration, suggesting that both the distribution and initial presentation of BCC as a composite lesion may be influenced by the nature of the underlying lesion.

Nevus sebaceus-associated cases were the largest specific subgroup and typically involved congenital scalp or frontotemporal plaques with new papules, nodules, or elevation in adulthood. This temporal pattern is consistent with the broader literature on secondary neoplasms in nevus sebaceus. The distinction matters because trichoblastoma and other benign basaloid proliferations may be classified differently across series. In our framework, these cases are more appropriately considered secondary tumors arising in a hamartomatous background than simple incidental collisions.

The distribution of BCC subtypes may suggest a pathogenesis distinct from that of conventional head and neck BCC. Compared with the external cohort,7 superficial BCC was significantly enriched in our cohort, while aggressive subtypes were markedly less frequent. Thus, composite lesions may not represent random cross-sections of conventional BCC, but rather a set of distinctive scenarios shaped by the microenvironment of the background lesion. However, the presence of a composite lesion should not reduce vigilance for high-risk components. Current consensus continue to classify infiltrative, micronodular, sclerosing/morpheaform, and basosquamous subtypes as higher-risk.9 The single infiltrative BCC in this cohort coexisted with Bowen disease, highlighting the need for caution when a new deep component develops in a superficial keratinocytic lesion.

BCC cases with coexisting components present different diagnostic pitfalls. Nevus sebaceus is characterized by increased mature sebaceous glands. When trichoblastoma-like structures are also present, the relationship between the tumor and epidermis, stromal features, and immunohistochemical markers PHLDA1 may be integrated into the diagnostic assessment.8 Bowen disease/SCC in situ and actinic keratosis often present with erythema, scaling, erosion or eczematous change. On dermoscopy, dotted vessels and surface scale without classic maple leaf-like areas, spoke-wheel structures, or arborizing vessels, should prompt consideration of intraepidermal squamous atypia rather than isolated BCC. Histopathologically, both full-thickness epidermal atypia and dermal basaloid tumor nests should be sought; BerEP4 and EMA may assist in distinguishing BCC from squamous differentiation. Regarding adnexal/cyst-associated lesions, duct-like structures, decapitation secretion, bilayered cuboidal lining, trichoblastoma-like structures, or cyst rupture with giant cell reaction may help identify the non-BCC component.5 Adequate sampling and clinicopathologic correlation are required to confirm a genuine second component.

The present findings suggest that most cases have favorable short-term outcomes after local treatment, while prognosis remain determined primarily by the most aggressive component and disease stage. No recurrence or metastasis was recorded in our cohort, which did not differ statistically from the approximately 5% recurrence rate reported by Di Maio et al,7 but the comparison should be interpreted cautiously given the small sample size, non-uniform follow-up, and the exploratory, cross-study nature of the comparison. Recent head and neck cohorts emphasize aggressive histology and positive margins as recurrence-relevant factors.9–11 Accordingly, the absence of recurrence here should be interpreted as a preliminary trend rather than evidence that composite lesions inherently have a more favorable prognosis.

The recent evidence synthesis in Table S1 sharpens the contrast between our cohort and conventional head and neck BCC populations. Contemporary surgical cohorts are dominated by older patients and anatomically complex sun-exposed lesions.7,10 These differences are coherent with the high frequency of congenital or long-standing background lesions in our cases and argue that composite BCC lesions should not be interpreted as a random sample of routine head and neck BCC.

The literature also emphasizes why histologic subtype and anatomic site must remain central even when the overall pattern appears indolent. Population-based studies have shown that tumor-free margins ranged from 60.6% in morpheaform BCC to 83.6% in nodular BCC,12 with incomplete excision rates of 20.6–37.0% for high-risk lesions on the nose, ear, scalp, and periorbital area.13 These findings support our emphasis on spatially resolving each component and specifying the BCC subtype, particularly when a composite lesion includes a new deep, infiltrative, or poorly circumscribed focus.

Treatment and recurrence comparisons require careful calibration. Low recurrence rates in structured surgical cohorts,7,10 should not be generalized to incompletely excised or advanced disease. Recurrence rates are reported higher: 46% of incompletely excised head and neck BCCs,14 30.30% of recurrent BCCs,11 and 44% recurrence, 18% metastasis, and 12% BCC-specific death in locally advanced cases.15 The absence of documented recurrence in our cohort is therefore better presented as a favorable short-term observation, not as evidence that composite lesions are intrinsically low-risk.

This study has several limitations. It was retrospective, single-center, and limited by a small sample size. Follow-up information remained incomplete for 4 of 17 cases, and intervals were not uniform. The available data remain insufficient for a rigorous analysis of recurrence or survival. The classification used was pragmatic, and not all cases met the narrow definition of a collision tumor. The external comparison was exploratory and cross-study. Patient-level and lesion-level measures should be interpreted cautiously.

Conclusion

BCC coexisting with another tumor or precancerous lesion often presented as a recent change within a long-standing head and neck lesion. The three working categories may provide a practical framework for clinicopathologic assessment, but require validation in larger series. Diagnosis should identify each component and the highest-risk BCC subtype. Most patients have favorable short-term outcomes after local treatment; however, prognosis remains determined by the most aggressive tumor component and its stage. Prospective multicenter studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are needed to validate this classification and determine prognostic significance.
