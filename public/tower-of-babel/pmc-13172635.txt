Anabolics in Cancer Patients: To Use or Not to Use?
Authors: A Ram Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With improving cancer survival, the management of osteoporosis in cancer survivors has become increasingly important. Cancer treatment-induced bone loss and aging place many survivors at high risk of fragility fractures. Anabolic agents, including parathyroid hormone (PTH) receptor agonists and the anti-sclerostin antibody romosozumab, effectively stimulate bone formation and reduce fracture risk in the general population. However, their use in cancer patients remains limited because of safety concerns and insufficient clinical evidence. Preclinical studies suggested a potential risk of osteosarcoma with PTH analogues and possible effects on tumor growth or bone metastasis through modulation of the bone microenvironment, although long-term human studies have not demonstrated an increased malignancy risk. In clinical practice, PTH analogues are generally avoided in patients with active malignancy, bone metastases, hypercalcemia, or a history of osteosarcoma. In selected cancer survivors with a sufficiently long diseasefree interval, their cautious use may be considered depending on recurrence risk. Romosozumab may represent another potential option, although supporting evidence remains limited. Overall, the use of anabolic therapy in cancer patients should be individualized, carefully balancing fracture risk against potential oncologic safety concerns.

INTRODUCTION

Cancer survival rates have markedly improved worldwide owing to advances in early diagnosis, rapid progress in treatment modalities, and improved access to multidisciplinary cancer care. The definition of a ‘cancer survivor’ remains complex and controversial, ranging from individuals from the time of diagnosis throughout their lifetime to those who have completed curative-intent treatment (e.g., ≥5 years post-treatment) [1]. As survivorship improves, there is an increasing need to manage longterm adverse effects of cancer therapy and chronic comorbidities, including bone health. Cancer treatment-induced bone loss (CTIBL) refers to rapid and severe bone loss associated with anticancer therapies [2], most commonly in breast and prostate cancers [3]. Moreover, because cancer predominantly affects older individuals, survivors are particularly vulnerable to osteoporosis and fragility fractures, which are associated with increased risks of hospitalization and mortality [4,5].

Osteoporosis is a systemic skeletal disorder characterized by low bone mass and structural deterioration, resulting in increased fracture susceptibility [6]. Anti-osteoporosis therapies are broadly classified into antiresorptive and anabolic agents based on their mechanisms of action. Antiresorptive agents inhibit osteoclast activity, thereby reducing bone remodeling and increasing bone mineral density (BMD) [7]. Current clinical guidelines recommend antiresorptive therapies, such as bisphosphonates and denosumab, as first-line treatment for patients at high risk of fracture [8,9]. In contrast, anabolic agents stimulate new bone formation—an effect not achievable with antiresorptive therapy alone—and are recommended as initial therapy for patients at very high fracture risk, including those with recent fractures, multiple fractures, or very low BMD T-scores. Given the detrimental effects of CTIBL and the advanced age of many cancer survivors, this population is frequently categorized as being at very high fracture risk and may therefore benefit from anabolic therapy.

However, significant safety concerns have limited the real-world use of anabolic agents in patients with cancer. Because of the lack of robust clinical evidence, current guidelines generally adopt a conservative approach. Given the growing population of cancer survivors and the expanding clinical use of anabolic therapies, reassessment of their role in this setting is warranted. This review summarizes key safety concerns, highlights relevant preclinical and clinical evidence, and discusses practical considerations for the use of anabolic agents in cancer patients.

PROVEN EFFICACY OF ANABOLIC THERAPY FOR THE TREATMENT OF OSTEOPOROSIS IN GENERAL POPULATION

The anabolic agents currently used in clinical practice include parathyroid hormone (PTH) receptor agonists (teriparatide and abaloparatide) and the anti-sclerostin antibody romosozumab. Abaloparatide is not yet available in Korea. Teriparatide (PTH [1–34]) and abaloparatide (PTH-related protein [1–34]) activate the PTH receptor, stimulating both bone resorption and formation primarily through remodeling-based bone formation. In contrast, romosozumab, a humanized monoclonal antibody against sclerostin, enhances modeling-based bone formation while simultaneously reducing bone resorption through modulation of the Wnt signaling pathway, resulting in a dual effect [10].

In the pivotal FPT trial, daily teriparatide (20 μg) significantly increased BMD in postmenopausal women after 18 months (lumbar spine 9.7%, femoral neck 2.8%, and total hip 2.6%) and reduced vertebral and non-vertebral fractures by 65% and 53%, respectively, compared with placebo [11]. Similar benefits have been reported in men [12], and teriparatide has also demonstrated superior efficacy to alendronate in glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis [13].

The efficacy of abaloparatide was demonstrated in the ACTIVE trial, where daily abaloparatide (80 μg) for 18 months significantly increased BMD (lumbar spine 11.2%, femoral neck 3.6%, and total hip 4.2%) and reduced vertebral and nonvertebral fractures by 86% and 43%, respectively, compared with placebo [14]. Sequential therapy with abaloparatide followed by alendronate in the ACTIVExtend study maintained fracture risk reduction [15], and similar BMD improvements have been observed in men [16].

Romosozumab has demonstrated robust anabolic efficacy. In the FRAME trial, monthly romosozumab (210 mg) administered for 12 months increased BMD (lumbar spine 13.3%, femoral neck 5.2%, and total hip 6.8%) and reduced the risk of vertebral fractures by 73% compared with placebo [17]. Sequential treatment with romosozumab followed by denosumab further increased BMD and reduced fractures in the FRAME Extension study [18]. The ARCH trial demonstrated greater BMD gains and fracture risk reduction of romosozumab compared with alendronate, and its efficacy in men was confirmed in the BRIDGE study [19,20].

MAJOR CONCERNS REGARDING ANABOLIC THERAPY IN CANCER PATIENTS

There are no dedicated studies evaluating the efficacy and safety of anabolic agents in osteoporotic patients with cancer, and the available clinical evidence remains limited. Although anabolic therapy appears to be effective in improving BMD and reducing fracture risk in patients with cancer as well, its use in this population remains limited due to unresolved safety concerns.

Development of malignancies, including primary bone tumors

The most concerning safety issue associated with anabolic agents is the potential risk of osteosarcoma, initially suggested by preclinical studies in Fischer 344 rats receiving long-term high-dose PTH (1–34) [21]. These studies demonstrated a dose-dependent increase in osteosarcoma incidence in both male and female rats, without increases in other cancer types. Although the pivotal FPT study of teriparatide was terminated early because of these findings, it still demonstrated significant improvements in BMD and reductions in fracture risk [11]. In that study, 40 women developed cancer, with a higher incidence in the placebo group (4%) than in the teriparatide group (2%). Based on these results, teriparatide was approved in 2002 for the treatment of postmenopausal osteoporosis at high risk of fracture. A boxed warning was subsequently included, advising against its use in patients at increased risk of osteosarcoma, such as those with metabolic bone diseases (e.g., Paget’s disease), bone metastases, prior skeletal malignancies, or prior skeletal irradiation. However, long-term patient registries and post-marketing surveillance studies have since demonstrated no increased risk of osteosarcoma associated with teriparatide use in humans [22,23], leading to the recent removal of the boxed warning.

Similarly, preclinical studies of abaloparatide in Fischer 344 rats demonstrated a dose-dependent increase in osteosarcoma without increases in other cancers [24]. In the ACTIVE trial, the incidence of neoplasms (including malignancies) was similar between the abaloparatide (2.4%) and placebo (3.5%) groups [14]. Following its regulatory approval in 2017 for postmenopausal osteoporosis at high fracture risk, abaloparatide carried a boxed warning similar to that of teriparatide, which was removed in 2022.

In a large real-world study of 44,728 patients aged ≥50 years, the incidence of primary bone cancer was 0.02% among users of teriparatide or abaloparatide compared with 0.05% in nonusers, suggesting no increased risk associated with these agents [25].

Although the Wnt signaling pathway regulates the growth of several cell lineages, preclinical studies have not demonstrated increased tumor incidence with romosozumab [26]. Consistently, the pivotal FRAME and ARCH trials showed no significant differences in cancer incidence between the romosozumab and placebo or alendronate groups [17,20].

Impact on bone metastasis and tumor progression

In addition to concerns regarding primary bone tumor, anabolic agents may theoretically promote the growth of existing bone metastases or facilitate cancer dissemination to new skeletal sites. Metastasis is a complex, multistep process in which cancer cells detach from the primary tumor, enter the circulation, and colonize specific niches within the bone [27]. The bone marrow microenvironment—particularly the osteoblast-rich endosteal niche—provides a favorable environment for metastatic cancer cell engraftment.

As PTH acts on the PTH1 receptor expressed on osteoblasts through a remodeling-based mechanism, it may theoretically enhance cancer cell proliferation in response to resorption-related signals. Several preclinical studies have investigated the effects of PTH analogues on bone metastasis or tumor progression (Table 1). Intermittent PTH has been shown to increase metastatic prostate cancer cell foci [28,29], whereas findings in breast cancer models have been inconsistent. Swami et al. [30] reported reduced breast cancer cell engraftment and bone metastasis with prolonged survival, while Kawaragi et al. [31] observed no exacerbation of bone metastases in a breast cancer mouse model. Because the PTH1 receptor is expressed in both osteoblasts and breast cancer cells, PTH may exert protective effects against bone metastasis in certain contexts. PTH treatment has been shown to reduce tumor-derived parathyroid hormone related-peptide (PTHrP) expression, which may attenuate the osteoclast-driven ‘vicious cycle’ of bone metastasis [32]. In contrast, Brown et al. [33] reported increased tumor burden in non-hindlimb skeletal sites without changes in hindlimb metastases. In multiple myeloma models, PTH increased bone density, reduced tumor burden, and delayed tumor progression [34]. These heterogeneous findings likely reflect differences in experimental conditions, including the mode of PTH administration and cancer type.

The role of PTHrP in bone metastasis remains incompletely understood, as both tumor-promoting and tumor-suppressive effects have been reported [35,36]. This uncertainty has raised concern regarding the use of abaloparatide in cancer patients. However, a recent preclinical study demonstrated that abaloparatide increased bone formation and bone volume without promoting tumor progression in breast cancer bone metastasis models [37]. These findings suggest that, despite sharing the same receptor target, teriparatide and abaloparatide may exert differential effects in breast cancer-related bone disease, underscoring the importance of an agent-specific therapeutic approach.

There is a human study that has evaluated the effects of PTH analogues in patients with pre-existing cancer. Diamond et al. [38] reported that 12 months of daily teriparatide (20 μg) increased spine BMD and the bone formation marker procollagen type 1 N-terminal propeptide in 12 patients with multiple myeloma and severe osteoporosis, without new fractures or osteolytic lesions.

A few preclinical studies have examined the effects of anti-sclerostin antibodies in this context (Table 2). In breast cancer models, findings have been inconsistent: one study reported promotion of bone metastasis in Wnt-responsive tumors [39], whereas others demonstrated reduced metastasis and protection against cancer-induced bone destruction [40,41]. In contrast, studies in multiple myeloma models consistently showed increased bone formation and reduced osteolytic lesions [42,43]. Sclerostin selectively inhibits Wnt1 ligands and therefore primarily acts on preosteoblasts. Overall, preclinical studies in breast cancer and multiple myeloma models have shown either neutral or protective effects on tumor progression, with only one study suggesting a potential pro-metastatic effect.

Although a history of cancer is not a formal contraindication to romosozumab use, patients with prior malignancy were excluded from the pivotal FRAME and ARCH trials. Consequently, clinical evidence in cancer patients remains limited. However, emerging data appear encouraging. In a recent single-center prospective observational study, 19 postmenopausal women with cancer received romosozumab for at least 6 months. Approximately half had breast cancer or hematologic malignancies including multiple myeloma, and no cases of cancer progression or recurrence were observed during follow-up, with only one patient developing a new malignancy [44].

PRACTICAL USE OF ANABOLIC AGENTS FOR OSTEOPOROSIS IN PATIENTS WITH CANCER

Considering their therapeutic efficacy and the substantial unmet need in patients at high risk of fracture, the use of anabolic agents in patients with cancer should be approached with caution. Although the tumorigenic effects of PTH analogues observed in animal models appear to be confined to skeletal tissue, and no clinical evidence has demonstrated an increased risk of malignancy in patients treated with these agents, a recent history of cancer is still commonly regarded as a contraindication to their use in clinical practice. Although there is no established definition of what constitutes a ‘recent’ interval, from a pragmatic perspective, a disease-free period of approximately 5 years may represent a reasonable threshold. This raises the question of whether PTH analogues can be safely used in all patients with a remote history of cancer who are currently in remission. For example, the use of PTH analogues may warrant greater caution in cancers with a high propensity for recurrence or bone metastasis, such as breast or prostate cancer. Prostate cancer is typically associated with osteoblastic metastases, whereas breast cancer can present with osteoblastic, osteolytic, or mixed bone metastases. Preclinical studies have shown that PTH treatment increased bone metastases in prostate cancer models with preexisting osteoblastic activity. In contrast, responses in breast cancer models have been inconsistent, depending on the experimental model and the type of PTH analogue used (teriparatide vs. abaloparatide). At present, providing definitive recommendations for these clinical scenarios remains challenging because of the lack of robust clinical evidence. Therefore, multidisciplinary consultation with the treating oncologist is essential to carefully evaluate the risks and benefits before considering PTH analogues. Nevertheless, PTH analogues may be considered after a sufficiently long disease-free interval in cancers associated with a relatively low risk of bone metastasis or recurrence, such as differentiated thyroid cancer. Bone metastasis itself is currently regarded as a contraindication to the use of PTH analogues regardless of cancer type; however, clinical decision-making may need to consider the characteristics of bone metastases (osteoblastic, osteolytic, or mixed) as well as the underlying cancer type. Hypercalcemia is another contraindication to the use of PTH analogues and may be further complicated by hypercalcemia of malignancy in patients with bone metastases. Hypercalcemia of malignancy can arise through several mechanisms, including osteolytic bone destruction, secretion of PTHrP, and increased production of 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D.

Romosozumab is a monoclonal antibody and, therefore, is not expected to directly interfere with cellular DNA or exert genotoxic effects. It remains unclear whether romosozumab confers protection against tumor progression or promotes tumor regression in patients with multiple myeloma or breast cancer, as suggested by preclinical models. However, to date, no tumor progression has been reported in a recent human study involving patients with active cancer, although none had bone metastases [44]. These observations suggest, with caution, that romosozumab may be considered in selected patients with cancer, including those with a history of bone malignancy or prior skeletal radiation therapy, which are typically regarded as contraindications for PTHrP analogues. Collectively, romosozumab may represent a potential therapeutic option for cancer survivors with osteoporosis or at high risk of fracture, although the available evidence remains limited. CONSIDERATION FOR THE OFF-LABEL USE OF ANABOLIC AGENTS IN CANCER PATIENTS

Beyond their role in osteoporosis management, anabolic agents are sometimes used off-label to promote fracture healing or the resolution of osteonecrosis of the jaw (ONJ). In such cases, anabolic agents are typically used for a shorter duration (e.g., 3 to 6 months) than in standard osteoporosis treatment; therefore, their potential benefits may outweigh the adverse effects.

Fracture healing

Given its anabolic effects on the skeleton, PTH administration has been proposed as a potential strategy to promote fracture healing. Experimental studies suggest that teriparatide accelerates chondrocyte recruitment and differentiation—key processes in early endochondral ossification—and enhances osteoblast activity while upregulating fibroblast growth factor-2 expression [45]. Early animal studies supported this concept [46]; however, clinical evidence in humans has been inconsistent. A recent meta-analysis of 11 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving 1,452 patients found no significant effect of PTH analogues on fracture healing rates, although teriparatide was associated with improvements in pain and functional outcomes [47]. Another meta-analysis similarly reported no significant benefit in fracture healing in non-cancer populations, largely due to heterogeneity and the overall low quality of the included studies [48].

Evidence regarding abaloparatide in fracture healing remains limited to animal models, which have demonstrated enhanced callus formation, osteogenesis, and fracture bridging, suggesting potential benefits for bone repair [49,50].

Romosozumab is theoretically expected to facilitate fracture healing through activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, supported by promising findings in animal models [51,52]. However, two recent small RCTs in humans did not demonstrate significant improvements in fracture healing time or physical performance [53,54]. The lack of clinical benefit may be related to treatment timing, as romosozumab was initiated immediately after fracture in these studies. Moreover, fracture healing largely depends on remodeling processes, whereas romosozumab primarily enhances modeling-based bone formation.

To date, evidence regarding the effects of anabolic agents on fracture healing in patients with cancer remains limited, and further studies are required to clarify their potential role in this population.

Osteonecrosis of jaw

ONJ remains a significant concern in cancer patients receiving antiresorptive therapy for bone metastases or osteoporosis. The reported incidence ranges from approximately 1% to 15%, with rates of up to 10% in patients with bone metastases treated with denosumab [55]. Identified risk factors include antiresorptive therapy, diabetes mellitus, immunosuppression, poor oral hygiene, and concomitant glucocorticoid use. Resolution rates vary according to ONJ stage and comorbidities, highlighting the importance of preventive dental care and appropriate management.

Teriparatide has shown potential benefits in the treatment of ONJ by enhancing bone remodeling and promoting bone healing. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 26 studies involving 111 patients reported an overall ONJ resolution rate of 59.5% [56]. Among these cases, 45.1% of patients received teriparatide for an average duration of approximately 7 months, and more than half underwent combination therapy, including surgery, antibiotics, or laser treatment. Successful resolution was more common in early-stage ONJ and in patients receiving combination therapy.

A RCT evaluated the effect of teriparatide in cancer patients with ONJ, including those with breast cancer, prostate cancer, multiple myeloma, and malignant bone disease [55]. Thirty-four patients with 47 lesions were randomized to receive either placebo or teriparatide 20 μg daily for 8 weeks. At 52 weeks, ONJ healing occurred in 45.4% of lesions in the teriparatide group, which was significantly higher than in the placebo group (33.3%). Teriparatide also reduced bony defect size and showed an earlier therapeutic response, without evidence of new malignancies or progression of existing cancers during the 12-month follow-up.

In contrast, clinical data regarding the use of abaloparatide or romosozumab for ONJ are currently lacking.

CONCLUSIONS

The use of anabolic agents for osteoporosis in patients with cancer requires careful consideration. PTH analogues (teriparatide and abaloparatide) are generally not recommended in patients with active malignancy, bone metastases, hypercalcemia, or a history of osteosarcoma. However, PTH analogues may be considered in cancer survivors with a remote history of malignancy who have maintained a disease-free interval of ≥5 years depending on cancer type and recurrence risk. It remains unclear whether PTH analogues have detrimental effects on tumor progression or bone metastasis, and further studies are needed to clarify this issue. Of note, a history of cancer is not considered a contraindication for romosozumab, and it may represent a potential therapeutic option in these settings, although the available clinical evidence remains limited. Teriparatide may help improve fracture-related pain or functional outcomes; however, further studies are needed to clarify its benefits for fracture healing. In contrast, abaloparatide and romosozumab are not currently recommended for fracture healing because of limited evidence. For ONJ, teriparatide has shown some potential benefit, including in patients with cancer, whereas data for abaloparatide and romosozumab are currently lacking. Overall, decisions regarding the initiation of anabolic agents in patients with cancer should be individualized, taking into account cancer status, fracture risk, and the balance between proven therapeutic benefits and potential safety risks.
