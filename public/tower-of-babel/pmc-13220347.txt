A prospective cohort study testing whether oral cavity mucosal tissue variability influences elastic scattering spectroscopy (ESS) measures
Authors: M Sakharkar, G Spokas, P Jalihal, L Berry, E Rodriguez-Diaz, IJ Bigio, GA Grillone, GP Krisciunas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:Elastic Scattering Spectroscopy (ESS) has been shown to accurately discriminate between benign and malignant tissue in the oral cavity, which makes it a promising technology to assist with intraoperative oral cancer margin guidance. However, some spectroscopy-based technologies are influenced by locoregional histopathological differences, which can influence diagnostic accuracy and data acquisition procedures. The objective of this study was to assess the extent to which ESS spectral readings of mucosal tissue varied between different anatomic regions of the oral cavity in a cohort of healthy adults.Methods:The study included 30 participants with no known history of smoking, vaping, or other substance use-related carcinogenic exposures, with normal oral mucosa, and without history of head or neck cancer or other inflammatory conditions. ESS measurements were obtained at predefined anatomic sites within the oral cavity, and spectra were grouped into five classes based on histopathologic similarities of tissue architecture (Class 1: soft palate, mucosal lip, buccal mucosa; Class 2: retromolar trigone, hard palate, gingiva; Class 3: dorsal tongue; Class 4: lateral tongue; Class 5: floor of mouth, ventral tongue). Random forests were used to build predictive models and Leave-One-Patient-Out (LOPO) cross-validation used to obtain performance statistics. Statistical analyses included sensitivity, specificity, negative predictive value (NPV), positive predictive value (PPV), and overall model accuracy.Results:Overall analysis yielded sensitivity = 52.2%, specificity = 88.4%, and accuracy = 0.52. The overall mean weighted F1 score was 0.53. Using a one-vs-all approach of the five anatomic sites, sensitivity = 43.4%–62.1%, specificity = 85%–91%, PPV = 29.2%–61.5%, and NPV = 78.3%–95.5%.Conclusion:Discriminatory accuracy between pairs of oral cavity subsites was moderate, but still significantly above random chance (0.20 for five classes). This supports the contention that the success of ESS in identifying malignancy is robust against locoregional variations in “normal” tissues within the oral cavity. These data further support the application of ESS as a valuable adjunct tool for oral cancer margin guidance by demonstrating that ESS is tissue agnostic in the oral cavity.Keywords: Elastic scattering spectroscopy, Machine learning, Technology, Mouth neoplasms, Spectrum analysis

Introduction

The oral cavity gives rise to nearly 3% of all new cancers in the US annually, comprising nearly 60,000 individuals, an interval increase over the last decade [1,2]. Most nonmetastatic oral cavity cancers are treated surgically [3,4]. In these cases, surgical margins during resection are an independent prognostic factor for mortality, and are a factor that surgeons can significantly influence [4]. Achieving negative margins on first frozen section has been associated with enhanced patient outcomes, quality of life and 5-year survival rates, and with reduced overall costs, morbidity, and recurrence rates. [5-7]. However, assessing margins currently relies on intraoperative visual inspection and palpation by the surgeon, and there are no objective methods of assessment that can be used intraoperatively [5].

While frozen section assessment remains the gold standard for intraoperative margin assessment, it has some limitations. Definitions of margin negativity are inconsistent between institutions. Accuracy can be limited by the need for real-time feedback to surgeons, and frozen section analyses are subject to sampling errors [8,9].

Several spectroscopy-based technologies have been explored to enhance standard margin assessment practice, specifically within the context of the oral cavity [10]. One challenge associated with some of these spectroscopic technologies is that tissue composition and variation by site within the oral cavity may act as a confounding factor [10]. One example is Raman spectroscopy, which is sensitive to biomolecular composition but requires longer integration times and dark background, hence is less clinically friendly. Raman spectroscopy has demonstrated high degrees of accuracy in oral cavity site classification, margin guidance, and pathology discrimination [11]. However, these spectral patterns have been shown to be highly tissue dependent [12]. As a result, using Raman spectroscopy necessitates adjustment based on tissue composition for accurate classification and analysis.

Elastic Scattering Spectroscopy (ESS) is an in vivo optical measurement methodology that is sensitive to microstructural changes in cells and ECM, and that can be utilized to understand different backscattered spectral patterns and, by extension, physical properties of tissue [13-17]. It has been applied in a wide variety of diagnostic settings, focusing on dysplastic and cancerous tissue identification including melanoma, leukoplakia, esophageal dysplasia, thyroid cancer, breast cancer, and colon cancer [18-28]. ESS has also yielded promising initial results for discriminating between benign and malignant tissue in the oral cavity [20,29]. However, the influence of any anatomic sub-site histologic differences on ESS accuracy has not been explored. Thus, this study's aim was to test for locoregional differences in ESS reflectance patterns acquired in the oral cavity among healthy adults. The data aimed to inform whether ESS data processing and analysis would require “adjusting” for anatomic sub-location to maximize ESS tissue classification accuracy.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Boston University Medical Center. Informed consent was obtained from all participants in the study. All methods involving participants were carried out in accordance with IRB policies and guideline regulations, in accordance with the 1964 Helsinki declaration and comparable ethical standards.

Instrumentation

The ESS system invokes a broadband pulsed xenon arc lamp (Hamamatsu Corp., Bridgewater, NJ, USA), spectrometer/microcontroller board (Avantes Inc., Louisville, CO, USA), power supply, and built-in computer with custom software, housed in a clinically adaptable enclosure. ESS measurements involve short pulses of light (<1 ms) encompassing the near-ultraviolet spectral region to through the visible to near-infrared regions (300–900 nm). The backscattered spectrum is read by a spectrometer with a detector array and fast electronic shutter that minimizes effects of background light. As these are in vivo measurements, UV light at wavelengths shorter than 320 nm (UVB and UVC light) is filtered out to minimize risk. Backscattered optical reflectance was recorded. A comprehensive description of the ESS system and probes has been previously described in detail. [30].

Study participants

Data from healthy adult volunteers were collected in an academic university campus setting. Volunteer participants consisted of students, researchers and trainees in this setting. All students and trainees recruited did not report to, nor were under the authority of, leaders in this study. Participants were identified using recruitment materials and relied on self-reported confirmation of demographic and inclusion criteria.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

All participants were pre-screened through self-reporting before consent to ensure they met inclusion and exclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria were healthy adults who speak English. Exclusion criteria were a past medical history of inflammatory conditions, head or neck cancer, or dysplastic oral tissue, vaping or smoking in the last six months, and chewing tobacco in the last six months.

Clinical measurements

Relevant demographics were collected from participants through self-report. Acquisition of ESS measurements involved utilizing a 2-mm fiberoptic probe by gently pressing the probe tip onto various surfaces within the oral mucosal tissues. A button was pressed on the ESS device to initiate the spectral sequence and to acquire measures at each location. Up to four separate measurements were taken for each site in the oral mucosa, with five rapid (~3 s) bursts of spectral reflectance per measurement (enabling averaging to reduce noise). In total, 11 bilateral sites within the oral mucosa were sampled from the upper mucosal lip, lower mucosal lip, gingiva, buccal mucosa, lateral tongue, dorsal tongue, ventral tongue, hard palate, soft palate, floor of mouth, and retromolar trigones. To calibrate for system response, spectral reference measures were acquired with by taking measurements from a diffuse reflectance standard (Spectralon, Labsphere Inc., North Hutton NH USA) with each probe. The 11 oral mucosa sites were grouped based on known histopathological characteristics into five total subsites (histopathological classes) for analysis [31-33].

Data processing and analysis

All ESS spectral averages were processed prior to analysis. Raw measurements contained n = 288 bands between 320 and 900 nm. Pre-processing steps were performed to effect smoothing. These included applying a moving average filter with a window of 3 points (or 5 nm) and normalizing all spectra to the measured intensity at 650 nm. A predictive machine learning model was then developed using a random forest model that compared spectral measurements across the predefined histopathological classes and among various individual mucosal measurement sites. A pictorial representation of the class-by-class groupings of the oral cavity subsites.

Leave-one-patient-out cross-validation was used to obtain performance estimates. This approach trains a classification model using data from all but one patient, using data from the excluded patient for testing. The process is repeated until each patient has been excluded from the training process. Such a statistical approach tends to be overly optimistic but is well suited for identifying trends.

Overall accuracy (ACC), positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), sensitivity (SN), specificity (SP) and F1 score metrics were used to assess model performance. These metrics were calculated, where appropriate, using a one-versus-all approach.

Results

A total of 30 participants were enrolled, generating 551 ESS measures that met appropriate quality criteria (adequate signal-to-noise ratio) to be included in the analysis. Of the participants, 70% were male. The average age of participants was 27.3 +/−5.5 years. As these were healthy young volunteers, collection of additional demographic data was deemed unnecessary.

A multiclass random forest classifier was trained to evaluate whether ESS could differentiate between five distinct anatomical subsites in the oral cavity with the results displayed in a confusion matrix (Fig. 2). The classifier achieved an overall accuracy of 52.1% with macro F1 of 0.482 and weighted F1 of 0.533.

Model performance varied between different classes, so individual per-class accuracies were also computed by computing the ratio of true positives for each class to the total number samples for each class. Discussion

In this project, we aimed to study whether ESS could accurately differentiate among normal oral cavity tissue classes. The model demonstrated discriminant accuracies that were higher than 20%, or chance probability, between any pair of the 5 tissue classes. The classification accuracy was moderate overall for the 5 oral subsites, and the model performed relatively well in distinguishing between the adjacent regions (lateral and ventral tongue, for example). This suggests that ESS captures real, reproducible differences in spectral data among different mucosal tissue classes. Given previous reports demonstrating high accuracy in differentiating between normal and cancerous tissues, it is possible that when detecting malignancy in the oral cavity, the ESS signatures can be accurately trained, despite the fact that ESS is also sensitive to anatomic variations in the mucosa. We speculate that this may be due to the fact that on a subcellular level, cancerous tissue may be so different from host tissue that any variations in subcellular profiles between anatomic regions of the oral cavity induce minor perturbations in the spectra. Also, since the ESS algorithms and AI-facilitated statistical approaches were trained on a larger dataset that included measured obtained from various anatomic locations within the oral cavity, the machine learning may be able to account for any variations in normal mucosal tissue.

Our histological classes were predefined based on known characteristics of the oral mucosal tissues. Prior histopathological studies have identified key subcellular similarities between different areas of the oral mucosa [31,33]. Histologically, oral mucosa can be divided into three layers, the oral epithelium, lamina propria, and dense irregular connective tissue. Classifications of oral mucosa include lining mucosa, masticatory mucosa, and specialized mucosa. Lining mucosa is also known as non-keratinized epithelium and includes buccal, labial mucosa. Prior histopathological studies have chosen to group oral mucosa by type of oral epithelium – whether keratinized or non-keratinized [34,35]. Keratinized layers include all masticatory mucosal surfaces – including the dorsal tongue, hard palate, and gingiva. Uniquely, the hard palate is partially keratinized epithelium compared to the other masticatory mucosal surfaces. These histopathological characteristics of oral mucosa subtype and degree of keratinization informed our classifications.

Our classifications were further informed by multiple prior spectral classification studies. Class 1 was defined as the soft palate, mucosal lip, and buccal mucosa. McGee et al. utilized anatomy-based algorithms to develop spectral similarity scores and found that the soft palate and buccal mucosa had a similarity score of 77%, with an AUC of 0.91 when divided into classes [31]. Histopathologically, the soft palate, mucosal lip, and buccal mucosa are all non-keratinized mucosa. Class 2 was defined as the retromolar trigone, hard palate, and gingiva. Muller et al. utilized spectral patterns of reflectance of superficial keratin to define keratinized tissue types, including gingiva and hard palate [35]. The retromolar trigone is histologically dissimilar to the hard palate and gingiva and may present as an extension of the buccal mucosa. However, due to underlying dentin, this area may spectrally reflect more like gingiva and hard palate. Class 3 was defined as the dorsal tongue. The dorsal tongue can be uniquely delineated due to its relatively low similarity scores in prior spectral studies, unique histopathological characteristics, including papillae and taste buds, and low class overlap when observing porphyrin-like fluorescence wavelengths. Furthermore, autofluorescence characteristics of healthy oral mucosa at various anatomical sites demonstrated a low number of classification errors in the dorsal tongue when classified as a standalone [36]. Class 4 was defined as the lateral tongue. The lateral tongue was uniquely characterized due to its relatively increased vascularity patterns. Unique porphyrin peaks are also found in the lateral tongue [36]. Additionally, a prior study utilizing ESS to predict participants with laryngeal leukoplakia versus laryngeal cancer found that the lateral tongue was the most sensitive and specific measurement for abnormal distal pathology [37]. Therefore, we chose to delineate the lateral tongue as a unique class. Class 5 was classified as the floor of mouth and ventral tongue. The floor of mouth and ventral tongue are non-keratinized tissues. In comparison to Class 1, these tissues have thinner epithelium and are highly vascular. Furthermore, prior spectral studies of these tissues have shown high degrees of anatomical-based spectral modeling overlap with an AUC of 0.97 and with 93% similarity scores [31].

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first of its kind that approaches subsite analysis based on histopathological criteria in the oral cavity using ESS. Our results indicate that ESS exhibits some sensitivity to normal mucosal variation outside of the oral cavity, which is supported by prior research [33,38]. Other spectral techniques, such as Raman spectroscopy, have been shown to detect subtle differences in normal tissue architecture and composition within the oral cavity [11,12,32,39,40]. However, given Raman spectroscopy is uniquely sensitive to background light and to noise from tissue architecture in the oral cavity, this has led to poor model performance on pathologic specimens without adjustment for tissue type for in-vivo measurements [12]. As a result, this poses a major limitation of Raman spectroscopy utility and ease of application clinically. The ability for ESS to accurately differentiate between normal and malignant tissue without adjustment for tissue type in the oral cavity is a relative advantage that could facilitate clinical implementation.

To ensure capture of truly “normal” mucosa, this study chose to sample a healthy, non-smoking cohort to limit any pathological or inflammatory variants in local tissue measurement. Smoking can cause major cellular differences in the oral mucosa in comparison to nonsmokers. There is an increase in the number of nucleolar organizer regions (NORs) which indicates higher cellular proliferation and activity within the smoking population [41,42]. Additionally, smoking can increase binucleation in comparison to non-smokers [43]. Langerhan cells, cells within the oral mucosa important for the immune response, were found to be increased as well within smokers in comparison to nonsmokers [44]. There are many cellular architecture differences between smokers and non-smokers, and ESS has the ability to detect these differences within the oral mucosa [20,45,46]. Thus, this study design was limited to non-smokers to help isolate the differences among oral cavity subsites that are unrelated to smoking-induced inflammatory changes.

This study is not without limitations. First, the sample size utilized for this study was relatively small (n = 30). Second, the inclusion criteria utilized a group of students and trainees in our institution, which may limit generalizability (e.g., relative to age). However, by utilizing a group of students and trainees that were on average, a younger demographic, we were able to mitigate age-related pathologies and environmental exposures that could differently affect and confound our spectral findings, so this approach could be considered a strength of the study design. Finally, there is no universally accepted or standardized metric on how to approach groupings of tissues. However, we believe our a-priori classes were appropriate, since they were empirically informed by previous histological and spectral studies.

Conclusion

ESS technology differentiates natural variations in mucosal histology among 5 anatomic regions within the oral cavity with moderate accuracy. However, the ability to differentiate between anatomic regions does not seem to appreciably influence ESS accuracy for cancer detection, making ESS well suited for future clinical application.
