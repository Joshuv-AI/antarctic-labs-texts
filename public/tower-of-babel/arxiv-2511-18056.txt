Hierarchical Linkage Clustering Beyond Binary Trees and Ultrametrics
Maximilien Dreveton, Matthias Grossglauser, Daichi Kuroda, Patrick Thiran

Abstract
Hierarchical clustering seeks to uncover nested structures in data by constructing a tree of clusters, where deeper levels reveal finer-grained relationships. Traditional methods, including linkage approaches, face three major limitations: (i) they always return a hierarchy, even if none exists, (ii) they are restricted to binary trees, even if the true hierarchy is non-binary, and (iii) they are highly sensitive to the choice of linkage function. In this paper, we address these issues by introducing the notion of a valid hierarchy and defining a partial order over the set of valid hierarchies. We prove the existence of a finest valid hierarchy, that is, the hierarchy that encodes the maximum information consistent with the similarity structure of the data set. In particular, the finest valid hierarchy is not constrained to binary structures and, when no hierarchical relationships exist, collapses to a star tree. We propose a simple two-step algorithm that first constructs a binary tree via a linkage method and then prunes it to enforce validity. We establish necessary and sufficient conditions on the linkage function under which this procedure exactly recovers the finest valid hierarchy, and we show that all linkage functions satisfying these conditions yield the same hierarchy after pruning. Notably, classical linkage rules such as single, complete, and average satisfy these conditions, whereas Ward’s linkage fails to do so.

Introduction

Hierarchical clustering is the task of inferring a tree of clusters, where deeper levels of the hierarchy uncover finer-grained structures. Unlike flat clustering, which partitions the dataset into
k
groups, hierarchical clustering identifies nested clusters and captures their containment relationships. A hierarchy is represented by a rooted tree whose leaves correspond to individual clusters, and internal vertices to the merge of smaller clusters into larger overarching groups.

Hierarchical clustering algorithms typically fall into two main categories: divisive (
top-down
) and agglomerative (
bottom-up
). Top-down algorithms (such as bisection
k
-means or recursive sparsest cut) construct the hierarchy by recursively splitting the data into two parts, working from the root downward
(
8
;
23
)
. In contrast, bottom-up algorithms, such as linkage, iteratively merge the two most similar clusters and recompute the similarity between the newly formed cluster with the non-merged ones, thus building the hierarchy from the bottom upward
(
13
;
18
;
20
)
.
A key advantage of hierarchical clustering is that it does not require specifying the number of clusters in advance, which is a significant benefit when the true structure of the data is unknown, and provides the user with a finer representation of the data than flat clustering.

Nonetheless, these hierarchical clustering algorithms have a major drawback: the inferred tree representing the hierarchy is always binary. Indeed, top-down approaches recursively split the data into two parts, whereas bottom-up methods merge clusters in pairs. This is problematic, as these algorithms (i) cannot recover non-binary hierarchies and (ii) always return a hierarchy, even when the data does not exhibit one. This can in particular lead to infer hierarchical structures that do not exist. Finally, (iii) the outcome of agglomerative algorithms is highly sensitive to the choice of linkage criterion, which defines how cluster similarities are computed. Indeed, at each iteration, the similarity between the newly formed cluster and the remaining ones can be defined in several ways, such as the minimum (single linkage), maximum (complete linkage), or average (average linkage) pairwise distance between their elements. Different linkage functions can yield drastically different trees on the
same
dataset, with no principled way to determine which is more appropriate. Together, these issues raise fundamental questions about the robustness and interpretability of traditional hierarchical clustering.

The purpose of this paper is to overcome these limitations. To address them, we enlarge the hypothesis class from binary hierarchies to the class of all hierarchical tree structures (allowing arbitrary branching, including the degenerate “star-tree” structure). This expansion ensures realizability: any true underlying hierarchy can now be represented in the model class. However, this expansion also dramatically increases the combinatorial complexity of the search space: while there are already
\Theta(4^{k-1})
binary trees with
k
labeled leaves,
1

C_{k-1}
binary trees with
k

C_{k}=\frac{1}{k}\binom{2k}{k}

C_{k}\sim 4^{k}k^{-3/2}\pi^{-1/2}

To navigate through this huge space of all possible hierarchies, we introduce the notion of a
valid hierarchy
over a finite set
\mathcal{X}
of items equipped with a similarity measure
s\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
. We define a natural partial order over valid hierarchies and show that this partially ordered set admits a unique greatest element. Because this greatest element dominates all other valid hierarchies under the partial order, we refer to it as the
finest valid hierarchy
, as it encodes the maximum information compatible with the similarity function. Importantly, this finest valid hierarchy is not necessarily binary. Moreover, in the absence of any meaningful hierarchical structure, the finest valid hierarchy degenerates into a star tree, where all elements connect directly to the root, and thus correctly captures the absence of a meaningful hierarchy.

Moreover, we observe that the finest valid hierarchy extends the classical correspondence between ultrametrics and dendrograms
(
18
;
7
;
9
;
10
)
. Indeed, when the similarity function
s
arises from an ultrametric, the finest valid hierarchy coincides with the unique dendrogram representing that ultrametric.
Thus, our framework generalizes this well-known equivalence to the broader class of arbitrary similarities for which no additional metric or ultrametric assumption is made. We discuss this point in more details in Section
4.1
.

To construct the finest valid hierarchies, we propose a two-step algorithm. In the first step, we build a binary tree using a linkage method. Because the resulting tree may not define a valid hierarchy, the second step prunes it by removing the internal vertices that violate validity, thereby removing incorrect splits. Unlike traditional linkage methods, this two-step bottom-up procedure (i) does not necessarily yield a binary tree, and (ii) defaults to a star tree when the data contains no hierarchical structure. Consequently, it provides a principled and robust approach to hierarchical clustering that simultaneously addresses the shortcomings of classical linkage-based methods.

We establish necessary and sufficient conditions on the linkage function under which this two-step procedure exactly recovers the finest valid hierarchy. Importantly, these conditions imply that all linkage functions satisfying them yield the same pruned hierarchy, regardless of their specific merging criteria. This result resolves one of the major drawbacks of agglomerative clustering, namely its strong dependence on the choice of linkage. Crucially, we also show that classical linkage rules such as single, complete, and average linkage satisfy these necessary and sufficient conditions, whereas Ward’s linkage does not. This highlights a sharp conceptual distinction between these methods with important practical implications for real-world applications.

Moreover, many axiomatic approaches to hierarchical clustering have sought to characterize linkage rules from first principles, and a recurring outcome of these analyses is the special status of single linkage. In particular, when enforcing natural invariance and consistency axioms, single linkage often emerges as the unique admissible rule (see, for example,
17
;
26
;
9
and the discussion in Section
4
). However, despite its appealing theoretical properties, single linkage is well known to suffer from the chaining effect, where clusters may grow by successively linking distant points through intermediate ones. This behavior often produces elongated, “caterpillar-like” trees that poorly capture compact cluster structure.
Our results show that pruning eliminates this degeneracy. More broadly, our validity-based framework does not privilege any particular linkage function: any linkage rule satisfying the necessary and sufficient conditions of Theorem
5
recovers the same finest valid hierarchy after pruning. In this way, pruning unifies the behavior of linkage methods within a single theoretical framework.

2.2
Valid Clusters and Valid Hierarchies

Consider a function
s\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
that measures the similarity between pairs of items in the finite set
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}
. We only assume that (i)
s
is symmetric,
i.e.

s(x,y)=s(y,x)
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
, and that (ii) self-similarity dominates pairwise similarity,
i.e.

s(x,x)>s(x,y)
whenever
x\neq y
.

We refer to any subset of
\mathcal{X}
as a
cluster
. A cluster is said to be
valid
with respect to the similarity function
s
if the similarity between any two items inside the cluster is strictly greater than their similarity with any item outside the cluster.

Definition 2

(Valid Cluster)
.

A subset
C\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is a
valid cluster
with respect to
s
if

\forall x,y\in C\>\forall z\in\mathcal{X}\setminus C\colon\quad s(x,y)>s(x,z).

In particular, all singletons
\{x_{1}\},\dots,\{x_{k}\}
are valid clusters, as well as the full set
\mathcal{X}
itself. This notion of validity has appeared in prior work under different names: in
4
it is referred to as
strict separation
,
while in
1
it is called
nice clustering
. A more detailed discussion of these connections can be found in Section
4.2
.

Before extending the notion of validity from individual clusters to entire hierarchies, it is useful to note a simple structural property: two valid clusters cannot partially overlap. This property ensures that the collection of all valid clusters forms a laminar family, which will later justify viewing it as a valid hierarchy.

Lemma 1

(Validity implies laminarity)
.

Let
C_{1},C_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
be two valid clusters with respect to
s
. Then
C_{1}\cap C_{2}\in\{\emptyset,C_{1},C_{2}\}
.

Proof.

Assume, for contradiction, that
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
overlap but that neither contains the other.
Then there exist
a\in C_{1}\cap C_{2}
,
b\in C_{1}\setminus C_{2}
, and
c\in C_{2}\setminus C_{1}
.
Because
C_{1}
is valid, we have
s(a,b)>s(a,c)
, while the validity of
C_{2}
yields
s(a,c)>s(a,b)
, a contradiction. Hence the two sets
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
satisfy the laminarity property.
∎

Next, observe that any tree
T\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{X})
defines a hierarchy among its leaves
\{x_{1}\},\cdots,\{x_{k}\}
: from the bottom up, leaves of
T
merge into branches, and these branches merge further up into larger branches until they reach the root. Each vertex
t
of a tree is a cluster, which may or may not be valid (albeit the root
\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{k}\}
and the leaves
\{x_{1}\},\cdots,\{x_{k}\}
of any tree are always valid). We say that the hierarchy defined by a tree
T\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{X})
is
valid
with respect to the similarity function
s
if all the vertices of
T
are valid clusters. This leads to the following definition.

Definition 3
.

Consider a finite set
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{k}\}
, a similarity function
s\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and a tree
T\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{X})
.

is a
valid hierarchy
over
\mathcal{X}
with respect to

iff every vertex

is a valid cluster. In other words, we must have

\displaystyle\forall t\in T\colon\quad\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in t,\\
z\in\mathcal{X}\backslash t\end{subarray}}\,s(x,y)-s(x,z)\ >\ 0.

(2.1)

We denote by
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{X},s)
the set of valid hierarchies over
\mathcal{X}
with respect to
s
.

This definition imposes that the similarity between two distinct items
x
and

that are close in the hierarchy defined by

is strictly larger than the similarity with a third item

located further away in the hierarchy. More precisely, the validity condition imposes that for all triplets of items
x,y,z\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\mathrm{lca}(x,y)\subsetneq\mathrm{lca}(x,z)
, we have
s(x,y)>\max\{s(x,z),s(y,z)\}
.

2.3
Partial Order between Hierarchies

The previous sections introduced the basic objects of interest: trees, clusters, and valid hierarchies. We now formalize how hierarchies can be compared.

Definition 4

(Partial order on
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{X})
)
.

For two trees
T,T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{X})
, we write

T\subseteq T^{\prime}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\forall\,t\in T\colon t\in T^{\prime}.

In words,
T\subseteq T^{\prime}
means that every cluster contained in
T
also appears in
T^{\prime}
.

The inclusion relation
\subseteq
is reflexive, antisymmetric, and transitive, and thus defines an order on the set of trees
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{X})
. Continuing the example of Figure
1
, we have
T_{1}\subseteq T_{2}
, and we say that
T_{2}

contains

T_{1}
. Because neither
T_{3}\subseteq T_{1}
nor
T_{1}\subseteq T_{3}
holds, the relationship
\subseteq
is only a
partial order
. Finally, we write
T\subsetneq T^{\prime}
if
T^{\prime}
contains
T
but
T^{\prime}\neq T
.

This order
\subseteq
naturally restricts to the subset of valid hierarchies
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{X},s)\subseteq\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{X})
, allowing us to compare hierarchies according to the refinement of their cluster families. Intuitively, if
T\subseteq T^{\prime}
, then
T^{\prime}
represents a
finer

T
while potentially adding new intermediate groupings. The greatest element of this order will later be shown to be the finest valid hierarchy
T_{*}
.

3.1
Existence of the Finest Valid Hierarchy

Using the convention that
\min(\emptyset)=\infty
, we observe that the star tree
T_{0}
, where all leaves
\{x_{1}\},\cdots,\{x_{k}\}
connect directly to the root
\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{k}\}
, verifies Condition (
2.1
) for
any
similarity function
s
. This implies that the set
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{X},s)
of valid hierarchy is never empty. Nonetheless, because it always belongs to
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{X},s)
, irrespectively of the similarity function
s
,
T_{0}
does not bring any non-trivial information about the structure induced by the similarity function
s
over the set of items
\mathcal{X}
. Therefore, when the star tree
T_{0}
is the only valid hierarchy, it indicates an
absence of hierarchy
among
\mathcal{X}
.

We are, however, left with the following question:
If there are several trees defining valid hierarchies, which one should be preferred?
To answer this question, first observe that any tree
T\neq T_{0}
defining a valid hierarchy should be preferred over the star tree
T_{0}
, because
T
offers more insights on the hierarchical structure of
\mathcal{X}
than
T_{0}
. Moreover, given a tree
T\in\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{X},s)
defining a valid hierarchy, we notice that any tree
T^{\prime}\subseteq T
also defines a valid hierarchy. Indeed, the set of inequalities implied by the valid hierarchy
T^{\prime}
is included in the set of inequalities implied by
T
. Therefore, we should prefer
T
over any
T^{\prime}\subseteq T
. For instance, the trees
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
in Figure
1
are both valid with respect to the similarity function given in Example
1
, but
T_{2}
captures more details about the hierarchy than
T_{1}
. Another implication of
T_{1}\subseteq T_{2}
is that
T_{2}
being a valid hierarchy implies that
T_{1}
is also valid; the converse is not true.
2
2

2

To see that, consider for example the vertex
\{4,5\}\in T_{2}\backslash T_{1}
. The fact that
T_{1}
is a valid hierarchy does not imply that for any

,

, which is required to establish that

is a valid hierarchy.

However, the relationship
\subseteq
defines only a
partial order
over the set of valid hierarchies
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{X},s)
. We could therefore encounter two valid hierarchies

and
T^{\prime}
, such that none contains the other. The following theorem solves this issue by establishing that the set of valid hierarchies admits a greatest element
T_{*}(\mathcal{X},s)
for the partial order
\subseteq
. Recall that in a partially ordered set, the greatest element, if it exists, is always unique and by definition satisfies
T\subseteq T_{*}(\mathcal{X},s)
for any valid hierarchy
T\in\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{X},s)
. Therefore, the tree
T_{*}(\mathcal{X},s)
is
the
valid hierarchy that contains the most information about the hierarchical structure of

the

of

with respect to

Theorem 2

(Existence of the Finest Valid Hierarchy)
.

Let
T_{*}(\mathcal{X},s)
be the set of all valid clusters.
Then,
T_{*}(\mathcal{X},s)
is the greatest element of the partially ordered set
(\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{X},s),\subseteq)
.

Proof.

Let
T_{*}=T_{*}(\mathcal{X},s)
be the set of all valid clusters.

By definition, any valid hierarchy

. Therefore,

is an upper bound of

Therefore,

3.2
Construction of the Finest Valid Hierarchy

We now demonstrate how to construct the finest valid hierarchy.

3.2.1
Linkage Algorithms

In this section, we present a simple algorithm for recovering the finest valid hierarchy. Traditionally, hierarchy recovery is performed using variants of
linkage
. A linkage method is specified by a rule that updates the similarities between clusters as they are iteratively merged. Starting from the partition of
\mathcal{X}
into the singletons (
\{x_{1}\},\cdots,\{x_{k}\}
), at each step the two most similar clusters are merged, and the similarity between the resulting cluster and the remaining ones is determined by the chosen rule.
For instance, suppose that the items
\{x_{1}\}
and
\{x_{2}\}
are merged at the first step. The linkage rule then prescribes how to compute the similarity between the new cluster
\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
and the remaining clusters
\{x_{a}\}
for
a\in\{3,\dots,k\}
.

There is a large variety of linkage rules. Moreover, different linkage rules typically produce different hierarchical structures, and the choice of the rule has a direct impact on the recovered hierarchy. To capture this impact more generally, we assume a
general linkage process
defined by an update function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}\times\mathbb{N}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
. When two clusters
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
are merged to form
t_{1}\cup t_{2}
, the similarity between
t_{1}\cup t_{2}
and any other cluster
t_{3}
is computed as

\displaystyle s(t_{1}\cup t_{2},t_{3})\ =\ f\big(s(t_{1},t_{2}),s(t_{2},t_{3}),s(t_{3},t_{1}),|t_{1}|,|t_{2}|,|t_{3}|\big).

(3.1)

Thus, the update depends only on the pairwise similarities among
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}
and on their sizes
|t_{1}|,|t_{2}|,|t_{3}|
. Observe that because
t_{1}\cup t_{2}=t_{2}\cup t_{1}
, the function
f
must satisfy

f\big(s(t_{1},t_{2}),s(t_{2},t_{3}),s(t_{3},t_{2}),|t_{1}|,|t_{2}|,|t_{3}|\big)\ =\ f\big(s(t_{2},t_{1}),s(t_{1},t_{3}),s(t_{3},t_{1}),|t_{2}|,|t_{1}|,|t_{3}|\big).

Moreover, we will also assume that
f
is continuous with respect to its first coordinate.

Assumption 1
.

The linkage update function

4.1
Ultrametrics and Dendrograms

From ultrametrics to dendrograms.

A dissimilarity
d\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is an
ultrametric
if, for every distinct
x,y,z\in\mathcal{X}
,

\displaystyle d(x,y)\ \leq\ \max\{d(x,z),d(y,z)\}.

(4.1)

This “strong triangle inequality” implies in particular that the largest value among the three distances
\{d(x,y),d(x,z),d(y,z)\}
occurs at least twice. Consequently, every triplet
(x,y,z)
admits a consistent
closeness relation
: exactly one pair is no farther apart than the other two, so that each triangle with corners
x,y,z
is isosceles. These local relations collectively define a family of nested clusters, and there exists a unique rooted tree
T_{d}
with leaf set
\mathcal{X}
and an associated height function
h\colon T_{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that

d(x,y)\ =\ h(\mathrm{lca}_{T_{d}}(x,y)),

where
\mathrm{lca}_{T_{d}}(x,y)
denotes the lowest common ancestor of
x
and
y
in
T_{d}
.

The pair
(T_{d},h)
is called a
dendrogram
(or a dated tree). Conversely, every dendrogram induces an ultrametric via this construction. Hence the space of ultrametrics on
\mathcal{X}
is in bijection with the space of (real-valued) dendrograms on
\mathcal{X}
, and this relationship has been extensively investigated in the literature
(
18
;
16
;
5
;
22
;
9
;
10
)
.

From ultrametricity to validity.

Replacing dissimilarities by similarities converts the ultrametric condition (
4.1
) into

s(x,y)\geq\min\{s(x,z),s(y,z)\},\qquad\forall\,x,y,z\in\mathcal{X}.

(4.2)

This inequality expresses the ultrametric property in terms of
similarities: among any three points, the smallest similarity value
occurs at least twice.

The following lemma (whose proof is straightforward and deferred to Appendix
B.3
) demonstrates that when
s
satisfies (
4.2
), the finest valid hierarchy
T_{*}(\mathcal{X},s)
can be equipped with a height function to form a dendrogram. This shows that, in the ultrametric case, the finest valid hierarchy coincides with the unique dendrogram associated with
s
.

Lemma 8
.

Let
s
be an ultrametric similarity function and let
T_{*}=T_{*}(\mathcal{X},s)
be the finest valid hierarchy with respect to
s
. Then, there exists a function
h\colon T_{*}(\mathcal{X},s)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
satisfying
h(t)>h(t^{\prime})
for any
t\subsetneq t^{\prime}
and such that

\displaystyle\forall(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\colon\quad s(x,y)\ =\ h(\mathrm{lca}_{T_{*}}(x,y)).

Imposing the ultrametric condition (
4.2
) is, however, very restrictive. It enforces a global consistency among all triplet comparisons, which rarely holds in practice. In contrast, Condition (
2.1
) is much weaker: it defines valid hierarchies for an arbitrary similarity function
s
, requiring only that the self-similarity of an item with itself exceeds its similarity with any other item. This broader definition allows our framework to handle non-ultrametric similarity functions while still producing a well-defined hierarchical structure.

To illustrate the contrast, consider the star tree
T_{0}
in Figure
2
. If
s
is constrained to be ultrametric, then
T_{0}
is the dendrogram associated with
s

if and only if
s(x,y)=p\,\mathbf{1}(x=y)+q\,\mathbf{1}(x\neq y)

for some constants
p>q
. This constraint is highly restrictive. Without the ultrametric constraint, the same star tree naturally represents the absence of hierarchical structure in a similarity function
s
, corresponding to a similarity matrix with no meaningful nested organization. The same reasoning applies to more complex examples (see Figure
3
).

Beyond ordered similarities: Symbolic ultrametrics and future directions.

A related and elegant generalization of ultrametrics replaces real-valued distances by symbolic ones. A
symbolic ultrametric

(
7
)
is a symmetric map
\delta\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\Sigma
taking values in an arbitrary set of symbols
\Sigma
(where no order exists in
\Sigma
), such that for every distinct triple
(x,y,z)\in\mathcal{X}^{3}
the multiset
\{\delta(x,y),\delta(x,z),\delta(y,z)\}
contains at most two distinct symbols.
This purely combinatorial constraint is both necessary and sufficient for
\delta
to be representable as a
symbolically dated tree
: there exists a unique rooted tree whose internal nodes are labeled by symbols so that
\delta(x,y)
equals the label of the lowest common ancestor of
x
and
y
. Hence symbolic ultrametrics stand in a one-to-one correspondence with symbolically labeled dendrograms, paralleling the classical bijection between real-valued ultrametrics and weighted trees.

This correspondence suggests an intriguing direction for future work. Our validity framework relies on inequality comparisons between similarities, and therefore presupposes an ordering on the similarity values. A natural question is whether a
symbolic characterization of valid hierarchies
could be developed in the same spirit as symbolic ultrametrics—for instance, by encoding for each triplet
(x,y,z)
a symbolic relation designating which pair among the three is “closer”, and requiring these relations to be globally consistent with respect to an unobserved ultrametric distance, as done in
2
. Such a formulation would yield a purely symbolic analogue of our validity notion in Definition
2
, extending the link between ultrametrics and hierarchical structures beyond the ordered setting.

4.2
Related Axiomatic, Optimization, and Probabilistic Perspectives

A rich line of research has sought to characterize hierarchical clustering procedures through axioms or optimization principles. Our validity-based perspective is closely connected to these efforts yet differs in scope: rather than prescribing a specific algorithm, it formalizes what it means for a hierarchy to be compatible (or valid) with a given similarity function.

Axiomatic characterizations.

The seminal work of
9
proposed a set of axioms for hierarchical clustering viewed as a map from metric spaces to ultrametric spaces.
Imposing invariance under isometries and stability with respect to perturbations of the input metric, they showed that the only admissible method satisfying their axioms is
single linkage
. Their setting, however, treats hierarchical clustering as a procedure producing an ultrametric rather than a hierarchy, and it merges all pairs achieving the current maximal similarity simultaneously, yielding possibly non-binary trees.
In contrast, our framework provides an intrinsic definition of when a given tree is consistent with the similarities, without assuming any particular hierarchical clustering algorithm.

More recently,
3
introduced an axiomatic view for density-based hierarchical clustering, focusing on the stability of density level sets. While their approach also aims at defining hierarchies independently of specific algorithms, it operates in the continuous domain of
measure-based cluster trees. Our notion of validity plays a similar structural role in the discrete similarity setting, identifying the family of hierarchies that can be supported by a given pairwise function.

Objective-based formulations.

An alternative perspective, initiated by
11
and further developed by
10
, formulates hierarchical clustering as an optimization problem over all possible trees.
In that line of work, the goal is to find a hierarchy minimizing (or maximizing) a global cost functional depending on the pairwise similarities.
However, the optimal tree minimizing the cost function is always binary.
In contrast, our approach is purely
feasibility-based
: a hierarchy is not optimized but required to satisfy a local set of validity inequalities.
The resulting family of valid hierarchies could thus be viewed as the feasible region underlying those global optimization formulations.

Relation to our analysis.

In summary, previous axiomatic and objective formulations identify either a particular algorithm (e.g., single linkage) or a preferred hierarchy optimizing a cost.
Our contribution complements these perspectives by providing a foundational notion of
validity
that characterizes the entire space of hierarchies consistent with the similarity function. This structural viewpoint isolates the combinatorial core of hierarchical consistency, and it can accommodate both algorithmic and axiomatic specializations as particular instances.

Probabilistic tree inference and network hierarchy models.

Beyond axiomatic and cost-based frameworks, a significant body of work treats hierarchical clustering as the inference of tree structures under probabilistic or model-selection criteria. For instance,
6
propose the Bayesian Rose Trees approach, a greedy-agglomerative algorithm exploring a posterior over trees with arbitrary branching. Extensions of this idea include non-parametric priors over hierarchies such as the tree-structured stick-breaking process
(
14
)
and coalescent-based Bayesian clustering models
(
24
)
.
In the network domain, recent work considers hierarchical community detection in random graphs via linkage or recursive partitioning methods
(
19
;
12
;
15
)
.
These probabilistic and network-driven methodologies complement our validity-based framework: whereas they typically select or infer a single best tree under a likelihood or prior, our approach focuses on characterizing the entire feasible set of hierarchies consistent with a given similarity function. In this sense, our notion of validity may serve as a constraint or prior domain for tree-inference methods, suggesting a promising direction for future integration between combinatorial validity and probabilistic modeling.

if and only if
v\in\mathcal{C}_{active}^{(m)}
and
v\notin\mathcal{C}_{active}^{(m-1)}
.

Initially,
\mathcal{C}^{(0)}_{active}=\{\{x_{1}\},\cdots,\{x_{k}\}\}
. Moreover, at all times
m
, we have
\bigcup_{y\in C^{(m)}_{active}}y\ =\ \mathcal{X}
and

\displaystyle y\cap y^{\prime}\ =\ \emptyset\quad\forall y\neq y^{\prime}\in C^{(m)}_{active}.

(A.1)

Finally, for any
m\in[k-1]
, we define the property
\mathcal{P}(m)
:

\displaystyle\forall t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}\in\mathcal{C}_{active}^{(m)},\text{ if }\quad\exists t\in T\colon t_{1},t_{2}\subsetneq t,t_{3}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\backslash t\Rightarrow s(t_{1},t_{2})>\max\left(s(t_{1},t_{3}),s(t_{2},t_{3})\right).

(A.2)

Recall that
T
is a valid hierarchy. Hence, if
t_{1},t_{2}
and
t_{3}
are three leaves such that
t_{1},t_{2}\subsetneq t
and
t_{3}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\backslash t
, then the condition
s(t_{1},t_{2})>\max\left(s(t_{1},t_{3}),s(t_{2},t_{3})\right)
is automatically verified because of (
2.1
), independently of the linkage function. But, when
t_{1}
,
t_{2}
and
t_{3}
are not all leaves, the condition
s(t_{1},t_{2})>\max\left(s(t_{1},t_{3}),s(t_{2},t_{3})\right)
is not guaranteed anymore. Indeed, the similarity between non-leaves elements depends on the linkage function, and only assumptions on the linkage function can ensure that
s(t_{1},t_{2})>\max\left(s(t_{1},t_{3}),s(t_{2},t_{3})\right)
.

Step 2. If
\mathcal{P}(m)
holds for every
m\in[k-1]
, then
T\subseteq\hat{T}_{\mathrm{linkage}}
.

Assume that
\mathcal{P}(m)
holds for every
m\in[k-1]
. We will show that
T\subseteq\hat{T}_{\mathrm{linkage}}
.

By contradiction, assume that
T\not\subseteq\hat{T}_{\mathrm{linkage}}
. This is equivalent to the existence of a cluster
t\in T
such that
t\not\in\hat{T}_{\mathrm{linkage}}
. For this cluster
t
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{t}\ =\ \{v\in\hat{T}_{\mathrm{linkage}}\colon v\cap t\not\in\{\emptyset,v,t\}\}.

Because
\hat{T}_{\mathrm{linkage}}
is a binary tree and
t\not\in\hat{T}_{\mathrm{linkage}}
, Lemma
9
asserts that
\mathcal{B}_{t}

is non-empty.

Because
\mathcal{B}_{t}
is non-empty, we can define
u=\argmin_{v\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}m_{\mathrm{b}}(v)
, and let
m^{\prime}=m_{\mathrm{b}}(u)
be the iteration in which the first element of
\mathcal{B}_{t}
is created.

Because
C^{(0)}_{active}
is composed of the leaves (which are the singletons
\{x_{i}\}
), for any
v\in C^{(0)}_{active}
we have
v\cap t\in\{\emptyset,v\}\subseteq\{\emptyset,v,t\}
. Hence
|u|\geq 2
and
u
is created at an iteration
m^{\prime}\geq 1
by merging two clusters
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
, defined by

\displaystyle\{u_{1},u_{2}\}\ \in\ \argmax_{u^{\prime}_{1}\neq u^{\prime}_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{active}^{(m^{\prime}-1)}}s(u^{\prime}_{1},u^{\prime}_{2}).

(A.3)

Moreover, because of the definition of
u
, we have
u_{1},u_{2}\not\in\mathcal{B}_{t}
, otherwise,
u\neq\argmin_{v\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}m_{\mathrm{b}}(v)
, hence
u_{i}\cap t\in\{\emptyset,u_{i},t\}
(
i\in\{1,2\}
).
Therefore, we have one of the following five cases:

(i)

u_{1}\cap t=t
or
u_{2}\cap t=t
;

(ii)

u_{1}\cap t=\emptyset
and
u_{2}\cap t=\emptyset
;

(iii)

u_{1}\cap t=u_{1}
and
u_{2}\cap t=\emptyset
;

(iv)

u_{1}\cap t=\emptyset
and
u_{2}\cap t=u_{2}
;

(v)

u_{1}\cap t=u_{1}
and
u_{2}\cap t=u_{2}
.

First, we can rule out Case (i). Indeed, if
u_{i}\cap t=t
for some
i\in\{1,2\}
, then we would have
t\cap u=t\cap(u_{1}\cup u_{2})=t
, which is impossible because
u\in\mathcal{B}_{t}
.

Case (ii) implies that
(u_{1}\cup u_{2})\cap t=\emptyset
. However, this is again impossible; indeed,
u=u_{1}\cup u_{2}\in\mathcal{B}_{t}
implies that
(u_{1}\cup u_{2})\cap t\notin\{\emptyset,u_{1}\cup u_{2},t\}
.
Similarly, case (v) is also impossible because it would imply
u=u_{1}\cup u_{2}\subseteq t
, again an impossibility due to
u\in\mathcal{B}_{t}
.
As a result, only cases (iii) and (iv) are possible.

Consider case (iii) where
u_{1}\cap t=u_{1}
and
u_{2}\cap t=\emptyset
, and notice that the symmetric case (iv) can be treated similarly.
Because case (i) is impossible,
u_{1}\neq t
. Together with
\bigcup_{y\in C^{(m^{\prime}-1)}_{active}}y=\mathcal{X}
, it therefore implies the existence of a set
u_{1}^{\prime}\in C^{(m^{\prime}-1)}_{active}
such that
u_{1}^{\prime}\neq u_{1}
and
u_{1}^{\prime}\cap t\neq\emptyset
.

Because
m_{\mathrm{b}}(u_{1}^{\prime})=m^{\prime}-1<m^{\prime}=m_{\mathrm{b}}(u)
and because of the definition of
u
, we have
u_{1}^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{B}_{t}
. Combined with
u_{1}^{\prime}\cap t\neq\emptyset
, it implies that
u_{1}^{\prime}\cap t\in\{u_{1}^{\prime},t\}
. Moreover, because
u_{1},u_{1}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{active}^{(m^{\prime}-1)}
are disjoint by (
A.1
), we have
(t\cap u_{1})\cap u_{1}^{\prime}=\emptyset
. Combined with
u_{1}\cap t=u_{1}\neq\emptyset
, this rules out
u_{1}^{\prime}\cap t=t
and forces
u_{1}^{\prime}\cap t=u_{1}^{\prime}
.

To summarize, we have
u_{1},u_{1}^{\prime},u_{2}\in C^{(m^{\prime}-1)}_{active}
such that
u_{1},u_{1}^{\prime}\subsetneq t
and
u_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\setminus t
. Therefore, the condition
\mathcal{P}(m^{\prime}-1)
implies that
s(u_{1},u_{1}^{\prime})>s(u_{1},u_{2})
. This contradicts (
A.3
) and therefore proves that
T\subseteq\hat{T}_{\mathrm{linkage}}
.

Step 3. Inductive proof that
\mathcal{P}(m)
holds for every
m\in[k-1]
.

We proceed by induction on
m
.

Let
m=0
. We have
\mathcal{C}_{active}^{(0)}=\{\{x_{1}\},\cdots,\{x_{k}\}\}
. Let
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}\in\mathcal{C}_{active}^{(0)}
and suppose there exists
t\in T
such that
t_{1},t_{2}\subsetneq t
and
t_{3}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\backslash t
.
Note that
\mathcal{C}_{active}^{(0)}=\mathcal{X}
and thus
\mathcal{P}(0)
corresponds to the following statement:

\displaystyle\forall x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in\mathcal{X}\colon\exists t\in T\colon x_{1},x_{2}\in t,x_{3}\in\mathcal{X}\backslash t\Rightarrow s\left(x_{1},x_{2}\right)>\max\left(s\left(x_{1},x_{3}\right),s\left(x_{2},x_{3}\right)\right),

which we can rewrite as

\displaystyle\forall t\in T\colon\forall x_{1},x_{2}\in t,x_{3}\in\mathcal{X}\backslash t\Rightarrow s\left(x_{1},x_{2}\right)>\max\left(s\left(x_{1},x_{3}\right),s\left(x_{2},x_{3}\right)\right).

(A.4)

Because
T
is a valid hierarchy, the statement (
A.4
) holds. This establishes
\mathcal{P}(0)
.

Let
m\geq 0
and suppose that
\mathcal{P}(m^{\prime})
holds for any
m^{\prime}\in\{0,\cdots,m\}
. We prove that
\mathcal{P}(m+1)
holds. Let
\{t_{1}^{\prime},t_{2}^{\prime}\}\in\argmax_{v_{1}\neq v_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{active}^{(m)}}s(v_{1},v_{2})
and denote
t_{new}=t_{1}^{\prime}\cup t_{2}^{\prime}
. Recall that, by definition,
\mathcal{C}_{active}^{(m+1)}\cup\{t_{1}^{\prime},t_{2}^{\prime}\}=\mathcal{C}_{active}^{(m)}\cup\{t_{new}\}
. We consider vertex
t\in T
and a triplet of three different sets
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}\in\mathcal{C}_{active}^{(m+1)}
such that
t_{1},t_{2}\subseteq t
and
t_{3}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\backslash t
.
Because
\mathcal{C}_{active}^{(m+1)}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{active}^{(m)}=\{t_{new}\}
, we have the following four cases to consider.

•

If
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}\in\mathcal{C}_{active}^{(m)}
, then the induction property
\mathcal{P}(m)
implies that

s(t_{1},t_{2})>\max\left(s(t_{1},t_{3}),s(t_{2},t_{3})\right).

•

Suppose that
t_{1}=t_{new}=t_{1}^{\prime}\cup t_{2}^{\prime}
. Because
t_{1},t_{2}\subseteq t
, we have
t_{1}^{\prime},t_{2}^{\prime},t_{2}\subseteq t
. Moreover,
t_{1}^{\prime},t_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{(m)}_{active}
, hence the induction property
\mathcal{P}(m)
implies that

\displaystyle\forall i\in\{1,2\}\colon\quad s(t_{i}^{\prime},t_{2})>\max\left(s(t_{i}^{\prime},t_{3}),s(t_{2},t_{3})\right).

Thus, Condition
1
implies that

\displaystyle s(t_{1}^{\prime}\cup t_{2}^{\prime},t_{2})>\max\left(s(t_{1}^{\prime}\cup t_{2}^{\prime},t_{3}),s(t_{2},t_{3})\right).

Because
t_{1}=t_{1}^{\prime}\cup t_{2}^{\prime}
, this is equivalent to
s(t_{1},t_{2})>\max\left(s(t_{1},t_{3}),s(t_{2},t_{3})\right)
.

•

The case
t_{2}=t_{new}
can be treated similarly to the previous case, and leads again to
s(t_{1},t_{2})>\max\left(s(t_{1},t_{3}),s(t_{2},t_{3})\right)
.

•

Finally, suppose that
t_{3}=t_{new}
. Because
t_{3}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\backslash t
, we also have
t_{1}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\backslash t
. Moreover, because
t_{1}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{(m)}_{active}
, the induction property
\mathcal{P}(m)
implies that

\displaystyle s(t_{1},t_{2})>\max\left(s(t_{1},t_{1}^{\prime}),s(t_{2},t_{1}^{\prime})\right),

Because we also have
t_{2}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\backslash t
and
t_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{(m)}_{active}
, we establish similarly that

\displaystyle s(t_{1},t_{2})>\max\left(s(t_{1},t_{2}^{\prime}),s(t_{2},t_{2}^{\prime})\right).

Hence, by combining the last two inequalities, we have

\displaystyle s(t_{1},t_{2})>\max\left(s(t_{1},t_{1}^{\prime}),s(t_{2},t_{1}^{\prime}),s(t_{1},t_{2}^{\prime}),s(t_{2},t_{2}^{\prime})\right).

Condition
2
therefore implies that
s(t_{1},t_{2})>\max\left(s(t_{1},t_{1}^{\prime}\cup t_{2}^{\prime}),s(t_{2},t_{1}^{\prime}\cup t_{2}^{\prime})\right)
. Because
t_{3}=t_{1}^{\prime}\cup t_{2}^{\prime}
, this is equivalent to
s(t_{1},t_{2})>\max\left(s(t_{1},t_{3}),s(t_{2},t_{3})\right)
.

The combination of these three cases proves
\mathcal{P}(m+1)
.

A.2
Proof of Lemma
4
: Necessity of Conditions
1
and
2

We call an update rule
f

valid
if it satisfies

\displaystyle\forall(\mathcal{X},s)\colon\quad T_{*}(\mathcal{X},s)\subseteq\hat{T}_{\mathrm{linkage}}(\mathcal{X},s,f).

(A.5)

We will show that every valid update rule
f
must satisfy Conditions
1
and
2
. By contraposition, it suffices to prove the following statement:

Any update rule
f
that violates Condition
1
or Condition
2
is not valid.

Case (i): Violation of Condition
1

A violation of Condition
1
means that there exist four disjoint clusters
t_{1},\cdots,t_{4}
such that
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
are merged at some step, but

\displaystyle\forall i\in\{1,2\}\colon s(t_{i},t_{3})\ >\ \max\left(s(t_{i},t_{4}),s(t_{3},t_{4})\right)

(A.6)

and

\displaystyle f(s_{12},s_{23},s_{31},n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})\ \leq\ \max\left(f(s_{12},s_{24},s_{41},n_{1},n_{2},n_{4}),s_{34}\right),

(A.7)

where we use the shorthand
s_{ij}=s(t_{i},t_{j})
and
n_{i}=|t_{i}|
. Because
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
are the clusters merged by the algorithm, we also have
s(t_{1},t_{2})\in\argmax_{i\in[4]}s(t_{i},t_{j})
and thus

\displaystyle s(t_{1},t_{2})\geq s(t_{i},t_{j})\quad\text{for all }i,j\in[4],i\neq j.

(A.8)

Construction of a counterexample.

Suppose
f
violates Condition
1
but is valid. We will construct a dataset
(\mathcal{X}_{1},s_{1})
for which

T_{*}(\mathcal{X}_{1},s_{1})\not\subseteq\hat{T}_{\mathrm{linkage}}(\mathcal{X}_{1},s_{1},f)
,
contradicting (
A.5
) and thus the validity of
f
.

Let
t_{1}^{\prime},t_{2}^{\prime},t_{3}^{\prime},t_{4}^{\prime}
be disjoint sets with
|t_{i}^{\prime}|=n_{i}
, and define
\mathcal{X}_{1}=\bigcup_{i\in[4]}t_{i}^{\prime}
.
Define the similarity function
s_{1}\colon\mathcal{X}_{1}\times\mathcal{X}_{1}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
by

\displaystyle s_{1}(x,y)\ =\ \begin{cases}s_{ij},&\text{if }x\in t_{i}^{\prime},\;y\in t_{j}^{\prime}\text{ for some }i\neq j\in\{1,2,3,4\},\\
s_{12}+1,&\text{if }x,y\in t_{i}^{\prime}\text{ for some }i\in\{1,2,3,4\}.\end{cases}

By (
A.6
) and (
A.8
),
for each
i\in\{1,2\}
, we have

s_{12}\geq s_{i3}>\max(s_{i4},s_{34}).

Hence, the union
t_{1}^{\prime}\cup t_{2}^{\prime}\cup t_{3}^{\prime}
forms a valid cluster with respect to
s_{1}
,
i.e.,

t_{1}^{\prime}\cup t_{2}^{\prime}\cup t_{3}^{\prime}\in T_{*}(\mathcal{X}_{1},s_{1}).

By Lemma
11
, the clusters
t_{1}^{\prime},\cdots,t_{4}^{\prime}
appear as active clusters before any cross-merging occurs, so for all
1\leq i<j\leq 4
,

\displaystyle s_{1}(t_{i}^{\prime},t_{j}^{\prime})\ =\ s_{ij}.

Moreover, since
t_{1}^{\prime}
and
t_{2}^{\prime}
are merged first (by (
A.8
)), the similarity between the cluster
t_{1}^{\prime}\cup t_{2}^{\prime}
formed by their merger and
t_{3}^{\prime}
satisfies

\displaystyle s_{1}(t_{1}^{\prime}\cup t_{2}^{\prime},t_{3}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\ =\ f(s_{12},s_{23},s_{31},n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})

\displaystyle\ \leq\ \max\left(f(s_{12},s_{24},s_{41},n_{1},n_{2},n_{4}),s_{34}\right)

(A.9)

\displaystyle\ =\ \max\left(s_{1}(t_{1}^{\prime}\cup t_{2}^{\prime},t_{4}^{\prime}),s_{1}(t_{3}^{\prime},t_{4}^{\prime})\right),

where (
A.9
) follows from (
A.7
).
If the inequality (
A.9
) is strict, then the algorithm at the next step merges either the pair
(t_{1}^{\prime}\cup t_{2}^{\prime},t_{4}^{\prime})
or the pair
(t_{3}^{\prime},t_{4}^{\prime})
, preventing the formation of the true cluster
t_{1}^{\prime}\cup t_{2}^{\prime}\cup t_{3}^{\prime}
.
Even if equality holds, the similarity of the pair
(t_{1}^{\prime}\cup t_{2}^{\prime},t_{3}^{\prime})
merely ties with the similarity of another pair, and tie-breaking may again exclude the valid cluster.

In both cases,

T_{*}(\mathcal{X}_{1},s_{1})\not\subseteq\hat{T}_{\mathrm{linkage}}(\mathcal{X}_{1},s_{1},f),

contradicting (
A.5
). Thus, a valid
f
cannot violate Condition
1
.

Case (ii): Violation of Condition
2

A violation of Condition
2
means that there exist four disjoint clusters
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4}
such that
t_{3}
and
t_{4}
are merged, i.e.

\displaystyle s(t_{3},t_{4})\geq s(t_{i},t_{j})\quad\text{for all }i,j\in[4],i\neq j,

(A.10)

and simultaneously

\displaystyle s_{12}>\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\{1,2\},\,j\in\{3,4\}\end{subarray}}s_{ij}\quad\text{and}\quad s_{12}\leq\max_{i\in\{1,2\}}f(s_{34},s_{4i},s_{3i},n_{3},n_{4},n_{i}).

(A.11)

Construction of a counterexample.
Assume again that
f
is valid but violates Condition
2
.

Let
t_{1}^{\prime},t_{2}^{\prime},t_{3}^{\prime},t_{4}^{\prime}
be disjoint sets with
|t_{i}^{\prime}|=n_{i}
, and define
\mathcal{X}_{1}=\bigcup_{i\in[4]}t_{i}^{\prime}
.
Define the similarity function
s_{1}\colon\mathcal{X}_{1}\times\mathcal{X}_{1}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
by

\displaystyle s_{1}(x,y)\ =\ \begin{cases}s_{ij},&\text{if }x\in t_{i}^{\prime},\;y\in t_{j}^{\prime}\text{ for some }i\neq j\in\{1,2,3,4\},\\
s_{34}+1,&\text{if }x,y\in t_{i}^{\prime}\text{ for some }i\in\{1,2,3,4\}.\end{cases}

From (
A.11
),
s_{12}
is strictly larger than
\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\{1,2\},\,j\in\{3,4\}\end{subarray}}s_{ij}
, hence

t_{1}^{\prime}\cup t_{2}^{\prime}\in T_{*}(\mathcal{X}_{1},s_{1}).

By Lemma
11
,
t_{1}^{\prime},\cdots,t_{4}^{\prime}
appear as active clusters before any inter-cluster merge, and

s_{1}(t_{i}^{\prime},t_{j}^{\prime})\ =\ s_{ij}\quad\forall i<j.

By (
A.11
), we have

\displaystyle s_{1}(t_{1}^{\prime},t_{2}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\ =\ s_{12}

\displaystyle\ \leq\ \max_{i\in\{1,2\}}f(s_{34},s_{4i},s_{3i},n_{3},n_{4},n_{i})

(A.12)

\displaystyle\ =\ \max_{i\in\{1,2\}}s_{1}(t_{3}^{\prime}\cup t_{4}^{\prime},t_{i}^{\prime}).

Therefore, the linkage procedure either merges one of the spurious pairs
(t_{3}^{\prime}\cup t_{4}^{\prime},t_{i}^{\prime})
or ties with the valid pair
(t_{1}^{\prime},t_{2}^{\prime})
, preventing the formation of the true cluster
t_{1}^{\prime}\cup t_{2}^{\prime}
.
Hence

T_{*}(\mathcal{X}_{1},s_{1})\not\subseteq\hat{T}_{\mathrm{linkage}}(\mathcal{X}_{1},s_{1},f),

contradicting (
A.5
). Therefore a valid
f
cannot violate Condition
2
.

Remark 1
.

Without Assumption
1
, we can only show
f(q^{+},q,q,\cdots)=q
for any
q^{+}>q
(Lemma
11
) and
f(q,q,q,\cdots)=q
might not hold. This forbids us from using the ultrametric similarities (i.e.,
s(x,y)
that takes the same value for any

). However, careful constructions of
(\mathcal{X}_{1},s_{1})
that serve as the counterexamples that are similar to those in the above proof (but more complex), are still possible but require more tedious arguments.

A.3.1

A.1

The following technical lemma is used to prove the sufficiency of Conditions
1
and
2
in Lemma

.

Lemma 9
.

Let
v\subseteq\mathcal{X}
. Let
T_{bin}\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{X})
be a binary tree. The following holds:

\displaystyle v\not\in T_{bin}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\exists u\in T_{bin}\colon u\cap v\not\in\{\emptyset,u,v\}.

(A.13)

Proof.

Let
v\subseteq\mathcal{X}
such that
v\not\in T_{bin}
.
We show that there exists
u_{*}\in T_{bin}
such that
u_{*}\cap v\not\in\{\emptyset,u_{*},v\}
.

Because
v\notin T_{bin}
while
\mathcal{X}\in T_{bin}
, we have
v\subsetneq\mathcal{X}
.
Denote by
\mathcal{B}
the collection of ancestors of
v
in the tree

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}\ =\ \{u\in T_{bin}\colon v\subsetneq u\}.

Because
\mathcal{X}\in\mathcal{B}
, the set
\mathcal{B}
is not empty. Let

\displaystyle u_{0}\ =\ \argmin\{|u|\colon u\in\mathcal{B}\}

be the smallest cluster of
T_{bin}
strictly containing
v
.

Because
v\subsetneq u_{0}
,
u_{0}
is not a leaf. Because
T_{bin}
is a binary tree,
u_{0}
has exactly two children
u_{1}
and
u_{2}\in T_{bin}
satisfying
u_{1}\cup u_{2}=u_{0}
and
u_{1}\cap u_{2}=\emptyset
.

By minimality of
u_{0}
, neither child fully contains
v
; otherwise that child would belong to
\mathcal{B}
with smaller cardinality that
u_{0}
.
Hence,

\displaystyle v\not\subseteq u_{1}\quad\text{ and }\quad v\not\subseteq u_{2}.

Moreover, at least one child is not contained in
v
,
i.e.,

\displaystyle u_{1}\not\subseteq v\text{ or }u_{2}\not\subseteq v.

Indeed, by contradiction, suppose that both
u_{1}\subseteq v
and
u_{2}\subseteq v
hold. Then,
u_{0}=u_{1}\cup u_{2}\subseteq v
, contradicting
v\subsetneq u_{0}
.

Without loss of generality, suppose that
u_{1}\not\subseteq v
, and let
u_{*}=u_{1}
. We have
u_{*}\in T_{bin}
. Because
u^{*}
neither contains
v
nor is disjoint from it, we have

\displaystyle u_{*}\cap v\ \not\in\ \{\emptyset,u_{*},v\}.

This ends the proof.
∎

(\mathcal{X},s)

t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{5}\subsetneq\mathcal{X}
, the union
t_{3}\cup t_{5}
is a valid cluster, and for some iteration
m
,

t_{1}\cup t_{2},\;t_{3},\;t_{5}\in\mathcal{C}_{active}^{(m)}(\mathcal{X},s).

Then necessarily,

s(t_{1}\cup t_{2},t_{3})<s(t_{3},t_{5}).

3.

Combining the previous two statements, if there exists
(\mathcal{X},s)
such that

(t_{1}\cup t_{2}\cup t_{3}\cup t_{4},s[t_{1}\cup t_{2}\cup t_{3}\cup t_{4}])
satisfies the assumptions of point 1 and

(t_{1}\cup t_{2}\cup t_{3}\cup t_{5},s[t_{1}\cup t_{2}\cup t_{3}\cup t_{5}])
satisfies those of point 2, then

s(t_{3},t_{4})<s(t_{1}\cup t_{2},t_{3})<s(t_{3},t_{5}).

Proof.

1.

Because
t_{1}\cup t_{2}\cup t_{3}
is a valid cluster, the algorithm must merge
t_{3}
with
t_{1}\cup t_{2}
before any merge involving
t_{4}
. If

t_{3}
and

could occur first, violating the validity of

s(t_{1}\cup t_{2},t_{3})>s(t_{3},t_{4})

2.

Because
t_{3}\cup t_{5}
is a valid cluster, the linkage must merge
t_{3}
and
t_{5}
before any merge involving
t_{1}\cup t_{2}
. If
s(t_{1}\cup t_{2},t_{3})\geq s(t_{3},t_{5})

t_{3}\cup t_{5}
. Thus,
s(t_{1}\cup t_{2},t_{3})<s(t_{3},t_{5})

3.

Follows directly from combining the inequalities in points 1 and 2.

The following lemma formalizes a key property of any valid update function
f
: when both its second and third arguments are equal to
q
, the function must output
q
, i.e.,
f(q^{+},q,q,\cdots)=q
for all
q^{+}\geq q
.

Lemma 11
.

Let
f
be a valid update function.
Consider any
(\mathcal{X},s)
and disjoint clusters
t_{1},t_{2}
such that
s(x,y)=q
for all
x\in t_{1},y\in t_{2}
.
If there exists an iteration
m
such that
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{active}^{(m)}(\mathcal{X},s)
, then

s(t_{1},t_{2})=q.

Moreover, for any
q^{+}\geq q
and any
n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}\in\mathbb{N}
,

f(q^{+},q,q,n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})=q.

Proof.

We prove the claim by induction on the cluster sizes
n_{1},n_{2}
.

Claim.

For any
n_{1},n_{2}
and any
q\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists
m
such that if
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{active}^{(m)}
with
|t_{1}|=n_{1}
,
|t_{2}|=n_{2}
, and
s(x,y)=q
for all
x\in t_{1},y\in t_{2}
, then
s(t_{1},t_{2})=q
.

Base case (
n_{1}=n_{2}=1
).

Trivial, since the similarity between singletons equals
s(x,y)=q
by definition.

Inductive hypothesis.

Assume the claim holds for all
n_{1}\leq b_{1}
and
n_{2}\leq b_{2}
.
Without loss of generality, assume
b_{1}\geq b_{2}
(since
s
is symmetric).

Inductive step.

We prove the claim for all
(n_{1},n_{2})
with

n_{1}\leq\max(2b_{1},b_{2})
and
n_{2}\leq\min(2b_{1},b_{2})
.

Let
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4},t_{5}
be disjoint clusters such that

|t_{1}|,|t_{2}|,|t_{4}|,|t_{5}|\leq b_{2}
and
|t_{3}|\leq b_{1}
.
Fix arbitrary real numbers
q_{0}>q^{+}>q+\delta
for some
\delta>0
.
Define
\mathcal{X}=t_{1}\cup t_{2}\cup t_{3}\cup t_{4}\cup t_{5}
and a similarity function
s:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
by

s(x,y)\ =\ \begin{cases}q_{0},&(x,y)\in t_{i}\times t_{i}\text{ for some }i\in[5],\\
q^{+},&(x,y)\in t_{1}\times t_{2},\\
q,&(x,y)\in(t_{1}\cup t_{2})\times(t_{3}\cup t_{4}),\\
q+\delta,&(x,y)\in t_{3}\times t_{4},\\
q-\delta,&(x,y)\in(t_{1}\cup t_{2}\cup t_{3}\cup t_{4})\times t_{5}.\end{cases}

Observe that all
t_{i}
are valid clusters with respect to
s
. By the inductive hypothesis, at every step of the linkage process:

s(u,v)\ =\ \begin{cases}q_{0},&u,v\subsetneq t_{i},\\
q^{+},&u\subseteq t_{1},v\subseteq t_{2},\\
q,&u\subsetneq(t_{1}\cup t_{2}),\ v\subsetneq(t_{3}\cup t_{4}),\\
q+\delta,&u,v\subsetneq t_{3}\times t_{4},\\
q-\delta,&u\subsetneq(t_{1}\cup t_{2}\cup t_{3}\cup t_{4}),\ v\subseteq t_{5}.\end{cases}

Hence, there exist
m_{1},m_{2}
such that

t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4}\in\mathcal{C}_{active}^{(m_{1})}(t_{1}\cup t_{2}\cup t_{3}\cup t_{4},s[t_{1}\cup t_{2}\cup t_{3}\cup t_{4}]),

and

t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{5}\in\mathcal{C}_{active}^{(m_{2})}(t_{1}\cup t_{2}\cup t_{3}\cup t_{5},s[t_{1}\cup t_{2}\cup t_{3}\cup t_{5}]).

Moreover, by the construction of
s
,

s(t_{i},t_{j})\ =\ \begin{cases}q^{+},&(i,j)=(1,2),\\
q,&i\in\{1,2\},j\in\{3,4\},\\
q+\delta,&(i,j)=(3,4),\\
q-\delta,&i\in[4],j=5.\end{cases}

Because
s(t_{1},t_{2})
is the unique maximum among all pairwise similarities
s(t_{i},t_{j})
, we have:

t_{1}\cup t_{2},t_{3},t_{4}\in\mathcal{C}_{active}^{(m_{1}+1)}(t_{1}\cup t_{2}\cup t_{3}\cup t_{4},s[t_{1}\cup t_{2}\cup t_{3}\cup t_{4}]),

and

t_{1}\cup t_{2},t_{3},t_{5}\in\mathcal{C}_{active}^{(m_{2}+1)}(t_{1}\cup t_{2}\cup t_{3}\cup t_{5},s[t_{1}\cup t_{2}\cup t_{3}\cup t_{5}]).

Moreover,
t_{1}\cup t_{2},t_{1}\cup t_{2}\cup t_{3}
are valid clusters with respect to
(t_{1}\cup t_{2}\cup t_{3}\cup t_{4},s[t_{1}\cup t_{2}\cup t_{3}\cup t_{4}])
, and
t_{3}\cup t_{5}
is a valid cluster with respect to
(t_{1}\cup t_{2}\cup t_{3}\cup t_{5},s[t_{1}\cup t_{2}\cup t_{3}\cup t_{5}])
.

Applying Lemma
10
(point 3) yields:

s(t_{3},t_{4})\ =\ q-\delta\ <\ s(t_{1}\cup t_{2},t_{3})\ <\ s(t_{3},t_{5})\ =\ q+\delta.

Because this holds for any
\delta>0
, we conclude that

s(t_{1}\cup t_{2},t_{3})\ =\ q.

Thus the claim holds for all relevant cluster sizes, completing the induction.

Finally, this implies that

f(q^{+},q,q,n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})=q

for all
q^{+}>q
.
By the right-continuity of
f
(Assumption
1
), the equality also holds for
q^{+}=q
.
∎

T\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{X})
, let us look at the following condition:

\displaystyle\forall t\in T\colon\quad\min_{x,y\in t}\,s(x,y)\ >\ \max_{x\in t,z\in\mathcal{X}\backslash t}s(x,z).

(C.1)

By defining a valid hierarchy as a tree verifying this new condition, we can derive analogous results to Theorems
2
and
5
(with small modifications to Algorithm
2
). Because this new condition (
C.1
) is more restrictive than (
2.1
), a tree verifying (
C.1
) is also valid according to Definition
3
.

C.2
Efficient Implementation of Algorithm
2

The vertices to trim are exactly the ones that violate Condition (
2.1
). A naive implementation consists of iterating over all
t\in\hat{T}_{\mathrm{linkage}}
from the root to the leaves to check for vertices
t\in\hat{T}_{\mathrm{linkage}}
violating Condition (
2.1
). Using the following lemma, we can avoid re-verifying the same condition several times.

Lemma 16
.

Let
\varrho(t)
denote the parent of
t\in T
. Condition (
2.1
) in Definition
3
is equivalent to the following condition

\displaystyle\forall t\in T\colon\quad\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in t,\\
z\in\varrho(t)\backslash t\end{subarray}}\,s(x,y)-s(x,z)\ >\ 0.

(C.2)

Proof of Lemma
16
.

Let
T
be a rooted tree defined on
\mathcal{X}
. Let
t
be any vertex on
T
. The claim of the lemma is equivalent to

\displaystyle\bigcup_{t\in T}\left\{(x,y,z)\colon x\in t,y\in t,z\in\left(\varrho(t)\backslash t\right)\right\}\ =\ \bigcup_{t\in T}\left\{(x,y,z)\colon x\in t,y\in t,z\in\mathcal{X}\backslash t\right\}.

(C.3)

Because
\left(\varrho(t)\backslash t\right)\subseteq\mathcal{X}\backslash t
, we have

\displaystyle\left\{(x,y,z)\colon x\in t,y\in t,z\in\left(\varrho(t)\backslash t\right)\right\}\ \subseteq\ \left\{(x,y,z)\colon x\in t,y\in t,z\in\mathcal{X}\backslash t\right\},

(C.4)

and thus

\displaystyle\bigcup_{t\in T}\left\{(x,y,z)\colon x\in t,y\in t,z\in\left(\varrho(t)\backslash t\right)\right\}\subseteq\bigcup_{t\in T}\left\{(x,y,z)\colon x\in t,y\in t,z\in\mathcal{X}\backslash t\right\}.

Now, denoting by
\varrho^{m}(t)
the parent of
t
at the
m^{\text{th}}
generation, i.e.,

\displaystyle\varrho^{0}(t)=t,\;\varrho^{1}(t)=\varrho(t),\;\varrho^{2}(t)=\varrho(\varrho(t)),\;\ldots,\;\varrho^{m}(t)=\varrho(\varrho^{m-1}(t)),

we can recast
\mathcal{X}\backslash t=\bigcup_{m\geq 1}\left(\varrho^{m}(t)\backslash\varrho^{m-1}(t)\right)
, so that

\displaystyle\left\{(x,y,z)\colon x\in t,y\in t,z\in\mathcal{X}\backslash t\right\}

\displaystyle\ =\ \left\{(x,y,z)\colon x\in t,y\in t,z\in\bigcup_{m\geq 1}\left(\varrho^{m}(t)\backslash\varrho^{m-1}(t)\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\ \subseteq\ \bigcup_{m\geq 1}\left\{(x,y,z)\colon x\in\varrho^{m-1}(t),y\in\varrho^{m-1}(t),z\in\left(\varrho^{m}(t)\backslash\varrho^{m-1}(t)\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\ \subseteq\ \bigcup_{t\in T}\left\{(x,y,z)\colon x\in t,y\in t,z\in\left(\varrho(t)\backslash t\right)\right\}.

(C.5)

Combining (
C.4
) and (
C.5
) yields Equation (
C.3
).
∎
