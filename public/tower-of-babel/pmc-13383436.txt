Effective and cost-effective strategies for recruiting rural adults into a civic engagement and health behavior change research study
Authors: Rebecca A. Seguin-Fowler, Meredith L. Graham, Karla L. Hanson, Deyaun L. Villarreal, Galen D. Eldridge, Angel Christou, Anita On, Meghan Kershaw, Sara C. Folta, Jay E. Maddock, Elena Andreyeva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRural populations have worse health outcomes than urban populations. Community-based health interventions can help to improve these outcomes, but rural populations are underrepresented in research. The goals of the study were to describe and examine the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of strategies to recruit into a rural, community-based health intervention study.MethodsThe Change Club study is a community-based health intervention conducted in medically underserved rural communities in Texas and New York to assess whether a civic engagement program improves health among intervention participants and their communities. The study was promoted online, via mailings, email, educators, flyers/posters, and traditional media, and encouraged referrals from friends and family members. For each recruitment strategy, the resulting number of enrolled participants and costs were compiled.ResultsThe most effective recruitment strategy was letters, which contributed 37.7% of participants, followed by friends/family (23.2%), postcards (22.3%), social media advertisements (13.3%), and flyers/posters (8.4%). The overall cost per enrolled participant was 12.36). The least cost-effective strategy was local educators’ phone calls and texts, which cost $6,803 and yielded no enrollments.ConclusionsMailing of recruitment letters, word-of-mouth referrals from family and friends, advertisements on social media, and the distribution and posting of flyers/posters were effective and cost-effective strategies and we recommend them for future studies in similar locations. Leveraging the close-knit social ties in rural communities to recommend study participation may be particularly fruitful, but researchers should investigate potential selection bias.Trial registrationClinicaltrials.gov, NCT05002660 Registered on 04 August 2021.

Introduction

Rural communities face significant health challenges compared to urban areas, such as experiencing higher rates of chronic diseases, including obesity [1], heart disease [2], and cancer [2]. Compared to urban areas, rural residents are more likely to smoke [3] and less likely to meet physical activity guidelines [4]. Unique circumstances influence access to healthcare resources in rural areas. These include healthcare provider shortages, limited access to services for disease prevention programs and treatment, limited public transportation to reach healthcare providers, and lack of broadband internet, which can restrict access to health information and telehealth services [5]. Clinical trials play a critical role in developing effective evidence-based prevention and treatment strategies to improve health outcomes; however, rural adults are significantly underrepresented in these trials [6, 7]. This lack of participation creates a gap in understanding how best to address health issues in rural populations, which may affect the quality of care and contribute to and exacerbate existing health disparities.

These unique circumstances may also have implications for which methods work for research trial recruitment [5]. For example, the National Institutes of Health’s All of Us aimed to study individuals previously underrepresented in research, including rural areas [8]. Yet only 3.1% of the All of Us participants were classified as rural, compared to 15–20% of United States residents that are rural [8]. Research trial recruitment often relies on patient samples from large medical institutions [9] and email/online recruitment methods [10]. Rural residents must travel farther to urban medical centers for clinical trials; for example, one study of clinical trials at academic medical centers in Arizona, Florida, and the Midwest found that rural residents traveled an average of 103 miles to the study location, compared to 68 miles for urban residents [11]. A representative multistate research study with rural residents found that only 8 of the 81 participants (10%) enrolled due to online recruitment strategies (mass emails or social media) [12]. These rural residents were much more likely to be recruited via community outreach or clinic/pharmacy referrals; these two strategies accounted for 77% of enrolled participants in the study [12]. While recruiting for clinical trials presents challenges in all geographic areas (e.g., time constraints, lack of interest, participation burden, potential risks) [13], recruitment for community-based, rural research projects face unique challenges and needs. These unique challenges include limited study staffing and resources, unfavorable attitudes or distrust towards clinical research trials, limited support from healthcare providers and social circles, and lack of behavioral trial understanding and awareness [14, 15]. Furthermore, enrolling a representative sample within rural areas presents a challenge as there are nuances to successfully recruiting and enrolling diverse participants (e.g., recruiting men in rural communities may require specific outreach strategies) [16].

Considering the unique needs and challenges of recruiting for participants in rural contexts, a limited number of studies have found mailing tailored study brochures with postcards [17], a combination of web-based and tailored in-person events [18], and a dedicated social media page [19] to be most effective. Furthermore, community engagement in the form of consultation (feedback solicited from communities for research procedures) and collaboration (partnerships formed with communities to work together on all procedures) promoted recruitment of participants in rural settings [20]. Working with schools and/or churches was another recruitment method suggested by rural-dwelling individuals [6]. Understanding the culture of the population, identifying individuals within the community to help recruit, and building trust with stakeholders were also important considerations [21]. Effective recruitment strategies require dedicating financial resources [22]; however, the cost-effectiveness of recruitment approaches depends on the target audience and study type [23, 24]. Online and social media-based advertising offer a promising avenue for cost-effective recruitment [24–26], although the degree of cost-effectiveness varies based on the specific study [27].

To our knowledge, there are no studies that report cost-effectiveness for recruitment for rural research studies with both men and women in the United States. Timely recruitment is an important part of research studies; recruitment delays increase costs and may reduce overall sample size, which can result in inadequate statistical power to detect a true difference between groups, limiting a study’s robustness. In order to plan and budget for recruitment for rural research studies, investigators must be able to accurately predict the cost of recruitment per participant, as well as which recruitment methods are likely to be most cost-effective in order to make the best use of study funding. This paper reports on the recruitment strategies employed by a community randomized controlled intervention trial in rural communities – the Change Club (CC) study, which is described in detail elsewhere [28]. The CC study aimed to evaluate changes in health outcomes among residents of intervention communities compared to control communities. The study had an enrollment target of 2,260 adults across three samples: CC members (n = 10–14 per location, 12 locations), friends and family of CC members (n = 90–112 per location), and community members (n = 80–100 per location). Friends and family of CC members and community members do not participate in the intervention program but are included to assess whether there is an impact of the intervention on the communities. The CC intervention is a stepwise built environment change program led by local Extension educators that begins by building group rapport, identity, and norms and then is subsequently focused on community assessment, issue identification, and action planning. The expected outcome from each community’s CC is a built environment change such as improving walking trails or offering healthy foods at high school football games within the broader communities [28]. The study is being conducted in Texas (TX) and New York (NY) and all communities were rural as characterized by a Rural–Urban Commuting Area version 2.0 definition (RUCA2) score of 4 or higher [29]. All of the locations were medically underserved areas (MUAs) and/or health professional shortage areas (HPSAs). MUAs are specific geographic areas that experience a shortage of primary healthcare services [30] and HPSAs identify areas that experience a shortage of healthcare professionals [31]. Communities were paired based on location characteristics of rurality [29] and population size and within each pair, one location was randomized to intervention and the other to control.

The specific aims of this paper were to: (1) describe the recruitment strategies used locally and by the study team; (2) to assess the effectiveness of the recruitment strategies based on yielding the highest enrollments; (3) to evaluate the recruitment strategies’ cost-effectiveness. The results aim to inform recommended recruitment strategies for future community-based health studies to promote health equity for rural communities.

Methods

Study recruitment began in June 2022 and ended in April 2023. This sample includes the 11 rural medically underserved locations in TX and NY that had complete recruitment data. Nine locations recruited for the entire study recruitment period, while two locations were only open for recruitment for three months (Fig. 1). Individuals interested in the study completed an online Qualtrics survey and were eligible for the study if they were 18 years of age or older, lived in a study location, and, for CC members, met at least one health criteria for being at-risk for cardiovascular disease (e.g., physical inactivity, high cholesterol, high blood pressure) [28]. Eligible individuals were then shown an online consent form. Individuals who screened eligible and consented to the study are included in the sample. All three study roles (CC members, friends and family members, and community residents) were evaluated together for this study. The Texas A&M University Institutional Review Board (protocol # IRB2021-1490) approved this study (Clinical Trials #: NCT05002660).

Recruitment strategies and related costs

Oversight of recruitment was managed by the research team and included 15 local and centralized strategies in seven categories: online, mailing, email, encouraging referrals of friends and family, educator activities, flyers/posters, and traditional media (see Table 1 for descriptions of all recruitment strategies). Note that during the recruitment period, due to low enrollment, two TX sites were dropped, and one different TX site and two additional NY sites were added. In this paper, we report on recruitment for 11 of the 12 sites in the final sample. One site had incomplete recruitment data and is excluded. All recruitment efforts were based on the zip codes that were part of each community selected for the study. Recruitment methods for how each community was selected is described in the study protocol [28]; they included being rural per the RUCA2 criteria [29] and designated as MUAs [30] and/or HPSAs [31]. For mailings, letters were sent to each household in this list of zip codes with at least one adult living in the home. Phone calls were categorized as incoming calls educators answered from interested individuals or calls made by educators to individuals who indicated they were interested in participating and completed a contact interest form at an in-person study recruitment event. Some strategies were added later to boost enrollment. For example, mailings began with postcards (which had been effective in our previous studies), but in this study yielded lower than predicted enrollment and the need to reach enrollment goals necessitated trial of different strategies over time (e.g., mailing of letters instead of postcards). Also based on previous experience with Extension educators, they had the flexibility to deploy strategies they thought would be most effective in their area. Educators reported weekly on each local recruitment strategy used and estimated time via an online Qualtrics survey. Costs for each local recruitment strategy were obtained from invoices and by asking educators. The total cost for the educators’ effort was estimated as total hours spent on each strategy multiplied by $25/hour (based on an Indeed search for salaries of Extension educators with 5 + years of experience). The research team employed centralized recruitment strategies targeting zip codes within study locations. The dates each centralized strategy was used and the costs were obtained from invoices and tracked in Excel. The cost of the research team’s time was not estimated. Educator-reported local recruitment strategies, centralized recruitment strategies, and all related costs were tabulated in Excel.

Effectiveness analysis

Individuals were asked how they heard about the study as part of the online eligibility screener with both closed-choice and open-ended response options offered, and multiple responses allowed. Responses were reviewed and 15 strategy indicator variables created. The percentages of enrolled participants who reported hearing about the study via each recruitment strategy were calculated for each strategy overall and across locations. Equivalence in effectiveness of each recruitment strategy across locations was tested using Chi-square analyses with 95% confidence and Bonferroni correction for the 15 tests (α ≤ 0.003). Equivalence of effectiveness of each recruitment strategy between older adults (65 years and older) or younger adults (less than 65 years old) was tested using Chi-square analyses with 95% confidence and the same Bonferroni correction (α ≤ 0.003). All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 29.0.1.1 (IBM Corp.).

To visualize the change in effectiveness throughout recruitment, the number of enrolled participants per month is shown in Fig. 1. Below the line graph, a timeline is shown depicting when each recruitment strategy was implemented per month. When a recruitment strategy was used at least one time, the cell for that month was shaded. The cell shading only represents if a recruitment strategy was implemented and not variability in cost, frequency, or effort.

Cost-effectiveness analysis

To calculate the cost per enrolled participant, the total cost of each recruitment strategy was divided by the weighted count of participants who heard about the study via that recruitment strategy. The weighted number of enrolled participants was calculated by equally apportioning individuals who reported hearing about the study through multiple strategies. For example, an individual who reported hearing about the study via two different recruitment strategies was counted as 0.5 toward each. All cost effectiveness analyses were done in Excel.

Results

Overall study recruitment

By the end of the 11-month recruitment period, CC study enrollment across the 11 sites included in this paper was 2,265 [32] and 2,263 responded to the screener question about how they heard about the study and are included herein. The number of enrolled participants varied across time and visible spikes in recruitment corresponded to sending recruitment mailings (Fig. 1).

Educators in all locations reported attending events & meetings, sending emails, and making phone calls and sending texts; and almost all used flyers or posters [10], made social media posts [9], and used local newspapers [9] (Table 2). Events and meetings were the most time-consuming local recruitment activity (175.5 h) and the most expensive (6,802.50) and email (120.3 h, 3,075.00).

Effectiveness

The mailing of letters was the most effective strategy for recruiting participants into the CC study (37.7% of enrolled participants; Table 4). Referrals from friends or family was the second most effective strategy (23.2% of enrolled participants). The third most effective strategy was postcards (22.3% of enrolled participants). The other strategies that yielded at least 5% of enrolled participants were: advertisements on social media platforms (13.3%), flyers/posters (8.4%), and events and meetings (6.2%). The effectiveness of each recruitment strategy differed across locations for 4 of the 15 strategies -- postcards, letters, events & meetings, and friends & family. There were significant differences in recruitment strategy by age (65 years old or older vs. not) for postcards, letters, events and meetings, newspapers, and friends and family members (Table 4). A higher percentage of participants 65 years old and older (versus younger adults) were recruited using postcards, letters, events and meetings, and newspaper ads, while a higher percentage of younger adults than adults aged 65 years old or older were recruited via friends and family.

Cost-effectiveness

The overall average cost per enrolled participant in the CC study was $88.51 (Table 5). The most cost-effective strategy for recruiting participants was free referral by word of mouth from friends or family. The least cost-effective strategy was local educators’ efforts spent calling and texting potential participants, which cost 85 per person: flyers/posters (12.84), church bulletin (37.10), social media posts (56.47), and advertisements on social media platforms ($84.25).

Discussion

To our knowledge, no studies on participant recruitment costs include both men and women in rural communities in the United States. In order to effectively budget and plan for rural research studies, investigators must be aware of the potential average recruitment cost per participant and which methods may be most cost-effective. To reach study recruitment goals of over 2,000 individuals in these rural, medically underserved communities, a variety of local and central strategies were used. Our analyses suggest two recruitment strategies were highly effective and cost-effective—mailing letters and word-of-mouth referrals—and two strategies were effective and cost-effective—advertisements on social media and flyers/posters. These are common recruitment strategies in community-based research studies [8, 33–36].

The mailing of letters to all mailing addresses in targeted zip codes was highly effective, yielding the highest number of enrolled participants at a cost less than $60 per person. Furthermore, the letters were used later in the recruitment process and still produced substantial gains in recruitment. The effectiveness of letters varied across locations, with the highest yield in locations where they were the first recruitment strategy (locations 7 and 9), and in no location did fewer than 21% of enrolled participants report hearing about the study from a letter. Notably, letters were much more effective than postcards despite being sent to the same address lists, which is similar to two recent studies that used both postcard and letter mailings [37, 38]. Postcard costs are about half the cost of letters, but letters may have better long-term memory effects than postcards, since, with a letter, the envelope must be opened, the letter removed and read, and then then stored or discarded [37]. Hearing about the study via postcards and letters was reported in a higher percentage of older adults (≥ 65 years old) versus younger adults (< 65 years old).

Word of mouth referral from friends or family was also highly effective yielding the second highest number of enrolled participants at no cost. In terms of age, hearing about the study via friends and family was reported in a higher percentage of younger adults (< 65 years old) versus older adults (≥ 65 years old). “Snow-ball sampling” techniques like these referrals can bias samples and limit generalizability [39]. Overall, the enrolled participants in the CC study appear similar to the population in study locations for some demographic characteristics and not others. For example, 13.4% of the enrolled participants were 65 or older, which is similar to the 16.2% in the local populations [40]. However, more of the CC study enrolled participants were non-Hispanic white (80.7%) compared to the local populations (70.3%) [40], and more were women (66%) than in the local populations (50%) [40]. Higher representation of non-Hispanic whites in all research studies [41] and women in other similar research studies [35] is similar to the CC study. However, bias due to word-of-mouth recruitment does not appear to be a large problem in this sample.

Secondarily, advertisements on social media and the distribution and posting of flyers/posters also appear to be effective strategies given that both yielded at least 5% of the enrolled participants and cost less than $85 per person. Their effectiveness varied across locations in ways that mirrored local recruitment efforts. For example, social media advertisements appeared somewhat more effective in location 8, where the local educator ran additional social media advertisements to complement the social media advertisements centrally purchased for all zip codes. The flyer/poster strategy appeared more effective in locations 1 and 6 where populations were smaller; the educator distributed large quantities of flyers/posters and may have saturated those smaller population locations.

Although recruitment took place at a variety of rural locations across time, there was consistency across the sites in which strategies were most effective. Letters were either the most or second most effective across all 11 sites, and hearing about the study from friends and family was in the top three strategies across all 11 sites. In this descriptive study, it does not appear that time of year of recruitment or location would moderate the findings; future studies could be designed to test moderation of these types of variables.

Overall, the average cost per enrolled participants in the CC study was $88.51. To our knowledge, no prior studies that include both men and women in rural communities in the United States and report on recruitment costs have been conducted. However, three tangential studies on cost-effectiveness of recruitment strategies in rural areas, two in Australia and one with men only, are included here for comparison [34–36]. The cost per enrolled participant in Bracken et al. was AU$594 per person in 2018 [36] (1 AU, US213 per participant enrolled [35]. A recent diabetes prevention trial cost an average of AU680.54) [34]. The CC study’s overall cost per enrolled participant of $88.51 is favorable compared to these other studies [34–36]. However, some studies required onsite screenings [34, 36], which add substantial cost to enrollment. In addition, Estabrooks et al. cost calculations included research staff time [35], which the CC study did not account for. If university research staff time is excluded from their cost calculations [35], the average cost is reduced to 213 per person.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the findings may be attributable to differences in the timing and location of recruitment strategies, which were not controlled for in the analyses. Second, research team time and effort related to recruitment strategies were not tracked. The study staff on the project were a full-time project manager, two half-time staff, and one half-time analyst that monitored weekly recruitment activities and circulated reports to the study team. Considerable time was spent on many aspects of recruitment managed by the study team that are not reflected in the cost estimates including administrative effort on the mailings, creation and management of the social media and internet advertising accounts, designing and printing of flyers, etc. Although this study is similar to many others in which staff time is not included in cost-effectiveness analyses [27, 34, 36, 42], future studies that capture staff and local educators’ efforts would enhance information related to effective recruitment strategies. Third, strategies were self-reported by participants in close- and open-ended questions and may have been categorized incorrectly (e.g., as an internet advertisement when what they may have seen was an advertisement on social media). Researchers should utilize tracking the source of internet traffic and other recruitment methods, such as setting custom URL values for online recruitment strategies [36] or directly in Qualtrics surveys, for more nuanced and accurate tracking and reporting of which recruitment methods led to survey responses. To help researchers stay up to date on best practices and guidelines for online recruitment strategies, it is recommended that research staff are hired with expertise in this fast-changing area [33].

Conclusions

The mailing of recruitment letters, word-of-mouth referrals from family and friends, advertisements on social media, and the distribution and posting of flyers/posters were effective and cost-effective recruitment strategies and we recommend them for future studies in similar locations. Leveraging the close-knit social ties in rural communities to recommend study participation may be particularly fruitful but researchers should investigate potential selection bias. The mailing of postcards and attending or hosting local events and meetings still yielded at least 5% of enrolled participants but were more expensive; if there is funding available for these strategies, future studies in similar locations may consider using them. All other recruitment strategies used appear to be limited in their effectiveness and would not be recommended for similar studies and locations. In particular, the local educators’ efforts on phone calls and text messages and on posting on their websites yielded 0% of participants and are not recommended.
