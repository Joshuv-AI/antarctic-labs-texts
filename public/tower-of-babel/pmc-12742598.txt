Exploring EGCG for Leishmaniasis: A Natural Strategy for Neglected Disease Intervention
Authors: Khan Aejaz Ahmed, Usama Ahmad, Juber Akhtar, Anas Islam, Mohd Muazzam Khan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Neglected tropical diseases (NTDs) primarily affect populations in economically disadvantaged regions globally, yet they remain underfunded and understudied. Leishmaniasis, an infection caused by parasites and transmitted by vectors, originates from protozoa in the Leishmania family, and affects 700 000 to a million new cases yearly. Despite its significant health burden and potentially fatal consequences, research into new treatments remains minimal. Current treatments like pentavalent antimonials, amphotericin B, and miltefosine have considerable side effects, high costs, lengthy treatment periods, and face drug resistance. These limited therapeutic options emphasize the need for safer, more effective treatments accessible in resource-constrained settings. Epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), a polyphenolic compound from green tea (Camellia sinensis), shows promise as an antileishmanial agent. It disrupts the parasite’s redox balance through the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), inhibits growth, and enhances immune responses. However, poor bioavailability and rapid metabolism limit its clinical use; nanoformulations may improve its effectiveness. This review highlights the mechanism of action of EGCG against Leishmania parasites, examining its impact on parasite metabolism and cellular processes. It also explores various preclinical studies, therapeutic limitations and challenges, as well as advancements in drug delivery of EGCG for treating leishmaniasis.

Introduction

Leishmaniasis represents a vector-mediated parasitic disease instigated by protozoa belonging to the genus Leishmania, disseminated through the bites of infected sandflies. Leishmania parasites cycle between sand fly vectors (promastigote stage) and vertebrate hosts (intracellular amastigote stage). Transmission occurs when infected sand flies inoculate metacyclic promastigotes into the skin, where they invade macrophages, transform into amastigotes, and proliferate. Completing the cycle, sand flies ingest infected macrophages during blood meals, with amastigotes differentiating back into infectious promastigotes in the fly’s digestive tract (Figure 1). This health issue touches a wide range of individuals globally, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, and is acknowledged as a neglected tropical disease (NTD) by the World Health Organization (WHO) because of its remarkably significant impact on low-income populations, along with chronic insufficiencies in financial resources for research and significant medical developments [1,2]. The condition is evident in three essential presentations: visceral leishmaniasis (VL), cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL), and mucocutaneous leishmaniasis (MCL), with a predicted incidence of 70 000 to 1 million new cases and over 20 000 deaths annually, primarily affecting marginalized communities [3]. The available antileishmanial options are hindered by severe limitations: pentavalent antimonials, the historical first-line therapy, are plagued by cardiotoxicity and widespread parasite resistance; amphotericin B carries a high risk of nephrotoxicity, with its safer lipid formulations being prohibitively expensive for most endemic regions; and the sole oral drug, miltefosine, is teratogenic, precluding its use in women of childbearing age. This precarious therapeutic landscape is further compounded by the persistent lack of an effective human vaccine [4,5]. These impediments are further intensified in settings with limited resources, where inadequate infrastructure, environmental conditions, and socioeconomic obstacles obstruct access to medical care and control initiatives [2,5]. In light of these constraints, there exists a pressing necessity for the development of novel, safer, and more economically viable therapeutic options.

Natural elements have always formed a significant source of therapeutic resources and are currently experiencing a comeback of interest as credible alternatives for treating NTDs, such as leishmaniasis [6]. These agents, recognized for their bioactive nature, are made up of alkaloids, flavonoids, terpenoids, and phenolic substances, and they contribute to numerous mechanisms of action, which involve the hindrance of enzymes, the onset of oxidative stress, and the stimulation of apoptotic signaling pathways [7]. Among these bioactive compounds, EGCG, a significant catechin sourced from green tea (Camellia sinensis). Its established safety profile, based on a long history of human consumption and extensive clinical data, strongly supports its translational relevance. EGCG is a particularly compelling candidate because its pleiotropic bioactivities address the infection’s complexity on multiple fronts. Its direct antiparasitic effects have been observed within the Leishmania parasite itself, where it inhibits essential metabolic enzymes like trypanothione reductase (TR), leading to a surge in oxidative stress and apoptotic cell death. Simultaneously, its immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory properties are seen in host immune cells; for example, EGCG enhances the parasite-killing capacity of macrophages by promoting a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, while also controlling excessive tissue damage by inhibiting key signaling pathways such as NF-κB [8]. Furthermore, its well-documented antioxidant capacity helps neutralize pathological oxidative stress at the site of infection [9]. These diverse mechanisms render EGCG a compelling natural compound, warranting further investigation for its application in the treatment of leishmaniasis. This review intends to integrate the current research findings on EGCG’s ability to act against leishmaniasis and examine its viability as a therapeutic solution in the expansive landscape of natural product methodologies for NTDs (Table 1).

Epigallocatechin Gallate (EGCG)

EGCG is primarily sourced from green tea (Camellia sinensis), where it constitutes approximately 50% of the total polyphenol content [20]. It is the ester of epigallocatechin and gallic acid. Chemically, EGCG is a flavan-3-ol, featuring a complex structure comprising two benzene rings connected by a three-carbon chain. This arrangement creates a heterocyclic C-ring, with multiple hydroxyl groups attached to the aromatic rings, contributing to its potent antioxidant properties. The presence of a galloyl moiety further enhances its bioactivity [21,22]. EGCG is naturally sourced from green tea, specifically C. sinensis [23]. Baiyacha (BYC), a wild tea plant (C. gymnogyna) found in Fujian, China, is noted for high contents of theacrine and 3′′-methyl-epigallocatechin gallate [24]. This indicates that certain wild tea varieties may offer unique chemical profiles compared to common C. sinensis cultivars. Due to its polyhydroxy structure, EGCG has limitations in lipid solubility, stability, and bioavailability. Esterification techniques have been used to modify EGCG, enhancing its efficacy. Both chemical (using catalysts like pyridine and triethylamine) and enzymatic (using lipases) methods are employed for esterification. Esterified EGCG derivatives exhibit higher bioavailability and greater stability compared to native [1] (Figure 2).

EGCG demonstrates a wide range of pharmacological properties across various disease models. It acts as a strong antioxidant, neutralizing ROS and enhancing endogenous antioxidant defenses such as glutathione peroxidase and catalase. It also downregulates pro-oxidant enzymes, including NADPH oxidase. As an anti-inflammatory agent, EGCG inhibits signaling pathways like NF-κB and MAPKs, leading to reduced expression of cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6. Beyond its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory roles, EGCG exhibits antiparasitic, antibacterial, antiviral, and anticancer effects. It disrupts membrane integrity in pathogens, inhibits key metabolic enzymes, and induces apoptosis through mitochondrial pathways. In cancer models, EGCG has shown the ability to block angiogenesis, suppress proliferation, and trigger cell death in various tumor types [25,26]. As we delve deeper, the next section will unravel how EGCG’s molecular interactions disrupt Leishmania survival, offering a natural weapon against this persistent parasite.

Antileishmanial Mechanisms of EGCG

Disruption of Parasite Redox Homeostasis and Metabolic Integrity

EGCG exhibits marked antileishmanial action by disrupting the redox equilibrium within the Leishmania parasite. A central function is its competitive inhibition of TR, a crucial enzyme for maintaining the parasite’s redox homeostasis [27,28]. This compromises the trypanothione pathway, which is critical for the detoxification of ROS [28,29]. The resulting disruption is heightened by EGCG-induced mitochondrial depolarization, which diminishes intracellular ATP concentrations and further elevates ROS generation, ultimately leading to parasitic demise [28,30].

The integral role of ROS, particularly hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), is substantiated by the reversal of these leishmanicidal effects with the enzyme PEG-catalase [28,30]. Furthermore, EGCG exacerbates this oxidative imbalance by inhibiting arginase, an enzyme involved in polyamine production essential for the parasite’s lifespan [31]. Collectively, these ROS-mediated pathways induce an apoptosis-like cell death, which underpins EGCG’s capacity to impede parasite replication and decrease parasitic burdens [32].

This multi-pronged strategy is particularly effective because Leishmania is heavily reliant on its unique redox apparatus, centered on the trypanothione biosynthetic pathway, to mitigate oxidative stress from host macrophages [33,34]. Given the considerable toxicity and drug resistance associated with contemporary therapies, EGCG’s ability to selectively target the parasite’s metabolism marks it as a strong candidate for therapeutic development [27,35,36].

Modulation of Host Immune Response

EGCG significantly augments the innate immune response against Leishmania by stimulating macrophage activation and enhancing their microbicidal capabilities. A key process driving this phenomenon is the attachment of EGCG to the 67-kDa laminin receptor (67LR), which then triggers the p38 and JNK MAPK signaling pathways. This process results in a heightened generation of ROS and nitric oxide (NO), two essential effector molecules that are crucial for the intracellular removal of parasites [37,38]. Interestingly, EGCG also influences the NLRP3 inflammasome in a way that leads to reduced release of IL-1β. Although this may seem paradoxical, it effectively mitigates excessive inflammatory responses and tissue injury, thereby enabling macrophages to concentrate on the eradication of parasites [39]. Furthermore, a crucial aspect of EGCG’s influence pertains to metabolic reprogramming: Leishmania infection biases macrophage metabolism towards oxidative phosphorylation and anti-inflammatory M2 polarization. By reinstating glycolysis, EGCG counteracts this alteration, fostering an M1-like pro-inflammatory phenotype that enhances parasite eradication [40,41]. In murine models infected with L. major, treatment with EGCG significantly enhanced macrophage phagocytic activity and ROS/NO production, resulting in a greater than 60% reduction in amastigote viability compared to untreated controls [37].

In addition to its role in augmenting macrophage functionality, EGCG meticulously modulates the cytokine milieu to foster an effective adaptive immune response against Leishmania. It collaborates with interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) to raise interleukin-12 (IL-12), a significant cytokine that affects Th1 immune polarization, which is necessary for the stimulation of infected macrophages and aids in parasite eradication [42,43]. Additionally, EGCG facilitates the creation of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) by activating NF-κB, which in turn raises macrophage-related cytotoxicity [37]. Conversely, EGCG reduces the expression of immunosuppressive cytokines such as IL-10 and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), which are acknowledged for their ability to hinder macrophage activation and facilitate parasite persistence [42,44]. It also inhibits the SOCS1/3 proteins, which are frequently upregulated in Leishmania-infected cells and disrupt Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 1 (STAT1) signaling, consequently impairing responses to IFN-γ. EGCG reinstates STAT1 phosphorylation, thus rejuvenating macrophage responsiveness to IFN-γ and fortifying cellular immunity [42]. In experimental investigations regarding visceral leishmaniasis, it was found that EGCG treatment caused a 70% decrease in the splenic parasite burden, correlating well with increased IFN-γ and decreased IL-10 levels [40], underlining its dual purpose of enhancing host defense while lowering immune suppression. These mechanistic insights set the stage for examining EGCG’s efficacy in preclinical studies, where its translational potential begins to take shape.

Preclinical Studies of EGCG for Leishmaniasis

A comprehensive investigation into EGCG against Leishmania amazonensis revealed remarkable antileishmanial efficacy in both in vitro and in vivo experimental settings. In vitro, macrophages infected with Leishmania and isolated from Swiss mice were subjected to EGCG treatment at concentrations varying between 3 and 12 µM, culminating in a significant diminution of the infection index after a duration of 72 hours. Microscopic examination of over 200 cells per slide substantiated the reduction in intracellular amastigote quantities. Concurrent viability assays spanning a broader concentration spectrum (3-3000 µM) facilitated the determination of the IC₅₀ for both macrophages and amastigotes, resulting in a favorable selectivity index that corroborated parasite-specific cytotoxicity. In vivo, BALB/c mice infected intradermally with L. amazonensis received oral administration of 30 mg/kg/day EGCG for a period extending up to 52 days. Mice subjected to treatment exhibited notable reductions in the size of ear lesions and parasite burden, comparable to those animals administered meglumine antimoniate. Limiting dilution assays validated a decrease in viable parasite loads, while biochemical evaluations (AST, ALT, creatinine) revealed no significant systemic toxicity. The investigation’s primary limitation is that further toxicity studies, including genotoxicity, and the determination of optimal dose and therapeutic regimen, are still needed for EGCG to be considered as a leishmaniasis treatment [45].

A thorough analysis was conducted to assess the antileishmanial efficacy of four catechins extracted from green tea, specifically aimed at L. amazonensis, through in vitro macrophage infection evaluations and in vivo mouse research. The investigation into catechins showed that EGCG and epigallocatechin (EGC) demonstrated notable efficacy, with their IC50 values measured under 59.6 µg/mL against intracellular amastigotes. These compounds exhibited a favorable selectivity index (SI > 1), signifying selective cytotoxicity towards the parasites while preserving the viability of host THP1 monocytic cells. Microscopic evaluation validated significant reductions in the intracellular parasite load, all while maintaining the integrity of host cell viability. In the in vivo component, BALB/c mice afflicted with established cutaneous leishmaniasis received topical treatment with 15% EGCG incorporated into a hydrophilic ointment for 18 consecutive days. This treatment resulted in a marked reduction in lesion size, achieving an 80.4% decrease in parasite burden, closely aligning with the 85.1% efficacy reported for the reference drug Glucantime administered via the intraperitoneal route. All in all, these observations stress the noteworthy dual antiamastigote activity of EGCG in both controlled and natural environments, indicating its potential as a safer topical alternative to usual antimonial treatments for cutaneous leishmaniasis. The study’s limitations include higher in vitro IC50 values for EGCG/EGC compared to the reference drug, the influence of experimental conditions like incubation temperature on IC50 values, the initiation of topical EGCG treatment only after the infection was established, and the lack of investigation into the synergistic effects between EGCG and EGC for improved efficacy [46] (Figure 3 a,b).

A comprehensive analysis investigated the antileishmanial properties of EGCG against L. braziliensis, leveraging both in vitro and in vivo testing strategies. In vitro investigations indicated that EGCG displayed cytotoxicity towards both promastigote and intracellular amastigote forms, influenced by dosage and duration. The level at which half of the top inhibitory action takes place (IC50) for promastigotes was discovered to be approximately 278 µM after a 72-hour exposure. In contrast, the IC50 values for intracellular amastigotes within macrophages fluctuated between 3.4 and 3.7 mM, showcasing a remarkable level of effectiveness within the cells. It was found that EGCG plays a role in the significant generation of ROS, especially H2O2, which subsequently caused mitochondrial membrane depolarization, reduced ATP, and ultimately resulted in the death of the parasite. The application of PEG-catalase, an agent that neutralizes H2O2, successfully reversed these effects, thereby confirming that ROS-mediated oxidative stress is fundamental to EGCG’s mechanism of action. Administration of EGCG orally in living BALB/c mice infected with L. braziliensis achieved a reduction exceeding 92% in parasite count and a significant shrinkage of lesions, without any noticeable toxicity, as shown by stable serum liver enzyme levels (AST, ALT) and creatinine. Collectively, the data support EGCG as a reliable and safe treatment for leishmaniasis, as it targets mitochondrial function and energy pathways in parasites, justifying its continued research as a non-toxic, plant-sourced therapeutic option for this disease. Therefore, while the initial findings are promising, the path to clinical application requires further research to define EGCG’s precise molecular mechanism, establish an optimal therapeutic regimen, and conduct comprehensive toxicity studies, all while navigating the challenges posed by inadequate experimental models for key species like L. braziliensis [30] (Figure 3 c,d).

An extensive laboratory examination was carried out to assess the effectiveness of EGCG when used alongside Glucantime against L. tropica. Employing 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assays on both promastigote and intramacrophage amastigote cultures, the investigation revealed that EGCG demonstrated a dose-dependent antiproliferative effect that exceeded that of Glucantime at comparable concentrations. Importantly, EGCG exhibited a marginally superior efficacy in diminishing intracellular amastigote loads at lower dosages. When both agents were co-administered, a synergistic inhibitory effect was noted, significantly facilitating parasite clearance in a dose-responsive manner. Flow cytometric analyses revealed that EGCG triggered apoptosis in L. tropica, with rising apoptotic cell populations corresponding to increasing concentrations of the drug, thus implying that EGCG’s cytotoxic effects are facilitated via programmed cell death mechanisms. EGCG’s influence on the immune response appears to involve an increase in Th1-related gene expression, notably IL-12, IL-1β, and iNOS, alongside a reduction in IL-10, a cytokine tied to Th2-induced immune suppression. This specific gene expression profile underscores the role of EGCG in promoting macrophage activation and augmenting cellular immunity. Cytotoxicity assays further corroborated that both EGCG and Glucantime exhibited selective toxicity towards parasites in comparison to host macrophages, as evidenced by high selectivity indices, thereby reinforcing the therapeutic promise of EGCG as a safe and effective antileishmanial agent, both in isolation and in conjunction with conventional therapeutic strategies. Therefore, the study’s primary limitation is its exclusive reliance on in vitro experiments; while the findings are promising, the complete absence of in vivo data means the observed antileishmanial effects are preliminary and require validation in a living biological system to determine their true therapeutic potential [32].

Another detailed investigation rigorously examined the antileishmanial activity of EGCG in terms of both laboratory experiments and practical biological applications. Experiments in vitro with macrophages from BALB/c mice infected with Leishmania parasites revealed considerable anti-amastigote action, demonstrated by an EC50 value of 2.6 µM, thereby suggesting that just a modest quantity of EGCG is sufficient to lower the intracellular load of parasites by half. This pronounced efficacy, noted after a 72-hour incubation period across a concentration spectrum of 3-48 µM, underscores the robust therapeutic promise of EGCG at comparatively low dosages. In harmony with these reports, in vivo assessments using BALB/c mice infected with L. infantum evaluated the oral performance of EGCG over a 5-day course, with twice-daily administration at 12.5, 25, and 50 mg/kg/day. A reduction in the burden of hepatic parasites was observed, with estimated ED50 values of 12.4 mg/kg/day and ED50 of 21.5 mg/kg/day, confirming that oral administration of EGCG can lead to notable therapeutic benefits. In summary, the in vitro and in vivo evidence together indicate that EGCG may serve as a valuable candidate for managing VL, potentially providing a safer, orally available substitute for existing chemotherapy options. Therefore, while the study provides a strong proof-of-concept for EGCG’s efficacy, its call to determine an optimal dose and regimen implicitly frames the research as a foundational first step. It highlights that a comprehensive evaluation, including detailed toxicological studies, mechanistic elucidation, and long-term safety assessments, is still required to fully establish the compound’s clinical potential [47].

In another study, the in vitro investigation examined the antiproliferative properties of EGCG on L. amazonensis promastigotes cultured in Schneider’s Drosophila medium augmented with fetal calf serum. EGCG was administered within a concentration spectrum of 0.065 to 0.250 mM across incubation intervals ranging from 24 to 144 hours, resulting in a distinct dose- and time-dependent inhibition of parasitic proliferation. The IC50 at the 120-hour mark was ascertained to be 0.063 mM, with complete growth cessation attained at the maximal concentration tested. Mechanistic analyses indicated that EGCG induces mitochondrial dysfunction, as demonstrated by prominent mitochondrial swelling and inner membrane impairment observed through transmission electron microscopy, in conjunction with cytoplasmic vacuolization. Rhodamine 123 staining further substantiated a loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), suggestive of bioenergetic collapse. This mitochondrial impairment is likely responsible for diminished ATP synthesis and the ensuing demise of the parasite. The study concluded that the leishmanicidal efficacy of EGCG is predominantly mediated by mitochondrial disruption, with a significant correlation established between mitochondrial damage and growth inhibition, thereby positioning EGCG as a promising candidate for targeting the energy metabolism of parasites. However, the investigation’s fundamental limitation is its exclusive reliance on in vitro data; as the authors acknowledge, the promising leishmanicidal effects observed in the lab, such as mitochondrial collapse, must be validated in a murine model to confirm the compound’s efficacy in a complex living system [48]. Researchers explored the effectiveness of green tea polyphenols in combating leishmaniasis, particularly focusing on catechin, epicatechin (EC), epicatechin gallate (ECG), and EGCG, by employing a comprehensive method that combined molecular docking simulations with laboratory tests on promastigotes targeting L. infantum. The computational docking analysis indicated that these compounds demonstrated significant interaction with two pivotal enzymes of the parasite, trypanothione synthetase (TS) and arginase (ARG-L) by engaging their active sites and potentially obstructing their enzymatic functions. Subsequent experiments supported these interactions in vitro, demonstrating that cultured promastigotes exposed to gradually increasing concentrations of the compounds experienced growth inhibition that correlated with both time and dosage, as evaluated through MTT assays carried out over 24, 48, and 72 hours. In the evaluation of the compounds, EGCG displayed the strongest antileishmanial action, demonstrated by the lowest IC50 and IC90 numbers, with ECG next in line, while EC had the minimal impact. Notably, the alignment of computational predictions with empirical findings underscores the promise of EGCG and ECG as prospective candidates for the development of antileishmanial therapeutics, highlighting the significance of green tea polyphenols in targeting essential metabolic enzymes within the parasite. The research findings are limited by an exclusively in vitro and in silico approach, revealing that the tested compounds require significant chemical optimization and subsequent in vivo validation to demonstrate any therapeutic advantage over existing treatments like pentamidine [49] (Table 2). Therapeutic Limitations and Challenges

EGCG’s clinical utility is significantly constrained by several key limitations, primarily concerning its stability, bioavailability, and potential drug interactions [9]. EGCG is notably unstable under physiological conditions, degrading in response to factors such as air exposure, alkaline pH, and elevated temperatures [50-52]. Strategies such as cool storage, encapsulation in nanoparticles, and glycosylation have been explored to mitigate degradation and preserve its bioactivity [53-56]. In addition to stability issues, EGCG suffers from poor bioavailability, with only a small fraction reaching systemic circulation due to gastrointestinal degradation, extensive metabolism, and efflux by transporter proteins [52,57-59]. Innovative approaches such as nanoparticle-based delivery, structural modification, and bioenhancer co-administration (eg, with piperine) have shown promise [60,61]. Furthermore, EGCG may interact with various drugs by modulating cytochrome P450 enzymes and transporter proteins, which can influence drug metabolism and efficacy [59,62]. Other considerations include variability in EGCG content across preparations, potential pro-oxidant activity under specific conditions, dose-dependent toxicity, and interindividual differences due to genetic polymorphisms like catechol-o-methyltransferase (COMT) [63,64]. Collectively, these limitations underscore the need for continued research into formulation technologies and personalized strategies to optimize EGCG’s therapeutic potential while ensuring safety and efficacy.

Despite its promising therapeutic effects, EGCG exhibits very low oral bioavailability (<5%) in humans, due to its chemical instability, poor solubility, and active efflux by cellular transporters. Chemically, EGCG is prone to degradation in alkaline environments, such as the intestine, and undergoes auto-oxidation upon exposure to air or light [52,65]. Stabilization methods, including cool, dry storage and encapsulation in β-lactoglobulin nanoparticles, have been shown to mitigate these effects [66,67]. Additionally, EGCG’s low lipid solubility hinders its intestinal absorption. Formulation strategies such as co-administration with piperine or encapsulation in nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs) have been reported to improve absorption by 2-3 fold [68,69]. Furthermore, EGCG is a substrate for efflux transporters like P-glycoprotein (P-gp) and multidrug resistance-associated proteins (MRPs), which actively limit intracellular accumulation. This can be counteracted by using natural inhibitors such as fish oil and vitamin C [61,70].

A major pharmacokinetic limitation of EGCG is its extensive first-pass metabolism, which rapidly reduces its systemic concentration. In the liver and intestines, EGCG undergoes phase II conjugation reactions, including sulfation (via SULT1A1) and glucuronidation (via UGT1A1), yielding largely inactive metabolites [9,61]. Additionally, methylation of EGCG’s hydroxyl groups is accelerated by genetic polymorphisms in the COMT gene, leading to individual variability in drug response. Gut microbiota also plays a role; microbial biotransformation of EGCG produces phenolic acids such as valerolactones, which possess reduced bioactivity. Notably, strains like Aspergillus niger can completely degrade EGCG within 48 hours [71]. Encapsulation technologies, such as chitosan-silica nanoparticles, and protein co-administration (eg, albumin) have been shown to slow metabolism and extend EGCG’s plasma half-life from 1.5 to 5 hours, thereby enhancing its pharmacokinetic profile [72,73].

Despite robust preclinical efficacy, clinical translation of EGCG remains limited. Many animal studies use doses between 20-100 mg/kg/day, which exceed the human equivalent doses (typically ≤10 mg/kg/day), complicating direct extrapolation [74,75]. Clinical trials, though sparse, reveal mixed outcomes. For instance, the PERSEUS trial (NCT04775035) demonstrated the safety of EGCG in children with Down syndrome but showed variable effects on cognitive endpoints [75]. In oncology, Phase I/II trials have been conducted for breast cancer, with one reporting only a 30% partial response rate, reflecting inconsistent clinical benefits [76,77]. Moreover, population-specific risks have emerged. In reproductive-aged women, high doses (800 mg/day) caused transient hepatotoxicity in about 5% of participants, necessitating dose adjustments and increased safety monitoring [78].

EGCG has a narrow therapeutic index, and its toxicity profile becomes concerning at higher doses. This dose-dependent cytotoxicity highlights the importance of precision in dosing strategies. Furthermore, excessive EGCG intake (>1000 mg/day) has been linked to acute liver injury, often attributed to mitochondrial dysfunction and glutathione depletion, as documented in several case reports [78,79]. Long-term toxicity is also a concern. In mice, chronic administration of EGCG at 100 mg/kg led to a 25% reduction in trabecular bone density, likely due to DYRK1A inhibition, raising concerns about its impact on bone health [80]. Advances in Drug Delivery and Future Prospects

Nanoencapsulation has emerged as a groundbreaking method in antileishmanial therapy, effectively confronting longstanding issues such as drug solubility, systemic toxicity, and resistance. Lipid-centric systems, including liposomes and solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs), in combination with polymeric carriers such as chitosan and poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), play a significant role in today’s research due to their heightened effectiveness in targeting macrophages, a key aspect for the elimination of intracellular Leishmania [81,82]. A notable instance of successful clinical application of this methodology is liposomal amphotericin B (AmB), which has demonstrated superior efficacy and safety relative to its unencapsulated counterpart by mitigating nephrotoxicity while enhancing accumulation in infected macrophages [83]. Innovative advancements are also seen in plant-based and hybrid nanocarriers. Chitosan-gellan gum nanohydrogels exhibit effective encapsulation of hydrophilic compounds such as EGCG, thereby enhancing both stability and bioavailability [84]. Hybrid systems such as zein-EGCG nanoparticles facilitate controlled drug release and retention, thus augmenting therapeutic longevity [85]. Furthermore, redox-responsive systems, exemplified by EGCG/cystamine complexes, are engineered to capitalize on oxidative conditions present at infection sites, allowing for stimuli-triggered release and enhanced targeted efficacy [86].

EGCG has garnered significant interest not solely for its intrinsic antiparasitic properties but also for its immunomodulatory effects, rendering it a potent adjuvant in leishmaniasis treatment. It promotes the elimination of Leishmania through mitochondrial membrane disruption and stimulates Th1 cytokine production, thereby activating macrophages to enhance parasite clearance [68,84]. In conjunction with traditional pharmacological agents such as miltefosine or AmB, EGCG has exhibited synergistic effects, resulting in a reduction of the required drug dosages by 30-50%, and potentially diminishing associated toxicities [87,88]. The efficacy of EGCG is further amplified through nanoformulations. As a case in point, EGCG-loaded poly(n-butylcyanoacrylate) nanoparticles (EGCG/PBCA-NPs) have indicated better pulmonary delivery in silicosis studies, an approach that might be relevant for visceral leishmaniasis [89]. Additionally, redox-sensitive EGCG/cystamine nanoparticles have exhibited enhanced targeting capabilities in inflammatory contexts such as atherosclerotic plaques, indicating potential applicability in chronic leishmanial inflammation [86]. Yet, the pH volatility and the rapid breakdown of EGCG introduce major difficulties, which call for the advancement of cutting-edge delivery systems like metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) or covalent organic frameworks (COFs) to support lasting drug release [51].

Although nanoformulations exhibit considerable potential, the translation of these innovations into clinical practice continues to pose significant challenges. Currently, AmB and paromomycin are the sole products incorporated into regular clinical practices, whereas preparations like EGCG-loaded zein nanoparticles are still in the early exploratory phase and do not have validation from phase III trials [81,85,90,91]. An additional important dimension is the presence of ongoing clinical trials investigating novel antileishmanial therapies: for example, a pilot clinical study in Iran evaluated topical nanoliposomal AmB 0.4% in cutaneous leishmaniasis patients, achieving 92-95% cure rates and establishing a basis for Phase III development [92]. Also, although not a nano-carrier study per se, a controlled human infection model (CHIM) of sand fly-transmitted cutaneous leishmaniasis was established in 2022-2023 [93], which paves the way for more rigorous clinical evaluation of delivery systems in the future. Another trial (NCT00344188) remains registered to explore natural history and treatment outcomes of Leishmania infections, creating infrastructure for future nano-carrier clinical translation.

Another major concern relates to the increase in drug resistance, which is aggravated by parasitic methods, including heightened ABC transporter expression and biofilm development. Innovative strategies, including the co-delivery of EGCG alongside nitric oxide donors or immunostimulatory agents (for instance, CpG oligonucleotides), may effectively circumvent these resistance mechanisms [94,95]. Furthermore, there exists a conspicuous deficiency in the integration of real-time bioimaging techniques. Advanced technologies, such as quantum dots or fluorescent markers for monitoring nanoparticle biodistribution and release kinetics, remain underexploited within the context of leishmaniasis research [96,97]. Future initiatives should adopt a multi-omics-guided approach to formulation design, leveraging proteomics and metabolomics to pinpoint parasite-specific targets, including TR [98]. EGCG’s ability to reduce inflammation could be purposefully employed to improve the performance of checkpoint inhibitors in fighting mucosal leishmaniasis [76,99]. Concurrently, sustainability has emerged as a pivotal consideration for biodegradable carriers such as cellulose nanocrystals, presenting environmentally friendly alternatives that exhibit reduced toxicity [100,101].

The advent of nanoencapsulation and EGCG-based combination therapies signifies a transformative shift in the therapeutic management of leishmaniasis. This set of strategies confronts the issues related to drug administration and the adjustment of immune responses, consequently ensuring safer and more potent alternatives to the treatments we have now. Ultimately, closing the gap that exists between experimental advancements in laboratories and their practical use in healthcare demands the collaboration of diverse disciplines, better pharmacokinetic simulations, and a commitment to manufacturing techniques that can grow with demand. Future investigational efforts should prioritize patient-centered solutions, such as oral or transdermal nanoformulations, to augment compliance within endemic regions and broaden access to innovative therapeutic options [102,103]. As we conclude, these innovations underscore the transformative role of natural products in redefining leishmaniasis treatment.

Conclusion

EGCG has surfaced as a remarkable antileishmanial compound, attributed to its multiple mechanisms of action, which include the disruption of redox states through the inhibition of TR, the initiation of oxidative stress, and immunomodulatory actions that promote macrophage-driven clearance of parasites. Preclinical investigations reveal its efficacy in diminishing parasitic load and lesion advancement, particularly when employed in conjunction with traditional therapeutic agents such as miltefosine and amphotericin B. Nonetheless, notwithstanding its therapeutic promise, EGCG is confronted with substantial pharmacological obstacles, including suboptimal bioavailability, rapid metabolic degradation, and dose-dependent hepatotoxicity, which impede its clinical applicability. To address these obstacles, sophisticated drug delivery mechanisms like nanoencapsulation, lipid-based carriers, and redox-responsive formulations necessitate refinement to boost stability, targeted delivery, and prolonged release. Moreover, comprehensive clinical assessments are crucial for verifying safety, effectiveness, and the best dosing strategies among human groups. The investigation of EGCG elucidates the broader import of natural compounds in the context of NTDs, where traditional therapies frequently encounter limitations related to toxicity, resistance, and accessibility.
