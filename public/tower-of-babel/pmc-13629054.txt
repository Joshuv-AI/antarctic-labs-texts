Extrachromosomal DNA as a platform for epigenetic reprogramming in cancer
Authors: Bi-xia Liu, Lu Zhou, Shuzhen Lai, Xueqiong Han, Xi-Jian Dai, Rui Liu, Yuxin Wang, Daosong Chen, Jialiang Wei, Hongkang Hu, Wenhan Zu, Lei Jiang, Jin Chang, Lisha Zha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExtrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA) has emerged as a major contributor to cancer aggressiveness, yet its role extends far beyond its classical definition as a vehicle for oncogene amplification. Recent studies indicate that ecDNA functions as a dynamic regulatory platform that reshapes chromatin accessibility, enhancer activity, three-dimensional genome organization, nuclear architecture, and transcriptional output. Through these properties, ecDNA enables cancer cells to amplify oncogenic signaling, generate transcriptional plasticity, and rapidly adapt to selective pressures. In contrast to chromosomal amplification, ecDNA is structurally independent, frequently circular, acentromeric, and inherited through noncanonical mitotic behaviors, allowing unequal segregation, coordinated co-inheritance, and persistent propagation of advantageous regulatory states. Emerging evidence further shows that ecDNA can hijack pre-existing enhancers, rewire enhancer–oncogene communication, activate noncanonical regulatory elements, and assemble into transcriptionally active nuclear hubs and condensate-associated structures, thereby transforming genome rearrangement into epigenetic innovation. These mechanisms position ecDNA as a key driver of intratumoral heterogeneity, clonal evolution, metastatic adaptation, and therapeutic resistance. At the same time, the high transcriptional burden and specialized regulatory architecture of ecDNA-positive tumors may create selective vulnerabilities, including transcription–replication conflict and dependence on ecDNA-associated hubs, condensates, and regulatory modules. In this review, we synthesize current advances in the formation, regulation, inheritance, and functional consequences of ecDNA, with a particular focus on its emerging role as a platform for epigenetic reprogramming in cancer. We also discuss how this conceptual shift from copy-number biology to regulatory biology may redefine ecDNA as both a mechanistic framework for tumor plasticity and a potential frontier for precision oncology.Graphical abstract Keywords: Extrachromosomal DNA, Epigenetic reprogramming, Chromatin accessibility, Enhancer rewiring, Transcriptional plasticity, Tumor evolution

Introduction

Extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA) has emerged as one of the most intriguing forms of cancer-associated genome plasticity [1–3]. Historically, ecDNA was primarily regarded as a cytogenetic manifestation of oncogene amplification, often observed as double minutes or other acentromeric extrachromosomal elements in highly aggressive tumors [4]. In this conventional view, the major biological significance of ecDNA lies in its ability to increase oncogene copy number, thereby enhancing malignant growth and conferring selective advantages under stress. Early studies therefore framed ecDNA largely as a structural consequence of genome instability and a genomic vehicle for amplified oncogenes.

However, this copy-number-centered interpretation is no longer sufficient to explain the increasingly diverse functions now attributed to ecDNA in cancer. Large-scale pan-cancer analyses have demonstrated that ecDNA is not a rare or incidental genomic abnormality, but a recurrent feature across multiple tumor types, where it is strongly associated with oncogene enrichment, aggressive clinical behavior, and poor patient outcomes [5–9]. These observations established ecDNA as a clinically relevant hallmark of malignant progression rather than a passive byproduct of chromosomal disruption. More recent cohort-level studies further revealed that ecDNA is particularly enriched in metastatic and heavily treated tumors, suggesting that its biological importance extends beyond tumor initiation to disease evolution, adaptation, and therapeutic resistance. Together, these findings have shifted the conceptual framework of ecDNA from a simple amplification structure to an active participant in cancer progression.

A major reason for this shift is that ecDNA does far more than increase gene dosage. Emerging evidence indicates that ecDNA can reshape gene regulation at multiple levels, including chromatin accessibility, enhancer activity, three-dimensional genomic interactions, nuclear organization, and transcriptional output [3, 10–13]. In contrast to amplified regions embedded within linear chromosomes, ecDNA exists outside canonical chromosomal constraints, allowing oncogenes and regulatory elements to be repositioned into novel spatial and epigenetic contexts. This structural freedom may facilitate unusually open chromatin states, enhancer hijacking, regulatory rewiring, and high-density transcriptional activity, thereby enabling ecDNA-bearing cells to generate gene expression programs that cannot be fully explained by copy number alone. In this sense, ecDNA is increasingly being recognized not merely as amplified DNA, but as a dynamic chromatin entity capable of reorganizing the regulatory landscape of cancer cells. This emerging view places ecDNA at the intersection of genome instability and epigenetic plasticity [1, 14]. Cancer progression depends not only on the acquisition of genetic alterations, but also on the capacity of tumor cells to flexibly reprogram transcriptional states in response to developmental cues, microenvironmental stress, and therapeutic pressure [9]. ecDNA appears to be particularly well suited to support such adaptability. Because it lacks centromeres and can segregate unevenly during mitosis, ecDNA can rapidly generate cell-to-cell variation in oncogene dosage and regulatory potential. At the same time, its unique structural and chromatin features may permit the assembly of highly active transcriptional hubs that amplify oncogenic signaling and promote rapid phenotypic diversification. These properties suggest that ecDNA is not simply a marker of tumor aggressiveness, but a mechanistic engine of tumor evolution.

Against this background, it is increasingly reasonable to view ecDNA as a platform for epigenetic reprogramming in cancer [15–17].The term “platform” is particularly appropriate because ecDNA appears to provide a physical and regulatory scaffold on which multiple oncogenic processes converge: genomic rearrangement, enhancer rewiring, chromatin activation, nuclear clustering, transcriptional amplification, and adaptive selection. Rather than functioning as an isolated genetic anomaly, ecDNA may create a permissive regulatory architecture that supports cancer cell plasticity and accelerates the emergence of aggressive and therapy-resistant phenotypes [18]. This conceptual transition is important because it shifts ecDNA research from simply cataloguing extrachromosomal oncogene amplification to explaining how ecDNA rewires gene regulation, supports tumor adaptation, and creates targetable dependencies in cancer cells.

In this review, we discuss how ecDNA functions as an epigenetically active platform that reshapes the transcriptional and evolutionary landscape of cancer. We first summarize the biological foundations of ecDNA, including its formation, structural features, and general oncogenic significance. We then examine how ecDNA contributes to epigenetic reprogramming through open chromatin states, enhancer hijacking, regulatory innovation, and three-dimensional nuclear reorganization. Next, we explore how these features promote transcriptional plasticity, intratumoral heterogeneity, clonal evolution, and therapy resistance. Finally, we highlight emerging therapeutic opportunities and technological advances that are transforming our understanding of ecDNA biology. By framing ecDNA as a dynamic chromatin and transcriptional platform rather than a simple amplification product, this review aims to provide an updated perspective on how structural genome alterations can drive cancer through epigenetic and transcriptional mechanisms. Importantly, ecDNA is not restricted to late-stage or terminally unstable cancers. In Barrett’s oesophagus-associated tumorigenesis, ecDNA can emerge during the transition from high-grade dysplasia to oesophageal adenocarcinoma, indicating that ecDNA formation may be an early event in malignant transformation rather than only a late consequence of genome instability [19].

Biological foundations of ecDNA: formation, structure, and general oncogenic impact

Before ecDNA can be discussed as a platform for epigenetic reprogramming, it is necessary to establish its basic biological features. Although ecDNA is often introduced as a form of focal oncogene amplification, accumulating evidence indicates that it differs fundamentally from chromosomal amplification in its physical organization, inheritance pattern, and regulatory potential [20–24]. These distinctive properties provide the foundation for understanding why ecDNA can exert effects that extend beyond copy-number gain alone. In parallel, recent work has begun to clarify how ecDNA is generated, linking its emergence to catastrophic genome remodeling, replication-associated stress, and dysregulated DNA repair. Together, these features position ecDNA as both a product and a driver of cancer genome plasticity.

What is ecDNA and why is it distinct from chromosomal amplification?

Extrachromosomal DNA refers to circular, acentromeric DNA elements that exist outside canonical chromosomes and frequently harbor oncogenes or regulatory sequences in cancer cells [25, 26].Unlike chromosomal amplifications, which remain embedded within linear chromosomes and are constrained by chromosome architecture, ecDNA is physically separated from the chromosome body and therefore escapes many of the structural and topological rules that govern linear genomic regions. This distinction is biologically consequential because ecDNA does not simply represent “more copies” of the same genomic segment; rather, it constitutes a structurally independent DNA entity with distinct regulatory behavior.

One defining feature of ecDNA is the absence of centromeres. Because centromeres are required for conventional mitotic segregation, ecDNA is not inherited in the same orderly manner as chromosomal DNA [27, 28].Instead, ecDNA can segregate unevenly during cell division, producing substantial cell-to-cell variation in oncogene dosage and contributing to rapid intratumoral diversification. This non-Mendelian mode of inheritance provides a plausible explanation for how ecDNA-bearing tumors can generate phenotypic heterogeneity more rapidly than tumors driven solely by chromosomal amplification. In this sense, ecDNA is not only a structural genomic alteration but also a mechanism for dynamic variation under selective pressure.

Another important distinction is that ecDNA-associated oncogene expression is often disproportionately high relative to copy number. Pan-cancer analyses have shown that ecDNA amplifications are associated with markedly elevated oncogene transcription and worse clinical outcomes compared with chromosomal amplifications [13, 29]. This suggests that ecDNA provides a more permissive context for gene activation than linear chromosomal amplicons. The reason is likely multifactorial: ecDNA can concentrate oncogenes together with regulatory elements, adopt unusually accessible chromatin states, and occupy spatial environments that favor robust transcription. Thus, the biological impact of ecDNA cannot be reduced to gene dosage alone; its structural configuration appears to amplify the functional output of amplified loci.

Recent studies further support the view that ecDNA has properties that distinguish it from standard chromosomal amplification at both the genomic and spatial levels [30, 31]. van Leen and colleagues emphasized the genomic and spatial mobility of ecDNA and argued that its influence may extend beyond local oncogene dosage to broader disruption of gene regulatory programs [32]. Likewise, Lange and colleagues highlighted the evolutionary consequences of ecDNA, showing that ecDNA-mediated amplification promotes aggressive growth, drug resistance, and shorter survival in ways that are consistent with its capacity for unstable inheritance and rapid selection [33]. Together, these studies suggest that ecDNA should be understood as a highly plastic genomic unit whose independence from chromosome structure enables both regulatory flexibility and evolutionary advantage. The large-scale analysis by Bailey and colleagues further reinforced this shift in perspective. By examining 14,778 patients across 39 tumor types, they showed that ecDNA is present in 17.1% of tumor samples and is strongly associated with poor outcome and treatment resistance. These data support the idea that ecDNA is not a rare cytogenetic curiosity, but a recurrent and clinically meaningful genomic architecture in cancer. When viewed together with the earlier pan-cancer study by Kim and colleagues, these findings establish ecDNA as a major class of oncogenic amplification that differs from chromosomal amplification not only structurally, but also functionally and clinically [34].

Taken together, ecDNA differs from chromosomal amplification in at least four fundamental respects: it is structurally independent and frequently circular; it lacks centromeres and is therefore inherited unevenly; it can drive unusually high transcriptional output relative to copy number; and it confers a level of regulatory and evolutionary plasticity that conventional chromosomal amplicons do not readily achieve. These features make ecDNA uniquely suited to act as a dynamic substrate for malignant adaptation and, as discussed in later sections, a platform for epigenetic reprogramming. Mechanisms of ecDNA generation: micronuclei rupture, chromothripsis, replication stress, and aberrant repair

Current evidence indicates that ecDNA formation is not simply the result of gradual linear amplification, but can arise through multiple genome-disruptive routes, including chromothripsis, micronuclei rupture, replication-associated DNA damage, breakage–fusion–bridge-like cycles, and aberrant DNA repair responses [43–48]. In other words, ecDNA often emerges from a permissive genomic environment shaped by replication stress, chromosome fragmentation, aberrant repair, and large-scale structural rearrangement. This view places ecDNA formation within the broader context of genome instability rather than treating it as an isolated amplification event.

One of the most important recent advances in this area came from Engel and colleagues, who showed that the Fanconi anemia pathway can paradoxically promote chromothripsis and ecDNA formation [49]. In their study, pathological activation of this DNA repair pathway drove widespread chromosome fragmentation, generating complex genomic rearrangements and ecDNA that contributed to acquired resistance to anticancer therapy. This finding is conceptually significant because it reframes ecDNA biogenesis as an active consequence of abnormal genome maintenance. Rather than merely failing to protect the genome, dysregulated DNA repair machinery can directly generate the fragmented chromosomal substrates from which ecDNA arises. This mechanistic framework is consistent with the idea that chromothripsis is a fertile source of ecDNA. Chromothripsis produces extensive chromosome shattering and reassembly in a single or limited number of events, creating genomic fragments that can be aberrantly ligated, reinserted, lost, or circularized. ecDNA can therefore be understood as one possible structural outcome of catastrophic chromosome damage. Importantly, ecDNA formation is not restricted to canonical oncogene-containing loci. In principle, any genomic DNA fragment generated during catastrophic chromosome damage, micronuclei rupture, or aberrant repair may become incorporated into circular extrachromosomal structures [50, 51]. However, ecDNAs carrying oncogenes, enhancers, super-enhancer elements, or other fitness-promoting regulatory sequences are more likely to undergo positive selection and clonal retention during tumor evolution. Importantly, this model helps explain why ecDNA often contains complex rearrangements, multiple genomic fragments, and highly oncogenic sequence combinations [52]. A related conceptual point is that ecDNA formation should not be interpreted as a purposeful cellular attempt to remove oncogenic checkpoints, induce differentiation, or normalize the genome. Although ecDNA can carry drivers involved in RTK–RAS signaling, TP53 regulation, and cell-cycle control, the available evidence is more consistent with a non-teleological selection model. In this model, genome damage, chromosome mis-segregation, micronuclei rupture, and aberrant repair generate diverse circular DNA products, whereas ecDNAs carrying oncogenes, enhancers, or other fitness-promoting elements are preferentially retained and expanded during tumor evolution. Whole-genome doubling and polyploid intermediates may further increase the probability of ecDNA generation by creating a permissive background for chromosome mis-segregation, DNA bridges, micronucleation, and catastrophic rearrangement [53, 54]. The neosis-like cycle and polyploid giant cancer cell states have been proposed as routes through which stressed cancer cells undergo polyploidization followed by depolyploidization, thereby producing genetically diverse progeny. Although these processes should not be regarded as established ecDNA-specific mechanisms, they may increase the pool of damaged or mis-segregated chromosomal material from which circular extrachromosomal DNA can arise. Consistent with this possibility, large-scale cancer genome analyses have linked ecDNA presence with whole-genome duplication, structural-variant burden, and genome instability in a tumor-type-dependent manner [42]. Micronuclei-associated DNA processing provides another potential link between genome-damage clearance and ecDNA biogenesis. DNA trapped within micronuclei can undergo defective replication, nuclear-envelope rupture, DNA damage accumulation, and chromothripsis-like fragmentation. Ruptured micronuclei may also expose chromatin to cytosolic sensing and selective autophagy pathways, including cGAS-associated signaling and p62/SQSTM1-linked recognition of damaged micronuclear material. These pathways may eliminate some damaged DNA, but they may also coexist with the persistence, re-entry, or aberrant repair of DNA fragments that escape complete degradation. Therefore, micronuclear rupture and chromatin autophagy should be discussed as stress-associated DNA-processing events that may contribute indirectly to ecDNA formation, rather than as intentional mechanisms of genome normalization [55, 56]. It also explains why ecDNA is frequently associated with highly unstable tumor genomes and with selective contexts, such as treatment exposure, that intensify replication stress and DNA damage. Micronuclei rupture provides another important route through which fragmented chromosomal DNA may be converted into ecDNA. Lagging chromosomes or acentric chromosomal fragments can become enclosed within micronuclei during mitosis. Because micronuclei often undergo defective DNA replication, impaired DNA damage repair, and nuclear envelope rupture, the DNA trapped inside them is highly susceptible to fragmentation and chromothripsis-like rearrangement. After rupture, these fragments can re-enter the main nucleus and be processed by error-prone repair pathways, creating opportunities for circularization and the generation of extrachromosomal DNA molecules. This model is particularly relevant because it explains how ecDNA formation can be linked to mitotic errors, catastrophic DNA damage, and defective nuclear compartmentalization rather than only to gradual amplification of a pre-existing oncogene locus.

Saito-Adachi and colleagues provide complementary support for this broader view by mapping structural aberration patterns in gastric cancer genomes [57]. Although their study was not exclusively focused on ecDNA, it identified rearrangement signatures and locally clustered structural variants associated with extensive amplification processes, including patterns consistent with ecDNA involvement. This type of evidence is useful because it places ecDNA formation within a continuum of complex structural genome remodeling. It suggests that ecDNA does not arise in isolation, but is embedded within a larger landscape of rearrangement processes that reshape the cancer genome at multiple scales. Another major conceptual advance came from Pradella and colleagues, who developed a strategy to engineer large focal amplifications mediated by ecDNA in a spatiotemporally controlled manner [58]. Their work showed that engineered ecDNAs can accumulate spontaneously in primary cells, promote proliferation, immortalization, and transformation, and drive tumor formation in an autochthonous hepatocellular carcinoma model. This study is important for two reasons. First, it demonstrates that ecDNA can be generated experimentally rather than merely observed retrospectively in established cancers. Second, it provides direct evidence that ecDNA is not just a byproduct of tumorigenesis but can itself promote malignant transformation. In the context of ecDNA biogenesis, this finding argues that understanding how ecDNA forms is biologically important not only for reconstructing cancer evolution, but also for identifying an early and functionally relevant oncogenic process.

Taken together, these studies support a model in which ecDNA formation arises from the convergence of mitotic errors, micronuclei rupture, chromothripsis, replication-associated stress, and aberrant DNA repair. Catastrophic events generate fragmented chromosomal material; micronuclei rupture exposes mis-segregated DNA to further damage and rearrangement; dysregulated repair pathways, including the Fanconi anemia pathway under pathological conditions, promote abnormal processing of these fragments; and structurally permissive contexts allow some DNA fragments to circularize and persist as ecDNA. Importantly, the initial DNA cargo of ecDNA may be diverse and not necessarily limited to oncogenes. During tumor evolution, however, ecDNA molecules that carry oncogenes, enhancer elements, or other regulatory sequences capable of increasing cellular fitness are preferentially retained and expanded. Once formed, these ecDNA elements can become potent oncogenic units that amplify cancer drivers and reconfigure chromatin and transcriptional states in ways that further accelerate tumor evolution.

In this light, ecDNA formation should be viewed as both a consequence and an extension of genome instability. This framework is also consistent with longitudinal evidence from Barrett’s oesophagus, in which ecDNA was detected before or at the diagnosis of oesophageal adenocarcinoma and increased in copy number and structural complexity with disease progression. It reflects a cancer cell’s failure to preserve chromosomal integrity, but it also creates a new structural substrate for oncogenic selection. This dual identity is central to the biology of ecDNA: genome instability gives rise to ecDNA, and ecDNA in turn transforms that instability into a durable regulatory and evolutionary advantage [59]. ecDNA replication, maintenance, and replication stress

In addition to its formation, the replication of ecDNA has emerged as an important determinant of ecDNA persistence and vulnerability. Unlike chromosomal DNA, which replicates within a highly organized chromosomal context, ecDNA exists as an extrachromosomal circular element and may experience distinct constraints in origin usage, replication timing, copy-number control, and DNA damage surveillance. This distinction is biologically important because ecDNA-positive cells must not only generate ecDNA but also duplicate and maintain high-copy extrachromosomal DNA molecules across repeated cell divisions [60, 61]. Recent studies suggest that ecDNA replication is closely linked to replication stress and DNA damage responses. Kang and colleagues showed that ecDNA replication and maintenance are coupled with DNA damage pathway activity in tumors, supporting the idea that ecDNA persistence depends on replication-associated repair and stress-response programs [29]. This finding expands the view of ecDNA from a static amplification structure to a replicating genomic entity whose propagation requires active coordination with DNA damage responses. In parallel, Jaworski and colleagues reported that ecDNA replication is disorganized and vulnerable to replication stress, further indicating that ecDNA-bearing cells may rely on stress-buffering mechanisms to sustain extrachromosomal DNA propagation [43]. These observations refine the relationship between ecDNA and replication stress. Replication stress in ecDNA-positive tumors should not be understood solely as a downstream consequence of transcription–replication conflict caused by hyperactive oncogene transcription. It may also arise from the intrinsic replication behavior of ecDNA itself, including disorganized replication, high-copy extrachromosomal template burden, and dependence on DNA repair pathways for maintenance. Thus, ecDNA replication represents both a mechanism of ecDNA persistence and a potential source of therapeutic vulnerability.

Conceptually, this places ecDNA at the intersection of three processes: genome instability generates ecDNA, replication-associated programs maintain ecDNA, and transcriptional or replicative stress exposes liabilities in ecDNA-positive cancer cells. This framework helps explain why ecDNA can function as a durable regulatory platform rather than a transient byproduct of chromosomal damage.

ecDNA as an epigenetically active chromatin unit

Having established that ecDNA differs from chromosomal amplification in structure, inheritance, and evolutionary behavior, this section focuses specifically on its chromatin-level regulatory features. Three mechanisms are emphasized: increased chromatin accessibility, enhancer rewiring, and activation of noncanonical regulatory elements [62, 63]. These observations collectively support the view that ecDNA is not merely amplified DNA, but an epigenetically active chromatin unit that can reshape gene regulation in cancer. This perspective is important because it reframes ecDNA from a structural abnormality into a regulatory platform. Unlike chromosomal amplicons, which remain embedded in relatively fixed topological and epigenetic environments, ecDNA is structurally independent and can adopt regulatory states that are more dynamic, more accessible, and more favorable to oncogenic transcription. In this sense, ecDNA functions not only as a carrier of oncogenes, but also as a substrate for regulatory innovation [64]. Its contribution to cancer therefore depends not only on what sequences it contains, but also on how those sequences are organized, accessed, and transcriptionally engaged. Open chromatin and transcription-permissive states on ecDNA

One of the clearest indications that ecDNA exerts regulatory effects beyond copy-number amplification is the observation that ecDNA-associated oncogenes are often transcribed more strongly than equally amplified genes residing on chromosomes [75–77]. This discrepancy suggests that ecDNA provides a distinct chromatin environment that enhances transcriptional competence. Indeed, recent work indicates that ecDNA is frequently characterized by elevated chromatin accessibility and a transcription-permissive state, helping explain why ecDNA-driven oncogene expression is often disproportionately high relative to DNA abundance.

Purshouse and colleagues provided particularly important evidence for this concept by showing that oncogene expression from ecDNA is driven by hijacked chromatin accessibility [37]. Their work demonstrated that ecDNA does not simply increase the number of oncogene copies available for transcription, but instead creates a regulatory context in which oncogene-containing regions become more accessible and therefore more efficiently transcribed. This finding is conceptually significant because it shifts the explanatory model from one based on gene dosage alone to one based on epigenetic activation. In other words, ecDNA-associated oncogene expression cannot be understood merely as a quantitative effect of amplification; it also reflects a qualitative shift in chromatin state.

This view is further supported by the broader conceptual framework proposed by van Leen and colleagues, who emphasized that the genomic and spatial mobility of ecDNA may influence gene regulation in ways that extend beyond focal amplification [32]. ecDNA is physically liberated from the chromosomal environment in which a genomic segment originally resided, and this liberation may allow it to access nuclear regions that are more favorable to transcription or to escape local repressive influences that would otherwise constrain gene activity. Although the precise mechanisms vary across tumor contexts, the broader implication is that ecDNA can alter the relationship between sequence, structure, and expression in a manner that is fundamentally different from chromosomal amplification [78–82]. The pan-cancer analysis by Bailey and colleagues also reinforces this perspective. Their large-scale study established ecDNA as a recurrent and clinically relevant genomic architecture in cancer and highlighted its association with aggressive biology and poor outcome [42]. Importantly, these clinical associations are difficult to explain by copy number alone. If ecDNA were simply an alternative packaging form of amplified DNA, one might expect it to behave similarly to high-copy chromosomal amplicons. Instead, ecDNA-positive tumors often display disproportionately aggressive phenotypes, suggesting that ecDNA carries a functional advantage that likely includes a permissive chromatin context and enhanced transcriptional capacity.

Taken together, these findings support a model in which ecDNA serves as a privileged transcriptional substrate. Its architecture appears to favor chromatin openness, reduce constraints imposed by the linear chromosome, and amplify the transcriptional consequences of oncogene amplification. In this framework, hijacked chromatin accessibility is not a secondary feature of ecDNA biology, but a defining property of ecDNA-driven gene activation. This concept also provides the mechanistic bridge to the next level of regulation: if ecDNA is unusually accessible, it becomes especially capable of engaging enhancers, reorganizing regulatory contacts, and driving new forms of oncogenic control.

Enhancer hijacking and enhancer rewiring on ecDNA

Beyond chromatin accessibility, ecDNA can reorganize enhancer–oncogene relationships. During ecDNA formation and evolution, oncogenes may be circularized together with nearby or distal regulatory elements, creating enhancer–promoter configurations that differ from those in the linear genome [83, 84]. A useful way to conceptualize this process is in three steps. First, ecDNA can capture pre-existing enhancers. During ecDNA formation, genomic fragments containing oncogenes may be excised together with nearby or even more distant regulatory elements. Once circularized, these elements can remain physically linked in a new structural configuration, preserving or even intensifying their regulatory influence [49, 85]. Second, ecDNA can rewire enhancer–oncogene communication. Because the linear genomic context is broken and reassembled in circular form, enhancer–promoter relationships can be redefined, allowing oncogenes to come under the control of regulatory elements that they would not normally contact on chromosomes. Third, ecDNA may generate entirely new regulatory activity by placing sequences into a chromatin environment that enables latent or noncanonical elements to function as enhancers. An additional layer of regulatory remodeling can occur when circular DNA fragments reintegrate into the linear genome. Koche and colleagues showed in neuroblastoma that extrachromosomal circular DNA can undergo chimeric circularization and fragmented reinsertion into chromosomes, generating circle-derived rearrangements that affect the expression of cancer-relevant genes on linear chromosomes [86]. This mechanism is distinct from classical homogeneous staining regions and indicates that circular DNA can reshape gene regulation not only while remaining extrachromosomal, but also after partial reintegration into chromosomal DNA. Together, these mechanisms illustrate how ecDNA acts as a platform for regulatory rewiring.

Chapman and colleagues provided a particularly important example of this principle in medulloblastoma. Their study showed that ecDNA is associated with poor outcome and frequent relapse, but beyond its clinical significance, it also demonstrated that ecDNA can undergo extensive enhancer rewiring [38]. In this setting, ecDNA was not simply a passive structure carrying amplified oncogenes; rather, it reorganized the regulatory landscape in ways that likely promoted sustained oncogenic transcription. This work is highly relevant to the current review because it directly supports the idea that ecDNA contributes to malignant progression by reconfiguring regulatory logic. More recently, Chowdhury and colleagues further advanced this concept by reconstructing the three-dimensional architecture of ecDNA and showing that its circular topology can reorganize chromatin contacts. This is important because enhancer rewiring is not only a matter of linear sequence juxtaposition; it is also a question of three-dimensional regulatory communication. ecDNA enables regulatory elements and target genes to occupy new topological relationships, potentially generating high-efficiency enhancer–oncogene interactions that are impossible or unfavorable in the linear genome. Thus, ecDNA should be viewed not simply as carrying enhancers, but as creating a new structural framework in which enhancer function is redefined.

Taghbalout and colleagues complement this view from the perspective of nuclear organization. Their work showed that ecDNA associates with nuclear condensates and can promote oncogenic transcription through reorganized chromatin structure [41]. This finding is especially useful in the context of enhancer rewiring because it suggests that enhancer function on ecDNA may be reinforced not only by structural proximity but also by coalescence into highly active transcriptional environments. In other words, ecDNA may not merely reposition enhancers; it may also bring them into regulatory assemblies that magnify their effect on gene expression. Purshouse and colleagues, although focused more directly on chromatin accessibility, also contribute to this discussion by reinforcing the idea that ecDNA-mediated transcriptional activation depends on a permissive epigenetic context [37]. Enhancer hijacking and chromatin accessibility should therefore not be viewed as separate processes. Rather, they are mechanistically intertwined: ecDNA becomes more accessible, that accessibility facilitates enhancer engagement, and enhancer rewiring then helps sustain or escalate oncogenic transcription.

From the perspective of this review, enhancer hijacking and enhancer rewiring represent one of the clearest manifestations of ecDNA-driven epigenetic reprogramming. They show that ecDNA is not merely a quantitative amplifier of pre-existing oncogenic programs, but a qualitative re-architect of gene regulation. This is also one of the most important points that distinguishes an ecDNA-centered regulatory review from a conventional summary of ecDNA-associated amplifications [14, 69, 87]. The key question is no longer simply which genes are amplified on ecDNA, but how ecDNA reconstructs the enhancer logic that governs their expression. Importantly, ecDNA-driven enhancer rewiring should be viewed as a dynamic and reversible process rather than a uniformly activating mechanism. Brückner and colleagues reported that oncogene-containing ecDNA can undergo micronucleation, followed by suppressive chromatin remodeling and reduced oncogene transcription. This finding introduces an important negative-feedback layer: the same extrachromosomal architecture that enables high-level oncogene expression may also be vulnerable to mitotic mis-segregation, micronuclear sequestration, and transcriptional silencing. Therefore, ecDNA-dependent epigenetic regulation should be discussed not only in terms of enhancer activation and transcriptional escalation, but also in terms of mechanisms that constrain ecDNA-driven oncogene output.

ecDNA and the emergence of noncanonical regulatory elements

An especially exciting recent development in the field is the recognition that ecDNA may do more than capture existing enhancers: it may also enable the emergence of noncanonical regulatory elements. This idea considerably broadens the significance of ecDNA in cancer biology [39, 50, 88, 89].Rather than serving only as a vehicle for preformed oncogene–enhancer combinations, ecDNA may function as a permissive chromatin environment in which sequences that are normally silent, weakly active, or non-regulatory acquire enhancer-like behavior.

Kraft and colleagues provided a major advance in this area by showing that transposable elements can contribute to enhancer activation on ecDNA [39]. In MYC-amplified colorectal cancer, their work suggested that ecDNA can exploit transposable element-derived sequences as functional regulatory components, effectively converting genomic material that would not traditionally be considered a canonical enhancer into part of an active oncogenic regulatory circuit. This finding is particularly important because it expands the concept of enhancer rewiring into one of regulatory innovation. ecDNA does not only rearrange known regulatory elements; it may create opportunities for new regulatory logic to emerge. This concept has several implications. First, it suggests that ecDNA-associated chromatin states are sufficiently permissive to unlock latent regulatory potential in the genome. Second, it indicates that ecDNA may contribute to oncogenic transcription not only by preserving favorable regulatory interactions, but also by generating novel ones. Third, it raises the possibility that the regulatory repertoire of ecDNA is more diverse than previously appreciated, incorporating repetitive elements, transposable sequences, and perhaps other classes of noncanonical regulatory DNA into functional oncogenic circuits.

The structural perspective provided by Chowdhury and colleagues helps reinforce this idea [40]. If ecDNA reorganizes three-dimensional architecture and creates new topological relationships, then the activation of noncanonical elements may be facilitated not only by local chromatin accessibility but also by their incorporation into new regulatory contact networks. Likewise, the findings of Taghbalout and colleagues suggest that once such elements become active, they may participate in ecDNA-associated transcriptional hubs or condensate-like environments that further potentiate their function [41]. In this framework, the emergence of noncanonical regulatory elements is not an isolated epigenetic oddity, but part of a broader reorganization of regulatory space on ecDNA. This aspect of ecDNA biology is particularly valuable for a forward-looking review because it pushes the field beyond the now-familiar concept of enhancer hijacking. Enhancer hijacking implies that ecDNA borrows established regulatory elements. Regulatory innovation on ecDNA, by contrast, implies that ecDNA can generate new regulatory activity from sequences not previously recognized as major enhancers [50, 88, 89]. This distinction matters because it suggests that ecDNA can increase the adaptive capacity of cancer cells not only by amplifying existing oncogenic pathways, but also by expanding the range of regulatory mechanisms available to support those pathways.

Taken together, these findings support a broader model in which ecDNA acts as an epigenetically active and regulatory generative unit. It can promote chromatin accessibility, capture and rewire enhancer relationships, and create conditions in which noncanonical sequences become functional regulatory elements. These properties make ecDNA uniquely capable of driving transcriptional plasticity in cancer. More importantly, they position ecDNA as a central interface between genome rearrangement and epigenetic innovation, thereby explaining why its impact on tumor biology extends so far beyond simple amplification. Three-dimensional rewiring and nuclear architecture of ecDNA

The regulatory consequences of ecDNA cannot be fully understood by considering sequence composition or copy number alone. Once oncogenic loci are excised from linear chromosomes and reassembled as extrachromosomal circles, their regulatory behavior is no longer constrained by the conventional architectural rules of the chromosomal genome [1, 90]. In this sense, ecDNA introduces not only a structural alteration in DNA localization, but also a profound reorganization of regulatory topology. Its circular conformation, extrachromosomal existence, and dynamic spatial behavior create opportunities for new chromatin contacts, altered enhancer–promoter communication, and concentrated transcriptional activity within the nucleus [91].This shift from linear chromosomal organization to ecDNA-centered spatial regulation is highly relevant to the concept of epigenetic reprogramming. On chromosomes, gene regulation is shaped by topologically associating domains, boundary elements, chromatin compartments, and the spatial segregation of active and repressed regions. ecDNA can partially bypass or reconfigure these constraints. As a result, oncogenes carried on ecDNA may enter new topological contexts, form previously inaccessible regulatory interactions, and become incorporated into nuclear assemblies that amplify transcriptional output. Thus, ecDNA should be considered not merely as an amplified DNA circle, but as a structural unit capable of reorganizing three-dimensional regulatory space.

ecDNA reshapes three-dimensional chromatin interactions

A key feature that distinguishes ecDNA from chromosomal amplification is that ecDNA changes the topology of the genome itself [92–94]. Linear chromosomes impose physical limits on which regulatory elements can interact with a given gene, because promoter–enhancer communication is constrained by genomic distance, topological domain boundaries, and the broader folding logic of chromatin. By contrast, ecDNA is circular and structurally independent, allowing genomic fragments that were previously distant, insulated, or even unrelated in the linear genome to be repositioned within the same extrachromosomal unit. This circular topology creates the potential for new regulatory contact networks that cannot be readily achieved on chromosomes.

Chowdhury and colleagues provided an especially important advance in this area by reconstructing the three-dimensional architecture of ecDNA and demonstrating that ecDNA reshapes chromatin interactions in ways that are distinct from linear amplicons [40]. Their work showed that the circular organization of ecDNA is not a passive geometric feature, but a regulatory determinant that can reorganize chromatin contact patterns and alter the relationship between oncogenes and regulatory elements. This is mechanistically important because it establishes that ecDNA changes not only what sequences are amplified, but also how those sequences are spatially connected. In other words, ecDNA alters not only sequence dosage but also regulatory topology. This concept is consistent with the broader framework proposed by van Leen and colleagues, who emphasized the genomic and spatial mobility of ecDNA [32]. Their work suggests that ecDNA should be viewed as a dynamic genomic structure capable of moving through nuclear space and engaging regulatory environments that differ from those of chromosomal loci. Such mobility may allow ecDNA-containing oncogenes to escape unfavorable local chromatin contexts and instead participate in more active transcriptional neighborhoods. Although not every detail of these interactions has been resolved, the central principle is clear: ecDNA introduces a level of spatial regulatory freedom that linear chromosomal amplifications do not possess. Chapman and colleagues further support this model through their work in medulloblastoma, where ecDNA was associated with enhancer rewiring and aggressive disease behavior [38]. Although their study focused primarily on the functional consequences of rewired regulatory logic, it also implies an underlying topological reorganization. Enhancer rewiring on ecDNA is not merely a linear rearrangement of sequences; it depends on the establishment of new spatial relationships between regulatory elements and their target oncogenes. Thus, studies of enhancer hijacking and studies of 3D architecture are not separate lines of evidence, but complementary views of the same phenomenon: ecDNA generates new regulatory topologies that sustain oncogenic transcription [91, 95].

Taken together, these findings indicate that ecDNA redefines chromatin interaction landscapes at multiple levels. First, circularization can compress or juxtapose fragments that were previously separated by large genomic distances. Second, ecDNA can bring oncogenes into sustained proximity with powerful regulatory elements. Third, because ecDNA is no longer embedded within the original chromosomal domain structure, it may evade topological boundaries that would ordinarily restrict enhancer communication. The consequence is a form of three-dimensional rewiring in which oncogene regulation becomes less dependent on native chromosomal architecture and more dependent on the newly constructed topology of ecDNA [96–101]. This topological perspective is essential for understanding why ecDNA has such strong regulatory effects. If ecDNA simply amplified genes without altering chromatin interactions, then its transcriptional output might be explained largely by copy number. Instead, the evidence suggests that circular topology enables regulatory contacts that are constrained on linear chromosomes, thereby magnifying the functional consequences of amplification. For this reason, three-dimensional rewiring should be regarded as a core mechanism by which ecDNA drives epigenetic reprogramming in cancer.

Nuclear hubs, ecDNA clustering, and transcriptional condensates

Beyond reshaping local chromatin interactions, ecDNA also appears to organize itself within the nucleus in ways that enhance transcriptional activity [44, 102, 103]. A particularly important concept emerging from recent studies is that ecDNA does not behave as a collection of isolated circles scattered randomly through nuclear space. Rather, ecDNA can form spatial clusters or hubs, where multiple ecDNA molecules converge into concentrated regulatory assemblies [104].These hubs provide a structural basis for intensified transcriptional output and may represent an important mechanism through which ecDNA amplifies oncogenic signaling beyond the effects of gene dosage alone.

Yi and colleagues provided one of the earliest and most influential demonstrations of this idea by using live-cell imaging to show the existence of transcriptionally active ecDNA hubs [35]. Their study revealed that ecDNA molecules can cluster together within the nucleus and that these hubs are associated with enhanced transcriptional activity. This finding was highly significant because it shifted the field away from viewing ecDNA as merely amplified DNA fragments and toward understanding ecDNA as a spatially organized regulatory entity. In this framework, the biological importance of ecDNA lies not only in how many copies are present, but also in how those copies are arranged and engaged within nuclear space. The concept of ecDNA clustering was further developed by Taghbalout and colleagues, who showed that ecDNA associates with nuclear condensates and contributes to oncogenic transcription through reconfigured chromatin organization [41]. This work is especially important because it connects ecDNA biology with the broader field of transcriptional condensates and nuclear compartmentalization. Regulatory proteins such as MED1 and BRD4 are known to accumulate in highly active transcriptional assemblies, and the association of ecDNA with such condensate-like environments suggests that ecDNA may exploit pre-existing transcriptional machinery or even help stabilize these active nuclear domains [105]. In this way, ecDNA can be understood not only as a structural carrier of oncogenes, but also as an organizer of transcriptionally favorable nuclear microenvironments.

The implications of this model are substantial. If ecDNA molecules cluster into hubs and interact with condensate-associated cofactors, then oncogene activation becomes a collective spatial phenomenon rather than a purely locus-specific event. Multiple ecDNA circles may cooperate within the same nuclear territory, concentrating transcriptional activators, enhancer-associated machinery, and RNA polymerase components in a way that strengthens gene expression [31, 106–108]. This provides a plausible explanation for why ecDNA-associated oncogenes can achieve exceptionally high expression levels even beyond those predicted by copy number and chromatin accessibility alone. van Leen and colleagues provide a useful conceptual bridge here as well, because their emphasis on ecDNA mobility and spatial behavior supports the idea that ecDNA is not statically confined, but dynamically engages nuclear architecture. Likewise, the structural findings of Chowdhury and colleagues help explain why ecDNA clustering may be functionally potent: if each ecDNA molecule already contains reconfigured regulatory contacts, then clustering multiple such molecules in the same nuclear microenvironment could further intensify transcriptional output. Thus, three-dimensional rewiring at the level of individual ecDNA circles and nuclear clustering at the level of ecDNA assemblies may act together rather than independently.

From a mechanistic perspective, nuclear hubs and transcriptional condensates likely represent a higher-order layer of ecDNA-mediated regulation. The first layer is structural excision and circularization. The second is the rewiring of local chromatin contacts within ecDNA. The third is the spatial organization of ecDNA into nuclear assemblies that enhance transcriptional efficiency. Seen in this way, ecDNA progressively escalates oncogenic regulation from sequence amplification to chromatin reconfiguration to nuclear-scale coordination. This hierarchical model also reinforces one of the central arguments of the present review: ecDNA functions as a platform for epigenetic reprogramming because it reorganizes gene regulation across multiple spatial scales [102, 109–111]. It changes which sequences are linked, how they interact, and where they are positioned within the nucleus. Nuclear clustering and condensate association are therefore not secondary curiosities of ecDNA biology, but central mechanisms through which ecDNA converts structural amplification into amplified regulatory activity.

Taken together, current evidence supports the view that ecDNA acts not as isolated circles, but as spatially organized regulatory assemblies. By forming hubs and associating with transcriptional condensates, ecDNA can amplify oncogenic transcription beyond structural amplification alone. This emerging model places nuclear architecture at the center of ecDNA biology and provides an important conceptual bridge between genome structure, epigenetic regulation, and tumor cell plasticity. ecDNA as a driver of transcriptional plasticity and oncogenic addiction

The preceding sections establish that ecDNA is structurally distinct from chromosomal amplification, epigenetically permissive, and capable of reshaping regulatory architecture in three-dimensional nuclear space. The next question is functional: what does this distinctive regulatory organization allow cancer cells to do? A growing body of evidence suggests that ecDNA provides two interrelated advantages. First, it supports transcriptional plasticity, allowing cancer cells to rapidly adjust the magnitude and configuration of oncogenic transcriptional programs [112–117]. Second, over time, these same ecDNA-dependent regulatory states can mature into forms of oncogenic addiction, in which tumor survival and progression become increasingly dependent on the transcriptional circuitry maintained by ecDNA. This dual role is biologically important. Plasticity enables adaptation under changing conditions, including microenvironmental stress and therapeutic pressure [51, 117–121].Addiction, by contrast, reflects the consolidation of that flexibility into a stable and essential oncogenic state. In this way, ecDNA can be viewed as both a source of regulatory adaptability and a platform for the emergence of deep transcriptional dependency. This logic closely parallels the conceptual framework of super-enhancer biology and transcriptional addiction: an initially permissive and hyperactive regulatory architecture may ultimately become indispensable for maintaining malignant identity.

Amplification is not enough: ecDNA-driven transcriptional escalation

One of the most important conceptual advances in ecDNA biology is the recognition that amplification alone is not sufficient to explain the intensity of oncogene expression observed in ecDNA-positive cancers. If copy number were the only relevant determinant, then highly amplified chromosomal loci and highly amplified ecDNA loci would be expected to generate similar transcriptional outputs. Yet multiple studies indicate that ecDNA-bearing oncogenes are often expressed at disproportionately high levels, suggesting that ecDNA enables a form of transcriptional escalation that exceeds the simple arithmetic of dosage [122–125].

Purshouse and colleagues provide a central mechanistic explanation for this phenomenon. Their work showed that oncogene expression from ecDNA is driven by hijacked chromatin accessibility, indicating that ecDNA-associated loci are not merely more abundant, but more transcriptionally available [37]. This finding is critical because it introduces a qualitative dimension to ecDNA-driven amplification: ecDNA creates a chromatin environment in which amplified genes are more effectively engaged by the transcriptional machinery. In this model, copy number provides the substrate, but chromatin accessibility determines the functional intensity of output.

Taghbalout and colleagues extend this concept by demonstrating that ecDNA participates in nuclear condensates containing the MED1 transcriptional co-activator and that ecDNA-specific regulatory modules are required for condensate formation and target transcription [41]. Their study suggests that ecDNA-driven transcriptional escalation is not only a matter of open chromatin, but also of higher-order transcriptional organization. When ecDNA becomes integrated into condensate-associated regulatory assemblies, amplified oncogenes can access concentrated pools of co-activators and transcriptional machinery, thereby producing levels of expression that are difficult to achieve through structural amplification alone. This is a crucial insight because it shows that ecDNA organizes transcription spatially as well as genetically.

The work of Tang and colleagues adds an important functional and therapeutic dimension to this argument. They showed that ecDNA-positive cancers are especially vulnerable to enhanced transcription–replication conflict, indicating that ecDNA-associated transcription is not merely strong, but excessively strong enough to create stress liabilities [69]. This observation is highly informative mechanistically. It implies that ecDNA-driven transcriptional escalation pushes cancer cells toward a boundary condition in which the machinery needed to sustain extreme oncogene expression becomes a source of replication stress and vulnerability. In other words, ecDNA does not simply increase transcriptional output; it can drive transcription into a pathologically intensified state with selective consequences. The large pan-cancer studies by Kim and by Bailey provide the broader biological context for these mechanistic findings [34, 42]. Both studies showed that ecDNA is associated with aggressive tumor behavior and poor clinical outcome across diverse cancer types, and such associations are likely to reflect more than the presence of amplified oncogenes per se. The most plausible interpretation is that ecDNA confers a regulatory advantage: it packages amplified oncogenes into a structural and chromatin context optimized for high-level transcription. From this perspective, ecDNA-driven malignancy is not just a consequence of “more DNA,” but of “better organized oncogenic transcription.”

Taken together, these findings support a clear conclusion: amplification is necessary but not sufficient to explain the oncogenic potency of ecDNA. The true selective advantage of ecDNA lies in its ability to couple amplification to permissive chromatin accessibility, spatially concentrated transcriptional machinery, and hyperactive gene expression programs. ecDNA therefore acts as an engine of transcriptional escalation, transforming structural amplification into amplified regulatory output.

From transcriptional plasticity to oncogenic addiction

If ecDNA-driven transcriptional escalation explains how ecDNA intensifies oncogenic output, the next question is how this output shapes tumor evolution over time. A useful way to understand this process is to distinguish between plasticity and addiction. Early in tumor evolution or under fluctuating selective conditions, ecDNA offers flexibility: its dynamic inheritance, variable abundance, and rewired regulatory architecture allow cancer cells to explore multiple transcriptional states [126, 127]. However, as selection proceeds, some of these ecDNA-dependent states may prove especially advantageous, become stabilized, and eventually form the basis of a new oncogenic dependency. In this sense, ecDNA initially provides adaptive flexibility, but sustained ecDNA-dependent regulatory programs can evolve into oncogenic addiction.

Lange and colleagues are particularly important for this transition from flexibility to dependency [33]. Their work on the evolutionary dynamics of ecDNA in human cancers emphasizes that ecDNA contributes to somatic variation and selection in ways that promote aggressive growth, drug resistance, and poor survival. This evolutionary framework is highly relevant because it suggests that ecDNA is not merely a fixed driver event, but a mechanism through which tumors continually generate transcriptionally and genetically distinct subpopulations. Over time, however, selective pressure can favor certain ecDNA-associated oncogenic programs, allowing a once-flexible regulatory system to harden into a dominant survival strategy.

Chapman and colleagues provide a complementary example in medulloblastoma, where ecDNA is associated with poor prognosis, relapse, and enhancer rewiring [38]. Their findings imply that ecDNA can sustain powerful oncogenic transcriptional programs through reconfigured regulatory logic. Importantly, such rewired programs are unlikely to be biologically neutral. Once tumor cells become organized around ecDNA-dependent enhancer–oncogene interactions, they may become increasingly reliant on those interactions to preserve proliferation, survival, and malignant identity [128–132]. This is precisely the kind of transition that defines oncogenic addiction: what begins as an advantageous regulatory innovation becomes an essential operating system for the cancer cell.

Taghbalout and colleagues strengthen this argument by showing that ecDNA-specific regulatory modules are required for condensate formation and target transcription, and that disrupting these assemblies suppresses oncogenic transcription and promotes cell death [41]. These findings suggest that some ecDNA-associated transcriptional states are not merely permissive but obligatory. Once ecDNA-dependent hubs and condensates are established, the tumor may become dependent on the maintenance of those structures and the transcriptional circuitry they support. This observation links ecDNA biology directly to the broader concept of transcriptional addiction, in which malignant cells become exceptionally reliant on hyperactive regulatory nodes.

The study by Pradella and colleagues adds an important causal dimension. By engineering ecDNA-mediated focal amplifications, they showed that ecDNA can promote proliferation, immortalization, transformation, and tumorigenesis [58]. This work is highly relevant to the question of addiction because it indicates that ecDNA is not only associated with advanced cancers but can actively create oncogenic states that tumors subsequently rely upon. In other words, ecDNA is capable of initiating regulatory programs that later become essential. This observation helps close the conceptual loop between ecDNA as a generator of plasticity and ecDNA as a stabilizer of malignant dependency. From a broader conceptual standpoint, the transition from ecDNA-driven plasticity to ecDNA-driven addiction closely resembles the logic of super-enhancer-dependent cancers. Super-enhancers initially emerge as highly active regulatory assemblies that intensify lineage-defining or oncogenic transcription. Over time, however, the resulting transcriptional circuitry can become indispensable, rendering tumor cells exquisitely sensitive to disruption of the underlying regulatory architecture. ecDNA appears to operate according to a related principle, except that its regulatory flexibility is enhanced by its structural independence, noncanonical inheritance, and capacity for enhancer rewiring and nuclear clustering. This may make ecDNA an especially powerful substrate for the evolution of transcriptional addiction.

Taken together, current evidence supports a staged model of ecDNA function. At first, ecDNA expands the adaptive landscape of cancer cells by increasing regulatory flexibility, transcriptional diversity, and responsiveness to selection. As tumor evolution proceeds, ecDNA-dependent regulatory programs that confer the greatest fitness advantage are retained and reinforced. Eventually, these programs can become so central to malignant fitness that the tumor enters a state of oncogenic addiction, in which disruption of ecDNA-associated transcriptional organization leads to profound loss of viability. In this framework, ecDNA is not only a driver of transcriptional plasticity but also a cradle of new transcriptional dependencies in cancer. ecDNA, intratumoral heterogeneity, and tumor evolution

A defining feature of ecDNA biology is that it links structural genome alteration to evolutionary flexibility. Whereas chromosomal amplifications are propagated through relatively stable chromosome-based inheritance, ecDNA follows distinct rules of segregation, maintenance, and selection. This difference has profound consequences for tumor evolution. By varying in copy number across daughter cells, redistributing across subclones, and dynamically responding to environmental or therapeutic stress, ecDNA can accelerate the generation of intratumoral heterogeneity and expand the adaptive landscape available to cancer cells. In this sense, ecDNA is not only a marker of aggressive disease, but also a mechanism through which tumors diversify, evolve, and survive selective pressure [133–136]. This evolutionary role is especially important because tumor progression depends not simply on the presence of oncogenic drivers, but on the ability of cancer cells to generate variation on which selection can act. ecDNA appears particularly well suited to support this process. Its acentromeric nature permits unequal inheritance, its circular structure facilitates regulatory rewiring, and its abundance can fluctuate rapidly across cell populations. Together, these features allow ecDNA-positive tumors to generate phenotypic diversity more quickly than would be expected from chromosome-bound alterations alone. Recent studies using live-cell imaging, single-cell multi-omics, and spatial analysis now provide direct evidence that ecDNA contributes to cellular diversification, clonal branching, and treatment-adaptive evolution across multiple tumor contexts [35].

Unequal segregation and rapid generation of cellular diversity

One of the most fundamental biological reasons why ecDNA can accelerate tumor evolution is that it does not segregate through canonical centromere-guided mitosis. Because ecDNA lacks centromeres, its inheritance is not tightly coupled to chromosome segregation. Instead, ecDNA can be unevenly distributed between daughter cells during mitosis, creating immediate differences in oncogene dosage and, consequently, in downstream transcriptional output [103, 136]. This property gives ecDNA-bearing tumors a direct mechanism for generating cell-to-cell variation without requiring additional rounds of mutation or chromosome-level change. Yi and colleagues provided especially important evidence for this principle by directly tracking ecDNA in live cells [72]. Their study demonstrated that ecDNA segregates unevenly and randomly during cell division and that this process is associated with the formation of transcriptionally active ecDNA hubs. This work was conceptually transformative because it offered direct visualization of a mechanism by which ecDNA can generate rapid heterogeneity at the single-cell level. In practical terms, unequal segregation means that daughter cells can inherit markedly different ecDNA burdens and therefore diverge quickly in oncogenic state, even if they arise from the same immediate ancestor.

Lange and colleagues extended this concept into an explicitly evolutionary framework. They argued that ecDNA introduces a nonchromosomal mode of oncogene inheritance that reshapes somatic variation and selection in human cancers [33]. In their model, ecDNA does not simply amplify existing drivers; it increases the speed at which heritable phenotypic diversity can emerge, thereby enhancing the ability of tumors to explore multiple adaptive solutions. This is highly relevant to intratumoral heterogeneity because it means that ecDNA-bearing tumors can generate selectable diversity not only through mutational accumulation, but also through dynamic changes in ecDNA copy number and distribution. An important refinement to the unequal segregation model came from Hung and colleagues, who showed that ecDNA inheritance is not always entirely independent or random at the level of multiple ecDNA species [65]. Their study demonstrated that cooperative ecDNA species can undergo coordinated inheritance through mitotic co-segregation, maintaining correlated copy-number relationships across daughter cells. This finding adds an important nuance. ecDNA can generate diversity through uneven segregation, but it can also preserve advantageous ecDNA combinations when multiple ecDNA species cooperate functionally. Thus, ecDNA biology supports both diversification and coordinated retention, allowing tumors to balance exploratory variability with preservation of high-fitness oncogenic configurations.

Taken together, these findings explain why ecDNA can produce adaptive potential more rapidly than traditional chromosomal amplification. Chromosome-based amplifications are constrained by stable inheritance and slower structural evolution, whereas ecDNA can vary sharply across daughter cells in a single mitosis. At the same time, when cooperation between ecDNA species is advantageous, coordinated inheritance can preserve those beneficial combinations. This dual capacity for rapid diversification and selective stabilization makes ecDNA a uniquely powerful generator of intratumoral heterogeneity.

Single-cell and spatial evidence for ecDNA-driven tumor evolution

The idea that ecDNA promotes tumor evolution was initially inferred from bulk genomics and live-cell imaging, but recent technological advances have made it possible to examine ecDNA directly at single-cell and spatial resolution. This has significantly deepened the field. Instead of viewing ecDNA as a tumor-wide feature with averaged effects, researchers can now ask which cells carry ecDNA, how ecDNA correlates with transcriptional state, how ecDNA-positive populations are distributed across tumor space, and how these properties change over time [4, 22]. This methodological shift has transformed ecDNA from a structural observation into a tractable evolutionary variable.

A useful way to understand this progression is as a sequence of technological advances that progressively sharpened the evolutionary picture. Live-cell imaging first established that ecDNA behaves dynamically within dividing cells. Single-cell transcriptome coupling then allowed investigators to connect ecDNA content with transcriptional consequences in individual cells. More recently, single-cell ATAC-based approaches have made it possible to identify ecDNA-containing populations through chromatin accessibility patterns, while spatial profiling has revealed that ecDNA-associated oncogene amplification can vary across distinct tumor regions [137, 138]. Together, these tools have shown that ecDNA-driven heterogeneity is not theoretical; it is directly observable at the cellular and spatial scales most relevant to tumor evolution.

Chamorro González and colleagues made a major contribution with scEC&T-seq, a method for parallel sequencing of extrachromosomal circular DNAs and transcriptomes in single cancer cells [66]. This approach enabled direct coupling of ecDNA content with transcriptional state at single-cell resolution. The importance of this work lies in its ability to move beyond averaged tumor-level associations and demonstrate that ecDNA contributes to heterogeneity in a cell-specific manner. It supports the idea that ecDNA is not uniformly distributed or functionally equivalent across a tumor, but instead defines transcriptionally distinct cellular subpopulations.

Noorani and colleagues provided an especially compelling spatial and evolutionary perspective in glioblastoma [68]. By analyzing treatment-naive human IDH-wildtype glioblastomas, they showed that ecDNA-amplified oncogenes display marked spatial heterogeneity and evolutionary diversification across tumor regions. This is important because it demonstrates that ecDNA is not only a source of variability among individual cells, but also a driver of distinct regional evolutionary trajectories within the same tumor mass. In glioblastoma, where spatial architecture is tightly linked to invasion, microenvironmental adaptation, and treatment response, this observation strongly supports the view that ecDNA contributes to clinically meaningful spatial evolution.

Jiang and colleagues further extended the single-cell perspective by introducing scCirclehunter, a framework that identifies ecDNA-containing cells using scATAC-seq data and assigns ecDNA to specific cell populations [73]. Applied to glioblastoma datasets, this method uncovered intercellular heterogeneity among ecDNA-carrying cells and enabled reconstruction of ecDNA-associated population structure at the chromatin level. This is particularly valuable because it adds an epigenomic dimension to ecDNA evolution. Instead of inferring ecDNA from copy number or transcript abundance alone, scCirclehunter makes it possible to identify ecDNA-positive populations through accessibility patterns and to track how ecDNA-bearing states relate to chromatin organization and lineage structure. The broader conceptual framework provided by van Leen and colleagues also supports these observations, as they emphasized that ecDNA possesses genomic and spatial mobility that may disrupt genome-wide gene expression beyond simple dosage effects [32]. Likewise, the three-dimensional reconstruction work by Chowdhury and colleagues suggests that ecDNA-driven heterogeneity is shaped not only by the presence or absence of ecDNA, but also by the specific topological and regulatory interactions that ecDNA creates in different cellular contexts [40]. Thus, single-cell and spatial studies do more than validate ecDNA heterogeneity; they reveal that ecDNA contributes to evolution through dynamic interactions among copy number, chromatin state, nuclear organization, and regional tumor ecology.

Taken together, these studies show that technology has fundamentally changed how ecDNA is understood. What began as a cytogenetic abnormality observable in bulk tumor material can now be traced across individual cells, chromatin states, and spatial tumor regions. This has made it increasingly clear that ecDNA is a major driver of tumor evolution at precisely the scales where evolution occurs: between daughter cells, among subclones, and across distinct ecological niches within tumors.

ecDNA and clonal selection under therapeutic pressure

If unequal segregation and spatial diversification explain how ecDNA generates heterogeneity, therapeutic selection explains why that heterogeneity matters clinically. A central idea emerging from recent work is that ecDNA-bearing tumors are unusually well equipped to adapt under treatment pressure because ecDNA provides a rapidly tunable source of oncogenic variation. Subclones with favorable ecDNA content, structure, or associated regulatory programs can be enriched during therapy, while less fit configurations are depleted. In this way, ecDNA converts heterogeneity into selective opportunity [2, 26, 133]. This selection process may involve not only high-copy ecDNA states, but also low-copy ecDNA states. Montuori and colleagues showed that ecDNA-driven oncogene dosage heterogeneity influences treatment response in MYCN-amplified cancers [139]. In their model, MYC(N)-high cells were more prone to apoptosis after doxorubicin exposure, whereas MYC(N)-low ecDNA cells entered a therapy-induced senescence-like state and could later contribute to treatment adaptation. This finding adds an important refinement to the conventional view that ecDNA promotes resistance only by increasing oncogene dosage. Instead, ecDNA copy-number heterogeneity may generate both high-output proliferative states and low-output stress-tolerant reservoirs.

Choudhuri and colleagues provide a clear example of this process in small cell lung cancer [67]. Using patient-derived xenograft models, they showed that amplifications of MYC paralogs on ecDNA are recurrent drivers of acquired cross-resistance. This is a particularly strong demonstration of ecDNA-mediated therapeutic evolution because it links ecDNA directly to the emergence of a refractory state after initial treatment sensitivity. Rather than being a static genomic feature present from the outset, ecDNA in this context behaves as a selected adaptive mechanism that supports survival across multiple therapeutic insults.

Kim and colleagues broadened this argument at the cohort level by mapping ecDNA amplifications across newly diagnosed primary tumors, untreated metastases, and heavily pretreated cancers [34]. They found that ecDNA occurs at significantly higher frequency in untreated metastatic and pretreated tumors than in newly diagnosed cancers, and that chemotherapy-pretreated tumors show particularly high ecDNA frequency. These findings strongly suggest that ecDNA provides competitive advantages during progression and metastasis and that treatment can enrich for ecDNA-bearing disease states [90, 140–142].This is exactly what would be expected if ecDNA functions as an adaptive substrate under selective pressure.

Kanayama and colleagues add an important mechanistic layer by showing that ecDNA dynamics contribute to intratumoral genetic heterogeneity in receptor tyrosine kinase amplification and to resistance against RTK-targeted therapies [70]. Their study emphasizes that ecDNA does not merely exist in resistant tumors; rather, dynamic changes in ecDNA content can actively generate heterogeneous RTK expression patterns that undermine therapeutic uniformity. This is highly relevant because it connects ecDNA-mediated heterogeneity directly to a concrete drug-response problem: even when a therapy effectively suppresses one ecDNA-associated state, other states can persist or emerge, sustaining resistance. The evolutionary logic outlined by Lange and colleagues helps integrate these findings. If ecDNA creates variation on which selection can act, then therapy becomes one of the most powerful selective filters shaping ecDNA-positive populations [33]. Under treatment, ecDNA-bearing tumors are not merely passively subjected to pressure; they may actively exploit their capacity for copy-number fluctuation, regulatory rewiring, and heterogeneous inheritance to discover resistant solutions. Noorani and colleagues further reinforce this perspective by showing that ecDNA-associated oncogene patterns in glioblastoma are spatially heterogeneous and evolutionarily structured, suggesting that different tumor regions may serve as distinct reservoirs of treatment-adaptable ecDNA states [68].

Taken together, these studies support a model in which ecDNA is repeatedly selected, retained, and expanded under therapeutic pressure. It enables tumors to generate diverse subclonal states before treatment, to preserve advantageous configurations during treatment, and to expand resistant populations after treatment. This makes ecDNA a central driver of clonal selection in advanced cancer and helps explain why ecDNA-positive tumors are often associated with relapse, metastasis, and poor clinical outcome. From this perspective, ecDNA is not simply an aggressive biomarker, but a dynamic evolutionary instrument through which tumors adapt to intervention. Inheritance, retention, and maintenance of ecDNA as a regulatory platform

A major question in ecDNA biology is not only how these extrachromosomal elements arise, but how they persist. If ecDNA were merely a transient byproduct of genome instability, its biological impact would likely be limited. However, recent evidence suggests that ecDNA can be maintained across multiple generations of cancer cells, allowing its regulatory and oncogenic effects to accumulate over time. This persistence is especially important in the context of the present review, because ecDNA can function as a platform for epigenetic reprogramming only if it is stably retained long enough to support recurrent transcriptional activation, enhancer rewiring, and clonal selection. For many years, ecDNA persistence was often interpreted as a largely passive consequence of selection: ecDNA-bearing cells survived because ecDNA amplified advantageous oncogenes, and ecDNA therefore remained detectable in expanding clones [143–145]. While this view is partly correct, recent work suggests that it is incomplete. A more nuanced model is now emerging in which ecDNA persistence depends not only on selective benefit, but also on specific biological mechanisms that facilitate inheritance, retention, and nuclear propagation. These findings are conceptually important because they imply that ecDNA is not merely tolerated by dividing cancer cells, but may be actively managed in ways that preserve oncogenic regulatory states across generations.

Mitotic behavior and coordinated inheritance

Yi and colleagues provided direct evidence for this phenomenon by using live-cell imaging to visualize ecDNA dynamics in dividing cells [35]. Their study showed that ecDNA can segregate unevenly into daughter cells, generating marked cell-to-cell differences in ecDNA burden within a single generation. This finding was important because it established mitotic behavior as a central determinant of ecDNA-driven heterogeneity. In contrast to chromosomal amplifications, which are stably transmitted through chromosome inheritance, ecDNA can fluctuate rapidly between cells due to noncanonical segregation. As a result, ecDNA-positive tumors gain a mechanism for producing phenotypic diversity at exceptional speed. Unequal segregation therefore has two consequences at once: it destabilizes ecDNA inheritance at the single-cell level, yet it enhances population-level adaptability by continuously generating variation.

However, randomness is not the whole story. An important refinement came from Hung and colleagues, who showed that multiple ecDNA species can undergo coordinated inheritance through mitotic co-segregation [65]. This finding is particularly significant because it suggests that ecDNA inheritance can preserve not only individual amplified elements, but also advantageous combinations of ecDNA molecules. In tumors where different ecDNA species carry complementary oncogenes or cooperative regulatory modules, coordinated inheritance may help maintain high-fitness oncogenic configurations across generations. Thus, ecDNA mitotic behavior appears to span a spectrum from stochastic unequal segregation to coordinated co-inheritance, depending on the biological context and the relationships among ecDNA species. This shift from purely random segregation to partially coordinated inheritance has important implications for multi-oncogene cooperation. If two or more ecDNA species encode interacting oncogenic drivers, signaling components, or regulatory modules, coordinated co-segregation would allow these elements to be propagated together, preserving their combined functional effect. In this way, ecDNA biology may support not only rapid diversification, but also the stable inheritance of cooperative oncogenic networks [146]. This idea is especially relevant to aggressive tumors, where malignant fitness may depend on the simultaneous maintenance of several amplified drivers rather than a single dominant oncogene. More broadly, the coexistence of unequal segregation and coordinated inheritance illustrates a central principle of ecDNA biology: ecDNA maintenance is dynamic rather than binary. Tumors can exploit stochasticity to generate variation and exploit coordination to preserve useful combinations. This dual strategy is highly advantageous from an evolutionary perspective. It allows ecDNA-positive populations to remain plastic enough to adapt to changing conditions while also being sufficiently organized to retain powerful oncogenic configurations once they emerge. Thus, mitotic behavior is not merely a cell biological detail of ecDNA; it is a core mechanism through which ecDNA balances diversification and continuity.

Chromosomal tethering, nuclear retention, and stable propagation

Although mitotic segregation explains how ecDNA may be distributed between daughter cells, it does not fully explain how ecDNA can persist over longer periods in proliferating cancer populations. If ecDNA were simply free-floating circular DNA, one might expect a substantial fraction to be diluted, lost, or degraded over repeated cell divisions. Yet many ecDNA-positive tumors maintain ecDNA-bearing clones over time, indicating that additional mechanisms must contribute to ecDNA stability. Recent studies now suggest that ecDNA persistence involves more than passive survival and selective enrichment. Instead, it may rely on active biological programs that help retain ecDNA in the nucleus and promote its propagation across generations [8, 147, 148].

Nichols and colleagues provided a major advance in this area by showing that chromosomal tethering and mitotic transcription contribute to ecDNA nuclear inheritance [71]. Their work demonstrated that ecDNA can associate with chromosomes during mitosis, thereby facilitating its retention during cell division rather than allowing it to drift passively and unpredictably. This finding is conceptually important because it challenges the earlier view that ecDNA inheritance is governed entirely by random partitioning. Instead, ecDNA can exploit chromosome-associated mechanisms to enhance its probability of remaining in daughter nuclei. In addition, Nichols and colleagues identified a role for mitotic transcription in this process, suggesting that the inheritance of ecDNA may be functionally linked to the maintenance of its transcriptional activity [71]. This raises the intriguing possibility that ecDNA propagation is coordinated not only with structural retention, but also with preservation of regulatory competence. The work of Sankar and colleagues further strengthened this emerging model by identifying genetic elements that promote ecDNA retention in cancer cells [72]. Their study suggests that ecDNA can contain specific retention elements that enhance its persistence across divisions. This is a particularly important advance because it indicates that ecDNA stability may, at least in part, be encoded within ecDNA itself. In other words, some ecDNA molecules may carry intrinsic sequence features that facilitate their long-term maintenance. This concept considerably deepens the field: ecDNA is not merely a structural product of genome instability, but a genomic entity that may evolve features favoring its own persistence.

Hung and colleagues provide useful supporting context here as well. Their finding that cooperative ecDNA species can co-segregate during mitosis suggests that ecDNA persistence may involve higher-order organizational principles rather than the independent survival of isolated circles [65]. Coordinated inheritance implies that ecDNA species may participate in shared propagation mechanisms or physically related mitotic behaviors, thereby increasing the efficiency with which beneficial ecDNA constellations are retained. When considered together with chromosomal tethering and retention elements, these observations support a more integrated model in which ecDNA maintenance is actively reinforced at multiple levels.

These mechanisms also have important implications for the regulatory role of ecDNA. If ecDNA persistence depends on tethering, retention elements, and possibly transcription-linked propagation, then the continued presence of ecDNA is not merely preserving amplified DNA sequences; it is also preserving oncogenic regulatory states. ecDNA carries enhancers, rewired regulatory modules, and transcriptionally permissive chromatin configurations [149–151]. To retain ecDNA, therefore, is to retain an entire epigenetic and transcriptional microprogram. This point is central to the present review. The persistence of ecDNA is biologically meaningful not simply because it maintains copy number, but because it stabilizes the regulatory architecture through which amplified oncogenes are activated. This perspective allows a broader interpretive shift. ecDNA persistence is not merely passive survival, but may involve active inheritance programs that help preserve oncogenic regulatory states. Such programs could include chromosome-associated tethering, sequence-encoded retention, coordinated co-segregation of cooperating ecDNA species, and transcription-coupled nuclear propagation. Together, these mechanisms would allow ecDNA-positive cancer cells to do more than transiently exploit amplified oncogenes; they would enable the long-term maintenance of ecDNA-dependent transcriptional circuits that support malignant growth, adaptation, and resistance.

Taken together, current evidence supports a model in which ecDNA maintenance is a multi-layered process. Unequal segregation generates diversity, coordinated inheritance preserves beneficial combinations, chromosomal tethering enhances nuclear transmission, and retention elements promote long-term persistence. Through this combination of stochastic and structured mechanisms, ecDNA can remain present long enough to serve as a durable regulatory platform in cancer. This durability helps explain why ecDNA is repeatedly associated with aggressive disease, relapse, and evolutionary flexibility: once established, ecDNA is not necessarily fleeting, but can be propagated in ways that preserve both oncogenic sequence content and oncogenic regulatory function. Therapeutic implications: can ecDNA-dependent epigenetic reprogramming be targeted?

The mechanisms discussed above suggest that ecDNA-positive tumors may depend on several processes that are potentially targetable: ecDNA replication and maintenance, enhancer-driven transcription, nuclear hub formation, and stress-response pathways. This section therefore focuses on therapeutic opportunities that arise from these dependencies, rather than restating the regulatory mechanisms described in previous sections.

Targeting ecDNA replication stress and transcription–replication vulnerabilities

One of the most compelling therapeutic ideas to emerge from recent ecDNA research is that ecDNA-positive cancers may carry intrinsic replication-associated vulnerabilities. These vulnerabilities arise from at least two related sources. First, ecDNA must be replicated and maintained as high-copy extrachromosomal circular DNA, a process that appears to be less orderly than canonical chromosomal replication and more dependent on DNA damage response pathways. Second, ecDNA-associated oncogenes often undergo exceptionally strong transcription, creating transcription–replication conflict and additional replication stress [25, 152, 153]. Thus, replication stress in ecDNA-positive tumors should be considered not only a consequence of hyperactive transcription, but also a direct feature of ecDNA replication and maintenance.

Tang and colleagues provided the clearest evidence for this concept by showing that ecDNA-containing cancers are vulnerable to enhanced transcription–replication conflict [69]. Their work demonstrated that ecDNA-positive tumors exhibit high transcriptional output associated with replication stress and that targeting this liability can selectively suppress ecDNA-bearing cancer cells. This study is especially important because it converts a mechanistic insight into a therapeutic principle: ecDNA does not simply amplify oncogenic activity, it also imposes a physiological burden that may be exploited pharmacologically. This therapeutic logic is further strengthened by studies focusing directly on ecDNA replication. Kang and colleagues showed that ecDNA replication and maintenance are coupled with DNA damage pathway activity in tumors [29]. This suggests that ecDNA-bearing cells may require replication-associated repair programs to sustain ecDNA propagation across cell divisions. Jaworski and colleagues further reported that ecDNA replication is disorganized and vulnerable to replication stress [154]. Together, these findings indicate that ecDNA-associated replication stress is not solely produced by collisions between transcription and replication machinery, but may also reflect intrinsic defects and dependencies in the replication of ecDNA itself. In this framework, exploiting ecDNA-associated replication stress may offer a selective therapeutic window. This principle is also consistent with the broader observation that ecDNA often emerges and expands under strong selective conditions, including treatment exposure. Choudhuri and colleagues showed in small cell lung cancer that MYC paralog amplification on ecDNA is a driver of acquired cross-resistance, underscoring how ecDNA can support survival under therapeutic stress [67]. Yet the same adaptation may deepen dependence on extreme transcriptional programs, potentially rendering such tumors more vulnerable to strategies that target stress created by those programs. Thus, resistance and vulnerability may arise from the same ecDNA-dependent regulatory state. Kanayama and colleagues likewise showed that ecDNA dynamics contribute to receptor tyrosine kinase heterogeneity and resistance to targeted therapy [70]. Their findings reinforce the idea that ecDNA can help tumors evade specific inhibitors by dynamically adjusting amplified signaling states. At the same time, such adaptive flexibility may depend on maintaining high levels of transcriptional and replicative activity, suggesting that therapies aimed at stress-response pathways could complement pathway-directed inhibitors. In other words, although ecDNA can promote resistance to targeted therapy, it may simultaneously create a broader dependency on replication stress management. From a translational perspective, these observations support a broader therapeutic logic. Rather than targeting each ecDNA-borne oncogene individually, it may be possible to target a shared downstream consequence of ecDNA biology: the burden of sustaining excessive transcription. This is appealing because ecDNA-positive tumors may carry diverse oncogenes across different cancer types, whereas transcription–replication conflict may represent a more convergent vulnerability. Such an approach could be especially valuable in tumors with complex ecDNA landscapes where direct targeting of every amplified driver is impractical.

Taken together, current evidence suggests that replication-associated stress is one of the most tractable ecDNA-associated liabilities identified so far. This vulnerability has two mechanistic layers: disorganized or stress-prone replication of ecDNA itself, and transcription–replication conflict driven by hyperactive ecDNA-associated oncogene expression. By forcing cancer cells to replicate, maintain, and transcribe high-copy extrachromosomal DNA, ecDNA creates a condition in which DNA damage response and replication stress pathways may become therapeutically actionable. Therefore, ecDNA replication should be viewed not only as a mechanism of ecDNA persistence, but also as a potential therapeutic entry point.

Disrupting ecDNA hubs, condensates, and regulatory architecture

A second major therapeutic strategy is to interfere with the higher-order regulatory structures that make ecDNA transcriptionally effective. As discussed in earlier sections, ecDNA functions within a spatial and architectural framework that includes clustered ecDNA hubs, condensate-associated transcriptional assemblies, rewired enhancer–oncogene relationships, and noncanonical regulatory elements. These structures are not simply descriptive features of ecDNA biology; they are part of the mechanism through which ecDNA amplifies transcriptional output. This raises a key translational question: if ecDNA-dependent oncogenic programs rely on specialized regulatory architecture, can that architecture itself be disrupted?

Taghbalout and colleagues provide particularly strong support for this possibility. Their study showed that ecDNA associates with nuclear condensates and that ecDNA-specific regulatory modules are required for condensate formation and oncogenic transcription [41]. Importantly, disrupting these assemblies suppressed ecDNA-driven transcription and promoted cell death, indicating that ecDNA hubs are not passive accumulations of DNA but functionally important regulatory structures. This finding suggests that hub disruption or condensate perturbation could serve as a therapeutic approach in ecDNA-positive tumors, particularly when those tumors have become dependent on ecDNA-organized transcriptional microenvironments.

Chowdhury and colleagues add a complementary architectural dimension by showing that ecDNA reorganizes three-dimensional chromatin interactions [40]. Their work implies that ecDNA-driven transcription depends not only on local chromatin accessibility, but also on specific topological configurations that bring enhancers, oncogenes, and regulatory elements into productive spatial relationships. This opens the door to strategies aimed at destabilizing ecDNA-specific chromatin organization. Although such strategies remain early in development, the principle is important: ecDNA-mediated oncogenic transcription may be vulnerable not just to pathway inhibition, but to perturbation of the physical regulatory framework that sustains it.

Kraft et al. further expand this idea by showing that ecDNA can activate enhancer-like function from transposable element-derived sequences [39]. This finding is especially relevant therapeutically because it suggests that ecDNA-dependent tumors may rely on regulatory modules that are both unusual and context-specific. If ecDNA creates novel enhancer-centered circuitry from noncanonical elements, then these elements may represent points of selective vulnerability not shared by normal cells or even by chromosomally amplified tumors. Targeting ecDNA-specific regulatory modules, therefore, could become an enhancer-centered strategy that exploits the distinctive regulatory logic of ecDNA rather than simply targeting amplified oncogenes one by one. More broadly, these studies support a layered model of architectural intervention. One layer would involve disrupting ecDNA clustering and hub formation. Another would involve perturbing condensate-associated transcriptional machinery such as co-activator-rich assemblies [155–157].A third would focus on ecDNA-specific enhancer logic, including rewired or newly activated regulatory elements. Although these strategies remain conceptually and technically challenging, they are attractive because they target how ecDNA works rather than merely what genes it carries. This distinction matters: oncogene content may vary across tumors, but the dependence of ecDNA-positive tumors on specialized regulatory organization may prove to be a more unifying therapeutic feature. In this sense, ecDNA biology suggests a broader therapeutic paradigm shift. Instead of treating ecDNA as a difficult-to-target amplification phenomenon, it may be more productive to view ecDNA-positive tumors as diseases of misassembled regulatory architecture. If that architecture can be destabilized, then ecDNA-driven transcription may collapse even without direct elimination of every ecDNA molecule. This possibility is particularly appealing in tumors with multiple cooperating ecDNA species, where destroying a common architectural dependency may be more feasible than individually targeting all amplified drivers.

Challenges for ecDNA-targeted therapy

Despite growing therapeutic promise, translating ecDNA biology into treatment remains difficult. One major challenge is heterogeneity. ecDNA is often unevenly distributed across cells, dynamically variable over time, and spatially heterogeneous across tumor regions [120, 158, 159]. This means that any ecDNA-targeted therapy must operate in a biological context where the abundance, structure, and regulatory role of ecDNA may differ substantially from one cell population to another. A treatment that is effective against one ecDNA-dependent state may leave behind others with distinct regulatory configurations.

The cohort-level work by Kim and colleagues illustrates this problem clearly. Their analysis showed that ecDNA is enriched in untreated metastatic tumors and in heavily pretreated cancers, indicating that ecDNA prevalence changes with disease stage and treatment history. This suggests that ecDNA is not a static feature that can be assumed to behave similarly across all clinical settings. Instead, ecDNA-positive disease may represent a moving target whose abundance and biological significance evolve over time. Such dynamism complicates both patient selection and therapeutic timing.

Spatial heterogeneity presents an additional challenge. Noorani and colleagues showed in glioblastoma that ecDNA-associated oncogene amplification can vary across tumor regions and follow distinct spatial evolutionary trajectories [68]. This means that biopsy-based assessment of ecDNA may underrepresent the full ecDNA landscape of a tumor, particularly in highly heterogeneous cancers. From a clinical perspective, regional ecDNA variation raises the risk of incomplete targeting and highlights the need for better methods to capture ecDNA diversity across tumor space. Bailey and colleagues provide a broader reminder that ecDNA is widespread across cancer types but not necessarily uniform in its biology [42]. Their large pan-cancer analysis established ecDNA as a recurrent architecture associated with poor outcome and treatment resistance, but it also implies that ecDNA-driven states may differ across lineages, oncogene cargo, and evolutionary contexts. Accordingly, therapies that work in one ecDNA-positive cancer may not translate directly to another. Cancer-type dependence remains a major issue, particularly when ecDNA maintenance mechanisms and regulatory architectures are not fully conserved across tumors. Another important challenge is that ecDNA maintenance itself may be mechanistically diverse. Sankar and colleagues identified genetic elements that promote ecDNA retention, suggesting that at least some ecDNA molecules carry intrinsic features favoring long-term persistence [72]. This is an important conceptual advance, but it also complicates therapy. If different ecDNA species rely on different retention elements or maintenance mechanisms, there may be no single universal strategy for destabilizing ecDNA across all tumors. In other words, nonuniform maintenance biology may limit the effectiveness of one-size-fits-all ecDNA-targeted approaches. Detection also remains a practical barrier. ecDNA is increasingly identifiable through computational reconstruction, single-cell approaches, and spatial analyses, but these tools are not yet uniformly accessible or standardized for routine clinical use. This matters because ecDNA-targeted therapy will require reliable identification of the tumors most dependent on ecDNA-associated regulatory states. Without robust and scalable detection methods, even biologically compelling therapeutic ideas may be difficult to implement in the clinic.

Taken together, these challenges indicate that ecDNA-targeted therapy is promising but not straightforward. The field must contend with dynamic heterogeneity, incomplete detection, lineage-specific biology, and diverse maintenance mechanisms. Yet these obstacles do not diminish the importance of ecDNA as a therapeutic concept. Rather, they suggest that future progress will likely depend on integrating ecDNA detection, evolutionary monitoring, and context-specific intervention strategies. The most successful approaches may be those that do not assume ecDNA is a single therapeutic entity, but instead treat it as a family of related regulatory dependencies whose vulnerabilities must be defined with mechanistic precision [160, 161]. Another underexplored constraint is the metabolic ceiling imposed by ecDNA maintenance. An ecDNA-dependent regulatory platform is unlikely to be energetically neutral. Sustaining high-copy extrachromosomal DNA requires DNA replication, chromatin assembly, DNA damage surveillance, repair activity, nuclear organization, and often exceptionally high transcriptional output. These processes may impose substantial demands on nucleotide pools, ATP availability, chromatin-modifying enzymes, transcriptional machinery, and proteostasis. Therefore, ecDNA-positive tumors may face a trade-off: ecDNA provides strong oncogenic and regulatory advantages, but only up to the point at which the energetic and biosynthetic costs of maintaining this platform remain tolerable. Defining this metabolic ceiling may help identify new vulnerabilities in ecDNA-driven cancers.

Technologies that are transforming ecDNA research

The rapid conceptual expansion of the ecDNA field has been driven not only by biological discovery, but also by methodological innovation. For many years, ecDNA was primarily recognized through cytogenetic observations or inferred indirectly from focal amplification patterns in bulk genomic data. While such approaches were sufficient to establish ecDNA as a recurrent feature of aggressive cancers, they provided only limited insight into how ecDNA behaves, how it is inherited, and how it regulates transcription [6, 117, 162, 163]. In this sense, the early phase of ecDNA research was largely descriptive: investigators could often determine that ecDNA existed, but not how it functioned. Recent technological advances have fundamentally changed this situation. A new generation of imaging, sequencing, single-cell, and structural reconstruction methods has enabled researchers to follow ecDNA across multiple biological scales, from mitotic dynamics to chromatin accessibility to three-dimensional nuclear organization. These approaches have transformed ecDNA from a genomic curiosity into a mechanistically tractable regulatory entity. Importantly, they have made it possible to ask not only where ecDNA is present, but how ecDNA shapes gene regulation, tumor heterogeneity, and evolutionary behavior [61, 164]. A useful way to understand this methodological shift is to view it as a progression from detection to mechanism. Early bulk analyses established the presence and clinical relevance of ecDNA. Live-cell imaging then revealed that ecDNA behaves dynamically in dividing cells and can form transcriptionally active hubs. Biochemical isolation methods enabled structural resolution of large ecDNA molecules. Single-cell approaches connected ecDNA status with transcriptomic and chromatin states in individual cells. Finally, three-dimensional reconstruction strategies began to resolve how ecDNA reorganizes chromatin topology and regulatory contacts. Together, these tools have allowed the field to move from seeing ecDNA to understanding how ecDNA works.

Live-cell imaging and the visualization of ecDNA dynamics

One of the earliest major conceptual breakthroughs in recent ecDNA research came from live-cell imaging. Yi and colleagues used this strategy to visualize ecDNA behavior in living cancer cells, demonstrating that ecDNA segregates unevenly during mitosis and can form transcriptionally active hubs within the nucleus [35]. This was a critical advance because it provided direct visual evidence that ecDNA is not statically inherited or randomly inert. Instead, ecDNA behaves as a dynamic nuclear entity whose distribution changes across cell divisions and whose spatial organization is linked to transcriptional activity. The importance of live-cell imaging extends beyond descriptive visualization. By directly showing uneven segregation and hub formation, this approach helped establish the mechanistic basis for ecDNA-driven heterogeneity and high transcriptional output. In other words, live-cell imaging enabled the field to connect structure with behavior. It transformed ecDNA from a genomic feature inferred from sequencing into a real-time biological process observable in individual cells.

CRISPR-CATCH and the structural isolation of ecDNA

Another major advance came from the development of approaches capable of physically isolating large ecDNA molecules. Hung and colleagues introduced CRISPR-CATCH, which enabled targeted profiling of large human ecDNAs [36]. This was especially important because ecDNA is often structurally complex, large in size, and difficult to resolve using conventional sequencing alone. By allowing more direct characterization of ecDNA composition, CRISPR-CATCH opened the door to more precise analysis of ecDNA structure, rearrangement patterns, and associated regulatory elements [43, 165–167]. The conceptual importance of this approach lies in the fact that it helps bridge the gap between computational inference and molecular reality. Many early ecDNA studies relied on reconstructing circular amplicons from bulk sequencing patterns, but CRISPR-CATCH made it possible to enrich and examine ecDNA more directly. This strengthened confidence in ecDNA structural models and provided a foundation for later work on chromatin organization, enhancer content, and functional architecture.

Single-cell coupling of ecDNA and transcriptional states

A major leap forward in understanding ecDNA function came with the move into single-cell analysis. Chamorro González and colleagues developed scEC&T-seq, a method that enables parallel sequencing of extrachromosomal circular DNAs and transcriptomes in single cancer cells [66]. This was a transformative step because it allowed researchers to directly couple ecDNA content with transcriptional state at the level of individual cells. This capability is crucial for at least two reasons. First, it reveals that ecDNA is heterogeneously distributed across cells within the same tumor. Second, it allows that heterogeneity to be linked to functional outputs such as oncogene expression programs. Thus, scEC&T-seq moved the field beyond bulk averages and made it possible to study ecDNA as a driver of cellular diversity and transcriptional heterogeneity. In practical terms, this method helped show that ecDNA is not simply present in tumors, but actively defines distinct subpopulations with different regulatory states.

scCirclehunter and single-cell chromatin-based identification of ecDNA-positive cells

If scEC&T-seq connected ecDNA with transcriptomic states, the next major advance was to connect ecDNA with chromatin states at single-cell resolution. Jiang and colleagues addressed this challenge by developing scCirclehunter, a framework that identifies ecDNA-containing cells from single-cell ATAC-seq data [73]. This approach was particularly important because it expanded ecDNA analysis from gene expression into chromatin accessibility and epigenetic state. The value of scCirclehunter is that it enables the assignment of ecDNA to specific cell populations within complex tumors while simultaneously revealing how those populations are organized at the chromatin level. This is especially relevant for a review centered on epigenetic reprogramming, because it allows investigators to ask how ecDNA-bearing cells differ in accessibility patterns, regulatory identity, and lineage structure. More broadly, scCirclehunter illustrates how the ecDNA field is increasingly merging with the single-cell epigenomics revolution.

ec3D and Hi-C-based reconstruction of ecDNA architecture

A final major technological frontier has been the resolution of ecDNA in three-dimensional space. Chowdhury and colleagues developed ec3D, a framework for reconstructing the three-dimensional architecture of ecDNA using Hi-C-based data [40]. This approach marked a major advance because it moved the field from identifying ecDNA sequences to understanding how ecDNA folds, how its internal contacts are organized, and how its circular topology reshapes regulatory communication. This is especially important because ecDNA-mediated gene regulation cannot be understood fully at the linear sequence level alone. The functional impact of ecDNA depends on which enhancers, promoters, and other regulatory elements are brought into contact within its circular architecture. By reconstructing these interactions, ec3D provided a way to study ecDNA as a three-dimensional regulatory structure rather than simply an amplified DNA circle. This has been essential for clarifying how ecDNA rewires enhancer–oncogene communication and reorganizes nuclear architecture.

From detecting ecDNA to decoding ecDNA regulation

Taken together, these methods illustrate a broader evolution in the field. Live-cell imaging revealed ecDNA dynamics. CRISPR-CATCH improved structural isolation and validation. scEC&T-seq linked ecDNA to single-cell transcriptional programs. scCirclehunter enabled chromatin-based identification of ecDNA-positive populations [49, 167]. ec3D and related Hi-C-based approaches reconstructed ecDNA architecture and regulatory topology. Each method contributed something distinct, but their collective impact has been even more important: they have shifted ecDNA research from asking whether ecDNA is present to asking how ecDNA is organized, inherited, and functionally deployed. This methodological transition has changed the conceptual status of ecDNA. ecDNA is no longer viewed merely as a genomic abnormality that can be counted or mapped. It is now increasingly understood as a dynamic, multilayered regulatory system that must be studied through integrated structural, spatial, transcriptional, and epigenetic approaches [33, 65, 74]. In that sense, technology has not simply accelerated ecDNA research; it has redefined the kinds of questions that can be asked. For the field moving forward, this will remain crucial. Therapeutic targeting of ecDNA-dependent states will require accurate identification of ecDNA-positive cells, precise mapping of ecDNA regulatory architecture, and sensitive monitoring of ecDNA dynamics under treatment. Thus, technological innovation is not merely supporting ecDNA biology; it is enabling the very framework through which ecDNA can be understood as a platform for epigenetic reprogramming. Conclusions and future perspectives

Over the past several years, the conceptual status of extrachromosomal DNA in cancer biology has changed profoundly. ecDNA was once viewed primarily as an unusual structural manifestation of oncogene amplification, a cytogenetic correlate of aggressive disease that increased gene dosage and thereby promoted malignant growth. That view remains important, but it is now incomplete. The studies discussed throughout this review collectively support a broader and more mechanistic perspective: ecDNA is not merely amplified DNA located outside chromosomes, but a dynamic regulatory platform capable of reshaping chromatin accessibility, enhancer logic, three-dimensional genome organization, nuclear architecture, transcriptional output, and tumor evolution. In this sense, ecDNA should now be understood as a platform for epigenetic reprogramming in cancer. This conceptual shift matters because it changes the central question of the field. The key issue is no longer only which oncogenes are amplified on ecDNA, but how ecDNA reorganizes the regulatory environment in which those oncogenes operate. By existing outside canonical chromosomal constraints, ecDNA can generate open and transcriptionally permissive chromatin states, capture and rewire enhancers, assemble into transcriptionally active hubs, and persist across cell divisions through noncanonical inheritance and retention mechanisms. These features allow ecDNA to function as more than a structural amplification product: it becomes a flexible substrate for malignant adaptation and, in some contexts, a stabilizer of oncogenic dependency. At the same time, ecDNA biology has revealed a broader principle in cancer. Structural genome instability does not simply create random damage; under selective pressure, it can generate new regulatory architectures that are actively exploited by tumor cells. ecDNA is perhaps one of the clearest examples of this principle. It emerges from genome disruption, yet it can be stabilized, propagated, and integrated into transcriptional programs that promote aggressive fitness. Thus, ecDNA is both a consequence of instability and a mechanism through which instability is converted into durable oncogenic function.

From copy-number biology to regulatory biology

A major future direction for the field is the continued transition from copy-number biology to regulatory biology. Early ecDNA research understandably focused on identifying amplified oncogenes and measuring their clinical associations. However, recent work has made it increasingly clear that copy number alone cannot explain the functional impact of ecDNA. Studies of hijacked chromatin accessibility, enhancer rewiring, nuclear condensates, transposable element-derived enhancer activation, and ecDNA-specific three-dimensional architecture all point to the same conclusion: the major oncogenic power of ecDNA lies not simply in how much DNA it contains, but in how that DNA is organized and regulated [135, 168]. This transition will likely redefine the field in the coming years. Rather than asking only whether ecDNA amplifies EGFR, MYC, or other oncogenes, future studies will need to determine which chromatin states, enhancer networks, and nuclear assemblies are most essential for ecDNA function [169, 170]. The conceptual vocabulary of the field may also shift accordingly, moving from terms such as “extrachromosomal amplification” toward a more integrated language of “regulatory topology,” “ecDNA-associated chromatin states,” and “ecDNA-dependent transcriptional programs.” Such a shift will be particularly important for aligning ecDNA research with broader fields such as enhancer biology, transcriptional condensates, and cancer epigenetics.

Cancer-type-specific logic of ecDNA formation, maintenance, and function

A second major direction is the recognition that ecDNA biology is unlikely to be fully universal across cancers. Pan-cancer studies have shown that ecDNA is widespread, but this does not mean that all ecDNA-positive tumors use ecDNA in the same way. Different cancer types may differ in how ecDNA is generated, what cargo it carries, how it is maintained, and how strongly tumors depend on ecDNA-driven regulatory programs. For example, the frequency of ecDNA in metastatic versus primary disease, the degree of spatial heterogeneity in glioblastoma, the dynamic contribution of ecDNA to RTK-targeted therapy resistance, and the possibility of sequence-encoded retention programs all suggest that ecDNA biology is context dependent. This has important implications for both mechanism and translation. Some tumors may rely heavily on enhancer rewiring and nuclear clustering, whereas others may be more strongly shaped by dynamic copy-number fluctuation or by specific maintenance mechanisms. Likewise, the same therapeutic principle may not apply equally across all ecDNA-positive cancers. Future work will therefore need to move beyond broad pan-cancer generalizations and define cancer-type-specific ecDNA logic. This includes determining whether distinct tumor lineages favor different routes of ecDNA formation, different modes of nuclear inheritance, or different forms of ecDNA-dependent transcriptional addiction [63, 92, 171].

ecDNA as a bridge between genome instability and epigenetic plasticity

A third major conceptual advance is the realization that ecDNA may represent a central bridge between genome instability and epigenetic plasticity. This is one of the most intellectually important ideas emerging from the field. On the one hand, ecDNA is born from instability: chromothripsis, replication stress, aberrant DNA repair, and other catastrophic structural processes contribute to its formation. On the other hand, once established, ecDNA creates an architecture that supports flexible and unusually potent transcriptional regulation. It therefore links two hallmarks of aggressive cancer that are often studied separately: genomic disorder and regulatory adaptability. This bridging function may prove to be one of the most defining features of ecDNA [172, 173]. Cancer cells do not benefit merely from instability itself; they benefit when instability is translated into selectable phenotypic states. ecDNA appears exceptionally well suited for this role. It can arise from disordered chromosomes, yet rapidly become integrated into open chromatin, enhancer-rich regulatory units, nuclear hubs, and persistent transcriptional programs. In this way, ecDNA transforms structural chaos into regulatory opportunity. Future studies should therefore examine ecDNA not only as a genomic aberration, but as a mechanistic framework through which genome instability is operationalized into cancer cell plasticity. A related but still unresolved question is whether ecDNA formation intersects with therapy-induced polyploidy, depolyploidization, senescence-associated cell-state transitions, or mitotic-to-meiotic-like pathway competition in specific cancer contexts. These processes may generate chromosomal instability, micronucleation, and DNA damage states that favor circular DNA formation. However, direct evidence linking such programs to the selective production of oncogene-bearing ecDNA remains limited. Future studies combining live-cell imaging, single-cell DNA sequencing, ecDNA mapping, and lineage tracing will be required to determine whether these cell-state transitions actively promote ecDNA biogenesis or simply create a permissive background for ecDNA selection.

ecDNA-dependent vulnerabilities as a new frontier in precision oncology

A fourth major future direction is therapeutic. If ecDNA supports malignant fitness through distinctive regulatory and structural mechanisms, then ecDNA-dependent tumors may possess vulnerabilities that differ from those of chromosomally amplified cancers. Recent studies already suggest several such opportunities, including transcription–replication conflict, condensate-associated transcriptional dependencies, ecDNA-specific regulatory modules, and architectural dependencies linked to three-dimensional organization. These findings raise the possibility that ecDNA-dependent vulnerabilities could become a new class of precision oncology targets. This possibility is especially attractive because it shifts therapy away from a gene-by-gene approach. Many ecDNA-positive tumors harbor heterogeneous or multiple amplified oncogenes, making direct targeting of every driver impractical [174–176]. By contrast, targeting shared consequences of ecDNA biology, such as excessive transcriptional stress or dependence on ecDNA-associated regulatory architecture, may provide a more convergent strategy. However, success in this area will require much greater mechanistic precision. It will be necessary to determine which vulnerabilities are broadly ecDNA-associated and which are specific to certain cancer types, oncogene cargos, or architectural states. Precision oncology in this context may therefore need to evolve beyond mutation matching toward regulatory-state matching, in which ecDNA structure, chromatin features, and nuclear organization guide treatment decisions.

Concluding perspective

Taken together, the emerging literature suggests that ecDNA should no longer be considered a secondary feature of advanced cancers, but a central player in how cancers regulate, diversify, and adapt. It is at once a structural product of genome instability, a generator of regulatory innovation, a driver of intratumoral heterogeneity, a scaffold for transcriptional escalation, and a potential source of therapeutic vulnerability. These multiple roles explain why ecDNA has become such a rapidly expanding area of cancer research and why it is likely to remain central to the field in the coming years. The most important challenge now is synthesis. The next phase of ecDNA research must integrate genome structure, chromatin state, nuclear topology, single-cell heterogeneity, and treatment response into a unified framework. Only then will it be possible to fully understand how ecDNA reshapes cancer biology and how its distinctive dependencies can be therapeutically exploited. In that broader framework, ecDNA is not simply an extrachromosomal amplification mechanism. It is a regulatory system. Recognizing this may be the key step that transforms ecDNA research from descriptive cancer genomics into a mature, mechanistically grounded field of cancer epigenetics and precision oncology.
