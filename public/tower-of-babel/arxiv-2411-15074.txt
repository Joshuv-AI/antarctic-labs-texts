Learning to Stabilize Faces
J. Bednarik and E. Wood and V. Choutas and T. Bolkart and D. Wang and C. Wu and T. Beeler Google

Abstract
Nowadays, it is possible to scan faces and automatically register them with high quality. However, the resulting face meshes often need further processing: we need to stabilize them to remove unwanted head movement. Stabilization is important for tasks like game development or movie making which require facial expressions to be cleanly separated from rigid head motion. Since manual stabilization is labor-intensive, there have been attempts to automate it. However, previous methods remain impractical: they either still require some manual input, produce imprecise alignments, rely on dubious heuristics and slow optimization, or assume a temporally ordered input. Instead, we present a new learning-based approach that is simple and fully automatic. We treat stabilization as a regression problem: given two face meshes, our network directly predicts the rigid transform between them that brings their skulls into alignment. We generate synthetic training data using a 3D Morphable Model (3DMM), exploiting the fact that 3DMM parameters separate skull motion from facial skin motion. Through extensive experiments we show that our approach outperforms the state-of-the-art both quantitatively and qualitatively on the tasks of stabilizing discrete sets of facial expressions as well as dynamic facial performances. Furthermore, we provide an ablation study detailing the design choices and best practices to help others adopt our approach for their own uses. Supplementary videos can be found on the project webpage syntec-research.github.io/FaceStab .

Introduction

High-fidelity face data is generally captured using multiple cameras, where
a 3D mesh of fixed topology is non-rigidly registered to a collection of images.
This is either done using traditional multi-view stereo and model-based optimization
[
EST∗20
]
, or more modern deep learning techniques
[
BLB23
,
LLB∗21
,
LCC∗22
]
.

Often, a capture session will involve a single subject performing multiple expressions.
When comparing meshes corresponding to different expressions, one observes inter-sample vertex motion caused by two phenomena.
First, the skin deforms as the subject contracts underlying facial muscles to perform expressions, and
second, the whole head rigidly translates and rotates as the subject moves their neck and body.
This rigid motion can be small, e.g. accidental shifts of the head in a sequence of static expressions, or large, e.g. dramatic head shakes in an expressive performance.
For many use cases, we are only interested in the former, with the latter representing undesirable residual motion that pollutes the data.

The goal of
stabilization
is hence to remove unwanted rigid head motion, such as the one shown in
.
This is an essential step for
building personalized face rigs for animation
[
ARL∗09
,
SEL17
]
,
facial deformation transfer and retargeting
[
BWP13
,
LYYB13
,
CCGB22
]
, and
creating data-driven linear bases for 3D Morphable Models (3DMM)
[
EST∗20
]
.

Stabilization can be done manually by a skilled artist, but this is highly labor-intensive, so attempts have been made to automate it.
Automatic stabilization would be easy if some parts of the face never moved during expressions, but unfortunately, this is not the case.
The ultimate solution might track and align the skull itself, e.g. using X-Ray,
but since this is impractical we must do our best with the visible parts of the face.
Alas, high-quality fully automatic solutions are not yet readily available.
Existing solutions either rely on imprecise Procrustes alignment of the skin vertices
[
WBLP11
,
BWP13
,
WSS18
,
CCWL18
]
,
require manually annotated landmarks
[
BB14
,
WBGB16
]
,
have prohibitive constraints on the input format
[
FNH∗17
,
LLD18
]
,
or only produce approximate results by unposing a 3DMM
[
LBB∗17
]
.

We present the first learning-based method for stabilization.
We treat stabilization as a regression problem: given an input pair of meshes, we use a neural network to predict the unwanted rigid transform between the two underlying skulls.
We train our network using a large and diverse synthetic dataset, generated by randomly sampling both a 3DMM and a database of registered faces.
Crucially, we exploit the fact that 3DMMs have a stable skull by design, which lets us freely recombine identity and expression into novel training samples.
We show that such a model trained on synthetic data works well on real face meshes and achieves state-of-the-art accuracy both visually and quantitatively.

2
Related Work

Being an essential step of many face synthesis and analysis tasks, stabilization has been studied both on its own and as a part of a larger problem.
Traditional approaches rely on rigid Procrustes alignment
[
Gow75
]
either directly, or combined with ICP
[
AHB87
]
when mesh correspondences are not known
[
WBLP11
,
BWP13
]

To prevent bad alignments often caused by jaw movement, these approaches typically only use the upper face region for alignment.

A different body of work achieves higher accuracy than Procrustes by considering the real physiology of the human skull and skin
[
ZBGB19
]
.

[
BB14
,
WBGB16
]
first estimate the shape of the underlying skull and optimize its pose via anatomically motivated heuristics involving skin thickness and skin-bone sliding.
While accurate, the methods are not fully automatic and require per-subject one-time skull shape initialization, which involves costly manual landmark annotation.

A fully automated method was proposed in
[
LLD18
]
, which assumes that given a reference coordinate frame of the skull, every facial surface point moves around its rest position. The authors rely on a heuristic linking the sharpness of the per-point position histogram to the stabilization quality and design a corresponding optimization scheme. The method requires an input in the form of a smooth facial performance and thus cannot be used to stabilize arbitrary expression pairs.

Head stabilization is often addressed as a step within face reconstruction and tracking pipelines
[
FNH∗17
,
WSS18
,
CCWL18
]
. The method of
[
FNH∗17
]
initializes the stabilization using Procrustes alignment, followed by finding axis-aligned rotations of the reconstructed mesh that best explain the observation. This method relies on an optimization scheme and requires the availability of reconstructed eyeballs.
[
WSS18
]
convert the meshes to geometry images
[
GGH02
]
and show that Procrustes alignment can perform well as long as it is applied only on a proper region of the head which is learned in a data-driven fashion. Similarly,
[
CCWL18
]
divide the template face mesh into segments contributing to the rigid stabilization optimization with different weights, which are a function of the input. Similarly to
[
LLD18
]
, the approach only works for facial performances and thus cannot be used to stabilize expression sets.

Other approaches use 3DMMs to stabilize faces. A common practice is to fit the model to the observed data and then use the model parameters to undo any undesirable global motion due to rigid transformation or neck rotation
[
EST∗20
,
LBB∗17
]
. The main drawback is the sensitivity to the quality of the 3DMM fits. Any residual fitting error is arbitrarily distributed between the model parameters and the global rigid motion. Furthermore, 3DMM parameters underlying the registered meshes are not always available, which is typically the case for modern deep-learning-based solutions
[
BLB23
,
LLB∗21
,
LCC∗22
]
.

In contrast to prior work, our approach is fully automatic.
We do not require a temporally consistent sequence of frames; our method works on just two meshes at a time.
Finally, we only use a 3DMM to train our method; at inference time we require only 3D meshes.

3.1
Problem Formulation

Our goal is to remove unwanted rigid transformation between any two head meshes from a single subject so that the underlying skulls are aligned.

Formally, let
H=\left(V,W\right)
be a head consisting of
N_{V}
observable exterior points
V\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times N_{V}}
and
N_{W}
unobservable skull points
W\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times N_{W}}
in homogeneous coordinates.
Given two misaligned heads
H_{s}=(V_{s},W_{s})
and
H_{t}=(V_{t},W_{t})
, representing a
source
and a
target
facial expression of the same subject, our goal is to find a rigid transformation
S^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times 4}
which best aligns the source and target skulls, i.e.
S^{*}=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,min}}_{S}\mathcal{E}(W_{s},W_{t},S)
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(W_{s},W_{t},S)=\lVert SW_{s}-W_{t}\rVert_{\text{F}}.

(1)

Note, that in the ideal noiseless scenario, the skulls align perfectly and
\mathcal{E}(W_{s},W_{t},S^{*})=0
.

Since we only observe the facial exterior points
V_{i}
rather than the skull points
W_{i}
, we cannot directly minimize the energy
\mathcal{E}
of
Eq.

1
. Instead, our aim is to find a function

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}(V_{s},V_{t}):\mathbb{R}^{2\times 4\times N_{V}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4\times 4}},

(2)

which infers the transformation
S^{*}
by only considering observable vertices
V_{s},V_{t}
.

We propose modeling
\mathcal{S}
as a neural network trained in a supervised way to minimize the energy
\mathcal{E}
of
Eq.

1
for any pair of exterior vertices
(V_{s},V_{t})
.
However, for an observed
V_{i}
, the mapping
V_{i}\rightarrow W_{i}
is generally unknown and thus the energy
\mathcal{E}
cannot be readily evaluated during the training.

To address this problem, we propose using a 3DMM to sidestep the missing link between the facial exterior and the skull
V,W
, thus evaluating the energy
\mathcal{E}
of
Eq.

1
even when
W
is unknown. The details are described in the following sections.

3.2
3DMMs to the Rescue

Following the literature
[
LBB∗17
,
PKA∗09
,
WBH∗21
]
, we formulate our 3DMM as a function

\mathcal{M}_{\Psi}(\Theta):\mathbb{R}^{|\Theta|}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4\times N_{V}}

which, given model data
\Psi
, takes parameters
\Theta
and generates
N_{V}\!=\!17\,821
vertices in homogeneous coordinates.
Our 3DMM model is that of
[
WBH∗21
]
with the following differences: (i) we define a custom topology, (ii) we employ 3rd party expression blendshapes
[
Pol
]
, and (iii) we use custom artist-painted skinning weights, see
Fig.

4
and
Fig.

3
for more details. Like
[
WBH∗21
]
, our 3DMM includes the eyeballs and teeth.

Model parameters
{\Theta=\left(\beta,\phi,\theta,\tau\right)}
include identity
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{\lvert\beta\rvert}
, expression
\phi\in\mathbb{R}^{\lvert\phi\rvert}
, rotations of
K\!=\!4
joints
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times 3}
and global translation
\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Model data
\Psi=\left(T,J,\mathbf{I},\mathbf{E},\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{W},P\right)

includes the
template face vertices
T\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times N_{V}}
,
template joint locations
J\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times K}
,
linear vertex identity basis
\mathbf{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\beta|\times 4\times N_{V}}
,
linear expression basis
\mathbf{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\phi|\times 4\times N_{V}}
,
linear joint identity basis
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\beta|\times 4\times K}
,
skinning weights
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times N_{V}}
, and
skeletal hierarchy
P\in\mathbb{Z}^{K}
.

Formally, vertices are generated as follows:

\mathcal{M}_{\Psi}(\Theta)=\mathcal{L}\left(V_{\text{bind}},\mathbf{X},\mathbf{W}\right)

\mathcal{L}
is a standard Linear Blend Skinning function that transforms bind-pose vertices
V_{\text{bind}}
by skinning transforms
\mathbf{X}
, with weights
\mathbf{W}
controlling how each vertex is affected by each joint.

\mathbf{X}=\mathcal{X}\left(J_{\text{bind}},\theta,\tau;P\right)

\mathcal{X}
builds skinning transforms by propagating per-joint rotations
\theta
down the kinematic tree defined by
P
, taking bind-pose joint locations
J_{\text{bind}}
and root joint translation
\tau
into account.
Vertices and joints in the bind pose are determined using linear bases:

V_{\text{bind}}=T+\textstyle{\sum_{i=1}^{\lvert\beta\rvert}}\beta_{i}\mathbf{I}_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{\lvert\phi\rvert}\phi_{i}\mathbf{E}_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad J_{\text{bind}}=J+\sum_{i=1}^{\lvert\beta\rvert}\beta_{i}\mathbf{Q}_{i}

Data provenance.

Our vertex identity basis
\mathbf{I}
is computed by performing PCA on a dataset of registered 3D heads obtained using a multi-camera capture studio. We use a custom optimization-based registration pipeline with standard steps of detecting facial landmarks, enforcing photometric consistency and regularizing surface deformation as done before
[
FNH∗17
,
LBB∗17
,
CCWL18
]
.
The dataset contains
38\,360
frames of
2\,519
subjects performing a variety of different expressions.
Each registration contains a mesh with
N_{V}
vertices and estimated 3DMM parameters
\Theta
.
See
Fig.

1
for an example.
Our expression basis
\mathbf{E}
is FACS-like
[
EF78
]
and was authored by an artist, specifically, we purchased a set of blendshapes Polywink
[
Pol
]
. Each basis controls a localized area and guarantees a stable skull. Template joint positions
J
and skinning weights
\mathbf{W}
, too, were designed by an artist, see
Fig.

4
.
Joint identity basis
\mathbf{Q}
is computed as an average of the vertices corresponding to
\mathbf{I}
weighted by
\mathbf{W}
.

3DMMs have a stable skull.

Generally, 3DMMs do not explicitly model the skull.
But, by design, changing expression parameters
\phi
should only deform the exterior vertices
V
,
without affecting the hypothetical underlying skull
W
.
Thus, for any pair of source and target expressions where

V_{s}=\mathcal{M}(\beta,\phi_{s},\theta,\tau)
and
V_{t}=\mathcal{M}(\beta,\phi_{t},\theta,\tau)
,
the corresponding skulls should align, i.e.
W_{s}=W_{t}
.

Suppose we rigidly transform the target skin vertices
V_{t}
by any rigid transformation
Z
, the skull follows and
W_{t}=ZW_{s}
. By plugging this in the
Eq.

1
we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(W_{s},W_{t},S)=\lVert SW_{s}-ZW_{s}\rVert_{\text{F}}=\lVert(S-Z)W_{s}\rVert_{\text{F}},

(3)

and thus
Z=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,min}}_{S}\mathcal{E}(W_{s},W_{t},S)
.

In other words, our 3DMM lets us generate facial exterior vertices
V
of various expressions and arbitrary rigid transformations
Z
which can be used to evaluate the energy
\mathcal{E}
even without having access to the actual skull
W
.
We use this convenient property to generate expressions
V_{i}
perturbed by rigid transformations
Z_{i}
(see
Sec.

3.3
), and train a neural network to predict
Z_{i}
which, as we showed above, is the sought-after minimum of energy
\mathcal{E}
of
Eq.

1
.

3.3
Data-driven Transformation Predictor

We model the function
\mathcal{S}
as a neural network, which takes a pair of misaligned face vertices and produces a rigid transformation
S
.

Preprocessing.

To make the problem easier for
\mathcal{S}
we preprocess input meshes by
(i) removing less-relevant parts of the mesh that may distract the network (see
Sec.

4.5
), and
(ii) pre-aligning each mesh using a naive method.
Our preprocessing function
(V_{s},V_{t})\rightarrow(\widehat{V}_{s},\widehat{V}_{t})
is defined as follows.
Let
\mathcal{C}:\mathbb{R}^{4\times N_{V}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4\times N_{C}}
be a mask extracting
N_{C}<N_{V}
vertices corresponding to the frontal face area (see
Fig.

2
),
\mathcal{P}(X,Y)
a Procrustes alignment of points
X
to
Y
, and
\widehat{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times N_{C}}
a centered and axis aligned neutral face mesh. We obtain the
(\widehat{V}_{s},\widehat{V}_{t})
as (see also
Fig.

2
):

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}_{j}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}\left(V_{j}\right),j\in\{s,t\}

\displaystyle\widehat{V}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}\left(\widetilde{V}_{t},\widehat{T}\right),

\displaystyle\widehat{V}_{s}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}\left(\widetilde{V}_{s},\widehat{V}_{t}\right).

Although our 3DMM contains teeth, we do not include them in masked faces
\widetilde{V}_{i}
.
This is because the teeth are rarely observed in practice, so we cannot rely on them being accurately registered. After extracting the frontal face region via
\mathcal{C}
, the meshes
\widetilde{V}_{i}
have
6\,663
vertices.

Transformation predictor.

We model the function
\mathcal{S}
as neural network with trainable parameters
\Omega
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\Omega}(V_{s},V_{t})=\mathcal{R}\left(\mathcal{F}(V_{s})\oplus\mathcal{F}(V_{t})\right),

(4)

where
\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{R}^{4N_{V}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{L}
is a global feature extractor mapping flattened skin vertices
V_{i}
into a
L
-dimensional latent code
\mathbf{z}_{i}
, the operator
\oplus
represents vector concatenation and
\mathcal{R}:\mathbb{R}^{2L}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4\times 4}
is a rigid transformation predictor producing the transformation
S
consisting of rotation
R
and translation
\mathbf{t}
best aligning the input pair
(V_{s},V_{t})
. See
Fig.

2
for the architecture overview. In practice, both
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{R}
are modeled as multi-layer perceptrons (MLP) accepting a flattened vector of mesh vertices and the combined latent code respectively.

Synthesizing training data.

We synthesize training data for
\mathcal{S}_{\Omega}
using our 3DMM.
Each training sample is a random pair of faces
(\widehat{V}_{s},\widehat{V}_{t})_{i}
along with the corresponding ground-truth (GT) transformation
\overline{S}_{i}
.
Generating realistic and diverse random samples using parametric models is a long-standing open problem, as supported by the body of relevant literature
[
BKL∗16
,
PCG∗19
,
ZBX∗20
,
DRC∗22
,
TAL∗22
]
.

Our strategy is simple.
We start with a training dataset of
N
registered meshes and their estimated 3DMM parameters
\mathbf{B}=\{\beta_{i}|1\leq i\leq N\}
,
\bm{\Phi}=\{\phi_{i}|1\leq i\leq N\}
.
We then fit a normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(\beta_{\mu},\text{Diag}(\beta_{\sigma}))
to
\mathbf{B}
.
For a single training pair, we draw one random identity vector from the identity distribution (see
Fig.

3(a)
),
and draw two random expression vectors directly from
\bm{\Phi}
(see
Fig.

3(b)
).
We slightly perturb the expression vectors with a small amount of noise
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\text{Diag}(\epsilon_{\phi}\mathbf{1}))
, and pose the meshes with
\mathcal{M}
.
Sampled meshes are preprocessed the same way as shown in
Fig.

2
but each Procrustes pre-alignment
\mathcal{P}
is complemented by a small random rigid transformation to mimic the noise encountered when aligning real-world meshes.

Let
\mathcal{Q}(X,Y)\rightarrow Q\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times 4}
compute a rigid Procrustes alignment of points
X
to
Y
(i.e.
\mathcal{Q}
is equivalent to
\mathcal{P}
but retrieves the transformation), and let
\mathcal{G}(\epsilon_{R},\epsilon_{T})
sample a random rigid transformation given limits
\epsilon_{R},\epsilon_{T}\in\mathbb{R}
(see Appendix). The process of generating one data sample
(\widehat{V}_{s},\widehat{V}_{t},\overline{S})
is described in
Alg.

1
.

One might worry: is synthetic data good enough for
\mathcal{S}_{\Omega}
to generalize to real-world registered meshes?
What about the dreaded domain gap?
Fortunately, our results in
Sec.

4.4
indicate
that our dataset synthesis scheme works, and
\mathcal{S}_{\Omega}
does indeed generalize to real-world data.

Loss functions.

Since we know the GT transformation

for each source-target pair, we train the transformation predictor in a supervised way with the following loss function:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{R}+\alpha_{T}\mathcal{L}_{T},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{R}

\displaystyle=\lVert\overline{R}-R\rVert_{\text{F}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{T}

\displaystyle=\lVert\overline{\mathbf{t}}-\mathbf{t}\rVert,

where
\overline{R},\overline{\mathbf{t}}

\alpha_{T}

3.4
Training Details

The feature extractor
\mathcal{F}
is modeled as a
3
-layered Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) with sizes
(1024,512,512)
, the dimension of the latent space
L=256
and the transformation regressor
\mathcal{R}
is a
3
-layered MLP with sizes
(512,512,512)
. All MLP layers use ReLU except the last linear layer. The network is trained with the Adam optimizer and learning rate
5e^{-5}
for
125\,000
iterations. The weight parameter
\alpha_{T}
of
Eq.

5

is empirically set to
1
in all our experiments. The output translation
\mathbf{t}
is represented as a
3
D vector and the output rotation
R
as a
6
D representation
[
ZBJ∗19
]
.

Experiments

In the following text we describe the test data and metrics we use to evaluate our method (OUR), as well as previous methods we compare against.
Furthermore, we present an ablation study and analysis, thus shedding light on the inner workings of our method.

4.1
Training and Evaluation Data

We use a real-world dataset of registered meshes introduced in
Fig.

3
. The GT stabilization of the samples is unknown, however, and hard to obtain
[
BB14
,
WSS18
,
CCWL18
]
. We follow
[
BB14
]
and manually stabilize
45
expressions across
15
randomly selected subjects.

We only select those
45
expressions with visible upper teeth, as it is the only visible head part, which does not deform with changing facial expressions. For one expression per subject, we manually annotate 2D keypoints on the
6
upper frontal teeth in
5
camera views and triangulate them to obtain a 3D polyline.

Finally, to stabilize an annotated expression to the remaining ones, an operator manually transforms the source mesh until (i) the 3D teeth polyline projected to the camera view visually aligns with the visible teeth, and (ii) until the two meshes appear visually aligned in a 3D viewer too, see Fig.
7

Let
S

be the predicted and GT transformation respectively, and

and
\overline{V}=\overline{S}V_{s}
be the predicted and GT stabilized source vertices respectively. Furthermore, let

denote
j

i

{1\leq j\leq M}

Mean vertex distance (

{m_{d}=\frac{1}{NM}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\lVert\overline{V}^{(i)}_{j}-V^{(i)}_{j}\rVert}

Maximum vertex distance (

Following
[

with the maximum vertex distance averaged over the

{m_{x}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\max_{j}\lVert\overline{V}^{(i)}_{j}-V^{(i)}_{j}\rVert}

m_{AUC}

[0,5]

3DMM unposing.

Each registered face comes with estimated 3DMM parameters
so we can stabilize the pair by unposing both the source and the target to the bind pose, as done in
[

]
.
Using our 3DMM introduced in

\Theta_{x}=(\beta_{x},\phi_{x},\theta_{x},\tau_{x})

x\in\{s,t\}

with
\mathbf{0}_{\theta},\mathbf{0}_{\tau}

S
is thus fully defined by
\theta_{s},\tau_{s},\theta_{t},\tau_{t}

Since only expressions of the same subject are stabilized, we also consider the cases where we refit the 3DMM model to the source and target meshes so that the identity parameters

\beta_{s}=\beta_{t}
.
We refer to these two flavours as

and
\text{UNPOSE}_{\text{id}}

Also note that unposing a 3DMM comes with a particular drawback.
When fitting the 3DMM to the observed meshes, the non-zero fitting error is arbitrarily distributed between the global pose and the 3DMM parameters, which precludes perfect stabilization.
Furthermore, the necessary step of fitting the 3DMM to the observed meshes renders the method slower than the other approaches.

[

]
, and trained it on our own data.
We found the original formulation produced unsatisfactory results, on par with the basic
\text{PROC}_{\text{head}}

Therefore, we modified the method to encourage high global contrast and local spatial consistency, and found the best set of hyper-parameters on the validation set, please see the Appendix for more details.
We only experiment with the modified variant and refer to it as CMAP.

4.5
Ablation Study and Method Analysis

Here we discuss the choices taken to design and train the model.

Head coverage.

The registered head meshes in our dataset contain the full head and neck. However, the goal is to align the underlying skulls and furthermore, as shown in the literature
[
WBLP11
,
BWP13
]
, not all parts of the face are equally relevant for stabilization. Theoretically, an MLP-based model, which takes flattened meshes on the input, should be able to learn to ignore the irrelevant parts. However, we experimentally show this not to be the case.

We choose three regions of the input meshes shown in
Tab.

2
and train OUR only on the corresponding vertices. The result of the evaluation on the validation dataset is summarized in
Tab.

2
. It is clear that both feeding the model irrelevant mesh regions (neck and back of the head of
Full
), and pruning the parts that potentially carry a useful signal (cheeks and jaw of
Superhero
) are detrimental to the performance.

Training dataset size.

Figure 8
:
Impact of the size of the training set on the performance of our method.

As discussed in
Sec.

3.3
, a 3DMM allows us to generate a dataset of unlimited size. How big a dataset is necessary to reach a good performance? We trained OUR on the randomly generated datasets of various sizes and evaluated them on the validation dataset, the results are presented in
Fig.

8
. It can be seen that training our model on datasets larger than
~3000
samples yields diminishing returns. While OUR was trained on a dynamically generated (thus de facto infinite) dataset, in scenarios with limited computation resource, much smaller datasets will suffice.

5
Conclusion

We presented a novel learning-based approach for rigidly stabilizing face meshes with arbitrary expressions.
Synthetic data played a key part.
We designed a simple but effective scheme for synthesizing training pairs of misaligned expressive faces using a 3DMM.
We used the resulting dataset to train a neural network that directly predicts the rigid transform between any two input meshes so the underlying skulls are aligned.

Our method does not require the input meshes to be temporally consistent.
That is, any pair of arbitrarily differing expressions can be stabilized. This makes our approach generally useful for practitioners seeking to stabilize a continuous facial performance or random expression sets alike, where the typical downstream applications include character deformation transfer or building a custom human head parametric model, where spurious global transformations degrade the expressive capacity of the model.

As our method operates on independent mesh pairs, it can be heavily parallelized allowing for fast processing of large datasets. For example, a performance of
1\,000
frames is stabilized in
\sim 6
seconds on an Nvidia A100 GPU.
Finally, we show through quantitative and qualitative experiments that our approach outperforms prior work.

Limitations remain.
First, our method relies on sampling a 3DMM, which first needs to be built.
However, such a task is well understood as evidenced by a rich body of literature
[
EST∗20
,
LBB∗17
,
PKA∗09
]
.
Second, the method operates on registered meshes with a common topology and thus
it is intended to be used with production studio capture pipelines rather than in-the-wild raw 3D scans for which the method would not work out-of-the-box. Finally, to sample diverse yet realistic faces at training time, our method needs access to a large dataset of 3DMM parameters fitted to scan meshes.

Sampling Random Rigid Transformations

The Algorithm 1 in the main paper describes the process of generating the misaligned pairs of face meshes to train our method. We introduced the function
\mathcal{G}(\epsilon_{R},\epsilon_{T}):\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4\times 4}
which, given two scalars
\epsilon_{R},\epsilon_{T}
, generates a random rigid transformation

\displaystyle S_{\epsilon}=\begin{bmatrix}R&\mathbf{t}\\
\mathbf{0}^{\top}&1\end{bmatrix},

where
R\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
is a rotation matrix and
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a translation vector.

We generate the rotation matrix
R
by sampling an angle
\alpha
from a normal distribution parameterized by
\epsilon_{R}
and a random axis
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
as follows:

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\epsilon_{R})

\displaystyle x,y,z

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{U}(-1,1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}

\displaystyle=[x,y,z]^{\top}

\displaystyle S_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}(\alpha,\mathbf{a}),

where
\mathcal{A}
converts an angle and an axis to the rotation matrix. Finally, we generate the translation vector
\mathbf{t}
as follows:

\displaystyle x,y,z

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\epsilon_{T})

\displaystyle\mathbf{t}

\displaystyle=[x,y,z]^{\top}.

Learned Confidence Map Implementation

As discussed in Section 4.3. of the main paper, we re-implemented the existing work of
[
WSS18
]
, referred to as CMAP, but found the original formulation to produces unsatisfactory results. This section details the modification and training procedure we applied to boost its performance.

At its core, CMAP performs a Procrustes alignment. However, the strength of the methods come from the learned facial mask, which determines the facial regions to be considered for the rigid alignment. Let
U_{s},U_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times N}
be the source and target vertices in homogeneous coordinates,
\mathbf{w}\in[0,1]^{N}
per-vertex weights,
\mathcal{P}(U_{s},U_{t})
Procrustes alignment producing the source vertices aligned to the target, and

Hadamard product. We can compute weighted source and target vertices as

\displaystyle=W\odot U_{s}

\displaystyle=W\odot U_{t}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{data}}
