Remarkable response of metaplastic breast cancer to a novel targeted AKT inhibitor TAS-117 in a Cowden syndrome patient: a case report
Authors: Eva Godina, Marta Fernández Carcaño, Burak Uzunparmak, Thomas Pepper, Pooja Shah, Jordi Rodon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PTEN hamartoma tumour syndrome (PHTS) is characterized by a germline PTEN mutation and a substantial increase in lifetime breast cancer risk to 85% with lower age at first diagnosis. Despite the availability of agents targeting PTEN-regulated PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, there are currently no approved treatments for PHTS patients with cancer. This is the first report of a patient with triple-negative metaplastic breast cancer and PHTS in the literature. We present an exceptional response of this aggressive stage IV tumour in a 34-year-old woman with PHTS to treatment with the novel AKT1-3 inhibitor, TAS-117. Despite previous rapid progression and resistance to multi-agent chemotherapy and radiotherapy, disease stabilisation was ongoing at 3.8 years. Cell-free DNA analysis of blood samples at baseline and during treatment with TAS-117 revealed a complete marker response to treatment. Therapy has been well tolerated, and the patient continues the therapy without severe adverse events, dose reduction, or treatment interruptions. These findings support further investigation of targeted interventions for malignancies developing in the context of PHTS.

Introduction

PTEN, phosphatase and tensin homologue, is a tumour suppressor gene located on 10q23 that dampens the PI3K/AKT/mTOR growth-promoting signalling cascade [1]. The absence of functional PTEN in cancer cells leads to constitutive activation of downstream components of the PI3K pathway including the AKT and mTOR kinases [2]. Drugs targeting these kinases, or PI3K itself, might have significant therapeutic activity in PTEN-mutated cancers [3].

Germline mutations in the PTEN gene have been found in a range of syndromes, which are known under the umbrella term, PTEN hamartoma tumour syndrome (PHTS). PHTS includes Cowden syndrome (CS), Bannayan-Riley-Ruvalcaba syndrome, Proteus- and Proteus-like syndrome [4].

Genetic cancer syndromes such as PHTS affect multiple organs and can significantly reduce the quality of life and increase cancer-related mortality in young patient populations [5]. In childhood, individuals with PHTS often present with macrocephaly, developmental delay, vascular anomalies, lipomas and dermatological features such as penile freckling [6] By their late 20 s, more than 90% of individuals with PHTS have clinical manifestations of the disease [4]. Current treatment is essentially supportive for individual symptoms with a focus on age-appropriate cancer surveillance. There are currently no health authority approved treatments for PHTS. Patients with PHTS and cancer are treated as if they have sporadic disease [7].

Due to the rare nature of PHTS, there has been limited research on targeted therapies for these patients in clinical trials. However, data generated from clinical trials in cancers harbouring the more prevalent somatic PTEN mutations may be relevant for cancers with germline PTEN mutations [5]. There are currently several Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved targeted agents acting on the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. These include PI3K inhibitors such as alpelisib for breast cancer (BC), idelalisib for chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (CLL), and duvelisib for CLL and small lymphocytic lymphoma [8–11]. Three FDA-approved mTOR inhibitors, nab-sirolimus, temsirolimus, and everolimus, are available for treatment of perivascular epithelioid cell tumours and renal cell carcinoma. Everolimus is also approved for neuroendocrine tumours and BC [11].

In November 2023, FDA approved the first-in-class pan-AKT catalytic inhibitor, capivasertib, for use in combination with fulvestrant for hormone receptor (HR)-positive, human epidermal growth factor receptor-2 (HER2)-negative, locally advanced or metastatic BC with alterations in one or more of the PIK3CA, AKT1 or PTEN genes. Of note, the licensed indication (which reflects the pivotal trials) does not specify germline or somatic PTEN mutation, and anecdotal data suggests that capivasertib may have promising efficacy in patients with BC due to germline PTEN mutation [2, 12].

Based on currently available literature, AKT targeted therapy has been proposed as effective for germline PTEN-mutated cancer [13].

In this report, we describe a PHTS patient with a rare, aggressive BC (triple-negative with metaplastic features) who was successfully treated with the novel AKT-targeted therapy, TAS-117, an allosteric AKT1-3 inhibitor with no known off-target kinase inhibition, previously tested in phase I and II clinical trials [13–15].

Case presentation

Clinical course prior to TAS-117

A 34-year-old female presented in February 2020 with a rapidly enlarging, self-detected mass in her left breast. A biopsy revealed triple-negative breast cancer with metaplastic features. A staging CT scan showed a 15 × 17 cm centrally necrotic, left breast mass with thickening of the skin, and a 2.6 × 3 cm left axillary metastatic lymph node. The scan also showed a 4.3 × 5.1 cm multinodular thyroid mass, and a 2.2 cm indeterminate left renal lesion. The renal lesion was thought likely to be another primary tumour. Given the small size of the renal tumour and the co-presence of the aggressive breast tumour, active surveillance was decided for the renal lesion. A biopsy of the thyroid revealed a benign lesion, only requiring surveillance. An MRI of the abdomen showed an atypical haemangioma in the right hepatic lobe. The breast cancer stage at diagnosis was cT4cN1M0, corresponding to stage IIIB according to the AJCC/UICC 8th edition staging system. Starting a month after diagnosis, neoadjuvant chemotherapy was given with four cycles of dose-dense doxorubicin and cyclophosphamide, followed by paclitaxel and carboplatin.

At the first visit at MD Anderson in October 2020, the patient presented with bleeding and pain in a fungating left breast on physical examination, which was managed with 1600 cGy of haemostatic radiation therapy. A PET-CT scan done on the same day showed hypermetabolic lesions in both breasts, suspicious for multifocal malignancy. Four days later, a bilateral mastectomy with axillary lymph node dissection was performed. A 24.4 × 15.5 cm left breast tumour with 1/22 positive left axillary nodes was found, as well as a 15 × 15 cm right breast tumour with 0/17 positive right axillary nodes. Pathology showed a left triple-negative breast tumour, and a right oestrogen receptor-positive (ER + ), progesterone receptor-positive (PR + ), HER2-negative breast tumour.

Germline genetic testing (34-gene panel test—CancerNext+ RNA insight™, Ambry Genetics, Aliso Viejo, California, United States) revealed the presence of a known pathogenic germline PTEN mutation (c.697 C > T(p.Arg233Ter)), 601728.0002, [16], R233*. Genetic analysis of the tumour samples of the left breast showed an additional somatic pathogenic mutation in the PTEN gene, c.640 C > T(p.Gln214Ter), 601728.0015, [17], Q214* suggesting a diagnosis of multiple synchronous cancers (bilateral breast and an additional renal malignancy) in the context of Cowden syndrome. Three months after the bilateral mastectomy, in January 2021, a biopsy of the left chest wall revealed recurrence of the triple-negative BC. Treatment with capecitabine was started, but was only given once as the disease progressed. The patient was then treated with eribulin for two cycles, but progressed again. At the end of February 2021, the left chest wall lesion was resected, and the chest wall was bilaterally irradiated with 5400 cGy. A biopsy of the left kidney was performed in April 2021, revealing a papillary type I grade 2 renal cell cancer, which was ablated on the same day.

A breast ultrasound-guided biopsy of the chest wall demonstrated further recurrence of the triple-negative disease in June 2021, three months after radiotherapy, and the patient was referred to the Phase I Unit for treatment with an inhibitor of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway.

In May 2021, the patient underwent formal workup for Cowden syndrome/PHTS, which revealed additional clinical features consistent with PHTS, including benign multinodular thyroid, hepatic cysts, and benign polypoid endometrium. Additionally, dermatologic examination showed hyperkeratotic and red vascular papules on the trunk, and benign pigmented macules on the trunk and extremities. PHTS features additionally reported by the patient included macrocephaly, skin papules, and a history of learning difficulties. As per standard practice, colonoscopy was recommended, which was declined by the patient.

Following identification of the proband’s pathogenic PTEN mutation, cascade genetic testing using the CancerNext+RNAinsight™ panel (Ambry Genetics) was offered to first-degree relatives as per clinical genetics guidelines.Of note, the patient’s mother was diagnosed with colon cancer at 32 years of age, while her father was diagnosed with kidney cancer and multiple myeloma at 55 years old. However, the patient’s parents as well as her sister were all PTEN negative, (her brother had not been tested) suggesting the patient’s germline PTEN mutation was de novo, rather than inherited. The patient´s daughter underwent genetic testing and was PTEN negative, her son did not get tested. A three-generation pedigree illustrating family structure, cancer history, and PTEN testing results is provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Clinical response to TAS-117

In July 2021, the patient was enroled in a trial investigating the safety and efficacy of the novel allosteric AKT 1–3 inhibitor, TAS-117, in patients with advanced cancer, including patients with germline PTEN-mutated tumours [13]. At baseline, the patient’s scans showed a 6.5 × 4 cm multinodular thyroid with mediastinal extension, a 5.2 × 4.5 cm soft tissue mass in the left axillary region that extended to the left chest wall measuring 5.2 × 4.5 cm, a hypervascular lesion on the tail of the pancreas suspicious for a small neuroendocrine tumour, and a 2.9 cm low-density lesion in the left kidney with post ablation changes, mixed lytic and sclerotic foci in multiple cervical vertebrae consistent with bone metastases.

The patient started her treatment with TAS-117 at 20 mg on a 4 days on, 3 days off regimen, in July 2021. Since treatment initiation in July 2021, the axillary/chest wall lesion has remained stable until the present date, 3.8 years after initiating the AKT-targeted treatment (and ongoing). Follow-up scans during these years revealed no recurrent or metastatic kidney disease, a slow increase in the thyroid mass, stable liver cysts, and a stable focus in the pancreatic tail suggestive of a potential neuroendocrine tumour. Lesion evolution through time is shown in Figs. 2, 3. During treatment, the patient presented with spotting symptoms, for which an endometrial biopsy was performed in July 2022. This showed a benign polypoid endometrium. Treatment has been very well-tolerated, with good compliance and a grade-1 rash as the only treatment-related adverse event with no treatment interruptions required.

Biomarker response to TAS-117

During treatment with TAS-117, we collected blood samples for cell-free (cf) DNA analysis, one at baseline and three on treatment with TAS-117. Analysis was performed using the BostonGene Liquid Biopsy test (216-gene NGS panel). Somatic mutations decreased from 11 at baseline to 0 at Cycle 14, with tumour mutational burden declining from 15.9 to 0 mutations/Mb. The analysis revealed a complete marker response to treatment at the fourth timepoint (cycle 14 of treatment, 3 months after treatment initiation) in comparison to the baseline (see Fig. 4). Patient perspective

“I was diagnosed with PHTS and triple-negative metaplastic breast cancer in 2020. In July 2021, I was referred to Phase I Clinical Trials unit at MD Anderson where I was enroled in a trial in the context of which I received treatment with TAS-117. I have shown exceptional response to this novel therapy, whereby the disease in my chest and other organs has remained stable. This treatment has not only improved my symptoms, but I have also experienced a drastic improvement in my quality of life”.

Discussion

Our case describes a PHTS patient with triple-negative metaplastic breast cancer (MpBC) who achieved durable disease stabilization with TAS-117, despite an aggressive initial presentation and resistance to both radiotherapy and chemotherapy.

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of MpBC in a PHTS patient.

MpBC is a rare and aggressive subtype of breast cancer, characterized by triple-negative receptor status and typically occurring in women over 50 years old. It accounts for only ~1% of primary invasive breast cancers and has a poor prognosis, with a 5-year overall survival rate of approximately 60% in non-metastatic cases [18]. MpBC is often resistant to systemic therapies commonly used for other triple-negative breast cancers. The development of novel targeted and immunotherapeutic approaches may provide promising strategies to improve treatment outcomes [19].

PHTS is associated with the development of hamartomatous and tumour lesions in various organs, with a lifetime BC risk of up to 85%. BC in PHTS patients differs clinically and genomically from sporadic BC [20–23]. PHTS-BC patients are typically diagnosed younger, with multifocality and an increased risk of second primary BC [20–24]. Mean age for diagnosis of PHTS-BC is 41–46 years [22–24]. Our patient was diagnosed at 34 with an earlier onset of disease than reported in the literature. This highlights the overall trend of earlier BC onset in PHTS, most notably in our case.

Literature reports a high occurrence of bilateral BC in PHTS at 42%, in comparison to less than 20% in the sporadic BC group [22, 23]. Bilaterality was also confirmed in our patient´s PET-CT scan several months after diagnosis of the primary tumour. These findings are even more pronounced than in BRCA1 and BRCA2 carriers, where the bilateral BC rate is typically around 30% [25].

The most reported hormone receptor status in PHTS-BC is HR + , HER2-, seen in 80-100% of tumours [22, 23]. Of note, one review article [23]. reported four (13%) triple-negative BC cases. Our patient presented with triple-negative BC, a relatively rare receptor profile in PHTS. Ductal carcinoma appears to be the most common BC histological subtype in PHTS patients, with a prevalence of 85% [23, 24]. Furthermore, based on a review of the COSMIC database for PTEN mutations in our case, specifically Q214* [26] and R233* [27], ductal carcinoma would have been the most likely subtype (Supplementary Material 2). Nevertheless, our patient’s disease showed metaplastic features, representing an uncommon presentation of PHTS-BC.

Most of the PHTS-BC patients reported in literature had an early-stage BC. One of the cohorts [23] reports BC tumours stages I, II and III at similar frequencies (25%, 29%, 31%), and no stage IV tumours. Another cohort [24] reports early-stage BC at first diagnosis, where only two patients had positive lymph nodes. In contrast, our patient presented with a stage IIIB BC.

On a genomic level PHTS-BC shows higher rates of second somatic hits in PTEN (22.7% vs 5.6%), lower PIK3CA mutation frequency (22.7% vs 33.4%), and increased genomic instability compared to sporadic BC [20, 21]. Our case aligns with these findings, as we identified two hits: a germline R233* and a somatic Q214*. When comparing these mutations to available literature in the ClinGen Allele Registry, we found no cancer cases associated with Q214*, and only one ambiguous case for R233*, involving gastric and colorectal polyps [28, 29]. This may be due to the young age of patients in the registry, most of whom were under 12 years old, while cancers related to PHTS typically emerge in the 40 s [27].

Studies show that PHTS-BC have several distinct copy number variations peaks compared to sporadic BC and a distinct immunologic cell type signature suggestive of immune evasion. [20, 21].

The type of PTEN mutation impacts PHTS-BC risk, whereby truncating mutations are associated with a 2–3 times higher BC risk, compared to missense mutations [21]. Truncating PTEN mutations closer to the N-terminus result in loss of phosphatase function and, therefore, result in an inability to inhibit the PI3K/AKT pathway [22]. BC risk increases twofold when the phosphatase domain of PTEN is mutated, compared to the C2 domain [21]. In our patient, next-generation sequencing (NGS) testing identified two truncating mutations located in the C2 domain of PTEN, suggesting that the patient had a higher risk of developing cancer.

Literature on treatment of PHTS-BC is scarce, and there is no approved standard of care for this patient group. In our report we describe successful treatment with a targeted agent TAS-117. TAS-117, an AKT1-3 inhibitor demonstrated clinical efficacy in a phase II trial conducted in an Asian population, targeting PIK3CA-mutated endometrial and ovarian cancers, PIK3CA or AKT1-mutated breast cancers, and ovarian clear cell carcinoma [30]. It was further assessed in a phase I/II trial involving a Western population with advanced cancers, including those with germline PTEN mutations [13]. In the non-molecularly selected dose escalation part of the trial, seven out of 15 patients achieved stable disease, with the most notable outcome observed in the patient described here, who maintained stable disease for at least 3.8 years following treatment initiation. Of note, amongst those who underwent tumour genetic assessment in the trial, our patient was the only one who harboured a PTEN mutation (in fact, the patient had both germline and somatic PTEN mutations in an aggressive triple-negative BC setting) [15]. The study has completed enrolment and final results are being published [13].

The available literature on AKT-targeted therapy for tumours with germline PTEN mutations is limited. To our knowledge, the only other reported use of AKT inhibitors in this patient group is a case report describing two BC patients with germline PTEN mutations who were treated with capivasertib. [2, see Supplementary Material 3] One of these patients had triple-negative disease, but metaplastic features were not mentioned. Both patients achieved a durable complete response to capivasertib, a catalytic AKT1-3 inhibitor [2]. Disappointing results were reported in a phase II trial of the experimental agent miransertib, an allosteric pan-AKT1 inhibitor. Amongst nine patients with advanced BC and somatic PTEN mutation, no responses were observed, and 6-month progression-free survival was only 11% (one patient - a patient with triple-negative BC) [30]. An additional patient with both a PIK3CA and PTEN mutations also did not respond to treatment. Importantly, none of these patients were noted to have a germline PTEN mutation or clinical features of PHTS. Furthermore, although five patients had triple-negative disease, metaplastic BC was not reported [30].

Other than AKT-targeted therapy, neoadjuvant chemotherapy has been reported in three patients a report of 12 patients [22], notably one patient achieved complete pathologic response.

In summary, while the excellent response to TAS-117 in our patient is promising, it is challenging to directly extrapolate this to all PHTS-BC cases. AKT-targeting agents show varying results: TAS-117 and capivasertib showed good responses, while miransertib did not. Most PHTS-BCs are HR + , HER2- ductal carcinomas with high second-hit rates, typically diagnosed at an early stage in younger patients. However, our patient’s case deviated from these generalizations - her hormone receptor status, cancer morphology, and stage were rarer and more aggressive and chemo-resistant. Despite these worse prognostic factors, the patient responded remarkably well to TAS-117 with no side effects. It is possible that the type of mutation resulted in better response. This suggests that PHTS-BC patients may benefit from AKT-targeted therapies, even in more challenging cases.

Conclusion

Currently, patients with cancer and a germline PTEN mutation are treated as if they have sporadic disease. This report aims to provide an overview of what is currently known about PHTS-related BC and its treatment, and to highlight a case with an exceptional response to targeted therapy.

We present the first reported case of a PHTS patient with triple-negative, metaplastic breast cancer—an aggressive and rare subtype. Additionally, to our knowledge, this is the first report documenting such a durable response to targeted therapy in a PHTS patient with cancer. Treatment with the AKT inhibitor TAS-117, resulted in clinically and radiologically stable disease with a cfDNA response that was ongoing at 3.8 years. This outcome was markedly better than the patient’s previous responses to multiagent chemotherapy and radiotherapy, despite the relatively modest activity of AKT inhibitors in patients with unspecified or somatic PTEN mutations. These encouraging observations may have been due to the inactivation of both copies of PTEN in the tumour (due to concurrent germline and somatic missense mutations). These findings support further investigation of targeted interventions for malignancies in patients with PHTS.
