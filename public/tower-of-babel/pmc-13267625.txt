Operative Management and Time to Procedure for Transferred versus Direct-arrival Supracondylar Humerus Fractures at a Non–trauma-designated Children’s Hospital
Authors: David R Nelson, Alexander J Kinstedt, Zachary T Sheff, Brett W Engbrecht, Jonathan H Wilhite
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo evaluate operative management patterns and time to procedure for pediatric supracondylar humerus fractures at a non–trauma-designated children’s hospital, comparing interfacility transfers with direct arrivals, and to assess whether transfer status may serve as an early indicator of surgical resource needs.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort study of patients aged ≤14 years with supracondylar humerus fractures presenting to the emergency department of a Midwestern non–trauma-designated children’s hospital (2010-2020). Outcomes included operative management, immediate surgery during the index encounter versus deferred surgery (return visit), and receiving-hospital arrival–to–procedure start time. Firth’s penalized logistic regression was used for binary outcomes and linear regression of log-transformed time to identify independent predictors.ResultsAmong 464 patients, 136 (29.3%) were interfacility transfers. Transferred patients more frequently underwent surgery (94.1% vs 44.2%) and immediate surgery during the index encounter (91.9% vs 37.5%). Despite traveling a mean of 46 miles, transfers had shorter receiving-hospital arrival–to–procedure start times than direct arrivals (mean: 4.6 vs 6.3 h; 27% shorter; P < .001). In adjusted models, transfer status was associated with operative management (adjusted odds ratio [aOR]: 19.9; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 9.4-42.2), immediate surgery (aOR: 7.0; 95% CI: 2.0-24.2), and shorter receiving-hospital arrival–to–procedure start time (adjusted time ratio: 0.86; model P < .001). Introduction

Supracondylar humerus fractures account for approximately 60% of pediatric elbow fractures and are among the most common indications for urgent operative fracture care in children, occurring predominantly in preschool and early school-age children after a fall on an outstretched hand [1,2]. Displaced fractures (commonly Gartland types II and III) are often treated with closed reduction and percutaneous pinning to restore alignment and reduce risks of malunion, neurologic injury, and vascular compromise [2]. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons Appropriate Use Criteria (AUC) do not provide a precise operational definition of “emergent” timing for supracondylar fracture surgery, a limitation identified in studies of AUC adherence [3]. Contemporary evidence supports nonemergent fixation within approximately 24 h when vascular status is stable [4,5].

Despite the frequency of this injury, most published data on surgical timing derive from level I pediatric trauma centers [6,7]. National trends show increasing centralization: interfacility transfers of supracondylar fracture patients rose from 5.6% to 9.1% between 2006 and 2011, originating disproportionately from nontrauma, nonteaching, and nonmetropolitan hospitals [8]. Yet, pediatric trauma centers remain geographically limited and many children lack timely access to these facilities [9]. Level I centers report that transferred patients experienced longer times from initial presentation to orthopaedic consult and to surgery than direct arrivals, with transfer times averaging over 3 h independent of distance or urgency [7,10]. Whether these transfer-associated timing patterns are present across institutional settings—particularly at non–trauma-designated pediatric hospitals that manage a substantial volume of these injuries—remains unclear.

We analyzed a decade of pediatric supracondylar humerus fractures presenting to a non–trauma-designated children’s hospital that did not have a dedicated 24-h orthopaedic trauma operating room during the study period; urgent cases were performed after hours, and nonurgent cases were placed into reserved daytime add-on operating room time. Our objectives were to (1) describe operative management patterns and timing relative to the index encounter, (2) evaluate transfer status as an indicator of operative management and time to procedure, and (3) explore associations among transfer distance, transport duration, and time to surgery among within-system transfers.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study examined pediatric patients with supracondylar humerus fractures presenting to a 158-bed children’s hospital in the Midwestern United States between January 2010 and December 2020. The hospital was not trauma-designated during the study period and achieved American College of Surgeons level I pediatric trauma center status in 2023.

During the study period, pediatric supracondylar fractures were managed through rotating orthopaedic call coverage involving in-house pediatric orthopaedic surgeons and affiliated orthopaedic surgeons. Evaluation in the study-center emergency department (ED) was not mandated by institutional policy for all incoming transfers; evaluation pathway reflected clinical circumstances at presentation, accepting-surgeon workflow, and local service logistics. The institution did not have a dedicated 24-h orthopaedic trauma operating room during the study period. Reserved add-on operating room time was available during daytime hours for acute orthopaedic cases. Cases judged to require same-night surgery proceeded regardless of hour, whereas cases that could safely wait were scheduled into the next available daytime operative slot.

Four arrival pathways were observed in the source population: (1) direct arrival to the study-center ED; (2) interfacility transfer to the study-center ED; (3) interfacility transfer for direct inpatient admission under the hospitalist service with orthopaedic consultation, bypassing the study-center ED; and (4) direct presentation to an orthopaedic practice without prior ED or hospitalist admission. The primary analysis was restricted to the two ED-evaluated pathways because both reflect acute, unscheduled presentation in which the arrival-to-procedure interval captures a comparable operational construct. The study was approved by the institutional review board (Protocol No. R20210039).

Selection of participants

Patients aged ≤14 years with supracondylar humerus fractures were identified using diagnosis and procedure codes across the hospital system (n = 624). Patients were excluded from the primary ED-based analysis if they presented to a satellite ED and were not subsequently transferred to the central campus (n = 60), were transferred for direct inpatient admission under the hospitalist service without study-center ED evaluation (n = 46), or presented directly to an outpatient orthopaedic practice without study-center ED evaluation (n = 54). The final primary analytic cohort comprised 464 patients evaluated in the study-center ED—328 direct arrivals and 136 patients with prior outside-facility evaluation arriving at the study-center ED (interfacility transfers; see Measurements).

Data collection

Data were abstracted from the electronic medical record into a REDCap database [11,12]. Demographic variables included age, sex, race/ethnicity, and primary language. Race/ethnicity was caregiver-reported at registration and is presented as a social construct; it was included to describe the cohort and evaluate differences in transfer patterns. Injury characteristics included mechanism, laterality, neurovascular compromise, paresthesia, multisystem injury, and suspected abuse. Gartland classification [13] was abstracted from radiology reports, orthopaedic consultation notes, and operative reports when documented contemporaneously; documentation practices and missingness patterns are described under Fracture Classification Documentation. Gartland was not included in primary models but was evaluated in sensitivity analyses.

Measurements

Exposure

The primary exposure was the mode of presentation: direct arrival versus interfacility transfer. For the primary analysis, transfer status was defined operationally as prior outside-facility evaluation followed by arrival at the study-center ED, irrespective of whether complete formal interfacility transfer documentation was present. This definition reflects the actual case mix arriving at the study-center ED after outside evaluation, including a small number of patients who presented after outside-facility evaluation rather than through formal interfacility transfer. For formal interfacility transfers, patients were accepted by the on-call orthopaedic surgeon at the study center following remote review of imaging from the referring facility, enabling pre-arrival surgical planning and operating room coordination when operative treatment was anticipated. Transfer patients were further categorized as within system (shared electronic records enabled extraction of referring-facility timestamps) or outside system.

Distance and timing variables

The driving distance was approximated from geocoded referring facilities using Euclidean distance with road-network multipliers to provide a consistent distance estimate across sites (details in Supplement). For all patients, receiving-hospital arrival time and procedure start time were extracted from the electronic health record (EHR) (procedure start defined as operative procedure start time recorded in the EHR, distinct from operating room entry time). For within-system transfers with complete segment data (n = 44; 42 underwent immediate surgery and comprised the exploratory timing cohort), additional intervals were calculated: time at the first hospital (first-facility arrival to departure), transport time, and time at the receiving hospital prior to procedure start. For analyses of arrival and procedure-start timing, we used the following intervals: daytime (07:00-16:59), evening (17:00-21:59), overnight (22:00-06:59), and after hours (evening plus overnight, 17:00-06:59).

Fracture classification documentation

Gartland classification was abstracted from the medical record as documented at the time of clinical care. The study team did not retrospectively assign Gartland classifications from imaging when contemporaneous documentation was absent. During the 2010-2020 study period, Gartland classification at our institution was used as a clinical and teaching reference rather than as a required structured documentation element. Treatment decisions were made after clinical and radiographic assessment but the formal Gartland grade was not consistently recorded in the chart, particularly for patients managed nonoperatively who did not generate an operative report referencing fracture grade. Patients without contemporaneous Gartland documentation are reported in tables as “Not documented.”

Outcomes

Three primary endpoints were evaluated: (1) operative management (yes/no), (2) operative timing relative to the index encounter (immediate vs deferred among surgical patients), and (3) receiving-hospital arrival–to–procedure start time (among patients undergoing immediate surgery).

Operative management was defined as closed or open reduction with percutaneous pinning performed either during the index encounter or after discharge with a subsequent operative encounter within 14 days. Immediate surgery was defined as operative treatment during the index hospital encounter. Deferred surgery was defined as discharge after the index encounter followed by a subsequent scheduled operative encounter within 14 days.

Receiving-hospital arrival–to–procedure start time was defined as the elapsed interval between recorded hospital arrival and procedure start, analyzed among patients undergoing immediate surgery to characterize receiving-hospital processes preceding operative fixation.

Exploratory analysis of transfer coordination

Among within-system transfers undergoing immediate surgery (n = 42), we examined relationships between transfer distance and cumulative time to procedure start. To evaluate transport efficiency, a travel time residual was calculated as observed minus the expected transport time, with expected time estimated from linear regression of transport time on distance among within-system transfers. Positive residuals indicated slower-than-expected transport. We tested whether transport time residual was associated with receiving-hospital arrival–to–procedure start time.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated as means with standard deviations and medians with interquartile ranges for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Comparisons between groups used Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher exact tests for categorical variables.

Multivariable Firth’s penalized logistic regression was used for binary endpoints to address potential sparse data [14]. For receiving-hospital arrival–to–procedure start time, linear regression was performed with log transformation. Candidate predictors included transfer status, age, sex, daytime arrival, weekend arrival, neurovascular compromise, and multisystem injury. Initial variable selection used variance preservation optimization (a machine learning–based approach to identify informative predictors while minimizing redundancy and reducing overfitting) [15]; variables with false discovery rate–adjusted P < .05 were retained [16]. Sensitivity analyses including Gartland classification were performed among patients with documented fracture severity.

For exploratory analyses, Pearson correlations examined relationships between transfer distance and time segments. Multivariable linear regression examined predictors of receiving-hospital arrival–to–procedure start time, including distance, daytime arrival, and transport time residual.

Analyses were performed using Altair Analytics Workbench (Altair Engineering Inc., Troy, MI). Figures were generated using Python (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE); smoothed trends were estimated using locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) with a 12-mile bandwidth selected to balance local detail with smoothing stability.

Results

Study population

Of 624 pediatric patients with supracondylar humerus fractures identified during the study period, 464 met inclusion criteria (Supplemental Fig. S1). Exclusions included presentation to a satellite emergency department without subsequent transfer to the central campus (n = 60), interfacility transfer for direct inpatient admission under the hospitalist service without study-center ED evaluation (n = 46), and direct presentation to an outpatient orthopaedic practice without prior ED or hospitalist admission (n = 54).

Operative management and operative timing

Overall, 273 patients (58.8%) underwent operative management. Operative management differed by transfer status: 94.1% of transfers versus 44.2% of direct arrivals underwent operative treatment (Table 2). Of 273 surgical patients, 248 (90.8%) underwent immediate surgery and 25 (9.2%) underwent deferred surgery. Immediate surgery was more common among transfers (97.7%) than direct arrivals (84.8%).

Among the 136 patients in the transfer cohort, 8 did not undergo operative management (Supplemental Table S1). Detailed chart review showed that 3 had complete documentation of formal interfacility transfer, 1 was redirected from an urgent care facility after imaging upload, and 4 presented to the study-center ED after outside-facility evaluation. None had multisystem injury, additional fracture, or associated injury influencing management.

Among the 22 direct-arrival patients (6.7%) and 3 transfer patients (2.2%) who underwent deferred surgery after ED discharge, median time from ED discharge to deferred surgery was 3.1 days [interquartile range (IQR): 1.6-3.9] for direct arrivals and 1.7 days [IQR: 0.7-5.2] for transfers; where Gartland classification was documented, these were predominantly lower-severity patterns.

Receiving-hospital arrival–to–procedure start time

Among 248 patients undergoing immediate surgery, transferred patients had shorter unadjusted receiving-hospital arrival–to–procedure start times than direct arrivals (mean: 4.6 vs 6.3 h; median: 3.6 vs 4.5 h; P < .001). Transfers traveled a mean of 46.4 miles before arriving at the study institution. Overall mean receiving-hospital arrival–to–procedure start time was 5.4 h (median: 4.0 h).

Multivariable models

In adjusted models with false discovery rate correction, transfer status was the only covariate significantly associated with operative management (adjusted odds ratio [aOR]: 19.9, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 9.4-42.2), immediate surgery (aOR: 7.0, 95% CI: 2.0-24.2), and receiving-hospital arrival–to–procedure start time (adjusted time ratio: 0.86; β = −0.15, P < .001).

Operative details

Operative time was longer for transfers than direct arrivals (mean: 66.2 vs 54.7 min, P < .001), consistent with higher injury severity. No significant differences were observed in surgical approach, complication rates, or inpatient admission (Table 2).

Arrival and surgery timing

Among operative ED-evaluated patients, arrival timing and procedure-start timing differed markedly. Among direct ED operative patients (n = 143), 70% arrived after hours (53% evening, 17% overnight), whereas 57% underwent after-hours surgery (27% evening, 30% overnight), and 30% underwent overnight surgery. Among transfer via ED operative patients (n = 128), 73% arrived after hours (41% evening, 31% overnight), whereas 77% underwent after-hours surgery (27% evening, 51% overnight), and 51% underwent overnight surgery. After-hours arrival rates were similar between the 2 ED-evaluated cohorts but after-hours surgery rates diverged substantially, indicating that the surgery-timing difference was not solely an artifact of arrival timing (Supplemental Fig. S5).

Among the 100 patients managed through non-ED pathways during the study period, 46 were transferred for direct inpatient admission under the hospitalist service and 54 presented directly to an outpatient orthopaedic practice without prior study-center ED evaluation or hospitalist admission. All patients in both non-ED groups ultimately underwent operative management (Supplemental Tables S2 and S3). Direct-to-orthopaedic-practice patients had the shortest documented median arrival-to-procedure interval (2.1 h [IQR: 1.5-2.9]), consistent with a scheduled operative pathway and predominant daytime arrival.

The contrast between the 2 transfer pathways was particularly informative for evaluating whether bypassing the ED accelerated operative care. Hospitalist-routed transfers (n = 46) and ED-routed transfers (n = 128) arrived at the institution with statistically indistinguishable hour-of-day distributions (P = .75) but had markedly different downstream timing. The median arrival-to-procedure interval was substantially longer for hospitalist-routed transfers (8.4 h [IQR: 4.6-12.2]) than for ED-routed transfers (3.1 h [IQR: 1.9-5.2]). The surgical timing profiles diverged sharply (P < .0001; Supplemental Fig. S5) among hospitalist-routed transfers, 46% underwent next-day daytime surgery and 24% deferred surgery beyond 24 h, with only 7% operated overnight, whereas among ED-routed transfers, 47% underwent overnight surgery and 29% evening surgery, with only 2% deferred beyond 24 h. Because arrival timing was equivalent between the 2 transfer pathways, this divergence reflects routing-related differences in acuity assessment and operative scheduling rather than time of presentation. Bypassing the ED did not shorten arrival-to-procedure time in this cohort; the hospitalist pathway functioned as a planned-admission, morning OR route appropriate for less time-critical fractures.

Exploratory analysis: Transfer distance and timing

Among within-system transfers undergoing immediate surgery (n = 42), transfer distance was not correlated with cumulative time from first-hospital arrival to procedure start (r = 0.17, P = .28), including transfers < 100 miles (r = 0.02, P = .91) (Fig. 1, Supplemental Table S4). Distance was strongly correlated with transport time (r = 0.93, P < .001) but not with time at the receiving hospital (r = −0.14, P = .36).

Sensitivity analyses

In analyses restricted to patients with documented Gartland classification, Gartland severity explained much of the transfer–operative management association (transfer no longer significant) but transfer remained independently associated with immediate surgery (P = .0499) and receiving-hospital arrival–to–procedure start time (P = .004; Gartland not significant, P = .54).

Discussion

In this decade-long experience at a non–trauma-designated children’s hospital, transfer status was associated with operative management, immediate surgery, and shorter receiving-hospital arrival–to–procedure start time. Transferred patients—despite traveling a mean of 46 miles—had shorter receiving-hospital intervals to procedure start than direct arrivals. This contrasts with level I trauma center findings: Nihalani et al. [7] reported longer times from initial presentation to orthopaedic consultation and to surgery for transferred patients at a level I pediatric center. Together, these observations are consistent with the possibility that pre-arrival communication and coordination may compress downstream processes at the receiving institution in settings with different workflow constraints.

Transfer status as a pragmatic early marker

Transfer status is known immediately upon arrival, whereas Gartland classification requires radiographs and orthopaedic interpretation [17,18]. In sensitivity analyses, the association between transfer status and operative management was attenuated after adjustment for Gartland severity, consistent with confounding by fracture severity. In contrast, for receiving-hospital arrival–to–procedure start time—an operationally important endpoint—Gartland severity was not significant and the transfer association persisted after adjustment. Transfer status may, therefore, serve as a pragmatic early marker reflecting referring clinicians’ assessment of severity and may support earlier team notification while definitive classification is pending. The high nonoperative rate among direct arrivals (55.8%) underscores this distinction. Referring clinicians triaged transfers with high-surgical yield (94.1% underwent operative treatment), which may shorten diagnostic and decision-making steps after arrival while also limiting direct comparability between transfer and direct-arrival populations.

Timing in context

Mean receiving-hospital arrival–to–procedure start time (5.4 h overall; 4.6 for transfers and 6.3 for direct arrivals) compared favorably with recent level I trauma center reports, acknowledging differences in case mix and time-to-surgery definitions. In a level I cohort evaluating a dedicated early-morning orthopaedic trauma operating room, mean time from presentation to surgery ranged from 7.5 to 9.4 h [19]. In a prospective level I study, patients initially evaluated at outside facilities had longer times than direct arrivals [7]. Nihalani et al. reported a high-volume level I experience over a one-year period; differences in volume, competing trauma demand, and timing definitions may partly explain why transfer-associated delays observed in some level I settings were not observed here. During the study period, our institution had fellowship-trained pediatric orthopaedic surgeons and 24/7 pediatric postanesthesia care unit coverage but had no trauma designation and no dedicated orthopaedic trauma operating room. Despite the absence of a dedicated 24-h orthopaedic trauma operating room, receiving-hospital time to procedure compared favorably with published level I reports, suggesting that reserved daytime add-on operating room time, combined with institutional workflow and pre-arrival coordination, can support timely operative care for transferred pediatric supracondylar fractures at non–trauma-designated centers.

Coordination implications

Although interfacility transfer adds travel distance and transport time, transferred patients in our cohort experienced shorter receiving-hospital intervals from arrival to procedure start, particularly among those undergoing immediate surgery. This pattern persisted after adjustment and was not explained by geography alone: transfer distance correlated strongly with transport time but not with time spent at the receiving hospital, and distance did not correlate with cumulative time from first-hospital arrival to procedure start among within-system transfers. Within the receiving-hospital interval measured in this study, transfer distance up to approximately 100 miles was not associated with longer arrival-to-procedure time. This supports a model in which pre-arrival coordination and receiving-hospital workflow may be more relevant to in-hospital efficiency than geography alone, while recognizing that transfer still adds upstream system time not captured by this endpoint.

While we did not directly measure prenotification, orthopaedic team activation, or operating room booking timestamps, formal interfacility transfers at our institution did include surgeon-led acceptance after remote imaging review, providing a documented pre-arrival coordination step that may be reproducible at similar non–trauma-designated centers. Similar “longer total time but shorter receiving-center interval” patterns have been described in other time-sensitive conditions. For example, in ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) systems of care, transferred patients may have longer symptom-to-balloon times overall but shorter receiving-hospital door-to-balloon times, attributed in part to pre-alerting and parallelized evaluation [20,21]. Similar patterns—longer total system time but shorter receiving-center intervals—have been reported in endovascular stroke therapy [22,23] and adult interhospital transfers [24,25].

Gartland documentation as a real-world data quality finding

Gartland classification was not documented in the medical record for 65.5% of direct-arrival patients during the study period, with substantially lower non-documentation among transferred and operative cases. Prior studies have primarily evaluated the reliability and concordance of the Gartland classification when applied, whereas routine documentation completeness across clinical pathways is less well described, particularly outside high-volume pediatric trauma settings. This finding has implications for multi-institutional real-world evidence studies because inconsistent documentation of standardized injury severity may limit adjustment for case mix or lead to systematic exclusion of patients or hospitals from comparative analyses.

Arrival pathway and timing across the source population

The 2 non-ED pathway groups described in Supplemental Tables S2 and S3 illustrate how arrival pathway interacts with operative timing in this institutional context. Patients presenting directly to an outpatient orthopaedic practice had the shortest documented median arrival-to-procedure interval, consistent with a scheduled operative pathway and a noncomparable time zero. Patients transferred for direct inpatient admission under the hospitalist service more commonly underwent next-day or later surgery, consistent with planned admission and daytime operative scheduling rather than acute throughput. Notably, hospitalist-routed and ED-routed transfers arrived at the institution with comparable hour-of-day distributions but had substantially different arrival-to-procedure intervals, indicating that bypassing the ED was not associated with faster operative care for this population. These pathway-specific patterns suggest that arrival mode reflects underlying differences in clinical urgency, scheduling intent, and care logistics, supporting the decision to analyze ED-evaluated patients as a coherent acute-care cohort while reporting the non-ED pathways separately.

Operating room capacity and after-hours surgery

The high proportion of evening and overnight operative cases in this cohort warrants explicit interpretation. One reading is that our institution managed pediatric supracondylar fractures in a timely manner without a dedicated 24-h orthopaedic trauma operating room, supported by reserved daytime add-on operating room time and pre-arrival coordination for transferred patients. A complementary institutional-planning reading is that this timeliness required substantial after-hours operative work under the existing operating room structure: 57% of direct ED operative patients and 77% of transfer via ED operative patients underwent after-hours surgery, with overnight surgery rates of 30% and 51%, respectively. The contrast between transfer pathways is informative: hospitalist-routed transfers, for whom planned overnight admission was clinically appropriate, had only 7% overnight surgery and 46% next-day daytime surgery, suggesting that the daytime add-on block effectively absorbed cases when an overnight hold was clinically acceptable. By contrast, the higher overnight rate among ED-routed transfers likely reflects a combination of fracture acuity, elapsed time since injury, surgeon judgment, and operating room availability. These data cannot determine which individual overnight cases would have shifted to daytime surgery if a dedicated 24-h orthopaedic trauma operating room had been available, but they do quantify the after-hours operative burden required to achieve timely care under the existing model. Reporting both interpretations may help administrators and surgeons at similar nondesignated centers evaluate after-hours staffing, call coverage, and operating-room planning needs.

Exploratory findings on transfer timing

Among within-system transfers, cumulative time from first-hospital arrival to procedure start was not meaningfully associated with transfer distance across most observed distances. Instead, longer cumulative times clustered among patients with transport times exceeding expected duration, suggesting that potentially modifiable delays may be more related to pathway and timing factors than to geography alone. The causes of prolonged transport in our cohort are uncertain and may include mode of transport, traffic conditions, dispatch availability, or delays at the referring facility in arranging transport [26]. Late or overnight arrivals may also experience longer waits related to operating room capacity constraints [27,28]. These findings warrant prospective study to identify actionable targets for reducing avoidable delays in transfer-to-procedure pathways.

Implications for regional workforce planning

These findings may also inform regional workforce planning. Pediatric orthopaedic surgeons remain unevenly distributed across the United States, with “surgeon deserts” in rural and socioeconomically disadvantaged regions where families may travel long distances for subspecialty care [29]. In our cohort, a non–trauma-designated center achieved receiving-hospital arrival–to–procedure start times that were favorable relative to several level I reports, while acknowledging differences in case mix and timing definitions across studies. One potential explanation is that centers with established pediatric orthopaedic coverage but lower competing trauma volume may be able to prioritize transferred patients more readily, reducing receiving-hospital delays. This raises the hypothesis that there may be a functional “middle-volume” niche in which coordinated transfer pathways can deliver timely operative care without the access barriers of long-distance referral or the throughput constraints that may affect high-volume trauma centers.

Testing this hypothesis will require multicenter analyses with standardized timing definitions across institutional tiers and reliable capture of key clinical and operational variables. Our institution’s subsequent level I trauma designation was accompanied by increased emphasis on standardized documentation to support outcomes research, underscoring that rigorous data capture—including consistent fracture severity documentation (e.g., Gartland classification) and transfer process timestamps where feasible—is essential for non–trauma-designated centers to benchmark performance and to inform regional workforce and system-design decisions [30].

Differences in race and ethnicity

Observed differences in race and ethnicity between transfer and direct-arrival populations may reflect regional referral patterns and structural factors influencing access to pediatric subspecialty care. These differences should not be interpreted as biologic and warrant further study to assess equity in referral and transfer decisions.

Limitations

The retrospective single-center design limits generalizability. Transfer is a clinical decision and is therefore subject to selection bias and confounding by indication, with potential residual confounding despite adjustment. The transfer cohort included a small number of patients who presented to the study-center ED after outside-facility evaluation but without complete formal interfacility transfer documentation; these patients were retained because the operational definition of transfer status reflected prior outside-facility evaluation, consistent with the real-world case mix arriving at non–trauma-designated children’s hospitals. Gartland classification was not documented for a substantial proportion of patients—particularly among direct arrivals—likely reflecting real-world documentation practices rather than random loss of data. Although incomplete Gartland documentation does not affect ascertainment of the primary outcomes, which were derived from documented treatment decisions and operative records, it does limit our ability to fully adjust for fracture severity in regression analyses; sensitivity analyses restricted to documented cases yielded directionally consistent findings but the differential non-documentation should be considered when interpreting severity-adjusted estimates. We also lacked measures of prenotification timing, operating room booking practices, staffing levels, and surgeon availability that would directly test the coordination hypothesis; the surgeon-led imaging-review and acceptance process is therefore reported as a documented coordination step rather than a quantitatively measured causal pathway. Insurance status was not captured, an important limitation given its potential relationship to access, referral patterns, and transfer decisions. The exploratory transfer timing analysis involved a small sample (n = 42), and the observed-versus-expected transport metric—adapted from delay decomposition methods used in other time-sensitive pathways [21]—has not been previously validated for pediatric orthopaedic transfers. The primary timing outcome begins at arrival to the study-center ED and therefore measures receiving-institution efficiency rather than total elapsed time from injury, outside-facility presentation, transfer initiation, or referring-facility departure.

In summary, transferred patients at a non–trauma-designated children’s hospital had shorter receiving-hospital arrival–to–procedure start times than direct arrivals despite traveling a mean of 46 miles. These findings suggest that coordinated transfer workflows can compress in-hospital time and that transfer status—known at arrival—may serve as a practical trigger for early orthopaedic team notification and operating room planning. Prospective multicenter studies incorporating prenotification timestamps, operating room booking data, and staffing measures are needed to identify which specific coordination practices drive this efficiency and whether daytime transfer arrival further optimizes time to definitive treatment.
