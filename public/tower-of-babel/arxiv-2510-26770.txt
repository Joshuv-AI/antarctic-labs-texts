Geometry of the stability scattering diagram for P 2 \mdmathbb{P}^{2} and applications
Mark Gross, Fatemeh Rezaee

Abstract
. We give a detailed analysis of the stability scattering diagram for P 2 {\mdmathbb{P}}^{2} introduced by Bousseau in [ 7 ] . This scattering diagram lives in a subset of R 2 {\mdmathbb{R}}^{2} , and we decompose this subset into three regions, R � , R  R_{\Delta},R_{\lozenge} and R unbdd R_{\mathrm{unbdd}} . The region R � R_{\Delta} has a chamber structure whose chambers are in one-to-one correspondence with strong exceptional triples. No ray of the stability scattering diagram enters the interior of such a triangle, replicating a result of Prince [ 34 ] and generalizing a result of Bousseau. The region R  R_{\lozenge} is decomposed into diamonds , which are in one-to-one correspondence with exceptional bundles. Each diamond has a vertical diagonal corresponding to a rank zero object and is traversed by a dense set of rays. Crucially, however, there are no collisions of rays inside diamonds, making it still possible to control the scattering diagram in R  R_{\lozenge} . Finally, the behaviour of R unbdd R_{\mathrm{unbdd}} is chaotic, in that every rational point inside it is a collision of an infinite number of rays. We show that the bounded region R bdd = R � ∪ R  R_{\mathrm{bdd}}=R_{\Delta}\cup R_{\lozenge} has as upper boundary the Le Potier curve, thus showing that this curve arises naturally through the algorithmic scattering process. We give an application of these results by describing the first wall-crossing for the moduli space of one-dimensional rank zero objects on P 2 {\mdmathbb{P}}^{2} . In the sequel [ 22 ] , we apply these results to describe the full Bridgeland wall-crossing for Hilb n ⁡ ( P 2 ) \Hilb^{n}(\mdmathbb{P}^{2}) for any n n .

Introduction

Moduli spaces of sheaves on projective varieties
X
have been studied for decades and play a crucial role in algebraic and enumerative geometry.
From a modern viewpoint, it has proved to be very fruitful to study these
moduli spaces by examining how they change under variation of stability
conditions. More specifically, fix a non-singular projective
variety
X
. If
\gamma
is a fixed
Chern character, and
\sigma
is a Bridgeland stability condition

[
10
]
on

D^{b}(X)
, denote by

{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}
the moduli space of
S
-equivalence classes
of
\sigma
-semistable objects with Chern character
\gamma
on
X
.
Then as
\sigma
varies, this moduli space may undergo changes as

\sigma
passes through a “wall” in the space of Bridgeland stability
conditions. These changes typically preserve the birational equivalence
class of the moduli space, but perform some interesting non-trivial
birational operation, such as a flop. Wall-crossing of this nature for three-folds has been studied in
[
39
,
38
,
44
,
17
,
35
,
36
]
, where the authors use complicated methods to even list the walls. An initial motivation for the current article was to introduce a correspondence between the space of stability conditions on
{\mdmathbb{P}}^{3}
and a “stability scattering diagram for
{\mdmathbb{P}}^{3}
” , to handle the traditional wall-crossing more systematically. This motivation led us to study the stability scattering diagram for
{\mdmathbb{P}}^{2}
more closely and discover several interesting facts which should eventually be applicable to the higher dimensional case as well.

There is an even longer history of study of moduli spaces of sheaves
specifically on
{\mdmathbb{P}}^{2}
. The
pioneering work of Drezet and Le Potier
[
15
]
initiated
a systematic study of these moduli spaces. They explored
the structure of exceptional bundles, whose moduli spaces consist of
reduced points, and determined when a moduli space of
semi-stable sheaves of fixed Chern character is non-empty.
More recently, the wall-crossing point of view has been explored extensively for
{\mdmathbb{P}}^{2}
in many works, including
[
1
,
30
,
31
,
5
,
32
,
42
,
41
,
12
,
4
,
6
]
. In particular, the wall-crossing point of view
underpins the program of study of
the birational geometry of Hilbert schemes of points in
{\mdmathbb{P}}^{2}
initiated
in
[
1
]
.

The moduli space of Bridgeland stability conditions is a complex manifold
of dimension
3
, and thus it is rather difficult to visualize walls
and think about them. However, Bridgeland
[
10
]

constructed an action of
the universal cover of
\mathrm{GL}_{2}({\mdmathbb{R}})^{+}
on the moduli space of
stability conditions; this action preserves moduli spaces, and in particular
all walls are pulled back from the quotient, which is now of real dimension

2
.

Work of Bousseau
[
7
]

then was able to give a much cleaner picture of the structure of
wall-crossing on
{\mdmathbb{P}}^{2}
. He chose a particular two-dimensional
slice of the above-mentioned group action, and made a clever choice
of coordinates on this two-dimensional slice.
He showed that in this two-dimensional slice, one could build an
object called a
scattering diagram
which encodes the topology
of moduli spaces of semi-stable objects. Scattering diagrams
are a notion arising in the study of mirror symmetry, initially
introduced by Kontsevich and Soibelman in
[
28
]
and then given a
vast generalization
by Gross and Siebert in
[
23
]
. Since then scattering diagram
technology has found widespread applications. In particular,
Bousseau showed
[
8
]

that the scattering diagram constructed from moduli spaces of semi-stable
objects on
{\mdmathbb{P}}^{2}
was directly related to the
scattering diagram which arises in constructing the mirror to
{\mdmathbb{P}}^{2}
.
This latter scattering diagram, in the case of the mirror to
{\mdmathbb{P}}^{2}
, was first
studied by Carl, Pumperla and Siebert in
[
11
]
, and further analyzed by
Prince in
[
34
]
.
Further applications of the
{\mdmathbb{P}}^{2}
scattering diagram for enumerative
purposes were explored by Gräfnitz in
[
24
]
,

[
25
]
and by Gräfnitz, Ruddat and Zaslow in

[
27
]
.

We now turn to the specifics of this paper.
Fix a lattice
M={\mdmathbb{Z}}^{2}
and
M_{{\mdmathbb{R}}}=M\otimes_{{\mdmathbb{Z}}}{\mdmathbb{R}}
, and consider
the open subset

U:=\{(x,y)\,|\,y>-x^{2}/2\}

where
x,y
are coordinates on

M_{{\mdmathbb{R}}}
. For the purpose of this introduction,
one can view a scattering diagram

\mathfrak{D}
as a collection of
rays

(\mathfrak{d},H_{\mathfrak{d}})
, where

\mathfrak{d}\subseteq{\mdmathbb{R}}^{2}
is a ray of the form
(a_{0},b_{0})-{\mdmathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}(a,b)\subseteq\overline{U}

with
a,b\in{\mdmathbb{Z}}
relatively prime,
and
H_{\mathfrak{d}}
is a formal power series in the monomial
z^{(a,b)}\in{\mdmathbb{Q}}[M]

with no constant term.
Associated to crossing a ray from one side to the other is an automorphism
of a certain algebra. A consistent scattering diagram is one for which
a composition of automorphisms associated to wall-crossings around a closed
loop in
U
is always the identity.
[
28
]

showed that one could construct a consistent scattering
diagram given an initial set of rays via an algorithmic procedure. Even
though this procedure is quite simple (to the extent that it can easily be put
on a computer), the resulting scattering diagram
can be exceedingly complicated and it can be very difficult to control.

Bousseau’s main result
[
7
]
says that there is
a scattering diagram
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}
, constructed via the
Kontsevich-Soibelman algorithm from an initial choice of rays, such that
the functions attached to rays are generating functions for Euler
characteristics of moduli spaces of coherent sheaves on
{\mdmathbb{P}}^{2}
with
respect to various Bridgeland stability conditions. For a review of the
precise statements, see §
1.2
and in
particular Theorem
1.15
. However, roughly, if

\sigma\in U
is contained in some ray
(\mathfrak{d},H_{\mathfrak{d}})
, then
the function
H_{\mathfrak{d}}
will encode the Euler characteristic of
the moduli space of Bridgeland stable objects
{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}

for those Chern characters
\gamma
such that
Z^{\sigma}(\gamma)\in i{\mdmathbb{R}}_{>0}
, where
Z^{\sigma}
is the central charge associated to the stability
condition
\sigma
.

The fact that
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}
essentially
coincides with the scattering diagram arising from mirror symmetry
as studied in
[
11
]
and
[
34
]
is a surprisng observation.
See
[
8
, §7]
for
a heuristic explanation for why these two scattering diagrams should
agree. This proved to be a powerful
tool in
[
8
]
, where Bousseau used sheaf
theory to solve a long-standing problem about enumeration of certain kinds
of curves on
{\mdmathbb{P}}^{2}
.
Going backwards, one may use results obtained about
the mirror symmetry scattering diagram to understand
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}
.
For example, Prince in
[
34
]
proved
some structure results for the scattering diagram arising from mirror
symmetry. Let us still write
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}
for the diagram that
Prince studied, even though it arose in a completely different way.
Prince showed that there was a region of
{\mdmathbb{R}}^{2}

split into a countable number of triangles, such that the interior of
each triangle is disjoint from any ray in
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}
.

From our point of view,
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}
gives us a powerful way of
visualizing wall-crossing for moduli spaces of sheaves. The basic idea
is as follows. Fix a possible Chern character
\gamma
for a sheaf of
interest (excluding the Chern character of skyscraper sheaves). Define

\displaystyle L:=\{\sigma\in M_{{\mdmathbb{R}}}\,|\,Z^{\sigma}(\gamma)\in i{\mdmathbb{R}}\};

this is in fact a line in
M_{{\mdmathbb{R}}}
.
Then
L\cap U
has two connected components provided

{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}
is non-empty for some
\sigma
(this follows
from the Bogomolov-Gieseker inequality).
If
\sigma\in L

and the moduli space
{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}
is non-empty (and with
non-zero Euler characteristic), then there
will be a ray
(\mathfrak{d},H_{\mathfrak{d}})\in\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}

with
\sigma\in\mathfrak{d}
and
\mathfrak{d}\subseteq L
. Typically, for

\sigma
near the endpoint of one of the two connected components of

L\cap U
, the moduli space

{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}
is empty, but for
\sigma
very far from the
endpoint, the moduli space stabilizes and remains unchanged as
\sigma
goes
off to infinity in the plane. As
\sigma
moves from one extreme to the
other, the moduli space
{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}
is expected to undergo
a number of birational changes. This may or may not result in a change
of Euler characteristic, so may or may not be reflected in a change of
the functions
H_{\mathfrak{d}}
. Regardless, this gives us a strong hint as to
where to look for wall-crossing.

With this motivation in mind, in this paper we prove some finer points
of the structure of
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}
. To state the results, we
introduce some notation. We define

R:=\big\{(x,y)\in{\mdmathbb{R}}^{2}\,|\,\hbox{$y\geq nx-3n^{2}/2$ for some $n\in{\mdmathbb{Z}}$}\big\}.

This set has the feature that all rays of
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}

are contained in
R
, and the initial rays needed to construct

\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}
inductively are contained in the lines

y=nx-3n^{2}/2
for various
n
, so that the boundary of
R
is
contained in the union of these rays.
We then have a decomposition of
R
into three regions,

R=R_{\Delta}\cup R_{\lozenge}\cup R_{\mathrm{unbdd}}.

Here,
R_{\Delta}
decomposes as a countable union of triangles,
indexed by strong exceptional triples containing at most two line bundles, with the
property that the interior of each triangle is disjoint from any ray
of
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}
. This is Prince’s chamber structure.

The region

R_{\lozenge}
, on the other hand, consists of a countable union of
diamond shapes. There is a one-to-one correspondence between these diamonds
and exceptional bundles. Further, each diamond
\lozenge_{v}
has a vertex

v
we call the
base
of the diamond, and this vertex determines
the diamond
\lozenge_{v}
, hence the notation. Most crucially, the only rays
of
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}
which meet the interior of
\lozenge_{v}
have
end point at
v
, and if
\mathfrak{d}:=v-{\mdmathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}(a,b)
with
a,b\in{\mdmathbb{Z}}

intersects the interior of
\lozenge_{v}
, then there is a ray of

\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}
with support
\mathfrak{d}
. Hence,
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}

is in fact very complicated inside
\lozenge_{v}
in the sense that it is
dense, but nevertheless it is quite controlled.

Finally,
R_{\mathrm{unbdd}}
is truly chaotic. We show that
every

rational point of
R_{\mathrm{unbdd}}
is contained in an infinite
number of distinct rays of
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}
, and hence we expect
that it will be impossible to make any general statements in

R_{\mathrm{unbdd}}
. Nevertheless, as we shall see in the sequel paper

[
22
]
, where we apply the results in this paper to study wall-crossing
for
\mathrm{Hilb}^{n}({\mdmathbb{P}}^{2})
,
it is still possible to get useful information in
R_{\mathrm{unbdd}}
.
Here, in §
6
, we apply our results
to describe the first walls for moduli spaces of sheaves of rank
0
but
non-zero first Chern class. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first description of such moduli spaces, thanks to the powerful machinery of the scattering diagram.

Another lovely aspect of the scattering diagram technology is that many
classical aspects of the theory of coherent sheaves on
{\mdmathbb{P}}^{2}
appear
algorithmically. As already mentioned, the triangles of
R_{\Delta}

are indexed by strong exceptional triples. The rays which make up the boundaries
of the triangles are indexed by exceptional bundles. The
Le Potier
curve

C_{LP}

[
15
]
,
[
31
, Def. 1.6]

is visible as the union over all diamonds of the upper two
edges of the diamond (what we call the
roof
of the diamond). We
explain this last point in §
7
.

We now turn to more precise statements of our main results.

0.1.
Main results

As mentioned above, the scattering diagram
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}
is
generated by a set
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}_{\mathrm{in}}

of initial rays, this set being
in one-to-one correspondence with
\{{\mathcal{O}}(d),{\mathcal{O}}(-d)[1]\,|\,d\in{\mdmathbb{Z}}\}
.
We construct an additional set of rays, which we call the
discrete
rays
,
\mathfrak{D}_{\mathrm{discrete}}
, which are in one-to-one correspondence
with the set

\{{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{E}}[1]\,|\,\hbox{${\mathcal{E}}$ is an exceptional vector bundle of rank $>1$}\}.

We also define a set
\mathfrak{D}_{\mathrm{dense}}
of rays which are the rays
with endpoints being bases of diamonds. Then one helpful way of describing our
analysis is:

Theorem A

(See Corollaries
2.13

and
4.15
)
.

The two scattering diagrams
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}
and

\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}_{\mathrm{in}}\cup\mathfrak{D}_{\mathrm{discrete}}\cup\mathfrak{D}_{\mathrm{dense}}

are equivalent (see Definition
1.10
) in
R_{\mathrm{bdd}}:=R_{\Delta}\cup R_{\lozenge}
.

Theorem B

(See Corollary
5.4
)
.

Every point in
R_{\mathrm{unbdd}}
with rational coordinates
is contained in the
interior of a countably infinite number of non-trivial rays of

\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}
.

These statements are really statements at the numerical level, in the sense
that the scattering diagram only records the Euler characteristic of
moduli spaces and may not see non-empty moduli spaces of Euler characteristic
zero. For applications to wall-crossing, we need stronger results, and show
the following:

Theorem C

(See Theorems
2.7
and
4.8
)
.

We have the following additional information for moduli spaces for

\sigma\in R_{\mathrm{bdd}}
:

(i)

For
\sigma
in the interior of a triangle chamber of
R_{\Delta}

and
\gamma
a Chern character with
Z^{\sigma}(\gamma)\in i{\mdmathbb{R}}_{>0}
, the
moduli space
{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}
is empty.

(ii)

If
\sigma
is in the interior of a diamond chamber
\lozenge_{v}
of

R_{\lozenge}
and
\gamma
a Chern character with
Z^{\sigma}(\gamma)\in i{\mdmathbb{R}}_{>0}
, then
{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}
is non-empty only if
L

passes through
v
.

In particular, the first item yields Prince’s chamber structure as
a consequence of the properties of exceptional triples. The second item
is more involved and requires a careful combinatorial analysis.
In general, the non-empty moduli spaces for

\sigma
in the interior of a diamond are generalizations of Steiner bundles.

Here, we state several new interesting
consequences for wall-crossing for rank
zero sheaves. We note that the line
L
is vertical if and only
if
\gamma
is the Chern character of a rank zero sheaves with
non-zero first Chern class.
Furthermore,
we show in Theorem
4.1
that the line segments connecting
the base of a diamond to its apex are also vertical. We call this line segment
the
vertical diagonal
of a diamond.

Theorem D

(See Theorem
6.11
)
.

Writing a Chern character
\gamma
as the triple
({\mathrm{ch}}_{0},{\mathrm{ch}}_{1},{\mathrm{ch}}_{2})
,
let
\gamma=(0,u,v)
or
(0,-u,-v)
, for
u\in{\mdmathbb{Z}}^{>0},v\in\frac{{\mdmathbb{Z}}}{2}
.
Suppose
\gamma
is a primitive Chern character and
L

contains a vertical diagonal of a diamond. Then the value of

\sigma\in L
with the smallest
y
-coordinate such that

{\mathcal{M}}_{n\gamma}^{\sigma}\not=\emptyset
for some
n>0
is given by the
the base of the diamond, with value

\sigma=(\frac{v}{u},\frac{5}{8}-\frac{1+v^{2}}{2u^{2}})
.

Theorem E

(See Theorem
6.19
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{E}}
be
an exceptional bundle, and consider the diamond
\lozenge_{v}
whose base

v
corresponds to the exceptional bundle
{\mathcal{E}}
. We call the upper right
edge of
\lozenge_{v}
the
right roof
of
\lozenge_{v}
. Then the
image of the projection of the right roof onto the
x
-axis is the closed
interval

\left[\frac{d}{r}-\frac{3}{2},\frac{d}{r}-\frac{\sqrt{9r^{2}-4}}{2r}\right].

If
\gamma=(0,u,v)
is a Chern character of a rank zero sheaf such that

v/u
lies in the interior of this interval, then the value of

\sigma\in L
with the smallest
y
-coordinate such that

{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}_{n\gamma}\not=\emptyset
for some
n>0
is given by
the intersection of
L
with the right roof of
\lozenge_{v}
.

See §
6
for more results, which give a complete
description of first walls for (multiples of) Chern characters of
rank zero sheaves. The above result is also used in §
7

to show how the Le Potier curve arise as the union of roofs of all
diamonds.
In particular, the following theorem
follows immediately from
[
15
]

and our own results, but is a good summation of these results:

Theorem F

(See Corollary
7.2
)
.

Let
(x_{0},y_{0})\in U
be a point with rational coordinates. Then

(x_{0},y_{0})
is the intersection of two rays of

\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}
with distinct tangent lines if and only if

(x_{0},y_{0})=\left(\frac{{\mathrm{ch}}_{1}({\mathcal{E}})}{{\mathrm{ch}}_{0}({\mathcal{E}})}-\frac{3}{2},\frac{3d-\chi({\mathcal{E}})}{{\mathrm{ch}}_{0}({\mathcal{E}})}\right)

Background: Stability conditions and scattering diagram for
\mdmathbb{P}^{2}

1.1.
Preliminaries and stability conditions on
\mdmathbb{P}^{2}

We set

(1.1)

\Gamma:=K_{0}(D^{b}({\mdmathbb{P}}^{2})).

We identify the latter with a rank
3 lattice in two different ways. First, the map

\Gamma\rightarrow{\mdmathbb{Z}}^{3},\quad{\mathcal{E}}\mapsto(r({\mathcal{E}}),d({\mathcal{E}}),\chi({\mathcal{E}}))

is an isomorphism, where
r,d,\chi
denote rank, degree and Euler
characteristic respectively. Second, we use

{\mathrm{ch}}:\Gamma\rightarrow{\mdmathbb{Z}}^{2}\oplus(1/2){\mdmathbb{Z}},\quad{\mathcal{E}}\mapsto({\mathrm{ch}}_{0}({\mathcal{E}}),{\mathrm{ch}}_{1}({\mathcal{E}}),{\mathrm{ch}}_{2}({\mathcal{E}})),

where
{\mathrm{ch}}_{i}
denotes the
i
th Chern character. Note that

{\mathrm{ch}}_{0}({\mathcal{E}})=r({\mathcal{E}})
and
{\mathrm{ch}}_{1}({\mathcal{E}})=d({\mathcal{E}})
, but by Riemann-Roch,

(1.2)

\chi({\mathcal{E}})={\mathrm{ch}}_{2}({\mathcal{E}})+{3\over 2}{\mathrm{ch}}_{1}({\mathcal{E}})+{\mathrm{ch}}_{0}({\mathcal{E}})={\mathrm{ch}}_{2}({\mathcal{E}})+{3\over 2}d({\mathcal{E}})+r({\mathcal{E}}).

In particular,
{\mathrm{ch}}
induces an isomorphism with its image

(1.3)

\{(r,d,e)\in{\mdmathbb{Z}}^{\oplus 2}\oplus(1/2){\mdmathbb{Z}})\,|\,d/2+e\in{\mdmathbb{Z}}\}.

We often write
(r,d,e)
instead of

({\mathrm{ch}}_{0},{\mathrm{ch}}_{1},{\mathrm{ch}}_{2})
.

The lattice
\Gamma
has a pairing
(\cdot,\cdot)
given by

(1.4)

\left((r,d,\chi),(r^{\prime},d^{\prime},\chi^{\prime})\right)=-3dr^{\prime}-rr^{\prime}-dd^{\prime}+r\chi^{\prime}+\chi r^{\prime}.

This arises from
Riemann-Roch (see
[
7
, Lemma 2.2]
):
if
{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}
are sheaves on
{\mdmathbb{P}}^{2}
, then

(1.5)

\chi({\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime})=\sum_{i=0}^{2}(-1)^{i}\dim\operatorname{Ext}^{i}({\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime})=\left((r({\mathcal{E}}),d({\mathcal{E}}),\chi({\mathcal{E}})),(r({\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}),d({\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}),\chi({\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}))\right).

Denote by
\mathrm{Stab}(\mdmathbb{P}^{2})
the space of all Bridgeland
stability conditions on
D^{b}({\mdmathbb{P}}^{2})
. We recall from

[
2
]

a two-dimensional
subfamily of
\mathrm{Stab}({\mdmathbb{P}}^{2})
parameterized by two real parameters

\alpha\in\mdmathbb{R}^{+},\beta\in\mdmathbb{R}
.

First define the twisted Chern characters

\mathrm{ch}^{\beta}({\mathcal{E}})=(\mathrm{ch}_{0}^{\beta}({\mathcal{E}}),\mathrm{ch}_{1}^{\beta}({\mathcal{E}}),\mathrm{ch}_{2}^{\beta}({\mathcal{E}}))=e^{-\beta H}.\mathrm{ch}({\mathcal{E}}),

where
\mathrm{H}
denotes the hyperplane class.
Define a torsion pair of subcategories of
\Coh({\mdmathbb{P}}^{2})
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\beta}:=\text{{\lx@text@lbrace${\mathcal{E}}\in\mathrm{Coh}(\mdmathbb{P}^{2})\colon\mu_{\beta}({\mathcal{G}})>0$ for all ${\mathcal{E}}\twoheadrightarrow{\mathcal{G}}$}\lx@text@rbrace},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\beta}:=\text{{\lx@text@lbrace${\mathcal{E}}\in\mathrm{Coh}(\mdmathbb{P}^{2})\colon\mu_{\beta}({\mathcal{F}})\leq 0$ for all ${\mathcal{F}}\hookrightarrow{\mathcal{E}}$}\lx@text@rbrace},

where

if
\mathrm{ch}_{0}({\mathcal{E}})\neq 0
, and
\mu_{\beta}({\mathcal{E}})=+\infty
otherwise. Note

, where

These produce via tilting a

We recall this is the subcategory of

The central charge of the stability condition parameterized by

The relevant

For

Write

Definition 1.2

A

(1)

We refer to

of the ray.

(2)

H_{\mathfrak{d}}\in\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{m_{\mathfrak{d}}}

The
support
of a ray or line

is the set
\mathfrak{d}
.
A ray or line

{\mdmathbb{Q}}

For any positive integer

Definition 1.3
.

Given

, we obtain an automorphism

\theta_{H}:=\exp([H,\cdot]):\mathfrak{g}\widehat{\otimes}\widehat{R}\rightarrow\mathfrak{g}\widehat{\otimes}\widehat{R}

\theta_{H}(f):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{[H,[H,\ldots[H,f]\cdots]]\over k!},

where the

in

as
H\in\mathfrak{g}\widehat{\otimes}\mathfrak{m}
.

For the most part, we will work with
\mathfrak{g}={\mdmathbb{Q}}[M]
, with the obvious

M

Definition 1.4
.

The skew-form
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
induces a Lie
bracket
on
{\mdmathbb{Q}}[M]
, with

[z^{m_{1}},z^{m_{2}}]=\langle m_{1},m_{2}\rangle z^{m_{1}+m_{2}}.

Remark 1.5
.

We can unwind the construction of
\theta_{H}
for
H=H(z^{m},t)\in{\mdmathbb{Q}}[z^{m}]\widehat{\otimes}\mathfrak{m}
as follows, assuming that
H\equiv 0\mod z^{m}
.

First, we introduce some additional notation.
For
m\in M
,
\langle m,\cdot\rangle\in N
. Explicitly, if
m=ae_{1}+be_{2}
, then
\langle m,\cdot\rangle=3be_{1}^{*}-3ae_{2}^{*}
. We write this as
\check{m}\in N
.
Thus

[z^{m},z^{m^{\prime}}]=\check{m}(m^{\prime})z^{m+m^{\prime}}.

Writing
H=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}h_{k}(t)z^{km}
, we then see that

[H,z^{m^{\prime}}]=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}h_{k}(t)k\check{m}(m^{\prime})z^{km+m^{\prime}}=\check{m}(m^{\prime})z^{m^{\prime}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}kh_{k}(t)z^{km}\right).

Repeating, and noting that
[H,z^{m}]=0
, we obtain

\theta_{H}(z^{m^{\prime}})=z^{m^{\prime}}\exp\left(\check{m}(m^{\prime})\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}kh_{k}(t)z^{km}\right).

If we set

(1.9)

f:=\exp\left(3\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}kh_{k}(t)z^{km}\right),

then we can write

\theta_{H}=\bar{\theta}_{f},

where

\bar{\theta}_{f}(z^{m^{\prime}})=z^{m^{\prime}}f^{\check{m}(m^{\prime})/3}.

The reason for the factor of
3
in the definition of
f
and
dividing by
3
above is that if
m
is primitive, so is

\check{m}/3
.

Remark 1.20
.

In fact, in
[
7
]
, Bousseau constructs a
scattering diagram which he writes as
\mathfrak{D}_{u,v}^{{\mdmathbb{P}}^{2}}
. We will
not give the definition here, as it is somewhat more complicated than

\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}
. Crucially, however, rays in this scattering
diagram encode more information about each moduli space
{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}
,
namely the Hodge polynomial associated to the intersection cohomology
of
{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}
. Since this Hodge polynomial is non-zero if

{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}
is non-empty,

the scattering diagram sees all non-empty moduli spaces. It
is possible that in passing to
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}
we lose
some information as some moduli spaces might have Euler characteristic
zero. Bousseau’s main result is in fact about
\mathfrak{D}_{u,v}^{{\mdmathbb{P}}^{2}}
, and
the result for
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}
is then obtained after taking
Euler characteristics. In particular,
\mathfrak{D}_{u,v}^{{\mdmathbb{P}}^{2}}
is obtained
from an intial scattering diagram
\mathfrak{D}_{u,v}^{\mathrm{in}}
in the
same way that
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}
is.

Most of the results
of this paper stated for
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}
also hold for

\mathfrak{D}_{u,v}^{{\mdmathbb{P}}^{2}}
. Indeed, results about the non-existence of certain rays
are proved by showing the corresponding moduli spaces are empty, and hence these
rays don’t exist in either
\mathfrak{D}^{{\mdmathbb{P}}^{2}}_{u,v}
or
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}
. Results
about the existence of certain rays, however, are proven in

\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}
using results about scattering, and this implies
existence of rays in
\mathfrak{D}^{{\mdmathbb{P}}^{2}}_{u,v}
.

On the other hand,
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}
is easier to work with
as the type of scattering which occurs there has been much studied (and
in particular the results of
[
26
]
can be easily
applied) and is algorithmically simple to implement.

1.3.

Definition 2.3
.

(1)

An

{\mdmathbb{P}}^{2}
is a bundle

such that

\operatorname{Hom}({\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{E}})={\mdmathbb{C}}
and
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}({\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{E}})=\operatorname{Ext}^{2}({\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{E}})=0

(2)

An ordered tuple of exceptional bundles

is a
exceptional collection

if we have
\operatorname{Ext}^{\bullet}({\mathcal{E}}_{i},{\mathcal{E}}_{j})=0
for
i>j

(3)

An exceptional collection is a
strong exceptional collection

if
\operatorname{Ext}^{k}({\mathcal{E}}_{i},{\mathcal{E}}_{j})=0
for
k>0
.

Given a strong exceptional pair
({\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{F}})
,
one defines the left and right mutations via the exact sequences

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathcal{E}}}{\mathcal{F}}\rightarrow\operatorname{Hom}({\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{F}})\otimes{\mathcal{E}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{F}}\rightarrow 0,

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}\rightarrow\operatorname{Hom}({\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{F}})^{*}\otimes{\mathcal{F}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{R}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}{\mathcal{E}}\rightarrow 0,

with the second and first non-zero maps in the two rows respectively
given canonically by evaluation.

It is well-known (see
[
18
]
,
[
37
]
)
that all strong exceptional collections on
{\mdmathbb{P}}^{2}
can
be produced by a process of mutation, starting an
initial
exceptional collection
with

, replacing at each step
a strong exceptional collection
{\mathcal{C}}=({\mathcal{E}}_{0},{\mathcal{E}}_{1},{\mathcal{E}}_{2})
with
either

{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathcal{C}}}:=({\mathcal{E}}_{0},{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{1}}{\mathcal{E}}_{2},{\mathcal{E}}_{1})
or
{\mathcal{R}}_{{\mathcal{C}}}:=({\mathcal{E}}_{1},{\mathcal{R}}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{1}}{\mathcal{E}}_{0},{\mathcal{E}}_{2})
.

We recall a few facts. For a sheaf
{\mathcal{E}}
, as usual we write the slope

\mu({\mathcal{E}})=d({\mathcal{E}})/r({\mathcal{E}})
. Note that the
slope triple for the initial exceptional collection is
(\mu_{0},\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=(m,m+1,m+2)
.

(Properties of strong exceptional triples)
.

Let

(1)

The rank triple of
{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathcal{C}}}

(r_{0},3r_{0}r_{1}-r_{2},r_{1})

and the rank triple of
{\mathcal{R}}_{{\mathcal{C}}}
is
(r_{1},3r_{1}r_{2}-r_{0},r_{2})

(2)

\mu_{0}<\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}
.

(3)

Provided that
{\mathcal{C}}
is not an initial exceptional collection,
then

\mu_{1}-\mu_{0}\leq 1/2,\quad\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}\leq 1/2.

(4)

There are exact sequences

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{1}}{\mathcal{E}}_{2}\rightarrow\operatorname{Hom}({\mathcal{E}}_{1},{\mathcal{E}}_{2})\otimes{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\rightarrow{\mathcal{E}}_{2}\rightarrow 0,

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{0}\rightarrow\operatorname{Hom}({\mathcal{E}}_{0},{\mathcal{E}}_{1})^{*}\otimes{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\rightarrow{\mathcal{R}}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{1}}{\mathcal{E}}_{0}\rightarrow 0,

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{2}(-3)\rightarrow\operatorname{Hom}({\mathcal{E}}_{2}(-3),{\mathcal{E}}_{0})^{*}\otimes{\mathcal{E}}_{0}\rightarrow{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{1}}{\mathcal{E}}_{2}\rightarrow 0,

\dim\operatorname{Hom}({\mathcal{E}}_{0},{\mathcal{E}}_{1})=3(d_{1}r_{0}-d_{0}r_{1})

There is at most one exceptional bundle of a given slope.

Theorem 2.7

(Moduli emptiness)
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a strong exceptional triple not equal to

({\mathcal{O}}(m),{\mathcal{O}}(m+1),{\mathcal{O}}(m+2))
for any

, i.e., not an initial exceptional
collection. Then for
\sigma
in the interior of an edge of

\Delta_{{\mathcal{C}}}
, we have

H_{\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}},\sigma}=-\operatorname{li}_{2}(-z^{m_{{\mathcal{F}}}})

for some object

{\mathcal{F}}={\mathcal{E}}
or
{\mathcal{E}}[1]
, for
{\mathcal{E}}
an exceptional bundle.
Further, for any
\sigma\in\operatorname{Int}(\Delta_{{\mathcal{C}}})
, there are no stable
objects
{\mathcal{E}}
in
{\mathcal{A}}^{\sigma}
with
\real Z^{\sigma}(\gamma({\mathcal{E}}))=0

Proof.

Step I.

Identifying
D^{b}({\mdmathbb{P}}^{2})
with
a quiver representation category.

Since
{\mathcal{C}}=({\mathcal{E}}_{0},{\mathcal{E}}_{1},{\mathcal{E}}_{2})
is not an initial exceptional collection,
we can write it as the mutation of another strong exceptional collection

{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}=({\mathcal{E}}_{0}^{\prime},{\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{\prime},{\mathcal{E}}_{2}^{\prime})
.

Let
\mathbf{T}={\mathcal{E}}_{0}\oplus{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{E}}_{2}
, and let

A_{0}:=\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbf{T},\mathbf{T})^{\mathrm{op}}.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
be the abelian category of finitely generated left
A_{0}
-modules.
By a result due to Bondal, see
[
33
, Thm. 3.11]
,
there is an equivalence of categories

D^{b}({\mdmathbb{P}}^{2})\rightarrow D^{b}({\mathcal{A}}_{0}),\quad E\mapsto{\bf R}\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbf{T},E).

This allows us to view
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
as a subcategory of
D^{b}({\mdmathbb{P}}^{2})
.

We note that
A_{0}
is the quotient of the path algebra associated
to a quiver with vertices
v_{0},v_{1},v_{2}
and with

\dim\operatorname{Hom}({\mathcal{E}}_{i},{\mathcal{E}}_{i+1})
arrows from
v_{i+1}
to
v_{i}
.
We may label these arrows with a choice of basis
\{e_{i,j}\}
for

\operatorname{Hom}({\mathcal{E}}_{i},{\mathcal{E}}_{i+1})
.
We divide out
by an ideal of relations coming from the kernel of the composition
map

\operatorname{Hom}({\mathcal{E}}_{1},{\mathcal{E}}_{2})\otimes\operatorname{Hom}({\mathcal{E}}_{0},{\mathcal{E}}_{1})\rightarrow\operatorname{Hom}({\mathcal{E}}_{0},{\mathcal{E}}_{2}).

In other words, if
\sum_{j_{1},j_{0}}c_{j_{1},j_{0}}e_{1,j_{1}}\otimes e_{0,j_{0}}

lies in the kernel, then we include the corresponding linear combination
of paths
\sum_{j_{1},j_{0}}c_{j_{1},j_{0}}e_{1,j_{1}}e_{0,j_{0}}
in the ideal.
Thus a finite-dimensional left
A_{0}
-module consists of the data
of finite-dimensional
{\mdmathbb{C}}
-vector spaces
V_{0},V_{1},V_{2}
, and for
every arrow between
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
a linear transformation
V_{i}\rightarrow V_{j}
. These must satisfy the given relations.

Note that
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
then has simple objects
S_{0},S_{1},S_{2}
with dimension vectors

(1,0,0)
,
(0,1,0)
and
(0,0,1)
respectively, with
S_{j}
given by

V_{j}={\mdmathbb{C}}
and
V_{k}=0
,
j\not=k
.

Step II.

Identifying simple objects.

We analyze right and left mutations separately.

Right mutations.
Suppose
{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{R}}_{{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}}
.
In this case

({\mathcal{E}}_{0},{\mathcal{E}}_{1},{\mathcal{E}}_{2})=({\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{\prime},{\mathcal{R}}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{E}}_{0}^{\prime},{\mathcal{E}}_{2}^{\prime}).

The following triple

that

defines the
A_{0}
-module

for

. In other words, we need to check that

\operatorname{Ext}^{k}({\mathcal{E}}_{i},{\mathcal{S}}_{j})=\begin{cases}0&\hbox{if $k\not=0$ or $i\not=j$}\\
{\mdmathbb{C}}&k=0,i=j\end{cases}

These are all mostly straightforward using the fact that
{\mathcal{C}}
and

{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}
are strong exceptional collections. For example,

\operatorname{Ext}^{*}({\mathcal{E}}_{0},{\mathcal{S}}_{0})=\operatorname{Ext}^{*}({\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{\prime},{\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{\prime})
which is
{\mdmathbb{C}}
in degree
0

and
0
otherwise. On the other hand,
\operatorname{Ext}^{*}({\mathcal{E}}_{0},{\mathcal{S}}_{2})=\operatorname{Ext}^{*}({\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{\prime},{\mathcal{E}}_{2}^{\prime}(-3)[2])=\operatorname{Ext}^{*+2}({\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{\prime},{\mathcal{E}}_{2}^{\prime}(-3))=\operatorname{Ext}^{-*}({\mathcal{E}}_{2}^{\prime},{\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{\prime})^{\vee}=0
by Serre duality and again the
fact that
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}
is an strong exceptional collection.

The only slightly subtle calculation is that of

\operatorname{Ext}^{*}({\mathcal{E}}_{1},{\mathcal{S}}_{1})=\operatorname{Ext}^{*+1}({\mathcal{R}}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{E}}_{0}^{\prime},{\mathcal{E}}_{0}^{\prime})
. Applying

\operatorname{Ext}^{*}(\cdot,{\mathcal{E}}_{0}^{\prime})
to the defining exact sequence for

{\mathcal{R}}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{E}}_{0}^{\prime}
, we have a long exact sequence

\rightarrow\operatorname{Hom}({\mathcal{E}}_{0}^{\prime},{\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{\prime})^{*}\otimes\operatorname{Ext}^{i}({\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{\prime},{\mathcal{E}}_{0}^{\prime})\rightarrow\operatorname{Ext}^{i}({\mathcal{E}}_{0}^{\prime},{\mathcal{E}}_{0}^{\prime})\rightarrow\operatorname{Ext}^{i+1}({\mathcal{R}}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{E}}_{0}^{\prime},{\mathcal{E}}_{0}^{\prime})\rightarrow\operatorname{Hom}({\mathcal{E}}_{0}^{\prime},{\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{\prime})^{*}\otimes\operatorname{Ext}^{i+1}({\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{\prime},{\mathcal{E}}_{0}^{\prime})\rightarrow,

using

\operatorname{Ext}^{i}(\operatorname{Hom}({\mathcal{E}}_{0}^{\prime},{\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{\prime})^{*}\otimes{\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{\prime},{\mathcal{E}}_{0}^{\prime})=\operatorname{Hom}({\mathcal{E}}_{0}^{\prime},{\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{\prime})^{*}\otimes\operatorname{Ext}^{i}({\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{\prime},{\mathcal{E}}_{0}^{\prime})
.
Since
\operatorname{Ext}^{*}({\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{\prime},{\mathcal{E}}_{0}^{\prime})=0
and
{\mathcal{E}}_{0}^{\prime}
is exceptional, we obtain

\operatorname{Ext}^{i}({\mathcal{E}}_{1},{\mathcal{S}}_{1})=\begin{cases}{\mdmathbb{C}}&i=0\\
0&i\not=0.\end{cases}

The central charges of the simple objects

2.3.
Determinants of vertices and Markov numbers.

We recall a Markov triple
(x,y,z)
is a triple of positive integers satisfying
the equation

x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=3xyz.

Given a Markov triple
(x,y,z)
, any permutation is also a Markov triple.
Also, if
(x,y,z)

(x,y,3xy-z)

at

x
and
y

are defined similarly. The
Markov tree is obtained by taking as vertices Markov triples up to permutation,
and connecting two Markov triples by an edge
if they are related by permutation and one mutation.
The Markov tree has one univalent vertex, corresponding to
(1,1,1)
,
an adjacent bivalent vertex, corresponding to
(1,1,2)
, also adjacent
to
(1,2,5)
. All remaining vertices, including
(1,2,5)

Proposition 2.23

(Closed formula for
R_{j}(D)
)
.

Let
m=\lfloor j/2\rfloor
.
Then

(2.24)

\displaystyle R_{j}(D)=\sum_{l=0}^{m}(-1)^{l}{j-l-1\choose l}\big(3D\big)^{j-2l-1}.

Here we use the convention that
{m-1\choose m}=0
.

Proof.

We prove this by induction. For
j=0
and
1
, the formula obviously holds. Now, we assume that the formula holds for
j\leq p-1
, and we want to show that it holds for
j=p
as well.

Let
m^{\prime}:=\lfloor(p-1)/2\rfloor
and
m^{\prime\prime}=\lfloor(p-2)/2\rfloor
. Then

m^{\prime\prime}=m-1
and either
m=m^{\prime}
or
m^{\prime}=m^{\prime\prime}
depending on whether
p
is odd
or even respectively. We get

\displaystyle R_{p}(D)={}

\displaystyle(3D)R_{p-1}(D)-R_{p-2}(D)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{m^{\prime}}(-1)^{l}{p-l-2\choose l}\big(3D\big)^{p-2l-1}-\sum_{l=0}^{m^{\prime\prime}}(-1)^{l}{p-l-3\choose l}\big(3D\big)^{p-2l-3}

\displaystyle={}

5.
Characterization of
R_{\mathrm{unbdd}}
and moduli non-emptiness

In this section, we show that the behaviour in the unbounded region,

R_{\mathrm{unbdd}}
, is extremely chaotic.
We do this by analyzing scattering at points of
C_{\mathrm{roofs}}
, showing
that there are cones based at each such point in which every ray of rational
slope appears as the support of a ray in
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{stab}}
. We
have two different behaviours, depending on whether the point is
an
apex point
, i.e., the intersection of the left and right roofs
of the diamond, or a degenerate diamond.

First, we prove several results about scattering in the setting
of the scattering diagrams introduced at the beginning of
§

.

6.
An application: First wall-crossing for the moduli spaces of one-dimensional rank-zero objects

In this section, we will show how the scattering diagram allows us to
easily determine the location of first (actual) walls for one-dimensional rank
0

objects. Recall the
K
-theory lattice
\Gamma
as in (

),
which is identified via the Chern character with a sublattice of

{\mdmathbb{Z}}\times{\mdmathbb{Z}}\times{\mdmathbb{Z}}/2
as in (

In particular, the Chern characters of one-dimensional rank 0
objects are of the form
(0,u,v)
with either
u
even and
v
an integer,
or
u
is odd and
v
is of the form

(2k+1)/2
. We will focus on primitive Chern characters. The reason
for this
is that we eventually want to get a correspondence between the rational
vertical rays
and the structure sheaves of schemes which are unions of
C\cup Z
where

C
is a plane curve and
Z
is a finite length scheme, with
C
and
Z

having as small a degree as possible.
We call an object with a primitive Chern character a

.

Lemma 6.1

(Diamonds/one-dimensional rank
0
objects correspondence)
.

The one-dimensional rank-zero objects of
\D^{b}({\mdmathbb{P}}^{2})
correspond to vertical lines.
In particular, there is a one-to-one correspondence between (outer or degenerate diamonds) diamonds and primitive Chern characters of one-dimensional
rank-zero objects.

Proof.

The first sentence is evident as a one-dimensional rank-zero object with Chern character
(0,u,v)
, for
u\neq 0
, corresponds to the line
x=-v/u
.
For the second statement, by Theorem
4.16
, the
line
x=-v/u
intersects
C_{\mathrm{roofs}}
at one point. If this point
is the apex of an outer diamond, we associate this rank-zero object
with this outer diamond. Otherwise the intersection is a degenerate
diamond in the sense of Definition

.
This gives the desired correspondence.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

It is worth noting that rank

objects play a special role here. For example,
at a vertex
V_{{\mathcal{E}}}
for
{\mathcal{E}}
an exceptional bundle, one may check
that for any fixed

(Discrete and dense rational number/line)

Lemma 6.5

(Characterization of the Chern character of the vertical diagonals of outer/dense diamonds)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}r=u,\\
