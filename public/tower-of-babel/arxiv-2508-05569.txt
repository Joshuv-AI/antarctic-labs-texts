Harmonic analysis and automatic continuity in the context of generalized differential subalgebras
Felipe I. Flores

Abstract
For appropriate parameters k , p , q k,p,q , we introduce and systematically study the class of ( k , p , q ) (k,p,q) -differential subalgebras. This is a vast class of Banach ∗ -algebras defined by their relation with their C ∗ C^{*} -envelopes. Some examples are given by normable two-sided ∗ -ideals, domains of closed ∗ -derivations, full Hilbert algebras, and some weighted convolution algebras of various kinds. We prove that this class of algebras possesses various interesting properties, such as closedness under a functional calculus based on smooth functions, ∗ -regularity, Wiener’s property ( W ) (W) , and even properties of automatic continuity.

Introduction

Let
(\mathfrak{A},\norm{\cdot}_{\mathfrak{A}})
,
(\mathfrak{B},\norm{\cdot}_{\mathfrak{B}})
be Banach
∗
-algebras, such that
\mathfrak{A}
is a Banach
∗
-subalgebra of
\mathfrak{B}
. We say that
\mathfrak{A}
is a
differential subalgebra
of
\mathfrak{B}
if there is a constant
C>0
such that

\norm{ab}_{\mathfrak{A}}\leq C(\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{A}}\norm{b}_{\mathfrak{B}}+\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{B}}\norm{b}_{\mathfrak{A}}),\quad\textup{ for all }a,b\in\mathfrak{A}.

The concept of differential subalgebras appeared first in the articles
[
8
,
33
]
. This property gained some popularity after its appearance in the articles by Gröchenig and Klotz
[
26
,
27
]
, where it was used to provide norm bounds on the holomorphic functional calculus of
\mathfrak{A}
.

After the breakthroughs of Gröchenig and Klotz, various authors have studied examples of inclusions
\mathfrak{A}\subset\mathfrak{B}
satisfying the ‘generalized differential subalgebra condition of order
\theta
’, greatly extending their results (see
[
55
,
20
,
56
,
19
]
and the references therein). Let us explain what this property is: Given the parameter
\theta\in(0,1)
and algebras
\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B}
as above, the generalized differential subalgebra condition of order
\theta
is satisfied if there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\norm{a^2}_{\mathfrak{A}}\leq C\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{A}}^{1-\theta}\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{B}}^{1+\theta},\quad\textup{ for all }a\in\mathfrak{A}.

(1.1)

In the future and for the sake of simplicity, we will refer to these algebras simply as ‘generalized differential subalgebras’.

Recently, and during his study of
L^{1}
-algebras of Fell bundles
[
25
]
, the author encountered an example of a Banach
∗
-algebra
\mathfrak{E}
, canonically associated with a large class of Fell bundles, which satisfies

\norm{a^4}_{\mathfrak{E}}\leq C\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{E}}^{p}\norm{a}_{{\rm C^{*}}(\mathfrak{E})}^{q},\quad\textup{ for all }a\in\mathfrak{E},

(1.2)

where
p,q,C>0
and
p+q=4
. Interestingly,
\mathfrak{E}
is constructed as a weighted (Beurling type) subalgebra of a generalized convolution
\ell^{1}
-type algebra. Furthermore, the work of Shin and Sun
[
56
]
contains another example of an algebra satisfying the condition (
1.2
). Both of these examples will be discussed in the next section.

These findings mark the birth of the present article, as they show that more general differential conditions still appear in nature. It also illustrates a connection between the theory of differential subalgebras and the study of Beurling algebras, making them a natural place to study problems related to abstract harmonic analysis.

The present article has two main purposes. The first one is to introduce a very general differential condition that includes all examples above and provides a general framework, allowing us to prove results for all of them at the same time. The second purpose is to exploit the above-mentioned connection to prove harmonic analytic properties for both abstract and concrete algebras, in the hopes of shedding some light on the study of these properties. Just to illustrate the last point, we mention that our results apply to Beurling algebras associated with groups of strong subexponential growth (see Subsection
2.5
), marking the first time that such results have been obtained for this class of groups. The specific nature of these results will be discussed in the next paragraphs.

To achieve our goals, we introduce the
(k,p,q)
-differential condition. It is defined as follows, let
\mathfrak{A}
be a Banach
∗
-subalgebra of the
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathfrak{B}
such that the involution is continuous in
\mathfrak{A}
. Given
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\setminus\{1\}
and
p,q>0
such that
p+q=k
, we will say that
\mathfrak{A}
is a
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebra
of
\mathfrak{B}
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\|{a^{k}}\|_{\mathfrak{A}}\leq C\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{A}}^{p}\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{B}}^{q},\quad\textup{ for all }a\in\mathfrak{A}.

This condition clearly generalizes (
1.1
) and (
1.2
), making them particular examples of our study. In fact, the class of subalgebras satisfying such a condition is arguably very wide, and it includes examples of seemingly different natures. Concrete examples will be given in the next section of the paper, which is very much devoted to such a goal, but we anticipate some of them here: normable two-sided
∗
-ideals, domains of unbounded
∗
-derivations, full Hilbert algebras, and some weighted convolution algebras of various kinds.

We remark that one could study the same condition for general Banach algebras; there is no logical impediment to make
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
just Banach algebras satisfying the identity above. However, we chose to keep
\mathfrak{B}
a
C^{*}
-algebra to take advantage of the rigidity of
C^{*}
-norms. Indeed, we hope to convince the reader that
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebras are ‘
C^{*}
-like’, and that we can derive for them many of the properties that
C^{*}
-algebras have.

The first result in this direction is the presence of a functional calculus based on smooth functions. We recall that all Banach algebras have a holomorphic functional calculus, whereas
C^{*}
-algebras have a functional calculus based on continuous functions. In fact, one could argue that this is the defining property that
C^{*}
-algebras have. In this paper, we will construct a functional calculus for
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebras based on the Dixmier-Baillet construction
[
16
,
3
]
. Similar calculi have been constructed in
[
33
,
25
]
. In fact, Kissin and Shul’man constructed a functional calculus for differential subalgebras
[
33
]
, so our construction somehow generalizes theirs. No result like this was available for generalized differential subalgebras.

The main difference with the functional calculus of Kissin and Shul’man is that their calculus involves all smooth functions, but, in our case and given the notable increase in generality, we can only guarantee the existence of the calculus for a smaller class of functions. We proceed to describe such functions now.

Given the real parameter
\tau\in(0,1)
, we consider the algebra
\mathfrak{D}_{\tau}
, consisting of functions that satisfy

f(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{f}(t)e^{itx}{\rm d}t,

where
\widehat{f}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and

\norm{f}_{\tau}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\widehat{f}(t)|e^{|t|^{\tau}}{\rm d}t<\infty.

Under the norm
\norm{\cdot}_{\tau}
,
\mathfrak{D}_{\tau}
is a Banach subalgebra of
C_{0}(\mathbb{R})
. It is an old result of Domar that
\mathfrak{D}_{\tau}
defines a regular commutative Banach algebra
[
17
]
, meaning that this algebra is robust enough to separate points and closed sets in
\mathbb{R}
. This property makes a functional calculus based on
\mathfrak{D}_{\tau}
-functions very robust.

Our first main theorem precisely states the existence of such calculus. In it, we use
\widetilde{\mathfrak{A}}
to denote the minimal unitization of
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{A}(a)
to denote the smallest closed subalgebra containing the element
a\in\mathfrak{A}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebra of
\mathfrak{B}
. Then there exists a
\tau\in(0,1)
such that, for every
a=a^{*}\in\mathfrak{A}
, one has

\norm{e^{ita}}_{\widetilde{\mathfrak{A}}}=O(e^{|t|^{\tau}}),\quad\textup{ as }|t|\to\infty.

Furthermore, the following are true.

(i)

The following Bochner integral exists in
\widetilde{\mathfrak{A}}(a,1)
:

f(a)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{f}(t)e^{ita}{\rm d}t.

Moreover, if
f(0)=0
, then
f(a)\in\mathfrak{A}(a)
.

(ii)

For any non-degenerate
∗
-representation
\Pi:\widetilde{\mathfrak{A}}(a,1)\to\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})
, we have
{\Pi}\big(f(a)\big)=f\big(\Pi(a)\big)
.

(iii)

{\rm Spec}_{\widetilde{\mathfrak{A}}(a,1)}\big(f(a)\big)=f\big({\rm Spec}_{\widetilde{\mathfrak{A}}(a,1)}(a)\big)
.

(iv)

There is a (continuous)
∗
-homomorphism
\varphi_{a}:\mathfrak{D}_{\tau}\to\widetilde{\mathfrak{A}}(a,1)
defined by
\varphi_{a}(f)=f(a)
, such that
\varphi_{a}(g)=1
if
g\equiv 1
on a neighborhood of
{\rm Spec}_{\widetilde{\mathfrak{A}}(a,1)}(a)
and
\varphi_{a}(g)=a
if
g(x)=x
on a neighborhood of
{\rm Spec}_{\widetilde{\mathfrak{A}}(a,1)}(a)
.

∗
-regularity, and representation-theoretic properties like the Wiener property.

To be concrete, an

(ii)

Theorem 1.2
.

Let

Based on the results from

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebra of

with the property that all closed
∗
-ideals of finite codimension have a bounded left approximate identity. Let

be a weak Banach

-bimodule and
\theta:\mathfrak{A}\to\mathcal{X}
an

-intertwining operator. Then
\theta
is continuous if and only if
\mathscr{I}(\theta)
is closed. In particular,
\theta
is continuous if
\mathcal{X}
is a Banach
\mathfrak{A}
-bimodule.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a unital
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebra of
\mathfrak{B}
. Also consider a Banach
\mathfrak{A}
-bimodule
\mathcal{X}_{1}
, a weak Banach
\mathfrak{A}
-bimodule
\mathcal{X}_{2}
, and an
\mathfrak{A}
-intertwining operator
\theta:\mathcal{X}_{1}\to\mathcal{X}_{2}

has no proper closed two-sided ideals with finite codimension, then
\theta
is continuous.

If one applies the previous theorem to, say, the setting of Subsection

, one finds that every intertwining operator over the natural weighted

(

by any nonamenable hyperbolic group, as their reduced

are central to the theory, as they relate to the (non-)existence of discontinuous homomorphisms

.

2
The class of
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebras

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(\mathfrak{A},\norm{\cdot}_{\mathfrak{A}})
be a Banach
∗
-subalgebra of the
C^{*}
-algebra
(\mathfrak{B},\norm{\cdot}_{\mathfrak{B}})
such that the involution is continuous in
\mathfrak{A}
. Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\setminus\{1\}
and
p,q>0
such that
p+q=k
. We say that
\mathfrak{A}
is a
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebra
of
\mathfrak{B}
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\|{a^{k}}\|_{\mathfrak{A}}\leq C\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{A}}^{p}\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{B}}^{q},\quad\textup{ for all }a\in\mathfrak{A}.

(2.1)

The following lemma shows the result of applying the identity (
2.1
) successively.

Lemma 2.2
.

If
\mathfrak{A}
is a
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebra of
\mathfrak{B}
. Then there exists a constant
C>0

\|a^{k^{n}}\|_{\mathfrak{A}}\leq C^{n}\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{A}}^{p^{n}}\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{B}}^{k^{n}-p^{n}}

holds for all
a\in\mathfrak{A}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
.

Proof.

We will prove it by induction. Clearly
C
is the constant given by the definition of
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebra. For
n=1
, the identity is precisely the equality in (
2.1
). For
n=m+1
, we have

\displaystyle\big\|a^{k^{m+1}}\big\|_{\mathfrak{A}}

\displaystyle=\big\|(a^{k})^{k^{m}}\big\|_{\mathfrak{A}}

\displaystyle\leq C^{m}\big\|a^{k}\big\|_{\mathfrak{A}}^{p^{m}}\big\|a^{k}\big\|_{\mathfrak{B}}^{k^{m}-p^{m}}

\displaystyle\leq C^{m+1}\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{A}}^{p^{m+1}}\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{B}}^{p^{m}q}\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{B}}^{k^{m+1}-kp^{m}}

\displaystyle=C^{m+1}\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{A}}^{p^{m+1}}\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{B}}^{k^{m+1}+(q-k)p^{m}}=C^{m+1}\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{A}}^{p^{m+1}}\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{B}}^{k^{m+1}-p^{m+1}},

which proves the claim.
∎

Let us now argue that this class of Banach
∗
-algebras is vast and interesting. Obviously, we plan to do so by providing lots of examples of different natures.

2.1 Normable ideals

Let
\mathfrak{B}
be a
C^{*}
-algebra. A two-sided
∗
-ideal
I\subset\mathfrak{B}
is called a
normable ideal
if there exists a norm
\norm{\cdot}_{I}
on
I
that makes it a Banach
∗
-algebra and such that
I\ni a\mapsto a^{*}\in I
is continuous and

\norm{ax}_{I}\leq\norm{a}_{B}\norm{x}_{I},\quad\text{ and }\quad\norm{xa}_{I}\leq\norm{x}_{I}\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{B}},\quad\text{ for all }a\in\mathfrak{B},x\in I.

If
I\subset\mathfrak{B}
is a normable ideal, then it is obviously a
(2,1,1)
-differential subalgebra.

Example 2.3
.

Given a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and
p\in[1,\infty)
, the class of Schatten
p
-operators
S_{p}(\mathcal{H})
is a dense
∗
-ideal of the
C^{*}
-algebra of compact operators
\mathbb{K}(\mathcal{H})
; moreover, it is a normable ideal under the standard Schatten
p
-norm

\norm{T}_{S_{p}(\mathcal{H})}={\rm Tr}(|T|^{p})^{1/p}.

Example 2.4
.

A proper
H^{*}
-algebra
\mathfrak{A}
is a Banach
∗
-algebra which is also a Hilbert space under the same norm and where
a^{*}
is the unique element in
\mathfrak{A}
satisfying

\langle ab,c\rangle=\langle b,a^{*}c\rangle\textup{
and }\langle ba,c\rangle=\langle b,ca^{*}\rangle\textup{, for all }b,c\in\mathfrak{A}.

Then the left regular representation
\mathfrak{A}\ni a\mapsto L_{a}\in\mathbb{B}(\mathfrak{A})
is a contractive
∗
-monomorphism. We then have

\norm{ab}_{\mathfrak{A}}\leq\norm{L_a}_{\mathbb{B}(\mathfrak{A})}\norm{b}_{\mathfrak{A}},\textup{ for all }a,b\in\mathfrak{A}.

Furthermore, the involution map
a\mapsto a^{*}
must necessarily be isometric
[
48
, pg. 1211]
and therefore the reversed inequality also holds:

\norm{ab}_{\mathfrak{A}}\leq\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{A}}\norm{L_b}_{\mathbb{B}(\mathfrak{A})},\textup{ for all }a,b\in\mathfrak{A}.

So that
\mathfrak{A}
is a differential subalgebra of
\mathfrak{B}=\overline{L(A)}^{\norm{\cdot}_{\mathbb{B}(\mathfrak{A})}}\cong{\rm C^{*}}(\mathfrak{A})
. In particular, when
K
is a compact group, equipped with the normalized Haar measure,
L^{2}(K)
becomes a proper
H^{*}
-algebra, with the operations inherited from
L^{1}(K)
.

2.2 Full Hilbert algebras

Example
2.4
can be generalized to the class of Hilbert algebras, and we will do that here. The following definition is classical and taken from
[
48
, pg. 1214]
. For a general treatment of Hilbert algebras and their properties, we recommend
[
48
, Chapter 11.7]
.

Definition 2.5
.

A
Hilbert algebra
is a
∗
-algebra
\mathfrak{A}
that is a pre-Hilbert space under the same linear structure and an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
satisfying:

(i)

One has
\langle ab,c\rangle=\langle b,a^{*}c\rangle,
for all
a,b,c\in\mathfrak{A}
.

(ii)

For every
a\in\mathfrak{A}
, there exists a constant
C_{a}>0
such that
\norm{ab}_{\mathcal{H}}\leq C_{a}\norm{b}_{\mathcal{H}}
, for all
b\in\mathfrak{A}
. Here
\norm{a}_{\mathcal{H}}=\langle a,a\rangle^{1/2}
.

(iii)

\langle a,b\rangle=\langle b^{*},a^{*}\rangle,
for all
a,b\in\mathfrak{A}
.

(iv)

\mathfrak{A}^{2}=\Span\{ab\mid a,b\in\mathfrak{A}\}
is dense in
\mathfrak{A}
, under the topology given by the inner product.

Hilbert algebras are a central object of study in von Neumann algebra theory, at least in the setting of type
\mathrm{III}
factors and Tomita-Takesaki theory (see
[
60
, Chapter VI]
). The most obvious example is precisely
L^{2}(G)
, for a compact group
G
. The following example takes that idea as a starting point.

Example 2.6
.

let
G
be an unimodular locally compact group and take
\mathfrak{A}=C_{\rm c}(G)
, the set of compactly supported, complex-valued functions on
G
. Then
\mathfrak{A}
is a Hilbert algebra under the operations

\Phi*\Psi(x)=\int_{G}\Phi(y)\Psi(y^{-1}x){\rm d}y\quad\text{ and }\quad\Phi^{*}(x)=\Phi(x^{-1})^{*},

valid for all
\Phi,\Psi\in\mathfrak{A}
and
x\in G
. The inner product is obviously given by

\langle\Phi,\Psi\rangle=\int_{G}\Phi(x)\overline{\Psi(x)}{\rm d}x,\quad\forall\Phi,\Psi\in\mathfrak{A}.

It is obvious from the definition that, on a Hilbert algebra
\mathfrak{A}
whose Hilbert space completion is
\mathcal{H}
, every element
a\in\mathfrak{A}
defines bounded operators
L_{a},R_{a}\in\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})
given by the extension of left/right multiplication:

L_{a}(b)=ab\quad\text{ and }\quad R_{a}(b)=ba,\quad\forall b\in\mathfrak{B}.

The definition of full Hilbert algebras, however, depends on the notion of bounded vectors, which is a slight generalization.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a Hilbert algebra with Hilbert space completion
\mathcal{H}
. For
x\in\mathcal{H}
, define the operators
L_{x},R_{x}:\mathfrak{A}\subset\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
by

L_{x}(a)=R_{a}(x)\quad\text{ and }\quad R_{x}(a)=L_{a}(x),\quad\forall a\in\mathfrak{B}.

The vector
x\in\mathcal{H}
is said to be bounded if both
L_{x}
and
R_{x}
define bounded operators in
\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})
. The set of bounded vectors in
\mathcal{H}
is denoted by
\overline{\mathfrak{A}}
, and we say that
\mathfrak{A}
is
full
if
\mathfrak{A}=\overline{\mathfrak{A}}
.

If
\mathfrak{A}
is a full Hilbert algebra, then endowing it with the norm

\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{A}}=\norm{a}_{\mathcal{H}}+\norm{L_a}_{\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})},

makes
\mathfrak{A}
a Banach
∗
-algebra with isometric involution
[
48
, Theorem 11.7.10]
. It is obvious that
\mathfrak{A}
is a
(2,1,1)
-differential subalgebra of
\mathfrak{B}=\overline{\{L_{a}\mid a\in\mathfrak{A}\}}^{\norm{\cdot}_{\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})}}
. Indeed, one has

\displaystyle\norm{a^2}_{\mathfrak{A}}

\displaystyle=\norm{a^2}_{\mathcal{H}}+\norm{L_{a^2}}_{\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})}

\displaystyle\leq\norm{L_a}_{\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})}\norm{a}_{\mathcal{H}}+\norm{L_a}_{\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})}^{2}\leq\norm{L_a}_{\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})}\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{A}},

for all
a\in\mathfrak{A}
.

2.3 Domains of closed
∗
-derivations

Following
[
11
,
32
]
, let us define (densely defined) unbounded derivations on
C^{*}
-algebras. In order to distinguish the situation from the (everywhere defined) derivations of Section
4
, we will denote them with the symbol
\delta
, instead of just
D
.

Let
\mathfrak{B}
be a
C^{*}
-algebra. A closed derivation
\delta
of
\mathfrak{B}
is a linear mapping from a dense subalgebra
\mathfrak{A}
into
\mathfrak{B}
that satisfies

(i)

\delta(ab)=a\delta(b)+\delta(a)b
, for all
a,b\in\mathfrak{A}
.

(ii)

If
a_{n}\in\mathfrak{A}
,
a_{n}\to a
and
\delta(a_{n})\to b
, then
a\in\mathfrak{A}
and
b=\delta(a)
.

If, in addition,
x\in\mathfrak{A}
implies
x^{*}\in\mathfrak{A}
and
\delta(x^{*})=\delta(x)^{*}
, then
\delta
is called a closed
∗
-derivation.

If
\delta
is a closed
∗
-derivation, then
\mathfrak{A}
is a Banach
∗
-algebra when endowed with the norm

\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{A}}=\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{B}}+\norm{\delta(a)}_{\mathfrak{B}}.

The obvious example of a closed
∗
-derivation is given by
\delta(f)=f^{\prime}
on
\mathfrak{B}=C(\mathbb{T})
, and in this case,
\mathfrak{A}
is precisely
C^{1}(\mathbb{T})
, with its usual norm. For this reason, derivations are usually viewed as a noncommutative differential operator and therefore form a central object in the study of noncommutative geometry
[
14
]
.

It is easy to observe that
\mathfrak{A}
is a
(2,1,1)
-differential subalgebra of
\mathfrak{B}
. Indeed, it is shown by the following simple calculation, valid for all
a\in\mathfrak{A}
.

\displaystyle\norm{a^2}_{\mathfrak{A}}

\displaystyle=\norm{a^2}_{\mathfrak{B}}+\norm{\delta(a^2)}_{\mathfrak{B}}

\displaystyle\leq\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{B}}^{2}+\norm{a\delta(a)}_{\mathfrak{B}}+\norm{\delta(a)a}_{\mathfrak{B}}

\displaystyle\leq\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{B}}\big(\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{B}}+2\norm{\delta(a)}_{\mathfrak{B}}\big)\leq 2\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{B}}\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{A}}.

In this section,

The universal

\big(\Phi*\Psi\big)(x)=\sum_{y\in G}\Phi(y)\bullet\Psi(y^{-1}x)

\Phi^{*}(x)=\Phi(x^{-1})^{\bullet}\,,

in terms of the operations

on the Fell bundle. Note that the adjoint operation

Definition 2.8
.

A weight on the group

In addition, the weight

for all

If

Given a weight

It is a Banach
∗
-subalgebra of

\norm{\cdot}_{1,\nu}

2.7 Matrix algebras with polynomial off-diagonal decay

The following are examples of algebras of matrices which are generalized differential subalgebras of

The same paper of Shin and Sun

Fix

3
Aspects of harmonic analysis

Now that we have established the relevant definition and shown a plenitude of examples, it is time to develop the systematic study that we promised in the introduction.

3.1 Symmetry

In what follows,
\widetilde{\mathfrak{A}}
will denote the minimal unitization of
\mathfrak{A}
(if
\mathfrak{A}
was already unital, we simply set
\widetilde{\mathfrak{A}}=\mathfrak{A}
). Symmetry is a spectral property, so it seems natural to fix our notations for the spectrum and the spectral radius now. As usual,

{\rm Spec}_{\mathfrak{A}}(a)=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\mid a-\lambda 1\textup{ is not invertible in }\widetilde{\mathfrak{A}}\}

will denote the spectrum of an element
a\in\mathfrak{A}
, while

\rho_{\mathfrak{A}}(a)=\sup\{|\lambda|\mid\lambda\in{\rm Spec}_{\mathfrak{A}}(a)\}

denotes its spectral radius. Gelfand’s formula for the spectral radius also gives the following, very useful, equality

\rho_{\mathfrak{A}}(a)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\norm{a^{n}}_{\mathfrak{A}}^{1/n}.

Definition 3.1
.

A Banach
∗
-algebra
\mathfrak{A}
is called
symmetric
if the spectrum of
a^{*}a
is non-negative for every
a\in\mathfrak{A}
.

In fact, the celebrated Shilari-Ford theorem says that a Banach
∗
-algebra
\mathfrak{A}
is symmetric if and only if the spectrum of any self-adjoint element is real
[
48
, Theorem 11.4.1]
.

The idea now is to prove that
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebras are symmetric. For that purpose, we recall the famous lemma of Barnes-Hulanicki
[
7
]
.

Lemma 3.2

(Barnes-Hulanicki)
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a Banach
∗
-algebra,
S
a
∗
-subalgebra of
\mathfrak{A}
and
\pi:\mathfrak{A}\to\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})
a faithful
∗
-representation. If
\mathfrak{A}
is unital, we assume that the inclusion
\pi(\mathfrak{A})\subset\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})
is unital.
If

\rho_{\mathfrak{A}}(a)=\|\pi(a)\|_{\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})},

for all
a\in S_{\rm sa}
, then

{\rm Spec}_{\mathfrak{A}}(a)={\rm Spec}_{\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})}(\pi(a))

for every
a\in S
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebra of
\mathfrak{B}
. Then

{\rm Spec}_{\mathfrak{A}}(a)={\rm Spec}_{\mathfrak{B}}(a)

for all
a\in\mathfrak{A}
. In particular,
\mathfrak{A}
is symmetric.

Proof.

By the GNS construction, we can find a (necessarily isometric) faithful
∗
-representation
\pi:\mathfrak{B}\to\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})
and so we identify
\mathfrak{B}
with
\pi(\mathfrak{B})
. Then we use the definition of differential subalgebra to compute the spectral radius using Gelfand’s formula. Indeed, for
a\in\mathfrak{A}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, one has

\|a^{kn}\|_{\mathfrak{A}}\leq C\|a^{n}\|_{\mathfrak{A}}^{p}\|a^{n}\|_{\mathfrak{B}}^{{k}-p},

so

\rho_{\mathfrak{A}}(a)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\|a^{kn}\|_{\mathfrak{A}}^{1/(kn)}\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}C^{1/(kn)}\|a^{n}\|_{\mathfrak{A}}^{p/(kn)}\|a^{n}\|_{\mathfrak{B}}^{q/(kn)}=\rho_{\mathfrak{A}}(a)^{p/k}\rho_{\mathfrak{B}}(a)^{q/k},

implying that
\rho_{\mathfrak{A}}(a)^{1-p/k}=\rho_{\mathfrak{A}}(a)^{q/k}\leq\rho_{\mathfrak{B}}(a)^{q/k}
and
\rho_{\mathfrak{A}}(a)\leq\rho_{\mathfrak{B}}(a)
. The reverse inequality trivially holds, so we get
\rho_{\mathfrak{A}}(a)=\rho_{\mathfrak{B}}(a)
. For a self-adjoint element
a
, one has
\rho_{\mathfrak{B}}(a)=\|a\|_{\mathfrak{B}}
, so the proof finishes by invoking the Barnes-Hulanicki Lemma.
∎

The result we just obtained says that the spectrum of an element
a\in\mathfrak{A}\subset\mathfrak{B}
is independent of the algebra of reference. This implies that the holomorphic functional calculi of both algebras coincide. The idea behind the following section is to prove the same about a slightly bigger functional calculus.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
,
1<\gamma<k
and let

be a sequence of non-negative real numbers such that

(i)

and

a_{n}=O(e^{n^{\tau}}),\textup{ as }n\to\infty

Proof.

Because of

a_{k^{i}}\leq a_{1}^{\gamma^{i}}.

For a general

n=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\epsilon_{i}k^{i}
, where
\epsilon_{i}\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}
and

a_{n}\leq\prod_{i=0}^{m}a_{k^{i}}^{\epsilon_{i}}\leq\prod_{i=0}^{m}a_{1}^{\epsilon_{i}\gamma^{i}}\leq a_{1}^{({k}-1)(m+1)\gamma^{m}}\leq e^{A(k-1)(2+\log_{k}(n))kn^{\log_{k}(\gamma)}},

(3.1)

from which the desired property follows.
∎

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a

-differential subalgebra of

\norm{u(ta)}_{\mathfrak{A}}=O(e^{|t|^{\tau}}),\textup{ as }|t|\to\infty,

Proof.

We will, of course, make use of Lemma
3.4
. For that matter, let
\gamma>1
be such that
\max\{k-1,p\}\leq\gamma\leq k

a_{n}=D(\norm{u(na)}_{\mathfrak{A}}+1)

\displaystyle a_{n+m}

\displaystyle=D(\norm{u(na)u(ma)+u(na)+u(ma)}_{\mathfrak{A}}+1)

\displaystyle\leq D(\norm{u(na)}_{\mathfrak{A}}+1)(\norm{u(ma)}_{\mathfrak{A}}+1)\leq a_{n}a_{m}.

Proving part

is a lot more involved. We need to make some heavy computations, so let us introduce the following auxiliary notations

z:=u(na)\quad\text{ and }\quad x:=e^{ina}=z+1.

Also note that

\sum_{\beta=0}^{k-2}\binom{k-1}{\beta}+\sum_{\alpha=0}^{k-2}\sum_{\beta=0}^{\alpha}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}=2^{k}-2.

(3.2)

Using the above-mentioned notation, we see that

\displaystyle u(kna)

\displaystyle=(e^{ina})^{k}-1=x^{k}-1=(x-1)\sum_{\alpha=0}^{k-1}x^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=z\sum_{\alpha=0}^{k-1}(z+1)^{\alpha}=\sum_{\alpha=0}^{k-1}\sum_{\beta=0}^{\alpha}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}z^{\beta+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta=0}^{k-1}\binom{k-1}{\beta}z^{\beta+1}+\sum_{\alpha=0}^{k-2}\sum_{\beta=0}^{\alpha}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}z^{\beta+1}

\displaystyle=z^{k}+\sum_{\beta=0}^{k-2}\binom{k-1}{\beta}z^{\beta+1}+\sum_{\alpha=0}^{k-2}\sum_{\beta=0}^{\alpha}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}z^{\beta+1}

Now we consider separately the cases
\norm{u(na)}_{\mathfrak{A}}\leq 1
and
\norm{u(na)}_{\mathfrak{A}}>1

\displaystyle a_{kn}

\displaystyle=D(\|u(kna)\|_{\mathfrak{A}}+1)

\displaystyle=D\Big(\Big\|z^{k}+\sum_{\beta=0}^{k-2}\binom{k-1}{\beta}z^{\beta+1}+\sum_{\alpha=0}^{k-2}\sum_{\beta=0}^{\alpha}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}z^{\beta+1}\Big\|_{\mathfrak{A}}+1\Big)

\displaystyle\leq D\Big(2+\sum_{\beta=0}^{k-2}\binom{k-1}{\beta}+\sum_{\alpha=0}^{k-2}\sum_{\beta=0}^{\alpha}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}\Big)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{combinatorics}}{=}2^{k}D

\displaystyle\leq 2^{k}D(\norm{u(na)}_{\mathfrak{A}}+1)^{\gamma}.

Now, if
\norm{u(na)}_{\mathfrak{A}}>1

\displaystyle a_{kn}

\displaystyle=D\Big(\Big\|z^{k}+\sum_{\beta=0}^{k-2}\binom{k-1}{\beta}z^{\beta+1}+\sum_{\alpha=0}^{k-2}\sum_{\beta=0}^{\alpha}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}z^{\beta+1}\Big\|_{\mathfrak{A}}+1\Big)

\displaystyle\leq D\Big(\|{u(na)^{k}}\|_{\mathfrak{A}}+\sum_{\beta=0}^{k-2}\binom{k-1}{\beta}\|{u(na)}\|_{\mathfrak{A}}^{\beta+1}+\sum_{\alpha=0}^{k-2}\sum_{\beta=0}^{\alpha}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}\norm{u(na)}_{\mathfrak{A}}^{\beta+1}+1\Big)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{combinatorics}}{\leq}D\Big(\|{u(na)^{k}}\|_{\mathfrak{A}}+(2^{k}-2)\norm{u(na)}_{\mathfrak{A}}^{k-1}+1\Big)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{kpq}}{\leq}D(2^{q}C\norm{u(na)}_{\mathfrak{A}}^{p}+(2^{k}-1)\norm{u(na)}_{\mathfrak{A}}^{k-1})

\displaystyle\leq D(2^{q}C+2^{k}-1)\norm{u(na)}_{\mathfrak{A}}^{\gamma}.

Therefore, setting
D^{\gamma-1}\geq\max\{2^{k},2^{q}C+2^{k}-1\}
, one concludes that
a_{kn}\leq a_{n}^{\gamma}
, irrespective of the value of
\norm{u(na)}_{\mathfrak{A}}
. By invoking Lemma
3.4
, we obtain the conclusion

\norm{u(na)}_{\mathfrak{A}}=O(e^{n^{\tau}}),\textup{ as }n\to\infty.

(3.3)

Now that we have the desired result for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, it remains to argue that the general result follows from that. Indeed, for
t>0
(the case
t<0
is analogous), we take
n=\lfloor t\rfloor
and observe that

\displaystyle\norm{u(ta)}_{\mathfrak{A}}

\displaystyle=\norm{u\big((t-n)a+na\big)}_{\mathfrak{A}}

\displaystyle=\norm{u((t-n)a)u(na)+u((t-n)a)+u(na)}_{\mathfrak{A}}

\displaystyle\leq\norm{u((t-n)a)}_{\mathfrak{A}}\norm{u(na)}_{\mathfrak{A}}+\norm{u(na)}_{\mathfrak{A}}+\norm{u((t-n)a)}_{\mathfrak{A}}

\displaystyle\leq(e^{\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{A}}}+2)\norm{u(na)}_{\mathfrak{A}}+e^{\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{A}}}+1,

(3.4)

so
\norm{u(ta)}_{\mathfrak{A}}
is
O(\norm{u(\floor{t}a)}_{\mathfrak{A}})=O(e^{{\lfloor t\rfloor}^{\tau}})=O(e^{t^{\tau}})
, as
t\to\infty
. The proof is finished. ∎

Remark 3.6
.

The above proposition shows that, for a fixed
a\in\mathfrak{A}_{\rm sa}
and a large enough
|t|
, there is a constant
B>0
so that

\norm{u(ta)}_{\mathfrak{A}}\leq Be^{|t|^{\tau}}

(3.5)

We remark that the proofs of Proposition
3.5
and Lemma
3.4
reveal that the constant

only depends on the quantity
\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{A}}
. Indeed, the asymptotics (
3.3
) are obtained from the usage of Lemma
3.4
. However, if one looks at the proof of Lemma
3.4
, the only constant that is not fixed by the data
k,p,q,C
is the
A
in (
3.1
). But in the context of Proposition
3.5
, one has

A=\ln(\|u(a)\|_\A+1)+\ln(D)\leq\ln(e^{\|a\|_\A}+1)+\ln(D).

The passage to a general

done in (

) only depends on
\|a\|_{\mathfrak{A}}
and
\norm{u(n a)}_{\mathfrak{A}}
.

It therefore follows, from the same reasoning, that the growth conclusion (
3.5
) can be obtained simultaneously for a family of elements

, provided that

.

Before introducing our theorem about functional calculus, let us introduce some notation. Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a Banach algebra.

Definition 3.7
.

Let

be a semisimple commutative Banach algebra with spectrum

.

is called

for all

.

Regularity means, quite literally, that the Banach algebra

, when identified as a subalgebra of

will be very rich. It is proven in
[

that regular algebras are also ‘normal’. This is a very useful feature, so we record it in the next remark.

Remark 3.8
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a regular commutative Banach algebra with spectrum
\Delta
. Then, for every closed set

and every compact subset

, there exists an element

such that

in

and

in

.

Assumption 3.9

3.3 Preservation of spectra

Let
\Pi:\mathfrak{A}\to\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{X})
be a representation of
\mathfrak{A}
on the Banach space
\mathcal{X}
. The idea of this subsection is to understand the spectrum of
\Pi(a)
, at least for a self-adjoint
a
. This is particularly important (and also easier) in the case of
∗
-representations, as it allows us to understand the
C^{*}
-norms in
\mathfrak{A}
. We also provide applications to the ideal theory of
\mathfrak{A}
.

If
\mathfrak{A}
is a reduced Banach
∗
-algebra, then
\iota_{\mathfrak{A}}:\mathfrak{A}\to{\rm C^{*}}(\mathfrak{A})
will denote its canonical embedding into the enveloping
C^{*}
-algebra
{\rm C^{*}}(\mathfrak{A})
. The spaces
{\rm Prim}\,{\rm C^{*}}(\mathfrak{A})
,
{\rm Prim}\mathfrak{A}
and
{\rm Prim}_{*}\mathfrak{A}
denote, respectively, the space of primitive ideals of
{\rm C^{*}}(\mathfrak{A})
, the space of primitive ideals of
\mathfrak{A}
and the space of kernels of topologically irreducible

∗
-representations of
\mathfrak{A}
, all of them equipped with the Jacobson topology. It is known that
\iota_{\mathfrak{A}}
induces a continuous surjection
{\rm Prim}\,{\rm C^{*}}(\mathfrak{A})\to{\rm Prim}_{*}\mathfrak{A}

[
48
, Corollary
10.5.7]
. We recall that for a subset
S\subset\mathfrak{A}
, its hull corresponds to

h(S)=\{I\in{\rm Prim}_{*}\mathfrak{A}\mid S\subset I\},

while the kernel of a subset
F\subset{\rm Prim}_{*}\mathfrak{A}
is

k(F)=\bigcap\{I\mid I\in F\}.

Definition 3.13
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a reduced Banach
∗
-algebra.

(i)

\mathfrak{A}
is called
C^{*}
-unique if there is a unique
C^{*}
-norm on
\mathfrak{A}
.

(ii)

\mathfrak{A}
is called
∗
-regular if the surjection
{\rm Prim}\,{\rm C^{*}}(\mathfrak{A})\to{\rm Prim}_{*}\mathfrak{A}
is a homeomorphism.

It is well-known that
∗
-regularity implies
C^{*}
-uniqueness. In fact, the latter may be rephrased as the following:
\mathfrak{A}
is
C^{*}
-unique if and only if, for every closed ideal
I\subset{\rm C^{*}}(\mathfrak{A})
, one has
I\cap\mathfrak{A}\not=\{0\}
. It might also be referred to as the ’ideal intersection property’. The next theorem shows that
∗
-regularity is much stronger.

Theorem 3.14
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebra of
\mathfrak{B}
. Then
\mathfrak{A}
is
∗
-regular. In particular, the following are true.

(i)

For any pair of
∗
-representations
\Pi_{1},\Pi_{2}
of
\mathfrak{A}
the inclusion
{\rm ker}\,\Pi_{1}\subset{\rm ker}\,\Pi_{2}
implies
\norm{\Pi_2(a)}\leq\norm{\Pi_1(a)}
, for all
a\in\mathfrak{A}
.

(ii)

Let
I
be a closed ideal of
\mathfrak{B}
, then
\overline{\mathfrak{A}\cap I}^{\norm{\cdot}_{\mathfrak{B}}}=I
.

(iii)

If
I\in{\rm Prim}\,\mathfrak{A}
, then
\overline{I}^{\norm{\cdot}_{\mathfrak{B}}}\in{\rm Prim}\,\mathfrak{B}
.

Proof.

∗
-regularity follows from
[
5
, Theorem 4.2]
and
[
48
, Theorem 10.5.18]
. The rest of the assertions are consequences of
∗
-regularity. Indeed,
(i)
follows from
[
10
, Satz 2]
and
(ii)
from
[
48
, Theorem 10.5.19]
. For
(iii)
, see
[
25
]
.
∎

Remark 3.15
.

If
\mathfrak{A}
is a
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebra of

, then, by the previous theorem,

must admit a unique

-norm. This means that

\|a\|_{\mathfrak{B}}=\|a\|_{{\rm C^{*}}(\mathfrak{A})},\quad\text{ for all }a\in\mathfrak{A}.

In particular, the inclusion

must lift to an isometric
∗

and

\|{a^{2^{k}}}\|_{\mathfrak{A}}\leq C\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{A}}^{p}\norm{a}_{{\rm C^{*}}(\mathfrak{A})}^{q},\quad\textup{ for all }a\in\mathfrak{A}.

(3.6)

This makes the concept of

-differential subalgebra actually very intrinsic to

. For that reason, in the future we might refer to

as a

-differential subalgebra, without any explicit mention to

.

We finish the subsection with a result that shows that any unital homomorphism preserves the spectral radius. For

-algebras, this is a classical result of Rickart

Theorem 3.16

Let

be a

-differential subalgebra. Let

be a unital Banach algebra and

(ii)

For a general

,

{\rm Spec}_{\widetilde{\mathfrak{A}}/I}(a+I)={\rm Spec}_{\mathfrak{B}}\big(\varphi(a)\big)\cup\overline{{\rm Spec}_{\mathfrak{B}}\big(\varphi(a^{*})\big)}.

Proof.

This is an application of
[
6
, Theorem 2.2]
and Theorem
3.3
.
\mathfrak{A}
is
∗
-quotient inverse closed because of local regularity (see

3.4 Minimal ideals of a given hull

Now we turn our attention to the following problem: Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebra. Show the existence of minimal ideals of
\mathfrak{A}
with a given hull in
{\rm Prim}_{*}\mathfrak{A}
.

The main application of such a property will be to show the Wiener property, but we postpone that until the next subsection. In order to prove the existence of minimal ideals of a given hull, we follow the approach given in
[
43
]
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebra. If
F\subset{\rm Prim}_{*}\mathfrak{A}
is any closed subset, we set

\norm{a}_{F}=\sup_{{\rm ker}\,\Pi\in F}\norm{\Pi(a)},\quad\text{ for all }a\in\mathfrak{A},

with the convention that
\norm{a}_{\emptyset}=0
. Set also

\displaystyle m(F)=

\displaystyle\{f(a)\mid a\in\mathfrak{A}_{\rm sa},\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{A}}\leq 1,f\in\mathfrak{D}_{\tau},f\equiv 0\text{ on a neighborhood of }[-\norm{a}_{F},\norm{a}_{F}]\}.

We let
j(F)
be the closed two-sided ideal of
\mathfrak{A}
generated by
m(F)
. Note that for
F=\emptyset
, we have

\displaystyle m(\emptyset)=

\displaystyle\{f(a)\mid a\in\mathfrak{A}_{\rm sa},\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{A}}\leq 1,f\in\mathfrak{D}_{\tau},f\equiv 0\text{ on a neighborhood of }0\}.

Lemma 3.17
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebra and
F\subset{\rm Prim}_{*}\mathfrak{A}
be closed. Then the hull of
j(F)
is
F
.

Proof.

We first prove that
F\subset h(j(F))
. Indeed, let
\Pi
be a
∗
-representation with
{\rm ker}\,\Pi\in F
and
f(a)\in m(F)
. Then
\Pi\big(f(a)\big)=f\big(\Pi(a)\big)=0
, since
f\equiv 0
on the spectrum of
\Pi(a)
. Hence
m(F)\subset j(F)\subset{\rm ker}\,\Pi
and therefore
{\rm ker}\,\Pi\in h(j(F))
and
F\subset h(j(F))
.

On the other hand, let
\Pi
be a
∗
-representation with
{\rm ker}\,\Pi\in{\rm Prim}_{*}\mathfrak{A}\setminus F
, then, because of Theorem
3.14
there exists
a\in\mathfrak{A}
such that
\norm{a}_{F}<\norm{\Pi(a)}
. In fact,
a
can be chosen to be self-adjoint and have
\norm{a}_{\mathfrak{A}}\leq 1
. In particular, and because of Remark
3.8
, we can find a function
f\in\mathfrak{D}_{\tau}
such that
f\equiv 0
on a neighborhood of
[-\norm{a}_{F},\norm{a}_{F}]
and
f(\norm{\Pi(a)})\not=0
. Since
a
is self-adjoint,
\norm{\Pi(a)}
lies in the spectrum of
\Pi(a)
and

\Pi\big(f(a)\big)=f\big(\Pi(a)\big)\not=0,

from which it follows that
{\rm ker}\,\Pi\not\in h(j({F}))
.
∎

Theorem 3.18
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebra and
F\subset{\rm Prim}_{*}\mathfrak{A}
be closed. There exists a closed two-sided ideal
j({F})
of
\mathfrak{A}
, with
h(j({F}))={F}
, which is contained in every two-sided closed ideal I with
h(I)\subset{F}
.

Proof.

Take a non-zero
f(a)\in m({F})
and, invoking Remark
3.8
, choose
g\in\mathfrak{D}_{\tau}
such that
g\equiv 0
in
[-\norm{a}_{F},\norm{a}_{F}]
and
g\equiv 1
in
f({\rm Spec}_{\mathfrak{A}}(a))\cap{\rm Supp}(f)
(which is compact). Thus

f(a)=(f\cdot g)(a)=f(a)g(a)

and
g(a)\in m({F})
. This implies that
\mathfrak{A}
satisfies the conditions of
[
43
, Lemma 2]
and the conclusion follows.
∎

3.5 The Wiener property

We now consider the following property, which is intended as an abstract generalization of Wiener’s tauberian theorem.

Definition 3.19
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a Banach
∗
-algebra. We say that
\mathfrak{A}
has the Wiener property
(W)
if for every proper closed two-sided ideal
I\subset\mathfrak{A}
, there exists a topologically irreducible
∗
-representation
\Pi:\mathfrak{A}\to\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})
, such that
I\subset{\rm ker}\,\Pi
.

In a moment, we will assume the existence of bounded approximate identities. The next remark is extremely useful if one wants to minimize assumptions.

Remark 3.20
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a Banach
∗
-algebra with a continuous involution. If
\mathfrak{A}
contains a bounded left approximate identity, then it contains a self-adjoint (two-sided) bounded approximate identity.

Indeed, if
(a_{i})_{i\in I}
is a bounded left approximate identity, then
(a^{*}_{i}+a_{j}-a^{*}_{i}a_{j})_{i,j\in I^{2}}
is a two-sided bounded approximate identity. If
(b_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
is a two-sided bounded approximate identity, then
(b^{*}_{\lambda}b_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
is a self-adjoint bounded approximate identity.

Lemma 3.21
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebra and let
f\in\mathfrak{D}_{\tau}
such that
f(0)=0
and
f(1)=1
. Suppose that
(b_{\alpha})_{\alpha}\subset\mathfrak{A}
is a self-adjoint bounded approximate identity. Then

\lim_{\alpha}\,\norm{f(b_{\alpha})a-a}_{\mathfrak{A}}=0,

for all
a\in\mathfrak{A}
.

Proof.

Let
B\geq\sup\norm{b_\alpha}_{\mathfrak{A}}
and without loss of generality, we assume that
B\geq 1
. Since
0=f(0)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{f}(t){\rm d}t
and
1=f(1)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{f}(t)e^{it}{\rm d}t
, one sees that

\displaystyle\norm{f(b_{\alpha})a-a}_{\mathfrak{A}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\Big\|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{f}(t)\big(u(tb_{\alpha})-e^{it}\big)a\,{\rm d}t\Big\|_{\mathfrak{A}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\Big\|{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{f}(t)\big(e^{itb_{\alpha}}-e^{it}\big)a\,{\rm d}t\Big\|}_{\mathfrak{A}}.

Let
R>0
be fixed and let us argue that

\lim_{\alpha}\Big\|\int_{|t|\leq R}\widehat{f}(t)\big(e^{itb_{\alpha}}-e^{it}\big)a\,{\rm d}t\Big\|_{\mathfrak{A}}=0.

(3.7)

Indeed, the desired convergence follows from the computation

\displaystyle\Big\|\int_{|t|\leq R}\widehat{f}(t)\big(e^{itb_{\alpha}}-e^{it}\big)a\,{\rm d}t\Big\|_{\mathfrak{A}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\widehat{f}\|_{C_{0}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{|t|\leq R}\big\|\big(e^{itb_{\alpha}}-e^{it}\big)a\big\|_{\mathfrak{A}}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq\|\widehat{f}\|_{C_{0}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{|t|\leq R}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}\big\|b_{\alpha}^{k}a-a\big\|_{\mathfrak{A}}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq 2R\|\widehat{f}\|_{C_{0}(\mathbb{R})}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{R^{k}}{k!}\|b_{\alpha}^{k}a-a\|_{\mathfrak{A}}

\displaystyle\leq 2R\|\widehat{f}\|_{C_{0}(\mathbb{R})}\|b_{\alpha}a-a\|_{\mathfrak{A}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{R^{k}}{k!}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}B^{j}

\displaystyle\leq 2BR^{2}e^{BR}\|\widehat{f}\|_{C_{0}(\mathbb{R})}\|b_{\alpha}a-a\|_{\mathfrak{A}}.

We now turn our attention to the rest of the integral. We see that

\displaystyle\Big\|\int_{|t|>R}\widehat{f}(t)\big(e^{itb_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle-e^{it}\big)a\,{\rm d}t\Big\|_{\mathfrak{A}}\leq\int_{|t|>R}|\widehat{f}(t)|\|e^{itb_{\alpha}}a\|_{\mathfrak{A}}{\rm d}t+\|a\|_{\mathfrak{A}}\Big|\int_{|t|>R}\widehat{f}(t)e^{it}\,{\rm d}t\Big|.

So it is enough to find
R>0
such that, given any
\alpha
and any
\epsilon>0
, one has

\int_{|t|>R}|\widehat{f}(t)|\|e^{itb_{\alpha}}a\|_{\mathfrak{A}}{\rm d}t<\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

(3.8)

That is because, in such a case, it is easy to enlarge
R
to also guarantee

\Big|\int_{|t|>R}\widehat{f}(t)e^{it}\,{\rm d}t\Big|<\frac{\epsilon}{2\|a\|_{\mathfrak{A}}}

and that, combined with (
3.7
), will yield the result.

Indeed, because of Remark
3.6
, one can find a constant
A>0
such that, independently of
\alpha
,

\norm{u(tb_\alpha)}_{\mathfrak{A}}\leq Ae^{|t|^{\tau}},\quad\text{ for all }\alpha.

We use this bound to see that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\widehat{f}(t)|\|e^{itb_{\alpha}}a\|_{\mathfrak{A}}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq\|a\|_{\mathfrak{A}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\widehat{f}(t)|(1+\|u(tb_{\alpha})\|_{\mathfrak{A}}){\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq\|a\|_{\mathfrak{A}}\|\widehat{f}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}+A\|a\|_{\mathfrak{A}}\|f\|_{\tau}<\infty.

Since the integral
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\widehat{f}(t)|\|e^{itb_{\alpha}}a\|_{\mathfrak{A}}{\rm d}t
converges independently of
\alpha
, one can find
R>0
big enough such that (
3.8
) holds. This finishes the proof. ∎

Remark 3.22
.

If one compares Lemma
3.21
with its analog in
[
25
]
, one will see that our proof here is a lot simpler. The difference, of course, is the fact that the subexponential growth obtained in Proposition
3.5
only depends on the norm of the corresponding element
a\in\mathfrak{A}
(Remark
3.6
), whereas the polynomial growth estimate obtained in
[
25
]
depends on the support and, more critically, on the
L^{2}
-norm of the corresponding cross-sections. It seems unlikely that the bounded approximate unit fixed in
[
25
]
could be bounded in
L^{2}
-norm, making the application of something like Remark
3.6
impossible and resulting in a more involved proof.

With Lemma
3.21
at hand, we are finally able to derive the Wiener property for
\mathfrak{A}
.

Theorem 3.23
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebra that contains a self-adjoint bounded approximate identity. Then
\mathfrak{A}
has the Wiener property.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.21
,
j(\emptyset)
contains all of
\mathfrak{A}
. Now, if
I
is a closed two-sided ideal of
\mathfrak{A}
such that
h(I)=\emptyset
, then
\mathfrak{A}=j(\emptyset)\subset I
by Theorem
3.18
.
∎

4
Intertwining operators and automatic continuity

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a Banach algebra. A Banach space
\mathcal{X}
which is also an
\mathfrak{A}
-bimodule is called a
Banach
\mathfrak{A}
-bimodule
if the maps

\mathfrak{A}\times\mathcal{X}\ni(a,\xi)\mapsto a\xi\in\mathcal{X}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{X}\times\mathfrak{A}\ni(\xi,a)\mapsto\xi a\in\mathcal{X}

are jointly continuous. If we only have the continuity of the maps

\mathcal{X}\ni\xi\mapsto a\xi\in\mathcal{X}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{X}\ni\xi\mapsto\xi a\in\mathcal{X}

for each
a\in\mathfrak{A}
, then
\mathcal{X}
is called a
weak Banach
\mathfrak{A}
-bimodule
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a Banach algebra and
\mathcal{X}_{1},\mathcal{X}_{2}
be weak Banach
\mathfrak{A}
-bimodules. A linear map
\theta:\mathcal{X}_{1}\to\mathcal{X}_{2}
is called an
\mathfrak{A}
-intertwining operator
if for each
a\in\mathfrak{A}
, the maps

\mathcal{X}_{1}\ni\xi\mapsto\theta(a\xi)-a\theta(\xi)\in\mathcal{X}_{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{X}_{1}\ni\xi\mapsto\theta(\xi a)-\theta(\xi)a\in\mathcal{X}_{2}

are continuous.

We mentioned in the introduction that intertwining operators generalize algebra homomorphisms, derivations, and bimodule homomorphisms. We will make this precise in the following example.

Example 4.2
.

(i)

Every
\mathfrak{A}
-bimodule homomorphism between weak Banach
\mathfrak{A}
-bimodules is an
\mathfrak{A}
-intertwining operator.

(ii)

If
\theta:\mathfrak{B}\to\mathfrak{A}
is an algebra homomorphism, then
\mathfrak{A}
can be made into a weak Banach
\mathfrak{B}
-bimodules with the actions

ab=a\theta(b)\quad\text{ and }\quad ba=\theta(b)a,\quad\text{for }a\in\mathfrak{A},b\in\mathfrak{B}.

With respect to this actions,
\theta
is a
\mathfrak{B}
-intertwining operator.

(iii)

Let
\mathcal{X}
a weak Banach
\mathfrak{A}
-bimodule. A derivation is a linear map
D:\mathfrak{A}\to\mathcal{X}
satisfying

D(ab)=D(a)b+aD(b).

Every derivation is an
\mathfrak{A}
-intertwining operator.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a Banach algebra, and
\theta:\mathcal{X}_{1}\to\mathcal{X}_{2}
an
\mathfrak{A}
-intertwining operator between weak Banach
\mathfrak{A}
-bimodules. Then

\mathscr{I}(\theta)=\{a\in\mathfrak{A}\mid\text{the maps }\mathcal{X}_{1}\ni\xi\mapsto\theta(a\xi)\in\mathcal{X}_{2},\,\mathcal{X}_{1}\ni\xi\mapsto\theta(\xi a)\in\mathcal{X}_{2}\text{ are continuous }\forall a\in\mathfrak{A}\}

is the
continuity ideal
of
\theta
.

Note that
\mathscr{I}(\theta)
is not necessarily closed, unless
\mathcal{X}_{2}
is a Banach
\mathfrak{A}
-bimodule. In order to introduce the next theorem, we now recall the notion of a set of synthesis.

Definition 4.4
.

A closed subset
C\subset{\rm Prim}_{*}\mathfrak{A}
will be called a
set of synthesis
if
k(C)
is the unique closed two-sided ideal
I\subset\mathfrak{A}
such that
h(I)=C
.

The following theorem was proven in
[
24
]
as a generalization of
[
54
, Theorem 2.3, Theorem 3.2]
. The reader should note that we have already shown that our algebras automatically satisfy most of the assumptions, so the theorem can be applied with great generality. Any Banach algebra
\mathfrak{A}
will be considered a bimodule over itself with left/right multiplication, as described in Example
4.2
.

Theorem 4.5

(
[
24
]
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a reduced Banach
∗
-algebra,
\mathcal{X}_{1}
a Banach
\mathfrak{A}
-bimodule,
\mathcal{X}_{2}
a weak Banach
\mathfrak{A}
-bimodule and
\theta:\mathcal{X}_{1}\to\mathcal{X}_{2}
an
\mathfrak{A}
-intertwining operator. Further suppose that

(i)

For all
a\in\mathfrak{A}_{\rm sa}
,
\mathfrak{A}(a)
is a regular Banach algebra.

(ii)

\mathfrak{A}
has the Wiener property
(W)
.

(iii)

Every finite subset
F\subset{\rm Prim}_{*}\mathfrak{A}
such that all
P\in F
has finite codimension is a set of synthesis for
\mathfrak{A}
.

Then
\overline{\mathscr{I}(\theta)}^{\norm{\cdot}_{\mathfrak{A}}}
has finite codimension in
\mathfrak{A}
. Furthermore, if
\mathcal{X}_{1}=\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{A}
also satisfies

(iv)

Every closed two-sided
∗
-ideal
I\subset\mathfrak{A}
of finite codimension has a bounded left approximate identity.

Then
\theta
is continuous if and only if
\mathscr{I}(\theta)
is closed.

Proof.

This is exactly
[
24
, Theorem 3.6]
, except for conditions
(i)
and
(iv)
. Condition
(i)
is obviously stronger now, so let us explain why the weakening of Condition
(iv)
still implies the same result. Indeed, following the proof of
[
24
, Theorem 3.6]
, one sees that

\overline{\mathscr{I}(\theta)}^{\norm{\cdot}_{\mathfrak{A}}}=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\supset\mathscr{I}(\theta)\\
P\in{\rm Prim}_{*}\mathfrak{A}\end{subarray}}P=k\big(\mathscr{I}(\theta)\big),

which shows that
\overline{\mathscr{I}(\theta)}^{\norm{\cdot}_{\mathfrak{A}}}
is a
∗
-ideal. Now, we note that condition
(iv)
was only required to ensure that
\overline{\mathscr{I}(\theta)}^{\norm{\cdot}_{\mathfrak{A}}}
contains a left approximate identity.
∎

As in
[
24
]
, condition
(iv)
in Theorem
4.5
is the most restrictive. In fact, we do not know of any simple way to imply it with generality. It is satisfied by
C^{*}
-algebras and amenable Banach algebras
[
30
, Proposition VII.2.31]
. The amenability condition, however, seems way too restrictive.

Condition
(iv)
can obviously be bypassed if nontrivial ideals of finite codimension do not exist inside
\mathfrak{A}
. The next result aims in that direction.

Theorem 4.6

(
[
24
]
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a symmetric, reduced, unital Banach
∗
-algebra,
\mathcal{X}_{1}
a Banach
\mathfrak{A}
-bimodule,
\mathcal{X}_{2}
a weak Banach
\mathfrak{A}
-bimodule and
\theta:\mathcal{X}_{1}\to\mathcal{X}_{2}
an
\mathfrak{A}
-intertwining operator. Further suppose that

(i)

For all
a\in\mathfrak{A}_{\rm sa}
,
\mathfrak{A}(a)
is a regular Banach algebra.

(ii)

\mathfrak{A}
has the Wiener property
(W)
.

(iii)

{\rm C^{*}}(\mathfrak{A})
has no proper closed two-sided ideals with finite codimension.

Then
\theta
is continuous.

Note that conditions
(i)
and
(ii)
were already proven for
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebras (see Theorem
3.12
and Theorem
3.23
). So the next theorem is an immediate application of Theorem
4.6
.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a unital
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebra. Also consider a Banach
\mathfrak{A}
-bimodule
\mathcal{X}_{1}
, a weak Banach
\mathfrak{A}
-bimodule
\mathcal{X}_{2}
, and an
\mathfrak{A}
-intertwining operator
\theta:\mathcal{X}_{1}\to\mathcal{X}_{2}
. If
{\rm C^{*}}(\mathfrak{A})
has no proper closed two-sided ideals with finite codimension, then
\theta
is continuous.

In order to also apply Theorem
4.5
in our setting, we still need to verify its condition
(iii)
. We will do that now. We follow an approach inspired by
[
4
, Lemma 10]
.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebra. Let
I
be a closed two-sided
∗
-ideal of
\mathfrak{A}
that contains a bounded left approximate identity. If
a\in I
and
\epsilon>0
, then there exist
b_{1},b_{2}\in I
such that

\norm{a-b_1a}_{\mathfrak{A}}<\epsilon\quad\text{ and }\quad b_{2}b_{1}=b_{1}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
I
contains a self-adjoint bounded approximate identity
b_{\alpha}\in I
(see Remark
3.20
).

Let
f\in\mathfrak{D}_{\tau}
such that
f(0)=0
and
f(1)=1
. Note that
f(b_{\alpha})\in I
and, repeating the proof of Lemma
3.21
, one gets that

\lim_{\alpha}\|f(b_{\alpha})a-a\|_{\mathfrak{A}}=0,\quad\text{ for all }a\in I.

If we further suppose that
f
is compactly supported and pick
g\in\mathfrak{D}_{\tau}
such that
g\equiv 1
on
{\rm Supp}(f)
, then we also have

g(b_{\alpha})f(b_{\alpha})=(g\cdot f)(b_{\alpha})=f(b_{\alpha}).

Choosing
\alpha
large enough proves the claim.
∎

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebra and suppose that every closed two-sided
∗
-ideal
I\subset\mathfrak{A}
of finite codimension has a bounded left approximate identity. Let
F
be a finite closed subset of
\,{\rm Prim}_{*}\mathfrak{A}
such that every
P\in F
has finite codimension. Then
F
is a set of synthesis.

Proof.

Define

M=\{b\in k(F)\mid\exists a\in k(F)\text{ such that }ab=b\}.

One obviously has that
h(M)\supset F
, but Lemma
4.8
actually implies
h(M)=F
. Indeed,
k(F)=\cap_{P\in F}P
is a finite codimensional, closed two-sided
∗
-ideal, so for every
a\in k(F)
there is a sequence
b_{n}\in M
making
a=\lim_{n\to\infty}b_{n}a
. Therefore
a\in P^{\prime}
, for all
P^{\prime}\in h(M)
and
h(M)=F
.

It clear that
F\subset h(a)
for all the elements
a
involved in the definition of
M
. Now, if
I
is a closed two-sided ideal of
\mathfrak{A}
with
h(I)=F
, then because of
[
43
, Lemma 2]
, we have
M\subset I
. Then applying Lemma
4.8
again, we have
k(F)\subset I
and hence
I=k(F)
.
∎

With this, we can state our general criterion for automatic continuity.

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebra where all closed
∗
-ideals of finite codimension have a bounded left approximate identity. Let
\mathcal{X}
be a weak Banach
\mathfrak{A}
-bimodule and
\theta:\mathfrak{A}\to\mathcal{X}
an
\mathfrak{A}
-intertwining operator. Then
\theta
is continuous if and only if
\mathscr{I}(\theta)
is closed.

Proof.

It follows from Theorem
4.5
.
(i)
and
(ii)
were proven in Theorem
3.12
and Theorem
3.23
. Condition
(iii)
is covered by Theorem
4.9
and condition
(iv)
is taken as an assumption.
∎

In the setting of the above theorem, if
\mathcal{X}
is a Banach
\mathfrak{A}
-bimodule instead of a weak Banach
\mathfrak{A}
-bimodule, then
\mathscr{I}(\theta)
is closed and we obtain the (automatic) continuity of
\theta
. This is recorded in the next corollaries.

Corollary 4.11
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebra where all closed
∗
-ideals of finite codimension have a bounded left approximate identity. Let
\mathcal{X}
be a Banach
\mathfrak{A}
-bimodule and
\theta:\mathfrak{A}\to\mathcal{X}
an
\mathfrak{A}
-intertwining operator. Then
\theta
is continuous.

Corollary 4.12
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be an amenable
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebra. Let
\mathcal{X}
be a Banach
\mathfrak{A}
-bimodule and
\theta:\mathfrak{A}\to\mathcal{X}
an
\mathfrak{A}
-intertwining operator. Then
\theta
is continuous.

Finally, we also consider the specific case of derivations. In such a case, we can appeal to the recent results in
[
23
]
to obtain the following two theorems. They show that finding dense
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebras can be a useful tool to obtain results about automatic continuity for the ambient algebra.

Theorem 4.13
.

Let
\mathfrak{B}
be a Banach algebra that contains a
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebra
\mathfrak{A}
as a Banach subalgebra. Let
D:\mathfrak{B}\to\mathcal{X}
be a derivation into a Banach
\mathfrak{B}
-bimodule. Further suppose that
\mathfrak{A}
is unital and that
{\rm C^{*}}(\mathfrak{A})
has no proper closed two-sided ideals of finite codimension. Then
D
is continuous.

Theorem 4.14
.

Let
\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B},\mathfrak{C}
be Banach algebras such that
\mathfrak{A}
is a
(k,p,q)
-differential subalgebra and such that the inclusions
\mathfrak{A}\subset\mathfrak{B},\mathfrak{C}\subset{\rm C^{*}}(\mathfrak{A})
are continuous. Then every derivation
D:\mathfrak{B}\to\mathfrak{C}
is continuous.

When applied to a differential subalgebra, the above theorem recovers
[
33
, Theorem 13.1(ii)]
, and, in the particular case of domains of closed
∗
-derivations (see Subsection
2.3
), it recovers Longo’s relative boundedness result
[
41
]
.
