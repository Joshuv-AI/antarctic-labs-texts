Characterization of NAT2 Using Long‐Read Sequencing: Allele, Diplotype, and Phenotype Call Accuracy Compared to Other Testing Strategies
Authors: Shobana John, Erin C Boone, Byunggil Yoo, Laura B Ramsey, Andrea Gaedigk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe NAT2 pharmacogene is essential in drug metabolism, particularly for aromatic amines and hydrazines. Genetic variations in NAT2 categorize individuals as rapid, intermediate, or poor metabolizers based on their acetylation capacity to inform dosing guidelines. Genotyping and short‐read sequencing allele calling methodologies are unable to phase variants, leading to ambiguous diplotype and phenotype calls while long‐read sequencing technologies not only provide variant detection but also variant phasing to unequivocally establish diplotype. This study utilized long‐read sequencing to analyze NAT2 genetic variation in a large cohort of 1828 long‐read and 662 paired short‐read patient samples from the Genomic Answers for Kids program, comparing call accuracy with short‐reads and simulated SNP panel tests. A custom tool, staR‐NAT2, identified 11 new star alleles and four new suballeles. Long‐reads demonstrated high diplotype accuracy (> 99%) with pb‐StarPhase and Aldy, excluding novel haplotypes, while short‐reads showed a significant reduction to 64% in diplotype concordance, but only affecting 4% of phenotype calls. Despite poor diplotype resolution and accuracy compared with long‐read data, short‐read sequencing and a 4‐SNP panel accurately predicted phenotype for over 95% of subjects. However, the 4‐SNP and 5‐SNP panels both resulted in discordant or ambiguous phenotypes for non‐white individuals at 4.5% and 25%, respectively. Additionally, population‐specific alleles were identified among 1144 unrelated individuals, including NAT2*14 (8.9%) and *43 (3.57%) in Blacks and *7 (7.35%) in Hispanics. These findings underscore the limitations of short‐read sequencing and support long‐read sequencing as a robust approach for accurate and equitable NAT2 pharmacogenetic testing to guide individualized drug therapy.

N‐acetyltransferase 2 (NAT2), a phase II enzyme, metabolizes aromatic amines and hydrazines among other chemical entities. 1 Genetic variation in NAT2 affects enzyme stability, substrate affinity, and expression, resulting in variable acetylation activity. Slow acetylators have reduced drug clearance and higher risk of toxicity, such as isoniazid‐induced hepatotoxicity 2 , 3 while rapid acetylators may experience subtherapeutic drug levels due to accelerated clearance, supporting genotype‐guided prescribing. 4 , 5 , 6 The first Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC) guideline for NAT2 has recently been published. 7

As NAT2 was transitioned to PharmVar in 2024, significant changes were made to NAT2 nomenclature, which are summarized in a PharmVar GeneFocus article. 8 One notable change is that NAT2*1 is now the reference allele, and *4 which previously served as the reference is now classified as a fully functional variant. The CPIC Pharmacogene Curation Expert Panel for NAT2 assigned increased, decreased, uncertain, or unknown function to the star alleles listed by PharmVar at the time of writing. 9 , 10 , 11

Four common NAT2 variants, c.191G>A (p.R64Q), c.341T>C (p.I114T), c.590G>A (p.R197Q), and c.857G>A (p.G286E) that occur within numerous haplotypes are among the most functionally relevant and frequently tested, and have been reported to accurately predict phenotypes in the majority of individuals of European ancestry. 12 However, in light of the recent updates to NAT2 nomenclature, 8 the designation of numerous novel star alleles by PharmVar, and also the discovery of 11 new star alleles in this study, it is essential to evaluate the accuracy of diplotype assignment and phenotype prediction across diverse populations, especially when testing is limited to a small subset of variants, such as those mentioned above.

Short‐read next‐generation sequencing is widely used for variant detection due to its cost affordability and high accuracy of variant calling. However, it lacks the ability to phase variants to fully resolve haplotypes, which may lead to reduced accuracy in allele and diplotype assignments, particularly in large, complex, or diverse genomic regions. 13 , 14 Long‐read sequencing such as PacBio HiFi overcomes many of these challenges. With accuracy rates exceeding 99%, long‐read sequencing supports more reliable star allele, diplotype, and phenotype frequency estimates across populations, including pharmacogenes. 15 , 16 , 17 However, long‐read phasing accuracy and variant detection depend heavily on the number of reads. 18 , 19 Several computational tools have been developed to assist with star allele‐based diplotype calling from short‐read and long‐read data. These include Aldy, PyPGx (the successor of Stargazer), StellarPGx, and pb‐StarPhase. Despite these advances, challenges such as ambiguous or incorrect diplotype calls and inconsistencies in results across tools and sequencing platforms persist. 20 , 21 , 22 These issues may hamper sequencing‐based PGx testing and implementation, ultimately limiting its broader adoption and impact.

In this study, we used sequence data from the Genomic Answers for Kids (GA4K) program to understand underlying genetic variation in rare diseases to characterize NAT2 variation. Our objectives were to (i) compare the performance of NAT2 diplotype calling and phenotype prediction across long‐read and short‐read whole genome sequencing (abbreviated as LRS and SRS from here on) using various diplotype callers, (ii) identify and characterize novel NAT2 haplotypes, and (iii) determine star allele, diplotype, and phenotype frequencies. Additionally, to evaluate the accuracy of diplotype and phenotype assignments when testing is limited to a small set of variants, we compared the LRS‐based diplotypes to those that would have been obtained using 4‐SNP and 5‐SNP genotyping panels.

METHODS

Study cohort

LRS and SRS data were obtained from the Genomic Answers for Kids (GA4K) program, an IRB‐approved pediatric genomics initiative at Children's Mercy, Kansas City (CMH‐IRB‐11120514). 23 Self‐identified race and ethnicity, collected at intake, were classified as White, Black, Hispanic, Multiracial, Other (including Asians and American Indians), and Unknown, and used to assess interethnic differences in NAT2 star allele, diplotype, and phenotype frequencies. Relatedness was collected at intake, with samples often also collected from the index participants' parents and/or siblings.

Long‐read and short‐read sequencing

PacBio HiFi libraries were prepared using the Express Template Prep Kit and sequenced on Sequel IIe or Revio platforms, targeting > 25× coverage. Long‐reads were aligned to the GRCh38 reference genome using pbmm2. Single nucleotide variants (SNVs) were called using DeepVariant and phased with WhatsHap. Small indels and copy number variation were detected using pbsv and HiFiCNV, respectively.

Libraries for SRS were prepared with the Illumina TruSeq PCR‐Free kit and sequenced on a NovaSeq6000 platform to ≥ 30× depth. Libraries for short‐read whole exome sequencing (SR‐WES) were prepared using the same kit with 10 PCR cycles and enriched using the IDT xGen Exome Research Panel v2.

Sequencing, primary analysis, alignment (GRCh38), and variant calling with Illumina DRAGEN were performed by the Children's Mercy Center for Genomic Medicine. 24

4‐SNP and 5‐SNP test panel evaluation

All NAT2 diplotypes identified by LRS for subjects with long‐read coverage were manually assigned to their corresponding diplotypes had they been determined using a 4‐SNP or 5‐SNP genotyping panel. The 4‐SNP panel included c.191G>A, c.341T>C, c.590G>A and c.857G>A, while the 5‐SNP panel also included c.803G>A. Concordance with their LRS‐based results was assessed at diplotype and phenotype levels. Alternate diplotype calls resulting in differing or indeterminate phenotypes were considered discordant.

NAT2 diplotype calling, accuracy assessment, and novel haplotype discovery

Reads mapping to pharmacogenes were extracted from LRS and SRS‐(binary alignment map) BAM files using Bedtools (v2.30) and a PGx‐specific BED file based on GRCh38 coordinates and indexed with Samtools (v1.22). NAT2 diplotypes were called from LRS data using pb‐StarPhase, Aldy, and staR‐NAT2, and from SRS data with Aldy and StellarPGx (Figure 1 ).

Tools for diplotype calling

pb‐StarPhase (v1.4.0), 25 a phase‐aware caller designed for PacBio HiFi data, integrates PharmVar, CPIC, and International Immunogenetics Information System–Human Leukocyte Antigen (IMGT‐HLA) definitions to assign star alleles for over 30 pharmacogenes, including NAT2, using phased VCFs. Aldy (v4.6), 26 a Python‐based tool uses integer linear programming for haplotype reconstruction that supports BAM, Compressed Reference‐oriented Alignment Map (CRAM), and VCF inputs and structural variant detection. Aldy was applied to LRS and SRS data, and novel variant calling was performed using tool‐specific commands (‐‐param debug_novel=True) as recommended by the developer. StellarPGx (v1.2.7) 27 performs graph‐based alignments of SRS‐BAM files for diplotype calling. staR‐NAT2 is a custom, rule‐based R‐script designed to assign NAT2 diplotypes and was used on variant data extracted from phased LRS‐VCFs, limited to the NAT2 gene locus. It matches known variant patterns to star alleles and flags novel or unmatched combinations (i.e., variants not found in any star allele at the time of analysis) for manual review or annotation using the biomaRt package (staR‐NAT2 does not assess structural variants). Phenotypes were assigned according to ClinPGx NAT2 reference tables for allele function and genotype‐to‐phenotype translation. 9

Novel haplotype discovery

Novel NAT2 haplotypes identified by Aldy and/or staR‐NAT2 were evaluated using trio‐based LRS and SRS data, if available. SRS was used as an orthogonal method to confirm SNVs by reviewing variants visually in Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV). 28 The functional impact of variants was assessed using Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD), 29 PolyPhen‐2, 30 and SpliceAI. 31 All new haplotypes were submitted to PharmVar for star allele designation.

Accuracy assessment

Analyses focused only on subjects with an average long‐read coverage of ≥ 10× across the NAT2 gene and no novel haplotypes. Diplotype‐calling accuracy was evaluated by comparing outputs from pb‐StarPhase (LRS‐VCF), Aldy (LRS/SRS, BAM/VCF), and StellarPGx (SRS‐BAM) against staR‐NAT2 calls. Calls were classified as “not‐called,” “one‐call” (e.g., NAT2*5/*14), “two‐calls” (e.g., NAT2*1/*29 or *5/*46), or “three‐calls” (e.g., NAT2*4/*29 or *5/*14 or *16/*46) for each individual. Single diplotype calls were considered high confidence, while those producing two or more possible diplotypes indicated ambiguity. Diplotype concordance was defined when the called diplotype matched the staR‐NAT2 result, and discordance was noted for ambiguous (two or three possible) or inconsistent calls. Phenotype concordance was defined when the predicted phenotype matched the staR‐NAT2 result, while discordance included any inconsistent or conflicting phenotype classifications arising from ambiguous diplotypes, that is, IM or PM for two‐calls or IM or PM or IM for three‐calls. If all possible diplotypes yielded the same phenotype as staR‐NAT2, calls were considered concordant.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize cohort demographics and NAT2 star allele, diplotype, and phenotype frequencies. Concordance between pb‐StarPhase, Aldy, and StellarPGx and staR‐NAT2 outputs is reported as counts and percentages. Discordant cases were quantified and further analyzed by comparing diplotype calls, inspecting variant‐level mismatches, and evaluating predicted phenotype. All analyses were performed in RStudio (v2024.09.1) and Python (v3.13.3).

RESULTS

Study cohort

Among the participants enrolled in GA4K between 2019 and 2024, 23 LRS data were available for 1828 individuals of which 1435 had ≥ 10 long‐reads across the NAT2 gene. Among those were 1144 unrelated individuals including both parents from trios (n = 192), probands from duos (n = 157), probands without parental data (n = 635), both parents without probands (n = 19), probands from proband+sibling‐only data (n = 107), parents from parent–sibling data (n = 5), and one sibling from sibling‐only data (n = 29). Participants self‐identified as White (62%), Black (2%), Hispanic/Latino (5%), multiracial (3%), or Other (1%). No race/ethnicity data were available for 27% of the participants and were categorized as Unknown (Figure S1 ).

Discovery of novel haplotypes

LRS identified 15 novel haplotypes with unique combinations of known or novel synonymous and/or non‐synonymous variants. All novel haplotypes were found in individuals with ≥ 10 long‐reads, except one which was detected among the subjects with lower read coverage. PharmVar designated 11 as new star alleles (NAT2*65‐*75) while four were classified as suballeles of NAT2*4 or *6 (Table 1 ). NAT2*65.001 was found in seven individuals and *66.001 in three; the most common novel suballele was NAT2*4.008. The remaining new star alleles occurred in one or two individuals. Variant effect predictions (Table S1 ) suggest that seven of the novel star alleles have variants that impact function; six cause amino acid changes, and one introduces a premature translation stop codon (p.R197X). Accuracy of NAT2 diplotype and phenotype assignment

To evaluate diplotype and phenotype assignment accuracy, outputs from pb‐StarPhase, Aldy, and StellarPGx were compared to those generated by staR‐NAT2. Subjects with novel haplotypes (n = 19, 1.3%) were excluded, as these alleles are not called by the tools and thus were expected to be inconsistent. This analysis also excluded subjects with less than 10 long‐reads. pb‐StarPhase achieved 100% concordance for both diplotype and phenotype for all 1416 individuals. Aldy showed high concordance using LRS‐BAM input (99% for diplotypes; 99.5% for phenotypes). By contrast, when LRS‐VCFs were used for Aldy, multiple or ambiguous diplotype calls were produced for 37.3% of the subjects, reducing diplotype concordance to 62.7% albeit phenotype discordance remained low at 2%. For SRS, Aldy showed comparable performance across BAM and VCF inputs, with diplotype concordance rates of 63.8% and 65.7%, respectively, and 96% for phenotype. StellarPGx achieved 61.9% diplotype and 97.5% phenotype concordance (Tables 2 and 3). All staR‐NAT2 diplotype calls were consistent for all trios with a read coverage of ≥ 10×. Lastly, we note that Aldy v4.6 reports had variant positions shifted by one base relative to the correct NAT2 GRCh38 coordinates, which did, however, not impact star allele calls.

Star allele, diplotype, and phenotype frequencies

Among White individuals, NAT2*5 (40%), *6 (29%), and *4 (24%) were the most frequently observed alleles. In Black individuals, NAT2*5 (28.5%) and *6 (28%) were common, but several other alleles were also frequently found, including NAT2*1 (7.1%), *14 (9%), and *43 (3.5%). Among Hispanics, NAT2*4 (34%) and *7 (7.35%) were notably enriched. Several alleles were detected only within specific ethnic groups: NAT2*16 in Whites (2.7%), NAT2*36 and *47 in Blacks (3.57% and 1.79%, respectively), and NAT2*41 in Multiracial individuals (1.3%) (Figure 2 ). Star allele frequencies are summarized in Table S4 .

Table 4 provides diplotype and phenotype distributions. Within the unrelated cohort, PMs were the most prevalent phenotype overall among both White (58%) and Black (53.6%) participants. This predominance was primarily driven by the high frequencies of the NAT2*5/*6 (23%), *5/*5 (17.3%), and *6/*6 (8.8%) diplotypes. By contrast, IMs were more commonly observed among Hispanics (44.1%), multiracial individuals (42.1%), and Others (38.5%) due to the increased prevalence of NAT2*4/*5 and *4/*6 diplotypes. Additionally, RMs were also relatively frequent in Hispanics (14.7%) due to 15% of participants having a NAT2*4/*4 diplotype. Indeterminate metabolizers were found across all groups, with the highest proportion among Black participants (7.1%).

Genotyping vs. sequencing for phenotype prediction

LRS‐based diplotypes and their respective genotype calls if testing were done either with a 4‐SNP or 5‐SNP genotype test panel are provided in Table S5 . For example, as shown in Figure S2 , a subject identified by LRS as having a NAT2*14/*36 genotype should be classified as *34/*46 if tested with the 4‐SNP panel and as *6/*14 or *4/*15 if tested with the 5‐SNP panel based on current star allele definitions (of the latter, *6/*14 is the more likely diplotype). If subjects would have been genotyped with a 4‐SNP test panel, the resulting genotypes largely preserved the LRS‐based phenotype distribution among the 704 unrelated Whites at 99.5%, although diplotype resolution was low, meaning that only 18% had a diplotype that matched their LRS‐based diplotype. Phenotype concordance was lower among non‐Whites at 95%. By contrast, diplotype resolution would have improved to 56.68% and 51.32% if subjects had been genotyped with the 5‐SNP test panel for Whites and non‐Whites, respectively, but phenotype concordance would have declined to 75% and 75.2% due to an increase in ambiguous calls (Table 5 ).

DISCUSSION

LRS improves the accuracy of star allele calls and thus diplotype and phenotype assignments. Furthermore, LRS enables the discovery of novel haplotypes and provides comprehensive sequence data for designating PharmVar star alleles.

Discovery of novel haplotypes using LRS data

LRS supports direct phasing and structural variant detection, facilitating the identification of previously uncharacterized alleles. In our cohort, 15 novel NAT2 haplotypes were discovered among 29 individuals, including 11 newly designated star alleles (NAT2*65‐*75). For example, one individual was heterozygous for three variants, c.341T>C, c.803G>A, and c.845A>G (p.K828R), of which the latter was not part of any prior defined star allele. Both Aldy and pb‐StarPhase failed to detect the c.845A>G variant, leading to an incorrect NAT2*4/*5 diplotype call and misclassification as IM. However, detection of the computationally predicted function‐impairing variant (c.845A>G), in cis with c.803G>A, led to the designation of a novel haplotype, NAT2*74. Consequently, the diplotype was reassigned to NAT2*5/*74, and the phenotype revised to indeterminate based on CPIC's “unknown function” classification of this novel star allele. This example demonstrates how phasing enables resolution of complex haplotypes and discovery of novel variant configurations that are frequently missed by SRS or limited panel‐based genotyping methods.

Notably, all novel haplotypes were discovered in individuals who self‐identified as White; a population often considered well‐characterized. The implications for underrepresented groups, where baseline genetic data may still be sparse, are even greater. Detecting and phasing novel alleles in these populations with LRS holds potential for improving pharmacogenomic equity and refining drug‐response predictions. 32 Seven of the 14 newly identified variants (c.823T>A (*67), c.580C>G (*68), c.596T>C (*70), c.704T>G (*71), c.67A>C (*72), c.802A>G (*73), c.845A>G (*74)) were predicted to be deleterious or probably damaging (Table S1 ).

Performance of allele calling tools for novel haplotype detection varied. While staR‐NAT2 correctly flagged alleles not yet catalogued by PharmVar, pb‐StarPhase most often defaulted novel haplotypes to the closest known match (e.g., NAT2*65 reported as *4), potentially leading to misclassified phenotypes. Aldy with LRS data supported detection of novel variants via extended flags, but its output was limited by internal allele‐matching logic. These differences highlight the value of multi‐tool analysis for capturing novel haplotypes and checking for potential call discrepancies involving known haplotypes, as well as the need to improve tools. PyPGx, 33 another popular tool, was not assessed, as it did not support updated PharmVar nomenclature at the time of analysis.

The accuracy of diplotype calling varies across different sequencing platforms and tools

Accurate diplotype assignment is critical for phenotype‐based dosing strategies, minimizing adverse drug reactions, and optimizing therapeutic outcomes. This study revealed differences in diplotype‐calling accuracy and phenotype assignment across sequencing platforms and star allele‐based diplotype‐calling tools. For example, pb‐StarPhase and Aldy achieved high diplotype and phenotype concordance with LRS data after excluding subjects (n = 19) with novel haplotypes. By contrast, using SRS data, Aldy produced ambiguous or discordant diplotype calls for nearly 36% of the subjects, which led to an incorrect phenotype classification for 4.6% of individuals. StellarPGx produced a single (confident) diplotype call for 98% of subjects. However, 36.4% of these were discordant and resulted in incorrect phenotype assignments for 2.51%. Overall, high‐confidence diplotype calls from LRS data reduced ambiguity and therefore improved phenotype prediction, while SRS data often yielded ambiguous or inconsistent results due to phasing challenges, and even confident, non‐ambiguous diplotype calls may not reflect the actual diplotype or phenotype for NAT2.

Even with LRS data, tool choice and variant file input format played key roles and contributed to discrepancies in diplotype calls across tools. For example, Aldy assigned a single (confident) diplotype for all the subjects when LRS‐BAM files were used, leaving 1% of calls discordant when compared to staR‐NAT2. A review of Aldy's internal VCF output from BAM files revealed missing, extra, or misclassified homozygous variants when compared to the original LRS and SRS‐VCF. Although BAM‐based realignment improves indel handling and structural variant discovery, especially in intricate loci such as CYP2D6, 34 , 35 , 36 its effect on simpler genes like NAT2 remains unclear. Our dataset showed discrepancies in diplotype calls for NAT2*4/*6 and *4/*5, with Aldy assigning them as *6/*6 and *4/*4, respectively. For example, based on the original VCFs, staR‐NAT2 and pb‐StarPhase called NAT2*4/*6, which is supported by two heterozygous variants (c.282C>T and c.590G>A) and one homozygous variant (c.803G>A). By contrast, Aldy interpreted all three positions as homozygous, likely due to variant normalization and alignment differences, which may have led to incorrect NAT2*6/*6 calls. By contrast, using Aldy on LRS‐VCFs produced two or three diplotype calls for 37.3% of the subjects, despite the input being phased. One explanation may be that the current version of Aldy is not fully optimized to interpret phased variants and treats them similarly to unphased data, leading to ambiguous haplotype‐unresolved calls, despite phased input.

Although phenotype discordance was low overall (2%), it occurred when alternate diplotypes led to different phenotype classifications. Phenotype misclassification can lead to differing dosing recommendations and potentially impact drug exposure, efficacy, or toxicity risk. For hydralazine therapy, for example, NAT2 PMs have higher hydralazine exposure compared with IMs or RMs, increasing their risk for hydralazine‐associated adverse effects, including lupus. 5 Thus, the CPIC guideline recommends different dosing strategies for PMs than IMs and RMs. 7

While previous studies reported > 99% accuracy for Aldy with LRS data, discrepancies were observed for complex genes. 21 Our findings demonstrate that discrepancies can also arise in a less‐complex and relatively small gene like NAT2, underscoring the need for tool‐specific interpretation regardless of gene complexity. Likewise, input format matters: BAM files provide richer information (e.g., coverage and structural variants) but can still lead to misclassifications. VCF files, even when phased, may introduce ambiguity in diplotype and phenotype assignments. Tools like StellarPGx may report only single (high confidence) diplotype calls without guaranteeing diplotype or phenotype concordance. Given these tool‐specific assumptions and filter approaches, cross‐validation and gene‐aware tool selection are essential for robust pharmacogenomic analysis.

We also evaluated how accurately phenotype is predicted had the subjects been genotyped with a panel that only interrogates a few selected variants. In our analysis, 82% of the genotypes determined by a 4‐SNP genotype test did not match their LRS‐based genotypes, although phenotype discordance was low for Whites at 0.5%. This was expected as the 4‐SNP panel collapses (or defaults) many distinct haplotypes to NAT2*5, *34, *40, and *46 as illustrated in the example shown in Figure S2 . Based on PharmVar nomenclature, NAT2*34 and *40, rather than *6 and *7, respectively, are the accurate default assignments in the absence of testing for c.803G>A. However, laboratories may nonetheless default and report these as NAT2*6 and *7. As NAT2*6, *7, *34, and *40 all have decreased function, the phenotype is accurately predicted, though. Notably, genotype‐to‐phenotype discordance was higher among non‐Whites at 4.5% and 24.8% for the 4‐SNP and 5‐SNP test simulations, respectively, reflecting greater haplotypic diversity and limited resolution of haplotypes with variants that eluded detection. If subjects had been tested with the 5‐SNP genotype test, diplotype concordance improved to 56.68%, but phenotype misclassification rose to 25% among Whites, as inclusion of c.803G>A increased ambiguity for diplotype calls, which led to conflicting phenotype assignments. The increase in phenotype discordance was driven by ambiguous diplotype calls of which one contains a NAT2*30 allele (*6/*16 (PM) or *4/*30 (IM), *5/*6 (PM) or *1/*30 (IM), *6/*16 (PM), or *4/*30 (IM)). NAT2*30 was not observed in our cohort and appears to be rather rare globally (< 0.01%). 37 To the best of our knowledge, there is only a single experimentally confirmed observation of NAT2*30 37 , 38 while all other reports inferred this allele computationally. 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 Therefore, individuals with ambiguous diplotype calls most likely do not have the NAT2*30‐containing diplotype.

Although limited testing accurately predicts phenotypes for most patients, rare haplotypes elude detection, clearly demonstrating the limitations of this approach. Considering the recent discoveries of novel star alleles across racial/ethnic groups, the number of undetected haplotypes, and thus, incorrect phenotype predictions, may further increase as more haplotypes are being discovered. Our findings highlight the limitations of panel‐based genotyping approaches that interrogate only the most common NAT2 variants. LRS provides added value, enabling accurate resolution of population‐specific diversity and granularity needed as guidelines may become available for additional NAT2 gene‐drug pairs. Regarding generalizability, our cohort includes both pediatric and adult participants (parents). Furthermore, age does not influence NAT2 phenotype assignment from genotype 7 and therefore does not affect the interpretation of our findings.

NAT2 allele frequency

This is the first study to report experimentally phased NAT2 allele frequencies from LRS using updated PharmVar nomenclature. In the White ancestry subset, 3.4% had haplotypes other than NAT2*4, *5, *6, *7, and *14, including novel alleles. Among Blacks, 9% had other haplotypes most notably NAT2*43 (3.57%), consistent with previous reports describing higher haplotypic diversity in this population, 42 , 43 and among Hispanics, *7 and *4 were enriched, suggesting a higher proportion of IMs or RMs. 44 Moreover, we identified several rare, ancestry‐specific alleles, such as NAT2*36, and *47 (3.57% and 1.79% in Blacks), and *41 (1.32% in Multiracial individuals). These findings underscore the need to tailor test panels to include variants that are relevant for the patient population undergoing testing, as well as the development of reference materials to ensure testing accuracy and clinical interpretation, particularly in multiracial or underrepresented populations. Direct comparisons of allele frequencies with those reported by prior studies are somewhat difficult due to the recent overhaul of allele designations by PharmVar, as well as the limitation that many studies computationally inferred haplotypes from sequence data and/or limited testing strategies. Regardless, NAT2 allele frequencies in our cohort were found to align with those of a recent report that used updated PharmVar NAT2 nomenclature 45 but differed noticeably from those presented by another. 42

Study limitations

Although the cohort was diverse, allele frequencies for some subgroups were based on relatively small sample sizes, and thus, allele frequency data are estimates. Also, functional effects of novel variants were inferred from in silico predictions and not experimentally characterized. These limitations may affect the interpretation of novel variants and race/ethnic‐specific frequency estimates but do not alter the principal findings regarding the advantages of long‐read sequencing for NAT2 variant detection, phasing, diplotype assignment, and phenotype prediction.

CONCLUSIONS

This study demonstrates that LRS enables highly accurate NAT2 diplotype and phenotype assignments compared to SRS and panel‐based genotyping methods and showed that diplotype caller performance and file input format can considerably impact call accuracies. Phenotype misclassification varied by method and ethnicity ranging from 2.5 to 4.6% for SRS, and 0.5% for Whites and 4.5% for non‐Whites if genotypes were determined by a 4‐SNP test panel that only covers the most common functionally relevant variants. These findings support the use of LRS for precise, population‐aware pharmacogenomic analysis.
