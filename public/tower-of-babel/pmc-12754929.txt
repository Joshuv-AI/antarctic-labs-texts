Sustained hippocampal neuroinflammation and subsequent glutamatergic dysfunction in juvenile idiopathic arthritis: evidence from proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H-MRS)
Authors: Haiwei Han, Yifei Weng, Shumu Yang, Chengkun Han, Cuili Yi, Hua Wu, Jihong Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic pain and psychiatric comorbidities in juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) persist even during disease remission, suggesting central nervous system (CNS) alterations. This study used proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H-MRS) to investigate neurometabolic changes in the right hippocampus of JIA patients across active and inactive disease phases.MethodsA cohort of 248 JIA patients (61 treatment-naïve patients with active JIA and 187 patients with inactive JIA) and 57 healthy controls (HCs) underwent 1H-MRS of the right hippocampus. Metabolite ratios of total N-acetylaspartate (tNAA), total choline (tCho), myo-inositol (mI), glutamate (Glu), and glutamate-glutamine complex (Glx) relative to total creatine (tCr) were quantified. Associations with systemic inflammation (ESR and CRP) and clinical indices (JADAS-27 and CHAQ) were evaluated.ResultsCompared with HCs, both JIA groups showed elevated mI/tCr and reduced Glu/tCr and Glx/tCr in the inactive JIA group (all, P < 0.05). mI/tCr was positively correlated with ESR (rho = 0.269) and CRP (rho = 0.287) in active JIA patients. However, the results did not survive the rigid multiple correction. Notably, tNAA/tCr, tCho/tCr, Glu/tCr and Glx/tCr showed no significant correlations with systemic inflammation and clinical indices for treatment-naïve and inactive JIA patients.ConclusionsSustained hippocampal neuroinflammation (indicated by elevated mI/tCr) and the subsequent glutamatergic synaptic dysfunction (indicated by reduced Glu/tCr and Glx/tCr) were identified in JIA patients. Peripheral inflammation may drive microglial activation during active disease, highlighting the hippocampus as a vulnerable CNS target in chronic inflammatory states.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13075-025-03695-5.

Background

Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) is a heterogeneous condition that begins in childhood or early adolescence and typically presents with joint swelling and pain, along with potential systemic complications [1]. It is categorized into seven different subtypes and affects approximately 1 in 1000 children, depending on the population. JIA’s clinical course is marked by inflammation in multiple joints, often leading to progressive functional limitations and disability, characterized by variable exacerbations and remissions [2]. Beyond articular manifestations, emerging evidence highlights the profound detrimental impact of prolonged or chronic systemic inflammation on cognitive development, potentially preventing millions of affected children from reaching their full developmental potential [3]. JIA can also have a debilitating impact on children and adolescents as they often experience lower quality of life because of various factors, such as pain, methotrexate-induced nausea, sleep disturbances, and frequent hospital visits [4].

Pain is the most common and distressing symptom of JIA. In recent years, the introduction of biologic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs earlier in the disease course has improved the outcome for JIA patients. However, this has not directly translated into better pain outcomes as some children experience pain during inactive disease despite adequate treatment and effective disease control [5]. Recent studies of JIA patients reported that compared to their peers, children and adolescents with JIA had a higher risk of psychiatric diagnoses, particularly of depression and adjustment disorders [6–8]. In addition, they may also experience impaired educational performance [9, 10]. In a recent population-based study of educational performance, Lim et al. found that patients with childhood-onset chronic rheumatic diseases performed worse than their age-, sex-, and postal code-matched peers on their grade 12 standardized test results and were less likely to be enrolled in grade 12 by the appropriate age (17 years). Furthermore, patients with childhood-onset chronic rheumatic disease showed worse educational performance, independent of sociodemographic and mental health factors known to affect education outcomes [10]. In summary, despite advances in biological therapies, many patients experience residual pain, psychiatric comorbidities (e.g., depression, anxiety), and cognitive deficits, implicating CNS involvement.

One of the crucial and complex brain regions implicated in JIA may be the hippocampus. Its functional role has been well characterized, with critical roles in learning, memory processes, spatial navigation, regulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis function, and modulation of emotional behavior [11]. Based on its cellular composition, the Cornu Ammonis is divided into four parts, the so-called Sommer’s sectors CA1 to CA4. The Cornu Ammonis continues inferomedially in the parahippocampal gyrus, which is a gray matter structure that forms the transition area between the basal and mesial areas of the temporal lobe. The subiculum is the medial and superior edge of the parahippocampal gyrus and its site of union with the Cornu Ammonis. The hippocampus is surrounded by several fissures which are referred to as perihippocampal fissures. The transverse fissure of Bichat is the lateral extension of the ambient cistern, which separates the superiorly located thalamus from the inferiorly located parahippocampal gyrus and hippocampus. The superolateral extension of the transverse fissure is the choroidal fissure. The inferolateral extension of the transverse fissure is the hippocampal fissure, which extends between the dentate gyrus and the subiculum and is often obliterated and not visible on MR [12, 13]. Several notable connections in and around the hippocampus are central to these functions [14]. The hippocampus can be adversely affected by various pathogens, neurotoxins, diseases, injuries, and environmental insults, and it also has exquisite vulnerability to neuroinflammatory processes, with some of its subregions found to be specific sites of neuroinflammatory pathology in ex vivo studies [15, 16]. It has often been suggested that the harmful effects of these insults may be greater on the hippocampus than on other brain areas. Thus, we hypothesized that disruption of normal hippocampus function occurs in children and adolescents with JIA.

Proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H-MRS) enables non-invasive quantification of neurochemicals linked to glial activation (e.g., myo-inositol) and synaptic function (e.g., glutamate) [4, 17]. The acquisition of the spatially localized metabolic information available from proton spectra can be correlated with MR images and other imaging methods. This correlation may lead to the development of a set of physiological, anatomical, and biochemical indices, which may be a powerful approach for investigating the underlying basis of many clinical disorders [18]. Recent studies suggest the presence of abnormal hippocampal brain neurochemistry in several chronic disorders. For example, some patients suffering from chronic diseases like asthma, chronic pancreatitis, schizophrenia, and fibromyalgia have altered metabolite levels in the hippocampus [19–22]. Dysfunction of neuronal cell and reactive glial cells indicating by the lower N-acetylaspartate/creatine (NAA/Cr) ratio and the greater choline/Cr ratio were investigated in several brain regions of patients with different rheumatic autoimmune diseases [23]. Given the rapid developmental trajectory of the central nervous system (CNS) in pediatric populations, this period is characterized by heightened neuroplasticity concomitant with increased vulnerability to pathological perturbations [24]. Nevertheless, hippocampal neurometabolic alterations in JIA—a prototypical pediatric inflammatory disorder—remain uncharacterized.

This study addresses this gap by analyzing hippocampal metabolites in JIA patients and exploring their relationship with systemic inflammation. Total N-acetylaspartate (tNAA), glutamate (Glu), the complex of glutamate and glutamine (Glx), myo-inositol (mI), total choline (tCho), and total creatine (tCr), including Cr and phosphocreatine (pCr), which are all considered to be key brain metabolites, are believed to provide information on the status of neurons and glial cells within the tissue, cell wall metabolism, neurotransmitters, and the overall energy status. To our knowledge, no study has used neuroimaging tools like 1H-MRS to examine the hippocampal neurometabolic changes in JIA patients and study the association between peripheral inflammation and hippocampal neurometabolites in children and adolescents with JIA.

Methods

Participants

In this study, we enrolled 187 inactive JIA patients and 61 JIA treatment-naïve patients who had active disease and were newly diagnosed according to the International League of Associations for Rheumatology (ILAR) classification criteria between November 2022 and December 2023 were recruited from the Pediatric Clinic of The First Affiliated Hospital of Xiamen University, Fujian, China. The ILAR subtypes of the 61 treatment-naïve patients were as follows: oligoarticular (n = 25), enthesitis-related (n = 18), polyarticular (n = 12), systemic (n = 5), and undifferentiated (n = 1). The ILAR subtypes of the 187 patients in remission (JADAS ≤ 1) were as follows: oligoarticular (n = 87), enthesitis-related (n = 47), polyarticular (n = 29), systemic (n = 28), and undifferentiated (n = 6). For patients in both groups, all self-reported symptoms, medical imaging results, and laboratory blood tests were recorded in routine clinical settings. Furthermore, 57 age-matched children without neurological/psychiatric disorders were included in this study. These healthy controls (HCs) underwent no laboratory blood test but were followed up for at least 1-year by an experienced rheumatologist to exclude JIA. JIA patients and HCs with visible brain anatomical anomaly identified on routine MRI by two experienced radiologists were excluded from this study. All the JIA patients and HCs had a medial temporal lobe atrophy score of 0 and showed no abnormal signal intensity. This study was performed according to the seventh revision (2013) of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Hospital Affiliated to Xiamen University. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The parents or guardians of all participants gave written informed consent before the neurological tests and MRI scanning.

Neuroimaging protocol and 1H-MRS data processing

All scans were performed on a 3.0-T MR scanner (Siemens Tim Verio, Erlangen, Germany), and an eight-channel phase-array coil was used to provide an optimum signal-to-noise ratio for MR imaging and spectroscopy data. While in the supine position, the participant’s head was immobilized using expandable ear cushions. Sagittal T1-weighted images were acquired using 3-dimensional magnetization prepared rapid gradient echo (MP-RAGE) sequence [2150 ms, 1100 ms, 4.38 ms, and 1, representing TR, inversion time (TI), TE, and signal average respectively]. With an in-plane resolution of 256 × 256, slice thickness of 1 mm, field of view of 256 mm, and 162 slices, an isotropic voxel resolution of 1 mm3 was obtained. Using a multiplanar reconstruction (MPR) routine provided by the manufacturer, the sagittal images were used to generate axial and coronal images. These images had the same resolution as the sagittal images. Axial T2-weighted turbo spin-echo images were acquired [8000 ms, 107, and 2, representing TR, effective TE, and signal average, respectively] with a turbo factor of 15 and using a 256 × 256 image matrix and slice thickness of 5 mm for transverse direction. The approach was to first cover the whole brain and then delineate the hippocampal anatomy in finer detail with better slice resolution (2.0 mm) to increase the accuracy of placing the MRS voxel.

Single-voxel PRESS spectroscopy focused on the right hippocampus was performed with the following parameters: TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30 ms, voxel size = 1.3 × 1.5 × 3.5 cm3, and signal average = 48 (Fig. 1). Water-suppressed and unsuppressed spectra were analyzed using the LC model (v6.3–1 J), with metabolite concentrations normalized to tCr. Clinical and laboratory assessments

Before MRI scanning, patients were assessed using the validated neuropsychological and clinical scales. The Juvenile Arthritis Disease Activity Scores (JADAS-27) [26] and Childhood Health Assessment Questionnaire (CHAQ) [27] were used to assess the disease severity and the impact of the disability on life in JIA treatment-naïve active and JIA-inactive groups. The JADAS-27 was used to evaluate the disease activity of certain JIA subgroups (oligoarticular JIA and polyarticular JIA). Moreover, the Juvenile Spondyloarthritis Disease Activity Index (JSpADA) and the systemic Juvenile Arthritis Disease Activity Score (sJADAS) were used for enthesitis-related arthritis and systemic JIA, respectively [28]. Notably, remission in JIA was defined as JADAS ≤ 1 [28]. A visual analog scale (VAS) was used to measure the intensity of the pain involving joint discomfort in the JIA-active and JIA-inactive groups. Venous blood samples were analyzed for ESR and CRP.

Statistical analysis

Clinical categorical characteristics are summarized as frequencies, and comparisons of proportions among groups were performed using the chi-square test. Normality was assessed per group using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K-S) test. For analyses adjusting for a covariate, we tested for homogeneity of regression slopes (parallelism) by evaluating the group*covariate interaction term in a preliminary model. If the assumptions of normality, homogeneity of variances (assessed by Levene’s test), and parallelism were satisfied, we performed analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) to compare group differences while adjusting for the covariate. If the data severely violated the normality assumption and/or the assumption of parallelism was not met, the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis (K-W) H-test was used instead to compare the groups without covariate adjustment. After a significant overall ANCOVA F-test or K-W H-test, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni correction. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS statistics 21.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL), and a two-sided P value of < 0.05 was considered significant. Spearman rank correlations were used to assess metabolite–clinical/inflammatory relationships. The strength of the correlation is reported as the correlation coefficient along with its 95% confidence interval (95% CI), and the statistically significant threshold was set at a corrected P < 0.05, using the False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction method to account for multiple comparisons.

Moreover, compared to the JIA-inactive group, the JIA treatment-naïve group had significantly higher JADAS-27 scores, VAS scores for joint pain, CHAQ score, and levels of ESR and CRP (all P < 0.05) (Table 1).

Inter-group comparisons of neurometabolites for the right hippocampus

The normality assessment on metabolites shows that the ratio of tCho/tCr (P = 0.866), mI/tCr (P = 0.536), and Glu/tCr (P = 0.117) had normal data distribution, whereas the ratio of tNAA/tCr (P = 0.001) and Glx/tCr (P = 0.004) showed non-normal data distribution. The results of normality assessment are also shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. Inter-group comparisons of neurometabolite concentrations were performed in the right hippocampus, which include sex-by-group interaction terms in models. As the group comparison of the tCho/tCr ratio was significantly affected by a gender × group interaction (P = 0.035) and gender imbalance between groups, we chose to use the Kruskal–Walls H-test to eliminate the influence of these factors. Besides, for normally distributed data, we used ANCOVA for further group comparisons, and for non-normally distributed data, we used the Kruskal–Walls H-test. Significant differences in the Glu/tCr (Glu is included in the Glx), Glx/tCr, and mI/tCr ratios were found among the three groups (Table 2). No significant difference was found for tNAA/tCr and tCho/tCr for the three groups. We used covariance analysis to adjust for the gender disparity among groups, and pairwise comparisons were conducted by Bonferroni analysis. The neurometabolite level of mI/tCr was significantly higher in the JIA treatment-naïve group than in the HC group and JIA-inactive group. The level of Glu/tCr was significantly lower in the JIA-inactive group than in the HCs; the ratio of Glx/tCr was also significantly lower in JIA-inactive groups than in the HCs (Table 3). The data quality of 1H-MRS was assessed by visual inspection to identify artifacts, spectroscopic curve distortions, and signal-to-noise ratio disturbances that could invalidate the findings.

Intra-group correlations of neurometabolite levels with different indices

Owing to the significant inter-group differences in the levels of Glu/tCr, Glx/tCr, and mI/tCr in the right hippocampus, we studied the intra-group correlation of these neurometabolites with other indices. Analysis of the JIA treatment-naïve group indicated a tendency of positive correlations between mI/tCr and peripheral levels of CPR, ESR [mI/tCr and CPR: rho = 0.287, 95% CI of (−0.343, 0.739), Puncorrected = 0.024; mI/tCr and ESR: rho = 0.269, Puncorrected = 0.036, 95% CI of (−0.360, 0.731); Fig. 2]. However, the significance did not survive the FDR correction. All the corrected results are shown in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. Other neurometabolites for Glu/tCr, Glx/tCr, and tCho/tCr were not found to be significantly correlated with peripheral inflammatory markers, such as CPR and ESR, in the JIA treatment-naïve group. No significant correlations were noted between any neurometabolites of the right hippocampus and clinical indices like CHAQ, JADAS scores, and VAS scores in JIA treatment-naïve group. Discussion

This study provides novel evidence of sustained hippocampal neuroinflammation and glutamatergic dysfunction in JIA, independent of disease activity. These findings may indirectly reflect the vulnerability of hippocampal microstructure in the context of chronic peripheral inflammation [29]. Elevated mI/tCr, a biomarker of glial activation, suggests chronic microglial priming, which is potentially driven by peripheral cytokines crossing a compromised blood–brain barrier (BBB) or via vagal afferent signaling [30]. Reduced Glu/tCr and Glx/tCr ratios imply disrupted glutamate–glutamine cycling, possibly resulting from reactive astrocytosis impairing synaptic glutamate reuptake. Notably, a preserved tNAA/tCr ratio indicates no overt neuronal or axonal loss, distinguishing JIA from neurodegenerative conditions. To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate persistent neuroinflammation and dysfunction of glutamatergic synaptic transmission in the right hippocampus of JIA patients by using an in vivo imaging method.

Our findings of elevated mI/tCr ratios in the right hippocampus of both active and inactive JIA groups suggested sustained neuroinflammation in the hippocampus caused by microglial cell activation. Notably, mI is present freely in the blood and as phosphatidylinositol in cell membranes, particularly in those of CNS tissues, wherein it peaks at 3.56 ppm on short TE1H-MRS (TE = 30 ms or 35 ms) [31, 32]. Furthermore, it is involved in osmoregulatory processes and membrane and myelin sheath structures due to a key precursor of membrane phospho-inositides and phospholids, and mI also plays an important role in second messenger systems [33]. Notably, mI, which is synthesized rapidly from glucose, is stored in the microglia before being used in the neuronal phosphatidylinositol second messenger system [34]. Moreover, mI is preferentially concentrated in glial cells and is used as a putative marker of microglial activation or gliosis [35]. Neuroinflammation is characterized by an increase in mI levels, and increased mI levels in neural regions have been found to be associated with and precede dementia [36]. During neuroimmune activation, increased mI levels could reflect glial proliferation or an increase in glial cell size and have been linked to subsequent cognitive impairment [37].

The hippocampus is critical for several aspects of learning and memory and is unique among other cortical regions in its structure, function, and potential for plasticity. This remarkable region recapitulates development throughout the lifespan with enduring neurogenesis and well-characterized plasticity. The structure and traits of the hippocampus that distinguish it from other brain regions, however, may also be behind its increased vulnerability to insult and injury [15]. It has been suggested that due to the intrinsic fragility of its vascular network, the hippocampal blood–brain barrier (BBB) is less able to cope with hypoperfusion and anoxia compared to other cortical areas [38]. Moreover, a recent study using dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI suggested that the BBB provides less protection to the hippocampus and hippocampal formation relative to other brain structures [39]. In summary, these findings indicate that weakness of the BBB may underlie the potential selective vulnerability of the hippocampus to various insults.

Chronic peripheral inflammatory conditions, such as arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, and inflammatory bowel disease, are associated with both altered neurogenesis and behavior abnormalities [29, 40, 41]. Peripheral inflammatory signals can enter into the CNS by several routes to induce neuroinflammation in the context of chronic inflammatory arthritis. Locally produced proinflammatory cytokines activate primary afferent pathways like the vagus nerve, and circulating proinflammatory cytokines access the brain via the circumventricular organs and saturable transport system [42]. Proinflammatory cytokines disrupt the BBB, allowing activated lymphocytes and cytokines to circulate between the peripheral immune system and the brain. As the brain’s resident immune cells, microglia rapidly respond to inflammation and engage in classic immune functions, such as cytokine and chemokine production and phagocytosis of pathogens and cellular debris [43]. When activated by peripheral and central inflammatory signaling, microglia release multiple proinflammatory molecules, including cytokines, chemokines, reactive oxygen species, and nitric oxide, resulting in neuroinflammation. Some animal studies indicated that microglia in the hippocampal neuronal system of adult mice have a higher proliferative capability in response to LPS-induced inflammatory stimulation, relative to other brain regions examined [11].

In the JIA cohort, key drivers of inflammation, including elevated levels of IL-1, IL-6, IL-17, and TNF-α and pathogenic T helper cell activation, have been implicated in hippocampal dysfunction [1]. The hippocampus contains a very high density of IL-1 receptors that mediate inflammatory processes, with the highest expression of IL-1 receptors located in the granule cell layer of the dentate gyrus and pyramidal cell layers (CA 1–4) of the hippocampus [44, 45]. Owing to the potential selective vulnerability of the BBB, the higher proliferative capability of microglia and IL-1 receptors in the hippocampus, and the higher vulnerability of the hippocampus to insults, persistent neuroinflammation in the right hippocampus occurred in both active and inactive JIA patients.

In this study, dysfunction of the glutamatergic synaptic transmission occurred in inactive JIA patients under the condition of persistent neuroinflammation in the hippocampus. Cellular homeostasis of the neurotransmitter glutamate is essential for normal brain function. Glutamate is not only the main excitatory neurotransmitter of the brain but also a metabolic hub connecting glucose and amino acid metabolism with synaptic transmission [46]. Glutamate is extensively recycled between neurons and astrocytes in a process known as the glutamate–glutamine cycle; the recycling of glutamate is closely linked to brain energy metabolism and is essential to sustain glutamatergic transmission [47]. Astrocytes, the most abundant cells in the CNS, are responsible for neuronal activity regulation, circuit refinement, and vascular function maintenance, ensuring tissue homeostasis from development to aging [48]. Under neuroinflammatory conditions, the bidirectional crosstalk between astrocytes and microglia becomes consequential. When peripheral proinflammatory cytokines infiltrate the brain tissue, microglia undergo transcriptional reprogramming and consequently release a plethora of cytokines and inflammatory mediators that dynamically communicate with astrocytes [49, 50]. Conversely, reactive astrocytes can further modulate microglial activity, creating a feedback loop that perpetuates neuroinflammatory responses [51]. In our study, the decrease in the Glu/tCr and Glx/tCr ratios in the right hippocampus in inactive JIA patients indicated impaired functioning of the glutamate–glutamine cycle. Astrocytes play a vital role in the glutamate–glutamine cycle, and in the right hippocampus, reactive astrocytes under persistent neuroinflammation exhibit impaired physiological function in the glutamate–glutamine cycle. Therefore, our findings highlight a potential mechanism by which persistent neuroinflammation disrupts hippocampal excitatory neurotransmission in JIA, irrespective of peripheral disease activity.

Additionally, we found no significant differences in the levels of tNAA/tCr between JIA patients and healthy controls. The NAA is synthesized in the mitochondria of neurons and then transported into neuronal cytoplasm and intercellularly along the axon. The rates of NAA synthesis are indicative of the rate of glucose consumption by neurons, thus providing information on the level of neuron activity [52]. No significant differences in the tNAA/tCr ratio were noted among the three groups; our findings suggested that no neuronal or axonal losses occurred in JIA patients. Notably, patients with JIA did not typically suffer severe neuronal injury, and consequently, they did not exhibit gross anatomical changes to the CNS. Instead, they were likely to experience subtle changes that can influence the pattern of connections between different neural networks, which is consistent with our previous findings [53], or can result in neurochemical changes detectable only by advanced neuroimaging techniques like 1H-MRS [17].

In this study, there was no significant correlation between hippocampal metabolites and clinical indices like JADSA, CHAQ, and VAS scores in both active and inactive JIA patients. This is an interesting finding, and the underlying reasons for this remain unclear. We speculate that the hippocampal metabolic changes may be dissociated from current joint disease activity or pain severity, instead representing a more persistent hippocampal vulnerability. This finding requires further validation in the further studies.

This study has some limitations. First, the healthy control cohort, despite being matched in terms of age, happened to be significantly different in terms of gender. Despite the statistical adjustment, gender imbalance in HCs may have confounded our results. Furthermore, we measured cytokine levels only in a subset of JIA treatment-naïve patients, and we will correlate cytokine levels with the neurometabolites in a future study. Second, we only focused on the metabolic profile of the right hippocampus and not the bilateral hippocampus in JIA patients. In future studies, it would be interesting to assess whether similar neurometabolic alterations occur in other brain regions as well, such as the amygdala. Third, the specific cellular source of each of the MRS-visible metabolites cannot be determined with certainty. For instance, although Glu and Glx in 1H-MRS are commonly referred to as “excitatory neurotransmitters,” it is notable that these metabolites are involved in various cell functions, including cell signaling and cell metabolism. Thus, whether mI elevations reflect neuroinflammatory processes remains to be further evaluated [54]. Finally, it would be interesting to cover different hippocampal subregions; however, to achieve a satisfying signal-to-noise ratio in MRS measurements, a minimal volume of interest size is necessary.

Conclusion

This 1H-MRS study identifies persistent hippocampal neuroinflammation and glutamatergic dysfunction in JIA, underscoring the CNS as a target of chronic inflammation. Therefore, therapeutic strategies aimed at mitigating peripheral inflammation may concurrently protect hippocampal integrity in pediatric populations with JIA.
