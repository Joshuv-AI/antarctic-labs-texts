Introduction

Aorto-left ventricular tunnel (ALVT) is a rare congenital cardiac defect consisting of an extracardiac communication between the ascending aorta and the left ventricle that bypasses the aortic valve.1–3 Its embryological basis remains uncertain, but abnormal development and separation of the cushions contributing to the aortic and pulmonary roots have been proposed.1–3 Because ALVT may produce an eccentric diastolic jet into the left ventricle, it can be mistaken for primary aortic regurgitation.1,2

Most patients are diagnosed in infancy or childhood because of heart failure, progressive left ventricular volume overload, or aortic valve dysfunction, although asymptomatic presentation with a murmur and survival into adulthood have been reported.1,3 We report a young adult with incidentally detected ALVT in whom real-time three-dimensional transthoracic echocardiography improved anatomical understanding and helped guide management.

Case presentation

A 19-year-old man was referred after an abnormal murmur was detected during a routine health examination. He did not have exertional dyspnoea, chest pain, palpitations, syncope, prolonged fever, or previous cardiac disease. Physical examination revealed a grade 3/6 systodiastolic murmur at the third left sternal border. Blood pressure was 100/70 mmHg, and heart rate was 70 b.p.m. Peripheral pulses were normal. There were no Corrigan, Musset, Quincke, or Traube signs, and no Marfan features were identified. Electrocardiography showed sinus rhythm with left ventricular hypertrophy. Chest radiography showed no cardiomegaly or pulmonary plethora.

Two-dimensional transthoracic echocardiography showed a dilated left ventricle and abnormal flow entering the left ventricular cavity from the aortic root region. In the parasternal short-axis view, a tunnel-like anechoic space was identified adjacent to the commissural region between the right coronary cusp and the non-coronary cusp. Colour Doppler imaging demonstrated two distinct turbulent jets: one arising from the tunnel and another corresponding to aortic regurgitation. These findings raised suspicion of ALVT rather than isolated primary aortic valve regurgitation (Figures 1 and 2; Supplementary material online, Videos S1 and S2).

Real-time three-dimensional transthoracic echocardiography provided clearer spatial visualization of the tunnel. It demonstrated the relationship between the tunnel-like defect, the aortic root, and the left ventricular cavity, supporting the diagnosis of ALVT and improving anatomical confidence before further imaging (Figure 3; Supplementary material online, Video S3).

Real-time three-dimensional transthoracic echocardiography. Three-dimensional short-axis view demonstrating the spatial anatomy of the aorto-left ventricular tunnel and its relationship to the aortic valve and left ventricular outflow region.

Computed tomography angiography confirmed an extracardiac communication between the ascending aorta and the left ventricle. The tunnel ran near the commissural region between the right coronary cusp and the non-coronary cusp and did not involve the coronary arteries (Figure 4). Computed tomography is useful in ALVT because it can delineate the tunnel course and clarify the relationship between the tunnel and the coronary arteries before intervention.1,3,4

Aorto-left ventricular tunnel has been classified according to tunnel morphology and the presence of extracardiac aneurysmal dilatation, intracardiac aneurysmal dilatation, aortic valve distortion, or coronary involvement.1–3 Type I describes a simple tunnel with a slit-like aortic opening and no significant aortic valve distortion.2,3 In this case, the tunnel arose near the right coronary cusp/non-coronary cusp commissural region and entered the left ventricle without coronary involvement, marked aneurysmal dilatation, or significant aortic valvular distortion; therefore, it was classified as type I ALVT.

Although transcatheter closure may be feasible in selected patients with favourable anatomy, particularly type I ALVT, procedural selection must consider the proximity of the tunnel to the coronary ostia, aortic cusps, and commissural tissue.2,3 Surgery was selected because the tunnel was adjacent to the right coronary cusp/non-coronary cusp commissural region. Direct surgical closure allowed secure treatment of the aortic end of the tunnel and reinforcement of the aortic annular/commissural region under direct visualization, minimizing the risks of residual shunt, aortic valve distortion, device-related interference, and coronary compromise. The patient underwent successful closure (Figure 5), with uneventful post-operative recovery.

Discussion

Aorto-left ventricular tunnel is an uncommon congenital anomaly with a reported incidence of less than 0.1% of congenital heart disease, and adult presentation is rare.1–3 The clinical picture depends on tunnel size, left ventricular volume overload, and associated aortic valve pathology. Published reviews show that infants often present with heart failure, whereas older patients may be identified because of a murmur.1,3

The key diagnostic challenge is differentiation from primary aortic regurgitation. Both may produce an eccentric diastolic jet entering the left ventricle. In this patient, the systodiastolic murmur, preserved valve morphology, tunnel-like structure adjacent to the aortic cusp commissural region, and two distinct colour Doppler jets supported ALVT rather than isolated valvular disease. Echocardiography is the primary imaging modality for suspected ALVT and can define the location, size, and effect of the tunnel on the aortic valve.2,3

In this case, two-dimensional echocardiography and colour Doppler suggested the diagnosis, whereas real-time three-dimensional transthoracic echocardiography provided clearer spatial delineation. Computed tomography angiography was complementary because it confirmed the extracardiac course and excluded coronary involvement. Previous reports also emphasize the value of CT in defining complex aortic root anatomy, tunnel trajectory, and coronary relationships before repair.1,3,4

Anatomical classification is clinically relevant. Hovaguimian’s classification includes type I, a simple tunnel with a slit-like aortic opening and no aortic valve distortion; type II, a tunnel with extracardiac aneurysmal dilatation; type III, a tunnel with intracardiac aneurysmal dilatation; and type IV, combined extracardiac and intracardiac aneurysmal components.2,3 Our patient had type I ALVT, a relatively favourable anatomy when repaired before irreversible aortic valve damage or severe ventricular remodelling.

Early closure is generally recommended to prevent progressive left ventricular volume overload, aortic valve distortion, and late complications.1,3,5 Surgical repair remains the most frequently reported treatment, while transcatheter closure has emerged as an option in carefully selected cases.2,3 In a surgical cohort, patients without preoperative aortic valve issues had no deaths or reoperations during follow-up, whereas patients with preoperative aortic valve issues had higher rates of death and reoperation.5 In this patient, surgery was chosen because the aortic opening was close to the right coronary cusp/non-coronary cusp commissural region and direct repair allowed annular/commissural reinforcement. The uneventful post-operative course supports this anatomy-based approach.
