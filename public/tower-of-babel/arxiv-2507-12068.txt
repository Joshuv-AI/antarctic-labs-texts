Analyzing the Geometry of Immersions of Co–Dimension One via Shape Operator Dynamics
MOHAMMADJAVAD HABIBIVOSTAKOLAEI

Abstract
We study the extrinsic geometry of isometric immersions into Riemannian manifolds of co–dimension one via a fourth–order geometric evolution of the shape operator. Motivated by bi–harmonic map theory and the generalized Chen’s conjecture, we introduce the moduli flow: a tensorial gradient flow that decreases a natural energy measuring curvature variation. We prove global existence, entropy monotonicity, and exponential convergence to minimal immersions under curvature bounds. The flow evolves intrinsically on the moduli space of symmetric tensors modulo orthogonal conjugation. Minimal immersions emerge as unique attractors, and we establish their linear stability. Our results offer a new analytic framework for understanding rigidity, stability, and asymptotic behavior of curvature–driven flows in submanifold geometry.



Keywords:
Bi–Harmonic map heat flow, Moduli space, Isometric Immersion, Non–Positive Sectional Curvature, Moduli Dynamics, Entropy functional.

MSC Classification:
53C42; 35K55; 58J35.

Consider

•

•

We are going to show that

Theorem 1.1
.

Every bi–harmonic immersion

Although the minimality of certain isometric immersions into Riemannian manifolds with non-positive sectional curvature has previously been established by Jiang in the context of biharmonic maps (see

22

), the novelty of this work lies in its entirely different approach. Rather than assuming biharmonicity, we study the evolution of the second fundamental form via a moduli flow governed by curvature dynamics and variational principles. Our framework introduces energy and entropy functionals for the shape operator and derives minimality as a consequence of flow convergence. This dynamical viewpoint provides an alternative geometric mechanism for rigidity and opens new directions beyond the classical setting.

This theorem can be seen as a special case of generalized Chen’s conjecture. One of the central problems in the study of submanifold theory is the generalized Chen’s conjecture, which asserts that every biharmonic isometric immersion into a Riemannian manifold with non-positive sectional curvature must be minimal. Originally posed by B.-Y. Chen in the early 1990s (see
[
8
]
), this conjecture generalizes the classical result that biharmonic submanifolds in Euclidean spaces are minimal. Although the conjecture holds in several important cases, counterexamples have since been found in certain non-Euclidean ambient spaces. Nevertheless, it continues to serve as a guiding question in the analysis of curvature-related differential operators and geometric variational problems. One of the foundational results supporting the conjecture was given by Jiang
[
15
]
, who introduced the notion of biharmonic maps and showed that the Euler–Lagrange equation for bi–harmonicity generalizes harmonic map theory. Later, Chen and Ishikawa
[
9
]
studied biharmonic submanifolds in Euclidean and space form settings, establishing that biharmonic hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with constant mean curvature must be minimal. These results laid the groundwork for a wide body of subsequent research. However, the conjecture’s validity in general ambient spaces was challenged by counterexamples. A significant turning point came with the work of Caddeo, Montaldo, and Piu
[
10
]
, who constructed explicit non-minimal biharmonic immersions into spheres and certain warped products. These examples showed that the conjecture does not hold universally in all Riemannian manifolds. Moreover, Nakauchi and Urakawa
[
21
]
proved the conjecture for a wide class of biharmonic submanifolds in certain non-compact symmetric spaces. These modern contributions demonstrate that while the conjecture fails in full generality, it remains valid under specific geometric or analytic constraints, thus continuing to shape the direction of biharmonic submanifold theory.

1.1
Preliminaries and initial set up

A smooth immersion
x:M^{m}\rightarrow N^{n}
between Riemannian manifolds is called a minimal immersion if its image
x\left(M\right)
is a minimal submanifold of
N^{n}
; that is, the mean curvature vector field
H
of the submanifold vanishes identically. Equivalently,
x
is a critical point of the area (or volume) functional with respect to compactly supported variations, making minimal submanifolds natural geometric generalizations of geodesics to higher dimensions. Locally, minimal submanifolds satisfy a second-order elliptic partial differential equation and are characterized by harmonicity of the immersion. Globally, their behavior is influenced by the curvature and topology of the ambient space, and they may exhibit rigidity, completeness, or singularity phenomena depending on context. Minimal immersions play a central role in differential geometry, the calculus of variations, and geometric measure theory (see
[
4
,
10
,
24
]
).

A smooth map
x:\left(M,g\right)\rightarrow\left(N,h\right)
between Riemannian manifolds is called biharmonic
[
14
]
if it is a critical point of the bienergy functional

E_{2}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{M}|\tau(x)|^{2}\,dv_{g},

where
\tau(x)
is the tension field of
x
. The Euler–Lagrange equation corresponding to this variational problem is given by

\tau_{2}(x):=\Delta\tau(x)-\operatorname{trace}_{g}R^{N}(dx,\tau(x))dx=0,

and maps satisfying this fourth-order elliptic equation are referred to as biharmonic maps. To study their deformation behavior, the biharmonic map heat flow is introduced as the evolution equation

\frac{\partial x}{\partial t}=-\tau_{2}(x),

which represents the
L^{2}
-gradient flow of the bienergy functional. When
x
is an isometric immersion, the flow evolves the submanifold in the ambient manifold while preserving the immersion condition, that is, for an isometric immersion, the tension field becomes
\tau\left(x\right)=m\overrightarrow{H}
, where
\overrightarrow{H}
is the mean curvature vector field. Thus the flow becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\partial x}{\partial t}=-\Delta\overrightarrow{H}+\operatorname{Tr}_{g}R^{N}\left(\overrightarrow{H},dx\right)dx.

This is the extrinsic bi–harmonic map flow for isometric immersion. The biharmonic map heat flow generalizes the harmonic map flow and has been analyzed under various curvature and regularity assumptions. Notably,
[
19
]
, introduced an entropy-monotonicity formula for the extrinsic biharmonic map heat flow in Euclidean space, offering a Li–Yau type inequality in dimensions
n\leq 4
. Furthermore,
[
18
]
established diffusive stability and self-similar decay estimates for the biharmonic map heat flow in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, extending known results for harmonic map flows. These contributions mark significant advances in understanding long-time behavior and entropy methods for higher-order flows.

We recall that, the shape operator is a fundamental geometric tool that describes how a surface bends within an ambient space. It measures the rate of change of the surface’s normal vector as one moves along the surface. Closely related to curvature, the shape operator encodes principal directions and helps classify points as flat, elliptic, or hyperbolic. The shape operator
A
(also called the Weingarten map)
[
12
]
is defined as the bundle endomorphism

\displaystyle A:TM\rightarrow TM,

such that, for every vector field
X\in\Gamma\left(TM\right)
,

\displaystyle A\left(X\right):=-\nabla_{X}^{N}\nu\in\Gamma\left(TM\right),

that is,
A
measures the tangential part of the derivative of the normal field along
M
. The minus sign is a convention that ensures compatibility with the second fundamental form. As a tensorial viewpoint, the second fundamental form
\Pi
is defined as a symmetric bilinear form

\displaystyle\Pi\left(X,Y\right):=\langle\nabla_{X}^{N}Y,\nu\rangle=\langle A\left(X\right),Y\rangle=\langle X,A\left(Y\right)\rangle.

Therefore, the shape operator
A
corresponds to the tensor field

\displaystyle A\in\Gamma\left(\operatorname{Sym}\left(T^{*}M\otimes TM\right)\right)\subset\Gamma\left(T^{*}M\otimes TM\right).

That is,

•

For every point
p\in M
,
A_{p}:T_{p}M\rightarrow T_{p}M
is self–adjoint with respect to
g
, i.e.,
g\left(A_{p}\left(X\right),Y\right)=g\left(X,A_{p}\left(Y\right)\right)
.

•

A
is a smooth section of the bundle of symmetric
\left(1,1\right)
–tensors.

We now define the space of all smooth symmetric tensor fields of the shape operator type modulo orthogonal equivalence, as a moduli space; let

•

\operatorname{Sym}\left(T^{*}M\otimes TM\right)\rightarrow M
be the bundle whose fiber at each
p\in M
is the space of symmetric linear operators
A_{p}:T_{p}M\rightarrow T_{p}M
.

•

\Gamma\left(\operatorname{Sym}\left(T^{*}M\otimes TM\right)\right)
be the space of all smooth symmetric
\left(1,1\right)
–tensor fields on
M
.

Let
\mathcal{O}\left(TM\right)
denotes the bundle of orthogonal automorphisms of the tangent bundle

•

Each fiber is
\mathcal{O}\left(T_{p}M,g_{p}\right)\cong\mathcal{O}\left(m\right):=\{G\in GL\left(m,\mathbb{R}\right)|G^{T}G=I\}.

•

The group
\mathcal{O}\left(TM\right)
acts on
\Gamma\left(\operatorname{Sym}\left(T^{*}M\otimes TM\right)\right)
via conjugation

\displaystyle R.A:=R^{-1}AR,\,\,\text{for}\,\,\,R\in\mathcal{O}\left(TM\right),\,\,\,\,A\in\Gamma\left(\operatorname{Sym}\left(T^{*}M\otimes TM\right)\right).

Thus, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{m}:=\Gamma\left(\operatorname{Sym}\left(T^{*}M\otimes TM\right)\right)/\penalty\mathcal{O}\left(TM\right).

(1)

This is the space of symmetric
\left(1,1\right)
–tensor fields on
M
modulo orthogonal bundle automorphisms, i.e., up to point-wise orthogonal conjugation. The moduli space (
1
), captures the intrinsic geometric data of the immersion, abstracting away coordinate choices or local trivialization.

We are going to study the evolution of shape operators under a fourth–order geometric flow–called the moduli flow–(which will be provided next section) designed to decrease a natural energy functional associated with the immersion. This flow aims to deform a given immersion toward a minimal one, revealing deep connections to minimal isometric immersions. Our approach uses variational techniques, curvature analysis, and long–time behavior to establish convergence results and characterize the uniqueness of stationary points in terms of geometric minimality.

2
The moduli flow as a gradient flow of the moduli energy functional

Let
\nu\in\Gamma\left(\nu\left(M\right)\right)
be a global unit normal vector field (possible since co–dim
=1
). Then the shape operator is defined as the field of bundle maps
A\in\Gamma\left(\operatorname{End}\left(TM\right)\right)
, such that for any vector fields
X,Y\in\Gamma\left(TM\right)
, we have

\displaystyle h\left(\nabla_{X}^{N}Y,\nu\right)=g\left(A\left(X\right),Y\right)=g\left(X,A\left(Y\right)\right),

where
\nabla^{N}
denotes the Levi–Civita connection on
N
. Equivalently, the second fundamental form is the symmetric bilinear form

\displaystyle\alpha\left(X,Y\right):=h\left(\nabla^{N}_{X}Y,\nu\right)=g\left(A\left(X\right),Y\right),

and hence we identify
A\in\Gamma\left(\operatorname{Sym}\left(TM\right)\right)\cong\Gamma\left(\operatorname{Sym}\left(T^{*}M\otimes TM\right)\right)
.

We now define a geometric flow on
\mathcal{M}_{m}
(
1
) which evolves a shape operator

A\in\Gamma\left(\operatorname{Sym}\left(T^{*}M\otimes TM\right)\right)
in time. The moduli flow is the negative
L^{2}
–gradient flow of the functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\left(A\right):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{M}|\nabla A|^{2}d\mu_{g},

(2)

where
\nabla A\in\Gamma\left(T^{*}M\otimes T^{*}M\otimes TM\right)
is the Levi–Civita covariant derivative of
A
, and
|\nabla A|^{2}=g^{ik}g^{jl}h\left(\nabla_{i}A_{i},\nabla_{k}A_{k}\right)
under a local frame. Thus, the evolution equation is

\displaystyle\partial_{t}A=-\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta A}=-\nabla^{*}\nabla A+\mathcal{R}\left(A,\nabla A,\nabla^{2}A\right),

where
\nabla^{*}\nabla=\Delta
is the rough Laplacian acting on tensor fields. Now compute the full expression of this flow.

Let
A
be the shape operator of the immersion
x:M\rightarrow N
and let
A_{\epsilon}:=A+\epsilon B
, for a variation
B\in\Gamma\left(\operatorname{Sym}\left(T^{*}M\otimes TM\right)\right)
, then

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\mathcal{F}\left(A_{\epsilon}\right)|_{\epsilon=0}=\int_{M}\langle\nabla A,\nabla B\rangle d\mu_{g}.

By integrating by parts,

\displaystyle\int_{M}\langle\nabla A,\nabla B\rangle d\mu_{g}=-\int_{M}\langle\Delta A,B\rangle d\mu_{g},

so the gradient part is

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta A}=-\Delta A,

where
\delta
is the Euler–Lagrange operator. But
A
depends nonlinearly on the immersion and the ambient curvature of
N
, so we must add the nonlinear correction terms from the curvature contribution. We use the general Simons–type identity
[
24
]
for the shape operator under the ambient Riemannian geometry, i.e.,

\displaystyle\Delta A=\nabla^{2}H+A*A*A+R^{N}*A,

where
H=\operatorname{tr}_{g}A
is the mean curvature function, and
R^{N}
is the Riemann curvature tensor of
\left(N,h\right)
pulled back via the immersion
x
. All products are contractions consistent with the tensor structure. Hence, the moduli flow equation becomes

\displaystyle\partial_{t}A=-\Delta^{2}A+\mathcal{R}\left(A,\nabla A,\nabla^{2}A\right),

(3)

where from classical submanifold geometry (e.g., see
[
24
,
26
]
)
\mathcal{R}
is given explicitly as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle:=\nabla^{2}\left(A*A\right)+\nabla\left(R^{N}*A\right)+A*A*\nabla^{2}A+R^{N}*\nabla^{2}A

\displaystyle+A*A*A*A+R^{N}*A*A+\nabla R^{N}*\nabla A,

where

•

A*A
denotes the multiplication and contraction of symmetric operators.

•

R^{N}*A
means ambient curvature acting on
A
.

•

\nabla R^{N}
denotes that the ambient covariant derivative pulled back along the immersion.

•

All terms are expressed tensorially using the pullback metric
g
and the normal field
\nu
.

If
Sec_{N}\leq 0
and
R^{N},\nabla R^{N}
are uniformly bounded, these terms can be controlled. Thus

\displaystyle\partial_{t}A

\displaystyle=-\Delta^{2}A+\nabla^{2}\left(A*A\right)+\nabla\left(R^{N}*A\right)+A*A*\nabla^{2}A

\displaystyle+R^{N}*\nabla^{2}A+A*A*A*A+R^{N}*A*A+\nabla R^{N}*\nabla A.

Lemma 2.1
.

(Well–posedness of the moduli flow)

Let
\left(M^{m},g\right)
be a smooth compact Riemannian manifold without boundary, and let
\left(N^{m+1},h\right)
be a complete Riemannian manifold with non–positive sectional curvature. Consider an isometric immersion
x_{0}:M\rightarrow N
, and let
A_{0}
be the corresponding shape operator. If
\mathcal{M}_{m}
denotes the moduli bundle over
M
(
1
), then there exists a unique smooth short–time solution to the moduli flow (
3
).

Proof.

Let
\bar{x}_{t}:M\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{m}
be the moduli map defined by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bar{x}_{t}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\pi\left(\Delta^{2}A_{t}+\mathcal{R}\left(A_{t},\nabla A_{t},\nabla^{2}A_{t}\right)\right),

\displaystyle\bar{x}_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\bar{x}_{0},

where
A_{t}\in\Gamma\left(\operatorname{Sym}\left(TM\right)\right)
is the time–dependent shape operator induced by the evolving immersion and
\pi:\operatorname{Sym}\left(TM\right)\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{m}
is the natural projection. Let
x_{t}:M\rightarrow N
be the solution to the bi–harmonic map heat flow
\partial_{t}x_{t}=-\Delta_{g}\tau\left(x_{t}\right)
, where
\tau\left(x_{t}\right)
is the tension field of the map. Since
x_{t}
remains an isometric immersion (at least for short time, by standard geometric flow theory), the tension field satisfies

\displaystyle\tau\left(x_{t}\right)=m\overrightarrow{H}_{t},

where
\overrightarrow{H}_{t}
is the mean curvature vector field. The evolution of
A_{t}
under the bi–harmonic map heat flow can be computed in local coordinates. Let
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
be a local orthonormal frame on
M
, and let
A_{ij}\left(t\right):=\langle A_{t}\left(e_{i}\right),e_{j}\rangle
then using geometric evolution equations (e.g.,
[
9
]
) and previous computations, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}A_{ij}=-\left(\Delta^{2}A_{ij}\right)+\mathcal{R}_{ij}\left(A,\nabla A,\nabla^{2}\right).

In detail, the principal term is

\displaystyle\left(\Delta^{2}A\right)_{ij}=\Delta\left(\Delta A_{ij}\right)=g^{kl}g^{pq}\nabla_{k}\nabla_{l}\nabla_{p}\nabla_{q}A_{ij}.

Thus, in local coordinates, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}A_{ij}=-g^{kl}g^{pq}\nabla_{k}\nabla_{l}\nabla_{p}\nabla_{q}A_{ij}+\mathcal{R}_{ij}.

The flow of
A_{t}\in\Gamma\left(\operatorname{Sym}\left(TM\right)\right)
is equivariant under the action the orthogonal frame bundle
\mathcal{O}\left(TM\right)
. Therefore, the flow descends to a flow on the quotient bundle
\mathcal{M}_{m}
, and the moduli map
\bar{x}_{t}:M\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{m}
, defined pointwise as
\bar{x}_{t}\left(p\right)=\left[A_{t}\left(p\right)\right]
, evolves via

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bar{x}_{t}=-\pi\left(\Delta^{2}A_{t}+\mathcal{R}\left(A_{t},\nabla A_{t},\nabla^{2}A_{t}\right)\right),

where
\pi
is the canonical projection and
\mathcal{R}
collects all lower–order curvature and nonlinear terms. We now verify that this evolution is strictly parabolic modulo gauge symmetry. Consider the principal part of the evolution

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(A\right):=-\Delta^{2}A.

This is a linear fourth–order elliptic operator acting on symmetric
2

\zeta\in T^{*}_{p}M
be a cotangent vector. The principal symbol
\sigma_{\zeta}\left(\mathcal{L}\right)
acting on
\operatorname{Sym}\left(T_{p}M\right)
is

\displaystyle\sigma_{\zeta}\left(\mathcal{L}\right)=|\zeta|^{4}.\operatorname{Id}_{\operatorname{Sym}\left(T_{p}M\right)}.

Thus, the operator is strongly elliptic, and the flow is fourth–order parabolic in the DeTurk–Gauge. Since the projection

to the moduli bundle is smooth and compatible with the flow, the evolution of the moduli map
\bar{x}_{t}\in\Gamma\left(\mathcal{M}_{m}\right)
is a well–defined fourth–order geometric parabolic flow. By general theory of quasilinear parabolic PDEs on compact manifolds (e.g.,

•

The fourth–order parabolic system for

has a unique smooth solution for small

.

•

Since
A_{t}
evolves equivariantly, the moduli map
\bar{x}_{t}=\left[A_{t}\right]\in\Gamma\left(\mathcal{M}_{m}\right)
also evolves smoothly.

•

The projected flow is unique (up to gauge) and smooth for short time.

Thus, the moduli flow is well–posed.

Lemma 2.2
.

(Gradient structure of the moduli flow)

Let
x:M^{m}\rightarrow N^{m+1}
be a smooth isometric immersion from a compact Riemannian manifold without boundary
\left(M,g\right)
into a complete Riemannian manifold with non–positive curvature
\left(N,h\right)
, also let
A\in\Gamma\left(\operatorname{Sym}\left(T^{*}M\otimes TM\right)\right)
be the shape operator with respect to this immersion. Then the moduli flow (
3
) is the
L^{2}
–gradient flow of the moduli energy functional (
2
).

Proof.

Let
A_{\epsilon}=A+\epsilon B
, with
B\in\Gamma\left(\operatorname{Sym}\left(TM\right)\right)
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\left(A_{\epsilon}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{M}||\nabla A+\epsilon\nabla B||^{2}d\mu_{g}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{M}\left(||\nabla A||^{2}+2\epsilon\langle\nabla A,\nabla B\rangle+\epsilon^{2}||\nabla B||^{2}\right)d\mu_{g}.

So

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\mathcal{F}\left(A_{\epsilon}\right)|_{\epsilon=0}=\int_{M}\langle\nabla A,\nabla B\rangle d\mu_{g}.

We integrate by parts

\displaystyle\int_{M}\langle\nabla A,\nabla B\rangle=-\int_{M}\langle\Delta A,B\rangle,

where
\Delta:=\nabla^{*}\nabla
is the rough Laplacian on
\Gamma\left(\operatorname{Sym}\left(TM\right)\right)
so the first variation becomes

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{F}\left(A\right)\left[B\right]=-\int_{M}\langle\Delta A,B\rangle d\mu_{g},

and thus

\displaystyle\operatorname{grad}_{L^{2}}\mathcal{F}\left(A\right)=-\Delta A.

We recall that under the bi–harmonic heat flow

\displaystyle\partial_{t}x=-\Delta\tau\left(x\right)=-m\Delta H,

the mean curvature vector
H
evolves via a fourth–order equation, and the corresponding shape operator satisfies

\displaystyle\partial_{t}A=-\Delta^{2}A+\mathcal{R}\left(A,\nabla A,\nabla^{2}A\right),

as derived previously using Codazzi and Gauss equations, written explicitly in local coordinates. This evolution is consistent with the gradient flow of
\mathcal{F}\left(A\right)
, whose leading (principal) part is

\displaystyle\partial_{t}A=\Delta^{2}A,

and the actual geometric flow include curvature terms that modify the evolution (
3

\mathcal{F}\left(A\right)
up to lower–order geometric terms.

3
Lyapunov monotonicity and long–time solution

In the analysis of partial differential equations on manifolds, Sobolev spaces play a central role in controlling the regularity of functions. For a smooth compact Riemannian manifold
M
, the Sobolev space
H^{k}(M)
consists of functions whose weak derivatives up to order
k
are square integrable
[
1
]
. The corresponding Sobolev norm is defined as

\|u\|_{H^{k}(M)}^{2}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\int_{M}|\nabla^{j}u|^{2}\,dv_{g},

which provides a natural framework for energy estimates and the well-posedness of elliptic and parabolic equations. In the context of geometric flows, a Lyapunov monotonicity refers to the property that a certain energy functional–typically the Dirichlet energy or bienergy–decreases monotonically along the flow. This structure implies that the flow dissipates energy over time, guiding solutions toward more stable or canonical geometric configurations. Further, we are going to show the Lyapunov monotonicity which is essential in establishing global existence, convergence, and rigidity results(e.g.,
[
16
]
).

Lemma 3.1
.

(Lyapunov Monotonicity)

Let
x:\left(M^{m},g\right)\rightarrow\left(N^{m+1},h\right)
be a smooth isometric immersion of a compact Riemannian manifold
M
without boundary into a complete Riemannian manifold
N
with non–positive sectional curvature and uniformly bounded curvature tensor up to second order i.e.,
\|R^{N}\|_{C^{2}\left(N\right)}\leq\Lambda\leq 0
. Also assume
A\left(t\right)\in\Gamma\left(\operatorname{Sym}\left(TM\right)\right)
is a smooth family of shape operators of immersions evolving under the moduli flow (
3
). Then, for small enough
\|A\|_{H^{3}}
, the moduli energy functional (
2
) satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{F}\left(A\left(t\right)\right)\leq 0,

and equality holds if and only if
\nabla A\equiv 0
, implying minimality.

Proof.

Using the product rule and time derivative of
A
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{F}\left(A\right)=\int_{M}\langle\nabla A,\nabla\left(\partial_{t}A\right)\rangle d\mu_{g}=\int_{M}g^{ik}g^{jl}\nabla_{i}A_{jk}\nabla_{i}\left(\partial_{t}A_{jl}\right)d\mu_{g}.

Substitute flow equation
\partial_{t}A_{jl}=-\Delta^{2}A_{jl}+\mathcal{R}_{jl}
, so

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{F}\left(A\right)=-\int_{M}g^{ik}g^{jl}\nabla_{i}A_{jk}\nabla_{i}\left(\Delta^{2}A_{jl}\right)d\mu_{g}+\int_{M}g^{ik}g^{jl}\nabla_{i}A_{ij}\nabla_{i}\mathcal{R}_{jl}d\mu_{g}.

Let us denote
T:=\int_{M}g^{ik}g^{jl}\nabla_{i}A_{jk}\nabla_{i}\left(\Delta^{2}A_{jl}\right)d\mu_{g}
. By integration by parts twice, we get

\displaystyle T=-\int_{M}g^{ik}g^{jl}\nabla_{i}\nabla_{i}A_{jk}\Delta^{2}A_{jl}d\mu_{g}=\int_{M}g^{jl}\Delta\nabla A_{jl}d\mu_{g}.

So we have

\displaystyle\int_{M}\langle\nabla A,\nabla\Delta^{2}A\rangle=\int_{M}\|\Delta\nabla A\|^{2}d\mu_{g}.

Hence

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{F}\left(A\right)=-\int_{M}\|\Delta\nabla A\|^{2}d\mu_{g}+\int_{M}\langle\nabla A,\nabla\mathcal{R}\rangle d\mu_{g}.

We now estimate (Cauchy–Schwarz inequality)

\displaystyle\int_{M}\langle\nabla A,\nabla\mathcal{R}\rangle\leq\|\nabla A\|_{L^{2}}.\|\nabla\mathcal{R}\|_{L^{2}}.

From the polynomial structure of
\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{R}\leq C_{\Lambda}\left(|\nabla^{3}A|+|\nabla^{2}A||\nabla A|+|\nabla A|^{3}+|A|\right).

Thus,

\displaystyle\|\nabla\mathcal{R}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq C\left(\|\nabla^{3}A\|^{2}+\|\nabla^{2}A\|^{2}\|\nabla A\|^{2}+\|\nabla A\|^{6}+\|A\|^{2}\right).

By using elliptic estimates

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{3}A\|^{2}\leq\epsilon\|\Delta\nabla A\|^{2}+C_{\epsilon}\|A\|^{2}_{H^{2}}.

Putting this back

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{F}\left(A\right)\leq-\left(1-C_{1}\epsilon\right)\int_{M}\|\Delta\nabla A\|^{2}+C_{2}\left(\|A\|^{2}_{H^{2}}+\|\nabla A\|^{6}+\|A\|^{2}\right),

choosing
\epsilon<<1
and
\|A\|_{H^{3}}<<1
, lower–order terms are absorbed (note that because the curvature and its derivatives are uniformly bounded, we can choose
\epsilon
small enough), thus for
\delta:=\left(1-C_{1}\epsilon\right)
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{F}\left(A\right)\leq-\delta\int_{M}\|\Delta\nabla A\|^{2}+C_{2}\left(\|A\|^{2}_{H^{2}}+\|\nabla A\|^{6}+\|A\|^{2}\right)\leq 0.

If equality holds
\Delta\nabla A=0
then
\nabla A=0
on compact
M
. This implies that
A
is parallel, so by Codazzi identity implies
\overrightarrow{H}=0
, thus the immersion is minimal.
∎

This monotonicity ensures that the energy decreases along the flow, yielding crucial a priori estimates that prevent blow-up phenomena caused by energy concentration. In particular, such decay properties help control higher-order norms of the evolving map and play a key role in extending local solutions to global ones e.g.,
[
19
]
.

Remark 3.2
.

In Lemma
3.1
, the monotonicity of the moduli energy functional
F(A)
is established under the assumption that
\|A\|_{H^{3}}
is sufficiently small. This ensures that the nonlinear terms involving curvature and higher derivatives can be absorbed into the leading dissipative term
\|\Delta\nabla A\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
.

However, this smallness assumption does not directly provide a uniform-in-time a priori bound on
\|\nabla^{2}A\|_{L^{2}}
in general. To address this, we emphasize that the global existence result (Theorem
3.3
) does not depend on such a smallness condition. Instead, it relies on the Lyapunov monotonicity of
F(A(t))
, the compactness of
M
, the bounded geometry of the ambient manifold
N
, and standard parabolic regularity theory for fourth-order geometric flows.

In particular, the energy decay
F(A(t))\leq F(A(0))
implies uniform control over
\|\nabla A\|_{L^{2}}
, and elliptic estimates applied to the moduli flow equation allow us to derive bounds on
\|\nabla^{2}A\|_{L^{2}}
and higher norms iteratively. Thus, while Lemma
3.1
uses a smallness condition to obtain strict energy monotonicity, the global control in Theorem 3.2 follows from a more general argument based on a priori estimates derived from the flow structure.

.

Let

4
Minimal immersions as natural attractors of moduli dynamics

The shape operator
A
, as a symmetric tensor field associated with the second fundamental form of an immersion
x:M^{m}\rightarrow N^{m+1}
, encodes the extrinsic curvature of the submanifold. Under the moduli flow, defined by the high–order parabolic equation (
3
), the evolution of
A
reflects the geometric deformation of the immersion toward configurations of reduced extrinsic complexity. This flows decreases the natural moduli energy functional
\mathcal{F}\left(A\right)
and the critical points of
\mathcal{F}
–namely, the stationary points of the flow–correspond to immersions where
\Delta A=0
, i.e., where the shape operator is harmonic. Among these, minimal immersions (i.e., those with parallel shape operator
\nabla A=0
) are the simplest and most symmetric configurations. In this context, minimal immersions arise as natural attractors of the moduli dynamics. The investigation of the linear stability of such immersions is essential to understand their role in the long–time behavior of the flow. The following lemma establishes the linear stability of minimal immersions under the moduli flow, providing the first–order approximation of the flow near these critical configurations.

Lemma 4.1
.

(Linear stability of minimal immersions)

Let
A_{\infty}\in\Gamma\left(\operatorname{Sym}\left(TM\right)\right)
be a parallel shape operator (i.e.,
\nabla A_{\infty}=0
), corresponding to a minimal isometric immersion
x:M\rightarrow N
. Also assume the curvature tensor of
\left(N,h\right)
is uniformly bounded up to second–order. Then, for sufficiently small perturbations
P\left(0\right)\in H^{k}
, the solution
A\left(t\right)=A_{\infty}+P\left(t\right)
of the moduli flow satisfies

\displaystyle\|\nabla P\left(t\right)\|_{L^{2}}\rightarrow 0,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{as}\,\,\,\,\,\,t\rightarrow\infty,

with exponential decay rate. Hence,
A_{\infty}
is linearly stable under the moduli flow.

Proof.

Consider the associated energy functional as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\left(A\right)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{M}\|\nabla A\|^{2}d\mu_{g}.

Let
A_{\infty}\in\Gamma\left(\operatorname{Sym}\left(TM\right)\right)
be a critical point of
\mathcal{F}
, i.e.,
\nabla A_{\infty}\equiv 0
, thus
A_{\infty}
is parallel, hence the immersion is minimal. Let
A\left(t\right)=A_{\infty}+P\left(t\right)
, where
P\left(t\right)\in\Gamma\left(\operatorname{Sym}\left(TM\right)\right)
is a perturbation. We will linearize the moduli flow around
A_{\infty}
to determine whether small perturbations decay over time.

Recall the evolution equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}A=-\Delta^{2}A+\mathcal{R}\left(A,\nabla A,\nabla^{2}\right).

Now substitute
A\left(t\right)=A_{\infty}+P\left(t\right)
, and expand the right–hand side using Taylor expansion around
A_{\infty}
, assuming that
A_{\infty}
is parallel and fixed

\displaystyle\partial_{t}P

\displaystyle=-\Delta^{2}P+D\mathcal{R}\left[A_{\infty}\right]\left(P,\nabla P,\nabla^{2}P\right)

\displaystyle=-\Delta^{2}P+L\left(P\right),

where
L\left(P\right)
is a second–order differential operator (including curvature term) obtained from the linearization of
\mathcal{R}
. Under the assumption that
A_{\infty}
is parallel and
N
has bound curvature and bounded derivatives,
L
contains at most second–order terms with bounded coefficients. Hence, the linearized equation becomes

\displaystyle\partial_{t}P=-\Delta^{2}P+L\left(P\right).

We say that
A_{\infty}
is linearly stable under the moduli flow if for small perturbations
P\left(0\right)
, the solution
P\left(t\right)
to the linearized equation satisfies

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|P\left(t\right)\|_{H^{k}}=0,

for an appropriate Sobolev norm
H^{k}
, and remains bounded for all
t>0
. We aim to show exponential decay under the flow in the linearized setting.

Define the perturbation energy functional

\displaystyle E\left(P\right):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{M}\|\nabla P\|^{2}d\mu_{g}.

We compute its evolution

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E\left(P\right)=\int_{M}\langle\nabla P,\nabla\partial_{t}P\rangle

\displaystyle=-\int_{M}\langle\nabla P,\nabla\Delta^{2}P\rangle d\mu_{g}

\displaystyle+\int_{M}\langle\nabla P,\nabla L\left(P\right)\rangle d\mu_{g}.

Using integration by parts;

•

For the principal term

\displaystyle-\int_{M}\langle\nabla P,\nabla\Delta^{2}P\rangle=-\int_{M}\|\Delta\nabla P\|^{2}d\mu_{g}.

•

For the lower–order term

\displaystyle\int_{M}\langle\nabla P,\nabla L\left(P\right)\rangle\leq C\left(\|\nabla P\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\nabla^{2}P\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\right),

where
C
depends on the curvature bounds.

Now we use interpolation inequality

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}P\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq\delta\|\Delta\nabla P\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+C_{\delta}\|\nabla P\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

Putting this together

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E\left(P\right)\leq-\left(1-C\delta\right)\|\Delta\nabla P\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+C_{\delta}\|\nabla P\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

Choosing
\delta
small enough such that
1-C\delta>0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E\left(P\right)\leq-\alpha\|\Delta\nabla P\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+C^{\prime}\|\nabla P\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

Now we want to control
\|\Delta\nabla P\|^{2}
from below in terms of
\|\nabla P\|^{2}
. Since
M
is compact and smooth, and
P\in\Gamma\left(\operatorname{Sym}\left(TM\right)\right)
, we can apply a coercivity estimate (elliptic regularity)

\displaystyle\|\Delta\nabla P\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\geq\lambda\|\nabla P\|^{2}_{L^{2}},

for some constant
\lambda>0
depending only on the geometry of
M
. Therefore, we plug this into the differential inequality

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E\left(t\right)

\displaystyle\leq-\alpha\lambda\|\Delta P\left(t\right)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+C^{\prime}\|\nabla P\left(t\right)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=-2\left(\alpha\lambda-C^{\prime}\right).E\left(t\right).

Set
\beta:=2\left(\alpha\lambda-C^{\prime}\right)>0
, this gives

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E\left(t\right)\leq-\beta E\left(t\right).

This is a classical Grönwall differential inequality, thus, by integration we see

\displaystyle E\left(t\right)\leq E\left(0\right)e^{-\beta t}.

Thus, we conclude

\displaystyle\int_{M}|\nabla P\left(t\right)|^{2}d\mu_{g}\leq\int_{M}|\nabla P\left(0\right)|^{2}d\mu_{g}.e^{-\beta t},

which gives exponential decay of the perturbation energy, hence exponential convergence of
A\left(t\right)\rightarrow A_{\infty}
in
H
norm.
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

(Global convergence to minimal immersions)

Let
x_{0}:M^{m}\rightarrow N^{m+1}
be the smooth isometric immersion with
M
compact and without boundary and suppose
N
has non–positive sectional curvature with uniformly bounded curvature tensor up to second–order. Let
A\left(t\right)
be the solution to the moduli flow equation(
3
) and the shape operator of the immersion
x\left(t\right)
, also assume the initial immersion satisfies suitable regularity condition (e.g.,
A\left(0\right)\in H^{k}
for
k
sufficiently large), then

•

As
t\rightarrow\infty
,
A\left(t\right)\rightarrow A_{\infty}
in
C^{\infty}
–topology, where
A_{\infty}
is a parallel shape operator.

•

The limiting immersion
x_{\infty}
is a minimal immersion, and under
\operatorname{Sec}_{N}\leq 0
, any immersion with parallel shape operator must be minimal, so
x_{\infty}
is the unique stationary point of the flow.

Proof.

Consider the moduli energy functional (
2
). From earlier Lemma
3.1
, we established the inequality

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{F}\left(A\left(t\right)\right)\leq-C\mathcal{F}\left(A\left(t\right)\right),\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{for some}\,\,\,\,C>0.

Solving this ODE, gives exponential decay

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\left(A\left(t\right)\right)\leq\mathcal{F}\left(A\left(0\right)\right)e^{-Ct},

this implies

\displaystyle\int_{M}|\nabla A\left(t\right)|^{2}d\mu_{g}\rightarrow 0,\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{as}\,\,\,\,t\rightarrow\infty.

We now bootstrap this convergence. Since
A\left(t\right)
satisfies a
4
th–order parabolic PDE

\displaystyle\partial_{t}A=-\Delta^{2}A+\mathcal{R}\left(A,\nabla A,\nabla^{2}A,R^{N},\nabla R^{N}\right),

and the right–hand side is smooth in
A
and its derivatives with
R^{N}
,
\nabla R^{N}
uniformly bounded, parabolic regularity theory ensures bounds on higher derivatives of
A\left(t\right)
. Thus, by Sobolev embedding and interpolation inequalities we have convergence

\displaystyle A\left(t\right)\rightarrow A_{\infty}\in C^{\infty}\left(M\right),\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{with}\,\,\,\,\nabla A_{\infty}=0.

Let
A_{\infty}\in\Gamma\left(\operatorname{Sym}\left(TM\right)\right)
be the
C^{\infty}
–limit with
\nabla A_{\infty}=0
. Recall that the mean curvature vector is

\displaystyle H=\operatorname{tr}_{g}A\in\Gamma\left(T^{\perp}M\right).

Since
A
is parallel, the eigenvalues (principal curvatures) are constant. Moreover, the Laplacian of
A
vanishes

\displaystyle\Delta A=0,\,\,\,\,\,\Delta^{2}A=0.

Hence the flow equation becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\left(A,\nabla A,\nabla^{2}A,R^{N},\nabla R^{N}\right)=0,\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{with}\,\,\,\,\nabla A=0.

All terms in
\mathcal{R}
depending on
\nabla A
,
\nabla^{2}A
vanish. Then, under
\operatorname{Sec}_{N}\leq 0
, classical results (e.g.,
[
26
,
27
]
) imply that if
A\in\Gamma\left(\operatorname{Sym}\left(TM\right)\right)
is parallel and
M
is compact, then the mean curvature vector
H\equiv 0
. Therefore, the limiting immersion
x_{\infty}:M\rightarrow N
is minimal.

Suppose
A\in\Gamma\left(\operatorname{Sym}\left(TM\right)\right)
be a stationary point of the moduli flow (
3
). We take the
L^{2}
–inner product with
A
and integrate

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{M}\langle\Delta^{2}A,A\rangle d\mu_{g}-\int_{M}\langle\mathcal{R},A\rangle d\mu_{g}

\displaystyle=\int_{M}|\Delta A|^{2}d\mu_{g}-\int_{M}\langle\mathcal{R},A\rangle d\mu_{g}.

If
\mathcal{R}
is lower–order and
R^{N}
bounded, we use

\displaystyle|\int_{M}\langle\mathcal{R},A\rangle|\leq\epsilon\int_{M}|\Delta A|^{2}+L_{\epsilon}\int_{M}|A|^{2}.

Then,

\displaystyle\int_{M}|\Delta A|^{2}\leq\epsilon\int_{M}|\Delta A|^{2}+L_{\epsilon}\int_{M}|A|^{2},

where
L_{\epsilon}
is a constant dependent to
\epsilon
. Choosing
\epsilon<<1
implies
\Delta A=0
and
\nabla A=0
so
A
is parallel. By the same argument as before,
A
is parallel so
H=0
. Hence, any stationary point is a minimal immersion.
∎

While the moduli flow framework and associated energy functional provide a powerful tool for analyzing the convergence toward minimal immersions in the co-dimension one case under geometric constraints (such as compactness of
M
, and non-positive sectional curvature of
N
), this approach faces significant limitations in more general settings. In higher co-dimension, the shape operator becomes a vector-valued symmetric bilinear form, complicating both the analytic structure of the flow and the behavior of its nonlinear terms. Moreover, the non-positivity of the ambient curvature is crucial in controlling the interaction terms arising from curvature in the evolution equation. Without such curvature assumptions or compactness of the domain, the coercivity and monotonicity properties of the energy functional may fail, and uniform a priori estimates may no longer be available. As a result, extending this methodology to arbitrary immersions or general ambient geometries would require substantially new analytical tools and structural insights.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
x:M^{m}\rightarrow N^{m+1}
be a smooth isometric immersion of a compact Riemannian manifold
\left(M,g\right)
without boundary into a complete Riemannian manifold
\left(N,h\right)
with non–positive sectional curvature and uniformly bounded curvature tensor up to second order. Assume that
A\left(t\right)\in\Gamma\left(\operatorname{Sym}\left(TM\right)\right)
evolves under the moduli flow (
3
). Then, the following statements are equivalent

•

Every bi–harmonic immersion
x:M^{m}\rightarrow N^{m+1}
satisfying the above assumptions, is minimal (i.e.,
\overrightarrow{H}\equiv 0
).

•

For every initial second fundamental form
A\left(0\right)
, the solution
A\left(t\right)
to the moduli flow (
3
) exists globally and converges smoothly as
t\rightarrow\infty
to a stationary point
A_{\infty}
with
\nabla A_{\infty}=0
. Moreover, all stationary points are minimal immersions.

Proof.

Assume that every bi–harmonic immersion into
N
is minimal. We have already proven the followings

•

The moduli flow preserves the isometric immersion condition.

•

The moduli energy functional (
2
) is non–increasing along the flow and strictly decreasing unless
\nabla A\equiv 0
.

The flow is globally well–posed under the uniform curvature assumptions and the compactness of
M
, due to higher–order parabolic regularity theory. The limit
A_{\infty}=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}A\left(t\right)
exists in the
C^{\infty}
–topology. By smooth convergence and the fact that
\partial_{t}A\rightarrow 0
, we find

\displaystyle 0=-\Delta^{2}A_{\infty}+\mathcal{R}\left(A_{\infty},\nabla A_{\infty},\nabla^{2}A_{\infty}\right).

But since
\nabla A_{\infty}=0
, this reduces to

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}A_{\infty}=0.

This is,
A_{\infty}
corresponds to a bi–harmonic immersion. By the assumption,
A_{\infty}
must be minimal. Hence, all solutions of the moduli flow (
3
) converge to minimal immersions, and the only stationary points of the flow are minimal.

Now suppose, the moduli flow (
3
) globally converges to stationary points, and the only stationary points are minimal immersions. Let
x:M\rightarrow N
be a bi–harmonic immersion under the assumptions of the theorem. This means the tension field of
x
,
\tau\left(x\right)=m\overrightarrow{H}
, satisfies

\displaystyle\Delta\overrightarrow{H}+\operatorname{tr}_{g}R^{N}\left(dx\left(.\right),\overrightarrow{H}\right)dx\left(.\right)=0.

This corresponds to the vanishing of the first variation of the bi–energy functional, i.e.,
x
is a critical point of the bi–energy. On the level of the moduli flow this implies that
A
is a stationary point of the moduli flow
\partial_{t}A=0
, thus,

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}A=\mathcal{R}\left(A,\nabla A,\nabla^{2}A\right).

Hence, by assumption, the only such stationary points are those for which
\nabla A=0
, i.e., parallel second fundamental form, which implies the mean curvature vector
\overrightarrow{H}
is constant. By the Codazzi equations and
\operatorname{Sec}_{N}\leq 0
, one concludes that a parallel shape operator implies
\overrightarrow{H}\equiv 0
i.e., the immersion is minimal (see
[
26
,
27
]
). Therefore, every bi–harmonic immersion with those assumptions must be minimal.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

Jiang proved that any biharmonic map from a compact manifold without boundary into a Riemannian manifold with non-positive sectional curvature must be harmonic (see
[
22
]
, Theorem 12.8). In the special case of isometric immersions, harmonicity is equivalent to minimality. Thus, his result implies that any biharmonic isometric immersion under the same curvature conditions must be minimal.

The conclusion of Theorem
4.3
overlaps with Jiang’s theorem in the case of compact domains and non-positive curvature. However, the framework presented here is entirely different: our result arises from the evolution of the shape operator under the moduli flow and is based on the analysis of geometric energy and entropy functionals. In particular, we do not assume biharmonicity at any stage. The minimality emerges as a consequence of curvature dynamics and variational principles, rather than from higher-order harmonicity. This offers a distinct perspective and may lead to further generalizations beyond the scope of Jiang’s setting.

5
Entropy functional analysis for the moduli flow

Entropy functionals play a central role in geometric flows by capturing irreversible complexity decay and giving strong control on the flow behavior–particularly in relation to singularities, long–time limits, and uniqueness. They generalize energy monotonicity and sometimes offer finer analytic tools than Lyapunov functionals. In the context of moduli flow, which evolves the shape operator
A\in\Gamma\left(\operatorname{Sym}\left(T^{*}M\otimes TM\right)\right)
we now define a natural entropy functional
\mathcal{W}\left(A,f,\eta\right)
that satisfies a monotonicity property similar to Perelman’s entropy in Ricci flow or analogous constructions in biharmonic heat flows (e.g.,
[
5
,
19
,
23
]
).

Let
\left(M^{m},g\right)
be a compact Riemannian manifold without boundary and
A\left(t\right)\in\Gamma\left(\operatorname{Sym}\left(TM\right)\right)
be a solution to the moduli flow

\displaystyle\partial_{t}A=-\Delta^{2}A+\mathcal{R}\left(A,\nabla A,\nabla^{2}A,R^{N},\nabla R^{N}\right),

where
\Delta=\nabla^{*}\nabla
is the rough Laplacian on tensor fields and
\mathcal{R}
is the curvature–reaction term defined in (
3
). Then, we define the moduli entropy functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}\left(A,f,\eta\right):=\int_{M}\left(\eta|\nabla A|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|A|^{2}+\eta^{2}|\Delta A|^{2}+f\right).ud\mu_{g},

where

•

A
is the shape operator (symmetric
\left(1,1\right)
–tensor).

•

\eta>0
is a time–scale parameter.

•

f:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a smooth weight function.

•

u:=\frac{e^{-f}}{\left(4\pi\eta\right)^{\frac{m}{2}}}
is a normalized heat kernel weight, satisfying

\displaystyle\int_{M}ud\mu_{g}=1.

This entropy measures how non–uniform or oscillatory the shape operator
A
is across scales controlled by
\eta
, penalizing high curvature and concentration of bending energy. Let
A\left(t\right)
evolve by the moduli flow, and let
f\left(t\right)
solve the adjoint heat equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f=-\Delta f+|\nabla f|^{2}-\operatorname{tr}\left(A^{2}\right)+\frac{m}{2\eta},

with
\eta=T-t
, so
\frac{d}{dt}\eta=-1
. This is a backward heat–type equation aligned with Perelman–style functionals.

We apply the time derivative

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{W}=\int_{M}\left(\partial_{t}\mathcal{E}.u+\mathcal{E}.\partial_{t}u\right)d\mu_{g},

where
\mathcal{E}=\eta|\nabla A|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|A|^{2}+\eta^{2}|\Delta A|^{2}+f
, and
\partial_{t}u=u\left(-\partial_{t}f-\frac{m}{2\eta}\right)
. Let us compute
\partial_{t}\mathcal{E}
term–by–term,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\eta|\nabla A|^{2}\right)=-|\nabla A|^{2}+2\eta\langle\nabla A,\nabla\left(\partial_{t}A\right)\rangle.

Use integration by parts identity

\displaystyle\int_{M}\langle\nabla A,\nabla\left(\partial_{t}A\right)\rangle u=-\int_{M}\langle\Delta A,\partial_{t}A\rangle u+\int_{M}\langle\nabla A,\partial_{t}A\rangle.\nabla f.u.

So

\displaystyle\int_{M}\partial_{t}\left(\eta|\nabla A|^{2}\right)u

\displaystyle=-\int_{M}|\nabla A|^{2}u-2\eta\int_{M}\langle\Delta A,\partial_{t}A\rangle u

\displaystyle+2\eta\int_{M}\langle\nabla A,\partial_{t}A\rangle.\nabla f.u.

Now consider

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\frac{1}{2}|A|^{2}\right)=\langle A,\partial_{t}A\rangle.

So

\displaystyle\int_{M}\partial_{t}\left(\frac{1}{2}|A|^{2}\right)u=\int_{M}\langle A,\partial_{t}A\rangle u.

For
\eta^{2}|\Delta A|^{2}
we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\eta^{2}|\Delta A|^{2}\right)=-2\eta|\Delta A|^{2}+2\eta^{2}\langle\Delta A,\Delta\left(\partial_{t}A\right)\rangle,

now integrate

\displaystyle\int_{M}\partial_{t}\left(\eta^{2}|\Delta A|^{2}\right)u=-2\eta\int_{M}|\Delta A|^{2}u+2\eta\int_{M}\langle\Delta A,\Delta\left(\partial_{t}A\right)\rangle u,

where by integrating by parts for the last term, we get

\displaystyle\int_{M}\langle\Delta A,\Delta\left(\partial_{t}A\right)\rangle u

\displaystyle=-\int_{M}\langle\nabla\Delta A,\nabla\left(\partial_{t}A\right)\rangle u

\displaystyle+\int_{M}\langle\Delta A,\nabla\left(\partial_{t}A\right)\rangle.\nabla f.u.

Also for a smooth weight function
f
,

\displaystyle\int_{M}\partial_{t}f.ud\mu_{g}+\int_{M}f.\partial_{t}ud\mu_{g}.

Since
\partial_{t}u=u\left(-\partial_{t}f-\frac{m}{2\eta}\right)
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{M}f.\partial_{t}u=-\int_{M}f.u.\left(\partial_{t}f+\frac{m}{2\eta}\right).

These two together give

\displaystyle\int_{M}\left(\partial_{t}f+f.\left(-\partial_{t}f-\frac{m}{2\eta}\right)\right)u

\displaystyle=\int_{M}\left(\partial_{t}f-f\partial_{t}f-\frac{m}{2\eta}f\right)u

\displaystyle=\int_{M}\left(\left(1-f\right)\partial_{t}f-\frac{m}{2\eta}f\right)u.

Now, putting everything together

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{W}

\displaystyle=\int_{M}\left(-|\nabla A|^{2}-2\eta|\Delta A|^{2}-2\eta\langle\Delta A,\partial_{t}A\rangle+2\eta\langle\nabla A,\partial_{t}A\rangle.\nabla f+\langle A,\partial_{t}A\rangle\right)u

\displaystyle+\int_{M}\left(-2\eta^{2}\langle\nabla\Delta A,\nabla\left(\partial_{t}A\right)\rangle+2\eta^{2}\langle\Delta A,\nabla\left(\partial_{t}A\right)\rangle.\nabla f\right).u

\displaystyle+\int_{M}\left(\left(1-f\right)\partial_{t}f-\frac{m}{2\eta}f\right)u.

Now plug in the moduli flow

\displaystyle\partial_{t}A=-\Delta A+\mathcal{R},

and recall that

•

\langle\Delta A,\partial_{t}A\rangle=-\langle\Delta A,\Delta^{2}A\rangle+\langle\Delta A,\mathcal{R}\rangle
.

•

\langle A,\partial_{t}A\rangle=-\langle A,\Delta^{2}A\rangle+\langle A,\mathcal{R}\rangle
.

From this, the leading (principal) terms are

•

-2\eta\int_{M}\langle\Delta A,-\Delta^{2}A\rangle u=2\eta\int_{M}|\nabla\Delta A|^{2}u
.

•

-\int_{M}|\nabla A|^{2}u
.

•

-2\eta\int_{M}|\Delta A|^{2}u
.

Now under smooth evolution by the moduli flow and assuming bounded curvature of the ambient manifold and compactness of
M
, we conclude

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{W}\left(A,f,\eta\right)\leq 0,

Due to
\operatorname{Sec}_{N}\leq 0
, for
\mathcal{W}\rightarrow 0
, we must have
|\nabla A|^{2}\rightarrow 0
(parallelism) and
|A|^{2}\rightarrow 0
. By elliptic regularity,
\nabla A=0
implies
A
is smooth and constant. Minimality
\left(\operatorname{tr}\left(A\right)=0\right)
then forces
A\equiv 0
, as a trace free constant tensor must vanish. Thus, the equality holds if and only if
x
is totally geodesic (see
[
26
]
). This completes the Theorem
1.1
.

Appendix A: Compare with the classic Willmore flow

The Willmore flow is a classical fourth-order geometric flow that evolves an immersion
u:\Sigma^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
by the
L^{2}
-gradient descent of the Willmore energy (for recent work see e.g.,
[
11
]
),

\mathcal{W}_{\text{Willmore}}(u)=\int_{\Sigma}|H|^{2}\,d\mu,

where
H
denotes the mean curvature scalar and
\mu
is the induced surface area form. The associated flow equation,

\partial_{t}u=-\Delta H-2|A|^{2}H+H\cdot\mathrm{Ric}(\nu,\nu),

decreases total bending energy and is well-known for its conformal invariance in dimension two.

In contrast, the moduli flow developed in this work evolves an isometric immersion
x:M^{m}\to N^{m+1}
by deforming its shape operator
A\in\Gamma(\operatorname{Sym}(T^{*}M\otimes TM))
, according to the gradient flow of the moduli energy

\mathcal{F}\left(A\right)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{M}|\nabla A|^{2}\,d\mu_{g}.

This energy captures the spatial variation of the second fundamental form and provides a higher-order tensorial measure of extrinsic complexity. The flow equation,

\partial_{t}A=-\Delta^{2}A+\mathcal{R}(A,\nabla A,\nabla^{2}A,R^{N},\nabla R^{N}),

acts directly on the moduli space of symmetric tensor fields modulo orthogonal conjugation.

While both flows share a variational structure and fourth-order analytic nature, they differ substantially in geometric character. The Willmore flow acts at the scalar level of mean curvature, is deeply rooted in conformal surface geometry, and admits singularities. The moduli flow, by contrast, governs the full tensorial curvature data, admits global existence and exponential convergence under curvature assumptions, and has minimal immersions as its unique attractors.
