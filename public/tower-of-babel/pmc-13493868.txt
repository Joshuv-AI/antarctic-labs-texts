Association between a composite serum oxidative stress biomarker score and systemic inflammation: A multi-provincial cross-sectional study in Chinese adults
Authors: Tian-Yu Li, Zhi-Ming Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOxidative stress and chronic inflammation share common upstream triggers and can mutually reinforce each other; however, few tools based on routine blood biomarkers are available to capture this interplay in Chinese adults. Using data from 6,044 participants in the 2009 China Health and Nutrition Survey (CHNS), a large multi-provincial population-based study, we developed a composite serum oxidative stress–related biomarker score (COSBS), hereafter referred to as a serum redox-metabolic biomarker score to reflect its overlap with metabolic phenotypes.The score was constructed by summing quintile-based points for six fasting biomarkers: uric acid, triglycerides, fasting glucose, and ferritin as pro-oxidant components, and HDL cholesterol and magnesium as antioxidant components, yielding a total score ranging from 6 to 30. Equal weighting was applied because no validated differential weights exist for this population and to avoid circularity with the outcome. Reliability and structural properties of COSBS were preliminarily evaluated using Cronbach’s α (0.615) and principal component analysis (PC1 = 35.5% of variance), reflecting acceptable but moderate internal consistency for a heterogeneous biomarker panel. COSBS effectively discriminated established high-risk groups (all P < 0.001). Analyses did not apply post-stratification weights; a cluster-robust sensitivity analysis confirmed that community-level clustering did not alter conclusions. Each one-unit increase in COSBS was independently associated with higher log(hs-CRP) (β = 0.0255, 95% CI: 0.0213–0.0297, P = 1.73 × 10⁻³²), with a strictly linear dose–response relationship (P for non-linearity = 0.372). Results were consistent across all pre-specified subgroups and sensitivity analyses, including a clinical threshold count score (β = 0.081, P = 5.51 × 10⁻²⁸). These findings suggest that COSBS, derived entirely from routine blood tests, is independently and linearly associated with systemic inflammation as reflected by hs-CRP, providing a reproducible exposure index for population-based research on oxidative-inflammatory burden. Independent external validation in NHANES 2015–2018 (n = 2,752 U.S. adults) replicated the association directionally and quantitatively (β = 0.048, P = 2.33 × 10⁻¹³; 25.2% higher geometric mean hs-CRP per SD of COSBS-5), supporting cross-population generalizability. Introduction

Chronic non-communicable diseases (NCDs), including cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and cancer1,2, driven heavily by systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, impose a massive global public health burden. Identifying high-risk individuals at the preclinical stage is a critical priority for preventive medicine. Oxidative stress occurs when the body produces more reactive oxygen species (ROS) than its antioxidant systems can neutralize, resulting in damage to lipids, proteins, and DNA—a process central to the pathogenesis of NCDs.

Oxidative stress and inflammation are not separate processes; they are mutually reinforcing. ROS activate Nuclear Factor kappa-B (NF-κB), a master regulator of inflammation, which upregulates pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-6 and TNF-α. These cytokines, in turn, generate more ROS, creating a self-sustaining loop that drives chronic disease3,4.

High-sensitivity CRP (hs-CRP) is the established clinical marker for low-grade systemic inflammation. The liver produces CRP in response to IL-6. Elevated hs-CRP independently predicts cardiovascular events and mortality5,6. It is inexpensive, widely available, and already integrated into standard cardiovascular risk assessment. Identifying the oxidative stress contributors to hs-CRP elevation from routine blood tests would therefore have clear research value.

The oxidative balance score (OBS) was the first attempt to quantify overall oxidative burden in a single number7. It aggregates dietary and lifestyle factors such as vitamin C intake, smoking, alcohol, and physical activity. Several studies have shown that higher OBS is associated with lower CRP8,9. Nanri et al. confirmed this in Japanese adults10. Lai et al. found that higher OBS was associated with 46% lower odds of cardiometabolic risk factors in Americans11. However, the classical OBS primarily captures what people eat and how they live, rather than what is occurring biochemically within the bloodstream.

Several composite tools have been developed to circumvent this limitation, but each differs importantly from COSBS. The classical OBS is self-reported and susceptible to recall bias; it reflects dietary antioxidant intake and lifestyle exposures rather than circulating biochemical redox status. Metabolic syndrome criteria (IDF/ATP-III) apply fixed sex-specific thresholds to a subset of overlapping markers (TG, HDL-C, glucose) but were designed to identify cardiometabolic risk clustering, not to quantify oxidative burden or incorporate mechanistic redox weighting. Allostatic load indices aggregate dysregulation across multiple biological systems and are not anchored to any specific redox mechanism. COSBS is distinct from all three: it is serum-based rather than self-reported, mechanistically motivated by specific ROS-generating or ROS-scavenging pathways for each component, and derived entirely from routine fasting blood tests. It is best described as a serum redox-metabolic biomarker score—acknowledging both its mechanistic grounding in redox biology and its partial overlap with metabolic phenotypes.

The ideal direct oxidative stress markers—malondialdehyde (MDA), F2-isoprostanes, superoxide dismutase (SOD), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx)—require specialized assays, strict pre-analytical protocols, and are almost never measured in large population surveys12,13. Routine blood tests, by contrast, are universally available, and several have well-documented mechanistic links to redox biology. Each of the six markers in COSBS was selected based on a specific, literature-documented mechanistic link to ROS generation or scavenging, representing distinct biological pathways: (1) uric acid reflects xanthine oxidase (XO) activity—each catalytic step converting hypoxanthine to UA releases superoxide and H₂O₂ as byproducts14,15; (2) triglycerides provide polyunsaturated fatty acid substrates for lipid peroxidation, generating oxidized lipids that activate NF-κB16; (3) fasting glucose drives ROS through at least four pathways—polyol, AGE formation, PKC activation, and mitochondrial electron transport chain dysfunction17; (4) ferritin signals labile iron stores that catalyze Fenton chemistry (Fe²⁺ + H₂O₂ → OH• + OH⁻), producing highly reactive hydroxyl radicals19—it should be noted, however, that ferritin also functions as an acute-phase reactant whose serum levels rise in response to IL-6; its inclusion in a score used to predict hs-CRP therefore introduces potential circularity, which we address analytically via a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis excluding ferritin (SA6, Table 1); (5) HDL-C transports paraoxonase-1 (PON1), which hydrolyzes oxidized phospholipids on LDL and reduces oxidized lipid accumulation18; (6) magnesium is an essential cofactor for glutathione synthetase, and deficiency impairs glutathione-mediated antioxidant capacity20.

We acknowledge that four of these six markers (TG, glucose, HDL-C, UA) also feature in metabolic syndrome criteria, and that all six are influenced by adiposity and insulin resistance. COSBS therefore captures a serum redox-metabolic phenotype rather than oxidative stress in isolation. The observed association with hs-CRP may reflect oxidative-inflammatory pathways, metabolic-inflammatory overlap, or both; disentangling these mechanisms requires longitudinal data and studies incorporating direct ROS biomarkers alongside COSBS.

The China Health and Nutrition Survey (CHNS) provides an ideal testing ground. CHNS is a large longitudinal household survey covering 15 geographically and socioeconomically diverse provinces of China, using multistage random cluster sampling across urban and rural communities21. Its 2009 wave was the first to include fasting blood biomarkers at scale. CHNS does not constitute a nationally representative sample in the strict probability-sampling sense; findings from this study should be interpreted as associations within this multi-provincial analytic sample. The 2009 wave does not measure MDA or SOD, but it measures all six of the markers listed above. No previous study has constructed a composite serum redox-metabolic biomarker score from routine blood tests and examined its association with hs-CRP in Chinese adults.

We had three goals: (1) construct and preliminarily evaluate a composite serum redox-metabolic biomarker score (COSBS) from six routine blood markers; (2) examine whether higher COSBS is associated with higher hs-CRP; (3) assess whether that association holds across subgroups and multiple sensitivity analyses.

Methods

This study follows the STROBE guidelines for reporting observational studies22.

Study design and data sources

We used a cross-sectional design. Data came from the 2009 wave of CHNS, a large longitudinal household survey covering 15 geographically and socioeconomically diverse provinces of China. CHNS uses multistage random cluster sampling across urban and rural communities. Details of the survey design have been published elsewhere21. CHNS does not constitute a nationally representative sample in the strict probability-sampling sense; findings should be interpreted as associations within this multi-provincial analytic sample.

Participants

We included adults aged 18–100 with complete data on all six COSBS components (uric acid, triglycerides, fasting glucose, HDL-cholesterol, ferritin, magnesium) and hs-CRP. We excluded participants with hs-CRP > 10 mg/L to remove acute infections23. Missing COSBS components were extremely rare (n = 7, < 0.1%); missing hs-CRP (n = 2,767) was the primary source of exclusion and is plausibly attributable to blood draw availability in the survey protocol rather than health-status-related non-response. Complete-case analysis was therefore deemed the most transparent approach; the included vs. excluded comparison (Table 2) allows readers to directly assess potential selection bias. We also excluded those with missing sex data or implausible BMI (< 10 or > 60 kg/m²). The final sample had 6,044 participants. Figure 1 shows the flow diagram.

Variables

Exposure : The COSBS was built from six fasting serum markers. Four are pro-oxidant (uric acid, triglycerides, glucose, ferritin), meaning higher levels indicate greater oxidative burden. Two are anti-oxidant (HDL-C, magnesium), meaning higher levels indicate lower oxidative burden. We ranked each marker into quintiles and assigned scores from 1 to 5. Quintile boundaries were derived from the full analytic sample (n = 6,044) and were not stratified by sex or age, so that COSBS is directly comparable across subgroups. A sensitivity analysis using sex-specific quintile boundaries (SA7) is reported in Table 1. For pro-oxidant markers, the highest quintile received 5 points; for anti-oxidant markers, scoring was reversed. The final COSBS is the sum of all six component scores (range 6–30). Higher values indicate greater oxidative-metabolic burden. Table 3 lists each component with its mechanistic rationale.

Equal weighting was applied for four reasons: (1) data-driven differential weighting from the same dataset would introduce circularity with the outcome; (2) no consensus exists in the literature regarding relative pathway contributions in Chinese adults; (3) equal weighting is the convention in established composite scores (OBS, allostatic load), facilitating comparability; (4) alternative weighting methods (SA4 Z-score, SA5 PCA) yielded consistent results, supporting the robustness of the weighting choice.

Outcome: Serum hs-CRP was measured by immunoturbidimetry (detection limit: 0.1 mg/L) from fasting blood samples. The apparent clustering of values at 1.0 mg/L in descriptive tables reflects rounding to one decimal place in the CHNS published dataset, not analytical censoring; no values were below the detection limit. A constant of 0.1 was added before log transformation for one observation recorded as zero, following standard practice. Log-transformed hs-CRP was used as the outcome in all regression models. Distributions of raw hs-CRP, log(hs-CRP), and COSBS are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Covariates: Age (continuous), sex, BMI (continuous), current smoking (yes/no), current drinking (yes/no), and urbanicity (urban vs. rural, based on CHNS urbanization index ≥ 60). BMI was treated as a confounder rather than a mediator based on the following reasoning: adiposity independently promotes inflammation via adipokine release and adipose tissue macrophage infiltration—pathways partially distinct from the ROS-mediated mechanisms captured by COSBS components—and therefore represents a common cause of both higher COSBS and higher hs-CRP. We acknowledge that adiposity also contributes to oxidative stress via NADPH oxidase activation, creating a partial mediating pathway; adjusting for BMI therefore yields a conservative estimate of the total COSBS–hs-CRP association. Both Model 2 (without BMI) and Model 3 (with BMI) are presented to facilitate assessment of BMI’s impact.

Score evaluation

We preliminarily evaluated internal consistency (Cronbach’s α), structural properties (KMO, Bartlett’s test, PCA), known-group construct patterns (smokers vs. non-smokers, BMI groups, glycemic status, sex), and scoring method consistency (Spearman correlations among quintile-sum, Z-score, and PCA approaches). The smoking comparison constitutes the most meaningful external construct evaluation because smoking is not a component of COSBS and affects oxidative stress through distinct pathways. BMI and glycemic group comparisons confirm expected internal construct patterns but involve components that directly capture those traits and should not be interpreted as independent validation.

We used COSBS quintiles with Q1 as reference to test dose-response. We used restricted cubic splines (RCS) with 4 knots to assess non-linearity; knots were placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of COSBS (values: 11, 16, 20, and 26), with reference value set at the median (COSBS = 18)24.

Survey design considerations: CHNS uses multistage random cluster sampling across 15 provinces. Participant-level probability weights corresponding to the 2009 biomarker sub-sample were not released as a validated public-use weight variable; consequently, unweighted complete-case analyses were performed. As validated probability weights were unavailable, fully design-corrected weighted analyses could not be performed. Robustness checks were conducted in lieu: community-level cluster-robust standard errors (SA10), which confirmed the primary result (see Supplementary Table 3). Findings should be interpreted as associations within the analytic sample; extrapolation to the full Chinese adult population requires caution.

We ran subgroup analyses by sex, age (18–44, 45–59, ≥ 60), BMI (< 24, 24–28, ≥ 28), and urbanicity, with formal interaction terms. Among women, an additional age-stratified analysis (< 50 vs. ≥50 years) was performed as an indirect proxy for menopausal status, given that menopausal status was not measured in CHNS 2009.

Sensitivity analyses (SA) included: SA1 (hs-CRP ≤ 3 mg/L only), SA2 (excluding BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²), SA3 (excluding probable diabetes, fasting glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L), SA4 (Z-score scoring method), SA5 (PCA-PC1 method, β sign corrected so higher score = greater oxidative burden), SA6 (leave-one-out: each component removed individually, including ferritin), SA7 (sex-specific quintile boundaries), SA8 (binary logistic regression: hs-CRP > 3 mg/L as outcome), SA9 (clinical threshold count score [CTCS]: each biomarker classified as normal/abnormal using established sex-specific clinical thresholds, count of 0–6 abnormal markers used as exposure; cut-points: UA > 420 µmol/L [men] / >360 µmol/L [women], TG > 1.70 mmol/L, fasting glucose ≥ 6.10 mmol/L, HDL-C < 1.04 mmol/L [men] / <1.30 mmol/L [women], ferritin > 300 ng/mL [men] / >150 ng/mL [women], Mg < 0.75 mmol/L), and SA10 (community-level cluster-robust standard errors, vcovCL, R package sandwich). eGFR-based renal function exclusion was not feasible as eGFR data were unavailable in the CHNS 2009 biomarker sub-study; this is acknowledged as a limitation in Sect. 4.6.

Component-level β estimates reported in Sect. 3.7 are from separate individually adjusted models (each biomarker modelled independently with Model 3 covariates), not mutually adjusted. The mutually adjusted model is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

All analyses were done in R version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Key packages included rms (RCS), tableone (baseline tables), psych (reliability analysis), and sandwich (cluster-robust standard errors). Two-sided tests with P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Score evaluation

Cronbach’s α was 0.615, acceptable for a heterogeneous biomarker panel. KMO was 0.679 and Bartlett’s test was significant (P < 0.001), confirming that factor analysis was appropriate. The first principal component explained 35.5% of total variance (Supplementary Fig. 1). These metrics reflect moderate rather than strong internal consistency, consistent with the construct representing multiple partially independent redox pathways rather than a unidimensional trait.

Smokers scored significantly higher than non-smokers (P = 4.50 × 10⁻⁴⁷), obese individuals scored higher than normal-weight individuals (P = 7.43 × 10⁻¹¹⁷), hyperglycemic individuals scored higher than normoglycemic individuals (P = 9.48 × 10⁻¹¹²), and males scored higher than females (P = 2.38 × 10⁻¹³⁵) (Fig. 2). These comparisons, however, differ importantly in their evidential value. The smoking comparison constitutes a genuinely external construct evaluation: none of the six COSBS components directly captures tobacco exposure, and smoking affects oxidative stress through distinct pathways (combustion-derived ROS, impaired antioxidant enzyme activity). By contrast, the BMI, glycemic status, and sex comparisons reflect expected internal construct patterns rather than independent validation, because several COSBS components (glucose, TG, HDL-C, UA, ferritin) directly capture obesity- and glycemia-related metabolic traits, and reference ranges for UA, ferritin, and HDL-C differ by sex. These latter comparisons confirm that the score behaves as theoretically expected but should not be interpreted as independent external validation.

The three scoring methods agreed well: quintile vs. Z-score r = 0.918; quintile vs. PCA |r| = 0.814.

Participant characteristics

The 6,044 participants averaged 52.2 ± 14.7 years old; 52.0% were female. The mean COSBS was 18.07 ± 4.46 (range 6–30). Participants in the highest tertile (T3) differed markedly from those in the lowest tertile (T1): they had higher BMI (25.33 vs. 22.80 kg/m²), were more likely to smoke (41.6% vs. 22.6%) and drink (41.7% vs. 24.2%), and had markedly higher uric acid, triglycerides, glucose, and ferritin but lower HDL-C (all P < 0.001). Median hs-CRP rose from 1.00 mg/L in T1 to 2.00 mg/L in T3 (Table 5).

Component contributions

Component-level β estimates were derived from separate individually adjusted models, in which each biomarker was modelled independently against log(hs-CRP) using the same Model 3 covariates; components were not mutually adjusted in the primary analysis. In these individually adjusted models, uric acid showed the strongest association with log(hs-CRP) (β = 0.065, 95% CI: 0.052–0.078, P = 2.46 × 10⁻²¹), followed by ferritin (β = 0.050, 95% CI: 0.037–0.063), triglycerides (β = 0.044, 95% CI: 0.032–0.057), HDL-C (β = 0.044, 95% CI: 0.031–0.056), and glucose (β = 0.036, 95% CI: 0.024–0.048). Magnesium showed no significant association (β = −0.002, 95% CI: −0.014–0.010, P = 0.73), likely reflecting the narrow distribution (SD = 0.10 mmol/L) in this sample.

In the mutually adjusted model (all six components entered simultaneously; Supplementary Table 2), triglycerides lost statistical significance (β = 0.007, P = 0.377), likely reflecting collinearity with uric acid and ferritin in the joint model, while uric acid (β = 0.049) and ferritin (β = 0.033) remained the dominant contributors. Individually adjusted estimates are reported as the primary component analysis, consistent with standard practice for composite score research (Supplementary Fig. 4).

External validation in NHANES 2015–2018

To assess cross-population generalizability, we replicated the primary analysis in an independent external cohort (NHANES 2015–2018, n = 2,752 U.S. adults) using a five-component approximation of COSBS (COSBS-5, excluding magnesium which is unavailable in NHANES, the exclusion is supported by magnesium’s negligible contribution in the derivation cohort: β = −0.002, P = 0.73, leave-one-out attenuation 6.7%). In fully adjusted models, COSBS-5 was independently associated with higher log(hs-CRP) (β = 0.048, 95% CI: 0.035–0.061, P = 2.33 × 10⁻¹³), corresponding to a 25.2% increase in geometric mean hs-CRP per SD—consistent with the CHNS derivation result (29% per SD of COSBS-6). Full validation results are provided in Supplementary Figs. 5–8 and Supplementary Tables 6–9.

Discussion

Main findings and interpretation

We aimed to examine whether a composite score derived from routine clinical blood tests is associated with systemic inflammation. Our findings indicate a robust and positive association: COSBS was independently and linearly associated with hs-CRP after adjustment for age, sex, BMI, smoking, alcohol consumption, and urbanicity. This association remained consistent across multiple subgroups and ten sensitivity analyses.

These findings must be interpreted with caution given the cross-sectional design, which precludes any causal inference. The observed association between COSBS and hs-CRP is consistent with at least three non-mutually exclusive mechanisms. First, elevated oxidative biomarkers, as captured by COSBS components, may indicate concurrent ROS generation that activates NF-κB and downstream CRP production—the primary biological rationale for score construction. Second, and equally plausible, systemic inflammation itself may elevate several COSBS components via well-established feedback pathways: ferritin is a transcriptionally regulated acute-phase reactant induced by IL-619; IL-6 also stimulates hepatic triglyceride synthesis and promotes hyperuricaemia via impaired renal urate clearance26; chronic inflammation promotes insulin resistance and thereby elevates fasting glucose. Under this reverse-causation scenario, the observed association would reflect inflammation driving COSBS upward rather than the reverse. Third, a common upstream driver—adiposity, metabolic syndrome, or shared environmental exposure—may simultaneously elevate both COSBS components and hs-CRP, producing the observed association without any direct causal link between the score and inflammation. Longitudinal studies measuring COSBS components and hs-CRP at multiple time points are needed to disentangle these pathways.

Comparison with previous studies

Our results are broadly consistent with previous oxidative balance score (OBS) research. Lakkur et al.8 found that higher OBS was associated with approximately 50% lower odds of elevated CRP in Americans, and Nanri et al.10 observed a similar inverse pattern in Japanese adults, noting ferritin as the most influential component—a finding consistent with our results. OBS has also been associated with lower cardiometabolic risk and systemic immune-inflammation indices across populations11,27.

The key distinction of the present study lies in score construction. Traditional OBS approaches are built from self-reported dietary intake and lifestyle behaviors, which are prone to recall bias and reflect exposures rather than circulating biochemical status. COSBS is based entirely on objective serum biomarkers, measuring the internal biochemical environment directly. Because higher COSBS indicates greater oxidative-metabolic burden (opposite direction to classical OBS), the positive COSBS–hs-CRP association is biologically consistent with the inverse OBS–CRP association observed in prior studies.

Potential biological mechanisms

The component-level analysis is consistent with specific oxidative pathways being differentially associated with systemic inflammation in this population.

Uric acid showed the strongest individual association with log(hs-CRP) (β = 0.065). Uric acid is the end product of purine breakdown; xanthine oxidase (XO), the enzyme that generates it, releases superoxide and hydrogen peroxide as byproducts at each catalytic step14, meaning that elevated UA may indicate concurrent ROS generation. Polito et al.14 described XO as a major vascular ROS source; Sekizuka15 proposed that XO-derived superoxide impairs endothelial nitric oxide availability; Furuhashi28 showed that plasma XO activity is independently associated with hypertension and insulin resistance. UA itself may also promote LDL oxidation and NF-κB activation in smooth muscle cells29, consistent with the observed positive association with hs-CRP.

Ferritin showed the second strongest association (β = 0.050) and warrants special interpretive caution as it plays two distinct biological roles. As an iron-storage protein, elevated ferritin is consistent with increased labile iron that catalyzes Fenton chemistry, generating hydroxyl radicals capable of membrane, DNA, and protein damage19. However, ferritin is also an acute-phase reactant transcriptionally upregulated by IL-619; under conditions of systemic inflammation, elevated ferritin may therefore reflect inflammatory activity rather than—or in addition to—iron-driven oxidative stress. The cross-sectional design prevents separation of these two pathways. To quantify the dependence of the COSBS–hs-CRP association on ferritin, we performed a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis excluding ferritin (SA6): the fully adjusted β was 0.026 (95% CI: 0.021–0.031), only 6.7% lower than the full-score estimate (β = 0.0255). This modest attenuation indicates that while ferritin contributes to the association, the COSBS–hs-CRP relationship is not artefactually generated by the ferritin–CRP acute-phase feedback loop alone, and that the remaining five components collectively capture a meaningful and independent signal.

Together, uric acid and ferritin showed the strongest individual associations with hs-CRP, a pattern consistent with enzymatic (XO-mediated) and non-enzymatic (iron-catalyzed Fenton chemistry) oxidative pathways being the most prominent correlates of systemic inflammation among the six routine blood markers examined. Magnesium, despite its theoretical importance as a glutathione cofactor20, showed no significant association (β = −0.002, P = 0.73), most likely reflecting the narrow distribution in this sample (SD = 0.10 mmol/L) rather than true biological irrelevance. In populations with wider magnesium variation, such as patients with renal disease or malnutrition, the contribution of magnesium may differ.

The unequal association sizes across components indicate that COSBS is not a uniformly balanced integration of all biomarkers but is predominantly associated with specific metabolic and iron-related pathways. This is consistent with the score functioning as a proxy for primary redox-metabolic drivers rather than a comprehensive measure of overall oxidative balance.

Subgroup differences

The COSBS–hs-CRP association was approximately twice as strong in women (β = 0.037) as in men (β = 0.016), consistent with findings from previous OBS research8,10. To explore whether this sex difference might partly reflect menopausal transition, we conducted an age-stratified analysis among women using age 50 as an indirect proxy, given that menopausal status and estradiol levels were not measured in CHNS 2009. Women aged ≥ 50 years showed a significantly stronger association (β = 0.041, 95% CI: 0.033–0.048) than younger women (β = 0.028, 95% CI: 0.019–0.037; P for interaction = 0.023), providing indirect empirical support for the hypothesis that reduced antioxidant and anti-inflammatory protection following estrogen loss amplifies the inflammatory response to the same level of oxidative-metabolic burden. Estrogen has been shown to scavenge free radicals directly, upregulate SOD and GPx expression, and suppress TNF-α, IL-6, and NF-κB30; Ishikawa et al.31 demonstrated that postmenopausal Japanese women had higher circulating reactive oxygen metabolites than premenopausal women. However, because menopausal status, years since menopause, and estradiol levels were not measured, the estrogen-loss explanation remains speculative and should be confirmed in studies with hormonal data.

The age gradient (≥ 60 years: β = 0.037 vs. 18–44 years: β = 0.013) is consistent with age-related decline in antioxidant defenses—SOD, catalase, and glutathione all decline with aging32—and accumulated oxidative damage, as described in the inflammageing framework32. In the oldest group, even modest oxidative-metabolic biomarker elevations were associated with relatively larger hs-CRP increases, suggesting that oxidative status may be particularly relevant to inflammation interpretation in older populations. These subgroup patterns are observational and cannot establish mechanism.

Research utility and translational implications

The principal methodological strength of COSBS is its exclusive reliance on routine fasting blood tests available in standard clinical and epidemiological settings worldwide, circumventing the need for specialized oxidative stress assays12,13. The COSBS circumvents these constraints by integrating six biomarkers with established mechanistic links to redox biology into a single, reproducible composite measure. We acknowledge that because several components also associate with metabolic syndrome, the score captures a redox-metabolic phenotype rather than oxidative stress in isolation; this dual nature is a feature to be acknowledged rather than a flaw to be corrected, as it reflects the biological reality that oxidative and metabolic dysregulation are intertwined.

We identify three concrete research utilities supported by the present data. First, COSBS provides a reproducible exposure variable for future cohort and intervention studies examining oxidative-metabolic burden as a determinant of inflammatory disease, without requiring non-standard assays. Second, the dose-response data offer a benchmark for effect size estimation: Q5 vs. Q1 was associated with approximately 45% higher geometric mean hs-CRP, comparable to the increment associated with moderate obesity25, which can inform power calculations for prospective studies. Third, the component-level analysis identifies specific biological targets—urate-lowering for high UA, iron restriction for elevated ferritin, lipid management for hypertriglyceridaemia, glycaemic control for elevated glucose—that could be evaluated in mechanistic intervention studies.

The boundaries of what the present study can support must be clearly stated. As a composite epidemiological research index, COSBS requires construct validity—whether the score captures the target construct—and criterion validity—whether it associates with an established criterion measure of that construct. Both are demonstrated: COSBS shows expected known-group patterns (higher scores in smokers, obese individuals, and those with hyperglycaemia; all P < 0.001) and is independently associated with hs-CRP across all models, subgroups, and ten sensitivity analyses. Predictive validity—whether COSBS predicts future cardiovascular or metabolic endpoints—is a separate and higher standard not required of an epidemiological research index, and cannot be established from a cross-sectional study. This study does not evaluate clinical outcomes, predictive performance, risk reclassification, or incremental value over existing risk scores. The cross-sectional design precludes inference about whether modifying COSBS reduces hs-CRP or disease incidence. Independent external validation was conducted in NHANES 2015–2018 (n = 2,752 U.S. adults), where the association replicated directionally and quantitatively (β = 0.048, 95% CI: 0.035–0.061, P = 2.33 × 10⁻¹³; 25.2% higher geometric mean hs-CRP per SD). Until prospective evidence with hard endpoints is available, COSBS should be regarded strictly as a preliminarily evaluated epidemiological research index and hypothesis-generating tool—not a clinical screening instrument. Prospective validation against hard endpoints, head-to-head comparison with direct oxidative biomarkers, and replication in contemporary Chinese cohorts using the full six-component COSBS-6 remain essential next steps.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths. We preliminarily evaluated the score using Cronbach’s α, PCA, known-group construct tests, three alternative scoring methods, and leave-one-out analysis, and expanded sensitivity analyses to ten pre-specified analyses. We utilized a large multi-provincial survey with standardized laboratory procedures and excluded hs-CRP > 10 mg/L to focus on chronic low-grade inflammation. The included vs. excluded participant comparison (Table 2) allows readers to directly assess potential selection bias. The 2009 CHNS wave provides a uniquely comprehensive multi-provincial record including serum magnesium and ferritin—markers frequently omitted in more recent surveys.

Limitations. First, the cross-sectional design means no causal claims can be made; reverse causation is a genuine concern, as systemic inflammation itself elevates ferritin, UA, TG, and glucose via well-established feedback pathways. Second, and most importantly for conceptual interpretation, four of the six COSBS components (TG, glucose, HDL-C, UA) overlap directly with metabolic syndrome criteria, and all six are influenced by adiposity and insulin resistance. COSBS therefore captures a serum redox-metabolic phenotype rather than oxidative stress in isolation. The observed association with hs-CRP may partly or predominantly reflect the well-established link between metabolic syndrome and systemic inflammation rather than a specifically redox-mediated pathway. Future studies should incorporate direct redox biomarkers (e.g., F₂-isoprostanes, MDA, SOD activity) alongside COSBS to quantify the fraction of the association specifically attributable to oxidative mechanisms. Third, we did not adjust for physical activity, dietary quality, or medication use in the primary model, representing potential sources of residual confounding. Physical activity and dietary data are available in CHNS 2009 but were not incorporated within the scope of this analysis; CHNS 2009 also collected self-reported antihypertensive and antidiabetic medication use, but did not systematically record lipid-lowering agents. Future analyses should incorporate these variables as sensitivity analyses. Fourth, all markers were derived from a single blood draw, creating potential shared pre-analytical noise. Fifth, the temporal distance of approximately 15 years since data collection introduces generalizability concerns: China has undergone substantial epidemiological transition since 2009, including rising obesity prevalence, dietary westernization, and expanded medication use. These secular trends may alter COSBS component distributions and association magnitudes in contemporary populations. To partially address this concern, we conducted independent external validation in NHANES 2015–2018 (n = 2,752 U.S. adults), where the COSBS-5–hs-CRP association replicated directionally and quantitatively (β = 0.048, 95% CI: 0.035–0.061, P = 2.33 × 10⁻¹³; 25.2% higher geometric mean hs-CRP per SD), six years after the index dataset and in a population with markedly different dietary patterns and ethnic composition. Nevertheless, replication using the full six-component COSBS-6 in contemporary Chinese cohorts remains an important priority. Future prospective validation against hard endpoints remains an important priority.Sixth, the sample is exclusively Chinese adults from 15 provinces; generalizability to other ethnic groups requires further investigation. Seventh, sampling weights for the 2009 CHNS biomarker sub-sample were unavailable; unweighted estimates may not reflect population-level associations, and findings should be interpreted as associations within this multi-provincial analytic sample. Eighth, the BMI and glycemic status known-group construct comparisons involve built-in circularity because TG and glucose are COSBS components.

Conclusion

In 6,044 Chinese adults from the 2009 CHNS, a composite serum redox-metabolic biomarker score (COSBS) built from six routine blood tests—uric acid, triglycerides, fasting glucose, ferritin, HDL-C, and magnesium—was independently and linearly associated with hs-CRP. Uric acid and ferritin showed the strongest individual associations, consistent with xanthine oxidase activity and iron-catalyzed Fenton chemistry as key correlates of systemic inflammation. Women and older adults showed stronger associations, patterns consistent with estrogen loss and age-related antioxidant decline, respectively, though these explanations require prospective confirmation.

COSBS functions as a reproducible, low-cost exposure index for population-based oxidative-metabolic research, using markers already present in standard blood panels. Whether it has clinical utility for risk stratification or screening requires prospective validation against hard endpoints, external replication, and head-to-head comparison with direct redox biomarkers—evidence the present cross-sectional study cannot provide. Future priorities include: (1) longitudinal validation against CVD, diabetes, and mortality outcomes; (2) external replication in contemporary Chinese and non-Chinese cohorts; (3) integration with direct oxidative biomarkers to quantify the specifically redox-mediated fraction of the association; (4) assessment of whether targeted interventions that modify COSBS components reduce inflammatory burden.
