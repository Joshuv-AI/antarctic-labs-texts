Knowledge of energy-based devices among surgical residents: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Can Uc, Yigit Turk, Pınar Uc, Recep Temel, Murat Ozdemır, Ozer Makay
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study evaluates the knowledge of surgical residents in a tertiary hospital regarding the operational principles, potential complications, and mechanisms of energy devices used in surgery.MethodsA 16-question test was developed using educational materials in the “Fundamental Use of Surgical Energy (FUSE)” program, prepared by the Society of American Gastrointestinal and Endoscopic Surgeons (SAGES) for educational purposes, and from literature on energy devices. The test was shared online with surgical branch residents via Google Forms, and they were asked to complete it. Participants were given one week to complete the test and return the results online.ResultsA total of 108 residents voluntarily participated in the study. The average number of correct answers was 6.5 out of 16. There was no significant difference between the correct answer rates for surgical branches. However, an increase in surgical seniority was associated with a higher number of correct answers (P < 0.001).ConclusionTheoretical education about energy devices in surgical training needs to be improved. Surgical residents reported insufficient knowledge regarding the devices’ operational principles and complication mechanisms.Keywords: Electrosurgery, Education, Surgical equipment

INTRODUCTION

With energy devices, operation times have shortened, intraoperative and postoperative complications have decreased, minimally invasive surgical techniques have advanced, and new surgical techniques have been developed. Despite these benefits, energy devices can cause adverse effects such as thermal damage leading to intestinal perforations, injuries to surrounding tissues, and operating room fires. Each energy-based device has different working mechanisms, and with a better understanding of these mechanisms, they can be used reliably and effectively, with greater awareness in this field preventing complications [1,2,3].

There is a lack of knowledge among surgeons and residents, who should be more educated on this subject to prevent complications and in pursuance of more effective use of newly developed energy devices [4,5]. This study evaluates the knowledge of surgical residents in a tertiary hospital setting regarding the working principles, potential complications, and mechanisms of energy devices.

METHODS

Ethics statements

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ege University (No. 23-3T/2) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All of the participants’ names were anonymized, and informed consent was obtained. All data generated or analyzed during this study are included in this published article, and its supplementary information files have been uploaded. Informed consent was obtained from all of the participants.

Participant selection

In a tertiary research and educational hospital, all surgical branch residents were sent a questionnaire via Google Forms, and residents voluntarily participated in the study. Participants were given 1 week to complete the test online. The participants were grouped according to their surgical branch and years of residency.

Individual knowledge level and confidence

The participants were asked about how much they know regarding energy device working principles, safe usage, and possible complications. They evaluated their knowledge as adequate, partially adequate, or inadequate. In addition, participants were asked whether they needed theoretical training in the use of energy devices.

The questionnaire

A test was prepared from the educational materials in the “Fundamental Use of Surgical Energy (FUSE)” program, prepared by the Society of American Gastrointestinal and Endoscopic Surgeons (SAGES) for educational purposes, and from the literature on energy devices.

Nine questions were asked regarding the basic working principles of energy devices, and 7 questions were asked regarding complications and their mechanisms. In total, 16 questions were asked (Appendix 1). The answers to the questions were evaluated as correct or incorrect.

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 26.0 (IBM Corp.) was used for the analysis. Normality was evaluated by the Kolmogrov-Smirnow test in the comparison of groups, the independent t-test was applied in the comparison of paired groups with normal distribution, and the Mann-Whitney U-test was applied in the comparison of paired groups without normal distribution. Group answers were compared. In the comparison of 2 or more groups, a one-way analysis of variance was applied for normally distributed data, and the Kruskall-Wallis test was applied for non-normally distributed data. The confidence interval was set at 95%, with a 0.05 limit for the significance of the P-value.

RESULTS

The online forms of 132 participants were collected, 24 of which were excluded from the study due to a lack of data. Thus, 108 participants with complete data were included in the study. A total of 108 residents participated, comprising 34 from general surgery, 24 from gynecology and obstetrics, 12 from urology, 8 from pediatric surgery, 4 from cardiovascular surgery, 6 from orthopedics, 4 from plastic and reconstructive surgery, 6 from neurosurgery, and 10 from otolaryngology (Fig. 1). Of the participating residents, 16 were in their first year of training, 28 were in their 2nd year, 31 were in their 3rd year, 23 were in their 4th year, and 10 were in their 5th year (Fig. 2).

Overall, 50% of the participants stated that they had a partially adequate level of knowledge about energy devices, 18% said they had sufficient knowledge, and 33% said they had insufficient knowledge. While 29% of the participants stated that they were aware of the complications that may develop with energy devices, 71% said that they were not.

The average score for correct answers was 6.5 out of 16, with a mean of 3.8 for basic principles and 2.7 for complications. The most correctly answered question was the 15th question, with 78 participants (72.3%) answering it correctly. The least correctly answered question was the 13th question, with only 18 participants (16.7%) answering it correctly. This was followed by the 5th question with 21 correct answers (19.5%) and the 3rd question with 24 correct answers (22.2%). There was no significant difference between the correct answer rate and the surgical branch (P = 0.137); however, an increase in seniority correlated with more correct answers (P < 0 .001). The mean correct answers according to the surgical branch are shown in Figs. 3 and 4 and the mean correct answers according to residency year are shown in Fig. 5. When the correct answers given by the participants were compared with their responses on the adequacy of their knowledge, it was determined that the residents who evaluated themselves as competent gave fewer correct answers than those who evaluated their knowledge as partially adequate (P < 0.001). While 90 participants (83.3%) stated that they needed education regarding energy devices, 18 participants (16.7%) stated that they did not need education.

DISCUSSION

Energy devices are used around the world in almost every surgical procedure. Although they are safe and effective, they can cause serious problems. Surgeons and healthcare professionals are expected to be familiar with their basic working principles, the mechanisms of complications, and the management of complications [4]. This study reveals a substantial knowledge gap among residents regarding these critical aspects.

Incorporating diverse surgical branches strengthens this study by highlighting that knowledge deficits are consistent across specialties, underscoring the universal need for enhanced education on energy devices. In our study, the rate for correct answers was 40.8%. This result is similar to those reported in the literature. Ha et al. [6] compared the knowledge levels of surgery specialists and residents. They reported correct answer rates of 54.9% and 48.6%, respectively, with no significant difference between the 2 groups. They noted that there was no standardized education program on the safe use of energy devices and that both specialists and residents needed more knowledge.

When the incorrectly answered questions were examined, it was found that the most frequently incorrect ones were related to the wave characteristics of the ‘cut’ and ‘coagulation’ modes, the basic properties of electrical current, and the mechanisms of complications. The FUSE program was developed by SAGES to fill the knowledge gap [7]. Feldman et al. [8] administered a test with similar questions, and the correct answer rate rose to 90% after a half-day FUSE training; however, while the training was shown to be effective, the knowledge was forgotten over time, and the training required repetition. Nduka et al. [9] noted that complications related to the use of energy devices can only be prevented by an adequate knowledge level and understanding of the working principles. Theoretical information on energy devices should be given at least once a year during surgical training so that this information is permanently retained. In our study, only a test was administered to the participants. No positive or negative feedback was given, and the test was not repeated after the training session. Therefore, the contribution of theoretical training could not be demonstrated in this study, which is one of its limitations.

Education regarding the devices is based on operating room and industry-sponsored events. There is no theoretical education on the working principles of energy devices [3]. This explains the increase in correct answers given in the test as surgical seniority increased. In a study conducted by Madani et al. [5], residents who received training on energy devices and gained extra experience provided significantly more correct answers Surgical training and simulations related to energy devices were lacking among the residents, and training in this field should be improved [10].

It was realized that the residents who considered themselves as having sufficient knowledge about the working principles of energy devices actually did not have enough knowledge; they lacked theoretical knowledge about the working principles of the energy devices. This situation can be explained by the Dunning-Kruger effect. The Dunning-Kruger effect refers to a cognitive bias where people with low ability, knowledge, or expertise in a particular area overestimate their own competence. Essentially, they lack the self-awareness to recognize their limitations, leading to overconfidence [11].

One limitation of this study is its focus solely on surgical residents, whereas a comprehensive educational approach should include all members of the surgical team, including nurses and technicians. As a surgical team’s experience increases, complication rates decrease, and operation times shorten [12]. In contrast to multi-clinic studies, another limitation of this study is the small number of participants. The power of the study could be increased with a multicentric and multi-clinic sample including specialists.

Taking everything into consideration, it can be concluded that theoretical education regarding energy devices in surgical training is insufficient, and surgical residents feel that their knowledge on the working principles of energy devices and the mechanisms of complications is inadequate. A structured educational curriculum is necessary to ensure the safe and effective use of energy devices across all surgical disciplines.
