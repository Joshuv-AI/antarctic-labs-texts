Risk stratification of advanced extranodal extension in retropharyngeal lymph nodes for nasopharyngeal carcinoma: a multi-center study
Authors: Junyi Liu, Zhenzhang Chen, Bolin Lu, Tong Bu, Tong Jin, Changyong He, Hongping Zhou, Yinsu Zhu, Shengfu Huang, Xiaojing Du, Jin Gao, Xiaozhong Chen, Xia He, Lirong Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to refine risk stratification of advanced extranodal extension (ENE) in retropharyngeal lymph nodes (RLNs) and evaluate its prognostic significance.MethodsA total of 1,373 non-metastatic NPC patients at three centers between 2011 and 2021 were retrospectively enrolled. For advanced RLN ENE, we documented all involved structures, maximum axial diameter (MAD), presence of lymph node necrosis (LNN), and bilaterality. High-risk structures were identified using adjusted hazard ratios and LASSO-Cox models. Multivariable Cox models and Kaplan-Meier analyses were used to evaluate associations with survival outcomes.ResultsOf the 1,373 patients, 1061 had RLN metastasis, including 168 with advanced RLN ENE. Involvement of interval structures, the lower cranial nerves region, internal jugular vein, and longus capitis muscle was classified as high-risk, whereas isolated internal carotid artery involvement was low-risk. Advanced RLN ENE with high-risk structure involvement or LNN was defined as high-risk RLN ENE. High-risk RLN ENE was an independent adverse prognostic factor for NPC. N1/N2 patients with high-risk RLN ENE showed survival comparable to N3 disease.ConclusionsHigh-risk RLN ENE is an independent adverse prognostic factor in NPC. N1/N2 patients with high-risk RLN ENE exhibit N3-like survival, supporting its potential incorporation into future refinements of N staging.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40644-026-01067-z.

Introduction

Accurate tumor staging is essential for assessing disease extent and guiding appropriate treatment [1, 2]. The 9th edition AJCC/UICC TNM staging system for nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), implemented in 2025, introduced a key change to the N category by incorporating advanced cervical lymph node (CLN) extranodal extension (ENE) [3]. ENE is a well-established adverse prognostic factor in head and neck cancers [4–8]. During the era of the 8th edition AJCC/UICC system, pathologically confirmed or clinically determined ENE was integrated into N staging for head and neck malignancies, with the exceptions of NPC and human papillomavirus (HPV)–related oropharyngeal carcinoma [9–11]. This exclusion largely reflected the reliance on imaging for diagnosing ENE in NPC and the observation from two early studies that imaging-identified ENE (iENE) did not confer prognostic discrimination across key endpoints [12, 13]. A potential explanation for this finding is the lack of reasonable grading criteria for iENE.

Subsequent studies established several grading frameworks for iENE in NPC [4–7]. The commonly adopted grading systems classify iENE into three grades: (1) infiltration of surrounding fat plane only, (2) matted or coalescent nodes, and (3) invasion of adjacent structures. Grade-3 iENE has been validated as an independent adverse prognostic factor in NPC, and N1/N2 patients with grade-3 iENE have outcomes approximating those of N3 disease [14]. Accordingly, the 9th edition AJCC/UICC staging system upstages advanced CLN ENE to N3 [3].

Previous studies have predominantly focused on CLN ENE, whereas evidence on retropharyngeal lymph node (RLN) ENE remains limited. This research gap is primarily attributed to the difficulty in distinguishing RLN ENE from primary tumor invasion on imaging [4, 15]. Consequently, the 9th edition AJCC/UICC staging system does not currently incorporate RLN ENE.

A six-center study from endemic regions recently examined the impact of advanced RLN ENE on N classification [15]. The investigators reported that advanced RLN ENE was not an independent adverse prognostic factor; N1/N2 patients with advanced RLN ENE had better survival than those with N3 disease. Notably, the survival gap between N1/N2 patients with advanced RLN ENE and N3 patients was much smaller than the gap between other N1/N2 patients and N3 patients, and no difference was observed in regional recurrence–free survival (RRFS). This indicates that the prognosis of the former is closer to the N3 stage level. These findings imply that further subclassification of advanced RLN ENE may help delineate independent prognostic determinants in NPC. A study by Zhong et al. [16] demonstrated that among N1 stage patients, lymph node necrosis (LNN) and matted nodes (MNs) were adverse lymph node (LN) features, with a significant prognostic impact when both are present. However, no studies have yet performed further risk stratification of advanced RLN ENE, including identifying the specific structures involved by advanced RLN ENE. Additionally, there is a lack of research on advanced RLN ENE combined with other adverse nodal features.

Furthermore, the aforementioned study on advanced RLN ENE was conducted in NPC-endemic regions; validation in non-endemic settings remains necessary. Therefore, this study aims to develop an imaging-based risk stratification model for advanced RLN ENE to enable more refined prognostic assessment and to inform individualized treatment decision-making.

Methods

Study population

A total of 1,373 newly diagnosed patients with pathologically confirmed non-metastatic (M0) NPC in Jiangsu Cancer Hospital, Zhejiang Cancer Hospital, and The First Affiliated Hospital of USTC from January 2011 to December 2021 were retrospectively analyzed. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of all participating institutions, with a waiver of informed consent owing to its retrospective, anonymized design. Inclusion criteria were: (1) histologically confirmed NPC; (2) complete pretreatment demographic, medical history, physical examination, and laboratory data; (3) head and neck MRI performed within 2 weeks before treatment initiation; and (4) receipt of definitive intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT). Exclusion criteria were: (1) concurrently diagnosed with other types of tumors; (2) distant metastasis at diagnosis; and (3) lack of follow-up information. All cases were retrospectively restaged according to the 9th edition AJCC/UICC TNM classification.

Treatment

All patients underwent radical IMRT using simultaneous integrated boost (SIB), as previously described [17]. Target tumor volumes were delineated according to the regulations of the International Commission on Radiation Units and Measurements Reports 50 and 62. The prescribed doses were 66–74 Gy/ 66–72 Gy in 32–34 fractions to the planning target volume (PTV) of primary gross tumor volume and the volume of positive lymph nodes. The PTVs for high-risk and low-risk clinical target volumes (CTVs) received 56–60 Gy/32 fractions and 50–52 Gy/28 fractions. Stage IA patients received IMRT alone. For Stage IB-III disease, platinum-based chemotherapy was administered every three weeks either before, during, or after radiotherapy. The cumulative chemotherapy cycles did not exceed six cycles throughout the treatment course.

Follow up

Patients underwent follow-up assessments every three months during the first two years post-treatment, every six months from years 3 to 5, and annually thereafter until the last visit or death. The routine evaluations included history collection, physical examination, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the head and neck, chest X-ray or contrast-enhanced CT, and abdominal ultrasound or CT. Local and regional recurrences were confirmed by histopathological diagnosis. Distant metastases were diagnosed via imaging modalities (including MRI, CT, or ¹⁸F-FDG PET/CT) when biopsy was unfeasible or contraindicated.

Imaging

All patients underwent MRI using a 1.5-T scanner (Achieva, Philips Medical Systems, the Netherlands). The MRI scanning protocol included unenhanced T1-weighted sequences, T2-weighted sequences, enhanced T1-weighted sequences, and fat-suppressed sequences. Detailed MRI acquisition parameters are provided in Table A1. RLNs were considered positive if meeting either criterion: Any visible node in the median retropharyngeal group or Short-axis diameter ≥ 5 mm in the lateral retropharyngeal group [18]. Advanced RLN ENE was defined as iENE with involvement of adjacent muscles, skin, and/or neurovascular bundle [14, 15]. For advanced RLN ENE (Fig. 1), we recorded all adjacent structures involved, including the internal carotid artery, internal jugular vein, lower cranial nerves region (Direct lower cranial nerves invasion is difficult to detect radiologically; therefore, we documented cases where advanced RLN ENE completely invades the region where the lower cranial nerves are located), longus capitis muscle, interval structures (external carotid artery, vertebral vessels, and longissimus cervicis muscle). Imaging criteria for advanced RLN ENE were adapted from the AOSNHNR–ASHNR–ESHNR Joint Task Force recommendations for imaging-defined ENE. Detailed imaging criteria for advanced RLN ENE are provided in the Supplementary Methods. We also documented the corresponding RLN’s maximum axial diameter (MAD), the presence of LNN, and whether advanced RLN ENE was bilateral.

Statistical analysis

Study endpoints included overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), locoregional relapse-free survival (LRRFS), and distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS). All time-to-event outcomes were calculated from the date of initial diagnosis to the event or last follow-up. A random sample of 100 MRI examinations was used to assess inter- and intra-reader agreement, quantified by Cohen’s κ. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables that were not normally distributed were compared with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were applied to identify factors associated with outcomes. For structure-specific risk stratification of RLN ENE, N1 patients with RLN metastasis but excluding advanced RLN ENE served as the reference group. Candidate structures were ranked by adjusted hazard ratios (AHRs) from multivariable Cox models and LASSO–Cox selection [19]. Two-sided p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted in R-Studio (https://www.rstudio.com/).

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 1373 patients were included. Table A2 summarizes demographics, overall stage, T and N categories, RLN characteristics, and treatment patterns for the entire cohort and by center. RLN metastasis was present in 1061 of 1373 patients (77.3%) (Table 1); the median age was 49 years, and the male-to-female ratio was 2.60:1. Among RLN-positive patients, N1, N2, and N3 accounted for 531 (50.0%), 298 (28.1%), and 232 (21.9%), respectively. During follow-up, 341 patients (32.1%) experienced disease progression and 181 (17.1%) died; locoregional relapse (LRR) and distant metastasis (DM) occurred in 148 (13.9%) and 201 (18.9%) patients, respectively. The 5-year OS, PFS, LRRFS, and DMFS rates were 84.8%, 69.4%, 86.8%, and 81.0%, respectively.

Characteristics of advanced RLN ENE

Among 1061 RLN-positive patients, 168 (15.8%) had advanced RLN ENE. Intra- and inter-reader agreement for diagnosing advanced RLN ENE were 0.805 and 0.768. By N category, the proportions of advanced RLN ENE were 56/531 (10.5%) in N1, 44/298 (14.8%) in N2, and 68/232 (29.3%) in N3. On structure-specific review, the most frequently involved structure was the internal carotid artery (162/168, 96.4%), followed by the longus capitis muscle (95/168, 56.5%), lower cranial nerves region (41/168, 24.4%), the internal jugular vein (28/168, 16.7%), and interval structures (15/168, 8.9%). Among patients with advanced RLN ENE, concomitant LNN, MAD ≥ 2 cm, and bilateral advanced RLN ENE were present in 41/168 (24.4%), 78/168 (46.4%), and 38/168 (22.6%), respectively (Table A3).

Structure-specific risk ranking for RLN ENE

To evaluate the survival impact of structure-specific involvement by RLN ENE, we fitted structure-specific multivariable Cox proportional hazards models for OS and PFS, adjusting for age, sex, T category, and treatment modality (Table A4, Table A5). The reference group was defined as N1 patients with RLN metastasis but excluding advanced RLN ENE. The AHROS were as follows: internal carotid artery, 2.50; internal jugular vein, 3.16; longus capitis muscle, 3.46; lower cranial nerves region, 3.72; and interval structures, 5.24 (Fig. 2A). Kaplan–Meier curves illustrated distinct survival differences across involved structures (Fig. 2B). Subsequently, validation with a LASSO–Cox model showed the largest positive coefficients for longus capitis, lower cranial nerves region, and interval structures, indicating predominant adverse effects, whereas isolated internal carotid artery involvement carried a negative coefficient, suggesting limited independent risk after adjustment (Fig. 2C). In Fig. 2D, advanced RLN ENE–positive patients stratified by the LASSO–Cox score into high-risk and low-risk groups exhibited a significant OS separation (p < .001). Similar findings were observed for PFS (Figure A1). Accordingly, isolated internal carotid artery involvement was classified as low-risk structural involvement, whereas involvement of the internal jugular vein, lower cranial nerves region, longus capitis muscle, or interval structures was classified as high-risk structural involvement.

Risk stratification of advanced RLN ENE

We entered advanced RLN ENE with high-risk structural involvement, advanced RLN ENE with LNN, advanced RLN ENE with MAD ≥ 2 cm, and bilateral advanced RLN ENE—together with age, sex, T category, N category, and treatment modality—into multivariable Cox models (Table 2). Advanced RLN ENE with high-risk structural involvement was significantly associated with worse OS and DMFS (AHROS:2.10, 95%CI:1.32–3.36, p = .002; AHRDMFS:1.81, 95%CI:1.13–2.89, p = .013). Advanced RLN ENE with LNN was significant for PFS and DMFS (AHRPFS:2.04, 95%CI:1.33–3.12, p = .001; AHRDMFS:1.88, 95%CI:1.11–3.18, p = .018). Univariate analysis is presented in Table A6.

Based on these results, we defined high-risk RLN ENE as advanced RLN ENE with high-risk structural involvement and/or LNN; all other cases were classified as low-risk RLN ENE. Kaplan–Meier curves with log-rank tests demonstrated significantly worse outcomes for high-risk RLN ENE compared with patients without advanced RLN ENE (5-year OS: 64.4% vs. 87.3% p < .001; 5-year PFS: 43.4% vs. 72.6% p < .001) (Figure A2). In multivariable analysis, high-risk RLN ENE remained an independent adverse prognostic factor (AHROS:1.69, 95%CI:1.18–2.41, p = .004; AHRPFS:1.43, 95%CI:1.08–1.91, p = .013; AHRDMFS:1.56, 95%CI:1.09–2.22, p = .014), whereas low-risk RLN ENE showed outcomes comparable to advanced RLN ENE–negative patients (AHROS:0.45, 95%CI:0.17–1.24, p = .122; AHRPFS:0.62, 95%CI:0.34–1.15, p = .128) (Table A7).

Survival comparison between N1/N2 patients with high-risk RLN ENE and N3 disease

Given the prognostic impact of high-risk RLN ENE, we evaluated its potential incorporation into N classification. Kaplan–Meier curves showed that N1/N2 patients with high-risk RLN ENE had a prognosis similar to that of N3 patients, with comparable 5-year OS, PFS, LRRFS, and DMFS rates (5-year OS: 68.2% vs. 67.1% vs. 71.7%; PFS: 53.1% vs. 49.2% vs. 48.5%) (Fig. 3). Multivariable Cox regression analysis adjusted for confounding factors revealed that N1 patients with high-risk RLN ENE (AHROS = 0.77, 95%CI: 0.42–1.42, p = .402; AHRPFS = 0.72, 95%CI: 0.43–1.19, p = .199) and N2 patients with high-risk RLN ENE (AHROS = 0.68, 95%CI: 0.35–1.31, p = .247; AHRPFS = 0.70, 95%CI: 0.41–1.19, p = .186) had outcomes comparable to those of N3 patients (Table 3). Similar results were observed for the endpoints of LRRFS and DMFS (Table 3).

Discussion

This study pioneered a finer subdivision of advanced RLN ENE based on the specific structures involved. Using AHRs from multivariable Cox models together with LASSO–Cox selection, isolated internal carotid artery involvement was classified as low-risk structural involvement, whereas involvement of the internal jugular vein, lower cranial nerves region, longus capitis muscle, external carotid artery, vertebral vessels, and longissimus cervicis constituted the high-risk group. We also assessed the RLN MAD, the presence of LNN, and bilaterality among advanced RLN ENE–positive nodes. In multivariable analyses, both advanced RLN ENE with high-risk structural involvement and advanced RLN ENE with LNN were independent adverse prognostic factors. Accordingly, we defined high-risk RLN ENE as advanced RLN ENE with high-risk structural involvement and/or LNN. High-risk RLN ENE was identified as an independent adverse prognostic factor for OS, PFS, and DMFS in NPC patients. N1/N2 NPC patients with high-risk RLN ENE exhibited N3-like survival.

RLNs are a common site of metastasis in NPC [20]. RLNs lie within the retropharyngeal space, bounded anteriorly by the pharyngobasilar fascia and posteriorly by the alar fascia, which continues laterally to the carotid sheath [21]. As one of the first-echelon nodal stations in NPC, the RLNs are frequently involved, with reported metastasis rates of 63.4%–88.2% [22, 23]. Increasing attention has been paid to RLN disease in recent years, and multiple studies have shown that RLN involvement is associated with inferior OS, PFS, and DMFS [18, 24]. In the 8th edition AJCC/UICC TNM staging system for NPC, unilateral or bilateral RLN metastasis was classified as N1 [11].

As noted above, ENE is an adverse prognostic factor in head and neck cancers, including NPC [4, 25]. During the 8th edition staging era, however, ENE was not incorporated into the N category for NPC owing to insufficient evidence [12, 13]. Building on work by Mao, Chin, and others, iENE was formalized into three grades; grade 3—i.e., advanced CLN ENE with invasion of adjacent structures—was shown to be prognostically relevant in NPC and was consequently adopted into the N3 category in the 9th edition [3–5]. Notably, these ENE frameworks did not encompass RLN ENE, and few investigations have addressed it. Recently, Jiang et al. [15] conducted the first systematic evaluation of RLN ENE: although advanced RLN ENE was not an independent prognostic factor for OS, DFS, or DMFS, it significantly affected RRFS; moreover, among N1/N2 patients with RLN ENE, the survival gap versus N3 disease was smaller than that observed for other N1/N2 patients, with AHRs approaching 1 (and p values > 0.05 in some cohorts). These observations suggest that the current construct of advanced RLN ENE remains broad and internally heterogeneous, warranting further risk stratification.

Beyond ENE, LNN, MAD, and bilaterality have been extensively investigated. Results regarding the prognostic impact of LNN are heterogeneous across studies. Feng et al. [26], assessing LNN in both RLNs and CLNs, proposed upstaging N1/N2 patients with LNN to N2/N3. Fu et al. [27] further showed that, among N1 patients, the presence of LNN was associated with worse outcomes than its absence. By contrast, Qiu et al. [28] reported that cervical nodal necrosis (CNN) did not significantly affect survival; notably, in their cohort most CNN co-occurred with advanced CLN ENE, potentially masking the independent effect of LNN. More recently, Zhong et al. [16] found that the coexistence of LNN and MNs markedly worsened survival, suggesting synergistic interactions among nodal features. The prognostic value of unilateral versus bilateral RLN metastasis likewise remains unsettled: some studies have advocated reclassifying bilateral RLN involvement from N1 to N2, whereas others have observed no significant differences across endpoints [24, 29, 30]. To date, no study has systematically evaluated how advanced RLN ENE interacts with other adverse nodal characteristics.

In our cohort, advanced RLN ENE most commonly extended posterolaterally, with the internal carotid artery being the most frequently involved structure. Integrating the multivariable Cox and LASSO–Cox results, internal carotid artery involvement was classified as a low-risk pattern. Anatomically, the internal carotid artery, internal jugular vein, and lower cranial nerves course within or along the carotid sheath. When advanced RLN ENE only invades or encircles the internal carotid artery, its risk is relatively lower than that of involvement of other structures. On the other hand, it is also radiologically challenging to determine whether the carotid sheath has been penetrated and the intra-fascial tissues have been invaded. By contrast, involvement of the internal jugular vein or lower cranial nerves region typically implies full-thickness encasement of the sheath and more extensive spread, which supports their classification as high-risk structures. The longus capitis muscle, situated medial to the lateral RLNs, was the muscle most frequently invaded. Notably, longus capitis invasion often co-occurred with internal carotid artery involvement, indicating concurrent medial-lateral invasion extension that may further worsen prognosis. We also assessed the RLN MAD, the presence of LNN, and bilaterality. In multivariable analyses, advanced RLN ENE with LNN was significantly associated with worse PFS, suggesting a close synergistic interaction between advanced RLN ENE and LNN, in line with recent observations by Zhong et al. [16].

This study has several limitations. First, it is a retrospective analysis; despite multivariable adjustment, residual confounding cannot be excluded, and prospective validation is warranted. Second, the cohort was drawn from non-endemic regions; although more than one thousand patients were included, larger multicenter studies in endemic areas are needed to confirm generalizability. Third, in some patients, the RLNs were confluent with the primary tumor, making it difficult to determine whether the involved structures were attributable to advanced RLN ENE invasion. Therefore, such cases were not classified as advanced RLN ENE in this study. Finally, plasma EBV DNA levels were not analyzed. Although these biomarkers have recognized prognostic value in NPC, the lack of standardized assays across institutions and eras posed practical barriers to inclusion.

Conclusions

Since the introduction of the 9th edition staging, ENE has garnered increasing attention, yet its reliable determination remains challenging. Geographic (endemic vs. non-endemic), healthcare resource, demographic, and biological differences may all modulate the prognostic impact of advanced RLN ENE. A structure-specific approach offers a pragmatic solution. In this study, involvement of interval structures, longus capitis muscle, internal jugular vein, and the lower cranial nerves region—as well as concomitant LNN—effectively stratify advanced RLN ENE into two groups with significant survival differences. We therefore defined high-risk RLN ENE as advanced RLN ENE with high-risk structural involvement and/or LNN; this category was an independent adverse prognostic factor for NPC. N1/N2 patients harboring high-risk RLN ENE demonstrated N3-like outcomes.
