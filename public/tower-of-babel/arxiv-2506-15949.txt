Abstract
. Let T c , β T_{c,\beta} denote the smallest t ⩾ 1 t\geqslant 1 that a continuous, self-similar Gaussian process with self-similarity index α > 0 \alpha>0 moves at least ± c ​ t β \pm ct^{\beta} units. We prove that: (i) If β > α \beta>\alpha , then T c , β = ∞ T_{c,\beta}=\infty with positive probability; (ii) If β < α \beta<\alpha then T c , β T_{c,\beta} has moments of all order; and (iii) If β = α \beta=\alpha and X X is strongly locally nondeterministic in the sense of Pitt [ 88 ] , then there exists a continuous, strictly decreasing function λ : ( 0 , ∞ ) → ( 0 , ∞ ) \lambda:(0\,,\infty)\to(0\,,\infty) such that E ⁡ ( T c , β μ ) \mathrm{E}(T_{c,\beta}^{\mu}) is finite when 0 < μ < λ ⁡ ( c ) 0<\mu<\lambda(c) and infinite when μ > λ ⁡ ( c ) \mu>\lambda(c) . Together these results extend a celebrated theorem of Breiman [ 14 ] and Shepp [ 94 ] for passage times of a Brownian motion on the critical square-root boundary. We briefly discuss two examples: One about fractional Brownian motion, and another about a family of linear stochastic partial differential equations.

1.
Introduction

Choose and fix a number
c>0
,
let
w=\{w_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
denote the simple symmetric random walk on
\mathbb{Z}
, and define

\tau_{c}=\inf\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\,|w_{n}|>c\sqrt{n},\ c\sqrt{n}>1\}
, where

\inf\varnothing=\infty
. It is not hard to see that

\mathrm{P}\{\tau_{c}<\infty\}=1
for every
c>0
. This follows for example from the law of the iterated
logarithm (LIL). Blackwell and Freedman
[
11
]
proved that

(1.1)

\mathrm{E}(\tau_{c})<\infty\quad\text{if and only if}\quad c<1.

Moreover,
w
can be replaced for example by any random walk that
satisfies
\mathrm{E}(w_{1})=0

and
\Var(w_{1})=1
; see Gundy and Siegmund
[
47
]
.

This type of result has been studied in the continuum
as well. Let
W
denote a standard
1-D Brownian motion, and define

T_{c}=\inf\left\{t\geqslant 1:\,|W(t)|>ct^{1/2}\right\}\qquad\forall c>0.

By the LIL,
T_{c}<\infty
a.s. for every
c>0
.
Breiman
[
14
]
and Shepp
[
94
]
proved
at the same time that, for every
c,\mu>0
,

\mathrm{E}(T_{c}^{\mu})<\infty\quad\text{if and only if}\quad c<\mathcal{z}(\mu),

where
\mathcal{z}(\mu)
denotes the smallest positive root
of
x\mapsto M(-\mu\,,1/2\,,x^{2}/2)
where
M
denotes
Kummer’s function. The function
\mu\mapsto\mathcal{z}(\mu)
cannot be evaluated
explicitly but it is known to have a continuous inverse
c\mapsto\mathcal{z}^{-1}(c)
, and

(1.2)

\begin{split}\mathcal{z}(\mu)=\frac{1+\mathcal{o}(1)}{\sqrt{\mu}}\text{ as }\mu\to\infty,\quad&\mathcal{z}(\mu)=\sqrt{(2+\mathcal{o}(1))|\log\mu|}\text{ as }\mu\to 0^{+},\\
\mathcal{z}^{-1}(c)=\frac{1+\mathcal{o}(1)}{c^{2}}\text{ as }c\to 0^{+},\quad&\mathcal{z}^{-1}(c)=\exp\left(-c^{2}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}+\mathcal{o}(1)\right]\right)\text{ as }c\to\infty.\end{split}

Moreover, some special evaluations of
\mathcal{z}
are also known exactly

[
14
,
94
]
; for instance,

(1.3)

\textstyle\mathcal{z}(1)=1\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{z}(2)=\sqrt{3-\sqrt{6}}.

Novikov
[
82
]
presents
exact formulas for closely related passage times. And
Breiman (
loc. cit.
) also derived an invariance principle for a large
class of random walks in the domain of attraction of
W
that include
the simple walk. In particular, Breiman’s theory implies that

\mathrm{E}(\tau_{c}^{\mu})<\infty
if and only if
c<\mathcal{z}(\mu).

Breiman’s theorem includes (
1.1
) thanks to (
1.3
). It also includes
the fact that

if and only if

established originally by Chow and Teicher
[

.
See also Teicher and Zhang
[

]

Alili and Patie
[

discuss some of the latest developments
of passage times of Brownian motion on curved boundaries.

The following summarizes what we currently know about the passage times of
self-similar, continuous Gaussian processes on curved boundaries of
the form
ct^{\beta}
, and can be viewed as a nontrivial generalization
of the results of Breiman
[
14
]
and Shepp
[
94
]
, valid
in the absence of both the theories of Markov processes and martingales.

Consider the following stopping times of the Gaussian process
X
:

T_{c,\beta}=\inf\{t\geqslant 1:\ |X(t)|>ct^{\beta}\}\qquad\forall c,\beta>0.

The following is the main result of this paper. The super- and the sub-critical
cases of the next result [parts (1) and (2)] are related
to recent testing methodologies that are proposed in
clinical trials
[
59
,
60
,
106
]
when

X
denotes a fractional Brownian motion.

Theorem 1.1
.

(1)

If
\beta>\alpha
, then

0<\mathrm{P}\{T_{c,\beta}=\infty\}<1

for all
c>0
;

(2)

If
0<\beta<\alpha
, then

(1.4)

\mathrm{E}\left({\rm e}^{A|\log T_{c,\beta}|^{2}}\right)<\infty,

for all
c>0
and all
sufficiently small
A=A(c\,,\beta)>0
. Consequently,

\mathrm{E}(T_{c,\beta}^{\mu})<\infty
for all
c,\mu>0
in this case;

(3)

For every
c>0

there exists a real number
\lambda(c)=\lambda(c\,,X)\geqslant 0
such that

\mathrm{P}\{T_{c,\alpha}>t\}=t^{-\lambda(c)+o(1)}\quad\text{as $t\to\infty$}.

In particular,

\mathrm{E}(T_{c,\alpha}^{\mu})<\infty
if
0<\mu<\lambda(c)
, and

\mathrm{E}(T_{c,\alpha}^{\mu})=\infty
if
\mu>\lambda(c)
.
Furthermore,
\lambda
is non increasing, convex, and
continuous on
(0\,,\infty)
, and
\lambda(c)\to 0
as
c\to\infty
; and

(4)

If, in addition,
X
satisfies (
1
) then
\lambda(c)>0
for all
c>0
,

\lambda(c)\to\infty
as
c\to 0^{+}
, and
\lambda
is strictly decreasing on
(0\,,\infty)
.

We might refer to the function
\lambda
as the
critical boundary crossing exponent

of
X
, or “exponent” for short.

In the special case that
X
denotes Brownian motion [
\alpha=1/2
],

\lambda(c)=\mathcal{z}^{-1}(c)
for every
c>0
.
We do not know if
\lambda=\mathcal{z}^{-1}
for other
\frac{1}{2}
-self similar
Gaussian processes of the type studied here, and can present only partial results in this direction
in §
3.3
.
But we are able to offer the following which shows the genericity of
aspects of (
1.2
).
But first let us recall that “
f(c)\lesssim g(c)
as
c\to d
”
and “
f(c)\gtrsim g(c)
as
c\to d
” respectively mean
that “
f(c)=\mathcal{O}(g(c))
as
c\to d
” and “
g(c)=\mathcal{O}(f(c))

as
c\to d
.”

Note that the following is new even for
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
since
the Brownian motion is not the only centered,
\frac{1}{2}
-self similar, continuous
Gaussian process.

Corollary 1.2
.

It is always the case that

(1.5)

\lambda(c)\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{c^{2}+\mathcal{o}(1)}{2\Var[X(1)]}\right)\qquad\text{as $c\to\infty$}.

Furthermore:

(1)

If there exist
K,\delta>0
such that

\mathrm{E}(|X(t)-X(s)|^{2})\leqslant K|t-s|^{2\delta}
for all
s,t\in[0\,,1]
, then

(1.6)

\lambda(c)\lesssim c^{-1/\delta}\qquad\text{as $c\to 0^{+}$;}

(2)

If
X
satisfies (
1
), then

\lambda(c)\gtrsim c^{-1/\alpha}
as
c\to 0^{+}
and

(1.7)

\lambda(c)\geqslant\exp\left(-\frac{c^{2}+\mathcal{o}(1)}{2{l}_{{}_{X}}}\right)\quad\text{as $c\to\infty$}.

Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.2

will be proved in §
2
below, followed by some comments
on aspects of the theory that might be needed to estimate
the exponent
\lambda
by simulation.

2.
Proof of Theorem

and Corollary

We verify Theorem
1.1
in order,
first in the supercritical case [
\beta>\alpha
]. Then, we
prove the remainder of theorem first
in the subcritical case [
\beta<\alpha
], followed by
the critical case
[\beta=\alpha]
, both under
the condition (
1
). Corollary
1.2

is then proved at the end of this section.

2.1.
The supercritical case

We begin by proving part (1) of Theorem
1.1
. Namely,

Proposition 2.1
.

\mathrm{P}\{T_{c,\beta}=\infty\}\in(0\,,1)
for all
c>0
and
\beta>\alpha
.

The proof hinges on two simple lemmas, the first of which is

Lemma 2.2
.

\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathrm{P}\left\{|X(s)|\leqslant cs^{\beta}\ \forall s\geqslant t\right\}=1

for every
c>0
and
\beta>\alpha
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that, with probability one,

(2.1)

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{|X(t)|}{t^{\alpha}\sqrt{\log\log t}}\leqslant\sqrt{2\Var[X(1)]}.

There is a large literature on the law of the iterated logarithm for
Gaussian processes that includes many variations and improvements
of
2.1
. For the sake of completeness,
we present a short modern proof that
does not require any additional technical assumptions.

Let
S(t)=\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|X(s)|
for all
t>0
.
According to Talagrand’s regularity theorem
[
97
]
, the continuity of
X

ensures that
M=\mathrm{E}|S(1)|<\infty
, whence

\mathrm{E}|S(t)|=Mt^{\alpha}
for all
t>0
by scaling.
Thus, it follows from concentration of measure – more precisely
Borell’s inequality
[
13
]
– that, for every

t,z>0
,

\mathrm{P}\{|S(t)|\geqslant Mt^{\alpha}+z\}\leqslant 2\exp\left(-\frac{z^{2}}{2\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\Var[X(s)]}\right)=2\exp\left(-\frac{z^{2}}{2t^{2\alpha}\Var[X(1)]}\right),

thanks to scaling. This proves that for every fixed
c>0
,

\mathrm{P}\left\{|S(t)|\geqslant t^{\alpha}\sqrt{c\log\log t}\right\}\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{(c+\mathcal{o}(1))\log\log t}{2\Var[X(1)]}\right)\qquad\text{as $t\to\infty$}.

We apply this for an arbitrary choice of
c>2\Var[X(1)]
to deduce
(
2.1
) from a standard application of the Borel-Cantelli lemma.
∎

The following is the remaining lemma we need for the proof of Proposition

2.1
.

Lemma 2.3
.

\mathrm{P}\{|X(s)|\leqslant c\ \forall s\in[a\,,b]\}>0
for every
c>0
and
b>a>0
.

Proof.

Choose and fix
a,b,c>0
as in the statement of the lemma. Evidently,

\Delta:=\inf_{s\in[a,b]}\mathrm{P}\{|X(s)|\leqslant c\}=\inf_{s\in[a,b]}\mathrm{P}\{|Z|\leqslant c/(\tau s^{\alpha})\}\geqslant\mathrm{P}\{|Z|\leqslant c/(\tau b^{\alpha})\}>0,

where
\tau^{2}=\Var[X(1)]
,
and
Z
has a standard normal distribution. Next, we use continuity
in order to ensure the existence of a number
n\in\mathbb{N}

such that

\textstyle{E}:=\mathrm{P}\left\{\sup_{s,t\in[a,b]:\ |t-s|\leqslant 1/n}|X(t)-X(s)|\geqslant c/2\right\}\leqslant\Delta/2.

Define
t_{i}=a+i(b-a)n^{-1}
for all
i=0,\ldots,n
, and observe that

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathrm{P}\left\{|X(t_{i})|\leqslant c/2\right\}\leqslant\mathrm{P}\left\{|X(s)|\leqslant c\ \forall s\in[t_{i}\,,t_{i+1}]\right\}+{E}

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathrm{P}\left\{|X(s)|\leqslant c\ \forall s\in[t_{i}\,,t_{i+1}]\right\}+\frac{\Delta}{2},

for all
i=1,\ldots,n-1
, whence

\min_{0\leqslant i\leqslant n-1}\mathrm{P}\left\{|X(s)|\leqslant c\ \forall s\in[t_{i}\,,t_{i+1}]\right\}\geqslant\Delta/2.

Consequently, the Gaussian correlation inequality
[
92
,
64
]
yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\left\{|X(s)|\leqslant c\ \forall s\in[a\,,b]\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{P}\left\{\sup_{s\in[t_{i},t_{i+1}]}|X(s)|\leqslant c\ \forall i=0,\ldots,n-1\right\}

\displaystyle\geqslant\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathrm{P}\left\{|X(s)|\leqslant c\ \forall s\in[t_{i}\,,t_{i+1}]\right\}\geqslant(\Delta/2)^{n}>0.

This completes the proof.
∎

We are ready to demonstrate Proposition
2.1
, thereby
conclude the section.

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

In accord with the Gaussian correlation inequality

[
92
,
64
]
, for every
\beta>\alpha
,
m\geqslant 1
,
and
c>0
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\{T_{c,\beta}=\infty\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{P}\left\{|X(s)|\leqslant cs^{\beta}\ \forall s\geqslant 1\right\}

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathrm{P}\left\{|X(s)|\leqslant cs^{\beta}\ \forall s\in[1\,,m]\right\}\cdot\mathrm{P}\left\{|X(s)|\leqslant cs^{\beta}\ \forall s\geqslant m\right\}

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathrm{P}\left\{|X(s)|\leqslant c\ \forall s\in[1\,,m]\right\}\cdot\mathrm{P}\left\{|X(s)|\leqslant cs^{\beta}\ \forall s\geqslant m\right\}.

Choose and fix a sufficiently large
m\gg 1
in order to deduce
from Lemma
2.2
that

\mathrm{P}\{|X(s)|\leqslant cs^{\beta}\ \forall s\geqslant m\}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}
.
These observations and Lemma
2.3
together prove that

\mathrm{P}\{T_{c,\beta}=\infty\}>0
. It therefore remains to prove
that
\mathrm{P}\{T_{c,\beta}=\infty\}<1
, but that follows easily
from the fact that

\mathrm{P}\{T_{c,\beta}<\infty\}\geqslant\mathrm{P}\{|X(2)|>c2^{\beta}\}
is strictly
positive since
X(2)
has a centered normal distribution.
This concludes the proof.
∎

2.2.
The subcritical case

The following implies part (2) of Theorem
1.1
.

Proposition 2.4
.

If
c,\mu>0
and
\beta\in(0\,,\alpha)
,
then (
1.4
) holds for every

0<A<(\alpha-\beta)/(4\beta^{2}).

Proof.

We aim to prove that for all
c>0
and
\beta\in(0\,,\alpha)
,

(2.2)

\mathrm{P}\{T_{c,\beta}>t\}\leqslant\exp\left\{-\left(\frac{\alpha-\beta}{4\beta^{2}}\right)(\log t)^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\log t)\right\}\qquad\text{as $t\to\infty$}.

In particular, this would imply that

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\left\{\exp(A|\log_{+}T_{c,\beta}|^{2})>n\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{P}\left\{T_{c,\beta}>\exp\left[\sqrt{A^{-1}\log n}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant n^{-(\alpha-\beta+\mathcal{o}(1))/(4A\beta^{2})}\qquad\text{as $n\to\infty$
in $\mathbb{N}$},

which is summable provided that
A<(\alpha-\beta)/(4\beta^{2})
.
It remains to prove (
2.2
).

Let
Y(t)={\rm e}^{-t\alpha}X({\rm e}^{t})
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Let
\mathcal{X}(t)

denote the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
X(s)
for
s\in[0\,,{\rm e}^{t}]
.
Then, the process
Y
is adapted to the filtration

\{\mathcal{X}({\rm e}^{t})\}_{t\geqslant 0}
, and for every integer
j\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\left\{|X({\rm e}^{j})|\leqslant c\,{\rm e}^{j\beta}\mid\mathcal{X}({\rm e}^{j-1})\right\}=\mathrm{P}\{|Y(j)|\leqslant c\,{\rm e}^{-(\alpha-\beta)j}\mid\mathcal{X}({\rm e}^{j-1})\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\Var[Y(j)\mid\mathcal{X}({\rm e}^{j-1})]}}\int_{-c\exp\{-(\alpha-\beta)j\}}^{c\exp\{-(\alpha-\beta)j\}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left|x-\mathrm{E}[Y(j)\mid\mathcal{X}({\rm e}^{j-1})]\right|^{2}}{\Var[Y(j)\mid\mathcal{X}({\rm e}^{j-1})]}\right){\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2c\exp\{-(\alpha-\beta)j\}}{\sqrt{2\pi\Var[Y(j)\mid\mathcal{X}({\rm e}^{j-1})]}}.

Thanks to (
1
),

\displaystyle\Var[Y(j)\mid\mathcal{X}({\rm e}^{j-1})]

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{-2j\alpha}\Var[X({\rm e}^{j})\mid\mathcal{X}({\rm e}^{j-1})]

\displaystyle\geqslant{l}_{{}_{X}}{\rm e}^{-2j\alpha}\left({\rm e}^{j}-{\rm e}^{j-1}\right)^{2\alpha}={l}_{{}_{X}}(1-{\rm e}^{-1})^{2\alpha}.

Therefore, for every integer
j\geqslant 1
,

\mathrm{P}\left\{|X({\rm e}^{j})|\leqslant c\,{\rm e}^{j\beta}\mid\mathcal{X}({\rm e}^{j-1})\right\}\leqslant\frac{\sqrt{2/({l}_{{}_{X}}\pi)}}{(1-{\rm e}^{-1})^{\alpha}}\,{\rm e}^{-(\alpha-\beta)j},

almost surely. We now apply successive conditioning in order
to find that

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\{T_{c,\beta}>{\rm e}^{m\beta}\}\leqslant\mathrm{P}\left\{|X({\rm e}^{j})|\leqslant c\,{\rm e}^{j\beta}\ \forall 1\leqslant j\leqslant m\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2/({l}_{{}_{X}}\pi)}}{(1-{\rm e}^{-1})^{\alpha}}\,{\rm e}^{-(\alpha-\beta)j}\right)=\left(\frac{\sqrt{2/({l}_{{}_{X}}\pi)}}{(1-{\rm e}^{-1})^{\alpha}}\right)^{m}{\rm e}^{-(\alpha-\beta)m(m+1)/2}.

Set
K=2\log_{+}(\sqrt{2/({l}_{{}_{X}}\pi)}(1-{\rm e}^{-1})^{-\alpha}).

If
n\geqslant{\rm e}^{\beta}
is an integer then there exists a unique integer
m\geqslant 1
such that

{\rm e}^{m\beta}\leqslant n\leqslant{\rm e}^{(m+1)\beta},

whence

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\{T_{c,\beta}>n\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathrm{P}\{T_{c,\beta}>{\rm e}^{m\beta}\}\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{(\alpha-\beta)m(m+1)-Km}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{\frac{1}{2}(\alpha-\beta)(\log n)^{2}}{2\beta^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(\log n)\right),\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$}.

This yields (
2.2
) as
t\to\infty
along integers.
Since
\log t\sim\log\lfloor t\rfloor
as
t\to\infty
,
(
2.2
) holds as
t\to\infty
along reals as well.
This completes the proof.
∎

2.3.
The critical case

We have already proved parts (1) and (2) of Theorem

1.1
. The remaining assertions of Theorem
1.1

are included in the following.

Proposition 2.5
.

Even without condition (
1
),

\lambda(c)=-\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log t}\log\mathrm{P}\left\{{\textstyle\sup_{s\in[b,t]}\left(|X(s)|/s^{\alpha}\right)}\leqslant c\right\}

exists for every
b,c>0
, is finite, and does not depend on
b
.
Moreover,
\lambda(c)
satisfies
\lim_{c\to\infty}\lambda(c)=0
, and
\lambda
is a
non increasing, convex, continuous function on
(0\,,\infty)
.
If, in addition,
X
satisfies (
1
), then
\lambda(c)>0

for all
c>0
,
\lim_{c\downarrow 0}\lambda(c)=\infty
, and
\lambda
is strictly decreasing.

Proof.

First of all we may note that
X(t)\to 0
in probability as
t\downarrow 0

because of scaling. Therefore the continuity of
X
ensures that
X(0)=0
.
Now we follow Lamperti
[
61
]

and define a process
Y=\{Y(t)\}_{t\in[-\infty,\infty)}
as follows:

(2.3)

Y(-\infty)=0\quad\text{and}\quad Y(t)={\rm e}^{-t\alpha}X({\rm e}^{t})\qquad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}.

The process
Y
is the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process associated to
X
,
and made a brief appearance earlier in the proof of
Proposition
2.4
.
A direct computation yields

\mathrm{E}[Y(t)Y(s)]={\rm e}^{-\alpha|t-s|}\mathrm{E}[X({\rm e}^{|t-s|})X(1)]\qquad\forall s,t\in\mathbb{R}.

Therefore, it follows that
\{Y(r)\}_{r\in(-\infty,\infty)}
is a stationary,
time-reversible Gaussian process, and that
Y
has continuous sample functions
on
[-\infty\,,\infty)
. Now,

\displaystyle\textstyle\mathrm{P}\left\{\sup_{s\in[b,t]}\left(|X(s)|/s^{\alpha}\right)\leqslant c\right\}

\displaystyle\textstyle=\mathrm{P}\left\{\sup_{r\in[\log b,\log t]}|Y(r)|\leqslant c\right\}

\displaystyle\textstyle=\mathrm{P}\left\{\sup_{r\in[0,\log(t/b)]}|Y(r)|\leqslant c\right\},

for all
t>b>0
and
c>0
.
Choose and fix a number
c>0
and define

\textstyle f(t)=\mathrm{P}\left\{|Y(r)|\leqslant c\quad\forall r\in[0\,,t]\right\}\qquad[t\geqslant 0].

Thanks to the Gaussian correlation inequality
[
92
,
64
]
,
the function
f
satisfies the following
for all
s,t>0
:

\displaystyle\textstyle f(t+s)

\displaystyle\textstyle=\mathrm{P}\left\{\sup_{r\in[0,t]}|Y(r)|\leqslant c\,,\sup_{r\in[t,t+s]}|Y(r)|\leqslant c\right\}

\displaystyle\textstyle\geqslant\mathrm{P}\left\{\sup_{r\in[0,t]}|Y(r)|\leqslant c\right\}\mathrm{P}\left\{\sup_{r\in[t,t+s]}|Y(r)|\leqslant c\right\}

\displaystyle\textstyle=\mathrm{P}\left\{\sup_{r\in[0,t]}|Y(r)|\leqslant c\right\}\mathrm{P}\left\{\sup_{r\in[0,s]}|Y(r)|\leqslant c\right\}=f(t)f(s),

where we have used stationarity in the last line.
That is,
f
is supermultiplicative.

Next, let us suppose that

f(t)=0
for some
t>0
. Because
f
is supermultiplicative,
f(t)\geqslant[f(t/2)]^{2}

for all
t>0
, and hence
f(t/2)=0
. Repeat this inductively to see that

f(t/2^{n})=0
for every integer
n\geqslant 0
. It follows from the continuity of
Y
that

0=\lim_{n\to\infty}f(t/2^{n})=\mathrm{P}\{|Y(0)|\leqslant c\}.

This cannot be since
Y(0)
has a non-degenerate centered normal
distribution. Therefore, it follows that
f(t)>0
for all
t>0
. That and the supermultiplicative
property of
f
together show that
-\log f
is subadditive and hence

\lambda(c)=-\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}\log f(t)=-\sup_{t\geqslant 1}t^{-1}\log f(t),

thanks to the Fekete lemma, generally ascribed to
[
36
]
. We can also see that

\lambda(c)\leqslant-\log f(1)<\infty
. Moreover,

(2.4)

\textstyle\lambda(c)\leqslant-\log\mathrm{P}\left\{\sup_{r\in[0,1]}|Y(r)|\leqslant c\right\}\downarrow 0,

as
c\uparrow\infty
, since
Y
is continuous hence locally bounded.

By the log-concavity of Gaussian measures
(see Borell
[
12
, Corollary 2.1]
), for every
s\in(0\,,1)
,
a,b>0
,
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\textstyle\log\mathrm{P}\left\{\sup_{r\in[0,n]}|Y(r)|\leqslant sa+(1-s)b\right\}

\displaystyle\hskip 36.135pt\textstyle\geqslant s\log\mathrm{P}\left\{\sup_{r\in[0,n]}|Y(r)|\leqslant a\right\}+(1-s)\log\mathrm{P}\left\{\sup_{r\in[0,n]}|Y(r)|\leqslant b\right\}.

Divide both sides by
-n
and then let
n\to\infty
in order to see that

\lambda(sa+(1-s)b)\leqslant s\lambda(a)+(1-s)\lambda(b).

In other words,
\lambda
is convex,
whence continuous, on
(0\,,\infty)
. The asserted (weak) monotonicity of
\lambda
is
manifest.

Finally, suppose that
X
satisfies (
1
). Recall the stationary process
Y

in (
2.3
), and define
\mathcal{Y}(t)
to be the
\sigma
-algebra generated
by
\{Y(s)\}_{s\in(-\infty,t]}
. Thanks to (
1
),
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,

(2.5)

\begin{split}&\Var[Y(n)\mid\mathcal{Y}(n-1)]={\rm e}^{-2n\alpha}\Var\left[X({\rm e}^{n})\mid\mathcal{X}({\rm e}^{n-1})\right]\\
&\hskip 93.95122pt\geqslant{l}_{{}_{X}}{\rm e}^{-2n\alpha}({\rm e}^{n}-{\rm e}^{n-1})^{2\alpha}={l}_{{}_{X}}(1-{\rm e}^{-1})^{2\alpha},\end{split}

where first identity hinges on the already-proved fact that
X(0)=0
.
Since conditionally Gaussian laws are themselves Gaussian,

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\left\{|Y(n)|\leqslant c\mid\mathcal{Y}(n-1)\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\Var[Y(n)\mid\mathcal{Y}(n-1)]}}\int_{-c}^{c}\exp\left(-\frac{|x-\mathrm{E}(Y(n)\mid\mathcal{Y}(n-1))|^{2}}{2\Var[Y(n)\mid\mathcal{Y}(n-1)]}\right){\rm d}x.

One-dimensional Gaussian laws are unimodal, as can be checked
directly, or deduced from the much more general
Anderson’s shifted-ball inequality
[
2
]
.
This implies that

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\left\{|Y(n)|\leqslant c\mid\mathcal{Y}(n-1)\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\Var[Y(n)\mid\mathcal{Y}(n-1)]}}\int_{-c}^{c}\exp\left(-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2\Var[Y(n)\mid\mathcal{Y}(n-1)]}\right){\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\mathrm{P}\left\{|Z|\leqslant\frac{c}{\sqrt{\Var[Y(n)\mid\mathcal{Y}(n-1)]}}\right\}\leqslant\mathrm{P}\left\{|Z|\leqslant\frac{c}{\sqrt{{l}_{{}_{X}}}{(1-{\rm e}^{-1})}^{\alpha}}\right\},

where
Z
denotes a standard normal random variable, and the
last inequality is obtained by applying (
2.5
).
In particular,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\textstyle\mathrm{P}\left\{\sup_{s\in[1,\exp(n)]}\left(|X(s)|/s^{\alpha}\right)\leqslant c\right\}=\mathrm{P}\left\{\sup_{r\in[0,n]}|Y(r)|\leqslant c\right\}\\
&\hskip 57.81621pt\leqslant\mathrm{P}\left\{\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}|Y(i)|\leqslant c\right\}\leqslant\mathrm{P}\left\{|Z|\leqslant\frac{c}{\sqrt{{l}_{{}_{X}}}{(1-{\rm e}^{-1})}^{\alpha}}\right\}^{n},\end{split}

thanks to successive conditioning.
Take the logarithm of both sides, divide both
sides by
-n
, and let
n\to\infty
in order to see that

(2.7)

\lambda(c)\geqslant-\log\mathrm{P}\left\{|Z|\leqslant\frac{c}{\sqrt{{l}_{{}_{X}}}{(1-{\rm e}^{-1})}^{\alpha}}\right\},

which is
>0
for all
c>0

and tends to infinity when
c\downarrow 0
.

Since
\lambda
is strictly
positive and tends to zero, its convexity readily also implies its strict
decrease under condition (
1
). In fact, if
\lambda(a)=\lambda(b)
for some
b>a>0
,
then the convexity and monotonicity of
\lambda

on
(a\,,\infty)
together imply that
\lambda
is constant on

[b\,,\infty)
and since
\lim_{c\to\infty}\lambda(c)=0
, this constant
must be 0 and thus
\lambda(b)=0
. We proved in (
2.7
) that
(
1
) implies that
\lambda>0

everywhere. Therefore, we are led to a contradiction.
This implies the strict decrease of
\lambda
under (
1
),
and completes the proof of Proposition
2.5
.
∎

2.4.
Proof of Corollary
1.2

First we establish the asserted behavior of
\lambda

near infinity. Let
Y
be the process that was defined in (
2.3
).

Accoding to Fernique’s theorem
[
37
]
, the continuity of
Y
ensures
that
C=\mathrm{E}\sup_{r\in[0,1]}|Y(r)|
is finite. Therefore,
Borell’s inequality
[
13
]
, and the fact that
\Var[Y(r)]=\Var[X(1)]

for all
r>0
, together imply that

\mathrm{P}\left\{\textstyle\sup_{r\in[0,1]}|Y(r)|\leqslant c\right\}\geqslant 1-2\exp\left(-\frac{c^{2}+\mathcal{o}(1)}{2\Var[X(1)]}\right)\qquad\text{as $c\to\infty$}.

This and (
2.4
) together imply (
1.5
).

In order to establish (
1.6
), we may assume
that
\mathrm{E}(|X(t)-X(s)|^{2})\leqslant K|t-s|^{2\delta}
for all
s,t\geqslant 0
.

Now, for every
1\geqslant t\geqslant s\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}(|Y(t)-Y(s)|^{2})=\mathrm{E}\left(\left|\frac{X({\rm e}^{t})}{{\rm e}^{\alpha t}}-\frac{X({\rm e}^{s})}{{\rm e}^{\alpha s}}\right|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\textstyle\leqslant 2{\rm e}^{-2\alpha t}\mathrm{E}\left(\left|X({\rm e}^{t})-X({\rm e}^{s})\right|^{2}\right)+2\left(1-{\rm e}^{-\alpha(t-s)}\right)^{2}\mathrm{E}(|Y(s)|^{2})

\displaystyle\textstyle\leqslant 2K{\rm e}^{2(\delta-\alpha)t}\left(1-{\rm e}^{-(t-s)}\right)^{2\delta}+2\alpha^{2}(t-s)^{2}\mathrm{E}(|Y(0)|^{2})\leqslant L|t-s|^{2\delta},

where
L=L(K\,,\alpha\,,\delta)>0
. We have used the fact that,
thanks to the hypothesis of this part and scaling,

\mathrm{E}(|X(t)-X(s)|^{2})\leqslant K|t-s|^{2\delta}
for all
s,t\in[0\,,{\rm e}]
.
Since
X
– hence
Y
– is assumed to be non degenerate, this forces
\delta
to be
in
(0\,,1]
. With this observation in mind, we may define
a distance
d
on
\mathbb{R}
by setting

d(s\,,t)=L|t-s|^{\delta}\qquad\forall s,t\in\mathbb{R}.

Let
N
denote the metric entropy of the metric space

([0\,,1]\,,d)
. That is,
for every
\varepsilon\in(0\,,1)
, let
N(\varepsilon)
denote the
minimum number of
d
-balls of radius
\varepsilon
needed
to cover
[0\,,1]
. The form of the distance function
d

readily implies that
N(\varepsilon)\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1/\delta}

uniformly for all
\varepsilon\in(0\,,1)
. Therefore, we may apply the Gaussian correlation inequality
[
92
,
64
]
and Lemma 2.2 of
Talagrand
[
98
]
– see also Dalang, Lee, Mueller, and Xiao
[
24
, Lemma 3.4]

for a more precise statement – in order to see that there exists
M=M(K\,,\alpha\,,\delta)>0

such that for all
c\in(0\,,1)
,

\displaystyle\textstyle\mathrm{P}\left\{\sup_{s\in[0,1]}|Y(s)|\leqslant c\right\}

\displaystyle\textstyle\geqslant\mathrm{P}\left\{|Y(0)|\leqslant c/2\right\}\cdot\mathrm{P}\left\{\sup_{t,s\in[0,1]}|Y(t)-Y(s)|\leqslant c/2\right\}

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathrm{P}\left\{|Y(0)|\leqslant c/2\right\}\cdot M^{-1}\exp(-M/c^{1/\delta}).

Since
\Var[Y(0)]=\Var[X(1)]>0
, the preceding together with (
2.4
) implies that
\lambda(c)\leqslant Mc^{-1/\delta}(1+\mathcal{o}(1))
as
c\to 0^{+}
. This proves (
1.6
).

Next, we observe that, if
Z
follows a standard normal distribution, then

\mathrm{P}\left\{\textstyle|Z|\leqslant c\,{l}_{{}_{X}}^{-1/2}\right\}\geqslant 1-2{\rm e}^{-c^{2}/(2{l}_{{}_{X}})}\qquad\forall c>0.

Thus, we can deduce (
1.7
)
since (
2.7
) is valid when
X
satisfies (
1
).

It remains to show the lower bound for
\lambda(c)
as
c\to 0^{+}
.
For each
c\in(0\,,1)
, similarly to the way (
2.6
) was proved, we may take
m=\lfloor c^{-1/\alpha}n\rfloor
and deduce that

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\left\{\sup_{s\in[1,\exp(n)]}\left(|X(s)|/s^{\alpha}\right)\leqslant c\right\}=\mathrm{P}\left\{\sup_{r\in[0,n]}|Y(r)|\leqslant c\right\}

\displaystyle\hskip 43.36243pt\leqslant\mathrm{P}\left\{\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m}|Y(c^{1/\alpha}i)|\leqslant c\right\}\leqslant\mathrm{P}\left\{|Z|\leqslant\frac{c}{\sqrt{{l}_{{}_{X}}}(1-\exp(-c^{1/\alpha}))^{\alpha}}\right\}^{m}.

Then, take the logarithm of both sides, divide both
sides by
-n
, and let
n\to\infty
to see that

\displaystyle\lambda(c)\geqslant-c^{-1/\alpha}\log\mathrm{P}\left\{|Z|\leqslant\frac{c}{\sqrt{{l}_{{}_{X}}}(1-\exp(-c^{1/\alpha}))^{\alpha}}\right\}.

Since
c(1-\exp(-c^{1/\alpha}))^{-\alpha}\to 1
as
c\to 0^{+}
,
it follows that

\lambda(c)\geqslant c^{-1/\alpha}\left[-\log\mathrm{P}\big\{|Z|\leqslant{l}_{{}_{X}}^{-1/2}\big\}+\mathcal{o}(1)\right]\qquad\text{as $c\to 0^{+}$},

which completes the proof of Corollary
1.2
.∎

3.
Examples

In this section we present a few families of self-similar Gaussian processes
to which all of the assertions of Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.2

apply. In the next section we discuss a number of relations between the two examples
of this section.

3.1.
Fractional Brownian motion

Let us choose and fix a number

H\in(0\,,1)
and let
B=\{B(t)\}_{t\geqslant 0}
denote a fractional Brownian motion
(fBm, for short) with index

H
; that is,
B
is a centered Gaussian process with

(3.1)

\Cov[B(t)\,,B(s)]=\tfrac{1}{2}\left[t^{2H}+s^{2H}-|t-s|^{2H}\right]\qquad\forall s,t\geqslant 0.

As an immediate consequence of (
3.1
) we can see

\text{$B$ is $H$-self similar, and }\mathrm{E}(|X(t)-X(s)|^{2})=|t-s|^{2H}\qquad\forall s,t\geqslant 0.

Moreover, Pitt
[
88
]
has proved that

fBm satisfies (
1
) with
\alpha=H
.

As such, all of the findings
of Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.2
apply to
B
.

One might think of
B(t)
as the position of a one-dimensional particle
at time
t
when the mean-squared displacement is

\mathrm{E}(|B(t)|^{2})^{1/2}=|t|^{H}
for
every
t\geqslant 0
.
When
H=\frac{1}{2}
the process
B
is a classical model for diffusion,
and it can serve as a model for 1-D anomalous diffusion when
H\neq\frac{1}{2}

[
71
]
. A large body of applied work
defines fBm as the solution to the Langevin equation
{\rm d}B(t)=\dot{\eta}(t)\,{\rm d}t
where
\dot{\eta}

is a centered Gaussian noise with

\Cov[\dot{\eta}(t)\,,\dot{\eta}(s)]=\text{const}\cdot|t-s|^{-H-2}.

Of course this type of exposition has to be interpreted
loosely since
t\mapsto|t|^{-H-2}
is not locally integrable
and hence cannot be a covariance functional.
But certainly
B
has continuous sample functions
[
56
,
72
]
and so

\dot{\eta}={\rm d}B(t)/{\rm d}t
is a well-defined generalized
Gaussian random field
[
34
,
77
,
35
,
45
]
.
Lysy et al
[
69
]

present a rigorous comparative analysis of fBm and other Gaussian models of
anomalous diffusion in biological fluids, with additional pointers to the
physics and biology literature on this topic; see also Verdier et al
[
100
]

and their extensive bibliography.

3.2.
Linear SPDEs

Mueller and Wu
[
80
]
describe a connection between the
increments of a fBm of index
H
(the “pinned string”) and a family of
stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs, for short).
Their work builds on earlier linearization ideas
[
67
,
103
,
54
,
48
,
38
]

in the SPDE literature.
Here, we present an extension, as well as a refinement, of these results.

Throughout this discussion we choose and fix some
d\in\mathbb{N}
and,
for every real number
\gamma>0
, let

\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}=-(-\Delta)^{\gamma/2}

denote the fractional Laplace operator
of index
\gamma/2
, acting on real-valued functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Recall
that
\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}
is a pseudo-differential operator
from symbolic calculus, defined via
its Fourier multiplier
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\gamma}
as

\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\gamma}(\xi)=-\|\xi\|^{\gamma}\qquad\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

It follows that the fundamental solution to the
corresponding heat operator
\partial_{t}-\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}

is of the form
G(s\,,y\,;t\,,x)=G(t-s\,,x-y)
,
where

(3.2)

\hat{G}(t\,,\xi)=\exp(-t\|\xi\|^{\gamma})\qquad\forall t>0,\ \xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

and
\hat{\phantom{a}}
denotes the Fourier transform in the spatial [
x
] variable.

Let us collect some elementary facts about the heat kernel
G
. The following
result is a well-known folklore fact, but we were not able to find a complete proof of
these particular statements explicitly, particularly that of part (5),
and so we will supply the short proof
for the sake of completeness. As is customary, let
\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})

denote the collection of all infinitely differentiable functions on
\mathbb{R}

that vanish at infinity.

Lemma 3.1
.

(1)

G(t)\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
t>0
;

(2)

If
0<\gamma\leqslant 2
then
G(t)>0
is a probability density function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for all
t>0
;

(3)

G(t)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(t\,,x)\,{\rm d}x=1
for all
t>0
;

(4)

G(t+s)=G(t)*G(s)
for all
t,s>0
;

(5)

If
\gamma>2
, then
G(t)
is signed for all
t>0
.

Proof.

First of all,
G(t)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
t>0
since

\hat{G}(t)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
[Plancherel’s theorem].
Now, for every multi-index
{\bf a}\in\{1\,,\ldots\,,d\}
,
(
3.2
) and the inversion formula for Fourier transforms together yield

(3.3)

\partial^{{\bf a}}_{x}G(t\,,x)=\frac{(-i)^{|{\bf a}|}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\rm e}^{-ix\cdot\xi-t\|\xi\|^{\gamma}}\textstyle\prod_{j\in{\bf a}}\xi_{j}\,{\rm d}\xi

as a version of the
|{\bf a}|
-th spatial derivative of
G
, where
\partial^{0}_{x}G=G
.
Since the preceding integral converges absolutely, the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma implies part (1). Part (2)
is basically saying that when
G
denotes the transition probabilities of an isotopic

\gamma
-stable process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, itself a consequence of the Lévy-Khintchine
formula
[
7
,
58
,
93
]
, and the strict positivity of

G(t)
is a well-known consequence of subordination
[
93
]
.
This reduces the proof of (3)
to the case that
\gamma>2
. With that case in mind,
consider the differential operator,

\textstyle D=\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}\partial^{d+1}_{\xi_{j}}\qquad[n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}].

The inverse Fourier multiplier of
D
is given by
\check{D}(x)=i^{d+1}\|x\|^{d+1}
.
Therefore, the inversion formula yields

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x\|^{d+1}|G(t\,,x)|\lesssim\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|D\hat{G}(t\,,\xi)|\,{\rm d}\xi,

which can be seen directly to be finite since
\gamma>2
.
With this under way, and since we have seen that
G(t)
is bounded for every
t>0

[part (1)], it follows
that

|G(t\,,x)|\lesssim(1+\|x\|)^{-(d+1)},

uniformly for
all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, whence follows that
G(t)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for every
t>0
.
Because
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(t\,,x)\,{\rm d}x=\hat{G}(t\,,0)=1
, this proves (3).
We can deduce (4) from this latter
L^{1}
-inclusion
since
\hat{G}(t+s)=\hat{G}(t)\hat{G}(s)
for all
s,t>0
.

In order to prove the more interesting part (5), we first note
using (
3.3
) the following scaling property:

c^{d/\gamma}G(ct\,,c^{1/\gamma}x)=G(t\,,x)\quad\forall c,t>0,\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Consequently,
G(t)>0
[resp.
G(t)\geqslant 0
] for one
t>0
if and only if
G(t)>0
[resp.
G(t)\geqslant 0
] for all
t>0
.
Because
\hat{G}(t\,,0)=1
for all
t>0
, it follows from part (3)
that
G(t)>0
(for one hence all
t>0
)
if and only if
\{G(t)\}_{t>0}
is a convolution semigroup of probability densities on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
This is equivalent to saying that
G(t)>0
for some, hence all,
t>0

if and only if there exists a Lévy process

\{X_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\mathrm{E}\exp\{i\xi\cdot X_{t}\}=\exp(-t\|\xi\|^{\gamma})

for all
t>0
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
. Part (5)
is now an immediate consequences of the Lévy-Khintchine formula
[
93
,
7
,
58
]
.
∎

Now let us choose and fix parameters
0\leqslant\nu\leqslant 1
and
0<\beta\leqslant d
,
and consider the constant-coefficient SPDE,

(3.4)

\left[\begin{split}&\partial_{t}U(t\,,x)=\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}U(t\,,x)+\dot{W}(t\,,x)&\forall(t\,,x)\in(0\,,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
&\text{subject to }U(0\,,x)=0&\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\end{split}\right.

where
\dot{W}
is a centered generalized Gaussian noise and has covariance

\Cov[\dot{W}(t\,,x)\,,\dot{W}(s\,,y)]=\kappa_{\nu}(|t-s|)\kappa_{\beta}(\|x-y\|)\quad\forall s,t\geqslant 0,\ (x\,,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\setminus\{0\},

where

\kappa_{\beta}(z)=|z|^{-\beta}\ \forall z>0\ \text{ if }0<\beta<d,\quad\text{and }\kappa_{1}=\delta_{0}\text{ if }\beta=d=1\text{ or }\nu=1.

Since
K_{\beta}=\kappa_{\beta}(\|\,\cdot\|)\in L^{1}_{\textit{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
when
0<\beta<d
,
we can view
K_{\beta}
as a distribution, and it is known that its Fourier transform
satisfies
\hat{K}_{\beta}=cK_{d-\beta}
for an explicitly computable
number
c=c(d\,,\beta)>0

[
75
, (12.10)]
. In particular,

K_{\beta}
is positive definite in the sense of distributions, for the entire range of
\beta
,
and therefore the generalized Gaussian noise
\dot{W}
exists according
to general theory
[
34
,
77
,
35
,
45
]
.

We will frequently appeal to the fact
that, when
\beta\in(0\,,d)
,

(3.5)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\psi(x)\phi(y)}{\|x-y\|^{\beta}}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y=\frac{c(d\,,\beta)}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\hat{\psi}(\xi)\overline{\hat{\phi}(\xi)}}{\|\xi\|^{d-\beta}}\,{\rm d}\xi\qquad\forall\psi,\phi\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

This can be viewed as a non-trivial
extension of the Parseval identity from
\phi,\psi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

to the
L^{1}
-case,
and follows from Lemma 12.12 of Mattila
[
75
]
when
\psi
and
\phi
are in addition nonnegative.
Because both sides of (
3.5
) define bilinear forms [in
(\phi\,,\psi)
]
the above general case follows from that by considering various combinations
of
\psi^{\pm}
and
\phi^{\pm}
.

According to the theory (and notation) of Dalang

[
23
]
and Walsh
[
103
]
,
(
3.4
) has a random-field solution,

(3.6)

\gamma>\frac{\beta}{2-\nu}.

Proof.

(d\,,\beta\,,\gamma)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}S^{(n)}(t)f(t)\,{\rm d}t=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}W_{1}({\rm d}\xi)\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\ f(t)\partial^{n}_{t}\kappa(t\,,\xi)

\displaystyle=(t^{n}-s^{n})^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\xi\|^{\beta-d+(2n-1)\gamma}{\rm e}^{-2t\|\xi\|^{\gamma}}{\rm d}\xi,

\displaystyle Q_{2}

\displaystyle=s^{2n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\xi\|^{\beta-d+(2n-1)\gamma}\left({\rm e}^{-s\|\xi\|^{\gamma}}-{\rm e}^{-t\|\xi\|^{\gamma}}\right)^{2}\,{\rm d}\xi.

Choose and fix two arbitrary numbers
\varepsilon
and
M
that satisfy

0<\varepsilon<1<M
. Since

t^{n}-s^{n}=n^{-1}\int_{s}^{t}x^{n-1}\,{\rm d}x
,

Q_{1}\leqslant n^{-2}M^{2n-2}(t-s)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\xi\|^{\beta-d+(2n-1)\gamma}{\rm e}^{-2\varepsilon\|\xi\|^{\gamma}}{\rm d}\xi\lesssim(t-s)^{2},

uniformly for all
s,t\in[\varepsilon\,,M]
. Also,

\displaystyle Q_{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant M^{2n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\xi\|^{\beta-d+(2n-1)\gamma}\left(1-{\rm e}^{-|t-s|\|\xi\|^{\gamma}}\right)^{2}{\rm e}^{-2\varepsilon\|\xi\|^{\gamma}}{\rm d}\xi

\displaystyle\leqslant M^{2n}(t-s)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\xi\|^{\beta-d+(2n+1)\gamma}{\rm e}^{-2\varepsilon\|\xi\|^{\gamma}}{\rm d}\xi\propto(t-s)^{2},

uniformly for all
s,t\in[\varepsilon\,,M]
.
The implied constants above depend only on

(d\,,\beta\,,\gamma\,,n\,,\varepsilon\,,M)
.
Thus, we can deduce from Kolmogorov’s continuity theorem
that
S^{(n)}
is continuous on
(0\,,\infty)
.
This concludes the proof of Lemma
3.9
.
∎

Lemma 3.10
.

If
\nu=1
and either
0<\beta<d
or
\beta=d=1
, then

B(t):=\frac{X(t)+S(t)}{K_{0}}\qquad[t\geqslant 0]

is an
\fBm
with index
\frac{1}{2}(1-\beta\gamma^{-1})
,
where
K_{0}
was defined in (
3.15
).

Proof.

For every
t,h\geqslant 0
we may write

\displaystyle X(t+h)-X(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{(0,t)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[G(t+h-r\,,z)-G(t-r\,,z)\right]W({\rm d}r\,{\rm d}z)

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{[t,t+h)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(t+h-r\,,z)\,W({\rm d}r\,{\rm d}z)=:I_{1}+I_{2},

where
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
are independent since the noise
W
is white in time when
\nu=1
.
Thanks to (
3.5
) and (
3.16
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\Var(I_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{c(d\,,\beta)}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}r\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\rm d}\xi\left({\rm e}^{-(t+h-r)\|\xi\|^{\gamma}}-{\rm e}^{-(t-r)\|\xi\|^{\gamma}}\right)^{2}\|\xi\|^{\beta-d}

\displaystyle=\frac{c(d\,,\beta)}{2(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(1-{\rm e}^{-h\|\xi\|^{\gamma}}\right)^{2}\left(1-{\rm e}^{-2t\|\xi\|^{\gamma}}\right)\frac{{\rm d}\xi}{\|\xi\|^{d+\gamma-\beta}}

\displaystyle=\frac{c(d\,,\beta)}{2(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(1-{\rm e}^{-h\|\xi\|^{\gamma}}\right)^{2}\frac{{\rm d}\xi}{\|\xi\|^{d+\gamma-\beta}}-\mathrm{E}\left(|S(t+h)-S(t)|^{2}\right),

and

\displaystyle\Var(I_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{c(d\,,\beta)}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{t}^{t+h}{\rm d}r\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\rm d}\xi\,{\rm e}^{-2(t+h-r)\|\xi\|^{\gamma}}\|\xi\|^{\beta-d}

\displaystyle=\frac{c(d\,,\beta)}{2(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(1-{\rm e}^{-2h\|\xi\|^{\gamma}}\right)\frac{{\rm d}\xi}{\|\xi\|^{d+\gamma-\beta}}.

Then, we may combine these two expressions, use independence
of
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
, and apply scaling to find that

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left(|X(t+h)-X(t)|^{2}\right)=K_{0}^{2}h^{1-(\beta/\gamma)}-\mathrm{E}\left(|S(t+h)-S(t)|^{2}\right).

Lemma
3.9
implies that
B=\{B(t)\}_{t\geqslant 0}

is a continuous centered Gaussian process and the preceding
shows that
\mathrm{E}(|B(t+h)-B(t)|^{2})=h^{1-(\beta/\gamma)}
.
Therefore,
B
is a fractional Brownian motion with index

\frac{1}{2}(1-\beta\gamma^{-1})
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.7
.

Since
X
and
S
are independent centered Gaussian processes,
we may apply conditionally Anderson’s shifted-ball inequality

[
2
]
to see that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\left\{|X(t)|\leqslant c\,t^{\alpha}\ \forall t\in[1\,,n]\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{P}\left\{K_{0}^{-1}|X(t)|\leqslant K_{0}^{-1}c\,t^{\alpha}\ \forall t\in[1\,,n]\right\}

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathrm{P}\left\{K_{0}^{-1}|X(t)+S(t)|\leqslant K_{0}^{-1}c\,t^{\alpha}\ \forall t\in[1\,,n]\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{P}\left\{|B(t)|\leqslant K_{0}^{-1}c\,t^{\alpha}\ \forall t\in[1\,,n]\right\};

see Lemma
3.10
for the last line.
Apply a logarithm to both sides, divide by
\log n
, and let
n\to\infty
in order to deduce
from Proposition
2.5
that
\lambda_{X}(c)\leqslant\lambda_{B}(c/K_{0})
for all
c>0
.
∎

4.
On simulation analysis

One of our original goals was to devise relatively fast simulation methods for computing
the boundary crossing exponent
\lambda
for a self-similar Gaussian process,
such as
\fBm
or the solution to a nice linear
stochastic PDEs, that satisfies (
1
) and has good
Hölder regularity properties. At present we are only aware of
Monte-Carlo type methods. Because we are trying to estimate large-deviation
exponents, those methods tend to be computationally expensive and we could
not realistically use them on a standard personal laptop computer.
Nevertheless, we hope that good methods might exist.
In anticipation of that, we will address a theoretical matter
that we feel most, if not all, simulation analyses will need resolved. In order to
be concrete,

from now on, we assume that
X
is an
\fBm
with Hurst index
\alpha
,

though all of what follows applies a fair bit more generally.

For every
c>0
define

T^{*}_{c}=\inf\{{\rm e}^{n}:\,n\in\mathbb{N}\,,\,|X({\rm e}^{n})|>c{\rm e}^{n\alpha}\}.

One can readily adjust and reuse the very
same argument that was used to
prove Theorem
1.1
in order to prove the following
whose details are therefore omitted.

Proposition 4.1
.

For every
c>0
there exists a real number
\lambda_{*}(c)=\lambda_{*}(c\,,X)\geqslant 0
such that

\mathrm{P}\{T^{*}_{c}>{\rm e}^{n}\}={\rm e}^{-n(\lambda_{*}(c)+\mathcal{o}(1))}\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$}.

Because
T^{*}_{c}\geqslant T_{c,\alpha}
it follows that

\lambda_{*}(c)\leqslant\lambda(c)
for every
c>0
.
We do not know whether or not the inequality is
sharp, and therefore ask the following.

Open Problem 4.2
.

Is
\lambda_{*}(c)=\lambda(c)
for all
c>0
?

Fortunately, there are variations of this problem that we know how to solve.
In order to describe the details, let us choose and fix an unbounded sequence
of numbers
1\leqslant t_{1}<t_{2}<\ldots
, and consider
the stopping times

\hat{T}_{c}=\inf\{t_{n}:\,n\in\mathbb{N}\,,\,|X(t_{n})|>ct_{n}^{\alpha}\}\qquad\forall c>0.

Since
\hat{T}_{c}\geqslant T_{c,\alpha}
, we always have

(4.1)

\mathrm{P}\{\hat{T}_{c}>t_{n}\}\geqslant\mathrm{P}\{T_{c,\alpha}>t_{n}\}.

The following is a way to turn the preceding inequality in reverse direction.

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose that
t_{n+1}-t_{n}=\mathcal{o}(t_{n})
as
n\to\infty
.
Then, there exists a constant
K>1
with the following property:
Whenever
m(n)
is an integer in
(1\,,n)
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathrm{P}\{\hat{T}_{c}>t_{n}\}\leqslant\mathrm{P}\{T_{c+\varepsilon,\alpha}>t_{n}/t_{m(n)}\}+K\sum_{j=m(n)}^{n}\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{K}\left|\frac{t_{j}}{t_{j+1}-t_{j}}\right|^{2\alpha}\right),

for every
c,\varepsilon>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Clearly,

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\{\hat{T}_{c}>t_{n}\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{P}\left\{|X(t_{j})|\leqslant ct_{j}^{\alpha}\ \forall j=1,\ldots,n\right\}

(4.2)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathrm{P}\left\{|X(t)|\leqslant(c+\varepsilon)t^{\alpha}\ \forall t\in[t_{m(n)}\,,t_{n}]\right\}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{P}\left\{\exists j\in\{m(n)\,,\ldots,n\}:\ \sup_{t\in[t_{j},t_{j+1}]}|X(t)-X(t_{j})|\geqslant\varepsilon t_{j}^{\alpha}\right\}.

The self-similarity of
X
implies that the first term on the right-hand side of (
4.2
)
is equal to

\mathrm{P}\{|X(t)|\leqslant(c+\varepsilon)t^{\alpha}\ \forall t\in[1\,,t_{n}/t_{m(n)}]\}=\mathrm{P}\{T_{c+\varepsilon,\alpha}>t_{n}/t_{m(n)}\}.

Therefore, it suffices to study the second term on the right-hand side of (
4.2
).

Choose and fix an integer
j\geqslant 1
. The Gaussian process
Y(t)=X(t)-X(t_{j})

[
t_{j}\leqslant t\leqslant t_{j+1}
] satisfies

\mathrm{E}(|Y(t)-Y(s)|^{2})=|t-s|^{2\alpha}

uniformly for every integer
j\geqslant 1
and all
s,t\in[t_{j}\,,t_{j+1}]
.
Let
N
denote the metric entropy of
[t_{j}\,,t_{j+1}]
with respect to the
metric
d(t\,,s)=\|Y(t)-Y(s)\|_{2}
for every
s,t\in[t_{j}\,,t_{j+1}]
. That is,

N(r)
denotes the minimum number of closed
d
-balls of radius
r>0

needed to cover
[t_{j}\,,t_{j+1}]
. The preceding
shows that

N(r)\leqslant(t_{j+1}-t_{j})r^{-1/\alpha}\qquad\forall r\in(0\,,(t_{j+1}-t_{j})^{\alpha}).

It also shows that the
d
-diameter
D
of
[t_{j}\,,t_{j+1}]
satisfies

D=\max_{s,t\in[t_{j}\,,t_{j+1}]}d(s\,,t)\leqslant(t_{j+1}-t_{j})^{\alpha}.

It follows from Dudley’s theorem

[
33
]
and the definition of the process
Y
that
there exist universal numbers
C_{1},C_{2}>0
— independently of
j
— such that

(4.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathrm{E}\sup_{t\in[t_{j},t_{j+1}]}|X(t)-X(t_{j})|=\mathrm{E}\sup_{t\in[t_{j},t_{j+1}]}|Y(t)|\\
&\leqslant C_{1}\int_{0}^{C_{1}(t_{j+1}-t_{j})^{\alpha}}|\log_{+}((t_{j+1}-t_{j})r^{-1/\alpha})|^{1/2}\,{\rm d}r\leqslant C_{2}(t_{j+1}-t_{j})^{\alpha},\end{split}

where
\log_{+}(x)=\log({\rm e}+x)
for all
x\geqslant 0
.
Thanks to concentration of measure
(see for example Ledoux
[
65
, Chapter 2]
),

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\left\{\sup_{t\in[t_{j},t_{j+1}]}|X(t)-X(t_{j})|\geqslant C_{2}(t_{j+1}-t_{j})^{\alpha}+z\right\}

\displaystyle\hskip 72.26999pt\leqslant 2\exp\left(-\frac{z^{2}}{2\sup_{t\in[t_{j},t_{j+1}]}\mathrm{E}(|X(t)-X(t_{j})|^{2})}\right)\qquad\forall z>0.

This and (
4.3
)
implicitly yield a constant
C_{3}
— independent of
(j\,,z)
— such that

\sup_{t\in[t_{j},t_{j+1}]}\mathrm{E}(|X(t)-X(t_{j})|^{2})\leqslant C_{3}(t_{j+1}-t_{j})^{2\alpha},

and hence, for every
z>0
:

\mathrm{P}\left\{\sup_{t\in[t_{j}\,,t_{j+1}]}|X(t)-X(t_{j})|\geqslant C_{2}(t_{j+1}-t_{j})^{\alpha}+z\right\}\leqslant 2\exp\left(-\frac{z^{2}}{2C_{3}(t_{j+1}-t_{j})^{2\alpha}}\right).

In particular, if
j
is large enough to ensure that

t_{j+1}-t_{j}\leqslant(\varepsilon/(2C_{2}))^{1/\alpha}t_{j}
, then

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\left\{\sup_{t\in[t_{j},t_{j+1}]}|X(t)-X(t_{j})|\geqslant\varepsilon t_{j}^{\alpha}\right\}

\displaystyle\hskip 72.26999pt\leqslant\mathrm{P}\left\{\sup_{t\in[t_{j},t_{j+1}]}|X(t)-X(t_{j})|\geqslant C_{2}(t_{j+1}-t_{j})^{\alpha}+(\varepsilon/2)t_{j}^{\alpha}\right\}

\displaystyle\hskip 72.26999pt\leqslant 2\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{8C_{3}}\left|\frac{t_{j}}{t_{j+1}-t_{j}}\right|^{2\alpha}\right).

We can now appeal to (
4.2
) and the above, using a union bound,
in order to deduce the lemma from the facts that
t_{j+1}-t_{j}=\mathcal{o}(t_{j})

and
m(n)\to\infty
as
j,n\to\infty
.
∎

One can apply Lemma
4.3
in order to produce a number of examples
of sequences
\{t_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
along which,

(4.4)

\mathrm{P}\left\{|X(t_{j})|\leqslant ct_{j}^{\alpha}\ \forall j=1,\ldots,n\right\}=t_{n}^{-\lambda(c)+\mathcal{o}(1)}\qquad\text{as $n\to\infty$},

for the same
\lambda
as in Theorem
1.1
. Recall that
X
denotes a

\fBm
throughout this discussion.

Example 4.4
.

For a simple example consider
t_{n}=n
and
m(n)=\lfloor n^{\delta}\rfloor

where
\delta\in(0\,,1)
. Lemma
4.3
and Theorem
1.1

together yield the following: For every
\varepsilon,c>0
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\left\{|X(j)|\leqslant cj^{\alpha}\ \forall j=1,\ldots,n\right\}\leqslant n^{-(1-\delta)\lambda(c+\varepsilon)+\mathcal{o}(1)}+(K+\mathcal{o}(1))\sum_{n^{\delta}\leqslant j\leqslant n}{\rm e}^{-\varepsilon^{2}j^{2\alpha}/K}

\displaystyle\hskip 43.36243pt=n^{-(1-\delta)\lambda(c+\varepsilon)+\mathcal{o}(1)}+\exp\left(-\frac{(\varepsilon^{2}+\mathcal{o}(1))n^{2\alpha\delta}}{K}\right)=n^{-(1-\delta)\lambda(c+\varepsilon)+\mathcal{o}(1)},

as
n\to\infty
. In light of (
4.1
) and Theorem
1.1
,
the preceding shows that the sequence defined by
t_{j}=j

satisfies (
4.4
).

Example 4.5
.

Open Problem
4.2
asks whether (
4.4
) holds
for the sequence
t_{n}=\exp(n)
. Among other things,
this example will imply that
if
\alpha\approx 1
then (
4.4
) can hold for
sequences whose growth rate fall barely below
\exp(n^{2/3})
.
Consider
q\in(0\,,1)
, and define

t_{n}=\exp(n^{q})
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Since

\frac{t_{j}}{t_{j+1}-t_{j}}=\frac{j^{1-q}}{q+\mathcal{o}(1)}\qquad\text{as $j\to\infty$},

it follows from Lemma
4.3

and Theorem
1.1
that, as long as
m(n)\to\infty
strictly monotonically
and
m(n)=\mathcal{o}(n)
when
n\gg 1
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\left\{|X(t_{j})|\leqslant ct_{j}^{\alpha}\ \forall j=1,\ldots,n\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left(-(\lambda(c+\varepsilon)+\mathcal{o}(1))\left[n^{q}-m(n)^{q}\right]\right)+\sum_{j=m(n)}^{n}\exp\left(-\frac{(\varepsilon^{2}+\mathcal{o}(1))j^{2(1-q)\alpha}}{K}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left(-(\lambda(c+\varepsilon)+\mathcal{o}(1))\left[n^{q}-m(n)^{q}\right]\right)+\exp\left(-\frac{(\varepsilon^{2}+\mathcal{o}(1))m(n)^{2(1-q)\alpha}}{K}\right).

We want the first term on the right-hand side to dominate the second; that is,

n^{q}-m(n)^{q}<m(n)^{2(1-q)\alpha},\qquad m(n)\ll n.

Try
m(n)=n^{p}
where
0<p<1
in order to see that this happens if and only if

q<\frac{2\alpha p}{1+2\alpha p}.

Since
p
can be as close to one as we would like,
it follows that if
q<2\alpha/(1+2\alpha)
, then (
4.4
)
is satisfied for the sequence defined by
t_{n}=\exp(n^{q})
.

Example 4.6
.

For our third and final example we consider the sequence defined by

t_{n}=\exp(|\log n|^{q}),

where
q>1
is fixed. Since

\frac{t_{j+1}-t_{j}}{t_{j}}=\frac{(q+\mathcal{o}(1))|\log j|^{q-1}}{j}\qquad\text{as $j\to\infty$},

We may apply Lemma
4.3
with
m(n)=n^{\delta}
, where
\delta>0

is fixed but as small as we like, in order to see that the sequence
t_{n}=\exp(|\log n|^{q})

satisfies (
4.4
) when
q>1
.
