Sleep Pattern and Structural Damage in Older Adults with Glaucoma: A United States and China Study
Authors: Zhonghua Luo, Zihao Lin, Yuefeng Yu, Yangyang Du, Yue Xu, Yu Yang, Shengsong Huang, Ying Han, Jingjing Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo investigate sleep patterns in older adults with glaucoma and evaluate their associations with glaucomatous damage and emotional disturbances.DesignA cross-sectional observational study.ParticipantsAdults aged ≥60 years from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2005-2008 in the United States (n = 3317) and the Elderly Glaucoma and Sleep Study (EGSS) in China (n = 200).MethodsParticipants in NHANES and EGSS completed standardized sleep questionnaires and underwent ophthalmic examinations, while wearable derived-sleep measures and urinary melatonin metabolite assays were additionally performed in the EGSS cohort. In EGSS, multivariable linear regression models were used to examine associations of the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) global score with demographic, clinical, and ocular factors.Main Outcome MeasuresOverall sleep disorder in NHANES and PSQI global score in EGSS.ResultsIn NHANES, older adults with glaucoma reported more frequent sleep disturbances than controls, showing higher odds of sleep disorders and difficulty initiating sleep, as well as longer sleep latency (all P < 0.05). In EGSS, patients with primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) showed poorer sleep quality, higher device-estimated light sleep time, and a lower 6-sulfatoxymelatonin/creatinine ratio than controls (all P < 0.05), while no significant differences were found for primary angle-closure glaucoma. In multivariable analyses, higher 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire scores and thinner peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer thickness were independently associated with higher PSQI scores in POAG (all P < 0.05).ConclusionsOlder adults with glaucoma had poorer sleep quality. Participants with POAG showed poorer subjective sleep quality, a longer wearable-derived light sleep duration, and lower urinary melatonin metabolite levels. In POAG, poorer sleep quality was significantly associated with more severe glaucomatous structural damage and a greater depressive symptom burden.Financial Disclosure(s)The authors have no proprietary or commercial interest in any materials discussed in this article.Keywords: Glaucoma, Older adults, Peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer, Sleep pattern

Glaucoma, a leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide, is an age-related optic neuropathy characterized by progressive degeneration of retinal ganglion cells and corresponding visual field loss.1,2 With the global population aging, the number of older adults affected by glaucoma is steadily increasing, imposing a substantial medical and socioeconomic burden on individuals, families, and health care systems.3

Sleep disturbances are highly prevalent among older adults, characterized by reduced sleep duration and efficiency, increased fragmentation, and diminished rapid eye movement and slow-wave sleep.4 Growing evidence suggests an association between sleep patterns and glaucoma. Both short and long sleep duration, as well as sleep disturbances, have been associated with an increased risk of glaucoma.5,6 In addition, sleep disorders appear to be more prevalent and more severe in patients with glaucoma than in those without glaucoma.7, 8, 9 Concurrently, the increasing availability and validation of wearable sleep devices provide a practical foundation for large-scale investigations.10 In this context, further exploring the relationship between sleep patterns and glaucoma through the integration of multimodal data from multinational cohorts may have clinical value, particularly in older adults, in whom sleep problems are especially prominent.

Therefore, we conducted this study to comprehensively characterize sleep patterns in older adults with glaucoma by integrating 2 independent data sets: the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) from the United States and the Elderly Glaucoma and Sleep Study (EGSS) from China. Sleep patterns were assessed using standardized questionnaires, wrist-worn devices, and urine analysis. We further examined the associations between sleep parameters and glaucomatous damage in this population.

Methods

Data Source

We conducted a cross-sectional analysis integrating 2 independent data sets: the NHANES 2005 to 2008 from the United States and the EGSS from China.

The 2005 to 2008 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey in the United States

The NHANES is a nationally representative survey of the US civilian, noninstitutionalized population conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics using a complex, multistage probability sampling design. All protocols were approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Institutional Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This secondary analysis of NHANES data was exempted from review by the Institutional Review Board of Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center, Sun Yat-sen University.

We included participants aged 60 years or older from the 2005 to 2008 cycles who underwent ophthalmic assessments and completed sleep-related questionnaires. Participants were categorized into a glaucoma group or a control group without a history of glaucoma. In NHANES, glaucoma status was classified based on optic nerve head (ONH) fundus photographic assessment using a nonmydriatic fundus camera (CR6-45NM; Canon USA). Each image was assessed for quality, cup-to-disc ratio (CDR), neuroretinal rim notching, optic cup excavation, optic disc hemorrhage, disc tilting, disc size, and glaucoma status (graded as none, possible, probable, definite, or ungradable).11 The CDR was graded at the University of Wisconsin Fundus Photograph Reading Center, and, as a quality-control measure, 3 ophthalmologists at the Wilmer Eye Institute regraded all images with a vertical CDR >0.6 and a random subset of images with a vertical CDR <0.6 in 2012.12 Glaucoma was defined when either eye was graded as “probable” or “definite” by expert assessment. Participants with a vertical CDR <0.6 in both eyes were considered nonglaucomatous.11 This photograph-based definition was used for case-control classification in NHANES and should not be interpreted as equivalent to a contemporary clinical diagnosis based on integrated structural and functional assessments. Participants were excluded if they had missing data on key variables (e.g., sleep parameters) or if they had major ocular comorbidities, including retinal disease, optic nerve disorders, or a history of ocular trauma.

The Elderly Glaucoma and Sleep Study in China

The EGSS is a single-center, prospective, observational cohort study conducted at Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center, Sun Yat-sen University (Guangzhou, China), which consecutively enrolled participants aged 60 years or older starting in January 2025. It was approved by the Institutional Review Board/Ethics Committee of the Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center, Sun Yat-sen University (ID: 2024KYPJ164) and was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. It adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines.

Participants were included if 2 specialists (J.H. and Y.H.) diagnosed primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) or primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG) based on standardized ophthalmic examinations.13 The control group was documented to be free of glaucoma. Exclusion criteria were: (1) presence of other ocular diseases such as severe cataract, high myopia, or corneal, retinal, choroidal, or optic nerve disorders; (2) history of ocular trauma, intraocular or laser surgery, excluding pterygium excision; (3) systemic diseases, including major neurologic disorders (e.g., stroke, Parkinson disease, dementia, or epilepsy), major psychiatric disorders (e.g., schizophrenia or bipolar disorder), active cancer, or autoimmune disease; (4) alcohol or drug abuse, night-shift work (>3 shifts per week), or transmeridian travel within 3 months before enrollment; (5) use of medication affecting melatonin or dopamine levels (e.g., melatonin supplements and anti-inflammatory drugs); (6) use of medications potentially affecting sleep and mood, including nifedipine, fluvoxamine, benzodiazepines, and nonbenzodiazepine hypnotics (e.g., zaleplon, zolpidem, and zopiclone).

Demographic and Clinical Covariates

For the NHANES data set, self-reported sociodemographic factors included age, sex, race, ethnicity (Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, or other race), and educational levels (less than high school, 9th to 11th grade, high school or equivalent, college, or above college).14 Clinical characteristics obtained through self-report included height, weight, smoking and drinking status (never, current, or former), and history of systemic conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, lung disease, and heart disease.15

In EGSS, demographic and clinical covariates were collected, including age, sex, height, weight, blood pressure, education (primary, secondary, and university or above), smoking and drinking status (never, former, and current), and detailed medical and personal histories. Diabetes was defined as fasting plasma glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L, glycated hemoglobin ≥6.5%, or a self-reported previous diagnosis of diabetes.16 Hypertension was defined as blood pressure ≥140/90 mmHg or self-reported use of antihypertensive medication.17 Blood pressure and heart rate measurements were taken after 5 minutes of rest, with no eating or exercise for 30 minutes. Three readings were obtained at 3-minute intervals, and the average of the 3 was used.18

Ocular Examinations

In NHANES, each participant underwent nonmydriatic 45° fundus photography of the macula and optic disc of both eyes (CR6-45NM; Canon USA) and, if they had at least light-perception vision and no ocular infection, completed a 19-point suprathreshold screening test using the N-30-5 pattern on a Matrix frequency doubling technology perimetry device (Carl Zeiss Meditec).19

In EGSS, participants underwent a comprehensive ocular examination, comprising best-corrected visual acuity measurement, intraocular pressure measurement (Goldmann applanation tonometer), slit-lamp–based examination of the anterior and posterior ocular segment, fundus photography (CLARUS 500; Carl Zeiss Meditec), ocular biometry (IOLmaster; Carl Zeiss Meditec), OCT (Heidelberg Engineering) and OCT angiography (TowardPi Medical Technology Co., Ltd). Participants with suspected glaucoma also underwent gonioscopy and completed Humphrey visual field testing (SITA Fast 30-2; Carl Zeiss Meditec, Inc).

In EGSS, when glaucoma was suspected, participants underwent Humphrey Field Analyzer (Carl Zeiss Meditec) Swedish Interactive Threshold Algorithm Fast 30-2 visual field testing, and glaucomatous visual field defects were confirmed on at least 3 visual field examinations. Only reliable test results were included, which were defined as fixation losses of less than 20%, false-negative rates of less than 15%, and false-positive rates of less than 15%.20 Eyes with glaucoma that were blind (best-corrected visual acuity < 20/400) and unable to undergo testing were assigned a visual field mean deviation of –32 dB.21

In EGSS, spectral-domain OCT (Heidelberg Engineering) with an 870-nm light source was used to measure peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (pRNFL) thickness. A circular scan with a diameter of 3.5 mm, centered on the optic disc, was performed to automatically segment and calculate pRNFL thickness in the temporal, nasal, superior, and inferior quadrants.22 OCT angiography (TowardPi Medical Technology), operating at 400 000 A-scans per second, was used for macular and ONH imaging. A 6 × 6 mm macular scan centered on the fovea was acquired to assess ganglion cell complex (consisting of the ganglion cell layer, internal plexiform layer, and retinal nerve fiber layer) thickness, whole-image superficial vessel density (the internal limiting membrane to 10 μm above the inner plexiform layer), and choroidal parameters (choroidal vascularity index, vascular volume, and stromal volume).23,24 A 4.5 × 4.5 mm scan centered on the ONH was obtained to evaluate circumpapillary capillary density.25 Images with an OCT quality score <15 or OCT angiography quality score <7 (range, 0–10) were excluded.24,26

Sleep Patterns and Depression

In NHANES, subjective sleep patterns were assessed with a standardized questionnaire adapted from the Functional Outcomes of Sleep Questionnaire, which included items on sleep initiation and maintenance, early-morning awakening, daytime sleepiness, insufficient sleep, use of sleep medications, and sleep duration and sleep latency.11 Several sleep-related items were recorded using 5-level ordinal response categories (never, rarely, sometimes, often, and almost always), with responses of “often” or “almost always” classified as positive for analysis. Depressive symptoms in NHANES and EGSS were assessed using the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), a validated instrument for depression screening.27 Questionnaire-based variables were collected using standardized questionnaires administered by trained interviewers during the NHANES household interview.28

In EGSS, subjective sleep patterns were further assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), one of the most widely used and validated instruments in sleep research.29 This validated 19-item questionnaire covers sleep quality, latency, duration, efficiency, disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction over the past month. Habitual sleeping position was collected separately as an additional subjective sleep-related variable. Objective sleep parameters were measured using a wearable actigraphy device (Honor Band 8; Huawei Technologies) worn continuously on the nondominant wrist for 7 consecutive days, with a minimum requirement of 7 valid nights.30,31 The consecutive 7-day monitoring period covered both weekdays and weekends and captured sleep variation across a typical week. The device recorded estimated total sleep time, deep sleep time, light sleep time, rapid eye movement sleep time, and wear time.32 Participants were instructed to wear the device continuously on the nondominant wrist, except for bathing and charging, and daytime naps were excluded from the sleep metrics used for analysis. All questionnaires in EGSS were administered by trained personnel (Z. Luo), and responses were validated for completeness.

Urine Sample Collection

In EGSS, first-morning mid-stream urine samples were collected from participants who slept in the dark to measure 6-sulfatoxymelatonin (aMT6s), the primary urinary metabolite of melatonin, as a biomarker of circadian rhythm.33 Samples were collected in sterile containers, immediately stored at 4° C, transported to a central laboratory within 2 hours and stored at –80° C until analysis.34 Levels of aMT6s were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (IBL International) and normalized to urinary creatinine levels (aMT6s/creatinine ratio) to account for variations in urine concentration. Samples were processed in duplicate, and the mean value was used for analysis.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed with R (version 4.2.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing), SPSS (version 26.0; IBM Corp), and Stata (version 18.0; StataCorp LLC). All measurements were obtained from both eyes, and the eye with the worse visual field status was used for group assignment and analysis. Normality of each continuous outcome was first assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation and compared using analysis of variance or t tests for normally distributed data. Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages and compared using chi-square or Fisher exact tests. For adjusted comparisons of key sleep outcomes in the EGSS cohort, covariate-adjusted marginal means were estimated from multivariable linear regression models, followed by Bonferroni post hoc pairwise comparisons. In NHANES, we applied sample weights to account for the complex NHANES survey design and ensure national representativeness. All analyses incorporated the complex survey design, including strata (SDMVSTRA) and primary sampling units (SDMVPSU) and applied a 4-year examination weight constructed by dividing WTMEC2YR by 2 for the combined 2005-2008 NHANES cycles. Further, we fitted survey-weighted multivariable regression models to assess whether glaucoma status was independently associated with sleep outcomes. In the EGSS cohort, multivariable linear regression models were applied to examine associations between total PSQI score and demographic, clinical, and ocular parameters. Covariates for multivariable models were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and prior literature. Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors, and sensitivity analyses were additionally performed. Regression coefficient (β) and odd ratio with 95% confidence interval (CI) are reported. All tests were 2-sided, and P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant for the primary outcomes. The main outcomes of interest for the present analyses were overall sleep disorder in NHANES and PSQI global score in the EGSS cohort. Other sleep domains, subgroup comparisons, and correlation analyses were considered exploratory and interpreted accordingly.

Results

In the US NHANES 2005-2008, 6499 participants aged 60 years or older were considered for inclusion, and those with ocular diseases other than glaucoma (n = 3067) or missing key sleep variables (n = 115) were excluded (Fig S1A, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). In the US NHANES 2005 to 2008 (Table 1), a total of 3317 participants aged ≥60 years were analyzed, including 392 with glaucoma and 2925 controls. Participants with glaucoma had a higher proportion of non-Hispanic Black individuals (13.01% vs. 8.02%) and differed in smoking status, with more former smokers and fewer current smokers (P = 0.001). The mean PHQ-9 score was markedly higher in the glaucoma group (6.03 ± 2.43 vs. 2.12 ± 1.20; P < 0.001), indicating a greater prevalence of depressive symptoms among individuals with glaucoma.

Of the 225 participants initially enrolled in the EGSS cohort, 25 were excluded because of poor-quality images (n = 7), failure to complete 7 consecutive days of wearable monitoring (n = 15), or loss to follow-up (n = 3), as shown in Figure S1B. The final EGSS cohort comprised 200 participants: 90 with POAG, 50 with PACG, and 60 controls (Table 2). The age, sex, and educational level were comparable across groups. Diabetes was more common in the PACG group (34.00%) than in the POAG (12.22%) or control group (23.33%) (P = 0.009). Systolic blood pressure in patients with POAG was significantly lower than that in patients with PACG or in controls (P = 0.001). The mean PHQ-9 score was the highest in the POAG group (5.07 ± 4.21), followed by PACG (3.00 ± 3.68) and controls (2.00 ± 2.44), indicating a greater prevalence of depressive symptoms in POAG (P < 0.001). Table S3 (available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org) summarizes ocular parameters in EGSS participants. Both POAG and PACG eyes exhibited significantly higher intraocular pressure, larger CDR, thinner pRNFL, and lower ONH whole-image vessel density compared with controls (all P < 0.001). Visual field damage, as indicated by mean deviation, was more severe in PACG than in POAG (–23.28 ± 8.16 dB vs. –16.20 ± 10.27 dB, P < 0.001).

Data presented in Table 4 indicate that older adults classified as having glaucoma on fundus photographic assessment in the NHANES cohort reported a higher prevalence of sleep disorders than controls (36.18% vs. 28.54%; P = 0.014). They were more likely to experience difficulty falling asleep (21.73% vs. 16.02%) and waking up during the night (23.49% vs. 21.24%). Mean sleep latency was significantly longer in glaucoma participants compared with controls (25.28 ± 19.70 min vs. 20.14 ± 18.62 min; P < 0.001), whereas total sleep duration did not differ between groups (P = 0.314). In survey-weighted multivariable models, glaucoma remained associated with a higher burden of sleep disturbance (Tables S5 and S6, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org): sleep disorders (odds ratio = 1.37; 95% CI, 1.12–1.54; P < 0.001), greater difficulty initiating sleep (odds ratio = 1.17; 95% CI, 1.03–1.38; P = 0.040), and longer sleep latency (β = 4.59; 95% CI, 2.56–6.62; P < 0.001). In sensitivity analyses excluding PHQ-9 from the multivariable models, these associations remained materially unchanged and statistically significant.

As shown in Figure 2, patients with POAG exhibited significantly higher PSQI global scores than those with PACG and controls (P < 0.001), indicating poorer overall sleep quality among POAG patients in the EGSS cohort. In exploratory group comparisons, the POAG group also showed longer sleep latency, reduced sleep efficiency, and greater daytime dysfunction (all P < 0.05). Exploratory wearable-derived sleep analyses (Fig 3) suggested that POAG participants had a longer light sleep time and a higher proportion of light sleep compared with controls (all P < 0.05). Notably, aMT6s/creatinine ratio, a biomarker of melatonin metabolism, was markedly lower in POAG than in both PACG and controls (P < 0.01), suggesting possible circadian-related disturbance. After adjustment for covariates, the between-group differences in PSQI global score and device-estimated light sleep time remained evident (Fig S4, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). As illustrated in Figure 5, patients with POAG appeared to show a more dispersed distribution of sleep-onset times, with a visually higher frequency of lateral decubitus positions.

Discussion

This study integrated 2 independent data sets, the NHANES from the United States and the EGSS from China, to characterize sleep patterns and examine their associations with glaucomatous damage in older adults. By combining subjective questionnaires, wearable actigraphy, and urinary melatonin metabolite analyses, this study provided a multidimensional assessment of sleep patterns and melatonin-related circadian indicators. In NHANES, older adults with glaucoma on fundus photographic assessment had a higher prevalence of sleep disturbances than controls. In the EGSS cohort, further subgroup analyses showed that patients with POAG had poorer sleep quality and greater abnormalities in sleep-related measures than those with PACG or controls. Finally, our findings from population- and hospital-based cohorts provide additional evidence of an association between sleep-related disturbances and glaucoma in older adults.

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis showed that sleep disorders are common in patients with glaucoma, with a pooled prevalence of 47%.9 Further extending this evidence, our results showed that older adults with glaucoma exhibited a higher prevalence of sleep disturbances, including difficulty initiating sleep, despite comparable total sleep duration. Impaired sleep quality in this population likely reflects the dual impact of age-related changes in neuroendocrine functions and glaucoma-associated neurodegeneration.35,36 Glaucoma may exacerbate age-related circadian instability by damaging intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells, thereby reducing light input to the suprachiasmatic nucleus and impairing melatonin regulation. This disruption of circadian signaling may contribute to fragmented and less restorative sleep in the elderly.

Although prior Asian studies reported poorer PSQI-defined sleep in PACG, their findings were not consistent with ours.37,38 This discrepancy may be related to differences in sleep assessment methods and population characteristics. Within the EGSS cohort in China, patients with POAG showed poorer subjective sleep quality and altered wearable-derived sleep measures than those with PACG. However, this subgroup contrast should be interpreted cautiously. Primary open-angle glaucoma and PACG also differed in several clinically relevant characteristics, including diabetes prevalence, systolic blood pressure, depressive symptoms, and visual field severity. Therefore, the observed differences may reflect differences in disease severity and residual systemic confounding.

Building upon these findings, wearable-derived measures suggested a higher proportion of light sleep among older adults with POAG in our EGSS cohort. Similarly, a prior actigraphy-based study reported that patients with open-angle glaucoma had greater wake after sleep onset.8 Light sleep represents a more unstable sleep stage susceptible to environmental and physiological perturbations.39 This higher device-estimated light sleep proportion may be related to reduced melatonin secretion, as indicated by the lower urinary aMT6s/creatinine ratio in POAG patients in the current study. Intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells transmit light information to the brain’s circadian center, the suprachiasmatic nucleus, which controls melatonin release from the pineal gland.40 When the intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cell function is impaired, melatonin production decreases, and circadian-related regulation may be affected. Given the possible and suggested roles of melatonin in circadian regulation and neuroprotection, further long-term clinical studies are warranted to clarify its potential clinical relevance in patients with POAG.41

In the current study, poorer sleep quality in POAG was significantly correlated with thinner pRNFL and worse depressive symptoms. A prior study has demonstrated that both short and long sleep duration are associated with retinal nerve fiber layer thinning.5 Moreover, sleep disturbances in glaucoma patients are associated with worse psychological status, particularly increased depressive symptoms.42 Taken together, sleep disturbances, glaucomatous structural damage, and depressive symptoms in patients with POAG are not isolated phenomena, but may be interconnected through shared neurobiological mechanisms. Specifically, disruptions in circadian rhythms and neurodegenerative processes could constitute a neurobehavioral-structural network linking functional, emotional, and structural alterations. Sleep disturbances in glaucoma patients have been associated with reduced intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cell function and melatonin dysregulation, which not only compromise sleep quality but may also exacerbate retinal ganglion cell loss. Similarly, depressive symptoms may arise as a consequence of visual impairment and sleep disruption, while chronic mood and sleep disturbances may further promote neuroinflammation and oxidative stress, accelerating retinal neurodegeneration. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to explore these complex interactions further and clarify causality.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design of this study cannot establish a causal relationship between sleep disturbances and glaucomatous damage. A prospective longitudinal study by our team is being conducted to further explore the potential impact of sleep disruption on glaucoma progression. In addition, glaucoma classification in NHANES was based on pragmatic fundus photographic criteria, and some case-control misclassification is possible. Second, wearable monitoring for at least 7 consecutive days enabled assessment of habitual sleep patterns but may not fully capture longer-term variability. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously in the absence of polysomnography. Third, differences in insurance coverage and retirement-age policies between China (EGSS) and the United States (NHANES) may contribute to residual confounding in cross-cohort comparisons. Fourth, our findings may not be generalizable to younger patients with glaucoma. Fifth, other medications that may influence sleep or mood, including systemic beta-blockers, topical glaucoma medications, and corticosteroids, were not comprehensively analyzed. In addition, because multiple comparisons were performed, findings beyond the primary outcomes for the present analyses should be interpreted as exploratory. Nevertheless, the findings provide preliminary insights that warrant confirmation in larger and multicenter studies.

In summary, older adults with glaucoma reported poor sleep quality. Primary open-angle glaucoma shows poorer subjective sleep quality, longer wearable-derived light sleep time, and reduced melatonin secretion. Poorer sleep quality in older adults with POAG was associated with more severe glaucomatous structural impairment and greater depressive symptom burden. The findings highlight the importance of incorporating sleep and emotion evaluation into glaucoma management. Future longitudinal and interventional studies are warranted to determine whether improving sleep quality and circadian stability may have clinical benefit in older adults with glaucoma.
