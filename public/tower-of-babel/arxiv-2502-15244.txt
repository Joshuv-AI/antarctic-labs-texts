Learning Maritime Inventory Routing Optimization
Rui Chen, Defeng Liu, Nan Jiang, Rishabh Gupta, Mustafa Kilinc, Andrea Lodi

Abstract
We propose a machine learning-based local search approach for finding feasible solutions of large-scale maritime inventory routing optimization problems. Given the combinatorial complexity of the problems, we integrate a graph neural network-based neighborhood selection method to enhance local search efficiency. Our approach enables a structured exploration of vessel neighborhoods, improving solution quality while maintaining computational efficiency. Through extensive computational experiments on realistic instances, we demonstrate that our method outperforms direct mixed-integer programming in solution time.

Introduction

Maritime transportation is a cornerstone of global trade, facilitating over 80% of international commerce. Despite its slower pace, it offers a cost-effective solution for transporting large quantities of goods over long distances, making it indispensable for both developed and developing nations. Developing countries, in particular, heavily rely on maritime shipping to participate in the global market. The significance of maritime transportation in maintaining robust and economical supply chains underscores the necessity of optimizing operations. Efficient maritime inventory routing is crucial for minimizing costs, reducing transit times, and ensuring timely deliveries. Moreover, the International Maritime Organization has been implementing stricter regulations on shipping emissions, further emphasizing the need for meticulous planning and optimization of maritime operations.

In this work, we concentrate on maritime inventory routing that we informally define in the next section. In the remainder of the introduction, we present some literature review (Section

), describe the formulation we consider and its challenges (Section
1.3
), and outline the contribution of this work. Such a contribution is then detailed in Sections

, and computationally evaluated in Section

1.1
Maritime Inventory Routing

Maritime inventory routing optimization (MIRO) is a special case of inventory routing optimization (IRO)
(
Bell et al. 1983
)
that has received significant attention in recent years due to the growth in the e-commerce industry. Both MIRO and IRO involve the simultaneous planning of inventory management decisions at supply and (or) demand sites and vehicle routing decisions. The primary distinction between the two lies in the mode of transportation. MIRO specifically addresses shipping goods via sea vessels, which introduces unique challenges such as longer travel times, larger shipment volumes, and the necessity to account for port constraints. In contrast, IRO typically considers land-based transportation, dealing with smaller vehicles and shorter delivery distances. Despite their shared goal of managing inventory levels while optimizing delivery routes, the distinct considerations and constraints of maritime transportation render MIRO a unique and important area of study.

There is no consensus in the academic literature on what constitutes a MIRO problem. Many variants, motivated by specific applications, have appeared in the literature, with key applications including the transportation of petroleum products and raw commodities across the world. The most typical formulation
(
Song and Furman 2013
)
for MIRO proposed in the literature considers shipping cost minimization for the transport of a single product from a set of production ports, with or without a given production rate, to discharge sites, with or without a consumption rate. Each port has a pre-specified storage capacity that needs to be maintained using a heterogeneous fleet of vessels. The most common application for this variant, which is also the most prevalent use case for MIRO, is the transport of liquefied natural gas (LNG)
(
Papageorgiou et al. 2014b
)
. Other variants considered in the literature extend the problem to multiple products that may require allocation to separate compartments
(
Ronen 2002
,
Agra et al. 2015
)
, vessel speed optimization
(
Norstad et al. 2011
,
Eide et al. 2020
)
, as well as robust scheduling of vessel operations under uncertainty
(
Agra et al. 2018a
,
Liu et al. 2021a
)
.

The diversity of these problem formulations introduces significant computational challenges, requiring careful modeling choices to accurately represent both temporal and spatial dynamics. Depending on the specific requirements of an application, different modeling strategies have been proposed in the literature. Discrete-time and continuous-time formulations have been developed to capture the temporal evolution of port inventories and vessel schedules, while spatial flows are often modeled using arc-flow and path-flow paradigms. These modeling approaches offer different trade-offs in terms of tractability, solution quality, and computational efficiency, shaping the way MIRO problems are formulated and solved in practice. The readers are referred to detailed reviews by
Papageorgiou et al. 2014b
,
Fagerholt et al. 2023
, and
Christiansen et al. 2025
for a more detailed overview of MIRO modeling paradigms.

1.2
Literature Review

A variety of solution methodologies have been proposed for the MIRO problem, ranging from exact methods to metaheuristics and hybrid approaches. While exact methods offer optimality guarantees, they struggle to scale beyond small problem instances, making them impractical for real-world applications with long planning horizons. As a result, heuristic methods – particularly Large Neighborhood Search (LNS) – have emerged as the state-of-the-art approach for large-scale MIRO problems, including LNG inventory routing, which often requires planning over 365 time periods. In this section, we review both exact and heuristic methods, highlighting the motivation for our focus on LNS and the challenges associated with its implementation.

1.2.1
Exact Methods

Early approaches formulated MIRO as a mixed-integer programming problem and attempted to solve it using commercial solvers, sometimes enhanced with custom cutting-plane strategies to strengthen the formulation during branch and bound
(
Engineer et al. 2012
,
Andersson et al. 2017
)
. However, these methods have proven computationally intractable for large-scale problems due to the exponential growth of the solution space
(
Shaabani et al. 2023
)
.

To improve tractability, several papers have applied Dantzig-Wolfe and Benders decomposition methods. For instance,
Fagerholt and Christiansen 2000
considered the use of column generation to solve the linear optimization relaxation at each node of a branch-and-bound tree during the solution of a MIRO problem.
Grønhaug et al. 2010
developed a branch-and-price method for an LNG inventory routing problem, with the master problem handling inventory and port capacity constraints while subproblems generate ship route columns. Similarly,
Papageorgiou et al. 2014a
proposed a two-stage Benders decomposition approach that first determined inter-regional routing decisions and then optimized intra-regional loading and discharging operations.
Agra et al. 2018b
extended this approach to stochastic MIRO settings, incorporating uncertainty in sailing times and port waiting periods through a branch-and-cut framework that iteratively generated Benders cuts.

Despite these methodological advances, exact approaches remain impractical for real-world instances with long planning horizons.
Shao et al. 2015
demonstrated that Dantzig-Wolfe decomposition struggles to close the optimality gap for LNG MIRO problems with a 365-day planning horizon, emphasizing the scalability limitations of exact methods. This has motivated the development of heuristic and hybrid approaches that can provide high-quality solutions within reasonable computing time.

1.2.2
Heuristic and Metaheuristic Methods

Given the computational challenges of exact methods, heuristic methods have been widely studied to solve large-scale MIRO problems. Among these, evolutionary algorithms – specifically genetic algorithms and particle swarm optimization are particularly well studied
(
Siswanto et al. 2019
,
Al-Haidous et al. 2022
)
. However, evolutionary methods often require extensive parameter tuning and are not always well-suited for problems with strict operational constraints.

In contrast, LNS
(
Ahuja et al. 2002
)
has gained significant traction due to its ability to efficiently explore large solution spaces by iteratively destroying and repairing parts of a solution. This makes it particularly well suited for LNG MIRO problems, where long planning horizons and operational constraints necessitate scalable and effective solution approaches.

In the MIRO literature, LNS has been especially adapted to LNG inventory routing due to the need for long-term scheduling over planning horizons of up to 365 days.
Goel et al. 2012
adapted the discrete-time arc-flow framework proposed by
Song and Furman 2013
for the LNG MIRO problem, incorporating real-world constraints such as seasonal production rates and berth availability at ports. Their approach used a construction heuristic to generate an initial solution, followed by LNS with time-window and two-ship neighborhoods to iteratively improve it.

Building on this,
Shao et al. 2015
introduced a more sophisticated framework that applies a greedy randomized adaptive search procedure (GRASP) and a rolling-time heuristic for initial solution generation. They then applied a suite of small, diverse neighborhood searches, outperforming the LNS approach of
Goel et al. 2012
. More recently,
Munguía et al. 2019
explored parallel computing techniques to enhance the efficiency of LNS algorithms, demonstrating their potential for solving large-scale MIRO instances more effectively.

While LNS has proven to be a powerful tool for large-scale MIRO, a key challenge remains: The selection of neighborhoods is typically done heuristically or at random, even though not all neighborhoods contribute equally to improving the solution. Moreover, evaluating a neighborhood requires solving an optimization problem, making each exploration step computationally expensive.

In this work, we propose using machine learning (ML) to guide the selection of neighborhoods in an LNS scheme designed to solve large-scale instances of the LNG-IRO problem. By learning patterns from high-quality solutions, our approach aims to identify promising neighborhoods more effectively, reducing unnecessary evaluations and improving the overall efficiency of the search process. This represents a novel direction in the application of machine learning to large-scale optimization problems, addressing a key limitation of existing LNS methods.

1.2.3
ML-augmented Local Search

The last decade has witnessed an impressive amount of work devoted to exploring the synergies between machine learning and discrete optimization. Those synergies go both ways, with combinatorial optimization (CO) applied to many significant learning problems, for example in the area of interpretable ML
(
Rudin and Ertekin 2018
,
Rudin et al. 2022
)
, and with ML applied in a variety of ways to help solving of combinatorial optimization problems
(
Bengio et al. 2021
,
Kotary et al. 2021
,
Scavuzzo et al. 2024
)
.

For the latter case, the first attempted paradigm has been “end-to-end” learning, that is constructing a heuristic solution directly by training an ML model
(
Khalil et al. 2017
,
Nazari et al. 2018
,
Kool et al. 2018
,
Zhang et al. 2020
,
Liu et al. 2021b
)
. Those methods are typically designed for specific CO problems that are not (tightly) constrained, and a heuristic solution can be easily constructed. Conversely, since a wide range of constrained CO problems can be formulated into a mixed-integer programming (MIP) model, there has also been increasing interest in learning decision rules to improve MIP algorithms
(
Gasse et al. 2019
,
Sonnerat et al. 2021
,
Nair et al. 2020
,
Bonami et al. 2022
)
. The role of
graph neural networks
(GNNs)
(
Gori et al. 2005
,
Scarselli et al. 2008
,
Hamilton et al. 2017
,
Cappart et al. 2023
)
in developing ML methods for CO has been impressive.

Especially relevant in the context of the current paper are the efforts to improve local search, especially LNS algorithms for combinatorial optimization, see, e.g.,
Liu et al. 2023
,
Liu et al. 2022
. There, the scope is to learn structural properties of the neighborhoods that are iteratively explored by local search and metaheuristic methods to compute high-quality solutions in a reasonable amount of computing time, that is, when solving the problem of interest to proven optimality is out of reach. Such learning might be crucial for multiple reasons, the most common one being that the local search scheme can pick a “good” neighborhood without resorting to computationally expensive analyses, for example by learning to imitate an expensive expert.

This is precisely what we are proposing to do here: It is computationally expensive to find even a single feasible solution for the MIRO formulation discussed in the next section, so we construct an associated feasibility problem and we solve it by local search, exploring large neighborhoods learned by sophisticated ML methods. The approach is detailed in Sections
2
and
3
.

1.3
Problem Formulation and Challenges

Modern LNG inventory routing consists of a substantial set of components to model real-world operations. The LNG inventory routing problem considered in this paper involves the scheduling of a heterogeneous set of vessels, as well as a set of non-vessel operations (such as connecting two parties who respectively have to buy and sell interests), over a given planning horizon, to fulfill a heterogeneous set of buying and selling contracts and meet various practical operational requirements (e.g., tankfarm inventory limits, vessel-contract-LNG compatibility, vessel bunkering and maintenance, port capacity). The input data can be classified into two types: individual attributes and pair attributes. Individual attributes are attributes associated with individual components in the problem, including attributes associated with a specific

1.

Vessel (e.g., vessel type and starting location);

2.

Port (e.g., part capacity, unloading duration);

3.

Tankfarm (e.g., inventory limits, LNG production);

4.

Contract (e.g., time requirement, location requirement).

There are also pair attributes associated with pairs of the components above, e.g., sailing duration is associated with pairs of ports, and contract-vessel compatibility is associated with contract-vessel pairs.

The problem formulation we use for our LNG inventory routing problem is built on top of the time-space network (TSN) formulation proposed by
Song and Furman 2013
, which discretizes the continuous planning horizon into discrete time points (days in our case). Each vessel activity is restricted to start at one of these different discrete time points. With that, one can model the activities of individual vessels on individual vessel TSNs. Each vessel TSN is represented by a directed graph, where nodes represent the time-space status of the vessel and arcs represent feasible time-space status transitions (including the associated vessel activities) of the vessel. For each vessel TSN, there is a source node and a sink node representing the initial and ending status of the vessel, respectively, and each vessel schedule is represented by a directed path from the source node to the sink node. Figure
1
shows an example of a TSN having only one production port and one regas port, with transition duration from one port to the other equal to 1. In general, the problem will have multiple production ports, multiple regas ports, and a much longer planning horizon (usually around a year).

Figure 1:
A TSN with a single production port and a single regas port

We focus on creating a feasible schedule that meets all the operational constraints. Specifically, the MIP model takes the following form:

The restricted MIP subproblems are solved using a commercial solver with a given computational budget. We will specify our large neighborhood search strategies later.

2
ML Model for Finding Good Neighborhoods

Local search heuristics have been used extensively for solving decomposable optimization problems, such as LNG inventory routing
(
Goel et al. 2012
,
Shao et al. 2015
)
. The idea is to improve the current solution and restrict the search for new solutions in a lower dimensional space so that restricted subproblems can be solved efficiently. Specifically, local search moves from one solution to another by applying local changes to a set of free variables. For an optimization problem of the form
\max_{x}\{f(x):x\in S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\}
, at a particular iteration with the incumbent solution
x=\widehat{x}
, we choose a subset
F\subseteq[n]
of the variable indices and solve the problem
\max_{x}\{f(x):x\in S;~x_{i}=\widehat{x}_{i},~i\in[n]\setminus F\}
to obtain a new solution. This local search step is repeated until a satisfactory solution is found. A key decision in this local search process is the choice of the index set
F
in each iteration.

2.1
Vessel Neighborhoods

For combinatorial optimization problems with specific structures like LNG inventory routing, the index set
I
in each local search step is often selected based on neighborhoods defined by specific components of the problem. In the context of LNG inventory routing, such neighborhoods can be defined by vessels, time windows, contracts, or locations. For simplicity, we considered only neighborhoods defined by vessels. However, the techniques we developed are not specific to vessel neighborhoods and can be used for other more general neighborhoods as well.

Building on the two-ship improvement heuristic proposed in
Goel et al. 2012
, we specifically tested local search approaches using two-vessel neighborhoods. In this approach, the free variables
x_{I}
are defined by
(x_{v},x_{v^{\prime}})
associated with a pair
\{v,v^{\prime}\}\subseteq V\cup\{0\}
with
v\neq v^{\prime}
.

2.2
Neighborhood selection rules and the chosen expert

Since the number of pairs of vessels that one can potentially use in a local search is large, finding the optimal pair of vessels to use is non-trivial (even the definition of “optimal” is not trivial in this case).
We tested a variety of different neighborhood selection rules, namely

1.

Random
: uniformly sample a pair
\{v,v^{\prime}\}
from
V\cup\{0\}
;

2.

LP
: solve the linear programming relaxations associated with all possible pairs
\{v,v^{\prime}\}
and select the one that leads to he best dual bound;

3.

Root
: solve the subMIPs associated with all possible pairs
\{v,v^{\prime}\}
without branching, i.e., at the root node, and select the one that leads to the best dual bound;

4.

Exact
: solve the subMIPs associated with all possible pairs
\{v,v^{\prime}\}
until hitting the time limit, and select the one that leads to the best primal bound.

Based on several preliminary experiments comparing the performance of these neighborhood selection rules in the context of LNG inventory routing, as one might expect,
Random
>
LP
>
Root
>
Exact
in terms of the running time. On the other hand,
Random
<
LP
<
Root
<
Exact
in terms of the quality of the selected neighborhoods, and the difference in quality can be significant. For example, for one particular test instance,
Random
takes
188
iterations to find a completely feasible schedule while
Exact
takes only
25
iterations. Therefore, it is evident that local search can be significantly accelerated by using strong neighborhood selection rules. However, directly applying
LP
,
Root
, or
Exact
in our solution framework would be computationally infeasible as they are very computationally prohibitive (often orders of magnitude slower than applying
Random
). We then explore the use of machine learning tools to imitate the behavior of these strong neighborhood selection rules.

In our setting, we select
Root
as the expert,
aiming to strike a balance between policy quality and the amount of effort required for data collection.

2.3
Graph Neural Networks for Neighborhood Search

Recently, different works (see, e.g., the review paper by
(
Cappart et al. 2023

) have explored the use of graph neural networks in the context of combinatorial optimization. As a learning framework, GNNs have many favorable properties, such as permutation invariance, sparsity, and scalability. In the construction of the input graph, key elements in CO are encoded by nodes with (multi-dimensional) attributes, and the relationships between different elements are encoded by edges with (multi-dimensional) attributes. GNNs then iteratively update the nodes by aggregating information from their neighbors with the goal of minimizing a particular loss function.

2.3.1
Feature Engineering

We represent the information available to select neighborhoods as a multipartite graph with attributes. The attributes are very high-dimensional in comparison to the number of data points we have, as many features are indexed by combinations of vessels/ports and dates. Therefore, some feature engineering/selection is critical here to avoid overfitting the model. The selected features are also listed in Table
1
.

2.3.2
GNN Architecture

Our GNN design includes a succession of graph convolutions along this graph. Then, the vessel nodes, whose embeddings now encode (after the convolutions) information about the whole graph, are input to a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) that takes embeddings of pairs of nodes and returns a scalar representing a score for that pair. Finally, a softmax normalizes these scalars into a distribution over these pairs of vessels.

2.3.3
Loss Functions

The simplest strategy is to treat the problem as a classification problem, where vessel pairs that had the highest expert score (could have been selected by the expert) are given the label 1, and the others are given the label 0. We then train with a cross-entropy loss to increase the probability of those pairs with label 1, and reduce the probability of those pairs with label 0.

Alternatives include giving label 1 to every vessel pair that has top 5 best expert scores, instead of top 1. We have experimented with both two labeling strategies and the results of our performance are reported in Section
4.2.1
.

2.3.4
Data Collection

As we have a limited number of instances available for training, it seems tricky to learn anything from a small set of instances at first glance. However, note that the neighborhood decisions have to be made multiple times during the solution process. Therefore, we can collect multiple samples in one run. Furthermore, we can apply the following procedure to generate multiple (potentially different) solution paths for each instance:

1.

At the beginning of each local search step, we flip a biased coin (with a random number generator) with the head being “expert” (80
\%
chance) and the tail being “random” (20
\%
chance).

2.

If the outcome is the head (i.e., “expert”), then we apply the expert
Root
neighborhood selection rule, run the local search with the neighborhood selected by the expert, and collect the expert sample.

3.

Otherwise, we randomly select the neighborhood and run the local search with the random neighborhood.

4.

We then continue with the local search.

With different random seeds, we run local searches on each instance multiple times following the hybrid neighborhood decision rules mentioned above. By doing so, we can collect even more data samples as (potentially) different sequences of samples can be collected within different runs of the same instance.

2.3.5
The DAGGER Algorithm

After collecting the initial training dataset following the approach described in Section
2.3.4
, the next step is to design the algorithm for training the GNN models on the collected dataset. The most straightforward way is to define the training problem as a one-time supervised learning problem, where the training data are collected offline before training and fixed forever. Then the GNNs will be trained by minimizing the supervised loss presented in Section
2.3.3
on the training dataset.

A different line of research has been focusing on improving the out-of-sample performance of the GNN model. As our problem is essentially an online learning problem (decisions are made in a sequential manner), we are considering the DAGGER algorithm
(
Ross et al. 2011
)
to train our GNN model. Specifically, the GNN model is trained iteratively using aggregated data that is collected from each iteration. In the first iteration, the data is generated from the trajectory given by the
Root
policy (
\alpha\%
chance following
Root
and
(100-\alpha)\%
chance selecting a random neighborhood for
\alpha\in\{60,70,80,90,100\}
). In the subsequent iterations, the data is generated by roughly following the policy associated with the GNN trained in the last iteration (
\alpha\%
chance following GNN and
(100-\alpha)\%
selecting a random neighborhood for
\alpha\in\{60,70,80,90,100\}
). As the algorithm requires several rounds of data collection and training, it is more time-consuming than the vanilla one-time approach.

3
Proposed Algorithms

The instances we consider can be categorized into two classes based on the constraints they include. The first class, which we refer to as combinatorial instances, consists of instances where only combinatorial constraints are present. The second class, called hybrid instances, includes both inventory and combinatorial constraints. For combinatorial instances (i.e., the first class), we apply a local search method: Our objective is to find a feasible solution to Problem (
1
) such that
\sum_{i\in I}s_{i}=0
in the objective function of Problem (
1
).
For hybrid instances (the second class), we take a two-phase approach. In the first phase, we initially ignore the inventory constraints and focus solely on the combinatorial constraints. We first perform the local search to obtain the best solution under these constraints. Once this preliminary solution is found, in the second phase, we refine it by incorporating the inventory constraints and using off-the-shelf solvers to further optimize the solution.

3.2
Feasibility Refinement via Gurobi

After obtaining a high-quality solution from the local search phase, we refine it by incorporating inventory constraints and solving the full MIP model using Gurobi. This refinement phase ensures that the final solution is feasible to Problem (
1
) with both combinatorial constraints and inventory constraints. Naturally, since the local search phase (phase 1) only considers combinatorial constraints, the resulting solution may not fully satisfy inventory constraints. To address this, we initialize the MIP model that explicitly enforces these inventory constraints and combinatorial constraints and use the best solution from local search (i.e., the output of Algorithm
1
) as a warm start in Gurobi. The refinement phase is outlined in Algorithm

Computational Results

All experiments were run on a computer with an Intel(R) Xeon(R) Gold 6258R processor running at 2.7 GHz, with up to four threads used. All optimization problems were solved using the optimization solver Gurobi with version 12.0.0. First, we provide a full description of the instances.

4.1
Description of Instances

We evaluate the performance of the proposed algorithm on a set of synthetic problem instances designed to closely resemble real-world LNG-IRO problems faced by a major vertically integrated energy company. In this work, we provide only a high-level overview of these instances to give the reader an understanding of their size and complexity (refer to Table
2
for a summary of the key statistics of the problem instances). The full set of instances, along with their best-known benchmarks, will be released in a future publication.

Each instance is characterized primarily by the total LNG volume transported from supply locations to demand locations over a 365-day planning horizon. In this study, we vary this total volume between 8 and 20 million metric tonnes.

Once the total volume for an instance is determined, we allocate it across two types of supply sources: 1) Third-party buying contracts, and 2) Tank farms. Here, each instance is randomly assigned between 0 and 2 tank farms, where inventory levels must be maintained within specified limits. On the demand side, we generate a set of sell contracts, ensuring that the total demand is slightly lower than the total supply. Any excess supply beyond contractual obligations can be sold through the spot market.

For each buy and sell contract, we impose constraints on the minimum and maximum number of deliveries or purchases required over different time intervals (e.g., 1, 3, 6, and 12 months).

Each contract and tank farm is associated with a specific port that serves as the physical location for LNG loading and unloading. Ports have a predetermined number of berths, limiting the number of vessels that can be serviced simultaneously. We randomly assign real-world geographic locations to these ports, ensuring that their placement aligns with actual LNG supply and demand centers. Sailing times between ports range from less than a day to over 65 days.

We generate vessels with capacities ranging from 155,000 to 175,000 cubic meters. Each instance includes up to 25 vessels, each randomly assigned one of two fuel modes: heavy fuel oil, which requires additional bunkering for fuel replenishment, and Boil-off LNG, which uses a portion of the transported LNG as fuel, eliminating the need for separate bunkering.

The generated problem instances are of realistic size and incorporate all key real-world operational constraints encountered in the LNG-IRO problem. They capture the complexities of supply and demand balancing, contractual obligations, port and berth limitations, sailing times, and vessel fuel considerations.

Conclusion

In this work, we proposed a machine learning-based local search approach for finding feasible solutions of large-scale maritime inventory routing optimization problems. Given the combinatorial complexity of MIRO, particularly in cases with inventory constraints, we integrated a GNN-based neighborhood selection method to enhance local search efficiency. Our approach enables a structured exploration of vessel neighborhoods, improving solution quality while maintaining computational efficiency. Through extensive computational experiments on synthetic instances, we demonstrated that our method outperforms direct MIP approaches in solution time. Beyond its immediate application in MIRO, our methodology offers broader implications for learning-based optimization in large-scale industrial logistics problems. Future research directions include extending the GNN framework to handle additional problem structures, incorporating uncertainty in demand and transit times, and exploring different learning techniques for further improving neighborhood selection strategies.
